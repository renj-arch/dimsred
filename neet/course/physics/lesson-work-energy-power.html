1. Work and Energy: Fundamental Concepts

1.1 Definition of Work

Work is defined as the product of the component of a force acting along the direction of displacement and the magnitude of that displacement. When a force F acts on a particle that moves through a displacement s, the work done by the force is W = F·s = F s cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. This definition makes work a scalar quantity; it has magnitude but no direction, and it can be positive, negative, or zero depending on the relative orientation of force and motion. The concept of work links the mechanical action of a force to the energy transferred to or from a system, providing the bridge between Newton’s laws of motion and the energy formulation of mechanics.

Because work depends on the path taken only through the displacement, it is independent of the time taken for the motion. A force that is always perpendicular to the instantaneous displacement, such as the magnetic component of the Lorentz force on a charged particle, does no work because cos 90° = 0. Conversely, a force that has a component opposite to the displacement, like kinetic friction, performs negative work, removing kinetic energy from the system. Understanding these sign conventions is essential for correctly applying the work‑energy theorem later in the chapter.

1.2 Scalar Nature of Work

The scalar nature of work means that the total work done by several forces acting simultaneously is the algebraic sum of the individual works. If forces F₁, F₂, …, Fₙ act on a body that undergoes a displacement s, the net work is Wₙₑₜ = Σ (Fᵢ·s). This additive property follows directly from the linearity of the dot product and allows us to treat complex force systems by superposition. It also implies that work can be represented as the area under a force‑versus‑displacement graph when the force is collinear with the displacement, a graphical interpretation that becomes powerful for variable forces.

Because work is a scalar, it does not obey vector addition rules; there is no concept of a “direction of work.” This distinguishes work from impulse, which is a vector quantity equal to the integral of force over time. The scalar character of work is the reason why kinetic energy, which is derived from work, is also a scalar, and why the work‑energy theorem relates a scalar change in kinetic energy to the net scalar work done.

1.3 Units and Sign Conventions

The SI unit of work is the joule (J), defined as one newton metre (N·m). One joule is the work done when a force of one newton moves an object one metre in the direction of the force. In the CGS system the unit is the erg, where 1 J = 10⁷ erg. Positive work indicates that the force component along the displacement adds energy to the system, while negative work indicates energy removal. Zero work occurs when the force is perpendicular to the displacement or when there is no displacement at all. These conventions are universally adopted in physics examinations and must be applied consistently when evaluating work done by gravity, friction, tension, or any other force.

2. Work Done by Constant Forces

2.1 Force Parallel to Displacement

When a constant force acts exactly along the line of motion, the angle θ is zero and cos θ = 1. The work simplifies to W = F s. This situation appears frequently in problems involving a block pulled horizontally by a rope, a car pushed by a constant engine force, or a weight lifted vertically at constant speed. Because the force magnitude and direction do not change, the work is directly proportional to the displacement. If the displacement doubles, the work doubles, illustrating the linear relationship that makes constant‑force problems straightforward to solve.

An important consequence of this linear relationship is that the work done by a constant force can be represented graphically as the area of a rectangle on a force‑versus‑displacement plot. The height of the rectangle is the constant force magnitude, and the width is the displacement. This geometric interpretation provides a quick visual check: the area must equal the numerical value of F s. It also sets the stage for understanding how the area under a curve generalises to variable forces.

2.2 Force at an Angle

In many practical situations the applied force is not collinear with the displacement. A classic example is a lawn‑mower pushed at an angle θ to the horizontal. Only the horizontal component F cos θ contributes to work, while the vertical component F sin θ does no work because there is no vertical displacement (assuming the mower stays on the ground). The work done is therefore W = F s cos θ. As the angle increases from 0° to 90°, the work decreases continuously, becoming zero at 90°. If the angle exceeds 90°, the cosine becomes negative and the force does negative work, opposing the motion.

This angular dependence explains why pulling a sled with a rope at a shallow angle is more efficient than pulling at a steep angle: a larger fraction of the applied force is directed along the displacement. In examination problems, students are often asked to compute the work done by a force given its magnitude, the displacement, and the angle, or to find the angle required for a specified amount of work. Mastery of the cosine factor is therefore essential.

2.3 Work by Multiple Constant Forces

When several constant forces act simultaneously, the net work is the algebraic sum of the works done by each force. Consider a block on a rough horizontal surface pulled by a horizontal force F while kinetic friction fₖ opposes the motion. The work done by the applied force is W_F = F s, the work done by friction is W_f = –fₖ s, and the normal and weight forces do zero work because they are perpendicular to the displacement. The net work is Wₙₑₜ = (F – fₖ) s. This net work equals the change in kinetic energy of the block, a direct illustration of the work‑energy theorem.

The principle of superposition of work simplifies the analysis of complex force systems. Instead of finding the resultant force first, one may compute the work of each force separately and then add them. This approach is particularly useful when forces act at different angles, because each work term involves its own cosine factor. The additive property also underlies the definition of potential energy for conservative forces, where the work done by the force depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken.

Example: Work Done by a Constant Force at an Angle
A 10 kg crate is pulled across a horizontal floor by a force of 50 N directed at 30° above the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the crate and the floor is 0.2. The crate moves a distance of 5 m. Calculate (a) the work done by the applied force, (b) the work done by friction, and (c) the net work done on the crate.
(a) The horizontal component of the applied force is Fₓ = 50 cos 30° = 50 × √3/2 ≈ 43.3 N. Work done by the applied force: W_F = Fₓ × s = 43.3 N × 5 m ≈ 216.5 J. (b) Normal force N = mg – F sin 30° = (10 × 9.8) – 50 × 0.5 = 98 – 25 = 73 N. Friction force fₖ = μₖ N = 0.2 × 73 = 14.6 N. Work done by friction: W_f = –fₖ × s = –14.6 N × 5 m = –73 J. (c) Net work Wₙₑₜ = W_F + W_f = 216.5 J – 73 J = 143.5 J. The positive net work indicates an increase in the crate’s kinetic energy.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem gives a force at an angle, always resolve the force into components parallel and perpendicular to the displacement before computing work. The perpendicular component does zero work regardless of its magnitude, and forgetting this step is a common source of sign errors in multiple‑choice questions.

3. Work Done by Variable Forces

3.1 Integral Formulation

For a force that varies in magnitude or direction along the path, the work cannot be obtained by a simple product. Instead, the work is defined as the line integral of the force vector dot the infinitesimal displacement vector: W = ∫_C F·dr. If the motion is constrained to a straight line along the x‑axis and the force varies only with position x, the integral reduces to W = ∫x_ix_f F(x) dx. This formulation captures the cumulative effect of a continuously changing force and is the foundation for analysing springs, gravitational fields near massive bodies, and any non‑uniform force field.

The integral approach also clarifies why work is path‑dependent for non‑conservative forces. If the force has a component perpendicular to the displacement at some points, the dot product automatically excludes that component. For a closed loop, the net work of a conservative force is zero, whereas for a non‑conservative force like friction the integral around a closed path is negative, reflecting energy dissipation. Understanding the integral definition is crucial for deriving potential energy functions and for solving problems where the force is given as a function of position.

3.2 Graphical Interpretation

When a variable force acts along a straight line, the work done is equal to the area under the force‑versus‑displacement curve between the initial and final positions. Positive areas (above the displacement axis) correspond to positive work, while negative areas (below the axis) correspond to negative work. This geometric picture allows quick estimation of work without performing an explicit integration, provided the graph is simple (e.g., triangles, rectangles, or trapezoids). In examinations, students are often presented with a piecewise‑linear F‑x graph and asked to compute the work by summing the areas of the constituent shapes.

The area interpretation also reinforces the scalar nature of work: the sign of the area directly gives the sign of the work. If the force changes direction relative to the displacement, the graph will cross the axis, and the total work is the algebraic sum of the signed areas. This visual method is especially helpful for checking the plausibility of an analytical integration result.

3.3 Example: Spring Force

An ideal spring obeys Hooke’s law, F = –k x, where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from the natural length. The negative sign indicates that the spring force always opposes the displacement. The work done by the spring when it is stretched or compressed from x_i to x_f is W = ∫x_ix_f (–k x) dx = –½ k (x_f² – x_i²). If the spring is released from rest at x_i = A and moves to the equilibrium position x_f = 0, the work done by the spring is W = ½ k A², a positive quantity because the force and displacement are in the same direction during the return motion.

This result shows that the spring stores energy when deformed; the magnitude of the stored elastic potential energy is U = ½ k x². The work done by the spring is the negative of the change in this potential energy, a hallmark of conservative forces. In problems involving a mass attached to a spring, the work‑energy theorem combined with the spring work expression yields the speed of the mass at any position without solving the differential equation of motion.

4. Kinetic Energy and the Work‑Energy Theorem

4.1 Derivation of Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is defined as the energy a body possesses by virtue of its motion. Starting from Newton’s second law, F = m a, and the definition of work for a constant force, W = F s, we can write W = m a s. Using the kinematic relation v_f² = v_i² + 2 a s (valid for constant acceleration), we eliminate a s to obtain W = ½ m v_f² – ½ m v_i². The quantity ½ m v² is identified as the kinetic energy K. This derivation shows that the work done by the net force on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy, regardless of whether the force is constant or variable, provided the work is evaluated as the integral of the net force.

The kinetic energy formula K = ½ m v² is a scalar, always non‑negative, and depends only on the speed, not the direction of motion. Its SI unit is the joule, the same as work. Because kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy, a fact that has profound implications for vehicle safety, projectile penetration, and energy budgeting in collisions.

4.2 Work‑Energy Theorem Statement

The work‑energy theorem states that the net work done by all forces acting on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy: Wₙₑₜ = ΔK = K_f – K_i. This theorem is a direct consequence of Newton’s second law and the definition of work, and it holds for any force, constant or variable, conservative or non‑conservative. It provides a powerful alternative to solving Newton’s second law differential equations when only the initial and final speeds are required.

In practice, the theorem is applied by computing the work of each force (including friction, gravity, normal, tension, spring, etc.) and summing them to obtain Wₙₑₜ. The result is then set equal to ½ m v_f² – ½ m v_i². If the initial speed is known, the final speed follows immediately. Conversely, if the final speed is known, the theorem can be used to find an unknown force magnitude or coefficient of friction. The theorem also extends to systems of particles by summing the kinetic energies of all particles and the works of all external and internal forces.

4.3 Applications to Variable Forces

When forces vary with position, the work‑energy theorem remains valid because the work integral automatically accounts for the variation. For a particle moving under a position‑dependent force F(x), the net work is ∫ F(x) dx. Equating this to the kinetic energy change yields an equation that can be solved for the speed as a function of position: v(x) = √[v_i² + (2/m) ∫x_ix F(x') dx']. This approach is the basis for the concept of potential energy, where the integral of a conservative force is expressed as the negative change in a potential function.

A classic application is the motion of a particle in a gravitational field where the force varies as F = –GMm/r². The work integral gives the change in gravitational potential energy, and the work‑energy theorem then provides the speed at any distance without solving the orbital equations. Similarly, for a mass on a spring, the theorem combined with the spring work expression yields the simple harmonic motion energy exchange between kinetic and elastic potential energy.

5. Potential Energy and Conservative Forces

5.1 Gravitational Potential Energy

Gravitational potential energy near the Earth’s surface is defined as U = m g h, where h is the vertical height measured from an arbitrary reference level. The work done by gravity when an object moves vertically from height h_i to h_f is W_g = –mg (h_f – h_i) = –ΔU. The negative sign indicates that gravity does positive work when the object descends (ΔU negative) and negative work when it ascends. Because the work depends only on the initial and final heights, gravity is a conservative force.

For larger distances where the gravitational force varies as F = –GMm/r², the potential energy is defined as U(r) = –GMm/r (zero at infinity). The work done by gravity in moving from r_i to r_f is again the negative change in this potential energy. This universal form is essential for orbital mechanics and for understanding escape velocity, where the total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) determines whether a body remains bound.

5.2 Elastic Potential Energy

As derived in the variable‑force section, the work done by an ideal spring is W_s = –½ k (x_f² – x_i²). Defining the elastic potential energy as U_s = ½ k x² makes the work equal to the negative change in potential energy: W_s = –ΔU_s. This energy is stored in the spring when it is stretched or compressed and is fully recoverable if the spring is ideal and no damping occurs. The quadratic dependence on displacement means that doubling the extension quadruples the stored energy, a principle exploited in devices such as bow‑and‑arrow, catapults, and vehicle suspension systems.

In a mass‑spring system oscillating on a frictionless surface, the total mechanical energy E = K + U_s remains constant. At the turning points the kinetic energy is zero and all energy is potential; at the equilibrium position the potential energy is zero and all energy is kinetic. This continuous exchange illustrates the conservation of mechanical energy for a system acted upon only by conservative forces.

5.3 Conservative vs Non‑Conservative Forces

A force is conservative if the work done by it on a particle moving between two points is independent of the path taken, or equivalently, if the work done around any closed loop is zero. Gravity, electrostatic force, and the ideal spring force are conservative. For such forces a potential energy function can be defined, and the total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) is conserved when only conservative forces do work.

Non‑conservative forces, such as kinetic friction, air resistance, and tension in a rope that is being pulled by an external agent, dissipate mechanical energy into internal energy (heat, sound). The work done by non‑conservative forces depends on the path length, and the mechanical energy of the system changes by an amount equal to the work done by these forces: ΔE_mech = W_nc. In problems involving both types of forces, the work‑energy theorem is written as W_c + W_nc = ΔK, where W_c = –ΔU. Rearranging gives ΔK + ΔU = W_nc, a form that directly shows how non‑conservative work alters the total mechanical energy.

6. Collisions and Conservation Laws

6.1 Elastic Collisions in One Dimension

An elastic collision is one in which both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. For two particles of masses m₁ and m₂ with initial velocities u₁ and u₂ (taken positive in the same direction), the conservation equations are m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂ = m₁ v₁ + m₂ v₂ (momentum) and ½ m₁ u₁² + ½ m₂ u₂² = ½ m₁ v₁² + ½ m₂ v₂² (kinetic energy). Solving these simultaneously yields the well‑known formulas for the final velocities: v₁ = (m₁ – m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) u₁ + (2 m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) u₂ and v₂ = (2 m₁)/(m₁ + m₂) u₁ + (m₂ – m₁)/(m₁ + m₂) u₂. These expressions show that in a head‑on elastic collision the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation, a useful shortcut for quick calculations.

Special cases illustrate the physics: if the masses are equal, the velocities are exchanged; if a light particle strikes a massive stationary target, the light particle rebounds with nearly the same speed while the massive target acquires a small velocity; if a massive projectile hits a light stationary target, the projectile continues almost unchanged while the light target shoots forward with approximately twice the projectile’s speed. These limiting behaviours are frequently tested in multiple‑choice questions.

6.2 Inelastic Collisions and Coefficient of Restitution

In an inelastic collision kinetic energy is not conserved, although momentum always is (provided no external impulses act). The extreme case is a perfectly inelastic collision, where the two bodies stick together and move with a common final velocity v = (m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂)/(m₁ + m₂). The loss in kinetic energy appears as internal energy (heat, sound, deformation). For partially elastic collisions the coefficient of restitution e quantifies the degree of elasticity: e = (v₂ – v₁)/(u₁ – u₂), where the numerator is the relative speed of separation and the denominator is the relative speed of approach. The value of e ranges from 0 (perfectly inelastic) to 1 (perfectly elastic).

Using e together with momentum conservation allows the final velocities to be found for any given e. The kinetic energy loss can then be computed as ΔK = ½ μ (1 – e²) (u₁ – u₂)², where μ = m₁ m₂/(m₁ + m₂) is the reduced mass. This formula shows that the energy loss is proportional to 1 – e² and to the square of the relative speed, explaining why high‑speed collisions are far more destructive even for the same coefficient of restitution.

6.3 Two‑Dimensional Collisions

When collisions occur in two dimensions, momentum conservation must be applied as a vector equation: m₁ u⃗₁ + m₂ u⃗₂ = m₁ v⃗₁ + m₂ v⃗₂. Kinetic energy conservation (for elastic collisions) remains a scalar equation. The problem typically provides the initial velocities and the scattering angle of one particle; the unknowns are the final speeds and the direction of the second particle. Solving requires resolving the momentum equation into components (usually x and y) and using the energy equation as the third independent relation.

A common simplification is the “glancing collision” where one particle is initially at rest. In that case the momentum vectors form a triangle, and the angle between the final velocities is 90° for equal masses in an elastic collision. This geometric property follows from the vector addition of momentum and the Pythagorean relation implied by energy conservation. Understanding the vector nature of momentum and the scalar nature of energy is essential for correctly setting up the equations and avoiding sign errors in the component equations.

7. Power and Its Applications

7.1 Definition and Units of Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. The average power over a time interval Δt is P_avg = W/Δt. The instantaneous power is the time derivative of work: P = dW/dt = F·v, where v is the instantaneous velocity of the point of application of the force. The SI unit of power is the watt (W), equal to one joule per second (J/s). In mechanical contexts, horsepower (hp) is still encountered, with 1 hp = 746 W. Power quantifies how quickly a machine can perform a task; a high‑power engine can do the same work in less time than a low‑power one.

Because power is the dot product of force and velocity, it is a scalar that can be positive, negative, or zero. Positive power means the force adds energy to the system (e.g., a motor driving a wheel), negative power means the force removes energy (e.g., regenerative braking), and zero power occurs when the force is perpendicular to the velocity (e.g., centripetal force in uniform circular motion). This sign convention mirrors that of work and is consistent with the work‑energy theorem expressed in rate form: P_net = dK/dt.

7.2 Average vs Instantaneous Power

Average power is useful when the force or velocity varies over a long interval and only the total work and total time are known. For example, a car that accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 10 s, doing 200 kJ of work, has an average power of 20 kW.

Work–Energy Theorem in Non‑Inertial Frames

The work–energy theorem Wnet = ΔK is derived assuming an inertial reference frame. In a frame accelerating with acceleration a0 a pseudo force Fp = –m a0 acts on every particle of mass m. This force is non‑conservative in general because its work depends on the path taken in the accelerating frame. The net work in the non‑inertial frame becomes Wnet = Wreal + Wpseudo, and the theorem reads Wreal + Wpseudo = ΔK. If the pseudo force is constant (uniformly accelerating frame) its work is Wpseudo = –m a0·Δr, which can be absorbed into a potential Upseudo = m a0·r. In that special case a modified potential energy can be defined and mechanical energy is conserved if only conservative real forces act. In a rotating frame two pseudo forces appear: the centrifugal force Fcf = m ω² r (directed radially outward) and the Coriolis force Fcor = –2m ω × v. The centrifugal force is conservative; its potential is Ucf = –½ m ω² r². The Coriolis force is always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity in the rotating frame, so its work is zero and it does not alter kinetic energy. Consequently, for a particle moving under conservative real forces in a uniformly rotating frame, the quantity E' = K + Ureal + Ucf remains constant. A classic illustration is a bead sliding on a frictionless rotating hoop: the effective potential combines gravity and the centrifugal term, and equilibrium positions are found by differentiating this effective potential. When the frame acceleration is non‑uniform, the pseudo force becomes time‑dependent and no scalar potential exists; the work–energy theorem must then be applied with the explicit pseudo‑work term. Examiners often test the sign of pseudo‑work: in an elevator accelerating upward, the pseudo force points downward, so a block displaced upward does negative pseudo‑work, reducing its kinetic energy in the elevator frame.

Potential Energy Curves and Equilibrium Analysis

A one‑dimensional potential energy function U(x) provides a complete qualitative picture of the motion of a particle of mass m under a conservative force F(x) = –dU/dx. The total mechanical energy E = K + U(x) is constant. Turning points occur where K = 0, i.e. U(x) = E; the particle cannot enter regions where U(x) > E. Equilibrium positions satisfy F = 0 → dU/dx = 0. Stability is decided by the second derivative: if d²U/dx² > 0 the equilibrium is stable (potential minimum), if d²U/dx² < 0 it is unstable (potential maximum), and if d²U/dx² = 0 higher‑order terms determine neutral or metastable behaviour. For small oscillations about a stable minimum, expand U(x) ≈ U(x₀) + ½ k (x – x₀)² with k = d²U/dx²|ₓ₀; the motion is simple harmonic with angular frequency ω = √(k/m). This method underlies the analysis of molecular vibrations (Lennard‑Jones potential), the motion of a particle in a double‑well potential, and the effective radial potential in central‑force problems Ueff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2mr²), where the centrifugal term creates a barrier that can produce stable circular orbits. A common examiner twist is to give a potential graph and ask for the number and type of equilibria, the range of energies for bounded motion, or the period of small oscillations using the curvature at the minimum. Students must remember that the force is the negative slope of the curve; a positive slope means a force directed toward negative x. Misreading the sign is a frequent source of error.

Worked Example: Equilibrium and Oscillation Frequency
A particle of mass 2 kg moves along the x‑axis under the potential U(x) = 4x⁴ – 8x² + 3 (joules). Find all equilibrium positions, classify their stability, and compute the angular frequency of small oscillations about each stable equilibrium.
First derivative: dU/dx = 16x³ – 16x = 16x(x² – 1). Setting to zero gives x = 0, x = ±1. Second derivative: d²U/dx² = 48x² – 16. At x = 0, d²U/dx² = –16 < 0 → unstable equilibrium (potential maximum). At x = ±1, d²U/dx² = 48 – 16 = 32 > 0 → stable equilibria (potential minima). For small oscillations, effective spring constant k = d²U/dx² = 32 N/m. Angular frequency ω = √(k/m) = √(32/2) = √16 = 4 rad/s. Both stable points have the same curvature, so the oscillation frequency is 4 rad/s about either minimum.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a potential‑energy graph appears, sketch the force curve mentally (negative slope). Mark turning points for a given total energy E by drawing a horizontal line at U = E. Count intersections with the U‑curve: each pair of intersections bounds an allowed region. For small‑oscillation questions, differentiate the potential twice at the minimum; the frequency formula ω = √(U''/m) is a one‑line answer if you identify the correct minimum.

Energy Transfer in Rotational Motion

The rotational analogue of the work–energy theorem states that the net work done by external torques equals the change in rotational kinetic energy: Wτ = ΔKrot = ½ I ω² – ½ I ω₀², where I is the moment of inertia about the fixed axis and ω the angular speed. For a constant torque τ acting through an angular displacement Δθ, the work is W = τ Δθ. If the torque varies, the integral W = ∫ τ dθ applies. Power in rotation is the rate of doing work: P = τ ω (scalar product, sign indicates whether energy is added or removed). A frequent application is a rolling object without slipping. The total kinetic energy is the sum of translational and rotational parts: K = ½ M v² + ½ ICM ω². Using the no‑slip condition v = R ω, this becomes K = ½ (M + ICM/R²) v². The effective mass Meff = M + ICM/R² determines the acceleration down an incline: a = g sinθ / (1 + ICM/MR²). Energy methods avoid dealing with friction forces because static friction does no work (its point of application is instantaneously at rest). However, if slipping occurs, kinetic friction does negative work and mechanical energy is not conserved. Another subtle point: when a torque is applied about a moving axis (e.g., a spinning wheel on a moving vehicle), the work done by the torque in the ground frame includes a term from the translation of the axis. The general expression for power delivered by a force F applied at a point with velocity v is P = F·v; for a pure torque the equivalent is P = τ·ω only if the reference point is instantaneously at rest or the torque is a pure couple. Examiners often ask for the fraction of total kinetic energy that is rotational for a given rolling body; the answer is Krot/K = (ICM/MR²) / (1 + ICM/MR²).

Collisions in the Centre‑of‑Mass Frame

Transforming to the centre‑of‑mass (CM) frame simplifies collision analysis because the total momentum is zero. For two particles of masses m₁, m₂ with laboratory velocities u₁, u₂, the CM velocity is VCM = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂). Velocities in the CM frame are u₁' = u₁ – VCM, u₂' = u₂ – VCM. In this frame the momenta are equal and opposite: m₁ u₁' = –m₂ u₂'. For an elastic collision, kinetic energy conservation in the CM frame implies that the speeds remain unchanged: |v₁'| = |u₁'|, |v₂'| = |u₂'|. Only the directions change; the scattering angle θ in the CM frame is the angle between u₁' and v₁'. The laboratory scattering angles are obtained by vector addition of VCM. A key result: for equal masses in an elastic collision with one initially at rest, the CM frame velocities are equal in magnitude and opposite; after collision they simply reverse direction, giving a 90° angle between the final laboratory velocities. For inelastic collisions with coefficient of restitution e, the relative speed in the CM frame is reduced by factor e: |v₁' – v₂'| = e |u₁' – u₂'|. Since the total momentum in the CM frame is zero, the final speeds are v₁' = e u₁', v₂' = e u₂' (directions unchanged for head‑on collisions). The kinetic energy loss in the lab frame equals the loss in the CM frame because the CM kinetic energy ½ (m₁+m₂) VCM² is unchanged. This frame is especially powerful for two‑dimensional collisions: the problem reduces to finding the scattering angle in the CM frame, which is often given or can be deduced from impact parameter models. A typical examiner question provides the initial lab velocities and the CM scattering angle, asking for the final lab velocities; the solution proceeds by (1) computing VCM, (2) finding initial CM velocities, (3) rotating one of them by the given angle, (4) transforming back to the lab frame.

Power Delivered by Variable Forces and Energy Flow

When a force varies with time or position, instantaneous power is still P(t) = F(t)·v(t). For a one‑dimensional motion with F(x) and v(x), the work–energy theorem gives ½ m v² = ∫ F dx. Differentiating with respect to time yields m v dv/dt = F v, confirming P = F v. Average power over a time interval Δt is Pavg = (1/Δt) ∫ P dt = W/Δt. In periodic systems such as a mass‑spring oscillator, the instantaneous power oscillates between positive (energy flowing into kinetic) and negative (energy flowing into potential). Over a full cycle the average power is zero for a conservative system. If a damping force Fd = –b v acts, the instantaneous power dissipated is Pd = –b v², always negative. The average power loss per cycle equals the energy lost per cycle divided by the period. For a driven oscillator with driving force Fdr = F₀ cos ωt, the average power supplied by the driver is Pavg = ½ F₀ v₀ cos φ, where v₀ is the velocity amplitude and φ the phase lag. At resonance φ = 0 and power transfer is maximum. In rotational systems with variable torque, e.g. an engine delivering torque τ(θ), the work per revolution is W = ∮ τ dθ and average power is Pavg = W f where f is the rotation frequency. A practical twist: a car engine produces a torque curve τ(ω); the power curve is P(ω) = τ(ω) ω. The gear ratio is chosen so that the engine operates near the peak of the power curve for maximum acceleration. Students should be comfortable converting between force‑velocity and torque‑angular‑velocity representations, and recognising that the sign of power indicates the direction of energy flow.

  • Assuming the work–energy theorem holds unchanged in an accelerating frame without adding pseudo‑work.
  • Confusing the sign of the centrifugal potential; it is negative, so the effective potential is lowered at larger radii.
  • Treating static friction as doing work in rolling‑without‑slipping problems.
  • Forgetting that the Coriolis force does no work because it is always perpendicular to velocity in the rotating frame.
  • Using the laboratory scattering angle directly in CM‑frame formulas without transforming velocities.
  • Neglecting the phase factor when computing average power in driven oscillators.
  • Misapplying the reduced‑mass kinetic‑energy loss formula to collisions where external impulses act during the impact.

Work–Energy Theorem for Systems of Particles and Rigid Bodies

The work–energy theorem for a single particle states that the net work done by all forces equals the change in its kinetic energy. For a system of particles the theorem extends by summing over all particles: the total work done by external forces plus the work done by internal forces equals the change in the total kinetic energy of the system. Internal forces occur in action–reaction pairs; if they are conservative (e.g. gravitational attraction between the particles) their net work can be expressed as the negative change in the system’s internal potential energy. If internal forces are non‑conservative (e.g. friction between sliding blocks) they contribute to an irreversible loss of mechanical energy that appears as heat. For a rigid body the internal forces do no net work because the distance between any two particles remains constant; the work of internal forces cancels pairwise. Consequently the work–energy theorem for a rigid body reduces to the work of external forces equals the change in the sum of translational kinetic energy of the centre of mass and rotational kinetic energy about the centre of mass: Wext = Δ(½ M VCM² + ½ ICM ω²). This form is the basis for analysing rolling, sliding, and combined motions without tracking each particle individually.

When external forces include both conservative and non‑conservative components, it is convenient to separate the work: Wext = Wc + Wnc. The conservative part can be written as −ΔU, where U is the total potential energy associated with those forces (gravitational, elastic, etc.). The theorem then becomes Wnc = ΔK + ΔU = ΔEmech. This equation shows that the mechanical energy of a system changes only by the work of non‑conservative external forces. In many textbook problems the only non‑conservative external force is kinetic friction or an applied force that does not derive from a potential. The sign of Wnc tells whether mechanical energy increases (positive work, e.g. a motor driving a shaft) or decreases (negative work, e.g. braking friction).

A subtle point arises when the system boundary is chosen to include the Earth for gravitational problems. Then gravity becomes an internal conservative force and its work is accounted for by the change in gravitational potential energy of the system. If the Earth is excluded, gravity is an external conservative force and its work appears explicitly as Wc. Both approaches give identical results provided the bookkeeping is consistent. Examiners often test this by asking for the work done by gravity on a block sliding down an incline while the Earth is either part of the system or not; the answer differs by a sign but the final speed of the block is unchanged.

For a rigid body undergoing general plane motion, the kinetic energy can be expressed either as the sum of translational and rotational parts about the centre of mass, or as pure rotation about the instantaneous centre of zero velocity (IC). The work of external forces is path‑independent for conservative forces, so the change in kinetic energy between two configurations depends only on the initial and final positions of the centre of mass and the orientation. This path independence is exploited in problems where a body rolls without slipping down a curved track: the work done by gravity equals M g Δh, and the final angular speed follows from ½ M v² + ½ I ω² = M g Δh with v = R ω for pure rolling.

Energy Dissipation by Non‑Conservative Forces and the Concept of Lost Work

Non‑conservative forces such as kinetic friction, air resistance, and inelastic deformation convert mechanical energy into internal energy (heat, sound, permanent deformation). The work done by these forces is path‑dependent and cannot be recovered as mechanical energy. In the work–energy balance this appears as a negative contribution Wnc that reduces the total mechanical energy Emech = K + U. The magnitude of the lost mechanical energy is often called “lost work” or “energy dissipated”. For a block of mass m sliding a distance s on a horizontal surface with coefficient of kinetic friction μk, the friction force is f = μk m g, the work done by friction is Wfric = − f s = − μk m g s, and the lost work equals μk m g s. This energy is transferred to the surface and the block as thermal energy.

When a non‑conservative force acts together with conservative forces, the work–energy theorem reads Wc + Wnc = ΔK. Rearranging gives ΔK + ΔU = Wnc. If the only non‑conservative force is kinetic friction, Wnc is negative and the mechanical energy decreases. In many problems the dissipated energy is asked for directly: “Find the heat generated” or “Find the loss in mechanical energy”. The answer is simply −Wnc (a positive quantity). For a mass‑spring system with a damping force Fd = − b v, the instantaneous power dissipated is Pd = Fd·v = − b v². Integrating over one period gives the energy lost per cycle, which equals the work done by the damping force.

An important distinction is between “lost work” and “work done by internal non‑conservative forces”. In a system of two blocks sliding relative to each other with friction, the friction forces are internal. The work done by these internal forces does not appear in the external work term but shows up as a decrease in the system’s mechanical energy. If the system is defined to include both blocks, the work–energy theorem for the system becomes Wext = ΔK + ΔU + Ediss, where Ediss is the positive energy dissipated by internal friction. This formulation is useful for analysing collisions that are partially elastic: the coefficient of restitution e relates the relative speed after impact to that before impact, and the kinetic energy loss ΔK = ½ μ (1 − e²) urel² (μ is the reduced mass) is exactly the energy dissipated as heat and sound.

In thermodynamics the first law ΔU = Q − W uses a sign convention where work done by the system is positive. In mechanics the work–energy theorem uses the opposite sign for work done on the system. When a problem mixes mechanical and thermal concepts (e.g. a block sliding to rest and heating up), it is essential to keep the sign conventions separate: the mechanical work done by friction on the block is negative, the heat added to the block is positive, and the increase in internal energy of the block equals the magnitude of the mechanical work lost.

Worked Example: Block on Rough Incline with Spring
A block of mass 2 kg is released from rest at the top of a 30° incline of length 1.5 m. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. At the bottom the block compresses a spring of constant 500 N/m. Find the maximum compression of the spring.

Step 1: Compute the work done by gravity. Height drop h = 1.5 sin 30° = 0.75 m. Wg = m g h = 2 × 9.8 × 0.75 = 14.7 J.

Step 2: Compute the work done by friction. Normal force N = m g cos 30° = 2 × 9.8 × 0.866 = 16.97 N. Friction force f = μ N = 0.2 × 16.97 = 3.394 N. Work by friction Wf = − f × 1.5 = −5.09 J.

Step 3: Net work on block before spring contact = Wg + Wf = 14.7 − 5.09 = 9.61 J. This equals the kinetic energy at the bottom: K = 9.61 J.

Step 4: During spring compression, gravity and friction still act over the additional distance x (compression). Work by gravity during compression = m g x sin 30° = 2 × 9.8 × 0.5 x = 9.8 x. Work by friction = − f x = −3.394 x. Spring work = −½ k x² = −250 x².

Step 5: Apply work–energy from bottom to maximum compression (final K = 0): K + (9.8 x − 3.394 x) − 250 x² = 0 → 9.61 + 6.406 x − 250 x² = 0.

Step 6: Solve quadratic: 250 x² − 6.406 x − 9.61 = 0. Positive root x = [6.406 + √(6.406² + 4·250·9.61)] / (2·250) ≈ 0.203 m.

Maximum compression ≈ 0.20 m.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves both conservative and non‑conservative forces, write the energy balance as ΔK + ΔU = Wnc before substituting numbers. Identify every non‑conservative force (friction, air drag, inelastic impact) and compute its work separately; this avoids sign errors. If a spring is present, include its potential energy ½ k x² in ΔU. For rolling without slipping, remember that static friction does no work, so it does not appear in Wnc. Always check that the final expression for lost work is a positive quantity when the question asks for “energy dissipated” or “heat generated”.

Variable Mass Systems: Rocket Propulsion and the Work–Energy Relation

Systems that gain or lose mass, such as rockets expelling exhaust or a conveyor belt receiving sand, require a modified work–energy analysis. The standard work–energy theorem assumes a constant mass; for variable mass the momentum balance must be used first. Consider a rocket of instantaneous mass m moving with velocity v in an inertial frame, ejecting mass at rate dm/dt (negative) with exhaust velocity u relative to the rocket. The thrust force is Fthrust = u |dm/dt|. The equation of motion is m dv/dt = Fext + u dm/dt (with dm/dt negative). Multiplying by v and integrating gives the work–energy relation: ∫ Fext·v dt + ∫ u v dm = Δ(½ m v²). The term ∫ u v dm represents the work done by the thrust force on the rocket; it is not simply the kinetic energy of the ejected mass because the exhaust carries away kinetic energy relative to the inertial frame.

A more transparent energy accounting treats the rocket plus the ejected mass as a combined system of constant total mass. The external work done on this combined system equals the change in total kinetic energy of rocket plus exhaust. If no external forces act (free space), the total kinetic energy of the combined system increases because chemical energy of the fuel is converted into kinetic energy of both rocket and exhaust. The rocket’s kinetic energy alone can increase, decrease, or stay constant depending on the ratio of exhaust velocity to rocket velocity. The famous Tsiolkovsky rocket equation Δv = u ln(m₀/m) follows from momentum conservation; the corresponding energy relation shows that the kinetic energy gained by the rocket is ½ m v² − ½ m₀ v₀² = ½ u² (m₀ − m) − u (m₀ v₀ − m v) + … (higher‑order terms). In practice, examiners ask for the final speed of a rocket after burning a known mass of fuel, assuming constant exhaust velocity and no gravity or drag. The solution uses the rocket equation directly; the work–energy approach is rarely required but provides insight into energy distribution.

For a system gaining mass, such as a cart collecting rain vertically, the incoming mass has zero horizontal momentum. The cart’s horizontal momentum is conserved, so its speed decreases as mass increases. The kinetic energy of the cart decreases even though no external horizontal force acts; the “lost” kinetic energy goes into internal energy of the cart–rain system (inelastic collisions of raindrops with the cart). The work–energy theorem for the cart alone would incorrectly suggest an external force doing negative work. The correct approach is to write the energy balance for the cart plus collected rain: external work = ΔKcart+rain. Since external work is zero, the total kinetic energy of the combined system is constant only if the rain sticks without splashing; in reality some energy is dissipated as heat and sound.

In problems where a chain or rope is pulled from a heap, the mass being accelerated changes continuously. The force required to pull the chain at constant speed v is F = λ v², where λ is the linear mass density. The power delivered is P = F v = λ v³. The work done in pulling a length L is W = ½ λ L v², which equals the kinetic energy of the moving portion of the chain. This result is often derived by considering the rate of change of momentum of the chain segment being lifted from rest to speed v.

Constrained Motion and the Principle of Virtual Work

When a mechanical system is subject to holonomic constraints (relations among coordinates that do not involve time explicitly), the forces of constraint do no work for any virtual displacement consistent with the constraints. The principle of virtual work states that for a system in static equilibrium, the total virtual work of the applied forces is zero: Σ Fi·δri = 0, where δri are arbitrary virtual displacements that satisfy the constraints. This principle eliminates the need to solve for constraint forces (normal reactions, tensions in inextensible strings) and is the foundation of Lagrangian mechanics. In dynamics, D’Alembert’s principle extends virtual work by adding inertial forces −m a: Σ (Fi − mi ai)·δri = 0. Choosing generalized coordinates qj that automatically satisfy the constraints leads to the Euler–Lagrange equations d/dt (∂L/∂q̇j) − ∂L/∂qj = Qj, where L = T − V and Qj are generalized non‑conservative forces.

For a simple example, consider a bead of mass m sliding on a frictionless wire shaped as y = f(x). The constraint is holonomic; the normal reaction does no work because the virtual displacement is tangent to the wire. The kinetic energy is T = ½ m (ẋ² + ẏ²) = ½ m (1 + f'²) ẋ². The potential energy is V = m g f(x). The Lagrangian L = T − V yields the equation of motion (1 + f'²) ẍ + f' f'' ẋ² + g f' = 0. This single scalar equation replaces the two vector equations with an unknown normal force. Examiners often ask for the condition for equilibrium (ẍ = ẋ = 0) which reduces to f' = 0, i.e. the bead rests at a stationary point of the wire.

In systems with non‑holonomic constraints (e.g. a rolling wheel without slipping where the constraint involves velocities), the principle of virtual work in its simple form does not apply because the virtual displacements cannot be chosen independently of the velocities. However, the method of Lagrange multipliers can still enforce such constraints. For a cylinder rolling down an incline, the no‑slip condition v = R ω is a holonomic constraint because it integrates to x = R θ, making it effectively holonomic. The Lagrangian with the constraint incorporated gives the same result as the force–torque method: a = g sinθ / (1 + I/(mR²)). The virtual work of the static friction force is zero because the point of contact is instantaneously at rest.

A practical shortcut for exam problems: if the question asks only for accelerations or tensions in a system of pulleys and masses with inextensible strings, write the constraint equations relating the displacements (e.g. x₁ + 2 x₂ = constant). Differentiate twice to get acceleration relations. Then apply Newton’s second law to each mass. The work done by tension forces cancels when the total work–energy equation for the whole system is written, because the string is inextensible and massless. This cancellation is a direct consequence of the principle of virtual work.

Power in Coupled Mechanical–Electrical Systems

Many modern devices convert mechanical power to electrical power and vice versa. The instantaneous mechanical power delivered to a rotating shaft is Pmech = τ ω, where τ is torque and ω angular velocity. For an electrical machine (motor or generator) the electrical power is Pelec = V I, where V is terminal voltage and I current. In an ideal lossless converter, Pmech = Pelec. Real machines have losses: copper losses I²R, iron losses (hysteresis and eddy currents), mechanical friction, and windage. The efficiency η = Pout/Pin is always less than 1. For a DC motor, the back emf Eb = k ω opposes the applied voltage V, so V = Eb + I R. The mechanical power developed is Pmech = Eb I = k ω I. The torque is τ = k I. Thus Pmech = τ ω, consistent with the mechanical definition. The electrical input power is V I = (k ω + I R) I = Pmech + I²R. The I²R term is the copper loss.

In an AC synchronous generator, the mechanical power input from a turbine is Pmech = τ ω. The electrical output power per phase is Pelec = Vph Iph cos φ, where φ is the power factor angle. The torque angle δ between the rotor field and the stator voltage determines the power transfer: P = (3 Vph Ef / Xs) sin δ, where Ef is the excitation voltage and Xs the synchronous reactance. Maximum power occurs at δ = 90°. This expression is the electrical analogue of the mechanical power transmitted through a shaft with torsional stiffness.

Energy storage elements couple the two domains. A flywheel stores kinetic energy ½ J ω²; a capacitor stores electrical energy ½ C V²; an inductor stores magnetic energy ½ L I². In a regenerative braking system, the motor acts as a generator, converting the vehicle’s kinetic energy into electrical energy that charges a battery (chemical storage). The power flow is bidirectional: during acceleration, battery → inverter → motor → wheels; during braking, wheels → motor → inverter → battery. The instantaneous power at each interface must match (neglecting losses). Control strategies aim to keep the motor operating near its maximum efficiency point, which corresponds to a specific torque–speed curve.

Examiners may present a problem where a mass m falls, turning a generator that charges a capacitor. The rate of loss of gravitational potential energy mgv equals the rate of increase of kinetic energy plus the electrical power C V dV/dt. Equating mgv = C V dV/dt and using v = dy/dt leads to a differential equation for V(t). Solving gives the voltage across the capacitor as a function of the distance fallen. Such problems test the ability to write power balance across domains and to recognise that the generator’s torque is proportional to current (τ = k I) and the back emf is proportional to speed (E = k ω).

  • Treating the work of internal friction as external work when the system boundary excludes the interacting bodies.
  • Assuming static friction does work in rolling‑without‑slipping problems; it does not because the contact point is instantaneously at rest.
  • Using the single‑particle work–energy theorem for a variable‑mass system without accounting for the momentum carried away by ejected mass.
  • Neglecting the sign of non‑conservative work when computing “energy lost” – the lost energy is the magnitude of the negative work.
  • Applying the principle of virtual work to non‑holonomic constraints without using Lagrange multipliers.
  • Confusing mechanical power τ ω with electrical power V I in coupled machines and forgetting the efficiency factor.
  • Forgetting that the back emf in a motor reduces the net voltage across the armature resistance, so the electrical input power is not simply V I = τ ω.

Work–Energy Theorem for Deformable Bodies and Strain Energy

When a solid body deforms under external forces, the work done by those forces is stored partly as kinetic energy of the moving material points and partly as elastic strain energy within the material. For a linearly elastic, isotropic solid the strain energy density u at a point is given by u = ½ σij εij, where σij and εij are the stress and strain tensors. Integrating over the volume V yields the total strain energy U = ∫V ½ σij εij dV. The work–energy theorem for a deformable body then reads Wext = ΔK + ΔU + Wdiss, where Wext is the work of external forces, ΔK the change in kinetic energy, ΔU the change in strain energy, and Wdiss the energy dissipated as heat through internal friction or plastic deformation. In quasi‑static loading the kinetic term vanishes and the external work equals the increase in strain energy plus dissipated work. For a simple uniaxial bar of length L, cross‑section A, Young’s modulus E, subjected to a gradually applied force F, the elongation is δ = FL/(AE) and the strain energy stored is U = ½ Fδ = F²L/(2AE). This result is the foundation of energy methods such as Castigliano’s theorems, which allow displacements and rotations in statically indeterminate structures to be obtained by differentiating the total strain energy with respect to the corresponding generalized forces. The principle of minimum potential energy states that among all kinematically admissible displacement fields the actual equilibrium configuration minimizes the total potential energy Π = U − Wext. This variational statement underlies the finite‑element method used in modern structural analysis.

In problems involving impact on elastic bodies, the work–energy balance must account for the conversion of kinetic energy into strain energy during the compression phase and its partial recovery during restitution. The coefficient of restitution e for a collision between a rigid mass and an elastic bar can be related to the ratio of the maximum strain energy stored to the initial kinetic energy. For a perfectly elastic bar (no internal damping) the collision is perfectly elastic (e = 1) and the mass rebounds with the same speed. Real materials exhibit hysteresis; the area of the stress–strain loop during a loading–unloading cycle equals the energy dissipated per unit volume. This dissipated energy appears as a temperature rise and must be included in the work–energy balance for high‑rate loading such as ballistic impact.

Energy Methods in Lagrangian Mechanics: Generalized Forces and Virtual Work

Lagrangian mechanics reformulates the work–energy theorem in terms of generalized coordinates qk (k = 1…n) that describe the configuration of a system with constraints. The kinetic energy T is expressed as a homogeneous quadratic function of the generalized velocities q̇k: T = ½ Σ akl(q) q̇k q̇l. The virtual work δW of the applied forces for a virtual displacement δqk defines the generalized forces Qk = Σ Fi·∂ri/∂qk. For conservative forces a potential energy function V(q) exists such that Qk = −∂V/∂qk. The Lagrange equations d/dt(∂T/∂q̇k) − ∂T/∂qk = Qk are equivalent to the work–energy theorem for the whole system. When non‑conservative forces (friction, damping) are present they appear as additional generalized forces Qknc that do not derive from a potential. The rate of change of the total mechanical energy E = T + V is then dE/dt = Σ Qknc q̇k, which is precisely the power delivered by the non‑conservative forces.

The principle of virtual work states that for a system in equilibrium the total virtual work of all applied forces (including constraint forces that do no work) vanishes for any virtual displacement consistent with the constraints: Σ Qk δqk = 0. This principle is the starting point for deriving the equations of motion via d’Alembert’s principle, where inertial forces −m a are treated as additional applied forces. In the Hamiltonian formulation the total energy is expressed as a function of generalized coordinates and conjugate momenta pk = ∂T/∂q̇k. The Hamiltonian H(q,p,t) = Σ pk q̇k − L (with L = T − V) yields Hamilton’s equations q̇k = ∂H/∂pk, ṗk = −∂H/∂qk. For scleronomic constraints H equals the total mechanical energy and is conserved if the Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence. Energy methods in Lagrangian mechanics are especially powerful for systems with many degrees of freedom, such as coupled oscillators, rotating rigid bodies, and electromechanical transducers, because they automatically incorporate constraint forces without the need to solve for them explicitly.

Worked Example: Generalized Force for a Damped Pendulum
A simple pendulum of length l and mass m swings in a vertical plane. A viscous damping torque τd = −c θ̇ acts at the pivot, where θ is the angular displacement from the downward vertical. Using the generalized coordinate θ, find the generalized force Qθ associated with the damping torque and write the Lagrange equation of motion.
The kinetic energy is T = ½ m l² θ̇². The potential energy (zero at the lowest point) is V = m g l (1 − cos θ). The damping torque is a non‑conservative generalized force. Virtual work of the torque for a virtual displacement δθ is δW = τd δθ = (−c θ̇) δθ. Hence the generalized force is Qθ = −c θ̇. The Lagrange equation d/dt(∂T/∂θ̇) − ∂T/∂θ = −∂V/∂θ + Qθ gives m l² θ̈ + m g l sin θ = −c θ̇, or θ̈ + (g/l) sin θ + (c/(m l²)) θ̇ = 0. For small angles sin θ ≈ θ, the equation reduces to the familiar damped harmonic oscillator θ̈ + 2β θ̇ + ω₀² θ = 0 with ω₀² = g/l and 2β = c/(m l²).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for the equation of motion of a constrained system with damping or driving forces, first write the kinetic and potential energies in terms of a minimal set of generalized coordinates. Identify non‑conservative forces and compute their virtual work to obtain the generalized forces. Then apply the Lagrange equations directly; this avoids the algebraic labor of eliminating constraint forces from Newtonian free‑body diagrams.

Power in Alternating‑Current Circuits: Real, Reactive, and Apparent Power

In sinusoidal steady state the instantaneous voltage and current in a linear circuit element are v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φv) and i(t) = Im cos(ωt + φi). The instantaneous power p(t) = v(t)i(t) = VmIm cos(ωt + φv) cos(ωt + φi). Using the product‑to‑sum identity this becomes p(t) = ½ VmIm [cos(φv − φi) + cos(2ωt + φv + φi)]. The first term is constant; the second oscillates at twice the supply frequency and averages to zero over a period. The average (real) power is P = ½ VmIm cos φ = Vrms Irms cos φ, where φ = φv − φi is the phase angle by which voltage leads current. The quantity Q = Vrms Irms sin φ is called reactive power; it represents the peak rate of energy exchange between the source and reactive elements (inductors and capacitors) but does not contribute to net energy transfer. The apparent power S = Vrms Irms is the magnitude of the complex power S = P + jQ. The power factor is defined as pf = cos φ = P/S. For a purely resistive load φ = 0, pf = 1 and P = S. For a purely inductive or capacitive load φ = ±90°, pf = 0 and P = 0 while Q = ±S.

In a network containing several branches the total real power is the sum of the real powers of each branch, and the total reactive power is the algebraic sum of the branch reactive powers (inductive positive, capacitive negative). The total apparent power is not simply the sum of individual apparent powers because the phase angles differ; it must be obtained from the total complex power Stotal = Σ Sk. Power factor correction is achieved by adding a capacitor bank in parallel with an inductive load so that the net reactive power is reduced, raising the overall power factor toward unity. This reduces the current drawn from the supply for the same real power, decreasing I²R losses in transmission lines. The instantaneous power in a three‑phase balanced system is constant: ptotal(t) = 3 Vph Iph cos φ, where Vph and Iph are phase rms values. This constant power is a key advantage of three‑phase power for rotating machines.

Energy Conversion in Electrochemical Cells: Thermodynamic Limits and Overpotentials

An electrochemical cell converts chemical free energy into electrical work. The maximum electrical work obtainable from a cell operating reversibly at constant temperature and pressure equals the decrease in Gibbs free energy ΔG of the overall cell reaction: Wmax = −ΔG = n F Ecellrev, where n is the number of electrons transferred per mole of reaction, F is Faraday’s constant, and Ecellrev is the reversible cell voltage (open‑circuit voltage). The reversible voltage is related to the standard electrode potentials by Ecellrev = Ecathode⁰ − Eanode⁰ − (RT/nF) ln Q, where Q is the reaction quotient (Nernst equation). In a real cell the terminal voltage under load V is lower than Ecellrev because of overpotentials: activation overpotential ηact (kinetic barrier at electrode surfaces), ohmic overpotential ηohm = I Rint (internal resistance), and concentration overpotential ηconc (mass‑transport limitations). The cell voltage is V = Ecellrev − ηact − ηohm − ηconc. The electrical power delivered to an external load is P = V I. The energy efficiency of the cell is η = P / (−ΔĠ) = V I / (n F Ecellrev I) = V / Ecellrev. For a rechargeable battery the charging process requires an applied voltage greater than Ecellrev to overcome the same overpotentials; the round‑trip efficiency is the product of discharge and charge efficiencies.

In fuel cells the reactants are supplied continuously; the theoretical maximum efficiency is the ratio of the Gibbs free energy change to the enthalpy change ΔH of the fuel oxidation: ηmax = ΔG/ΔH. For hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells at 298 K, ΔG ≈ 237 kJ/mol and ΔH ≈ 286 kJ/mol, giving ηmax ≈ 83 %. Practical fuel cells achieve 40–60 % efficiency due to overpotentials and system losses. The concept of exergy (available work) provides a unified framework: the exergy destruction in each irreversible step equals T₀ ΔSgen, where T₀ is the ambient temperature. Minimizing exergy destruction by operating close to reversible conditions (low current density, high reactant pressure, optimal temperature) is the central design goal for high‑efficiency electrochemical energy conversion.

Work–Energy Balance in Fluid Flow: Bernoulli’s Equation and Viscous Dissipation

For an inviscid, incompressible, steady flow along a streamline the work–energy theorem reduces to Bernoulli’s equation: p + ½ ρ v² + ρ g z = constant, where p is pressure, ρ density, v speed, g gravitational acceleration, and z elevation. This equation states that the sum of pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy per unit volume remains constant when no work is done by or on the fluid and no energy is dissipated. When a pump or turbine is present, the shaft work per unit mass ws is added: p₁/ρ + ½ v₁² + g z₁ + ws = p₂/ρ + ½ v₂² + g z₂. For a real viscous fluid the mechanical energy is not conserved; a head loss hL accounts for the conversion of mechanical energy into internal energy (heat) due to viscous shear. The extended Bernoulli equation becomes p₁/ρg + v₁²/(2g) + z₁ + hp = p₂/ρg + v₂²/(2g) + z₂ + ht + hL, where hp is pump head, ht turbine head, and hL the head loss. The Darcy–Weisbach formula gives hL = f (L/D) v²/(2g) for pipe flow, with friction factor f depending on Reynolds number and relative roughness.

The rate of viscous dissipation per unit volume in a Newtonian fluid is Φ = μ (∂vi/∂xj + ∂vj/∂xi)² / 2, where μ is dynamic viscosity. Integrating Φ over the flow domain yields the total power dissipated as heat. In turbulent flow the dissipation occurs mainly in small eddies; the mean kinetic energy is transferred from large scales to the Kolmogorov microscales where viscosity finally converts it to heat. The work–energy balance for a control volume in integral form is d/dt ∫CV (½ ρ v² + ρ e) dV + ∫CS (½ ρ v² + ρ h + p/ρ) v·n dA = Q̇ − Ẇshaft, where e is internal energy per unit mass, h = e + p/ρ enthalpy, Q̇ heat transfer rate, and Ẇshaft shaft work rate. This equation is the basis for analyzing compressors, turbines, nozzles, and diffusers in thermodynamics and fluid machinery. In problems involving flow through a Venturi meter or an orifice plate, the pressure drop is related to the velocity increase via Bernoulli, and the irreversible loss is accounted for by a discharge coefficient Cd < 1 that reduces the actual flow rate compared with the ideal inviscid prediction.

  • Treating strain energy as kinetic energy in impact problems and forgetting the dissipation term.
  • Using the single‑particle work–energy theorem for a deformable body without integrating the strain energy density over the volume.
  • Confusing generalized forces with constraint forces; constraint forces do no virtual work for ideal holonomic constraints.
  • Assuming apparent power adds arithmetically in AC circuits; it must be obtained from the vector sum of complex powers.
  • Neglecting concentration overpotential in high‑current electrochemical cells, leading to overestimation of terminal voltage.
  • Applying Bernoulli’s equation across a hydraulic jump or a region with strong viscous shear where head loss is significant.
  • Forgetting that the power factor correction capacitor supplies reactive power locally and does not change the real power consumed by the load.

Work–Energy Theorem in Relativistic Dynamics

In special relativity the kinetic energy of a particle of rest mass m moving with speed v is K = (γ − 1) m c², where γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²) and c is the speed of light. The work–energy theorem retains its form: the net work done by the resultant force equals the change in relativistic kinetic energy, W = ΔK. The relativistic momentum is p = γ m v, and the force is defined as the time derivative of momentum, F = dp/dt. The instantaneous power delivered by the force is P = F·v = dK/dt, which follows directly from differentiating K with respect to time. At low speeds (v ≪ c) the binomial expansion of γ yields K ≈ ½ m v², recovering the classical expression. In particle accelerators the work done by electric fields is computed by integrating q E·dx along the trajectory; the result matches the increase in γ m c². A common misconception is that relativistic mass m_rel = γ m should be used in F = m_rel a; the correct approach is to keep the invariant rest mass and use the four‑force formalism. The four‑force F^μ = dp^μ/dτ (with proper time τ) ensures covariance, and its spatial component reduces to dp/dt in the laboratory frame. Energy–momentum conservation in collisions is expressed by the invariance of the total four‑momentum P^μ = Σ p_i^μ. For a completely inelastic collision of two identical particles moving toward each other with equal speeds, the final composite particle has rest mass greater than the sum of the initial rest masses, the excess being the kinetic energy converted into internal energy. This mass–energy equivalence is the relativistic counterpart of the classical lost‑work concept.

Power and Energy in Rotating Reference Frames

When Newton’s laws are written in a frame rotating with constant angular velocity ω, two fictitious forces appear: the centrifugal force F_cf = m ω × (ω × r) and the Coriolis force F_Cor = 2 m ω × v', where r is the position vector and v' the velocity measured in the rotating frame. The centrifugal force is conservative; it derives from the potential U_cf = −½ m (ω × r)² = −½ m ω² r_⊥², where r_⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the rotation axis. The work done by the centrifugal force when a particle moves from point A to B equals the negative change in this potential. The Coriolis force, being always perpendicular to v', does no work (F_Cor·v' = 0) and therefore does not change the kinetic energy in the rotating frame. The total mechanical energy in the rotating frame is E' = K' + U + U_cf, where K' = ½ m v'² and U is the potential of real forces. If the real forces are conservative and the rotation is steady, E' is conserved. This conserved quantity is known as the Jacobi integral. In meteorology the effective potential U + U_cf explains the formation of geostrophic balance and the shape of the free surface in a rotating bucket. In rotating machinery (turbines, centrifuges) the power extracted or supplied is computed from the torque τ and angular velocity ω as P = τ·ω. When a fluid element moves radially outward in a centrifugal pump, the work done by the impeller equals the increase in the fluid’s kinetic energy plus the increase in centrifugal potential energy. A frequent error is to attribute work to the Coriolis force in energy balances; because it is velocity‑dependent and orthogonal to the instantaneous velocity, its contribution to the work integral vanishes identically.

Worked Example: Relativistic Kinetic Energy
A proton (rest mass mₚ = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) is accelerated to a speed v = 0.8 c. Calculate its relativistic kinetic energy in joules and in MeV. (c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J)
First compute γ = 1 / √(1 − 0.8²) = 1 / √(0.36) = 1 / 0.6 = 1.6667. Relativistic kinetic energy K = (γ − 1) mₚ c² = (0.6667) × (1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) × (9.00 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²) = 0.6667 × 1.5057 × 10⁻¹⁰ J ≈ 1.004 × 10⁻¹⁰ J. Convert to MeV: 1.004 × 10⁻¹⁰ J ÷ (1.602 × 10⁻¹³ J/MeV) ≈ 627 MeV. The work required to accelerate the proton from rest to 0.8 c equals this kinetic energy, 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ J or 627 MeV.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives speeds above 0.1 c, always check whether the relativistic kinetic energy formula is required. Write γ explicitly before substituting numbers; this avoids arithmetic errors. Remember that the work–energy theorem W = ΔK holds in relativity with the relativistic K. For rotating‑frame questions, identify the fictitious potentials first; only the centrifugal potential contributes to energy changes. The Coriolis force never appears in a work integral.

Electromagnetic Work and the Poynting Theorem

The Lorentz force on a charge q moving with velocity v in electric field E and magnetic field B is F = q (E + v × B). The instantaneous power delivered to the charge is P = F·v = q E·v because the magnetic term q (v × B)·v vanishes identically. Thus magnetic fields do no work on free charges; they only change the direction of motion. In a continuous distribution the work done per unit volume per unit time by the fields on the current density J is J·E. The electromagnetic energy density is u = ½ (ε₀ E² + μ₀⁻¹ B²) (in vacuum). The Poynting vector S = E × H (with H = B/μ₀) represents the energy flux density. Poynting’s theorem, derived from Maxwell’s equations, states ∂u/∂t + ∇·S = −J·E. The left side is the rate of decrease of field energy plus the net outward flux; the right side is the negative of the work done on charges. In a DC circuit the Poynting vector points from the battery through the surrounding space into the resistive wire, showing that energy flows in the fields, not inside the conductors. For time‑harmonic fields the time‑averaged Poynting vector ⟨S⟩ = ½ Re(E × H*) gives the real power flow. In waveguides and antennas the integral of ⟨S⟩ over a cross‑section yields the transmitted power. A common pitfall is to assume that the magnetic force does work in devices like electric motors; the actual work is done by the induced electric field (back EMF) that arises from the changing magnetic flux. The mechanical power output equals the integral of J·E over the conductor volume, which matches the rate of decrease of field energy minus the radiated power.

Thermodynamic Work and the First Law: Mechanical Equivalents

Thermodynamic work is defined as energy transfer across a system boundary due to macroscopic forces. The most common form is pressure–volume work δW = p_ext dV for a simple compressible system, where p_ext is the external pressure. For a reversible process p_ext = p (the system pressure) and W = ∫ p dV. Shaft work (e.g., from a turbine) and electrical work (δW_elec = V I dt) are other modes. The sign convention used in physics and chemistry is that work done by the system on the surroundings is positive; engineering often adopts the opposite. The first law states ΔU = Q − W, where U is internal energy, Q heat added to the system, and W work done by the system. Joule’s paddle‑wheel experiment established the mechanical equivalent of heat: 1 cal = 4.184 J. In a cyclic process the net change in U is zero, so ∮ δQ = ∮ δW. For a steady‑flow device (compressor, turbine) the first law per unit mass becomes h₁ + ½ v₁² + g z₁ + q = h₂ + ½ v₂² + g z₂ + w_s, where h = u + p/ρ is enthalpy, q heat transfer per unit mass, and w_s shaft work per unit mass. The flow work p/ρ accounts for the energy required to push fluid across the control surface. In irreversible processes the actual work differs from the reversible work; the difference is the lost work W_lost = T₀ ΔS_gen, where T₀ is the ambient temperature and ΔS_gen the entropy generated. This concept links directly to the exergy destruction discussed in electrochemical and fluid‑flow contexts.

Energy Transfer in Coupled Oscillators and Normal Mode Analysis

Consider two identical masses m connected by three identical springs of stiffness k in a line, with the outer springs attached to fixed walls. The equations of motion are m ẍ₁ = −2k x₁ + k x₂ and m ẍ₂ = k x₁ − 2k x₂. Seeking normal‑mode solutions x_i = A_i cos(ω t + φ) leads to the eigenvalue problem (2k − m ω²) A₁ − k A₂ = 0, −k A₁ + (2k − m ω²) A₂ = 0. The characteristic equation gives two normal‑mode frequencies: ω₁ = √(k/m) (symmetric mode, A₁ = A₂) and ω₂ = √(3k/m) (antisymmetric mode, A₁ = −A₂). The total mechanical energy E = ½ m (ẋ₁² + ẋ₂²) + ½ k (x₁² + (x₁ − x₂)² + x₂²) is constant. In the symmetric mode the central spring is unstretched, so energy is equally shared between kinetic and potential forms at each instant. In the antisymmetric mode the masses move oppositely, stretching the central spring maximally when the outer springs are at equilibrium. If only one mass is displaced initially, the motion is a superposition of the two normal modes, producing beats: energy sloshes back and forth between the masses with a beat frequency ω_beat = ω₂ − ω₁. The period of complete energy transfer from mass 1 to mass 2 is T_beat = 2π / (ω₂ − ω₁). This phenomenon underlies the operation of coupled pendulum clocks, vibration absorbers, and quantum‑mechanical tunnelling in double‑well potentials. A frequent mistake is to treat the beat envelope as a separate oscillation with its own energy; the envelope merely modulates the amplitude of the two normal modes, each of which carries a constant energy.

  • Assuming the relativistic mass concept in force equations instead of using four‑momentum.
  • Attributing work to the Coriolis force in rotating‑frame energy balances.
  • Believing magnetic forces do work on free charges; only the electric field contributes to J·E.
  • Using the wrong sign convention for thermodynamic work when applying the first law.
  • Treating the beat envelope in coupled oscillators as an independent energy‑carrying mode.
  • Forgetting that the Poynting vector describes energy flow in the fields, not inside conductors.
  • These traps highlight the need to distinguish between real and fictitious forces, field and mechanical energy channels, and proper relativistic and thermodynamic conventions.

Work–Energy Theorem for Continuous Media and Stress Power

Stress Power Density

In a deformable continuum the mechanical power delivered to a material volume V is the integral of the stress power density over that volume. The Cauchy stress tensor σ_ij and the velocity gradient ∂v_i/∂x_j combine to give the stress power per unit volume as σ_ij ∂v_i/∂x_j. Using the symmetry of σ_ij this equals σ_ij D_ij where D_ij = ½(∂v_i/∂x_j + ∂v_j/∂x_i) is the rate‑of‑deformation tensor. The total stress power is ∫_V σ_ij D_ij dV. This quantity appears on the right‑hand side of the global energy balance for a continuum: the rate of change of kinetic energy plus the rate of change of internal energy equals the stress power plus the heat supply minus the heat flux divergence.

Relation to Strain Energy

For hyperelastic materials a strain‑energy density function W(F) exists, where F is the deformation gradient. The stress power density then equals the material time derivative of W, i.e. σ_ij D_ij = dW/dt. Integrating over the reference configuration gives the total elastic energy stored in the body. In the presence of plasticity or viscoelasticity the stress power splits into a recoverable part (rate of change of stored energy) and a dissipative part that appears as heat. The Clausius–Duhem inequality requires the dissipative part to be non‑negative, which imposes restrictions on constitutive models.

Application to Thin Structures

For beams, plates and shells the three‑dimensional stress power reduces to resultant forces and moments acting on generalized strain rates. The virtual work principle δW = ∫ (N δε + M δκ) dA, where N and M are membrane forces and bending moments, ε and κ are membrane strains and curvatures, provides the foundation for finite‑element formulations. The work‑energy theorem for a discretized structure becomes ∑ (F_i · δu_i) = δK + δU, where K is kinetic energy and U is strain energy. This form is directly used in dynamic analysis codes to enforce energy consistency at each time step.

Energy Transfer in Wave Propagation and Radiation

Energy Flux in Mechanical Waves

For a one‑dimensional longitudinal wave in a rod the instantaneous power crossing a section is P = −σ v A, where σ is the axial stress, v the particle velocity and A the cross‑sectional area. Using the linear constitutive law σ = E ε and the wave relation v = c ε (c = √(E/ρ)), the time‑averaged power over a period becomes ⟨P⟩ = ½ ρ c A ω² ξ₀², where ξ₀ is the displacement amplitude. This expression shows that the energy flux is proportional to the square of the amplitude and to the wave impedance Z = ρ c A.

Electromagnetic Radiation and the Poynting Vector

In free space the electromagnetic energy density is u = ½(ε₀ E² + μ₀ H²) and the energy flux density is the Poynting vector S = E × H. For a plane wave E = E₀ cos(kz−ωt) x̂, H = H₀ cos(kz−ωt) ŷ with H₀ = E₀/η₀ (η₀ = √(μ₀/ε₀) ≈ 377 Ω), the time‑averaged Poynting vector magnitude is ⟨S⟩ = ½ E₀ H₀ = E₀²/(2η₀). The total radiated power of an antenna is obtained by integrating ⟨S⟩ over a closed surface surrounding the source. In the near field the reactive power oscillates between electric and magnetic forms and does not contribute to net radiation; only the real part of the complex Poynting vector represents transmitted power.

Acoustic Radiation from a Vibrating Surface

A baffled piston of radius a vibrating with velocity amplitude v₀ radiates sound power W = ½ ρ₀ c₀ π a² v₀² [1 − J₁(2ka)/(ka)] for ka ≪ 1, where ρ₀ and c₀ are the ambient density and sound speed, k = ω/c₀, and J₁ is a Bessel function. At low frequencies the radiation resistance is proportional to (ka)², so most of the mechanical input power is stored as reactive near‑field energy. As ka increases the radiation resistance approaches ρ₀ c₀ π a² and the piston becomes an efficient radiator. This frequency‑dependent coupling is the basis for loudspeaker design and for understanding energy leakage from vibrating machinery.

Worked Example: Power Radiated by a Dipole
A short electric dipole of length ℓ ≪ λ carries a sinusoidal current I(t) = I₀ cos ωt. Derive the time‑averaged total radiated power.
The far‑field electric field of a Hertzian dipole is E_θ = j η₀ k I₀ ℓ sinθ e−jkr/(4πr). The magnetic field is H_φ = E_θ/η₀. The complex Poynting vector is S = ½ E × H* = ½ |E_θ|²/η₀ r̂. Substituting |E_θ|² = (η₀ k I₀ ℓ sinθ)²/(16π² r²) gives ⟨S⟩ = η₀ k² I₀² ℓ² sin²θ/(32π² r²) r̂. Integrating over a sphere: P_rad = ∫ ⟨S⟩·r̂ r² sinθ dθ dφ = η₀ k² I₀² ℓ²/(32π²) ∫₀2π dφ ∫₀^π sin³θ dθ = η₀ k² I₀² ℓ²/(32π²) · 2π · 4/3 = η₀ (I₀ ℓ)² k²/(12π). Using k = ω/c and η₀ = μ₀ c yields P_rad = μ₀ ω⁴ (I₀ ℓ)²/(12π c). This is the standard Larmor formula for a short dipole.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for radiated power, first identify the far‑field expressions for E and H. Compute the time‑averaged Poynting vector magnitude, then integrate over a spherical surface. Remember that only the 1/r terms contribute to net radiation; near‑field 1/r² and 1/r³ terms integrate to zero net power. Keep the angular dependence (sin²θ for a dipole) explicit to avoid missing the 4/3 factor from the θ integral.

Work Done by Non‑Conservative Forces in Thermodynamic Cycles

Cycle Work and the First Law

For a closed thermodynamic cycle the net change in internal energy is zero, so the cyclic integral of heat equals the cyclic integral of work: ∮ δQ = ∮ δW. The work done by the system includes boundary work ∮ p dV and any shaft or electrical work. In a reversible cycle the work is the area enclosed by the cycle on a p‑V diagram. For irreversible cycles the actual work is less than the reversible work for a power cycle (or greater for a refrigeration cycle) because of entropy generation. The difference is the lost work W_lost = T₀ ΔS_gen, where T₀ is the temperature of the environment.

Exergy Destruction in Real Engines

Exergy (availability) measures the maximum useful work obtainable when a system comes to equilibrium with a reference environment at T₀, p₀. For a steady‑flow device the exergy balance reads ∑ (1 − T₀/T_k) Q_k − W_s = ΔΦ + T₀ ΔS_gen, where Φ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀) + ½ v² + gz is the specific flow exergy. The term T₀ ΔS_gen quantifies the irreversibility. In a combined‑cycle power plant the exergy destruction in the combustion chamber dominates because of the large temperature difference between flame and working fluid. Reducing this destruction requires staged combustion or fuel cells that convert chemical exergy directly to electrical work.

Work in Cycles with Variable Composition

In reacting flows the chemical work associated with species diffusion and reaction must be included. The Gibbs free energy change of reaction ΔG_r provides the maximum non‑expansion work obtainable from a reaction at constant T and p. For a fuel cell the electrical work per mole of fuel is −ΔG_r, while the heat released is −ΔH_r. The difference ΔH_r − ΔG_r = T ΔS_r appears as irreversible heat if the cell operates at finite current density. The voltage efficiency is V_cell / (−ΔG_r/nF), where n is the number of electrons transferred and F is Faraday’s constant. This framework unifies the treatment of work in combustion engines, fuel cells, and electrolyzers.

Power in Electromechanical Transducers and Back‑EMF

Energy Conversion in a Linear Motor

A linear electromagnetic actuator consists of a coil moving in a magnetic field B. The mechanical equation is m ẍ = B ℓ i − k x − c ẋ, where ℓ is the conductor length, i the current, k the spring constant and c the damping coefficient. The electrical equation is v = R i + L di/dt + B ℓ ẋ, where the term B ℓ ẋ is the back‑EMF. Multiplying the electrical equation by i gives the instantaneous electrical power input: v i = R i² + L i di/dt + B ℓ i ẋ. The term B ℓ i ẋ is the mechanical power developed; it appears with opposite sign in the mechanical power balance m ẍ ẋ = B ℓ i ẋ − k x ẋ − c ẋ². Thus the back‑EMF mediates the conversion between electrical and mechanical power without loss in the ideal case.

Piezoelectric Transducers

For a piezoelectric stack the constitutive relations are S = s^E T + d^t E and D = d T + ε^T E, where S is strain, T stress, E electric field, D electric displacement, s^E compliance at constant field, d piezoelectric coefficient, and ε^T permittivity at constant stress. The electrical power input is ∫ v i dt = ∫ E dD/dt dV. Substituting the constitutive laws shows that the electrical energy splits into stored electrostatic energy ½ ε^T E², stored elastic energy ½ s^E T², and the coupling term d T E. Under open‑circuit conditions the mechanical work done on the stack appears as electrical energy; under short‑circuit conditions the mechanical energy is dissipated in the external circuit resistance. The electromechanical coupling factor k² = d²/(s^E ε^T) quantifies the fraction of energy that can be converted.

Loss Mechanisms and Efficiency

Real transducers exhibit hysteresis, eddy‑current, and dielectric losses. In a voice‑coil motor the force constant K_f = B ℓ and the back‑EMF constant K_e = B ℓ are equal in SI units, so the motor constant K_m = K_f/√R determines the force per square‑root of electrical power loss. The maximum efficiency occurs when the load resistance matches the internal resistance (impedance matching). For piezoelectric devices the mechanical quality factor Q_m and the dielectric loss tangent tan δ determine the bandwidth‑efficiency trade‑off. Designers often operate at the anti‑resonance frequency where the electrical impedance is maximal, minimizing current for a given voltage and reducing I²R losses.

Energy Dissipation in Viscoelastic Materials and Hysteresis

Linear Viscoelastic Models

Viscoelastic behavior is described by hereditary integrals: σ(t) = ∫−∞^t G(t−τ) dε(τ)/dτ dτ for stress relaxation, where G(t) is the relaxation modulus. For a sinusoidal strain ε(t) = ε₀ sin ωt the steady‑state stress is σ(t) = ε₀ [G'(ω) sin ωt + G''(ω) cos ωt], where G' is the storage modulus and G'' the loss modulus. The complex modulus G* = G' + i G''. The energy dissipated per cycle per unit volume is ΔW = π ε₀² G''(ω). The loss factor η = G''/G' = tan δ, where δ is the phase lag between stress and strain.

Hysteresis Loops and Dissipated Energy

For nonlinear hysteretic materials the stress–strain curve forms a closed loop during a loading–unloading cycle. The area enclosed by the loop equals the energy dissipated per unit volume. In a Bouc‑Wen model the restoring force z evolves as ż = A ẋ − β |ẋ| |z|n−1 z − γ ẋ |z|^n. The dissipated energy per cycle can be computed numerically by integrating z dx over one period. For a bilinear kinematic hardening model the loop consists of elastic branches and a plastic plateau; the dissipated energy is the product of the yield stress and the plastic strain range. These models are essential for seismic isolation bearings and for predicting fatigue life of polymer components.

Thermo‑Viscoelastic Coupling

In high‑frequency loading the dissipated energy raises the local temperature, which in turn shifts the viscoelastic spectrum (time‑temperature superposition). The heat equation ρ c_p ∂T/∂t = ∇·(k ∇T) + σ_ij D_ij^diss couples the mechanical dissipation to the temperature field. For a rubber engine mount operating at 50 Hz the temperature rise can exceed 30 °C, reducing the storage modulus by a factor of two. Accurate prediction requires simultaneous solution of the mechanical and thermal fields, often implemented via a staggered finite‑element scheme where the mechanical step provides the dissipation source for the thermal step.

  • Assuming the Poynting vector inside a conductor represents power delivered to the lattice; the real power flow is in the surrounding fields.
  • Neglecting the reactive near‑field energy when calculating radiation resistance of small antennas.
  • Treating lost work in a thermodynamic cycle as a simple subtraction of heat transfers without accounting for entropy generation at the correct reservoir temperature.
  • Equating back‑EMF voltage with resistive drop in a motor; back‑EMF is a non‑dissipative conversion term.
  • Using the linear loss factor tan δ for large‑amplitude hysteresis where the loop area is amplitude‑dependent.
