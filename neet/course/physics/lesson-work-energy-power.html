1. Work and Energy: Fundamental Concepts

1.1 Definition of Work

Work is defined as the product of the component of a force acting along the direction of displacement and the magnitude of that displacement. When a force F acts on a particle that moves through a displacement s, the work done by the force is W = F·s = F s cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. This definition makes work a scalar quantity; it has magnitude but no direction, and it can be positive, negative, or zero depending on the relative orientation of force and motion. The concept of work links the mechanical action of a force to the energy transferred to or from a system, providing the bridge between Newton’s laws of motion and the energy formulation of mechanics.

Because work depends on the path taken only through the displacement, it is independent of the time taken for the motion. A force that is always perpendicular to the instantaneous displacement, such as the magnetic component of the Lorentz force on a charged particle, does no work because cos 90° = 0. Conversely, a force that has a component opposite to the displacement, like kinetic friction, performs negative work, removing kinetic energy from the system. Understanding these sign conventions is essential for correctly applying the work‑energy theorem later in the chapter.

1.2 Scalar Nature of Work

The scalar nature of work means that the total work done by several forces acting simultaneously is the algebraic sum of the individual works. If forces F₁, F₂, …, Fₙ act on a body that undergoes a displacement s, the net work is Wₙₑₜ = Σ (Fᵢ·s). This additive property follows directly from the linearity of the dot product and allows us to treat complex force systems by superposition. It also implies that work can be represented as the area under a force‑versus‑displacement graph when the force is collinear with the displacement, a graphical interpretation that becomes powerful for variable forces.

Because work is a scalar, it does not obey vector addition rules; there is no concept of a “direction of work.” This distinguishes work from impulse, which is a vector quantity equal to the integral of force over time. The scalar character of work is the reason why kinetic energy, which is derived from work, is also a scalar, and why the work‑energy theorem relates a scalar change in kinetic energy to the net scalar work done.

1.3 Units and Sign Conventions

The SI unit of work is the joule (J), defined as one newton metre (N·m). One joule is the work done when a force of one newton moves an object one metre in the direction of the force. In the CGS system the unit is the erg, where 1 J = 10⁷ erg. Positive work indicates that the force component along the displacement adds energy to the system, while negative work indicates energy removal. Zero work occurs when the force is perpendicular to the displacement or when there is no displacement at all. These conventions are universally adopted in physics examinations and must be applied consistently when evaluating work done by gravity, friction, tension, or any other force.

2. Work Done by Constant Forces

2.1 Force Parallel to Displacement

When a constant force acts exactly along the line of motion, the angle θ is zero and cos θ = 1. The work simplifies to W = F s. This situation appears frequently in problems involving a block pulled horizontally by a rope, a car pushed by a constant engine force, or a weight lifted vertically at constant speed. Because the force magnitude and direction do not change, the work is directly proportional to the displacement. If the displacement doubles, the work doubles, illustrating the linear relationship that makes constant‑force problems straightforward to solve.

An important consequence of this linear relationship is that the work done by a constant force can be represented graphically as the area of a rectangle on a force‑versus‑displacement plot. The height of the rectangle is the constant force magnitude, and the width is the displacement. This geometric interpretation provides a quick visual check: the area must equal the numerical value of F s. It also sets the stage for understanding how the area under a curve generalises to variable forces.

2.2 Force at an Angle

In many practical situations the applied force is not collinear with the displacement. A classic example is a lawn‑mower pushed at an angle θ to the horizontal. Only the horizontal component F cos θ contributes to work, while the vertical component F sin θ does no work because there is no vertical displacement (assuming the mower stays on the ground). The work done is therefore W = F s cos θ. As the angle increases from 0° to 90°, the work decreases continuously, becoming zero at 90°. If the angle exceeds 90°, the cosine becomes negative and the force does negative work, opposing the motion.

This angular dependence explains why pulling a sled with a rope at a shallow angle is more efficient than pulling at a steep angle: a larger fraction of the applied force is directed along the displacement. In examination problems, students are often asked to compute the work done by a force given its magnitude, the displacement, and the angle, or to find the angle required for a specified amount of work. Mastery of the cosine factor is therefore essential.

2.3 Work by Multiple Constant Forces

When several constant forces act simultaneously, the net work is the algebraic sum of the works done by each force. Consider a block on a rough horizontal surface pulled by a horizontal force F while kinetic friction fₖ opposes the motion. The work done by the applied force is W_F = F s, the work done by friction is W_f = –fₖ s, and the normal and weight forces do zero work because they are perpendicular to the displacement. The net work is Wₙₑₜ = (F – fₖ) s. This net work equals the change in kinetic energy of the block, a direct illustration of the work‑energy theorem.

The principle of superposition of work simplifies the analysis of complex force systems. Instead of finding the resultant force first, one may compute the work of each force separately and then add them. This approach is particularly useful when forces act at different angles, because each work term involves its own cosine factor. The additive property also underlies the definition of potential energy for conservative forces, where the work done by the force depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken.

Example: Work Done by a Constant Force at an Angle
A 10 kg crate is pulled across a horizontal floor by a force of 50 N directed at 30° above the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the crate and the floor is 0.2. The crate moves a distance of 5 m. Calculate (a) the work done by the applied force, (b) the work done by friction, and (c) the net work done on the crate.
(a) The horizontal component of the applied force is Fₓ = 50 cos 30° = 50 × √3/2 ≈ 43.3 N. Work done by the applied force: W_F = Fₓ × s = 43.3 N × 5 m ≈ 216.5 J. (b) Normal force N = mg – F sin 30° = (10 × 9.8) – 50 × 0.5 = 98 – 25 = 73 N. Friction force fₖ = μₖ N = 0.2 × 73 = 14.6 N. Work done by friction: W_f = –fₖ × s = –14.6 N × 5 m = –73 J. (c) Net work Wₙₑₜ = W_F + W_f = 216.5 J – 73 J = 143.5 J. The positive net work indicates an increase in the crate’s kinetic energy.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem gives a force at an angle, always resolve the force into components parallel and perpendicular to the displacement before computing work. The perpendicular component does zero work regardless of its magnitude, and forgetting this step is a common source of sign errors in multiple‑choice questions.

3. Work Done by Variable Forces

3.1 Integral Formulation

For a force that varies in magnitude or direction along the path, the work cannot be obtained by a simple product. Instead, the work is defined as the line integral of the force vector dot the infinitesimal displacement vector: W = ∫_C F·dr. If the motion is constrained to a straight line along the x‑axis and the force varies only with position x, the integral reduces to W = ∫x_ix_f F(x) dx. This formulation captures the cumulative effect of a continuously changing force and is the foundation for analysing springs, gravitational fields near massive bodies, and any non‑uniform force field.

The integral approach also clarifies why work is path‑dependent for non‑conservative forces. If the force has a component perpendicular to the displacement at some points, the dot product automatically excludes that component. For a closed loop, the net work of a conservative force is zero, whereas for a non‑conservative force like friction the integral around a closed path is negative, reflecting energy dissipation. Understanding the integral definition is crucial for deriving potential energy functions and for solving problems where the force is given as a function of position.

3.2 Graphical Interpretation

When a variable force acts along a straight line, the work done is equal to the area under the force‑versus‑displacement curve between the initial and final positions. Positive areas (above the displacement axis) correspond to positive work, while negative areas (below the axis) correspond to negative work. This geometric picture allows quick estimation of work without performing an explicit integration, provided the graph is simple (e.g., triangles, rectangles, or trapezoids). In examinations, students are often presented with a piecewise‑linear F‑x graph and asked to compute the work by summing the areas of the constituent shapes.

The area interpretation also reinforces the scalar nature of work: the sign of the area directly gives the sign of the work. If the force changes direction relative to the displacement, the graph will cross the axis, and the total work is the algebraic sum of the signed areas. This visual method is especially helpful for checking the plausibility of an analytical integration result.

3.3 Example: Spring Force

An ideal spring obeys Hooke’s law, F = –k x, where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from the natural length. The negative sign indicates that the spring force always opposes the displacement. The work done by the spring when it is stretched or compressed from x_i to x_f is W = ∫x_ix_f (–k x) dx = –½ k (x_f² – x_i²). If the spring is released from rest at x_i = A and moves to the equilibrium position x_f = 0, the work done by the spring is W = ½ k A², a positive quantity because the force and displacement are in the same direction during the return motion.

This result shows that the spring stores energy when deformed; the magnitude of the stored elastic potential energy is U = ½ k x². The work done by the spring is the negative of the change in this potential energy, a hallmark of conservative forces. In problems involving a mass attached to a spring, the work‑energy theorem combined with the spring work expression yields the speed of the mass at any position without solving the differential equation of motion.

4. Kinetic Energy and the Work‑Energy Theorem

4.1 Derivation of Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is defined as the energy a body possesses by virtue of its motion. Starting from Newton’s second law, F = m a, and the definition of work for a constant force, W = F s, we can write W = m a s. Using the kinematic relation v_f² = v_i² + 2 a s (valid for constant acceleration), we eliminate a s to obtain W = ½ m v_f² – ½ m v_i². The quantity ½ m v² is identified as the kinetic energy K. This derivation shows that the work done by the net force on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy, regardless of whether the force is constant or variable, provided the work is evaluated as the integral of the net force.

The kinetic energy formula K = ½ m v² is a scalar, always non‑negative, and depends only on the speed, not the direction of motion. Its SI unit is the joule, the same as work. Because kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the speed, doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy, a fact that has profound implications for vehicle safety, projectile penetration, and energy budgeting in collisions.

4.2 Work‑Energy Theorem Statement

The work‑energy theorem states that the net work done by all forces acting on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy: Wₙₑₜ = ΔK = K_f – K_i. This theorem is a direct consequence of Newton’s second law and the definition of work, and it holds for any force, constant or variable, conservative or non‑conservative. It provides a powerful alternative to solving Newton’s second law differential equations when only the initial and final speeds are required.

In practice, the theorem is applied by computing the work of each force (including friction, gravity, normal, tension, spring, etc.) and summing them to obtain Wₙₑₜ. The result is then set equal to ½ m v_f² – ½ m v_i². If the initial speed is known, the final speed follows immediately. Conversely, if the final speed is known, the theorem can be used to find an unknown force magnitude or coefficient of friction. The theorem also extends to systems of particles by summing the kinetic energies of all particles and the works of all external and internal forces.

4.3 Applications to Variable Forces

When forces vary with position, the work‑energy theorem remains valid because the work integral automatically accounts for the variation. For a particle moving under a position‑dependent force F(x), the net work is ∫ F(x) dx. Equating this to the kinetic energy change yields an equation that can be solved for the speed as a function of position: v(x) = √[v_i² + (2/m) ∫x_ix F(x') dx']. This approach is the basis for the concept of potential energy, where the integral of a conservative force is expressed as the negative change in a potential function.

A classic application is the motion of a particle in a gravitational field where the force varies as F = –GMm/r². The work integral gives the change in gravitational potential energy, and the work‑energy theorem then provides the speed at any distance without solving the orbital equations. Similarly, for a mass on a spring, the theorem combined with the spring work expression yields the simple harmonic motion energy exchange between kinetic and elastic potential energy.

5. Potential Energy and Conservative Forces

5.1 Gravitational Potential Energy

Gravitational potential energy near the Earth’s surface is defined as U = m g h, where h is the vertical height measured from an arbitrary reference level. The work done by gravity when an object moves vertically from height h_i to h_f is W_g = –mg (h_f – h_i) = –ΔU. The negative sign indicates that gravity does positive work when the object descends (ΔU negative) and negative work when it ascends. Because the work depends only on the initial and final heights, gravity is a conservative force.

For larger distances where the gravitational force varies as F = –GMm/r², the potential energy is defined as U(r) = –GMm/r (zero at infinity). The work done by gravity in moving from r_i to r_f is again the negative change in this potential energy. This universal form is essential for orbital mechanics and for understanding escape velocity, where the total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) determines whether a body remains bound.

5.2 Elastic Potential Energy

As derived in the variable‑force section, the work done by an ideal spring is W_s = –½ k (x_f² – x_i²). Defining the elastic potential energy as U_s = ½ k x² makes the work equal to the negative change in potential energy: W_s = –ΔU_s. This energy is stored in the spring when it is stretched or compressed and is fully recoverable if the spring is ideal and no damping occurs. The quadratic dependence on displacement means that doubling the extension quadruples the stored energy, a principle exploited in devices such as bow‑and‑arrow, catapults, and vehicle suspension systems.

In a mass‑spring system oscillating on a frictionless surface, the total mechanical energy E = K + U_s remains constant. At the turning points the kinetic energy is zero and all energy is potential; at the equilibrium position the potential energy is zero and all energy is kinetic. This continuous exchange illustrates the conservation of mechanical energy for a system acted upon only by conservative forces.

5.3 Conservative vs Non‑Conservative Forces

A force is conservative if the work done by it on a particle moving between two points is independent of the path taken, or equivalently, if the work done around any closed loop is zero. Gravity, electrostatic force, and the ideal spring force are conservative. For such forces a potential energy function can be defined, and the total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) is conserved when only conservative forces do work.

Non‑conservative forces, such as kinetic friction, air resistance, and tension in a rope that is being pulled by an external agent, dissipate mechanical energy into internal energy (heat, sound). The work done by non‑conservative forces depends on the path length, and the mechanical energy of the system changes by an amount equal to the work done by these forces: ΔE_mech = W_nc. In problems involving both types of forces, the work‑energy theorem is written as W_c + W_nc = ΔK, where W_c = –ΔU. Rearranging gives ΔK + ΔU = W_nc, a form that directly shows how non‑conservative work alters the total mechanical energy.

6. Collisions and Conservation Laws

6.1 Elastic Collisions in One Dimension

An elastic collision is one in which both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. For two particles of masses m₁ and m₂ with initial velocities u₁ and u₂ (taken positive in the same direction), the conservation equations are m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂ = m₁ v₁ + m₂ v₂ (momentum) and ½ m₁ u₁² + ½ m₂ u₂² = ½ m₁ v₁² + ½ m₂ v₂² (kinetic energy). Solving these simultaneously yields the well‑known formulas for the final velocities: v₁ = (m₁ – m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) u₁ + (2 m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) u₂ and v₂ = (2 m₁)/(m₁ + m₂) u₁ + (m₂ – m₁)/(m₁ + m₂) u₂. These expressions show that in a head‑on elastic collision the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation, a useful shortcut for quick calculations.

Special cases illustrate the physics: if the masses are equal, the velocities are exchanged; if a light particle strikes a massive stationary target, the light particle rebounds with nearly the same speed while the massive target acquires a small velocity; if a massive projectile hits a light stationary target, the projectile continues almost unchanged while the light target shoots forward with approximately twice the projectile’s speed. These limiting behaviours are frequently tested in multiple‑choice questions.

6.2 Inelastic Collisions and Coefficient of Restitution

In an inelastic collision kinetic energy is not conserved, although momentum always is (provided no external impulses act). The extreme case is a perfectly inelastic collision, where the two bodies stick together and move with a common final velocity v = (m₁ u₁ + m₂ u₂)/(m₁ + m₂). The loss in kinetic energy appears as internal energy (heat, sound, deformation). For partially elastic collisions the coefficient of restitution e quantifies the degree of elasticity: e = (v₂ – v₁)/(u₁ – u₂), where the numerator is the relative speed of separation and the denominator is the relative speed of approach. The value of e ranges from 0 (perfectly inelastic) to 1 (perfectly elastic).

Using e together with momentum conservation allows the final velocities to be found for any given e. The kinetic energy loss can then be computed as ΔK = ½ μ (1 – e²) (u₁ – u₂)², where μ = m₁ m₂/(m₁ + m₂) is the reduced mass. This formula shows that the energy loss is proportional to 1 – e² and to the square of the relative speed, explaining why high‑speed collisions are far more destructive even for the same coefficient of restitution.

6.3 Two‑Dimensional Collisions

When collisions occur in two dimensions, momentum conservation must be applied as a vector equation: m₁ u⃗₁ + m₂ u⃗₂ = m₁ v⃗₁ + m₂ v⃗₂. Kinetic energy conservation (for elastic collisions) remains a scalar equation. The problem typically provides the initial velocities and the scattering angle of one particle; the unknowns are the final speeds and the direction of the second particle. Solving requires resolving the momentum equation into components (usually x and y) and using the energy equation as the third independent relation.

A common simplification is the “glancing collision” where one particle is initially at rest. In that case the momentum vectors form a triangle, and the angle between the final velocities is 90° for equal masses in an elastic collision. This geometric property follows from the vector addition of momentum and the Pythagorean relation implied by energy conservation. Understanding the vector nature of momentum and the scalar nature of energy is essential for correctly setting up the equations and avoiding sign errors in the component equations.

7. Power and Its Applications

7.1 Definition and Units of Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. The average power over a time interval Δt is P_avg = W/Δt. The instantaneous power is the time derivative of work: P = dW/dt = F·v, where v is the instantaneous velocity of the point of application of the force. The SI unit of power is the watt (W), equal to one joule per second (J/s). In mechanical contexts, horsepower (hp) is still encountered, with 1 hp = 746 W. Power quantifies how quickly a machine can perform a task; a high‑power engine can do the same work in less time than a low‑power one.

Because power is the dot product of force and velocity, it is a scalar that can be positive, negative, or zero. Positive power means the force adds energy to the system (e.g., a motor driving a wheel), negative power means the force removes energy (e.g., regenerative braking), and zero power occurs when the force is perpendicular to the velocity (e.g., centripetal force in uniform circular motion). This sign convention mirrors that of work and is consistent with the work‑energy theorem expressed in rate form: P_net = dK/dt.

7.2 Average vs Instantaneous Power

Average power is useful when the force or velocity varies over a long interval and only the total work and total time are known. For example, a car that accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 10 s, doing 200 kJ of work, has an average power of 20 kW.

Work–Energy Theorem in Non‑Inertial Frames

The work–energy theorem Wnet = ΔK is derived assuming an inertial reference frame. In a frame accelerating with acceleration a0 a pseudo force Fp = –m a0 acts on every particle of mass m. This force is non‑conservative in general because its work depends on the path taken in the accelerating frame. The net work in the non‑inertial frame becomes Wnet = Wreal + Wpseudo, and the theorem reads Wreal + Wpseudo = ΔK. If the pseudo force is constant (uniformly accelerating frame) its work is Wpseudo = –m a0·Δr, which can be absorbed into a potential Upseudo = m a0·r. In that special case a modified potential energy can be defined and mechanical energy is conserved if only conservative real forces act. In a rotating frame two pseudo forces appear: the centrifugal force Fcf = m ω² r (directed radially outward) and the Coriolis force Fcor = –2m ω × v. The centrifugal force is conservative; its potential is Ucf = –½ m ω² r². The Coriolis force is always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity in the rotating frame, so its work is zero and it does not alter kinetic energy. Consequently, for a particle moving under conservative real forces in a uniformly rotating frame, the quantity E' = K + Ureal + Ucf remains constant. A classic illustration is a bead sliding on a frictionless rotating hoop: the effective potential combines gravity and the centrifugal term, and equilibrium positions are found by differentiating this effective potential. When the frame acceleration is non‑uniform, the pseudo force becomes time‑dependent and no scalar potential exists; the work–energy theorem must then be applied with the explicit pseudo‑work term. Examiners often test the sign of pseudo‑work: in an elevator accelerating upward, the pseudo force points downward, so a block displaced upward does negative pseudo‑work, reducing its kinetic energy relative to the ground frame.

Potential Energy Curves and Equilibrium Analysis

A one‑dimensional potential energy function U(x) provides a complete qualitative picture of the motion of a particle of mass m under a conservative force F(x) = –dU/dx. The total mechanical energy E = K + U(x) is constant. Turning points occur where K = 0, i.e. U(x) = E; the particle cannot enter regions where U(x) > E. Equilibrium positions satisfy F = 0 → dU/dx = 0. Stability is decided by the second derivative: if d²U/dx² > 0 the equilibrium is stable (potential minimum), if d²U/dx² < 0 it is unstable (potential maximum), and if d²U/dx² = 0 higher‑order terms determine neutral or metastable behaviour. For small oscillations about a stable minimum, expand U(x) ≈ U(x₀) + ½ k (x – x₀)² with k = d²U/dx²|ₓ₀; the motion is simple harmonic with angular frequency ω = √(k/m). This method underlies the analysis of molecular vibrations (Lennard‑Jones potential), the motion of a particle in a double‑well potential, and the effective radial potential in central‑force problems Ueff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2mr²), where the centrifugal term creates a barrier that can produce stable circular orbits. A common examiner twist is to give a potential graph and ask for the number and type of equilibria, the range of energies for bounded motion, or the period of small oscillations using the curvature at the minimum. Students must remember that the force is the negative slope of the curve; a positive slope means a force directed toward negative x. Misreading the sign is a frequent source of error.

Worked Example: Equilibrium and Oscillation Frequency
A particle of mass 2 kg moves along the x‑axis under the potential U(x) = 4x⁴ – 8x² + 3 (joules). Find all equilibrium positions, classify their stability, and compute the angular frequency of small oscillations about each stable equilibrium.
First derivative: dU/dx = 16x³ – 16x = 16x(x² – 1). Setting to zero gives x = 0, x = ±1. Second derivative: d²U/dx² = 48x² – 16. At x = 0, d²U/dx² = –16 < 0 → unstable equilibrium (potential maximum). At x = ±1, d²U/dx² = 48 – 16 = 32 > 0 → stable equilibria (potential minima). For small oscillations, effective spring constant k = d²U/dx² = 32 N/m. Angular frequency ω = √(k/m) = √(32/2) = √16 = 4 rad/s. Both stable points have the same curvature, so the oscillation frequency is 4 rad/s about either minimum.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a potential‑energy graph appears, sketch the force curve mentally (negative slope). Mark turning points for a given total energy E by drawing a horizontal line at U = E. Count intersections with the U‑curve: each pair of intersections bounds an allowed region. For small‑oscillation questions, differentiate the potential twice at the minimum; the frequency formula ω = √(U''/m) is a one‑line answer if you identify the correct minimum.

Energy Transfer in Rotational Motion

The rotational analogue of the work–energy theorem states that the net work done by external torques equals the change in rotational kinetic energy: Wτ = ΔKrot = ½ I ω² – ½ I ω₀², where I is the moment of inertia about the fixed axis and ω the angular speed. For a constant torque τ acting through an angular displacement Δθ, the work is W = τ Δθ. If the torque varies, the integral W = ∫ τ dθ applies. Power in rotation is the rate of doing work: P = τ ω (scalar product, sign indicates whether energy is added or removed). A frequent application is a rolling object without slipping. The total kinetic energy is the sum of translational and rotational parts: K = ½ M v² + ½ ICM ω². Using the no‑slip condition v = R ω, this becomes K = ½ (M + ICM/R²) v². The effective mass Meff = M + ICM/R² determines the acceleration down an incline: a = g sinθ / (1 + ICM/MR²). Energy methods avoid dealing with friction forces because static friction does no work (its point of application is instantaneously at rest). However, if slipping occurs, kinetic friction does negative work and mechanical energy is not conserved. Another subtle point: when a torque is applied about a moving axis (e.g., a spinning wheel on a moving vehicle), the work done by the torque in the ground frame includes a term from the translation of the axis. The general expression for power delivered by a force F applied at a point with velocity v is P = F·v; for a pure torque the equivalent is P = τ·ω only if the reference point is instantaneously at rest or the torque is a pure couple. Examiners often ask for the fraction of total kinetic energy that is rotational for a given rolling body; the answer is Krot/K = (ICM/MR²) / (1 + ICM/MR²).

Collisions in the Centre‑of‑Mass Frame

Transforming to the centre‑of‑mass (CM) frame simplifies collision analysis because the total momentum is zero. For two particles of masses m₁, m₂ with laboratory velocities u₁, u₂, the CM velocity is VCM = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂). Velocities in the CM frame are u₁' = u₁ – VCM, u₂' = u₂ – VCM. In this frame the momenta are equal and opposite: m₁ u₁' = –m₂ u₂'. For an elastic collision, kinetic energy conservation in the CM frame implies that the speeds remain unchanged: |v₁'| = |u₁'|, |v₂'| = |u₂'|. Only the directions change; the scattering angle θ in the CM frame is the angle between u₁' and v₁'. The laboratory scattering angles are obtained by vector addition of VCM. A key result: for equal masses in an elastic collision with one initially at rest, the CM frame velocities are equal in magnitude and opposite; after collision they simply reverse direction, giving a 90° angle between the final laboratory velocities. For inelastic collisions with coefficient of restitution e, the relative speed in the CM frame is reduced by factor e: |v₁' – v₂'| = e |u₁' – u₂'|. Since the total momentum in the CM frame is zero, the final speeds are v₁' = e u₁', v₂' = e u₂' (directions unchanged for head‑on collisions). The kinetic energy loss in the lab frame equals the loss in the CM frame because the CM kinetic energy ½ (m₁+m₂) VCM² is unchanged. This frame is especially powerful for two‑dimensional collisions: the problem reduces to finding the scattering angle in the CM frame, which is often given or can be deduced from impact parameter models. A typical examiner question provides the initial lab velocities and the CM scattering angle, asking for the final lab velocities; the solution proceeds by (1) computing VCM, (2) finding initial CM velocities, (3) rotating one of them by the given angle, (4) transforming back to the lab frame.

Power Delivered by Variable Forces and Energy Flow

When a force varies with time or position, instantaneous power is still P(t) = F(t)·v(t). For a one‑dimensional motion with F(x) and v(x), the work–energy theorem gives ½ m v² = ∫ F dx. Differentiating with respect to time yields m v dv/dt = F v, confirming P = F v. Average power over a time interval Δt is Pavg = (1/Δt) ∫ P dt = W/Δt. In periodic systems such as a mass‑spring oscillator, the instantaneous power oscillates between positive (energy flowing into kinetic) and negative (energy flowing into potential). Over a full cycle the average power is zero for a conservative system. If a damping force Fd = –b v acts, the instantaneous power dissipated is Pd = –b v², always negative. The average power loss per cycle equals the energy lost per cycle divided by the period. For a driven oscillator with driving force Fdr = F₀ cos ωt, the average power supplied by the driver is Pavg = ½ F₀ v₀ cos φ, where v₀ is the velocity amplitude and φ the phase lag. At resonance φ = 0 and power transfer is maximum. In rotational systems with variable torque, e.g. an engine delivering torque τ(θ), the work per revolution is W = ∮ τ dθ and average power is Pavg = W f where f is the rotation frequency. A practical twist: a car engine produces a torque curve τ(ω); the power curve is P(ω) = τ(ω) ω. The gear ratio is chosen so that the engine operates near the peak of the power curve for maximum acceleration. Students should be comfortable converting between force‑velocity and torque‑angular‑velocity representations, and recognising that the sign of power indicates the direction of energy flow.

  • Assuming the work–energy theorem holds unchanged in an accelerating frame without adding pseudo‑work.
  • Confusing the sign of the centrifugal potential; it is negative, so the effective potential is lowered at larger radii.
  • Treating static friction as doing work in rolling‑without‑slipping problems.
  • Forgetting that the Coriolis force does no work because it is always perpendicular to velocity in the rotating frame.
  • Using the laboratory scattering angle directly in CM‑frame formulas without transforming velocities.
  • Neglecting the phase factor when computing average power in driven oscillators.
  • Misapplying the reduced‑mass kinetic‑energy loss formula to collisions where external impulses act during the impact.

Work–Energy Theorem for Systems of Particles and Rigid Bodies

The work–energy theorem for a single particle, W = ΔK, extends to a system of N particles by summing the work done by all external and internal forces. The total kinetic energy of the system is the sum of the kinetic energies of each particle: K = Σ ½ mi vi². Using König’s theorem, this total kinetic energy can be decomposed into the kinetic energy of the centre of mass (CM) plus the kinetic energy relative to the CM: K = ½ M VCM² + Krel, where M = Σ mi and VCM is the CM velocity. The work done by external forces equals the change in total kinetic energy: Wext = ΔK. Internal forces do net work only if the system is deformable; for a rigid body the internal forces are constraint forces that do no net work because the distance between any two particles remains constant. Consequently, for a rigid body the work–energy theorem reduces to Wext = Δ(½ M VCM² + ½ ICM ω²), where ICM is the moment of inertia about the CM and ω the angular velocity. This form is essential for problems involving combined translation and rotation, such as a cylinder rolling down an incline without slipping. The friction force in pure rolling is static and does no work, yet it provides the torque that converts translational kinetic energy into rotational kinetic energy. A common examiner twist is to ask for the fraction of total kinetic energy that is rotational; the answer is Krot/K = (ICM/M R²) / (1 + ICM/M R²). Students must also remember that if the body slips, kinetic friction does negative work and the simple energy conservation equation must include the work done by friction.

Energy Conservation in Vertical Circular Motion and Loop‑the‑Loop Problems

Vertical circular motion provides a classic application of energy conservation combined with Newton’s second law for centripetal acceleration. Consider a particle of mass m attached to a light string or moving on a circular track of radius R. The only forces doing work are gravity (conservative) and the normal reaction or tension (always perpendicular to displacement, hence zero work). Taking the lowest point as the reference for gravitational potential energy, the total mechanical energy E = ½ m v² + m g h remains constant. At the top of the circle (height 2<R</em>), the minimum speed required to maintain contact with the track (or keep the string taut) is obtained from the centripetal condition m vtop²/R = m g + N, where the normal reaction N ≥ 0. The limiting case N = 0 gives vtop² = g R. Using energy conservation between the bottom (speed vbot) and the top: ½ m vbot² = ½ m vtop² + m g (2R). Substituting vtop² = g R yields vbot² = 5 g R. If the particle is released from rest at a height h above the bottom, the condition to complete the loop is h ≥ 2.5 R. For a bead on a frictionless wire the same analysis holds; for a rolling object the rotational kinetic energy must be included, raising the required height. Examiners often test the distinction between a string (which can go slack) and a rigid track (which can provide an outward normal force). Another trap is to assume the speed is constant around the circle, ignoring the continuous exchange between kinetic and potential energy.

Technique: Energy–Centripetal Coupling in a Loop‑the‑Loop
A small block of mass 0.2 kg is released from rest at a height h on a frictionless track that leads into a vertical circular loop of radius 0.5 m. Find the minimum h such that the block just completes the loop without losing contact with the track.
At the top of the loop the normal reaction N becomes zero for the limiting case. The centripetal force is provided solely by gravity: m g = m vtop²/R → vtop² = g R = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m²/s². Energy conservation between release point (height h, speed 0) and top of loop (height 2<R</em> = 1.0 m, speed vtop): m g h = ½ m vtop² + m g (2R). Cancel m: g h = ½ g R + 2 g R = 2.5 g R. Thus h = 2.5 R = 2.5 × 0.5 = 1.25 m. The block must be released from at least 1.25 m above the bottom of the loop.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a vertical‑circle problem appears, first write the energy equation between the known point and the top of the circle. Then write the centripetal force equation at the top with the appropriate normal force (zero for minimum speed). Solve the two equations simultaneously. This two‑step method avoids the common error of using only energy or only dynamics.

Work Done by Non‑Conservative Forces: Friction, Air Resistance, and Energy Dissipation

Non‑conservative forces are path‑dependent and remove mechanical energy from the system, usually converting it into internal energy (heat, sound). The work–energy theorem in the presence of non‑conservative forces reads Wnc = ΔK + ΔU, where Wnc is the work done by all non‑conservative forces. For kinetic friction of constant magnitude fk acting over a displacement s along the surface, Wfr = –fk s (negative because the force opposes relative motion). Static friction does no work in pure rolling because the point of contact is instantaneously at rest; however, if the body rolls with slipping, static friction is replaced by kinetic friction and does negative work. Air resistance (drag) is often modelled as Fd = –b v (linear) or –c v² (quadratic). The work done by drag over a path is Wd = ∫ Fd·d r = –∫ b v·v dt = –b ∫ v² dt for the linear model, which is always negative. In a damped harmonic oscillator the average power dissipated per cycle equals the energy lost per cycle divided by the period. A frequent examiner trap is to treat the work done by friction as positive when the frictional force acts in the direction of motion of the centre of mass (e.g., a block on an accelerating conveyor belt). The sign must be decided from the relative displacement at the contact surface, not the absolute displacement of the centre of mass.

Power, Efficiency, and Mechanical Advantage in Simple Machines

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred: P = dW/dt = F·v for a force acting on a moving point. In a simple machine (lever, pulley system, inclined plane, wedge, screw) the input work Win is supplied by an effort force Fe moving through a distance de, while the output work Wout is delivered to a load force Fl moving through a distance dl. For an ideal (frictionless) machine, energy conservation gives Win = Wout, so Fe de = Fl dl. The mechanical advantage (MA) is defined as MA = Fl/Fe = de/dl. The velocity ratio (VR) is the ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load; for ideal machines MA = VR. Real machines have friction, so Wout < Win and the efficiency η = Wout/Win × 100 % is less than 100 %. The actual mechanical advantage (AMA) is Fl/Fe measured experimentally, while the ideal mechanical advantage (IMA) is the VR. The relationship η = AMA/IMA × 100 % is often tested. Power calculations for machines involve the rate of doing work: Pin = Fe ve, Pout = Fl vl, and η = Pout/Pin. A typical problem gives a pulley system with a known VR and an efficiency, asking for the effort required to lift a load at a certain speed. Students must convert between force, distance, and power domains without confusing MA with VR.

Variable Mass Systems: Rocket Propulsion and the Work–Energy Theorem

Systems that gain or lose mass (rockets, conveyor belts, falling raindrops) require a modified work–energy analysis because the standard theorem W = ΔK assumes constant mass. For a rocket ejecting exhaust at a constant relative velocity u (measured in the rocket frame), momentum conservation gives the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation: Δv = u ln(m0/m), where m0 is the initial mass and m the instantaneous mass. The kinetic energy of the rocket plus exhaust system increases due to the conversion of chemical energy. The work done by the thrust force Fth = u |dm/dt| on the rocket is Wth = ∫ Fth v dt. However, the total kinetic energy change of the rocket alone is not equal to Wth because the exhaust carries away kinetic energy. The correct energy balance for the rocket‑exhaust system is: chemical energy released = ΔKrocket + ΔKexhaust + losses. In the instantaneous rest frame of the rocket, the power delivered to the rocket is Procket = Fth v, while the power carried away by the exhaust is ½ |dm/dt| u². The sum equals the rate of chemical energy consumption. A common examiner error is to apply the constant‑mass work–energy theorem to the rocket alone, ignoring the kinetic energy of the expelled mass. Another trap is to assume the thrust force does work equal to the change in kinetic energy of the rocket; the correct statement is that the thrust power plus the rate of kinetic energy carried by the exhaust equals the rate of chemical energy release.

  • Forgetting that internal forces in a deformable system can do net work, unlike in a rigid body.
  • Assuming the normal force does work in vertical circular motion; it is always perpendicular to displacement.
  • Using the wrong sign for friction work when the contact surface moves relative to the ground.
  • Confusing mechanical advantage with velocity ratio in real machines and neglecting efficiency.
  • Applying the constant‑mass work–energy theorem to a rocket without accounting for the kinetic energy of the exhaust.
  • Treating static friction as dissipative in pure rolling problems.
  • Neglecting the rotational kinetic energy term when a rolling object enters a vertical loop.
