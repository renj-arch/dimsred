1. Electric Charges & Fields

Electrostatics is the branch of physics that deals with stationary electric charges, the forces between them, and the electric fields they produce. This is one of the most fundamental topics in NEET Physics and forms the basis for understanding electric circuits, magnetism, and electromagnetic induction. Approximately 3–4 questions appear from electrostatics in NEET, with another 3–4 from current electricity.

Properties of Electric Charge

Electric charge is an intrinsic property of matter that gives rise to electric force. The two types of charge are positive (carried by protons) and negative (carried by electrons). Like charges repel each other; unlike charges attract.

  • Additivity of charge: Total charge of a system is the algebraic sum of all individual charges. A charge of +3e and −e gives a net charge of +2e.
  • Quantization of charge: Charge exists in discrete packets: q = ne, where n is an integer and e = 1.602 × 10−19 C is the elementary charge. Millikan’s oil drop experiment verified this quantization.
  • Conservation of charge: In an isolated system, total charge remains constant. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another.
  • Invariance of charge: Charge is independent of the frame of reference. It does not change with velocity (unlike mass in relativity).
NEET Fact
The elementary charge e = 1.6 × 10−19 C is the smallest free-existing charge. Quarks have fractional charges (±e/3, ±2e/3) but they are never found free in nature due to confinement.

Charging Methods

There are three primary methods of charging a body:

  • Charging by friction: When two bodies are rubbed together, electrons transfer from one to the other. The body that gains electrons becomes negatively charged; the one that loses electrons becomes positively charged. Example: rubbing a glass rod with silk makes the rod positive and silk negative.
  • Charging by conduction: A charged body touches a neutral body, sharing charge. Both bodies acquire the same type of charge. The total charge is conserved and distributed based on their capacitances.
  • Charging by induction: A charged body is brought near a neutral body without contact. The near side acquires opposite charge and the far side acquires the same charge. If the far side is grounded momentarily, the neutral body acquires a net opposite charge. Induction does not require direct contact and conserves total charge.

Example 1: Quantization of Charge
A body has a charge of −4.8 × 10−19 C. How many excess electrons does it have? (e = 1.6 × 10−19 C)
Solution: Number of excess electrons, n = q/e = (−4.8 × 10−19) / (−1.6 × 10−19) = 3 electrons.
Example 2: Charge by Induction
A negatively charged rod is brought near (not touching) an isolated neutral metallic sphere. If the sphere is momentarily grounded and then the rod is removed, what is the final charge on the sphere?
Solution: When the negatively charged rod approaches, positive charge is induced on the near side of the sphere and negative on the far side. Grounding allows the negative charge to flow to earth. When the rod is removed, the sphere is left with a net positive charge.

Coulomb’s Law

Coulomb’s law describes the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges. The magnitude of the force is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force acts along the line joining the two charges.

F = k · q1q2 / r2

where k = 1/(4πε0) = 9 × 109 N·m2/C2 is the electrostatic constant and ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 C2/N·m2 is the permittivity of free space. The force is attractive for opposite charges and repulsive for like charges. In vector form: F12 = k q1q2 / r2 · r̂12.

Relative permittivity (dielectric constant): When placed in a medium with dielectric constant εr, the force reduces to Fmedium = Fvacuum / εr. So F = (1/4πε0εr) · q1q2/r2.

Example 3: Coulomb Force Between Two Charges
Two point charges of +3 μC and +6 μC are placed 3 cm apart in air. Find the magnitude of the force between them. (k = 9 × 109 N·m2/C2)
Solution: F = k q1q2 / r2 = 9×109 × 3×10−6 × 6×10−6 / (0.03)2 = 9×109 × 18×10−12 / (9×10−4) = 162×10−3 / 9×10−4 = 180 N. The force is repulsive since both charges are positive.
Example 4: Coulomb Force in a Medium
Two charges of 2 μC and −4 μC are placed 5 cm apart in water. Find the force between them. (εr for water = 80)
Solution: Fair = 9×109 × 2×10−6 × (−4×10−6) / (0.05)2
Fair = 9×109 × (−8×10−12) / (2.5×10−3) = −28.8 N (attractive)
Fwater = Fair / εr = −28.8 / 80 = −0.36 N
Shortcut: Force Proportionality
F ∝ q1q2; F ∝ 1/r2. If both charges are doubled, force becomes 4×. If distance is halved, force becomes 4×. If both are doubled and distance is halved, force becomes 16×. Use these proportionalities to solve NEET problems in seconds without plugging into the full formula.

Principle of Superposition of Forces

The electrostatic force on a charge due to multiple other charges is the vector sum of individual forces. If charges q1, q2, ..., qn exert forces F1, F2, ..., Fn on a test charge q0, the net force is Fnet = F1 + F2 + ... + Fn. For NEET, you often need to resolve forces into components and cancel symmetric terms.

Example 5: Superposition Principle
Three equal charges of +2 μC each are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side 10 cm. Find the net force on any one charge.
Solution: Force between any two charges: F = 9×109 × (2×10−6)2 / (0.1)2 = 9×109 × 4×10−12 / 0.01 = 3.6 N.
On charge at A, forces from B and C each = 3.6 N at 60° to each other.
Net force = √(F2+F2+2F2cos60°) = √(3.62+3.62+2(3.62)(0.5)) = √(12.96+12.96+12.96) = √38.88 = 6.24 N along the outward bisector.
Example 6: Point of Zero Force
Two charges of +2 μC and +8 μC are placed 10 cm apart. Find the point on the line joining them where a third charge experiences zero net force.
Solution: Let x be the distance from the 2 μC charge. For zero force, forces from both charges must be equal and opposite:
k(2)/x2 = k(8)/(10 − x)2
Taking square roots: √2/x = √8/(10 − x) ⇒ √2/x = 2√2/(10 − x)
1/x = 2/(10 − x) ⇒ 10 − x = 2x ⇒ x = 10/3 = 3.33 cm from the smaller charge.
Example 7: Superposition with Three Collinear Charges
Three point charges q1 = +4 μC, q2 = −6 μC, and q3 = +2 μC are placed on the x-axis at x = 0, x = 4 cm, and x = 10 cm respectively. Find the net force on q2.
Solution: Force on q2 due to q1: F21 = 9×109 × (4×10−6)(6×10−6) / (0.04)2 = 9×109 × 24×10−12 / 0.0016 = 135 N (attractive, towards left)
Force on q2 due to q3: F23 = 9×109 × (6×10−6)(2×10−6) / (0.06)2 = 9×109 × 12×10−12 / 0.0036 = 30 N (attractive, towards right)
Net force = 135 − 30 = 105 N towards left (q1 direction).

Continuous Charge Distribution

For a continuous charge distribution, the total charge is distributed over a line, surface, or volume. We define charge density for each type:

  • Linear charge density (λ): Charge per unit length, λ = dq/dl. SI unit: C/m.
  • Surface charge density (σ): Charge per unit area, σ = dq/dA. SI unit: C/m2.
  • Volume charge density (ρ): Charge per unit volume, ρ = dq/dV. SI unit: C/m3.

The total electric field due to a continuous distribution is obtained by integrating contributions from infinitesimal elements: dE = (k dq / r2) r̂. The integration is often simplified using symmetry considerations.

Example 8: Linear Charge Distribution
A straight wire of length L has a uniform linear charge density λ. Find the electric field at a point on the axis of the wire at a distance a from one end.
Solution: Consider an element dx at distance x from the point. dE = k λ dx / x2. Integrate from x = a to x = a+L:
E = ∫ kλ dx/x2 = kλ[−1/x]aa+L = kλ(1/a − 1/(a+L)).
For L → ∞, E = kλ/a for axial field.
Charge Density Mnemonic
Think of a power cord: λ (linear) for a thin wire, σ (surface) for a sheet of paper, ρ (volume) for a solid block. The Greek letter order: λ (line), σ (surface), ρ (volume) — alphabetical order matches 1D, 2D, 3D.

Electric Field

The electric field at a point is defined as the force per unit positive test charge placed at that point. If a test charge q0 experiences force F, the electric field is E = F / q0. The electric field due to a point charge Q at a distance r is:

E = kQ / r2 (magnitude), E = kQ / r2 · r̂ (vector)

The direction of E is radially outward from a positive charge and radially inward toward a negative charge. The electric field also obeys the superposition principle: the net field is the vector sum of fields due to each charge.

Detailed Derivation: Electric Field on the Axis of a Charged Ring

Consider a ring of radius R with total charge Q uniformly distributed. The linear charge density λ = Q/(2πR). Take an element dl at angle θ. The charge dq = λ dl = (Q/2πR)(R dθ) = Q dθ/2π. The distance from this element to point P on the axis at distance x is r = √(R2 + x2). The field due to dq has magnitude dE = k dq / (R2+x2). Only the axial component (dE cosφ) survives due to symmetry: cosφ = x/√(R2+x2). Thus:

E = ∫ dE cosφ = ∫ k dq x / (R2+x2)3/2 = kQx / (R2+x2)3/2

At the centre (x = 0): E = 0. For x >> R: E ≈ kQ/x2 (point charge behaviour). E is maximum when dE/dx = 0, which gives x = R/√2, and Emax = kQ/(2√3 R2) = kQ/(3.464 R2).

Electric Field on the Axis of a Uniformly Charged Disc

A disc of radius R with uniform surface charge density σ can be treated as a collection of concentric rings. Consider a ring of radius r and width dr. Its charge dq = σ · 2πr dr. The field due to this ring at axial point P (distance x) is dE = k dq x / (r2+x2)3/2. Integrating from r = 0 to r = R:

E = (σ/2ε0)[1 − x/√(R2+x2)]

For R → ∞ (infinite sheet): E = σ/(2ε0), independent of x. For x >> R: using binomial expansion, E ≈ σR2/(4ε0x2) = kQ/x2, where Q = σπR2.

Key Result for NEET: Disc vs Ring
For a ring, E ∝ x/(R2+x2)3/2. For a disc, E ∝ [1 − x/√(R2+x2)]. The disc field approaches σ/(2ε0) for large R. Both reduce to kQ/x2 for x >> R. For x = 0: ring E = 0, disc E = σ/(2ε0).
Example 9: Electric Field of a Point Charge
What is the electric field at a point 20 cm from a point charge of 4 μC? (k = 9 × 109 N·m2/C2)
Solution: E = kQ/r2 = 9×109 × 4×10−6 / (0.2)2 = 36×103 / 0.04 = 9 × 105 N/C. The field is directed radially outward from the charge.
Example 10: Maximum Electric Field of a Ring
A ring of radius 5 cm has a total charge of 1 μC uniformly distributed. Find the maximum electric field on its axis and the position where it occurs.
Solution: E is maximum at x = R/√2 = 5/√2 = 3.54 cm.
Emax = kQ/(2√3 R2) = 9×109 × 1×10−6 / (2 × 1.732 × 0.052) = 9×103 / (2 × 1.732 × 0.0025) = 9000 / 0.00866 = 1.04 × 106 N/C.
Visualization Tip
Electric field lines start at positive charges and end at negative charges. The density of field lines indicates the field strength. Lines never cross each other. For a uniform field, lines are parallel and equally spaced. For a point charge, lines are radial and equally spaced in angle.

Properties of Electric Field Lines

  • Field lines originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges.
  • Field lines never intersect each other. If they did, there would be two directions of E at the same point, which is impossible.
  • The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the electric field at that point.
  • The number of lines per unit area (normal to the lines) is proportional to the magnitude of E.
  • For a uniform field, lines are parallel, straight, and equally spaced.
  • In the region between two equal and opposite charges, field lines go from positive to negative smoothly.
  • For a dipole, field lines emerge from the positive charge and curve around to enter the negative charge.
  • Field lines in a conductor are always perpendicular to the surface.

Electric Field due to Various Configurations

For a continuous charge distribution, the total electric field is obtained by integrating contributions from infinitesimal charge elements: dE = k dq / r2 · r̂. Key results for NEET:

  • Charged ring at centre: E = 0 (by symmetry, all components cancel).
  • Charged ring far away (x >> R): E ≈ kQ/x2, behaving like a point charge.
  • Charged ring at x = R/√2: Electric field is maximum. Emax = kQ / (2√3 · R2).
  • Infinite line charge: E ∝ 1/r (cylindrical symmetry).
  • Infinite plane sheet: E is constant, independent of distance. This is a key result: the field does not decrease with distance from an infinite sheet.
  • Two parallel oppositely charged plates: E = σ/ε0 between them (both sheets contribute σ/2ε0 in the same direction), and E = 0 outside (fields cancel). This is the parallel plate capacitor configuration.
  • Charged spherical shell outside: Behaves as if all charge is at centre (E = kQ/r2).
  • Charged spherical shell inside: E = 0 (this is a key property of conductors in electrostatic equilibrium).
Example 11: Field of a Charged Disc
A disc of radius 10 cm has a uniform surface charge density of 1 μC/m2. Find the electric field at a point 5 cm from the centre along the axis. (ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m)
Solution: E = (σ/2ε0)[1 − x/√(R2+x2)] = (10−6/(2×8.85×10−12))[1 − 0.05/√(0.01+0.0025)] = (10−6/1.77×10−11)[1 − 0.05/√0.0125] = 5.65×104 [1 − 0.05/0.1118] = 5.65×104 × (1 − 0.447) = 5.65×104 × 0.553 = 3.12 × 104 N/C.

Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges +q and −q separated by a small distance 2a (or d). The electric dipole moment p = q × 2a (or p = q × d), directed from −q to +q. The SI unit of dipole moment is C·m.

  • Axial field (end-on position): E = 2kp / r3 (along the dipole axis, in the direction of p).
  • Equatorial field (broad-side position): E = kp / r3 (opposite to the direction of p).
  • General expression for field at any point: In polar coordinates (r, θ) where θ is measured from the dipole axis: Er = (2kp cosθ)/r3 and Eθ = (kp sinθ)/r3. The magnitude E = (kp/r3)√(1+3cos2θ).
  • Torque on a dipole in a uniform electric field: τ = pE sinθ = p × E. Torque is zero when p is parallel or antiparallel to E. Torque is maximum when p is perpendicular to E (τmax = pE).
  • Potential energy of a dipole in a uniform electric field: U = −pE cosθ = −p · E. Minimum energy Umin = −pE (stable equilibrium when p || E). Maximum energy Umax = +pE (unstable equilibrium when p antiparallel to E).
  • Work done to rotate a dipole: W = pE(cosθ1 − cosθ2). Work done to rotate from θ = 0° to θ = 180° is W = 2pE.
  • Electric field of dipole in vector form: Eaxial = 2kp/r3; Eequatorial = −kp/r3.
  • Force on a dipole in non-uniform field: F = p(dE/dr) cosθ. A dipole experiences a net force only in a non-uniform electric field, being pulled toward regions of stronger field if aligned with the field.
Example 12: Dipole in Uniform Field
An electric dipole of moment 2 × 10−8 C·m is placed at 60° to a uniform electric field of 104 N/C. Find (a) the torque acting on it, and (b) the work required to rotate it to the equilibrium position.
Solution:
(a) τ = pE sinθ = (2 × 10−8)(104)(sin 60°) = 2 × 10−4 × (√3/2) = 1.73 × 10−4 N·m
(b) Work = Ufinal − Uinitial = (−pE cos0°) − (−pE cos60°)
= −pE + pE/2 = −pE/2 = −(2×10−8)(104)/2 = −10−4 J. The negative sign means work is done by the field.
Example 13: Dipole Field Calculation
An electric dipole of moment 5 × 10−10 C·m is placed at the origin with its axis along the x-axis. Find the electric field at a point on the x-axis at a distance of 20 cm from the centre.
Solution: Axial position: E = 2kp/r3 = 2 × 9×109 × 5×10−10 / (0.2)3 = 2 × 9×109 × 5×10−10 / 0.008 = 90 × 10−1 / 0.008 = 9 / 0.008 = 1125 N/C along +x direction.
NEET Shortcut: Dipole Field Ratio
At the same distance r, Eaxial : Eequatorial = 2 : 1. If the axial field is 20 N/C, the equatorial field at the same distance is 10 N/C. This is frequently tested in NEET.

Conductors, Insulators, and Semiconductors

Materials are classified based on their ability to conduct electric charge:

  • Conductors: Materials with free electrons that can move easily. Metals like copper, silver, aluminium are good conductors. In electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside a conductor is zero, and any excess charge resides entirely on the surface.
  • Insulators: Materials where electrons are tightly bound and cannot move freely. Examples: rubber, glass, plastic, wood. They do not conduct electricity.
  • Semiconductors: Materials with intermediate conductivity that increases with temperature. Examples: silicon, germanium. Unlike metals, their resistivity decreases with increasing temperature.

Gauss’s Law

Gauss’s law relates the net electric flux through a closed surface to the net charge enclosed by the surface. It is one of Maxwell’s equations and is particularly useful for computing electric fields of symmetric charge distributions.

ΦE = ∮ E · dA = Qenc / ε0

The closed surface is called a Gaussian surface. The key to applying Gauss’s law is choosing a Gaussian surface that matches the symmetry of the charge distribution so that E is constant over parts of the surface and the dot product simplifies.

Applying Gauss’s Law
Step 1: Identify symmetry (spherical, cylindrical, or planar). Step 2: Choose Gaussian surface that matches symmetry. Step 3: Compute flux = ∮ E·dA = E × (area where E is perpendicular and constant). Step 4: Compute enclosed charge Qenc. Step 5: Equate E · A = Qenc/ε0 and solve for E.

Detailed Gauss Law Derivations

1. Spherical shell (radius R, charge Q):
For r > R: Choose a concentric sphere of radius r as Gaussian surface. By symmetry, E is radial and constant over the surface. Flux = E × 4πr2. Qenc = Q. So E × 4πr2 = Q/ε0 ⇒ E = Q/(4πε0r2) = kQ/r2.
For r < R: Gaussian sphere of radius r encloses no charge (Qenc = 0). Hence E = 0 inside the shell.

2. Uniformly charged solid sphere (radius R, total charge Q):
For r > R: Same as shell, E = kQ/r2.
For r < R: Volume charge density ρ = Q/(4/3 πR3). Qenc = ρ × (4/3 πr3) = Qr3/R3. Gauss: E × 4πr2 = Qr3/(ε0R3) ⇒ E = Qr/(4πε0R3) = kQr/R3. The field increases linearly from centre to surface.

3. Infinite line charge (linear density λ):
Choose a coaxial cylinder of radius r and length L as Gaussian surface. Flux through curved surface: E × 2πrL (flux through ends is zero as E is parallel to them). Qenc = λL. Gauss: E × 2πrL = λL/ε0 ⇒ E = λ/(2πε0r).

4. Infinite plane sheet (surface density σ):
Choose a cylindrical pillbox of cross-sectional area A that passes through the sheet, with half on each side. Flux = E × A + E × A = 2EA (E is perpendicular to sheet and outward on both flat faces; flux through curved surface is zero). Qenc = σA. Gauss: 2EA = σA/ε0 ⇒ E = σ/(2ε0). Note: E is independent of distance from the sheet.

5. Two parallel oppositely charged plates:
For plates with charge densities +σ and −σ, between the plates each sheet contributes σ/(2ε0) in the same direction, so Ebetween = σ/ε0. Outside the plates, the fields cancel giving E = 0.

Example 14: Gauss’s Law for a Spherical Conductor
A spherical conductor of radius 10 cm has a charge of 2 μC uniformly distributed on its surface. Find the electric field at (a) 5 cm from the centre, and (b) 15 cm from the centre.
Solution:
(a) r = 5 cm < R = 10 cm. Inside a conductor in equilibrium, E = 0 (all charge resides on the surface).
(b) r = 15 cm > R. E = kQ/r2 = 9×109 × 2×10−6 / (0.15)2 = 18×103 / 0.0225 = 8 × 105 N/C, radially outward.
Example 15: Infinite Plane Sheet
An infinite plane sheet has a uniform surface charge density of 2 μC/m2. Find the electric field at a point 5 cm from the sheet. (ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m)
Solution: For an infinite plane sheet, E = σ/(2ε0) = (2 × 10−6) / (2 × 8.85 × 10−12) = 10−6 / 8.85 × 10−12 = 1.13 × 105 N/C. Note that E does not depend on the distance from the sheet.
Example 16: Gauss Law for Solid Sphere
A uniformly charged solid sphere of radius 6 cm has total charge 3 μC. Find the electric field at a point (a) 3 cm from the centre, and (b) 9 cm from the centre.
Solution:
(a) r = 3 cm < R: E = kQr/R3 = 9×109 × 3×10−6 × 0.03 / (0.06)3 = 9×109 × 9×10−8 / 2.16×10−4 = 810 / 2.16×10−4 = 3.75 × 106 N/C
(b) r = 9 cm > R: E = kQ/r2 = 9×109 × 3×10−6 / (0.09)2 = 27000 / 0.0081 = 3.33 × 106 N/C
Important NEET Fact
For a charged conductor, the electric field just outside the surface is E = σ/ε0 (perpendicular to surface), while just inside, E = 0. The field at the surface is double that of an isolated plane sheet (σ/2ε0) because all the charge is on one side.

Van de Graaff Generator

The Van de Graaff generator is an electrostatic machine that produces high voltages (up to several million volts). It works on the principle of corona discharge and charge accumulation on a hollow conductor. A moving belt carries charge to a large hollow metallic sphere, where it accumulates, producing a very high potential. The maximum potential is limited by the dielectric breakdown of air (about 3 × 106 V/m). Van de Graaff generators are used in particle accelerators and high-voltage physics experiments.

Van de Graaff in NEET
Van de Graaff generator operates on two principles: (1) The action of sharp points (corona discharge) — charge leaks from sharp points onto a moving belt. (2) A hollow conductor accumulates all charge on its outer surface, building up very high potential. The maximum voltage is limited by the breakdown of the surrounding medium.

2. Electric Potential & Capacitance

Electric potential is a scalar quantity that represents the electric potential energy per unit charge at a point in an electric field. Unlike electric field (a vector), potential can be added algebraically, making calculations much simpler for many problems.

Electric Potential Due to a Point Charge

The electric potential at a distance r from a point charge Q is:

V = kQ / r

The potential is taken as zero at infinity. For a positive charge, the potential is positive and decreases with distance. For a negative charge, the potential is negative and increases (becomes less negative) with distance. For multiple point charges: V = Σ kqi / ri.

  • Potential difference: ΔV = VB − VA = WAB/q, where WAB is the work done by the electric field in moving charge q from A to B.
  • Work done by external agent: Wext = q(VB − VA) = qΔV. The external agent does positive work to move a positive charge from lower to higher potential.
  • Relation between E and V: E = −dV/dr (in one dimension). In three dimensions, E = −∇V. The electric field points in the direction of the steepest decrease in potential.
  • Electron-volt (eV): 1 eV = 1.6 × 10−19 J. It is the energy gained by an electron when accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V. This is a convenient unit in atomic and nuclear physics.
Example 17: Electric Potential
Two charges of +2 μC and −2 μC are placed at points A(0,0) and B(0,3 m) respectively. Find the electric potential at point C(4 m, 0).
Solution: Distance from A to C: r1 = 4 m
Distance from B to C: r2 = √(42 + 32) = 5 m
V = kq1/r1 + kq2/r2 = 9×109[2×10−6/4 + (−2×10−6)/5]
V = 9×109[0.5×10−6 − 0.4×10−6] = 9×109 × 0.1×10−6 = 900 V
Example 18: Work Done in Moving a Charge
How much work is done by an external agent in moving a charge of +5 μC from a point at 100 V to a point at 500 V?
Solution: Wext = q(VB − VA) = 5 × 10−6 × (500 − 100) = 5 × 10−6 × 400 = 2 × 10−3 J = 2 mJ

Potential due to Various Charge Configurations

  • Electric dipole: V = kp cosθ / r2 (at a point with polar coordinates r, θ). On the axial line (θ = 0): V = kp/r2. On the equatorial line (θ = 90°): V = 0.

Equipotential Surfaces

An equipotential surface is a surface over which the electric potential is constant. Key properties for NEET:

  • No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface (since ΔV = 0).
  • The electric field is always perpendicular (normal) to equipotential surfaces.
  • Equipotential surfaces are closer together where the electric field is stronger (since E = −dV/dr, a larger E means V changes faster with distance).
  • For a point charge, equipotential surfaces are concentric spheres centred on the charge.
  • For a uniform field, equipotential surfaces are parallel planes perpendicular to the field.
  • For a dipole, equipotential surfaces are complex but always perpendicular to field lines.
  • Equipotential surfaces never cross each other.
  • The surface of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is always an equipotential surface.
NEET Tip: Equipotential Surfaces
Equipotential surfaces are to electric fields what contour lines are to gravitational potential on a map. Just as water flows perpendicular to contour lines, charges move perpendicular to equipotential surfaces. The closer the equipotential lines, the stronger the field.
Memory Aid: E ↔ V
E = −dV/dr. The electric field is the negative gradient of potential. Think of a hill: gravitational potential increases with height, but the force (field) points downhill. Similarly, E points from high electric potential to low electric potential. In a uniform field, E = V/d where d is the separation between equipotential surfaces.

Electric Potential Energy

The electric potential energy of a system of charges is the work done to assemble the charges from infinity to their positions. For two point charges:

U = k q1q2 / r

For three charges: U = k(q1q2/r12 + q2q3/r23 + q3q1/r31). The potential energy is positive for like charges and negative for unlike charges. A system with negative potential energy is stable (bound state).

Capacitors

A capacitor is a device that stores electric charge and electrical energy. It consists of two conductors separated by an insulating medium (dielectric). The capacitance C is defined as the ratio of charge Q on one plate to the potential difference V across the plates: C = Q/V. The SI unit is farad (F). One farad is a very large unit; practical capacitors range from picofarads (pF, 10−12 F) to microfarads (μF, 10−6 F).

Parallel Plate Capacitor

The capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor is directly proportional to the plate area A and inversely proportional to the plate separation d:

C = ε0A / d (without dielectric)

C = ε0εrA / d (with dielectric of constant εr)

The electric field between the plates: E = V/d = σ/ε0 (without dielectric). With dielectric, E = V/(εrd) and the induced charge on the dielectric surfaces reduces the net field.

Example 22: Parallel Plate Capacitor Calculation
A parallel plate capacitor has plates of area 0.1 m2 separated by 2 mm of air. Find its capacitance. (ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m)
Solution: C = ε0A/d = 8.85×10−12 × 0.1 / (2×10−3) = 8.85×10−13 / 2×10−3 = 4.425 × 10−10 F = 442.5 pF

Capacitor with Dielectric — Detailed Derivation

When a dielectric slab of thickness t and dielectric constant εr is inserted between the plates of a parallel plate capacitor (plate separation d, area A):

  • The dielectric becomes polarized. Molecules align such that bound charges appear on the dielectric surfaces facing the plates.
  • The induced surface charge density σp = σ(1 − 1/εr), where σ is the free charge density on the plates.
  • The net field inside the dielectric: Ed = E0/εr = σ/(ε0εr).
  • Potential difference: V = E0(d − t) + Ed t = (σ/ε0)(d − t) + (σ/(ε0εr))t
  • After simplification: C = Q/V = ε0A / [d − t(1 − 1/εr)]
  • If t = d (completely filled): C = ε0εrA/d, which matches the standard formula.
Example 23: Capacitor with Partial Dielectric
A parallel plate capacitor has plate area 0.02 m2 and separation 3 mm. A dielectric slab of εr = 4, thickness 2 mm, is inserted between the plates. Find the new capacitance.
Solution: C = ε0A / [d − t(1 − 1/εr)] = 8.85×10−12 × 0.02 / [0.003 − 0.002(1 − 1/4)]
= 1.77×10−13 / [0.003 − 0.002(0.75)] = 1.77×10−13 / [0.003 − 0.0015] = 1.77×10−13 / 0.0015 = 1.18 × 10−10 F = 118 pF

Energy Stored in a Capacitor

The energy stored in a charged capacitor is the work done to charge it. As charge is built up on the plates, work must be done against the repulsive forces. The energy can be expressed in three equivalent forms:

U = ½ QV = ½ CV2 = Q2 / (2C)

Derivation: When a small charge dq is transferred from one plate to the other at potential difference v = q/C, the work done is dW = v dq = (q/C) dq. Total work: W = ∫ (q/C) dq from 0 to Q = Q2/(2C) = ½CV2.

The energy is stored in the electric field between the plates. The energy density (energy per unit volume) in an electric field is:

u = ½ ε0 E2

This is a universal result valid for any electric field configuration, not just capacitors.

Dielectrics

A dielectric is an insulating material placed between the plates of a capacitor. It increases the capacitance by a factor of k = εr (dielectric constant). The dielectric reduces the effective electric field due to polarization of the material.

  • With battery connected (constant V): Inserting a dielectric increases C, so Q = CV increases. The battery supplies additional charge. Energy stored increases (U = ½CV2).
  • With battery disconnected (constant Q): Inserting a dielectric increases C, so V = Q/C decreases. Energy stored decreases (U = Q2/2C). The dielectric is pulled in, doing work on the system.
  • Electric displacement field: D = ε0E + P, where P is the polarization of the dielectric.
  • Polarization: Aligns molecules in the dielectric, creating bound surface charges that partially cancel the external field.

3. Current Electricity

Current electricity deals with the flow of electric charge through conductors. The electric current is defined as the rate of flow of charge through a cross-section: I = dQ/dt. For steady current, I = Q/t. The SI unit is ampere (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s. By convention, the direction of current is taken as the direction of flow of positive charges (opposite to electron flow in metals).

Drift Velocity and Mobility

In a conductor, free electrons move randomly at very high speeds (~105 m/s) but their net drift velocity in the absence of an electric field is zero. When an electric field is applied, electrons acquire a small drift velocity vd in the direction opposite to E.

Derivation of drift velocity: Consider an electron of mass m and charge −e in an electric field E. The force on the electron is F = −eE. Acceleration a = F/m = −eE/m. Between collisions, the electron accelerates. If the average time between collisions (relaxation time) is τ, the average drift velocity is:

vd = −(eE/m)τ

The magnitude is |vd| = eEτ/m. The current density is given by:

J = I/A = ne vd

where n is the number density of free electrons and e is the electronic charge. Substituting vd:

J = (ne2τ/m) E

The conductivity σ = ne2τ/m, and resistivity ρ = 1/σ = m/(ne2τ).

The mobility μ is defined as:

μ = |vd| / E = eτ / m

The SI unit of mobility is m2/V·s. Conductivity σ = neμ.

Ohm’s Law & Resistance

Ohm’s law states that at constant temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it:

V ∝ I ⇒ V = IR

The constant of proportionality R is the resistance. The resistance of a conductor depends on its geometry and material:

R = ρL / A

where ρ is the resistivity (Ω·m), L is length (m), and A is cross-sectional area (m2). The reciprocal of resistance is conductance G = 1/R (unit: siemens, S). The reciprocal of resistivity is conductivity σ = 1/ρ (unit: S/m).

Resistivity & Temperature Dependence

The resistivity of conductors increases with temperature. The variation is given by:

ρT = ρ0[1 + α(T − T0)]

RT = R0[1 + α(T − T0)]

where α is the temperature coefficient of resistance (in /°C or /K). For metals, α is positive (R increases with temperature). For semiconductors and insulators, α is negative (R decreases with temperature). For alloys like constantan and manganin, α is very close to zero (their resistance is nearly independent of temperature, making them ideal for precision resistors and standard resistors).

Electrical Energy and Power

When current flows through a resistor, electrical energy is converted into heat. The power dissipated is:

P = VI = I2R = V2/R

The heat generated in time t is: H = I2Rt = VIt = V2t/R (Joule’s law of heating).

  • Commercial unit of energy: 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh) = 3.6 × 106 J. Electricity bills are based on kWh (units).
  • Electric bulb rating: A bulb marked “60 W, 220 V” has resistance R = V2/P = (220)2/60 ≈ 806.7 Ω.
  • Bulbs in series: The bulb with higher resistance (lower wattage) glows brighter because P = I2R and I is same.
  • Bulbs in parallel: The bulb with lower resistance (higher wattage) glows brighter because P = V2/R and V is same.

(b) Energy (J) = P × t = 1100 × 2 × 3600 = 7.92 × 106 J

Circuit Analysis

Circuit analysis involves determining currents, voltages, and power in electrical networks. The two fundamental laws are Ohm’s law (V = IR) and Kirchhoff’s laws.

  • For two resistors in parallel: Req = R1R2/(R1 + R2). This is a very useful formula for NEET.
  • Shortcut for n equal resistors R in parallel: Req = R/n.

Kirchhoff’s Laws

Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) / Junction Rule: The algebraic sum of all currents meeting at a junction is zero. Equivalently, the sum of currents entering equals the sum of currents leaving. This follows from the conservation of charge.

Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL) / Loop Rule: The algebraic sum of all potential differences (voltage drops and rises) around any closed loop is zero. This follows from the conservation of energy.

Sign convention for KVL: When traversing a loop:

  • If we go from negative to positive terminal of a cell (through the cell), the EMF is taken as positive (+ε).
  • If we go from positive to negative terminal (through the cell), the EMF is negative (−ε).
  • If we traverse a resistor in the direction of current, the voltage drop is −IR.
  • If we traverse a resistor opposite to current, the voltage drop is +IR.
Example 41: KVL Application
A circuit has a 12 V battery, a 1 Ω internal resistance, and two external resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω in series. Find the current in the circuit using KVL.
Solution: Apply KVL (clockwise loop):
ε − Ir − IR1 − IR2 = 0
12 − I(1) − I(4) − I(6) = 0
12 − 11I = 0 ⇒ I = 12/11 = 1.09 A
Example 42: KCL Application
At a junction in a circuit, currents I1 = 3 A and I2 = 2 A enter, while I3 = 1 A and I4 leave. Find I4.

Example 43: Complex Circuit with Multiple Loops
A circuit has two loops: Loop 1 has a 10 V battery and two resistors 2 Ω and 3 Ω. Loop 2 shares the 3 Ω resistor and has a 5 V battery and 4 Ω resistor. Find the currents in all branches using KVL.
Solution: Let I1 in Loop 1, I2 in Loop 2. Current through shared 3 Ω = I1 − I2 (downward).

Loop 2 (clockwise): 5 − 4I2 + 3(I1 − I2) = 0 ⇒ 5 − 7I2 + 3I1 = 0 ⇒ 3I1 − 7I2 = −5
Solving: I1 = 2.5 A, I2 = 1.5 A. Current through 3 Ω = 2.5 − 1.5 = 1.0 A (downward).

Wheatstone Bridge

The Wheatstone bridge is a network of four resistors P, Q, R, S arranged in a diamond shape. A galvanometer is connected between the junctions B and D. The bridge is balanced when:

P / Q = R / S

At balance, no current flows through the galvanometer, and the voltage at B equals the voltage at D. The Wheatstone bridge is used to measure unknown resistance precisely. The unknown resistance X is placed in one arm, and known resistances are adjusted until balance is achieved.

Meter Bridge

The meter bridge is a practical form of Wheatstone bridge. It uses a 1 m long uniform resistance wire (of constantan or manganin) stretched along a meter scale. The unknown resistance X is in the left gap, and a known resistance R (from a resistance box) is in the right gap. A jockey is moved along the wire to find the null point. At balance:

X / R = l1 / l2 ⇒ X = R · (l1 / l2)

where l1 is the length from left end to the balance point, and l2 = 100 − l1 (in cm). The end resistances should be minimized for accurate results. A known resistance is sometimes used to find the resistance per unit length of the wire.

Example 44: Meter Bridge
In a meter bridge experiment, the balance point is at 40 cm from the left end when a known resistance of 5 Ω is in the right gap. Find the unknown resistance in the left gap.
Solution: X/R = l1/l2 where l1 = 40 cm, l2 = 100 − 40 = 60 cm
X/5 = 40/60 ⇒ X = 5 × 40/60 = 5 × 2/3 = 3.33 Ω
Example 45: Meter Bridge with Error
In a meter bridge experiment, the null point is at 45 cm. If the known resistance is 10 Ω, find the unknown resistance. If the end correction is 1 cm at each end, find the corrected value.
Solution: Without correction: X = 10 × 45/55 = 10 × 0.818 = 8.18 Ω
With end correction (1 cm each): Effective l1 = 45 + 1 = 46 cm, l2 = 55 + 1 = 56 cm
Xcorrected = 10 × 46/56 = 10 × 0.821 = 8.21 Ω

Potentiometer

A potentiometer is a device used to measure EMF of a cell or potential difference without drawing any current from the source. It consists of a long uniform wire of high resistance connected to a standard cell. The principle: the potential drop across any length of a uniform wire is directly proportional to its length (V ∝ L) when a constant current flows through it.

  • Measuring EMF of a cell: The cell’s EMF is balanced against the potential drop along the wire: ε = kL, where k is the potential gradient (V/L).
  • Comparing EMFs of two cells: ε1/ε2 = L1/L2.
  • Measuring internal resistance of a cell: If L1 is the balancing length for EMF (open circuit) and L2 is the balancing length when connected to external resistance R, then r = R(L1/L2 − 1).
  • Advantage: The potentiometer draws no current from the cell being measured, giving the true EMF.
Example 46: Potentiometer
The balancing length for a cell is 60 cm when connected to a potentiometer. When a 10 Ω resistor is connected across the cell terminals, the balancing length becomes 50 cm. Find the internal resistance of the cell.
Solution: r = R(L1/L2 − 1) = 10(60/50 − 1) = 10(1.2 − 1) = 10 × 0.2 = 2 Ω
Example 47: Comparing EMFs
In a potentiometer experiment, the balancing lengths for two cells are 80 cm and 64 cm respectively. Find the ratio of their EMFs.
Solution: ε1/ε2 = L1/L2 = 80/64 = 5/4 = 1.25

4. Electrical Measuring Instruments

Galvanometer

A galvanometer is a sensitive instrument used to detect and measure small electric currents. It works on the principle that a current-carrying coil placed in a magnetic field experiences a torque. The deflection is proportional to the current passing through it. A typical moving-coil galvanometer has a full-scale deflection current Ig (typically 10 μA to 10 mA) and a coil resistance G (typically 10 Ω to 1000 Ω).

Conversion of Galvanometer to Ammeter

To convert a galvanometer into an ammeter, a small resistance called a shunt (S) is connected in parallel with the galvanometer coil. The shunt bypasses most of the current, allowing only Ig to pass through the galvanometer for full-scale deflection when the total current is I.

S = IgG / (I − Ig)

The effective resistance of the ammeter is RA = GS/(G+S), which is very small (ideal ammeter has zero resistance). The ammeter is always connected in series with the circuit.

Conversion of Galvanometer to Voltmeter

To convert a galvanometer into a voltmeter, a high resistance called a multiplier (R) is connected in series with the galvanometer. The multiplier drops most of the voltage, allowing full-scale deflection when the voltage across the combination is V.

R = V/Ig − G

The effective resistance of the voltmeter is RV = G + R, which is very high (ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance). The voltmeter is always connected in parallel with the component being measured.

Example 48: Galvanometer to Ammeter
A galvanometer of resistance 100 Ω gives full-scale deflection for a current of 1 mA. Convert it to an ammeter of range 0–1 A.
Solution: Shunt: S = IgG / (I − Ig) = (10−3 × 100) / (1 − 0.001) = 0.1/0.999 = 0.1001 Ω
Example 49: Galvanometer to Voltmeter
A galvanometer of resistance 100 Ω and full-scale current 1 mA is to be converted to a voltmeter of range 0–10 V. Find the series resistance.
Solution: R = V/Ig − G = 10/10−3 − 100 = 10000 − 100 = 9900 Ω
Example 50: Voltmeter Range Extension
A voltmeter of range 0–3 V has resistance 3000 Ω. How will you extend its range to 30 V?
Solution: Ig = V/G = 3/3000 = 0.001 A = 1 mA
To measure 30 V: Rtotal = V/Ig = 30/0.001 = 30000 Ω
Extra series resistance needed = 30000 − 3000 = 27000 Ω

Multirange Instruments

Multirange ammeters and voltmeters use a selector switch to choose different shunt or multiplier resistors, allowing different measurement ranges. For an ammeter with ranges I1, I2, I3, separate shunt resistors are calculated for each range and connected via a switch. For a voltmeter with ranges V1, V2, V3, additional series resistors are added for each range.

Sensitivity of a Galvanometer

The current sensitivity of a galvanometer is defined as the deflection per unit current: SI = θ/I (in divisions per A or rad/A). The voltage sensitivity is the deflection per unit voltage: SV = θ/V = SI/G. A galvanometer with higher sensitivity can detect smaller currents. Sensitivity is increased by using a stronger magnet, increasing the number of turns, or increasing the area of the coil.

5. Heating & Chemical Effects of Current

Joule’s Law of Heating

When an electric current flows through a conductor, heat is produced due to the resistance offered by the conductor. The amount of heat produced is directly proportional to:

  • The square of the current (H ∝ I2)
  • The resistance of the conductor (H ∝ R)
  • The time for which the current flows (H ∝ t)

H = I2Rt (Joule’s law)

In SI units, H is in joules. Using V = IR, alternative forms: H = VIt = V2t/R. In practical contexts, heat is often expressed in calories: 1 cal = 4.186 J.

  • Electric fuse: A thin wire of low melting point (usually tin-lead alloy) that melts when current exceeds a safe value, protecting the circuit from overloading.
  • Electric heater / iron / kettle: Use high-resistance heating elements made of nichrome (an alloy of Ni, Cr, Fe) that dissipate power as heat.
  • Incandescent bulb: The filament (tungsten, melting point ~3400°C) glows white-hot. About 90% of the energy is wasted as heat; only ~10% is converted to light.
Example 51: Joule Heating
A 50 Ω resistor carries a current of 2 A for 5 minutes. Find (a) the heat produced in joules, and (b) the power dissipated.
Solution:
(a) H = I2Rt = (2)2 × 50 × (5 × 60) = 4 × 50 × 300 = 60,000 J
(b) P = I2R = 4 × 50 = 200 W
Example 52: Fuse Wire
A fuse wire of radius 0.2 mm melts at a current of 5 A. What should be the radius of a fuse wire of the same material that melts at 10 A?
Solution: For fuse wire, heat produced per second = I2R = I2ρL/(πr2). Heat lost per second is proportional to surface area ∝ r (for unit length). At melting point, heat produced = heat lost. So I2/r2 ∝ r ⇒ I2 ∝ r3. Hence I ∝ r3/2. r2/r1 = (I2/I1)2/3 = (10/5)2/3 = 22/3 = 1.587. r2 = 0.2 × 1.587 = 0.317 mm.

Thermoelectric Effect

The thermoelectric effect is the direct conversion of temperature differences to electric voltage and vice versa. There are three related phenomena:

Seebeck Effect

When two dissimilar metals (or semiconductors) are joined at two junctions kept at different temperatures, an EMF is generated in the circuit. This is called the Seebeck effect. The EMF produced is proportional to the temperature difference between the junctions. The circuit is called a thermocouple.

  • Thermoelectric series: Metals arranged in order such that when any two are joined, current flows from the earlier metal to the later at the hot junction.
  • Neutral temperature (Tn): The temperature at which the thermoelectric EMF is maximum.
  • Inversion temperature (Ti): The temperature at which the EMF reverses direction; Ti = 2Tn − T0.
  • Applications: Thermocouple thermometers (measuring high temperatures, furnace temperatures), thermopile (sensitive heat detector).

Peltier Effect

The reverse of the Seebeck effect. When a current flows through a junction of two dissimilar metals, heat is either absorbed or evolved at the junction, depending on the direction of current. If the current direction at the junction is from metal A to metal B, the junction may cool (if A precedes B in the thermoelectric series) or heat (if B precedes A).

  • Peltier coefficient (π): Heat absorbed/released per unit charge at the junction. π = T · dε/dT, where T is the temperature in Kelvin.
  • Applications: Thermoelectric cooling (Peltier coolers), no moving parts, used in portable refrigerators and electronic cooling.

Thomson Effect

When a current flows through a conductor with a temperature gradient along its length, heat is absorbed or evolved throughout the conductor (not just at junctions). This is called the Thomson effect.

Electrolysis — Faraday’s Laws

Electrolysis is the process of chemical decomposition of an electrolyte (a substance that conducts electricity when molten or in solution) by passing an electric current through it. The electrodes are called anode (positive) and cathode (negative). Positive ions (cations) move to the cathode and negative ions (anions) move to the anode.

Faraday’s First Law: The mass of a substance liberated (or deposited) at an electrode during electrolysis is directly proportional to the quantity of electric charge passed through the electrolyte.

m ∝ Q ⇒ m = ZQ = ZIt

where m is the mass deposited (kg), Q is the charge (C), I is current (A), t is time (s), and Z is the electrochemical equivalent (kg/C) of the substance.

Faraday’s Second Law: When the same quantity of charge is passed through different electrolytes, the masses of substances liberated are proportional to their chemical equivalents (equivalent weights).

m1 / m2 = E1 / E2

The equivalent weight E = M/n, where M is the molar mass (atomic weight) and n is the valency (number of electrons transferred per ion).

Faraday’s constant (F): The charge required to liberate one gram-equivalent of a substance. F = 96485 C/mol ≈ 96500 C/mol. F = NAe = 6.022 × 1023 × 1.602 × 10−19 = 96485 C/mol.

m = (E / F) × Q = (EIt) / F

Example 53: Faraday’s First Law
A current of 2 A is passed through a copper sulphate solution for 30 minutes. How much copper is deposited? (Z for copper = 3.29 × 10−7 kg/C)
Solution: m = ZIt = 3.29×10−7 × 2 × (30 × 60) = 3.29×10−7 × 3600 = 1.184 × 10−3 kg = 1.184 g
Example 54: Faraday’s Second Law
Same quantity of charge is passed through AgNO3 and CuSO4 solutions. If 10.8 g of silver is deposited (M = 108 g/mol, valency = 1), find the mass of copper deposited (M = 63.5 g/mol, valency = 2).
Solution: EAg = 108/1 = 108, ECu = 63.5/2 = 31.75
mCu/mAg = ECu/EAg ⇒ mCu/10.8 = 31.75/108
mCu = 10.8 × 31.75/108 = 10.8 × 0.294 = 3.175 g

Cells, EMF & Internal Resistance

A cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy. The electromotive force (EMF, ε) of a cell is the potential difference between its terminals when no current is drawn (open circuit). A real cell has internal resistance r. When current I flows, the terminal voltage V is:

V = ε − Ir

When connected to an external load R, the current is:

I = ε / (R + r)

The terminal voltage across the load: V = IR = εR/(R+r).

Maximum Power Transfer Theorem

The power delivered to the load is P = I2R = [ε/(R+r)]2 R. For maximum power:

R = r

The maximum power is Pmax = ε2/(4r). At maximum power, the efficiency is only 50% (half the power is dissipated in the internal resistance).

Example 55: Cell with Internal Resistance
A cell of EMF 12 V and internal resistance 1 Ω is connected to a 5 Ω external resistor. Find (a) the circuit current, (b) terminal voltage, (c) power delivered to the load, and (d) power dissipated internally.
Solution:
(a) I = ε/(R+r) = 12/(5+1) = 2 A
(b) V = IR = 2 × 5 = 10 V [or V = ε − Ir = 12 − 2 = 10 V]
(c) Pload = I2R = 4 × 5 = 20 W
(d) Pinternal = I2r = 4 × 1 = 4 W
Check: Total power = εI = 12 × 2 = 24 W = 20 + 4 ✓
Example 56: Maximum Power
A cell of EMF 6 V has internal resistance 2 Ω. Find the load resistance for maximum power and the maximum power delivered.
Solution: For maximum power, R = r = 2 Ω
I = ε/(R+r) = 6/(2+2) = 1.5 A
Pmax = I2R = (1.5)2 × 2 = 2.25 × 2 = 4.5 W
Alternative: Pmax = ε2/(4r) = 36/(8) = 4.5 W
Example 57: Internal Resistance Measurement
A cell of EMF 2 V gives a current of 0.4 A when connected to a 4 Ω resistor. Find the internal resistance.
Solution: I = ε/(R+r) ⇒ 0.4 = 2/(4+r) ⇒ 0.4(4+r) = 2 ⇒ 1.6 + 0.4r = 2 ⇒ 0.4r = 0.4 ⇒ r = 1 Ω

Common Misconceptions Clarified

Misconception 1: “Electric field inside a conductor is always zero.” Correction: It is zero only in electrostatic equilibrium. When current flows, there is a non-zero electric field inside the conductor (E = V/L).

Misconception 2: “The electric field of an infinite sheet is σ/(2ε0) and depends on distance.” Correction: The field is constant and independent of distance from the sheet.

Misconception 3: “Capacitance depends on charge and voltage.” Correction: C = Q/V is a definition, not a dependence. Capacitance depends only on geometry and dielectric.

Misconception 4: “Battery provides constant current.” Correction: A battery provides nearly constant EMF; the current depends on the load resistance (I = ε/R).

Misconception 5: “Ammeter is connected in parallel and voltmeter in series.” Correction: Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel. Connecting them incorrectly can damage the instruments.

Misconception 6: “Resistance of a wire depends on voltage and current.” Correction: Resistance is a property of the wire (material, length, area, temperature), not of voltage or current.

Misconception 7: “The direction of current is the direction of electron flow.” Correction: Conventional current is opposite to electron flow (electrons flow from − to +, conventional current from + to −).

Meter bridge: (1) End resistances should be minimized by using thick copper strips. (2) The jockey should be lightly touched to avoid damaging the wire. (3) The null point should be near the centre of the wire (between 30–70 cm) for maximum accuracy. (4) Several readings should be taken by interchanging X and R.

Potentiometer: (1) The primary cell must have a higher EMF than the cell being measured. (2) The wire should have uniform cross-section. (3) The current through the wire should be constant. (4) The balancing length should be as long as possible to reduce percentage error.

Galvanometer conversion: (1) The shunt resistance should be calculated accurately using the formula. (2) The ammeter should be connected in series; the voltmeter in parallel. (3) Never connect a voltmeter in series or an ammeter in parallel, as this can damage the instruments.

Common NEET Question Types with Solutions

Type 1: Force Between Multiple Charges (Equilibrium Problems)

A classic NEET problem involves finding where to place a third charge so that it experiences zero net force, or finding the nature of equilibrium of a charge in a system. The key is to equate forces from individual charges and solve for distance. Remember: for like charges, the null point lies between them; for unlike charges, it lies outside on the side of the smaller charge.

Example 80: Equilibrium of Three Charges
Three charges +q, +4q, and +Q are placed along a line. The first two are 10 cm apart. Where should +Q be placed so that it is in equilibrium? Find the magnitude and sign of Q for the system to be in equilibrium.
Solution: For Q to be in equilibrium, forces from +q and +4q must be equal and opposite. Let Q be at distance x from +q. Then kqQ/x2 = k(4q)Q/(10-x)2. Cancelling kQ: 1/x2 = 4/(10-x)2. Taking square roots: 1/x = 2/(10-x). So 10-x = 2x, x = 10/3 = 3.33 cm from +q. For the system to be in equilibrium, each charge must experience zero net force. This requires Q to be negative (opposite sign) to provide attraction. The magnitude can be found by considering equilibrium of +q: force from +4q cancelled by force from Q.

Type 2: Electric Field Due to Continuous Distributions

NEET frequently asks about the electric field at the centre or axis of rings, discs, or arcs. The approach is to integrate contributions from infinitesimal elements, using symmetry to simplify. For arcs and segments, the angle subtended at the centre determines the field.

Example 81: Field at Centre of a Charged Arc
A thin wire of length L is bent into an arc of radius R subtending an angle theta at the centre. The wire has total charge Q uniformly distributed. Find the electric field at the centre.
Solution: Linear charge density lambda = Q/L = Q/(R*theta). Due to symmetry, only the component along the bisector survives. dE = k dq/R2. The component along the bisector is dE cos(phi) where phi is the angle from the bisector. Integrating: E = (2k lambda/R) sin(theta/2) = (2kQ sin(theta/2))/(R2*theta). For a semicircle (theta = pi): E = 2kQ/(pi*R2). For a full circle: E = 0 (by symmetry).

Type 3: Capacitor Networks with Switches

Problems involving opening and closing switches in capacitor networks test understanding of charge conservation and voltage distribution. The approach: when switches change configuration, total charge is conserved in isolated sections.

Example 82: Switch in Capacitor Circuit
A 2 mu-F capacitor charged to 10 V is connected to an uncharged 3 mu-F capacitor through a switch. Find the final voltage and energy loss when the switch is closed.
Solution: Initial charge Q = 2x10 = 20 mu-C. After closing, total C = 2+3 = 5 mu-F. Final voltage V = Q/C = 20/5 = 4 V. Initial energy Ui = 1/2 x 2 x 100 = 100 mu-J. Final energy Uf = 1/2 x 5 x 16 = 40 mu-J. Energy loss = 100 - 40 = 60 mu-J (dissipated as heat).

Type 4: Meter Bridge Error Analysis

NEET problems on meter bridges often involve end corrections, resistance of connecting wires, or finding the resistance per unit length.

Example 83: Meter Bridge with Two Unknowns
In a meter bridge, the balance point is at 30 cm from the left when R = 5 ohm is in the right gap. When R is replaced by 10 ohm, the balance point shifts to 50 cm. Find the two unknown resistances that could be in the left gap.
Solution: Let X be unknown. First case: X/5 = 30/70 so X = 5 x 30/70 = 2.14 ohm. Second case: X/10 = 50/50 = 1 so X = 10 ohm. The two different X values mean there is an end resistance or the wire is not uniform. If both readings are for the same X, there must be an end correction. Let alpha be end correction. Then X/5 = (30+alpha)/(70+alpha) and X/10 = (50+alpha)/(50+alpha) = 1 so X = 10. Substituting: 10/5 = (30+alpha)/(70+alpha), so 2(70+alpha) = 30+alpha, 140+2alpha = 30+alpha, alpha = -110 cm. This negative value indicates the assumption is wrong. Actually the two readings correspond to different unknown resistors.

Example 84: Symmetry in Resistor Networks
Twelve identical resistors each of resistance R form the edges of a cube. Find the equivalent resistance between (a) adjacent corners, (b) opposite corners, and (c) face diagonal corners.
Solution:
(a) Adjacent corners: 7R/12.
(b) Opposite corners (body diagonal): 5R/6.
(c) Face diagonal: 3R/4.
These results are derived using symmetry. The current distribution is symmetric, so equipotential points can be identified and connected.

Example 85: Multirange Voltmeter Design
A galvanometer of resistance 50 ohm has full-scale deflection for 2 mA. Design a multirange voltmeter with ranges 5 V, 25 V, and 50 V.
Solution: For voltmeter: R = V/Ig - G = V/0.002 - 50.
For 5 V: R1 = 5/0.002 - 50 = 2500 - 50 = 2450 ohm.
For 25 V: R2 = 25/0.002 - 50 = 12500 - 50 = 12450 ohm (total series R = 12450, so additional R = 12450-2450 = 10000 ohm).

Type 7: Heating and Power Distribution

Example 86: Series-Parallel Bulb Problem
Two bulbs rated 100 W, 220 V and 60 W, 220 V are connected (a) in series, and (b) in parallel across a 220 V supply. In each case, find which bulb glows brighter.
Solution: R1 = V2/P1 = 48400/100 = 484 ohm. R2 = 48400/60 = 806.7 ohm.
(a) Series: I same, P = I2R, so P proportional to R. The 60 W bulb (higher R) glows brighter.
(b) Parallel: V same, P = V2/R, so P inversely proportional to R. The 100 W bulb (lower R) glows brighter.
Check: In series, total power = V2/(R1+R2) = 48400/1290.7 = 37.5 W (less than either rating).

Additional Important Derivations for NEET

Derivation of Energy Stored in a Capacitor

Consider a capacitor of capacitance C being charged by a battery. At an intermediate stage, let the charge on the capacitor be q and the potential difference across it be v = q/C. When an additional small charge dq is transferred from the negative plate to the positive plate, the work done against the electric field is dW = v dq = (q/C) dq. The total work done to charge the capacitor from q = 0 to q = Q is:

W = ∫ (q/C) dq = [q2/(2C)]0Q = Q2/(2C) = ½ CV2 = ½ QV

This work is stored as electrical potential energy in the capacitor. The energy is stored in the electric field between the plates. For a parallel plate capacitor, the volume of the field is Ad, and the energy density is u = U/Ad = (1/2*CV2)/(Ad) = 1/2*(epsilon0A/d)V2/(Ad) = 1/2*epsilon0(V/d)2 = 1/2*epsilon0 E2.

Derivation of Drift Velocity

In a metallic conductor with free electron density n, each electron experiences a force F = −eE in an applied electric field E. The acceleration is a = F/m = −eE/m. Between successive collisions, the electron accelerates for an average time tau (relaxation time). The velocity gained just before a collision is vmax = a*tau = eE*tau/m (in the direction opposite to E). The average drift velocity is half of this:

vd = one-half vmax = (eE*tau)/(2m)

However, more rigorous statistical mechanics gives vd = eE*tau/m (without the factor 1/2), where tau is the mean free time. The current density is J = I/A = ne vd = (ne2tau/m)E = sigma*E, where conductivity sigma = ne2tau/m and resistivity rho = 1/sigma = m/(ne2tau).

Derivation of Resistance and Resistivity Relation

Consider a conductor of length L and uniform cross-sectional area A. The electric field E = V/L. The current I = JA = sigma*E*A = sigma*(V/L)A = (sigma*A/L)V. Comparing with V = IR, we get R = L/(sigma*A) = rho*L/A, where rho = 1/sigma is the resistivity. This shows that resistance is proportional to length and inversely proportional to area, with resistivity as the material-dependent constant.

Derivation of the Potentiometer Equation

Consider a uniform wire of length L, resistance Rw, carrying current I from a driver cell. The potential gradient k = V/L = IRw/L. If the wire has uniform cross-section A and resistivity rho, then Rw = rho*L/A, so k = I*rho*L/(AL) = I*rho/A = J*rho = E, the electric field inside the wire. Thus the potential gradient equals the electric field in the wire.

Derivation of the Meter Bridge Formula

The meter bridge is a Wheatstone bridge where resistance P = lambda*l1 (left segment of wire), Q = lambda*l2 (right segment), where lambda is the resistance per unit length of the wire. Unknown resistance X is in the left gap and known resistance R is in the right gap. At balance, X/R = P/Q = lambda*l1/(lambda*l2) = l1/l2, giving X = R(l1/l2).

Derivation of Shunt Resistance for Ammeter

A galvanometer (resistance G, full-scale current Ig) is to measure current up to I. The shunt S is connected in parallel so that only Ig flows through the galvanometer and the rest (I − Ig) flows through the shunt. The voltage across the parallel combination is the same: IgG = (I − Ig)S. Therefore, S = IgG/(I − Ig). The effective resistance of the ammeter is RA = GS/(G+S) which is very small (ideal ammeter has zero resistance).

Derivation of Multiplier Resistance for Voltmeter

A galvanometer (resistance G, full-scale current Ig) is to measure voltage up to V. A series resistance R is added to drop the excess voltage. The total voltage across the combination is V = Ig(G + R). Therefore, R = V/Ig − G. The effective resistance of the voltmeter is RV = G + R = V/Ig, which is very high for an ideal voltmeter.

If the Peltier coefficient for a Cu-Fe junction at 0 deg C is 2.6 x 10-3 V, find the heat absorbed or evolved when a current of 5 A flows for 10 seconds.

Example 79: Symmetry in Capacitor Networks
Find the equivalent capacitance between points A and B of an infinite ladder of capacitors each of value C.
Solution: Let Ceq be the equivalent capacitance. The first section has C in series with (C in parallel with Ceq). Ceq = C + (C x Ceq)/(C + Ceq). Multiply: Ceq(C+Ceq) = C(C+Ceq) + C*Ceq. So C*Ceq + Ceq2 = C2 + C*Ceq + C*Ceq. So Ceq2 - C*Ceq - C2 = 0. Solving: Ceq = C(1+sqrt5)/2 = C x 1.618 (golden ratio).

Additional Solved Problems for NEET Practice

Example 61: Electric Field of a Charged Semicircular Ring
A semicircular ring of radius R has total charge Q uniformly distributed. Find the electric field at its centre.
Solution: Linear charge density λ = Q/(πR). Consider an element dθ at angle θ. dq = λR dθ. The field dE = k dq/R2. By symmetry, the vertical components cancel. The horizontal component dEx = dE cosθ = kλ cosθ dθ/R. Integrate from θ = −π/2 to +π/2: E = kλ/R × ∫ cosθ dθ = kλ/R × 2 = 2kλ/R = 2kQ/(πR2).

A ring of radius 10 cm has a charge of 2 μC. Find the potential at a point on the axis 5 cm from the centre.

Example 63: Capacitor Network with Switch
Two capacitors C1 = 2 μF and C2 = 4 μF are initially charged to 6 V and 3 V respectively with opposite polarities. They are then connected in parallel (positive plate to positive plate). Find the final common voltage and the charge on each.
Solution: Initial charges: Q1 = 2×6 = 12 μC, Q2 = 4×3 = 12 μC. Since polarities are same, total Q = 12+12 = 24 μC. Total C = 2+4 = 6 μF. Common voltage V = Q/C = 24/6 = 4 V. Q1′ = 2×4 = 8 μC, Q2′ = 4×4 = 16 μC.

A circuit has three resistors 2 Ω, 3 Ω, and 5 Ω forming a delta network with batteries 10 V and 6 V in two branches. Find current through the 3 Ω resistor using loop analysis.

▶ Frequently Made Mistakes

❌ Coulomb's Law sign: F is a vector. Positive F = repulsion (same charges), negative = attraction (opposite). Use vector form for multiple charges.

❌ E vs V: E is vector (units N/C or V/m), V is scalar (J/C or volts). Don't add E with V in equations.

❌ Gauss's Law misuse: Only useful for symmetric charge distributions (spherical, cylindrical, planar). For nonsymmetric distributions, use integration.

❌ Capacitor with dielectric: Battery connected → V constant, Q changes. Battery disconnected → Q constant, V changes.

❌ Kirchhoff sign convention: Voltage drop = −IR when traversing resistor in current direction. EMF positive when going from − to + terminal.

❌ Wheatstone bridge condition: P/Q = R/S only when balanced. Don't apply when galvanometer shows deflection.

❌ Color code of resistors: Black(0), Brown(1), Red(2), Orange(3), Yellow(4), Green(5), Blue(6), Violet(7), Grey(8), White(9). Mnemonic: BB ROY of Great Britain has Very Good Wife.
