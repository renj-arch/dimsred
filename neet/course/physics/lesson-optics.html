1. Ray Optics & Optical Instruments

Reflection

Reflection is the phenomenon where light bounces off a surface. The laws of reflection state: (i) the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane, and (ii) the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (θi = θr). Reflection can be specular (smooth surface) or diffuse (rough surface). For NEET, specular reflection from plane and spherical surfaces is most relevant.

Mirrors & Mirror Formula

Spherical mirrors are classified as concave (converging) and convex (diverging). The mirror formula relates object distance (u), image distance (v), and focal length (f):

1/f = 1/v + 1/u

Magnification: m = -v/u. Sign convention is critical � NEET uses the Cartesian sign convention.

Example 1 � Mirror Formula
An object is placed 15 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find the position and nature of the image.

a) 30 cm in front, real   b) 30 cm behind, virtual   c) 6 cm in front, real   d) 6 cm behind, virtual
Solution: Using mirror formula 1/f = 1/v + 1/u. Here u = -15 cm, f = -10 cm (concave). 1/v = 1/f - 1/u = -1/10 - (-1/15) = -1/10 + 1/15 = (-3 + 2)/30 = -1/30. So v = -30 cm. Since v is negative, the image is real and located 30 cm in front of the mirror. Magnification m = -v/u = -(-30)/(-15) = -2 (inverted and magnified). Hence option (a) is correct.
Tip 1 � Sign Convention Shortcut
In mirror formula problems, always take u as negative. For concave mirrors, f is negative; for convex mirrors, f is positive. If v comes out negative, the image is real (in front). If v is positive, it is virtual (behind). For lenses, the same logic applies: converging lenses have positive f, diverging lenses have negative f.

Refraction

Refraction is the bending of light when it travels from one transparent medium to another. This occurs because the speed of light changes between media. The absolute refractive index of a medium is n = c/v, where c is the speed of light in vacuum and v is the speed in the medium.

Snell's Law

Snell's law gives the relationship between angles of incidence and refraction: n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2. When light travels from a rarer to a denser medium, it bends toward the normal. From denser to rarer, it bends away from the normal.

Example 2 � Snell's Law
Light travels from air (n = 1) to water (n = 4/3). If the angle of incidence is 45°, find the angle of refraction.

a) 32.1°   b) 28.6°   c) 36.8°   d) 41.2°
Solution: Using Snell's law: nair sin θi = nwater sin θr. 1 × sin 45° = (4/3) × sin θr. sin θr = (3/4) × 0.7071 = 0.5303. θr = sin-1(0.5303) = 32.1°. Hence option (a) is correct.
Tip 2 � Snell's Law Memory Aid
Remember: sin i / sin r = n2 / n1 = v1 / v2 = λ1 / λ2. When light enters a denser medium, it slows down, wavelength decreases, and the ray bends toward the normal. For NEET numericals, always check which medium is denser first.

Total Internal Reflection

Total internal reflection (TIR) occurs when light travels from a denser to a rarer medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle. The critical angle is given by: sin C = n2/n1 where n1 > n2. Applications of TIR include optical fibres, mirages, and brilliance of diamonds.

Table 4: Critical Angles for Common Interfaces

Tip 3 � TIR Condition Check
For TIR, three conditions must be simultaneously satisfied: (i) light must travel from denser to rarer medium, (ii) angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle, and (iii) the surface must be smooth. In numericals, first compute C using sin C = nr/nd, then compare with the given incidence angle.
Example 3 � Total Internal Reflection
A ray of light travelling in a glass slab (n = 1.5) strikes the glass-air interface at 50°. Will it undergo TIR? What is the critical angle?

a) C = 41.8°, TIR occurs   b) C = 48.6°, TIR occurs   c) C = 41.8°, no TIR   d) C = 35.2°, TIR occurs
Solution: sin C = n2/n1 = 1/1.5 = 0.6667. So C = sin-1(0.6667) = 41.8°. Since the incidence angle (50°) exceeds C (41.8°) and light travels from glass (denser) to air (rarer), TIR will occur. Hence option (a) is correct.

Lenses

Lenses are transparent optical devices that refract light to form images. Convex lenses (converging) are thicker at the centre; concave lenses (diverging) are thinner at the centre. The lens formula is: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u.

Lens Maker's Formula

The lens maker's formula relates focal length to the refractive index and radii of curvature: 1/f = (n - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2). Here, n is the refractive index of the lens relative to the surrounding medium, and R1, R2 are the radii of curvature (with sign).

Tip 4 � Lens Maker's Sign Rule
For lens maker's formula, use the Cartesian sign convention: R is positive if the centre of curvature lies on the right (outgoing side), and negative if on the left (incident side). For a biconvex lens in air, R1 is positive and R2 is negative, so (1/R1 - 1/R2) becomes 1/R1 + 1/|R2|.
Example 4 � Lens Maker's Formula
A biconvex lens has radii of curvature 20 cm and 30 cm. If the refractive index of the glass is 1.5, find its focal length in air.

a) 24 cm   b) 30 cm   c) 36 cm   d) 40 cm
Solution: Using lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2). For biconvex, R1 = +20 cm, R2 = -30 cm. 1/f = (1.5 - 1)(1/20 - 1/(-30)) = 0.5 × (1/20 + 1/30) = 0.5 × (3 + 2)/60 = 0.5 × 5/60 = 2.5/60 = 1/24. So f = 24 cm. Hence option (a) is correct.

Power & Magnification

Power of a lens is defined as P = 1/f (f in metres). Its SI unit is dioptre (D). For a combination of lenses in contact, net power P = P1 + P2 + P3 + .... Linear magnification: m = v/u. For a lens, m is positive for virtual images (erect) and negative for real images (inverted).

Example 5 � Power of Lens Combination
A convex lens of power +4 D and a concave lens of power -2 D are placed in contact. What is the focal length of the combination?

a) 25 cm   b) 50 cm   c) 100 cm   d) 75 cm
Solution: Net power P = P1 + P2 = +4 - 2 = +2 D. Focal length f = 1/P = 1/2 = 0.5 m = 50 cm. The positive sign indicates the combination behaves as a converging lens. Hence option (b) is correct.
Tip 5 � Lens Power Shortcut
When two thin lenses are separated by distance d, the net power is P = P1 + P2 - dP1P2. For contact (d = 0), P = P1 + P2. For NEET, remember that a combination of convex and concave lens can behave as converging, diverging, or a plane glass depending on relative powers.

Prism

A prism deviates light due to refraction at two surfaces. For a thin prism of angle A and refractive index n, the deviation: δ = (n - 1)A. For a prism at minimum deviation, the ray passes symmetrically through the prism. The refractive index is: n = sin[(A + δm)/2] / sin(A/2) where δm is the minimum deviation angle.

Tip 6 � Prism Formula Trick
At minimum deviation: i = e, r1 = r2 = A/2. For a thin prism (A ≤ 10°), δ = (n - 1)A directly. The deviation is independent of the angle of incidence for thin prisms. In dispersion, δ varies with colour � red deviates least, violet deviates most.
Example 6 � Prism Deviation
A thin prism of angle 6° and refractive index 1.5 produces a deviation of:

a) 2°   b) 3°   c) 4°   d) 6°
Solution: For a thin prism, δ = (n - 1)A = (1.5 - 1) × 6° = 0.5 × 6° = 3°. Hence option (b) is correct. Chromatic aberration in lenses arises because different colours have different n values, causing different deviations.

Example 7 � Telescope Magnifying Power
An astronomical telescope has an objective of focal length 100 cm and eyepiece of focal length 5 cm. Its magnifying power in normal adjustment is:

a) 10   b) 20   c) 25   d) 50
Solution: Magnifying power of telescope in normal adjustment: M = -fo/fe = -100/5 = -20. The magnitude is 20. The negative sign indicates an inverted image. Hence option (b) is correct.
Tip 7 � Instrument Magnification
For microscopes, both objective and eyepiece should have short focal lengths for high magnification. For telescopes, the objective should have a long focal length and the eyepiece a short focal length. In normal adjustment, the final image is at infinity (least eye strain).

2. Wave Optics

Huygens Principle

Huygens principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets. The new wavefront is the envelope of these secondary wavelets. This principle explains reflection, refraction, and diffraction. Using Huygens principle, the laws of reflection and Snell's law can be derived geometrically. For refraction: sin i / sin r = v1/v2 = n2/n1.

Interference

Interference is the superposition of two or more coherent light waves, producing alternating bright and dark fringes. For constructive interference (bright fringe), path difference must be an integral multiple of wavelength: Δx = nλ. For destructive interference (dark fringe): Δx = (2n - 1)λ/2.

Table 7: Conditions for Interference

Young's Double Slit Experiment (YDSE)

Young's double slit experiment demonstrated the wave nature of light. Two coherent sources from a single source produce an interference pattern on a screen. Fringe width: β = λD/d. Angular fringe width: θ = λ/d. The central fringe is always bright (constructive interference at zero path difference).

Tip 8 � YDSE Shortcut
For YDSE, remember: (i) fringe width β ∝ λ and D; ∝ 1/d. (ii) If immersed in water, β reduces by factor n. (iii) If one slit is covered, the interference pattern is replaced by single-slit diffraction. (iv) For white light, coloured fringes with a white central fringe are observed.
Example 8 � YDSE Fringe Width
In YDSE, slit separation = 0.5 mm, screen distance = 1 m, wavelength = 500 nm. Find the fringe width.

a) 0.5 mm   b) 1.0 mm   c) 1.5 mm   d) 2.0 mm
Solution: β = λD/d = (500 × 10-9 × 1) / (0.5 × 10-3) = 5 × 10-7 / 5 × 10-4 = 1.0 × 10-3 m = 1.0 mm. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 9 � Intensity Ratio in YDSE
In YDSE, the intensities of two slits are in the ratio 4:1. Find the ratio of maximum to minimum intensity in the interference pattern.

a) 9:1   b) 25:9   c) 16:1   d) 4:1
Solution: I1/I2 = 4/1, so amplitude ratio A1/A2 = √(I1/I2) = 2. Imax/Imin = (A1 + A2)2 / (A1 - A2)2 = (2+1)2/(2-1)2 = 9/1. Hence option (a) is correct.

Diffraction

Diffraction is the bending of light around obstacles or apertures. For a single slit of width a, the condition for minima is: a sin θ = nλ. The central maximum is twice as wide as other maxima and contains most of the intensity. Angular width of central maximum: 2θ = 2λ/a.

Tip 9 � Diffraction Key Points
For single-slit diffraction: (i) Width of central maximum = 2λD/a. (ii) For small angles, θ ≈ λ/a. (iii) Diffraction is most pronounced when slit width is comparable to wavelength. (iv) Resolving power of a telescope = D/1.22λ where D is the aperture diameter.
Example 10 � Single Slit Diffraction
In a single-slit diffraction experiment, slit width = 0.2 mm, wavelength = 600 nm. Find the angular width of the central maximum.

a) 3 × 10-3 rad   b) 6 × 10-3 rad   c) 1.2 × 10-3 rad   d) 9 × 10-3 rad
Solution: Angular width of central maximum = 2λ/a = 2 × 600 × 10-9 / 0.2 × 10-3 = 1200 × 10-9 / 2 × 10-4 = 6 × 10-3 rad. Hence option (b) is correct.

Polarisation

Polarisation refers to the orientation of oscillations in a transverse wave. Unpolarised light has oscillations in all directions perpendicular to propagation. A polaroid transmits only the component along its transmission axis. Malus' law: I = I0 cos2θ. For unpolarised light through a polariser: I = I0/2.

Brewster's law: when light is incident at the polarising angle θp, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular: tan θp = n. At this angle, the reflected light is completely plane-polarised.

Tip 10 � Polarisation Shortcuts
(i) Unpolarised light → polariser → intensity halves → plane polarised. (ii) Two polaroids at 60° give I = I0/8. (iii) Sky appears blue due to scattering; scattered light is partially polarised. (iv) Liquid crystals and 3D glasses use polarisation.
Example 11 � Brewster's Angle
The polarising angle for a medium is 57°. What is its refractive index?

a) 1.33   b) 1.54   c) 1.62   d) 1.75
Solution: Using Brewster's law: n = tan θp = tan 57° = 1.54. Hence option (b) is correct. At this angle, the reflected light is completely plane-polarised, while the refracted light is partially polarised.
Tip 11 � Exam Strategy
For NEET optics, focus heavily on: (i) Mirror and lens formula with sign conventions, (ii) Snell's law and TIR, (iii) YDSE fringe width calculations, (iv) Power of lens combinations, and (v) Optical instruments formulas. These topics account for 70-80% of optics questions in the exam.

Detailed Topic Explanations

Reflection at Spherical Surfaces

Spherical mirrors are part of a hollow sphere. The centre of the sphere is the centre of curvature (C). The midpoint of the mirror is the pole (P). The principal axis is the line joining P and C. For a concave mirror, the reflecting surface is curved inward; for a convex mirror, outward. The focal point F is midway between P and C: f = R/2 where R is the radius of curvature.

Ray diagrams for concave mirrors: (i) A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through F after reflection. (ii) A ray through F becomes parallel after reflection. (iii) A ray through C retraces its path. (iv) A ray incident at the pole P reflects symmetrically about the principal axis.

Ray diagrams for convex mirrors: (i) A ray parallel to the principal axis appears to diverge from F behind the mirror. (ii) A ray directed toward C retraces its path. (iii) A ray directed toward F becomes parallel after reflection. Convex mirrors always produce virtual, erect, and diminished images, making them ideal for rear-view mirrors.

Example 12 � Concave Mirror with Object at C
An object is placed at the centre of curvature of a concave mirror (R = 30 cm). Find the position, nature, and magnification of the image.

a) At C, real, same size, m = -1   b) Beyond C, real, magnified   c) Between F and C, real, diminished   d) At F, real, highly magnified
Solution: f = R/2 = 15 cm. When u = -30 cm (at C), using mirror formula: 1/v = 1/f - 1/u = 1/(-15) - 1/(-30) = -1/15 + 1/30 = -2/30 + 1/30 = -1/30. So v = -30 cm = C. Image is real, inverted, same size. m = -v/u = -(-30)/(-30) = -1. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 13 � Convex Mirror Focal Length
A convex mirror forms a virtual image of half the size of the object placed at 20 cm from it. Find the focal length.

a) 10 cm   b) 15 cm   c) 20 cm   d) 25 cm
Solution: For a convex mirror, m = +1/2 (virtual, erect). m = -v/u. u = -20 cm. So v = -m � u = -(1/2) � (-20) = +10 cm. Using mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u = 1/10 + 1/(-20) = 1/10 - 1/20 = 1/20. f = +20 cm. Hence option (c) is correct.

Refraction Through a Glass Slab

When light passes through a rectangular glass slab, it undergoes refraction at both surfaces. The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced. The lateral shift depends on the thickness of the slab, angle of incidence, and refractive index. The formula for lateral shift: d = t sin(i - r) / cos r where t is slab thickness. This concept explains why objects appear displaced when viewed through a glass slab.

Tip 12 � Glass Slab Shortcut
For a glass slab, the emergent ray is always parallel to the incident ray. The net deviation is zero (unlike a prism). The lateral shift depends on the thickness and the refractive index. For normal incidence (i = 0), the lateral shift is zero. For NEET questions, remember that the slab does not change the direction of light, only displaces it.

Combination of Lenses and Mirrors

In optical instruments, lenses and mirrors are often used in combination. For two thin lenses in contact, the combined focal length is given by: 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2. For lenses separated by distance d: 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2 - d/(f1f2). For a lens-mirror combination, the image formed by the lens acts as the object for the mirror, and vice versa.

Example 14 � Lens-Mirror Combination
A convex lens of focal length 20 cm and a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm are placed coaxially 40 cm apart. An object is placed 30 cm from the lens on the opposite side of the mirror. Find the position of the final image.

a) 30 cm from mirror   b) 20 cm from mirror   c) 15 cm from mirror   d) 10 cm from mirror
Solution: For the lens: u1 = -30 cm, f1 = +20 cm. 1/v1 = 1/f1 + 1/u1 = 1/20 + 1/(-30) = 1/20 - 1/30 = 1/60. v1 = +60 cm (right of lens). This image is 60 - 40 = 20 cm behind the mirror (virtual object for mirror). For mirror: u2 = +20 cm, f2 = -15 cm. 1/v2 = 1/f2 - 1/u2 = -1/15 - 1/20 = -(4+3)/60 = -7/60. v2 = -60/7 ≈ -8.6 cm. The final image is real at about 8.6 cm from the mirror. Hence option (d) is closest.

Dispersion of Light

Dispersion is the splitting of white light into its constituent colours when it passes through a prism. This occurs because the refractive index of the prism material varies with wavelength (colour). Violet light (shortest wavelength) bends the most, while red light (longest wavelength) bends the least. The angular dispersion: θ = δv - δr. The dispersive power of a medium: ω = (nv - nr) / (ny - 1).

Tip 13 � Dispersion Memory Aid
VIBGYOR: Violet deviates most, Red deviates least. V = highest n, R = lowest n. Rainbow formation is a natural example of dispersion � sunlight is dispersed by water droplets in the atmosphere. The primary rainbow is formed by two refractions and one internal reflection; the secondary rainbow involves two reflections.

Scattering of Light

Rayleigh scattering: scattering intensity ∝ 1/λ4. Blue light (shorter λ) scatters more than red light (longer λ). This explains: (i) the blue colour of the sky, (ii) the red colour of the sun at sunrise and sunset (light travels through more atmosphere, blue is scattered away), and (iii) the reddish-orange colour of smoke. Tyndall effect: scattering by colloidal particles in suspension.

Example 15 � Rayleigh Scattering
The intensity of scattered light for blue light (λ = 450 nm) compared to red light (λ = 700 nm) is approximately:

a) 2 times   b) 4 times   c) 6 times   d) 8 times
Solution: I ∝ 1/λ4. Iblue/Ired = (λred/λblue)4 = (700/450)4 ≈ (1.556)4 ≈ 5.85 ≈ 6. Hence option (c) is correct.

Optical Fibres � Applications of TIR

Optical fibres consist of a core (high n) surrounded by cladding (low n). Light entering the fibre at an angle less than the acceptance angle undergoes repeated TIR, allowing signal transmission over long distances with minimal loss. The numerical aperture (NA) of the fibre is: NA = sin θmax = √(n12 - n22) where n1 and n2 are the refractive indices of core and cladding. Applications: telecommunications, endoscopy, internet cables, sensors.

Tip 14 � Optical Fibre Facts
(i) Step-index fibre: core has uniform n, cladding has lower n. (ii) Graded-index fibre: n decreases gradually from centre to edge. (iii) Multimode fibres are used for short distances; single-mode fibres for long-distance telecom. For NEET, focus on the TIR principle and acceptance angle concept.

Resolving Power of Optical Instruments

Resolving power is the ability of an instrument to distinguish between two closely spaced objects. According to Rayleigh criterion, two point sources are resolved when the central maximum of one falls on the first minimum of the other. For a telescope: Resolving power = 1/θ = D/1.22λ. For a microscope: Resolving power = 2n sin α / λ where n sin α is the numerical aperture.

Huygens Principle � Detailed Derivation

Huygens principle: Every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary spherical wavelets. These wavelets travel forward at the wave speed. The new wavefront is the forward envelope of these secondary wavelets. This principle provides a geometric construction for wave propagation.

Proof of Snell's Law using Huygens principle: Consider a plane wavefront AB incident on the interface between two media at an angle i. In medium 1, the wave speed is v1; in medium 2, speed is v2. By the time the wavelet from B reaches B' (distance = v1t), the wavelet from A has travelled into medium 2 a distance v2t. By geometry, sin i = BB'/AB' = v1t/AB' and sin r = AA'/AB' = v2t/AB'. Thus sin i/sin r = v1/v2 = n2/n1.

Coherent Sources and Conditions for Interference

Two sources are coherent if they have the same frequency, same wavelength, and a constant phase difference. Laser light is highly coherent; ordinary light sources are incoherent. For sustained interference: (i) sources must be coherent, (ii) amplitudes should be equal for maximum contrast, (iii) the distance between sources should be small compared to screen distance, and (iv) sources should be monochromatic or have a narrow bandwidth.

Intensity Distribution in YDSE

The electric fields from the two slits superpose: E = E1 + E2. If both slits have equal amplitude A0, E = A0[sin(ωt) + sin(ωt + φ)] = 2A0 cos(φ/2) sin(ωt + φ/2). The intensity I = I0 cos�(φ/2) where I0 = 4A0� is the maximum intensity. When φ = 0, 2π, 4π..., I = I0 (max). When φ = π, 3π, 5π..., I = 0 (min). The average intensity over all phases is I0/2.

Example 17 � YDSE with Thin Film
In YDSE, a thin film of refractive index 1.5 and thickness 2 μm is placed in front of one slit. If λ = 600 nm, find the shift in the central fringe.

a) 5 fringes   b) 3 fringes   c) 2 fringes   d) 4 fringes
Solution: The film introduces an extra optical path difference = (n - 1)t = 0.5 � 2 � 10-6 = 10-6 m. Number of fringes shifted = extra path/λ = 10-6/(6 � 10-7) = 1.67 ≈ 2 fringes (approx 2). Hence option (c) is correct.

Diffraction Grating

A diffraction grating consists of N parallel slits per unit length. The grating element (slit separation) d = 1/N. For a maximum: d sin θ = nλ. The angular position of the nth order maximum depends on the wavelength; hence a grating can be used to measure wavelengths accurately. The resolving power of a grating: R = λ/Δλ = nN where N is the total number of lines on the grating.

Tip 15 � Grating vs Prism
Both gratings and prisms produce spectra. A grating spectrum has uniform dispersion (equal spacing in wavelength), while a prism spectrum has non-uniform dispersion (more spread for violet). Gratings are preferred for precision spectroscopy because the deviation is directly proportional to wavelength.

Doppler Effect in Light

When a source of light moves relative to an observer, the observed frequency changes � the Doppler effect. For a source approaching the observer: λobs = λsource (1 - v/c) (blue shift). For a source receding: λobs = λsource (1 + v/c) (red shift). The relativistic Doppler formula is: λobs = λsource √[(1 + v/c)/(1 - v/c)]. Applications: measuring stellar velocities, detecting exoplanets, cosmology (expanding universe).

Example 18 � Doppler Shift
A star emits light of wavelength 500 nm. The light received on Earth has wavelength 502 nm. Is the star moving towards or away from Earth? What is its speed?

a) Away, 1.2 � 106 m/s   b) Towards, 1.2 � 106 m/s   c) Away, 2.4 � 106 m/s   d) Towards, 2.4 � 106 m/s
Solution: Observed λ (502 nm) > emitted λ (500 nm), so red shift occurs � the star is moving away. Using Δλ/λ = v/c: v = (2/500) � 3 � 108 = 1.2 � 106 m/s. Hence option (a) is correct.

Optical Instruments in Detail

Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)

A simple microscope is a single convex lens. The object is placed between the lens and its focal point. The image is virtual, erect, and magnified. Angular magnification when the image is formed at the near point (D = 25 cm): M = 1 + D/f. When the image is at infinity (least strain): M = D/f.

Compound Microscope

A compound microscope has an objective lens (short fo) and an eyepiece (longer fe). The objective forms a real, inverted, magnified image near the focal point of the eyepiece. The eyepiece acts as a simple microscope to further magnify this intermediate image. Total magnification: M = Mo � Me. In normal adjustment (final image at infinity): M = -(L/fo) � (D/fe) where L is the tube length.

Astronomical Telescope

An astronomical telescope has an objective lens (long fo) and an eyepiece (short fe). Parallel rays from a distant object form a real image at the focal plane of the objective. The eyepiece magnifies this image. In normal adjustment (final image at infinity): M = -fo/fe. The length of the telescope: L = fo + fe.

Applications in Daily Life and Technology

Rainbow formation: Sunlight is dispersed by water droplets. Primary rainbow: one internal reflection, angle 42°. Secondary rainbow: two internal reflections, angle 51°, colours reversed. Mirages: Due to TIR in layers of air with different temperatures (different refractive indices). On hot days, the air near the ground is hotter (less dense, lower n), causing light to bend upward, creating an illusion of water. Brilliance of diamonds: High refractive index (2.42) gives a very small critical angle (24.4°), causing most light entering the diamond to undergo TIR multiple times before emerging. Blue colour of sky: Rayleigh scattering ∝ 1/λ4. Red colour of sun at sunset: Longer path through atmosphere; blue light scattered away. Optical fibres: TIR in core-cladding system for high-speed data transmission. LASER: Coherent, monochromatic, high-intensity light used in surgery, barcodes, communication.

Tip 16 � NEET Optics Quick Facts
(i) Maximum magnification of a simple microscope: M = 1 + D/f (at near point). (ii) Maximum magnification of a compound microscope: when the final image is at the near point. (iii) For a telescope, M is maximum when the final image is at the near point: M = -fo/fe(1 + fe/D). (iv) The minimum distance between two objects that can be resolved by the human eye (normal vision) is about 0.1 mm at 25 cm.

Wave Nature of Light � Historical Context

Christian Huygens (1678) proposed the wave theory of light. Thomas Young's double-slit experiment (1801) provided strong evidence. Augustin-Jean Fresnel refined the wave theory mathematically. James Clerk Maxwell (1865) showed that light is an electromagnetic wave. Heinrich Hertz (1887) experimentally generated and detected radio waves. Max Planck (1900) and Albert Einstein (1905) introduced the quantum theory (photoelectric effect), showing that light also behaves as particles (photons). Modern physics accepts wave-particle duality � light exhibits both wave and particle properties depending on the experiment.

Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Sign convention confusion: For mirrors, u is always negative. For lenses, u is negative when the object is on the incident side. Always use Cartesian sign convention rigorously.
  • Lens vs mirror formula: Mirror formula uses 1/v + 1/u, while the lens formula uses 1/v - 1/u. This is a very common source of error.
  • Real vs virtual: For mirrors, v negative = real image (in front). For lenses, v positive = real image (on opposite side). Know the difference!
  • TIR conditions: Students often forget that TIR requires light to travel from denser to rarer medium. TIR cannot occur when going from rarer to denser.
  • Fringe width in YDSE: β = λD/d, not λd/D. Remember: D (large) is in numerator, d (small) is in denominator.
  • Polarisation vs Malus: Unpolarised light through first polariser reduces intensity to I0/2. Through the analyser, Malus' law I = I0 cos�θ applies.

Important Derivations for NEET

Derivation of Mirror Formula

Consider an object AB placed beyond C of a concave mirror. Using similar triangles A'B'F and ABF, along with sign conventions, we arrive at 1/f = 1/v + 1/u. The derivation uses the relation: A'B'/AB = (A'F)/(AF) where A'F = v + f (with signs) and AF = u - f.

Derivation of Lens Maker's Formula

Consider a thin lens with two spherical surfaces of radii R1 and R2. Apply refraction formula at the first surface: n2/v1 - n1/u = (n2 - n1)/R1. Then apply at the second surface: n1/v - n2/v1 = (n1 - n2)/R2. Adding the two equations and simplifying gives 1/f = (n - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2).

Derivation of Fringe Width in YDSE

For two slits separated by d at distance D from the screen, the path difference for a point P at distance y from the centre is Δx = d sin θ ≈ d(y/D). For constructive interference: Δx = nλ. So y = nλD/d. The fringe width β = yn+1 - yn = λD/d.

Advanced Concepts for NEET

Chromatic Aberration

Chromatic aberration occurs because a lens has different focal lengths for different colours (wavelengths). For a convex lens, fviolet < fred because nviolet > nred. The longitudinal chromatic aberration: fred - fviolet. An achromatic doublet (crown + flint glass) is used to minimise this. Condition for achromatism: ω1/f1 + ω2/f2 = 0 where ω is the dispersive power.

Spherical Aberration

Spherical aberration occurs when paraxial and marginal rays have different focal points. It can be reduced by: (i) using a stop (aperture), (ii) using a plano-convex lens with the convex side facing parallel light, (iii) using a combination of lenses with opposite aberrations, or (iv) using parabolic mirrors instead of spherical (for telescopes).

Rayleigh Criterion for Resolution

The Rayleigh criterion states that two point sources are just resolved when the central maximum of the diffraction pattern of one falls on the first minimum of the other. The minimum angular separation resolvable: θmin = 1.22λ/D for a circular aperture of diameter D. The resolving power is defined as 1/θmin.

Newton's Rings

Newton's rings are a circular interference pattern observed when a plano-convex lens is placed on a flat glass plate. The fringes are circles concentric with the point of contact. The radius of the nth dark ring: rn = √(nλR) where R is the radius of curvature of the lens. For bright rings: rn = √((2n-1)λR/2). The centre is dark (point of contact has zero path difference but π phase change at the glass-air interface). This experiment is used to determine the wavelength of light and the radius of curvature of lenses.

a) 489 nm   b) 546 nm   c) 613 nm   d) 588 nm

Thin Film Interference

When light reflects from a thin film (soap bubble, oil slick), interference occurs between light reflected from the top and bottom surfaces. Condition for constructive interference in reflected light: 2nt cos r = (2n - 1)λ/2 (accounting for π phase change at the denser medium). For transmitted light, the conditions are reversed. The beautiful colours of soap bubbles and oil slicks are due to thin film interference with white light.

Tip 21 � Thin Film Memory Aid
For reflected light: If both reflections are from the same type (both rarer-to-denser or both denser-to-rarer), there is no extra path difference. If one reflection is from a rarer-to-denser and the other from denser-to-rarer, there is an extra λ/2 path difference due to the phase change of π. In soap bubbles, the thin air film between two soap layers produces the colours.

Interference in Wedge-Shaped Films

A wedge-shaped thin film (air wedge) produces straight, parallel fringes. The fringe width depends on the wedge angle θ: β = λ/(2θ) for small angles. The fringes are localised at the top surface. The number of fringes per unit length is a measure of the wedge angle. Applications: testing optical flatness, measuring small thicknesses.

Fabry-Perot Interferometer

This instrument uses multiple reflections between two parallel, partially reflecting surfaces to produce very sharp interference fringes. The condition for maxima: 2nd cos θ = mλ. It is used for high-resolution spectroscopy, measuring small wavelength differences, and as a laser resonator cavity. The finesse of the interferometer determines the sharpness of the fringes.

Optical Activity and Polarimetry

Certain substances (sugar solutions, quartz) rotate the plane of polarisation of light passing through them. This is called optical activity. The angle of rotation: θ = αlc where α is specific rotation, l is path length, and c is concentration. A polarimeter measures the concentration of optically active substances � used in the sugar industry and pharmaceutical analysis.

Holography

Holography is a technique for recording and reconstructing three-dimensional images using laser light. It uses the interference pattern between a reference beam and light scattered from the object. The recorded pattern (hologram) contains both amplitude and phase information, allowing the reconstruction of a realistic 3D image. Unlike photography, holography records the complete wavefront. Applications: security (currency holograms), data storage, medical imaging.

Diffraction of X-Rays by Crystals

Max von Laue (1912) discovered that X-rays are diffracted by crystals, proving both the wave nature of X-rays and the periodic atomic structure of crystals. Bragg's law: 2d sin θ = nλ where d is the interplanar spacing. X-ray diffraction (XRD) is used to determine crystal structures, measure lattice parameters, and identify unknown crystalline materials. This technique is fundamental in materials science and chemistry.

LASER � Light Amplification

LASER (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) produces coherent, monochromatic, intense, and highly directional light. Key principles: population inversion (more atoms in excited state than ground state), stimulated emission (photon triggers emission of identical photon), and optical feedback (mirrors at both ends). Types: He-Ne laser (red, 632.8 nm), Ruby laser (pulsed, 694.3 nm), CO2 laser (infrared, 10.6 μm), Semiconductor laser (used in barcode scanners, fibre optics). Applications: surgery, cutting, welding, barcode readers, CD/DVD players, optical communication, LIDAR, holography.

Tip 22 � LASER vs Ordinary Light
LASER light is: (i) Monochromatic (single wavelength), (ii) Coherent (same phase at all points), (iii) Collimated (highly directional, minimal divergence), (iv) Intense (high energy density). Ordinary light is: polychromatic, incoherent, divergent, and low intensity. The word LASER is an acronym for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

Photometry

Photometry deals with the measurement of light as perceived by the human eye. Key quantities: Luminous flux (lumen, lm), Luminous intensity (candela, cd � SI base unit), Illuminance (lux, lx = lm/m�), Luminance (cd/m�). Inverse square law: Illuminance E = I/r�. Lambert's cosine law: E = (I cos θ)/r� for a surface tilted at angle θ to the incident light. The human eye is most sensitive to green-yellow light (555 nm).

📐 Derivations

▶ Derivation of Lens Maker's Formula

Consider a thin lens with refractive index n₂ surrounded by medium n₁.

For refraction at first surface (radius R₁): n₂/v₁ − n₁/u = (n₂−n₁)/R₁

For refraction at second surface (radius R₂): n₁/v − n₂/v₁ = (n₁−n₂)/R₂

Adding both equations: n₁/v − n₁/u = (n₂−n₁)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

When u = ∞, v = f: n₁/f − n₁/∞ = (n₂−n₁)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

→ n₁/f = (n₂−n₁)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

→ 1/f = (n₂/n₁ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

→ 1/f = (n−1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) where n = n₂/n₁ (relative RI of lens material)

▶ Derivation of Fringe Width in YDSE

Two slits S₁ and S₂ separated by distance d, at distance D from screen.

Path difference: S₂P − S₁P = d sinθ ≈ d�y/D (for small θ)

For bright fringe (constructive): d�yn/D = nλ

→ yn = nλD/d

For dark fringe (destructive): d�yn/D = (2n+1)λ/2

→ yn = (2n+1)λD/2d

Fringe width: β = yn+1 − yn = (n+1)λD/d − nλD/d = λD/d

Thus β = λD/d � independent of n, so fringes are equally spaced.


NEET Note: Changing medium (water instead of air): λmedium = λair/μ, so βmedium = βair/μ. Fringes get closer in denser medium.

⚠️ NEET Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

▶ Frequently Made Mistakes

❌ Sign confusion in lens/mirror formula: Use Cartesian sign convention always. u is negative for real object (left side). For mirrors: v is negative for real image (same side as object). For lenses: v is positive for real image (opposite side).

❌ f = R/2 for mirrors, but not for lenses: This formula applies to spherical mirrors only. For lenses, focal length depends on refractive index and radii of curvature.

❌ Mixing up real and virtual images: Real images can be projected on a screen (convex lens, concave mirror). Virtual images cannot (concave lens, convex mirror, plane mirror).

❌ Lens maker's formula sign of R: R₁ is positive if center of curvature is on the right side, negative if on left. For biconvex: R₁ positive, R₂ negative.

❌ Prism deviation: δ = i + e − A. Minimum deviation when i = e. At minimum deviation: δm = 2i − A, and n = sin[(A+δm)/2]/sin(A/2).

❌ YDSE � wrong formula for fringe width: β = λD/d, NOT β = λd/D. D is screen distance (large), d is slit separation (small).

❌ Polarisation vs plane of vibration: Light waves are transverse � electric field vector vibrates perpendicular to direction of propagation. Polarisation proves transverse nature.
