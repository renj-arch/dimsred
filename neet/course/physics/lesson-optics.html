What is Optics?

Optics is the branch of physics that studies light and its interactions with matter. It is divided into geometrical optics (ray optics) and physical optics (wave optics). For NEET, key topics include reflection and refraction, lenses and mirrors, optical instruments, interference, diffraction, and polarization. Understanding ray diagrams and wave properties is essential.

Key Concepts

Reflection: Angle of incidence = angle of reflection. Mirror Formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u, where f is focal length, v is image distance, u is object distance. Magnification m = -v/u. Snell's Law: n1 sinθ1 = n2 sinθ2. Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u. Lensmaker's formula: 1/f = (n - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2). Critical Angle: sin C = 1/n (for total internal reflection). Young's Double Slit Experiment: Fringe width β = λD/d.

Solved Examples

Example 1
An object is placed 15 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find the position and nature of the image.

a) 30 cm, real b) -30 cm, real c) 6 cm, virtual d) -6 cm, virtual
Solution: Using mirror formula 1/f = 1/v + 1/u. u = -15 cm, f = -10 cm. 1/v = 1/f - 1/u = -1/10 - (-1/15) = -1/10 + 1/15 = (-3 + 2)/30 = -1/30. So v = -30 cm. Negative sign indicates image is real and in front of the mirror. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 2
Light travels from air (n = 1) to water (n = 4/3). If the angle of incidence is 45°, find the angle of refraction.

a) 32.1° b) 28.6° c) 36.8° d) 41.2°
Solution: Using Snell's law: n1 sinθ1 = n2 sinθ2. 1 × sin45° = (4/3) × sinθ2. sinθ2 = (3/4) × sin45° = (3/4) × 0.707 = 0.530. θ2 = sin-1(0.530) = 32.1°. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 3
In Young's double slit experiment, the slit separation is 0.5 mm and the screen is 1 m away. If the wavelength of light used is 500 nm, find the fringe width.

a) 0.5 mm b) 1.0 mm c) 1.5 mm d) 2.0 mm
Solution: Fringe width β = λD/d = (500 × 10-9 × 1)/(0.5 × 10-3) = (500 × 10-9)/0.5 × 10-3 = 1000 × 10-6 = 1.0 × 10-3 m = 1.0 mm. Hence option (b) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For lenses and mirrors, memorize sign conventions: distances in direction of incident light are positive.
  • In refraction problems, always draw the normal at the point of incidence to avoid errors.
  • For total internal reflection, check if the light is traveling from denser to rarer medium and incidence angle > critical angle.
  • In interference, for dark fringes (destructive), path difference = (2n - 1)λ/2.

Paraxial Matrix Formalism for Ray Tracing

The paraxial matrix method, also known as the ABCD matrix formalism, provides a systematic way to propagate rays through any sequence of optical elements that operate in the paraxial approximation. Each element is represented by a 2 × 2 matrix that relates the output ray vector (height y, angle θ) to the input ray vector. For a ray travelling a distance d in a medium of refractive index n, the translation matrix is

1 d/n 0 1

A thin lens of focal length f is described by

1 0 -1/f 1

Refraction at a spherical surface of radius R separating media of indices n₁ and n₂ yields

1 0 (n₁-n₂)/(n₂R) n₁/n₂

Multiplying the matrices in the order the ray encounters them gives the system matrix. The overall effective focal length F of the system follows from the element C of the system matrix M = [[A, B], [C, D]] as F = -1/C (provided C ≠ 0). The principal planes are located at distances (D-1)/C from the input plane and (1-A)/C from the output plane. This formalism makes it straightforward to analyse compound lenses, thick lenses, and optical cavities. For a thick lens of thickness t, refractive index n, and radii R₁, R₂, the system matrix is the product of refraction at the first surface, translation through the glass, and refraction at the second surface. The resulting matrix elements give the exact paraxial focal length and principal plane positions without resorting to the lensmaker’s approximation. The method also extends to Gaussian beam propagation by replacing the ray vector with the complex beam parameter q, where the same ABCD law applies: q_out = (A q_in + B)/(C q_in + D). This unification of ray and beam optics is a powerful tool for designing laser resonators and fibre optic systems.

When applying the matrix method, sign conventions must be consistent: distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive; radii of curvature are positive if the centre of curvature lies to the right of the surface. A common error is to mix the Cartesian sign convention (used in the mirror formula) with the matrix convention, leading to incorrect focal lengths. The matrix approach also clarifies the concept of optical power: the power of a system is simply -C (in dioptres when distances are in metres). For a combination of thin lenses in contact, the system matrix reduces to a single thin‑lens matrix with power equal to the sum of individual powers, reproducing the familiar additive rule.

Coherence Requirements in Interference Phenomena

Interference fringes are observable only when the superposing waves maintain a fixed phase relationship over the observation time and across the aperture. Temporal coherence is quantified by the coherence length L_c = c/Δν, where Δν is the spectral linewidth of the source. For a source of central wavelength λ and bandwidth Δλ, the coherence length can be approximated as L_c ≈ λ²/Δλ. In Young’s double‑slit experiment, the path difference between the two arms must be less than L_c for high‑contrast fringes. Spatial coherence, on the other hand, describes the correlation between the field at two points transverse to the propagation direction. The Van Cittert–Zernike theorem states that the complex degree of coherence at a plane illuminated by an extended incoherent source is the normalized Fourier transform of the source intensity distribution. For a circular source of angular diameter α, the transverse coherence width at distance z is roughly λ/α. This explains why a distant star (small α) produces high spatial coherence across a telescope aperture, while an extended laboratory source requires a spatial filter (pinhole) to achieve the same effect.

In practical interferometers such as the Michelson or Mach‑Zehnder, the coherence length limits the maximum permissible path‑difference scan. White‑light interferometry exploits the short coherence length of broadband sources to localize fringes at zero path difference, enabling precise surface profiling. Conversely, laser sources with Δν < 1 MHz have coherence lengths exceeding hundreds of metres, allowing interference over large delays. The visibility V of fringes is given by V = (I_max - I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = |γ(τ)|, where γ(τ) is the complex degree of temporal coherence at delay τ. For a Lorentzian spectrum, |γ(τ)| = exp(-πΔν|τ|); for a Gaussian spectrum, |γ(τ)| = exp[-(πΔν τ)²/4ln2]. Understanding these functional forms is essential when designing Fourier‑transform spectrometers, where the measured interferogram is the cosine Fourier transform of the spectrum.

Worked Example: Coherence Length of a Spectral Line
A sodium lamp emits the D‑line doublet at 589.0 nm and 589.6 nm with a natural linewidth of 0.01 nm for each component. Estimate the coherence length of the light and comment on the maximum path difference for observable fringes in a Michelson interferometer.
The effective bandwidth Δλ is the separation of the two components plus their individual widths. The doublet separation is 0.6 nm, which dominates the 0.01 nm natural width. Taking Δλ ≈ 0.6 nm, the coherence length L_c ≈ λ²/Δλ = (589 nm)² / 0.6 nm ≈ 5.8 × 10⁵ nm = 0.58 mm. In a Michelson interferometer the optical path difference is twice the mirror displacement, so fringes remain visible up to a mirror travel of about 0.29 mm. Beyond this the two doublet components produce fringe patterns that shift relative to each other, reducing visibility.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question gives a source wavelength and a spectral width, first compute the coherence length using λ²/Δλ. Then compare the required path difference in the described interferometer. If the path difference exceeds the coherence length, the answer will involve reduced or absent fringe visibility. Remember that for a doublet the effective Δλ is the separation between the lines, not the individual linewidths.

Diffraction Grating Equation and Resolving Power

A diffraction grating consists of a periodic array of slits or grooves that imposes a phase modulation on the incident wavefront. The grating equation for normal incidence is d sin θ_m = m λ, where d is the grating period, θ_m the diffraction angle of order m, and λ the wavelength. For oblique incidence at angle θ_i, the generalized equation becomes d(sin θ_m + sin θ_i) = m λ. The angular dispersion dθ_m/dλ = m/(d cos θ_m) shows that higher orders and smaller periods increase dispersion, but the free spectral range (the wavelength interval before adjacent orders overlap) shrinks as Δλ_FSR = λ/m. The resolving power R of a grating is defined as R = λ/Δλ_min, where Δλ_min is the smallest resolvable wavelength difference. For a grating illuminated over N illuminated grooves, the theoretical limit is R = m N (Rayleigh criterion). This result follows from the width of the principal maxima, which is inversely proportional to the total number of illuminated grooves. In practice, the achieved resolving power is limited by the beam size, grating imperfections, and aberrations in the focusing optics.

Blazed gratings concentrate most of the diffracted energy into a specific order by shaping each groove as a sawtooth with blaze angle θ_b. The blaze condition for maximum efficiency in order m is 2 d sin θ_b = m λ (Littrow configuration). Volume phase holographic gratings achieve high efficiency with low scattered light by recording a sinusoidal refractive index modulation in a photosensitive medium. Echelle gratings operate at high diffraction orders (m ≈ 50–100) with a large blaze angle, providing very high dispersion and resolving power in a compact format; they are cross‑dispersed with a prism or low‑order grating to separate overlapping orders. The instrumental profile of a spectrometer is the convolution of the slit image, the grating diffraction pattern, and detector pixel response. Accurate wavelength calibration requires measuring known spectral lines and fitting a polynomial to the pixel‑wavelength mapping.

Polarization by Reflection, Brewster Angle, and Wave Plates

When unpolarized light strikes a dielectric interface, the reflected component becomes partially polarized. At the Brewster angle θ_B, the reflected ray is perfectly s‑polarized (electric field perpendicular to the plane of incidence) and the reflected p‑component vanishes. The Brewster condition follows from the Fresnel equations: tan θ_B = n₂/n₁, where n₁ and n₂ are the refractive indices of the incident and transmitted media. For an air‑glass interface (n₁≈1, n₂≈1.5), θ_B ≈ 56.3°. This principle is exploited in polarizing beam splitters and in reducing glare from reflective surfaces. The transmitted light at Brewster incidence is partially p‑polarized; stacking multiple plates at Brewster angle yields a high‑extinction polarizer.

Wave plates (retarders) introduce a controlled phase delay between orthogonal polarization components. A half‑wave plate (λ/2) rotates the plane of linear polarization by twice the angle between the incident polarization and the fast axis. A quarter‑wave plate (λ/4) converts linear polarization at 45° to the axes into circular polarization, and vice versa. The retardance Γ = (2π/λ) Δn t, where Δn is the birefringence and t the plate thickness. Zero‑order wave plates are made by cementing two plates of opposite birefringence to achieve the desired net retardance while minimizing wavelength sensitivity. Multiple‑order plates are thinner but exhibit strong wavelength dependence; they are used when a specific wavelength is fixed. The Jones matrix formalism provides a compact description: for a linear retarder with fast axis horizontal, the matrix is [[1,0],[0,eiΓ]]. Cascading elements corresponds to matrix multiplication, enabling design of polarization controllers and elliptical polarizers.

Optical Instruments: Compound Microscope and Astronomical Telescope

The compound microscope uses an objective lens of short focal length f_o (typically 4 mm–20 mm) to form a real, inverted, magnified intermediate image at the tube length L (standardized at 160 mm for finite‑tube designs). The eyepiece of focal length f_e then acts as a simple magnifier, producing a virtual image at the near point (25 cm) or at infinity for relaxed viewing. The total angular magnification is M = (L/f_o) × (D/f_e), where D = 25 cm is the least distance of distinct vision. For infinity‑corrected microscopes, a tube lens forms the intermediate image at its focal plane, and the magnification becomes M = (f_tube/f_o) × (D/f_e). Numerical aperture NA = n sin α determines the resolving power: the minimum resolvable distance d_min = 0.61 λ/NA (Rayleigh criterion). Oil immersion objectives increase NA by raising n to ≈1.51, pushing resolution below 200 nm for visible light.

The astronomical telescope in its simplest form (refracting) consists of an objective of large focal length f_o and an eyepiece of short focal length f_e. The angular magnification is M = -f_o/f_e (negative sign indicates an inverted image). The light‑gathering power is proportional to the objective area (πD²/4), while the resolving power is limited by diffraction: θ_min = 1.22 λ/D (Dawes’ limit for a circular aperture). Reflecting telescopes replace the objective lens with a concave primary mirror, eliminating chromatic aberration and allowing larger apertures. The Cassegrain configuration adds a convex secondary mirror to fold the light path, yielding a long effective focal length in a compact tube. Modern professional telescopes use adaptive optics to correct atmospheric turbulence, achieving near‑diffraction‑limited performance. The exit pupil diameter = D/|M| must match the observer’s pupil (≈2–7 mm) for optimal brightness; otherwise vignetting occurs.

  • Mixing Cartesian sign convention with matrix sign convention when computing system matrices.
  • Assuming the coherence length of a doublet source is set by the individual linewidths rather than the line separation.
  • Using the grating equation d sin θ = m λ for oblique incidence without adding the incident angle term.
  • Confusing the theoretical resolving power m N with the actual instrumental resolution limited by slit width and detector sampling.
  • Applying the Brewster angle formula tan θ_B = n₂/n₁ for internal reflection (n₁ > n₂) without swapping the indices.
  • Treating a half‑wave plate as a polarization rotator for any input polarization; it only rotates linear polarization, leaving circular polarization unchanged.
  • Neglecting the tube length standardization when calculating microscope magnification, leading to errors of 10–20 %.

Fourier Optics and Spatial Filtering

Fourier optics treats the propagation of light through free space and thin lenses as a sequence of Fourier transforms. A thin lens of focal length f performs an optical Fourier transform of the complex field at its front focal plane onto its back focal plane, with spatial frequency coordinates u = x/(λf) and v = y/(λf). The Fraunhofer diffraction pattern of an aperture is therefore the squared modulus of the Fourier transform of the aperture transmission function. This equivalence underpins spatial filtering: placing a mask in the back focal plane modifies the spatial frequency spectrum, and a second lens reconstructs the filtered image at its image plane. Low‑pass filtering (a circular aperture) smooths edges and removes high‑frequency noise; high‑pass filtering (an opaque central stop) enhances edges and fine detail. The 4‑f correlator, consisting of two lenses separated by 2f, implements real‑time convolution and correlation operations used in pattern recognition and optical computing. The point‑spread function (PSF) of an imaging system is the inverse Fourier transform of its pupil function; the optical transfer function (OTF) is the autocorrelation of the pupil. For a circular pupil of diameter D, the OTF falls to zero at the cutoff frequency νc = D/(λf), defining the diffraction‑limited resolution. Aberrations introduce phase errors in the pupil, reducing the OTF magnitude and shifting its phase; the Strehl ratio, the peak intensity of the aberrated PSF relative to the ideal, quantifies image quality. In practice, spatial light modulators (SLMs) placed at the Fourier plane enable programmable filtering, phase conjugation, and adaptive optics correction. The mathematical framework extends to partially coherent illumination via the mutual intensity function and the van Cittert–Zernike theorem, which states that the far‑field coherence function is the Fourier transform of the source intensity distribution.

Laser Fundamentals: Gain Media, Population Inversion, and Cavity Modes

A laser (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) requires three ingredients: an active gain medium, a pumping mechanism to achieve population inversion, and an optical resonator that provides feedback. Population inversion occurs when the upper laser level population N2 exceeds the lower level population N1 multiplied by the degeneracy ratio g2/g1. For a four‑level system (e.g., Nd:YAG), the lower laser level empties rapidly, making inversion easier than in a three‑level system (e.g., ruby) where the ground state is the lower laser level. The gain coefficient g(ν) = σ(ν)(N2 – (g2/g1)N1) depends on the stimulated‑emission cross‑section σ(ν) and the lineshape function. Threshold condition: round‑trip gain equals round‑trip loss, 2gthL = αiL + ln(1/R1R2), where L is the gain length, αi internal loss, and R1, R2 mirror reflectivities. Longitudinal cavity modes satisfy νq = q c/(2nL) (q integer, n refractive index). The free spectral range (FSR) is c/(2nL). Transverse modes are described by Hermite‑Gaussian (TEMmn) or Laguerre‑Gaussian (TEMpl) functions; the fundamental TEM00 mode has a Gaussian intensity profile with waist w0 determined by mirror curvatures and cavity length via the stability condition 0 ≤ (1 – L/R1)(1 – L/R2) ≤ 1. Mode selection is achieved with apertures, etalons, or by designing the cavity for single‑mode operation. Q‑switching (active or passive) stores energy in the gain medium and releases it in a short high‑peak‑power pulse; mode‑locking forces a fixed phase relationship among many longitudinal modes, producing femtosecond to picosecond pulse trains. Typical pulse energies range from nanojoules (mode‑locked Ti:sapphire) to joules (Q‑switched Nd:YAG). Thermal lensing in high‑average‑power solid‑state lasers distorts the wavefront; compensation techniques include composite crystals, adaptive optics, and thin‑disk geometry.

Worked Example: Threshold Pump Power for a Nd:YAG Laser
A Nd:YAG rod (length 10 cm, diameter 6 mm) is end‑pumped by a diode laser at 808 nm. The upper‑state lifetime τ = 230 µs, emission cross‑section σ = 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ cm², refractive index n = 1.82. The cavity mirrors have reflectivities R₁ = 99.9 % and R₂ = 95 %. Internal round‑trip loss αᵢL = 0.02. Calculate the threshold pump power assuming quantum efficiency ηq = 0.8 and pump absorption efficiency ηa = 0.9.
1. Threshold gain coefficient: gth = (αᵢL + ln(1/(R₁R₂))) / (2L). ln(1/(0.999 × 0.95)) = ln(1/0.94905) ≈ 0.0523. αᵢL = 0.02. Sum = 0.0723. 2L = 0.20 m = 20 cm. gth = 0.0723 / 20 cm = 3.615 × 10⁻³ cm⁻¹. 2. Required population inversion ΔNth = gth/σ = (3.615 × 10⁻³) / (2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 1.29 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. 3. Volume of pumped region V = π (0.3 cm)² × 10 cm ≈ 2.83 cm³. Total inverted ions Ntot = ΔNth × V ≈ 3.65 × 10¹⁶. 4. Pump rate required: Rp = Ntot/τ = 3.65 × 10¹⁶ / 2.3 × 10⁻⁴ s ≈ 1.59 × 10²⁰ s⁻¹. 5. Pump photon energy at 808 nm: Ep = hc/λ ≈ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s × 3 × 10⁸ m/s) / 808 × 10⁻⁹ m ≈ 2.46 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. 6. Optical pump power Popt = Rp Ep / (ηq ηa) = (1.59 × 10²⁰ × 2.46 × 10⁻¹⁹) / (0.8 × 0.9) ≈ 54 W. Therefore the threshold pump power is approximately 54 W.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a laser problem gives mirror reflectivities, internal loss, and rod dimensions, first compute the round‑trip loss term ln(1/R₁R₂). Many candidates forget the factor 2 in the denominator of the threshold gain expression (2L). Keep units consistent: convert lengths to cm if cross‑section is in cm². Remember that the pump wavelength determines photon energy; use hc = 1240 eV·nm for quick conversion. If the question asks for slope efficiency, use ηs = ηq ηa (λp/λl) (Toc/(Toc+Li)), where Toc is output coupler transmission and Li internal loss.

Fiber Optics: Mode Theory, Dispersion, and Nonlinear Effects

Optical fibers guide light by total internal reflection between a high‑index core (n1) and a lower‑index cladding (n2). The normalized frequency V = (2πa/λ) NA, where a is core radius and NA = √(n1² – n2²), determines the number of guided modes. For V < 2.405 the fiber is single‑mode (SMF‑28 at 1550 nm: a ≈ 4.1 µm, NA ≈ 0.12). Multimode fibers (MMF) with V ≫ 2.405 support many modes; the approximate mode count is M ≈ V²/2 for step‑index and M ≈ V²/4 for graded‑index profiles. Modal dispersion in MMF limits bandwidth‑length product; graded‑index fibers reduce differential mode delay by parabolic index grading, achieving bandwidths > 1 GHz·km. Chromatic dispersion arises from material dispersion (dn/dλ) and waveguide dispersion (dependence of mode field diameter on λ). The total dispersion parameter D = Dmat + Dwg (ps/(nm·km)). For standard SMF, zero‑dispersion wavelength λ0 ≈ 1310 nm; dispersion‑shifted fibers move λ0 to 1550 nm for low loss and low dispersion simultaneously. Polarization‑mode dispersion (PMD) stems from random birefringence; the differential group delay (DGD) follows a Maxwellian distribution with mean Δτ = DPMD√L. Nonlinear effects become significant at high powers: self‑phase modulation (SPM) broadens spectra, four‑wave mixing (FWM) generates new frequencies, stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) and stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) transfer power to Stokes waves. The nonlinear coefficient γ = 2πn2/(λAeff) (W⁻¹km⁻¹) quantifies Kerr nonlinearity; typical SMF γ ≈ 1.3 W⁻¹km⁻¹. Soliton formation balances anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0) with SPM; the fundamental soliton peak power P0 = |β₂|/(γT₀²) where T₀ is pulse width parameter. Erbium‑doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) provide gain in the C‑band (1530–1565 nm) with noise figure ~5 dB; Raman amplifiers extend gain bandwidth using distributed pumping. Fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs) written by UV interference act as narrowband reflectors for wavelength filtering, dispersion compensation, and sensor interrogation.

Optical Aberrations: Seidel Theory, Wavefront Error, and Correction Methods

Real optical systems deviate from the ideal paraxial model; Seidel aberration theory expresses the wavefront error W(ρ,θ) as a polynomial in normalized pupil coordinates (ρ,θ) and field angle (H). The five primary (third‑order) Seidel aberrations are spherical aberration (W₀₄₀ ρ⁴), coma (W₁₃₁ H ρ³ cosθ), astigmatism (W₂₂₂ H² ρ² cos²θ), field curvature (W₂₂₀ H² ρ²), and distortion (W₃₁₁ H³ ρ cosθ). Spherical aberration is symmetric and independent of field; it can be minimized by bending the lens (choosing optimal shape factor) or by using an aspheric surface. Coma varies linearly with field and produces comet‑like blur; it is corrected by satisfying the Abbe sine condition or by symmetric doublet design. Astigmatism and field curvature are linked through the Petzval sum Σ (Φi/ni), where Φi is surface power; a flat field requires zero Petzval sum, achieved by combining positive and negative lenses of different refractive indices. Distortion does not blur the image but displaces points radially; barrel (negative) and pincushion (positive) distortion are corrected by placing the stop at the center of symmetry or by using a symmetric lens pair. Higher‑order aberrations (fifth‑order, seventh‑order) become significant in fast (low f‑number) systems and wide‑field designs; they are handled by numerical ray tracing and optimization algorithms (damped least squares). Wavefront error is often expressed in Zernike polynomials Zn⁽ᵐ⁾(ρ,θ), which are orthogonal over the unit circle; the root‑mean‑square (RMS) wavefront error σW relates to Strehl ratio S ≈ exp[–(2πσW/λ)²] (Maréchal approximation). Adaptive optics uses deformable mirrors (DM) with actuators to cancel measured wavefront errors; typical DM stroke ~10 µm, actuator pitch 0.5–2 mm. In lithography, projection optics achieve < λ/20 RMS wavefront error across a 26 mm × 33 mm field at 193 nm, requiring extensive aspheric correction and rigorous thermal control. Freeform surfaces, described by XY polynomials or NURBS, enable compact off‑axis systems with no rotational symmetry, increasingly used in head‑mounted displays and illumination optics.

Nonlinear Optics: Second‑Harmonic Generation, Phase Matching, and Optical Parametric Oscillators

Nonlinear polarization P(2) = ε₀ χ⁽²⁾ E E drives second‑harmonic generation (SHG) where two photons at frequency ω combine to produce one photon at 2ω. The coupled‑wave equations for plane waves in a lossless medium give the SHG intensity I(2ω) = (2ω² deff² L² / (nω² n2ω c³ ε₀)) I(ω)² sinc²(Δk L/2), where deff = χ⁽²⁾/2 is the effective nonlinear coefficient, L is interaction length, and Δk = k(2ω) – 2k(ω) is the phase‑mismatch. Perfect phase matching (Δk = 0) yields quadratic power scaling; otherwise coherence length Lc = π/|Δk| limits conversion. Birefringent phase matching exploits the difference between ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices: Type I (oo→e) and Type II (oe→e) configurations in negative uniaxial crystals (e.g., BBO, KDP) satisfy ne(2ω,θ) = no(ω). The phase‑matching angle θpm is found from 1/ne²(θ) = cos²θ/no² + sin²θ/ne². Quasi‑phase matching (QPM) periodically poling the crystal (period Λ = 2π/Δk) reverses the sign of deff every coherence length, allowing first‑order QPM with effective coefficient deff = (2/π) d33. Typical QPM periods in PPLN for 1064 nm → 532 nm conversion are ~7 µm. Optical parametric oscillators (OPOs) use a χ⁽²⁾ crystal in a cavity to convert a pump photon (ωp) into signal (ωs) and idler (ωi) photons satisfying ωp = ωs + ωi and momentum conservation. The oscillation threshold pump power Pth = (π² np ns ni λs λi V) / (8 deff² Qs Qi L²), where V is mode volume and Q are cavity quality factors. Singly resonant OPOs (SRO) resonate only the signal; doubly resonant OPOs (DRO) resonate both signal and idler, lowering threshold but requiring tighter cavity length control. Broadly tunable OPOs based on PPLN cover 1.4–4.5 µm with pump at 1064 nm. Third‑order nonlinearity χ⁽³⁾ gives rise to Kerr effect, third‑harmonic generation, and four‑wave mixing; the nonlinear index n₂ relates to χ⁽³⁾ via n₂ = 3 χ⁽³⁾/(4 n₀² ε₀ c). In silica fibers n₂ ≈ 2.6 × 10⁻²⁰ m²/W, enabling supercontinuum generation in photonic‑crystal fibers pumped by femtosecond pulses.

  • Confusing the sign convention for phase‑matching angle in negative versus positive uniaxial crystals.
  • Assuming the coherence length Lc = π/Δk applies to QPM; QPM uses the full crystal length with periodic reversal, not a single coherence length.
  • Neglecting walk‑off (Poynting vector deviation) in birefringent SHG, which limits effective aperture and conversion efficiency.
  • Using the small‑signal gain formula for an OPO above threshold without accounting for pump depletion and saturation.
  • Treating the Seidel coefficients as independent of wavelength; they scale with λ⁻¹ for transverse aberrations and λ⁻² for wavefront error.
  • Forgetting that the Strehl ratio approximation S ≈ exp[–(2πσ/λ)²] is valid only for σ/λ < 0.1 (Maréchal criterion).
  • Mixing the normalized frequency V definition for step‑index fiber with the graded‑index mode count formula M ≈ V²/4.
