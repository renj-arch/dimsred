Elasticity of Solids

1.1 Stress and Strain

When a solid body is subjected to external forces, internal forces develop that resist deformation; the intensity of these internal forces per unit area is called stress, measured in pascals (Pa) or newtons per square metre. Stress can be normal, acting perpendicular to a surface, or shear, acting parallel to it, and the resulting deformation is quantified by strain, a dimensionless ratio of change in dimension to the original dimension. For a uniform rod pulled along its axis, the normal stress equals the applied force divided by the cross‑sectional area, while the longitudinal strain equals the change in length divided by the original length. The linear relationship between stress and strain for small deformations is the foundation of Hooke’s law, which states that stress is directly proportional to strain within the elastic limit of the material.

Because stress and strain are defined at a point, they allow engineers to predict how a component will behave under complex loading by integrating the local response over the whole volume. The proportionality constant in Hooke’s law is the elastic modulus, whose value depends on the type of stress applied; thus a single material possesses several moduli, each describing resistance to a specific mode of deformation.

1.2 Young’s Modulus

Young’s modulus, denoted by Y, is the ratio of normal stress to longitudinal strain for a material stretched or compressed along one axis while the lateral surfaces are free to contract or expand. It quantifies the stiffness of a solid in tension or compression and is expressed in pascals. A high Young’s modulus indicates that a large stress produces only a small strain, which is characteristic of metals such as steel (Y ≈ 2×10¹¹ Pa) compared with polymers (Y ≈ 10⁹ Pa). The modulus is determined experimentally by measuring the extension of a wire of known length and cross‑section under a series of known loads, then plotting stress versus strain and taking the slope of the initial linear portion.

In design, Young’s modulus allows calculation of the elongation of structural members under service loads, ensuring that deflections remain within permissible limits. It also enters the formula for the natural frequency of a vibrating beam, linking static stiffness to dynamic behaviour.

1.3 Shear Modulus and Bulk Modulus

The shear modulus, G, relates shear stress to shear strain and describes the material’s resistance to shape change without volume change; it is essential for analysing torsion of shafts and the propagation of transverse waves. The bulk modulus, K, relates hydrostatic pressure to volumetric strain and measures resistance to uniform compression; it appears in the study of sound waves in fluids and in the compressibility of solids under high pressure. For isotropic materials the three moduli are not independent; they satisfy the relations Y = 2G(1+ν) and Y = 3K(1−2ν), where ν is Poisson’s ratio, the negative ratio of lateral strain to axial strain. Poisson’s ratio typically lies between 0 and 0.5 for stable isotropic solids, with values near 0.3 for many metals.

Understanding the interplay of these moduli enables the prediction of how a material will respond to complex stress states, such as combined tension and torsion, by using the generalized Hooke’s law in tensor form.

1.4 Elastic Limit, Yield Point and Plastic Deformation

The elastic limit is the maximum stress that a material can sustain without permanent deformation; beyond this point the stress–strain curve deviates from linearity and the material enters the plastic regime. The yield point marks the onset of noticeable plastic flow, often accompanied by a drop in stress for mild steel (upper and lower yield points). Once yielded, the material undergoes strain hardening, where further deformation requires increasing stress until the ultimate tensile strength is reached, after which necking leads to fracture. The area under the stress–strain curve up to fracture represents the toughness, a measure of the energy absorbed before failure.

Design codes specify allowable stresses as fractions of the yield strength to ensure that structures remain in the elastic domain under normal service conditions, while the ultimate strength provides a safety margin against catastrophic collapse.

Fluid Statics: Pressure and Buoyancy

2.1 Pressure in a Fluid at Rest

In a fluid at rest, pressure acts equally in all directions at a given point, a consequence of the inability of a static fluid to sustain shear stresses. The pressure at depth h below a free surface increases linearly with depth according to P = P₀ + ρgh, where P₀ is the pressure at the surface, ρ is the fluid density, and g is the acceleration due to gravity. This hydrostatic pressure distribution explains why the force on a submerged horizontal surface is simply the pressure at its centroid times its area, while on an inclined surface the resultant force acts through the centre of pressure, which lies below the centroid because pressure increases with depth.

The concept of gauge pressure, measured relative to atmospheric pressure, and absolute pressure, measured relative to a perfect vacuum, is essential when applying the hydrostatic equation to manometers and barometers. A mercury barometer, for instance, balances atmospheric pressure against a column of mercury about 760 mm high at sea level, providing a direct measurement of P₀.

2.2 Pascal’s Principle and Hydraulic Machines

Pascal’s principle states that a change in pressure applied to an enclosed incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every portion of the fluid and to the walls of its container. This principle underlies hydraulic presses, where a small force applied to a small piston produces a large force on a larger piston, the force multiplication factor being the ratio of the piston areas. The work done on both pistons remains equal (neglecting friction), so the small piston moves a greater distance than the large piston, conserving energy.

Hydraulic brakes in automobiles exploit the same principle: the driver’s foot force on the master cylinder creates a pressure that is transmitted to wheel cylinders, generating the large clamping forces needed to stop the vehicle. The incompressibility of the brake fluid ensures rapid and reliable pressure transmission.

2.3 Archimedes’ Principle and Buoyancy

Archimedes’ principle asserts that a body wholly or partially immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body. The buoyant force acts through the centre of buoyancy, which coincides with the centroid of the displaced volume. For a floating body, the weight of the body equals the buoyant force, leading to the condition that the volume of fluid displaced equals the body’s weight divided by ρg. Stability of a floating object depends on the relative positions of the centre of gravity and the metacentre; if the metacentre lies above the centre of gravity, the equilibrium is stable.

Submarines control buoyancy by adjusting the volume of water in ballast tanks, thereby changing the overall density of the vessel relative to the surrounding water. Hot‑air balloons achieve lift because the heated air inside is less dense than the cooler ambient air, producing a buoyant force that exceeds the total weight of the balloon system.

2.4 Measurement of Pressure: Manometers and Barometers

A simple U‑tube manometer measures pressure differences by balancing the weight of a liquid column against the unknown pressure; the height difference Δh of the liquid columns gives ΔP = ρgΔh. When one limb is open to the atmosphere, the device reads gauge pressure directly. A differential manometer connects two pressure sources, allowing measurement of the pressure drop across a filter or an orifice. The mercury barometer, a sealed tube inverted into a mercury reservoir, provides an absolute pressure reading; the height of the mercury column is proportional to atmospheric pressure, and corrections for temperature and local gravity are applied for precise work.

Modern pressure transducers convert pressure into an electrical signal using piezoelectric or strain‑gauge elements, enabling continuous monitoring in industrial processes and scientific research.

Example
A solid cube of side 0.1 m and density 8000 kg/m³ is fully submerged in water (density 1000 kg/m³). Calculate the apparent weight of the cube when it is suspended from a spring balance while completely immersed.
The volume of the cube is V = (0.1 m)³ = 0.001 m³. Its actual weight is W = mg = ρ_cube V g = 8000 kg/m³ × 0.001 m³ × 9.8 m/s² = 78.4 N. The buoyant force equals the weight of displaced water: F_b = ρ_water V g = 1000 kg/m³ × 0.001 m³ × 9.8 m/s² = 9.8 N. The apparent weight recorded by the spring balance is W_app = W − F_b = 78.4 N − 9.8 N = 68.6 N.
Tip
When solving buoyancy problems, always compute the displaced volume first; the buoyant force depends only on the fluid density and that volume, not on the object’s material. This avoids the common mistake of using the object’s density in the buoyancy formula.

Fluid Dynamics: Bernoulli’s Principle

3.1 Streamlines, Steady Flow and the Continuity Equation

In steady flow the velocity of fluid particles at any fixed point does not change with time, and the path followed by a particle is called a streamline. The continuity equation expresses mass conservation for an incompressible fluid: the product of cross‑sectional area A and velocity v remains constant along a streamtube, A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Consequently, when a pipe narrows, the fluid speed increases, and when it widens, the speed decreases. This relationship is a direct consequence of the fact that the same volume of fluid must pass every cross‑section per unit time in the absence of sources or sinks.

The continuity equation also applies to compressible flows if density variations are included, becoming ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂. In many engineering applications involving liquids, density is treated as constant, simplifying analysis considerably.

3.2 Derivation of Bernoulli’s Equation

Bernoulli’s equation is obtained by applying the work‑energy theorem to a fluid element moving along a streamline in steady, inviscid, incompressible flow. The work done by pressure forces on the element equals the change in its kinetic and potential energy. For two points 1 and 2 on the same streamline, the result is P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂, where P is the static pressure, ρ the density, v the speed, g the gravitational acceleration, and h the elevation above a reference level. Each term has dimensions of pressure; the sum is called the total head and remains constant along a streamline.

The assumptions of inviscid (zero viscosity) and incompressible flow limit the direct applicability of Bernoulli’s equation to idealised situations, but it provides an excellent approximation for many low‑speed aerodynamic and hydrodynamic problems where viscous effects are confined to thin boundary layers.

3.3 Applications of Bernoulli’s Principle

One classic application is the Venturi meter, where a constriction in a pipe causes a pressure drop that can be measured with a manometer; the flow rate is then deduced from the pressure difference using Bernoulli’s equation combined with the continuity equation. Another is the lift on an aerofoil: the faster flow over the upper surface creates a lower pressure relative to the lower surface, generating an upward force. The principle also explains the operation of a carburettor, where air speeding through a narrow throat draws fuel into the airstream, and the phenomenon of a spinning ball curving in flight (Magnus effect), where circulation modifies the velocity distribution and pressure field around the ball.

In medical diagnostics, the Bernoulli principle is used in Doppler ultrasound to estimate pressure gradients across heart valves from measured blood velocities, aiding the assessment of stenosis severity.

3.4 Limitations and Corrections

Real fluids possess viscosity, which causes energy dissipation and a pressure drop along a pipe even when the cross‑section is constant; this is accounted for by the Darcy–Weisbach equation or the Hagen–Poiseuille law for laminar flow. At high speeds, compressibility effects become significant, and the simple Bernoulli form must be replaced by the compressible flow energy equation involving enthalpy. Moreover, Bernoulli’s equation applies only along a streamline; across streamlines the total head can differ if the flow is rotational. In turbulent flow, time‑averaged velocities satisfy a modified Bernoulli relation with an additional turbulent kinetic energy term.

Engineers therefore use Bernoulli’s equation as a first‑order estimate and apply correction factors or resort to computational fluid dynamics for precise predictions in complex geometries.

Viscosity and Laminar Flow

4.1 Definition of Viscosity and Newton’s Law of Viscosity

Viscosity is the property of a fluid that resists relative motion between adjacent layers; it arises from intermolecular momentum transfer in liquids and from molecular collisions in gases. Newton’s law of viscosity states that the shear stress τ between parallel fluid layers is proportional to the velocity gradient dv/dy perpendicular to the layers: τ = η (dv/dy), where η is the dynamic viscosity, measured in pascal‑seconds (Pa·s). Fluids that obey this linear relationship are called Newtonian fluids; water, air, and most common oils are Newtonian over a wide range of shear rates. Non‑Newtonian fluids, such as ketchup or blood, exhibit a shear‑dependent viscosity, requiring more complex constitutive models.

The kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ, with units of square metres per second (m²/s), appears frequently in dimensionless groups such as the Reynolds number, which characterises the relative importance of inertial and viscous forces.

4.2 Laminar Flow in Pipes: Hagen–Poiseuille Law

For steady, fully developed laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid in a circular pipe of radius R, the velocity profile is parabolic: v(r) = (ΔP / 4ηL) (R² − r²), where ΔP is the pressure drop over length L and r is the radial coordinate. The maximum velocity occurs at the centreline (r = 0) and equals twice the average velocity. Integrating the velocity profile over the cross‑section yields the volumetric flow rate Q = πR⁴ΔP / (8ηL), known as the Hagen–Poiseuille law. This law shows that flow rate is extremely sensitive to pipe radius (fourth power), a fact exploited in the design of capillary viscometers and in understanding vascular resistance in physiology.

The pressure drop per unit length is linear with flow rate for laminar flow, in contrast to the quadratic dependence observed in turbulent flow, making laminar regimes desirable for precise fluid metering.

4.3 Reynolds Number and Transition to Turbulence

The Reynolds number Re = ρvD/η (or Re = vD/ν) is a dimensionless quantity that predicts the flow regime in a pipe of diameter D. For Re below approximately 2000 the flow is typically laminar; between 2000 and 4000 it is transitional; above 4000 it becomes turbulent. The transition is not a sharp threshold but depends on surface roughness, inlet conditions, and disturbances. In turbulent flow the velocity profile is flatter near the centre and steeper near the wall, and the friction factor becomes a function of both Re and relative roughness, described by the Moody chart.

Understanding the Reynolds number allows engineers to scale laboratory results to full‑size systems; dynamic similarity requires matching Re (and other relevant dimensionless groups) between model and prototype.

4.4 Viscous Drag and Stokes’ Law

When a small sphere of radius r moves slowly through a viscous fluid, the drag force is given by Stokes’ law: F_d = 6πηrv, valid for Re ≪ 1 (creeping flow). This linear drag law underpins the measurement of viscosity by observing the terminal velocity of a falling sphere in a viscometer. For larger objects or higher speeds, the drag coefficient becomes a function of Re, and the drag force follows the quadratic law F_d = ½ C_d ρ A v², where C_d is the drag coefficient and A the projected area. The transition from Stokes drag to quadratic drag illustrates how viscous effects dominate at low Re while inertial effects dominate at high Re.

In biological systems, the low Reynolds number regime governs the locomotion of microorganisms; they must use non‑reciprocal strokes to achieve net displacement because reciprocal motions produce no net force in Stokes flow (the scallop theorem).

Applications and Problem Solving

5.1 Design of Pressure Vessels Using Elastic Moduli

Thin‑walled pressure vessels such as gas cylinders and boiler drums are analysed by relating the hoop stress and longitudinal stress to the internal pressure, radius, and wall thickness. For a cylindrical vessel the hoop stress σ_h = Pr/t and the longitudinal stress σ_l = Pr/(2t), where P is the gauge pressure, r the internal radius, and t the wall thickness. The material’s yield strength and Young’s modulus determine the allowable pressure and the elastic deformation (change in diameter) under service conditions. Design codes incorporate safety factors and require that the maximum stress remain below the yield strength divided by the factor of safety, while the elastic strain must not exceed limits that would cause leakage or fatigue.

In spherical vessels the stress is uniform and equal to Pr/(2t), making spheres more material‑efficient for a given pressure, but cylindrical shapes are often preferred for manufacturing and space constraints.

5.2 Flow Measurement Devices

Orifice plates, Venturi tubes, and flow nozzles are differential pressure flow meters that rely on Bernoulli’s principle and the continuity equation. The pressure drop across the constriction is measured, and the flow rate is calculated using the discharge coefficient C_d, which accounts for viscous losses and vena contracta effects. The discharge coefficient is determined experimentally and varies with Reynolds number; for a well‑designed Venturi, C_d is close to 0.98, while for a sharp‑edged orifice it is typically 0.6–0.65. Proper installation with sufficient straight pipe runs upstream and downstream ensures fully developed flow and accurate readings.

Ultrasonic and electromagnetic flow meters provide non‑intrusive alternatives, measuring velocity directly without a pressure drop, and are increasingly used in large‑diameter water and wastewater pipelines.

5.3 Lubrication and Bearing Design

Hydrodynamic lubrication relies on the generation of a pressure field in a thin viscous film between moving surfaces, described by the Reynolds equation derived from the Navier–Stokes equations under the lubrication approximation. The load‑carrying capacity of a journal bearing depends on the viscosity, speed, clearance, and geometry; the Sommerfeld number S = (ηN/P)(r/c)² characterises the operating regime, where N is the rotational speed, P the load per unit area, r the journal radius, and c the radial clearance. In the hydrodynamic regime (high S) the surfaces are completely separated by the fluid film, eliminating solid‑to‑solid contact and wear. In the mixed or boundary lubrication regimes (low S) asperity contact occurs, and additives in the lubricant form protective films.

Viscosity index improvers are added to oils to reduce the variation of viscosity with temperature, ensuring adequate film thickness across the operating temperature range of engines and gearboxes.

5.4 Biomedical Fluid Mechanics

Blood flow in arteries is a pulsatile, non‑Newtonian flow in compliant tubes; however, in large arteries the shear rates are high enough that blood behaves approximately as a Newtonian fluid with viscosity around 3–4 mPa·s. The Womersley number α = r√(ωρ/η) characterises the relative importance of unsteady inertial forces to viscous forces; for the aorta α ≈ 15, indicating a flat velocity profile with a thin oscillatory boundary layer. Atherosclerosis alters the geometry, creating stenoses that increase local velocity and shear stress, potentially damaging the endothelium and promoting plaque progression. Computational fluid dynamics combined with patient‑specific imaging is used to predict wall shear stress patterns and assess the risk of rupture.

In the microcirculation, the Fahraeus–Lindqvist effect causes the apparent viscosity to decrease in vessels smaller than about 300 µm because red blood cells migrate toward the centre, leaving a cell‑free plasma layer near the wall. This phenomenon reduces the resistance to flow in capillaries and is essential for efficient oxygen delivery.

The integration of solid mechanics and fluid mechanics principles enables engineers and scientists to analyse and design a vast array of systems, from microscopic biomedical devices to massive hydraulic structures, with confidence grounded in fundamental physical laws.

Non‑Newtonian Fluid Behaviour and Rheological Models

Many engineering fluids deviate from Newton’s linear relation between shear stress τ and shear rate γ̇. In a Newtonian fluid τ = η γ̇ with constant viscosity η. Non‑Newtonian fluids exhibit a shear‑dependent apparent viscosity ηₐ = τ/γ̇. The most common classifications are shear‑thinning (pseudoplastic), shear‑thickening (dilatant), yield‑stress (Bingham plastic, Herschel–Bulkley), and time‑dependent (thixotropic, rheopectic) behaviours.

Power‑law (Ostwald–de Waele) model

For shear‑thinning and shear‑thickening fluids without a yield stress the power‑law τ = K γ̇ⁿ models the behaviour over a wide range. K is the consistency index (Pa·sⁿ) and n the flow behaviour index. n < 1 indicates shear‑thinning (e.g., polymer solutions, blood), n > 1 shear‑thickening (e.g., concentrated cornstarch suspensions). The apparent viscosity ηₐ = K γ̇ⁿ⁻¹ decreases or increases with γ̇ accordingly.

Bingham plastic and Herschel–Bulkley models

Materials that behave as a rigid solid until a yield stress τᵧ is exceeded are described by the Bingham model τ = τᵧ + ηₚ γ̇ for τ > τᵧ, where ηₚ is the plastic viscosity. The Herschel–Bulkley model generalises this to τ = τᵧ + K γ̇ⁿ, allowing a non‑linear post‑yield response. Typical examples include toothpaste, drilling muds, and certain food pastes.

Time‑dependent effects

Thixotropy is a reversible decrease of viscosity with time under constant shear, followed by recovery at rest. Rheopecty is the opposite. These are modelled by structural kinetic equations linking a structure parameter λ to the shear history. In practice a rotational rheometer performs shear‑rate sweeps and step‑change tests to identify the model parameters.

Engineering implications

Pipe‑flow pressure drop for a power‑law fluid in a circular tube of radius R and length L follows the Metzner–Reed generalisation of Hagen–Poiseuille: ΔP = (4L/D) K (8V/D)ⁿ ((3n+1)/(4n))ⁿ, where V is the average velocity. For Bingham plastics the Buckingham–Reiner equation gives the friction factor as a function of the Hedström number He = τᵧ ρ D²/ηₚ². Accurate rheological characterisation is essential for designing pumps, mixers, and pipelines handling slurries, polymer melts, or biological fluids.

Boundary Layer Theory and Separation

When a fluid flows over a solid surface, viscosity confines the velocity gradient to a thin region called the boundary layer. Within this layer the no‑slip condition holds, and the velocity rises from zero at the wall to the free‑stream value U∞. The boundary‑layer thickness δ grows downstream; for a laminar flat‑plate flow the Blasius similarity solution gives δ ≈ 5.0 x / √Reₓ, where Reₓ = ρ U∞ x / η.

Displacement and momentum thickness

The displacement thickness δ* = ∫₀^∞ (1 − u/U∞) dy quantifies the outward shift of the external streamlines caused by the layer. The momentum thickness θ = ∫₀^∞ (u/U∞)(1 − u/U∞) dy measures the momentum deficit. The shape factor H = δ*/θ distinguishes laminar (H ≈ 2.59) from turbulent (H ≈ 1.3–1.4) profiles.

Adverse pressure gradient and separation

An adverse pressure gradient (dp/dx > 0) decelerates the near‑wall fluid. When the wall shear stress τ_w = η (∂u/∂y)₍y=0₎ falls to zero, the flow reverses locally and the boundary layer separates, creating a recirculation zone. The separation criterion for a laminar layer is often expressed as the dimensionless pressure‑gradient parameter λ = (θ²/ν) (dU/dx) with λ_sep ≈ −0.09 (Thwaites method). Turbulent layers tolerate stronger adverse gradients before separating because of enhanced momentum transport.

Control strategies

Boundary‑layer suction, vortex generators, and surface roughness can delay separation. In turbomachinery, blade‑profile design aims to keep the pressure gradient favourable over as much of the suction surface as possible. The Reynolds analogy links skin‑friction coefficient C_f to Stanton number St for heat‑transfer predictions in both laminar and turbulent regimes.

Worked example: laminar separation point on a flat plate with pressure gradient
A laminar boundary layer develops on a flat plate where the external velocity varies as Uₑ(x) = U₀ (1 − x/L) for 0 ≤ x ≤ L. Using Thwaites’ method, estimate the separation location x_sep.
Thwaites’ parameter λ = (θ²/ν) dUₑ/dx. For the given Uₑ, dUₑ/dx = −U₀/L (constant). The momentum thickness for a Falkner–Skan wedge flow with β = −0.5 (adverse gradient) is approximated by θ² = 0.45 ν x / Uₑ. Substituting gives λ = 0.45 (x/Uₑ) (−U₀/L) = −0.45 (x/L) (U₀/Uₑ). Separation occurs when λ = −0.09. Solving −0.45 (x_sep/L) (U₀/Uₑ) = −0.09 yields x_sep/L = 0.2 (Uₑ/U₀). Since Uₑ = U₀ (1 − x_sep/L), iteration gives x_sep ≈ 0.17 L. Thus separation occurs at roughly 17 % of the plate length from the leading edge.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the Blasius δ ≈ 5.0 x/√Reₓ and the Thwaites critical λ = −0.09. When a question asks for separation location, first identify whether the flow is laminar or turbulent; use the appropriate shape factor H to decide which correlation applies. Write the governing integral equation (momentum integral) before substituting numbers — this shows the examiner you understand the physics, not just the formula.

Compressible Flow Fundamentals: Mach Number and Shock Waves

When flow velocities approach or exceed the local speed of sound a, compressibility effects become dominant. The Mach number M = V/a characterises the regime: subsonic (M < 1), transonic (M ≈ 1), supersonic (M > 1), and hypersonic (M ≫ 1). For an ideal gas a = √(γ R T) where γ = cₚ/cᵥ, R is the specific gas constant, and T the absolute temperature.

Isentropic flow relations

For adiabatic, reversible flow of a perfect gas the stagnation (total) conditions are constant along a streamline. The temperature, pressure, and density ratios between static and stagnation states are functions of M alone: T₀/T = 1 + (γ−1)/2 M², p₀/p = (1 + (γ−1)/2 M²)γ/(γ−1), ρ₀/ρ = (1 + (γ−1)/2 M²)1/(γ−1). The area‑Mach relation for a quasi‑one‑dimensional duct is A/A* = (1/M) [(2/(γ+1)) (1 + (γ−1)/2 M²)](γ+1)/(2(γ−1)), where A* is the throat area at M = 1. This relation shows that a convergent‑divergent nozzle can accelerate flow to supersonic speeds only if the throat is choked (M = 1).

Normal shock waves

A normal shock is a thin discontinuity across which flow properties change abruptly while mass, momentum, and energy are conserved. The Rankine–Hugoniot relations for a perfect gas give the downstream Mach number M₂² = [(γ−1)M₁² + 2] / [2γ M₁² − (γ−1)], pressure ratio p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ/(γ+1) (M₁² − 1), and temperature ratio T₂/T₁ = [p₂/p₁] [(ρ₁/ρ₂)]. The total pressure drops across the shock: p₀₂/p₀₁ = [(γ+1)M₁²/((γ−1)M₁²+2)]γ/(γ−1) [(γ+1)/(2γ M₁²−(γ−1))]1/(γ−1). Entropy increases, making the process irreversible.

Oblique shocks and expansion fans

When a supersonic flow encounters a wedge of angle θ, an oblique shock forms at angle β satisfying the θ‑β‑M relation: tan θ = 2 cot β (M₁² sin² β − 1) / [M₁² (γ + cos 2β) + 2]. For a given M₁ and θ there are two possible β (weak and strong shock). The Prandtl–Meyer expansion fan turns the flow away from a convex corner; the turn angle ν(M) = √((γ+1)/(γ−1)) arctan √((γ−1)/(γ+1)(M²−1)) − arctan √(M²−1). The flow remains isentropic through the fan.

Applications

Supersonic inlets, rocket nozzles, and high‑speed aircraft wings rely on these relations. Choked flow limits the mass‑flow rate through a throat: ṁ_max = A* p₀ √(γ/(R T₀)) (2/(γ+1))(γ+1)/(2(γ−1)). Shock‑boundary‑layer interaction can cause separation and unsteady loads, a key design concern for transonic compressors and turbine blades.

Viscoelasticity of Solids: Time‑Dependent Deformation

Many polymeric and biological solids exhibit both elastic and viscous responses. Under a step stress σ₀ the strain ε(t) grows (creep); under a step strain ε₀ the stress relaxes (stress relaxation). Linear viscoelasticity is described by convolution integrals with a relaxation modulus G(t) or creep compliance J(t): σ(t) = ∫₀ᵗ G(t−τ) dε/dτ dτ, ε(t) = ∫₀ᵗ J(t−τ) dσ/dτ dτ.

Standard mechanical models

The Maxwell model (spring E and dashpot η in series) gives stress relaxation G(t) = E e−t/τ with relaxation time τ = η/E, but predicts unlimited creep. The Kelvin–Voigt model (spring and dashpot in parallel) yields creep compliance J(t) = (1/E)(1 − e−t/τ) with τ = η/E, but no stress relaxation. The standard linear solid (Zener model) combines a Maxwell arm in parallel with an additional spring, providing both finite creep and relaxation: G(t) = E_∞ + (E₀ − E_∞) e−t/τ, J(t) = 1/E₀ − (1/E₀ − 1/E_∞) e−t/τ. Here E₀ is the instantaneous modulus, E_∞ the equilibrium modulus.

Dynamic mechanical analysis

Under sinusoidal strain ε = ε₀ eiωt the complex modulus E* = E′ + iE″ relates stress amplitude to strain amplitude.

Thermal Stresses and Thermoelastic Coupling

When a solid undergoes a temperature change ΔT, it tends to expand or contract by the free thermal strain εth = α ΔT, where α is the coefficient of linear thermal expansion. If the body is constrained, mechanical stresses develop to suppress this strain. For an isotropic linear elastic material the constitutive relation is σij = Cijkl(εkl − α ΔT δkl), where Cijkl is the stiffness tensor and δkl the Kronecker delta. In a fully constrained bar the axial stress is σ = −E α ΔT, independent of length. In a biaxially constrained plate the in‑plane stresses are σx = σy = −E α ΔT/(1 − ν). These expressions assume uniform temperature; non‑uniform fields produce bending moments and warping that must be evaluated from the compatibility equations combined with the heat‑conduction equation.

Thermoelastic damping

In vibrating structures, cyclic temperature gradients caused by adiabatic compression and expansion lead to irreversible heat flow and energy dissipation known as thermoelastic damping. The loss factor for a beam in flexure is ηTED = (E α² T₀)/(ρ cp) × (ω τ)/(1 + ω² τ²), where τ = h²/(π² κ) is the thermal diffusion time across the thickness h, κ the thermal diffusivity, and ω the angular frequency. This mechanism sets a fundamental limit on the quality factor of micro‑electromechanical resonators and contributes to thermomechanical noise in atomic force microscopy.

Thermal shock resistance

Rapid quenching generates steep temperature gradients and high transient stresses. The critical temperature difference for crack initiation in a brittle slab is ΔTc = σf (1 − ν)/(E α) × f(geometry), where σf is the fracture strength. Materials with low α, high thermal conductivity, and high fracture toughness (e.g., silicon carbide) exhibit superior thermal shock resistance. Design guidelines often use the Biot number Bi = hc L/k to decide whether surface convection or internal conduction controls the gradient.

Fracture Mechanics: Stress Intensity and Energy Release Rate

Linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM) characterises the singular stress field near a crack tip by the stress‑intensity factor K. For mode I (opening) loading of a through‑thickness crack of length 2a in an infinite plate under remote tension σ, KI = σ √(π a). Finite‑geometry corrections are introduced through a dimensionless factor Y: KI = Y σ √(π a). The energy release rate G, defined as the decrease in potential energy per unit crack‑area advance, is related to K by G = K²/E′ where E′ = E for plane stress and E′ = E/(1 − ν²) for plane strain. Fracture occurs when K reaches the material’s fracture toughness KIc (or G reaches GIc).

R‑curve and stable crack growth

Many ductile and quasi‑brittle materials exhibit rising fracture resistance with crack extension, described by an R‑curve GR(Δa). Stable growth requires dG/da < dGR/d(Δa); instability (pop‑in) occurs when the driving force curve becomes tangent to the R‑curve. This concept is essential for assessing pressure‑vessel integrity where subcritical cracks may grow stably before catastrophic failure.

Mixed‑mode fracture

When modes I, II (in‑plane shear), and III (anti‑plane shear) act simultaneously, an equivalent stress‑intensity factor is sometimes defined as Keq = √(KI² + KII² + KIII²) for isotropic materials. The maximum‑energy‑release‑rate criterion predicts a propagation angle θc given by the Erdogan‑Sih criterion: KI sin θc + KII (3 cos θc − 1) = 0. This predicts the initial propagation direction of a crack under combined loading.

Worked Example: Critical Crack Length in a Pressure Vessel
A thin‑walled cylindrical pressure vessel of radius R = 0.5 m and wall thickness t = 10 mm is made of a steel with fracture toughness KIc = 120 MPa√m and yield strength σy = 450 MPa. The vessel operates at an internal pressure p = 12 MPa. Assuming a semi‑elliptical surface crack of depth a and aspect ratio a/c = 0.5, estimate the maximum allowable crack depth before unstable fracture. Use the geometry factor Y = 1.12 for a surface crack in tension.
The hoop stress in the wall is σθ = pR/t = (12 × 10⁶ Pa)(0.5 m)/(0.01 m) = 600 MPa. This exceeds the yield strength, so the vessel would yield before fracture; however, for a fracture‑mechanics check we use the elastic stress. The stress‑intensity factor is KI = Y σθ √(π a). Setting KI = KIc gives ac = (KIc/(Y σθ))² / π = (120 × 10⁶ / (1.12 × 600 × 10⁶))² / π = (0.1786)² / π = 0.0319 / π = 0.0102 m ≈ 10.2 mm. Since the critical crack depth (10.2 mm) exceeds the wall thickness (10 mm), a through‑thickness crack would not cause immediate fracture under these elastic stresses, though the vessel would have yielded already. In practice the vessel would be designed to keep stresses below yield, and a safety factor on KIc would be applied.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Fracture Mechanics
Memorise the three basic K‑formulas: centre crack (Y = 1), edge crack (Y = 1.12), and penny‑shaped embedded crack (Y = 2/π). When a problem gives a geometry factor, use it directly; do not re‑derive Y. Always check whether plane‑stress or plane‑strain conditions apply — the conversion E′ = E/(1 − ν²) changes G by about 10 % for ν = 0.3 (up to 30 % for ν approaching 0.5). Write the energy‑balance equation G = K²/E′ before substituting numbers; this shows the examiner you understand the thermodynamic basis.

Plasticity and Yield Criteria for Metals

Beyond the elastic limit, metals undergo permanent deformation governed by yield criteria, flow rules, and hardening laws. The von Mises criterion states that yielding begins when the second deviatoric stress invariant J₂ reaches a critical value: √(3 J₂) = σy, where σy is the uniaxial yield stress. In principal stresses this becomes (σ₁ − σ₂)² + (σ₂ − σ₃)² + (σ₃ − σ₁)² = 2 σy². The Tresca criterion uses the maximum shear stress: max(|σ₁ − σ₂|, |σ₂ − σ₃|, |σ₃ − σ₁|) = σy. Von Mises predicts a smooth cylindrical yield surface in principal‑stress space; Tresca gives a hexagonal prism. For most ductile metals von Mises matches experiments more closely.

Associated flow rule and hardening

The plastic strain increment dεijp is normal to the yield surface: dεijp = dλ ∂f/∂σij, where f(σij, κ) = 0 is the yield function and κ a hardening parameter. Isotropic hardening expands the yield surface uniformly: σy = σy0 + H εp (linear) or a power law σy = K εpⁿ. Kinematic hardening translates the surface in stress space (Prager‑Ziegler rule), capturing the Bauschinger effect. Combined hardening models are used in cyclic‑loading simulations such as low‑cycle fatigue.

Limit analysis and collapse loads

Upper‑bound and lower‑bound theorems of plasticity provide rigorous estimates of collapse loads for structures. For a simply supported beam of rectangular cross‑section (width b, height h) under a central point load P, the plastic moment is Mp = σy b h²/4. The collapse load is Pc = 4 Mp/L. These theorems are the basis for plastic design codes in steel structures and for estimating the burst pressure of thick‑walled cylinders using the Tresca criterion: pb = 2 σy ln(ro/ri).

Surface Tension, Capillarity, and Marangoni Effects

Surface tension γ (N/m) arises from cohesive forces at a liquid interface. The Young‑Laplace equation gives the pressure jump across a curved interface: Δp = γ (1/R₁ + 1/R₂), where R₁, R₂ are principal radii of curvature. For a spherical droplet Δp = 2γ/R; for a cylindrical jet Δp = γ/R. Capillary rise in a tube of radius r is h = 2γ cos θ/(ρ g r), where θ is the contact angle. Contact‑angle hysteresis (advancing θa > receding θr) leads to pinning of contact lines and stick‑slip motion.

Marangoni convection

A surface‑tension gradient ∇sγ drives flow from low‑γ to high‑γ regions. For a temperature gradient, ∇sγ = (dγ/dT) ∇sT. The resulting Marangoni number Ma = |dγ/dT| ΔT L/(μ α) compares thermocapillary stress to viscous dissipation (μ dynamic viscosity, α thermal diffusivity). In welding pools and crystal growth, Ma ≫ 1 produces vigorous surface‑driven flow that alters heat and mass transfer. Surfactants reduce γ and can immobilise the interface (Gibbs elasticity), suppressing Marangoni flow.

Dynamic wetting and droplet impact

When a droplet of radius R impacts a solid at velocity V, the Weber number We = ρ V² R/γ and Reynolds number Re = ρ V R/μ govern the outcome. For We < 10 the droplet spreads and recedes; for 10 < We < 100 a thin lamella forms and may splash; for We > 100 prompt splashing with secondary droplets occurs. The maximum spread factor βmax = Dmax/(2R) scales as βmax ∼ We1/4 Re1/5 in the inertial‑capillary regime. These correlations are used in inkjet printing, spray cooling, and pesticide application.

Turbulence Modeling: Reynolds‑Averaged Equations and Closure Models

Turbulent flow is described by the Reynolds‑averaged Navier‑Stokes (RANS) equations: ∂(ρ ūi)/∂xi = 0 and ρ ūj ∂ūi/∂xj = −∂p̄/∂xi + ∂/∂xj[μ (∂ūi/∂xj + ∂ūj/∂xi) − ρ u'iu'j]. The Reynolds stress tensor −ρ u'iu'j must be modelled. The Boussinesq eddy‑viscosity hypothesis assumes −ρ u'iu'j = μt(∂ūi/∂xj + ∂ūj/∂xi) − ⅔ ρ k δij, where k = ½ u'iu'i is the turbulent kinetic energy and μt = ρ Cμ k²/ε.

Two‑equation models

The standard k‑ε model solves transport equations for k and its dissipation rate ε. The k‑ω model uses specific dissipation ω = ε/k and performs better near walls. The SST (shear‑stress‑transport) model blends k‑ω in the inner layer with k‑ε in the outer layer, providing accurate separation prediction for adverse‑pressure‑gradient flows. Low‑Reynolds‑number variants integrate to the wall without wall functions, resolving the viscous sublayer.

Reynolds‑stress models and LES

Reynolds‑stress models (RSM) solve transport equations for each component of u'iu'j, capturing anisotropy but at higher computational cost. Large‑eddy simulation (LES) resolves the energy‑containing eddies and models only the subgrid scales via a filter width Δ. The Smagorinsky model gives μt = ρ (Cs Δ)² |S̄|. Wall‑modelled LES (WMLES) reduces the near‑wall resolution requirement, making high‑Reynolds‑number external aerodynamics feasible on modern clusters.

  • Assuming thermal stress is simply E α ΔT without checking constraint conditions — a partially constrained body develops lower stress.
  • Using plane‑stress fracture toughness Kc for a thick component that is in plane strain; the correct value is the plane‑strain fracture toughness KIc, which is lower.
  • Applying the von Mises criterion to brittle materials (ceramics, concrete) where the maximum‑principal‑stress or Mohr‑Coulomb criterion is appropriate.
  • Neglecting contact‑angle hysteresis in capillary‑rise problems; the equilibrium height lies between the advancing and receding limits.
  • Using the standard k‑ε model for flows with strong curvature, rotation, or separation without validation — SST or RSM is usually required.
  • Confusing the Marangoni number with the Rayleigh number; Ma drives surface‑tension‑driven flow, Ra drives buoyancy‑driven convection.
  • Treating the plastic moment Mp as the elastic moment capacity; Mp = 1.5 My for a rectangular section, a factor often missed in quick calculations.

Acoustic Wave Propagation in Fluids and Solids

Sound propagates as a mechanical disturbance governed by the linearized wave equation. In a fluid the compressional wave speed is c = √(K/ρ), where K is the adiabatic bulk modulus and ρ the density. For an ideal gas K = γ p giving c = √(γ R T / M). In an isotropic solid two independent wave speeds exist: the longitudinal (P‑wave) speed cₗ = √[(λ+2μ)/ρ] and the shear (S‑wave) speed cₛ = √(μ/ρ), with Lamé constants λ, μ. The acoustic impedance Z = ρ c governs reflection and transmission at a planar interface; the pressure reflection coefficient is R = (Z₂−Z₁)/(Z₂+Z₁) and the transmission coefficient T = 2 Z₂/(Z₂+Z₁). Attenuation in fluids arises from classical viscosity and thermal conduction (absorption ∝ ω²) and from molecular relaxation processes that introduce a frequency‑dependent complex bulk modulus. In solids, attenuation is due to thermoelastic damping, dislocation motion, and grain‑boundary scattering. Practical uses include ultrasonic non‑destructive testing (0.5–10 MHz), medical imaging (2–18 MHz), and sonar (10–100 kHz). The wavelength λ = c/f determines spatial resolution; for steel (cₗ ≈ 5900 m/s) a 5 MHz probe yields λ ≈ 1.2 mm.

Cavitation and Bubble Dynamics

Cavitation occurs when the local pressure in a liquid falls below its vapour pressure pᵥ, causing vapour‑filled cavities to nucleate, grow, and collapse. The dimensionless cavitation number σ = (p∞−pᵥ)/(½ ρ V²) characterises the propensity for cavitation in a flow of speed V and far‑field pressure p∞. The dynamics of a single spherical bubble of radius R(t) are described by the Rayleigh–Plesset equation:
ρ (R R̈ + ³/₂ Ṙ²) = p_g(R) − p∞(t) − 2γ/R − 4μ Ṙ/R,
where p_g(R) is the gas pressure inside the bubble (often modelled as polytropic p_g = p₀ (R₀/R)3κ), γ surface tension, μ liquid viscosity. During inertial collapse the last term is negligible and the collapse time for an empty cavity in an inviscid liquid is t_c ≈ 0.915 R₀ √(ρ/Δp). The collapse generates pressures of order Δp ∼ ρ (R₀/t_c)² and temperatures on the order of 10⁴ K, producing sonoluminescence. Two regimes are distinguished: inertial cavitation (violent collapse, σ < 1) and stable cavitation (oscillatory bubbles, σ > 1). Cavitation erosion damages pump impellers, propeller blades, and hydraulic valves; controlled cavitation is exploited in ultrasonic cleaning and shock‑wave lithotripsy.

Worked Example: Cavitation Number and Collapse Time
A water jet issues at 30 m/s into a tank where the ambient pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and the vapour pressure of water at 20 °C is 2.3 × 10³ Pa. The density of water is 998 kg/m³. (a) Compute the cavitation number σ. (b) If a spherical vapour bubble of initial radius 0.5 mm forms, estimate its inertial collapse time using the Rayleigh formula.
(a) σ = (p∞−pᵥ) / (½ ρ V²) = (1.0×10⁵ − 2.3×10³) / (0.5 × 998 × 30²) = 9.77×10⁴ / (0.5 × 998 × 900) = 9.77×10⁴ / 4.491×10⁵ ≈ 0.217. (b) Δp ≈ p∞−pᵥ = 9.77×10⁴ Pa. Collapse time t_c ≈ 0.915 R₀ √(ρ/Δp) = 0.915 × 5×10⁻⁴ × √(998 / 9.77×10⁴) = 4.575×10⁻⁴ × √(0.01022) = 4.575×10⁻⁴ × 0.1011 ≈ 4.6×10⁻⁵ s = 46 µs.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the cavitation number definition and the Rayleigh collapse‑time scaling (t_c ∝ R₀ √(ρ/Δp)). In multiple‑choice questions the examiner often swaps ρ and Δp or omits the 0.915 factor; recognise the correct proportionality. For Rayleigh–Plesset, write the full equation once, then identify which terms dominate for the asked regime (inertial vs. viscous).

Porous Media Flow and Darcy’s Law

Flow through a rigid porous matrix at low Reynolds number obeys Darcy’s law: the volumetric flux q (m³/s per unit cross‑section) is proportional to the pressure gradient, q = −(K/μ) ∇p, where K (m²) is the intrinsic permeability and μ the dynamic viscosity. For a packed bed of spherical grains of diameter d and porosity φ, the Kozeny–Carman relation gives K = φ³ d² / [180 (1−φ)²]. When the pore‑scale Reynolds number Re_p = ρ q d / (μ φ) exceeds ≈ 1–10, inertial corrections become significant and the Forchheimer equation ∇p = −(μ/K) q − β ρ q² is used, with β an empirical coefficient. The effective stress principle states that the total stress σ_total in a saturated porous solid is shared between the solid skeleton and the pore fluid: σ_total = σ' + α p I, where σ' is the effective stress, α the Biot coefficient (≈ 1 for most soils), and p the pore pressure. Applications include groundwater modelling, petroleum reservoir simulation, and design of filtration media. In a confined aquifer of thickness b and transmissivity T = K b, the Theis solution gives drawdown s(r,t) = (Q/4πT) W(u) with u = r² S/(4Tt) and W(u) the well function.

Contact Mechanics: Hertzian Theory and Applications

When two elastic bodies with curved surfaces are pressed together, the contact area is small compared with the body dimensions and the pressure distribution follows Hertz theory. For a sphere of radius R on a flat plane, the contact radius a = (3 P R / 4 E*)1/3, where P is the normal load and E* = [ (1−ν₁²)/E₁ + (1−ν₂²)/E₂ ]-1 the combined modulus. The pressure profile is p(r) = p₀ √(1 − r²/a²) with maximum p₀ = 3P/(2π a²). The subsurface stress field shows a maximum shear stress at depth z ≈ 0.48 a for a sphere, a key factor in rolling‑contact fatigue. For elliptical contacts (e.g., ball‑on‑raceway) the semi‑axes a, b are obtained from the curvature sum and difference; the pressure becomes p(x,y) = p₀ √(1 − x²/a² − y²/b²). Adhesion modifies the contact: the Johnson–Kendall–Roberts (JKR) model adds a surface‑energy term γ giving a larger contact radius at zero load, while the Derjaguin–Muller–Toporov (DMT) model treats adhesion as a force acting outside the contact area. Rough surfaces are described by the Greenwood–Williamson statistical model, where the real contact area is the sum of many micro‑asperity Hertz contacts. These results underpin bearing life calculations (ISO 281), gear tooth stress analysis, and nano‑indentation hardness testing.

Fatigue, Creep, and Time‑Dependent Failure of Solids

Cyclic loading below the monotonic yield strength can cause fatigue fracture after many cycles. The stress‑life (S‑N) curve for high‑cycle fatigue is often expressed by Basquin’s law: σ_a = σ_f' (2 N_f)^b, where σ_a is the stress amplitude, σ_f' the fatigue strength coefficient, b the fatigue strength exponent (typically −0.05 to −0.12), and N_f the number of reversals to failure. For variable‑amplitude loading, Miner’s linear damage rule sums the cycle ratios: ∑ (n_i / N_i) = 1. Creep is time‑dependent plastic deformation at elevated temperature (typically > 0.4 T_m). The steady‑state creep rate follows Norton’s power law: ε̇ = A σ^n exp(−Q/RT), with stress exponent n (3–5 for dislocation creep, 1 for diffusion creep), activation energy Q, and gas constant R. Creep curves exhibit three stages: primary (decelerating strain rate), secondary (constant ε̇), and tertiary (accelerating to rupture). The Larson–Miller parameter P = T (C + log t_r) correlates rupture time t_r across temperatures. Fatigue‑creep interaction is critical in gas‑turbine blades; a common design approach uses a damage‑summation diagram where fatigue damage and creep damage are each limited to a fraction of unity. Time‑temperature superposition allows construction of master curves for viscoelastic polymers, shifting data at different temperatures along the log‑frequency axis using the Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) equation.

  • Confusing the acoustic impedance of a solid (ρ cₗ) with that of a fluid (ρ c) when computing reflection coefficients at a solid–fluid interface.
  • Using the Rayleigh collapse time formula for a bubble that contains a significant amount of non‑condensable gas; the gas pressure term in Rayleigh–Plesset then dominates and lengthens the collapse.
  • Applying Darcy’s law beyond its low‑Reynolds‑number validity without the Forchheimer correction, leading to under‑prediction of pressure drop in high‑velocity filtration.
  • Assuming the Hertzian contact pressure distribution remains valid when the contact radius approaches the radius of curvature of the bodies; the half‑space assumption breaks down.
  • Treating Miner’s rule as exact for all load spectra; sequence effects (e.g., overloads) can cause crack‑closure or residual‑stress interactions that violate linear damage accumulation.
  • Neglecting the temperature dependence of the fatigue strength coefficient σ_f' when extrapolating S‑N data to service temperatures.
  • Using the plane‑stress fracture toughness K_c for a thick component that is in plane strain; the correct value is the plane‑strain fracture toughness K_Ic, which is lower.
