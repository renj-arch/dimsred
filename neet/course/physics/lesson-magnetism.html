1. Moving Charges & Magnetism

Moving charges (electric currents) are the fundamental source of magnetic fields. This section explores how currents produce magnetic fields (Biot-Savart and Ampere's laws), how magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges (Lorentz force), and the application of these principles in devices like the cyclotron. For NEET, expect 3-4 questions from this chapter, primarily numericals on magnetic field calculations and force on charged particles.

Biot-Savart Law

The Biot-Savart law gives the differential magnetic field dB produced at a point by a small current element I dl. It is the magnetic analogue of Coulomb's law in electrostatics.

|dB| = (μ0 / 4π) · I |dl × r| / r3 = (μ0 / 4π) · I dl sinθ / r2

where μ0 = 4π × 10-7 Tm/A is the permeability of free space, dl is the length element in the direction of current, r is the distance from the element to the observation point, and θ is the angle between dl and r. The direction of dB is given by the cross product (dl × r) using the right-hand rule.

Key Results Derived from Biot-Savart Law:

  • Infinite straight wire: B = μ0I / (2πr) — field lines are concentric circles around the wire
  • Finite straight wire: B = μ0I (sinθ1 + sinθ2) / (4πr)
  • Circular loop (centre): B = μ0I / (2R); for N turns: B = μ0NI / (2R)
  • Circular loop (axial point): B = μ0IR2 / [2(R2 + x2)3/2]
  • Arc of a circle (centre): B = μ0Iθ / (4πR) where θ is in radians
Key Tip: Right-Hand Thumb Rule
Point your right thumb along the direction of conventional current. Your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field lines encircling the conductor. For a circular loop, curl your fingers along the current direction and your thumb points in the direction of B at the centre. This is the fastest way to determine field direction in MCQs.
Example 1: Field at Centre of Circular Coil
A circular coil of radius 10 cm has 50 turns and carries 2 A current. Find the magnetic field at its centre. (μ0 = 4π × 10-7 Tm/A)

a) 2π × 10-4 T   b) 4π × 10-4 T   c) 6.28 × 10-4 T   d) 8π × 10-4 T
Solution: B = μ0NI / (2R) = (4π × 10-7 × 50 × 2) / (2 × 0.10) = (4π × 10-5) / 0.20 = 2π × 10-4 T. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 2: Magnetic Field of an Arc
A wire carrying 3 A current is bent into an arc of radius 15 cm subtending 60° at the centre. Find B at the centre.

a) 2.09 × 10-6 T   b) 4.19 × 10-6 T   c) 6.28 × 10-6 T   d) 8.38 × 10-6 T
Solution: B = μ0Iθ/(4πR). θ = 60° = π/3 rad. B = (4π × 10-7 × 3 × π/3) / (4π × 0.15) = (4π × 10-7 × π) / (4π × 0.15) = (π × 10-7) / 0.15 = 2.09 × 10-6 T. Option (a).

Ampere's Law

Ampere's circuital law relates the line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop to the current passing through the loop. It is the magnetic analogue of Gauss's law in electrostatics.

∮ B · dl = μ0 Ienc

Ampere's law is useful for calculating B only when the current distribution has sufficient symmetry (cylindrical, planar, or solenoidal). The choice of Amperian loop is crucial for successful application.

Configuration Magnetic Field (B) Notes
Infinite straight wire μ0I / (2πr) r = perpendicular distance from wire
Ideal solenoid μ0nI (inside); B = 0 (outside) n = N/L = turns per unit length
Toroid μ0NI / (2πr) N = total turns, r = radial distance
Infinite current sheet μ0K / 2 K = current per unit width
Cylindrical conductor (inside) μ0Ir / (2πR2) r < R, R = radius of conductor
Exam Tip: Choosing an Amperian Loop
Select a loop where B is constant in magnitude along the path and B · dl simplifies to B · dl or zero. For wires use circles, for solenoids use rectangles, for toroids use concentric circles. The loop must pass through the point where B is to be determined.
Example 3: Magnetic Field Inside a Solenoid
A solenoid of length 50 cm has 1000 turns and carries 3 A current. Find B inside the solenoid (assume ideal).

a) 2.4π × 10-3 T   b) 4.8π × 10-3 T   c) 7.2π × 10-3 T   d) 9.6π × 10-3 T
Solution: n = N/L = 1000/0.50 = 2000 turns/m. B = μ0nI = 4π × 10-7 × 2000 × 3 = 4π × 10-7 × 6000 = 24π × 10-4 = 2.4π × 10-3 T. Option (a).
Example 4: Field Inside a Cylindrical Conductor
A cylindrical conductor of radius 4 cm carries a uniformly distributed current of 10 A. Find B at a distance 2 cm from the axis.

a) 2.5 × 10-5 T   b) 5.0 × 10-5 T   c) 1.25 × 10-5 T   d) 7.5 × 10-5 T
Solution: For r < R: B = μ0Ir/(2πR2) = (4π × 10-7 × 10 × 0.02)/(2π × (0.04)2) = (8π × 10-8)/(3.2π × 10-3) = 2.5 × 10-5 T. Option (a).

Force on Moving Charges

A charged particle moving in a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the magnetic field. This is the fundamental principle behind electric motors, mass spectrometers, and particle accelerators. Since the force is always perpendicular to velocity, it does no work and can only change the direction of motion, not the speed.

Lorentz Force

The total electromagnetic force on a charged particle is:

F = q(E + v × B)

For purely magnetic field (E = 0): F = qvB sinθ. The direction is perpendicular to both v and B (right-hand rule for positive charges).

Key Results for Motion in a Uniform Magnetic Field:

  • v ⊥ B: Circular motion, radius r = mv/(qB), time period T = 2πm/(qB)
  • v ∥ B: No force, particle continues in straight line
  • v at an angle θ: Helical path with radius r = mvsinθ/(qB), pitch p = (2πmvcosθ)/(qB)
  • Angular frequency (cyclotron): ω = qB/m (independent of velocity!)

Force on Current-Carrying Conductor:

  • Straight conductor: F = I(L × B), magnitude F = ILB sinθ
  • Force per unit length between parallel wires: F/L = μ0I1I2/(2πd) — like currents attract, opposite currents repel
  • Torque on a current loop: τ = NIAB sinθ = mB sinθ where m = NIA is the magnetic moment
Key Tip: Fleming's Left-Hand Rule
For a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field: extend left hand with thumb, first finger, and second finger mutually perpendicular. First finger = B field (North to South), Second finger = I (current), Thumb = F (force/motion). Mnemonic: FBI — Field, Current, Motion/Force.
Example 5: Charged Particle in Magnetic Field
A proton (m = 1.67 × 10-27 kg, q = 1.6 × 10-19 C) enters B = 0.5 T with v = 4 × 106 m/s perpendicular to B. Find the radius of its circular path.

a) 6.68 cm   b) 8.35 cm   c) 10.44 cm   d) 12.52 cm
Solution: r = mv/(qB) = (1.67 × 10-27 × 4 × 106) / (1.6 × 10-19 × 0.5) = (6.68 × 10-21) / (8 × 10-20) = 0.0835 m = 8.35 cm. Option (b).
Example 6: Force Between Parallel Wires
Two long parallel wires separated by 5 cm carry 4 A and 6 A in the same direction. Find the force per unit length between them.

a) 9.6 × 10-5 N/m (attractive)   b) 4.8 × 10-5 N/m (repulsive)
c) 9.6 × 10-5 N/m (repulsive)   d) 4.8 × 10-5 N/m (attractive)
Solution: F/L = μ0I1I2/(2πd) = (4π × 10-7 × 4 × 6) / (2π × 0.05) = (96π × 10-7) / (0.1π) = 9.6 × 10-5 N/m. Same direction currents attract. Option (a).

Cyclotron

A cyclotron accelerates charged particles (protons, deuterons, α-particles) to high energies using a uniform magnetic field and a high-frequency alternating electric field. It consists of two D-shaped electrodes (dees) placed between the poles of a strong electromagnet.

Working Principle: A particle is injected at the centre. The magnetic field bends it into a circular path. Each time it crosses the gap between the dees, the alternating electric field accelerates it. The radius increases with energy, but the time period T = 2πm/(qB) remains constant — this resonance condition is the key to cyclotron operation.

Key Formulas:

  • Cyclotron frequency: f = qB/(2πm); angular frequency ω = qB/m
  • Maximum KE: Kmax = q2B2R2/(2m) — where R is the dee radius
  • Maximum velocity: vmax = qBR/m
Important: Cyclotron Limitation
Cyclotrons cannot accelerate electrons because relativistic effects (mass increase: m = m0/√(1 - v²/c²)) disrupt the resonance condition at modest energies. Synchrotrons and linear accelerators are used for electrons and ultra-high-energy particles.
Example 7: Cyclotron Frequency Calculation
A cyclotron has B = 1.2 T. Find the cyclotron frequency for deuterons (m = 3.34 × 10-27 kg, q = 1.6 × 10-19 C).

a) 9.15 MHz   b) 18.3 MHz   c) 27.45 MHz   d) 36.6 MHz
Solution: f = qB/(2πm) = (1.6 × 10-19 × 1.2) / (2π × 3.34 × 10-27) = (1.92 × 10-19) / (2.098 × 10-26) = 9.15 × 106 Hz = 9.15 MHz. Option (a).

2. Magnetism & Matter

This chapter explores how materials respond to magnetic fields, the origin of Earth's magnetism, and the concept of magnetic moment. Understanding magnetic materials is crucial for NEET as questions often test the classification and properties of dia, para, and ferro magnetic substances.

Magnetic Materials

When a material is placed in an external magnetic field, it acquires a magnetic moment. The magnetization M and magnetic field B inside the material are related by: B = μ0(H + M), where H is the magnetising field. The magnetic susceptibility χm = M/H and relative permeability μr = 1 + χm.

Memory Aid: Magnetic Materials
CUBO + GAS — Copper, Water, Bismuth, Gold, Zinc are Diamagnetic. Aluminium, Platinum, Oxygen are Paramagnetic. Iron, Cobalt, Nickel are Ferromagnetic. Diamagnetic materials have all electrons paired; paramagnetic have unpaired electrons.
Example 8: Curie's Law Application
A paramagnetic material has χm = 3 × 10-4 at 300 K. Find χm at 600 K.

a) 1.5 × 10-4   b) 3 × 10-4   c) 6 × 10-4   d) 9 × 10-4
Solution: Curie's law: χ ∝ 1/T. χ2/χ1 = T1/T2. χ2 = 3 × 10-4 × (300/600) = 1.5 × 10-4. Option (a).

Earth's Magnetism

The Earth behaves like a giant magnet with its magnetic field lines emerging from the southern hemisphere and entering the northern hemisphere. The magnetic axis is inclined at about 11.5° to the geographic axis. Key elements of Earth's magnetic field:

  • Declination (θ): Angle between geographic meridian and magnetic meridian
  • Inclination/Dip (δ): Angle made by Earth's total field BE with the horizontal
  • Horizontal component: BH = BE cosδ
  • Vertical component: BV = BE sinδ

At the magnetic equator: δ = 0°, BV = 0, BH = BE. At magnetic poles: δ = 90°, BH = 0, BV = BE. Typical Earth's field strength BE ≈ 0.32 × 10-4 T at the equator.

Important: Magnetic vs Geographic Poles
The Earth's magnetic south pole is near the geographic north pole (Canada region). The north pole of a compass needle points toward geographic north because it is attracted to the magnetic south pole. This is a common NEET trick question — remember: opposites attract!
Example 9: Calculating Angle of Dip
At a location, BH = 0.28 × 10-4 T and BV = 0.28√3 × 10-4 T. Find the angle of dip.

a) 30°   b) 45°   c) 60°   d) 75°
Solution: tanδ = BV/BH = (0.28√3)/(0.28) = √3. Therefore δ = tan-1(√3) = 60°. Option (c).

Magnetic Moment

The magnetic moment measures the strength of a magnetic source. For a planar current loop: m = NIA (magnitude m = NIA). The direction of A (area vector) follows the right-hand rule: curl fingers along current, thumb gives direction of m.

Torque on a magnetic dipole in a uniform B: τ = m × B (τ = mB sinθ). Potential energy: U = -m · B = -mB cosθ. Minimum energy (stable equilibrium) at θ = 0°; maximum energy at θ = 180°.

Example 10: Magnetic Moment of a Coil
A circular coil of radius 5 cm has 100 turns and carries 0.5 A current. Find its magnetic moment.

a) 0.393 Am2   b) 0.196 Am2   c) 0.785 Am2   d) 0.098 Am2
Solution: m = NIA = 100 × 0.5 × π(0.05)2 = 100 × 0.5 × π × 25 × 10-4 = 1250π × 10-4 = 0.393 Am2. Option (a).

3. Electromagnetic Induction

Electromagnetic induction is the phenomenon of generating an emf across a conductor when it experiences a changing magnetic field. This principle is at the heart of electric generators, transformers, and inductors. For NEET, this chapter typically contributes 2-3 questions on flux calculations, induced emf, and inductance.

Faraday's Laws

Michael Faraday discovered that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an emf. First Law: Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an induced emf is produced. Second Law: The magnitude of induced emf equals the rate of change of magnetic flux:

ε = -dΦB/dt   and   ε = -N dΦB/dt (for N turns)

Magnetic flux: ΦB = B · A = BA cosθ. Flux can change by: (i) changing B, (ii) changing area A, (iii) changing angle θ (rotation), or (iv) moving the loop in/out of the field.

Important: Induced Charge Formula
The total charge that flows through a circuit due to induced emf is Q = NΔΦ/R, which is independent of the time taken! This is a favourite NEET concept. Q depends only on the total change in flux and the circuit resistance, not on the rate of change.
Example 11: Induced EMF Calculation
A coil of 200 turns has its magnetic flux changing from 0.03 Wb to 0.08 Wb in 0.5 s. Find the magnitude of induced emf.

a) 10 V   b) 15 V   c) 20 V   d) 25 V
Solution: |ε| = N dΦ/dt = 200 × (0.08 - 0.03)/0.5 = 200 × 0.05/0.5 = 200 × 0.1 = 20 V. Option (c).
Example 12: Charge Flow Due to Induction
A coil of resistance 10 Ω has 50 turns. The flux through each turn changes from 0.02 Wb to 0.12 Wb. Find the total charge that flows through the circuit.

a) 0.25 C   b) 0.50 C   c) 0.75 C   d) 1.00 C
Solution: Q = NΔΦ/R = 50 × (0.12 - 0.02)/10 = 50 × 0.10/10 = 0.5 C. Option (b).

Lenz's Law

Lenz's law gives the direction of the induced current: The induced current always flows in such a direction as to oppose the change in magnetic flux that caused it. The negative sign in Faraday's law (ε = -dΦ/dt) is the mathematical expression of Lenz's law, which follows from the conservation of energy.

Applications: When a magnet approaches a coil, the induced current creates a repelling pole (opposes approach). When a magnet moves away, the induced current creates an attracting pole (opposes recession). Eddy currents in conductors oppose relative motion (electromagnetic braking in trains).

Quick Rule: Lenz's Law
The induced current always tries to maintain the original flux. If flux through a loop increases, the induced current creates a field opposing the increase. If flux decreases, it creates a field supporting it. This is simply the law of conservation of energy in electromagnetic form.
Example 13: Lenz's Law Direction
A north pole of a bar magnet is moved towards a closed conducting loop. What is the direction of induced current as seen from the magnet?

a) Clockwise   b) Anticlockwise   c) No current   d) Depends on speed
Solution: As north pole approaches, magnetic flux (directed towards the loop) increases. The induced current opposes this by creating a north pole facing the approaching magnet (repulsion). Using the right-hand rule, this requires anticlockwise current as seen from the magnet. Option (b).

Self & Mutual Inductance

Self-Inductance (L): The induced emf in a coil due to change in its own current: ε = -L dI/dt. For a solenoid: L = μ0n2Al, where n = N/l is turns per unit length, A is cross-sectional area, and l is length. Energy stored in an inductor: U = ½LI2.

Mutual Inductance (M): The induced emf in coil 2 due to change in current in coil 1: ε2 = -M dI1/dt. For two coaxial solenoids: M = μ0n1n2A l. The coupling coefficient k = M/√(L1L2) ranges from 0 to 1.

Induced emf ε = -L dI/dt ε2 = -M dI1/dt
Energy stored U = ½LI2 U = ½L1I12 + ½L2I22 ± MI1I2

NEET Shortcut: Motional EMF
For a conducting rod of length L moving with velocity v perpendicular to B: ε = BvL. Direction is given by Fleming's right-hand rule: thumb = motion, first finger = field, second finger = induced current (emf). This formula is a direct application of Lorentz force on free electrons in the rod.
Example 14: Energy Stored in an Inductor
A solenoid has self-inductance 5 H and carries a steady current of 2 A. Find the energy stored in its magnetic field.

a) 5 J   b) 10 J   c) 15 J   d) 20 J
Solution: U = ½LI2 = ½ × 5 × (2)2 = ½ × 5 × 4 = 10 J. Option (b).

Generators & Transformers

AC Generator (Alternator): Converts mechanical energy into electrical energy. A coil rotated in a uniform magnetic field produces sinusoidally varying emf: ε = NBAω sin(ωt). Peak emf: ε0 = NBAω. The frequency of generated AC equals the rotational frequency of the coil.

Transformer: A device that changes alternating voltage based on mutual induction. For an ideal transformer:

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np = Ip/Is   and   VpIp = VsIs (for 100% efficiency)

Parameter Step-Up Transformer Step-Down Transformer
Turns ratio Ns > Np Ns < Np
Output voltage Vs > Vp Vs < Vp
Output current Is < Ip Is > Ip
Primary wire Thick wire (high I) Thin wire (low I)
Application Power transmission (reduces I2R loss) Domestic appliances, battery chargers
Example 15: Transformer Turns Ratio
A transformer has 200 turns in the primary and 1000 turns in the secondary. If Vp = 50 V AC, find Vs (ideal transformer).

a) 125 V   b) 250 V   c) 500 V   d) 1000 V
Solution: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np. Vs = 50 × (1000/200) = 50 × 5 = 250 V. Option (b).
Common NEET Trap: Transformer Losses
Real transformers have: (i) Eddy current losses — minimised by laminated silicon steel core, (ii) Hysteresis losses — minimised by using soft iron, (iii) Flux leakage — minimised by winding coils on top of each other, (iv) Copper losses (I2R). Efficiency = Output Power / Input Power. NEET often asks about methods to minimise these losses.

4. Alternating Current

Alternating current (AC) is the form of electricity delivered to homes and industries. Unlike DC, AC voltage and current vary sinusoidally with time. This chapter covers RMS values, LCR circuits, resonance, and power. NEET usually asks 1-2 questions from this chapter, focusing on impedance and resonance calculations.

RMS Values

For sinusoidal AC: I = I0 sin(ωt) and V = V0 sin(ωt), where I0 and V0 are peak values. The root-mean-square (RMS) values represent the equivalent DC quantities that produce the same heating effect:

Irms = I0/√2 ≈ 0.707 I0   Vrms = V0/√2 ≈ 0.707 V0

Domestic AC supply in India: Vrms = 230 V, frequency = 50 Hz. This means V0 = 230√2 ≈ 325 V and ω = 2πf = 100π rad/s. Average value over half cycle: Vavg = 2V0/π ≈ 0.637 V0. Average over full cycle is zero.

Memory Aid: RMS vs Peak
For sinusoidal waveforms: Irms = 0.707 I0 and Iavg (half) = 0.637 I0. The ratio I0 : Irms : Iavg = √2 : 1 : 2/π ≈ 1.414 : 1 : 0.637. Remember: RMS is always greater than average but less than peak for sinusoidal AC.
Example 16: RMS Current Calculation
The peak value of an AC current is 14.14 A. Find the RMS current.

a) 7.07 A   b) 10 A   c) 14.14 A   d) 20 A
Solution: Irms = I0/√2 = 14.14/1.414 = 10 A. Option (b).

LCR Circuits

An LCR series circuit consists of a resistor R, inductor L, and capacitor C connected in series with an AC source. The impedance Z is the AC analogue of DC resistance:

Z = √[R2 + (XL - XC)2]   where XL = ωL, XC = 1/(ωC)

Phase angle φ between V and I: tanφ = (XL - XC)/R. When XL > XC, circuit is inductive (V leads I). When XC > XL, circuit is capacitive (I leads V). Ohm's law for AC: V = IZ (using RMS values).

Component Resistance/Reactance Phase (V relative to I) Power Factor cosφ
Resistor (R) R (constant) In phase (φ = 0) 1 (unity)
Inductor (L) XL = ωL (increases with f) Voltage LEADS current by 90° 0 (purely reactive)
Capacitor (C) XC = 1/(ωC) (decreases with f) Current LEADS voltage by 90° 0 (purely reactive)
Example 17: Impedance of LCR Series Circuit
An LCR series circuit has R = 30 Ω, XL = 50 Ω, XC = 10 Ω. Find the impedance.

a) 30 Ω   b) 40 Ω   c) 50 Ω   d) 60 Ω
Solution: Z = √[R2 + (XL - XC)2] = √[302 + (50 - 10)2] = √[900 + 1600] = √2500 = 50 Ω. Option (c).
Memory Aid: ELI the ICE Man
ELI: In an L circuit, E (voltage) Leads I (current). ICE: In a C circuit, I (current) leads E (voltage). So voltage leads current in inductive circuits, and current leads voltage in capacitive circuits. This is one of the most useful mnemonics for AC circuits.

Resonance in LCR Circuits

In a series LCR circuit, resonance occurs when inductive reactance equals capacitive reactance: XL = XC, i.e., ωL = 1/(ωC). At resonance:

  • Resonant angular frequency: ω0 = 1/√(LC)
  • Resonant frequency: f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))
  • Impedance is minimum: Z = R (purely resistive circuit)
  • Current is maximum: Imax = V/R
  • Power factor: cosφ = 1 (unity)
  • Quality factor (Q): Q = ω0L/R = 1/(ω0CR) = (1/R)√(L/C)
  • Bandwidth: Δω = ω0/Q = R/L

High Q-factor means sharp resonance and good selectivity (used in radio tuning). Low Q means broad resonance.

NEET Focus: Resonance Applications
Resonance is used in radio receivers for tuning to a specific frequency. By varying the capacitance of a variable capacitor, the resonant frequency of the LCR circuit is matched to the desired station's frequency. At resonance, the current is maximum and the signal is??est. This is a classic NEET application question.
Example 18: Resonant Frequency
An LCR circuit has L = 0.5 H and C = 2 μF. Find the resonant frequency.

a) 50.3 Hz   b) 100.6 Hz   c) 159.2 Hz   d) 318.5 Hz
Solution: ω0 = 1/√(LC) = 1/√(0.5 × 2 × 10-6) = 1/√(10-6) = 1000 rad/s. f0 = ω0/(2π) = 1000/(2π) = 159.2 Hz. Option (c).
Example 19: Q-Factor Calculation
A series LCR circuit has L = 2 mH, C = 5 μF, R = 10 Ω. Find the Q-factor.

a) 10   b) 20   c) 30   d) 40
Solution: Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = (1/10) × √(2 × 10-3 / 5 × 10-6) = 0.1 × √(400) = 0.1 × 20 = 20. Option (b).

Power in AC Circuits

The average power consumed in an AC circuit over a complete cycle is:

Pavg = Vrms Irms cosφ

where cosφ = R/Z is the power factor. Key points:

  • Pure resistor: cosφ = 1, P = VrmsIrms (maximum power)
  • Pure inductor or capacitor: cosφ = 0, P = 0 (wattless current)
  • Power factor is always between 0 and 1 for an LCR circuit
  • Wattless current: Iw = Irms sinφ — component that consumes no power
  • Power factor correction: adding capacitors in parallel with inductive loads improves cosφ and reduces transmission losses
Important: Power Factor Range
Power factor cosφ ranges from 0 to 1. Unity power factor (cosφ = 1) means maximum power utilisation. Low power factor means more current is required for the same power delivery, leading to higher I2R losses in transmission lines. Electricity boards penalise industrial consumers with low power factor.
Example 20: Average Power in AC
An AC circuit has Vrms = 200 V, Irms = 5 A, and power factor = 0.8. Find the average power consumed.

a) 600 W   b) 800 W   c) 1000 W   d) 1200 W
Solution: Pavg = VrmsIrms cosφ = 200 × 5 × 0.8 = 800 W. Option (b).
Example 21: Power Factor from Circuit Parameters
An LCR circuit has R = 40 Ω and Z = 50 Ω. Find the power factor.

a) 0.6   b) 0.7   c) 0.8   d) 0.9
Solution: cosφ = R/Z = 40/50 = 0.8. Option (c).

5. Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves are produced by accelerating charges and consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation. James Clerk Maxwell theoretically predicted EM waves through his famous equations, and Heinrich Hertz experimentally confirmed their existence. This chapter contributes 1-2 NEET questions on EM spectrum properties.

EM Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum spans a vast range of frequencies and wavelengths. All EM waves travel at speed c = 3 × 108 m/s in vacuum, related by c = fλ. The energy of EM radiation is E = hf = hc/λ.

Memory Aid: EM Spectrum Order
Order of increasing frequency: Radio, Microwave, Infrared, Visible, Ultraviolet, X-ray, Gamma. Mnemonic: Ricky Martin In Very Unlikely X-rated Games. Decreasing wavelength is the reverse order (Gamma has shortest wavelength, highest frequency, and most energy per photon).

Properties & Uses of EM Waves

Key Properties:

  • Transverse in nature — E and B fields are perpendicular to the direction of propagation
  • E and B are mutually perpendicular to each other and are in phase
  • Speed in vacuum: c = 3 × 108 m/s (constant, independent of frequency)
  • Energy density: u = ½ε0E2 + B2/(2μ0) where E and B are instantaneous values
  • Average intensity (Poynting vector): I = <S> = ½cε0E02 = cB02/(2μ0)
  • Carry momentum: p = U/c (energy divided by speed of light)
  • Exert radiation pressure: P = I/c (for complete absorption) or 2I/c (for perfect reflection)
  • Can be polarised — evidence of transverse nature
  • Follow superposition principle, show interference and diffraction

Maxwell's Equations (qualitative):

  • Gauss's law for electricity: ∮ E · dA = Q/ε0 — electric charges produce electric fields
  • Gauss's law for magnetism: ∮ B · dA = 0 — no magnetic monopoles exist
  • Faraday's law of induction: ∮ E · dl = -dΦB/dt — changing B produces E
  • Ampere-Maxwell law: ∮ B · dl = μ0I + μ0ε0 dΦE/dt — current or changing E produces B
Electromagnetic wave showing perpendicular E (green) and B (purple) fields propagating along the x-axis.
Key Insight: Displacement Current
Maxwell's key contribution was adding the displacement current term Id = ε0 dΦE/dt to Ampere's law. This made the equations symmetric and predicted the existence of EM waves. Displacement current exists wherever electric flux changes, such as between capacitor plates during charging/discharging. It is zero in steady-state DC circuits.
Example 22: EM Wave Wavelength
An electromagnetic wave has frequency 10 MHz. Find its wavelength in vacuum. (c = 3 × 108 m/s)

a) 10 m   b) 20 m   c) 30 m   d) 40 m
Solution: λ = c/f = (3 × 108) / (10 × 106) = 30 m. Option (c).
Example 23: Photon Energy of EM Wave
Find the energy of a photon of EM radiation with frequency 5 × 1014 Hz. (h = 6.63 × 10-34 Js)

a) 2.65 × 10-19 J   b) 3.31 × 10-19 J   c) 4.97 × 10-19 J   d) 6.63 × 10-19 J
Solution: E = hf = 6.63 × 10-34 × 5 × 1014 = 3.315 × 10-19 J ≈ 3.31 × 10-19 J. Option (b).

Example 24: Motional EMF in a Moving Rod
A conducting rod of length 0.5 m moves with velocity 20 m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.3 T. Find the induced emf between its ends.

a) 1.5 V   b) 2.0 V   c) 2.5 V   d) 3.0 V
Solution: ε = BvL = 0.3 × 20 × 0.5 = 3.0 V. Option (d).
Example 25: Torque on a Current Loop
A rectangular coil of area 0.04 m2 has 50 turns and carries 2 A current. It is placed in a uniform B = 0.1 T field. Find the maximum torque on the coil.

a) 0.2 Nm   b) 0.4 Nm   c) 0.6 Nm   d) 0.8 Nm
Solution: τmax = NIAB = 50 × 2 × 0.04 × 0.1 = 0.4 Nm. Option (b).
Example 26: Displacement Current
A parallel plate capacitor with plate area 0.1 m2 is being charged at dE/dt = 2 × 1011 V/ms. Find the displacement current. (ε0 = 8.85 × 10-12 F/m)

a) 0.177 A   b) 0.354 A   c) 0.531 A   d) 0.708 A
Solution: Id = ε0 dΦE/dt = ε0A dE/dt = 8.85 × 10-12 × 0.1 × 2 × 1011 = 0.177 A. Option (a).
Common NEET Mistakes to Avoid
(1) Forgetting cyclotron frequency is independent of particle speed. (2) Confusing Fleming's left-hand rule (motor) with right-hand rule (generator). (3) Using peak values instead of RMS in AC power. (4) Forgetting Lenz's law negative sign. (5) Assuming transformers work with DC. (6) Confusing magnetic and geographic poles. (7) Mixing up self and mutual inductance formulas.
Quick Revision: Key Constants
μ0 = 4π × 10-7 Tm/A, Earth's B ≈ 0.3 × 10-4 T, c = 3 × 108 m/s, Domestic AC: 230 V (rms), 50 Hz. Magnetic moment unit: Am2, Self-inductance unit: Henry (H) = Vs/A, 1 T = 104 G (Gauss). Memorise these constants for speed in NEET.
