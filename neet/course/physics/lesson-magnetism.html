What is Magnetism?

Magnetism deals with magnetic fields, their sources, and their effects on moving charges and magnetic materials. For NEET, key topics include Biot-Savart law, Ampere's circuital law, force on a current-carrying conductor, magnetic moment, Earth's magnetism, and electromagnetic induction. This topic bridges electricity and magnetism, forming the basis of electromagnetism.

Key Concepts

Biot-Savart Law: dB = (μ0/4π)(Idl × r)/r3. Magnetic field due to straight conductor: B = (μ0I)/(2πr). Force on a moving charge: F = q(v × B). Force on a current-carrying conductor: F = I(L × B). Ampere's Law: &oblique8;B·dl = μ0Ienc. Magnetic moment: M = IA for a current loop. Faraday's Law: Induced emf ε = -dΦB/dt. Lenz's Law: Induced current opposes the change causing it.

Solved Examples

Example 1
A long straight wire carries a current of 5 A. Find the magnetic field at a distance of 2 cm from the wire. (μ0 = 4π × 10-7 Tm/A)

a) 5 × 10-5 T b) 2.5 × 10-5 T c) 1 × 10-4 T d) 4 × 10-5 T
Solution: B = (μ0I)/(2πr) = (4π × 10-7 × 5)/(2π × 0.02) = (20π × 10-7)/(0.04π) = (20 × 10-7)/(0.04) = 5 × 10-5 T. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 2
A proton enters a magnetic field of 0.2 T with a velocity of 106 m/s perpendicular to the field. Find the radius of its circular path. (mp = 1.67 × 10-27 kg, q = 1.6 × 10-19 C)

a) 2.6 cm b) 5.2 cm c) 1.3 cm d) 10.4 cm
Solution: For circular motion in magnetic field, qvB = mv2/r. r = mv/(qB) = (1.67 × 10-27 × 106)/(1.6 × 10-19 × 0.2) = (1.67 × 10-21)/(3.2 × 10-20) = 0.052 m = 5.2 cm. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 3
A circular coil of radius 5 cm has 100 turns and carries a current of 2 A. Find the magnetic field at its center.

a) 8π × 10-4 T b) 4π × 10-4 T c) 2π × 10-3 T d) 8π × 10-3 T
Solution: B at center of circular coil = (μ0NI)/(2r) = (4π × 10-7 × 100 × 2)/(2 × 0.05) = (8π × 10-5)/(0.1) = 8π × 10-4 T. Hence option (a) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For magnetic field direction, use the right-hand thumb rule: thumb points in direction of current, fingers curl in direction of magnetic field.
  • For force between two parallel current-carrying wires, currents in same direction attract, opposite directions repel.
  • In electromagnetic induction, the induced emf is maximum when the coil rotates perpendicular to the magnetic field.
  • For a solenoid, B = μ0nI, where n is number of turns per unit length.
Pro Tip
For NEET, learn Fleming's left-hand rule (for force on current-carrying conductor) and right-hand rule (for induced current in generators). These mnemonics save time in the exam and reduce sign errors.

Magnetic Materials and Hysteresis Loops

Magnetic materials are classified by their response to an applied magnetic field. Diamagnetic substances develop a weak magnetisation opposite to the field, with a relative permeability slightly less than one. Paramagnetic materials align their atomic dipoles with the field, giving a permeability marginally greater than one. Ferromagnetic materials exhibit strong, cooperative alignment of magnetic domains, producing permeabilities that can reach several thousand. The macroscopic behaviour of a ferromagnet is captured by its hysteresis loop, a plot of magnetic flux density B versus magnetic field strength H for a complete magnetisation cycle. The loop encloses an area proportional to the energy dissipated as heat per unit volume per cycle, known as hysteresis loss. Key points on the loop include the saturation flux density Bs, where all domains are aligned; the remanent flux density Br, the flux remaining after the external field is removed; and the coercive field Hc, the reverse field required to bring the flux back to zero. Soft magnetic materials such as silicon‑steel have narrow loops, low Hc and low hysteresis loss, making them suitable for transformer cores and inductors. Hard magnetic materials like Alnico or rare‑earth alloys possess wide loops, high Hc and high Br, serving as permanent magnets. The Steinmetz equation, Ph = kh f Bmaxn, quantifies hysteresis loss per unit volume, where f is frequency, Bmax the peak flux density, and n ≈ 1.6–2.0 for typical electrical steels. Eddy‑current loss, another core loss component, scales with the square of frequency and the square of lamination thickness; therefore, thin insulated laminations are used to suppress it. Understanding the shape and parameters of the hysteresis loop is essential for designing efficient magnetic circuits, selecting core material for a given operating frequency, and estimating temperature rise in transformers and rotating machines.

Motional Electromotive Force and Faraday's Law in Moving Media

When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, the magnetic component of the Lorentz force acts on the free charges, creating a potential difference known as motional emf. For a straight rod of length L moving with velocity v perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field B, the emf induced between its ends is ε = B L v. This result follows directly from the magnetic force q(v × B) on a charge q and the definition of emf as work per unit charge. In a more general situation, the emf around a closed loop C moving and deforming in a time‑varying magnetic field is given by the flux rule ε = − dΦB/dt, where ΦB = ∫S B·dA is the magnetic flux through any surface S bounded by C. The total time derivative includes both the explicit time variation of B and the change of flux due to the motion of the boundary. The equivalence of the motional emf expression and the flux rule can be demonstrated by applying the Leibniz integral rule to the moving surface. A classic illustration is the sliding rod on parallel rails forming a rectangular circuit; as the rod moves, the area of the loop increases, the flux increases, and an emf appears that drives a current. The direction of the induced current is given by Lenz’s law: it opposes the change in flux, which in this case means the magnetic force on the current‑carrying rod opposes the external pulling force. In rotating machines, the same principle generates alternating emf in the armature windings. For a coil of N turns rotating at angular speed ω in a uniform field B, the instantaneous emf is ε = N B A ω sin(ωt), where A is the coil area. This sinusoidal emf is the basis of AC generators. The concept of motional emf also underlies electromagnetic flow meters, where the voltage induced across a conducting fluid moving in a pipe is proportional to the flow velocity.

Worked Example: Motional EMF in a Rotating Rod
A conducting rod of length 0.5 m rotates about one end in a plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.3 T. The rod makes 60 revolutions per second. Calculate the emf induced between the fixed end and the free end of the rod.
The rod sweeps out a circular area. For a small element at distance r from the pivot moving with speed v = ω r, the motional emf contributed is dε = B v dr = B ω r dr. Integrating from 0 to L gives ε = ∫0L B ω r dr = ½ B ω L². The angular speed ω = 2π × 60 = 120π rad/s. Substituting B = 0.3 T, L = 0.5 m: ε = ½ × 0.3 × 120π × (0.5)² = 0.15 × 120π × 0.25 = 4.5π ≈ 14.1 V. The emf between the ends is approximately 14.1 V.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves a moving conductor, first decide whether the flux‑rule (ε = −dΦ/dt) or the direct motional‑emf formula (ε = ∫ (v × B)·dl) is simpler. For straight rods moving uniformly, the direct formula often yields the answer in one step. For rotating or deforming loops, the flux rule with a clear choice of surface avoids sign errors. Always draw the velocity, field, and length vectors to apply the right‑hand rule for the direction of induced current.

AC Circuit Resonance, Power Factor, and Phasor Analysis

In a series RLC circuit driven by a sinusoidal voltage v(t) = Vm sin(ωt), the steady‑state current is i(t) = Im sin(ωt − φ), where φ is the phase angle between voltage and current. The impedance Z = R + j(ωL − 1/ωC) combines resistance R, inductive reactance XL = ωL, and capacitive reactance XC = 1/ωC. The magnitude of impedance is |Z| = √[R² + (XL − XC)²] and the phase angle satisfies tan φ = (XL − XC)/R. Resonance occurs when XL = XC, i.e. ω0 = 1/√(LC). At resonance the impedance is purely resistive (Z = R), the current amplitude is maximum (Im = Vm/R), and the voltage across L and C can be Q times the source voltage, where the quality factor Q = ω0L/R = 1/(ω0CR). The bandwidth Δω = ω0/Q defines the frequency range over which the power delivered to the resistor is at least half its maximum value. Power factor cos φ = R/|Z| indicates the fraction of apparent power that is real power. A lagging power factor (φ > 0) means the circuit is inductive; a leading power factor (φ < 0) means it is capacitive. Power factor correction is achieved by adding a parallel capacitor to an inductive load, bringing the overall φ closer to zero and reducing the current drawn from the supply for the same real power. Phasor diagrams provide a geometric representation: voltage phasors for R, L, and C are drawn with appropriate phase shifts, and their vector sum equals the source voltage phasor. This visual method simplifies the analysis of series and parallel combinations, especially when multiple frequencies are present. In three‑phase systems, balanced loads allow per‑phase analysis using the same phasor techniques, with line‑to‑line voltages √3 times the phase voltages and a 30° phase shift.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation, Polarization, and Poynting Vector

Maxwell’s equations in free space predict transverse electromagnetic waves travelling at the speed of light c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. For a plane wave propagating in the +z direction, the electric and magnetic fields can be written as E(z,t) = E₀ cos(kz − ωt) x̂ and B(z,t) = B₀ cos(kz − ωt) ŷ, with E₀ = c B₀ and k = ω/c. The fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation, confirming the transverse nature. The instantaneous Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀) E × B represents the power flux density (W/m²). For the plane wave above, S = (E₀²/μ₀c) cos²(kz − ωt) ẑ. The time‑averaged intensity I = ⟨S⟩ = ½ ε₀ c E₀² = ½ (E₀²/μ₀c). In a dielectric medium with relative permittivity εr and permeability μr, the wave speed becomes v = c/√(εrμr) and the intrinsic impedance η = √(μ/ε). Polarization describes the orientation of the electric field vector. Linear polarization occurs when E oscillates along a fixed direction. Circular polarization results from two orthogonal linear components of equal amplitude and a 90° phase difference; the tip of the E vector traces a helix. Elliptical polarization is the general case with unequal amplitudes or a phase difference other than 90°. Polarization can be produced by reflection (Brewster’s angle), by passage through anisotropic crystals (birefringence), or by antenna geometry. At a dielectric interface, the Fresnel equations give the reflection and transmission coefficients for s‑ (perpendicular) and p‑ (parallel) polarizations. Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from a higher‑index medium to a lower‑index medium at an incidence angle greater than the critical angle θc = sin⁻¹(n₂/n₁). In waveguides and optical fibres, boundary conditions quantise the allowed modes, each with a distinct field distribution and cutoff frequency. The concept of group velocity vg = dω/dk becomes important for pulses, as it determines the speed of energy and information transfer, which can differ from the phase velocity vp = ω/k in dispersive media.

Displacement Current and the Complete Set of Maxwell's Equations

Ampere’s circuital law in its original form, ∮ B·dl = μ₀ Ienc, fails for time‑varying electric fields because it does not account for the current that “flows” through a capacitor gap. Maxwell introduced the displacement current density Jd = ε₀ ∂E/∂t, so that the generalized Ampere‑Maxwell law reads ∮ B·dl = μ₀ (Icond + ε₀ dΦE/dt), where ΦE = ∫ E·dA is the electric flux through the surface bounded by the loop. This term restores continuity of current: in a charging capacitor, the conduction current in the leads equals the displacement current between the plates, ensuring that the magnetic field around the loop is uniquely defined regardless of the chosen surface. The four Maxwell equations in differential form are: ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss’s law), ∇·B = 0 (no magnetic monopoles), ∇×E = −∂B/∂t (Faraday’s law), and ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t (Ampere‑Maxwell law). Together they imply the wave equation ∇²E − μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t² = 0 in source‑free regions, confirming electromagnetic wave propagation. The displacement current is essential for the self‑consistency of the theory; without it, the divergence of the Ampere‑Maxwell law would give ∇·J = 0, contradicting charge conservation ∇·J + ∂ρ/∂t = 0. In materials, the equations incorporate polarization P and magnetisation M through D = ε₀E + P and H = B/μ₀ − M, leading to the macroscopic forms ∇·D = ρfree, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×H = Jfree + ∂D/∂t. The boundary conditions at interfaces follow from the integral forms: the tangential component of E is continuous, the normal component of D jumps by the free surface charge, the tangential component of H jumps by the free surface current, and the normal component of B is continuous. These conditions are used to derive reflection and transmission coefficients for waves at dielectric and conducting boundaries, and to analyse waveguides, cavities, and radiation from antennas.

  • Confusing the direction of induced emf in motional problems: always apply the right‑hand rule to v × B, not the left‑hand rule.
  • Assuming the resonance frequency of a parallel RLC circuit is the same as the series formula; the parallel resonant frequency shifts when the resistance is not negligible.
  • Neglecting the phase angle when calculating real power; using P = VI instead of P = VI cos φ leads to overestimation.
  • Treating the Poynting vector as instantaneous power flow in a standing wave; the time‑averaged Poynting vector is zero for a pure standing wave.
  • Forgetting that the displacement current term is required for consistency with charge conservation, not merely a mathematical convenience.
  • Using the free‑space wave speed c in a dielectric without adjusting for the refractive index n = √(εrμr).
  • Misapplying the Fresnel equations by swapping s and p polarizations or using the wrong angle (incidence vs. transmission).

These advanced topics deepen the conceptual framework needed for high‑level problem solving and should be revisited regularly during preparation.

Magnetic Vector Potential and Gauge Freedom

The magnetic field B is solenoidal (∇·B = 0), which guarantees the existence of a vector potential A such that B = ∇×A. This potential is not unique: the transformation A′ = A + ∇ψ, where ψ is any scalar field, leaves B unchanged because the curl of a gradient vanishes. The freedom to choose ψ is called gauge freedom. Two gauges are standard in advanced problem solving. The Coulomb gauge (∇·A = 0) decouples the scalar potential φ from A in the static limit, yielding Poisson’s equation ∇²φ = −ρ/ε₀ and a vector Poisson equation ∇²A = −μ₀J. The Lorenz gauge (∇·A + μ₀ε₀ ∂φ/∂t = 0) is Lorentz invariant and leads to symmetric wave equations for both potentials: □A = −μ₀J and □φ = −ρ/ε₀, where □ = ∇² − μ₀ε₀ ∂²/∂t². In the Lorenz gauge the retarded potentials are A(r,t) = (μ₀/4π) ∫ J(r′,t_r)/|r−r′| d³r′ and φ(r,t) = (1/4πε₀) ∫ ρ(r′,t_r)/|r−r′| d³r′, with retarded time t_r = t − |r−r′|/c. The Aharonov–Bohm effect demonstrates that A has physical significance beyond a mathematical convenience: a charged particle encircling a region of confined magnetic flux acquires a phase shift Δφ = (q/ħ)∮A·dl even though B = 0 along its path. In examinations, recognising which gauge simplifies a given boundary‑value problem (Coulomb for static configurations, Lorenz for radiation) is a decisive step.

Energy, Momentum, and the Maxwell Stress Tensor

The electromagnetic field stores energy and momentum. The instantaneous energy density is u = ½(ε₀E² + B²/μ₀). The Poynting vector S = E×H gives the energy flux density (power per unit area). Integrating the Poynting theorem over a volume V bounded by surface Σ yields dU/dt = −∮_Σ S·dA − ∫_V J·E dV, where U = ∫_V u dV. The field momentum density is g = S/c² = ε₀E×B. The total momentum in V changes according to dP_field/dt = −∮_Σ T·dA − ∫_V ρE + J×B dV, where T is the Maxwell stress tensor with components T_ij = ε₀(E_iE_j − ½δ_ijE²) + (1/μ₀)(B_iB_j − ½δ_ijB²). The tensor gives the force per unit area (stress) acting on a surface: the i‑th component of the electromagnetic force on a volume is F_i = ∮_Σ T_ij n_j dA − d/dt ∫_V g_i dV. For a plane wave in vacuum, the time‑averaged stress tensor is diagonal with T_xx = T_yy = 0 and T_zz = u, leading to a radiation pressure p = u on a perfect absorber and p = 2u on a perfect reflector. These relations are essential for problems involving radiation pressure on sails, optical tweezers, or the mechanical stability of high‑power cavities.

Radiation pressure on a perfect reflector
A linearly polarised plane wave of intensity I = 1.0 kW/m² is incident normally on a perfectly conducting mirror. Compute the time‑averaged radiation pressure exerted on the mirror.
For a perfect reflector the momentum change per photon is twice the incident momentum. The time‑averaged energy density of the wave is u = I/c. The radiation pressure on a perfect reflector is p = 2u = 2I/c. Using c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s, p = 2×(1.0×10³ W/m²)/(3.00×10⁸ m/s) = 6.67×10⁻⁶ N/m². The same result follows from the Maxwell stress tensor: the zz component of the time‑averaged tensor for a standing wave formed by incident and reflected waves is ⟨T_zz⟩ = 2⟨u⟩ = 2I/c.
Exam‑day strategy
When a problem asks for force or pressure from fields, first identify whether the configuration is a travelling wave, standing wave, or static field. For travelling waves use p = I/c (absorber) or 2I/c (reflector). For static fields compute the stress tensor components directly from the given E and B. Remember that the tensor is symmetric and its divergence gives the volume force density. A quick check: the trace of T_ij in vacuum is zero, which can be used to verify algebraic manipulations.

Waveguides, Cavity Resonators, and Cut‑off Conditions

Metallic waveguides support transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes characterised by integer indices (m,n). For a rectangular guide of dimensions a (broad wall) and b (narrow wall) with a > b, the cutoff wavenumber is k_c = √((mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²). The corresponding cutoff frequency is f_c = (c/2)√((m/a)² + (n/b)²). The dominant mode is TE₁₀ with f_c = c/(2a); it has the lowest cutoff and no longitudinal electric field component. Above cutoff the propagation constant is β = √(k² − k_c²) where k = ω/c. The phase velocity v_p = ω/β exceeds c, while the group velocity v_g = dω/dβ = c²/v_p is less than c, ensuring energy travels sub‑luminally. The wave impedance for TE modes is Z_TE = η/√(1 − (f_c/f)²) with η = √(μ₀/ε₀) ≈ 377 Ω; for TM modes Z_TM = η√(1 − (f_c/f)²). A resonant cavity is a waveguide section terminated by conducting walls at z = 0 and z = d. The allowed longitudinal wavenumber is k_z = pπ/d (p = 1,2,…). The resonant frequencies are f_mnp = (c/2)√((m/a)² + (n/b)² + (p/d)²). The quality factor Q = ωU/P_loss, where U is stored energy and P_loss is power dissipated in the walls, scales as Q ∝ √f for good conductors. Cavity perturbations (dielectric insertion, shape deformation) shift resonant frequencies according to first‑order perturbation theory: Δf/f = −(∫_V Δε|E|² dV)/(2∫_V ε|E|² dV). These formulas appear in problems on particle accelerators, microwave filters, and superconducting quantum circuits.

Radiation from Time‑Varying Currents: Larmor Formula and Antenna Fundamentals

An accelerated charge radiates. The non‑relativistic Larmor formula gives the total radiated power P = (μ₀ q² a²)/(6π c), where a is the instantaneous acceleration. For a harmonically oscillating dipole moment p(t) = p₀ cos ωt ẑ, the far‑field electric field is E_rad = (k²/4πε₀) (ẑ×r̂)×r̂ (p₀ ei(kr−ωt)/r) with k = ω/c. The magnetic field is B_rad = (1/c) r̂×E_rad. The time‑averaged Poynting vector magnitude is ⟨S⟩ = (μ₀ p₀² ω⁴)/(32π² c) sin²θ / r². Integrating over a sphere yields the total radiated power P = (μ₀ p₀² ω⁴)/(12π c). A short dipole of length l ≪ λ carrying current I₀ has p₀ = I₀ l/ω, giving radiation resistance R_rad = 2P/I₀² = 80π² (l/λ)². For a half‑wave dipole (l = λ/2) the current distribution is approximately sinusoidal, leading to R_rad ≈ 73 Ω and a directive gain of 2.15 dBi. The far‑field pattern of a half‑wave dipole is proportional to cos(π/2 cosθ)/sinθ. Mutual impedance between two dipoles determines array behaviour; for two parallel half‑wave dipoles spaced λ/2, the mutual resistance is about 12 Ω. These results underpin the design of Yagi‑Uda arrays, log‑periodic antennas, and the analysis of radiation from printed circuit board traces.

Skin Effect, Eddy Currents, and Magnetic Shielding in Conductors

When an alternating current flows in a conductor, the current density concentrates near the surface. The skin depth δ = √(2/(ωμσ)) defines the 1/e decay distance of the field amplitude. For copper at 60 Hz, δ ≈ 8.5 mm; at 1 MHz, δ ≈ 0.066 mm. The AC resistance per unit length of a round wire of radius a ≫ δ is R_ac ≈ 1/(2πaδσ), which can be orders of magnitude larger than the DC resistance. Eddy currents are induced loops in bulk conductors exposed to time‑varying magnetic fields; their density follows the same skin‑depth law. The power dissipated per unit volume is p_eddy = (π² B₀² f² d²)/(6ρ) for a thin sheet of thickness d ≪ δ in a uniform perpendicular field B₀ sin ωt, where ρ = 1/σ. Magnetic shielding exploits the reflection and absorption of fields by conductive or high‑permeability materials. For a plane wave incident on a thick conductor, the shielding effectiveness (SE) in decibels is SE = R + A + B, where reflection loss R = 168 + 10 log₁₀(σ/(μ_r f)) dB, absorption loss A = 8.68 t/δ dB (t = thickness), and B is a correction for multiple reflections (negligible when A > 10 dB). High‑permeability alloys (μ‑metal, permalloy) provide low‑frequency shielding by diverting magnetic flux through their high‑μ path; the attenuation factor for a long cylindrical shield in a uniform axial field, with inner radius a, outer radius b, and relative permeability μ_r, is approximately (1 + (μ_r−1)(b²−a²)/(2a²))⁻¹. In examinations, distinguishing between skin‑effect‑limited resistance, eddy‑current loss in laminations, and shielding effectiveness of a given enclosure is a common multi‑part question.

  • Confusing the Coulomb gauge condition (∇·A = 0) with the Lorenz gauge condition (∇·A + μ₀ε₀ ∂φ/∂t = 0) when setting up wave equations for potentials.
  • Using the free‑space impedance η₀ = 377 Ω for waveguide mode impedances instead of the frequency‑dependent Z_TE or Z_TM.
  • Applying the non‑relativistic Larmor formula to relativistic particles; the correct generalisation is the Liénard formula P = (μ₀ q² γ⁶)/(6π c) (a² − (v×a)²/c²).
  • Assuming the skin depth formula δ = √(2/(ωμσ)) holds for ferromagnetic conductors without accounting for the strong frequency dependence of μ.
  • Neglecting the multiple‑reflection correction B in shielding effectiveness calculations when the absorption loss A is less than 10 dB.
  • Treating the radiation resistance of a short dipole as 80π²(l/λ)² for lengths approaching λ/2, where the sinusoidal current distribution must be used.
  • Forgetting that the Maxwell stress tensor gives the electromagnetic force on a volume only when integrated over a closed surface enclosing that volume, and that the field momentum term must be included for time‑varying fields.

These advanced topics complete the conceptual toolkit required for high‑level electromagnetic problem solving.

Relativistic Transformation of Electric and Magnetic Fields

The electric and magnetic fields are components of the electromagnetic field tensor Fμν, not separate entities. Under a Lorentz boost with velocity v (β = v/c, γ = 1/√(1−β²)) the fields transform as E′∥ = E∥, B′∥ = B∥, E′⊥ = γ(E⊥ + v × B), B′⊥ = γ(B⊥ − v × E/c²). Parallel components are unchanged; perpendicular components mix. This mixing explains why a pure electrostatic field in one frame appears as both electric and magnetic fields in another. The invariants E² − c²B² and E·B are unchanged by any boost. If E·B = 0 and E² > c²B² a frame exists with B′ = 0; if E² < c²B² a frame exists with E′ = 0. These results underpin particle motion in accelerators, the fields of a moving charge (Liénard‑Wiechert potentials), and plane‑wave polarization transformation. The electromagnetic stress‑energy tensor Tμν transforms covariantly, ensuring energy‑momentum conservation in all inertial frames.

Electromagnetic Angular Momentum and the Spin of Light

An electromagnetic field carries linear momentum density g = ε₀E × B and angular momentum density l = r × g. For a monochromatic plane wave, the time‑averaged linear momentum flux is S/c², and the angular momentum flux splits into orbital (spatial mode structure, e.g., Laguerre‑Gaussian beams) and spin (circular polarization) parts. The spin angular momentum per photon is ±ħ, corresponding to left‑ or right‑handed circular polarization. The total angular momentum flux through a surface is ∫(r × S/c²)·dA + ∫Sspin·dA, where the spin flux density for a circularly polarized plane wave is ±(ε₀E₀²/ω) k̂. In a dielectric the Abraham‑Minkowski debate modifies the momentum density, but total angular momentum (field + material) is conserved. Experiments measuring torque on a birefringent plate or rotation of a trapped particle in optical tweezers directly demonstrate spin angular momentum transfer. Orbital angular momentum, characterized by an azimuthal phase factor exp(iℓφ), carries ℓħ per photon and enables multiplexing in optical communications. Separating spin and orbital contributions is crucial for radiation pressure, optical torque, and angular momentum balance in antenna arrays.

Worked Example: Spin Angular Momentum of a Circularly Polarized Beam
A Gaussian beam of wavelength λ = 800 nm carries power P = 10 mW and is perfectly right‑circularly polarized. Compute the torque exerted on a perfectly absorbing circular aperture that intercepts the entire beam.
Photon energy is Eph = hc/λ. Photon flux is N = P/Eph. Each photon carries spin angular momentum +ħ. Torque (angular momentum per second) is τ = N·ħ = P·ħ·λ/(hc) = P·λ/(2πc). Substituting P = 0.01 W, λ = 8×10⁻⁷ m, c = 3×10⁸ m/s gives τ ≈ 0.01 × 8×10⁻⁷ / (2π × 3×10⁸) ≈ 4.2×10⁻¹⁸ N·m. This torque is measurable with a sensitive torsion balance and confirms the spin angular momentum of light.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for torque or force from an electromagnetic wave, first identify whether the wave is linearly or circularly polarized. For circular polarization use the spin angular momentum per photon ±ħ; for linear polarization the net spin averages to zero and only orbital or radiation‑pressure terms contribute. Remember the invariant P·λ/(2πc) for a fully absorbed circular beam.

Superconductivity: Meissner Effect, London Equations, and Type I/II Classification

Below the critical temperature Tc a superconductor expels magnetic flux — the Meissner effect — distinguishing it from a perfect conductor. The London equations, ∂Js/∂t = (nse²/m)E and ∇ × Js = −(nse²/m)B, yield the London penetration depth λL = √(m/μ₀nse²). Magnetic field decays exponentially as B(x) = B₀exp(−x/λL). The thermodynamic critical field Hc satisfies μ₀Hc²/2 = Fn − Fs, the free‑energy difference between normal and superconducting states. Type I superconductors (e.g., lead, mercury) show a complete Meissner state up to Hc then transition abruptly to normal. Type II superconductors (e.g., NbTi, YBCO) have two critical fields Hc1 < Hc2; between them flux penetrates as quantized vortices each carrying Φ₀ = h/2e ≈ 2.07×10⁻¹⁵ Wb, forming a mixed (Shubnikov) state. The Ginzburg‑Landau parameter κ = λL/ξ (ξ = coherence length) determines the type: κ < 1/√2 → Type I, κ > 1/√2 → Type II. The upper critical field Hc2 = Φ₀/(2πξ²) can exceed 100 T for high‑temperature superconductors. Vortex pinning by defects yields a critical current density Jc essential for magnets and power transmission. The AC response is described by complex conductivity σ(ω) = σ₁ − iσ₂, with σ₂ = 1/μ₀ωλL² at low frequencies, giving surface resistance Rs ∝ ω² well below Tc.

Magnetohydrodynamics: Alfven Waves and Magnetic Reconnection

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) treats a conducting plasma as a single fluid coupled to the magnetic field. The induction equation ∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B) + η∇²B (η = 1/μ₀σ) governs field evolution. In ideal MHD (η → 0) magnetic flux is frozen into the fluid (Alfvén’s theorem). Linearising about uniform B₀ and density ρ₀ yields three wave modes: fast and slow magnetosonic (compressive) and shear Alfvén (incompressive). The Alfvén wave propagates parallel to B₀ with phase velocity vA = B₀/√(μ₀ρ₀) (group velocity equals phase velocity); perturbations satisfy δv ⊥ B₀, δB ⊥ B₀, and δv = ±δB/√(μ₀ρ₀). Energy equipartition holds between kinetic and magnetic perturbations. Magnetic reconnection occurs when non‑ideal effects (resistivity, Hall term, electron inertia) break the frozen‑in condition, enabling topological rearrangement and conversion of magnetic energy to kinetic and thermal energy. The Sweet‑Parker model gives reconnection rate vin/vA ∼ S⁻¹ᐟ² (S = Lundquist number = L vA/η). The Petschek model, with standing slow shocks, predicts a faster rate ∼ (ln S)⁻¹. In solar flares and magnetospheric substorms, observed energy release requires fast reconnection, often attributed to plasmoid instability or kinetic effects. The dimensionless reconnection rate in collisionless plasmas is typically 0.01–0.1, independent of system size.

Metamaterials and Negative Refractive Index in Electromagnetics

Metamaterials are engineered structures with sub‑wavelength unit cells exhibiting effective permittivity εeff and permeability μeff not found in nature. When both are negative over a frequency band, the refractive index n = −√(εeffμeff) becomes negative, producing backward waves where the Poynting vector S is antiparallel to the wavevector k. The first experimental demonstration used split‑ring resonators (negative μ) and metallic wires (negative ε) at microwave frequencies. The dispersion relation is ω = c|k|/√(|εeffμeff|) with negative slope dω/dk, implying negative group velocity. Snell’s law still holds but the refracted ray bends to the same side of the normal as the incident ray. A negative‑index slab can focus light without curvature — the Veselago lens — and if ε = μ = −1 achieves perfect sub‑diffraction imaging by amplifying evanescent waves. Losses (Im ε, Im μ > 0) limit resolution and bandwidth. The figure of merit FOM = |Re n|/Im n quantifies performance; typical microwave metamaterials achieve FOM ≈ 10–30. At optical frequencies, plasmonic resonances in metal‑dielectric nanostructures give negative ε, while magnetic responses are weaker; all‑dielectric Mie resonators now provide low‑loss magnetic activity. Applications include superlenses, cloaking via transformation optics, and compact antennas with enhanced bandwidth. The effective medium description breaks down when unit cell size approaches λ/10; spatial dispersion (non‑locality) and bianisotropy must then be included.

  • Confusing the London penetration depth λL with the Ginzburg‑Landau coherence length ξ; they govern different physics (field screening vs. order‑parameter variation).
  • Assuming the Alfvén speed vA = B/√(μ₀ρ) applies unchanged in a partially ionised plasma; neutral‑ion collisions introduce damping and modify wave dispersion.
  • Treating negative refractive index as simply n = −√(εμ) without verifying that both ε and μ are simultaneously negative and that the branch cut is chosen correctly for passive media.
  • Neglecting the Hall term in the generalized Ohm’s law when estimating reconnection rates in collisionless plasmas; the Hall effect enables fast reconnection independent of resistivity.
  • Using the non‑relativistic Larmor formula for radiation from a charge moving at relativistic speeds in a magnetic field; the correct synchrotron power scales as γ⁴.
  • Forgetting that the Meissner effect is a thermodynamic equilibrium phenomenon, whereas perfect conductivity only guarantees flux conservation, not expulsion.
  • Applying the Sweet‑Parker reconnection rate to solar flare timescales without accounting for the plasmoid instability that drastically increases the rate.
