Vectors in a Plane

1.1 Vectors and Their Representation

A vector is a physical quantity that possesses both magnitude and direction, distinguishing it from a scalar which has magnitude only. In a plane a vector can be represented graphically by an arrow whose length is proportional to the magnitude and whose orientation indicates the direction; analytically it is expressed by its components along two perpendicular axes, usually the x‑axis and the y‑axis. The component form v = (vₓ, vᵧ) allows algebraic manipulation such as addition, subtraction and multiplication by a scalar, and it is the basis for all subsequent kinematic analysis in two dimensions. The magnitude of a vector is obtained from the Pythagorean theorem as |v| = √(vₓ² + vᵧ²) and its direction is given by the angle θ = tan⁻¹(vᵧ / vₓ) measured from the positive x‑axis.

Unit vectors î and ĵ of magnitude one along the x and y directions provide a compact notation: v = vₓ î + vᵧ ĵ. This notation makes it straightforward to write the equations of motion for a particle moving in a plane because the time derivatives of the components give the velocity and acceleration components directly. The concept of a position vector r = x î + y ĵ is introduced early, and its derivative with respect to time yields the velocity vector v = dr/dt = (dx/dt) î + (dy/dt) ĵ, while a second derivative gives the acceleration vector a = dv/dt.

1.2 Vector Addition and Subtraction

When two vectors a and b act simultaneously, their resultant is found by vector addition. The triangle law states that if the tail of b is placed at the head of a, the resultant is the vector from the tail of a to the head of b; the parallelogram law gives the same resultant as the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by a and b as adjacent sides. Both graphical methods are equivalent and lead to the component rule a + b = (aₓ + bₓ) î + (aᵧ + bᵧ) ĵ, which is the most convenient for calculations. Subtraction is defined as a – b = a + (–b), where –b has the same magnitude as b but opposite direction; graphically this corresponds to reversing the arrow of b before applying the triangle law.

The commutative property a + b = b + a and the associative property (a + b) + c = a + (b + c) hold for vector addition, allowing any number of vectors to be combined in any order. These algebraic properties are essential when dealing with multiple forces or velocities acting on a body, because they guarantee that the net effect is independent of the sequence in which the individual contributions are summed.

1.3 Resolution of Vectors into Components

Any vector in a plane can be resolved into two perpendicular components, usually along the coordinate axes. The process uses the trigonometric relations vₓ = |v| cos θ and vᵧ = |v| sin θ, where θ is the angle the vector makes with the positive x‑axis. Resolution is the inverse of vector addition: given the components, the original vector is reconstructed by the Pythagorean theorem and the inverse tangent function. This technique is indispensable for analysing motion on an inclined plane, projectile motion, and any situation where forces or velocities are not aligned with the axes.

When a vector is resolved, the components can be treated independently in the equations of motion because the x and y directions are orthogonal. For example, in projectile motion the horizontal component of velocity remains constant (neglecting air resistance) while the vertical component changes under the influence of gravity. The ability to separate a two‑dimensional problem into two one‑dimensional problems is a direct consequence of the linear nature of vector components.

Relative Velocity

2.1 Concept of Relative Velocity

Relative velocity describes how the velocity of one object appears from the reference frame of another moving object. If object A moves with velocity v_A and object B moves with velocity v_B in a common inertial frame, the velocity of A relative to B is v_AB = v_A – v_B. This definition follows directly from vector subtraction and reflects the fact that an observer moving with B must subtract B’s own motion to perceive A’s motion. The relative velocity is itself a vector, possessing magnitude and direction, and it obeys the same addition rules as any other vector.

The concept is symmetric: v_BA = –v_AB, meaning that the velocity of B relative to A is the negative of the velocity of A relative to B. This symmetry is a direct consequence of the vector nature of velocity and is useful for checking the consistency of calculations. In practical problems the relative velocity is often the quantity of interest, for instance when determining the apparent speed of a train from a moving car or the drift of a boat crossing a flowing river.

2.2 Relative Velocity in One and Two Dimensions

In one dimension the relative velocity reduces to a simple algebraic difference of speeds with appropriate signs for direction. In two dimensions the vector subtraction must be performed component‑wise: v_ABₓ = v_Aₓ – v_Bₓ and v_ABᵧ = v_Aᵧ – v_Bᵧ. The magnitude of the relative velocity is then √(v_ABₓ² + v_ABᵧ²) and its direction is given by tan⁻¹(v_ABᵧ / v_ABₓ). This component approach makes it straightforward to handle cases where the two objects move at arbitrary angles to each other.

When both objects have constant velocities, the relative velocity is also constant, and the relative motion is a straight line at constant speed. If either object accelerates, the relative velocity becomes time‑dependent and the relative trajectory can be curved. The analysis of such situations follows the same principles: differentiate the relative position vector r_AB = r_A – r_B to obtain v_AB, and differentiate again for the relative acceleration a_AB = a_A – a_B.

2.3 Applications to River Crossing and Aircraft Navigation

A classic application is a boat attempting to cross a river that flows with velocity v_r. The boat’s engine provides a velocity v_b relative to the water. The resultant velocity of the boat relative to the ground is v_g = v_b + v_r. To reach a point directly opposite the starting point, the boat must aim upstream at an angle such that the upstream component of v_b cancels the river’s downstream flow. The required heading angle θ satisfies v_b sin θ = v_r, and the crossing time is the river width divided by the component of v_b perpendicular to the banks, v_b cos θ.

In aircraft navigation the wind velocity v_w plays the role of the river current. The pilot sets an airspeed v_a and a heading; the ground track is the vector sum v_g = v_a + v_w. Navigators use the wind triangle, a graphical representation of this vector addition, to determine the heading needed to achieve a desired ground track and to compute the ground speed. These problems illustrate how relative velocity concepts translate directly into practical navigation techniques used daily in aviation and maritime operations.

Example: River Crossing
A river 200 m wide flows eastward at 3 m s⁻¹. A boat can move at 5 m s⁻¹ relative to the water. The captain wants to land at a point directly north of the starting point. Find the heading angle relative to north and the time taken to cross.
Let the river flow be along +x (east) and the desired crossing direction be +y (north). The river velocity v_r = 3 î m s⁻¹. The boat’s velocity relative to water has magnitude 5 m s⁻¹ and makes an angle θ west of north, so its components are v_b = –5 sin θ î + 5 cos θ ĵ. For zero net x‑displacement, the x‑component of the ground velocity must vanish: –5 sin θ + 3 = 0 → sin θ = 3/5 → θ = 36.9°. The y‑component of ground velocity is 5 cos θ = 5 × 0.8 = 4 m s⁻¹. Crossing time = width / v_y = 200 m / 4 m s⁻¹ = 50 s. The boat must head 36.9° west of north and will take 50 seconds to cross.
Exam‑Day Insight
When solving relative‑velocity problems, always draw a vector diagram first; assign a consistent coordinate system and write the vector equation v_ground = v_object + v_medium. The sign of each component follows directly from the diagram, preventing the common error of adding magnitudes instead of vectors.

Projectile Motion

3.1 Kinematic Equations for a Projectile

A projectile is any object that is launched into the air and moves under the influence of gravity alone, neglecting air resistance. The motion can be analysed by separating the initial velocity u into horizontal and vertical components: uₓ = u cos α and uᵧ = u sin α, where α is the launch angle measured from the horizontal. Because there is no horizontal force (ignoring drag), the horizontal acceleration is zero and the horizontal velocity remains constant at uₓ throughout the flight. The vertical motion is uniformly accelerated with acceleration aᵧ = –g, where g = 9.8 m s⁻² directed downward.

The standard kinematic equations apply independently to each component. For the horizontal direction: x = uₓ t. For the vertical direction: y = uᵧ t – ½ g t², vᵧ = uᵧ – g t, and vᵧ² = uᵧ² – 2 g y. These equations are derived from the definitions of velocity and acceleration and are valid as long as the acceleration remains constant, which is true for the vertical component under the uniform gravity approximation.

3.2 Time of Flight, Maximum Height and Range

The time of flight T is the total time the projectile remains airborne before returning to the same vertical level from which it was launched. Setting y = 0 in the vertical displacement equation gives 0 = uᵧ T – ½ g T², yielding T = 2 uᵧ / g = 2 u sin α / g. The maximum height H is reached when the vertical velocity becomes zero; using vᵧ = 0 gives H = uᵧ² / (2 g) = u² sin² α / (2 g). The horizontal range R is the horizontal distance covered during the time of flight: R = uₓ T = u cos α × (2 u sin α / g) = u² sin 2α / g. These three formulae are the cornerstone of projectile‑motion problems and are frequently tested in examinations.

It is important to note that the range formula assumes the launch and landing heights are equal. If the projectile lands at a different height, the quadratic equation for y must be solved for the appropriate time, and the range becomes R = uₓ t_land where t_land is the positive root of the vertical displacement equation. The symmetry of the trajectory about the apex is a direct consequence of the constant horizontal velocity and the symmetric nature of the vertical motion under uniform acceleration.

3.3 Effect of Launch Angle on Range

For a given initial speed u, the range R = u² sin 2α / g depends on the launch angle through the factor sin 2α. The sine function attains its maximum value of 1 when 2α = 90°, i.e. α = 45°. Therefore the maximum possible range on level ground is R_max = u² / g, achieved at a 45° launch angle. Complementary angles α and (90° – α) give the same range because sin 2α = sin (180° – 2α). This symmetry explains why a projectile launched at 30° and another at 60° with the same speed land at the same horizontal distance, although their maximum heights and times of flight differ.

3.4 Projectile Motion with Air Resistance (Qualitative)

When air resistance is considered, the horizontal velocity is no longer constant because a drag force opposite to the velocity acts on the projectile. The drag force is often modelled as proportional to the square of the speed, F_d = –k v², where k depends on the shape, size, and density of air, and the drag coefficient. This introduces a non‑linear differential equation that generally requires numerical solution. Qualitatively, air resistance reduces the range, lowers the maximum height, and makes the trajectory asymmetric: the descent is steeper than the ascent because the vertical component of drag adds to gravity during the fall and opposes gravity during the rise. The optimal launch angle for maximum range shifts to a value less than 45°, typically around 30°–35° for typical projectiles.

Uniform Circular Motion

4.1 Angular Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

Uniform circular motion occurs when a particle moves along a circular path of radius r with constant speed v. The angular displacement θ (in radians) is related to the arc length s by s = r θ. The angular velocity ω is defined as the rate of change of angular displacement: ω = dθ/dt. For uniform motion ω is constant and related to the linear speed by v = ω r. The period T, the time for one complete revolution, is T = 2π / ω, and the frequency f = 1/T = ω / 2π.

Even though the speed is constant, the velocity vector changes direction continuously, so there is an acceleration. The acceleration vector always points toward the centre of the circle and is called centripetal acceleration. Its magnitude is a_c = v² / r = ω² r. This result follows from differentiating the velocity vector v = ω r (–sin ωt î + cos ωt ĵ) with respect to time, yielding a = –ω² r (cos ωt î + sin ωt ĵ), which has magnitude ω² r and direction radially inward.

4.2 Centripetal Force and Its Sources

Newton’s second law requires a net force equal to mass times acceleration. For uniform circular motion the required net force is the centripetal force F_c = m a_c = m v² / r = m ω² r, directed toward the centre. This force is not a new type of force; it is the resultant of real forces such as tension, gravity, friction or normal reaction, depending on the physical situation. For a satellite in orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force; for a car on a banked curve, the horizontal component of the normal reaction (and possibly friction) provides it; for a stone whirled on a string, the tension in the string is the centripetal force.

4.3 Relationship Between Linear and Angular Quantities

The linear quantities (displacement s, velocity v, acceleration a) and angular quantities (θ, ω, α) are linked by the radius r. The fundamental relations are s = r θ, v = r ω, and a_t = r α for tangential acceleration. In uniform circular motion the angular acceleration α is zero, so the tangential acceleration vanishes and the total acceleration is purely centripetal. These relationships allow one to switch between linear and angular descriptions seamlessly, which is particularly useful when dealing with rotating rigid bodies where different points have different linear speeds but share the same angular velocity.

Applications and Problem‑Solving Strategies

5.1 Combining Vector Methods with Kinematics

Many two‑dimensional problems require the simultaneous use of vector addition, resolution, and the kinematic equations. A systematic approach begins by drawing a clear diagram showing all known vectors, choosing a convenient coordinate system, and writing the vector equations for position, velocity and acceleration. The components are then treated as independent one‑dimensional motions, each governed by the appropriate constant‑acceleration formulae. After solving for the unknown components, the results are recombined to obtain the magnitude and direction of the required vector quantity. This method reduces the chance of algebraic errors and makes the physical reasoning transparent.

5.2 Systematic Approach to Multi‑Stage Problems

Problems that involve several phases — for example a projectile launched from a moving platform, or a particle that moves in a circle and then is released — are best tackled by dividing the motion into stages. For each stage, identify the forces acting, determine the acceleration vector, and write the kinematic equations with appropriate initial conditions taken from the final state of the preceding stage. Continuity of position and velocity at the stage boundaries provides the linking conditions. Keeping a table of known and unknown variables for each stage helps to organise the information and to verify that the number of independent equations matches the number of unknowns.

5.3 Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

A frequent mistake is to treat the magnitude of a vector sum as the sum of magnitudes; always perform vector addition component‑wise or graphically. Another error is to use the range formula R = u² sin 2α / g when the launch and landing heights differ; in such cases the full quadratic for vertical displacement must be solved. In circular motion, students sometimes confuse centripetal acceleration with tangential acceleration; remember that centripetal acceleration exists whenever the direction of velocity changes, even if the speed is constant, whereas tangential acceleration appears only when the speed changes. Careful reading of the problem statement, consistent sign conventions, and a final check of units and physical plausibility are essential habits for accurate solutions.

Mastering the interplay of vectors, relative motion, projectile trajectories and circular dynamics equips a student to analyse a wide variety of physical situations encountered in both academic examinations and real‑world engineering challenges.

Non‑Uniform Circular Motion and Tangential Acceleration

When the speed of a particle moving on a circular path changes, the motion is no longer uniform. The acceleration vector then has two orthogonal components: a radial (centripetal) component ar = v² / r = ω² r directed toward the centre, and a tangential component at = r α directed along the instantaneous velocity, where α = dω/dt is the angular acceleration. The total acceleration is the vector sum a = ar + at, and its magnitude is √(ar² + at²). The direction of the total acceleration makes an angle φ = tan⁻¹(at/ar) with the radial line. This decomposition is essential for problems where a torque or a tangential force acts, such as a car accelerating on a banked curve, a particle on a string being pulled, or a rotating shaft with a varying angular speed.

The work–energy theorem in circular motion takes a simple form because the radial force does no work (it is always perpendicular to the displacement). Only the tangential force Ft = m at changes the kinetic energy: dK = Ft·ds = m at·(r dθ) = m r α·(r dθ) = m r² α dθ. Integrating gives the change in kinetic energy in terms of the angular displacement. Power delivered by the tangential force is P = Ft v = m at v = m r α·(r ω) = m r² ω α. These relations allow one to connect torque, angular acceleration, and energy without solving the full trajectory.

A common textbook situation is a particle of mass m attached to a light string that passes through a hole in a smooth horizontal table. The particle moves in a circle of radius r while the string is pulled downwards with a constant force F. Because the force is radial, angular momentum L = m r² ω is conserved, so ω ∝ 1/r². The radial equation of motion gives m (r̈ – r ω²) = –F. Substituting ω = L/(m r²) yields a differential equation for r(t) that can be solved to find the time evolution of the radius and the tension in the string. This example illustrates how conservation of angular momentum couples with the radial dynamics when the motion is non‑uniform.

In problems involving a rotating rigid body, different points share the same angular velocity ω and angular acceleration α, but their linear speeds and tangential accelerations differ proportionally to their distances from the axis. The total acceleration of a point at radius r is therefore a = r α θ̂ – r ω² r̂. When analysing a rolling wheel that is accelerating, the instantaneous centre of rotation is the contact point with the ground, and the acceleration of the centre of mass is acm = R α horizontally, while the top point has acceleration 2 R α horizontally. Recognising these relationships prevents the frequent error of assigning the same linear acceleration to all points of a rotating body.

Projectile Motion on an Inclined Plane

When a projectile is launched from a point on a plane inclined at an angle α to the horizontal, it is convenient to choose axes along the plane (x‑axis) and perpendicular to the plane (y‑axis). The initial velocity components become ux = u cos(θ – α) and uy = u sin(θ – α), where θ is the launch angle measured from the horizontal. The acceleration components are ax = –g sin α (down the plane) and ay = –g cos α (into the plane). The projectile leaves the plane when y = 0 again, giving the time of flight T = 2 u sin(θ – α) / (g cos α). The range measured along the plane is then R = ux T + ½ ax T², which simplifies to the well‑known formula R = (u² / (g cos² α)) [ sin(2θ – α) – sin α ].

The maximum height reached above the inclined plane occurs when the perpendicular velocity component becomes zero: tmax = u sin(θ – α) / (g cos α). Substituting into the y‑displacement gives Hmax = (u² sin²(θ – α)) / (2 g cos² α). For a given launch speed u and plane inclination α, the range is maximised when 2θ – α = 90°, i.e. θ = 45° + α/2. This result reduces to the familiar θ = 45° for a horizontal plane (α = 0). The maximum range on the inclined plane is Rmax = u² / (g (1 + sin α)). If the projectile is launched down the plane (θ < α), the same formulas apply with appropriate sign conventions; the time of flight is still given by the positive root of the quadratic in t obtained from y = 0.

An important extension is the case where the launch point and the landing point are at different heights on the inclined plane. Let the vertical separation along the plane be Δy (positive if the landing point is higher). The vertical motion equation becomes Δy = u sin(θ – α) t – ½ g cos α t². Solving this quadratic for t yields two possible times; the larger positive root is the time of flight. The range along the plane is then R = u cos(θ – α) t – ½ g sin α t². This formulation is essential for problems involving a projectile fired from a cliff onto a slope, or a ball launched from a ramp onto another ramp.

When air resistance is considered qualitatively, the trajectory is no longer a parabola in the plane coordinates. The drag force opposes the instantaneous velocity, reducing both the range and the maximum height. The optimal launch angle for maximum range shifts to a value smaller than 45° + α/2 because the projectile spends more time in the air at higher angles, suffering greater cumulative drag. In competitive examinations, the qualitative effect is often tested by asking how the range changes if the launch angle is increased beyond the vacuum optimum, or by comparing the range on an inclined plane with and without air resistance.

Worked Example: Range on an Inclined Plane
A projectile is launched with speed 20 m/s at an angle of 60° to the horizontal from the foot of a plane inclined at 30° to the horizontal. Find the range along the plane and the maximum height above the plane. Take g = 10 m/s².
Choose axes along the plane (x) and perpendicular (y). The inclination α = 30°. Launch angle relative to horizontal θ = 60°, so θ – α = 30°. Initial components: uₓ = 20 cos 30° = 20 × √3/2 = 10√3 m/s; uᵧ = 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s. Acceleration components: aₓ = –g sin α = –10 × ½ = –5 m/s²; aᵧ = –g cos α = –10 × √3/2 = –5√3 m/s². Time of flight from y = 0: T = 2 uᵧ / (g cos α) = 2×10 / (10×√3/2) = 20 / (5√3) = 4/√3 s ≈ 2.31 s. Range along plane: R = uₓ T + ½ aₓ T² = (10√3)(4/√3) + ½(–5)(16/3) = 40 – 40/3 = 80/3 m ≈ 26.7 m. Maximum height above plane: H = uᵧ² / (2 g cos α) = 100 / (2×10×√3/2) = 100 / (10√3) = 10/√3 m ≈ 5.77 m.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a projectile problem involves an inclined plane, immediately rotate the axes to align with the plane. Write down the components of g along and perpendicular to the plane; this avoids sign errors. Memorise the compact range formula R = (u²/(g cos²α))[sin(2θ–α)–sinα] and the condition for maximum range θ = 45°+α/2. If the launch and landing heights differ, set up the quadratic in time from the perpendicular displacement and solve for the positive root. Always check the limiting case α = 0 to verify that your expressions reduce to the standard horizontal‑plane formulas.

Relative Motion in Rotating Reference Frames

Analyzing motion from a frame that rotates with constant angular velocity ω introduces two fictitious accelerations: the centrifugal acceleration acf = ω × (ω × r) directed radially outward, and the Coriolis acceleration acor = 2 ω × vrot perpendicular to the velocity in the rotating frame. The complete transformation of acceleration from an inertial frame to a rotating frame is ainertial = arot + 2 ω × vrot + ω × (ω × r) + dω/dt × r. The last term vanishes for constant ω. These terms are not real forces; they appear because the basis vectors of the rotating frame change with time. In problems where a particle moves on a rotating turntable, or where the motion of a projectile is observed from the rotating Earth, the fictitious accelerations must be included to apply Newton’s second law in the rotating frame.

Consider a particle sliding without friction in a straight radial groove on a horizontal turntable rotating with constant angular speed ω. In the inertial frame the particle moves in a straight line (no horizontal forces), but in the rotating frame it experiences a centrifugal force m ω² r outward and a Coriolis force 2 m ω vrot perpendicular to the groove. The equation of motion along the groove is m r̈ = m ω² r, giving r = r₀ cosh(ω t) + (v₀/ω) sinh(ω t). The Coriolis force does not affect the radial motion because it is perpendicular to the groove, but it exerts a normal reaction on the groove walls. This example shows how the fictitious forces simplify the description of constrained motion in a rotating frame.

On the Earth, the angular velocity vector ωE points along the polar axis. For a projectile fired horizontally at latitude λ, the Coriolis acceleration has a vertical component 2 ωE v cos λ (affecting the apparent weight) and a horizontal component 2 ωE v sin λ deflecting the trajectory to the right in the Northern Hemisphere. The centrifugal acceleration slightly reduces the effective gravity and makes the plumb line deviate from the true radial direction. In most introductory problems these effects are neglected, but they become significant for long‑range artillery, satellite launches, and the Foucault pendulum, where the plane of oscillation rotates at a rate ωE sin λ.

A frequent examination question asks for the trajectory of a particle projected radially outward on a rotating platform with initial speed u relative to the platform. In the rotating frame the radial equation is r̈ – ω² r = 0 (since the Coriolis force is azimuthal). The solution is r = (u/ω) sinh(ω t) for zero initial radius. The azimuthal motion is governed by the Coriolis force: m (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇) = 0, which integrates to r² θ̇ = constant = 0 if the particle starts from rest in the inertial frame. Thus the particle moves radially outward in the rotating frame while lagging behind in the inertial frame. Recognising which terms belong to which frame is the key to avoiding sign errors.

Vector Calculus Approach to Planar Motion

Describing planar motion with vectors in a fixed Cartesian basis is straightforward, but polar coordinates often reveal the physics more transparently. Let the position vector be r = r r̂, where r̂ and θ̂ are the radial and transverse unit vectors. Their time derivatives are dr̂/dt = θ̇ θ̂ and dθ̂/dt = –θ̇ r̂. The velocity is v = dr/dt = ṙ r̂ + r θ̇ θ̂. The acceleration follows by differentiating again: a = (r̈ – r θ̇²) r̂ + (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇) θ̂. The radial component contains the centripetal term –r θ̇² and the transverse component contains the Coriolis‑like term 2 ṙ θ̇. For uniform circular motion ṙ = 0, θ̈ = 0, so a = –r ω² r̂, recovering the familiar centripetal acceleration.

These expressions are powerful for problems where the radius changes with time, such as a bead sliding on a rotating wire, a particle moving under a central force, or a spiral trajectory. For a central force F = –k r̂, the transverse equation gives d/dt (r² θ̇) = 0, i.e. conservation of angular momentum L = m r² θ̇. Substituting θ̇ = L/(m r²) into the radial equation yields a one‑dimensional effective‑potential problem: m r̈ = –k + L²/(m r³). This reduction is the standard method for analysing planetary orbits and Rutherford scattering.

When the motion is described in a rotating frame with angular velocity Ω, the unit vectors rotate with the frame, and the time derivative of any vector A is (dA/dt)inertial = (dA/dt)rot + Ω × A. Applying this to the position vector gives the velocity transformation vinertial = vrot + Ω × r, and a second application yields the acceleration transformation quoted earlier. The vector‑calculus derivation makes the origin of the Coriolis and centrifugal terms completely transparent: they arise from the Ω × operator acting on the velocity and position vectors respectively. This formalism is indispensable for advanced problems in rigid‑body dynamics and for understanding the motion of particles in magnetic fields, where the Lorentz force q v × B plays a role analogous to the Coriolis force.

An illustrative example is a particle moving in an Archimedean spiral r = a θ with constant angular speed θ̇ = ω. Then ṙ = a ω, r̈ = 0. The velocity is v = a ω r̂ + a ω θ θ̂, and the acceleration is a = (–a ω² θ) r̂ + (2 a ω²) θ̂. The radial acceleration is not simply centripetal; it contains a term proportional to θ because the radius grows linearly with angle. The transverse acceleration is constant, reflecting the constant Coriolis‑type term 2 ṙ θ̇. Such problems test the ability to differentiate vector expressions in polar coordinates and to interpret each term physically.

Applications of Relative Velocity in Collision Avoidance

The concept of relative velocity is the cornerstone of collision‑avoidance analysis for two moving objects. Let the position vectors of objects A and B be rA and rB, and their velocities vA and vB. The relative position is r = rB – rA and the relative velocity is v = vB – vA. In the frame of A, B moves with constant velocity v (assuming no acceleration). The distance between them at time t is |r + v t|. The time of closest approach is found by minimising this distance: differentiate |r + v t|² with respect to t and set to zero, yielding tca = – (r·v) / |v|². If tca > 0, the closest approach occurs in the future; if tca ≤ 0, the objects are already separating.

The minimum separation distance is dmin = |r × v| / |v|. A collision occurs if and only if dmin = 0, which is equivalent to the condition that r is parallel to v (i.e. r × v = 0) and tca > 0. In practical terms, two ships on constant courses will collide if the bearing of one from the other remains constant (constant bearing, decreasing range). This is the classic “constant bearing” rule used in maritime navigation. The same mathematics applies to aircraft, automobiles at intersections, and particles in a gas.

When the objects have finite sizes, the collision condition becomes dmin ≤ RA + RB, where R are the effective radii. If the objects can manoeuvre, the problem becomes one of choosing a velocity change Δv for one object such that the new relative velocity v' = v + Δv yields a safe dmin. The minimum‑Δv avoidance manoeuvre is often perpendicular to the original relative velocity vector, because that maximises the increase in dmin per unit Δv. This principle underlies the “collision avoidance manoeuvre” algorithms used in air traffic control and autonomous vehicle software.

A typical examination problem: two particles are projected simultaneously from points separated by a horizontal distance D. Particle 1 is launched at speed u₁ and angle θ₁; particle 2 at speed u₂ and angle θ₂. Determine whether they collide in mid‑air. The relative motion is a projectile with initial relative velocity v = u₂ (cos θ₂ î + sin θ₂ ĵ) – u₁ (cos θ₁ î + sin θ₁ ĵ) and relative acceleration a = 0 (since both experience the same gravity). The relative position at time t is r(t) = r₀ + v t, where r₀ is the initial separation vector. A collision requires r(t) = 0 for some t > 0, i.e. r₀ must be anti‑parallel to v and the time t = –|r₀|/|v| must be positive and less than the time of flight of both projectiles. Projectile Motion with Linear Air Resistance: Analytical Solutions

When a projectile moves through a fluid at low Reynolds numbers the drag force is approximately proportional to the instantaneous velocity, Fd = –k v, where k is a positive constant that depends on the fluid viscosity and the size of the object. The equation of motion in vector form becomes m dv/dt = m g – k v. Because the drag is linear, the horizontal and vertical components decouple and each satisfies a first‑order linear differential equation. For the horizontal component vx we have m dvx/dt = –k vx, giving vx(t) = ux e–(k/m)t and x(t) = (m ux/k)(1 – e–(k/m)t). The vertical component obeys m dvy/dt = –mg – k vy. Its solution is vy(t) = (uy + mg/k) e–(k/m)t – mg/k and y(t) = (m/k)(uy + mg/k)(1 – e–(k/m)t) – (mg/k)t. The time of flight T is found by solving y(T) = 0 (for launch and landing at the same height), which yields a transcendental equation (uy + mg/k)(1 – e–(k/m)T) = (mg/k)T. No closed‑form elementary solution exists, but for small k a perturbation expansion T ≈ 2uy/g – (8k uy2)/(3m g2) is accurate. The range R = x(T) is then R ≈ (2uxuy/g) – (8k uxuy2)/(3m g2). These expressions show that linear drag reduces both time of flight and range, and that the optimal launch angle for maximum range becomes less than 45°; the correction to the angle is of order k u/(m g). In examinations, candidates are often asked to derive the velocity components, to set up the transcendental equation for T, or to obtain the first‑order correction to the range using a series expansion. Mastery of the exponential decay of velocity components and the ability to linearise the transcendental equation are the key skills tested.

Relative Motion in Uniformly Accelerating Frames: Pseudo Forces and Trajectory Transformation

Consider a frame S' that translates with a constant acceleration a0 relative to an inertial frame S. The position vectors are related by r = r' + r0(t), where r0(t) = ½ a0 t2 + v0 t + r0(0). Differentiating twice gives a = a' + a0. Newton’s second law in S is m a = F; substituting yields m a' = F – m a0. The term –m a0 is a pseudo force that acts on every particle in S' as if it were a uniform gravitational field of magnitude a0 directed opposite to the frame’s acceleration. Consequently, the trajectory of a projectile observed in S' is the same as that in a uniform gravitational field g' = g – a0. If S' accelerates upward with a0 = g, the effective gravity vanishes and the projectile moves in a straight line at constant velocity. If S' accelerates horizontally, the effective gravity is tilted, and the projectile’s path is a parabola whose axis is aligned with g'. The range measured in S' is obtained by replacing g with |g'| and using the launch angle relative to the direction of g'. A common examination problem: a projectile is fired from a cart accelerating horizontally with acceleration a. Find the angle relative to the cart at which the projectile must be launched so that it lands back in the cart. In the cart’s frame the effective gravity is g' = –g ĵ – a î. The condition for return to the cart is that the horizontal displacement relative to the cart is zero, which leads to tan θ = g/a where θ is measured from the horizontal of the cart. This result is independent of the launch speed, illustrating the power of the pseudo‑force method. Students must be careful to distinguish between the acceleration of the frame and the acceleration of the particle relative to the frame, and to apply the pseudo force only in the non‑inertial frame.

Worked Example: Range with Linear Drag
A particle of mass 0.2 kg is projected with speed 20 m/s at 30° to the horizontal in a medium where the drag coefficient k = 0.04 kg/s. Using the first‑order perturbation formula, estimate the horizontal range. Take g = 10 m/s².
The drag parameter is k/m = 0.04/0.2 = 0.2 s⁻¹. The initial velocity components are ux = 20 cos 30° = 10√3 ≈ 17.32 m/s, uy = 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s. The zero‑drag range is R₀ = 2 ux uy/g = 2 × 17.32 × 10 / 10 = 34.64 m. The first‑order correction is ΔR = –(8 k ux uy²)/(3 m g²) = –(8 × 0.04 × 17.32 × 100)/(3 × 0.2 × 100) = –(55.424)/(60) ≈ –0.924 m. Hence the estimated range is R ≈ 34.64 – 0.92 = 33.72 m.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mentions “small drag” or “linear resistance”, immediately write the decoupled differential equations and their exponential solutions. If the question asks for a correction to range or time of flight, expand the transcendental equation to first order in k/m; the algebra is standard and yields quick marks. For accelerating frames, draw the effective gravity vector g' = g – a0 and treat the problem as ordinary projectile motion in that tilted field. This transforms a seemingly new situation into a familiar one.

Planar Motion Under Central Forces: Conservation Laws and Orbital Equation

A central force is one that is always directed toward a fixed point (the centre) and whose magnitude depends only on the distance r from that point: F = f(r) r̂. Because the torque τ = r × F = 0, the angular momentum L = m r × v is conserved. The motion is therefore confined to a plane perpendicular to L. In polar coordinates (r, θ) the velocity is v = ṙ r̂ + r θ̇ θ̂ and the angular momentum magnitude is L = m r² θ̇. The radial equation of motion is m( r̈ – r θ̇² ) = f(r). Substituting θ̇ = L/(m r²) gives m r̈ – L²/(m r³) = f(r). It is often convenient to change the independent variable from t to θ using the Binet substitution u = 1/r. Then ṙ = –(L/m) du/dθ and r̈ = –(L² u²/m²) d²u/dθ². The radial equation becomes the Binet equation: d²u/dθ² + u = –(m/L²) f(1/u) / u². For an inverse‑square law f(r) = –k/r² (gravitational or electrostatic attraction) the right‑hand side is a constant mk/L², yielding the solution u = (mk/L²)(1 + e cos(θ – θ₀)), which is the equation of a conic section with eccentricity e. The total energy E = ½ m v² + V(r) is also conserved because the force is conservative. For bound orbits (E < 0) the trajectory is an ellipse; for E = 0 a parabola; for E > 0 a hyperbola. The areal velocity dA/dt = L/(2m) is constant (Kepler’s second law). In competitive examinations, typical questions ask to derive the Binet equation, to find the orbit equation for a given force law (e.g., f(r) = –kr for a harmonic oscillator, giving elliptical orbits centred at the origin), or to compute the period of a circular orbit using T = 2π r² m/L. A thorough grasp of the conservation of angular momentum, the Binet transformation, and the classification of orbits by energy is essential.

Instantaneous Center of Zero Velocity in Planar Rigid Body Motion

Any planar motion of a rigid body can be described at a given instant as a pure rotation about a point called the instantaneous center of zero velocity (IC). If the velocities of two points A and B on the body are known, the IC lies at the intersection of the lines perpendicular to vA and vB drawn through A and B respectively. The angular velocity of the body is ω = |vA| / |rA/IC| = |vB| / |rB/IC|. Once the IC is located, the velocity of any other point P is vP = ω × rP/IC, directed perpendicular to the line joining P to the IC. This method converts a general planar motion problem into a simple rotation problem. For a rolling wheel of radius R moving without slipping, the contact point with the ground is the IC; the centre moves with speed v = ω R and the top point has speed 2v. In a four‑bar linkage, the IC of the coupler link can be found using the Aronhold‑Kennedy theorem: the three instantaneous centers of three bodies in relative motion are collinear. This theorem allows rapid determination of velocities in mechanisms. Examination problems often provide a diagram of a mechanism with some velocities given and ask for the angular velocity of a link or the velocity of a specific point. The steps are: (1) identify all links and their known velocities, (2) locate the fixed instantaneous centers (pin joints), (3) use the Kennedy theorem to find the unknown IC, (4) compute ω from a known velocity and its distance to the IC, (5) find the required velocity using ω and the distance from the IC. A common pitfall is assuming the IC is always within the physical body; it can lie outside, and the perpendicular construction still holds. Another error is confusing the instantaneous center of zero velocity with the center of mass; they coincide only for pure rotation about the center of mass.

Complex Number Representation of Planar Vectors and Rotations

A two‑dimensional vector v = (x, y) can be represented as a complex number z = x + i y. Vector addition corresponds to complex addition. Rotation by an angle φ about the origin is achieved by multiplication with eiφ = cos φ + i sin φ: z' = z eiφ. The dot product and cross product (scalar in 2D) have simple complex forms: v·w = Re(z w*) and v × w = Im(z w*), where w* is the complex conjugate. The time derivative of a rotating vector z(t) = r(t) eiθ(t) is ż = (ṙ + i r θ̇) eiθ, which directly yields the radial and transverse components. The second derivative is z̈ = (r̈ – r θ̇² + i (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇)) eiθ, reproducing the polar acceleration components. This formalism is especially powerful for problems involving multiple rotating vectors, such as the motion of a particle on a rotating turntable or the kinematics of a slider‑crank mechanism. For example, the position of a point on a link of length L rotating with angle θ(t) is z = L eiθ. If the link is also translating with complex velocity v0, the total velocity is ż = v0 + i L θ̇ eiθ. In examinations, candidates may be asked to derive the velocity and acceleration of a point on a rigid body using complex numbers, or to solve a relative motion problem by writing the relative position as a complex number and differentiating. The method reduces vector algebra to complex algebra, often simplifying the manipulation of trigonometric expressions. Mastery requires comfort with Euler’s formula, complex conjugates, and the identification of real and imaginary parts as the Cartesian components.

Projectile Motion with Quadratic Drag: Numerical and Approximate Methods

When the drag force is proportional to the square of the speed, the equations of motion become a coupled non‑linear system that cannot be solved in closed form using elementary functions. The drag force vector is Fd = –k v² v̂, where k = ½ ρ Cd A, ρ is the fluid density, Cd the drag coefficient, and A the cross‑sectional area. The equations in Cartesian coordinates are:

  • m dvx/dt = –k v vx
  • m dvy/dt = –mg – k v vy

with v = √(vx² + vy²). Because the drag term couples the components, analytical progress is limited to perturbation expansions for small k or to the use of dimensionless parameters. A common dimensionless group is the ballistic coefficient β = m/(k R), where R is a characteristic range. For β ≫ 1 the trajectory approaches the vacuum parabola; for β ≪ 1 the projectile quickly reaches a terminal speed vt = √(mg/k) and falls nearly vertically.

Numerical integration schemes

Examination problems that require a quantitative answer with quadratic drag expect the candidate to set up a step‑by‑step algorithm such as the fourth‑order Runge–Kutta method. A typical time step Δt is chosen so that the change in speed per step is less than 1 %. The algorithm updates the state vector (x, y, vx, vy) at each step. Because the drag force depends on the instantaneous speed, the acceleration must be recomputed at each sub‑step. Candidates should be able to write the pseudo‑code or describe the sequence of k1…k4 evaluations for the velocity components.

Approximate analytical formulas

For many practical purposes, the range correction due to quadratic drag can be expressed as a series in the small parameter ε = k R/m. The first‑order correction to the vacuum range R0 = (v₀² sin 2θ)/g is ΔR ≈ –(4/3) ε R0 (1 + sin²θ). The maximum height is reduced by ΔH ≈ –(1/2) ε H0 (1 + sin²θ). These formulas are derived by expanding the equations of motion in ε and integrating the unperturbed trajectory. They are valid for ε < 0.1 and are frequently used in multiple‑choice questions where a quick estimate is required.

Terminal velocity and asymptotic behaviour

When a projectile is launched upward, it may reach a maximum height where the vertical velocity becomes zero, then accelerate downward until the drag balances weight. The terminal speed vt = √(mg/k) is approached exponentially with a time constant τ = vt/g. The time of flight is therefore longer than the vacuum value, and the impact speed is less than the launch speed. In problems involving a projectile fired from a height, the asymptotic vertical speed is vt and the horizontal speed decays to zero as t → ∞.

Coriolis Effect on Planar Projectile Motion on a Rotating Earth

The Earth’s rotation introduces a fictitious Coriolis acceleration aC = –2 Ω × v in the rotating frame, where Ω is the Earth’s angular velocity vector (magnitude 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s) and v is the velocity relative to the Earth. For motion confined to a local tangent plane (east–north–up coordinates), the Coriolis acceleration components are:

  • aE = 2 Ω sin φ vN – 2 Ω cos φ vU
  • aN = –2 Ω sin φ vE
  • aU = 2 Ω cos φ vE

where φ is the latitude, and subscripts E, N, U denote east, north, and up. The centrifugal acceleration is usually absorbed into the effective g. For typical projectile speeds (≤ 1 km/s) and ranges (≤ 100 km), the Coriolis deflection is small but measurable. The horizontal deflection after time of flight T is approximately Δx ≈ (2/3) Ω cos φ v₀ T² sin θ for an eastward launch, and Δy ≈ –Ω sin φ v₀ T² cos θ for a northward launch. These formulas assume a flat Earth and constant g; they are derived by integrating the Coriolis acceleration using the unperturbed vacuum trajectory.

Deflection of a falling body

A classic problem asks for the eastward deflection of an object dropped from height h at latitude φ. The time of fall is T = √(2h/g). The eastward velocity acquired is vE = 2 Ω cos φ g T²/3 = (4/3) Ω cos φ √(2h³/g). The deflection is Δx = (2/3) Ω cos φ √(2h³/g). For h = 100 m at φ = 45°, Δx ≈ 1.6 cm. This result is often tested as a numerical estimate.

Long‑range artillery and the Eötvös effect

For very long range projectiles (hundreds of kilometres), the curvature of the Earth and the variation of g with altitude become significant. The Eötvös effect – the change in apparent weight due to the eastward velocity component – modifies the effective g by Δg/g ≈ 2 vE Ω cos φ / g. This effect is included in advanced ballistic models but is rarely required in standard examinations. Candidates should, however, recognise that the Coriolis deflection is to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere for horizontal motion.

Worked Example: Coriolis Deflection of a Projectile
A projectile is fired due east with initial speed 500 m/s at latitude 30° N. Assuming a flat Earth, g = 9.8 m/s², and neglecting air resistance, estimate the northward deflection when the projectile returns to ground level.
The time of flight for a symmetric trajectory is T = 2 v₀ sin θ / g. For maximum range, θ = 45°, so T = 2 × 500 × sin 45° / 9.8 ≈ 72.2 s. The northward Coriolis acceleration is aN = –2 Ω sin φ vE. Using the unperturbed eastward velocity vE ≈ v₀ cos θ = 353.6 m/s (approximately constant), the northward velocity after time t is vN(t) = –2 Ω sin φ vE t. Integrating once more gives the northward displacement Δy = –Ω sin φ vE T². Substituting Ω = 7.292×10⁻⁵ rad/s, sin 30° = 0.5, vE = 353.6 m/s, T = 72.2 s yields Δy ≈ –7.292×10⁻⁵ × 0.5 × 353.6 × (72.2)² ≈ –66 m. The negative sign indicates a deflection to the south (rightward in the northern hemisphere). The magnitude is about 66 m.
Exam Strategy for Coriolis Problems
Memorise the sign convention: in the northern hemisphere, horizontal motion is deflected to the right. For a quick estimate, use Δ ≈ Ω v₀ T² times the appropriate trigonometric factor. Always state the assumption of a flat Earth and constant g unless the problem explicitly asks for curvature corrections. If the launch angle is not 45°, compute T from the vertical motion first, then apply the horizontal Coriolis integral.

Planar Motion of a Charged Particle in Crossed Electric and Magnetic Fields

When a particle of charge q and mass m moves in a plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field B = B k̂ and a uniform electric field E = E î, the Lorentz force is F = q(E + v × B). Choosing the plane as the x–y plane, the equations of motion are:

  • m dvx/dt = q E + q B vy
  • m dvy/dt = –q B vx

Defining the cyclotron frequency ωc = qB/m and the drift velocity vd = E/B, the system can be solved by shifting to a frame moving with the drift velocity. Let u = v – vd ĵ. Then the equations become du/dt = ωc (u × k̂), which describes uniform circular motion with frequency ωc. The general solution is a superposition of a circular motion (cyclotron orbit) and a constant drift perpendicular to both E and B. The trajectory in the lab frame is a cycloid if the initial velocity is zero, or a trochoid for arbitrary initial conditions.

Cycloid and trochoid trajectories

For a particle starting from rest at the origin, the parametric equations are:

  • x(t) = (vd/ωc) (ωc t – sin ωc t)
  • y(t) = (vd/ωc) (1 – cos ωc t)

This is a cycloid with cusps on the x‑axis. The maximum displacement in y is 2 vd/ωc = 2 mE/(qB²). The drift speed is E/B, independent of the particle’s charge and mass. If the particle has an initial velocity v₀, the path becomes a trochoid: the radius of the circular component is r = |v₀ – vd|/ωc. When |v₀| = vd, the circular component vanishes and the particle moves in a straight line with the drift velocity.

Applications in velocity selectors and mass spectrometers

A velocity selector uses crossed E and B fields such that only particles with v = E/B pass through undeflected. This condition is derived by setting the net force to zero: qE = qvB ⇒ v = E/B. In a mass spectrometer, after velocity selection, particles enter a region with only a magnetic field and execute circular motion with radius r = mv/(qB). Measuring r allows determination of the charge‑to‑mass ratio q/m. Examination questions often combine both stages: first find the condition for no deflection in the selector, then compute the radius in the magnetic sector.

General Plane Motion of Rigid Bodies: Instantaneous Center of Acceleration

While the instantaneous center of zero velocity (IC) simplifies velocity analysis, the instantaneous center of zero acceleration (IA) serves a similar role for accelerations. For a rigid body in general plane motion, the acceleration of any point P is aP = aO + α × rOP – ω² rOP, where O is a reference point, α is the angular acceleration, and ω is the angular velocity. The IA is the point where the total acceleration is zero at that instant. It lies at the intersection of lines perpendicular to the acceleration vectors of two points, but the construction is more involved because the centripetal component ω² r is always directed toward the center of rotation.

Locating the IA

Given the accelerations of two points A and B, the IA is found by drawing lines through A and B in the directions of aA and aB. The IA is the point from which the vectors to A and B are perpendicular to aA and aB respectively, after subtracting the centripetal components. A practical method: compute the acceleration of the IC (which is not zero in general) using aIC = –ω² rIC/O + α × rIC/O. Then the IA is the point Q such that aQ = 0. This leads to a vector equation that can be solved graphically or analytically.

Use in mechanism analysis

In a four‑bar linkage, the IA of the coupler link can be used to find the acceleration of any point on the coupler without differentiating the velocity equations. The steps are: (1) determine ω and α of each link using the IC method or relative acceleration equations, (2) locate the IA of the coupler, (3) the acceleration of any point P on the coupler is then aP = α × rP/IA – ω² rP/IA. This reduces the problem to a pure rotation about the IA. A common examination problem provides the angular velocities and accelerations of the input link and asks for the acceleration of a specific point on the coupler. The IA method is often faster than writing the full relative acceleration equations for each link.

Pursuit Curves and Interception Problems in Relative Motion

A pursuit curve describes the path of a particle (the pursuer) that always moves directly toward a moving target (the evader) with a constant speed ratio. In the plane, if the evader moves along a straight line with constant speed ve and the pursuer moves with constant speed vp, the relative motion can be analysed by placing the origin at the pursuer. The line‑of‑sight (LOS) angle λ satisfies the differential equation dλ/dt = (ve sin λ)/r, where r is the separation. The capture condition is vp > ve. The time to capture is T = (r₀ vp)/(vp² – ve²) for a head‑on initial geometry, where r₀ is the initial separation.

Constant bearing interception

In many guidance problems, the pursuer aims to maintain a constant bearing (i.e., constant LOS angle) to the target. This is achieved by setting the pursuer’s velocity component perpendicular to the LOS equal to the target’s perpendicular component: vp sin(θp – λ) = ve sin(θe – λ). If the pursuer can adjust its heading continuously, the required turn rate is ωp = (ve/r) sin(θe – λ). This is the basis of proportional navigation, where the commanded acceleration is proportional to the LOS rate. Examination questions may ask for the minimum pursuer speed to intercept a target moving on a known trajectory, or the pursuer’s heading as a function of time for a given initial geometry.

Discrete‑time interception

When the pursuer can only change heading at discrete intervals, the problem becomes a sequence of straight‑line segments. The optimal strategy is to aim at the predicted future position of the target at the end of the next interval. This leads to a set of algebraic equations that can be solved iteratively. In competitive examinations, a simplified version appears: two ships A and B move with constant velocities; find the course A must steer to intercept B in minimum time. The solution uses relative velocity: the relative velocity vAB = vA – vB must be directed along the line from A to B at the initial instant. The magnitude of vA is given, so the direction is found by constructing a vector triangle.

Projectile Motion with Constant Horizontal Wind Force

When a projectile moves through a uniform horizontal wind that exerts a constant force, the motion separates into independent vertical and horizontal components, but the horizontal acceleration is no longer zero. Let the wind force be Fw acting in the positive x‑direction, and let the projectile mass be m. The horizontal acceleration is ax = Fw/m, constant throughout the flight. The vertical motion remains governed by gravity alone: ay = –g. If the launch speed is u at angle θ to the horizontal, the initial velocity components are ux = u cos θ and uy = u sin θ.

The parametric equations become:

  • x(t) = u cos θ · t + ½ (Fw/m) t²
  • y(t) = u sin θ · t – ½ g t²

The time of flight T is obtained from y(T) = 0 (assuming launch and landing at same height), giving T = 2u sin θ / g, identical to the vacuum case because the wind does not affect vertical motion. The horizontal range R is then

R = u cos θ · T + ½ (Fw/m) T² = (2u² sin θ cos θ)/g + (2Fw u² sin² θ)/(m g²).

The first term is the standard vacuum range; the second term is the wind‑induced correction. For a tailwind (Fw > 0) the range increases, while a headwind reduces it. The optimum launch angle for maximum range is no longer 45°. Differentiating R with respect to θ and setting the derivative to zero yields

tan θopt = (u g) / (Fw u / m + g u) which simplifies to θopt = ½ arctan( mg / Fw ) for small wind forces. In the limit Fw → 0, θopt → 45°.

Maximum height H remains u² sin² θ / (2g) because vertical motion is unchanged. The trajectory equation y(x) is obtained by eliminating t; it is a parabola with a shifted vertex. The curvature at the apex is unaffected, but the whole parabola is displaced horizontally by the wind term. In examinations, a common variant asks for the range when the wind force is expressed as a constant horizontal acceleration aw; simply replace Fw/m with aw in the formulas above.

Planar Motion of a Rigid Body Rolling Without Slipping

A rigid body rolling without slipping on a fixed horizontal surface exhibits a combination of translation of its centre of mass and rotation about that centre. The no‑slip condition links the linear velocity vCM of the centre of mass to the angular velocity ω by vCM = ω R for a body of radius R (cylinder, sphere, hoop). The instantaneous centre of zero velocity (IC) is the point of contact with the ground; at that instant the body rotates purely about the IC. Consequently, the velocity of any point P on the body is vP = ω × rP/IC, where rP/IC is the vector from the contact point to P.

The acceleration of the centre of mass has two contributions: tangential at = α R (if angular acceleration α exists) and centripetal ac = ω² R directed toward the IC. The acceleration of a general point P is

aP = aCM + α × rP/CM – ω² rP/CM.

For a body rolling down an inclined plane of angle φ, the equations of motion are

  • m g sin φ – f = m aCM (translation)
  • f R = ICM α (rotation)
  • aCM = α R (no‑slip)

Eliminating the friction force f gives aCM = g sin φ / (1 + ICM/(m R²)). The denominator contains the dimensionless factor k = ICM/(m R²): k = ½ for a solid cylinder, k = ⅔ for a thin spherical shell, k = 1 for a hoop. The friction force required to maintain rolling is f = (k/(1+k)) m g sin φ; it is static and does no work. If the incline is frictionless, the body slides without rolling (α = 0).

The kinetic energy of a rolling body splits into translational and rotational parts: K = ½ m vCM² + ½ ICM ω² = ½ m vCM² (1 + k). This energy partition is frequently used in conservation‑of‑energy problems where a body rolls down a curved track or up a second incline. The instantaneous centre of acceleration (IA) for a rolling body is not the contact point unless α = 0; it lies on the line normal to the surface at a distance aCM/α from the centre of mass. Problems often ask for the acceleration of a point on the rim (e.g., the top point has acceleration 2 aCM horizontally and ω² R vertically).

Worked Example: Range with Constant Horizontal Wind
A projectile of mass 0.5 kg is launched at 30 m/s at 40° to the horizontal. A constant horizontal wind exerts a force of 2 N in the direction of motion. Take g = 9.8 m/s². Find the horizontal range.
Horizontal acceleration aw = Fw/m = 2/0.5 = 4 m/s². Time of flight T = 2u sinθ / g = 2×30×sin40° / 9.8 ≈ 60×0.6428 / 9.8 ≈ 3.94 s. Range R = u cosθ·T + ½ aw T² = 30×cos40°×3.94 + 0.5×4×(3.94)² ≈ 30×0.7660×3.94 + 2×15.52 ≈ 90.5 + 31.0 ≈ 121.5 m.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem states “constant horizontal force” or “constant horizontal acceleration”, treat the horizontal motion as uniformly accelerated motion while the vertical motion remains free fall. Write the two parametric equations first, then eliminate time only if the question asks for the trajectory equation. For rolling bodies, always check whether the no‑slip condition is given; if it is, use vCM = ωR and aCM = αR to connect translation and rotation. Remember that static friction does no work, so mechanical energy is conserved if no other non‑conservative forces act.

Constrained Planar Motion: Bead on a Rotating Wire

A bead of mass m slides without friction along a straight wire that rotates in a horizontal plane with constant angular velocity Ω about a fixed vertical axis through one end of the wire. Let r be the distance of the bead from the axis. In the inertial frame the bead’s position vector is r = r êr, where êr rotates with the wire. The velocity is v = ṙ êr + r Ω êθ and the acceleration is a = (r̈ – r Ω²) êr + (2 ṙ Ω) êθ. The only real force acting on the bead is the normal reaction from the wire, which constrains it to move along êr. Hence the transverse component of acceleration must be zero, giving 2 ṙ Ω = 0 → ṙ = 0 if Ω ≠ 0. This would imply the bead cannot move radially, which contradicts the assumption of a free bead. The resolution is that the wire must exert a transverse force (a Coriolis‑type constraint force) to keep the bead on the wire; the bead is not free in the inertial frame.

In the rotating frame attached to the wire, the bead experiences a centrifugal force m Ω² r outward and a Coriolis force –2m Ω ṙ perpendicular to the wire. Since the bead is constrained to the wire, the Coriolis force is balanced by the normal reaction, and the equation of motion along the wire is m r̈ = m Ω² r, i.e. r̈ – Ω² r = 0. The general solution is r(t) = A eΩ t + B e–Ω t. For a bead released from rest at r = r₀ at t = 0, we have ṙ(0) = 0 and r(0) = r₀, giving A = B = r₀/2 and r(t) = r₀ cosh(Ω t). The bead accelerates outward exponentially; the wire supplies the necessary centripetal force to keep it rotating.

If the wire is not horizontal but inclined at angle α to the horizontal, gravity adds a component –mg sin α along the wire (taking outward as positive). The equation becomes r̈ – Ω² r = –g sin α. The particular solution is rp = (g sin α)/Ω², and the homogeneous solution is as before. The bead may oscillate about the equilibrium position if initial conditions are chosen appropriately. This system illustrates how constraint forces do work in the inertial frame (the normal reaction has a component along the velocity) but not in the rotating frame where the constraint is workless.

Planar Motion of a Variable‑Mass System: Rocket in a Plane

A rocket moving in a vertical plane ejects mass at a constant rate ṁ = –dm/dt > 0 with exhaust velocity ue relative to the rocket. Let the rocket’s mass at time t be m(t), its velocity vector v, and the angle of its velocity relative to the horizontal be θ. The thrust force is FT = ṁ ue directed opposite to the exhaust, i.e. along the rocket’s instantaneous orientation. If the rocket’s orientation is controlled to follow the velocity vector (gravity turn), the thrust is always aligned with v. The equations of motion in vector form are

m dv/dt = m g + ṁ ue êv,

where êv is the unit vector along v and g is the gravitational acceleration vector (downward). Resolving into horizontal (x) and vertical (y) components:

  • m dvx/dt = ṁ ue cos θ
  • m dvy/dt = –m g + ṁ ue sin θ

Because vx = v cos θ and vy = v sin θ, one can derive an equation for the flight‑path angle θ:

dθ/dt = –(g/v) cos θ.

This is the same as for a constant‑mass projectile; the thrust does not directly change the direction if it is aligned with velocity. The speed equation follows from the magnitude of the vector equation:

dv/dt = –g sin θ + (ṁ/m) ue.

Integrating with m(t) = m₀ – ṁ t gives the velocity history. The range and maximum altitude are obtained by integrating the velocity components. For a rocket launched vertically (θ = 90° initially), the motion stays vertical and the equations reduce to the classic Tsiolkovsky rocket equation in a gravity field: v(t) = ue ln(m₀/m) – g t. The burnout velocity is vb = ue ln(m₀/mf) – g tb, where mf is the final mass and tb = (m₀ – mf)/ṁ. After burnout, the rocket coasts as a projectile with initial velocity vb.

If the rocket pitches over at a constant rate (programmed pitch), the thrust vector is no longer aligned with velocity, and the equations acquire additional terms. The optimal pitch program for maximum range is a classic problem in calculus of variations; the solution is a gravity turn where the angle of attack is zero. Examination questions often provide the mass flow rate, exhaust velocity, and initial mass, and ask for the burnout altitude, velocity, or the maximum height reached after burnout. The key is to treat the powered phase and the coasting phase separately, applying the rocket equation during thrust and standard projectile equations afterward.

  • Confusing the wind‑force range correction with the drag‑force correction; wind adds a constant horizontal acceleration, drag adds a velocity‑dependent deceleration.
  • Assuming the instantaneous centre of zero velocity for a rolling body is also the centre of zero acceleration; they coincide only when angular acceleration is zero.
  • Neglecting the Coriolis constraint force in the bead‑on‑rotating‑wire problem and writing r̈ = Ω² r without the transverse reaction.
  • Using the vacuum rocket equation v = ue ln(m₀/m) when gravity is present; the term –gt must be included during powered flight.
  • Treating the thrust force as acting at the centre of mass only; if the thrust line does not pass through the centre of mass, it produces a torque that changes the body’s angular momentum.
  • Forgetting that static friction in rolling without slipping does no work, so mechanical energy conservation applies only when friction is the sole non‑conservative force and it is static.
  • Applying the constant‑bearing interception formula without verifying that the pursuer’s turn rate capability exceeds the required line‑of‑sight rate.

These traps highlight the subtle distinctions that separate a correct solution from a plausible but flawed one.

Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Formulation of Planar Motion

The Lagrangian approach replaces Newton’s vector equations with a single scalar function L = T – V, where T is the kinetic energy and V the potential energy expressed in generalized coordinates. For a particle of mass m moving in the xy‑plane, a convenient choice is polar coordinates (r, θ) or Cartesian coordinates (x, y). In Cartesian form T = ½ m(ẋ² + ẏ²) and the Euler–Lagrange equations d/dt(∂L/∂ẋ) – ∂L/∂x = 0, d/dt(∂L/∂ẏ) – ∂L/∂y = 0 reproduce the familiar mẍ = –∂V/∂x, mÿ = –∂V/∂y. The power of the method appears when constraints are present: a bead on a rotating wire, a particle on a moving curve, or a projectile with a velocity‑dependent potential (e.g. magnetic Lorentz force) can be handled by adding the appropriate generalized forces or by enlarging the Lagrangian with a vector potential A such that L = ½ mv² + q v·A – qφ.

The Hamiltonian formulation follows from the Legendre transform H(q, p, t) = Σ pᵢ ẋᵢ – L, where pᵢ = ∂L/∂ẋᵢ are the canonical momenta. Hamilton’s equations ẋᵢ = ∂H/∂pᵢ, ṗᵢ = –∂H/∂qᵢ give a first‑order system that is symplectic and conserves phase‑space volume (Liouville’s theorem). For a central potential V(r) the Hamiltonian in polar coordinates is H = (pr² + pθ²/r²)/(2m) + V(r). Since θ is cyclic, pθ = m r² θ̇ = constant, which is the angular momentum. The radial motion reduces to an effective one‑dimensional problem with potential Veff(r) = V(r) + pθ²/(2m r²). This formulation makes the analysis of bounded orbits, scattering, and stability transparent and is the starting point for canonical perturbation theory used in celestial mechanics.

In problems with non‑conservative forces (air drag, thrust) the Lagrangian can be extended by a Rayleigh dissipation function ℛ = ½ k v² for linear drag, leading to generalized forces Qᵢ = –∂ℛ/∂ẋᵢ. The Hamiltonian is no longer conserved, but the equations of motion remain in the same structural form, allowing numerical integrators that preserve the symplectic structure for the conservative part while treating dissipation explicitly. This split is the basis of modern geometric integrators for long‑term orbital propagation.

Stability of Circular Orbits under Small Perturbations

A circular orbit of radius r₀ in a central potential V(r) satisfies the equilibrium condition dVeff/dr|r₀ = 0, i.e. V′(r₀) = pθ²/(m r₀³). The orbit is stable if the effective potential has a local minimum at r₀, which requires d²Veff/dr²|r₀ > 0. Computing the second derivative gives V″(r₀) + 3 pθ²/(m r₀⁴) > 0. Substituting the equilibrium condition to eliminate pθ yields the classic stability criterion V″(r₀) + 3 V′(r₀)/r₀ > 0. For a power‑law potential V = k rⁿ (with k > 0 for attraction) this becomes n(n + 1) k r₀ⁿ⁻² + 3 n k r₀ⁿ⁻² > 0 ⇒ n(n + 4) > 0. Hence circular orbits are stable for n > 0 (e.g. harmonic oscillator n = 2) and for –4 < n < 0 (e.g. Newtonian gravity n = –1). The borderline n = –4 corresponds to a force ∝ r⁻⁵ where the effective potential is flat and the orbit is marginally stable.

When a small perturbing force δF(r, θ, t) is added, the radial equation linearised about r₀ becomes a driven harmonic oscillator: δr̈ + ωr² δr = δFr/m, where the radial epicyclic frequency ωr² = (1/m) d²Veff/dr²|r₀. For the Kepler problem ωr = ωθ (the angular frequency), so the perturbation produces a closed ellipse. For a general central force ωr ≠ ωθ and the orbit precesses; the precession per revolution is Δθ = 2π(ωθ/ωr – 1). This formula underlies the calculation of Mercury’s perihelion advance in general relativity, where the effective potential acquires an extra term ∝ r⁻³, shifting ωr relative to ωθ.

In the presence of non‑central perturbations (e.g. a weak uniform magnetic field, or a small thrust not directed radially) the angular momentum is no longer strictly conserved. One can still use the Hamiltonian framework with a time‑dependent perturbation H₁(q, p, t) and apply canonical perturbation theory (Lie transforms or averaging) to obtain secular changes in the orbital elements. The method of variation of parameters (Gauss planetary equations) expresses the rates of change of semi‑major axis, eccentricity, inclination, etc., directly in terms of the perturbing force components. These tools are standard in astrodynamics for mission design and in the analysis of satellite drag.

Worked Example: Stability of a Circular Orbit in a Modified Potential
A particle of mass m moves in a central potential V(r) = –k/r + α/r² with k > 0, α > 0. Determine the condition on α for a circular orbit of radius r₀ to be stable.

Effective potential: Veff = –k/r + α/r² + L²/(2m r²) where L = pθ is the conserved angular momentum. Combine the 1/r² terms: Veff = –k/r + (α + L²/(2m))/r². Equilibrium: dVeff/dr = k/r₀² – 2(α + L²/(2m))/r₀³ = 0 ⇒ L²/(2m) = k r₀/2 – α. Second derivative: d²Veff/dr² = –2k/r³ + 6(α + L²/(2m))/r⁴. Evaluate at r₀ and substitute the equilibrium expression: d²Veff/dr²|r₀ = –2k/r₀³ + 6(k r₀/2)/r₀⁴ = –2k/r₀³ + 3k/r₀³ = k/r₀³ > 0. The stability condition is independent of α provided the equilibrium exists, i.e. L²/(2m) = k r₀/2 – α > 0 ⇒ α < k r₀/2. Thus a circular orbit exists and is stable for any α satisfying α < k r₀/2.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about stability of a circular orbit, first write the effective potential including the centrifugal term. Compute the first derivative to find the equilibrium radius (or the required angular momentum). Then compute the second derivative; a positive value guarantees linear stability. Remember that the sign of the second derivative is the same as the sign of the radial epicyclic frequency squared. If the potential contains a parameter, the stability condition often reduces to an inequality on that parameter after eliminating the angular momentum using the equilibrium relation.

Impact and Collision Dynamics in Two Dimensions

Two‑dimensional collisions are governed by conservation of linear momentum in both the x and y directions and, for elastic collisions, conservation of kinetic energy. The standard approach introduces a line of centres (the normal direction) at the instant of contact and a tangential direction perpendicular to it. For smooth (frictionless) spheres the impulse acts only along the normal; the tangential components of velocity of each body remain unchanged. Let the masses be m₁, m₂, initial velocities u₁, u₂, and final velocities v₁, v₂. Decompose each velocity into normal (n) and tangential (t) components: uᵢ = uᵢₙ n̂ + uᵢₜ t̂. The normal components obey the one‑dimensional collision formulas with coefficient of restitution e: v₁ₙ = (m₁ u₁ₙ + m₂ u₂ₙ + e m₂(u₂ₙ – u₁ₙ))/(m₁ + m₂), v₂ₙ = (m₁ u₁ₙ + m₂ u₂ₙ + e m₁(u₁ₙ – u₂ₙ))/(m₁ + m₂). The tangential components are unchanged: v₁ₜ = u₁ₜ, v₂ₜ = u₂ₜ. Re‑assemble the vectors to obtain the post‑collision velocities.

When friction is present during impact, an impulse also acts in the tangential direction. The Coulomb friction model limits the tangential impulse Jₜ to |Jₜ| ≤ μ |Jₙ|, where μ is the coefficient of friction and Jₙ the normal impulse. If the tangential relative velocity at the contact point reverses sign before the friction limit is reached, the bodies stick in the tangential direction (rolling contact) and the final tangential relative velocity is zero. Otherwise sliding persists and the tangential impulse saturates at ±μ |Jₙ|. This leads to a piecewise solution that must be checked for consistency. The same framework extends to collisions of rigid bodies with rotation: the contact point velocities include contributions from angular velocities, and the impulse changes both linear and angular momenta according to Δp = J, ΔL = r × J where r is the vector from the centre of mass to the contact point.

Oblique impact on a massive fixed plane is a common textbook problem. For a particle striking a horizontal plane with speed u at angle θ to the normal, the normal component becomes e u cos θ (reversed) while the tangential component remains u sin θ if the plane is smooth. The rebound angle φ satisfies tan φ = (tan θ)/e. For a rough plane with friction coefficient μ, the tangential impulse reduces the tangential speed; if μ ≥ tan θ/e the particle can leave the surface with zero tangential velocity (purely normal rebound). These results are frequently tested in multiple‑choice questions where the examinee must identify the correct expression for the rebound angle or the condition for rolling without slip after impact.

Optimal Control of Planar Trajectories Using Pontryagin's Maximum Principle

Many aerospace and robotics problems ask for the control history (thrust direction, magnitude, or steering angle) that transfers a vehicle from an initial state to a target state while minimising a cost such as fuel consumption or time. Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle (PMP) provides necessary conditions for optimality. For a planar system with state vector x = (x, y, vₓ, vᵧ) and control u (e.g. thrust angle α with bounded magnitude Tₘₐₓ), the dynamics are ẋ = f(x, u). The Hamiltonian of PMP is H = λ·f(x, u) + λ₀ L(x, u), where λ are the costate variables and λ₀ ≤ 0 is a constant (usually –1 for minimisation). The optimal control maximises H pointwise: u*(t) = arg maxu∈U H(x, u, λ, t). The costates evolve according to λ̇ = –∂H/∂x, with boundary conditions determined by the terminal constraints (fixed final state, free final time, etc.).

For the classic minimum‑fuel planar rocket with constant exhaust velocity and thrust magnitude bounded by Tₘₐₓ, the optimal thrust direction aligns with the velocity costate vector (the primer vector). The primer vector satisfies a linear adjoint equation; its magnitude determines whether the thrust is on (bang) or off (coast). The well‑known “bang‑bang” structure appears: full thrust when the switching function is positive, zero thrust when negative, and possibly singular arcs when the switching function vanishes over an interval. In the planar gravity‑turn problem the optimal pitch program is exactly the gravity turn (zero angle of attack), which can be derived by showing that the primer vector remains parallel to the velocity vector throughout the powered phase.

Time‑optimal interception of a moving target in the plane (the “homicidal chauffeur” or “missile guidance” problem) also falls under PMP. The state includes the relative position and velocity; the control is the pursuer’s lateral acceleration bounded by aₘₐₓ. The optimal law is a proportional navigation law with a time‑varying gain that can be obtained from the costate solution. For a non‑maneuvering target the optimal trajectory is a straight line with constant bearing (the constant‑bearing interception condition). When the target maneuvers, the problem becomes a differential game; the Isaacs equation replaces the Hamiltonian, and the solution involves a barrier surface separating capture from escape regions. These concepts are the theoretical foundation of modern missile guidance laws such as augmented proportional navigation.

Relativistic Corrections to Planar Projectile Motion

At speeds approaching a significant fraction of the speed of light c, Newtonian kinematics must be replaced by special relativity. The relativistic momentum is p = γ m v with γ = 1/√(1 – v²/c²). The equation of motion under a constant force F (e.g. uniform gravity mg near Earth’s surface) becomes d(γ m v)/dt = F. For a projectile launched with initial speed v₀ at angle θ₀, the horizontal component of momentum is conserved because the gravitational force is vertical: γ m vₓ = constant = γ₀ m v₀ cos θ₀. The vertical motion satisfies d(γ m vᵧ)/dt = –m g. Integrating gives γ vᵧ = γ₀ v₀ sin θ₀ – g t. The trajectory can be expressed parametrically: x(t) = ∫ vₓ dt, y(t) = ∫ vᵧ dt. Because γ depends on the total speed, the horizontal speed is not strictly constant; it decreases slightly as the vertical speed changes, an effect of order (v²/c²).

The range R for a given launch angle can be expanded in powers of v₀²/c². To first order the relativistic correction reduces the range compared with the Newtonian prediction: R ≈ R₀ [1 – (3/4)(v₀²/c²) sin² θ₀], where R₀ = (v₀²/g) sin 2θ₀ is the vacuum range. The maximum range no longer occurs at 45°; the optimal angle shifts to slightly less than 45° because the relativistic mass increase penalises the larger vertical component. The time of flight also increases due to the effective increase of inertia. These corrections are tiny for ordinary projectiles (v₀ ~ 1 km/s gives v₀²/c² ~ 10⁻⁸) but become relevant for hypervelocity projectiles, particle beams, or astrophysical jets.

In a uniform magnetic field B perpendicular to the plane, the relativistic Lorentz force gives a circular motion with radius r = γ m v/(|q| B) and cyclotron frequency ω = |q| B/(γ m). The frequency decreases with energy, a key principle in synchrotron design. For crossed electric and magnetic fields (E ⟂ B) the drift velocity v_d = E×B/B² remains unchanged by relativity because it is independent of mass and charge, but the particle’s trajectory in the drift frame is a relativistic cycloid with a modified curvature. These results are standard in accelerator physics and are occasionally tested in advanced physics examinations that include a relativity module.

  • Assuming the normal impulse alone determines the post‑collision velocities when friction is present; the tangential impulse can be comparable and must be checked against the Coulomb limit.
  • Using the Newtonian range formula for projectiles with speeds above ~0.1 c without applying the relativistic correction; the error grows as v⁴/c⁴.
  • Neglecting the costate boundary conditions in Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle; a free final time introduces the transversality condition H(t_f) = 0 which often determines the optimal switching structure.
  • Treating the primer vector as constant in a gravity‑turn problem; it actually rotates with the velocity vector only when the thrust is aligned with velocity, otherwise its direction changes according to the adjoint equation.
  • Confusing the stability criterion for circular orbits in a central potential with the condition for closed orbits; stability requires a minimum of the effective potential, while closed orbits require the ratio of radial to angular frequency to be rational.
  • Applying the one‑dimensional coefficient of restitution directly to the vector velocities in an oblique impact; the restitution applies only to the normal component of relative velocity at the contact point.
  • Forgetting that the Hamiltonian in Pontryagin’s principle is maximised, not minimised, even for a minimisation problem; the sign convention λ₀ = –1 flips the optimisation direction.
