Vectors in a Plane

1.1 Vectors and Their Representation

A vector is a physical quantity that possesses both magnitude and direction, distinguishing it from a scalar which has magnitude only. In a plane a vector can be represented graphically by an arrow whose length is proportional to the magnitude and whose orientation indicates the direction; analytically it is expressed by its components along two perpendicular axes, usually the x‑axis and the y‑axis. The component form v = (vₓ, vᵧ) allows algebraic manipulation such as addition, subtraction and multiplication by a scalar, and it is the basis for all subsequent kinematic analysis in two dimensions. The magnitude of a vector is obtained from the Pythagorean theorem as |v| = √(vₓ² + vᵧ²) and its direction is given by the angle θ = tan⁻¹(vᵧ / vₓ) measured from the positive x‑axis.

Unit vectors î and ĵ of magnitude one along the x and y directions provide a compact notation: v = vₓ î + vᵧ ĵ. This notation makes it straightforward to write the equations of motion for a particle moving in a plane because the time derivatives of the components give the velocity and acceleration components directly. The concept of a position vector r = x î + y ĵ is introduced early, and its derivative with respect to time yields the velocity vector v = dr/dt = (dx/dt) î + (dy/dt) ĵ, while a second derivative gives the acceleration vector a = dv/dt.

1.2 Vector Addition and Subtraction

When two vectors a and b act simultaneously, their resultant is found by vector addition. The triangle law states that if the tail of b is placed at the head of a, the resultant is the vector from the tail of a to the head of b; the parallelogram law gives the same resultant as the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by a and b as adjacent sides. Both graphical methods are equivalent and lead to the component rule a + b = (aₓ + bₓ) î + (aᵧ + bᵧ) ĵ, which is the most convenient for calculations. Subtraction is defined as a – b = a + (–b), where –b has the same magnitude as b but opposite direction; graphically this corresponds to reversing the arrow of b before applying the triangle law.

The commutative property a + b = b + a and the associative property (a + b) + c = a + (b + c) hold for vector addition, allowing any number of vectors to be combined in any order. These algebraic properties are essential when dealing with multiple forces or velocities acting on a body, because they guarantee that the net effect is independent of the sequence in which the individual contributions are summed.

1.3 Resolution of Vectors into Components

Any vector in a plane can be resolved into two perpendicular components, usually along the coordinate axes. The process uses the trigonometric relations vₓ = |v| cos θ and vᵧ = |v| sin θ, where θ is the angle the vector makes with the positive x‑axis. Resolution is the inverse of vector addition: given the components, the original vector is reconstructed by the Pythagorean theorem and the inverse tangent function. This technique is indispensable for analysing motion on an inclined plane, projectile motion, and any situation where forces or velocities are not aligned with the axes.

When a vector is resolved, the components can be treated independently in the equations of motion because the x and y directions are orthogonal. For example, in projectile motion the horizontal component of velocity remains constant (neglecting air resistance) while the vertical component changes under the influence of gravity. The ability to separate a two‑dimensional problem into two one‑dimensional problems is a direct consequence of the linear nature of vector components.

Relative Velocity

2.1 Concept of Relative Velocity

Relative velocity describes how the velocity of one object appears from the reference frame of another moving object. If object A moves with velocity v_A and object B moves with velocity v_B in a common inertial frame, the velocity of A relative to B is v_AB = v_A – v_B. This definition follows directly from vector subtraction and reflects the fact that an observer moving with B must subtract B’s own motion to perceive A’s motion. The relative velocity is itself a vector, possessing magnitude and direction, and it obeys the same addition rules as any other vector.

The concept is symmetric: v_BA = –v_AB, meaning that the velocity of B relative to A is the negative of the velocity of A relative to B. This symmetry is a direct consequence of the vector nature of velocity and is useful for checking the consistency of calculations. In practical problems the relative velocity is often the quantity of interest, for instance when determining the apparent speed of a train from a moving car or the drift of a boat crossing a flowing river.

2.2 Relative Velocity in One and Two Dimensions

In one dimension the relative velocity reduces to a simple algebraic difference of speeds with appropriate signs for direction. In two dimensions the vector subtraction must be performed component‑wise: v_ABₓ = v_Aₓ – v_Bₓ and v_ABᵧ = v_Aᵧ – v_Bᵧ. The magnitude of the relative velocity is then √(v_ABₓ² + v_ABᵧ²) and its direction is given by tan⁻¹(v_ABᵧ / v_ABₓ). This component approach makes it straightforward to handle cases where the two objects move at arbitrary angles to each other.

When both objects have constant velocities, the relative velocity is also constant, and the relative motion is a straight line at constant speed. If either object accelerates, the relative velocity becomes time‑dependent and the relative trajectory can be curved. The analysis of such situations follows the same principles: differentiate the relative position vector r_AB = r_A – r_B to obtain v_AB, and differentiate again for the relative acceleration a_AB = a_A – a_B.

2.3 Applications to River Crossing and Aircraft Navigation

A classic application is a boat attempting to cross a river that flows with velocity v_r. The boat’s engine provides a velocity v_b relative to the water. The resultant velocity of the boat relative to the ground is v_g = v_b + v_r. To reach a point directly opposite the starting point, the boat must aim upstream at an angle such that the upstream component of v_b cancels the river’s downstream flow. The required heading angle θ satisfies v_b sin θ = v_r, and the crossing time is the river width divided by the component of v_b perpendicular to the banks, v_b cos θ.

In aircraft navigation the wind velocity v_w plays the role of the river current. The pilot sets an airspeed v_a and a heading; the ground track is the vector sum v_g = v_a + v_w. Navigators use the wind triangle, a graphical representation of this vector addition, to determine the heading needed to achieve a desired ground track and to compute the ground speed. These problems illustrate how relative velocity concepts translate directly into practical navigation techniques used daily in aviation and maritime operations.

Example: River Crossing
A river 200 m wide flows eastward at 3 m s⁻¹. A boat can move at 5 m s⁻¹ relative to the water. The captain wants to land at a point directly north of the starting point. Find the heading angle relative to north and the time taken to cross.
Let the river flow be along +x (east) and the desired crossing direction be +y (north). The river velocity v_r = 3 î m s⁻¹. The boat’s velocity relative to water has magnitude 5 m s⁻¹ and makes an angle θ west of north, so its components are v_b = –5 sin θ î + 5 cos θ ĵ. For zero net x‑displacement, the x‑component of the ground velocity must vanish: –5 sin θ + 3 = 0 → sin θ = 3/5 → θ = 36.9°. The y‑component of ground velocity is 5 cos θ = 5 × 0.8 = 4 m s⁻¹. Crossing time = width / v_y = 200 m / 4 m s⁻¹ = 50 s. The boat must head 36.9° west of north and will take 50 seconds to cross.
Exam‑Day Insight
When solving relative‑velocity problems, always draw a vector diagram first; assign a consistent coordinate system and write the vector equation v_ground = v_object + v_medium. The sign of each component follows directly from the diagram, preventing the common error of adding magnitudes instead of vectors.

Projectile Motion

3.1 Kinematic Equations for a Projectile

A projectile is any object that is launched into the air and moves under the influence of gravity alone, neglecting air resistance. The motion can be analysed by separating the initial velocity u into horizontal and vertical components: uₓ = u cos α and uᵧ = u sin α, where α is the launch angle measured from the horizontal. Because there is no horizontal force (ignoring drag), the horizontal acceleration is zero and the horizontal velocity remains constant at uₓ throughout the flight. The vertical motion is uniformly accelerated with acceleration aᵧ = –g, where g = 9.8 m s⁻² directed downward.

The standard kinematic equations apply independently to each component. For the horizontal direction: x = uₓ t. For the vertical direction: y = uᵧ t – ½ g t², vᵧ = uᵧ – g t, and vᵧ² = uᵧ² – 2 g y. These equations are derived from the definitions of velocity and acceleration and are valid as long as the acceleration remains constant, which is true for the vertical component under the uniform gravity approximation.

3.2 Time of Flight, Maximum Height and Range

The time of flight T is the total time the projectile remains airborne before returning to the same vertical level from which it was launched. Setting y = 0 in the vertical displacement equation gives 0 = uᵧ T – ½ g T², yielding T = 2 uᵧ / g = 2 u sin α / g. The maximum height H is reached when the vertical velocity becomes zero; using vᵧ = 0 gives H = uᵧ² / (2 g) = u² sin² α / (2 g). The horizontal range R is the horizontal distance covered during the time of flight: R = uₓ T = u cos α × (2 u sin α / g) = u² sin 2α / g. These three formulae are the cornerstone of projectile‑motion problems and are frequently tested in examinations.

It is important to note that the range formula assumes the launch and landing heights are equal. If the projectile lands at a different height, the quadratic equation for y must be solved for the appropriate time, and the range becomes R = uₓ t_land where t_land is the positive root of the vertical displacement equation. The symmetry of the trajectory about the apex is a direct consequence of the constant horizontal velocity and the symmetric nature of the vertical motion under uniform acceleration.

3.3 Effect of Launch Angle on Range

For a given initial speed u, the range R = u² sin 2α / g depends on the launch angle through the factor sin 2α. The sine function attains its maximum value of 1 when 2α = 90°, i.e. α = 45°. Therefore the maximum possible range on level ground is R_max = u² / g, achieved at a 45° launch angle. Complementary angles α and (90° – α) give the same range because sin 2α = sin (180° – 2α). This symmetry explains why a projectile launched at 30° and another at 60° with the same speed land at the same horizontal distance, although their maximum heights and times of flight differ.

3.4 Projectile Motion with Air Resistance (Qualitative)

When air resistance is considered, the horizontal velocity is no longer constant because a drag force opposite to the velocity acts on the projectile. The drag force is often modelled as proportional to the square of the speed, F_d = –k v², where k depends on the shape, size, and density of air, and the drag coefficient. This introduces a non‑linear differential equation that generally requires numerical solution. Qualitatively, air resistance reduces the range, lowers the maximum height, and makes the trajectory asymmetric: the descent is steeper than the ascent because the vertical component of drag adds to gravity during the fall and opposes gravity during the rise. The optimal launch angle for maximum range shifts to a value less than 45°, typically around 30°–35° for typical projectiles.

Uniform Circular Motion

4.1 Angular Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

Uniform circular motion occurs when a particle moves along a circular path of radius r with constant speed v. The angular displacement θ (in radians) is related to the arc length s by s = r θ. The angular velocity ω is defined as the rate of change of angular displacement: ω = dθ/dt. For uniform motion ω is constant and related to the linear speed by v = ω r. The period T, the time for one complete revolution, is T = 2π / ω, and the frequency f = 1/T = ω / 2π.

Even though the speed is constant, the velocity vector changes direction continuously, so there is an acceleration. The acceleration vector always points toward the centre of the circle and is called centripetal acceleration. Its magnitude is a_c = v² / r = ω² r. This result follows from differentiating the velocity vector v = ω r (–sin ωt î + cos ωt ĵ) with respect to time, yielding a = –ω² r (cos ωt î + sin ωt ĵ), which has magnitude ω² r and direction radially inward.

4.2 Centripetal Force and Its Sources

Newton’s second law requires a net force equal to mass times acceleration. For uniform circular motion the required net force is the centripetal force F_c = m a_c = m v² / r = m ω² r, directed toward the centre. This force is not a new type of force; it is the resultant of real forces such as tension, gravity, friction or normal reaction, depending on the physical situation. For a satellite in orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force; for a car on a banked curve, the horizontal component of the normal reaction (and possibly friction) provides it; for a stone whirled on a string, the tension in the string is the centripetal force.

4.3 Relationship Between Linear and Angular Quantities

The linear quantities (displacement s, velocity v, acceleration a) and angular quantities (θ, ω, α) are linked by the radius r. The fundamental relations are s = r θ, v = r ω, and a_t = r α for tangential acceleration. In uniform circular motion the angular acceleration α is zero, so the tangential acceleration vanishes and the total acceleration is purely centripetal. These relationships allow one to switch between linear and angular descriptions seamlessly, which is particularly useful when dealing with rotating rigid bodies where different points have different linear speeds but share the same angular velocity.

Applications and Problem‑Solving Strategies

5.1 Combining Vector Methods with Kinematics

Many two‑dimensional problems require the simultaneous use of vector addition, resolution, and the kinematic equations. A systematic approach begins by drawing a clear diagram showing all known vectors, choosing a convenient coordinate system, and writing the vector equations for position, velocity and acceleration. The components are then treated as independent one‑dimensional motions, each governed by the appropriate constant‑acceleration formulae. After solving for the unknown components, the results are recombined to obtain the magnitude and direction of the required vector quantity. This method reduces the chance of algebraic errors and makes the physical reasoning transparent.

5.2 Systematic Approach to Multi‑Stage Problems

Problems that involve several phases — for example a projectile launched from a moving platform, or a particle that moves in a circle and then is released — are best tackled by dividing the motion into stages. For each stage, identify the forces acting, determine the acceleration vector, and write the kinematic equations with appropriate initial conditions taken from the final state of the preceding stage. Continuity of position and velocity at the stage boundaries provides the linking conditions. Keeping a table of known and unknown variables for each stage helps to organise the information and to verify that the number of independent equations matches the number of unknowns.

5.3 Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

A frequent mistake is to treat the magnitude of a vector sum as the sum of magnitudes; always perform vector addition component‑wise or graphically. Another error is to use the range formula R = u² sin 2α / g when the launch and landing heights differ; in such cases the full quadratic for vertical displacement must be solved. In circular motion, students sometimes confuse centripetal acceleration with tangential acceleration; remember that centripetal acceleration exists whenever the direction of velocity changes, even if the speed is constant, whereas tangential acceleration appears only when the speed changes. Careful reading of the problem statement, consistent sign conventions, and a final check of units and physical plausibility are essential habits for accurate solutions.

Mastering the interplay of vectors, relative motion, projectile trajectories and circular dynamics equips a student to analyse a wide variety of physical situations encountered in both academic examinations and real‑world engineering challenges.

Non‑Uniform Circular Motion and Tangential Acceleration

When the speed of a particle moving on a circular path changes, the motion is no longer uniform. The acceleration vector then has two orthogonal components: a radial (centripetal) component ar = v² / r = ω² r directed toward the centre, and a tangential component at = r α directed along the instantaneous velocity, where α = dω/dt is the angular acceleration. The total acceleration is the vector sum a = ar + at, and its magnitude is √(ar² + at²). The direction of the total acceleration makes an angle φ = tan⁻¹(at/ar) with the radial line. This decomposition is essential for problems where a torque or a tangential force acts, such as a car accelerating on a banked curve, a particle on a string being pulled, or a rotating shaft with a varying angular speed.

The work–energy theorem in circular motion takes a simple form because the radial force does no work (it is always perpendicular to the displacement). Only the tangential force Ft = m at changes the kinetic energy: dK = Ft·ds = m at·(r dθ) = m r α·(r dθ) = m r² α dθ. Integrating gives the change in kinetic energy in terms of the angular displacement. Power delivered by the tangential force is P = Ft v = m at v = m r α·(r ω) = m r² ω α. These relations allow one to connect torque, angular acceleration, and energy without solving the full trajectory.

A common textbook situation is a particle of mass m attached to a light string that passes through a hole in a smooth horizontal table. The particle moves in a circle of radius r while the string is pulled downwards with a constant force F. Because the force is radial, angular momentum L = m r² ω is conserved, so ω ∝ 1/r². The radial equation of motion gives m (r̈ – r ω²) = –F. Substituting ω = L/(m r²) yields a differential equation for r(t) that can be solved to find the time evolution of the radius and the tension in the string. This example illustrates how conservation of angular momentum couples with the radial dynamics when the motion is non‑uniform.

In problems involving a rotating rigid body, different points share the same angular velocity ω and angular acceleration α, but their linear speeds and tangential accelerations differ proportionally to their distances from the axis. The total acceleration of a point at radius r is therefore a = r α θ̂ – r ω² r̂. When analysing a rolling wheel that is accelerating, the instantaneous centre of rotation is the contact point with the ground, and the acceleration of the centre of mass is acm = R α horizontally, while the top point has acceleration 2 R α horizontally. Recognising these relationships prevents the frequent error of assigning the same linear acceleration to all points of a rotating body.

Projectile Motion on an Inclined Plane

When a projectile is launched from a point on a plane inclined at an angle α to the horizontal, it is convenient to choose axes along the plane (x‑axis) and perpendicular to the plane (y‑axis). The initial velocity components become ux = u cos(θ – α) and uy = u sin(θ – α), where θ is the launch angle measured from the horizontal. The acceleration components are ax = –g sin α (down the plane) and ay = –g cos α (into the plane). The projectile leaves the plane when y = 0 again, giving the time of flight T = 2 u sin(θ – α) / (g cos α). The range measured along the plane is then R = ux T + ½ ax T², which simplifies to the well‑known formula R = (u² / (g cos² α)) [ sin(2θ – α) – sin α ].

The maximum height reached above the inclined plane occurs when the perpendicular velocity component becomes zero: tmax = u sin(θ – α) / (g cos α). Substituting into the y‑displacement gives Hmax = (u² sin²(θ – α)) / (2 g cos² α). For a given launch speed u and plane inclination α, the range is maximised when 2θ – α = 90°, i.e. θ = 45° + α/2. This result reduces to the familiar θ = 45° for a horizontal plane (α = 0). The maximum range on the inclined plane is Rmax = u² / (g (1 + sin α)). If the projectile is launched down the plane (θ < α), the same formulas apply with appropriate sign conventions; the time of flight is still given by the positive root of the quadratic in t obtained from y = 0.

An important extension is the case where the launch point and the landing point are at different heights on the inclined plane. Let the vertical separation along the plane be Δy (positive if the landing point is higher). The vertical motion equation becomes Δy = u sin(θ – α) t – ½ g cos α t². Solving this quadratic for t yields two possible times; the larger positive root is the time of flight. The range along the plane is then R = u cos(θ – α) t – ½ g sin α t². This formulation is essential for problems involving a projectile fired from a cliff onto a slope, or a ball launched from a ramp onto another ramp.

When air resistance is considered qualitatively, the trajectory is no longer a parabola in the plane coordinates. The drag force opposes the instantaneous velocity, reducing both the range and the maximum height. The optimal launch angle for maximum range shifts to a value smaller than 45° + α/2 because the projectile spends more time in the air at higher angles, suffering greater cumulative drag. In competitive examinations, the qualitative effect is often tested by asking how the range changes if the launch angle is increased beyond the vacuum optimum, or by comparing the range on an inclined plane with and without air resistance.

Worked Example: Range on an Inclined Plane
A projectile is launched with speed 20 m/s at an angle of 60° to the horizontal from the foot of a plane inclined at 30° to the horizontal. Find the range along the plane and the maximum height above the plane. Take g = 10 m/s².
Choose axes along the plane (x) and perpendicular (y). The inclination α = 30°. Launch angle relative to horizontal θ = 60°, so θ – α = 30°. Initial components: uₓ = 20 cos 30° = 20 × √3/2 = 10√3 m/s; uᵧ = 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s. Acceleration components: aₓ = –g sin α = –10 × ½ = –5 m/s²; aᵧ = –g cos α = –10 × √3/2 = –5√3 m/s². Time of flight from y = 0: T = 2 uᵧ / (g cos α) = 2×10 / (10×√3/2) = 20 / (5√3) = 4/√3 s ≈ 2.31 s. Range along plane: R = uₓ T + ½ aₓ T² = (10√3)(4/√3) + ½(–5)(16/3) = 40 – 40/3 = 80/3 m ≈ 26.7 m. Maximum height above plane: H = uᵧ² / (2 g cos α) = 100 / (2×10×√3/2) = 100 / (10√3) = 10/√3 m ≈ 5.77 m.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a projectile problem involves an inclined plane, immediately rotate the axes to align with the plane. Write down the components of g along and perpendicular to the plane; this avoids sign errors. Memorise the compact range formula R = (u²/(g cos²α))[sin(2θ–α)–sinα] and the condition for maximum range θ = 45°+α/2. If the launch and landing heights differ, set up the quadratic in time from the perpendicular displacement and solve for the positive root. Always check the limiting case α = 0 to verify that your expressions reduce to the standard horizontal‑plane formulas.

Relative Motion in Rotating Reference Frames

Analyzing motion from a frame that rotates with constant angular velocity ω introduces two fictitious accelerations: the centrifugal acceleration acf = ω × (ω × r) directed radially outward, and the Coriolis acceleration acor = 2 ω × vrot perpendicular to the velocity in the rotating frame. The complete transformation of acceleration from an inertial frame to a rotating frame is ainertial = arot + 2 ω × vrot + ω × (ω × r) + dω/dt × r. The last term vanishes for constant ω. These terms are not real forces; they appear because the basis vectors of the rotating frame change with time. In problems where a particle moves on a rotating turntable, or where the motion of a projectile is observed from the rotating Earth, the fictitious accelerations must be included to apply Newton’s second law in the rotating frame.

Consider a particle sliding without friction in a straight radial groove on a horizontal turntable rotating with constant angular speed ω. In the inertial frame the particle moves in a straight line (no horizontal forces), but in the rotating frame it experiences a centrifugal force m ω² r outward and a Coriolis force 2 m ω vrot perpendicular to the groove. The equation of motion along the groove is m r̈ = m ω² r, giving r = r₀ cosh(ω t) + (v₀/ω) sinh(ω t). The Coriolis force does not affect the radial motion because it is perpendicular to the groove, but it exerts a normal reaction on the groove walls. This example shows how the fictitious forces simplify the description of constrained motion in a rotating frame.

On the Earth, the angular velocity vector ωE points along the polar axis. For a projectile fired horizontally at latitude λ, the Coriolis acceleration has a vertical component 2 ωE v cos λ (affecting the apparent weight) and a horizontal component 2 ωE v sin λ deflecting the trajectory to the right in the Northern Hemisphere. The centrifugal acceleration slightly reduces the effective gravity and makes the plumb line deviate from the true radial direction. In most introductory problems these effects are neglected, but they become significant for long‑range artillery, satellite launches, and the Foucault pendulum, where the plane of oscillation rotates at a rate ωE sin λ.

A frequent examination question asks for the trajectory of a particle projected radially outward on a rotating platform with initial speed u relative to the platform. In the rotating frame the radial equation is r̈ – ω² r = 0 (since the Coriolis force is azimuthal). The solution is r = (u/ω) sinh(ω t) for zero initial radius. The azimuthal motion is governed by the Coriolis force: m (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇) = 0, which integrates to r² θ̇ = constant = 0 if the particle starts from rest in the inertial frame. Thus the particle moves radially outward in the rotating frame while lagging behind in the inertial frame. Recognising which terms belong to which frame is the key to avoiding sign errors.

Vector Calculus Approach to Planar Motion

Describing planar motion with vectors in a fixed Cartesian basis is straightforward, but polar coordinates often reveal the physics more transparently. Let the position vector be r = r r̂, where r̂ and θ̂ are the radial and transverse unit vectors. Their time derivatives are dr̂/dt = θ̇ θ̂ and dθ̂/dt = –θ̇ r̂. The velocity is v = dr/dt = ṙ r̂ + r θ̇ θ̂. The acceleration follows by differentiating again: a = (r̈ – r θ̇²) r̂ + (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇) θ̂. The radial component contains the centripetal term –r θ̇² and the transverse component contains the Coriolis‑like term 2 ṙ θ̇. For uniform circular motion ṙ = 0, θ̈ = 0, so a = –r ω² r̂, recovering the familiar centripetal acceleration.

These expressions are powerful for problems where the radius changes with time, such as a bead sliding on a rotating wire, a particle moving under a central force, or a spiral trajectory. For a central force F = –k r̂, the transverse equation gives d/dt (r² θ̇) = 0, i.e. conservation of angular momentum L = m r² θ̇. Substituting θ̇ = L/(m r²) into the radial equation yields a one‑dimensional effective‑potential problem: m r̈ = –k + L²/(m r³). This reduction is the standard method for analysing planetary orbits and Rutherford scattering.

When the motion is described in a rotating frame with angular velocity Ω, the unit vectors rotate with the frame, and the time derivative of any vector A is (dA/dt)inertial = (dA/dt)rot + Ω × A. Applying this to the position vector gives the velocity transformation vinertial = vrot + Ω × r, and a second application yields the acceleration transformation quoted earlier. The vector‑calculus derivation makes the origin of the Coriolis and centrifugal terms completely transparent: they arise from the Ω × operator acting on the velocity and position vectors respectively. This formalism is indispensable for advanced problems in rigid‑body dynamics and for understanding the motion of particles in magnetic fields, where the Lorentz force q v × B plays a role analogous to the Coriolis force.

An illustrative example is a particle moving in an Archimedean spiral r = a θ with constant angular speed θ̇ = ω. Then ṙ = a ω, r̈ = 0. The velocity is v = a ω r̂ + a ω θ θ̂, and the acceleration is a = (–a ω² θ) r̂ + (2 a ω²) θ̂. The radial acceleration is not simply centripetal; it contains a term proportional to θ because the radius grows linearly with angle. The transverse acceleration is constant, reflecting the constant Coriolis‑type term 2 ṙ θ̇. Such problems test the ability to differentiate vector expressions in polar coordinates and to interpret each term physically.

Applications of Relative Velocity in Collision Avoidance

The concept of relative velocity is the cornerstone of collision‑avoidance analysis for two moving objects. Let the position vectors of objects A and B be rA and rB, and their velocities vA and vB. The relative position is r = rB – rA and the relative velocity is v = vB – vA. In the frame of A, B moves with constant velocity v (assuming no acceleration). The distance between them at time t is |r + v t|. The time of closest approach is found by minimising this distance: differentiate |r + v t|² with respect to t and set to zero, yielding tca = – (r·v) / |v|². If tca > 0, the closest approach occurs in the future; if tca ≤ 0, the objects are already separating.

The minimum separation distance is dmin = |r × v| / |v|. A collision occurs if and only if dmin = 0, which is equivalent to the condition that r is parallel to v (i.e. r × v = 0) and tca > 0. In practical terms, two ships on constant courses will collide if the bearing of one from the other remains constant (constant bearing, decreasing range). This is the classic “constant bearing” rule used in maritime navigation. The same mathematics applies to aircraft, automobiles at intersections, and particles in a gas.

When the objects have finite sizes, the collision condition becomes dmin ≤ RA + RB, where R are the effective radii. If the objects can manoeuvre, the problem becomes one of choosing a velocity change Δv for one object such that the new relative velocity v' = v + Δv yields a safe dmin. The minimum‑Δv avoidance manoeuvre is often perpendicular to the original relative velocity vector, because that maximises the increase in dmin per unit Δv. This principle underlies the “collision avoidance manoeuvre” algorithms used in air traffic control and autonomous vehicle software.

A typical examination problem: two particles are projected simultaneously from points separated by a horizontal distance D. Particle 1 is launched at speed u₁ and angle θ₁; particle 2 at speed u₂ and angle θ₂. Determine whether they collide in mid‑air. The relative motion is a projectile with initial relative velocity v = u₂ (cos θ₂ î + sin θ₂ ĵ) – u₁ (cos θ₁ î + sin θ₁ ĵ) and relative acceleration a = 0 (since both experience the same gravity). The relative position at time t is r(t) = r₀ + v t, where r₀ is the initial separation vector. A collision requires r(t) = 0 for some t > 0, i.e. r₀ must be anti‑parallel to v and the time t = –|r₀|/|v| must be positive and less than the time of flight of both projectiles. This vector approach reduces a seemingly two‑dimensional problem to a simple algebraic condition.

  • Treating the magnitude of a vector sum as the sum of magnitudes when combining velocities or accelerations.
  • Using the horizontal‑plane range formula for a projectile launched on an inclined plane without adjusting for the plane’s inclination.
  • Forgetting the Coriolis term 2 ω × vrot when writing equations of motion in a rotating frame.
  • Confusing the radial acceleration r̈ – r θ̇² with the centripetal acceleration –r θ̇² in polar coordinates.
  • Assuming the time of closest approach is always positive; it can be negative if the objects are already moving apart.
  • Neglecting the condition tca > 0 when checking for a collision between two projectiles.
  • Applying the constant‑bearing rule without verifying that both objects maintain constant velocities.
