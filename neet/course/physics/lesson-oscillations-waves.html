Simple Harmonic Motion Fundamentals

1.1 Restoring Force and Hooke’s Law

In any system that executes simple harmonic motion the displacement from equilibrium produces a restoring force that is directly proportional to the displacement and directed toward the equilibrium position. This linear relationship is expressed by Hooke’s law, F = –k x, where k is the force constant of the spring measured in newton per metre and x is the instantaneous displacement. The negative sign indicates that the force always opposes the displacement, which is the essential cause of the oscillatory behaviour. Because the force is linear, the resulting acceleration a = F/m = –(k/m) x is also proportional to the displacement, leading to a second‑order differential equation whose solution is a sinusoidal function of time.

The proportionality constant k/m determines the natural angular frequency ω of the oscillator through ω = √(k/m). The period T, the time for one complete oscillation, follows as T = 2π/ω = 2π√(m/k). Frequency f, the reciprocal of the period, is f = 1/T = ω/(2π). These three quantities — angular frequency, period, and frequency — are the primary descriptors that appear in every examination question on simple harmonic motion, and they are all expressed in SI units: rad s⁻¹ for ω, seconds for T, and hertz for f.

1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

The total mechanical energy of an ideal simple harmonic oscillator remains constant because the restoring force is conservative. At any instant the energy is shared between kinetic energy K = ½ m v² and elastic potential energy U = ½ k x². When the mass passes through the equilibrium position the displacement is zero, so the potential energy vanishes and the kinetic energy reaches its maximum value ½ m v_max² = ½ k A², where A is the amplitude. Conversely, at the turning points the velocity is zero and the entire energy resides as potential energy ½ k A². The constancy of the sum K + U = ½ k A² provides a powerful check on calculations and is frequently used to find the amplitude when the maximum speed is known.

Because the energy exchange is periodic, the average kinetic energy over a cycle equals the average potential energy, each being one quarter of the total energy. This equipartition result is a direct consequence of the sinusoidal nature of the motion and is often tested in conceptual questions that ask for the fraction of total energy that is kinetic at a given displacement.

1.3 Phase, Phase Constant and the General Solution

The complete description of a simple harmonic oscillator requires not only its amplitude and frequency but also its phase at t = 0. The general solution of the equation of motion can be written as x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) or equivalently x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ′), where φ (or φ′) is the phase constant determined by the initial displacement and velocity. If the oscillator is released from rest at x = A, the phase constant is zero and the motion follows a pure cosine. If it is given an initial velocity from the equilibrium position, the phase constant becomes –π/2 and the motion follows a sine function. The phase constant therefore encodes the initial conditions and is essential when superposing several oscillations, because the relative phase determines whether the superposition is constructive or destructive.

In problems that involve two oscillators of the same frequency, the phase difference Δφ = φ₂ – φ₁ decides the resultant amplitude through the relation A_res² = A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cos Δφ. This formula is a direct application of the phasor addition method and appears regularly in questions on interference of waves that share a common frequency.

Pendulum Motion and Energy

2.1 Simple Pendulum Approximation

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass suspended by a light, inextensible string of length L. For small angular displacements θ (typically θ ≤ 10°) the restoring torque is τ = –mgL sin θ ≈ –mgL θ, which is linear in the angular displacement. The equation of motion becomes I α = –mgL θ, where the moment of inertia I = mL² for a point mass. This yields an angular frequency ω = √(g/L) and a period T = 2π√(L/g) that is independent of the mass and the amplitude, provided the small‑angle approximation holds. The independence of mass is a classic demonstration of the equivalence principle and is often asked as a conceptual check.

The small‑angle approximation introduces a systematic error that grows with amplitude. For larger amplitudes the exact period involves an elliptic integral and can be expressed as a series T = 2π√(L/g) [1 + (1/16)θ₀² + (11/3072)θ₀⁴ + …], where θ₀ is the maximum angular displacement in radians. Examination questions sometimes ask for the percentage increase in period when the amplitude is increased from 5° to 20°, requiring the student to apply the first‑order correction term.

2.2 Physical Pendulum and Moment of Inertia

A physical pendulum is any rigid body swinging about a horizontal axis that does not pass through its centre of mass. The restoring torque is τ = –mgh sin θ, where h is the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass. For small angles the equation of motion is I α = –mgh θ, giving ω = √(mgh/I) and T = 2π√(I/(mgh)). The moment of inertia I must be evaluated about the pivot axis, often using the parallel‑axis theorem I = I_cm + mh². This formula reduces to the simple pendulum result when the body is a point mass at distance L = h, because then I = mL² and T = 2π√(L/g).

Typical problems provide a uniform rod of length L pivoted at one end, for which I = (1/3)mL² and h = L/2, yielding T = 2π√(2L/(3g)). Another common configuration is a circular disc of radius R pivoted at a point on its rim, where I = (3/2)mR² and h = R, giving T = 2π√(3R/(2g)). These specific results are frequently memorised because they appear in multiple‑choice questions that test the ability to apply the parallel‑axis theorem quickly.

2.3 Energy Considerations in Pendular Motion

Just as for the spring‑mass system, the total mechanical energy of a pendulum is conserved in the absence of damping. The potential energy relative to the lowest point is U = mgh(1 – cos θ) ≈ ½ mgh θ² for small angles, and the kinetic energy is K = ½ I ω². At the maximum angular displacement θ₀ the kinetic energy is zero and the total energy equals the potential energy mgh(1 – cos θ₀). At the bottom of the swing the potential energy is zero and the kinetic energy reaches its maximum ½ I ω_max² = mgh(1 – cos θ₀). This energy balance allows one to find the maximum angular speed ω_max = √[2gh(1 – cos θ₀)/I] without solving the differential equation.

When a pendulum is used as a timing device, the constancy of the period for small amplitudes is a direct consequence of the linear restoring torque. Any non‑linearity, such as large amplitude or a non‑uniform gravitational field, introduces amplitude dependence and thus limits the accuracy of a pendulum clock. This historical context explains why the development of the balance spring in watches was a crucial advance for portable timekeeping.

Worked Example: Period of a Physical Pendulum
A uniform rod of length 1.20 m and mass 0.80 kg is pivoted at one end and allowed to oscillate in a vertical plane. Calculate the period of small oscillations. Take g = 9.80 m s⁻².
The moment of inertia of a uniform rod about one end is I = (1/3) m L² = (1/3) × 0.80 kg × (1.20 m)² = 0.384 kg m². The distance from the pivot to the centre of mass is h = L/2 = 0.60 m. The angular frequency for a physical pendulum is ω = √(m g h / I) = √(0.80 kg × 9.80 m s⁻² × 0.60 m / 0.384 kg m²) = √(12.288 / 0.384) = √32.0 = 5.66 rad s⁻¹. The period is T = 2π/ω = 2π / 5.66 s⁻¹ = 1.11 s. Therefore the rod completes one small oscillation in approximately 1.11 seconds.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the period of a physical pendulum, always identify the pivot‑to‑centre‑of‑mass distance h and the moment of inertia about the pivot before substituting into T = 2π√(I/(mgh)). A common mistake is to use the moment of inertia about the centre of mass without applying the parallel‑axis theorem.

Wave Equation and Propagation

3.1 Derivation of the One‑Dimensional Wave Equation

Consider a taut string of linear mass density μ under tension T. A small segment of length Δx experiences a net transverse force due to the difference in the vertical components of tension at its ends. For small slopes the vertical component is T ∂y/∂x, so the net force is T(∂y/∂x|x+Δx – ∂y/∂x|_x) ≈ T ∂²y/∂x² Δx. Applying Newton’s second law, μ Δx ∂²y/∂t² = T ∂²y/∂x² Δx, which simplifies to ∂²y/∂t² = (T/μ) ∂²y/∂x². The constant v = √(T/μ) has dimensions of speed and is identified as the wave speed. The resulting partial differential equation ∂²y/∂t² = v² ∂²y/∂x² is the one‑dimensional wave equation, valid for any linear, non‑dispersive medium where the restoring force is proportional to the curvature.

The same equation governs longitudinal waves in a rod, where the tension is replaced by the Young’s modulus Y and the linear density by the volume density ρ, giving v = √(Y/ρ). For sound waves in a fluid the bulk modulus B replaces Y, yielding v = √(B/ρ). Recognising the analogous forms allows a student to transfer the mathematical techniques learned for a string to acoustic and seismic problems.

3.2 General Solution and Travelling Waves

The general solution of the one‑dimensional wave equation can be written as a superposition of a right‑travelling wave f(x – vt) and a left‑travelling wave g(x + vt). Any twice‑differentiable function of the argument (x ∓ vt) satisfies the equation, which means the shape of the disturbance is preserved as it propagates. A particularly important case is the sinusoidal travelling wave y(x,t) = A sin(kx – ωt + φ), where the wave number k = 2π/λ, the angular frequency ω = 2πf, and the phase velocity v = ω/k = λf. The parameters A, λ, f, and φ completely specify a monochromatic wave and are the quantities most often asked for in numerical problems.

Because the wave equation is linear, the principle of superposition holds: the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the displacements produced by each wave individually. This linearity is the mathematical foundation for interference, beats, and the formation of standing waves, all of which are explored in the next section.

3.3 Standing Waves and Boundary Conditions

When two identical sinusoidal waves travel in opposite directions along a string fixed at both ends, their superposition produces a standing wave described by y(x,t) = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). The spatial factor sin(kx) creates nodes at positions where sin(kx) = 0, i.e. x = nλ/2 (n = 0,1,2,…), and antinodes where sin(kx) = ±1. The fixed ends impose the boundary condition y(0,t) = y(L,t) = 0, which forces the allowed wavelengths to satisfy L = nλ/2. Consequently the permitted frequencies are f_n = n v/(2L) = n f₁, where f₁ = v/(2L) is the fundamental frequency. These discrete frequencies are called the normal modes of the string.

The same analysis applies to air columns in pipes. For a pipe closed at one end and open at the other, the closed end is a displacement node (pressure antinode) and the open end is a displacement antinode (pressure node). The allowed wavelengths are λ_n = 4L/(2n–1) and the frequencies are odd multiples of the fundamental: f_n = (2n–1)v/(4L). For a pipe open at both ends the condition is identical to the string, giving all integer multiples. These formulas are standard examination material and are often combined with end‑correction factors in more advanced problems.

Superposition, Interference and Beats

4.1 Principle of Superposition and Interference

The principle of superposition states that when two or more waves overlap in a linear medium, the resultant displacement at any point and time is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements. For two sinusoidal waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in the same direction, y₁ = A sin(kx – ωt) and y₂ = A sin(kx – ωt + Δφ), the resultant is y = 2A cos(Δφ/2) sin(kx – ωt + Δφ/2). The amplitude of the combined wave is therefore A_res = 2A cos(Δφ/2). When Δφ = 0 the waves are in phase and constructive interference yields an amplitude of 2A; when Δφ = π they are out of phase and destructive interference gives zero amplitude. This simple cosine dependence on phase difference is the basis for all interference phenomena, from thin‑film colours to the operation of interferometers.

In a typical examination problem the phase difference arises from a path difference Δx, with Δφ = (2π/λ) Δx. Constructive interference occurs when Δx = nλ and destructive interference when Δx = (n + ½)λ. The ability to convert between path difference, phase difference, and the resulting intensity (which is proportional to the square of the amplitude) is a core skill tested in both multiple‑choice and descriptive questions.

4.2 Beats: Amplitude Modulation from Nearby Frequencies

When two waves of slightly different frequencies f₁ and f₂ superpose, the resultant displacement can be expressed as y = 2A cos[π(f₁ – f₂)t] sin[π(f₁ + f₂)t]. The fast oscillation at the average frequency (f₁ + f₂)/2 is modulated by a slowly varying envelope of frequency |f₁ – f₂|. The listener perceives a periodic variation in loudness called beats, with a beat frequency f_beat = |f₁ – f₂|. The beat phenomenon is a direct consequence of the linear superposition of two nearly equal frequencies and does not require any non‑linearity in the medium.

Beats are used practically to tune musical instruments: a musician sounds a reference tone and the instrument’s tone simultaneously, then adjusts the tension or length until the beats disappear, indicating that the frequencies have been matched. In physics examinations, a common question provides two tuning forks of known frequencies and asks for the number of beats heard in a given time interval, which is simply f_beat × Δt. Another variant gives the beat frequency and one of the component frequencies, requiring the student to solve for the unknown frequency, remembering that two solutions are possible (f₂ = f₁ ± f_beat).

4.3 Standing Waves as a Special Case of Superposition

A standing wave can be viewed as the superposition of two identical travelling waves moving in opposite directions. The resultant displacement y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt) shows that the amplitude varies with position but the phase is the same for all points between adjacent nodes. Energy is not transported along the string; instead it oscillates between kinetic and potential forms within each segment. The nodes are points of zero displacement at all times, while antinodes experience maximum amplitude. The distance between consecutive nodes or antinodes is λ/2, a fact that is often used to determine the wavelength from a photograph of a standing wave pattern.

In a laboratory setting, standing waves are produced on a string driven by a vibrator at one end and fixed at the other. By adjusting the driving frequency, the string can be made to resonate at one of its normal modes. The resonance condition f = n v/(2L) is identical to the condition derived from the boundary‑value problem, illustrating the equivalence of the forced‑oscillation and free‑oscillation viewpoints. Examination questions may ask for the length of the string given the frequency of the third harmonic and the wave speed, or vice versa.

Doppler Effect and Applications

5.1 Derivation of the Doppler Shift for Sound

The Doppler effect describes the change in observed frequency when there is relative motion between a source and an observer in a material medium. For sound waves the medium (air) provides a preferred reference frame. If the source moves toward a stationary observer with speed v_s, the wavelength in front of the source is compressed to λ′ = (v – v_s)/f, where v is the speed of sound in the medium. The observer receives these wavefronts at a rate f′ = v/λ′ = f v/(v – v_s). Conversely, if the observer moves toward a stationary source with speed v_o, the relative speed of the wavefronts is v + v_o, giving f′ = (v + v_o)/λ = f (v + v_o)/v. When both source and observer move along the line joining them, the combined formula is f′ = f (v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s), where the upper signs correspond to motion toward each other and the lower signs to motion away.

It is crucial to note that the speed of sound v depends on the medium’s temperature and composition, not on the motion of the source or observer. This distinguishes acoustic Doppler shifts from the relativistic Doppler effect for light, where the speed of light is invariant and the formula involves the Lorentz factor. In examinations, the acoustic formula is applied to problems involving moving vehicles, rotating sirens, or astronomical objects where the medium can be approximated as stationary air.

5.2 Doppler Effect for Light and Relativistic Considerations

For electromagnetic waves in vacuum there is no material medium, so the classical derivation using a preferred frame fails. The relativistic Doppler shift for light observed when the source and observer recede from each other with relative speed u is f′ = f √[(1 – β)/(1 + β)], where β = u/c and c is the speed of light. For approach the sign of β is reversed. In the low‑speed limit (β ≪ 1) this reduces to the familiar approximation f′ ≈ f (1 ∓ u/c), which mirrors the acoustic formula with v replaced by c. The relativistic formula also predicts the transverse Doppler shift (observed when the motion is perpendicular to the line of sight), a purely relativistic effect with no classical analogue, given by f′ = f/γ where γ = 1/√(1 – β²).

Although the relativistic Doppler effect is more appropriate for astrophysics, many introductory physics examinations restrict the Doppler discussion to sound waves. When a question explicitly mentions light or asks for the redshift of a galaxy, the relativistic formula must be used. Recognising the domain of applicability of each formula is a key discriminator in multiple‑choice questions.

5.3 Practical Applications and Problem‑Solving Strategies

The Doppler effect finds applications in radar speed guns, medical ultrasound, and astronomy. A police radar emits microwaves at a known frequency; the reflected wave from a moving vehicle experiences a double Doppler shift (once upon reception by the vehicle, once upon re‑emission), yielding a frequency shift Δf = 2f v_r/c, where v_r is the radial component of the vehicle’s velocity. In medical Doppler ultrasound, the frequency shift of sound reflected from moving blood cells provides a measure of blood flow velocity. The standard problem‑solving strategy is to identify the source and observer velocities relative to the medium, choose the correct sign convention, and substitute into the appropriate formula. Drawing a diagram with velocity vectors and indicating the direction of wave propagation helps avoid sign errors, which are the most common source of mistakes in Doppler calculations.

Another frequent examination scenario involves a source moving in a circle around a stationary observer. The observed frequency varies sinusoidally between a maximum when the source moves directly toward the observer and a minimum when it moves directly away. The maximum and minimum frequencies are given by f_max = f v/(v – v_s) and f_min = f v/(v + v_s). The difference f_max – f_min can be used to determine the source speed v_s if the emitted frequency and the speed of sound are known. This type of problem tests the student’s ability to apply the Doppler formula to a time‑varying geometry.

The study of oscillations and waves thus connects the simple harmonic motion of a mass on a spring to the propagation of disturbances in continuous media, the interference of coherent waves, the perception of beats, and the frequency shifts caused by relative motion, forming a coherent framework that underpins much of classical and modern physics.
