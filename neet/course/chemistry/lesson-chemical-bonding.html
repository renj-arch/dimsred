Ionic Bonding and Lattice Energy

1.1 Formation of Ionic Bonds

Ionic bonding arises when atoms with a large difference in electronegativity transfer electrons so that each achieves a stable noble‑gas configuration. The metal atom, typically from Group 1 or Group 2, loses one or two electrons to become a cation, while the non‑metal atom, often from Group 16 or Group 17, gains those electrons to become an anion. The electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions then holds the crystal lattice together. This process is driven by the overall decrease in potential energy of the system, which can be quantified by the lattice energy released when gaseous ions combine to form a solid.

1.2 Factors Influencing Lattice Energy

The magnitude of lattice energy depends primarily on the charges of the ions and the distance between their nuclei. According to the Born‑Landé equation, lattice energy is directly proportional to the product of the ionic charges and inversely proportional to the sum of the ionic radii. Consequently, a compound such as MgO, with doubly charged Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions, possesses a much larger lattice energy than NaCl, where both ions carry a single charge. Smaller ions also lead to shorter inter‑ionic distances, further increasing the lattice energy. These trends explain why high‑charge, small‑radius ionic solids have very high melting points and are often hard and brittle.

1.3 Born‑Haber Cycle and Thermodynamic Consistency

The Born‑Haber cycle provides a thermodynamic pathway that links the enthalpy of formation of an ionic solid to measurable quantities such as ionisation energy, electron affinity, sublimation energy, bond dissociation energy, and lattice energy. By applying Hess’s law, the cycle demonstrates that the sum of all steps equals the standard enthalpy of formation. This relationship allows chemists to calculate lattice energies indirectly when direct measurement is impractical, and it also serves as a check on the internal consistency of experimental thermochemical data.

Covalent Bonding and Bond Polarity

2.1 Electron Sharing and Bond Formation

Covalent bonds result from the sharing of one or more pairs of electrons between atoms of comparable electronegativity. Each shared pair constitutes a single bond; two shared pairs give a double bond, and three give a triple bond. The overlap of atomic orbitals creates a region of increased electron density between the nuclei, lowering the potential energy of the system. The strength of a covalent bond is reflected in its bond dissociation energy, which varies with the extent of orbital overlap and the number of shared electron pairs.

2.2 Polar Covalent Bonds and Electronegativity Difference

When the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the shared electron pair is drawn closer to the more electronegative atom, creating a dipole. The degree of polarity is commonly estimated from the Pauling electronegativity difference: a difference below 0.4 is considered essentially non‑polar, between 0.4 and 1.7 polar covalent, and above 1.7 largely ionic. This continuum explains why bonds such as H–F are strongly polar while C–H bonds are only weakly polar. The resulting molecular dipole moment depends not only on individual bond dipoles but also on the molecular geometry, which determines whether bond dipoles cancel or add vectorially.

2.3 Resonance and Delocalisation

In many molecules a single Lewis structure cannot adequately describe the electron distribution. Resonance structures represent alternative arrangements of π‑electrons and lone pairs that differ only in the position of electrons, not nuclei. The true electronic structure is a resonance hybrid, with delocalised electrons spread over several atoms. Delocalisation lowers the overall energy of the molecule, often resulting in bond lengths intermediate between typical single and double bonds, as seen in benzene where all C–C bonds are equivalent at approximately 1.39 Å.

Example 1: Determining Bond Polarity
Predict whether the bond in hydrogen chloride (HCl) is non‑polar covalent, polar covalent, or ionic. Use Pauling electronegativity values: H = 2.20, Cl = 3.16.
The electronegativity difference Δχ = 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96. Since 0.4 < Δχ < 1.7, the H–Cl bond is classified as polar covalent. The chlorine atom bears a partial negative charge (δ⁻) and hydrogen a partial positive charge (δ⁺), giving HCl a permanent dipole moment of about 1.08 D.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the “type of bond” based on electronegativity difference, always quote the numerical Δχ and the conventional boundaries (0.4 and 1.7). Examiners award marks for showing the calculation, not just the final label.

VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

3.1 Electron‑Pair Repulsion Principle

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model predicts molecular shapes by assuming that electron domains — bonding pairs and lone pairs — arrange themselves around a central atom to minimise repulsions. The order of repulsion strength is lone‑pair–lone‑pair > lone‑pair–bonding‑pair > bonding‑pair–bonding‑pair. Consequently, lone pairs occupy more space and compress adjacent bond angles relative to the ideal geometry. For example, in water (H₂O) the two lone pairs on oxygen push the H–O–H angle down to 104.5°, compared with the tetrahedral angle of 109.5°.

3.2 Common Steric Numbers and Their Geometries

The steric number (SN) equals the number of σ‑bonds plus the number of lone pairs on the central atom. SN = 2 gives a linear arrangement (180°), SN = 3 gives trigonal planar (120°), SN = 4 gives tetrahedral (109.5°), SN = 5 gives trigonal bipyramidal (90° and 120°), and SN = 6 gives octahedral (90°). When lone pairs are present, the molecular geometry — the arrangement of atoms only — deviates from the electron‑domain geometry: SN = 4 with one lone pair yields a trigonal pyramidal shape (e.g., NH₃), while SN = 4 with two lone pairs yields a bent shape (e.g., H₂O).

3.3 Predicting Shapes of Polyatomic Ions

VSEPR applies equally to cations and anions. The ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) has SN = 4 and no lone pairs, so it is tetrahedral. The nitrate ion (NO₃⁻) has SN = 3 with one double bond and two single bonds resonance‑delocalised; the electron‑domain geometry is trigonal planar and the molecular geometry is also trigonal planar. The xenon tetrafluoride molecule (XeF₄) has SN = 6 with two lone pairs occupying axial positions, giving a square‑planar molecular geometry. These predictions are routinely tested by asking students to draw the Lewis structure, count electron domains, and name both the electron‑domain and molecular geometries.

Hybridisation and Orbital Overlap

4.1 Concept of Hybrid Orbitals

Hybridisation is a mathematical mixing of atomic orbitals on the same atom to produce a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals that are oriented in space according to the VSEPR‑predicted geometry. The number of hybrid orbitals formed equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed. For instance, one s and three p orbitals combine to give four sp³ hybrids directed toward the corners of a tetrahedron. The hybrid orbitals then overlap with orbitals on neighbouring atoms to form σ‑bonds, while any unhybridised p orbitals can overlap side‑on to form π‑bonds.

4.2 Common Hybridisation Schemes

sp hybridisation (linear, 180°) involves one s and one p orbital, leaving two unhybridised p orbitals for two π‑bonds as in acetylene (C₂H₂). sp² hybridisation (trigonal planar, 120°) uses one s and two p orbitals, leaving one p orbital for a π‑bond, typical of ethene (C₂H₄) and the carbonyl carbon in aldehydes. sp³ hybridisation (tetrahedral, 109.5°) uses all three p orbitals, giving four σ‑bonds as in methane (CH₄). For expanded octets, d orbitals participate: sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal) and sp³d² (octahedral) hybridisations are invoked for molecules such as PCl₅ and SF₆ respectively.

4.3 Hybridisation and Bond Angles in Distorted Geometries

When lone pairs occupy hybrid orbitals, the ideal bond angles are reduced because lone‑pair orbitals exert greater repulsion. In ammonia (NH₃) the nitrogen is sp³ hybridised; one hybrid holds a lone pair, and the three N–H bonds use the remaining three hybrids, giving an H–N–H angle of 107°, slightly less than the tetrahedral angle. In water, two sp³ hybrids hold lone pairs, compressing the H–O–H angle further to 104.5°. The concept of “partial hybridisation” is sometimes used to rationalise intermediate angles, but the standard VSEPR‑hybridisation framework remains the primary tool for exam‑level predictions.

Molecular Orbital Theory and Bond Order

5.1 Formation of Molecular Orbitals

Molecular Orbital (MO) theory treats electrons in a molecule as occupying orbitals that extend over the entire nuclear framework. Atomic orbitals combine linearly to give an equal number of molecular orbitals: bonding orbitals (lower energy, constructive interference) and antibonding orbitals (higher energy, destructive interference). For diatomic molecules of the second period, the ordering of MOs depends on the energy gap between the 2s and 2p atomic orbitals. In molecules up to N₂ the σ₂p orbital lies above the π₂p orbitals, whereas from O₂ onward the σ₂p drops below the π₂p due to increased s‑p mixing.

5.2 Bond Order and Magnetic Behaviour

Bond order is defined as half the difference between the number of electrons in bonding MOs and the number in antibonding MOs. A bond order of zero indicates no bond; a bond order of one corresponds to a single bond, two to a double bond, and three to a triple bond. MO theory correctly predicts the paramagnetism of O₂, which has two unpaired electrons in the degenerate π*₂p antibonding orbitals, giving a bond order of two. In contrast, N₂ has all electrons paired and a bond order of three, consistent with its strong triple bond and diamagnetism.

5.3 Application to Heteronuclear Diatomics and Polyatomics

For heteronuclear diatomics such as CO or NO, the atomic orbitals contribute unequally to each MO, polarising the electron density toward the more electronegative atom. The MO diagram still yields a bond order that matches experimental bond lengths and strengths. In polyatomic molecules, group theory is used to construct symmetry‑adapted linear combinations of atomic orbitals, leading to delocalised MOs that explain phenomena such as the aromatic stability of benzene (six π electrons occupying three bonding π MOs). While a full group‑theoretical treatment is beyond the introductory syllabus, the qualitative MO picture of delocalisation and bond order remains a powerful conceptual tool.

Hydrogen Bonding and Its Consequences

6.1 Nature of the Hydrogen Bond

A hydrogen bond is a special type of dipole–dipole interaction that occurs when a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F) experiences an attractive force toward a lone pair on a neighbouring electronegative atom. The interaction energy typically ranges from 10 to 40 kJ mol⁻¹, stronger than ordinary van der Waals forces but weaker than covalent bonds. The directionality of the hydrogen bond — preferring a near‑linear X–H···Y arrangement — arises from the overlap of the hydrogen 1s orbital with the lone‑pair orbital on the acceptor atom.

6.2 Effects on Physical Properties

Hydrogen bonding dramatically elevates boiling points, melting points, and viscosities of substances that can form extensive networks. Water’s anomalously high boiling point (100 °C) compared with H₂S (−60 °C) is a classic illustration: each water molecule can donate two and accept two hydrogen bonds, creating a three‑dimensional network. In contrast, hydrogen sulfide lacks sufficiently electronegative sulfur to form strong hydrogen bonds, so only weak dipole–dipole forces operate. Similar reasoning explains why HF boils at 19.5 °C while HCl boils at −85 °C, and why ammonia (−33 °C) has a higher boiling point than phosphine (−87 °C).

6.3 Hydrogen Bonding in Biological Macromolecules

In biology, hydrogen bonds stabilise the secondary structures of proteins (α‑helices and β‑sheets) and the double helix of DNA. In an α‑helix, the carbonyl oxygen of residue i accepts a hydrogen bond from the amide hydrogen of residue i + 4, creating a regular helical geometry. In DNA, complementary base pairs (A–T with two hydrogen bonds, G–C with three) provide the specificity required for replication and transcription. The cumulative effect of many relatively weak hydrogen bonds yields a stable yet reversible association, essential for processes such as strand separation during DNA replication.

Understanding the interplay between ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonding, together with the predictive power of VSEPR, hybridisation, and molecular orbital theory, equips students to rationalise molecular structure, reactivity, and macroscopic properties across the whole of chemistry.
