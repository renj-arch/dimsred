Ionic Bonding and Lattice Energy

1.1 Formation of Ionic Bonds

Ionic bonding arises when atoms with a large difference in electronegativity transfer electrons so that each achieves a stable noble‑gas configuration. The metal atom, typically from Group 1 or Group 2, loses one or two electrons to become a cation, while the non‑metal atom, often from Group 16 or Group 17, gains those electrons to become an anion. The electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions then holds the crystal lattice together. This process is driven by the overall decrease in potential energy of the system, which can be quantified by the lattice energy released when gaseous ions combine to form a solid.

1.2 Factors Influencing Lattice Energy

The magnitude of lattice energy depends primarily on the charges of the ions and the distance between their nuclei. According to the Born‑Landé equation, lattice energy is directly proportional to the product of the ionic charges and inversely proportional to the sum of the ionic radii. Consequently, a compound such as MgO, with doubly charged Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions, possesses a much larger lattice energy than NaCl, where both ions carry a single charge. Smaller ions also lead to shorter inter‑ionic distances, further increasing the lattice energy. These trends explain why high‑charge, small‑radius ionic solids have very high melting points and are often hard and brittle.

1.3 Born‑Haber Cycle and Thermodynamic Consistency

The Born‑Haber cycle provides a thermodynamic pathway that links the enthalpy of formation of an ionic solid to measurable quantities such as ionisation energy, electron affinity, sublimation energy, bond dissociation energy, and lattice energy. By applying Hess’s law, the cycle demonstrates that the sum of all steps equals the standard enthalpy of formation. This relationship allows chemists to calculate lattice energies indirectly when direct measurement is impractical, and it also serves as a check on the internal consistency of experimental thermochemical data.

Covalent Bonding and Bond Polarity

2.1 Electron Sharing and Bond Formation

Covalent bonds result from the sharing of one or more pairs of electrons between atoms of comparable electronegativity. Each shared pair constitutes a single bond; two shared pairs give a double bond, and three give a triple bond. The overlap of atomic orbitals creates a region of increased electron density between the nuclei, lowering the potential energy of the system. The strength of a covalent bond is reflected in its bond dissociation energy, which varies with the extent of orbital overlap and the number of shared electron pairs.

2.2 Polar Covalent Bonds and Electronegativity Difference

When the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the shared electron pair is drawn closer to the more electronegative atom, creating a dipole. The degree of polarity is commonly estimated from the Pauling electronegativity difference: a difference below 0.4 is considered essentially non‑polar, between 0.4 and 1.7 polar covalent, and above 1.7 largely ionic. This continuum explains why bonds such as H–F are strongly polar while C–H bonds are only weakly polar. The resulting molecular dipole moment depends not only on individual bond dipoles but also on the molecular geometry, which determines whether bond dipoles cancel or add vectorially.

2.3 Resonance and Delocalisation

In many molecules a single Lewis structure cannot adequately describe the electron distribution. Resonance structures represent alternative arrangements of π‑electrons and lone pairs that differ only in the position of electrons, not nuclei. The true electronic structure is a resonance hybrid, with delocalised electrons spread over several atoms. Delocalisation lowers the overall energy of the molecule, often resulting in bond lengths intermediate between typical single and double bonds, as seen in benzene where all C–C bonds are equivalent at approximately 1.39 Å.

Example 1: Determining Bond Polarity
Predict whether the bond in hydrogen chloride (HCl) is non‑polar covalent, polar covalent, or ionic. Use Pauling electronegativity values: H = 2.20, Cl = 3.16.
The electronegativity difference Δχ = 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96. Since 0.4 < Δχ < 1.7, the H–Cl bond is classified as polar covalent. The chlorine atom bears a partial negative charge (δ⁻) and hydrogen a partial positive charge (δ⁺), giving HCl a permanent dipole moment of about 1.08 D.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the “type of bond” based on electronegativity difference, always quote the numerical Δχ and the conventional boundaries (0.4 and 1.7). Examiners award marks for showing the calculation, not just the final label.

VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

3.1 Electron‑Pair Repulsion Principle

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model predicts molecular shapes by assuming that electron domains — bonding pairs and lone pairs — arrange themselves around a central atom to minimise repulsions. The order of repulsion strength is lone‑pair–lone‑pair > lone‑pair–bonding‑pair > bonding‑pair–bonding‑pair. Consequently, lone pairs occupy more space and compress adjacent bond angles relative to the ideal geometry. For example, in water (H₂O) the two lone pairs on oxygen push the H–O–H angle down to 104.5°, compared with the tetrahedral angle of 109.5°.

3.2 Common Steric Numbers and Their Geometries

The steric number (SN) equals the number of σ‑bonds plus the number of lone pairs on the central atom. SN = 2 gives a linear arrangement (180°), SN = 3 gives trigonal planar (120°), SN = 4 gives tetrahedral (109.5°), SN = 5 gives trigonal bipyramidal (90° and 120°), and SN = 6 gives octahedral (90°). When lone pairs are present, the molecular geometry — the arrangement of atoms only — deviates from the electron‑domain geometry: SN = 4 with one lone pair yields a trigonal pyramidal shape (e.g., NH₃), while SN = 4 with two lone pairs yields a bent shape (e.g., H₂O).

3.3 Predicting Shapes of Polyatomic Ions

VSEPR applies equally to cations and anions. The ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) has SN = 4 and no lone pairs, so it is tetrahedral. The nitrate ion (NO₃⁻) has SN = 3 with one double bond and two single bonds resonance‑delocalised; the electron‑domain geometry is trigonal planar and the molecular geometry is also trigonal planar. The xenon tetrafluoride molecule (XeF₄) has SN = 6 with two lone pairs occupying axial positions, giving a square‑planar molecular geometry. These predictions are routinely tested by asking students to draw the Lewis structure, count electron domains, and name both the electron‑domain and molecular geometries.

Hybridisation and Orbital Overlap

4.1 Concept of Hybrid Orbitals

Hybridisation is a mathematical mixing of atomic orbitals on the same atom to produce a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals that are oriented in space according to the VSEPR‑predicted geometry. The number of hybrid orbitals formed equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed. For instance, one s and three p orbitals combine to give four sp³ hybrids directed toward the corners of a tetrahedron. The hybrid orbitals then overlap with orbitals on neighbouring atoms to form σ‑bonds, while any unhybridised p orbitals can overlap side‑on to form π‑bonds.

4.2 Common Hybridisation Schemes

sp hybridisation (linear, 180°) involves one s and one p orbital, leaving two unhybridised p orbitals for two π‑bonds as in acetylene (C₂H₂). sp² hybridisation (trigonal planar, 120°) uses one s and two p orbitals, leaving one p orbital for a π‑bond, typical of ethene (C₂H₄) and the carbonyl carbon in aldehydes. sp³ hybridisation (tetrahedral, 109.5°) uses all three p orbitals, giving four σ‑bonds as in methane (CH₄). For expanded octets, d orbitals participate: sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal) and sp³d² (octahedral) hybridisations are invoked for molecules such as PCl₅ and SF₆ respectively.

4.3 Hybridisation and Bond Angles in Distorted Geometries

When lone pairs occupy hybrid orbitals, the ideal bond angles are reduced because lone‑pair orbitals exert greater repulsion. In ammonia (NH₃) the nitrogen is sp³ hybridised; one hybrid holds a lone pair, and the three N–H bonds use the remaining three hybrids, giving an H–N–H angle of 107°, slightly less than the tetrahedral angle. In water, two sp³ hybrids hold lone pairs, compressing the H–O–H angle further to 104.5°. The concept of “partial hybridisation” is sometimes used to rationalise intermediate angles, but the standard VSEPR‑hybridisation framework remains the primary tool for exam‑level predictions.

Molecular Orbital Theory and Bond Order

5.1 Formation of Molecular Orbitals

Molecular Orbital (MO) theory treats electrons in a molecule as occupying orbitals that extend over the entire nuclear framework. Atomic orbitals combine linearly to give an equal number of molecular orbitals: bonding orbitals (lower energy, constructive interference) and antibonding orbitals (higher energy, destructive interference). For diatomic molecules of the second period, the ordering of MOs depends on the energy gap between the 2s and 2p atomic orbitals. In molecules up to N₂ the σ₂p orbital lies above the π₂p orbitals, whereas from O₂ onward the σ₂p drops below the π₂p due to decreased s‑p mixing.

5.2 Bond Order and Magnetic Behaviour

Bond order is defined as half the difference between the number of electrons in bonding MOs and the number in antibonding MOs. A bond order of zero indicates no bond; a bond order of one corresponds to a single bond, two to a double bond, and three to a triple bond. MO theory correctly predicts the paramagnetism of O₂, which has two unpaired electrons in the degenerate π*₂p antibonding orbitals, giving a bond order of two. In contrast, N₂ has all electrons paired and a bond order of three, consistent with its strong triple bond and diamagnetism.

5.3 Application to Heteronuclear Diatomics and Polyatomics

For heteronuclear diatomics such as CO or NO, the atomic orbitals contribute unequally to each MO, polarising the electron density toward the more electronegative atom. The MO diagram still yields a bond order that matches experimental bond lengths and strengths. In polyatomic molecules, group theory is used to construct symmetry‑adapted linear combinations of atomic orbitals, leading to delocalised MOs that explain phenomena such as the aromatic stability of benzene (six π electrons occupying three bonding π MOs). While a full group‑theoretical treatment is beyond the introductory syllabus, the qualitative MO picture of delocalisation and bond order remains a powerful conceptual tool.

Hydrogen Bonding and Its Consequences

6.1 Nature of the Hydrogen Bond

A hydrogen bond is a special type of dipole–dipole interaction that occurs when a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F) experiences an attractive force toward a lone pair on a neighbouring electronegative atom. The interaction energy typically ranges from 10 to 40 kJ mol⁻¹, stronger than ordinary van der Waals forces but weaker than covalent bonds. The directionality of the hydrogen bond — preferring a near‑linear X–H···Y arrangement — arises from the overlap of the hydrogen 1s orbital with the lone‑pair orbital on the acceptor atom.

6.2 Effects on Physical Properties

Hydrogen bonding dramatically elevates boiling points, melting points, and viscosities of substances that can form extensive networks. Water’s anomalously high boiling point (100 °C) compared with H₂S (−60 °C) is a classic illustration: each water molecule can donate two and accept two hydrogen bonds, creating a three‑dimensional network. In contrast, hydrogen sulfide lacks sufficiently electronegative sulfur to form strong hydrogen bonds, so only weak dipole–dipole forces operate. Similar reasoning explains why HF boils at 19.5 °C while HCl boils at −85 °C, and why ammonia (−33 °C) has a higher boiling point than phosphine (−87 °C).

6.3 Hydrogen Bonding in Biological Macromolecules

In biology, hydrogen bonds stabilise the secondary structures of proteins (α‑helices and β‑sheets) and the double helix of DNA. In an α‑helix, the carbonyl oxygen of residue i accepts a hydrogen bond from the amide hydrogen of residue i + 4, creating a regular helical geometry. In DNA, complementary base pairs (A–T with two hydrogen bonds, G–C with three) provide the specificity required for replication and transcription. The cumulative effect of many relatively weak hydrogen bonds yields a stable yet reversible association, essential for processes such as strand separation during DNA replication.

Understanding the interplay between ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonding, together with the predictive power of VSEPR, hybridisation, and molecular orbital theory, equips students to rationalise molecular structure, reactivity, and macroscopic properties across the whole of chemistry.

Symmetry-Adapted Linear Combinations and Group Theory in Molecular Orbital Construction

Molecular orbital (MO) theory for polyatomic molecules becomes predictive when the symmetry of the nuclear framework is exploited. The first step is to assign the molecule to a point group (e.g., C₂ᵥ for H₂O, D₃h for BF₃, T_d for CH₄). Each atomic orbital (AO) on the constituent atoms is then classified according to the irreducible representations (irreps) of that point group. By forming symmetry‑adapted linear combinations (SALCs) of AOs that transform as a given irrep, one obtains basis functions that can only interact with other SALCs of the same symmetry. This block‑diagonalises the Hamiltonian, reducing a large secular determinant into smaller, independent sub‑determinants — one for each irrep.

For a concrete illustration, consider the σ‑framework of methane (CH₄, T_d). The four hydrogen 1s orbitals generate SALCs of a₁ and t₂ symmetry. The carbon 2s orbital belongs to a₁, while the three 2p orbitals span t₂. Only the a₁ SALC mixes with C 2s, and the three t₂ SALCs mix with the C 2p set, giving four bonding MOs (one a₁, three t₂) and four antibonding counterparts. The resulting MO diagram explains the tetrahedral bond angle of 109.5° without invoking hybridisation. In contrast, for a bent molecule such as H₂O (C₂ᵥ), the two O‑H σ‑bonding SALCs transform as a₁ and b₂, while the oxygen lone‑pair orbitals occupy a₁ and b₁. The Walsh diagram for AH₂ molecules emerges naturally from tracking how the energies of these symmetry‑labelled MOs vary with the H‑A‑H angle.

Group theory also dictates selection rules for electronic spectroscopy. An electronic transition is allowed only if the direct product of the irreps of the initial and final MOs contains the irrep of the electric‑dipole operator (x, y, z). This predicts, for example, that the lowest π→π* transition in ethylene (D₂h) is symmetry‑forbidden (g→u) but becomes weakly allowed through vibronic coupling. In examinations, students may be asked to construct SALCs for a given set of ligand orbitals, to label the resulting MOs with irreps, or to use correlation tables to predict how MO ordering changes upon a geometry distortion (e.g., D₃h → C₂ᵥ). Mastery of character tables, projection operators, and the descent‑in‑symmetry method is therefore essential for advanced MO problems.

π‑Backbonding and the Spectrochemical Series in Transition Metal Complexes

The spectrochemical series orders ligands by the magnitude of the d‑orbital splitting Δ they produce in an octahedral field: I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO. Crystal field theory (CFT) treats this trend as a purely electrostatic effect, but it cannot explain why π‑acceptor ligands such as CO and CN⁻ generate exceptionally large Δ values. Ligand field theory (LFT) incorporates covalent metal–ligand orbital interactions, and the key to the series lies in π‑backbonding.

In an octahedral complex, the metal t₂g set (d_xy, d_xz, d_yz) can overlap with ligand π* orbitals of appropriate symmetry (t₂g in O_h). When a ligand possesses low‑lying empty π* orbitals (CO, CN⁻, NO⁺), electron density from filled metal t₂g orbitals delocalises into these π* orbitals. This π‑backbonding stabilises the t₂g set, increasing the t₂g–e_g gap Δ_o. Conversely, ligands with filled π orbitals (halides, OH⁻) can donate π‑electron density into empty metal t₂g orbitals (π‑donation), raising the t₂g energy and decreasing Δ_o. The net Δ_o is therefore a balance between σ‑donation (which raises e_g) and π‑interactions (which shift t₂g).

Quantitative LFT expresses Δ_o as Δ_o = Δ_o(σ) + Δ_o(π), where Δ_o(π) is negative for π‑acceptors and positive for π‑donors. Spectroscopic data (UV‑Vis, IR) confirm this: the C≡O stretching frequency in metal carbonyls drops by 100–200 cm⁻¹ relative to free CO, directly evidencing π‑backbonding. The series also correlates with metal oxidation state and identity: higher oxidation states and 4d/5d metals have more diffuse d‑orbitals, enhancing π‑overlap and magnifying the ligand‑dependent splitting. Examination questions often require students to rationalise the position of a ligand in the series using π‑acceptor/donor arguments, to predict spin‑state changes upon ligand substitution, or to calculate Δ_o from absorption maxima using the relation Δ_o (cm⁻¹) = 1/λ (cm).

Worked Example: Predicting Spin State from Ligand Field Parameters
A d⁶ octahedral complex of Fe(II) shows an absorption band at 12 500 cm⁻¹ assigned to the ¹A₁g → ¹T₁g transition. The pairing energy P for Fe(II) is 12 000 cm⁻¹. Determine whether the complex is high‑spin or low‑spin and justify your answer using the spectrochemical series.
The observed band corresponds to the ligand‑field splitting Δ_o = 12 500 cm⁻¹. For a d⁶ ion, the high‑spin configuration (t₂g⁴ e_g²) requires Δ_o < P, while the low‑spin configuration (t₂g⁶ e_g⁰) requires Δ_o > P. Here Δ_o (12 500 cm⁻¹) > P (12 000 cm⁻¹), so the complex is low‑spin. Low‑spin Fe(II) is favoured by strong‑field ligands such as CN⁻, CO, or phenanthroline, which are high in the spectrochemical series because they are π‑acceptors that increase Δ_o via π‑backbonding. Therefore the ligand set must consist of strong‑field π‑acceptors.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to rank ligands by field strength, first separate them into π‑acceptors (CO, CN⁻, NO⁺), σ‑only donors (NH₃, H₂O), and π‑donors (halides, OH⁻). Then apply the qualitative rule: π‑acceptors > σ‑only > π‑donors. This avoids memorising the entire series and works for unseen ligands.

Quantitative Treatment of Van der Waals Interactions and London Dispersion Forces

While hydrogen bonding dominates the physical properties of many molecular solids, London dispersion forces (LDF) are universal and often decisive for non‑polar species. The instantaneous dipole–induced dipole interaction energy between two identical spherical atoms at distance R can be expressed by the London formula: E_disp = –(3/4) (α² I) / R⁶, where α is the static dipole polarisability and I the first ionisation energy. For hetero‑atomic pairs, the combination rule E_disp = –(3/2) (α_A α_B I_A I_B) / (I_A + I_B) R⁶ is used. These expressions show that dispersion scales with the product of polarisabilities and inversely with the sixth power of separation, explaining why heavier, more polarisable atoms (e.g., I₂, Xe) exhibit larger boiling points despite lacking permanent dipoles.

In molecular systems, the total dispersion energy is a sum over all atom‑pair contributions, but many‑body effects (Axilrod–Teller–Muto three‑body term) introduce a non‑additive correction proportional to (1 + 3 cos θ₁ cos θ₂ cos θ₃) / (R₁₂ R₂₃ R₃₁)³. Modern force fields (e.g., DFT‑D3, DFT‑D4) incorporate environment‑dependent C₆ coefficients derived from atomic polarisabilities and coordination numbers, achieving chemical accuracy for intermolecular binding energies. The C₆ coefficient for a pair of atoms A and B is related to their dynamic polarisabilities α_A(iω) and α_B(iω) via the Casimir–Polder integral: C₆ = (3/π) ∫₀^∞ α_A(iω) α_B(iω) dω. This quantum‑mechanical foundation allows prediction of dispersion interactions for exotic species (e.g., noble‑gas clusters, fullerene dimers) where experimental data are scarce.

Examination problems may provide polarisability volumes (α′ in Å³) and ionisation energies (in eV) for two atoms and ask for the dispersion energy at a given internuclear distance. Students must convert units consistently (1 a.u. of energy = 27.2114 eV, 1 a₀ = 0.529 Å) and apply the London formula. A typical trap is to forget the R⁻⁶ dependence or to use the ionisation energy in eV without converting to Hartree. Another common error is to treat the C₆ coefficient as a constant for a given element, ignoring its dependence on hybridisation and oxidation state — for instance, sp³ carbon in methane has a smaller α than sp² carbon in graphene, leading to weaker dispersion per carbon atom.

Bond Dissociation Enthalpies, Thermochemical Cycles, and the Born–Haber Extension to Covalent Networks

Bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE) is the enthalpy change for the homolytic cleavage of a specific bond in the gas phase: A–B(g) → A·(g) + B·(g). Unlike bond energies averaged over a molecule, BDEs are state‑specific and can be measured directly by photoionisation mass spectrometry or derived from thermochemical cycles. The Born–Haber cycle, originally devised for ionic lattices, can be extended to covalent network solids (e.g., diamond, SiC) by replacing lattice enthalpy with the enthalpy of atomisation of the solid. For diamond, the cycle reads: C(graphite) → C(g) ΔH_atom = 716.7 kJ mol⁻¹; C(g) → C⁺(g) + e⁻ IE = 1086 kJ mol⁻¹; C⁺(g) + e⁻ → C(g) EA = –122 kJ mol⁻¹; and the sum of BDEs for the C–C bonds broken in the hypothetical gas‑phase polymerisation yields the cohesive energy.

In molecular thermochemistry, the enthalpy of reaction Δ_rH° can be estimated from BDEs using Δ_rH° ≈ Σ BDE(bonds broken) – Σ BDE(bonds formed). This approximation neglects changes in zero‑point vibrational energy and thermal corrections, but it is remarkably accurate for gas‑phase reactions involving similar bond types. For example, the combustion of methane: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Using average BDEs (C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 799, O–H 463 kJ mol⁻¹) gives Δ_rH° ≈ [4×413 + 2×498] – [2×799 + 4×463] = –802 kJ mol⁻¹, close to the experimental –890 kJ mol⁻¹. The discrepancy arises because BDEs in CO₂ and H₂O are not identical to the average values used.

Advanced examinations may ask students to construct a thermochemical cycle for the formation of a covalent solid from its elements in their standard states, to calculate the average BDE in a network solid from its enthalpy of atomisation, or to evaluate the effect of isotopic substitution (H/D) on BDEs via zero‑point energy differences. A frequent pitfall is confusing bond dissociation enthalpy (a 0 K quantity) with bond energy (often quoted at 298 K) and neglecting the temperature correction ΔH(298 K) – ΔH(0 K) = ∫₀²⁹⁸ ΔC_p dT. Another trap is applying the simple BDE summation to reactions where significant resonance stabilisation changes (e.g., aromatic ring opening) occur, because the BDE of a bond in a resonance‑stabilised system does not transfer directly to the product.

  • Assuming that all ligands of a given donor atom type (e.g., N‑donors) have identical field strength; π‑acceptor ability of CN⁻ versus π‑donor character of NH₃ creates a large Δ_o difference.
  • Using the London dispersion formula with ionisation energy in electron‑volts without converting to Hartree, leading to errors of orders of magnitude.
  • Treating the C₆ coefficient as invariant for an element across different hybridisations; sp, sp², and sp³ carbon have measurably different polarisabilities.
  • Applying the simple BDE summation ΔH ≈ ΣBDE(broken) – ΣBDE(formed) to reactions involving aromatic systems without accounting for resonance energy loss or gain.
  • Confusing bond dissociation enthalpy at 0 K with bond energy at 298 K and omitting the heat‑capacity correction in thermochemical cycles.
  • Neglecting the three‑body Axilrod–Teller–Muto term when estimating dispersion energies in condensed phases, which can contribute 10–20 % of the total interaction.
  • Overlooking that the spectrochemical series order can invert for metal ions with very high oxidation states (e.g., Mn(VII) in MnO₄⁻) because π‑donation from oxo ligands dominates.

Valence Bond Theory and Resonance Hybrid Construction Beyond Simple Lewis Structures

Valence bond (VB) theory describes covalent bonding as the overlap of atomic orbitals that retain their individual identity, in contrast to the delocalised molecular orbitals of MO theory. In its modern formulation, VB theory employs hybrid orbitals (sp, sp², sp³, dsp², d²sp³) to rationalise observed geometries, but the true power lies in the linear combination of multiple VB structures — resonance hybrids — to capture electron delocalisation. A resonance hybrid is not a rapid interconversion of discrete Lewis structures; it is a single quantum‑mechanical wavefunction expressed as a weighted sum of contributing VB structures. The weights are determined by the variational principle: structures that minimise the energy contribute more. For benzene, the two Kekulé structures each receive a weight of approximately 0.5, while the three Dewar structures contribute < 0.05 each. The resulting π‑electron density is uniformly distributed over the ring, explaining equal C–C bond lengths (1.39 Å) and the large resonance energy (≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹). In heteroaromatics such as pyridine, the electronegativity of nitrogen skews the weights toward structures that place negative charge on nitrogen, a fact that can be quantified by calculating the coefficients in a VB configuration interaction (CI) expansion. Modern VB programs (e.g., XMVB, VB2000) perform full CI within a chosen orbital basis, yielding quantitative resonance energies that agree with thermochemical data. Students should recognise that the “resonance hybrid” label in textbooks is a qualitative shorthand for a multi‑reference wavefunction, and that the number of significant contributors grows rapidly with system size — naphthalene already requires > 30 structures for a converged description. Examination questions often ask to draw all reasonable resonance forms for a given conjugated system and to rank them by expected contribution; the ranking follows three rules: (1) maximise the number of covalent bonds, (2) minimise formal charge separation, (3) place negative charge on the more electronegative atom. Violating any rule reduces the weight dramatically.

Beyond π‑systems, VB theory handles hypervalent molecules without invoking d‑orbital participation. In SF₆, the six S–F bonds are described by six equivalent sp³d² hybrids in a simple hybridisation picture, but a proper VB treatment uses three‑centre four‑electron (3c‑4e) bonds: each S–F interaction is a resonance hybrid of ionic S⁺–F⁻ and covalent S–F structures, with the ionic form contributing ≈ 30 %. This explains the relatively low bond dissociation enthalpy (≈ 280 kJ mol⁻¹) compared with a pure covalent single bond. The same 3c‑4e model applies to XeF₂, ClF₃, and the bifluoride anion HF₂⁻. In each case the central atom uses p‑orbitals to form two half‑bonds that share a pair of electrons, a concept that predates MO theory but is fully consistent with it. Advanced problems may provide a set of VB structures for a hypervalent species and ask for the bond order derived from the weighted sum; the answer is obtained by counting the number of electron pairs shared between the two atoms across all structures, multiplied by their respective coefficients.

Electron Localization Function and Atoms-in-Molecules Topology for Bond Characterization

The electron localization function (ELF), introduced by Becke and Edgecombe in 1990, provides a real‑space measure of the probability of finding a second electron with the same spin near a reference electron. ELF values range from 0 (complete delocalisation) to 1 (perfect localisation). In a covalent bond, a basin of high ELF (≈ 0.8–0.9) appears between the nuclei, corresponding to the shared electron pair. Lone pairs appear as basins centred on a single nucleus. The topology of ELF basins — their number, population, and shape — offers a rigorous, basis‑set‑independent classification of bonding: a two‑centre basin indicates a classical two‑electron bond; a monosynaptic basin signals a lone pair; a polysynaptic basin involving three or more nuclei identifies multi‑centre bonding (e.g., 3c‑2e bonds in diborane). The integration of the electron density over an ELF basin yields the basin population, which for a typical C–C single bond is ≈ 1.9 e⁻, while a C=C double bond shows two basins each with ≈ 1.7 e⁻. In transition‑metal complexes, ELF can distinguish σ‑donation (basin between metal and donor atom) from π‑back‑donation (basin on the ligand π* region).

Complementary to ELF, Bader’s atoms‑in‑molecules (AIM) theory partitions the total electron density ρ(r) into atomic basins bounded by zero‑flux surfaces ∇ρ·n = 0. Each basin contains exactly one nucleus (except for non‑nuclear attractors in some metallic clusters). The bond critical point (BCP) — a (3,‑1) critical point on the inter‑nuclear line — characterises a bond path. The electron density ρ_BCP and its Laplacian ∇²ρ_BCP at the BCP are widely used descriptors: shared‑shell (covalent) interactions exhibit ρ_BCP > 0.2 a.u. and ∇²ρ_BCP < 0; closed‑shell (ionic, hydrogen‑bond, van der Waals) interactions show ρ_BCP < 0.1 a.u. and ∇²ρ_BCP > 0. The total energy density H_BCP = G_BCP + V_BCP (kinetic + potential) further refines the classification: H_BCP < 0 indicates a stabilising covalent interaction, while H_BCP > 0 points to a purely electrostatic contact. For hydrogen bonds, typical ρ_BCP values lie between 0.002 and 0.04 a.u., and the correlation between ρ_BCP and the H‑bond energy is approximately linear (E_HB ≈ 0.5 ρ_BCP a.u. in kJ mol⁻¹). Examination questions may present a table of ρ_BCP and ∇²ρ_BCP for a series of X–H···Y contacts and ask to rank them by H‑bond strength; the correct ranking follows ρ_BCP magnitude. Another common task is to identify the bond type in a metal–ligand complex from AIM data: a ρ_BCP of 0.12 a.u. with ∇²ρ_BCP = +0.35 a.u. and H_BCP = –0.02 a.u. signifies a dative bond with partial covalency.

Halogen Bonding, Chalcogen Bonding, and Other σ‑Hole Interactions

Halogen bonding (XB) is a non‑covalent interaction in which a halogen atom (Cl, Br, I) acts as an electrophilic site, attracting a Lewis base (lone pair, π‑system, anion). The electrophilicity originates from a region of positive electrostatic potential on the outer side of the halogen, the σ‑hole, which arises because the halogen’s p‑orbital involved in the covalent bond to a carbon or another halogen is depleted of electron density. The magnitude of the σ‑hole increases down the group (I > Br > Cl > F) and with the electron‑withdrawing nature of the substituent attached to the halogen. Typical XB distances are 10–20 % shorter than the sum of van der Waals radii, and interaction energies range from 5 to 180 kJ mol⁻¹, overlapping with strong hydrogen bonds. The directionality is highly linear (C–X···Y angle ≈ 180°), reflecting the σ‑hole’s location on the extension of the C–X bond. Chalcogen bonding (ChB) is the analogous interaction for Group 16 elements (S, Se, Te). In a molecule such as R–Se–R′, the selenium atom exhibits two σ‑holes opposite the Se–R bonds, allowing bifurcated acceptor geometries. The σ‑hole depth correlates with the element’s polarizability and the oxidation state; Se(IV) in SeO₂ shows a stronger σ‑hole than Se(II) in R₂Se. Pnictogen bonding (Group 15), tetrel bonding (Group 14), and aerogen bonding (noble gases) follow the same σ‑hole paradigm. In all cases the interaction energy can be decomposed by symmetry‑adapted perturbation theory (SAPT) into electrostatic, induction, dispersion, and exchange‑repulsion components; electrostatics typically contributes 40–60 % for halogen bonds, while dispersion dominates for heavier chalcogen bonds. Experimental evidence comes from crystal engineering (co‑crystals of iodoperfluoroarenes with pyridine), solution‑phase NMR titration (downfield shift of the donor nucleus), and gas‑phase rotational spectroscopy (precise X···Y distances). In biological systems, halogen bonds are exploited in ligand design: the thyroid hormone thyroxine uses an iodine σ‑hole to bind its receptor, and several kinase inhibitors incorporate a halogen bond to a backbone carbonyl. Examination problems may provide a molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) map and ask to identify the σ‑hole location and predict the preferred approach vector of a nucleophile; the answer is the region of maximum positive potential on the extension of the covalent bond to the halogen/chalcogen.

Band Structure and Chemical Bonding in Extended Solids

In infinite periodic solids, the discrete molecular orbitals of a finite cluster broaden into continuous energy bands. The bonding picture is most transparent in the tight‑binding (linear combination of atomic orbitals) approximation: each atomic orbital gives rise to a band whose width is proportional to the overlap integral between neighbouring atoms. For a simple s‑band metal (e.g., lithium), the half‑filled 2s band yields metallic conductivity because electrons can be accelerated by an electric field into empty states within the same band. In covalent network solids such as diamond, the sp³ hybrids form a completely filled valence band (bonding states) separated by a large band gap (5.5 eV) from the empty conduction band (antibonding states); the gap corresponds to the energy required to break a C–C bond and promote an electron across the crystal. The band gap in semiconductors (Si 1.1 eV, GaAs 1.4 eV) is small enough for thermal excitation at room temperature, giving rise to intrinsic conductivity. The density of states (DOS) near the Fermi level determines many properties: a high DOS at E_F (as in transition metals with partially filled d‑bands) leads to large electronic specific heat and Pauli paramagnetism; a vanishing DOS (insulators) yields zero conductivity at 0 K. The concept of bond order extends to solids via the crystal orbital overlap population (COOP) or crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) curves, which resolve the contribution of each orbital interaction to the band structure. A negative COHP indicates bonding character, positive indicates antibonding. Integrating COHP up to the Fermi level yields a quantitative bond order per atom pair; for example, the Fe–Fe bond order in bcc iron is ≈ 0.6, reflecting the partial filling of antibonding d‑states. In ionic crystals (NaCl), the valence band is derived mainly from Cl 3p orbitals (filled) and the conduction band from Na 3s (empty); the large gap (≈ 8 eV) reflects the energy cost of transferring an electron from Cl⁻ to Na⁺. The Madelung energy, which stabilises the ionic lattice, appears in the band picture as the electrostatic potential that lowers the anion‑derived bands relative to the cation‑derived bands. Advanced exam questions may ask to sketch the qualitative band diagram for a given structure (e.g., zinc blende vs wurtzite) and to explain how the band gap changes with lattice constant (gap decreases as overlap increases) or with anion substitution (replacing O by S raises the valence band, reducing the gap). Another common task is to interpret a COHP plot for a metal–metal bond in an intermetallic compound and to state whether the interaction is net bonding or antibonding at the Fermi level.

Relativistic Contraction and Expansion Effects on Bonding in Heavy Main‑Group and Transition‑Metal Compounds

For elements with atomic number Z > 50, relativistic effects become chemically significant. The Dirac equation predicts that s‑ and p₁/₂‑orbitals contract and stabilise because the electron’s velocity near the nucleus approaches a significant fraction of the speed of light, increasing its relativistic mass. Conversely, d‑ and f‑orbitals expand and destabilise due to improved screening by the contracted s/p core. The net result is a “relativistic contraction” of the valence s orbital (e.g., 6s in gold, mercury, thallium) and a “relativistic expansion” of the valence d orbitals (5d in gold, 5d in platinum). These changes profoundly affect bonding. In gold, the 6s contraction lowers the 6s orbital energy by ≈ 2 eV, making Au⁺ (5d¹⁰6s⁰) unusually stable and giving gold its noble character; the Au–Au bond in Au₂ is strengthened (bond dissociation energy ≈ 226 kJ mol⁻¹) compared with Ag₂ (≈ 160 kJ mol⁻¹) despite gold’s larger size. The 5d expansion enhances π‑back‑donation in gold(I) complexes such as [Au(CN)₂]⁻, where the Au–C bond has significant covalent character. In mercury, the 6s² pair is so stabilised that Hg–Hg bonding in Hg₂²⁺ is weak (bond energy ≈ 60 kJ mol⁻¹) and mercury remains liquid at room temperature because the metallic bond is weakened by the inert‑pair effect amplified by relativity. For heavy main‑group elements (Tl, Pb, Bi), the 6s contraction deepens the inert‑pair effect, stabilising lower oxidation states (Tl⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺) relative to the higher ones. In transition‑metal chemistry, relativistic effects shift the spectrochemical series: 5d metals (Pt, Au, Hg) exhibit larger ligand‑field splittings Δ_o than their 4d and 3d congeners, partly because the expanded d orbitals overlap more effectively with ligand orbitals. This explains why Pt(II) complexes are almost exclusively low‑spin square planar, while Ni(II) (3d⁸) can be tetrahedral. Relativistic density‑functional calculations (e.g., ZORA, DKH) are now routine for accurate geometry and energetics of heavy‑element compounds. Examination questions may ask to predict the trend in M–M bond lengths down Group 11 (Cu–Cu > Ag–Ag < Au–Au) and to rationalise the anomaly using relativistic contraction of the 6s orbital in gold. Another typical problem provides the experimental Δ_o values for [MCl₆]²⁻ (M = Ni, Pd, Pt) and asks to explain the increase Ni < Pd < Pt in terms of radial extension of the d orbitals and relativistic effects.

  • Assuming all halogen bonds are stronger than hydrogen bonds; interaction energies overlap but depend strongly on the halogen’s substituent and the donor’s basicity.
  • Neglecting that band gaps in covalent solids decrease with increasing lattice constant because orbital overlap diminishes, not increases.
  • Treating COHP/COOP curves as qualitative only; the integrated value up to the Fermi level gives a quantitative bond order that can be compared across structures.
  • Overlooking the relativistic stabilisation of the 6s orbital in gold and mercury, leading to incorrect predictions of oxidation‑state preferences and bond strengths.
  • Misapplying the inert‑pair effect to lighter congeners (e.g., Sn²⁺ vs Sn⁴⁺) where relativistic contributions are negligible.
  • Forgetting that the Madelung energy in ionic crystals appears as a rigid shift of anion‑derived bands, not as a change in band width.

Wade‑Mingos Rules and Polyhedral Skeletal Electron Pair Theory

The Wade‑Mingos framework extends the electron‑counting logic of borane clusters to a broad family of main‑group and transition‑metal polyhedra. The central idea is that a cluster with n vertices requires a specific number of skeletal electron pairs (SEPs) to adopt a closed, deltahedral geometry. For a closo (closed) deltahedron the SEP count is n + 1; for a nido (nest‑like) structure derived by removing one vertex it is n + 2; for an arachno (web‑like) structure derived by removing two vertices it is n + 3; and for a hypho (net‑like) structure it is n + 4. Each vertex contributes its valence electrons, but only those not used in exo‑terminal bonds (e.g., B–H, M–CO) enter the skeletal pool. In boranes each BH unit contributes two electrons (one from B, one from H) to the skeletal framework; a bare B atom contributes three. For transition‑metal carbonyl clusters each metal contributes its valence d‑electrons plus the electrons donated by CO ligands (two per CO) after subtracting the 18‑electron count for each metal centre. The total skeletal electron count is then divided by two to give SEPs.

Classic examples illustrate the predictive power. B₆H₆²⁻ has six BH units (12 e⁻) plus a 2‑charge (2 e⁻) = 14 e⁻ → 7 SEPs = n + 1 (closo octahedron). B₅H₉ contains five BH units (10 e⁻) plus four bridging hydrogens each donating one electron (4 e⁻) = 14 e⁻ → 7 SEPs = n + 2 (nido square‑pyramid). B₄H₁₀ gives 4 BH (8 e⁻) + 6 bridging H (6 e⁻) = 14 e⁻ → 7 SEPs = n + 3 (arachno). In transition‑metal chemistry, [Fe₄(CO)₁₃]²⁻ contains four Fe(0) d⁸ centres (32 e⁻) and 13 CO (26 e⁻) plus a 2‑charge (2 e⁻) = 60 e⁻. Subtracting 4 × 18 = 72 e⁻ for the 18‑electron rule leaves –12 e⁻, which after correcting for metal‑metal bonding yields 6 SEPs = n + 2, consistent with a nido tetrahedral Fe₄ core. The rules also rationalise the stability of Zintl clusters such as Sn₉⁴⁻ (closo, 10 SEPs) and the geometry of metallacarboranes where a metal vertex replaces a BH unit.

Metal‑Metal Multiple Bonding: Quadruple Bonds and δ‑Orbital Contributions

Quadruple bonds between transition‑metal centres arise when four bonding interactions — one σ, two π, and one δ — are simultaneously occupied while the corresponding antibonding orbitals remain empty. The σ bond derives from head‑on overlap of dz² orbitals, the two π bonds from side‑on overlap of dxz and dyz, and the δ bond from face‑to‑face overlap of dxy orbitals. Because the δ overlap is intrinsically weaker, the δ bond is highly sensitive to ligand rotation; a 45° twist of one metal coordination plane relative to the other destroys the δ interaction and reduces the bond order to three. The archetypal quadruple‑bonded dimer is [Re₂Cl₈]²⁻, where each Re(III) is d⁴. The Re–Re distance of 2.24 Å is markedly shorter than a typical Re–Re single bond (~2.75 Å), confirming a bond order of four. The isoelectronic [Mo₂Cl₈]⁴⁻ (Mo(II), d⁴) shows a similar Mo–Mo distance of 2.14 Å. Chromium(II) acetate, Cr₂(O₂CCH₃)₄·2H₂O, also possesses a Cr–Cr quadruple bond (2.36 Å) despite the presence of bridging carboxylates that enforce a paddle‑wheel geometry. In these complexes the δ bond is populated by the two electrons occupying the dxy–dxy bonding combination, giving a formal bond order of four. More exotic quintuple bonds have been reported for sterically protected Cr–Cr and Mo–Mo complexes (e.g., Cr₂(μ‑Terphenyl)₂) where a δ* orbital remains empty and a fifth bonding interaction (often described as a φ bond involving f‑orbitals or a second δ) is invoked, but such species lie beyond the standard textbook scope.

Worked Example
Determine the skeletal electron pair count for B₅H₉ and predict its polyhedral geometry using Wade‑Mingos rules.
Each BH unit contributes 2 e⁻ (5 × 2 = 10 e⁻). The four bridging hydrogens each donate 1 e⁻ (4 e⁻). Total skeletal electrons = 14 e⁻ → 7 SEPs. For n = 5 vertices, a closo cluster requires n + 1 = 6 SEPs, nido requires n + 2 = 7 SEPs, arachno requires n + 3 = 8 SEPs. Since the count matches n + 2, B₅H₉ adopts a nido geometry derived from a closo octahedron by removing one vertex, i.e., a square‑pyramidal framework.

A quick check: if the resulting SEPs equal n + 1, the cluster is closo; if n + 2, nido; etc. This avoids re‑deriving the count under time pressure.

Cation‑π and Anion‑π Interactions in Supramolecular Chemistry

Cation‑π interactions arise when a positively charged species (alkali metal cation, ammonium, guanidinium) approaches the electron‑rich face of an aromatic ring. The attraction is a combination of electrostatics (the cation interacts with the quadrupole moment of the π‑system), induction (polarisation of the π‑cloud), and dispersion. Typical binding energies for Na⁺–benzene are 70–90 kJ mol⁻¹, while K⁺–benzene is slightly weaker (50–70 kJ mol⁻¹) because the larger ion sits farther from the ring plane. The geometry is highly directional: the cation prefers the centroid of the ring, with the M⁺–ring distance around 2.5–3.0 Å. In proteins, cation‑π contacts between Arg/Lys side chains and Phe/Tyr/Trp residues contribute significantly to tertiary structure stability and ligand recognition. Anion‑π interactions, by contrast, involve electron‑deficient aromatic systems — such as hexafluorobenzene, 1,3,5‑trifluorobenzene, or pyridinium rings — that possess a positive quadrupole moment. Anions (Cl⁻, F⁻, NO₃⁻) are attracted to the region above the ring where the low‑lying π* orbitals can accept electron density. Binding energies range from 30 to 80 kJ mol⁻¹, generally weaker than cation‑π but highly tunable through ring substitution. Both interaction types are exploited in crystal engineering to direct the assembly of ionic frameworks, in anion‑transport receptors where a calixarene or pyrrole‑based scaffold uses anion‑π contacts to bind halides, and in the design of synthetic ion channels that mimic natural selectivity filters.

Spin‑State Energetics and Spin‑Crossover in Transition‑Metal Complexes

For octahedral d⁴–d⁷ metal centres the competition between ligand‑field splitting (Δo) and electron‑pairing energy (P) determines whether a complex is high‑spin (maximum unpaired electrons) or low‑spin (minimum unpaired electrons). When Δo ≈ P the system can undergo a spin‑crossover (SCO) transition induced by temperature, pressure, or light. The classic example is [Fe(phen)₂(NCS)₂] (phen = 1,10‑phenanthroline), an Fe(II) d⁶ complex that is low‑spin (t₂g⁶, S = 0) below ~180 K and converts to high‑spin (t₂g⁴eg², S = 2) on heating. The transition is accompanied by a colour change (pale violet → colourless) and a volume expansion of ~3 % due to the longer Fe–N bonds in the high‑spin state. Hysteresis loops are often observed because the cooperative elastic interactions between neighbouring molecules create an energy barrier. Light‑induced excited spin‑state trapping (LIESST) allows the low‑spin state to be converted to the metastable high‑spin state at cryogenic temperatures by green‑light irradiation; the reverse (reverse‑LIESST) uses red light. The thermodynamics are described by ΔH and ΔS of the spin transition; typical ΔH values are 5–15 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS 50–100 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Accurate prediction of SCO behaviour requires DFT calculations with hybrid functionals (e.g., B3LYP*, TPSSh) and appropriate basis sets, because pure GGA functionals often over‑stabilise the high‑spin state. In the solid state, SCO materials are candidates for molecular switches, sensors, and data‑storage devices.

Quantum Chemical Bond Descriptors: Wiberg Index, NBO, and Delocalization Indices

Modern quantum chemistry provides numerical descriptors that go beyond the qualitative bond‑order concept. The Wiberg bond index (WBI), derived from Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) analysis, is computed as the sum of squares of off‑diagonal density‑matrix elements between two atoms in the orthogonalised atomic orbital basis. For a typical C–C single bond WBI ≈ 1.0, a C=C double bond ≈ 2.0, and a C≡C triple bond ≈ 3.0. In benzene the C–C WBI is ~1.5, reflecting delocalised π‑bonding. NBO second‑order perturbation theory quantifies donor‑acceptor interactions (e.g., lone‑pair → σ* hyperconjugation) by the stabilization energy E(2) = ΔE = qiF(i,j)²/(εj–εi), where qi is the donor occupancy, F(i,j) the Fock matrix element, and ε the orbital energies. Large E(2) values (> 20 kJ mol⁻¹) signal significant delocalisation. The delocalization index (DI) from Atoms‑in‑Molecules (AIM) topology integrates the exchange‑correlation density between two atomic basins: DI(A,B) = 2∫∫ ρxc(r₁,r₂) dr₁ dr₂. DI correlates closely with WBI but is basis‑set independent and directly linked to the electron‑pair distribution. For a C–C single bond DI ≈ 1.0, for a double bond ≈ 2.0, and for a metal–ligand bond in a highly covalent complex (e.g., Fe–CO in Fe(CO)₅) DI can reach 0.8–1.0, indicating substantial electron sharing. These descriptors enable quantitative comparison of bond strengths across isomeric structures, assessment of aromaticity (via multicenter indices), and evaluation of covalency in f‑element complexes where traditional oxidation‑state formalisms break down.

  • Overlooking the cooperative lattice effects in spin‑crossover solids; isolated‑molecule ΔH/ΔS values often differ from bulk measurements.

  • Neglecting basis‑set dependence of NBO E(2) values; small basis sets artificially inflate donor‑acceptor stabilisation energies.

Noncovalent Interactions in Crystal Engineering: π‑Stacking and C‑H···π Contacts

Crystal engineering relies on the predictable assembly of molecular building blocks through noncovalent forces. Among these, π‑stacking and C‑H···π contacts are ubiquitous in organic solids and dictate packing motifs, polymorphism, and mechanical properties. π‑Stacking arises from the interaction between aromatic π‑systems; the dominant contributions are dispersion, electrostatic quadrupole–quadrupole terms, and a modest charge‑transfer component. Parallel‑displaced and T‑shaped geometries are the two energy minima for benzene dimer, with interaction energies of roughly −10 to −12 kJ mol⁻¹. Substituents alter the quadrupole moment: electron‑withdrawing groups (e.g., –CF₃) make the ring more positive at the centre, favouring face‑to‑face stacking with electron‑rich partners, while electron‑donating groups (e.g., –OMe) invert the preference. In crystal structures, π‑stacked columns often propagate along a single axis, creating one‑dimensional charge‑transport pathways exploited in organic semiconductors.

C‑H···π contacts involve a C‑H bond acting as a weak proton donor toward the π‑cloud of an aromatic ring. Though individually weaker (≈ −2 to −5 kJ mol⁻¹), their high directionality and abundance make them decisive in steering molecular conformation and lattice energy. The optimal geometry places the H atom 2.7–3.0 Å from the ring centroid with a C‑H···centroid angle near 180°. In many pharmaceuticals, C‑H···π interactions lock bioactive conformations and influence solubility. Crystal‑structure databases (CSD) show that over 60 % of organic crystals contain at least one C‑H···π contact shorter than the sum of van der Waals radii. Cooperative effects are common: a network of C‑H···π and π‑stacking interactions can produce a cumulative stabilization exceeding 50 kJ mol⁻¹ per formula unit, rivaling a single hydrogen bond.

Design rules for crystal engineering exploit these interactions. Introducing planar heteroaromatics (pyridine, triazine) tunes quadrupole moments and adds nitrogen lone pairs for additional C‑H···N hydrogen bonds. Halogen substitution (Cl, Br) enhances polarizability, strengthening dispersion contributions to π‑stacking. However, over‑reliance on a single interaction type can lead to kinetic trapping of metastable polymorphs. Temperature‑dependent single‑crystal X‑ray diffraction reveals that π‑stacking distances contract on cooling, while C‑H···π angles become more linear, indicating a subtle balance between enthalpy and lattice entropy. Computational lattice‑energy minimisation (e.g., DFT‑D3, PIXEL) now routinely ranks polymorphs by quantifying each noncovalent term, guiding experimental screening.

Bonding in Main‑Group Clusters: Electron‑Deficient Multicenter Bonds

Main‑group clusters such as boranes, carboranes, and elemental Zintl phases exhibit bonding that cannot be described by conventional two‑center two‑electron (2c‑2e) bonds alone. The seminal Wade‑Mingos rules rationalise the stability of polyhedral boranes by counting skeletal electron pairs (SEP): a closo cluster with n vertices requires n + 1 SEP, nido requires n + 2, arachno n + 3, and hypho n + 4. Each vertex contributes one orbital to the skeletal framework; the total number of skeletal bonding molecular orbitals equals n + 1 for closo, leading to a fully filled set of bonding orbitals and a large HOMO–LUMO gap. In B₁₂H₁₂²⁻ (icosahedral closo), 13 SEP fill 13 bonding MOs, giving exceptional thermal stability.

Electron‑deficient multicenter bonds manifest as three‑center two‑electron (3c‑2e) B‑H‑B bridges in diborane (B₂H₆) and as delocalised bonding over the entire polyhedron in higher boranes. The 3c‑2e bond involves two boron sp³ hybrids and one hydrogen 1s orbital forming a banana‑shaped MO that holds two electrons. This motif repeats in larger clusters, generating a network of delocalised orbitals that distribute electron density evenly across the cage. Carboranes (C₂B₁₀H₁₂) replace two BH vertices with CH units; the carbon atoms contribute one extra electron each, preserving the SEP count and the closo geometry. The resulting C‑C bond in ortho‑carborane is unusually short (≈ 1.60 Å) due to strong σ‑delocalisation into the cage.

Zintl phases extend multicenter bonding to intermetallic compounds. In NaTl, the Tl⁻ sublattice forms a diamond‑like covalent network where each Tl is four‑coordinate, satisfying the 8‑N rule (N = 3 for group 13). The Na⁺ cations donate electrons to the Tl framework, creating a valence‑precise semiconductor. More complex Zintl clusters such as [Sn₉]⁴⁻ adopt a tricapped trigonal prism (n = 9, SEP = 10, nido) with 22 skeletal electrons. The bonding is described by spherical jellium models or by explicit molecular‑orbital calculations that reveal delocalised orbitals spanning the entire cluster. These clusters serve as building blocks for cluster‑assembled materials with tunable electronic properties.

Experimental verification comes from photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) of gas‑phase anions, which measures vertical detachment energies correlating with HOMO energies predicted by Wade‑Mingos counting. NMR chemical shifts of boron in boranes show characteristic upfield shifts for bridging hydrogens, reflecting the 3c‑2e bond’s reduced electron density at boron. X‑ray diffraction reveals equalised B‑B distances in closo cages, consistent with delocalised bonding. Misapplication of the 2c‑2e model leads to erroneous oxidation‑state assignments; for example, treating B₁₀H₁₀²⁻ as B(III)–H ignores the multicenter nature and overestimates formal charge.

Worked Example: Electron Counting in a Nido Carborane
Determine the skeletal electron pair count and predict the geometry of the nido‑C₂B₈H₁₀ cluster.
Each BH vertex contributes 2 skeletal electrons (one from B, one from H). Each CH vertex contributes 3 skeletal electrons (C provides 3 valence electrons to the cage, H provides 1, but one electron is used for the exo C‑H bond, leaving 3 for skeletal bonding). For C₂B₈H₁₀: 2 CH vertices × 3 e⁻ = 6 e⁻; 8 BH vertices × 2 e⁻ = 16 e⁻. Total skeletal electrons = 22 e⁻ = 11 SEP. The cluster has n = 10 vertices. For a nido cluster, required SEP = n + 2 = 12. The count is one pair short, indicating a mono‑anionic charge (C₂B₈H₁₀⁻) would give 24 e⁻ = 12 SEP, matching nido. The neutral C₂B₈H₁₀ is therefore a 10‑vertex arachno species (SEP = n + 3 = 13) if it existed; the observed stable anion is nido‑C₂B₈H₁₀⁻ with a geometry derived from an 11‑vertex closo polyhedron missing one vertex (a bicapped square antiprism).

When a cluster formula is given, first separate exo‑terminal ligands (H, halides) from skeletal atoms. Compare SEP to n + 1 (closo), n + 2 (nido), n + 3 (arachno). The difference directly tells you the charge needed for a given geometry. Practice with both neutral and anionic clusters to recognise the pattern quickly.

Computational Approaches to Bonding: DFT Functionals and Basis Set Effects

Density‑functional theory (DFT) is the workhorse for quantitative bonding analysis, but the choice of functional and basis set critically affects predicted bond lengths, dissociation energies, and charge distributions. Generalised gradient approximation (GGA) functionals such as PBE systematically over‑delocalise electron density, underestimating band gaps and over‑stabilising metallic states. Hybrid functionals (B3LYP, PBE0) incorporate a fraction of exact Hartree–Fock exchange (typically 20–25 %), improving thermochemistry and reducing self‑interaction error. For transition‑metal complexes, range‑separated hybrids (ωB97X‑D, CAM‑B3LYP) better describe charge‑transfer excitations and metal‑ligand covalency. Double‑hybrid functionals (B2PLYP, DSD‑PBEP86) add a perturbative MP2‑like correlation term, achieving near‑chemical accuracy for main‑group bond dissociation energies (mean absolute error ≈ 2 kJ mol⁻¹) at higher computational cost.

Basis‑set convergence follows a hierarchy: minimal (STO‑3G) → split‑valence (6‑31G) → polarized (6‑31G(d,p)) → diffuse‑augmented (6‑31+G(d,p)) → correlation‑consistent (cc‑pVXZ, X = D,T,Q,5). For accurate bond energies, at least a triple‑ζ quality with polarization (def2‑TZVP, cc‑pVTZ) is recommended; diffuse functions are essential for anions, hydrogen‑bonded complexes, and Rydberg states. Basis‑set superposition error (BSSE) artificially strengthens intermolecular interactions; the counterpoise correction (CP) is standard for noncovalent binding energies. However, CP can over‑correct for large basis sets; the geometrical counterpoise (gCP) and the more recent DFT‑D3(BJ) dispersion correction with three‑body terms (D3‑ABC) provide balanced treatment without explicit CP.

Relativistic effects become non‑negligible for elements beyond Kr. Scalar relativistic corrections (DKH2, ZORA) contract s and p orbitals and expand d and f orbitals, shortening bonds involving heavy atoms (e.g., Au–Au, Hg–Hg). Spin‑orbit coupling splits degenerate states and influences magnetic anisotropy in lanthanide and actinide complexes. For routine main‑group chemistry, scalar relativistic effective core potentials (ECPs) such as Stuttgart‑RSC or def2‑ECP replace core electrons and incorporate scalar relativistic effects efficiently.

Validation against experimental data is mandatory. Benchmark sets like GMTKN55 (general main‑group thermochemistry, kinetics, noncovalent interactions) and TMC151 (transition‑metal complexes) guide functional selection. For example, ωB97X‑D3 ranks top for noncovalent interactions, while PBE0‑D3 excels for transition‑metal spin‑state energetics. Reporting the functional, basis set, dispersion correction, and solvation model (if any) ensures reproducibility. Examiners often test awareness of functional‑dependent errors: a question may ask why B3LYP underestimates the barrier for a pericyclic reaction (missing dispersion) or why a GGA functional predicts a metallic ground state for a Mott insulator (self‑interaction error).

Excited‑State Bonding and Photochemistry: Conical Intersections and Bond Rearrangement

Photochemical reactivity is governed by the topology of potential‑energy surfaces (PES) near points where electronic states become degenerate. A conical intersection (CI) is a seam of degeneracy between two electronic states (typically S₁ and S₀) that provides a funnel for ultrafast non‑radiative decay. Unlike an avoided crossing, the degeneracy is lifted linearly in two directions (the gradient difference and derivative coupling vectors), forming a double‑cone topology. Passage through a CI can occur on femtosecond timescales, dictating product branching ratios in reactions such as cis‑trans isomerisation of retinal, electrocyclic ring opening of cyclohexadiene, and dissociation of ozone.

The location and geometry of a CI are determined by the interplay of σ and π orbitals. In ethylene, the S₁/S₀ CI corresponds to a twisted geometry (≈ 90°) where the π and π* orbitals become degenerate; the system can decay to either the ground‑state twisted geometry (leading to isomerisation) or return to planar ethylene. In larger chromophores, substituent effects shift the CI energy and alter the branching space vectors, enabling rational design of photo‑switches with high quantum yields. Computational location of CIs uses state‑averaged CASSCF or XMS‑CASPT2 methods; DFT‑based approaches (TD‑DFT) cannot correctly describe the degeneracy because of the single‑reference nature.

Bond rearrangement via excited states often involves biradical or zwitterionic character. The photodissociation of Cl₂ proceeds through a repulsive ¹Πu state correlating to two Cl(²P) atoms; the transition is allowed and yields a broad absorption band. In contrast, the Norrish type‑I cleavage of ketones involves n→π* excitation to a singlet state that undergoes intersystem crossing to a triplet biradical, followed by α‑cleavage. The triplet biradical’s lifetime (nanoseconds to microseconds) allows conformational relaxation before bond scission, influencing product distribution. Time‑resolved spectroscopy (femtosecond transient absorption, time‑resolved photoelectron spectroscopy) directly observes wavepacket motion toward CIs and subsequent branching.

Understanding CIs is essential for interpreting quantum yields, designing molecular machines, and predicting photostability of drugs and polymers. Examiners may ask to sketch the branching space vectors for a given CI or to explain why a particular substituent increases the quantum yield of a photo‑isomerisation (by lowering the CI energy relative to the Franck‑Condon point). A common trap is confusing a conical intersection with an avoided crossing; the former requires two independent nuclear coordinates to lift degeneracy, the latter only one.

Bonding in High‑Pressure Chemistry: Pressure‑Induced Polymerization and Metallization

Applying hydrostatic pressure (GPa to TPa range) dramatically alters chemical bonding by reducing interatomic distances and increasing orbital overlap. Molecular solids such as N₂, CO₂, H₂O, and CH₄ undergo sequential phase transitions: molecular → extended covalent networks → metallic states. In nitrogen, above 110 GPa the triple‑bonded N₂ molecules transform into a single‑bonded polymeric nitrogen (cg‑N) with a layered structure of N–N single bonds (≈ 1.45 Å). This polymeric phase is a high‑energy‑density material; its decomposition releases ≈ 30 kJ g⁻¹. The transition is driven by the enthalpy gain from forming more numerous, albeit weaker, bonds, overcoming the large kinetic barrier of breaking the N≡N triple bond (945 kJ mol⁻¹).

Carbon dioxide forms a silica‑like CO₂‑V phase above 40 GPa, where each carbon is tetrahedrally coordinated by four oxygens in a network of corner‑sharing CO₄ tetrahedra. The C–O bond length shortens from 1.16 Å (molecular) to ≈ 1.30 Å (network), reflecting a shift from double‑bond to single‑bond character. Further compression yields a metallic CO₂ phase with delocalised electrons. Water ice transforms from hydrogen‑bonded molecular ice VII to symmetric ice X (≈ 60 GPa) where protons sit midway between oxygens, eliminating the distinction between covalent O–H and hydrogen bonds. At terapascal pressures, ice becomes a superionic conductor with mobile protons in an oxygen lattice, and eventually a metallic fluid.

Hydrogen, the simplest element, is predicted to metallise near 400–500 GPa, transitioning from a molecular insulator (phase I) through several molecular phases (II, III, IV) to an atomic metallic phase (phase V). The metallisation involves band‑gap closure as the H₂ σ‑bonding and σ*‑antibonding bands broaden and overlap. Experimental claims of metallic hydrogen remain controversial; diamond‑anvil cell studies report reflective surfaces at ≈ 495 GPa, but reproducibility is debated. Theoretical work using quantum Monte Carlo and DFT‑based methods predicts a first‑order transition to a monatomic body‑centered cubic metal with a high superconducting critical temperature (Tc ≈ 200–300 K) due to strong electron‑phonon coupling.

Pressure also induces novel stoichiometries and bonding motifs absent at ambient conditions. For example, NaCl forms stable Na₃Cl, NaCl₃, and NaCl₇ compounds above 20 GPa, violating the 1:1 ratio dictated by ionic radii. These phases feature polyanionic Clₙ clusters (Cl₃⁻ linear, Cl₇⁻ pentagonal bipyramid) stabilised by pressure‑enhanced electron transfer from Na. Similarly, Xe–Fe compounds become stable in Earth’s core conditions, with xenon occupying interstitial sites in iron’s lattice, altering the core’s seismic properties. High‑pressure synthesis combined with in‑situ X‑ray diffraction and Raman spectroscopy provides direct evidence of bond‑length changes, coordination‑number increases, and electron‑density redistribution. Examiners often test the concept that pressure favours higher coordination numbers and lower bond orders, and may ask to predict the sequence of structural transitions for a given molecular solid based on its compressibility and bond‑energy landscape.
