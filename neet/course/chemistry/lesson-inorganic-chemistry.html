1. 1.1 Historical Development and Modern Form

The periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number so that elements with similar chemical properties fall in the same vertical group. Dmitri Mendeleev's 1869 table left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties with remarkable accuracy, but the modern form follows Henry Moseley's 1913 discovery that atomic number, not atomic mass, is the fundamental ordering principle. The long form of the table contains eighteen groups and seven periods, with the f-block elements placed separately at the bottom to keep the table's width manageable. Each period corresponds to the filling of a principal quantum shell, while each group reflects a common valence electron configuration that dictates chemical behaviour.

2. 1.2 Periodic Trends in Atomic and Ionic Radii

Atomic radius decreases across a period because the nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same principal shell, so the effective nuclear charge experienced by the outermost electrons rises steadily. Down a group, a new principal shell is added, which outweighs the increased nuclear charge and causes the radius to increase. Ionic radii follow the same trends, but cations are always smaller than their parent atoms because the loss of electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and often removes an entire shell, while anions are larger because added electrons increase repulsion without increasing nuclear charge. For isoelectronic species such as N3-, O2-, F-, Na+, Mg2+, and Al3+, the radius decreases as nuclear charge increases, giving the order N3- > O2- > F- > Na+ > Mg2+ > Al3+.

3. 1.3 Ionization Energy, Electron Affinity, and Electronegativity

First ionization energy generally increases across a period because the increasing effective nuclear charge holds electrons more tightly, and it decreases down a group because the outermost electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded. Exceptions occur at Group 13 (B, Al, Ga) where removal of a p-electron is easier than removal of an s-electron from the preceding Group 12 element, and at Group 16 (O, S, Se) where electron-electron repulsion in a paired p-orbital lowers the energy needed to remove one electron. Electron affinity becomes more exothermic across a period and less exothermic down a group, though the trend is less regular because of small orbital sizes and repulsion effects. Electronegativity, measured on the Pauling scale, follows the same periodic pattern as ionization energy; fluorine (3.98) is the most electronegative element and caesium (0.79) the least, and the difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms determines the ionic character of the bond.

4. 1.4 Metallic Character and Oxidation States

Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group, mirroring the trends in ionization energy and electronegativity. Elements on the left of the table lose electrons readily to form cations, while those on the right gain electrons to form anions. Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states because the (n-1)d and ns orbitals are close in energy, allowing different numbers of electrons to be lost or shared. The highest oxidation state of a transition metal usually equals its group number up to manganese (Group 7, +7 in MnO4-), after which the increasing nuclear charge makes higher oxidation states less stable. In the p-block, the inert pair effect makes the oxidation state two less than the group valence increasingly stable down Groups 13-16, so Tl(I) is more stable than Tl(III), Pb(II) more stable than Pb(IV), and Bi(III) more stable than Bi(V).

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

5. 2.1 Ionic Bonding and Lattice Energy

Ionic bonds form when the electronegativity difference between two atoms is large enough (typically >1.7 on the Pauling scale) that electron transfer creates oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction. The stability of an ionic compound is quantified by its lattice energy, the energy released when gaseous ions combine to form one mole of solid crystal. The Born-Landé equation shows that lattice energy is proportional to the product of the ionic charges and inversely proportional to the sum of the ionic radii, so compounds with small, highly charged ions such as MgO (Mg2+ and O2-) have very high lattice energies and high melting points. The Born-Haber cycle applies Hess's law to relate lattice energy to measurable quantities: enthalpy of formation, enthalpy of atomization, ionization energies, electron affinities, and enthalpy of sublimation, allowing lattice energies to be determined indirectly.

6. 2.2 Covalent Bonding and Lewis Structures

Covalent bonds arise from the sharing of electron pairs between atoms of similar electronegativity. A Lewis structure represents valence electrons as dots and shows how atoms achieve noble-gas configurations by sharing. The octet rule works well for second-period elements but fails for molecules with odd numbers of electrons (NO, NO2), electron-deficient species (BF3, BeCl2), and expanded octets in third-period and heavier elements (PF5, SF6, XeF4) where d-orbitals or, more accurately, hypervalent bonding involving three-centre four-electron bonds can accommodate extra electrons. Formal charge, calculated as valence electrons minus non-bonding electrons minus half the bonding electrons, helps select the most plausible Lewis structure; the structure with formal charges closest to zero and negative charges on the more electronegative atoms is preferred.

7. 2.3 VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model predicts molecular shapes by assuming that electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimize repulsion. The order of repulsion strength is lone pair-lone pair > lone pair-bond pair > bond pair-bond pair, so lone pairs occupy more space and compress bond angles. The steric number (number of sigma bonds plus lone pairs) determines the electron-pair geometry: 2 gives linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal bipyramidal, and 6 octahedral. The molecular geometry is the shape of the atoms only; for example, four electron pairs with one lone pair (steric number 4, one lone pair) give a tetrahedral electron-pair geometry but a trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry (NH3, bond angle 107°), while two lone pairs give a bent geometry (H2O, bond angle 104.5°). In trigonal bipyramidal arrangements, lone pairs always occupy equatorial positions to minimize 90° repulsions, leading to seesaw (SF4), T-shaped (ClF3), and linear (XeF2) molecular geometries.

8. 2.4 Hybridization and Orbital Overlap

Hybridization mixes atomic orbitals on the same atom to form equivalent hybrid orbitals that overlap more effectively with orbitals on neighbouring atoms. The number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed, and the hybridization scheme corresponds to the steric number from VSEPR: sp (linear, 180°), sp2 (trigonal planar, 120°), sp3 (tetrahedral, 109.5°), sp3d (trigonal bipyramidal), and sp3d2 (octahedral). Sigma bonds form by head-on overlap of hybrid orbitals or s-orbitals, while pi bonds form by sideways overlap of unhybridized p-orbitals. In ethene (C2H4), each carbon is sp2 hybridized; three sp2 orbitals form sigma bonds (two C-H and one C-C), and the remaining p-orbitals overlap to form a pi bond, giving a rigid planar structure. In ethyne (C2H2), sp hybridization leaves two p-orbitals for two pi bonds, producing a linear molecule with a short, strong triple bond.

9. 2.5 Molecular Orbital Theory for Homonuclear Diatomics

Molecular orbital (MO) theory treats electrons as delocalized over the entire molecule, forming bonding, antibonding, and non-bonding orbitals from linear combinations of atomic orbitals. For homonuclear diatomics of the second period, the ordering of MOs depends on the energy gap between 2s and 2p orbitals. For Li2 to N2, the order is σ2s, σ*2s, π2px=π2py, σ2pz, π*2px=π*2py, σ*2pz; for O2 and F2, the σ2pz drops below the π2p orbitals because of reduced s-p mixing. Bond order = ½ (bonding electrons - antibonding electrons) predicts stability and bond length: N2 (bond order 3) has a very short, strong triple bond; O2 (bond order 2) is paramagnetic with two unpaired electrons in degenerate π* orbitals, a result MO theory explains but Lewis structures cannot; F2 (bond order 1) has a weak single bond; Ne2 (bond order 0) does not exist.

Example 1
Predict the shape and bond angle of ClF3 using VSEPR theory, and state the hybridization of the central chlorine atom.
Solution: Chlorine in ClF3 has seven valence electrons. Three are used to form sigma bonds with three fluorine atoms, leaving four electrons (two lone pairs). The steric number is therefore 5 (three bond pairs + two lone pairs), giving a trigonal bipyramidal electron-pair geometry. To minimize repulsion, the two lone pairs occupy equatorial positions, resulting in a T-shaped molecular geometry. The bond angles are slightly less than 90° and 180° due to lone pair-bond pair repulsion being stronger than bond pair-bond pair repulsion. The hybridization corresponding to steric number 5 is sp3d. Thus ClF3 is T-shaped with sp3d hybridization.
Exam Insight
In NEET, questions on VSEPR often ask for the shape of ions such as ICl4- or XeF4. Remember that for steric number 6 with two lone pairs, the lone pairs occupy opposite axial positions in the octahedral electron-pair geometry, giving a square planar molecular geometry. The bond angles are exactly 90° because the lone pairs are trans to each other and their repulsions cancel in the equatorial plane.

Coordination Compounds

10. 3.1 Werner's Coordination Theory

Alfred Werner's 1893 coordination theory revolutionized the understanding of complex compounds by proposing that metal ions exhibit two types of valence: primary valence (oxidation state), satisfied by anions, and secondary valence (coordination number), satisfied by ligands attached directly to the metal ion in a fixed geometry. Werner's experiments with cobalt(III) chloride-ammonia complexes, such as CoCl3·6NH3 (which precipitates all three chlorides as AgCl), CoCl3·5NH3 (two chlorides precipitated), and CoCl3·4NH3 (one chloride precipitated), demonstrated that only the chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are ionizable. He also resolved optical isomers of [Co(en)3]3+ and geometric isomers of [Co(NH3)4Cl2]+, proving that coordination compounds have definite three-dimensional structures, not chain structures as previously believed.

11. 3.2 Nomenclature and Isomerism

IUPAC nomenclature for coordination compounds follows a systematic order: ligands are named alphabetically before the metal, with prefixes di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa- for simple ligands and bis-, tris-, tetrakis- for complex ligand names. Anionic ligands end in -o (chloro, cyano, hydroxo), neutral ligands keep their common names (aqua, ammine, carbonyl, nitrosyl), and cationic ligands end in -ium. The metal name takes its element name for cationic or neutral complexes and the -ate suffix for anionic complexes (ferrate, cuprate, cobaltate), with the oxidation state in Roman numerals in parentheses. Isomerism in coordination compounds includes structural isomerism (ionization, hydrate, linkage, coordination, and coordination position isomerism) and stereoisomerism (geometric and optical). Geometric isomerism arises in square planar (MA2B2 type) and octahedral (MA4B2, MA3B3) complexes; optical isomerism occurs in octahedral complexes lacking a plane of symmetry, such as [M(AA)3] and [M(AA)2B2] (cis form), where AA is a bidentate ligand like ethylenediamine.

12. 3.3 Crystal Field Theory and Spectrochemical Series

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) treats ligands as point negative charges that split the degenerate d-orbitals of the central metal ion. In an octahedral field, the five d-orbitals split into a lower-energy t2g set (dxy, dyz, dzx) and a higher-energy eg set (dz2, dx2-y2), with a splitting energy Δo. In a tetrahedral field, the splitting is inverted and smaller (Δt ≈ 4/9 Δo). The magnitude of Δo depends on the metal ion (increasing down a group and with higher oxidation state) and the ligand. The spectrochemical series orders ligands by field strength: I- < Br- < SCN- (S) < Cl- < NO3- < F- < OH- < C2O42- < H2O < NCS- (N) < NH3 < en < bipy < phen < NO2- < PPh3 < CN- < CO. Strong-field ligands cause large splitting and favour low-spin configurations; weak-field ligands cause small splitting and favour high-spin configurations.

13. 3.4 Magnetic Properties and Colour

The magnetic behaviour of a complex depends on the number of unpaired electrons. High-spin complexes follow Hund's rule, maximizing unpaired electrons, while low-spin complexes pair electrons in the lower t2g orbitals before occupying eg. For example, [Fe(CN)6]4- (d6, strong field) is low-spin t2g6eg0 with no unpaired electrons (diamagnetic), whereas [Fe(H2O)6]2+ (d6, weak field) is high-spin t2g4eg2 with four unpaired electrons (paramagnetic). The spin-only magnetic moment μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons, gives a good approximation for first-row transition metals. Colour arises from d-d transitions: absorption of visible light promotes an electron from t2g to eg, and the complementary colour is observed. The wavelength absorbed corresponds to Δo; thus [Ti(H2O)6]3+ (d1) absorbs at about 20,000 cm-1 (green-yellow) and appears purple. Charge-transfer transitions, where an electron moves from ligand to metal (LMCT) or metal to ligand (MLCT), are often more intense and account for the deep colours of MnO4- (purple) and [Fe(phen)3]2+ (red).

s-Block Elements

14. 4.1 Group 1: Alkali Metals

The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) have a single ns1 valence electron, which they lose readily to form M+ ions, giving them a uniform +1 oxidation state. Their low ionization energies and low electronegativities make them the most electropositive elements. Lithium shows anomalous behaviour due to its small size and high charge density: it forms a stable nitride (Li3N) directly with nitrogen, its carbonate and fluoride are sparingly soluble, and it forms covalent organolithium compounds. The standard electrode potentials (E° M+/M) become more negative down the group (Li -3.04 V, Na -2.71 V, K -2.93 V, Rb -2.98 V, Cs -3.03 V), but lithium's highly negative value is due to its large hydration enthalpy rather than a low ionization energy. All alkali metals react vigorously with water to give MOH and H2, with reactivity increasing down the group. They form ionic oxides (M2O), peroxides (M2O2), and superoxides (MO2); the larger cations stabilize the larger anions, so Li forms mainly the oxide, Na forms the peroxide, and K, Rb, Cs form superoxides.

15. 4.2 Group 2: Alkaline Earth Metals

The alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) have an ns2 configuration and predominantly show a +2 oxidation state. Their first ionization energies are higher than those of alkali metals, but the second ionization energy is compensated by the high lattice energies or hydration enthalpies of the M2+ compounds. Beryllium is anomalous: it forms covalent compounds, its oxide and hydroxide are amphoteric, and it does not react with water even at high temperatures. Magnesium reacts slowly with hot water, while Ca, Sr, Ba react readily with cold water. The solubility of hydroxides increases down the group (Be(OH)2 and Mg(OH)2 are sparingly soluble, Ba(OH)2 is quite soluble), while the solubility of sulfates and carbonates decreases down the group due to the faster decrease in hydration enthalpy compared to lattice energy. Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases down the group because larger cations stabilize the large anions better; BeCO3 is unstable, MgCO3 decomposes at 540°C, CaCO3 at 900°C, and BaCO3 at 1360°C.

16. 4.3 Anomalous Behaviour and Diagonal Relationship

Lithium and magnesium exhibit a diagonal relationship due to their similar charge densities (Li+ 76 pm, Mg2+ 72 pm) and electronegativities (Li 0.98, Mg 1.31). Both form nitrides on heating in nitrogen, both form covalent organometallic compounds, both have carbonates that decompose on heating, and both have hydroxides and fluorides that are sparingly soluble. Beryllium and aluminium show a similar diagonal relationship: both form amphoteric oxides and hydroxides, both form covalent halides that are Lewis acids, both form complex ions like [BeF4]2- and [AlF6]3-, and both are rendered passive by concentrated nitric acid due to a protective oxide layer. These relationships arise because moving down a group increases size while moving across a period decreases size, so the two effects partially cancel along the diagonal.

p-Block Elements

17. 5.1 Group 13: Boron Family

Group 13 elements (B, Al, Ga, In, Tl) have an ns2np1 configuration. Boron is a non-metal with a high ionization energy and forms only covalent compounds; it is electron-deficient and acts as a Lewis acid, forming adducts with Lewis bases (BF3·NH3) and complex anions like [BF4]-. Aluminium is a metal but forms a protective oxide layer (Al2O3) that makes it corrosion-resistant. The +3 oxidation state is common for B, Al, Ga, In, but the inert pair effect makes +1 increasingly stable down the group; Tl(I) is more stable than Tl(III), and Tl+ resembles alkali metal ions in size and chemistry. The trihalides are Lewis acids, with acid strength order BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3 (due to pπ-pπ back-bonding in BF3 reducing electron deficiency). Boron forms unusual hydrides (boranes) like B2H6 with three-centre two-electron bonds, and borax (Na2B4O7·10H2O) gives a bead test for metal ions.

18. 5.2 Group 14: Carbon Family

Group 14 (C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb) shows a transition from non-metal to metal. Carbon's unique ability to catenate (form C-C bonds) leads to the vast field of organic chemistry. The +4 oxidation state dominates for C and Si, but the inert pair effect makes +2 increasingly stable for Ge, Sn, and Pb; Pb(II) is more stable than Pb(IV), and PbO2 is a strong oxidizing agent. All elements form tetrahalides (MX4), but stability decreases down the group; PbI4 does not exist. The dioxides (CO2, SiO2, GeO2, SnO2, PbO2) show decreasing acidity: CO2 and SiO2 are acidic, GeO2 and SnO2 are amphoteric, PbO2 is weakly acidic. Silicates, based on the SiO44- tetrahedron, form the bulk of the Earth's crust; they polymerize by sharing corners to give chains, sheets, and three-dimensional frameworks (zeolites).

19. 5.3 Group 15: Nitrogen Family

Group 15 (N, P, As, Sb, Bi) has ns2np3 configuration. Nitrogen differs sharply from the others: it is a diatomic gas (N≡N, bond energy 945 kJ/mol), forms multiple bonds readily (pπ-pπ), and shows oxidation states from -3 to +5. Phosphorus exists as P4 tetrahedra (white P) and polymeric forms (red, black P). The hydrides (NH3, PH3, AsH3, SbH3, BiH3) show decreasing thermal stability and basicity down the group; NH3 is a strong ligand and hydrogen-bonds, while PH3 is a weak ligand and does not hydrogen-bond. Oxides include N2O, NO, N2O3, NO2, N2O5 for nitrogen and P4O6, P4O10 for phosphorus. The oxoacids of nitrogen (HNO2, HNO3) and phosphorus (H3PO2, H3PO3, H3PO4) show varying oxidation states and reducing/oxidizing properties; H3PO2 (hypophosphorous acid) is a strong reducing agent due to two P-H bonds.

20. 5.4 Group 16: Oxygen Family

Group 16 (O, S, Se, Te, Po) has ns2np4. Oxygen is a diatomic gas with strong pπ-pπ bonding; it shows -2 oxidation state (except in OF2 and peroxides) and does not expand its octet. Sulphur forms S8 rings and catenates extensively (S-S bonds up to 20 atoms). The hydrides H2O, H2S, H2Se, H2Te show decreasing bond angles (104.5°, 92°, 91°, 90°) and increasing acidity. Oxides: SO2 (bent, reducing agent), SO3 (trigonal planar, anhydride of H2SO4). Sulphuric acid is manufactured by the Contact process (V2O5 catalyst, 450°C, 2 atm). The oxoacids of sulphur include H2SO3, H2SO4, H2S2O3 (thiosulphuric), H2S2O7 (disulphuric), and peroxoacids like H2SO5 (peroxymonosulphuric, Caro's acid).

21. 5.5 Group 17: Halogens

Group 17 (F, Cl, Br, I, At) are the most electronegative elements, needing one electron to complete an octet.
