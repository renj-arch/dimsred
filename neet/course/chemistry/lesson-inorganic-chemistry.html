1. Periodic Classification and Properties

Modern Periodic Table

Blocks, Groups, Periods

The modern periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number. Elements are classified into four blocks s, p, d, f based on the subshell that receives the last electron. There are 18 groups and 7 periods. Group 1 to 2 constitute the s-block, Groups 13 to 18 the p-block, Groups 3 to 12 the d-block, and the two rows at the bottom are the f-block (lanthanoids and actinoids). Period 1 has 2 elements, Periods 2 and 3 have 8 each, Periods 4 and 5 have 18 each, Period 6 has 32, and Period 7 is incomplete.

Mendeleev vs Modern Periodic Table

Mendeleev arranged 63 known elements in order of increasing atomic mass, leaving gaps for undiscovered elements (e.g., eka-aluminium = Ga, eka-silicon = Ge). He predicted their properties with remarkable accuracy. The modern table is based on atomic number (Mosley's law) which removed anomalies like the inverted pairs (Ar-K, Co-Ni, Te-I) where atomic mass order conflicted with chemical properties.

Periodic Trends

Atomic Radius

Atomic radius decreases across a period due to increased effective nuclear charge (Zeff) pulling electrons closer. It increases down a group due to addition of new shells. Covalent radius is half the distance between two bonded atoms; metallic radius is half the distance between adjacent nuclei in a metal lattice; van der Waals radius is for non-bonded atoms (largest of the three).

Ionization Enthalpy

Ionization enthalpy (IE) is the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom. IE increases across a period and decreases down a group. Exceptions: Group 2 elements have higher IE than Group 13 due to stable s2 configuration; Group 15 has higher IE than Group 16 due to half-filled p3 stability. Successive ionization enthalpies increase sharply: IE1 less than IE2 less than IE3 because removal of an electron from a positive ion requires more energy.

Trick: IE exceptions
Group 2 greater than Group 13 and Group 15 greater than Group 16 due to extra stability of half-filled or full-filled subshells. Example: Mg (738) greater than Al (578); P (1012) greater than S (1000). Mg has 3s2 (filled), Al has 3s2 3p1; P has 3p3 (half-filled), S has 3p4.

Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom to attract shared electrons. It increases across a period and decreases down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (4.0 on Pauling scale). Electronegativity values help predict bond polarity: the greater the difference, the more polar the bond. For NEET, remember: EN of F = 4.0, O = 3.5, Cl = 3.0, N = 3.0, C = 2.5, H = 2.1.

Electron Affinity

Electron affinity (EA) is the energy released when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom. Halogens have the highest electron affinity (Cl greater than F greater than Br greater than I). Chlorine has a higher EA than fluorine because fluorine's small size causes electron-electron repulsion in its 2p subshell. For NEET, remember: EA of O is -141 kJ/mol less than S (-200 kJ/mol), and EA of N is about zero (stable half-filled p3 configuration). Nitrogen does not accept an electron readily.

Key Fact: EA trends
EA values (kJ/mol): Cl = -349, F = -328, Br = -325, I = -295, O = -141, S = -200, N = +7 (endothermic). Chlorine has the highest magnitude EA. Noble gases and alkaline earth metals have positive EA (energy required to add electron).

s, p, d, f Block Characteristics

s-block: Groups 1 and 2. ns1-2 configuration. Highly reactive metals, low ionization energy, form ionic compounds (except Li, Be). Show fixed oxidation states: +1 (Group 1), +2 (Group 2). Hydration enthalpy decreases down the group.

p-block: Groups 13 to 18. ns2 np1-6 configuration. Includes metals, metalloids, and non-metals. Show variable oxidation states. Inert-pair effect prominent in heavier elements. Many form covalent compounds.

d-block: Groups 3 to 12. (n-1)d1-10 ns1-2. Transition metals with variable oxidation states, coloured ions, paramagnetism, catalytic activity. All are metals, hard, high melting points.

f-block: Lanthanoids (4f1-14) and Actinoids (5f1-14). Lanthanoids show +3 common oxidation state, lanthanoid contraction. Actinoids are radioactive, show multiple oxidation states.

Periodic Trends Exceptions

1. IE of Group 2 > Group 13: Filled ns2 is more stable than ns2 np1. Example: Mg (738) > Al (578).

2. IE of Group 15 > Group 16: Half-filled np3 is more stable than np4. Example: P (1012) > S (1000).

3. EA of Cl > F: Small size of F causes electron-electron repulsion in compact 2p orbitals.

4. Atomic radius of Ga < Al: Due to presence of 10 d-electrons in Ga (poor shielding), effective nuclear charge increases, contracting the atomic radius (d-block contraction or scandide contraction).

5. Melting point of Group 2 > Group 1: Two valence electrons contribute to stronger metallic bonding in alkaline earth metals compared to alkali metals.

Example 1
Arrange the following in order of increasing ionization energy: Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar.
Solution: Across period 3, IE increases generally: Na less than Al less than Mg less than Si less than S less than P less than Cl less than Ar. Exceptions: Mg (3s2) greater than Al (3s2 3p1) and P (3p3) greater than S (3p4). Na has the lowest IE (496) because it has only one valence electron easily removed.
Example 2
Which element has the highest electron affinity in the periodic table? a) F b) Cl c) Br d) I
Solution: Chlorine (b). Although fluorine is the most electronegative, its small size causes inter-electronic repulsion, reducing its electron affinity. Chlorine has the highest EA (-349 kJ per mol). In fact, Cl has a larger atomic radius allowing incoming electron to experience less repulsion.
Example 3
Why does gallium have a smaller atomic radius than aluminium despite being below it in the same group?
Solution: This is due to the presence of 10 d-electrons (3d10) in Ga which do not shield the nuclear charge effectively (poor shielding effect of d-orbitals). This increases the effective nuclear charge, contracting the atomic radius. This phenomenon is called d-block contraction.

Diagonal Relationship

The diagonal relationship arises because the charge-to-size ratio (ionic potential) of the first element of a period is similar to that of the second element of the next group in the next period. This is due to the combined effect of increasing charge (moving right) and increasing size (moving down) which roughly balance.

PairSimilarities
Li (Group 1) - Mg (Group 2)Both form mononitrides (Li3N, Mg3N2); carbonates decompose on heating; hydroxides are weak bases; chlorides are deliquescent
Be (Group 2) - Al (Group 3)Both form amphoteric oxides/hydroxides; chlorides are covalent (BeCl2, AlCl3); carbides give methane on hydrolysis; both are relatively inert to acids
B (Group 13) - Si (Group 14)Both are metalloids; form covalent hydrides (boranes, silanes); oxides are acidic (B2O3, SiO2); halides hydrolyse readily
Key Mnemonic: Diagonal Relationship
Li similar to Mg, Be similar to Al, B similar to Si. Use the chant: Li-Mg, Be-Al, B-Si. Remember it as "LiMg BeAl BoSi". This occurs because the charge or size ratio of the second-period element resembles the third-period element diagonally below it.
Example 4
Which property of Li is similar to Mg? 1. Formation of nitride 2. Nature of hydroxide 3. Solubility of carbonate 4. All of these
Solution: All of these (d). Li forms Li3N like Mg forms Mg3N2 (other alkali metals do not form nitrides). LiOH is a weak base like Mg(OH)2 (other alkali hydroxides are strong bases). Li2CO3 decomposes on heating like MgCO3 (other alkali carbonates are thermally stable).

2. Hydrogen

Position in Periodic Table

Hydrogen resembles both alkali metals (Group 1) and halogens (Group 17). It has one electron like alkali metals (forms H+) but also gains an electron to form H- like halogens. Its unique behaviour arises from its very small size and high ionization energy (1312 kJ per mol). Hydrogen is placed separately in the periodic table because it does not fit perfectly into any group.

Isotopes of Hydrogen

Hydrogen has three isotopes: Protium (1H1, 99.985%), Deuterium (2H1 or D, 0.015%), and Tritium (3H1 or T, radioactive, half-life 12.3 years). Protium and deuterium are stable; tritium is obtained by bombarding Li with neutrons: 6Li + n goes to 4He + 3H. The isotope effect is most pronounced for protium vs deuterium due to 100% mass difference.

Example 5
Which of the following hydrides is basic? a) NH3 b) CH4 c) H2O d) HF
Solution: NH3 (a) accepts a proton to form NH4+, making it a Bronsted base. CH4 is neutral, H2O is amphoteric, HF is acidic (donates H+).
Example 6
Which hydride reacts with water to liberate hydrogen gas? a) CH4 b) NH3 c) NaH d) H2S
Solution: NaH (c) is an ionic hydride. NaH + H2O goes to NaOH + H2. CH4, NH3, and H2S do not react with water to produce H2.

Water: Hard and Soft

Hard water contains Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions (also Fe3+, Al3+) which form scum with soap. Hardness is of two types: Temporary hardness (due to bicarbonates of Ca and Mg) removed by boiling: Ca(HCO3)2 goes to CaCO3 + H2O + CO2. Permanent hardness (due to chlorides and sulphates of Ca and Mg) removed by adding washing soda (Na2CO3), ion exchange resins, or by Calgon process (NaPO3)6.

Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2)

Hydrogen peroxide has a non-planar, open-book structure with a dihedral angle of 111.5 degrees (gas phase) and 90.2 degrees (solid). It acts as both an oxidising and reducing agent. Important reactions:

2 H2O2 goes to 2 H2O + O2 (decomposition, catalysed by MnO2).
H2O2 + 2 I- + 2 H+ goes to I2 + 2 H2O (oxidation of iodide).
2 MnO4- + 5 H2O2 + 6 H+ goes to 2 Mn2+ + 8 H2O + 5 O2 (reduction in acidic medium).
PbS + 4 H2O2 goes to PbSO4 + 4 H2O (restoration of old lead paintings).

Uses: Bleaching agent (textiles, hair), antiseptic, rocket fuel, and as a source of oxygen.

Heavy Water (D2O)

Heavy water is deuterium oxide. It is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors to slow down neutrons. Its physical constants differ from ordinary water: density (1.105 g per mL), melting point (3.82 deg C), boiling point (101.42 deg C). It is obtained by exhaustive electrolysis of ordinary water or by fractional distillation.

Hydrogen Economy and Dihydrogen as Fuel

Hydrogen economy refers to using hydrogen as a clean fuel for transportation, heating, and power generation. Hydrogen burns to produce only water (no CO2). Dihydrogen (H2) has a high calorific value (150 kJ per g) compared to petrol (48 kJ per g). Challenges: storage (requires high pressure or liquefaction at -253 deg C), production (electrolysis is expensive), and transportation. Fuel cells convert H2 and O2 directly into electricity with water as a byproduct.

NEET Fact: Hydrogen fuel
H2 has the highest calorific value per gram among all fuels. It is produced by steam reforming: CH4 + H2O ▶ CO + 3 H2, followed by water-gas shift: CO + H2O ▶ CO2 + H2. It is also produced by electrolysis of water and as a byproduct in the chlor-alkali industry.

3. s-Block Elements (Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals)

General Characteristics

Alkali metals (Group 1): Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr. They are soft, highly reactive metals with low ionization energy, low melting points, and strong reducing power. Reactivity increases down the group. They form ionic hydrides (M+ H-), oxides (M2O, M2O2, MO2 depending on metal), and hydroxides (MOH as strong bases). Li is the hardest and has the highest melting point among alkali metals.

Alkaline earth metals (Group 2): Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra. They are harder, have higher melting points, and are less reactive than alkali metals. Be and Mg form covalent compounds due to high polarising power. Ca, Sr, Ba form ionic compounds. Reactivity increases down the group.

Anomalous Behaviour of Li and Be

Lithium shows anomalous behaviour: it is harder, has higher melting point, forms Li2O (not Li2O2), and LiOH is a weak base. Lithium alone forms Li3N (with N2) while other alkali metals do not. Li has a high polarising power (small size + single charge) which gives it covalent character. LiCl is deliquescent and soluble in organic solvents like pyridine.

Beryllium is also anomalous: it forms covalent compounds (BeCl2 is a polymer in solid), its oxide and hydroxide are amphoteric, and it does not liberate H2 from acids readily. Be forms [BeF4]2- complex (like Al forms [AlF6]3-). BeO has a high melting point and is used in ceramics.

Mnemonic: Li anomalous
Li is HARDer, forms Li3N (alone), Li2O (not peroxide), LiOH is weak base. Remember: Li is a little different because it's small like Mg (diagonal relationship).

Important Compounds of s-Block

Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)

Manufactured by electrolysis of brine (chlor-alkali process): 2 NaCl + 2 H2O goes to 2 NaOH + Cl2 + H2. Uses: soap, paper, textile, petroleum refining, and as a laboratory reagent.

Sodium Bicarbonate (NaHCO3)

Manufactured by Solvay process: NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O goes to NaHCO3 + NH4Cl. NaHCO3 is sparingly soluble and precipitates out. It is then calcined to give Na2CO3: 2 NaHCO3 goes to Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2. Uses: baking soda, antacid, fire extinguisher.

Sodium Carbonate (Na2CO3)

Manufactured by Solvay process (ammonia soda process). The overall reaction: 2 NaCl + CaCO3 goes to Na2CO3 + CaCl2. Steps: (1) NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O goes to NaHCO3 + NH4Cl. (2) 2 NaHCO3 goes to Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2. (3) CaCO3 goes to CaO + CO2. (4) CaO + 2 NH4Cl goes to CaCl2 + 2 NH3 + H2O (ammonia is recycled). Uses: glass, soap, paper, water softening.

Calcium Oxide (CaO) and Calcium Hydroxide (Ca(OH)2)

CaO (quicklime) is obtained by calcination of limestone: CaCO3 goes to CaO + CO2. CaO reacts with water to form Ca(OH)2 (slaked lime) with hissing sound. Uses: whitewashing, cement manufacturing, flue gas desulfurization. Plaster of Paris (CaSO4.1/2 H2O) is obtained by heating gypsum (CaSO4.2H2O) at 373 K.

Biological Importance

Sodium (Na): Main extracellular cation. Regulates fluid balance, nerve impulse transmission. Na+/K+ ATPase pump maintains gradient.

Potassium (K): Main intracellular cation. Involved in muscle contraction, nerve function, heart rhythm.

Calcium (Ca): Major component of bones and teeth (as hydroxyapatite Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2). Essential for blood clotting, muscle contraction, nerve transmission.

Magnesium (Mg): Central atom in chlorophyll molecule. Cofactor for many enzymes, ATP metabolism. Maintains heart rhythm.

Example 7
Which of the following alkali metals forms a superoxide? a) Li b) Na c) K d) All of these
Solution: K, Rb, Cs form superoxides (MO2). Li forms Li2O (normal oxide), Na forms Na2O2 (peroxide). The stability of superoxides increases down the group due to the larger size of the cation stabilizing the large O2- anion. Hence option (c).
Example 8
Be shows diagonal relationship with which element? a) Al b) Mg c) Li d) B
Solution: Be shows diagonal relationship with Al (a). Both form amphoteric oxides and hydroxides, form covalent chlorides (BeCl2, AlCl3), and their carbides give methane on hydrolysis.
Example 9
Sodium metal is stored under which liquid? a) Water b) Alcohol c) Kerosene d) Benzene
Solution: Sodium is stored under kerosene (c) to prevent contact with moisture and oxygen. It reacts violently with water and alcohol.
Example 10
The correct order of hydration enthalpies of alkali metal ions is: a) Li+ > Na+ > K+ > Rb+ > Cs+ b) Cs+ > Rb+ > K+ > Na+ > Li+ c) Na+ > Li+ > K+ > Rb+ > Cs+ d) Li+ > Cs+ > K+ > Na+ > Rb+
Solution: a. Hydration enthalpy decreases with increasing ionic size. Li+ is the smallest (76 pm) and has the highest hydration enthalpy. Cs+ is the largest (167 pm) and has the lowest hydration enthalpy.

4. p-Block Elements

Group 13 (Boron Family)

B, Al, Ga, In, Tl. Boron is a metalloid; others are metals. The inert-pair effect (reluctance of s-electrons to participate in bonding) becomes prominent down the group: Tl shows +1 oxidation state more stable than +3. Boron forms electron-deficient compounds like BF3 (trigonal planar, sp2 hybridized) and BCl3. BF3 acts as a Lewis acid (accepts electron pairs).

Borax (Na2B4O7.10H2O)

Borax is the most important boron compound. On heating, it loses water of crystallization and swells to form a porous mass (borax bead). The borax bead test is used to identify coloured metal ions in qualitative analysis: Na2B4O7 goes to 2 NaBO2 + B2O3; B2O3 + CuO goes to Cu(BO2)2 (blue coloured bead).

Diborane (B2H6)

Diborane has a unique structure with hydrogen-bridge bonding (3-centre-2-electron bonds). Each B atom uses sp3 hybrid orbitals: two form normal B-H bonds with terminal H, and two form bridge bonds with H atoms sharing electrons between two boron atoms. The terminal B-H bond length is 119 pm, while the bridge B-H-B bond length is 131 pm. Diborane burns with a green flame: B2H6 + 3 O2 goes to B2O3 + 3 H2O.

Trick: Electron deficiency of B
Boron has only 3 valence electrons but needs 8 to complete octet. So B compounds like BF3 are electron-deficient (Lewis acids). They accept lone pairs to form adducts: BF3 + NH3 goes to H3N-BF3. AlCl3 also behaves similarly (dimerizes to Al2Cl6).

Group 14 (Carbon Family)

C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb. Carbon exhibits catenation (self-linking) due to strong C-C bonds. Silicon and germanium are semiconductors. Inert-pair effect: Pb(II) is stable, Pb(IV) is strongly oxidising.

Allotropes of Carbon

Diamond: sp3 hybridized, tetrahedral 3D network, hardest natural substance, insulator (no free electrons), high thermal conductivity. Each C is bonded to 4 other C atoms.

Graphite: sp2 hybridized, layered structure with hexagonal rings. Each C is bonded to 3 others (trigonal planar), leaving one free electron per C which moves freely (good conductor of electricity). Layers held by weak van der Waals forces (lubricant).

Fullerenes: C60 (buckminsterfullerene, soccer-ball shape with 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons), C70, etc. Discovered in 1985. Each C is sp2 hybridized. Used in nanotechnology, lubricants, and medicine.

Silicones and Silicates

Silicones: Synthetic organosilicon polymers with Si-O-Si backbone and organic groups (methyl, phenyl) attached to Si. General formula (R2SiO)n. Used as sealants, lubricants, water-repellent coatings, and in medical implants due to their thermal stability and inertness.

Silicates: Minerals containing SiO4 tetrahedra as basic structural units. Types: orthosilicates (isolated SiO4 4-), pyrosilicates (Si2O7 6-), cyclic, chain (pyroxenes), double chain (amphiboles), sheet (micas, clays), and 3D network (quartz, feldspar). Zeolites are aluminosilicates used as molecular sieves and catalysts.

Example 11
Which of the following allotropes of carbon is a good conductor of electricity?
Solution: Graphite. Each C in graphite has one free (delocalized) electron that moves freely between the layers, making graphite a good conductor of electricity. Diamond does not have free electrons. Fullerene is a semiconductor.

Group 15 (Nitrogen Family)

N, P, As, Sb, Bi. Nitrogen is a diatomic gas (N triple bond N, very stable with bond energy 945 kJ per mol). Phosphorus exists as P4 (white), red phosphorus, and black phosphorus.

Ammonia (NH3) - Haber Process

Manufacture: N2 + 3 H2 goes to 2 NH3 (delta H = -92.4 kJ per mol). Optimum conditions: 400-500 deg C, 150-200 atm, Fe catalyst with Mo promoter. NH3 is a Lewis base with trigonal pyramidal shape (lone pair on N). It forms NH4+ salts with acids. Used in fertilizers, HNO3 manufacture, explosives, refrigerants.

Nitric Acid (HNO3) - Ostwald Process

Manufacture: 4 NH3 + 5 O2 goes to 4 NO + 6 H2O (Pt gauge catalyst, 800 deg C). 2 NO + O2 goes to 2 NO2. 4 NO2 + 2 H2O + O2 goes to 4 HNO3. HNO3 is a strong oxidising agent (oxidizes I2 to HIO3, S to H2SO4). Fuming nitric acid is HNO3 with dissolved NO2.

Phosphine (PH3) and Allotropes of Phosphorus

PH3: Prepared by hydrolysis of Ca3P2: Ca3P2 + 6 H2O goes to 3 Ca(OH)2 + 2 PH3. PH3 is less basic than NH3 due to larger size and lower electron density on P. It is highly poisonous and spontaneously flammable in air (traces of P2H4 cause spontaneous combustion).

Allotropes of P: White P (P4 tetrahedral, highly reactive, glows in dark - chemiluminescence), Red P (polymeric, more stable, used in matchboxes), Black P (most stable, orthorhombic, conductor).

Mnemonic: Haber process conditions
For NH3 manufacture: 400-500 deg C, 150-200 atm, Fe catalyst. Remember as 4-5-1-5-2-0, Fe. High pressure favours product (less moles). Moderate temperature balances rate and yield (exothermic reaction).

Group 16 (Oxygen Family)

O, S, Se, Te, Po. Ozone (O3) is an allotrope of oxygen, bent shape, acts as a bleaching agent. Sulphur exists as S8 (crown structure).

Ozone Layer

Ozone in the stratosphere (10-50 km) absorbs harmful UV radiation (200-315 nm). The ozone layer is depleted by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs): CCl2F2 + hv goes to Cl + CClF2; Cl + O3 goes to ClO + O2; ClO + O goes to Cl + O2 (chain reaction, each Cl atom destroys up to 10^5 O3 molecules). Montreal Protocol (1987) banned CFCs.

H2SO4 - Contact Process

Manufacture of sulphuric acid (Oil of Vitriol):

Step 1: S + O2 goes to SO2 (or 4 FeS2 + 11 O2 goes to 2 Fe2O3 + 8 SO2).
Step 2: 2 SO2 + O2 goes to 2 SO3 (V2O5 catalyst, 400-450 deg C, 1-2 atm).
Step 3: SO3 + H2SO4 goes to H2S2O7 (oleum). H2S2O7 + H2O goes to 2 H2SO4.

Uses: Fertilizers (superphosphate, ammonium sulphate), explosives, detergents, pigments, petroleum refining. H2SO4 is a strong dehydrating agent (charring of sugar: C12H22O11 goes to 12 C + 11 H2O).

H2O2 Structure and Properties

H2O2 has a non-planar structure with a dihedral angle of 111.5 degrees (gas phase). It acts as both an oxidising agent (in acidic medium: H2O2 + 2 H+ + 2 e- goes to 2 H2O) and a reducing agent (in acidic medium: H2O2 goes to O2 + 2 H+ + 2 e-). Storage: kept in wax-lined bottles with stabilizers like acetanilide or phosphoric acid to slow decomposition.

Lanthanoids and Actinoids

Lanthanoid Contraction

Lanthanoids (4f series): Ce (58) to Lu (71). They exhibit lanthanoid contraction which is a steady decrease in ionic radius with increasing atomic number due to poor shielding of 4f electrons by the diffuse 4f orbitals. As the nuclear charge increases, the 4f electrons do not shield effectively, so the effective nuclear charge on outer electrons increases, contracting the ionic radius. Consequence: similar chemical properties make separation of lanthanoids very difficult (requires ion exchange chromatography).

6. Coordination Compounds

Werner Theory

Alfred Werner proposed that metal ions have two types of valencies: primary (ionizable) and secondary (non-ionizable). Primary valency corresponds to oxidation state; secondary valency corresponds to coordination number. For CoCl3.6NH3 (Co(NH3)6 Cl3), Co has primary valency 3 and secondary valency 6. Modern theory uses the concept of coordinate covalent bonds between the metal (Lewis acid) and ligands (Lewis bases). Coordination compounds help understand bonding and structure of complexes.

Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

Structural isomerism:

1. Ionization isomerism: (Co(NH3)5Br)SO4 (red-violet) vs (Co(NH3)5SO4)Br (red). Different ions in solution.

2. Linkage isomerism: Ambidentate ligands. NO2- can bind through N (nitro, -NO2) or O (nitrito, -ONO). SCN- can bind through S (thiocyanato) or N (isothiocyanato).

3. Coordination isomerism: (Cr(NH3)6)(Co(CN)6) vs (Co(NH3)6)(Cr(CN)6). Exchange of ligands between cation and anion.

4. Hydrate isomerism: Cr(H2O)6Cl3 (violet), Cr(H2O)5Cl Cl2.H2O (grey-green), Cr(H2O)4Cl2 Cl.2H2O (dark green).

Stereoisomerism:

1. Geometrical isomerism: In square planar complexes: Pt(NH3)2Cl2 has cis (cisplatin, anticancer) and trans (transplatin, inactive) isomers. In octahedral complexes: (Co(NH3)4Cl2)+ has cis (violet) and trans (green) isomers; (Co(en)2Cl2)+ has cis (chiral) and trans (achiral).

2. Optical isomerism: Non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers). Example: (Co(en)3)3+, (Cr(ox)3)3-, cis-(Co(en)2Cl2)+. Optical isomers rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions.

Valence Bond Theory (VBT) and Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

VBT: Metal ion uses hybrid orbitals to accept lone pairs from ligands. Inner orbital (low spin) complexes use (n-1)d orbitals for hybridization (e.g., d2sp3). Outer orbital (high spin) complexes use nd orbitals (e.g., sp3d2). Example: (CoF6)3- (high spin, paramagnetic, sp3d2, 4 unpaired e-) vs (Co(NH3)6)3+ (low spin, diamagnetic, d2sp3, 0 unpaired e-).

CFT: In octahedral field, d-orbitals split into t2g (dxy, dyz, dzx) which is lower energy, and eg (dx2-y2, dz2) which is higher energy. The splitting parameter (delta o) depends on ligand field strength.

High Spin vs Low Spin

Weak field ligands (I-, Br-, Cl-, F-) cause small delta o, favouring high spin complexes (electrons occupy all five d-orbitals singly before pairing). Strong field ligands (CO, CN-, NO2-, en, NH3) cause large delta o, favouring low spin complexes (electrons pair in t2g orbitals first).

Stability and Applications

Stability of coordination complexes depends on: 1) Charge on metal ion (higher charge gives more stable), 2) Nature of ligand (chelating ligands form more stable complexes due to chelate effect), 3) Size of the metal ion (smaller size gives higher charge density, more stable). The chelate effect: multidentate ligands (EDTA, en) form more stable complexes than monodentate ligands due to favourable entropy change (more free molecules released).

Applications: EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) is a hexadentate ligand used in chelation therapy for heavy metal poisoning (Pb, Hg, Cd). Cis-platin (Pt(NH3)2Cl2) is an anticancer drug that crosslinks DNA. Coordination complexes are used in electroplating (Ag(CN)2-), water softening (EDTA sequesters Ca2+ and Mg2+), photography (AgBr-thiosulfate complex), and as catalysts (Wilkinson's catalyst RhCl(PPh3)3 for hydrogenation).

NEET Fact: EDTA
EDTA is hexadentate (6 donor atoms: 4 O from COOH groups, 2 N from amino groups). It forms stable complexes with Ca2+, Mg2+, Pb2+, Hg2+. Used in treatment of heavy metal poisoning. The chelate effect makes EDTA complexes highly stable.

7. Metallurgy

Principles of Metallurgy

Metallurgy is the process of extracting metals from their ores. It involves three main stages:

1. Concentration of ore: Removal of gangue from ore. Methods include hydraulic washing (gravity separation for heavy ores), magnetic separation (for Fe, Mn ores), froth flotation (for sulphide ores using pine oil as frother), and leaching (chemical method: cyanide process for Au/Ag, Bayer process for Al, acid leaching for Cu).

2. Extraction of metal: Reduction of the concentrated ore to the free metal. Methods: Calcination (heating in limited/absence of air, e.g., ZnCO3 goes to ZnO + CO2), Roasting (heating in excess air, e.g., 2 ZnS + 3 O2 goes to 2 ZnO + 2 SO2), Smelting (reduction using carbon in a blast furnace, e.g., Fe2O3 + 3 C goes to 2 Fe + 3 CO), and electrolytic reduction (Hall-Heroult for Al, Down process for Na).

3. Purification: Distillation (for Zn, Hg), liquation (for Sn, Pb), electrolytic refining (for Cu, Ag, Au, impure metal as anode, pure as cathode), zone refining (for Si, Ge, Ga as semiconductors, based on impurity solubility difference), vapour phase refining (Mond process for Ni: Ni + 4 CO goes to Ni(CO)4 followed by decomposition at 450 K), Van Arkel process for Ti, Zr: M + 2 I2 goes to MI4 (volatile) goes to M + 2 I2 (on hot filament).

Detailed Extraction Processes

Extraction of Iron (Blast Furnace)

Haematite (Fe2O3) is reduced in a blast furnace. The furnace is charged with iron ore, coke (C), and limestone (CaCO3) as flux. Hot air is blown at the bottom. Reactions:

C + O2 goes to CO2 (combustion, exothermic).
CO2 + C goes to 2 CO (at 1000 deg C, endothermic).
Fe2O3 + 3 CO goes to 2 Fe + 3 CO2 (reduction of ore, indirect reduction).
Fe2O3 + 3 C goes to 2 Fe + 3 CO (direct reduction at higher temperature).
CaCO3 goes to CaO + CO2 (decomposition of limestone).
CaO + SiO2 goes to CaSiO3 (slag formation, removes gangue).

Pig iron from the blast furnace contains 2-4% C, along with Si, Mn, P, S. It is cast into moulds called pigs. Wrought iron (purest form of commercial iron, 0.1-0.2% C) is obtained by refining pig iron in a puddling furnace. Steel (0.2-2% C) is produced by the Bessemer process or open hearth process.

Extraction of Aluminium (Hall-Heroult Process)

Bauxite (Al2O3.2H2O) is first purified by the Bayer process: crushed bauxite is treated with NaOH solution at 150-200 deg C under pressure. Al2O3 (amphoteric) dissolves: Al2O3 + 2 NaOH + 3 H2O goes to 2 Na(Al(OH)4). Impurities (Fe2O3, TiO2) remain undissolved. The solution is seeded with Al(OH)3 to precipitate: Na(Al(OH)4) goes to Al(OH)3 + NaOH. Al(OH)3 is calcined at 1500 K to give pure Al2O3.

Hall-Heroult process: Pure Al2O3 is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na3AlF6) at 950 deg C. The mixture is electrolysed. At cathode: Al3+ + 3 e- goes to Al (molten, tapped out). At anode: 2 O2- goes to O2 + 4 e- (O2 reacts with C anode to give CO, CO2). Cryolite lowers the melting point and increases conductivity.

Extraction of Copper

Copper pyrite (CuFeS2) is concentrated by froth flotation. The concentrated ore is roasted: 2 CuFeS2 + O2 goes to Cu2S + 2 FeS + SO2 (partial removal of Fe and S). Then smelted in a reverberatory furnace with SiO2 flux: FeS + SiO2 goes to FeSiO3 (slag). The molten matte (Cu2S + FeS) is transferred to a Bessemer converter: 2 Cu2S + 3 O2 goes to 2 Cu2O + 2 SO2; 2 Cu2O + Cu2S goes to 6 Cu + SO2 (self-reduction). Blister copper (98% pure) is obtained. It is electrolytically refined (99.99% pure).

Extraction of Zinc

Zinc blende (ZnS) is concentrated by froth flotation. Roasted: 2 ZnS + 3 O2 goes to 2 ZnO + 2 SO2. The ZnO is reduced by carbon in a retort: ZnO + C goes to Zn + CO. Zinc vapour is distilled (Zn boils at 1180 K, impurities remain). Pure Zn can also be obtained by electrolytic refining.

Mnemonic: Metallurgy order
Concentrate, Extract, Purify as Come Eat Pasta. For specific processes: Froth flotation for sulphide ores; Leaching for Au, Ag, Al; Electrolytic refining for Cu, Ag, Au.
Trick: Reducing agents in metallurgy
Carbon is used for moderately reactive metals (Fe, Zn, Cu). Electrolysis is used for highly reactive metals (Na, K, Mg, Al, Ca). Thermite reduction (Al as reducing agent) is used for Cr, Mn. Self-reduction (auto-reduction) occurs for Cu2S + O2 goes to 2 Cu + SO2. Aluminothermy: Fe2O3 + 2 Al goes to 2 Fe + Al2O3 (used in welding).

Ellingham Diagram

The Ellingham diagram plots delta G0 vs temperature for the formation of oxides and sulphides. Key points: Lower the line on the diagram, more stable the oxide. Any metal can reduce the oxide of another metal if its oxide formation line lies below the other. For NEET: carbon can reduce ZnO above 1100 K (the C-CO line goes below ZnO line). Al lies below Fe2O3, so Al can reduce Fe2O3 (thermite reaction). The CO line slopes downward (delta S positive for 2 C + O2 goes to 2 CO). Mg can reduce TiO2 above certain temperature.

NEET Tip: Ellingham diagram
The line for C to CO slopes downwards (positive entropy change, 2 C + O2 goes to 2 CO, gas molecules increase). The line for CO2 formation is nearly flat (C + O2 goes to CO2, no gas change). Above 1100 K, C can reduce ZnO, FeO, SnO2, but not CaO or Al2O3 (too stable).

Hydrogen Bonding in Inorganic Compounds

Hydrogen bonding occurs when hydrogen is bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O, N) and is attracted to another electronegative atom. It is of two types: intermolecular (between molecules, e.g., H2O, NH3, HF) and intramolecular (within the same molecule, e.g., o-nitrophenol). Hydrogen bonding explains the anomalously high boiling points of H2O (100C), NH3 (-33C), and HF (20C) compared to their hydrides.

In ice, each H2O molecule forms four hydrogen bonds (two through H atoms, two through lone pairs on O), giving a tetrahedral open structure that makes ice less dense than water. HF forms zigzag chains due to hydrogen bonding. NH3 has a lower boiling point than H2O because N is less electronegative than O, so N-H...N bonds are weaker than O-H...O bonds.

8. Industrial Inorganic Chemistry

Manufacture of Common Industrial Chemicals

Sulphuric Acid (H2SO4) - Contact Process

Raw materials: Sulphur or pyrites (FeS2), air, water. Steps: (1) S + O2 ▶ SO2. (2) 2 SO2 + O2 ▶ 2 SO3, using V2O5 catalyst at 400-450C, 1-2 atm pressure. (3) SO3 is absorbed in 98% H2SO4 to form oleum (H2S2O7), which is then diluted to get H2SO4. Uses: Fertilizers (50%), petroleum refining, explosives, detergents, pigments, batteries (lead-acid batteries use 30% H2SO4 as electrolyte).

Nitric Acid (HNO3) - Ostwald Process

Raw materials: NH3, air, water. Steps: (1) 4 NH3 + 5 O2 ▶ 4 NO + 6 H2O, Pt catalyst at 800C. (2) 2 NO + O2 ▶ 2 NO2 (cooling required). (3) 3 NO2 + H2O ▶ 2 HNO3 + NO (NO is recycled). 50-60% HNO3 is obtained directly; concentration to 98% is done by distillation with concentrated H2SO4. Uses: Fertilizers (ammonium nitrate), explosives (TNT, nitroglycerine), metal pickling.

Ammonia (NH3) - Haber Process

Raw materials: N2 from air, H2 from steam reforming of natural gas. Reaction: N2 + 3 H2 ▶ 2 NH3, delta H = -92.4 kJ/mol. Conditions: 400-500C, 200 atm, Fe catalyst promoted with K2O, Al2O3, Mo. The process is exothermic and involves a decrease in moles (4 to 2), so high pressure favours yield but moderate temperature is a compromise between rate and equilibrium. Ammonia is separated by liquefaction; unreacted gases are recycled.

Sodium Carbonate (Na2CO3) - Solvay Process

Raw materials: NaCl, CaCO3 (limestone), NH3, CO2. Steps: (1) CaCO3 ▶ CaO + CO2 (calcination). (2) NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O ▶ NaHCO3 + NH4Cl. (3) 2 NaHCO3 ▶ Na2CO3 + CO2 + H2O (calcination, CO2 recycled). (4) CaO + 2 NH4Cl ▶ CaCl2 + 2 NH3 + H2O (NH3 recycled). Advantages: economical (NH3 and CO2 recycled), uses cheap raw materials. Limitations: low yield (70-75%), CaCl2 is a byproduct with limited use.

10. Chemical Bonding in Inorganic Compounds

Types of Chemical Bonds

Inorganic compounds exhibit various types of bonding: ionic (electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions), covalent (sharing of electron pairs), coordinate (both electrons from same atom), and metallic (electron sea model). Fajan's rules determine the covalent character in ionic compounds: smaller cation size, larger anion size, and higher charge on ions increase covalent character. For example, LiCl has more covalent character than NaCl (Li+ is smaller).

Electron pairs (bonding and lone pairs) arrange themselves to minimize repulsion. Lone pair-lone pair repulsion > lone pair-bond pair repulsion > bond pair-bond pair repulsion.

Fajan's rules state that covalent character in an ionic compound increases with: (1) Small size of cation (higher polarising power), (2) Large size of anion (higher polarizability), (3) High charge on either ion. Important NEET Distinctions

1. Roasting vs Calcination: Roasting involves heating in excess air (for sulphide ores, produces SO2). Calcination involves heating in limited/absence of air (for carbonate/hydroxide ores, produces CO2). Example: ZnS is roasted, ZnCO3 is calcined.

2. Ore vs Mineral: All ores are minerals, but not all minerals are ores. An ore is a mineral from which a metal can be economically extracted. For example, bauxite (Al2O3.2H2O) is an ore of aluminium, but clay (also Al-containing) is not an ore because extraction is not economical.

3. Hydrometallurgy vs Pyrometallurgy: Hydrometallurgy uses aqueous solutions (leaching) for extraction (Au, Ag). Pyrometallurgy uses high temperatures (smelting, roasting) for extraction (Fe, Cu, Zn).

4. Lanthanoids vs Actinoids: Lanthanoids show limited oxidation states (+3 mostly); actinoids show multiple oxidation states (+3 to +7). Lanthanoids are less radioactive; actinoids are highly radioactive. 4f orbitals are less diffused than 5f orbitals.

5. Fluorine vs Other Halogens: F has no d-orbitals, so it cannot expand its octet (no FCl3, FCl5). F always shows -1 OS. F is the strongest oxidizing agent. HF is a weak acid due to strong H-F bond and H-bonding.

Mnemonic: Roasting vs Calcination
ROASTING = Remove Oxygen by Air Sulphide To IN oxide + Gas (SO2). CALCINATION = Carbonate/hydroxide Absence of air Limited CO2 IN oxide. Remember: Roasting for sulphides, Calcination for carbonates.
Example 41
Which of the following statements is correct?
(a) All minerals are ores (b) All ores are minerals (c) Minerals and ores are same (d) None
Solution: b. Ores are a subset of minerals. A mineral is a naturally occurring substance; an ore is a mineral from which metal can be extracted profitably. For example, clay is a mineral but not an ore of Al.
Example 42
Which of the following shows the highest bond dissociation energy? a) F-F b) Cl-Cl c) Br-Br d) I-I
Solution: b. Cl-Cl bond has the highest dissociation energy (243 kJ/mol). F-F bond is weak (158 kJ/mol) due to lone pair repulsion between the two small F atoms. Trend: Cl2 > Br2 > F2 > I2.

Inorganic Chemistry Numericals for NEET

Example 43
Calculate the magnetic moment (in BM) of Cr3+ ion (atomic number 24).
Solution: Cr3+ electronic config: (Ar) 3d3. n = 3 unpaired electrons. mu = sqrt(n(n+2)) = sqrt(3x5) = sqrt(15) = 3.87 BM.
Example 44
0.5 g of a metal on reaction with excess dilute H2SO4 gives 200 mL of H2 gas at STP. Identify the metal if it is divalent.
Solution: For divalent metal M: M + H2SO4 ▶ MSO4 + H2. 200 mL H2 at STP = 200/22400 = 0.00893 mol H2 = 0.00893 mol M. Molar mass of M = 0.5/0.00893 = 56 g/mol. The metal is Fe (atomic mass 56).
Example 45
What volume of 0.1 M K2Cr2O7 is required to oxidize 20 mL of 0.2 M FeSO4 in acidic medium?
Solution: Cr2O7 2- + 6 Fe2+ + 14 H+ ▶ 2 Cr3+ + 6 Fe3+ + 7 H2O. n-factor of K2Cr2O7 = 6. n-factor of FeSO4 = 1. Applying n1V1M1 = n2V2M2: 6 x V x 0.1 = 1 x 20 x 0.2. V = (20 x 0.2)/(6 x 0.1) = 4/0.6 = 6.67 mL.
Example 46
An element has electronic configuration (Ar) 3d6 4s2. Its ion in +3 oxidation state has magnetic moment of: a) 5.92 BM b) 4.90 BM c) 3.87 BM d) 1.73 BM
Solution: The element with (Ar) 3d6 4s2 is Fe. Fe3+: (Ar) 3d5, n = 5 unpaired electrons. mu = sqrt(5x7) = sqrt(35) = 5.92 BM. Option (a) is correct.
Example 47
Which of the following statements about lanthanoids is INCORRECT?
(a) Lanthanoid contraction is due to poor shielding of 4f electrons
(b) All lanthanoids show +3 oxidation state
(c) Ce shows +4 and Eu shows +2 oxidation states
(d) Lanthanoids are radioactive elements
Solution: d is incorrect. Only Promethium (Pm) is radioactive among lanthanoids. Other lanthanoids are not radioactive (they are not naturally radioactive, though some have radioactive isotopes).
Example 48
The geometry of (Ni(CN)4)2- ion is: a) Tetrahedral b) Square planar c) Octahedral d) Linear
Solution: b. In (Ni(CN)4)2-, Ni2+ (d8) undergoes dsp2 hybridization with CN- as a strong field ligand, giving square planar geometry (all electrons paired, diamagnetic). With weak field ligands like Cl-, Ni2+ gives tetrahedral geometry (sp3, paramagnetic).
Example 49
The hybridization of Xe in XeO3 is: a) sp3 b) sp3d c) sp3d2 d) sp
Solution: a. XeO3 has 3 bond pairs and 1 lone pair on Xe (total 4 electron pairs). So hybridization is sp3. The shape is trigonal pyramidal (like NH3). Remember: XeO3 and XeOF4 are important for NEET - XeO3 is sp3, XeOF4 is sp3d2.

Covalent vs Ionic Hydrides
Ionic hydrides: Formed by s-block elements (except Be, Mg). Covalent hydrides: Formed by p-block. Metallic hydrides: Formed by d- and f-block. Mnemonic: Ionic s-block, Covalent p-block, Metallic d/f-block.
Oxidation States of Transition Metals
Maximum oxidation state of a transition metal = number of unpaired d-electrons + 2 (from ns). For Mn (3d5 4s2), max OS = +7. For Cr (3d5 4s1), max OS = +6. For Fe (3d6 4s2), max OS = +3 (common) and +6 (rare, ferrate). Mnemonic: Mn shows +7, Cr shows +6, Fe shows +3, Cu shows +2.
Cisplatin Mechanism
Cis-platin [Pt(NH3)2Cl2] works by crosslinking DNA. The Cl ligands are replaced by water, then the Pt binds to guanine bases in DNA, preventing replication and transcription. Trans-platin is inactive because its geometry prevents proper DNA binding.

Common NEET Traps and Mistakes

1. Zn is NOT a transition element: Zn has d10 configuration in +2 state (no partially filled d-orbitals). Ag+ (d10) is also not a transition element. But Cu+ (d10) IS because Cu2+ (d9) has partially filled d-orbitals.

2. Highest EA is Cl, not F: Many students think F has the highest electron affinity because it is the most electronegative. But Cl has the highest EA due to less electron repulsion.

3. XeF2 is linear, not bent: XeF2 has sp3d hybridization with 3 lone pairs in equatorial positions and 2 F atoms in axial positions giving linear shape.

4. Mn2+ has 5 unpaired electrons: Mn2+ is d5 (half-filled), not d7. Common mistake: confusing Mn2+ with Mn (atomic number 25, but after losing 2 e- from 4s2, configuration becomes 3d5).

5. Alkali metals form different oxides: Li forms Li2O only. Na forms Na2O2 (peroxide). K, Rb, Cs form MO2 (superoxides). Not all alkali metals form superoxides.

Example 33
Which of the following statements is INCORRECT? a) Zn is not a transition element b) Cl has higher EA than F c) XeF2 is linear d) Na forms superoxide
Solution: d is incorrect. Na forms peroxide (Na2O2), not superoxide. Only K, Rb, Cs form superoxides (MO2). Li forms Li2O (normal oxide).
Example 34
The correct order of the oxidation state of chlorine in HOCl, HClO2, HClO3, and HClO4 is: a) +1, +3, +5, +7 b) +1, +5, +3, +7 c) +7, +5, +3, +1 d) +3, +1, +5, +7
Solution: a. In oxyacids of chlorine, OS of Cl increases with number of O atoms. HOCl: +1 (H-O-Cl), HClO2: +3, HClO3: +5, HClO4: +7. Acid strength also increases in the same order.
Example 35
What is the shape of ClF3? a) Trigonal planar b) T-shaped c) Trigonal bipyramidal d) Square planar
Solution: b. ClF3 has sp3d hybridization with 3 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs. The lone pairs occupy equatorial positions of a trigonal bipyramid, giving a T-shaped molecular geometry (bent T).
Example 36
Which of the following pairs of ions have same magnetic moment? a) Mn2+ and Fe3+ b) Fe2+ and Co3+ c) Ni2+ and Cu2+ d) Cr3+ and V3+
Solution: a. Mn2+ (d5, n=5) and Fe3+ (d5, n=5) both have 5 unpaired electrons, so magnetic moment mu = sqrt(35) = 5.92 BM for both. Fe2+ (d6) and Co3+ (d6) also have the same n in high spin. Cr3+ (d3) and V3+ (d3) also have same n.
Example 37
Which of the following is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors? a) H2O b) D2O c) H2O2 d) None
Solution: b. Heavy water (D2O) is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors to slow down fast neutrons. It has a higher neutron slowing-down power and lower neutron absorption cross-section compared to ordinary water.
Example 38
In the brown ring test for nitrate, the brown colour is due to: a) Fe(NO3)3 b) (Fe(H2O)5NO)2+ c) NO2 d) FeSO4
Solution: b. The brown ring is due to the formation of the complex ion (Fe(H2O)5NO)2+. NO is produced by reduction of NO3- by Fe2+ in acidic medium. The complex is paramagnetic and has a characteristic brown colour.
