Foundations of Organic Chemistry

1.1 The Carbon Skeleton and Tetravalency

Organic chemistry is built upon the unique ability of carbon to form four covalent bonds, a consequence of its four valence electrons and the relatively small energy gap between the 2s and 2p orbitals. This tetravalency allows carbon atoms to link with each other in chains, branches, and rings, creating an essentially infinite variety of molecular frameworks. The strength of the carbon–carbon single bond (approximately 347 kJ mol⁻¹) and the carbon–hydrogen bond (approximately 413 kJ mol⁻¹) provides the thermodynamic stability that makes organic compounds both abundant and chemically versatile. Because each carbon can adopt sp³, sp², or sp hybridisation, the geometry around carbon can be tetrahedral, trigonal planar, or linear, respectively, and this geometric diversity underpins the three‑dimensional shapes that dictate reactivity and physical properties.

1.2 Hybridisation and Bonding in Hydrocarbons

In alkanes every carbon is sp³ hybridised, giving rise to sigma (σ) bonds only and a tetrahedral bond angle of 109.5°. Alkenes contain at least one carbon–carbon double bond; each doubly bonded carbon is sp² hybridised, forming three σ bonds in a trigonal planar arrangement and one π bond that results from the side‑on overlap of the remaining unhybridised p orbitals. Alkynes feature a carbon–carbon triple bond where each carbon is sp hybridised, producing two σ bonds in a linear geometry and two orthogonal π bonds. Aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzene possess a delocalised π system in which each carbon is sp² hybridised, but the six π electrons are shared over the ring, granting extra stability described by Hückel’s 4n + 2 rule. The progressive increase in s‑character from sp³ to sp leads to shorter, stronger σ bonds and higher electronegativity of the carbon atom, which in turn influences acidity of terminal alkynes and the reactivity of the π system.

1.3 Classification of Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are classified primarily by the nature of the carbon–carbon bonds present. Saturated hydrocarbons, the alkanes, contain only single bonds and follow the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for acyclic members. Unsaturated hydrocarbons include alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ) with at least one double bond, alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂) with at least one triple bond, and aromatic hydrocarbons that contain a conjugated cyclic π system, the simplest being benzene (C₆H₆). Cycloalkanes are saturated rings with formula CₙH₂ₙ, while cycloalkenes and cycloalkynes incorporate unsaturation within a ring. The degree of unsaturation, also called the index of hydrogen deficiency, can be calculated from the molecular formula and provides a quick check on the number of rings and multiple bonds present in an unknown structure.

IUPAC Nomenclature of Hydrocarbons

2.1 Principles of the IUPAC System

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) nomenclature provides a systematic method to name every organic compound uniquely. The system is based on three hierarchical decisions: identification of the longest continuous carbon chain (the parent), numbering of that chain to give the lowest set of locants to substituents and multiple bonds, and citation of substituents, functional groups, and unsaturation in alphabetical order with appropriate prefixes and suffixes. The parent name derives from the number of carbon atoms (meth‑, eth‑, prop‑, but‑, pent‑, hex‑, hept‑, oct‑, non‑, dec‑) and the saturation level (‑ane, ‑ene, ‑yne). When both double and triple bonds are present, the suffixes are combined as ‑en‑yne, and the chain is numbered to give the lowest possible numbers to the multiple bonds considered together, with double bonds receiving priority over triple bonds only when a tie exists.

2.2 Naming Substituted Alkanes and Cycloalkanes

For a branched alkane the longest chain is selected; if two chains of equal length exist, the one with the greater number of substituents is chosen. Substituents are named by replacing the ‑ane ending of the corresponding alkane with ‑yl (methyl, ethyl, propyl, etc.). The positions of substituents are indicated by locants placed before the substituent name, separated by commas, and the whole substituent list is preceded by the parent name without a space (e.g., 2,3‑dimethylpentane). In cycloalkanes the ring itself is the parent; the prefix “cyclo‑” is added to the alkane name (cyclopentane). Numbering of a monosubstituted cycloalkane starts at the substituted carbon, while for polysubstituted rings the numbering proceeds around the ring to give the lowest set of locants, applying the “first point of difference” rule.

2.3 Nomenclature of Alkenes, Alkynes, and Aromatics

Alkenes receive the suffix ‑ene; the position of the double bond is indicated by the lower‑numbered carbon of the double bond (e.g., pent‑2‑ene). If more than one double bond exists, the suffix becomes ‑diene, ‑triene, etc., and each double bond receives a locant (e.g., hexa‑1,3‑diene). Alkynes are named analogously with the suffix ‑yne (e.g., but‑1‑yne). When both double and triple bonds are present, the compound is an enyne; the chain is numbered to give the lowest set of locants to the multiple bonds considered together, and the “ene” part is cited before “yne” (e.g., hex‑3‑en‑1‑yne). Aromatic hydrocarbons retain the traditional names for the simplest members (benzene, toluene, xylene, naphthalene) but systematic names are also permitted (e.g., methylbenzene for toluene). Substituents on an aromatic ring are indicated by locants (ortho, meta, para are discouraged in systematic nomenclature) and the ring is numbered to give the lowest numbers to the substituents.

Worked Example: Systematic Naming
Provide the IUPAC name for the hydrocarbon with the structure CH₃‑CH₂‑C(CH₃)=CH‑CH₂‑C≡CH.
The longest continuous chain contains seven carbon atoms, so the parent is hept‑. The chain contains one double bond between C‑3 and C‑4 and one triple bond between C‑6 and C‑7. Exam‑Day Insight
When a molecule contains both a double and a triple bond, always number the chain to give the lowest combined set of locants; the double bond does not automatically outrank the triple bond unless the sets are identical, in which case the double bond receives the lower number.

Isomerism in Hydrocarbons

3.1 Structural (Constitutional) Isomerism

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but differ in the connectivity of atoms. In alkanes, chain isomerism arises from different carbon skeletons (e.g., n‑pentane versus isopentane versus neopentane). Position isomerism occurs when a functional group or multiple bond occupies different positions on the same carbon skeleton (e.g., pent‑1‑ene versus pent‑2‑ene). Functional group isomerism is not observed within pure hydrocarbons because the only functional groups are the carbon–carbon multiple bonds themselves; however, cycloalkanes and alkenes can be functional‑group isomers of each other (e.g., cyclopentane C₅H₁₀ and pent‑1‑ene C₅H₁₀). The number of possible structural isomers grows rapidly with carbon count; for C₁₀H₂₂ there are 75 distinct alkane isomers, illustrating the combinatorial richness of carbon chemistry.

3.2 Stereoisomerism: Geometric (cis‑trans) Isomerism

Geometric isomerism arises when rotation about a bond is restricted, most commonly across a carbon–carbon double bond or within a ring. For an alkene to exhibit cis‑trans isomerism, each doubly bonded carbon must bear two different substituents. The cis isomer has the higher‑priority groups on the same side of the double bond, while the trans isomer has them on opposite sides. The Cahn‑Ingold‑Prelog (CIP) priority rules assign priority based on atomic number of the atoms directly attached to the double‑bonded carbons; this system extends to E/Z notation, which is unambiguous for all substituted alkenes. In cycloalkanes, cis‑trans isomerism describes the relative orientation of substituents on the ring (both up or both down versus one up one down). The energy barrier for interconversion is high because it would require breaking the π bond or severely distorting the ring, so the isomers are isolable at room temperature.

3.3 Optical Isomerism and Chirality in Hydrocarbons

Optical isomerism (enantiomerism) requires a chiral centre, typically a carbon bearing four different substituents. While simple alkanes rarely possess such centres, substituted alkenes, alkynes, and especially polycyclic aromatic systems can become chiral through axial chirality (e.g., allenes with cumulated double bonds) or helical chirality (e.g., helicenes). An allene (C=C=C) with different substituents on the terminal carbons is chiral because the two π bonds lie in perpendicular planes, creating a non‑superimposable mirror image. The specific rotation of each enantiomer is equal in magnitude but opposite in sign. Racemic mixtures contain equal amounts of both enantiomers and are optically inactive. Resolution of enantiomers can be achieved by formation of diastereomeric derivatives with a chiral resolving agent, followed by separation and regeneration of the pure enantiomers.

Reaction Mechanisms Fundamental to Hydrocarbon Chemistry

4.1 Homolytic and Heterolytic Bond Cleavage

Chemical reactions of hydrocarbons proceed through either homolytic or heterolytic cleavage of covalent bonds. Homolytic fission splits a bond evenly, each atom retaining one electron, generating radicals (species with an unpaired electron). This pathway dominates in high‑temperature or photochemical processes such as the chlorination of methane, where a chlorine radical abstracts a hydrogen atom to give a methyl radical, which then couples with another chlorine radical. Heterolytic fission assigns both bonding electrons to one atom, producing a cation and an anion; this is typical in polar reactions involving electrophiles and nucleophiles, for example the addition of HBr to an alkene via a carbocation intermediate. The relative stability of the resulting intermediates—radicals, carbocations, carbanions—governs which pathway is favoured under a given set of conditions.

4.2 Carbocation Stability and Rearrangements

Carbocations are electron‑deficient species with a positively charged carbon bearing only six valence electrons. Their stability follows the order tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl, a trend explained by hyperconjugation and inductive donation from adjacent alkyl groups. Allylic and benzylic carbocations enjoy additional resonance stabilisation, making them unusually stable even when formally primary. Because carbocations can undergo rapid 1,2‑hydride or 1,2‑alkyl shifts to form a more stable cation, reaction pathways often involve rearrangements. For instance, the acid‑catalysed dehydration of 3‑methyl‑2‑butanol proceeds via a secondary carbocation that rearranges to a more stable tertiary carbocation before elimination yields the more substituted alkene (Zaitsev product). Recognising possible rearrangements is essential for predicting the major product in addition, elimination, and substitution reactions of hydrocarbons.

4.3 Radical Chain Mechanisms: Initiation, Propagation, Termination

Radical halogenation of alkanes exemplifies a chain mechanism. Initiation requires an energy input (UV light or heat) to homolytically cleave the halogen molecule (Cl₂ → 2 Cl·). Propagation steps involve the radical abstracting a hydrogen atom from the alkane (Cl· + RH → HCl + R·) followed by the carbon radical reacting with another halogen molecule (R· + Cl₂ → RCl + Cl·), regenerating the chlorine radical. The chain continues until termination steps combine two radicals (Cl· + Cl· → Cl₂, R· + Cl· → RCl, R· + R· → R‑R). The selectivity of hydrogen abstraction follows the stability order of the resulting carbon radical (tertiary > secondary > primary), which mirrors carbocation stability. Consequently, chlorination of 2‑methylbutane yields a mixture dominated by the tertiary chloride, whereas bromination, being more selective, gives an even higher proportion of the tertiary bromide.

4.4 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) Mechanism

Aromatic hydrocarbons undergo substitution rather than addition because addition would destroy the aromatic π system and its associated stabilization energy (≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹). In electrophilic aromatic substitution, a strong electrophile (e.g., NO₂⁺ in nitration, Cl⁺ in chlorination) attacks the π cloud, forming a resonance‑stabilised σ‑complex (Wheland intermediate) in which the positive charge is delocalised over the ring. Deprotonation at the carbon bearing the electrophile restores aromaticity and yields the substituted product. The rate‑determining step is the formation of the σ‑complex; substituents already present on the ring influence both the rate and the position of further substitution. Electron‑donating groups (alkyl, –OH, –OR) activate the ring and direct ortho/para, while electron‑withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –COOH, –SO₃H) deactivate and direct meta. Understanding these directing effects is crucial for planning multi‑step syntheses of aromatic compounds.

Chemistry of Alkanes, Alkenes, Alkynes, and Aromatic Hydrocarbons

5.1 Physical Properties and Trends

Alkanes are non‑polar molecules whose only intermolecular forces are London dispersion forces; consequently, boiling points increase smoothly with molecular weight and surface area. Branching reduces surface contact, lowering boiling points (e.g., n‑pentane 36 °C vs neopentane 9.5 °C). Alkenes and alkynes have slightly higher polarity due to the π bond, giving marginally higher boiling points than the corresponding alkanes of equal carbon number. Aromatic hydrocarbons are planar and possess a delocalised π cloud, leading to stronger π‑π stacking interactions; benzene boils at 80 °C, higher than cyclohexane (81 °C) despite having fewer electrons, because the quadrupole moment enhances intermolecular attraction. All hydrocarbons are essentially insoluble in water but dissolve readily in non‑polar organic solvents.

5.2 Characteristic Reactions of Alkanes

Alkanes are relatively inert under ambient conditions because all bonds are strong σ bonds. The most important reaction is free‑radical halogenation, which proceeds via the chain mechanism described earlier. Combustion in excess oxygen yields carbon dioxide and water, releasing large amounts of heat (ΔH_combustion ≈ ‑4700 kJ mol⁻¹ for octane). Catalytic cracking at high temperature breaks larger alkanes into smaller, more valuable alkenes and alkanes, a cornerstone of petroleum refining. Isomerisation over acidic catalysts (e.g., Pt/Al₂O₃) converts straight‑chain alkanes into branched isomers to improve octane rating. Dehydrogenation of alkanes to alkenes (e.g., propane → propene) is achieved industrially using chromium oxide or platinum catalysts at 500–600 °C.

5.3 Reactivity of Alkenes: Addition Reactions

The π bond of alkenes is a region of high electron density, making alkenes nucleophilic toward electrophiles. Hydrogen halides add across the double bond following Markovnikov’s rule: the hydrogen attaches to the carbon bearing more hydrogens, generating the more stable carbocation intermediate. In the presence of peroxides, HBr adds via a radical chain mechanism giving anti‑Markovnikov product (Kharasch addition). Halogen addition (Br₂, Cl₂) proceeds through a cyclic halonium ion, giving vicinal dihalides with anti stereochemistry. Hydration (acid‑catalysed addition of water) yields alcohols, while hydroboration‑oxidation provides anti‑Markovnikov alcohols with syn addition. Catalytic hydrogenation (H₂, Pd/C) reduces the double bond to a single bond, a reaction that is stereospecifically syn. Ozonolysis cleaves the double bond, producing carbonyl compounds (aldehydes or ketones) after reductive work‑up (Zn/AcOH) or oxidative work‑up (H₂O₂).

5.4 Reactivity of Alkynes: Addition and Acidity

Alkynes possess two π bonds; the first addition reaction often stops at the alkene stage if a bulky reagent or a poisoned catalyst (Lindlar’s catalyst) is used, giving cis‑alkenes. Full catalytic hydrogenation with Pd/C yields alkanes. Halogen addition can add one or two equivalents, giving vicinal dihalides or tetrahalides. Hydrogen halides add twice; the first addition follows Markovnikov orientation, and the second addition yields a geminal dihalide. Terminal alkynes are unusually acidic (pK_a ≈ 25) because the sp‑hybridised carbon holds the negative charge in an orbital with 50 % s‑character, stabilising the acetylide anion. Deprotonation with strong bases (NaNH₂, n‑BuLi) generates nucleophilic acetylides that undergo SN2 reactions with alkyl halides, enabling carbon‑carbon bond formation. Oxidative cleavage with hot KMnO₄ or ozonolysis yields carboxylic acids (or carbon dioxide for terminal alkynes).

5.5 Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Stability and Substitution

Benzene’s exceptional stability arises from the cyclic delocalisation of six π electrons, satisfying Hückel’s 4n + 2 rule (n = 1). This aromatic stabilization energy makes addition reactions thermodynamically unfavourable; instead, electrophilic aromatic substitution preserves the aromatic sextet. Nitration (conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄) introduces a nitro group, sulfonation (fuming H₂SO₄) gives a sulfonic acid, halogenation (Cl₂/FeCl₃) yields chlorobenzene, and Friedel‑Crafts alkylation/acylation (R‑Cl/AlCl₃ or RCOCl/AlCl₃) attach alkyl or acyl groups. The directing effects of existing substituents dictate the position of the incoming electrophile: activating groups (alkyl, –OH, –NH₂) are ortho/para directors, while deactivating groups (–NO₂, –COOH, –SO₃H) are meta directors, except halogens which are deactivating yet ortho/para directing due to resonance donation. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons such as naphthalene and anthracene exhibit similar chemistry but with increased reactivity at the α‑positions. Understanding these patterns enables the rational synthesis of complex aromatic molecules used in pharmaceuticals, dyes, and polymers.

The systematic study of nomenclature, isomerism, reaction mechanisms, and the distinct chemical behaviour of each hydrocarbon class provides the conceptual framework required to predict structures, design syntheses, and rationalise the vast diversity of organic compounds encountered in advanced chemistry.

Regioselectivity and Stereoselectivity in Alkene Addition Reactions

Addition to a carbon–carbon double bond can generate two new σ bonds. The orientation of the incoming groups (regioselectivity) and their relative three‑dimensional arrangement (stereoselectivity) are governed by the nature of the electrophile, the reaction medium, and the stability of the intermediate. In electrophilic addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene, the proton adds to the carbon bearing the greater number of hydrogens, producing the more substituted carbocation; this is the classic Markovnikov rule. When a peroxide initiator is present, HBr adds by a radical chain mechanism and the bromine atom attaches to the less substituted carbon, giving the anti‑Markovnikov product (Kharasch addition). Halogen addition (Br₂, Cl₂) proceeds through a cyclic halonium ion; nucleophilic attack by the halide occurs from the backside, delivering anti addition and a vicinal dihalide with defined stereochemistry. Hydroboration‑oxidation employs syn addition of boron and hydrogen across the double bond; oxidation with H₂O₂/NaOH replaces boron by hydroxyl with retention of configuration, furnishing anti‑Markovnikov alcohols. Catalytic hydrogenation (H₂, Pd/C) is also syn, delivering both hydrogens to the same face of the former π bond. Ozonolysis followed by reductive work‑up (Zn/AcOH) cleaves the double bond to carbonyl compounds, while oxidative work‑up (H₂O₂) yields carboxylic acids. The stereochemical outcome of each pathway can be predicted by drawing the relevant intermediate (carbocation, halonium ion, borane complex) and applying the principle of least‑motion or backside attack.

Key Factors Influencing Selectivity

  • Substituent electronic effects – electron‑donating alkyl groups stabilise adjacent carbocations, directing protonation to the more substituted carbon.
  • Steric hindrance – bulky reagents (e.g., t‑BuOOH in radical addition) favour approach to the less hindered carbon.
  • Reaction medium – polar protic solvents stabilise ionic intermediates, enhancing Markovnikov pathways; non‑polar solvents favour radical chains.
  • Catalyst/ligand environment – chiral ligands on transition‑metal catalysts can induce enantioselective hydrogenation.

Predictive Workflow

  1. Identify the alkene substitution pattern.
  2. Choose the reagent class (electrophilic, radical, concerted).
  3. Draw the key intermediate (carbocation, halonium, borane).
  4. Apply stereoelectronic rules (backside attack, syn delivery).
  5. Assign absolute configuration if a chiral centre is created.

Carbocation Rearrangements: Hydride and Alkyl Shifts

Carbocations are electron‑deficient sp² centres that readily undergo 1,2‑shifts of adjacent σ bonds to generate a more stable cation. A hydride shift moves a hydrogen with its bonding electrons from a neighbouring carbon to the cationic centre; an alkyl shift transfers a carbon group (methyl, ethyl, etc.) in the same fashion. The driving force is the increase in substitution level: primary → secondary → tertiary → resonance‑stabilised (allylic, benzylic). The Wagner‑Meerwein rearrangement exemplifies a skeletal reorganisation where a bridgehead carbocation in a bicyclic system undergoes a series of alkyl shifts to relieve angle strain. In solvolysis of neopentyl halides, a primary carbocation is never observed; instead a rapid 1,2‑methyl shift yields a tertiary cation that captures solvent. Rearrangements are concerted with nucleophilic capture when the nucleophile is present in high concentration, but they can also be intercepted by elimination to give alkenes (E1 pathway). The rate of a shift correlates with the ability of the migrating group to donate electron density; hydride shifts are generally faster than alkyl shifts because the C–H bond is more polarizable. Isotopic labelling (deuterium at the migration origin) provides kinetic isotope evidence for the concerted nature of the shift. In synthetic planning, anticipating rearrangements is crucial: a substrate that would form a less stable cation may be redirected by using a non‑rearranging leaving group (e.g., triflate) or by employing a neighbouring‑group participation strategy.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the product of an addition reaction, first classify the reagent (electrophilic, radical, concerted). Then write the key intermediate (carbocation, radical, halonium, borane). Apply the regiochemical rule (Markovnikov vs anti‑Markovnikov) and the stereochemical rule (syn vs anti). If a chiral centre is created, state whether a racemic mixture or a single enantiomer is expected. This systematic approach avoids missing rearrangements or stereochemical inversions.

Directing Effects and Limitations in Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

Electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) proceeds through a σ‑complex (Wheland intermediate) in which the aromatic sextet is temporarily lost. The position of attack is dictated by the electronic nature of substituents already present on the ring. Activating groups donate electron density by resonance (+R) or hyperconjugation (+I), stabilising the σ‑complex at ortho and para positions; they are ortho/para directors and accelerate the reaction relative to benzene. Typical activators include alkyl, –OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂. Deactivating groups withdraw electron density (–I, –R), destabilising the σ‑complex; they are meta directors because the ortho and para σ‑complexes place a positive charge directly on the carbon bearing the withdrawing group. Common deactivators are –NO₂, –CF₃, –SO₃H, –COOH, –COOR, –CN. Halogens are unique: they are deactivating by –I but donate by +R, making them ortho/para directors despite overall rate retardation. Steric hindrance can override electronic preferences; bulky electrophiles (e.g., t‑BuCl/AlCl₃) give predominantly para products even with ortho/para directors. Friedel‑Crafts alkylation suffers from polyalkylation and carbocation rearrangements; acylation avoids rearrangement because the acylium ion (R‑C≡O⁺) is resonance‑stabilised and does not rearrange. However, acylation deactivates the ring, preventing further substitution. Strongly deactivated rings (e.g., nitrobenzene) do not undergo Friedel‑Crafts reactions at all. Sulfonation is reversible; heating the sulfonated product in dilute acid regenerates the parent arene, a useful protecting‑group strategy. Nitration of phenol requires careful temperature control to avoid oxidation; the –OH group strongly activates the ring, giving a mixture of ortho and para nitrophenols. Understanding these nuances allows the chemist to plan multi‑step syntheses with predictable regioselectivity.

Polymerization of Alkenes: Ziegler‑Natta and Metallocene Catalysis

The industrial production of polyethylene and polypropylene relies on coordination polymerisation catalysed by transition‑metal complexes. Classical Ziegler‑Natta catalysts consist of a titanium tetrachloride (TiCl₄) supported on magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) combined with an organoaluminium co‑catalyst such as triethylaluminium (AlEt₃). The active site is a Ti³⁺ centre with a vacant coordination site that binds the alkene π bond; insertion into the Ti–C bond propagates the polymer chain. The heterogeneous nature of the catalyst yields polymers with broad molecular‑weight distributions (Đ ≈ 5–10) and limited control over tacticity. Metallocene catalysts, discovered in the 1980s, are homogeneous single‑site complexes of the type Cp₂MCl₂ (M = Zr, Hf, Ti) activated by methylaluminoxane (MAO). The well‑defined geometry of the metallocene (ansa‑bridged, C₂‑symmetric, or Cₛ‑symmetric) dictates the stereochemistry of monomer insertion, enabling production of isotactic, syndiotactic, or atactic polypropylene with narrow molecular‑weight distributions (Đ ≈ 2). The polymerisation follows a Cossee‑Arlman mechanism: alkene coordination, migratory insertion into the metal‑alkyl bond, and chain propagation. Chain transfer to aluminium (β‑hydride transfer to Al) and β‑hydride elimination to metal control molecular weight. Copolymerisation of ethylene with α‑olefins (1‑hexene, 1‑octene) introduces short‑chain branches, tailoring density and mechanical properties (linear low‑density polyethylene, LLDPE). Recent developments include post‑metallocene catalysts (e.g., phenoxy‑imine Ni/ Pd complexes) that operate at lower temperatures and tolerate polar functional groups, expanding the monomer scope to polar olefins. Understanding the relationship between catalyst symmetry, active‑site electronics, and polymer microstructure is essential for designing materials with targeted properties.

  2. Coordination: Alkene π‑bond binds to the vacant site on the metal.
  3. Migratory insertion: The alkyl group migrates to the coordinated alkene, extending the chain by one repeat unit.
  4. Chain transfer/termination: β‑hydride elimination to metal or transfer to aluminium yields a saturated chain end and regenerates the active species.

Oxidative Cleavage and Functional Group Interconversion of Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

Oxidative cleavage converts carbon–carbon multiple bonds into carbonyl or carboxyl functionalities, providing a powerful tool for structure elucidation and synthesis. Ozonolysis of alkenes proceeds via a primary ozonide (molozonide) that rearranges to a stable ozonide; reductive work‑up (Zn/AcOH, Me₂S, PPh₃) yields aldehydes or ketones, while oxidative work‑up (H₂O₂) oxidises aldehydes to carboxylic acids. For terminal alkenes, reductive work‑up gives formaldehyde (often lost as gas) and a carbonyl fragment; oxidative work‑up gives carbon dioxide and a carboxylic acid. Alkynes undergo ozonolysis to give α‑dicarbonyl ozonides that hydrolyse to two carboxylic acids (or CO₂ for terminal alkynes). Hot, concentrated KMnO₄ cleaves alkenes to ketones (if both carbons are substituted) or carboxylic acids (if a carbon bears at least one hydrogen); the reaction proceeds through a cyclic manganate ester that fragments. Alkynes are oxidised by KMnO₄ to diketones (glyoxal derivatives) which further hydrolyse to carboxylic acids. The Lemieux–Johnson reagent (OsO₄/NaIO₄) performs dihydroxylation followed by periodate cleavage, delivering carbonyl compounds under mild, neutral conditions. These transformations are stereospecific: syn dihydroxylation by OsO₄ gives vicinal diols that retain the original alkene geometry; subsequent periodate cleavage breaks the C–C bond between the two hydroxyl‑bearing carbons. In synthetic planning, oxidative cleavage is used to fragment complex polyenes into identifiable carbonyl fragments (e.g., ozonolysis of a natural product to deduce the carbon skeleton). Care must be taken with over‑oxidation: prolonged exposure to KMnO₄ can oxidise aldehydes to acids and cleave adjacent C–C bonds. Protective groups (acetals for aldehydes) or milder reagents (OsO₄/NaIO₄) mitigate side reactions.

  • Neglecting carbocation rearrangements in solvolysis or addition reactions – always check for possible 1,2‑hydride or alkyl shifts.
  • Treating all halogen substituents as meta directors – halogens are ortho/para directors despite being deactivating.

  • Assuming metallocene catalysts behave like Ziegler‑Natta – single‑site metallocenes give narrow Đ and precise tacticity control.

Conformational Analysis of Cycloalkanes and Reactivity Implications

Cycloalkanes adopt non‑planar conformations to relieve angle and torsional strain. Cyclopropane is forced into a planar triangle with 60° bond angles, resulting in high angle strain (≈115 kJ mol⁻¹) and eclipsed C–H bonds; its reactivity resembles that of alkenes in ring‑opening reactions. Cyclobutane puckers to a folded conformation (≈20° dihedral), reducing eclipsing interactions while retaining considerable angle strain. Cyclopentane adopts an envelope conformation in which four carbons are nearly coplanar and the fifth flaps out; rapid pseudorotation interconverts five equivalent envelopes, giving an average strain energy of ≈26 kJ mol⁻¹.

Cyclohexane is the benchmark for conformational analysis. The chair conformation eliminates both angle strain (bond angles ≈111°) and torsional strain (all C–H bonds staggered). Two chair forms interconvert via a ring‑flip that passes through higher‑energy half‑chair, twist‑boat, and boat conformers. Substituents occupy either axial (parallel to the C₃ axis) or equatorial (roughly in the ring plane) positions. The equatorial orientation is favoured because it avoids 1,3‑diaxial steric repulsions; the free‑energy difference is quantified by the A‑value (e.g., methyl A‑value ≈7.6 kJ mol⁻¹). In reactions such as SN2 displacement on a cyclohexyl halide, an axial leaving group is more accessible to backside attack, whereas elimination (E2) requires an axial hydrogen anti‑periplanar to the leaving group. Thus, conformational preferences directly dictate regio‑ and stereochemical outcomes in substitution and elimination chemistry of saturated rings.

Key Conformational Parameters

  • Ring‑strain energy: cyclopropane > cyclobutane > cyclopentane > cyclohexane (near zero).
  • Barrier to ring‑flip in cyclohexane ≈45 kJ mol⁻¹; rapid at room temperature.
  • A‑values provide a quantitative measure of substituent equatorial preference.
  • Anomeric effect analogues in heterocycles are absent in pure hydrocarbons.

Pericyclic Reactions of Hydrocarbons: Diels–Alder, Electrocyclic, and Sigmatropic Processes

Pericyclic reactions proceed through a cyclic, concerted transition state with no intermediates. The Woodward–Hoffmann rules predict allowedness based on conservation of orbital symmetry. For a thermal [4 + 2] cycloaddition (Diels–Alder), the diene contributes four π‑electrons (HOMO) and the dienophile two π‑electrons (LUMO); suprafacial‑suprafacial interaction is symmetry‑allowed. The reaction is stereospecific: the relative configuration of substituents on the diene and dienophile is retained in the cycloadduct. The endo rule states that substituents on the dienophile orient under the diene π‑system in the transition state, maximising secondary orbital overlap; this kinetic preference often gives the endo adduct as the major product.

Electrocyclic reactions involve ring closure or opening of a conjugated π‑system. The stereochemical mode (conrotatory vs disrotatory) depends on the number of π‑electrons and the reaction condition (thermal or photochemical). For a 4π system (e.g., cyclobutene → butadiene), thermal ring opening is conrotatory, while photochemical opening is disrotatory. For a 6π system (e.g., 1,3,5‑hexatriene → cyclohexadiene), the thermal mode is disrotatory and the photochemical mode conrotatory. Sigmatropic rearrangements entail migration of a σ‑bond across a π‑framework. The [1,5]‑hydrogen shift in 1,3‑pentadiene is thermally allowed suprafacially, whereas a [1,3]‑shift is forbidden thermally but allowed photochemically. The Cope rearrangement ([3,3]‑sigmatropic) of 1,5‑dienes proceeds through a chair‑like six‑membered transition state, and the Claisen rearrangement (oxygen analogue) follows the same orbital symmetry principles.

Predicting Diels–Alder Stereochemistry
When cyclopentadiene reacts with maleic anhydride under thermal conditions, which stereochemical outcome is observed and why?
The reaction proceeds via a concerted suprafacial‑suprafacial [4+2] cycloaddition. Maleic anhydride is a dienophile with two electron‑withdrawing carbonyl groups locked in a cis configuration. In the transition state, the carbonyl π‑systems align under the diene π‑cloud, giving secondary orbital overlap that stabilises the endo approach. Consequently, the newly formed bicyclic adduct has the anhydride bridge oriented endo (syn to the newly formed bridgehead hydrogens). The product is the endo‑bicyclo[2.2.1]hept‑5‑ene‑2,3‑dicarboxylic anhydride. This outcome is a classic illustration of the endo rule and is the major kinetic product; the exo adduct is thermodynamically more stable but forms slowly.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the endo rule and the Woodward–Hoffmann selection rules for pericyclic reactions; they appear frequently in mechanism elucidation questions. When a Diels–Alder problem is presented, first identify the diene and dienophile substitution pattern, then apply the endo preference unless steric bulk overwhelmingly favours the exo pathway.

Metal‑Catalyzed C–H Activation of Simple Alkanes

Direct functionalisation of inert C–H bonds in alkanes remains a central challenge. The Shilov system (Pt(II)/Pt(IV) in aqueous solution) demonstrated that methane can be oxidised to methanol via electrophilic platinum(II) coordination to the C–H bond, followed by oxidation to Pt(IV) and reductive elimination. Modern catalysts operate through several mechanistic manifolds: oxidative addition (common for late‑transition metals such as Ir, Rh, Pd), σ‑bond metathesis (early‑transition metals and f‑block elements), and concerted metalation‑deprotonation (CMD) assisted by a basic ligand. For example, Cp*Rh(III) complexes activate methane via a CMD pathway where an acetate ligand abstracts the proton while the Rh–C bond forms. In contrast, Pd(II)/Pd(0) cycles often involve oxidative addition of a C–H bond to Pd(0) generated in situ, followed by functionalisation and reductive elimination.

Selectivity is governed by a combination of bond dissociation energies, steric accessibility, and directing groups. In the absence of directing groups, primary C–H bonds of terminal alkanes are often favoured statistically, but catalyst design can invert this preference. For instance, Fe‑based catalysts with bulky N‑heterocyclic carbene ligands have been reported to show enhanced selectivity for secondary C–H bonds via a radical rebound mechanism. The reaction conditions (oxidant, solvent, temperature) critically influence the oxidation state of the metal and the nature of the active species. Over‑oxidation to CO₂ is a common side reaction when strong oxidants (e.g., H₂O₂, O₂) are used without careful control. Recent advances employ photocatalytic hydrogen‑atom transfer (HAT) to generate alkyl radicals that are subsequently trapped by a metal centre, merging radical and organometallic pathways.

Representative Catalytic Cycles

  • Shilov Pt(II)/Pt(IV): electrophilic C–H cleavage → Pt(IV)‑alkyl → oxidation → reductive elimination to alcohol.
  • Cp*Rh(III)/acetate: CMD C–H activation → Rh‑alkyl → functionalisation (e.g., carbonylation) → reductive elimination.
  • Pd(0)/Pd(II): oxidative addition of C–H to Pd(0) → Pd(II)‑alkyl → transmetalation / insertion → reductive elimination.
  • Fe‑NHC/HAT: photo‑generated alkoxy radical abstracts H → alkyl radical → Fe‑alkyl → functionalisation.

Kinetic versus Thermodynamic Control in Electrophilic Addition to Conjugated Dienes

Conjugated dienes undergo electrophilic addition at two distinct positions: 1,2‑addition (kinetic product) and 1,4‑addition (thermodynamic product). The reaction proceeds via a resonance‑stabilised allylic carbocation intermediate. At low temperatures (e.g., –78 °C), the nucleophile attacks the more substituted carbon of the allylic cation (the 1,2‑position) because this pathway has a lower activation barrier; the product distribution reflects kinetic control. As temperature rises, reversible formation of the allylic cation allows equilibration, and the more substituted, more stable 1,4‑adduct predominates (thermodynamic control). For addition of HBr to 1,3‑butadiene, the 1,2‑adduct (3‑bromo‑1‑butene) forms rapidly at –78 °C, whereas warming to 25 °C shifts the ratio toward the 1,4‑adduct (1‑bromo‑2‑butene). The presence of peroxides initiates a radical chain that gives anti‑Markovnikov 1,2‑addition exclusively, bypassing the carbocation manifold.

Substituent effects modulate the energy gap between the two pathways. Electron‑donating groups on the diene increase the stability of the allylic cation, lowering the barrier for both additions but often enhancing the thermodynamic preference for the 1,4‑product. Conversely, electron‑withdrawing groups destabilise the cation, making the kinetic 1,2‑pathway relatively more competitive. Solvent polarity also influences the lifetime of the carbocation; polar solvents stabilise the ion, facilitating equilibration and thermodynamic control. In synthetic planning, chemists exploit this dichotomy: low‑temperature addition delivers the kinetically favoured regioisomer for further functionalisation, while thermodynamic conditions provide the more substituted, often more stable, alkene.

Hyperconjugation and Stabilization of Carbocations, Radicals, and Alkenes

Hyperconjugation describes the delocalisation of electrons from a filled σ‑bond (usually C–H or C–C) into an adjacent empty or partially filled p‑orbital or π‑system. In carbocations, the vacant p‑orbital on the positively charged carbon accepts electron density from neighbouring σ‑bonds, lowering the energy of the cation. The stabilisation follows the order tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl, reflecting the number of β‑hydrogen atoms available for donation. Each β‑hydrogen contributes on the order of 1–2 kcal mol⁻¹ of stabilisation, a value estimated by comparing heats of hydrogenation of isomeric alkenes or by measuring rates of solvolysis of alkyl halides. In radicals, the singly occupied p‑orbital similarly benefits from σ‑bond donation, which explains why tertiary radicals are more persistent than primary ones. For alkenes, hyperconjugation between the π‑bond and adjacent σ‑bonds accounts for the increased stability of more substituted double bonds; the heat of hydrogenation decreases by about 2–3 kcal mol⁻¹ per alkyl substituent. Experimental probes include the measurement of kinetic isotope effects when β‑hydrogens are replaced by deuterium; a normal secondary KIE (k_H/k_D ≈ 1.1–1.3) supports hyperconjugative involvement in the rate‑determining step. Computational analyses (NBO, NRT) visualise the donor‑acceptor interactions as delocalisation energies that correlate with observed reactivity trends. Understanding hyperconjugation is essential for predicting the outcome of carbocation rearrangements, the regioselectivity of electrophilic additions, and the relative stability of reaction intermediates in hydrocarbon chemistry.

Solvent and Medium Effects on Reaction Pathways

The polarity, proticity, and hydrogen‑bonding ability of a solvent can dramatically alter the mechanism and selectivity of hydrocarbon reactions. In polar protic solvents (water, alcohols), carbocations are strongly stabilised by solvation, which lowers the activation barrier for ionisation steps and favours SN1 or E1 pathways. Conversely, polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMSO, DMF) stabilise anions but not cations, accelerating SN2 reactions and disfavouring carbocation formation. Non‑polar solvents (hexane, benzene) provide minimal stabilisation for charged species; reactions that proceed through radical or concerted pathways dominate under these conditions. The dielectric constant (ε) quantifies the solvent’s ability to screen charge; a high ε reduces the energy of ion pairs and can shift an equilibrium from a tight ion pair to a solvent‑separated ion pair, influencing product distribution in electrophilic additions. Specific hydrogen‑bond donation can also coordinate to heteroatoms in reagents, altering their electrophilicity; for example, the rate of bromination of alkenes in acetic acid is faster than in carbon tetrachloride because acetic acid hydrogen‑bonds to Br₂, polarising the Br–Br bond. In radical chain processes, solvent viscosity affects the diffusion‑controlled termination step; more viscous media increase the lifetime of radicals, often leading to higher molecular weight polymers in alkene polymerisation. Supercritical fluids (scCO₂) combine low polarity with high diffusivity, enabling unique selectivity in hydrogenation and oxidation reactions. When designing a synthetic sequence, chemists must match the solvent to the desired mechanistic pathway: polar protic for carbocation‑mediated rearrangements, polar aprotic for nucleophilic substitutions, and non‑polar or supercritical media for radical or pericyclic processes. Ignoring solvent effects is a frequent source of error in mechanistic predictions and in the interpretation of kinetic data.

Mechanistic Diagnosis Using Solvent Variation
A student observes that the addition of HBr to 1‑methylcyclohexene gives predominantly the Markovnikov product in acetic acid, but a mixture of Markovnikov and anti‑Markovnikov products in carbon tetrachloride with a peroxide initiator. Explain the mechanistic origin of the solvent‑dependent selectivity.
In acetic acid (polar protic), HBr is fully ionised to H⁺ and Br⁻; the proton adds to the alkene to give the more stable tertiary carbocation, which is rapidly trapped by Br⁻ → Markovnikov product. In carbon tetrachloride (non‑polar), HBr remains largely molecular; the peroxide initiates a radical chain: RO· abstracts H from HBr giving Br·, which adds to the alkene at the less substituted carbon (anti‑Markovnikov) because the resulting carbon‑centred radical is more stable (tertiary). The radical then abstracts H from another HBr molecule, propagating the chain. Thus solvent polarity dictates whether the reaction proceeds via an ionic (carbocation) or radical pathway, and the peroxide provides the radical initiator only in the non‑polar medium.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the effect of changing solvent, first identify the charge development in the rate‑determining step. If a carbocation forms, polar protic solvents accelerate the reaction; if an anion or radical is involved, polar aprotic or non‑polar solvents are favoured. Sketch a quick energy diagram to visualise the stabilisation.

Kinetic Isotope Effects as Mechanistic Probes

Kinetic isotope effects (KIEs) arise when a bond to a light isotope (¹H, ¹²C) is broken or formed in the rate‑determining step, and the same bond to a heavier isotope (²H, ¹³C) reacts more slowly. A primary KIE (k_H/k_D ≈ 2–7) indicates that the C–H (or C–D) bond is cleaved in the transition state, typical for hydrogen‑atom abstraction, proton transfer, or β‑hydride elimination. A secondary KIE (k_H/k_D ≈ 1.0–1.3) reflects a change in hybridisation or hyperconjugation without bond rupture; for example, the conversion of sp³ to sp² at a carbon bearing deuterium gives a normal secondary KIE because the C–D bond is slightly stronger in the sp³ state. In hydrocarbon chemistry, KIEs discriminate between concerted and stepwise pathways: the Diels–Alder reaction shows a negligible primary KIE because no C–H bonds are broken, whereas a radical H‑atom transfer from an alkane to a chlorine atom exhibits a large primary KIE (≈ 6). The magnitude of the KIE also informs on the transition‑state structure; a “late” transition state (product‑like) generally gives a larger KIE than an “early” one. Temperature dependence of the KIE (Arrhenius plots for each isotopologue) can reveal tunnelling contributions, especially at low temperatures where H‑atom tunnelling becomes significant. Experimental determination typically involves parallel reactions of isotopically labelled substrates under identical conditions, followed by quantitative analysis (GC‑MS, NMR). Care must be taken to avoid isotope‑exchange side reactions that scramble the label. In mechanistic studies of alkane C–H activation, a primary KIE > 3 strongly supports a rate‑determining C–H cleavage step, whereas a KIE ≈ 1 suggests a pre‑equilibrium or a non‑C–H‑breaking rate‑determining step. Mastery of KIE interpretation enables the student to distinguish between electrophilic aromatic substitution (no primary KIE) and benzylic oxidation (large primary KIE) in exam problems.

Stereoelectronic Requirements in E2 Elimination Reactions

The E2 elimination proceeds through a single concerted transition state in which the base abstracts a β‑hydrogen while the leaving group departs from the α‑carbon. A strict stereoelectronic requirement is that the C–H bond being broken and the C–LG bond being broken must be anti‑periplanar (dihedral angle ≈ 180°) to allow optimal orbital overlap between the σ_C–H orbital and the σ*_C–LG orbital. In acyclic systems, rotation about the C–C bond can achieve the anti‑periplanar geometry, but the conformational distribution influences the rate; the more stable conformer that places the β‑hydrogen anti to the leaving group reacts fastest. In cyclic systems, the requirement is rigid: only axial β‑hydrogens on a chair‑conformed cyclohexane can be anti‑periplanar to an axial leaving group. Consequently, elimination from a trans‑diaxial arrangement is rapid, whereas a diequatorial or axial‑equatorial arrangement is slow or does not occur. This principle explains why neopentyl halides (no β‑hydrogen anti to the leaving group) resist E2 elimination, and why the Hofmann product (less substituted alkene) can dominate when the more substituted β‑hydrogen cannot achieve the anti geometry. The base strength and steric bulk also modulate selectivity: bulky bases (t‑BuOK) favour abstraction of the more accessible, less hindered β‑hydrogen, often leading to the Hofmann alkene even when the Zaitsev alkene is thermodynamically favoured. In deuterium‑labelled substrates, the anti‑periplanar requirement manifests as a large primary KIE only for the correctly aligned hydrogen, providing a diagnostic tool. Understanding these stereoelectronic constraints is essential for predicting elimination outcomes in complex polycyclic substrates and for designing synthetic routes that exploit or avoid E2 pathways.

Catalytic Hydrogenation: Heterogeneous and Homogeneous Mechanisms

Catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes and alkynes converts π‑bonds to σ‑bonds using H₂ and a metal catalyst. Heterogeneous catalysts (Pd/C, PtO₂, Raney Ni) operate by adsorption of H₂ onto the metal surface, dissociative chemisorption to give surface metal‑hydride species, and subsequent syn‑addition of two hydrogen atoms to the same face of the coordinated alkene. The reaction is typically irreversible, proceeds under mild pressure (1–5 atm), and shows little sensitivity to substrate stereochemistry beyond steric hindrance; cis‑alkenes hydrogenate faster than trans‑alkenes because they bind more strongly to the surface. Poisoning agents (quinoline, sulfur compounds) can modulate activity and selectivity, enabling partial hydrogenation of alkynes to cis‑alkenes (Lindlar catalyst: Pd/CaCO₃ poisoned with lead acetate and quinoline). Homogeneous catalysts (Wilkinson’s catalyst RhCl(PPh₃)₃, Crabtree’s catalyst [Ir(cod)(PCy₃)(py)]PF₆) follow a well‑defined organometallic cycle: oxidative addition of H₂ to a low‑valent metal centre gives a dihydride complex; alkene coordination, migratory insertion into a metal‑hydride bond, and reductive elimination deliver the alkane and regenerate the catalyst. These systems allow precise control of enantioselectivity through chiral phosphine ligands, enabling asymmetric hydrogenation of prochiral alkenes (e.g., Noyori’s Ru‑BINAP catalysts for β‑ketoesters). The rate law for homogeneous hydrogenation is often first order in catalyst and H₂, and zero order in alkene at high substrate concentration, reflecting saturation of the metal centre. In contrast, heterogeneous hydrogenation rates depend on surface area, H₂ pressure, and mass‑transport limitations. Both manifolds are susceptible to catalyst deactivation: heterogeneous catalysts sinter at high temperature; homogeneous catalysts can undergo ligand dissociation or formation of inactive metal clusters. Knowledge of the distinct mechanistic steps, kinetic signatures, and selectivity trends equips the student to choose the appropriate catalytic system for a given synthetic target and to rationalise experimental observations in exam questions.

  • Assuming that a primary KIE always proves a concerted mechanism; a stepwise pathway with a rate‑determining C–H cleavage also gives a large primary KIE.
  • Neglecting the anti‑periplanar requirement in E2 eliminations and predicting Zaitsev products for substrates that cannot adopt the required geometry.
  • Treating solvent polarity as a simple “polar vs non‑polar” switch without considering specific hydrogen‑bonding or donor‑acceptor interactions that can change the reaction coordinate.
  • Confusing the syn‑addition of heterogeneous hydrogenation with the anti‑addition of dissolving‑metal reductions; the stereochemical outcome is opposite.
  • Overlooking that hyperconjugation stabilises both carbocations and radicals, leading to misassignment of the dominant intermediate in a reaction that could proceed via either pathway.
  • Applying the Lindlar catalyst to internal alkynes without recognising that it stops at the cis‑alkene; further hydrogenation requires a different catalyst.
  • Ignoring the effect of catalyst poisoning on heterogeneous hydrogenation rates, which can be exploited for selective partial reductions.

These advanced concepts integrate physical organic principles with practical synthetic decision‑making, providing a robust framework for tackling complex hydrocarbon mechanism problems.
