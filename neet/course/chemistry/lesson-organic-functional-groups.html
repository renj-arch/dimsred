Haloalkanes: Structure, Nomenclature, and Physical Properties

1.1 General Structure and Classification

Haloalkanes are organic compounds in which one or more hydrogen atoms of an alkane are replaced by halogen atoms such as fluorine, chlorine, bromine, or iodine. The carbon–halogen bond is polar because halogen is more electronegative than carbon, giving the carbon a partial positive charge that makes it susceptible to nucleophilic attack. Depending on the number of halogen substituents, haloalkanes are classified as mono‑, di‑, tri‑, or poly‑halogenated derivatives, and the carbon bearing the halogen can be primary, secondary, or tertiary, which strongly influences reactivity.

The IUPAC nomenclature treats the halogen as a substituent prefix (fluoro‑, chloro‑, bromo‑, iodo‑) with the locant indicating the carbon atom bearing the halogen. For example, CH₃CH₂Cl is named chloroethane, while (CH₃)₃CCl is 2‑chloro‑2‑methylpropane. When multiple halogens are present, prefixes such as dichloro‑ or trichloro‑ are used, and the lowest set of locants rule determines the numbering of the parent chain.

1.2 Physical Properties and Trends

Haloalkanes are generally colourless liquids or low‑melting solids with densities greater than water for the heavier halogens (bromine, iodine). Boiling points increase with molecular weight and with the size of the halogen because larger halogen atoms increase van der Waals forces; thus iodoalkanes boil higher than the corresponding bromo‑, chloro‑, and fluoro‑alkanes. The polarity of the C–X bond also contributes to dipole‑dipole interactions, making haloalkanes more polar than the parent alkanes, yet they remain largely insoluble in water because the hydrocarbon framework dominates the intermolecular forces.

Solubility in organic solvents such as ether, benzene, or chloroform is high, which is why haloalkanes are frequently used as solvents or reaction media. The C–F bond is exceptionally strong (≈ 485 kJ mol⁻¹) and short, giving fluoroalkanes unusually high thermal stability and low reactivity compared with the other halogen analogues. In contrast, the C–I bond is the weakest (≈ 240 kJ mol⁻¹) and longest, making iodoalkanes the most reactive toward nucleophilic substitution and elimination.

1.3 Spectroscopic Identification

In infrared spectroscopy, the C–X stretching vibrations appear in the region 500–800 cm⁻¹, with the exact position shifting to lower wavenumbers as the halogen becomes heavier. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy shows a deshielding effect on the carbon bearing the halogen; the ¹³C signal moves downfield by 20–40 ppm relative to the corresponding alkane carbon. Proton NMR of the α‑hydrogen(s) also shifts downfield (δ ≈ 3.5–4.5 ppm) because of the electron‑withdrawing halogen, providing a convenient diagnostic tool for structural confirmation.

Preparation of Haloalkanes

2.1 Halogenation of Alkanes via Free‑Radical Mechanism

Direct halogenation of alkanes with Cl₂ or Br₂ under UV light or high temperature proceeds through a free‑radical chain mechanism. Initiation generates halogen radicals, propagation abstracts a hydrogen atom from the alkane to give an alkyl radical, which then captures a halogen atom to form the haloalkane. The reaction is not selective; a mixture of mono‑, di‑, and poly‑halogenated products is obtained, and the relative reactivity of hydrogen atoms follows the order tertiary > secondary > primary, reflecting the stability of the intermediate alkyl radicals.

Because of the lack of selectivity, this method is mainly used for simple alkanes such as methane (producing chloromethane, dichloromethane, chloroform, and carbon tetrachloride) or for industrial production of chloro‑ and bromo‑alkanes where separation of isomers is feasible. Fluorination is too violent for direct radical halogenation, while iodination is thermodynamically unfavorable and does not proceed under normal conditions.

2.2 Halogen Exchange Reactions (Finkelstein and Swarts)

The Finkelstein reaction converts an alkyl chloride or bromide to the corresponding iodide by treatment with sodium iodide in dry acetone. The equilibrium is driven forward because sodium chloride or sodium bromide precipitates out of the polar aprotic solvent, while sodium iodide remains soluble. This method is especially valuable for preparing primary and secondary alkyl iodides, which are otherwise difficult to obtain directly.

The Swarts reaction achieves the opposite transformation, converting alkyl chlorides or bromides to fluorides using metallic fluorides such as AgF, Hg₂F₂, or SbF₃, often in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst. The reaction proceeds via an SN2 pathway for primary substrates and is the principal laboratory route to fluoroalkanes, which are otherwise inaccessible by direct fluorination.

2.3 Preparation from Alcohols Using Hydrogen Halides and Phosphorus Halides

Alcohols react with concentrated hydrogen halides (HX) to give haloalkanes; the reaction follows an SN1 mechanism for tertiary alcohols and an SN2 mechanism for primary alcohols. The Lucas test (ZnCl₂/HCl) distinguishes alcohol classes by the rate of cloudiness formation: tertiary alcohols react instantly, secondary within a few minutes, and primary hardly at all at room temperature.

Phosphorus tribromide (PBr₃) and phosphorus pentachloride (PCl₅) provide milder, non‑acidic conditions for converting alcohols to bromides and chlorides respectively. The reaction proceeds through formation of a good leaving group (alkyl phosphite or alkyl chlorophosphate) which is then displaced by halide ion. Thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) is particularly advantageous because the by‑products (SO₂ and HCl) are gases, simplifying work‑up and giving clean yields of alkyl chlorides.

2.4 Preparation from Alkenes via Addition of HX and Halogen Addition

Hydrogen halides add across the double bond of alkenes following Markovnikov’s rule: the hydrogen attaches to the carbon bearing more hydrogens, and the halogen attaches to the more substituted carbon, generating the more stable carbocation intermediate. In the presence of peroxides, HBr adds anti‑Markovnikov via a radical chain mechanism (Kharasch addition), a rare example of peroxide effect limited to HBr.

Halogen addition (Cl₂, Br₂) to alkenes yields vicinal dihalides through a cyclic halonium ion intermediate. The reaction is stereospecifically anti, giving trans‑dihalides from cyclic alkenes. This method is useful for preparing 1,2‑dihaloalkanes which can be further transformed into alkynes by double elimination.

Worked Example: Selecting the Best Method to Prepare 2‑Bromo‑2‑methylpropane
A student needs to prepare 2‑bromo‑2‑methylpropane (tert‑butyl bromide) in high yield. Which of the following reagents would be most appropriate and why? (a) Br₂/UV light, (b) HBr/peroxides, (c) PBr₃, (d) conc. HBr.
The target molecule is a tertiary bromide. Direct free‑radical bromination (a) would give a mixture of products because the tertiary hydrogen is only one of several equivalent positions in the starting alkane (isobutane). Anti‑Markovnikov addition of HBr (b) is irrelevant because there is no alkene precursor. PBr₃ (c) reacts with alcohols, not alkanes. Concentrated HBr (d) reacts rapidly with the corresponding tertiary alcohol (tert‑butanol) via an SN1 pathway to give the tertiary bromide in excellent yield. Therefore, the best route is to start from tert‑butanol and treat it with conc. HBr.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the “most suitable reagent” to convert an alcohol to a halide, always match the alcohol class (primary, secondary, tertiary) with the mechanism: primary → SN2 reagents (PBr₃, SOCl₂, NaI/acetone), tertiary → SN1 reagents (conc. HX, Lucas reagent). The peroxide effect applies only to HBr addition to alkenes, never to alcohol substitution.

Nucleophilic Substitution and Elimination Reactions of Haloalkanes

3.1 SN1 Mechanism: Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution

The SN1 pathway proceeds in two steps: first, the carbon–halogen bond heterolytically cleaves to generate a carbocation and a halide ion; second, the nucleophile attacks the planar carbocation. The rate‑determining step is the formation of the carbocation, so the reaction rate depends only on the concentration of the haloalkane (first‑order kinetics). Tertiary haloalkanes react fastest because they form the most stable carbocations, while primary haloalkanes rarely undergo SN1 due to the instability of primary carbocations.

Solvent polarity strongly influences SN1 rates; polar protic solvents such as water, ethanol, or acetic acid stabilize both the leaving group and the carbocation through solvation, accelerating the reaction. Rearrangements (hydride or alkyl shifts) can occur if a more stable carbocation can be formed, leading to product mixtures that reflect the rearranged skeleton.

3.2 SN2 Mechanism: Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution

In the SN2 pathway, the nucleophile attacks the carbon bearing the leaving group from the backside in a single concerted step, resulting in inversion of configuration at a chiral centre (Walden inversion). The rate law is second order overall: rate = k[haloalkane][nucleophile]. Primary haloalkanes are the most reactive because steric hindrance is minimal, whereas tertiary haloalkanes are essentially inert to SN2 due to severe steric crowding around the electrophilic carbon.

Polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMSO, DMF) enhance SN2 rates by solvating cations but leaving the nucleophile relatively “naked” and more reactive. Strong nucleophiles such as I⁻, CN⁻, N₃⁻, and RS⁻ favour SN2, while weak nucleophiles (H₂O, ROH) tend to give SN1 or solvolysis products. The competition between SN1 and SN2 is governed by substrate structure, nucleophile strength, solvent, and temperature.

3.3 Elimination Reactions: E1 and E2 Pathways

Elimination of HX from haloalkanes yields alkenes. The E1 mechanism mirrors SN1: carbocation formation followed by deprotonation of a β‑hydrogen by a base. It is favoured by tertiary substrates, weak bases, and polar protic solvents. The E2 mechanism is a single concerted step where a strong base abstracts a β‑hydrogen while the halide leaves, requiring an anti‑periplanar geometry. E2 is favoured by primary substrates with strong, sterically hindered bases (e.g., t‑BuOK) and by high temperatures.

Zaitsev’s rule predicts that the more substituted alkene (the thermodynamically more stable) is the major product in both E1 and E2, unless a bulky base forces the formation of the less substituted Hofmann product. The stereochemical requirement of anti‑periplanar alignment in E2 often dictates which β‑hydrogen is removed, influencing the geometry (E/Z) of the resulting alkene.

3.4 Named Reactions Involving Haloalkanes

The Wurtz reaction couples two alkyl halides with sodium metal in dry ether to form a higher alkane (R–R). It is limited to symmetrical coupling because cross‑coupling gives mixtures. The Fittig reaction extends this idea to aryl halides, producing biaryl compounds. The Grignard reaction converts alkyl or aryl halides into organomagnesium reagents (RMgX) which serve as powerful nucleophiles and bases in synthesis. The Hofmann elimination, although typically applied to quaternary ammonium hydroxides, conceptually parallels the elimination of haloalkanes with bulky bases to give the less substituted alkene.

Alcohols and Phenols: Structure, Preparation, and Acidic Behavior

4.1 Classification and Nomenclature of Alcohols

Alcohols contain a hydroxyl group (–OH) attached to an sp³ hybridised carbon. They are classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary according to the number of carbon atoms attached to the carbon bearing the –OH group. IUPAC names replace the terminal “‑e” of the parent alkane with “‑ol” and indicate the locant of the hydroxyl group; for example, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH is propan‑1‑ol, while (CH₃)₂CHOH is propan‑2‑ol. Polyhydric alcohols (diols, triols) receive suffixes “‑diol”, “‑triol” with appropriate locants.

Phenols differ structurally because the –OH group is attached directly to an sp² carbon of an aromatic ring. This conjugation delocalises the lone pair on oxygen into the ring, increasing acidity dramatically compared with aliphatic alcohols. The simplest phenol is hydroxybenzene (C₆H₅OH); substituted phenols are named as derivatives of phenol (e.g., 2‑methylphenol, 4‑nitrophenol) or by the hydroxy prefix on a benzene parent (e.g., 3‑hydroxybenzoic acid).

4.2 Preparation of Alcohols

Alcohols are most commonly prepared by nucleophilic addition to carbonyl compounds. Reduction of aldehydes with NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ gives primary alcohols; reduction of ketones yields secondary alcohols. Grignard reagents (RMgX) add to formaldehyde, aldehydes, ketones, esters, and carbon dioxide, after acidic work‑up providing primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols, and carboxylic acids respectively. Hydroboration‑oxidation of alkenes (BH₃·THF, H₂O₂/NaOH) gives anti‑Markovnikov alcohols with syn stereochemistry.

Industrial routes include hydration of alkenes (direct addition of water in the presence of acid catalyst) and the oxo process (hydroformylation of alkenes with CO/H₂ followed by reduction). Fermentation of sugars by yeast produces ethanol on a large scale. Phenols are industrially produced by the cumene process: cumene (isopropylbenzene) is oxidised to cumene hydroperoxide, which on acid cleavage yields phenol and acetone.

4.3 Acidic Properties and Reactions of Alcohols and Phenols

The acidity of alcohols (pKₐ ≈ 16–18) is comparable to water; they react with strong bases such as NaH or Na metal to give alkoxides (RO⁻) and hydrogen gas. Alkoxides are strong nucleophiles and bases, used in Williamson ether synthesis and in elimination reactions. Phenols are considerably more acidic (pKₐ ≈ 10) because the phenoxide ion is resonance‑stabilised over the aromatic ring. Consequently, phenols dissolve in aqueous NaOH to give sodium phenoxide, but they do not react with NaHCO₃.

Esterification of alcohols with carboxylic acids under acid catalysis (Fischer esterification) is reversible; removal of water drives the reaction forward. Phenols undergo esterification more readily with acid chlorides or anhydrides (Schotten–Baumann conditions) because the phenoxide is a better nucleophile than the neutral phenol. Both alcohols and phenols can be converted to alkyl halides using HX, PX₃, or SOCl₂, but phenols require more forcing conditions due to the partial double‑bond character of the C–O bond.

4.4 Oxidation and Dehydration Reactions

Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes (with PCC, DMP, or Swern reagent) and further to carboxylic acids (with KMnO₄, CrO₃/H₂SO₄). Secondary alcohols give ketones under the same oxidants; tertiary alcohols resist oxidation because there is no α‑hydrogen. Dehydration of alcohols with concentrated H₂SO₄ or Al₂O₃ at elevated temperature yields alkenes following Zaitsev’s rule; the reaction proceeds via an E1 mechanism for secondary and tertiary alcohols, while primary alcohols require higher temperatures and often give rearranged products.

Phenols undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution readily because the –OH group activates the ring ortho/para. Nitration, sulfonation, halogenation, and Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation occur under milder conditions than for benzene. The Reimer–Tiemann reaction (CHCl₃/NaOH) formylates phenols ortho to the –OH group, giving salicylaldehyde after acid work‑up. The Kolbe reaction (phenoxide + CO₂ under pressure) yields salicylic acid, a key precursor to aspirin.

Ethers and Amines: Synthesis, Basicity, and Important Named Reactions

5.1 Structure, Nomenclature, and Physical Properties of Ethers

Ethers contain an oxygen atom bonded to two carbon groups (R–O–R′). They are named by listing the alkyl groups alphabetically followed by “ether” (e.g., ethyl methyl ether) or, in IUPAC, as alkoxyalkanes (e.g., methoxyethane). The C–O–C bond angle is approximately 111°, and the oxygen bears two lone pairs, making ethers Lewis bases. Ethers have relatively low boiling points compared with isomeric alcohols because they cannot hydrogen‑bond to each other; they are, however, good solvents for many organic reactions due to their ability to solvate cations while leaving anions relatively free.

Cyclic ethers such as tetrahydrofuran (THF) and dioxane are widely used as polar aprotic solvents. Epoxides (oxiranes) are three‑membered cyclic ethers with high ring strain, rendering them highly reactive toward nucleophilic ring‑opening under both acidic and basic conditions.

5.2 Preparation of Ethers

The Williamson ether synthesis is the most general method: an alkoxide (generated in situ from an alcohol and NaH or Na metal) undergoes SN2 attack on a primary alkyl halide or tosylate. The reaction fails with secondary or tertiary halides because elimination competes. Symmetrical ethers can also be prepared by acid‑catalysed dehydration of two alcohol molecules (e.g., 2 CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃ + H₂O) but this is limited to primary alcohols and gives low yields for unsymmetrical ethers.

Industrial production of diethyl ether uses the vapor‑phase dehydration of ethanol over a solid acid catalyst (γ‑Al₂O₃) at 140 °C. Methyl tert‑butyl ether (MTBE) is made by acid‑catalysed addition of methanol to isobutylene. Epoxides are prepared by peracid oxidation of alkenes (Prilezhaev reaction) using m‑CPBA or peracetic acid.

5.3 Structure, Basicity, and Preparation of Amines

Amines are derivatives of ammonia in which one, two, or three hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups, giving primary (RNH₂), secondary (R₂NH), and tertiary (R₃N) amines. Quaternary ammonium salts (R₄N⁺X⁻) have a positively charged nitrogen with four carbon substituents. The nitrogen lone pair makes amines basic (pKₐ of conjugate acid ≈ 10–11 for aliphatic amines) and nucleophilic. Aromatic amines (anilines) are far less basic (pKₐ ≈ 4.6) because the lone pair is delocalised into the ring.

Aliphatic amines are prepared by nucleophilic substitution of alkyl halides with ammonia (excess NH₃ gives primary amine; further alkylation yields mixtures). The Gabriel phthalimide synthesis avoids over‑alkylation: phthalimide anion reacts with an alkyl halide, and subsequent hydrazinolysis or base hydrolysis releases the primary amine. Reductive amination of aldehydes or ketones with ammonia or amines in the presence of NaBH₃CN or H₂/Pd gives secondary or tertiary amines cleanly. The Hofmann rearrangement converts primary amides to primary amines with one fewer carbon using Br₂/NaOH.

5.4 Important Named Reactions of Amines and Ethers

The Hofmann elimination treats a quaternary ammonium hydroxide (formed from a tertiary amine and excess CH₃I followed by Ag₂O/H₂O) with heat to give the least substituted alkene (Hofmann product) via an E2 mechanism that favours removal of the most accessible β‑hydrogen. The Curtius rearrangement involves thermal decomposition of an acyl azide to an isocyanate, which upon hydrolysis yields a primary amine with loss of CO₂. The Schmidt reaction uses hydrazoic acid (HN₃) with a carboxylic acid under strong acid to give an amine after rearrangement.

For ethers, the cleavage of ethers with strong acids (HI, HBr) proceeds via SN2 at the less hindered alkyl group for primary ethers, while tertiary ethers cleave via SN1 to give a tertiary carbocation. The Claisen rearrangement is a [3,3]‑sigmatropic rearrangement of allyl vinyl ethers to γ,δ‑unsaturated carbonyl compounds, a powerful C–C bond‑forming reaction. The Mitsunobu reaction (DIAD/PPh₃) converts alcohols into ethers (or esters) with inversion of configuration, using a nucleophile such as a carboxylate or phenoxide.
