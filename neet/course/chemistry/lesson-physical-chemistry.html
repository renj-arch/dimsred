1. Mole Concept & Stoichiometry

Mole and Avogadro's Number

A mole is defined as the amount of substance that contains exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 elementary entities (Avogadro's number, NA). This fundamental constant bridges the microscopic world of atoms and molecules with the macroscopic world of grams. One mole of any substance contains the same number of particles as 12 g of pure carbon-12. The mole concept is the cornerstone of all quantitative chemistry, allowing chemists to count particles by weighing.

n = mass / molar mass = N / NA = V(gas at STP) / 22.4 L = M × V(L)
Important: 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L at STP (273 K, 1 atm). At NTP (293 K, 1 atm): 24.05 L. At SATP (298 K, 1 bar): 24.79 L. NEET typically uses STP conditions.

Calculations involving moles

Key relations: Number of molecules = n × NA. Number of atoms = n × NA × atomicity. For ionic compounds, formula units replace molecules. Number of electrons = n × NA × electrons per particle.

Example 1: Mole to Particles
Calculate the number of molecules and total number of atoms in 36 g of water. (NA = 6.022 × 1023)
(a) 6.022×1023, 18.066×1023   (b) 1.204×1024, 3.613×1024   (c) 3.011×1023, 9.033×1023   (d) 2.408×1024, 7.224×1024
Solution: Molar mass H2O = 18 g/mol. Moles = 36/18 = 2 mol. Molecules = 2 × 6.022 × 1023 = 1.204 × 1024. Each H2O has 3 atoms. Total atoms = 3 × 1.204 × 1024 = 3.613 × 1024. Option (b).
Example 2: Mass from Atom Count
What is the mass of 3.011 × 1023 atoms of oxygen? (Atomic mass O = 16 g/mol)
(a) 4 g   (b) 8 g   (c) 16 g   (d) 32 g
Solution: Moles O = 3.011 × 1023 / 6.022 × 1023 = 0.5 mol. Mass = 0.5 × 16 = 8 g. Option (b).
NEET Shortcut: Quick Mass from Count
Dividing given number of particles by 6.022 × 1023 gives moles directly. For quick estimates, 6 × 1023 ≈ NA. So 3 × 1023 atoms ≈ 0.5 mol.

Percentage Yield & Purity

Actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained from a reaction. Theoretical yield is the maximum possible amount calculated from stoichiometry. Percentage yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100. Percentage purity = (Mass of pure substance / Mass of impure sample) × 100. These concepts are frequently tested in NEET numerical problems involving industrial processes.

Example 2C: Percentage Yield
In the reaction CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, 50 g of CaCO3 (100% pure) gives 22 g of CaO. What is the percentage yield? (Ca=40, C=12, O=16)
(a) 78.6%   (b) 88.2%   (c) 92.4%   (d) 68.5%
Solution: Moles CaCO3 = 50/100 = 0.5 mol. Theoretical CaO = 0.5 mol = 0.5 × 56 = 28 g. % yield = (22/28) × 100 = 78.57%. Option (a).
Yield vs Purity
Don't confuse yield (how much product formed vs theoretical) with purity (how much pure substance in sample). An impure reactant affects theoretical yield calculation. Always convert to moles of pure substance first!

Percentage Composition

Percentage composition gives the mass percentage of each element in a compound. Mass % of element = (mass of element in 1 mol / molar mass) × 100. This is used to verify the purity of a sample and is the starting point for empirical formula determination.

Example 2B: Percentage Composition
Calculate the percentage of water in CuSO4·5H2O. (Cu=63.5, S=32, O=16, H=1)
(a) 36%   (b) 28%   (c) 32%   (d) 40%
Solution: Molar mass = 63.5+32+64+5(18) = 249.5 g/mol. Mass of water = 5×18 = 90 g. % = (90/249.5)×100 = 36.07%. Option (a).

Example 2D: Molecular Formula from Combustion Data
0.30 g of an organic compound on complete combustion gives 0.44 g CO2 and 0.18 g H2O. Its molecular mass is 60. Find the molecular formula (C=12, H=1, O=16).
(a) C2H4O2   (b) CH2O   (c) C3H6O   (d) C2H6O
Solution: Mass of C = (12/44) × 0.44 = 0.12 g. Mass of H = (2/18) × 0.18 = 0.02 g. Mass of O = 0.30 - 0.12 - 0.02 = 0.16 g. Moles: C=0.01, H=0.02, O=0.01. Ratio: C:H:O = 1:2:1. EF = CH2O (mass=30). n=60/30=2. MF = C2H4O2. Option (a). This is acetic acid!

Stoichiometry & Limiting Reagent

Stoichiometry uses balanced equations to relate amounts of reactants/products. For aA + bB → cC: moles of A / a = moles of B / b = moles of C / c. The limiting reagent is the reactant consumed first, determined by dividing given moles by stoichiometric coefficient — the smallest ratio is limiting.

Example 4: Limiting Reagent
N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). 28 g N2 mixed with 10 g H2. Find limiting reagent and mass of NH3 formed. (N=14, H=1)
(a) N2 limiting, 34 g   (b) H2 limiting, 34 g   (c) N2 limiting, 17 g   (d) H2 limiting, 56.7 g
Solution: Moles N2 = 28/28 = 1. Moles H2 = 10/2 = 5. N2 ratio = 1/1 = 1. H2 ratio = 5/3 = 1.67. Since 1 < 1.67, N2 is limiting. 1 mol N2 → 2 mol NH3 = 34 g. Option (a).
Limiting Reagent Trick
Always compare moles / coefficient. Smallest ratio = limiting. Never compare raw moles when coefficients differ. This is the #1 mistake in NEET stoichiometry problems.

The atom consists of three fundamental particles discovered over time: electron (J.J. Thomson, 1897), proton (Rutherford, 1911), and neutron (Chadwick, 1932). Their properties determine atomic behavior.

Photoelectric Effect

Einstein explained the photoelectric effect (1905) using Planck's quantum theory: hν = φ + KEmax, where φ = work function (minimum energy to eject electron), KEmax = ½mv2 = hν - φ. Threshold frequency ν0 = φ/h. Below ν0, no electrons are ejected regardless of intensity. This demonstrated the particle nature of light (photons) and won Einstein the Nobel Prize.

Atomic Spectra

When atoms absorb energy, electrons jump to higher energy levels. When they return, they emit photons of specific wavelengths, producing line spectra. Hydrogen spectrum: Lyman series (n1=1, UV), Balmer series (n1=2, visible), Paschen series (n1=3, IR), Brackett (n1=4), Pfund (n1=5). Rydberg formula: 1/λ = RH(1/n12 - 1/n22), where RH = 109677 cm-1 = 1.097 × 107 m-1. For He+ (Z=2): 1/λ = Z2RH(1/n12 - 1/n22).

NEET fact: Balmer series (visible) has n1=2. The first line of Balmer (H-α) is red (n=3→2), second is green-blue (n=4→2), third is violet (n=5→2). Only four lines of Balmer are visible to the naked eye.

Atomic Models Timeline

Thomson (1904): Plum pudding — electrons in positive sphere. Rutherford (1911): Nuclear model — gold foil experiment showed most mass in tiny positive nucleus. Bohr (1913): Fixed circular orbits with quantized energy En = -13.6/n2 eV. Limitations of Bohr: Failed for multi-electron atoms, couldn't explain Zeeman effect, Stark effect, or fine structure.

Quantum Mechanical Model

Developed by Schrödinger, Heisenberg, Dirac. Electrons are described by wave functions (ψ). |ψ|2 gives probability density. Orbitals are regions of high probability (typically > 90%). Each orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons (Pauli).

Quantum Numbers

Four quantum numbers uniquely identify every electron in an atom. No two electrons can share the same set of four (Pauli's exclusion principle).

Which set is NOT allowed?

"PAMS": Principal, Azimuthal, Magnetic, Spin. Think of it like a mailing address: country (n), city (l), street (ml), house number (ms).

Aufbau, Pauli, Hund's Rules

Aufbau: Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing (n+l). If equal, lower n first. Sequence: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p. Pauli: Max 2 electrons per orbital with opposite spins. Hund: Fill degenerate orbitals singly first (parallel spins) before pairing.

Exceptions: Cr (24): [Ar] 4s1 3d5 (not 4s2 3d4). Cu (29): [Ar] 4s1 3d10 (not 4s2 3d9). Half-filled and fully-filled d subshells have extra stability.

Electronic Configuration

Notation: n lelectron count. Examples: Na (11): 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1. Fe (26): [Ar] 4s2 3d6. Use the periodic table block method: s-block = ns1-2, p-block = ns2 np1-6, d-block = (n-1)d1-10 ns1-2, f-block = (n-2)f1-14.

Write Configurations Fast
Locate element on periodic table. Period = n for s/p block. Group tells electrons. For d-block: period = n, but d-subshell = (n-1). Just follow the blocks left to right, top to bottom!

de Broglie & Heisenberg Principle

de Broglie (1924): All moving particles have wavelength λ = h/p = h/(mv). For an electron accelerated through V volts: λ = h/√(2meV) = 12.27/√V Å. Heisenberg (1927): Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π. Simultaneous precise measurement of position and momentum is impossible for microscopic particles.

λ = h/mv    Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π    λ(Å) = 12.27/√V (for electron)
Example 6: de Broglie Wavelength
de Broglie wavelength of electron accelerated through 100 V? (h=6.626×10-34 Js, me=9.1×10-31 kg)
(a) 1.227 Å   (b) 0.1227 Å   (c) 12.27 Å   (d) 0.01227 Å
Solution: λ = 12.27/√V = 12.27/√100 = 12.27/10 = 1.227 Å. Option (a).
Memorize: de Broglie for Electron
λ (in Å) = 12.27 / √V. Just memorize this. No need to derive each time in NEET. Works for any accelerating potential V in volts.

3. Chemical Bonding & Molecular Structure

Ionic Bond

Ionic bonding involves complete electron transfer from a metal (electropositive) to a non-metal (electronegative), resulting in electrostatic attraction. Ionic compounds have high melting points, are soluble in polar solvents, and conduct electricity when molten or aqueous. Lattice energy is the energy released when gaseous ions form one mole of ionic solid.

Fajan's Rules: Covalent character increases when: (1) small cation, (2) large anion, (3) high charge. Example: AlCl3 > MgCl2 > NaCl in covalent character. LiI is more covalent than LiF.

Covalent Bond

Covalent bonds involve sharing of electron pairs. Lewis dot structures represent valence electrons. Octet rule: atoms achieve 8 valence electrons (exceptions: H, Li, Be, B, and expanded octet for period 3+). Bond order ∝ bond strength ∝ 1/bond length.

Bond Parameters

Bond length: Distance between nuclei of bonded atoms. Decreases with bond order (C≡C 1.20 Å < C=C 1.34 Å < C-C 1.54 Å). Bond angle: Angle between two bonds from the same atom. Bond energy: Energy required to break one mole of bonds in gaseous state. σ-bonds are stronger than π-bonds. Bond order: Number of bonds between two atoms. Higher bond order = shorter, stronger bond.

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals: bonding (lower energy) and antibonding (higher energy, marked *). Bond order = ½(Nb - Na). BO > 0 indicates stable molecule. BO = 0 or negative means molecule does not exist.

Bond Order = ½ (bonding e- - antibonding e-)
MO order for O2, F2, Ne2: σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < σ2pz < π2px=π2py < π*2px=π*2py < σ*2pz. For B2, C2, N2: π2p orbitals are below σ2pz. O2 is paramagnetic (2 unpaired e- in π*2p) — MOT correctly predicts this while VBT fails.
MOT Memory Trick
"Before Oxygen" (B2, C2, N2): π2p is below σ2p. "Oxygen onward" (O2, F2, Ne2): σ2p is below π2p. The dividing line is between N2 and O2.

Dipole Moment

Dipole moment (μ = q × d) measures bond polarity. Unit: Debye (D). Net dipole is vector sum of bond dipoles. Symmetrical molecules have μ=0: CO2, BF3, CCl4, CH4, BeCl2, PCl5, SF6. Asymmetric molecules have μ>0: H2O, NH3, CHCl3, CH3Cl, HCl.

Symmetry & Dipole
If a molecule has a center of symmetry or identical bonds arranged symmetrically, net μ = 0. CCl4 has μ = 0, but CHCl3 has μ > 0 because replacing one Cl with H breaks symmetry.

Kinetic Theory of Gases

Postulates: molecules in random motion, perfectly elastic collisions, no intermolecular forces, KE ∝ T. The average kinetic energy per mole = (3/2)RT. Per molecule = (3/2)kT (k = R/NA = 1.38 × 10-23 J/K). At a given temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy, but lighter molecules move faster.

Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shows the distribution of molecular speeds at a given temperature. As temperature increases, the curve broadens and shifts right (more molecules at higher speeds). The area under the curve = total number of molecules. The peak corresponds to the most probable speed.

Speed equations: vmp = √(2RT/M), vavg = √(8RT/πM), vrms = √(3RT/M). Order: vrms > vavg > vmp. Ratio: √3 : √(8/π) : √2 ≈ 1.225 : 1.128 : 1. These speeds increase with temperature and decrease with molecular mass. At a given T, lighter gases move faster (Graham's law of effusion).

KEavg = (3/2)RT per mole   vrms : vavg : vmp = 1.225 : 1.128 : 1

Combined Gas Law & Density Calculations

The three gas laws can be combined into: P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2 (for fixed n). For changing moles, use: P1V1/n1T1 = P2V2/n2T2 = R (universal gas constant). Gas density: ρ = PM/RT (from PV = nRT = (m/M)RT). This is useful for calculating density at different conditions.

Van der Waals Equation (Real Gases)

(P + an2/V2)(V - nb) = nRT   Z = PV/nRT

For ideal gas Z=1. Z<1: attraction dominates (negative deviation). Z>1: repulsion dominates (positive deviation). Boyle temperature: TB = a/Rb where real gas behaves ideally over a wide pressure range.

Van der Waals Constants Trend
'a' increases with molecular size and intermolecular attraction (larger for non-polar molecules with more electrons). 'b' increases with molecular size. For He: a is very small, b is very small (near ideal). For NH3: a is large (strong H-bonding).

Liquid State

Liquids have definite volume but no fixed shape. Key properties: Vapour pressure: Pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. It increases with temperature and depends on intermolecular forces (stronger forces = lower VP). Boiling occurs when VP equals atmospheric pressure. Surface tension: Force per unit length on the surface, caused by inward pull of molecules. Unit: N/m. Decreases with temperature. Viscosity: Internal resistance to flow. Unit: poise. Decreases with temperature (unlike gases). Newtonian vs non-Newtonian: Water and organic solvents are Newtonian (constant viscosity). Polymer solutions and colloidal dispersions are non-Newtonian.

Trouton's rule: For many non-polar liquids, ΔHvap/Tb ≈ 85 J/mol-K. This is an approximation; polar liquids (with H-bonding) deviate significantly. Water has a high ΔHvap (40.7 kJ/mol at 100°C) due to extensive hydrogen bonding.

Crystal Defects

Real crystals have imperfections. Point defects: (1) Schottky defect: vacancy of equal numbers of cations and anions, occurs in NaCl, KCl; decreases density. (2) Frenkel defect: cation moves to interstitial site leaving vacancy, occurs in AgCl, ZnS; no change in density. (3) Interstitial defect: extra atom in interstitial space. (4) Substitutional impurity: foreign atom replaces lattice atom. Line defects: Dislocations (edge, screw) that affect mechanical properties.

Bragg's Law: nλ = 2d sinθ. Used in X-ray crystallography to determine crystal structure. d = interplanar spacing, θ = angle of incidence, λ = wavelength of X-rays, n = order of reflection.

Electrical & Magnetic Properties

Conductors: Metals conduct due to delocalized electrons. Conductivity decreases with T. Semiconductors: Si, Ge - conductivity increases with T (negative temperature coefficient). Insulators: Diamond - no free electrons. Doping: Adding impurities to enhance conductivity. n-type (P, As, Sb added to Si - extra electrons). p-type (B, Al, Ga added to Si - electron holes). Magnetic types: Paramagnetic (unpaired e-, attracted weakly), Diamagnetic (all paired, repelled), Ferromagnetic (permanent magnetism - Fe, Co, Ni), Antiferromagnetic (MnO), Ferrimagnetic (Fe3O4).

Second Law & Entropy

The second law states that the entropy of the universe always increases in spontaneous processes. Entropy is a measure of disorder or randomness. ΔSuniverse = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0 for spontaneous change. For reversible processes, ΔStotal = 0. For irreversible (real) processes, ΔStotal > 0.

ΔS = qrev/T   ΔS° = ΣS°(products) - ΣS°(reactants)

Entropy trends: Sgas >> Sliquid > Ssolid. Entropy increases with temperature, molecular complexity, and number of particles. Phase changes: ΔSfusion > 0, ΔSvaporization >> 0. Dissolution generally increases entropy. Third Law: The entropy of a perfectly crystalline substance is zero at absolute zero (0 K). This allows absolute entropies (S°) to be determined experimentally.

Gibbs Free Energy & Spontaneity

Gibbs free energy (G = H - TS) determines spontaneity at constant T and P. ΔG < 0: spontaneous. ΔG = 0: equilibrium. ΔG > 0: non-spontaneous.

6. Chemical Equilibrium

Van't Hoff Equation (Temperature Dependence of K)

The equilibrium constant changes with temperature according to the van't Hoff equation: d(ln K)/dT = ΔH/RT2. Integrated form: ln(K2/K1) = -(ΔH/R)(1/T2 - 1/T1), or log(K2/K1) = (ΔH/2.303R)(1/T1 - 1/T2). For exothermic reactions (ΔH < 0), K decreases with increasing T. For endothermic (ΔH > 0), K increases with T. This is consistent with Le Chatelier's principle.

Relation Between Kp and Kc

Kp = Kc(RT)Δng. For reactions with no change in moles of gas (Δng = 0): Kp = Kc. Example: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇄ 2HI(g). For reactions with increase in gas moles (Δng > 0): Kp > Kc. Example: PCl5(g) ⇄ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g). For decrease (Δng < 0): Kp < Kc. Example: N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇄ 2NH3(g).

Units of K: Kc has units (mol/L)Δn. Kp has units (atm)Δn. In thermodynamic treatment, K is dimensionless (using activities). For NEET, you can use concentrations/pressures directly in the expression.

Degree of Dissociation & Vapour Density

For gaseous reactions involving dissociation (like PCl5 ⇄ PCl3 + Cl2), the degree of dissociation α is related to vapour density: α = (D - d) / (n-1)d, where D = theoretical VD before dissociation, d = observed VD at equilibrium, n = number of moles after dissociation per mole before dissociation. For PCl5 (n=2): α = (D-d)/d, and Kp = α2P/(1-α2) where P = total pressure.

Law of Mass Action & K

For aA + bB ⇄ cC + dD at equilibrium: Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b. Kp = Kc(RT)Δng. Pure solids and liquids are omitted. K depends ONLY on temperature.

Kp = Kc(RT)Δng   Δng = (c+d) - (a+b)

Reaction quotient Q: Same form as K but at any time. Q < K: forward. Q > K: reverse. Q = K: equilibrium.

Le Chatelier's Principle

If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to counteract the disturbance. Concentration: adding reactant shifts forward. Pressure: increasing P favors side with fewer gas moles. Temperature: increasing T favors endothermic direction. Catalyst: does NOT change K or composition, only speeds up attainment.

Equilibrium Shift Mnemonic
"CPT": Concentration, Pressure, Temperature. Think: Counteract the Perturbation by Tilting equilibrium. Remember Haber: high pressure, moderate temp, Fe catalyst.

10. Solutions & Colligative Properties

Henry's Law

Henry's law states that at constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid. m = KH × P, where m = mass of gas dissolved, KH = Henry's constant, P = partial pressure. Alternatively, P = KH × χ (mole fraction in solution). Applications: Carbonated drinks (CO2 under high P), scuba diving (N2 dissolves in blood at high P, causes bends on rapid ascent), altitude sickness (low O2 partial pressure at high altitude).

KH trends: KH increases with temperature (gas solubility decreases as T increases - opposite to solids!). KH also depends on the nature of gas and solvent. Higher KH means lower solubility.

Raoult's Law & Vapour Pressure

For volatile liquids: PA = PA°χA, PB = PB°χB. PT = PA + PB. Ideal solutions: obey Raoult's law at all concentrations, ΔHmix=0, ΔVmix=0 (benzene-toluene, n-hexane-n-heptane, CCl4-SiCl4).

Positive deviation (ethanol-water, acetone-ethanol): weaker A-B interactions than A-A and B-B, leading to higher VP than ideal. ΔHmix > 0, ΔVmix > 0. Negative deviation (acetone-chloroform, phenol-aniline, CHCl3-CH3COCH3): stronger A-B interactions, lower VP than ideal. ΔHmix < 0, ΔVmix < 0.

Azeotropes

An azeotrope is a mixture that boils at constant composition, like a pure substance. Minimum boiling azeotropes (positive deviation): ethanol-water (95.4% ethanol, bp 78.2°C). Maximum boiling azeotropes (negative deviation): HCl-water (20.2% HCl, bp 108.6°C), HNO3-water (68% HNO3, bp 120.5°C). Azeotropes cannot be separated by simple distillation.

Raoult's law for non-volatile solute: Psolution = Psolvent° × χsolvent. Relative lowering of VP = (P° - P)/P° = χsolute. This is the basis for determining molar mass of non-volatile solutes.

Van't Hoff Factor (i)

Accounts for dissociation/association: ΔTf = iKfm, ΔTb = iKbm, π = iCRT.

i = actual particles / formula units   For dissociation: i = 1 + (n-1)α   For association: i = 1 - (1-1/n)α

Examples: Glucose (i=1, non-electrolyte). NaCl (i=2). BaCl2 (i=3). Al2(SO4)3 (i=5). Benzoic acid dimerization in benzene: i → 0.5 for complete dimerization.

Abnormal Molar Mass
Observed molar mass = Normal molar mass / i. If i > 1 (dissociation), observed M < normal M. If i < 1 (association), observed M > normal M.

11. Redox Reactions

Oxidation Number & Rules

Oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if electrons were transferred completely. Key rules: (1) Free elements = 0. (2) Monatomic ions = charge. (3) H = +1 (except metal hydrides: -1). (4) O = -2 (except peroxides: -1, OF2: +2). (5) Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2. (6) Halogens = -1 (except with O or higher halogen). (7) Sum in neutral compound = 0, in ion = charge.

Example R1: Finding Oxidation Number
Oxidation number of Cr in K2Cr2O7?
(a) +3   (b) +4   (c) +5   (d) +6
Solution: 2(+1) + 2(Cr) + 7(-2) = 0. 2 + 2x - 14 = 0. 2x = 12. x = +6. Option (d).
Example R2: Oxidation Number in Ion
Oxidation number of S in SO42-?
(a) +4   (b) +5   (c) +6   (d) +7
Solution: x + 4(-2) = -2. x - 8 = -2. x = +6. Option (c).

Balancing Redox Reactions

Redox reactions involve simultaneous oxidation (loss of electrons, increase in ON) and reduction (gain of electrons, decrease in ON).

Oxidation Number Method

Steps: (1) Assign ON to all atoms. (2) Identify oxidation and reduction half. (3) Calculate change in ON per atom. (4) Multiply to equalize total change. (5) Balance atoms other than H and O. (6) Balance O by adding H2O. (7) Balance H by adding H+ (acidic) or OH- (basic). (8) Charge balance.

Example R3: Balancing by ON Method
Balance: Cu + HNO3 → Cu(NO3)2 + NO + H2O
(a) 3Cu + 8HNO3 → 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO + 4H2O
(b) Cu + 4HNO3 → Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO + 2H2O
(c) 2Cu + 6HNO3 → 2Cu(NO3)2 + NO + 3H2O
(d) 3Cu + 4HNO3 → 3Cu(NO3)2 + NO + 2H2O
Solution: Cu: 0 → +2 (oxidation, ΔON = 2). N in HNO3: +5 → N in NO: +2 (reduction, ΔON = 3). Multiply Cu by 3, N by 2: 3Cu + 2HNO3 (reduced) → 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO. Balance remaining atoms: 8HNO3 total (6 for nitrate + 2 reduced) → 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO + 4H2O. Option (a).

Ion-Electron (Half-Reaction) Method

Steps: (1) Write separate half-reactions. (2) Balance atoms other than H and O. (3) Balance O by adding H2O. (4) Balance H by adding H+ (acidic) or H2O + OH- (basic). (5) Balance charge by adding e-. (6) Multiply to equalize e-. (7) Add half-reactions.

Example R4: Ion-Electron Method (Acidic)
Balance in acidic medium: MnO4- + Fe2+ → Mn2+ + Fe3+
(a) MnO4- + 5Fe2+ + 8H+ → Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O
(b) MnO4- + 3Fe2+ + 4H+ → Mn2+ + 3Fe3+ + 2H2O
(c) MnO4- + Fe2+ + 2H+ → Mn2+ + Fe3+ + H2O
(d) MnO4- + 4Fe2+ + 4H+ → Mn2+ + 4Fe3+ + 2H2O
Solution: Reduction half: MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- → Mn2+ + 4H2O. Oxidation half: Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e-. Multiply oxidation by 5 and add: MnO4- + 5Fe2+ + 8H+ → Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O. Option (a).
Example R5: Balancing in Basic Medium
Balance in basic medium: Cr(OH)3 + IO3- → I- + CrO42-
(a) 2Cr(OH)3 + IO3- + 4OH- → I- + 2CrO42- + 5H2O
(b) Cr(OH)3 + IO3- + 2OH- → I- + CrO42- + 2H2O
(c) 3Cr(OH)3 + IO3- + 6OH- → I- + 3CrO42- + 6H2O
(d) 2Cr(OH)3 + IO3- + 2OH- → I- + 2CrO42- + 3H2O
Solution: Oxidation: Cr(OH)3 + 5OH- → CrO42- + 4H2O + 3e-. Reduction: IO3- + 3H2O + 6e- → I- + 6OH-. Multiply oxidation by 2 and add. Option (a).
Redox Balancing Shortcuts
In acidic medium, add H+ and H2O. In basic medium, add OH- and H2O (or balance as if acidic, then convert OH- = H2O - H+). The ion-electron method is preferred for NEET as it's systematic.

Adsorption Isotherms

Factors Affecting Adsorption

Surface area: Greater surface area of adsorbent = more adsorption (activated charcoal has high surface area). Nature of adsorbate: More easily liquefiable gases (higher critical temperature) adsorb more. SO2 > NH3 > CO2 > CH4 > H2 (order of adsorption on charcoal). Nature of adsorbent: Polar adsorbents (silica gel, alumina) adsorb polar molecules; non-polar adsorbents (charcoal) adsorb non-polar molecules.

For the adsorption of a gas on charcoal, the Freundlich isotherm is x/m = kP1/3. At P = 1 atm, x/m = 0.5 g/g. What is x/m at P = 8 atm?

Properties & Applications

Tyndall effect: Light scattering by colloidal particles due to size comparable to light wavelength. The Tyndall cone is visible when a beam of light passes through a colloid. Brownian motion: Random zigzag motion caused by bombardment by solvent molecules. Prevents sedimentation under gravity. Electrophoresis: Charged particles move towards oppositely charged electrode under applied electric field. Used for electrodeposition (paint spraying, rubber coating). Electro-osmosis: Movement of dispersion medium when colloidal particles are immobilized.

Coagulation or precipitation: Destabilization of colloids by: (1) Adding electrolytes - ions neutralize particle charge. (2) Heating - increases kinetic energy, reduces solvation. (3) Adding another colloid with opposite charge - mutual coagulation. (4) Electrophoresis - particles migrate and discharge at electrode. Hardy-Schulze rule: The ion with higher charge is more effective at coagulating. For As2S3 (negative colloid): Al3+ > Ba2+ > Na+. For Fe(OH)3 (positive colloid): [Fe(CN)6]4- > SO42- > Cl-. Coagulation value: Millimoles of electrolyte needed to coagulate 1 L of colloid. Lower value = higher coagulating power.

Protection of colloids: Lyophilic colloids (like gelatin) protect lyophobic colloids from coagulation. Gold number: The number of milligrams of protective colloid required to prevent the coagulation of 10 mL of gold sol when 1 mL of 10% NaCl is added. Smaller gold number = better protective power. Gelatin (0.005-0.01), gum arabic (0.15-0.25), starch (~25).

Purification: (1) Dialysis - passes ions and small molecules through semipermeable membrane but retains colloids. Used for artificial kidney. (2) Electro-dialysis - accelerates dialysis using electric field. (3) Ultra-filtration - filters through special membranes with fine pores (colloidal particles pass through, larger particles are retained). (4) Ultra-centrifugation - high-speed centrifugation separates particles by density.

Important applications of colloids: (1) Sewage treatment - Al2(SO4)3 (alum) coagulates colloidal impurities. (2) Delta formation - river water colloids coagulate in seawater. (3) Rubber extraction - latex is coagulated by adding acetic acid. (4) Blue color of sky - scattering of sunlight by colloidal dust particles. (5) Blood coagulation - Ce4+ or Al3+ salts stop bleeding. (6) Cottrell precipitator - electrostatic precipitation of industrial smoke.

Emulsions & Applications

Colloids Key Points
Gold numbers measure protective power of lyophilic colloids. Smaller gold number = better protection. Example: gelatin has very small gold number (high protective power).

Emulsions & Applications

Oil-in-water (O/W): Oil droplets dispersed in water. Examples: milk (butterfat in water), vanishing cream. Stabilized by hydrophilic emulsifiers. Water-in-oil (W/O): Water droplets dispersed in oil. Examples: butter, cold cream. Stabilized by hydrophobic emulsifiers. Emulsifiers are substances that stabilize emulsions by reducing interfacial tension. Soap and detergents are common emulsifiers (hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tail).

Pharmaceutical applications: Colloidal medicines are more easily absorbed. Argyrol (colloidal silver) is an antiseptic. Colloidal gold is used in diagnostics. Industrial: Sewage treatment uses Al2(SO4)3 (alum) to coagulate colloidal impurities. Delta formation occurs when river water (colloidal clay) meets seawater (electrolytes), causing coagulation.

14. NEET Problem-Solving & Common Pitfalls

Dos and Don'ts for Physical Chemistry

DO: Unit Consistency
Always convert all quantities to consistent units. For the ideal gas equation: P in atm, V in L, n in mol, T in K, R = 0.0821 L-atm/mol-K. For SI: P in Pa, V in m³, R = 8.314 J/mol-K. For density: convert pm to cm (1 pm = 10⁻¹² cm).
DON'T: Incorrect Mole Ratio
Never compare raw moles of reactants when coefficients differ. Always divide by stoichiometric coefficient to find limiting reagent. N2 + 3H2: if you have 1 mol each, N2 ratio = 1/1 = 1, H2 ratio = 1/3 = 0.33. H2 is limiting, not N2!
DO: Sign Conventions in Thermodynamics
q > 0 = heat absorbed (endothermic). q < 0 = heat released (exothermic). w > 0 = work done on system (compression). w < 0 = work done by system (expansion). ΔU = q + w. For ΔG: negative = spontaneous. For E°: positive = spontaneous.
DON'T: Confusing Ksp with Solubility
Higher Ksp does NOT always mean higher solubility when comparing different formula types. Compare s (solubility), not Ksp. For AgCl (Ksp = 1.8×10⁻¹⁰, s = 1.3×10⁻⁵ M) and Ag2CrO4 (Ksp = 1.1×10⁻¹², s = 6.5×10⁻⁵ M), Ag2CrO4 has higher solubility despite smaller Ksp!
DO: Log Values to Remember
log 2 = 0.30, log 3 = 0.48, log 5 = 0.70, log 7 = 0.85. These are essential for pH and Nernst equation calculations. π = 3.14, √2 = 1.414, √3 = 1.732. R = 8.314 J/mol-K, 1/2.303R = 1/19.147 = 0.0522.
DON'T: Forgetting Units in Rate Constants
k units depend on overall order. Zero order: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. First order: s⁻¹. Second order: L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹. If k has units of s⁻¹, the reaction MUST be first order. If mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, it's zero order.

Additional Solved Examples for Practice

Example D: Mole Fraction from Molality
Calculate the mole fraction of glucose in 1 m aqueous glucose solution.
(a) 0.018   (b) 0.0177   (c) 0.016   (d) 0.015
Solution: 1 m = 1 mol glucose in 1 kg water. nglucose = 1. nwater = 1000/18 = 55.56. χglucose = 1/(1+55.56) = 1/56.56 = 0.0177. Option (b).
Example E: Equilibrium Constant from Concentrations
For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇄ 2HI(g), initial [H2] = [I2] = 0.1 M. At equilibrium, [HI] = 0.16 M. Find Kc.
(a) 64   (b) 32   (c) 128   (d) 256
Solution: H2 consumed = I2 consumed = 0.16/2 = 0.08 M. At eq: [H2] = [I2] = 0.1-0.08 = 0.02 M, [HI] = 0.16 M. Kc = (0.16)²/(0.02×0.02) = 0.0256/0.0004 = 64. Option (a).
Example F: pH of Salt Hydrolysis
Calculate the pH of 0.1 M CH3COONa solution. Ka of CH3COOH = 1.8 × 10-5.
(a) 8.87   (b) 9.47   (c) 8.35   (d) 7.85
Solution: Salt of weak acid + strong base. pH = 7 + ½(pKa + log C). pKa = -log(1.8×10-5) = 4.74. pH = 7 + ½(4.74 + log 0.1) = 7 + ½(4.74 - 1) = 7 + ½(3.74) = 7 + 1.87 = 8.87. Option (a).

Mass percentage = (Mass of component/Total mass) × 100

Mole fraction: xA = nA/(nA+nB)

Molality (m) = moles of solute/Mass of solvent (kg)

NEET Tip: Molarity depends on temperature (volume changes with T). Molality does NOT depend on temperature — use molality for boiling point/elevation problems.

▶ 2. Atomic Structure (Chemistry)

Planck's quantum: E = hν = hc/λ

Bohr radius: rn = 0.529·n²/Z Å

Energy: En = −13.6·Z²/n² eV/atom = −1312·Z²/n² kJ/mol

Velocity: vn = 2.18×10⁶·Z/n m/s

Wavelength (de Broglie): λ = h/mv

Heisenberg Uncertainty: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π

Quantum numbers: n (1,2,3...), l (0 to n−1), ml (−l to +l), ms (+½, −½)

Maximum electrons in shell = 2n², in subshell = 2(2l+1)

Aufbau: electrons fill lowest energy orbitals first (n+l rule)

Hund's: each orbital in subshell gets 1 electron before pairing

Pauli: no two electrons have all 4 QN same

NEET Tip: n+l rule: lower n+l → lower energy. If n+l same, lower n → lower energy. 4s (4+0=4) fills before 3d (3+2=5).

▶ 3. Chemical Bonding

Octet rule: atoms tend to have 8 electrons in valence shell

Ionic bond: complete electron transfer (Na⁺Cl⁻). Lattice energy ∝ q₁q₂/r

Covalent bond: electron sharing (single, double, triple). Bond length: triple < double < single

Bond order = (Nb − Na)/2. Higher BO → shorter, stronger bond

Dipole moment: μ = q×d (debye units). Vector sum for molecules

Fajan's rules: covalent character ↑ with small cation, large anion, high charge

Hybridization: sp (linear, 180°), sp² (trigonal planar, 120°), sp³ (tetrahedral, 109°28'), sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal), sp³d² (octahedral)

VSEPR: lone pairs repel more than bond pairs → geometry adjusts

Molecular orbital theory: Bond order = (bonding − antibonding e⁻)/2

Paramagnetic: unpaired electrons present. Diamagnetic: all paired

NEET Tip: Bond order of O₂ = 2 (paramagnetic!). N₂ = 3 (diamagnetic, strongest diatomic bond).

▶ 4. Thermodynamics (Chemistry)

ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT

ΔH°reaction = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)

ΔH = Σ(Bond energies of reactants) − Σ(Bond energies of products)

Hess's Law: Enthalpy change depends only on initial and final states, not path

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

ΔG° = −RT lnK = −2.303RT logK

At equilibrium: ΔG = 0 (spontaneous when ΔG < 0)

Gibbs free energy: ΔG = ΔG° + RT lnQ (Q = reaction quotient)

Temperature for spontaneous reaction: ΔG < 0 → T > ΔH/ΔS (if ΔH>0, ΔS>0)

NEET Tip: For a reaction to be spontaneous at all temperatures, ΔH < 0 and ΔS > 0. For non-spontaneous at all temperatures, ΔH > 0 and ΔS < 0.

▶ 5. Equilibrium

Kc = [C]c[D]d/[A]a[B]b (for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD)

Kp = Kc(RT)Δn

Relation between K: Kreverse = 1/Kforward, Kmultiply by n = (K)n

Le Chatelier: stress (T↑, P↑, conc↑) shifts equilibrium to oppose the change

For exothermic (ΔH<0): T↑ → equilibrium shifts left (backward)

For endothermic (ΔH>0): T↑ → equilibrium shifts right (forward)

Pressure change affects only if Δng ≠ 0


Acid-Base: pH = −log[H⁺], pOH = −log[OH⁻], pH + pOH = 14

Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C

Ka × Kb = Kw for conjugate pairs

Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log[salt/acid], pOH = pKb + log[salt/base]

Buffer capacity = (d[base]/d(pH)) — maximum when [salt]/[acid] = 1

Solubility product: Ksp = [An+]m[Bm−]n for AnBm

Ionic product > Ksp → precipitate forms. IP < Ksp → no precipitate

NEET Tip: For strong acid + strong base titration, pH at equivalence = 7. For weak acid + strong base, pH > 7 at equivalence. For strong acid + weak base, pH < 7.

▶ 6. Electrochemistry

Conductivity: κ = 1/ρ (ρ = resistivity). Molar conductivity: Λm = κ/C

Λm = Λm° − A√C (Kohlrausch's law for strong electrolytes)

Λm° = λ°cation + λ°anion (Kohlrausch's law of independent migration)

Faraday's 1st: m ∝ Q (mass deposited ∝ charge passed)

Faraday's 2nd: m = (M/nF)·I·t (M = molar mass, n = n-factor, F = 96500 C/mol)

1 Faraday = 96500 C = charge on 1 mole of electrons

Cell EMF: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Nernst equation: E = E° − (0.0591/n) logQ (at 25°C)

E = E° − (RT/nF) lnQ → for non-standard conditions

ΔG° = −nFE°cell

Relation: E°cell = (RT/nF) lnKeq = (0.0591/n) logKeq

Electrolytic cell: electrical energy → chemical energy (non-spontaneous)

Galvanic cell: chemical energy → electrical energy (spontaneous)

NEET Tip: More positive E°red = stronger oxidizing agent (easier to reduce). Lower (more negative) E°red = stronger reducing agent.

▶ 7. Chemical Kinetics

Rate = −d[R]/dt = k[R]n (n = order)

Zero order: [R] = [R]₀ − kt. t½ = [R]₀/2k. Unit of k: mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹

First order: log[R] = log[R]₀ − kt/2.303. t½ = 0.693/k. Unit of k: s⁻¹

Second order: 1/[R] = 1/[R]₀ + kt. t½ = 1/k[R]₀. Unit of k: L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹

Arrhenius equation: k = Ae−Ea/RT

log(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/2.303R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)

Pseudo-first order: when one reactant is in large excess, order appears as 1

Molecularity: number of molecules colliding (always whole number). Order can be fractional.

Activation energy: minimum energy required for reaction. Catalyst lowers Ea.

NEET Tip: For first order: 99.9% completion = 10 half-lives. 93.75% = 4 half-lives. 50% = 1 half-life. Graph of log[R] vs t is straight line with slope = −k/2.303.

▶ 8. Solutions & Colligative Properties

Raoult's Law: PA = xA·P°A (vapour pressure of A in solution)

Ptotal = xAP°A + xBP°B (for miscible liquids)

Ideal solution: ΔHmix = 0, ΔVmix = 0, follows Raoult's law

Non-ideal: +ve deviation (ΔH>0, ΔV>0, P>Raoult), −ve deviation (ΔH<0, ΔV<0, P<Raoult)


Colligative properties (depend only on number of solute particles):

Relative lowering of VP: ΔP/P° = xsolute = nsolute/(nsolvent+nsolute)

Elevation in BP: ΔTb = Kb·m (Kb = ebullioscopic constant)

Depression in FP: ΔTf = Kf·m (Kf = cryoscopic constant)

Osmotic pressure: π = iCRT (C = molar concentration)

van't Hoff factor: i = (observed colligative property)/(theoretical value)

For dissociation: i > 1. For association: i < 1.

Degree of dissociation: α = (i−1)/(n−1)

Abnormal molar mass: Mobserved = Mtheoretical/i

NEET Tip: For electrolytes, use i × C instead of C in colligative formulas. For NaCl, i ≈ 2 (dissociates into Na⁺ + Cl⁻). For CaCl₂, i ≈ 3.

▶ 9. Surface Chemistry

Adsorption: accumulation of molecules on surface. Desorption: removal.

Physisorption: weak van der Waals forces (reversible, multilayer, ΔH ∼ 20 kJ/mol)

Chemisorption: chemical bond formation (irreversible, monolayer, ΔH ∼ 200 kJ/mol)

Freundlich isotherm: x/m = k·P1/n or log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)log P

Langmuir isotherm: θ = KP/(1+KP) (monolayer adsorption)

Catalyst: increases rate without being consumed. Provides alternate path with lower Ea.

Enzyme catalysis: Lock and key model. Optimal pH and temperature. Michaelis-Menten kinetics.

Colloids: particle size 1-100 nm. Tyndall effect (scattering of light). Brownian motion.

Coagulation: neutralization of charge on colloid particles. Hardy-Schulze rule.

Gold number: protection power of lyophilic colloid (lower GN = better protection).

NEET Tip: Coagulation power follows Hardy-Schulze: ion with higher charge coagulates more effectively. Al³⁺ > Ba²⁺ > Na⁺ (for negative colloid).

⚠️ NEET Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

▶ Frequently Made Mistakes

❌ Molarity vs Molality: Molarity = moles/L of SOLUTION (T dependent). Molality = moles/kg of SOLVENT (T independent). NEET often tricks by asking which is temperature independent.

❌ ΔH vs ΔU: ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT. Remember this for reactions with gas evolution/consumption.

❌ Kp vs Kc: Kp = Kc(RT)Δn. If Δn = 0, Kp = Kc. Don't forget the exponent — many NEET questions test this.

❌ pH of strong acid/base: For 10⁻⁸ M HCl, pH ≈ 6.96 (NOT 8 — water's autoionization dominates at very low concentrations).

❌ Nernst equation log vs ln: At 25°C, E = E° − (0.0591/n) logQ. The constant 0.0591 = RT·ln10/F = (8.314×298×2.303)/96500.

❌ Order vs Molecularity: Order is experimental (can be fraction, zero). Molecularity is theoretical (always integer). Order can be > 3, molecularity rarely > 3.

❌ van't Hoff factor: For KCl, i = 2 (100% dissociated). For K₃[Fe(CN)₆], i = 4. Calculate: i = 1 + α(n−1).

❌ Electrochemical series: Li has most negative E° (strongest reducing agent). F₂ has most positive E° (strongest oxidizing agent).

⚡ For first order: t = (2.303/k) log(a/(a−x)).

⚡ For nth order (n≠1): t½ ∝ 1/[A]₀n−1
