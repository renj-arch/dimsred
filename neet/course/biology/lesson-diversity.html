The Living World

The living world is characterised by immense biodiversity. Millions of species exist on Earth, and new ones are still being discovered. Understanding, naming, and classifying this diversity is the foundation of biology.

What is Living?

Living organisms are distinguished from non-living by several defining features: Growth � increase in mass and number of cells (internal growth). Non-living things also grow (external accumulation). Reproduction � production of offspring (not a defining property for all � mules, worker bees are living but sterile). Metabolism � sum of all chemical reactions in the body (catabolic + anabolic). No non-living object shows metabolism. Cellular organisation � all living organisms are made of cells. Consciousness � ability to sense environment and respond to stimuli. The most basic feature of life is the presence of metabolism.

Diversity in the Living World

Approximately 1.7-1.8 million species have been described globally, but estimates of total species range from 5-50 million. India alone has about 45,000 plant species and 91,000 animal species, making it one of the 17 mega-diverse countries. Biodiversity is not uniform � tropical rainforests cover only 7% of Earth's land surface but harbour more than 50% of all species. To manage this immense diversity, we need robust systems of classification (grouping based on similarities), nomenclature (standardised naming), and identification (determining if an organism belongs to a known group).

Biodiversity Hotspots: Regions with high endemism and significant habitat loss. India has four hotspots: Western Ghats, Eastern Himalayas, Indo-Burma, and Sundaland. These areas are prioritised for conservation due to their rich but threatened biodiversity.

Species Concept: The Biological Species Concept (Ernst Mayr) defines a species as a group of interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups. Alternative concepts include the Morphological Species Concept (based on physical features) and the Phylogenetic Species Concept (based on evolutionary history).

Taxonomy & Nomenclature

Taxonomy

Taxonomy is the science of classification, naming, and identification of organisms. Systematics is a broader field that includes taxonomy and evolutionary relationships (phylogeny). The term was coined by Linnaeus, who is called the Father of Taxonomy.

Taxonomic Hierarchy

Organisms are classified in a hierarchical system with seven main ranks (descending order): Kingdom → Phylum (Division for plants) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. A mnemonic: King Philip Came Over For Good Soup. Species is the fundamental unit of classification � a group of organisms capable of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring (biological species concept by Ernst Mayr).

Binomial Nomenclature

Introduced by Carolus Linnaeus in his book Species Plantarum (1753). Rules: (i) Each organism has a two-word scientific name � Genus (capitalised) followed by species epithet (lowercase). (ii) Both words are italicised or underlined separately. (iii) The name of the author is written after the species in abbreviated form (e.g., Mangifera indica Linn.). Examples: Homo sapiens (human), Panthera tigris (tiger), Felis catus (domestic cat).

ICBN and ICZN: International Code of Botanical Nomenclature (ICBN) governs plant naming � now called International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants (ICN). International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN) governs animal naming. These codes ensure that each organism has a universally accepted, unique scientific name. Rules include: (i) priority � the first validly published name is accepted, (ii) type specimen � a specific specimen is designated as the name-bearing type, (iii) homonyms � identical names for different taxa are not allowed.

Taxonomic Categories Detail: Species is the basic unit (e.g., Panthera tigris). Related species are grouped into a Genus (e.g., Panthera includes tiger, lion, leopard). Related genera are grouped into a Family (e.g., Felidae � cats). Families into Order (e.g., Carnivora), Orders into Class (e.g., Mammalia), Classes into Phylum (e.g., Chordata), and Phyla into Kingdom (e.g., Animalia). Each rank is called a taxon (plural: taxa). The hierarchy helps in understanding evolutionary relationships and facilitates information retrieval.

Taxonomic Aids

Tools used to identify and classify organisms: Herbarium � collection of dried, pressed plant specimens on sheets. Botanical gardens � live plant collections for research. Museum � preserved animal and plant specimens. Zoological parks � live wild animal collections. Key � a taxonomic tool using contrasting characters for identification (usually dichotomous � two choices at each step). Flora � account of plants of a region. Monograph � detailed account of one taxonomic group. Manual � guide for identification of species.

Biological Classification

Early Classification Systems

Aristotle classified plants into herbs, shrubs, and trees, and animals based on red blood cells (Enaima vs Anaima). Linnaeus used a two-kingdom system (Plantae and Animalia) based on the presence of cell walls and mobility. However, this system had limitations � fungi, bacteria, and unicellular eukaryotes were difficult to place.

Five-Kingdom Classification (Whittaker, 1969)

R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the Five Kingdom system based on: (i) cell structure (prokaryotic/eukaryotic), (ii) body organisation (unicellular/multicellular), (iii) mode of nutrition (autotrophic/heterotrophic), (iv) reproduction, and (v) phylogenetic relationships. The five kingdoms are: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia.

Limitations of Five-Kingdom System: (1) Viruses are not placed anywhere. (2) Lichens (symbiotic associations) do not fit a single kingdom. (3) Unicellular algae are placed in both Protista and Plantae. (4) The distinction between Protista and other eukaryotic kingdoms is not always clear. (5) Multicellularity evolved independently in different kingdoms, making phylogeny-based classification challenging.

Kingdom Monera

All prokaryotic organisms. Characteristics: no membrane-bound nucleus or organelles, 70S ribosomes, cell wall of peptidoglycan (except Mycoplasma), asexual reproduction by binary fission. Includes: Bacteria, Archaebacteria, Cyanobacteria, Mycoplasma.

Archaebacteria

Live in extreme environments. Methanogens (methane-producing, found in marshy areas and cow gut � used in biogas production), Halophiles (salt-loving, in salt lakes), Thermoacidophiles (heat and acid-loving, in hot springs). Their cell walls lack peptidoglycan. Archaebacteria have unique membrane lipids (ether-linked isoprenoids) and their rRNA sequences differ significantly from other bacteria, leading some taxonomists (Carl Woese) to propose a separate domain � Archaea. They are not sensitive to antibiotics that target bacterial cell walls.

Bacteria

Based on shape: Cocci (spherical) � e.g., Streptococcus (chains), Staphylococcus (clusters); Bacilli (rod-shaped) � e.g., Bacillus subtilis, E. coli; Vibrio (comma-shaped) � e.g., Vibrio cholerae; Spirilla (spiral) � e.g., Spirillum volutans. Based on Gram staining: Gram-positive (thick peptidoglycan, stains purple) � e.g., Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Bacillus; Gram-negative (thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane, stains pink) � e.g., E. coli, Pseudomonas, Vibrio cholerae. Nutrition: autotrophic (photosynthetic � cyanobacteria, green sulphur bacteria; chemosynthetic � Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter) or heterotrophic (saprophytic � decomposers; parasitic � pathogens; symbiotic � Rhizobium). Reproduction: mainly binary fission (asexual), also conjugation (transfer of genetic material through pilus), transformation (uptake of free DNA), and transduction (DNA transfer via bacteriophage).

Cyanobacteria (Blue-Green Algae)

Photosynthetic prokaryotes with chlorophyll a and phycobilins. Some fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts. Examples: Nostoc, Anabaena, Oscillatoria, Spirulina (used as food supplement).

Mycoplasma

Smallest living cells (0.1-0.3 μm). Lack cell wall, so are pleomorphic (variable shape). Resistant to antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis (penicillin). Can survive without oxygen. Cause diseases like pleuropneumonia in cattle.

Which of the following lacks a cell wall?

Kingdom Protista

Unicellular eukaryotic organisms. Bridge between prokaryotes and multicellular eukaryotes. Mostly aquatic. They are the simplest eukaryotes and exhibit the greatest diversity in cell structure among all kingdoms. Reproduction: asexual (binary fission) and sexual (conjugation). Includes:

Chrysophytes: Diatoms (Bacillariophyceae) � have siliceous cell walls/frustules arranged in two halves (epitheca and hypotheca), chief producers in oceans (responsible for ~20-25% of global primary productivity), fossilised deposits form diatomaceous earth (used in polishing, filtration, insulation). Desmids � freshwater green algae with symmetrical cells. Dinoflagellates (Dinophyceae): Two flagella (one longitudinal, one transverse � creating a whirling motion), cell wall with cellulose plates, responsible for red tide (e.g., Gonyaulax � produces neurotoxins that accumulate in shellfish, causing paralytic shellfish poisoning). Some are bioluminescent (e.g., Noctiluca). Euglenoids (Euglenophyceae): Have both plant (photosynthetic chloroplasts with chlorophyll a and b) and animal (flagellum for movement, eyespot/stigma for light detection) features. Euglena is a connecting link between plants and animals. Cell wall absent � instead a pellicle (flexible outer covering). In the absence of light, they can become heterotrophic (absorb organic nutrients from surroundings). Slime moulds (Myxomycetes): Saprophytic, form plasmodium (multinucleate, amoeboid mass) during feeding stage, later form fruiting bodies with spores. They exhibit characteristics of both fungi and protozoans. Protozoans: Heterotrophic, unicellular, classified into Amoeboids (locomotion by pseudopodia, e.g., Amoeba proteus, Entamoeba histolytica � causes amoebic dysentery), Flagellates (locomotion by flagella, e.g., Trypanosoma gambiense � African sleeping sickness, Giardia lamblia � giardiasis, Leishmania donovani � Kala-azar), Ciliates (locomotion by cilia, e.g., Paramoecium caudatum � has two nuclei: micronucleus for reproduction and macronucleus for daily functions), Sporozoans (spore-forming, non-motile, e.g., Plasmodium vivax/vivax � causes malaria, transmitted by female Anopheles mosquito).

Kingdom Fungi

Heterotrophic eukaryotes with chitinous cell walls. Mostly multicellular (except yeast). Nutrition: saprophytic (decomposers), parasitic, or symbiotic (lichens, mycorrhiza). Body consists of thread-like hyphae (network called mycelium). Reproduction: asexual (spores, budding), sexual (fusion of gametes). Storage carbohydrate: glycogen.

Classification of Fungi

Phycomycetes (Algal fungi): Aseptate hyphae, spores in sporangium. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), Mucor, Albugo (white rust of crucifers). Ascomycetes (Sac fungi): Septate hyphae, ascospores in ascus. Examples: Aspergillus, Penicillium (antibiotic, cheese), Saccharomyces (yeast � fermentation), Morchella (edible morel), Claviceps (ergot of rye). Basidiomycetes (Club fungi): Septate hyphae, basidiospores on basidium. Examples: Agaricus (mushroom), Ustilago (smut), Puccinia (rust). Deuteromycetes (Imperfect fungi): Sexual stage not known. Examples: Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma.

Lichens

Symbiotic association between a fungus (mycobiont) and an alga (phycobiont). The alga produces food via photosynthesis; the fungus provides shelter, water, and minerals. Lichens are sensitive to air pollution � used as bioindicators of SO2 pollution. Types: Crustose (crust-like), Foliose (leaf-like), Fruticose (shrub-like).

Mycorrhiza

Mutualistic association between fungi and plant roots. The fungus helps in nutrient (especially phosphorus) absorption, and the plant provides carbohydrates. Types: Ectomycorrhiza (fungal sheath outside root), Endomycorrhiza (fungus inside root cells). Important for reforestation and plant health.

Example 3 � Fungi
Penicillium belongs to which class of fungi?

a) Phycomycetes   b) Ascomycetes   c) Basidiomycetes   d) Deuteromycetes
Solution: Option (b). Penicillium is an Ascomycete (sac fungus) that produces ascospores in an ascus. It is used in antibiotic (penicillin) production and cheese processing.
Example 9 � Fungi Classification
Which class of fungi is characterised by the absence of a known sexual stage?

a) Ascomycetes   b) Basidiomycetes   c) Deuteromycetes   d) Phycomycetes
Solution: Option (c). Deuteromycetes (Imperfect fungi) have no known sexual stage and reproduce only by asexual spores (conidia).

Lichens are bioindicators of which pollutant?

Solution: Option (b). Lichens are highly sensitive to sulphur dioxide (SO2) pollution and are used as bioindicators of air quality.

Plant Kingdom

Classification Systems

Artificial system (Linnaeus): Based on a few morphological characters (e.g., androecium structure � number of stamens). Simple but did not reflect natural relationships. Natural system (Bentham & Hooker): Based on many natural characters (morphology, anatomy, embryology, phytochemistry). More comprehensive, considered the most useful practical system. Phylogenetic system (Engler & Prantl, Hutchinson, Cronquist, APG): Based on evolutionary relationships determined by genetic, molecular, and fossil evidence. Modern systems use DNA sequence data (Angiosperm Phylogeny Group � APG system) for classification.

Plant Groups

Algae: Simple, thalloid plants without differentiation into root, stem, leaf. Mostly aquatic. Chlorophyll-bearing, autotrophic.

Economic Importance of Algae: (1) Food: Porphyra (nori), Laminaria (kombu), Sargassum, Spirulina (protein-rich food supplement). (2) Agar: from Gelidium and Gracilaria � used in microbiology (culture media) and food industry (ice cream, jellies). (3) Carrageenan: from red algae � used as stabiliser and emulsifier. (4) Alginates: from brown algae � used in ice creams, cosmetics, dental impressions. (5) Biofuels: algal oils for biodiesel production. (6) Fertiliser: as soil conditioner (seaweed manure). (7) Oxygen production: algae contribute ~50% of global oxygen through photosynthesis. (8) Negative impacts: algal blooms (eutrophication), toxin production (red tide).

Economic Importance of Bryophytes: (1) Sphagnum (peat moss) � used as fuel (peat), packing material (high water-holding capacity), soil conditioner, and in horticulture. (2) Mosses prevent soil erosion by forming dense mats. (3) Some bryophytes are bioindicators of heavy metal pollution. (4) In some ecosystems, they play a key role in water retention and nutrient cycling. (5) Medicinal uses: some liverworts produce compounds with antimicrobial activity.

Alternation of Generations

Plants show alternation between haploid gametophyte (produces gametes) and diploid sporophyte (produces spores). In algae, the gametophyte is dominant. In bryophytes, the gametophyte is dominant. In pteridophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms, the sporophyte is dominant. This alternation is called haplo-diplontic life cycle.

Animal Kingdom

Classification Basis

Animals are classified based on: Level of organisation � cellular (Porifera), tissue (Cnidaria), organ (Platyhelminthes), organ system (higher phyla). Symmetry � asymmetrical (Porifera), radial (Cnidaria, Echinodermata adults), bilateral (most others). Germ layers � diploblastic (two layers: ectoderm + endoderm, e.g., Cnidaria) or triploblastic (three layers: ectoderm + mesoderm + endoderm). Coelom � body cavity: acoelomate (no coelom, e.g., Platyhelminthes), pseudocoelomate (false coelom, e.g., Aschelminthes), coelomate (true coelom, e.g., annelids to chordates). Segmentation � body divided into segments (e.g., earthworm). Notochord � present in chordates, absent in non-chordates.

Example 12 � Animal Classification Basis
An organism with organ-level organisation, bilateral symmetry, triploblastic and acoelomate condition belongs to:

a) Cnidaria   b) Platyhelminthes   c) Annelida   d) Aschelminthes
Solution: Option (b). Platyhelminthes (flatworms) have organ-level organisation, bilateral symmetry, triploblastic and acoelomate body plan.

Non-Chordate Phyla

Porifera (Sponges): Cellular level organisation, asymmetrical, canal system with choanocytes (collar cells) for water current and food capture, spicules (calcareous/siliceous) or spongin fibres for skeleton. Mostly marine. Reproduction by budding (asexual) and gemmule formation (resistant bodies). Examples: Sycon (solitary), Euspongia (bath sponge), Spongilla (freshwater sponge).

Cnidaria (Coelenterata): Tissue level organisation, radial symmetry, diploblastic (ectoderm + endoderm with mesogloea), cnidoblasts (stinging cells called nematocysts) for defence and prey capture, two body forms � polyp (sessile, e.g., Hydra) and medusa (free-swimming, e.g., Aurelia). Examples: Obelia (both polyp and medusa), Physalia (Portuguese man-of-war � a colonial form), Pennatula (sea pen), Adamsia (sea anemone).

Platyhelminthes (Flatworms): Organ level organisation, bilateral symmetry, triploblastic, acoelomate, flattened body (increases surface area for gas exchange), mostly parasitic with suckers/hooks, flame cells for osmoregulation and excretion. Examples: Taenia solium (tapeworm � causes taeniasis), Fasciola hepatica (liver fluke � causes fascioliasis), Planaria (free-living, shows remarkable regeneration).

Aschelminthes (Roundworms / Nematodes): Pseudocoelomate, bilaterally symmetrical, complete digestive system (mouth + anus), unsegmented, mostly microscopic. Many are parasitic causing diseases. Examples: Ascaris lumbricoides (roundworm � causes ascariasis), Wuchereria bancrofti (causes filariasis/elephantiasis), Ancylostoma duodenale (hookworm � causes anaemia).

Annelida (Segmented worms): True coelom (schizocoelom), metameric segmentation (body divided into segments), closed circulatory system (haemoglobin in blood), nephridia for excretion, setae/chitinous bristles for locomotion. Examples: Pheretima posthuma (earthworm � farmer's friend), Hirudinaria granulosa (leech � used in medicine), Nereis (marine).

Arthropoda: Largest phylum (over 2/3 of all described species). Jointed appendages, exoskeleton of chitin (periodically shed by moulting/ecydsis), open circulatory system (haemocoel), body divided into head, thorax, abdomen. Includes insects (largest class), crustaceans, arachnids, myriapods. Examples: Periplaneta americana (cockroach), Apis indica (honey bee � apiculture), Bombyx mori (silk moth � sericulture), Laccifer lacca (lac insect � shellac), Palaemon (prawn).

Mollusca: Second largest phylum. Soft-bodied animals with a calcareous shell (except some like octopus), body divided into head, muscular foot, visceral hump, and mantle (piercing the shell). Radula (file-like rasping organ) for feeding. Open circulatory system. Examples: Pila globosa (apple snail), Unio (freshwater mussel � pearl formation), Octopus (devil fish, intelligent), Sepia (cuttlefish � internal shell called cuttlebone), Loligo (squid).

Echinodermata: Spiny-skinned, exclusively marine, radial symmetry in adults (bilateral in larvae � showing evolutionary link), pentaradial symmetry, water vascular system with tube feet for locomotion, food capture, and respiration. Endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles. Excellent regeneration ability. Examples: Asterias (starfish), Echinus (sea urchin), Antedon (feather star), Holothuria (sea cucumber).

Hemichordata: Small phylum with a notochord-like structure called stomochord (present in proboscis). Open circulatory system, gill slits present. Previously considered a subphylum of chordates, now classified as a separate non-chordate phylum due to fundamental differences. Example: Balanoglossus (acorn worm). Other examples: Saccoglossus.

Chordate Phyla

Three defining features at some stage: (i) Notochord � a flexible rod supporting the body; (ii) Dorsal hollow nerve cord � develops into the central nervous system; (iii) Pharyngeal gill slits � present at some life stage, may be modified for filter-feeding or become ear structures in mammals; (iv) Post-anal tail � extends beyond the anus. Additional features include a ventral heart and a closed circulatory system.

Viruses & Subviral Particles

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites � cannot reproduce outside a host cell. They are not truly living (no cellular organisation, no metabolism). They have either DNA or RNA (never both) surrounded by a protein coat (capsid). Discovered by Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892, tobacco mosaic virus). TMV was the first virus discovered. Viruses cause diseases like common cold (Rhinovirus), AIDS (HIV), COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), rabies (Rhabdovirus).

Viroids: Infectious RNA particles without a protein coat (discovered by T.O. Diener, 1971). Cause potato spindle tuber disease. Prions: Infectious protein particles (discovered by Stanley Prusiner). Cause bovine spongiform encephalopathy (mad cow disease) in cattle and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease in humans. Prions are abnormally folded proteins that convert normal proteins into the misfolded form.

Bacteriophages: Viruses that infect bacteria. They consist of a nucleic acid core (usually dsDNA) surrounded by a protein capsid with a tail structure. They attach to bacterial cell surface via tail fibres and inject their genetic material. Discovered by Frederick Twort (1915) and Felix d'Herelle (1917). T4 bacteriophage is a classic example used in molecular biology research. Bacteriophages exhibit two life cycles: lytic (virulent � host cell is lysed) and lysogenic (temperate � viral genome integrates into host genome as a prophage).

Which of the following is correct about viroids?

NEET Mnemonic � Virus vs Viroid vs Prion
Virus: DNA or RNA + protein coat. Viroid: RNA only (no protein coat). Prion: Protein only (no nucleic acid). Remember: V-V-P = D/R + P, R only, P only.
NEET Key Points � Five Kingdom Criteria
Whittaker's criteria: Cell structure (prokaryote/eukaryote), Body organisation (unicellular/multicellular), Mode of nutrition (auto/hetero), Reproduction, Phylogeny. Viruses and lichens are not placed in the five-kingdom system.
NEET Key Points � Exceptions to Remember
(1) Mycoplasma � prokaryote with no cell wall. (2) Euglena � unicellular eukaryote with both plant and animal features. (3) Yeast (Saccharomyces) � unicellular fungus, not a protist. (4) Penicillium � an ascomycete (sac fungus), not a deuteromycete. (5) Crocodile � reptile with 4-chambered heart. (6) Duck-billed platypus � egg-laying mammal (monotreme). (7) Sponges � cellular level organisation, not tissue level. (8) Echinoderms � radial symmetry in adults, bilateral in larvae. (9) Chondrichthyes � marine fish with cartilaginous skeleton, ureotelic, internal fertilisation. (10) Archaea � have unique membrane lipids and rRNA, different from true bacteria.

Key NEET Differences

  • Prokaryote vs Eukaryote: Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound nucleus and organelles, 70S ribosomes, peptidoglycan cell wall (if present); Eukaryotes have membrane-bound nucleus, 80S ribosomes, membrane-bound organelles.
  • Gram-positive vs Gram-negative: Gram-positive has thick peptidoglycan (stains purple), sensitive to penicillin; Gram-negative has thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane (stains pink), resistant to penicillin.
  • Autotroph vs Heterotroph: Autotrophs synthesise own food (photosynthesis/chemosynthesis); Heterotrophs obtain food from external sources (saprophytic/parasitic/holozoic).
  • Amoeba vs Paramoecium: Amoeba � pseudopodia, single nucleus, contractile vacuole for osmoregulation; Paramoecium � cilia, two nuclei (macro + micro), definite shape, oral groove for feeding.
  • Bryophytes vs Pteridophytes: Bryophytes � non-vascular, gametophyte dominant, require water for fertilisation, no true roots; Pteridophytes � vascular (xylem + phloem), sporophyte dominant, true roots, spore-producing.
  • Gymnosperms vs Angiosperms: Gymnosperms � naked seeds, no fruits/flowers, tracheids only (no vessels except Gnetales), mostly evergreen; Angiosperms � seeds enclosed in fruits, flowers present, vessels present, diverse life forms.
  • Monocot vs Dicot: Monocot � one cotyledon, parallel venation, fibrous roots, trimerous flowers, scattered vascular bundles, no secondary growth; Dicot � two cotyledons, reticulate venation, taproot, tetramerous/pentamerous flowers, ringed vascular bundles, secondary growth present.
  • Radial vs Bilateral symmetry: Radial � body can be divided into equal halves by any plane through centre (Cnidaria, adult Echinodermata); Bilateral � body can be divided only by one sagittal plane (most other phyla).
  • Acoelomate vs Pseudocoelomate vs Coelomate: Acoelomate � no body cavity, mesoderm fills space (Platyhelminthes); Pseudocoelomate � cavity not lined by mesoderm (Aschelminthes); Coelomate � cavity lined by mesoderm (annelids, arthropods, molluscs, echinoderms, chordates).
  • Open vs Closed circulatory system: Open � blood flows into sinuses/haemocoel, direct contact with tissues, low pressure (Arthropoda, Mollusca); Closed � blood flows through vessels, high pressure, efficient transport (Annelida, Chordata).
  • Oviparous vs Viviparous vs Ovoviviparous: Oviparous � lay eggs outside, embryo develops externally (most fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, monotremes); Viviparous � give birth to live young, embryo develops internally (most mammals); Ovoviviparous � eggs retained inside body, hatch internally, young born live (some sharks, reptiles).
  • Virus vs Viroid vs Prion: Virus � DNA or RNA + protein coat, obligate intracellular parasite; Viroid � RNA only, no protein coat, small (<400 nucleotides); Prion � protein only, no nucleic acid, abnormally folded, self-propagating.
  • Chitin vs Cellulose vs Peptidoglycan: Chitin � N-acetylglucosamine polymer, structural (Fungi cell wall, Arthropoda exoskeleton); Cellulose � glucose polymer, structural (Plant cell wall); Peptidoglycan � sugar + amino acid polymer, bacterial cell wall.
  • Artificial vs Natural vs Phylogenetic classification: Artificial � based on few characters (Linnaeus); Natural � based on overall similarities (Bentham & Hooker); Phylogenetic � based on evolutionary relationships (Engler & Prantl, Hutchinson).
  • Simple vs Compound leaves (for plant kingdom context): Simple leaf � leaf blade undivided (mango); Compound leaf � leaf blade divided into leaflets (neem, rose) � may be pinnately or palmately compound.
  • Endothermic vs Ectothermic: Endothermic (warm-blooded) � maintain constant body temperature (Aves, Mammalia); Ectothermic (cold-blooded) � body temperature varies with environment (fish, amphibians, reptiles).
  • Axial vs Appendicular skeleton (chordate context): Axial skeleton � skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum; Appendicular skeleton � limbs, pectoral and pelvic girdles.
  • Notochord vs Vertebral column: Notochord � flexible rod of mesodermal origin, present in all chordate embryos, replaced by vertebral column in vertebrates; Vertebral column � segmented bony/cartilaginous structure protecting the spinal cord.

NEET Exam Strategy � Diversity & Classification

This chapter forms the foundation for NEET Biology. Key focus areas: (1) Memorise the five-kingdom classification criteria and exceptions. (2) Understand taxonomic hierarchy with at least one example from plants and animals. (3) Learn phylum-wise examples � at least 3 examples per phylum for NEET. (4) Practice one-character one-example questions (e.g., "pseudocoelomate example" → roundworms). (5) Pay special attention to exceptions: Mycoplasma (no cell wall), Euglena (mixotrophic), virus/viroid/prion differences. (6) For plant kingdom, remember the dominant generation for each group � this is a frequently tested concept. (7) Animal kingdom questions often ask "which phylum has X feature" � practice matching features to phyla. Common NEET question types: match the following, odd one out, true/false, statement-based, and diagram-based questions on taxonomic hierarchy and classification.
