Digestive System Overview

1.1 Anatomy of the Alimentary Canal

The alimentary canal extends from the oral cavity to the anus and is divided into distinct regions each specialized for a particular phase of digestion. The mouth, pharynx, and oesophagus constitute the upper gastrointestinal tract where mechanical breakdown and the initiation of carbohydrate digestion occur. The stomach, a muscular J‑shaped organ, provides an acidic environment that denatures proteins and activates pepsinogen to pepsin. The small intestine, comprising the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, is the principal site for enzymatic digestion and nutrient absorption, its mucosa amplified by villi and microvilli that increase surface area by several hundredfold. The large intestine, including the caecum, colon, rectum, and anal canal, primarily absorbs water and electrolytes while harbouring a dense microbial flora that ferments undigested carbohydrates.

1.2 Accessory Glands and Their Secretions

Three major accessory glands deliver secretions into the lumen of the alimentary canal. The salivary glands — parotid, submandibular, and sublingual — secrete saliva containing water, electrolytes, mucus, and the enzyme salivary amylase (ptyalin) that begins starch hydrolysis at a near‑neutral pH. The pancreas, a mixed exocrine‑endocrine organ, releases pancreatic juice rich in bicarbonate to neutralise gastric chyme and a suite of enzymes: pancreatic amylase for carbohydrates, trypsinogen and chymotrypsinogen (activated to trypsin and chymotrypsin) for proteins, pancreatic lipase for triglycerides, and nucleases for nucleic acids. The liver produces bile, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, which contains bile salts, phospholipids, cholesterol, and bilirubin; bile salts emulsify fat globules, increasing the interfacial area for lipase action.

1.3 Mechanical and Chemical Digestion

Mechanical digestion reduces food particle size, exposing a larger surface for enzymatic attack. Mastication in the oral cavity, churning by gastric peristalsis, and segmentation movements in the small intestine all contribute. Chemical digestion proceeds through a cascade of hydrolytic reactions catalysed by specific enzymes that operate at optimal pH values: salivary amylase at pH 6.7‑7.0, pepsin at pH 1.5‑2.5, pancreatic enzymes at pH 7.5‑8.5, and brush‑border disaccharidases and peptidases at the microvillar membrane. The coordinated release of secretions is regulated by neural reflexes (vagal stimulation) and hormonal signals (gastrin, secretin, cholecystokinin) that ensure enzymes and bile arrive in synchrony with the arrival of chyme.

1.4 Regulation of Digestive Activity

Digestive activity is governed by the enteric nervous system, extrinsic autonomic inputs, and a suite of gastrointestinal hormones. Gastrin, released by G cells in the antrum, stimulates parietal cells to secrete HCl and chief cells to release pepsinogen. Secretin, released by S cells in the duodenum in response to acidic chyme, prompts pancreatic duct cells to secrete bicarbonate‑rich fluid. Cholecystokinin (CCK), released by I cells upon detection of fatty acids and amino acids, contracts the gallbladder, relaxes the sphincter of Oddi, and stimulates pancreatic enzyme secretion. These hormones also feed back to the brain to modulate appetite and gastric emptying, illustrating the integration of local and systemic control.

Digestion of Macronutrients

2.1 Carbohydrate Digestion

Carbohydrate digestion begins with salivary amylase, which hydrolyses α‑1,4‑glycosidic bonds in starch to produce maltose, maltotriose, and α‑limit dextrins. In the duodenum, pancreatic amylase continues this process. The resulting oligosaccharides are further cleaved by brush‑border enzymes: maltase, sucrase‑isomaltase, and lactase, yielding the monosaccharides glucose, fructose, and galactose. These monosaccharides are then transported across the apical membrane of enterocytes — glucose and galactose via the Na⁺‑dependent SGLT1 cotransporter, fructose via the facilitative GLUT5 transporter — and exit the basolateral membrane through GLUT2 into the portal circulation.

2.2 Protein Digestion

Protein digestion initiates in the stomach where pepsin, activated from pepsinogen by HCl, cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic amino acids, producing large polypeptides. In the duodenum, trypsinogen is activated to trypsin by enteropeptidase; trypsin then activates chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidases, and proelastase. These pancreatic proteases hydrolyse polypeptides into smaller peptides and free amino acids. Brush‑border peptidases (aminopeptidases, dipeptidases) complete the process. Amino acids and di‑/tripeptides are absorbed: neutral amino acids use the Na⁺‑dependent B⁰AT1 transporter, while di‑/tripeptides employ the H⁺‑coupled PEPT1 transporter. Intracellular peptidases hydrolyse any remaining peptides before basolateral exit via various amino‑acid transporters.

2.3 Lipid Digestion

Lipid digestion is limited in the stomach by gastric lipase, which preferentially hydrolyses short‑ and medium‑chain triglycerides. The bulk of triglyceride digestion occurs in the duodenum where bile salts emulsify fat globules into micelles, dramatically increasing the surface area for pancreatic lipase. Pancreatic lipase, assisted by colipase, cleaves the sn‑1 and sn‑3 positions of triglycerides, yielding two free fatty acids and a 2‑monoacylglycerol. Phospholipase A₂ hydrolyses phospholipids to lysophospholipids and fatty acids, while cholesterol esterase liberates free cholesterol. The products — fatty acids, monoacylglycerols, lysophospholipids, cholesterol, and fat‑soluble vitamins — are incorporated into mixed micelles and diffuse across the unstirred water layer to the enterocyte apical membrane.

2.4 Absorption Mechanisms

Inside the enterocyte, fatty acids and monoacylglycerols are re‑esterified to triglycerides in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, then packaged with apolipoprotein B‑48, phospholipids, cholesterol, and cholesterol esters into chylomicrons. Chylomicrons are secreted by exocytosis into the lacteals of the lymphatic system, bypassing the portal vein. Short‑ and medium‑chain fatty acids, being more water‑soluble, can enter the portal blood directly. Water‑soluble vitamins and minerals follow specific carrier‑mediated or paracellular routes. The coordinated action of transporters, intracellular processing, and lipoprotein assembly ensures efficient delivery of nutrients to the systemic circulation.

Worked Example: Lipid Digestion and Absorption
A 25‑year‑old male consumes a meal rich in triglycerides. Describe the sequential steps of lipid digestion and absorption, naming the enzymes, bile salts, and transport vehicles involved.

Within the enterocyte, fatty acids and monoacylglycerols are re‑esterified to triglycerides in the smooth ER. 7. Triglycerides are assembled with apolipoprotein B‑48, phospholipids, and cholesterol into chylomicrons. 8. Chylomicrons are secreted into the lacteals, entering the lymphatic system and eventually the thoracic duct to reach the bloodstream.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the effect of increased CO₂ on the oxygen‑hemoglobin dissociation curve, recall the Bohr effect: higher CO₂ (and lower pH) shifts the curve to the right, decreasing Hb affinity for O₂ and promoting O₂ release in metabolically active tissues. This concept is frequently tested with graph‑interpretation items.

Breathing and Exchange of Gases

3.1 Respiratory Tract Anatomy

The respiratory system is divided into the conducting zone and the respiratory zone. The conducting zone — nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and bronchioles down to the terminal bronchioles — warms, humidifies, and filters inspired air but does not participate in gas exchange. The respiratory zone begins with respiratory bronchioles, continues through alveolar ducts, and ends in alveolar sacs composed of numerous alveoli. Each alveolus is lined by a thin simple squamous epithelium (type I pneumocytes) and interspersed with type II pneumocytes that secrete pulmonary surfactant, a mixture of phospholipids and proteins that reduces surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse during expiration.

3.2 Mechanics of Ventilation

Ventilation is driven by pressure gradients generated by the respiratory muscles. During quiet inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and descends, while the external intercostal muscles elevate the ribs, expanding the thoracic cavity and lowering intrapleural pressure to about –3 cmH₂O relative to atmosphere. This pressure drop causes air to flow into the alveoli until alveolar pressure equals atmospheric pressure. Expiration at rest is passive: elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall raises intrapleural pressure, pushing air out. During forced breathing, accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalene, abdominal muscles) augment inspiratory and expiratory volumes, allowing tidal volumes to exceed the resting 500 mL.

3.3 Gas Exchange at the Alveoli

Gas exchange occurs by diffusion across the alveolar‑capillary barrier, which consists of the alveolar epithelium, the fused basement membranes, and the capillary endothelium — a total thickness of ~0.5 µm. The partial pressure gradient for O₂ (PAO₂ ≈ 100 mmHg, PaO₂ ≈ 40 mmHg) drives O₂ into pulmonary capillary blood, while the gradient for CO₂ (PACO₂ ≈ 40 mmHg, PaCO₂ ≈ 45 mmHg) drives CO₂ out. Diffusion is rapid because the surface area of the alveolar membrane (~70 m²) is large and the diffusion distance is minimal. The ventilation‑perfusion (V/Q) ratio is ideally 1.0; regional variations (apex V/Q > 1, base V/Q < 1) cause slight differences in gas tensions that are normally compensated by hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction.

3.4 Transport of Respiratory Gases in Blood

Oxygen is transported primarily bound to haemoglobin (Hb) — each Hb tetramer binds four O₂ molecules cooperatively, giving a sigmoidal O₂‑Hb dissociation curve. The P₅₀ (partial pressure at 50 % saturation) is ~26 mmHg at pH 7.4, 37 °C, and normal 2,3‑BPG levels. Factors that shift the curve rightward (increased temperature, H⁺, CO₂, 2,3‑BPG) lower Hb affinity, facilitating O₂ unloading in tissues. Carbon dioxide is transported in three forms: dissolved in plasma (~10 %), as bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) after hydration catalysed by carbonic anhydrase in erythrocytes (~70 %), and as carbamino compounds bound to Hb (~20 %). The chloride shift (exchange of HCO₃⁻ for Cl⁻ across the red cell membrane) maintains electroneutrality during CO₂ transport.

Circulation and Body Fluids

4.1 Heart Structure and Cardiac Cycle

The human heart is a four‑chambered muscular pump: two atria receive blood, two ventricles eject it. The right atrium receives systemic venous return (~5 L/min) via the superior and inferior vena cavae and the coronary sinus; the left atrium receives oxygenated pulmonary venous return via four pulmonary veins. The cardiac cycle comprises systole (ventricular contraction) and diastole (ventricular relaxation). Atrial systole contributes ~20 % of ventricular filling (the “atrial kick”). Ventricular systole generates pressures of ~120 mmHg in the left ventricle and ~25 mmHg in the right ventricle, opening the aortic and pulmonary valves respectively. The sequence of electrical activation — SA node → AV node → bundle of His → Purkinje fibres — ensures coordinated contraction. Heart sounds S₁ (AV valve closure) and S₂ (semilunar valve closure) mark the onset of systole and diastole.

4.2 Blood Vessels and Hemodynamics

Arteries, with thick tunica media rich in elastic fibres (large arteries) or smooth muscle (muscular arteries), conduct blood under high pressure and dampen pulsatility. Arterioles are the primary resistance vessels; their radius, regulated by sympathetic tone, local metabolites, and endothelial factors (NO, endothelin), determines systemic vascular resistance and thus mean arterial pressure (MAP = CO × SVR). Capillaries, composed of a single endothelial layer with intercellular clefts or fenestrations, are the exchange sites for nutrients, gases, and waste. Venules and veins have larger lumens, thinner walls, and valves (especially in limbs) to aid venous return; they serve as capacitance vessels holding ~60 % of blood volume. The lymphatic system returns interstitial fluid (≈2 L/day) to the venous circulation via the thoracic duct.

4.3 Composition and Functions of Blood

Blood consists of plasma (≈55 % volume) and formed elements (≈45 %). Plasma water carries proteins — albumin (maintains oncotic pressure), globulins (immunoglobulins, transport proteins), fibrinogen (clotting) — electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste. Erythrocytes (RBCs) contain haemoglobin for O₂/CO₂ transport; their biconcave shape maximises surface‑to‑volume ratio and deformability. Leukocytes (WBCs) mediate immunity: neutrophils (phagocytosis), lymphocytes (adaptive immunity), monocytes/macrophages, eosinophils, basophils. Platelets (thrombocytes) are cell fragments essential for primary haemostasis (adhesion, activation, aggregation) and provide a phospholipid surface for coagulation factor assembly. The balance of pro‑ and anticoagulant mechanisms prevents thrombosis while allowing rapid clot formation at injury sites.

4.4 Lymphatic System and Tissue Fluid

Interstitial fluid forms by filtration at the arterial end of capillaries (hydrostatic pressure > oncotic pressure) and is largely reabsorbed at the venous end (oncotic > hydrostatic). The net filtration (~2 L/day) enters blind‑ended lymphatic capillaries, which have overlapping endothelial cells acting as one‑way flaps. Lymph passes through lymph nodes where antigens are presented to lymphocytes, then converges into larger collecting vessels, the thoracic duct (draining most of the body) and the right lymphatic duct (draining right upper quadrant). Lymph flow is propelled by intrinsic smooth muscle contractions, external compression from skeletal muscle pumps, and respiratory pressure changes. The lymphatic system also transports dietary lipids (chylomicrons) from intestinal lacteals, linking digestion to systemic circulation.

Excretion and Osmoregulation

5.1 Kidney Anatomy and Nephron Function

Each kidney contains ~1 million nephrons, the functional units of urine formation. A nephron comprises a renal corpuscle (glomerulus enclosed by Bowman's capsule), a proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), a loop of Henle (descending and ascending limbs), a distal convoluted tubule (DCT), and a collecting duct that drains into the renal pelvis. The glomerulus is a high‑pressure capillary network (glomerular hydrostatic pressure ~45 mmHg) fenestrated to allow filtration of plasma water and solutes while retaining cells and large proteins. The juxtaglomerular apparatus (macula densa, granular JG cells, extraglomerular mesangial cells) monitors distal tubular NaCl delivery and renal perfusion pressure, regulating renin release.

5.2 Formation of Urine: Filtration, Reabsorption, Secretion

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in a healthy adult is ~125 mL/min (≈180 L/day). The filtrate is iso‑osmotic to plasma. In the PCT, ~65 % of filtered Na⁺, water, glucose, amino acids, bicarbonate, and phosphate are reabsorbed via active and passive transporters (Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase on basolateral membrane, SGLT2 for glucose, NHE3 for Na⁺/H⁺ exchange). The descending limb of Henle is highly water‑permeable (aquaporin‑1) but impermeable to solutes, concentrating tubular fluid. The thick ascending limb actively reabsorbs Na⁺, K⁺, 2Cl⁻ via NKCC2, generating a medullary interstitial gradient while remaining water‑impermeable. The DCT and collecting duct fine‑tune Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, and water reabsorption under hormonal control. Secretion of H⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, and certain drugs occurs mainly in the PCT and collecting duct, allowing excretion of substances not filtered or requiring additional removal.

5.3 Hormonal Regulation of Water and Electrolyte Balance

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) released from the posterior pituitary in response to increased plasma osmolality (>285 mOsm/kg) or decreased effective circulating volume acts on V₂ receptors in the late DCT and collecting duct, inserting aquaporin‑2 water channels and increasing water permeability, thus concentrating urine. Aldosterone, secreted by the adrenal cortex in response to angiotensin II and hyperkalaemia, enhances Na⁺ reabsorption (via ENaC) and K⁺ secretion in the cortical collecting duct, expanding extracellular fluid volume. Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), released by atrial stretch, inhibits Na⁺ reabsorption in the collecting duct and suppresses renin and aldosterone, promoting natriuresis and diuresis. The renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system (RAAS) integrates renal perfusion, systemic pressure, and electrolyte status to maintain homeostasis.

5.4 Role of Other Excretory Organs

While the kidneys are the primary excretory organs, the lungs eliminate volatile acids (CO₂) and the skin excretes water, electrolytes, and urea in sweat, especially during heat stress. The liver converts toxic ammonia to urea via the urea cycle, and also conjugates bilirubin for biliary excretion; the intestine eliminates bile pigments and unabsorbed substances in feces. These auxiliary routes complement renal function, ensuring that metabolic waste products, excess ions, and xenobiotics are removed from the body, preserving the internal milieu required for cellular processes.

Regulation of Gastric Acid Secretion and Clinical Correlates

Gastric acid (HCl) secretion is orchestrated by three principal stimuli — acetylcholine (ACh) from vagal efferents, gastrin from G‑cells, and histamine from enterochromaffin‑like (ECL) cells — converging on the parietal cell H⁺/K⁺‑ATPase. ACh binds M₃ muscarinic receptors, activating phospholipase C, raising intracellular Ca²⁺, and stimulating protein kinase C. Gastrin acts via CCK₂ receptors on ECL cells to release histamine, which then binds H₂ receptors on parietal cells, triggering a cAMP‑PKA cascade that phosphorylates the proton pump and promotes its translocation to the canalicular membrane. Histamine is the dominant amplifier; H₂‑receptor antagonists (e.g., ranitidine) and proton‑pump inhibitors (e.g., omeprazole) exploit this hierarchy. Negative feedback includes somatostatin from D‑cells (inhibits ACh, gastrin, histamine release) and the enterogastric reflex triggered by duodenal acidity (pH < 3) and hyperosmolar chyme, which reduces vagal tone and gastrin secretion. Clinically, chronic H. pylori infection disrupts this regulation: urease‑generated ammonia neutralises gastric acid, prompting hypergastrinaemia and parietal‑cell hyperplasia, while cytotoxin‑associated gene A (CagA) strains provoke mucosal inflammation that can progress to atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and adenocarcinoma. Zollinger‑Ellison syndrome, a gastrin‑secreting neuroendocrine tumour, produces massive acid output (>10 mEq/h) refractory to standard dosing, requiring high‑dose PPIs or surgical resection. Stress‑related mucosal disease (Curling’s ulcer in burns, Cushing’s ulcer in intracranial pathology) reflects loss of prostaglandin‑mediated mucosal defence rather than acid hypersecretion per se.

Ventilation‑Perfusion Matching and Shunt Physiology

Efficient gas exchange requires regional matching of alveolar ventilation (V̇A) to pulmonary capillary perfusion (Q̇). The V̇/Q̇ ratio in a healthy upright lung varies from ~0.6 at the base (high perfusion, moderate ventilation) to ~3 at the apex (high ventilation, low perfusion). Gravity‑dependent hydrostatic pressure gradients create this distribution: pleural pressure is less negative at the base, expanding basal alveoli less but increasing capillary blood volume. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) — a unique response of pulmonary arterioles to alveolar PO₂ < 70 mmHg — redirects flow from poorly ventilated regions, improving overall V̇/Q̇ matching. In disease, V̇/Q̇ mismatch is the commonest cause of hypoxaemia: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) creates low V̇/Q̇ units (airway obstruction with preserved perfusion), while pulmonary embolism generates high V̇/Q̇ units (ventilation without perfusion). True shunt (V̇/Q̇ = 0) occurs when perfused alveoli receive no ventilation — e.g., atelectasis, pneumonia, or intracardiac right‑to‑left shunt. Shunt fraction (Qs/Qt) can be estimated by the classic shunt equation: Qs/Qt = (Cc′O₂ − CaO₂) / (Cc′O₂ − CvO₂). Unlike V̇/Q̇ mismatch, shunt‑induced hypoxaemia is poorly corrected by supplemental O₂ because shunted blood never contacts alveolar gas. Clinically, a PaO₂ < 60 mmHg on 100 % FiO₂ suggests a shunt > 20 %. Positive end‑expiratory pressure (PEEP) recruits collapsed alveoli, converting shunt to low V̇/Q̇ units and improving oxygenation, but excessive PEEP can overdistend compliant regions, increase dead space, and reduce venous return.

Examiner's twist
A 55‑year‑old smoker presents with dyspnoea. ABG on room air shows pH 7.38, PaCO₂ 48 mmHg, PaO₂ 55 mmHg. On 100 % O₂, PaO₂ rises only to 85 mmHg. Which mechanism best explains the limited response to high FiO₂?
The minimal rise in PaO₂ despite 100 % O₂ indicates a significant true shunt (V̇/Q̇ = 0). In pure V̇/Q̇ mismatch, high FiO₂ would raise PaO₂ substantially because even low‑ventilation units receive oxygen‑rich gas. Shunted blood bypasses ventilated alveoli entirely, so increasing alveolar PO₂ cannot oxygenate that fraction. The ABG also shows mild hypercapnia, consistent with COPD, but the refractory hypoxaemia points to an additional shunt component such as pneumonia‑related atelectasis or an intracardiac defect.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question gives ABG values on room air and on 100 % O₂, calculate the expected PaO₂ rise for pure V̇/Q̇ mismatch (≈5‑6 × FiO₂). If the observed rise is far lower, select shunt. Remember that HPV is inhibited by volatile anaesthetics, high PaCO₂, and alkalosis — useful for physiology‑pharmacology crossover items.

Cardiac Electrophysiology: Action Potentials and Conduction System

The cardiac action potential (AP) differs markedly between pacemaker (SA node, AV node) and contractile (atrial, ventricular, Purkinje) cells. Pacemaker cells lack a stable resting potential; spontaneous diastolic depolarisation (phase 4) is driven by the funny current (I_f, mixed Na⁺/K⁺ influx via HCN channels) and a decaying K⁺ outward current (I_K). When the membrane reaches ~‑40 mV, T‑type Ca²⁺ channels open (phase 0 upstroke), followed by L‑type Ca²⁺ channel activation that sustains the plateau (phase 2). Repolarisation (phase 3) occurs via delayed‑rectifier K⁺ currents (I_Kr, I_Ks). Contractile cells exhibit a rapid phase 0 mediated by fast Na⁺ channels (I_Na), a brief phase 1 notch (I_to), a prolonged plateau (phase 2) from L‑type Ca²⁺ influx balanced by K⁺ efflux, and a terminal repolarisation (phase 3) dominated by I_Kr/I_Ks. Phase 4 is stable at ~‑85 mV (ventricular) due to inward‑rectifier K⁺ current (I_K1). The conduction hierarchy — SA node (60‑100 bpm) → atrial pathways → AV node (40‑60 bpm) → bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibres (20‑40 bpm) — ensures coordinated contraction. AV nodal delay (~120 ms) allows atrial kick to complete ventricular filling. Pharmacologically, class I antiarrhythmics block I_Na (use‑dependent), class III prolong repolarisation by blocking I_Kr, class IV (verapamil, diltiazem) inhibit L‑type Ca²⁺ channels, and β‑blockers reduce I_f and Ca²⁺ current, slowing SA‑node rate. Long QT syndrome (congenital or drug‑induced) reflects delayed repolarisation (reduced I_Kr or enhanced late I_Na), predisposing to torsades de pointes. Brugada syndrome involves loss‑of‑function SCN5A mutations, reducing I_Na in right‑ventricular epicardium, creating a transmural voltage gradient that manifests as ST‑elevation in V₁‑V₃.

Renal Acid‑Base Handling: Bicarbonate Reabsorption and Ammonium Excretion

The kidney maintains systemic pH by reclaiming filtered bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and generating new HCO₃⁻ via net acid excretion (NAE = NH₄⁺ + titratable acidity − HCO₃⁻ urine). Approximately 4 320 mmol/day of HCO₃⁻ is filtered (GFR × plasma [HCO₃⁻] ≈ 180 L × 24 mmol/L). > 90 % is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule: luminal carbonic anhydrase (CA IV) catalyses H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O; CO₂ diffuses into the cell, where intracellular CA II reforms H₂CO₃, dissociating to H⁺ and HCO₃⁻. H⁺ is secreted apically via NHE3 (Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger) and H⁺‑ATPase; HCO₃⁻ exits basolaterally via NBCe1 (Na⁺/3HCO₃⁻ cotransporter). The thick ascending limb and distal tubule reclaim the remaining ~10 % via similar mechanisms, with pendrin (Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger) in type B intercalated cells mediating HCO₃⁻ secretion during alkalosis. New HCO₃⁻ generation occurs primarily in the collecting duct: α‑intercalated cells secrete H⁺ via H⁺‑ATPase and H⁺/K⁺‑ATPase; luminal H⁺ combines with filtered phosphate (HPO₄²⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻) and ammonia (NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺). Ammonia originates from glutamine deamination in proximal tubular cells (glutaminase → glutamate → α‑ketoglutarate + NH₄⁺). NH₃ diffuses into the lumen, is trapped as NH₄⁺, and excreted; each NH₄⁺ excreted yields one new HCO₃⁻ added to blood. In chronic metabolic acidosis, renal adaptation includes up‑regulation of NHE3, H⁺‑ATPase, glutaminase, and pendrin down‑regulation, increasing NAE to > 200 mmol/day. Conversely, metabolic alkalosis suppresses ammoniagenesis and enhances HCO₃⁻ secretion via pendrin, limiting new HCO₃⁻ generation.

Integrated Response to Exercise: Cardiovascular, Respiratory, and Metabolic Adjustments

During dynamic exercise, oxygen consumption (V̇O₂) can increase 20‑fold, demanding coordinated cardiovascular, respiratory, and metabolic responses. Central command (feed‑forward from motor cortex) and muscle metaboreflex (group III/IV afferents sensing metabolites) drive sympathetic outflow, raising heart rate (HR) and contractility (↑ stroke volume, SV) while causing vasoconstriction in splanchnic, renal, and cutaneous beds (except when thermoregulation demands cutaneous vasodilation). Mean arterial pressure (MAP) rises modestly (≈ 20 mmHg) because total peripheral resistance falls due to massive vasodilation in active skeletal muscle mediated by local metabolites (adenosine, K⁺, H⁺, NO, prostaglandins). Cardiac output (CO = HR × SV) can reach 25‑30 L/min in elite athletes. Ventilation (V̇E) increases proportionally to V̇CO₂ (ventilatory equivalent for CO₂ ≈ 30) via both central chemoreceptor drive (rising PaCO₂) and peripheral chemoreceptor stimulation (falling PaO₂, rising H⁺). The anaerobic threshold (≈ 50‑60 % V̇O₂max) marks the onset of disproportionate lactate accumulation; beyond this point, ventilation rises faster than V̇CO₂ (ventilatory compensation for metabolic acidosis). Muscle substrate utilisation shifts from fatty‑acid oxidation at low intensity to carbohydrate oxidation at high intensity, reflected by a rising respiratory exchange ratio (RER = V̇CO₂/V̇O₂) approaching 1.0. Post‑exercise, excess post‑exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC) restores phosphocreatine, clears lactate (Cori cycle), and repays O₂ debt; heart rate recovery (HR drop in first minute) is a prognostic marker of autonomic function. Training adaptations include increased plasma volume, enhanced ventricular compliance, higher mitochondrial density, and a rightward shift of the lactate threshold, collectively improving V̇O₂max and exercise efficiency.

Gut Microbiome Modulation of Nutrient Absorption and Immune Signaling

The human colon harbors a dense microbial ecosystem that ferments indigestible carbohydrates, producing short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Butyrate serves as the principal energy source for colonocytes, while acetate and propionate enter portal circulation and contribute approximately 5‑10 % of daily caloric requirement. SCFAs activate G‑protein‑coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43 on enteroendocrine L‑cells, stimulating secretion of glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) and peptide YY (PYY), which enhance insulin release, suppress glucagon, and promote satiety. Microbial bile‑salt hydrolases deconjugate primary bile acids, altering the bile‑acid pool composition and modulating farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and Takeda G‑protein‑coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) signaling in the ileum and liver, thereby influencing glucose homeostasis and lipid metabolism. The microbiota also synthesizes vitamin K₂ (menaquinone) and several B‑vitamins (biotin, folate, cobalamin), which are absorbed in the distal small intestine and colon. Beyond metabolism, microbial‑associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) such as lipopolysaccharide and flagellin engage Toll‑like receptors on dendritic cells and macrophages, driving differentiation of regulatory T‑cells (Tregs) and production of interleukin‑10, which maintains mucosal tolerance. Dysbiosis — reduced diversity, loss of butyrate‑producers, expansion of Proteobacteria — is linked to increased intestinal permeability, endotoxemia, and low‑grade systemic inflammation that contributes to insulin resistance and atherosclerosis. Therapeutic manipulation via prebiotics (inulin, fructooligosaccharides), probiotics (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium strains), and fecal microbiota transplantation aims to restore SCFA production, reinforce barrier integrity, and re‑establish immune homeostasis.

Ventilation‑Perfusion Ratio Heterogeneity and Clinical Implications

Effective gas exchange requires matching of alveolar ventilation (V̇A) to pulmonary capillary perfusion (Q̇). The overall V̇A/Q̇ ratio in a healthy adult at rest is approximately 0.8, but regional gravity‑dependent gradients create a spectrum: in the upright lung, the apex (Zone 1) receives relatively high ventilation but low perfusion (V̇A/Q̇ > 3), while the base (Zone 3) receives high perfusion and moderate ventilation (V̇A/Q̇ ≈ 0.6). Zone 2 exhibits intermediate matching. This heterogeneity is quantified by the multiple‑inert‑gas elimination technique (MIGET), which reveals a log‑normal distribution of V̇A/Q̇ ratios with a narrow dispersion in health. Pathological states widen this distribution. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), destruction of alveolar septa reduces capillary bed, creating high V̇A/Q̇ units (dead‑space‑like) alongside low V̇A/Q̇ units from airway obstruction, producing both increased physiological dead space and hypoxemia. Pulmonary embolism abruptly eliminates perfusion to ventilated alveoli, generating infinite V̇A/Q̇ (dead space) and reducing overall Q̇, which raises the alveolar‑arterial oxygen gradient without necessarily causing hypercapnia because ventilation to remaining units increases. Conversely, atelectasis or pneumonia creates shunt (V̇A/Q̇ = 0) where perfused alveoli receive no ventilation; shunt is refractory to supplemental oxygen because blood bypasses ventilated units. The body compensates for V̇A/Q̇ mismatch by hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV), which diverts flow from poorly ventilated regions, but HPV is blunted by acidosis, sepsis, and volatile anesthetics. Clinically, the alveolar gas equation (PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O) − PaCO₂/R) and the calculation of the A‑a gradient (PAO₂ − PaO₂) help differentiate pure hypoventilation (normal A‑a gradient) from V̇A/Q̇ mismatch or shunt (elevated A‑a gradient). Understanding these patterns guides ventilator strategies: low tidal volumes with positive end‑expiratory pressure (PEEP) recruit collapsed alveoli, reducing shunt, while avoiding overdistension that creates high V̇A/Q̇ zones.

Worked Blood‑Gas Interpretation
A 55‑year‑old patient with acute dyspnea has the following arterial blood gas on room air (FiO₂ = 0.21) at sea level (PB = 760 mmHg): pH = 7.32, PaCO₂ = 55 mmHg, PaO₂ = 58 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ = 28 mmol/L. Calculate the alveolar PO₂, the A‑a gradient, and identify the primary acid‑base disorder with compensation.
Alveolar PO₂ (PAO₂) = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O) − PaCO₂/R. Using PH₂O = 47 mmHg, R = 0.8: PAO₂ = 0.21 × (760 − 47) − 55/0.8 = 0.21 × 713 − 68.75 = 149.73 − 68.75 ≈ 81 mmHg. A‑a gradient = PAO₂ − PaO₂ = 81 − 58 = 23 mmHg (normal for age ≈ 10‑15 mmHg, so mildly elevated). pH < 7.40 with elevated PaCO₂ indicates primary respiratory acidosis. Expected acute compensation: HCO₃⁻ rises 1 mmol/L per 10 mmHg PaCO₂ increase → 24 + (55−40)/10 ≈ 25.5 mmol/L. Measured HCO₃⁻ = 28 mmol/L, higher than acute prediction, indicating a superimposed metabolic alkalosis or chronic compensation (chronic: +4 mmol/L per 10 mmHg → 24 + 6 = 30 mmol/L). The value sits between acute and chronic, suggesting a partially compensated chronic respiratory acidosis with a mild metabolic alkalosis. The elevated A‑a gradient points to V̇A/Q̇ mismatch or shunt rather than pure hypoventilation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a blood‑gas question provides FiO₂, PB, and PaCO₂, always compute PAO₂ first using the alveolar gas equation; the A‑a gradient then tells you whether hypoxemia is due to hypoventilation (normal gradient) or V̇A/Q̇ mismatch/shunt (elevated gradient). For acid‑base, apply the stepwise method: identify primary disorder from pH and the corresponding primary parameter (PaCO₂ for respiratory, HCO₃⁻ for metabolic), then check expected compensation using the appropriate formula (acute vs chronic). If measured compensation deviates, a mixed disorder is present.

Cardiac Conduction System Dynamics and Arrhythmogenesis

The heartbeat originates in the sinoatrial (SA) node, where spontaneous diastolic depolarization (phase 4) is driven by the funny current (I_f) carried by HCN channels, complemented by T‑type Ca²⁺ current and delayed rectifier K⁺ current decay. The action potential propagates through atrial myocardium to the atrioventricular (AV) node, where slow conduction (due to low Na⁺ channel expression and predominant L‑type Ca²⁺ current) provides a physiological delay allowing atrial contraction before ventricular activation. The bundle of His and Purkinje fibers express high densities of fast Na⁺ channels (Nav1.5), enabling rapid ventricular depolarization (phase 0 upstroke velocity > 200 V/s). Ventricular repolarization (phases 1‑3) involves transient outward K⁺ current (I_to), L‑type Ca²⁺ current (I_CaL), and delayed rectifier K⁺ currents (I_Kr, I_Ks). Heterogeneity in action‑potential duration across epicardial, mid‑myocardial (M‑cells), and endocardial layers creates a transmural dispersion of repolarization that underlies the T‑wave on the ECG. Arrhythmias arise from three fundamental mechanisms: enhanced automaticity (e.g., ectopic foci in pulmonary veins firing at rates > SA node), triggered activity (early afterdepolarizations from prolonged repolarization, late afterdepolarizations from Ca²⁺ overload), and re‑entry (a circuit of unidirectional block and slow conduction). Atrial fibrillation exemplifies re‑entry maintained by multiple wavelets in a substrate of atrial fibrosis and shortened refractory periods. Ventricular tachycardia in post‑infarction scar relies on fixed anatomical circuits within border zones. Pharmacological modulation targets specific currents: Class I agents block Na⁺ channels (use‑dependent), Class III prolong repolarization by blocking I_Kr/I_Ks, β‑blockers reduce cAMP‑mediated I_f and I_CaL, and Ca²⁺ channel blockers depress AV nodal conduction. Catheter ablation eliminates critical components of re‑entrant circuits (e.g., pulmonary vein isolation for atrial fibrillation, isthmus ablation for typical atrial flutter). Genetic channelopathies (long QT syndrome types 1‑3, Brugada syndrome, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia) illustrate how mutations in KCNQ1, KCNH2, SCN5A, RYR2, or CASQ2 alter ionic currents and predispose to lethal arrhythmias despite structurally normal hearts.

Renal Tubular Transport of Organic Anions, Cations, and Drugs

The proximal tubule is the principal site for secretion of endogenous organic anions (urate, prostaglandins, ketone bodies) and cations (creatinine, histamine, dopamine) as well as a vast array of drugs (penicillins, furosemide, methotrexate, metformin, antiviral agents). Basolateral uptake of anions is mediated primarily by organic anion transporter 1 (OAT1, SLC22A6) and OAT3 (SLC22A8), which exchange intracellular α‑ketoglutarate for extracellular anions in a tertiary active process driven by the Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase‑maintained gradient. Apical efflux into the tubular lumen occurs via multidrug resistance‑associated proteins MRP2 (ABCC2) and MRP4 (ABCC4), and the urate transporter URAT1 (SLC22A12) mediates urate reabsorption. For organic cations, basolateral entry uses organic cation transporter 2 (OCT2, SLC22A2), driven by the negative intracellular membrane potential. Apical exit is facilitated by multidrug and toxin extrusion proteins MATE1 (SLC47A1) and MATE2‑K (SLC47A2), which exchange intracellular cations for luminal H⁺. These transporters exhibit broad substrate overlap, explaining drug‑drug interactions: probenecid inhibits OAT1/3, reducing penicillin clearance; cimetidine blocks OCT2 and MATEs, elevating metformin plasma concentrations and risk of lactic acidosis. Hormonal regulation modulates expression: parathyroid hormone up‑regulates OAT1/3, enhancing urate excretion; glucocorticoids increase OCT2 transcription. In chronic kidney disease, down‑regulation of OATs and OCT2 diminishes secretory capacity, necessitating dose adjustment for renally cleared drugs. The distal nephron contributes minimally to organic solute transport but expresses the peptide transporter PEPT2 for reabsorption of small peptides and β‑lactam antibiotics. Understanding these pathways is essential for predicting nephrotoxicity (e.g., tenofovir accumulation via OAT1/3) and for designing renally safe dosing regimens.

Integrated Physiological Adaptations to Chronic Hypoxia

Sustained exposure to low inspired PO₂ (high altitude, chronic lung disease) activates the hypoxia‑inducible factor (HIF) pathway. Under normoxia, HIF‑1α and HIF‑2α are hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs) using O₂ as a substrate, leading to von Hippel‑Lindau (VHL)‑mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Hypoxia inhibits PHDs, stabilizing HIF‑α subunits, which dimerize with HIF‑1β, translocate to the nucleus, and bind hypoxia‑response elements (HREs) in target genes. Key transcriptional responses include: (1) erythropoietin (EPO) synthesis in renal peritubular fibroblasts and hepatic cells, raising hematocrit and O₂‑carrying capacity; (2) vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and angiopoietin‑2 expression, promoting angiogenesis and increasing capillary density in skeletal muscle and myocardium; (3) up‑regulation of glycolytic enzymes (hexokinase, phosphofructokinase‑1, pyruvate kinase M2) and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), which phosphorylates and inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase, shifting metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to anaerobic glycolysis — the Pasteur effect; (4) increased 2,3‑bisphosphoglycerate (2,3‑BPG) in erythrocytes, right‑shifting the O₂‑hemoglobin dissociation curve and facilitating tissue O₂ unloading; (5) pulmonary vascular remodeling via HIF‑mediated endothelin‑1 and smooth‑muscle proliferation, causing pulmonary hypertension and right‑ventricular hypertrophy (cor pulmonale). Renal adaptation includes enhanced bicarbonate reabsorption (via HIF‑stimulated NHE3 and NBCe1) to compensate for respiratory alkalosis from hyperventilation. Chronic mountain dwellers (e.g., Andeans, Tibetans) exhibit distinct phenotypes: Andeans maintain high hemoglobin concentrations, while Tibetans show blunted EPO response, higher resting ventilation, and greater nitric oxide bioavailability, reflecting genetic selection on EPAS1 (HIF‑2α) and EGLN1 (PHD2) loci. Therapeutic HIF‑prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors (roxadustat, daprodustat) now exploit this pathway to treat anemia of chronic kidney disease by stimulating endogenous EPO production.

  • Assuming all organic anions use a single transporter; OAT1 and OAT3 have distinct substrate affinities and inhibitor profiles.
  • Neglecting that HIF‑1α and HIF‑2α regulate overlapping but non‑identical gene sets; EPO is primarily HIF‑2α‑dependent.
  • Overlooking the role of MATE transporters in apical cation efflux; OCT2 inhibition alone does not fully block renal cation secretion.
  • Treating V̇A/Q̇ mismatch and shunt as interchangeable; shunt is refractory to 100 % O₂, while mismatch improves with supplemental O₂.
  • Forgetting that chronic respiratory alkalosis at altitude triggers renal bicarbonate excretion, not retention.
  • Misapplying the alveolar gas equation by omitting water vapor pressure (47 mmHg) or using an incorrect respiratory quotient.
