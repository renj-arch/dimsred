Digestive System Overview

1.1 Anatomy of the Alimentary Canal

The alimentary canal extends from the oral cavity to the anus and is divided into distinct regions each specialized for a particular phase of digestion. The mouth, pharynx, and oesophagus constitute the upper gastrointestinal tract where mechanical breakdown and the initiation of carbohydrate digestion occur. The stomach, a muscular J‑shaped organ, provides an acidic environment that denatures proteins and activates pepsinogen to pepsin. The small intestine, comprising the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, is the principal site for enzymatic digestion and nutrient absorption, its mucosa amplified by villi and microvilli that increase surface area by several hundredfold. The large intestine, including the caecum, colon, rectum, and anal canal, primarily absorbs water and electrolytes while harbouring a dense microbial flora that ferments undigested carbohydrates.

1.2 Accessory Glands and Their Secretions

Three major accessory glands deliver secretions into the lumen of the alimentary canal. The salivary glands — parotid, submandibular, and sublingual — secrete saliva containing water, electrolytes, mucus, and the enzyme salivary amylase (ptyalin) that begins starch hydrolysis at a near‑neutral pH. The pancreas, a mixed exocrine‑endocrine organ, releases pancreatic juice rich in bicarbonate to neutralise gastric chyme and a suite of enzymes: pancreatic amylase for carbohydrates, trypsinogen and chymotrypsinogen (activated to trypsin and chymotrypsin) for proteins, pancreatic lipase for triglycerides, and nucleases for nucleic acids. The liver produces bile, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, which contains bile salts, phospholipids, cholesterol, and bilirubin; bile salts emulsify fat globules, increasing the interfacial area for lipase action.

1.3 Mechanical and Chemical Digestion

Mechanical digestion reduces food particle size, exposing a larger surface for enzymatic attack. Mastication in the oral cavity, churning by gastric peristalsis, and segmentation movements in the small intestine all contribute. Chemical digestion proceeds through a cascade of hydrolytic reactions catalysed by specific enzymes that operate at optimal pH values: salivary amylase at pH 6.7‑7.0, pepsin at pH 1.5‑2.5, pancreatic enzymes at pH 7.5‑8.5, and brush‑border disaccharidases and peptidases at the microvillar membrane. The coordinated release of secretions is regulated by neural reflexes (vagal stimulation) and hormonal signals (gastrin, secretin, cholecystokinin) that ensure enzymes and bile arrive in synchrony with the arrival of chyme.

1.4 Regulation of Digestive Activity

Digestive activity is governed by the enteric nervous system, extrinsic autonomic inputs, and a suite of gastrointestinal hormones. Gastrin, released by G cells in the antrum, stimulates parietal cells to secrete HCl and chief cells to release pepsinogen. Secretin, released by S cells in the duodenum in response to acidic chyme, prompts pancreatic duct cells to secrete bicarbonate‑rich fluid. Cholecystokinin (CCK), released by I cells upon detection of fatty acids and amino acids, contracts the gallbladder, relaxes the sphincter of Oddi, and stimulates pancreatic enzyme secretion. These hormones also feed back to the brain to modulate appetite and gastric emptying, illustrating the integration of local and systemic control.

Digestion of Macronutrients

2.1 Carbohydrate Digestion

Carbohydrate digestion begins with salivary amylase, which hydrolyses α‑1,4‑glycosidic bonds in starch to produce maltose, maltotriose, and α‑limit dextrins. In the duodenum, pancreatic amylase continues this process. The resulting oligosaccharides are further cleaved by brush‑border enzymes: maltase, sucrase‑isomaltase, and lactase, yielding the monosaccharides glucose, fructose, and galactose. These monosaccharides are then transported across the apical membrane of enterocytes — glucose and galactose via the Na⁺‑dependent SGLT1 cotransporter, fructose via the facilitative GLUT5 transporter — and exit the basolateral membrane through GLUT2 into the portal circulation.

2.2 Protein Digestion

Protein digestion initiates in the stomach where pepsin, activated from pepsinogen by HCl, cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to aromatic amino acids, producing large polypeptides. In the duodenum, trypsinogen is activated to trypsin by enteropeptidase; trypsin then activates chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidases, and proelastase. These pancreatic proteases hydrolyse polypeptides into smaller peptides and free amino acids. Brush‑border peptidases (aminopeptidases, dipeptidases) complete the process. Amino acids and di‑/tripeptides are absorbed: neutral amino acids use the Na⁺‑dependent B⁰AT1 transporter, while di‑/tripeptides employ the H⁺‑coupled PEPT1 transporter. Intracellular peptidases hydrolyse any remaining peptides before basolateral exit via various amino‑acid transporters.

2.3 Lipid Digestion

Lipid digestion is limited in the stomach by gastric lipase, which preferentially hydrolyses short‑ and medium‑chain triglycerides. The bulk of triglyceride digestion occurs in the duodenum where bile salts emulsify fat globules into micelles, dramatically increasing the surface area for pancreatic lipase. Pancreatic lipase, assisted by colipase, cleaves the sn‑1 and sn‑3 positions of triglycerides, yielding two free fatty acids and a 2‑monoacylglycerol. Phospholipase A₂ hydrolyses phospholipids to lysophospholipids and fatty acids, while cholesterol esterase liberates free cholesterol. The products — fatty acids, monoacylglycerols, lysophospholipids, cholesterol, and fat‑soluble vitamins — are incorporated into mixed micelles and diffuse across the unstirred water layer to the enterocyte apical membrane.

2.4 Absorption Mechanisms

Inside the enterocyte, fatty acids and monoacylglycerols are re‑esterified to triglycerides in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, then packaged with apolipoprotein B‑48, phospholipids, cholesterol, and cholesterol esters into chylomicrons. Chylomicrons are secreted by exocytosis into the lacteals of the lymphatic system, bypassing the portal vein. Short‑ and medium‑chain fatty acids, being more water‑soluble, can enter the portal blood directly. Water‑soluble vitamins and minerals follow specific carrier‑mediated or paracellular routes. The coordinated action of transporters, intracellular processing, and lipoprotein assembly ensures efficient delivery of nutrients to the systemic circulation.

Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the effect of increased CO₂ on the oxygen‑hemoglobin dissociation curve, recall the Bohr effect: higher CO₂ (and lower pH) shifts the curve to the right, decreasing Hb affinity for O₂ and promoting O₂ release in metabolically active tissues. This concept is frequently tested with graph‑interpretation items.

Breathing and Exchange of Gases

3.1 Respiratory Tract Anatomy

The respiratory system is divided into the conducting zone and the respiratory zone. The conducting zone — nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and bronchioles down to the terminal bronchioles — warms, humidifies, and filters inspired air but does not participate in gas exchange. The respiratory zone begins with respiratory bronchioles, continues through alveolar ducts, and ends in alveolar sacs composed of numerous alveoli. Each alveolus is lined by a thin simple squamous epithelium (type I pneumocytes) and interspersed with type II pneumocytes that secrete pulmonary surfactant, a mixture of phospholipids and proteins that reduces surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse during expiration.

3.2 Mechanics of Ventilation

Ventilation is driven by pressure gradients generated by the respiratory muscles. During quiet inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and descends, while the external intercostal muscles elevate the ribs, expanding the thoracic cavity and lowering intrapleural pressure to about –3 cmH₂O relative to atmosphere. This pressure drop causes air to flow into the alveoli until alveolar pressure equals atmospheric pressure. Expiration at rest is passive: elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall raises intrapleural pressure, pushing air out. During forced breathing, accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalene, abdominal muscles) augment inspiratory and expiratory volumes, allowing tidal volumes to exceed the resting 500 mL.

3.3 Gas Exchange at the Alveoli

Gas exchange occurs by diffusion across the alveolar‑capillary barrier, which consists of the alveolar epithelium, the fused basement membranes, and the capillary endothelium — a total thickness of ~0.5 µm. The partial pressure gradient for O₂ (PAO₂ ≈ 100 mmHg, PaO₂ ≈ 40 mmHg) drives O₂ into pulmonary capillary blood, while the gradient for CO₂ (PACO₂ ≈ 40 mmHg, PaCO₂ ≈ 45 mmHg) drives CO₂ out. Diffusion is rapid because the surface area of the alveolar membrane (~70 m²) is large and the diffusion distance is minimal. The ventilation‑perfusion (V/Q) ratio is ideally 1.0; regional variations (apex V/Q > 1, base V/Q < 1) cause slight differences in gas tensions that are normally compensated by hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction.

3.4 Transport of Respiratory Gases in Blood

Oxygen is transported primarily bound to haemoglobin (Hb) — each Hb tetramer binds four O₂ molecules cooperatively, giving a sigmoidal O₂‑Hb dissociation curve. The P₅₀ (partial pressure at 50 % saturation) is ~26 mmHg at pH 7.4, 37 °C, and normal 2,3‑BPG levels. Factors that shift the curve rightward (increased temperature, H⁺, CO₂, 2,3‑BPG) lower Hb affinity, facilitating O₂ unloading in tissues. Carbon dioxide is transported in three forms: dissolved in plasma (~10 %), as bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) after hydration catalysed by carbonic anhydrase in erythrocytes (~70 %), and as carbamino compounds bound to Hb (~20 %). The chloride shift (exchange of HCO₃⁻ for Cl⁻ across the red cell membrane) maintains electroneutrality during CO₂ transport.

Circulation and Body Fluids

4.1 Heart Structure and Cardiac Cycle

The human heart is a four‑chambered muscular pump: two atria receive blood, two ventricles eject it. The right atrium receives systemic venous return (~5 L/min) via the superior and inferior vena cavae and the coronary sinus; the left atrium receives oxygenated pulmonary venous return via four pulmonary veins. The cardiac cycle comprises systole (ventricular contraction) and diastole (ventricular relaxation). Atrial systole contributes ~20 % of ventricular filling (the “atrial kick”). Ventricular systole generates pressures of ~120 mmHg in the left ventricle and ~25 mmHg in the right ventricle, opening the aortic and pulmonary valves respectively. The sequence of electrical activation — SA node → AV node → bundle of His → Purkinje fibres — ensures coordinated contraction. Heart sounds S₁ (AV valve closure) and S₂ (semilunar valve closure) mark the onset of systole and diastole.

4.2 Blood Vessels and Hemodynamics

Arteries, with thick tunica media rich in elastic fibres (large arteries) or smooth muscle (muscular arteries), conduct blood under high pressure and dampen pulsatility. Arterioles are the primary resistance vessels; their radius, regulated by sympathetic tone, local metabolites, and endothelial factors (NO, endothelin), determines systemic vascular resistance and thus mean arterial pressure (MAP = CO × SVR). Capillaries, composed of a single endothelial layer with intercellular clefts or fenestrations, are the exchange sites for nutrients, gases, and waste. Venules and veins have larger lumens, thinner walls, and valves (especially in limbs) to aid venous return; they serve as capacitance vessels holding ~60 % of blood volume. The lymphatic system returns interstitial fluid (≈2 L/day) to the venous circulation via the thoracic duct.

4.3 Composition and Functions of Blood

Blood consists of plasma (≈55 % volume) and formed elements (≈45 %). Plasma water carries proteins — albumin (maintains oncotic pressure), globulins (immunoglobulins, transport proteins), fibrinogen (clotting) — electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste. Erythrocytes (RBCs) contain haemoglobin for O₂/CO₂ transport; their biconcave shape maximises surface‑to‑volume ratio and deformability. Leukocytes (WBCs) mediate immunity: neutrophils (phagocytosis), lymphocytes (adaptive immunity), monocytes/macrophages, eosinophils, basophils. Platelets (thrombocytes) are cell fragments essential for primary haemostasis (adhesion, activation, aggregation) and provide a phospholipid surface for coagulation factor assembly. The balance of pro‑ and anticoagulant mechanisms prevents thrombosis while allowing rapid clot formation at injury sites.

4.4 Lymphatic System and Tissue Fluid

Interstitial fluid forms by filtration at the arterial end of capillaries (hydrostatic pressure > oncotic pressure) and is largely reabsorbed at the venous end (oncotic > hydrostatic). The net filtration (~2 L/day) enters blind‑ended lymphatic capillaries, which have overlapping endothelial cells acting as one‑way flaps. Lymph passes through lymph nodes where antigens are presented to lymphocytes, then converges into larger collecting vessels, the thoracic duct (draining most of the body) and the right lymphatic duct (draining right upper quadrant). Lymph flow is propelled by intrinsic smooth muscle contractions, external compression from skeletal muscle pumps, and respiratory pressure changes. The lymphatic system also transports dietary lipids (chylomicrons) from intestinal lacteals, linking digestion to systemic circulation.

Excretion and Osmoregulation

5.1 Kidney Anatomy and Nephron Function

Each kidney contains ~1 million nephrons, the functional units of urine formation. A nephron comprises a renal corpuscle (glomerulus enclosed by Bowman's capsule), a proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), a loop of Henle (descending and ascending limbs), a distal convoluted tubule (DCT), and a collecting duct that drains into the renal pelvis. The glomerulus is a high‑pressure capillary network (glomerular hydrostatic pressure ~45 mmHg) fenestrated to allow filtration of plasma water and solutes while retaining cells and large proteins. The juxtaglomerular apparatus (macula densa, granular JG cells, extraglomerular mesangial cells) monitors distal tubular NaCl delivery and renal perfusion pressure, regulating renin release.

5.2 Formation of Urine: Filtration, Reabsorption, Secretion

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in a healthy adult is ~125 mL/min (≈180 L/day). The filtrate is iso‑osmotic to plasma. In the PCT, ~65 % of filtered Na⁺, water, glucose, amino acids, bicarbonate, and phosphate are reabsorbed via active and passive transporters (Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase on basolateral membrane, SGLT2 for glucose, NHE3 for Na⁺/H⁺ exchange). The descending limb of Henle is highly water‑permeable (aquaporin‑1) but impermeable to solutes, concentrating tubular fluid. The thick ascending limb actively reabsorbs Na⁺, K⁺, 2Cl⁻ via NKCC2, generating a medullary interstitial gradient while remaining water‑impermeable. The DCT and collecting duct fine‑tune Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, and water reabsorption under hormonal control. Secretion of H⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, and certain drugs occurs mainly in the PCT and collecting duct, allowing excretion of substances not filtered or requiring additional removal.

5.3 Hormonal Regulation of Water and Electrolyte Balance

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) released from the posterior pituitary in response to increased plasma osmolality (>285 mOsm/kg) or decreased effective circulating volume acts on V₂ receptors in the late DCT and collecting duct, inserting aquaporin‑2 water channels and increasing water permeability, thus concentrating urine. Aldosterone, secreted by the adrenal cortex in response to angiotensin II and hyperkalaemia, enhances Na⁺ reabsorption (via ENaC) and K⁺ secretion in the cortical collecting duct, expanding extracellular fluid volume. Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), released by atrial stretch, inhibits Na⁺ reabsorption in the collecting duct and suppresses renin and aldosterone, promoting natriuresis and diuresis. The renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system (RAAS) integrates renal perfusion, systemic pressure, and electrolyte status to maintain homeostasis.

5.4 Role of Other Excretory Organs

While the kidneys are the primary excretory organs, the lungs eliminate volatile acids (CO₂) and the skin excretes water, electrolytes, and urea in sweat, especially during heat stress. The liver converts toxic ammonia to urea via the urea cycle, and also conjugates bilirubin for biliary excretion; the intestine eliminates bile pigments and unabsorbed substances in feces. These auxiliary routes complement renal function, ensuring that metabolic waste products, excess ions, and xenobiotics are removed from the body, preserving the internal milieu required for cellular processes.

Regulation of Gastric Acid Secretion and Clinical Correlates

Parietal cells in the oxyntic mucosa secrete hydrochloric acid through the H⁺/K⁺‑ATPase (proton pump) located on the apical canalicular membrane. Basolateral uptake of K⁺ and Cl⁻ via NKCC1 and Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchangers supplies the substrates, while carbonic anhydrase catalyzes CO₂ + H₂O → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; the generated H⁺ is pumped into the lumen in exchange for K⁺, and HCO₃⁻ exits basolaterally, producing the “alkaline tide” in portal blood.

Three principal secretagogues act synergistically: gastrin (released from G cells), histamine (from enterochromaffin‑like cells), and acetylcholine (from vagal post‑ganglionic fibers). Gastrin and acetylcholine bind CCK₂ and M₃ receptors respectively, raising intracellular Ca²⁺; histamine binds H₂ receptors, increasing cAMP. Both pathways converge on protein kinase cascades that mobilize H⁺/K⁺‑ATPase‑containing vesicles to the apical membrane. Somatostatin from D cells provides negative feedback by inhibiting gastrin release, histamine secretion, and parietal cell activity via Gi‑coupled SSTR₂ receptors.

Pharmacological targeting exploits these pathways. H₂‑receptor antagonists (ranitidine, famotidine) blunt the cAMP arm; proton‑pump inhibitors (omeprazole, pantoprazole) covalently block the H⁺/K⁺‑ATPase after activation in the acidic canaliculus. Anticholinergics are rarely used because of systemic side‑effects. In Zollinger‑Ellison syndrome, gastrin‑secreting neuroendocrine tumors cause massive acid output, refractory to standard doses of PPIs; high‑dose PPI therapy and surgical resection of the tumor are required.

Pathophysiologically, chronic hypersecretion predisposes to peptic ulcer disease, especially when mucosal defense (mucus‑bicarbonate layer, prostaglandin‑mediated blood flow) is compromised by NSAIDs or Helicobacter pylori infection. H. pylori urease generates ammonia, neutralizing gastric acid locally and inducing inflammation that disrupts somatostatin signaling, creating a vicious cycle of acid excess and mucosal injury.

Clinical assessment includes basal acid output (BAO) and maximal acid output (MAO) after pentagastrin stimulation; a BAO > 15 mmol/h or MAO > 40 mmol/h suggests hypersecretory states. Endoscopic findings of multiple duodenal ulcers, prominent gastric folds, and elevated fasting serum gastrin (>1000 pg/mL) support the diagnosis of gastrinoma.

Ventilation‑Perfusion Matching and Shunt Physiology

Efficient gas exchange requires that alveolar ventilation (V̇A) and pulmonary capillary perfusion (Q̇) be matched. The ventilation‑perfusion ratio (V̇A/Q̇) varies regionally: in upright lungs, gravity creates a gradient from apex (high V̇A/Q̇ ≈ 3) to base (low V̇A/Q̇ ≈ 0.6). West zones describe the relationship between alveolar pressure (Pₐ), arterial pressure (Pₐ), and venous pressure (Pᵥ): Zone 1 (Pₐ > Pₐ > Pᵥ) has no flow; Zone 2 (Pₐ > Pₐ > Pᵥ) shows flow dependent on arterial‑alveolar pressure difference; Zone 3 (Pₐ > Pᵥ > Pₐ) has continuous flow governed by arterial‑venous gradient.

Low V̇A/Q̇ units behave like shunt: blood passes ventilated alveoli but leaves poorly oxygenated, lowering arterial PO₂. True anatomic shunt (e.g., bronchial circulation, thebesian veins) adds deoxygenated blood directly to the left heart; even 100 % inspired O₂ cannot fully correct hypoxemia from shunt because the shunted fraction never contacts alveolar gas. High V̇A/Q̇ units approximate dead space: ventilation exceeds perfusion, wasting inspired gas without contributing to oxygenation.

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) is a local autoregulatory mechanism that diverts blood from poorly ventilated regions, improving V̇A/Q̇ matching. HPV is mediated by inhibition of voltage‑gated K⁺ channels in pulmonary artery smooth muscle, causing depolarization, Ca²⁺ entry, and vasoconstriction. Chronic hypoxia (high altitude, COPD) remodels pulmonary vasculature, leading to sustained pulmonary hypertension.

Clinical quantification uses the alveolar‑arterial (A‑a) gradient: PAO₂ = FiO₂(PB − PH₂O) − PaCO₂/R; a widened gradient indicates V̇A/Q̇ mismatch or shunt. The shunt fraction (Qs/Qt) can be estimated by the classic equation Qs/Qt = (Cc′O₂ − CaO₂)/(Cc′O₂ − CvO₂), where Cc′O₂ is end‑capillary O₂ content derived from PAO₂. In acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), widespread low V̇A/Q̇ and shunt produce refractory hypoxemia; prone positioning improves dorsal lung perfusion and recruitment, reducing shunt.

Therapeutic strategies target V̇A/Q̇ optimization: supplemental O₂ raises PAO₂ in low V̇A/Q̇ units; positive end‑expiratory pressure (PEEP) recruits collapsed alveoli, converting shunt to low V̇A/Q̇; inhaled nitric oxide selectively vasodilates ventilated regions, enhancing HPV‑mediated redistribution. Understanding these principles guides ventilator management in critical care.

Applied Physiology Question
A 55‑year‑old patient with severe COPD presents with PaO₂ = 55 mmHg on room air (FiO₂ = 0.21). After 100 % O₂ via non‑rebreather mask, PaO₂ rises only to 85 mmHg. Calculate the approximate shunt fraction and explain why the response to 100 % O₂ is limited.
First compute PAO₂ on 100 % O₂: PAO₂ = FiO₂(PB − PH₂O) − PaCO₂/R. Assuming PB = 760 mmHg, PH₂O = 47 mmHg, PaCO₂ = 50 mmHg, R = 0.8 → PAO₂ ≈ 1.0 × (713) − 62.5 ≈ 650 mmHg. End‑capillary O₂ content (Cc′O₂) ≈ 1.34 × Hb × 1.0 + 0.003 × 650. With Hb = 15 g/dL, Cc′O₂ ≈ 20.1 + 1.95 ≈ 22 mL/dL. Arterial O₂ content (CaO₂) at PaO₂ = 85 mmHg: SaO₂ ≈ 0.97 → CaO₂ ≈ 1.34 × 15 × 0.97 + 0.003 × 85 ≈ 19.5 + 0.26 ≈ 19.8 mL/dL. Mixed venous O₂ content (CvO₂) typical ≈ 15 mL/dL. Shunt fraction Qs/Qt = (22 − 19.8)/(22 − 15) ≈ 2.2/7 ≈ 0.31 (31 %). The limited rise in PaO₂ despite 100 % O₂ occurs because shunted blood bypasses ventilated alveoli; increasing alveolar PO₂ cannot oxygenate the shunted fraction, so arterial PO₂ plateaus.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorize the shunt equation and the alveolar gas equation; practice converting between PO₂, SaO₂, and O₂ content using the 1.34 mL/g Hb constant. In multiple‑choice questions, a PaO₂ that fails to rise above 100 mmHg on 100 % O₂ is a classic clue for significant shunt (>20 %).

Cardiac Electrophysiology and Arrhythmia Mechanisms

The cardiac action potential differs between pacemaker (SA node, AV node) and contractile (atrial, ventricular) cells. Pacemaker cells lack a stable resting potential; spontaneous diastolic depolarization (phase 4) is driven by the funny current (I_f, mixed Na⁺/K⁺) and T‑type Ca²⁺ channels, reaching threshold for L‑type Ca²⁺ channel opening (phase 0). Repolarization (phase 3) occurs via delayed‑rectifier K⁺ currents (I_Kr, I_Ks). Contractile cells exhibit a rapid phase 0 upstroke from fast Na⁺ channels (I_Na), a brief phase 1 notch from transient outward K⁺ current (I_to), a prolonged plateau (phase 2) balanced by L‑type Ca²⁺ influx and delayed‑rectifier K⁺ efflux, and final repolarization (phase 3) by I_Kr/I_Ks; phase 4 is stable due to inward‑rectifier K⁺ current (I_K1).

Arrhythmias arise from disorders of impulse formation (automaticity, triggered activity) or impulse conduction (re‑entry). Enhanced automaticity can occur in Purkinje fibers or atrial tissue under catecholaminergic stress, producing focal tachycardias. Triggered activity results from afterdepolarizations: early afterdepolarizations (EADs) during phase 2/3 (e.g., long QT syndrome, drug‑induced I_Kr block) and delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs) after full repolarization (e.g., digitalis toxicity, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia). Both can reach threshold and fire premature beats.

Re‑entry requires a closed loop of tissue with unidirectional block and slow conduction, allowing the impulse to re‑excite recovered tissue. The classic substrate is a region of fibrosis or scar (post‑myocardial infarction) creating anisotropic conduction. Functional re‑entry can also occur without fixed anatomy, as in atrial fibrillation where multiple wavelets circulate in electrically remodeled atria. The wavelength (λ = conduction velocity × refractory period) determines the minimum circuit size; shortening of refractory period (e.g., by hyperkalemia) or slowing conduction (e.g., by Na⁺ channel blockers) promotes re‑entry.

Channelopathies illustrate genotype‑phenotype links. Loss‑of‑function mutations in SCN5A (Naᵥ1.5) cause Brugada syndrome (ST‑elevation in V₁‑V₃, risk of ventricular fibrillation) and cardiac conduction disease. Gain‑of‑function mutations in the same gene produce long QT syndrome type 3 (prolonged QT, pause‑dependent torsades). KCNH2 (hERG) loss‑of‑function yields long QT type 2; KCNQ1 loss‑of‑function yields long QT type 1. These syndromes guide gene‑specific therapy: β‑blockers for LQT1/2, Na⁺ channel blockers (mexiletine) for LQT3, and ICD implantation for high‑risk phenotypes.

Pharmacological modulation targets specific phases. Class I antiarrhythmics block I_Na (use‑dependent), Class II are β‑blockers (reduce automaticity and triggered activity), Class III prolong repolarization by blocking I_Kr/I_Ks (amiodarone, sotalol), and Class IV block L‑type Ca²⁺ channels (verapamil, diltiazem) slowing AV nodal conduction. The Vaughan‑Williams classification, while imperfect, remains a teaching framework for mechanism‑based drug selection.

Renal Acid‑Base Handling and Compensation

The kidneys maintain systemic pH by reclaiming filtered bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and generating new bicarbonate through net acid excretion (NAE). In the proximal tubule, ~80‑90 % of filtered HCO₃⁻ is reabsorbed via Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger (NHE3) and H⁺‑ATPase; secreted H⁺ combines with luminal HCO₃⁻ to form CO₂ and H₂O, which diffuse into the cell and are reconverted to HCO₃⁻ by carbonic anhydrase. The distal nephron (type A intercalated cells) secretes H⁺ via H⁺‑ATPase and H⁺/K⁺‑ATPase, generating new HCO₃⁻ that enters blood via basolateral Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger (AE1).

Net acid excretion equals titratable acidity (TA) plus ammonium (NH₄⁺) excretion minus bicarbonate loss. TA reflects H⁺ buffered by urinary phosphate (HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ → H₂PO₄⁻) and other weak acids; its capacity is limited by filtered phosphate load. Ammonium production in proximal tubule cells from glutamine yields NH₄⁺ and HCO₃⁻; NH₄⁺ is secreted into the lumen via NHE3 (substituting for H⁺) and trapped in the medullary collecting duct by low pH and high medullary NH₃ gradient, allowing high NH₄⁺ excretion without large free H⁺ concentration. In chronic metabolic acidosis, renal ammoniagenesis up‑regulates (via increased glutaminase and PEPCK), raising NH₄⁺ excretion to >200 mmol/day.

Respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis follows the Winter formula: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × [HCO₃⁻] + 8 ± 2. For metabolic alkalosis, compensation is limited; expected PaCO₂ = 0.7 × [HCO₃⁻] + 20 ± 5. Renal compensation for respiratory disorders requires 3‑5 days: in chronic respiratory acidosis, HCO₃⁻ rises ~4 mmol/L per 10 mmHg PaCO₂ increase; in chronic respiratory alkalosis, HCO₃⁻ falls ~5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg PaCO₂ decrease.

The Henderson‑Hasselbalch equation (pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/(0.03 × PaCO₂))) links the three variables. A primary metabolic acidosis (low HCO₃⁻) with appropriate respiratory compensation yields a low PaCO₂ and pH near normal; if PaCO₂ is higher than predicted, a concurrent respiratory acidosis exists. The anion gap (AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)) helps differentiate high‑AG metabolic acidosis (lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, renal failure, toxins) from normal‑AG (renal tubular acidosis, diarrhea). The delta‑gap (ΔAG/ΔHCO₃⁻) reveals mixed disorders: ΔAG > ΔHCO₃⁻ suggests concurrent metabolic alkalosis.

Clinical application: In diabetic ketoacidosis, AG is elevated, HCO₃⁻ low, PaCO₂ appropriately reduced; treatment with insulin and fluids restores HCO₃⁻ generation and reduces keto‑acid production. Over‑rapid correction can cause cerebral edema. In chronic kidney disease, reduced ammoniagenesis limits NH₄⁺ excretion, leading to normal‑AG metabolic acidosis; oral bicarbonate supplementation slows CKD progression and improves muscle protein balance.

Integration of Metabolic Waste Clearance Across Organ Systems

Metabolic waste products are cleared by coordinated action of the liver, lungs, kidneys, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. The liver converts toxic ammonia (from amino‑acid deamination) to urea via the urea cycle (ornithine transcarbamylase, argininosuccinate synthetase, argininosuccinate lyase, arginase). Urea diffuses into blood, is freely filtered at the glomerulus, and ~50 % is passively reabsorbed in the proximal tubule and collecting duct, contributing to medullary osmotic gradient. The remaining urea is excreted, accounting for ~80‑90 % of nitrogenous waste.

Carbon dioxide, the end‑product of oxidative metabolism, is transported in blood as dissolved CO₂, bicarbonate (via carbonic anhydrase in erythrocytes), and carbamino‑hemoglobin. The lungs eliminate ~200 mL/min of CO₂ at rest; ventilation is regulated by central chemoreceptors sensitive to CSF pH (driven by PaCO₂) and peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies) responding to PaO₂, PaCO₂, and pH. In metabolic acidosis, hyperventilation lowers PaCO₂, providing rapid compensation.

The kidneys excrete non‑volatile acids (phosphate, sulfate, organic anions) and regulate bicarbonate. They also clear drugs and toxins via glomerular filtration, tubular secretion (organic anion and cation transporters), and limited reabsorption. The skin eliminates water, electrolytes, and small amounts of urea and lactate in sweat; during heat stress, sweat rates can exceed 1 L/h, contributing to fluid and electrolyte loss but minor nitrogen excretion.

The gastrointestinal tract excretes bilirubin (conjugated by hepatic UDP‑glucuronosyltransferase) into bile; intestinal bacteria convert bilirubin to urobilinogen, partly reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation) and partly excreted in feces as stercobilin. The gut also eliminates unabsorbed dietary oxalate, heavy metals, and certain drug metabolites via feces. In renal failure, the gut assumes a larger role in potassium and phosphate excretion through colonic secretion.

Failure of any single system increases load on others. Hepatic failure reduces urea synthesis, causing hyperammonemia and cerebral edema; renal failure impairs acid excretion, leading to metabolic acidosis that stimulates respiratory compensation (Kussmaul breathing). In severe COPD, chronic CO₂ retention blunts chemoreceptor sensitivity, making the patient reliant on hypoxic drive; supplemental O₂ can precipitate hypercapnic respiratory failure. Understanding these interdependencies guides multi‑organ support in critical illness.

  • Confusing the roles of H₂‑receptor antagonists and proton‑pump inhibitors in acid suppression; PPIs require acidic environment for activation, H₂ blockers do not.
  • Assuming 100 % O₂ fully corrects hypoxemia from shunt; true shunt is refractory because shunted blood never contacts alveolar gas.
  • Misapplying the Winter formula to metabolic alkalosis; it is valid only for metabolic acidosis.
  • Overlooking that ammonium excretion, not titratable acidity, is the major adaptive response in chronic metabolic acidosis.
  • Thinking the anion gap alone diagnoses the cause of metabolic acidosis; a normal anion gap does not exclude toxic alcohol ingestion early in the course.
  • Believing that hepatic urea production is the sole route for nitrogen disposal; gut bacterial urease also recycles urea nitrogen.
  • Neglecting that renal compensation for respiratory disorders requires days, not hours, and that acute respiratory acidosis shows minimal bicarbonate rise.
