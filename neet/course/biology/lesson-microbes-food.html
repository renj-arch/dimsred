1. Microbes in Food Production

1.1 Fermentation by Lactic Acid Bacteria

Lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacillus, Streptococcus and Leuconostoc convert fermentable sugars into lactic acid through homolactic or heterolactic pathways, lowering the pH of the substrate and inhibiting spoilage organisms. In dairy processing, the acidification coagulates casein, giving yogurt its characteristic gel texture and tangy flavour, while in vegetable fermentations such as sauerkraut and kimchi the same acid preservation extends shelf life for months without refrigeration. The metabolic activity of these bacteria also produces bacteriocins, exopolysaccharides and flavour compounds such as diacetyl, which contribute to the sensory profile of the final product. Because the fermentation is anaerobic and occurs at relatively low temperatures (20–30 °C), it is energy‑efficient and can be carried out at household scale as well as in large industrial vats.

1.2 Yeast in Bread and Alcoholic Beverages

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is the principal yeast used in bread making and in the production of beer, wine and distilled spirits. In dough, yeast ferments maltose and glucose to carbon dioxide and ethanol; the gas expands the gluten network, causing the dough to rise, while the ethanol evaporates during baking, leaving a light crumb structure. In brewing, the same glycolytic pathway yields ethanol and carbon dioxide, but the process is managed under controlled temperature, pH and nutrient regimes to obtain desired alcohol content and flavour signatures. Different strains of S. cerevisiae and related species such as Saccharomyces pastorianus (lager yeast) have been selected for specific fermentation temperatures, flocculation behaviour and ester profiles, illustrating how microbial diversity is harnessed for product differentiation.

1.3 Traditional Fermented Products and Their Microbial Consortia

Many traditional foods rely on complex, naturally occurring consortia rather than pure cultures. Idli and dosa batters in South India are fermented by a mixture of Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Lactobacillus plantarum and yeasts such as Candida spp., which together produce carbon dioxide for leavening and lactic acid for sourness. Similarly, the Indonesian tempeh is produced by the filamentous fungus Rhizopus oligosporus that binds soybeans into a compact cake while hydrolysing anti‑nutritional factors. In West African ogi, a slurry of maize, millet or sorghum is fermented by Lactobacillus, Enterococcus and Saccharomyces species, improving protein digestibility and vitamin B‑complex content. These examples demonstrate that the functional outcome of a fermentation depends on the synergistic interactions among multiple microbial groups, each contributing distinct metabolic capabilities.

2. Microbes in Industrial Production

2.1 Antibiotics from Filamentous Fungi and Actinomycetes

The discovery of penicillin from Penicillium notatum (later Penicillium chrysogenum) in 1928 marked the beginning of large‑scale microbial antibiotic production. Filamentous fungi such as Penicillium and Cephalosporium secrete β‑lactam antibiotics into the fermentation broth, while actinomycetes, notably Streptomyces species, are the source of a vast repertoire including streptomycin, tetracycline, erythromycin and the glycopeptide vancomycin. Industrial strains are improved through classical mutagenesis, genome shuffling and, more recently, CRISPR‑based genome editing to increase titre, reduce by‑product formation and simplify downstream purification. Fermentations are carried out in fed‑batch bioreactors of 100–500 m³, with controlled dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature and carbon‑nitrogen ratios to maintain the secondary‑metabolite production phase, which typically begins after the primary growth phase has ceased.

2.2 Enzyme Production for Detergents and Textiles

Proteases, amylases, lipases and cellulases produced by Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus licheniformis and Aspergillus niger dominate the global enzyme market. In detergent formulations, alkaline proteases from Bacillus spp. hydrolyse protein stains at pH 9–11 and temperatures up to 60 °C, while amylases remove starch‑based soils. The textile industry uses cellulases for bio‑polishing of cotton, reducing pilling and improving fabric softness, and uses pectinases for degumming of ramie and hemp. Enzyme production employs submerged fermentation on inexpensive carbon sources such as corn steep liquor, wheat bran or molasses, followed by concentration through ultrafiltration and spray‑drying to yield stable granular preparations. Protein engineering has produced variants with enhanced thermostability, oxidative resistance and reduced allergenicity, expanding the operational window of these biocatalysts.

2.3 Organic Acids, Vitamins and Bio‑fuels

Citric acid, the most widely produced organic acid, is manufactured by Aspergillus niger in aerobic submerged fermentations exceeding 2 million tonnes per year; the fungus accumulates citrate when grown under high sugar, low phosphate and limited manganese conditions. Lactic acid, a platform chemical for polylactic acid (PLA) bioplastics, is produced by Lactobacillus species in anaerobic fermentations that achieve >150 g L⁻¹ with high optical purity. Vitamin B₁₂ (cobalamin) is commercially obtained from Propionibacterium freudenreichii and Pseudomonas denitrificans through fed‑batch processes that require cobalt supplementation. In the bio‑fuel sector, Clostridium acetobutylicum ferments sugars to acetone‑butanol‑ethanol (ABE) while engineered Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains produce isobutanol, farnesene and other advanced bio‑fuels. These processes illustrate how metabolic pathway manipulation and process integration enable microbes to replace petrochemical routes for a growing list of commodities.

Example Question
A wastewater treatment plant uses an activated sludge process followed by an anaerobic digester. The activated sludge reactor operates at a hydraulic retention time of 6 hours and a mixed liquor suspended solids concentration of 3 g L⁻¹. The anaerobic digester receives the waste sludge at a solids retention time of 20 days and produces biogas with 60 % methane. Calculate the approximate daily methane volume (in m³) generated if the plant treats 10 000 m³ of wastewater per day with an influent chemical oxygen demand (COD) of 400 mg L⁻¹, assuming 80 % COD removal in the activated sludge stage and 90 % of the removed COD is converted to methane in the digester (1 g COD → 0.35 L CH₄ at STP).
First, compute the total COD load entering the plant: 10 000 m³ day⁻¹ × 400 mg L⁻¹ = 4 000 000 g day⁻¹ = 4 000 kg day⁻¹. The activated sludge removes 80 % of this COD, so removed COD = 0.80 × 4 000 kg = 3 200 kg day⁻¹. Of the removed COD, 90 % is directed to the anaerobic digester as sludge, giving digester COD = 0.90 × 3 200 kg = 2 880 kg day⁻¹. Using the conversion factor 1 g COD → 0.35 L CH₄, the methane volume = 2 880 000 g × 0.35 L g⁻¹ = 1 008 000 L = 1 008 m³ day⁻¹. Since the biogas contains 60 % methane, the total biogas volume would be 1 008 m³ ÷ 0.60 ≈ 1 680 m³ day⁻¹, but the question asks for methane volume, which is 1 008 m³ day⁻¹.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem gives COD removal percentages for sequential units, always apply each percentage to the load exiting the previous unit, not to the original influent load. This stepwise mass‑balance approach avoids the common error of double‑counting removal efficiencies.

3. Microbes in Sewage Treatment

3.1 Aerobic Activated Sludge Process

The activated sludge process relies on a dense suspension of aerobic microorganisms—bacteria, protozoa and small metazoa—maintained in an aeration tank where fine‑bubble diffusers supply dissolved oxygen at 1–3 mg L⁻¹. The microbial flocs oxidise soluble organic carbon (measured as BOD₅) to carbon dioxide and water, while simultaneously nitrifying ammonia to nitrite and nitrate through the action of ammonia‑oxidising bacteria (Nitrosomonas) and nitrite‑oxidising bacteria (Nitrobacter). A portion of the mixed liquor is continuously recycled from the secondary clarifier to maintain a high mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) concentration, typically 2–4 g L⁻¹, which ensures a high specific substrate utilisation rate. The sludge age (solids retention time) is controlled by wasting a fraction of the biomass; typical values of 5–15 days balance nitrification capacity with sludge production costs.

3.2 Anaerobic Digestion and Methane Recovery

Primary and secondary sludge from the aerobic stage are thickened and fed to anaerobic digesters where hydrolytic, acidogenic, acetogenic and methanogenic communities act sequentially. Hydrolytic bacteria break down complex polymers (proteins, lipids, polysaccharides) into soluble monomers; acidogens convert these to volatile fatty acids (VFAs), alcohols, hydrogen and carbon dioxide; acetogens further transform VFAs to acetate, hydrogen and carbon dioxide; finally, methanogenic archaea (Methanosaeta, Methanosarcina) produce methane either by acetoclastic cleavage of acetate or by hydrogenotrophic reduction of carbon dioxide. The process operates at mesophilic (35 °C) or thermophilic (55 °C) temperatures, with hydraulic retention times of 15–30 days. The biogas generated (typically 55–65 % CH₄) can be combusted in combined heat and power units, providing electricity for plant operation and heat for digester temperature control.

3.3 Trickling Filters and Biofilm Reactors

Trickling filters distribute wastewater over a packed bed of inert media (rock, plastic rings) on which a biofilm of aerobic microbes develops. As the liquid trickles down, oxygen diffuses from the air into the biofilm, supporting oxidation of organic matter and nitrification. The biofilm thickness self‑regulates: excessive growth sloughs off and is captured in a secondary clarifier. Modern variants include rotating biological contactors (RBCs) where discs rotate through the wastewater, and moving‑bed biofilm reactors (MBBRs) that use free‑floating carriers to increase surface area while maintaining a suspended‑growth component. These systems are valued for their low energy demand (no mechanical aeration) and resilience to shock loads, making them suitable for small‑to‑medium municipalities and industrial pretreatment.

4. Microbes in Biogas Production

4.1 Methanogenic Archaea and Their Metabolic Pathways

Methanogens belong to the domain Archaea and are strict anaerobes that generate methane as the terminal step of anaerobic respiration. The two principal pathways are acetoclastic methanogenesis, performed mainly by Methanosaeta (high affinity for acetate) and Methanosarcina (versatile, can also use methylated compounds), and hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, carried out by genera such as Methanobacterium, Methanobrevibacter and Methanothermobacter, which reduce carbon dioxide with hydrogen to methane. A minor methylotrophic route uses methanol, methylamines or dimethyl sulfide. The overall stoichiometry for acetate conversion is CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₄ + CO₂, while for hydrogenotrophic reduction it is 4 H₂ + CO₂ → CH₄ + 2 H₂O. The relative contribution of each pathway depends on substrate composition, temperature and the microbial community structure, which can be monitored by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing or quantitative PCR of functional genes such as mcrA.

4.2 Feedstock Pre‑treatment and Process Optimisation

Lignocellulosic residues (agricultural straw, corn stover, sugarcane bagasse) require pre‑treatment to disrupt the recalcitrant lignin‑carbohydrate matrix and increase cellulose accessibility. Physical methods (milling, steam explosion), chemical methods (dilute acid, alkaline hydrolysis) and biological methods (fungal pretreatment with Phanerochaete chrysosporium) are employed singly or in combination. After pre‑treatment, the hydrolysate is fed to a continuously stirred tank reactor (CSTR) or a plug‑flow digester where the organic loading rate (OLR) is maintained between 1–5 kg VS m⁻³ day⁻¹ to avoid VFA accumulation and process instability. Co‑digestion of nitrogen‑rich substrates (animal manure, food waste) with carbon‑rich lignocellulose balances the C/N ratio near 20–30, optimising microbial growth and methane yield. Trace element supplementation (Ni, Co, Fe, Mo) is often required because methanogens depend on nickel‑containing cofactors (F₄₃₀) for the methyl‑coenzyme M reductase reaction.

4.3 Utilisation of Biogas for Energy and Upgrading to Biomethane

Raw biogas typically contains 55–65 % methane, 35–45 % carbon dioxide, trace hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), water vapour and siloxanes. For combined heat and power (CHP) units, the gas is desulfurised (often by biological scrubbers using Thiobacillus spp.) and dried before combustion in gas engines achieving electrical efficiencies of 35–42 % and overall efficiencies above 80 % when heat is recovered. Upgrading to biomethane (>95 % CH₄) for injection into natural‑gas grids or use as vehicle fuel employs pressure‑swing adsorption, water scrubbing, amine absorption or membrane separation to remove CO₂ and H₂S. The upgraded biomethane can be compressed (CNG) or liquefied (LNG), providing a renewable substitute for fossil natural gas while closing the carbon loop of the original feedstock.

5. Microbes as Biocontrol Agents

5.1 Bacterial Insecticides such as Bacillus thuringiensis

Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) produces crystal (Cry) proteins during sporulation that are toxic to specific insect orders when ingested. Different subspecies (e.g., kurstaki for Lepidoptera, israelensis for Diptera, tenebrionis for Coleoptera) express distinct Cry toxin repertoires, allowing targeted pest control with minimal impact on non‑target organisms. Formulated as wettable powders, suspension concentrates or encapsulated granules, Bt products are applied to foliage where larvae feed; the alkaline midgut of susceptible insects solubilises the crystals, proteases activate the toxins, which then bind to specific midgut receptors, forming pores that cause cell lysis and death. Resistance management strategies include refuge planting, gene pyramiding in transgenic crops (Bt cotton, Bt maize) and rotation with other modes of action.

5.2 Fungal Antagonists like Trichoderma Species

Trichoderma harzianum, T. viride and T. atroviride are widely used as biocontrol agents against soil‑borne phytopathogens such as Rhizoctonia solani, Sclerotium rolfsii and Fusarium oxysporum. Their mechanisms include mycoparasitism (coiling and penetration of pathogen hyphae), secretion of cell‑wall degrading enzymes (chitinases, glucanases, proteases), competition for nutrients and space, and induction of systemic resistance in the host plant via salicylic‑acid and jasmonic‑acid pathways. Commercial formulations are produced by solid‑state fermentation on agricultural by‑products (wheat bran, rice husk) to generate high conidial counts (>10⁹ cfu g⁻¹) that remain viable after drying. Application methods range from seed coating and root dipping to soil drenching and foliar spray, with efficacy enhanced when combined with organic amendments that support fungal establishment.

5.3 Viral and Protozoan Biocontrol Options

Baculoviruses, particularly nucleopolyhedroviruses (NPVs) and granuloviruses (GVs), infect Lepidopteran pests with high specificity; the occlusion bodies protect virions in the environment and dissolve in the alkaline insect midgut, releasing virions that replicate in host nuclei, leading to liquefaction and death. Commercial NPV products (e.g., Helicoverpa armigera NPV) are applied as aqueous sprays and are compatible with integrated pest management because they do not affect beneficial insects. Protozoan pathogens such as Nosema locustae (a microsporidian) have been used against grasshoppers and locusts; the spores are ingested, germinate in the gut, and cause chronic infection that reduces fecundity and mobility. While viral and protozoan agents offer exquisite host specificity, their field persistence can be limited by UV radiation, requiring formulation with UV protectants or evening application.

Probiotic Microbes and Their Mechanisms in Human Gut Health

Probiotic microorganisms, primarily Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and certain Saccharomyces strains, confer health benefits when administered in adequate viable numbers. Their principal mechanisms include competitive exclusion of pathogens for mucosal adhesion sites, production of bacteriocins and organic acids that lower luminal pH, modulation of host immune responses through dendritic‑cell activation and cytokine profiling, and enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity by up‑regulating tight‑junction proteins such as claudin‑1 and occludin. Clinical evidence indicates strain‑specific effects; for example, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG has been shown to reduce the duration of acute infectious diarrhoea in children, while Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis 35624 has demonstrated benefit in alleviating symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome. The survival of probiotic cells through gastric acidity and bile salts is a critical quality attribute; encapsulation in alginate‑chitosan matrices or spray‑drying with protective carriers (e.g., skim milk, trehalose) can improve gastric transit viability significantly (e.g., from <10 % to >70 % in some studies). Regulatory guidelines (FAO/WHO 2002) require identification to the strain level, demonstration of safety (absence of transferable antibiotic‑resistance genes), and evidence of efficacy in well‑designed human trials.

Strain‑Level Identification and Genomic Safety

Whole‑genome sequencing has become the standard for confirming taxonomic assignment and screening for virulence factors, mobile genetic elements, and antimicrobial‑resistance determinants. A probiotic candidate must lack genes encoding toxins (e.g., cyl operon in Enterococcus) and must not harbour plasmids capable of horizontal transfer. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) list streamlines assessment for well‑characterised species, but each novel strain still requires a dossier covering phenotypic and genotypic data.

Delivery Formats and Dose‑Response Relationships

Typical commercial products deliver 10⁸–10¹⁰ colony‑forming units (cfu) per dose. Meta‑analyses suggest a dose‑response plateau around 10⁹ cfu day⁻¹ for many gastrointestinal endpoints; higher doses have not consistently shown improved outcomes and may increase cost. Synbiotic formulations that combine probiotics with prebiotic fibres (inulin, fructo‑oligosaccharides) enhance colonisation by providing fermentable substrates that stimulate growth of the administered strains and indigenous beneficial microbes.

Metabolic Engineering of Industrial Microorganisms for Chemical Synthesis

Rational redesign of central carbon metabolism in Escherichia coli, Corynebacterium glutamicum, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae enables the production of platform chemicals such as succinic acid, 1,3‑propanediol, and isobutanol at high titres (exceeding 100 g L⁻¹ in some fed‑batch bioreactors). Key strategies include (i) deletion of competing pathways (e.g., ldhA, pta, ackA in E. coli to reduce lactate and acetate formation), (ii) overexpression of heterologous enzymes with superior kinetic properties (e.g., Clostridium acetobutylicum CoA‑dependent aldehyde dehydrogenase for butanol pathway), and (iii) dynamic regulation using metabolite‑responsive promoters or CRISPR‑i circuits that balance growth and production phases. Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under product‑selective pressure can improve tolerance; for example, evolved C. glutamicum strains have been reported to tolerate >120 g L⁻¹ L‑lysine, representing a significant increase over the parental line. Process integration—such as in‑situ product removal by membrane extraction or reactive distillation—mitigates product inhibition and reduces downstream purification costs.

Genome‑Scale Models and Flux Balance Analysis

Genome‑scale metabolic models (GEMs) like iML1515 for E. coli and Yeast8 for S. cerevisiae enable in silico prediction of gene knockout targets that maximise theoretical yields. Flux balance analysis (FBA) coupled with thermodynamic feasibility constraints (tFBA) identifies bottlenecks such as cofactor imbalance (NADPH/NADP⁺) that are resolved by introducing transhydrogenase cycles or pentose‑phosphate pathway overexpression. The Design‑Build‑Test‑Learn (DBTL) cycle, accelerated by automated liquid‑handling robots and high‑throughput screening, shortens strain development timelines from years to months.

Case Study: Bio‑Based 1,4‑Butanediol (BDO)

A commercial route to BDO has been developed using a genetically modified E. coli strain expressing a heterologous pathway from Clostridium and Pseudomonas that converts glucose to 4‑hydroxybutyrate, then to BDO via a CoA‑dependent reductase. Reported processes have achieved titres around 95 g L⁻¹ with yields of ~0.48 g g⁻¹ glucose at large scale (e.g., 500 m³ fermenters), demonstrating the scalability of metabolically engineered microbes for drop‑in replacements of petrochemical intermediates.

Examiner's Twist: Cofactor Balancing in Engineered Pathways
A recombinant S. cerevisiae strain expresses a bacterial pathway for 3‑hydroxypropionic acid (3‑HP) that consumes two NADPH per mole of product. The native yeast metabolism generates NADPH primarily via the oxidative pentose‑phosphate pathway (PPP). If the PPP flux is limited, 3‑HP titre drops sharply. Propose a genetic intervention that restores NADPH supply without compromising growth.
Introduce a heterologous NADP⁺‑dependent malic enzyme (e.g., from Aspergillus niger) to convert malate to pyruvate while generating NADPH. Simultaneously overexpress the native ZWF1 (glucose‑6‑phosphate dehydrogenase) to boost PPP flux. This dual approach raises the NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio, sustaining 3‑HP production while maintaining glycolytic flux for biomass formation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a metabolic engineering solution, first identify the limiting cofactor or precursor, then name a specific enzyme (with organism source) that addresses the gap. Cite the DBTL cycle and mention GEM‑guided target selection to show depth.

Microbial Bioremediation of Heavy Metals and Persistent Organic Pollutants

Microorganisms detoxify contaminated environments through biosorption, bioaccumulation, biotransformation, and biomineralisation. Gram‑negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas putida KT2440 express metal‑binding peptides (e.g., metallothioneins) and efflux pumps (CzcCBA) that can sequester Cd²⁺, Zn²⁺, and Pb²⁺ at concentrations up to 5 mM in laboratory studies. Fungal mycelia of Aspergillus niger and Penicillium chrysogenum provide high surface‑area biosorbents; dead biomass has been shown to remove >90 % of Cu²⁺ from aqueous solutions at pH 5.0 within 30 minutes in batch experiments. For persistent organic pollutants (POPs) like polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), aerobic degraders (Rhodococcus sp., Mycobacterium sp.) initiate ring‑hydroxylating dioxygenase attacks, while anaerobic dehalogenators (Dehalococcoides mccartyi) perform reductive dechlorination of highly chlorinated congeners. Consortium approaches combine complementary catabolic pathways; a defined co‑culture of Pseudomonas sp. strain LB400 (PCB upper pathway) and Dehalococcoides sp. CBDB1 (lower pathway) has been reported to achieve complete mineralisation of Aroclor 1260 in microcosm studies.

Genetic Tools for Enhanced Bioremediation

CRISPR‑Cas9 mediated genome editing enables insertion of catabolic operons into robust hosts. Integration of the bph operon (biphenyl degradation) into the chromosome of Pseudomonas putida KT2440 eliminates plasmid instability and improves survival in soil. Synthetic riboswitches responsive to target pollutants (e.g., a toluene‑responsive riboswitch controlling tod operon expression) provide auto‑induction, reducing metabolic burden when contaminants are absent.

Field‑Scale Considerations

Bioaugmentation success depends on inoculum viability, nutrient amendment (N:P ratio ~10:1), and redox management. In situ biostimulation with electron donors (acetate, lactate) promotes anaerobic dechlorination, while forced aeration supports aerobic PAH degradation. Monitoring uses quantitative PCR of functional genes (bphA, tceA) and compound‑specific isotope analysis (CSIA) to verify biodegradation versus dilution.

Regulatory Frameworks and Safety Assessment of Microbial Products

Microbial products intended for food, feed, agriculture, or environmental release are governed by overlapping regulatory regimes. In the European Union, the Novel Food Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 requires a pre‑market safety dossier for any microorganism not used as food before 1997; the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) evaluates taxonomic identity, absence of toxigenic potential, antibiotic‑resistance profile, and history of safe use. The United States FDA applies the Generally Recognised As Safe (GRAS) notification pathway for probiotics and enzyme preparations, while the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) regulates microbial pesticides under the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA), requiring tiered toxicity testing (acute oral, inhalation, dermal, and non‑target organism assays). For genetically modified microorganisms (GMMs), the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety and national biosafety laws (e.g., India’s Rules 1989 under the Environment Protection Act) mandate risk assessment covering horizontal gene transfer, survival, and ecological impact. Dossier preparation follows the OECD “Guidance Document for the Risk Assessment of Microorganisms” which structures data into identity, hazard characterisation, exposure assessment, and risk management.

Antibiotic‑Resistance Gene Transfer Risk

A critical safety criterion is the absence of transferable resistance determinants. Whole‑genome sequencing coupled with plasmid replicon typing identifies mobile elements. Phenotypic confirmation uses broth microdilution (EUCAST breakpoints) and conjugation assays with recipient strains (e.g., E. coli J53). Strains harbouring chromosomal resistance without mobile flanking sequences are generally acceptable if the resistance is not clinically relevant.

Post‑Market Surveillance

Regulators increasingly require post‑market monitoring plans. For probiotic foods, adverse‑event reporting systems (e.g., FDA’s MedWatch, EFSA’s Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed) capture rare infections in immunocompromised consumers. For microbial biopesticides, field‑level resistance monitoring of target pests (e.g., Helicoverpa armigera to Bt toxins) informs resistance‑management strategies such as refuge deployment and rotation with chemical modes of action.

Synthetic Biology Approaches to Design Microbial Consortia

Engineered microbial consortia distribute metabolic labour among specialised members, improving pathway robustness and product yields compared with single‑strain platforms. A classic design pairs a “producer” strain that converts a cheap substrate (e.g., glycerol) into an intermediate (3‑hydroxypropionaldehyde) with a “converter” strain that transforms the intermediate into the final product (1,3‑propanediol). Spatial organisation is achieved by co‑immobilisation in alginate beads or by synthetic adhesion modules (e.g., cohesin‑dockerin pairs) that enforce proximity. Dynamic control uses quorum‑sensing circuits (LuxI/LuxR, LasI/LasR) to synchronise population ratios; a “population‑control” module expresses a toxin‑antitoxin system that limits overgrowth of the faster‑growing partner. Recent advances include the use of orthogonal ribosomes and tRNA synthetases to insulate synthetic pathways from host regulation, and the implementation of metabolic “division of labour” where each member lacks a different essential amino acid, forcing cross‑feeding and stabilising coexistence.

Modelling Consortium Dynamics

Multi‑species genome‑scale models (e.g., COMETS, BacArena) simulate metabolite exchange, spatial gradients, and evolutionary pressures. These tools predict optimal inoculation ratios (often 1:3 to 1:10 producer:converter) and identify emergent auxotrophies that can be engineered as safety locks. Experimental validation employs microfluidic chemostats with real‑time fluorescence reporters for each population, enabling parameter estimation for model refinement.

Applications Beyond Chemicals

Consortia are being deployed for nitrogen fixation in cereal crops (synthetic Azotobacter‑Klebsiella pairs), for in situ degradation of mixed contaminants (aerobic PAH degrader + anaerobic dechlorinator), and for modular biomanufacturing where each module (precursor supply, redox balancing, product secretion) resides in a separate chassis, allowing rapid re‑configuration for new targets.

  • Assuming all probiotic strains behave identically; strain‑specific genomic and phenotypic data are essential for safety and efficacy claims.
  • Overlooking cofactor balancing when designing heterologous pathways; NADPH/NADH mismatches are a primary cause of low titres.
  • Treating biosorption as irreversible removal; desorption can occur with pH shifts, so immobilised biomass must be managed as hazardous waste.
  • Confusing GRAS status with regulatory approval for all uses; GRAS applies only to the notified food use, not to environmental release.
  • Neglecting population‑control mechanisms in synthetic consortia; without them, the faster grower outcompetes the partner, collapsing the division of labour.
  • Using only 16S rRNA for strain identification in dossiers; whole‑genome sequencing is now the regulatory expectation for novel microbes.
  • Believing that higher probiotic doses always improve outcomes; clinical data show a plateau around 10⁹ cfu day⁻¹ for most gastrointestinal endpoints.

Microbial Fuel Cells and Electrochemical Synthesis

Microbial fuel cells (MFCs) convert the chemical energy of organic substrates directly into electrical current through the catalytic activity of electroactive microorganisms. The anode‑respiring bacteria, notably Geobacter sulfurreducens and Shewanella oneidensis, oxidise acetate, lactate or wastewater organics while transferring electrons to a solid anode via outer‑membrane c‑type cytochromes, conductive pili (nanowires) or soluble redox mediators. The cathode reaction is typically the reduction of oxygen to water, catalysed by a platinum‑group metal or a non‑precious metal catalyst such as nitrogen‑doped carbon. Typical power densities for laboratory‑scale single‑chamber MFCs range from 0.1 to 1 W m⁻² of anode surface, with coulombic efficiencies of 60–90 % with acetate as sole electron donor. Stacking multiple units in series or parallel raises voltage and current to levels suitable for low‑power sensors, remote monitoring devices, or trickle‑charging of capacitors.

Beyond electricity generation, the same electrode‑microbe interface can be operated in reverse as a microbial electrosynthesis cell (MES). Supplying a cathodic potential more negative than the redox potential of CO₂/acetate enables acetogenic microbes such as Clostridium ljungdahlii or Sporomusa ovata to fix carbon dioxide into multicarbon products (acetate, butyrate, ethanol) using electrons derived from the cathode. The overall energy efficiency of MES is limited by overpotentials at both electrodes and by the thermodynamic requirement of at least −0.28 V versus the standard hydrogen electrode for CO₂ reduction to acetate. Recent designs incorporate gas‑diffusion cathodes and membrane‑electrode assemblies to reduce ohmic losses and separate compartments, improving product selectivity and current density.

Key engineering challenges include biofilm thickness management, internal resistance reduction, and long‑term stability of the anode biofilm under fluctuating substrate loads. Periodic polarity reversal or low‑amplitude alternating current has been shown to control excessive biofilm growth without loss of electroactivity. For scale‑up, modular stack architectures with shared electrolyte recirculation are preferred over monolithic large‑area electrodes because they simplify hydraulic distribution and allow individual module replacement. Economic analyses indicate that MFCs become competitive only when coupled to wastewater treatment where the value of pollutant removal offsets the modest electricity revenue; standalone power generation remains uneconomical at current performance levels.

Polyhydroxyalkanoate Production by Bacteria

Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) are intracellular polyesters accumulated by many bacteria as carbon and energy reserves under nutrient‑limited, carbon‑excess conditions. The most widely studied producer, Cupriavidus necator (formerly Ralstonia eutropha), can accumulate up to 80 % of cell dry weight as poly‑3‑hydroxybutyrate (PHB) when grown on glucose, glycerol or waste cooking oil under nitrogen limitation. The biosynthetic pathway comprises three core enzymes: β‑ketothiolase (PhaA) condenses two acetyl‑CoA molecules to acetoacetyl‑CoA; acetoacetyl‑CoA reductase (PhaB) reduces this to (R)‑3‑hydroxybutyryl‑CoA using NADPH; and PHA synthase (PhaC) polymerises the hydroxyacyl‑CoA monomers into the polyester chain. Variability in monomer composition is achieved by supplying diverse carbon precursors (e.g., valerate for 3‑hydroxyvalerate, or odd‑chain fatty acids for 3‑hydroxyhexanoate), yielding copolymers such as poly(3‑hydroxybutyrate‑co‑3‑hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV) with improved thermal and mechanical properties.

Industrial production employs fed‑batch fermentations where the carbon feed is maintained at a high C/N ratio after an initial growth phase. Volumetric productivities of 1.5–3 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹ and final PHA concentrations of 80–120 g L⁻¹ have been achieved. Downstream recovery relies on cell disruption (high‑pressure homogenisation or chemical lysis) followed by solvent extraction (chloroform, propylene carbonate) or non‑solvent methods such as supercritical CO₂ extraction and enzymatic digestion of non‑PHA cell mass. The choice of recovery route strongly influences polymer molecular weight and residual solvent limits for biomedical applications.

Metabolic engineering has expanded the substrate spectrum to include lignocellulosic hydrolysates, crude glycerol from biodiesel, and even methane via engineered methanotrophs. Knock‑out of competing pathways (e.g., phaZ depolymerase, ackA‑pta acetate kinase) and over‑expression of NADPH‑generating enzymes (glucose‑6‑phosphate dehydrogenase, malic enzyme) raise the intracellular NADPH pool, alleviating the redox bottleneck at the PhaB step. CRISPR‑based multiplex editing in Pseudomonas putida KT2440 has enabled simultaneous integration of a heterologous PHA operon and deletion of β‑oxidation genes, achieving 65 % cell dry weight PHA on glucose with reduced fermentation time.

Examiner's trap
A question states: "In a microbial fuel cell, the anode potential is set at −0.2 V vs SHE and the cathode at +0.3 V vs SHE. Calculate the theoretical maximum cell voltage and explain why the observed voltage is lower."
The theoretical open‑circuit voltage equals the difference between cathode and anode potentials: +0.3 V − (−0.2 V) = 0.5 V. In practice the measured voltage is lower because of activation overpotentials at both electrodes (kinetic barriers to electron transfer), ohmic losses across the electrolyte and membrane, and concentration overpotentials caused by substrate depletion or product accumulation at the biofilm surface. These losses typically reduce the operating voltage to 0.2–0.35 V under load.
Exam‑day strategy
When a numerical MFC problem appears, first write the Nernst equation for each half‑reaction, then subtract anode from cathode to obtain the reversible cell voltage. List the three major overpotential categories (activation, ohmic, concentration) and assign typical magnitude ranges (50–200 mV each) to show quantitative reasoning even if exact values are not given.

Bacteriophage Therapy and Phage‑Derived Biocontrol

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that infect and lyse specific bacterial hosts, offering a targeted alternative to broad‑spectrum antibiotics. Therapeutic phages are strictly lytic, lack lysogeny genes, and are selected for broad host‑range within the target species (e.g., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli). Phage cocktails combine 3–10 distinct phages to minimise resistance emergence; each phage uses a different receptor (lipopolysaccharide, outer‑membrane protein, flagellum) so that simultaneous mutation at multiple loci is statistically unlikely. Clinical compassionate‑use cases have reported pathogen clearance in chronic wound infections, cystic fibrosis lung infections, and prosthetic joint infections when phages are administered topically, via aerosol, or intravenously at doses of 10⁸–10¹⁰ plaque‑forming units per millilitre.

In agriculture, phage biocontrol products target phytopathogens such as Erwinia amylovora (fire blight), Xanthomonas campestris (bacterial spot), and Ralstonia solanacearum (bacterial wilt). Formulations are applied as foliar sprays or soil drenches at 10⁷–10⁹ PFU mL⁻¹, often combined with adjuvants that protect phage particles from UV inactivation. Field trials have shown disease incidence reductions of 60–90 % versus untreated controls, with viable phages persisting on leaf surfaces for 48–72 hours. In several jurisdictions, phage preparations are regulated as biological pesticides, requiring environmental fate data, non‑target toxicity testing, and batch‑to‑batch consistency of titre and host range.

Engineering approaches enhance phage efficacy: genome editing (CRISPR‑Cas) can delete anti‑CRISPR genes, insert depolymerase enzymes that degrade capsular polysaccharides, or swap tail‑fiber genes to broaden host range. Synthetic phage‑like particles (phagemids) deliver programmable CRISPR payloads that selectively kill antibiotic‑resistant subpopulations while sparing commensals. Safety assessment focuses on the absence of toxin genes, lysogeny modules, and transduction capability; whole‑genome sequencing and comparative genomics against virulence factor databases are now standard dossier requirements.

CRISPR‑Cas Systems for Precision Strain Engineering

CRISPR‑Cas adaptive immune systems have been repurposed as versatile genome‑editing tools across industrial microorganisms. The Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 nuclease, guided by a single‑guide RNA (sgRNA), introduces a double‑strand break at a 20‑nt target adjacent to an NGG protospacer‑adjacent motif (PAM). In Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, homologous recombination with a linear donor (500–1000 bp homology arms) yields precise edits at efficiencies of 10⁻³–10⁻² per viable cell. For organisms with low recombination rates (e.g., Clostridium spp., Streptomyces spp.), counter‑selection markers (pyrF, upp) or CRISPR‑mediated plasmid curing are employed to enrich edited clones.

Multiplex editing is achieved by expressing multiple sgRNAs from a single array processed by endogenous RNase III or by using Csy4/Cas6 cleavage sites. This enables simultaneous pathway rewiring: for example, deleting ldhA, pta, and ackA in E. coli redirects flux from lactate and acetate toward succinate, increasing yields from ~0.3 to ~1.2 mol mol⁻¹ glucose. Base editors (cytosine or adenine deaminase fused to nCas9) introduce single‑nucleotide changes without double‑strand breaks, useful for fine‑tuning promoter strength or eliminating premature stop codons. CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) uses a catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) to block transcription, providing reversible gene knockdown for dynamic pathway control.

Delivery vectors range from temperature‑sensitive replicons (pSC101ts) for plasmid‑based Cas9/sgRNA expression to integrative cassettes at neutral chromosomal loci (e.g., attB sites). In large‑scale fermentation, plasmid stability is a concern; therefore, genome‑integrated Cas9 under an inducible promoter (e.g., arabinose‑PBAD) combined with transient sgRNA expression from a curing plasmid minimises metabolic burden. Off‑target mutation rates can be <1 per genome when high‑fidelity Cas9 variants (eSpCas9, HiFi Cas9) are used. Regulatory dossiers for genetically modified production strains increasingly require evidence of marker‑free edits, absence of antibiotic‑resistance cassettes, and stability over ≥50 generations in production media.

  • Confusing the anode potential in MFCs with the cathode potential; the anode is negative relative to the cathode.
  • Assuming all PHA‑producing strains accumulate polymer at the same carbon‑to‑nitrogen ratio; optimal C/N varies by species and substrate.
  • Treating phage cocktails as a single entity; each phage must be characterised for host range, stability, and absence of lysogeny genes.
  • Believing CRISPR‑Cas9 edits are always scar‑free; residual plasmid backbone or selection markers can remain unless explicitly cured.
  • Overlooking the need for NADPH balancing in PHA pathways; NADPH shortage is a primary cause of low polymer content.
  • Neglecting UV protection in field phage applications; unprotected phages lose >90 % infectivity within 2 hours of sunlight exposure.
  • Assuming higher MFC power density always improves wastewater treatment; excessive current can inhibit anode biofilm activity and reduce COD removal.
