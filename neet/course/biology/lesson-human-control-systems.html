Organization of the Nervous System

1.1 Central Nervous System

The central nervous system consists of the brain and the spinal cord, which together serve as the primary processing centers for all sensory information and the origin of most motor commands. The brain is encased within the cranial cavity and protected by three meningeal layers — the dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater — while the spinal cord runs through the vertebral canal and enjoys the same meningeal coverings. Cerebrospinal fluid, produced by the choroid plexuses in the ventricles, cushions the delicate neural tissue, provides a stable chemical environment, and removes metabolic waste. The brain itself is subdivided into the cerebrum, cerebellum, diencephalon, brainstem, and limbic structures, each contributing distinct functions such as higher cognition, coordination of movement, autonomic regulation, and emotional processing.

1.2 Peripheral Nervous System

The peripheral nervous system comprises all neural elements that lie outside the central nervous system, including the twelve pairs of cranial nerves and the thirty‑one pairs of spinal nerves. These nerves are organized into a somatic division that conveys voluntary motor commands to skeletal muscle and carries sensory information from the skin, joints, and muscles, and an autonomic division that regulates involuntary activities of cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, and glands. Sensory neurons enter the spinal cord through dorsal roots, while motor neurons exit via ventral roots; the two roots unite to form mixed spinal nerves that distribute both afferent and efferent fibers to the body periphery.

1.3 Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system is further divided into sympathetic and parasympathetic branches that generally exert opposing influences on target organs. Sympathetic preganglionic fibers originate in the thoracolumbar spinal cord (T1–L2) and synapse in paravertebral or prevertebral ganglia, releasing acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors; postganglionic fibers then release norepinephrine onto adrenergic receptors to prepare the body for “fight‑or‑flight” responses such as increased heart rate, bronchodilation, and mobilization of glucose. Parasympathetic preganglionic fibers arise from cranial nuclei (III, VII, IX, X) and sacral spinal segments (S2–S4), synapse in terminal ganglia near or within the effector organ, and release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors to promote “rest‑and‑digest” activities like decreased heart rate, enhanced gastrointestinal motility, and pupil constriction.

Neural Signaling and Synaptic Transmission

2.1 Resting Membrane Potential and Action Potential

At rest, the neuronal membrane maintains a potential of approximately –70 mV, generated by the unequal distribution of ions across the lipid bilayer and the selective permeability of the membrane to potassium ions. The Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase pump actively transports three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions into the cell per ATP hydrolyzed, establishing steep concentration gradients. When a depolarizing stimulus opens voltage‑gated Na⁺ channels, sodium rushes inward, driving the membrane potential toward +30 mV; this rapid rise constitutes the rising phase of the action potential. Subsequent inactivation of Na⁺ channels and opening of voltage‑gated K⁺ channels allows potassium efflux, repolarizing the membrane and often producing a brief hyperpolarization before the resting potential is restored by the pump.

2.2 Propagation of Action Potential

In myelinated axons, the action potential jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next in a process called saltatory conduction, which dramatically increases conduction velocity while conserving metabolic energy. The myelin sheath, formed by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system, acts as an electrical insulator that prevents ion flow across the membrane except at the nodes, where voltage‑gated channels are densely clustered. In unmyelinated fibers, the action potential spreads continuously along the membrane, resulting in slower conduction speeds that are proportional to the square root of the axon diameter.

2.3 Synaptic Transmission

When an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, depolarization opens voltage‑gated Ca²⁺ channels, allowing calcium influx that triggers vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane and release of neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, which may be ionotropic ligand‑gated channels producing fast excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials, or metabotropic G‑protein‑coupled receptors that initiate slower, modulatory signaling cascades. Termination of the signal occurs through reuptake transporters, enzymatic degradation (for example, acetylcholinesterase hydrolyzes acetylcholine), or diffusion away from the cleft, ensuring temporal precision of neural communication.

Example Question
A neuron has a resting membrane potential of –70 mV. During an action potential, the membrane potential rises to +30 mV and then falls to –80 mV before returning to rest. Explain the ionic basis for each phase of this voltage change, naming the specific ion channels and transporters involved.
The initial depolarization to +30 mV is caused by the rapid opening of voltage‑gated Na⁺ channels, allowing Na⁺ influx down its electrochemical gradient. The subsequent repolarization and overshoot to –80 mV result from the inactivation of Na⁺ channels together with the opening of voltage‑gated K⁺ channels, which permits K⁺ efflux. The brief hyperpolarization below the resting level occurs because K⁺ channels remain open slightly longer than needed, driving the membrane potential closer to the K⁺ equilibrium potential. Finally, the Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase pump restores the original ion distribution by actively extruding three Na⁺ and importing two K⁺ per ATP, re‑establishing the –70 mV resting potential.
Exam Insight
When a question asks for the sequence of ionic events during an action potential, always mention the Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase in the recovery phase; examiners frequently award marks for recognizing that the pump restores the resting gradients rather than the channels alone.

Endocrine System and Hormonal Regulation

3.1 Major Endocrine Glands

The endocrine system consists of ductless glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream. The hypothalamus, located at the base of the diencephalon, produces releasing and inhibiting hormones that regulate the anterior pituitary via the hypophyseal portal system and also synthesizes oxytocin and vasopressin for posterior pituitary release. The anterior pituitary secretes tropic hormones such as ACTH, TSH, LH, FSH, GH, and prolactin, which in turn stimulate peripheral glands. The thyroid gland releases thyroxine (T₄) and triiodothyronine (T₃) that set basal metabolic rate, while the parathyroid glands secrete parathyroid hormone to control calcium homeostasis. The adrenal cortex produces glucocorticoids (cortisol), mineralocorticoids (aldosterone), and androgens; the adrenal medulla releases catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine) as part of the sympathetic response. The pancreas contains islets of Langerhans that secrete insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose, and the gonads (testes and ovaries) produce sex steroids and gametogenic hormones.

3.2 Hormone Classification and Mechanism

Hormones are broadly classified as steroid hormones, peptide hormones, and amine hormones based on chemical structure, which dictates their route of transport, receptor location, and mechanism of action. Steroid hormones, derived from cholesterol, are lipophilic and diffuse through the plasma membrane to bind intracellular nuclear receptors; the hormone‑receptor complex then acts as a transcription factor, altering gene expression over hours to days. Peptide hormones, composed of amino acid chains, are hydrophilic and cannot cross the membrane; they bind to cell‑surface receptors, typically G‑protein‑coupled receptors or receptor tyrosine kinases, triggering second‑messenger cascades such as cAMP, IP₃/DAG, or Ca²⁺ that produce rapid cellular responses. Amine hormones (e.g., epinephrine, thyroid hormones) share features of both classes; catecholamines act via membrane receptors, whereas thyroid hormones behave like steroids by entering cells and binding nuclear receptors.

3.3 Feedback Loops

Endocrine regulation relies heavily on negative feedback loops to maintain homeostasis. For instance, rising cortisol levels inhibit CRH release from the hypothalamus and ACTH release from the anterior pituitary, thereby reducing further cortisol synthesis. Positive feedback loops are rarer but crucial in specific physiological events; the LH surge that triggers ovulation is driven by estrogen‑mediated positive feedback on the anterior pituitary, and oxytocin release during parturition amplifies uterine contractions in a self‑reinforcing cycle until delivery occurs. Disruption of these feedback mechanisms underlies many endocrine disorders such as Cushing syndrome, Graves disease, and diabetes mellitus.

Integration of Neural and Endocrine Control

4.1 Neuroendocrine Interface

The hypothalamus serves as the principal neuroendocrine integration center, converting neural signals into hormonal output. Neurosecretory neurons in the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei synthesize oxytocin and vasopressin, which travel down axons to the posterior pituitary for release into the systemic circulation. In the anterior pituitary, hypothalamic releasing hormones such as TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH, and somatostatin are secreted into the hypophyseal portal vessels, reaching the adenohypophysis within seconds to modulate tropic hormone secretion. This arrangement allows the central nervous system to exert rapid, precise control over endocrine axes in response to sensory input, stress, and circadian cues.

4.2 Stress Response

Acute stress activates the hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal (HPA) axis: CRH from the hypothalamus stimulates ACTH release from the anterior pituitary, which in turn prompts the adrenal cortex to secrete cortisol. Cortisol mobilizes energy substrates, suppresses non‑essential immune functions, and enhances cardiovascular responsiveness. Simultaneously, the sympathetic‑adrenal‑medullary (SAM) axis triggers the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine, producing immediate “fight‑or‑flight” effects such as increased heart rate, bronchodilation, and glycogenolysis. Chronic activation of these pathways can lead to metabolic syndrome, immunosuppression, and mood disorders.

4.3 Circadian Rhythms

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus functions as the master circadian pacemaker, receiving direct photic input from intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells via the retinohypothalamic tract. The SCN synchronizes peripheral clocks in organs such as the liver, pancreas, and adipose tissue through neural projections and hormonal signals, notably melatonin secreted by the pineal gland during darkness. Melatonin feeds back on the SCN to reinforce the night‑time phase, while cortisol exhibits a robust diurnal peak in the early morning, preparing the organism for activity. Disruption of this timing system, as occurs in shift work or jet lag, impairs glucose tolerance, immune function, and cognitive performance.

Muscular System and Locomotion

5.1 Muscle Types

Vertebrates possess three distinct muscle types that differ in structure, control, and functional role. Skeletal muscle is attached to bone by tendons, exhibits striations due to highly ordered sarcomeres, and is under voluntary somatic control via motor neurons releasing acetylcholine at neuromuscular junctions. Cardiac muscle forms the heart wall, is also striated but consists of branched, mononucleated cells connected by intercalated discs that allow rapid electrical coupling; it contracts rhythmically under involuntary autonomic modulation. Smooth muscle lines the walls of hollow viscera such as the gastrointestinal tract, blood vessels, and urinary bladder; it lacks striations, contains a single central nucleus per cell, and contracts slowly and sustainably under autonomic and hormonal regulation.

5.2 Sliding Filament Mechanism

Muscle contraction in striated muscle follows the sliding filament model, wherein thick (myosin) and thin (actin) filaments slide past each other without changing length. An action potential traveling down a motor neuron triggers acetylcholine release, depolarizing the sarcolemma and propagating into the transverse tubule system. This depolarization opens voltage‑sensitive dihydropyridine receptors, which mechanically couple to ryanodine receptors on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, releasing Ca²⁺ into the cytosol. Calcium binds to troponin C, causing a conformational shift that moves tropomyosin away from myosin‑binding sites on actin. Myosin heads then attach, undergo a power stroke powered by ATP hydrolysis, detach, and re‑cock, repeating the cycle as long as Ca²⁺ and ATP are available. Relaxation occurs when Ca²⁺ is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by the SERCA pump, allowing tropomyosin to re‑cover the binding sites.

5.3 Modes of Locomotion

Locomotion strategies reflect the mechanical demands of the environment and the specialization of muscle fiber types. Terrestrial mammals rely on a mixture of slow‑twitch (type I) oxidative fibers for posture and endurance, and fast‑twitch (type IIa and IIx) glycolytic fibers for rapid, powerful movements such as sprinting or jumping; the lever system of long bones amplifies force at the expense of speed. Aquatic vertebrates like fish employ segmented myomeres arranged in a W‑shaped pattern that generates undulatory waves along the body, with a high proportion of oxidative fibers to sustain continuous swimming. Birds exhibit a unique adaptation: the pectoralis major and supracoracoideus muscles power the downstroke and upstroke of wings, respectively, and are composed predominantly of fast‑oxidative fibers that combine high power output with fatigue resistance, enabling sustained flight. In each case, the architecture of the musculoskeletal system, the fiber‑type composition, and the neural recruitment pattern are co‑adapted to optimize locomotor performance.
