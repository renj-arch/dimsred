Human Health & Microbes in Human Welfare

Updated 20 Nov 2026 · 22,993 words · 2 sections · illustrated
NEET Biology covering Human Health & Disease (immunity, infectious diseases, cancer, AIDS, drugs) and Microbes in Human Welfare (household, industrial, sewage, biogas, biocontrol, biofertilisers).

Human Health & Disease

Health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being (WHO definition), not merely the absence of disease. It can be influenced by genetic disorders, infections, lifestyle (diet, exercise, stress), and environmental factors.

Common Human Diseases

Bacterial diseases: Typhoid (Salmonella typhi — Widal test), Pneumonia (Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae — alveoli fill with fluid), Plague (Yersinia pestis), Diphtheria (Corynebacterium diphtheriae), Tetanus (Clostridium tetani), Cholera (Vibrio cholerae — rice-water stools), TB (Mycobacterium tuberculosis).

Viral diseases: Common cold (Rhinovirus), Influenza (Orthomyxovirus), AIDS (HIV), Dengue (Flavivirus — Aedes mosquito), Chikungunya (Togavirus — Aedes), COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), Hepatitis B (Hepadnavirus), Rabies (Rhabdovirus — infected animal bite), Polio (Enterovirus — now eradicated in most countries).

Protozoan diseases: Malaria (Plasmodium vivax/falciparum/malariae/ovale — transmitted by female Anopheles mosquito), Amoebic dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica), Kala-azar/Leishmaniasis (Leishmania donovani — sandfly), Sleeping sickness (Trypanosoma gambiense — tsetse fly).

Fungal diseases: Ringworm (Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton), Candidiasis (Candida albicans).

Helminthic diseases: Ascariasis (Ascaris lumbricoides — roundworm), Filariasis/Elephantiasis (Wuchereria bancrofti — mosquito), Tapeworm infection (Taenia solium — pork).

Which mosquito transmits malaria?

a) Aedes   b) Culex   c) Female Anopheles   d) Male Anopheles
Solution: Option (c). Female Anopheles mosquito transmits the malarial parasite Plasmodium. Only female mosquitoes bite (males feed on nectar).

Immunity

The body's ability to recognise and defend against pathogens. Two types: Innate (non-specific) — present from birth, not pathogen-specific. Components: Physical barriers (skin, mucous membranes), Physiological barriers (stomach acid HCl, lysozyme in tears, saliva), Cellular barriers (phagocytes — neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells), Cytokine barriers (interferons — protect against viral infections). Acquired (specific) — develops after exposure to a pathogen. Attributes: Specificity, Diversity, Memory (faster and stronger response on second exposure — immunological memory).

Types of Acquired Immunity

Humoral immunity: B-lymphocytes produce antibodies (immunoglobulins) that circulate in blood and lymph. Antibody structure: 4 polypeptide chains (2 heavy + 2 light) forming a Y-shape. There are 5 classes: IgG (most abundant, crosses placenta — provides passive immunity to foetus), IgA (present in secretions — colostrum, saliva, tears), IgM (first antibody produced in primary response), IgE (involved in allergic reactions), IgD (B-cell receptor). Cell-mediated immunity: T-lymphocytes directly attack infected cells. Types: Helper T cells (TH) (activate B cells and other T cells), Cytotoxic T cells (TC) (kill infected cells), Suppressor T cells (Ts) (regulate immune response).

Vaccination & Immunisation

A vaccine contains weakened/dead pathogens or antigens that stimulate the immune system to produce memory cells. On subsequent exposure, the response is rapid and strong. Edward Jenner (1796) developed the first vaccine for smallpox using cowpox virus. Smallpox has been eradicated globally (WHO, 1980). The Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI) includes vaccines for TB (BCG), Polio (OPV/IPV), Diphtheria-Pertussis-Tetanus (DPT), Measles, MMR, Hepatitis B, and more.

Example 1B — Immunity Types
Which type of immunity is provided by colostrum to a newborn?

a) Active natural   b) Passive natural   c) Active artificial   d) Passive artificial
Solution: Option (b). Colostrum (the first milk) contains IgA antibodies that provide passive natural immunity to the newborn. The baby receives ready-made antibodies from the mother. Vaccination is active artificial immunity; anti-venom is passive artificial immunity.

Lymphatic System & Immune Organs

The lymphatic system plays a crucial role in immunity. Lymph is the fluid that leaks from blood capillaries into intercellular spaces. It contains WBCs (especially lymphocytes) and is circulated through lymphatic vessels. Primary lymphoid organs: Bone marrow (B-lymphocytes mature here) and Thymus gland (T-lymphocytes mature here — located behind the sternum, largest in infants, atrophies with age). Secondary lymphoid organs: Lymph nodes (filter lymph, contain B and T cells), Spleen (filters blood, contains lymphocytes and macrophages — the graveyard of RBCs), Tonsils (protect respiratory tract), Peyer's patches (in the small intestine — protect gut), Appendix (vestigial but contains lymphoid tissue). Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (MALT) — located in respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts forming the first line of defence at mucosal surfaces.

NEET Key — Lymphoid Organs
Bone marrow and thymus are PRIMARY lymphoid organs — lymphocytes are produced and mature here. All others (lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, Peyer's patches, MALT) are SECONDARY lymphoid organs — lymphocytes interact with antigens here. Memory: B for Bone marrow, T for Thymus.
Example 1C — Immune Organs
T-lymphocytes mature in which organ?

a) Bone marrow   b) Thymus   c) Spleen   d) Lymph node
Solution: Option (b). T-lymphocytes (T cells) mature in the thymus gland. B-lymphocytes (B cells) mature in the bone marrow. Both are produced in the bone marrow but differentiate in different primary lymphoid organs.

Allergies & Autoimmune Diseases

Allergy: Exaggerated (hypersensitive) immune response to harmless environmental substances called allergens (pollen, dust mites, pet dander, certain foods, drugs). Mechanism: First exposure → allergens sensitise mast cells (IgE binds to FcεRI receptors). Subsequent exposure → allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells → mast cells degranulate → release histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins → vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, increased mucus, itching. Symptoms: sneezing, runny nose, watery eyes, rashes (urticaria), asthma, anaphylaxis (severe, life-threatening). Diagnosis: Skin prick test, serum IgE levels. Treatment: Antihistamines (block histamine H1 receptors — cetirizine, loratadine), corticosteroids, epinephrine (adrenaline — for anaphylaxis), immunotherapy (allergy shots — desensitisation).

Autoimmune diseases: The immune system mistakenly attacks self-antigens (failure of self-tolerance). Causes include genetic predisposition (HLA associations) and environmental triggers (infection, stress). Examples: Rheumatoid arthritis (autoantibodies against IgG (RF factor) and anti-CCP antibodies), Type 1 diabetes (T cells destroy pancreatic β-cells), Multiple sclerosis (T cells attack myelin sheath of neurons), Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE — autoantibodies against DNA, nuclear antigens — butterfly rash on face), Graves' disease (autoantibodies stimulate TSH receptor on thyroid → hyperthyroidism), Hashimoto's thyroiditis (autoantibodies against thyroid → hypothyroidism), Myasthenia gravis (autoantibodies block acetylcholine receptors at neuromuscular junction → muscle weakness).

Immune System Disorders — Primary Immunodeficiencies

Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID): A group of rare genetic disorders with absence of both B and T cell function (combined humoral and cell-mediated deficiency). Most severe form is X-linked SCID (mutation in IL2RG gene encoding the common gamma chain of cytokine receptors). "Bubble boy" disease — patients must live in sterile environments. Treatment: bone marrow transplantation, gene therapy (first successful human gene therapy was for ADA-SCID in 1990). Without treatment, death occurs within the first year from severe infections.

DiGeorge Syndrome (22q11.2 deletion syndrome): Microdeletion on chromosome 22. Results in thymic aplasia/hypoplasia (absent/underdeveloped thymus) → T-cell deficiency. Associated with cardiac abnormalities, characteristic facial features, cleft palate. Treatment: thymus transplantation, supportive care.

Chediak-Higashi Syndrome: Autosomal recessive disorder (LYST gene mutation). Defective phagocytosis due to abnormal lysosomal trafficking — neutrophils have giant granules that cannot fuse with phagosomes. Recurrent pyogenic infections, albinism (partial), peripheral neuropathy. Treatment: bone marrow transplant.

Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD): X-linked (most common) or autosomal recessive. Defect in NADPH oxidase enzyme complex in phagocytes → inability to produce ROS to kill engulfed bacteria. Recurrent infections with catalase-positive organisms (Staphylococcus aureus, Serratia, Aspergillus). Granuloma formation in various organs. Diagnosis: Nitroblue Tetrazolium (NBT) test — fails to turn blue (negative). Treatment: IFN-γ therapy, prophylactic antibiotics, BMT, gene therapy.

Hyper-IgE Syndrome (Job Syndrome): Autosomal dominant (STAT3 mutation). Extremely high serum IgE (>2000 IU/mL), recurrent staphylococcal skin abscesses ("cold abscesses" — lack classical inflammation), pneumonia with pneumatocele formation, eczema, retained primary teeth, skeletal abnormalities. Treatment: prophylactic antibiotics, antifungals.

Example 2G — Immunodeficiency
The NBT test is used to diagnose which immunodeficiency disorder?

a) SCID   b) DiGeorge syndrome   c) Chronic Granulomatous Disease   d) Hyper-IgE syndrome
Solution: Option (c). The Nitroblue Tetrazolium (NBT) test diagnoses Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD). In CGD, phagocytes cannot produce ROS due to NADPH oxidase defect. NBT dye remains yellow (normal cells reduce it to blue/purple formazan).

AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome)

Caused by HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) — a retrovirus (RNA virus with reverse transcriptase). HIV infects and destroys Helper T cells (CD4+ T cells), weakening the immune system. Transmission: Sexual contact (most common), Blood transfusion, Sharing needles, Mother-to-child (vertical transmission). Not transmitted: Handshake, sharing utensils, mosquito bites, hugging. Symptoms: Flu-like initial stage (2-6 weeks), asymptomatic latency (8-10 years), then AIDS (opportunistic infections — TB, Pneumocystis pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma, dementia). Diagnosis: ELISA (screening), Western blot (confirmatory). Treatment: HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy) — combination of drugs including Reverse transcriptase inhibitors (AZT, nevirapine), Protease inhibitors, Integrase inhibitors. No cure yet, but can be managed. Prevention: Safe sex (condoms), sterile needles, blood screening, awareness through NACO and WHO programmes.

NEET Key — HIV Timeline
HIV was first reported in 1981 in the USA. In India, the first case was reported in 1986. The virus was isolated by Luc Montagnier (France) and Robert Gallo (USA). World AIDS Day: December 1st. HIV is a retrovirus — it uses reverse transcriptase to convert its RNA into DNA, which integrates into the host genome.
Example 2B — AIDS
The enzyme used by HIV to convert its RNA into DNA is:

Solution: Option (c). HIV is a retrovirus — it carries reverse transcriptase that converts viral RNA into DNA, which then integrates into the host genome. Integrase helps in integration but reverse transcriptase performs the RNA→DNA conversion.

NEET Key — Primary vs Secondary Response
Primary response (first exposure): lag phase of 5-7 days, IgM appears first, followed by IgG, antibody titre is low. Secondary response (second exposure): lag phase of 1-3 days, rapid IgG production, antibody titre is much higher and lasts longer. This is the basis of vaccination — it primes the immune system for a strong secondary response.

Complement System & Interferons

The complement system is a group of ~30 serum proteins that enhance (complement) the ability of antibodies and phagocytes to clear pathogens. It is activated by three pathways: Classical (antibody-dependent), Alternative (spontaneous activation on pathogen surfaces), and Lectin pathway (mannose-binding lectin binds to bacterial carbohydrates). Functions include: opsonisation (coating pathogens for phagocytosis), chemotaxis (attracting immune cells), lysis (forming membrane attack complex that punches holes in bacterial cell walls), and inflammation (triggering mast cell degranulation). Interferons are cytokines (signalling proteins) released by virus-infected cells. They are part of the innate immune system (cytokine barrier). Interferons signal neighbouring uninfected cells to produce antiviral proteins that inhibit viral replication. They also activate NK cells and macrophages. Types: IFN-α (leukocytes), IFN-β (fibroblasts), IFN-γ (T cells and NK cells). Interferon therapy is used in the treatment of hepatitis B, hepatitis C, multiple sclerosis, and certain cancers. Interferons were discovered by Isaacs and Lindenmann (1957).

Example 1D — Interferons
Interferons are produced in response to which type of infection?

a) Bacterial   b) Fungal   c) Viral   d) Protozoan
Solution: Option (c). Interferons are produced by virus-infected cells as part of the innate immune response. They signal nearby cells to produce antiviral proteins, creating an antiviral state. They are classified as cytokine barriers in the innate immune system.

Monoclonal Antibodies (mAbs)

Monoclonal antibodies are identical antibodies produced by a single clone of B-lymphocytes that recognise the same specific epitope on an antigen. They are produced using hybridoma technology, developed by K—hler and Milstein (1975, Nobel Prize 1984).

Production (Hybridoma Technology): (1) A mouse is immunised with a specific antigen. (2) B-lymphocytes producing antibodies against the antigen are isolated from the mouse's spleen. (3) These B cells are fused with myeloma cells (immortalised plasma cell tumour cells) using polyethylene glycol (PEG) or electrical fusion. (4) The fused cells (hybridomas) are cultured in HAT medium (Hypoxanthine-Aminopterin-Thymidine) — unfused B cells die (short-lived), unfused myeloma cells die (HGPRT-deficient, killed by aminopterin). Only hybridomas survive. (5) Hybridomas are screened for desired antibody production and cloned. (6) Selected clones produce large quantities of monoclonal antibodies. mAbs can be chimeric (mouse variable + human constant) or humanised (only CDRs from mouse) to reduce immunogenicity.

Applications: (1) Diagnosis — ELISA tests, pregnancy tests (detect hCG), blood typing, detection of hormones and pathogens. (2) Therapy — Cancer (rituximab for B-cell lymphomas, trastuzumab/Herceptin for HER2+ breast cancer), autoimmune diseases (infliximab for RA), transplant rejection (muromonab-CD3, basiliximab), infectious diseases (palivizumab for RSV), cardiovascular (abciximab). (3) Targeted drug delivery — antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) like trastuzumab emtansine. (4) Immunoassay — pregnancy test uses mAbs against hCG. When hCG binds gold-labelled anti-hCG antibodies, immobilised anti-hCG antibodies capture the complex, producing a visible line.

Examples of therapeutic mAbs: Rituximab (anti-CD20 — B-cell lymphoma, RA), Trastuzumab (anti-HER2 — breast cancer), Infliximab (anti-TNF-α — RA, Crohn's), Adalimumab (anti-TNF-α — RA, psoriasis), Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF — colorectal cancer, inhibits angiogenesis), Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1 — checkpoint inhibitor). mAb naming suffixes: -omab = mouse, -ximab = chimeric, -zumab = humanised, -umab = fully human.

ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay)

ELISA is a sensitive immunological test to detect and quantify antigens or antibodies using enzyme-labelled reagents. The enzyme converts a colourless substrate to a coloured product, measured spectrophotometrically. Developed by Engvall and Perlmann (1971).

Principle: An antigen-antibody reaction is detected by an enzyme conjugated to one reactant. The enzyme catalyses a substrate reaction producing a measurable colour change — intensity proportional to the amount of antigen/antibody.

Types of ELISA:

Direct ELISA: Antigen is immobilised on a microtitre plate. Enzyme-labelled primary antibody binds directly to the antigen. Colour proportional to antigen. Used to detect antigen in sample.

Indirect ELISA: Antigen is immobilised. Unlabelled primary antibody (patient serum) binds. Enzyme-labelled secondary antibody (anti-human IgG) binds the primary. Colour proportional to primary antibody present. Used for HIV screening — detects anti-HIV antibodies in patient serum.

Sandwich ELISA: Capture antibody is immobilised. Sample antigen binds to capture antibody. Enzyme-labelled detection antibody (against a different epitope) binds the captured antigen. Highly specific (two antibodies, two epitopes). Used for pregnancy test (hCG), cytokines.

Competitive ELISA: Antigen in sample competes with labelled antigen for limited antibody. More antigen → less labelled antigen binds → weaker colour. Inverse relationship. Used for small molecules (haptens, drugs, hormones).

Steps in Indirect ELISA (HIV diagnosis): (1) Coating: HIV antigens adsorbed onto microtitre wells. (2) Blocking: Unbound sites blocked with BSA to prevent non-specific binding. (3) Washing: Remove excess blocking agent. (4) Primary antibody: Patient serum added — anti-HIV antibodies bind immobilised antigens. (5) Washing: Unbound antibodies removed. (6) Secondary antibody: Enzyme-labelled anti-human IgG added — binds patient antibodies. (7) Washing: Unbound conjugate removed. (8) Substrate: Enzyme substrate (TMB for HRP) added — colour develops if positive (blue → yellow on stop). (9) Reading: Absorbance at 450 nm. ELISA is a screening test for HIV — positive results confirmed by Western blot.

Applications: HIV screening (indirect ELISA), Pregnancy test (sandwich ELISA for hCG), Food allergen detection, Drug monitoring, Hormone assays (thyroid, cortisol), Autoantibody detection (ANA in SLE), Plant virus detection, GMO detection, Cytokine measurement. ELISA is highly sensitive (picogram to nanogram quantities), quantitative, and automatable.

Vaccine Types — Detailed Mechanisms

Vaccines work by exposing the immune system to an antigen in a safe form, generating immunological memory. Different vaccine platforms use different strategies to present antigens to the immune system:

Live Attenuated Vaccines: Contain a weakened (attenuated) form of the live pathogen that cannot cause disease in immunocompetent individuals. Attenuation is achieved by passaging the pathogen through non-human cells or animals until it loses virulence genes. Mechanism: The vaccine strain replicates in the host (limited replication), stimulating both humoral and cell-mediated immunity without causing disease. Advantages: Strong, long-lasting immunity (often lifelong with 1-2 doses), activates both B and T cells. Disadvantages: Cannot be given to immunocompromised individuals, requires cold chain, risk of reversion to virulence (very rare). Examples: BCG (TB), OPV (polio), MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), Rotavirus, Yellow fever, Varicella (chickenpox).

Inactivated/Killed Vaccines: Contain whole pathogens killed by heat, chemicals (formaldehyde, β-propiolactone), or radiation. Mechanism: The inactivated pathogen cannot replicate but intact surface antigens are still recognised. Primarily elicits humoral immunity (antibody response). Advantages: Safe for immunocompromised individuals, cannot revert to virulence, more stable. Disadvantages: Weaker immune response — requires multiple doses (3-4) and boosters, mainly antibody-mediated. Examples: IPV (polio), Rabies, Hepatitis A, Influenza (injected), Pertussis (whole-cell).

Toxoid Vaccines: Contain inactivated toxins (toxoids) produced by bacteria. Treated with formaldehyde to destroy toxicity while retaining immunogenicity. Mechanism: Immune system produces antitoxin antibodies that neutralise the bacterial toxin. Advantages: Safe, stable, induces strong antibody response. Disadvantages: Requires boosters, weak cellular immunity. Examples: Diphtheria toxoid (DPT), Tetanus toxoid (TT).

Recombinant/Subunit Vaccines: Contain specific antigens produced by genetic engineering. Mechanism: Only the immunogenic component is presented — no infectious material. Advantages: Very safe, can be given to immunocompromised, precisely defined, fewer side effects. Disadvantages: Requires adjuvants, often needs multiple doses. Examples: Hepatitis B (HBsAg in yeast), HPV (virus-like particles — Gardasil, Cervarix), Acellular pertussis.

Conjugate Vaccines: Polysaccharide antigens linked to a carrier protein to make them immunogenic in infants. Mechanism: Pure polysaccharides are T-independent antigens — weak response without memory in children under 2. Conjugation to a carrier protein (tetanus toxoid, CRM197) makes them T-dependent, generating strong antibody responses and memory. Advantages: Protective in infants, induces memory, herd immunity. Examples: Hib, PCV13 (pneumococcal), MenACWY (meningococcal), Typhoid conjugate vaccine.

mRNA Vaccines: Contain synthetic mRNA encoding the target antigen, encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles. Mechanism: mRNA is taken up by host cells and translated into antigen protein by host ribosomes. The antigen is presented on MHC molecules, stimulating both humoral and cell-mediated immunity. mRNA does not enter the nucleus or integrate into host genome. Advantages: Rapid development and manufacturing, safe, induces both B and T cell responses. Disadvantages: Requires ultra-cold storage (-20°C to -80°C), reactogenicity. Examples: Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) and Moderna (mRNA-1273) COVID-19 vaccines.

Viral Vector Vaccines: Use a harmless virus (adenovirus, modified vaccinia Ankara) as a vector to deliver genetic material encoding the target antigen. Mechanism: The viral vector enters host cells and expresses the target antigen. Antigen is presented on MHC molecules, stimulating strong T cell and antibody responses. The vector is often replication-defective. Advantages: Strong cellular and humoral immunity, single dose may suffice, stable at fridge temperatures (some). Disadvantages: Pre-existing immunity to vector can reduce efficacy. Examples: Oxford-AstraZeneca (ChAdOx1-S), Johnson & Johnson (Ad26.COV2.S) for COVID-19; Ervebo (Ebola — VSV vector).

Cancer

Uncontrolled cell division caused by genetic mutations in oncogenes (promote cell division — when mutated, become hyperactive) and tumour suppressor genes (e.g., p53 — normally prevent cancer; when mutated, lose function). The cellular origin of oncogenes was discovered by Bishop and Varmus (Nobel Prize). Cancer results from accumulation of multiple mutations over time, not a single change.

Cancer Biology — Oncogenes vs Tumour Suppressor Genes

Cancer arises from the accumulation of mutations in two broad classes of genes: proto-oncogenes (which promote cell growth) and tumour suppressor genes (which inhibit cell growth). A single mutation is rarely sufficient — multiple "hits" are required (Knudson's two-hit hypothesis for retinoblastoma).

Cancer Staging and Grading

Cancer staging describes the extent of cancer spread. The most commonly used system is the TNM system (Tumour, Node, Metastasis): T describes the size of the primary tumour (T0-T4), N describes lymph node involvement (N0-N3), M describes distant metastasis (M0 or M1). Stage I: localised cancer. Stage II-III: locally advanced with lymph node involvement. Stage IV: metastatic cancer (spread to distant organs). Staging guides treatment decisions and predicts prognosis.

Cancer grading describes how much tumour cells resemble normal cells (differentiation). Grade 1 (well-differentiated) — cells look relatively normal, grow slowly, better prognosis. Grade 3 (poorly differentiated/anaplastic) — cells look abnormal, grow aggressively. Grade 4 (undifferentiated) — cells bear no resemblance to normal tissue.

Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy in Cancer

Traditional chemotherapy targets all rapidly dividing cells, causing significant side effects. Targeted therapy uses drugs that specifically target molecular abnormalities in cancer cells:

Imatinib (Gleevec): The first successful targeted cancer drug. Targets the BCR-ABL fusion protein — a constitutively active tyrosine kinase produced by the Philadelphia chromosome (t(9;22) translocation) in Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia (CML). Imatinib binds to the ATP-binding pocket of BCR-ABL, inhibiting its activity. Treated CML patients have near-normal life expectancy. Also targets c-KIT (for GIST).

Gefitinib (Iressa) and Erlotinib (Tarceva): Target EGFR tyrosine kinase — mutated and overactive in some non-small cell lung cancers (EGFR-mutated NSCLC). These inhibitors block signalling pathways driving cancer cell proliferation.

Trastuzumab (Herceptin): A monoclonal antibody targeting HER2/neu receptor — overexpressed in ~20% of breast cancers (HER2-positive). Blocks receptor signalling and triggers antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC).

Vemurafenib: Targets the BRAF V600E mutation — found in ~50% of melanomas. BRAF is a kinase in the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway. The V600E mutation makes BRAF constitutively active.

Immunotherapy (Checkpoint Inhibitors): Cancer cells evade the immune system by expressing inhibitory molecules that turn off T cells. PD-1/PD-L1 pathway: Cancer cells express PD-L1 which binds to PD-1 on T cells, suppressing T cell activity. Anti-PD-1 antibodies (pembrolizumab/Keytruda, nivolumab/Opdivo) block this interaction, restoring T cell killing. Approved for melanoma, NSCLC, kidney cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma. CTLA-4 pathway: Ipilimumab (Yervoy) blocks CTLA-4 on T cells, enhancing T cell activation. Used for melanoma. Checkpoint inhibitors can cause immune-related adverse events (irAEs) — colitis, pneumonitis, dermatitis, endocrinopathies. CAR-T cell therapy: Patient's own T cells are genetically engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) targeting cancer cells. Approved for B-cell ALL (tisagenlecleucel/Kymriah — anti-CD19) and DLBCL (axicabtagene ciloleucel/Yescarta).

Transplantation Immunology

Transplantation is the transfer of cells, tissues, or organs from one site to another. The immune system recognises transplanted tissues as foreign (unless perfectly matched), leading to graft rejection. The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) — called HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen) in humans — is the primary determinant of transplant compatibility.

Types of grafts:

Autograft: Transplant from one site to another in the same individual (e.g., skin grafts in burn patients, bone grafts, saphenous vein grafts in coronary bypass). No immune rejection — recognised as "self".

Isograft (Syngeneic graft): Transplant between genetically identical individuals (identical twins). No immune rejection — genetically identical.

Allograft (Homograft): Transplant between genetically different individuals of the same species (e.g., kidney, heart, liver transplant). Most common type. Recipient's immune system recognises donor HLA molecules as foreign → graft rejection. Requires HLA matching and immunosuppression.

Xenograft (Heterograft): Transplant between different species (e.g., pig heart valves to humans). Faces hyperacute rejection due to pre-existing antibodies against galactose-α-1,3-galactose epitopes on pig tissues. Genetically modified pigs are being developed to overcome this.

Graft rejection mechanisms: Hyperacute rejection: Occurs within minutes to hours — pre-existing antibodies bind to donor endothelial antigens → complement activation → thrombosis. Prevented by ABO matching and cross-matching. Acute rejection (HVGR — Host vs Graft): Occurs within days to weeks — T cell-mediated (CD8+ CTLs) and antibody-mediated attack on the graft. Managed by immunosuppressive drugs. Chronic rejection: Occurs over months to years — fibrosis, vessel occlusion (transplant vasculopathy), gradual graft loss. Difficult to treat.

Graft vs Host Disease (GVHD): Occurs when donor immune cells (in bone marrow or stem cell transplants) recognise recipient tissues as foreign and attack them. Recipient is immunocompromised and cannot reject donor cells. Acute GVHD (within 100 days) affects skin (rash), liver (jaundice), and gut (diarrhoea). Chronic GVHD (after 100 days) resembles autoimmune disease. Prevention: T-cell depletion, immunosuppression (methotrexate, cyclosporine), HLA matching.

Immunosuppression in transplantation: Calcineurin inhibitors: Cyclosporine (from Trichoderma polysporum), Tacrolimus — inhibit T-cell activation by blocking calcineurin → prevent IL-2 production. Antiproliferatives: Azathioprine, Mycophenolate mofetil — block lymphocyte proliferation. Corticosteroids: Prednisolone — broad anti-inflammatory. Antibody-based: Muromonab-CD3 (OKT3 — anti-CD3 mAb), Basiliximab (anti-IL-2 receptor mAb), Anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG). HLA matching focuses on HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-DR loci. A "full match" (6/6) between siblings gives best outcomes.

HLA System: MHC in humans is located on chromosome 6. Class I HLA (A, B, C) — on all nucleated cells, recognised by CD8+ T cells. Class II HLA (DP, DQ, DR) — on antigen-presenting cells, recognised by CD4+ T cells. High polymorphism of HLA genes makes finding matched unrelated donors challenging. Chance of full siblings sharing identical HLA haplotypes is 25%.

Drugs & Alcohol Abuse

Drug abuse is the harmful use of psychoactive substances. The most commonly abused substances in India: (1) Opioids (heroin, morphine, codeine) — derived from opium poppy (Papaver somniferum). Heroin (brown sugar, smack) is diacetylmorphine — highly addictive, snorted or injected. (2) Cannabinoids — from Cannabis sativa (marijuana, hashish, ganja, charas). THC (tetrahydrocannabinol) is the active compound — affects mood, perception, appetite. (3) Cocaine — from coca plant (Erythroxylum coca) — central nervous system stimulant, highly addictive ("crack" is the freebase form). (4) Hallucinogens — LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), mescaline (peyote cactus), psilocybin (magic mushrooms) — alter perception and mood. (5) Amphetamines — synthetic stimulants (speed, ecstasy/MDMA). (6) Barbiturates, benzodiazepines — sedatives (tranquillisers). (7) Tobacco — nicotine, tar, carbon monoxide — associated with lung cancer, COPD, cardiovascular disease. (8) Alcohol — legal, but excessive use causes cirrhosis, pancreatitis, dependence.

Dependence: Psychological (craving) and physical (withdrawal symptoms — nausea, tremors, seizures) occur when the substance is stopped. Tolerance: Need for increasing doses to achieve the same effect. Treatment: De-addiction centres (counselling, CBT), pharmacotherapy (methadone for heroin, bupropion for tobacco), 12-step programmes (Narcotics Anonymous), family and community support. Microbes in Human Welfare

Microbes (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, viruses) are not always harmful — they play crucial beneficial roles in human welfare, industry, and the environment.

Microbes in Household Products

Microbes have been used for thousands of years in food preparation, long before the discovery of their existence. Common household products made using microbes:

Curd/Yoghurt: Lactobacillus (bacteria) and Lactobacillus bulgaricus + Streptococcus thermophilus ferment lactose to lactic acid, coagulating milk proteins. Microbes in Sewage Treatment

Sewage (municipal waste water) contains organic matter, pathogens, and suspended solids that must be removed before the water can be released into natural water bodies. The treatment process involves multiple stages:

Primary treatment: Physical process — sewage is passed through screens and grit chambers to remove large objects (rags, sticks, plastic), then enters sedimentation tanks where suspended solids settle as sludge and oils/grease float to the surface (skimming). Removes about 50-60% of suspended solids.

Secondary treatment (Biological): The effluent from primary treatment enters aeration tanks where air is bubbled through the liquid. Aerobic microbes (bacteria, fungi, protozoa) grow and form flocs (masses of microbes held together by slime layers). These microbes consume organic matter (BOD reduction). After aeration, the mixture enters a settling tank where the microbial flocs settle as activated sludge. Some sludge is recycled to maintain the microbial population in the aeration tank. The remaining sludge is transferred to an anaerobic digester. The clarified effluent (treated water) is disinfected (chlorinated or UV treated) before being released into rivers or reused for irrigation. The activated sludge process reduces BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand) by 90-95%.

Sludge treatment: The settled sludge is digested anaerobically by methanogenic bacteria (Methanobacterium) in a closed digester tank. This produces biogas (methane) which can be used as fuel. The remaining digested sludge is used as fertiliser (rich in nutrients). BOD: Amount of oxygen (in mg/L) required by aerobic microbes to decompose organic matter in a water sample over 5 days at 20°C. High BOD = high organic pollution. Clean water has BOD < 5 mg/L; raw sewage has BOD of 200-600 mg/L. BOD is a key parameter for assessing water quality and treatment efficiency.

Microbes in Biogas Production

Biogas is a clean, renewable fuel produced by the anaerobic digestion of organic matter. Composition: Methane (CH4: 50-70%), Carbon dioxide (CO2: 30-40%), Hydrogen (H2: 1-5%), Hydrogen sulphide (H2S: traces). Biogas is produced by the action of methanogenic bacteria (Methanobacterium, Methanococcus, Methanosarcina) — these are archaebacteria found naturally in marshes, swamps, and the gut of ruminant animals (cows, buffaloes).

Biogas plant: A typical biogas plant (e.g., Gobar gas plant) has: (1) Mixing tank — cow dung and water are mixed into a slurry. (2) Digester — a sealed, underground concrete tank where anaerobic digestion occurs. The slurry remains for about 40-50 days. (3) Gas holder — a movable steel or fibreglass dome that collects the biogas produced. (4) Outlet — the spent slurry (rich in nutrients) flows out and is used as fertiliser. (5) Gas pipe — connects to stoves, lamps, or generators. A family-sized plant (2-4 m3 gas/day) requires about 25-50 kg of cow dung daily. Benefits: clean fuel (reduces smoke indoors), fertiliser from slurry, waste disposal, reduces deforestation (less wood burning), and reduces greenhouse gas emissions (methane is captured rather than released from open manure pits).

NEET Key — Biogas Production Conditions
Anaerobic digestion requires: (1) Absence of oxygen — digester is sealed. (2) Temperature: 30-40°C (mesophilic) or 50-60°C (thermophilic). (3) pH: 6.5-7.5 (neutral). Microbes as Biocontrol Agents

Using natural predators/pathogens to control pests, avoiding chemical pesticides. Biofertilisers are cost-effective, eco-friendly, and reduce the need for chemical fertilisers.

Microbial Fermentation — Kinetics and Bioreactors

Industrial fermentation involves the large-scale cultivation of microorganisms to produce valuable products. Understanding fermentation kinetics is crucial for optimising yield and productivity.

Fermentation kinetics: Microbial growth in a closed system (batch culture) follows distinct phases: Lag phase — microbes adapt to the new environment, synthesising enzymes needed for substrate utilisation (no net growth). Log (exponential) phase — cells divide at a constant maximum rate (μ_max, the specific growth rate). Nutrients are abundant, waste products are minimal. Stationary phase — growth rate equals death rate due to nutrient depletion and accumulation of toxic metabolites (ethanol, organic acids). This is when secondary metabolites (antibiotics, statins) are often produced — the idophase (trophophase = growth phase, idophase = production phase). Death phase — number of viable cells declines exponentially. Key parameters: Specific growth rate (μ, h⁻¹), doubling time (t_d = ln2/μ), substrate utilisation rate, product yield (Y_p/s = g product/g substrate consumed), and productivity (g product/L/h).

Batch Fermentation: All nutrients are added at the beginning in a closed vessel. Microbes grow through all phases. Simple, low risk of contamination, flexible. Disadvantages: Low productivity due to downtime between batches (cleaning, sterilisation, re-inoculation), substrate inhibition at high initial concentrations, variable product quality between batches. Used for: High-value products (antibiotics — penicillin), enzymes, speciality chemicals.

Continuous Fermentation: Fresh nutrients are continuously added and culture fluid is continuously removed at the same rate (steady-state conditions). The system maintains cells in log phase (constant growth rate) by controlling dilution rate. Advantages: Higher productivity (no downtime), consistent product quality, better control of growth conditions. Disadvantages: Higher risk of contamination (long operation), strain degeneration (mutants may take over), complex control systems required. Used for: Single-cell protein production, ethanol production (some processes), waste treatment.

Fed-batch Fermentation: Nutrients are added incrementally during the process, but culture is not removed until the end. Combines advantages of batch and continuous — avoids substrate inhibition, allows high cell density, extends production phase. Commonly used for penicillin production — glucose is fed continuously to maintain optimal concentration without causing catabolite repression (high glucose represses penicillin synthesis).

Bioreactors and Immobilised Enzymes

A bioreactor (or fermenter) is a vessel designed to provide optimal conditions for microbial growth and product formation. Key features: (1) Agitation system — impellers (Rushton turbine) for mixing and mass transfer. (2) Aeration system — sparger (ring with holes) to introduce sterile air. (3) Temperature control — cooling jacket or internal coils. (4) pH control — acid/base addition via pumps. (5) Sensors — for temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), foam detection. (6) Sterile ports — for inoculation, sampling, and addition of nutrients. (7) Foam control — foam breaker or antifoam addition. (8) Computer control system (SCADA) for monitoring and automation.

Immobilised enzymes: Enzymes that are attached to an inert support material (matrix) to allow re-use and continuous operation. Advantages: Reusability (reduces cost), increased stability (heat, pH, organic solvents), easier product recovery (enzyme separated from product), continuous processing in packed-bed reactors. Methods of immobilisation: (1) Adsorption — physical binding to a solid support (activated charcoal, silica, alumina) via weak forces (van der Waals, ionic). Simple but reversible. (2) Covalent bonding — chemical bonds between enzyme and support (agarose, cellulose, polyacrylamide). Strong binding, not reversible. (3) Entrapment — enzymes are trapped in a gel matrix (alginate beads, polyacrylamide gel, silica sol-gel). Substrate and product diffuse in/out. (4) Encapsulation — enzymes are enclosed in semi-permeable membranes (microcapsules, liposomes). (5) Cross-linking — enzymes are cross-linked to each other (CLEAs — Cross-Linked Enzyme Aggregates) using glutaraldehyde. Applications: Immobilised glucose isomerase (production of high-fructose corn syrup — the largest industrial use of immobilised enzymes), immobilised penicillin acylase (production of semi-synthetic penicillins), immobilised lipases (biodiesel production), immobilised lactase (lactose-free milk), immobilised aminoacylase (production of L-amino acids), biosensors (glucose sensor using immobilised glucose oxidase).

Industrial Production of Penicillin

Penicillin, the first antibiotic discovered (Fleming, 1928), is produced industrially by large-scale fermentation using Penicillium chrysogenum (a higher-yielding strain than P. notatum). The production process involves the following steps:

Step 1 — Strain improvement: Wild-type P. chrysogenum produces very low levels of penicillin. Strain improvement is done by mutation (UV radiation, chemical mutagens like NTG) and selection — modern strains produce >50,000 times more penicillin than Fleming's original isolate. Genetic engineering has also been used to introduce additional copies of biosynthetic gene clusters.

Step 2 — Inoculum preparation: A pure culture of the selected strain is grown in shake flasks to produce a seed culture. The seed culture is then transferred to larger seed fermenters to build up sufficient biomass for inoculation of the production fermenter (typically 5-10% v/v inoculum).

Step 3 — Production fermentation: The production fermenter (100,000-200,000 L capacity) contains a complex medium with: Carbon source (lactose or glucose — glucose is fed slowly to avoid catabolite repression), Nitrogen source (corn steep liquor — a byproduct of corn processing, rich in amino acids and nitrogen), Precursor (phenylacetic acid or phenoxyacetic acid — incorporated into the penicillin side chain), and minerals (phosphate, magnesium, sulfate). Fermentation conditions: Temperature 25-27°C, pH 6.5-6.8, dissolved oxygen maintained at >30% saturation by aeration (0.5-1 vvm) and agitation. Fermentation lasts 5-7 days in fed-batch mode.

Step 4 — Penicillin is a secondary metabolite: It is produced during the stationary (idiophase) phase, not during active growth (trophophase). As glucose is depleted and growth slows, the fungus enters the production phase where penicillin biosynthetic enzymes are activated. Continuous feeding of glucose (fed-batch) maintains the culture in a prolonged production phase.

Step 5 — Recovery and purification: After fermentation, the broth is filtered to remove mycelial biomass (fungal cells). The filtrate (containing penicillin) is cooled to 0-5°C and acidified (pH 2.0-2.5) with phosphoric acid. Penicillin (an organic acid) is extracted into an organic solvent (amyl acetate or butyl acetate) by liquid-liquid extraction at low pH. The organic phase is separated and penicillin is back-extracted into a neutral aqueous buffer at pH 7.0-7.5. The buffer solution is treated with activated charcoal to remove impurities. Penicillin is crystallised by adjusting pH and adding suitable solvents. Crystals are washed, dried, and packaged under sterile conditions.

Step 6 — Semi-synthetic penicillins: Natural penicillin (Penicillin G — benzylpenicillin) can be modified to produce semi-synthetic penicillins with improved properties. The enzyme penicillin acylase (or chemical hydrolysis) removes the side chain to produce 6-APA (6-aminopenicillanic acid). Different side chains are then attached chemically to produce: Amoxicillin (broader spectrum, oral), Ampicillin (broader spectrum, oral/injectable), Methicillin (penicillinase-resistant — but now MRSA has evolved), Piperacillin (anti-pseudomonal). Semi-synthetic penicillins overcome limitations of natural penicillin: narrow spectrum, acid lability (destroyed by stomach acid — penicillin V is acid-stable), and susceptibility to β-lactamases.

NEET Key — Fermentation Conditions for Penicillin

Carbon source: Lactose or controlled glucose feed (to avoid catabolite repression — high glucose suppresses penicillin biosynthesis). Precursor: Phenylacetic acid. NEET Key — Trophophase vs Idophase
Trophophase (growth phase) — primary metabolites produced (biomass, ethanol, organic acids). Idophase (production phase) — secondary metabolites produced (antibiotics, statins, alkaloids). Growth-associated products (e.g., ethanol from yeast): produced during log phase. Non-growth-associated products (e.g., penicillin): produced during stationary/idophase. Mixed-growth-associated products: produced during late log/early stationary phase.

Population Control & Family Planning

India was the first country in the world to launch a national family planning programme (1952). The goal is to stabilise the population at a level consistent with sustainable economic development. Life Cycle of Malarial Parasite (Plasmodium)

Malaria is caused by four species of Plasmodium: P. vivax (benign tertian malaria — fever every 48 hours), P. falciparum (malignant tertian — most severe, fever irregular, cerebral malaria), P. malariae (quartan malaria — fever every 72 hours), P. ovale (rare). Life cycle involves two hosts:

In female Anopheles mosquito (definitive host): Sexual reproduction occurs. When the mosquito bites an infected person, it ingests gametocytes (male and female) from the blood. In the mosquito's gut, gametocytes fuse to form a zygote → ookinete → oocyst → sporozoites (infective stage). Sporozoites migrate to the salivary glands.

In humans (intermediate host): (1) Sporozoites are injected when an infected mosquito bites. (2) They travel via bloodstream to the liver (hepatic stage) and invade hepatocytes. (3) In the liver, they multiply asexually (exo-erythrocytic schizogony) producing thousands of merozoites. (4) Merozoites burst out of liver cells and invade red blood cells (erythrocytic stage). (5) In RBCs, they grow and multiply (erythrocytic schizogony), rupturing RBCs and releasing more merozoites — this rupture causes the characteristic periodic fevers (chills and fever spikes). (6) Some merozoites differentiate into gametocytes (sexual stage) which can be picked up by another mosquito. Diagnosis: Blood smear examination (Gold standard) — thick and thin smears stained with Giemsa stain. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) detect Plasmodium antigens. Treatment: Chloroquine (first-line for uncomplicated malaria), Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT — for drug-resistant P. falciparum), Primaquine (to prevent relapse in P. vivax by killing hypnozoites in the liver). Prevention: Mosquito control (insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying), chemoprophylaxis for travellers, eliminating mosquito breeding sites (stagnant water).

Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR)

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, viruses, parasites) develop the ability to survive exposure to drugs that previously killed them or inhibited their growth. This is a natural evolutionary process, but it is accelerated by the misuse and overuse of antimicrobial drugs in humans, animals, and agriculture. AMR is one of the top global public health threats — it is estimated that by 2050, 10 million deaths per year could be attributable to AMR (more than cancer). Mechanisms of resistance: (1) Enzymatic inactivation — bacteria produce enzymes that destroy the drug (e.g., β-lactamases destroy penicillin — ESBL-producing bacteria are resistant to most β-lactam antibiotics). (2) Target modification — bacteria alter the drug's target site (e.g., MRSA — methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus has altered PBP protein). (3) Efflux pumps — bacteria pump the drug out of the cell before it can act. (4) Reduced permeability — bacteria reduce drug entry through porins (e.g., carbapenem resistance in Pseudomonas aeruginosa). (5) Biofilm formation — bacteria form a protective matrix that prevents drug penetration. Contributing factors: Over-prescription of antibiotics, incomplete courses of treatment (bacteria survive and develop resistance), use of antibiotics in animal feed (growth promotion), lack of new drug development, poor infection control in hospitals (HAI — hospital-acquired infections). Solutions: Antibiotic stewardship (rational prescribing), completing full courses, developing new antibiotics, vaccination (prevents infections and reduces antibiotic use), improved hygiene and sanitation, infection prevention and control, limiting antibiotic use in agriculture. The WHO Global Action Plan on AMR (2015) calls for coordinated action across all sectors. India launched the National Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (NAP-AMR) in 2017.

NEET Key — AMR Mechanisms

(2) VRE (Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus) — altered cell wall precursor. (3) ESBL (Extended-spectrum β-lactamase) — enzymes that hydrolyse cephalosporins. (4) Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) — "nightmare bacteria" — produce carbapenemases (NDM-1 — New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase) that inactivate carbapenems (last-resort antibiotics). (5) MDR-TB (Multidrug-resistant TB) — resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin. XDR-TB (Extensively drug-resistant TB) — also resistant to fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides.

Cytokine Signaling and the JAK‑STAT Pathway

Cytokines bind to specific receptor complexes that lack intrinsic kinase activity. Instead, they associate with Janus kinases (JAKs) — JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2 — which become activated by trans‑phosphorylation upon ligand binding. The activated JAKs phosphorylate tyrosine residues on the receptor cytoplasmic tails, creating docking sites for Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription (STATs). STAT proteins (STAT1‑6) contain SH2 domains that bind these phosphotyrosines, are themselves phosphorylated by JAKs, dimerize, and translocate to the nucleus to regulate transcription of target genes such as IRF1, SOCS, and BCL2. The pathway is tightly controlled by negative feedback: Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling (SOCS) proteins bind JAKs or receptor phosphotyrosines, blocking further STAT recruitment, while protein inhibitors of activated STAT (PIAS) block DNA binding. Dysregulation underlies several pathologies: gain‑of‑function JAK2 V617F drives myeloproliferative neoplasms; loss‑of‑function JAK3 mutations cause severe combined immunodeficiency; and constitutive STAT3 activation promotes tumour immune evasion. Therapeutic JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib, tofacitinib) are approved for rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and myelofibrosis, illustrating the translational relevance of this cascade.

Different cytokine families preferentially engage distinct JAK/STAT combinations. Type I interferons (IFN‑α/β) signal through IFNAR1/2 → JAK1/TYK2 → STAT1/STAT2/IRF9 (ISGF3), inducing antiviral genes. IL‑6 family cytokines (IL‑6, IL‑11, OSM) use gp130 → JAK1/JAK2/TYK2 → STAT3, driving acute‑phase response and Th17 differentiation. IL‑12 and IL‑23 signal via JAK2/TYK2 → STAT4 and STAT3 respectively, shaping Th1 and Th17 lineages. IL‑4 and IL‑13 activate JAK1/JAK3 → STAT6, promoting Th2 responses and IgE class switching. Understanding these pairings allows prediction of immune phenotypes in genetic deficiencies and guides biologic therapy selection.

Cross‑talk with other pathways modulates JAK‑STAT output. MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and NF‑κB pathways can phosphorylate STATs on serine residues, altering transcriptional potency. Conversely, STATs can induce expression of cytokine receptors, creating feed‑forward loops. In chronic viral infections (e.g., hepatitis C), persistent IFN‑α signaling leads to STAT1‑driven expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD‑L1, contributing to T‑cell exhaustion. This interplay underscores why JAK inhibition can both dampen autoimmunity and impair antiviral immunity.

Immune Checkpoint Regulation Beyond Cancer

Immune checkpoints are co‑inhibitory or co‑stimulatory receptors that fine‑tune T‑cell activation, preventing immunopathology while permitting effective pathogen clearance. The best‑characterised inhibitory checkpoints are PD‑1 (programmed cell death protein 1) and CTLA‑4 (cytotoxic T‑lymphocyte‑associated protein 4). PD‑1 binds PD‑L1 and PD‑L2, which are expressed on antigen‑presenting cells, tumour cells, and inflamed tissues. Engagement recruits SHP‑2 phosphatase, dephosphorylating CD28 and TCR proximal signaling molecules, thereby raising the activation threshold. CTLA‑4 competes with CD28 for B7‑1 (CD80) and B7‑2 (CD86) on dendritic cells, delivering a dominant negative signal that limits early T‑cell priming in lymph nodes. Blockade of these pathways with monoclonal antibodies (anti‑PD‑1, anti‑PD‑L1, anti‑CTLA‑4) has revolutionised oncology, but their physiological roles extend to chronic infections, autoimmunity, and transplantation.

Additional checkpoints include LAG‑3 (lymphocyte‑activation gene 3), TIM‑3 (T‑cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain‑3), TIGIT (T‑cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains), and VISTA (V‑domain Ig suppressor of T‑cell activation). LAG‑3 binds MHC class II with high affinity, inhibiting CD4⁺ T‑cell proliferation and cytokine production. TIM‑3 interacts with galectin‑9, phosphatidylserine, and HMGB1, marking exhausted T cells in chronic viral infections (HIV, HCV) and tumours. TIGIT competes with CD226 (DNAM‑1) for CD155 (PVR) and CD112, delivering inhibitory signals via its ITIM motif. VISTA, expressed on myeloid cells and naïve T cells, suppresses T‑cell activation in a ligand‑independent manner. Co‑expression of multiple checkpoints defines a deeply exhausted phenotype that is less reversible by single‑agent blockade, informing combination immunotherapy strategies.

In autoimmunity, checkpoint deficiency leads to uncontrolled self‑reactivity. CTLA‑4 haploinsufficiency causes a syndrome of lymphoproliferation, autoimmunity, and hypogammaglobulinaemia. PD‑1 knockout mice develop dilated cardiomyopathy and lupus‑like disease. Conversely, checkpoint agonists (e.g., CTLA‑4‑Ig fusion protein abatacept) are used to treat rheumatoid arthritis by restoring inhibitory signals. In transplantation, checkpoint pathways contribute to graft tolerance; PD‑1/PD‑L1 interactions promote regulatory T‑cell function and limit alloreactive effector responses. Therapeutic manipulation of checkpoints therefore requires context‑dependent balancing of immunity and tolerance.

Examiner's trap
A question states: "PD‑1 blockade enhances T‑cell responses in all clinical settings." Identify the flaw and explain the clinical nuance.
The statement is false because PD‑1 blockade can exacerbate autoimmunity, trigger immune‑related adverse events (colitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies), and is ineffective in tumours lacking pre‑existing T‑cell infiltration (cold tumours). Moreover, in chronic infections such as HIV, PD‑1 blockade alone does not restore functional immunity without concurrent antiretroviral therapy. The nuance: checkpoint inhibition works best when antigen‑specific T cells are present but restrained; it does not create de novo responses.

Antigen Cross‑Presentation and Dendritic Cell Subsets

Cross‑presentation is the process by which exogenous antigens are presented on MHC class I molecules to CD8⁺ T cells, a prerequisite for cytotoxic immunity against viruses, tumours, and intracellular bacteria that do not infect professional antigen‑presenting cells. In mice, the conventional dendritic cell type 1 (cDC1) subset, defined by XCR1⁺, CLEC9A⁺, and dependence on the transcription factor Batf3, is the principal cross‑presenting population. Human equivalents are CD141⁺ (BDCA‑3⁺) cDC1s, also Batf3‑dependent and XCR1⁺. These cells excel at phagosome‑to‑cytosol antigen export, proteasomal degradation, and TAP‑dependent peptide loading onto MHC I. The endosomal receptor CLEC9A (DNGR‑1) detects exposed F‑actin on necrotic cells, directing dead‑cell antigens into the cross‑presentation pathway.

Mechanistically, two routes exist: the vacuolar pathway, where antigens are processed within the phagosome by cathepsins and loaded onto recycling MHC I, and the cytosolic pathway, where antigens escape into the cytosol via Sec61 or other channels, undergo proteasomal degradation, and are transported by TAP into the ER or phagosomal membrane for loading. The cytosolic route is dominant for soluble antigens and requires NADPH oxidase‑generated ROS to limit phagosomal acidification, preserving antigen integrity. cDC1s express high levels of the transcription factor IRF8, which drives expression of the machinery for both routes, including the chaperone HSP90 and the ER‑phagosome fusion protein Sec22b.

cDC2s (CD1c⁺ in humans, CD11b⁺ in mice) are superior at MHC II presentation and Th2/Th17 polarization but have limited cross‑presentation capacity. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) specialise in type I interferon production via TLR7/9 and are poor cross‑presenters. Targeting antigens to cDC1‑specific receptors (XCR1, CLEC9A, DEC‑205) enhances CD8⁺ T‑cell priming in vaccine design. Conversely, tumours often impair cDC1 recruitment (via VEGF, PGE₂) or induce tolerogenic cDC1s that express IDO and PD‑L1, contributing to immune evasion. Restoring cDC1 function with FLT3L, STING agonists, or Batf3‑inducing cytokines is an active area of immunotherapy research.

Immunosenescence and Age‑Related Immune Decline

Immunosenescence describes the progressive remodelling of the immune system with age, leading to increased susceptibility to infections, reduced vaccine efficacy, higher cancer incidence, and a chronic low‑grade inflammatory state termed inflammaging. Thymic involution begins after puberty, causing a steep decline in naïve T‑cell output. The peripheral T‑cell pool shifts toward memory and effector phenotypes, with oligoclonal expansions of CMV‑specific CD8⁺ T cells (memory inflation) occupying a large fraction of the repertoire. These cells often express senescence markers (CD57, KLRG‑1, loss of CD28) and have shortened telomeres, limiting proliferative capacity.

Innate immunity also alters: neutrophils exhibit impaired chemotaxis and phagocytosis; macrophages show reduced TLR signaling and a skew toward an M2‑like phenotype; NK cells retain cytotoxicity but produce less IFN‑γ. Dendritic cells from older individuals have diminished migration to lymph nodes and reduced co‑stimulatory molecule expression, impairing priming of naïve T cells. B‑cell repertoire diversity contracts, with fewer naïve B cells and increased autoreactive antibodies. Serum levels of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6, TNF‑α, CRP) rise, driven by senescent cell SASP (senescence‑associated secretory phenotype), gut microbiota translocation, and chronic viral infections.

Clinically, immunosenescence explains the poor response to influenza, pneumococcal, and COVID‑19 vaccines in the elderly. Strategies to mitigate it include high‑dose or adjuvanted vaccines (MF59, AS01), recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) which elicits robust CD4⁺ T‑cell responses despite age, and interventions targeting inflammaging (e.g., metformin, senolytics). Thymic regeneration via IL‑7 or keratinocyte growth factor remains experimental. Understanding the molecular drivers — mTOR hyperactivity, DNA damage response, epigenetic drift — informs geriatric immunology and vaccine scheduling.

Vaccine Adjuvants: Mechanisms and Clinical Relevance

Adjuvants enhance the magnitude, breadth, and durability of vaccine‑induced immunity by stimulating innate sensors that shape adaptive responses. Aluminium salts (alum) have been used since the 1920s; they form a depot at the injection site, recruit monocytes and neutrophils, and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL‑1β and IL‑18 release. However, alum biases toward Th2 responses and is weak at inducing CD8⁺ T‑cell immunity. Oil‑in‑water emulsions such as MF59 (squalene‑based) and AS03 create a local immunocompetent environment, enhancing antigen uptake by dendritic cells and promoting Th1/Th2 balanced responses; they are licensed in seasonal and pandemic influenza vaccines.

TLR agonists represent a rational adjuvant class. Monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL), a detoxified TLR4 agonist, is a component of AS04 (used in HPV and hepatitis B vaccines) and AS01 (liposome‑based, combined with QS‑21 saponin). CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (TLR9 agonists) drive strong Th1 and cytotoxic T‑cell responses and are incorporated in the hepatitis B vaccine Heplisav‑B. The STING agonist cyclic‑di‑AMP and cGAMP are under investigation for their ability to induce type I interferon and cross‑presentation. Saponin‑based adjuvants (QS‑21, ISCOMATRIX) form cholesterol‑dependent pores in antigen‑presenting cell membranes, facilitating cytosolic antigen delivery and cross‑presentation.

Adjuvant selection tailors the immune profile to the pathogen. For intracellular viruses and cancers, adjuvants that promote CD8⁺ T‑cell and Th1 immunity (TLR3/4/9 agonists, STING agonists, saponins) are preferred. For extracellular bacteria and toxins, Th2/Th17‑skewing adjuvants (alum, MF59) may suffice. Combination adjuvants (e.g., AS01 = MPL + QS‑21 in liposomes) exploit synergistic pathways: MPL activates TLR4 → MyD88/TRIF → NF‑κB/IRF3, while QS‑21 enhances antigen cross‑presentation and inflammasome activation. Safety monitoring focuses on reactogenicity (local pain, fever) and rare autoimmune phenomena; large‑scale post‑licensure surveillance continues to inform benefit‑risk assessments.

  • Equating immunosenescence solely with thymic involution; innate dysfunction, inflammaging, and CMV‑driven memory inflation are equally important.
  • Believing alum induces strong CD8⁺ T‑cell responses; alum is Th2‑biased and poor at cross‑presentation.

T Cell Exhaustion: Molecular Drivers and Therapeutic Reversal

Chronic antigen exposure in persistent viral infections (e.g., hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV) and in the tumour microenvironment drives a distinct differentiation state known as T cell exhaustion. Exhausted CD8⁺ T cells progressively lose effector functions in a hierarchical order: first IL‑2 production and proliferative capacity, then TNF‑α secretion, and finally IFN‑γ release and cytotoxic granule exocytosis. The transcriptional programme is orchestrated by the transcription factor TOX, which cooperates with NR4A family members to enforce an epigenetic landscape that stabilises the exhausted phenotype. Persistent TCR signalling sustains high expression of inhibitory receptors — PD‑1, TIM‑3, LAG‑3, TIGIT, CTLA‑4 — each delivering non‑redundant negative signals that dampen PI3K‑AKT‑mTOR and RAS‑MAPK pathways. Metabolic profiling reveals a shift from glycolysis toward fatty‑acid oxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, further limiting effector capacity. Therapeutically, blockade of PD‑1/PD‑L1 can partially reinvigorate a subset of “progenitor exhausted” T cells that retain TCF‑1 expression and stem‑like proliferative potential. However, terminally exhausted cells (TCF‑1⁻, TOX⁺⁺) are largely refractory to checkpoint inhibition alone. Combination strategies under investigation include dual checkpoint blockade (PD‑1 + TIM‑3 or LAG‑3), epigenetic modulators (DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, HDAC inhibitors) to remodel chromatin accessibility, and cytokine support (IL‑21, IL‑7) to restore metabolic fitness. Adoptive transfer of engineered T cells (CAR‑T or TCR‑T) that lack endogenous inhibitory receptors or express dominant‑negative PD‑1 represents a parallel avenue to bypass exhaustion. Clinical correlates show that the frequency of TCF‑1⁺ progenitor exhausted cells predicts response to PD‑1 blockade in melanoma and non‑small cell lung cancer, whereas a high proportion of terminally exhausted cells correlates with primary resistance.

Innate Lymphoid Cells: Classification, Functions, and Role in Barrier Immunity

Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) are lymphocyte‑lineage cells that lack rearranged antigen receptors and mirror the effector cytokine profiles of CD4⁺ T helper subsets. They are classified into five groups based on transcription factor dependence and cytokine output: ILC1 (T‑bet⁺, IFN‑γ), ILC2 (GATA‑3⁺, IL‑5, IL‑13), ILC3 (RORγt⁺, IL‑17, IL‑22), lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells (RORγt⁺, LTαβ), and natural killer (NK) cells (Eomes⁺, perforin/granzyme). ILCs reside predominantly at mucosal surfaces — intestinal lamina propria, lung, skin — where they respond rapidly to alarmins (IL‑33, IL‑25, TSLP) released by epithelial cells upon barrier breach. ILC2s drive type‑2 immunity against helminths and contribute to tissue repair via amphiregulin production; dysregulated ILC2 activity underlies allergic asthma and atopic dermatitis. ILC3s maintain intestinal homeostasis by producing IL‑22, which induces antimicrobial peptides (RegIIIγ, β‑defensins) and promotes epithelial regeneration; they also present antigen to CD4⁺ T cells via MHC‑II, influencing adaptive responses. LTi cells are essential for secondary lymphoid organogenesis during embryogenesis and for the maintenance of lymphoid structures in adults. NK cells, while sharing developmental origin, are distinguished by cytotoxic granules and expression of activating/inhibitory receptors (NKG2D, KIRs) that enable missing‑self recognition of virally infected or transformed cells. Crosstalk between ILCs and the microbiota is bidirectional: microbial metabolites (short‑chain fatty acids, aryl hydrocarbon receptor ligands) shape ILC3 function, while ILC‑derived cytokines modulate microbial composition. Therapeutic targeting of ILC pathways — anti‑IL‑33, anti‑TSLP, or RORγt inhibitors — is being explored for inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, and severe asthma.

Immunometabolism: Metabolic Reprogramming of Immune Cells in Health and Disease

Immune cell activation is inseparable from metabolic rewiring. Naïve T cells and resting macrophages rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty‑acid oxidation (FAO) for basal ATP production. Upon activation, effector T cells (Th1, Th17, CD8⁺ cytotoxic) switch to aerobic glycolysis — the Warburg‑like effect — to generate biosynthetic precursors (nucleotides, amino acids, lipids) required for rapid proliferation and cytokine production. This shift is driven by mTORC1 activation downstream of PI3K‑AKT signalling and is reinforced by HIF‑1α stabilisation even under normoxia. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and memory CD8⁺ T cells preferentially utilise FAO and mitochondrial respiration, a programme enforced by AMPK activation and the transcription factor Foxp3. M1‑polarised macrophages adopt glycolysis and succinate accumulation, which stabilises HIF‑1α and sustains IL‑1β production; M2 macrophages favour OXPHOS and FAO, supporting tissue repair. Metabolites themselves act as signalling molecules: succinate inhibits prolyl hydroxylases, enhancing HIF‑1α; itaconate (produced by IRG1) alkylates KEAP1, activating NRF2‑dependent anti‑oxidant responses; α‑ketoglutarate promotes TET‑mediated DNA demethylation, influencing epigenetic programming. In the tumour microenvironment, nutrient competition (glucose, glutamine) and accumulation of lactate, adenosine, and kynurenine suppress effector T cell metabolism while favouring Treg and myeloid‑derived suppressor cell function. Therapeutic modulation includes mTOR inhibitors (rapamycin) to promote memory formation, IDO1 inhibitors to block tryptophan catabolism, and agents that enhance mitochondrial fitness (e.g., metformin, bezafibrate) to improve checkpoint blockade efficacy. Understanding immunometabolic checkpoints provides biomarkers (e.g., lactate dehydrogenase, kynurenine/tryptophan ratio) for patient stratification.

Viral Immune Evasion Strategies: From Antigen Presentation Blockade to Cytokine Mimicry

Viruses have evolved multilayered mechanisms to subvert both innate and adaptive immunity. Herpesviruses (HSV‑1, CMV, EBV) encode proteins that interfere with MHC class I peptide loading: CMV US2 and US11 target nascent heavy chains for ER‑associated degradation; HSV ICP47 blocks TAP transporter function; EBV BNLF2a inhibits TAP tapasin interaction. These actions reduce surface peptide‑MHC complexes, limiting CD8⁺ T cell recognition. Many viruses also down‑regulate MHC class II or invariant chain to impair CD4⁺ T cell help. To evade NK cell “missing‑self” detection, CMV expresses UL18 (an MHC‑I homologue) and UL40 peptides that engage inhibitory NK receptors (CD94/NKG2A). Poxviruses secrete soluble cytokine receptors (e.g., vaccinia B18R binds type I IFN; myxoma M‑T7 binds IFN‑γ) that act as decoys, neutralising antiviral cytokines. Herpesviruses encode viral IL‑10 homologues (CMV UL111A, EBV BCRF1) that suppress dendritic cell maturation and Th1 polarization. Influenza NS1 protein inhibits RIG‑I/MAVS signalling and blocks PKR activation, dampening interferon production. HIV Nef down‑regulates CD4 and MHC‑I, while Vpu antagonises tetherin (BST‑2) to facilitate virion release. SARS‑CoV‑2 ORF6 blocks STAT1/STAT2 nuclear import, and ORF3b suppresses IFN‑β promoter activation. Some viruses manipulate host cell death pathways: adenovirus E1B‑19K mimics Bcl‑2 to inhibit apoptosis; CMV encodes vICA (UL36) that blocks caspase‑8. Understanding these evasion tactics informs vaccine design — e.g., inclusion of conserved epitopes that escape MHC down‑regulation, or engineering viral vectors lacking immune‑modulatory genes to enhance immunogenicity.

Microbiome–Immune System Crosstalk: Mechanisms and Clinical Implications

The commensal microbiota shapes immune development from birth. Germ‑free mice exhibit under‑developed gut‑associated lymphoid tissue, reduced IgA, fewer Th17 and Treg cells, and defective ILC3 function. Microbial colonisation restores these compartments through pattern‑recognition receptor (PRR) signalling (TLR2, TLR4, NOD2) on epithelial and immune cells. Short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — acetate, propionate, butyrate — produced by anaerobic fermentation of dietary fibre, act as histone deacetylase inhibitors, promoting Foxp3⁺ Treg differentiation and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity via HIF‑1α stabilisation. Segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) induce Th17 cells in the lamina propria through serum amyloid A‑mediated dendritic cell activation. Bile acid metabolites (e.g., lithocholic acid) engage the nuclear receptor FXR and the GPCR TGR5, modulating macrophage cytokine output and NKT cell function. Tryptophan catabolites (indole‑3‑aldehyde, kynurenine) activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) on ILC3s and intraepithelial lymphocytes, driving IL‑22 production and mucosal defence. Dysbiosis — loss of diversity, expansion of pathobionts (Enterobacteriaceae, adherent‑invasive E. coli) — is linked to inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, and extra‑intestinal autoimmunity (multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis). Therapeutic avenues include faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection and experimental use in ulcerative colitis; defined bacterial consortia (e.g., VE303, SER‑109) targeting specific immune pathways; and prebiotic/probiotic strategies to boost SCFA production. Emerging data show that antibiotic‑induced microbiota depletion impairs checkpoint inhibitor efficacy in melanoma, while enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila correlates with improved response. Integrating microbiome profiling with immunophenotyping may enable personalised immunomodulation.

  • Attributing all exhausted T cell dysfunction to PD‑1 alone; TIM‑3, LAG‑3, TIGIT, and metabolic insufficiency contribute non‑redundantly.
  • Assuming glycolysis is universally detrimental; effector T cells require glycolysis for rapid proliferation, while memory formation depends on FAO.
  • Overlooking viral decoy receptors that bind cytokines (e.g., vaccinia B18R for type I IFN) and treating them as host‑derived regulators.
  • Equating microbiome diversity with health in all contexts; certain disease states (e.g., colorectal cancer) show specific pathobiont enrichment rather than global diversity loss.
  • Believing that SCFAs only act via HDAC inhibition; they also signal through GPR43/GPR41 and modulate metabolic checkpoints such as mTOR.
  • Neglecting the role of AhR ligands from tryptophan metabolism in ILC3 maintenance and barrier immunity.

Regulatory T Cell Subsets and Their Clinical Modulation

Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are a heterogeneous population defined by constitutive expression of the transcription factor Foxp3 and the high‑affinity IL‑2 receptor CD25. In humans, the major thymus‑derived (tTreg) subset expresses Helios and neuropilin‑1, whereas peripherally induced (pTreg) cells arise from naïve CD4⁺ T cells under TGF‑β and retinoic acid signaling and often lack Helios. A third subset, follicular regulatory T (Tfr) cells, expresses CXCR5 and Bcl‑6, localises to germinal centres, and restrains follicular helper T (Tfh)‑driven antibody responses. Tissue‑resident Tregs (trTregs) display distinct surface markers such as CCR8, ST2, and GATA‑3 in visceral adipose tissue, or RORγt in the gut lamina propria, enabling adaptation to local cytokine milieus.

Clinical modulation exploits these phenotypic differences. Low‑dose IL‑2 selectively expands CD25⁺⁺ Tregs without activating conventional effector T cells, a strategy tested in graft‑versus‑host disease and type‑1 diabetes. Rapamycin (sirolimus) preferentially spares Tregs while inhibiting mTOR‑driven effector proliferation, forming the backbone of tolerance‑inducing regimens in kidney transplantation. Anti‑CTLA‑4 antibodies (ipilimumab) deplete intratumoral Tregs via Fc‑γR‑mediated ADCC, contributing to checkpoint‑inhibitor efficacy but also precipitating immune‑related adverse events. Conversely, agonist anti‑GITR or anti‑OX40 antibodies can transiently destabilise Treg suppression to boost anti‑tumour immunity, though dosing windows are narrow to avoid systemic autoimmunity.

Emerging adoptive‑cell approaches isolate autologous Tregs, expand them ex‑vivo with rapamycin and IL‑2, and reinfuse them for autoimmune indications such as refractory lupus nephritis. Gene‑editing (CRISPR‑Cas9) of the FOXP3 locus or insertion of chimeric antigen receptors (CAR‑Tregs) directed against allo‑HLA or autoantigens (e.g., myelin basic protein) aims to generate antigen‑specific suppression with reduced off‑target effects. Biomarker monitoring includes demethylated FOXP3 TSDR (Treg‑specific demethylated region) status, Ki‑67 proliferation index, and serum IL‑2/IL‑10 ratios to gauge therapeutic Treg fitness.

Trained Immunity: Epigenetic Reprogramming of Innate Cells

Trained immunity describes a memory‑like functional enhancement of innate immune cells — monocytes, macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells — after exposure to certain vaccines or microbial components. Unlike adaptive memory, it does not involve antigen‑specific receptors but relies on epigenetic rewiring of chromatin at promoters and enhancers of inflammatory genes. The Bacillus Calmette‑Guérin (BCG) vaccine is the prototypical inducer; a single intradermal dose reprograms circulating monocytes for months, increasing histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and H3K27ac at the TNF, IL‑6, and IL‑1β loci. Metabolic shifts accompany these marks: a transient Warburg‑like aerobic glycolysis driven by mTOR‑HIF‑1α signaling supplies acetyl‑CoA for histone acetyltransferases, while glutaminolysis fuels α‑ketoglutarate‑dependent demethylases.

β‑glucan (dectin‑1 agonist) and the adjuvant CpG‑ODN (TLR9 ligand) also elicit trained phenotypes, but the durability and cytokine profile differ. BCG‑trained monocytes display heightened IL‑1β and TNF upon heterologous challenge with Candida albicans or Staphylococcus aureus, whereas β‑glucan training skews toward IL‑6 and IL‑10. NK cells acquire enhanced IFN‑γ production and cytotoxicity after cytokine pre‑activation (IL‑12/IL‑15/IL‑18), a process dependent on STAT4‑mediated chromatin opening at the IFNG locus. Importantly, trained immunity can be detrimental: in atherosclerosis, BCG‑induced monocyte training amplifies foam‑cell formation via up‑regulated scavenger receptors; in severe COVID‑19, pre‑existing trained monocytes may contribute to hyper‑inflammation.

Therapeutic exploitation focuses on harnessing beneficial training while limiting pathology. Recombinant BCG strains engineered to over‑express antigens from unrelated pathogens (e.g., SARS‑CoV‑2 spike) aim to combine trained immunity with adaptive priming. Small‑molecule inhibitors of histone methyltransferases (e.g., MM‑102 targeting MLL1) or metabolic modulators (2‑deoxy‑glucose) are being tested to reverse maladaptive training in chronic inflammatory diseases. Clinical readouts include ATAC‑seq of peripheral monocytes, plasma cytokine kinetics after ex‑vivo LPS restimulation, and epigenetic clock assays to gauge training persistence.

Mucosal IgA Induction and Oral Tolerance Mechanisms

Secretory IgA (sIgA) is the dominant antibody isotype at mucosal surfaces, produced as dimeric IgA linked by J‑chain and transported across epithelium via the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR). Induction occurs in gut‑associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) — Peyer’s patches, isolated lymphoid follicles, and mesenteric lymph nodes — where dendritic cells (DCs) sample luminal antigens. CD103⁺ CX3CR1⁻ DCs migrate to draining nodes, present antigen to naïve B cells, and provide TGF‑β, retinoic acid (RA), and APRIL/BAFF signals that drive class‑switch recombination to IgA. RA imprints gut‑homing receptors α4β7 and CCR9 on plasmablasts, ensuring their return to the lamina propria.

Oral tolerance prevents immune responses to dietary proteins and commensal antigens. Two non‑exclusive mechanisms operate: (1) anergy/deletion of antigen‑specific T cells via steady‑state presentation by CD103⁺ DCs in the absence of inflammation, and (2) active suppression by Foxp3⁺ Tregs induced by the same DCs under TGF‑β/RA. High‑dose antigen favors clonal deletion; low‑dose antigen favors Treg expansion. Disruption of either pathway underlies food allergy and inflammatory bowel disease. Notably, the microbiota shapes the tolerogenic DC phenotype: segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) promote Th17 differentiation, while Clostridia clusters IV and XIVa enhance colonic Treg accumulation via short‑chain fatty acids (butyrate) that inhibit histone deacetylases.

Vaccination strategies targeting mucosal IgA exploit these pathways. Intranasal or oral vaccines adjuvanted with cholera toxin B subunit (CTB) or double‑mutant heat‑labile toxin (dmLT) enhance DC migration and RA production, boosting IgA‑secreting plasmablast generation. Recent phase‑I trials of an oral SARS‑CoV‑2 spike vaccine using a recombinant adenovirus vector demonstrated durable salivary IgA and reduced viral shedding, supporting the concept that mucosal IgA can block infection at the portal of entry. Monitoring includes fecal IgA ELISA, salivary sIgA quantification, and flow cytometric assessment of α4β7⁺ IgA⁺ plasmablasts in peripheral blood as a surrogate for gut homing.

Antibody‑Dependent Enhancement in Viral Pathogenesis

Antibody‑dependent enhancement (ADE) occurs when sub‑neutralising or non‑neutralising antibodies facilitate viral entry into Fc‑γ‑receptor‑bearing cells, increasing infection severity. The classic model is dengue virus (DENV): primary infection with one serotype generates serotype‑specific neutralising antibodies; a secondary heterologous infection yields cross‑reactive antibodies that bind but do not neutralise the new serotype. These immune complexes engage FcγRIIA on monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, promoting viral uptake, replication, and a cytokine storm (elevated IL‑6, TNF‑α, IFN‑γ) that drives vascular leakage and shock.

ADE has also been documented for SARS‑CoV‑1, MERS‑CoV, and in vitro for SARS‑CoV‑2, though clinical relevance for COVID‑19 remains debated. In SARS‑CoV‑1, anti‑spike IgG from convalescent patients enhanced infection of FcγRII‑expressing B‑cell lines, and vaccine candidates eliciting high‑titer non‑neutralising antibodies caused eosinophilic lung immunopathology in animal models. For SARS‑CoV‑2, monoclonal antibodies targeting the N‑terminal domain (NTD) of spike have shown Fc‑mediated enhancement in cell culture, prompting selection of Fc‑silenced (LALA‑PGL) variants for therapeutic use. Conversely, broadly neutralising antibodies targeting the receptor‑binding domain (RBD) with high affinity and Fc‑engineered effector functions (e.g., enhanced ADCC) have not demonstrated ADE in vivo.

Mitigation strategies focus on vaccine design and antibody engineering. Epitope‑focused immunogens that present only the RBD or prefusion‑stabilised spike reduce off‑target NTD responses. Fc engineering (e.g., N297A, LALA) ablates FcγR binding while preserving half‑life via FcRn interaction. In dengue, the tetravalent live‑attenuated vaccine (TAK‑003) aims to induce balanced neutralising titers against all four serotypes, minimising the window for ADE. Surveillance for ADE in clinical trials includes FcγR‑dependent infection assays using primary monocytes, measurement of cytokine signatures (IL‑10, IL‑6) after immune‑complex stimulation, and longitudinal monitoring of antibody avidity and neutralisation breadth.

Host‑Directed Therapies for Intracellular Pathogens

Host‑directed therapy (HDT) targets host cellular pathways essential for pathogen survival, reducing selective pressure for microbial resistance. For Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the most advanced HDTs repurpose existing drugs: metformin activates AMPK, enhancing mitochondrial ROS production and phagolysosomal fusion; statins inhibit cholesterol synthesis, disrupting the lipid‑rich niche M. tuberculosis exploits; and vitamin D supplementation up‑regulates cathelicidin (LL‑37) and autophagy via the VDR‑CAMP axis. Clinical trials (e.g., NCT02652778) combining metformin with standard anti‑TB regimens showed accelerated sputum conversion without increased hepatotoxicity.

In viral infections, HDTs modulate innate sensing or metabolic checkpoints. JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib, baricitinib) dampen cytokine‑driven immunopathology in severe influenza and COVID‑19 by blocking IFN‑γ/IL‑6 signaling, yet they may impair viral clearance if administered too early. Conversely, agonists of the STING pathway (e.g., diABZI) boost type I IFN production, enhancing antiviral states against RNA viruses; however, systemic STING activation risks cytokine release syndrome. Autophagy inducers (rapamycin, trehalose) promote xenophagy of intracellular bacteria (Salmonella, Listeria) and viruses (Zika, HSV‑1) by delivering cytosolic pathogens to lysosomes. The selectivity of autophagy cargo receptors (p62, NDP52, optineurin) determines pathogen‑specific efficacy.

Emerging HDTs exploit epigenetic regulators. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (vorinostat) reactivate latent HIV proviruses (“shock‑and‑kill”) while simultaneously enhancing CTL recognition. BET bromodomain inhibitors (JQ1) suppress HIV transcription by displacing BRD4 from the viral LTR, offering a “block‑and‑lock” alternative. For hepatitis B, capsid assembly modulators (CAMs) combined with TLR7 agonists (vesatolimod) aim to restore exhausted T‑cell function and achieve functional cure. Biomarker panels for HDT response include plasma IP‑10/CXCL10 (IFN‑γ‑inducible), monocyte HLA‑DR expression, and metabolomic signatures (kynurenine/tryptophan ratio) reflecting IDO activity.

  • Attributing ADE solely to antibody concentration — epitope specificity, Fc‑glycosylation, and FcγR polymorphism critically determine enhancement versus neutralisation.

  • Treating host‑directed therapies as universally safe — metabolic modulators (e.g., metformin) can impair protective immunity if timing or dosing is inappropriate.

Germinal Center Reaction: B Cell Selection, Affinity Maturation, and Memory Formation

The germinal center (GC) is a transient micro‑anatomical structure that forms within secondary lymphoid follicles after T‑cell‑dependent antigen encounter. It is divided into a dark zone (DZ) where centroblasts proliferate and undergo somatic hypermutation (SHM), and a light zone (LZ) where centrocytes compete for survival signals. The enzyme activation‑induced cytidine deaminase (AID) initiates SHM at a rate of approximately 10⁻³ mutations per base pair per division, targeting the immunoglobulin variable region. AID also mediates class‑switch recombination (CSR) by introducing double‑strand breaks in switch regions, allowing IgM⁺ B cells to become IgG, IgA, or IgE producers.

Selection in the LZ depends on the affinity of the B‑cell receptor (BCR) for antigen displayed on follicular dendritic cells (FDCs). High‑affinity centrocytes receive CD40L and cytokine signals (IL‑21, IL‑4) from T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, which express the transcription factor BCL6. BCL6 represses DNA‑damage response genes, permitting continued proliferation, while its antagonist BLIMP1 drives plasma‑cell differentiation. Cells that fail to acquire sufficient affinity undergo apoptosis, a process monitored by the pro‑apoptotic protein BIM. The output of the GC reaction comprises long‑lived plasma cells that home to bone marrow and memory B cells that recirculate, providing rapid secondary responses.

Defects in this pathway produce clinically distinct phenotypes. AID deficiency causes Hyper‑IgM syndrome type 2, characterized by normal IgM but absent class‑switched immunoglobulins and poor affinity maturation. Mutations in the transcription factor BCL6 or its regulator MEF2B are recurrent in diffuse large B‑cell lymphoma (DLBCL), reflecting the oncogenic potential of dysregulated GC B‑cell programs. Conversely, excessive GC activity underlies autoantibody production in systemic lupus erythematosus, where defective clearance of apoptotic nuclei fuels chronic Tfh stimulation.

Key quantitative landmarks for examiners: the average GC persists 2–3 weeks in mice and up to several months in humans; each centroblast undergoes 6–10 rounds of division; the mutation frequency in the complementarity‑determining region (CDR) can reach 5–10 % of nucleotides, generating a 10⁴‑fold affinity increase. Understanding these numbers allows rapid estimation of the diversity generated during a primary response.

Neutrophil Extracellular Traps: Formation, Pathogen Killing, and Role in Autoimmune Disease

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are web‑like structures composed of decondensed chromatin decorated with granular proteins such as neutrophil elastase (NE), myeloperoxidase (MPO), cathepsin G, and antimicrobial peptides (LL‑37). Their release, termed NETosis, can follow a suicidal pathway requiring NADPH oxidase‑derived reactive oxygen species (ROS) and peptidyl‑arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)‑mediated histone citrullination, or a vital pathway where live neutrophils expel mitochondrial DNA without lysis. PAD4 citrullinates histone H3 arginine residues, weakening chromatin–histone interactions and permitting chromatin expansion.

NETs immobilize and kill bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae), fungi (Candida albicans), and parasites (Leishmania) by concentrating proteases and oxidative enzymes at the pathogen surface. However, excessive or dysregulated NET formation contributes to pathology. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), impaired DNase I activity leads to accumulation of NET‑derived nucleosomes that become targets for anti‑dsDNA antibodies, forming immune complexes that deposit in kidneys. In ANCA‑associated vasculitis, proteinase‑3 and MPO displayed on NETs are recognized by anti‑neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, amplifying vascular inflammation. NETs also provide a scaffold for platelet adhesion and thrombin generation, linking infection to thrombosis in sepsis and COVID‑19.

Therapeutic strategies aim to degrade NETs or inhibit their formation. Recombinant human DNase I (dornase alfa) reduces NET burden in cystic fibrosis airway mucus and has shown benefit in murine lupus models. Small‑molecule PAD4 inhibitors (GSK484, JBI‑589) block histone citrullination and NET release without impairing phagocytosis. Clinical trials are evaluating PAD4 inhibition in rheumatoid arthritis and severe viral pneumonia. Biomarkers such as circulating citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) and MPO‑DNA complexes correlate with disease activity and NET‑driven organ damage.

A 32‑year‑old woman with SLE presents with active lupus nephritis. Laboratory studies reveal elevated serum levels of citrullinated histone H3 and MPO‑DNA complexes, while DNase I activity is low. The elevated CitH3 and MPO‑DNA complexes indicate abundant neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. Therefore, NETosis is the key process.

Host Genetic Determinants of Infectious Disease Susceptibility and Outcome

Human genetic variation shapes the risk of acquisition, severity, and clinical trajectory of many infections. The most replicated associations involve genes encoding immune receptors, signaling adapters, and metabolic enzymes. The CCR5 Δ32 deletion (a 32‑base‑pair frameshift) abolishes surface expression of the HIV‑1 coreceptor CCR5; homozygotes are highly resistant to R5‑tropic HIV infection, while heterozygotes show delayed disease progression. This variant reaches 10–15 % allele frequency in Northern European populations but is rare elsewhere.

HLA class I and II alleles dictate peptide presentation repertoires. HLA‑B*57:01 presents conserved HIV Gag epitopes, correlating with elite controller status; the same allele confers hypersensitivity to abacavir, necessitating pre‑treatment screening. HLA‑B*27:05 is strongly linked to ankylosing spondylitis and also influences clearance of hepatitis C virus (HCV). In malaria, the sickle‑cell trait (HbAS) reduces severe Plasmodium falciparum disease by impairing parasite growth in sickled erythrocytes, while glucose‑6‑phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency provides partial protection but predisposes to hemolysis under oxidative stress.

Innate immune sensors show functional polymorphisms. The IFITM3 rs12252‑C allele produces a truncated protein that fails to restrict influenza A virus entry, associating with severe pandemic H1N1 and seasonal influenza. TLR4 Asp299Gly and Thr399Ile variants blunt lipopolysaccharide responsiveness, increasing susceptibility to Gram‑negative sepsis. Mannose‑binding lectin (MBL) deficiency, caused by promoter and structural variants (e.g., MBL2*O), predisposes to recurrent respiratory infections and invasive meningococcal disease.

FUT2 secretor status determines expression of ABH antigens on mucosal surfaces. Non‑secretors (homozygous for the G428A nonsense mutation) lack α‑1,2‑fucosylated glycans, reducing norovirus binding and conferring resistance to many strains, yet they exhibit altered gut microbiota composition and increased risk of Crohn’s disease. These examples illustrate how single‑gene effects can be large (CCR5 Δ32) or modest (IFITM3), and how the same variant may have opposing effects on different pathogens or immune‑mediated diseases.

Mechanisms of Resistance to Immune Checkpoint Blockade in Cancer

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD‑1/PD‑L1 and CTLA‑4 have transformed oncology, yet a majority of patients either fail to respond (primary resistance) or relapse after initial benefit (acquired resistance). Resistance mechanisms are broadly categorized into tumor‑intrinsic, tumor‑extrinsic, and host‑related factors.

Tumor‑intrinsic loss of antigen presentation is a hallmark of acquired resistance. Inactivating mutations in β2‑microglobulin (B2M) disrupt MHC class I surface expression, rendering tumor cells invisible to CD8⁺ T cells. Similarly, loss‑of‑function mutations in JAK1 or JAK2 abrogate interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ) signaling, preventing up‑regulation of MHC molecules and PD‑L1, and blunting the inflammatory feedback loop that sustains T‑cell activity. Genomic analyses of melanoma and non‑small‑cell lung cancer (NSCLC) progressing on anti‑PD‑1 therapy reveal B2M mutations in 20–30 % of resistant lesions and JAK1/2 mutations in 10–15 %.

Adaptive immune evasion includes up‑regulation of alternative inhibitory receptors on exhausted T cells. LAG‑3, TIM‑3, TIGIT, and VISTA are co‑expressed with PD‑1 on tumor‑infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and deliver non‑redundant suppressive signals. Dual blockade (e.g., anti‑PD‑1 + anti‑LAG‑3) has shown improved response rates in melanoma (RELATIVITY‑047 trial). The tumor microenvironment (TME) also recruits immunosuppressive populations: regulatory T cells (Tregs) expressing CTLA‑4, myeloid‑derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) producing arginase‑1 and ROS, and cancer‑associated fibroblasts secreting TGF‑β, which excludes T cells from the tumor core.

Metabolic competition constitutes another barrier. Indoleamine‑2,3‑dioxygenase (IDO) and tryptophan‑2,3‑dioxygenase (TDO) catabolize tryptophan to kynurenine, activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) on T cells and promoting Treg differentiation. Adenosine generated by CD39/CD73 ectonucleotidases engages A2A receptors on T cells, inhibiting cytokine production. Pharmacologic IDO inhibitors (epacadostat) combined with ICIs failed in phase III melanoma trials, highlighting the redundancy of metabolic checkpoints.

Host factors influencing ICI efficacy include germline HLA diversity (higher heterozygosity correlates with better outcomes), gut microbiome composition (Akkermansia muciniphila, Bifidobacterium spp. associate with response), and antibiotic exposure prior to treatment (which diminishes benefit). Emerging biomarkers — tumor mutational burden (TMB), neoantigen quality, IFN‑γ gene signature, and circulating T‑cell receptor clonality — aim to predict resistance and guide combination strategies such as radiotherapy, oncolytic viruses, or epigenetic modulators to restore antigen presentation.

  • Confusing somatic hypermutation with class‑switch recombination — SHM diversifies the variable region, while CSR changes the constant region; both require AID but have distinct DNA substrates.
  • Assuming all NETosis is suicidal — vital NETosis releases mitochondrial DNA without cell death and is PAD4‑independent in some contexts.
  • Attributing CCR5 Δ32 protection to all HIV strains — only R5‑tropic viruses use CCR5; X4‑tropic or dual‑tropic strains can infect Δ32 homozygotes.
  • Equating HLA‑B*57:01 with universal HIV control — the allele’s benefit depends on the infecting viral clade and the host’s overall HLA repertoire.
  • Believing JAK1/2 mutations are the sole cause of IFN‑γ pathway loss — epigenetic silencing of STAT1 or IFNGR1 can produce identical phenotypes without coding mutations.
  • Overlooking the role of alternative checkpoints in primary resistance — tumors with high baseline LAG‑3 or TIM‑3 expression may never respond to PD‑1 blockade alone.
  • Treating microbiome modulation as a guaranteed adjuvant — fecal microbiota transplantation improves response in some melanoma cohorts but lacks standardized donor criteria and safety data.

cGAS‑STING Pathway: Cytosolic DNA Sensing, Autoimmunity, and Therapeutic Targeting

The cyclic GMP‑AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) axis is the principal cytosolic DNA surveillance system in mammalian cells. cGAS binds double‑stranded DNA (dsDNA) in a length‑dependent manner, catalyzing the synthesis of the second messenger 2′3′‑cGAMP from ATP and GTP. This cyclic dinucleotide binds to STING, a transmembrane adaptor resident on the endoplasmic reticulum, inducing a conformational change that traffics STING to the Golgi apparatus. There, STING recruits TANK‑binding kinase 1 (TBK1), which phosphorylates interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3). Phosphorylated IRF3 dimerizes, translocates to the nucleus, and drives transcription of type I interferons (IFN‑α/β) and a suite of interferon‑stimulated genes (ISGs). Parallel activation of NF‑κB via the same TBK1 complex amplifies pro‑inflammatory cytokine production.

Physiologically, the pathway detects viral genomes (e.g., herpesviruses, retroviruses), bacterial DNA that escapes phagosomes, and mitochondrial DNA released during cellular stress. However, self‑DNA can inadvertently activate cGAS‑STING, leading to sterile inflammation. Loss‑of‑function mutations in the exonuclease TREX1, which degrades cytosolic DNA, cause Aicardi‑Goutières syndrome, a severe type I interferonopathy. Similarly, gain‑of‑function mutations in STING (e.g., STING‑V155M) underlie STING‑associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI). These monogenic diseases illustrate how unchecked cGAS‑STING signaling drives autoimmunity.

In cancer, the pathway has a dual role. Tumor‑intrinsic cGAS‑STING activation by chromosomal instability‑derived micronuclei can promote anti‑tumor immunity through dendritic cell cross‑priming and CD8⁺ T‑cell recruitment. Conversely, chronic STING signaling in the tumor microenvironment may induce immunosuppressive cytokines, up‑regulate PD‑L1, and foster T‑cell exhaustion. Pharmacologic STING agonists (e.g., ADU‑S100, MK‑1454) have entered clinical trials as intratumoral adjuvants to boost checkpoint blockade efficacy. Systemic STING activation, however, risks cytokine storm; thus, targeted delivery and dose scheduling remain critical.

Therapeutic inhibition of cGAS or STING is being explored for interferon‑driven diseases. Small‑molecule cGAS inhibitors (RU.521, G150) block enzymatic activity, while STING antagonists (H‑151, SN‑011) covalently modify a conserved cysteine (Cys91 in human STING) to prevent palmitoylation and downstream signaling. Preclinical models of lupus, SAVI, and COVID‑19‑associated hyperinflammation show reduced IFN‑α signatures and tissue pathology with these agents. Biomarker development — measuring 2′3′‑cGAMP in serum or ISG expression in peripheral blood — aims to stratify patients for pathway‑targeted therapy.

Inflammasome Assembly, Pyroptosis, and Clinical Relevance in Autoinflammatory Diseases

Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes that activate caspase‑1, leading to proteolytic maturation of interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β) and IL‑18 and to gasdermin‑D (GSDMD)‑mediated pyroptotic cell death. Canonical inflammasomes require a sensor (NLRP3, NLRC4, AIM2, or pyrin), the adaptor ASC (apoptosis‑associated speck‑like protein containing a CARD), and pro‑caspase‑1. NLRP3 is the most versatile sensor, responding to diverse danger signals: potassium efflux, lysosomal rupture, mitochondrial ROS, and extracellular ATP via P2X7 receptor. NLRC4 detects bacterial flagellin and type III secretion system components delivered into the cytosol. AIM2 binds cytosolic dsDNA, while pyrin senses inactivation of Rho GTPases by bacterial toxins.

Upon activation, sensor oligomerization nucleates ASC into filamentous specks that recruit pro‑caspase‑1, enabling autoproteolytic activation. Active caspase‑1 cleaves pro‑IL‑1β and pro‑IL‑18 into mature cytokines and cleaves GSDMD at Asp275, releasing the N‑terminal pore‑forming domain that oligomerizes in the plasma membrane. The resulting ~10‑15 nm pores permit cytokine release and osmotic lysis — pyroptosis. Non‑canonical inflammasomes (caspase‑11 in mice, caspase‑4/5 in humans) directly bind cytosolic LPS, cleave GSDMD, and can secondarily trigger NLRP3 via potassium efflux.

Dysregulated inflammasome activity underlies several autoinflammatory syndromes. Cryopyrin‑associated periodic syndromes (CAPS) result from gain‑of‑function NLRP3 mutations (e.g., A352V, D303N) causing constitutive IL‑1β production. Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) stems from MEFV mutations affecting pyrin regulation. Deficiency of IL‑1 receptor antagonist (DIRA) and deficiency of IL‑36 receptor antagonist (DITRA) illustrate downstream cytokine‑specific pathology. Therapeutic IL‑1 blockade (anakinra, canakinumab, rilonacept) dramatically improves clinical outcomes in these disorders, validating the inflammasome‑IL‑1 axis as a druggable target.

Beyond monogenic diseases, NLRP3 inflammasome activation contributes to gout (monosodium urate crystals), atherosclerosis (cholesterol crystals), type 2 diabetes (islet amyloid polypeptide), and neurodegenerative diseases (amyloid‑β, α‑synuclein). Small‑molecule NLRP3 inhibitors (MCC950, OLT1177, dapansutrile) have shown efficacy in murine models and early‑phase human trials. GSDMD inhibitors (disulfiram, necrosulfonamide) block pore formation without affecting cytokine maturation, offering a strategy to uncouple pyroptosis from inflammation. Biomarkers such as circulating ASC specks, caspase‑1 p20, and mature IL‑18 are being evaluated for patient stratification and pharmacodynamic monitoring.

Mechanism‑Based Reasoning
A 12‑year‑old child presents with recurrent fevers, urticaria‑like rash, and sensorineural hearing loss. Genetic testing reveals a heterozygous NLRP3 mutation (p.A352V). Explain the molecular consequence of this mutation and why IL‑1 blockade is effective.
The p.A352V substitution in the NACHT domain of NLRP3 lowers the activation threshold, causing ligand‑independent oligomerization and spontaneous ASC speck formation. This leads to constitutive caspase‑1 activation, excessive cleavage of pro‑IL‑1β to mature IL‑1β, and GSDMD‑mediated pyroptosis. The resulting systemic IL‑1β excess drives fever, rash, and inflammation‑mediated hearing loss. Anakinra (recombinant IL‑1 receptor antagonist) competitively blocks IL‑1β binding to IL‑1R1, interrupting the downstream inflammatory cascade and providing rapid clinical remission.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links a specific mutation to a clinical phenotype, first identify the protein domain affected, then trace the signaling consequence (gain‑of‑function vs loss‑of‑function), and finally match the therapeutic target (upstream sensor, adaptor, cytokine, or receptor). This stepwise mapping avoids conflating NLRP3‑CAPS with pyrin‑FMF or AIM2‑related DNA sensing defects.

CAR‑T Cell Therapy: Engineering, Cytokine Release Syndrome, and Mechanisms of Relapse

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‑cell therapy redirects autologous T lymphocytes against a predefined surface antigen, most notably CD19 on B‑cell malignancies. A CAR construct comprises an extracellular single‑chain variable fragment (scFv) for antigen recognition, a hinge region, a transmembrane domain (typically CD28 or CD8α), and intracellular signaling domains. First‑generation CARs contain only CD3ζ; second‑generation adds one costimulatory domain (CD28 or 4‑1BB); third‑generation incorporates two costimulatory domains. CD28‑based CARs drive rapid effector differentiation and potent early cytotoxicity but exhibit limited persistence. 4‑1BB‑based CARs promote mitochondrial biogenesis, central memory formation, and longer in‑vivo survival, which correlates with durable remissions in chronic lymphocytic leukemia.

Manufacturing involves leukapheresis, T‑cell activation with anti‑CD3/CD28 beads, lentiviral or retroviral transduction, expansion in cytokine‑supplemented media (IL‑2, IL‑7, IL‑15), and cryopreservation. Lymphodepleting chemotherapy (fludarabine + cyclophosphamide) before infusion enhances homeostatic cytokine availability (IL‑7, IL‑15) and reduces regulatory T‑cell competition, improving engraftment and expansion.

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is the most frequent severe toxicity, driven by massive T‑cell activation and subsequent macrophage‑derived IL‑6, IFN‑γ, and TNF‑α. Grading follows the ASTCT consensus: grade 1‑2 (fever, constitutional symptoms) managed with supportive care; grade 3‑4 (hypotension, hypoxia, organ dysfunction) requires tocilizumab (IL‑6R blockade) ± corticosteroids. Neurotoxicity (immune effector cell‑associated neurotoxicity syndrome, ICANS) often coincides with CRS but can occur independently; its pathophysiology involves endothelial activation, blood‑brain barrier disruption, and microglial activation. Early tocilizumab administration does not compromise anti‑tumor efficacy in most studies.

Relapse after CD19 CAR‑T therapy occurs via antigen‑dependent and antigen‑independent mechanisms. Antigen loss (CD19‑negative escape) arises from alternative splicing, exon skipping, or lineage switch. Antigen‑independent relapse involves T‑cell exhaustion (up‑regulation of PD‑1, TIM‑3, LAG‑3), tonic CAR signaling causing premature differentiation, and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment factors (TGF‑β, adenosine). Strategies to overcome relapse include dual‑target CARs (CD19 + CD22), armored CARs secreting IL‑12 or checkpoint‑blocking scFvs, and combination with PD‑1 blockade. Next‑generation platforms explore allogeneic “off‑the‑shelf” CAR‑T cells (TCR‑knockout, HLA‑E overexpression) and CAR‑NK cells to reduce manufacturing time and cost.

Tissue‑Resident Memory T Cells: Generation, Maintenance, and Role in Barrier Immunity

Tissue‑resident memory T cells (TRM) are a non‑circulating lymphocyte subset that permanently occupies non‑lymphoid tissues — skin, lung, gut, genital mucosa, and brain — providing rapid frontline defense against reinfection. TRM differentiation is instructed by local cues during the effector phase: TGF‑β signaling via SMAD2/3 induces expression of the integrin αEβ7 (CD103), which binds E‑cadherin on epithelial cells, anchoring CD8⁺ TRM. CD4⁺ TRM often lack CD103 but express CD69, which antagonizes sphingosine‑1‑phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1) to prevent egress. Transcriptional programs driven by Hobit, Blimp‑1, Runx3, and Notch signaling repress KLF2 and T‑bet, locking cells in a tissue‑adapted state.

Metabolic adaptation distinguishes TRM from circulating memory. TRM rely on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial spare respiratory capacity, supported by exogenous lipid uptake via CD36 and FABP4/5. Autophagy and mitophagy maintain mitochondrial quality. In contrast, circulating central memory T cells (TCM) favor glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation fueled by glucose. This metabolic imprinting is established early; forced glycolysis during priming impairs TRM formation.

TRM provide immediate effector function upon antigen re‑encounter: rapid IFN‑γ, TNF‑α, and granzyme B production, and recruitment of circulating memory and innate cells via chemokines (CXCL9/10, CCL5). In skin, CD8⁺ TRM control herpes simplex virus reactivation; in lung, they mediate heterosubtypic influenza protection; in gut, they limit Salmonella and Clostridioides difficile recurrence. However, dysregulated TRM contribute to pathology: psoriasis (IL‑17‑producing CD8⁺ TRM), vitiligo (autoreactive CD8⁺ TRM targeting melanocytes), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (cytotoxic TRM driving tissue destruction).

Vaccination strategies increasingly aim to generate TRM at relevant portals of entry. Intranasal or aerosolized vaccines induce lung TRM superior to systemic immunization for respiratory pathogens. Prime‑pull regimens — systemic prime followed by local chemokine (CXCL9/10) or antigen boost — recruit circulating effectors into tissues and promote TRM differentiation. Adjuvants that activate tissue‑resident dendritic cells (e.g., TLR3/7/8 agonists) and provide TGF‑β mimetics enhance CD103⁺ TRM formation. Understanding TRM longevity — which can persist for years without replenishment — informs booster scheduling and correlates of protection for mucosal vaccines.

Bispecific Antibodies and Antibody‑Drug Conjugates: Design Principles and Clinical Applications

Bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) simultaneously engage two distinct epitopes, enabling mechanisms unattainable with monospecific IgG. The most clinically advanced format is the T‑cell engager (BiTE), which links an anti‑CD3 scFv to a tumor‑associated antigen scFv (e.g., blinatumomab targeting CD19). This forces immunological synapse formation between cytotoxic T cells and target cells independent of MHC restriction, triggering perforin/granzyme‑mediated killing. Other formats include dual‑variable‑domain IgG (DVD‑IgG), knob‑into‑hole Fc heterodimerization, and CrossMab technologies that preserve Fc‑mediated effector functions and half‑life.

Antibody‑drug conjugates (ADCs) combine monoclonal antibody specificity with cytotoxic payloads via a linker. Three components dictate therapeutic index: (1) target antigen — high, homogeneous expression on tumor cells, low on normal tissues (e.g., HER2, TROP2, CD30); (2) linker — cleavable (valine‑citrulline dipeptide for cathepsin B, acid‑labile hydrazone) or non‑cleavable (thioether) influencing bystander killing; (3) payload — microtubule inhibitors (MMAE, DM1, DM4) or DNA‑damaging agents (calicheamicin, duocarmycin, topoisomerase I inhibitor DXd). Drug‑to‑antibody ratio (DAR) optimization (typically 2‑4 for site‑specific conjugation) balances potency and pharmacokinetics.

Clinical milestones: trastuzumab emtansine (T‑DM1) for HER2⁺ breast cancer; enfortumab vedotin (Nectin‑4 ADC) for urothelial carcinoma; sacituzumab govitecan (TROP2 ADC) for triple‑negative breast cancer; and trastuzumab deruxtecan (HER2 ADC with cleavable linker and high DAR = 8) showing activity in HER2‑low disease. Bispecifics beyond T‑cell engagers include CD3 × BCMA (teclistamab) for multiple myeloma, CD3 × CD20 (mosunetuzumab) for follicular lymphoma, and PD‑1 × LAG‑3 (tebotelimab) to co‑block inhibitory pathways. Emerging formats incorporate conditional activation (protease‑cleavable masks) to restrict activity to the tumor microenvironment, reducing on‑target off‑tumor toxicity.

Resistance mechanisms mirror those of CAR‑T therapy: antigen loss or mutation, downregulation of death receptors, upregulation of anti‑apoptotic proteins (BCL‑2, MCL‑1), and impaired internalization for ADCs. Combination strategies — ADCs with checkpoint inhibitors, bsAbs with cytokine support (IL‑2 variants), or dual‑targeted bsAbs — aim to forestall escape. Pharmacodynamic monitoring includes circulating tumor DNA for antigen status, soluble target levels, and cytokine signatures (IL‑6, IFN‑γ) for CRS risk with T‑cell engagers.

Central and Effector Memory T Cell Differentiation: Transcriptional Control and Functional Heterogeneity

Memory T cells are broadly divided into central memory (TCM) and effector memory (TEM) subsets, each defined by distinct homing receptors, proliferative capacity, and transcriptional programmes. TCM cells express CCR7 and CD62L, enabling lymph‑node recirculation, and retain high IL‑2 production and robust proliferative responses upon antigen re‑encounter. Their differentiation is driven by the transcription factor TCF‑1 (encoded by TCF7), which maintains a stem‑like chromatin state and sustains expression of LEF1 and BCL6. In contrast, TEM cells lack CCR7/CD62L, express CX3CR1 and KLRG1, and display immediate cytotoxic function with high granzyme B and perforin. The transcriptional repressor BLIMP‑1 (PRDM1) and the AP‑1 family member BATF cooperate with IRF4 to enforce the effector programme, while down‑regulating TCF‑1.

Intermediate populations, termed transitional memory (TTM) or stem‑cell‑like memory (TSCM), co‑express CCR7 and CD45RO and retain self‑renewal capacity. TSCM cells are characterised by high CD95, CD122, and CXCR3, and depend on Wnt/β‑catenin signalling together with TCF‑1 for longevity. Chronic antigen exposure, as seen in persistent viral infections (e.g., CMV, HIV) or tumours, drives progressive loss of TCF‑1 and acquisition of an exhausted phenotype marked by TOX, NR4A, and sustained PD‑1 expression. Notably, TOX is required for the epigenetic remodelling that locks cells into exhaustion, whereas its absence preserves a TCM-like state even under chronic stimulation.

Cytokine milieu fine‑tunes these fates: IL‑12 and type I IFNs promote TEM differentiation via STAT4 and T‑bet, while IL‑7 and IL‑15 support homeostatic proliferation of both TCM and TEM through STAT5. IL‑21, produced by follicular helper T cells, enhances B‑cell help and sustains TCM formation. Therapeutically, vaccines that generate abundant TSCM (e.g., adenoviral vectors with IL‑15 co‑expression) correlate with durable protection, whereas CAR‑T products enriched for TCM display superior persistence in vivo.

B Cell Subset Diversity: Marginal Zone, B‑1, and Age‑Associated B Cells in Health and Disease

Beyond conventional follicular (FO) B cells, the spleen and peritoneal cavity harbour specialised subsets with distinct developmental origins and functional niches. Marginal zone (MZ) B cells reside in the splenic marginal sinus, express high CD21/CD35, IgMhiIgDlo, and the transcription factor NOTCH2‑dependent KLF2. They respond rapidly to blood‑borne polysaccharide antigens in a T‑independent manner, producing early IgM and class‑switched IgG via TLR‑mediated activation. MZ B cells require BAFF‑R signalling for survival and are depleted in BAFF‑deficient mice.

B‑1 cells, subdivided into B‑1a (CD5+) and B‑1b (CD5−), originate from fetal liver progenitors and self‑renew in the peritoneal and pleural cavities. They generate natural IgM that provides first‑line defence against encapsulated bacteria and contributes to homeostatic clearance of apoptotic cells. B‑1a cells are the principal source of phosphatidylcholine‑specific IgM (T15 idiotype) and are implicated in atheroprotection. Their development is driven by the transcription factor PLZF (ZBTB16) and requires BCR signalling of low affinity for self‑antigens.

Age‑associated B cells (ABCs) accumulate in elderly individuals and in autoimmune settings such as systemic lupus erythematosus. Phenotypically they are CD11c+T‑bet+CD21−CD23− and arise from FO B cells under chronic TLR7/9 stimulation combined with IFN‑γ signalling. ABCs are potent producers of autoantibodies (e.g., anti‑dsDNA) and secrete pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑6). Their expansion is driven by the transcription factor T‑bet (TBX21) and the epigenetic regulator EZH2. Targeting the TLR7‑MyD88 axis or depleting CD11c+ B cells ameliorates disease in murine lupus models, highlighting therapeutic relevance.

Conceptual Integration Question
A 65‑year‑old patient presents with high‑titer anti‑dsDNA antibodies, low complement C3/C4, and an expanded CD11c+T‑bet+ B‑cell population in peripheral blood. Which B‑cell subset is most likely expanded, and which transcription factor–cytokine axis drives its differentiation?
The expanded subset is age‑associated B cells (ABCs). Their differentiation is driven by chronic TLR7/9 engagement together with IFN‑γ signalling, leading to STAT1‑dependent induction of the transcription factor T‑bet (TBX21). T‑bet then programmes the CD11c+CD21− phenotype and promotes autoantibody class‑switching to IgG2a/c. Therefore, the key axis is IFN‑γ → STAT1 → T‑bet.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question describes an expanded CD11c+T‑bet+ B‑cell population in autoimmunity or ageing, immediately map it to ABCs and recall the IFN‑γ/STAT1/T‑bet axis. Pair this with the knowledge that MZ B cells are NOTCH2‑dependent and B‑1 cells are PLZF‑dependent to discriminate subsets quickly.

RNA Sensing Pathways: RIG‑I, MDA5, and TLR3/7/8 in Antiviral Immunity and Autoimmunity

Cytosolic RNA sensors RIG‑I (DDX58) and MDA5 (IFIH1) detect distinct viral RNA signatures. RIG‑I preferentially binds 5′‑triphosphate blunt‑ended dsRNA and short dsRNA (<1 kb), typical of many negative‑sense RNA viruses (influenza, RSV, VSV). MDA5 recognises long dsRNA (>2 kb) generated during replication of positive‑sense RNA viruses (picornaviruses, flaviviruses) and certain dsRNA viruses. Both sensors contain N‑terminal CARD domains that, upon ligand binding, undergo K63‑linked ubiquitination by TRIM25 (RIG‑I) or RIPLET (MDA5), enabling interaction with the mitochondrial adaptor MAVS (IPS‑1). MAVS polymerisation recruits TBK1 and IKKε, phosphorylating IRF3/7 and driving type I IFN transcription.

Endosomal TLRs complement cytosolic sensing: TLR3 detects dsRNA, TLR7 recognises ssRNA rich in guanosine/uridine, and TLR8 (human) senses similar motifs. TLR3 signals via TRIF, while TLR7/8 signal through MyD88, both converging on IRF7 (plasmacytoid dendritic cells) or NF‑κB (myeloid cells). Genetic deficiencies illustrate non‑redundant roles: IFIH1 loss‑of‑function predisposes to severe enterovirus infections, whereas DDX58 mutations cause Singleton‑Merten syndrome with auto‑inflammatory features due to constitutive IFN signalling.

Autoimmunity arises when self‑RNA gains access to these sensors. In systemic lupus erythematosus, immune complexes containing U1‑snRNA engage TLR7 in plasmacytoid DCs, driving pathogenic IFN‑α production. Gain‑of‑function TLR7 variants (e.g., Y264H) cause severe early‑onset lupus. Similarly, mutations in RNASEH2 or TREX1 impair cytosolic RNA/DNA clearance, leading to Aicardi‑Goutières syndrome with chronic MAVS‑dependent IFN activation. Therapeutic strategies include TLR7/8 antagonists (e.g., enpatoran) and JAK inhibitors that block downstream IFN signalling.

Immune Checkpoint Molecules Beyond PD‑1/CTLA‑4: LAG‑3, TIM‑3, TIGIT, VISTA and Clinical Targeting

While PD‑1 and CTLA‑4 dominate checkpoint blockade, co‑inhibitory receptors LAG‑3 (CD223), TIM‑3 (HAVCR2), TIGIT (VSIG9), and VISTA (VSIR) shape T‑cell exhaustion and regulatory networks. LAG‑3 binds MHC‑II with higher affinity than CD4, delivering inhibitory signals via its KIEELE motif that recruits phosphatases (SHP‑1/2) and disrupts TCR signalling. LAG‑3 is co‑expressed with PD‑1 on exhausted CD8+ T cells in chronic infection and cancer; dual blockade (relatlimab + nivolumab) improves progression‑free survival in melanoma.

TIM‑3 recognises phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cells, galectin‑9, HMGB1, and CEACAM1. Its cytoplasmic tail contains tyrosine residues that, when phosphorylated, recruit BAT3 (HLA‑B‑associated transcript 3) in resting cells, but in exhausted cells BAT3 is displaced, allowing Fyn‑mediated inhibition of TCR signalling. TIM‑3 is also expressed on dendritic cells and myeloid‑derived suppressor cells, broadening its immunosuppressive reach. Anti‑TIM‑3 antibodies (e.g., sabatolimab) are in trials combined with PD‑1 blockade for AML and solid tumours.

TIGIT competes with CD226 (DNAM‑1) for binding to CD155 (PVR) and CD112 on antigen‑presenting cells. TIGIT engagement recruits SHIP1, dampening PI3K‑AKT signalling and reducing cytokine production. TIGIT+ NK cells and T cells exhibit reduced cytotoxicity. Tiragolumab (anti‑TIGIT) plus atezolizumab (anti‑PD‑L1) showed activity in NSCLC with high TIGIT expression. VISTA, predominantly expressed on myeloid cells and naïve T cells, maintains quiescence via a yet‑undefined receptor (possibly PSGL‑1). VISTA blockade (e.g., CA‑170) reverses myeloid‑mediated suppression and synergises with PD‑1 inhibition in pre‑clinical models.

CRISPR‑Engineered Immune Cells: Universal CAR‑T, TCR Editing, and Safety Switches

Genome editing enables creation of “off‑the‑shelf” allogeneic CAR‑T products by disrupting endogenous TCR (TRAC) and HLA‑class I (B2M) loci, preventing graft‑versus‑host disease and host rejection. Multiplexed CRISPR‑Cas9 ribonucleoprotein electroporation achieves >90 % biallelic knockout efficiency in primary human T cells. Simultaneous insertion of a CAR cassette into the TRAC locus via homology‑directed repair yields uniform CAR expression under endogenous TCR promoter control, reducing tonic signalling and exhaustion.

TCR editing replaces the native TCR with a defined tumour‑specific TCR (e.g., NY‑ESO‑1, MART‑1) while preserving physiological regulation. CRISPR‑mediated knock‑in at the TRAC locus ensures single‑copy integration and avoids mispairing with endogenous chains. Clinical trials (e.g., NCT03399448) demonstrate feasible manufacturing and persistence of TCR‑edited T cells in metastatic melanoma.

Safety switches mitigate cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity. Inducible caspase‑9 (iCasp9) fused to a drug‑binding domain (FKBP12‑F36V) triggers apoptosis upon administration of the dimerising agent rimiducid (AP1903). Alternatively, truncated EGFR (EGFRt) or CD20 epitopes allow antibody‑mediated depletion (cetuximab, rituximab). Emerging “logic‑gated” circuits use synthetic Notch receptors to restrict CAR activation to dual‑antigen recognition, enhancing tumour selectivity. Off‑target editing assessment employs GUIDE‑seq and DISCOVER‑seq; clinical products must meet <0.1 % off‑target indel frequency at predicted sites.
