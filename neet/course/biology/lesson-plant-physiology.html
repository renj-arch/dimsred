1. Transport in Plants

Transport in plants involves the movement of water, minerals, and organic nutrients over both short distances (cell-to-cell) and long distances (through vascular tissues). Unlike animals, plants lack a circulatory pump; instead, they rely on physical forces such as diffusion, osmosis, transpiration pull, and root pressure to move substances throughout the plant body.

Water Potential

Water potential (Ψw) is a measure of the free energy of water per unit volume. It determines the direction of water movement: water always moves from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential. The concept was introduced by Slatyer and Taylor and is fundamental to understanding water relations in plants.

Ψw = Ψs + Ψp + Ψg   (Ψg is usually negligible in plant cells)

Components of water potential:

  • Solute potential (Ψs): Also called osmotic potential. Always negative. The presence of solutes lowers the free energy of water. Pure water at standard conditions has Ψs = 0.
  • Pressure potential (Ψp): Can be positive (turgor pressure in a turgid cell) or negative (tension in xylem vessels).
  • Matrix potential (Ψm): Due to adsorption of water to colloidal surfaces. Important in cell walls and dry seeds.

When a plant cell is placed in a solution, water movement depends on the relative water potentials. In a hypotonic solution (higher Ψw outside), water enters the cell, causing it to become turgid. In a hypertonic solution (lower Ψw outside), water leaves the cell, causing plasmolysis.

Absorption of Water

Water absorption occurs primarily through root hairs, which are epidermal extensions that greatly increase the surface area for absorption. The pathway of water from root hairs to the xylem can be apoplastic (through cell walls and intercellular spaces) or symplastic (through the cytoplasm via plasmodesmata). The Casparian strip in the endodermis blocks apoplastic flow, forcing water into the symplastic pathway for selective mineral uptake.

Root Pressure

Root pressure is a positive pressure generated in the xylem of roots due to the active absorption of minerals and the subsequent osmotic entry of water. It is most evident at night when transpiration is low and humidity is high. The phenomenon of guttation — the exudation of water droplets from leaf margins through hydathodes — is a direct manifestation of root pressure. Root pressure alone cannot explain water movement in tall trees exceeding 20–30 metres.

Transpiration Pull

The transpiration pull is the primary force driving water movement in plants during the day. The Cohesion-Tension Theory (Dixon & Joly, 1894) explains water ascent: water evaporates from leaf mesophyll cells, creating tension that pulls the continuous water column upward through xylem vessels. Cohesion (H-bonding between water molecules) and adhesion (attraction to xylem walls) maintain the integrity of the water column under tension.

Ascent of Sap

The ascent of sap refers to the upward movement of water and dissolved minerals through the xylem. The Cohesion-Tension Theory is the most widely accepted explanation. Experimental evidence includes:

  • Stem girdling: Removing phloem does not stop water flow, proving xylem is the conducting tissue.
  • Dye experiments: Coloured solutions like eosin rise through the xylem and can be traced visually.
  • Pressure bomb: Scholander’s pressure bomb measures xylem tension (negative pressure) directly.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from aerial plant parts. It is described as a necessary evil because it drives water and mineral transport but can cause water deficit. Types include stomatal (80–90%, through stomata), cuticular (5–10%, through the cuticle), and lenticular (1–2%, through lenticels).

Stomatal Mechanism

Guard cells regulate stomatal opening and closing through changes in turgor pressure. K+ influx into guard cells (driven by H+-ATPase proton pumps) lowers water potential, causing water entry and stomatal opening. K+ efflux reverses the process. ABA triggers Ca2+ signalling that activates K+ efflux channels, closing stomata during water stress. Blue light receptors (phototropins) activate the H+-ATPase to initiate opening.

Phloem Transport

Phloem translocates organic solutes (primarily sucrose) from sources (net producers like mature leaves) to sinks (net consumers like roots, fruits, and young leaves). Sieve tube elements (living but enucleate) and companion cells (with nuclei and ribosomes) form the functional unit of phloem.

Pressure Flow Hypothesis

Proposed by Ernst Münch (1930), this hypothesis explains phloem transport: (1) Sucrose is actively loaded into sieve tubes at the source, lowering water potential. (2) Water enters from xylem by osmosis, creating high hydrostatic pressure. (3) Sucrose is unloaded at the sink, raising water potential. (4) Water leaves, creating low pressure. (5) The pressure gradient drives bulk flow from source to sink. Phloem sap typically contains 10–25% sucrose and can flow at 1–2 m h−1.

Example 1 — Water Potential Calculation
A plant cell has a solute potential (Ψs) of −0.8 MPa and a pressure potential (Ψp) of +0.4 MPa. What is its water potential (Ψw)? In which direction will water flow when this cell is placed in a solution with Ψw = −0.2 MPa?
Solution: Ψw = Ψs + Ψp = (−0.8) + (+0.4) = −0.4 MPa. Water moves from higher to lower water potential, so water moves from the external solution (−0.2 MPa) into the cell (−0.4 MPa). The cell will become turgid.
Example 2 — Plasmolysis Identification
A student places onion epidermal cells in a sugar solution and observes the protoplast pulling away from the cell wall. Name this phenomenon and explain the water potential changes.
Solution: This is plasmolysis. The external solution is hypertonic (lower Ψw than the cell). Water leaves the cell by exosmosis down the water potential gradient. The protoplast shrinks, and pressure potential drops to zero. Plasmolysis confirms the cell is living; dead cells do not undergo plasmolysis.
NEET Shortcut — Stomatal Opening Sequence
Remember the sequence: Blue light → Phototropin → H+-ATPase → H+ efflux → Hyperpolarisation → K+ influx via inward rectifier K+ channels → Water enters guard cells → Stoma opens. For closing: ABA → Ca2+ influx → K+ efflux → Water leaves → Stoma closes.

2. Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants convert light energy into chemical energy. The overall equation is:

6CO2 + 12H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O   (ΔG = +2870 kJ mol−1)

The process occurs in two phases: light reactions (thylakoid membranes, require light) and dark reactions (stroma, do not directly require light). The organelle is the chloroplast, which has a double membrane envelope, thylakoid system (grana and stroma lamellae), and stroma.

Pigments

Pigments absorb specific wavelengths of light. The absorption spectrum shows which wavelengths a pigment absorbs; the action spectrum shows which wavelengths drive the highest rate of photosynthesis.

  • Chlorophyll a: Primary pigment (blue-green). Reaction centre in both PS I and PS II. Peaks at 430 nm (blue) and 662 nm (red).
  • Chlorophyll b: Accessory pigment (yellow-green). Absorbs at 453 nm and 642 nm. Transfers energy to chlorophyll a.
  • Carotenoids: β-carotene (orange) and xanthophylls (yellow). Absorb blue light (400–500 nm). Protect against photooxidation by quenching triplet chlorophyll and scavenging ROS.
  • Phycobilins: Phycoerythrin and phycocyanin in cyanobacteria and red algae. Absorb green/yellow light for deep-water survival.

Light Reactions

Light reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes. They produce ATP, NADPH, and O2 (from water photolysis).

Photosystems

Each photosystem comprises a light-harvesting complex (200–300 pigment molecules) and a reaction centre (special pair of chlorophyll a molecules):

  • Photosystem II (P680): Located in granal thylakoids. Involved in water photolysis and initiating electron flow. The oxygen-evolving complex (OEC) contains Mn2+, Ca2+, and Cl−.
  • Photosystem I (P700): Located in stromal thylakoids and grana margins. Involved in NADP+ reduction via ferredoxin.

Electron Transport

The Z-scheme describes non-cyclic electron flow:

  1. Light excites P680 in PS II, ejecting an electron to pheophytin (primary acceptor).
  2. The electron passes through plastoquinone (PQ), the cytochrome b6f complex (proton pump), and plastocyanin (PC) to PS I.
  3. Photolysis of water replaces the electron lost by PS II: 2H2O → 4H+ + 4e− + O2.
  4. Light excites P700 in PS I, ejecting another electron that passes through ferredoxin (Fd) and reduces NADP+ to NADPH via ferredoxin-NADP+ reductase (FNR).

Photophosphorylation:

  • Non-cyclic: Involves both PS II and PS I. Linear flow. Produces ATP + NADPH + O2.
  • Cyclic: Involves only PS I. Electrons cycle back from ferredoxin to PQ, generating ATP only. No NADPH or O2.

ATP is synthesised by chemiosmosis (Peter Mitchell, Nobel 1978): the proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane drives ATP synthase (CF0-CF1 complex).

Dark Reactions

Dark reactions (Calvin cycle) use ATP and NADPH to fix CO2 into carbohydrates. They occur in the stroma of the chloroplast.

Calvin Cycle

Discovered by Melvin Calvin (Nobel 1961). Three phases:

  1. Carboxylation: CO2 + RuBP (5C) → 2 × 3-PGA (3C). Catalysed by RuBisCO — the most abundant protein on Earth (30–50% of leaf protein).
  2. Reduction: 3-PGA is phosphorylated (ATP) and reduced (NADPH) to form glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P).
  3. Regeneration: 5 out of 6 G3P molecules are used to regenerate RuBP, consuming ATP.
6 CO2 + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH → 1 Glucose (C6H12O6) + 18 ADP + 12 NADP+

C4 Pathway

The Hatch-Slack pathway is an adaptation in plants growing in hot, dry environments (maize, sugarcane, sorghum). Key features:

  • Kranz anatomy: Bundle sheath cells arranged in a wreath-like pattern around vascular bundles.
  • In mesophyll cells: CO2 + PEP (3C) → OAA (4C) via PEP carboxylase (high affinity for CO2, no oxygenase activity).
  • OAA is converted to malate or aspartate and transported to bundle sheath cells.
  • In bundle sheath cells: Malate is decarboxylated, releasing CO2 for the Calvin cycle.
  • Pyruvate returns to mesophyll cells and is regenerated to PEP using ATP.

Advantage: Concentrates CO2 in bundle sheath cells, suppressing photorespiration. C4 plants have higher water-use efficiency and higher optimal temperature (30–45 °C).

CAM Pathway

Crassulacean Acid Metabolism is found in succulent plants (cacti, Agave, Kalanchoe, pineapple). It features temporal separation of carbon fixation:

  • Night (stomata open): CO2 is fixed by PEP carboxylase into OAA, then reduced to malate and stored in the vacuole.
  • Day (stomata closed): Malate is decarboxylated, and CO2 enters the Calvin cycle. This minimises water loss.

Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Blackman’s Law of Limiting Factors (1905): When a process is affected by multiple factors, its rate is limited by the factor in shortest supply.

  • Light intensity: Increases linearly to a saturation point; very high light causes photoinhibition.
  • CO2 concentration: Rate increases up to saturation (~1,000 ppm for C3, higher for C4).
  • Temperature: C3 optimum 20–30 °C; C4 optimum 30–45 °C. Above optimum, photorespiration increases in C3.
  • Water: Stress closes stomata, reducing CO2 uptake.
  • Oxygen: High O2 promotes photorespiration in C3 plants (Warburg effect).

A plant has optimum temperature 38 °C, OAA as the first stable product, and bundle sheath cells arranged around vascular bundles. Classify and name the carbon fixation pathway.
Solution: C4 plant using the Hatch-Slack pathway. Indicators: (1) high optimum temperature (38 °C), (2) OAA as first stable product (4C compound), (3) Kranz anatomy. Examples include maize, sugarcane, sorghum, and amaranthus.

3. Respiration in Plants

Respiration is the oxidative breakdown of organic compounds to release ATP. It occurs continuously (day and night) in all living cells. The overall equation is the reverse of photosynthesis:

C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + 36–38 ATP

Respiration has three main stages: glycolysis (cytoplasm), Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix), and electron transport chain (inner mitochondrial membrane).

Glycolysis

The Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. It is common to both aerobic and anaerobic respiration.

  • Glucose (6C) is phosphorylated twice (using 2 ATP) to form fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.
  • The 6C sugar is split into two 3C molecules: DHAP and G3P (interconvertible).
  • G3P is oxidised (NAD+ → NADH) and substrate-level phosphorylation yields ATP.
  • Net products per glucose: 2 Pyruvate + 2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH.

Krebs Cycle

The TCA cycle (citric acid cycle) takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. Before entering the cycle, pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (releasing 1 CO2 and producing 1 NADH per pyruvate).

  • Acetyl-CoA (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C).
  • Through a series of reactions: citrate → α-ketoglutarate → succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate.
  • Products per turn: 3 NADH + 1 FADH2 + 1 GTP + 2 CO2.
  • Per glucose: 6 NADH + 2 FADH2 + 2 GTP.

Electron Transport Chain

The ETC is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane and consists of four protein complexes (I–IV) plus ATP synthase (Complex V).

  • Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase): Accepts e− from NADH, pumps 4 H+.
  • Complex II (Succinate dehydrogenase): Accepts e− from FADH2, does not pump H+.
  • Complex III (Cytochrome bc1): Pumps 4 H+.
  • Complex IV (Cytochrome c oxidase): Transfers e− to O2 (forming H2O), pumps 2 H+.
  • ATP synthase (Complex V): Uses the proton gradient to synthesise ATP by chemiosmosis.

ATP yield: Each NADH produces ~2.5 ATP; each FADH2 produces ~1.5 ATP. Total: ~30–32 ATP per glucose.

Fermentation

Fermentation is the anaerobic pathway that allows glycolysis to continue when O2 is unavailable (regenerates NAD+). Only 2 ATP per glucose are produced.

  • Alcoholic fermentation (yeast, some plant roots): Pyruvate → Acetaldehyde + CO2 → Ethanol. Enzyme: alcohol dehydrogenase.
  • Lactic acid fermentation (animals, some bacteria): Pyruvate → Lactate. Enzyme: lactate dehydrogenase.

Respiratory Quotient

RQ = CO2 released / O2 consumed
  • Carbohydrates: RQ = 1.0 (C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O)
  • Fats: RQ ~ 0.7 (require more O2 per CO2 released)
  • Proteins: RQ ~ 0.8–0.9
  • Organic acids: RQ > 1 (e.g., oxalic acid RQ = 4)
  • Anaerobic: RQ = ∞ (no O2 consumed)
Example 4 — RQ Calculation
A respiring tissue releases 60 mL of CO2 and consumes 75 mL of O2 in 20 minutes. Calculate RQ and identify the probable substrate.
Solution: RQ = 60 / 75 = 0.8. An RQ of 0.8 is characteristic of protein respiration. Carbohydrates give RQ = 1.0, fats give RQ ~ 0.7. The tissue is likely respiring a mixed substrate or proteins.
Quick Recall — RQ Values
RQ at a glance: Carbs = 1.0 (perfect ratio), Fats = 0.7 (more O2), Organic acids > 1 (less O2), Anaerobic = ∞ (no O2 consumed). In germinating fatty seeds (e.g., groundnut), RQ starts below 1 and approaches 1.0 as stored fats are converted to carbohydrates.

4. Plant Growth & Development

Plant growth is an irreversible increase in size, volume, and weight. Development includes all changes from germination to senescence.

Phases of Growth

  • Meristematic phase: Cells in apical and lateral meristems divide actively. Rich in protoplasm, large nuclei, thin cell walls.
  • Elongation phase: Cells enlarge and elongate. Vacuoles form and coalesce. Most increase in plant size occurs here.
  • Maturation phase: Cells differentiate into specialised types (xylem, phloem, fibres, etc.).

Growth rates: Arithmetic (one daughter cell continues dividing — root elongation) and Geometric (both daughters divide — exponential, J-shaped). The grand period of growth follows an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve. Growth requires water, oxygen, nutrients, light, and appropriate temperature.

Plant Hormones

Phytohormones are organic compounds produced in low concentrations that regulate physiological processes. They are classified as promoters (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins) and inhibitors (ABA, ethylene).

Auxins

IAA is the most common natural auxin. It is synthesised in shoot tips and young leaves and transported basipetally via polar transport. Key effects: cell elongation (acid-growth hypothesis: H+-ATPase activation loosens cell walls), apical dominance (apex suppresses lateral buds), phototropism (asymmetric auxin distribution causes bending), and adventitious root formation. Synthetic auxin 2,4-D is used as a selective herbicide against dicot weeds.

Gibberellins

GAs are diterpenoid acids (GA3 is gibberellic acid). They stimulate stem elongation (subapical meristem), bolting in rosette plants, seed germination (induction of α-amylase in barley aleurone cells — used in malting), and fruit growth (parthenocarpic fruits like seedless grapes). Dwarf mutants often lack functional GA synthesis.

Cytokinins

Cytokinins (zeatin, kinetin) are adenine derivatives synthesised in root tips. They promote cell division (synergistically with auxins), shoot initiation in tissue culture, delay senescence (Richmond-Lang effect), and mobilise nutrients. The auxin:cytokinin ratio determines root vs shoot formation in callus culture.

ABA

Abscisic acid is a sesquiterpenoid (15C) from the carotenoid pathway. It is the stress hormone. Functions: induces stomatal closure via Ca2+-dependent K+ efflux, promotes seed dormancy (ABA:GA ratio determines dormancy vs germination), induces desiccation tolerance proteins (LEA proteins), and promotes abscission.

Ethylene

Ethylene is a gaseous hormone synthesised from methionine (Yang cycle). Key effects: fruit ripening (climacteric rise, starch → sugar conversion), senescence and abscission of leaves, flowers, and fruits, epinasty (downward leaf bending), and the triple response in etiolated seedlings (inhibited stem elongation, stem thickening, horizontal growth). Commercially, ethephon is used to release ethylene for uniform fruit ripening.

Example 5 — Hormone Identification
Waterlogged plants develop aerenchyma and adventitious roots. Ethylene application produces similar effects. Which hormone mediates this response and what is its adaptive significance?
Solution: Ethylene mediates aerenchyma formation in waterlogged roots. Ethylene accumulation triggers programmed cell death, creating air-filled channels (aerenchyma) that facilitate O2 diffusion to submerged roots. This adaptation allows survival under waterlogged conditions by maintaining aerobic respiration in roots.

Photoperiodism

Photoperiodism is the response of plants to relative lengths of day and night. The photoperiodic stimulus is perceived by leaves, and a transmissible signal — florigen (the FT protein) — moves to the shoot apex to induce flowering.

  • Short-day plants (SDP): Flower when day length is shorter than a critical period (e.g., Chrysanthemum, Soybean, Rice, Tobacco). They actually require a continuous dark period longer than a critical length. A flash of light during the dark period prevents flowering.
  • Long-day plants (LDP): Flower when day length exceeds a critical period (e.g., Wheat, Spinach, Radish, Sugar Beet). A long night inhibits flowering.
  • Day-neutral plants (DNP): Flowering is unaffected by day length (e.g., Tomato, Sunflower, Cucumber, Maize). They flower when they reach a certain maturity.

The phytochrome system detects photoperiods. Phytochrome exists in two forms: Pr (inactive, absorbs red light at 660 nm) and Pfr (active, absorbs far-red light at 730 nm). Red light converts Pr → Pfr; far-red light converts Pfr → Pr. The ratio of Pfr/Pr determines the flowering response.

Vernalisation

Vernalisation is the induction of flowering by prolonged cold exposure (0–5 °C for several weeks). It is perceived by the shoot apical meristem (not leaves). Cold treatment induces epigenetic silencing of the flowering repressor FLC (FLOWERING LOCUS C) through histone modification (H3K27me3). This allows the expression of FT (florigen) under favourable (spring) conditions. Examples: winter wheat, sugar beet, cabbage, and many biennials.

Key Comparison
Photoperiodism vs Vernalisation: Photoperiodism involves light perceived by leaves via phytochrome (Pfr active form). Vernalisation involves cold perceived by the shoot apex via epigenetic silencing of FLC. Both ultimately regulate FT expression and flowering.

5. Mineral Nutrition

Mineral nutrition deals with the uptake and functions of essential mineral elements. Arnon and Stout’s criteria (1939): An element is essential if (1) it is necessary for the plant to complete its life cycle, (2) its function cannot be replaced by another element, and (3) it is directly involved in plant metabolism.

Essential Elements

Macronutrients (required in large amounts, >10 mmol kg−1 dry matter): C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S. Micronutrients (trace amounts, ≤10 mmol kg−1): Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, Mo, B, Cl, Ni.

Deficiency Symptoms

Deficiency symptoms depend on element mobility. Mobile elements (N, P, K, Mg, Zn, Mo) are translocated from older to younger tissues; deficiency appears first in older leaves. Immobile elements (Ca, B, Fe, S, Cu) cannot be readily retranslocated; deficiency appears in younger leaves and growing points.

  • Chlorosis: N, Mg, Fe, Mn, S deficiency
  • Necrosis: Ca, K, Mg, Cu deficiency
  • Stunted growth: N, P, K, Zn, Ca deficiency
  • Purple pigmentation: P deficiency (anthocyanin accumulation)
  • Toxicity: Excess of one element can induce deficiency of another (e.g., excess Mn induces Fe deficiency)
Memory Aid — Mobile vs Immobile
Mobile elements (deficiency in older leaves): "N-P-K-Mg-Mo" — Not-Particularly-Keen-Moving-Molybdenum. Immobile elements (deficiency in young leaves): "Ca-B-Fe-S" — Calcium Boron Iron Sulphur are not moving. Remember: mobile elements are recycled; immobile elements are not.

Nitrogen Metabolism

Nitrogen is the most limiting nutrient. Although the atmosphere is 78% N2, plants cannot use it directly; it must be fixed into NH3 or NO3−.

Nitrogen Fixation

Biological nitrogen fixation is carried out by prokaryotes possessing the nitrogenase enzyme:

  • Symbiotic: Rhizobium in legume root nodules. Bacteria enter via infection threads, differentiate into bacteroids surrounded by the peribacteroid membrane. Leghaemoglobin (pink) maintains low O2 for O2-sensitive nitrogenase.
  • Free-living: Azotobacter (aerobic), Clostridium (anaerobic), Anabaena (cyanobacteria, used as biofertiliser with Azolla in rice paddies).
N2 + 8H+ + 8e− + 16 ATP → 2NH3 + H2 + 16 ADP + 16 Pi

Nitrate assimilation: NO3− → NO2− (nitrate reductase, cytoplasm) → NH4+ (nitrite reductase, chloroplast). Ammonia is incorporated into amino acids via the GS-GOGAT pathway.

Nitrification: NH3 → NO2− (Nitrosomonas) → NO3− (Nitrobacter). Denitrification: NO3− → N2 (Pseudomonas) — returns nitrogen to the atmosphere.

Example 6 — Leghaemoglobin Role
Why is leghaemoglobin essential for nitrogen fixation? What colour change occurs when a nodule is cut and exposed to air?
Solution: Leghaemoglobin maintains microaerophilic conditions in the nodule — low enough O2 to protect O2-sensitive nitrogenase but enough O2 to support bacterial respiration. It is pink when functional (oxygenated). When exposed to air, it oxidises and turns brown.
Example 7 — Deficiency Analysis
A plant shows interveinal chlorosis in young leaves. Which elements are likely deficient? How would you differentiate between them?
Solution: Interveinal chlorosis in young leaves indicates deficiency of an immobile element — most likely Fe or Mn. Fe deficiency shows fine green veins, while Mn deficiency produces grey/white necrotic spots. Fe deficiency is common in alkaline soils; Mn deficiency in organic/sandy soils. A foliar spray of Fe-EDTA restores green colour in Fe deficiency.

Photorespiration: Mechanism, Glycolate Pathway, and Metabolic Cost

Photorespiration is a light‑dependent process that competes with the Calvin cycle for ribulose‑1,5‑bisphosphate (RuBP) when the oxygenase activity of Rubisco dominates. At atmospheric O₂ concentrations (≈21 %) and typical leaf temperatures, Rubisco fixes O₂ to RuBP, producing one molecule of 3‑phosphoglycerate (3‑PGA) and one molecule of 2‑phosphoglycolate (2‑PG). The latter is a dead‑end metabolite that must be salvaged through the glycolate pathway, which spans three subcellular compartments: the chloroplast, the peroxisome, and the mitochondrion. In the chloroplast, 2‑PG is dephosphorylated to glycolate. Glycolate then diffuses to the peroxisome where glycolate oxidase converts it to glyoxylate, releasing H₂O₂ that is scavenged by catalase. Glyoxylate is subsequently transaminated to glycine. Two glycine molecules enter the mitochondrion, where the glycine decarboxylase complex releases CO₂, NH₃, and a one‑carbon unit transferred to tetrahydrofolate, forming serine. Serine returns to the peroxisome, is transaminated to hydroxypyruvate, reduced to glycerate, and finally phosphorylated in the chloroplast to re‑enter the Calvin cycle as 3‑PGA. The net stoichiometry of the pathway consumes 3.5 ATP and 2 NAD(P)H per two oxygenation events, and releases one CO₂ and one NH₃ that must be reassimilated via the GS‑GOGAT cycle, adding further energetic cost. Environmental factors that raise the O₂/CO₂ ratio at the Rubisco active site — high temperature, low intercellular CO₂, high light intensity — increase the oxygenation/carboxylation ratio (Vo/Vc). C₄ and CAM plants suppress photorespiration by concentrating CO₂ around Rubisco: C₄ plants use a CO₂‑pumping cycle (PEP carboxylase in mesophyll, decarboxylation in bundle‑sheath), while CAM plants temporally separate CO₂ uptake (night) from Calvin cycle operation (day). In C₃ plants, genetic engineering of a synthetic glycolate bypass that converts glycolate directly to glycerate in the chloroplast has been shown to reduce the ATP/NADPH penalty and improve biomass under field conditions.

The regulation of photorespiratory flux involves several metabolic checkpoints. The activity of glycolate oxidase is modulated by the redox state of the peroxisomal NAD⁺/NADH pool, while the mitochondrial glycine decarboxylase complex is allosterically activated by its substrate glycine and inhibited by high NADH/NAD⁺ ratios. Additionally, the expression of key photorespiratory genes (GOX, GDH, SHMT) is up‑regulated by high light and low CO₂ through retrograde signalling from the chloroplast. Mutants lacking functional glycolate oxidase (e.g., Arabidopsis gox1 gox2 double mutants) accumulate glycolate, exhibit severe growth retardation in ambient air, but grow normally under elevated CO₂, confirming the essential nature of the pathway under normal atmospheric conditions. The energetic burden of photorespiration is a major factor limiting the quantum yield of C₃ photosynthesis, especially under future climate scenarios with higher temperatures and more frequent drought‑induced stomatal closure.

From an applied perspective, breeding for reduced photorespiratory loss targets either Rubisco specificity (increasing the carboxylation/oxygenation ratio) or the efficiency of the glycolate pathway. Natural variation in Rubisco specificity factor (Sc/o) exists among species; for example, Rubisco from the red alga Galdieria partita shows a Sc/o ≈ 120, compared with ≈ 80 for typical C₃ crop Rubisco. However, higher specificity often correlates with slower catalytic turnover (kcat), creating a trade‑off. Synthetic biology approaches that install a bacterial glycolate dehydrogenase (GlcD) in the chloroplast, converting glycolate directly to glycerate without CO₂ release, have demonstrated up to 40 % increase in photosynthetic efficiency in model plants. These strategies illustrate how a deep mechanistic understanding of photorespiration translates into concrete crop improvement targets.

In summary, photorespiration is not merely a wasteful side reaction but a metabolically integrated pathway that recycles carbon and nitrogen at a significant energetic cost. Its magnitude is governed by the kinetic properties of Rubisco, the leaf gas‑exchange environment, and the capacity of the glycolate pathway enzymes. Manipulating any of these nodes — Rubisco kinetics, CO₂ concentration mechanisms, or glycolate bypasses — offers a route to enhance photosynthetic productivity in C₃ crops.

Photoinhibition and Photoprotection: D1 Turnover, Xanthophyll Cycle, and Non‑Photochemical Quenching

Photoinhibition refers to the light‑induced decline in photosynthetic quantum efficiency, primarily caused by irreversible damage to the D1 protein of photosystem II (PSII). Under excess photon flux, the rate of charge separation in PSII exceeds the capacity of downstream electron acceptors, leading to the accumulation of the primary quinone acceptor QA⁻ and the formation of the long‑lived chlorophyll triplet state (³Chl*). Energy transfer from ³Chl* to ground‑state O₂ generates singlet oxygen (¹O₂), a highly reactive oxygen species that attacks the D1 protein, especially the DE loop that coordinates the Mn₄CaO₅ cluster. The damaged D1 is proteolytically removed by the FtsH and Deg proteases embedded in the thylakoid membrane, and a newly synthesized D1 precursor is inserted co‑translationally by the Sec/YidC translocase, followed by reassembly of the Mn‑cluster and restoration of PSII activity. This repair cycle consumes ATP and reducing power and is temperature‑sensitive; at low temperatures the synthesis step becomes rate‑limiting, causing chronic photoinhibition.

Plants deploy a suite of photoprotective mechanisms that dissipate excess excitation energy before it reaches the reaction centre. The fastest response is non‑photochemical quenching (NPQ), which comprises three components: qE (energy‑dependent), qT (state‑transition), and qI (photoinhibitory). qE is triggered within seconds by the transthylakoid proton gradient (ΔpH) that acidifies the lumen, activating the PsbS protein and the violaxanthin de‑epoxidase (VDE). VDE converts violaxanthin to antheraxanthin and zeaxanthin (the xanthophyll cycle), which bind to light‑harvesting complex II (LHCII) and promote conformational changes that increase thermal dissipation of excitation energy as heat. The reverse reaction, catalysed by zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP), occurs in the stroma when the lumen pH rises, restoring the antenna to a light‑harvesting state. Mutants lacking PsbS (npq4) or VDE (npq1) show severely reduced qE and heightened susceptibility to photoinhibition under fluctuating light.

State transitions (qT) redistribute excitation energy between PSII and PSI by reversible phosphorylation of LHCII mediated by the STN7 kinase (activated by a reduced plastoquinone pool) and the PPH1/TAP38 phosphatase. Under PSII‑favoring light (high red), LHCII migrates to PSI (state 2), balancing the excitation pressure. This process operates on a minutes timescale and contributes to long‑term acclimation. The qI component reflects sustained quenching due to accumulated photodamage; it relaxes only after D1 repair is complete. In addition to NPQ, the water‑water cycle (Mehler reaction) and cyclic electron flow around PSI (via PGR5/PGRL1 and NDH complexes) provide alternative electron sinks that prevent over‑reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain, thereby limiting ROS formation.

The xanthophyll cycle also interacts with the antioxidant network. Zeaxanthin scavenges lipid peroxyl radicals in the thylakoid membrane, while the ascorbate–glutathione cycle detoxifies H₂O₂ generated by superoxide dismutase in the chloroplast stroma. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) uses ascorbate to reduce H₂O₂ to water, producing monodehydroascorbate, which is regenerated by monodehydroascorbate reductase (MDHAR) using NADPH. Glutathione reductase (GR) maintains the reduced glutathione pool required for dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR). Overexpression of APX or GR enhances tolerance to high light, whereas knock‑down lines exhibit elevated lipid peroxidation and reduced growth. The coordination of these processes is orchestrated by retrograde signals such as 3′‑phosphoadenosine‑5′‑phosphate (PAP) and the redox state of the plastoquinone pool, which modulate nuclear gene expression for antioxidant enzymes and chaperones.

From a crop‑improvement perspective, enhancing the capacity and speed of NPQ relaxation (i.e., faster qE reversal) has been shown to increase canopy photosynthetic efficiency under fluctuating light, because prolonged quenching limits light use during sunflecks. Transgenic expression of a faster‑relaxing PsbS variant or engineering of the VDE/ZEP kinetic properties are active research avenues. Simultaneously, improving D1 repair capacity — for example by overexpressing FtsH proteases or optimizing the translation elongation factor EF‑Tu — can raise the threshold of light intensity at which photoinhibition becomes chronic. Understanding the quantitative balance between photoprotection and photochemical quenching is essential for predicting plant performance in dynamic light environments.

Worked Example: Calculating the Energetic Cost of Photorespiration
A C₃ leaf at 30 °C and 21 % O₂ exhibits a Rubisco oxygenation/carboxylation ratio (Vo/Vc) of 0.25. Assuming the Calvin cycle fixes 100 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹, estimate the additional ATP and NADPH consumed per second per m² due to the glycolate pathway. Use the stoichiometry: 2 oxygenations → 1 CO₂ released + 3.5 ATP + 2 NAD(P)H consumed.
Vo/Vc = 0.25 means for every 4 carboxylations there is 1 oxygenation. With Vc = 100 µmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹, Vo = 0.25 × 100 = 25 µmol O₂ m⁻² s⁻¹ (oxygenation events). Two oxygenations constitute one glycolate cycle, so number of cycles = Vo/2 = 12.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Each cycle costs 3.5 ATP and 2 NAD(P)H. Additional ATP = 12.5 × 3.5 = 43.75 µmol ATP m⁻² s⁻¹. Additional NAD(P)H = 12.5 × 2 = 25 µmol NAD(P)H m⁻² s⁻¹. Thus photorespiration imposes an extra demand of ~44 µmol ATP and 25 µmol NADPH per m² per second under these conditions.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the net cost of photorespiration, first compute the oxygenation rate from the given Vo/Vc and carboxylation rate. Remember that the glycolate pathway processes two oxygenations per cycle, and the textbook stoichiometry is 3.5 ATP + 2 NAD(P)H per cycle. Do not confuse this with the ATP/NADPH requirement of the Calvin cycle (3 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO₂ fixed). Write the steps explicitly; examiners award partial credit for correct stoichiometric reasoning even if the final arithmetic has a slip.

Stomatal Signalling: Blue‑Light Photoreceptors, CO₂ Sensing, and ABA Integration

Stomatal aperture is governed by the turgor of guard cells, which in turn depends on the coordinated activity of ion channels, pumps, and metabolic enzymes. Blue light (≈400–500 nm) perceived by the phototropins PHOT1 and PHOT2 initiates a signalling cascade that activates the plasma‑membrane H⁺‑ATPase (AHA1) via phosphorylation of its penultimate threonine by the kinase BLUE‑LIGHT‑SIGNALING1 (BLUS1). The resulting hyperpolarisation drives K⁺ influx through the inward‑rectifying K⁺ channel KAT1 and accompanying Cl⁻ influx, lowering guard‑cell water potential and causing swelling. Simultaneously, blue light stimulates starch degradation to malate²⁻, providing counter‑ions for K⁺ and contributing to osmotic adjustment. Mutants lacking PHOT1/PHOT2 (phot1 phot2) fail to open stomata in response to blue light but retain normal red‑light and CO₂ responses, confirming the specificity of this pathway.

Elevated intercellular CO₂ (Ci) triggers stomatal closure through a mechanism that involves the β‑carbonic anhydrases CA1 and CA4, which hydrate CO₂ to bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) in the guard‑cell cytosol and apoplast, respectively. The resulting pH shift and/or bicarbonate binding to the HT1 kinase complex modulates the activity of the SLAC1 anion channel. HT1 (HIGH LEAF TEMPERATURE1) is a negative regulator of SLAC1; when CO₂ rises, HT1 is inhibited, allowing the SnRK2‑type kinases (OST1/SnRK2.6) to phosphorylate SLAC1, opening the channel for malate²⁻ and Cl⁻ efflux. The consequent depolarisation activates outward‑rectifying K⁺ channels (GORK), leading to K⁺ loss, water efflux, and stomatal closure. The ca1 ca4 double mutant shows a severely impaired CO₂ response, whereas overexpression of CA1 enhances sensitivity.

Abscisic acid (ABA) integrates drought signals with the above pathways. ABA binds to the PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, inhibiting the PP2C phosphatases (ABI1, ABI2) and thereby releasing SnRK2 kinases (OST1, SnRK2.2, SnRK2.3) from inhibition. Active OST1 phosphorylates multiple targets: the SLAC1 and QUAC1 anion channels, the KAT1 inward channel (inhibiting K⁺ influx), the NADPH oxidase RBOHF (producing ROS that amplify Ca²⁺ signals), and the transcription factor ABF2 (inducing stress‑responsive genes). The Ca²⁺‑dependent protein kinases (CPKs) provide a parallel phosphorylation route to SLAC1, ensuring robustness. Crosstalk occurs at the level of OST1, which is also activated by blue‑light‑induced H⁺‑ATPase phosphorylation, and by CO₂‑mediated HT1 inhibition. Thus, the final stomatal aperture reflects the algebraic sum of blue‑light activation, CO₂ inhibition, and ABA‑driven closure signals.

Recent work has identified the guard‑cell‑specific metabolite sucrose as a signalling molecule that modulates stomatal movements independently of its osmotic role. High sucrose levels, indicative of high photosynthetic activity, promote opening by inhibiting the SnRK1 kinase, which otherwise phosphorylates and activates the ABA‑responsive transcription factor ABI5. Conversely, low sucrose (e.g., during night or carbon starvation) relieves SnRK1 inhibition, enhancing ABA sensitivity and favouring closure. This metabolic feedback links source strength directly to gas‑exchange regulation. Additionally, the circadian clock component TOC1 (TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION1) rhythmically regulates the expression of KAT1 and SLAC1, imposing a diurnal pattern on stomatal conductance that anticipates dawn and dusk transitions.

From a physiological standpoint, the speed of stomatal response determines water‑use efficiency (WUE). Fast opening maximises CO₂ uptake during transient sunflecks, while rapid closure limits transpiration during sudden vapour‑pressure‑deficit spikes. Genetic variation in the kinetics of OST1 activation, SLAC1 gating, and H⁺‑ATPase dephosphorylation underlies natural differences in WUE among crop cultivars. Breeding for “smart” stomata — those that open quickly under high light but close promptly under rising CO₂ or ABA — is a promising avenue for improving drought resilience without sacrificing photosynthetic capacity.

Hormonal Crosstalk in Stress Responses: ABA–Ethylene, JA–SA, and GA–DELLA Networks

Plant hormone signalling pathways do not operate in isolation; extensive crosstalk enables the integration of multiple environmental cues into coherent developmental and defensive outputs. The antagonism between abscisic acid (ABA) and ethylene is a classic example. ABA promotes stomatal closure and stress‑responsive gene expression, while ethylene often stimulates senescence, fruit ripening, and root growth inhibition. At the molecular level, the ABA‑activated SnRK2 kinases phosphorylate and stabilize the ethylene‑insensitive transcription factor EIN3, enhancing ethylene‑responsive gene expression under drought. Conversely, ethylene signalling via the EIN2/EIN3 module can up‑regulate the expression of the ABA‑catabolic enzyme CYP707A, reducing ABA levels and promoting recovery after stress. The balance between these hormones determines whether a plant adopts a “stay‑green” drought‑avoidance strategy (high ABA, low ethylene) or a “escape” strategy (accelerated senescence, high ethylene).

Jasmonic acid (JA) and salicylic acid (SA) mediate defence against necrotrophic and biotrophic pathogens, respectively, and their mutual antagonism is well documented. JA signalling proceeds through the COI1‑JAZ co‑receptor complex, leading to JAZ degradation and release of MYC2 transcription factors that activate defence genes (e.g., PDF1.2). SA signalling involves the NPR1 protein, which, upon reduction in the cytosol, translocates to the nucleus and interacts with TGA transcription factors to induce PR genes. SA suppresses JA signalling by promoting the expression of the JAZ repressor JAZ10 via the transcription factor WRKY70, while JA can inhibit SA accumulation by down‑regulating ISOCHORISMATE SYNTHASE1 (ICS1). This reciprocal inhibition ensures that resources are allocated to the appropriate defence programme, but it also creates a vulnerability: pathogens that manipulate one pathway can suppress the other. For instance, the bacterial effector AvrPtoB targets the JA pathway, indirectly enhancing SA‑mediated resistance.

Gibberellins (GA) and the DELLA repressor proteins form a central growth‑regulatory hub that integrates light, temperature, and stress signals. GA binds to its receptor GID1, promoting the interaction with DELLA proteins (e.g., RGA, GAI) and targeting them for SCF^SLY1‑mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. DELLAs, when stable, restrain growth by sequestering transcription factors such as PIFs (PHYTOCHROME‑INTERACTING FACTORS) and by activating stress‑responsive genes. Under drought, ABA stabilises DELLAs by inhibiting GA biosynthesis (via NCED‑mediated ABA accumulation) and by promoting the expression of the GA‑inactivating enzyme GA2ox. Stabilised DELLAs then enhance the expression of antioxidant genes (e.g., SOD, CAT) and promote stomatal closure through interaction with the ABA‑responsive kinase OST1. This GA–DELLA module thus links growth inhibition to stress tolerance.

Another layer of crosstalk involves brassinosteroids (BR) and auxin. BR signalling via BZR1/BES1 transcription factors up‑regulates auxin biosynthetic genes (YUCCA) and transport proteins (PIN), creating a positive feedback loop that drives cell elongation. However, under high temperature, the BR pathway is attenuated by the transcription factor BZR1‑interacting protein BZS1, which redirects resources toward heat‑shock protein synthesis. Simultaneously, auxin levels drop due to increased expression of the auxin‑conjugating enzyme GH3.5, reducing growth and conserving water. The integration of these signals is mediated by shared downstream targets such as the EXPANSIN genes, whose promoters contain both auxin‑responsive (AuxRE) and BR‑responsive (BRRE) elements.

Understanding hormonal crosstalk is essential for designing crops with tailored stress responses. For example, engineering a version of the JAZ repressor that is insensitive to JA but retains SA‑mediated degradation could decouple the JA–SA antagonism, allowing simultaneous resistance to both necrotrophs and biotrophs. Similarly, modifying the DELLA protein to retain growth‑repressive function under favourable conditions but to lose stress‑protective activity could uncouple yield penalty from drought tolerance. These strategies rely on precise knowledge of protein–protein interaction domains, phosphorylation sites, and transcriptional networks that constitute the crosstalk circuitry.

Source–Sink Dynamics and Phloem Loading Strategies: Apoplastic, Symplastic, and Polymer‑Trap Mechanisms

The distribution of photoassimilates from mature source leaves to growing sinks (roots, fruits, developing leaves) is governed by the interplay of phloem loading, long‑distance transport, and unloading. In most herbaceous species, loading occurs via an apoplastic route: sucrose synthesized in the mesophyll is exported to the cell wall space by SWEET efflux transporters (e.g., SWEET11/12 in Arabidopsis), then actively taken up into the companion cell–sieve element complex by the H⁺/sucrose symporter SUT1 (SUC2). This step is energised by the plasma‑membrane H⁺‑ATPase, creating a proton gradient that drives sucrose accumulation to concentrations of 0.5–1 M in the phloem, generating the osmotic pressure that drives bulk flow (Münch pressure flow). Mutants lacking SUT1 (suc2) show severe phloem loading defects, carbohydrate accumulation in source leaves, and stunted growth, confirming the centrality of the apoplastic loader.

In many woody perennials and some herbaceous species (e.g., cucurbits, melons), a symplastic loading pathway operates. Plasmodesmata between bundle‑sheath cells and companion cells are highly numerous and dilated, allowing diffusion of sucrose and, crucially, larger oligosaccharides such as raffinose and stachyose (raffinose family oligosaccharides, RFOs). The “polymer‑trap” model proposes that sucrose entering the companion cell is enzymatically converted to RFOs by galactinol synthase (GolS) and raffinose synthase (RS). Because RFOs are too large to diffuse back through plasmodesmata, they become trapped, maintaining a steep concentration gradient that drives continued sucrose influx. This mechanism eliminates the need for an energy‑dependent sucrose transporter at the plasma membrane, but it requires high metabolic flux through the RFO pathway. Transgenic expression of GolS and RS in apoplastic loaders (e.g., tobacco) can confer a polymer‑trap phenotype, increasing phloem sucrose concentration and enhancing biomass under high light.

Phloem unloading at sinks can be either symplastic or apoplastic, depending on sink type and developmental stage. In developing seeds and tubers, unloading is predominantly apoplastic: sucrose is released into the apoplast by SWEET unloaders (e.g., SWEET15 in Arabidopsis seeds) and then taken up by filial tissues via SUT or SWEET importers. In growing meristems and young leaves, symplastic unloading via plasmodesmata predominates, allowing direct cytoplasmic continuity. The switch between modes is regulated by the expression of plasmodesmal‐modifying proteins such as PDLP (PLASMODESMATA‑LOCATED PROTEIN) and by the callose synthase CALS3, which deposits callose at plasmodesmata to restrict symplastic continuity. Hormonal signals — particularly auxin and cytokinin — modulate these proteins, linking sink strength to developmental programmes.

Sugar signalling feeds back on source activity through the hexokinase (HXK1) and trehalose‑6‑phosphate (T6P) pathways. High sucrose in source leaves elevates T6P, which inhibits SnRK1, relieving repression of biosynthetic genes and promoting starch synthesis. Conversely, low T6P activates SnRK1, triggering catabolic genes and autophagy. In the phloem, T6P also regulates the expression of SUT transporters, creating a homeostatic loop that matches loading capacity to photosynthetic output. Disruption of T6P synthase (TPS1) leads to embryo lethality, underscoring the essential nature of this signalling metabolite. Additionally, the circadian clock controls the diurnal rhythm of SUT expression, ensuring that phloem loading peaks during the light period when sucrose supply is maximal.

From an agronomic perspective, manipulating source–sink relationships offers a route to increase harvest index. Overexpression of SUT1 in source leaves can raise phloem sucrose concentration, but without a corresponding increase in sink demand, the excess carbohydrate feeds back to inhibit photosynthesis (source limitation). A more balanced approach is to enhance sink strength — for example, by expressing cell‑wall invertases (CWIN) in developing fruits to maintain a steep sucrose gradient, or by delaying senescence via cytokinin biosynthesis (IPT) to prolong the source period. The polymer‑trap mechanism is attractive for engineering because it can increase phloem loading capacity without additional ATP expenditure, provided the metabolic cost of RFO synthesis is offset by higher transport efficiency. Field trials in rice expressing a cucumber GolS/RS cassette have shown modest yield gains under well‑watered conditions, but the benefit diminishes under drought, highlighting the context‑dependency of source–sink engineering.

Chloroplast Retrograde Signalling and Nuclear Gene Expression

Chloroplasts communicate their functional state to the nucleus through retrograde signals that adjust the expression of photosynthesis‑associated nuclear genes (PhANGs). The best‑characterised pathway involves the GUN (GENOMES UNCOUPLED) proteins. GUN1, a pentatricopeptide repeat protein localized in the plastid, integrates signals from tetrapyrrole biosynthesis, plastid gene expression, and redox status. When plastid translation is inhibited (e.g., by lincomycin) or when the Mg‑protoporphyrin IX pool accumulates, GUN1 acts with the transcription factor ABI4 and the plastid‑encoded RNA polymerase to repress PhANG transcription. A parallel route uses the redox state of the plastoquinone pool: a highly reduced pool activates the kinase STN7, which phosphorylates LHCII and triggers a signalling cascade that modifies nuclear gene expression via downstream transcription factors. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated at photosystem II (singlet oxygen) and photosystem I (superoxide/hydrogen peroxide) also act as retrograde messengers; they oxidise specific cysteine residues on the transcription factor RAP2.4, altering its DNA‑binding affinity. The net effect is a dynamic re‑programming of nuclear genes encoding chlorophyll‑binding proteins, Calvin‑cycle enzymes, and antioxidant systems, ensuring that the nuclear genome matches the photosynthetic capacity of the organelle. Mutants defective in GUN1 or ABI4 fail to down‑regulate PhANGs under photoxidative stress, leading to photoinhibition and reduced fitness. Recent work shows that the metabolite 3′‑phosphoadenosine‑5′‑phosphate (PAP), produced in the chloroplast during drought, travels to the nucleus and inhibits the 5′‑to‑3′ exoribonuclease XRN2, stabilising stress‑responsive transcripts. This metabolite‑based retrograde signal links chloroplast metabolism directly to nuclear RNA turnover.

Circadian Regulation of Photosynthetic Capacity

The plant circadian clock orchestrates the timing of photosynthetic gene expression, stomatal opening, and metabolic fluxes to anticipate daily light cycles. Core clock components — CCA1 (CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1) and LHY (LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL) — are MYB transcription factors that peak at dawn and repress evening‑phased genes such as TOC1 (TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1) and the PRR (PSEUDO‑RESPONSE REGULATOR) family. In turn, TOC1 and PRRs repress CCA1/LHY, generating a ~24 h feedback loop. This oscillator drives rhythmic transcription of key photosynthetic genes: RBCS (Rubisco small subunit), CAB (chlorophyll a/b‑binding protein), and PSI/PSII subunits show peak mRNA levels 2–4 h before subjective dawn, ensuring protein accumulation coincides with light onset. The clock also gates the expression of the starch‑degrading enzyme β‑amylase (BAM3) and the sucrose‑phosphate synthase (SPS), synchronising carbon mobilisation with the photoperiod. Light input pathways — phytochrome B (red/far‑red) and cryptochrome 1/2 (blue) — feed into the clock by promoting the degradation of PIF (PHYTOCHROME‑INTERACTING FACTOR) proteins, which otherwise repress photosynthetic genes in darkness. Temperature compensation is achieved through the alternative splicing of PRR7 and the action of the kinase CK2, which phosphorylates CCA1/LHY to modulate their stability. Disruption of the clock (e.g., cca1 lhy double mutants) leads to mistimed stomatal opening, reduced water‑use efficiency, and a significant drop in biomass under fluctuating light. Agronomically, natural variation in circadian clock gene promoters correlates with flowering time and yield in rice, illustrating how circadian tuning can be harnessed for crop improvement.

Examiner's twist
A question asks: "Which of the following statements about the circadian control of photosynthesis is INCORRECT? (a) CCA1 and LHY peak at dawn and repress TOC1. (b) The clock gates starch degradation via BAM3. (c) Phytochrome B stabilises PIF proteins in red light. (d) PRR7 alternative splicing contributes to temperature compensation."
Option (c) is incorrect. Phytochrome B activated by red light promotes the degradation of PIF proteins, not their stabilisation. Alternative Respiratory Pathways: Alternative Oxidase and Uncoupling Proteins

Plant mitochondria possess non‑phosphorylating electron‑transport routes that dissipate excess reducing power and limit ROS formation. The alternative oxidase (AOX) accepts electrons from ubiquinol and reduces O₂ to water without proton translocation, bypassing complexes III and IV. AOX transcription is induced by mitochondrial retrograde signals such as the redox state of the ubiquinone pool, nitric oxide, and the transcription factor ANAC017. In the presence of high NADH/NAD⁺ ratios (e.g., during chilling or pathogen attack), AOX activity can consume up to 30 % of total respiratory O₂ uptake, lowering the mitochondrial membrane potential and curbing superoxide production at complex I. Uncoupling proteins (UCPs), particularly UCP1 in Arabidopsis, mediate a controlled proton leak across the inner membrane, further decreasing ΔΨ and ROS. UCPs are activated by fatty acids and superoxide, creating a feedback loop where ROS stimulate their own mitigation. Both AOX and UCPs are essential for thermogenesis in certain species (e.g., skunk cabbage) and for maintaining redox balance during photoinhibition. Mutants lacking AOX1a display heightened sensitivity to combined high light and low temperature, while UCP1 knockouts accumulate more H₂O₂ under drought. Importantly, the alternative pathway does not contribute to ATP synthesis; its energetic cost is offset by the protective benefit. The balance between cytochrome and alternative pathways is modulated by the cellular ADP/ATP ratio and by post‑translational modification of AOX (α‑keto‑acid activation). Understanding this flexibility explains why respiration measurements in stressed tissues often show a lower P/O ratio than textbook values.

TOR Kinase Signalling Integrates Nutrient, Energy, and Hormone Cues

The Target of Rapamycin (TOR) kinase is a central hub that couples carbon and nitrogen availability to growth processes. In plants, TOR forms a complex with RAPTOR and LST8, analogous to mammalian TORC1. Activation requires the small GTPase RHEB, which is itself regulated by the TSC1/2 complex; however, plants lack a clear TSC orthologue, and instead the SnRK1 kinase phosphorylates RAPTOR to inhibit TOR under energy deficit (high AMP/ATP). When sucrose and amino acids are abundant, TOR phosphorylates the translation initiation factor eIF3h and the S6 kinase (S6K), promoting ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis. TOR also phosphorylates the transcription factor E2Fa, driving cell‑cycle progression. Conversely, SnRK1 phosphorylates and activates the autophagy‑related kinase ATG1, initiating macroautophagy to recycle nutrients. Hormonal inputs converge on TOR: auxin stimulates TOR activity via auxin‑signalling components, while ABA activates SnRK1, creating an antagonistic module that balances growth and stress tolerance. Nitrate signalling through the NRT1.1 transceptor feeds into TOR by modulating the phosphorylation status of the NLP7 transcription factor, linking nitrogen status to anabolic gene expression. Rapamycin‑sensitive TOR mutants exhibit dwarfism, reduced leaf size, and delayed senescence, whereas TOR overexpression leads to hyper‑growth but hypersensitivity to carbon starvation. The TOR‑SnRK1 seesaw thus integrates metabolic, hormonal, and environmental information to decide whether the plant invests in biomass accumulation or activates catabolic survival programmes.

Long‑Distance Signalling via Phloem‑Mobile RNAs and Proteins

The phloem transports not only photoassimilates but also macromolecular signals that coordinate development and stress responses across the plant. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) move through plasmodesmata into sieve elements and travel to distant sinks. In Arabidopsis, the 21‑nt siRNA derived from the TAS3 locus moves from shoot to root, where it directs cleavage of ARF3/ARF4 transcripts, shaping root architecture. The mobile protein FT (FLOWERING LOCUS T) acts as a florigen; synthesized in leaves under inductive photoperiods, it travels via the phloem to the shoot apical meristem, where it interacts with FD to activate floral identity genes. Another mobile protein, CmPP16 (PHLOEM PROTEIN 16) from pumpkin, functions as an RNA‑binding chaperone that facilitates the transport of specific mRNAs, including those encoding transcription factors such as KNOTTED1. Grafting experiments demonstrate that the movement of these macromolecules is selective: only RNAs with specific sequence motifs (e.g., the “phloem‑entry” motif in the 3′ UTR) and proteins with a conserved “phloem‑localization” domain enter the sieve tube. The plasmodesmal size‑exclusion limit is dynamically regulated by callose deposition mediated by CALS3 and by PDLP proteins, which are themselves targets of hormonal regulation (auxin, cytokinin). Viruses exploit this pathway; the movement protein of Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) modifies plasmodesmata to allow viral RNA passage. From a biotechnological perspective, engineering mobile siRNAs that silence susceptibility genes in roots offers a strategy for systemic disease resistance without transgenic rootstocks. The discovery of phloem‑mobile peptides such as CLE (CLAVATA3/ESR‑RELATED) further expands the signalling repertoire, linking nutrient status to meristem activity.

Photoreceptor Signalling Networks: Phytochromes, Cryptochromes, and Phototropins

Higher plants perceive light quality, quantity, direction, and duration through three major photoreceptor families. Phytochromes (phyA–phyE) are red/far‑red reversible chromoproteins existing as Pr (red‑absorbing) and Pfr (far‑red‑absorbing) forms; the Pfr conformation interacts with PHYTOCHROME‑INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs) to promote their phosphorylation and degradation, releasing repression of light‑responsive genes. PhyA mediates very‑low‑fluence and far‑red high‑irradiance responses, whereas phyB–phyE regulate shade avoidance, seed germination, and circadian entrainment. Cryptochromes (cry1, cry2) are blue‑light flavoproteins lacking DNA‑photolyase activity; they inhibit COP1/SPA ubiquitin ligase activity in the nucleus, stabilising transcription factors such as HY5 and HFR1. Phototropins (phot1, phot2) are serine/threonine kinases with LOV domains that autophosphorylate upon blue‑light absorption, triggering chloroplast relocation, stomatal opening, and phototropic curvature via the NPH3/RPT2 scaffold. Crosstalk is extensive: phyB and cry1 converge on the COP1/SPA complex, while phot1‑mediated auxin redistribution requires functional phyB for full hypocotyl bending. Mutant analyses show that cry1cry2 double mutants are blind to blue‑light inhibition of hypocotyl elongation yet retain phototropism, demonstrating pathway bifurcation. In crop improvement, allelic variation at PHYB and CRY2 modulates canopy architecture and flowering time, suggesting potential targets for dense planting systems.

Starch Metabolism and Diurnal Carbon Partitioning

Transitory starch in chloroplasts buffers the mismatch between daytime photosynthetic carbon fixation and nighttime metabolic demand. During the light period, ADP‑glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) is activated by 3‑phosphoglycerate (3‑PGA) and inhibited by inorganic phosphate (Pi), directing triose‑phosphate into starch biosynthesis via starch synthases (SS) and branching enzymes (BE). At dusk, the circadian clock component CCA1/LHY represses expression of β‑amylase (BAM3) and starch‑excess4 (SEX4), preventing premature degradation. Nighttime starch breakdown follows a linear pathway: phosphorylation of glucans by glucan‑water dikinase (GWD) and phosphoglucan‑water dikinase (PWD) creates phosphoglucan chains attacked by β‑amylases (BAM1, BAM3) and debranching enzyme ISA3, releasing maltose and glucose. Maltose is exported to the cytosol via the maltose transporter MEX1 and converted to glucose by disproportionating enzyme 2 (DPE2), while glucose exits via the plastidic glucose transporter pGlcT. The degradation rate is set so starch reserves are nearly exhausted by dawn; mutants defective in GWD (sex1) or BAM3 (bam3) retain excess starch and show growth retardation under short days. Metabolic modelling indicates that starch turnover rate correlates with night length, a feature that informs breeding strategies for high‑yield varieties under variable photoperiods.

Worked Example: Predicting Starch Phenotype
A loss‑of‑function mutation in the GWD gene (sex1) eliminates glucan phosphorylation. Predict the starch content at the end of a 12‑hour night in a sex1 mutant versus wild type, and explain the impact on nocturnal sucrose levels.
In wild type, GWD‑mediated phosphorylation enables β‑amylase access, so starch is linearly degraded and nearly exhausted by dawn; nocturnal sucrose is maintained by maltose‑derived glucose. In sex1, starch cannot be phosphorylated, β‑amylase activity is severely reduced, and a large proportion of the starch pool remains at dawn. Consequently, cytosolic sucrose drops sharply during the night because maltose export is limited, leading to carbon starvation symptoms such as reduced root growth and activation of SnRK1‑dependent stress genes.

When a question asks for the consequence of a specific enzyme knockout in starch turnover, first identify the step it catalyses (phosphorylation, hydrolysis, transport). Then trace the metabolic flux: does the block cause substrate accumulation or product depletion? Link the metabolic change to a known signalling node (e.g., SnRK1 activation by low sucrose) to earn full marks.

Calcium‑Dependent Signalling in Guard Cells and Stress Responses

Cytosolic Ca²⁺ elevations act as a versatile second messenger linking environmental cues to stomatal aperture and stress‑adaptive gene expression. In guard cells, abscisic acid (ABA) triggers production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via NADPH oxidases (RBOHD/F), which activate plasma‑membrane Ca²⁺‑permeable channels (e.g., OSCA1.3, GLR3.1/3.3). The resulting Ca²⁺ spikes are decoded by CALCINEURIN B‑LIKE (CBL) proteins that bind and activate CBL‑INTERACTING PROTEIN KINASES (CIPKs). The CBL1/CBL9–CIPK23 module phosphorylates the inward‑rectifying K⁺ channel KAT1, promoting its internalisation and reducing K⁺ influx, while CBL4 (SOS3)–CIPK24 (SOS2) activates the plasma‑membrane Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter SOS1 for salt tolerance. Simultaneously, Ca²⁺‑dependent protein kinases (CDPKs/CPKs) phosphorylate the anion channel SLAC1, driving Cl⁻ and malate²⁻ efflux, membrane depolarisation, and K⁺ efflux through outward‑rectifying channels (GORK), culminating in stomatal closure. Specificity arises from distinct Ca²⁺ signatures: ABA generates repetitive, low‑amplitude oscillations, whereas pathogen‑associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) elicit a single high‑amplitude transient. Mutants lacking CPK3/CPK6 display impaired ABA‑induced closure but normal PAMP responses, illustrating pathway segregation. In drought‑prone crops, overexpression of a constitutively active CIPK23 variant reduces transpiration without compromising CO₂ assimilation under moderate light, a trait under field evaluation.

Nitrate Sensing and Signalling via the NRT1.1 Transceptor

The dual‑affinity nitrate transporter NRT1.1 (CHL1) functions as a transceptor, sensing external nitrate concentrations and modulating both transport activity and downstream signalling. At low nitrate (<0.5 mM), NRT1.1 is phosphorylated at Thr101 by the CBL1/CBL9–CIPK23 complex, converting it to a high‑affinity transporter (Km ≈ 50 µM) and enabling recruitment of the transcription factor NLP7 to nitrate‑responsive promoters. At high nitrate (>5 mM), dephosphorylation by the PP2C phosphatase ABI2 switches NRT1.1 to a low‑affinity mode (Km ≈ 5 mM) and triggers a rapid Ca²⁺ influx through the channel activity of NRT1.1 itself, activating the MAPK cascade MPK3/MPK6. This bifurcated output coordinates root architecture: high‑affinity mode promotes lateral root elongation toward nitrate patches, while low‑affinity mode suppresses lateral root initiation via auxin transport inhibition (NRT1.1 interacts with AUX1). The NRT1.1‑dependent Ca²⁺ signal also feeds into the TOR kinase pathway, linking nitrogen status to protein synthesis. In Arabidopsis, nrt1.1 loss‑of‑function mutants fail to induce NIA1/NIA2 (nitrate reductase) expression after nitrate resupply, confirming the transceptor’s role as the primary nitrate sensor. Agronomic strategies under investigation include editing the Thr101 phosphorylation site to lock NRT1.1 in high‑affinity mode, potentially improving nitrogen use efficiency under low‑input fertilisation regimes.

Phloem Unloading Mechanisms at Sink Organs

Delivery of photoassimilates from sieve elements to recipient cells occurs via two fundamentally distinct routes: symplastic unloading through plasmodesmata and apoplastic unloading followed by active transport across plasma membranes. In developing roots and young leaves, high plasmodesmal density and low callose deposition permit bulk flow of sucrose and signalling molecules directly into the sink symplast; this route is facilitated by the callose‑degrading β‑1,3‑glucanase PDLP5 and the sucrose‑responsive transcription factors SWEET11/12 that maintain plasmodesmal aperture. In contrast, storage organs such as tubers, fruits, and developing seeds often employ apoplastic unloading: sucrose is released into the apoplast by SWEET efflux transporters (e.g., SWEET15 in tomato fruit), then taken up by high‑affinity sucrose/H⁺ symporters (SUT1/2) on parenchyma cells. The choice between routes is developmentally programmed and modulated by turgor pressure gradients; elevated sink turgor promotes symplastic continuity, whereas low turgor favors apoplastic release. Mutants defective in SWEET11/12 exhibit reduced phloem unloading in Arabidopsis roots, leading to carbohydrate accumulation in source leaves and feedback inhibition of photosynthesis. In cereal grains, the maternal nucellar projection releases sucrose into the apoplast, where the filial endosperm expresses SUT1 to import sucrose for starch synthesis; disruption of this step causes shrunken kernels. Understanding the regulatory nodes — callose turnover, SWEET/SUT expression, and turgor sensing — supports targeted breeding approaches for improved harvest index under water‑limited conditions.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Homeostasis and Signalling

Reactive oxygen species — superoxide (O₂⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), hydroxyl radical (·OH) and singlet oxygen (¹O₂) — are by‑products of photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport. In chloroplasts, the Mehler reaction and photosystem II over‑reduction generate O₂⁻, rapidly dismutated to H₂O₂ by stromal and thylakoid‑bound superoxide dismutases (SODs). Mitochondria produce O₂⁻ at complexes I and III, especially when the alternative oxidase pathway is limited. Peroxisomes contribute H₂O₂ during photorespiratory glycolate oxidation. The cellular redox state is buffered by the ascorbate–glutathione cycle: ascorbate peroxidase (APX) reduces H₂O₂ to water using ascorbate, yielding monodehydroascorbate (MDHA), which disproportionates spontaneously or is reduced by MDHA reductase (MDHAR) using NADPH; dehydroascorbate (DHA) is then reduced by dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR) using glutathione (GSH), producing oxidised glutathione (GSSG) recycled by glutathione reductase (GR). Parallel systems include catalase (CAT) in peroxisomes, peroxiredoxins (Prx) and glutathione peroxidases (GPX) that use thioredoxin or glutaredoxin as electron donors. ROS are not merely toxic; they act as second messengers. H₂O₂ diffuses through aquaporins (PIPs) to the cytosol where it oxidises cysteine residues on target proteins — MAP kinase kinases (MKKs), phosphatases (PP2C), and transcription factors including HSFA4A — activating stress‑responsive gene expression. The “oxidative burst” triggered by pathogen‑associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) depends on the plasma‑membrane NADPH oxidase RBOHD, phosphorylated by the calcium‑dependent protein kinase CPK5 and the BIK1 kinase downstream of the FLS2‑BAK1 complex. In guard cells, ABA‑induced H₂O₂ production by RBOHF amplifies Ca²⁺ influx, promoting stomatal closure. Genetic evidence: Arabidopsis rbohD rbohF double mutants lack the rapid ROS burst and show compromised pattern‑triggered immunity, while overexpression of cytosolic APX enhances tolerance to high light and drought. The balance between ROS production and scavenging determines the “redox window” permitting signalling without oxidative damage; perturbations shift this window, causing either growth inhibition (excess ROS) or impaired stress acclimation (deficient ROS).

Vacuolar Ion Transport and pH Regulation

The plant vacuole occupies up to 90 % of cell volume and serves as the principal store for inorganic ions, metabolites and secondary products. Its membrane (tonoplast) hosts transporters that establish and maintain vacuolar pH (typically 5.0–5.5) and ion composition. The vacuolar H⁺‑ATPase (V‑ATPase) and H⁺‑pyrophosphatase (V‑PPase) pump protons from the cytosol into the lumen, creating an electrochemical gradient (ΔpH ≈ 2–3 units, ΔΨ ≈ +20 mV, lumen positive). This gradient drives secondary active transport: cation/H⁺ antiporters (NHX1/NHX2) exchange luminal H⁺ for cytosolic Na⁺ or K⁺, contributing to salt tolerance and K⁺ homeostasis; the CAX family mediates Ca²⁺/H⁺ exchange, shaping cytosolic Ca²⁺ signatures; the vacuolar chloride channel (CLC‑a/CLC‑b) operates as a 2Cl⁻/H⁺ exchanger, balancing charge during nitrate accumulation. The tonoplast also harbours vacuolar aquaporins (TIPs) for water flux, and sugar transporters (SWEETs, TMTs) that sequester sucrose and hexoses. Regulation occurs at multiple levels: (i) transcriptional control by ABA, salt, and drought via AREB/ABF and DREB2A transcription factors; (ii) post‑translational phosphorylation of V‑ATPase subunits by SnRK2 kinases, enhancing pump activity under osmotic stress; (iii) interaction with 14‑3‑3 proteins that modulate NHX1 activity in response to cytosolic pH changes. Mutants lacking NHX1 display reduced vacuolar Na⁺ sequestration and heightened salt sensitivity, whereas overexpression of V‑PPase (AVP1) improves drought and salt tolerance by augmenting proton motive force. Vacuolar pH also influences hydrolytic enzymes (proteases, nucleases) during senescence and programmed cell death, linking ion transport to developmental programmes.

Worked Example: Calculating Vacuolar Proton Motive Force
A mesophyll cell maintains a cytosolic pH of 7.2 and a vacuolar pH of 5.3. The tonoplast potential (Ψₜ) is measured at +25 mV (lumen positive). Calculate the proton motive force (PMF) across the tonoplast in millivolts.
The PMF for protons is Δp = ΔΨ – (2.303 RT/F) ΔpH. At 25 °C, 2.303 RT/F ≈ 59 mV. ΔpH = pH_cytosol – pH_vacuole = 7.2 – 5.3 = 1.9. Chemical component = 59 mV × 1.9 ≈ 112 mV. Electrical component = Ψₜ = +25 mV (lumen positive, so protons moving inward experience a negative electrical potential). Δp = 25 mV – 112 mV = –87 mV. The negative sign indicates that the electrochemical gradient favours proton influx into the vacuole, providing ~87 mV of driving force for H⁺‑coupled antiporters.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the direction of ion movement driven by the vacuolar H⁺ gradient, first write the PMF equation, substitute the given pH and voltage values, and state the sign convention. Examiners award marks for showing the calculation steps, not just the final answer.

Seed Dormancy, Germination, and Hormonal Control

Seed dormancy prevents germination under unfavourable conditions and is imposed by the balance between abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellins (GA). During seed maturation, the transcription factor ABI3, together with ABI4 and ABI5, activates ABA‑responsive genes (e.g., LEA proteins, storage proteins) and represses GA biosynthetic genes (GA3ox). High ABA/GA ratio maintains dormancy; after‑ripening, dry storage, or cold stratification reduces ABA levels via CYP707A‑mediated catabolism and enhances GA synthesis through up‑regulation of GA3ox1/2. Upon imbibition, GA perception by the GID1 receptor triggers the SCF^SLY1/GID2 ubiquitin ligase to poly‑ubiquitinate DELLA repressors (RGA, GAI, RGL2), targeting them for 26S proteasomal degradation. DELLA removal derepresses transcription factors such as PIF1 (PIL5) and SOMNUS, which activate hydrolytic enzymes (α‑amylases, proteases, lipases) in the aleurone layer and embryo. Light signals modulate this network: phytochrome B (phyB) activation promotes GA synthesis and represses ABA synthesis, while far‑red enrichment (low R:FR) stabilises PIF1, reinforcing dormancy. Nitric oxide (NO) and reactive nitrogen species also break dormancy by S‑nitrosylating ABA‑signalling components. Mutants illustrate the hierarchy: abi3, abi4, abi5 are nondormant; ga1‑3 (GA‑deficient) fail to germinate unless rescued by exogenous GA; rgl2 loss‑of‑function suppresses ga1 dormancy. “Seed coat‑imposed” dormancy involves mechanical restraint and ABA release from the endothelium; mutants in transparent testa (tt) genes have altered flavonoid deposition and reduced dormancy. Environmental cues (temperature fluctuations, nitrate, smoke‑derived karrikins) converge on the ABA/GA balance via distinct signalling modules, ensuring germination occurs only when seedling establishment probability is high.

Senescence and Nutrient Remobilisation

Leaf senescence is a genetically programmed, nutrient‑recycling process preceding organ abscission. It is initiated by developmental age, photoperiod, and stress signals (darkness, nutrient deficiency, pathogen attack). The core regulatory network centres on the NAC transcription factor ORE1 (ANAC092), repressed by microRNA miR164 in young leaves. As miR164 declines with age, ORE1 accumulates and activates senescence‑associated genes (SAGs) such as SAG12 (cysteine protease), SEN1 (senescence‑associated nuclease), and chlorophyll catabolic enzymes (NYC1, PPH, PAO). Ethylene and jasmonic acid (JA) accelerate senescence by stabilising EIN3/EIL1 and MYC2, which cooperate with ORE1. Conversely, cytokinin delays senescence via the AHK3‑ARR2 pathway that up‑regulates miR164 and represses ORE1. Nutrient remobilisation is tightly coupled: nitrogen from Rubisco and other proteins is liberated by proteases (SAG12, RD21) and transported as amino acids (mainly glutamine, asparagine) through the phloem via amino acid permeases AAP1/2. Phosphate is remobilised by vacuolar phosphate transporters and purple acid phosphatases (PAPs) that hydrolyse organic phosphates. Potassium and micronutrients (Fe, Zn) are exported by tonoplast transporters including NHX1 and ZIP family members. The “stay‑green” phenotype (e.g., Arabidopsis ore1‑1, rice OsNAP knock‑down) retains chlorophyll and photosynthetic capacity but often shows reduced nitrogen remobilisation efficiency, leading to lower grain protein content. In crops, manipulation of senescence timing — via overexpression of cytokinin biosynthesis gene IPT under a senescence‑specific promoter (SAG12::IPT) — extends grain‑filling duration and improves yield under terminal drought. Understanding the transcriptional hierarchy (miR164‑ORE1‑SAGs) and hormonal cross‑talk (CK‑ethylene‑JA) provides targets for breeding varieties with optimized nutrient use efficiency.

Root System Architecture Modulation by Phosphate and Iron Signalling

Root architecture plasticity enables plants to forage heterogeneously distributed nutrients. Low phosphate (Pi) triggers primary root growth inhibition, enhanced lateral root density, and prolific root hair formation. The central regulator is the SPX domain‑containing proteins (SPX1‑4) that sense cellular Pi status. Under Pi sufficiency, SPX proteins interact with the transcription factor PHR1 (PHOSPHATE STARVATION RESPONSE 1) and inhibit its activity. Pi deficiency reduces SPX‑PHR1 interaction, freeing PHR1 to bind the P1BS element in promoters of Pi‑starvation‑induced (PSI) genes — high‑affinity Pi transporters (PHT1;1, PHT1;4), acid phosphatases (APases), and the miR399 precursor. miR399 moves shoot‑to‑root via phloem and targets the ubiquitin‑conjugating enzyme PHO2 (UBC24) for degradation, relieving PHO2‑mediated ubiquitination of PHT1 transporters and the Pi exporter PHO1, thereby enhancing Pi uptake and translocation. Iron (Fe) deficiency elicits a distinct but overlapping programme: Strategy I (non‑graminaceous) plants acidify the rhizosphere via plasma‑membrane H⁺‑ATPase AHA2, reduce Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ by ferric‑chelate reductase FRO2, and uptake Fe²⁺ through transporter IRT1. The bHLH transcription factor FIT heterodimerises with bHLH38/39/100/101 to activate FRO2 and IRT1. In Strategy II (grasses), mugineic‑acid family phytosiderophores are synthesised by NAS, DMAS, and SAMS enzymes and secreted by TOM1; Fe³⁺‑phytosiderophore complexes are imported by YS1/YSL transporters. Cross‑talk exists: Pi starvation up‑regulates FIT and IRT1, while Fe deficiency induces miR399, linking the two nutrient circuits. Local perception of Pi at the root tip involves receptor‑like kinase LPR1 (LOW PHOSPHATE ROOT 1) and its partner PDR2 (P5‑type ATPase), which modulate meristem activity via the STOP1‑ALMT1 malate exporter, adjusting root growth toward Pi‑rich patches. Mutants lpr1 and pdr2 maintain primary root elongation under low Pi, confirming the signalling module. Agronomic exploitation includes breeding for altered SPX/PHR1 affinity or miR399 expression to improve Pi acquisition in low‑input soils.

Photorespiration

Photorespiration is a wasteful process that occurs when RuBisCO fixes O2 instead of CO2. This oxygenase activity of RuBisCO increases under high temperature, high O2, and low CO2 conditions — typically when stomata close during water stress.

The photorespiratory pathway:

  • RuBisCO oxygenase: RuBP + O2 → 3-PGA (3C) + Phosphoglycolate (2C).
  • Phosphoglycolate is dephosphorylated to glycolate, which moves to the peroxisome, where it is converted to glycine.
  • Glycine moves to the mitochondrion, where two glycine molecules are converted to one serine + CO2 (releasing previously fixed carbon).
  • The serine returns to the peroxisome and is converted to glycerate, which re-enters the Calvin cycle.

Consequences: Photorespiration consumes ATP and NADPH without producing sugar. It releases fixed CO2 — C3 plants can lose 25–50% of their fixed carbon this way. Unlike dark respiration, photorespiration does not generate ATP. C4 plants avoid photorespiration by concentrating CO2 in bundle sheath cells (high CO2 suppresses RuBisCO oxygenase activity).

NEET Comparison — C3 vs C4 vs CAM
Three key differences to memorise: (1) First product: C3 = 3-PGA, C4 = OAA, CAM = OAA (at night). (2) CO2 acceptor: C3 = RuBP, C4 = PEP, CAM = PEP (night). (3) Photorespiration: C3 = high, C4 = negligible, CAM = low. Also remember: C3 plants have no Kranz anatomy; C4 plants have Kranz anatomy; CAM plants are succulent with temporal separation.
Example 8 — Photorespiration Analysis
Why does photorespiration increase on a hot, dry day in C3 plants? How do C4 plants avoid this problem?
Solution: On hot, dry days, plants close their stomata to conserve water. This reduces CO2 availability inside the leaf, while O2 levels rise due to continued photosynthesis. Under these conditions, RuBisCO’s oxygenase activity predominates, leading to photorespiration. C4 plants avoid this by concentrating CO2 in bundle sheath cells via the C4 cycle (PEP carboxylase feeds CO2 into the Calvin cycle at high concentrations), suppressing the oxygenase activity of RuBisCO.

Plant Water Relations — Detailed Mechanisms

Imbibition: Adsorption of water by hydrophilic colloids (e.g., proteins, pectins, cellulose). It is the initial step in seed germination. Imbibition causes swelling of seeds and generates considerable pressure (imbibition pressure).

Osmosis: The net movement of water across a semi-permeable membrane down its water potential gradient. Types of solutions:

  • Hypotonic: Lower solute concentration outside than inside; water enters the cell (endosmosis); cell becomes turgid.
  • Hypertonic: Higher solute concentration outside; water leaves the cell (exosmosis); cell becomes plasmolysed.
  • Isotonic: Equal solute concentrations; no net water movement.

Plasmolysis: Shrinkage of the protoplast due to water loss in a hypertonic solution. It proves that (1) the cell membrane is semi-permeable, and (2) the cell is living. Deplasmolysis occurs when a plasmolysed cell is placed in a hypotonic solution — the protoplast swells back to its original state.

6. Plant Response to Environmental Stimuli

Tropisms

Tropisms are directional growth responses of plants to environmental stimuli:

  • Phototropism: Growth towards (positive) or away from (negative) light. Mediated by auxin redistribution. Shoots are positively phototropic; roots are negatively phototropic.
  • Geotropism (Gravitropism): Growth in response to gravity. Roots are positively geotropic (grow downward); shoots are negatively geotropic (grow upward). Statoliths (amyloplasts) in root cap cells function as gravity sensors.
  • Hydrotropism: Growth towards water. Roots show positive hydrotropism.
  • Thigmotropism: Growth in response to physical contact. Seen in tendrils of climbing plants.
  • Chemotropism: Growth in response to chemicals. Pollen tube growth towards ovule (positive chemotropism to calcium ions and specific peptides).

Nastic Movements

Nastic movements are non-directional responses to stimuli. Examples include:

  • Seismonasty (Thigmonasty): Response to mechanical shock. The touch-sensitive Mimosa pudica (touch-me-not) folds its leaflets when touched. The response involves rapid loss of turgor in pulvini (specialised motor organs at leaflet bases) due to K+ and water efflux.
  • Photonasty: Response to light. Nyctinasty (sleep movements) — flowers opening in response to light (e.g., morning glory) or closing at night.
  • Thermonasty: Response to temperature. Some flowers open when temperature rises (e.g., tulips).
Example 9 — Tropism Identification
A student places a potted plant on its side. After 72 hours, the root bends downward and the shoot bends upward. Identify the type of tropism and explain the mechanism.
Solution: This is geotropism (gravitropism). In the root tip, amyloplasts (statoliths) sediment to the lower side, triggering asymmetric auxin redistribution. Higher auxin on the lower side of the root inhibits cell elongation (roots are sensitive to auxin), causing the root to bend downward (positive geotropism). In the shoot, higher auxin on the lower side promotes cell elongation, causing the shoot to bend upward (negative geotropism).
Memory Aid — Tropisms
Directional responses: Photo = light, Geo = gravity, Hydro = water, Thigmo = touch, Chemo = chemicals. Remember: Roots are positively geotropic and hydrotropic but negatively phototropic. Shoots are positively phototropic but negatively geotropic. Auxin is the key hormone mediating these responses.

7. Seed Germination

Seed germination is the resumption of active growth by the embryo after a period of dormancy. It requires favourable conditions: adequate water, oxygen, appropriate temperature, and (for some seeds) light.

Types of Seed Germination

  • Epigeal germination: The hypocotyl elongates and pushes the cotyledons above the soil. The cotyledons become photosynthetic. Examples: bean, cucumber, sunflower, castor.
  • Hypogeal germination: The epicotyl elongates, but the cotyledons remain below the soil. The cotyledons serve as storage organs and do not photosynthesise. Examples: pea, maize, wheat, groundnut.

Seed Dormancy

Seed dormancy is the temporary inability of a viable seed to germinate under favourable conditions. Causes include:

  • Hard seed coat: Impermeable to water or gases (mechanical dormancy). Scarification (mechanical or acid treatment) breaks dormancy.
  • Physiological dormancy: Requires cold stratification (e.g., apple seeds), light (positive photoblastic — lettuce), or darkness (negative photoblastic).
  • Chemical dormancy: Presence of germination inhibitors (ABA) that must be leached away.

Methods to break dormancy: Mechanical scarification, acid scarification (H2SO4), cold stratification, GA application, light treatment.

8. Essential Mineral Functions — In Depth

Nitrogen Cycle Summary

The nitrogen cycle involves the following transformations:

  • Nitrogen fixation: N2 → NH3 (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Anabaena; also industrial Haber-Bosch and atmospheric fixation by lightning).
  • Nitrification: NH3 → NO2− (Nitrosomonas) → NO3− (Nitrobacter).
  • Assimilation: NO3− → NO2− → NH4+ → amino acids.
  • Ammonification: Organic N → NH4+ (decomposers).
  • Denitrification: NO3− → N2 (Pseudomonas).

Hydroponics

Hydroponics is the cultivation of plants in a nutrient solution without soil. It is used to:

  • Determine which elements are essential for plant growth (by omitting specific elements and observing deficiency symptoms).
  • Grow crops in areas with poor or contaminated soil (commercial hydroponics).
  • Study mineral nutrient uptake and utilisation under controlled conditions.
Quick Revision — Deficiency Symptoms
Chlorosis (first in older leaves): N, Mg, K, Mo. Chlorosis (first in young leaves): Fe, S, Mn. Necrosis: Ca, K, Cu. Stunted growth: N, P, Zn. Purple leaves: P deficiency. Remember: mobile elements (N, P, K, Mg) cause deficiency in older leaves; immobile elements (Ca, B, Fe, S) cause deficiency in younger leaves.

Example 10 — Integrated Problem
A C3 plant is placed in a sealed chamber with 14CO2 and illuminated. After 5 seconds, the first radioactive compound detected is 3-PGA. After 10 seconds, the label appears in G3P and RuBP. If the chamber temperature is raised from 25 °C to 40 °C, the rate of 14CO2 fixation decreases. Explain.
Solution: The first radioactive compound is 3-PGA (3C), confirming the C3 pathway (Calvin cycle). The label then appears in G3P (product of 3-PGA reduction) and RuBP (regenerated). At 40 °C, RuBisCO’s oxygenase activity increases (photorespiration), so more RuBP reacts with O2 instead of CO2, reducing net CO2 fixation. This is also the Warburg effect — high O2 and high temperature promote photorespiration in C3 plants.

11. Movement of Water and Minerals in Detail

Pathways of Water Movement

Water moves through the root via three pathways before entering the xylem:

  • Apoplast pathway: Movement through cell walls and intercellular spaces. Rapid and unimpeded. Water and minerals move freely through the continuous matrix of cell walls. This pathway is \blocked by the Casparian strip in the endodermis.
  • Symplast pathway: Movement through the cytoplasm via plasmodesmata. Water moves from cell to cell through cytoplasmic connections. This pathway allows selective uptake of minerals as it passes through living protoplasts.
  • Transmembrane (vacuolar) pathway: Movement across cell membranes through aquaporins. Water enters and exits cells sequentially. This pathway is the most controlled, allowing the cell to regulate water flow through aquaporin channels.

The Casparian strip in the endodermis forces water and minerals entering the stele to pass through the symplast pathway, providing a checkpoint for selective mineral uptake. This ensures that only desired ions enter the xylem.

Aquaporins

Aquaporins are integral membrane proteins that form water channels. They facilitate the rapid movement of water across cell membranes (up to 109 water molecules per second per channel). Aquaporins are regulated by phosphorylation, pH, and heavy metals. Some aquaporins also allow the passage of small solutes (glycerol, urea, CO2).

Significance of Transpiration

Transpiration is not merely a wasteful process; it serves several critical functions:

  • Transpiration pull: The primary force for water ascent in plants, driving water from roots to the tallest leaves.
  • Mineral transport: The flow of water carries dissolved minerals from roots to all parts of the plant.
  • Cooling: Evaporation of water from leaf surfaces dissipates heat, preventing thermal damage to photosynthetic tissues. A single corn plant can transpire up to 200 L of water per growing season, cooling the plant by several degrees.
  • Maintains turgor: Water loss is balanced by water uptake, maintaining cell turgidity necessary for growth and stomatal function.

Potometer — Measuring Transpiration Rate

The potometer (also called a transpirometer) is a device used to measure the rate of transpiration. There are two types:

  • Simple (bubble) potometer: Measures water uptake by a cut shoot. A bubble introduced into a capillary tube moves as water is taken up. The rate of bubble movement gives the transpiration rate.
  • Weight potometer: The whole set-up is weighed over time; the loss in weight equals water lost by transpiration.
Lab Technique — Potometer Usage
When using a potometer: (1) Cut the shoot underwater to prevent air embolism in xylem vessels. (2) Ensure the set-up is completely watertight. (3) Introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube. (4) Measure the distance the bubble moves per unit time. The rate can be compared under different conditions (light, dark, wind, fans, etc.) to study factors affecting transpiration.
Example 11 — Potometer Experiment
In a potometer experiment, the air bubble moves 4 cm in 10 minutes under normal conditions. When a fan is turned on at a low speed, the bubble moves 7 cm in 10 minutes. When a high-speed fan is directed at the leaf, the bubble moves only 2 cm in 10 minutes. Explain these observations.
Solution: Under normal conditions, baseline transpiration occurs. Low wind speed (from the fan) increases transpiration by removing the humid boundary layer of air around the leaf, steepening the diffusion gradient. This explains the increase from 4 to 7 cm. However, high wind speed causes stomatal closure as a protective response to prevent excessive water loss — the leaf conserves water by closing stomata (mediated by ABA signalling), reducing transpiration dramatically (2 cm in 10 minutes). This demonstrates the dual effect of wind on transpiration: moderate wind increases it, but very strong wind decreases it.

4. The oxygen evolved during photosynthesis comes from:
a) Water (H2O)
b) Carbon dioxide (CO2)
c) RuBP
d) Glucose
Solution: Option (a). The oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water during photolysis (2H2O → 4H+ + 4e− + O2), which occurs at the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC) of Photosystem II. This was conclusively demonstrated by Ruben and Kamen (1941) using isotopic labelling with 18O.
5. The RuBisCO enzyme is found in:
a) Cytoplasm
b) Thylakoid membrane
c) Stroma of chloroplast
d) Inner mitochondrial membrane
Solution: Option (c). RuBisCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) is located in the stroma of the chloroplast, where it catalyses the carboxylation of RuBP in the Calvin cycle. It is the most abundant protein on Earth, constituting 30–50% of soluble leaf protein in C3 plants.
6. Which of the following plant hormones is primarily responsible for cell division?
a) Auxin
b) Gibberellin
c) Cytokinin
d) Ethylene
Solution: Option (c). Cytokinins promote cytokinesis (cell division) in conjunction with auxins. They also promote shoot initiation in tissue culture, delay leaf senescence (Richmond-Lang effect), and mobilise nutrients. Kinetin (a synthetic cytokinin) and zeatin (natural) are commonly studied examples.
7. The cofactor required for nitrogenase enzyme is:
a) Calcium
b) Zinc
c) Molybdenum
d) Copper
Solution: Option (c). Nitrogenase is a Mo-Fe protein (molybdenum-iron). It catalyses N2 + 8H+ + 8e− + 16 ATP → 2NH3 + H2 + 16 ADP + 16 Pi. The enzyme is O2-sensitive and requires anaerobic conditions. In some bacteria, vanadium can substitute for molybdenum.
8. In the pressure flow hypothesis, phloem sap moves from:
a) High water potential to low water potential
b) High hydrostatic pressure to low hydrostatic pressure
c) Low solute concentration to high solute concentration
d) Sink to source
Solution: Option (b). The pressure flow hypothesis states that phloem sap flows from regions of high hydrostatic pressure (source: active sucrose loading → water entry → high pressure) to regions of low hydrostatic pressure (sink: sucrose unloading → water exit → low pressure). This pressure gradient drives bulk flow of sap through sieve tubes.

14. Detailed Study of Plant Hormone Interactions

Synergistic and Antagonistic Interactions

Plant hormones rarely act in isolation. Their interactions determine the final physiological response:

  • Auxin + Cytokinin: Synergistic in cell division. The ratio determines differentiation direction — high auxin:cytokinin favours root formation; low auxin:cytokinin favours shoot formation. This principle is used extensively in plant tissue culture for micropropagation.
  • Auxin + Gibberellin: Synergistic in stem elongation and fruit development. Both promote cell expansion, though through different mechanisms.
  • ABA + GA: Antagonistic in seed dormancy and germination. ABA promotes dormancy; GA breaks dormancy and promotes germination. The ABA:GA ratio determines the dormant state of seeds.
  • Ethylene + Auxin: Antagonistic in abscission. Auxin delays abscission; ethylene promotes it. As fruits mature, auxin levels decline and ethylene levels rise, triggering abscission.
  • Ethylene + GA: Antagonistic in sex expression in cucurbits. GA promotes male flower formation; ethylene promotes female flower formation.

15. Photomorphogenesis and Phytochrome

Photomorphogenesis refers to light-regulated plant development, independent of photosynthesis. The key photoreceptor involved is phytochrome, a chromoprotein discovered by Butler, Norris, Siegelman, and Hendricks (1959).

Properties of Phytochrome

  • Phytochrome is a biliprotein — consisting of a protein (apoprotein) and a chromophore (phytochromobilin, an open-chain tetrapyrrole).
  • Exists in two interconvertible forms:
    • Pr (inactive, λmax = 660 nm, red light): Synthesised in this form in the dark.
    • Pfr (active, λmax = 730 nm, far-red light): Formed when Pr absorbs red light.
  • Pfr is the biologically active form that triggers various photomorphogenic responses.
  • In the dark, Pfr slowly converts back to Pr (thermal reversion or dark reversion).

Photomorphogenic Responses Regulated by Phytochrome

  • Seed germination (photoblasty): Some seeds require red light (converting Pr → Pfr) to germinate (e.g., lettuce). These are positive photoblastic seeds. Far-red light reverses this.
  • Stem elongation and leaf expansion: In shade, the lower red:far-red ratio (due to canopy filtering) converts Pfr back to Pr, promoting stem elongation (shade avoidance response).
  • Chloroplast development: Light, mediated by phytochrome, is required for the differentiation of etioplasts (in dark-grown seedlings) into functional chloroplasts.
  • Flowering: Phytochrome mediates photoperiodic flowering. The critical night length measurement involves the conversion of Pfr to Pr in the dark.

16. Detailed Cellular Respiration — In Depth

Mitochondrial Structure in Relation to Function

  • Outer membrane: Permeable to small molecules (porins allow free diffusion of molecules up to 5,000 Da).
  • Intermembrane space: Accumulates H+ pumped from the matrix during electron transport. Site of high H+ concentration (low pH).
  • Inner membrane: Impermeable to most molecules. Folded into cristae to increase surface area. Contains ETC complexes and ATP synthase.
  • Matrix: Contains Krebs cycle enzymes, mitochondrial DNA (circular, maternally inherited), ribosomes (70S type).

Regulation of Respiration

Key regulatory enzymes and their control mechanisms:

  • Phosphofructokinase (PFK) — glycolysis: Inhibited by ATP and citrate; activated by ADP and AMP (feedback regulation).
  • Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex: Inhibited by NADH, acetyl-CoA, and ATP (product inhibition).
  • Isocitrate dehydrogenase — Krebs cycle: Inhibited by NADH and ATP; activated by ADP.
  • α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase: Inhibited by succinyl-CoA, NADH, and ATP.

This regulation ensures that respiration operates at a rate matching the cell’s energy demand — energy charge regulation (high ATP = low respiration; high ADP = high respiration).

The Pentose Phosphate Pathway (PPP)

Also called the hexose monophosphate shunt, the PPP is an alternative pathway for glucose oxidation that occurs in the cytoplasm:

  • Produces NADPH (not NADH) — important for reductive biosynthesis (fatty acids, nucleotides).
  • Produces ribose-5-phosphate — required for nucleotide and nucleic acid synthesis.
  • Does not produce ATP directly; its main products are reducing power (NADPH) and pentose sugars.
  • Active in tissues with high biosynthetic activity (e.g., meristems, developing seeds, adipose tissue in animals).
  • Can operate in the absence of glycolysis and is not affected by the same regulatory mechanisms.

17. Practical Applications of Plant Physiology in Agriculture

Improving Crop Yield

  • C4 engineering: Researchers are attempting to introduce the C4 pathway into C3 crops (like rice) to increase photosynthetic efficiency, reduce photorespiration, and improve water-use efficiency in a warming climate.
  • Hydroponics and aeroponics: Soil-less cultivation for efficient use of water and nutrients, particularly valuable in arid and urban environments.
  • Drought-tolerant crops: Understanding ABA signalling and stomatal regulation helps in breeding drought-resistant varieties.
  • Plant growth regulators in agriculture: Used to synchronise flowering, improve fruit set, regulate ripening, prevent lodging (GA inhibitors), and control weed growth.
  • Nitrogen fixation enhancement: Developing non-leguminous crops with nitrogen-fixing capabilities (through genetic engineering of symbiotic associations).

Post-Harvest Physiology

  • Ethylene management: Controlling ethylene levels in storage facilities can delay ripening and extend the shelf life of fruits and vegetables. 1-Methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) is an ethylene action inhibitor used commercially to maintain fruit quality.
  • Controlled atmosphere storage: Lowering O2 and elevating CO2 in storage environments reduces respiration rates and delays senescence.
  • Cold storage: Slowing metabolic processes through low-temperature storage. Knowledge of chilling injury and cold acclimation is critical for optimal storage.

18. Environmental Factors and Plant Distribution

Ecological Adaptations

  • Xerophytes: Adapted to arid conditions. Features include thick cuticle, sunken stomata, reduced leaf surface area (spines in cactus), succulent stems for water storage, deep root systems, and CAM photosynthesis.
  • Hydrophytes: Adapted to aquatic conditions. Features include thin cuticle (unnecessary), stomata on upper surface of floating leaves, reduced vascular tissue (water supports the plant), aerenchyma for buoyancy and gas exchange.
  • Halophytes: Adapted to saline soils. Features include salt glands to excrete excess salt, succulent leaves for dilution, and specialised ion transporters to maintain ionic balance.
Example 12 — Adaptation Analysis
A plant species found in deserts has thick fleshy stems, spines instead of leaves, a thick cuticle, and opens its stomata only at night. To which ecological group does it belong? Name the photosynthetic pathway it uses and explain how each feature aids survival.
Solution: This is a xerophyte (desert-adapted plant). It uses the CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) pathway. Adaptations: (1) Thick fleshy stems — water storage for dry periods. (2) Spines instead of leaves — reduce surface area for transpiration and deter herbivores. (3) Thick cuticle — minimises cuticular transpiration. (4) Night-time stomatal opening — reduces water loss by opening when temperatures are lower and humidity is higher; CO2 is fixed at night as malate and used during the day for photosynthesis (CAM).
