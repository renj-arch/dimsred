Carbohydrates

1.1 Monosaccharides and Their Structural Diversity

Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates and serve as the fundamental building blocks for more complex sugars. Their general formula CₙH₂ₙOₙ reflects a carbonyl group either as an aldehyde (aldoses) or a ketone (ketoses) together with multiple hydroxyl groups that create numerous stereoisomers. The presence of chiral centres gives rise to D‑ and L‑configurations, of which the D‑forms predominate in biological systems because enzymes have evolved to recognise them. This stereochemical specificity determines how monosaccharides are metabolised, transported, and incorporated into polymers.

Glucose, the most abundant monosaccharide, exists predominantly in a cyclic hemiacetal form (α‑ and β‑anomers) in aqueous solution. The interconversion between open‑chain and cyclic forms, known as mutarotation, influences the reactivity of the anomeric carbon and therefore the type of glycosidic bond that can be formed. Other important monosaccharides include fructose, a ketohexose that is sweeter than glucose, and ribose, a pentose essential for nucleotide structure. The diversity of monosaccharide structures underlies the variety of biological functions ranging from energy storage to cell‑surface recognition.

1.2 Disaccharides: Formation and Physiological Roles

Disaccharides arise when two monosaccharide units join through a glycosidic bond formed by a condensation reaction that eliminates a molecule of water. The nature of the bond — α‑1,4 in maltose, β‑1,4 in lactose, and α‑1,2 in sucrose — dictates the enzymatic specificity required for hydrolysis. Maltose, produced during starch digestion, is cleaved by maltase; lactose, the milk sugar, requires lactase, whose deficiency leads to lactose intolerance; sucrose, the transport sugar in plants, is hydrolysed by sucrase‑isomaltase in the intestinal brush border.

Because disaccharides are too large to cross membranes directly, their digestion to monosaccharides is a prerequisite for absorption. The rate of hydrolysis can become a limiting factor in nutrition, as seen in congenital sucrase‑isomaltase deficiency. Moreover, the presence of specific disaccharides in the diet influences gut microbiota composition, since certain bacteria possess the glycosidases needed to ferment them, producing short‑chain fatty acids that affect host metabolism.

1.3 Polysaccharides: Storage and Structural Functions

Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharide residues linked by glycosidic bonds, and their architecture determines whether they serve as energy reserves or structural components. Starch, the principal storage polysaccharide in plants, consists of amylose (linear α‑1,4‑linked glucose) and amylopectin (branched with α‑1,6 linkages). The branching increases the number of non‑reducing ends, allowing rapid mobilisation by amylases during germination or in the human digestive tract.

Glycogen, the animal counterpart of starch, is more highly branched, providing an even greater density of terminal glucose units for swift release during muscular activity or fasting. In contrast, cellulose is a linear β‑1,4‑linked glucose polymer that forms extensive hydrogen‑bonded microfibrils, giving plant cell walls tensile strength. Humans lack cellulase, so cellulose acts as dietary fibre, influencing gut transit time and cholesterol absorption. Chitin, a β‑1,4‑linked N‑acetylglucosamine polymer, provides rigidity to arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls, illustrating how a simple change in the monomer (addition of an acetylamino group) alters material properties.

1.4 Glycoconjugates and Cellular Recognition

When carbohydrates are covalently attached to proteins or lipids, they form glycoconjugates such as glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and glycolipids. The oligosaccharide chains on cell‑surface glycoproteins act as ligands for lectins, mediating cell‑cell adhesion, immune recognition, and pathogen attachment. For example, the ABO blood‑group antigens are terminal trisaccharides on red‑cell glycolipids; mismatched transfusion triggers agglutination because antibodies recognise these carbohydrate epitopes.

Proteoglycans, composed of a core protein bearing long glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains, create hydrated gels that resist compressive forces in cartilage and provide lubrication in synovial fluid. The negative charge of sulfate and carboxylate groups on GAGs attracts water, generating the swelling pressure essential for joint function. Alterations in glycosylation patterns are hallmarks of cancer progression, as aberrant sialylation can mask tumour antigens from immune surveillance, demonstrating the profound biological consequences of carbohydrate diversity.

Proteins

2.1 Amino Acid Chemistry and Peptide Bond Formation

Proteins are polymers of α‑amino acids linked by peptide bonds, each bond formed by a condensation reaction between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next, releasing water. The twenty standard amino acids differ in their side chains (R groups), which confer distinct chemical properties — hydrophobic, polar uncharged, acidic, or basic — that dictate folding and function. The peptide bond has partial double‑bond character due to resonance, restricting rotation and imposing a planar geometry that influences secondary structure propensity.

The sequence of amino acids, known as the primary structure, is encoded by the gene and determines all higher‑order structures. Post‑translational modifications such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, and proteolytic cleavage expand functional diversity without altering the genetic code. For instance, phosphorylation of serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues creates docking sites for signalling proteins, linking extracellular cues to intracellular responses.

2.2 Secondary Structure: α‑Helix and β‑Sheet

Local hydrogen bonding between backbone carbonyl oxygens and amide hydrogens gives rise to regular secondary structures. The α‑helix is a right‑handed coil stabilised by intra‑chain hydrogen bonds between residue i and i+4, producing 3.6 residues per turn and a macrodipole with a partial positive charge at the N‑terminus. Proline, lacking an amide hydrogen, disrupts helices and often introduces kinks. β‑Sheets form when extended strands align side‑by‑side, with hydrogen bonds between adjacent strands; they can be parallel or antiparallel, the latter being more stable due to optimal bond geometry.

The propensity of a given amino‑acid sequence to adopt helix or sheet conformations is encoded in its primary structure and can be predicted by algorithms such as Chou‑Fasman or GOR. Secondary structure elements serve as building blocks for tertiary folding, and their arrangement determines the protein’s overall topology. Mutations that replace a helix‑favoring residue with a β‑sheet‑favoring one can dramatically alter folding pathways, leading to misfolding diseases such as cystic fibrosis (ΔF508 in CFTR) or prion disorders.

2.3 Tertiary and Quaternary Structure

Tertiary structure describes the three‑dimensional arrangement of all atoms in a single polypeptide chain, stabilised by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bridges, and disulfide bonds between cysteine residues. The hydrophobic effect drives non‑polar side chains into the protein core, while polar residues remain solvent‑exposed, creating a specific folding funnel that guides the polypeptide to its native state. Molecular chaperones such as Hsp70 and GroEL/ES assist folding in the crowded cellular environment, preventing aggregation.

Quaternary structure arises when multiple folded subunits associate to form a functional oligomer. Hemoglobin, a tetramer of two α and two β globin chains, exemplifies cooperative oxygen binding: binding of O₂ to one subunit induces conformational changes that increase the affinity of the remaining subunits. This allosteric regulation is central to physiological oxygen transport. Disruption of subunit interfaces by mutations can cause diseases like sickle‑cell anemia, where a single valine substitution creates a hydrophobic patch that polymerises deoxy‑hemoglobin into fibers.

2.4 Protein Denaturation, Folding Diseases, and Engineering

Denaturation is the loss of native structure without cleavage of peptide bonds, caused by heat, extreme pH, organic solvents, or chaotropic agents. While some proteins can refold spontaneously (Anfinsen’s experiment with ribonuclease A), many require chaperone assistance; failure leads to aggregation into amyloid fibrils, implicated in Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and type‑2 diabetes. Understanding the energy landscape of folding has enabled protein engineering: directed evolution and rational design produce enzymes with altered specificity, stability, or catalytic rate for industrial and therapeutic applications.

Recombinant DNA technology allows the production of human proteins in microbial or mammalian hosts, but proper folding and post‑translational modification remain challenges. Fusion tags such as maltose‑binding protein or glutathione‑S‑transferase enhance solubility, while co‑expression of chaperones improves yield. The ability to design de novo proteins with novel folds expands the toolkit for synthetic biology, promising custom catalysts, biosensors, and nanomaterials.

Example Question
An enzyme follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics. In a series of assays the initial velocity (v₀) was measured at various substrate concentrations [S] as follows: [S] = 0.5 mM, v₀ = 12 µmol·min⁻¹; [S] = 1.0 mM, v₀ = 20 µmol·min⁻¹; [S] = 2.0 mM, v₀ = 28 µmol·min⁻¹; [S] = 5.0 mM, v₀ = 36 µmol·min⁻¹. Using a Lineweaver–Burk plot, determine the apparent Vmax and Km for this enzyme.
First convert each data point to its double‑reciprocal form: 1/[S] (mM⁻¹) and 1/v₀ (min·µmol⁻¹). The pairs are (2.0, 0.0833), (1.0, 0.0500), (0.5, 0.0357), (0.2, 0.0278). Plotting 1/v₀ versus 1/[S] yields a straight line. Linear regression gives a slope of approximately 0.028 min·mM·µmol⁻¹ and a y‑intercept of 0.025 min·µmol⁻¹. From the Lineweaver–Burk equation 1/v₀ = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax, the y‑intercept equals 1/Vmax, so Vmax = 1/0.025 = 40 µmol·min⁻¹. The slope equals Km/Vmax, thus Km = slope × Vmax = 0.028 × 40 = 1.12 mM. Therefore the enzyme exhibits an apparent Vmax of 40 µmol·min⁻¹ and a Km of about 1.1 mM.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question provides a small data set for a Lineweaver–Burk plot, calculate the double‑reciprocal values carefully; a single arithmetic error in 1/[S] or 1/v₀ will shift both slope and intercept, leading to incorrect Vmax and Km. Verify the linearity by checking that the points roughly fall on a straight line before performing regression.

Lipids

3.1 Fatty Acids: Saturation, Chain Length, and Nomenclature

Fatty acids are long‑chain carboxylic acids, typically containing 12 to 24 carbon atoms. Saturation refers to the presence or absence of carbon‑carbon double bonds; saturated fatty acids have only single bonds, giving them a straight conformation that packs tightly, while unsaturated fatty acids contain one (monounsaturated) or more (polyunsaturated) cis‑double bonds that introduce kinks, reducing packing efficiency and lowering melting points. The systematic nomenclature uses the Δ notation (e.g., Δ⁹‑octadecenoic acid for oleic acid) or the ω (omega) system counting from the methyl end (ω‑9 for oleic acid), which is nutritionally relevant because humans cannot synthesize ω‑3 and ω‑6 fatty acids de novo.

Chain length influences physical properties: short‑chain fatty acids (≤6 carbons) are liquid at room temperature and are rapidly oxidised for energy, whereas very‑long‑chain fatty acids (≥20 carbons) are components of sphingolipids and myelin. The degree of unsaturation also affects membrane fluidity; organisms adjust the fatty‑acid composition of phospholipids in response to temperature changes (homeoviscous adaptation), a principle exploited by cold‑water fish that enrich their membranes with polyunsaturated fatty acids.

3.2 Triacylglycerols: Energy Storage and Metabolism

Triacylglycerols (TAGs) consist of three fatty acids esterified to a glycerol backbone. They are the principal form of stored energy in animals and plants because the reduced carbon atoms yield more ATP per gram upon complete oxidation than carbohydrates. In adipocytes, TAGs are packaged into lipid droplets coated with perilipin proteins that regulate access by lipases. Hormone‑sensitive lipase (HSL) and adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) sequentially hydrolyse TAGs to free fatty acids and glycerol, which enter the bloodstream bound to albumin.

During fasting, the liver converts fatty acids into ketone bodies (acetoacetate, β‑hydroxybutyrate) that serve as alternative fuels for the brain and heart. Excessive TAG accumulation in non‑adipose tissues (ectopic fat) contributes to insulin resistance and lipotoxicity, underlying metabolic syndrome. Conversely, deficiencies in TAG synthesis enzymes such as diacylglycerol acyltransferase cause lipodystrophy, illustrating the tight physiological regulation of neutral lipid metabolism.

3.3 Phospholipids and Membrane Architecture

Phospholipids are amphipathic molecules with a glycerol backbone, two fatty‑acid chains, and a polar head group attached via a phosphate ester. The most abundant classes are phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylserine (PS), and phosphatidylinositol (PI). Their spontaneous assembly into bilayers in aqueous solution creates the fundamental barrier of cellular membranes, with hydrophobic tails sequestered from water and hydrophilic heads facing the cytosol and extracellular space.

The fluid mosaic model describes membranes as dynamic two‑dimensional fluids where proteins diffuse laterally. Lipid composition modulates fluidity: cholesterol intercalates between phospholipids, restricting motion at high temperatures and preventing excessive rigidity at low temperatures. Asymmetric distribution of phospholipids (e.g., PS enriched in the inner leaflet) is maintained by flippases, floppases, and scramblases; exposure of PS on the outer surface signals apoptosis. Lipid rafts, enriched in sphingolipids and cholesterol, serve as platforms for signal transduction.

3.4 Sterols, Eicosanoids, and Lipid Signalling

Sterols such as cholesterol are tetracyclic hydrocarbons with a hydroxyl group at C‑3 and a flexible side chain at C‑17. Cholesterol is a precursor for steroid hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, sex steroids), bile acids, and vitamin D. Its synthesis via the mevalonate pathway is tightly regulated by feedback inhibition of HMG‑CoA reductase, the target of statin drugs that lower plasma LDL cholesterol.

Eicosanoids are oxygenated derivatives of twenty‑carbon polyunsaturated fatty acids (mainly arachidonic acid) produced by cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase pathways. Prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes act as local hormones regulating inflammation, platelet aggregation, vascular tone, and gastric mucosal protection. Non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibit COX, reducing prostaglandin synthesis and thereby alleviating pain and fever but also risking gastric ulceration and renal impairment. The balance between pro‑inflammatory and pro‑resolving eicosanoids determines the outcome of immune responses.

Nucleic Acids

4.1 Nucleotide Chemistry and Polymerisation

Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides, each composed of a nitrogenous base (purine or pyrimidine), a pentose sugar (ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA), and one or more phosphate groups. The phosphodiester bond links the 3′‑hydroxyl of one sugar to the 5′‑phosphate of the next, creating a directional backbone with a 5′→3′ polarity. This polarity is essential for template‑directed synthesis, as polymerases add nucleotides only to the 3′‑OH of the growing strand.

The bases pair specifically: adenine with thymine (or uracil in RNA) via two hydrogen bonds, and guanine with cytosine via three hydrogen bonds. This complementarity underlies replication, transcription, and the formation of double‑helical structures. Modified bases such as 5‑methylcytosine in DNA and pseudouridine in RNA expand functional diversity, influencing gene expression and RNA stability.

4.2 DNA Structure, Topology, and Chromatin Organisation

The Watson‑Crick double helix adopts a right‑handed B‑form under physiological conditions, with a major groove and a minor groove that serve as recognition sites for proteins. The helical twist (~10.5 base pairs per turn) and base‑stacking interactions confer stability, while the negative charge of the phosphate backbone requires counter‑ions (Mg²⁺, polyamines) and histone proteins for compaction. In eukaryotes, DNA wraps around histone octamers to form nucleosomes, the basic units of chromatin.

Higher‑order folding creates a hierarchy: nucleosome arrays fold into a 30‑nm fiber, which further loops and attaches to a nuclear scaffold, forming topologically associating domains (TADs). Chromatin remodeling complexes and histone modifications (acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation) regulate accessibility of DNA to transcription factors and polymerases. Aberrant chromatin architecture is implicated in developmental disorders and cancer, where mutations in remodelers such as SWI/SNF alter gene expression programmes.

4.3 RNA Species: Coding, Regulatory, and Catalytic Roles

RNA molecules are single‑stranded but fold into complex secondary and tertiary structures through intramolecular base pairing. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries the coding sequence from DNA to ribosomes, with a 5′ cap and 3′ poly(A) tail that enhance stability and translation initiation. Transfer RNA (tRNA) adopts a cloverleaf secondary structure and an L‑shaped three‑dimensional form, positioning the anticodon loop and the amino‑acid acceptor stem for accurate codon‑anticodon pairing.

Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) constitutes the catalytic core of the ribosome, catalysing peptide‑bond formation (ribozymatic activity). Small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) participate in spliceosome assembly, while microRNAs (miRNAs) and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) guide the RNA‑induced silencing complex (RISC) to target mRNAs for degradation or translational repression. Long non‑coding RNAs (lncRNAs) scaffold chromatin‑modifying complexes and regulate nuclear architecture, illustrating the versatility of RNA beyond information transfer.

4.4 DNA Replication, Repair, and Recombination

DNA replication is semi‑conservative: each parental strand serves as a template for a new complementary strand. In bacteria, a single origin of replication (oriC) initiates bidirectional synthesis; in eukaryotes, multiple origins fire during S phase. The replisome includes helicase (unwinding), primase (RNA primer synthesis), DNA polymerase III (leading strand) and DNA polymerase I (lagging strand Okazaki fragment processing), and ligase (nick sealing). Proofreading 3′→5′ exonuclease activity of polymerases and mismatch repair correct replication errors, maintaining a fidelity of ~10⁻⁹ per base pair.

DNA damage from UV radiation, oxidative stress, and chemical agents is repaired by distinct pathways: nucleotide excision repair (NER) removes bulky lesions, base excision repair (BER) corrects small base modifications, and double‑strand break repair proceeds via homologous recombination (HR) or non‑homologous end joining (NHEJ). Defects in these pathways cause hereditary cancer syndromes such as xeroderma pigmentosum (NER), Lynch syndrome (mismatch repair), and BRCA1/2‑associated breast/ovarian cancer (HR). Programmed recombination during meiosis generates genetic diversity, while V(D)J recombination assembles immunoglobulin and T‑cell receptor genes in lymphocytes.

Enzyme Kinetics

5.1 Michaelis–Menten Model and Its Assumptions

The Michaelis–Menten equation v₀ = (Vmax [S])/(Km + [S]) describes the initial rate of an enzyme‑catalysed reaction as a function of substrate concentration. It assumes a rapid equilibrium or steady‑state between free enzyme (E), substrate (S), and the enzyme‑substrate complex (ES), with product formation being irreversible and much slower than the binding steps. The constant Km, the substrate concentration at half‑maximal velocity, reflects the affinity of the enzyme for its substrate under these conditions; a low Km indicates high affinity.

Vmax represents the maximal rate when all enzyme molecules are saturated with substrate, equal to kcat × [E]total, where kcat (turnover number) is the number of substrate molecules converted per enzyme per unit time. The ratio kcat/Km, known as the catalytic efficiency, combines binding and catalytic steps and is limited by diffusion (~10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹) for perfect enzymes such as triose‑phosphate isomerase. Deviations from Michaelis–Menten behaviour (e.g., sigmoidal kinetics) signal allosteric regulation or cooperativity.

5.2 Linear Transformations and Parameter Estimation

Because the hyperbolic Michaelis–Menten curve is difficult to linearise by eye, several double‑reciprocal (Lineweaver–Burk), Eadie–Hofstee, and Hanes–Woolf plots are used to estimate Km and Vmax from experimental data. The Lineweaver–Burk plot (1/v₀ versus 1/[S]) yields a straight line with slope Km/Vmax and intercept 1/Vmax, but it gives disproportionate weight to low‑[S] points where experimental error is largest. The Eadie–Hofstee plot (v₀ versus v₀/[S]) and Hanes–Woolf plot ([S]/v₀ versus [S]) distribute error more evenly, providing more reliable parameter estimates.

Modern practice favours non‑linear regression fitting of the original hyperbolic equation using software that weights data points by their variance. This approach avoids the distortion inherent in linear transformations and yields confidence intervals for Km and Vmax. Nevertheless, understanding the linear plots remains essential for interpreting classic literature and for quick manual checks during examinations.

5.3 Enzyme Inhibition: Competitive, Non‑competitive, and Uncompetitive

Inhibitors reduce the catalytic rate by binding to the enzyme. Competitive inhibitors resemble the substrate and bind reversibly to the active site, increasing the apparent Km while leaving Vmax unchanged; they can be overcome by raising substrate concentration. Non‑competitive inhibitors bind to a distinct site on both free enzyme and ES complex, decreasing Vmax without affecting Km, because substrate binding does not prevent inhibitor binding. Uncompetitive inhibitors bind only to the ES complex, lowering both Vmax and Km proportionally, a pattern often seen with multisubstrate enzymes where the inhibitor mimics a reaction intermediate.

Mixed inhibition combines features of non‑competitive and uncompetitive types, with the inhibitor binding to both E and ES but with different affinities, altering both Km and Vmax in a non‑proportional manner. The inhibition constant Ki quantifies inhibitor potency; for competitive inhibition Ki = [I] at which Km doubles. Irreversible inhibitors form covalent bonds with active‑site residues (e.g., aspirin acetylates COX), permanently inactivating the enzyme until new protein is synthesised. Distinguishing inhibition modes experimentally requires measuring initial rates at several substrate and inhibitor concentrations and analysing the pattern of Lineweaver–Burk line intersections.

5.4 Allosteric Regulation, Cooperativity, and Metabolic Control

Allosteric enzymes possess regulatory sites distinct from the catalytic site; binding of effectors (activators or inhibitors) induces conformational changes that modulate activity. Positive cooperativity, exemplified by hemoglobin and aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase), yields a sigmoidal v₀ versus [S] curve described by the Hill equation v₀ = Vmax [S]ⁿ/(K₀.₅ⁿ + [S]ⁿ), where the Hill coefficient n > 1 indicates the degree of cooperativity. Negative cooperativity (n < 1) is rarer but occurs in some kinases.

Feedback inhibition, where the end product of a pathway inhibits an early enzyme (e.g., CTP inhibiting ATCase), provides rapid metabolic control without altering enzyme concentration. Covalent modification (phosphorylation, acetylation) and proteolytic activation (zymogen cleavage) add further regulatory layers. The concept of metabolic control analysis quantifies how changes in each enzyme’s activity affect pathway flux, revealing that control is often distributed rather than residing in a single “rate‑limiting” step. Understanding these principles enables rational manipulation of metabolic pathways in biotechnology and drug design.
