1. Cell Theory & Origin of Life

Historical Milestones

The study of cells began with the invention of the microscope. Robert Hooke in 1665 first observed cork cells under a compound microscope and coined the term "cell" from the Latin word "cellula" meaning small room. He observed the honeycomb-like structure of dead cork cells in his book Micrographia. However, what Hooke observed were actually empty cell walls, not living cells.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch scientist, improved microscope lenses and became the first person to observe living cells. In 1674, he described protozoa and bacteria, which he called "animalcules". He observed red blood cells, sperm cells, and various microorganisms, establishing that a hidden world of microscopic life exists. His observations were fundamental in establishing microbiology.

The actual formulation of cell theory occurred nearly two centuries later. Matthias Schleiden, a German botanist, concluded in 1838 that all plants are composed of cells. Simultaneously, Theodor Schwann, a German zoologist, extended this to animals in 1839. Together they proposed that cells are the basic building blocks of all living organisms. Their work marked the birth of unified cell theory and is one of the most important conceptual frameworks in biology.

Rudolf Virchow, a German physician, made the crucial addition in 1855. He proposed "Omnis cellula e cellula" meaning all cells arise from pre-existing cells. This rejected spontaneous generation and established that cell division is the mechanism by which new cells form. Virchow completed the classical cell theory, and his principle remains fundamental to modern biology.

Key Scientists and Their Contributions

Robert Hooke (1665): First to observe and name cells. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1674): First to observe living cells. Matthias Schleiden (1838): Plants are made of cells. Theodor Schwann (1839): Animals are made of cells. Rudolf Virchow (1855): Cells arise from pre-existing cells. These five scientists are the pillars of modern cell biology.

Postulates of Modern Cell Theory

Modern cell theory incorporates several key postulates beyond the classical formulation. First, all living organisms are composed of one or more cells. This includes unicellular organisms like bacteria and amoeba as well as multicellular organisms like humans and trees. The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of life, meaning all physiological processes ultimately occur at the cellular level. Second, all cells arise from pre-existing cells through cell division, as stated by Virchow.

Third, the cell contains hereditary information (DNA) passed from parent to daughter cells during division. DNA carries genetic instructions for structure, function, and development. The flow follows the central dogma: DNA to RNA to protein. All cells use DNA as genetic material except some viruses that use RNA. Energy flow occurs through metabolic pathways like glycolysis, Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.

Fourth, all cells have similar chemical composition: water, carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and inorganic ions. The metabolic reactions are remarkably similar across all life forms, reflecting common evolutionary origin. Fifth, organism activity depends on combined cellular activities. In multicellular organisms, cells specialize into tissues and organs for specific functions.

Exceptions to Cell Theory

Viruses are acellular entities composed of DNA or RNA surrounded by a protein coat (capsid). They cannot reproduce independently and must infect host cells. They lack cellular organization and metabolism and occupy a gray area between living and non-living. Viroids consist only of short circular single-stranded RNA without any protein coat. Discovered by Theodor Diener in 1971, they cause plant diseases like potato spindle tuber. Prions are infectious protein particles causing neurodegenerative diseases like mad cow disease (BSE) and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, containing no nucleic acid.

Additionally, coenocytic fungi have multinucleate cells without compartmentalization. Skeletal muscle cells form syncytia with multiple nuclei. Sieve tube elements in plants lose nuclei at maturity. The giant alga Acetabularia grows to several centimeters as a single cell, challenging the idea that cells must be microscopic.

2. Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells are the simplest and most ancient cell type, belonging to domains Bacteria and Archaea. The name comes from Greek: "pro" meaning before and "karyon" meaning nucleus. Prokaryotes lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Their genetic material is concentrated in the nucleoid region, not enclosed by a nuclear membrane. DNA is typically circular and not associated with histone proteins. Prokaryotes are smaller than eukaryotes, ranging from 0.5 to 5 micrometers. They reproduce by binary fission.

The cell wall is present in most prokaryotes and provides structural support. In bacteria, the cell wall is composed of peptidoglycan (murein), a unique polymer of sugars and amino acids. Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes (50S + 30S subunits), which are targets for antibiotics like tetracycline and streptomycin that inhibit protein synthesis without affecting eukaryotic 80S ribosomes.

Bacterial Cell Structure

Bacteria have several characteristic structures. The cell envelope consists of cell wall, cell membrane, and sometimes an outer capsule. The capsule (glycocalyx) is a sticky polysaccharide layer that protects against phagocytosis and aids attachment. Flagella rotate like propellers powered by proton motive force. Pili are hair-like structures for attachment; sex pili are involved in conjugation (bacterial mating). Mesosomes are plasma membrane invaginations involved in cell division and respiration.

The bacterial chromosome is a single circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid region. Plasmids are small circular extra-chromosomal DNA carrying non-essential genes like antibiotic resistance. Bacterial ribosomes (70S) are targets for many antibiotics. Endospores are highly resistant dormant structures formed by some bacteria (e.g., Bacillus, Clostridium) under unfavorable conditions, capable of surviving extreme heat, radiation, and chemicals.

Archaea: The Third Domain

Archaea were once classified as bacteria but are now recognized as a distinct domain. They share features with bacteria (prokaryotic organization, circular DNA) but have unique characteristics. Their cell walls lack peptidoglycan and contain pseudopeptidoglycan or other polysaccharides. Archaeal membrane lipids have ether linkages instead of ester linkages. Many archaea are extremophiles: thermophiles (hot springs), halophiles (salt lakes), and acidophiles (acidic environments).

Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells are structurally more complex. "Eukaryote" means "true nucleus" from Greek "eu" (true) and "karyon" (nucleus). They contain numerous membrane-bound organelles: nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and peroxisomes. They are larger than prokaryotes, typically 10-100 micrometers. Eukaryotic DNA is linear and organized into multiple chromosomes complexed with histone proteins to form chromatin. Eukaryotes have 80S ribosomes (60S + 40S).

Animal Cells

Animal cells lack cell walls and chloroplasts. They are enclosed by a flexible plasma membrane. Animal cells contain centrioles for organizing spindle fibers during cell division. They have various organelles including mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, and peroxisomes. Energy is stored as glycogen. Shape varies widely from small red blood cells (7 micrometers) to nerve cells over a meter long.

Plant Cells

Plant cells have a rigid cell wall of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin providing structural support. They contain chloroplasts for photosynthesis with chlorophyll and other pigments. A large central vacuole surrounded by the tonoplast maintains turgor pressure and stores nutrients and waste. Plant cells lack centrioles and use microtubule organizing centers. Energy is stored as starch. Plasmodesmata are cytoplasmic channels connecting adjacent plant cells.

3. Cell Membrane & Transport

Fluid Mosaic Model

The fluid mosaic model was proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972. The plasma membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins that can move laterally. "Fluid" refers to lateral diffusion of lipids and proteins; "mosaic" refers to the pattern of proteins scattered through the lipid bilayer. This dynamic structure allows flexibility, self-sealing, and diverse functions.

Membrane Components

Phospholipids are amphipathic: hydrophilic heads (phosphate group) face outward, hydrophobic tails (fatty acid chains) face inward. Cholesterol in animal membranes provides stability by preventing excessive fluidity at high temperatures and solidification at low temperatures. Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer and function as channels, carriers, enzymes, and receptors. Peripheral proteins are attached to the surface and involved in signaling and cell shape.

Glycoproteins and glycolipids have carbohydrate chains for cell-cell recognition, adhesion, and immune function. Membrane asymmetry means outer and inner leaflets have different lipid and protein compositions. The glycocalyx is the carbohydrate-rich zone on the cell surface important for protection and recognition.

Functions of the Plasma Membrane

The plasma membrane acts as a selective permeability barrier, facilitates cell-cell recognition via glycoproteins, enables intercellular communication through receptors, anchors the cytoskeleton, maintains cell shape, and provides a surface for enzymatic reactions. It also plays a crucial role in signal transduction, allowing cells to respond to external stimuli.

Passive Transport

Passive transport moves substances without energy expenditure, driven by concentration gradients. Simple diffusion moves small non-polar molecules (O2, CO2, N2) directly through the lipid bilayer. Rate depends on concentration gradient, temperature, molecule size, and surface area. Small polar molecules like water and ethanol diffuse slowly; large polar molecules like glucose and ions cannot diffuse without assistance.

Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion uses specific transport proteins without energy. Carrier proteins (transporters) bind specific molecules and change conformation (e.g., GLUT transporters for glucose, amino acid transporters). Channel proteins form hydrophilic pores for ions (ion channels are often gated by chemical, electrical, or mechanical signals). Aquaporins are specialized water channels facilitating rapid osmosis. Facilitated diffusion is saturable and exhibits specificity.

Osmosis

Osmosis is water diffusion across a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves from lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration. Tonicity describes relative solute concentrations: hypotonic (water enters, cell swells), hypertonic (water leaves, cell shrinks/crenates), isotonic (no net movement). In plant cells, turgor pressure develops when water enters the cell against the cell wall. Plasmolysis occurs when plant cells lose water in hypertonic solutions.

Active Transport

Active transport moves substances against their gradient and requires ATP. Primary active transport directly uses ATP. The Na+/K+ ATPase pump is the classic example: it pumps 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per ATP hydrolyzed, creating electrochemical gradients essential for nerve impulses, nutrient uptake, and cell volume regulation. The proton pump (H+ ATPase) in plant and fungal membranes creates electrochemical gradients for secondary transport.

Secondary active transport uses the electrochemical gradient established by primary transport. Symport (co-transport) moves substances in the same direction (e.g., Na+-glucose symporter uses the Na+ gradient to import glucose). Antiport (counter-transport) moves substances in opposite directions (e.g., Na+-Ca2+ exchanger pumps Ca2+ out using Na+ influx).

Bulk Transport

Bulk transport moves large molecules and particles via vesicles. Endocytosis brings material in by membrane invagination: phagocytosis (cell eating, engulfs large particles), pinocytosis (cell drinking, takes in fluids and solutes), and receptor-mediated endocytosis (specific, clathrin-coated pits internalize receptor-bound molecules like LDL cholesterol). Exocytosis releases materials by fusing vesicles with the plasma membrane, important for hormone secretion, neurotransmitter release, and membrane protein insertion. The SNARE protein complex mediates vesicle docking and fusion.

4. Cell Wall

Plant Cell Wall

The plant cell wall is a rigid structure surrounding the plasma membrane that provides mechanical strength and protection. It determines cell shape and prevents over-expansion from water uptake. The wall is permeable to water and dissolved substances. Cellulose is the primary component, a polysaccharide of beta-1,4-linked glucose units forming long microfibrils with high tensile strength.

Primary Cell Wall

The primary cell wall is deposited during cell growth. It is thin and flexible, allowing expansion. Composition includes cellulose microfibrils, hemicellulose (cross-linking glycans), pectin (gelatinous polysaccharide for hydration and adhesion), and proteins (expansins, extensins). Pectin forms the middle lamella that cements adjacent cells. The primary wall is synthesized by the growing protoplast.

Secondary Cell Wall

The secondary cell wall is deposited inside the primary wall after growth ceases. It is thicker and more rigid with significant lignin content, a complex polymer providing waterproofing and compressive strength. Lignin is responsible for woodiness. Other components include suberin (waterproofing in cork cells) and cutin (cuticle of leaves). Multiple layers (S1, S2, S3) have alternating microfibril orientations for maximum strength.

Plasmodesmata

Plasmodesmata are microscopic channels traversing cell walls of adjacent plant cells, enabling communication and transport. They are lined by the plasma membrane and contain a desmotubule derived from ER. They allow passage of water, nutrients, signaling molecules, and small proteins. The symplastic pathway uses plasmodesmata for intercellular transport, while the apoplastic pathway uses the cell wall continuum.

Bacterial Cell Wall

The bacterial cell wall is composed of peptidoglycan (murein), a unique polymer of N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) cross-linked by peptide chains. It maintains shape and protects against osmotic lysis. Cell wall structure forms the basis for Gram staining.

Gram-Positive Bacteria

Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer (20-80 nm) retaining crystal violet stain (purple). They contain teichoic acids (glycerol or ribitol phosphate polymers) for structural support and antigenicity. No outer membrane. Examples: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Bacillus subtilis. More susceptible to penicillin that inhibits peptidoglycan synthesis.

Gram-Negative Bacteria

Gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer (2-7 nm) and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharides (LPS). They appear pink/red after Gram staining (safranin counterstain). The outer membrane provides antibiotic protection and contains porins for small molecule passage. The periplasmic space lies between inner and outer membranes. Examples: Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhi, Pseudomonas aeruginosa. LPS (endotoxin) can cause severe immune responses.

Fungal Cell Wall

Fungal cell walls contain chitin, a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine with beta-1,4 linkages, as the primary structural component. Additional components include glucans (beta-glucans) and mannoproteins. Chitin provides strength and is a target for antifungal drugs like echinocandins. Fungal cell walls are important for maintaining shape and host-pathogen interactions.

5. Endomembrane System

Endoplasmic Reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is an extensive network of membrane-enclosed tubules and sacs (cisternae) extending from the nuclear envelope. It is the largest organelle in many cells, accounting for over half of total membrane content. The ER lumen is a continuous compartment. The ER is divided into rough ER (RER, with ribosomes) and smooth ER (SER, without ribosomes).

Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum

RER has ribosomes on its cytoplasmic surface giving a rough appearance. It is involved in protein synthesis and processing. Proteins destined for secretion, membrane insertion, or lysosomes are synthesized by RER ribosomes and translocated into the lumen. Proteins undergo folding with chaperones and post-translational modifications: glycosylation (addition of oligosaccharides) and disulfide bond formation. RER is abundant in cells specialized for secretion (plasma cells for antibodies, pancreatic acinar cells for digestive enzymes).

Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum

SER lacks ribosomes and appears smooth. Functions vary by cell type: lipid and steroid synthesis (cholesterol, phospholipids, steroid hormones), carbohydrate metabolism, drug detoxification (cytochrome P450 enzymes), and Ca2+ storage and release. In muscle cells, specialized SER (sarcoplasmic reticulum) stores Ca2+ for contraction. SER is abundant in liver cells, adipocytes, and steroid-producing cells.

Golgi Apparatus

Discovered by Camillo Golgi in 1898, the Golgi apparatus consists of stacks of flattened cisternae (typically 4-8 per stack). It has distinct polarity: the cis face near the ER receives transport vesicles, the trans face near the plasma membrane releases modified proteins in secretory vesicles. The medial cisternae lie between. Proteins are processed (further glycosylation, phosphorylation, sulfation) as they move through cisternae.

The Golgi sorts proteins into different vesicles: secretory vesicles for exocytosis, lysosomal vesicles for delivery to lysosomes, and membrane vesicles for plasma membrane insertion. The cisternal maturation model proposes cisternae progress from cis to trans over time. The Golgi also synthesizes complex polysaccharides like pectin and hemicellulose in plant cells.

Lysosomes

Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles containing hydrolytic enzymes (acid hydrolases) including proteases, nucleases, lipases, glycosidases, and phosphatases. The lumen has acidic pH of about 5, maintained by a vacuolar H+ ATPase proton pump. The membrane is protected by heavily glycosylated proteins preventing self-digestion. Lysosomes participate in phagocytosis (fusion with phagosomes), autophagy (degradation of damaged organelles), and autolysis (self-digestion after cell death). Genetic defects cause lysosomal storage diseases like Tay-Sachs (hexosaminidase A deficiency), Gaucher, and Pompe diseases.

Vacuoles

In plant cells, a large central vacuole occupies up to 90% of cell volume, surrounded by the tonoplast membrane. It maintains turgor pressure essential for plant rigidity, stores water, nutrients, ions, pigments (anthocyanins for flower color), and defensive compounds. When the vacuole loses water, the plant wilts. In animal cells, vacuoles are smaller: food vacuoles contain ingested material; contractile vacuoles in freshwater protists expel excess water. Vacuoles are part of the endomembrane system derived from ER and Golgi.

Organelles Excluded from Endomembrane System

Mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes are NOT part of the endomembrane system. They are not connected to ER or Golgi and do not receive vesicles from them. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes (semi-autonomous). Peroxisomes contain oxidative enzymes for fatty acid oxidation and H2O2 detoxification.

6. Mitochondria & Plastids

Mitochondria: The Powerhouse

Mitochondria are double-membrane organelles responsible for ATP production through aerobic respiration. They are found in almost all eukaryotic cells. Number varies by energy demand: liver cells have about 2000 mitochondria, muscle cells and sperm cells have even more. Red blood cells lack mitochondria. The endosymbiotic theory proposes mitochondria evolved from aerobic bacteria engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells.

Structure

The outer membrane is smooth with porin channels allowing free passage of molecules under 5000 daltons. The inner membrane is highly folded into cristae, greatly increasing surface area for ATP production. The intermembrane space is between the membranes. The matrix is the fluid enclosed by the inner membrane, containing enzymes for the Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA (circular, 16.6 kb in humans), 70S ribosomes, tRNAs, and metabolic enzymes.

The inner membrane contains the electron transport chain (ETC Complex I-IV) and ATP synthase (Complex V, F0-F1 particles). The ETC creates a proton gradient driving ATP synthesis via chemiosmosis (Peter Mitchell theory). The F0 portion is embedded in the membrane; the F1 portion projects into the matrix and catalyzes ATP synthesis. Each ATP synthase produces about 100 ATP/second.

Semi-Autonomous Nature

Mitochondria have their own DNA (circular, like bacteria), 70S ribosomes, and replicate independently by binary fission. Most mitochondrial proteins (about 99%) are nuclear-encoded and imported; only 13 proteins are encoded by mitochondrial DNA in humans. Mitochondrial inheritance is maternal as sperm mitochondria are degraded after fertilization. Evidence for endosymbiotic theory includes: circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membrane (inner = original bacterial membrane), and independent replication.

Plastids

Plastids are membrane-bound organelles in plant cells and some protists, derived from endosymbiotic cyanobacteria. Like mitochondria, they are semi-autonomous with their own DNA and ribosomes. Plastids differentiate from proplastids (precursor plastids in meristematic cells). Three main types based on pigment and function: chloroplasts, chromoplasts, and leucoplasts.

Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are sites of photosynthesis, converting light energy to chemical energy. They contain chlorophyll (green) and carotenoid pigments. The double membrane encloses an internal membrane system of thylakoids stacked into grana. The thylakoid membrane contains chlorophyll and ETC for light-dependent reactions. The stroma is the fluid around thylakoids containing Calvin cycle enzymes, circular DNA (120-160 kb), and 70S ribosomes. Light reactions produce ATP and NADPH; Calvin cycle fixes CO2 into glucose. Photolysis of water releases O2.

Chromoplasts and Leucoplasts

Chromoplasts contain carotenoid pigments (beta-carotene, lycopene) giving yellow, orange, and red colors to fruits, flowers, and autumn leaves. They develop from chloroplasts during fruit ripening. Leucoplasts are colorless storage plastids in non-photosynthetic tissues (roots, seeds, tubers). Types: amyloplasts (starch storage in potato tubers), elaioplasts (oil storage in seeds), and aleuroplasts (protein storage in seeds). Amyloplasts also function in gravity sensing (geotropism).

7. Nucleus & Chromatin

Nuclear Envelope

The nucleus is the largest organelle in most eukaryotic cells, containing genetic material (DNA). It is enclosed by the nuclear envelope, a double membrane separating nuclear contents from cytoplasm. The outer membrane is continuous with ER and studded with ribosomes. The inner membrane has attachment sites for the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of lamin intermediate filaments providing structural support. The perinuclear space lies between the membranes and is continuous with the ER lumen.

Nuclear Pores

Nuclear pores are large protein complexes (nuclear pore complex, NPC) perforating the nuclear envelope. NPC has a molecular weight of about 125 million daltons, composed of about 30 nucleoporins. It has eightfold symmetry with a 9 nm central channel. Small molecules under 40 kDa diffuse passively; larger molecules require active transport with nuclear localization signals (NLS) or nuclear export signals (NES) and transport receptors (importins, exportins). The Ran GTPase system provides energy and directionality for transport. mRNA is exported, ribosomal subunits are exported, and nuclear proteins (histones, transcription factors) are imported.

Chromatin Structure

Chromatin is the DNA-protein complex in the nucleus. The nucleosome is the basic unit: 146 bp DNA wrapped around a histone octamer (two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4). Histone H1 (linker histone) binds between nucleosomes. This packaging condenses DNA about 10,000-fold, fitting 2 meters of DNA into a 10-micrometer nucleus.

Euchromatin vs Heterochromatin

Euchromatin is loosely packed, transcriptionally active chromatin appearing light in electron micrographs. Genes are accessible to transcription factors. Heterochromatin is tightly packed, transcriptionally inactive chromatin appearing dark. Constitutive heterochromatin is permanently condensed (centromeres, telomeres). Facultative heterochromatin is conditionally condensed and can switch between active and inactive states (e.g., inactive X chromosome/Barr body in female mammals).

Nucleosome Model

DNA winds around histone octamers in 1.65 left-handed superhelical turns, forming "beads on a string." Nucleosomes further coil into a 30 nm fiber (solenoid) with histone H1. The 30 nm fiber organizes into loop domains attached to the nuclear scaffold. During cell division, chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes with two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The centromere contains specialized histone CENP-A essential for kinetochore assembly.

Nucleolus

The nucleolus is the largest nuclear subcompartment, site of rRNA synthesis and ribosome assembly. It is not membrane-bound and forms around nucleolar organizer regions (NORs) containing rRNA genes on specific chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22 in humans). Three regions: fibrillar center (FC, rRNA genes), dense fibrillar component (DFC, rRNA transcription/processing), and granular component (GC, ribosome assembly). The nucleolus transcribes 45S pre-rRNA, processes it into 28S, 18S, and 5.8S rRNA, and assembles ribosomal subunits.

Nucleoplasm

Nucleoplasm is the semi-fluid matrix within the nucleus containing nucleotides, enzymes (DNA/RNA polymerases, topoisomerases, helicases), transcription factors, splicing factors, and signaling molecules. It provides the environment for DNA replication, transcription, RNA processing (capping, splicing, polyadenylation), and DNA repair. The nuclear matrix provides structural organization and attachment sites for chromatin loops.

Inactive X chromosome in female mammals is:

Solution: The inactive X chromosome (Barr body) is facultative heterochromatin, conditionally condensed and transcriptionally inactive. It can be reactivated in germ cells. Constitutive heterochromatin is permanently condensed (centromeres, telomeres). Hence option (c).

8. Ribosomes

Structure and Composition

Ribosomes are complex molecular machines of rRNA and proteins, the site of protein synthesis. Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S with large 50S (23S rRNA, 5S rRNA, 34 proteins) and small 30S (16S rRNA, 21 proteins) subunits. Eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S with large 60S (28S, 5.8S, 5S rRNA, 49 proteins) and small 40S (18S rRNA, 33 proteins) subunits. The Svedberg (S) unit is not additive: 50S + 30S = 70S due to shape-dependent sedimentation.

Functions

Ribosomes translate mRNA into polypeptides. The small subunit binds mRNA and ensures codon-anticodon accuracy. The large subunit catalyzes peptide bond formation via peptidyl transferase activity (a ribozyme function of 23S/28S rRNA). Three tRNA binding sites: A site (aminoacyl-tRNA entry), P site (peptidyl-tRNA), E site (exit). Free ribosomes synthesize proteins for cytoplasmic use; bound ribosomes (on RER) synthesize proteins for secretion or membrane insertion. Signal recognition particle (SRP) directs ribosomes to ER when the nascent polypeptide has an ER signal sequence.

Polysomes

Polysomes (polyribosomes) are clusters of multiple ribosomes translating the same mRNA simultaneously, allowing efficient production of multiple identical protein copies. Ribosomes are spaced 80-100 nucleotides apart. Polysomes can be free or membrane-bound and appear as helical arrays under electron microscopy.

9. Cytoskeleton

Microtubules

Microtubules are the largest cytoskeletal component (25 nm diameter, hollow center), composed of alpha- and beta-tubulin heterodimers polymerizing into 13 protofilaments. They exhibit polarity: plus end (beta-tubulin, faster growing) and minus end (alpha-tubulin, slower growing). Dynamic instability allows rapid assembly and disassembly. Microtubules provide structural support, serve as tracks for motor proteins (kinesin moves to plus end, dynein to minus end), form cilia and flagella (9+2 arrangement), and organize the mitotic spindle. The centrosome (microtubule organizing center) contains centrioles with 9+0 arrangement.

Cilia and Flagella

Cilia and flagella have a 9+2 microtubule arrangement: nine outer doublets surrounding two central singlets. Outer doublets have A and B tubules with dynein arms. Dynein motor protein causes doublet sliding, producing bending. The basal body has 9+0 arrangement (triplet microtubules, identical to centrioles). Cilia are short, numerous, and beat in coordinated waves (respiratory tract mucus clearance, oviduct egg transport). Flagella are long (1-2 per cell) for motility (sperm). Kartagener syndrome (defective dynein) causes immotile cilia, leading to respiratory infections and infertility.

Microfilaments

Microfilaments (actin filaments, 7 nm) are composed of globular actin (G-actin) monomers polymerizing into helical F-actin filaments. ATP regulates polymerization. Actin has polarity: barbed end (plus, faster growing) and pointed end (minus). Functions include mechanical support, cell motility (lamellipodia, filopodia), cytokinesis (contractile ring with myosin II), and muscle contraction (sliding filament mechanism with myosin). Microfilaments form the core of microvilli in intestinal epithelium, increasing absorptive surface area.

Intermediate Filaments

Intermediate filaments (10 nm) are rope-like fibers providing mechanical strength. Unlike microtubules and microfilaments, they lack polarity and motor proteins. They are composed of diverse tissue-specific proteins: keratin (epithelial cells), vimentin (mesenchymal cells), desmin (muscle cells), neurofilaments (neurons), and nuclear lamins (nuclear lamina). Keratin mutations cause skin blistering diseases like epidermolysis bullosa simplex. The nuclear lamina supports nuclear structure and organizes chromatin.

10. Cell Cycle

Interphase

The cell cycle is the ordered sequence of events by which a cell duplicates and divides. Interphase occupies about 90% of the cycle and includes G1 (Gap 1), S (Synthesis), and G2 (Gap 2). During interphase, the cell grows, replicates DNA, and prepares for division. Duration varies: yeast (90 minutes), human liver cells (days to years).

G1 Phase

G1 is the first stage after cell division. The cell grows in size, synthesizes proteins, and produces organelles. This is the most variable phase. Cells not dividing enter G0 from G1. The G1/S checkpoint (restriction point) determines whether the cell commits to division. If conditions are unfavorable (insufficient nutrients, DNA damage, lack of growth signals), the cell halts and may enter G0.

S Phase

DNA replication occurs in S phase. Each chromosome replicates into two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. Replication is semi-conservative: each daughter DNA has one parent strand and one new strand. Replication initiates at multiple origins. Histones are synthesized to package new DNA. At the end of S phase, DNA content doubles from 2C to 4C.

G2 Phase

G2 is the gap between DNA replication and mitosis. The cell continues growing and synthesizes proteins needed for division (tubulin, cyclin B). The G2/M checkpoint ensures DNA replication is complete and undamaged. p53 plays crucial roles at G1/S and G2/M checkpoints by activating DNA repair or initiating apoptosis if damage is irreparable.

Cell Cycle Checkpoints

Checkpoints monitor cell cycle fidelity. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and cyclins regulate progression: cyclin D-CDK4/6 (G1), cyclin E-CDK2 (G1/S), cyclin A-CDK2 (S), cyclin B-CDK1/MPF (G2/M). The G1/S checkpoint checks DNA damage and nutrients. The G2/M checkpoint checks DNA replication completion. The M checkpoint (spindle assembly checkpoint/SAC) monitors chromosome attachment to spindle fibers. The retinoblastoma protein (Rb) regulates G1/S by inhibiting E2F transcription factors. SAC proteins (Mad2, BubR1) inhibit APC/C until all chromosomes are attached.

G0 Phase

G0 is a reversible quiescent state where cells are metabolically active but not dividing. Cells enter G0 from G1 under unfavorable conditions. Some cells remain permanently in G0 (neurons, cardiac muscle). Others can be stimulated to re-enter (liver cells). Stem cells cycle slowly with G0 periods. Cancer cells often have defective G1/S regulation and fail to enter G0.

11. Mitosis (Equational Division)

Overview

Mitosis results in two daughter nuclei with identical chromosome numbers (2n to 2n). It occurs in somatic cells for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction. Total duration is about 1-2 hours. Stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase (PMAT).

Prophase

Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes (two sister chromatids joined at centromere). The nucleolus disappears. The nuclear envelope fragments. Centrosomes move to opposite poles, organizing the mitotic spindle. Spindle fibers (astral, kinetochore, polar microtubules) begin extending from centrosomes. Prophase occupies about 40-50% of mitotic time.

Prometaphase

The nuclear envelope completely breaks down. Kinetochores (protein complexes at centromeres) attach to spindle microtubules. Each chromosome attaches to fibers from both poles. Chromosomes begin moving toward the equator (congression).

Metaphase

Chromosomes align at the metaphase plate (equatorial plane), maximally condensed. Each sister chromatid is attached to opposite poles via kinetochores, establishing tension. The spindle assembly checkpoint monitors attachment and tension. Metaphase is the best stage for chromosome counting and morphology study.

Anaphase

The shortest stage. APC/C becomes active, targeting securin for degradation. Separase cleaves cohesin, allowing sister chromatid separation. Anaphase A: chromatids move to poles as kinetochore microtubules shorten. Anaphase B: spindle poles move apart. Each chromatid is now called a daughter chromosome.

Telophase

Chromosomes decondense at poles. Nuclear envelope reforms around each set. Nucleoli reappear. Spindle disassembles. Two distinct nuclei form.

Cytokinesis

In animal cells, a cleavage furrow forms at the metaphase plate as an actin-myosin contractile ring constricts, pinching the cell into two. In plant cells, Golgi-derived vesicles carrying cell wall materials fuse at the metaphase plate to form the cell plate, which expands outward to become the new cell wall. Cytokinesis begins during late anaphase/telophase.

Significance

Growth (increase in cell number), repair (wound healing), regeneration (liver), and asexual reproduction (protists, some animals). Maintains genetic stability by producing genetically identical daughter cells. Errors cause aneuploidy associated with cancer.

12. Meiosis (Reduction Division)

Overview

Meiosis occurs in germ cells to produce gametes (sperm/eggs in animals, spores in plants). Two successive divisions (meiosis I and II) with one round of DNA replication produce four haploid (n) genetically distinct daughter cells. Meiosis reduces chromosome number by half, essential for maintaining constant chromosome number across generations.

Meiosis I: Reductional Division

Homologous chromosomes separate. Sister chromatids remain attached. Prophase I is the longest and most complex stage, subdivided into five substages.

Prophase I Substages

Leptotene: Chromosomes condense into thin threads. Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes undergo synapsis, forming bivalents/tetrads. The synaptonemal complex forms between homologs. Pachytene: Full synapsis, crossing over occurs (genetic recombination catalyzed by recombinase enzymes). Diplotene: Synaptonemal complex dissolves; chiasmata visible at crossing over sites. In human females, cells arrest at diplotene until ovulation. Diakinesis: Chromosomes re-condense, nuclear envelope breaks down, spindle forms.

Metaphase I, Anaphase I, Telophase I

Metaphase I: Bivalents align at the metaphase plate. Random orientation causes independent assortment, a major source of genetic variation. Each bivalent attaches to one spindle pole per homolog. Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate (disjunction). Sister chromatids remain attached. Nondisjunction causes aneuploidy. Telophase I: Chromosomes arrive at poles, nuclear envelope may reform. Cytokinesis produces two haploid cells. No DNA replication between meiosis I and II.

Meiosis II: Equational Division

Similar to mitosis. Sister chromatids separate. Stages: prophase II (NE breakdown, spindle re-forms), metaphase II (chromosomes align individually), anaphase II (centromeres split, sister chromatids separate), telophase II (NE reforms, decondensation, cytokinesis). Result: four haploid daughter cells, each genetically unique.

Significance of Meiosis

Maintains chromosome number across generations. Generates genetic variation through crossing over (prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I). In humans, independent assortment produces 2^23 = over 8 million combinations; crossing over increases diversity further.

13. Carbohydrates

Classification

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones with general formula (CH2O)n. They are the most abundant biomolecules on Earth, serving as energy sources, structural components, and metabolic intermediates. Three major groups: monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides.

Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates, classified by carbon number (trioses C3, tetroses C4, pentoses C5, hexoses C6, heptoses C7) and carbonyl position (aldoses or ketoses). Important: glucose (blood sugar, C6H12O6), fructose (fruit sugar), galactose (milk sugar), ribose (RNA), deoxyribose (DNA). Glucose exists as alpha and beta anomers differing in -OH orientation on carbon 1. Monosaccharides form cyclic structures (pyranose for 6-membered, furanose for 5-membered). Reducing sugars have a free aldehyde/ketone group that reduces Cu2+ to Cu+ in Benedict reagent. All monosaccharides are reducing sugars.

Disaccharides

Disaccharides form by condensation of two monosaccharides via glycosidic bonds. Sucrose (table sugar) = glucose + fructose (alpha-1,2 bond, non-reducing). Maltose = glucose + glucose (alpha-1,4 bond, reducing). Lactose = galactose + glucose (beta-1,4 bond, reducing). Lactose intolerance results from lactase deficiency. Oligosaccharides contain 3-10 monosaccharide units, often attached to proteins/lipids as glycoconjugates.

Polysaccharides

Starch is plant energy storage: amylose (linear, alpha-1,4) and amylopectin (branched, alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6). Starch stains blue-black with iodine (amylose-iodine complex). Glycogen is animal storage, highly branched, stored in liver and muscles. Cellulose is the most abundant organic molecule, forming plant cell walls with beta-1,4 linkages forming strong microfibrils. Humans cannot digest cellulose (no cellulase); it serves as dietary fiber. Chitin is in fungal cell walls and arthropod exoskeletons, composed of N-acetylglucosamine (beta-1,4). Inulin is a fructose polymer for storage in some plants. Agar from seaweed is used as culture medium.

14. Proteins

Amino Acids

Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Each amino acid has a central alpha-carbon bonded to -NH2, -COOH, H, and variable R group. Classification by R group: non-polar (hydrophobic), polar uncharged, polar charged (acidic/basic), and special (glycine, proline, cysteine). There are 20 standard amino acids. Essential amino acids cannot be synthesized by humans: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine. Conditionally essential: arginine, glutamine.

Protein Structure Levels

Primary structure: linear amino acid sequence determined by genetic code. Secondary structure: local folding stabilized by hydrogen bonds between carbonyl O and amide H of the backbone. Alpha-helix (right-handed coil, hydrogen bonds every 4th amino acid) and beta-pleated sheet (parallel/antiparallel chains linked by hydrogen bonds). Tertiary structure: 3D folding of one chain stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and disulfide bridges (cysteine to cystine). Quaternary structure: arrangement of multiple subunits (e.g., hemoglobin has 4 subunits: 2 alpha-globin, 2 beta-globin, 4 heme groups).

Protein Functions

Enzymes catalyze reactions (DNA polymerase, hexokinase). Structural proteins provide support (collagen, keratin, elastin). Transport proteins carry molecules (hemoglobin, transferrin, lipoproteins). Contractile proteins enable movement (actin, myosin). Defense proteins protect (antibodies, complement). Regulatory proteins control processes (insulin, transcription factors). Receptor proteins mediate signal responses.

15. Lipids

Classification

Lipids are hydrophobic biomolecules insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (chloroform, ether, benzene). They are not polymers and lack monomeric units. Functions: energy storage, membrane structure, signaling, thermal insulation. Classes: simple lipids (fats, oils, waxes), compound lipids (phospholipids, glycolipids, lipoproteins), derived lipids (steroids, terpenes, fatty acids).

Simple Lipids: Fats and Oils

Triacylglycerols (triglycerides) are esters of glycerol and three fatty acids. Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds (palmitic C16:0, stearic C18:0), solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fatty acids have double bonds (oleic C18:1, linoleic C18:2, linolenic C18:3), liquid at room temperature due to cis double bond kinks preventing tight packing. Animal fats are mostly saturated; plant oils are mostly unsaturated. Essential fatty acids (linoleic, linolenic) cannot be synthesized by humans and are precursors for omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids important for brain function, inflammation, and membranes. Waxes are esters of long-chain fatty acids and alcohols, providing water-resistant coatings.

Compound Lipids

Phospholipids are major membrane components, amphipathic with polar phosphate heads and non-polar fatty acid tails. Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin) is the most common. Glycolipids have carbohydrate groups for cell recognition. Lipoproteins (chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, HDL) transport lipids in blood.

Derived Lipids: Steroids

Steroids have a four-ring structure (three six-membered, one five-membered). Cholesterol is the most important: component of animal cell membranes, precursor for steroid hormones (estrogen, testosterone, cortisol, aldosterone), bile acids, and vitamin D. Plant sterols (beta-sitosterol) reduce cholesterol absorption. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) is "good" cholesterol; LDL is "bad" cholesterol associated with atherosclerosis.

16. Nucleic Acids

DNA: The Genetic Material

DNA carries genetic information in all cellular organisms. It is a polymer of deoxyribonucleotides, each with deoxyribose sugar, phosphate, and nitrogenous base. Bases: adenine (A), guanine (G) = purines (double ring); cytosine (C), thymine (T) = pyrimidines (single ring). Chargaff rules: A = T, G = C, purine/pyrimidine ratio = 1:1.

The double helix model (Watson and Crick, 1953) based on Rosalind Franklin X-ray data: two antiparallel polynucleotide strands wound in a right-handed helix. Sugar-phosphate backbone outside, bases inside paired by hydrogen bonds: A-T (2 H-bonds), G-C (3 H-bonds, more stable). Strands are complementary. Helix diameter = 2 nm, pitch = 3.4 nm per turn, 10 base pairs per turn. Nucleotides linked by 3'-5' phosphodiester bonds. B-DNA is the most common form; Z-DNA (left-handed) and A-DNA are alternatives.

RNA Types and Functions

RNA differs from DNA: ribose sugar (2'-OH), uracil replaces thymine, typically single-stranded with secondary structure. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries genetic code from DNA to ribosomes. Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids; has cloverleaf structure with anticodon loop. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is the catalytic/structural component of ribosomes. Other types: snRNA (splicing), miRNA and siRNA (gene regulation via RNA interference), snoRNA (rRNA modification), lncRNA (regulatory).

The Central Dogma

Proposed by Francis Crick: DNA transcribed to RNA, translated to protein. Transcription occurs in nucleus (eukaryotes) via RNA polymerase. Primary transcript undergoes processing (capping, splicing, polyadenylation). Translation occurs on ribosomes using the genetic code: 64 codons specify 20 amino acids and stop signals. The code is degenerate (multiple codons per amino acid), universal (almost all organisms), and non-overlapping.

17. Enzymes

Classification

Enzymes are biological catalysts (mostly proteins, except ribozymes) that lower activation energy. Six IUBMB classes: oxidoreductases (redox reactions, e.g., lactate dehydrogenase), transferases (group transfer, e.g., hexokinase), hydrolases (hydrolysis, e.g., trypsin, lipase), lyases (group addition/removal without hydrolysis, e.g., pyruvate decarboxylase), isomerases (isomerization, e.g., phosphoglucoisomerase), ligases (joining using ATP, e.g., DNA ligase).

Mechanism of Action

Enzymes bind substrates at the active site, forming an enzyme-substrate complex. Lock and key model (Emil Fischer, 1894): active site pre-shaped for substrate. Induced fit model (Daniel Koshland, 1958): substrate binding induces conformational changes optimizing the active site. Mechanisms: proximity/orientation, acid-base catalysis, covalent catalysis, metal ion catalysis. Many enzymes require cofactors: coenzymes (organic: NAD+, FAD, CoA) or metal ions (Fe2+, Mg2+, Zn2+, Ca2+). Holoenzyme = apoenzyme (protein) + cofactor. Prosthetic groups are tightly bound cofactors (heme, FAD).

Factors Affecting Activity

Temperature: increases rate to optimum (37-40 C for human enzymes), then denaturation. pH: optimum varies (pepsin pH 2, trypsin pH 8, most cellular pH 7.4). Enzyme and substrate concentration affect rate; saturation reaches Vmax. Michaelis-Menten equation: V = (Vmax x [S]) / (Km + [S]). Km = substrate concentration at half Vmax, indicating enzyme affinity (low Km = high affinity). Lineweaver-Burk plot linearizes this relationship. Turnover number (kcat) = substrate molecules converted per enzyme per second at saturation.

Enzyme Inhibition

Competitive inhibition: inhibitor resembles substrate, binds active site, reversible by increasing [S]. Vmax unchanged, Km increases. Examples: methotrexate (competes with folic acid), statins (compete with HMG-CoA). Non-competitive inhibition: inhibitor binds allosteric site, alters conformation. Vmax decreases, Km unchanged. Examples: heavy metals (Pb2+, Hg2+), cyanide. Uncompetitive inhibition: inhibitor binds enzyme-substrate complex only; both Vmax and Km decrease. Irreversible inhibition: covalent modification (DFP for serine proteases, penicillin for transpeptidase). Allosteric regulation: effectors bind allosteric sites causing conformational changes. Allosteric enzymes show cooperative binding and sigmoidal kinetics.

Ribozymes and Abzymes

Ribozymes are catalytic RNA (RNase P, ribosomal peptidyl transferase, self-splicing introns), supporting the RNA world hypothesis. Abzymes (catalytic antibodies) are antibodies engineered to catalyze reactions by binding transition state analogs.

18. Metabolism & Bioenergetics

Anabolism and Catabolism

Metabolism includes all chemical reactions in organisms. Catabolism breaks down complex molecules releasing energy (exergonic): glycolysis, beta-oxidation, proteolysis. Energy is trapped in ATP, NADH, FADH2. Anabolism synthesizes complex molecules requiring energy (endergonic): protein synthesis, DNA replication, gluconeogenesis. Amphibolic pathways (e.g., Krebs cycle) serve both functions. Metabolic pathways are regulated by allosteric control, covalent modification, and hormonal signals.

ATP: The Energy Currency

ATP consists of adenine, ribose, and three phosphates. ATP hydrolysis releases 30.5 kJ/mol (standard) or 50-60 kJ/mol (cellular). The human body turns over its body weight in ATP daily. ATP is produced by substrate-level phosphorylation (glycolysis, Krebs cycle) and oxidative phosphorylation (ETC + chemiosmosis). Aerobic oxidation of one glucose yields 36-38 ATP. Other high-energy compounds: GTP, phosphocreatine (muscle reserve), NADPH (biosynthetic reducing power).

Major Metabolic Pathways

Glycolysis (cytoplasm): glucose (6C) to 2 pyruvate (3C), net 2 ATP + 2 NADH. No oxygen required. Anaerobic: pyruvate to lactate (lactic acid fermentation) or ethanol (alcoholic fermentation). Aerobic: pyruvate enters mitochondria. Krebs cycle (matrix): each turn produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, 2 CO2. Electron transport chain (inner membrane): electrons from NADH and FADH2 pass through Complex I-IV, with O2 as final acceptor. Proton gradient drives ATP synthase (chemiosmotic coupling by Peter Mitchell).

Redox Reactions

NAD+ accepts 2 electrons + 1 proton to form NADH. FAD accepts 2 electrons + 2 protons to form FADH2. NADPH is used for biosynthesis, not energy production. Standard reduction potentials determine electron flow direction in the ETC.
