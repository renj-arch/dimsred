Plant Morphology & Anatomy

NEET Biology covering morphology of root, stem, leaf, inflorescence, flower, fruit, seed, and internal anatomy of flowering plants including tissues and secondary growth.

Morphology of Flowering Plants

Plant morphology is the study of external form and structure of plants. Flowering plants (angiosperms) show immense diversity in form, yet share a common structural plan: the plant body is differentiated into root, stem, leaf, flower, and fruit. Angiosperms are divided into two major classes: Dicotyledons (dicots � two cotyledons, taproot system, reticulate venation, tetramerous/pentamerous flowers, vascular bundles in a ring in stems) and Monocotyledons (monocots � one cotyledon, fibrous root system, parallel venation, trimerous flowers, scattered vascular bundles). Understanding morphology is essential for identifying plants, understanding their adaptations, and classifying them into families � all of which are key NEET topics.

The Root System

Roots anchor the plant, absorb water and minerals, and store food. There are two main types: Taproot system (in dicots � a main root from which lateral roots arise; e.g., Mangifera � mango) and Fibrous root system (in monocots � cluster of roots of similar size arising from the stem base; e.g., Triticum � wheat). Adventitious roots arise from parts of the plant other than the radicle (e.g., roots from stem in Banyan prop roots, roots from leaves in Bryophyllum).

Modifications of roots: Storage (taproot � carrot, turnip; fibrous � sweet potato), Prop roots (banyan tree), Stilt roots (sugarcane, maize), Pneumatophores/Respiratory roots (mangroves like Rhizophora for gas exchange), Clinging roots (ivy), Sucking roots (parasitic plants like Cuscuta).

Regions of the Root

The root tip is protected by the Root cap (calyptra) � a thimble-like structure that secretes mucilage to lubricate passage through soil. The root cap is absent in aquatic plants like Pistia (where it is replaced by root pockets). Just above the cap lies the Meristematic zone (region of cell division) � composed of apical meristem cells that divide actively to produce new cells. Above this is the Elongation zone (region of cell elongation) � cells increase in length, pushing the root tip forward. The Maturation zone (region of differentiation) follows � cells differentiate into various permanent tissues; root hairs arise from this zone, increasing surface area for water and mineral absorption. The root cap is derived from the calyptrogen in some plants and from the dermatogen in others.

The Stem

The stem is the ascending part of the plant bearing branches, leaves, flowers, and fruits. It conducts water and minerals from roots to leaves and food from leaves to storage organs. The stem has nodes (points where leaves arise) and internodes (regions between nodes). The region between the leaf and stem axis is called the axil � buds here form branches or flowers.

Modifications of stems: Underground storage � tubers (potato), rhizomes (ginger, turmeric), bulbs (onion, garlic), corms (colocasia). Sub-aerial � runners (grass), stolons (strawberry), offsets (water hyacinth � Eichhornia). Aerial � tendrils (grapevine, cucumber), thorns (citrus, Bougainvillea), phylloclades (cactus) where stem performs photosynthesis. Potato tubers are modified stems (they have eyes/nodes).

NEET Tip � Root Modifications
High-yield comparisons: (1) Carrot vs Radish vs Turnip � all modified taproots, but carrot stores food in primary phloem (red/orange), radish in primary xylem (white), turnip in secondary xylem. (2) Sweet potato is a modified adventitious/fibrous root (tuberous root), not a taproot. (3) Pneumatophores (mangroves) have lenticels for gas exchange � key for swamp adaptation. (4) Banyan prop roots are pillar-like adventitious roots providing mechanical support. (5) Stilt roots in sugarcane and maize arise from lower nodes of stem � they are adventitious.
NEET Tip � Stem Modifications
Quick identification: Has nodes? It's a stem! Potato (eyes = nodes), Ginger (nodes with scaly leaves), Onion bulb (stem disc at base with fleshy leaf bases). Cactus phylloclade: flattened green stem with spines (modified leaves). Grapevine tendril: stem modification arising from axil. Thorn of Bougainvillea and Citrus: also stem modifications from axils. ALL underground stems can be identified by presence of nodes and internodes. Runner (grass) vs Stolon (strawberry): runner roots at nodes; stolon grows above ground then roots.
Example 1 � Root vs Stem
Which of the following is a modified stem?

a) Carrot   b) Sweet potato   c) Potato tuber   d) Radish
Solution: Option (c). Potato is a modified stem (tuber) � it has eyes (nodes) and arises from a stolon. Carrot, sweet potato, and radish are modified taproots.

The Leaf

The leaf is the main photosynthetic organ. Parts of a leaf: Leaf base (attaches leaf to stem; expanded to form pulvinus in some � Mimosa), Petiole (stalk), Lamina/Blade (the flat green part with midrib and veins). Leaves with a single lamina are simple; leaves with divided lamina into leaflets are compound (pinnately compound � leaflets along a common rachis, e.g., rose, neem; palmately compound � leaflets at the tip of petiole, e.g., silk cotton).

Venation: Reticulate (net-like veins � dicots, e.g., mango, peepal) and Parallel (parallel veins � monocots, e.g., grasses, banana). Venation patterns help in identifying plant families � e.g., reticulate in dicots, parallel in monocots. Phyllotaxy: Arrangement of leaves on stem � Alternate (one leaf per node � china rose, sunflower), Opposite (two leaves per node � guava, calotropis), Whorled (more than two leaves per node � oleander, Nerium). Opposite phyllotaxy can be further classified as opposite decussate (each pair at right angle to next � Calotropis) or opposite superposed (pairs directly above each other � guava).

Leaf morphology details: Leaves can be classified based on shape (ovate, lanceolate, linear, cordate, sagittate), margin (entire, serrated, dentate, lobed), apex (acute, acuminate, obtuse, mucronate, tendrillar), and base (rounded, cordate, oblique, sagittate, hastate, peltate). Stipules: Outgrowths at the base of the leaf � free (china rose), adnate (rose � fused with petiole), interpetiolar (between petioles of opposite leaves � Ixora), intrapetiolar (between petiole and stem � Acacia), ochreate (sheath-like � Polygonaceae). Stipules may be modified into spines (Acacia), tendrils (smilax), or scales. Leaf modifications: Tendrils (pea), spines (cactus � reduce transpiration), phyllode (Australian acacia � petiole flattened into leaf-like structure), leaf pitchers (nepenthes � insectivorous), bladder (utricularia), scales (onion bulb � fleshy leaf bases store food), and carnivorous leaves (drosera � sundew with insect-trapping hairs).

Inflorescence

The arrangement of flowers on the floral axis. Racemose (indeterminate � flowers develop in acropetal succession, oldest at base; the main axis continues to grow). Types: Raceme (main axis elongated, flowers stalked � mustard, radish, Brassica). Panicle (branched raceme � asparagus, mango, neem � racemose branches on a racemose main axis). Spike (similar to raceme but flowers sessile � wheat, barley, Achyranthes). Catkin/Ament (pendulous spike of unisexual flowers � mulberry, birch, oak � deciduous after flowering). Spadix (spike with fleshy axis, enclosed by a large bract called spathe � colocasia, palm, banana, aroids � characteristic of Araceae). Corymb (lower flower stalks longer, all flowers at same level � candytuft, cauliflower). Umbel (flowers with equal stalks arising from a common point, like umbrellar ribs � carrot, coriander, fennel � characteristic of Apiaceae; a whorl of bracts at base = involucre). Head/Capitulum (flowers on a flattened receptacle, surrounded by involucre of bracts � sunflower, marigold, dandelion � characteristic of Asteraceae; two types of florets: ray florets (ligulate/pistillate/sterile) and disc florets (tubular/bisexual)). Hypanthodium (receptacle forms a hollow cavity with a small opening; flowers develop on the inner wall � fig, Ficus � characteristic of Moraceae).

Cymose (determinate � oldest flower at top/centre, younger below; main axis terminates in a flower). Types: Monochasial/Uniparous (main axis terminates, one lateral branch arises � continued sympodially; types: helicoid � flowers on one side like in Freesia; scorpioid � alternately on both sides like in Gossypium). Dichasial/Biparous (main axis terminates in a flower, two lateral branches arise below � each also terminates � Dianthus, Mirabilis, Stellaria). Polychasial/Multiparous (main axis terminates, more than two lateral branches arise below � Calotropis, Euphorbia). Cymose umbel (like umbel but with a terminal flower � onion, garlic). The key difference: racemose is indeterminate (apex continues growing), cymose is determinate (apex becomes a flower).

NEET Mnemonic � Inflorescence Types
Racemose types: RPS CCHU � Raceme, Panicle, Spike, Catkin, Corymb, Head (Capitulum), Umbel (and Spadix is special). Spadix has a fleshy axis with large bract (spathe) � characteristic of Araceae (colocasia). Cymose types: Monochasial (Mono = one branch per node), Dichasial (Di = two), Polychasial (Poly = many). Key difference: In racemose, apical meristem continues growing (indeterminate); in cymose, apical flower terminates the axis (determinate). Hypanthodium is a special type (receptacle forms a hollow cavity with flowers inside � fig, Ficus).

The Flower

The flower is the reproductive unit of angiosperms. Parts: Calyx (sepals � outermost whorl, protects the bud, can be gamosepalous/united or polysepalous/free). The calyx may be caducous (falls off early � poppy), deciduous (falls after fertilisation � mustard), or persistent (remains attached � tomato, brinjal). Corolla (petals � attractive, coloured, attracts pollinators; gamopetalous/united or polypetalous/free). Corolla shapes: tubular (sunfl,ower), campanulate/bell-shaped (bellflower), rotate (tomato), bilabiate/two-lipped (Ocimum), personate (snapdragon � bilabiate with closed mouth), ligulate/strap-shaped (sunflower ray florets), cruciform/cross-shaped (mustard � Brassicaceae). Perianth: when calyx and corolla are not differentiated (e.g., lily, tulip � Liliaceae); each segment is called a tepal. Androecium (male reproductive part � stamens: filament + anther). Each anther has two lobes (dithecous); each lobe has two pollen sacs (microsporangia). Based on filament length: didynamous (two long, two short � Ocimum, Salvia), tetradynamous (four long, two short � Brassicaceae/mustard). Cohesion of stamens: monadelphous (filaments fused into one bundle � cotton, Hibiscus), diadelphous (filaments fused into two bundles � pea, bean � 9+1 arrangement), polyadelphous (many bundles � citrus). Adhesion of stamens: epipetalous (attached to petals � Solanaceae), epiphyllous (attached to perianth � Liliaceae), gynandrous (attached to gynoecium � orchids). Gynoecium (female reproductive part � carpels: stigma + style + ovary). Based on number of carpels: monocarpellary (pea), bicarpellary (mustard), tricarpellary (rice, bamboo), tetracarpellary, pentacarpellary, multicarpellary. Syncarpous (carpels fused � tomato) vs Apocarpous (carpels free � lotus, strawberry). The ovary contains ovules attached to placentae. Stigma receives pollen; style connects stigma to ovary.

Placentation: Arrangement of ovules in the ovary. Marginal (pea � ovules along the fused ventral margin of a single carpel), Axile (tomato, lemon � ovules on central column formed by fusion of septa), Parietal (mustard, argemone � ovules on inner ovary wall or extensions from it), Free central (Dianthus, primrose � ovules on a central column not connected to ovary wall by septa), Basal (sunflower, marigold � one ovule at the ovary base), Superficial (water lily � ovules all over the inner ovary surface). Placentation is determined by the number of carpels and the mode of their fusion.

NEET Mnemonic � Placentation Types
MAPS FB: Marginal (pea), Axile (tomato), Parietal (mustard), Superficial (water lily), Free central (Dianthus), Basal (sunflower).

Floral symmetry: Actinomorphic (radially symmetrical � can be divided into equal halves through any plane; e.g., mustard, tomato). Zygomorphic (bilaterally symmetrical � only one plane divides into equal halves; e.g., pea, bean, Ocimum). Asymmetric (no plane � Canna). Floral symmetry is often correlated with pollination syndrome � actinomorphic flowers are usually visited by generalist pollinators (bees, flies), while zygomorphic flowers are adapted for specialist pollinators (e.g., bees in Fabaceae, birds in many zygomorphic flowers).

Pollination and fertilisation context: After pollination, the pollen grain germinates on the stigma to produce a pollen tube that grows through the style to reach the ovule. The pollen tube enters the ovule through the micropyle (porogamy � most common; chalazogamy � entry through chalaza in Casuarina, Cucurbita; mesogamy � through integuments in Pinus). Double fertilisation (unique to angiosperms): one sperm fuses with the egg → diploid zygote (embryo); the other sperm fuses with two polar nuclei → triploid primary endosperm nucleus (PEN) → endosperm. This triploid endosperm is the nutritive tissue for the developing embryo. After fertilisation: ovary → fruit, ovule → seed, integuments → seed coat (testa + tegmen), micropyle remains as a small pore, funicle becomes the stalk (funicular scar = hilum).

Position of floral parts on thalamus: Based on the position of calyx, corolla, and androecium relative to the ovary: Hypogynous (superior ovary � thalamus is slightly swollen; sepals, petals, and stamens inserted below the ovary; e.g., mustard, tomato, brinjal). Perigynous (half-inferior � thalamus forms a cup around the ovary; sepals, petals, stamens inserted on the rim of the cup at the same level; ovary is in the centre but not fused with the cup; e.g., rose, peach, plum). Epigynous (inferior ovary � thalamus completely fuses with the ovary wall; sepals, petals, stamens appear to arise from the top of the ovary; e.g., apple, sunflower, cucumber).

Aestivation: Arrangement of sepals/petals in the bud. Valvate (petals just touch at margins � Calotropis), Twisted (one margin overlaps next � china rose, cotton), Imbricate (one set completely outside, another inside � Cassia, gulmohar), Vexillary (characteristic of Fabaceae � 1 large standard petal outside, 2 wing petals, 2 fused keel petals � pea).

NEET Tip � Floral Formula Interpretation
Floral formula symbols: Br (bracteate), ⊕ (actinomorphic), % (zygomorphic), ♂ (male), ♀ (female), ⚥ (bisexual), K (calyx), C (corolla), P (perianth), A (androecium), G (gynoecium). Superior ovary: G with line below; Inferior ovary: G with line above. Example: Fabaceae � % ⚥ K(5) C1+2+(2) A(9)+1 G1; Solanaceae � ⊕ ⚥ K(5) C(5) A5 G(2); Liliaceae � ⊕ ⚥ P3+3 A3+3 G(3). The position of the ovary relative to other floral parts determines hypogynous/perigynous/epigynous condition.

The Fruit

The fruit is a mature/ripened ovary developed after fertilisation. Parts: Pericarp (fruit wall) and Seed(s). The pericarp has three layers: Epicarp (outer), Mesocarp (middle), Endocarp (inner). True fruit develops from the ovary (mango); false fruit develops from other floral parts also (apple � thalamus, strawberry � receptacle; cashew � peduncle is the fruit, the nut is the true fruit). Parthenocarpic fruits develop without fertilisation � they are seedless (banana, grapes � naturally; tomato, papaya � induced by growth regulators). Based on origin: Simple (one flower, one ovary � mango, tomato), Aggregate (one flower, multiple carpels � strawberry, raspberry, custard apple), Multiple/Composite (multiple flowers of an inflorescence � pineapple, mulberry, fig, jackfruit).

Fleshy fruits detail: Drupe (one-seeded with stony endocarp � mango, coconut, peach, plum, almond � in almond, the edible part is the seed inside the stone). Berry (multi-seeded, entire pericarp fleshy � tomato, grape, banana, brinjal, guava, papaya � banana is a parthenocarpic berry). Pepo (specialised berry with hard rind, inferior ovary � cucumber, watermelon, pumpkin, squash � characteristic of Cucurbitaceae). Hesperidium (leathery epicarp with oil glands, spongy mesocarp, juicy endocarp hairs � lemon, orange, grapefruit � characteristic of Rutaceae/citrus family). Pome (false fruit where fleshy thalamus encloses the core containing true fruit � apple, pear, quince � the core is the true fruit from ovary). Balausta (pomegranate � fleshy seeds with succulent testa; the fruit wall is leathery). Amphisarca (wood apple � woody shell, pulpy interior).

Dry fruits detail: Dehiscent � Legume/Pod (one carpel, splits along both sutures � pea, bean), Follicle (one carpel, splits along one suture � Calotropis, magnolia), Capsule (multicarpellary, various dehiscence patterns � cotton, Datura, poppy), Siliqua (two-valved with false septum � mustard, radish � Brassicaceae), Silicula (short siliqua � Capsella/shepherd's purse). Indehiscent � Achene (single seed, pericarp free from seed � sunflower, marigold), Caryopsis/Grain (pericarp fused with seed coat � rice, wheat, maize � characteristic of Poaceae), Nut (hard woody wall � cashew, walnut, chestnut, litchi), Cypsela (from inferior ovary � sunflower, marigold � actually a type of achene). Schizocarpic � Cremocarp (splits into two one-seeded mericarps � coriander, cumin, fennel � Apiaceae), Lomentum (legume-like but constricted between seeds � rain tree, groundnut), Regma (splits into cocci � castor, geranium), Carcerulus (splits into four nutlets � Ocimum, mint � Lamiaceae).

Morphology of Key Plant Families (NEET Focus)

NEET syllabus covers three major angiosperm families: Fabaceae, Solanaceae, and Liliaceae. Understanding their floral characters, formulae, and economic importance is essential.

Fabaceae (Leguminosae/Papilionaceae): The pea family. Vegetative: Taproot system, compound leaves (pinnately), reticulate venation. Floral: Inflorescence � racemose. Flower � bisexual, zygomorphic, pentamerous, hypogynous. Calyx � 5 sepals, gamosepalous, valvate/imbricate. Corolla � 5 petals, polypetalous, vexillary aestivation (1 standard + 2 wings + 2 keel). Androecium � 10 stamens, diadelphous (9+1), dithecous anthers. Gynoecium � monocarpellary, superior ovary, marginal placentation. Fruit � legume/pod. Floral formula: % ⚥ K(5) C1+2+(2) A(9)+1 G1. Examples: pea (Pisum sativum), bean (Phaseolus), gram (Cicer arietinum), groundnut (Arachis hypogaea), soyabean, Mimosa (sensitive plant � but subfamily Mimosoideae with actinomorphic flowers). Economic importance: pulses, fodder, green manure (nitrogen fixation via Rhizobium symbiosis), oils (groundnut, soyabean).

Solanaceae (Potato/Tobacco family): Vegetative: Taproot, simple leaves, reticulate venation, sometimes stem with spines (Solanum). Floral: Inflorescence � cymose (solitary or cymose). Flower � bisexual, actinomorphic, pentamerous, hypogynous. Calyx � 5 sepals, gamosepalous, persistent (remains attached to fruit in tomato, brinjal). Corolla � 5 petals, gamopetalous, rotate or tubular. Androecium � 5 stamens, epipetalous (attached to corolla tube), dithecous, alternate with petals. Gynoecium � bicarpellary, syncarpous, superior ovary, axile placentation (obliquely placed � swollen placenta). Fruit � berry or capsule. Floral formula: ⊕ ⚥ K(5) C(5) A5 G(2). Examples: tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), potato (S. tuberosum), brinjal (S. melongena), chilli (Capsicum), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), petunia (Petunia hybrida). Economic importance: vegetables (tomato, brinjal, chilli), tuber food (potato), tobacco, ornamentals (petunia), medicinal (Atropa belladonna � source of atropine).

Liliaceae (Lily family): Vegetative: Fibrous root system, stem underground (bulb/rhizome/corm), simple leaves, parallel venation. Floral: Inflorescence � cymose (solitary/terminal). Flower � bisexual, actinomorphic, trimerous (3-membered), hypogynous; often epigynous in some. Perianth � 6 tepals (3+3), often petaloid (showy), free or united. Androecium � 6 stamens (3+3), epiphyllous (attached to perianth), dithecous. Gynoecium � tricarpellary, syncarpous, superior ovary (sometimes inferior), axile placentation. Fruit � capsule or berry. Floral formula: ⊕ ⚥ P3+3 A3+3 G(3). Examples: lily (Lilium), tulip (Tulipa), onion (Allium cepa), garlic (A. sativum), aloe (Aloe vera), asparagus, Colchicum autumnale (source of colchicine). Economic importance: vegetables (onion, garlic, asparagus), ornamentals (lily, tulip, gladiolus), medicinal (aloe, colchicum).

The Seed

A seed develops from the ovule after fertilisation. It consists of a seed coat (testa � outer, tegmen � inner), a cotyledon (one � monocot, two � dicot), and an embryo (radicle + plumule + cotyledonary node). The hilum is the scar where the seed was attached to the ovary wall/funicle. The micropyle is a small pore through which water enters during germination. The funicle (stalk) attaches the ovule to the placenta; after fertilisation it may form a fleshy aril (litchi, nutmeg � mace).

Seed types based on endosperm: Albuminous (Endospermic) � endosperm is present and stores food; cotyledons are thin and absorb food during germination (castor, coconut, maize, wheat). Exalbuminous (Non-endospermic) � endosperm is completely consumed during seed development; cotyledons become thick and store food (pea, groundnut, bean, gram, tamarind). Perispermic � the nucellus persists as perisperm and stores food (black pepper, beetroot, coffee, custard apple).

Seed germination: Epigeal germination � cotyledons are brought above ground due to elongation of hypocotyl; they become photosynthetic and later fall off (bean, castor, onion). Hypogeal germination � cotyledons remain underground; the epicotyl elongates and the plumule comes out (pea, maize, coconut, gram). Vivipary � seed germinates while still attached to the parent plant (mangroves like Rhizophora � radicle grows out through the fruit wall).

Seed dormancy: Seeds may remain dormant for extended periods due to hard seed coat (impermeable to water/gases), presence of growth inhibitors, or underdeveloped embryo. Dormancy can be broken by scarification (mechanical/chemical), stratification (low temperature treatment), light exposure, or hormone treatment (gibberellins). Seed viability varies � some seeds remain viable for decades (date palm, lotus � centuries-old viable seeds found).

Example 2 � Flower Morphology
Which type of placentation is found in pea?

a) Axile   b) Parietal   c) Marginal   d) Free central
Solution: Option (c). The pea (Fabaceae family) has marginal placentation � ovules are arranged along the fused ventral margin of a single carpel.
Example 4 � Leaf Modification
Identify the modification: A plant has flattened, green, leaf-like structures bearing small leaves on them. What is this modification?

a) Phyllode   b) Phylloclade   c) Cladode   d) Spines
Solution: Option (b). Phylloclade is a modified stem that is flattened and green (photosynthetic) with leaves/spines on it � e.g., cactus (Opuntia). Phyllode is a modified petiole (Australian acacia). Cladode is a one-internode phylloclade (asparagus). Spines are modified leaves or stipules (cactus, Acacia).
Example 5 � Inflorescence Type
A plant bears flowers in such a way that the oldest flower is at the apex and younger flowers are below. The inflorescence is:

a) Racemose   b) Cymose   c) Spike   d) Panicle
Solution: Option (b). In cymose inflorescence, the apical meristem terminates in a flower, so the oldest flower is at the apex/centre and younger ones are below/peripheral. In racemose, the oldest flowers are at the base and the apex keeps growing.
Example 6 � Secondary Growth / Annual Rings
A tree trunk shows distinct annual rings. The lighter part of each ring represents:

a) Autumn wood   b) Spring wood   c) Heartwood   d) Sapwood
Solution: Option (b). Springwood (early wood) is formed in spring when cambium is more active � cells are larger with thin walls, appearing lighter. Autumnwood (late wood) has smaller, thick-walled cells and appears darker.
Example 7 � Dicot vs Monocot Leaf Anatomy
Which of the following is characteristic of a monocot leaf?

a) Differentiated mesophyll with palisade and spongy parenchyma   b) Stomata only on lower surface   c) Bulliform cells in upper epidermis   d) Reticulate venation
Solution: Option (c). Bulliform cells (motor cells) are large, thin-walled cells found in the upper epidermis of monocot leaves � they help in leaf folding during water stress. Monocots have undifferentiated mesophyll and stomata on both surfaces.
Example 8 � Aestivation Type
A flower has 5 petals: one large petal completely covers the others, two are lateral and two are innermost and fused. The aestivation is:

a) Valvate   b) Twisted   c) Imbricate   d) Vexillary
Solution: Option (d). Vexillary aestivation (characteristic of Fabaceae) has a large standard petal (outermost), two wing petals (lateral), and two keel petals (innermost, fused). Imbricate has one petal outside, one inside, and three with one margin out one in.
Example 9 � Placentation from Ovary
A bicarpellary syncarpous ovary has ovules attached to the inner wall of the ovary. The placentation is:

a) Axile   b) Parietal   c) Marginal   d) Free central
Solution: Option (b). Parietal placentation � ovules develop on the inner wall of the ovary (or on extensions of the wall). This occurs when carpels are fused at margins and ovules attach along these fused margins (e.g., mustard � Brassicaceae).
Example 10 � Plant Family from Floral Characters
A flower has: bisexual, actinomorphic, pentamerous, gamosepalous, gamopetalous, epipetalous stamens, bicarpellary syncarpous superior ovary with axile placentation. This belongs to family:

a) Fabaceae   b) Solanaceae   c) Liliaceae   d) Brassicaceae
Solution: Option (b). Solanaceae characters: bisexual, actinomorphic, � pentamerous, gamosepalous, gamopetalous, epipetalous stamens, bicarpellary syncarpous, superior ovary, axile placentation (e.g., tomato, potato, brinjal). Fabaceae has zygomorphic, polypetalous, vexillary aestivation. Brassicaceae has tetradynamous stamens, parietal placentation. Liliaceae is trimerous, often perianth present.

Anatomy of Flowering Plants

Plant anatomy deals with the internal structure of plants. The plant body is made up of tissues � groups of cells performing a common function.

Meristematic Tissues

Cells capable of division. Meristems are classified based on position, origin, and function.

Based on position: Apical meristem (at tips of root and stem � responsible for primary growth/elongation; protoderm → epidermis; procambium → primary vascular tissues; ground meristem → cortex/pith). Intercalary meristem (at the base of leaves/internodes � e.g., in grasses, members of Poaceae; responsible for growth of internodes; also called basal meristem � it is actually a part of apical meristem separated by differentiation). Lateral meristem (cambium � responsible for increase in girth/secondary growth; includes vascular cambium and cork cambium).

Based on origin: Primary meristem (derived directly from promeristem/embryonic cells � apical meristem and intercalary meristem). Secondary meristem (derived from permanent tissues that regain dividing capacity � cork cambium/phellogen; also interfascicular cambium is secondary as it arises from medullary ray cells).

Based on function: Protoderm (gives rise to epidermis), Procambium (gives rise to primary xylem and phloem), Ground meristem (gives rise to cortex, endodermis, pericycle, pith � all ground tissues). In roots, the apical meristem has three tiers: dermatogen (epidermis), periblem (cortex), pleurome (stele). Root apex is protected by the root cap; stem apex has tunica (outer layers, anticlinal divisions � increases surface) and corpus (inner mass, divisions in all planes � increases volume) in dicots.

Permanent Tissues

Differentiated cells that have lost the ability to divide.

Simple tissues: Parenchyma (thin-walled, living, storage, photosynthesis � chlorenchyma when it contains chloroplasts, aeration � aerenchyma when it has large air spaces for buoyancy in aquatic plants). Parenchyma is the most common tissue � found in all plant parts (cortex, pith, mesophyll, medullary rays). Cells are isodiametric with intercellular spaces. Collenchyma (living, thick walls at corners due to cellulose and pectin deposition, provides mechanical support � found in leaf petioles and stems, absent in roots). Cell walls are unevenly thickened; no intercellular spaces. Collenchyma is elastic and allows for bending of young stems. Types: angular (thickening at corners � typical), lamellar (thickening on tangential walls � celery), lacunate (intercellular spaces present). Sclerenchyma (dead cells, thick lignified walls � fibres (long, narrow, with pointed ends � linen from flax fibres, jute) and sclereids (isodiametric, branched � grit cells in pear fruit, stone cells in coconut endocarp). Provides strength and mechanical support; present in stems, seed coats, fruit walls, leaf veins. Fibres may be grouped as bundles (hemp, flax) or scattered.

Complex tissues: Xylem (water and mineral conduction from roots to leaves). Components: Tracheids (dead, elongated, tapered ends with pits � present in all vascular plants; main water-conducting cells in gymnosperms and pteridophytes; water moves through pits). Vessels/Tracheae (dead, tube-like, perforation plates at ends � present in most angiosperms; absent in gymnosperms except Gnetum, Ephedra; more efficient water conduction than tracheids). Xylem fibres (dead, mechanical support � libriform fibres, fibre tracheids). Xylem parenchyma (living, storage of starch/fats/tannins). Xylem is of two types: Primary xylem (derived from procambium) � further divided into protoxylem (first formed, narrower vessels with annular/spiral thickening) and metaxylem (later formed, wider vessels with pitted/reticulate thickening). In stems, protoxylem is towards pith (endarch); in roots, protoxylem is towards periphery (exarch); in leaves, protoxylem is towards upper side (mesarch). Phloem (food conduction from source leaves to sink organs). Components: Sieve tube elements (living, elongated, lack nucleus at maturity, have sieve plates � transverse or oblique end walls with pores; contain phloem sap and P-protein/slime body). Companion cells (living, nucleated, dense cytoplasm � connected to sieve tubes by plasmodesmata; control sieve tube function; absent in gymnosperms). Phloem fibres/Bast fibres (dead, thick lignified walls � jute, flax, hemp are commercial phloem fibres). Phloem parenchyma (living, storage of starch, fats, resins). Phloem types: Primary phloem � protophloem (first formed) and metaphloem (later formed). In angiosperms: sieve tubes + companion cells; in gymnosperms and pteridophytes: sieve cells + albuminous cells (equivalent to companion cells). Protophloem sieve tubes are narrow and become non-functional early; metaphloem replaces them.

Tissue Systems

Tissues are organised into three tissue systems based on their position and function, as described by Sachs.

1. Epidermal Tissue System: The outermost protective layer covering the entire plant body. Includes Epidermis (single layer of tightly packed cells with a cuticle � a waxy layer of cutin that prevents water loss; in roots, the epidermis is called rhizodermis/epiblema and lacks cuticle). Stomata (microscopic pores for gas exchange � each stoma consists of two guard cells surrounding the stomatal pore, plus subsidiary cells). Guard cells are the only epidermal cells with chloroplasts; they regulate transpiration by changing shape based on turgor (K⁺ ion influx causes water uptake → guard cells become turgid → stomata open). Stomata are classified based on subsidiary cell arrangement (Metcalfe and Chalk system): Anomocytic (Ranunculaceous) � irregularly arranged subsidiary cells, indistinguishable from other epidermal cells (Ranunculaceae, many families). Anisocytic (Cruciferous) � three unequal subsidiary cells, one distinctly smaller (Brassicaceae/Solanaceae). Paracytic (Rubiaceous) � two subsidiary cells arranged parallel to the long axis of guard cells (Rubiaceae, Magnoliaceae). Diacytic (Caryophyllaceous) � two subsidiary cells arranged perpendicular to guard cells (Caryophyllaceae, Acanthaceae). Gramineous � dumbbell-shaped guard cells with two subsidiary cells on sides (Poaceae/grasses). Trichomes: Epidermal outgrowths that vary widely � unicellular (cotton fibre � epidermal hair of seed coat), multicellular, branched, stellate (star-shaped in many Malvaceae), peltate (shield-shaped), glandular (secretory � stinging hairs of nettle contain histamine/acetylcholine), nonglandular (protective � reduce water loss and herbivory). Root hairs are also trichomes � unicellular extensions of rhizodermal cells that increase surface area for absorption. The epidermis may also contain specialized cells like silica cells (grasses), bulliform cells (monocot leaves), and cork cells.

2. Ground Tissue System: Forms the bulk of the plant body � all tissues between epidermis and vascular bundles. Includes Cortex (hypodermis � outer protective/mechanical layer: collenchyma in dicot stems, sclerenchyma in monocot stems; general cortex � parenchyma for storage; endodermis � innermost cortical layer: Casparian strips in roots, starch sheath in dicot stems). Pericycle (outermost layer of stele � sclerenchyma/parenchyma; gives rise to lateral roots and part of vascular cambium). Pith (central region of parenchyma in stems; small/absent in dicot roots, large in monocot roots). Medullary rays (parenchyma connecting pith to cortex � radial transport). In dicot stems, the ground tissue is clearly differentiated into distinct zones (cortex, pericycle, pith, medullary rays). In monocot stems, the ground tissue is undifferentiated � no distinct cortical or pith layers; the entire region between hypodermis and centre is called ground tissue, with vascular bundles scattered within it.

3. Vascular Tissue System: Composed of xylem and phloem arranged in vascular bundles. Bundle arrangement varies: Radial (xylem and phloem on alternate radii � found in roots). Conjoint (xylem and phloem on the same radius � found in stems and leaves). Conjoint bundles are further classified: Collateral (xylem on the inner side, phloem on the outer side � typical of stems; open if cambium is present � dicots; closed if cambium is absent � monocots). Bicollateral (phloem present on both sides of xylem � outer and inner phloem; xylem in the middle with cambium on both sides � found in Cucurbitaceae/pumpkin and Solanaceae/potato � this is an exceptional arrangement). Concentric (one vascular tissue completely surrounds the other � two types: Amphicribral/Hadrocentric � xylem in centre surrounded by phloem; found in ferns; Amphivasal/Leptocentric � phloem in centre surrounded by xylem; found in Dracaena/Yucca � monocot anomalous secondary growth). Distribution of bundles: in dicot stems, bundles are arranged in a ring (separating cortex from pith); in monocot stems, bundles are scattered throughout the ground tissue. Bundle orientation: collateral bundles are endarch (xylem development from inside outwards) in stems; exarch in roots (xylem development from outside inwards); mesarch in leaves (from middle both ways).

Structure of Dicot Root (e.g., Sunflower)

Internal layers (from outside to inside):

Epiblema/Rhizodermis: Outermost layer with unicellular root hairs for absorption; cuticle and stomata are absent. Root hairs are ephemeral (short-lived) � they arise from trichoblasts (specialised epidermal cells). The epiblema is replaced by periderm in older roots during secondary growth.

Cortex: Several layers of large, thin-walled parenchyma cells with intercellular spaces. Stores food (starch grains). The innermost layer of cortex differentiates into the endodermis.

Endodermis: Single layer of barrel-shaped cells with Casparian strips (suberised band-like thickenings on radial and transverse walls). Casparian strips block the apoplast pathway � forcing water and ions to enter the symplast pathway for selective uptake. Some endodermal cells opposite xylem groups remain thin-walled � these are passage cells that allow water to pass through.

Pericycle: Single/multilayered layer just inside endodermis; composed of parenchyma cells. Important functions: gives rise to lateral roots (endogenous origin � originate from pericycle opposite xylem groups), and in dicots, part of it becomes meristematic to form vascular cambium during secondary growth.

Vascular bundles: Radial arrangement � xylem and phloem are present on different radii, alternating with each other. Xylem is exarch (protoxylem towards periphery, metaxylem towards centre). Number of xylem bundles: usually 2�8 (diarch, triarch, tetrarch, pentarch, hexarch, octarch). Phloem groups alternate with xylem. Conjunctive tissue (parenchyma) lies between xylem and phloem.

Pith: Small or absent; if present, it consists of a small central region of parenchyma cells. In some dicot roots, the pith is completely absent (the centre is occupied by metaxylem).

Structure of Monocot Root (e.g., Maize)

Similar to dicot root in basic organisation but with key differences:

Epiblema: Same as dicot � root hairs present, no cuticle. Cortex: Much broader than in dicots � made of many layers of parenchyma with large intercellular spaces. Endodermis: Thick-walled (often suberised or lignified) with Casparian strips; passage cells present. Pericycle: Gives rise to lateral roots only � does NOT participate in secondary growth (no cambium formed). Vascular bundles: Polyarch � 8 or more xylem bundles (typically 8�20). Xylem is exarch. Phloem groups alternate with xylem. Pith: Large and well-developed � occupies the central region; made of parenchyma cells that may become sclerenchymatous in older roots. Conjunctive tissue: May be sclerenchymatous in some monocots. No secondary growth occurs in monocot roots due to absence of cambium.

Structure of Dicot Stem (e.g., Sunflower)

Layers from outside to inside:

Epidermis: Single layer of flattened cells with a thick cuticle (cutinised outer wall), stomata (for gas exchange), and multicellular/unicellular trichomes (hair-like structures � may be glandular, stellate, or simple). The epidermis is protective and prevents water loss.

Cortex: Divided into three zones. Hypodermis: 2�4 layers of collenchyma (angular collenchyma) just below the epidermis � provides mechanical support to the young stem. Outer cortex: Few layers of chlorenchyma (parenchyma with chloroplasts) � performs photosynthesis. Inner cortex: Several layers of parenchyma with stored food (starch). Endodermis (Starch sheath): Innermost layer of cortex � barrel-shaped cells rich in starch grains. Unlike roots, the endodermis in stems lacks Casparian strips (it is a starch sheath, not a true endodermis).

Pericycle: Present between endodermis and vascular bundles � composed of a few layers of sclerenchyma fibres (forming a discontinuous band) and parenchyma cells. Provides mechanical strength and can become meristematic during secondary growth.

Vascular bundles: Conjoint, collateral, open � arranged in a ring (forming a distinct boundary between cortex and pith). Each bundle has: Phloem on the outer side (towards epidermis) � protophloem outside, metaphloem inside; phloem fibres often present. Cambium (intrafascicular cambium) � a thin layer of meristematic cells between xylem and phloem; responsible for secondary growth (produces secondary xylem and phloem). Xylem on the inner side (towards pith) � endarch xylem (protoxylem towards pith, metaxylem towards cambium); metaxylem vessels are wider and have pitted thickenings; protoxylem has annular/spiral thickenings. Medullary rays (Pith rays): Parenchymatous cells present between vascular bundles � connect pith and cortex for radial transport of water and nutrients. During secondary growth, medullary ray cells dedifferentiate to form interfascicular cambium.

Pith (Medulla): Large central region composed of thin-walled parenchyma cells with intercellular spaces. Stores food (starch grains) and provides mechanical support through turgor pressure. Some dicots may have a hollow pith (cucurbits). In many stems, the pith cells are alive and may contain tannins, crystals (calcium oxalate), or laticifers (latex-containing cells in some families).

Structure of Monocot Stem (e.g., Maize)

Layers from outside to inside:

Epidermis: Single layer with a thick cuticle (often silicified), trichomes may be present. Stomata are relatively rare compared to dicot stems. The epidermis is protective and reduces transpiration.

Hypodermis: 2�4 layers of sclerenchyma fibres just below the epidermis � provides mechanical support (makes the stem strong but non-elastic). This is in contrast to dicots which have a collenchymatous hypodermis.

Ground tissue: The region between the hypodermis and the centre is composed of ground tissue � undifferentiated parenchyma (no distinct cortex, pericycle, endodermis, or pith layers). The ground tissue is continuous and stores food. The peripheral part of the ground tissue may be more compact (sometimes called the cortical region), while the central part is loosely packed (sometimes called the pith region), but there is no clear separation.

Vascular bundles: Conjoint, collateral, closed � scattered throughout the ground tissue (not arranged in a ring). The scattered arrangement is a key characteristic of monocot stems. Each bundle is surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath (thick-walled fibres that provide support). The bundle has: Phloem on the outer side (towards periphery) � protophloem and metaphloem; sieve tubes and companion cells present. Xylem on the inner side � endarch (protoxylem towards centre, metaxylem towards periphery). The xylem typically has two large metaxylem vessels (with pitted thickenings) and one or two protoxylem vessels (with annular/spiral thickenings). A protoxykem lacuna (schizogenous cavity) is often present where the protoxylem vessels have been stretched and destroyed during elongation � this cavity is a characteristic feature of monocot stem vascular bundles (especially in maize). Cambium: Absent � since the bundles are closed, no secondary growth occurs in most monocot stems (exceptions: some monocots like Dracaena, Yucca show anomalous secondary growth through a secondary cambium that forms a complete ring and produces secondary vascular bundles).

Example 3 � Stem Anatomy
A conjoint, collateral, open vascular bundle with cambium is found in:

a) Monocot stem   b) Dicot stem   c) Monocot root   d) Both monocot and dicot stems
Solution: Option (b). Dicot stems have conjoint, collateral, OPEN (cambium present between xylem and phloem) vascular bundles arranged in a ring. Monocot stems have conjoint, collateral, CLOSED (no cambium) bundles scattered in ground tissue.

Structure of Dicot Leaf (e.g., Sunflower)

Dicot leaves are dorsiventral (upper and lower surfaces differ in structure and stomatal distribution). Layers from upper to lower surface:

Upper epidermis: Single layer of compactly arranged cells with a thick cuticle (reduces water loss). It is covered by a waxy layer (cuticle) composed of cutin. Stomata are few or absent on the upper surface; more abundant on the lower surface (hypostomatic). Trichomes may be present for protection against herbivores and to reduce water loss.

Mesophyll: Differentiated into two regions. Palisade parenchyma: Upper region, 1�3 layers of elongated cylindrical cells, tightly packed with minimal intercellular spaces, contain abundant chloroplasts � the main site of photosynthesis. Spongy parenchyma: Lower region, irregularly shaped cells with large intercellular air spaces (for gas exchange), fewer chloroplasts. Air spaces connect to stomata through substomatal chambers for efficient CO₂ diffusion.

Lower epidermis: Single layer with numerous stomata. Each stoma has two kidney-shaped guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by subsidiary cells. The guard cells regulate opening and closing of the stomatal pore based on turgor pressure changes (mediated by K⁺ ion movement). Below each stoma is a substomatal chamber continuous with the mesophyll air spaces.

Vascular bundles (veins): Each vein has xylem towards the upper (adaxial) side and phloem towards the lower (abaxial) side � this is called a conjoint, collateral, closed bundle (no cambium in leaves). Each bundle is surrounded by a bundle sheath (parenchyma cells). In C₄ plants, the bundle sheath cells are large, contain chloroplasts, and are arranged in a ring � this is called Kranz anatomy (maize, sugarcane � reduces photorespiration). The midrib region may contain additional mechanical tissue (collenchyma).

Structure of Monocot Leaf (e.g., Maize)

Monocot leaves are isobilateral � both surfaces are similar in structure; mesophyll is undifferentiated (no distinction between palisade and spongy parenchyma).

Epidermis: Single-layered on both surfaces with a thick cuticle. Stomata are present on both surfaces (amphistomatic) � roughly equal in number. Guard cells are dumbbell-shaped in grasses (with thin ends and thick middle) � specialised subsidiary cells flank them. Silicified cells and cork cells are commonly present in the epidermis of grasses (give silica deposition � helps in defence against herbivores).

Bulliform cells (Motor cells): Large, thin-walled, vacuolated cells present in groups in the upper epidermis. They are arranged in parallel rows along the veins. When water is abundant, bulliform cells become turgid, keeping the leaf flat. During water stress, they lose turgor and become flaccid, causing the leaf to roll inward � reducing transpiration (a xerophytic adaptation).

Mesophyll: Undifferentiated � consists of only spongy parenchyma-like cells (no palisade cells). Cells are irregular with intercellular spaces; contain chloroplasts. No differentiation into palisade and spongy.

Vascular bundles: Parallel venation � veins run parallel to each other, connected by small transverse commissural veins. Each bundle is conjoint, collateral, and closed (no cambium). Surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath. In C₄ monocots (maize, sugarcane, sorghum), bundle sheath cells are large, parenchymatous, and contain chloroplasts � Kranz anatomy (dimorphic chloroplasts: bundle sheath chloroplasts are large, agranal; mesophyll chloroplasts are smaller, granal). The xylem is towards the upper side, phloem towards the lower side.

Secondary Growth

Increase in girth occurs in dicot stems and roots (not in monocots) due to lateral meristems: Vascular cambium and Cork cambium (Phellogen).

Vascular cambium: A thin layer between xylem and phloem in dicot stems. It is composed of fusiform initials (vertical) and ray initials (horizontal). It divides to produce secondary xylem (towards inside � wood) and secondary phloem (towards outside � inner bark). The amount of secondary xylem produced is much more than secondary phloem. In woody stems, the cambium forms a complete ring (intrafascicular + interfascicular cambium).

Springwood and Autumnwood: In temperate regions, cambium is more active in spring producing springwood (early wood) � large vessels, thin walls, lighter colour, more abundance. In autumn, cambium activity slows, producing autumnwood (late wood) � smaller vessels, thick walls, darker colour. One springwood + one autumnwood = one annual ring. Counting annual rings gives the age of the tree (dendrochronology). In tropical trees, annual rings are less distinct due to lack of seasonal variation, but growth rings may still form due to wet/dry cycles.

Wood types based on porosity: Ring-porous wood � vessels in springwood are distinctly larger than in autumnwood (e.g., oak, elm, ash). Diffuse-porous wood � vessels of similar size throughout the ring (e.g., maple, birch, poplar). This distinction helps in timber identification.

Heartwood and Sapwood: Heartwood (Duramen) � central, dark-coloured, dead, filled with tannins, resins, oils; non-functional but provides strength and resistance to decay. Sapwood (Alburnum) � peripheral, light-coloured, living, functional � conducts water and minerals. Heartwood is formed as sapwood cells gradually die and accumulate organic compounds.

Cork cambium (Phellogen): Arises in the cortex/pericycle. It produces cork (phellem) on the outside (dead cells with suberin � waterproof, protective) and phelloderm (secondary cortex) on the inside (living, parenchymatous). All three layers (phellogen + phellem + phelloderm) constitute the periderm. The periderm replaces the epidermis in older stems and roots. Lenticels are openings in the bark for gas exchange (seen as raised spots on cork � filled with loosely arranged complementary cells).

Bark: All tissues outside the vascular cambium (including primary and secondary phloem, periderm). Rough bark forms when successive layers of periderm are produced (e.g., oak). Scaling bark peels off in plates (e.g., eucalyptus, pine). Ring bark peels in concentric rings (e.g., grapevine). Commercial cork is obtained from Quercus suber (cork oak) � the phellem layer is harvested without damaging the tree.

NEET Tip � Secondary Growth Facts
Key NEET points: (1) Secondary growth occurs in dicots and gymnosperms � NOT in monocots (exception: some monocots like Dracaena show anomalous secondary growth via accessory cambium). (2) Vascular cambium is a lateral meristem � composed of fusiform initials (give rise to axial system: vessels, tracheids, fibres) and ray initials (give rise to radial system: medullary rays). (3) Cork cambium arises from cortex/pericycle � first periderm forms in the sub-epidermal layer. (4) Phellogen cuts off phellem (cork) on outside and phelloderm on inside. (5) Lenticels are regions where phellogen is more active � complementary cells fill the opening for gas exchange. (6) Bark includes everything outside vascular cambium � it is not synonymous with periderm. (7) Annual rings are distinct only in temperate regions; tropical trees show less distinct rings. (8) Heartwood is darker due to deposition of tannins, resins, gums � it is non-conducting but provides mechanical strength. (9) Sapwood is functional and conducts water; it eventually gets converted to heartwood. (10) Dendrochronology uses annual ring patterns for dating wooden artifacts and studying past climate.
NEET Key Differences � Dicot vs Monocot
(1) Roots: Dicot � taproot, 2-8 xylem bundles, small pith. Monocot � fibrous, polyarch (8+), large pith. (2) Stems: Dicot � bundles in a ring, open (cambium present), secondary growth. Monocot � bundles scattered, closed (no cambium), no secondary growth. (3) Leaves: Dicot � reticulate venation, dorsiventral, palisade + spongy. Monocot � parallel venation, isobilateral, only spongy mesophyll. (4) Flowers: Dicot � tetramerous/pentamerous. Monocot � trimerous.

Anomalous Secondary Growth

Some plants show secondary growth that deviates from the typical dicot pattern (anomalous secondary growth). These variations are important for NEET:

In monocots (Dracaena, Yucca, Agave): A secondary cambium arises in the pericycle/ground tissue, outside the primary vascular bundles. This cambium forms a complete ring and produces a new set of vascular bundles (secondary bundles) embedded in ground tissue (conjunctive tissue). The secondary bundles are collateral, closed, and scattered in the newly formed ground tissue. The vascular cambium is amphivasal (phloem surrounded by xylem) in Dracaena. Monocots like palms increase in girth by a different mechanism � repeated formation of new bundles and expansion of ground tissue (diffuse secondary growth), but this is not true secondary growth.

In Bougainvillea and Mirabilis: Accessory cambia arise successively in the pericycle region, each producing a ring of vascular bundles. These successive cambia form concentric rings of xylem and phloem (included phloem), giving the stem a characteristic striped appearance in cross-section. This is called included phloem or interxylary phloem.

In Chenopodiaceae (Beta vulgaris � beetroot): Successive cambia rings form � each cambium produces secondary xylem inwards and secondary phloem outwards, but the secondary phloem of one cambium may appear as included phloem. This results in the characteristic concentric rings seen in beetroot cross-section (used to demonstrate anomalous secondary growth in lab).

Wood anatomy terms: Porous wood (hardwood � angiosperms with vessels) vs Non-porous wood (softwood � gymnosperms without vessels, only tracheids). Grain of wood: straight, interlocked, spiral, wavy � determines timber quality. Tyloses: Outgrowths of xylem parenchyma cells into vessel lumens through pits; common in heartwood � block water flow and provide resistance to decay. Reaction wood: Compression wood (formed on lower side of leaning branches in gymnosperms) and Tension wood (formed on upper side in angiosperms) � both respond to gravitational stress.

Commercial cork: Obtained from Quercus suber (cork oak). The first harvest of cork is called virgin cork (rough, low quality). Subsequent harvests produce reproductive cork (high quality, used for bottle stoppers). Cork cells are dead, filled with tannins, and have suberised walls � making them impermeable to water and gases, light in weight, elastic, and resistant to decay. Cork is also used in soundproofing, insulation, and flooring.

Key NEET Differences � High-Yield One-Liner Facts

  • Root cap is absent in aquatic plants like Pistia (replaced by root pockets).
  • Calyptrogen is the meristem that gives rise to root cap in some plants.
  • Casparian strips are suberised bands in the endodermis � they block passive water movement (apoplast pathway).
  • Pericycle is the outermost layer of the stele � gives rise to lateral roots.
  • Passage cells are endodermal cells opposite xylem groups � remain thin-walled for water passage.
  • Endodermis is absent in stems but present in roots (starch sheath in dicot stems is analogous).
  • Bundle sheath in C4 plants has Kranz anatomy � large, chloroplast-rich cells around vascular bundles.
  • Interfascicular cambium arises from cells of medullary rays (secondary meristem).
  • Intrafascicular cambium is primary (present between xylem and phloem in open bundles).
  • Phellogen is also called cork cambium � it is a secondary lateral meristem.
  • Bark includes all tissues outside the vascular cambium � primary phloem + secondary phloem + periderm.
  • Lenticels allow gas exchange through the bark � complementary cells are loosely arranged.
  • Springwood has large, thin-walled vessels; autumnwood has small, thick-walled vessels.
  • Heartwood is non-functional, dark, and resistant to decay; sapwood is functional and light-coloured.
  • Dendrochronology is the study of annual rings � used in dating (also called tree-ring dating).
  • Vessels are the main water-conducting cells in angiosperms; tracheids in gymnosperms.
  • Sieve tubes have sieve plates; companion cells control their function � both are derived from the same mother cell.
  • Callose is a polysaccharide deposited on sieve plates during dormancy or injury.
  • P-protein (slime body) is found in sieve tube elements � may help in sealing wounds.
  • Chlorenchyma is parenchyma with chloroplasts (photosynthesis); aerenchyma has air spaces (buoyancy in aquatic plants).
  • Collenchyma is absent in roots; sclerenchyma fibres are present in stems, roots, and leaves.
  • Monocot stems have scattered vascular bundles with sclerenchymatous bundle sheaths � no secondary growth.
  • Dicot stems have vascular bundles in a ring with cambium � secondary growth possible.
  • Xylem is tetrarch (4 bundles) in most dicot roots; polyarch (8+) in monocot roots.
  • Palisade parenchyma has more chloroplasts than spongy parenchyma � palisade is the main photosynthetic tissue.
  • Bulliform cells are found only in monocot leaves � they help in leaf folding (hygroscopic movement).
  • Dorsiventral leaf = dicot leaf (upper and lower surfaces different); Isobilateral = monocot leaf (similar surfaces).
  • Actinomorphic flowers are radially symmetrical; zygomorphic are bilaterally symmetrical.
  • Vexillary aestivation is characteristic of Fabaceae (Papilionaceae).
  • Perianth is undifferentiated calyx+corolla � common in Liliaceae (lily, tulip).
  • Epipetalous stamens are attached to petals (Solanaceae); epiphyllous are attached to perianth (Liliaceae).
  • Syncarpous ovary has fused carpels; apocarpous has free carpels (lotus � multicarpellary apocarpous).
  • False fruits develop from thalamus/receptacle (apple, strawberry, cashew); true fruits develop from ovary.
  • Parthenocarpic fruits develop without fertilisation � seedless (banana, grapes).
  • Polyembryony: more than one embryo in a seed (citrus, onion).
  • Albuminous seeds have endosperm (castor, coconut); exalbuminous lack it (pea, groundnut).
  • Radicle grows into root; plumule grows into shoot.
  • Hypogeal germination: cotyledons remain underground (pea); epigeal: cotyledons come above ground (bean).
  • Floral formula of Brassicaceae: ⊕ ⚥ K2+2 C4 A2+4 G(2); Fabaceae: % ⚥ K(5) C1+2+(2) A(9)+1 G1; Solanaceae: ⊕ ⚥ K(5) C(5) A5 G(2); Liliaceae: ⊕ ⚥ P3+3 A3+3 G(3).
  • Stomatal apparatus = guard cells + pore + subsidiary cells.
  • Anomocytic: irregular subsidiary cells; Anisocytic: three unequal; Paracytic: two parallel; Diacytic: two perpendicular.
