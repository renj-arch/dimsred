Reproduction in Organisms

Reproduction is the biological process by which new individual organisms (offspring) are produced from their parents. It is a fundamental feature of all known life.

Asexual Reproduction

Involves a single parent producing genetically identical offspring (clones). No gamete formation or fertilisation. Fast and efficient but lacks genetic variation. Common modes: Binary fission (bacteria, Amoeba � equal division), Multiple fission (Plasmodium � many daughter cells), Budding (yeast, Hydra � small outgrowth detaches), Fragmentation (Spirogyra, Planaria � each fragment grows into new organism), Regeneration (Hydra, Planaria � lost body parts can regrow into whole organism), Spore formation (fungi, ferns � sporangiospores, conidia), Vegetative propagation (plants � runners, rhizomes, bulbs, tubers, grafting, cuttings).

Sexual Reproduction

Involves two parents producing genetically diverse offspring through fusion of male and female gametes (fertilisation). Sequential events: Pre-fertilisation (gametogenesis, gamete transfer), Fertilisation (syngamy), Post-fertilisation (embryogenesis, development). Sexual cycles: Annual (one reproductive season per year), Biennial (two years), Perennial (multiple seasons). In animals: oviparous (egg-laying � birds, fish), viviparous (live birth � mammals), ovoviviparous (eggs retained in body � some snakes).

Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

Flower Structure

The flower is the reproductive shoot. The essential whorls are Androecium (stamens � male) and Gynoecium (carpels � female). A typical stamen has a filament (stalk) and anther (pollen-producing structure). Each anther has four microsporangia in young stage (two thecae, each with two microsporangia � dithecous). The microsporangium undergoes development into the pollen sac containing microspore mother cells (2n) that undergo meiosis to form haploid microspores (pollen grains).

Microsporogenesis

Formation of microspores (pollen grains) from microspore mother cells (MMC) by meiosis. Each MMC produces 4 haploid microspores (tetrad). The microspores separate and develop into pollen grains. Pollen grain structure: Exine (outer wall � made of sporopollenin, the most resistant organic material known; has apertures/germ pores) and Intine (inner wall � cellulose + pectin). Each pollen grain has a generative cell (divides to form 2 male gametes) and a vegetative cell (tube cell, larger, provides nourishment).

Pollen Grain Structure � Detailed Description

A mature pollen grain is typically two-celled (three-celled in some species � Brassica, Ageratum) with two distinct wall layers. The exine is the outer, thick layer composed of sporopollenin � the most resistant organic compound known, capable of withstanding high temperature, strong acids, and alkalis. It is acetolysis-resistant, which is why pollen grains are well-preserved in fossils (palynology). The exine has characteristic germ pores (apertures) through which the pollen tube emerges during germination. The intine is the inner, thin layer made of cellulose and pectin. The protoplast contains two cells: a large vegetative cell (tube cell) with abundant food reserves and a prominent nucleus, and a small generative cell that divides mitotically in the pollen tube to form two male gametes (sperm cells). Pollen viability varies � from a few minutes (in some cereals like wheat, rice) to several months (in members of Fabaceae, Rosaceae, Solanaceae). Pollen grains are stored in pollen banks at low temperature for crop breeding and conservation programs.

Example 4 � Pollen Grain Structure
Which of the following correctly lists the layers of a pollen grain from outermost to innermost?

a) Intine → Exine → Generative cell
b) Exine → Intine → Vegetative cell + Generative cell
c) Exine → Generative cell → Intine

Solution: Option (b). The pollen grain wall has exine (outer, sporopollenin) and intine (inner, cellulose+pectin). Inside are the vegetative cell and generative cell. The exine is the outermost layer, followed by intine, then the cellular contents.

Megasporogenesis

Formation of megaspores from the megaspore mother cell (MMC) in the nucellus of the ovule. The MMC (2n) undergoes meiosis to form 4 haploid megaspores. Usually, only one functional megaspore (the chalazal one) survives; the other three degenerate. The functional megaspore develops into the female gametophyte (embryo sac). The ovule is attached to the placenta by the funicle. The junction where the funicle meets the ovule is the hilum. Types: orthotropous (straight), anatropous (inverted � most common), campylotropous, amphitropous.

Embryo Sac Development

The functional megaspore (n) undergoes 3 mitotic divisions to form 8 nucleate embryo sac (Polygonum type/monosporic). The 8 nuclei arrange into: 3 antipodals (at chalazal end, degenerate after fertilisation), 2 polar nuclei (central cell � fuse to form secondary nucleus, which is 2n), 2 synergids (at micropylar end with filiform apparatus to guide pollen tube), 1 egg cell (female gamete). The filiform apparatus at the micropylar end of synergids secretes chemicals to attract the pollen tube.

The Polygonum type (monosporic, 8-nucleate) is the most common pattern, found in ~80% of angiosperms. However, other developmental patterns exist based on the number of megaspores that participate and the number of mitotic divisions:

Pollination

Transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma. Self-pollination (autogamy � same flower, e.g., pea, wheat; geitonogamy � different flowers of same plant � genetically self-pollination). Cross-pollination (xenogamy � different plants � increases genetic diversity). Agents: Wind (anemophilous � grass, maize � flowers small, without scent/nectar, large feathery stigma), Water (hydrophilous � Vallisneria, Hydrilla), Insects (entomophilous � bright petals, scent, nectar � bee, butterfly, moth), Birds (ornithophilous � large, bright, tubular flowers � hummingbird). Adaptations for self-pollination: Cleistogamous flowers (never open � Commelina, Viola) � ensures guaranteed seed set even without pollinators.

Fertilisation

The pollen grain germinates on the stigma, producing a pollen tube that grows through the style towards the embryo sac. The pollen tube enters the ovule through the micropyle (porogamy). The two sperm cells are released into the embryo sac. Double fertilisation (unique to angiosperms, discovered by Nawaschin): One sperm fuses with the egg cell (forming the diploid zygote). The other sperm fuses with the two polar nuclei forming the triploid (3n) primary endosperm cell. Thus, the endosperm is triploid in angiosperms � provides nourishment to the developing embryo. The zygote develops into the embryo (dicot: two cotyledons formed � the embryo axis with radicle and plumule). The triploid cell develops into the endosperm (cellular � coconut, nuclear � castor, helobial).

Double Fertilisation � Process and Significance

Discovered by Nawaschin (1898) in Lilium and Fritillaria. Double fertilisation is a defining and unique feature of angiosperms. Two sperm cells are released from the pollen tube into the embryo sac through the degenerated synergid:

  • Syngamy (Generative fertilisation): One sperm cell (n) fuses with the egg cell (n) → forms the diploid zygote (2n), which develops into the embryo.
  • Triple fusion (Vegetative fertilisation): The second sperm cell (n) fuses with the two polar nuclei (n + n = 2n) → forms the triploid primary endosperm cell (3n), which develops into the nutritive endosperm tissue.

Significance of Double Fertilisation: (1) Ensures endosperm development only occurs after successful fertilisation � no wastage of resources. (2) The triploid endosperm provides richer nutrition to the developing embryo compared to the haploid female gametophyte tissue in gymnosperms. (3) Double fertilisation is considered a key evolutionary innovation contributing to the dominance and adaptive success of angiosperms.

Seed & Fruit Formation

The fertilised ovule becomes the seed (containing embryo + sometimes endosperm + seed coat). The ovary develops into the fruit. Apomixis � seed formation without fertilisation (e.g., dandelion, some grasses). Polyembryony � more than one embryo in a seed (e.g., orange, lemon). Parthenocarpy � fruit development without fertilisation (seedless fruits � banana, grape). Parthenogenesis � development of an egg into an embryo without fertilisation (honey bee drones, some lizards).

Comparison with Gymnosperms: In gymnosperms, the endosperm is haploid (n) and develops from the female gametophyte before fertilisation. In angiosperms, the endosperm is triploid (3n) and develops only after double fertilisation. This makes angiosperm reproduction more efficient � resources are not invested in endosperm unless the egg is fertilised. Gymnosperms lack double fertilisation, vessels, and fruits. Understanding these differences helps explain why angiosperms are the dominant plant group today.

Embryogeny � Embryo Development

Dicot Embryo Development

The zygote (2n) divides transversely to form a smaller terminal cell and a larger basal cell. The terminal cell gives rise to the proembryo, while the basal cell forms the suspensor (attaches the embryo to the nucellus). The proembryo passes through distinct morphological stages: Globular stage (spherical mass of cells � undifferentiated), Heart-shaped stage (bilateral symmetry appears � two cotyledon primordia emerge, giving a heart shape), Torpedo stage (elongation of the embryo axis between cotyledons), and Cotyledonary stage (mature embryo with fully developed cotyledons). The mature dicot embryo has an embryo axis (tissue between cotyledons) with radicle (future root, towards micropyle) and plumule (future shoot, towards chalaza). The cotyledons may become photosynthetic and emerge above ground (epigeal) or remain underground as storage organs (hypogeal).

Monocot Embryo

In monocots (e.g., maize, wheat, rice), the mature embryo has a single, shield-shaped cotyledon called the scutellum (absorbs digested nutrients from the endosperm). The embryo axis is enclosed by two protective sheaths: coleoptile (covers the plumule and emerges first during germination) and coleorhiza (covers the radicle). The shoot apex is lateral, not terminal, in monocot embryos. The scutellum lies laterally between the embryo axis and the endosperm.

Human Reproduction

Male Reproductive System

Located in the pelvic region. The primary sex organs are paired testes (within the scrotum � maintains temperature 2-3 degrees C below body for spermatogenesis). Each testis has ~250 compartments (testicular lobules), each containing 1-3 highly coiled seminiferous tubules (site of spermatogenesis). Between the tubules are interstitial cells/Leydig cells (produce testosterone). Accessory ducts: Rete testis → Vasa efferentia → Epididymis (storage and maturation of sperm) → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct → Urethra. Accessory glands: Seminal vesicles (secrete fructose-rich fluid for sperm nutrition), Prostate gland (alkaline secretion neutralises vaginal acidity), Bulbourethral glands/Cowper's glands (pre-ejaculate, lubrication). Semen = sperm + secretions from all accessory glands.

Spermatogenesis

Process of sperm formation in seminiferous tubules. Begins at puberty. Takes ~64-74 days. Stages: Spermatogonia (2n) (mitotic divisions) → Primary spermatocytes (2n) → Meiosis I → Secondary spermatocytes (n) → Meiosis II → Spermatids (n) → Spermatozoa (n) via spermiogenesis (transformation � acrosome formation, tail, condensation of nucleus). Sertoli cells (nurse cells) nourish developing sperm. Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal axis: GnRH → FSH (acts on Sertoli cells) + LH (acts on Leydig cells → testosterone). One primary spermatocyte produces 4 functional sperm. Sperm structure: Head (acrosome + haploid nucleus), Neck (mitochondria + centriole), Middle piece (mitochondrial spiral � energy for motility), Tail (flagellum � propulsion). Human sperm count: 100-200 million/mL; if below 20 million/mL, there may be fertility issues.

Female Reproductive System

Primary sex organs: paired ovaries (produce ova, secrete estrogen and progesterone). Accessory ducts: Oviducts/Fallopian tubes (3 parts: infundibulum with fimbriae → ampulla → isthmus). Fertilisation occurs in the ampullary-isthmic junction. Uterus (womb) � three layers: Perimetrium (outer), Myometrium (thick muscular layer), Endometrium (inner lining, sheds in menstruation). Cervix (lower part of uterus). Vagina (birth canal + copulatory organ). External genitalia: Vulva (mons pubis, labia majora/minora, clitoris � homologous to penis). Mammary glands: Modified sweat glands � each has 15-20 mammary lobes separated by adipose tissue; lobes contain alveoli (milk-secreting cells) which drain into lactiferous ducts.

Oogenesis

Formation of mature egg (ovum) in the ovary. Begins before birth � Oogonia (2n) multiply mitotically in the fetal ovary → become Primary oocytes (2n) arrested at prophase I. At puberty, 60,000-80,000 primary oocytes remain. Each menstrual cycle, 1-2 primary oocytes complete Meiosis I → one Secondary oocyte (n) + one Polar body. The secondary oocyte is arrested at metaphase II. Ovulation: Release of secondary oocyte from the Graafian follicle. Meiosis II is completed only if fertilisation occurs. If fertilised, the secondary oocyte divides into Ovum (n) + second polar body. One primary oocyte produces 1 functional ovum + 3 polar bodies (unlike spermatogenesis where 4 functional sperm are produced from one primary spermatocyte).

NEET Tip � Gametogenesis Key Facts
Spermatogenesis: 1 primary spermatocyte → 4 functional sperm. Continuous, from puberty to death. Hormones: FSH (Sertoli cells) + LH (Leydig cells → testosterone). Oogenesis: 1 primary oocyte → 1 functional ovum + 3 polar bodies. Cyclic (monthly), with arrested stages. Hormones: FSH (follicle growth), LH (ovulation). Key difference: Meiosis II completes after fertilisation in oogenesis, but before release in spermatogenesis.

Menstrual Cycle

Monthly reproductive cycle in primates (~28 days). Regulated by hormones. Menstrual phase (Days 1-5): Endometrium sheds � bleeding (3-5 days). Follicular/Proliferative phase (Days 6-14): FSH stimulates follicle growth; estrogen rises; endometrium thickens. Ovulation (Day 14): LH surge triggers release of secondary oocyte. Luteal/Secretory phase (Days 15-28): LH transforms ruptured follicle into Corpus luteum (secretes progesterone to maintain endometrium). If fertilisation occurs � hCG from embryo maintains corpus luteum (pregnancy). If not � corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone drops → menstruation begins. Key hormones: GnRH (hypothalamus), FSH/LH (pituitary), Estrogen/Progesterone (ovaries).

Fertilisation, Implantation & Pregnancy

Fertilisation occurs in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. Only about 100-300 sperm reach the egg. The sperm penetrates the zona pellucida (capacitation + acrosome reaction). The entry of sperm activates the egg � completes Meiosis II. The male and female pronuclei fuse (syngamy) forming the zygote (2n). Cleavage: Rapid mitotic divisions of zygote producing smaller cells (blastomeres). The Morula (8-16 cells) forms at ~3-4 days. The Blastocyst (with inner cell mass + trophoblast) forms at ~5-7 days. Implantation: The blastocyst embeds in the endometrium (~7-8 days after fertilisation). The trophoblast cells invade the endometrium, secreting hCG (human Chorionic Gonadotropin) � detected in pregnancy tests.

Embryonic development: Germ layers form (gastrulation): Ectoderm (skin, nerves, eyes), Mesoderm (muscles, skeleton, kidneys, blood), Endoderm (gut lining, lungs). Extra-embryonic membranes: Amnion (fluid-filled cavity protects embryo), Chorion (outer, contributes to placenta), Allantois (waste storage), Yolk sac (early nutrition). Placenta: Formed by chorionic villi + uterine tissue. Functions: nutrition, respiration, excretion, hormone production (hCG, hPL, relaxin, estrogen, progesterone). The umbilical cord connects foetus to placenta. Gestation period: ~280 days (40 weeks) in humans. Parturition: Birth � triggered by oxytocin (from posterior pituitary) stimulating uterine contractions. The foetus is expelled through the cervix and vagina.

Placenta � Detailed Structure and Functions

The placenta is a temporary endocrine organ formed by the interdigitation of chorionic villi (foetal tissue) and uterine endometrium (maternal tissue). It serves as the critical interface between mother and foetus for:

  • Nutrition: Glucose, amino acids, vitamins, minerals, and ions pass from maternal blood to foetal blood via diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and active transport.
  • Respiration: O₂ diffuses from mother to foetus across the placental barrier; CO₂ diffuses in the reverse direction (from foetus to mother).
  • Excretion: Foetal waste products (urea, uric acid, CO₂) diffuse into maternal blood for elimination through maternal kidneys and lungs.
  • Endocrine function: The placenta secretes several essential hormones:
    • hCG (human Chorionic Gonadotropin): Maintains the corpus luteum during the first trimester; detected in pregnancy tests (ELISA-based urine test).
    • hPL (human Placental Lactogen): Regulates maternal glucose and lipid metabolism to ensure adequate nutrient supply to the foetus; has lactogenic activity.
    • Relaxin: Relaxes the pelvic ligaments and cervical smooth muscles, facilitating childbirth.
    • Progesterone: Maintains the endometrial lining, suppresses uterine contractions (quietens the uterus), supports implantation.
    • Estrogen: Promotes uterine growth, development of mammary glands, and regulation of other placental hormones.

Pregnancy Detection

Pregnancy tests detect hCG (human Chorionic Gonadotropin) in the mother's urine or blood. hCG is secreted by the trophoblast cells (syncytiotrophoblast) of the blastocyst soon after implantation (detectable ~6-12 days after fertilisation). The most common method is an ELISA-based (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) urine test using monoclonal antibodies specific for the beta-subunit of hCG. The test uses a "sandwich" immunoassay format: immobilised anti-hCG antibodies capture hCG from urine, and a second enzyme-linked antibody produces a colour change when hCG is present. Results are available within minutes. Blood tests (quantitative serum beta-hCG) can detect pregnancy as early as 6-8 days after ovulation, while home urine test kits are typically reliable after the missed period (~14 days post-ovulation).

Lactation

The mammary glands produce milk after childbirth under the influence of prolactin (from anterior pituitary). The initial milk colostrum (yellowish, rich in antibodies � IgA) is secreted for 2-3 days. Milk ejection (let-down) is controlled by oxytocin (reflex triggered by suckling). Breastfeeding provides passive immunity to the infant and helps the mother's uterus contract back to normal size.

Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs)

Infections transmitted through sexual contact. Bacterial: Gonorrhoea (Neisseria gonorrhoeae), Syphilis (Treponema pallidum), Chlamydiasis. Viral: HIV/AIDS (Human Immunodeficiency Virus), Genital herpes (Herpes simplex virus), Genital warts (HPV � Human Papilloma Virus), Hepatitis B. Protozoan: Trichomoniasis (Trichomonas vaginalis). Fungal: Candidiasis (Candida albicans). Most STDs are curable except HIV, genital herpes, and hepatitis B. Prevention: using condoms, avoiding multiple partners, prompt treatment. Early symptoms: itching, discharge, sores, pain during urination.

Infertility & Assisted Reproductive Technologies (ART)

Infertility: inability to conceive after 1 year of unprotected intercourse. Causes can be male (low sperm count, motility issues), female (blocked fallopian tubes, ovulation problems, endometriosis), or both. IVF (In Vitro Fertilisation): Egg and sperm fused in a petri dish; the zygote is cultured to 8-cell stage and transferred to the uterus. Test-tube baby = IVF embryo transfer (ET). Louise Brown (1978) � first test-tube baby. ZIFT (Zygote Intrafallopian Transfer) � zygote transferred to fallopian tube. ICSI (Intra Cytoplasmic Sperm Injection) � single sperm injected into egg (for male infertility). GIFT (Gamete Intrafallopian Transfer) � eggs and sperm transferred to fallopian tube. AI (Artificial Insemination) � semen collected and injected into the female tract (IUI � Intra Uterine Insemination). Surrogacy: Another woman carries the pregnancy for the intended parents. Sperm/egg banks: Freezing and storing gametes for future use.

Common Disorders of the Reproductive System

Male Reproductive Disorders

  • Cryptorchidism: Failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum during fetal development. If untreated, can lead to infertility due to higher abdominal temperature affecting spermatogenesis. Treated by orchidopexy (surgical descent) before age 2.
  • Varicocele: Abnormal enlargement of the veins within the spermatic cord (pampiniform plexus). Present in ~15% of males and ~40% of male infertility cases. Causes increased testicular temperature and oxidative stress.
  • Hydrocele: Fluid accumulation in the tunica vaginalis of the testis. Causes scrotal swelling but usually painless. Treated by aspiration or surgery.
  • Prostate disorders: Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) � non-cancerous enlargement of prostate in older men, causing urinary obstruction. Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers in men.
  • Erectile Dysfunction (ED): Inability to achieve or maintain an erection. Causes: vascular disease, diabetes, psychological factors, hormonal imbalances. Treated with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil � Viagra).

Female Reproductive Disorders

  • Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS): Most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-age women (5-15% prevalence). Characterised by hyperandrogenism (elevated male hormones), anovulation (irregular or absent periods), and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. Associated with insulin resistance, obesity, hirsutism, acne. Managed with lifestyle changes, metformin, oral contraceptives.
  • Endometriosis: Presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus (on ovaries, fallopian tubes, pelvic peritoneum). Causes severe pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea (painful periods), and infertility. Diagnosis by laparoscopy. Treatment includes hormonal therapy (GnRH agonists, oral contraceptives) and surgery.
  • Uterine fibroids (Leiomyomas): Benign smooth muscle tumours of the uterus. Very common (20-50% of women). May cause heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure, infertility. Treated with myomectomy or hysterectomy if symptomatic.
  • Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID): Infection of the upper female genital tract (uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries). Usually caused by untreated STDs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea). Can lead to tubal scarring, ectopic pregnancy, and infertility.
  • Ectopic pregnancy: Implantation of the embryo outside the uterine cavity (most commonly in the fallopian tube). A medical emergency � can cause tubal rupture and life-threatening haemorrhage. Incidence: ~1-2% of pregnancies.
  • Ovarian cysts: Fluid-filled sacs in the ovary. Most are functional and benign. Large cysts may cause pain, rupture, or torsion (twisting of the ovary).

Expanded Topics in Reproductive Health

Population Explosion and Birth Control

The world population has grown exponentially from ~2 billion in 1900 to over 7.9 billion today. India itself has crossed 1.4 billion, making it the most populous country. This rapid growth is attributed to: (1) Decline in death rates due to improved healthcare and sanitation, (2) Decline in maternal and infant mortality, (3) Increased life expectancy. The Statistical Manual / Family Planning Programme in India was initiated in 1951 to promote birth control methods. The current National Population Policy (NPP) 2000 aims to stabilise population by 2045. Key strategies: promoting small family norm (Hum Do Hamare Do), free contraceptives, incentives for sterilisation, promoting female education and raising marriage age. The ideal contraceptive should be effective, reversible, easily available, affordable, and have no long-term side effects. Contraceptive prevalence in India has increased from 13% in 1970 to ~67% currently, but unmet need remains high in rural areas.

Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)

MTP refers to the intentional termination of pregnancy before the foetus becomes viable (before 20 weeks in India, though definitions vary). The MTP Act (1971, amended 2021) in India permits abortion under specific conditions: (1) Up to 20 weeks with the opinion of one doctor, (2) 20-24 weeks with the opinion of two doctors for specific categories (rape survivors, minors, vulnerable women), (3) Beyond 24 weeks only if substantial foetal abnormalities are diagnosed by a medical board. Unsafe abortions account for ~8% of maternal deaths worldwide (WHO). MTP is safer when performed in the first trimester (up to 12 weeks). Contraceptive failure is a common reason for seeking MTP. Sex-selective abortion is illegal under the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PCPNDT) Act, 1994. Amniocentesis (foetal chromosome analysis) is banned for sex determination but allowed for detecting genetic disorders like Down syndrome, haemophilia, thalassemia, and muscular dystrophy.

Prenatal Development � Stages in Detail

Human prenatal development spans ~280 days (40 weeks, 9 calendar months) and is divided into three trimesters. First trimester (Weeks 1-12): Fertilisation to completion of organogenesis. The embryonic period (weeks 3-8) sees formation of all major organ systems � heart starts beating by week 4, limb buds appear week 5, facial features form weeks 6-8. By week 12, the foetus is ~9 cm long and all organs are present. Second trimester (Weeks 13-27): Rapid growth and maturation. Foetal movements felt by mother (quickening, ~18-20 weeks). Hair, nails, and fingerprints form. The foetus can hear sounds by week 20. Viability (ability to survive outside the womb) begins around week 24. Third trimester (Weeks 28-40): Rapid weight gain. Lungs mature (surfactant production increases from week 34). The foetus assumes the head-down position (engagement) by week 36-38. Full term is 37-40 weeks. Premature birth (before 37 weeks) carries risks of respiratory distress, temperature instability, and feeding difficulties.

Summary � Key Terms in Reproduction

  • Gametogenesis: Formation of haploid gametes (sperm and ovum) by meiosis.
  • Microsporogenesis: Formation of pollen grains from microspore mother cells in the anther.
  • Megasporogenesis: Formation of megaspores from megaspore mother cells in the nucellus.
  • Pollination: Transfer of pollen from anther to stigma.
  • Double fertilisation: Fusion of one sperm with egg (syngamy) and another with polar nuclei (triple fusion).
  • Endosperm: Triploid nutritive tissue in angiosperm seeds.
  • Cleavage: Rapid mitotic divisions of the zygote forming blastomeres.
  • Morula: Solid ball of 8-16 cells, ~3-4 days after fertilisation.
  • Blastocyst: Fluid-filled structure with inner cell mass and trophoblast, ~5-7 days.
  • Implantation: Embedding of blastocyst in the endometrium (~7-8 days).
  • Gastrulation: Formation of three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm).
  • Placenta: Temporary endocrine organ formed by chorionic villi and uterine tissue.
  • Parturition: Childbirth � expulsion of foetus from uterus.
  • Lactation: Milk production and secretion from mammary glands.
  • Apomixis: Seed formation without fertilisation.
  • Parthenocarpy: Fruit development without fertilisation (seedless fruits).
  • Polyembryony: Multiple embryos in a single seed.
  • Parthenogenesis: Development of egg into embryo without fertilisation.
  • Capacitation: Changes in sperm membrane enabling fertilisation, occurring in female tract.
  • Acrosome reaction: Release of hydrolytic enzymes from sperm acrosome to penetrate egg coats.

Key NEET Differences � High-Yield Comparison Points

  • Microsporogenesis vs Megasporogenesis: Micro � pollen formation in anther (microsporangium); Mega � megaspore formation in nucellus of ovule (megasporangium).
  • Exine vs Intine: Exine � outer, thick, sporopollenin (resistant); Intine � inner, thin, cellulose + pectin (grows into pollen tube).
  • Self vs Cross Pollination: Autogamy (same flower) → inbreeding; Xenogamy (different plant) → outbreeding, genetic variation.
  • Syngamy vs Triple Fusion: Syngamy � sperm + egg → zygote (2n); Triple fusion � sperm + 2 polar nuclei → primary endosperm (3n).
  • Nuclear vs Cellular vs Helobial Endosperm: Nuclear � free divisions first; Cellular � walls with each division; Helobial � intermediate, two chambers.
  • Radicle vs Plumule: Radicle → future root (towards micropyle); Plumule → future shoot (towards chalaza).
  • Epigeal vs Hypogeal Germination: Epigeal � cotyledons above ground (hypocotyl elongates); Hypogeal � cotyledons underground (epicotyl elongates).
  • Spermatogenesis vs Oogenesis: 4 functional sperm per primary spermatocyte vs 1 functional ovum + 3 polar bodies; continuous vs cyclic.
  • Sertoli vs Leydig Cells: Sertoli (nurse cells) � nourish sperm, FSH target; Leydig (interstitial cells) � produce testosterone, LH target.
  • FSH vs LH: FSH � follicle/sperm development; LH � ovulation / testosterone production.
  • Estrogen vs Progesterone: Estrogen � proliferative phase, peaks before ovulation; Progesterone � secretory/luteal phase, maintains endometrium.
  • hCG vs hPL: hCG � maintains corpus luteum (pregnancy test target); hPL � regulates maternal metabolism for foetal nutrition.
  • Oxytocin vs Prolactin: Oxytocin � milk ejection, uterine contraction (parturition); Prolactin � milk production (lactation).
  • Vasectomy vs Tubectomy: Vasectomy � cut/tie vas deferens (male); Tubectomy � cut/tie fallopian tubes (female). Both permanent.
  • IUD Types: Non-medicated (Lippes loop) vs Copper-releasing (CuT) vs Hormone-releasing (Progestasert).
  • MTP vs Amniocentesis: MTP � medical termination of pregnancy; Amniocentesis � foetal genetic testing (banned for sex determination in India).
  • IVF vs ICSI vs GIFT vs ZIFT: IVF � fertilisation in vitro; ICSI � sperm injected into egg; GIFT � gametes to tube; ZIFT � zygote to tube.
  • Placental Hormones: hCG (early pregnancy), hPL (metabolism), Relaxin (pelvic relaxation), Progesterone (maintains pregnancy), Estrogen (uterine growth).

Always read the question carefully � NEET often asks "which is NOT correct" or "which is the exception".
