Lie Theory — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras & the Classification of Semisimple Groups

Lie theory studies continuous symmetry — the mathematical framework underlying gauge theories, particle physics, and geometry. A Lie group G is a smooth manifold endowed with smooth group operations (multiplication and inversion). Its Lie algebra 𝔤 = TeG is the tangent space at the identity, equipped with a bilinear bracket [·,·] that satisfies antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity. The exponential map exp: 𝔤 → G connects infinitesimal generators to group elements. The classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras via Dynkin diagrams (An, Bn, Cn, Dn, E6, E7, E8, F4, G2) is a crowning achievement of 19th- and 20th-century mathematics. The Weyl character formula and highest weight theory describe all finite-dimensional irreducible representations. Applications permeate modern physics: the Standard Model gauge group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1), the Poincaré group and Wigner's classification, and the exceptional group E8 in string theory.

"Lie groups are the mathematical language of continuous symmetry."
— Sophus Lie
Part 1

📏 Lie Groups and Their Lie Algebras

Foundations
Smooth Manifolds, Lie Brackets, and the Exponential Map
A Lie group G is a smooth manifold with smooth multiplication μ: G×G → G and inversion ι: G → G. The Lie algebra 𝔤 = TeG carries the Lie bracket [X,Y] = ad(X)(Y) induced by the adjoint action of G on itself. Explicitly, for X,Y ∈ 𝔤, [X,Y] = d/dt|_{t=0} Ad(exp(tX))(Y). The bracket is antisymmetric [X,Y] = −[Y,X] and satisfies the Jacobi identity: [X,[Y,Z]] + [Y,[Z,X]] + [Z,[X,Y]] = 0. The exponential map exp: 𝔤 → G is a local diffeomorphism near 0; for matrix groups, exp(X) = Σk=0^∞ X^k/k!. The adjoint representation Ad: G → GL(𝔤) is defined by Ad(g)(X) = gXg^{-1} (for matrix groups), and its derivative ad: 𝔤 → 𝔤𝔩(𝔤) satisfies ad(X)(Y) = [X,Y]. The Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula: exp(X)exp(Y) = exp(X + Y + ½[X,Y] + 1/12[X,[X,Y]] − 1/12[Y,[X,Y]] + ...). A fundamental theorem: there is a bijection between Lie subalgebras of 𝔤 and connected immersed Lie subgroups of G. Engel's theorem: a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is nilpotent iff ad(X) is nilpotent for every X ∈ 𝔤. Lie's theorem: every finite-dimensional irreducible representation of a solvable Lie algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0 has a common eigenvector — hence every such representation is 1-dimensional. The Levi decomposition states that any finite-dimensional Lie algebra is a semidirect product 𝔤 = 𝔯 ⋊ 𝔰 of its radical 𝔯 (maximal solvable ideal) and a semisimple subalgebra 𝔰 (the Levi factor).
The Lie algebra linearizes the group near the identity; the exponential map recovers local group structure, and the bracket encodes infinitesimal non-commutativity.
Part 2

🌍 Semisimple Lie Algebras and the Killing Form

Semisimple
Cartan's Criterion, Root Systems, and Dynkin Diagrams
The Killing form B(X,Y) = tr(ad(X)∘ad(Y)) is a symmetric bilinear form on 𝔤. Cartan's first criterion: 𝔤 is solvable iff B(X,Y) = 0 for all X ∈ 𝔤 and Y ∈ [𝔤,𝔤]. Cartan's second criterion: 𝔤 is semisimple (a direct sum of simple ideals) iff B is non-degenerate. A semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 decomposes into simple ideals: 𝔤 = ⊕i 𝔤i, each with no nontrivial ideals. Choose a Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 — a maximal abelian subalgebra consisting of ad-diagonalizable elements (a toral subalgebra). For 𝔤 = 𝔰𝔩(n,ℂ), 𝔥 consists of diagonal matrices with trace zero. The root space decomposition: 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⊕α∈Δ 𝔤α where 𝔤α = {X∈𝔤: [h,X]=α(h)X for all h∈𝔥} and α∈𝔥^* are roots. The root system Δ ⊂ 𝔥^* is a finite set satisfying: (i) Δ spans 𝔥^*; (ii) α∈Δ ⇒ −α∈Δ; (iii) for α,β∈Δ, the reflection sα(β) = β − 2⟨β,α⟩/⟨α,α⟩·α lies in Δ; (iv) 2⟨β,α⟩/⟨α,α⟩ ∈ ℤ (integrality). The simple roots Π = {α1,...,αr} form a basis such that every root is a linear combination with integer coefficients all of the same sign. The Cartan matrix Aij = 2⟨αi,αj⟩/⟨αj,αj⟩ encodes the geometry: Aii = 2, Aij ≤ 0 for i≠j. The Dynkin diagram is a graph with r nodes (simple roots), with AijAji edges between node i and j (0,1,2,3) and an arrow pointing to the shorter root when edges > 1. The classification of complex simple Lie algebras yields four infinite families: An (𝔰𝔩(n+1,ℂ)), Bn (𝔰𝔬(2n+1,ℂ)), Cn (𝔰𝔭(2n,ℂ)), Dn (𝔰𝔬(2n,ℂ)); and five exceptional types: E6, E7, E8, F4, G2.
The Killing form detects semisimplicity; root systems provide a combinatorial blueprint for the entire structure of a semisimple Lie algebra, classified by Dynkin diagrams.
Part 3

🧮 The Weyl Character Formula and Highest Weight Theory

Representations
Highest Weight Modules, the Weyl Group, and Character Formulas
The Weyl group W is the finite reflection group generated by simple reflections si acting on 𝔥^*: si(α) = α − 2⟨α,αi⟩/⟨αi,αi⟩·αi. It permutes the roots and is isomorphic to the quotient NG(𝔥)/ZG(𝔥). The dominant Weyl chamber is the closed convex cone {λ∈𝔥^*: ⟨λ,αi⟩ ≥ 0 for all simple roots αi}. A highest weight module V(λ) is an irreducible representation with a vector vλ (the highest weight vector) such that: eα·vλ = 0 for all positive roots α, and h·vλ = λ(h)vλ for h∈𝔥. The theorem of the highest weight: every finite-dimensional irreducible representation of a complex semisimple Lie algebra has a unique highest weight λ ∈ 𝔥^* (dominant integral), and there is a bijection between dominant integral weights and isomorphism classes of irreducible representations. The representation V(λ) can be constructed as V(λ) = U(𝔤) / J(λ) where J(λ) is the left ideal generated by positive root spaces 𝔫+ and elements h−λ(h). The Weyl character formula: ch V(λ) = (Σw∈W ε(w) e^{w(λ+ρ)}) / (Πα>0 (1−e^{−α})), where ρ = ½Σα>0 α is the half-sum of positive roots and ε(w) = (−1)^{ℓ(w)} is the sign of w. The Weyl dimension formula: dim V(λ) = Πα>0 ⟨λ+ρ,α⟩ / ⟨ρ,α⟩. The Borel-Weil-Bott theorem: for a dominant integral weight λ, the irreducible representation V(λ) can be realized as H0(G/B, Lλ), the space of holomorphic sections of a line bundle Lλ on the flag variety G/B. More generally, Hq(G/B, Lλ) = 0 for q ≠ ℓ(w) when λ + ρ is regular, and equals V(w(λ+ρ)−ρ) for q = ℓ(w). The Kostant multiplicity formula: the multiplicity mλ(μ) of a weight μ in V(λ) is given by mλ(μ) = Σw∈W ε(w) P(w(λ+ρ)−μ−ρ), where P is the Kostant partition function counting ways to express a weight as a sum of positive roots.
Highest weight theory reduces the classification of irreducible representations to combinatorics; the Weyl character formula is one of the deepest results in representation theory.
Part 4

🔥 Real Forms and Applications to Physics

Physics
Cartan Decomposition, Iwasawa Decomposition, and Particle Physics
A real form of a complex semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤ℂ is a real Lie algebra 𝔤ℝ such that 𝔤ℂ = 𝔤ℝ ⊗ℝ ℂ. Every complex semisimple Lie algebra has a compact real form 𝔤u — the fixed points of a Cartan involution θ (a Lie algebra involution with negative-definite Killing form on the −1 eigenspace). The Cartan decomposition: 𝔤 = 𝔨 ⊕ 𝔭, where 𝔨 is the +1 eigenspace of θ (a maximal compact subalgebra) and 𝔭 is the −1 eigenspace. For SL(n,ℝ), the Cartan involution is θ(X) = −XT, giving 𝔨 = 𝔰𝔬(n) and 𝔭 = symmetric matrices. The Iwasawa decomposition at the group level: G = KAN (unique factorization), where K is a maximal compact subgroup, A is abelian (from a maximal split torus), and N is nilpotent. Real forms are classified by Satake diagrams (Dynkin diagrams with involutions and painted nodes): types AI, AII, AIII, BDI, CI, CII, DIII, EI–EIX, FI, FII, G, etc. Symmetric spaces G/K are Riemannian manifolds with a geodesic-reversing isometry at every point — these are the non-compact analogues of compact Lie groups. Applications in physics: the Standard Model gauge group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) is a product of compact Lie groups. Quarks transform in the fundamental representation of SU(3) (triplet) and SU(2) (doublet); leptons are SU(3) singlets and SU(2) doublets. The Poincaré group ISO(3,1) = ℝ4 ⋊ SO(3,1) (semidirect product of translations and Lorentz transformations) governs relativistic quantum mechanics. Wigner's classification: irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group are classified by mass m ≥ 0 and spin s ∈ ½ℤ (for m>0, the little group is SU(2); for m=0, the little group is E(2) — the Euclidean group in 2D). The spin groups Spin(n) are the double covers of SO(n); they are essential for describing fermions. The universal cover of the Lorentz group is SL(2,ℂ). The exceptional Lie group E8 plays a central role in the E8×E8 heterotic string theory — its root system has 240 roots and its Weyl group has order 696,729,600. The classification of conjugacy classes in the Weyl group and the study of nilpotent orbits (the Jacobson-Morozov theorem, the Bala-Carter classification) have deep connections to the geometry of the flag variety and the Springer correspondence.
Real forms of Lie algebras correspond to different signatures of invariant metrics; Lie theory provides the language for gauge theories, particle classification, and string theory.
