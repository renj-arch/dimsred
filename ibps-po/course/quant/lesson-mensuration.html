What is Mensuration?

Mensuration deals with the measurement of geometric figures — their area, perimeter, surface area, and volume. the exam typically asks 2-3 questions from this topic covering 2D shapes (triangles, circles, rectangles) and 3D shapes (cubes, cylinders, cones, spheres).

Key Concepts

1. 2D Shapes: Area of triangle = (1/2) * base * height. Area of circle = pi*r^2. Circumference = 2*pi*r. Area of rectangle = l * b. Area of square = side^2. Area of trapezium = (1/2) * (sum of parallel sides) * height.

2. 3D Shapes: Volume of cube = side^3. Surface area = 6 * side^2. Volume of cuboid = l * b * h. Volume of cylinder = pi*r^2*h. Curved surface area = 2*pi*r*h. Volume of cone = (1/3)*pi*r^2*h. Volume of sphere = (4/3)*pi*r^3. Surface area = 4*pi*r^2.

3. Combined & Hollow Shapes: Volume of hollow cylinder = pi*h*(R^2 - r^2). Volume of frustum of cone = (1/3)*pi*h*(R^2 + r^2 + Rr).

4. Heron Formula: For a triangle with sides a, b, c: semi-perimeter s = (a+b+c)/2. Area = sqrt[s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)].

Solved Examples

Example 1 (2D — Circle)
Find the area of a circle whose circumference is 88 cm. (Use pi = 22/7)

a) 616 cm^2 b) 628 cm^2 c) 640 cm^2 d) 656 cm^2
Solution: a) 616 cm^2. 2*pi*r = 88. r = 88/(2*pi) = 14 cm. Area = pi*r^2 = (22/7) * 196 = 616 cm^2.
Example 2 (3D — Cylinder)
Find the volume of a cylinder with radius 7 cm and height 10 cm. (Use pi = 22/7)

a) 1440 cm^3 b) 1540 cm^3 c) 1600 cm^3 d) 1650 cm^3
Solution: b) 1540 cm^3. Volume = pi*r^2*h = (22/7) * 49 * 10 = 1540 cm^3.
Example 3 (Triangle — Heron)
Find the area of a triangle with sides 13 cm, 14 cm, and 15 cm.

a) 64 cm^2 b) 74 cm^2 c) 84 cm^2 d) 94 cm^2
Solution: c) 84 cm^2. s = (13+14+15)/2 = 21. Area = sqrt[21(21-13)(21-14)(21-15)] = sqrt[21*8*7*6] = sqrt7056 = 84 cm^2.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Use pi = 22/7 or 3.14 based on the numbers. If radius is a multiple of 7, use 22/7 for exact calculation.
  • For area of equilateral triangle, use sqrt3/4 * side^2. sqrt3 approx = 1.732.
  • Surface area to volume ratio decreases as size increases. This helps in checking answers for similar shapes.
  • For combined solids, add individual volumes but be careful with surface areas — common faces are not counted.
Pro Tip: The exam often gives pi = 22/7 in the problem statement. If not specified, use 22/7 when radius is a multiple of 7, and 3.14 otherwise. Memorise common squares and cubes for faster calculation.

Derivation and Interrelationships of 3D Formulas

Understanding how the standard volume and surface‑area formulas arise from first principles gives a decisive edge when a question asks for a “missing dimension” or when a solid is sliced in an unconventional way. The volume of a cylinder, V = πr²h, follows directly from stacking infinitesimally thin circular discs of area πr² along the height h. The curved surface area, CSA = 2πrh, is the lateral area of the rectangle that wraps the cylinder when it is unrolled — its width equals the circumference 2πr and its height equals h. A cone can be visualised as a pyramid with a circular base; its volume is one‑third of the cylinder that shares the same base and height, giving V = ⅓πr²h. The slant height l = √(r² + h²) appears when the lateral surface is flattened into a sector of a circle of radius l; the sector’s arc length equals the base circumference 2πr, so the curved surface area becomes πrl. For a sphere, the classic derivation uses the method of exhaustion or Cavalieri’s principle: the volume equals the volume of a cylinder of radius r and height 2r minus the volume of two cones of the same dimensions, yielding V = ⁴⁄₃πr³. The total surface area 4πr² follows from differentiating the volume with respect to r (dV/dr = surface area) or from integrating the surface area of infinitesimal zones. Recognising these links lets you reconstruct a forgotten formula in seconds and spot when a problem expects you to combine a cylinder and a cone sharing a common radius.

When a solid is truncated, the frustum formulas emerge by subtracting the removed top portion from the original solid. For a cone frustum with radii R (bottom) and r (top) and height h, the volume is V = ⅓πh(R² + r² + Rr). The curved surface area uses the slant height of the frustum L = √[(R−r)² + h²] and becomes π(R + r)L. A hollow cylinder (pipe) is simply the difference between two coaxial cylinders: V = πh(R² − r²) and total surface area = outer CSA + inner CSA + area of two annular ends = 2πh(R + r) + 2π(R² − r²). Memorising the “difference of squares” pattern R² − r² = (R−r)(R+r) accelerates arithmetic, especially when the wall thickness t = R−r is given instead of the two radii.

Similarity ratios provide a powerful shortcut: if two solids are geometrically similar with linear scale factor k, then their surface areas scale by k² and volumes by k³. This principle eliminates the need to compute individual dimensions when a problem states “the radius of a sphere is doubled” — the volume becomes eight times larger and the surface area four times larger. The same logic applies to any pair of similar cones, cylinders, or pyramids. In competitive papers, a typical twist is to give the ratio of volumes and ask for the ratio of heights or radii; applying the cubic root or square root respectively yields the answer instantly.

Another interrelationship appears in the “surface‑area‑to‑volume” ratio, which decreases as size increases. For a sphere, the ratio is 3/r; for a cube, it is 6/a. This concept is frequently tested in questions about heat loss, material cost, or biological scaling. Knowing that a larger sphere retains heat more efficiently than a smaller one can guide you to the correct qualitative answer even before any numeric computation.

Finally, the concept of “net” of a solid — the 2‑D layout obtained by cutting along edges — connects surface‑area problems to planar geometry. The net of a cylinder consists of a rectangle (CSA) and two circles (ends). The net of a cone is a sector of a circle (CSA) plus a base circle. Visualising nets helps when a question asks for the area of material required to fabricate a container, including allowances for seams or overlaps.

Combined and Hollow Solids: Volume and Surface Area Strategies

Problems that fuse two or more basic solids — such as a cylinder capped by a hemisphere, a cone mounted on a cylinder, or a hollow sphere with a cylindrical bore — test the ability to decompose a composite figure into manageable parts. The golden rule: volumes always add, while surface areas add only the exposed faces. When a hemisphere sits on a cylinder, the circular interface is internal and must be subtracted from the total surface area. The combined volume is simply V_cyl + V_hemi = πr²h + ⅔πr³. The total surface area becomes CSA_cyl + CSA_hemi + area of bottom base = 2πrh + 2πr² + πr² = 2πrh + 3πr². A common error is to include the top base of the cylinder or the flat face of the hemisphere; both are hidden.

For a solid formed by drilling a cylindrical hole through the centre of a sphere, the remaining volume equals the sphere’s volume minus the cylinder’s volume plus the volume of the two spherical caps that were removed with the cylinder. However, a classic shortcut (the “napkin ring” theorem) states that the remaining volume depends only on the height h of the cylindrical hole, not on the sphere’s radius. The formula V = πh³/6 is a favourite in higher‑difficulty sets. Recognising this theorem can turn a seemingly messy integration into a one‑line answer.

Hollow solids such as pipes, spherical shells, and conical shells are handled by the “outer minus inner” principle. For a spherical shell with outer radius R and inner radius r, volume = ⁴⁄₃π(R³ − r³). The total surface area includes both outer and inner curved surfaces: 4πR² + 4πr². If the shell is open (e.g., a hemispherical bowl with thickness), only the exposed surfaces count. When thickness t is given instead of r, substitute r = R − t and expand using binomial identities to avoid large numbers.

Combined solids often appear in “water‑displacement” or “capacity” questions. A typical scenario: a cylindrical tank of radius R and height H contains a solid cone of radius r and height h placed vertex‑down. The water level rise equals the cone’s volume divided by the tank’s base area: ΔH = (⅓πr²h) / (πR²) = (r²h) / (3R²). If the cone floats, only the submerged volume matters, introducing density ratios. Practising these decomposition steps builds the habit of writing “V_total = ΣV_parts” and “A_exposed = ΣA_parts − ΣA_hidden” on the rough sheet before any arithmetic.

Surface‑area optimisation in combined solids is another frequent twist. For a given volume, a cylinder with a hemispherical top has minimum surface area when the cylinder’s height equals its radius. Deriving this uses calculus (dA/dh = 0) but the result can be memorised: h = r for the cylinder portion. Knowing such optimal proportions lets you answer “which dimensions minimise material cost?” without performing differentiation in the exam hall.

Worked Example — Composite Solid
A solid toy is made by attaching a hemisphere of radius 7 cm to the top of a right circular cylinder of the same radius. The total height of the toy is 31 cm. Find the total surface area of the toy. (Use π = 22/7)
The cylinder height = total height − hemisphere radius = 31 cm − 7 cm = 24 cm.
Curved surface area of cylinder = 2πrh = 2 × (22/7) × 7 × 24 = 1056 cm².
Curved surface area of hemisphere = 2πr² = 2 × (22/7) × 49 = 308 cm².
Base of cylinder (bottom) = πr² = (22/7) × 49 = 154 cm².
The circular face where hemisphere meets cylinder is internal, so it is not counted.
Total surface area = 1056 + 308 + 154 = 1518 cm².
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a composite solid appears, sketch a quick net on the rough sheet. Label every face as “exposed” or “hidden”. Write the volume sum and surface‑area sum formulas before plugging numbers. This prevents the most common mistake — counting an internal interface twice — and saves 30–40 seconds per question.

Optimization Problems: Maximising Volume for Given Surface Area

Optimization questions ask for the dimensions that give the greatest volume (or least surface area) under a constraint. The classic pair is a cylinder with a fixed total surface area S. The total surface area of a closed cylinder is S = 2πr² + 2πrh. Express h = (S − 2πr²) / (2πr) and substitute into the volume V = πr²h to obtain V = (Sr)/2 − πr³. Differentiating with respect to r and setting dV/dr = 0 yields S/2 − 3πr² = 0, so r = √(S / 6π) and h = 2r. Hence the optimal closed cylinder has height equal to its diameter. For an open‑top cylinder (no top lid), the surface area is S = πr² + 2πrh; the same process gives h = r. Memorising these two results — h = 2r for closed, h = r for open — allows instant answers without calculus.

A sphere encloses the maximum volume for a given surface area among all solids (isoperimetric inequality). If a question asks “which shape gives the largest volume for a fixed surface area?”, the answer is always a sphere. Conversely, for a fixed volume, the sphere has the minimum surface area. This principle underpins many “material‑saving” problems: a manufacturer wanting to minimise tin for a given capacity should choose a spherical container, but practical constraints often force a cylindrical shape, leading to the cylinder optimisation above.

For a cone with a given slant height l, the volume is maximised when the semi‑vertical angle is 45°, i.e., when r = h = l/√2. The derivation uses V = ⅓πr²√(l² − r²) and differentiation. The maximum volume equals (2πl³) / (27√3). While the exact constant is rarely required, knowing the condition r = h helps in multiple‑choice questions that present dimension pairs.

In a composite solid such as a cylinder topped by a hemisphere (a “silos” shape) with fixed total surface area, the volume is maximised when the cylinder’s height equals its radius. The proof follows the same substitution method: total surface area S = 2πrh + 3πr² (cylinder CSA + hemisphere CSA + bottom base). Express h = (S − 3πr²) / (2πr), substitute into V = πr²h + ⅔πr³, differentiate, and solve to get h = r. This result is a favourite in advanced papers because it combines two solids and tests the “hidden face” concept simultaneously.

When the constraint is a fixed sum of dimensions (e.g., r + h = constant) rather than surface area, the optimisation changes. For a cylinder with r + h = k, volume V = πr²(k − r). Differentiating gives 2r(k − r) − r² = 0 → r = 2k/3, h = k/3. Recognising the pattern “quadratic in r” lets you solve such problems in under a minute.

Ratio and Proportion in Similar Solids

Similarity is the single most powerful tool for scaling problems. If two solids are similar with linear scale factor k (every length in the second solid is k times the corresponding length in the first), then:
• Corresponding areas (base area, curved surface area, total surface area) scale by k².
• Volumes scale by k³.
This holds for any pair of similar cones, cylinders, pyramids, spheres, or any combination thereof. The scale factor can be derived from any given pair of corresponding linear measurements — radii, heights, slant heights, or even perimeters of bases.

A typical question: “The volumes of two similar cylinders are in the ratio 27 : 64. Find the ratio of their heights.” Since volume ratio = k³, k = ∛(27/64) = 3/4. Height ratio = k = 3 : 4. Another variant gives the ratio of curved surface areas (which is k²) and asks for the ratio of radii — again k = √(area ratio). The key is to identify which measurement the given ratio refers to (linear, area, or volume) and then apply the appropriate root.

When a solid is cut by a plane parallel to its base, the resulting top piece is similar to the original solid. For a cone of height H cut at height h from the vertex, the small cone’s linear scale factor is h/H. Its volume is (h/H)³ times the original volume. The frustum’s volume is the difference: V_frustum = V_original [1 − (h/H)³]. This relationship is often used in “water‑level” problems where a cone‑shaped vessel is partially filled.

In problems involving a sphere inscribed in a cube or a cylinder inscribed in a sphere, similarity does not apply directly, but the ratio of dimensions is fixed by geometry. For a sphere inscribed in a cube, the sphere’s diameter equals the cube’s side a. The volume ratio is (π/6) ≈ 0.5236. For a cylinder inscribed in a sphere of radius R, the cylinder’s height h and radius r satisfy r² + (h/2)² = R². The maximum volume of such a cylinder occurs when h = 2R/√3 and r = R√(2/3). Knowing these standard configurations saves derivation time.

Proportional reasoning also extends to “thickness” problems. A hollow sphere of outer radius R and thickness t has inner radius r = R − t. The volume of material is ⁴⁄₃π(R³ − r³). If the thickness is small relative to R, the binomial approximation R³ − (R−t)³ ≈ 3R²t gives a quick estimate: material volume ≈ 4πR²t, which is simply the surface area of the outer sphere times the thickness. This approximation is frequently used in “thin‑shell” questions where exact arithmetic would be cumbersome.

Mensuration in Data Interpretation Sets

Modern quantitative sections embed mensuration inside data‑interpretation (DI) tables, bar charts, or caselets. A typical DI set might present a table of cylindrical tanks with varying radii and heights, asking for the total volume of water stored, the tank with the highest surface‑area‑to‑volume ratio, or the cost of painting the curved surfaces at a given rate per square metre. The workflow: extract the numeric parameters, decide which formula applies to each row, compute the required quantity, and then perform the comparative or aggregate operation (sum, average, maximum). Speed comes from recognising that many rows share the same radius or height, allowing factor‑wise computation.

For example, a table lists five cylinders with radii 7, 14, 21, 28, 35 cm and a common height of 10 cm. The curved surface area of each is 2πrh = 20πr. Since 20π is constant, the CSA values are directly proportional to the radii. The total CSA = 20π Σr. Computing the sum of radii (105) and multiplying once is far faster than five separate multiplications. The same principle applies to volumes: V = πr²h = 10πr²; sum of squares of radii can be pre‑computed or looked up from a squares table.

Caselets often describe a manufacturing process: “A factory produces hemispherical bowls of inner radius 10 cm and thickness 0.5 cm. The material costs ₹120 per cm³. Find the cost per bowl.” Here the volume of material = outer volume − inner volume = ⅔π(R³ − r³) with R = 10.5, r = 10. Using the binomial approximation R³ − r³ ≈ 3r²t = 3×100×0.5 = 150 gives material volume ≈ ⅔π×150 = 100π ≈ 314 cm³. Multiplying by ₹120 yields ≈ ₹37,680. The approximation is acceptable when answer choices are spaced widely; otherwise exact arithmetic with π = 22/7 is required.

Another DI pattern: a bar chart shows the heights of several cones with the same base radius. The question asks for the ratio of the largest volume to the smallest volume. Since volume ∝ height for fixed radius, the ratio equals the ratio of the corresponding bar heights — no formula needed beyond reading the chart. Recognising such direct proportionality eliminates unnecessary calculations.

Finally, some sets combine mensuration with percentages: “A cylindrical pipe’s radius is increased by 20 % and its height decreased by 10 %. Find the percentage change in volume.” Volume ∝ r²h, so new volume factor = (1.2)² × 0.9 = 1.44 × 0.9 = 1.296, i.e., a 29.6 % increase. Mastering the “percentage‑change‑in‑product” shortcut (sum of percentage changes with interaction term) turns a multi‑step problem into a one‑liner.

  • Counting an internal face twice when adding surface areas of combined solids.
  • Using the cylinder volume formula for a cone (forgetting the ⅓ factor).
  • Applying the similarity area ratio (k²) when the problem gives a volume ratio (k³).
  • Ignoring the “hidden” base in a solid that sits on another solid, leading to an extra πr² term.
  • Mixing π = 22/7 and π = 3.14 in the same calculation, producing a non‑matching answer choice.
  • Assuming the optimal cylinder for minimum surface area has h = r for a closed cylinder (the correct relation is h = 2r).
  • Forgetting that the frustum slant height uses the difference of radii, not the sum, when computing curved surface area.

Composite Solids with Non‑Standard Cross‑Sections

When a solid is built by joining or removing pieces whose cross‑sections are not simple circles or rectangles, the usual formula sheet is insufficient. The key is to decompose the solid into a sum or difference of known volumes and surface areas, then adjust for any interior faces that become exposed or hidden. For example, a solid formed by drilling a square‑section hole through the centre of a cylinder requires the cylinder volume minus the volume of a square prism of side a and height equal to the cylinder height. The curved surface area of the cylinder remains, but the inner lateral area of the square hole (4 × a × h) must be added, while the two square ends of the hole are not part of the external surface. A systematic approach: (1) list every primitive solid, (2) write its volume and total surface area, (3) identify shared faces, (4) subtract the area of each shared face twice (once from each solid) because it is interior, (5) add any newly exposed interior faces. This method works for any combination — hemispherical caps on a rectangular box, a conical cavity inside a sphere, or a torus approximated by a cylinder bent into a circle. In competitive papers the composite solid is often described verbally; drawing a quick net or cross‑section diagram prevents missing a hidden face.

A frequent twist is the “thin‑walled” composite where the thickness t is small compared with the overall dimensions. Using the binomial approximation (R + t)³ − R³ ≈ 3R²t converts a volume‑of‑material calculation into a surface‑area‑times‑thickness estimate. For a hollow sphere of outer radius R and thickness t, material volume ≈ 4πR²t. For a hollow cylinder of mean radius r, height h and thickness t, material volume ≈ 2πrh t. These approximations are accepted when answer options are spaced by more than 2 % and the problem statement says “approximately”.

Mensuration in Coordinate Geometry: Distance and Locus Methods

Many advanced questions embed a 3‑D figure in a coordinate system and ask for a volume or surface area expressed in terms of coordinates. The distance formula in three dimensions, √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²], gives edge lengths of a tetrahedron whose vertices are known points. Once the six edge lengths are known, the volume can be obtained from the Cayley‑Menger determinant: V² = (1/288) | 0 1 1 1 1; 1 0 d₁₂² d₁₃² d₁₄²; 1 d₂₁² 0 d₂₃² d₂₄²; 1 d₃₁² d₃₂² 0 d₃₄²; 1 d₄₁² d₄₂² d₄₃² 0 |. Although the determinant looks heavy, in exam questions the coordinates are chosen so that many distances are equal or zero, reducing the determinant to a simple product. A more common pattern: a right circular cone has its vertex at the origin and its base lying in the plane z = h. The base circle is the intersection of the plane with the sphere x² + y² + z² = R². Solving gives the base radius r = √(R² − h²). The volume then follows the standard (1/3)πr²h. Recognising that the base is a circle obtained by slicing a sphere saves the step of computing the circle’s centre and radius separately.

Locus problems appear when a point moves such that its distance from a fixed line (axis) and a fixed plane (base) maintain a constant ratio. The resulting surface is a cone or a paraboloid. For instance, the set of points P(x,y,z) satisfying distance(P, z‑axis) = k × distance(P, plane z = 0) describes a right circular cone with semi‑vertical angle tan⁻¹(k). The volume of the portion cut off by the plane z = h is (1/3)π(kh)²h. In a timed test, converting the locus description into the standard cone parameters (height, base radius) is faster than integrating.

Worked Example: Volume of a Tetrahedron from Coordinates
Find the volume of the tetrahedron with vertices A(0,0,0), B(4,0,0), C(0,3,0), D(0,0,5).
The edges from A are along the coordinate axes, so AB = 4, AC = 3, AD = 5. The three edges meeting at A are mutually perpendicular, therefore the tetrahedron is a right‑angled corner of a rectangular box. Its volume is (1/6) × AB × AC × AD = (1/6) × 4 × 3 × 5 = 10 cubic units. (The general formula (1/3) × base area × height gives the same result: base ABC lies in the xy‑plane with area (1/2) × 4 × 3 = 6; height from D to that plane is 5; volume = (1/3) × 6 × 5 = 10.)
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a coordinate geometry question asks for a volume, first check whether the given points form a right‑angled corner (two edges along axes, dot products zero). If yes, use the (1/6) × product‑of‑three‑edges shortcut. Only resort to the Cayley‑Menger determinant when no orthogonal edges are evident.

Error Propagation and Approximation Strategies

Quantitative aptitude papers often test the effect of measurement errors on computed volumes or surface areas. If a linear dimension is measured with a relative error of ±δ (expressed as a decimal), the relative error in a quantity that depends on that dimension to the power n is approximately n δ. For a cylinder, V = πr²h, so ΔV/V ≈ 2(Δr/r) + (Δh/h). For a sphere, V = (4/3)πr³, giving ΔV/V ≈ 3(Δr/r). Surface area of a sphere, A = 4πr², yields ΔA/A ≈ 2(Δr/r). These linearised formulas are derived from the total differential and are accurate when δ < 0.05. In multiple‑choice questions the options are usually spaced widely enough that the linear approximation picks the correct choice.

A related technique is the “percentage‑change‑in‑product” shortcut. If radius increases by p % and height decreases by q %, the volume factor is (1 + p/100)² × (1 − q/100). Expanding and ignoring the product of the two small percentages gives an approximate percentage change of 2p − q. For example, a 10 % increase in radius and a 5 % decrease in height gives ≈ 20 % − 5 % = 15 % increase; the exact factor is 1.1² × 0.95 = 1.1495, i.e. 14.95 % — well within rounding tolerance. When the problem asks for “maximum possible error” given tolerances on each measurement, add the absolute contributions: max ΔV/V = 2|Δr/r| + |Δh/h|. This worst‑case bound is often the answer when the wording says “maximum percentage error”.

Engineering Applications: Pipes, Tanks, and Material Costing

Real‑world mensuration problems appear as caselets describing water‑supply pipes, fuel tanks, or metal‑sheet fabrication. A typical pipe problem gives inner radius r, outer radius R, length L, and asks for the weight of the pipe given the material density ρ. The material volume is π(R² − r²)L; weight = volume × ρ. If the pipe is to be painted on the outer curved surface only, the area is 2πRL; cost = area × rate per unit area. When the pipe is bent into a circular ring (torus), the length L becomes the mean circumference 2πRₘ where Rₘ is the distance from the centre of the torus to the centre of the pipe cross‑section. The volume of the torus is (cross‑sectional area) × (mean circumference) = π(R² − r²) × 2πRₘ = 2π²Rₘ(R² − r²). This Pappus‑centroid theorem (volume = area × distance travelled by centroid) is a powerful shortcut for any solid of revolution generated by rotating a plane area about an external axis.

Tank problems often involve a cylindrical tank with hemispherical ends (a capsule). Total volume = πr²h + (4/3)πr³, where h is the cylindrical portion length. Total inner surface area (for coating) = 2πrh + 4πr². If the tank is partially filled to a depth d measured from the bottom of the lower hemisphere, the filled volume splits into a spherical cap (if d ≤ r) plus a cylindrical segment (if d > r). The spherical cap volume is (πd²/3)(3r − d). Recognising the cap formula avoids integration. Costing questions may ask for the cost of the metal sheet used to fabricate the tank, given sheet price per square metre and a waste factor (e.g., 5 % extra). Multiply the total surface area by 1.05 before applying the rate.

Advanced Frustum and Truncated Pyramid Relationships

A frustum of a right circular cone (or pyramid) appears when the top is sliced off by a plane parallel to the base. Let the original cone have height H and base radius R; the removed top cone has height h and base radius r. Similarity gives r/R = h/H = k. The frustum height is H − h = H(1 − k). Volume of frustum = (1/3)πH(R² + Rr + r²) = (1/3)πH R²(1 + k + k²). Curved surface area = π(R + r) l, where l = √[(R − r)² + (H − h)²] = H√[(1 − k)² + (1 − k)²]? Wait, correct slant height: l = √[(R − r)² + (H − h)²] = H√[(1 − k)² + (1 − k)²]? Actually (R − r) = R(1 − k), (H − h) = H(1 − k), so l = (1 − k)√(R² + H²) = (1 − k)L where L is the slant height of the original cone. Hence CSA = πR(1 + k) × (1 − k)L = πRL(1 − k²). This shows CSA of frustum = CSA of original cone × (1 − k²). For a truncated pyramid with square bases of side a (bottom) and b (top) and height h, the volume is (h/3)(a² + ab + b²). The lateral area consists of four trapezoids; each trapezoid area = (a + b)/2 × slant height. The slant height is √[h² + ((a − b)/2)²]. These formulas are standard, but the exam twist is to give the volume of the frustum and ask for the height of the original cone, or to give the ratio of volumes of two similar frustums and ask for the ratio of their heights. Using the similarity ratio k, volume ratio = k³, so height ratio = k. If the problem states “the volume of the frustum is 7/8 of the original cone”, then 1 − k³ = 7/8 ⇒ k³ = 1/8 ⇒ k = 1/2, so the cutting plane is at half the original height.

Another advanced concept: a frustum can be viewed as a prismatoid, and its volume can also be computed by the prismoidal formula V = (h/6)(A₁ + 4Aₘ + A₂), where A₁ and A₂ are the areas of the two parallel faces and Aₘ is the area of the mid‑section (section halfway between them). For a conical frustum, A₁ = πR², A₂ = πr², and the mid‑section radius is (R + r)/2, giving Aₘ = π((R + r)/2)². Substituting yields the same (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²). Knowing the prismoidal formula provides a cross‑check and works for any solid whose cross‑sectional area varies quadratically with height.

  • Using the full cylinder volume formula for a conical frustum (forgetting the 1/3 factor).
  • Applying the similarity area ratio k² when the problem supplies a volume ratio k³.
  • Neglecting the hidden base area when two solids share a face, leading to an extra πr² term.
  • Mixing π = 22/7 and π = 3.14 in the same calculation, producing a non‑matching answer choice.

Pappus Centroid Theorem and Solids of Revolution

The Pappus‑Guldin theorems give a direct link between a plane figure’s area, the path of its centroid, and the volume or surface area generated when the figure revolves about an external axis. The first theorem states that the volume of a solid of revolution equals the product of the generating area and the distance travelled by its centroid. The second theorem states that the lateral surface area equals the product of the generating curve’s length and the distance travelled by its centroid. Both theorems hold provided the axis of rotation does not intersect the generating figure.

For a plane region of area A whose centroid lies at a perpendicular distance d from the axis, the volume V = A × (2πd). If the generating curve has length L and its centroid is at distance d from the axis, the curved surface area S = L × (2πd). These formulas bypass integration and are especially powerful when the centroid of the generating shape is known from standard tables. For example, a semicircle of radius r has area (πr²)/2 and its centroid is at a distance 4r/(3π) from the diameter. Rotating the semicircle about its diameter produces a sphere; applying the first theorem gives V = (πr²/2) × (2π × 4r/(3π)) = (4/3)πr³, the familiar sphere volume.

In competitive problems the theorem is often hidden inside a description of a “bent pipe” or a “ring‑shaped tank”. Recognising that the pipe’s centreline is the path of the centroid of the cross‑section allows the volume to be written as cross‑sectional area × mean circumference. The same reasoning applies to a torus generated by revolving a circle of radius a whose centre is at distance R from the axis: V = (πa²) × (2πR) = 2π²Ra² and curved surface area = (2πa) × (2πR) = 4π²Ra. When the generating shape is a rectangle of width w and height h whose centroid is at distance d from the axis, the solid is a cylindrical shell; the theorem reproduces the standard formulas V = 2πdwh and S = 2πd × (2w+2h) for the lateral area.

A common examiner twist is to give the volume of a solid of revolution and ask for the distance of the centroid from the axis, or to give the centroid distance and ask for the generating area. Because the relationship is linear, a simple division yields the missing quantity. Another twist combines two different generating figures on the same axis; the total volume is the sum of the individual A·2πd terms, which is often simpler than setting up a composite integral.

Spherical Caps, Segments, and Zones: Volume and Surface Area

A spherical cap is the portion of a sphere cut off by a plane. If the sphere has radius R and the cap height (measured from the base plane to the top of the cap) is h, the cap volume is V = (πh²/3)(3R − h). The curved surface area of the cap (excluding the base) is A = 2πRh. A spherical segment is the region between two parallel planes; its height is the distance between the planes. When the two planes are at distances a and b from the sphere centre (with a < b), the segment height is h = b − a. The segment volume can be obtained by subtracting two cap volumes: V = (πh/6)(3a² + 3b² + h²). The curved surface area of a segment (a zone) is simply A = 2πRh, independent of the positions of the cutting planes; only the segment height matters.

These formulas are derived by integrating thin disks or by applying the Pappus theorem to a circular arc. The zone area result follows because the generating arc length is Rθ and its centroid travels a circle of radius R sin(θ/2); the product simplifies to 2πRh. In examinations, a problem may describe a “spherical tank partially filled to depth d”. If d ≤ R the filled part is a cap; if d > R it is a hemisphere plus a cap of height d − R. Recognising the cap formula avoids the need for integration and reduces the chance of algebraic error.

A spherical sector is the solid bounded by a conical surface with vertex at the sphere centre and the spherical cap at its base. Its volume is V = (2/3)πR²h, where h is the cap height. The total surface area (curved cap plus conical surface) is A = 2πRh + πRl, where l = √(R² + (R−h)²) is the slant height of the cone. Problems sometimes ask for the volume of a “spherical wedge” obtained by cutting a sector with two half‑planes through the axis; the wedge volume is a fraction θ/(2π) of the sector volume.

When a sphere is intersected by a cylinder whose axis passes through the sphere centre, the remaining solid (a “spherical ring” or “napkin ring”) has a volume that depends only on the height of the cylindrical hole, not on the sphere radius. This surprising result follows from the zone area formula and is a favourite conceptual question.

Worked Example: Partially Filled Spherical Tank
A spherical tank of radius 6 m is filled with water to a depth of 4 m measured from the bottom of the sphere. Find the volume of water in the tank.
The water forms a spherical cap of height h = 4 m on a sphere of radius R = 6 m. Using the cap volume formula V = (πh²/3)(3R − h): V = (π × 4² / 3) × (3×6 − 4) = (16π/3) × (18 − 4) = (16π/3) × 14 = (224π/3) m³ ≈ 234.6 m³. If the depth had been 10 m (greater than R), the volume would be the hemisphere volume (2/3)πR³ plus a cap of height 4 m on the upper hemisphere.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the cap volume (πh²/3)(3R−h) and zone area 2πRh as a pair; they appear together in almost every spherical‑tank caselet. When a problem gives a filling depth d, first compare d with R. If d ≤ R use the cap formula directly. If d > R compute the hemisphere volume (2/3)πR³ and add a cap of height d−R. This two‑step mental split saves time and prevents sign errors.

Toroidal Solids: Volume, Surface Area, and Applications

A torus is generated by revolving a circle of radius r (the tube radius) about an axis in the same plane at a distance R (the major radius) from the circle centre, with R > r. The volume is V = (πr²) × (2πR) = 2π²Rr², a direct application of the first Pappus theorem. The total surface area (including the inner and outer curved surfaces) is A = (2πr) × (2πR) = 4π²Rr, from the second theorem. If the torus is “solid” (a doughnut), these are the complete measures. If the torus is a thin‑walled tube, the same formulas give the material volume and the area available for coating or insulation.

Problems often present a toroidal pipe bent from a straight cylindrical pipe of length L, inner radius rᵢ, outer radius rₒ. The mean radius of the torus is Rₘ = L/(2π). The cross‑sectional area of the pipe wall is π(rₒ² − rᵢ²). The material volume is therefore π(rₒ² − rᵢ²) × L, which matches the straight‑pipe volume, confirming that bending does not change volume. The outer curved surface area for painting becomes 2πrₒ × L, while the inner surface area (if the pipe is open) is 2πrᵢ × L. When the pipe is closed to form a torus, the inner surface becomes a toroidal surface of area 4π²Rₘrᵢ and the outer surface area is 4π²Rₘrₒ.

A more advanced twist asks for the volume of a “toroidal sector” cut by two half‑planes through the axis, subtending an angle θ at the centre. The sector volume is (θ/2π) × 2π²Rr² = θπRr². The corresponding curved surface area is (θ/2π) × 4π²Rr = 2θπRr. These proportional results follow because the generating circle is simply rotated through a fraction of the full revolution.

In engineering contexts, the torus appears as a “ring main” in water distribution or as a “magnetic confinement chamber” in fusion devices. The exam may give the mass of a toroidal casting and the material density, asking for the tube radius r when the major radius R is known. Rearranging V = mass/density = 2π²Rr² yields r = √(mass/(2π²R·density)). Such inverse problems test algebraic manipulation as much as formula recall.

Elliptical Cylinders and Cones: Cross‑Sectional Geometry

When the base of a right cylinder or cone is an ellipse rather than a circle, the standard formulas adapt by replacing the circular base area πr² with the elliptical area πab, where a and b are the semi‑major and semi‑minor axes. The volume of a right elliptical cylinder of height h is V = πabh. The lateral surface area of an elliptical cylinder has no elementary closed form; it is expressed using the complete elliptic integral of the second kind E(e), where e = √(1 − b²/a²) is the eccentricity. The exact lateral area is A = 4a h E(e). In competitive examinations the lateral area is rarely asked; instead, the problem may give the perimeter of the ellipse approximated by Ramanujan’s formula P ≈ π[3(a+b) − √((3a+b)(a+3b))] and ask for an approximate lateral area A ≈ P·h.

For a right elliptical cone with base semi‑axes a, b and height h, the volume is V = (1/3)πabh, identical in form to the circular cone. The slant height is not uniform; each generator has a different length. The lateral surface area again involves elliptic integrals. However, the area of an elliptical cross‑section at a distance x from the vertex (measured along the axis) scales with the square of the linear factor (x/h). Thus the cross‑sectional semi‑axes are a·(x/h) and b·(x/h), and the area is πab(x/h)². This quadratic variation allows the use of the prismoidal formula for volume verification.

An examiner may combine an elliptical cylinder with a circular cylinder sharing a common axis, asking for the volume of the intersection. Because the intersection cross‑section is an ellipse truncated by a circle, the problem reduces to integrating the area of the overlapping region along the axis. In practice, such questions are simplified by giving the dimensions such that the intersection is a known shape (e.g., the circular cylinder fully contains the elliptical one). Recognising the scaling of elliptical areas with the square of the linear dimension is the key shortcut.

Another common variant replaces the ellipse with a “stadium” shape (a rectangle capped by semicircles). The area is then rectangle area + circle area, and the perimeter is rectangle perimeter + circle circumference. The volume of a prism with a stadium base is simply base area × height. These composite cross‑sections appear in “pipe‑in‑pipe” insulation problems where the outer jacket is a stadium shape.

Mensuration of Regular and Semi‑Regular Polyhedra

The five Platonic solids — tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron — have congruent regular polygonal faces and identical vertices. Their volumes and surface areas can be expressed in terms of the edge length a. For the tetrahedron: V = a³/(6√2), A = √3 a². Cube: V = a³, A = 6a². Octahedron: V = √2 a³/3, A = 2√3 a². Dodecahedron: V = (15+7√5)a³/4, A = 3√(25+10√5) a². Icosahedron: V = 5(3+√5)a³/12, A = 5√3 a². These formulas are derived by decomposing each solid into pyramids with the centre as apex; the pyramid height is the inradius, and the base area is the face area.

The thirteen Archimedean (semi‑regular) solids have regular faces of two or more types but identical vertices. Their volumes and surface areas are also expressible in closed form using the edge length. For example, the truncated icosahedron (soccer‑ball shape) with edge a has V = (125+43√5)a³/4 and A = (3√(25+10√5)+5√3)a². In examinations, the exact constants are rarely required; instead, a problem may give the volume of a regular tetrahedron and ask for the edge length, testing the ability to invert V = a³/(6√2) → a = (6√2 V)1/3.

A useful relationship connects the circumradius R, inradius r, and midradius ρ (radius of the sphere tangent to edges) for each Platonic solid. For the tetrahedron: R = a√6/4, r = a√6/12, ρ = a√2/4. For the cube: R = a√3/2, r = a/2, ρ = a/√2. For the octahedron: R = a√2/2, r = a√6/6, ρ = a/2. These radii appear in problems where a sphere is inscribed in or circumscribed about a polyhedron, or where a polyhedron is inscribed in a sphere of given radius. The volume of the polyhedron can then be found by first determining the edge length from the given radius.

When a regular polyhedron is truncated at its vertices, the resulting Archimedean solid’s volume can be obtained by subtracting the volumes of the removed pyramids from the original Platonic solid. Each removed pyramid has a regular polygonal base (the new face) and height equal to the original inradius minus the new inradius. This subtraction method is often faster than memorising the Archimedean formulas.

Common Traps

  • Applying the circular cylinder lateral area formula 2πrh to an elliptical cylinder without using the elliptic integral or an approved approximation.
  • Confusing the spherical cap height h with the distance from the sphere centre to the cutting plane; the cap height is measured from the base plane to the pole.
  • Using the torus volume formula 2π²Rr² when the generating circle intersects the axis (R ≤ r), which violates the Pappus theorem conditions.
  • Assuming the lateral surface area of a right elliptical cone equals π(a+b)l where l is an average slant height; the true area requires elliptic integrals.
  • Forgetting that the volume of a regular tetrahedron scales with a³/(6√2) and mistakenly using the cube formula a³.
  • Treating the inradius of a Platonic solid as the distance from centre to face centre for all solids; the inradius differs for each solid and must be looked up or derived.
  • Neglecting the waste factor in material‑costing problems involving toroidal or elliptical cross‑sections, leading to an under‑estimate of required sheet area.

Oblique Prisms and Cylinders: Volume, Lateral Area, and Centroid

An oblique prism is defined by two parallel congruent polygonal bases joined by lateral faces that are parallelograms rather than rectangles. The volume of any prism, right or oblique, depends only on the area of the base B and the perpendicular distance h between the two base planes: V = B × h. This follows directly from Cavalieri’s principle — slicing the solid with planes parallel to the bases yields equal cross‑sectional areas at equal heights. The lateral surface area, however, differs from the right‑prism case. For an oblique prism with base perimeter P and slant height l (the length of a lateral edge), the lateral area is Aₗ = P × l only when all lateral edges are equal and the lateral faces are congruent parallelograms. In the general case each lateral face must be treated individually: Aₗ = Σ (base edge length × corresponding slant height). The centroid of an oblique prism lies on the line joining the centroids of the two bases, at a distance h/2 from each base plane, exactly as for a right prism, because the mass distribution is symmetric with respect to the mid‑plane.

An oblique cylinder is the limiting case of an oblique prism with a circular base. Its volume remains V = π r² h where h is the perpendicular distance between the circular bases. The lateral surface, however, is not a rectangle when developed; it is a parallelogram of width equal to the circumference 2πr and height equal to the slant length l of a generating line. Hence the lateral area is Aₗ = 2πr l. The total surface area adds the two base areas: A = 2πr² + 2πr l. A frequent examiner twist is to give the slant length l and the angle θ between the axis and the base normal, then ask for the volume. The perpendicular height is h = l cos θ, so V = π r² l cos θ. Recognising that the volume formula never uses the slant length directly is the key shortcut.

When the bases are not congruent (a truncated oblique prism), the solid becomes a prismoid. The prismoidal formula V = (h/6)(A₁ + 4Aₘ + A₂) applies provided the cross‑sectional area varies quadratically with height, which holds for any solid whose lateral edges are straight lines. This includes oblique frustums of pyramids and cones. The centroid of such a prismoid lies on the line joining the centroids of the end faces, at a distance from the larger base given by h (A₁ + 2Aₘ) / (A₁ + 4Aₘ + A₂). These relations are often tested in combination with coordinate geometry, where the vertices of the end faces are given and the student must compute areas via the shoelace formula before applying the prismoidal rule.

Variable Cross‑Section Solids: Prismoidal Formula and Numerical Integration

Many engineering solids — dam sections, ship hulls, railway cuttings — have cross‑sectional areas that change smoothly but not linearly along their length. When the area function A(x) is known at equally spaced stations x₀, x₁, …, xₙ (with spacing Δx), the volume can be approximated by Simpson’s 1/3 rule: V ≈ (Δx/3)[A₀ + 4A₁ + 2A₂ + 4A₃ + … + 2Aₙ₋₂ + 4Aₙ₋₁ + Aₙ]. This is exactly the prismoidal formula applied repeatedly over each pair of sub‑intervals. The rule is exact for any solid whose cross‑sectional area is a cubic polynomial in x, which covers all prismoids and most practical shapes. If the number of stations is even, Simpson’s 3/8 rule can be used for the last three intervals: V₃₈ = (3Δx/8)(Aₙ₋₃ + 3Aₙ₋₂ + 3Aₙ₋₁ + Aₙ).

In examinations, the area values are often given in a table (a “dip table” for a horizontal tank). The candidate must decide whether the spacing is uniform; if not, the trapezoidal rule V ≈ Σ (Δxᵢ/2)(Aᵢ + Aᵢ₊₁) is the safe fallback, though it under‑estimates volume for convex area curves. A common variant asks for the volume of a solid generated by rotating a curve y = f(x) about the x‑axis between x = a and x = b. The cross‑sectional area is A(x) = π f(x)². Substituting into Simpson’s rule yields a numerical estimate of the volume of revolution without performing the integral analytically. This technique is valuable when f(x) is given only as discrete data points (e.g., from a CAD model). The error of Simpson’s rule is proportional to the fourth derivative of A(x); for smooth engineering profiles the error is typically below 0.1 % with 10–12 stations.

A related concept is the “mean area” method used in earthwork calculations: V = L × Ā, where Ā is the average of the end areas only. This is equivalent to the trapezoidal rule with a single interval and is acceptable only when the area varies nearly linearly. Examiners often present a scenario where the end‑area method gives a volume 5 % different from the prismoidal result and ask which is more accurate. The correct answer is the prismoidal (Simpson) result, because the area of a typical cutting or embankment varies quadratically due to side‑slope geometry. Understanding the order of accuracy — trapezoidal O(Δx²), Simpson O(Δx⁴) — allows a quick justification without lengthy computation.

Worked Example: Prismoidal Volume from Dip Table
A horizontal cylindrical tank of radius 2 m and length 10 m has liquid depths measured at five equally spaced stations along its axis (including the ends). The cross‑sectional areas of the liquid segment at these stations are: 0 m², 6.28 m², 12.57 m², 6.28 m², 0 m². Estimate the liquid volume using Simpson’s 1/3 rule.
The station spacing Δx = 10 m / 4 = 2.5 m. Apply Simpson’s rule with n = 4 (even number of intervals): V ≈ (Δx/3)[A₀ + 4A₁ + 2A₂ + 4A₃ + A₄] = (2.5/3)[0 + 4×6.28 + 2×12.57 + 4×6.28 + 0] = (0.8333)[25.12 + 25.14 + 25.12] = 0.8333 × 75.38 ≈ 62.82 m³. The exact volume of a half‑filled horizontal cylinder is (πr²/2)×L = (π×4/2)×10 = 62.83 m³, confirming the accuracy of Simpson’s rule for this symmetric profile.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a table of cross‑sectional areas is given, first check whether the spacing is uniform. If yes, default to Simpson’s 1/3 rule — it is both faster and more accurate than the trapezoidal rule. Write the formula symbolically before substituting numbers; this earns method marks even if arithmetic slips. If the number of intervals is odd, apply Simpson’s 1/3 to the first even block and Simpson’s 3/8 to the last three intervals. Always state the assumption that the area varies as a cubic polynomial, which is standard for prismoidal solids.

Paraboloids and Hyperboloids of Revolution: Surface Area and Volume

A paraboloid of revolution is generated by rotating the parabola y = k x² (or x = k y²) about its axis. For the solid bounded by the paraboloid and a plane perpendicular to the axis at x = h, the volume is V = (π/2) k² h⁵ when the generating curve is y = k x² and the axis is the x‑axis. A more common parametrisation uses the focal length f: the parabola y² = 4 f x rotated about the x‑axis gives a paraboloid with volume V = (π/2) f h² where h is the axial depth. The lateral surface area (excluding the base) is A = (π/6 f²)[(4 f h + 1)³ᐟ² − 1]. When h ≫ f, the surface area approximates A ≈ π h √(h² + 4 f h). These formulas appear in problems on satellite dishes, headlight reflectors, and liquid‑mirror telescopes.

A hyperboloid of one sheet is generated by rotating the hyperbola x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 about the x‑axis. The volume between x = x₁ and x = x₂ is V = π b² [x₂ √(x₂²/a² − 1) − x₁ √(x₁²/a² − 1) − a ln((x₂ + √(x₂² − a²))/(x₁ + √(x₁² − a²)))]. The surface area integral does not have an elementary closed form; it is expressed in terms of elliptic integrals. In competitive examinations, the hyperboloid is usually replaced by a “hyperbolic cooling tower” approximation where the meridian curve is a catenary y = c cosh(x/c). The volume of a catenoid segment can be found by Pappus’ centroid theorem: the generating curve’s centroid travels a circular path. For a catenary arc from x = −L to x = L, the centroid lies on the axis of symmetry, so the volume is simply the area under the curve times 2π times the centroid’s distance from the axis (which is zero), giving zero — a reminder that Pappus applies to the area swept by a plane figure, not to a curve alone. The correct approach is to treat the solid as a surface of revolution and integrate 2π y ds for area, π y² dx for volume.

Examiners often combine a paraboloid and a cone sharing a vertex, asking for the volume of the region common to both. Because both solids have quadratic cross‑sectional area variation, the intersection volume can be obtained by integrating the minimum of the two area functions. If the cone has semi‑vertical angle α and the paraboloid has focal length f, the cross‑sectional radii are r_c = x tan α and r_p = √(4 f x). Equating gives the transition point x₀ = 4 f cot² α. The volume is then V = ∫₀x₀ π r_c² dx + ∫x₀h π r_p² dx. This piecewise integration is a standard “split‑integral” technique that avoids memorising a combined formula.

Tetrahedron Volume from Vertex Coordinates in 3D Space

Given four non‑coplanar points A(x₁,y₁,z₁), B(x₂,y₂,z₂), C(x₃,y₃,z₃), D(x₄,y₄,z₄), the volume of the tetrahedron ABCD is V = |det(M)| / 6, where M is the 3×3 matrix formed by the vectors AB, AC, AD as rows (or columns). Explicitly, V = |(x₂−x₁)[(y₃−y₁)(z₄−z₁) − (z₃−z₁)(y₄−y₁)] − (y₂−y₁)[(x₃−x₁)(z₄−z₁) − (z₃−z₁)(x₄−x₁)] + (z₂−z₁)[(x₃−x₁)(y₄−y₁) − (y₃−y₁)(x₄−x₁)]| / 6. This determinant formula is a direct consequence of the scalar triple product |AB · (AC × AD)| / 6. It is the most robust method when coordinates are given, bypassing the need to compute edge lengths and face areas.

A frequent variant provides the coordinates of the vertices of a regular tetrahedron inscribed in a sphere of radius R. The edge length is a = 2√(2/3) R, and the volume follows from V = a³/(6√2). However, examiners may give an irregular tetrahedron where three edges meeting at a vertex are mutually perpendicular (a “right‑angled tetrahedron”). If the perpendicular edges have lengths p, q, r, the volume simplifies to V = p q r / 6. This is a special case of the determinant formula where the matrix becomes diagonal. Recognising this pattern saves time: the volume of a tetrahedron with a tri‑rectangular vertex is simply one‑sixth the product of the three orthogonal edges.

Another twist involves the distance from a point to a plane. The volume of a tetrahedron can also be expressed as V = (1/3) × (area of base) × (perpendicular height). If the base triangle is defined by points B, C, D, its area is ½ |BC × BD|. The height from A to the plane BCD is the absolute value of the scalar projection of AB onto the unit normal of the plane. Combining these gives the same determinant result. In coordinate‑geometry heavy papers, candidates are expected to switch between vector and determinant forms fluidly. A useful shortcut: if the coordinates are integers, compute the determinant using integer arithmetic (e.g., by row reduction) and divide by 6 at the end to avoid fractions.

Horizontal Cylindrical Tank Calibration: Segment Area and Dip Tables

A horizontal cylindrical tank of radius R and length L is a standard storage vessel. The liquid volume for a given depth h (measured from the bottom) is V = L × A_seg, where A_seg is the area of the circular segment cut off by a chord at distance R − h from the centre. The segment area is A_seg = R² arccos((R−h)/R) − (R−h) √(2Rh − h²). This formula is derived from the sector area minus the triangle area. In examinations, the depth h is often given as a fraction of the diameter, e.g., “the tank is 30 % full”. The candidate must convert percentage to depth: h = 0.3 × 2R only if the tank is vertical; for a horizontal tank the relationship is non‑linear and must be solved using the segment area formula or a dip table.

Dip tables list the volume (or percentage capacity) for a set of standard depths, usually at 1 % or 5 % intervals. To construct a dip table, one computes A_seg for each depth and multiplies by L. Modern exams may provide a partial dip table and ask for interpolation. Linear interpolation between tabulated depths is acceptable only when the depth interval is small (≤ 2 % of diameter) because the volume‑depth curve is convex near the bottom and top and concave near the mid‑height. A better approximation uses the derivative dV/dh = L × 2√(2Rh − h²) (the chord length times length) to perform a first‑order Taylor correction: V(h+Δh) ≈ V(h) + L × 2√(2Rh − h²) Δh. This is essentially the trapezoidal rule on the volume‑depth curve and yields errors < 0.2 % for Δh up to 5 % of diameter.

A practical complication is the presence of hemispherical or ellipsoidal end caps. The total volume is then V_total = V_cylinder + 2 V_cap. For a hemispherical cap of radius R, the volume of liquid in the cap at depth h (measured from the cap’s pole) is V_cap = π h² (R − h/3). When the tank is partially filled such that the liquid occupies part of the cylindrical section and part of one cap, the depth in the cap is h_cap = h − L (if the cylinder length is L and the coordinate origin is at the cylinder‑cap junction). The combined volume is piecewise: for h ≤ L use the cylindrical segment formula; for h > L add the full cylinder volume plus the cap volume at depth h − L. Examiners often test the ability to set up the correct piecewise expression and evaluate it for a given fill percentage.

  • Applying the right‑prism lateral area formula P × h to an oblique prism instead of summing edge‑wise slant heights.
  • Using the trapezoidal rule for volume estimation when the cross‑sectional area varies quadratically, thereby under‑estimating the true volume.
  • Confusing the focal length f of a paraboloid with the parameter k in y = k x²; the volume formula changes by a factor of 2.
  • Assuming the surface area of a hyperboloid of revolution has an elementary closed form and attempting to integrate 2π y ds without elliptic integrals.
  • Forgetting to take the absolute value of the scalar triple product when computing tetrahedron volume from coordinates, leading to a negative volume.
  • Treating the depth‑volume relationship in a horizontal cylinder as linear and using simple proportion instead of the segment area formula.
  • Neglecting the piecewise nature of volume in a horizontal tank with end caps, resulting in an incorrect total for fill levels that extend into the caps.

Mensuration of Solids with Variable Wall Thickness and Hollow Sections

When a solid such as a pipe, a pressure vessel, or a hollow beam has a wall thickness that varies along its length, the standard formulas for volume and surface area of a uniform hollow cylinder no longer apply. The volume of material is obtained by integrating the cross‑sectional area of the wall along the axis. For a hollow cylinder whose inner radius r(x) and outer radius R(x) are functions of the axial coordinate x (0 ≤ x ≤ L), the material volume is

V = π ∫₀ᴸ [R(x)² − r(x)²] dx.

If the thickness t(x) = R(x) − r(x) is small compared with the mean radius, the integrand can be approximated by 2π Rₘ(x) t(x), where Rₘ(x) = (R(x)+r(x))/2. This linearised form is often used in engineering cost estimates because it avoids squaring large radii. The lateral surface area (both inner and outer) is

A = 2π ∫₀ᴸ [R(x) + r(x)] √(1 + (dR/dx)²) dx (outer) + 2π ∫₀ᴸ [R(x) + r(x)] √(1 + (dr/dx)²) dx (inner).

When the wall profile is piecewise linear (common in tapered pipes), the integrals reduce to sums of frustum formulas. For a conical frustum with end radii R₁, R₂ and length L, the volume of material with constant thickness t is V = π t (R₁ + R₂) L + (π t²/3) L (1/R₁ + 1/R₂)⁻¹? Actually the exact formula for a hollow conical frustum of uniform thickness is derived by subtracting the inner frustum volume from the outer frustum volume. If the outer frustum has radii R₁, R₂ and the inner frustum has radii r₁ = R₁ − t, r₂ = R₂ − t, then

V = (π L/3) [(R₁² + R₁R₂ + R₂²) − (r₁² + r₁r₂ + r₂²)].

Expanding gives a polynomial in t that can be evaluated quickly. In examinations, a typical twist is to give the outer dimensions and the mass of the pipe, asking for the thickness assuming a known density. The candidate must set up the volume equation, substitute mass = density × volume, and solve the resulting quadratic in t. Another common variant is a hollow sphere with radially varying thickness t(r) = k r; the material volume becomes ∫ 4π r² t(r) dr = 4π k ∫ r³ dr, which is straightforward.

Surface area calculations for variable thickness often require the slant height of each differential element. For a thin‑walled tapered tube, the outer surface area is approximately the lateral area of the outer frustum, Aₒ = π (R₁ + R₂) sₒ, where sₒ = √[(R₂−R₁)² + L²]. The inner surface area uses the inner radii. If the thickness is not constant, the mean radius at each end must be used. Candidates should remember that the total surface area exposed to fluid (inner) or atmosphere (outer) is the sum of both, unless the ends are closed by caps, in which case the annular end areas π(R²−r²) are added.

Mensuration of Intersecting Solids: Common Volume and Surface Area

Problems involving the intersection of two standard solids — such as a cylinder piercing a sphere, two cylinders crossing at right angles (Steinmetz solid), or a cone intersecting a cylinder — appear frequently in advanced quantitative sections. The common volume is the region that satisfies the inequalities defining both solids simultaneously. For a right circular cylinder of radius a whose axis coincides with the z-axis (x² + y² ≤ a²) and a sphere of radius R centred at the origin (x² + y² + z² ≤ R²), the intersection volume is symmetric about the xy-plane. Using cylindrical coordinates, the limits for z are ±√(R² − r²) while r runs from 0 to a. The volume integral is

V = ∫₀²π ∫₀ᵃ ∫₋√(R²−r²)⁺√(R²−r²) r dz dr dθ = 4π ∫₀ᵃ r √(R² − r²) dr.

Substituting u = R² − r² gives V = (4π/3) (R³ − (R² − a²)3/2). When a = R (cylinder radius equals sphere radius), the volume simplifies to (4π/3) R³ (1 − 0) = 4πR³/3, which is exactly the volume of the sphere — the cylinder completely contains the sphere’s equatorial cross‑section. For the classic Steinmetz solid formed by two perpendicular cylinders of equal radius a, the common volume is V = 16 a³/3. The derivation uses symmetry: one‑eighth of the solid lies in the first octant where x, y, z ≥ 0 and x² + y² ≤ a², x² + z² ≤ a². Integrating in the order dz dy dx yields the result.

Surface area of the intersection is more involved. For the cylinder‑sphere intersection, the curved surface consists of two parts: the portion of the cylinder inside the sphere and the portion of the sphere inside the cylinder. The cylinder part area is A_cyl = 2π a × 2√(R² − a²) = 4π a √(R² − a²). The spherical part is a zone of height h = R − √(R² − a²) on each side, so total spherical area = 4π R h = 4π R (R − √(R² − a²)). The total surface area of the intersecting solid is the sum of these two contributions. In exams, a typical question gives the radius of the cylinder and the sphere and asks for the volume of the material removed when a cylindrical hole is drilled through the centre of a sphere. The answer is the sphere volume minus the intersection volume, which simplifies to (4π/3)(R² − a²)3/2. Recognising this “napkin ring” result — that the remaining volume depends only on the height of the ring, not on the original sphere radius — is a major time‑saver.

Worked Example: Cylindrical Hole Through a Sphere
A sphere of radius 13 cm has a cylindrical hole of radius 5 cm drilled through its centre. Find the volume of the remaining solid.
The height of the remaining “napkin ring” is h = 2√(R² − a²) = 2√(13² − 5²) = 2√(169 − 25) = 2√144 = 24 cm. The volume of a napkin ring depends only on h: V = π h³ / 6 = π × 24³ / 6 = π × 13824 / 6 = 2304π cm³. Alternatively, compute sphere volume (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π×2197 = 8788π/3 ≈ 2929.33π, subtract intersection volume (4π/3)(R³ − (R²−a²)3/2) = (4π/3)(2197 − 1443/2) = (4π/3)(2197 − 1728) = (4π/3)×469 = 1876π/3 ≈ 625.33π. Difference = (8788π/3 − 1876π/3) = 6912π/3 = 2304π cm³. Both methods give 2304π cm³.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem describes a hole drilled through a sphere or a cylinder intersecting another solid, first identify whether the “napkin ring” height can be found directly. If the height h of the remaining ring is given or easily computed, use V = π h³ / 6 instantly. This bypasses integration and eliminates algebraic errors. For Steinmetz solids, memorise V = 16 a³/3 for two equal cylinders; for unequal radii a and b (a ≤ b), the volume is V = 8 a² √(b² − a²) + (16/3) a³? Actually the general formula is more complex; in exams they usually restrict to equal radii. Always sketch the cross‑section in the plane containing both axes to visualise limits.

Mensuration of Solids of Revolution: Washer and Cylindrical Shell Methods

While the Pappus centroid theorem gives a quick route for volumes of revolution when the centroid of the generating area is known, many exam problems present regions bounded by curves where the centroid is not obvious. The washer (disc) method and the cylindrical shell method are the two standard integration techniques. For a region bounded by y = f(x), the x‑axis, and vertical lines x = a, x = b, revolved about the x‑axis, the volume by washers is

V = π ∫ₐᵇ [f(x)]² dx.

If the same region is revolved about the y‑axis, the shell method is often simpler: V = 2π ∫ₐᵇ x f(x) dx. The choice of method depends on the axis of revolution and the ease of expressing the inverse function. For example, the region under y = √x from x = 0 to 4 revolved about the y‑axis: washers require x = y², giving V = π ∫₀² (4² − (y²)²) dy = π ∫₀² (16 − y⁴) dy = π [16y − y⁵/5]₀² = π (32 − 32/5) = 128π/5. Shells give V = 2π ∫₀⁴ x √x dx = 2π ∫₀⁴ x3/2 dx = 2π [2/5 x5/2]₀⁴ = 2π × (2/5) × 32 = 128π/5. Both match.

When the region is bounded by two curves y = f(x) (upper) and y = g(x) (lower), the washer volume about the x‑axis is V = π ∫ₐᵇ ([f(x)]² − [g(x)]²) dx. The shell volume about the y‑axis is V = 2π ∫ₐᵇ x (f(x) − g(x)) dx. A common trap is to use the washer formula about the y‑axis without inverting the functions, leading to incorrect limits. Candidates should practice switching between methods: if the integral with one method involves a difficult inverse, the other method may yield a polynomial or simple rational function.

Surface area of revolution also has two forms. For y = f(x) (f ≥ 0) revolved about the x‑axis, the area is A = 2π ∫ₐᵇ f(x) √(1 + [f'(x)]²) dx. About the y‑axis, A = 2π ∫ₐᵇ x √(1 + [f'(x)]²) dx. If the curve is given parametrically (x = x(t), y = y(t)), the formulas become A = 2π ∫ y(t) √(x'(t)² + y'(t)²) dt (x‑axis) and A = 2π ∫ x(t) √(x'(t)² + y'(t)²) dt (y‑axis). In competitive exams, the parametric form appears when the curve is a cycloid, astroid, or ellipse. For an ellipse x = a cos θ, y = b sin θ revolved about the x‑axis, the surface area integral leads to an elliptic integral; examiners usually ask for the volume (which is elementary: V = 4π a b²/3) and only test the surface area as a conceptual multiple‑choice question where the answer is “cannot be expressed in elementary functions”.

Mensuration of Spherical Triangles: Girard's Theorem and Applications

Spherical triangles are formed by three great‑circle arcs on a sphere of radius R. Unlike planar triangles, the sum of interior angles exceeds π. Girard’s theorem states that the area of a spherical triangle is A = R² (α + β + γ − π), where α, β, γ are the interior angles in radians. The quantity E = α + β + γ − π is called the spherical excess. For a unit sphere (R = 1), the area equals the excess directly. This theorem is the spherical analogue of the planar formula A = ½ ab sin C and is frequently used in navigation and astronomy problems that appear in quantitative aptitude under the guise of “geometry on a sphere”.

The sides of a spherical triangle are measured by the central angles they subtend, denoted a, b, c (in radians). The spherical law of cosines relates sides and angles: cos a = cos b cos c + sin b sin c cos α. The spherical law of sines is sin a / sin α = sin b / sin β = sin c / sin γ. These allow computation of unknown angles when three sides are given, or vice versa. In exam questions, a typical scenario: “On a sphere of radius 6370 km (Earth), a spherical triangle has vertices at latitudes/longitudes … Find its area.” The candidate converts coordinates to central angles using the haversine formula or spherical cosine law, computes the three interior angles via the spherical cosine law for angles (cos α = −cos β cos γ + sin β sin γ cos a), then applies Girard’s theorem.

Another application is the spherical Pythagorean theorem for right spherical triangles (γ = π/2): cos c = cos a cos b. When the sides are small compared with R, the spherical triangle approximates a planar triangle and the excess E ≈ (area of planar triangle)/R². This approximation is sometimes used to estimate the area of a large survey plot on Earth’s surface. Candidates should also know the formula for the area of a spherical polygon with n sides: A = R² (Σ interior angles − (n−2)π). This generalises Girard’s theorem and appears in problems about spherical quadrilaterals formed by lines of latitude and longitude.

Mensuration of Ellipsoids: Volume, Approximate Surface Area, and Practical Use

An ellipsoid with semi‑axes a, b, c (aligned with coordinate axes) has the equation x²/a² + y²/b² + z²/c² = 1. Its volume is a straightforward extension of the sphere: V = (4/3) π a b c. This formula is derived by the linear transformation (x, y, z) → (x/a, y/b, z/c) which maps the ellipsoid to a unit sphere; the Jacobian determinant is abc, giving the volume scaling factor. In competitive exams, the volume of an ellipsoid is often asked directly or as part of a composite solid (e.g., an ellipsoidal tank with hemispherical ends).

The surface area of a general ellipsoid has no elementary closed form; it is expressed in terms of elliptic integrals. However, several accurate approximations are standard. The most common is Knud Thomsen’s formula: A ≈ 4π [(a^p b^p + a^p c^p + b^p c^p)/3]1/p with p = 1.6075. For a spheroid (two equal axes), simpler exact formulas exist. For an oblate spheroid (a = b > c), the surface area is A = 2π a² + π (c²/e) ln((1+e)/(1−e)), where e = √(1 − c²/a²) is the eccentricity. For a prolate spheroid (a = b < c), A = 2π a² + 2π (a c / e) arcsin(e), with e = √(1 − a²/c²). These exact forms are occasionally tested when the solid is described as “an ellipsoid of revolution”.

In practical problems, an ellipsoidal tank may be partially filled. The volume of liquid up to a height h measured along the c‑axis requires integrating cross‑sectional ellipses. If the ellipsoid is oriented with its c‑axis vertical, the horizontal cross‑section at height z is an ellipse with semi‑axes a(z) = a √(1 − z²/c²) and b(z) = b √(1 − z²/c²). The area is π a b (1 − z²/c²). Integrating from z = −c to z = h − c gives V(h) = π a b [ (h) − (h³)/(3c²) + (2c)/3 ]? Actually the definite integral from bottom (−c) to height h (measured from bottom) yields V = π a b [ h + (h²)/(c) − (h³)/(3c²) ]? Let's derive: let z' = z + c, so z' runs 0 to h. Then z = z' − c. The cross‑section area = π a b (1 − (z'−c)²/c²) = π a b (1 − (z'² − 2c z' + c²)/c²) = π a b (2z'/c − z'²/c²). Integrate from 0 to h: V = π a b [ (z'²/c) − (z'³)/(3c²) ]₀ʰ = π a b (h²/c − h³/(3c²)). This is the volume of liquid in an ellipsoidal tank of vertical axis c, filled to depth h from the bottom. Examiners may ask for the depth corresponding to a given volume, requiring solution of a cubic equation; they often provide the cubic and ask for the approximate root using one iteration of Newton‑Raphson.

Another practical aspect is the moment of inertia of an ellipsoid about its principal axes, which appears in engineering‑oriented quantitative sections. For a solid ellipsoid of mass M, I_x = (1/5) M (b² + c²), I_y = (1/5) M (a² + c²), I_z = (1/5) M (a² + b²). These formulas are derived by scaling the sphere’s inertia tensor. While not strictly mensuration, they are frequently bundled with volume and surface area in “solid geometry” question sets.

  • Assuming the surface area of a general ellipsoid can be computed with a simple formula like 4π (abc)2/3 leads to large errors; always use Thomsen’s approximation or the exact spheroid formulas when two axes are equal.
  • Forgetting that the washer method requires the inner radius when the region does not touch the axis of revolution; omitting the inner radius term gives the volume of the solid generated by the outer curve only.
  • Using the planar triangle area formula ½ ab sin C for a spherical triangle instead of Girard’s theorem; the spherical excess must be computed from the interior angles.
  • Treating the depth‑volume relationship in an ellipsoidal tank as linear or quadratic without integrating the varying elliptical cross‑sections.
  • Confusing the eccentricity formulas for oblate and prolate spheroids, resulting in an imaginary logarithm or arcsine argument.
  • Applying the shell method about the y‑axis without multiplying by the distance x, i.e., using ∫ f(x) dx instead of ∫ x f(x) dx.
  • Neglecting to convert latitude/longitude differences to central angles before applying spherical cosine laws, causing angle errors of orders of magnitude.
