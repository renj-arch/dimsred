Foundations of Ratio and Proportion

1.1 Ratio Fundamentals

A ratio expresses the relative magnitude of two quantities measured in the same unit, written as a:b or a/b, and it remains unchanged if both terms are multiplied or divided by the same non‑zero number. When a ratio is reduced to its simplest form the two terms share no common factor other than one, which makes comparison across problems straightforward. The concept of antecedent and consequent is essential: the first term is the antecedent, the second the consequent, and the order cannot be reversed without altering the meaning of the relationship.

In practical examinations the ratio often appears in compound form, where two or more simple ratios are linked through a common term. To combine A:B = 2:3 and B:C = 5:7 the value of B must be made identical in both ratios; the least common multiple of 3 and 5 is 15, giving A:B = 10:15 and B:C = 15:21, so the required A:C becomes 10:21. This technique of equalising the middle term is a recurring pattern in partnership profit sharing and mixture problems alike.

1.2 Proportion and Its Properties

A proportion states that two ratios are equal, written a:b = c:d or a/b = c/d, and it implies the product of the extremes equals the product of the means (a·d = b·c). This cross‑multiplication property is the algebraic engine behind most ratio‑based equations and allows the transformation of a proportion into a linear equation in a single unknown. When four quantities are in proportion, the first and fourth are called extremes, the second and third means, and the relationship remains valid if each term is increased or decreased by the same amount, provided the operation respects the original units.

Two important derived properties are componendo and dividendo. If a/b = c/d then (a+b)/(a‑b) = (c+d)/(c‑d), provided the denominators are non‑zero. This rule converts a proportion into a new proportion that often isolates the unknown more directly, especially when the unknown appears in both numerator and denominator of a complex fraction. The invertendo property (b/a = d/c) and alternendo property (a/c = b/d) are also available, giving the solver a toolkit of equivalent forms to choose the most convenient algebraic path.

1.3 Direct and Inverse Variation

When two quantities vary directly, their ratio remains constant; if x varies directly as y, then x = k·y for some constant k, and the graph of x against y is a straight line through the origin. In inverse variation the product of the two quantities is constant, so x·y = k and the graph is a rectangular hyperbola. Many partnership problems involve direct variation of profit with capital and time, while mixture replacement problems often exhibit inverse variation between the quantity removed and the concentration that remains. Recognising which variation applies allows the student to set up the correct equation without resorting to trial and error.

Mixture and Alligation

2.1 The Alligation Principle

Alligation is a rule for finding the ratio in which two ingredients at different prices or concentrations must be mixed to obtain a mixture of a given mean price or concentration. The rule states that the quantity of the cheaper ingredient to the quantity of the dearer ingredient equals the difference between the dearer price and the mean price to the difference between the mean price and the cheaper price. This relationship is derived directly from the weighted average formula and is valid only when the mean lies between the two extremes; if the required mean falls outside the range, the problem has no physical solution.

The alligation diagram, often drawn as a cross, places the two extreme values at the left and right, the mean in the centre, and the differences along the arms. The visual layout makes it immediate to read off the required ratio without writing algebraic equations. Because the same arithmetic works for percentages, speeds, and any weighted average situation, the alligation cross is a versatile tool that extends far beyond simple price mixing.

2.2 Application to Concentration Problems

When the ingredients are solutions of different strengths, the price is replaced by concentration expressed as a percentage or a fraction. For example, mixing a 20 % acid solution with a 50 % acid solution to obtain a 30 % solution follows the identical alligation steps: (50‑30) : (30‑20) = 20 : 10 = 2 : 1. The ratio tells the relative volumes of the two solutions required. If the total volume of the final mixture is specified, the individual volumes are obtained by dividing the total in the same ratio.

It is important to note that alligation assumes the volumes are additive, which holds for ideal solutions but may fail for real liquids exhibiting volume contraction. Examination questions, however, always treat volumes as additive unless explicitly stated otherwise, so the student can safely apply the rule without correction factors.

2.3 Mixing More Than Two Ingredients

When three or more ingredients are involved, the problem is usually reduced to a series of binary alligations. One common approach is to first mix two ingredients to obtain an intermediate mean, then alligate that intermediate with the third ingredient. The order of pairing can affect the intermediate values but the final ratio of all components remains unique. An alternative method uses the concept of weighted average directly: if the final mean is M and the component values are x₁, x₂, …, xₙ with unknown weights w₁, w₂, …, wₙ, then Σ wᵢ xᵢ = M Σ wᵢ. Solving the resulting linear system yields the required proportions. In examinations the binary step‑wise method is preferred for its speed and lower algebraic burden.

Example 1 (Alligation with Three Ingredients)
A grocer mixes three varieties of tea costing Rs 120/kg, Rs 150/kg and Rs 180/kg in the ratio 2:3:5. Find the average price per kilogram of the mixture.
Solution: The weighted average price = (2×120 + 3×150 + 5×180) ÷ (2+3+5) = (240 + 450 + 900) ÷ 10 = 1590 ÷ 10 = Rs 159/kg.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a mixture question gives the final ratio of components, compute the weighted average directly; when it gives the final average and asks for the ratio, draw the alligation cross. The two approaches are inverses of each other and recognising which data are supplied saves precious seconds.

Successive Replacement and Dilution

3.1 The Replacement Formula

When a fixed volume of a homogeneous mixture is removed and replaced by an equal volume of another substance, the fraction of the original component that remains after one operation equals 1 minus the ratio of the replaced volume to the total volume. If the operation is repeated k times, the remaining fraction becomes (1 – r/V)ᵏ, where r is the volume removed each time and V is the constant total volume. This exponential decay model is the mathematical expression of successive dilution and appears frequently in problems involving milk‑water replacement, chemical concentration, and even population genetics analogies.

The derivation is straightforward: after the first removal the amount of original substance is V – r; after replacement the total volume returns to V, so the concentration becomes (V – r)/V = 1 – r/V. Each subsequent operation multiplies the concentration by the same factor because the mixture is always homogeneous before removal. Hence after k operations the concentration is (1 – r/V)ᵏ, and the absolute quantity of the original substance is V·(1 – r/V)ᵏ.

3.2 Variable Volume Replacement

In some variations the total volume changes because the replacement volume differs from the removed volume, or because the vessel is not refilled to the original level. The general principle remains: track the absolute amount of the component of interest after each step. If x₀ is the initial amount, and at step i a volume rᵢ is removed from a total Vᵢ, the amount left is xᵢ₋₁·(1 – rᵢ/Vᵢ). Then the added substance changes the total volume to Vᵢ₊₁ = Vᵢ – rᵢ + aᵢ, where aᵢ is the volume added. Iterating this recurrence yields the final amount. Although the algebra looks heavier, the step‑wise tracking prevents the common mistake of applying the constant‑volume formula when it is not valid.

3.3 Mixed Replacement with Two Components

When the replacement substance is itself a mixture, the problem becomes a system of two simultaneous recurrences, one for each component. For instance, if a vessel contains milk and water and each operation removes some mixture and adds a different milk‑water blend, the milk amount and water amount must be updated together. The total volume may stay constant, but the concentration of each component follows its own recurrence. Solving such problems is most reliable by writing the recurrence for the component asked about, substituting the known added concentration, and iterating. The alligation cross can sometimes shortcut the first step by giving the effective concentration of the added blend, but the iterative method guarantees correctness for any number of steps.

Partnership and Profit Sharing

4.1 Capital‑Time Contribution

In a partnership the profit (or loss) is distributed in proportion to each partner’s capital multiplied by the time for which that capital remains invested. If partner A invests C₁ for T₁ months and partner B invests C₂ for T₂ months, the profit sharing ratio is C₁T₁ : C₂T₂. This rule follows directly from the definition of simple interest on capital, treating profit as interest earned on the capital‑time product. When a partner joins or leaves mid‑year, the time factor is adjusted to the actual number of months the capital was at risk, and the ratio is computed with those effective months.

It is crucial to distinguish between capital contributed at the start and additional capital introduced later. Each tranche of capital generates its own capital‑time product, and the sum of all such products for a partner forms that partner’s total claim. The examination often tests this by giving a series of investments and withdrawals, requiring the candidate to construct a timeline and compute the weighted sum accurately.

4.2 Working and Sleeping Partners

A working partner receives a salary or commission for managing the business before the remaining profit is divided according to capital‑time ratios. The salary is treated as an expense of the firm and is deducted from the gross profit; the net profit is then shared. If the working partner also has a capital share, that share participates in the net profit division exactly like a sleeping partner’s share. The order of deduction matters: first subtract all agreed remunerations, then apply the capital‑time ratio to the remainder. Misplacing the salary after the division is a frequent error that changes the final amounts substantially.

4.3 Goodwill and Admission of a New Partner

When a new partner is admitted, the existing partners may sacrifice a portion of their profit share in favour of the newcomer. The sacrifice ratio is usually the old profit sharing ratio unless a different agreement exists. Goodwill, representing the value of the firm’s reputation, is often brought in by the new partner and distributed to the old partners in their sacrifice ratio. If goodwill is not brought in cash, it is adjusted through the capital accounts by crediting the old partners and debiting the new partner. Understanding the flow of goodwill entries is essential for questions that ask for the new capital balances after admission.

Coin and Value Problems

5.1 Denomination‑Based Equations

Coin problems present a total value and a total number of coins, often with a relationship between the counts of different denominations. Let the number of coins of denominations d₁, d₂, … be x₁, x₂, … . The two fundamental equations are Σ xᵢ = total number of coins and Σ dᵢ xᵢ = total value. When a third condition such as “the number of 50‑paise coins is twice the number of 1‑rupee coins” is given, it provides a linear relation among the variables, turning the system into a solvable set of simultaneous equations. The standard technique is to express all variables in terms of one unknown using the ratio conditions, substitute into the value equation, and solve.

Because Indian currency denominations are fixed (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 paise and 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 2000 rupees), the coefficients in the value equation are known constants. This makes the arithmetic predictable and allows the student to anticipate whether the solution will be integral; a non‑integral result signals an error in setting up the equations or in the arithmetic.

5.2 Ratio of Coins to Value

Sometimes the problem gives the ratio of the number of coins of each denomination and the total value, asking for the number of each coin. If the ratio of counts is a:b:c for denominations p, q, r, then the total value contributed by each denomination is proportional to a·p : b·q : c·r. The share of the total value belonging to the first denomination is (a·p) / (a·p + b·q + c·r) times the total value. Dividing that share by the denomination p yields the number of coins of that type. This method bypasses simultaneous equations entirely and is extremely fast when the ratio of counts is given directly.

5.3 Mixed Coin‑Note Problems

Modern examinations occasionally combine coins and currency notes in a single problem, treating notes as higher denominations with the same algebraic structure. The only difference is that notes are usually counted in whole numbers while coins may be counted in large quantities, but mathematically they are identical variables. The same two‑equation framework applies, and the ratio‑of‑value shortcut works equally well. Care must be taken to keep units consistent: if the total value is given in rupees and some denominations are in paise, convert everything to a single unit (preferably paise) before forming equations to avoid decimal errors.

Mastery of these five topic groups equips the candidate to handle every standard question on ratio, proportion, mixture, partnership, and coin problems that appears in the quantitative aptitude section.

Compound Ratio and Continued Proportion

A compound ratio is formed by multiplying the corresponding terms of two or more simple ratios. If the ratios are a:b, c:d and e:f, the compound ratio is (a×c×e) : (b×d×f). This operation is associative and commutative, so the order of multiplication does not affect the result. In examinations, compound ratios appear when a problem states that the ratio of A to B is 2:3, the ratio of B to C is 4:5 and the ratio of C to D is 6:7; the overall ratio A:B:C:D is obtained by aligning the common terms and multiplying across. The alignment step requires making the middle terms equal by taking the least common multiple of the overlapping quantities.

Continued proportion extends the idea of proportion to three or more quantities. Four numbers a, b, c, d are in continued proportion when a:b = b:c = c:d. This implies b² = a×c, c² = b×d and a×d = b×c. The middle terms are called mean proportionals; the first and last are extremes. If only three numbers a, b, c are given with a:b = b:c, then b is the mean proportional between a and c, and b = √(a×c). The third proportional to a and b is the number c such that a:b = b:c, giving c = b²/a. These relationships are frequently tested in questions that ask for a missing term when the product of extremes equals the product of means.

A practical shortcut for continued proportion problems is to express all terms as powers of a common base. For instance, if a:b = b:c = c:d = k, then b = a·k, c = a·k², d = a·k³. The ratio a:b:c:d becomes 1:k:k²:k³. This geometric progression representation simplifies algebraic manipulation, especially when the sum or product of the terms is given. The examiner often hides the constant ratio k inside a word problem about successive discounts, population growth or compound interest, requiring the candidate to recognise the underlying continued proportion.

When compound ratios and continued proportions appear together, the problem typically provides a chain of ratios and asks for a specific term after a series of operations such as addition, subtraction or replacement. The systematic approach is: (1) write each given ratio in fractional form, (2) equalise the common terms using LCM, (3) multiply across to obtain the compound ratio, (4) if a continued proportion is indicated, verify the mean proportional condition, (5) apply any additional arithmetic operations on the resulting terms. Errors usually arise from skipping the equalisation step or from treating the compound ratio as a simple sum of the individual ratios.

Advanced applications include mixture problems where three ingredients are combined in a compound ratio and then a portion is replaced by another mixture. The replacement step creates a new continued proportion among the remaining quantities. Solving such problems demands writing the initial compound ratio, applying the replacement formula (final amount = initial amount × (1 – fraction replaced)ⁿ) to each component separately, and then re‑expressing the final quantities as a compound ratio. Mastery of this layered technique separates high scorers from average candidates.

Alligation with Three or More Components and Weighted Average

The classical alligation rule handles two ingredients by placing their costs on the left, the mean price in the centre and the differences on the right. For three or more components the same principle extends through the concept of weighted average. If components with costs C₁, C₂, C₃ are mixed in quantities Q₁, Q₂, Q₃ to obtain a mean cost M, the equation Σ(Qᵢ·Cᵢ) = M·ΣQᵢ holds. Rearranging gives ΣQᵢ·(Cᵢ – M) = 0. The terms (Cᵢ – M) are the deviations of each component cost from the mean. Components cheaper than M have negative deviations, dearer ones positive. The ratio of quantities is inversely proportional to the absolute deviations, but only after grouping the deviations so that their algebraic sum is zero.

A practical method for three components is the alligation alternate. Write the three costs in ascending order. Pair the lowest with the highest, compute their differences with the mean, and treat the middle cost as a pivot. For example, costs 30, 40, 50 and mean 42. Deviations: 30→–12, 40→–2, 50→+8. To balance, the total negative deviation (–12 –2 = –14) must be offset by the positive deviation (+8). This is impossible with only these three numbers, indicating that the given mean cannot be achieved with all three components present in positive quantities. The examiner often uses this impossibility as a trap; the correct response is to state that no such mixture exists unless one component quantity is zero.

When the mean lies between the lowest and highest costs, a feasible mixture exists. The standard algorithm: (1) list costs in ascending order, (2) compute deviations from the mean, (3) separate negative and positive deviations, (4) find the least common multiple of the absolute deviations, (5) assign provisional quantities as LCM/|deviation| for each component, (6) adjust the provisional quantities by a common factor to satisfy any additional constraints such as total quantity or a given ratio between two components. This method reduces the problem to integer arithmetic and avoids solving simultaneous equations.

Weighted average interpretation provides an alternative viewpoint. The mean cost M is the weighted average of the component costs with weights equal to the quantities. If the problem gives the ratio of quantities directly, the mean can be computed instantly: M = (Σ ratioᵢ·Cᵢ) / Σ ratioᵢ. Conversely, if the mean and all but one quantity ratio are known, the missing ratio is obtained by solving the weighted average equation for the unknown weight. This approach is especially fast when the number of components exceeds three, because the alligation diagram becomes cumbersome.

Examiners also test the reverse scenario: given the final mixture cost and the ratio of two components, find the cost of the third component. This is a linear equation in the unknown cost. The candidate must set up the weighted average, substitute the known ratios, and solve. A common mistake is to treat the unknown cost as a deviation and apply the alligation diagram incorrectly. The safe route is always to fall back on the weighted average formula, which works for any number of components and any combination of known and unknown variables.

Technique: Alligation Alternate for Three Components
A grocer mixes three varieties of rice costing ₹30, ₹40 and ₹50 per kg. He wants the mixture to cost ₹42 per kg. In what ratio must he mix the three varieties?
Step 1: Write costs in ascending order: 30, 40, 50. Mean = 42.
Step 2: Compute deviations: 30–42 = –12, 40–42 = –2, 50–42 = +8.
Step 3: Sum of negative deviations = –14. Sum of positive deviations = +8. Since –14 ≠ +8, the deviations cannot balance with all three components present in positive quantities.
Step 4: Check feasibility: The mean 42 lies between 30 and 50, so a mixture of only the cheapest and dearest can achieve it. Using two‑component alligation: (50–42):(42–30) = 8:12 = 2:3. Thus rice at ₹30 and ₹50 must be mixed 2:3. The ₹40 rice cannot be part of a mixture costing ₹42 unless its quantity is zero.
Answer: The three‑component mixture is impossible; the required ratio for a feasible two‑component mixture is 2:3 (₹30:₹50).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a three‑component alligation question appears, first compute the deviations from the mean. If the algebraic sum of deviations cannot be zero with all components positive, immediately conclude that the mixture cannot contain all three varieties. This saves time and avoids forced algebraic solutions that yield negative quantities.

Partnership with Changing Capital and Time‑Weighted Profit Sharing

In a dynamic partnership, partners may inject additional capital, withdraw part of their investment, or change the period of involvement. The profit‑sharing ratio is based on the product of capital and the time for which that capital remains employed, commonly called capital‑time units. If partner A invests ₹1,00,000 for 6 months, then adds ₹50,000 for the next 4 months, and finally withdraws ₹30,000 for the last 2 months, the capital‑time contribution is calculated piecewise: (1,00,000×6) + (1,50,000×4) + (1,20,000×2) = 6,00,000 + 6,00,000 + 2,40,000 = 14,40,000 capital‑month units. Each partner’s share of profit = (individual capital‑time units / total capital‑time units) × net profit.

A frequent variation introduces a working partner who receives a fixed monthly salary before profit division. The salary is an expense deducted from gross profit; the remainder is distributed according to capital‑time ratios. If the working partner also contributes capital, that capital participates in the net profit division exactly like a sleeping partner’s capital. The order of operations is critical: (1) compute gross profit, (2) subtract all agreed salaries and commissions, (3) calculate capital‑time units for every partner including the working partner, (4) allocate net profit proportionally. Reversing steps (2) and (3) inflates the working partner’s share and is a classic examiner trap.

When a new partner is admitted mid‑year, the old partners’ capital‑time units are computed for the full year, while the new partner’s units are computed only for the remaining months. Goodwill brought in by the new partner is credited to the old partners in their sacrifice ratio, which is usually the old profit‑sharing ratio unless a different agreement exists. If goodwill is not paid in cash, the adjustment is made through capital accounts: debit the new partner’s capital account and credit the old partners’ capital accounts in the sacrifice ratio. Subsequent profit sharing uses the new capital balances as the basis for capital‑time calculations.

Problems may also involve a partner retiring before the year‑end. The retiring partner’s capital‑time units are calculated up to the date of retirement. The remaining partners continue with their existing capitals, possibly re‑valuing assets and liabilities. The retiring partner’s share of profit up to retirement is settled either in cash or by transferring to a loan account. The new profit‑sharing ratio among continuing partners is often given explicitly; if not, it defaults to their capital‑time ratio after retirement.

Advanced questions combine multiple events: additional investments, withdrawals, salary, admission, and retirement within the same accounting period. The systematic solution is to draw a timeline for each partner, mark every capital change with the date, compute the capital‑time product for each interval, sum them, and then apply the profit‑distribution hierarchy (salary → goodwill adjustment → net profit). Using a table with columns Partner, Period, Capital, Months, Capital‑Time eliminates omission errors and makes the arithmetic transparent.

Coin Problems with Constraints and Diophantine Solutions

Coin problems that provide only the total value and total number of coins lead to a linear Diophantine equation of the form a·x + b·y = V with x + y = N, where a and b are denominations in paise, V is total value in paise, and N is total coin count. Eliminating one variable yields (a–b)·x = V – b·N. Since a, b, V, N are known integers, x must be an integer. The equation has a solution only if (V – b·N) is divisible by (a–b). This divisibility condition is a quick check: if it fails, the problem data is inconsistent or the candidate has mis‑read the denominations.

When three or more denominations appear, the system becomes a set of linear equations with more unknowns than equations. Additional constraints such as “the number of 50‑paise coins is twice the number of 1‑rupee coins” supply the missing equations. The standard technique is to express all variables in terms of a single parameter using the ratio constraints, substitute into the value equation, and solve for the parameter. Because Indian denominations are fixed, the coefficients are small integers, making the arithmetic manageable.

A powerful shortcut for ratio‑of‑coins problems: if the ratio of counts is p:q:r for denominations d₁, d₂, d₃, the value contributed by each denomination is proportional to p·d₁ : q·d₂ : r·d₃. The total value V is split in that ratio. The number of coins of the first denomination = (p·d₁ / Σ) × V / d₁ = p·V / Σ, where Σ = p·d₁ + q·d₂ + r·d₃. This eliminates simultaneous equations entirely and works even when the total number of coins is not given.

Mixed coin‑note problems treat notes as higher denominations (e.g., ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500, ₹2000). The same algebraic structure applies. The only caution is unit consistency: convert everything to paise or to rupees before forming equations. A common error is to mix rupees and paise, producing decimal coefficients that obscure the integer nature of the solution. Converting to paise guarantees integer arithmetic and makes the divisibility test straightforward.

Some examinations ask for the minimum or maximum number of coins of a particular denomination under given constraints. This transforms the problem into an integer optimisation: maximise or minimise x subject to a·x + b·y = V, x + y = N, x, y ≥ 0 integers. Because the feasible region is a line segment in the integer lattice, the extreme values occur at the endpoints where one variable is as large as possible while the other remains non‑negative. The candidate can find the endpoints by setting y = 0 (or x = 0) and checking integrality, then stepping by the denominator of the reduced fraction (a–b)/gcd(a,b) to locate the nearest integer solution.

  • Forgetting to deduct working partner salary before computing capital‑time profit shares.
  • Treating goodwill brought by a new partner as part of net profit instead of adjusting capital accounts first.
