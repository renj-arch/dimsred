Foundations of Commercial Arithmetic: Cost Price and Selling Price

1.1 The Transaction Cycle and the Definition of Cost Price

Every commercial transaction begins with an acquisition, and the monetary value assigned to that acquisition forms the bedrock of all subsequent profit or loss calculations. The cost price represents the total outlay incurred by a trader to bring an article into a saleable condition, encompassing not only the invoice price paid to the supplier but also unavoidable overheads such as freight, octroi, import duties, and any expenditure on repair or refurbishment before the goods are offered for sale. It is critical to understand that cost price is not merely a static label on a ledger; it is a dynamic aggregate that determines the baseline from which profitability is measured. When a question states that a shopkeeper buys an article for a certain sum, the examinee must instinctively verify whether that sum represents the pure purchase price or the effective cost price after including incidentals, because an error in identifying the true cost price propagates through every subsequent percentage calculation.

1.2 Selling Price as the Realisation of Value

The selling price is the consideration received by the seller upon transferring ownership of the goods to the buyer, and it serves as the sole metric for determining whether the venture resulted in a gain or a loss. Unlike the cost price, which looks backward at expenditure, the selling price looks forward at revenue realisation. The relationship between these two figures is binary and absolute: if the selling price exceeds the cost price, the difference constitutes a profit; if the cost price exceeds the selling price, the difference constitutes a loss. There is no third state in elementary commercial arithmetic, though a break-even scenario where selling price equals cost price represents the mathematical boundary between the two. Students must internalise that profit and loss are always calculated as a percentage of the cost price unless the problem explicitly directs otherwise, a convention rooted in the principle that the investor measures return on the capital actually deployed.

1.3 The Algebra of Profit and Loss Percentages

The standard formulae — Profit Percentage equals Profit divided by Cost Price multiplied by one hundred, and Loss Percentage equals Loss divided by Cost Price multiplied by one hundred — are not arbitrary rules but direct expressions of the rate of return on investment. When a problem states that an article was sold at a profit of twenty percent, it implies that the selling price is one hundred and twenty percent of the cost price, allowing the student to construct the equation Selling Price equals Cost Price multiplied by one plus the profit percentage divided by one hundred. Conversely, a loss of twenty percent means the selling price is eighty percent of the cost price. This multiplicative relationship is the engine that drives almost every variation of the topic, from finding the cost price when the selling price and profit percentage are known, to solving complex chains of transactions where an article passes through multiple hands, each adding a layer of profit or loss. Mastery of this algebraic translation — moving fluently between the language of percentages and the language of fractions — is the single most determinative skill for speed and accuracy in the examination hall.

1.4 The Concept of Equal Percentage Profit and Loss on Identical Selling Prices

A frequently examined nuance arises when two articles are sold at the same selling price, one at a profit of x percent and the other at a loss of x percent. Intuition often suggests that the profit and loss cancel each other out, resulting in a break-even situation, but the mathematics dictates otherwise because the cost prices of the two articles are necessarily different. The article sold at a profit had a lower cost base, while the article sold at a loss had a higher cost base; therefore, the absolute rupee amount of the loss exceeds the absolute rupee amount of the profit. The net result is always a loss, and its percentage is given by the formula x squared divided by one hundred. This result is independent of the actual selling price and depends solely on the magnitude of the percentage x. Recognising this pattern allows a candidate to solve such problems in seconds without constructing simultaneous equations, turning a potential time sink into a guaranteed mark.

Marked Price, Discount, and the Mechanics of Price Reduction

2.1 The Role of Marked Price in Retail Strategy

The marked price, often labelled as the list price or the printed price, is the price tag attached to the article before any negotiation or promotional reduction takes place. It functions as a strategic anchor set by the retailer, typically above the cost price to provide a buffer for bargaining, seasonal sales, or volume discounts. The difference between the marked price and the cost price is known as the markup, and the markup percentage is calculated on the cost price, reflecting the trader's intended gross margin before any customer-facing concessions. Understanding the distinction between markup (calculated on cost price) and discount (calculated on marked price) is essential because the two percentages operate on different bases; a markup of fifty percent followed by a discount of fifty percent does not return the trader to the cost price but results in a loss, since the discount is applied to a larger base than the markup.

2.2 Discount Calculation and its Impact on Selling Price

Discount is a reduction offered on the marked price to incentivise a sale, and it is always expressed as a percentage of the marked price. The selling price is therefore derived by subtracting the discount amount from the marked price, or equivalently, by multiplying the marked price by the factor one minus the discount percentage divided by one hundred. This direct relationship means that if any two of the three variables — marked price, discount percentage, and selling price — are known, the third can be determined instantly. Examination questions often reverse this logic, providing the selling price and the discount percentage and asking for the marked price, which requires the candidate to divide the selling price by the factor one minus the discount percentage divided by one hundred. Fluency in this inversion is a hallmark of a well-prepared student.

2.3 Interlinking Cost Price, Marked Price, and Selling Price

The complete commercial chain connects cost price to marked price through markup, and marked price to selling price through discount. A typical problem might state that a shopkeeper marks his goods forty percent above the cost price and allows a discount of ten percent; the task is to find the effective profit percentage. Here, the candidate must recognise that the selling price equals the cost price multiplied by one plus the markup percentage divided by one hundred, further multiplied by one minus the discount percentage divided by one hundred. The effective profit percentage is then the deviation of this composite multiplier from unity, expressed as a percentage. This chaining of multipliers is the most robust method for solving such problems because it avoids the pitfall of calculating intermediate rupee values that may not be integers, keeping the arithmetic clean and the logic transparent.

2.4 The Scenario of Successive Markups or Discounts on the Same Base

Occasionally, a problem presents a situation where a price is increased by a certain percentage and then decreased by another percentage, or vice versa, with both percentages applied to the same original base rather than successively. This is distinct from successive percentage changes, where the second change applies to the result of the first. When changes apply to the same base, the net effect is the algebraic sum of the individual percentages; a markup of twenty percent followed by a discount of fifteen percent on the original marked price yields a net increase of five percent on that base. However, the standard retail model assumes successive application — the discount applies to the already marked-up price — and candidates must read the wording with precision to distinguish between these two fundamentally different mathematical structures.

Illustrative Problem: Markup, Discount, and Effective Profit
A trader marks his goods 50% above the cost price. He sells half the stock at the marked price, one quarter at a discount of 20% on the marked price, and the rest at a discount of 40% on the marked price. What is his overall profit percentage?
Solution: Let the total cost price of the stock be Rs. 100 units (or any convenient base). Since the markup is 50%, the total marked price of the stock is Rs. 150 units. The stock is divided into three parts: half (50% of cost base), one quarter (25% of cost base), and the remaining quarter (25% of cost base). However, it is simpler to assume the cost price of each unit is Re. 1 and total units are 4. Total CP = Rs. 4. Marked Price per unit = Rs. 1.50. Total MP = Rs. 6. Sale 1: Half the stock (2 units) at MP. Revenue = 2 × 1.50 = Rs. 3. Sale 2: One quarter (1 unit) at 20% discount. SP = 1.50 × 0.80 = Rs. 1.20. Revenue = Rs. 1.20. Sale 3: Remaining quarter (1 unit) at 40% discount. SP = 1.50 × 0.60 = Rs. 0.90. Revenue = Rs. 0.90. Total Selling Price = 3 + 1.20 + 0.90 = Rs. 5.10. Total Cost Price = Rs. 4.00. Profit = Rs. 1.10. Profit Percentage = (1.10 / 4.00) × 100 = 27.5%.
Examination Insight
In questions involving partial sales at different discount rates, always assume a convenient total quantity (like 4, 10, or 100 units) or a convenient total cost price (like Rs. 100) rather than an algebraic variable. This converts abstract percentages into concrete arithmetic, drastically reducing the chance of algebraic slips under time pressure.

Successive Discounts and the Equivalent Single Discount

3.1 The Mechanism of Successive Percentage Reductions

When a trader offers two or more discounts in sequence — for example, a festival discount of twenty percent followed by a loyalty discount of ten percent — the second discount is applied not to the original marked price but to the reduced price that results from the first discount. This compounding effect means that the total reduction is not the arithmetic sum of the two percentages but a slightly smaller figure, because the base for the second discount has already been shrunk by the first. The formula for the equivalent single discount when two discounts of x percent and y percent are applied successively is x plus y minus the product of x and y divided by one hundred. This formula is derived directly from the multiplicative logic: the final selling price equals the marked price multiplied by (one minus x/100) multiplied by (one minus y/100), and expanding this product yields the equivalent single discount factor.

3.2 Extending the Logic to Three or More Successive Discounts

The principle extends naturally to three or more successive discounts. If discounts of x percent, y percent, and z percent are offered in succession, the equivalent single discount can be found by first combining x and y into an intermediate equivalent discount d1 using the two-discount formula, and then combining d1 with z using the same formula again. Alternatively, the candidate can multiply the three retention factors (100 minus x)/100, (100 minus y)/100, and (100 minus z)/100, subtract the product from one, and multiply by one hundred. The multiplicative approach is generally faster and less error-prone for three or more discounts because it avoids the iterative application of the subtraction formula and keeps the arithmetic in the domain of fractions.

3.3 Comparing Successive Discounts with a Single Discount of Equal Sum

A classic comparative question asks whether a single discount of thirty percent is better for the buyer than two successive discounts of twenty percent and ten percent. Since the equivalent single discount for twenty percent and ten percent is twenty-eight percent (20 + 10 - 2), the single discount of thirty percent is more favourable to the buyer, yielding a lower selling price. This comparison tests the candidate's understanding that successive discounts are always less beneficial to the buyer — and more beneficial to the seller — than a single discount equal to their sum. The difference, which is the product term divided by one hundred, represents the "discount on the discount" that the seller retains by structuring the concession in stages.

3.4 Successive Increases and the Symmetry of the Formula

The same mathematical structure governs successive percentage increases, such as a price hike of ten percent followed by a further hike of twenty percent. The equivalent single increase is given by x plus y plus the product of x and y divided by one hundred, where the sign of the product term is positive because both changes amplify the base. If a problem mixes an increase and a decrease — for instance, a price is increased by twenty percent and then decreased by twenty percent — the equivalent single change is a decrease of four percent (20 - 20 - 400/100 = -4). This demonstrates that an equal percentage increase followed by an equal percentage decrease never restores the original value; it always results in a net decrease equal to the square of the percentage divided by one hundred, a direct analogue of the profit-loss symmetry discussed earlier.

Dishonest Dealings: False Weights and Measures

4.1 The Fundamental Deception of False Weight

A distinct category of problems deals with a trader who claims to sell at cost price or at a stated profit percentage but uses a weighing scale that shows a higher quantity than the actual quantity delivered. This practice allows the trader to charge for goods not actually given, creating a hidden profit that is purely a function of the discrepancy between the true weight and the false weight. The standard formula for the profit percentage in such cases is the difference between the true weight and the false weight divided by the false weight, multiplied by one hundred. The denominator is the false weight because that represents the actual quantity the trader parts with — his effective cost base — while the numerator represents the quantity he charges for but does not deliver, which is his illicit gain.

4.2 Combining False Weight with Stated Profit or Loss

Problems often compound the deception by stating that the shopkeeper sells at a nominal profit of ten percent while using a weight of nine hundred grams for a kilogram. In this scenario, the overall profit percentage is not a simple sum. The correct approach is to treat the transaction as two successive multiplications on the cost price: first, the stated profit multiplier (1.10), and second, the weight cheating multiplier (True Weight / False Weight = 1000/900 = 10/9). The overall multiplier is the product of these two factors, and the overall profit percentage is the deviation of this product from one, expressed as a percentage. This multiplicative chaining ensures that the profit on the cheating is calculated on the already inflated selling price, capturing the compounding effect that a simple additive approach would miss.

4.3 The Case of the Dishonest Buyer

The mirror image of the dishonest seller is the dishonest buyer who uses a false weight while purchasing, obtaining more quantity than he pays for. If a buyer uses a scale that shows nine hundred grams when the true weight is one kilogram, he receives one thousand grams for the price of nine hundred. His gain percentage is calculated using the same logic: the extra quantity obtained (100 grams) divided by the quantity paid for (900 grams), multiplied by one hundred, yielding 11.11 percent. When a dishonest buyer meets a dishonest seller in a single transaction chain, the overall effect is the product of their individual gain multipliers, a scenario that occasionally appears in advanced data interpretation sets or caselets.

4.4 Liquid Adulteration as a Volume Analogue

The principle of false weight extends seamlessly to liquid adulteration, where a trader mixes water (assumed to have zero cost) with milk or another liquid and sells the mixture at the cost price of the pure liquid. If one litre of milk is mixed with two hundred millilitres of water and the resulting 1.2 litres is sold at the cost price of one litre of pure milk, the trader effectively sells 1.2 litres for the price of 1 litre. The profit percentage is the volume of water added divided by the volume of pure milk, multiplied by one hundred (200/1000 × 100 = 20%). This is mathematically identical to the false weight problem, with volume replacing weight, and the same multiplicative logic applies if the mixture is sold at a stated profit or discount.

Special Transaction Structures: Equal SP, CP of X equals SP of Y, and Partnership

5.1 The "Cost Price of X Articles Equals Selling Price of Y Articles" Model

A perennially favourite examination pattern states that the cost price of x articles is equal to the selling price of y articles, asking the candidate to find the profit or loss percentage. This relationship implies that the revenue from selling y articles exactly covers the cost of x articles. If x is greater than y, the cost base is larger than the revenue base for the same number of transactions, indicating a loss; if y is greater than x, it indicates a profit. The profit percentage is given by the formula (y minus x) divided by x multiplied by one hundred when y > x, and the loss percentage is (x minus y) divided by x multiplied by one hundred when x > y. This formula is derived by equating the total cost price of x articles to the total selling price of y articles, assuming a unit cost price, and solving for the percentage deviation. Recognising this pattern allows for an immediate answer without any intermediate calculation of per-unit prices.

5.2 Transactions Involving Free Articles or "Buy X Get Y Free" Offers

Modern retail promotions such as "Buy two get one free" or "Buy three get two free" are mathematically equivalent to the CP-SP equality model. In a "Buy 2 get 1 free" offer, the customer pays for two articles and receives three. The seller's cost is for three articles, but his revenue is from two. This maps directly to the formula with x = 3 (cost articles) and y = 2 (paid articles), resulting in a loss percentage of (3-2)/3 × 100 = 33.33% if the marked price equals the cost price. However, if the marked price includes a markup, the effective profit or loss must be calculated by comparing the total revenue (marked price × paid quantity) with the total cost (cost price × total quantity delivered). The multiplicative framework — Revenue Multiplier = (Paid Qty / Total Qty) × (Marked Price / Cost Price) — handles all such variations uniformly.

5.3 Partnership and Profit Sharing Ratios

While partnership is often taught as a separate chapter, its arithmetic core is identical to profit and loss: the division of a net gain (or loss) among partners in proportion to their capital contributions weighted by the time for which the capital was employed. The profit sharing ratio is the ratio of the products of capital and time for each partner. If a partner joins or leaves mid-year, or if additional capital is introduced or withdrawn, the time-weighting must be calculated in months or days with precision. A common trap is to average the capitals or to ignore the time factor entirely, leading to an incorrect ratio. The connection to the current topic is explicit when a question asks for the profit percentage of the firm overall, which is calculated on the total capital employed over the period, and then the individual shares are derived from the agreed ratio.

5.4 The Concept of Marked Price with Sales Tax or GST

Contemporary problems sometimes introduce a statutory tax such as Goods and Services Tax (GST) levied on the selling price. The marked price is the pre-tax price tag; the selling price inclusive of tax is the marked price plus the tax amount. If a problem states that a customer pays a certain amount inclusive of tax, and the trader offers a discount on the marked price, the candidate must first strip the tax from the amount paid to find the actual selling price (revenue to the trader), and then work backwards to the marked price or cost price. The tax is a pass-through liability for the trader and does not form part of his revenue or cost base for profit calculation; confusing the tax-inclusive price with the selling price is a frequent and costly error.

Advanced Problem Solving: Chains, Cycles, and Data Sufficiency

6.1 Multi-Stage Transaction Chains

Examination questions frequently construct a chain where a manufacturer sells to a wholesaler at a profit, the wholesaler sells to a retailer at a profit, and the retailer sells to a consumer at a profit or after a discount. The final selling price to the consumer is given, and the candidate is asked to find the manufacturer's cost price. The most efficient method is to work backwards from the consumer price, dividing by the retailer's multiplier (1 + profit%/100 or 1 - discount%/100), then by the wholesaler's multiplier, and finally by the manufacturer's multiplier. This reverse engineering avoids the accumulation of rounding errors that can occur when working forward with an assumed variable, and it naturally handles the different bases (each stage calculates profit on its own cost price) without confusion.

6.2 Cyclic Transactions and the Illusion of Profit

A cyclic transaction occurs when A sells to B, B sells to C, and C sells back to A, each at a stated profit percentage. If the cycle closes at the same price at which it started, the product of all the multipliers must equal one. If the product is greater than one, the final price is higher than the initial price, implying that the last buyer (A) pays more than his original cost, which is impossible in a closed loop unless external money enters. Such problems test the candidate's ability to set up the equation: Initial Price × (1 + p1/100) × (1 + p2/100) × (1 + p3/100) = Final Price, and solve for the unknown percentage or the initial price. The key insight is that every participant in the chain calculates profit on their own cost price, so the multipliers chain multiplicatively, not additively.

6.3 Data Sufficiency in Profit and Loss Contexts

In the Data Sufficiency format, the candidate must determine whether the given statements provide enough information to answer the question, without necessarily solving it. For profit and loss, a common question asks for the profit percentage. Statement I might give the ratio of cost price to selling price (sufficient, as profit% = (SP/CP - 1) × 100). Statement II might give the absolute profit in rupees (insufficient alone, as the cost price is unknown). Statement III might give the marked price and discount percentage (sufficient, as SP can be found, but CP is still needed unless markup info is also given). The ability to instantly recognise which combinations of variables (CP, SP, MP, Profit%, Loss%, Discount%, Markup%) are sufficient to determine the target variable is a high-yield skill that separates high scorers from average performers.

6.4 Approximation and Fraction-Percentage Equivalence for Speed

Finally, computational speed in this topic is heavily dependent on the candidate's fluency with common fraction-percentage equivalents. Knowing that 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/6 = 16.66%, 1/7 ≈ 14.28%, 1/9 = 11.11%, and 1/11 = 9.09% allows the conversion of awkward percentages like 37.5% (3/8) or 62.5% (5/8) into simple fractions, turning multiplications into cancellations. For instance, a profit of 37.5% means SP = CP × (1 + 3/8) = CP × 11/8. If the selling price is given as Rs. 440, the cost price is 440 × 8/11 = 320, solved mentally in seconds. Building and maintaining a mental table of these equivalences up to at least 1/20 is not a shortcut but a fundamental requirement for attempting the quantitative section within the prescribed time limit.

Profit and Loss in Installment and Hire‑Purchase Transactions

Installment sales separate the cash price of an article from the hire‑purchase price, which includes interest or finance charges. The cash price is the amount a buyer would pay outright; the hire‑purchase price is the sum of the down payment plus all periodic installments. For the seller, the difference between the hire‑purchase price and the cash price represents finance income, while the profit on the goods themselves is calculated on the cash price (or on the cost price if the cash price is not given). The buyer’s effective cost of credit is the internal rate of return that equates the present value of the installment stream to the cash price. In examination problems, the cash price, down payment, number of installments, and installment amount are typically given, and the candidate must find either the rate of interest per annum (simple or compound) or the profit percentage earned by the dealer on the cash price. When the interest is simple, the total interest = (hire‑purchase price – cash price). The annual simple interest rate = (total interest × 100) ÷ (cash price × loan period in years). When the interest is compounded, the present value of an annuity formula is used: cash price – down payment = installment × [1 – (1 + r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r, where r is the periodic rate and n the number of periods. Solving for r usually requires trial‑and‑error or the use of standard annuity tables; however, many competitive‑exam questions keep the numbers small so that r is a recognizable fraction such as 1/12 (≈8.33 % per month) or 1/20 (5 % per month). A common twist is to ask for the dealer’s profit percentage on cost when the cash price itself includes a markup. In that case, first recover the cost price from the cash price using the given markup percentage, then compute profit on cost as (cash price – cost price) ÷ cost price × 100. Another variation introduces a “flat rate” of interest quoted on the original cash price rather than on the reducing balance; the effective annual rate is then higher than the quoted flat rate, and the candidate must convert the flat rate to an effective rate using the formula: effective rate ≈ (2 × n × flat rate) ÷ (n + 1) for monthly installments, where n is the number of installments. Mastery of these relationships allows rapid navigation of installment‑based profit‑loss questions without resorting to lengthy algebraic manipulation.

Break‑Even Analysis and Contribution Margin in Pricing Decisions

Break‑even analysis translates the profit‑loss framework into a decision‑making tool for pricing and volume planning. The fundamental identity is: total revenue = total variable cost + total fixed cost + profit. At the break‑even point, profit = 0, so total revenue = total variable cost + total fixed cost. The contribution margin per unit is defined as selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit. It represents the amount each unit contributes toward covering fixed costs and generating profit. The break‑even quantity (in units) = total fixed cost ÷ contribution margin per unit. The break‑even revenue = break‑even quantity × selling price per unit, or equivalently, total fixed cost ÷ contribution margin ratio, where the contribution margin ratio = contribution margin per unit ÷ selling price per unit. A target profit before tax simply adds the desired profit to fixed costs in the numerator: required quantity = (fixed cost + target profit) ÷ contribution margin per unit. The margin of safety measures how far actual or budgeted sales can fall before a loss occurs: margin of safety (units) = actual sales quantity – break‑even quantity; margin of safety (%) = margin of safety (units) ÷ actual sales quantity × 100. Operating leverage quantifies the sensitivity of profit to changes in sales volume: degree of operating leverage = contribution margin ÷ profit. A high degree indicates that a small percentage change in sales produces a large percentage change in profit, which is typical of businesses with high fixed costs and low variable costs (e.g., software, manufacturing with heavy automation). In exam questions, candidates are often given a table of costs at two activity levels and asked to separate fixed and variable components using the high‑low method: variable cost per unit = (cost at high activity – cost at low activity) ÷ (high units – low units); fixed cost = total cost at either level – variable cost per unit × units at that level. Once the cost structure is known, any of the above formulas can be applied. A frequent examiner twist is to present a scenario where the selling price is reduced by a certain percentage and the variable cost per unit also changes (perhaps due to economies of scale), then ask for the new break‑even point or the percentage change in profit at a given volume. The candidate must recompute contribution margin per unit with the revised figures and re‑apply the break‑even formula. Another twist involves multiple products with a given sales mix; the weighted average contribution margin per unit = Σ (mix proportion × contribution margin of each product). The break‑even quantity for the bundle = total fixed cost ÷ weighted average contribution margin. Understanding these mechanics enables rapid solution of multi‑product break‑even problems that otherwise appear cumbersome.

Break‑Even with Price Reduction and Volume Increase
A firm sells a single product at ₹200 per unit. Variable cost is ₹120 per unit and total fixed cost is ₹1,60,000. The management proposes a 10 % price cut, expecting a 25 % increase in sales volume. At the current sales level of 5,000 units, determine (a) the current profit, (b) the new break‑even quantity, and (c) whether the proposal increases total profit.
Current contribution margin per unit = 200 – 120 = ₹80. Current profit = (80 × 5,000) – 1,60,000 = ₹4,00,000 – ₹1,60,000 = ₹2,40,000. New selling price = 200 × 0.90 = ₹180. New contribution margin = 180 – 120 = ₹60. New break‑even quantity = 1,60,000 ÷ 60 = 2,667 units (rounded up). Expected new volume = 5,000 × 1.25 = 6,250 units. New profit = (60 × 6,250) – 1,60,000 = ₹3,75,000 – ₹1,60,000 = ₹2,15,000. The proposal reduces profit from ₹2,40,000 to ₹2,15,000 despite higher volume, because the contribution margin falls faster than volume rises.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Break‑Even Questions
Memorise the three core formulas: break‑even units = FC ÷ CM, break‑even revenue = FC ÷ CMR, target units = (FC + target profit) ÷ CM. When a price change is described, first compute the new contribution margin; do not recompute total revenue and total cost from scratch. For multi‑product mixes, calculate the weighted average contribution margin once and reuse it. If the high‑low method is needed, write the two equations side by side to avoid arithmetic slips. Always check whether the question asks for units or revenue, and convert using the selling price only at the final step.

Tiered Volume Discounts and the Effective Discount Rate

Volume discounts are offered in tiers: a lower discount rate applies to the first bracket of quantity, a higher rate to the next bracket, and so on. The effective discount rate for the whole order is not the simple average of the tier rates; it is the weighted average based on the proportion of units falling in each tier. Suppose a supplier offers 5 % discount on the first 100 units, 10 % on the next 200 units, and 15 % on units above 300. For an order of 500 units at a list price of ₹1,000 per unit, the revenue = (100 × 1,000 × 0.95) + (200 × 1,000 × 0.90) + (200 × 1,000 × 0.85) = ₹95,000 + ₹1,80,000 + ₹1,70,000 = ₹4,45,000. The undiscounted value would be 500 × 1,000 = ₹5,00,000. The effective discount = (5,00,000 – 4,45,000) ÷ 5,00,000 × 100 = 11 %. This single figure can be compared directly with a flat discount offer of, say, 12 % to decide which is more favourable. In many exam problems, the list price is not given; instead, the candidate is told that a buyer paid a certain net amount after tiered discounts and must find the list price or the effective discount percentage. The algebraic approach is to let the list price be L, write the revenue expression as a piecewise linear function of L, equate it to the given net amount, and solve for L. When the number of tiers is large, a shortcut is to compute the average discount factor = Σ (quantity in tier × (1 – discount rate)) ÷ total quantity. The effective discount = 1 – average discount factor. Another common variant mixes a trade discount (applied on the list price before any volume discount) with the tiered volume discount. The trade discount reduces the base price for all tiers, so the effective discount on the original list price becomes 1 – (1 – trade discount) × (1 – effective volume discount). Candidates must be careful to apply the trade discount first, then the volume tiers on the reduced price. A further twist introduces a “cumulative” volume discount where the discount rate for a tier applies to all units once the cumulative quantity crosses the threshold, rather than only to the incremental units. In that case, the effective discount is simply the highest tier rate reached, because all units enjoy that rate. Distinguishing between incremental (non‑cumulative) and cumulative structures is essential; the wording “on the first 100 units, on the next 200 units” signals incremental, while “once the order exceeds 300 units, a 15 % discount applies to the entire order” signals cumulative. Practising both patterns with varying bracket sizes builds the intuition needed to set up the correct revenue expression under time pressure.

Consignment Sales, Commission Structures, and Profit Allocation

Consignment arrangements separate ownership from possession: the consignor (principal) retains title to the goods until they are sold by the consignee (agent). The consignee earns a commission, which may be a straight percentage of the gross selling price, a del‑credere commission (extra percentage for guaranteeing collection from credit customers), or a tiered commission that increases after a sales target is met. The consignor’s profit is calculated on the cost price of goods actually sold, not on goods merely shipped. Unsold inventory remains on the consignor’s books at cost (or at lower of cost and net realizable value). When the consignee remits the net proceeds after deducting commission and any allowable expenses (freight, insurance, godown rent), the consignor records revenue equal to the gross selling price, commission expense, and any reimbursed expenses. The profit on consignment sales = (gross sales – cost of goods sold – commission – reimbursed expenses). If the consignee also bears bad debts (under del‑credere), the consignor’s profit is unaffected by those losses; the consignee absorbs them in exchange for the higher commission. Examination questions often present a consignment account with opening stock, goods sent, expenses incurred by both parties, sales (cash and credit), closing stock, and commission rates. The candidate must prepare the consignment account, the consignee’s account, and compute the profit or loss. A typical twist is to give the consignee’s remittance amount and ask for the gross sales figure. The relationship is: remittance = gross sales – commission – consignee’s expenses + advance (if any). Rearranging yields gross sales = (remittance + commission + expenses – advance) ÷ (1 – commission rate) when commission is a percentage of gross sales. Another twist involves “invoice price” consignments where goods are invoiced at a price higher than cost (to hide the true cost from the consignee). The consignor must adjust the consignment account by creating a “stock reserve” for the unrealized profit on unsold inventory: stock reserve = (invoice price – cost price) × unsold units. This reserve is deducted from the consignment profit to arrive at the true profit. When the consignee sells at a discount on the invoice price, the gross sales figure used for commission calculation is the actual selling price, not the invoice price. The candidate must track three price levels: cost, invoice, and selling. Mastery of these adjustments ensures accurate profit determination in consignment scenarios, which are a staple of advanced commercial arithmetic papers.

Dynamic Pricing, Markdown Optimization, and Revenue Management

Dynamic pricing adjusts selling prices in response to real‑time demand signals, competitor actions, and inventory levels. In a retail context, markdown optimization decides when and by how much to reduce the price of seasonal or perishable stock to maximize total revenue before the selling season ends. The basic trade‑off: a deeper markdown increases the sales rate (units sold per period) but reduces the contribution margin per unit. The optimal markdown depth equates the marginal revenue from an additional unit sold at the lower price to the marginal cost (which is often just the variable cost, since fixed costs are sunk). If demand follows a linear price‑response function, quantity sold = a – b × price, where a and b are positive constants estimated from historical data. Revenue = price × (a – b × price) = a·price – b·price². Maximizing revenue with respect to price gives price* = a ÷ (2b). The corresponding maximum revenue = a² ÷ (4b). When a cost per unit c is introduced, profit = (price – c) × (a – b × price). The profit‑maximizing price = (a + b·c) ÷ (2b). In exam problems, the demand function is rarely given explicitly; instead, candidates receive a table of price‑quantity pairs and must infer the linear relationship. Two points (p₁, q₁) and (p₂, q₂) determine the slope b = (q₂ – q₁) ÷ (p₁ – p₂) and intercept a = q₁ + b·p₁. Once a and b are known, the optimal price formulas above can be applied. A common variation adds a constraint: the price cannot fall below a minimum (e.g., cost plus a mandated margin) or cannot exceed a maximum (e.g., MRP). The constrained optimum is then the bound if the unconstrained optimum lies outside the feasible interval. Another scenario involves multiple products competing for the same shelf space; the retailer must allocate markdown budgets across SKUs to maximize total profit. This becomes a knapsack‑type allocation where each SKU’s marginal profit per rupee of markdown is computed and the budget is spent on the highest marginal returns first. While full optimization is beyond the scope of a timed test, the examiner may ask for the marginal profit of a single SKU’s next markdown step, which is simply (new price – cost) × (new quantity – old quantity) – (old price – cost) × (old quantity – new quantity). Understanding the calculus‑free marginal reasoning allows quick answers. Finally, revenue management in services (hotels, airlines) uses similar logic but with capacity constraints and time‑based fare classes. The principle of “protecting high‑yield demand” translates to setting booking limits for low‑fare classes so that expected marginal revenue from a low‑fare booking does not exceed the expected marginal revenue from a potential high‑fare booking. In quantitative aptitude, this appears as a problem where a hotel has 100 rooms, two fare classes (₹5,000 and ₹8,000), and demand distributions; the candidate must compute the optimal number of rooms to reserve for the high fare. The solution uses the critical fractile: reserve rooms until the probability that high‑fare demand exceeds the reservation level falls below (high fare – low fare) ÷ high fare. Practising these concepts with simple discrete demand tables builds the intuition needed for the occasional advanced revenue‑management question.

  • Confusing flat‑rate interest with reducing‑balance interest in installment problems, leading to an understated effective rate.
  • Using the simple average of tiered discount percentages instead of the quantity‑weighted average to compute the effective discount.
  • Forgetting to adjust the consignment profit for the stock reserve when goods are invoiced above cost.
  • Applying the break‑even formula with total cost instead of fixed cost only, inflating the break‑even quantity.
  • Treating a cumulative volume discount as incremental (or vice‑versa) and mis‑calculating the revenue.
  • Neglecting the del‑credere commission’s effect on bad‑debt risk allocation in consignment accounts.
  • Assuming the unconstrained revenue‑maximizing price is always feasible without checking minimum‑price or capacity constraints.

These advanced topics extend the core profit‑loss framework into realistic commercial decision‑making and are frequently tested in higher‑difficulty quantitative sections.

Profit and Loss in Multi‑Stage Supply Chains with Transfer Pricing

When a product passes through several related entities — manufacturer, distributor, wholesaler, retailer — each stage may set a transfer price that differs from the open‑market price. The transfer price becomes the cost base for the next entity and the revenue base for the previous one. In quantitative aptitude problems the examiner usually supplies the transfer price as a percentage of the preceding stage’s cost or selling price, and asks for the overall profit or loss percentage of the final seller relative to the original manufacturer’s cost. The key is to treat each stage as an independent profit‑loss transaction and then chain the results multiplicatively. If the manufacturer’s cost is C, the first transfer price is C × (1 + p₁/100), the second is that amount × (1 + p₂/100), and so on. The final selling price S = C × ∏(1 + pᵢ/100). The overall profit percentage = (S − C)/C × 100 = [∏(1 + pᵢ/100) − 1] × 100. A common twist is to insert a stage that sells at a loss (negative pᵢ) or to give the final selling price and ask for an unknown intermediate transfer‑price percentage. In such cases, work backwards using division by the known factors. Another variation introduces a tax or duty levied on the transfer price; the tax amount is added to the cost base of the next stage but does not generate profit for the transferring entity. Always separate the tax component before applying the profit‑loss factor.

Profit and Loss in E‑Commerce Marketplace Commission and Fee Structures

Online marketplaces charge sellers a combination of referral fees (percentage of the selling price), fixed per‑order fees, payment‑gateway charges, and sometimes a subscription fee. The seller’s net revenue = selling price − (referral % × selling price) − fixed fee − gateway % × (selling price − referral fee). The cost price remains the purchase or manufacturing cost. Profit percentage is then calculated on the cost price using the net revenue as the effective selling price. Examiners often hide the gateway charge inside the “payment processing fee” line and ask for the effective commission rate. To find the effective rate, compute total deductions ÷ selling price × 100. A frequent trap is to apply the referral percentage on the price after the fixed fee has been subtracted; the correct order is referral on the full selling price, then fixed fee, then gateway on the remainder. When a seller offers a discount coupon funded by the marketplace, the coupon value reduces the selling price before commission is calculated, but the seller still receives the full pre‑coupon amount minus commissions. In such cases, treat the coupon as a reduction in the selling price for commission purposes only. Problems may also involve tiered commission slabs (e.g., 5 % up to ₹10,000, 8 % above). Compute the commission piecewise and sum the parts. Finally, subscription fees are period costs; allocate them proportionally to the number of orders in the period if the question asks for per‑order profit.

Technique: Piecewise Commission with Fixed and Gateway Fees
A seller lists an item at ₹2,500. The marketplace charges a referral fee of 10 % on the selling price, a fixed fee of ₹30 per order, and a payment‑gateway fee of 2 % on the amount after referral fee. The seller’s cost price is ₹1,600. Find the profit percentage on cost.
Referral fee = 10 % × 2,500 = ₹250. Amount after referral = 2,500 − 250 = ₹2,250. Gateway fee = 2 % × 2,250 = ₹45. Total deductions = 250 + 30 + 45 = ₹325. Net revenue = 2,500 − 325 = ₹2,175. Profit = 2,175 − 1,600 = ₹575. Profit % = (575 ÷ 1,600) × 100 = 35.9375 % ≈ 35.94 %.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem lists multiple fees, write them in the order they are applied: percentage on selling price → fixed fee → percentage on reduced amount. Compute each step in a column; this prevents the common error of applying the gateway fee on the original selling price. If a subscription fee appears, divide it by the number of orders given in the same period before adding to per‑order deductions.

Profit and Loss with Cash Discount, Trade Discount, and Quantity Discount Interactions

Three distinct discount types often appear together: trade discount (offered on the catalogue price to all buyers), quantity discount (additional reduction when order volume crosses a threshold), and cash discount (early‑payment incentive). The catalogue price is the starting marked price. Trade discount is applied first, yielding the invoice price. Quantity discount, if applicable, is applied on the invoice price. Cash discount is applied on the net amount after the previous two discounts, provided payment is made within the stipulated period. The effective selling price = catalogue price × (1 − t/100) × (1 − q/100) × (1 − c/100). Profit or loss is then measured against the cost price. A typical examiner twist is to state that the buyer availed the cash discount but the seller had already accounted for a lower quantity discount slab; the candidate must recompute the quantity discount based on the actual ordered quantity. Another variation gives the final amount received by the seller and asks for the catalogue price; work backwards by dividing sequentially by (1 − c/100), (1 − q/100), (1 − t/100). When discounts are expressed as “5 % trade discount, 2 % quantity discount, 1 % cash discount”, the single equivalent discount is not 8 % but 1 − (0.95 × 0.98 × 0.99) = 7.73 %. Always compute the product of the complements.

Profit and Loss in Leasing Agreements with Residual Value and Money Factor

Leasing problems appear as a variant of installment transactions. The lessor purchases an asset at cost C and leases it for n periods with a periodic lease rental R. At the end of the term the lessee may return the asset or purchase it at a predetermined residual value V. The lessor’s implicit interest rate (money factor) is derived from the present value equation: C = R × [1 − (1 + i)⁻ⁿ]/i + V × (1 + i)⁻ⁿ. In aptitude tests the money factor is often given directly as a decimal (e.g., 0.0025 per month) and the candidate must compute the lease rental: R = (C − V × (1 + i)⁻ⁿ) ÷ [1 − (1 + i)⁻ⁿ]/i. Profit for the lessor over the lease term = n × R + V − C (if the asset is sold at residual). If the lessee defaults and the lessor recovers the asset, the loss is the shortfall of received rentals plus the market value of the recovered asset minus C. A common trap is to treat the residual value as a profit component without discounting it to present value; the correct profit comparison uses the same time‑value basis for all cash flows. When the problem asks for the effective annual interest rate from a monthly money factor, use (1 + i)¹² − 1. Conversely, if the annual percentage rate (APR) is given, convert to monthly i = (1 + APR)¹ᐟ¹² − 1 before applying the lease formula.

Profit and Loss in Subscription‑Based Revenue Models with Churn and Acquisition Cost

Modern business models sell recurring subscriptions. The seller incurs a customer acquisition cost (CAC) upfront and receives a monthly recurring revenue (MRR) per subscriber. Subscribers churn at a monthly rate χ (expressed as a decimal). The expected lifetime of a subscriber = 1/χ months. Lifetime value (LTV) = MRR × (1/χ). Profit per acquired customer = LTV − CAC. In exam questions, the data may be given as “CAC = ₹500, MRR = ₹200, monthly churn = 5 %”. Then LTV = 200 ÷ 0.05 = ₹4,000, profit = 4,000 − 500 = ₹3,500. A twist is to introduce a discount rate r for the time value of money; the present value of the perpetuity with churn becomes MRR ÷ (χ + r). Another variation asks for the maximum CAC that keeps the business profitable given a target payback period of k months: CAC ≤ MRR × k × (1 − χ)ᵏ (approximation). When multiple plans exist (basic, premium), compute weighted average MRR and churn using the subscriber mix. The examiner may also combine a one‑time setup fee with the subscription; treat the setup fee as immediate revenue reducing CAC. Always verify whether churn is applied on the beginning‑of‑month base or end‑of‑month; the standard formula assumes beginning‑of‑month churn.

  • Applying a single equivalent discount by adding percentages instead of multiplying complements.
  • Calculating marketplace gateway fee on the original selling price rather than on the post‑referral amount.
  • Ignoring the present‑value discounting of residual value in lease profit calculations.
  • Treating churn as a simple subtraction from MRR without converting to expected lifetime.
  • Mixing up the order of trade, quantity, and cash discounts when working backwards from net receipt.
  • Forgetting to allocate subscription fees proportionally across orders in a given period.
  • Using the annual percentage rate directly in monthly lease formulas without converting to a monthly rate.
