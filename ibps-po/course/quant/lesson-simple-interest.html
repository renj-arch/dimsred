Foundations of Interest

1.1 Definition of Interest and Principal

Interest is the compensation paid by a borrower to a lender for the use of money over a specified period, and the principal is the original sum of money on which that compensation is calculated. In every financial transaction the principal remains the reference point, while the interest accrues according to the agreed rate and the length of time the money is employed. Understanding this relationship is essential because all subsequent formulas for simple and compound interest are built on the premise that the principal either stays unchanged or grows as interest is added.

1.2 Simple Interest Concept

Simple interest assumes that the interest earned in each period is calculated solely on the original principal, regardless of any interest that has already been paid or accrued. Consequently the interest amount is the same for every year, half‑year, or quarter, and the total interest grows linearly with time. This linearity makes simple interest easy to compute and predictable, which is why it is often used for short‑term loans, certain government securities, and educational problems that test basic arithmetic.

1.3 Compound Interest Concept

Compound interest, by contrast, adds the interest earned in each period to the principal so that the next period’s interest is calculated on the increased amount. This process creates exponential growth because the base on which interest is computed expands continuously. The frequency with which interest is added — annually, half‑yearly, quarterly, monthly, or even daily — determines how quickly the amount grows, and the effective annual rate rises as the compounding frequency increases.

Simple Interest Calculations

2.1 Formula Derivation

The simple interest formula SI = (P × R × T) / 100 follows directly from the definition: the interest for one year is P × R / 100, and for T years it is multiplied by T because the principal does not change. The amount A after T years is simply the sum of principal and interest, A = P + SI. This derivation shows why the formula is linear in both rate and time, and why any change in rate or time produces a proportional change in interest.

2.2 Amount and Interest Relationship

Because the amount is the principal plus the simple interest, any problem that gives the amount and asks for the principal, rate, or time can be solved by rearranging the basic equation. For instance, if the amount after 3 years at 8 % per annum is known, the principal can be isolated as P = A / (1 + R T/100). This algebraic flexibility is a key skill tested in examinations, where candidates must decide which variable is unknown and manipulate the formula accordingly.

2.3 Applications with Varying Time Periods

When the time period is not a whole number of years, the formula still applies by expressing T in years, using fractions for months or days. For example, 6 months becomes 0.5 years, and 90 days becomes 90/365 years (or 90/360 in banking conventions). The linear nature of simple interest ensures that the interest for a partial period is exactly the same fraction of the annual interest, which simplifies calculations for loans that start or end mid‑year.

Example: Simple Interest on a Short‑Term Loan
A sum of ₹12 000 is lent at 9 % per annum simple interest for 8 months. Find the interest earned and the total amount due at the end of the period.
Solution: Convert 8 months to years: T = 8/12 = 2/3 year. SI = (12000 × 9 × 2/3) / 100 = (12000 × 9 × 2) / 300 = 720. Amount = 12000 + 720 = ₹12 720.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem states “per annum” but gives a period in months or days, always convert the period to years before substituting into the simple interest formula; forgetting this conversion is the most common source of avoidable errors.

Compound Interest Calculations

3.1 Annual Compounding Formula

With annual compounding the amount after T years is A = P (1 + R/100)^T, and the compound interest is CI = A – P. The exponent T reflects the number of times the interest is added to the principal. Because the base (1 + R/100) is greater than one, the amount grows faster than in simple interest, and the difference widens as T increases. This formula is the cornerstone for all compound interest problems and must be memorised in its exact form.

3.2 Half‑Yearly and Quarterly Compounding

When interest is compounded half‑yearly, the annual rate R is halved to R/2 and the number of periods doubles to 2T, giving A = P (1 + R/200)^(2T). For quarterly compounding the rate becomes R/4 and periods become 4T, so A = P (1 + R/400)^(4T). The general rule is to divide the annual rate by the number of compounding periods per year and multiply the number of years by the same factor. This adjustment ensures that the effective rate per period matches the nominal annual rate.

3.3 Effective Annual Rate

The effective annual rate (EAR) is the single annual rate that would produce the same final amount as the given nominal rate with its compounding frequency. It is calculated as EAR = (1 + R/(100 × n))^n – 1, where n is the number of compounding periods per year. Knowing the EAR allows direct comparison between different compounding schemes, a frequent requirement in data‑interpretation questions where two investment options are presented with different nominal rates and compounding intervals.

Comparison Between Simple and Compound Interest

4.1 Difference for Two Years

For a principal P at rate R % per annum, the difference between compound interest and simple interest over two years is CI – SI = P (R/100)2. This result arises because the extra interest in the second year under compounding is earned on the first year’s interest, which equals P × R/100. The formula is independent of the principal’s magnitude beyond the factor P, making it a powerful shortcut for problems that give the difference and ask for the principal or the rate.

4.2 Difference for Three Years

Extending the logic to three years yields CI – SI = P (R/100)2 (300 + R)/100. The additional term (300 + R)/100 accounts for the interest on interest accumulated over the second and third years. Although the expression looks more involved, it follows the same principle: each year’s interest becomes part of the principal for subsequent years, and the cumulative effect can be captured algebraically.

4.3 Practical Implications

In real‑world borrowing, simple interest is rarely used for long‑term loans because it understates the cost of credit compared with compound interest. Financial institutions therefore quote annual percentage rates that reflect compounding, and regulators often require the disclosure of the effective annual rate. For examination purposes, recognising whether a problem describes simple or compound interest — and whether the compounding is annual, half‑yearly, or quarterly — determines which formula to apply and whether the difference formulas are relevant.

Installment Problems

5.1 Present Value Approach

An installment problem asks for the equal periodic payment that will repay a loan with interest over a given number of periods. The present value of each installment, discounted at the periodic interest rate, must sum to the principal borrowed. This principle leads to the formula PV = X [1 – (1 + r)-n] / r, where X is the installment amount, r is the periodic rate, and n is the number of installments. Understanding present value is crucial because it unifies the treatment of loans, annuities, and sinking funds.

5.2 Equal Annual Installments

When installments are annual and the interest is compounded annually, the periodic rate r equals the annual rate R/100. Solving the present value equation for X gives X = P r / [1 – (1 + r)-n]. This formula is frequently tested in a disguised form, for example by providing the installment amount and asking for the principal, or by giving the principal and asking for the number of years required to repay.

5.3 Installments with Different Compounding Frequencies

If the loan compounds half‑yearly but installments are paid annually, the effective annual rate must first be computed, then used as r in the annual installment formula. Conversely, if installments are half‑yearly while the nominal rate is annual, the periodic rate is R/200 and the number of periods is 2n. The key is always to align the compounding period with the payment period before applying the present value formula.

Rate and Time Determination

6.1 Finding Rate from Interest Difference

When the difference between compound and simple interest for two years is given, the rate can be isolated using R = 100 √[(CI – SI) / P]. This follows directly from the two‑year difference formula CI – SI = P (R/100)2. Taking the square root yields R/100, and multiplying by 100 gives the annual rate in percent. The same approach works for three years, though the algebra involves solving a quadratic in R.

6.2 Finding Time from Amount

If the principal, rate, and final amount are known, the time T can be found by solving A = P (1 + R/100)^T for T. Taking logarithms gives T = log(A/P) / log(1 + R/100). In examinations where logarithms are not expected, the problem usually provides numbers that make T an integer, allowing trial of successive powers of (1 + R/100) until the amount matches.

6.3 Mixed Problems

Many questions combine simple and compound interest in a single scenario, for example a loan that charges simple interest for the first year and compound interest thereafter. The solution requires splitting the time line, applying the appropriate formula to each segment, and carrying forward the amount as the new principal for the next segment. Careful reading and a step‑by‑step layout prevent the common mistake of applying a single formula to the whole period.

Advanced Problem‑Solving Strategies

7.1 Using Fractional Equivalents

Converting common percentages to fractions speeds up mental calculations: 5 % = 1/20, 10 % = 1/10, 12.5 % = 1/8, 20 % = 1/5, 25 % = 1/4. When the rate is a simple fraction, the compound factor (1 + R/100) becomes a rational number such as 21/20 for 5 % or 11/10 for 10 %, and powers of these fractions can be evaluated quickly by hand. This technique is especially valuable in time‑pressured examinations where calculators are not permitted.

7.2 Approximation Techniques

For large exponents, the binomial approximation (1 + x)^n ≈ 1 + n x for small x can give a quick estimate of the amount, which is sufficient for multiple‑choice questions that have widely spaced options. However, the approximation must be used with caution because the error grows with n and x; it is safest when the rate is below 5 % and the number of periods is modest.

7.3 Avoiding Common Errors

Typical errors include forgetting to adjust the rate and time for half‑yearly or quarterly compounding, confusing the nominal rate with the effective rate, and applying the simple interest formula when the problem explicitly states compound interest. A systematic checklist — identify principal, rate, time, compounding frequency, and whether interest is simple or compound — eliminates most of these mistakes. Practising this checklist on a variety of problems builds the habit of correct interpretation under exam conditions.

Mastering these concepts and techniques equips a candidate to solve any simple interest, compound interest, or installment question that appears in the quantitative aptitude section.

Variable Rate Loans and Piecewise Compounding

Many loans do not carry a single fixed rate for the entire tenure. A common structure is a teaser rate for the first few years followed by a higher floating rate linked to a benchmark such as the repo rate or MCLR. To evaluate the true cost, the loan timeline must be split into segments, each with its own rate and compounding frequency. The amount at the end of the first segment becomes the principal for the second segment, and the process repeats until maturity. This piecewise approach also applies when a borrower negotiates a rate revision mid‑term or when a regulatory change forces a reset. The key is to treat each sub‑period as an independent compound interest problem and chain the results sequentially.

When the rate changes annually but compounding remains quarterly, the periodic rate for each year is the annual nominal rate divided by four. The number of quarters in each year is four, so the factor for year k is (1 + Rₖ/400)⁴. Multiplying the factors for all years yields the overall growth factor. If the compounding frequency also changes — for example, monthly in the first two years and half‑yearly thereafter — the periodic rate and period count must be adjusted for each block before multiplication. A systematic table with columns for segment, nominal rate, compounding frequency, periodic rate, number of periods, and growth factor prevents omission errors.

Piecewise compounding also appears in “step‑up” or “step‑down” deposit schemes where the bank revises the interest rate at predefined intervals. The same chaining principle works, but the direction of cash flow is reversed: each deposit earns the rate applicable for the remaining sub‑periods. When deposits are made at irregular intervals, the present‑value method is preferable: discount each cash flow at the rate prevailing for its specific period, using the appropriate periodic discount factor. This avoids the need to compute a single equivalent rate, which may not exist in closed form.

Examiners often disguise variable‑rate questions by giving the final amount after several years and asking for the initial principal, or by providing the principal and final amount and asking for the unknown rate in one segment while the others are known. Solving such problems requires setting up the product of growth factors equal to the known ratio A/P and isolating the unknown. Because the equation is usually polynomial in the unknown rate, the test data are chosen so that the rate is a simple fraction (e.g., 5 %, 8 %, 10 %) allowing trial‑and‑error or factorisation rather than a full algebraic solution.

A practical shortcut is to convert each annual rate to its effective annual rate (EAR) first, then multiply the EARs. For a nominal rate R compounded m times a year, EAR = (1 + R/(100 m))^m − 1. The overall growth factor over n years with rates R₁, R₂, …, Rₙ is ∏(1 + EARₖ). This reduces the number of exponentiations and aligns the calculation with the way banks quote “effective” yields. Remember that the EAR is always higher than the nominal rate when m > 1, so ignoring the compounding frequency understates the true cost.

Annuity Due versus Ordinary Annuity in Installment Calculations

Standard installment formulas assume payments are made at the end of each period — an ordinary annuity. In many loan agreements, however, the first installment is due immediately at disbursement, making the stream an annuity due. The present value of an annuity due is simply the ordinary annuity present value multiplied by (1 + r), where r is the periodic rate. Consequently, the installment amount X for a given principal P is lower under an annuity due because each payment is made one period earlier (one less period of discounting). The formula becomes X = P r / [(1 − (1 + r)^(−n)) (1 + r)].

Conversely, if the loan stipulates a “moratorium” or grace period during which no payments are made but interest accrues, the effective number of payment periods shrinks while the principal grows. The amount at the end of the moratorium, P (1 + r)^g (g = number of grace periods), becomes the new principal for the annuity calculation. This two‑step process — accumulate during grace, then amortise — is a frequent twist in competitive exams. Candidates must recognise that the grace period does not change the periodic rate; it only changes the principal on which the annuity formula operates.

When installments are not level but increase at a constant rate g (a growing annuity), the present value formula changes to PV = X [1 − ((1 + g)/(1 + r))^n] / (r − g), provided r ≠ g. This appears in “step‑up EMI” products where the borrower expects income growth. The examiner may give the first installment, the growth rate, the interest rate, and the number of installments, asking for the loan amount. The same formula works in reverse to find the first installment when the loan amount is known. Care must be taken to express both r and g as periodic decimals, not percentages.

Another variation is the “balloon payment” where a large final payment settles the remaining balance after a series of smaller equal installments. The present value of the balloon payment discounted at the periodic rate is added to the present value of the ordinary annuity of the smaller installments. The equation P = X [1 − (1 + r)^(−n)]/r + B (1 + r)^(−n) (B = balloon amount) can be solved for any unknown. Because the balloon payment is usually a round number, the resulting equation often yields a neat integer for X.

Finally, when the compounding frequency differs from the payment frequency — for instance, monthly compounding with quarterly installments — the effective rate per payment period must be derived. If the nominal annual rate is R compounded monthly, the monthly rate is R/1200. The effective quarterly rate is (1 + R/1200)³ − 1. This effective quarterly rate then serves as r in the annuity formulas. Ignoring this conversion and using R/400 directly is a classic error that leads to an understated installment.

Worked Example: Annuity Due with Grace Period
A loan of ₹10,00,000 is sanctioned at 12 % per annum compounded monthly. The borrower enjoys a 6‑month moratorium (no payments, interest accrues). Thereafter, 36 equal monthly installments are paid at the beginning of each month (annuity due). Find the monthly installment amount.

Step 1: Monthly rate r = 12/1200 = 0.01.

Step 2: Amount after 6‑month grace = P (1 + r)⁶ = 10,00,000 × (1.01)⁶ ≈ 10,00,000 × 1.06152 = ₹10,61,520.

Step 3: This amount becomes the principal for an annuity due of 36 months. Present value of annuity due factor = (1 − (1 + r)^(−36))/r × (1 + r). Compute (1.01)^(−36) ≈ 0.6989. Factor = (1 − 0.6989)/0.01 × 1.01 = 0.3011/0.01 × 1.01 = 30.11 × 1.01 ≈ 30.41.

Step 4: Installment X = Principal / Factor = 10,61,520 / 30.41 ≈ ₹34,900 (rounded to nearest rupee).

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mentions “first installment paid immediately” or “installments at the beginning of each period”, instantly flag it as an annuity due and multiply the ordinary annuity factor by (1 + r). If a moratorium or grace period is described, first accumulate the principal for that period using the compound factor, then apply the annuity formula. Write the two steps side‑by‑side on your rough sheet; this visual separation prevents mixing the grace‑period growth with the annuity discounting.

Continuous Compounding and the Limit of Discrete Periods

Continuous compounding represents the theoretical limit when the number of compounding periods per year tends to infinity. The amount after time T years at an annual nominal rate R (expressed as a decimal r = R/100) is A = P e^(rT), where e ≈ 2.71828 is the base of natural logarithms. This formula emerges from the limit of (1 + r/m)^(mT) as m → ∞. Although most banking products use discrete compounding, continuous compounding appears in advanced finance questions, especially those involving option pricing models or the valuation of perpetuities with continuously paid coupons.

The effective annual rate under continuous compounding is EAR = e^r − 1. For small r, the approximation e^r ≈ 1 + r + r²/2 shows that continuous compounding yields a slightly higher EAR than daily compounding. In exam problems, you may be asked to compare the final amount under annual, quarterly, monthly, daily, and continuous compounding for the same nominal rate. The ranking is always: continuous > daily > monthly > quarterly > annual. Knowing this ordering lets you eliminate options without full calculation when the question asks “which compounding frequency gives the highest amount?”.

When installments are paid continuously — a “continuous annuity” — the present value of a constant payment stream of C per year for n years at continuous rate r is PV = C (1 − e^(−rn))/r. This is the continuous analogue of the ordinary annuity formula. If the payment stream grows at a continuous rate g, the present value becomes PV = C (1 − e^(-(r−g)n))/(r − g), provided r > g. These formulas are rarely required in full but may appear in a conceptual multiple‑choice question where you must identify the correct expression among distractors.

A practical use of continuous compounding in exams is the “force of interest” concept. The force of interest δ(t) is defined as the instantaneous rate of growth of the accumulation factor. For a constant force δ, the accumulation factor over T years is e^(δT). If the force varies with time, the accumulation factor is exp(∫₀^T δ(s) ds). Problems may give a piecewise constant force (e.g., δ = 0.05 for first 3 years, δ = 0.07 thereafter) and ask for the accumulated value of a single deposit. The solution is simply the product of e^(δ₁T₁) × e^(δ₂T₂) … which reduces to e^(ΣδᵢTᵢ). This additive property of the exponent makes continuous compounding algebraically simpler than discrete piecewise compounding.

Finally, the relationship between nominal rate R (percent per annum) compounded m times a year and the equivalent continuous rate δ is given by δ = m ln(1 + R/(100 m)). Conversely, the nominal rate equivalent to a continuous rate δ with m compounding periods is R = 100 m (e^(δ/m) − 1). These conversion formulas are useful when a question mixes continuous and discrete compounding in the same scenario, for example a loan that charges continuous interest but the borrower repays via monthly installments. Align the rates by converting one to the other’s basis before applying annuity formulas.

Loan Amortization Schedules and Early Repayment Penalties

An amortization schedule breaks each installment into interest and principal components, showing the outstanding balance after every payment. For a standard loan with principal P, periodic rate r, and n equal installments X, the interest portion of the k‑th payment is Iₖ = Outstandingₖ₋₁ × r, and the principal repaid is Pₖ = X − Iₖ. The outstanding balance after k payments is Outstandingₖ = Outstandingₖ₋₁ − Pₖ. This recursive definition can be expressed in closed form: Outstandingₖ = P (1 + r)^k − X [(1 + r)^k − 1]/r. Understanding this formula allows you to compute the balance after any number of payments without constructing the full table.

Early repayment (prepayment) penalties are often expressed as a percentage of the outstanding principal at the time of prepayment, or as a fixed number of months’ interest. To evaluate the true cost of prepaying after k installments, calculate the outstanding balance using the closed‑form expression, add the penalty, and compare the total cash outflow with the present value of the remaining installments discounted at the original rate. If the penalty exceeds the interest saved, prepayment is not beneficial. Exam questions may ask for the “break‑even” month where prepayment becomes advantageous, requiring you to solve an inequality involving the penalty function.

Some loans feature “partial prepayment” where the borrower pays a lump sum in addition to the regular installment, reducing the principal but keeping the installment amount unchanged. The new schedule shortens the loan tenure. The revised number of remaining installments n′ can be found by solving Outstandingₖ − LumpSum = X [1 − (1 + r)^(−n′)]/r for n′. Because n′ must be an integer, the final installment may be slightly lower than X. This scenario tests the ability to manipulate the present‑value annuity formula algebraically and to handle the integer‑rounding nuance.

Another twist is a “step‑down EMI” where the installment amount reduces after a certain number of payments, usually because the borrower’s income drops. The loan is effectively two annuities back‑to‑back: the first with installment X₁ for n₁ periods, the second with X₂ for n₂ periods. The present value of the whole stream must equal the original principal. Given three of the four variables (X₁, X₂, n₁, n₂), the fourth can be solved. The key is to discount the second annuity back to the start of the loan by multiplying its present value at the switch date by (1 + r)^(−n₁).

Finally, “negative amortization” occurs when the installment is set below the interest due, causing the outstanding balance to increase. This is common in certain mortgage products with initial “teaser” payments.

Interest Rate Conversion Across Compounding Frequencies

Financial products rarely share a common compounding basis. A loan may quote a nominal annual rate compounded monthly while a competing deposit offers the same nominal rate compounded quarterly. The effective annual rate (EAR) provides a common denominator, but questions often require conversion in the opposite direction: given an effective rate, find the nominal rate for a specified compounding frequency. The relationship is EAR = (1 + rm/m)m − 1, where rm is the nominal rate per annum compounded m times a year. Solving for rm yields rm = m[(1 + EAR)1/m − 1]. When the effective rate is expressed as a decimal i, the formula becomes rm = m[(1 + i)1/m − 1]. This conversion is essential for comparing a 9 % p.a. monthly‑compounded loan (EAR ≈ 9.38 %) with a 9.25 % p.a. quarterly‑compounded deposit (EAR ≈ 9.55 %). The higher EAR reveals the deposit is more attractive despite the lower nominal quote.

Questions also test the reverse: given a nominal rate for one frequency, find the equivalent nominal rate for another frequency that yields the same EAR. Set the two EAR expressions equal and solve for the unknown nominal rate. For example, a 12 % p.a. rate compounded semi‑annually has EAR = (1 + 0.12/2)2 − 1 = 0.1236. The equivalent monthly nominal rate r12 satisfies (1 + r12/12)12 = 1.1236, giving r12 ≈ 11.71 %. Mastery of this algebra avoids the common pitfall of simply dividing or multiplying the nominal rate by the ratio of periods.

A practical shortcut uses the natural logarithm for continuous‑compounding equivalence: δ = ln(1 + EAR). The nominal rate for any discrete frequency m that matches a continuous rate δ is rm = m(eδ/m − 1). This link appears in questions that mix continuous and discrete compounding, such as a loan charging a force of interest δ = 0.08 while repayments are monthly. Convert δ to a monthly nominal rate first, then apply the ordinary annuity formula. Always keep at least six decimal places during intermediate steps; rounding too early can shift the final answer by several basis points, enough to change the option selected.

Present Value of Growing Annuities and Perpetuities

Standard annuity formulas assume level payments. Many real‑world cash‑flow streams — salary‑linked loan repayments, rental escalations, dividend growth — increase at a constant rate g per period. The present value of a growing ordinary annuity with first payment C, growth rate g, discount rate r (r ≠ g), and n periods is PV = C × [1 − ((1 + g)/(1 + r))n] / (r − g). If payments are made at the beginning of each period (annuity due), multiply the ordinary‑annuity PV by (1 + r). When r = g the denominator vanishes; the formula simplifies to PV = n × C / (1 + r) because each payment’s present value equals C/(1+r).

A growing perpetuity (n → ∞) converges only when r > g. Its present value is PV = C / (r − g). This is the Gordon growth model used for equity valuation; it also appears in loan‑structuring questions where a borrower agrees to pay an initial amount that grows annually forever — essentially a consol with escalating coupons. Questions may disguise the perpetuity by stating “the borrower will pay ₹10,000 at the end of year 1, increasing by 5 % each year indefinitely; the applicable interest rate is 12 %”. The answer is 10,000 / (0.12 − 0.05) = ₹142,857.14.

When the growth rate exceeds the discount rate (g > r), the series diverges and no finite present value exists. Questions sometimes ask “which of the following statements is correct?” and include a distractor claiming a finite PV for g > r. Recognise the divergence condition instantly. Another twist: the first payment may occur after a deferral period k. Compute the PV at the start of the payment stream using the growing‑annuity formula, then discount that lump sum back k periods at rate r. The combined expression is PV₀ = C × [1 − ((1 + g)/(1 + r))n] / [(r − g)(1 + r)k]. Practice algebraic manipulation of this formula; it reduces the risk of arithmetic errors under time pressure.

Technique: Growing Annuity with Deferral
A loan requires the first repayment of ₹5,000 at the end of year 3. Subsequent repayments increase by 4 % per year for a total of 10 repayments. The annual effective interest rate is 9 %. Find the present value of the repayment stream at time 0.
Step 1: Identify parameters. C = 5,000, g = 0.04, r = 0.09, n = 10, deferral k = 2 (because first payment at end of year 3 means two full years of deferral before the first payment).
Step 2: Compute PV at the beginning of year 3 (i.e., at t = 2) using the growing‑annuity formula: PV₂ = 5,000 × [1 − ((1.04)/(1.09))10] / (0.09 − 0.04).
Calculate ratio = 1.04/1.09 ≈ 0.954128. Ratio10 ≈ 0.627412.
Numerator = 1 − 0.627412 = 0.372588.
PV₂ = 5,000 × 0.372588 / 0.05 = 5,000 × 7.45176 = ₹37,258.80.
Step 3: Discount PV₂ back two years: PV₀ = 37,258.80 / (1.09)2 = 37,258.80 / 1.1881 ≈ ₹31,363.50.
Answer: ₹31,363.50 (rounded to nearest paisa).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a growing annuity appears, first check whether r > g. If not, the PV is infinite or undefined — eliminate any finite‑value options immediately. Next, note the timing of the first payment; a deferral of k periods means discount the annuity‑value by (1+r)−k. Keep a small table of (1+g)/(1+r) powers for common n (5, 10, 15) to avoid repeated exponentiation. Finally, verify the answer’s magnitude: a growing annuity PV should lie between the level‑annuity PV (using C) and the perpetuity PV (C/(r−g)).

Balloon Payments and Partial Amortization Structures

Not all loans are fully amortised by equal installments. A balloon loan requires regular payments that cover only part of the principal, leaving a large final payment (the balloon) at maturity. The present‑value equation for a loan of principal P, periodic rate r, n regular payments of amount X, and a balloon B paid at the end of period n is P = X × [1 − (1 + r)−n]/r + B × (1 + r)−n. Given any four variables, the fifth can be solved. Questions often ask for the balloon amount when the borrower wishes to keep the regular EMI at a specific affordable level. Rearranging gives B = [P − X × (1 − (1 + r)−n)/r] × (1 + r)n.

Partial amortisation also appears in “interest‑only” periods followed by full amortisation. For the first k periods the borrower pays only interest I = P × r. The outstanding balance remains P. At period k+1 the loan converts to a standard amortising schedule over the remaining n−k periods. The new installment X satisfies P = X × [1 − (1 + r)−(n−k)]/r. This two‑stage structure tests the ability to split the timeline and apply the annuity formula to the second stage only. A common error is to discount the second‑stage annuity back to time 0 using (1+r)−k while forgetting that the principal at the start of stage 2 is still P (not reduced). The correct approach: compute X from the stage‑2 equation, then verify that the present value of the entire cash‑flow stream (interest‑only payments for k periods plus the annuity) equals P.

Another variant is a “step‑up EMI” where the installment increases by a fixed percentage after a certain number of payments. This is a concatenation of two level annuities with different payment amounts. Let the first n₁ payments be X₁ and the next n₂ payments be X₂ = X₁(1 + s). The loan principal equals the present value of the first annuity plus the present value of the second annuity discounted back n₁ periods: P = X₁ × [1 − (1 + r)−n₁]/r + X₂ × [1 − (1 + r)−n₂]/r × (1 + r)−n₁. Solving for X₁ (or s) requires careful algebraic isolation. Always keep the discount factor (1+r)−n₁ explicit to avoid sign errors.

Sinking Fund Method and Capital Recovery Factor

The sinking‑fund approach separates the interest payment from the principal recovery. The borrower pays interest I = P × r each period to the lender and simultaneously deposits an amount S into a sinking fund that earns the same rate r. After n periods the accumulated fund equals the original principal P, which is then used to retire the debt. The sinking‑fund deposit S is the level payment that accumulates to P at rate r over n periods: S = P × r / [(1 + r)n − 1]. The total periodic outlay is X = I + S = P × r + P × r / [(1 + r)n − 1] = P × r × (1 + r)n / [(1 + r)n − 1]. This total outlay is exactly the standard amortising installment derived from the annuity formula, confirming the equivalence of the two methods.

The capital recovery factor (CRF) is the multiplier that converts a present sum into an equivalent uniform annual series: CRF = r(1 + r)n / [(1 + r)n − 1]. It appears in engineering‑economics questions where a machine costing P must be replaced after n years, and the firm wants to set aside equal annual amounts. The annual sinking‑fund deposit is P × CRF. When the sinking‑fund earns a different rate i from the loan rate r, the deposit becomes S = P × i / [(1 + i)n − 1] and the total outlay is P × r + S. This mismatch is a common examiner twist: “A loan of ₹10 lakh at 10 % p.a. is to be repaid by a sinking fund earning 8 % p.a. Find the annual deposit.” Compute interest = 1,00,000; sinking‑fund deposit = 10,00,000 × 0.08 / [(1.08)n − 1]. The sum is the total annual cash outflow.

Questions may also ask for the “sinking‑fund balance” after k periods. The accumulated value of the deposits made so far is SFk = S × [(1 + i)k − 1]/i. The outstanding loan balance (if the loan were amortised) would be P(1+r)k − X[(1+r)k−1]/r. The difference between the loan balance and the sinking‑fund balance represents the net equity (or shortfall) of the borrower. Tracking this difference over time often appears in multi‑part questions.

Tax‑Adjusted Yield and Post‑Tax Effective Rate

Interest income is often taxable, while interest expense may be deductible. The post‑tax yield for an investor receiving nominal rate r compounded m times a year, with marginal tax rate t, is obtained by first computing the effective annual rate EAR = (1 + r/m)m − 1, then applying tax: Post‑tax EAR = (1 + EAR)1−t − 1 only if tax is levied on the effective yield continuously, which is rare. The standard treatment: tax is applied to the interest earned each period. For discrete compounding, the after‑tax periodic rate is rafter = (r/m) × (1 − t). The post‑tax effective annual rate becomes EARafter = (1 + rafter)m − 1. For a 12 % p.a. nominal rate compounded quarterly (m = 4) and a 30 % tax rate, r/4 = 3 %, after‑tax quarterly rate = 2.1 %, EARafter = (1.021)4 − 1 ≈ 8.67 %.

For a borrower, the after‑tax cost of debt is the nominal rate multiplied by (1 − t) if the entire interest is deductible. When the loan uses compound interest, the effective after‑tax cost is EARcost = (1 + r/m × (1 − t))m − 1. This is lower than the pre‑tax EAR, reflecting the tax shield. Questions may combine both perspectives: “A company issues a bond at 10 % p.a. semi‑annual coupon, tax rate 25 %. An investor in the 30 % bracket buys the bond. Compute the investor’s post‑tax yield and the issuer’s after‑tax cost.” The investor uses after‑tax periodic rate = 5 % × 0.70 = 3.5 %; EAR = (1.035)2 − 1 = 7.12 %. The issuer’s after‑tax periodic rate = 5 % × 0.75 = 3.75 %; EAR = (1.0375)2 − 1 = 7.64 %.

When tax is levied only on the interest component of each installment (as in a loan amortisation schedule), the after‑tax cash flow for period k is X − t × Ik, where Ik is the interest portion. The present value of these after‑tax cash flows at the after‑tax discount rate yields the net cost. This level of detail may appear in case‑let questions where you must construct a mini‑amortisation table for 3‑4 periods, apply tax to interest, and compute the NPV. The key is to keep the pre‑tax schedule intact, then adjust each interest line by (1 − t) before discounting.

  • Confusing nominal‑rate conversion with effective‑rate comparison; always convert to a common basis (EAR or periodic rate) before deciding which product is cheaper.
  • Forgetting the divergence condition g ≥ r in growing perpetuities; a finite PV exists only when r > g.
  • Treating a balloon payment as an extra installment in the annuity formula instead of discounting it separately as a lump sum.
  • Mixing the loan interest rate and the sinking‑fund earning rate when they differ; use the correct rate for each component.
  • Applying tax to the effective annual rate instead of to each period’s interest; tax is levied on periodic interest, not on the compounded EAR.
  • Neglecting the deferral period in a growing annuity; discount the annuity value back the exact number of periods before the first payment.
  • Rounding intermediate conversion factors (e.g., (1+r/m) or (1+g)/(1+r)) to fewer than six decimals, which can shift the final answer across option boundaries.
