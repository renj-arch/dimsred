Introduction to Percentage

1.1 Definition and Fundamental Meaning

The term percentage originates from the Latin phrase per centum, which translates directly to "by the hundred." In quantitative aptitude, it serves as a universal standard for comparing quantities of different magnitudes by normalizing the base to one hundred. When we state that a student scored 80 percent, we imply that for every one hundred marks available, the student secured eighty. This normalization is the reason percentage dominates competitive examinations; it allows the examiner to test a candidate's ability to handle ratios, proportions, and relative changes without the clutter of large or awkward numbers. The concept acts as the backbone for nearly every other arithmetic chapter, including profit and loss, simple and compound interest, time and work, and data interpretation. A shaky grasp of percentage fundamentals inevitably cascades into errors across the entire quantitative section.

1.2 Conversion Between Fractions, Decimals, and Percentages

The fluid movement between fractions, decimals, and percentages is the single most valuable computational skill for the examination hall. A percentage is fundamentally a fraction with a denominator of one hundred. To convert a fraction into a percentage, we multiply the fraction by one hundred and append the percent symbol. Conversely, to convert a percentage back into a fraction, we divide by one hundred and remove the symbol, often simplifying the resulting fraction to its lowest terms. Decimal conversion follows the same logic: shifting the decimal point two places to the right converts a decimal to a percentage, while shifting two places to the left converts a percentage to a decimal. This bidirectional fluency allows a candidate to recognize that 0.125, 12.5%, and 1/8 are identical values, enabling the selection of the most convenient form for a specific calculation. Choosing the fraction 1/8 over the decimal 0.125 when multiplying by 640 transforms a potentially tedious multiplication into a simple division by eight.

1.3 The Base Concept: Why the Denominator Matters

Every percentage calculation rests on an implicit or explicit base, the "whole" against which the "part" is measured. The most common error in percentage problems arises from misidentifying this base. If a salary increases by 20 percent and then decreases by 20 percent, the final salary is not the original amount because the base for the decrease is the new, higher salary, not the original one. The denominator changes. This principle extends to data interpretation sets where a graph might show percentage growth year over year; the actual value for year three depends on the actual value of year two, not year one. Understanding that the percentage operator is not commutative with respect to the base — that x% of y is mathematically equal to y% of x, but the context of "base" in word problems assigns specific roles to x and y — is critical for avoiding the trap answers designed by examiners.

1.4 Essential Fraction-Percentage Equivalents

Memorizing the reciprocal percentage equivalents of integers up to at least twenty is not a shortcut; it is a necessity for time management. The examination expects candidates to instantly recognize that 1/7 equals 14.28 percent, 1/12 equals 8.33 percent, and 1/17 equals 5.88 percent. This knowledge base allows for the immediate decomposition of complex percentages into manageable sums. For instance, finding 51 percent of a number becomes a two-step mental operation: 50 percent (half) plus 1 percent (one hundredth). Finding 37.5 percent triggers the recognition of 3/8. The table below summarizes the most frequently tested equivalents. Mastery of this table reduces the cognitive load during the exam, freeing working memory for the logical structure of the problem rather than the arithmetic mechanics.

Percentage Change and Application

2.1 Calculating Absolute and Relative Change

Percentage change quantifies the relative magnitude of a variation between an initial value and a final value. The formula — (Final Value minus Initial Value) divided by Initial Value, multiplied by one hundred — anchors the change to the starting point. This anchoring is why a rise from 50 to 75 is a 50 percent increase, but a fall from 75 back to 50 is only a 33.33 percent decrease. The absolute change (25 units) remains identical, but the base shifts from 50 to 75, altering the relative weight. In examination problems, candidates must rigorously identify the "initial value" from the narrative. Phrases like "increased by," "more than," or "greater than" signal a positive change relative to the subject of the sentence, whereas "decreased by," "less than," or "reduced to" signal a negative change. The distinction between "reduced by 20 percent" (subtract 20% of base) and "reduced to 20 percent" (become 20% of base) is a classic linguistic trap that separates prepared candidates from the rest.

2.2 Increase and Decrease: The Asymmetry of Base

The asymmetry of percentage change has profound implications for problems involving restoration of original values. If a commodity's price increases by 25 percent, it must decrease by only 20 percent to return to the original price, not 25 percent. This occurs because the increased price (125) becomes the new base for the decrease. To restore the original value (100), the reduction required is 25 units on a base of 125, yielding 20 percent. The general rule: if a value increases by x percent, the percentage decrease required to revert to the original value is 100x / (100 + x) percent. Conversely, if a value decreases by x percent, the percentage increase required to revert is 100x / (100 - x) percent. This formula is derived directly from the definition of percentage change and appears frequently in questions comparing two quantities, such as "A's income is 25% more than B's; by what percent is B's income less than A's?"

2.3 Product Constancy: Expenditure, Consumption, and Price

A major category of percentage problems relies on the principle of product constancy, where two variables multiply to give a constant product. The most common manifestation is Expenditure = Price × Quantity (Consumption). If the price of sugar rises by 20 percent and a household wishes to keep expenditure constant, consumption must fall. The fractional decrease in consumption is not 20 percent but 1/6 (16.67 percent), derived from the formula x / (100 + x) × 100 for an increase in price. Conversely, if price falls by 20 percent, consumption can rise by 1/4 (25 percent) to maintain the same expenditure, using the formula x / (100 - x) × 100. This logic extends to Time-Speed-Distance (Distance constant), Work-Efficiency-Time (Work constant), and Area-Length-Breadth (Area constant) problems. Recognizing the constant product structure allows the candidate to bypass algebraic equation formation and apply the fractional change rule directly, saving precious seconds.

2.4 Comparison of Two Quantities Using Percentage

Comparison problems ask: "If A is x percent more than B, by what percent is B less than A?" or "A is what percent of B?" The phrase "A is 20% more than B" translates mathematically to A = B + 0.2B = 1.2B. The base for the percentage is always the object following "than" or "of." To find what percent A is of B, we compute (A/B) × 100 = 120%. To find what percent B is of A, we compute (B/A) × 100 = (1/1.2) × 100 = 83.33%. The difference (100% - 83.33% = 16.67%) is the percentage by which B is less than A. This mirrors the restoration formula discussed earlier. Examiners often chain these comparisons: "A is 20% more than B, B is 25% less than C, C is 10% more than D." Solving these requires establishing a single reference variable (usually D = 100) and propagating the multipliers (1.2, 0.75, 1.1) through the chain to find the relative standing of A and D.

Successive Percentage Change

3.1 Derivation of the Net Change Formula

When a quantity undergoes two or more percentage changes consecutively, the net effect is not the arithmetic sum of the individual percentages. Consider a number N increased by x percent and then by y percent. After the first change, the value becomes N(1 + x/100). The second change applies to this new value, yielding N(1 + x/100)(1 + y/100). Expanding this product gives N[1 + (x/100) + (y/100) + (xy/10000)]. The term inside the brackets represents the total multiplier. Subtracting 1 and multiplying by 100 to express as a percentage yields the net percentage change formula: x + y + (xy/100). The cross-term xy/100 captures the "interest on interest" effect — the second percentage applies not only to the original principal but also to the increment generated by the first percentage. If the changes are decreases, x and y are treated as negative values, and the formula handles the signs automatically.

3.2 Application to Successive Increases and Decreases

The formula x + y + xy/100 is universal for any combination of signs. A 10 percent increase followed by a 10 percent decrease yields 10 + (-10) + (10 × -10)/100 = -1 percent, a net decrease of 1 percent. This counter-intuitive result — that an equal percentage increase and decrease never cancel out — occurs because the decrease operates on a larger base than the increase did. The order of operations does not affect the final result mathematically because multiplication is commutative: (1 + x/100)(1 + y/100) = (1 + y/100)(1 + x/100). However, in word problems involving "markup then discount" versus "discount then markup," the intermediate values (marked price, selling price) differ, which matters if the question asks for the marked price or the discount amount rather than just the net percentage change. For three successive changes x, y, z, the formula extends to x + y + z + (xy + yz + zx)/100 + xyz/10000, though in examinations, applying the two-change formula iteratively (combining x and y first, then combining the result with z) is faster and less error-prone than memorizing the three-variable expansion.

3.3 Successive Discounts and Marked Price Problems

Successive discounts are a direct application of successive percentage change where both x and y are negative. A shopkeeper offering successive discounts of 20 percent and 10 percent provides a single equivalent discount of -20 - 10 + (200/100) = -28 percent, i.e., a 28 percent discount. This is always less than the arithmetic sum (30 percent) because the second discount applies to a reduced price. Problems often ask for the marked price given the selling price after successive discounts, or the profit/loss percentage if the cost price is known. The chain is: Cost Price → (Markup %) → Marked Price → (Discount 1 %) → Intermediate Price → (Discount 2 %) → Selling Price. Using the multiplier method — multiplying by (1 + markup/100), then (1 - disc1/100), then (1 - disc2/100) — keeps the calculation linear and avoids the confusion of calculating discount amounts on shifting bases. The single equivalent discount formula x + y - xy/100 (for discounts x and y) is a specialized version of the general formula and should be used only when the question explicitly asks for the "single discount equivalent."

3.4 Geometric Interpretation: Area and Volume Changes

Successive percentage change finds a natural home in mensuration. If the length of a rectangle increases by 20 percent and the breadth increases by 10 percent, the area, being the product of length and breadth, undergoes a net change of 20 + 10 + (200/100) = 32 percent increase. If the radius of a sphere increases by 10 percent, the surface area (proportional to r²) changes by two successive 10 percent changes: 10 + 10 + 1 = 21 percent. The volume (proportional to r³) changes by three successive 10 percent changes: first two give 21 percent, combining with the third: 21 + 10 + 2.1 = 33.1 percent. This geometric perspective reinforces why the cross-term exists: area is a two-dimensional product, volume a three-dimensional one. Example: Successive Change in Geometry and Price
The length of a rectangle is increased by 20% and the breadth is decreased by 10%. Simultaneously, the price of a commodity is increased by 20% and the consumption is decreased by 10%. What is the net percentage change in the area of the rectangle and the expenditure on the commodity respectively?
Solution: Both scenarios involve the product of two variables. For the rectangle, Area = Length × Breadth. For expenditure, Expenditure = Price × Consumption. In both cases, the first variable increases by 20% (+20) and the second decreases by 10% (-10). Using the successive change formula: Net % Change = x + y + (xy/100). Substituting x = 20, y = -10: Net Change = 20 + (-10) + (20 × -10)/100 = 10 - 2 = 8%. Since the result is positive, there is an 8% increase in both the area of the rectangle and the expenditure on the commodity. The mathematical structure is identical because both represent a constant product relationship where the percentage changes are applied to the factors.
Exam Insight: The "Constant Product" Pattern
Whenever a problem defines a relationship Output = Factor1 × Factor2 (Area, Expenditure, Distance, Work, Revenue), and gives percentage changes in Factor1 and Factor2, the net percentage change in Output is always calculated using the successive change formula x + y + xy/100. Do not calculate new values for Factor1 and Factor2 and then multiply; the formula is derived exactly for this structure and is significantly faster. If the question asks for the percentage change in the second factor to keep the output constant (Product Constancy), use the shortcut: % change in Factor2 = (100 × % change in Factor1) / (100 ± % change in Factor1), with the sign in the denominator opposite to the change in Factor1.

Population Growth and Depreciation Models

4.1 Compound Growth Formula for Population

Population problems are essentially compound interest problems disguised in demographic language. The standard formula P(1 + r/100)^n calculates the population after n years given a current population P and an annual growth rate r percent. The growth is compounded because the base for each subsequent year includes the births (and net migration) of the previous year. If the growth rate varies by year — say r1 percent in year one, r2 in year two — the formula becomes P(1 + r1/100)(1 + r2/100). This is a direct application of the successive percentage change concept. A frequent variation asks for the population n years ago, which requires dividing the current population by the growth multiplier: P / (1 + r/100)^n. Candidates must be careful with the phrase "rate of increase is r% per annum" versus "population increases by r% every year"; they are mathematically identical, but the latter phrasing sometimes tricks students into using simple interest logic (adding r% of original P every year) rather than compounding.

4.2 Depreciation of Asset Value Over Time

Depreciation is the mirror image of population growth. The value of a machine, vehicle, or asset decreases by a fixed percentage every year. The formula is V(1 - r/100)^n, where V is the initial value and r is the annual depreciation rate. Because the base shrinks every year, the absolute amount of depreciation (the currency value lost) decreases annually, even though the rate remains constant. For example, a machine worth 1,00,000 depreciating at 10% loses 10,000 in year one (value 90,000), but only 9,000 in year two (value 81,000). Examiners often ask for the total depreciation over a period, which is Initial Value - Final Value, or the average annual depreciation, which is Total Depreciation / n. A twist involves the "scrap value" or "residual value" where depreciation stops, or a change in depreciation method (e.g., switching from percentage to fixed amount) after a certain period.

4.3 Variable Rates Over Different Periods

Real-world scenarios and difficult exam questions rarely maintain a constant rate. A town's population might grow at 5% for the first two years, shrink by 2% in the third year due to migration, and then grow at 8% for the next two years. The solution requires chaining the multipliers: P × 1.05 × 1.05 × 0.98 × 1.08 × 1.08. This is computationally intensive without a calculator, so the examiner expects the candidate to use approximation or fraction equivalents. For instance, 5% = 1/20, so multiplier is 21/20. 2% decrease = 49/50. 8% = 2/25, multiplier 27/25. The product becomes P × (21/20)² × (49/50) × (27/25)². Often, the question asks for the percentage change over the total period, allowing the candidate to ignore P and focus on the net multiplier. The net percentage change is (Final Multiplier - 1) × 100. This approach transforms a messy arithmetic problem into a manageable fraction multiplication exercise.

4.4 Reverse Calculation: Finding Past Values

Finding the population or value n years in the past is a common question type that tests the understanding of the direction of time in the compound formula. If the current population is P and the growth rate is r%, the population n years ago is P / (1 + r/100)^n. A frequent error is multiplying by (1 - r/100)^n, which would be the formula for a value that *decreased* at rate r to reach P. However, if the population *grew* at rate r to reach P, the past value must be smaller than P, and dividing by a number greater than 1 achieves this. Multiplying by (1 - r/100)^n implies a decay process, which is mathematically distinct from reversing a growth process unless r is very small. For example, reversing a 10% growth for one year: divide by 1.1 (multiply by 0.909...). Reversing via decay logic: multiply by 0.9. The results differ (0.909 vs 0.9). The correct operation is always the inverse of the forward process.

Election and Voting Problems

5.1 Structure of Vote Share: Valid, Invalid, and NOTA

Election problems are essentially percentage distribution problems with a specific vocabulary. The total votes polled (or total electorate) form the universal base. From this, a percentage is often declared invalid (spoiled ballots) or NOTA (None of the Above). The remainder constitutes valid votes. The winning candidate's share is usually given as a percentage of valid votes, not total votes. The critical step is establishing the correct base at each stage. If total votes are 1,00,000 and 10% are invalid, valid votes are 90,000. If Candidate A gets 60% of valid votes, A gets 54,000 votes. A common distractor option in the answer key will be 60,000 (60% of total votes), punishing the candidate who missed the "valid votes" qualifier. Similarly, NOTA votes are typically treated as a subset of valid votes in modern exam patterns, reducing the pool available for candidates. The hierarchy is: Total Votes → Invalid Votes → Valid Votes → NOTA Votes → Votes for Candidates.

5.2 Margin of Victory and Runner-up Calculations

The margin of victory is the difference between the votes secured by the winner and the runner-up. If there are only two candidates (or two effective candidates after ignoring NOTA/others), and the winner gets W% of valid votes, the runner-up gets (100 - W)%. The margin is (2W - 100)% of valid votes. If the absolute margin (e.g., 5,000 votes) is given, the valid vote count is Margin / (2W - 100)%. For example, a winner with 60% valid votes wins by a margin of 20% of valid votes. If the margin is 4,000 votes, valid votes = 4,000 / 0.20 = 20,000. This "margin percentage" shortcut bypasses calculating individual vote counts for winner and loser separately. In multi-cornered contests (three or more candidates), the margin is between the top two vote-getters, and the percentage shares of all candidates must sum to 100% of valid votes. The problem then becomes a system of equations or a logical deduction based on the constraints provided (e.g., "Candidate C got 10% more votes than Candidate B").

5.3 Scenarios with Multiple Candidates and Alliances

Advanced election problems introduce alliances or vote transfers. "Candidate A and B form an alliance; their combined vote share is 55%. If A gets 60% of the alliance votes, find A's individual percentage." This requires calculating 60% of 55% (33% of valid votes). Another variant: "10% of Candidate C's voters switch to Candidate B." This is a successive percentage change on a subset. If C had 20% of valid votes, 10% of that (2% of valid votes) moves to B. C drops to 18%, B rises by 2 percentage points. These problems test the ability to track "percentage of a percentage" and distinguish between "percentage points" (absolute difference between percentages) and "percent change" (relative difference). A rise from 20% to 22% is a rise of 2 percentage points but a 10% increase relative to the original 20%. The wording "increased by 10%" versus "increased by 10 percentage points" changes the answer entirely.

5.4 Data Interpretation Sets Based on Election Data

In the mains examination, election data frequently appears in Data Interpretation (DI) sets — tables showing constituency-wise votes, bar charts comparing vote shares across years, or pie charts showing demographic breakdowns. The percentage concepts remain the same, but the data extraction layer adds complexity. A table might give "Total Electors," "Turnout %," "Invalid Votes %," and "Vote Share % for Top 3 Candidates." The candidate must calculate: Polled Votes = Electors × Turnout%; Valid Votes = Polled Votes × (100 - Invalid%); Winner Votes = Valid Votes × Winner Share%. Multi-constituency comparison questions might ask: "In how many constituencies did the winner secure more than 50% of total electors (not just valid votes)?" This requires calculating the winner's votes as a percentage of the *electorate* base for each row, a classic "change of base" trap embedded in a DI set.

Percentage in Mixture and Alligation Problems

Mixture problems frequently appear in quantitative sections as applications of weighted averages, but they can be solved more efficiently by treating the concentration of a component as a percentage and applying the alligation rule. When two solutions of different concentrations are mixed, the concentration of the resulting mixture is the weighted average of the two concentrations, with weights equal to the volumes (or quantities) mixed. If solution A has concentration c₁% and solution B has concentration c₂%, and they are mixed in the ratio q₁ : q₂, the mixture concentration cₘ satisfies cₘ = (c₁q₁ + c₂q₂) / (q₁ + q₂). Rearranging gives the alligation diagram: the differences cₘ - c₁ and c₂ - cₘ are proportional to q₂ and q₁ respectively. This allows rapid determination of the mixing ratio when the desired concentration is known.

A common variant involves replacing part of a mixture with another substance. Suppose a vessel contains V litres of a solution with x% solute. If y litres are removed and replaced with pure solvent (0% solute), the new concentration becomes x × (V - y) / V%. Repeating this operation n times yields a concentration of x × (1 - y/V)ⁿ%. This is a direct application of successive percentage decrease on the solute amount, where the base (total volume) remains constant. The same logic applies when the replacement liquid has a non‑zero concentration; the recurrence becomes Cₙ = Cₙ₋₁ × (1 - y/V) + cᵣ × (y/V), where cᵣ is the concentration of the replacement liquid.

Alligation also handles three or more components. For three solutions with concentrations c₁, c₂, c₃ mixed to obtain cₘ, the ratios are not unique unless an additional constraint (such as a fixed proportion between two components) is given. In such cases, treat the problem as a system of linear equations: q₁ + q₂ + q₃ = Q and c₁q₁ + c₂q₂ + c₃q₃ = cₘQ. The percentage framework simplifies the arithmetic because all concentrations are expressed on a common base of 100.

Examiners often embed mixture questions in Data Interpretation sets where a table gives the percentage composition of several alloys or chemical solutions. The candidate must compute the quantity of a specific element in the final blend. The key step is to convert each percentage to an absolute amount using the given total weight, sum the absolute amounts, and then express the result as a percentage of the new total weight. This “change of base” is the most frequent source of error.

Percentage Change in Ratios and Proportions

When two quantities are in a ratio a : b, their percentage representations relative to the sum are a/(a+b) × 100% and b/(a+b) × 100%. If the quantities change by different percentages, the ratio changes in a non‑linear way. Suppose quantity A increases by p% and quantity B increases by q%. The new ratio becomes a(1 + p/100) : b(1 + q/100). The percentage change in the ratio (expressed as a fraction of the original ratio) is not simply p - q; it is [(1 + p/100)/(1 + q/100) - 1] × 100%. This distinction is critical in problems comparing growth rates of two populations, two investments, or two sides of a geometric figure.

A classic problem type: “The ratio of boys to girls in a school is 3:2. If the number of boys increases by 20% and the number of girls increases by 10%, find the new ratio and the percentage increase in the total number of students.” The new ratio is 3×1.2 : 2×1.1 = 3.6 : 2.2 = 18:11. The total increases from 5 parts to 5.8 parts, a 16% increase. Note that the overall percentage increase is the weighted average of the individual percentage increases, weighted by the original shares: (3/5)×20% + (2/5)×10% = 16%. This weighted‑average property holds for any number of components and is a direct consequence of the linearity of the sum.

Another frequent twist involves “percentage points” versus “percent change” in the context of ratios. If the share of boys in the total rises from 60% to 66%, that is an increase of 6 percentage points, but a 10% increase relative to the original 60%. Confusing these two leads to incorrect answers in questions asking “by what percent did the proportion of boys increase?” The correct interpretation is almost always the relative change (10%), not the absolute difference (6 percentage points), unless the wording explicitly says “percentage points”.

Problems may also ask for the percentage change in the ratio itself when the ratio is expressed as a single number (e.g., boys/girls). If the original ratio is R = a/b and the new ratio is R' = a(1+p/100) / b(1+q/100), then the percentage change in the ratio is (R'/R - 1) × 100% = [(1+p/100)/(1+q/100) - 1] × 100%. This formula is symmetric and avoids the need to compute absolute numbers.

Worked Example: Ratio Change with Different Growth Rates
The ratio of milk to water in a mixture is 4:1. Milk volume increases by 25% and water volume increases by 50%. Find the new ratio of milk to water and the percentage change in the concentration of milk (milk as a percentage of total mixture).
Let initial milk = 4x, water = x. Total = 5x. Milk concentration = (4x/5x)×100% = 80%. After increase: milk = 4x × 1.25 = 5x; water = x × 1.5 = 1.5x. New total = 6.5x. New milk concentration = (5x/6.5x)×100% = 76.923...%. Percentage change in concentration = ((76.923 - 80)/80)×100% = -3.846% (a decrease of about 3.85%). New ratio milk:water = 5x : 1.5x = 10:3. The concentration decreased because water grew faster than milk, even though both grew. The ratio changed from 4:1 (4.0) to 10:3 (3.333), a percentage change in the ratio value of ((3.333/4) - 1)×100% = -16.67%.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives a ratio and percentage changes for each component, first write the ratio as parts (e.g., 3k and 2k). Apply the percentage multipliers directly to the parts. This avoids large numbers and keeps the arithmetic clean. For concentration questions, always compute the new total before finding the new percentage; the denominator change is the most common trap.

Successive Percentage Change with Three or More Stages

The net effect of two successive percentage changes a% and b% is given by a + b + ab/100%. For three successive changes a%, b%, c%, the formula extends to a + b + c + (ab + bc + ca)/100 + abc/10000%. This pattern follows from expanding the product (1 + a/100)(1 + b/100)(1 + c/100). In general, for n successive changes p₁, p₂, …, pₙ, the overall multiplier is the product of the individual multipliers. The net percentage change is (∏(1 + pᵢ/100) - 1) × 100%. This multiplicative approach is more reliable than memorising extended formulas, especially when the number of stages varies.

A practical application is the calculation of effective annual rate when interest is compounded at different rates for different periods. For example, an investment earns 8% in year 1, 10% in year 2, and 6% in year 3. The overall growth factor is 1.08 × 1.10 × 1.06 = 1.25928, corresponding to a net increase of 25.928% over three years. The average annual rate (geometric mean) is (1.25928)^(1/3) - 1 ≈ 7.98%, not the arithmetic mean of 8%.

When the stages include both increases and decreases, the same product rule applies. A price increased by 20%, then decreased by 15%, then increased by 10% yields a multiplier 1.20 × 0.85 × 1.10 = 1.122, a net increase of 12.2%. Note that the order of operations does not affect the final multiplier because multiplication is commutative; however, the intermediate values (which may be asked in multi‑part questions) do depend on order.

Problems sometimes ask for the equivalent single percentage change that would produce the same final result. This is simply the net percentage change computed above. Another variant: given the final amount and the sequence of percentage changes, find the initial amount. This is a reverse calculation: divide the final amount by the product of the multipliers. For instance, if after a 20% increase, a 25% decrease, and a 10% increase the value becomes 990, the initial value is 990 / (1.20 × 0.75 × 1.10) = 990 / 0.99 = 1000.

In Data Interpretation sets, a table may show year‑on‑year percentage changes for a company’s revenue over five years. The candidate must compute the overall change from the base year to the terminal year. The product‑of‑multipliers method is the only robust way; adding the yearly percentages is incorrect because each year’s base differs.

Percentage Applications in Profit, Loss, and Discount Chains

Profit and loss problems are fundamentally percentage problems where the base is the cost price (CP) for profit/loss percentage and the marked price (MP) for discount percentage. A chain of transactions — manufacturer to wholesaler to retailer to customer — creates successive percentage changes on different bases. If the manufacturer sells at a 20% profit on CP, the wholesaler’s CP is 1.20 × manufacturer’s CP. If the wholesaler then sells at a 15% profit on his CP, the retailer’s CP is 1.15 × 1.20 × manufacturer’s CP = 1.38 × manufacturer’s CP. The overall profit percentage from manufacturer to retailer is 38%, not 35% (the sum). This illustrates the multiplicative nature of successive profit percentages.

Discount chains operate similarly. A marked price is discounted by 10%, then a further 5% discount is offered on the reduced price. The effective discount is not 15% but 1 - (0.90 × 0.95) = 1 - 0.855 = 0.145 = 14.5%. The formula for two successive discounts d₁% and d₂% is d₁ + d₂ - d₁d₂/100. For three discounts d₁, d₂, d₃, the effective discount is 1 - (1 - d₁/100)(1 - d₂/100)(1 - d₃/100). This is identical to the successive percentage change formula with negative signs.

A common twist combines profit and discount: “A shopkeeper marks his goods 40% above CP and allows a discount of 10%. Find his profit percentage.” The marked price is 1.40 × CP. Selling price after 10% discount is 0.90 × 1.40 × CP = 1.26 × CP. Profit = 26%. The shortcut: net percentage change = 40 - 10 - (40×10)/100 = 26%. This works because the discount is applied on the marked price, which is itself a percentage increase on CP.

When a problem states “a loss of 20% on selling price”, the base is the selling price (SP), not CP. If loss is 20% on SP, then CP = SP + 0.20 SP = 1.20 SP, so SP = CP/1.20. The loss percentage on CP is (CP - SP)/CP × 100% = (1 - 1/1.20) × 100% = 16.67%. Confusing the base (SP vs CP) is a frequent error. Always identify the base explicitly: profit/loss % is on CP unless stated otherwise; discount % is on MP.

Advanced problems may involve a series of transactions with different profit/loss percentages and a final selling price given. The candidate must work backwards through the chain, dividing by the appropriate multipliers. For example, a product passes through three hands: first gains 10%, second gains 20%, third loses 5%. If the final SP is 1134, the original CP is 1134 / (1.10 × 1.20 × 0.95) = 1134 / 1.254 = 904.31. Rounding to the nearest rupee may be required.

Percentage in Population with Migration and Net Growth

Standard population growth models assume a closed system where change is driven only by birth and death rates (natural increase). Real‑world problems often introduce migration — immigration (inflow) and emigration (outflow) — which add or subtract absolute numbers each period. If the annual natural growth rate is r% and net migration is a constant M persons per year (positive for net immigration), the population after n years follows the recurrence Pₙ = Pₙ₋₁(1 + r/100) + M. This is a linear non‑homogeneous recurrence. Its closed form is Pₙ = P₀(1 + r/100)ⁿ + M × [(1 + r/100)ⁿ - 1] / (r/100), provided r ≠ 0. If r = 0, the population simply grows linearly: Pₙ = P₀ + nM.

When migration itself is expressed as a percentage of the current population (e.g., “net migration is 2% of the population each year”), it merges with the natural growth rate to give an effective growth rate of (r + m)%, where m is the net migration rate. The population then follows the standard compound growth formula with rate r + m. However, if immigration and emigration rates are given separately (e.g., immigration 3%, emigration 1%), the net migration rate is 2% and the same logic applies.

A more complex scenario involves variable migration: for the first k years net migration is M₁ per year, then it changes to M₂. The population after n years (n > k) is computed in two stages: first apply the recurrence with M₁ for k years to get Pₖ, then use Pₖ as the new initial value and apply the recurrence with M₂ for the remaining n - k years. This stepwise approach mirrors the variable‑rate population problems already covered, but with an additive constant each year.

Depreciation models with salvage value also fit this framework. An asset depreciates at d% per year on the diminishing balance, but its value cannot fall below a scrap value S. The value after n years is max(V₀(1 - d/100)ⁿ, S). If the asset is sold before reaching scrap value, the book value is the diminished value. Problems may ask for the year in which the book value first drops below a certain threshold, requiring logarithms or trial with the formula.

In Data Interpretation, a table may show yearly population figures along with birth rate, death rate, and net migration. The candidate must verify whether the given net change matches the sum of natural increase and net migration, and then project future population using the derived effective growth rate. This tests both the understanding of components and the ability to handle mixed absolute and relative changes.

  • Treating a percentage change on a ratio as a simple difference of the individual percentage changes instead of using the multiplicative factor (1+p/100)/(1+q/100).
  • Confusing percentage points with percent change when the share of a component in a mixture or population shifts.
  • Adding successive profit, discount, or growth percentages arithmetically rather than multiplying the corresponding factors.
  • Using the wrong base (selling price instead of cost price, or marked price instead of cost price) for profit/loss percentage calculations.
  • Ignoring the additive migration term in population models and applying pure compound growth when a constant net inflow or outflow exists.
  • Assuming the order of successive percentage changes matters for the final multiplier; it does not, but intermediate values do depend on order.
  • Forgetting to convert a “loss on selling price” statement to the equivalent loss on cost price before comparing with other profit/loss figures.

Percentage in Compound Interest with Varying Rates and Partial Periods

Standard compound interest assumes a constant annual rate applied to the principal at the end of each full year. In many quantitative problems the rate changes after a certain number of years, or the interest is compounded more frequently than annually (half‑yearly, quarterly, monthly). When the rate varies, the amount after n years is the product of the successive growth factors: A = P × (1 + r₁/100) × (1 + r₂/100) × … × (1 + rₙ/100). If the rate changes mid‑year, the year is split into two sub‑periods with their own rates; the factor for that year becomes (1 + r₁/100)t × (1 + r₂/100)1‑t where t is the fraction of the year at the first rate. For intra‑year compounding, replace the annual rate r by the periodic rate r/k and raise the factor to the power k × n (k = 2 for half‑yearly, 4 for quarterly, 12 for monthly). The effective annual rate (EAR) that equates the intra‑year compounding to a single annual compounding is EAR = (1 + r/k)k – 1. A common twist is to give the nominal rate and ask for the amount after a non‑integer number of years; the candidate must compute the integer‑year product first, then apply the fractional‑year factor using the appropriate periodic rate. When the principal itself changes because of additional deposits or withdrawals, treat each cash‑flow as a separate principal with its own compounding horizon and sum the future values. This principle underlies the valuation of recurring deposits and systematic investment plans.

Percentage in Installment Buying and Equated Monthly Installments

Installment problems present a cash price C and a scheme: down payment D followed by n equal monthly installments I. The interest is usually quoted as a flat rate or a reducing‑balance rate. Under a flat rate, interest is calculated on the original loan amount L = C – D for the whole tenure: Total Interest = L × r/100 × (n/12). The installment is then I = (L + Total Interest) / n. This method overstates the effective cost because the borrower repays principal gradually. Under reducing‑balance (the standard banking practice), the outstanding principal declines each month. The monthly rate i = r/12/100 applies to the opening balance. The installment satisfies the present‑value annuity formula: L = I × [1 – (1 + i)‑n] / i. Solving for I gives I = L × i / [1 – (1 + i)‑n]. The examiner may ask for the effective annual rate implied by a flat‑rate quote; equate the two present‑value expressions and solve for r numerically or by approximation. Another variation: a certain number of installments are paid, then the borrower wishes to settle the loan early. The outstanding balance after k payments is the present value of the remaining n‑k installments: Balanceₖ = I × [1 – (1 + i)‑(n‑k)] / i. If a penalty of p% on the outstanding balance is levied, the settlement amount becomes Balanceₖ × (1 + p/100). Problems may also mix a down payment expressed as a percentage of the cash price with a flat‑rate interest on the reduced amount; always identify the base for each percentage before forming equations.

Worked Example: Flat vs Reducing Balance
A refrigerator costs 24,000. The buyer pays 4,000 down and the rest in 12 monthly installments. The dealer quotes a flat interest rate of 12% per annum. Find the monthly installment under the flat method and the effective annual rate under reducing‑balance method (assume monthly compounding).
Loan amount L = 24,000 – 4,000 = 20,000. Flat interest for 1 year = 20,000 × 12/100 = 2,400. Total payable = 22,400. Monthly installment (flat) = 22,400 / 12 = 1,866.67. For reducing balance, monthly rate i = r/12 unknown. Present value equation: 20,000 = 1,866.67 × [1 – (1 + i)‑12] / i. Solve by trial: i = 0.018 (1.8% per month) gives factor ≈ 10.90, PV ≈ 20,350; i = 0.019 gives factor ≈ 10.78, PV ≈ 20,120; i = 0.0195 gives factor ≈ 10.71, PV ≈ 20,000. So i ≈ 1.95% per month. Effective annual rate = (1 + 0.0195)12 – 1 ≈ 0.261 ≈ 26.1% per annum. The flat 12% quote hides an effective cost of about 26%.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When both flat and reducing‑balance rates appear, compute the installment first using the flat method (it is arithmetic). Then treat that installment as the known cash flow and back‑solve the reducing‑balance rate using the annuity formula. A quick approximation: effective rate ≈ 2 × flat rate × (n+1)/n for monthly installments; use it to bracket the true rate before refining.

Percentage in Inflation Adjustment and Real Rate of Return

Inflation erodes the purchasing power of money. If an investment yields a nominal return R% per period and the inflation rate is I%, the real rate of return r satisfies (1 + r/100) = (1 + R/100) / (1 + I/100). For small rates the approximation r ≈ R – I is often used, but the exact formula is required when rates exceed 5‑10%. In multi‑year horizons with varying inflation, the cumulative real growth factor is the product of the yearly real factors: ∏ (1 + Rₖ/100) / (1 + Iₖ/100). A typical problem gives a series of nominal returns and a constant inflation rate, asking for the real value of an initial investment after n years. Compute the nominal amount Aₙ = P × ∏ (1 + Rₖ/100), then deflate by the cumulative inflation factor (1 + I/100)ⁿ to obtain the real amount Aₙ(real) = Aₙ / (1 + I/100)ⁿ. Conversely, if a target real amount is specified, work backwards: find the required nominal amount by multiplying the target by the inflation factor, then determine the nominal return needed. Tax on nominal gains adds another layer: post‑tax nominal return = R × (1 – t/100). The real post‑tax return uses the same division by the inflation factor. Indexation benefits in capital‑gains taxation effectively replace the purchase price by its inflation‑adjusted value, which is mathematically equivalent to computing the gain in real terms and then applying tax.

Percentage in Partnership: Capital Contribution, Time Weighting, and Profit Distribution

Partnership problems allocate profit (or loss) in proportion to the capital‑time product of each partner. If partner A invests C₁ for t₁ months and partner B invests C₂ for t₂ months, the profit share ratio is C₁t₁ : C₂t₂. When capitals change during the period (additional investment or withdrawal), split the tenure into sub‑periods with constant capital and sum the products: Capital‑time = Σ Cᵢ × Δtᵢ. A frequent twist introduces a working partner who receives a fixed salary or a percentage of profit before the remaining profit is divided in the capital‑time ratio. If the working partner’s remuneration is S (absolute) or p% of total profit, deduct it first: Distributable Profit = Total Profit – S (or Total Profit × (1 – p/100)). Then apply the capital‑time ratio to the distributable profit. Another variation: interest on capital at a given rate is paid before profit sharing. Compute interest for each partner as Interest = Capital × Rate × Time/12, subtract total interest from profit, then divide the remainder by capital‑time ratio. If a partner joins after the business has started, his capital‑time product uses only the months he was active. When the profit‑sharing ratio is given directly (e.g., 3:2) but capitals and times are known, verify consistency: the ratio of capital‑time products must equal the given profit ratio; otherwise the problem contains a hidden salary or interest component. Problems may also ask for the capital contributed by a partner given his profit share, the total profit, and the other partners’ capital‑time data; set up the proportion and solve for the unknown capital.

Percentage in Index Numbers: Base Year Shifts, Chain Indices, and Deflation

Index numbers express the level of a variable relative to a base period. A fixed‑base index for year t is Iₜ = (Valueₜ / Value₀) × 100. When the base year is shifted from year 0 to year k, the new index becomes Iₜ' = (Valueₜ / Valueₖ) × 100 = Iₜ / Iₖ × 100. Chain indices avoid a single distant base by linking each year to the previous one: Chainₜ = (Valueₜ / Valueₜ₋₁) × 100. The chain‑base index for year t relative to year 0 is the product of the yearly chain indices divided by 100t‑1: ChainIndexₜ = (∏j=1t Chainⱼ) / 100t‑1. Deflating a nominal series uses a price index (e.g., CPI) to obtain real values: Realₜ = Nominalₜ × (100 / CPIₜ) if the CPI base is 100. If the CPI base year differs from the nominal series base, first rebase the CPI to the nominal base using the shift formula. Splicing two index series with overlapping years requires a linking factor: compute the ratio of the two indices in the overlap year and multiply the older series by that factor. A common examiner trap is to ask for the percentage change in real wages given nominal wage indices and CPI indices with different base years; the candidate must rebase both to a common year before forming the real wage index. Another twist: a composite index (e.g., Index of Industrial Production) is a weighted average of sub‑indices. If weights are given as percentages, the composite index is Σ (Weightᵢ × SubIndexᵢ) / 100. Changing weights over time (chain‑weighted indices) follow the same principle but with period‑specific weights.

  • Treating a flat‑rate installment interest as the true annual percentage rate without converting to reducing‑balance equivalent.
  • Using the approximation real rate ≈ nominal – inflation when rates are large, leading to material error.
  • Forgetting to deduct salary, commission, or interest on capital before applying the capital‑time profit‑sharing ratio.
  • Applying a fixed‑base index shift incorrectly by multiplying instead of dividing by the old base index.
  • Mixing nominal and real quantities in the same equation without deflating or inflating consistently.
  • Assuming the order of successive percentage changes matters for the final multiplier when the base remains the same; it does not, but intermediate values differ.
  • Ignoring the additive migration term in population models and applying pure compound growth when a constant net inflow or outflow exists.

Percentage in Time, Speed, and Distance with Variable Rates

Problems that combine percentage change with the classic time‑speed‑distance relationship appear frequently in advanced quantitative sections. The core idea is that speed, time, or distance may be altered by a given percentage, and the new values must satisfy the invariant Distance = Speed × Time. When speed increases by p%, the new speed becomes S × (1 + p/100). If the distance remains unchanged, the time required changes inversely: new time = Distance / New Speed. This yields a percentage change in time of ‑p/(100+p) × 100 (a decrease). Conversely, a percentage increase in time with constant speed produces a proportional increase in distance.

Successive Percentage Changes on Speed

If a vehicle’s speed is increased by a% and later decreased by b%, the net speed multiplier is (1 + a/100)(1 – b/100). The overall percentage change in speed is [(1 + a/100)(1 – b/100) – 1] × 100. Because distance is often fixed in such problems, the net percentage change in time is the negative of the net percentage change in speed only when the base remains the same; however, the base for time is the original time, so the percentage change in time is ‑[net speed change%] / (1 + net speed change/100) × 100. This asymmetry is a common source of error.

Variable Distance Scenarios

When distance itself changes by a percentage (e.g., a detour adds 15% to the route), the new distance is D × (1 + d/100). If speed also changes, the new time becomes New Distance / New Speed. The combined effect can be computed by multiplying the distance multiplier and the reciprocal of the speed multiplier. For example, a 20% increase in distance and a 10% increase in speed give a time multiplier of 1.20 / 1.10 ≈ 1.0909, i.e., a 9.09% increase in time.

Round‑Trip and Average Speed

In round‑trip problems, the average speed for the whole journey is the harmonic mean of the two speeds when distances are equal: 2 × S₁ × S₂ / (S₁ + S₂). If each leg’s speed is altered by different percentages, compute the new speeds first, then apply the harmonic mean formula. The percentage change in average speed relative to the original average speed is not simply the average of the individual percentage changes; it must be derived from the new harmonic mean.

Application to Relative Motion

When two objects move towards or away from each other, their relative speed is the sum or difference of their individual speeds. A percentage change in one object’s speed changes the relative speed by the same absolute amount, but the percentage change in relative speed depends on the original relative speed. For instance, if two trains approach each other at 60 km/h and 40 km/h (relative speed 100 km/h) and the first train’s speed increases by 20%, the new relative speed becomes 72 + 40 = 112 km/h, a 12% increase, not 20%.

Percentage in Work Efficiency and Man‑Day Calculations

Work‑time problems often state that a worker’s efficiency changes by a certain percentage. Efficiency is inversely proportional to the time taken to complete a fixed amount of work. If a worker’s efficiency increases by e%, the time required decreases by e/(100+e) × 100%. For a group of workers, the total work done per day (man‑day output) is the sum of individual efficiencies. A percentage change in the number of workers or in their individual efficiencies alters the total daily output multiplicatively.

Changing Workforce Size

If the workforce is increased by w% and each worker’s efficiency remains constant, the total daily output rises by w%. The time to complete a fixed job then falls by w/(100+w) × 100%. When both workforce size and efficiency change simultaneously, the net daily output multiplier is (1 + w/100)(1 + e/100). The overall percentage reduction in completion time is [1 – 1/((1 + w/100)(1 + e/100))] × 100.

Partial Work and Mid‑Project Changes

A common twist: a project starts with a certain crew, and after a fraction of the work is done, the crew size or efficiency changes. Let the total work be W man‑days. Suppose the first phase completes f × W with initial daily output O₁. The time for phase one is fW / O₁. The remaining work (1‑f)W is done at a new daily output O₂ = O₁ × (1 + Δ/100). Total time = fW/O₁ + (1‑f)W/O₂. The overall percentage change in total time compared to the original plan (which would have taken W/O₁) can be expressed as a function of f and Δ.

Alternate Day Working and Rest Days

If workers operate on alternate days or take periodic rest, the effective daily output is reduced by a factor equal to the working‑day fraction. A percentage increase in efficiency applies only on working days. For example, a crew works 5 days a week (5/7 ≈ 71.43% of calendar days). A 20% efficiency boost on working days raises the calendar‑day average output by 0.7143 × 1.20 = 0.8571 of the original full‑time output, i.e., a 20% increase on the working‑day base but only a 14.3% increase on the calendar‑day base.

Combined Efficiency of Heterogeneous Workers

When workers have different base efficiencies, a uniform percentage increase applies to each individually. The group’s total efficiency is the sum of individual efficiencies. If worker A contributes 60% of total output and worker B 40%, a 10% raise for both yields a group increase of 10% (since the weights sum to 1). However, if only the more efficient worker gets a raise, the group increase is weighted: 0.60 × 10% = 6%. This weighted‑average approach is essential for problems where incentives target specific segments.

Worked Example: Mid‑Project Workforce Change
A construction project is estimated to be completed in 40 days by 50 workers working at constant efficiency. After 10 days, 20% of the workers leave. The remaining workers increase their efficiency by 25% to compensate. How many total days will the project take?

Total work = 50 workers × 40 days = 2000 man‑days (assuming efficiency = 1 unit per worker per day).

Work done in first 10 days = 50 × 10 = 500 man‑days.

Remaining work = 2000 – 500 = 1500 man‑days.

After 10 days, workers left = 20% of 50 = 10, so remaining workers = 40.

New efficiency per worker = 1 × (1 + 25/100) = 1.25 units/day.

New daily output = 40 × 1.25 = 50 man‑days per day (same as original daily output).

Time for remaining work = 1500 / 50 = 30 days.

Total time = 10 + 30 = 40 days. The project finishes on schedule.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mixes percentage changes in workforce and efficiency, always convert everything to a common “man‑day” or “output per day” base. Compute the work done before the change, then recalculate the daily output after the change using the multiplicative formula (1 + w/100)(1 + e/100). This avoids the trap of averaging percentages incorrectly.

Percentage in Data Interpretation: Comparative Percentage Tables

Data interpretation sets often present multiple tables where each cell is a percentage of a different base — row total, column total, or grand total. The key to solving these quickly is identifying the base for each percentage. A row‑percentage table shows each entry as a share of the row sum; a column‑percentage table uses the column sum; a total‑percentage table uses the overall sum. Converting between these views requires the marginal totals.

Reconstructing Absolute Values

If a table gives row percentages and the row totals are provided, absolute values = row total × (cell percentage / 100). If only column totals and column percentages are given, absolute values = column total × (cell percentage / 100). When neither marginal total is given but the grand total is known, you can set up a system of equations: the sum of all cells equals the grand total, and each cell equals its row (or column) percentage times its row (or column) total. Solving yields the missing marginals.

Percentage Point Change vs Percent Change

A frequent examiner trap is confusing a change in percentage points with a percent change. If a category’s share rises from 20% to 25%, the increase is 5 percentage points, but the percent increase relative to the original share is (25‑20)/20 × 100 = 25%. Always read the question wording: “by how many percentage points” asks for the absolute difference; “by what percent” asks for the relative change.

Year‑Over‑Year Growth in Stacked Tables

When the same categories are shown for two years in separate tables, compute the absolute values for each year using the respective bases, then find the year‑over‑year growth rate for each category: (Value₂ – Value₁) / Value₁ × 100. If the bases differ (e.g., total market size changes), the category’s percentage share may fall even while its absolute value rises. This divergence is a classic insight tested in advanced sets.

Weighted Average of Percentages

If a table shows segment‑wise growth rates and the segment weights (share of total), the overall growth rate is the weighted average of the segment growth rates: Σ (weightᵢ × growthᵢ). The weights must be based on the same period (usually the base year). Using current‑year weights produces a different figure (Paasche vs Laspeyres index). The problem statement usually specifies which base to use; if not, the base‑year weighted average (Laspeyres) is the standard convention.

Missing Data Inference

Some DI sets hide one or two cells and ask for the missing percentage. Use the fact that row percentages sum to 100% (or column percentages sum to 100%). If a row has three categories with percentages 30%, 45%, and a missing value, the missing value is 25%. When absolute values are partially known, you can often deduce the missing percentage by equating the known absolute value to the product of its row total and its percentage.

Percentage in Taxation: Slab Rates, Surcharge, and Effective Tax Rate

Taxation problems model progressive slab systems where income is taxed at different rates for different brackets. The effective tax rate is the total tax paid divided by total income, expressed as a percentage. Understanding how surcharges, cess, and rebates interact with slabs is crucial for accurate computation.

Slab‑wise Tax Calculation

Suppose the slabs are: 0–250,000 at 0%; 250,001–500,000 at 5%; 500,001–1,000,000 at 20%; above 1,000,000 at 30%. For an income of 1,200,000, tax = 0 on first 250,000 + 5% of 250,000 + 20% of 500,000 + 30% of 200,000 = 12,500 + 100,000 + 60,000 = 172,500. Effective rate = 172,500 / 1,200,000 × 100 ≈ 14.375%.

Surcharge on High Income

A surcharge is an additional percentage applied on the tax amount (not on income) when income exceeds a threshold. For example, a 10% surcharge on tax for income > 1,000,000. In the above example, tax before surcharge = 172,500. Surcharge = 10% of 172,500 = 17,250. Total tax = 189,750. Effective rate = 189,750 / 1,200,000 × 100 = 15.8125%.

Health and Education Cess

A cess is a fixed percentage on the total tax including surcharge. If a 4% health and education cess applies, cess = 4% of 189,750 = 7,590. Final tax = 197,340. Effective rate = 197,340 / 1,200,000 × 100 ≈ 16.445%.

Rebate under Section 87A Type Provisions

A rebate reduces the tax liability (before surcharge and cess) by a fixed amount for incomes up to a certain limit. If a rebate of 12,500 applies for income ≤ 500,000, and the computed tax before rebate is 12,500, the tax after rebate becomes zero. The rebate does not create a refund; it only nullifies tax up to the rebate amount. This can change the effective rate dramatically for borderline incomes.

Marginal vs Effective Rate Comparison

The marginal rate is the rate applied to the last rupee of income (the highest slab rate). The effective rate is always lower than the marginal rate in a progressive system (unless there is a single flat rate). Problems may ask: “By what percentage does the effective rate differ from the marginal rate?” Compute both and find the relative difference: (Marginal – Effective) / Marginal × 100.

Percentage in Depreciation: Straight Line vs Written Down Value Methods

Asset depreciation is a classic application of percentage decrease over time. Two standard methods are Straight Line Method (SLM) and Written Down Value (WDV) method, also called Reducing Balance Method. Both use percentages but produce different book values each year.

Straight Line Method

Under SLM, a fixed amount is depreciated each year. Annual depreciation = (C – R) / n. The depreciation rate on the depreciable amount is (1/n) × 100%.0% of (C – R). Book value after k years = C – k × (C – R)/n. The depreciation amount is constant; the percentage of the current book value declines each year.

Written Down Value Method

Under WDV, a fixed percentage (say d%) is applied to the book value at the beginning of each year. Book value after 1 year = C × (1 – d/100). After k years = C × (1 – d/100)^k. The depreciation amount decreases geometrically. The rate d is often chosen so that the book value after n years approximates the residual value: d = 1 – (R/C)^(1/n). This rate is not simply 1/n × 100; it is higher in early years.

Comparing Total Depreciation Over a Period

For the same asset, SLM and WDV yield different accumulated depreciation at any intermediate year. WDV front‑loads depreciation, giving higher expense early, lower later. The total depreciation over the full life is the same (C – R) if the WDV rate is calibrated to reach R exactly at year n. However, if the WDV rate is given independently (e.g., 20% per annum), the book value after n years may not equal R; the difference is a gain or loss on disposal.

Switching Methods Mid‑Life

Some problems state that a company switches from SLM to WDV (or vice versa) after m years. The book value at the switch point becomes the new base for the subsequent method. For example, after 3 years of SLM, book value = C – 3×(C–R)/n. From year 4 onward, apply WDV rate d on this book value. The depreciation for year 4 = (book value at start of year 4) × d/100. This hybrid approach tests the ability to track book value across method changes.

Partial Year Depreciation

When an asset is acquired or sold mid‑year, depreciation is prorated. Under SLM, monthly depreciation = annual depreciation / 12. Under WDV, the common practice is to apply the annual rate for the fraction of the year: depreciation = book value × d/100 × (months held/12). Some jurisdictions use the half‑year convention (half a year’s depreciation in the year of acquisition and disposal). The problem will specify the convention; if not, assume pro‑rata monthly.

  • Confusing percentage point change with percent change in DI tables.
  • Applying a single percentage change to the wrong base (row vs column vs grand total).
  • Treating surcharge as a percentage of income instead of tax.
  • Forgetting to apply cess on tax plus surcharge, not on tax alone.
  • Using the SLM rate as the WDV rate without adjusting for the reducing base.
  • Ignoring the mid‑year convention when an asset is purchased or sold part‑way through the year.
  • Assuming the order of successive percentage changes matters for the final multiplier when the base remains the same; it does not, but intermediate values differ.
