Biomolecules — Carbohydrates, Proteins, Amino Acids, Enzymes, Vitamins & Nucleic Acids

Deep dive into Biomolecules — carbohydrates (monosaccharides like glucose and fructose, disaccharides like sucrose and lactose, polysaccharides like starch and cellulose, glycosidic bond formation and hydrolysis), proteins and amino acids (the 20 standard amino acids, peptide bonds, primary sequence, secondary structures α-helices and β-sheets, tertiary folding driven by hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bridges, quaternary assembly), enzymes and vitamins (Michaelis-Menten kinetics, competitive and non-competitive inhibition, cofactors and coenzymes, water-soluble vitamins B and C, fat-soluble vitamins A D E K), and nucleic acids (nucleotides and nucleosides, purines and pyrimidines, DNA double helix and Watson-Crick base pairing, semiconservative replication, transcription to mRNA, translation and the genetic code).

Part 1

🍞 Carbohydrates

Saccharides
Monosaccharides, Disaccharides, Polysaccharides, and Glycosidic Bonds
Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones with the general formula (CH₂O)n. Monosaccharides (the simplest sugars) include glucose (an aldohexose, C₆H₁₂O₆), fructose (a ketohexose), and ribose (an aldopentose). Monosaccharides exist in linear and cyclic forms; glucose cyclizes to a six-membered pyranose ring via a hemiacetal bond between the aldehyde at C1 and the hydroxyl at C5, creating the α and β anomers at the anomeric carbon. Disaccharides form when two monosaccharides join via a glycosidic bond — a covalent acetal linkage between the anomeric carbon of one sugar and a hydroxyl of another. Sucrose (α-D-glucopyranosyl-(1→2)-β-D-fructofuranoside) is a non-reducing disaccharide. Maltose (α-1→4 glycosidic linkage), lactose (β-1→4), and cellobiose (β-1→4) are reducing disaccharides. Polysaccharides are long polymers of monosaccharide units. Starch (amylose α-1→4 linear, amylopectin α-1→6 branched) is the plant energy storage polymer. Glycogen is the highly branched animal analogue. Cellulose (β-1→4 linked D-glucose) forms linear chains that hydrogen-bond into strong fibrils — the primary structural component of plant cell walls. Chitin (β-1→4 linked N-acetylglucosamine) is the structural polysaccharide in arthropod exoskeletons. Enzymes like amylase hydrolyze starch; cellulases are needed to break cellulose.
Carbohydrates serve both as energy sources (starch, glycogen) via glycosidic bond hydrolysis and as structural materials (cellulose, chitin) owing to the strength of extended hydrogen-bonded networks.
Part 2

🩸 Proteins & Amino Acids

Peptide Chains
Amino Acids, Peptide Bonds, and Protein Structure Levels
The 20 standard amino acids share a common structure: a central α-carbon bonded to an amino group (−NH₃), a carboxyl group (−COOH), a hydrogen atom, and a variable side chain R that determines chemical properties — hydrophobic (alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan), polar uncharged (serine, threonine, cysteine, asparagine, glutamine), positively charged (lysine, arginine, histidine), and negatively charged (aspartate, glutamate). At physiological pH (≈7.4), amino acids exist as zwitterions with −NH₃⁺ and −COO&supmin;. Peptide bonds form between the α-carboxyl of one amino acid and the α-amino of another via a dehydration reaction, creating a planar −CO−NH− linkage with partial double-bond character (restricted rotation). Primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids. Secondary structure includes the α-helix (hydrogen bonds between carbonyl O of residue i and amide H of residue i+4, 3.6 residues per turn, 5.4 Å pitch) and the β-sheet (hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms of adjacent chains, parallel or antiparallel). Tertiary structure is the three-dimensional folding driven by hydrophobic collapse, hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, and disulfide bridges (cysteine−cysteine covalent bonds). Quaternary structure describes the assembly of multiple polypeptide chains, as in hemoglobin (α₂β₂ tetramer). Protein denaturation (unfolding) occurs via heat, pH change, or denaturants like urea, disrupting non-covalent interactions.
Proteins adopt specific hierarchical structures determined by their amino acid sequence; the peptide bond’s planarity constrains backbone geometry while side chains dictate folding and function.
Part 3

⚡ Enzymes & Vitamins

Catalysis
Enzyme Kinetics, Inhibition, Cofactors, and Vitamins
Enzymes are biological catalysts that accelerate reactions by lowering activation energy. The Michaelis-Menten model describes enzyme kinetics: E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, with rate v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]), where Km is the substrate concentration at half-maximal velocity (indicating enzyme-substrate affinity) and Vmax = kcat[E]0. The turnover number kcat is the maximum number of substrate molecules converted per enzyme per second. The catalytic efficiency is kcat / Km. Competitive inhibition occurs when an inhibitor binds the active site, increasing apparent Km (same Vmax). Non-competitive inhibition binds elsewhere, reducing Vmax (same Km). Uncompetitive inhibition binds only the ES complex, reducing both. Cofactors (metal ions like Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺/³⁺) and coenzymes (organic molecules like NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A) are essential for many enzymes. Vitamins are organic compounds required in small amounts that the body cannot synthesize. Water-soluble vitamins (B-complex: B₁ thiamine, B₂ riboflavin, B₃ niacin, B₅ pantothenic acid, B₆ pyridoxine, B₇ biotin, B₉ folate, B₁₂ cobalamin; and vitamin C ascorbic acid) serve as coenzyme precursors and antioxidants. Fat-soluble vitamins (A retinol for vision, D calciferol for calcium metabolism, E tocopherol as antioxidant, K phylloquinone for blood clotting) are stored in adipose tissue and liver, and their deficiency or excess causes specific syndromes.
Enzymes achieve remarkable rate enhancement through specific binding and transition-state stabilization; vitamins are essential micronutrients that often function as coenzyme precursors.

🧪 Nucleic Acids

Genetic Material
Nucleotides, DNA Double Helix, Replication, and Transcription
Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides, each consisting of a nitrogenous base (purine: adenine A, guanine G; pyrimidine: cytosine C, thymine T in DNA, uracil U in RNA), a pentose sugar (2′-deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and a phosphate group. Nucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds between the 5′-phosphate of one nucleotide and the 3′-hydroxyl of the next, giving a 5′→3′ direction. The DNA double helix (Watson and Crick, 1953) consists of two antiparallel strands held together by Watson-Crick base pairing: A pairs with T via two hydrogen bonds, G pairs with C via three hydrogen bonds. The sugar-phosphate backbone is on the outside; the bases stack inside with π stacking interactions. Major groove and minor groove differ in width and provide binding sites for regulatory proteins. DNA replication is semiconservative: DNA helicase unwinds the double helix, DNA polymerase III (in prokaryotes) synthesizes the leading strand continuously and the lagging strand as Okazaki fragments (later joined by DNA ligase). Transcription produces mRNA from a DNA template: RNA polymerase binds the promoter, unwinds DNA, and synthesizes RNA 5′→3′ complementary to the template strand (with U replacing T). Translation on ribosomes converts the mRNA codon sequence (triplets of bases) into a polypeptide chain via tRNA adaptors carrying specific amino acids. The genetic code (64 codons for 20 amino acids and 3 stop codons) is degenerate, with multiple codons often specifying the same amino acid (wobble hypothesis by Crick).
The DNA double helix encodes genetic information through complementary base pairing; replication ensures faithful copying, while transcription and translation express this information as functional proteins.
