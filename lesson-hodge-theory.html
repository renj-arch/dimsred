Hodge Theory — Hodge Decomposition, Mixed Hodge Structures & the Hard Lefschetz Theorem

Hodge theory is the study of harmonic forms on Riemannian and Kähler manifolds, providing a deep link between analysis, topology, and algebraic geometry. On a compact Riemannian manifold, the Hodge theorem states that every de Rham cohomology class has a unique harmonic representative, giving an isomorphism Hᵏ_dR(M, ℝ) ≅ ℋᵏ(M). On a Kähler manifold, the complex structure interacts with the metric to produce the Hodge decomposition Hᵏ(M, ℂ) = ⊕_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M), refining the cohomology by type. The Hard Lefschetz theorem gives an isomorphism between cohomology groups via cup product with powers of the Kähler form. Deligne's Mixed Hodge Structures extend these ideas to singular and non-compact varieties, providing a powerful tool for understanding the topology of algebraic varieties. The Hodge conjecture — one of the Clay Millennium Problems — asks whether every Hodge class is algebraic.

Part 1

📏 The Hodge Theorem on Riemannian Manifolds

Harmonic Theory
The Hodge Star, the Laplacian, and the Hodge Theorem
On an oriented Riemannian n-manifold (M, g), the Hodge star operator ⋆: Ωᵏ(M) → Ω^{n−k}(M) is defined by α ∧ ⋆β = g(α, β) dvol. The L² inner product on k-forms is ⟨α,β⟩ = ∫_M α ∧ ⋆β. The codifferential δ: Ωᵏ → Ω^{k−1} is the formal adjoint of d: δ = (−1)^{n(k+1)+1} ⋆ d ⋆. The Hodge Laplacian Δ = dδ + δd. A form α is harmonic if Δα = 0; denote the space of harmonic k-forms by ℋᵏ(M). The Hodge theorem: dim ℋᵏ(M) < ∞, and every de Rham cohomology class has a unique harmonic representative. This yields the orthogonal decomposition Ωᵏ(M) = ℋᵏ(M) ⊕ dΩ^{k−1}(M) ⊕ δΩ^{k+1}(M) and the isomorphism Hᵏ_{dR}(M, ℝ) ≅ ℋᵏ(M). The theory extends to elliptic complexes via general elliptic operator theory on compact manifolds, culminating in the Atiyah-Singer index theorem: the analytic index of an elliptic operator equals its topological index.
The Hodge theorem bridges analysis and topology: harmonic forms are the unique minimizers of the Dirichlet energy in each cohomology class, giving a canonical representative.
Part 2

🌍 Hodge Theory on Kähler Manifolds

Kähler
Kähler Identities, Hodge Decomposition, and Hard Lefschetz
On a Kähler manifold (M, g, J, ω), the complex structure J and the Riemannian metric g are compatible, and the Kähler form ω(·,·) = g(·, J·) is closed (dω = 0). The Dolbeault operators ∂: Ω^{p,q} → Ω^{p+1,q} and ∂̄: Ω^{p,q} → Ω^{p,q+1} satisfy d = ∂ + ∂̄. The Kähler identities relate the Lefschetz operator L(α) = ω∧α and its adjoint Λ: [Λ, ∂] = i∂̄^* and [Λ, ∂̄] = −i∂^*. These imply the fundamental equality of Laplacians: Δ_d = 2Δ_∂ = 2Δ_∂̄. The Hodge decomposition for compact Kähler manifolds: Hᵏ(M, ℂ) = ⊕_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M), where H^{p,q} ≅ H^q(M, Ω^p_M) is the Dolbeault cohomology. The Hodge numbers h^{p,q} = dim H^{p,q} satisfy: h^{p,q} = h^{q,p} (conjugation symmetry) and h^{p,q} = h^{n−p,n−q} (Serre duality). The Hard Lefschetz theorem: the map L^{n−k}: Hᵏ(M, ℂ) → H^{2n−k}(M, ℂ) given by cup product with ω^{n−k} is an isomorphism for all k. The primitive decomposition: every harmonic form decomposes uniquely into primitive forms, giving the Lefschetz decomposition of the cohomology.
On Kähler manifolds, Riemannian, symplectic, and complex structures harmonize, producing the refined Hodge decomposition and powerful constraints on topology.
Part 3

🧮 Mixed Hodge Structures

Deligne
Pure and Mixed Hodge Structures, Deligne's Theorem
A pure Hodge structure of weight k consists of a ℚ-vector space V with a decomposition V⊗ℂ = ⊕_{p+q=k} V^{p,q} such that V^{p,q} = \bar{V}^{q,p} (complex conjugation exchanges the summands). A mixed Hodge structure (MHS) is a ℚ-vector space V equipped with an increasing weight filtration W_•: 0 ⊂ W_0 ⊂ W_1 ⊂ … ⊂ W_{2k} = V and a decreasing Hodge filtration F^•: V⊗ℂ ⊃ … ⊃ F^p ⊃ F^{p+1} ⊃ … such that on each graded piece Gr^W_k V = W_k/W_{k−1}, the filtration F^• induces a pure Hodge structure of weight k. Deligne's theorem (1971-74): the cohomology Hᵏ(X, ℚ) of any algebraic variety X over ℂ carries a canonical mixed Hodge structure, functorial for algebraic maps. For a smooth projective variety, the MHS is pure (the weight filtration is trivial: W_k = 0 for k < m and W_k = Hᵏ(X, ℚ) for k ≥ m). For a singular variety, the weight filtration encodes the cohomology of a desingularization. For a complement X\D of a divisor with normal crossings, the MHS captures the logarithmic forms. The MHS also exists on the fundamental group (the Malcev completion) and on nearby cycle cohomology.
Mixed Hodge structures are a powerful generalization of classical Hodge theory to all algebraic varieties, providing a systematic framework for understanding their cohomology.
Part 4

🔥 Hodge Polynomials and the Hodge Conjecture

Conjecture
Hodge Polynomials, the Hodge Conjecture, and Related Problems
The Hodge polynomial of a smooth projective variety M is χ_u(M) = Σ_{p,q} h^{p,q} u^{p+q} ∈ ℤ[u]. The Hodge-Tate polynomial refines this. The topological Euler characteristic recovers as χ(M) = Σ (−1)^{p+q} h^{p,q} = χ_u(M)|_{u=−1}. The Hodge conjecture (one of the seven Clay Millennium Problems): for a smooth projective variety X over ℂ, every Hodge class — a cohomology class in H^{p,p}(X, ℚ) ⊂ H^{2p}(X, ℚ) — is the cohomology class of an algebraic cycle (a ℚ-linear combination of subvarieties of codimension p). The conjecture is known in codimension 1 (the Lefschetz (1,1)-theorem: every (1,1)-class is the Chern class of a line bundle) and in codimension n−1 (by Hard Lefschetz and Poincaré duality). It is open in general. Cattani-Deligne-Kaplan proved the Hodge conjecture for "typical" Hodge structures (those with largest possible Hodge numbers), showing it holds for very general varieties. The conjecture relates to the Tate conjecture in étale cohomology and to the Bloch-Beilinson filtration on algebraic K-theory, which proposes a K-theoretic refinement of the Hodge conjecture.
The Hodge conjecture — seeking to characterize algebraic cycles via cohomological conditions — remains one of the deepest open problems in algebraic geometry.

L² Cohomology and the Zucker Conjecture on Non‑Compact Kähler Manifolds

On a complete Kähler manifold that is not compact, the ordinary de Rham cohomology no longer captures the analytic information carried by harmonic forms. The L² cohomology groups H⁽²⁾ᵏ(M) are defined as the cohomology of the complex of square‑integrable differential forms with respect to the Kähler metric. A fundamental result of Zucker (1979) and, independently, of Looijenga and Saper‑Stern identifies these groups with the intersection cohomology IH⁽ᵏ⁾(X) of a suitable projective compactification X of M, provided the compactification has normal‑crossing boundary divisors and the metric has Poincaré‑type growth near the boundary. The isomorphism H⁽²⁾ᵏ(M) ≅ IH⁽ᵏ⁾(X) is canonical and respects the mixed Hodge structures on both sides. Consequently, the Hodge decomposition extends to L² harmonic forms: each L² cohomology class contains a unique harmonic representative, and the Hodge numbers hp,q(2) = dim Hp,q(2)(M) satisfy the same symmetry and duality relations as in the compact case. This theory underpins the study of automorphic forms on locally symmetric varieties, where the L² cohomology computes the contribution of discrete series representations to the cohomology of arithmetic quotients.

Variation of Hodge Structure, Period Maps, and Griffiths Transversality

A variation of Hodge structure (VHS) over a smooth base S is a locally constant ℚ‑local system V together with a decreasing Hodge filtration F^• on the holomorphic vector bundle V⊗𝒪_S such that each fiber carries a pure Hodge structure of fixed weight and the filtration varies holomorphically. The period map Φ: S → ΓD sends a point s to the Hodge filtration on the fiber V_s, where D is the classifying period domain and Γ is the monodromy group. Griffiths transversality is the differential constraint dΦ(T_s S) ⊂ Fp-1/F^p ⊗ Ω¹_S, which follows from the fact that the Gauss‑Manin connection ∇ satisfies ∇(F^p) ⊂ Fp-1⊗Ω¹_S. This infinitesimal property forces the period map to be horizontal with respect to the natural homogeneous bundle structure on D. As a consequence, the image of Φ is an integral submanifold of the horizontal distribution, and its dimension is bounded by the rank of the Kodaira‑Spencer map. The theory of VHS provides the bridge between Hodge theory and moduli: the Torelli map for curves, the period map for K3 surfaces, and the construction of Shimura varieties all arise as period maps of specific variations of Hodge structure.

Worked Example: Griffiths Transversality for a Family of Elliptic Curves
Consider the Legendre family of elliptic curves E_λ: y² = x(x-1)(x-λ) over S = ℙ¹ {0,1,∞}. The first cohomology H¹(E_λ, ℂ) carries a weight‑1 Hodge structure with Hodge numbers h1,0=h0,1=1. Show that the period map Φ: S → ℍ/ SL₂(ℤ) satisfies Griffiths transversality.
The holomorphic 1‑form ω_λ = dx/y spans H1,0(E_λ). The Gauss‑Manin connection ∇ acts on ω_λ by ∇(ω_λ) = η ⊗ dλ where η is a (0,1)-form representing the Kodaira‑Spencer class. Since ∇(F¹) ⊂ F⁰⊗Ω¹_S and F¹ = ⟨ω_λ⟩, the derivative of the period map lands in Hom(F¹, F⁰/F¹) ≅ ℂ, which is precisely the horizontal tangent space of the period domain ℍ. Thus dΦ(T_λ S) lies in the horizontal subspace, verifying Griffiths transversality.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the differential of a period map, first identify the Hodge filtration on the relevant cohomology, then write the Gauss‑Manin connection on a local frame of F^p. The transversality condition is exactly the statement that the connection does not increase the filtration degree. Remember that the period domain is a homogeneous space G_ℝ/V, and the horizontal distribution is the G_ℝ‑invariant subbundle associated to the Lie algebra grading.

Mixed Hodge Modules and Saito’s Decomposition Theorem

Mixed Hodge modules, introduced by Morihiko Saito in the late 1980s, provide a sheaf‑theoretic framework that simultaneously generalizes variations of Hodge structure, perverse sheaves, and the classical mixed Hodge structures on cohomology of algebraic varieties. A mixed Hodge module on a complex algebraic variety X is a triple (M, F^•, W_•) where M is a regular holonomic D‑module, F^• is the Hodge filtration, and W_• is the weight filtration, satisfying a list of axioms that include strictness of the filtered de Rham complex and compatibility with the Riemann‑Hilbert correspondence. Saito proved that the category of mixed Hodge modules is abelian and stable under the six Grothendieck operations (pushforward, pullback, tensor product, duality, etc.). The decomposition theorem for a proper morphism f: X → Y of algebraic varieties states that the direct image f_* ℚ_X[dim X] splits in the derived category of mixed Hodge modules as a direct sum of shifted intersection cohomology mixed Hodge modules of subvarieties of Y, each equipped with a pure Hodge structure of the appropriate weight. This result refines the topological decomposition theorem of Beĭlinson‑Bernstein‑Deligne‑Gabber by adding Hodge‑theoretic data, and it implies the hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge‑Riemann bilinear relations for intersection cohomology of projective varieties.

Hard Lefschetz, Standard Conjectures, and the Bloch–Beilinson Filtration

The hard Lefschetz theorem for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n asserts that cup product with the Kähler class ωn-k gives an isomorphism Ln-k: H^k(X, ℚ) → H2n-k(X, ℚ). Grothendieck’s standard conjectures predict that this isomorphism is induced by an algebraic cycle, i.e. that the Lefschetz operator L is algebraic. Conjecture B (the Lefschetz type standard conjecture) states that the inverse Λ of L is also given by an algebraic correspondence. If true, the projectors onto the primitive cohomology P^k = ker(Ln-k+1) would be algebraic, yielding a canonical decomposition of the motive of X into primitive pieces. The Bloch–Beilinson filtration on Chow groups CH^p(X) is a conjectural decreasing filtration F^ν CH^p(X) whose graded pieces are expected to be isomorphic to the extensions groups Ext2ν-1MHS(ℚ(0), H2p-ν(X, ℚ(p))) in the category of mixed Hodge structures. The first non‑trivial step F¹ CH^p(X) is the subgroup of homologically trivial cycles, and the Abel‑Jacobi map identifies its quotient by algebraic equivalence with the intermediate Jacobian J2p-1(X). The hard Lefschetz isomorphism, together with the Hodge‑Riemann bilinear relations, provides the necessary positivity to define the filtration and to prove its compatibility with the conjectural motivic t‑structure. Progress on these conjectures includes the work of Künnemann on the standard conjectures for abelian varieties and the recent advances of Voisin and others on the Bloch–Beilinson filtration for certain complete intersections.

  • Confusing L² cohomology with ordinary de Rham cohomology on non‑compact manifolds; the former requires square‑integrability and a complete metric.
  • Assuming the period map is holomorphic without checking Griffiths transversality; the horizontal condition is essential for the image to lie in the period domain.
  • Treating mixed Hodge modules as ordinary mixed Hodge structures; they carry a D‑module structure and are subject to the six functor formalism.
  • Believing the hard Lefschetz isomorphism is always induced by an algebraic cycle; this is precisely the content of the standard conjecture B, which remains open in general.
  • Neglecting the weight filtration when applying the decomposition theorem; the theorem splits the direct image in the category of mixed Hodge modules, not merely in the derived category of ℚ‑sheaves.
  • Overlooking the role of the Hodge‑Riemann bilinear relations in defining the Bloch–Beilinson filtration; positivity of the intersection form on primitive classes is the analytic input.
  • Misidentifying the intermediate Jacobian with the full Hodge structure; it captures only the extension data of weight −1 Hodge structures.

These advanced topics illustrate how Hodge theory extends beyond compact Kähler manifolds into non‑compact geometry, moduli, sheaf‑theoretic categories, and the deep conjectural landscape linking cycles, motives, and cohomology.

Degeneration of Hodge Structures and the Nilpotent Orbit Theorem

When a family of smooth projective varieties degenerates to a singular central fibre, the Hodge structures on the cohomology of the smooth fibres do not extend naively as pure Hodge structures. Instead they acquire a monodromy operator T acting on the integral cohomology of a nearby fibre. The logarithm N = log(Tu) of the unipotent part of T is a nilpotent endomorphism that satisfies the weight filtration property N(Wk) ⊂ Wk-2. Schmid’s nilpotent orbit theorem asserts that the period map, after composing with the exponential of zN, extends holomorphically across the puncture. The limiting mixed Hodge structure on the nearby fibre is then defined by the pair (F•lim, W•) where F•lim is the limit of the Hodge filtration and W• is the monodromy weight filtration centred at the weight of the original pure Hodge structure. This construction provides a canonical mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of the singular fibre in the case of a semistable degeneration, and it underlies the proof of the SL2-orbit theorem which refines the asymptotic behaviour of the period map.

The theorem has several concrete consequences. First, the Hodge numbers of the limiting mixed Hodge structure satisfy the same symmetry hp,q = hq,p as in the pure case, but now the weight filtration introduces graded pieces GrWk that carry pure Hodge structures of weight k. Second, the monodromy weight filtration is uniquely characterised by the conditions N(Wk) ⊂ Wk-2 and Nk: GrWn+k → GrWn-k being an isomorphism for all k ≥ 0. Third, the nilpotent orbit theorem guarantees that the period domain compactifies in a way compatible with the Hodge‑theoretic data, enabling the construction of the Kato–Usui log‑mixed Hodge structure on the boundary of moduli spaces. In practice, one computes the limiting Hodge filtration by taking a local lift of the period map to the universal cover, applying the exp(-zN) factor, and evaluating at the origin. The resulting filtration is independent of the choices and yields the canonical extension of the Hodge bundles across the degeneration.

Applications range from the study of degenerations of Calabi–Yau threefolds in mirror symmetry to the proof of the invariant cycle theorem, which identifies the invariant part of the cohomology under monodromy with the image of the restriction map from the total space. The nilpotent orbit theorem also provides the analytic input for the construction of the mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of an arbitrary complex algebraic variety via simplicial resolutions, because any variety can be embedded in a smooth variety and the complement can be resolved to a normal crossing divisor, producing a semistable degeneration after a suitable base change.

Intersection Cohomology and the Hodge Theory of Singular Projective Varieties

For a singular projective variety X of complex dimension n, ordinary cohomology fails to satisfy Poincaré duality and the hard Lefschetz theorem. Intersection cohomology IH•(X, ℚ), introduced by Goresky and MacPherson, restores these properties by allowing only those chains that meet the strata of a stratification in a controlled way. Deligne proved that IH•(X, ℚ) carries a pure Hodge structure of weight k on each degree k, and that the hard Lefschetz isomorphism Ln-k: IHk(X) → IH2n-k(X) holds for any ample class. The Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations also extend, giving a positive definite form on the primitive intersection cohomology Pk = ker(Ln-k+1).

The construction of the mixed Hodge structure on intersection cohomology proceeds via the decomposition theorem for a proper map f: Y → X with Y smooth. The direct image f*ℚY[dim Y] splits in the derived category of mixed Hodge modules as a direct sum of shifted intersection cohomology complexes of subvarieties of X, each equipped with a pure Hodge structure. This splitting is canonical up to isomorphism and respects the weight filtration. Consequently, the Hodge numbers hp,q(IHk(X)) are well defined and satisfy hp,q = hq,p. Moreover, the Lefschetz decomposition IHk = ⊕r≥0 LrPk-2r is orthogonal with respect to the Hodge–Riemann form.

A concrete illustration is the case of a projective variety with isolated singularities. Let X be a hypersurface in ℙn+1 with only ordinary double points. The intersection cohomology coincides with ordinary cohomology except in middle degree, where the vanishing cycles contribute a pure Hodge structure of weight n that is isomorphic to the primitive cohomology of the Milnor fibre. The Hodge numbers of this piece can be computed from the spectrum of the singularity, linking the Hodge theory of X to the mixed Hodge structure on the Milnor fibre. In higher‑dimensional singular strata, the decomposition theorem expresses the intersection cohomology of X in terms of the intersection cohomology of the strata and the vanishing cycles along the normal directions, providing a recursive method for calculating Hodge numbers of singular varieties.

Worked Example: Limiting Mixed Hodge Structure of a Nodal Family
Consider the one‑parameter family of plane curves Ct : y2 = x(x-1)(x-t) over the punctured disc Δ*. The central fibre C0 is a nodal rational curve. Compute the monodromy operator on H1(Ct, ℤ), determine the nilpotent operator N, and describe the limiting mixed Hodge structure on H1(Ct).
The smooth fibres are elliptic curves, so H1 has rank 2 with Hodge numbers h1,0=h0,1=1. The vanishing cycle is a simple loop around the node; its class δ generates the kernel of the specialization map. Monodromy acts as a Picard–Lefschetz transformation T(γ) = γ + (γ·δ)δ. In a symplectic basis {α, β} with α·β = 1 and δ = α, the matrix of T is [[1,1],[0,1]]. Hence N = log(T) = [[0,1],[0,0]], which satisfies N2=0. The monodromy weight filtration centred at weight 1 is W0=0, W1=ℚδ, W2=H1. The limiting Hodge filtration F1lim is the line spanned by the limit of the holomorphic 1‑form ωt = dx/y, which extends to a logarithmic form with residue along the node. One finds F1lim = ℚ·(β + log(t)·α) modulo W1. The graded pieces are GrW1 ≅ ℚ(-1) (pure of type (0,0) after Tate twist) and GrW2 ≅ ℚ(0) (pure of type (1,0)+(0,1)). This matches the general pattern: the limiting mixed Hodge structure is an extension of a pure Hodge structure of weight 2 by a Tate Hodge structure of weight 0.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the limiting mixed Hodge structure of a degeneration, first identify the vanishing cycles and compute the monodromy matrix in a symplectic basis. The nilpotent operator N is always the logarithm of the unipotent part; its nilpotency index determines the length of the weight filtration. Remember that the weight filtration is centred at the original weight, and the Hodge filtration limit is obtained by multiplying the period matrix by exp(-zN) and setting z=0. Practice the Picard–Lefschetz formula on simple nodal families; it appears frequently in both theoretical and computational problems.

p‑adic Hodge Theory: Comparison Isomorphisms and Period Rings

Over a p‑adic field K (a finite extension of ℚp), the étale cohomology Hkét(XK̄, ℚp) of a smooth proper variety X carries a continuous action of the absolute Galois group GK. The fundamental insight of p‑adic Hodge theory is that this Galois representation can be compared with algebraic de Rham cohomology HkdR(X/K) via a hierarchy of period rings. The ring BdR of de Rham periods, constructed by Fontaine, is a complete discrete valuation field with residue field ℂp. There is a canonical GK-equivariant isomorphism Hkét(XK̄, ℚp) ⊗ℚp BdR ≅ HkdR(X/K) ⊗K BdR that respects the Hodge filtration on the right and the natural filtration on BdR. This isomorphism identifies the Hodge–Tate weights of the Galois representation with the jumps of the Hodge filtration.

The ring Bcris of crystalline periods sits inside BdR and is equipped with a Frobenius endomorphism φ. For varieties with good reduction, the comparison isomorphism factors through Bcris, yielding a φ-module structure on Hkcris(Xk/W(k))[1/p] that recovers the crystalline cohomology of the special fibre. The filtered φ-module Dcris(V) = (V ⊗ Bcris)GK classifies crystalline representations. Fontaine’s conjecture, proved by Faltings and Tsuji, states that for any smooth proper X the representation Hkét(XK̄, ℚp) is de Rham, and it is crystalline precisely when X has good reduction. The semistable case is handled by the ring Bst, which adds a monodromy operator N satisfying Nφ = pφN. The associated filtered (φ,N)-module Dst(V) encodes the weight monodromy filtration, mirroring the complex nilpotent orbit theorem.

These comparison isomorphisms have profound arithmetic consequences. The Hodge–Tate decomposition Hkét(XK̄, ℚp) ⊗ ℂp ≅ ⊕i+j=k Hj(X, Ωi) ⊗K ℂp(-i) shows that the Hodge numbers of X are invariants of the Galois representation. Moreover, the Bloch–Kato exponential map relates the Selmer groups of V = Hkét(XK̄, ℚp) to the de Rham cohomology, providing a p‑adic analogue of the Abel–Jacobi map. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts that any irreducible p‑adic Galois representation of Gℚ that is unramified outside finitely many primes and de Rham at p comes from the étale cohomology of some algebraic variety. This conjecture sits at the intersection of the Langlands programme and Hodge theory, and its known cases (modular forms, abelian varieties) are proved using the machinery of p‑adic Hodge theory.

Hodge Theory and Mirror Symmetry: The SYZ Conjecture and Homological Mirror Symmetry

Mirror symmetry originated in string theory as a duality between two Calabi–Yau threefolds X and X̌ that exchange the Hodge numbers h1,1 and h2,1. The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture gives a geometric formulation: a Calabi–Yau manifold admits a special Lagrangian torus fibration π: X → B such that the mirror X̌ is obtained by dualising the fibres. The base B carries an integral affine structure away from a discriminant locus, and the complex structure of X̌ is encoded in the affine geometry of B together with instanton corrections from holomorphic discs. On the Hodge‑theoretic side, the SYZ picture predicts that the variation of Hodge structure on H3(X) is isomorphic to the variation of Hodge structure on H3(X̌) after exchanging the roles of the Hodge filtrations F• and the complex conjugate filtrations. This exchange is precisely the statement that the A‑model (symplectic) and B‑model (complex) topological string theories are equivalent.

Kontsevich’s homological mirror symmetry conjecture refines this to an equivalence of triangulated categories: the derived Fukaya category Fuk(X) of Lagrangian branes on X (with symplectic form ω) is equivalent to the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves DbCoh(X̌) on the mirror. The Hochschild homology of Fuk(X) recovers the quantum cohomology of X, while the Hochschild homology of DbCoh(X̌) recovers the Hodge cohomology ⊕ Hq(X̌, Ωp). The equivalence therefore implies an isomorphism between the quantum cohomology ring of X (deformed by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants) and the Hodge ring of X̌. In particular, the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of X compute the periods of the mirror family, providing a powerful enumerative tool.

Recent progress includes the work of Gross–Siebert, who constructed mirror pairs using toric degenerations and logarithmic geometry, proving the SYZ conjecture for a large class of Calabi–Yau varieties. Their programme builds a canonical scattering diagram on the base B whose walls correspond to tropical curves; the consistent scattering diagram yields the instanton‑corrected affine structure and the mirror complex manifold. On the categorical side, the equivalence has been established for toric Calabi–Yau manifolds (Abouzaid–Auroux–Katzarkov) and for certain hypersurfaces in toric varieties (Sheridan). These results rely heavily on the interplay between the Hodge theory of the mirror family (variation of Hodge structure, limiting mixed Hodge structures at large complex structure limit) and the symplectic topology of the original manifold (Floer theory, Lagrangian correspondences). The Hodge‑theoretic input is the identification of the large complex structure limit point in the moduli space of X̌ with a maximally unipotent monodromy point, where the nilpotent orbit theorem gives a canonical integral basis of H3(X̌) that matches the symplectic basis of H3(X) under mirror symmetry.

Arithmetic Hodge Theory: The Hodge–Tate Decomposition and the Fontaine–Mazur Conjecture

For a smooth proper variety X over a number field F, the ℓ‑adic étale cohomology groups Hkét(XF̄, ℚℓ) form a compatible system of Galois representations of GF. At a prime v of F above ℓ, the restriction to the decomposition group GFv is a p‑adic Galois representation to which p‑adic Hodge theory applies. The Hodge–Tate decomposition states that Hkét(XF̄, ℚℓ) ⊗ℚℓ ℂℓ ≅ ⊕i+j=k Hj(X, Ωi) ⊗F ℂℓ(-i), where ℂℓ is the completion of an algebraic closure of Fv. The integers i appearing are the Hodge–Tate weights, and their multiplicities are the Hodge numbers hi,j. This decomposition is a direct consequence of the de Rham comparison isomorphism after tensoring with ℂℓ and using the fact that BdR has a canonical surjection onto ℂℓ with kernel the maximal ideal.

The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture asserts that an irreducible p‑adic Galois representation ρ: GF → GLn(ℚp) which is unramified outside a finite set of places and de Rham at all places above p arises as a subquotient of the étale cohomology of some smooth projective variety over F. In other words, the geometric Galois representations are exactly those satisfying these local conditions. The conjecture is known for n=2 (modular forms) by the work of Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor, and Kisin, and for certain higher‑dimensional cases attached to Shimura varieties (Kisin, Caraiani–Scholze). The proof strategy uses p‑adic Hodge theory to construct a deformation ring parametrising lifts of a residual representation that are de Rham with prescribed Hodge–Tate weights, and then identifies this ring with a Hecke algebra via the Taylor–Wiles method.

A key technical tool is the notion of a Hodge–Tate regular representation: the Hodge–Tate weights are distinct. For such representations, the associated filtered φ-module Dcris(ρ) has a basis in which the filtration is split, and the Sen operator has simple eigenvalues. This regularity condition is essential for the construction of eigenvarieties and for the proof of the weight‑monodromy conjecture in the p‑adic setting. The weight‑monodromy conjecture, proved by Rapoport–Zink and later by Ito, predicts that the monodromy filtration on the Weil–Deligne representation attached to ρ|GFv coincides with the weight filtration coming from the geometry of a proper model of X over the ring of integers. Together, the Hodge–Tate decomposition, the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture, and the weight‑monodromy conjecture form a triad that links the Hodge theory of complex varieties, the p‑adic Hodge theory of varieties over p‑adic fields, and the global arithmetic of Galois representations over number fields.

  • Assuming intersection cohomology of a singular variety always coincides with ordinary cohomology; they differ precisely in degrees where the singularities contribute vanishing cycles.
  • Treating the period ring BdR as a field of characteristic zero without its filtration; the filtration is essential for recovering the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology.
  • Believing the SYZ conjecture is proved for all Calabi–Yau threefolds; it is known only for large classes via toric degenerations and logarithmic geometry.
  • Thinking the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture is a theorem in general; it remains open beyond the cases of modular forms and certain Shimura varieties.
  • Neglecting the distinction between crystalline and semistable representations; the monodromy operator N appears only in the semistable case and encodes the weight filtration.
  • These traps highlight the need to keep track of the precise hypotheses under which each comparison isomorphism or structural theorem holds.

Hodge Theory on Non‑Kähler Complex Manifolds

On a compact complex manifold that is not Kähler the Hodge decomposition fails in general: the space of harmonic forms with respect to a Hermitian metric does not represent the full de Rham cohomology. The Frölicher spectral sequence, which starts at the Dolbeault cohomology groups Hp,qbar∂(X) and converges to the de Rham cohomology Hp+qdR(X;ℂ), replaces the Hodge decomposition. Its E1 page is the Hodge diamond of Dolbeault groups, and the differentials d_r measure the obstruction to the ∂bar∂‑lemma. For a compact complex surface the spectral sequence always degenerates at E1, but for higher dimensions non‑trivial differentials can appear, as shown by examples of Iwasawa manifolds and certain nilmanifolds. The failure of the ∂bar∂‑lemma means that the Hodge numbers hp,q are not symmetric and the Hodge filtration on cohomology does not split canonically.

Nevertheless, several weaker structures survive. The Hodge filtration F^pH^k(X;ℂ) defined by the image of H^k(X;Ω≥p) in de Rham cohomology still yields a decreasing filtration satisfying Griffiths transversality for families. The associated graded pieces are the Dolbeault cohomology groups only after passing to the E_∞ page of the Frölicher spectral sequence. In the non‑Kähler setting the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations are replaced by the notion of a balanced metric (the (n‑1)‑st power of the fundamental form is closed) or by Gauduchon metrics (the (n‑1)‑st power is co‑closed). Gauduchon proved that every conformal class of Hermitian metrics contains a unique Gauduchon metric up to scaling, and this metric provides a Hodge star operator that yields a decomposition of forms into exact, co‑exact, and harmonic parts with respect to the Chern connection rather than the Levi‑Civita connection.

Recent work by Teleman, Buchdahl, and others has shown that for a large class of non‑Kähler manifolds — in particular for compact complex surfaces of class VII0 with b2>0 — the Frölicher spectral sequence degenerates at E1 and the Hodge numbers satisfy the same symmetries as in the Kähler case. The proof uses the existence of a Gauduchon metric together with a careful analysis of the ∂bar∂‑lemma on the level of currents. These results illustrate that the boundary between Kähler and non‑Kähler Hodge theory is not absolute; many formal consequences of the Kähler identities persist under weaker curvature assumptions.

The Hodge–de Rham Spectral Sequence and Degeneration Criteria

The Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence is the spectral sequence of the stupid filtration on the de Rham complex Ω^•_X of a smooth proper variety X over a field of characteristic zero. Its E1 page is E1p,q=H^q(X,Ω^p_X) and it converges to the algebraic de Rham cohomology Hp+qdR(X). Deligne–Illusie proved that if X lifts to a smooth scheme over the ring of Witt vectors W2(k) in characteristic p>dim X, then the spectral sequence degenerates at E1. In characteristic zero the degeneration follows from Hodge theory on a complex analytic space via the comparison isomorphism between algebraic and analytic de Rham cohomology. The degeneration is equivalent to the ∂bar∂‑lemma on the associated complex manifold and to the existence of a Hodge decomposition on cohomology with complex coefficients.

Several criteria guarantee degeneration without invoking a Kähler metric. The most classical is the existence of a hard Lefschetz isomorphism Ln‑k:H^k(X)→H2n‑k(X) for some ample line bundle, which forces the spectral sequence to collapse by a weight argument. Another criterion uses the existence of a pure Hodge structure on each cohomology group: if the Hodge filtration splits in the derived category of mixed Hodge structures, then the spectral sequence degenerates. In the relative setting, for a smooth proper morphism f:X→S, the degeneration of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence at E1 is equivalent to the local freeness of the higher direct images R^qf_*Ω^pX/S and the vanishing of the Kodaira–Spencer map in the appropriate degrees. This relative degeneration underlies the construction of the Gauss–Manin connection and the variation of Hodge structure on the base S.

Counterexamples in positive characteristic show that the hypotheses are sharp. Raynaud constructed a smooth proper surface in characteristic two whose Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence does not degenerate at E1 because the surface does not lift to W2(k). In characteristic zero, the only known obstructions to degeneration are the failure of the ∂bar∂‑lemma on the associated complex manifold, which is equivalent to the existence of non‑trivial Massey products in the de Rham algebra. Thus the degeneration problem is a bridge between algebraic geometry, complex analysis, and rational homotopy theory.

Worked Example: Degeneration on a Hypersurface
Let X⊂ℙ4 be a smooth quintic threefold. Show that the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence of X degenerates at E1 and compute the Hodge numbers hp,q for p+q=3.
The quintic threefold is a smooth projective Calabi–Yau variety of dimension three. By the Kodaira vanishing theorem, H^q(X,Ω^p_X)=0 for p+q<3 and p,q>0. The only possibly non‑zero Hodge numbers in degree three are h3,0=h0,3=1 (the holomorphic three‑form and its conjugate) and h2,1=h1,2=101 (computed via the Griffiths–Dwork method or by the formula h2,1= (5−1)4−1)/5 = 101). Therefore the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence also degenerates at E1, and the Hodge numbers are exactly the dimensions of the Dolbeault cohomology groups listed above.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When asked to prove degeneration of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence, first identify whether the variety is known to be Kähler or lifts to characteristic zero. If a Kähler metric exists, cite the classical Hodge theorem. If only a lift to W2(k) is given, invoke the Deligne–Illusie theorem. In positive characteristic, check the dimension bound p>dim X; otherwise degeneration may fail. Always state the precise hypothesis you are using.

Hodge Theory for Orbifolds and Stacks

Orbifolds (global quotients of manifolds by finite group actions) and more general Deligne–Mumford stacks admit a Hodge theory that parallels the smooth case. The de Rham complex of an orbifold is defined using invariant forms on the local uniformizing charts; its cohomology computes the orbifold cohomology, which carries a natural Hodge decomposition when the orbifold is Kähler. For a global quotient X=M/G with M a compact Kähler manifold and G a finite group acting holomorphically, the orbifold Hodge numbers are the dimensions of the G‑invariant parts of the usual Hodge groups: hp,qorb(X)=dim Hp,q(M)^G. The Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations hold on the invariant subspace, and the hard Lefschetz theorem follows from the G‑equivariant Lefschetz isomorphism on M.

For a general Deligne–Mumford stack 𝔛 with coarse moduli space X, one can define the Hodge filtration on the hypercohomology of the logarithmic de Rham complex of a smooth proper representable morphism 𝔛→S. The resulting mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of the fibres is functorial and satisfies the same formal properties as in the variety case: strict compatibility with pull‑backs, push‑forwards, and the six operations of Grothendieck. The key technical tool is the existence of a simplicial hypercover by smooth schemes, which allows one to reduce statements to the smooth case and then descend using the fact that the Hodge filtration is a local property for the smooth topology.

Applications include the computation of Hodge numbers of moduli stacks such as ℳ_g (the moduli stack of smooth curves) and ℳg,n (with marked points). The orbifold Hodge numbers of ℳ_g coincide with the Hodge numbers of the coarse moduli space M_g away from the boundary, but they differ on the boundary strata where stacky stabilisers appear. The theory also underlies the definition of the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of a stack, which is additive under cut‑and‑paste and multiplicative under products, extending the classical Hodge–Deligne polynomial of varieties.

Hodge Theory and the Classification of Algebraic Cycles

The Hodge conjecture predicts that every Hodge class on a smooth projective variety over ℂ is a ℚ‑linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles. While the full conjecture remains open, Hodge theory provides powerful tools for constructing and detecting algebraic cycles. The Abel–Jacobi map AJ:CH^phom(X)→J2p‑1(X) sends a homologically trivial cycle to its class in the intermediate Jacobian, which is a complex torus built from the Hodge structure on H2p‑1(X). The kernel of AJ is the group of cycles algebraically equivalent to zero, and its image is a countable union of translates of abelian subvarieties of the Jacobian. Griffiths showed that the infinitesimal invariant of the normal function associated to a family of cycles can be expressed in terms of the Hodge filtration, giving a criterion for a Hodge class to be algebraic in families.

Bloch–Beilinson conjectures predict a filtration on the Chow groups CH^p(X)⊗ℚ whose graded pieces are controlled by the Hodge structure on cohomology. The first step of this filtration is the kernel of the cycle class map to H2p(X,ℚ(p)), the second is the kernel of the Abel–Jacobi map, and higher steps are defined using higher normal functions. The existence of such a filtration would imply that the Hodge conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the cycle class map is surjective onto the Hodge classes. Recent work by Voisin, Nori, and others has constructed candidates for the Bloch–Beilinson filtration using mixed Hodge modules and the theory of motives, but a complete unconditional construction is still missing.

In practice, Hodge theory is used to prove special cases of the Hodge conjecture. For example, Lefschetz (1,1) theorem proves the conjecture for divisor classes (p=1). The hard Lefschetz theorem together with the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations proves the conjecture for codimension‑2 cycles on abelian varieties (by work of Weil, Murre, and others). For complete intersections in projective space, the conjecture is known up to the middle dimension by the work of Zucker and later by Otwinowska. In each case the proof combines the Hodge decomposition, the Lefschetz decomposition, and a careful analysis of the primitive cohomology.

Hodge Theory in the Context of Derived Categories

Derived categories of coherent sheaves D^b(Coh X) on a smooth projective variety X carry a natural Hodge‑theoretic structure via the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism HH_*(X)≅⊕p‑q=*H^q(X,Ω^p_X). The Hochschild homology groups inherit a Hodge decomposition from the right‑hand side, and the Connes operator B corresponds to the de Rham differential under this identification. The categorical version of the Hodge decomposition is the statement that the periodic cyclic homology HP_*(X) splits as a direct sum of the Hodge components, which is a theorem of Kontsevich and Soibelman for smooth proper dg‑categories over ℂ.

Bridgeland stability conditions on D^b(Coh X) provide a bridge between Hodge theory and the geometry of the derived category. A stability condition consists of a heart of a bounded t‑structure and a central charge Z:K0(X)→ℂ that factors through the Chern character and the Hodge filtration on cohomology. The space of stability conditions Stab(X) carries a natural complex manifold structure, and its connected components are expected to be related to the variation of Hodge structure on the cohomology of X. For K3 surfaces, Bridgeland proved that a connected component of Stab(X) is isomorphic to the universal cover of the period domain for weight‑two Hodge structures on H2(X,ℤ), providing a categorical realisation of the global Torelli theorem.

Recent developments in non‑commutative Hodge theory (Kaledin, Katzarkov–Kontsevich–Pantev) extend these ideas to smooth proper dg‑categories that are not necessarily geometric. The non‑commutative Hodge filtration on periodic cyclic homology satisfies the same formal properties as the classical Hodge filtration: it is a decreasing filtration, it is compatible with the Connes operator, and it degenerates at the E1 page of the associated spectral sequence. This framework allows one to define Hodge numbers for non‑commutative spaces and to formulate a non‑commutative Hodge conjecture, which predicts that the Hochschild homology classes coming from algebraic cycles generate the Hodge filtration. The theory has already produced new proofs of classical results, such as the degeneration of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence for smooth proper varieties in characteristic zero, by reducing them to categorical statements that are invariant under derived equivalence.

  • Confusing the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology with the Hodge decomposition; the filtration exists on any smooth proper variety, while the decomposition requires the ∂bar∂‑lemma or a Kähler metric.
  • Treating orbifold Hodge numbers as the ordinary Hodge numbers of a resolution; they are the invariant part under the group action and can differ from any crepant resolution.
  • Believing the Abel–Jacobi map is injective on homologically trivial cycles; its kernel is precisely the subgroup of cycles algebraically equivalent to zero, which can be large.
  • Thinking the Bloch–Beilinson filtration is known unconditionally for all smooth projective varieties; it is a conjectural filtration whose existence would imply the Hodge conjecture.

Period Domains, Mumford–Tate Groups, and the Classification of Polarized Hodge Structures

The period domain is a homogeneous complex manifold that parametrizes all polarized Hodge structures of a fixed weight and Hodge numbers on a given rational vector space. For weight k and Hodge numbers hp,q the domain is the quotient D = Gℝ/V, where G is the real algebraic group of automorphisms preserving the polarization form and V is a compact subgroup stabilizing a reference Hodge filtration. The Griffiths transversality condition asserts that the differential of a period map lands in the horizontal subbundle Thor ⊂ TD defined by the infinitesimal period relation Fp → Fp−1/Fp. The Mumford–Tate group MT(H) of a polarized Hodge structure H is the smallest ℚ‑algebraic subgroup of GL(Hℚ) whose real points contain the circle action defining the Hodge decomposition. It controls the Hodge tensors: a rational class lies in Hp,p ∩ Hℚ iff it is fixed by MT(H). The Hodge conjecture for a smooth projective variety X is equivalent to the statement that every Hodge class is a MT(H2p(X))‑invariant algebraic cycle. The classification of possible Mumford–Tate groups for a given weight and Hodge numbers is a deep problem; for weight 1 it yields the classification of abelian varieties with complex multiplication, while for weight 2 it relates to the classification of K3 surfaces and their periods. The global period map from a moduli space of polarized varieties to the quotient Γ\D (with Γ an arithmetic subgroup) is a holomorphic map whose image is a quasi‑projective variety by the Borel extension theorem. The monodromy group of a variation of Hodge structure is a subgroup of the Mumford–Tate group, and the Mumford–Tate conjecture predicts that the ℓ‑adic Galois representation attached to a motive has image whose Zariski closure equals the Mumford–Tate group. This conjecture is known for abelian varieties of CM type and for many Shimura varieties, linking Hodge theory to the Langlands program.

Logarithmic Hodge Theory and the Mixed Hodge Structure on the Complement of a Normal Crossing Divisor

Let X be a smooth projective variety and D = ∪i Di a divisor with simple normal crossings. The logarithmic de Rham complex Ω•X(log D) consists of differential forms with at most logarithmic poles along D. Deligne proved that the hypercohomology Hk(X \ D, ℚ) carries a canonical mixed Hodge structure whose weight filtration W• is induced by the pole order filtration on the logarithmic complex, and whose Hodge filtration F• is the naive filtration by degree. The associated graded pieces GrWm Hk(X \ D) are direct sums of pure Hodge structures of weight m coming from the strata DI = ∩i∈I Di. In particular, the weight‑k part is the image of the natural map Hk(X) → Hk(X \ D), while the weight‑k+1 part is built from residues along the components of D. The residue exact sequence 0 → Ω•X → Ω•X(log D) → ⊕i Ω•−1Di(log D|Di) → 0 yields a long exact sequence of mixed Hodge structures that computes the mixed Hodge numbers explicitly. For a curve C with punctures, the mixed Hodge structure on H1(C \ {points}) has weights 1 and 2; the weight‑2 part is generated by the classes of loops around the punctures and is of type (1,1). The logarithmic Hodge theory extends to the case where X is singular and D is a normal crossing divisor on a resolution, giving rise to the mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of arbitrary algebraic varieties via the simplicial resolution of Deligne. The theory of nearby and vanishing cycles provides a description of the limit mixed Hodge structure when the divisor D moves in a family, leading to the nilpotent orbit theorem and the SL(2)‑orbit theorem of Schmid.

Worked Example: Computing the Mixed Hodge Numbers of a Punctured Elliptic Curve
Let E be a smooth elliptic curve and U = E {p1, p2} the complement of two distinct points. Determine the mixed Hodge numbers hp,q(H1(U)).
The cohomology H1(U) fits into the exact sequence 0 → H1(E) → H1(U) → ⊕i=12 ℚ(−1) → 0, where the last term is the residue map. H1(E) is pure of weight 1 with Hodge numbers h1,0 = h0,1 = 1. Each ℚ(−1) is pure of weight 2 and type (1,1). Hence the mixed Hodge structure on H1(U) has weight filtration W0 = 0, W1 = H1(E), W2 = H1(U). The graded pieces are GrW1 ≅ H1(E) (type (1,0)+(0,1)) and GrW2 ≅ ℚ(−1)⊕ℚ(−1) (type (1,1) with multiplicity 2). Therefore the mixed Hodge numbers are: h1,0 = 1, h0,1 = 1, h1,1 = 2, all others zero.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for mixed Hodge numbers of a complement of a normal crossing divisor, first write the residue exact sequence, identify the pure pieces (cohomology of the ambient variety and of the strata), then read off the weights and Hodge types. The weight filtration is always induced by the pole order; the Hodge filtration is the naive one. This algorithm works for any smooth X and simple normal crossing D.

Hodge Theory on Shimura Varieties and the Langlands Program

Shimura varieties are higher‑dimensional analogues of modular curves; they are quasi‑projective varieties defined over number fields whose complex points are quotients of a Hermitian symmetric domain D by an arithmetic subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℚ). The domain D is a period domain for a family of polarized Hodge structures with a fixed Mumford–Tate group G. The universal family of abelian varieties (or more general motives) over a Shimura variety carries a variation of Hodge structure whose monodromy group is essentially the derived group of G. The Hodge filtration on the relative de Rham cohomology gives a vector bundle with a flat connection (the Gauss–Manin connection) satisfying Griffiths transversality. The action of the Hecke correspondences on the cohomology of Shimura varieties realizes the local Langlands correspondence for the group G: the ℓ‑adic étale cohomology groups decompose as direct sums of irreducible representations of G(𝔸f) ⊗ Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ). The Hodge–Tate decomposition of the p‑adic étale cohomology (via p‑adic Hodge theory) matches the Hodge decomposition on the complex side, providing a bridge between automorphic forms and Galois representations. The Kuga–Satake construction embeds the Hodge structure of a K3 surface into that of an abelian variety, showing that the period domain for polarized K3 surfaces is a Shimura variety of orthogonal type. The André–Oort conjecture (now a theorem of Pila–Tsimerman and others) asserts that special subvarieties of a Shimura variety are precisely those containing a Zariski‑dense set of CM points; its proof uses o‑minimality and the Ax–Schanuel theorem for period maps. The Hodge theory of Shimura varieties also underlies the construction of canonical models over reflex fields, the theory of Shimura’s reciprocity law, and the definition of motives attached to automorphic forms via the cohomology of Shimura varieties.

Non‑Archimedean Hodge Theory: Berkovich Spaces and the p‑adic Simpson Correspondence

Classical Hodge theory studies the cohomology of complex algebraic varieties using the Hodge decomposition and the Hodge filtration. In the p‑adic setting, one replaces the complex analytic space by a Berkovich analytic space Xan over a p‑adic field K. The de Rham cohomology of a smooth proper variety over K carries a Hodge filtration, while its p‑adic étale cohomology carries a Galois action. The fundamental comparison isomorphisms of p‑adic Hodge theory (Fontaine’s BdR, Bcris, Bst) relate these two cohomology theories. The p‑adic Simpson correspondence, developed by Faltings, Abbes–Gros, and later by Scholze, establishes an equivalence between the category of semistable Higgs bundles on X (with nilpotent Higgs field) and the category of ℚp‑local systems on Xan that become trivial on a finite étale cover. This is the p‑adic analogue of the classical Simpson correspondence between Higgs bundles and local systems on a complex manifold. The correspondence is constructed using the period sheaves BdR+ and the pro‑étale site of Scholze. The Hodge–Tate spectral sequence E1i,j = Hj(X, ΩiX(−i)) ⇒ Hi+jét(Xℚ̄, ℚp) degenerates at E2 for smooth proper varieties, giving the Hodge–Tate decomposition Hkét(Xℚ̄, ℚp) ≅ ⊕i+j=k Hj(X, ΩiX)(−i). The theory of prismatic cohomology (Bhatt–Scholze) provides a unified framework that recovers both crystalline and de Rham cohomology and yields a Hodge–Tate comparison for arbitrary p‑adic formal schemes. The non‑archimedean Hodge theory also includes the study of the Hodge filtration on the cohomology of rigid analytic varieties, the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules, and the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp).

Tropical Hodge Theory and the Combinatorial Hodge Decomposition for Polyhedral Complexes

Tropical geometry replaces algebraic varieties over a valued field by polyhedral complexes equipped with a weight function. The tropical Hodge theory, pioneered by Mikhalkin, Itenberg, Katzarkov, and others, assigns to a tropical variety a graded vector space that mimics the Hodge decomposition of a complex algebraic variety. For a smooth tropical hypersurface in ℝn defined by a tropical polynomial, the tropical homology groups Hktrop(X) are defined using the chain complex of polyhedral chains with coefficients in the sheaf of integral affine functions. These groups carry a bigrading (p,q) with p+q = k, and satisfy a tropical Hodge symmetry hp,q = hq,p. The tropical Hodge numbers coincide with the classical Hodge numbers of a degeneration of the corresponding algebraic variety when the degeneration is maximally degenerate (e.g., a toric degeneration). The tropical Lefschetz theorem holds for tropical manifolds that are tropicalizations of projective varieties: the tropical Lefschetz operator L (intersection with a tropical hyperplane) induces isomorphisms Lk : Hn−ktrop(X) → Hn+ktrop(X) for k ≤ n. The tropical Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations, proved by Adiprasito, Huh, and Katz for matroids and by Jell, Shaw, and others for tropical varieties, state that the intersection pairing on primitive tropical homology is positive definite up to a sign. This combinatorial Hodge theory has applications to the log‑concavity of characteristic polynomials of matroids, the proof of the Dowling–Wilson conjecture, and the study of the topology of real algebraic varieties via Viro’s patchworking. The tropical Hodge decomposition also appears in the work of Gross–Hacking–Keel on mirror symmetry for cluster varieties, where the tropicalization of a cluster variety carries a natural Hodge structure that matches the Hodge structure on the mirror side.

Hodge Theory on K3 Surfaces and Hyperkähler Manifolds

A K3 surface is a simply connected compact complex surface with trivial canonical bundle. Its Hodge diamond is completely determined: h0,0=1, h1,0=h0,1=0, h2,0=h0,2=1, and h1,1=20. The second cohomology H2(X,ℤ) carries a pure Hodge structure of weight 2 polarized by the intersection form, which is an even unimodular lattice of signature (3,19) isomorphic to U3 ⊕ E8(-1)2. The period domain for polarized K3 surfaces is the 20‑dimensional type IV domain D = { [ω] ∈ ℙ(H2(X,ℂ)) | (ω,ω)=0, (ω,bar ω)>0 }. The global Torelli theorem (Piatetski‑Shapiro–Shafarevich) states that the period map from the moduli space of marked K3 surfaces to D is an open embedding; two K3 surfaces are isomorphic iff their periods coincide up to the action of the orthogonal group O(H2(X,ℤ)). The Lefschetz (1,1) theorem implies that every integral Hodge class of type (1,1) is algebraic, so the Hodge conjecture holds for divisors on K3 surfaces. For higher codimension the conjecture is trivial because H4 is one‑dimensional and generated by the class of a point.

Hyperkähler manifolds (also called irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds) are higher‑dimensional analogues of K3 surfaces. They are simply connected compact Kähler manifolds with H0(X,Ω2) spanned by a nowhere degenerate holomorphic 2‑form. The Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki quadratic form q on H2(X,ℤ) extends the intersection pairing and satisfies the Fujiki relation ∫_X α2n = c_X q(α)^n for α ∈ H2. The Hodge numbers satisfy h2,0=1 and h1,0=0. The period domain is again a type IV domain of dimension h1,1-1. Verbitsky’s global Torelli theorem (later refined by Markman) asserts that the period map is injective on the moduli space of marked hyperkähler manifolds up to monodromy. The subalgebra generated by H2 in the full cohomology ring is the whole cohomology (Verbitsky), which implies that all Hodge classes on a hyperkähler manifold deformation equivalent to a Hilbert scheme of points on a K3 surface are algebraic. The Hodge conjecture for arbitrary hyperkähler manifolds remains open, but it is known for those of K3^[n]‑type and for generalized Kummer varieties.

The monodromy group of a hyperkähler manifold is a finite‑index subgroup of the orthogonal group O(H2(X,ℤ),q). This group acts on the period domain and its arithmetic quotients parametrize polarized hyperkähler manifolds. The Hodge structure on H2 determines the manifold up to isomorphism, and the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations for the Beauville–Bogomolov form give positivity on the primitive part of H1,1. These structures underpin the derived Torelli theorems and the study of autoequivalences of the derived category of coherent sheaves on hyperkähler manifolds.

Hodge Numbers of Hypersurfaces and Complete Intersections: The Griffiths–Dwork Method

For a smooth hypersurface X_d ⊂ ℙn+1 of degree d, the primitive middle cohomology H^nprim(X_d,ℂ) admits a concrete description via the Jacobian ring. Let F ∈ ℂ[x0,…,xn+1] be a homogeneous polynomial of degree d defining X_d. The Jacobian ring is R = ℂ[x0,…,xn+1]/(∂F/∂x0,…,∂F/∂xn+1), graded by polynomial degree. Griffiths showed that there is an isomorphism of Hodge structures Hn-p,pprim(X_d) ≅ R(p+1)d - (n+2) for 0 ≤ p ≤ n. Consequently the Hodge numbers are hp,n-p = dim_ℂ R(p+1)d - (n+2). For a complete intersection Xd1,…,d_k ⊂ ℙn+k the same principle applies: the primitive cohomology is identified with the graded pieces of the Jacobian ring of the ideal generated by the defining equations, and the Hodge filtration corresponds to the degree filtration shifted by the sum of the degrees.

The method yields explicit formulas. For a quintic threefold (n=3, d=5) in ℙ4, the relevant degrees are (p+1)·5 - 5 = 5p. Thus h3,0=dim R0=1, h2,1=dim R5=101, h1,2=dim R10=101, h0,3=dim R15=1. The Hodge numbers of a smooth hypersurface of degree d in ℙn+1 satisfy the symmetry hp,n-p=hn-p,p and the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem gives hp,q=hp,q(ℙn+1) for p+q ≠ n. The Griffiths–Dwork method also computes the infinitesimal variation of Hodge structure: the Kodaira–Spencer map T[X]ℳ → Hom(Hn,0,Hn-1,1) corresponds to multiplication by the class of the derivative of F in the Jacobian ring. This identification underlies the proof of the local Torelli theorem for hypersurfaces with d ≥ 3 and n ≥ 2.

For complete intersections the Jacobian ring is replaced by the quotient of the polynomial ring by the ideal generated by the partial derivatives of all defining equations. The Hodge numbers are then dimensions of graded pieces of this ring. The method extends to compute Yukawa couplings and the higher order terms of the period map. It also provides a practical algorithm for checking the Hodge conjecture in low degrees: the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem guarantees algebraicity of Hodge classes in H2, while for H4 of a threefold the conjecture is equivalent to the statement that every Hodge class is a linear combination of products of divisor classes. For Calabi–Yau threefolds arising as hypersurfaces in toric varieties, the mirror symmetry prediction for h1,1 and h2,1 can be verified by the same combinatorial technique.

Let F be a homogeneous polynomial of degree 5 defining X. The Jacobian ring is R = ℂ[x0,…,x4]/(∂F/∂x_i). For a threefold (n=3) the primitive Hodge numbers are hp,3-p = dim R(p+1)·5 - 5 = dim R5p. We need h1,1 and h2,1. Note that h1,1 is not primitive; it receives a contribution from the hyperplane class. The primitive part of H2 is zero because h2,0=0 for a threefold. Thus h1,1 = 1 (the class of a hyperplane section). For h2,1 we use p=1: h2,1 = dim R5. The space of degree‑5 polynomials in 5 variables has dimension C(5+5-1,5)=126. The partial derivatives ∂F/∂x_i are degree‑4 polynomials; they generate a subspace of degree‑5 polynomials of dimension at most 5·C(4+5-1,4)=5·70=350, but the actual number of independent linear relations among the degree‑5 monomials given by the Jacobian ideal is 25 (the number of monomials of degree 5 minus the dimension of the quotient). A standard count gives dim R5 = 101. Hence h2,1=101. The full Hodge diamond of a quintic threefold is h0,0=1, h1,0=h0,1=0, h2,0=h0,2=0, h3,0=h0,3=1, h1,1=1, h2,1=h1,2=101.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Hodge Number Computations
When asked for Hodge numbers of a hypersurface or complete intersection, first write down the degree shifts: for a hypersurface of degree d in ℙn+1, the primitive piece Hp,n-pprim corresponds to degree (p+1)d - (n+2) in the Jacobian ring. Remember that non‑primitive classes come from the ambient projective space: hp,p gets +1 for each p ≤ n from the hyperplane class. For complete intersections, sum the degrees of the defining equations and adjust the shift accordingly. Memorise the quintic threefold numbers (h1,1=1, h2,1=101) as a benchmark; they appear frequently in mirror symmetry questions. If a problem gives a specific polynomial, you do not need to compute the full Jacobian ring — use the fact that for a generic polynomial the Jacobian ideal has the expected codimension, so the dimension of the graded piece equals the dimension of the space of polynomials of that degree minus the number of independent relations, which is the number of monomials of degree (shift - d_i) for each equation.

Hodge Theory of Moduli Spaces of Curves: Tautological Classes and the Faber–Pandharipande Conjecture

The moduli space M_g of smooth projective curves of genus g carries a rich Hodge‑theoretic structure. Its rational cohomology H^*(M_g,ℚ) is endowed with a mixed Hodge structure whose weight filtration reflects the boundary stratification of the Deligne–Mumford compactification M_g. The tautological ring R^*(M_g) ⊂ H^*(M_g,ℚ) is generated by the kappa classes κ_i = π_*(ψi+1) (where π: C_g → M_g is the universal curve and ψ is the first Chern class of the relative dualizing sheaf) and the psi classes ψ_j on the universal curve. The Hodge bundle E = π_* ωC_g/M_g has Chern classes λ_i = c_i(E); these are also tautological. All tautological classes are of Hodge type (p,p), i.e., they lie in the (p,p)‑part of the Hodge decomposition of the pure graded pieces Gr^W2k H^k(M_g).

The weight filtration on H^k(M_g) satisfies Wk-1=0 and W2k=H^k. The graded pieces Gr^Wk+ℓ H^k are isomorphic to the cohomology of the boundary strata of codimension ℓ in M_g. This description, due to Hain and others, allows the computation of Hodge numbers for low genus. For g ≤ 6 the cohomology of M_g is concentrated in even degrees and the Hodge numbers are known explicitly: e.g., for g=3, h2,2=1, h3,3=1, etc. The Faber–Pandharipande conjecture (proved by Pixton, Pandharipande, and others) gives a complete set of relations among tautological classes in R^*(Mg,n). It states that the tautological ring is a Gorenstein algebra with socle in degree g-2 (for M_g) and that all relations are generated by the Pixton relations. This result has profound implications for the Hodge theory of M_g: it implies that the tautological ring injects into the cohomology of the universal curve and that the cycle class map from the Chow ring of M_g to its cohomology is injective on the tautological subring.

The mixed Hodge structure on H^*(M_g) is also linked to the representation theory of the mapping class group Γ_g. The action of Γ_g on H^*(M_g) preserves the weight and Hodge filtrations, and the Hodge numbers can be extracted from the Γ_g‑invariant part of the cohomology of the Torelli group. Recent work of Chan–Galatius–Payne uses tropical geometry to compute the top weight cohomology of M_g, which carries a pure Hodge structure of weight k. The Hodge numbers of M_g for g up to 6 have been tabulated; they exhibit a pattern where hp,q=0 unless p=q, confirming that the tautological classes exhaust the algebraic part of the cohomology in these genera. The Hodge conjecture for M_g (that every Hodge class is a linear combination of tautological classes) is known to be false in general — there exist non‑tautological Hodge classes in genus ≥ 7 — but the precise structure of the Hodge ring remains an active area of research.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of Hilbert Schemes of Points on Surfaces

For a smooth projective surface S, the Hilbert scheme S[n] of n points is a smooth projective variety of dimension 2n. Its cohomology carries a natural pure Hodge structure. The Nakajima operators create an action of the Heisenberg algebra on the direct sum ⊕n≥0 H^*(S[n],ℚ), which identifies this space with the Fock space of the Heisenberg algebra. Under this identification the Hodge structure on H^*(S[n]) corresponds to the Hodge structure on the symmetric product Sym^n(S) after resolving singularities. Göttsche’s formula gives the generating series of the Hodge polynomials: ∑n≥0 e(S[n]; u,v) t^n = ∏m≥1 (1 - tm-1 u v)-h0,0(S) (1 - t^m u v)-h1,0(S) (1 - t^m u v)-h0,1(S) (1 - tm+1 u v)-h2,0(S) (1 - tm+1 u v)-h0,2(S) (1 - t^m u2 v2)-h1,1(S). This formula determines all Hodge numbers of S[n] from those of S.

The second cohomology splits as H2(S[n]) ≅ H2(S) ⊕ ℚ δ, where δ is the class of the exceptional divisor (the locus of non‑reduced subschemes). The Beauville–Bogomolov form on H2(S[n]) is given by q(α + cδ) = (α,α)_S - 2(n-1)c2. When S is a K3 surface or an abelian surface, S[n] is a hyperkähler manifold of K3^[n]‑type or generalized Kummer type, respectively. The Hodge structure on H2(S[n]) is then of weight 2 with h2,0=1, h1,1=h1,1(S)+1, and the period domain is a type IV domain. The global Torelli theorem for these hyperkähler manifolds (Verbitsky, Markman) implies that the Hodge structure on H2 determines S[n] up to isomorphism. Moreover, the Hodge conjecture for S[n] is known when S is a K3 surface or an abelian surface: every Hodge class is algebraic, generated by divisor classes and the class of the diagonal in S × S. For a general surface S, the Hodge conjecture for S[n] is open, but the tautological subring generated by the Nakajima operators is known to consist of algebraic classes.

The mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of the punctual Hilbert scheme (the fiber of the Hilbert–Chow morphism S[n] → Sym^n(S) over the origin) has been studied by de Cataldo–Migliorini and others. It carries a non‑trivial weight filtration whose graded pieces are related to the cohomology of the Hilbert schemes of points on the affine plane. The Hodge numbers of the punctual Hilbert scheme are given by the q‑Catalan numbers and satisfy a curious symmetry. These results connect the Hodge theory of Hilbert schemes to the representation theory of the symmetric group and to the geometry of the Hilbert scheme of points on the plane, which is a central object in geometric representation theory and the study of Macdonald polynomials.

  • Assuming the Hodge numbers of a hypersurface are symmetric in all degrees; the symmetry hp,q=hq,p holds, but the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem forces hp,q=hp,q(ℙn+1) for p+q ≠ n, so many Hodge numbers are fixed and not computed by the Jacobian ring.
  • Confusing the primitive cohomology of a hypersurface with the full cohomology; the Jacobian ring gives only the primitive part, and one must add the ambient projective space contributions for the non‑primitive Hodge numbers.
  • Believing the global Torelli theorem holds for all hyperkähler manifolds without the marked condition; the period map is injective only on the moduli space of marked pairs (X,η) where η is an isometry H2(X,ℤ) → Λ.
  • Thinking the tautological ring of M_g equals the whole cohomology ring; for g ≥ 7 there are non‑tautological classes, and the Hodge conjecture for M_g fails in general.
  • Neglecting the weight filtration in the mixed Hodge structure of M_g; the pure part Gr^W2k H^k(M_g) is not the whole cohomology, and the boundary strata contribute lower weight pieces.
  • Assuming the Hodge structure on H2(S[n]) is always of K3 type; it is of K3 type only when S is a K3 surface or an abelian surface, otherwise the Beauville–Bogomolov form is not defined and the manifold is not hyperkähler.
  • Misapplying Göttsche’s formula by forgetting the shift in the exponents for the Hodge numbers of S; the factors (1 - tm-1uv) and (1 - tm+1uv) correspond to h0,0 and h2,0 respectively, and the exponents depend on the Hodge numbers of S.

Hodge Theory and the Non‑Abelian Hodge Correspondence

The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, established by Corlette, Donaldson, Hitchin, and Simpson in the late 1980s and early 1990s, extends classical Hodge theory from variations of Hodge structure (abelian objects) to Higgs bundles and flat connections (non‑abelian objects). On a smooth projective complex variety X, a Higgs bundle is a pair (E, θ) where E is a holomorphic vector bundle and θ ∈ H⁰(X, End(E) ⊗ Ω¹X) satisfies θ ∧ θ = 0. The Hitchin–Kobayashi correspondence identifies polystable Higgs bundles with vanishing Chern classes with irreducible flat connections. Simpson proved that the moduli space MDol of semistable Higgs bundles and the moduli space MB of semisimple representations of π₁(X) are homeomorphic as complex analytic spaces, though not biholomorphic. The diffeomorphism is constructed via harmonic metrics: a Higgs bundle admits a harmonic metric if and only if it is polystable, and the associated flat connection is ∇ = ∂E + θ + θ* + ∂̄E. The Hodge structure on the cohomology of MDol is mixed, with a weight filtration induced by the ℂ*-action scaling the Higgs field θ ↦ tθ. The associated graded pieces carry pure Hodge structures, and the Hodge numbers satisfy a curious symmetry hp,q = hq,p = hd-p,d-q where d = dim MDol. For curves, the Hitchin fibration MDol → ⊕ H⁰(C, Ki) is an algebraically completely integrable system; its generic fibers are abelian varieties (Prym varieties of spectral curves). The perverse filtration on H*(MDol) induced by the Hitchin map coincides with the weight filtration on H*(MB) under the non‑abelian Hodge diffeomorphism — this is the P = W conjecture, proved for GLn by de Cataldo, Hausel, and Migliorini (2012) and extended to all reductive groups by Maulik and Shen (2022). The cohomology of MDol carries a natural action of the Heisenberg algebra generated by Nakajima operators, and the Hodge numbers are given by the Macdonald polynomials evaluated at q = t = 1. For higher‑rank Higgs bundles on curves, the Hodge numbers of the moduli space are computed by the formula of Hausel and Rodriguez‑Villegas using the arithmetic of character varieties over finite fields and the Weil conjectures.

Hodge Theory on Moduli Spaces of Sheaves on K3 Surfaces and Hyperkähler Manifolds

Let S be a projective K3 surface and v = (r, c₁, s) ∈ H*(S, ℤ) a primitive Mukai vector with v² ≥ 2. The moduli space Mv(S) of Gieseker‑stable sheaves with Mukai vector v is a smooth projective hyperkähler manifold of dimension v² + 2, deformation equivalent to the Hilbert scheme S[n] with n = v²/2 + 1. The Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki quadratic form q on H²(Mv, ℤ) is determined by the Mukai pairing: for α, β ∈ v⊥ ⊂ H*(S, ℤ), q(α, β) = −(α, β)Mukai. The Hodge structure on H²(Mv) is of K3 type: h2,0 = 1, h1,1 = 20, and the period domain is the type IV domain D = {ω ∈ ℙ(v⊥ ⊗ ℂ) | (ω, ω) = 0, (ω, ω̄) > 0}. The global Torelli theorem (Verbitsky 2013, Markman 2011) states that the period map from the moduli space of marked pairs (Mv, η) to D is surjective and its fibers are the orbits of the monodromy group Mon²(Mv) ⊂ O(H²(Mv, ℤ), q). The Hodge conjecture for Mv is known: every Hodge class is algebraic, generated by divisor classes and the class of the universal family (when it exists) or by twisted sheaves. The Verbitsky component V ⊂ H*(Mv) is the subalgebra generated by H²; it is isomorphic to the quotient of Sym*(H²) by the ideal generated by the orthogonal complement of the Beauville–Bogomolov form. The Hodge structure on V is completely determined by the Hodge structure on H². For the full cohomology, the Looijenga–Lunts–Verbitsky Lie algebra 𝔤 = 𝔰𝔬(H² ⊕ ℂ²) acts on H*(Mv), and the Hodge decomposition is compatible with the 𝔤‑action. The Hodge numbers of Mv are given by the Göttsche–Soergel formula: the generating series of Hodge polynomials ∑ e(Mv; u, v) tv²/2+1 equals the product over m ≥ 1 of (1 − tm−1uv)−h0,0(1 − tmuv)−h1,1(1 − tm+1uv)−h2,0 with the Hodge numbers of the K3 surface. For generalized Kummer varieties Kn(A) (fiber of the Albanese map A[n+1] → A for an abelian surface A), the Hodge numbers are given by the same formula with h1,0(A) = h0,1(A) = 1, h2,0(A) = 1. The Hodge structure on H²(Kn) has h2,0 = 1, h1,1 = 7, and the period domain is again type IV. The Hodge conjecture for Kn is also known (Huybrechts, 2003). For hyperkähler manifolds of OG10 and OG6 type (O'Grady's examples), the Hodge numbers are known and the Hodge conjecture is open but the Verbitsky component consists of algebraic classes.

For a K3 surface, h0,0 = h2,2 = 1, h1,0 = h0,1 = 0, h2,0 = h0,2 = 1, h1,1 = 20. The generating series is ∑n≥0 e(S[n]; u,v) t^n = ∏m≥1 (1 - tm-1uv)-1 (1 - t^m uv)-20 (1 - tm+1uv)-1 (1 - t^m u2 v2)-1. For weight 4 (p+q=4): we use the fact that the Verbitsky component V in degree 4 is Sym2 H2 modulo the Beauville-Bogomolov relation. dim Sym2 H2 = 23·24/2 = 276. When a question asks about the Hodge structure on a moduli space of sheaves on a K3 surface or an abelian surface, immediately identify the deformation type (S[n] or K_n(A)). The Hodge numbers of weight 2 are always h2,0=1, h1,1=b2-2. For S[n], b2 = 23; for K_n(A), b2 = 7. The period domain is type IV. The global Torelli theorem holds for marked pairs. The Hodge conjecture is known for these deformation types. For OG10/OG6, the Hodge numbers are different (b2 = 24 for OG10, b2 = 8 for OG6) and the Hodge conjecture is open. Hodge Theory and the P = W Phenomenon

The P = W phenomenon, conjectured by de Cataldo, Hausel, and Migliorini (2012) and proved in full generality by Maulik and Shen (2022), asserts a deep coincidence between two filtrations on the cohomology of character varieties and Higgs bundle moduli spaces. Let X be a smooth projective complex variety and G a complex reductive group. The moduli space MB(X, G) of G-local systems (Betti moduli space) carries a mixed Hodge structure with weight filtration W•. The moduli space MDol(X, G) of G-Higgs bundles (Dolbeault moduli space) carries the Hitchin fibration h: MDol → A = ⊕ H⁰(X, KXdi) where di are the degrees of fundamental invariants of G. The perverse filtration P• on H*(MDol) is defined by Pk Hm = Im(Hm(A, pτ≤k−m Rh* ℚ) → Hm(MDol, ℚ)). The non‑abelian Hodge diffeomorphism MDol ≅ MB identifies the underlying topological spaces. The P = W conjecture states that under this identification, Pk Hm(MDol) = Wm+k Hm(MB) for all m, k. This equality implies that the mixed Hodge structure on MB is completely determined by the perverse filtration on MDol, which is algebraic and computable via the Hitchin fibration. The proof for GLn uses the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin map and the support theorem for the perverse sheaves pRih* ℚ. The key geometric input is that the Hitchin fibration is a Lagrangian fibration with respect to the natural holomorphic symplectic form on MDol, and its singular fibers are controlled by the nilpotent cone. The perverse filtration satisfies the relative hard Lefschetz theorem with respect to the class η = c₁(det E) ∈ H²(MDol), which corresponds to the weight grading on MB. The P = W phenomenon extends beyond character varieties: for the moduli space of parabolic Higgs bundles on a curve, the perverse filtration of the Hitchin map coincides with the weight filtration on the corresponding wild character variety. For the moduli space of sheaves on a K3 surface, the perverse filtration of the Lagrangian fibration (when it exists) coincides with the Beauville–Bogomolov filtration. The P = W conjecture has profound implications for the topology of character varieties: it implies the curious Poincaré duality hp,q = hd-p,d-q for the mixed Hodge numbers of MB, and it determines the E‑polynomial of MB in terms of the perverse Hodge numbers of MDol. The perverse Hodge numbers are computed by the Hausel–Rodriguez‑Villegas formula using the arithmetic of the moduli space over finite fields and the Weil conjectures.

Hodge Theory for Singularities: Steenbrink's Mixed Hodge Structure on Vanishing Cohomology

Let f: (ℂn+1, 0) → (ℂ, 0) be a holomorphic function germ with an isolated critical point at the origin. The Milnor fiber F = f⁻¹(δ) ∩ Bε (0 < |δ| ≪ ε ≪ 1) is a smooth n-dimensional manifold with the homotopy type of a bouquet of μ n-spheres, where μ = dim ℂ{x₀,…,xn}/(∂f/∂x₀,…,∂f/∂xn) is the Milnor number. The cohomology Hn(F, ℂ) carries a canonical mixed Hodge structure constructed by Steenbrink (1976) using the logarithmic de Rham complex of a good compactification of the Milnor fibration. The monodromy operator T: Hn(F) → Hn(F) is quasi‑unipotent; its semisimple part Ts has eigenvalues that are roots of unity, and its unipotent part N = log Tu is nilpotent of index ≤ n+1. The weight filtration W• on Hn(F) is the monodromy weight filtration centered at n: N(Wk) ⊂ Wk-2 and Nk: GrWn+k → GrWn-k is an isomorphism. The Hodge filtration F• is induced by the pole order filtration on the Brieskorn lattice H''₀ = Ωn+1/df ∧ dΩn-1. The spectrum of f is the set of rational numbers α ∈ ℚ such that GrFp GrWn+p+q Hn(F) ≠ 0 for α = p + q; it satisfies the symmetry α n − α. For a quasi‑homogeneous singularity with weights w₀,…,wn and degree d, the spectrum is {∑ (ki+1)wi/d | 0 ≤ ki < d/wi − 1}. The Hodge numbers hp,q = dim GrFp GrWp+q Hn(F) are determined by the spectrum. The mixed Hodge structure on the vanishing cohomology is polarizable: the intersection form S on Hn(F) satisfies S(Nkx, y) = (−1)k S(x, Nky) and the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations hold on the primitive parts. The limit mixed Hodge structure on the nearby cycles ψf ℚ is isomorphic to the mixed Hodge structure on Hn(F) with the monodromy action. Steenbrink's construction extends to non‑isolated singularities using the Du Bois complex and the filtered de Rham complex of a log resolution. The Hodge filtration on the vanishing cohomology is related to the Hodge filtration on the cohomology of the complement ℂn+1 f⁻¹(0) via the Thom–Sebastiani theorem: for f + g, the mixed Hodge structure is the convolution of the mixed Hodge structures of f and g. The spectrum determines the Hodge numbers of the Milnor fiber and the asymptotic behavior of the oscillatory integral ∫γ etf ω as t → ∞.

Hodge Theory and the Generalized Hodge Conjecture

The generalized Hodge conjecture (GHC), formulated by Grothendieck (1969), refines the classical Hodge conjecture by predicting the origin of Hodge substructures inside the cohomology of a smooth projective variety X. For a pure Hodge structure H of weight k, a Hodge substructure is a ℚ-subspace H' ⊂ H such that H' ⊗ ℂ = ⊕ H'p,q with H'p,q = H' ∩ Hp,q. The GHC states: for any Hodge substructure H' ⊂ Hk(X, ℚ), there exists a smooth projective variety Y and a morphism f: Y → X such that H' is a sub‑Hodge structure of the image of f*: Hk(X) → Hk(Y). Equivalently, the largest sub‑Hodge structure of Hk(X) supported on a closed algebraic subset Z ⊂ X of codimension c is exactly the image of Hk-2c(Z)(−c) → Hk(X). The GHC is known for abelian varieties (by the work of Weil, Tate, and others using the theory of abelian varieties and the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem), for K3 surfaces (where it follows from the global Torelli theorem and the surjectivity of the period map), and for hypersurfaces in projective space in certain degrees (by the work of Green, Voisin, and others using the Griffiths–Dwork method and the infinitesimal variation of Hodge structure). The GHC fails in general: there exist Hodge substructures not coming from algebraic cycles, as shown by the work of Zucker, Hain, and others on the cohomology of moduli spaces of curves. The level of a Hodge structure is the maximum of |p − q| for which Hp,q ≠ 0. The GHC implies that any Hodge substructure of level l is supported on a subvariety of codimension ≤ (k − l)/2. The coniveau filtration Nc Hk(X) = ∑codim Z ≥ c Ker(Hk(X) → Hk(X Z)) is the filtration by sub‑Hodge structures supported on codimension ≥ c subsets. The GHC is equivalent to the equality Nc Hk(X) = {x ∈ Hk(X) | x is a Hodge class of level ≤ k − 2c}. The Bloch–Beilinson filtration F• on Chow groups is conjecturally related to the coniveau filtration via the cycle class map. The GHC for a variety X implies the Hodge conjecture for all subvarieties of X and all self‑products X × ⋯ × X. The GHC is also related to the standard conjectures: the Lefschetz standard conjecture implies the GHC for abelian varieties. The variational Hodge conjecture, which concerns the locus in a family where a given Hodge class remains algebraic, is a stronger statement that implies the GHC.

  • Confusing the perverse filtration on the Dolbeault moduli space with the weight filtration on the Betti moduli space; they coincide under the non‑abelian Hodge diffeomorphism (P = W), but the diffeomorphism is not holomorphic and does not preserve the Hodge filtration.
  • Assuming the Hodge structure on the moduli space of sheaves on a K3 surface is always of K3 type; it is of K3 type only for the second cohomology, while higher cohomology carries a more complicated Hodge structure described by the Verbitsky component and the Looijenga–Lunts–Verbitsky Lie algebra action.
  • Misapplying Göttsche's formula by using the Hodge numbers of the surface without the correct exponent shifts: the factors (1 − tm−1uv), (1 − tmuv), (1 − tm+1uv) correspond to h0,0, h1,0+h0,1, h2,0+h0,2 respectively, and the exponent for (1 − tmu²v²) is h1,1.
  • Believing the generalized Hodge conjecture is equivalent to the classical Hodge conjecture; the GHC is strictly stronger and concerns the support of Hodge substructures, not just Hodge classes.
  • Neglecting the monodromy weight filtration in Steenbrink's mixed Hodge structure on vanishing cohomology; the weight filtration is centered at n and determined by the nilpotent operator N = log Tu, not by the geometry of the singularity alone.
  • Assuming the P = W phenomenon holds for all moduli spaces; it is proved for character varieties of curves and Higgs bundle moduli spaces, but for higher‑dimensional bases the perverse filtration of the Hitchin map is not defined in the same way.
  • Confusing the Hodge numbers of the Milnor fiber with the Hodge numbers of the singularity's resolution; the mixed Hodge structure on the vanishing cohomology is not the same as the mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of a resolution, though they are related by the Clemens–Schmid exact sequence.

Hodge Theory on the Moduli Space of Flat Connections and Character Varieties

The moduli space of flat GLn-connections on a compact Riemann surface Σ of genus g, denoted MdR(Σ, GLn), and the character variety MB(Σ, GLn) = Hom(π1(Σ), GLn)//GLn are linked by the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence. Simpson’s non‑abelian Hodge theorem equips both spaces with natural mixed Hodge structures that are canonically isomorphic after forgetting the Hodge filtration on the de Rham side and the weight filtration on the Betti side. The mixed Hodge structure on MB was constructed by Hausel and Thaddeus (2003) using the arithmetic harmonic analysis of the character variety over finite fields; the resulting Hodge–Deligne polynomial E(MB; u, v) satisfies the functional equation E(u, v) = (uv)d E(u−1, v−1) where d = 2(g−1)n2 + 2 is the complex dimension. For n = 2 and g = 2 the polynomial was computed explicitly: E = (uv)6 + 4(uv)5 + 10(uv)4 + 16(uv)3 + 19(uv)2 + 16uv + 10 + 4(uv)−1 + (uv)−2. The Hodge numbers hp,q of the compactly supported cohomology are read off from the coefficients; they exhibit the symmetry hp,q = hd−p,d−q predicted by Poincaré duality for the mixed Hodge structure. The weight filtration on Hkc(MB) is centered at k, and the graded pieces GrWk carry pure Hodge structures of weight k. On the de Rham side, the Hodge filtration arises from the Hodge filtration on the Higgs bundle moduli space MDol via the diffeomorphism MdR ≅ MDol. The P = W phenomenon, proved for character varieties by Hausel, Letellier, and Rodriguez‑Villegas (2011), identifies the perverse filtration on H•(MDol) induced by the Hitchin fibration with the weight filtration on H•(MB). This equality implies that the Hodge numbers of MB can be recovered from the topology of the Hitchin base and the geometry of the nilpotent cone. Recent work of Mellit (2017) gave a closed formula for the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of MB(Σ, GLn) for arbitrary n and g using the theory of Macdonald polynomials, confirming the curious Hard Lefschetz property for the perverse filtration.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the Du Bois Complex of Singular Varieties

For a separated scheme X of finite type over ℂ, the Du Bois complex ΩX• provides a functorial replacement of the algebraic de Rham complex that works for arbitrary singularities. It is constructed by taking a simplicial resolution π: X• → X with X• smooth and setting ΩX• = π*ΩX•• in the derived category. The stupid filtration σ≥pΩX• induces a Hodge filtration Fp on the hypercohomology ℍk(X, ΩX•). Du Bois (1981) proved that for a proper variety X the filtered complex (ΩX•, F) underlies a mixed Hodge structure on ℍk(X, ΩX•) which coincides with the classical mixed Hodge structure on Hk(X, ℚ) when X is smooth. The weight filtration W is defined via the simplicial structure; the associated graded pieces GrFpGrWk are isomorphic to the cohomology of the sheaves ΩXp of Kähler differentials on a log resolution. A variety X is called Du Bois if the natural map OX → ΩX• is a quasi‑isomorphism; equivalently, the Hodge filtration on H0(X, ΩX•) is trivial. Kovács and Schwede (2011) showed that Du Bois singularities are precisely those for which the Hodge numbers hp,q(X) = dim GrFpGrWp+qHp+q(X) agree with the Hodge numbers of a resolution. For a normal crossing divisor D ⊂ Y, the Du Bois complex of the pair (Y, D) is quasi‑isomorphic to the logarithmic de Rham complex ΩY•(log D), and the induced Hodge filtration matches the one defined by Deligne. The Du Bois complex also plays a central role in the construction of Saito’s mixed Hodge modules: the filtered de Rham complex of a mixed Hodge module is a filtered complex whose underlying complex is the Du Bois complex of the support. This compatibility allows one to compute the Hodge filtration on the cohomology of singular varieties arising as supports of mixed Hodge modules, for instance the intersection cohomology of projective varieties with rational singularities.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Numbers of the Moduli Space of Stable Maps

The moduli stack Mg,n(X, β) of stable maps from n‑pointed genus g curves to a smooth projective variety X with curve class β carries a virtual fundamental class [Mg,n(X, β)]vir of dimension ∫β c1(TX) + (dim X − 3)(1 − g) + n. The (orbifold) cohomology H•(Mg,n(X, β), ℚ) inherits a mixed Hodge structure from the fact that the stack is a proper Deligne–Mumford stack over ℂ; the Hodge filtration is defined via the Du Bois complex of a simplicial resolution. For X = pt the space reduces to Mg,n, whose Hodge numbers were computed by Getzler (1995) and later by Petersen–Tomasi (2014) using the theory of tautological rings. The Hodge diamond of Mg,n is concentrated on the diagonal p = q, reflecting the fact that its cohomology is generated by algebraic cycles (the tautological classes). For a Calabi–Yau threefold X, the virtual dimension is zero for g = 0, and the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants define the quantum cohomology ring QH•(X). The Hodge structure on H•(M0,n(X, β)) is expected to be pure of weight equal to the cohomological degree, and the Hodge numbers should be determined by the Hodge numbers of X together with the quantum product. In higher genus the virtual dimension is negative for Calabi–Yau threefolds, and the mixed Hodge structure on the virtual cohomology is not yet understood. The work of Ciocan‑Fontanine–Kim (2009) on the wall‑crossing formula for the virtual class suggests that the Hodge filtration on the virtual cohomology varies piecewise polynomially with the stability condition. For Fano varieties, the moduli space of stable maps is often a smooth projective variety (e.g., M0,0(ℙr, d) is a smooth projective variety of dimension (r+1)d + r − 3). Its Hodge numbers can be computed via the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem applied to the tangent bundle, which is a direct sum of line bundles on the Grassmannian of degree d maps. The Hodge numbers of M0,0(ℙr, d) are known to satisfy the Hodge symmetry and the Hard Lefschetz theorem with respect to the natural Kähler class coming from the determinant line bundle. The generalized Hodge conjecture for these moduli spaces remains open; it would imply that every Hodge substructure of Hk(M0,n(X, β)) is supported on a boundary stratum corresponding to a degeneracy locus of the evaluation maps.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Bundles

Let C be a smooth projective curve of genus g with marked points p1, …, pm and parabolic weights αi ∈ (0,1) at each point. The moduli space Mpar(C, r, d, α) of semistable parabolic bundles of rank r and degree d is a projective variety. Its non‑abelian Hodge counterpart is the moduli space Mpar‑Higgs of parabolic Higgs bundles, which carries a natural hyperkähler metric. The diffeomorphism Mpar ≅ Mpar‑Higgs (Biquard, 1997; Boalch, 2001) transports the Hodge filtration from the Dolbeault side to a mixed Hodge structure on the Betti side, the character variety with prescribed monodromy around the punctures. For rank 2, genus 0, and four marked points with generic weights, Mpar is a smooth affine surface of dimension 2. Its cohomology H1 carries a pure Hodge structure of weight 1 with Hodge numbers h1,0 = h0,1 = 1; H2 is pure of weight 2 with h2,0 = h0,2 = 0, h1,1 = 1. The mixed Hodge structure on the compactly supported cohomology is dual to this. Hausel and Mereb (2017) computed the Hodge–Deligne polynomial for arbitrary rank and genus 0 with m marked points using the arithmetic harmonic analysis of the corresponding character variety. The polynomial satisfies a functional equation reflecting the curious Hard Lefschetz property for the perverse filtration on the parabolic Hitchin fibration. The weight filtration on Hk(Mpar) is centered at k, and the graded pieces GrWk are pure Hodge structures of weight k. The perverse filtration on Hk(Mpar‑Higgs) induced by the parabolic Hitchin map coincides with the weight filtration under the non‑abelian Hodge diffeomorphism (the parabolic P = W theorem, proved by Mellit for genus 0 and by Hausel–Mellit for higher genus). This identification allows one to read off the Hodge numbers of Mpar from the topology of the parabolic Hitchin base, which is a vector space of dimension (r2−1)(g−1) + m(r−1). For g = 1, m = 1, rank 2, the moduli space is an elliptic surface; its Hodge numbers are h1,0 = h0,1 = 1, h2,0 = h0,2 = 1, h1,1 = 20, matching the Hodge diamond of a K3 surface, a fact that reflects the hyperkähler nature of the parabolic Higgs bundle moduli space in this case.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge–Deligne Polynomial in Motivic Hodge Theory

The Hodge–Deligne polynomial (or E‑polynomial) of a complex algebraic variety X is defined as e(X; u, v) = Σp,q,k (−1)k hp,q(Hkc(X)) up vq, where hp,q are the Hodge numbers of the mixed Hodge structure on compactly supported cohomology. This polynomial is a ring homomorphism from the Grothendieck ring of varieties K0(Varℂ) to ℤ[u, v], sending the class [X] to e(X; u, v). It factors through the Grothendieck ring of Hodge structures K0(HS), making it a motivic invariant. For a smooth projective variety, e(X; u, v) = Σ (−1)p+q hp,q(X) up vq coincides with the classical Hodge polynomial. The polynomial is additive with respect to stratifications: if X = Y ∪ Z with Y closed, then e(X) = e(Y) + e(Z Y). It also satisfies the Künneth formula e(X × Y) = e(X) e(Y). These properties allow the computation of e for moduli spaces by stratifying them into locally closed pieces whose classes are known in K0(Varℂ). For the moduli space of curves Mg,n, Getzler (1995) expressed its class in terms of tautological classes and computed e(Mg,n) for low genus. For character varieties, Hausel and Rodriguez‑Villegas (2008) used the arithmetic harmonic analysis of the corresponding moduli space over finite fields to obtain a closed formula for e(MB(Σg, GLn); u, v) as a plethystic exponential involving Macdonald polynomials. This formula reveals that the E‑polynomial is a polynomial in uv with palindromic coefficients, reflecting the Poincaré duality of the underlying mixed Hodge structure. The motivic nature of the E‑polynomial is essential for the P = W phenomenon: the equality of the perverse filtration on the Dolbeault moduli space and the weight filtration on the Betti moduli space implies that the mixed Hodge polynomial of the character variety equals the perverse Hodge polynomial of the Hitchin fibration. Katz (2015) used the motivic Hodge structure of the moduli space of curves to prove that the E‑polynomial of Mg,n satisfies the same functional equation as the E‑polynomial of the corresponding character variety, providing evidence for a deeper motivic correspondence between the two moduli problems. The Hodge–Deligne polynomial also appears in the study of the Hodge conjecture for moduli spaces: if a Hodge substructure of Hk(X) is generated by algebraic cycles, its E‑polynomial must be a polynomial with non‑negative coefficients when expressed in the basis of E‑polynomials of smooth projective varieties.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Vector Bundles on a Curve

The moduli space MC(r,d) of stable vector bundles of rank r and degree d on a smooth projective curve C of genus g ≥ 2 carries a natural Kähler metric induced by the Narasimhan–Seshadri correspondence with the moduli space of irreducible unitary representations of π1(C). This identification equips the rational cohomology H*(MC(r,d), ℚ) with a pure Hodge structure of weight equal to the cohomological degree. The Hodge numbers hp,q were first computed by Harder–Narasimhan (1975) using the Weil conjectures for the moduli stack over finite fields, and later by Atiyah–Bott (1983) via equivariant Morse theory on the space of connections. The resulting Poincaré polynomial satisfies the Hard Lefschetz theorem with respect to the natural ample class Θ (the determinant line bundle), and the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations hold on the primitive cohomology. Earl and Kirwan (2000) gave a complete description of the Hodge diamond for rank 2 and odd degree, showing that the only non‑zero Hodge numbers lie on the diagonal p = q, a phenomenon known as the “Hodge symmetry” of the moduli space. For higher rank, the Hodge numbers are not concentrated on the diagonal; the off‑diagonal terms reflect the non‑trivial mixed Hodge structure on the compactly supported cohomology of the moduli stack, as analysed by Hausel and Rodriguez‑Villegas (2008) using arithmetic harmonic analysis. The Hodge structure is also compatible with the action of the mapping class group on H*(MC(r,d)), which factors through the symplectic group Sp(2g, ℤ) and preserves the Hodge filtration.

  • Atiyah–Bott: equivariant perfect Morse stratification gives the Poincaré polynomial.
  • Earl–Kirwan: explicit Hodge diamond for rank 2, odd degree; diagonal concentration.
  • Hausel–Rodriguez‑Villegas: E‑polynomial for GLn via Macdonald polynomials; palindromic coefficients reflect Poincaré duality.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the Cyclic Homology of Smooth Algebraic Varieties

For a smooth algebraic variety X over a field of characteristic zero, the cyclic homology HC*(X) carries a natural Hodge filtration F•HC*(X) arising from the Connes–Tsygan spectral sequence. The Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg theorem identifies the Hochschild homology HH*(X) with the algebraic de Rham complex Ω*X, and the cyclic homology fits into the exact triangle HCn(X) → HHn(X) → HCn-2(X). The Hodge filtration on HC*(X) is defined by FpHCn(X) = Im(HCn(X, Ω≥p) → HCn(X)), where Ω≥p denotes the truncated de Rham complex. Feigin and Tsygan (1987) proved that the associated graded pieces satisfy grpFHCn(X) ≅ Hn-p(X, ΩpX) for n ≥ p, and vanish otherwise. This yields a Hodge decomposition HCn(X) ⊗ ℂ ≅ ⊕p+q=n Hq(X, ΩpX), which is compatible with the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology via the natural map HCn(X) → HndR(X). The degeneration of the Hodge‑to‑de Rham spectral sequence for smooth proper varieties (Deligne–Illusie, 1987) implies that the filtration splits canonically after tensoring with ℂ. Kontsevich’s formality theorem extends this picture to the cyclic homology of the category of coherent sheaves, where the Hodge filtration corresponds to the weight filtration on the Hochschild homology of the derived category. In the non‑commutative setting, the Hodge filtration on cyclic homology of a smooth and proper dg‑category provides a categorical analogue of the classical Hodge structure.

Key results

  • Feigin–Tsygan: grpFHCn ≅ Hn-p(X, Ωp).
  • Deligne–Illusie: degeneration of Hodge‑to‑de Rham spectral sequence in characteristic zero.
  • Kontsevich: formality and categorical Hodge filtration on Hochschild homology of DbCoh(X).

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Conjecture for Abelian Varieties

The Hodge conjecture predicts that every rational Hodge class on a smooth projective variety is a ℚ‑linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles. For abelian varieties, the conjecture is known in several important cases. Let A be an abelian variety of dimension g over ℂ. The Hodge structure on Hk(A, ℚ) is determined by the Mumford–Tate group MT(A) ⊂ GL(H1(A, ℚ)). A Hodge class in H2p(A, ℚ) corresponds to an MT(A)‑invariant vector in ∧2pH1(A, ℚ). Weil (1977) proved the conjecture for abelian varieties of CM type, where the Mumford–Tate group is a torus and all Hodge classes are generated by divisor classes. Tate (1966) showed that for abelian varieties over finite fields, the Tate conjecture (the ℓ‑adic analogue) implies the Hodge conjecture after base change to ℂ. Zarhin (1976) proved the conjecture for abelian varieties of dimension ≤ 4, and for products of elliptic curves. Hazama (1990) extended the result to abelian varieties whose Mumford–Tate group is a product of symplectic groups. The general case remains open; the main obstruction is the existence of exceptional Hodge classes not coming from algebraic cycles, which would correspond to non‑standard representations of the Mumford–Tate group. Deligne’s theory of absolute Hodge cycles (1982) shows that every Hodge class on an abelian variety is absolutely Hodge, but algebraicity is still not known in general. The conjecture for abelian varieties is equivalent to the statement that the Hodge ring of A (the subring of H*(A, ℚ) generated by Hodge classes) coincides with the ring of algebraic cycles modulo homological equivalence.

Key results

  • Weil: Hodge conjecture true for CM abelian varieties.
  • Tate: Tate conjecture over finite fields implies Hodge conjecture over ℂ.
  • Zarhin: proved for dim ≤ 4 and for products of elliptic curves.
  • Hazama: proved when MT(A) is a product of symplectic groups.
  • Deligne: all Hodge classes on abelian varieties are absolutely Hodge.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Numbers of the Moduli Space of Higgs Bundles on a Surface

Let S be a smooth projective surface and let MDol(S, r, c1, c2) be the moduli space of stable Higgs bundles of rank r with fixed Chern classes. Simpson’s non‑abelian Hodge correspondence (1994) provides a diffeomorphism between MDol and the Betti moduli space MB(S, r) of irreducible representations of π1(S) into GLr(ℂ). This diffeomorphism is not holomorphic, but it identifies the mixed Hodge structure on H*(MB) with the Hodge filtration on the hypercohomology of the Higgs complex on MDol. The Hodge numbers hp,q of MDol have been computed in several cases. For S = ℙ2 and rank 2, Hausel and Thaddeus (2003) used the Hitchin fibration and the P = W conjecture (proved for curves by de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini, 2012) to deduce that the Hodge polynomial equals the perverse Hodge polynomial of the Hitchin map. For K3 surfaces, the moduli space of Higgs bundles is hyperkähler, and its Hodge numbers are determined by the Beauville–Bogomolov form; the Hodge diamond coincides with that of the Hilbert scheme of points on the K3 surface (Markman, 2008). For general surfaces of general type, the Hodge numbers are not fully known, but the work of Maulik and Shen (2020) on the P = W phenomenon for surfaces shows that the perverse filtration on the Dolbeault side matches the weight filtration on the Betti side, giving a recursive algorithm for the Hodge numbers via the Hitchin base. The E‑polynomial e(MDol; u, v) is a polynomial in uv with palindromic coefficients, reflecting Poincaré duality of the underlying mixed Hodge structure.

Key results

  • Simpson: non‑abelian Hodge correspondence for surfaces.
  • Hausel–Thaddeus: Hodge polynomial for rank 2 on ℙ2 via P = W.
  • Markman: hyperkähler structure on Higgs moduli for K3 surfaces.
  • Maulik–Shen: P = W for surfaces, recursive computation of Hodge numbers.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Stable Maps to a Calabi‑Yau Threefold

Let X be a Calabi‑Yau threefold and let M̄g,n(X, β) be the moduli stack of stable maps of genus g with n marked points representing the curve class β ∈ H2(X, ℤ). The virtual fundamental class [M̄g,n(X, β)]vir ∈ Avd(M̄) defines a virtual cohomology H*vir(M̄) which carries a mixed Hodge structure constructed by Li and Tian (1998) using the perfect obstruction theory of Behrend–Fantechi (1997). The Hodge filtration on this virtual cohomology is induced by the Hodge filtration on the derived de Rham complex of the moduli stack. For g = 0, the virtual cohomology is pure of weight equal to the virtual dimension, and the Hodge numbers are concentrated on the diagonal p = q, a consequence of the fact that the virtual class is algebraic and the moduli space is a proper Deligne–Mumford stack with at worst finite quotient singularities. The Hodge numbers of M̄0,n(X, β) have been computed for X = ℙ3 and β = d[line] by Pandharipande (1999) using localization, and for the quintic threefold by Maulik and Pandharipande (2006) via the Gromov–Witten/Donaldson–Thomas correspondence. The generating function of the Hodge polynomials is a quasi‑modular form, reflecting the underlying integrable structure of the Gromov–Witten theory. For higher genus, the virtual cohomology is mixed, and the weight filtration is governed by the boundary stratification of the moduli space. The Hodge–Deligne polynomial e(M̄g,n(X, β); u, v) satisfies the same cut‑and‑join equations as the Gromov–Witten potentials, providing a Hodge‑theoretic refinement of the topological string partition function.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Numbers of the Moduli Space of Higgs Bundles on a Curve

Let C be a smooth projective curve of genus g ≥ 2 over ℂ and let MDol(r,d) denote the moduli space of semistable Higgs bundles of rank r and degree d with fixed determinant. The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence of Simpson identifies the underlying topological space of MDol with the character variety MB(r,d) of representations of π₁(C) into GLr(ℂ). This homeomorphism is not holomorphic; it intertwines the Hodge filtration on the Dolbeault side with the weight filtration on the Betti side only after the P = W identification proved by de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini for curves. Consequently the mixed Hodge structure on H*(MDol) is pure of weight equal to the cohomological degree, and the Hodge numbers satisfy hp,q = 0 unless p = q.

The Hodge polynomial (or E‑polynomial) e(MDol; u, v) = Σ (-1)k hp,q up vq has been computed explicitly for all ranks by Hausel and Rodriguez‑Villegas (2008) using the arithmetic harmonic analysis of the Hitchin fibration. The result is a product formula involving Macdonald polynomials; after setting u = v = t the polynomial becomes the Poincaré polynomial of the character variety. For rank 2 and odd degree the Hodge numbers are given by the coefficients of (1 + t²)2g-2 (1 + t⁴)g-1 … reflecting the cohomology of the Hitchin base and the generic fibre (an abelian variety of dimension (r²-1)(g-1)). The palindromic symmetry e(u,v) = (uv)dim e(u⁻¹,v⁻¹) follows from Poincaré duality on the pure Hodge structure.

When one passes from GLr to SLr the centre ℂ* acts freely on the stable locus, and the Hodge polynomial of the SLr moduli space is obtained by dividing the GLr polynomial by (uv - 1). This division removes the contribution of the central ℂ* factor and yields a polynomial with integer coefficients. The resulting Hodge numbers have been used to test the P = W conjecture on the Betti side, where the perverse filtration on H*(MB) induced by the Hitchin map matches the weight filtration after the non‑abelian Hodge diffeomorphism.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the Hochschild Homology of Smooth Varieties

For a smooth algebraic variety X over a field of characteristic zero, the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg (HKR) theorem provides a canonical isomorphism HHk(X) ≅ ⊕p-q=k Hq(X, ΩpX) between Hochschild homology and the Hodge components of algebraic de Rham cohomology. This isomorphism respects the Hodge filtration: the filtration FpHH*(X) induced by the stupid filtration on the Hochschild complex corresponds under HKR to the filtration ⊕i≥p Hq(X, ΩiX) on the right‑hand side. Consequently the Hodge‑to‑de‑Rham spectral sequence degenerates at E₁, and the Hodge filtration on Hochschild homology splits after tensoring with ℂ, yielding a pure Hodge structure of weight * on each graded piece.

The cyclic homology HC*(X) fits into Connes’ long exact sequence … → HHk → HCk → HCk-2 → HHk-1 → … . The Hodge filtration on cyclic homology is defined by the image of the Hodge filtration on Hochschild homology under the map HH → HC. For smooth proper varieties the resulting mixed Hodge structure on HC*(X) is pure of weight * and its Hodge numbers are determined by the Hodge numbers of X via the HKR decomposition. In particular, the Euler characteristic of the Hodge filtration on cyclic homology equals the topological Euler characteristic of X.

When X is singular, the HKR isomorphism fails, but the derived de Rham complex of Bhatt and the derived Hochschild homology of Toën–Vezzosi provide a replacement. The Hodge filtration on derived Hochschild homology recovers the Du Bois complex, and the associated graded pieces compute the Hodge cohomology of a resolution. This perspective underlies the proof of the degeneration of the Hodge‑to‑de‑Rham spectral sequence for proper varieties over ℂ via the comparison with periodic cyclic homology.

Technique: Computing Hodge Numbers via HKR
Let X be a smooth projective surface with Hodge numbers h0,0=1, h1,0=h0,1=2, h2,0=h0,2=1, h1,1=10. Determine the Hodge numbers of HH2(X) and HC2(X).
By HKR, HH2(X) ≅ H2(X, O_X) ⊕ H1(X, Ω1_X) ⊕ H0(X, Ω2_X). The summands have dimensions h0,2=1, h1,1=10, h2,0=1, so dim HH2 = 12. The Hodge filtration F^p HH2 is: F2 = H0(Ω2) (dimension 1), F1 = H0(Ω2) ⊕ H1(Ω1) (dimension 11), F0 = HH2 (dimension 12). For cyclic homology, the Connes sequence gives HC2 ≅ HH2 ⊕ HC0. HC0 ≅ H0(X, O_X) = ℂ (dimension 1). Hence dim HC2 = 13. The Hodge filtration on HC2 is induced from HH2, so F2 HC2 = F2 HH2 (dim 1), F1 HC2 = F1 HH2 (dim 11), F0 HC2 = HC2 (dim 13). The Hodge numbers hp,q of HC2 are therefore h2,0=1, h1,1=10, h0,2=1, plus an extra (0,0) from HC0 giving h0,0=1. All other hp,q=0.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for Hodge numbers of Hochschild or cyclic homology, first write down the HKR decomposition of HH_k as a direct sum of H^q(Ω^p) with p−q=k. Then read off the Hodge filtration: F^p HH_k = ⊕i≥p Hk+i(Ω^i). For cyclic homology, remember the exact sequence HC_k ≅ HH_k ⊕ HCk-2 (non‑canonically) and that the Hodge filtration on HC_k is the image of the filtration on HH_k. The extra summand HCk-2 contributes only in bidegree (0,0) after shifting. This algorithm avoids memorising case‑by‑case formulas.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Stable Sheaves on Calabi‑Yau Threefolds

Let X be a smooth projective Calabi‑Yau threefold (trivial canonical bundle, H¹(X, OX) = 0) and fix a numerical class v ∈ Heven(X, ℤ). The moduli space M(v) of Gieseker‑stable sheaves with Chern character v carries a symmetric perfect obstruction theory of virtual dimension zero, constructed by Thomas using the trace map Ext²(E,E) → H²(X, OX) ≅ ℂ. The associated virtual fundamental class [M(v)]vir ∈ A₀(M) defines a virtual cohomology H*vir(M) which, by work of Behrend–Fantechi and later of Maulik–Pandharipande, carries a mixed Hodge structure pure of weight *.

The Hodge filtration on virtual cohomology is induced by the Hodge filtration on the derived de Rham complex of the moduli stack, which in turn is compatible with the Hodge filtration on the obstruction theory. For zero‑dimensional virtual class the only non‑zero virtual cohomology group is H⁰vir, which is one‑dimensional and of Hodge type (0,0). When the virtual dimension is positive (e.g. for higher rank sheaves on a K3 surface viewed as a Calabi‑Yau twofold), the virtual cohomology is concentrated in middle degree and the Hodge numbers satisfy hp,q = 0 unless p = q, a consequence of the fact that the virtual class is algebraic and the obstruction theory is symmetric.

The Donaldson–Thomas (DT) partition function ZDT(q) = Σ χ(M(v), ν) qv (where ν is the Behrend function) is conjecturally equal to the topological string partition function. The Hodge‑theoretic refinement replaces the Euler characteristic χ by the Hodge–Deligne polynomial e(M(v); u, v). For local Calabi‑Yau threefolds such as the resolved conifold, the refined DT invariants have been computed by Iqbal–Kozcaz–Vafa and shown to be symmetric polynomials in u, v satisfying the wall‑crossing formula of Kontsevich–Soibelman. The generating series of these refined invariants is a (mock) modular form, reflecting the underlying integrable structure of the BPS state counting.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Conjecture for Calabi‑Yau Varieties

The Hodge conjecture predicts that every rational Hodge class on a smooth projective variety is a ℚ‑linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles. For Calabi‑Yau varieties the conjecture has a particularly rich interplay with mirror symmetry and the structure of the intermediate Jacobian. On a K3 surface (Calabi‑Yau twofold) the conjecture is a theorem: the Néron–Severi group has rank ρ ≤ 20 and the transcendental lattice carries no Hodge classes beyond those generated by algebraic cycles; this follows from the global Torelli theorem and the surjectivity of the period map.

For Calabi‑Yau threefolds the situation is far less understood. The only general result is that Hodge classes of degree 2 (i.e. in H⁴) are algebraic when the threefold is a complete intersection in a projective space or a toric variety, by work of Voisin and later of Otwinowska. For degree 4 Hodge classes (in H⁶) the conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the intermediate Jacobian J²(X) = H³(X, ℂ)/(F²H³ ⊕ H³(X, ℤ)) is an abelian variety; this holds for all known examples but remains open in general. Counterexamples to the integral Hodge conjecture (due to Atiyah–Hirzebruch and later Voisin) show that torsion Hodge classes need not be algebraic, but the rational conjecture is untouched.

Mirror symmetry provides a powerful heuristic: the Hodge numbers of a Calabi‑Yau threefold X and its mirror Xˇ satisfy hp,q(X) = h3-p,q(Xˇ). The algebraic cycles on X correspond to special Lagrangian submanifolds on Xˇ, and the Hodge conjecture translates into a statement about the existence of enough special Lagrangians. In the context of the SYZ conjecture, the Hodge conjecture for X would follow from a suitable compactification of the moduli space of special Lagrangian torus fibrations on Xˇ. Recent work of Maulik–Shen on the P = W phenomenon for the Hitchin system on a curve gives a parallel picture where the perverse filtration on the Dolbeault side matches the weight filtration on the Betti side, suggesting a deeper categorical framework that might eventually encompass the Calabi‑Yau threefold case.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the Algebraic K‑Theory of Smooth Varieties

For a smooth projective variety X over ℂ, the algebraic K‑theory groups Ki(X) carry a natural decreasing filtration called the gamma filtration γ•, defined via the lambda operations on K‑theory. The Chern character ch : Ki(X) ⊗ ℚ → ⊕p H2p−i(X, ℚ(p)) is an isomorphism of graded ℚ‑vector spaces (Riemann–Roch without denominators). Under this isomorphism the gamma filtration corresponds precisely to the Hodge filtration on the target: γpKi(X) ⊗ ℚ maps onto ⊕q≥p H2q−i(X, ℚ(q)). This identification, due to Bloch, Grayson and Soulé, realises the Hodge filtration as a motivic filtration on K‑theory. The associated graded pieces are the Adams eigenspaces for the Adams operations ψk, and the weight filtration on motivic cohomology (the Bloch–Ogus theory) matches the weight filtration on the Hodge side. In particular, for i=0 the gamma filtration on K0(X) recovers the coniveau filtration on the Chow groups, and the Hodge conjecture for X is equivalent to the statement that the cycle class map CHp(X) ⊗ ℚ → H2p(X, ℚ(p)) is surjective onto the Hodge classes. The Beilinson–Lichtenbaum conjecture (proved by Voevodsky, Rost, and others) identifies the motivic cohomology groups Hiℳ(X, ℚ(p)) with the higher Chow groups, and the Hodge filtration on these groups is induced by the gamma filtration on K‑theory via the isomorphism Ki(X)(p) ≅ H2p−iℳ(X, ℚ(p)). This framework underlies the construction of the motivic spectral sequence converging to algebraic K‑theory and explains the appearance of Hodge numbers in the ranks of K‑groups.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Connections

Let C be a smooth projective curve of genus g ≥ 2 with a reduced divisor D = Σ xi. A parabolic connection of rank r is a vector bundle E with a connection ∇ : E → E ⊗ Ω1C(D) having prescribed residues at the points of D. The moduli space MdR(r, α) of stable parabolic connections with fixed determinant and parabolic weights α is a quasi‑projective variety. By the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence for parabolic objects (Sabbah, Mochizuki), MdR is diffeomorphic to the moduli space MDol of stable parabolic Higgs bundles. The cohomology H•(MdR, ℚ) therefore carries a mixed Hodge structure transported from the Dolbeault side. The Hodge filtration F• on H•(MdR) is induced by the Hodge filtration on the Dolbeault moduli space, where the Higgs field provides a ℂ*‑action whose weight decomposition yields the Hodge grading. For generic weights the moduli space is smooth and the mixed Hodge structure is pure of weight equal to the cohomological degree; the Hodge numbers hp,q can be computed via the P = W phenomenon (de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini) which identifies the perverse filtration on the Hitchin fibration with the weight filtration on the Betti side. In rank two with four parabolic points on ℙ1, the moduli space is isomorphic to ℙ1 and the Hodge numbers are h0,0 = h1,1 = 1, all others zero. For higher rank the Hodge numbers are given by the Poincaré polynomials computed by Hausel–Rodriguez‑Villegas using the arithmetic harmonic analysis on the corresponding character varieties.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the Hochschild Homology of Singular Varieties

For a smooth variety X over ℂ, the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism identifies the Hochschild homology HHi(X) with the algebraic de Rham forms ΩiX/ℂ, and the Hodge filtration on HH• corresponds to the stupid filtration on the de Rham complex. For singular varieties this isomorphism fails, but the derived de Rham complex LΩX/ℂ (Illusie, Bhatt–Lurie) provides a replacement. The Hochschild homology of a singular X is canonically isomorphic to the derived de Rham homology, and the Hodge filtration on HH•(X) is defined as the filtration induced by the Hodge filtration on LΩX/ℂ. This filtration satisfies the expected properties: it is exhaustive, separated, and the associated graded pieces are the André–Quillen homology groups. For a variety with rational singularities, the Hodge‑to‑de Rham spectral sequence degenerates at E1 (Kovács–Schwede), and the Hodge filtration on HH• coincides with the filtration induced by the Du Bois complex. In the case of a hypersurface singularity defined by f = 0 in ℂn+1, the Hodge filtration on HH• can be computed via the Brieskorn lattice of the Milnor fibre, and the Hodge numbers are given by the spectrum of the singularity (Steenbrink). Recent work of Cortiñas–Haesemeyer–Schlichting–Weibel extends these results to the cyclic homology of singular schemes, showing that the Hodge filtration on cyclic homology is compatible with the Connes exact sequence and the Hodge filtration on Hochschild homology.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Conjecture for Hyperkähler Varieties

A compact hyperkähler manifold (irreducible holomorphic symplectic) X of dimension 2n carries a Beauville–Bogomolov quadratic form q on H2(X, ℤ). The Hodge conjecture for X predicts that every rational Hodge class in H2p(X, ℚ) is a linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles. For K3 surfaces (n=1) the conjecture is a theorem: the Néron–Severi group has rank ρ ≤ 20 and the transcendental lattice contains no Hodge classes beyond those generated by algebraic cycles (global Torelli). For Hilbert schemes X = S[n] of points on a K3 surface S, the conjecture was proved by Verbitsky (for n=2) and by Markman (all n) using the monodromy action on the Beauville–Bogomolov lattice and the surjectivity of the cycle class map on the Verbitsky component. For generalized Kummer varieties Kn(A) associated to an abelian surface A, Voisin proved the Hodge conjecture for all n by showing that the Hodge classes are generated by the divisor classes and the Chern classes of the tautological bundles. The remaining open cases include the two exceptional deformation types OG10 and OG6 constructed by O'Grady. For these, the Hodge conjecture is known only in low degrees: degree 2 (divisors) and degree 4 (by work of Mongardi–Saccà using the Lagrangian fibration structure). The generalized Hodge conjecture for hyperkähler varieties predicts that the coniveau filtration coincides with the filtration induced by the Beauville–Bogomolov form; this is known for the Verbitsky component but open in general.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the de Rham Complex of the Brieskorn Lattice for Isolated Hypersurface Singularities

Let f : (ℂn+1, 0) → (ℂ, 0) define an isolated hypersurface singularity. The Milnor fibre F = f−1(δ) ∩ Bε carries a mixed Hodge structure on its cohomology Hn(F, ℚ). The Brieskorn lattice H₀ = Ωn+1ℂn+1,0 / df ∧ dΩn−1ℂn+1,0 is a free ℂ[{t}]‑module of rank μ (the Milnor number) equipped with a connection ∇∂t = ∂t + (1/t) f. Steenbrink showed that the spectrum of the singularity, a set of rational numbers αi ∈ (0, n+1] with multiplicities, determines the Hodge numbers: hp,q = #{i | αi = p+1, q = n−p}. The monodromy operator T acts on H₀ and its semi‑simple part has eigenvalues exp(−2πi αi). The Hodge filtration satisfies the Griffiths transversality condition ∇∂t(Fp) ⊂ Fp−1 and the polarization is given by the intersection form on the Milnor lattice. For a quasi‑homogeneous singularity with weights w0, …, wn and degree d, the spectrum is { Σ (ki+1)wi/d | 0 ≤ ki ≤ d/wi−1 }. The Brieskorn lattice also underlies the construction of the Gauss–Manin connection on the cohomology of the Milnor fibre, and its irregular singularity at t = 0 reflects the mixed Hodge structure. Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the de Rham Complex of the Nilpotent Orbit and SL₂‑Orbit Theorems

Schmid’s nilpotent orbit theorem (1973) asserts that for a polarized variation of Hodge structure over a punctured disk, the period map is approximated by a nilpotent orbit exp(zN)·F∞ with error decaying exponentially in Im(z). The SL₂‑orbit theorem of Cattani, Kaplan and Schmid (1986) refines this: there exists a homomorphism φ : SL₂(ℝ) → Gℝ such that the period map is approximated by φ(exp(zN))·F∞ with polynomial error. The limiting mixed Hodge structure (H, W, F∞) is then a Hodge representation of this sl₂; the weight filtration W coincides with the monodromy weight filtration, and the Hodge filtration F∞ is the filtration induced by the eigenspaces of the semisimple element H = [N+, N−]. The SL₂‑orbit theorem implies that the period map extends to a holomorphic map on the compactified base after a suitable log‑blow‑up, a result used by Kashiwara and Kawai in the construction of the mixed Hodge module structure on the nearby cycles. The Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of the variation is the filtration by subbundles Fp = ⊕i≥p Hi,k−i; its associated graded pieces are the Hodge bundles Hp,q. Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the de Rham Complex of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Bundles

The moduli space Mpar(C, r, α) of rank r parabolic bundles on a smooth projective curve C with fixed determinant and a system of weights α is a smooth projective variety when the weights are generic. Its algebraic de Rham complex carries the Hodge filtration F• defined by the stupid filtration on the complex of algebraic differential forms. By the algebraic de Rham theorem of Grothendieck, the hypercohomology of this filtered complex computes the singular cohomology of Mpar with its pure Hodge structure of weight k on Hk(Mpar, ℚ). The associated graded pieces GrFpHk are canonically isomorphic to the Hodge cohomology groups Hk−p(Mpar, Ωp). When the weight system α is non‑generic, the moduli space acquires finite quotient singularities; the intersection cohomology complex IC(Mpar) then carries a mixed Hodge structure whose Hodge filtration is induced by the filtration on the Du Bois complex of the singular variety. Kirwan’s surjectivity theorem for the Kirwan map in equivariant cohomology, combined with the decomposition theorem of Beilinson‑Bernstein‑Deligne‑Gabber, shows that the Hodge filtration on the intersection cohomology coincides with the filtration induced by the perverse Leray filtration of the Hitchin‑type fibration on the associated parabolic Higgs bundle moduli space. The Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of Mpar can also be studied via the universal parabolic bundle ℰ on C × Mpar. The relative de Rham complex of ℰ along the projection to Mpar inherits a Hodge filtration from the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of C. Pushing forward yields a filtered complex on Mpar whose hypercohomology computes the Hodge structure on the cohomology of the fibres of the Hitchin map. The curvature formula of Griffiths applied to the Hodge bundles Hp,q shows that the Hodge metric has negative curvature in horizontal directions, reflecting the ampleness of the determinant line bundle det Fp. This positivity is used in the construction of the mixed Hodge module structure on the nearby cycles of the Hitchin fibration, as developed by Kashiwara and Kawai.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Bundles

For generic weights, the moduli space Mpar is smooth and projective, so its cohomology carries a pure Hodge structure. The Hodge numbers hp,q = dim Hq(Mpar, Ωp) satisfy the Hodge symmetry hp,q = hq,p and the Hard Lefschetz theorem with respect to the ample class of the determinant line bundle. The Hodge structure can be described explicitly using the Harder–Narasimhan stratification of the stack of all parabolic bundles. The stratification is indexed by Harder–Narasimhan types, and the cohomology of each stratum is a tensor product of cohomology of moduli spaces of semistable parabolic bundles of smaller rank. The associated spectral sequence degenerates at E2 because the strata are affine bundles over smooth projective bases, yielding a pure Hodge structure on the total space. This method, pioneered by Harder and Narasimhan for vector bundles and extended to parabolic bundles by Boden and Yokogawa, gives a recursive formula for the Hodge–Deligne polynomial E(Mpar; u, v) = Σ (−1)k hp,q up vq.

When the weights are non‑generic, the moduli space becomes a singular projective variety with finite quotient singularities. Its intersection cohomology carries a mixed Hodge structure whose weight filtration is the monodromy weight filtration of the nearby cycles of a smoothing family. The Hodge filtration on the intersection cohomology is the filtration induced by the Hodge filtration on the Du Bois complex. The mixed Hodge numbers can be computed via the decomposition theorem applied to the Kirwan resolution of the GIT quotient. The resulting mixed Hodge structure is pure in the middle degree and satisfies the Lefschetz decomposition with respect to the ample class. The purity of the middle intersection cohomology is a consequence of the fact that the Kirwan map is surjective in equivariant cohomology and the Hard Lefschetz theorem holds for the intersection cohomology of projective varieties with rational singularities.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Numbers of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Bundles

The Hodge numbers of Mpar(C, r, α) are known in closed form only for low rank and generic weights. For rank 2 and odd degree, the moduli space is a smooth projective variety whose cohomology is pure and the Hodge numbers are given by the Macdonald formula for the Hilbert scheme of points on the curve, as shown by Hausel and Rodriguez‑Villegas using the P = W conjecture proved for GL2 by de Cataldo, Hausel and Migliorini. The formula expresses the Hodge–Deligne polynomial as a plethystic exponential of the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of the curve. For higher rank, the Hodge numbers are not known in closed form, but the P = W conjecture (now a theorem for GLr) implies that the perverse filtration on the cohomology of the character variety MB matches the weight filtration on Mpar. This identification allows one to compute the mixed Hodge numbers of Mpar from the topology of the Hitchin fibration on the parabolic Higgs bundle moduli space. The fixed point loci of the ℂ*‑action scaling the Higgs field are products of moduli spaces of parabolic bundles and their cotangent bundles; their cohomology contributes to the associated graded pieces GrFp GrWk Hk(Mpar). The wall‑crossing formula of Kontsevich–Soibelman governs how the Hodge numbers change when the weight system α crosses a wall; the change is expressed in terms of the Hall algebra of the category of parabolic bundles.

For non‑generic weights, the moduli space is singular and its intersection cohomology carries a mixed Hodge structure. The mixed Hodge numbers can be extracted from the decomposition theorem applied to the Kirwan resolution. The weight filtration coincides with the perverse filtration of the Hitchin map on the associated parabolic Higgs bundle moduli space, and the Hodge filtration is induced by the Hodge filtration on the Dolbeault complex. The resulting mixed Hodge numbers satisfy the symmetry hp,q,k = hq,p,k and the Lefschetz decomposition with respect to the ample class. In the case of rank 2 and even degree, the moduli space has strictly semistable points and the intersection cohomology is not pure; the mixed Hodge numbers have been computed by Boden and Yokogawa using the Morse theory of the Yang–Mills functional.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the de Rham Complex of the Moduli Space of Higgs Bundles on a Surface

Let S be a smooth projective surface and MDol(S, r, c1, c2) the moduli space of rank r Higgs bundles with fixed Chern classes. This space is typically singular and non‑compact, but it carries a natural perfect obstruction theory and a virtual fundamental class. The algebraic de Rham complex of MDol is filtered by the Hodge filtration F• coming from the stupid filtration on the complex of differential forms on the ambient smooth stack. The hypercohomology of this filtered complex computes the virtual Hodge structure on the virtual cohomology groups Hkvir(MDol). The associated graded pieces are the virtual Hodge cohomology groups Hk−pvir(MDol, Ωpvir). When S is a K3 surface, the moduli space of Higgs bundles is holomorphic symplectic and its virtual cohomology coincides with the ordinary cohomology of a smooth compactification. In this case, the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex agrees with the Hodge filtration on the Dolbeault complex via the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, which identifies MDol with the character variety MB as a real analytic manifold. The Hodge numbers of the compactified moduli space of Higgs bundles on a K3 surface have been computed by Göttsche and Soergel using the orbifold Hodge theory of the Hilbert scheme of points on the surface.

For a general surface, the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of MDol is related to the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of the universal Higgs bundle on S × MDol. The relative de Rham complex of the universal bundle along the projection to MDol inherits a Hodge filtration from the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of S. Pushing forward yields a filtered complex whose hypercohomology computes the Hodge structure on the cohomology of the fibres of the Hitchin map. The curvature properties of the Hodge bundles are governed by the Griffiths curvature formula, which in the surface case involves the second Chern class of the universal bundle. The Hard Lefschetz theorem holds for the virtual cohomology with respect to the virtual ample class, and the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations are satisfied on the primitive virtual cohomology. These results are established using the theory of mixed Hodge modules on Artin stacks developed by Saito and the virtual fundamental class techniques of Behrend–Fantechi.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Higgs Bundles on a Surface

The cohomology of the moduli space MDol(S, r, c1, c2) carries a mixed Hodge structure when the space is singular or non‑compact. For a K3 surface, a smooth projective compactification exists (the moduli space of twisted sheaves), and its cohomology is pure. The Hodge numbers are determined by the Göttsche formula for the Hilbert scheme of points on the surface, together with the contribution of the Higgs field. The Hodge structure is of K3 type in the middle degree, and the Lefschetz decomposition holds with respect to the Beauville–Bogomolov form. For a general surface of general type, the moduli space is not compact and its cohomology is mixed. The weight filtration is the monodromy weight filtration of the nearby cycles of a proper degeneration, and the Hodge filtration is induced by the Hodge filtration on the Du Bois complex of a compactification with normal crossing boundary. The mixed Hodge numbers can be computed using the motivic Hall algebra of the category of Higgs sheaves on S, as developed by Joyce and Song. The wall‑crossing formula for the Hodge–Deligne polynomial when the stability condition varies is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing structure on the Hall algebra. In the case of rank 1, the moduli space is the cotangent bundle of the Picard variety, and its Hodge structure is the tensor product of the Hodge structure of the Picard variety with the Hodge structure of the affine space. For higher rank, the mixed Hodge structure is not pure, but the associated graded pieces with respect to the weight filtration are pure Hodge structures of known weights, and the Hodge filtration on each graded piece is induced by the Hodge filtration on the Dolbeault complex of the corresponding fixed point locus of the ℂ*‑action.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Structure on the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Local Systems on a Curve

The Betti moduli space MB(C, r) parametrises semisimple rank‑r local systems on a smooth projective curve C over ℂ. Via the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence it is diffeomorphic to the Dolbeault moduli space MDol(C, r, 0) of semistable Higgs bundles with vanishing Chern classes. This diffeomorphism is not holomorphic, but it identifies the natural weight filtration on the cohomology of MB with the perverse Leray filtration of the Hitchin map on MDol. The mixed Hodge structure on H⁎(MB, ℚ) is therefore pure of weight k in degree k; the Hodge numbers coincide with those of the corresponding Dolbeault moduli space. The purity follows from the decomposition theorem applied to the Hitchin fibration, together with the fact that the Hitchin map is proper and its generic fibre is an abelian variety. Consequently the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of MB can be computed by the same wall‑crossing techniques that apply to MDol, yielding explicit formulas in terms of the topology of the curve and the rank.

Weight and Hodge filtrations

The weight filtration W• on H⁎(MB) is the monodromy weight filtration of the nearby cycles of a one‑parameter degeneration of the curve to a nodal curve. The Hodge filtration F• is induced by the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of the universal local system over C × MB. The two filtrations are opposite in the sense of Deligne, giving a pure Hodge structure of weight k on each graded piece GrWkH⁎.

Relation to character varieties

When r = 1 the Betti moduli space is the Jacobian of C, whose cohomology carries the classical Hodge structure of an abelian variety. For higher rank the cohomology ring is generated by the universal characteristic classes of the universal local system; the Hodge numbers satisfy the curious Hard Lefschetz property with respect to the natural symplectic form on the character variety. This symplectic form is the Atiyah–Bott form, which corresponds under the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence to the hyperkähler form on MDol.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Numbers of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Higgs Bundles on a Curve

Let C be a smooth projective curve with a finite set of marked points D = {p₁,…,pn}. A parabolic Higgs bundle of rank r consists of a vector bundle E on C together with a flag of subspaces at each pi and a Higgs field φ : E → E ⊗ ΩC(D) preserving the flags. The moduli space Mpar(C, D, r, α) of semistable parabolic Higgs bundles with fixed weights α is a quasi‑projective variety carrying a natural mixed Hodge structure on its cohomology. When the weights are generic the space is smooth and the Hodge structure is pure; the Hodge numbers can be expressed in terms of the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of the underlying curve and the combinatorics of the flag types.

Wall‑crossing and the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula

As the weight system α varies, the moduli space undergoes birational transformations (flips) at walls where strictly semistable objects appear. The change in the Hodge–Deligne polynomial across a wall is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula applied to the motivic Hall algebra of parabolic Higgs sheaves. Explicitly, if α⁻ and α⁺ lie in adjacent chambers, then

E(Mpar(α⁺); u, v) = E(Mpar(α⁻); u, v) · ∏γ Ψ(γ)Ω(γ)

where the product runs over charge vectors γ that become semistable on the wall, Ω(γ) are the refined BPS invariants, and Ψ(γ) are the quantum dilogarithm factors. This formula determines the Hodge numbers recursively from the known Hodge numbers of the rank‑1 moduli space, which is a torus bundle over the parabolic Jacobian.

Fixed‑point localisation under the ℂ*‑action

The ℂ*‑action scaling the Higgs field has fixed points corresponding to parabolic bundles with nilpotent Higgs field. The Białynicki‑Birula decomposition yields a cell decomposition whose cells are affine spaces; the dimensions of these cells give the weights of the Hodge filtration. The associated graded pieces of the weight filtration are pure Hodge structures whose Hodge numbers are computed by the fixed‑point contributions. In particular, the middle cohomology carries a Hodge structure of weight equal to the complex dimension of the moduli space, and the Hard Lefschetz theorem holds with respect to the natural ample class coming from the determinant line bundle.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the de Rham Complex of the Cyclic Homology of Singular Varieties

For a singular complex algebraic variety X, the cyclic homology HC•(X) carries a mixed Hodge structure constructed by Cortiñas, Haesemeyer, Schlichting and Weibel using the Du Bois complex ΩX•. The Hodge filtration on HC•(X) is induced by the stupid filtration on the de Rham complex of a simplicial resolution of X. Concretely, if X• → X is a proper hypercover by smooth varieties, the cyclic homology is the homology of the total complex of the cyclic bicomplex of the de Rham complexes ΩX••. The column filtration of this bicomplex yields the Hodge filtration F• on HC•(X); the associated graded pieces are the negative cyclic homology groups HC•⁻(X) which inherit pure Hodge structures of weight equal to the homological degree.

Comparison with the Du Bois complex

The Du Bois complex ΩX• computes the sheaf cohomology of the constant sheaf ℚ on X and carries a natural Hodge filtration. There is a canonical filtered quasi‑isomorphism between the cyclic homology complex and the Du Bois complex shifted by one degree, compatible with the Hodge filtrations. Consequently the Hodge numbers of HC•(X) coincide with the Hodge numbers of the Du Bois cohomology H⁎(X, ΩX•). In particular, for a variety with rational singularities the Hodge filtration on cyclic homology degenerates at E₁, and the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of HC•(X) equals the Hodge–Deligne polynomial of the intersection cohomology of a resolution.

Application to hypersurface singularities

Let f : (ℂn+1, 0) → (ℂ, 0) be an isolated hypersurface singularity with Milnor fibre F. The cyclic homology of the singularity is isomorphic to the reduced cohomology of F shifted by one. The Hodge filtration on HC• coincides with the Steenbrink Hodge filtration on the vanishing cohomology. The spectrum of the singularity, which encodes the Hodge numbers of the mixed Hodge structure on Hⁿ(F), can therefore be read off from the cyclic homology. This provides a purely algebraic method to compute the spectrum via the Brieskorn lattice and the Gauss–Manin connection.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Conjecture for Products of Curves

Let C₁,…, Cr be smooth projective curves over ℂ. The Hodge conjecture for the product X = C₁ × ⋯ × Cr asserts that every Hodge class in H²k(X, ℚ) is a ℚ‑linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles. By the Künneth decomposition, H⁎(X) ≅ ⊗i H⁎(Ci). A Hodge class of type (k, k) corresponds to a sum of tensor products of classes of type (ai, ai) on each factor with Σ ai = k. Since each curve has only Hodge classes in degrees 0 and 2 (the point class and the fundamental class), any Hodge class on X is a polynomial in the pullbacks of the point classes and the fundamental classes. These are manifestly algebraic, being represented by products of points and divisors. Therefore the Hodge conjecture holds for all products of curves.

Generalisation to abelian varieties

The same argument extends to products of abelian varieties, because the Hodge ring of an abelian variety is generated by divisor classes. For a product of arbitrary smooth projective varieties the conjecture remains open; the product of curves is the simplest non‑trivial case where the full Hodge ring is known to be algebraic.

Relation to the generalised Hodge conjecture

The generalised Hodge conjecture predicts that the largest sub‑Hodge structure of H⁎(X) supported on a proper closed subset is generated by algebraic cycles. For products of curves, any proper closed subset is a union of products of curves and points, and the statement follows from the ordinary Hodge conjecture on the factors, which is trivial for curves.

Hodge Theory and the Hodge Filtration on the de Rham Complex of the Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves

Let X be a smooth projective variety over ℂ. The derived category Dᵇ(Coh X) carries a natural Hochschild homology HH•(X) which, by the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism, is canonically isomorphic to ⊕p H⁎(X, ΩXp)[−p]. The Hodge filtration on HH•(X) is defined as the filtration induced by the stupid filtration on the de Rham complex of a dg‑enhancement of Dᵇ(Coh X). Concretely, if A is a smooth and proper dg‑algebra quasi‑equivalent to Dᵇ(Coh X), the cyclic bar complex of A computes HH•(X) and its column filtration yields the Hodge filtration F•. The associated graded pieces are the negative cyclic homology groups, which are isomorphic to the de Rham cohomology of X with a shift in Hodge degree.

Compatibility with the Mukai pairing

The Mukai pairing on HH•(X) is a non‑degenerate symmetric bilinear form of degree −dim X. Under the HKR isomorphism it corresponds to the Serre duality pairing on ⊕ H⁎(X, Ωp). The Hodge filtration is orthogonal with respect to the Mukai pairing up to a shift: F⁽p⁾ ⊥ F⁽q⁾ unless p + q = dim X. This orthogonality is a categorical analogue of the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations.

Deformation invariance

The filtered cyclic homology of a smooth and proper dg‑category is invariant under derived equivalences. Consequently the Hodge filtration on HH•(X) is a derived invariant of X. For K3 surfaces, this recovers the classical fact that the Hodge structure on H² is a derived invariant. For higher‑dimensional varieties, the Hodge filtration on Hochschild homology provides a finer invariant than the usual Hodge numbers of X.
