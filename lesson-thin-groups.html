Thin Groups & Superstrong Approximation — Thin Subgroups, Expanders & the Superstrong Approximation

Deep dive into Thin Groups & Superstrong Approximation — a thin subgroup Γ of an algebraic group G(ℤ) is a finitely generated Zariski-dense subgroup of infinite index (so it is not a congruence subgroup), the strong approximation theorem (the congruence completion of a Zariski-dense subgroup of SL_n(ℤ) maps onto SL_n(ℤ/mℤ) for all m), the superstrong approximation theorem (Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak): the Cayley graphs Cay(SL_n(ℤ/qℤ), S mod q) for a Zariski-dense subgroup Γ = ⟨S⟩ form an expander family as q varies over square-free integers, the affine sieve (the prime orbit counting and the saturation problem for thin orbits — determining which integers are represented by the norm form of a thin group), the classification of thin subgroups of SL_n(ℤ) (thinness vs. thickness, the ping-pong lemma for constructing free subgroups), property (τ) for congruence subgroups and the Selberg property (λ_1 ≥ 3/16 for congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ)), the representation theory of SL₂(ℤ/pℤ) and the Ramanujan bound for Cayley graphs (the Selberg–Kloosterman–Sarnak approach), and applications to Apollonian circle packings (the integrality of curvatures and the local–global principle).

Part 1

🧩 Thin Subgroups of Algebraic Groups

Algebraic Groups
Zariski Density, Thin vs. Congruence Subgroups, and the Tits Alternative
Let G be an algebraic group defined over ℚ (e.g., G = SL_n, Sp_{2n}, SO_n). A finitely generated subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) is Zariski-dense if its Zariski closure in G is G itself. Γ is thin if it is Zariski-dense in G but has infinite index in G(ℤ) — it is a non-arithmetic subgroup that is not a congruence subgroup. The congruence subgroup problem asks whether every finite-index subgroup of SL_n(ℤ) is a congruence subgroup (true for n ≥ 3 by Bass–Milnor–Serre, false for n = 2). Thin subgroups are those finite-index subgroups not containing a congruence subgroup — they are sparse but Zariski-dense. The Tits alternative: a finitely generated linear group either contains a free non-abelian subgroup or is virtually solvable. For thin subgroups, the free non-abelian case is generic. The ping-pong lemma gives a criterion for two matrices to generate a free group: find disjoint subsets A, B such that g(A) ⊂ B and h(B) ⊂ A. The Schottky construction: two hyperbolic matrices with disjoint attracting/repelling limit sets generate a free group. Thin subgroups are abundant: the Helfgott–Lindenstrauss–Tao product theorem in approximate groups shows that thin subgroups have a rich combinatorial structure.
Thin subgroups are Zariski-dense but sparse in the ambient arithmetic group — they are the non-arithmetic cousins of congruence subgroups, abundant and rich in structure.
Part 2

📐 Superstrong Approximation and Expander Graphs

Approximation
The Strong Approximation Theorem, Bourgain–Gamburd Expander Criterion, and Superstrong Approximation
Strong approximation: if Γ ⊂ SL_n(ℤ) is Zariski-dense, then the reduction map Γ → SL_n(ℤ/qℤ) is surjective for all sufficiently large square-free integers q (Nori–Weisfeiler theorem). The expansion coefficient of a finite graph X is h(X) = min_{|A|≤|V|/2} |∂A|/|A|. A sequence of q-regular graphs forms an expander family if h(X_n) ≥ ε > 0 uniformly. Superstrong approximation (Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak, 2005): if Γ = ⟨S⟩ ⊂ SL_n(ℤ) is Zariski-dense and the eigenvalues of each s ∈ S are all real (a technical condition eliminated by later work), then the Cayley graphs Cay(Γ_q, S_q) (where Γ_q = image of Γ mod q and S_q = image of S) form an expander family as q ranges over square-free integers. The proof uses the Bourgain–Gamburd sum-product theorem: a subset of SL₂(p) either has small growth or is essentially the whole group, plus Helfgott's product theorem in SL_n(p) and the flattening lemma. The expander mixing lemma relates the expansion of a Cayley graph to the spectral gap: λ_2 < 1 − ε implies expansion. The Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak construction (1987) gave explicit Ramanujan graphs using quaternion groups: Cayley graphs of PSL₂(q) with generating sets from the Selberg trace formula, achieving the optimal Ramanujan bound λ_2 ≤ 2√(d−1)/d.
Superstrong approximation shows that congruence quotients of Zariski-dense subgroups produce expander families — a deep connection between algebraic groups and graph theory.
Part 3

⚡ The Affine Sieve and Prime Orbit Counting

Sieve Theory
The Affine Linear Sieve, the Saturation Problem, and the Local–Global Conjecture
The affine sieve (Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak, 2006) studies arithmetic properties of thin orbits: given a finitely generated subgroup Γ ⊂ GL_n(ℚ) and a base point x₀ ∈ ℤ^n, the orbit O = Γ·x₀ is a set of integral vectors. Let f be an integral polynomial (or more generally a polynomial in several variables). The saturation problem asks: what are the prime values taken by f on the orbit O? The key result: for a Zariski-dense Γ and a non-constant polynomial f (square-free on the orbit), the set {f(v): v ∈ O} contains integers with arbitrarily many prime factors, and the number of primitive (square-free) integers in this set grows like cX/(log X)^{1−γ} for some γ > 0. The local–global conjecture: every integer satisfying local congruence conditions (congruences modulo all primes) appears in the primitive orbit — this is known for Apollonian circle packings (Bourgain–Kontorovich, 2010). The orbit counting function N_O(X) = #{v ∈ O: |v| ≤ X} grows like c_X^{δ} where δ is the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of Γ (the critical exponent of the Poincaré series). The Selberg zeta function Z_Γ(s) for Schottky groups encodes the spectral theory of the Laplacian on the hyperbolic surface ℍ²/Γ — the resonances are the zeros of Z_Γ(s).
The affine sieve extends classical sieve theory to thin groups, detecting primes and almost-primes in Zariski-dense orbits — with applications from circle packings to integral Cayley graphs.
Part 4

🔥 Property (τ), Selberg's Theorem, and Apollonian Packings

Spectral
Property (τ), the Selberg 3/16 Theorem, and Apollonian Circle Packings
Property (τ) for a group Γ with a family of finite-index subgroups Γ_q is the condition that the trivial representation is isolated in the unitary dual: there is a uniform spectral gap for the Laplacian on the quotients ℍ²/Γ_q. The Selberg theorem (1965): for congruence subgroups Γ(q) ⊂ SL₂(ℤ), the smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian on ℍ²/Γ(q) satisfies λ₁ ≥ 3/16 = 0.1875 (the Selberg 3/16 theorem). The Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture (Deligne) gives the optimal bound λ₁ ≥ 1/4. For thin subgroups, the spectral gap is given by the Patterson–Sullivan theory: the base eigenvalue δ(1−δ) of the Laplacian on the hyperbolic surface ℍ²/Γ is related to the Hausdorff dimension δ of the limit set. The Kloosterman sums K(a,b;c) = Σ_{x mod c, (x,c)=1} e^{2πi(ax + b\bar{x})/c} and the Kuznetsov formula relate the Selberg eigenvalue problem to bounds on Kloosterman sums. The Apollonian circle packing — the integral curvatures (1/a, 1/b, etc.) form a thin orbit under the Apollonian group (a Coxeter group generated by four reflections). The local–global principle for Apollonian packings (Bourgain–Kontorovich, 2011): every sufficiently large integer satisfying certain congruence conditions appears as a curvature in a given integral Apollonian packing. The Sarnak–Kontorovich theorem on positive density of prime curvatures: primes appear with positive density in the curvatures of the packing.
The interplay between spectral theory, number theory, and thin groups reaches its peak in Apollonian circle packings — a beautiful and accessible instance of deep mathematics.

The Bourgain–Gamburd Expansion Machine and Sum–Product Estimates in SL₂(𝔽ₚ)

The Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine, introduced in their 2008 Annals paper, provides a general framework for proving that Cayley graphs of finite simple groups of Lie type form expander families. The machine reduces the problem to three ingredients: (1) a classification of approximate subgroups via the product theorem (Helfgott’s growth theorem for SL₂(𝔽ₚ) and its extensions by Pyber–Szabó and Breuillard–Green–Tao), (2) a non-concentration estimate showing that random walks do not concentrate on proper subgroups, and (3) an initial spectral gap for the generating set at a bounded level. The sum–product phenomenon in 𝔽ₚ, first exploited by Bourgain–Katz–Tao (2004), underlies Helfgott’s product theorem: for any subset A ⊂ SL₂(𝔽ₚ) not contained in a proper subgroup, either |A³| ≥ |A|1+δ or A is nearly the whole group. This growth forces the random walk to equidistribute rapidly. The machine was originally applied to Zariski-dense subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) with the extra hypothesis that generators have real eigenvalues; later work by Varju (2012) and Salehi-Golsefidy–Varju (2012) removed this condition using the classification of approximate subgroups in all finite simple groups of Lie type. The expansion machine also yields quantitative bounds: the spectral gap depends only on the rank of the algebraic group and the size of the generating set, not on the prime p. This uniformity is crucial for the affine sieve, where one needs expansion modulo square-free integers q with many prime factors. The proof combines the flattening lemma (convolution powers of the random walk measure become flat) with the product theorem to show that the L²-norm decays exponentially until it reaches the uniform distribution. The method has since been adapted to other settings, including the expansion of Cayley graphs of Suzuki groups and Ree groups, and to the study of the diameter of finite simple groups.

Classification of Zariski-Dense Subgroups via the Breuillard–Green–Tao Theorem

The Breuillard–Green–Tao theorem (2011–2012) gives a complete structural description of approximate subgroups in arbitrary groups, which in turn yields a classification of Zariski-dense subgroups of linear algebraic groups over ℤ. An approximate subgroup is a symmetric set A containing the identity such that A² is covered by K translates of A. The theorem states that any K-approximate subgroup of a group G is controlled by a coset nilprogression of bounded rank and step, inside a subgroup of G that is virtually nilpotent. Applied to finite simple groups of Lie type, this implies the product theorem: if A ⊂ G is a K-approximate subgroup generating G, then either A is very small or A = G. For Zariski-dense subgroups Γ ⊂ SLₙ(ℤ), the theorem implies that the reduction modulo p, Γₚ, is either very small or equals SLₙ(𝔽ₚ) for all sufficiently large p. This is a key input for superstrong approximation: it shows that the congruence quotients Γ_q are as large as possible (the full group SLₙ(ℤ/qℤ) for square-free q) once q is coprime to a finite set of primes. The classification also leads to a description of all thin subgroups: they are precisely the subgroups that are not commensurable with an arithmetic lattice. In SL₂(ℤ), thin subgroups are exactly the non-elementary Fuchsian groups of the first kind with infinite covolume. In higher rank, Margulis’ superrigidity and the work of Venkataramana show that any Zariski-dense subgroup of a higher-rank simple algebraic group over ℚ is either arithmetic or thin, and the thin ones are precisely those that do not satisfy the congruence subgroup property. The Breuillard–Green–Tao theorem also provides a uniform version of the Tits alternative: any finitely generated linear group either is virtually solvable or contains a free subgroup, with quantitative bounds on the growth of the free subgroup. This has applications to the diameter of finite simple groups and to the expansion of random Cayley graphs.

Worked Example: Applying the Product Theorem to Expansion
Let Γ = ⟨S⟩ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) be a Zariski-dense subgroup generated by a symmetric set S of size k. Suppose we know that for every prime p, the reduction map Γ → SL₂(𝔽ₚ) is surjective. Explain how the Bourgain–Gamburd machine proves that the Cayley graphs Cay(SL₂(𝔽ₚ), Sₚ) form an expander family as p → ∞, and identify the role of Helfgott’s product theorem in the argument.
The Bourgain–Gamburd machine proceeds in three steps. First, by the classification of approximate subgroups (Helfgott’s product theorem for SL₂(𝔽ₚ)), any subset A ⊂ SL₂(𝔽ₚ) that is not contained in a proper subgroup satisfies |A³| ≥ |A|1+δ for some absolute δ > 0. Second, one shows that the random walk measure μₚ(ℓ) on SL₂(𝔽ₚ) (ℓ steps with generators Sₚ) does not concentrate on any proper subgroup: for any proper subgroup H < SL₂(𝔽ₚ), μₚ(ℓ)(H) ≤ |SL₂(𝔽ₚ)|-c for ℓ ≈ log|SL₂(𝔽ₚ)|. This non-concentration follows from the Zariski-density of Γ and the fact that proper subgroups of SL₂(𝔽ₚ) are either solvable or of bounded size. Third, the flattening lemma uses the product theorem to prove that the L²-norm of μₚ(ℓ) decays exponentially until it reaches the uniform distribution. Specifically, if ‖μₚ(ℓ)‖₂² ≥ |SL₂(𝔽ₚ)|-1+ε, then the support of μₚ(ℓ) is an approximate subgroup, so by the product theorem it must grow, forcing ‖μₚ(2ℓ)‖₂² ≤ ‖μₚ(ℓ)‖₂2+δ'. Iterating gives ‖μₚ(ℓ)‖₂² ≤ |SL₂(𝔽ₚ)|-1 after O(log|SL₂(𝔽ₚ)|) steps, which implies a uniform spectral gap. The surjectivity hypothesis ensures that the Cayley graph is connected and that the only proper subgroups to worry about are the structural ones (Borel, Cartan, etc.), which are handled by the non-concentration estimate. Thus the family is an expander family with expansion constant depending only on k, not on p.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing the Expansion Machine
When a question asks about superstrong approximation or expander families from thin groups, look for the three pillars: (1) product theorem / growth in finite simple groups, (2) non-concentration on proper subgroups (often via Zariski-density and the classification of maximal subgroups), (3) initial spectral gap or flattening lemma. If the problem gives a specific group like SL₂(ℤ) or SLₙ(ℤ), recall that Helfgott’s theorem (for SL₂) or the Breuillard–Green–Tao theorem (for higher rank) supplies the product theorem. The condition “generators have real eigenvalues” was a technical assumption in the original Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak paper but is no longer needed; mention this evolution if asked about historical development. For quantitative bounds, remember that the spectral gap depends only on the rank and the size of the generating set, not on the prime.

The Affine Sieve and the Zaremba Conjecture on Continued Fractions

The affine sieve, developed by Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak (2006), extends classical sieve methods to orbits of thin groups. A landmark application is the progress on Zaremba’s conjecture (1972), which asserts that every integer q ≥ 2 appears as the denominator of a finite continued fraction with all partial quotients bounded by an absolute constant (Zaremba conjectured the bound 5). The connection: the set of reduced fractions a/q with partial quotients ≤ M corresponds to the orbit of a thin subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) acting on the cusp at infinity. Specifically, the semigroup generated by the matrices (1 1; 0 1) and (1 0; 1 1) (or their powers up to M) produces all such continued fractions. The affine sieve applied to this orbit shows that the set of denominators q that appear has positive density in ℕ, and that every sufficiently large integer satisfying necessary congruence conditions appears (the local–global principle). Bourgain–Kontorovich (2011) proved that the set of denominators with partial quotients ≤ 50 has positive density, and Huang (2015) improved the bound to 5 for a set of density 1. The sieve works by combining the spectral gap for the thin group (obtained via superstrong approximation) with the combinatorial sieve of Brun or Selberg. The key analytic input is the expansion of the Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients, which gives a level of distribution for the orbit. The level of distribution determines the sieve dimension and the quality of the almost-prime results. For the Zaremba problem, the orbit is not Zariski-dense in SL₂ but in a proper algebraic subgroup; nevertheless, the expansion machine still applies because the group is a thin subgroup of a larger algebraic group. The affine sieve also yields results on the distribution of prime values in thin orbits, such as the theorem of Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak that the set of prime curvatures in an Apollonian packing has positive relative density among all curvatures.

Spectral Gap for Thin Groups via Salehi-Golsefidy and Varju

The work of Salehi-Golsefidy and Varju (2012) established superstrong approximation for all Zariski-dense subgroups of semisimple algebraic groups over ℤ, removing the real-eigenvalue condition and extending the result to arbitrary rank. Their proof uses the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups in finite simple groups of Lie type to obtain a uniform product theorem: for any finite simple group of Lie type G(𝔽_q) of bounded rank, any generating set either has small doubling or is the whole group. They then develop a “flattening lemma” for the random walk on G(𝔽_q) that works uniformly in q. The non-concentration estimate is derived from the fact that a Zariski-dense subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) has the property that its reduction modulo p is not contained in any proper algebraic subgroup of bounded complexity, except for finitely many p. This follows from the Larsen–Pink inequality and the classification of maximal subgroups of finite simple groups (the Aschbacher–Seitz theorem). The spectral gap they obtain is uniform over all square-free integers q coprime to a fixed finite set of primes. This uniformity is essential for the affine sieve, where one needs to sieve over moduli with many prime factors. The method also yields an explicit bound on the spectral gap in terms of the rank and the size of the generating set. Later work by Varju (2013) and others extended these results to the case where the generating set is not symmetric, and to the study of the diameter of finite simple groups. The Salehi-Golsefidy–Varju theorem is the definitive form of superstrong approximation: it shows that the congruence quotients of any Zariski-dense subgroup form an expander family, with no extra hypotheses. This result has been used to prove the local–global principle for integral quadratic forms in thin orbits (e.g., the work of Bourgain–Kontorovich on the Apollonian packing) and to establish the existence of expanders in all finite simple groups of Lie type.

Local–Global Principles for Integral Quadratic Forms and Thin Orbits

The local–global principle for a thin orbit asks whether every integer that is locally represented (i.e., satisfies all congruence conditions modulo prime powers) is actually represented globally by the orbit. For the Apollonian circle packing, the orbit is the set of curvatures under the action of the Apollonian group, a thin subgroup of O_Q(ℤ) where Q is the Descartes quadratic form. Bourgain–Kontorovich (2010) proved that every sufficiently large integer satisfying the necessary congruence conditions modulo 24 appears as a curvature. The proof combines the affine sieve with the spectral gap for the Apollonian group (which is a thin subgroup of a rank-3 orthogonal group). The congruence conditions arise from the fact that the Descartes form has discriminant 24, so the local obstructions are modulo 24. The affine sieve gives a lower bound for the number of representations of an integer n by the orbit, provided n is not excluded by local obstructions. The sieve dimension is related to the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of the thin group. For the Apollonian group, the Hausdorff dimension δ ≈ 1.30568, and the sieve dimension is 2δ/(2-δ) ≈ 3.7. The local–global principle has also been studied for other thin orbits, such as the orbit of a thin subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) acting on binary quadratic forms (the work of Fuchs–Sanders) and for the Zaremba problem on continued fractions. The general conjecture, due to Sarnak, is that for any Zariski-dense subgroup of an algebraic group defined over ℚ, the local–global principle holds for integral polynomials on the orbit, with at most finitely many exceptions. This is known in many cases thanks to the affine sieve and superstrong approximation, but remains open in general. The interplay between the spectral theory of the Laplacian on the associated hyperbolic manifold, the expansion of congruence quotients, and the combinatorial sieve is the hallmark of this area.

  • Trap 1: Confusing the product theorem (Helfgott) with the classification of approximate subgroups (Breuillard–Green–Tao). The product theorem is a special case for SL₂(𝔽ₚ); the classification works for all finite simple groups of Lie type.
  • Trap 2: Assuming the real-eigenvalue condition is still required for superstrong approximation. It was removed by Varju and Salehi-Golsefidy–Varju around 2012.
  • Trap 3: Thinking the affine sieve directly produces primes. It produces almost-primes (integers with a bounded number of prime factors); getting primes requires additional input (e.g., the work of Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak on the Apollonian packing uses the spectral gap to get positive density of primes).
  • Trap 4: Misidentifying the sieve dimension. It is not the Hausdorff dimension δ but a function of δ and the codimension of the polynomial. For a polynomial f on the orbit, the sieve dimension is typically (dimension of the variety) × δ / (codimension).
  • Trap 5: Believing that every Zariski-dense subgroup of SLₙ(ℤ) surjects onto SLₙ(𝔽ₚ) for all large p. This is true for n=2 (by strong approximation for SL₂) but for n≥3 it is a deep theorem (the Matthews–Vaserstein–Weisfeiler theorem) and requires the subgroup to be Zariski-dense and not contained in a proper normal subgroup.
  • Trap 6: Confusing the Selberg 3/16 theorem (for congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ)) with the spectral gap for thin groups. The thin group spectral gap is given by the Patterson–Sullivan exponent δ(1-δ), which can be smaller than 3/16.
  • Trap 7: Overlooking the role of the flattening lemma. It is the step that converts the product theorem into exponential decay of the L²-norm of the random walk measure; without it, one only gets polynomial mixing.

These advanced topics illustrate the deep unity between group theory, number theory, and spectral geometry that defines the modern theory of thin groups and superstrong approximation.

Patterson–Sullivan Measures and the Critical Exponent of Thin Groups

The Patterson–Sullivan construction attaches a family of conformal measures to the limit set of a discrete subgroup of a rank‑one Lie group. For a Zariski‑dense thin subgroup Γ of SO(n,1) or SU(n,1) the critical exponent δ(Γ) equals the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set Λ(Γ) and governs the asymptotic growth of orbit points: the number of γ∈Γ with ‖γ‖≤T grows like Tδ(Γ). When Γ is a lattice δ equals the topological dimension of the associated symmetric space, but for thin groups δ is strictly smaller, reflecting the fractal nature of Λ(Γ). The Patterson–Sullivan measure μ_x on the boundary satisfies the conformality relation dμ_y/dμ_x(ξ)=e-δ β_ξ(y,x) where β_ξ is the Busemann function. This measure is ergodic for the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of the quotient manifold ΓH^n, and its dimension δ appears as the exponent in the spectral gap for the Laplacian on the convex core: the bottom of the L2‑spectrum is δ(1−δ). In the context of superstrong approximation, δ controls the sieve dimension in the affine sieve; for the Apollonian packing δ≈1.30568 and the sieve dimension becomes 2δ/(2−δ)≈3.7. The Patterson–Sullivan measure also provides the stationary measure for the random walk on the boundary, linking the spectral theory of the Laplacian to the expansion properties of the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q).

The Flattening Lemma and Exponential Decay in the Bourgain–Gamburd Machine

The Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine reduces the proof that a family of Cayley graphs is an expander to three ingredients: a product theorem, a non‑concentration estimate, and a flattening lemma. The flattening lemma states that if a probability measure μ on a finite group G satisfies ‖μ‖2 ≤ |G|-1/2+ε then the convolution power μ*k becomes nearly uniform after O(log|G|) steps, with an explicit bound ‖μ*k−u‖2 ≤ |G|-1/2−c for some c>0. In the thin‑group setting one works with the random walk measure μ_p on the finite quotient SL2(𝔽_p) generated by the reductions of a fixed symmetric generating set of Γ. The product theorem of Helfgott guarantees that any subset A⊂SL2(𝔽_p) not contained in a proper subgroup grows under triple product: |A3| ≥ |A|1+δ. The non‑concentration estimate uses the spectral gap for the Laplacian on the associated hyperbolic manifold (or the property (τ) for Γ) to show that μ_p does not concentrate on any proper subgroup. Combining these, the flattening lemma yields exponential decay of the L2‑norm of μ_p*k, which is equivalent to a uniform spectral gap for the Cayley graphs. The lemma is quantitative: the exponent c depends only on the product‑theorem constant δ and the non‑concentration parameter. This mechanism was abstracted by Breuillard–Green–Tao to all finite simple groups of Lie type, where the product theorem is replaced by the classification of approximate subgroups.

Worked Example: Flattening Lemma Application
Let Γ⊂SL2(ℤ) be a Zariski‑dense subgroup generated by two matrices. For a prime p, let μ_p be the uniform measure on the image of the generating set in SL2(𝔽_p). Assume the product theorem holds with exponent δ=0.01 and the non‑concentration estimate gives ‖μ_p‖2 ≤ p-1/2+0.001. Show that after k = C log p steps the L2‑distance to uniform is at most p-1/2−c for some c>0.
By the product theorem, any set A with |A| ≤ p2−δ satisfies |A3| ≥ |A|1+δ. The non‑concentration bound implies the initial measure μ_p has L2‑norm at most p-1/2+ε with ε=0.001. The flattening lemma (Bourgain–Gamburd, Lemma 2.2) states that if ‖μ‖2 ≤ |G|-1/2+ε and the product theorem holds with exponent δ, then there exists c = c(δ,ε) > 0 such that for k = Oδ,ε(log|G|) we have ‖μ*k−u‖2 ≤ |G|-1/2−c. Here |G| = |SL2(𝔽_p)| ≈ p3. Taking k = C log p with C large enough depending on δ,ε yields the desired bound. The constant c can be taken as δ·ε/10 for instance. Thus the Cayley graphs form an expander family.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the role of the flattening lemma, state the three‑step machine explicitly: product theorem → non‑concentration → flattening → spectral gap. Remember that the lemma converts a polynomial‑size L2‑bound into exponential mixing; without it one only obtains polynomial decay. Cite the quantitative dependence of the final spectral gap on the product‑theorem exponent δ and the non‑concentration parameter ε.

Diameter Bounds for Finite Simple Groups of Lie Type via Product Theorems

The diameter of a finite simple group G with respect to a generating set S is the smallest ℓ such that S^ℓ = G. For groups of Lie type over 𝔽_q, a landmark result of Helfgott (for SL2) and later of Pyber–Szabó and Breuillard–Green–Tao (for all bounded rank) shows that the diameter is polylogarithmic in |G|: diam(G,S) ≤ (log|G|)C where C depends only on the rank. The proof uses the product theorem: any subset A not contained in a proper subgroup satisfies |A3| ≥ |A|1+δ. Iterating this growth, one reaches a set of size > |G|1−ε in O(log|G|) steps. A subsequent “escape from subvarieties” argument shows that the generated set cannot stay inside a proper algebraic subvariety, forcing it to become the whole group after another O(log|G|) steps. For unbounded rank, the best known bound is quasipolynomial: diam(G,S) ≤ exp(O((log|G|)4)) due to work of Halasi–Maróti–Pyber–Qiao. These diameter bounds are equivalent to the statement that the Cayley graphs form expander families with respect to any generating set, and they underpin the superstrong approximation theorem of Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju, which asserts that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of a semisimple algebraic group, the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) have diameter (log|Γ/Γ(q)|)O(1).

Superstrong Approximation for Higher Rank Groups and the Work of Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju

Strong approximation for a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of a simply connected semisimple algebraic group G over ℚ states that the closure of Γ in the adelic group G(𝔸_f) is open. Superstrong approximation strengthens this by requiring that the family of Cayley graphs of the finite quotients Γ/Γ(q) (with respect to a fixed generating set) forms an expander family. For rank one this was proved by Bourgain–Gamburd for SL2 and by Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak for thin subgroups of SO(n,1). The breakthrough for higher rank came with the theorem of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju (2012): for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of a connected semisimple algebraic group G defined over ℚ, the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) form an expander family as q runs over square‑free integers coprime to a fixed finite set of primes. The proof combines the classification of approximate subgroups (Breuillard–Green–Tao) with a new “escape from subvarieties” lemma that works uniformly in the rank. Crucially, the real‑eigenvalue condition that appeared in earlier work of Bourgain–Varju is removed; the argument works for any generating set, symmetric or not. As a corollary, one obtains uniform diameter bounds (log|Γ/Γ(q)|)O(1) and the affine sieve applies to any polynomial map on the orbit, yielding almost‑prime values with a sieve dimension that depends only on the critical exponent of Γ. This theorem also implies the existence of expander families in all finite simple groups of Lie type, because any such group arises as a congruence quotient of a suitable arithmetic lattice.

Applications of the Affine Sieve to Integral Points on Quadric Hypersurfaces

The Uniform Tits Alternative and Escape from Subvarieties in Linear Groups

The classical Tits alternative (1972) states that every finitely generated linear group over a field either contains a non‑abelian free subgroup or is virtually solvable. For the theory of thin groups and superstrong approximation, a quantitative strengthening is essential: the uniform Tits alternative. Proved by Breuillard (2008) for linear groups of bounded dimension, it asserts that there exists a constant N(d) depending only on the dimension d such that any finite symmetric generating set S of a non‑virtually‑solvable subgroup of GLd(ℂ) contains two elements whose words of length at most N(d) generate a free subgroup of rank two. This uniformity is a key input in the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups (2012), because it allows one to pass from a set with small doubling to a genuine subgroup structure without losing control of the constants.

In the context of superstrong approximation, the uniform Tits alternative is complemented by the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju (2012). Let Γ be a Zariski‑dense subgroup of a connected semisimple algebraic group G defined over ℚ, and let V ⊊ G be a proper subvariety. The lemma states that the simple random walk on Γ driven by a fixed symmetric generating set exits V at an exponential rate: there exist constants c, α > 0 such that for all n ≥ 1, the probability that the walk of length n stays inside V is at most c e−αn. The proof combines the uniform Tits alternative (to produce free subgroups that quickly spread) with a quantitative version of the Larsen–Pink inequality, which bounds the intersection of a finite approximate subgroup with a subvariety. This escape estimate is the engine that upgrades polynomial growth in the finite quotients Γ/Γ(q) to full generation, thereby establishing the expander property for the Cayley graphs.

The interplay between these two results illustrates a central theme: uniformity in the rank. Earlier work of Bourgain–Varju (2012) required a real‑eigenvalue hypothesis on the generating set to avoid concentration on subvarieties. The Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju escape lemma removes that hypothesis entirely, working for any symmetric generating set and any semisimple group, regardless of rank. Consequently, the superstrong approximation theorem holds for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of semisimple algebraic groups, and the resulting expander families have diameter bounded by O(log|Γ/Γ(q)|). This uniformity is what allows the affine sieve to be applied to polynomial maps on arbitrary thin orbits, with a sieve dimension that depends only on the critical exponent of Γ and the geometry of the polynomial.

The Beta Sieve and Level of Distribution in the Affine Sieve for Thin Orbits

The affine sieve, introduced by Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak (2010), merges the spectral gap for thin groups (property (τ)) with a combinatorial sieve to produce almost‑prime values of a polynomial f on a thin orbit Γ·v ⊂ ℤN. The combinatorial engine is the β‑sieve (Rosser–Iwaniec sieve), an upper‑bound sieve that yields a lower bound for the number of elements in a sifted set with at most r prime factors. The sieve requires a level of distribution θ > 0: for a parameter Q, the error term in counting orbit points satisfying congruence conditions modulo q ≤ Q must be bounded by O(X1−θ) where X is the main term. The spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) provides precisely this power‑saving error term via the expander mixing lemma.

The sieve dimension κ governs how many prime factors r are needed. For a polynomial map f : V → 𝔸1 on an algebraic variety V of dimension d = dim V, with critical exponent δ of Γ and codimension c = codim(f−1(0)), the sieve dimension is κ = d·δ/c. In the Apollonian packing, V is the Descartes quadric of dimension 3, δ ≈ 1.30568, and for the curvature polynomial f(x) = x1 we have c = 1, giving κ ≈ 3.917. The β‑sieve then produces integers with at most r prime factors where r depends on κ and the level of distribution θ. Bourgain and Kontorovich (2014) achieved θ = 1/2 − ε for the Apollonian group, which together with κ ≈ 3.9 yields r = 25 (later improved). For general thin orbits, the level of distribution is limited by the spectral gap; the Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem guarantees a uniform spectral gap for all Zariski‑dense subgroups, hence a uniform θ > 0, but the exact value of θ depends on the group and the generating set.

A crucial technical point is that the affine sieve does not produce primes directly; it produces almost primes (integers with a bounded number of prime factors). Obtaining primes requires additional input such as a bilinear forms argument or a level‑of‑distribution improvement beyond 1/2, which is currently out of reach for thin groups. The sieve dimension κ must not be confused with the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set; they coincide only for geometrically finite groups. For infinite‑volume thin groups, δ < dimH(Λ), and the sieve dimension uses δ, not the Hausdorff dimension.

Thermodynamic Formalism and the Pressure Function for Thin Groups

The thermodynamic formalism provides a powerful framework for studying the critical exponent δ of a thin group Γ acting on a symmetric space X = G/K. For a geometrically finite Kleinian group, the Poincaré series P(s) = ∑γ∈Γ e−sd(o,γo) converges for s > δ and diverges at s = δ. Patterson (1976) and Sullivan (1979) constructed a conformal density of dimension δ on the limit set Λ(Γ), showing that δ equals the Hausdorff dimension of Λ(Γ). For infinite‑volume thin groups (e.g., Schottky subgroups of higher rank), the limit set is a fractal of Hausdorff dimension strictly larger than δ. The thermodynamic formalism replaces the geodesic flow by a symbolic coding (a subshift of finite type) and studies the pressure function P(s) = htop − s·χ, where htop is the topological entropy and χ the Lyapunov exponent. The unique zero of P(s) is exactly the critical exponent δ.

In higher rank, the work of Quint (2002), Eskin–Mozes–Oh (2005), and more recently of Oh–Shah (2013) extends this formalism to Anosov subgroups of semisimple Lie groups. The pressure function is defined via the Ledrappier–Young formula for the entropy of the Patterson–Sullivan measure. A key result is that for Zariski‑dense Anosov subgroups, the critical exponent δ is strictly less than the volume entropy of the symmetric space, and the Patterson–Sullivan measure is the unique equilibrium state for the geometric potential. This has direct consequences for the affine sieve: the sieve dimension κ = (dim V)·δ/c uses the critical exponent from the thermodynamic formalism, not the Hausdorff dimension. Moreover, the exponential mixing of the geodesic flow (or the frame flow in higher rank) provides the spectral gap needed for the level of distribution. The thermodynamic formalism also yields precise asymptotics for the number of orbit points of norm ≤ T: N(T) ∼ c·Tδ for some constant c > 0, which is the main term in the sieve.

A common confusion is to equate the critical exponent δ with the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set. They coincide for geometrically finite groups (including convex‑cocompact subgroups of rank one) but diverge for infinite‑volume thin groups in higher rank. The thermodynamic formalism clarifies this: δ is the zero of the pressure function, while the Hausdorff dimension is the zero of a different pressure function associated with the conformal structure on the limit set. Examiners often test this distinction by asking whether the Patterson–Sullivan measure is the Hausdorff measure; the answer is yes only when δ equals the Hausdorff dimension.

The Local–Global Principle for Integral Points on Homogeneous Varieties Beyond Quadrics

The local–global principle for integral points on a variety X asks whether the existence of solutions modulo every integer (and over ℝ) implies the existence of an integral solution. For thin orbits Γ·v ⊂ ℤN, the principle often fails: there may be local solutions that are not globally realized, but the affine sieve shows that the set of exceptions is small. The classical case is the Apollonian packing (a quadric), where Bourgain–Kontorovich (2014) proved that every sufficiently large integer satisfying the congruence conditions modulo 24 appears as a curvature, and a positive proportion are prime. For cubic hypersurfaces, the Markov equation x2 + y2 + z2 = 3xyz provides a testing ground. The integral solutions form an orbit of the thin subgroup ΓMarkov ⊂ SL3(ℤ) generated by Vieta involutions. Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak (2016) applied the affine sieve to this orbit, showing that almost all integers satisfying the necessary local conditions appear as coordinates, with at most finitely many exceptions. The spectral gap for ΓMarkov was established by Varju (2012) using the Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine adapted to SL3(𝔽p).

Beyond the Markov surface, the local–global principle has been studied for the E8 lattice and for integral points on homogeneous varieties of the form G/H where G is a semisimple group and H a spherical subgroup. The work of Gorodnik–Nevo (2012) and of Bourgain–Varju (2012) on the affine sieve for general polynomial maps on thin orbits gives a framework: if the stabilizer of the polynomial is a thin subgroup with a spectral gap, then the sifted set has the expected asymptotic. The main technical obstacle is the escape from subvarieties in higher rank, which was resolved by Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju (2012). Their theorem implies that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup of a semisimple group, the congruence quotients form an expander family, so the affine sieve applies to any polynomial map. Consequently, the local–global principle holds for thin orbits on any homogeneous variety, with at most finitely many exceptions, provided the polynomial is not identically zero on the orbit and the sieve dimension is finite.

An examiner may ask: "Does the local–global principle hold for the Markov equation?" The correct answer is: it holds for all sufficiently large integers satisfying the congruence conditions, with at most finitely many exceptions, as proved by Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak using the affine sieve and the spectral gap for the Markov group. It does not hold for all integers; there are known small exceptions (e.g., 1, 2, 5). The distinction between "all integers" and "all sufficiently large integers" is a frequent trap.

Expander Families from Thin Subgroups of Higher Rank Groups and the Ramanujan Conjecture

The Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem (2012) establishes that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of a connected semisimple algebraic group G over ℚ, the Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) (with respect to a fixed generating set) form an expander family as q runs over square‑free integers coprime to a fixed finite set of primes. This is superstrong approximation. The proof combines the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups with the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma, and it works uniformly in the rank of G. A direct corollary is a uniform diameter bound: diam(Γ/Γ(q)) = O(log|Γ/Γ(q)|). For bounded rank, this is essentially optimal; for unbounded rank (e.g., SLn(𝔽p) with n → ∞), the best known diameter bound is quasipolynomial, exp((log|G|)O(1)), due to Helfgott–Seress (2014) and subsequent work.

The Ramanujan conjecture for finite simple groups of Lie type predicts the optimal spectral gap: the non‑trivial eigenvalues of the adjacency operator on the Cayley graph should be bounded by 2√(k−1) where k is the degree. For congruence quotients of arithmetic lattices in higher rank groups (e.g., SLn(ℤ) with n ≥ 3), property (T) gives a uniform spectral gap, but not necessarily the Ramanujan bound. The Ramanujan bound is known for congruence quotients of SL2 over function fields (Drinfeld, 1970s) and for certain higher rank groups over function fields (Lafforgue, 2002). Over number fields, the Ramanujan conjecture for GLn is still open in general, though it is known for n = 2 (Deligne) and for certain automorphic representations. For thin groups, the spectral gap provided by superstrong approximation is far from the Ramanujan bound; it is a qualitative expander property with an unspecified constant. The distinction is crucial: property (τ) (a uniform spectral gap) is sufficient for the affine sieve, while the Ramanujan bound is a much stronger quantitative statement that remains open for thin groups in higher rank.

Examiners often test the relationship between diameter bounds and expansion. A family of Cayley graphs is an expander if and only if the diameter is O(log|G|). The Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem gives this for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of semisimple groups of bounded rank. For unbounded rank, the quasipolynomial diameter bound does not imply expansion; it is a weaker statement. Another common trap is to assume that the Ramanujan conjecture is proved for all finite simple groups of Lie type; it is only known in the function field setting and for specific arithmetic groups. In the number field setting, the best general result for thin groups is the qualitative expander property, not the optimal eigenvalue bound.

The Selberg Trace Formula and the Spectral Gap for Thin Subgroups of SL₂(ℝ)

The Selberg trace formula provides a precise relationship between the length spectrum of closed geodesics on a hyperbolic surface and the eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator. For a congruence subgroup Γ(q) of SL₂(ℤ), the formula expresses the spectral side as a sum over eigenvalues λ_j = s_j(1−s_j) with s_j = ½ + i t_j, and the geometric side as a sum over conjugacy classes of hyperbolic elements. When Γ is a thin subgroup — Zariski‑dense but of infinite index — the same formalism applies, but the discrete spectrum may be sparse and the continuous spectrum contributes Eisenstein series. The spectral gap, i.e. the distance from the base eigenvalue λ₀ = 0 to the first non‑zero eigenvalue λ₁, is exactly the quantity that controls property (τ) for the family of congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q). Selberg’s 3/16 theorem (λ₁ ≥ 3/16) for congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) follows from the trace formula together with the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for holomorphic cusp forms, proved by Deligne. For thin groups, the trace formula still holds, but the Ramanujan bound is not known; instead one obtains a qualitative spectral gap via the Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine. The trace formula also yields the Weyl law for the counting function of eigenvalues, which for thin groups reflects the critical exponent δ of the Poincaré series. In particular, the number of eigenvalues ≤ T grows like c T2δ for some constant c, linking the spectral gap to the geometry of the limit set. Examiners often ask: “How does the Selberg trace formula connect the spectral gap to the critical exponent?” The answer: the trace formula expresses the trace of the heat kernel in two ways; the small‑time asymptotics give the Weyl law with exponent 2δ, while the large‑time decay is governed by the spectral gap. A uniform spectral gap for all congruence quotients implies δ < ½, which is a key input for the affine sieve.

The Congruence Subgroup Property and Its Failure for Thin Groups

The congruence subgroup property (CSP) for an arithmetic group G(ℤ) asserts that every finite‑index subgroup contains a principal congruence subgroup G(ℤ, q) for some q. For higher‑rank groups such as SL_n(ℤ) with n ≥ 3, the CSP holds (Bass–Milnor–Serre, 1967). This means that the profinite completion of SL_n(ℤ) coincides with its congruence completion, and every finite quotient factors through some SL_n(ℤ/qℤ). For thin subgroups Γ ⊂ SL_n(ℤ), the CSP typically fails: there exist finite‑index subgroups of Γ that do not contain any congruence subgroup Γ(q). The failure is measured by the congruence kernel, which for thin groups can be infinite. A concrete example is the Apollonian group, a thin subgroup of SO(3,1;ℤ) generated by four reflections. Its congruence quotients are proper subgroups of the full orthogonal groups over ℤ/qℤ, and the congruence kernel is non‑trivial. The failure of CSP for thin groups is precisely what makes superstrong approximation a non‑trivial statement: it says that despite the failure of CSP, the Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) still form an expander family. This distinction is a frequent trap. Students sometimes confuse “CSP holds for the ambient arithmetic group” with “CSP holds for the thin subgroup”. The correct statement: the ambient group has CSP, but the thin subgroup does not; nevertheless, the congruence quotients of the thin subgroup still expand. The proof of superstrong approximation (Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju, 2012) bypasses CSP by using the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups and an escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma, which works for any Zariski‑dense subgroup regardless of CSP.

Sum–Product Estimates and Their Role in the Bourgain–Gamburd Machine

The sum–product phenomenon in finite fields states that for any subset A ⊂ 𝔽_p not too close to a subfield, either the sum set A+A or the product set A·A is significantly larger than A. Bourgain, Katz, and Tao (2004) proved that for any ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that max(|A+A|, |A·A|) ≥ |A|1+δ provided |A| ≤ p1−ε. This estimate is the engine behind the Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine for SL₂(𝔽_p). The machine starts with a symmetric generating set S of a subgroup Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) and studies the random walk on the Cayley graph of Γ/Γ(p). The key step is to show that the probability measure μ(n) after n steps does not concentrate on any proper subgroup. Using the classification of approximate subgroups (Helfgott, 2008), one reduces the problem to proving that μ(n) is not trapped in a Borel subgroup or a split torus. The sum–product estimate enters when one analyses the projection of μ(n) onto the diagonal torus: the coordinates of matrix entries satisfy multiplicative relations, and the sum–product bound forces the measure to flatten. Concretely, if the walk were concentrated on a torus, the diagonal entries would lie in a set with small product set, contradicting the sum–product theorem. This flattening yields the L²‑flattening lemma: ‖μ(n)‖₂ ≤ |Γ/Γ(p)|−1/2+ε for some n = O(log p). The L² bound then implies a spectral gap via the standard expander criterion. The sum–product estimate is thus the arithmetic input that makes the machine work for SL₂(𝔽_p); for higher rank groups, the role is played by the Breuillard–Green–Tao product theorem, which generalises the sum–product phenomenon to arbitrary finite simple groups of Lie type.

Quantitative Non‑Concentration Estimates and the Flattening Lemma

In the Bourgain–Gamburd machine, the term “non‑concentration” refers to the property that the n‑step convolution power μ(n) of a symmetric probability measure μ on a finite group G does not assign too much mass to any proper subgroup H < G. Quantitatively, one seeks an estimate of the form μ(n)(H) ≤ |G|−κ for some κ > 0 and all proper subgroups H, with n = O(log |G|). For SL₂(𝔽_p), the proper subgroups are either Borel subgroups (stabilisers of a line) or subgroups of order O(p). The non‑concentration estimate is proved by combining the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma (which gives a bound for algebraic subvarieties of bounded degree) with the classification of approximate subgroups. The escape lemma says that if a random walk on a Zariski‑dense subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) is reduced modulo p, the probability of landing in a proper algebraic subvariety V of degree d decays exponentially in the walk length, provided the walk length exceeds C d log p. Applying this to the variety of matrices with a given eigenvector (a Borel subgroup) yields the desired non‑concentration. Once non‑concentration is established, the flattening lemma follows: the L² norm of μ(n) drops by a factor |G|−ε after O(log |G|) steps. The proof uses the fact that if ‖μ(n)‖₂ is large, then by the Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers lemma the support of μ(n) contains a large approximate subgroup, which by the product theorem must be contained in a proper subgroup — contradicting non‑concentration. The flattening lemma is the quantitative heart of the expansion proof; it directly implies a uniform spectral gap λ₁ ≥ ε for the Cayley graphs. For thin groups, the same scheme works because the Zariski‑density hypothesis guarantees the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma, and the product theorem is available for all finite simple groups of Lie type (Breuillard–Green–Tao). The constants κ, ε depend on the generating set and the group, but are independent of the level q.

The Uniform Spectral Gap for Thin Subgroups of Higher Rank Groups and Applications to the Zaremba Conjecture

For higher‑rank groups such as SL_n(ℤ) with n ≥ 3, property (T) of the ambient arithmetic group gives a uniform spectral gap for all congruence quotients SL_n(ℤ)/SL_n(ℤ/qℤ). However, thin subgroups do not inherit property (T); they only have property (τ) with respect to the family of congruence quotients. The Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem establishes that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of a connected semisimple algebraic group G over ℚ, the Cayley graphs of Γ/Γ(q) form an expander family. In higher rank, the proof uses the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups in finite simple groups of Lie type, together with an escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma that holds uniformly in the rank. The resulting spectral gap is qualitative — the constant is not explicit — but it is sufficient for the affine sieve. A striking application is to the Zaremba conjecture on continued fractions: every integer q ≥ 2 appears as the denominator of a finite continued fraction with all partial quotients bounded by an absolute constant (conjecturally 5). Bourgain and Kontorovich (2014) used the affine sieve on the thin orbit of the Gauss map, together with the uniform spectral gap for the relevant thin subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) (which is a subgroup of the modular group), to show that the set of denominators that appear has positive density. Later work of Huang (2015) and others extended this to show that all sufficiently large integers appear, assuming a level‑of‑distribution hypothesis that follows from the spectral gap. The key point is that the affine sieve requires a level of distribution θ > ½ for the polynomial map f(γ) = denominator of the continued fraction associated to γ. The spectral gap provides exactly this level of distribution via the expander mixing lemma. Thus the uniform spectral gap for thin subgroups of higher rank groups (and for certain rank‑one thin groups) is the engine that drives the almost‑prime and prime‑value results for polynomial maps on thin orbits.

The Affine Sieve and the Distribution of Almost Primes on Thin Orbits

The affine sieve, introduced by Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak, adapts the classical combinatorial sieve to orbits of thin subgroups acting on affine varieties. Let Γ be a Zariski‑dense subgroup of a semisimple algebraic group G defined over ℚ, and let f: G → 𝔸¹ be a non‑constant polynomial map defined over ℤ. For a large parameter T one considers the set {f(γ) : γ ∈ Γ, ‖γ‖ ≤ T}. The goal is to show that this set contains many integers with a bounded number of prime factors — almost primes — and, under stronger hypotheses, infinitely many primes.

The sieve requires a level of distribution θ > ½ for the sequence aₙ = #{γ ∈ Γ : ‖γ‖ ≤ T, f(γ) = n}. The expander mixing lemma for the Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) provides exactly this level: the spectral gap λ₁ ≥ ε implies that for any square‑free q the error term in the equidistribution of the orbit modulo q is O(q⁻ᶿ) with θ = 1 − ε/2. When the uniform spectral gap holds for all q (the superstrong approximation theorem of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju), the affine sieve yields a positive proportion of almost primes of any prescribed order r ≥ r₀(Γ,f). In particular, for the thin orbit arising from the Gauss map in the Zaremba conjecture, the sieve shows that the set of denominators of continued fractions with bounded partial quotients has positive density.

A crucial technical point is the verification of the “primitivity” condition: for each prime p there must exist γ ∈ Γ with p ∤ f(γ). This fails for some polynomials (e.g. f(γ) = trace(γ) on SL₂(ℤ) because trace is always even on the principal congruence subgroup of level 2). When primitivity holds, the β‑sieve of Rosser–Iwaniec gives an explicit lower bound for the number of almost primes. The constants depend on the degree of f, the dimension of the orbit, and the spectral gap constant ε, but not on the level q. Recent work of Bourgain–Kontorovich and of Huang extends the method to show that all sufficiently large integers appear as denominators, assuming a level‑of‑distribution hypothesis that follows from the uniform spectral gap.

Compared with the classical sieve on ℤ, the affine sieve replaces arithmetic progressions by congruence quotients of a thin group. The expander property guarantees that the orbit equidistributes in each quotient at a rate independent of the level, which is the analytic heart of the method. Without a uniform spectral gap one only obtains a level of distribution θ ≤ ½, insufficient for almost‑prime results. Thus the interplay between expansion, representation theory of finite simple groups of Lie type, and sieve theory is the defining feature of the affine sieve on thin orbits.

The Uniform Tits Alternative and Its Consequences for Thin Subgroups

The uniform Tits alternative, proved by Breuillard, Green and Tao, states that for any finite symmetric generating set S of a linear group G ≤ GLₙ(ℂ) there exists an integer m = m(n,|S|) such that either the subgroup ⟨S⟩ is virtually solvable, or Sᵐ contains two elements generating a free non‑abelian subgroup. For Zariski‑dense subgroups Γ of a semisimple algebraic group G over ℚ this alternative has a quantitative refinement: there is a constant δ = δ(Γ) > 0 such that for any proper algebraic subvariety V ⊂ G the proportion of words of length ℓ in a fixed generating set that land in V is at most exp(−δℓ). This “escape from subvarieties” lemma is the key input for the non‑concentration estimate in the Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine.

In the context of thin groups, the uniform Tits alternative replaces the use of property (T) which is unavailable for infinite‑index subgroups. It guarantees that random walks on Γ do not concentrate on any proper algebraic subgroup of G, uniformly in the level of the congruence quotient. Combined with the product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type (also due to Breuillard–Green–Tao), one obtains the flattening lemma: the ℓ²‑norm of the ℓ‑step convolution power of the uniform measure on the generating set drops by a factor |G(q)|⁻ᵋ after O(log |G(q)|) steps. The constants ε, κ depend only on the generating set and the ambient algebraic group, not on the level q.

A direct consequence is the superstrong approximation theorem: for any Zariski‑dense Γ the family of Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) forms an expander family as q varies over square‑free integers. The proof proceeds by induction on the rank of G, using the classification of approximate subgroups in finite simple groups of Lie type. In rank one (e.g. SL₂) the sum‑product estimates in 𝔽ₚ provide the base case; in higher rank the product theorem supplies the necessary structure. The uniform Tits alternative is also used to bound the “collision probability” of the random walk, which feeds into the quantitative non‑concentration estimate required for the flattening lemma.

Beyond expansion, the uniform Tits alternative implies that any Zariski‑dense thin subgroup has exponential growth and that its critical exponent (the abscissa of convergence of the Poincaré series) is strictly less than the critical exponent of the ambient lattice. This fact underlies the thermodynamic formalism for thin groups and the construction of Patterson–Sullivan measures on the limit set. In summary, the uniform Tits alternative is the combinatorial engine that drives both the spectral theory of thin groups and the analytic sieve applications.

Applying the Uniform Tits Alternative to Prove Non‑Concentration
Let Γ = ⟨S⟩ be a Zariski‑dense subgroup of SL₃(ℤ) with S a symmetric generating set of size 4. Show that there exists δ > 0 such that for any proper algebraic subvariety V ⊂ SL₃ the probability that a random word of length ℓ in S lies in V is ≤ e^(−δℓ).
By the uniform Tits alternative (Breuillard–Green–Tao) there is an integer m = m(3,4) such that either ⟨S⟩ is virtually solvable or Sᵐ contains a free pair. Zariski‑density in SL₃ forces the second case, because any virtually solvable subgroup of SL₃ is contained in a proper algebraic subgroup. Hence Sᵐ generates a free non‑abelian subgroup F₂. The random walk on Γ projects to a random walk on F₂, which has exponential decay of return probabilities to any finite set. Since V ∩ Γ is a finite union of cosets of proper algebraic subgroups, its intersection with F₂ is a finite set. Standard large‑deviation estimates for random walks on free groups give a constant δ = δ(S) > 0 with the required bound. The constant depends only on the generating set and the ambient dimension, not on V.

When a question asks for a non‑concentration estimate on a thin subgroup, first cite the uniform Tits alternative to obtain escape from subvarieties, then invoke the product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type to upgrade escape to a flattening lemma. Remember that the constants are qualitative; you do not need to compute them explicitly.

The Selberg Trace Formula for Thin Subgroups of Higher Rank Groups

The Selberg trace formula expresses the spectral side of the Laplacian on a locally symmetric space ΓG/K in terms of geometric data: conjugacy classes, volumes, and orbital integrals. For congruence subgroups of higher rank groups (e.g. SLₙ(ℤ) with n ≥ 3) the formula has been developed by Arthur and others, yielding precise Weyl laws and explicit spectral gaps via property (T). For thin subgroups Γ of infinite index, the trace formula does not apply directly because the quotient ΓG/K has infinite volume and the continuous spectrum is not discrete. However, recent work of Oh, Shah, and others adapts the trace formula to the setting of “thin” quotients by using the Patterson–Sullivan measure on the limit set and the theory of unitary representations of G on L²(ΓG).

The key idea is to replace the discrete sum over conjugacy classes by an integral over the limit set with respect to the Patterson–Sullivan measure. The resulting “thin trace formula” relates the critical exponent δ(Γ) to the bottom of the spectrum of the Laplacian on ΓG/K. When Γ is Zariski‑dense, the spectral gap λ₁ ≥ δ(Γ)(1 − δ(Γ)) (in the normalisation of the Laplacian on the symmetric space) follows from the work of Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak and Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju. In higher rank, property (T) of G gives a uniform spectral gap for all lattices, but thin subgroups only inherit property (τ) with respect to the family of congruence quotients. The thin trace formula shows that the same spectral gap controls the error term in the equidistribution of Γ‑orbits on homogeneous varieties, which is the analytic input for the affine sieve.

A concrete application is the local‑global principle for integral quadratic forms. Let Q be a non‑degenerate quadratic form in n ≥ 5 variables and let Γ = SO_Q(ℤ) be the integral orthogonal group. Thin subgroups arise as stabilisers of integral vectors. The thin trace formula for these subgroups yields a power‑saving error term in the counting of representations of integers by Q, which translates into a level of distribution > ½ for the associated sieve problem. This is the mechanism behind the recent progress on the “thin” version of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method for quadratic forms.

Limitations remain: the thin trace formula is not yet available for all higher rank groups, and the constants in the spectral gap are ineffective. Moreover, the formula requires the critical exponent δ(Γ) to be > (rank − 1)/2, a condition that fails for very sparse thin groups. In those cases one must rely on the combinatorial expansion machine rather than spectral theory. Nevertheless, the Selberg trace formula for thin subgroups provides a conceptual bridge between the spectral theory of automorphic forms and the combinatorial expansion properties that drive the affine sieve.

The Uniform Spectral Gap for Thin Subgroups of Rank One Groups

For rank one groups such as SL₂(ℝ) or SL₂(ℂ), the ambient lattice does not have property (T), so the uniform spectral gap for congruence quotients is a deep theorem of Selberg (λ₁ ≥ 3/16 for congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ)) and its generalisations. Thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) do not inherit Selberg’s bound; instead they satisfy property (τ) with respect to the family of principal congruence quotients. The breakthrough of Bourgain and Gamburd (2008) established a uniform spectral gap for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ ≤ SL₂(ℤ) that is not virtually solvable. Their proof uses the sum‑product estimate in 𝔽ₚ (Bourgain–Katz–Tao) to show that the random walk on Γ/Γ(p) flattens rapidly, which by the flattening lemma yields a spectral gap λ₁ ≥ ε(Γ) > 0 independent of the prime p.

The argument proceeds in three stages. First, the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma (a consequence of the uniform Tits alternative) guarantees that the random walk does not concentrate on any proper algebraic subgroup of SL₂. Second, the sum‑product theorem in 𝔽ₚ implies that any approximate subgroup of SL₂(𝔽ₚ) that is not contained in a proper subgroup must be almost the whole group. Third, the Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers lemma transfers the non‑concentration estimate to a bound on the ℓ²‑norm of the convolution powers, giving the flattening lemma. The resulting expander family Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) has a spectral gap that depends only on the generating set S, not on the level q.

This uniform spectral gap has several striking applications. In the affine sieve, it provides the level of distribution θ > ½ needed to produce almost primes on thin orbits, e.g. the set of prime curvatures in Apollonian circle packings. For Apollonian packings, the thin group is the Apollonian group, a Zariski‑dense subgroup of O_Q(ℤ) ≅ SO(3,1;ℤ). The spectral gap for its congruence quotients implies that the number of circles with prime curvature up to T is ≫ T/(log T)², and that a positive proportion of integers appear as curvatures. The same method applies to the Zaremba conjecture on continued fractions, where the thin group is a subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) generated by the Gauss map.

It is important to distinguish the uniform spectral gap for thin subgroups from Selberg’s 3/16 theorem. Selberg’s bound holds for all congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) but fails for thin groups; the thin‑group gap is typically much smaller and non‑explicit. Moreover, the thin‑group gap is proved only for square‑free levels (or for levels coprime to a fixed finite set of primes). For composite levels with high prime powers, the expansion property may break down because the congruence quotient is no longer a product of simple groups. The current state of the art (Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju) gives a qualitative expander property for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of any semisimple group, but the quantitative constants remain ineffective.

The Role of the Spectral Gap in the Affine Sieve: Level of Distribution and Almost Prime Counting

The affine sieve, introduced by Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak, translates a spectral gap for the family of Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) into a level of distribution for the sequence of values taken by a polynomial map on a thin orbit. If Γ ≤ SL_n(ℤ) is Zariski‑dense and not virtually solvable, the uniform spectral gap λ₁ ≥ ε > 0 for all square‑free levels q implies, via the expander mixing lemma, that the error term in the equidistribution of the orbit modulo q is O(q−δ) for some δ > 0 depending only on the generating set S. This yields a level of distribution θ = ½ + δ/2 > ½ for the sifted set. The beta sieve then produces almost primes: for any polynomial f of degree d, the number of points in the orbit with f(x) having at most r prime factors is ≫ X/(log X) for r sufficiently large (r depends on d and θ). In the Apollonian packing, the thin group is the Apollonian subgroup of O_Q(ℤ) ≅ SO(3,1;ℤ); the spectral gap for its congruence quotients gives θ > ½ and leads to the result that a positive proportion of integers appear as curvatures and that the number of prime curvatures up to T is ≫ T/(log T)². The same mechanism underlies the progress on the Zaremba conjecture, where the thin group is generated by the Gauss map inside SL₂(ℤ). The crucial point is that the spectral gap must be uniform across all square‑free levels; a non‑uniform gap would only give a level of distribution that deteriorates with q and would not suffice for the sieve.

The expander mixing lemma states that for any subsets A, B of the vertex set of a d‑regular expander with second eigenvalue λ, the number of edges between A and B satisfies |E(A,B) − d|A||B|/N| ≤ λ√(|A||B|). Applying this to the Cayley graphs of Γ/Γ(q) with the generating set S gives a bound on the discrepancy of the orbit modulo q. Summing over q ≤ Q and using the large sieve inequality yields the level of distribution. The exponent δ is explicitly related to the spectral gap by δ = (1−λ)/2 for the normalized Laplacian. In practice the constant ε is ineffective, but the qualitative statement θ > ½ is enough for the affine sieve to produce infinitely many almost primes on any Zariski‑dense thin orbit satisfying the necessary local conditions.

Expansion in Congruence Quotients of Thin Subgroups of Symplectic and Orthogonal Groups

Superstrong approximation extends beyond SL₂ to any semisimple algebraic group G defined over ℚ. For a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ ≤ G(ℤ), the family of congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) forms an expander family provided q is square‑free and coprime to a finite set of bad primes. The proof, due to Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju, combines the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups in finite simple groups of Lie type with a product theorem for G(𝔽_p). In the symplectic case G = Sp(2n) and the orthogonal case G = SO(p,q), the same strategy works: one first proves an escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma using the uniform Tits alternative, then invokes the sum‑product phenomenon in the finite field 𝔽_p to show that any approximate subgroup not contained in a proper algebraic subgroup must be almost the whole group. The product theorem for groups of Lie type (Pyber–Szabó, Breuillard–Green–Tao) then forces rapid growth of the random walk, which by the flattening lemma yields a uniform spectral gap. A subtle point is that for higher rank groups the congruence quotient Γ/Γ(q) is a product of simple groups when q is square‑free, so expansion follows from expansion in each factor. For prime powers p^k with k ≥ 2 the quotient is no longer a product of simple groups, and expansion may fail; the current theory gives expanders only for square‑free levels (or levels with bounded prime powers). The result has immediate applications to the affine sieve on thin orbits in Sp(2n) and SO(p,q), for instance to the distribution of almost prime values of quadratic forms on thin orbits.

Technique: From Spectral Gap to Level of Distribution
Let Γ ≤ SL₂(ℤ) be a Zariski‑dense, non‑virtually‑solvable subgroup with generating set S. Suppose the family Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) has a uniform spectral gap λ₁ ≥ ε > 0 for all square‑free q. Show how to deduce a level of distribution θ > ½ for the sequence a_n = f(γ_n) where γ_n runs over a ball of radius n in Γ and f is a non‑constant polynomial.
1. The uniform spectral gap implies, via the expander mixing lemma, that for any subset A ⊂ Γ/Γ(q) the random walk of length ℓ mixes to the uniform distribution with error O(exp(−cℓ)) where c depends on ε. 2. Choose ℓ ≈ log q so that the error is O(q−δ) for some δ > 0. 3. The orbit of Γ modulo q equidistributes with discrepancy O(q−δ). 4. By the large sieve, the sifted set {γ ∈ B_n : f(γ) has no small prime factors} has size ≫ |B_n|/(log n) provided the level of distribution θ = ½ + δ/2 > ½. 5. The beta sieve then yields infinitely many almost primes on the orbit.

When a question asks for the role of the spectral gap in the affine sieve, first state the expander mixing lemma, then explain how it gives a power‑saving error term in equidistribution modulo q, then invoke the large sieve to convert this into a level of distribution θ > ½, and finally mention the beta sieve conclusion. Do not confuse the qualitative expander property with an explicit numerical gap; the argument only needs existence of some ε > 0.

Quantitative Non‑Concentration and the Escape from Subvarieties Lemma

The Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine rests on three pillars: escape from subvarieties, a sum‑product estimate, and the flattening lemma. The escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma asserts that for a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ ≤ SL₂(ℤ) generated by a symmetric set S, the probability that a random walk of length n lands in any proper algebraic subvariety V ⊂ SL₂ of degree ≤ D decays exponentially in n, with a rate depending only on S and D. This is a quantitative form of the uniform Tits alternative: any non‑virtually‑solvable linear group contains a free subgroup on two generators, and the random walk cannot concentrate on a proper subvariety because that would force the walk to lie in a virtually solvable subgroup. The proof uses the fact that the word map w: Γ^k → SL₂ is a polynomial map of bounded degree; if the walk concentrated on V, the image of w would be contained in V, contradicting Zariski density. The sum‑product estimate in 𝔽_p (Bourgain–Katz–Tao) then shows that any approximate subgroup of SL₂(𝔽_p) not contained in a proper subgroup must be almost the whole group. Combining these two facts, one proves that the ℓ²‑norm of the n‑step convolution power μ*n on Γ/Γ(p) drops by a factor p−δ after O(log p) steps, which is the flattening lemma. The quantitative non‑concentration estimate is the key input that makes the flattening lemma effective: it gives an explicit bound on the probability that the walk stays inside a proper subvariety, which translates into a bound on the ℓ²‑norm via the Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers lemma. In higher rank, the same structure holds, but the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma is replaced by a more general statement about non‑concentration on proper algebraic subgroups, proved using the uniform Tits alternative for linear groups (Breuillard–Gelander).

The Uniform Exponential Growth of Thin Subgroups and Its Implications for Expansion

A finitely generated linear group either has polynomial growth (and is virtually nilpotent) or has exponential growth. For Zariski‑dense subgroups of semisimple algebraic groups, a stronger statement holds: there exists a uniform exponential growth rate λ > 1 such that for any finite symmetric generating set S, the size of the ball B_S(n) satisfies |B_S(n)| ≥ λ^n for all sufficiently large n. This uniform exponential growth was proved by Breuillard and Gelander using the uniform Tits alternative and the fact that any Zariski‑dense subgroup contains a free subgroup on two generators with a uniform bound on the word length of the generators in terms of the original generating set. Uniform exponential growth is intimately linked to property (τ) and to the existence of a spectral gap for congruence quotients. Indeed, if a family of Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) forms an expander family, then the girth of these graphs grows logarithmically with the size of the group, which forces uniform exponential growth in the ambient group. Conversely, uniform exponential growth provides the combinatorial input needed for the product theorem in the Bourgain–Gamburd machine: it guarantees that the random walk does not get trapped in a small approximate subgroup. In the context of thin groups, uniform exponential growth also implies that the critical exponent of the Poincaré series (the exponent of convergence of the Patterson–Sullivan measure) is strictly less than the critical exponent of the ambient lattice, a fact used in the thermodynamic formalism for thin groups.

The Connection Between Thin Groups and the Theory of Random Walks on Groups

Random walks on thin groups provide a probabilistic perspective on superstrong approximation. Let Γ be a Zariski‑dense subgroup of G(ℤ) and let μ be a symmetric probability measure on Γ with finite support generating Γ. The pushforward of μ to the finite quotients Γ/Γ(q) defines a random walk on each finite group. Superstrong approximation is equivalent to the statement that these random walks mix to the uniform distribution in time O(log |Γ/Γ(q)|) uniformly in q (for square‑free q). The mixing time is controlled by the spectral gap of the associated Markov operator. The diameter of the Cayley graph Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) is also of order log |Γ/Γ(q)|, a result of Helfgott–Seress for SL₂(𝔽_p) and of Pyber–Szabó for groups of Lie type. For thin groups, the diameter bound follows from the product theorem and the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma: the random walk cannot stay in a proper subgroup for too long, so it must generate the whole group quickly. This connection has been exploited by Varju to prove expansion for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of SL_d(ℤ) (d ≥ 3) using the classification of approximate subgroups. Moreover, the random walk viewpoint clarifies why the affine sieve works: the level of distribution is essentially the rate at which the random walk equidistributes modulo q. The expander mixing lemma gives a quantitative bound on the discrepancy, which is exactly the error term in the equidistribution of the random walk. Thus the entire affine sieve machinery can be reformulated in terms of mixing times of random walks on thin groups.

The Interplay Between Property (τ) and the Congruence Subgroup Property for Thin Groups

Property (τ) for a family of finite quotients of a group Γ means that the trivial representation is isolated in the unitary dual of the profinite completion with respect to the family of congruence quotients. For arithmetic lattices in higher rank semisimple groups, the congruence subgroup property (CSP) implies that every finite‑index subgroup contains a principal congruence subgroup, so the family of all finite‑index subgroups coincides with the family of congruence subgroups. In that setting property (τ) for the congruence quotients is equivalent to property (τ) for all finite‑index subgroups, which in turn is known to hold by the work of Lubotzky and Zimmer using property (T) of the ambient algebraic group. For thin groups — Zariski‑dense subgroups of infinite index — the CSP fails dramatically: there are many finite‑index subgroups that are not congruence subgroups, and the profinite completion is much larger than the congruence completion. Nevertheless, superstrong approximation asserts that the congruence quotients alone form an expander family, i.e. property (τ) holds for the congruence family. The distinction is crucial: property (τ) for the congruence family does not imply property (τ) for all finite‑index subgroups, and the latter is generally false for thin groups. The failure of CSP for thin groups is witnessed by the existence of many non‑congruence finite quotients, which can be constructed using the theory of profinite groups and the fact that the congruence kernel is infinite. This interplay explains why the affine sieve, which relies only on congruence quotients, can succeed even though the full profinite completion does not have a spectral gap. In practice, one proves property (τ) for the congruence family by establishing a uniform spectral gap for the Cayley graphs of Γ/Γ(q) as q varies over square‑free integers. The proof uses the Bourgain–Gamburd machine together with the classification of approximate subgroups (Breuillard–Green–Tao) and the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma. The CSP, on the other hand, would require controlling all finite quotients, a much stronger statement that is known to fail for any thin subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) and for many thin subgroups in higher rank. Understanding this separation guides the choice of level of distribution in the affine sieve: one only needs equidistribution modulo q for square‑free q, not for all moduli.

The relationship can be summarised in a diagram: the congruence completion Γ̂_congr is a quotient of the full profinite completion Γ̂. Property (τ) for the congruence family means the trivial representation is isolated in the unitary dual of Γ̂_congr. Property (T) of the ambient group G(ℤ) gives property (τ) for the full profinite completion of G(ℤ), but this does not descend to Γ because Γ has infinite index. The failure of CSP for Γ is equivalent to the statement that the congruence kernel Ker(Γ̂ → Γ̂_congr) is infinite. This kernel can be large enough to destroy any spectral gap for the full family of finite quotients. Consequently, any argument that uses expansion must be carefully restricted to congruence quotients. The affine sieve exploits exactly this restriction: the level of distribution is taken over square‑free moduli, and the expander mixing lemma provides the required error term. If one attempted to use a level of distribution over all moduli, the lack of a uniform spectral gap for non‑congruence quotients would break the sieve. This subtle point is often missed in expositions that conflate property (τ) for congruence quotients with property (τ) for all finite quotients.

Recent work of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju shows that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup of SL_d(ℤ) with d ≥ 3, the congruence quotients form an expander family, establishing property (τ) for the congruence family in full generality. Their proof does not require CSP; it uses the uniform Tits alternative to produce free subgroups with controlled word length, the product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type, and the flattening lemma to bootstrap from non‑concentration to a spectral gap. The CSP remains an open problem for many thin groups in higher rank, but it is known to fail for thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) because the congruence kernel maps onto a free profinite group of infinite rank. In rank one, the CSP is known to fail for all non‑elementary thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) by a theorem of Lubotzky. Thus the interplay between property (τ) and CSP is a sharp dividing line: property (τ) for congruence quotients is a theorem for all Zariski‑dense subgroups in any rank, while CSP is a rare property that holds only for arithmetic lattices in higher rank and fails for thin groups. This distinction underlies the success of the affine sieve and the limitations of methods that would require full profinite expansion.

In applications, one often needs to know that the spectral gap is uniform with respect to the generating set. The Bourgain–Gamburd machine provides a qualitative expander property: there exists ε > 0 depending on the generating set such that the second eigenvalue of the adjacency operator is at most 1 − ε. The constant ε is not effectively computable in general because the proof uses compactness arguments (the classification of approximate subgroups). However, for specific thin groups arising from Apollonian packings or from integral points on quadrics, one can sometimes compute explicit spectral gaps using the Selberg trace formula or by relating the Cayley graphs to Hecke operators. The interplay with CSP also appears in the study of the Zaremba conjecture: the affine sieve applied to continued fractions uses expansion in congruence quotients of the thin group generated by the Gauss map, and the failure of CSP for that thin group is precisely why the sieve only works for square‑free denominators. Understanding this interplay is essential for correctly setting up the level of distribution in any affine sieve argument.

The Use of the Bourgain–Gamburd Machine for Exceptional Groups of Type G2 and F4

The Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine was originally developed for SL₂(𝔽_p) and later extended to all finite simple groups of Lie type by Breuillard, Green, and Tao. The machine requires three main ingredients: a sum‑product estimate in the underlying field, a product theorem for the finite group, and an escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma. For classical groups such as SL_d, Sp2d, and SO_d, the sum‑product estimates are known in full generality, and the product theorem follows from the classification of approximate subgroups. For exceptional groups of type G₂ and F₄, the situation is more delicate because the groups are not defined by standard matrix representations of bounded dimension over the field; they arise as automorphism groups of octonion algebras (for G₂) and of certain Jordan algebras (for F₄). Nevertheless, the work of Pyber and Szabó, and later of Breuillard, Green, Guralnick, and Tao, established the product theorem for all finite simple groups of Lie type, including the exceptional ones, with bounds that are polynomial in the rank. The sum‑product estimate in the field 𝔽_p is independent of the group type and is available in the form of the Bourgain–Katz–Tao theorem. The escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma, which guarantees that a random walk does not stay in a proper algebraic subvariety for too long, relies on the uniform Tits alternative and on the fact that any Zariski‑dense subgroup of an exceptional algebraic group contains a free subgroup with controlled word length. This was proved by Breuillard and Gelander for all linear groups, including exceptional ones. Putting these ingredients together, one obtains that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of G₂(ℤ) or F₄(ℤ), the Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) form an expander family as q runs over square‑free integers. This is the superstrong approximation theorem for exceptional groups.

The proof for exceptional groups follows the same three‑step structure as for classical groups. First, one shows that the random walk measure μ on Γ does not concentrate on any proper subvariety of the algebraic group G. This uses the uniform Tits alternative: there exists a constant C such that any symmetric generating set of Γ contains two elements that generate a free subgroup and whose word length is at most C. The free subgroup provides exponential growth, which forces the random walk to spread quickly. Second, one applies the product theorem: if a set A ⊂ G(𝔽_p) is not too large and not too small, then A·A·A is significantly larger than A unless A is close to a subgroup. The classification of approximate subgroups in G₂(𝔽_p) and F₄(𝔽_p) shows that the only approximate subgroups are either very small, very large, or contained in a proper subgroup. Third, the flattening lemma iterates the product theorem to show that the L²‑norm of the random walk measure decays exponentially fast, which is equivalent to a spectral gap. The constants in each step depend on the rank and the type of the group, but the qualitative conclusion — existence of a uniform spectral gap — holds for all exceptional types. The main technical difference is that the representation theory of G₂(𝔽_p) and F₄(𝔽_p) is more complicated, so the explicit bounds on the spectral gap are weaker than for SL₂. However, for the affine sieve one only needs the qualitative expander property, not an explicit constant.

Applications of superstrong approximation for exceptional groups are still emerging. One direction is the study of integral points on varieties with G₂ or F₄ symmetry, such as the variety of null vectors in the 7‑dimensional representation of G₂ or the 26‑dimensional representation of F₄. The affine sieve can be applied to thin orbits on these varieties to produce almost prime points, provided one has a suitable polynomial map whose fibres are controlled. Another direction is the construction of expander graphs from thin subgroups of exceptional groups, which may have applications in computer science because the groups have small rank but large dimension, yielding expanders with good degree‑size trade‑offs. The work of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju on uniform spectral gaps for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of SL_d(ℤ) (d ≥ 3) uses the product theorem for all finite simple groups of Lie type, so their proof automatically covers exceptional groups as well. The only additional requirement is that the thin subgroup be Zariski‑dense in the exceptional algebraic group, which is a condition on the generators. In practice, one often encounters thin subgroups of G₂(ℤ) arising from the automorphism group of the integral octonions, and the superstrong approximation theorem guarantees that their congruence quotients are expanders. This opens the possibility of using the affine sieve to study arithmetic properties of integral octonions, such as the distribution of prime norms.

It is important to note that the Bourgain–Gamburd machine does not give an effective spectral gap constant for exceptional groups; the constant depends on the generating set and on the implicit constants in the product theorem and the escape lemma. For explicit applications, one may need to compute the spectral gap numerically or use alternative methods such as the trace formula for the specific thin group. Nevertheless, the qualitative expander property is sufficient for the affine sieve to produce almost prime results with a level of distribution > ½. The machine also requires that the generating set be symmetric and generate a Zariski‑dense subgroup; if the subgroup is contained in a proper algebraic subgroup, the congruence quotients will not be the full finite simple group and the product theorem does not apply directly. In that case one must first pass to the Zariski closure and apply the machine there. The extension to exceptional groups demonstrates the robustness of the Bourgain–Gamburd method: once the three pillars are established for a family of finite simple groups, superstrong approximation follows for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of the corresponding integral algebraic group.

Technique: Distinguishing Property (τ) for Congruence Quotients from Full Profinite Expansion
A thin subgroup Γ ⊂ SL₃(ℤ) is given. Explain why the existence of a uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) (q square‑free) does not imply a spectral gap for all finite‑index subgroups of Γ. What property of Γ fails that would be needed for the latter?
The uniform spectral gap for congruence quotients is property (τ) with respect to the family of principal congruence subgroups. This family corresponds to the congruence completion Γ̂_congr. The full profinite completion Γ̂ maps onto Γ̂_congr with kernel the congruence kernel. For arithmetic lattices in higher rank, the congruence subgroup property (CSP) holds, meaning the kernel is finite, so Γ̂ ≅ Γ̂_congr and property (τ) for congruence quotients lifts to all finite quotients. For thin groups, CSP fails: the congruence kernel is infinite (often a free profinite group of infinite rank). Consequently, there exist many finite‑index subgroups of Γ that are not congruence subgroups, and the Cayley graphs of Γ modulo those subgroups need not form an expander family. The failure of CSP is precisely the property that prevents the spectral gap from extending to all finite quotients. Thus, property (τ) for the congruence family is strictly weaker than property (τ) for all finite‑index subgroups, and the latter is generally false for thin groups.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about expansion in thin groups, first identify whether the statement refers to congruence quotients or to all finite quotients. If it says "congruence quotients" or "square‑free level", the answer relies on superstrong approximation (property (τ) for the congruence family). If it says "all finite‑index subgroups" or "profinite completion", the answer is typically negative because CSP fails for thin groups. Remember that property (T) of the ambient lattice does not descend to infinite‑index subgroups, so never invoke property (T) to claim expansion for a thin group. Use the distinction between congruence completion and full profinite completion as a mental checklist.

The Application of Superstrong Approximation to the Distribution of Rational Points on Homogeneous Spaces

Superstrong approximation provides a powerful tool for studying the distribution of rational points on homogeneous varieties G/H where G is a semisimple algebraic group and H is a spherical subgroup. The classical setting is the action of a thin group Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) on the set of integral points of a homogeneous space X = G/H. When Γ is Zariski‑dense, the orbits of Γ on X(ℤ) are thin subsets of the full integral points. The affine sieve, powered by the spectral gap for congruence quotients, allows one to count points in these orbits that satisfy almost prime conditions. A typical application is to the variety of integral points on a quadric hypersurface Q(x) = 0 in ℤⁿ, where G = SO(Q) and H is the stabiliser of a point. The thin group Γ might be the Apollonian group acting on Descartes quadruples, or a subgroup of the orthogonal group generated by reflections. Superstrong approximation guarantees that the reduction maps Γ → Γ/Γ(q) are surjective for all square‑free q, and that the Cayley graphs form an expander family. This surjectivity is exactly the statement that the Γ‑orbit is equidistributed modulo q, which is the level of distribution needed for the sieve. The expander mixing lemma then gives a quantitative error term of size O(|Γ/Γ(q)|1/2+ε) for the discrepancy between the actual number of points in a congruence class and the expected number. Summing over q up to a power of the height parameter yields a level of distribution > ½, which is sufficient for the beta sieve to produce almost prime points.

The method extends beyond quadrics to any homogeneous space where the stabiliser H is a spherical subgroup, i.e. a subgroup with an open Borel orbit. For such spaces, the representation theory of G provides a decomposition of L²(G/H) into irreducible representations, and the spectral gap for the congruence quotients of Γ translates into a spectral gap for the action of Γ on L²(G/H) modulo q. This was carried out by Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak for the Apollonian packing (where G = SO(3,1) and H = SO(3)) and by Kontorovich and Oh for general spherical varieties. The key technical input is the classification of approximate subgroups in the finite simple groups G(𝔽_p), which ensures the product theorem needed for the Bourgain–Gamburd machine. Once the product theorem is available for all finite simple groups of Lie type (including exceptional types), the same argument works for any Zariski‑dense subgroup of G(ℤ) acting on any spherical homogeneous space. The result is a uniform level of distribution for the Γ‑orbit on X(ℤ) modulo square‑free q, with an error term that depends only on the rank of G and the degree of the polynomial defining the sieve condition.

An important nuance is that the affine sieve does not give prime points directly; it gives points whose coordinates have a bounded number of prime factors (almost primes). The number of prime factors allowed depends on the level of distribution: a level > ½ yields r‑almost primes for some r depending on the sieve dimension. For the Apollonian packing, the sieve dimension is 1, and the level of distribution obtained from the spectral gap is 1 − ε, which gives 2‑almost primes (numbers with at most two prime factors) for the curvatures. For higher‑dimensional homogeneous spaces, the sieve dimension can be larger, and one may need a stronger level of distribution to reach a given r. The superstrong approximation theorem provides a level of distribution that is essentially 1 − ε for any Zariski‑dense thin group, because the expander mixing lemma gives square‑root cancellation in the error term. This is the optimal level one can hope for without assuming the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis for the relevant L‑functions. In practice, the constant ε depends on the spectral gap, which is not effective in general, but the qualitative statement that the level exceeds ½ is enough to guarantee the existence of infinitely many almost prime points in the orbit.

Recent work has applied this framework to the distribution of rational points on homogeneous spaces of exceptional groups. For example, the action of a thin subgroup of G₂(ℤ) on the variety of null vectors in the 7‑dimensional representation yields a thin orbit of integral points. Superstrong approximation for G₂ (proved via the Bourgain–Gamburd machine for exceptional groups) gives a spectral gap for the congruence quotients, and the affine sieve produces almost prime points on this orbit. The same method works for F₄ acting on its 26‑dimensional representation. These results illustrate the uniformity of the superstrong approximation method: once the product theorem and escape lemma are established for a family of finite simple groups, the affine sieve machinery applies to any Zariski‑dense thin subgroup of the corresponding integral algebraic group, regardless of whether the group is classical or exceptional. The only remaining obstacle for prime points (as opposed to almost primes) is the parity problem of sieve theory, which is not solved by expansion alone.

The Role of the Spectral Gap in the Counting of Integral Points on Thin Orbits via the Circle Method

The Hardy–Littlewood circle method is a classical analytic tool for counting integral solutions to polynomial equations, especially when the variety is a complete intersection of high degree. For thin orbits, the circle method faces a fundamental difficulty: the set of points is not defined by a single polynomial equation but by the orbit of a thin group, which is a sparse subset of an algebraic variety. The spectral gap for congruence quotients of the thin group provides a way to overcome this difficulty by supplying a strong form of equidistribution modulo q, which is exactly the input needed for the major arcs analysis in the circle method. In the circle method, one writes the counting function as an integral over the unit interval of an exponential sum. The major arcs correspond to rational numbers a/q with small denominator q, and the contribution of each major arc is approximated by a product of a singular series (encoding local densities) and a singular integral (encoding the real density). For a thin orbit, the singular series involves the number of points in the orbit modulo q. Superstrong approximation tells us that the orbit is equidistributed in the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q), and the expander mixing lemma gives a quantitative error term of size O(qdim X/2 + ε) for the discrepancy. This square‑root cancellation is precisely what allows one to sum the major arcs contributions up to a denominator Q that is a power of the height parameter, yielding a level of distribution > ½. The minor arcs are then controlled using the spectral gap as well: the exponential sum over the thin orbit can be bounded by the L²‑norm of the random walk measure on the finite quotients, which decays exponentially thanks to the flattening lemma. This gives a power‑saving bound for the minor arcs, completing the circle method analysis.

The combination of the circle method with superstrong approximation was pioneered by Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak for the Apollonian packing, where they counted the number of circles with curvature ≤ T that are prime or almost prime. Their approach used the spectral gap for the thin Apollonian group to get equidistribution modulo q, and then applied the beta sieve (a combinatorial sieve) rather than the full circle method. Later, Kontorovich and Oh developed a version of the circle method directly for thin orbits on homogeneous varieties, using the spectral gap to handle both major and minor arcs. The key insight is that the spectral gap gives a uniform bound for the Fourier coefficients of the orbit measure on the finite quotients, which translates into a bound for the exponential sums on the minor arcs. This bound is of the form O(Tdim X − δ) for some δ > 0, where T is the height parameter. The major arcs contribute the main term, which is a product of local densities, and the error from the major arcs is controlled by the level of distribution coming from the expander mixing lemma. The final result is an asymptotic formula for the number of integral points in the thin orbit up to height T, with a power‑saving error term. Such asymptotic formulas are rare for thin orbits; they are only possible because the spectral gap provides the necessary equidistribution.

It is important to distinguish the role of the spectral gap in the circle method from its role in the affine sieve. In the affine sieve, the spectral gap is used to get a level of distribution > ½ for a combinatorial sieve, which yields almost prime results. In the circle method, the spectral gap is used to evaluate the major arcs accurately and to bound the minor arcs, which can in principle give an asymptotic for the number of prime points (not just almost primes) if one can also handle the singular series. However, the singular series for prime points involves the von Mangoldt function, and the circle method for primes typically requires the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis or at least a zero‑free region for L‑functions. The spectral gap alone does not solve the parity problem; it only provides the equidistribution needed to set up the circle method. In practice, the circle method for thin orbits has so far produced almost prime results as well, because the singular series for primes is not accessible without additional hypotheses. Nevertheless, the spectral gap is the essential analytic input that makes any asymptotic counting possible for thin orbits, whether via the sieve or the circle method.

Recent advances have extended the circle method to thin orbits on varieties of higher dimension and higher degree, using the uniform spectral gap for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of SL_d(ℤ) (d ≥ 3) proved by Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju. Their theorem gives a spectral gap that is uniform in the generating set, which means the implied constants in the circle method depend only on the rank and the degree of the defining polynomials, not on the particular thin group. This uniformity is crucial for applications where one varies the thin group in a family, such as in the study of the Zaremba conjecture for continued fractions. There, the thin group is the semigroup generated by the Gauss map matrices, and the circle method with superstrong approximation yields an asymptotic for the number of reduced fractions with denominator ≤ T that satisfy a given congruence condition. The spectral gap ensures that the error term is a power saving, which is enough to prove that the set of denominators has positive density. The interplay between the spectral gap, the expander mixing lemma, and the circle method thus forms a complete analytic framework for counting integral points on thin orbits, bridging the gap between combinatorial sieves and analytic number theory.

  • Believing the affine sieve produces prime points directly; it only yields almost primes, and the parity problem remains unsolved.

The Role of the Spectral Gap in the Quantitative Oppenheim Conjecture for Thin Orbits

The quantitative Oppenheim conjecture asks for an asymptotic formula with a power‑saving error term for the number of integral vectors in a thin orbit of a quadratic form that take values in a given interval. For a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of SO(Q)(ℤ) where Q is an indefinite quadratic form of signature (p,q) with p+q ≥ 3, the orbit Γ·v₀ is thin when Γ has infinite index in the arithmetic lattice. The spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) provides the equidistribution of the orbit on the homogeneous space G/Γ(q) with a rate that is uniform in the level q. This uniform rate translates, via the Duke–Rudnick–Sarnak effective equidistribution theorem, into a level of distribution θ > 1/2 for the values Q(γ·v₀) as γ ranges over a ball of radius T in Γ. The level of distribution is exactly the exponent that appears in the error term of the asymptotic for the number of representations of integers by the quadratic form along the thin orbit. When θ > 1/2, the beta sieve yields an asymptotic for almost prime values, and the circle method combined with the spectral gap can be pushed to give an asymptotic for prime values under the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis. The key mechanism is the expander mixing lemma: for any subsets A,B of the finite quotient Γ/Γ(q) one has |μ(A∩B)−μ(A)μ(B)| ≤ λ μ(A)¹ᐟ² μ(B)¹ᐟ² where λ < 1 is the spectral gap constant. This bound is uniform in q because the family of Cayley graphs forms a family of expanders. The uniformity is essential; without it the implied constants in the level of distribution would depend on the generating set of Γ and the quantitative Oppenheim conjecture would not hold for all thin subgroups simultaneously. Recent work of Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak, extended by Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju, shows that the spectral gap is uniform for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of SLₙ(ℤ) with n ≥ 3, and for thin subgroups of SO(Q)(ℤ) when the rank of Q is at least two. The quantitative Oppenheim conjecture for thin orbits is therefore a direct corollary of superstrong approximation together with the affine sieve machinery.

Uniform Expansion in Congruence Quotients of Thin Subgroups of Exceptional Groups

Exceptional algebraic groups of types G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈ admit faithful representations of relatively low dimension, which allows one to embed their integral points into SLₙ(ℤ) for modest n. A thin subgroup Γ of an exceptional group G(ℤ) is a finitely generated Zariski‑dense subgroup of infinite index. The Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine, originally developed for SL₂(𝔽ₚ), has been adapted to all finite simple groups of Lie type by Breuillard, Green, and Tao, and subsequently by Pyber and Szabó. The machine requires three ingredients: a product theorem (approximate subgroups are controlled), a non‑concentration estimate (random walks do not concentrate on proper subgroups), and a flattening lemma (the L² norm of the random walk measure decays exponentially). For exceptional groups the product theorem is supplied by the classification of approximate subgroups in the Breuillard–Green–Tao theorem, which is uniform across all types. The non‑concentration estimate follows from the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma, which uses the fact that any proper algebraic subgroup of an exceptional group has codimension at least two. The flattening lemma is proved by a sum‑product argument in the Lie algebra, exploiting the fact that the adjoint representation of an exceptional group is irreducible and has no non‑trivial invariant subspaces. The resulting spectral gap is uniform in the generating set of Γ, but the constant depends on the rank and the type of the exceptional group. This uniformity is crucial for applications such as the Zaremba conjecture for continued fractions in higher rank, where one varies the thin group in a family. The uniform expansion also implies a uniform diameter bound of the form O((log |G(𝔽_q)|)ᶜ) for the Cayley graphs of G(𝔽_q) with respect to the images of a fixed generating set of Γ. Such diameter bounds feed into the affine sieve to give a level of distribution that is independent of the particular thin subgroup, allowing one to treat families of thin groups simultaneously.

Worked Example: Spectral Gap Implies Level of Distribution
Let Γ be a Zariski‑dense subgroup of SL₃(ℤ) generated by a symmetric set S. Suppose the family of Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S mod q) is a family of expanders with spectral gap λ < 1 uniform in q. Show that for any polynomial map f: Γ → ℤ of degree d, the sequence {f(γ) : γ ∈ B_T} has level of distribution θ > 1/2, where B_T is the ball of radius T in the word metric.
The expander mixing lemma gives, for any subsets A,B ⊂ Γ/Γ(q), |μ_q(A∩B)−μ_q(A)μ_q(B)| ≤ λ μ_q(A)¹ᐟ² μ_q(B)¹ᐟ². Take A = {γ mod q : f(γ) ≡ 0 (mod q)} and B = the image of B_T. The measure of A is ≍ q⁻¹ because f is a non‑constant polynomial map on a Zariski‑dense subgroup, so its zero set modulo q has codimension one. The measure of B is ≍ |B_T|/|Γ/Γ(q)|. The mixing lemma then bounds the discrepancy between the actual number of γ ∈ B_T with f(γ) ≡ 0 (mod q) and the expected number by λ |B_T|¹ᐟ² q⁻¹ᐟ² |Γ/Γ(q)|¹ᐟ². Summing over q ≤ Q and using the uniform spectral gap, the total error is O(|B_T|¹ᐟ² Q¹ᐟ²). Choosing Q = |B_T|^(1−ε) yields an error o(|B_T|) provided ε > 0. Hence the level of distribution θ can be taken as any number < 1, in particular θ > 1/2.

This three‑step template (mixing lemma → discrepancy bound → level of distribution) appears in almost every problem on the affine sieve.

The Affine Sieve for Polynomial Maps on Thin Orbits and the Distribution of Values of Quadratic Forms

The affine sieve, introduced by Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak, is a combinatorial sieve that operates on the set of values f(γ) where γ runs over a thin orbit Γ·x₀ and f is a polynomial map defined over ℤ. The sieve requires a level of distribution θ > 1/2 for the sequence {f(γ)} in arithmetic progressions. Superstrong approximation provides exactly this level because the expander mixing lemma gives a power‑saving error term for the equidistribution of the orbit modulo q. For quadratic forms, one takes f(γ) = Q(γ·v₀) where Q is an integral quadratic form. The thin orbit is the set of values of Q on the orbit of a fixed vector v₀ under a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of the orthogonal group SO(Q)(ℤ). The affine sieve then produces an asymptotic formula for the number of γ in a ball of radius T such that Q(γ·v₀) has at most r prime factors, where r depends on the degree of f and the level of distribution. The parity problem prevents one from reaching r = 1 (prime values) unconditionally; the best unconditional results give almost primes with a bounded number of prime factors. The sieve weight is constructed from the beta sieve, and the error terms are controlled by the expander mixing lemma uniformly in the modulus q. The uniformity is essential because the sieve sums over moduli up to a power of T, and any dependence of the spectral gap on the generating set would destroy the asymptotic. Recent work of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju shows that the spectral gap is uniform for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of SLₙ(ℤ) with n ≥ 3, which implies a uniform level of distribution for all such thin groups. Consequently, the affine sieve yields almost prime values for quadratic forms on any thin orbit in higher rank, with constants that depend only on the rank and the degree of the form, not on the particular thin subgroup.

The Connection Between Thin Groups and the Theory of Automorphic Forms on Higher Rank Groups

Thin subgroups of higher rank semisimple groups give rise to automorphic forms on the associated symmetric spaces that are not square‑integrable on the full arithmetic quotient but are square‑integrable on the thin quotient. The spectral theory of the Laplacian on the locally symmetric space ΓG/K, where Γ is a thin subgroup, is intimately related to the representation theory of G(ℝ) and the congruence quotients of Γ. The superstrong approximation theorem implies that the family of congruence covers Γ/Γ(q) forms a family of expanders, which in turn implies a uniform spectral gap for the Laplacian on the finite quotients. This spectral gap lifts to a spectral gap for the continuous spectrum on the thin quotient via the Selberg trace formula adapted to thin groups. The trace formula for thin groups involves a sum over conjugacy classes in Γ and a spectral sum over the automorphic spectrum. The uniform spectral gap ensures that the continuous spectrum starts at a positive distance from zero, which is the analytic incarnation of property (τ) for the family of congruence quotients. This property (τ) is weaker than Kazhdan's property (T) but suffices for many applications, including the affine sieve and effective equidistribution. The connection with automorphic forms also appears in the work of Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak on the distribution of integral points on thin orbits: the counting function is expressed as an integral of a kernel against an automorphic form, and the spectral gap gives a power‑saving bound for the error term. In higher rank, the theory of Eisenstein series and the Langlands program provide a framework to understand the automorphic spectrum on thin quotients, and the uniform spectral gap is a key input for the meromorphic continuation and functional equations of the associated L‑functions.

Effective Equidistribution of Thin Orbits on Homogeneous Spaces via the Spectral Gap

Let G be a semisimple algebraic group over ℚ, Γ a Zariski‑dense subgroup of G(ℤ), and X = G(ℝ)/K a symmetric space. The orbit Γ·x₀ in X is dense when Γ is Zariski‑dense. Effective equidistribution asks for a rate of convergence of the averages (1/|B_T|) ∑γ∈B_T δγ·x₀ to the Haar measure on X, where B_T is a ball of radius T in the word metric. The spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) provides a quantitative version of the strong approximation theorem: the reduction map Γ → Γ/Γ(q) is surjective for all sufficiently large q, and the Cayley graphs are expanders. Using the expander mixing lemma, one obtains a bound for the discrepancy of the orbit modulo q that is uniform in q. This bound is then lifted to the archimedean quotient via the Duke–Rudnick–Sarnak method, which compares the orbit on X with its projections to the finite quotients. The result is an effective equidistribution statement with a power‑saving error term: for any smooth function f on X with compact support, |(1/|B_T|) ∑γ∈B_T f(γ·x₀) − ∫_X f dμ| ≤ C_f T−δ for some δ > 0 depending only on the spectral gap. The constant C_f depends on Sobolev norms of f. This effective equidistribution is the analytic engine behind the affine sieve and the circle method for thin orbits. It also implies a quantitative version of the Oppenheim conjecture for thin orbits, as well as bounds for the number of integral points on thin orbits in expanding horospheres. The uniformity of the spectral gap across all Zariski‑dense subgroups of a given higher rank group, proved by Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju, means that the exponent δ can be chosen uniformly for all such thin groups, which is essential for applications where the thin group varies in a family.

The Selberg Eigenvalue Conjecture for Thin Subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) and the Magee–Oh–Winter Theorem

The Selberg eigenvalue conjecture asserts that for any congruence subgroup Γ₀(N) ⊂ SL₂(ℤ), the smallest non‑zero eigenvalue λ₁ of the hyperbolic Laplacian on the modular surface Γ₀(N)\ℍ satisfies λ₁ ≥ ¼. For thin subgroups Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) that are Zariski‑dense but of infinite index, the conjecture is false in general: there exist thin groups with λ₁ arbitrarily close to 0. However, a uniform spectral gap — a lower bound λ₁ ≥ δ > 0 independent of the thin subgroup — is the analytic heart of superstrong approximation. The breakthrough of Magee, Oh, and Winter (2020–2022) established that for any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) generated by a fixed symmetric set S, the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) form a family of expanders with a spectral gap depending only on S, not on the thin group itself. Their proof combines the Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine with a new “escape from subvarieties” argument that works uniformly for all Zariski‑dense subgroups. The key innovation is a quantitative non‑concentration estimate for random walks on Γ that avoids the need for the sum–product theorem in SL₂(𝔽ₚ) to hold uniformly across all generating sets. Instead, they use the classification of approximate subgroups in SL₂(𝔽ₚ) (Helfgott’s theorem, extended by Breuillard–Green–Tao) to show that any symmetric generating set either expands or is trapped in a proper subgroup, and the latter case is ruled out by Zariski density. This yields a uniform spectral gap for the Laplacian on the finite quotients Γ/Γ(q), which lifts to a uniform spectral gap for the continuous spectrum on the infinite‑volume hyperbolic surface Γ\ℍ via the Selberg trace formula adapted to thin groups by Oh and Winter. The exponent δ in the spectral gap is effective but not explicit; it depends on the word length of the generators in SL₂(ℤ). This result resolves a long‑standing question of Sarnak and implies effective equidistribution of thin orbits on the modular surface with a power‑saving error term uniform in the thin group.

Thin Subgroups, Non‑Congruence Modular Forms, and the Atkin–Swinnerton‑Dyer Theory

Classical modular forms are functions on the upper half‑plane invariant under a congruence subgroup of SL₂(ℤ). Thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) that are not commensurable with any congruence subgroup give rise to non‑congruence modular forms. The theory of such forms, developed by Atkin and Swinnerton‑Dyer in the 1970s, reveals a striking arithmetic property: the Fourier coefficients of non‑congruence modular forms satisfy congruences modulo primes that are governed by the representation theory of the thin group. Specifically, if Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) is a thin subgroup of level N (the smallest integer such that Γ contains the principal congruence subgroup Γ(N)), then the space of modular forms of weight k for Γ carries an action of the Hecke operators at primes p ∤ N. Atkin and Swinnerton‑Dyer proved that the Fourier coefficients aₙ of a normalized Hecke eigenform satisfy aₙ ≡ aₙ₍ₚ₎ (mod p) for almost all primes p, where the congruence is controlled by the Frobenius eigenvalues in the ℓ‑adic Galois representation attached to the thin group. This is a thin‑group analogue of the Eichler–Shimura relation for congruence subgroups. The connection to superstrong approximation appears when one considers the reduction of the thin group modulo p: the expander property of the Cayley graphs Γ/Γ(p) implies that the Hecke eigenvalues are equidistributed with respect to the Sato–Tate measure, a result of Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak for certain thin groups. The Atkin–Swinnerton‑Dyer congruences also imply that the L‑functions of non‑congruence modular forms have analytic continuation and functional equation, but the precise nature of the Euler product remains mysterious. Recent work of Scholl and others has constructed ℓ‑adic Galois representations for non‑congruence forms, showing that the image of the Galois representation is closely related to the Zariski closure of the thin group. The failure of the congruence subgroup property for thin groups is reflected in the fact that these Galois representations are not induced from automorphic representations of GL₂ in the usual sense. This area connects the spectral theory of thin groups, the arithmetic of modular forms, and the Langlands program for non‑congruence subgroups.

Worked Example: Spectral Gap Implies Almost Prime Counting
Let Γ = ⟨ (1 2; 0 1), (1 0; 2 1) ⟩ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) be the thin subgroup generated by two parabolic elements. Let f(x,y) = x² + y² be a polynomial on the orbit Γ·(1,0). Using the affine sieve, show that the number of γ ∈ Γ with word length ≤ T such that f(γ·(1,0)) has at most R prime factors is ≫ T^δ / (log T) for some δ > 0, provided R is sufficiently large. Explain why the spectral gap for Γ/Γ(q) is essential for the level of distribution to exceed ½.
Step 1: Set up the affine sieve. The orbit Γ·(1,0) consists of primitive integer vectors (x,y) with x odd, y even. The polynomial f(x,y) = x² + y² takes values on this orbit. We want to count γ ∈ B_T (ball of radius T in word metric) such that f(γ·v₀) has ≤ R prime factors. Step 2: Level of distribution. The affine sieve requires a level of distribution θ > ½. This means that for any squarefree q ≤ Q = T^θ, the number of γ ∈ B_T with q | f(γ·v₀) is (1/q) |B_T| + O(|B_T| / q1+ε) uniformly in q. The main term comes from equidistribution of the orbit modulo q. Step 3: Expander mixing lemma. The Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S_q) are expanders with spectral gap δ > 0 uniform in q (by Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak for this specific Γ, or Magee–Oh–Winter uniformly). The expander mixing lemma gives |{γ ∈ B_T : γ ≡ g (mod q)}| = |B_T|/|Γ/Γ(q)| + O(|B_T| q-δ') for some δ' > 0 depending on the spectral gap. Summing over g with f(g·v₀) ≡ 0 (mod q) yields the level of distribution with θ = δ'/(1+δ') > ½ provided δ' > 1. Step 4: Beta sieve. With θ > ½, the beta sieve (Rosser–Iwaniec) produces a lower bound for the number of almost primes. The sifting parameter R must satisfy R > 2/θ. Since θ > ½, we can take R = 3 (or 4 depending on constants). The lower bound is ≫ |B_T| / (log T) ≫ T^δ / (log T) where δ is the growth exponent of Γ (the critical exponent of the Poincaré series). Step 5: Why spectral gap is essential. Without a uniform spectral gap, the error term in equidistribution modulo q could be as large as the main term, giving θ = 0. The expander property is exactly what upgrades qualitative equidistribution (strong approximation) to quantitative equidistribution with power‑saving error, which is the level of distribution θ > ½ needed for the sieve to produce almost primes.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing Qualitative vs Quantitative Approximation
Examiners often test whether you understand that strong approximation (surjectivity of Γ → Γ/Γ(q) for all large q) is qualitative, while superstrong approximation (expander family) is quantitative. In a proof‑outline question, always state: (1) strong approximation gives equidistribution modulo q but no rate; (2) the spectral gap for the Laplacian on Γ/Γ(q) gives a power‑saving error via the expander mixing lemma; (3) this power‑saving is the level of distribution θ > ½; (4) the beta sieve then converts θ > ½ into an almost‑prime lower bound. Missing any link loses marks. Remember: the parity problem prevents reaching primes (R=1); the affine sieve only gives almost primes (R ≥ 2).

Superstrong Approximation for Thin Subgroups of S‑Arithmetic Groups and Adelic Expansion

The classical theory of superstrong approximation considers a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) where G is a semisimple algebraic group over ℚ, and studies the expansion of the Cayley graphs of the finite quotients Γ/Γ(q) as q varies over integers. A far‑reaching generalization, due to Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju (2013) and extended by Liu and others, replaces ℤ by the ring of S‑integers 𝒪_S for a finite set of places S containing the archimedean place. Let G be a simply connected, absolutely almost simple algebraic group over a number field K, and let Γ ⊂ G(𝒪_S) be a Zariski‑dense subgroup. For each finite place v ∉ S, let Γ(v) be the kernel of reduction modulo v. The S‑arithmetic superstrong approximation theorem states that the family of Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(v), S_v) forms an expander family as v varies over all finite places outside S, provided Γ is not virtually solvable. The proof uses the Bourgain–Gamburd machine in the S‑arithmetic setting: one needs a product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type over the residue fields 𝒪_K/v (Breuillard–Green–Tao), a non‑concentration estimate for random walks on Γ (escape from subvarieties in the S‑arithmetic group), and a flattening lemma for the random walk measure on the adelic group G(𝔸_S). The adelic perspective is crucial: the group G(𝔸_S) is a locally compact group, and the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(v) are discrete subgroups of the compact open subgroups G(𝒪_v). The expander family is equivalent to a spectral gap for the averaging operator on L²₀(G(𝔸_S)/Γ), the space of L² functions on the adelic quotient orthogonal to constants. This adelic spectral gap implies strong approximation for the S‑arithmetic group (the diagonal embedding of Γ into G(𝔸_S) is dense) and effective equidistribution of S‑arithmetic orbits on homogeneous spaces. Applications include the affine sieve for S‑integral points on homogeneous varieties, the Zaremba conjecture for continued fractions with S‑integer partial quotients, and the distribution of rational points on thin orbits over global fields of positive characteristic (where the theory connects to the work of Breuillard–Varju on expansion in finite simple groups of Lie type over function fields). The S‑arithmetic setting also reveals that the spectral gap is uniform across all Zariski‑dense subgroups of a given G(𝒪_S), a result that fails if one allows the field K to vary.

Diophantine Approximation on Homogeneous Spaces and the Einsiedler–Katok–Lindenstrauss Theory for Thin Orbits

The connection between thin groups and Diophantine approximation arises from the action of a thin subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) on a homogeneous space X = G(ℝ)/H(ℝ), where H is a closed subgroup. The orbit Γ·x₀ is a discrete set in X, and one asks for the rate of approximation of a point y ∈ X by points of this orbit. The classical theory of Diophantine approximation on homogeneous spaces, developed by Margulis, Dani, Kleinbock, Margulis, and others, uses the dynamics of unipotent flows and the theory of measure rigidity. Einsiedler, Katok, and Lindenstrauss (2006) proved a breakthrough measure‑rigidity theorem for higher‑rank diagonalizable actions: any invariant ergodic measure for a higher‑rank diagonalizable action on a homogeneous space is algebraic. For thin orbits, the relevant action is not by a higher‑rank diagonalizable group but by the thin group itself, which is a discrete subgroup. However, the spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) provides a substitute for higher‑rank dynamics. Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak showed that the expander property implies a quantitative non‑divergence estimate for thin orbits: the orbit Γ·x₀ does not spend too much time in small neighbourhoods of proper subvarieties. This quantitative non‑divergence, combined with the effective equidistribution of thin orbits (itself a consequence of the spectral gap), yields a Diophantine approximation result: for any smooth function f on X with compact support, the discrepancy |(1/|B_T|) ∑γ∈B_T f(γ·x₀) − ∫_X f dμ| is bounded by C_f T-δ for some δ > 0. This power‑saving error term is exactly the input needed for the quantitative Oppenheim conjecture for thin orbits (proved by Gorodnik and Nevo for lattices, extended to thin groups by Oh and Winter). The connection to the Littlewood conjecture and the Duffin–Schaeffer conjecture appears when X is the space of lattices and Γ is a thin subgroup of SLₙ(ℤ) acting on the space of unimodular lattices. The spectral gap for thin groups gives a power‑saving bound for the number of γ ∈ B_T such that the lattice γ·Λ₀ has a short vector, which is a thin‑orbit version of the classical result of Dani on Diophantine approximation on the space of lattices. The Einsiedler–Katok–Lindenstrauss measure‑rigidity theorem for higher‑rank actions is used to classify the possible limiting measures of thin orbits under the action of a higher‑rank diagonalizable subgroup of G(ℝ), showing that any weak‑* limit of the orbit measures is algebraic, which in turn implies the effective equidistribution with power‑saving error.

Quantum Unique Ergodicity and Arithmetic Quantum Chaos for Thin Quotients

The spectral theory of thin groups has a profound connection to quantum chaos through the study of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on the hyperbolic surface Γ\ℍ for a thin subgroup Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ). Quantum unique ergodicity (QUE), conjectured by Rudnick and Sarnak, asserts that for a sequence of L²‑normalized eigenfunctions φ_j with eigenvalues λ_j → ∞ on a compact (or finite‑volume) hyperbolic surface, the probability measures |φ_j|² dμ converge weakly to the uniform (Liouville) measure. For congruence subgroups, QUE was proved by Lindenstrauss (2006) using measure‑rigidity for higher‑rank diagonalizable actions on the space of lattices, and by Soundararajan (2010) for holomorphic forms using L‑function methods. For thin subgroups, the situation is dramatically different: the surface Γ\ℍ has infinite volume, and the Laplacian has both discrete and continuous spectrum. The continuous spectrum is parametrized by Eisenstein series, and the discrete spectrum consists of Maass cusp forms. The work of Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak on the spectral gap for thin groups implies that the continuous spectrum starts at λ = ¼ + δ for some δ > 0 (the spectral gap). This means there are no small eigenvalues in the continuous spectrum, which is a prerequisite for QUE. However, the infinite volume introduces new phenomena: the Eisenstein series are not L², and the QUE conjecture must be formulated for the discrete spectrum only. Recent work of Magee, Naud, and Oh (2021) established a version of QUE for thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) with a spectral gap: the microlocal lifts of the discrete eigenfunctions equidistribute on the unit tangent bundle of Γ\ℍ with respect to the Liouville measure, provided the spectral gap is larger than ¼ (i.e., δ > 0). The proof uses the expander property of the congruence quotients to control the matrix elements of the Hecke operators, combined with the thermodynamic formalism for the geodesic flow on the infinite‑volume surface. The connection to the Selberg trace formula for thin groups appears in the analysis of the spectral measure: the trace formula expresses the spectral sum over discrete eigenvalues and the continuous integral in terms of a geometric sum over conjugacy classes in Γ. The spectral gap ensures that the continuous integral contributes only at high energy, allowing the discrete spectrum to be isolated. This area also connects to the theory of quantum chaos on graphs: the Cayley graphs Γ/Γ(q) are finite graphs, and their eigenfunctions (which are the restrictions of automorphic forms to the finite quotients) exhibit QUE as q → ∞, a result of Brooks and Lindenstrauss for regular graphs, extended to expander families by Anantharaman and Le Masson. The thin‑group setting thus provides a bridge between arithmetic quantum chaos on infinite‑volume hyperbolic surfaces and quantum chaos on expander graphs.

The Role of the Spectral Gap in the Quantitative Non‑Divergence of Unipotent Flows on Thin Orbits

Quantitative non‑divergence estimates for unipotent flows on homogeneous spaces are a cornerstone of modern homogeneous dynamics. For a thin subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) with G a semisimple algebraic group, the orbit ΓG(ℝ) carries a natural G(ℝ)‑action. The spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) — supplied by superstrong approximation — translates into an effective decay of matrix coefficients for the unitary representation of G(ℝ) on L²(ΓG(ℝ)). This decay, in turn, yields a quantitative version of the Dani–Margulis non‑divergence theorem: for any compact set K ⊂ ΓG(ℝ) and any ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that the measure of points whose forward unipotent trajectory leaves K before time T is bounded by C T⁻ᵟ. The exponent δ depends explicitly on the uniform spectral gap λ₁ of the family of Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) and on the dimension of the unipotent subgroup. In the rank‑one case G = SL₂, the work of Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak shows that λ₁ ≥ δ₀ > 0 for any Zariski‑dense Γ generated by a fixed symmetric set S. This uniform gap feeds directly into the effective equidistribution of expanding horospheres on the infinite‑volume hyperbolic surface Γ\ℍ, giving power‑saving error terms in the counting of lattice points on thin orbits. For higher rank groups, the Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem provides a uniform spectral gap for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of simply connected, absolutely almost simple groups, and the same mechanism produces quantitative non‑divergence for unipotent flows on ΓG(ℝ) with explicit exponents. The argument proceeds by lifting the unipotent flow to the adelic space, applying the expander mixing lemma on each congruence quotient, and then using a spectral gap to control the contribution of the continuous spectrum. The resulting bounds are uniform in the level q and are essential for applications such as the affine sieve, where one needs level of distribution exceeding ½.

The proof of quantitative non‑divergence via the spectral gap follows a three‑step scheme. First, one establishes a uniform spectral gap for the family of congruence quotients, i.e. property (τ) with respect to the family of principal congruence subgroups. Second, one uses the spectral gap to obtain an effective mean ergodic theorem for the unipotent action on L²(ΓG(ℝ)) with a power‑saving rate. Third, one applies the effective mean ergodic theorem to the characteristic function of a compact set, yielding the desired measure estimate. The uniformity of the spectral gap across all levels q is crucial; without it the implied constants would depend on q and the argument would not give a power‑saving bound uniform in the height of the orbit. In the thin‑group setting the congruence subgroup property fails, so the family of congruence quotients does not exhaust all finite quotients. Nevertheless, the superstrong approximation theorem guarantees that the congruence quotients form an expander family, which is exactly the input needed for the quantitative non‑divergence argument. This interplay between expansion and homogeneous dynamics is a recurring theme: the expander property provides the spectral gap, the spectral gap gives effective equidistribution, and effective equidistribution yields quantitative non‑divergence.

Applications of quantitative non‑divergence for thin orbits include the effective Oppenheim conjecture for indefinite quadratic forms evaluated on thin orbits, the distribution of integral points on quadric hypersurfaces, and the Zaremba conjecture on continued fractions. In each case the spectral gap for the relevant thin subgroup supplies the level of distribution required by the affine sieve. For example, in the work of Bourgain and Kontorovich on the Zaremba conjecture, the thin subgroup is the semigroup generated by the matrices (1 2; 0 1) and (1 0; 2 1) inside SL₂(ℤ). The uniform spectral gap for its congruence quotients, proved via the Bourgain–Gamburd machine, gives a level of distribution 1 − ε, which is sufficient to run the beta sieve and produce almost‑prime denominators. The same pattern appears in the work of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju on the affine sieve for higher rank groups: the uniform spectral gap for thin subgroups of SLₙ (n ≥ 3) yields a level of distribution arbitrarily close to 1, allowing the sieve to produce points with a bounded number of prime factors. Thus the spectral gap is not merely a spectral curiosity; it is the engine that drives quantitative arithmetic applications on thin orbits.

It is important to distinguish the uniform spectral gap for congruence quotients from a spectral gap for the Laplacian on the locally symmetric space ΓG(ℝ)/K. The latter is a statement about the continuous spectrum of the Laplacian and is known to fail for many thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) (the Selberg eigenvalue conjecture is false in general). The former, however, is a statement about the family of finite graphs Γ/Γ(q) and holds for all Zariski‑dense subgroups thanks to superstrong approximation. The quantitative non‑divergence argument uses only the expander property of the finite quotients, not a Laplacian spectral gap on the infinite‑volume space. This distinction is a frequent source of confusion: one might mistakenly believe that the Selberg eigenvalue conjecture is needed for the affine sieve, whereas the correct input is the uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients, which is a strictly weaker and provable statement.

In summary, the uniform spectral gap for congruence quotients of thin subgroups — the hallmark of superstrong approximation — provides the analytic input for quantitative non‑divergence of unipotent flows. Through the expander mixing lemma and effective mean ergodic theorems, this gap yields power‑saving error terms in equidistribution statements, which in turn supply the level of distribution required by the affine sieve. The mechanism works uniformly across all ranks and all Zariski‑dense subgroups, making it a central tool in the modern theory of thin groups.

The Use of the Bourgain–Gamburd Machine for Thin Subgroups of Exceptional Groups of Type E6, E7, E8

The Bourgain–Gamburd expansion machine, originally developed for SL₂(𝔽ₚ), has been extended to all finite simple groups of Lie type, including the exceptional groups E₆, E₇, E₈. The machine requires three ingredients: a product theorem (growth in the group), a non‑concentration estimate (escape from subvarieties), and a flattening lemma (random walk becomes equidistributed). For exceptional groups, the product theorem is supplied by the Breuillard–Green–Tao classification of approximate subgroups, which holds uniformly for all finite simple groups of Lie type of bounded rank. The non‑concentration estimate relies on the fact that any proper algebraic subgroup of an exceptional group has codimension at least 2, and on the existence of a strong generating set for the thin subgroup that avoids proper subvarieties. The flattening lemma uses the spectral gap for the random walk on the finite group, which follows from the product theorem and non‑concentration via the Bourgain–Gamburd iteration. When the thin subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) is Zariski‑dense in an exceptional group G of type E₆, E₇ or E₈, the reduction maps Γ → G(𝔽ₚ) are surjective for all sufficiently large primes p (by strong approximation for the simply connected cover). The Bourgain–Gamburd machine then shows that the Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(p), S) form a family of expanders with a uniform spectral gap depending only on the generating set S. This result is due to Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju, who proved superstrong approximation for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of simply connected, absolutely almost simple groups over ℤ, including the exceptional types.

The extension to exceptional groups is not a mere formality. The dimensions of the minimal representations of E₆, E₇, E₈ are large (27, 56, 248 respectively), and the structure of their proper algebraic subgroups is more intricate than for classical groups. The product theorem of Breuillard–Green–Tao provides a uniform bound: any K‑approximate subgroup of G(𝔽ₚ) is either contained in a proper subgroup or is almost the whole group, with constants depending only on the rank. For exceptional groups the rank is at most 8, so the constants are absolute. The non‑concentration estimate uses the fact that the word map w : Gᵏ → G given by a non‑trivial word is dominant, and its fibres have dimension strictly smaller than dim G. By a theorem of Larsen and Pink, the image of a Zariski‑dense subgroup under a word map cannot be concentrated in a proper subvariety unless the word is a law for the group. Since exceptional groups satisfy no non‑trivial laws, the non‑concentration estimate holds. The flattening lemma then follows from the standard Bourgain–Gamburd argument: the L²‑norm of the random walk measure decays exponentially after O(log |G|) steps, which implies a uniform spectral gap. The uniformity across all primes p is essential for superstrong approximation.

Applications of expansion in exceptional groups include the affine sieve for thin orbits in the 27‑dimensional representation of E₆, the 56‑dimensional representation of E₇, and the adjoint representation of E₈. For instance, the orbit of a highest weight vector under a thin subgroup of E₆(ℤ) yields integral points on a certain cubic hypersurface (the determinant on the exceptional Jordan algebra). The uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients gives a level of distribution exceeding ½, allowing the beta sieve to produce almost‑prime points on this hypersurface. Similarly, thin subgroups of E₇(ℤ) acting on the 56‑dimensional representation give integral points on a quartic hypersurface related to the Freudenthal triple system. The expansion property also implies effective equidistribution of these thin orbits on the corresponding homogeneous spaces, with power‑saving error terms. These results illustrate that the Bourgain–Gamburd machine is not limited to classical groups; it provides a uniform framework for expansion in all finite simple groups of Lie type, and consequently for superstrong approximation in all Zariski‑dense subgroups of simply connected algebraic groups over ℤ.

The proof of the product theorem for exceptional groups uses the classification of maximal subgroups due to Liebeck and Seitz. Any proper subgroup of G(𝔽ₚ) is either a subfield subgroup, a subgroup of a proper parabolic, or a subgroup of a maximal rank subgroup. The approximate subgroup argument shows that if a set A ⊂ G(𝔽ₚ) has small tripling, then it must be largely contained in one of these structural subgroups. By iterating the argument and using the fact that the rank is bounded, one obtains the desired growth. The non‑concentration estimate is proved by induction on the length of the word, using the fact that the derivative of the word map is surjective at a generic point. The flattening lemma then follows from the standard L²‑flattening argument, which relies on the quasirandomness of the finite simple groups (a lower bound on the dimension of non‑trivial representations). For exceptional groups the minimal non‑trivial representation dimensions are known explicitly, providing the necessary quasirandomness constant. The combination of these three ingredients yields the uniform spectral gap for the family of Cayley graphs, completing the Bourgain–Gamburd machine for exceptional groups.

It is worth noting that the uniform spectral gap for thin subgroups of exceptional groups does not imply property (T) for the thin subgroup itself. Property (T) is a property of the ambient lattice (which holds for higher rank groups) but does not pass to infinite‑index subgroups. The expander property is a weaker statement, often called property (τ) with respect to the family of congruence subgroups. The distinction is important: property (τ) is exactly what is needed for the affine sieve and for quantitative equidistribution, while property (T) would be too strong and is generally false for thin subgroups. The Bourgain–Gamburd machine thus provides the optimal spectral input for arithmetic applications on thin orbits in exceptional groups.

The Connection Between Thin Groups and the Theory of Random Matrix Theory for Congruence Quotients

Random matrix theory (RMT) predicts statistical properties of eigenvalues of large random matrices from classical compact groups. In the context of thin groups, the Hecke operators acting on the space of automorphic forms for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) have eigenvalue distributions that, after appropriate scaling, converge to the eigenvalue distributions of random matrices from the unitary, orthogonal or symplectic ensembles, depending on the symmetry type of the underlying representation. This connection was first observed by Sarnak for congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) and later extended to thin subgroups by Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak. The key input is the uniform spectral gap for the family of Cayley graphs, which implies that the Hecke operators are quasi‑random: their matrix coefficients decay rapidly, and their eigenvalues (apart from the trivial eigenvalue 1) lie in a disk of radius 1 − δ. When one considers the normalized eigenvalues λ/√(deg), the limiting distribution as q → ∞ matches the Sato–Tate distribution for the corresponding classical group. For thin subgroups of SL₂, the symmetry type is unitary (since the representation is complex), and the limiting distribution is the semicircle law. For thin subgroups of higher rank groups, the symmetry type can be orthogonal or symplectic, leading to the Tracy–Widom distributions at the edge. The proof uses the moment method: one computes the trace of powers of the Hecke operator, which equals the number of closed walks of a given length in the Cayley graph. The expander property ensures that the number of such walks is close to the expected number in a random regular graph, and the difference is controlled by the spectral gap. This yields convergence of moments to those of the appropriate random matrix ensemble.

The RMT connection has concrete arithmetic consequences. For example, the distribution of low‑lying zeros of L‑functions associated to automorphic forms on thin quotients is expected to follow the same symmetry types as the eigenvalues of the Hecke operators. This is the Katz–Sarnak philosophy, which predicts that the n‑level density of zeros matches the n‑level correlation of eigenvalues of random matrices from the corresponding classical compact group. For thin subgroups of SL₂, the symmetry type is unitary, so the zeros should behave like eigenvalues of random unitary matrices. Numerical experiments by Booker, Strömbergsson and others support this prediction. Moreover, the RMT framework provides a heuristic for the variance of error terms in equidistribution problems: the variance is governed by the two‑point correlation function of the random matrix ensemble, which can be computed explicitly. In the thin‑group setting, the uniform spectral gap ensures that the error terms are of size O(X¹ᐟ²⁺ᵋ) rather than the trivial O(X), and the RMT prediction refines the constant in front of the main term.

Technically, the convergence of eigenvalue distributions is proved by showing that the Cayley graphs Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S) are asymptotically Ramanujan: all non‑trivial eigenvalues λ satisfy |λ| ≤ 2√(k−1) + o(1), where k = |S|. For thin subgroups, the uniform spectral gap gives |λ| ≤ k − δ, which is stronger than the Ramanujan bound for large k. The moment method then shows that the empirical spectral measure converges weakly to the Kesten–McKay law (the spectral measure of a random k‑regular graph). For k fixed and q → ∞, the Kesten–McKay law converges to the semicircle law after appropriate scaling. This chain of limits — expander → Kesten–McKay → semicircle — is the rigorous backbone of the RMT connection. The same reasoning applies to higher rank groups, where the limiting spectral measure is the free convolution of several Kesten–McKay laws, corresponding to the product structure of the generating set.

An important subtlety is that the RMT predictions hold for the *finite* quotients Γ/Γ(q), not for the infinite‑volume space ΓG(ℝ)/K. The Laplacian on the latter has continuous spectrum, and its eigenvalue statistics are governed by the Selberg trace formula, not by random matrix theory. However, the Hecke operators on the finite quotients are exactly the operators whose eigenvalues are studied in RMT. The uniform spectral gap ensures that the finite‑quotient eigenvalues are well separated from the trivial eigenvalue, which is the condition needed for the moment method to work. Without the spectral gap, the eigenvalues could cluster near 1, destroying the convergence to the random matrix limit. Thus superstrong approximation is the precise hypothesis that makes the RMT connection rigorous for thin groups.

Applications of the RMT connection include the prediction of the distribution of Frobenius traces for families of elliptic curves over function fields (where the monodromy group is a thin subgroup of SL₂), the analysis of the variance of the error term in the prime geodesic theorem for thin quotients, and the design of expander‑based pseudorandom generators. In each case, the random matrix model provides a quantitative benchmark that can be proved using the expander property. The interplay between thin groups, expanders, and random matrix theory is a vibrant area of current research, with open problems such as proving the Tracy–Widom limit for the largest eigenvalue of Hecke operators on thin quotients of higher rank groups.

Effective Equidistribution of Horospherical Measures on Thin Quotients

Horospherical measures on homogeneous spaces are invariant measures supported on orbits of a maximal unipotent subgroup. For a thin subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ), the quotient ΓG(ℝ) carries a family of horospherical measures μₓ associated to points x in the expanding horosphere. The effective equidistribution of these measures as the horosphere expands is a central problem in homogeneous dynamics. The uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) — provided by superstrong approximation — yields a power‑saving error term in the equidistribution of horospherical measures. The argument proceeds by lifting the horospherical measure to the adelic space, decomposing it into a sum over congruence quotients, and applying the expander mixing lemma on each finite quotient. The spectral gap ensures that the contribution from non‑trivial representations decays like q⁻ᶜ, which after summation over q gives a power‑saving bound in the expanding parameter. This result was first obtained by Einsiedler, Lindenstrauss and others for lattices, and extended to thin subgroups by Mohammadi and Oh using the uniform spectral gap of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju.

The quantitative statement is as follows. Let G be a semisimple algebraic group over ℚ, Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) a Zariski‑dense subgroup, and U ⊂ G a maximal unipotent subgroup. For a compactly supported smooth function f on ΓG(ℝ), consider the average ∫ f(u·x) dμ_U(u) over a large ball in U. Then there exists δ > 0 such that |∫ f(u·x) dμ_U(u) − ∫ f dμ| ≤ C ‖f‖Sob T⁻ᵟ, where T is the expanding parameter (e.g. the height of the horosphere), μ is the G‑invariant probability measure on ΓG(ℝ), and ‖f‖Sob is a Sobolev norm. The exponent δ depends explicitly on the uniform spectral gap λ₁ of the family Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S). In the rank‑one case G = SL₂, the work of Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak gives λ₁ ≥ δ₀ > 0, which yields δ = δ₀/2. For higher rank groups, the Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem provides a uniform spectral gap for all Zariski‑dense subgroups, giving a δ that depends only on the rank and the generating set. The proof uses the spectral gap to bound the matrix coefficients of the unitary representation of G(ℝ) on L²(ΓG(ℝ)) restricted to the horospherical subgroup, and then applies a standard smoothing argument.

Effective equidistribution of horospherical measures has several important applications. First, it implies effective equidistribution of closed horocycles on the infinite‑volume hyperbolic surface Γ\ℍ, which is a key ingredient in the proof of the effective Oppenheim conjecture for thin orbits. Second, it provides the level of distribution needed for the affine sieve on thin orbits: the horospherical average corresponds to averaging a polynomial map over a thin orbit, and the power‑saving error translates into a level of distribution exceeding ½. Third, it yields effective counting of integral points on homogeneous varieties: the number of points of height ≤ T on a thin orbit is asymptotic to c Tᵈ with error O(Tᵈ⁻ᵟ). This was used by Gorodnik and Nevo to obtain effective counting for integral points on quadric hypersurfaces for thin subgroups of orthogonal groups. The uniformity of the spectral gap across all congruence levels is essential; without it the error term would depend on the level and the power‑saving would be lost.

The proof of effective equidistribution for thin quotients follows the strategy of Einsiedler–Lindenstrauss but replaces the use of property (T) of the ambient lattice (which fails for thin subgroups) with the uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients. The key step is a quantitative version of the Dani–Margulis linearization technique: one approximates the horospherical measure by a sum of measures on congruence quotients, and then uses the expander mixing lemma to show that each piece equidistributes with a power‑saving error. The linearization technique reduces the problem to bounding the correlation of the horospherical flow with eigenfunctions of the Hecke operators. The uniform spectral gap gives a uniform bound on these correlations, which after summation yields the desired power‑saving. This argument works for any semisimple group G and any Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ, provided the congruence quotients form an expander family. The Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem guarantees this for all simply connected, absolutely almost simple groups, so the effective equidistribution holds in full generality.

It is instructive to compare the thin‑group case with the lattice case. For a lattice Γ ⊂ G(ℤ), property (T) of G(ℝ) (when rank ≥ 2) gives a spectral gap for the Laplacian on ΓG(ℝ)/K, which directly implies effective equidistribution via the Howe–Moore theorem. For thin subgroups, property (T) does not pass to infinite‑index subgroups, so the Laplacian may have no spectral gap. Nevertheless, the congruence quotients still form an expander family, and this weaker property (τ) is sufficient for effective equidistribution of horospherical measures. The distinction highlights the power of the expander approach: it works for all Zariski‑dense subgroups, regardless of whether the ambient group has property (T) or the subgroup has finite index. This uniformity is what makes superstrong approximation a central tool in modern homogeneous dynamics and its arithmetic applications.

The Spectral Gap and the Decay of Matrix Coefficients for Thin Subgroups

The uniform spectral gap for congruence quotients of a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ ⊂ G(ℤ) has a direct representation‑theoretic consequence: matrix coefficients of the unitary representation of G(ℝ) on L²(ΓG(ℝ)) decay at a uniform exponential rate. For a lattice this decay follows from property (T) of the ambient group when the real rank is at least two, or from the Selberg eigenvalue bound in rank one. For thin subgroups property (T) does not pass to infinite‑index subgroups, so the Laplacian on ΓG(ℝ)/K may have no spectral gap. Nevertheless, the expander property of the family {Γ/Γ(q)} supplies a uniform bound on the Hecke operators at all levels q. By the work of Burger and Sarnak, and later of Oh and Winter, this uniform bound translates into a power‑saving decay of matrix coefficients for the action of the diagonal subgroup A on the space of functions orthogonal to the constants. Concretely, there exist constants C, η > 0 depending only on the generating set of Γ such that for any smooth vectors v, w with zero average, |⟨a_t v, w⟩| ≤ C e-η t ‖v‖ ‖w‖ for all t ≥ 0. The exponent η can be taken proportional to the spectral gap δ of the expander family. This decay is the engine behind effective equidistribution of horospherical measures, effective counting of integral points, and the level of distribution in the affine sieve. It also implies that the unitary representation of G(ℝ) on L²₀(ΓG(ℝ)) is tempered, a property that fails for many thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) if one only assumes a non‑uniform spectral gap. The argument proceeds by approximating the diagonal flow by a product of Hecke operators at carefully chosen levels, using the flattening lemma to control the error, and then summing over levels. The uniformity of the spectral gap across all levels is essential; without it the decay exponent would deteriorate as the level grows and the power‑saving would be lost. This mechanism shows that superstrong approximation is strictly stronger than the mere existence of a spectral gap for the Laplacian on the infinite‑volume space, and it explains why the expander approach works for all Zariski‑dense subgroups regardless of the real rank of G.

The Affine Sieve and the Distribution of Almost Primes in Thin Orbits of Rank One Groups

In rank one, the ambient group is typically SL₂(ℤ) or a conjugate thereof, and the congruence quotients are the groups SL₂(ℤ/qℤ). The affine sieve of Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak applies the beta sieve to the orbit Γ·x₀ ⊂ ℤⁿ of a polynomial map f : Γ → ℤ. The sieve requires a level of distribution θ > ½, which is supplied by the expander mixing lemma for the Cayley graphs of Γ/Γ(q). For thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) the uniform spectral gap was established by Bourgain and Gamburd (2008) for any finitely generated Zariski‑dense subgroup, and later extended to all such subgroups by Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju. The level of distribution obtained is θ = 1 - ε for any ε > 0, provided the polynomial map is not constant on any congruence class. This yields that the set of points in the orbit where f takes values with at most r prime factors has positive density for some r depending on the degree of f and the spectral gap. In particular, for the Apollonian circle packing (a thin orbit of a subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) acting on Descartes quadruples), the affine sieve shows that there are infinitely many circles with curvature having at most 25 prime factors; subsequent improvements using the uniform spectral gap reduced this bound. The parity problem prevents reaching primes directly, but the almost‑prime result is optimal for the sieve method. The proof combines the expander mixing lemma with a careful analysis of the local densities of the polynomial map modulo q, using the fact that the congruence quotients form an expander family with a uniform spectral gap. The same strategy works for any thin subgroup of SL₂(ℤ) and for thin subgroups of other rank one groups such as SU(2,1) over imaginary quadratic fields, where the congruence quotients are finite unitary groups and the expander property follows from the work of Breuillard, Green, and Tao on approximate subgroups. The key point is that the level of distribution exceeds ½, which is exactly the threshold where the beta sieve produces almost primes. Without the uniform spectral gap the error terms in the equidistribution modulo q would be too large to achieve θ > ½, and the sieve would fail.

Worked Example: Level of Distribution for a Thin Orbit
Let Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) be a Zariski‑dense subgroup generated by two matrices with entries bounded by 10. Consider the polynomial map f(γ) = trace(γ) on the orbit Γ·I. Explain why the expander mixing lemma gives a level of distribution θ > ½ for the values of f modulo q.
The congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) embed into SL₂(ℤ/qℤ). By the Bourgain–Gamburd theorem, the Cayley graphs of these quotients with respect to the fixed generating set form an expander family with a uniform spectral gap δ > 0. The expander mixing lemma then implies that for any subset A ⊂ Γ/Γ(q) the number of elements of A in a given coset differs from the expected proportion by at most O(q-δ/2) times the size of the group. Applying this to the level sets of f modulo q yields an error term of size O(q1-δ/2) for the count of γ ∈ Γ with f(γ) ≡ a (mod q) and word length ≤ T. Summing over q ≤ Q gives a total error O(Q2-δ/2) while the main term is of size Q². Hence the level of distribution is θ = 1 - δ/4 > ½ for sufficiently small δ. This exceeds the sieve threshold and allows the beta sieve to produce almost primes.

The level of distribution is then 1 - η/2. Always check that the polynomial map is non‑constant on congruence classes; otherwise the local densities vanish and the sieve fails.

The Uniform Spectral Gap for Thin Subgroups of Non‑Split Groups

The Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem establishes superstrong approximation for all Zariski‑dense subgroups of simply connected, absolutely almost simple algebraic groups over ℚ. This includes non‑split groups such as the special unitary groups SU(n,1) over imaginary quadratic fields, the groups of type G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈ that are not split over ℚ, and the spin groups of quadratic forms that are anisotropic at the real place. For these groups the congruence quotients are finite groups of Lie type that are not necessarily of the form G(𝔽_q) for a split group; they are twisted Chevalley groups or groups of the form G(𝔽_q) where G is a non‑split form. The proof of the uniform spectral gap proceeds by reducing to the split case via the theory of algebraic groups over finite fields and the classification of finite simple groups. One uses the fact that any non‑split group becomes split over a quadratic extension, and then applies the product theorem of Breuillard, Green, and Tao for the split group over the extension field. The escape from subvarieties lemma, which is a key ingredient in the Bourgain–Gamburd machine, must be verified for the non‑split groups; this is done by studying the action of the group on its building and using the fact that the subgroup is Zariski‑dense in the algebraic group over ℚ. The result is that the family of Cayley graphs of the congruence quotients forms an expander family with a spectral gap depending only on the generating set and the algebraic group, not on the level. This uniform gap has immediate consequences: effective equidistribution of horospherical measures on the associated locally symmetric spaces, the affine sieve for polynomial maps on thin orbits of non‑split groups, and quantitative non‑divergence of unipotent flows. It also implies that the Ramanujan conjecture for the automorphic representations generated by the thin subgroup holds at all unramified places, because the Hecke operators at those places are exactly the adjacency operators of the expander graphs. The non‑split case is technically more demanding because the congruence quotients are not simply products of groups of the form G(𝔽_p); they involve twisted groups and the level structure is more intricate. Nevertheless, the Salehi‑Golsefidy–Varju theorem shows that the expander property is a universal feature of Zariski‑dense subgroups of any absolutely almost simple simply connected group, split or not.

The Connection Between Thin Groups and the Theory of Automorphic Representations on Adele Groups

Thin subgroups give rise to automorphic representations of the adelic group G(𝔸) via the right regular representation on L²(ΓG(ℝ) × ∏_p G(ℚ_p)/K_p), where K_p are suitable compact open subgroups. The uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients translates into a spectral gap for the Hecke operators at all unramified primes. By the work of Clozel, Oh, and others, this implies that the automorphic representations occurring in the decomposition of L²(ΓG(ℝ)) are tempered at all unramified places. In particular, the Ramanujan conjecture holds for these representations: the Satake parameters at an unramified prime p lie on the unitary axis. This is a striking contrast with the situation for congruence subgroups, where the Ramanujan conjecture is known only for certain groups (e.g., GL₂ over ℚ by Deligne) and is open in general. For thin subgroups the expander property provides a proof of the Ramanujan bound at all unramified places, because the Hecke operators are exactly the adjacency operators of the expander graphs, and the spectral gap gives a uniform bound on their eigenvalues. Moreover, the automorphic representations arising from thin subgroups are not necessarily cuspidal; they can contain residual spectrum coming from the continuous spectrum of the Laplacian on the infinite‑volume space. The uniform spectral gap ensures that the continuous spectrum is also tempered, which is essential for the effective equidistribution results. The connection to automorphic forms also yields a trace formula for thin subgroups: the Selberg trace formula can be adapted to the infinite‑volume setting by truncating the Eisenstein series, and the spectral gap controls the contribution of the continuous spectrum. This trace formula has been used by Magee, Oh, and Winter to study the distribution of eigenvalues for thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) and to prove a version of the Selberg eigenvalue conjecture for these groups: the first eigenvalue is bounded below by a positive constant depending on the generating set, but it can be arbitrarily close to zero as the generating set varies. The adelic perspective also clarifies the relationship between property (τ) and the congruence subgroup property: the congruence subgroup property fails for thin subgroups, but property (τ) holds because the congruence quotients still form an expander family. This shows that property (τ) is strictly weaker than the congruence subgroup property, and it is exactly the property needed for the arithmetic applications of the affine sieve and effective equidistribution.

The Spectral Gap and the Mixing Rate of Geodesic Flows on Thin Quotients

The geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of the infinite‑volume hyperbolic manifold Γ\ℍ (or more generally on ΓG/K for a semisimple Lie group G) is the action of the one‑parameter diagonal subgroup A. The rate of mixing of this flow is governed by the decay of matrix coefficients of the unitary representation of G on L²(ΓG). As explained in the first section, the uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients implies a power‑saving decay of matrix coefficients for the A‑action on the orthogonal complement of the constants. First, it yields effective equidistribution of closed geodesics: the number of closed geodesics of length ≤ T on Γ\ℍ is asymptotic to eδ T/(δ T) with a power‑saving error, where δ is the critical exponent of Γ. Second, it gives effective counting of integral points on homogeneous varieties: the number of points of height ≤ T on a thin orbit is asymptotic to c T^d with error O(Td-η). Third, it provides the level of distribution needed for the affine sieve, as the mixing estimate is exactly the statement that the horospherical averages equidistribute with a power‑saving error. The proof of effective mixing for thin quotients follows the strategy of Dolgopyat for Anosov flows, but the spectral gap for the congruence quotients replaces the use of property (T) of the ambient lattice. One approximates the geodesic flow by a product of Hecke operators at carefully chosen levels, uses the flattening lemma to control the error, and then sums over levels. The uniformity of the spectral gap across all levels is again essential; without it the mixing exponent would deteriorate. This result shows that the geodesic flow on any infinite‑volume hyperbolic manifold arising from a Zariski‑dense subgroup of SL₂(ℝ) mixes at an exponential rate with a power‑saving error, a fact that was previously known only for lattices or for certain special thin groups. The same method works for higher rank groups, where the geodesic flow is replaced by the action of a maximal split torus, and the mixing rate is again governed by the uniform spectral gap of the congruence quotients.

Quantitative Bounds on Growth and Diameter in Thin Groups

The study of thin groups often begins with understanding their growth properties, which fundamentally distinguish them from lattices. For a finitely generated group Γ with a symmetric generating set S, the word growth function, denoted BΓ,S(k), counts the number of group elements that can be expressed as a product of at most k elements from S. For lattices in semisimple Lie groups, the word growth is typically polynomial. For example, in SL₂(ℤ), the growth is polynomial of degree 2. In stark contrast, thin subgroups exhibit exponential growth. This means there exist constants C > 0 and λ > 1 such that BΓ,S(k) ≈ Cλk for large k. The rate of this exponential growth is captured by the growth exponent, also known as the critical exponent, δ(Γ), which is defined as the limit of (log BΓ,S(k)) / k as k approaches infinity. This exponent is independent of the choice of finite generating set S.

The exponential growth of thin groups has profound implications for the diameter of their congruence quotients. Let Γ be a thin subgroup of an algebraic group G(ℤ), and consider the family of finite quotients {Γ/Γ(q)} where Γ(q) = Γ ∩ G(ℤ/qℤ) is a congruence subgroup modulo q. The superstrong approximation theorem, particularly in its quantitative forms, asserts that these quotients form an expander family. This implies that the Cayley graphs of Γ/Γ(q) with respect to the images of the generators of Γ have diameters that grow at most logarithmically with the size of the quotient. Specifically, the diameter of Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S_q) is bounded by C log |Γ/Γ(q)| for some constant C, independent of q. This is a direct consequence of the product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type, which states that for any generating set S of a finite simple group G, the product Sk covers G very quickly, often in a logarithmic number of steps. The Breuillard–Green–Tao theorem provides a uniform version of this product theorem, essential for establishing the uniform diameter bounds across the entire family of congruence quotients.

The uniformity of these diameter bounds is critical. Without it, the expansion property would degrade for larger moduli q, undermining many arithmetic applications. The constant C depends on the group G and the initial generating set S of Γ, but crucially not on q. This quantitative control over the diameter ensures that random walks on these finite quotients mix rapidly and that elements are "well-distributed" within the group, a property leveraged heavily by the affine sieve and effective equidistribution results. The exponential growth of the thin group itself, coupled with the logarithmic diameter of its finite quotients, highlights the intricate balance between the infinite-index nature of thin groups and the strong connectivity of their finite reductions.

Effective Prime Counting via the Affine Sieve: Level of Distribution and Error Terms

The affine sieve is a powerful tool designed to count points on thin orbits that satisfy certain arithmetic properties, most notably being "almost prime." A central concept in sieve theory is the level of distribution, denoted D. For classical sieve problems concerning sequences in arithmetic progressions, a level of distribution D > 1/2 is typically required to obtain non-trivial lower bounds for the number of primes or almost primes. The challenge for thin groups lies in establishing such a level of distribution for orbits that are sparse and lack the regular structure of arithmetic progressions.

Superstrong approximation provides the crucial input for the affine sieve by guaranteeing effective equidistribution of thin orbits modulo q. Specifically, for a thin orbit O = xΓ, the number of points in O of height up to X that fall into a given congruence class modulo q, denoted N(X, q, a), is shown to be approximately (1/|Γ/Γ(q)|) N(X) with a power-saving error term. This means that the points in the orbit are uniformly distributed across the residue classes modulo q, and the error in this distribution is small enough to be useful in sieve theory. More precisely, the error term is typically of the form O(N(X) q-ε) for some ε > 0, which allows the sieve to sum over moduli q up to Xθ for some θ > 1/2. This effectively establishes a level of distribution D = θ for the affine sieve applied to thin orbits.

With this enhanced level of distribution, the affine sieve can yield quantitative estimates for the number of almost primes on thin orbits. For instance, if one is counting points x ∈ O with height H(x) ≤ X such that f(x) is an almost prime (a number with a bounded number of prime factors), the sieve provides an asymptotic formula of the form:

Nalmost prime(X) = C (N(X) / log X) × (1 + O(1 / (log X)η))

where C is a positive constant, N(X) is the total number of points on the orbit up to height X, and η > 0 is a power-saving exponent. This result is analogous to classical prime number theorems but applies to the much sparser setting of thin orbits. It is important to note that the affine sieve, like other sieve methods, typically produces almost primes (numbers with at most k prime factors for some small k), rather than actual primes. This limitation is known as the parity problem in sieve theory, which states that sieve methods alone cannot distinguish between numbers with an even or odd number of prime factors. Overcoming the parity problem to count actual primes on thin orbits remains a significant open problem, even with the powerful tools of superstrong approximation.

Application of Level of Distribution
Consider a thin subgroup Γ of SL₂(ℤ) generated by two matrices. Let O be an orbit of a point under the action of Γ. The affine sieve is used to count points x in O such that the top-left entry of x, say a11, is an almost prime. If the number of points in O with height up to X is N(X) ≈ Xα for some α > 0, and superstrong approximation provides an effective equidistribution modulo q with an error term O(N(X)q-ε), determine the maximum level of distribution D that can be achieved for the affine sieve and explain its significance.
The level of distribution D is the maximum exponent θ such that the error term in the equidistribution estimate allows summing over moduli q up to Xθ. The equidistribution estimate is typically of the form:
|N(X, q, a) - (1/|Γ/Γ(q)|) N(X)| ≤ Error(X, q)
For the affine sieve to be effective, the sum of these error terms over q up to Xθ must be smaller than the main term. If the error term is O(N(X)q-ε), then the sum over q is roughly:
∑q ≤ Xθ O(N(X)q-ε) ≈ N(X) ∑q ≤ Xθ q-ε
This sum converges if ε > 1, but for practical purposes, we need it to be small relative to the main term. The crucial point is that the error term for individual q is typically bounded by a power of q, say q-δ for some δ > 0. The superstrong approximation theorem provides this power-saving error.
The level of distribution D is determined by the range of moduli q for which the equidistribution holds with sufficient accuracy. If the error term is O(N(X)q-ε), then the sieve can typically handle moduli q up to Xθ where θ is slightly less than 1. However, for a non-trivial lower bound in sieve theory, one needs a level of distribution D > 1/2. The superstrong approximation theorem, by providing a uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients, ensures that the error term is indeed small enough, allowing for D to be greater than 1/2.
Specifically, the quantitative mixing estimates derived from the uniform spectral gap imply that the level of distribution D can be taken as 1/2 + η for some small η > 0. This is significant because a level of distribution exactly 1/2 (or less) often leads to trivial lower bounds (e.g., zero), meaning the sieve cannot guarantee the existence of any almost primes. A D > 1/2 allows for the construction of a lower bound sieve that counts a positive proportion of almost primes, providing the asymptotic formula mentioned above. Without superstrong approximation, achieving D > 1/2 for thin orbits would be extremely difficult, if not impossible.
Distinguishing Sieve Results
When encountering questions about sieve theory and thin groups, pay close attention to whether the question asks for "primes" or "almost primes." The affine sieve, even with the powerful input from superstrong approximation, generally yields only almost primes due to the parity problem. Do not assume it directly counts primes unless specific additional techniques (which are usually much more complex and not part of the standard affine sieve) are mentioned. The key takeaway from superstrong approximation for the affine sieve is the ability to achieve a level of distribution D > 1/2, which is critical for non-trivial results.

Mixing Rates of Random Walks and Spectral Profile of Thin Group Quotients

The concept of a spectral gap for the family of Cayley graphs of congruence quotients is central to understanding the mixing properties of random walks on these groups. For a finite graph G, the spectral gap is the difference between the first two eigenvalues of its normalized adjacency matrix (or Laplacian). A large spectral gap implies that the graph is a good expander. For thin groups, the superstrong approximation theorem ensures that the family of Cayley graphs {Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S_q)} forms an expander family, meaning there is a uniform lower bound δ > 0 for the spectral gap of all these graphs, independent of q.

This uniform spectral gap directly translates into rapid mixing of random walks. Consider a simple random walk on Cay(Γ/Γ(q), S_q) starting from the identity element. The mixing time Tmix is the minimum number of steps required for the distribution of the random walk to be close to the uniform distribution over the group. Quantitatively, the mixing time is inversely proportional to the spectral gap. Specifically, Tmix is typically bounded by O((log |Γ/Γ(q)|) / δ). This logarithmic dependence on the size of the group is characteristic of expander graphs and signifies extremely fast convergence to equilibrium. In contrast, for non-expander graphs (e.g., a path graph), the mixing time can be polynomial in the size of the graph.

Beyond the mixing time, the spectral profile of the family of graphs provides a more detailed understanding of the eigenvalue distribution. While the uniform spectral gap guarantees that the first non-trivial eigenvalue λ₁ is bounded away from zero, the distribution of higher eigenvalues also plays a role in finer mixing properties. For Ramanujan graphs (a special class of expanders where all eigenvalues satisfy a specific bound), the mixing is exceptionally fast. While congruence quotients of thin groups are not always Ramanujan graphs in the strict sense, the superstrong approximation theorem implies a "Ramanujan-like" behavior in terms of the spectral gap. The connection between the spectral gap and the graph's connectivity is also captured by Cheeger's inequality, which relates the spectral gap to the Cheeger constant (a measure of graph expansion). A uniform spectral gap implies a uniform Cheeger constant, further reinforcing the expander property.

The rapid mixing of random walks on congruence quotients has far-reaching consequences. It is a key ingredient in proving effective equidistribution results for thin orbits on homogeneous spaces. When a thin group acts on a space, the distribution of its orbit points modulo q can be analyzed by considering random walks on the finite quotients. The rapid mixing ensures that these points are quickly and uniformly distributed, allowing for power-saving error terms in counting problems. This quantitative understanding of mixing rates is indispensable for applications ranging from the affine sieve to the quantitative Oppenheim conjecture and the effective equidistribution of horospherical measures.

Geometric Manifestations: Limit Sets, Hausdorff Dimension, and Critical Exponent

The algebraic and arithmetic properties of thin groups have profound geometric counterparts, particularly when these groups are viewed as discrete subgroups of Lie groups acting on associated symmetric spaces. For a thin subgroup Γ of a semisimple Lie group G (e.g., SL₂(ℝ) acting on the hyperbolic plane ℍ²), Γ acts discontinuously on the symmetric space X = G/K. Since Γ is of infinite index, the quotient space ΓX has infinite volume. The action extends to the boundary ∂X of the symmetric space.

The limit set Λ(Γ) is defined as the set of accumulation points in ∂X of an orbit Γx₀ for any x₀ ∈ X. For thin groups, unlike lattices, the limit set is often a proper subset of the entire boundary ∂X. It is typically a fractal set, reflecting the "thinness" of the group. The geometry of this limit set is intimately connected to the growth rate of the group. The critical exponent δ(Γ) of a thin group Γ is an algebraic invariant that measures the exponential growth rate of the number of elements in Γ with word length up to k, or equivalently, the growth rate of the number of points in an orbit within a ball of radius k in the symmetric space. A fundamental result, primarily due to Sullivan, establishes a deep connection between this algebraic critical exponent and the geometric properties of the limit set: the critical exponent δ(Γ) is equal to the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set, dimH(Λ(Γ)).

For lattices, the critical exponent equals the dimension of the symmetric space, and the limit set is the entire boundary. For thin groups, however, δ(Γ) < dim(X), and consequently, dimH(Λ(Γ)) < dim(∂X). This inequality geometrically captures the fact that thin groups "do not fill" the boundary of the symmetric space as densely as lattices do. The limit set of a thin group is often a highly intricate, self-similar fractal. The Patterson–Sullivan measure is a conformally invariant measure supported on the limit set, which plays a crucial role in understanding the distribution of orbit points and the dynamics of geodesic flows on the infinite-volume quotient ΓX. This measure provides a canonical way to "weight" points on the limit set, reflecting the group's action.

Understanding the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set for thin groups is a challenging problem, as it depends on the specific algebraic structure of the subgroup. For example, for certain thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ) acting on ℍ², the limit set is a subset of the real line (the boundary of ℍ²), and its Hausdorff dimension can be strictly between 0 and 1. This geometric perspective provides crucial insights into the asymptotic behavior of thin group orbits and their interaction with the ambient space, linking algebraic growth to fractal geometry.

The Arithmetic of Non-Congruence Modular Forms Arising from Thin Groups

The study of modular forms is deeply intertwined with the theory of discrete subgroups of SL₂(ℝ). Classically, modular forms are associated with congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ), which are defined by congruence conditions on their matrix entries. However, thin groups, by definition, are infinite-index Zariski-dense subgroups that are not congruence subgroups. This failure of the congruence subgroup property for thin groups opens up a rich and complex world of non-congruence modular forms.

A non-congruence modular form is a modular form for a discrete subgroup Γ of SL₂(ℤ) that is not a congruence subgroup. These forms exhibit arithmetic properties that are strikingly different from their congruence counterparts. For instance, the Fourier coefficients of classical modular forms for congruence subgroups often satisfy deep arithmetic congruences and are related to L-functions with Euler products. In contrast, the Fourier coefficients of non-congruence modular forms typically do not satisfy such simple congruences and their associated L-functions generally lack Euler products. This makes their arithmetic behavior much harder to analyze.

Despite these complexities, thin groups provide a natural source for studying non-congruence modular forms. The existence of a uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients of thin groups, as established by superstrong approximation, has implications for the spectral theory of operators on the associated infinite-volume hyperbolic surfaces. While the Selberg eigenvalue conjecture (λ₁ ≥ 1/4) does not hold for all thin subgroups of SL₂(ℤ), the uniform spectral gap for congruence quotients ensures that the spectral theory of these groups is still well-behaved in certain averaged senses. The work of Atkin–Swinnerton–Dyer on non-congruence modular forms, particularly their theory of "newforms" and "oldforms" for non-congruence subgroups, provides a framework for understanding their structure. However, extending these results to general thin groups is a significant challenge.

The connection between thin groups and non-congruence modular forms is an active area of research. It seeks to understand how the "thinness" of the group impacts the analytic and arithmetic properties of the modular forms it supports. For example, the distribution of values of non-congruence modular forms, the properties of their L-functions, and their relation to Galois representations are all areas where the unique structure of thin groups offers new perspectives. The tools developed for thin groups, such as the affine sieve and effective equidistribution, may eventually shed light on the distribution of Fourier coefficients of these elusive non-congruence forms, providing a bridge between geometric group theory and number theory.

Common Traps

  • Confusing prime counting with almost prime counting: The affine sieve, even with superstrong approximation, primarily yields results for almost primes, not necessarily primes, due to the parity problem.
  • Assuming a fixed spectral gap for all thin groups: While superstrong approximation guarantees a *uniform* spectral gap for the *family* of congruence quotients of a given thin group, the specific value of this gap depends on the generating set and the group itself, and it does not imply a universal lower bound like Selberg's 1/4 conjecture for all thin subgroups.
  • Overlooking the "uniformity" aspect of superstrong approximation: The power of superstrong approximation lies in the *uniformity* of the expander property (e.g., spectral gap, diameter bounds) across the entire family of congruence quotients, not just for individual moduli.
  • Misinterpreting the critical exponent: While the critical exponent δ(Γ) equals the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set, it is generally *less* than the dimension of the ambient symmetric space for thin groups, unlike for lattices.
  • Equating non-congruence modular forms with "badly behaved" forms: While non-congruence modular forms lack some of the simpler arithmetic properties of congruence forms (e.g., Euler products for L-functions), they are still well-defined objects with a rich structure, albeit one that is more complex to unravel.
  • Assuming the product theorem applies directly to infinite groups: The product theorem (e.g., Breuillard–Green–Tao) is typically stated for finite simple groups or approximate subgroups within them, and its implications for infinite thin groups are derived by applying it to their finite congruence quotients.

The Uniform Spectral Gap for Thin Subgroups of Classical Groups of Type An

For a Zariski‑dense subgroup Γ of SLn+1(ℤ) that is thin, the congruence quotients Γ/Γ(q) form a family of finite groups as q varies over square‑free integers. Superstrong approximation asserts that the Cayley graphs of these quotients with respect to a fixed symmetric generating set constitute a family of expanders. In the classical case n=1 this is the Bourgain–Gamburd theorem for SL₂(ℤ). For higher rank classical groups of type An the proof proceeds by combining the product theorem of Breuillard–Green–Tao with a flattening lemma adapted to the root system of SLn+1. The key new ingredient is a uniform non‑concentration estimate on proper algebraic subvarieties of the group, which follows from the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma applied to the adjoint representation. The resulting spectral gap is independent of the level q and depends only on the generating set and the rank n.

Applications of this uniform gap include diameter bounds of order (log |Γ/Γ(q)|)C for the congruence quotients, effective equidistribution of Γ‑orbits on the associated symmetric space, and a level of distribution for the affine sieve that is polynomial in the size of the sieve support. The method also yields a quantitative version of the strong approximation property: the index of the congruence kernel in the profinite completion of Γ is bounded by a power of the level. These results extend the rank‑one theory to all split classical groups of type A and provide the analytic backbone for many arithmetic applications.

The proof strategy mirrors the Bourgain–Gamburd machine: first establish a product theorem for approximate subgroups of SLn+1(𝔽p), then use the flattening lemma to convert growth in the group into decay of the random walk measure, and finally iterate to obtain a uniform spectral gap. The main technical obstacle is the presence of many proper parabolic subgroups, which forces a more delicate induction on the rank. Recent work of Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju overcomes this by introducing a “weighted” flattening lemma that tracks the contribution of each root subgroup separately.

An important corollary is that any thin subgroup of SLn+1(ℤ) with a spectral gap for its congruence quotients automatically satisfies property (τ) with respect to the family of congruence subgroups. This links the expander property to the representation theory of the adelic group and to the automorphic spectrum on the locally symmetric space ΓSLn+1(ℝ)/SO(n+1). The uniform gap also implies a uniform bound for the first non‑zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian on the congruence covers, a higher‑rank analogue of Selberg’s 3/16 theorem.

The Affine Sieve for Higher Degree Polynomial Maps on Thin Orbits

The classical affine sieve of Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak applies to linear polynomial maps f : ℤd → ℤ evaluated on a thin orbit Γ·x₀. When f is a polynomial of degree ≥ 2, new difficulties arise because the fibers of f are no longer cosets of a subgroup but algebraic varieties of higher complexity. The modern extension replaces the linear sieve weight by a β‑sieve adapted to the geometry of the level sets of f. The crucial input is a uniform upper bound for the number of points in Γ·x₀ that lie in a given proper subvariety of bounded degree, which follows from the escape‑from‑subvarieties lemma combined with a quantitative version of the Lang–Weil estimate for the reduction modulo p.

With this bound one can control the sifting density and obtain an asymptotic formula for the number of orbit points where f takes an almost‑prime value with a prescribed number of prime factors. The level of distribution achieved is typically a power of the height parameter, and the exponent depends on the degree of f and the spectral gap of the congruence quotients. For quadratic forms the result recovers the Zaremba‑type theorems on continued fractions; for cubic and higher forms it yields new density results for values represented by thin orbits.

A concrete example is the polynomial f(x,y,z)=x³+y³+z³ on the orbit of the Markov group acting on ℤ³. The uniform spectral gap for the Markov group (a thin subgroup of SL₃(ℤ)) together with the higher‑degree sieve shows that a positive proportion of integers represented by this cubic form are almost primes with at most r prime factors, where r depends explicitly on the spectral gap. The method also applies to norm forms on thin subgroups of division algebras, giving almost‑prime values of reduced norms.

The main limitation remains the parity problem: the sieve cannot distinguish between numbers with an even or odd number of prime factors, so prime values themselves are out of reach without additional bilinear structure. Nevertheless, the higher‑degree affine sieve provides the strongest unconditional results currently available for polynomial values on thin orbits and serves as a template for further refinements such as the weighted sieve or the incorporation of Type II information.

Let Γ be the thin subgroup of SL₃(ℤ) generated by the matrices A = [[1,1,0],[0,1,0],[0,0,1]] and B = [[1,0,0],[0,1,1],[0,0,1]]. Consider the cubic polynomial f(x,y,z)=x³+y³+z³ on the orbit Γ·(1,0,0). Reduce the polynomial modulo p and use the Lang–Weil estimate to show that for all but O(p−1/2) primes the fiber f ≡ a (mod p) is a geometrically irreducible hypersurface of dimension 2. 4. Insert these bounds into the β‑sieve with level of distribution Tθ where θ depends on ε. 5. Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “level of distribution” in an affine sieve problem, recall that it is the exponent θ such that the sieve works up to moduli q ≤ Tθ. The spectral gap ε enters through the flattening lemma: a larger ε gives a larger θ. Memorise the relationship θ = c·ε/(deg f) for a constant c depending only on the rank. This shortcut lets you compare different thin groups without re‑deriving the whole sieve.

The Interplay Between Thin Groups and the Theory of Random Walks on Buildings

Buildings associated to higher‑rank algebraic groups provide a geometric framework for studying random walks on thin subgroups. For a thin subgroup Γ of a split group G(ℚp) the action on the Bruhat–Tits building yields a locally finite simplicial complex on which Γ acts properly discontinuously. The spectral gap for the congruence quotients of Γ translates into a uniform spectral gap for the Markov operator on the building, which in turn implies exponential decay of the return probability for the simple random walk on the Γ‑quotient of the building. This decay rate is directly related to the critical exponent of Γ and to the Hausdorff dimension of its limit set in the spherical building at infinity.

The connection is made precise by the work of Lubotzky–Mozes–Raghunathan and later by Eskin–Mozes–Oh, who showed that property (τ) for a family of congruence quotients is equivalent to a uniform spectral gap for the random walk on the associated building. For thin groups the building is not a tree but a higher‑dimensional complex; the expander property of the links (which are spherical buildings of lower rank) is the key combinatorial input. The product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type guarantees that the links are expanders uniformly in the residue characteristic, and this uniformity lifts to the whole building via a Garland‑type argument.

Applications include effective equidistribution of Γ‑orbits on the building, quantitative non‑divergence of unipotent flows on the Γ‑quotient, and a proof of the uniform exponential growth of Γ. The building viewpoint also clarifies the role of the “weight” in the weighted flattening lemma: the weight corresponds to the distance from a fixed chamber in the building, and the flattening estimate is exactly a statement that the random walk measure becomes equidistributed on spheres of large radius.

Recent developments extend this picture to non‑split groups and to S‑arithmetic settings, where the building is replaced by a product of buildings. The resulting “multi‑building” random walks govern the congruence quotients of thin subgroups of S‑arithmetic groups, and the uniform spectral gap yields adelic expanders. This adelic expansion is the geometric incarnation of superstrong approximation for S‑arithmetic thin groups.

The Role of the Spectral Gap in the Quantitative Non‑Divergence of Diagonal Flows on Thin Orbits

Quantitative non‑divergence estimates control the measure of orbit segments of a diagonal flow that spend a long time near the cusp of a locally symmetric space. For thin subgroups Γ of SLn(ℝ) the space ΓSLn(ℝ)/SO(n) has infinite volume, and the diagonal flow at acts on the space of unimodular lattices. The classical Kleinbock–Margulis theorem gives a polynomial bound for the measure of points whose trajectory stays in a compact set up to time T. When Γ is thin, the presence of a uniform spectral gap for the congruence quotients improves the exponent in this bound.

The mechanism is as follows: the spectral gap implies a uniform decay of matrix coefficients for the unitary representation of Γ on L²₀(ΓG). This decay translates, via the Dani correspondence, into a quantitative estimate for the distribution of expanding horospheres. By integrating the horospherical estimate along the diagonal flow one obtains a non‑divergence bound with an exponent that depends linearly on the spectral gap. In particular, if the spectral gap is ε, the measure of points that diverge by time T is O(T−cε) for some explicit constant c depending only on the rank.

This refined non‑divergence has direct consequences for Diophantine approximation on thin orbits. For example, it yields a quantitative version of the Oppenheim conjecture for values of indefinite quadratic forms on Γ‑orbits: the set of values that are very close to zero has small measure, with an error term that reflects the spectral gap. It also feeds into the affine sieve by providing the “Type I” information needed to control the contribution of small prime factors.

The proof uses the spectral gap to establish a uniform mixing rate for the diagonal flow on the space of Γ‑invariant measures on the homogeneous space. The mixing rate is then combined with a covering argument à la Kleinbock–Margulis, where the spectral gap replaces the use of the spectral gap for the full lattice. The result is a clean illustration of how expander properties of finite quotients control the dynamics of the infinite‑volume quotient.

The Connection Between Thin Groups and the Theory of Automorphic Forms on Non‑Split Groups

Automorphic forms on non‑split algebraic groups arise naturally from thin subgroups that are not conjugate to subgroups of split groups over ℚ. A typical construction starts with a central simple algebra D over a number field F and a thin subgroup Γ of the norm‑one group SL₁(D). The congruence quotients of Γ are finite groups of Lie type that are inner forms of split groups. Superstrong approximation for these inner forms has been proved by Salehi‑Golsefidy and Varju using the product theorem for finite simple groups of Lie type, which holds uniformly for all inner forms.

The spectral gap for the congruence quotients implies a uniform bound for the first non‑zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian on the locally symmetric spaces attached to the adelic quotient ΓG(𝔸)/K. This bound is the non‑split analogue of Selberg’s eigenvalue conjecture and feeds into the theory of automorphic representations: it shows that the Ramanujan conjecture holds on average for the automorphic spectrum generated by Γ. In particular, the Satake parameters of the automorphic representations occurring in L²(ΓG(𝔸)) satisfy the temperedness condition at almost all places.

An important application is the construction of expander families from the congruence quotients of thin subgroups of exceptional groups of type G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈ that are not split. The product theorem for these groups was established by Breuillard–Green–Tao and later refined by Pyber–Szabó. The resulting expanders have applications to the Zaremba conjecture for continued fractions over number fields and to the distribution of rational points on homogeneous varieties defined by non‑split quadratic forms.

The theory also connects with the Langlands program: the thin subgroup Γ gives rise to a family of Galois representations via the automorphic representations it generates. The uniform spectral gap ensures that these Galois representations are “large” in the sense of having open image, which is a key input for potential modularity theorems. Thus thin groups provide a concrete source of automorphic forms on non‑split groups whose arithmetic properties can be studied using the expander machinery.
