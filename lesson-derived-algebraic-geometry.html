Derived Algebraic Geometry — Derived Schemes, the Cotangent Complex & Töen-Vezzosi Theory

Derived Algebraic Geometry (DAG) is the natural synthesis of homotopy theory and algebraic geometry. It was pioneered by Lurie, Toën, and Vezzosi to address geometric problems where classical schemes fail — such as intersecting badly-behaved subspaces, moduli spaces of perfect complexes, and structures with non-transverse intersections. The central idea is to replace commutative rings with simplicial commutative rings (or E∞-ring spectra) and to replace schemes with functors from simplicial rings to simplicial sets satisfying descent. This yields the cotangent complex LX/Y (the derived version of Kähler differentials) which controls deformation theory, the virtual fundamental class for derived moduli spaces, and shifted symplectic structures — a higher-categorical generalization of symplectic geometry. The theory has deep applications in enumerative geometry (Donaldson-Thomas invariants), the geometric Langlands program, and p-adic Hodge theory via spectral algebraic geometry.

"Derived algebraic geometry is what algebraic geometry becomes when you take homotopy theory seriously."
— Jacob Lurie
Part 1

📏 Simplicial Rings and Derived Schemes

Foundations
Simplicial Commutative Algebras, Derived Affine Schemes, and the Étale Topology
Let sAlgk denote the category of simplicial commutative algebras over a base ring k. A derived affine scheme is a functor Spec A: sAlgk → sSet given by Spec A(B) = MapsAlgk(A, B), where the mapping space is the derived mapping space (the geometric realization of the simplicial Hom). These form the ∞-category dAff of derived affine schemes. The derived étale topology is defined via the condition that a map A → B of simplicial commutative rings is étale if π0(A) → π0(B) is étale and the induced map on cotangent complexes is an equivalence. Descent for the étale topology is a homotopical version of Grothendieck descent. A derived scheme is a pair (X, OX) where X is a topological space and OX is a sheaf of simplicial commutative rings such that (X, π0OX) is an ordinary scheme and each πnOX is a quasi-coherent sheaf on (X, π0OX). The truncation functor t0: derived schemes → ordinary schemes is given by taking π0 of the structure sheaf. A derived scheme is locally of finite presentation if it is covered by derived affines Spec A with A a compact object in sAlgk. The category of derived schemes admits fiber products and the derived intersection of two subschemes Y, Z ⊂ X is given by Y ×hX Z — this captures the correct scheme-theoretic intersection when Y and Z meet non-transversely.
Derived schemes generalize ordinary schemes by incorporating homotopical information in the structure sheaf; the truncation recovers the classical scheme, while higher homotopy groups encode derived intersections and obstructions.
Part 2

🌍 The Cotangent Complex and Deformation Theory

Derived Differentials
The Cotangent Complex LX/Y, André-Quillen Homology, and Lurie's Smoothness Criterion
For a map of derived schemes f: X → Y, the cotangent complex LX/Y is the derived version of Kähler differentials ΩX/Y. It lives in the derived category D≤¹(X) and is a perfect complex when X is locally of finite presentation. For a surjection A → B of simplicial rings with kernel I, the relative cotangent complex fits into the fundamental exact triangle: I/I² → B ⊗A LA/k → LB/k → I/I²[1]. This is the André-Quillen homology of B over A. The cotangent complex controls deformation theory: for an infinitesimal extension of Y with square-zero ideal J, the obstructions to deforming X lie in Ext²(LX/Y, f*J), while deformations are classified by Ext¹(LX/Y, f*J). Lurie's theorem: a map of derived schemes f: X → Y is smooth (resp. étale) iff LX/Y is a locally free sheaf in degree 0 (resp. zero). The cotangent complex also satisfies base change: for a Cartesian square, LX×YY' / Y' ≅ p*LX/Y. Derived completion (the derived version of formal completion) uses the cotangent complex to produce a formal derived scheme from a derived scheme along a closed immersion. The Schlessinger's conditions in derived form ensure that the deformation functor is pro-representable by a derived Artin ring.
The cotangent complex is the universal derived linear approximation of a map of derived schemes; it encodes all deformation-theoretic information via its Ext groups.
Part 3

🧮 Derived Moduli Spaces and Virtual Fundamental Classes

Moduli
Derived Moduli of Perfect Complexes, Virtual Fundamental Classes, and Shifted Symplectic Structures
The moduli stack MP of perfect complexes on a scheme X classifies families of perfect complexes. Its derived enhancement ℝMP is a derived stack whose tangent complex at a point E is given by TEℝMP = ℝHom(E, E)[1]. The virtual dimension is rk(TM). The virtual fundamental class [MP]vir ∈ A*(MP, ℚ) is defined via the perfect obstruction theory given by TM = π*ℝℋom(E, E)[1]. This recovers the Behrend-Fantechi construction in derived language. The DT invariants count sheaves by virtual integration over the derived moduli of sheaves. The Pantev-Toën-Vezzosi-Benkhalifa shifted symplectic structures: a derived scheme X has a symplectic structure of degree k consisting of an oriented form ω ∈ Ω²(X) of degree k with a non-degenerate pairing TX ⊗ TX → OX[k] that is closed. When k = 0, this recovers an ordinary symplectic structure; when k = 1, it gives the derived critical locus of a function; when k = −1, it gives a Poisson structure. The Lagrangian correspondence in derived geometry returns the notion of a derived Lagrangian intersection, which carries a (−1)-shifted symplectic structure — mirroring the classical symplectic reduction. Borisov-Joyce developed the theory of d-manifolds and d-orbifolds as a differential-geometric approach to derived geometry, with applications to the virtual fundamental class and wall-crossing.
Derived moduli spaces carry natural perfect obstruction theories; shifted symplectic structures provide the geometric framework for understanding Lagrangians, critical loci, and Poisson geometry in derived settings.
Part 4

🔥 Spectral Algebraic Geometry and Applications

Spectral
E∞-Ring Spectra, Spectral Algebraic Geometry, Langlands, and BPS Invariants
Spectral algebraic geometry (Lurie) replaces simplicial commutative rings with E∞-ring spectra, yielding a richer and more flexible theory. The ∞-topos of sheaves on the étale site of a ring spectrum encodes p-adic Hodge theory: the Fargues-Fontaine curve is realized as a spectral scheme, giving a geometric home for p-adic Galois representations. The derived geometric Langlands program (Arinkin-Gaitsgory-Kazhdan-Raskin-Rozenblyum-Varshavsky) studies the moduli of G-bundles on a curve and the construction of Hecke eigensheaves via derived methods. The categorification of the geometric Satake equivalence uses derived algebraic geometry to upgrade the Satake isomorphism to an equivalence of ∞-categories of sheaves on the affine Grassmannian. The categorified Donaldson-Thomas theory (Joyce-Song) computes generalized DT invariants via integration over the derived moduli of sheaves, producing integer-valued invariants that count semistable objects in the derived category of coherent sheaves. The BPS invariants and wall-crossing formulas are expressed using potential functions on derived critical loci — these are the (0-shifted) symplectic structures arising from the derived moduli of sheaves on a Calabi-Yau 3-fold. The Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula has a derived interpretation via the motivic Hall algebra of the derived category. The Betti geometric Langlands (Ben-Zvi-Nadler) uses derived algebraic geometry to relate D-modules on BunG to coherent sheaves on the derived stack of local systems. These results demonstrate that derived algebraic geometry is not merely a technical enhancement but a necessary framework for the deepest aspects of modern geometric representation theory and enumerative geometry.
Spectral algebraic geometry unifies arithmetic, geometric, and categorical structures; from the Fargues-Fontaine curve to geometric Langlands and DT theory, derived methods are indispensable for modern mathematics.
