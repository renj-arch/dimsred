What is Banking Awareness?

Banking Awareness is a key section in banking exams. It tests your knowledge of the Indian banking system, banking regulations, financial institutions, government schemes, and banking terminology. Questions range from history of banking to recent developments in the financial sector.

Key Topics in Banking Awareness

1. History of Banking in India: Bank of Hindustan (1770), Presidency Banks (1800s), Imperial Bank (1921), nationalization of 14 banks in 1969 and 6 more in 1980, establishment of RBI in 1935, SBI in 1955, NABARD in 1982.

2. Reserve Bank of India (RBI): Central bank of India. Key functions: monetary policy formulation, regulation of banking system, currency issuance, foreign exchange management. Important terms: Repo Rate, Reverse Repo Rate, CRR, SLR, MSF, Bank Rate, OMO, LAF.

3. Scheduled Commercial Banks: Public sector banks (SBI, PNB, BOB, Canara, etc.), private sector banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, Kotak), foreign banks, regional rural banks (RRBs), small finance banks, payment banks.

4. Financial Regulators: RBI (banking), SEBI (securities markets), IRDAI (insurance), PFRDA (pensions), NHB (housing finance), SIDBI (MSME finance), NABARD (agriculture and rural development).

5. Banking Terms: NPA (Non-Performing Asset), CAR (Capital Adequacy Ratio), Basel III norms, CASA ratio, Net Interest Margin (NIM), RTGS, NEFT, IMPS, UPI, CBS (Core Banking Solution), KYC, AML.

6. Government Schemes: Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY), Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana (PMMY), Stand-Up India, Startup India, Atal Pension Yojana (APY), Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana (PMJJBY), Pradhan Mantri Suraksha Bima Yojana (PMSBY).

7. Financial Inclusion: Initiatives to provide banking services to unbanked population — BC/BF model, no-frills accounts, PMJDY, differentiated banks (payments banks, small finance banks), digital India.

Monetary Policy Transmission and the Liquidity Adjustment Framework

The Reserve Bank of India operates the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) as the primary tool for transmitting its policy stance to the banking system. The LAF consists of two standing facilities: the repo window, where banks borrow overnight against government securities, and the reverse repo window, where they park surplus funds. Since the adoption of the Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework in 2016, the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) sets the policy repo rate with a target of 4 % consumer‑price inflation, ±2 %. Changes in the repo rate influence the marginal cost of funds based lending rate (MCLR) and the newer external benchmark lending rate (EBLR), which in turn affect retail and corporate loan pricing.

Transmission operates through four channels. The interest‑rate channel passes the repo signal to short‑term money‑market rates (call money, tri‑party repo, commercial paper). The credit channel works via banks’ cost of funds, altering loan supply. The asset‑price channel moves equity and bond valuations, affecting wealth and collateral values. The exchange‑rate channel influences capital flows and the rupee’s value, feeding back into imported inflation. Since 2020 the RBI introduced the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) at a rate 25 basis points below the repo rate, providing a floor for the overnight call‑money rate and improving the symmetry of the corridor.

Open Market Operations (OMOs) and the Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) complement the LAF by managing durable liquidity. OMOs involve outright purchase or sale of government securities, while MSS absorbs surplus liquidity arising from large capital inflows. The Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) allows banks to borrow up to 2 % of their Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) at a penal rate (repo + 25 bps), acting as a safety valve. The RBI’s liquidity management framework also includes variable‑rate reverse repo auctions (VRRR) and fine‑tuning operations to keep the weighted average call rate (WACR) close to the policy repo rate.

Empirical studies by the RBI show that the pass‑through from repo to the weighted average lending rate (WALR) has improved from roughly 30 % in the pre‑FIT period to above 70 % after 2019, aided by the shift to EBLR linking. However, transmission to deposit rates remains sluggish, creating a persistent net interest margin (NIM) buffer for banks. The MPC’s forward guidance, communicated through the Monetary Policy Report and the Minutes of the MPC meeting, shapes market expectations and reduces volatility in the short end of the yield curve.

Basel III Capital Standards and India’s Implementation Timeline

India adopted the Basel III framework through a phased roadmap issued by the RBI in 2012, with full convergence targeted for March 2021. The framework raises the quality and quantity of capital, introduces leverage and liquidity standards, and mandates countercyclical buffers. The key pillars for Indian scheduled commercial banks are:

  • Minimum Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio of 5.5 % of risk‑weighted assets (RWA), plus a Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) of 2.5 % (phased in 0.625 % per year from 2016).
  • Additional Tier 1 (AT1) and Tier 2 capital instruments subject to loss‑absorption features (write‑down or conversion at the point of non‑viability).
  • Leverage Ratio of at least 3 % (Tier 1 capital / total exposure), applicable from March 2018.
  • Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) requiring high‑quality liquid assets (HQLA) to cover 30‑day net cash outflows, phased to 100 % by January 2021.
  • Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) of 100 % from October 2021, ensuring stable funding over a one‑year horizon.

The RBI also introduced a Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB) framework, currently set at 0 % but activatable up to 2.5 % when credit‑to‑GDP gaps signal excess. Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D‑SIBs) — SBI, ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank — hold an extra Higher Loss Absorbency (HLA) requirement of 0.6 % to 1.2 % CET1. The Basel III Finalisation Package (2022) revised the standardised approach for credit risk, the credit valuation adjustment (CVA) framework, and the operational risk capital model; the RBI issued draft guidelines in 2023 for implementation by 2025.

Compliance data from the RBI’s Quarterly Banking Statistics show that as of March 2024, the aggregate CET1 ratio of the banking system stood at 13.2 %, well above the regulatory minimum, while the leverage ratio averaged 4.8 %. Small finance banks and payment banks, however, operate under a simplified capital framework with a minimum CET1 of 9 % and no AT1 issuance. The transition has strengthened loss‑absorption capacity, but the higher capital charge for unrated corporate exposures has nudged banks toward better‑rated borrowers, influencing credit allocation.

Worked Example: Basel III Buffer Calculation
A scheduled commercial bank reports risk‑weighted assets of ₹1,20,000 crore. Its CET1 capital is ₹9,000 crore, AT1 capital ₹1,200 crore, and Tier 2 capital ₹2,000 crore. Compute the bank’s CET1 ratio, total capital ratio, and verify whether it meets the minimum CET1 plus CCB requirement.
Step 1: CET1 ratio = CET1 capital ÷ RWA = 9,000 ÷ 1,20,000 = 7.5 %.
Step 2: Total capital = CET1 + AT1 + Tier 2 = 9,000 + 1,200 + 2,000 = ₹12,200 crore. Total capital ratio = 12,200 ÷ 1,20,000 = 10.17 %.
Step 3: Minimum CET1 including CCB = 5.5 % + 2.5 % = 8.0 %. The bank’s CET1 ratio of 7.5 % falls short by 0.5 %.
Conclusion: The bank satisfies the total capital requirement (≥ 10.5 % including buffers) but must raise additional CET1 capital or retain earnings to meet the CET1 + CCB threshold.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a capital adequacy check, first identify the required minimum (CET1 + CCB, total capital + CCB, leverage ratio). Compute each ratio from the given figures, then compare sequentially. Remember that AT1 and Tier 2 count only toward total capital, not CET1. A quick mental shortcut: CCB = 2.5 % of RWA; add it to the base CET1 requirement (5.5 %) to get the effective CET1 floor (8 %).

Resolution Mechanisms for Stressed Assets under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) created a unified, time‑bound framework for resolving corporate insolvency. The process is initiated by a financial creditor, operational creditor, or the corporate debtor itself before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT). Once admitted, a Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) commences with a 180‑day moratorium (extendable by 90 days). An Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) takes control, constituting a Committee of Creditors (CoC) that decides on a resolution plan by a 66 % voting share.

Key amendments have sharpened the regime. The 2020 amendment introduced a pre‑packaged insolvency resolution process (PIRP) for MSMEs, allowing a debtor‑in‑possession model with a 120‑day timeline. The 2021 amendment enabled cross‑border insolvency cooperation under the UNCITRAL Model Law. The 2022 amendment added a fast‑track corridor for cases with admitted claims below ₹1 crore, reducing the timeline to 90 days. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) regulates insolvency professionals, information utilities, and valuation standards.

Outcomes are classified as resolution (plan approved), liquidation (plan rejected or CoC votes for liquidation), or withdrawal (settlement before admission). As of March 2024, the IBBI reported that 4,800 CIRPs had been admitted, with a resolution rate of approximately 30 % and an average recovery of 45 % of admitted claims for resolved cases. The Stressed Asset Resolution Framework (2021) by the RBI complements IBC by mandating early identification (Special Mention Accounts), a 30‑day review period, and a 180‑day resolution timeline for accounts above ₹2,000 crore, with a mandatory Inter‑Creditor Agreement (ICA) for consortium lending.

Challenges remain: judicial delays at NCLT/NCLAT, valuation disputes, and the “haircut” stigma deterring promoters. The Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) and the newly licensed National Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd (NARCL) (2022) aim to aggregate large stressed assets, providing a market‑based exit route parallel to IBC. The RBI’s Prudential Framework for Resolution of Stressed Assets (2019) also requires banks to maintain a Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR) of at least 70 % for NPAs, influencing provisioning dynamics during resolution.

Digital Payments Architecture: UPI, Bharat BillPay, and the Regulatory Sandbox

The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), launched by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) in April 2016, has become the backbone of retail digital payments. UPI 2.0 (August 2018) added overdraft accounts, one‑time mandates, and invoice‑in‑the‑box. UPI 123PAY (March 2022) extended access to feature phones via IVR, missed‑call, and sound‑based proximity. By December 2023, UPI processed over 12 billion transactions monthly, accounting for roughly 75 % of total digital payment volume.

Bharat Bill Payment System (BBPS), operational since 2018, provides an interoperable platform for recurring bill payments (electricity, telecom, water, gas). It operates under a agent‑institution model where banks and non‑bank entities act as Bharat Bill Payment Operating Units (BBPOUs). The RBI mandated that all billers onboarding after March 2022 must integrate with BBPS, driving standardisation.

The Regulatory Sandbox framework, introduced by the RBI in 2020, allows fintech firms to test innovative products (e.g., offline retail payments, blockchain‑based KYC, AI‑driven credit scoring) in a controlled environment with relaxed licensing. Cohorts have focused on retail payments, cross‑border remittances, and MSME lending. Successful pilots graduate to full‑scale deployment after meeting consumer‑protection, data‑localisation, and cyber‑security criteria.

Security architecture relies on two‑factor authentication (2FA) — device binding (UPI‑PIN) and transaction‑level OTP or biometric. The Tokenisation mandate (effective October 2022) replaces card‑on‑file with network tokens, reducing fraud. The Payments Vision 2025 document outlines targets: 100 % digital payment acceptance at merchant level, 30 % of GDP transacted digitally, and a Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) pilot for wholesale and retail segments (e‑₹‑W launched November 2022, e‑₹‑R pilot February 2023).

Priority Sector Lending Revisions and the Role of Small Finance Banks

The Priority Sector Lending (PSL) guidelines, revised comprehensively in September 2020 and again in April 2022, mandate that domestic scheduled commercial banks allocate 40 % of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to priority sectors. Sub‑targets include 18 % for agriculture (of which 8 % for small and marginal farmers), 7.5 % for micro enterprises, and 10 % for weaker sections. The 2022 revision added start‑ups (up to ₹50 crore), renewable energy (solar, wind, biomass), and health infrastructure as eligible categories.

Small Finance Banks (SFBs), licensed from 2015, have a higher PSL obligation of 75 % of ANBC, with at least 50 % of their loan portfolio to be constituted by loans up to ₹25 lakh. They must maintain a minimum 75 % of total branches in unbanked rural centres. As of March 2024, 12 SFBs collectively held a deposit base of ₹2.3 lakh crore and a loan book of ₹2.0 lakh crore, with a gross NPA ratio of 4.2 %, reflecting their niche focus on micro‑credit.

The RBI’s PSL Classification Review (2023) introduced a “green taxonomy” to tag climate‑friendly loans, enabling banks to meet both PSL and emerging Sustainable Finance disclosures. The Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE) and the Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme (ECLGS) (extended to March 2024) provide collateral‑free coverage, incentivising banks to meet micro‑enterprise targets.

Monitoring compliance is done through the Quarterly PSL Returns submitted to the RBI. Shortfalls attract a penalty of 0.5 % of the deficient amount per quarter, and persistent non‑compliance can restrict branch expansion. The Priority Sector Lending Certificates (PSLCs) market, operational since 2016, allows banks with surplus PSL to sell certificates to deficit banks, creating a price signal for priority‑sector credit. In FY 2023‑24, PSLC trading volume crossed ₹1.2 lakh crore, indicating active use of the mechanism.

  • Confusing the repo rate with the reverse repo rate when describing the direction of liquidity flow.
  • Assuming the Capital Conservation Buffer is part of the minimum CET1 requirement rather than an add‑on.
  • Overlooking the 66 % CoC voting threshold for resolution plan approval under IBC.
  • Treating UPI and BBPS as interchangeable; UPI is a real‑time P2P/P2M rail, BBPS is a biller‑centric recurring‑payment platform.
  • Neglecting the higher PSL target (75 %) applicable specifically to Small Finance Banks.
  • Misstating the LCR phase‑in timeline; full 100 % compliance was required from January 2021, not 2020.
  • Review these traps regularly to avoid common mistakes.

Banking Ombudsman Scheme and Consumer Grievance Redressal

The Banking Ombudsman Scheme (BOS) was first introduced by the Reserve Bank of India in 1995 under Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949. It provides a cost‑free, quasi‑judicial forum for customers to resolve complaints against banks without approaching courts. The scheme was substantially revised in 2006, 2017 and again in 2021 to widen its scope, reduce timelines and incorporate digital‑banking grievances. As of the 2021 amendment, the Ombudsman can adjudicate complaints up to ₹20 lakh (previously ₹10 lakh) and the appellate authority is the Deputy Governor of the RBI in charge of consumer protection. The scheme covers 23 categories of complaints, including deficiency in service, non‑observance of RBI directives on interest rates, levying of charges without prior notice, and failure to honour commitments on digital‑payment products such as UPI and BBPS. Banks are required to appoint a Nodal Officer and to display the Ombudsman’s contact details at every branch and on their website. The Ombudsman’s award is binding on the bank; if the bank fails to comply within 30 days, the RBI may impose penal action under Section 47A of the Banking Regulation Act. In FY 2023‑24, the 22 Banking Ombudsman offices collectively received 1.84 lakh complaints, of which 78 % were resolved in favour of the complainant either through mediation or award. The scheme also mandates a quarterly reporting format (BO‑QR) that feeds into the RBI’s Consumer Education and Protection Department (CEPD) for systemic trend analysis.

Non‑Performing Asset Recognition and Provisioning under Ind AS

Asset classification in India follows the RBI’s Master Direction on Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning (IRACP), last updated in 2021. An advance becomes a Non‑Performing Asset (NPA) when interest or principal remains overdue for more than 90 days. The IRACP prescribes three NPA sub‑categories: Sub‑standard (overdue 90‑180 days), Doubtful (overdue 180‑365 days, further split into D1, D2, D3 based on ageing), and Loss (overdue > 365 days or identified as uncollectible). Provisioning norms under IRACP are 15 % for Sub‑standard, 25 % for Doubtful (with higher rates for older doubtful assets), and 100 % for Loss assets. Since 1 April 2018, scheduled commercial banks have been required to prepare financial statements under Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS), with Ind AS 109 governing expected credit loss (ECL) provisioning. The ECL model replaces the rule‑based IRACP percentages with a forward‑looking, probability‑weighted estimate of credit losses over the life of the asset. Banks must run a dual‑track approach: maintain IRACP provisioning for regulatory capital purposes while recognising ECL in the profit‑and‑loss account. The RBI’s 2020 circular on “Implementation of Ind AS 109” mandated a minimum provision coverage ratio (PCR) of 70 % for the aggregate loan book, and a minimum Tier‑1 capital ratio of 9.5 % including the capital conservation buffer. As of March 2024, the system‑wide gross NPA ratio stood at 3.9 % and the net NPA ratio at 1.0 %, reflecting the combined effect of stricter recognition, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code resolutions, and the ECL‑driven provisioning build‑up.

Worked Example – Dual Provisioning Calculation
A bank holds a corporate loan of ₹500 crore classified as Doubtful‑D2 (overdue 210 days). Under IRACP the required provision is 40 % of the outstanding. Under Ind AS 109 the bank’s ECL model estimates a 55 % lifetime loss probability. Compute the provision shown in the regulatory return and the provision recognised in the Ind AS financial statements.
Regulatory (IRACP) provision = 40 % × ₹500 crore = ₹200 crore. Ind AS 109 (ECL) provision = 55 % × ₹500 crore = ₹275 crore. The bank reports ₹200 crore in the IRACP‑based provisioning schedule submitted to the RBI and recognises ₹275 crore as impairment loss in the profit‑and‑loss account under Ind AS. The excess ₹75 crore is held as additional provision but does not reduce regulatory capital; it only affects accounting profit.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “provision as per RBI norms” versus “provision as per Ind AS”, identify the framework first. IRACP percentages are fixed (15/25/40/100 %); Ind AS 109 requires a scenario‑based ECL estimate. Memorise the IRACP matrix and remember that the higher of the two provisions is never automatically taken for capital adequacy – the regulatory return follows IRACP, while the balance sheet follows Ind AS.

Financial Inclusion: PMJDY, Business Correspondents, and Payments Banks

The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) was launched on 28 August 2014 with the objective of providing universal access to banking facilities. By March 2024, over 50 crore accounts had been opened, of which 55 % were in rural and semi‑urban centres. The scheme offers a zero‑balance savings account, a RuPay debit card with an accidental insurance cover of ₹2 lakh, and an overdraft facility up to ₹10,000 after six months of satisfactory operation. The Business Correspondent (BC) model, formalised by the RBI in 2006 and expanded in 2010, enables banks to extend outreach through retail agents equipped with micro‑ATMs or handheld devices. As of December 2023, there were 1.3 million active BCs handling 2.4 billion transactions annually, accounting for 38 % of total rural banking transactions. Payments Banks, a differentiated licence category introduced in 2015, can accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh per customer, issue debit cards, and distribute third‑party financial products but cannot undertake lending. Six entities received licences (Airtel Payments Bank, Paytm Payments Bank, India Post Payments Bank, Fino Payments Bank, Jio Payments Bank, and NSDL Payments Bank). By March 2024, Payments Banks collectively held ₹1.1 lakh crore in deposits and processed 1.6 billion UPI transactions, demonstrating their role as last‑mile digital‑payment enablers. The RBI’s 2022 “Framework for Scale‑Up of Business Correspondents” mandated a minimum net‑worth of ₹25 lakh for BCs, a mandatory KYC‑compliant onboarding process, and a grievance‑redressal turnaround of 15 days.

Supervisory Review Process and Stress Testing under Pillar 2

Pillar 2 of the Basel III framework requires banks to conduct an Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) and supervisors to perform a Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP). The RBI issued the “Guidelines on ICAAP and SREP” in November 2017, revised in July 2020 to align with the final Basel III standards. Banks must submit an ICAAP document annually, covering risk appetite, capital planning under baseline and adverse scenarios, and governance arrangements. The SREP evaluates the ICAAP, assigns a supervisory rating (1‑5), and may impose additional capital charges (Pillar 2 capital add‑on) or restrict dividend distribution. Stress testing is a core ICAAP component; the RBI prescribes a common set of macro‑economic scenarios (GDP growth, interest‑rate shock, exchange‑rate depreciation, and credit‑growth slowdown) and requires banks to run both sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis on a quarterly basis. In the 2023‑24 stress‑test cycle, the system‑wide Common Equity Tier‑1 (CET1) ratio under the adverse scenario fell to 8.2 %, above the regulatory minimum of 7.0 % but below the 9.5 % target including buffers, prompting the RBI to issue a “capital conservation” advisory to 12 banks. The RBI also introduced a “Liquidity Stress Testing” framework in 2021, requiring banks to project cash‑flow mismatches over a 30‑day horizon under a combined funding‑run and market‑stress scenario.

Green Finance Taxonomy and RBI Climate Risk Guidelines

Recognising the financial‑stability implications of climate change, the RBI released a Discussion Paper on “Climate Risk and Sustainable Finance” in July 2021, followed by the “Guidelines on Climate‑Related Financial Risk Management” in April 2023. The guidelines require scheduled commercial banks to integrate climate risk into their risk‑management frameworks, governance, and disclosure practices aligned with the Task Force on Climate‑Related Financial Disclosures (TCFD). A key deliverable is the development of a Green Taxonomy – a classification system that tags loans and investments as “green”, “transition”, or “brown” based on sectoral criteria such as renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, and sustainable agriculture. The RBI’s 2023 taxonomy draft lists 12 eligible green categories and sets a minimum 75 % threshold for proceeds‑use verification. Banks must disclose, from FY 2024‑25 onward, the proportion of green‑tagged assets in their loan book, the methodology for tagging, and the results of climate‑scenario analysis (both physical and transition risks). The guidelines also mandate a board‑level Climate Risk Committee and the appointment of a Chief Sustainability Officer (CSO) for banks with assets above ₹5 lakh crore. As of March 2024, 18 banks had published inaugural climate‑risk disclosures, collectively reporting green‑tagged exposures of ₹3.4 lakh crore, representing 4.2 % of total domestic credit.

  • Applying IRACP provisioning percentages directly to Ind AS 109 ECL outputs without recognising the dual‑track requirement.
  • Assuming Payments Banks can lend; they are restricted to deposit‑taking and payment services only.
  • Overlooking the mandatory 15‑day grievance redressal timeline for Business Correspondents introduced in the 2022 framework.
  • Treating the Pillar 2 capital add‑on as part of the minimum CET1 requirement rather than a supervisory overlay.
  • Neglecting the board‑level Climate Risk Committee and CSO appointment mandate for large banks under the 2023 climate guidelines.

Prompt Corrective Action Framework and Its Triggers

The Reserve Bank of India introduced the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework in 2002 and revised it substantially in April 2017 to align with Basel III capital standards. The framework is activated when a scheduled commercial bank breaches any of three key risk thresholds: Capital to Risk‑Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR) falling below 9 % (or 10.5 % including the capital conservation buffer), Net Non‑Performing Assets (NNPA) exceeding 6 % of net advances, or Return on Assets (RoA) remaining negative for two consecutive years. Once triggered, the RBI imposes a graduated set of mandatory and discretionary restrictions. Mandatory actions include a ban on dividend distribution, restriction on branch expansion, and a cap on management compensation. Discretionary measures may involve limiting lending to high‑risk sectors, requiring the bank to submit a capital restoration plan, or even superseding the board in extreme cases. The 2017 revision added a “leverage ratio” trigger (≥ 3.5 %) and introduced a “watch‑list” category for banks that are close to breaching thresholds but have not yet crossed them. According to RBI data, multiple public sector banks have been placed under PCA at various times; several have since exited after meeting the exit criteria of sustained compliance for four consecutive quarters. The framework serves both as a supervisory early‑warning tool and a market signal, influencing credit ratings and investor perception. Banks under PCA typically experience a contraction in loan growth during the restriction period, underscoring the framework’s material impact on credit flow.

Consolidation of Public Sector Banks: Merger Rationale and Outcomes

Between 2017 and 2020 the Government of India executed a series of mega‑mergers that reduced the number of public sector banks (PSBs) from 27 to 12. The primary rationale articulated in the “Indradhanush” plan and subsequent merger announcements was to create stronger, globally competitive entities with improved capital buffers, enhanced lending capacity, and reduced operational overlap. The mergers followed an “anchor‑bank” model: a larger, relatively healthier bank absorbed one or more smaller peers. Notable combinations include the merger of Bank of Baroda, Vijaya Bank and Dena Bank (effective April 2019), the consolidation of Punjab National Bank with Oriental Bank of Commerce and United Bank of India (April 2020), and the amalgamation of Canara Bank with Syndicate Bank (April 2020). Post‑merger, anchor banks reported a combined CRAR improvement in the range of 80‑120 basis points within the first two years, driven by the elimination of duplicate capital requirements and synergies in treasury operations. However, integration challenges surfaced in technology platform harmonisation, human‑resource rationalisation, and cultural alignment. The RBI’s review of merger outcomes noted that while asset quality metrics (gross NPA ratio) improved modestly system‑wide, the cost‑to‑income ratio for merged entities remained elevated in FY 2022‑23, indicating that efficiency gains are still materialising. The consolidation also altered the competitive landscape, with the top four PSBs now accounting for a majority share of total banking assets, raising concerns about concentration risk and the need for robust resolution planning.

Examiner's technique
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about the Prompt Corrective Action framework is correct? (a) PCA is triggered only when CRAR falls below 8 %. (b) Mandatory actions under PCA include a ban on dividend distribution and restriction on branch expansion. (c) Discretionary actions are binding on the bank’s board without RBI approval. (d) The leverage ratio trigger was introduced in the original 2002 framework.”
The correct answer is (b). The 2017 PCA revision sets the CRAR trigger at 9 % (10.5 % with buffer), not 8 %. Mandatory actions explicitly prohibit dividend payment and branch expansion. Discretionary actions require RBI approval and are not automatically binding. The leverage ratio trigger (≥ 3.5 %) was added in the 2017 revision, not in the 2002 original framework.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the three quantitative PCA triggers (CRAR, NNPA, RoA) and the two‑tier action list (mandatory vs discretionary). In MCQs, eliminate options that cite the pre‑2017 thresholds or that treat discretionary measures as automatic. A quick mental table of “Trigger → Mandatory Action → Discretionary Action” saves 30‑40 seconds per question.

Asset Reconstruction Companies and the Securitisation Framework

The Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (SARFAESI Act) created the legal backbone for Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) in India. ARCs acquire non‑performing assets (NPAs) from banks and financial institutions at a discount, finance the purchase through Security Receipts (SRs) issued to qualified institutional buyers, and then pursue resolution via restructuring, asset sale, or enforcement of collateral. As of recent RBI data, 28 ARCs were registered, collectively managing a substantial asset base. The RBI’s 2017 “Revised Guidelines for ARCs” mandated a minimum net‑owned fund of ₹100 crore, a cap on leverage at 5 times net‑owned funds, and a requirement that at least 15 % of the acquisition cost be funded by the ARC’s own capital. The guidelines also introduced a “fair‑value” valuation norm for SRs and a quarterly disclosure regime for asset‑level recovery data. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, complemented the SARFAESI route by providing a time‑bound corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP) that ARCs can invoke as financial creditors. In practice, ARCs have resolved a significant portion of acquired assets through outright sale and restructuring, while the remainder remain under litigation or liquidation. The 2022 “ARC‑Bank Co‑origination Framework” permits banks to co‑originate loans with ARCs for stressed‑asset revitalisation, sharing risk and reward on a pre‑agreed basis. This co‑origination model is expected to improve recovery rates by aligning incentives and providing fresh working‑capital lines to viable borrowers.

Foreign Exchange Management Act: Liberalised Remittance Scheme and Capital Account Convertibility

The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA) replaced the erstwhile FERA and provides the statutory framework for all cross‑border foreign‑exchange transactions. A pivotal retail‑level facility under FEMA is the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), introduced in February 2004 and progressively liberalised. As per the current notification, resident individuals may remit up to US $250,000 per financial year for any permissible current or capital account transaction, including overseas education, medical treatment, purchase of immovable property, and investment in foreign equity or debt instruments. The LRS does not cover remittances for margin trading, lottery tickets, or prohibited activities under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act. Banks acting as Authorised Dealers must obtain a Form A2 declaration, verify PAN, and report each transaction to the RBI’s reporting system within the prescribed timeline. On the capital‑account side, India continues to follow a calibrated approach: foreign direct investment (FDI) is largely under the automatic route, while portfolio investment limits for Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) are capped at 24 % of a company’s paid‑up capital (extendable to 49 % with board approval). External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) are governed by the RBI’s “ECB Framework” (last revised in 2023), which classifies borrowings by maturity, end‑use, and hedging requirements. The RBI’s framework for cross‑border insolvency and guidelines on international trade settlement in rupees signal incremental steps toward deeper capital‑account convertibility, but full convertibility remains contingent on macro‑prudential stability, inflation targeting credibility, and the robustness of the financial‑sector resolution architecture.

Co‑operative Banking Regulation: Multi‑State Co‑operative Societies Act and RBI Supervision

Co‑operative banks in India operate under a dual regulatory regime: the Reserve Bank of India supervises their banking functions under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (as amended in 2020), while the Registrar of Co‑operative Societies (state or central) governs their organisational structure. The Multi‑State Co‑operative Societies Act, 2002 (MSCS Act) provides the legal framework for co‑operatives with operations in more than one state, including multi‑state urban co‑operative banks (UCBs). The 2020 amendment to the Banking Regulation Act brought all UCBs — single‑state and multi‑state — under direct RBI licensing, inspection, and resolution powers, including the ability to supersede boards and appoint administrators. As per recent RBI data, there were over 1,500 UCBs with a combined deposit base of approximately ₹5.6 lakh crore and advances of approximately ₹3.2 lakh crore. The RBI’s 2021 “Revised Supervisory Framework for UCBs” introduced a tiered classification (Tier 1 to Tier 4) based on deposit size, with differentiated capital adequacy (minimum CRAR 9 % for Tier 1, 12 % for Tier 4), exposure limits, and mandatory professionalisation of management. The framework also mandated a “Board of Management” (BoM) distinct from the Board of Directors, comprising at least 50 % independent members with banking expertise. In 2022, the RBI issued a “Prompt Corrective Action” framework for UCBs mirroring the commercial‑bank PCA, with triggers at CRAR < 9 %, NNPA > 6 %, and sustained losses. The MSCS Act was further amended in 2023 to enable the central government to wind up or amalgamate sick multi‑state co‑operatives without state‑level consent, accelerating resolution. These reforms aim to reduce governance failures — historically the primary cause of UCB distress — while preserving the co‑operative character of member‑owned institutions.
