What is Static GK?

Static GK (General Knowledge) covers facts that do not change frequently — capitals, currencies, important days, national parks, dams, rivers, books and authors, awards and honors, sports tournaments, international organizations, and Indian constitution basics. Static GK is tested in competitive exams as part of the General Awareness section.

Key Topics in Static GK

1. Capital Cities and Currencies: Country-capital-currency triads. Important: India (New Delhi, Rupee), USA (Washington DC, Dollar), UK (London, Pound), Japan (Tokyo, Yen), China (Beijing, Yuan), Russia (Moscow, Ruble), Germany (Berlin, Euro), France (Paris, Euro), Australia (Canberra, Dollar), Brazil (Brasilia, Real).

2. Important Days and Dates: Republic Day (Jan 26), Independence Day (Aug 15), Gandhi Jayanti (Oct 2), International Yoga Day (Jun 21), World Environment Day (Jun 5), Human Rights Day (Dec 10), etc.

3. Books and Authors: Indian authors: 'The God of Small Things' by Arundhati Roy, 'Midnight's Children' by Salman Rushdie, 'Discovery of India' by Jawaharlal Nehru, 'My Experiments with Truth' by Gandhi, 'Wings of Fire' by APJ Abdul Kalam. International: '1984' by George Orwell, 'To Kill a Mockingbird' by Harper Lee.

4. Awards and Honours: Bharat Ratna, Padma awards (Vibhushan, Bhushan, Shri), Nobel Prizes (Peace, Literature, Science categories), Oscar Awards, Booker Prize, Dadasaheb Phalke Award, Arjuna Award, Dronacharya Award.

5. National Parks and Wildlife Sanctuaries: Jim Corbett (Uttarakhand), Kaziranga (Assam), Sundarbans (West Bengal), Ranthambore (Rajasthan), Bandhavgarh (MP), Periyar (Kerala), Kanha (MP), Gir (Gujarat).

6. Dams and Rivers: Bhakra Nangal (Sutlej), Tehri (Bhagirathi), Hirakud (Mahanadi), Sardar Sarovar (Narmada), Nagarjuna Sagar (Krishna), Damodar Valley (Damodar).

7. International Organizations: UN (HQ: New York), UNESCO (Paris), WHO (Geneva), IMF (Washington DC), World Bank (Washington DC), WTO (Geneva), SAARC (Kathmandu), BRICS (Shanghai).

Solved Examples

Example 1
What is the currency of Japan?

a) Won b) Yen c) Yuan d) Ringgit
Solution: b) Yen is the currency of Japan. South Korea uses Won, China uses Yuan, Malaysia uses Ringgit.
Example 2
World Environment Day is celebrated on which date?

a) June 5 b) April 22 c) July 11 d) March 22
Solution: a) June 5. April 22 is Earth Day. July 11 is World Population Day. March 22 is World Water Day.
Example 3
Who wrote the book 'The Discovery of India'?

a) Mahatma Gandhi b) Jawaharlal Nehru c) Dr. B.R. Ambedkar d) Rabindranath Tagore
Solution: b) Jawaharlal Nehru wrote 'The Discovery of India' while imprisoned at Ahmednagar Fort in 1942-1945.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Use acronyms and mnemonics — e.g., 'My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles' for planet order, or 'Homely Rich Men' for HOMe and RUS (India's neighbouring countries).
  • Create mental maps — associate countries with their capitals using geographical location.
  • Group information — learn all countries in a region together (ASEAN, SAARC, G7, etc.) with their capitals.
  • For books-authors, remember the subject — e.g., if it's about India's freedom struggle, it's likely by an Indian leader.

Regional Groupings and Their Capitals

Understanding the institutional architecture of major regional groupings is a high‑yield area because examiners frequently ask for the headquarters city, the member‑state capitals, or the year of establishment. The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) was founded in 1985 and its secretariat sits in Kathmandu, Nepal. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) dates from 1967 with its secretariat in Jakarta, Indonesia. The European Union (EU) traces its origins to the 1951 European Coal and Steel Community; the current administrative centre is Brussels, Belgium, while the European Parliament holds sessions in Strasbourg, France, and the European Central Bank is headquartered in Frankfurt, Germany. The African Union (AU) succeeded the Organisation of African Unity in 2002 and operates from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The Organization of American States (OAS) was created in 1948 and its General Secretariat is in Washington, D.C., United States. The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) formed in 1981 and its secretariat is located in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) began in 2001 with its secretariat in Beijing, China. For each grouping, memorize the founding year, the headquarters city, and at least three member‑state capitals — this creates a mental map that lets you answer “Which of the following is not a member of ASEAN?” or “Where is the SAARC secretariat?” without hesitation.

Examiners also test knowledge of observer states and dialogue partners. For instance, the EU has candidate countries such as Montenegro, North Macedonia, Albania, Serbia, and Turkey, while ASEAN maintains dialogue partnerships with Australia, Canada, the EU, India, Japan, New Zealand, Russia, South Korea, and the United States. The SCO includes observer states like Afghanistan, Belarus, Iran, and Mongolia. Knowing the distinction between full members, observers, and dialogue partners prevents the common trap of selecting an observer as a full member in multiple‑choice questions.

Currency Regimes and Exchange Rate Mechanisms

Currency questions go beyond “What is the currency of Japan?” and often probe the regime that governs a currency’s value. A fixed‑exchange‑rate regime pegs the domestic currency to another currency or a basket, with the central bank intervening to maintain the parity. Classic examples include the Hong Kong dollar (pegged to the US dollar within a narrow band), the Saudi riyal (pegged to the US dollar), and the Danish krone (pegged to the euro via ERM II). A floating regime lets market forces determine the rate; the US dollar, euro, Japanese yen, British pound, and Swiss franc are the principal freely floating major currencies. A managed float (or dirty float) allows occasional central‑bank intervention to smooth volatility — India’s rupee and China’s renminbi operate under this model. Currency boards represent an extreme form of fixed regime where the monetary base is fully backed by foreign reserves; the Bulgarian lev and the Brunei dollar are contemporary examples.

Reserve‑currency status is another examiner favourite. The US dollar accounts for roughly 60 % of global foreign‑exchange reserves, the euro about 20 %, while the Japanese yen, British pound, Chinese renminbi, Canadian dollar, Australian dollar, and Swiss franc make up the remainder. The International Monetary Fund’s Special Drawing Right (SDR) basket currently comprises the US dollar, euro, Chinese renminbi, Japanese yen, and British pound, with weights reviewed every five years. Questions may ask which currency was added to the SDR basket in 2016 (the renminbi) or which currency left the basket in 1999 (the Deutsche mark, replaced by the euro). Understanding the mechanics of the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM II) — where non‑euro EU members keep their currencies within ±15 % of a central rate against the euro — helps answer questions on convergence criteria for euro adoption.

Worked Example
Which of the following currencies operates under a currency board arrangement? a) Indian Rupee b) Bulgarian Lev c) South Korean Won d) Brazilian Real
The Bulgarian Lev has been issued under a currency board since 1997, meaning every lev in circulation is backed by an equivalent amount of euro reserves. The Indian Rupee follows a managed float, the South Korean Won is a free float, and the Brazilian Real operates under a floating regime with occasional intervention. Therefore, the correct answer is b) Bulgarian Lev.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Create a two‑column table: regime type on the left, representative currencies on the right. Review it daily for a week before the test; the visual pattern makes it trivial to spot the odd one out in a multiple‑choice set.

Major International Awards: Criteria and Notable Recipients

Award questions often require matching the prize to its field, the awarding body, and a landmark laureate. The Nobel Prizes, established by Alfred Nobel’s will in 1895, are awarded annually in Physics, Chemistry, Physiology or Medicine, Literature, Peace, and Economic Sciences (the latter added in 1968 by the Sveriges Riksbank). The Peace Prize is decided by the Norwegian Nobel Committee in Oslo, while the other categories are selected by Swedish institutions. The Booker Prize, founded in 1969, recognises the best original novel written in English and published in the United Kingdom or Ireland; notable winners include Salman Rushdie for “Midnight’s Children” (1981) and Arundhati Roy for “The God of Small Things” (1997). The Pulitzer Prizes, administered by Columbia University since 1917, cover journalism, letters, drama, and music; the Fiction category has honoured authors such as Harper Lee (“To Kill a Mockingbird”, 1961) and Toni Morrison (“Beloved”, 1988). The Academy Awards (Oscars), presented by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences since 1929, reward cinematic achievements; the Best Picture winner “Parasite” (2020) marked the first non‑English language film to win. The Dadasaheb Phalke Award, India’s highest cinema honour, was instituted in 1969 and first awarded to Devika Rani. The Bharat Ratna, India’s highest civilian award, was instituted in 1954; recipients include C. V. Raman (1954), Sachin Tendulkar (2014), and Lata Mangeshkar (2001). The Padma awards — Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri — recognise distinguished service in any field; they are announced annually on Republic Day.

Examiners may ask for the year a specific laureate received the award, the category, or the institution that confers it. A reliable approach is to memorise a “top‑ten” list for each award: the first recipient, the most recent Indian recipient, and any historic firsts (e.g., first woman, first Asian, first non‑European). This limited set covers the majority of factual questions while keeping the memory load manageable.

Landmark Books and Their Historical Context

Beyond title‑author matching, questions probe the socio‑political backdrop of a work. “The Discovery of India” (1946) was written by Jawaharlal Nehru during his imprisonment at Ahmednagar Fort (1942‑1945) and reflects a nationalist narrative of Indian civilisation. “My Experiments with Truth” (1927‑1929) is Mahatma Gandhi’s autobiography, originally serialized in his journal “Young India”, offering insight into his philosophy of satyagraha. “Wings of Fire” (1999) by A. P. J. Abdul Kalam chronicles India’s missile programme and his vision for a developed India. Internationally, “1984” (1949) by George Orwell depicts a totalitarian surveillance state, influencing Cold War discourse. “To Kill a Mockingbird” (1960) by Harper Lee addresses racial injustice in the American South and won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1961. “The God of Small Things” (1997) by Arundhati Roy, set in Kerala, explores caste, forbidden love, and post‑colonial identity, earning the Booker Prize in 1997. “Midnight’s Children” (1981) by Salman Rushdie uses magical realism to narrate India’s transition from colonial rule to independence, winning the Booker and later the Booker of Bookers (1993). Knowing the publication year, the language of original composition, and the major award (if any) enables rapid elimination of distractors in matching‑type questions.

Important Days: Themes and UN Designations

International days are fixed by UN General Assembly resolutions and often carry a yearly theme. World Environment Day (5 June) was established in 1972 by UNGA Resolution 2994 (XXVII) and is hosted by a different country each year; the 2023 theme was “Solutions to Plastic Pollution”. World Health Day (7 April) marks the founding of the World Health Organization in 1948; the 2024 theme is “My Health, My Right”. International Day of Yoga (21 June) was proclaimed by UNGA Resolution 69/131 in 2014, recognizing yoga’s holistic benefits. Human Rights Day (10 December) commemorates the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948. International Women’s Day (8 March) originated from labour movements in the early 1900s and was officially recognised by the UN in 1977. World Water Day (22 March) was designated in 1993 (Resolution 47/193) to highlight freshwater sustainability. World Population Day (11 July) was established in 1989 (Resolution 45/216) to focus on demographic issues. Memorising the date, the UN resolution number (when feasible), and the typical thematic focus allows you to answer “Which day is observed on 22 March?” or “What was the 2022 theme of World Environment Day?” with confidence.

  • Assuming all European currencies use the euro; several EU members retain national currencies.
  • Mixing up the awarding bodies of the Nobel Peace Prize (Norwegian committee) and other Nobel categories (Swedish academies).
  • Attributing the Booker Prize to American authors; it is restricted to works published in the UK or Ireland.
  • Overlooking the distinction between a currency board and a conventional fixed peg.
  • Memorising only the date of an international day without its UN resolution or thematic focus.
  • Treating the Dadasaheb Phalke Award as a literary award rather than a cinema honour.

Mastering these advanced static‑GK dimensions equips you to handle the deeper, application‑oriented questions that differentiate top scorers.

Capital Cities with Multiple Official Capitals

Several sovereign states designate more than one city as an official capital, each serving a distinct constitutional or administrative function. South Africa operates with three capitals: Pretoria (executive), Cape Town (legislative), and Bloemfontein (judicial). This arrangement dates from the 1910 Union of South Africa and was retained after 1994 to balance regional interests. Bolivia’s constitution recognises Sucre as the constitutional capital, where the Supreme Court sits, while La Paz functions as the seat of government and hosts the executive and legislative branches. The Netherlands designates Amsterdam as the constitutional capital by law, yet The Hague houses the government, parliament, supreme court, and the royal residence. Malaysia’s federal constitution names Kuala Lumpur as the official capital, but the administrative centre was moved to Putrajaya in 1999; both cities retain capital status for different purposes. In Sri Lanka, Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte is the legislative capital while Colombo remains the commercial and executive hub. These multi‑capital systems often appear in matching‑type questions that ask candidates to pair a country with its executive, legislative, or judicial capital. Understanding the historical rationale — colonial legacy, ethnic balance, or decentralisation — helps eliminate distractors that list only the most famous city.

Examiners may also test the distinction between a “capital” and a “seat of government”. For instance, the Republic of Côte d’Ivoire moved its political capital from Abidjan to Yamoussoukro in 1983, yet Abidjan remains the economic capital and hosts many ministries. Similarly, Myanmar’s capital shifted from Yangon to Naypyidaw in 2005, a planned city built for administrative purposes. Knowing the year of relocation and the official designation (administrative, legislative, judicial) is essential for chronological questions. A reliable revision technique is to create a three‑column table: Country – Executive Capital – Legislative Capital – Judicial Capital, and populate it with the nine most frequently asked multi‑capital nations.

Currency Unions and Monetary Policy Coordination

A currency union exists when two or more states share a single currency or maintain irrevocably fixed exchange rates under a common monetary authority. The most prominent example is the Eurozone, comprising 20 of the 27 European Union members that have adopted the euro since 1999 (electronic) and 2002 (cash). The European Central Bank (ECB), headquartered in Frankfurt, conducts monetary policy for the union, while national central banks implement operations. Membership requires meeting the Maastricht convergence criteria: inflation within 1.5 percentage points of the three best‑performing EU states, government deficit ≤ 3 % of GDP, debt ≤ 60 % of GDP, exchange‑rate stability within ERM II for two years, and long‑term interest‑rate convergence. Countries such as Denmark and Sweden retain opt‑outs despite meeting criteria.

In Africa, the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU) and the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (CEMAC) each use the CFA franc, pegged to the euro at a fixed rate of 655.957 CFA = 1 EUR. The Banque Centrale des États de l’Afrique de l’Ouest (BCEAO) and the Banque des États de l’Afrique Centrale (BEAC) issue the currency and hold foreign‑exchange reserves at the French Treasury, guaranteeing convertibility. The East Caribbean Currency Union (ECCU) employs the East Caribbean dollar, pegged at 2.70 XCD = 1 USD, managed by the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank (ECCB) based in St. Kitts. The CFA franc zones and the ECCU illustrate a currency board arrangement: the domestic money supply is backed 100 % by foreign reserves, limiting independent monetary policy.

Dollarisation represents a unilateral adoption of a foreign currency without a formal union. Ecuador (2000), El Salvador (2001), and Zimbabwe (2009) replaced their national currencies with the US dollar to curb hyperinflation. Panama has used the US dollar alongside the balboa since 1904. In these cases, the adopting country loses seigniorage and cannot act as lender of last resort, making fiscal discipline critical. Examiners often contrast a currency board (fixed peg with full reserve backing) with a conventional fixed peg (partial reserves, discretionary intervention). Knowing the institutional framework — whether a supranational central bank exists (ECB, ECCB) or a national board operates under a foreign anchor — enables quick identification of the correct regime in scenario‑based questions.

Technique: Elimination by Institutional Anchor
Which of the following currency arrangements is characterised by a supranational central bank that sets a single monetary policy for all members? A) CFA franc zone (WAEMU) B) East Caribbean Currency Union C) Eurozone D) Ecuador’s dollarisation
The Eurozone is governed by the European Central Bank, a supranational institution that formulates monetary policy for all euro‑area members. The CFA franc zones have two separate central banks (BCEAO and BEAC) tied to the French Treasury, not a single supranational bank. The East Caribbean Currency Union has the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank, but it serves only eight small island economies and is not a supranational EU‑style body. Ecuador’s dollarisation has no central bank issuing the currency. Therefore, the correct answer is C) Eurozone.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to identify the monetary authority behind a currency union, first recall whether a single supranational central bank exists. The Eurozone (ECB) and the East Caribbean Currency Union (ECCB) are the only two unions with a truly supranational bank; the CFA franc zones operate two parallel national banks. Mark the union with a unique supranational bank as the answer and eliminate the others.

Award Categories and Selection Processes: Nobel, Booker, Oscar, Padma

The Nobel Prizes, established by Alfred Nobel’s 1895 will, are awarded in Physics, Chemistry, Physiology or Medicine, Literature, Peace, and Economic Sciences (the latter added in 1968 by the Sveriges Riksbank). The Peace Prize is decided by the Norwegian Nobel Committee appointed by the Norwegian Parliament, while the other categories are selected by Swedish academies: the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences (Physics, Chemistry, Economics), the Karolinska Institute (Medicine), and the Swedish Academy (Literature). Nominations are invited from qualified individuals — professors, previous laureates, members of academies — and remain confidential for 50 years. The first Nobel Prizes were awarded in 1901; notable firsts include Marie Curie (first woman, 1903 Physics, 1911 Chemistry), Rabindranath Tagore (first Asian Literature laureate, 1913), and Malala Yousafzai (youngest Peace laureate, 2014).

The Booker Prize, founded in 1969, originally rewarded the best novel written in English and published in the United Kingdom or Ireland. In 2014 the eligibility was expanded to any English‑language novel published in the UK or Ireland, regardless of the author’s nationality. The prize is administered by the Booker Prize Foundation; a panel of judges — typically authors, critics, and academics — reads submissions and announces a longlist, shortlist, and winner. The International Booker Prize (since 2005) honours translated fiction, with the £50,000 prize split equally between author and translator. Notable winners include Salman Rushdie (Midnight’s Children, 1981), Arundhati Roy (The God of Small Things, 1997), and Margaret Atwood (The Testaments, 2019, joint winner).

The Academy Awards (Oscars), presented by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences since 1929, recognise cinematic achievements across 23 competitive categories. The Best Picture award is voted on by all active Academy members using a preferential ballot. Historic milestones: “Parasite” (2020) became the first non‑English language film to win Best Picture; Kathryn Bigelow (2010) was the first woman to win Best Director; and “The Artist” (2012) was the first silent film to win since 1929. The Dadasaheb Phalke Award, India’s highest cinema honour, was instituted in 1969 and first awarded to Devika Rani; it is conferred by the Government of India’s Directorate of Film Festivals.

The Padma awards — Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri — are announced annually on Republic Day (26 January) by the President of India on the recommendation of the Padma Awards Committee, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary. They recognise distinguished service in any field including arts, literature, science, public affairs, and sports. The Bharat Ratna, India’s highest civilian award, is conferred by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister; it is not limited to Indian citizens (e.g., Nelson Mandela, 1990). The first Bharat Ratna recipients in 1954 were C. V. Raman, C. Rajagopalachari, and Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan. Knowing the conferring authority, the year of institution, and the first recipient for each award enables rapid elimination of distractors in matching questions.

Books That Shaped Public Policy and International Law

Certain publications have directly influenced legislation, diplomatic frameworks, or judicial reasoning. Adam Smith’s “The Wealth of Nations” (1776) laid the intellectual foundation for classical liberal economic policy and is cited in trade‑agreement negotiations. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels’ “The Communist Manifesto” (1848) shaped socialist movements and later the constitutional preambles of several 20th‑century states. John Maynard Keynes’ “The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money” (1936) provided the theoretical basis for counter‑cyclical fiscal policy adopted by Western governments after the Great Depression and again after the 2008 financial crisis.

Rachel Carson’s “Silent Spring” (1962) catalysed the modern environmental movement, leading to the United States’ ban on DDT (1972) and the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency (1970). The book’s impact is reflected in the Stockholm Declaration (1972) and the Rio Earth Summit (1992). In international law, Hersch Lauterpacht’s “International Law and Human Rights” (1950) influenced the drafting of the European Convention on Human Rights (1950) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966). The “Universal Declaration of Human Rights” (1948), while a UN General Assembly resolution rather than a book, is often studied alongside these texts because its preamble echoes the language of natural‑rights philosophy found in earlier works.

In the Indian context, B. R. Ambedkar’s “Annihilation of Caste” (1936, published 1941) informed the constitutional provisions on abolition of untouchability (Article 17) and affirmative action (Articles 15(4), 16(4)). Jawaharlal Nehru’s “The Discovery of India” (1946) articulated a civilisational narrative that underpinned the Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV). More recently, Amartya Sen’s “Development as Freedom” (1999) has been referenced in policy documents on human‑development indices and the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (2005). Examiners may ask for the publication year, the author, and the specific policy or legal instrument influenced. A concise revision table linking each work to its primary policy impact — e.g., “Silent Spring → US EPA creation, DDT ban” — covers the majority of such questions.

UN Observances: Resolution Numbers and Implementation Mechanisms

United Nations international days are established by General Assembly resolutions that specify the date, the thematic focus, and often the lead agency responsible for coordination. World Environment Day (5 June) originates from Resolution 2994 (XXVII) of 1972, which also created the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP). Each year a host country is designated; the 2023 host was Côte d’Ivoire with the theme “Solutions to Plastic Pollution”. World Health Day (7 April) commemorates the entry into force of the WHO Constitution in 1948; Resolution WHA 2.2 (1949) formalised the observance, and the 2024 theme is “My Health, My Right”. International Day of Yoga (21 June) was proclaimed by Resolution 69/131 (2014) following India’s initiative; the UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs coordinates global events.

Human Rights Day (10 December) marks the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights by Resolution 217 A (III) in 1948. The Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) leads the annual campaign. International Women’s Day (8 March) was recognised by the UN in 1977 through Resolution 32/142, building on earlier labour‑movement observances; UN Women now coordinates the theme. World Water Day (22 March) stems from Resolution 47/193 (1992) and is coordinated by UN‑Water, with the 2023 theme “Accelerating Change”. World Population Day (11 July) was established by Resolution 45/216 (1990) and is managed by the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA).

Implementation mechanisms vary: some days involve a global campaign with a designated theme, others require member states to submit national reports (e.g., World Water Day encourages voluntary national reporting on SDG 6). The UN Department of Global Communications issues press kits, social‑media assets, and a calendar of events. Knowing the resolution number, the lead UN entity, and whether a rotating host country is involved allows candidates to answer both factual (“Which resolution established World Environment Day?”) and analytical (“Which UN body coordinates the International Day of Yoga?”) questions. A compact flash‑card set pairing each day with its resolution number, lead agency, and host‑country practice is an efficient memorisation tool.

  • Treating the Booker Prize as open to American authors before the 2014 rule change.
  • Overlooking that the CFA franc is issued by two separate central banks (BCEAO and BEAC) rather than a single supranational bank.
  • Memorising only the date of an international day without its UN resolution number or lead agency.
  • Believing the Dadasaheb Phalke Award is a literary honour rather than India’s highest cinema award.
