Foundations of Ratio and Proportion

1.1 Ratio Fundamentals

A ratio expresses the relative magnitude of two quantities measured in the same unit, written as a:b or a/b, and it remains unchanged if both terms are multiplied or divided by the same non‑zero number. When a ratio is reduced to its simplest form the two terms share no common factor other than one, which makes comparison across problems straightforward. The concept of antecedent and consequent is essential: the first term is the antecedent, the second the consequent, and the order cannot be reversed without altering the meaning of the relationship.

In practical examinations the ratio often appears in compound form, where two or more simple ratios are linked through a common term. To combine A:B = 2:3 and B:C = 5:7 the value of B must be made identical in both ratios; the least common multiple of 3 and 5 is 15, giving A:B = 10:15 and B:C = 15:21, so the required A:C becomes 10:21. This technique of equalising the middle term is a recurring pattern in partnership profit sharing and mixture problems alike.

1.2 Proportion and Its Properties

A proportion states that two ratios are equal, written a:b = c:d or a/b = c/d, and it implies the product of the extremes equals the product of the means (a·d = b·c). This cross‑multiplication property is the algebraic engine behind most ratio‑based equations and allows the transformation of a proportion into a linear equation in a single unknown. When four quantities are in proportion, the first and fourth are called extremes, the second and third means, and the relationship remains valid if each term is increased or decreased by the same amount, provided the operation respects the original units.

Two important derived properties are componendo and dividendo. If a/b = c/d then (a+b)/(a‑b) = (c+d)/(c‑d), provided the denominators are non‑zero. This rule converts a proportion into a new proportion that often isolates the unknown more directly, especially when the unknown appears in both numerator and denominator of a complex fraction. The invertendo property (b/a = d/c) and alternendo property (a/c = b/d) are also available, giving the solver a toolkit of equivalent forms to choose the most convenient algebraic path.

1.3 Direct and Inverse Variation

When two quantities vary directly, their ratio remains constant; if x varies directly as y, then x = k·y for some constant k, and the graph of x against y is a straight line through the origin. In inverse variation the product of the two quantities is constant, so x·y = k and the graph is a rectangular hyperbola. Many partnership problems involve direct variation of profit with capital and time, while mixture replacement problems often exhibit inverse variation between the quantity removed and the concentration that remains. Recognising which variation applies allows the student to set up the correct equation without resorting to trial and error.

Mixture and Alligation

2.1 The Alligation Principle

Alligation is a rule for finding the ratio in which two ingredients at different prices or concentrations must be mixed to obtain a mixture of a given mean price or concentration. The rule states that the quantity of the cheaper ingredient to the quantity of the dearer ingredient equals the difference between the dearer price and the mean price to the difference between the mean price and the cheaper price. This relationship is derived directly from the weighted average formula and is valid only when the mean lies between the two extremes; if the required mean falls outside the range, the problem has no physical solution.

The alligation diagram, often drawn as a cross, places the two extreme values at the left and right, the mean in the centre, and the differences along the arms. The visual layout makes it immediate to read off the required ratio without writing algebraic equations. Because the same arithmetic works for percentages, speeds, and any weighted average situation, the alligation cross is a versatile tool that extends far beyond simple price mixing.

2.2 Application to Concentration Problems

When the ingredients are solutions of different strengths, the price is replaced by concentration expressed as a percentage or a fraction. For example, mixing a 20 % acid solution with a 50 % acid solution to obtain a 30 % solution follows the identical alligation steps: (50‑30) : (30‑20) = 20 : 10 = 2 : 1. The ratio tells the relative volumes of the two solutions required. If the total volume of the final mixture is specified, the individual volumes are obtained by dividing the total in the same ratio.

It is important to note that alligation assumes the volumes are additive, which holds for ideal solutions but may fail for real liquids exhibiting volume contraction. Examination questions, however, always treat volumes as additive unless explicitly stated otherwise, so the student can safely apply the rule without correction factors.

2.3 Mixing More Than Two Ingredients

When three or more ingredients are involved, the problem is usually reduced to a series of binary alligations. One common approach is to first mix two ingredients to obtain an intermediate mean, then alligate that intermediate with the third ingredient. The order of pairing can affect the intermediate values but the final ratio of all components remains unique. An alternative method uses the concept of weighted average directly: if the final mean is M and the component values are x₁, x₂, …, xₙ with unknown weights w₁, w₂, …, wₙ, then Σ wᵢ xᵢ = M Σ wᵢ. Solving the resulting linear system yields the required proportions. In examinations the binary step‑wise method is preferred for its speed and lower algebraic burden.

Example 1 (Alligation with Three Ingredients)
A grocer mixes three varieties of tea costing Rs 120/kg, Rs 150/kg and Rs 180/kg in the ratio 2:3:5. Find the average price per kilogram of the mixture.
Solution: The weighted average price = (2×120 + 3×150 + 5×180) ÷ (2+3+5) = (240 + 450 + 900) ÷ 10 = 1590 ÷ 10 = Rs 159/kg.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a mixture question gives the final ratio of components, compute the weighted average directly; when it gives the final average and asks for the ratio, draw the alligation cross. The two approaches are inverses of each other and recognising which data are supplied saves precious seconds.

Successive Replacement and Dilution

3.1 The Replacement Formula

When a fixed volume of a homogeneous mixture is removed and replaced by an equal volume of another substance, the fraction of the original component that remains after one operation equals 1 minus the ratio of the replaced volume to the total volume. If the operation is repeated k times, the remaining fraction becomes (1 – r/V)ᵏ, where r is the volume removed each time and V is the constant total volume. This exponential decay model is the mathematical expression of successive dilution and appears frequently in problems involving milk‑water replacement, chemical concentration, and even population genetics analogies.

The derivation is straightforward: after the first removal the amount of original substance is V – r; after replacement the total volume returns to V, so the concentration becomes (V – r)/V = 1 – r/V. Each subsequent operation multiplies the concentration by the same factor because the mixture is always homogeneous before removal. Hence after k operations the concentration is (1 – r/V)ᵏ, and the absolute quantity of the original substance is V·(1 – r/V)ᵏ.

3.2 Variable Volume Replacement

In some variations the total volume changes because the replacement volume differs from the removed volume, or because the vessel is not refilled to the original level. The general principle remains: track the absolute amount of the component of interest after each step. If x₀ is the initial amount, and at step i a volume rᵢ is removed from a total Vᵢ, the amount left is xᵢ₋₁·(1 – rᵢ/Vᵢ). Then the added substance changes the total volume to Vᵢ₊₁ = Vᵢ – rᵢ + aᵢ, where aᵢ is the volume added. Iterating this recurrence yields the final amount. Although the algebra looks heavier, the step‑wise tracking prevents the common mistake of applying the constant‑volume formula when it is not valid.

3.3 Mixed Replacement with Two Components

When the replacement substance is itself a mixture, the problem becomes a system of two simultaneous recurrences, one for each component. For instance, if a vessel contains milk and water and each operation removes some mixture and adds a different milk‑water blend, the milk amount and water amount must be updated together. The total volume may stay constant, but the concentration of each component follows its own recurrence. Solving such problems is most reliable by writing the recurrence for the component asked about, substituting the known added concentration, and iterating. The alligation cross can sometimes shortcut the first step by giving the effective concentration of the added blend, but the iterative method guarantees correctness for any number of steps.

Partnership and Profit Sharing

4.1 Capital‑Time Contribution

In a partnership the profit (or loss) is distributed in proportion to each partner’s capital multiplied by the time for which that capital remains invested. If partner A invests C₁ for T₁ months and partner B invests C₂ for T₂ months, the profit sharing ratio is C₁T₁ : C₂T₂. This rule follows directly from the definition of simple interest on capital, treating profit as interest earned on the capital‑time product. When a partner joins or leaves mid‑year, the time factor is adjusted to the actual number of months the capital was at risk, and the ratio is computed with those effective months.

It is crucial to distinguish between capital contributed at the start and additional capital introduced later. Each tranche of capital generates its own capital‑time product, and the sum of all such products for a partner forms that partner’s total claim. The examination often tests this by giving a series of investments and withdrawals, requiring the candidate to construct a timeline and compute the weighted sum accurately.

4.2 Working and Sleeping Partners

A working partner receives a salary or commission for managing the business before the remaining profit is divided according to capital‑time ratios. The salary is treated as an expense of the firm and is deducted from the gross profit; the net profit is then shared. If the working partner also has a capital share, that share participates in the net profit division exactly like a sleeping partner’s share. The order of deduction matters: first subtract all agreed remunerations, then apply the capital‑time ratio to the remainder. Misplacing the salary after the division is a frequent error that changes the final amounts substantially.

4.3 Goodwill and Admission of a New Partner

When a new partner is admitted, the existing partners may sacrifice a portion of their profit share in favour of the newcomer. The sacrifice ratio is usually the old profit sharing ratio unless a different agreement exists. Goodwill, representing the value of the firm’s reputation, is often brought in by the new partner and distributed to the old partners in their sacrifice ratio. If goodwill is not brought in cash, it is adjusted through the capital accounts by crediting the old partners and debiting the new partner. Understanding the flow of goodwill entries is essential for questions that ask for the new capital balances after admission.

Coin and Value Problems

5.1 Denomination‑Based Equations

Coin problems present a total value and a total number of coins, often with a relationship between the counts of different denominations. Let the number of coins of denominations d₁, d₂, … be x₁, x₂, … . The two fundamental equations are Σ xᵢ = total number of coins and Σ dᵢ xᵢ = total value. When a third condition such as “the number of 50‑paise coins is twice the number of 1‑rupee coins” is given, it provides a linear relation among the variables, turning the system into a solvable set of simultaneous equations. The standard technique is to express all variables in terms of one unknown using the ratio conditions, substitute into the value equation, and solve.

Because Indian currency denominations are fixed (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 paise and 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 2000 rupees), the coefficients in the value equation are known constants. This makes the arithmetic predictable and allows the student to anticipate whether the solution will be integral; a non‑integral result signals an error in setting up the equations or in the arithmetic.

5.2 Ratio of Coins to Value

Sometimes the problem gives the ratio of the number of coins of each denomination and the total value, asking for the number of each coin. If the ratio of counts is a:b:c for denominations p, q, r, then the total value contributed by each denomination is proportional to a·p : b·q : c·r. The share of the total value belonging to the first denomination is (a·p) / (a·p + b·q + c·r) times the total value. Dividing that share by the denomination p yields the number of coins of that type. This method bypasses simultaneous equations entirely and is extremely fast when the ratio of counts is given directly.

5.3 Mixed Coin‑Note Problems

Modern examinations occasionally combine coins and currency notes in a single problem, treating notes as higher denominations with the same algebraic structure. The only difference is that notes are usually counted in whole numbers while coins may be counted in large quantities, but mathematically they are identical variables. The same two‑equation framework applies, and the ratio‑of‑value shortcut works equally well. Care must be taken to keep units consistent: if the total value is given in rupees and some denominations are in paise, convert everything to a single unit (preferably paise) before forming equations to avoid decimal errors.

Mastery of these five topic groups equips the candidate to handle every standard question on ratio, proportion, mixture, partnership, and coin problems that appears in the quantitative aptitude section.

Compound Ratio and Continued Proportion

A compound ratio is formed by multiplying the corresponding terms of two or more simple ratios. If the ratios are a:b, c:d and e:f, the compound ratio is (a×c×e) : (b×d×f). This operation is associative and commutative, so the order of multiplication does not affect the result. In examinations, compound ratios appear when a problem states that the ratio of A to B is 2:3, the ratio of B to C is 4:5 and the ratio of C to D is 6:7; the overall ratio A:B:C:D is obtained by aligning the common terms and multiplying across. The alignment step requires making the middle terms equal by taking the least common multiple of the overlapping quantities.

Continued proportion extends the idea of proportion to three or more quantities. Four numbers a, b, c, d are in continued proportion when a:b = b:c = c:d. This implies b² = a×c, c² = b×d and a×d = b×c. The middle terms are called mean proportionals; the first and last are extremes. If only three numbers a, b, c are given with a:b = b:c, then b is the mean proportional between a and c, and b = √(a×c). The third proportional to a and b is the number c such that a:b = b:c, giving c = b²/a. These relationships are frequently tested in questions that ask for a missing term when the product of extremes equals the product of means.

A practical shortcut for continued proportion problems is to express all terms as powers of a common base. For instance, if a:b = b:c = c:d = k, then b = a·k, c = a·k², d = a·k³. The ratio a:b:c:d becomes 1:k:k²:k³. This geometric progression representation simplifies algebraic manipulation, especially when the sum or product of the terms is given. The examiner often hides the constant ratio k inside a word problem about successive discounts, population growth or compound interest, requiring the candidate to recognise the underlying continued proportion.

When compound ratios and continued proportions appear together, the problem typically provides a chain of ratios and asks for a specific term after a series of operations such as addition, subtraction or replacement. The systematic approach is: (1) write each given ratio in fractional form, (2) equalise the common terms using LCM, (3) multiply across to obtain the compound ratio, (4) if a continued proportion is indicated, verify the mean proportional condition, (5) apply any additional arithmetic operations on the resulting terms. Errors usually arise from skipping the equalisation step or from treating the compound ratio as a simple sum of the individual ratios.

Advanced applications include mixture problems where three ingredients are combined in a compound ratio and then a portion is replaced by another mixture. The replacement step creates a new continued proportion among the remaining quantities. Solving such problems demands writing the initial compound ratio, applying the replacement formula (final amount = initial amount × (1 – fraction replaced)ⁿ) to each component separately, and then re‑expressing the final quantities as a compound ratio. Mastery of this layered technique separates high scorers from average candidates.

Alligation with Three or More Components and Weighted Average

The classical alligation rule handles two ingredients by placing their costs on the left, the mean price in the centre and the differences on the right. For three or more components the same principle extends through the concept of weighted average. If components with costs C₁, C₂, C₃ are mixed in quantities Q₁, Q₂, Q₃ to obtain a mean cost M, the equation Σ(Qᵢ·Cᵢ) = M·ΣQᵢ holds. Rearranging gives ΣQᵢ·(Cᵢ – M) = 0. The terms (Cᵢ – M) are the deviations of each component cost from the mean. Components cheaper than M have negative deviations, dearer ones positive. The ratio of quantities is inversely proportional to the absolute deviations, but only after grouping the deviations so that their algebraic sum is zero.

A practical method for three components is the alligation alternate. Write the three costs in ascending order. Pair the lowest with the highest, compute their differences with the mean, and treat the middle cost as a pivot. For example, costs 30, 40, 50 and mean 42. Deviations: 30→–12, 40→–2, 50→+8. To balance, the total negative deviation (–12 –2 = –14) must be offset by the positive deviation (+8). This is impossible with only these three numbers, indicating that the given mean cannot be achieved with all three components present in positive quantities. The examiner often uses this impossibility as a trap; the correct response is to state that no such mixture exists unless one component quantity is zero.

When the mean lies between the lowest and highest costs, a feasible mixture exists. The standard algorithm: (1) list costs in ascending order, (2) compute deviations from the mean, (3) separate negative and positive deviations, (4) find the least common multiple of the absolute deviations, (5) assign provisional quantities as LCM/|deviation| for each component, (6) adjust the provisional quantities by a common factor to satisfy any additional constraints such as total quantity or a given ratio between two components. This method reduces the problem to integer arithmetic and avoids solving simultaneous equations.

Weighted average interpretation provides an alternative viewpoint. The mean cost M is the weighted average of the component costs with weights equal to the quantities. If the problem gives the ratio of quantities directly, the mean can be computed instantly: M = (Σ ratioᵢ·Cᵢ) / Σ ratioᵢ. Conversely, if the mean and all but one quantity ratio are known, the missing ratio is obtained by solving the weighted average equation for the unknown weight. This approach is especially fast when the number of components exceeds three, because the alligation diagram becomes cumbersome.

Examiners also test the reverse scenario: given the final mixture cost and the ratio of two components, find the cost of the third component. This is a linear equation in the unknown cost. The candidate must set up the weighted average, substitute the known ratios, and solve. A common mistake is to treat the unknown cost as a deviation and apply the alligation diagram incorrectly. The safe route is always to fall back on the weighted average formula, which works for any number of components and any combination of known and unknown variables.

Technique: Alligation Alternate for Three Components
A grocer mixes three varieties of rice costing ₹30, ₹40 and ₹50 per kg. He wants the mixture to cost ₹42 per kg. In what ratio must he mix the three varieties?
Step 1: Write costs in ascending order: 30, 40, 50. Mean = 42.
Step 2: Compute deviations: 30–42 = –12, 40–42 = –2, 50–42 = +8.
Step 3: Sum of negative deviations = –14. Sum of positive deviations = +8. Since –14 ≠ +8, the deviations cannot balance with all three components present in positive quantities.
Step 4: Check feasibility: The mean 42 lies between 30 and 50, so a mixture of only the cheapest and dearest can achieve it. Using two‑component alligation: (50–42):(42–30) = 8:12 = 2:3. Thus rice at ₹30 and ₹50 must be mixed 2:3. The ₹40 rice cannot be part of a mixture costing ₹42 unless its quantity is zero.
Answer: The three‑component mixture is impossible; the required ratio for a feasible two‑component mixture is 2:3 (₹30:₹50).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a three‑component alligation question appears, first compute the deviations from the mean. If the algebraic sum of deviations cannot be zero with all components positive, immediately conclude that the mixture cannot contain all three varieties. This saves time and avoids forced algebraic solutions that yield negative quantities.

Partnership with Changing Capital and Time‑Weighted Profit Sharing

In a dynamic partnership, partners may inject additional capital, withdraw part of their investment, or change the period of involvement. The profit‑sharing ratio is based on the product of capital and the time for which that capital remains employed, commonly called capital‑time units. If partner A invests ₹1,00,000 for 6 months, then adds ₹50,000 for the next 4 months, and finally withdraws ₹30,000 for the last 2 months, the capital‑time contribution is calculated piecewise: (1,00,000×6) + (1,50,000×4) + (1,20,000×2) = 6,00,000 + 6,00,000 + 2,40,000 = 14,40,000 capital‑month units. Each partner’s share of profit = (individual capital‑time units / total capital‑time units) × net profit.

A frequent variation introduces a working partner who receives a fixed monthly salary before profit division. The salary is an expense deducted from gross profit; the remainder is distributed according to capital‑time ratios. If the working partner also contributes capital, that capital participates in the net profit division exactly like a sleeping partner’s capital. The order of operations is critical: (1) compute gross profit, (2) subtract all agreed salaries and commissions, (3) calculate capital‑time units for every partner including the working partner, (4) allocate net profit proportionally. Reversing steps (2) and (3) inflates the working partner’s share and is a classic examiner trap.

When a new partner is admitted mid‑year, the old partners’ capital‑time units are computed for the full year, while the new partner’s units are computed only for the remaining months. Goodwill brought in by the new partner is credited to the old partners in their sacrifice ratio, which is usually the old profit‑sharing ratio unless a different agreement exists. If goodwill is not paid in cash, the adjustment is made through capital accounts: debit the new partner’s capital account and credit the old partners’ capital accounts in the sacrifice ratio. Subsequent profit sharing uses the new capital balances as the basis for capital‑time calculations.

Problems may also involve a partner retiring before the year‑end. The retiring partner’s capital‑time units are calculated up to the date of retirement. The remaining partners continue with their existing capitals, possibly re‑valuing assets and liabilities. The retiring partner’s share of profit up to retirement is settled either in cash or by transferring to a loan account. The new profit‑sharing ratio among continuing partners is often given explicitly; if not, it defaults to their capital‑time ratio after retirement.

Advanced questions combine multiple events: additional investments, withdrawals, salary, admission, and retirement within the same accounting period. The systematic solution is to draw a timeline for each partner, mark every capital change with the date, compute the capital‑time product for each interval, sum them, and then apply the profit‑distribution hierarchy (salary → goodwill adjustment → net profit). Using a table with columns Partner, Period, Capital, Months, Capital‑Time eliminates omission errors and makes the arithmetic transparent.

Coin Problems with Constraints and Diophantine Solutions

Coin problems that provide only the total value and total number of coins lead to a linear Diophantine equation of the form a·x + b·y = V with x + y = N, where a and b are denominations in paise, V is total value in paise, and N is total coin count. Eliminating one variable yields (a–b)·x = V – b·N. Since a, b, V, N are known integers, x must be an integer. The equation has a solution only if (V – b·N) is divisible by (a–b). This divisibility condition is a quick check: if it fails, the problem data is inconsistent or the candidate has mis‑read the denominations.

When three or more denominations appear, the system becomes a set of linear equations with more unknowns than equations. Additional constraints such as “the number of 50‑paise coins is twice the number of 1‑rupee coins” supply the missing equations. The standard technique is to express all variables in terms of a single parameter using the ratio constraints, substitute into the value equation, and solve for the parameter. Because Indian denominations are fixed, the coefficients are small integers, making the arithmetic manageable.

A powerful shortcut for ratio‑of‑coins problems: if the ratio of counts is p:q:r for denominations d₁, d₂, d₃, the value contributed by each denomination is proportional to p·d₁ : q·d₂ : r·d₃. The total value V is split in that ratio. The number of coins of the first denomination = (p·d₁ / Σ) × V / d₁ = p·V / Σ, where Σ = p·d₁ + q·d₂ + r·d₃. This eliminates simultaneous equations entirely and works even when the total number of coins is not given.

Mixed coin‑note problems treat notes as higher denominations (e.g., ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500, ₹2000). The same algebraic structure applies. The only caution is unit consistency: convert everything to paise or to rupees before forming equations. A common error is to mix rupees and paise, producing decimal coefficients that obscure the integer nature of the solution. Converting to paise guarantees integer arithmetic and makes the divisibility test straightforward.

Some examinations ask for the minimum or maximum number of coins of a particular denomination under given constraints. This transforms the problem into an integer optimisation: maximise or minimise x subject to a·x + b·y = V, x + y = N, x, y ≥ 0 integers. Because the feasible region is a line segment in the integer lattice, the extreme values occur at the endpoints where one variable is as large as possible while the other remains non‑negative. The candidate can find the endpoints by setting y = 0 (or x = 0) and checking integrality, then stepping by the denominator of the reduced fraction (a–b)/gcd(a,b) to locate the nearest integer solution.

Alligation with Variable Concentrations and Non‑Linear Mixing

Standard alligation assumes each ingredient has a fixed concentration and that the final mixture is a linear combination of those concentrations. In many advanced problems the concentration of an ingredient changes during the process — for example, a solution that evaporates water while the solute remains, or a chemical reaction that alters the proportion of active component. The key is to treat the process as a sequence of states, each described by a mass‑balance equation. Write the amount of solute and the total mass after every step; the concentration at any stage is the ratio of solute mass to total mass. When two such variable‑concentration streams are mixed, apply the alligation rule to the instantaneous concentrations at the moment of mixing, not to the initial labels. If the mixing itself is non‑linear — say the volume contracts by a known percentage — introduce a contraction factor k (0 < k ≤ 1) so that final volume = k × (sum of input volumes). The alligation diagram then uses effective concentrations = (solute mass) / (effective volume). A systematic table with columns Stage, Solute Mass, Total Mass, Concentration eliminates algebraic errors and makes the stepwise logic transparent.

When three or more streams with variable concentrations are combined, the pairwise alligation method fails because the deviation balance no longer closes. Instead, set up a system of linear equations: let x₁, x₂, …, xₙ be the volumes taken from each stream at the mixing instant. The total solute = Σ cᵢ xᵢ where cᵢ is the instantaneous concentration of stream i. The required final concentration C gives Σ cᵢ xᵢ = C Σ xᵢ. Together with any additional constraints (e.g., total volume fixed, ratio of two streams given) the system solves uniquely. Because concentrations are rational numbers, the solution will be rational; multiply by the LCM of denominators to obtain integer volumes, which is the form examiners expect.

A frequent twist is to embed a replacement cycle inside a variable‑concentration stream. For instance, a 40 % acid solution loses 10 % of its volume by evaporation each hour, after which a fixed volume of water is added. The concentration after t hours follows a recurrence: cₜ₊₁ = cₜ × (1 − 0.10) / (1 − 0.10 + w/Vₜ) where w is water added and Vₜ the volume before addition. Solving the recurrence yields a closed form that can be plugged into the alligation equation for the final mixture. Recognising the geometric‑progression pattern saves time compared with hour‑by‑hour simulation.

Partnership with Interest on Capital and Drawings

In a basic partnership the profit share is proportional to capital‑time units. Advanced problems introduce interest on capital and interest on drawings, both of which alter the effective capital base before profit distribution. Interest on capital is usually calculated on the opening capital for the full period, or on a weighted average if additional capital is introduced mid‑year. The interest amount is credited to the partner’s capital account and then participates in the profit‑sharing ratio. Interest on drawings is charged on the amount withdrawn, typically for the average period the money was out of the firm (often six months if dates are not given). This interest is debited to the partner’s capital account and reduces the profit available for distribution. The correct sequence is: (1) compute interest on capital for each partner, (2) compute interest on drawings, (3) adjust each partner’s capital by adding interest on capital and subtracting interest on drawings, (4) calculate net profit after these adjustments, (5) distribute net profit in the agreed profit‑sharing ratio, (6) finally add the distributed profit to the adjusted capitals.

When capitals change during the year, interest on capital must be computed for each sub‑period separately. For example, Partner A starts with ₹5,00,000, adds ₹1,00,000 on 1 July, and withdraws ₹50,000 on 1 October. Interest at 12 % per annum on capital is: (5,00,000 × 12/100 × 12/12) + (1,00,000 × 12/100 × 6/12) − (50,000 × 12/100 × 3/12). The withdrawal reduces the capital base for the remaining months, so interest on the withdrawn portion is not earned. Interest on drawings is calculated on the amount drawn for the period it remains outside the business. If the drawing date is unknown, the convention is to assume an average period of six months, i.e., interest = drawing × rate × 6/12.

Goodwill adjustments interact with interest calculations. When a new partner is admitted, goodwill may be raised in the books and then written off in the old profit‑sharing ratio. The goodwill amount increases each old partner’s capital before interest on capital is computed for the period after admission. Conversely, if goodwill is not brought in cash but adjusted through capital accounts, the adjusted capitals become the base for subsequent interest calculations. A timeline table with columns Partner, Period, Capital at Start, Capital Changes, Interest on Capital, Drawings, Interest on Drawings, Adjusted Capital, Profit Share, Closing Capital ensures no step is omitted. Examiners often test the order of operations: forgetting to deduct interest on drawings before profit distribution is a classic trap that inflates the final capital balances.

Technique
A and B are partners sharing profits 3:2. Capitals on 1 Jan: A ₹4,00,000, B ₹3,00,000. A introduces ₹1,00,000 on 1 Apr. B draws ₹50,000 on 1 Oct. Interest on capital @10 % p.a., interest on drawings @12 % p.a. Net profit before interest ₹1,20,000. Compute final capitals.
Step 1: Interest on capital. A: (4,00,000×10%×12/12)+(1,00,000×10%×9/12)=40,000+7,500=47,500. B: 3,00,000×10%×12/12=30,000. Step 2: Interest on drawings (average 6 months). B: 50,000×12%×6/12=3,000. Step 3: Adjusted capitals before profit. A: 4,00,000+1,00,000+47,500=5,47,500. B: 3,00,000−50,000+30,000−3,000=2,77,000. Step 4: Net profit after interest = 1,20,000−(47,500+30,000)+3,000 = 45,500. Step 5: Profit share 3:2 → A 27,300, B 18,200. Step 6: Final capitals: A 5,47,500+27,300=5,74,800. B 2,77,000+18,200=2,95,200.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Draw a vertical timeline for each partner; mark every capital infusion, withdrawal, and interest event with dates. Compute interest on capital and drawings in separate columns before touching the profit figure. This visual separation prevents the common error of mixing interest on drawings into the profit pool. Always verify that the sum of final capitals equals the sum of opening capitals plus net profit plus interest on capital minus interest on drawings.

Coin Problems with Multiple Constraints and Generating Functions

When a coin problem supplies more than two denominations and several independent constraints — total value, total number of coins, ratio of two denominations, and an upper bound on a third — the system becomes a set of linear Diophantine equations with more unknowns than equations. The standard algebraic elimination works but can be cumbersome. A generating‑function approach turns the counting problem into a coefficient extraction: the generating function for a denomination d with unlimited coins is 1 + xd + x2d + … = 1/(1 − xd). If the number of coins of that denomination is bounded by m, the factor becomes (1 − xd(m+1))/(1 − xd). Multiplying the factors for all denominations yields a polynomial (or rational function) whose coefficient of xV (where V is total value in paise) gives the number of ways to achieve that value. When a ratio constraint such as “number of 50‑paise coins is twice the number of 1‑rupee coins” is present, substitute the relation into the generating function, reducing the number of variables. The resulting single‑variable series can be expanded manually for small denominations because Indian coin values (50, 100, 200, 500 paise) keep the exponents manageable.

For optimisation questions — minimum or maximum coins of a particular denomination — the generating function can be combined with a linear‑programming insight: the feasible region in the integer lattice is a convex polytope defined by the linear constraints. The objective (e.g., maximise x₁) attains its extreme at a vertex where as many constraints as possible are tight. Practically, set the non‑target variables to their bounds (zero or the maximum allowed by the ratio) and solve the remaining two‑variable Diophantine equation. If the solution is not integral, step by the modulus (denomination difference divided by gcd) until integrality is restored. This stepping method is faster than enumerating all combinations and is guaranteed to find the optimum because the objective is linear.

A frequent examiner twist mixes notes and coins, e.g., ₹10, ₹20, ₹50 notes together with 50‑paise and ₹1 coins. Convert everything to paise (10 → 1000, 20 → 2000, 50 → 5000, 1 → 100, 0.5 → 50). The generating‑function factors become 1/(1 − x1000) etc. Because the exponents are large, direct expansion is impractical; instead, use the ratio‑of‑value shortcut: if the ratio of counts is p:q:r for denominations d₁,d₂,d₃, the value contributed is proportional to p·d₁ : q·d₂ : r·d₃. The total value V splits in that ratio, giving the number of pieces of each type directly as (p·V)/(p·d₁+q·d₂+r·d₃) divided by d₁, etc. This eliminates the need for generating functions when a clean ratio is given, but the generating‑function framework remains the rigorous backup when ratios are absent or only partial.

Ratio in Geometry: Similar Figures and Scale Factors

Ratio concepts extend naturally to geometry through similarity. Two figures are similar if corresponding angles are equal and corresponding lengths are in a constant ratio called the scale factor k. All linear dimensions (perimeter, altitude, median) scale by k; areas scale by k²; volumes scale by k³. In competitive problems, a common scenario gives the ratio of perimeters of two similar triangles and asks for the ratio of their areas, or vice versa. The direct rule: (Perimeter₁/Perimeter₂) = k, (Area₁/Area₂) = k². If the ratio of areas is given as 9:25, the scale factor is √(9/25) = 3/5, and the ratio of perimeters is also 3:5. When three similar solids are involved, the compound ratio of volumes is the product of individual scale factors cubed. For example, a model car built at 1:18 scale has volume 1/18³ of the real car; if a second model is 1:24, the ratio of their volumes is (1/18³) : (1/24³) = 24³ : 18³ = (4/3)³ = 64:27.

Problems often embed similarity in coordinate geometry or mensuration. A triangle with vertices (0,0), (a,0), (0,b) is similar to another with vertices (0,0), (ka,0), (0,kb). The ratio of their inradii equals k, the ratio of circumradii equals k, and the ratio of areas equals k². When a line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides in the ratio m:n, the smaller triangle formed is similar to the original with scale factor m/(m+n). The area of the smaller triangle is (m/(m+n))² times the original area. This property is frequently used in questions that ask for the area of a trapezium formed by cutting off the top of a triangle.

Advanced twists combine similarity with alligation. For instance, two similar cylindrical vessels of different heights are filled with liquids of different concentrations. When the liquids are poured into a third vessel, the final concentration can be found by treating the volumes as weights in an alligation diagram, where the volumes themselves are in the ratio of the cubes of the scale factors. Recognising that the volume ratio is the cube of the linear scale factor avoids the mistake of using the linear ratio directly. Similarly, when a solid is melted and recast into a similar solid of a different size, the mass (proportional to volume) scales with the cube of the linear dimension, and any impurity concentration remains unchanged because it is a mass‑ratio property.

Mixture Problems Involving Evaporation and Condensation

Evaporation and condensation change the total mass of a mixture without altering the mass of the non‑volatile component. In a salt‑water solution, only water evaporates; the salt mass stays constant. If a solution of concentration c (mass of solute / total mass) loses a fraction f of its water, the new concentration becomes c / (1 − f (1 − c)). Derivation: initial solute = cM, initial water = (1 − c)M. After evaporation, water = (1 − f)(1 − c)M, total mass = cM + (1 − f)(1 − c)M. Dividing solute by total mass yields the formula. This expression is useful when the problem states “10 % of the water evaporates” rather than “10 % of the solution evaporates”. The latter would simply multiply total mass by 0.9, leaving concentration unchanged.

Condensation adds water (or another volatile component) to the mixture, diluting it. If a mass w of pure water is added to a solution of mass M and concentration c, the new concentration is cM / (M + w). When evaporation and condensation occur in cycles — e.g., a solution is heated, 20 % of water evaporates, then the vapour is condensed and returned — the net effect is zero change in composition, but the intermediate concentrations may be asked. Track each step with a mass‑balance table: columns Step, Solute Mass, Water Mass, Total Mass, Concentration. This table also handles simultaneous evaporation of two volatile components with different rates, a scenario that appears in advanced chemistry‑based quantitative questions.

When multiple solutions undergo evaporation before being mixed, apply the evaporation formula to each separately, then use alligation on the resulting concentrations. For example, Solution A (30 % salt) loses 15 % of its water; Solution B (50 % salt) loses 10 % of its water. Compute post‑evaporation concentrations: A → 30 / (1 − 0.15 × 0.70) ≈ 34.9 %; B → 50 / (1 − 0.10 × 0.50) ≈ 52.6 %. Then mix in given volume ratio using the standard alligation rule. A common trap is to apply the evaporation percentage to the total mass instead of the water mass, which yields a lower concentration and leads to an incorrect alligation ratio. Always isolate the volatile component mass before applying the loss fraction.

  • Applying the two‑component alligation diagram to three variable‑concentration streams without solving the linear system.
  • Computing interest on capital after profit distribution instead of before, inflating final capitals.
  • Using the linear scale factor directly for volume ratios in similarity problems, ignoring the cubic relationship.
  • Treating evaporation percentage as a loss of total mixture mass rather than loss of the volatile component only.
  • Mixing rupee and paise units in coin generating‑function equations, producing non‑integer exponents.
  • Forgetting to adjust the capital base for drawings before calculating interest on capital.
  • Assuming the ratio of perimeters equals the ratio of areas in similar figures.

Alligation in Cost‑Price‑Profit Scenarios

Alligation is not limited to concentration problems; it becomes a powerful shortcut when the goal is to obtain a target selling price or profit percentage by mixing two or more goods purchased at different cost prices. The essential step is to convert every quoted price — whether cost price, marked price, or selling price after discount — into a common basis, usually the effective cost price per unit. Once all values are expressed as cost per unit, the standard alligation diagram applies: the mean cost price is placed in the centre, the individual cost prices at the ends, and the differences give the required mixing ratio. For instance, if a trader buys tea at 120 per kg and 150 per kg and wishes to sell the mixture at 165 per kg earning a 10 % profit, the mean cost price that yields this selling price is 165 ÷ 1.10 = 150 per kg. The alligation then reads 120 — 150 — 150, giving differences 30 and 0, which indicates that only the cheaper tea should be used; any inclusion of the dearer tea would raise the cost above the target. When a discount is offered on the marked price, the effective selling price becomes marked price × (1 − discount %). The same conversion to cost price is performed before drawing the alligation. A frequent twist introduces a third ingredient with a known proportion; the problem then reduces to a two‑component alligation after fixing the third component’s share. The algebraic check is straightforward: let the quantities be x, y, z; the weighted average cost equals (120x + 150y + 180z) ÷ (x + y + z). Equating this to the required mean cost yields a linear relation that can be solved together with the given proportion. Mastery of this conversion eliminates the need for simultaneous equations in most examination questions.

Partnership with Retirement, Death and Settlement of Accounts

When a partner retires or dies, the continuing partners must settle the outgoing partner’s capital account, which includes the share of accumulated profits, reserves, goodwill, and any revaluation surplus or deficit. The first step is to determine the new profit‑sharing ratio among the remaining partners; this is usually given or derived from the old ratio by removing the outgoing partner’s share. The gaining ratio — the difference between the new and old ratios for each continuing partner — decides how the outgoing partner’s share of goodwill is compensated. If goodwill is not recorded in the books, the continuing partners debit their capital accounts in the gaining ratio and credit the retiring partner’s account. Revaluation of assets and liabilities is carried out before settlement; any profit or loss on revaluation is distributed in the old ratio. The retiring partner’s capital after these adjustments is then paid either in cash or by transferring a loan account. In case of death, the deceased partner’s legal heir receives the settled amount, and the partnership may be dissolved or reconstituted. A critical nuance is the treatment of joint life policy proceeds: the sum received is credited to all partners in the old ratio, but the outgoing partner’s share is immediately transferred to his capital account. The final settlement account shows the opening capital, share of profit up to the date of retirement, share of goodwill, revaluation profit, drawings, interest on drawings, and the closing balance payable. All entries are dated as of the retirement or death date, ensuring that the time‑weighted capital contribution is respected.

Technique
A, B and C share profits in the ratio 5:3:2. C retires on 30 June. The goodwill of the firm is valued at 60 000. The new profit‑sharing ratio between A and B is 3:2. Pass the journal entry for goodwill adjustment assuming goodwill is not recorded in the books.
Old ratio = 5:3:2. New ratio = 3:2 = 0.6:0.4. Gaining ratio = New − Old for each continuing partner. A’s gain = 0.6 − 5/10 = 0.6 − 0.5 = 0.1. B’s gain = 0.4 − 3/10 = 0.4 − 0.3 = 0.1. Gaining ratio = 1:1. C’s share of goodwill = 2/10 × 60 000 = 12 000. This amount is debited to A and B in gaining ratio 1:1 → 6 000 each. Journal entry: A’s Capital A/c Dr 6 000, B’s Capital A/c Dr 6 000, To C’s Capital A/c 12 000 (Goodwill adjusted in gaining ratio).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a retirement or death question appears, first write down the old and new ratios side by side. Compute the gaining ratio immediately; it is the key to goodwill, revaluation profit, and any reserve distribution. If the problem states “goodwill is raised and written off”, remember that raising credits all partners in the old ratio, while writing off debits the continuing partners in the new ratio. Keep a small table with columns Partner, Old Share, New Share, Gain/Sacrifice — this prevents sign errors under time pressure.

Coin Problems Involving Exchange Rates and Mixed Currency Systems

Modern coin‑value questions often embed foreign‑currency conversion, requiring the aspirant to work with two or more monetary systems simultaneously. The core principle remains the same: the total value expressed in a common currency equals the sum of (number of coins × denomination × exchange rate). Suppose a bag contains 5‑rupee coins, 2‑rupee coins, and 1‑dollar coins. The exchange rate is 1 dollar = 80 rupees. If the total value is 1 200 rupees and the ratio of 5‑rupee to 2‑rupee coins is 3:4, while the number of dollar coins equals the number of 5‑rupee coins, the problem reduces to a single‑variable linear equation. Let the number of 5‑rupee coins be 3x; then 2‑rupee coins = 4x, dollar coins = 3x. Value in rupees = 5×3x + 2×4x + 80×3x = 15x + 8x + 240x = 263x. Equating 263x = 1 200 gives x = 1200 ÷ 263 ≈ 4.56, which is not an integer — signalling that either the total value or the ratio must be adjusted to yield integer solutions. In examination practice, the numbers are chosen so that x is an integer; the aspirant must verify divisibility before finalising the answer. A more advanced variant introduces a commission or transaction fee on currency conversion, effectively reducing the exchange rate for one direction only. The fee is applied after conversion, so the effective rate becomes rate × (1 − fee %). The same alligation logic can be used when two different exchange rates apply to buying and selling the foreign currency, treating the rates as “concentrations” and the desired effective rate as the mean. The mixing ratio then tells how many units to convert at each rate to achieve a target average cost.

Ratio and Proportion in Time‑Work‑Efficiency Chains

Time‑work problems become ratio exercises when efficiencies of workers or machines are expressed as fractions of a standard unit. If worker A completes a task in 12 days and worker B in 18 days, their efficiencies are in the ratio 1/12 : 1/18 = 3:2. When they work together, the combined efficiency is the sum of the individual efficiencies, and the time taken is the reciprocal of that sum. Chains appear when a third worker C joins after a certain period, or when a worker’s efficiency changes mid‑task (e.g., due to fatigue). The standard approach is to convert the whole schedule into “man‑days” or “efficiency‑days”. For a project requiring 360 efficiency‑units, A works alone for 4 days contributing 4 × 3 = 12 units, then A and B work together for 6 days contributing 6 × (3+2) = 30 units, then all three work together where C’s efficiency is 4 units per day. The remaining units are 360 − 12 − 30 = 318, and the combined daily efficiency becomes 3+2+4 = 9, so the final phase lasts 318 ÷ 9 = 35.33 days. When the problem asks for the fraction of work done by each worker, compute each worker’s total contribution in efficiency‑units and divide by the total. A common trap is to average the times instead of the efficiencies; the harmonic mean of times gives the correct combined time only when the work quantum is identical for each worker. Another twist introduces “alternate day” working patterns: A works on day 1, B on day 2, etc. The two‑day cycle efficiency is the sum of the two efficiencies, and the number of cycles needed is total work ÷ cycle efficiency. Any remainder day is handled by the worker whose turn it is. This cyclic method avoids day‑by‑day simulation and is essential for large numbers.

Mixture Problems with Sequential Chemical Reactions and Yield Losses

In industrial chemistry‑style mixture questions, two or more reactants are mixed in a given molar ratio, but the reaction does not go to completion; a yield percentage is specified. The aspirant must first determine the limiting reagent using the stoichiometric ratio, then apply the yield to the theoretical product amount. For example, 100 kg of A (molar mass 50) reacts with 80 kg of B (molar mass 40) in a 1:1 molar ratio to form C (molar mass 90) with a 75 % yield. Moles of A = 100 000 ÷ 50 = 2 000; moles of B = 80 000 ÷ 40 = 2 000. Neither is limiting; theoretical moles of C = 2 000. Actual moles = 2 000 × 0.75 = 1 500. Mass of C = 1 500 × 90 = 135 000 g = 135 kg. When the reactants are not in stoichiometric proportion, the limiting reagent dictates the theoretical product. The unreacted excess reagent remains in the mixture and may be recovered or discarded. A further layer adds a second reaction where the product C reacts with D to form E, again with a yield. The mass‑balance table becomes indispensable: columns for each species, rows for each stage (initial, after reaction 1, after reaction 2). The alligation concept reappears when two streams of different conversions are blended to meet a final specification; the “concentration” is now the mass fraction of the desired product, and the mixing ratio is obtained by the usual alligation on these fractions. Care must be taken that yield losses are applied to the reacting portion only, not to the entire stream mass.

Common Traps

  • Using selling price directly in alligation without converting to cost price, leading to an incorrect mixing ratio.
  • Confusing gaining ratio with sacrificing ratio when a partner retires, causing wrong goodwill allocation.
  • Forgetting to convert foreign‑currency denominations to a common base before forming equations, producing non‑integer coin counts.
  • Averaging times instead of efficiencies in combined work problems, which overestimates the time required.
  • Applying yield percentage to the total mass of reactants rather than to the theoretical product of the limiting reagent.
  • Neglecting to adjust the exchange rate for transaction fees when the problem specifies a commission on conversion.
  • Treating the unreacted excess reagent as waste without accounting for its mass in the final mixture balance.

These advanced sections equip aspirants to handle the most intricate ratio, mixture and partnership questions that appear in competitive examinations.

Ratio and Proportion in Pipes, Cisterns and Variable Flow Rates

Pipe‑cistern problems are a direct application of the work‑efficiency ratio where the “work” is the volume of a tank and the “efficiency” is the flow rate of each pipe. When all pipes have constant rates, the combined rate is the algebraic sum of individual rates, with inlet pipes taken as positive and outlet pipes as negative. The time to fill or empty the tank is then the tank capacity divided by the net rate. The ratio framework becomes essential when rates change during the process — for example, a pipe is opened after a certain interval, or a pipe’s diameter is altered, changing its rate proportionally to the square of the diameter.

Consider a tank of capacity 1200 litres. Pipe A fills at 30 litres per minute, pipe B fills at 20 litres per minute, and pipe C empties at 15 litres per minute. If A and B are opened together for 10 minutes, then C is also opened, the net rate after 10 minutes is 30 + 20 − 15 = 35 litres per minute. The volume filled in the first phase is (30 + 20) × 10 = 500 litres. The remaining 700 litres require 700 ÷ 35 = 20 minutes. Total time = 30 minutes. The same result is obtained by converting everything into “pipe‑minutes” — a ratio of contribution: A contributes 30 × 30 = 900 pipe‑minutes, B contributes 20 × 30 = 600, C contributes −15 × 20 = −300; the sum equals the tank capacity expressed in pipe‑minutes (1200). This ratio method avoids piecewise time calculations when many pipes switch on or off.

Variable flow rates appear when a pipe’s cross‑section changes. Since flow rate is proportional to the cross‑sectional area (assuming constant pressure), a diameter reduction to half reduces the rate to one‑quarter. If pipe A’s diameter is halved after 5 minutes, its rate drops from 30 to 7.5 litres per minute. The problem then splits into two intervals with different rate sets. The ratio of times spent at each rate can be found by equating the total volume contributed by each pipe to the tank capacity. A systematic approach: list each interval, write the net rate for that interval, multiply by the unknown duration, and solve the resulting linear equation. When more than two intervals exist, the same principle extends to a system of linear equations, which can be solved by substitution or matrix methods.

A frequent twist introduces a leak whose rate is proportional to the water height (i.e., the leak rate = k × height). Because height is proportional to volume for a uniform cross‑section tank, the leak rate becomes k′ × volume. This yields a differential equation dV/dt = net inlet rate − k′V, whose solution is an exponential approach to a steady state. In competitive examinations, such problems are simplified by assuming the leak rate is constant over a short interval, allowing the use of average rates. The aspirant must recognise when the constant‑rate approximation is valid (usually when the interval is small compared with the tank’s time constant) and when the exact exponential solution is required (often signalled by the phrase “rate proportional to the amount of water”).

Another advanced variant mixes pipes with different fluids (e.g., acid and water) where the concentration of the mixture matters. The alligation principle then applies to the concentrations, while the pipe‑rate ratio governs the volumes contributed. The combined problem is solved by first determining the volume ratio from the pipe rates and times, then applying alligation on the concentrations to find the final mixture strength. This dual‑layer reasoning — ratio for volume, alligation for concentration — is a hallmark of high‑difficulty questions.

Alligation in Alloy Blending and Percentage Composition Problems

Alloy blending questions treat the percentage of a constituent metal as the “concentration” in the alligation sense. When two alloys of known compositions are mixed to obtain a target composition, the mixing ratio is obtained by the standard alligation diagram: write the percentages of the desired metal in the two alloys at the ends, the target percentage in the middle, and take the differences diagonally. The ratio of the quantities of the two alloys equals the difference between the target and the opposite alloy’s percentage. This works because the mass of the desired metal is conserved and the total mass is the sum of the alloy masses.

For three or more alloys, the problem reduces to a weighted‑average system. If alloys A, B, C contain 30 %, 50 %, 70 % copper respectively, and the final alloy must contain 55 % copper, the aspirant can first mix A and C to get an intermediate 55 % alloy (since 55 lies between 30 and 70). The alligation ratio for A:C is (70‑55):(55‑30) = 15:25 = 3:5. This intermediate alloy can then be mixed with B (which already has 50 % copper) to reach 55 %. However, B’s percentage is lower than the target, so the second alligation would require a negative quantity of B, indicating that B cannot be part of a mixture that raises the copper content from 50 % to 55 % unless another alloy with >55 % copper is present. The correct approach is to set up a system of linear equations: let x, y, z be the masses of A, B, C. Then 0.30x + 0.50y + 0.70z = 0.55(x + y + z) and x + y + z = total mass. With two equations and three unknowns, one degree of freedom remains; the examiner usually fixes one quantity (e.g., “alloy B is 100 kg”) to obtain a unique solution.

Percentage composition problems often involve impurities or multiple constituents. For instance, an ore contains 60 % iron, 10 % silica, and the rest gangue. After beneficiation, the concentrate contains 80 % iron and 5 % silica. The mass balance for each element gives two independent equations, allowing the calculation of the mass of concentrate and tailings per tonne of ore. The alligation method can be applied separately to each element, but the ratios must be consistent across elements. A useful shortcut: treat the problem as a two‑component mixture where the “components” are the concentrate and the tailings, and the “concentration” is the iron percentage. The tailings iron percentage is found from the overall mass balance (often given or deduced). Then a single alligation yields the concentrate‑to‑tailings ratio.

When the blending involves cost optimisation, the target composition becomes a constraint and the objective is to minimise cost. This transforms the problem into a linear programming exercise, but in the examination context it is usually reduced to a corner‑point evaluation: the optimal blend uses at most two alloys (by the fundamental theorem of linear programming). The aspirant therefore tests all pairs of alloys that can achieve the target composition (i.e., the target lies between their percentages) and selects the pair with the lowest cost per unit mass of the final alloy. The alligation ratio for each feasible pair gives the exact proportions, and the cost is computed as the weighted average of the two alloy costs.

A common trap is to apply alligation on the wrong constituent (e.g., using silica percentage when the target is specified for iron). Because the sum of percentages is 100 %, the alligation ratio for one constituent is the inverse of the ratio for the complementary constituent only when there are exactly two components. With three or more components, the ratios differ, and the aspirant must work with the constituent explicitly mentioned in the target. Another trap is ignoring the mass loss during processing (e.g., slag formation) which changes the total mass and invalidates a simple mass‑balance alligation unless the loss is accounted for as a separate stream.

Worked Example: Alloy Blending with Cost Optimisation
Alloy X contains 40 % copper and costs ₹120 per kg. Alloy Y contains 70 % copper and costs ₹180 per kg. Alloy Z contains 90 % copper and costs ₹250 per kg. A manufacturer needs 1000 kg of an alloy with exactly 65 % copper at minimum cost. Determine the optimal blend and its total cost.
Step 1: Identify feasible pairs. The target 65 % lies between 40 % and 70 % (X and Y) and between 40 % and 90 % (X and Z). It does not lie between 70 % and 90 % (both above target). Step 2: For pair X‑Y, alligation ratio = (70‑65):(65‑40) = 5:25 = 1:5. So 1 part X to 5 parts Y. For 1000 kg, X = 1000 × 1/6 ≈ 166.67 kg, Y = 833.33 kg. Cost = 166.67 × 120 + 833.33 × 180 = 20 000 + 150 000 = ₹170 000. Step 3: For pair X‑Z, ratio = (90‑65):(65‑40) = 25:25 = 1:1. So 500 kg each. Cost = 500 × 120 + 500 × 250 = 60 000 + 125 000 = ₹185 000. Step 4: Compare costs. The X‑Y blend costs ₹170 000, which is lower. Therefore the optimal blend uses only alloys X and Y in the ratio 1:5, giving a total cost of ₹170 000 for 1000 kg.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a blending question asks for minimum or maximum cost, first list all pairs of ingredients whose concentrations bracket the target. Apply the alligation ratio to each feasible pair, compute the cost per unit of the blend, and pick the cheapest. Do not attempt a full linear‑programming tableau; the two‑ingredient corner‑point property guarantees the optimum lies on a pair. If the problem fixes the quantity of one ingredient, treat it as a constraint and solve the remaining two‑variable alligation directly.

Partnership Profit Sharing with Management Remuneration and Interest on Drawings

In a partnership, the profit‑sharing ratio is usually based on capital‑time contributions, but the partnership deed may provide for additional remuneration to working partners (salary, commission) and interest on capital or drawings. These items are appropriations of profit, not expenses, and therefore they are deducted from the gross profit before the residual profit is distributed in the agreed ratio. The order of appropriation matters: typically, interest on capital is allowed first, then partner’s salary/commission, then interest on drawings is charged, and finally the remaining profit is shared. If the deed is silent, the default rule (under the Partnership Act) is that no interest on capital or drawings is allowed, and no salary is payable unless the partner is a working partner and the deed provides for it.

Consider a firm with partners A and B. Capitals: A ₹5 00 000, B ₹3 00 000. Profit before appropriations: ₹2 00 000. Deed provides: interest on capital @10 % p.a., salary to A ₹24 000 p.a., commission to B @5 % of net profit after salary but before commission, interest on drawings @12 % p.a. (A drew ₹60 000, B drew ₹40 000). The calculation proceeds: Interest on capital: A ₹50 000, B ₹30 000. Salary to A: ₹24 000. Profit after these = 2 00 000 − 50 000 − 30 000 − 24 000 = ₹96 000. Commission to B = 5 % of 96 000 = ₹4 800. Profit after commission = 96 000 − 4 800 = ₹91 200. Interest on drawings: A ₹7 200, B ₹4 800 (added back to profit). Final divisible profit = 91 200 + 7 200 + 4 800 = ₹1 03 200. This is shared in the profit‑sharing ratio (say 3:2). A gets 3/5 × 1 03 200 = ₹61 920, B gets ₹41 280. Each partner’s total share = appropriation items + profit share. The aspirant must keep a clear ledger format to avoid double‑counting or omitting any appropriation.

When a new partner is admitted, goodwill valuation often uses the super‑profit method: super profit = average maintainable profit − normal profit (normal profit = capital employed × normal rate of return). Goodwill = super profit × number of years’ purchase. The new partner’s share of goodwill is credited to the old partners in their sacrificing ratio (old ratio − new ratio). If the new partner brings goodwill in cash, it is distributed to old partners; if not, the goodwill account is raised and written off in the new profit‑sharing ratio. The interplay of goodwill, capital adjustment, and profit‑sharing ratio changes requires a systematic approach: (1) compute sacrificing/gaining ratio, (2) adjust capitals to reflect new profit‑sharing ratio (often by bringing in or withdrawing cash), (3) record goodwill, (4) revalue assets/liabilities if required, (5) prepare the new balance sheet.

Interest on drawings is calculated on the amount drawn and the period for which it was used. If drawings are made at regular intervals (e.g., beginning of each month), the average period is 6.5 months; if at the end of each month, 5.5 months; if in the middle, 6 months. For irregular drawings, the product method (amount × months outstanding) is used, and interest = total product × rate ÷ 12. The interest on drawings is a gain to the firm and is added to the divisible profit. A common error is to subtract it instead of adding.

When a partner retires or dies, the continuing partners may agree to settle the outgoing partner’s account by paying a lump sum that includes share of goodwill, revaluation profit/loss, and the capital balance. The gaining ratio (new ratio − old ratio) determines how the outgoing partner’s share of goodwill is borne by the remaining partners. If the firm’s goodwill is not recorded in the books, the retiring partner’s share is debited to the continuing partners’ capital accounts in the gaining ratio. The settlement may be immediate or in instalments with interest; the interest on the loan account is an appropriation of profit and must be accounted for before profit distribution in subsequent years.

Coin Problems with Weight‑Value Constraints and Counterfeit Detection

Advanced coin problems introduce two independent attributes for each coin: denomination (face value) and weight. Genuine coins of a given denomination have a standard weight; counterfeit coins deviate by a known amount (lighter or heavier). The problem typically provides the total number of coins, the total face value, and the total weight, and asks for the number of counterfeit coins of each denomination. This yields a system of three linear equations in three unknowns (numbers of genuine and counterfeit coins for each denomination). Because the number of coins must be non‑negative integers, the solution must satisfy Diophantine constraints.

For example, a bag contains ₹1, ₹2, and ₹5 coins. Genuine weights: 5 g, 7 g, 9 g respectively. Counterfeit ₹1 coins weigh 4.5 g, counterfeit ₹2 weigh 6.5 g, counterfeit ₹5 weigh 8.5 g. Total coins = 200, total value = ₹500, total weight = 1300 g. Let x₁, x₂, x₅ be numbers of genuine ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 coins; y₁, y₂, y₅ be counterfeit counts. Equations: (1) x₁+x₂+x₅+y₁+y₂+y₅ = 200. (2) 1·x₁+2·x₂+5·x₅+1·y₁+2·y₂+5·y₅ = 500. (3) 5x₁+7x₂+9x₅+4.5y₁+6.5y₂+8.5y₅ = 1300. Subtracting (1) from (2) gives x₂+4x₅+y₂+4y₅ = 300. Subtracting 5×(1) from (3) gives 2x₂+4x₅+1.5y₂+3.5y₅ = 300. These two equations in four unknowns (x₂,x₅,y₂,y₅) can be reduced by expressing x₁ and y₁ from (1) and (2). The integer‑solution search is simplified by noting that the weight differences (0.5 g per counterfeit coin) create a linear relation between the total number of counterfeit coins and the weight shortfall. The shortfall from all‑genuine weight = (5·200) − 1300 = 1000 g? Wait, all‑genuine weight would be 5x₁+7x₂+9x₅, but we don’t know composition. A more robust method: treat the problem as a mixture of two “types” — genuine and counterfeit — for each denomination, and apply alligation on the average weight per coin of each denomination. However, because the denominations have different values, the value constraint couples the counts across denominations. The standard technique is to eliminate the genuine counts using the value equation, leaving a system in the counterfeit counts only, then solve the resulting Diophantine equations by modular arithmetic.

When the problem states that a certain number of coins are known to be genuine (e.g., “there are at least 50 genuine ₹5 coins”), this provides inequality constraints that narrow the solution space. The aspirant should first find the general parametric solution of the linear system, then apply the non‑negativity and inequality constraints to isolate the unique integer solution. In many examination questions, the numbers are chosen so that the solution is unique without exhaustive search.

Another variant involves multiple weighings on a balance scale (the classic “find the counterfeit coin among N coins in k weighings”). This is a combinatorial‑information‑theory problem: each weighing has three possible outcomes (left heavy, right heavy, balance), so k weighings can distinguish up to 3ᵏ possibilities. If there is one counterfeit coin that is known to be lighter, the maximum N = 3ᵏ. If the counterfeit may be heavier or lighter, the maximum N = (3ᵏ − 3)/2. The solution strategy is to partition the coins into three nearly equal groups at each step. While this is more a logical‑reasoning topic, it occasionally appears in quantitative sections under “coin problems”. The aspirant should recognise the information‑theoretic bound and the ternary‑search algorithm.

A frequent trap is to treat the weight equation as if all coins had the same denomination, ignoring the value constraint. This leads to an under‑determined system and multiple solutions. Another trap is to assume the counterfeit weight deviation is the same for all denominations; the problem statement usually specifies different deviations. Always set up separate variables for each denomination’s genuine and counterfeit counts, write the three conservation equations (count, value, weight), and solve systematically.

Ratio and Proportion in Data Interpretation: Proportional Reasoning in Tables and Charts

Data interpretation (DI) sets often require the aspirant to compute ratios, percentages, and growth rates from tables, bar charts, pie charts, or line graphs. The core skill is proportional reasoning: if a pie chart shows sector angles, the ratio of two sectors’ angles equals the ratio of the underlying quantities. For a bar chart, the ratio of bar heights (assuming a common baseline and linear scale) equals the ratio of the represented values. In stacked bar charts, the segment heights within a bar are proportional to the component values. The aspirant must be able to read off values, set up ratios, and solve for unknowns without necessarily calculating absolute numbers.

Consider a table showing the production of three factories (A, B, C) over four quarters. The total annual production is given as 1 20 000 units. The quarter‑wise percentages for each factory are provided. To find the ratio of A’s Q2 production to B’s Q3 production, compute A’s Q2 = total × (A’s annual share) × (Q2% of A). Since the total cancels in the ratio, the aspirant only needs the product of the two percentages for each factory. This “percentage of percentage” multiplication is a direct application of compound ratio: (A_annual% × A_Q2%) : (B_annual% × B_Q3%). The same principle applies when the data is presented as a line graph with indexed values (base year = 100). The ratio of two indices in a given year equals the ratio of the actual quantities.

Growth‑rate questions are essentially ratio problems. If a quantity grows from 200 to 260, the growth factor is 260/200 = 1.3, i.e., a 30 % increase. When two quantities grow at different rates, the ratio of their values after n periods is the initial ratio multiplied by the ratio of the growth factors raised to n. For example, Company X’s revenue grows at 10 % p.a., Company Y’s at 15 % p.a. If current revenues are in the ratio 4:3, after 3 years the ratio becomes 4 × 1.1³ : 3 × 1.15³. The aspirant can compute the factor ratio (1.1/1.15)³ and multiply by 4/3. Approximation techniques (binomial expansion for small rates) are useful when exact calculation is cumbersome.

In “caselet” DI, a paragraph describes a scenario with several inter‑related quantities (e.g., a school with boys and girls in different classes, participation rates in sports). The aspirant must translate the prose into a ratio table. A systematic method: identify the base population (total students), then break it down by the given ratios (boys:girls, class distribution, sport participation). Each ratio splits a quantity into parts; the parts can be expressed as fractions of the base. The final answer (e.g., “number of girls in class 10 who play basketball”) is obtained by multiplying the base by the product of the relevant fractions. This is exactly the compound‑ratio chain taught in the ratio fundamentals.

A common trap in DI is to confuse percentage points with percent change. If a share rises from 20 % to 25 %, the increase is 5 percentage points but the percent increase is (25‑20)/20 = 25 %. Another trap is to assume that the ratio of two percentages equals the ratio of the underlying quantities when the bases differ. For instance, “30 % of A’s employees are engineers” and “40 % of B’s employees are engineers” does not imply the engineer‑count ratio is 3:4 unless A and B have the same total employees. Always verify whether the percentages share a common base before forming ratios.

  • Applying alligation on the wrong constituent in multi‑component alloy problems, leading to an incorrect mixing ratio.
  • Forgetting to add back interest on drawings to divisible profit, thereby understating each partner’s final share.
  • Treating the weight equation in coin problems as independent of the value equation, producing non‑unique or non‑integer solutions.
  • Confusing percentage‑point change with percent change when computing growth ratios from charts.
  • Using the sacrificing ratio instead of the gaining ratio when allocating goodwill on a partner’s retirement.
  • Assuming a constant leak rate in pipe‑cistern problems where the leak is proportional to water height, resulting in an over‑estimated filling time.
  • Neglecting to convert all percentages to a common base before forming ratios in data‑interpretation caselets.
