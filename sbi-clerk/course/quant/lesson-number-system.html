What is Number System?

The number system is the foundation of arithmetic. It covers types of numbers — natural, whole, integers, rational, irrational, prime, composite, co-prime, and their properties. the exam frequently asks questions on divisibility rules, LCM & HCF, and unit digits.

Key Concepts

1. Divisibility Rules: A number is divisible by 2 if its last digit is even; by 3 if sum of digits is divisible by 3; by 4 if last two digits are divisible by 4; by 5 if last digit is 0 or 5; by 6 if divisible by both 2 and 3; by 9 if sum of digits is divisible by 9; by 11 if the difference between sum of digits at odd and even places is 0 or a multiple of 11.

2. LCM & HCF: LCM of two numbers is the smallest number divisible by both. HCF is the greatest number that divides both. For fractions, LCM = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators; HCF = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators.

3. Unit Digit Cyclicity: The unit digit of powers repeats in cycles of 4 for most digits. For example, 2^41 has the same unit digit as 2^1 = 2. Learn the cyclicity table: 2 → 2,4,8,6; 3 → 3,9,7,1; 4 → 4,6; 7 → 7,9,3,1; 8 → 8,4,2,6; 9 → 9,1.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Divisibility)
Which of the following numbers is divisible by 11?

a) 12345 b) 12122 c) 13321 d) 14443
Solution: c) 13321. Sum of digits at odd places = 1+3+1 = 5. Sum of digits at even places = 3+2 = 5. Difference = 0, hence divisible by 11.
Example 2 (Unit Digit)
Find the unit digit of 7^25.

a) 3 b) 5 c) 7 d) 9
Solution: c) 7. Cyclicity of 7 is 7,9,3,1 (cycle length 4). 25 / 4 = 6 remainder 1. So unit digit = 7^1 = 7.
Example 3 (LCM)
Find the LCM of 24, 36, and 48.

a) 124 b) 144 c) 164 d) 188
Solution: b) 144. Prime factors: 24=2^3*3, 36=2^2*3^2, 48=2^4*3. LCM = 2^4*3^2 = 16*9 = 144.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For unit digit questions, only the unit digit of the base matters. Ignore the rest.
  • For LCM of large numbers, divide all numbers by common prime factors and multiply the divisors and the final row.
  • For divisibility by 7: double the last digit, subtract from the rest. Repeat until you get a small number divisible by 7.
  • For HCF, use the difference trick: HCF(a,b) = HCF(a-b, b) for a > b.
Pro Tip: In the exam, at least 2-3 questions come from divisibility and unit digit. Memorise the cyclicity table for digits 2,3,4,7,8,9 and practice with negative remainders for faster calculations.

Chinese Remainder Theorem and Simultaneous Congruences

The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) provides a systematic way to solve a system of linear congruences with pairwise coprime moduli. Given congruences x ≡ a₁ (mod m₁), x ≡ a₂ (mod m₂), …, x ≡ aₖ (mod mₖ) where each mᵢ is coprime to every other, there exists a unique solution modulo M = m₁·m₂·…·mₖ. The constructive proof builds the solution as x = Σ aᵢ·Mᵢ·yᵢ where Mᵢ = M/mᵢ and yᵢ is the modular inverse of Mᵢ modulo mᵢ (i.e. Mᵢ·yᵢ ≡ 1 (mod mᵢ)). This inverse exists because gcd(Mᵢ, mᵢ) = 1. In examinations the moduli are often small (≤ 100) so the inverses can be found by inspection or the extended Euclidean algorithm. A common twist is to present moduli that are not coprime; in that case a solution exists only if the congruences are compatible on the shared prime factors, and the combined modulus becomes the least common multiple of the original moduli. Candidates should practice reducing such systems by eliminating redundant congruences before applying CRT.

Applications extend beyond pure remainder problems. For instance, finding the smallest positive integer that leaves remainders 2, 3, 4 when divided by 3, 4, 5 respectively is a direct CRT exercise. Another frequent pattern asks for the last three digits of a large exponentiation; rewriting the exponentiation modulo 1000 and splitting 1000 = 8·125 (coprime) lets CRT combine the results from modulo 8 and modulo 125, each of which is easier because of Euler’s theorem or simple cyclicity. Mastery of CRT therefore reduces many “last‑digit” or “last‑three‑digits” questions to a pair of smaller modular calculations.

Euler's Totient Function and Reduced Residue Systems

Euler’s totient function φ(n) counts the positive integers ≤ n that are coprime to n. For a prime power pᵏ, φ(pᵏ) = pᵏ − pᵏ⁻¹ = pᵏ⁻¹(p−1). Since φ is multiplicative for coprime arguments, φ(mn) = φ(m)φ(n) when gcd(m,n)=1. This property allows rapid computation of φ for any integer once its prime factorisation is known. The reduced residue system modulo n consists of exactly φ(n) integers that are pairwise incongruent and each coprime to n; any integer coprime to n is congruent to exactly one element of this system.

Euler’s theorem generalises Fermat’s little theorem: if gcd(a,n)=1 then a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). This is the engine behind many exponent‑reduction tricks. For example, to find the remainder of 71000 upon division by 20, note φ(20)=8, so 78 ≡ 1 (mod 20) and 71000 = (78)125 ≡ 1125 ≡ 1 (mod 20). When the base and modulus are not coprime, the theorem does not apply directly; one must factor out the common divisor or use the Carmichael function λ(n), which gives the smallest universal exponent for all units modulo n. In competitive papers the examiner often mixes coprime and non‑coprime cases in the same set, testing whether the candidate recognises the precondition gcd(a,n)=1.

A practical shortcut: for modulus 100, φ(100)=40, so any number ending in 1,3,7,9 raised to the 40th power ends in 01. This fact underpins many “last two digits” questions. Similarly, φ(1000)=400 helps with three‑digit endings. Candidates should memorise φ values for common moduli (10, 20, 25, 40, 50, 100, 125, 200, 250, 500, 1000) to avoid repeated factorisation during the test.

CRT with Non‑Coprime Moduli
Find the smallest positive integer x such that x ≡ 2 (mod 6) and x ≡ 5 (mod 9).
The moduli 6 and 9 share gcd 3. For a solution to exist the remainders must be congruent modulo 3: 2 ≡ 5 (mod 3) → 2 ≡ 2 (mod 3), condition satisfied. Reduce the system by dividing each congruence by the common factor where possible. Write x = 6k + 2. Substitute into the second: 6k + 2 ≡ 5 (mod 9) → 6k ≡ 3 (mod 9). Divide by 3: 2k ≡ 1 (mod 3). The inverse of 2 modulo 3 is 2, so k ≡ 2 (mod 3) → k = 3t + 2. Then x = 6(3t+2)+2 = 18t + 14. The smallest positive x is 14. Verify: 14 mod 6 = 2, 14 mod 9 = 5. Hence answer 14.
Exam‑Day Strategy for CRT and Totient Problems
When a question asks for a remainder modulo a composite number, first factor the modulus. If the factors are coprime, split the problem using CRT; solve each smaller modulus with cyclicity, Euler’s theorem, or direct computation, then recombine. If the factors share primes, check compatibility of the given remainders before attempting CRT. Keep a mental table of φ values for 10, 20, 25, 40, 50, 100, 125, 200, 250, 500, 1000 — this saves precious seconds on last‑digit and last‑three‑digit questions. Always verify the coprime condition before invoking Euler’s theorem; if it fails, factor out the gcd and treat the reduced base separately.

Advanced Divisibility Rules for 7, 13, 17, and 19

Standard divisibility tests cover 2,3,4,5,6,8,9,11. Higher primes require tailored algorithms that are still fast enough for timed tests. For 7, the “double‑last‑digit” rule: remove the last digit, double it, subtract from the truncated number; repeat until a recognisable multiple of 7 appears. Example: 203 → 20 − 2·3 = 14 → divisible. For 13, multiply the last digit by 4 and add to the rest (or multiply by 9 and subtract). For 17, multiply the last digit by 5 and subtract; for 19, multiply the last digit by 2 and add. These rules derive from finding a multiplier k such that 10k ≡ ±1 (mod p). For 7, 10·5 = 50 ≡ 1 (mod 7) gives the “add 5×last” variant; the subtraction variant uses 10·2 = 20 ≡ −1 (mod 7). Knowing both addition and subtraction forms lets the candidate choose the one that keeps numbers small.

Examiners often embed these rules in multi‑step problems: “Which of the following is divisible by 91?” Since 91 = 7·13, the candidate must test both 7 and 13. A faster route is to apply the combined rule for 91 directly: 10·9 = 90 ≡ −1 (mod 91), so subtract 9×last digit from the rest. However, memorising combined rules for every composite is impractical; the reliable method is to factor the divisor and apply the prime rules sequentially. Practice with numbers up to 10⁶ ensures the subtraction/addition steps remain mental arithmetic.

An exception arises when the number is very large (e.g., 50‑digit). In that case, modular arithmetic using the base‑10 representation is preferable: compute the remainder iteratively by processing digits left to right, updating remainder = (remainder·10 + digit) mod p. This method works uniformly for any divisor and avoids rule‑specific memorisation. The examiner may present a 30‑digit number and ask for divisibility by 17; the iterative modular method is the only feasible approach under time pressure.

LCM and HCF of Fractions, Decimals, and Algebraic Expressions

The definitions LCM = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators) and HCF = HCF(numerators) / LCM(denominators) hold for positive fractions in lowest terms. For decimals, convert to fractions by counting decimal places: 0.12 = 12/100 = 3/25 after reduction. Then apply the fraction formulas. A common trap is to apply the formulas directly to unreduced fractions, which yields incorrect results because the numerator/denominator pairs are not coprime. Always reduce each fraction first.

Algebraic expressions follow the same principle using factorisation. For polynomials, HCF is the product of common irreducible factors with the lowest exponent; LCM is the product of all irreducible factors with the highest exponent. Example: HCF of x³−x and x²−1 = x(x−1)(x+1) and (x−1)(x+1) is (x−1)(x+1). LCM is x(x−1)(x+1). When expressions contain numerical coefficients, treat the coefficients separately: HCF of 6x² and 9x³ is 3x²; LCM is 18x³. The examiner may mix numeric and algebraic parts, e.g., find LCM of 4/9, 5/12, and 7/18. Reduce each (already reduced), LCM of numerators 4,5,7 = 140; HCF of denominators 9,12,18 = 3; LCM = 140/3. Verify by checking that each original fraction divides 140/3 exactly.

Word problems often disguise these calculations: “Three bells toll at intervals of 0.6 s, 0.8 s, and 1.2 s. After how many seconds will they toll together?” Convert to fractions 3/5, 4/5, 6/5; LCM of numerators 3,4,6 = 12; HCF of denominators 5,5,5 = 5; LCM = 12/5 = 2.4 s. Recognising the decimal‑to‑fraction step is crucial; many candidates mistakenly compute LCM of the decimals directly.

Digit Sum Patterns, Digital Roots, and Casting Out Nines

The digital root of a positive integer is obtained by repeatedly summing its digits until a single digit remains; it equals the remainder when the number is divided by 9, with the convention that a remainder of 0 corresponds to digital root 9 (except for the number 0 itself). This property, known as casting out nines, provides a quick checksum for arithmetic operations. For addition, the digital root of the sum equals the digital root of the sum of the digital roots. For multiplication, the digital root of the product equals the digital root of the product of the digital roots. These rules hold because digit sum modulo 9 is a ring homomorphism from ℤ to ℤ/9ℤ.

In competitive questions, digital roots are used to eliminate options without full computation. Example: “Which of the following could be the square of an integer?” Compute digital roots of options; a perfect square modulo 9 can only be 0,1,4,7. Any option with digital root 2,3,5,6,8 is impossible. Similarly, for cubes the possible digital roots are 0,1,8. The examiner may ask for the remainder of a huge factorial sum modulo 9; since n! for n≥6 is a multiple of 9, only the first few terms matter.

A subtle point: casting out nines does not detect transposition errors where the digit sum remains unchanged (e.g., 123 vs 321). For stronger verification, casting out elevens (alternating sum of digits) can be combined. The alternating sum modulo 11 equals the remainder modulo 11. Using both mod 9 and mod 11 checks catches a wider class of errors. In exams, a question may present a long multiplication and ask which digit is wrong; applying both digital root and alternating sum narrows the error to a single position.

  • Assuming CRT applies without verifying pairwise coprime moduli.
  • Applying Euler’s theorem when base and modulus share a factor.
  • Using divisibility rules for 7, 13, 17, 19 on numbers that are too large for mental iteration instead of modular reduction.
  • Forgetting to reduce fractions before computing LCM or HCF of fractions.
  • Treating digital root 0 as 0 instead of 9 for non‑zero numbers.
  • Neglecting the compatibility condition for non‑coprime congruences in CRT problems.
  • Overlooking that casting out nines cannot detect all transcription errors, so a second check modulo 11 is often required.

Modular Exponentiation and Fast Power Algorithms

Modular exponentiation computes ab mod m without evaluating the full power, which quickly exceeds any calculator’s capacity. The binary exponentiation (also called exponentiation by squaring) reduces the number of multiplications from O(b) to O(log b). The algorithm maintains a result variable res = 1 and a base variable base = a mod m. While the exponent b > 0, if b is odd, set res = (res × base) mod m; then set base = (base × base) mod m and shift b right by one bit (b = b // 2). When the loop ends, res equals ab mod m. This method works for any modulus, prime or composite, and is the backbone of RSA encryption, Diffie‑Hellman key exchange, and primality tests such as Miller‑Rabin.

A common variant is the Montgomery reduction, which replaces the division by m with a multiplication by a pre‑computed constant, useful when the same modulus is used repeatedly. In competitive examinations, the binary method is sufficient; the examiner may ask for the last three digits of 712345 or the remainder of 21000 divided by 1009. Recognising that φ(1009) = 1008 (since 1009 is prime) allows a further reduction of the exponent modulo 1008 before applying binary exponentiation, cutting the loop length dramatically.

When the base and modulus are not coprime, Euler’s theorem cannot be applied directly. In such cases, factor the modulus, compute the exponent modulo each prime‑power component using the Carmichael function λ, and recombine with the Chinese Remainder Theorem. For example, to find 6200 mod 100, note 100 = 4 × 25. Compute 6200 mod 4 = 0 and 6200 mod 25 using λ(25)=20, giving exponent 200 mod 20 = 0, so the result is 1 mod 25. The simultaneous congruences x ≡ 0 (mod 4), x ≡ 1 (mod 25) yield x = 76. This layered approach is a frequent “advanced” twist in high‑difficulty papers.

Properties of Cyclic Numbers and Repeating Decimals

A cyclic number is an integer whose cyclic permutations are successive multiples of the number. The classic example is 142857, the repeating block of 1/7. Multiplying it by 1 through 6 yields 142857, 285714, 428571, 571428, 714285, 857142 — each a rotation of the original digits. Cyclic numbers arise from the full reptend primes: a prime p for which the decimal expansion of 1/p has period p‑1. The known full reptend primes below 100 are 7, 17, 19, 23, 29, 47, 59, 61, 97. For each such prime, the repetend length equals p‑1 and the repetend itself is a cyclic number.

The length of the repeating block of 1/n (with gcd(n,10)=1) equals the multiplicative order of 10 modulo n, denoted ordn(10). This order always divides φ(n). If n is prime, the period divides p‑1; it equals p‑1 precisely when 10 is a primitive root modulo p. Determining whether 10 is a primitive root can be done by checking that 10(p‑1)/q ≠ 1 (mod p) for every prime divisor q of p‑1. This test appears in questions asking “Find the smallest prime for which the decimal expansion of 1/p has period 6” — the answer is 7 because ord7(10)=6.

Repeating decimals can be converted to fractions by the standard algebraic trick: let x = 0.abc, multiply by 10k where k is the period, subtract, and solve. For mixed recurring decimals like 0.123, separate the non‑repeating part: x = 0.1 + 0.023. The non‑repeating part contributes a denominator of 10t (t = number of non‑repeating digits), while the repeating part contributes a denominator of 10t(10k‑1). Combining yields a single fraction that can be reduced. Examiners often embed this in word problems: “A rational number has decimal expansion 0.0142857. Express it in lowest terms.” Recognising the repetend as the cyclic number of 7 gives 1/70 directly.

Worked Example: Last Three Digits of a Large Power
Find the last three digits of 3500.

We need 3500 mod 1000. Since gcd(3,1000)=1, we can use Euler’s theorem: φ(1000)=1000×(1‑1/2)×(1‑1/5)=400. Thus 3400 ≡ 1 (mod 1000). Reduce the exponent: 500 mod 400 = 100. So we need 3100 mod 1000.

Apply binary exponentiation modulo 1000:

  • 31 = 3
  • 32 = 9
  • 34 = 81
  • 38 = 812 = 6561 ≡ 561
  • 316 = 5612 = 314721 ≡ 721
  • 332 = 7212 = 519841 ≡ 841
  • 364 = 8412 = 707281 ≡ 281

100 = 64 + 32 + 4. Multiply the corresponding residues: 281 × 841 × 81 mod 1000.

281 × 841 = 236321 ≡ 321. Then 321 × 81 = 26001 ≡ 1 (mod 1000).

Therefore the last three digits are 001.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the last k digits of ab, first check if a and 10k are coprime. If yes, reduce the exponent modulo φ(10k) = 4×10k‑1 before binary exponentiation. If not coprime, factor 10k into 2k·5k, solve each prime‑power modulus separately (using lifting exponent lemma for powers of 2 or 5), then combine with CRT. This two‑step reduction saves precious seconds.

Applications of Wilson’s Theorem and Factorial Modulo

Wilson’s theorem states that for a prime p, (p‑1)! ≡ ‑1 (mod p). The converse also holds: if (n‑1)! ≡ ‑1 (mod n) then n is prime. While the theorem is not a practical primality test for large numbers, it provides elegant shortcuts for factorial remainders. For example, to find 100! mod 101, Wilson gives 100! ≡ ‑1 ≡ 100 (mod 101). For composite moduli, the factorial often vanishes: if n has a prime factor ≤ n/2, then n divides (n‑1)!. Hence (n‑1)! ≡ 0 (mod n) for all composite n except 4 (since 3! = 6 ≡ 2 mod 4). This exception appears in “find the remainder of 99! divided by 100” — the answer is 0 because 100 = 2²·5² and both 2 and 5 appear multiple times in 99!.

When the modulus is a prime power pk, the factorial modulo can be evaluated using Legendre’s formula for the exponent of p in n! and the concept of p‑adic valuation. For instance, to compute 20! mod 25, first find the exponent of 5 in 20!: ⌊20/5⌋ + ⌊20/25⌋ = 4. Write 20! = 5⁴ × m where 5 ∤ m. Then 20! mod 25 = 0 because 5⁴ contains 5². In general, if the p‑adic valuation of n! is at least k, then n! ≡ 0 (mod pk). Otherwise, compute the product of the reduced residues modulo pk using the fact that the multiplicative group modulo pk is cyclic for odd p.

A frequent exam pattern asks for the remainder of a sum of factorials, e.g., Σi=110 i! mod 11. Since 11 is prime, Wilson gives 10! ≡ ‑1. For i ≥ 11, i! ≡ 0 (mod 11). Hence only terms up to 10! matter. Compute 1!+2!+…+9! modulo 11 directly (or use known values) and add 10! ≡ 10. The result is often a small integer that matches one of the options. Recognising the cutoff where factorial becomes a multiple of the modulus is the key insight.

Base Conversion and Representation in Non‑Decimal Systems

Converting between bases is a core skill that extends beyond the usual binary‑octal‑hexadecimal trio. The general algorithm for converting a positive integer N from base 10 to base b (b ≥ 2) repeatedly divides N by b, recording remainders as digits from least significant to most significant. For the fractional part, repeatedly multiply by b, taking the integer part as the next digit. This works for any integer base, including negative bases (e.g., base ‑2) and non‑integer bases (e.g., base φ, the golden ratio), though the latter are rarely examined.

In competitive papers, base conversion is often disguised: “A number written as 1234 in base 5 equals what in base 7?” The efficient route is to convert to decimal first: 1·5³ + 2·5² + 3·5 + 4 = 125 + 50 + 15 + 4 = 194. Then convert 194 to base 7: 194 ÷ 7 = 27 r 5; 27 ÷ 7 = 3 r 6; 3 ÷ 7 = 0 r 3 → digits 3,6,5 → 3657. When the source and target bases are both powers of the same prime (e.g., base 4 to base 16), grouping bits avoids decimal entirely: each base‑16 digit corresponds to two base‑4 digits.

Representation of fractions in other bases reveals whether a fraction terminates. A rational number a/b (in lowest terms) has a terminating expansion in base B iff every prime factor of b divides B. For example, 1/6 terminates in base 12 because 6 = 2·3 and both 2 and 3 divide 12, but it repeats in base 10 because 3 ∤ 10. Questions may ask: “In which of the following bases does 1/14 terminate?” Factor 14 = 2·7; the base must be a multiple of 7 and even. Among typical choices (8, 12, 14, 16), only base 14 and base 28 (if listed) satisfy. This rule also explains why binary floating‑point cannot exactly represent 0.1 (decimal) — 1/10 has factor 5, which does not divide 2.

Solving Linear Diophantine Equations Using Extended Euclidean Algorithm

A linear Diophantine equation in two variables has the form ax + by = c with integer coefficients. A solution exists iff gcd(a,b) divides c. The extended Euclidean algorithm not only computes g = gcd(a,b) but also finds integers x₀, y₀ such that ax₀ + by₀ = g. Multiplying this identity by c/g yields a particular solution (x₁, y₁) = (x₀·c/g, y₀·c/g). The general solution is then x = x₁ + (b/g)·t, y = y₁ – (a/g)·t for any integer t.

The extended algorithm proceeds by back‑substitution or by maintaining a table of quotients and remainders. For example, to solve 56x + 15y = 7: gcd(56,15)=1, so a solution exists. Euclidean steps: 56 = 3·15 + 11; 15 = 1·11 + 4; 11 = 2·4 + 3; 4 = 1·3 + 1; 3 = 3·1 + 0. Back‑substituting gives 1 = 4 – 1·3 = 4 – 1·(11 – 2·4) = 3·4 – 1·11 = 3·(15 – 1·11) – 1·11 = 3·15 – 4·11 = 3·15 – 4·(56 – 3·15) = 15·15 – 4·56. Thus x₀ = –4, y₀ = 15. Multiply by 7: x₁ = –28, y₁ = 105. General solution: x = –28 + 15t, y = 105 – 56t.

When the problem asks for non‑negative solutions or solutions within a range, impose inequalities on t. For instance, “Find the number of non‑negative integer solutions of 3x + 5y = 47.” gcd(3,5)=1 divides 47. A particular solution: 3·(–1) + 5·1 = 2 → multiply by 47/2 not integer, so find directly: 3·14 + 5·1 = 47 gives x₁=14, y₁=1. General: x = 14 + 5t, y = 1 – 3t. Non‑negative requires 14+5t ≥ 0 → t ≥ –2.8 → t ≥ –2; and 1–3t ≥ 0 → t ≤ 0.33 → t ≤ 0. Integer t = –2, –1, 0 → three solutions. This counting technique appears in “number of ways to make change” problems where coin denominations are coprime.

  • Assuming binary exponentiation works without reducing the exponent modulo φ(m) when base and modulus are coprime, leading to unnecessarily long loops.
  • Forgetting that Wilson’s theorem applies only to primes; applying it to composite moduli gives incorrect remainders.
  • Treating the period of 1/n as n‑1 for every prime n, ignoring that 10 must be a primitive root modulo n.
  • Converting fractions to other bases by converting numerator and denominator separately instead of using the termination criterion based on prime factors of the denominator.
  • Neglecting to divide the target constant c by gcd(a,b) before scaling the particular solution in Diophantine equations, producing non‑integer “solutions”.
  • Overlooking the sign of the parameter t when counting non‑negative solutions, resulting in off‑by‑one errors.
  • Using the standard Euclidean algorithm without the extended version, then attempting to guess the particular solution, which is unreliable under time pressure.

Lifting the Exponent Lemma and Prime Power Divisibility

The Lifting the Exponent Lemma (LTE) gives a precise formula for the exponent of a prime p in expressions of the form aⁿ − bⁿ or aⁿ + bⁿ. For an odd prime p dividing a − b with p∤ab, the lemma states vₚ(aⁿ − bⁿ) = vₚ(a − b) + vₚ(n). When p = 2 the condition changes: if a and b are odd and a ≡ b (mod 2) then v₂(aⁿ − bⁿ) = v₂(a − b) + v₂(a + b) + v₂(n) − 1 for even n, while for odd n the simpler v₂(aⁿ − bⁿ) = v₂(a − b) holds. The lemma also covers aⁿ + bⁿ when p divides a + b and n is odd, giving vₚ(aⁿ + bⁿ) = vₚ(a + b) + vₚ(n). These formulas turn seemingly huge exponentiations into elementary valuations.

Applying LTE requires checking the hypotheses carefully. The prime must divide the base difference (or sum) and must not divide the individual bases. If p divides both a and b the valuation is infinite and the lemma does not apply. For p = 2 the parity of a and b matters; the extra term v₂(a + b) appears only when a and b are odd. A common mistake is to use the odd‑prime formula for p = 2 without the correction, which over‑counts the power of two.

Typical competitive problems ask for the highest power of a prime dividing a large difference such as 3¹⁰⁰ − 1. Here a = 3, b = 1, p = 2. Since 3 − 1 = 2, v₂(3 − 1) = 1. Because n = 100 is even, v₂(3¹⁰⁰ − 1) = v₂(3 − 1) + v₂(3 + 1) + v₂(100) − 1 = 1 + 2 + 2 − 1 = 4. Thus 2⁴ = 16 divides the number but 2⁵ does not. The same technique solves “find the exponent of 5 in 7⁵⁰ − 2⁵⁰” by taking p = 5, a = 7, b = 2, noting 5 | 7 − 2.

LTE also simplifies problems involving sums. For example, to evaluate v₃(2⁹ + 1) we note 3 | 2 + 1 and n = 9 is odd, so v₃(2⁹ + 1) = v₃(2 + 1) + v₃(9) = 1 + 2 = 3. The lemma extends to multiple primes by applying it separately and combining the results. When a problem asks for the exact power of a composite modulus, factor the modulus and use LTE for each prime factor.

Edge cases appear when the base difference is a higher power of p. If vₚ(a − b) ≥ 2 the formula still holds, but one must ensure p∤ab. In some contest questions the bases are themselves powers, e.g. a = xᵏ, b = yᵏ. Then a − b may have extra factors; the lemma can be applied recursively. Mastery of LTE turns many “find the remainder when … is divided by pᵏ” problems into a few lines of valuation arithmetic.

Hensel's Lemma and Lifting Solutions Modulo Prime Powers

Hensel's Lemma provides a systematic way to lift a solution of a polynomial congruence f(x) ≡ 0 (mod p) to a solution modulo pᵏ for any k ≥ 1. The basic version requires a simple root: if f(r) ≡ 0 (mod p) and f′(r) ≢ 0 (mod p), then there exists a unique lift rₖ modulo pᵏ such that rₖ ≡ r (mod p) and f(rₖ) ≡ 0 (mod pᵏ). The lift is constructed iteratively: given rᵢ satisfying the congruence modulo pⁱ, set rᵢ₊₁ = rᵢ − f(rᵢ)·(f′(rᵢ))⁻¹ (mod pⁱ⁺¹), where the inverse is taken modulo p.

When the derivative vanishes modulo p (a multiple root), the simple lifting fails. In that case one must examine higher derivatives or use the “strong” form of Hensel’s lemma: if f(r) ≡ 0 (mod p²ᵐ) and vₚ(f′(r)) = m < k/2, then a lift exists but may not be unique. The number of lifts equals pvₚ(f′(r)). This situation often appears in problems asking for the number of solutions to x² ≡ a (mod pᵏ) when a is a quadratic residue modulo p but the derivative 2x is divisible by p.

The algorithmic steps are straightforward. Start with a root r₁ modulo p found by trial or by using known quadratic residues. Compute f(r₁) and f′(r₁). If f′(r₁) is invertible modulo p, compute the modular inverse and update r₂ = r₁ − f(r₁)·(f′(r₁))⁻¹ (mod p²). Repeat until the desired power pᵏ is reached. Each iteration doubles the precision in the p‑adic sense, so the number of steps equals k − 1.

Consider the congruence x² ≡ 2 (mod 7³). Modulo 7, the squares are 0,1,2,4; 2 is a residue with roots r₁ = 3 and r₁ = 4. Take r₁ = 3. f(x) = x² − 2, f′(x) = 2x. f′(3) = 6 ≡ −1 (mod 7) which is invertible. Compute f(3) = 9 − 2 = 7 ≡ 0 (mod 7). The inverse of 6 modulo 49 is 41 (since 6·41 = 246 ≡ 1). Then r₂ = 3 − 7·41 ≡ 3 − 287 ≡ 3 − 42 ≡ −39 ≡ 10 (mod 49). Verify 10² = 100 ≡ 2 (mod 49). Next lift to 7³ = 343: f(10) = 100 − 2 = 98, f′(10) = 20. Inverse of 20 modulo 343 is 274 (20·274 = 5480 ≡ 1). r₃ = 10 − 98·274 ≡ 10 − 26852 ≡ 10 − 196 ≡ −186 ≡ 157 (mod 343). Check 157² = 24649 ≡ 2 (mod 343). The other root lifts similarly to 186.

Hensel lifting is also the foundation of p‑adic expansions. The sequence of lifts r₁, r₂, … converges to a p‑adic integer satisfying f(x) = 0. In competitive exams, the lemma is often disguised as “find all integers x such that x² ≡ 5 (mod 125)”. Recognising the need to lift from modulo 5 to modulo 125 saves enormous trial‑and‑error time.

Hensel Lifting Technique
Find all solutions to x² ≡ 5 (mod 125).
Step 1: Solve modulo 5. Squares modulo 5 are 0,1,4. 5 ≡ 0 (mod 5), so x² ≡ 0 ⇒ x ≡ 0 (mod 5). The only root is r₁ = 0. Step 2: f(x) = x² − 5, f′(x) = 2x. At r₁ = 0, f′(0) = 0, so the simple Hensel condition fails. We must use the strong form. Since f(0) = −5 ≡ 0 (mod 5) but f(0) = −5 ≡ 20 (mod 25) ≠ 0, there is no lift to modulo 25. Therefore no solution exists modulo 125. Answer: no integer x satisfies the congruence.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a polynomial congruence involves a prime power modulus, first reduce modulo the prime. If the derivative at the root is non‑zero modulo that prime, apply the simple Hensel iteration; it is fast and deterministic. If the derivative vanishes, check whether the constant term already satisfies the higher power — often the answer is “no solution”. Memorise the iteration formula ri+1 = r_i − f(r_i)·(f′(r_i))-1 (mod pi+1) and practice computing modular inverses with the extended Euclidean algorithm.

Quadratic Residues, Legendre and Jacobi Symbols

A quadratic residue modulo an odd prime p is an integer a such that the congruence x² ≡ a (mod p) has a solution. The Legendre symbol (a|p) is defined as 1 if a is a non‑zero quadratic residue, −1 if a is a non‑residue, and 0 if p | a. Euler’s criterion gives a computational test: (a|p) ≡ a(p−1)/2 (mod p). This criterion turns the theoretical definition into an exponentiation that can be evaluated with binary exponentiation in O(log p) time.

The Legendre symbol obeys multiplicativity: (ab|p) = (a|p)(b|p). It also satisfies the supplementary laws: (−1|p) = (−1)(p−1)/2 and (2|p) = (−1)(p²−1)/8. The crown jewel is the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity: for distinct odd primes p and q, (p|q)(q|p) = (−1)((p−1)/2)((q−1)/2). This law allows one to flip the symbol and reduce the size of the numbers involved, making the evaluation of (a|p) for large a feasible by factoring a and applying reciprocity repeatedly.

The Jacobi symbol (a|n) extends the Legendre symbol to odd composite denominators n by multiplicativity over the prime factors of n. It retains the same formal properties — multiplicativity, supplementary laws, and a reciprocity law identical in shape — but (a|n) = 1 no longer guarantees that a is a quadratic residue modulo n; it only indicates that a is a residue modulo each prime factor an even number of times. This distinction is crucial in primality testing: the Solovay–Strassen test uses the Jacobi symbol to detect composites because for a prime p the congruence (a|p) ≡ a(p−1)/2 (mod p) holds for all a, while for many composites it fails.

In competitive problems, the Legendre symbol often appears in the guise “determine whether the congruence x² ≡ a (mod p) has a solution”. One factors a, applies the supplementary laws for −1 and 2, then uses quadratic reciprocity to flip symbols until the numerator becomes 1 or a small prime. For example, to decide if 17 is a quadratic residue modulo 41, compute (17|41). Since both are ≡ 1 (mod 4), reciprocity gives (17|41) = (41|17) = (7|17). Now 7 ≡ 3 (mod 4) and 17 ≡ 1 (mod 4), so (7|17) = (17|7) = (3|7). Finally (3|7) = −(7|3) = −(1|3) = −1. Hence 17 is a non‑residue modulo 41.

The Jacobi symbol is useful when the modulus is composite but the problem only asks for the value of the symbol, not the existence of a square root. For instance, “evaluate (12|35)” factors as (12|5)(12|7) = (2|5)(2|7) = (−1)(1) = −1. No square root modulo 35 is implied, but the symbol value is obtained instantly. Recognising whether a question requires a true Legendre test or merely a Jacobi evaluation avoids the trap of assuming solvability from a Jacobi value of 1.

Möbius Inversion and Applications to Divisor Sum Problems

The Möbius function μ(n) is defined by μ(1) = 1; μ(n) = (−1)^k if n is a product of k distinct primes; and μ(n) = 0 if n has a squared prime factor. This function encodes the inclusion–exclusion principle over the set of prime divisors. Its fundamental property is the Möbius inversion formula: if two arithmetic functions f and g satisfy g(n) = ∑d|n f(d) for all n, then f(n) = ∑d|n μ(d) g(n/d). Equivalently, the Dirichlet convolution of μ with the constant‑1 function yields the identity δ(n) (1 for n = 1, 0 otherwise).

A classic application is the evaluation of ∑d|n μ(d). By the inversion formula with f = δ and g = 1, this sum equals 1 if n = 1 and 0 otherwise. This identity underlies many combinatorial counting arguments, such as the number of square‑free integers up to x: ∑n≤x |μ(n)|. Another standard result is ∑d|n φ(d) = n, which follows by applying Möbius inversion to the identity n = ∑d|n φ(d). In contests, the formula ∑d|n μ(d) · ⌊x/d⌋ counts integers ≤ x that are coprime to n, a direct inclusion–exclusion over the prime factors of n.

Problems often ask for the sum of a multiplicative function over divisors. For a multiplicative f, the Dirichlet series ∑ f(n)/n^s factorises as an Euler product. Möbius inversion converts sums over divisors into products over primes. For example, to compute ∑d|n μ(d) · σ(d) where σ is the sum‑of‑divisors function, note that σ = 1 * id (Dirichlet convolution of 1 and the identity). Then μ * σ = μ * 1 * id = δ * id = id, so the sum equals n. Recognising such convolution patterns turns a seemingly heavy divisor sum into a one‑line answer.

Another frequent theme is the evaluation of ∑k=1n φ(k). Using the identity ∑d|m φ(d) = m and swapping summation order gives ∑k=1n φ(k) = ½ (1 + ∑d=1n μ(d) ⌊n/d⌋²). This formula allows O(√n) computation by grouping equal values of ⌊n/d⌋, a technique that appears in “sum of totients” problems with n up to 10¹¹. The key is to pre‑compute μ up to √n using a linear sieve, then apply the grouped summation.

When a problem presents a divisor sum with alternating signs, suspect Möbius inversion. For instance, “find ∑d|n (−1)ω(d) f(n/d)” where ω(d) is the number of distinct prime factors. Since (−1)ω(d) = μ(d) for square‑free d and 0 otherwise, the sum is exactly the Möbius transform of f restricted to square‑free divisors. Identifying the Möbius weight lets you replace the sum by a simpler expression, often just f evaluated at the radical of n.

  • Applying LTE without verifying that the prime does not divide the bases, leading to an infinite valuation.
  • Using the odd‑prime LTE formula for p = 2 and forgetting the extra v₂(a + b) + v₂(n) − 1 term.
  • Attempting simple Hensel lifting when the derivative vanishes modulo p, producing no lift or missing multiple lifts.
  • Confusing the Jacobi symbol value 1 with the existence of a square root modulo a composite modulus.
  • Forgetting that μ(n) = 0 for non‑square‑free n, which makes many divisor sums collapse to zero.
  • Misapplying Möbius inversion by swapping the roles of f and g, yielding the reciprocal relation instead of the desired function.
  • Neglecting to group equal floor values when evaluating ∑ μ(d)⌊n/d⌋, causing an O(n) loop instead of O(√n).

Primitive Roots, Orders, and the Structure of (Z/nZ)^×

The order of an integer a modulo n, denoted ordn(a), is the smallest positive integer k such that ak ≡ 1 (mod n). This concept is central because the powers of a repeat with period ordn(a). A primitive root modulo n is an integer g whose order equals φ(n), the size of the reduced residue system. When a primitive root exists, the multiplicative group (Z/nZ)^× is cyclic and every unit can be written as a power of g.

Primitive roots do not exist for all moduli. The complete classification is a classical theorem: (Z/nZ)^× is cyclic precisely for n = 1, 2, 4, pk, and 2pk where p is an odd prime and k ≥ 1. For all other n (e.g., n = 8, 12, 15, 24), the group is a direct product of two or more cyclic groups. In those cases the maximum possible order of any element is λ(n), the Carmichael function, which is strictly smaller than φ(n).

When a primitive root g exists, the number of distinct primitive roots modulo n is φ(φ(n)). This follows because the generators of a cyclic group of order m are exactly the φ(m) elements of order m. The existence of a primitive root allows us to convert multiplicative problems into additive ones via discrete logarithms (indices). If a ≡ gu (mod n) and b ≡ gv (mod n), then the congruence ax ≡ b (mod n) becomes ux ≡ v (mod φ(n)), a linear congruence in x.

The structure of (Z/nZ)^× for general n is given by the Chinese Remainder Theorem decomposition. For n = ∏ piki, the group is the direct product of the groups modulo each prime power. For odd prime powers pk, the group is cyclic of order φ(pk) = pk-1(p-1). For powers of 2, (Z/2Z)^× is trivial, (Z/4Z)^× is cyclic of order 2, and for k ≥ 3, (Z/2kZ)^× ≅ C2 × C2k-2, a product of a cyclic group of order 2 and a cyclic group of order 2k-2. This decomposition explains why primitive roots fail for 2k (k ≥ 3) and for any n with two odd prime factors.

Understanding the order of an element is crucial for problems asking for the smallest exponent e such that ae ≡ 1 (mod n), or for determining the period of a decimal expansion (which is ordn(10) when gcd(10,n)=1). The order always divides λ(n), and when a primitive root exists it divides φ(n). This divisibility property is often used to narrow down candidates when searching for orders manually.

Carmichael Function λ(n) and Exponent Reduction for Composite Moduli

The Carmichael function λ(n) is defined as the exponent of the multiplicative group (Z/nZ)^×, i.e., the least positive integer m such that am ≡ 1 (mod n) for every a coprime to n. It provides the minimal universal exponent, improving on Euler's theorem which guarantees aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) but φ(n) is not always the smallest such exponent.

The function is computed via prime‑power rules:

  • λ(2) = 1, λ(4) = 2, and for k ≥ 3, λ(2k) = 2k-2.
  • For an odd prime power pk, λ(pk) = φ(pk) = pk-1(p-1).
  • For general n = ∏ piki, λ(n) = lcm( λ(p1k1), …, λ(prkr) ).

Because λ(n) divides φ(n), it is often significantly smaller. For example, φ(24) = 8 but λ(24) = lcm(λ(8), λ(3)) = lcm(2, 2) = 2. Hence for any a coprime to 24, a2 ≡ 1 (mod 24). This makes λ(n) the preferred tool for reducing large exponents modulo composite n. To compute aE (mod n) with gcd(a,n)=1, first reduce E modulo λ(n): aE ≡ aE mod λ(n) (mod n), with the convention that if the reduced exponent is 0 we use λ(n) itself.

The Carmichael function also governs the order of any element: ordn(a) always divides λ(n). This is sharper than the statement that the order divides φ(n). In problems where one must find the order of a specific a modulo n, checking divisors of λ(n) is faster than checking divisors of φ(n). Moreover, λ(n) is the exact exponent of the group, so there exists at least one element whose order equals λ(n) (though not necessarily φ(n)).

A common pitfall is applying Euler's theorem when the base is not coprime to the modulus. Neither Euler nor Carmichael applies if gcd(a,n) > 1. In such cases one must factor the modulus, separate the coprime part, and handle the non‑coprime part by direct powering or by using the lifting exponent lemma (LTE) for prime powers. The Carmichael function is defined only for the coprime part, so always verify gcd(a,n)=1 before using λ(n).

Technique: Minimal Universal Exponent
Find the remainder when 71000 is divided by 24.

Step 1: Check gcd(7,24) = 1, so Carmichael reduction applies.

Step 2: Compute λ(24). Factor 24 = 23 × 3. λ(23) = 23-2 = 2. λ(3) = φ(3) = 2. λ(24) = lcm(2,2) = 2.

Step 3: Reduce the exponent modulo λ(24): 1000 mod 2 = 0. Since the reduced exponent is 0, we use λ(24) itself: 71000 ≡ 72 (mod 24).

Step 4: Compute 72 = 49. 49 mod 24 = 1.

Answer: The remainder is 1.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When reducing large exponents modulo a composite n, always compute λ(n) first. It is often much smaller than φ(n) and gives the minimal universal exponent. If the base shares a factor with n, split the modulus into coprime and non‑coprime parts; apply Carmichael only to the coprime part.

Generalized Chinese Remainder Theorem for Non‑Coprime Moduli

The classical Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) requires pairwise coprime moduli. In many contest problems the moduli are not coprime, yet a solution may still exist. The generalized CRT states: the system x ≡ ai (mod ni) for i = 1..k has a solution if and only if ai ≡ aj (mod gcd(ni, nj)) for every pair i, j. When a solution exists, it is unique modulo L = lcm(n1, …, nk).

To construct the solution, combine congruences two at a time. Given x ≡ a (mod m) and x ≡ b (mod n), let g = gcd(m,n). The consistency condition is a ≡ b (mod g). If satisfied, we can find integers u, v such that mu + nv = g (extended Euclidean algorithm). A particular solution is x = a + m * ((b-a)/g) * u. The combined modulus is lcm(m,n) = mn/g. Repeat until all congruences are merged.

This method is essential for problems like: “Find the smallest positive integer that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 6 and remainder 5 when divided by 9.” Here gcd(6,9)=3, and 2 ≡ 5 (mod 3) holds (both ≡ 2). The solution modulo lcm(6,9)=18 is x ≡ 14 (mod 18). The smallest positive is 14.

When the consistency condition fails, the system has no solution. For instance, x ≡ 1 (mod 4) and x ≡ 2 (mod 6) gives gcd(4,6)=2 but 1 ≢ 2 (mod 2), so no integer satisfies both.

The generalized CRT also underlies the solution of linear congruences with non‑prime moduli. A single congruence ax ≡ b (mod n) can be split into prime‑power congruences using the standard CRT (since prime powers are coprime). However, if one directly applies the generalized CRT to a system where moduli share factors, the consistency check automatically handles the common factors.

In competitive exams, the generalized CRT often appears disguised as “find the number that satisfies several remainder conditions” where the divisors are not coprime. Recognising the need for the consistency check saves time and avoids the trap of blindly applying the standard CRT formula which would produce an incorrect answer.

Dirichlet Convolution, Multiplicative Functions, and the Dirichlet Hyperbola Method

The Dirichlet convolution of two arithmetic functions f and g is defined by (f * g)(n) = ∑d|n f(d) g(n/d). This operation is commutative, associative, and has identity ε(n) where ε(1)=1 and ε(n)=0 for n>1. A function f is multiplicative if f(1)=1 and f(ab)=f(a)f(b) whenever gcd(a,b)=1. The convolution of two multiplicative functions is again multiplicative.

Key identities that every aspirant should recognise:

  • 1 * 1 = d(n) (number of divisors).
  • 1 * id = σ(n) (sum of divisors), where id(n)=n.
  • μ * 1 = ε (Möbius inversion).
  • φ = μ * id (Euler’s totient as convolution).
  • σk = 1 * idk where idk(n)=nk.

These identities allow instant evaluation of divisor sums. For example, ∑d|n μ(d) σ(d) = n because μ * σ = μ * (1 * id) = (μ * 1) * id = ε * id = id.

The Dirichlet hyperbola method is a technique to compute the summatory function ∑n≤x f(n) for multiplicative f in O(√x) time. The idea: write ∑n≤x (f * g)(n) = ∑a≤√x f(a) ∑b≤x/a g(b) + ∑b≤√x g(b) ∑a≤x/b f(a) − (∑a≤√x f(a))(∑b≤√x g(b)). Choosing f and g wisely (often f=1, g=f) reduces the double sum to two single sums plus a correction term.

Classic example: ∑n≤x d(n) = 2 ∑i≤√x ⌊x/i⌋ − ⌊√x⌋2. This formula is derived by taking f=g=1 in the hyperbola identity. Similarly, ∑n≤x σ(n) can be evaluated by writing σ = 1 * id and applying the method.

In exams, the hyperbola method appears in problems asking for ∑n=1N d(n) or ∑ φ(n) for large N (up to 1011 or more). The trick is to pre‑compute the values of the inner summatory function (e.g., ⌊N/i⌋) for all distinct values, which are only O(√N) many. This reduces a seemingly O(N) loop to O(√N), making the problem feasible within time limits.

Another application is evaluating ∑n≤x μ(n) ⌊x/n⌋, which counts integers ≤ x that are coprime to a given set of primes (inclusion–exclusion). Grouping equal values of ⌊x/n⌋ yields an O(√x) algorithm after a linear sieve for μ up to √x.

Solving Exponential Congruences and Discrete Logarithm Basics

An exponential congruence has the form ax ≡ b (mod n). The unknown x appears in the exponent. The solution strategy depends heavily on the structure of (Z/nZ)^×.

If a primitive root g exists modulo n (i.e., n = 1,2,4,pk,2pk), we can write a ≡ gu, b ≡ gv (mod n). The congruence becomes gux ≡ gv (mod n) ⇒ ux ≡ v (mod φ(n)). This is a linear congruence in x. It has solutions iff gcd(u, φ(n)) divides v. The number of solutions modulo φ(n) is exactly that gcd. Once a particular solution x0 is found, the general solution is x ≡ x0 (mod φ(n)/gcd(u,φ(n))).

When no primitive root exists, we factor n into prime powers and solve the congruence modulo each prime power using the structure of the group. For odd prime powers pk, the group is cyclic, so the primitive‑root method works. For 2k (k ≥ 3), the group is C2 × C2k-2. One can still solve by writing a and b in terms of the two generators (−1 and 5 are standard generators). Alternatively, use the Carmichael function λ(n) to reduce the exponent: if gcd(a,n)=1, then aλ(n) ≡ 1, so the exponent x can be considered modulo λ(n) after taking discrete logarithms in each cyclic component.

The discrete logarithm problem (given g, b, find x such that gx ≡ b (mod p)) is computationally hard in general, but for the prime moduli encountered in competitive exams (p up to 104 or 105) the baby‑step giant‑step algorithm solves it in O(√p) time and memory. The algorithm: let m = ⌈√p⌉. Pre‑compute baby steps gj for j=0..m−1. Then compute giant steps b·g−im for i=0..m and look for a match. This is feasible for p up to about 106 in typical time limits.

For composite moduli where a primitive root does not exist, the discrete logarithm may not be uniquely defined because the group is not cyclic. In such cases the equation ax ≡ b (mod n) is solved by reducing to prime‑power moduli via CRT, solving each (where the group is cyclic or a product of two cyclics), and recombining the solutions for x using the generalized CRT on the exponents (since the exponents live in different moduli).

A frequent exam question: “Find the smallest positive integer x such that 3x ≡ 7 (mod 17).” Here 17 is prime, 3 is a primitive root (check: order 16). Compute discrete log of 7 base 3: 311 ≡ 7 (mod 17). Then x ≡ 11 (mod 16). Smallest positive x = 11. If the base is not a primitive root, e.g., 2x ≡ 3 (mod 7), note that 2 has order 3 modulo 7. The equation has a solution only if 3 is in the subgroup generated by 2. Since 20=1, 21=2, 22=4, 3 is not in the subgroup, so no solution.

  • Assuming primitive roots exist for all moduli; they exist only for 1,2,4,pk,2pk.
  • Confusing φ(n) with λ(n) and using φ(n) when λ(n) gives a smaller universal exponent.
  • Forgetting the consistency condition ai ≡ aj (mod gcd(ni,nj)) in the generalized CRT.
  • Misapplying the Dirichlet hyperbola method by omitting the correction term −(∑a≤√x f(a))(∑b≤√x g(b)).
  • Treating discrete logarithm as always solvable by a simple linear congruence when the base is not a generator of the full group.
  • Overlooking that the order of an element divides λ(n), not necessarily φ(n), leading to missed smaller periods.
  • Using baby‑step giant‑step without verifying that the base generates a subgroup containing the target; if the target lies outside the subgroup, no solution exists.

These traps highlight the most frequent errors in advanced number system problems.
