Foundations of Time and Work

1.1 The Unitary Principle and the Concept of Total Work

The study of time and work rests on the simple observation that the amount of work completed is the product of the rate at which a worker or a machine operates and the duration for which it operates. In quantitative aptitude, the total work is conventionally taken as one whole unit, so that a person who finishes a job in n days contributes a daily fraction of 1/n of the whole. This fractional representation makes it possible to add the contributions of several agents working together, because the sum of their daily fractions equals the combined daily fraction. When the combined daily fraction is known, the total time required is simply the reciprocal of that fraction. The unitary method therefore converts a problem about days into a problem about fractions, which can be manipulated with ordinary arithmetic.

1.2 Choosing a Convenient Measure of Work: The LCM Technique

Although the fractional approach is mathematically sound, it often leads to cumbersome denominators when several workers with different individual times are involved. A widely adopted shortcut is to assume the total work to be the least common multiple (LCM) of all the given individual times. If A completes a task in 12 days and B in 18 days, the LCM of 12 and 18 is 36. Treating the whole job as 36 units of work turns A’s daily output into 3 units per day and B’s into 2 units per day. The combined daily output becomes 5 units, and the required time is 36 ÷ 5 = 7.2 days. This technique eliminates fractions entirely, reduces the chance of arithmetic error, and makes mental calculation feasible under examination pressure.

1.3 Inverse Relationship Between Time and Efficiency

Efficiency is defined as the amount of work done per unit time. Because the total work is fixed, a worker who needs fewer days to finish the same job must have a higher daily output; consequently, efficiency is inversely proportional to the time taken. If A finishes a task in half the time required by B, A’s efficiency is exactly twice that of B. This inverse proportionality allows us to replace statements such as “A is thrice as efficient as B” with the equivalent time ratio “A takes one‑third the time of B”. In combined‑work problems, the efficiencies of all participants are simply added, because each contributes independently to the total work done each day. The sum of efficiencies, expressed either as fractions of the whole or as LCM‑based units, gives the combined daily progress.

Efficiency and Combined Work

2.1 Adding Efficiencies When Workers Operate Simultaneously

When two or more persons work together from the start, their individual daily contributions accumulate without interference. If the total work is taken as the LCM of their individual times, each person’s efficiency becomes an integer number of work units per day. The combined efficiency is the arithmetic sum of these integers. The total time required is then the total work divided by the combined efficiency. This method works regardless of whether the workers have equal or unequal efficiencies, and it extends naturally to any number of participants. The key point is that simultaneous work implies a simple additive model for daily output.

2.2 Partial Participation and the Concept of Work Done Before Joining or Leaving

In many examination questions, a worker may start later, leave early, or work only for a specified number of days. The total work is still the LCM of all relevant times, but the contribution of each worker must be weighted by the actual number of days he or she works. For instance, if A works for 4 days and B works for the entire duration, A’s contribution is 4 × (efficiency of A) while B’s contribution is (total days) × (efficiency of B). The equation “sum of weighted contributions = total work” yields the unknown total days. This approach also handles situations where a worker’s efficiency changes midway, because the work can be split into phases, each with its own combined efficiency.

2.3 Efficiency Ratios Derived from Statements Like “A Is Twice As Good As B”

Verbal descriptions of relative efficiency are common in competitive tests. The phrase “A is twice as good as B” means that in the same time A completes twice the work of B, or equivalently A needs half the time of B for the same job. Translating such statements into numerical ratios is straightforward: if the efficiency ratio A : B = 2 : 1, then the time ratio A : B = 1 : 2. When three or more workers are compared, a chain of ratios can be built and then reduced to a common base, after which the LCM method can be applied. Mastery of this translation eliminates the need to set up algebraic equations for every comparative statement.

Example: Combined Work with a Late Joiner
A can complete a piece of work in 20 days and B can complete the same work in 30 days. A starts the work alone and after 5 days B joins him. In how many total days will the work be finished?
Take total work = LCM(20,30) = 60 units. Efficiency of A = 60/20 = 3 units/day. Efficiency of B = 60/30 = 2 units/day. In the first 5 days A does 5 × 3 = 15 units. Remaining work = 60 – 15 = 45 units. Combined efficiency after B joins = 3 + 2 = 5 units/day. Days needed for remaining work = 45/5 = 9 days. Total days = 5 + 9 = 14 days.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem mentions a worker joining or leaving, always compute the work done in the initial phase separately, then treat the remaining work as a fresh combined‑work problem. This two‑step separation prevents the common mistake of averaging times across the whole period.

Pipes and Cisterns

3.1 Modelling Inlet and Outlet Pipes as Positive and Negative Efficiencies

A pipe that fills a tank performs positive work, exactly analogous to a worker adding to the completed portion of a job. Conversely, a drain or outlet pipe removes water, which is negative work. If an inlet pipe can fill the tank in a hours, its hourly rate is +1/a of the tank. If an outlet pipe can empty the tank in b hours, its hourly rate is –1/b. When several pipes operate together, the net hourly rate is the algebraic sum of all individual rates. The time to fill (or empty) the tank is the reciprocal of the net rate, provided the net rate is positive for filling and negative for emptying. This direct translation of the work‑rate framework to pipes makes the topic a mere variation of the combined‑work model.

3.2 Using the LCM of Pipe Times to Avoid Fractions

Just as with human workers, the LCM of all given filling and emptying times can be taken as the tank’s capacity in arbitrary units. An inlet pipe that fills in 8 hours contributes LCM/8 units per hour; an outlet that empties in 6 hours contributes –LCM/6 units per hour. The net hourly contribution is the sum of these signed integers. The required time is then the capacity divided by the net hourly contribution. This integer‑based method is especially valuable when three or more pipes are involved, because it sidesteps the need to find a common denominator for several fractions.

3.3 Situations Where the Tank Is Partially Filled or Multiple Phases Occur

Examination questions often introduce a twist: the tank may already contain some water, or pipes may be opened and closed in stages. In such cases the problem is split into phases. For each phase, identify which pipes are active, compute the net rate for that phase using the LCM method, and determine how much the water level changes during the phase’s duration. The final answer is obtained by adding the durations of all phases until the tank reaches the desired level (full or empty). The phase‑wise approach mirrors the partial‑participation technique used for workers who join or leave.

Alternate Day Work and Interruptions

4.1 The Two‑Day Cycle Concept for Strict Alternation

When two workers operate on alternate days, the pattern of work repeats every two days. If A works on day 1, B on day 2, A on day 3, and so on, the combined work done in each two‑day cycle is the sum of A’s one‑day work and B’s one‑day work. By expressing each person’s one‑day work as a fraction of the total (or as LCM units), the work completed per cycle becomes a fixed quantity. The number of full cycles needed is the integer part of (total work ÷ work per cycle). The remainder after the last full cycle is then handled by simulating the next day(s) in the correct order until the work is finished. This cycle‑based reasoning avoids day‑by‑day simulation for large numbers of days.

4.2 Handling a Starter Other Than the First Worker

The problem statement may specify that B starts instead of A, or that the alternation begins after a certain number of days of solo work. In every case the first step is to determine the exact sequence of workers for the first few days, compute the work done in that initial segment, and then apply the regular two‑day cycle to the remaining work. The order of workers in the cycle is crucial because the remainder may be completed by the first worker of the next cycle, the second worker, or may require a partial day from the second worker. Writing down the first three or four days explicitly eliminates ambiguity.

4.3 Interruptions Such as Rest Days or Efficiency Drops

Some questions introduce a rest day after a fixed number of working days, or a sudden change in a worker’s efficiency (for example, due to fatigue). The solution strategy remains phase‑wise: treat each uninterrupted block as a separate combined‑work problem with its own set of active workers and efficiencies. Compute the work finished in each block, subtract from the total, and continue with the next block. Because the LCM method works with integer units, the transition between blocks is seamless — the remaining work is simply the original total minus the sum of work done in all previous blocks.

Wages and Distribution of Earnings

5.1 The Principle of Proportional Division Based on Work Done

When a group of workers completes a job for a fixed total wage, the fair division of that wage is in direct proportion to the amount of work each person actually performed. Since work done equals efficiency multiplied by the number of days worked, the share of each worker is (efficiency × days worked) divided by the sum of such products for all workers, multiplied by the total wage. This rule holds whether the workers labour simultaneously, sequentially, or in any mixed schedule. The underlying assumption is that the wage compensates for the contribution to the finished product, not merely for the time spent on site.

5.2 Applying the LCM Framework to Wage Problems

Using the LCM of individual times as the total work converts each worker’s efficiency into an integer number of units per day. The work contributed by a worker then becomes an integer (efficiency × days). The ratio of these integers is exactly the ratio of their wage shares. For example, if total work = 60 units, A’s efficiency = 3 units/day, B’s = 2 units/day, and A works 4 days while B works 6 days, A contributes 12 units and B contributes 12 units. The wage is split equally because the contributions are equal, even though their efficiencies differ. This integer‑based ratio method eliminates fractions and makes the division transparent.

5.3 Scenarios Involving Partial Payment, Penalties, or Bonus Conditions

Advanced questions may state that a worker receives a fixed daily wage plus a bonus for early completion, or that a penalty is deducted for each day of delay. In such cases the base wage is first divided according to work done, and then the additional terms are applied individually. If a bonus is shared in the same proportion as the base wage, the bonus amount is simply distributed using the same work‑ratio. Penalties that depend on the overall project duration affect all workers equally only if the contract specifies a collective penalty; otherwise each worker’s penalty is linked to his own attendance. Careful reading of the wording determines whether the extra amount is pooled before division or allocated per person.

Mastering these five thematic areas — foundations, efficiency, pipes, alternate‑day patterns, and wages — equips a candidate to solve every standard time‑and‑work question that appears in the quantitative aptitude section of banking examinations.

Pairwise Combined Efficiencies and the Three‑Worker System

When a problem gives the time taken by three workers A, B and C in pairs — A and B together finish in x days, B and C in y days, C and A in z days — the individual efficiencies can be recovered without solving a system of three equations. Let the total work be the LCM of x, y, z (or any convenient multiple). The combined efficiency of A and B is W/x units per day, of B and C is W/y, of C and A is W/z. Adding the three pairwise efficiencies gives twice the sum of individual efficiencies: 2(EA+EB+EC) = W(1/x+1/y+1/z). Hence the combined efficiency of all three is half of that sum. Individual efficiencies follow by subtraction: EA = (EA+EB+EC) – (EB+EC), and similarly for B and C. This shortcut eliminates fractions and works even when the pairwise times are not co‑prime.

Extension to Four Workers

For four workers the same principle extends: given the six pairwise combined times, the sum of all six pairwise efficiencies equals three times the sum of the four individual efficiencies. The factor changes from 2 to 3 because each worker appears in exactly three pairs. The combined efficiency of all four is one‑third of the sum of the six pairwise efficiencies. Individual efficiencies are then obtained by subtracting the appropriate three‑pair sums. The method scales to any number of workers provided all pairwise data are available.

When Only Some Pairs Are Known

If the problem supplies only two pairwise times and the time taken by all three together, the third pairwise time can be derived. Let the three‑worker combined time be t. Using the relation 2(EA+EB+EC) = sum of pairwise efficiencies, and knowing EA+EB+EC = W/t, we get the missing pairwise efficiency = 2W/t – (known pairwise efficiencies). This algebraic inversion is a commonly tested pattern.

Man‑Day Equivalence and Mixed Workforce Problems

The concept of man‑days (or person‑days) treats work as the product of the number of workers and the number of days they work, assuming uniform efficiency. When workers of different categories — men, women, children, or machines — have different efficiencies, the equivalence is established by expressing each category in terms of a base unit. For example, if 1 man = 2 women = 3 children in efficiency, then 1 man‑day = 2 woman‑days = 3 child‑days. The total work in base units is the sum over all categories of (number of workers × days × efficiency factor). This converts a heterogeneous workforce into a single homogeneous measure, allowing the standard LCM or unitary method to be applied.

Changing Composition Mid‑Project

A common variant adds or removes workers after a certain number of days. The work done in the first phase is computed with the initial composition; the remaining work is then tackled with the new composition. Because the base‑unit work is additive, the remaining work in base units is simply total base units minus work done in phase one. The time for phase two equals remaining base units divided by the new combined efficiency in base units per day. If the change occurs at a fractional day, the same arithmetic holds — compute work done up to the exact moment of change using the efficiencies active at that moment.

Multiple Shifts and Overlapping Teams

When two teams work in overlapping shifts (e.g., Team A works 8 am–4 pm, Team B works 12 pm–8 pm), the overlapping period has the sum of both teams’ efficiencies, while the non‑overlapping periods have only one team’s efficiency. The day can be split into intervals with constant combined efficiency. The total work per day is the sum over intervals of (interval length × combined efficiency). This interval method avoids the error of averaging efficiencies across the whole day.

Worked Example: Pairwise Efficiencies
A and B together can complete a work in 12 days. B and C together can complete it in 15 days. C and A together can complete it in 20 days. In how many days will A, B and C together complete the work?
Let total work = LCM(12,15,20) = 60 units. Combined efficiencies: A+B = 60/12 = 5 units/day; B+C = 60/15 = 4 units/day; C+A = 60/20 = 3 units/day. Sum of pairwise efficiencies = 5+4+3 = 12 units/day. This sum equals 2(A+B+C). Hence A+B+C = 12/2 = 6 units/day. Time for all three together = 60/6 = 10 days.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question gives pairwise times, immediately write down the LCM of those times as total work. Compute each pairwise efficiency as an integer. Add them, halve the sum to get the combined efficiency of all workers. This two‑step arithmetic (LCM → integer efficiencies → halve) is faster than solving simultaneous equations and eliminates fraction errors.

Variable Efficiency and Learning‑Curve Scenarios

Some problems state that a worker’s efficiency changes after a certain amount of work is done or after a fixed number of days — for instance, “A’s efficiency drops by 20 % after 5 days” or “B becomes twice as fast after completing half the work”. The solution is to split the project into phases where efficiency is constant. In each phase, compute the work done using the efficiency applicable to that phase. The transition point is either a day count or a work‑quantity threshold. If the trigger is a work quantity (e.g., half the work), first determine how many days the first phase lasts by dividing the trigger work by the initial efficiency. Then apply the new efficiency for the remaining work. Because the LCM method works with integer units, the trigger work can be expressed in the same units, keeping all arithmetic integral.

Gradual Efficiency Change

When efficiency changes gradually (e.g., “A’s efficiency increases by 1 unit per day”), the work done each day forms an arithmetic progression. The total work over n days is the sum of the progression: n/2 × (2a + (n‑1)d), where a is the first day’s efficiency and d the daily increment. Equate this sum to the total work (in LCM units) and solve the resulting quadratic for n. The quadratic typically yields an integer root in exam questions. If the root is not an integer, the last day is a partial day; compute the exact fraction by subtracting the work done in full days from the total and dividing by the efficiency on the next day.

Efficiency Drop Due to Fatigue

A fatigue model may state “after every 3 days of work, A rests for 1 day”. This is a periodic pattern, not a one‑time change. Treat each 4‑day block as a cycle: 3 working days at efficiency e, 1 rest day at efficiency 0. The work per cycle = 3e. Number of full cycles = floor(total work / 3e). Remainder work is handled by simulating the next working days in order. This cycle approach is identical to the alternate‑day cycle but with a longer period and a zero‑efficiency day included.

Multi‑Phase Pipe Networks with Leakage and Unknown Capacity

Advanced pipe problems introduce multiple inlets, multiple outlets, and a leak whose rate may be unknown. The standard sign convention remains: inlets positive, outlets and leaks negative. When the tank capacity is not given, assign a variable C (or use LCM of all given times as a proxy capacity). Write the net rate equation for each phase. For example, “Pipe A fills in 6 h, Pipe B fills in 8 h, Leak empties in 12 h. All three are opened together for 2 h, then the leak is plugged.” Phase 1 net rate = C/6 + C/8 – C/12 = (4C+3C–2C)/24 = 5C/24 per hour. Work in 2 h = 10C/24 = 5C/12. Remaining = 7C/12. Phase 2 net rate = C/6 + C/8 = 7C/24. Time for phase 2 = (7C/12) ÷ (7C/24) = 2 h. Total = 4 h. The capacity C cancels, confirming that the answer is independent of the actual volume.

Leak Rate Determined by Observation

If the leak rate is not given directly but the tank fills in a known time with all pipes open, the leak rate can be back‑calculated. Let the combined inlet rate be Rin and the observed fill time be T. Then net rate = C/T = Rin – Rleak. Hence Rleak = Rin – C/T. Once Rleak is known, any subsequent phase (e.g., leak plugged, or additional outlet opened) can be solved with the same sign convention.

Sequential Opening and Closing

When pipes are opened or closed at different times, break the timeline into intervals where the set of active pipes is constant. For each interval, compute the net rate using the active pipes’ efficiencies. Multiply by interval length to get work done. Sum across intervals equals total capacity. This interval method is identical to the overlapping‑shift technique for workers and avoids the common mistake of averaging rates over the whole duration.

Wage Distribution with Differential Rates and Penalty Clauses

Beyond simple proportional division, wages may be paid at different rates per unit of work for different workers, or a penalty may be levied on the whole group for delay. If worker A earns ₹p per unit and worker B earns ₹q per unit, the total wage = p × (work by A) + q × (work by B). The work by each is still efficiency × days. The ratio of their shares is p·EA·dA : q·EB·dB. This reduces to the standard ratio when p = q. When a penalty of ₹P is deducted for each day beyond the deadline, first compute the actual completion day. If the deadline is D days and the work finishes on day F > D, the total penalty = P × (F – D). The contract may specify that the penalty is shared in the same proportion as the base wage, or that each worker bears a penalty proportional to his own days of work. Read the wording: “penalty is shared equally” means divide P×(F–D) by number of workers; “penalty is deducted from each worker’s share in proportion to his work” means multiply each worker’s base share by (1 – penalty fraction).

Bonus for Early Completion

A bonus ₹B for finishing before the deadline is added to the total pool before division if the statement says “the bonus is distributed among the workers in the ratio of their work”. In that case, total distributable amount = base wage + B. Each worker’s share = (his work / total work) × (base wage + B). If the bonus is a fixed amount per worker (e.g., “each worker receives ₹500 bonus”), simply add that amount to each individual’s calculated share after the base division.

Partial Payment and Advance

When a worker receives an advance before the work starts, the advance is deducted from his final share. Compute the final share as per the work ratio, then subtract the advance. If the advance exceeds the final share, the worker owes the difference — a situation that sometimes appears as “find the amount to be recovered”. The arithmetic remains the same; only the sign of the final settlement changes.

  • Assuming pairwise times can be averaged instead of using the 2× sum rule.
  • Forgetting to convert mixed workforce into a common base unit before applying LCM.
  • Treating a gradual efficiency change as a single average efficiency rather than summing an arithmetic progression.
  • Ignoring the sign convention for leaks and outlets, leading to net rates that are too high.
  • Applying a penalty or bonus to the wrong pool (base wage vs. total distributable amount).
  • Misreading “shared equally” versus “shared in proportion to work” for penalties and bonuses.
  • Overlooking that an advance payment must be subtracted from the final calculated share, not added.

Work with Multiple Projects Sharing a Common Workforce

When a pool of workers is allocated to two or more projects that run concurrently, the total man‑day capacity of the pool is split across the projects. The key is to treat each project as a separate “work bucket” while the workforce is a single resource with a known daily efficiency. Suppose a team of 10 workers each having an efficiency of 1 unit per day works on Project X (total work 300 units) and Project Y (total work 200 units). If the team dedicates 6 workers to X and 4 to Y every day, the daily output on X is 6 units and on Y is 4 units. Project X finishes in 300 ÷ 6 = 50 days; Project Y finishes in 200 ÷ 4 = 50 days. Both finish together because the allocation ratio matches the work‑ratio.

If the allocation changes mid‑way, break the timeline into phases. In each phase the number of workers assigned to each project is constant, so the daily output per project is constant. Compute work done in each phase, subtract from the remaining work, and continue. The moment a project finishes, its workers become free and can be reassigned to the other project, altering the allocation for the next phase. This “re‑allocation on completion” is a frequent twist: the problem may state “when one project finishes, all its workers join the other”. Model it by solving the first project’s finish time, then adding its workforce to the second project’s rate for the remaining duration.

A useful shortcut is to work with “effective man‑days”. Let the total workforce be W workers each of efficiency e. The total daily capacity = W·e. If project i requires Wi work units, the fraction of capacity given to project i in a phase is fi (∑fi = 1). Then project i’s daily progress = fi·W·e. The time to finish project i in that phase = remaining Wi ÷ (fi·W·e). Because W·e cancels when comparing projects, you can often solve using only the ratios fi and the work ratios Wi.

When the problem gives only pairwise combined times (e.g., “A and B together finish Project 1 in 12 days, B and C together finish Project 2 in 15 days”) and asks for the time when all three work on both projects simultaneously, set up a system of equations for individual efficiencies. Let efficiencies be a, b, c. Then a+b = W1/12, b+c = W2/15. A third independent equation is needed to solve for a, b, c. Then compute the combined rate on each project when all three work together: a+b+c on Project 1 and a+b+c on Project 2. The finish time for each project is its work divided by the combined rate. The overall completion time is the maximum of the two, because both projects must be done.

Watch for hidden constraints: a worker cannot be split across projects in the same day unless the problem explicitly allows fractional allocation. If the statement says “each worker works on only one project per day”, the allocation must be integer numbers of workers. This turns the problem into a discrete optimisation (often a simple integer partition) rather than a continuous rate problem. In such cases, enumerate feasible allocations that satisfy the integer condition and pick the one that meets the given total time.

Work with Time‑Dependent Efficiency: Rate Proportional to Remaining Work

In many real‑world scenarios the work rate is not constant but depends on how much work is left. A classic model is “the rate of work is proportional to the remaining work”. Mathematically, if R(t) is the remaining work at time t and k is a constant of proportionality, then dR/dt = –k·R. The solution is exponential decay: R(t) = R₀·e^(–k t). The work is never truly finished in finite time, but for exam purposes a “completion threshold” (e.g., 99 % done) is given, or the problem asks for the time to reduce the work to a certain fraction.

When the proportionality constant k is expressed through a known “half‑time” or “time to complete a given fraction”, you can avoid calculus. Suppose a worker can finish 50 % of a job in 4 hours when working alone under this law. Then R(4) = 0.5·R₀ = R₀·e^(–k·4) ⇒ e^(–4k) = 0.5 ⇒ k = (ln 2)/4. To find the time for 90 % completion, set R(t) = 0.1·R₀ ⇒ e^(–k t) = 0.1 ⇒ t = (ln 10)/k = 4·(ln 10)/(ln 2) ≈ 13.3 hours. In many exam questions, logarithms can be avoided by using half‑life tables; the problem may give a convenient fraction like “the work halves every 3 days”. Then after n half‑lives the remaining fraction is (1/2)^n. Solve (1/2)^n = desired fraction by inspection or using the fact that 23 = 8, 24 = 16, etc.

When multiple workers operate under the same proportional law, their individual k values add because the differential equation is linear: dR/dt = –(k₁+k₂+…)·R. Thus the combined half‑time is shorter. If worker A halves the remaining work in 6 days (k₁ = ln 2 / 6) and worker B halves it in 12 days (k₂ = ln 2 / 12), together k = ln 2·(1/6+1/12) = ln 2·(1/4). The combined half‑time = 4 days. This additive property of k mirrors the additive property of constant efficiencies, but the time to reach a given fraction is not simply the harmonic sum of individual times; you must work with the k values.

A common variant mixes constant‑rate and proportional‑rate phases. For example, “A works at a constant rate of 10 units/day for the first 5 days, then his rate becomes proportional to the remaining work with half‑time 4 days”. Compute work done in the constant phase (50 units). Let total work be W. Remaining after 5 days = W–50. Then apply exponential decay on the remainder. If the question asks for total time to finish, set (W–50)·e^(–k t₂) = 0 (or a threshold) and solve for t₂. Because W is often unknown, use the LCM technique: assign a convenient total work that makes the constant‑rate phase an integer, then the proportional phase works with the same variable.

Examiners also test the “inverse proportional” law: rate ∝ 1/remaining work. Then dR/dt = –k/R ⇒ R dR = –k dt ⇒ R² = R₀² – 2k t. The work finishes when R = 0 at t = R₀²/(2k). If a worker finishes a job in T days under this law, then k = R₀²/(2T). Combined workers add their k values. This quadratic decay appears less often but is a recognised twist.

Technique: Mixed Constant and Proportional Phases
A tank of capacity 2400 litres is filled by Pipe A at a constant rate of 200 litres/hour for the first 3 hours. After that, a leak develops and the net inflow becomes proportional to the remaining empty volume with a half‑time of 2 hours. How long in total does it take to fill the tank?
Phase 1 (constant): In 3 hours Pipe A delivers 200 × 3 = 600 litres. Remaining empty volume = 2400 – 600 = 1800 litres. Phase 2 (proportional): Let empty volume be V(t). dV/dt = –k·V, with half‑time 2 h ⇒ k = ln 2 / 2. We need V(t) = 0 (practically, when V becomes negligible). Since exponential never reaches zero, the problem implies “filled” when V ≤ 1 litre (or 0.1 %). Using the half‑life method: after each 2 hours the empty volume halves. Starting from 1800: after 2 h → 900; after 4 h → 450; after 6 h → 225; after 8 h → 112.5; after 10 h → 56.25; after 12 h → 28.125; after 14 h → 14.06; after 16 h → 7.03; after 18 h → 3.5; after 20 h → 1.75; after 22 h → 0.875. So about 22 hours in Phase 2. Total ≈ 3 + 22 = 25 hours. (If a strict threshold like 1 litre is given, compute exactly: t = (1/k)·ln(1800/1) = (2/ln 2)·ln 1800 ≈ 22.1 h.)
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you see “rate proportional to remaining work”, immediately write the half‑life or the constant k. Convert the given data (time for 50 % or 90 % completion) into k = ln 2 / half‑time. For combined workers, add the k values. If a constant‑rate phase precedes the proportional phase, compute the work done in the first phase, then treat the remainder as the new initial amount for the exponential decay. Use the half‑life doubling/halving table (1, ½, ¼, ⅛…) to avoid logarithms unless the answer choices are exact expressions.

Work with Breakdown, Repair, and Downtime Modelling

Machines and workers are often subject to breakdowns that halt production for a random or fixed duration. The standard approach is to treat the breakdown‑repair cycle as a periodic reduction in effective efficiency. Suppose a machine works at 100 units/hour but breaks down every 5 hours of operation and requires 1 hour to repair. In each 6‑hour cycle it produces 5 × 100 = 500 units. Its average effective rate = 500 ÷ 6 ≈ 83.33 units/hour. If the total work is 2000 units, the number of full cycles needed = floor(2000 ÷ 500) = 4 cycles (2000 units exactly). Total time = 4 × 6 = 24 hours. If the work were 2100 units, 4 full cycles give 2000 units in 24 hours, leaving 100 units. The machine then works the next cycle: it needs 1 hour of operation (100 units) and no further breakdown because the cycle ends before the next scheduled breakdown. Total = 25 hours.

When multiple machines have different breakdown schedules, compute each machine’s average effective rate over the least common multiple (LCM) of their cycle lengths. For Machine A: cycle 5 h work + 1 h repair = 6 h, rate 100 units/h. Machine B: cycle 8 h work + 2 h repair = 10 h, rate 150 units/h. LCM of 6 and 10 = 30 hours. In 30 hours, A completes 5 cycles → 5 × 500 = 2500 units. B completes 3 cycles → 3 × (8×150) = 3600 units. Combined output in 30 hours = 6100 units. Average combined rate = 6100 ÷ 30 ≈ 203.33 units/h. Use this average rate for long‑duration problems; for short durations, simulate the timeline hour by hour because the alignment of breakdowns matters.

If breakdowns are probabilistic (e.g., “breaks down with probability 0.1 each hour”), the expected effective rate = nominal rate × (1 – probability of being down). For a machine that works 1 hour then possibly breaks for a geometrically distributed repair time, the steady‑state availability = mean uptime ÷ (mean uptime + mean downtime). This matches the deterministic cycle method when the distributions are exponential. Exam questions may simplify the probabilistic version to “on average the machine is idle 20 % of the time”, so just multiply the nominal rate by 0.8.

A twist is “breakdown occurs after a fixed amount of work, not time”. Example: a printer prints 500 pages then jams for 15 minutes. If the job is 3000 pages, it jams after every 500 pages. Number of jams = floor(3000 ÷ 500) = 6, but the last batch finishes exactly at a jam point, so only 5 repair periods are needed (the 6th jam occurs after the job ends). Total printing time = (3000 ÷ print rate) + 5 × 15 minutes. Always check whether the final breakdown coincides with completion; if it does, the repair time is not incurred.

When workers have rest days that follow a pattern (e.g., “works 6 days, rests 1 day”), treat the rest day as a zero‑efficiency day in a 7‑day cycle. The average daily efficiency = (6 × daily efficiency) ÷ 7. If the project spans a non‑multiple of 7 days, simulate the last partial cycle: count how many working days fall in the remainder. This is identical to the “alternate day work” concept but with a longer cycle.

Work with Contractual Milestones, Partial Acceptance, and Retention Money

Construction and service contracts often split the total payment into milestones linked to physical progress percentages. For quantitative problems, the milestone schedule translates into a piecewise‑linear payment curve. Suppose a contract worth ₹1,00,000 has milestones: 20 % on mobilisation, 30 % on 25 % completion, 30 % on 60 % completion, 20 % on 100 % completion. If the contractor achieves 40 % physical progress, the earned value = 20 % + 30 % + (40‑25)/(60‑25) × 30 % = 20+30+15×(30/35) ≈ 20+30+12.86 = 62.86 % of contract value. The amount due = 0.6286 × 1,00,000 = ₹62,860. A common approach is linear interpolation between milestones, unless the contract specifies “payment only on achieving the exact milestone”.

Retention money is a percentage (often 5‑10 %) withheld from each milestone payment and released after a defect‑liability period. If retention is 10 %, the contractor receives only 90 % of each milestone amount during execution. The retained sum accumulates and is paid at the end. For the above example, after 40 % progress the contractor has been paid 90 % of ₹62,860 = ₹56,574. The retention held = ₹6,286. At final completion, the remaining 20 % milestone (₹20,000) is also subject to 10 % retention, so the final release = ₹20,000 × 0.9 + ₹6,286 = ₹18,000 + ₹6,286 = ₹24,286. Total received = ₹56,574 + ₹24,286 = ₹80,860, which equals 90 % of the full contract value (₹90,000) minus the final 10 % retention that may be released later.

Partial acceptance occurs when the client accepts a portion of the work before full completion, triggering a pro‑rata release of the corresponding milestone. If the contract says “upon acceptance of each completed floor, 5 % of contract value is released”, and the building has 10 floors, each floor acceptance releases ₹5,000. If 3 floors are accepted, ₹15,000 is released regardless of overall percentage. This decouples payment from global progress and ties it to discrete deliverables. Problems may combine both: a global milestone at 50 % overall progress plus per‑floor releases. Compute each stream separately and sum.

Penalty clauses for delay may be expressed as a fixed amount per day of delay beyond the contractual completion date. If the contract duration is 100 days and the actual finish is 110 days, penalty = 10 × daily penalty. The penalty may be capped at a maximum percentage of contract value. When penalty and retention interact, the penalty is usually deducted from the retention pool first, then from the contractor’s dues. Read the wording: “penalty shall be recovered from retention money” means reduce the retention balance; if retention is insufficient, the balance is recovered from subsequent payments.

Bonus for early completion mirrors penalty: a fixed amount per day saved, often shared among the contractor and key subcontractors in a predefined ratio. If the bonus pool is ₹50,000 and the sharing ratio is 3:2 (contractor:subcontractor), and the project finishes 5 days early with a bonus of ₹2,000 per day, total bonus = ₹10,000. Contractor gets 3/5 × 10,000 = ₹6,000; subcontractor gets ₹4,000. Add these to the respective final settlements after retention release.

  • Assuming average effective rate works for short horizons where breakdown alignment matters.
  • Forgetting that the final breakdown may coincide with job completion, so its repair time should not be added.
  • Treating retention money as an extra payment instead of a withheld portion of each milestone.
  • Applying linear interpolation between milestones when the contract specifies “payment only on exact milestone achievement”.
  • Mixing up penalty deduction order: penalty first from retention, then from dues, not the reverse.
  • Ignoring that bonus sharing ratios apply to the bonus pool only, not to the whole contract value.
  • Using the nominal rate instead of the effective rate when a machine has a work‑triggered breakdown schedule.

Work with Piecewise Constant Efficiency and Phase Transitions

Many real‑world projects do not run at a single steady rate; the workforce or machinery may change efficiency at predetermined milestones. A typical model divides the total duration into phases, each with its own constant efficiency. If a project of total work W is split into k phases, phase i lasts tᵢ time units and the combined efficiency during that phase is Eᵢ. The work completed in phase i is Eᵢ × tᵢ. The sum over all phases must equal W: Σ(Eᵢ tᵢ) = W. When phase boundaries are given in terms of work completed (e.g., “after 30 % of the work the efficiency drops by 20 %”), convert the percentage to an absolute work amount using the LCM of the base efficiencies, then compute the time spent in each phase by dividing the phase work by the phase efficiency.

A common twist is a “learning‑curve” phase where efficiency rises linearly from a start value E₀ to a peak E₁ over a known work quantity ΔW. Because the rate of change is constant with respect to work, the average efficiency during that interval is (E₀ + E₁) ÷ 2. The time for the learning interval is ΔW ÷ average efficiency. After the learning interval the efficiency stays at E₁ until the next transition. If the problem states that the efficiency drops by a fixed percentage after a certain calendar time rather than after a work amount, treat the calendar time as a hard boundary: compute work done up to that moment with the old efficiency, then continue with the new efficiency for the remaining work.

When multiple workers each have their own phase schedule, align the schedules on a common time axis. The combined efficiency at any instant is the sum of the individual efficiencies active at that instant. Break the timeline at every instant where any worker changes efficiency; each sub‑interval then has a constant combined efficiency. Sum the work contributions of all sub‑intervals to obtain the total completion time. This piecewise‑constant approach avoids solving differential equations and fits neatly into the LCM framework used throughout the lesson.

Work with Overlapping Shifts and Shift Handover Losses

In continuous operations (factories, data centres, pipeline monitoring) work proceeds in overlapping shifts. Suppose shift A works 8 hours, shift B starts 2 hours before shift A ends, and shift C starts 2 hours before shift B ends. The overlap creates periods where two shifts are simultaneously active, effectively doubling the workforce for those hours. Model each shift as a worker with a known efficiency e. During an overlap of length Δt the combined efficiency is 2e; during solo periods it is e. The total work done in a 24‑hour cycle is the sum of (efficiency × duration) over all sub‑intervals.

Handovers often incur a loss: the outgoing shift spends the last h minutes briefing the incoming shift, during which its effective efficiency drops to a fraction α (0 ≤ α ≤ 1) of the normal rate. The incoming shift may also start at a reduced efficiency β for the first h minutes. If the handover duration is h minutes, the work lost relative to a seamless handover is (1 − α) e h + (1 − β) e h. Convert minutes to the same time unit used for the rest of the problem (usually hours) before inserting into the LCM calculation.

When the number of shifts per day is not an integer multiple of the shift length, the pattern repeats over a longer cycle (e.g., a 3‑shift 8‑hour schedule repeats every 24 hours, but a 2‑shift 12‑hour schedule with a 1‑hour overlap repeats every 23 hours). Determine the least common multiple of the cycle length and the project horizon, then multiply the per‑cycle work by the number of full cycles and add the work of the remaining partial cycle. This method mirrors the “alternate day work” technique but with a longer, shift‑defined cycle.

Technique: Overlap‑Adjusted LCM Cycle
A factory runs two 12‑hour shifts per day. Shift 1 works 06:00–18:00, shift 2 works 17:00–05:00 (next day). Each shift has an efficiency of 50 units/hour. During the 1‑hour overlap (17:00–18:00) both shifts are active, but the handover reduces each shift’s efficiency to 60 % for that hour. The tank to be filled requires 12 000 units. How many calendar days are needed?
Step 1: Compute work per 24‑hour cycle. Solo hours: shift 1 solo 11 h (06:00–17:00), shift 2 solo 11 h (18:00–05:00). Overlap hour: both at 0.6 × 50 = 30 units/h each, combined 60 units/h. Work = 11 × 50 + 11 × 50 + 1 × 60 = 550 + 550 + 60 = 1 160 units per cycle. Step 2: Number of full cycles = floor(12 000 ÷ 1 160) = 10 cycles → 11 600 units. Remaining work = 400 units. Step 3: Simulate the next cycle from 06:00. Shift 1 solo produces 50 units/h. After 8 h (14:00) it has produced 400 units. Completion occurs at 14:00 on the 11th day. Answer: 10 full days + 8 hours = 10 ⅓ days (or 10 days 8 hours).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem describes overlapping shifts, draw a 24‑hour timeline and mark solo and overlap blocks. Convert every block into “efficiency‑hours” (efficiency × hours). Sum the blocks to get work per cycle. Use the LCM of the cycle work and total work only if the numbers are large; otherwise simple division and a short simulation of the remainder is faster and less error‑prone.

Work with Resource Constraints and Bottleneck Identification

Some projects are limited not by labour but by a scarce resource such as a single crane, a testing rig, or a specialised licence. Suppose a project consists of n tasks, each requiring a certain amount of the scarce resource for a known duration. The resource can serve only one task at a time. The minimum project duration equals the sum of the resource‑occupied durations plus any idle time forced by task dependencies. This is a classic single‑machine scheduling problem; to minimise maximum lateness, order tasks by earliest due date; to minimise average completion time, order by shortest processing time. Makespan (total completion time) is fixed as the sum of processing times if no idle time is forced. When tasks have precedence constraints, construct a directed acyclic graph and compute the critical path where the resource is the bottleneck.

In quantitative aptitude questions the resource constraint often appears as “only one pipe can be opened at a time” or “only one worker can use the machine”. Treat the resource as a worker with efficiency equal to the rate of the task it serves. If multiple tasks share the resource, the effective combined efficiency is the efficiency of the currently active task. The total work is the sum of the individual task works. The schedule that minimises total time is to run the tasks back‑to‑back in any order (since the resource is never idle) unless a task cannot start before a certain calendar time (release date). In that case, insert idle gaps and add them to the makespan.

A frequent examiner twist: the resource itself has a maintenance window (e.g., the crane is unavailable 2 hours every 24 hours). Model the maintenance as a zero‑efficiency period in the resource’s cycle. Compute the effective resource availability per cycle = (cycle length − maintenance duration) ÷ cycle length. Multiply the nominal resource rate by this availability factor to obtain an average effective rate, then apply the LCM method. Verify whether the final maintenance window coincides with project completion; if it does, the maintenance time after completion should not be counted.

Work with Stochastic Breakdown and Expected Completion Time

When equipment fails randomly, the completion time becomes a random variable. A standard textbook model assumes breakdowns follow a Poisson process with rate λ (breakdowns per hour) and each repair takes a fixed deterministic time r. The machine works at efficiency e when operational. The expected fraction of time the machine is up is 1 ÷ (1 + λr). Hence the long‑run average effective efficiency is e ÷ (1 + λr). For a finite job of work W, the expected completion time is approximately W ÷ (e ÷ (1 + λr)) = W(1 + λr) ÷ e, provided the job is long enough for the law of large numbers to apply. If the job is short (expected number of breakdowns < 1), the exact distribution matters: the probability of zero breakdowns is e^(−λT) where T is the nominal time without breakdowns (W ÷ e). The expected time then becomes T + λrT = T(1 + λr) to first order, matching the long‑run formula.

When multiple machines operate in parallel and each has its own breakdown process, the combined effective efficiency is the sum of the individual effective efficiencies because breakdowns are independent. If a breakdown of one machine forces the whole line to stop (series system), the system up‑time is the product of individual up‑times, and the effective efficiency is approx. e_line × ∏(1/(1+λᵢrᵢ)). Problems rarely require the exact product; exams often use the additive approximation for parallel machines and the multiplicative approximation for series lines.

Examiners may ask for the “most likely” completion time rather than the expectation. For a Poisson breakdown process the mode of the completion‑time distribution is close to the nominal time plus the most likely number of breakdowns (which is floor(λT)). Compute the nominal time T = W ÷ e, find k = floor(λT), then most likely time ≈ T + k r. This shortcut avoids integration and is used in competitive‑exam solutions.

Work with Contractual Incentive Structures: Penalties, Bonuses, and Escalation Clauses

Beyond simple milestone payments, contracts often contain penalty clauses for delay, bonus clauses for early finish, and price‑escalation clauses tied to input‑cost indices. A penalty of ₹P per day of delay beyond the contractual date D reduces the contractor’s net receipt. If the actual finish is F (> D), penalty = P × (F − D). The penalty is usually capped at a percentage C of the contract value V: effective penalty = min(P × (F − D), C × V). A bonus of ₹B per day saved (finish before D) is similarly capped. When both penalty and bonus exist, only one applies depending on whether F > D or F < D.

Escalation clauses adjust the contract value based on an index I (e.g., wholesale price index). If the base index at contract signing is I₀ and the index at payment milestone m is Iₘ, the escalation factor is Iₘ ÷ I₀. The milestone amount Mₘ becomes Mₘ × (Iₘ ÷ I₀). When multiple milestones occur at different dates, compute each factor separately. Retention money (commonly 5‑10 %) is withheld from each escalated milestone payment. At final settlement the retained amount is released, sometimes with interest at a stipulated rate.

A common quantitative problem combines these elements: given a contract value, milestone schedule, penalty/bonus rates, escalation indices, and retention percentage, compute the net amount received by the contractor at a specific progress percentage. The solution steps are: (1) determine earned value at that progress using linear interpolation between milestones; (2) apply escalation factor for each milestone that has been reached; (3) deduct retention from each paid milestone; (4) if the project is complete, add bonus or subtract penalty, applying the cap; (5) release retention (with interest if specified). Keep all monetary values in the same unit and round only at the final step.

  • Treating a piecewise‑efficiency phase change as a simple average without weighting by the work done in each phase.
  • Ignoring handover loss minutes when converting shift overlaps into efficiency‑hours.
  • Assuming the scarce resource can be shared simultaneously; the resource is single‑threaded unless the problem explicitly states parallel capacity.
  • Using the long‑run average efficiency for a very short job where the probability of zero breakdowns is high.
  • Applying penalty and bonus simultaneously instead of mutually exclusively based on actual finish date.
  • Forgetting to escalate each milestone by its own index date before summing payments.
  • Counting a maintenance window that occurs after the project’s nominal completion as lost time.
