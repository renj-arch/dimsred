What is Number Series?

Number series questions present a sequence of numbers following a specific pattern. You must identify the pattern and find the next term or the wrong term in the series. These questions are common in SBI Clerk prelims and test your numerical aptitude and pattern recognition speed.

Common Patterns in Number Series

1. Arithmetic Progression (AP): The difference between consecutive terms is constant. Example: 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 (difference +4).

2. Geometric Progression (GP): Each term is multiplied by a fixed number. Example: 2, 6, 18, 54 (multiplied by 3).

3. Square/Cube Series: Terms are squares or cubes of natural numbers. Example: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 (1², 2², 3², 4², 5²).

4. Prime Number Series: Terms are prime numbers. Example: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13.

5. Mixed Operation Series: Each term is derived by a combination of operations like ×n ± m. Example: 2, 5, 11, 23, 47 (×2+1, ×2+1, ...).

6. Fibonacci Series: Each term is the sum of the two preceding terms. Example: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13.

7. Digit-Based Series: Pattern based on digits of numbers. Example: 12, 21, 13, 31, 14, 41 (swap digits).

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Find the Next Term)
Find the next term: 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, ?

a) 127 b) 125 c) 129 d) 126
Solution: a) 127. Pattern: ×2+1. 3×2+1=7, 7×2+1=15, 15×2+1=31, 31×2+1=63, 63×2+1=127.
Example 2 (Mixed Operation)
Find the next term: 5, 9, 20, 43, 90, ?

a) 185 b) 180 c) 190 d) 175
Solution: a) 185. Pattern: ×2-1, ×2+2, ×2+3, ×2+4, ... 5×2-1=9, 9×2+2=20, 20×2+3=43, 43×2+4=90, 90×2+5=185.
Example 3 (Find the Wrong Term)
Find the wrong term: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, 42, 56

a) 12 b) 20 c) 42 d) 56
Solution: d) 56. Pattern: 1×2=2, 2×3=6, 3×4=12, 4×5=20, 5×6=30, 6×7=42, 7×8=56.

Shortcut Techniques

  • First check if differences between consecutive terms are constant (AP) or increasing/decreasing by a pattern.
  • If differences don't show a pattern, check ratios — the series could be GP or mixed operation.
  • Check for alternate patterns — odd-position terms may follow one pattern, even-position another.
  • Learn squares up to 30, cubes up to 15, and prime numbers up to 100 for quick identification.
Pro Tip
In SBI Clerk, number series often include patterns like '×1+1, ×2+2, ×3+3' or '×2-1, ×3-2, ×4-3'. Always look for the operation on the term and a changing adjustment. If 5 seconds of inspection doesn't reveal the pattern, move on and come back later.

Difference Series and Second‑Order Differences

When the first‑order differences (the gaps between consecutive terms) do not form a constant or simple arithmetic progression, the next diagnostic step is to examine the second‑order differences — the differences of the first‑order differences. If the second‑order differences become constant, the original series follows a quadratic pattern of the form an² + bn + c. For example, the series 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 yields first differences 4, 6, 8, 10 and second differences 2, 2, 2, confirming a quadratic rule n² + n. In many competitive papers the quadratic coefficient a is 1 or ½, so recognising the constant second difference instantly narrows the search to a handful of candidate formulas.

If the second‑order differences themselves form an arithmetic progression, the underlying rule is cubic (an³ + bn² + cn + d). A typical cubic series such as 1, 8, 27, 64, 125 produces first differences 7, 19, 37, 61 and second differences 12, 18, 24 — an arithmetic progression with common difference 6. The constant third difference (6) signals a cubic term with coefficient 1. Examiners often hide the cubic nature by inserting a small linear perturbation (e.g., + n) so that the third difference remains constant but the second differences are not perfectly linear; checking the third difference eliminates this trap.

Higher‑order differences are rarely required beyond the third order in standard banking examinations, but the principle extends indefinitely: a constant k‑th order difference indicates a polynomial of degree k. Practically, aspirants should compute up to third differences within the first 30 seconds; if no constant appears, the series is likely non‑polynomial (exponential, factorial, digit‑based, or mixed). A useful shortcut is to write the terms in a column, subtract downwards, and repeat — this visual “difference table” makes pattern spotting faster than mental arithmetic.

Exceptions arise when the series alternates between two different polynomial rules (interleaved series) or when a polynomial is combined with a multiplicative factor (e.g., n² × 2ⁿ). In such hybrid cases the pure difference method fails after the first few rows because the multiplicative component introduces a geometric growth that contaminates the differences. The remedy is to separate the series into its constituent sub‑series (odd‑position terms, even‑position terms) before applying difference analysis.

Alternate and Interleaved Series

Many number‑series questions conceal two independent progressions running in parallel, one occupying the odd positions (1st, 3rd, 5th …) and the other the even positions (2nd, 4th, 6th …). Recognising an interleaved structure prevents the common error of forcing a single rule on the whole sequence. The first step is to split the given terms into two sub‑series and test each for standard patterns — arithmetic, geometric, quadratic, prime‑based, or digit‑manipulation.

For instance, the series 3, 4, 9, 8, 27, 16, 81 separates into odd‑position terms 3, 9, 27, 81 (×3 each step) and even‑position terms 4, 8, 16 (×2 each step). The next term belongs to the even sub‑series, so the answer is 32. Examiners frequently increase difficulty by letting the two sub‑series share a common operation but with different parameters, e.g., odd terms follow ×2+1 while even terms follow ×2‑1. In such cases the difference table of the combined series shows no regularity, but the split series each reveal a clean linear recurrence.

A second variant uses three interleaved strands (positions 1,4,7…; 2,5,8…; 3,6,9…). The same splitting principle applies: write the terms in three columns and analyse each column independently. This pattern appears in higher‑level clerk mains where time pressure is severe; a quick visual grouping (e.g., circling every third term) saves precious seconds.

Traps include series where the interleaving is not perfectly regular — for example, a missing term in one strand shifts the alignment. Always verify that the split produces equal‑length sub‑series (or differs by at most one term). If the lengths mismatch, the series may be a “broken” interleaving where a term has been omitted deliberately; the missing‑term question then asks for the omitted value, which can be found by reconstructing the intended sub‑series.

Worked Example – Interleaved Series
Find the missing term: 5, 2, 11, 4, 23, 8, ?
Split into odd positions: 5, 11, 23 → differences 6, 12 (×2). The pattern is ×2+1? 5×2+1=11, 11×2+1=23, so next odd term would be 23×2+1=47. Even positions: 2, 4, 8 → each term doubles. The missing term is the 7th position (odd), therefore answer = 47.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a series looks chaotic, immediately write the terms in two (or three) columns by position. If each column yields a recognisable pattern within 10 seconds, you have an interleaved series. Do not waste time trying to force a single rule on the combined line.

Polynomial and Power‑Based Series

Beyond simple quadratics and cubics, examiners embed series generated by explicit polynomial formulas such as n³ + n², 2n² + 3n + 1, or n⁴ − n². The hallmark of a pure polynomial series is that the k‑th order differences become constant at the degree k. For a quartic (degree 4) the fourth differences are constant; however, banking papers rarely go beyond cubic because the numbers explode quickly. When a quartic does appear, the first few terms are usually small (e.g., 1, 16, 81, 256 … = n⁴) and the constant fourth difference equals 24 × leading coefficient.

Power‑based series where the exponent itself varies (e.g., 2¹, 3², 4³, 5⁴ …) are classified as “exponential‑polynomial hybrids”. Their growth outpaces any fixed‑degree polynomial, so the difference table never stabilises. The detection cue is a rapidly widening gap between successive ratios: the ratio of consecutive terms increases rather than approaching a constant. A quick test — compute termₙ₊₁ / termₙ for the first three pairs; if the ratios themselves grow in a regular fashion (e.g., 2, 3, 4 …), the series is likely of the form (n+1)ⁿ or similar.

Another common family uses binomial coefficients: C(n,2), C(n,3), C(n,4) … which produce triangular numbers, tetrahedral numbers, etc. These are polynomial in disguise (degree 2, 3, 4 respectively) and therefore succumb to difference analysis. Recognising the combinatorial origin can shortcut the algebra: the nth triangular number is n(n+1)/2, the nth tetrahedral number is n(n+1)(n+2)/6.

Examiner twists include adding a constant offset to a polynomial (e.g., n² + 5) or multiplying the polynomial by a fixed integer (e.g., 3n²). Both operations preserve the order at which differences become constant, so the difference table remains a reliable diagnostic. However, adding a term that itself follows a different polynomial (e.g., n² + 2ⁿ) destroys the constant‑difference property; such hybrids are treated in the next section.

Series Involving Factorials and Combinatorial Patterns

Factorial growth (1, 2, 6, 24, 120, 720 …) is unmistakable because the ratio of successive terms equals the index: termₙ₊₁ / termₙ = n+1. When a series displays ratios 2, 3, 4, 5 … it is almost certainly factorial or a simple multiple thereof (e.g., 2·n!). A frequent variation multiplies each factorial by a linear factor: n! × (n+1) yields 2, 6, 24, 120, 720 … which coincides with (n+1)!. Recognising the equivalence prevents unnecessary computation.

Combinatorial series such as “choose” numbers appear as C(n, k) for fixed k while n increments. For k = 2 the series is 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 … (triangular numbers). For k = 3 the series is 1, 4, 10, 20, 35 … (tetrahedral numbers). These are polynomial of degree k, so the k‑th differences are constant. A quick identification method: write the first five terms, compute successive differences until a constant row appears; the order of that row equals k.

Mixed factorial‑polynomial series (e.g., n! + n²) are rare but possible in mains‑level papers. Their ratios do not follow a simple integer progression, and difference tables never stabilise because the factorial component dominates. The practical approach is to test the first three terms against known factorial values; if they match n! exactly, assume pure factorial. If they deviate by a predictable polynomial amount, subtract the factorial part and analyse the remainder.

Digit‑factorial hybrids (e.g., sum of factorials of digits) belong to the “digit‑based” family and are covered elsewhere. The key takeaway for factorial series is the ratio test: compute term₂/term₁, term₃/term₂, term₄/term₃. If the results are consecutive integers starting from 2, you have a factorial core.

  • Assuming a single polynomial rule when the series is actually interleaved — always split by position first.
  • Stopping at first‑order differences; many quadratic and cubic series require second‑ or third‑order checks.
  • Misidentifying a factorial series as exponential because the numbers grow fast — verify by ratio test.
  • Overlooking a constant offset added to a polynomial; the difference table still works, but the constant term changes the first few values.
  • Treating a hybrid factorial‑polynomial as pure factorial and ignoring the polynomial remainder.
  • Forgetting that a constant k‑th difference implies a degree‑k polynomial, not necessarily a simple nᵏ.
  • Ignoring the possibility of a broken interleaving where a missing term shifts the alignment of sub‑series.

Mastering these advanced patterns equips you to dissect any number series within the time limits of competitive examinations.

Recursive and Self‑Referential Series

A recursive series defines each term as a function of one or more preceding terms. The most familiar example is the Fibonacci sequence where termₙ = termₙ₋₁ + termₙ₋₂ with seeds 0, 1. In competitive papers the recursion may be hidden behind a description such as “each term after the second equals the sum of the previous two terms multiplied by 2”. The first step is to write the recurrence explicitly, then generate a few terms to see whether the given numbers match. When the recurrence involves a variable coefficient (e.g., termₙ = n × termₙ₋₁ + 1) the series grows faster than any fixed‑order polynomial, and the difference table will never stabilise. A practical detection cue is to compute the ratio termₙ / termₙ₋₁ for the first four terms; if the ratios themselves follow a simple arithmetic progression (2, 3, 4 …) the recurrence is likely of the form termₙ = n × termₙ₋₁ + c. Another common pattern is a second‑order linear recurrence with constant coefficients: termₙ = a·termₙ₋₁ + b·termₙ₋₂. Its characteristic equation r² – a r – b = 0 yields roots r₁, r₂; the closed form is termₙ = α·r₁ⁿ + β·r₂ⁿ. Even without solving the quadratic, the ratio of successive terms quickly approaches the dominant root, so the series behaves like a geometric progression after a few steps. Examiners often ask for the missing term in a long recursive series where only the first five terms are shown. The fastest method is to reconstruct the recurrence from the given terms, verify it on the known part, then apply it once to obtain the answer. When two different recurrences can produce the same initial segment, the one with the smallest integer coefficients is preferred.

Series with Modular Arithmetic and Remainder Patterns

Modular series exploit the fact that remainders repeat with a fixed period. A typical construction takes a base sequence (often a simple arithmetic or geometric progression) and replaces each term by its remainder modulo m. For example, the series 2, 4, 1, 3, 0, 2, 4 … is the sequence of powers of 2 modulo 5: 2¹≡2, 2²≡4, 2³≡3, 2⁴≡1, 2⁵≡2 … The period here is 4 because 2⁴≡1 (mod 5). In general, for any integer a coprime to m, the order of a modulo m divides φ(m) (Euler’s totient). Recognising the modulus is the key: compute successive differences; if they do not stabilise but the values stay within a small range (0 … m‑1), suspect a modular reduction. A second family adds a constant before taking the remainder: termₙ = (a·n + b) mod m. This produces a perfectly regular “saw‑tooth” pattern that repeats every m / gcd(a,m) steps. A third family uses the remainder of a polynomial: termₙ = (n² + 3n + 1) mod 7. Because a polynomial of degree d modulo m eventually becomes periodic with period dividing m, the series will show a repeating block after at most m terms. The examiner’s twist is to hide the modulus by presenting the series as ordinary integers that happen to be the remainders of a larger hidden sequence. The giveaway is that the maximum term never exceeds a certain bound and the pattern of gaps repeats exactly. To solve, write the first 12‑15 terms, look for the smallest repeating block, then deduce the modulus and the underlying generator.

Worked Example – Modular Arithmetic Series
Find the missing term: 3, 6, 2, 5, 1, 4, ?, 3, 6 …
Observe that all terms lie between 1 and 6. The block 3,6,2,5,1,4 repeats after six terms. The missing term is the seventh element, which starts the second block, so it equals the first element of the block: 3. The underlying rule is termₙ = (3n) mod 7, with the convention that remainder 0 is written as 7; the sequence of remainders 3,6,2,5,1,4,0,3,6 … becomes 3,6,2,5,1,4,7,3,6 … but the paper replaces 7 by 3 to keep the range 1‑6. Hence the answer is 3.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Modular Series
When a series stays inside a narrow band (e.g., 0‑9) and shows an exact repetition after 5‑12 terms, immediately write down the repeating block. Count the block length; the missing term is the element at the same position inside the block. This avoids any need to discover the hidden modulus or generator.

Series Involving Prime Numbers and Prime Gaps

Prime‑based series appear in two flavours: the terms themselves are primes, or the terms are derived from the gaps between consecutive primes. A direct prime series may list the first few primes (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 …) or a subsequence such as “primes of the form 6k ± 1” (5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19 …). The examiner can also ask for the nth prime, which is not feasible to compute mentally beyond the first 15‑20 primes; therefore the question will either provide a short list and ask for the next prime, or give a rule like “the nth term is the smallest prime greater than n²”. In the latter case, generate n² for the required n and test successive integers for primality using divisibility up to √candidate. The second flavour uses prime gaps: 1, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 2, 4, 6, 2 … (differences between successive primes). These gaps are irregular but bounded by known results (e.g., gaps ≤ 282 for primes below 10⁶). In a test, the gap series will be short (≤10 terms) and the missing term will be a gap that can be inferred from the surrounding primes. A useful shortcut: write the primes that correspond to the given gaps, then read the next gap. For instance, if the series is 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, ?, the underlying primes are 3,5,7,11,13,17,19 … giving gaps 2,2,4,2,4,2 … so the answer is 2. Hybrid series may combine a polynomial with a prime indicator, such as termₙ = n² + pₙ where pₙ is the nth prime. The growth is dominated by the quadratic part, but the prime addition creates a “jitter” that prevents constant differences. The detection cue is that the second differences are almost constant but show a small periodic deviation matching the prime gaps.

Series Based on Digit Manipulation and Base Conversions

Digit‑manipulation series treat each term as a string of decimal digits and apply a fixed transformation to obtain the next term. Common operations include reversing the digits (123 → 321), adding the sum of digits (123 → 123+6=129), multiplying the digits (123 → 1×2×3=6), or concatenating the term with its reverse (12 → 1221). Because the operation is deterministic, the series either enters a cycle or grows without bound. A typical exam question shows the first five terms and asks for the sixth; the candidate must infer the rule by comparing successive terms. For example, 12, 21, 12, 21 … clearly alternates by reversal. A more subtle rule: 13, 16, 22, 26, 38 … where each term adds the product of its digits (13+3=16, 16+6=22, 22+4=26, 26+12=38). The product‑of‑digits rule is identified by computing termₙ₊₁ – termₙ and checking whether the difference equals the product of the digits of termₙ. Base‑conversion series present numbers that are actually representations in another base. For instance, the series 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11 … is simply the natural numbers written in base 8. The giveaway is the sudden appearance of a digit equal to the base (here 8 never appears, but 10 appears after 7). To solve, hypothesize a base b, convert the given terms to decimal, and verify whether they follow a simple decimal pattern (usually consecutive integers). Another variant writes a known decimal sequence (squares, cubes, Fibonacci) in base b. The detection method: compute the decimal values of the first few terms assuming base b; if they match a familiar sequence, the base is correct. Examiners may also mix digit manipulation with base conversion, e.g., “write n² in base 5, then reverse the digits”. The solution proceeds in two stages: generate the decimal squares, convert to base 5, reverse, and read the result as a base‑5 number (or keep it as a string). Because the numbers stay small (usually ≤3 digits in the target base), manual conversion is fast.

  • Assuming a recursive series is polynomial and applying difference tables that never stabilise.
  • Overlooking the modulus in a modular series and trying to fit a polynomial to the remainders.
  • Treating a prime‑gap series as a simple arithmetic progression and missing the irregular jumps.
  • Ignoring the possibility that a digit‑manipulation rule changes after a few terms (e.g., sum of digits for the first three terms, then product of digits).
  • Confusing base‑b representation with decimal values and performing arithmetic in the wrong base.
  • Failing to verify that a detected recurrence works for all given terms before extrapolating.

Mastering these advanced families ensures that any unfamiliar number series can be decomposed into a known pattern within the time limits of the examination.

Series Built from Recursive Polynomial Coefficients

In many advanced number‑series questions the coefficient of the dominant polynomial term is not constant but follows its own linear recurrence. A typical construction is termₙ = cₙ·n² + dₙ·n + eₙ where the sequences {cₙ}, {dₙ}, {eₙ} each satisfy a first‑order or second‑order recurrence such as cₙ = cₙ₋₁ + cₙ₋₂ (Fibonacci‑type) or dₙ = 2·dₙ₋₁ − dₙ₋₂ (arithmetic progression of differences). Because the polynomial degree is low (usually 2 or 3) the overall growth is still polynomial, yet the ordinary difference table never becomes constant; instead the second‑order differences inherit the recurrence of the coefficients. The detection cue is to compute successive differences until a pattern emerges that is itself a known recurrence. For example, if the second differences are 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 … the Fibonacci recurrence is revealed, and the next second difference is 13. Adding this to the last first difference and then to the last term yields the missing value. When the coefficient recurrence is of order two, the series can be solved by writing the general term as a linear combination of n²·Fₙ, n·Fₙ and Fₙ where Fₙ is the Fibonacci sequence, then fitting the first three given terms to determine the combination constants. This method works because the space of sequences generated by polynomial‑times‑Fibonacci is closed under the forward‑difference operator. In examinations the series length is usually six to eight terms, enough to expose the coefficient recurrence but short enough to keep manual calculation feasible. A practical shortcut: write the terms, compute first differences, then second differences; if the second differences follow a simple recurrence (constant, arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, Lucas, or any linear recurrence with small integer coefficients) you have identified the hidden coefficient pattern. Once the recurrence is known, extend it one step, reconstruct the next first difference, and finally the next term. This technique also applies when the polynomial degree is three and the leading coefficient follows a recurrence; then the third differences carry the recurrence.

Series Involving Continued Fraction Convergents

Continued‑fraction convergents provide a rich source of integer sequences that appear in competitive examinations. The convergents of √2 are 1/1, 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, 41/29, 99/70 …; the numerators satisfy Nₙ = 2·Nₙ₋₁ + Nₙ₋₂ with N₀ = 1, N₁ = 3, and the denominators satisfy the same recurrence with D₀ = 1, D₁ = 2. A series may present only the numerators (1, 3, 7, 17, 41, ?) or only the denominators, or it may interleave them (1, 1, 3, 2, 7, 5, 17, 12 …). The recurrence is linear with constant coefficients, so the standard characteristic‑equation method gives the closed form Nₙ = ((1+√2)ⁿ⁺¹ + (1−√2)ⁿ⁺¹)/2. In an exam you rarely need the closed form; recognizing the recurrence 2·previous + previous‑previous is sufficient. Other quadratic irrationals generate similar recurrences: √3 gives Nₙ = 2·Nₙ₋₁ + 2·Nₙ₋₂, √5 gives Nₙ = 4·Nₙ₋₁ + Nₙ₋₂, and the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2 yields the Fibonacci recurrence Nₙ = Nₙ₋₁ + Nₙ₋₂. The constant term in the continued fraction (the periodic part) determines the coefficients of the recurrence. A useful detection strategy: compute the ratio of successive terms; if the ratios converge quickly to a quadratic surd (≈1.414, 1.732, 2.236, 1.618) the series is likely a convergent sequence. Then test whether the terms satisfy a second‑order linear recurrence with integer coefficients by solving for a, b in termₙ = a·termₙ₋₁ + b·termₙ₋₂ using the first three terms. If a and b are small integers (typically ≤4) the hypothesis is confirmed. Examiners sometimes mix convergents of two different surds, e.g., 1, 3, 2, 7, 5, 17, 12 … where odd positions are √2 numerators and even positions are √2 denominators. In such interleaved series, separate the two subsequences first, then apply the recurrence test to each. Another twist: the series may give the convergents of e = [2;1,2,1,1,4,1,1,6,…] whose numerators and denominators follow a more complicated but still linear recurrence with periodic coefficients. The periodic pattern (1,2,1,1,4,1,1,6,…) can be spotted by writing the continued‑fraction expansion and then using the standard recurrence pₙ = aₙ·pₙ₋₁ + pₙ₋₂, qₙ = aₙ·qₙ₋₁ + qₙ₋₂ where aₙ are the partial quotients. Because the partial quotients are small and follow a clear pattern, the resulting integer sequence can be generated quickly on paper.

Worked Example: Interleaved √2 Convergents
Find the missing term in the series: 1, 1, 3, 2, 7, 5, 17, 12, 41, ?
Separate odd‑position terms (1, 3, 7, 17, 41) and even‑position terms (1, 2, 5, 12). The odd subsequence follows Nₙ = 2·Nₙ₋₁ + Nₙ₋₂: 3 = 2·1+1, 7 = 2·3+1, 17 = 2·7+3, 41 = 2·17+7. The next odd term would be 2·41+17 = 99. The even subsequence follows the same recurrence with seeds 1, 2: 5 = 2·2+1, 12 = 2·5+2. The next even term is 2·12+5 = 29. Since the missing term is at position 10 (even), the answer is 29.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a series shows rapid convergence of successive ratios to a known surd, immediately write down the first four terms of the corresponding convergent sequence (numerators and denominators). Compare with the given series; if they match or interleave, the recurrence is almost certainly the standard second‑order linear one. This avoids lengthy difference tables and saves precious seconds.

Series with Alternating Operations and Conditional Rules

A frequent advanced pattern applies two or more distinct arithmetic operations in a fixed cyclic order, or chooses the operation based on a property of the current term (parity, magnitude, digit sum, etc.). For instance, the rule “if the term is even, divide by 2; if odd, multiply by 3 and add 1” generates the Collatz trajectory, but examiners use simpler deterministic cycles such as “add 5, multiply by 2, subtract 3, divide by 2” repeated every four steps. The series 4, 9, 18, 15, 20, 25, 50, 47 … follows +5, ×2, −3, ÷2 cyclically. Detection: compute the successive operations termₙ₊₁ → termₙ; if the operations repeat with a short period (2–6) you have found the cycle. Write the operations as a list and verify that applying the list to the first term reproduces the entire given sequence. Conditional rules are trickier: the operation may depend on whether the term is a perfect square, a multiple of 3, or has a certain digital root. Example: 2, 4, 8, 16, 5, 10, 20, 40, 13 … where the rule is “double the term; if the result exceeds 30, replace it by the sum of its digits”. The first four terms double normally (2→4→8→16). Doubling 16 gives 32 > 30, so replace 32 by 3+2 = 5. Then double 5→10→20→40; 40 > 30 → 4+0 = 4? But the series shows 13, so the rule might be “if >30, subtract 27”. The key is to test simple hypotheses: threshold‑based replacement, digit‑sum, digit‑product, modulo reduction. Because the condition is usually based on a single easily checked property (even/odd, >k, multiple of m), you can scan the series for the first point where the obvious pattern breaks; that breakpoint reveals the condition. Once the condition and the two (or three) operations are identified, extend the series by simulating the rule step by step. In examinations the series length is typically 8–10 terms, providing at least two full cycles of the alternating operations or two occurrences of the conditional branch, which is sufficient to confirm the rule.

Series Derived from Combinatorial Identities

Many number series are direct evaluations of well‑known combinatorial sequences: binomial coefficients, Catalan numbers, Stirling numbers, Bell numbers, or central binomial coefficients. The binomial row n=5 gives 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1; a series may present a diagonal of Pascal’s triangle such as the triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 … (C(n+1,2)) or the tetrahedral numbers 1, 4, 10, 20, 35 … (C(n+2,3)). Catalan numbers 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132 … satisfy Cₙ₊₁ = Σ Cᵢ·Cₙ₋ᵢ and also the closed form Cₙ = (1/(n+1))·C(2n,n). In a test you will rarely need the summation definition; the recurrence Cₙ₊₁ = (2(2n+1)/(n+2))·Cₙ is faster for mental computation. Stirling numbers of the second kind S(n,k) for fixed k produce polynomial‑like growth: S(n,2)=2ⁿ⁻¹−1, S(n,3)=½(3ⁿ⁻¹−2ⁿ+1). A series may list S(n,3) for n=3,4,5,6 … giving 1, 6, 25, 90 … Recognising the formula allows immediate prediction. Bell numbers Bₙ (number of set partitions) grow faster: 1, 1, 2, 5, 15, 52, 203 … and satisfy Bₙ₊₁ = Σ C(n,k)·Bₖ. The recurrence is cumbersome, but the first six terms are often memorised by serious aspirants. Central binomial coefficients C(2n,n) give 1, 2, 6, 20, 70, 252 … and appear in series where the term is the middle element of even rows of Pascal’s triangle. Detection strategy: compute ratios termₙ₊₁/termₙ; for Catalan the ratio tends to 4, for central binomial it tends to 4 as well but the exact rational values differ (Cₙ₊₁/Cₙ = 2(2n+1)/(n+2) vs C(2n+2,n+1)/C(2n,n) = 2(2n+1)/(n+1)). If the ratio sequence matches one of these rational patterns, the combinatorial identity is identified. Another clue: the terms are integers that appear in standard combinatorial tables; writing the first few rows of Pascal’s triangle or the Catalan triangle on scratch paper lets you spot the match instantly. Examiners may also embed a combinatorial sequence inside a polynomial wrapper, e.g., termₙ = n² + Cₙ. The polynomial part dominates for large n, but the early terms reveal the combinatorial “jitter”. Use the difference‑table method: the second differences of n² are constant (2), while the Catalan contribution adds a small varying amount; subtracting the constant 2 from the second differences isolates the Catalan second differences, which follow a known pattern.

Series Involving Modular Exponentiation and Cyclic Patterns

Modular exponentiation generates sequences that are eventually periodic, a property heavily exploited in number‑series questions. The classic example is aⁿ mod m for fixed a and m. For a=2, m=7 the powers are 2, 4, 1, 2, 4, 1 … with period 3. For a=3, m=10 the sequence is 3, 9, 7, 1, 3, 9, 7, 1 … period 4. The period (the multiplicative order of a modulo m) divides φ(m) when gcd(a,m)=1. In examinations the modulus is usually small (≤20) so the cycle can be written out manually within seconds. A series may present the first six terms of 5ⁿ mod 12: 5, 1, 5, 1, 5, 1 … and ask for the 10th term; the answer is 5 because the period is 2. More complex series combine two modular exponentiations, e.g., termₙ = (2ⁿ mod 7) + (3ⁿ mod 5). Compute each cycle separately (periods 3 and 4) then add termwise; the combined period is lcm(3,4)=12. The given series will usually show at least one full combined period (12 terms) or enough terms to deduce both individual cycles. Another variant uses the recurrence xₙ₊₁ = (a·xₙ + b) mod m (linear congruential generator). This produces a full period of m if parameters satisfy Hull‑Dobell conditions; the series may give the first 8–10 values and ask for the next. Because the recurrence is linear, you can recover a and b by solving two congruences from three consecutive terms: x₂ ≡ a·x₁ + b (mod m), x₃ ≡ a·x₂ + b (mod m). Subtract to get a·(x₂−x₁) ≡ x₃−x₂ (mod m); if gcd(x₂−x₁, m)=1 you can invert and find a, then b. When the modulus is not given, it can be inferred as the smallest integer that makes all terms fit a linear recurrence with constant coefficients modulo that integer. A practical shortcut: write the terms, compute successive differences; if the differences repeat after a few steps, the modulus is likely the length of the repeat or a divisor thereof. Examiners also use the sequence of remainders of n! modulo a prime p (Wilson‑type patterns). For p=7, n! mod 7 for n=1..6 is 1,2,6,3,1,6 … then 0 for n≥7. The sudden appearance of 0 signals that the factorial has reached the modulus. Recognising the factorial growth and the zero tail is a quick giveaway.

  • Assuming a single polynomial model when the coefficient itself follows a recurrence, leading to never‑stabilising difference tables.
  • Missing the interleaving of two convergent subsequences and trying to fit one recurrence to the combined series.
  • Overlooking a conditional branch that activates only after a threshold, causing the apparent pattern to break unexpectedly.
  • Confusing Catalan numbers with central binomial coefficients because both have ratio tending to 4, but their exact recurrences differ.
  • Failing to compute the combined period when two modular cycles are added, and instead extrapolating from the shorter individual cycle.
  • Treating a linear congruential generator as a simple arithmetic progression modulo m without solving for the multiplier and increment.
  • Ignoring the factorial‑mod‑prime zero tail and continuing to apply the pre‑zero recurrence beyond the modulus.
