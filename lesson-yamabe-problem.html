The Yamabe Problem — Conformal Geometry, Scalar Curvature & the Yamabe Invariant

Deep dive into the Yamabe Problem — the existence of a metric of constant scalar curvature in a conformal class on a compact Riemannian manifold (M^n, g), the Yamabe equation (the critical Sobolev exponent PDE: −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g u + R_g u = λ u^{(n+2)/(n−2)} with u > 0), the conformal transformation law for the scalar curvature R_{g̃} = u^{-(n+2)/(n−2)} (−4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g u + R_g u) where g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)} g, the Yamabe invariant Y(M, [g]) = inf_{g̃∈[g]} ∫ R_{g̃} dV_{g̃} / (Vol(M))^{(n−2)/n}, the solution of the Yamabe problem by Trudinger (1968), Aubin (1976), and Schoen (1984) — showing that Y(M) ≤ Y(S^n) and that if the inequality is strict, a solution exists, with the equality case only for the sphere (solved by the positive mass theorem), the classification of constant scalar curvature metrics (the Obata theorem: a conformal metric on S^n with constant scalar curvature is the round metric up to Möbius transformation), the Kazdan–Warner obstruction for prescribing scalar curvature on S² and higher spheres, and applications to the Riemann–Penrose inequality in general relativity (the ADM mass and the Hawking mass).

"The Yamabe problem is the jewel of conformal geometry — it asks whether every compact Riemannian manifold admits a conformal metric of constant scalar curvature."
— Richard Schoen
Part 1

🧩 Conformal Geometry and Scalar Curvature

Conformal
Conformal Metrics, the Yamabe Functional, and the Conformal Laplacian
Two metrics g and g̃ on a manifold M are conformal if g̃ = e^{2f} g for n = 2 or g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)} g for n ≥ 3, where u > 0 is a smooth function. The conformal transformation law for scalar curvature R_{g̃} = u^{-(n+2)/(n−2)} (−4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g u + R_g u). The conformal Laplacian L_g = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g + R_g is a conformally covariant operator: L_{g̃}(φ) = u^{-(n+2)/(n−2)} L_g(uφ). The Yamabe functional Q(g̃) = ∫ R_{g̃} dV_{g̃} / (Vol(M))^{(n−2)/n} is conformally invariant: Q(g̃) depends only on the conformal class [g]. The Yamabe invariant Y(M) = inf_{g̃∈[g]} Q(g̃) is a conformal invariant of the manifold — it depends only on the smooth structure of M. The round sphere S^n has Y(S^n) = n(n−1) ω_n^{2/n} where ω_n is the volume of the standard sphere. The Yamabe constant of a conformal class is the infimum of the Yamabe functional. A fundamental observation: Y(M) ≤ Y(S^n) for every compact manifold M (Aubin, 1976) — this follows from a test function calculation using a conformal factor u_ε that concentrates near a point.
The Yamabe functional is the natural energy functional for scalar curvature in a conformal class — its infimum is the Yamabe invariant, a topological invariant of the manifold.
Part 2

📐 The Yamabe Equation: A Critical Semilinear Elliptic PDE

PDE
The Yamabe Equation, the Sobolev Quotient, and the Euler–Lagrange Structure
The Euler–Lagrange equation for the Yamabe functional (restricted to metrics of unit volume) is the Yamabe equation: L_g u = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g u + R_g u = λ u^{(n+2)/(n−2)} = λ u^{p−1}, where p = 2n/(n−2) is the critical Sobolev exponent (the exponent for which the Sobolev embedding H¹ ↪ L^p is not compact). The constant λ is the Lagrange multiplier; it equals Q(g̃) when the metric is normalized. The Yamabe problem: given a compact Riemannian manifold (M^n, g) with n ≥ 3, does there exist a positive smooth solution u of the Yamabe equation? This is equivalent to finding a conformal metric g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)} g with constant scalar curvature. The Sobolev quotient is the Rayleigh–Ritz ratio: Q(u) = (∫ (a|∇u|² + R u²) dV) / (∫ |u|^p dV)^{2/p} where a = 4(n−1)/(n−2). The subcritical regularization replaces p−1 with q−1 for q < p, where Sobolev embedding is compact — one obtains a family of solutions u_ε for q = p − ε, and then takes the limit ε → 0. The difficulty is that the Sobolev constant for the critical embedding is not attained for general manifolds — the compactness of minimizing sequences fails due to possible bubble formation: concentration at a point where the solution looks like the standard bubble on ℝ^n: u(x) = (ε/(ε² + |x−x₀|²))^{(n−2)/2}.
The Yamabe equation is a critical exponent PDE — the lack of compactness of the Sobolev embedding leads to bubbling phenomena, which must be analyzed to prove existence.
Part 3

🧮 The Solution: Trudinger, Aubin, and Schoen

Existence
Aubin's Test Function, Schoen's Use of the Positive Mass Theorem, and the Full Solution
Trudinger (1968) proved existence when Y(M) ≤ 0 and the conformal class contains a metric with non-positive scalar curvature — using the subcritical regularization and the fact that the functional is bounded below. Aubin (1976) proved existence when n ≥ 6 and M is not locally conformally flat, by constructing a test function u_ε (a bubble concentrated at a point p) such that Q(u_ε) < Y(S^n). Since Y(M) = inf Q ≤ Y(S^n), if Y(M) < Y(S^n) a standard concentration-compactness argument gives a solution. Aubin showed that when the Weyl curvature W_g is non-zero at some point p, the test function Q(u_ε) can be pushed below Y(S^n). This left open the cases: (i) n = 3,4,5, (ii) n ≥ 3 for locally conformally flat manifolds, and (iii) n ≥ 6 where W_g ≡ 0. Schoen (1984) completed the solution: he showed that for any compact manifold M, if Y(M) = Y(S^n) then M must be conformal to S^n. The proof uses the positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau, 1979): the ADM mass of an asymptotically flat Riemannian manifold of non-negative scalar curvature is non-negative, and zero only for ℝ^n. Schoen constructed a conformal normal coordinate at a point and showed that if Y(M) = Y(S^n), the Green's function of L_g yields an asymptotically flat metric on M\{p} whose ADM mass must be zero — implying M is conformal to S^n. This deep use of general relativity completed the proof: every compact Riemannian manifold admits a constant scalar curvature metric in its conformal class.
The solution of the Yamabe problem is a masterpiece of geometric analysis — combining PDE, conformal geometry, and the positive mass theorem from general relativity.
Part 4

🔥 The Yamabe Invariant and Applications to General Relativity

Applications
The Classification of Constant Scalar Curvature Metrics, Kazdan–Warner, and the Penrose Inequality
The Obata theorem: a conformal metric on S^n with constant scalar curvature is the round metric (up to Möbius transformation) — proven by analyzing the conformal Killing fields. For general manifolds, constant scalar curvature metrics are not unique — the space of solutions (the Yamabe metrics) is compact in the smooth topology (the compactness theorem for Yamabe metrics, Schoen 1990). The Kazdan–Warner obstruction (1974): on S² and higher spheres, the function K = scalar curvature under a given conformal change must satisfy ∫ K X(f) dV = 0 for all conformal Killing fields X — providing obstructions to prescribing scalar curvature. The Yamabe invariant Y(M) is a topological invariant that distinguishes smooth structures: Y(ℂP²) > 0, Y(T^n) = 0, and Y(M) < 0 for most manifolds (e.g., surfaces of genus ≥ 2). The classification by sign: Y(M) > 0 iff M admits a conformal metric of positive scalar curvature (a deep condition related to the existence of spin structures and the Â-genus). The Riemann–Penrose inequality in general relativity: for an asymptotically flat 3-manifold with non-negative scalar curvature and an outermost minimal surface of area A, the ADM mass m satisfies m ≥ √(A/16π). The proof by Bray (2001) uses an inverse mean curvature flow and the positive mass theorem. The Hawking mass m_H = √(A/16π)(1 − (1/16π) ∫_Σ H² dA) and the Geroch monotonicity of the Hawking mass under inverse mean curvature flow prove the Penrose inequality for the time-symmetric case.
The Yamabe problem is not just a geometric PDE — it connects to general relativity through the positive mass theorem and the Penrose inequality, where mass, area, and scalar curvature are linked by conformal geometry.

Blow‑up Analysis and the Bubbling Phenomenon in the Yamabe Problem

The lack of compactness of the Sobolev embedding H¹ Lp (with p = 2n/(n−2)) is the central analytic difficulty of the Yamabe problem. When a minimizing sequence for the Yamabe functional approaches the infimum, it may concentrate energy at isolated points, producing “bubbles” conformally equivalent to the standard sphere. Blow‑up analysis, developed by Schoen (1984) and refined by Li‑Zhu (1995), shows that any sequence of solutions uk with bounded energy either converges smoothly or, after rescaling around concentration points, converges to the extremal functions for the Euclidean Sobolev inequality: Uε,y(x) = cn ε(n−2)/2 ( ε² + |x−y|² )(2−n)/2. These bubbles carry exactly the Yamabe energy of the round sphere Y(Sn). Bubble interaction is governed by the Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian; the leading-order interaction energy is expressed through the mass of the asymptotically flat metric obtained by removing the concentration point. This mass equals the ADM mass from the positive mass theorem, linking analytic bubbling to the geometric argument that rules out Y(M) = Y(Sn) unless M is conformal to the sphere.

In dimensions n ≥ 6 the Weyl curvature contributes negatively to the interaction energy, allowing Aubin’s test functions to push the Yamabe quotient below Y(Sn). In lower dimensions or the locally conformally flat case the Weyl term vanishes, and the next-order term involves the scalar curvature and its derivatives; this is why n = 3,4,5 and locally conformally flat manifolds required Schoen’s global argument using the positive mass theorem. The blow‑up profile also yields quantization of the loss of compactness: the total energy of a Palais‑Smale sequence splits into the limit solution’s energy plus an integer multiple of Y(Sn). This quantization underpins the compactness theory of Yamabe metrics and the study of prescribed scalar curvature.

Compactness of Yamabe Metrics and the Schoen Compactness Theorem

Once existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in each conformal class is established, a natural question is whether the set of all such metrics (Yamabe metrics) is compact in the smooth topology. Schoen (1991) proved that for a fixed compact manifold M of dimension 3 ≤ n ≤ 24, the space of unit‑volume Yamabe metrics is compact in C∞. The proof combines blow‑up analysis with study of the linearized Yamabe operator. If a sequence of Yamabe metrics gk = uk4/(n−2) g were to degenerate, the conformal factors uk would develop bubbles. The linearized operator around a bubble is the conformal Laplacian on the sphere, whose kernel consists of conformal Killing fields. Projecting the equation onto the kernel and using the Pohozaev identity yields a balancing condition forcing bubbles to be isolated and non‑interacting. In dimensions ≤ 24 the positive mass theorem (Schoen‑Yau, extended by Lohkamp) guarantees positive mass for each bubble, contradicting the balancing condition unless no bubbles form. Hence the sequence converges smoothly.

In dimensions n ≥ 25 compactness fails: Khuri, Marques and Neves (2009) constructed sequences of Yamabe metrics on certain manifolds that develop bubbles without a smooth limit. This relates to non‑trivial solutions of the linearized problem on the sphere with negative eigenvalues, which appear only in high dimensions. The compactness theorem implies the Yamabe invariant Y(M) is attained by a smooth metric, the set of constant scalar curvature metrics in a given conformal class is finite up to diffeomorphism (for generic metrics), and the moduli space of Yamabe metrics is a finite‑dimensional orbifold. These facts are used in the prescribed scalar curvature problem and Yamabe flow analysis.

Worked Example: Blow‑up Quantization
Let (M³, g) be a compact three‑manifold and {uk} a Palais‑Smale sequence for the Yamabe functional with energy bounded by Y(S³) + δ, δ > 0 small. Show that after passing to a subsequence either uk converges strongly in H¹ or there exists a point p ∈ M such that uk concentrates at p and the loss of energy is exactly Y(S³).
By the concentration‑compactness principle of Lions, the sequence of measures μk = |∇uk|² + Rguk² dVg converges weakly to a measure μ. If no concentration occurs, the embedding H¹ L⁶ is compact on bounded domains away from the concentration set, yielding strong convergence. If concentration occurs at p, rescale around p using conformal normal coordinates: define vk(x) = λk(n−2)/2 uk(expp(λkx)) with λk → 0 chosen so that the maximum of vk is 1. The rescaled metrics converge to the Euclidean metric, and vk converges to the standard bubble U(x) = (1+|x|²)(2−n)/2. The energy of the bubble is exactly the Yamabe energy of the round sphere, Y(S³). Since the total energy is bounded by Y(S³)+δ, at most one bubble can form, and the loss of energy equals Y(S³).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about compactness of Yamabe metrics, first state the dimension range (3 ≤ n ≤ 24) and cite Schoen’s 1991 theorem. Then outline the two‑step proof: (1) blow‑up analysis reduces degeneration to bubbling, (2) the positive mass theorem forces the mass of each bubble to be positive, which contradicts the Pohozaev balancing condition. For dimensions ≥ 25 mention the Khuri‑Marques‑Neves counterexample. This structure shows you understand both the analytic and geometric ingredients.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Boundary: Escobar’s Theorem

The Yamabe problem extends to compact manifolds with boundary. Given (M, g) with ∂M ≠ ∅, one seeks a conformal metric g̃ = u4/(n−2) g with constant scalar curvature in the interior and constant mean curvature on the boundary. The Euler‑Lagrange system is Lgu = λ u(n+2)/(n−2) in M, and Bgu = μ un/(n−2) on ∂M, where Lg = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δg + Rg is the conformal Laplacian and Bg = ∂/∂ν + (n−2)/2 Hg is the conformal boundary operator (Hg is the boundary mean curvature). The variational functional is the Yamabe‑Escobar quotient Q(u) = (∫M (a|∇u|² + Rgu²) dV + 2∫∂M Hgu² dS) / (∫M |u|p dV)2/p with p = 2n/(n−2). Escobar (1992) proved existence of a minimizer for all n ≥ 3 assuming the boundary is not umbilic or the Sobolev quotient is strictly below that of the half‑sphere. The proof uses a test function from the Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian with Neumann boundary condition, together with a positive mass theorem for asymptotically flat manifolds with boundary (the Shi‑Tam mass). When the boundary is umbilic and the quotient equals that of the half‑sphere, the manifold is conformal to a spherical cap and the minimizer is the first eigenfunction of the conformal Laplacian.

Escobar’s theorem yields a conformal invariant, the Yamabe‑Escobar invariant Y(M, ∂M), classifying conformal classes by the sign of the invariant. It also gives a sharp Sobolev trace inequality on manifolds with boundary, with extremals given by conformal factors of constant scalar curvature metrics. The boundary Yamabe problem relates to prescribed scalar and mean curvature problems; Kazdan‑Warner type obstructions appear as integrals of boundary mean curvature against conformal Killing fields. Recent work by Marques (2005) and Almaraz‑Barbosa‑do Carmo (2012) extended compactness theory to the boundary setting, showing the space of unit‑volume Yamabe‑Escobar metrics is compact in dimensions ≤ 24, with similar non‑compactness in higher dimensions.

The Yamabe Invariant, Exotic Smooth Structures, and the Positive Mass Theorem

The Yamabe invariant Y(M) = inf[g] Y([g]) is a diffeomorphism invariant. Its sign classifies: Y(M) > 0 iff M admits a metric of positive scalar curvature; Y(M) = 0 iff M admits zero scalar curvature but no positive scalar curvature metric; Y(M) < 0 otherwise. The connection with the positive mass theorem appears in the study of exotic spheres. In dimensions n ≥ 5, Gromov‑Lawson, Schoen‑Yau, and Stolz showed a simply connected spin manifold admits positive scalar curvature iff its Â‑genus vanishes. Exotic spheres bounding spin manifolds have vanishing Â‑genus, so they admit positive scalar curvature metrics and Y(M) > 0. However, the exact value of Y(M) can distinguish exotic smooth structures. Petean (1999) proved that for n ≥ 5, Y(M) = Y(Sn) iff M is conformally diffeomorphic to the standard sphere. For exotic spheres not conformally equivalent to the round sphere, 0 < Y(M) < Y(Sn). The proof uses the positive mass theorem for asymptotically flat manifolds with boundary (Shi‑Tam mass) applied to the Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian at a point where the Yamabe metric is not locally conformally flat; the mass is positive unless the manifold is conformal to the sphere, giving a strict inequality.

Bray‑Neves (2004) and Akutagawa‑Botvinnik (2003) relate the Yamabe invariant to minimal hypersurface structure. In dimension three, it connects to Heegaard genus and incompressible tori. In higher dimensions, the invariant for connected sums satisfies Y(M # N) = min(Y(M), Y(N)) when both are non‑positive; for positive invariants the formula is more subtle and involves the positive mass theorem. These results illustrate how the Yamabe invariant bridges conformal geometry, global analysis, and high‑dimensional topology.

The Yamabe Flow: Parabolic Approach and Convergence Results

The Yamabe flow, introduced by Hamilton (1989), is the gradient flow of the Yamabe functional restricted to unit-volume metrics. It evolves g(t) by ∂t g = (r − R) g, where R is the scalar curvature and r = (∫M R dV)/Vol(M) is the average scalar curvature (constant along the flow). In conformal gauge g(t) = u(t)4/(n−2) g0, the flow reduces to ∂t u = −(n−2)/4 u (Lg0u − r u(n+2)/(n−2)). The flow preserves the conformal class and volume, and the Yamabe functional is non‑increasing. Ye (1994) proved that for locally conformally flat manifolds with positive Yamabe invariant, the flow exists globally and converges smoothly to a constant scalar curvature metric. Schwetlick‑Struwe (2003) extended global existence to all n ≥ 3 assuming initial Yamabe energy below Y(Sn). Above this threshold singularities can form; Brendle (2005) showed that in dimension three the flow can develop Type‑II singularities modeled on the ancient solution on S³. Singularity analysis uses the same blow‑up techniques as the elliptic problem, plus Harnack inequalities for the linearized flow.

Convergence results have been refined. For n ≤ 5 the flow converges for any initial metric with positive Yamabe invariant (Brendle‑Marques‑Neves, 2011). In higher dimensions convergence holds for generic initial metrics, but non‑convergence examples exist due to non‑trivial ancient solutions. The Yamabe flow provides a dynamical perspective on the Yamabe problem and proves existence of constant scalar curvature metrics where variational methods are difficult. It also connects to the prescribed scalar curvature problem: adding a potential term yields a flow whose stationary points are metrics with given scalar curvature. Long‑time behavior is governed by the same compactness and bubbling phenomena as the elliptic theory.

  • Confusing the Yamabe invariant Y(M) with the Yamabe constant of a conformal class Y([g]); the former is a global infimum over all conformal classes, the latter is the infimum within a fixed class.
  • Assuming the positive mass theorem holds in all dimensions without the spin or dimension ≤ 7 hypothesis; Schoen‑Yau proved it for n ≤ 7, Witten’s spinor proof works for spin manifolds in any dimension, and Lohkamp extended it to all dimensions but with additional technical assumptions.
  • Overlooking the role of the Weyl tensor in Aubin’s test function; the negative contribution of the Weyl curvature is essential for dimensions ≥ 6 and non‑locally‑conformally‑flat manifolds.
  • Stating that the Yamabe flow always converges; convergence fails in high dimensions or for energies above Y(Sn) where bubbling singularities occur.
  • Neglecting the boundary term in the Escobar problem; the boundary operator Bg and the mean curvature H are crucial for the variational structure and for the positive mass theorem with boundary.
  • Claiming that the Yamabe invariant distinguishes all exotic smooth structures; it distinguishes some but not all — there exist exotic spheres with the same Yamabe invariant.
  • Misapplying the compactness theorem of Schoen to dimensions ≥ 25; the Khuri‑Marques‑Neves counterexamples show compactness fails in high dimensions.

The Positive Mass Theorem and Its Role in the Yamabe Problem

The positive mass theorem, proved by Schoen and Yau for dimensions up to seven and by Witten for spin manifolds in all dimensions, states that an asymptotically flat manifold with non‑negative scalar curvature has non‑negative ADM mass, with equality only for Euclidean space. In the Yamabe problem the theorem enters through the conformal Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian Lg = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δg + Rg. Near a pole the Green’s function behaves like the Euclidean fundamental solution plus a constant term called the mass. If the mass were negative, one could construct a test function that lowers the Yamabe functional below the infimum, contradicting the definition of the Yamabe invariant. Schoen’s 1984 argument shows that for a manifold not conformally equivalent to the round sphere the mass is strictly positive; this positivity forces the Yamabe invariant to be strictly less than the invariant of the sphere Y(Sn). The proof uses a conformal blow‑up at a point where the Yamabe metric is not locally conformally flat, producing an asymptotically flat end whose mass is computed from the asymptotic expansion of the Green’s function. The positive mass theorem then yields the strict inequality Y(M) < Y(Sn). This mechanism also underlies the classification of equality cases in the Aubin inequality and the proof that the Yamabe invariant distinguishes some exotic smooth structures. In dimensions above seven where the positive mass theorem is not fully established, the argument requires additional hypotheses such as spin structure or Lohkamp’s extension, which explains why the full classification of Yamabe invariants remains open in high dimensions.

The Weyl Tensor and the Aubin Test Function in Dimensions ≥ 6

Aubin’s construction of a test function that beats the spherical Yamabe constant relies on the asymptotic expansion of the Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian. In dimensions n ≥ 6 the leading correction term involves the square of the Weyl tensor |W|2 evaluated at the pole. Because the Weyl tensor measures the failure of local conformal flatness, its negative contribution to the expansion makes the Yamabe functional strictly smaller than Y(Sn) whenever the manifold is not locally conformally flat. In dimensions three, four, and five the Weyl tensor either vanishes identically (n = 3) or its contribution appears at higher order and is not sufficient to guarantee a strict inequality; this is why Aubin’s original argument works only for n ≥ 6. Schoen later completed the remaining low‑dimensional cases by a more delicate analysis of the mass term, using the positive mass theorem instead of the Weyl tensor. The interplay between the Weyl curvature and the mass term illustrates a general principle: in high dimensions the local conformal geometry (Weyl) dominates the variational problem, while in low dimensions the global topology (mass) takes over. This dichotomy also appears in the compactness theory for Yamabe metrics, where bubbling phenomena are governed by the Weyl tensor in dimensions ≥ 6 and by the mass in dimensions ≤ 5.

Worked Example: Computing the Weyl Contribution
Let (M6, g) be a compact six‑dimensional Riemannian manifold with Yamabe metric g. Show that the coefficient of ε2 in the expansion of the Yamabe functional for the Aubin test function uε is proportional to −|W|2(p) where p is the pole.
The Aubin test function is uε(x) = η(x) (ε/(ε2+|x|2))(n−2)/2 with a cut‑off η. Insert uε into the Yamabe functional Q(u) = ∫(4(n−1)/(n−2)|∇u|2+R u2) / (∫u2n/(n−2))(n−2)/n. Expand the metric in normal coordinates at p: gij = δij − 1/3 Rikjl xkxl + O(|x|3). The scalar curvature term contributes at order ε2 through the Ricci tensor, while the gradient term produces a contribution from the full Riemann tensor. Decomposing the Riemann tensor into Weyl, Ricci, and scalar parts, the Ricci and scalar pieces cancel against the scalar curvature term because g is a Yamabe metric (constant scalar curvature). The remaining piece is the Weyl tensor squared, giving a coefficient −Cn|W|2(p) ε2 with Cn > 0. Hence the functional is strictly below the spherical value whenever |W|(p) ≠ 0.

When a question asks why Aubin’s argument works only for n ≥ 6, answer in two steps: (1) write the expansion of the metric in normal coordinates and identify the Weyl term; (2) note that the Weyl tensor vanishes identically for n = 3 and its contraction yields zero for n = 4,5, so the leading negative term appears first at n = 6. Mention Schoen’s complementary mass argument for the low‑dimensional cases to show complete understanding.

Prescribed Scalar Curvature and the Kazdan–Warner Obstructions

The prescribed scalar curvature problem asks whether a given function f on a compact manifold M can be realized as the scalar curvature of a metric conformal to a background metric g0. In conformal gauge g = u4/(n−2) g0 the scalar curvature transforms as Rg = u−(n+2)/(n−2) Lg0 u. Setting Rg = f leads to the semilinear elliptic equation Lg0 u = f u(n+2)/(n−2). Kazdan and Warner discovered necessary integral obstructions: if f is the scalar curvature of a conformal metric, then for every conformal Killing vector field X of g0 one must have ∫M ⟨∇f, X⟩ u2n/(n−2) dVg0 = 0. On the standard sphere these obstructions reduce to the condition that f must be orthogonal to the first spherical harmonics. The obstructions are also sufficient in many cases: on the sphere, any positive function satisfying the Kazdan–Warner conditions is the scalar curvature of some conformal metric (Kazdan–Warner 1975). On manifolds with negative Yamabe invariant the problem is unobstructed for any negative f, while for positive Yamabe invariant the obstructions are the only known necessary conditions. The existence theory uses variational methods combined with blow‑up analysis; the lack of compactness is again governed by the same bubbling phenomena as in the Yamabe problem. Work by Marques, Neves, and others has extended the prescribed curvature results to manifolds with boundary and to higher order curvature equations such as the Q‑curvature problem.

The Yamabe Problem on Orbifolds and Singular Spaces

Orbifolds are spaces locally modeled on quotients of Euclidean space by finite group actions. The Yamabe problem extends naturally to this setting: one seeks a constant scalar curvature metric in a given conformal class on a compact Riemannian orbifold. The conformal Laplacian and the Yamabe functional are defined using the orbifold volume form and the scalar curvature of the orbifold metric, which is smooth away from the singular strata. Akutagawa and others proved that the Yamabe invariant of an orbifold is attained by a smooth orbifold metric, provided the invariant is less than the invariant of the model spherical orbifold. The proof follows the same scheme as in the smooth case: a minimizing sequence either converges or bubbles. Bubbling at a singular point produces a limit on the covering Euclidean space, and the positive mass theorem on the resolution of the singularity yields the required strict inequality. When the orbifold is globally conformally equivalent to a spherical space form, the invariant equals that of the model and the minimizer is the standard metric. The orbifold Yamabe problem has applications to the study of Einstein metrics on orbifolds and to the compactness theory for sequences of manifolds with bounded curvature and diameter collapsing to an orbifold limit.

Recent Advances: The Yamabe Problem on Noncompact Manifolds

On complete noncompact manifolds the Yamabe problem asks for a conformal metric of constant scalar curvature with finite volume or with prescribed asymptotic behavior. For asymptotically flat manifolds the natural invariant is the ADM mass; the positive mass theorem implies that if the scalar curvature is non‑negative and the mass is zero, the manifold is conformally Euclidean. Jin, Li, and others studied the Yamabe equation on manifolds with positive Yamabe invariant and proved existence of a conformal metric of constant positive scalar curvature under a volume growth condition. On manifolds with cylindrical ends the problem reduces to a boundary value problem on the cross‑section, linking to the Escobar problem. For manifolds with hyperbolic cusps the Yamabe invariant can be negative, and the existence of a constant negative scalar curvature metric is equivalent to the solvability of a Poincaré‑type inequality. Work by Khuri, Marques, and Neves has shown that compactness of Yamabe metrics can fail in sufficiently high dimensions even for noncompact manifolds, by constructing sequences of metrics that develop bubbles escaping to infinity. These results highlight the deep connection between the Yamabe problem, the positive mass theorem, and the geometry of ends.

  • Overlooking the role of the Weyl tensor in Aubin’s test function; the negative contribution of the Weyl curvature is essential for dimensions ≥ 6 and non‑locally‑conformally‑flat manifolds.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Conical Singularities

When a compact Riemannian manifold carries a metric with isolated conical singularities, the conformal class is well defined away from the singular points, and one may ask whether a constant scalar curvature metric exists in that class. The singularities are modeled on cones C(Σ) = (0,∞) × Σ with metric dr² + r² g_Σ, where Σ is a smooth compact manifold of dimension n‑1. The scalar curvature of a conical metric behaves like a distribution supported at the tip; its regular part satisfies the Yamabe equation on the smooth part, while the singular part contributes a Dirac mass proportional to the cone angle deficit. The variational formulation uses the Sobolev space H¹(M) defined with respect to the singular volume form; the Yamabe functional remains bounded below provided the cone angles are less than 2π. In dimensions n ≥ 3, the critical Sobolev exponent is unchanged, but the singular stratum modifies the best constant in the Sobolev inequality. Research by Akutagawa, Carron, and Mazzeo indicates that if the Yamabe invariant of the singular space is strictly less than the invariant of the model spherical cone, a minimizer exists and is smooth away from the tips. The proof follows the concentration‑compactness scheme: a minimizing sequence either converges or bubbles. Bubbling at a regular point produces a standard sphere bubble; bubbling at a conical tip produces a bubble on the Euclidean cone, whose energy is quantified by the cone angle. The positive mass theorem on the resolution of the cone (obtained by blowing up the tip) yields the strict inequality needed to rule out loss of mass. When the invariant equals that of the spherical cone, the minimizer is the standard conical metric, unique up to scaling. This theory has applications to the existence of Einstein metrics on orbifolds with isolated singularities and to the compactness theory for sequences of smooth manifolds collapsing to a conical limit.

The Yamabe Problem for Q‑Curvature and the Paneitz Operator

In dimension four, the natural higher‑order analogue of the Yamabe problem asks for a metric conformal to a given one with constant Q‑curvature, where Q = −(1/12)(ΔR − R² + 3|Ric|²) is the Branson Q‑curvature. Under a conformal change g_u = u² g, the Q‑curvature transforms by the Paneitz operator P_g u + 2 Q_g u = 2 Qg_u u³, where P_g = Δ² + δ( (2/3)R g − 2 Ric ) d is a fourth‑order conformally covariant operator. The associated variational problem is to minimize the functional ∫ (u P_g u + 2 Q_g u²) dV_g subject to ∫ u⁴ dV_g = 1. The critical exponent is 4, matching the dimension, so the problem is again critical. The existence theory parallels the second‑order case: if the Paneitz operator is non‑negative and its kernel consists only of constants, a minimizer exists provided the Q‑curvature invariant is less than that of the round sphere S⁴. A positive mass theorem for the Paneitz operator, established by Chang, Gursky, and Yang using the ADM mass of an asymptotically flat manifold obtained by stereographic projection, supplies the strict inequality. When the manifold is locally conformally flat, the Paneitz operator reduces to the bi‑Laplacian, and the classification of solutions on ℝ⁴ yields the explicit family of “bubbles” u_λ(x) = (2λ/(1+λ²|x|²))¹ᐟ². Blow‑up analysis shows that any sequence of solutions with bounded energy either converges or develops a finite number of such bubbles. The Q‑curvature problem also appears in the study of the determinant of the Laplacian and in the uniformization of four‑manifolds; the existence of a constant Q‑curvature metric is equivalent to the existence of a conformal metric with constant scalar curvature and constant σ₂‑curvature.

Worked Example: Computing the Paneitz Operator on the Round Sphere
Let (S⁴, g₀) be the unit round sphere. Show that the Paneitz operator Pg₀ acting on a function u is given by Pg₀ u = Δ² u + 2 Δ u, and deduce that the Q‑curvature of g₀ is Qg₀ = 3.
On the round sphere the Ricci tensor is Ric = 3 g₀ and the scalar curvature is R = 12. The Paneitz operator in dimension four is P_g = Δ² + δ( (2/3)R g − 2 Ric ) d. Substituting R = 12 and Ric = 3 g₀ gives (2/3)·12 g₀ − 2·3 g₀ = 8 g₀ − 6 g₀ = 2 g₀. Hence the lower‑order term is δ(2 g₀ d) = 2 Δ. Therefore Pg₀ = Δ² + 2 Δ. The Q‑curvature satisfies P_g 1 + 2 Q_g = 2 Q_g, so Q_g = (1/2) P_g 1. Since Δ 1 = 0, Pg₀ 1 = 0, but the transformation law for Q under conformal change yields Qg₀ = 3 for the unit sphere. This matches the known value QS⁴ = 3.

When a question asks for the Paneitz operator or Q‑curvature on a specific Einstein manifold, first write down the Ricci and scalar curvatures, then plug them into the general formula P_g = Δ² + δ( (2/3)R g − 2 Ric ) d. Remember that on an Einstein manifold Ric = (R/n) g, which simplifies the lower‑order term to a multiple of Δ. The constant Q‑curvature of the round sphere S⁴ is 3. Memorize this value; it appears frequently in comparison arguments.

The Yamabe Problem on Locally Conformally Flat Manifolds: Classification of Solutions

A Riemannian manifold is locally conformally flat if its Weyl tensor vanishes identically. In dimensions n ≥ 4 this condition is equivalent to the existence of a local conformal diffeomorphism to Euclidean space. For the Yamabe problem, local conformal flatness simplifies the analysis because the conformal Laplacian transforms under a conformal change exactly as in Euclidean space. On a locally conformally flat manifold (M, g), the developing map gives a conformal immersion of the universal cover into the sphere Sⁿ. The Yamabe equation −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g u + R_g u = λ u(n+2)/(n−2) becomes, after pulling back to Sⁿ via the developing map, the standard Yamabe equation on the sphere with a possible singular set corresponding to the limit set of the covering group. The classification of positive solutions of the Yamabe equation on ℝⁿ (or Sⁿ) is classical: all positive solutions are of the form u(x) = c_n (λ/(1+λ²|x−x₀|²))(n−2)/2, the so‑called Aubin‑Talenti bubbles. On a compact locally conformally flat manifold, any solution of the Yamabe equation lifts to a solution on Sⁿ invariant under the covering group Γ. If Γ is a finite group acting freely, the quotient is a spherical space form and the only solutions are the constant ones. If Γ is infinite (e.g., a Kleinian group), the structure of the limit set influences the extension of the lifted solution; under suitable geometric conditions the solution extends across the limit set by removable singularity theorems, forcing it to be a bubble centered at a point fixed by Γ. This yields a classification: every constant scalar curvature metric in the conformal class of a locally conformally flat manifold is either the standard metric on a spherical space form or a metric obtained by conformally transplanting a bubble from the sphere. Results of Schoen and of Li and Zhu, using the method of moving planes and conformal invariance, support this picture. It implies that the Yamabe invariant of a locally conformally flat manifold is either the invariant of the round sphere (if the manifold is conformally equivalent to a spherical space form) or is strictly smaller, in which case the minimizer is unique up to conformal diffeomorphism.

The Yamabe Problem and the Conformal Laplacian Spectrum

The conformal Laplacian L_g = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g + R_g is a self‑adjoint elliptic operator on a compact manifold. Its spectrum consists of a discrete sequence of eigenvalues λ₁ ≤ λ₂ ≤ … → ∞. The Yamabe invariant of the conformal class [g] can be expressed variationally as Y([g]) = infu>0 (∫ u L_g u dV_g) / (∫ u2n/(n−2) dV_g)(n−2)/n. The first eigenvalue λ₁ is the minimum of the Rayleigh quotient ∫ u L_g u / ∫ u², and the corresponding eigenfunction can be chosen positive. If λ₁ > 0, the conformal class contains a metric of positive scalar curvature; if λ₁ = 0, it contains a scalar‑flat metric; if λ₁ < 0, every metric in the class has negative scalar curvature somewhere. The sign of λ₁ is a conformal invariant. Moreover, the Yamabe invariant Y(M) = inf[g] Y([g]) is related to the spectrum of the conformal Laplacian on the whole manifold: Y(M) > 0 iff there exists a metric with λ₁ > 0. The eigenvalues of L_g transform under a conformal change g_u = u4/(n−2) g by Lg_u (v) = u-(n+2)/(n−2) L_g (u v). This covariance implies that the spectrum of L_g is not conformally invariant, but the sign of the first eigenvalue is. The spectral flow of the conformal Laplacian along the Yamabe flow provides a parabolic proof of the existence of a constant scalar curvature metric: the flow ∂g/∂t = −(R_g − r_g) g preserves the conformal class and evolves the scalar curvature toward its average r_g. The linearization of the flow at a critical metric is precisely the conformal Laplacian, and its kernel corresponds to the infinitesimal conformal deformations that preserve constant scalar curvature. In dimensions n ≥ 6, the Weyl tensor enters the second variation formula, and the Morse index of a Yamabe metric is related to the number of negative eigenvalues of L_g on the space of functions orthogonal to the kernel. This spectral perspective connects the Yamabe problem to the study of the conformal Laplacian on manifolds with boundary (the Escobar problem) and to the stability of constant scalar curvature metrics under perturbations.

The Yamabe Problem and the Einstein Metric Problem

An Einstein metric satisfies Ric = λ g for some constant λ. Every Einstein metric has constant scalar curvature R = n λ, so it is a solution of the Yamabe problem in its conformal class. Conversely, a Yamabe metric (a constant scalar curvature metric minimizing the Yamabe functional) need not be Einstein. The relationship between the two problems is subtle. In dimension three, a metric of constant scalar curvature is Einstein iff it has constant sectional curvature, so the Yamabe problem and the Einstein problem coincide only for space forms. In dimension four, the existence of a Yamabe metric with positive scalar curvature does not guarantee an Einstein metric; however, if the manifold admits a metric with positive Yamabe invariant and the Weyl tensor satisfies a suitable integral pinching condition, then a Yamabe metric can sometimes be deformed to an Einstein metric via the Ricci flow. In higher dimensions, results by Biquard and others indicate that if a manifold admits a Yamabe metric with positive scalar curvature and the Ln/2 norm of the Weyl tensor is sufficiently small, then the manifold also admits an Einstein metric. The Yamabe invariant provides an obstruction to the existence of Einstein metrics: if Y(M) ≤ 0, no Einstein metric with positive scalar curvature can exist. For negative Einstein metrics, the Yamabe invariant is negative and the Yamabe metric is the unique constant scalar curvature metric in its conformal class, but it may not be Einstein. The Einstein‑Hilbert functional restricted to a conformal class reduces to the Yamabe functional, so critical points of the Einstein‑Hilbert functional that lie in a given conformal class are precisely the Yamabe metrics that are also Einstein. This variational link explains why the positive mass theorem, which is used to solve the Yamabe problem, also plays a role in the existence of asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein metrics. The interplay between the two problems remains an active area of research, especially in the context of the Yamabe flow and its convergence to Einstein metrics.

  • Confusing the Yamabe invariant of a singular space with the invariant of its smooth part; the conical deficit contributes a distributional term that changes the infimum.
  • Assuming the Paneitz operator is always non‑negative; on many four‑manifolds it has negative eigenvalues, which obstructs the Q‑curvature problem.
  • Overlooking that local conformal flatness forces the Weyl tensor to vanish, but the Cotton tensor must also vanish in dimension three; the classification of solutions uses the developing map, not just the Weyl tensor.
  • Treating the first eigenvalue of the conformal Laplacian as a conformal invariant; only its sign is invariant, the numerical value changes under conformal rescaling.
  • Believing every Yamabe metric is Einstein; in dimensions ≥ 4 there are many constant scalar curvature metrics that are not Einstein, and the Einstein condition imposes additional algebraic constraints on the Ricci tensor.
  • Neglecting the role of the limit set in the classification on locally conformally flat manifolds with infinite covering groups; the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set determines whether bubbles can form.
  • Review these traps before the exam to avoid common misconceptions.

The Yamabe Problem on Asymptotically Hyperbolic Manifolds

On a complete non‑compact manifold M whose metric g is asymptotically hyperbolic, the conformal class [g] contains metrics that approach the hyperbolic metric at infinity. The Yamabe functional is defined by the same quotient Y(g) = (∫_M R_g dV_g) / (Vol_g(M))(n−2)/n, but the volume is infinite, so one works with a renormalised volume or with the mass aspect of the metric. The existence problem asks whether there is a metric in [g] with constant scalar curvature −n(n−1) (the scalar curvature of hyperbolic space). Solutions were developed using a barrier argument together with the positive mass theorem for asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds. The key step constructs a family of test functions concentrating near a point where the mass is minimal; the mass appears as the coefficient of the r−n term in the asymptotic expansion of the conformal factor. If the mass is positive, the infimum of the Yamabe functional is strictly less than the hyperbolic value, and a minimiser exists. When the mass vanishes, the infimum equals the hyperbolic value and the only constant scalar curvature metric is the hyperbolic metric itself. This dichotomy mirrors the compact case where the sign of the Yamabe invariant decides existence. The asymptotically hyperbolic Yamabe problem also connects to the AdS/CFT correspondence: the renormalised volume is the holographic central charge, and a constant scalar curvature metric corresponds to a vacuum solution of Einstein’s equations with negative cosmological constant.

The σ_k–Yamabe Problem: Fully Nonlinear Generalisation

The classical Yamabe problem is the Euler‑Lagrange equation of the functional ∫ R_g dV_g restricted to a conformal class. A natural fully nonlinear analogue replaces the scalar curvature by the k‑th elementary symmetric function σ_k(λ(g⁻¹A_g)) of the eigenvalues of the Schouten tensor A_g = (Ric_g − (R_g/2(n−1)) g)/(n−2). The resulting σ_k‑Yamabe problem asks for a metric g_u = u4/(n−2) g with constant σ_k curvature. The equation is σ_k(λ(g⁻¹Ag_u)) = const, a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE of order two. For k = 1 one recovers the Yamabe equation; for k = n/2 (when n is even) the equation is conformally invariant and related to the Q‑curvature. The existence theory requires the Γ_k+ cone condition: the eigenvalues of the Schouten tensor must lie in the Gårding cone Γ_k+ at every point. Under this structural hypothesis, a variational method using the σ_k‑Yamabe functional Y_k(g) = ∫ σ_k(λ(A_g)) dV_g / (Vol_g(M))(n−2k)/n yields a minimiser. The positive mass theorem again plays a role: Aubin‑type test functions are built from the Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian, and the mass term appears in the expansion of the σ_k functional. In dimensions n ≥ 3 and for 2 ≤ k ≤ n/2, the problem is solvable on any manifold with positive Yamabe invariant and a metric whose Schouten tensor lies in Γ_k+. Counterexamples show that without the cone condition the functional may be unbounded below, and bubbling phenomena similar to the classical case can occur.

Worked Example: Computing the σ₂‑Yamabe Functional on a Product Metric
Let M = S² × S² with the product of round metrics of radius 1. Compute the value of the σ₂‑Yamabe functional Y₂(g) for this metric and decide whether the Γ₂⁺ condition holds.
For a product of two Einstein manifolds with Einstein constants λ₁ = λ₂ = 1, the Ricci tensor is Ric = g and the scalar curvature is R = 4. The Schouten tensor is A = (Ric − (R/6)g)/2 = (g − (4/6)g)/2 = (1/6)g. Its eigenvalues are all 1/6. The elementary symmetric function σ₂ of four equal eigenvalues λ is C(4,2) λ² = 6·(1/36) = 1/6. The volume of S² × S² with unit radii is (4π)² = 16π². Hence Y₂(g) = (∫ σ₂ dV) / Vol(4−4)/4 = (1/6)·16π² / 1 = (8/3)π². Since all eigenvalues are positive, they lie in the Gårding cone Γ₂⁺, so the Γ₂⁺ condition is satisfied. Therefore the σ₂‑Yamabe problem on this manifold admits a minimiser, and the product metric itself is a critical point of the σ₂‑Yamabe functional.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the sign of the Yamabe invariant on a product of Einstein manifolds, first compute the scalar curvature of each factor, then the scalar curvature of the product. The Yamabe invariant of a product is not simply the sum of the invariants; use the formula Y(M₁×M₂) = Y(M₁)·Vol(M₂)(n₁−2)/n₁ + Y(M₂)·Vol(M₁)(n₂−2)/n₂ only when both factors have constant scalar curvature. For fully nonlinear σ_k problems, always check the Γ_k⁺ cone condition before invoking existence theorems.

The Yamabe Problem and the Conformal Killing Operator

The conformal Killing operator 𝒟_g : Γ(TM) → Γ(S²T*M) maps a vector field X to the trace‑free part of its Lie derivative: 𝒟_g X = ℒ_X g − (2/n)(div_g X) g. Its formal L² adjoint 𝒟_g^* appears in the linearisation of the scalar curvature map. A conformal Killing field satisfies 𝒟_g X = 0; such fields generate one‑parameter families of conformal diffeomorphisms. In the Yamabe problem, the kernel of the linearised Yamabe operator at a constant scalar curvature metric g is precisely the space of functions u such that u(n+2)/(n−2) g has the same constant scalar curvature. This kernel can be identified with the space of conformal Killing fields via the map X ↦ (div_g X) / n. When the manifold admits non‑trivial conformal Killing fields, the Yamabe functional has a degenerate critical manifold, and the Morse‑Bott index must be used instead of the ordinary Morse index. The dimension of the kernel equals the dimension of the conformal group of (M,g). For the round sphere Sⁿ, the conformal group is SO(n+1,1) of dimension (n+1)(n+2)/2, and the kernel of the linearised Yamabe operator has the same dimension. This degeneracy is responsible for the lack of compactness in the Yamabe problem on the sphere and underlies the bubbling phenomenon: sequences of minimisers can concentrate at points while being acted upon by the conformal group. The positive mass theorem is used to rule out bubbling on manifolds not conformally equivalent to the sphere; the argument essentially shows that the mass term provides a strict inequality that prevents the loss of compactness caused by the conformal Killing fields.

The Yamabe Problem and the ADM Mass in General Relativity

The ADM mass of an asymptotically flat manifold (M,g) is defined by the flux integral mADM = (1/2(n−1)ωn−1) ∫S_∞ (∂_j gij − ∂_i gjj) ν^i dS. The positive mass theorem states that if M has non‑negative scalar curvature and is asymptotically flat, then mADM ≥ 0, with equality only for Euclidean space. The Yamabe problem on a compact manifold M is reduced to the positive mass theorem by a conformal blow‑up: one removes a point p and considers the Green’s function G_p of the conformal Laplacian L_g. The metric ĝ = G_p4/(n−2) g is asymptotically flat on M{p}, and its ADM mass is proportional to the constant term in the expansion of G_p. If the Yamabe invariant of M is positive, the mass of ĝ is positive unless M is conformally equivalent to the sphere. This positivity yields the strict inequality Y(M) < Y(Sⁿ) needed to apply the direct method of the calculus of variations. Conversely, if Y(M) = Y(Sⁿ), the mass vanishes and the positive mass theorem forces ĝ to be flat, implying M is conformally the sphere. The same idea appears in the Riemannian Penrose inequality: the ADM mass of an asymptotically flat manifold with horizon boundary is bounded below by a geometric quantity involving the area of the horizon. The Yamabe invariant of the horizon cross‑section enters the inequality, linking the Yamabe problem to black hole thermodynamics.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Edge Singularities

An edge singularity is modelled on a product ℝ⁺ × Y where Y is a compact manifold (the link) and the metric near the edge takes the form dr² + r² g_Y + g_Z with g_Z a metric on the transverse directions. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks for a metric in the conformal class with constant scalar curvature, allowing the conformal factor to have a prescribed singular behaviour at the edge. The analysis uses weighted Hölder spaces Ck,α_δ where the weight δ measures the rate of decay or growth relative to the distance r to the edge. The indicial roots of the conformal Laplacian on the model cone determine the admissible weights: if δ avoids the indicial spectrum, the conformal Laplacian is Fredholm between appropriate weighted spaces. The Yamabe functional is then well defined on the completion of smooth compactly supported functions in the weighted Sobolev space H¹_δ. Existence of a minimiser follows from a concentration‑compactness argument adapted to the edge geometry; the main new difficulty is that bubbles can form not only at interior points but also at the edge. The edge‑bubbling profile is governed by the Yamabe problem on the link Y. If the Yamabe invariant of Y is positive, edge bubbling can be ruled out by a positive mass theorem on the edge model. When the link has non‑positive Yamabe invariant, the infimum of the Yamabe functional on the singular space equals the infimum on the regular part, and a minimiser exists without bubbling. This theory extends to iterated edge spaces and provides a framework for the Yamabe problem on stratified spaces arising in algebraic geometry.

  • Confusing the ADM mass of the blown‑up Green’s function metric with the mass of the original compact manifold; the former is a conformal invariant used in the proof, the latter is not defined for compact manifolds.
  • Assuming the Γ_k⁺ cone condition is automatically satisfied for any metric with positive scalar curvature; it is a pointwise algebraic condition on the Schouten eigenvalues and can fail even when R > 0.
  • Treating the kernel of the linearised Yamabe operator as consisting only of constant functions; on manifolds with conformal Killing fields the kernel is larger and equals the space of divergence of conformal Killing fields.
  • Neglecting the role of the indicial roots when working on edge singularities; choosing a weight that hits an indicial root destroys Fredholmness and invalidates the variational argument.
  • Believing the σ_k‑Yamabe functional is always bounded below; without the Γ_k⁺ condition the functional can be unbounded below, as shown by explicit counterexamples on certain product manifolds.
  • Overlooking that the positive mass theorem for asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds requires a spin structure in the original proof; later work removed this hypothesis but the spin assumption still appears in many textbook statements.
  • Confusing the Yamabe invariant of a singular space with the invariant of its smooth part; the edge or conical deficit contributes a distributional term that changes the infimum.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Cylindrical Ends and the Adiabatic Limit

A cylindrical end is modelled on a product [0,∞) × N where N is a closed Riemannian manifold of dimension n−1 and the metric takes the form dt² + g_N with t the cylindrical coordinate. The Yamabe problem on such a non‑compact manifold asks for a metric in the conformal class of the cylindrical metric that has constant scalar curvature and is complete. The analysis hinges on the behaviour of the conformal Laplacian L_g = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g + R_g on weighted Sobolev spaces H¹_δ where the weight δ measures exponential decay or growth as t → ∞. The indicial roots of L_g on the cylinder are determined by the spectrum of the Laplacian on N; they are λ = (n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)²/4 + μ) where μ runs over the eigenvalues of −Δ_N. Choosing a weight δ that avoids these roots makes L_g Fredholm between appropriate weighted spaces, allowing a variational formulation of the Yamabe functional.

The adiabatic limit arises when one considers a family of metrics g_ε = dt² + ε² g_N with ε → 0. In this limit the cross‑section N collapses and the Yamabe problem reduces to a one‑dimensional problem on the cylinder. The limiting Yamabe invariant is the infimum of the one‑dimensional functional ∫ (4(n−1)/(n−2) (u')² + R_N u²) dt / (∫ u2n/(n−2) dt)(n−2)/n where R_N is the scalar curvature of N. This reduction has been used in gluing constructions for constant scalar curvature metrics on manifolds with cylindrical ends: one solves the Yamabe problem on the compact core, on the cylinder, and then matches the solutions using a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction. The matching condition involves the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map of the conformal Laplacian on the cross‑section, and its solvability is equivalent to the non‑degeneracy of the linearised Yamabe operator on N.

Existence results depend critically on the sign of the Yamabe invariant of the cross‑section Y(N). If Y(N) > 0 the cylindrical end admits a positive Green’s function for the conformal Laplacian, and the mass of the associated asymptotically flat metric (obtained by a conformal inversion at infinity) is positive unless the cylinder is conformally a hemisphere. This positivity yields the strict inequality needed for the direct method. If Y(N) ≤ 0 the infimum of the Yamabe functional on the cylindrical manifold equals the infimum on the compact part, and a minimiser exists without bubbling at infinity. The adiabatic limit also reveals a bifurcation phenomenon: as ε varies, new branches of solutions can appear when the linearised operator on N acquires a kernel, leading to a rich moduli space of Yamabe metrics on cylindrical manifolds.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Cusp Singularities and the Mazzeo–Melrose Theory

A cusp singularity is modelled on a quotient of the hyperbolic space Hⁿ by a parabolic subgroup, giving a metric of the form dr²/r² + r² g_Y + g_Z near the cusp, where Y is a compact nilmanifold (the cusp cross‑section) and Z parametrises the transverse directions. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks for a conformal metric with constant scalar curvature that is complete and has a prescribed asymptotic behaviour at the cusp. The analysis uses the b‑calculus of Melrose and the edge‑calculus of Mazzeo, which replace ordinary Sobolev spaces by weighted b‑Sobolev spaces H^m_b,δ where the weight δ measures polynomial decay relative to the defining function r of the cusp.

The conformal Laplacian on a cusp manifold is a b‑elliptic operator. Its indicial family is obtained by freezing coefficients at the cusp and taking the Mellin transform in r. The indicial roots are the complex numbers σ for which the model operator on the cusp cross‑section has a non‑trivial kernel. For the Yamabe operator the indicial roots are σ = (n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)²/4 + λ) where λ runs over the eigenvalues of the sub‑Laplacian on the nilmanifold Y. Choosing a weight δ that avoids the real parts of these roots makes the conformal Laplacian Fredholm between weighted b‑Sobolev spaces. The Yamabe functional is then well defined on the completion of smooth compactly supported functions in H¹_b,δ.

Research on the cusp Yamabe problem has shown that if the Yamabe invariant of the cusp cross‑section Y(Y) is positive, then the infimum of the Yamabe functional on the cusp manifold is strictly less than the Yamabe invariant of the sphere, and a minimiser exists. The proof uses a concentration‑compactness argument adapted to the b‑geometry: bubbles can form at interior points or at the cusp. Cusp bubbling is ruled out by a positive mass theorem for asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds with cusp ends. When Y(Y) ≤ 0 the infimum equals the infimum on the regular part, and a minimiser exists without bubbling. The cusp Yamabe problem also connects to the theory of Eisenstein series and the spectral theory of the Laplacian on finite‑volume hyperbolic manifolds, because the constant scalar curvature metric in the conformal class of a cusp metric is closely related to the resolvent of the Laplacian at the bottom of the spectrum.

Worked Example: Indicial Roots on a Cusp
Consider a 4‑dimensional cusp manifold with cross‑section a 3‑dimensional Heisenberg nilmanifold Y. The sub‑Laplacian on Y has first non‑zero eigenvalue λ₁ = 4π² (illustrative value). Compute the indicial roots of the conformal Laplacian L_g = −4·3/2 Δ_g + R_g on this cusp.
For n = 4 the conformal Laplacian is L_g = −6 Δ_g + R_g. The indicial roots are σ = (n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)²/4 + λ) = 1 ± √(1 + λ). With λ = λ₁ = 4π² we obtain σ = 1 ± √(1 + 4π²). These two real roots determine the admissible weights δ for the b‑Sobolev spaces: any δ not equal to the real part of an indicial root (here the roots are real) yields a Fredholm operator. Thus one may choose δ = 0 if 0 is not a root, which holds because √(1+4π²) > 1.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the indicial roots of a conformal Laplacian on a singular space, first identify the model geometry (cone, edge, cusp, cylindrical end). Write down the general formula for the roots in terms of the eigenvalues of the cross‑section Laplacian or sub‑Laplacian. Then substitute the given eigenvalue. Remember that the weight δ must avoid the real parts of these roots; a common trap is to forget that the roots can be complex, in which case the condition is δ ≠ Re(σ).

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Fibred Boundary Geometry

A fibred boundary (or φ‑manifold) is a compact manifold with boundary ∂M equipped with a fibration φ: ∂M → B over a closed base B. Near the boundary the metric takes the form dx²/x⁴ + φ* g_B / x² + g_F where x is a boundary defining function, g_B is a metric on the base, and g_F is a family of metrics on the fibres. This geometry interpolates between cylindrical ends (when the fibres are points) and cusp ends (when the base is a point). The Yamabe problem on φ‑manifolds seeks a conformal metric with constant scalar curvature that is complete and has a prescribed singular behaviour at the boundary.

The analysis uses the φ‑calculus of Mazzeo and Melrose, which provides a pseudodifferential calculus adapted to the fibred boundary structure. The conformal Laplacian is a φ‑elliptic operator, and its indicial family is obtained by a partial Mellin transform in x and a Fourier transform along the fibres. The indicial roots depend on the spectrum of a family of operators on the fibres, the so‑called φ‑Laplacian. For the Yamabe operator the indicial roots are σ = (n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)²/4 + λ_F) where λ_F are the eigenvalues of the vertical Laplacian on the fibres. Choosing a weight δ that avoids these roots makes the conformal Laplacian Fredholm between weighted φ‑Sobolev spaces H^m_φ,δ.

Existence of a Yamabe metric on a φ‑manifold has been established under the assumption that the Yamabe invariant of the fibre Y(F) is positive. The proof follows a concentration‑compactness scheme in the φ‑setting: bubbles can form at interior points, at the boundary, or along the fibres. Boundary bubbling is controlled by a positive mass theorem for asymptotically locally hyperbolic manifolds with fibred boundary. When the fibre Yamabe invariant is non‑positive, the infimum of the Yamabe functional equals the infimum on the interior, and a minimiser exists without bubbling. The φ‑Yamabe problem also has applications to the Einstein constraint equations on manifolds with fibred boundary, where the conformal method reduces the Hamiltonian constraint to a Yamabe‑type equation.

The Yamabe Problem and the Conformal Invariant of the Conformal Laplacian on Manifolds with Corners

Manifolds with corners arise naturally in the study of polyhedral domains, in the compactification of moduli spaces, and in the analysis of edge singularities after iterated blow‑ups. A corner of codimension k is locally modelled on [0,∞)^k × ℝn−k. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks for a conformal metric with constant scalar curvature that is complete up to the corners and has a prescribed singular behaviour. The appropriate function spaces are weighted Hölder or Sobolev spaces where the weight is a multi‑index δ = (δ₁,…,δ_k) measuring decay relative to each boundary defining function x_i.

The conformal Laplacian on a manifold with corners is a degenerate elliptic operator in the sense of the b‑calculus iterated k times. Its indicial family is a multi‑parameter family obtained by successive Mellin transforms in each x_i. The indicial roots form a lattice in ℂ^k determined by the eigenvalues of the model operators on the corner faces. For the Yamabe operator the roots are σ = (σ₁,…,σ_k) with σ_i = (n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)²/4 + λ_i) where λ_i are eigenvalues of the Laplacian on the corresponding face. The operator is Fredholm between weighted spaces provided the weight multi‑index avoids the real parts of all indicial roots.

The variational formulation of the Yamabe problem on manifolds with corners requires a careful treatment of the boundary terms that appear when integrating by parts. The Yamabe functional includes contributions from the mean curvature of the faces and the dihedral angles at the corners. Research has shown that if the Yamabe invariant of each face is positive and the dihedral angles satisfy a certain convexity condition, then the infimum of the Yamabe functional is strictly less than the Yamabe invariant of the sphere, and a minimiser exists. The proof uses a blow‑up analysis at the corners: a bubble forming at a corner would converge to a solution of the Yamabe problem on the model corner [0,∞)^k × ℝn−k, which can be classified using the method of moving planes. The classification shows that no non‑trivial bubble can exist under the convexity condition, ruling out corner bubbling. This result has been extended to the σ_k‑Yamabe problem on manifolds with corners.

The CR Yamabe Problem: Conformal Geometry of CR Manifolds

The CR Yamabe problem is the analogue of the Yamabe problem for strictly pseudoconvex CR manifolds of dimension 2n+1. A CR manifold carries a contact distribution H and a complex structure J on H. A pseudohermitian structure is a choice of contact form θ annihilating H; its Webster scalar curvature R_θ plays the role of the Riemannian scalar curvature. Two contact forms θ and θ̂ = e2u θ are conformally related, and the Webster curvature transforms according to the CR Yamabe equation −4n Δ_b u + R_θ u = R_θ̂ u(n+2)/n where Δ_b is the sub‑Laplacian. The CR Yamabe invariant YCR(M) is the infimum of the CR Yamabe functional ∫ (4n |∇_b u|² + R_θ u²) θ ∧ (dθ)^n / (∫ u2(n+1)/n θ ∧ (dθ)^n)n/(n+1) over positive functions u.

The solution of the CR Yamabe problem was established for the sphere S2n+1 and for general CR manifolds. The proof follows the Riemannian strategy: the subcritical approximation, the concentration‑compactness principle, and the positive mass theorem for the CR invariant. The CR positive mass theorem was proved using the CR version of the Witten spinor argument. A key difference from the Riemannian case is that the CR sphere S2n+1 has a non‑compact symmetry group (the group of CR automorphisms), which leads to a lack of compactness even for the subcritical problem. This is handled by a refined blow‑up analysis that uses the classification of solutions of the CR Yamabe equation on the Heisenberg group (the flat model).

The CR Yamabe problem has deep connections with the embedding problem for CR manifolds, the Bergman kernel asymptotics, and the geometry of the Fefferman metric. In particular, a CR manifold with positive CR Yamabe invariant admits a pseudo‑Einstein contact form (constant Webster curvature) if and only if the CR obstruction tensor vanishes. The CR obstruction tensor is the analogue of the Weyl tensor in CR geometry and its vanishing is necessary for the existence of a CR pluriharmonic embedding into a sphere. Recent work has extended the CR Yamabe problem to the σ_k‑Yamabe problem for the CR Schouten tensor, leading to fully nonlinear CR invariant equations.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Asymptotically Conical Ends

An asymptotically conical (AC) manifold is a complete non‑compact Riemannian manifold (M,g) that outside a compact set is diffeomorphic to a cone C(Σ)= (0,∞)_r × Σ with metric dr2 + r2 g_Σ, where (Σ,g_Σ) is a compact cross‑section. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks whether one can find a conformal metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g of constant scalar curvature that preserves the asymptotic cone structure. The functional setting uses weighted Sobolev spaces Wk,p_δ with weight δ chosen away from the indicial roots of the conformal Laplacian L_g = -(4(n-1))/(n-2)Δ_g + R_g on the cone. The indicial roots are λ_± = -(n-2)/(2) ± √(bigl((n-2)/(2)bigr)2 + μ_j), where μ_j are eigenvalues of the Laplacian on Σ. If the weight δ lies between the two smallest positive indicial roots, the conformal Laplacian is Fredholm of index zero.

Existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in the conformal class of an AC metric was proved by Mazzeo, Pacard, and others using a variational method combined with a blow‑up analysis at infinity. The Yamabe invariant for AC manifolds is defined as the infimum of the Yamabe functional over the conformal class, with the volume term replaced by the renormalised volume of the cone. A key technical point is that the Sobolev embedding W1,2_δ L2n/(n-2)_δ holds precisely when δ > -(n-2)/(2). When the Yamabe invariant is strictly less than the invariant of the model cone, a minimiser exists and yields a smooth solution of the Yamabe equation L_g u = λ u(n+2)/(n-2) with u-1 ∈ W2,2_δ. If the invariant equals that of the cone, non‑existence can occur due to the formation of a bubble at infinity; the bubble is a solution on the exact cone, classified by the method of moving planes.

Applications include the construction of constant scalar curvature metrics on resolutions of orbifold singularities and the study of the ADM mass for asymptotically flat manifolds with conical ends. The positive mass theorem for AC manifolds, proved by a spinor argument adapted to the conical geometry, guarantees that the Yamabe invariant is positive whenever the cross‑section has positive Yamabe invariant and the cone is not isometric to Euclidean space.

The Yamabe Problem and the Stability of Constant Scalar Curvature Metrics

Once a constant scalar curvature metric tilde g in a conformal class [g] is obtained, a natural question is whether it is a local minimiser of the Yamabe functional, i.e. whether the second variation is non‑negative. The second variation formula at a solution u of the Yamabe equation reads δ2 Y(u)[φ] = ∫_M bigl( (4(n-1))/(n-2) |∇ φ|2 + Rtilde g φ2 - λ (n+2)/(n-2) u4/(n-2) φ2 bigr) dVtilde g. Stability is equivalent to the non‑negativity of the first eigenvalue of Ltilde g.

Schoen proved that on a compact manifold of dimension n ≥ 3, any Yamabe minimiser is stable. Conversely, if a constant scalar curvature metric is stable and the Yamabe invariant is positive, then it is a local minimiser. In dimensions n ≥ 6 the Weyl tensor enters the stability analysis through the Bochner formula for the conformal Killing operator; Aubin showed that on locally conformally flat manifolds the first eigenvalue of Ltilde g is strictly positive unless the metric is Einstein. For Einstein metrics with positive scalar curvature, the stability condition reduces to the positivity of the Lichnerowicz Laplacian on trace‑free symmetric 2‑tensors, which holds for many symmetric spaces but fails for certain Kähler–Einstein manifolds (e.g. CP2 with the Fubini–Study metric).

The stability question is closely linked to the uniqueness of constant scalar curvature metrics in a conformal class. In the positive Yamabe invariant case, the solution is unique up to scaling and the action of the conformal group; in the zero or negative case, uniqueness holds without scaling ambiguity. When stability fails, bifurcation phenomena can produce families of non‑constant scalar curvature metrics, a topic explored in the context of the prescribed scalar curvature problem and the Kazdan–Warner obstructions.

The Yamabe Problem and the Uniformization Theorem in Higher Dimensions

The classical uniformization theorem states that every closed Riemann surface admits a metric of constant curvature in its conformal class. The Yamabe problem is the natural higher‑dimensional analogue: on a compact manifold of dimension n ≥ 3, does every conformal class contain a metric of constant scalar curvature? The answer is affirmative, but the constant curvature metric is not unique in general because the conformal group of the sphere S^n is non‑compact. The uniformization picture becomes richer when one considers the sign of the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]). If Y>0, the conformal class contains a metric of positive constant scalar curvature; by a result of Gursky and Viaclovsky, if in addition the Weyl tensor vanishes and the manifold is not conformally equivalent to the sphere, the metric is unique up to the action of the conformal group. If Y=0, there is a unique (up to scaling) metric of zero scalar curvature; this is the higher‑dimensional counterpart of the flat metric on a torus. If Y<0, there is a unique metric of negative constant scalar curvature, analogous to the hyperbolic metric on surfaces of genus ≥ 2.

The proof of the Yamabe theorem follows a three‑step variational strategy: (1) subcritical approximation, (2) concentration‑compactness to rule out bubbling, (3) positive mass theorem to compare the Yamabe invariant with that of the sphere. In dimension two the positive mass theorem is replaced by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. The role of the Sobolev inequality is played by the Moser–Trudinger inequality in two dimensions. The classification of constant scalar curvature metrics on locally conformally flat manifolds (Schoen–Yau) mirrors the classification of constant curvature metrics on surfaces: they are quotients of the sphere, Euclidean space, or hyperbolic space by discrete groups of conformal transformations.

Recent work extends this uniformization perspective to orbifolds and manifolds with singularities. The CR Yamabe problem provides a uniformization theorem for strictly pseudoconvex CR manifolds, where the model spaces are the CR sphere and the Heisenberg group. The σ_k‑Yamabe problem gives a fully nonlinear uniformization for the Schouten tensor, with the σ_k‑curvature playing the role of scalar curvature.

The Yamabe Problem on Asymptotically Euclidean Manifolds and the ADM Mass

An asymptotically Euclidean manifold (M,g) of dimension n ≥ 3 is a complete non‑compact Riemannian manifold that outside a compact set is diffeomorphic to ℝⁿ B_R(0) and whose metric satisfies gij = δij + O(|x|-τ) with τ > (n-2)/2. The Yamabe problem on such a manifold asks for a conformal metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g of constant scalar curvature, where u > 0 tends to 1 at infinity. The conformal Laplacian L_g = -4(n-1)/(n-2) Δ_g + R_g appears in the Euler‑Lagrange equation L_g u = λ u(n+2)/(n-2). Because the manifold is non‑compact, the variational functional Q_g(u) = (∫_M (4(n-1)/(n-2) |∇u|² + R_g u²) dV_g) / (∫_M |u|2n/(n-2) dV_g)(n-2)/n is not automatically coercive; the loss of compactness is measured by the ADM mass.

The ADM mass mADM(g) is defined as the limit (1/2(n-1)ωn-1) ∫S_r (∂_j gij - ∂_i gjj) ν^i dσ as r → ∞. Schoen and Yau proved the positive mass theorem: if R_g ≥ 0 and (M,g) is asymptotically Euclidean, then mADM(g) ≥ 0 with equality only for Euclidean space. In the Yamabe context, the mass enters the expansion of the Yamabe functional evaluated on the Aubin test function u_ε(x) = (ε/(ε²+|x|²))(n-2)/2 transplanted to M via a conformal normal chart. One obtains Q_g(u_ε) = Y(Sⁿ) - C_n mADM(g) εn-2 + o(εn-2) for n ≥ 3, where C_n > 0. Hence if mADM(g) > 0 the strict inequality Q_g(u_ε) < Y(Sⁿ) holds, guaranteeing the existence of a minimiser by the concentration‑compactness principle. If the mass vanishes, the manifold is conformally flat and the problem reduces to the Euclidean case, where the minimiser is the standard bubble.

When the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) is negative, the functional is bounded below but the minimiser may not exist because the infimum can be approached by a sequence that drifts to infinity. The work of Bartnik, Chruściel, and others shows that on asymptotically Euclidean manifolds with non‑negative scalar curvature the Yamabe invariant equals the infimum of the ADM mass over the conformal class, linking the analytic problem to the geometric mass. This relationship underpins several approaches to the Riemannian Penrose inequality, where a Yamabe metric can be used to make the scalar curvature constant and express the ADM mass as a flux integral.

Key technical points: (1) The conformal Laplacian on an asymptotically Euclidean manifold is a Fredholm operator of index zero between weighted Sobolev spaces H2,δ → L2,δ-2 for δ ∈ (2-n,0). (2) The indicial roots of L_g at infinity are 0 and 2-n; the weight δ must avoid these to obtain invertibility. (3) The positive mass theorem is used precisely to rule out the case where a minimizing sequence concentrates at infinity, which would correspond to a zero‑mass bubble. (4) If the manifold is spin, Witten’s spinor proof of the positive mass theorem gives an alternative argument that avoids the minimal‑hypersurface technique.

Indicial Roots and Fredholm Theory for the Yamabe Problem on Asymptotically Conical Manifolds

An asymptotically conical manifold (M,g) of dimension n ≥ 3 has a cylindrical end diffeomorphic to (0,∞) × Σ where Σ is a closed (n-1)-dimensional manifold, and the metric takes the form g = dr² + r² h(r) with h(r) → h0 smoothly as r → ∞. The cross‑section (Σ, h0) is called the link. The Yamabe equation on such a manifold is L_g u = λ u(n+2)/(n-2) with u > 0. Because the geometry is scale‑invariant at infinity, the natural function spaces are weighted Hölder or Sobolev spaces r^δ Ck,α or r^δ H^k, where the weight δ measures the decay or growth rate of functions along the cone.

The conformal Laplacian L_g = -4(n-1)/(n-2) Δ_g + R_g is an elliptic operator with coefficients that are homogeneous of degree -2 at infinity. Its indicial operator is obtained by freezing coefficients at r = ∞ and looking for solutions of the form r-s φ(θ). The indicial roots s satisfy the eigenvalue problem on the link: Δh0 φ + ( (n-2)²/4 - s² ) φ = 0. Equivalently, s = (n-2)/2 ± √( (n-2)²/4 + μ_k ), where μ_k are the eigenvalues of the Laplacian on (Σ, h0). The set of indicial roots is discrete and symmetric about (n-2)/2. For the Yamabe problem one typically works in the weight interval δ ∈ (0, n-2) to ensure that the constant function 1 lies in the weighted space and that the nonlinearity u(n+2)/(n-2) maps the space to itself.

Fredholm theory for L_g on weighted spaces states that L_g : r^δ H2 → rδ-2 L2 is Fredholm of index zero provided δ is not an indicial root. The kernel consists of solutions that decay like r-δ at infinity; the cokernel is identified with the kernel of the adjoint operator, which has the same indicial roots. When δ crosses an indicial root, the kernel dimension jumps, reflecting the appearance of new homogeneous solutions. This jump is the analytic manifestation of the “bubbling” phenomenon: a sequence of Yamabe metrics with bounded energy can develop a bubble that, after rescaling, converges to a solution on the exact cone ℝ⁺ × Σ. The classification of such bubbles reduces to the classification of positive solutions of the Yamabe equation on the cone, which in turn depends on the spectrum of the link.

In dimensions n ≥ 6, if the link has positive Yamabe invariant, the first non‑zero indicial root is s1 = (n-2)/2 + √( (n-2)²/4 + μ1 ) > n-2. Choosing δ ∈ (0, n-2) avoids all indicial roots, so L_g is invertible. This invertibility is the key step in the implicit‑function argument that constructs constant scalar curvature metrics on asymptotically conical manifolds with prescribed asymptotic data. In low dimensions n = 3,4,5 the first indicial root may lie inside (0, n-2), and one must either work with a different weight or use a Lyapunov‑Schmidt reduction to handle the finite‑dimensional kernel. The work of Mazzeo, Pacard, and others shows that the obstruction to solving the Yamabe problem in these dimensions is precisely the vanishing of a certain “mass” term associated with the Green’s function of L_g, analogous to the ADM mass in the Euclidean case.

Renormalized Volume and the Mass in the Asymptotically Hyperbolic Yamabe Problem

An asymptotically hyperbolic manifold (M,g) of dimension n ≥ 3 is a complete Riemannian manifold whose metric near infinity can be written in the form g = (dr² + h_r)/r², where r is a defining function for the conformal boundary ∂M and h_r is a family of metrics on ∂M converging to a representative h0 of the conformal infinity. The Yamabe problem on such a manifold seeks a conformal metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g with constant scalar curvature tilde R = -n(n-1) (the scalar curvature of hyperbolic space). The conformal factor u is required to have a prescribed asymptotic behaviour u = r(n-2)/2 (1 + O(r)) so that tilde g remains asymptotically hyperbolic with the same conformal infinity.

The variational functional is the renormalized Einstein‑Hilbert action, which after conformal transformation becomes the Yamabe functional Q_g(u) with an additional boundary term that encodes the renormalized volume. The renormalized volume V_R(g) is defined via the Hadamard regularization of the divergent volume integral ∫_M dV_g. Graham and Zworski showed that the coefficient of the log term in the volume expansion is a conformal invariant (the Q‑curvature integral), while the finite part V_R changes under conformal rescaling by a formula involving the boundary Yamabe functional. In the Yamabe problem, the mass m(g) is defined as the coefficient of the rn term in the expansion of the volume of geodesic balls, or equivalently as the integral of the scalar curvature against a suitable test function. The positive mass theorem for asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds (Wang, Chruściel‑Herzlich) states that if R_g ≥ -n(n-1) then m(g) ≥ 0, with equality iff (M,g) is hyperbolic space.

In the existence proof, one uses the Aubin test function transplanted to the hyperbolic end. The expansion of Q_g(u_ε) yields Y(Sⁿ) - C_n m(g) εn + o(εn) for n ≥ 3. The strict inequality Q_g(u_ε) < Y(Sⁿ) follows from m(g) > 0 unless the manifold is hyperbolic. When the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) is less than the hyperbolic space invariant Y(Hⁿ) = Y(Sⁿ), a minimiser exists by concentration‑compactness. If Y(M,[g]) = Y(Hⁿ), the manifold is conformally equivalent to hyperbolic space (a rigidity result of Qing). The renormalized volume enters the compactness theory: a sequence of Yamabe metrics with bounded renormalized volume has a subsequence converging in C2,αloc to a Yamabe metric, and the limit’s renormalized volume is lower semicontinuous. This compactness is essential for the deformation method that constructs constant scalar curvature metrics in a given conformal class of the boundary.

Key distinctions from the Euclidean case: (1) The conformal infinity is a compact manifold without boundary, so the Yamabe problem on the boundary is the classical compact Yamabe problem. (2) The indicial roots of the conformal Laplacian at infinity are (n-2)/2 ± √( (n-2)²/4 + μ_k ), where μ_k are the eigenvalues of the conformal Laplacian on the boundary. The weight δ = (n-2)/2 corresponds to the natural decay of the conformal factor. (3) The mass is not the ADM mass but the “hyperbolic mass” defined via the renormalized volume; it is conformally invariant under changes of the defining function that preserve the conformal infinity.

Mellin Transform and the Yamabe Problem on Edge Singularities

An edge singularity is a non‑compact manifold M with a stratified boundary: near the singular stratum B (a closed manifold of dimension b) the metric takes the form g = dx² + x² g_Y + g_B, where x is a radial variable, g_Y is a metric on the link Y (a closed manifold of dimension f), and g_B is a metric on the base B. The total dimension is n = b + f + 1. The Yamabe problem on such a space asks for a conformal metric of constant scalar curvature that is polyhomogeneous at the edge, i.e. has an asymptotic expansion in powers of x and log x with coefficients smooth on B × Y.

The Mellin transform is the natural tool for analyzing elliptic operators on edge manifolds. For a function u(x,y,b) defined near x = 0, the Mellin transform in x is hat u(z,y,b) = ∫0^∞ xz-1 u(x,y,b) dx, which converts the x‑derivative ∂_x into multiplication by z. The conformal Laplacian L_g becomes a family of operators L(z) acting on sections over Y, parameterized by the complex variable z. The poles of the meromorphic family L(z)-1 are precisely the indicial roots, which are determined by the spectrum of the model operator on the infinite cone ℝ⁺ × Y. For the Yamabe equation L_g u = λ u(n+2)/(n-2), the nonlinearity is handled by working in weighted edge Sobolev spaces x^δ H^k_e, where the weight δ avoids the real parts of the indicial roots.

The Fredholm property of L_g : x^δ H2_e → xδ-2 L2_e holds for δ not equal to the real part of any indicial root. The kernel consists of solutions that are polyhomogeneous with leading term x-δ φ(y,b). The cokernel is identified via the adjoint operator, which has the same indicial roots. When δ crosses an indicial root, the kernel dimension changes, and one must add or remove asymptotic terms to maintain a parametrix. This is the edge analogue of the jump in Fredholm index for conical ends.

In the Yamabe problem, the existence of a constant scalar curvature metric is proved by a continuity method combined with a priori estimates in polyhomogeneous spaces. The key a priori estimate is a Moser iteration adapted to the edge geometry, which yields an L^∞ bound for positive solutions provided the Yamabe invariant of the link Y is positive. If the link has non‑positive Yamabe invariant, the iteration fails and one can construct counterexamples where no solution exists. The positive mass theorem for edge manifolds (proved by Akutagawa, Carron, and others) states that if the scalar curvature is non‑negative and the link has positive Yamabe invariant, then the “edge mass” defined via the Mellin transform is non‑negative, with equality only for the exact cone. This mass appears in the expansion of the Yamabe functional on test functions supported near the edge, guaranteeing the strict inequality needed for existence.

Practical computation: to find the indicial roots for a given edge metric, one writes the model operator on the cone C(Y) = ℝ⁺ × Y, separates variables, and solves the eigenvalue problem for the conformal Laplacian on Y. The roots are s = (n-2)/2 ± √( (n-2)²/4 + μ_k ), where μ_k are the eigenvalues of the conformal Laplacian on Y. The weight δ must be chosen in the interval (0, n-2) avoiding these values. For the standard edge metric on a product B × C(Y), the indicial roots are independent of the base B, which simplifies the analysis.

The σ_k–Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Boundary and the Neumann Condition

The σ_k–Yamabe problem is the fully nonlinear generalisation where one prescribes the σ_k‑curvature of the Schouten tensor A_g = (Ric_g - R_g g/(2(n-1)))/(n-2). For a conformal metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g, the Schouten tensor transforms as Atilde g = A_g - ∇² log u + d log u ⊗ d log u - ½ |∇ log u|² g. The σ_k‑curvature is the k‑th elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues of A_g. The equation σ_k(Atilde g) = constant is a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE of order two, which reduces to the Yamabe equation when k = 1.

On a compact manifold with boundary ∂M, one must impose a boundary condition. The natural Neumann‑type condition for the σ_k‑problem is that the second fundamental form of the boundary with respect to the conformal metric satisfies a prescribed σk-1‑curvature condition, or more simply that the conormal derivative ∂_ν u = 0 (which corresponds to keeping the boundary minimal in the conformal class). The variational functional is the integral of the σ_k‑curvature plus a boundary term involving the σk-1‑curvature of the boundary. The Euler‑Lagrange equation in the interior is σ_k(Atilde g) = λ, and the boundary condition is σk-1(Atilde g|∂M) = μ, where λ, μ are constants determined by the conformal class.

Existence theory for the σ_k–Yamabe problem with boundary was developed by Guan, Wang, and others. The main difficulties are: (1) The equation is fully nonlinear, so the maximum principle and Evans‑Krylov theory replace the linear Schauder estimates. (2) The boundary condition is oblique derivative type; one needs a barrier argument to control the gradient at the boundary. (3) The conformal invariance of the σ_k‑curvature holds only for k = 1,2; for k ≥ 3 the problem is not conformally invariant, and one works within a fixed conformal class of the background metric. The admissibility condition (the eigenvalues of A_g lie in the positive cone Γ_k) is required for ellipticity.

When the boundary is umbilic (i.e. the second fundamental form is proportional to the induced metric), the Neumann condition ∂_ν u = 0 is compatible with the σ_k‑equation. In this case one can use a continuity method starting from the background metric, provided the background metric is admissible and the boundary σk-1‑curvature is positive. The a priori estimates rely on the concavity of σ_k1/k on Γ_k and the maximum principle for the linearised operator. The compactness of the solution set follows from the C2,α estimates of Trudinger and Wang, which are uniform as long as the admissibility cone is not approached.

Applications: The σ2–Yamabe problem with boundary is related to the prescribed Gauss curvature problem for surfaces with boundary and to the construction of constant σ2‑curvature metrics in geometric contexts. The Neumann condition allows the induced metric to vary conformally while keeping the boundary minimal. In dimension four, the σ2‑curvature is a quadratic polynomial in Ricci and scalar curvature, and the boundary σ1‑curvature is the mean curvature. The existence of a solution with ∂_ν u = 0 gives a conformal metric with constant σ2 and minimal boundary, a higher‑dimensional analogue of the uniformization theorem for surfaces with boundary.

  • Confusing the indicial roots for asymptotically conical ends with those for edge singularities; the former involve the spectrum of the Laplacian on the link, the latter involve the spectrum of the model operator on the infinite cone over the link.
  • Assuming that the positive mass theorem for asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds holds without the condition that the conformal infinity has positive Yamabe invariant; the mass can be negative if the boundary Yamabe invariant is non‑positive.
  • Treating the σ_k–Yamabe problem as conformally invariant for all k; only k = 1,2 enjoy full conformal invariance, higher k require a fixed background conformal class.
  • Neglecting the admissibility condition (eigenvalues of the Schouten tensor in Γ_k) when applying the maximum principle; without it the equation is not elliptic.
  • Overlooking the role of the renormalized volume in the compactness theory for asymptotically hyperbolic Yamabe metrics; bounded renormalized volume is necessary for subsequential convergence.
  • Believing that the Neumann condition ∂_ν u = 0 is always the correct boundary condition for the σ_k–Yamabe problem; the natural condition involves the σk-1‑curvature of the boundary, which reduces to ∂_ν u = 0 only when the boundary is umbilic.
  • Forgetting that the Mellin transform converts the edge Yamabe problem into a family of problems on the link, and that the indicial roots are the poles of the inverse family; crossing a root changes the Fredholm index.

These advanced topics illustrate how the Yamabe problem extends beyond the classical compact setting into non‑compact geometries, singular spaces, and fully nonlinear curvature equations, each requiring a tailored analytic framework.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Polyhedral Singularities

Polyhedral singularities arise when a Riemannian manifold is modeled locally on a finite cone over a spherical polyhedron. The link of such a singularity is a spherical orbifold with cone angles that are rational multiples of π. In this setting the conformal Laplacian acquires an indicial family whose roots are determined by the eigenvalues of the Laplacian on the link. The Yamabe equation −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δu + R u = λ u(n+2)/(n−2) must be interpreted in a weighted Sobolev space that captures the asymptotic behaviour u ∼ rα near each vertex, where r is the distance to the singular stratum and α is an indicial root. Existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in the conformal class is equivalent to solving a variational problem on the resolution of the polyhedral space, where the resolution replaces each vertex by a smooth boundary component with a prescribed mean curvature condition.

An analytic framework in the literature shows that if the Yamabe invariant of the resolved manifold with boundary is positive, then a minimiser of the Yamabe functional exists and yields a smooth metric on the regular part that extends as a polyhedral metric across the singular strata. The key step is a blow‑up analysis at the vertices: any sequence of approximate minimisers either converges strongly or develops a bubble that is a solution of the Yamabe equation on the model cone. The classification of bubbles on cones uses the sharp Sobolev inequality on the cone, which holds precisely when the cone angle is less than the critical angle determined by the first eigenvalue of the spherical Laplacian on the link. When the cone angle exceeds this threshold, the Yamabe functional fails to be coercive and minimising sequences can lose mass at the vertex, leading to non‑existence.

Applications of this theory include the construction of constant scalar curvature metrics on orbifold compactifications of moduli spaces and on spaces arising from geometric flows with singularities. In particular, the polyhedral Yamabe problem provides a model for the formation of singularities in the Ricci flow on three‑manifolds, where the singular set is a collection of geodesic segments meeting at vertices with prescribed angles. The conformal deformation technique allows one to replace the singular metric by a smooth metric of constant scalar curvature on the resolution, thereby giving a canonical representative in the conformal class that can be used as an initial datum for a smooth Ricci flow.

The Yamabe Problem and the Conformal Invariant of the Paneitz Operator

The Paneitz operator P_g = Δ_g2 + δ( (n−2)J_g g − 4 A_g ) d + (n−4)/2 Q_g is a fourth‑order conformally covariant operator on a Riemannian manifold of dimension n ≥ 5, where J_g = R_g/(2(n−1)) and A_g is the Schouten tensor. Its principal part is the bi‑Laplacian, and the lower‑order terms involve the Ricci and scalar curvatures. Under a conformal change g̃ = u4/(n−4) g the Paneitz operator transforms as Pg̃ = u-(n+4)/(n−4) P_g u, and the Q‑curvature transforms according to P_g u + 2 Q_g u = 2 Qg̃ u(n+4)/(n−4). The Yamabe‑type problem for Q‑curvature asks whether, given a conformal class [g], one can find a metric with constant Q‑curvature. This leads to the critical fourth‑order equation P_g u = λ u(n+4)/(n−4).

The variational functional associated to this equation is the integral of Q‑curvature, which is conformally invariant only after adding a boundary term on manifolds with boundary. On a closed manifold the functional is not bounded below in general because the Paneitz operator may have negative eigenvalues. The existence theory requires a positivity condition on the conformal invariant Y4(M,[g]) = infg̃∈[g] ∫_M Qg̃ dVg̃ / (Vol(M))(n−4)/n. When Y4 > 0 and the kernel of P_g is trivial, a minimiser exists and yields a constant Q‑curvature metric. If the kernel is non‑trivial, one must impose an orthogonality condition and use a mountain‑pass argument. The sharp Sobolev inequality for the Paneitz operator on the sphere S^n plays the same role as the Aubin‑Talenti inequality for the Yamabe problem.

Connections with the classical Yamabe problem appear through the conformal Laplacian L_g = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g + R_g. In dimension four the Paneitz operator reduces to the square of the conformal Laplacian plus a divergence term, and the Q‑curvature equation becomes a second‑order equation after a suitable gauge fixing. Moreover, the positivity of the Yamabe invariant Y(M) implies the positivity of Y4 for locally conformally flat manifolds, but the converse fails in general. This hierarchy of conformal invariants governs the existence of metrics with prescribed curvature quantities and provides obstructions to the existence of Einstein metrics in the conformal class.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Asymptotically Locally Euclidean Ends

An asymptotically locally Euclidean (ALE) manifold is a complete non‑compact Riemannian manifold (M,g) that outside a compact set is diffeomorphic to (ℝ^n B_R)/Γ, where Γ is a finite subgroup of SO(n) acting freely on the sphere Sn−1. The metric approaches the flat quotient metric at a rate O(r−τ) with τ > (n−2)/2. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks for a conformal metric g̃ = u4/(n−2) g of constant scalar curvature that is also ALE with the same group at infinity. The conformal factor u is required to tend to 1 at infinity so that the volume growth and the ADM mass are preserved.

The functional setting uses weighted Sobolev spaces W2,2_δ with weight δ ∈ (2−n,0). The conformal Laplacian L_g = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g + R_g is Fredholm of index zero between W2,2_δ and L2δ−2 provided δ avoids the indicial roots of the model operator on the cone ℝ^n/Γ. The indicial roots are determined by the eigenvalues of the Laplacian on the link Sn−1/Γ; the smallest positive root is λ_+ = (2−n + √((n−2)2 + 4μ1))/2, where μ1 is the first eigenvalue on the link. Choosing δ ∈ (2−n, λ_+) guarantees that the kernel of L_g consists only of constants, which are excluded by the decay condition. Under the positive mass theorem for ALE spaces, the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) is positive unless (M,g) is conformally flat with zero mass, in which case the infimum is zero and is not attained.

When Y(M,[g]) > 0, a minimiser of the Yamabe functional exists and yields a constant scalar curvature ALE metric. The proof follows the concentration‑compactness method adapted to the non‑compact setting: any minimizing sequence either converges strongly or splits into a sum of a limit profile and a bubble that is a solution on the model space ℝ^n/Γ. The classification of bubbles on ALE spaces uses the positive mass theorem to rule out non‑trivial bubbles unless the mass is zero. In the zero‑mass case the space is conformally equivalent to the flat quotient, and the Yamabe problem reduces to the Euclidean problem on ℝ^n/Γ, which has no solution because the Sobolev inequality is strict. Thus the Yamabe problem on ALE manifolds has a solution exactly when the ADM mass is positive.

The Yamabe Problem and the Stability of Constant Scalar Curvature Metrics

Given a constant scalar curvature metric g in a conformal class [g₀], its stability under the Yamabe flow ∂_t g = −(R_g − r_g) g, where r_g is the average scalar curvature, is governed by the spectrum of the linearised operator L_g = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δ_g + R_g − (n+2)/(n−2) r_g. The metric is linearly stable if the first eigenvalue λ1(L_g) is positive. By the variational characterisation, λ1(L_g) = inf∫ u = 0 ∫ (4(n−1)/(n−2) |∇u|2 + (R_g − (n+2)/(n−2) r_g) u2) / ∫ u2. Since R_g = r_g is constant, this simplifies to λ1 = inf∫ u = 0 (4(n−1)/(n−2) ∫ |∇u|2 − 4/(n−2) r_g ∫ u2) / ∫ u2. Using the first non‑zero eigenvalue μ1 of −Δ_g, we obtain λ1 = 4(n−1)/(n−2) μ1 − 4/(n−2) r_g.

If the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g₀]) is positive, then r_g > 0 for the minimiser, and stability is equivalent to μ1 > r_g/(n−1). On the round sphere S^n the first eigenvalue is n, the scalar curvature is n(n−1), so λ1 = 0, reflecting the conformal Killing fields that generate the non‑uniqueness of Yamabe metrics. On a manifold with positive Yamabe invariant but not conformally equivalent to the sphere, the minimiser is unique up to conformal diffeomorphisms and λ1 > 0, giving linear stability. The nonlinear stability follows from the Łojasiewicz‑Simon gradient inequality for the Yamabe functional, which holds because the functional is real analytic on the Sobolev space H1.

When the Yamabe invariant is zero or negative, the constant scalar curvature metric has r_g ≤ 0. In the zero case the metric is Ricci‑flat and the linearised operator reduces to the conformal Laplacian; stability is equivalent to the non‑existence of non‑trivial conformal Killing fields with zero eigenvalue. In the negative case r_g < 0, the term −4/(n−2) r_g is positive, so λ1 > 0 automatically, and the metric is linearly stable. However, the Yamabe flow may not converge to the constant scalar curvature metric if the initial metric lies in a different component of the conformal class; the flow can develop singularities or converge to a different critical point. The global dynamics of the Yamabe flow on manifolds with negative Yamabe invariant remain an active area of research.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Fibred Cusps and the Adiabatic Limit

A fibred cusp manifold is a complete manifold whose end is diffeomorphic to (0,∞)_x × F, where F → B is a fibre bundle with compact fibre Z and base B, and the metric takes the form dx2 + x2 g_B + g_Z + lower order terms. This geometry appears in the compactification of locally symmetric spaces of rank one. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks for a conformal metric of constant scalar curvature that preserves the fibred cusp structure at infinity. The conformal factor u is sought in a weighted Sobolev space x^δ H^bb, where H^bb is the b‑Sobolev space adapted to the fibred boundary.

The indicial family of the conformal Laplacian is obtained by freezing coefficients at x = 0 and taking the Mellin transform in x. The resulting family of operators on the fibre Z depends on the spectral parameter σ ∈ ℂ. The indicial roots are the values of σ for which the operator fails to be invertible; they are determined by the eigenvalues of the vertical Laplacian on Z and the horizontal Laplacian on B. The admissible weights δ are those that avoid the real parts of the indicial roots. For the Yamabe problem one typically chooses δ ∈ (0, (n−2)/2) so that the conformal factor tends to a constant at infinity and the volume growth is unchanged.

Existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in the fibred cusp class has been established using a continuity method combined with a blow‑up analysis at the boundary. The key analytic input is the invertibility of the linearised operator on the model fibred cusp space, which follows from the positivity of the Yamabe invariant of the compactified manifold with boundary. The adiabatic limit corresponds to shrinking the fibre Z while keeping the base B fixed; in this limit the Yamabe equation reduces to a lower‑dimensional equation on B involving the effective potential coming from the fibre geometry. This reduction provides a bridge between the Yamabe problem on high‑dimensional fibred cusp manifolds and the classical Yamabe problem on the base, and it has been used to construct constant scalar curvature metrics on moduli spaces of Higgs bundles.

  • Assuming the Paneitz operator is conformally invariant in all dimensions; it is only conformally covariant, and the Q‑curvature functional is conformally invariant only after a boundary correction.
  • Neglecting the weight condition δ ∈ (2−n, λ_+) when working on ALE spaces; choosing δ outside this interval makes the conformal Laplacian non‑Fredholm and the variational problem ill‑posed.
  • Believing that a positive Yamabe invariant automatically implies stability of the Yamabe flow; linear stability requires the stricter spectral gap μ1 > r_g/(n−1).
  • Overlooking the role of the positive mass theorem in ruling out bubbles on ALE and fibred cusp manifolds; without the mass positivity the concentration‑compactness argument fails.
  • Treating the adiabatic limit as a simple product limit; the effective potential on the base depends non‑trivially on the geometry of the fibre and must be computed via the fibrewise Yamabe problem.
  • Forgetting that the sharp Sobolev inequality on a cone holds only when the first eigenvalue of the link exceeds the critical value; otherwise the Yamabe functional is not coercive and minimisers may not exist.

These advanced topics illustrate how the Yamabe problem extends into singular, non‑compact, and higher‑order curvature settings, each demanding a precise analytic framework and a clear understanding of the underlying geometric invariants.

The Yamabe Problem on Asymptotically Locally Hyperbolic Manifolds and the Renormalized Volume

An asymptotically locally hyperbolic (ALH) manifold is a complete Riemannian manifold (M,g) of dimension n ≥ 3 that admits a compactification bar M = M ∪ ∂M such that the metric g takes the form g = x-2(dx2 + h_x) near the boundary, where x is a defining function for ∂M and h_x is a family of metrics on ∂M with h0 a representative of the conformal infinity. The Yamabe problem on ALH manifolds asks for a metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g in the conformal class of g that has constant scalar curvature Rtilde g = -n(n-1) (the scalar curvature of the hyperbolic metric) and preserves the ALH structure. The conformal factor u is sought in a weighted Hölder or Sobolev space x^δ C2,α with weight δ ∈ (0, n/2) so that u → 1 at infinity and the volume growth remains hyperbolic.

The linearised operator at the hyperbolic metric is the conformal Laplacian L_g = -4(n-1)/(n-2) Δ_g + R_g. On an ALH manifold its indicial roots are 0 and n; the weight δ must avoid these values to obtain a Fredholm operator. The renormalized volume V_R(g), introduced by Henningson–Skenderis and Graham–Witten in the context of AdS/CFT, is a conformal invariant of the ALH structure. For a conformal change tilde g = u4/(n-2) g with u = 1 + O(x^n), the renormalized volume changes by a boundary integral involving the Q-curvature of the conformal infinity. Qing and later Chang–Qing established that if the Yamabe invariant of the conformal infinity is positive, then there exists a constant scalar curvature metric in the ALH class with the same renormalized volume as the hyperbolic metric. The proof employs a continuity method combined with a blow‑up analysis that rules out concentration of the Yamabe functional at infinity; the positive mass theorem for ALH manifolds (due to Wang, Chruściel–Herzlich) guarantees non‑negative mass aspect, which prevents bubbling.

When the conformal infinity has zero or negative Yamabe invariant, the problem becomes more subtle. In the zero case the renormalized volume is stationary at the hyperbolic metric, and the linearised operator has a non‑trivial kernel consisting of conformal Killing fields of the boundary. Stability of the constant scalar curvature metric is equivalent to the non‑existence of non‑trivial solutions of the linearised equation with the prescribed weight. In the negative case the Yamabe functional is coercive, but the flow may develop singularities if the initial metric lies in a different component of the conformal class. The renormalized volume then serves as a Lyapunov functional for the Yamabe flow on ALH manifolds, and its monotonicity under the flow was established by Brendle–Marques. These results illustrate how the renormalized volume, a purely conformal invariant, governs both the existence and the dynamical stability of constant scalar curvature metrics on asymptotically locally hyperbolic spaces.

The Yamabe Problem for Higher Order Curvature: The GJMS Operators and Q‑Curvature

The classical Yamabe problem concerns the second‑order conformal Laplacian. Graham, Jenne, Mason and Sparling (1992) constructed a family of conformally covariant differential operators P2k of order 2k (the GJMS operators) on any Riemannian manifold of dimension n > 2k. The principal symbol of P2k is Δ^k, and under a conformal change tilde g = e2ω g the operator transforms as P2ktilde g = e-(n/2 + k)ω P2k^g e(n/2 - k)ω. The associated curvature quantity is the Q-curvature Q2k, defined by P2k(1) = Q2k. For k=1 one recovers the conformal Laplacian and the scalar curvature; for k=2 the operator is the Paneitz operator and Q4 is the Branson Q-curvature.

The higher‑order Yamabe problem asks: given a conformal class [g] on a compact manifold M^n with n > 2k, does there exist a metric tilde g ∈ [g] with constant Q-curvature? The variational formulation uses the functional I_k(u) = ∫_M u P2k u dV_g / (∫_M u2n/(n-2k) dV_g)(n-2k)/n for positive functions u. The critical Sobolev exponent is 2n/(n-2k), and the sharp Sobolev inequality for P2k on the sphere S^n, established by Beckner and extended by Chang–Yang for k=2, provides the best constant. If the Yamabe invariant Y_k(M) = infu>0 I_k(u) is strictly less than the invariant of the sphere, a minimiser exists by the concentration‑compactness principle of Lions. The main obstruction to compactness is possible bubbling of sequences at points where the Q-curvature concentrates; the positive mass theorem for the GJMS operators (established by Qing–Raske for k=2 and by Case for general k on locally conformally flat manifolds) rules out such bubbles under positive mass conditions.

On locally conformally flat manifolds the GJMS operators coincide with powers of the Laplacian, and the problem reduces to the classical Yamabe problem for the conformal Laplacian after a suitable conformal change. In the general case the existence theory is incomplete: for k ≥ 3 the sharp Sobolev inequality is not known on arbitrary manifolds, and the positive mass theorem remains open. However, for four‑manifolds (k=2) the work of Gursky–Malchiodi, Hang–Yang, and others has established existence under generic conditions on the Weyl tensor and the Q-curvature integral. The higher‑order Yamabe invariant also detects exotic smooth structures: on certain exotic spheres the Q-curvature invariant differs from the standard sphere, providing a conformal invariant that distinguishes smooth structures in dimensions n ≥ 5.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Boundary: The Neumann Problem and the Boundary Yamabe Invariant

Let (M^n,g) be a compact Riemannian manifold with smooth boundary ∂M. The conformal Laplacian L_g = -4(n-1)/(n-2) Δ_g + R_g acts on functions u satisfying the Neumann boundary condition ∂_ν u + (n-2)/2 H_g u = 0 on ∂M, where H_g is the mean curvature of ∂M with respect to the inward normal ν. Under a conformal change tilde g = u4/(n-2) g with u>0, the scalar curvature and the boundary mean curvature transform as Rtilde g = u-(n+2)/(n-2) L_g u and Htilde g = u-n/(n-2) (∂_ν u + (n-2)/2 H_g u). The boundary Yamabe problem asks for a metric tilde g in the conformal class with constant scalar curvature Rtilde g = r and minimal boundary Htilde g = 0. This is equivalent to solving the Neumann problem L_g u = r u(n+2)/(n-2) in M, ∂_ν u + (n-2)/2 H_g u = 0 on ∂M.

The boundary Yamabe invariant is defined as Y(M,∂M) = infu>0 (∫_M (4(n-1)/(n-2) |∇u|2 + R_g u2) dV_g + 2(n-1) ∫∂M H_g u2 dS_g)/((∫_M u2n/(n-2) dV_g)(n-2)/n). Escobar (1992) showed that if Y(M,∂M) < Y(S^n_+, ∂S^n_+), where S^n_+ is the standard hemisphere, then a minimiser exists and yields a constant scalar curvature metric with minimal boundary. The proof employs blow‑up analysis at the boundary; the positive mass theorem for manifolds with boundary (due to Shi–Tam and later generalized by Miao) guarantees positive mass for any bubble, preventing loss of compactness. When the boundary is umbilic (H_g constant) and the Weyl tensor vanishes on ∂M, the invariant equals that of the hemisphere and the problem reduces to the classification of solutions on the ball by Chen–Li–Ou.

If the boundary is not minimal, one can also prescribe a constant boundary mean curvature Htilde g = h. The corresponding variational problem includes a boundary term ∫∂M u2(n-1)/(n-2) dS_g. The existence theory for this mixed problem was developed by Marques (2005) and later by Almaraz (2012). The boundary Yamabe invariant also appears in the study of the Bartnik mass and the quasi‑local mass in general relativity: the Hawking mass of a surface can be expressed in terms of the boundary Yamabe invariant of the enclosed region. This connection has been used by Miao–Tam and others to prove rigidity results for the Penrose inequality in the presence of a boundary.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Corners: Blow‑up Analysis and the Corner Singularities

A manifold with corners M is a compact space locally modelled on [0,∞)^k × ℝn-k for some k ≤ n. The metric g is assumed to be smooth up to the corners and to have a product structure near each corner: in coordinates (x1,…,x_k, y) with x_i ≥ 0, g = ∑ dx_i2 + h(y) + lower order. The conformal Laplacian L_g is defined on the interior and extends to a degenerate elliptic operator on the corner‑blown‑up space. The Yamabe problem on such spaces asks for a conformal metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g with constant scalar curvature and with the conformal factor u belonging to a weighted Sobolev space x^δ H1_b where x = ∏ x_i and δ avoids the indicial roots of L_g at the corners.

The indicial family at a corner of codimension k is obtained by freezing coefficients and taking the Mellin transform in each x_i. The resulting operator on the link Sk-1_+ × ∂_y M has eigenvalues λ_j; the indicial roots are σ = (n-2)/2 ± √((n-2)2/4 + λ_j). The admissible weights δ lie in intervals between successive real parts of these roots. For the Yamabe problem one typically chooses δ ∈ (0, (n-2)/2) so that u tends to a constant on each boundary face and the volume growth is unchanged. Akutagawa (2003) and Akutagawa–Botvinnik (2010) carried out the blow‑up analysis for minimizing sequences. They established that if the Yamabe invariant of the corner‑blown‑up manifold is strictly less than the invariant of the model corner ℝ^k_+ × Sn-k, then a minimiser exists. The key step is to rule out concentration at the corners; this is achieved by a positive mass theorem for the conformal Laplacian on manifolds with corners, established via the doubling trick and the classical positive mass theorem on the resulting manifold without boundary.

When the corner is of codimension two, the link is a circle with conical singularities, and the indicial roots are explicitly computable. In this case the Yamabe invariant can be expressed in terms of the angles at the corners. If all angles are less than π, the invariant is positive and the constant scalar curvature metric is unique up to scaling. If some angle exceeds π, the invariant may become negative, and the problem exhibits a bifurcation phenomenon: there can be multiple constant scalar curvature metrics in the same conformal class, distinguished by their behaviour at the corners. These results have applications to the uniformization of surfaces with conical singularities and to the construction of Einstein metrics on orbifolds with corner singularities.

The Yamabe Problem on Asymptotically Cylindrical Manifolds: Spectral Theory and Gluing Constructions

An asymptotically cylindrical manifold (M,g) of dimension n has an end diffeomorphic to [0,∞)_t × N, where N is a closed (n-1)-manifold, and the metric takes the form g = dt2 + h_t with h_t → h_∞ exponentially fast as t → ∞. The Yamabe problem on such manifolds seeks a conformal metric tilde g = u4/(n-2) g with constant scalar curvature Rtilde g = r and with u → 1 at infinity. The linearised operator is the conformal Laplacian L_g on the cylinder. Its spectrum on the cylindrical end is determined by the eigenvalues μ_j of the conformal Laplacian on the cross‑section (N, h_∞): the indicial roots are σ = (n-2)/2 ± √((n-2)2/4 + μ_j). For the Yamabe problem one works in weighted spaces eδt H2 with δ between the smallest positive and the largest negative indicial root, ensuring Fredholmness.

Mazzeo and Pacard (1999) introduced a gluing construction for constant scalar curvature metrics on asymptotically cylindrical manifolds. Given a compact manifold (M0,g0) with boundary ∂M0 = N and a cylindrical end [0,∞) × N, one can attach a long cylinder [0,T] × N and solve the Yamabe problem on the resulting manifold with boundary conditions at t=0 and t=T. As T → ∞, the solutions converge to a constant scalar curvature metric on the complete cylindrical manifold. The linearised operator on the glued manifold has a kernel corresponding to the kernel of the linearised operator on the cross‑section N; the adiabatic limit T → ∞ reduces the problem to a finite‑dimensional bifurcation equation on N. This technique has constructed constant scalar curvature metrics on moduli spaces of Higgs bundles and on the ends of asymptotically locally Euclidean gravitational instantons.

The spectral theory of the conformal Laplacian on the cylinder also governs the stability of the Yamabe flow on asymptotically cylindrical manifolds. If the first eigenvalue μ1 of the cross‑sectional conformal Laplacian satisfies μ1 > r/(n-1), the constant scalar curvature metric is linearly stable. When μ1 = r/(n-1), a neutral mode appears, causing a slow drift along the cylinder; this phenomenon is related to the existence of conformal Killing fields on the cross‑section. The positive mass theorem for asymptotically cylindrical manifolds (proved via the doubling trick and the classical positive mass theorem) ensures positive mass for any bubble in a minimizing sequence, essential for compactness of the Yamabe functional in the cylindrical setting.
