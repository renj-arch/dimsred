Computational Complexity — The Limits of Efficient Computation

Computational complexity theory classifies problems by the resources (time, space) needed to solve them. It asks: which problems are tractable, and which are inherently hard? The P vs NP question — whether every problem with efficiently verifiable solutions also admits efficient solutions — is the central open problem of computer science. The field spans time complexity (P, NP, EXP), space complexity (L, PSPACE), circuit complexity, interactive proofs (IP = PSPACE), and the polynomial hierarchy.

"P vs NP is the great unsolved problem of computer science — it asks whether creativity is fundamentally harder than verification."
— Stephen Cook
Part 1

⏱️ Time Complexity and the Class P

Tractable
Turing Machines, Big-O, and Polynomial-Time Computation
The Turing machine (1936) is the standard model of computation. Time complexity: the number of steps as a function of input length n. P (polynomial time) = {L | L is decided by a deterministic TM in O(n^k) time for some k}. P represents efficiently solvable problems — sorting, shortest paths, linear programming (Khachiyan, 1979), matching. The Cobham-Edmonds thesis: P corresponds to problems that are feasibly solvable in practice. (In practice, O(n³) is often OK; O(2ⁿ) is not for large n.) The time hierarchy theorem: for any time-constructible f(n) > n log n, DTIME(f(n)) ⊊ DTIME(f(2n)³). Problems in P are closed under composition, reduction, and polynomial transformations.
P captures the notion of efficiently solvable problems — sorting, shortest paths, and linear programming are all in P.
Part 2

❓ NP and NP-Completeness

Hard
NP, Cook-Levin Theorem, and the Canonical Hard Problems
NP (nondeterministic polynomial time) = {L | every yes-instance has a poly-size certificate verifiable in poly time}. Equivalently, problems solved by a nondeterministic TM in poly time. SAT (satisfiability of boolean formulas) is in NP. The Cook-Levin theorem (1971): SAT is NP-complete — every problem in NP reduces to SAT in polynomial time. This was the first NP-complete problem. Other NP-complete problems: 3-SAT, CLIQUE, VERTEX COVER, HAMILTONIAN PATH, TRAVELING SALESMAN (decision version), SUBSET SUM, INTEGER PROGRAMMING. Karp's 21 NP-complete problems (1972) established the breadth of NP-completeness. A problem is NP-hard if all NP problems reduce to it; it's NP-complete if also in NP. P ≠ NP would imply NP-complete problems have no polynomial algorithms. The polynomial hierarchy (PH) extends NP: Σ₁ = NP, Π₁ = co-NP, Σ_{k+1} = NP^{Σ_k}. If P = NP, then PH collapses to P.
NP-complete problems are the hardest in NP — solve any one efficiently and you solve every NP problem. P ≠ NP is generally believed but unproven.
Part 3

🧠 Space Complexity and PSPACE

Memory
L, NL, PSPACE, and the Power of Nondeterminism in Space
Space complexity measures the maximum number of tape cells used. L = log space, PSPACE = polynomial space. Savitch's theorem: NSPACE(s(n)) ⊆ DSPACE(s(n)²). For s(n) ≥ log n, nondeterministic space is at most quadratically more powerful than deterministic space. NL = NSPACE(log n). PATH (directed graph connectivity) is NL-complete under log-space reductions. The Immerman-Szelepcsényi theorem (1987): NL = co-NL — nondeterministic logspace is closed under complement (a surprise!). PSPACE contains NP and co-NP. TQBF (true quantified boolean formulas) is PSPACE-complete — determining the truth of formulas with alternating quantifiers (∀x∃y∀z...). The PSPACE hierarchy collapses: PSPACE = NPSPACE (by Savitch). Problems in PSPACE include: generalized geography, optimal game strategies, and regular expression equivalence.
Space complexity behaves differently from time — nondeterministic space is at most quadratically more powerful than deterministic space, and closure under complement holds.
Part 4

⚡ Circuit Complexity, IP, and Advanced Topics

Circuits
Circuit Complexity, Interactive Proofs, and the Power of Randomness
A boolean circuit is a DAG of AND, OR, NOT gates. Circuit complexity studies the circuit size (number of gates) needed to compute a function. P/poly is the class of problems solvable by polynomial-size circuits — it contains P and some undecidable problems. Karp-Lipton theorem: if NP ⊆ P/poly, then PH collapses to Σ₂. The circuit lower bounds are notoriously hard: we can't even prove NEXP ⊄ P/poly. Interactive proofs (IP): a polynomial-time verifier exchanges messages with an all-powerful prover. IP = PSPACE (Shamir, 1990) — a landmark result showing that interactive proofs characterize exactly the problems solvable in polynomial space. Probabilistically Checkable Proofs (PCP) theorem (AS, ALMSS, 1992): every NP proof can be encoded so that it can be verified by reading only O(1) bits. This gives hardness of approximation results — e.g., MAX-3SAT is NP-hard to approximate within 7/8+ε. Derandomization asks whether P = BPP (bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time).
Interactive proofs (IP = PSPACE) and PCPs revolutionized complexity theory — proof verification can be amazingly efficient, and approximating solutions is often as hard as finding exact ones.
