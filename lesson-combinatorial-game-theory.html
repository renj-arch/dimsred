Combinatorial Game Theory — Nimbers, the Sprague-Grundy Theorem & the Surreal Numbers

Combinatorial game theory is the mathematical analysis of two-player perfect-information games with no chance and no hidden information, played under normal play (the player who cannot move loses). The foundational result is the Sprague-Grundy theorem (1935–39): every impartial game is equivalent to a Nim-heap of a certain size, called its Grundy number. This reduces the analysis of impartial games to computing the XOR (Nim-sum) of Grundy numbers. The theory extends to partizan games (where Left and Right have different options) via Conway's surreal numbers — numbers defined as {L|R} where L and R are sets of previously constructed numbers. The temperature theory (cooling and thermographs) analyzes the urgency of moves in cold games. Applications include the analysis of Go endgames, Domineering, Hackenbush, Clobber, and Kayles. The theory is developed in Conway, Berlekamp, and Guy's "Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays".

"Combinatorial game theory is the art of winning at games without playing them."
— John H. Conway
Part 1

📏 Impartial Games and the Sprague-Grundy Theorem

Impartial
Nim, Nimbers, and the Sprague-Grundy Theorem
An impartial game is one in which both players have the same set of moves from any position. The canonical impartial game is Nim: given piles of pebbles, a move consists of removing any positive number of pebbles from a single pile (the player who takes the last pebble wins). Bouton's theorem (1901): a Nim position is a P-position (the previous player wins, i.e., the player to move loses) iff the Nim-sum (bitwise XOR) of the pile sizes is 0. The Sprague-Grundy theorem: every impartial game G is equivalent to a Nim-heap of size 𝒢(G), the Grundy number, defined recursively by 𝒢(G) = mex{𝒢(H) for all options H of G}, where mex(S) is the least non-negative integer not in S. For a Nim-heap of size n, 𝒢(n) = n. The disjunctive sum G+H (the player may move in exactly one component) satisfies 𝒢(G+H) = 𝒢(G) ⊕ 𝒢(H). This reduces the analysis of any impartial game to computing Grundy numbers and taking XORs. Examples: Kayles (bowling pins — knocking down one pin or two adjacent pins splits the row; Grundy numbers are periodic), Dawson's Kayles (a variant with period 34), Treblecross (placing X's on a line; the first to make three in a row wins; Grundy numbers follow a pattern), and Turning Turtles.
Every impartial game under normal play is equivalent to a Nim-heap; the Grundy number is computed via the mex rule and the disjunctive sum uses XOR.
Part 2

🌍 Partizan Games and the Theory of Numbers

Partizan
Partizan Games, Surreal Numbers, and Conway's Construction
A partizan game is a game where Left and Right may have different options. A game is written G = {L | R} where L is the set of Left's options and R is the set of Right's options. The simplest game is the zero game: 0 = { | }. From this, we construct: 1 = {0|}, −1 = {|0}, 2 = {1|}, ½ = {0|1}, and so on. The simplicity rule: {a|b} = the simplest number between a and b when a < b (the simplest number is the one born earliest). The surreal numbers (Conway, 1976) are the set of all games {L|R} where every element of L and R is a surreal number and no element of R is ≤ any element of L. Surreals include all real numbers, plus infinite numbers (ω = {0,1,2,…|}) and infinitesimals (ε = {0|1,½,¼,…}). The recursive arithmetic of surreals: x+y = {x^L+y, x+y^L | x^R+y, x+y^R}; −x = {−x^R | −x^L}; xy = {x^Ly+xy^L−x^Ly^L, x^Ry+xy^R−x^Ry^R | x^Ly+xy^R−x^Ly^R, x^Ry+xy^L−x^Ry^L}. A game is a number if all its left options are less than all its right options — such games are "cold" and behave like numbers. The theorem of the identity of a game: every game that is not a number can be expressed as a number plus a sum of infinitesimals (like ∗ = {0|0}, the upside-down star).
Partizan games are analyzed via Conway's surreal numbers; every cold game (where L < R) is equal to a surreal number, and the recursive definitions give the arithmetic.
Part 3

🧮 Cold Games and the Temperature Theory

Thermography
Temperature, Cooling, and the Thermograph
The temperature τ(G) of a game G measures the urgency of playing in G — how much a player gains by moving first. The mean m(G) of a game is the long-term advantage per move when both players play optimally in many copies of G. The cooling operator G_t = {G^L_t − t | G^R_t + t} reduces the temperature by t (for t ≥ 0). Thermographs are diagrams that plot the mean and temperature of a game as a function of the "tax" t. The thermograph theorem: the cooling process stabilizes at the mean once t drops below the game's temperature — the thermograph becomes a vertical line at the mean. Key infinitesimal games: ∗ (star) = {0|0} — a first-player win; ↑ (up) = {0|∗} — positive but infinitesimal; ↓ (down) = {∗|0} — negative but infinitesimal. The sum ↑+∗ is called tiny. The atomic weights assign a numerical value to infinitesimal positions. In the game of Go, endgame positions are typically numbers plus infinitesimals — the ceteris paribus principle (all else equal, play in the hottest game first). The theory of infinitesimals includes loopy games (games that can cycle indefinitely), analyzed via Etheridge's theory and the Norton multiplication for Go.
Temperature measures the urgency of a move; cooling reduces temperature to reveal the mean; thermographs show how game values change under taxation.
Part 4

🔥 Applications: Go, Domineering, and Hackenbush

Applications
Domineering, Hackenbush, Go Endgames, and the Conway Theory
Domineering: Left places vertical dominoes, Right places horizontal dominoes on a grid. Small boards have simple values — a 2×3 board is ½. The game Hackenbush: a rooted graph with colored edges — Left cuts blue edges, Right cuts red edges, green edges are impartial. The value of a Hackenbush tree is a surreal number (the colon principle: the value of a chain of edges is the simplest number). Green Hackenbush (all edges green) reduces to the analysis of impartial trees — the Grundy number of a tree is the XOR over branches. Clobber: place stones on a grid; a move removes an opponent's stone and replaces it with one's own; values are often infinitesimals. Go endgames: the Chuckle (a position by Conway) decomposes into a sum of numbers and ∗. The Japanese counting rules make Go endgames directly amenable to thermography. The Norton multiplication of games allows scaling and the analysis of large positions. Chomp: the first player always wins (strategy-stealing argument), but the winning move is not known in general. Misère Nim: the player who makes the last move loses — harder; solved by Bouton for Nim (remove all piles of size 1), and generalized for impartial games via the taming theory of misère games. Dawson's Kayles remains a famously difficult impartial game solved with computer assistance (period 34).
Combinatorial game theory has practical applications in Go, Domineering, and Hackenbush; misère play and Chomp remain active research areas.
