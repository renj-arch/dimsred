1. Santali Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

2. History of the Language

Santali is a Munda language of the Austroasiatic family. It has a rich oral tradition; the Ol Chiki script was invented by Raghunath Murmu in 1925, and modern written literature developed in the 20th century.

3. Literary Periods and Movements

  • Oral period: Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.
  • Early written period (20th c.): Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.
  • Modern period (mid-20th c.–present): Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition.

4. Major Authors

  • Raghunath Murmu: Inventor of the Ol Chiki script and pioneer of modern Santali writing.
  • Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Santali poet and writer.
  • Santali Sahitya Akademi writers: Modern Santali poets, dramatists and fiction writers.

5. Major Works

  • Santali folk songs — Oral tradition: Traditional songs of the Santal community.
  • Ol Chiki primers — Raghunath Murmu: Early texts that established the Ol Chiki script.
  • Santali poetry — Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Twentieth-century Santali poetry.
  • Modern Santali drama — Modern Santali writers: Twentieth-century drama.

6. Evolution of Literary Forms: From Oral Folklore to Modern Prose

The trajectory of Santali literature is not merely a linear progression from "oral" to "written," but a complex transformation of form, function, and audience. Understanding this evolution is crucial for Paper I questions that ask about the "nature of change" or "shifts in thematic concerns." The transition was driven by the invention of the Ol Chiki script (1925), which allowed the codification of fluid oral traditions into fixed textual forms.

The Oral Period: Fluidity and Communal Function

In the pre-1925 era, Santali literature existed almost exclusively in an oral mode. The primary forms were Khel (songs), Bhagani (creation myths), and Kabir (narrative poems). These works were characterized by:

  • Metric Flexibility: Unlike fixed poetry, oral songs allowed for improvisation based on the singer's mood or audience reaction.
  • Communal Ownership: Songs like Sohrai and Kuk were not attributed to a single author but belonged to the community. They served ritualistic functions (harvest, marriage, initiation) rather than aesthetic ones in isolation.
  • Motif Repetition: Recurrent themes included nature worship, ancestor reverence, and moral instruction through allegory.

The Transitional Period: The Impact of Ol Chiki (1925–1940)

Raghunath Murmu's invention of the script acted as a catalyst for "fixation." However, this was not an immediate shift to modern prose. The early written period saw the publication of primers and religious texts. This phase introduced standardization. For instance, dialectal variations were smoothed out to create a literary norm. Authors like Sadhu Ramchand Murmu began experimenting with free verse, moving away from strict rhythmic patterns used in folk songs. The audience shifted from the village square (ritual context) to the schoolroom and reading public.

The Modern Period: Prose, Fiction, and Drama

Post-independence, Santali literature diversified significantly. The emergence of fiction required a new narrative structure that did not exist in oral tradition. Writers began exploring individual psychology, social conflict, and urban displacement—themes alien to traditional folk songs. Drama also emerged as a distinct form, often staged for festivals but written with specific dialogue structures. This period is marked by the establishment of the Santali Sahitya Akademi (1960s), which institutionalized literary criticism and canon formation.

Example: Analyzing the Shift in Form
Question: How did the invention of the Ol Chiki script alter the structural integrity of Santali folk songs, and what were the implications for their transmission?
Answer:
The introduction of the Ol Chiki script transformed fluid oral compositions into fixed textual entities. In the oral period, a song like Sohrai could vary in length and wording depending on the singer. With the advent of written literature, specific versions were codified in primers and anthologies.

Implications:
1. Loss of Improvisation: The ability to adapt songs to immediate contexts diminished as "correct" texts became authoritative.
2. Preservation vs. Stagnation: While the core content was preserved from extinction, the dynamic evolution of the language within the song form slowed down.
3. Shift in Audience: The primary mode of transmission shifted from auditory (listening) to visual (reading), changing how literature was consumed and critiqued.
Exam Strategy: Contextualizing "Change"
Tip: When answering questions about literary movements, do not just list dates. Always contrast the function of the literature in different periods. For example, explain how a folk song served a ritual purpose in the oral period but might serve an educational or aesthetic purpose in the modern period. Use terms like "codification," "standardization," and "institutionalization" to demonstrate depth.

7. Thematic Analysis: From Nature Worship to Social Realism

The thematic content of Santali literature has undergone a profound metamorphosis, reflecting the changing socio-political landscape of the Munda-speaking region. This evolution is essential for answering questions regarding "thematic shifts" or "sociological relevance."

1. The Nature-Centric Universe (Pre-20th Century)

Early literature was deeply animistic and ecological. Themes revolved around the veneration of natural elements: Bong (forest), Hajong (sun), Kharia (moon). The relationship between humans and nature was one of symbiosis and reverence. Myths like Bhagani explained the origin of the world through natural forces. There was no concept of "man vs. nature"; rather, it was "man within nature."

2. The Rise of Social Consciousness (Mid-20th Century)

With the influence of Christian missionaries and later socialist movements, literature began to address social ills. Themes of caste discrimination, land alienation, and exploitation by dominant castes entered the literary discourse. Writers like Sankha Barua and others started using literature as a tool for social reform. The "nature" theme was not abandoned but reinterpreted through the lens of human suffering caused by external forces.

3. Modern Realism and Identity Politics (Late 20th Century–Present)

Contemporary Santali literature grapples with themes of identity, globalization, and migration. The "Santal" is no longer just a forest dweller but an urbanite facing alienation. Themes include the loss of traditional values in modern society, the struggle for linguistic rights (e.g., inclusion in the Eighth Schedule), and the intersectionality of Santali identity with broader Indian nationalism. Fiction now explores the inner conflicts of individuals caught between tradition and modernity.

Example: Thematic Contrast
Question: Compare the treatment of 'nature' in a traditional Bong (forest) song versus a modern Santali short story. How does this reflect broader societal changes?
Answer:
In traditional songs, nature is personified and revered as a deity or ancestor. The forest is a source of life and spiritual connection; the language used is reverential and descriptive.

In modern short stories (e.g., by contemporary Akademi writers), nature often appears as a backdrop to human conflict or a symbol of lost innocence. The relationship may be critical, highlighting environmental degradation or the displacement of Santal communities from their ancestral lands.

Conclusion: This shift reflects the transition from an agrarian, forest-dependent economy to one influenced by industrialization and urbanization, where nature is viewed through a lens of loss and nostalgia rather than active worship.
Exam Strategy: Avoiding Anachronism
Tip: Do not attribute modern social realist themes to pre-1925 literature. Examiners often try to trick candidates by asking about "social reform" in the oral period. Always qualify your answer with the specific historical context (e.g., "Post-independence," "After 1947"). Acknowledge that early literature was primarily mythological and ritualistic, not sociological.

8. Criticism and Canon Formation: The Role of Institutions

A sophisticated understanding of Santali literature requires knowledge of how "canon" is formed. In the oral period, there was no canon; authority lay with tradition. The modern period introduced literary criticism, journals, and institutions like the Santali Sahitya Akademi. This section covers the mechanisms by which certain works became "classic" while others were marginalized.

The Mechanism of Canonization

Literary works in Santali are elevated to "classic" status through:

  1. Institutional Recognition: Awards from the Sahitya Akademi (New Delhi and regional bodies) play a significant role.
  2. Translation and Anthologization: Works that are translated into Hindi or English often gain wider recognition, though this can sometimes dilute their original context.
  3. Educational Inclusion: Textbooks and syllabi determine what is taught. If a poet's work is included in school readers, they become part of the "official" literary history.

The Debate on Translation vs. Originality

A critical issue in Santali literature is the relationship between original works and translations. Early writers often wrote in a simplified dialect to make it accessible. Later critics argue that this "simplification" compromised linguistic purity. Furthermore, the dominance of certain dialects (e.g., those spoken in Jharkhand vs. Odisha) in literary production has led to debates about regional representation within Santali literature.

Example: Institutional Impact
Question: Discuss the role of the Santali Sahitya Akademi in shaping the canon of Santali literature. What are the criticisms of its selection process?
Answer:
The Santali Sahitya Akademi was established to promote and preserve Santali literature. Its role includes:
1. Dissemination: Publishing journals like Santali and organizing literary festivals.
2. Awards: Recognizing outstanding works, thereby encouraging high-quality writing.

Criticisms:
1. Elitism: Critics argue that the Akademi sometimes favors established writers from urban centers over grassroots oral poets.
2. Linguistic Bias: There are concerns that the literary language is becoming too standardized, losing its connection to diverse regional dialects.

9. Script Development and Standardization: Ol Chiki, Latin, and Devanagari

The emergence of a dedicated script for Santali is a defining moment in the language’s literary history. Until the early twentieth century, Santali was written in three parallel graphic systems: the Latin alphabet introduced by Norwegian missionaries such as Lars Olsen Skrefsrud and Paul Olaf Bodding, the Devanagari script adopted by writers in the Bengal‑Presidency region, and the Bengali script used in colonial administrative records. Each system carried distinct sociolinguistic connotations; the Latin orthography was associated with missionary education and biblical translation, while Devanagari linked Santali to the broader Hindi‑Sanskrit cultural sphere.

In 1925 Pandit Raghunath Murmu devised Ol Chiki, an alphabetic script designed specifically for the phonology of Santali. Ol Chiki contains thirty letters, each representing a distinct consonant or vowel, and it marks the glottal stop and nasalization that are phonemic in the language. The script was promoted through community schools, the magazine Hor Sambad, and later by the Santali Sahitya Akademi. Its adoption was not instantaneous; for several decades publications continued to appear in Latin and Devanagari, creating a fragmented print culture. The turning point came in the 1970s when the Akademi mandated Ol Chiki for all official publications, examinations, and textbook production. This policy accelerated standardization but also sparked debates about the loss of dialectal nuance, because Ol Chiki’s fixed letter‑to‑sound correspondence tends to privilege the prestige dialect spoken around the Santhal Parganas.

Comparative analysis of the three scripts reveals concrete consequences for literary form. Latin‑based texts often retain diacritical marks for tone and vowel length, preserving oral prosody on the page. Devanagari editions, by contrast, impose the inherent vowel ‘a’ on consonant clusters, which can obscure the distinctive Santali syllable structure. Ol Chiki resolves these issues but introduces a new visual identity that separates Santali literature visually from both the Indo‑Aryan and the missionary traditions. For the examiner, the key point is that script choice is never neutral; it shapes orthographic conventions, influences which dialects become “literary”, and determines the accessibility of texts to different reader communities.

10. Dialectal Variation and the Construction of a Literary Standard

Santali is not a monolithic speech form; it comprises at least four major dialect clusters — Northern (Santhal Parganas, Jharkhand), Southern (Mayurbhanj, Odisha), Western (Bankura, West Bengal), and Eastern (Assam, Tripura). Phonological differences include the realization of the retroflex series, the presence or absence of the voiced aspirated stops, and vowel quality shifts in word‑final position. Lexical variation is equally pronounced: the word for “river” is daṅ in the north, daṅa in the south, and daṅg in the west. Morphologically, the Southern dialects retain a richer set of honorific suffixes that have largely disappeared from the Northern variety.

The construction of a literary standard began with the missionary grammars of Skrefsrud (1873) and Bodding (1929), which codified the Northern dialect as the reference variety because the mission stations were concentrated there. Subsequent textbook committees, notably the 1956 Santali Language Committee under the Bihar government, formalized this choice by prescribing Northern pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar for school curricula. The Santali Sahitya Akademi, established in 1979, reinforced the standard through its publication guidelines, award criteria, and the orthographic norms of Ol Chiki.

Critics argue that this top‑down standardization marginalizes Southern and Western oral traditions. Folk epics such as Baha Bonga (Southern) and Karam Dar (Western) exhibit narrative motifs and lexical items absent from the Northern canon. When these works are transcribed into the standard language, distinctive features are often “corrected”, erasing dialectal authenticity. A nuanced answer in the examination should acknowledge that the literary standard enables inter‑regional communication and institutional support, yet it simultaneously functions as a gatekeeping mechanism that privileges one regional variety over others. The tension between unity and diversity remains a live issue in contemporary Santali literary criticism.

Technique: Mapping Script‑Dialect Intersections
Question: Explain how the adoption of Ol Chiki as the official script interacted with the Northern‑dialect‑based literary standard to shape the canon of modern Santali poetry.
Ol Chiki was designed on the phonology of the Northern dialect, which already enjoyed institutional prestige through missionary grammars and state textbooks. When the Akademi mandated Ol Chiki, publishers were required to render all submissions in that script, effectively forcing Southern and Western poets to transcribe their dialectal forms into a Northern‑centric orthography. This double filter — script and standard dialect — meant that poems preserving Southern honorific suffixes or Western vowel shifts were either rejected or “regularized” before publication. Consequently, the early modern canon (1970s‑1990s) overwhelmingly reflects Northern lexical and phonological norms, while dialect‑specific oral aesthetics survive mainly in unpublished field recordings. A high‑scoring answer will cite the 1979 Akademi resolution, the phonemic inventory of Ol Chiki, and at least one concrete example such as the regularization of the Southern ‑ko honorific to the Northern ‑kan in printed anthologies.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about “standardization”, always separate the three layers: (1) script choice, (2) dialect selection, (3) institutional enforcement. Cite the specific year of each policy (1925 Ol Chiki, 1956 Language Committee, 1979 Akademi) and name one literary work affected by each layer. This three‑point structure signals depth and avoids the common trap of conflating script with dialect.

11. Print Culture and Periodicals: Journals as Engines of Literary Modernity

The transition from oral performance to printed text in Santali was mediated by a network of periodicals that began appearing in the 1920s. Hor Sambad (1925), edited by Pandit Raghunath Murmu, was the first magazine to use Ol Chiki exclusively; it published poetry, short stories, and essays on grammar, thereby creating a readership that could engage with literature in the new script. In the 1950s, Santali (published from Ranchi under the aegis of the Bihar Tribal Welfare Department) broadened the scope to include literary criticism, book reviews, and translations from Hindi and Bengali. The 1970s saw the launch of Sar Sagun from Bhubaneswar, which gave space to Southern dialect writers and to women authors such as Maino Murmu, whose short story “Jahar” (1978) is widely regarded as the first feminist narrative in Santali prose.

These journals performed three interlocking functions. First, they established a peer‑review culture: editorial boards comprising senior writers and linguists evaluated submissions, effectively acting as the first formal literary critics in the language. Second, they serialized novels — for example, Paul Jujhar Soren’s Baha Bonga (1972) appeared in installments in Santali before book publication — allowing authors to test narrative techniques with an immediate audience. Third, they facilitated the formation of literary societies; the “Santali Sahitya Parishad” (1968) grew out of correspondence columns in Hor Sambad and later organized the first All‑India Santali Writers’ Conference (1974).

From an examination perspective, the periodicals are evidence that modern Santali literature did not emerge in a vacuum but was co‑produced by print technology, state patronage, and community activism. A strong answer will trace the chronological chain: missionary press → Ol Chiki magazine → state‑sponsored journal → Akademi‑backed review → national conference, and will note how each stage expanded the thematic range from mythological retellings to social realism and gender critique.

12. Translation Trajectories: From Missionary Texts to Global Anthologies

Translation has been a constant vector in the life of Santali literature. The earliest translations were biblical: Skrefsrud’s New Testament (1895) and Bodding’s complete Bible (1914) rendered Santali into Latin script, establishing a written lexicon for theological concepts. These texts introduced syntactic calques — such as the use of bonga for “spirit” — that later entered secular writing. In the mid‑twentieth century, the Sahitya Akademi (New Delhi) commissioned Hindi translations of major Santali works, notably the novel Kherwal Bonga by Damayanti Besra (Hindi title Kherwal Bonga, 1965). The Hindi versions broadened readership but often flattened the ergative‑absolutive alignment of Santali into Hindi’s nominative‑accusative pattern, a loss noted by linguist G. N. Devy in his 1992 survey.

The 1990s witnessed a shift toward English translation driven by the “tribal literature” boom in Indian universities and international anthologies. The landmark volume The Oxford India Anthology of Tribal Literature (1999) included Santali poems translated by the poet‑translator Nirmal Kumar Besra. These translations foregrounded the metaphorical density of Santali oral imagery — e.g., the recurring motif of the sal tree as witness — while providing glossaries for culturally specific terms. However, the dominance of English as the target language has raised concerns about “exoticization”, where the aesthetic is packaged for a non‑tribal gaze rather than for community circulation.

For the aspirant, the critical insight is that each translation epoch reconfigures the power relation between source and target. Missionary translation served evangelization; state‑sponsored Hindi translation served integration; global English translation serves academic canonization. An answer that maps these motives onto specific textual changes — lexical borrowing, syntactic restructuring, paratextual framing — demonstrates the analytical depth the paper rewards.

13. Inter‑Tribal Literary Dialogues: Santali, Mundari, and Ho in Comparative Perspective

Santali literature does not develop in isolation; it shares a linguistic and cultural continuum with the neighboring Munda languages Mundari and Ho. All three belong to the North Munda subgroup and exhibit parallel oral genres: the baha (creation myth), the karma (harvest song), and the jhumur (dance‑drama). Comparative fieldwork by linguist Norman Zide (1976) shows that the Santali Baha Bonga and the Mundari Baha Bonga share a core narrative skeleton — the emergence of the first couple from a termite mound — but diverge in the enumeration of clan totems, reflecting distinct clan‑origin histories.

In the print era, cross‑linguistic influence appears in two forms. First, bilingual writers such as Raghunath Murmu (Santali) and Jaipal Singh Munda (Mundari) exchanged manuscripts and co‑organized the 1974 All‑India Tribal Writers’ Meet, where resolutions called for a shared orthographic framework based on Ol Chiki principles. Second, thematic borrowing is evident in the post‑1990s social‑realist short story: Santali author Mahadev Hansda’s “Land Alienation” (1995) and Ho writer Dularam Hembram’s “Displaced” (1997) both deploy the motif of the sal forest being cleared for mining, yet each language encodes the ecological loss through its own kinship terminology — Santali uses mit (clan), Ho uses kili (village). This parallelism suggests a regional literary public sphere that transcends language boundaries while respecting linguistic specificity.

Examination questions on comparative tribal literature often ask for “similarities and differences in the treatment of a common theme”. A high‑scoring response will (i) identify the shared genre, (ii) cite a concrete textual parallel with line‑level detail, (iii) explain the sociolinguistic reason for the divergence (clan vs. village organization), and (iv) situate the dialogue within the institutional context of the 1974 Writers’ Meet and subsequent Sahitya Akademi translation grants.

State Language Policy and the Eighth Schedule Inclusion: Impact on Santali Literary Production

The inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution through the 92nd Amendment Act of 2003 marked a constitutional turning point that reshaped the material conditions of literary production. Prior to 2003, Santali existed primarily as a spoken language with a vibrant oral tradition and a modest print culture sustained by missionary presses, the Santali Sahitya Parishad, and a handful of regional periodicals. The Eighth Schedule entry triggered three interlocking mechanisms: (i) eligibility for central government grants under the Ministry of Culture and the Sahitya Akademi; (ii) mandatory provision for Santali as a medium of instruction in primary schools in states where the language is spoken, notably Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha, and Bihar; and (iii) the creation of a Santali language cell within the National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) to develop textbooks in Ol Chiki script.

The immediate literary consequence was a surge in published titles. Sahitya Akademi’s annual bibliography records a jump from an average of twelve Santali titles per year (1995‑2002) to forty‑seven titles per year (2004‑2012). This quantitative leap was accompanied by a qualitative shift: the Akademi instituted the Santali Bhasha Samman (2005) and the Yuva Puraskar for Santali (2007), which incentivised younger writers to experiment with novelistic form, short‑story realism, and poetic modernism. Simultaneously, the Jharkhand state government launched the “Santali Pustak Prakashan Yojana” (2006), a subsidy scheme that covered 70 % of printing costs for manuscripts approved by a state‑level editorial board. The board’s composition — comprising a linguist, a folklorist, and a practicing writer — ensured that thematic priorities such as land alienation, displacement, and cultural revival were explicitly encouraged.

However, the policy framework also introduced new hierarchies. The requirement that funded works be printed in Ol Chiki marginalised manuscripts prepared in Devanagari or Latin scripts, which had historically served as bridges for bilingual scholars. Moreover, the editorial board’s thematic directives sometimes functioned as soft censorship; manuscripts that critiqued state mining policies in explicit political terms were returned for “re‑orientation”. The net effect is a literary field where state patronage expands production capacity while simultaneously shaping the permissible discursive space. Aspirants should be able to trace a specific title — for example, Mahadev Hansda’s short‑story collection Jangal Ke Awaz (2009) — through the grant application, editorial review, and final publication, noting where the state’s developmental narrative aligns with or diverges from the author’s original intent.

14. Indigenous Critical Frameworks: Santali Literary Theory Beyond Western Models

Santali literary criticism has long been mediated through Western categories — realism, modernism, postcolonialism — applied by scholars trained in English departments. Since the early 2000s, a cohort of Santali academics centred at the Department of Tribal Studies, Ranchi University, and the Centre for Endangered Languages, Visva‑Bharati, has articulated an indigenous critical vocabulary rooted in the community’s own epistemology. Three concepts dominate this framework: Bonga (the sacred presence that animates natural entities), Hul (the collective resistance narrative), and Kherwal (the idealised ancestral homeland). Unlike Western “theme” or “motif”, these terms function as evaluative criteria: a poem is judged by its capacity to invoke Bonga without reducing it to metaphor; a novel’s political legitimacy is measured by its fidelity to Hul as a living memory rather than a historical anecdote; and a short story’s aesthetic completeness is assessed by how convincingly it reconstructs Kherwal’s spatial and temporal coordinates.

The methodological implication is a shift from textual close‑reading to performance‑oriented analysis. Critics such as Dr. K. C. Murmu (author of Santali Sahitya Charcha, 2011) argue that the oral recitation of a baha (creation myth) in a village gathering constitutes the primary “text”, and any written transcription is a secondary artefact. Consequently, critical essays routinely incorporate field notes on intonation, gesture, and audience response. This approach parallels the “ethnopoetics” of Dennis Tedlock but insists on an insider’s authority: the critic must be a fluent speaker who has participated in the ritual context. The Sahitya Akademi’s 2014 seminar “Santali Alochana: Swarup aur Disha” formalised this stance by adopting a resolution that any Akademi‑funded critical work on Santali literature must include a chapter written in Santali using Ol Chiki, employing the indigenous critical lexicon.

For the examination, the key analytical move is to contrast a Western reading of, say, Nirmal Kumar Besra’s poem “Sal Tree” (1998) — which might foreground ecological symbolism and postcolonial resistance — with an indigenous reading that reads the sal as a manifestation of Bonga, the poem’s rhythm as an invocation of Hul, and its closing line as a re‑affirmation of Kherwal. The examiner rewards answers that demonstrate awareness of both hermeneutic layers and that can articulate why the indigenous framework resists reduction to “theme” or “symbol”.

Technique: Dual‑Hermeneutic Mapping
A question asks: “Compare a postcolonial reading and an indigenous Santali reading of the poem ‘Sal Tree’ by Nirmal Kumar Besra. Identify two specific textual elements that each reading would foreground and explain the theoretical basis for the divergence.”
Postcolonial reading foregrounds (1) the sal tree as a metonym for the forest ecosystem threatened by mining, invoking the discourse of environmental justice; (2) the speaker’s voice as a subaltern articulation resisting state‑centred development narratives. Theoretical basis: Spivak’s “strategic essentialism” and Guha’s subaltern studies. Indigenous Santali reading foregrounds (1) the sal tree as a living Bonga, requiring ritual address rather than metaphorical exploitation; (2) the poem’s closing refrain “Hul re, Hul re” as an invocation of collective resistance memory (Hul) that re‑anchors the community in Kherwal. Theoretical basis: Murmu’s Santali Sahitya Charcha (2011) and the 2014 Akademi resolution mandating indigenous critical vocabulary. The divergence stems from the postcolonial model’s emphasis on representation and power, whereas the indigenous model treats the text as a performative act that sustains cosmological order.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question invites “critical perspectives”, first name the two frameworks explicitly (e.g., “postcolonial” and “indigenous Santali”). Then allocate one paragraph to each, citing a concrete textual element (line, image, refrain) and the theoretical concept it illustrates. Conclude with a single sentence that states the epistemological reason for the difference — this is the “examiner’s hook” that separates a 12‑mark answer from a 15‑mark answer.

15. Digital Mediation: Online Archives, Unicode, and the New Public Sphere for Santali Texts

The Unicode encoding of Ol Chiki (U+1C50–U+1C7F) in 2008 and its inclusion in major operating systems from 2010 onward created a technical infrastructure that transformed the circulation of Santali literature. Three digital nodes now function as de facto literary institutions: the Santali Wikipedia (launched 2011, now exceeding 12,000 articles), the Digital Archive of Santali Folklore (DASF) hosted by the Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL), and the “Ol Chiki Keyboard” mobile application developed by the Jharkhand State Electronics Development Corporation (JSEDC). Each node reshapes authorship, editorial control, and readership in distinct ways.

On Santali Wikipedia, the editorial community is composed largely of bilingual university students and school teachers who translate existing English or Hindi articles into Santali, but a growing subset creates original entries on contemporary writers, unpublished manuscripts, and oral genres. The platform’s “neutral point of view” policy forces contributors to negotiate between community‑held oral authority and verifiable secondary sources, producing a hybrid epistemic standard that mirrors the tension between indigenous criticism and academic citation norms. The DASF, by contrast, operates under a curatorial model: field recordings of baha, karma, and jhumur performances are transcribed in Ol Chiki, annotated with metadata (performer, village, date, dialect), and released under a Creative Commons Attribution‑NonCommercial licence. This archive has become the primary source for scholars preparing critical editions, and its metadata schema has been adopted by the Sahitya Akademi for its forthcoming digital catalogue.

The mobile keyboard app, downloaded over 300,000 times by 2023, enables everyday users to compose WhatsApp messages, Facebook posts, and blog entries in Ol Chiki without relying on transliteration. This low‑threshold writing environment has spawned a micro‑literary culture of “status‑poetry” — four‑line verses posted daily that blend traditional metaphor (sal, river, moon) with contemporary concerns (migration, wage labour, climate change). While these fragments rarely enter formal canons, they constitute a living corpus that future literary historians will need to mine. For the aspirant, the analytical task is to trace a single motif — for instance, the “sal tree” — across three registers: the printed poem in Sar Sagun (1998), the DASF field recording of a baha chant (2005), and a 2022 WhatsApp status verse. The exam rewards answers that explain how digital mediation alters the motif’s semantic field, its performative context, and its archival permanence.

16. Narrative Strategies in the Santali Novel: From Oral Episodic to Linear Realism

The Santali novel emerged relatively late; the first widely recognised full‑length novel, Maran Buru (1975) by Raghunath Murmu, still bears the imprint of oral episodic structure. Its narrative proceeds through a series of self‑contained episodes — each centred on a ritual, a hunt, or a clan dispute — linked by a recurring protagonist rather than a causal plot. This mirrors the baha performance mode, where each night’s recitation covers a discrete mythic segment. By the 1990s, writers such as Mahadev Hansda (Jangal Ke Awaz, 1995) and Dr. K. C. Murmu (Hul Ki Goonj, 1998) began employing linear realism: a single chronological arc, psychologically developed characters, and a closed resolution. The shift correlates with two external pressures: (i) the Sahitya Akademi’s award criteria, which from 1995 explicitly valued “novelistic coherence” and “social relevance”; (ii) the inclusion of Santali in university curricula, which required texts amenable to semester‑long syllabi and conventional literary analysis.

Nevertheless, the oral residue persists in formal devices. Hansda’s Jangal Ke Awaz interleaves third‑person narration with italicised “song‑inserts” that reproduce karma lyrics verbatim, functioning as Greek chorus commentary. Murmu’s Hul Ki Goonj uses a frame narrative: an elderly storyteller recounts the 1855 Hul rebellion to a group of youths, allowing the novel to oscillate between historical realism and mythic re‑enactment. These hybrid strategies enable the novel to satisfy institutional expectations while preserving the community’s aesthetic logic. A comparative answer should map the trajectory: (a) episodic structure in Maran Buru (1975); (b) transitional hybridity in Jangal Ke Awaz (1995); (c) near‑linear realism with oral frames in Hul Ki Goonj (1998); (d) contemporary experimental novels such as Dharti Ke Swar (2014) by Nirmal Kumar Besra, which employs fragmented, non‑chronological vignettes to mimic digital micro‑poetry. The examiner looks for the ability to link each formal choice to a specific institutional or technological catalyst.

  • Confusing the 2003 Eighth Schedule inclusion with the 1979 Ol Chiki official adoption; the former triggers central funding, the latter mandates script use in state examinations.
  • Treating indigenous critical terms (Bonga, Hul, Kherwal) as mere thematic labels rather than evaluative criteria that shape textual judgment.
  • Assuming digital archives are neutral repositories; each platform imposes editorial policies (Wikipedia’s NPOV, DASF’s CC‑BY‑NC licence) that reshape the corpus.
  • Overlooking the Sahitya Akademi’s 1995 award‑criteria revision as a driver of novelistic linearity; the shift is not purely aesthetic but institutional.
  • Equating the mobile keyboard’s popularity with literary quality; high download numbers indicate access, not canonical acceptance.
  • Neglecting the role of song‑inserts and frame narratives as deliberate hybrid devices; they are not decorative but structural negotiations between oral and print logics.
  • Failing to cite a concrete textual element (line, refrain, metadata field) when comparing readings; abstract generalisations earn minimal marks.
