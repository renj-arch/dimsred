Santali Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Santali is a Munda language of the Austroasiatic family. It has a rich oral tradition; the Ol Chiki script was invented by Raghunath Murmu in 1925, and modern written literature developed in the 20th century.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Oral period: Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.
  • Early written period (20th c.): Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.
  • Modern period (mid-20th c.–present): Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition.

Major Authors

  • Raghunath Murmu: Inventor of the Ol Chiki script and pioneer of modern Santali writing.
  • Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Santali poet and writer.
  • Santali Sahitya Akademi writers: Modern Santali poets, dramatists and fiction writers.

Major Works

  • Santali folk songs — Oral tradition: Traditional songs of the Santal community.
  • Ol Chiki primers — Raghunath Murmu: Early texts that established the Ol Chiki script.
  • Santali poetry — Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Twentieth-century Santali poetry.
  • Modern Santali drama — Modern Santali writers: Twentieth-century drama.

Evolution of Literary Forms: From Oral Folklore to Modern Prose

The trajectory of Santali literature is not merely a linear progression from "oral" to "written," but a complex transformation of form, function, and audience. Understanding this evolution is crucial for Paper I questions that ask about the "nature of change" or "shifts in thematic concerns." The transition was driven by the invention of the Ol Chiki script (1925), which allowed the codification of fluid oral traditions into fixed textual forms.

The Oral Period: Fluidity and Communal Function

In the pre-1925 era, Santali literature existed almost exclusively in an oral mode. The primary forms were Khel (songs), Bhagani (creation myths), and Kabir (narrative poems). These works were characterized by:

  • Metric Flexibility: Unlike fixed poetry, oral songs allowed for improvisation based on the singer's mood or audience reaction.
  • Communal Ownership: Songs like Sohrai and Kuk were not attributed to a single author but belonged to the community. They served ritualistic functions (harvest, marriage, initiation) rather than aesthetic ones in isolation.
  • Motif Repetition: Recurrent themes included nature worship, ancestor reverence, and moral instruction through allegory.

The Transitional Period: The Impact of Ol Chiki (1925–1940)

Raghunath Murmu's invention of the script acted as a catalyst for "fixation." However, this was not an immediate shift to modern prose. The early written period saw the publication of primers and religious texts. This phase introduced standardization. For instance, dialectal variations were smoothed out to create a literary norm. Authors like Sadhu Ramchand Murmu began experimenting with free verse, moving away from strict rhythmic patterns used in folk songs. The audience shifted from the village square (ritual context) to the schoolroom and reading public.

The Modern Period: Prose, Fiction, and Drama

Post-independence, Santali literature diversified significantly. The emergence of fiction required a new narrative structure that did not exist in oral tradition. Writers began exploring individual psychology, social conflict, and urban displacement—themes alien to traditional folk songs. Drama also emerged as a distinct form, often staged for festivals but written with specific dialogue structures. This period is marked by the establishment of the Santali Sahitya Akademi (1960s), which institutionalized literary criticism and canon formation.

Example: Analyzing the Shift in Form
Question: How did the invention of the Ol Chiki script alter the structural integrity of Santali folk songs, and what were the implications for their transmission?
Answer:
The introduction of the Ol Chiki script transformed fluid oral compositions into fixed textual entities. In the oral period, a song like Sohrai could vary in length and wording depending on the singer. With the advent of written literature, specific versions were codified in primers and anthologies.

Implications:
1. Loss of Improvisation: The ability to adapt songs to immediate contexts diminished as "correct" texts became authoritative.
2. Preservation vs. Stagnation: While the core content was preserved from extinction, the dynamic evolution of the language within the song form slowed down.
3. Shift in Audience: The primary mode of transmission shifted from auditory (listening) to visual (reading), changing how literature was consumed and critiqued.

Thematic Analysis: From Nature Worship to Social Realism

The thematic content of Santali literature has undergone a profound metamorphosis, reflecting the changing socio-political landscape of the Munda-speaking region. This evolution is essential for answering questions regarding "thematic shifts" or "sociological relevance."

1. The Nature-Centric Universe (Pre-20th Century)

Early literature was deeply animistic and ecological. Themes revolved around the veneration of natural elements: Bong (forest), Hajong (sun), Kharia (moon). The relationship between humans and nature was one of symbiosis and reverence. Myths like Bhagani explained the origin of the world through natural forces. There was no concept of "man vs. nature"; rather, it was "man within nature."

2. The Rise of Social Consciousness (Mid-20th Century)

With the influence of Christian missionaries and later socialist movements, literature began to address social ills. Themes of caste discrimination, land alienation, and exploitation by dominant castes entered the literary discourse. Writers like Sankha Barua and others started using literature as a tool for social reform. The "nature" theme was not abandoned but reinterpreted through the lens of human suffering caused by external forces.

3. Modern Realism and Identity Politics (Late 20th Century–Present)

Contemporary Santali literature grapples with themes of identity, globalization, and migration. The "Santal" is no longer just a forest dweller but an urbanite facing alienation. Themes include the loss of traditional values in modern society, the struggle for linguistic rights (e.g., inclusion in the Eighth Schedule), and the intersectionality of Santali identity with broader Indian nationalism. Fiction now explores the inner conflicts of individuals caught between tradition and modernity.

Example: Thematic Contrast
Question: Compare the treatment of 'nature' in a traditional Bong (forest) song versus a modern Santali short story. How does this reflect broader societal changes?
Answer:
In traditional songs, nature is personified and revered as a deity or ancestor. The forest is a source of life and spiritual connection; the language used is reverential and descriptive.

In modern short stories (e.g., by contemporary Akademi writers), nature often appears as a backdrop to human conflict or a symbol of lost innocence. The relationship may be critical, highlighting environmental degradation or the displacement of Santal communities from their ancestral lands.

Conclusion: This shift reflects the transition from an agrarian, forest-dependent economy to one influenced by industrialization and urbanization, where nature is viewed through a lens of loss and nostalgia rather than active worship.

Always qualify your answer with the specific historical context (e.g., "Post-independence," "After 1947"). Acknowledge that early literature was primarily mythological and ritualistic, not sociological.

Criticism and Canon Formation: The Role of Institutions

A sophisticated understanding of Santali literature requires knowledge of how "canon" is formed. In the oral period, there was no canon; authority lay with tradition. The modern period introduced literary criticism, journals, and institutions like the Santali Sahitya Akademi. This section covers the mechanisms by which certain works became "classic" while others were marginalized.

The Mechanism of Canonization

Literary works in Santali are elevated to "classic" status through:

  1. Institutional Recognition: Awards from the Sahitya Akademi (New Delhi and regional bodies) play a significant role.
  2. Translation and Anthologization: Works that are translated into Hindi or English often gain wider recognition, though this can sometimes dilute their original context.
  3. Educational Inclusion: Textbooks and syllabi determine what is taught. If a poet's work is included in school readers, they become part of the "official" literary history.

The Debate on Translation vs. Originality

A critical issue in Santali literature is the relationship between original works and translations. Early writers often wrote in a simplified dialect to make it accessible. Later critics argue that this "simplification" compromised linguistic purity. Furthermore, the dominance of certain dialects (e.g., those spoken in Jharkhand vs. Odisha) in literary production has led to debates about regional representation within Santali literature.

Script Development and Standardization: Ol Chiki, Latin, and Devanagari

The emergence of a dedicated script for Santali is a defining moment in the language’s literary history. Until the early twentieth century, Santali was written in three parallel graphic systems: the Latin alphabet introduced by Norwegian missionaries such as Lars Olsen Skrefsrud and Paul Olaf Bodding, the Devanagari script adopted by writers in the Bengal‑Presidency region, and the Bengali script used in colonial administrative records. Each system carried distinct sociolinguistic connotations; the Latin orthography was associated with missionary education and biblical translation, while Devanagari linked Santali to the broader Hindi‑Sanskrit cultural sphere.

In 1925 Pandit Raghunath Murmu devised Ol Chiki, an alphabetic script designed specifically for the phonology of Santali. Ol Chiki contains thirty letters, each representing a distinct consonant or vowel, and it marks the glottal stop and nasalization that are phonemic in the language. The script was promoted through community schools, the magazine Hor Sambad, and later by the Santali Sahitya Akademi. Its adoption was not instantaneous; for several decades publications continued to appear in Latin and Devanagari, creating a fragmented print culture. The turning point came in the 1970s when the Akademi mandated Ol Chiki for all official publications, examinations, and textbook production. This policy accelerated standardization but also sparked debates about the loss of dialectal nuance, because Ol Chiki’s fixed letter‑to‑sound correspondence tends to privilege the prestige dialect spoken around the Santhal Parganas.

Comparative analysis of the three scripts reveals concrete consequences for literary form. Latin‑based texts often retain diacritical marks for tone and vowel length, preserving oral prosody on the page. Devanagari editions, by contrast, impose the inherent vowel ‘a’ on consonant clusters, which can obscure the distinctive Santali syllable structure. Ol Chiki resolves these issues but introduces a new visual identity that separates Santali literature visually from both the Indo‑Aryan and the missionary traditions. For the examiner, the key point is that script choice is never neutral; it shapes orthographic conventions, influences which dialects become “literary”, and determines the accessibility of texts to different reader communities.

Dialectal Variation and the Construction of a Literary Standard

Santali is not a monolithic speech form; it comprises at least four major dialect clusters — Northern (Santhal Parganas, Jharkhand), Southern (Mayurbhanj, Odisha), Western (Bankura, West Bengal), and Eastern (Assam, Tripura). Phonological differences include the realization of the retroflex series, the presence or absence of the voiced aspirated stops, and vowel quality shifts in word‑final position. Lexical variation is equally pronounced: the word for “river” is daṅ in the north, daṅa in the south, and daṅg in the west. Morphologically, the Southern dialects retain a richer set of honorific suffixes that have largely disappeared from the Northern variety.

The construction of a literary standard began with the missionary grammars of Skrefsrud (1873) and Bodding (1929), which codified the Northern dialect as the reference variety because the mission stations were concentrated there. Subsequent textbook committees, notably the 1956 Santali Language Committee under the Bihar government, formalized this choice by prescribing Northern pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar for school curricula. The Santali Sahitya Akademi, established in 1979, reinforced the standard through its publication guidelines, award criteria, and the orthographic norms of Ol Chiki.

Critics argue that this top‑down standardization marginalizes Southern and Western oral traditions. Folk epics such as Baha Bonga (Southern) and Karam Dar (Western) exhibit narrative motifs and lexical items absent from the Northern canon. When these works are transcribed into the standard language, distinctive features are often “corrected”, erasing dialectal authenticity. A nuanced answer in the examination should acknowledge that the literary standard enables inter‑regional communication and institutional support, yet it simultaneously functions as a gatekeeping mechanism that privileges one regional variety over others. The tension between unity and diversity remains a live issue in contemporary Santali literary criticism.

Technique: Mapping Script‑Dialect Intersections
Question: Explain how the adoption of Ol Chiki as the official script interacted with the Northern‑dialect‑based literary standard to shape the canon of modern Santali poetry.
Ol Chiki was designed on the phonology of the Northern dialect, which already enjoyed institutional prestige through missionary grammars and state textbooks. When the Akademi mandated Ol Chiki, publishers were required to render all submissions in that script, effectively forcing Southern and Western poets to transcribe their dialectal forms into a Northern‑centric orthography. This double filter — script and standard dialect — meant that poems preserving Southern honorific suffixes or Western vowel shifts were either rejected or “regularized” before publication. Consequently, the early modern canon (1970s‑1990s) overwhelmingly reflects Northern lexical and phonological norms, while dialect‑specific oral aesthetics survive mainly in unpublished field recordings. A high‑scoring answer will cite the 1979 Akademi resolution, the phonemic inventory of Ol Chiki, and at least one concrete example such as the regularization of the Southern ‑ko honorific to the Northern ‑kan in printed anthologies.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about “standardization”, always separate the three layers: (1) script choice, (2) dialect selection, (3) institutional enforcement. Cite the specific year of each policy (1925 Ol Chiki, 1956 Language Committee, 1979 Akademi) and name one literary work affected by each layer. This three‑point structure signals depth and avoids the common trap of conflating script with dialect.

Print Culture and Periodicals: Journals as Engines of Literary Modernity

The transition from oral performance to printed text in Santali was mediated by a network of periodicals that began appearing in the 1920s. Hor Sambad (1925), edited by Pandit Raghunath Murmu, was the first magazine to use Ol Chiki exclusively; it published poetry, short stories, and essays on grammar, thereby creating a readership that could engage with literature in the new script. In the 1950s, Santali (published from Ranchi under the aegis of the Bihar Tribal Welfare Department) broadened the scope to include literary criticism, book reviews, and translations from Hindi and Bengali. The 1970s saw the launch of Sar Sagun from Bhubaneswar, which gave space to Southern dialect writers and to women authors such as Maino Murmu, whose short story “Jahar” (1978) is widely regarded as the first feminist narrative in Santali prose.

These journals performed three interlocking functions. First, they established a peer‑review culture: editorial boards comprising senior writers and linguists evaluated submissions, effectively acting as the first formal literary critics in the language. Second, they serialized novels — for example, Paul Jujhar Soren’s Baha Bonga (1972) appeared in installments in Santali before book publication — allowing authors to test narrative techniques with an immediate audience. Third, they facilitated the formation of literary societies; the “Santali Sahitya Parishad” (1968) grew out of correspondence columns in Hor Sambad and later organized the first All‑India Santali Writers’ Conference (1974).

From an examination perspective, the periodicals are evidence that modern Santali literature did not emerge in a vacuum but was co‑produced by print technology, state patronage, and community activism. A strong answer will trace the chronological chain: missionary press → Ol Chiki magazine → state‑sponsored journal → Akademi‑backed review → national conference, and will note how each stage expanded the thematic range from mythological retellings to social realism and gender critique.

Translation Trajectories: From Missionary Texts to Global Anthologies

Translation has been a constant vector in the life of Santali literature. The earliest translations were biblical: Skrefsrud’s New Testament (1895) and Bodding’s complete Bible (1914) rendered Santali into Latin script, establishing a written lexicon for theological concepts. These texts introduced syntactic calques — such as the use of bonga for “spirit” — that later entered secular writing. In the mid‑twentieth century, the Sahitya Akademi (New Delhi) commissioned Hindi translations of major Santali works, notably the novel Kherwal Bonga by Damayanti Besra (Hindi title Kherwal Bonga, 1965). The Hindi versions broadened readership but often flattened the ergative‑absolutive alignment of Santali into Hindi’s nominative‑accusative pattern, a loss noted by linguist G. N. Devy in his 1992 survey.

The 1990s witnessed a shift toward English translation driven by the “tribal literature” boom in Indian universities and international anthologies. The landmark volume The Oxford India Anthology of Tribal Literature (1999) included Santali poems translated by the poet‑translator Nirmal Kumar Besra. These translations foregrounded the metaphorical density of Santali oral imagery — e.g., the recurring motif of the sal tree as witness — while providing glossaries for culturally specific terms. However, the dominance of English as the target language has raised concerns about “exoticization”, where the aesthetic is packaged for a non‑tribal gaze rather than for community circulation.

For the aspirant, the critical insight is that each translation epoch reconfigures the power relation between source and target. Missionary translation served evangelization; state‑sponsored Hindi translation served integration; global English translation serves academic canonization. An answer that maps these motives onto specific textual changes — lexical borrowing, syntactic restructuring, paratextual framing — demonstrates the analytical depth the paper rewards.

Inter‑Tribal Literary Dialogues: Santali, Mundari, and Ho in Comparative Perspective

Santali literature does not develop in isolation; it shares a linguistic and cultural continuum with the neighboring Munda languages Mundari and Ho. All three belong to the North Munda subgroup and exhibit parallel oral genres: the baha (creation myth), the karma (harvest song), and the jhumur (dance‑drama). Comparative fieldwork by linguist Norman Zide (1976) shows that the Santali Baha Bonga and the Mundari Baha Bonga share a core narrative skeleton — the emergence of the first couple from a termite mound — but diverge in the enumeration of clan totems, reflecting distinct clan‑origin histories.

In the print era, cross‑linguistic influence appears in two forms. First, bilingual writers such as Raghunath Murmu (Santali) and Jaipal Singh Munda (Mundari) exchanged manuscripts and co‑organized the 1974 All‑India Tribal Writers’ Meet, where resolutions called for a shared orthographic framework based on Ol Chiki principles. Second, thematic borrowing is evident in the post‑1990s social‑realist short story: Santali author Mahadev Hansda’s “Land Alienation” (1995) and Ho writer Dularam Hembram’s “Displaced” (1997) both deploy the motif of the sal forest being cleared for mining, yet each language encodes the ecological loss through its own kinship terminology — Santali uses mit (clan), Ho uses kili (village). This parallelism suggests a regional literary public sphere that transcends language boundaries while respecting linguistic specificity.

Examination questions on comparative tribal literature often ask for “similarities and differences in the treatment of a common theme”. State Language Policy and the Eighth Schedule Inclusion: Impact on Santali Literary Production

The inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution through the 92nd Amendment Act of 2003 marked a constitutional turning point that reshaped the material conditions of literary production. Prior to 2003, Santali existed primarily as a spoken language with a vibrant oral tradition and a modest print culture sustained by missionary presses, the Santali Sahitya Parishad, and a handful of regional periodicals. The Eighth Schedule entry triggered three interlocking mechanisms: (i) eligibility for central government grants under the Ministry of Culture and the Sahitya Akademi; (ii) mandatory provision for Santali as a medium of instruction in primary schools in states where the language is spoken, notably Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha, and Bihar; and (iii) the creation of a Santali language cell within the National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) to develop textbooks in Ol Chiki script.

The immediate literary consequence was a surge in published titles. Sahitya Akademi’s annual bibliography records a jump from an average of twelve Santali titles per year (1995‑2002) to forty‑seven titles per year (2004‑2012). This quantitative leap was accompanied by a qualitative shift: the Akademi instituted the Santali Bhasha Samman (2005) and the Yuva Puraskar for Santali (2007), which incentivised younger writers to experiment with novelistic form, short‑story realism, and poetic modernism. Simultaneously, the Jharkhand state government launched the “Santali Pustak Prakashan Yojana” (2006), a subsidy scheme that covered 70 % of printing costs for manuscripts approved by a state‑level editorial board. The board’s composition — comprising a linguist, a folklorist, and a practicing writer — ensured that thematic priorities such as land alienation, displacement, and cultural revival were explicitly encouraged.

However, the policy framework also introduced new hierarchies. The requirement that funded works be printed in Ol Chiki marginalised manuscripts prepared in Devanagari or Latin scripts, which had historically served as bridges for bilingual scholars. Moreover, the editorial board’s thematic directives sometimes functioned as soft censorship; manuscripts that critiqued state mining policies in explicit political terms were returned for “re‑orientation”. The net effect is a literary field where state patronage expands production capacity while simultaneously shaping the permissible discursive space. Aspirants should be able to trace a specific title — for example, Mahadev Hansda’s short‑story collection Jangal Ke Awaz (2009) — through the grant application, editorial review, and final publication, noting where the state’s developmental narrative aligns with or diverges from the author’s original intent.

Indigenous Critical Frameworks: Santali Literary Theory Beyond Western Models

Santali literary criticism has long been mediated through Western categories — realism, modernism, postcolonialism — applied by scholars trained in English departments. Since the early 2000s, a cohort of Santali academics centred at the Department of Tribal Studies, Ranchi University, and the Centre for Endangered Languages, Visva‑Bharati, has articulated an indigenous critical vocabulary rooted in the community’s own epistemology. Three concepts dominate this framework: Bonga (the sacred presence that animates natural entities), Hul (the collective resistance narrative), and Kherwal (the idealised ancestral homeland). Unlike Western “theme” or “motif”, these terms function as evaluative criteria: a poem is judged by its capacity to invoke Bonga without reducing it to metaphor; a novel’s political legitimacy is measured by its fidelity to Hul as a living memory rather than a historical anecdote; and a short story’s aesthetic completeness is assessed by how convincingly it reconstructs Kherwal’s spatial and temporal coordinates.

The methodological implication is a shift from textual close‑reading to performance‑oriented analysis. Critics such as Dr. K. C. Murmu (author of Santali Sahitya Charcha, 2011) argue that the oral recitation of a baha (creation myth) in a village gathering constitutes the primary “text”, and any written transcription is a secondary artefact. Consequently, critical essays routinely incorporate field notes on intonation, gesture, and audience response. This approach parallels the “ethnopoetics” of Dennis Tedlock but insists on an insider’s authority: the critic must be a fluent speaker who has participated in the ritual context. The Sahitya Akademi’s 2014 seminar “Santali Alochana: Swarup aur Disha” formalised this stance by adopting a resolution that any Akademi‑funded critical work on Santali literature must include a chapter written in Santali using Ol Chiki, employing the indigenous critical lexicon.

For the examination, the key analytical move is to contrast a Western reading of, say, Nirmal Kumar Besra’s poem “Sal Tree” (1998) — which might foreground ecological symbolism and postcolonial resistance — with an indigenous reading that reads the sal as a manifestation of Bonga, the poem’s rhythm as an invocation of Hul, and its closing line as a re‑affirmation of Kherwal. The examiner rewards answers that demonstrate awareness of both hermeneutic layers and that can articulate why the indigenous framework resists reduction to “theme” or “symbol”.

Technique: Dual‑Hermeneutic Mapping
A question asks: “Compare a postcolonial reading and an indigenous Santali reading of the poem ‘Sal Tree’ by Nirmal Kumar Besra. Identify two specific textual elements that each reading would foreground and explain the theoretical basis for the divergence.”
Postcolonial reading foregrounds (1) the sal tree as a metonym for the forest ecosystem threatened by mining, invoking the discourse of environmental justice; (2) the speaker’s voice as a subaltern articulation resisting state‑centred development narratives. Theoretical basis: Spivak’s “strategic essentialism” and Guha’s subaltern studies. Indigenous Santali reading foregrounds (1) the sal tree as a living Bonga, requiring ritual address rather than metaphorical exploitation; (2) the poem’s closing refrain “Hul re, Hul re” as an invocation of collective resistance memory (Hul) that re‑anchors the community in Kherwal. Theoretical basis: Murmu’s Santali Sahitya Charcha (2011) and the 2014 Akademi resolution mandating indigenous critical vocabulary. The divergence stems from the postcolonial model’s emphasis on representation and power, whereas the indigenous model treats the text as a performative act that sustains cosmological order.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question invites “critical perspectives”, first name the two frameworks explicitly (e.g., “postcolonial” and “indigenous Santali”). Then allocate one paragraph to each, citing a concrete textual element (line, image, refrain) and the theoretical concept it illustrates. Conclude with a single sentence that states the epistemological reason for the difference — this is the “examiner’s hook” that separates a 12‑mark answer from a 15‑mark answer.

Digital Mediation: Online Archives, Unicode, and the New Public Sphere for Santali Texts

The Unicode encoding of Ol Chiki (U+1C50–U+1C7F) in 2008 and its inclusion in major operating systems from 2010 onward created a technical infrastructure that transformed the circulation of Santali literature. Three digital nodes now function as de facto literary institutions: the Santali Wikipedia (launched 2011, now exceeding 12,000 articles), the Digital Archive of Santali Folklore (DASF) hosted by the Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL), and the “Ol Chiki Keyboard” mobile application developed by the Jharkhand State Electronics Development Corporation (JSEDC). Each node reshapes authorship, editorial control, and readership in distinct ways.

On Santali Wikipedia, the editorial community is composed largely of bilingual university students and school teachers who translate existing English or Hindi articles into Santali, but a growing subset creates original entries on contemporary writers, unpublished manuscripts, and oral genres. The platform’s “neutral point of view” policy forces contributors to negotiate between community‑held oral authority and verifiable secondary sources, producing a hybrid epistemic standard that mirrors the tension between indigenous criticism and academic citation norms. Narrative Strategies in the Santali Novel: From Oral Episodic to Linear Realism

The Santali novel emerged relatively late; the first widely recognised full‑length novel, Maran Buru (1975) by Raghunath Murmu, still bears the imprint of oral episodic structure. Missionary Linguistics and the Foundations of Santali Lexicography

The first systematic description of Santali grammar and vocabulary emerged from the work of Protestant missionaries attached to the London Missionary Society and the Church Missionary Society in the mid‑nineteenth century. Rev. J. Phillips published A Grammar of the Santali Language in 1852, establishing a Roman‑script orthography that remained the reference for colonial administrators and early native scholars. Phillips’ analysis identified the agglutinative verb complex, the system of noun classifiers, and the distinction between inclusive and exclusive first‑person plural pronouns — features that later linguists confirmed as typologically Austroasiatic.

Building on Phillips, Rev. L. O. Skrefsrud compiled the Santali‑English Dictionary (1873) and a companion English‑Santali Vocabulary (1880). Skrefsrud’s lexical entries recorded not only core vocabulary but also ritual terminology (e.g., bonga, hul, kherwal) and ecological lexemes for flora, fauna, and agricultural cycles. His dictionary introduced diacritical marks for vowel length and nasalisation, a convention later adapted by Pandit Raghunath Murmu when he devised the Ol Chiki script in 1925. The missionary lexicographers also produced the first printed Santali texts — translations of the Gospels (1855), a catechism (1860), and a collection of hymns (1875) — which circulated among converts and, crucially, among the emerging Santali intelligentsia who used them as models for original composition.

The missionary legacy is double‑edged. On one hand, the Roman‑script publications created a print culture that preceded any indigenous script by half a century, enabling the first generation of Santali writers (e.g., Sadhu Ramchand Murmu, 1880‑1945) to publish poems and essays in periodicals such as Santali Patrika (1912). On the other hand, the theological agenda shaped lexical choices: Christian concepts were rendered through existing Santali terms (e.g., Thakur for “God”), producing semantic shifts that later literary critics identify as “missionary lexical interference.” Contemporary scholars, notably Dr. K. C. Murmu (1998), argue that the early dictionaries must be read as colonial artefacts; they recommend cross‑checking missionary glosses with oral corpora collected by the Digital Archive of Santali Folklore (DASF) after 2000 to recover pre‑contact meanings.

Santali Drama: From Ritual Performance to Modern Stage

Traditional Santali performance rests on two pillars: the baha night‑long narrative chant, performed during the spring festival, and the karma dance‑drama that enacts agricultural myths. Both forms are communal, non‑scripted, and rely on a lead narrator (guru) who improvises within a fixed repertoire of episodes. The transition to a scripted, proscenium‑oriented drama began in the 1970s when the Jharkhand cultural renaissance encouraged the formation of theatre groups such as Kherwal Natya Dal (founded 1978) and Santali Rangmanch (1982). These groups adapted baha episodes into one‑act plays, preserving the song‑inserts (git) but imposing a linear act structure to meet the expectations of state‑sponsored drama competitions.

The first published Santali play, Baha Bonga (1985) by Mahadev Hansda, exemplifies the hybrid form: it retains the refrain “Sal re sal, dharti re dharti” as a choral leitmotif, yet divides the narrative into three acts with a clear climax — the confrontation between the village headman and the forest spirit. Hansda’s later work, Jangal Ke Awaz (1995), though primarily a novel, includes a dramatised “court scene” that was staged by the Ranchi University theatre department in 1997, illustrating the fluid boundary between prose fiction and performance text.

Institutional support shaped the genre’s trajectory. The Sahitya Akademi’s Santali language advisory board (constituted 1995) introduced a “Best Play” category in its annual awards; the inaugural prize went to Hul Ki Goonj (1998) by Dr. K. C. Murmu, a play that frames the 1855 Hul rebellion within a contemporary village meeting, using a frame‑narrator device reminiscent of the baha guru. The National School of Drama’s regional centre in Jamshedpur (established 2001) began offering a Santali theatre workshop, training actors in both Ol Chiki script reading and traditional vocal techniques. By 2010, at least twelve full‑length Santali plays had been published, most employing a three‑act realist structure while retaining song‑inserts as “Greek chorus” commentary.

Metrical Traditions in Santali Poetry: Oral Meter and Print Adaptation

Santali oral poetry operates on two principal metrical systems. The karma song employs a seven‑plus‑seven syllable line (7+7) with a caesura after the fourth syllable, producing a rhythmic pattern that aligns with the dance steps of the harvest festival. The baha chant, by contrast, uses a flexible accentual verse: each line contains four strong beats, but the number of weak syllables varies to accommodate the improvisatory narration. Both systems rely on alliteration and vowel harmony rather than end‑rhyme, a feature documented by linguist Dr. P. K. Mishra in Santali Prosody (1992).

When Santali poets began publishing in print (first in Roman script, later in Ol Chiki), they faced the problem of representing oral meter visually. Early poets such as Sadhu Ramchand Murmu (1910s) adopted a free‑verse layout, breaking lines at natural breath pauses but abandoning the strict 7+7 count. The 1975 novel Maran Buru includes “song‑inserts” that preserve the 7+7 pattern by printing each half‑line on a separate line, a typographic convention later codified in the Sahitya Akademi’s Santali Poetics Handbook (1998).

The 1990s saw a deliberate revival of traditional meter in printed poetry. Mahadev Hansda’s collection Sal Phul (1994) marks each karma stanza with a double‑space indentation to signal the caesura, while Dr. K. C. Murmu’s Hul Ki Goonj (1998) prints baha refrains in bold type to visually echo the oral emphasis on the refrain. Contemporary micro‑poetry on WhatsApp (post‑2015) compresses the 7+7 line into a single 160‑character message, often sacrificing the caesura but retaining the alliterative kernel (e.g., “Sal sal, dharti dharti, hul hul”). Scholars argue that this compression constitutes a new “digital meter” governed by platform constraints rather than dance choreography.

Comparative Austroasiatic Literary Traits: Santali, Mundari, and Ho

Santali, Mundari, and Ho belong to the North Munda branch of the Austroasiatic family and share a core set of literary features rooted in a common oral heritage. All three communities organise their mythic corpus around a triad of narrative genres: bonga (spirit myths), hul (rebellion legends), and kherwal (heroic tales). Comparative fieldwork by the Anthropological Survey of India (1990‑1995) recorded parallel motifs — the “sal tree” as world axis, the “river crossing” as initiation, the “iron arrow” as cultural hero’s weapon — across the three languages, suggesting a proto‑Munda narrative substrate.

Script development diverged sharply. Santali adopted the indigenous Ol Chiki script (1925) and later gained Eighth Schedule status (2003). Mundari continues to use a Roman‑based orthography standardised by the Mundari Language Board (1998) and has not achieved scheduled language status. Ho employs a Devanagari‑derived script promoted by the Ho Language Development Council (2001) and received limited state recognition in Odisha (2011). These script choices affect literary production: Ol Chiki’s phonemic precision enables a one‑to‑one mapping of oral meter to print, whereas Roman orthographies for Mundari and Ho require diacritic conventions that are inconsistently applied, leading to greater variation in printed poetic form.

Institutional patronage also differs. The Sahitya Akademi established a Santali advisory board in 1995, a Mundari board only in 2010, and a Ho board in 2015. Consequently, Santali has a longer record of annual awards (first 1975), a dedicated literary journal (Santali Sahitya Patrika, 1995), and a university department (Ranchi University, 1990). Mundari and Ho literary output remains largely confined to community newsletters and NGO‑funded anthologies. However, recent collaborative projects — such as the Tri‑Language Folklore Anthology (2020) edited by Dr. S. K. Tudu — have begun to create a shared critical vocabulary, employing indigenous terms (bonga, hul, kherwal) as analytical categories rather than mere thematic labels.

Santali Short Story: From Oral Anecdote to Modernist Experiment

The Santali short story emerged as a distinct prose form in the 1920s when missionary presses in Calcutta began publishing vernacular readers for school use. The earliest printed narratives were didactic sketches — moral fables translated from Bengali — but by the 1930s writers such as Raghunath Murmu (creator of the Ol Chiki script, 1925) started composing original stories that retained the oral baha (folk‑tale) structure while experimenting with linear plot and psychological interiority. The 1947 partition and the subsequent formation of the Santali‑speaking belt across Bihar, West Bengal, Odisha and Jharkhand created a new readership that demanded stories reflecting displacement, labour migration and the tension between kherwal (community) values and modern wage labour.

A landmark anthology, Hul Kahani (1958), edited by Dr. S. K. Tudu, collected twenty‑three stories written between 1935 and 1955. The volume demonstrates a clear shift: early pieces such as “Bonga Raja” (1935) use a frame narrator and repetitive refrain, whereas later stories like “Damin Rail” (1954) employ a single‑point‑of‑view, dialogue‑driven scenes and an open ending — hallmarks of modernist short‑story technique. The Sahitya Akademi’s first Santali award (1975) went to Mahadev Hansda for his collection Jangal Ke Awaz, which fused the episodic oral mode with a realist depiction of forest‑rights struggles, signalling institutional recognition of the hybrid form.

From the 1990s onward, the short story became a laboratory for linguistic innovation. Writers began exploiting Ol Chiki’s diacritic system to render dialectal phonemes (e.g., the aspirated ʰ in “ᱚᱞᱟᱜᱟ”) that Devanagari could not capture, thereby turning orthography into a stylistic device. The quarterly Santali Sahitya Patrika (launched 1995) instituted a regular “Short‑Story Competition” that mandated a maximum of 2,500 words, forcing compression and sharpening of narrative focus. Contemporary authors such as Damayanti Besra and Rupchand Hansda use the form to interrogate gender, ecological loss and the politics of the hul (rebellion) memory, often embedding a single bonga invocation as a structural pivot rather than a decorative motif.

Santali Women Writers: Gender, Voice, and Publication Trajectories

Women’s entry into printed Santali literature was mediated by missionary girls’ schools in the 1910s, which taught literacy in Roman script before Ol Chiki’s adoption. Santali Autobiography and Life Writing: Self‑Representation in a Communal Culture

Autobiography in Santali is a relatively recent genre, the first full‑length life narrative being Mahadev Hansda’s Jangal Ke Awaz (1995), which blends personal memoir with collective history of the hul (1855‑56 rebellion). The genre’s delayed emergence reflects a cultural emphasis on kherwal (community) over individual self‑fashioning; early life writing appeared as “testimonies” in missionary periodicals, where converts narrated conversion experiences in a formulaic “before‑after” structure.

Santali Epic Tradition: The Baha Cycle and Its Literary Reworkings

The Baha (also rendered Baha Geet) constitutes the most extensive heroic‑epic corpus in Santali oral literature. Missionary linguists such as Rev. A. Campbell and Rev. J. Phillips recorded the first written versions in the 1880s and 1890s, transcribing performances by traditional bards (jogis) in the Latin‑based “Santali Roman” script. The cycle narrates the exploits of the culture hero Baha, his confrontations with the malevolent spirit Marang Buru, and the establishment of the Santal moral order (kherwal). Structurally, the epic unfolds in episodic cantos (daṅ) each anchored by a refrain (dhun) that signals a shift in narrative perspective — a feature that mirrors the performance practice of alternating solo and choral sections.

In the early twentieth century, the epic entered print through the efforts of the Santali Sahitya Parishad (founded 1924), which published a three‑volume edition (1931‑1934) edited by Pandit Raghunath Murmu. This edition imposed a linear chapter order, added explanatory footnotes in Bengali, and replaced the oral refrain with prose summaries, thereby reshaping the text for a literate readership. The 1970s saw a second major reworking when the Jharkhand State Government commissioned a “standardised” version for school curricula; the editorial committee, chaired by Dr. B. K. Murmu, introduced a uniform Ol Chiki orthography, excised dialectal variants from the Mayurbhanj and Santhal Parganas regions, and inserted moral‑didactic commentaries aligned with the National Curriculum Framework of 1975.

Contemporary Santali writers such as Damayanti Besra and Mahadev Hansda have appropriated Baha motifs in novels and poetry, treating the epic as a reservoir of archetypal imagery rather than a fixed narrative. Besra’s novel Baha Rekha (1998) re‑imagines the hero’s journey as a metaphor for post‑colonial land struggles, while Hansda’s poem “Baha‑r Hul” (2005) uses the refrain structure to critique industrial displacement. These literary reworkings illustrate a dynamic interplay between oral composition, print standardisation, and modern authorial intervention — a triad that examiners frequently probe through comparative close‑reading questions.

Santali Children’s Literature: Pedagogical Texts and the Construction of a Literary Canon for Youth

The emergence of a distinct children’s literature in Santali coincides with the expansion of formal schooling in the Santal Parganas after Independence. The first dedicated primer, Hul Puthi (1952), compiled by the Bihar Education Department, presented alphabet charts, simple folktales, and moral verses in the Roman script. Its pedagogical aim was literacy acquisition rather than aesthetic cultivation, and the texts were heavily sanitized — violent episodes from traditional tales were omitted, and gender roles were reinforced according to mid‑century nationalist ideals.

A turning point arrived with the launch of the monthly magazine Hul (1975) by the Santali Sahitya Parishad. Edited by Shri Lakhiram Murmu, the magazine serialized original short stories, poems, and illustrated folk narratives written expressly for children. Contributors such as Sushila Hansda and Babulal Soren introduced contemporary themes — environmental stewardship, school life, and inter‑generational dialogue — while retaining the rhythmic cadence of oral storytelling. The magazine’s editorial policy mandated Ol Chiki script from its third year (1978), aligning with the West Bengal government’s 1979 order for Ol Chiki in state examinations.

In the 1990s, the National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) commissioned a series of Santali supplementary readers (Classes I‑V) under the “Tribal Language Development Programme”. These readers, authored by a panel including Dr. R. K. Murmu and Prof. S. K. Besra, integrated scientific concepts with Santali cosmology, e.g., a chapter on the water cycle framed through the myth of the river goddess Jaher Ayo. The 2003 Eighth Schedule inclusion catalysed further production: the Jharkhand State Textbook Corporation released a revised Santali Bal Sahitya series (2006‑2010) featuring colour illustrations, activity pages, and a controlled vocabulary list of 1,200 headwords derived from the Santali Academy’s lexical database.

Scholarly analysis (e.g., K. K. Bhattacharya, Tribal Children’s Literature in India, 2012) argues that this corpus functions simultaneously as a language‑maintenance tool and a site of canon formation: the recurring selection of certain folktales (e.g., “The Clever Fox”, “The Origin of the Sal Tree”) across textbooks, magazines, and anthologies establishes a de facto “children’s canon” that privileges specific narrative motifs and moral frameworks. Examiners often ask candidates to trace the institutional trajectory from missionary primers to state‑sponsored readers, noting script shifts, editorial interventions, and the ideological framing of childhood.

Santali Environmental Imagination: Ecocritical Themes in Poetry and Prose

Ecocritical scholarship on Santali literature has grown since the 1990s, drawing attention to the pervasive presence of forest, river, and hill motifs as more than decorative backdrop. The Sal tree (Shorea robusta) functions as a cultural keystone: it appears in creation myths, in the Baha epic as the site of the hero’s first victory, and in modern poems such as “Sal‑r Dang” (1994) by Mahadev Hansda, where the tree’s seasonal shedding becomes a metaphor for community resilience against mining displacement. Similarly, the river Jaher Ayo is invoked in both oral songs (e.g., “Jaher Ayo Re”, recorded 1920) and in the novel Jaher‑r Hul (2002) by Damayanti Besra, where the river’s pollution by industrial effluents structures the plot’s central conflict.

Scholars such as N. K. Bhattacharya (Tribal Ecologies in Indian Literature, 2008) identify a distinct “animistic ecology” in Santali texts: non‑human entities (bonga spirits, ancestral trees) are granted agency, and human‑nature relations are mediated through ritual reciprocity (e.g., the annual Sohrai festival’s offering of first harvest to the earth). This worldview contrasts with the anthropocentric development discourse of post‑Independence state planning, a tension that surfaces in the short story “Kharkai” (1999) by Babulal Soren, where a dam project submerges a sacred grove and the protagonist’s resistance is framed as a defense of cosmological order rather than mere livelihood.

The institutional reception of ecocritical themes is evident in the Sahitya Akademi’s 2015 award to the poetry collection Hul‑r Baha (edited by R. K. Murmu), which foregrounds environmental justice. The award citation explicitly references “the articulation of tribal ecological knowledge as literary resistance”. For examination purposes, candidates should be prepared to map specific textual motifs (Sal, Jaher Ayo, bonga) to theoretical concepts (animism, biocultural diversity, environmental justice) and to cite the institutional markers (awards, curricula inclusion, anthology selection) that legitimise these readings.

Santali Diaspora Writing: Migration, Memory, and Transnational Publication

Santali communities have migrated historically to the tea gardens of Assam, the coal belts of West Bengal, and the industrial towns of Odisha, creating a diasporic literary strand that negotiates displacement and cultural continuity. The earliest diaspora texts appear in the 1930s: the Assam‑based periodical Santali Samachar (1934‑1942) published letters and poems from Santal labourers in the Dooars, written in Roman script and later transcribed into Ol Chiki by the Santali Sahitya Parishad in the 1980s. These writings articulate a “double belonging” — loyalty to the ancestral village (atu) and adaptation to the plantation hierarchy.

In the post‑1990s period, the rise of digital platforms enabled a new wave of transnational publication. The online journal Hul‑e‑Duniya (launched 2009, hosted by the Santali Academy) features contributions from Santali migrants in Bangladesh, Nepal, and the Gulf states. Notable works include the short story collection Desh‑r Bhinna (2014) by Rina Murmu, which juxtaposes the ritual of the Karam festival in a Jharkhand village with its truncated observance in a Dubai labour camp. The narrative employs code‑switching between Santali, Hindi, and English, reflecting the multilingual reality of the diaspora.

Academic analysis (e.g., S. K. Besra, “Diaspora and the Santali Literary Field”, Journal of Tribal Studies, 2017) argues that diaspora writing expands the Santali canon by introducing themes of labour migration, remittance economies, and digital mediation, while simultaneously prompting debates within the Santali Academy about orthographic standards for online texts. The Academy’s 2018 “Digital Orthography Guidelines” mandate Ol Chiki Unicode with standardized diacritics for diaspora submissions, a policy that examiners may reference when asking about language planning in transnational contexts.

Corpus Planning and Lexical Expansion in Santali after Eighth Schedule Inclusion

The inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution (2003) triggered a formal corpus‑planning apparatus that operates at three levels: the Santali Academy (established 2005 under the Jharkhand State Government), the Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL) Santali Centre (Mysore), and the Sahitya Akademi’s Santali Advisory Board. Their coordinated agenda encompasses dictionary compilation, terminology standardisation for administration and science, and the creation of a digital lexical database (Santali WordNet, launched 2012).

The first major output was the Santali‑Hindi‑English Trilingual Dictionary (2008), edited by Dr. R. K. Murmu, containing 25,000 headwords with grammatical tags, usage examples drawn from literary texts, and dialectal markers for the three major varieties (Northern, Southern, and Western). Subsequent terminology projects produced the Administrative Glossary (2011) — 3,200 terms for revenue, forest, and education departments — and the Science and Technology Lexicon (2015), which coined neologisms such as bijuli‑r kherwal (electricity) and computer‑r bhasha (computer language) following the principle of “root‑extension” (using existing morphemes rather than wholesale borrowing).

The digital WordNet integrates these resources, linking each synset to Ol Chiki, Devanagari, and Roman scripts, and provides morphological analysers developed in collaboration with the Indian Institute of Technology, Kharagpur. The platform’s usage statistics (reported in the Academy’s 2020 annual report) show 1.2 million queries per year, with a spike during the annual Santali language examinations. Santali Literary Historiography: Competing Periodizations and Their Ideological Underpinnings

Scholars of Santali literature have proposed at least three major periodizations, each reflecting a distinct ideological stance. The missionary‑colonial model, articulated by early linguists such as G. C. Ghosh and P. O. Bodding, divides the tradition into a pre‑script oral phase (pre‑1850), a missionary transcription phase (1850‑1925), and a modern print phase (post‑1925). This scheme foregrounds the role of European philology and the introduction of the Roman script, positioning the missionary as the primary agent of literary modernity. In contrast, the nationalist‑recovery model, championed by the Santali Sahitya Parishad after its formation in 1931, inserts a “renaissance” period (1925‑1950) marked by the invention of Ol Chiki by Pandit Raghunath Murmu, the emergence of native‑authored journals, and the first novels such as Kherwal Bonga. This periodization claims agency for Santal intellectuals and treats the script invention as a decolonial act. A third, post‑Eighth‑Schedule model, advanced by the Santali Academy and the Sahitya Akademi’s Santali Advisory Board after 2003, adds a “institutional consolidation” phase (2003‑present) characterised by state‑funded dictionaries, curriculum integration, and digital corpora. Each framework privileges different archives: missionary grammars, Parishad minutes, or Academy reports. Examiners often ask candidates to evaluate how the choice of periodization shapes the canon — for instance, whether the early missionary translations of Baha songs are treated as source texts or as colonial distortions. A nuanced answer acknowledges that the three models are not mutually exclusive but operate simultaneously in contemporary scholarship, and that the “period” label itself is a methodological decision with political consequences.

Santali Oral Poetry Performance Practice: Ethnomusicological Perspectives

Santali oral poetry is inseparable from its performance context. The Baha cycle, the Karam songs, and the Sohrai harvest chants are rendered in communal settings where music, dance, and ritual speech converge. Ethnomusicological fieldwork in the Santhal Parganas and the Chotanagpur plateau has documented a recurring structural template: an opening invocation (jap) addressed to the bonga spirits, a call‑and‑response section led by a guru (master singer) and answered by a chorus of women, and a closing refrain (dhun) that repeats a core melodic motif. Instruments such as the tumdak (single‑headed drum), banam (bowed lute), and flute provide a rhythmic scaffold that dictates the poetic meter — typically a four‑beat cycle with a syncopated off‑beat that mirrors the agricultural calendar. The guru’s improvisatory role is constrained by a repertoire of formulaic epithets (kherwal, marang) that encode genealogical and ecological knowledge. Recent recordings deposited in the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts (IGNCA) archive reveal regional variation: the northern variety (Dumka) favours a slower tempo and extended melismatic passages, while the southern variety (Jhargram) employs a faster pulse and tighter responsorial exchanges. These performance parameters affect textual transmission; when songs are transcribed into Ol Chiki for print, the refrain is often rendered as a repeated stanza, obscuring its function as a communal anchor. Candidates should be prepared to discuss how the loss of performative cues in written editions reshapes literary analysis, and to cite the IGNCA field recordings (catalogue numbers IGNCA‑SAN‑001 to IGNCA‑SAN‑015) as primary evidence.

Santali Language Contact and Code‑Switching in Urban Settings

Rapid urbanisation in Jharkhand, West Bengal, and Odisha has produced multilingual neighbourhoods where Santali speakers routinely alternate between Santali, Hindi, Bengali, and English. Sociolinguistic surveys conducted by the Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL) in Ranchi (2014) and Kolkata (2017) identify three recurrent code‑switching patterns. First, intra‑sentential switching occurs in market transactions, where numerals and measure terms are borrowed from Hindi (paisa, kilogram) while the syntactic frame remains Santali. Second, inter‑sentential switching appears in youth peer groups: a Santali narrative clause is followed by a Hindi comment clause (“Ato re̱ḵa, lekin kal exam hai”). Third, tag‑switching functions as a solidarity marker in formal meetings, where a Santali speech ends with an English tag (“Johar, thank you”). These patterns correlate with education level and media exposure; speakers with secondary schooling show higher rates of English tags, while those engaged in informal labour retain Hindi numeral borrowing. The Santali Academy’s 2019 “Urban Language Use” report notes that code‑switching is not random but follows a “matrix language frame” model where Santali supplies the grammatical skeleton and the contact languages supply lexical islands. This has implications for corpus planning: the Academy’s lexical expansion committee now tags borrowed numerals and technical terms with a “contact” flag in the Santali WordNet, enabling downstream tools to distinguish native morphology from contact‑induced forms. Candidates should be able to describe the matrix‑language framework, cite the CIIL survey methodology (structured interviews, 1,200 respondents), and explain how the contact‑flag system supports both lexicography and language‑teaching materials.

Santali Literary Translation into English: Strategies and Reception

Translation of Santali literature into English has passed through three identifiable stages. The missionary stage (1880‑1930) produced literal renderings of folk tales and hymns, exemplified by P. O. Bodding’s Santali Folk Tales (1925), which preserved the original syntax at the expense of English fluency. The nationalist stage (1950‑1990) saw native scholars such as R. K. Murmu and S. K. Besra adopt a “cultural equivalence” strategy: they replaced culture‑specific terms (bonga, kherwal) with explanatory glosses and added footnotes, aiming to make the texts accessible to Indian English readers while signalling cultural difference. The contemporary stage (post‑2000) is marked by collaborative anthologies — The Santali World (Sahitya Akademi, 2008) and Voices from the Margins (Zubaan, 2015) — where translators work with living authors to negotiate “dynamic equivalence”. In these projects, the translator’s preface often discusses the decision to retain Ol Chiki script for key terms (e.g., Ol Chiki itself) and to use italicised Santali words for ritual concepts, thereby foregrounding the source language’s orthography. Reception studies indicate that academic reviewers praise the footnote density of the nationalist stage but criticise its didactic tone, while literary journals commend the contemporary anthologies for readability but note occasional loss of performative rhythm. For examination purposes, candidates should be able to map each stage to its dominant translation theory (literalism, cultural equivalence, dynamic equivalence), name at least one representative work per stage, and articulate how the choice of strategy reflects the translator’s institutional affiliation (missionary society, university department, or independent press).

Santali Language Revitalization Movements: Grassroots Initiatives and State Support

Since the 1990s, a network of community‑led revitalisation initiatives has complemented state‑level language policy. The “Ol Chiki Schools” movement, initiated by the All India Santali Students’ Federation in 1994, established weekend schools in villages across the Santhal Parganas where children learn reading and writing exclusively in Ol Chiki before transitioning to the state curriculum. By 2010, the federation reported 212 such schools serving approximately 12,000 learners. Parallel to this, the Jharkhand government’s “Mother‑Tongue Education Scheme” (launched 2006) mandated Santali as a medium of instruction in primary grades in districts with a Santali‑speaking population exceeding 30 %. The scheme funded textbook development by the State Council of Educational Research and Training (SCERT), producing a series of graded readers that integrate traditional narratives (Baha, Karam) with contemporary themes (environment, digital literacy). Digital tools have amplified grassroots reach: the “Santali Keyboard” app (released 2017, developed by the Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur in collaboration with the Santali Academy) enables Ol Chiki input on Android devices, and the “Santali Voice” platform (2020) hosts user‑generated audio recordings of poems and stories, creating a living corpus for language learners. Evaluation reports from the National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT, 2021) indicate a measurable increase in literacy rates among Santali children in pilot districts (from 48 % to 62 % over five years). However, challenges persist: teacher recruitment remains insufficient, and the transition to Hindi/English medium in upper primary grades creates a “language shift” pressure.
