Santali Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Santali is a Munda language of the Austroasiatic family. It has a rich oral tradition; the Ol Chiki script was invented by Raghunath Murmu in 1925, and modern written literature developed in the 20th century.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Oral period: Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.
  • Early written period (20th c.): Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.
  • Modern period (mid-20th c.–present): Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition.

Major Authors

  • Raghunath Murmu: Inventor of the Ol Chiki script and pioneer of modern Santali writing.
  • Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Santali poet and writer.
  • Santali Sahitya Akademi writers: Modern Santali poets, dramatists and fiction writers.

Major Works

  • Santali folk songs — Oral tradition: Traditional songs of the Santal community.
  • Ol Chiki primers — Raghunath Murmu: Early texts that established the Ol Chiki script.
  • Santali poetry — Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Twentieth-century Santali poetry.
  • Modern Santali drama — Modern Santali writers: Twentieth-century drama.

Evolution of Literary Forms: From Oral Folklore to Modern Prose

The trajectory of Santali literature is not merely a linear progression from "oral" to "written," but a complex transformation of form, function, and audience. Understanding this evolution is crucial for Paper I questions that ask about the "nature of change" or "shifts in thematic concerns." The transition was driven by the invention of the Ol Chiki script (1925), which allowed the codification of fluid oral traditions into fixed textual forms.

The Oral Period: Fluidity and Communal Function

In the pre-1925 era, Santali literature existed almost exclusively in an oral mode. The primary forms were Khel (songs), Bhagani (creation myths), and Kabir (narrative poems). These works were characterized by:

  • Metric Flexibility: Unlike fixed poetry, oral songs allowed for improvisation based on the singer's mood or audience reaction.
  • Communal Ownership: Songs like Sohrai and Kuk were not attributed to a single author but belonged to the community. They served ritualistic functions (harvest, marriage, initiation) rather than aesthetic ones in isolation.
  • Motif Repetition: Recurrent themes included nature worship, ancestor reverence, and moral instruction through allegory.

The Transitional Period: The Impact of Ol Chiki (1925–1940)

Raghunath Murmu's invention of the script acted as a catalyst for "fixation." However, this was not an immediate shift to modern prose. The early written period saw the publication of primers and religious texts. This phase introduced standardization. For instance, dialectal variations were smoothed out to create a literary norm. Authors like Sadhu Ramchand Murmu began experimenting with free verse, moving away from strict rhythmic patterns used in folk songs. The audience shifted from the village square (ritual context) to the schoolroom and reading public.

The Modern Period: Prose, Fiction, and Drama

Post-independence, Santali literature diversified significantly. The emergence of fiction required a new narrative structure that did not exist in oral tradition. Writers began exploring individual psychology, social conflict, and urban displacement—themes alien to traditional folk songs. Drama also emerged as a distinct form, often staged for festivals but written with specific dialogue structures. This period is marked by the establishment of the Santali Sahitya Akademi (1960s), which institutionalized literary criticism and canon formation.

Example: Analyzing the Shift in Form
Question: How did the invention of the Ol Chiki script alter the structural integrity of Santali folk songs, and what were the implications for their transmission?
Answer:
The introduction of the Ol Chiki script transformed fluid oral compositions into fixed textual entities. In the oral period, a song like Sohrai could vary in length and wording depending on the singer. With the advent of written literature, specific versions were codified in primers and anthologies.

Implications:
1. Loss of Improvisation: The ability to adapt songs to immediate contexts diminished as "correct" texts became authoritative.
2. Preservation vs. Stagnation: While the core content was preserved from extinction, the dynamic evolution of the language within the song form slowed down.
3. Shift in Audience: The primary mode of transmission shifted from auditory (listening) to visual (reading), changing how literature was consumed and critiqued.
Exam Strategy: Contextualizing "Change"
Tip: When answering questions about literary movements, do not just list dates. Always contrast the function of the literature in different periods. For example, explain how a folk song served a ritual purpose in the oral period but might serve an educational or aesthetic purpose in the modern period. Use terms like "codification," "standardization," and "institutionalization" to demonstrate depth.

Thematic Analysis: From Nature Worship to Social Realism

The thematic content of Santali literature has undergone a profound metamorphosis, reflecting the changing socio-political landscape of the Munda-speaking region. This evolution is essential for answering questions regarding "thematic shifts" or "sociological relevance."

1. The Nature-Centric Universe (Pre-20th Century)

Early literature was deeply animistic and ecological. Themes revolved around the veneration of natural elements: Bong (forest), Hajong (sun), Kharia (moon). The relationship between humans and nature was one of symbiosis and reverence. Myths like Bhagani explained the origin of the world through natural forces. There was no concept of "man vs. nature"; rather, it was "man within nature."

2. The Rise of Social Consciousness (Mid-20th Century)

With the influence of Christian missionaries and later socialist movements, literature began to address social ills. Themes of caste discrimination, land alienation, and exploitation by dominant castes entered the literary discourse. Writers like Sankha Barua and others started using literature as a tool for social reform. The "nature" theme was not abandoned but reinterpreted through the lens of human suffering caused by external forces.

3. Modern Realism and Identity Politics (Late 20th Century–Present)

Contemporary Santali literature grapples with themes of identity, globalization, and migration. The "Santal" is no longer just a forest dweller but an urbanite facing alienation. Themes include the loss of traditional values in modern society, the struggle for linguistic rights (e.g., inclusion in the Eighth Schedule), and the intersectionality of Santali identity with broader Indian nationalism. Fiction now explores the inner conflicts of individuals caught between tradition and modernity.

Example: Thematic Contrast
Question: Compare the treatment of 'nature' in a traditional Bong (forest) song versus a modern Santali short story. How does this reflect broader societal changes?
Answer:
In traditional songs, nature is personified and revered as a deity or ancestor. The forest is a source of life and spiritual connection; the language used is reverential and descriptive.

In modern short stories (e.g., by contemporary Akademi writers), nature often appears as a backdrop to human conflict or a symbol of lost innocence. The relationship may be critical, highlighting environmental degradation or the displacement of Santal communities from their ancestral lands.

Conclusion: This shift reflects the transition from an agrarian, forest-dependent economy to one influenced by industrialization and urbanization, where nature is viewed through a lens of loss and nostalgia rather than active worship.
Exam Strategy: Avoiding Anachronism
Tip: Do not attribute modern social realist themes to pre-1925 literature. Examiners often try to trick candidates by asking about "social reform" in the oral period. Always qualify your answer with the specific historical context (e.g., "Post-independence," "After 1947"). Acknowledge that early literature was primarily mythological and ritualistic, not sociological.

Criticism and Canon Formation: The Role of Institutions

A sophisticated understanding of Santali literature requires knowledge of how "canon" is formed. In the oral period, there was no canon; authority lay with tradition. The modern period introduced literary criticism, journals, and institutions like the Santali Sahitya Akademi. This section covers the mechanisms by which certain works became "classic" while others were marginalized.

The Mechanism of Canonization

Literary works in Santali are elevated to "classic" status through:

  1. Institutional Recognition: Awards from the Sahitya Akademi (New Delhi and regional bodies) play a significant role.
  2. Translation and Anthologization: Works that are translated into Hindi or English often gain wider recognition, though this can sometimes dilute their original context.
  3. Educational Inclusion: Textbooks and syllabi determine what is taught. If a poet's work is included in school readers, they become part of the "official" literary history.

The Debate on Translation vs. Originality

A critical issue in Santali literature is the relationship between original works and translations. Early writers often wrote in a simplified dialect to make it accessible. Later critics argue that this "simplification" compromised linguistic purity. Furthermore, the dominance of certain dialects (e.g., those spoken in Jharkhand vs. Odisha) in literary production has led to debates about regional representation within Santali literature.

Example: Institutional Impact
Question: Discuss the role of the Santali Sahitya Akademi in shaping the canon of Santali literature. What are the criticisms of its selection process?
Answer:
The Santali Sahitya Akademi was established to promote and preserve Santali literature. Its role includes:
1. Dissemination: Publishing journals like Santali and organizing literary festivals.
2. Awards: Recognizing outstanding works, thereby encouraging high-quality writing.

Criticisms:
1. Elitism: Critics argue that the Akademi sometimes favors established writers from urban centers over grassroots oral poets.
2. Linguistic Bias: There are concerns that the literary language is becoming too standardized, losing its connection to diverse regional dialects.
