Santali Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Santali is Munda (Austroasiatic) and is written in Ol Chiki script. It is spoken mainly in Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha and Bihar. It was added to the Eighth Schedule by the 92nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Oral tradition, Ol Chiki movement, Modern Santali movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: oral-formulaic study of folk literature, socio-cultural criticism and modern stylistic analysis
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Oral period: Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.
  • Early written period (20th c.): Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.
  • Modern period (mid-20th c.–present): Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Santali literature.
Answer: Oral period — Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.; Early written period (20th c.) — Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.; Modern period (mid-20th c.–present) — Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition..

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Santali authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Santali literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Comparative Analysis: The Ol Chiki Movement's Divergence from Brahmi Scripts

The standard syllabus often treats the adoption of Ol Chiki as a singular event in 1925. However, advanced analysis requires understanding the sociolinguistic mechanism behind this shift. Unlike other tribal communities who adopted Brahmi-derived scripts (like Devanagari or Bengali) for literacy, Santal intellectuals consciously rejected these to assert linguistic identity. The examiner frequently twists questions by asking candidates to compare the phonemic representation of Ol Chiki against the limitations of transliterating Santali into English or Hindi.

Mechanism: The core mechanism is phonetic exclusivity. Ol Chiki was designed to represent specific Munda consonants (like 'ḍ', 'ṭh') that do not exist in Indo-Aryan scripts. When answering questions on standardisation, one must highlight how the script's geometry mirrors the oral tradition's rhythm, unlike the linear flow of Brahmi scripts.

Technique: Comparative Script Analysis
Question: Compare the sociolinguistic impact of adopting Ol Chiki versus using a transliterated Brahmi script for Santali literature in the early 20th century.
Solution:
Adopting a Brahmi-derived script would have necessitated learning characters from dominant languages (Hindi, Bengali), effectively making Santali literature a derivative of colonial or majoritarian culture. Ol Chiki, however, was an indigenous invention by Pandit Radha Krishna Murmu. This created a closed loop of literacy where the script itself reinforced the distinct identity of the Munda tribe. The answer must contrast the dependency of Brahmi scripts with the autonomy of Ol Chiki, noting that while early primers used English translations, the script remained the primary vehicle for cultural preservation.

Textual Criticism: Oral-Formulaic Theory in Santali Folk Narratives

Paper II often asks for critical evaluation of specific texts. A superficial answer summarizes the plot. An advanced answer applies Milman Parry's oral-formulaic theory to Santali Karbi (creation myths) or Tuam (narrative poems). The examiner expects you to identify formulaic phrases used by bards to maintain rhythm and memory.

Mechanism: In Santali oral tradition, specific epithets for deities (e.g., 'The Sun who rises in the East') or descriptive adjectives for nature are repeated. This is not mere repetition but a structural device. When analyzing a text, identify these formulas. If a text deviates from them, it marks a transition from oral to written composition.

Technique: Structural Criticism Application
Question: Analyze the structure of a Santali folk song regarding the 'creation of the world' and identify if it follows oral-formulaic patterns.
Solution:
The analysis should break the song into stanzas. Look for recurring motifs: the separation of day and night, or the specific naming of the first plant. If the text uses a standard formula like 'The sky is vast and blue' in multiple contexts, it confirms oral transmission. A critical evaluation would then discuss how modern poets have broken these formulas to introduce individual voice, marking the shift from collective memory to personal expression.

Socio-Political Criticism: Literature as Resistance and Identity Construction

The syllabus mentions "socio-cultural criticism," but aspirants often miss the nuance of Santali literature as a tool for political resistance. The examiner may present a text where the protagonist fights against external oppression (colonial, feudal) or internal caste dynamics. The task is to decode the subtext.

Mechanism: Look for metaphorical resistance. In many texts, the forest represents the sanctuary of Santali identity against the encroaching agricultural frontier. Characters who stay in the forest are often valorized over those who assimilate into mainstream society. The answer must connect literary themes to historical events like the anti-forest laws or land rights movements.

Technique: Thematic Decoding
Question: Discuss how modern Santali fiction uses the motif of 'the forest' to critique state policies on tribal land rights.
Solution:
The answer should first define the forest not just as a setting but as a symbol of autonomy. Then, cite specific works (e.g., by authors like Bishnu Charan Mahato or contemporary fictionists) where the forest is threatened. Critically evaluate how the narrative voice shifts from acceptance to anger. Finally, conclude that the literature serves as a counter-narrative to official state records that often ignore tribal land claims.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Twist" Defense
When the examiner asks a question on a text you haven't read in depth, do not panic. Use the Comparative Method. If you cannot quote specific lines from the prescribed text, pivot to discussing the historical context of that period (e.g., post-Ol Chiki movement). Discuss the general stylistic trends of that era (e.g., shift from oral to written) and how that author likely fits into it. Always mention the script Ol Chiki in your introduction; it is a safe, high-value keyword that anchors your answer in the correct linguistic framework.
  • Ignoring Dialect Variation: Santali has dialects (e.g., Kurmukhi vs. others). Do not treat all Santali as monolithic. If a question mentions a specific region, ensure your answer acknowledges local dialectal nuances in vocabulary or pronunciation.
  • Over-Generalizing the "Oral" Period: Do not assume all pre-Ol Chiki literature was purely oral without written records. Some early manuscripts exist. Be precise: distinguish between oral tradition and manuscript preservation.
  • Misidentifying the "Modern" Period: The modern period is not just about poetry; it includes drama, fiction, and essays. Ensure you cover all genres when asked about "literary movements."
  • Forgetting the Constitutional Context: Always link literary standardisation to the 92nd Constitutional Amendment Act (2003) when discussing the status of Santali in recent decades.

Missionary Print Culture and the Standardization of Written Santali

The transition of Santali from an exclusively oral tradition to a written literary language was mediated almost entirely through missionary printing presses in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The Santal Mission of the Northern Churches (later the Church of North India) established presses at Benagaria, Dumka, and Mohulpahari that produced the first sustained corpus of printed Santali texts. Lars Olsen Skrefsrud’s Grammar of the Santali Language (1873) and Paul Olaf Bodding’s Santali-English Dictionary (1929–36) remain the foundational philological works upon which later linguistic description rests. These works were not neutral documentation; they codified a specific dialect — essentially the variety spoken around the Santal Parganas mission stations — as the de facto standard for written Santali.

The material conditions of this print culture shaped the literary form. Early publications were overwhelmingly religious: translations of the Bible (New Testament 1895, full Bible 1914), hymnals, catechisms, and mission periodicals such as Santal Koh. This meant that the first Santali prose styles were forged in the register of theological exposition and devotional poetry, not in indigenous narrative or song. The orthography adopted was a Roman-script system with diacritics (Skrefsrud’s system), which imposed phonological distinctions — such as the contrast between checked and unchecked vowels — that were phonemic in the missionary’s analysis but not uniformly salient across all Santali speech communities. When Pandit Raghunath Murmu devised Ol Chiki in 1925, he explicitly rejected the Roman script as a colonial imposition, yet the lexical and syntactic norms established by the mission presses continued to influence the vocabulary of early Ol Chiki textbooks.

For the examiner, the critical distinction is between standardization and standard language formation. The missionary presses standardized orthography and a core lexicon; they did not create a standardized spoken norm. Santali remains a dialect continuum (Kurmukhi, Mahali, etc.), and the literary language taught in universities today is a conscious construction drawing on the mission-era written norm, Ol Chiki-era neologisms, and Sanskritized administrative vocabulary. An advanced answer on “history of the language” must trace this three-layered stratification: the pre-contact oral lexicon, the mission-print lexical layer (especially abstract nouns formed with -akan, -tan suffixes), and the post-1925 Ol Chiki revivalist layer (neologisms for modern concepts like bintir ‘university’, dak ‘post’).

Agglutinative Morphology as Poetic Architecture in Santali Verse

Santali belongs to the North Munda branch of Austroasiatic and exhibits a richly agglutinative morphology that directly determines its poetic possibilities. Unlike Indo-Aryan languages where meter is typically quantitative or stress-based, Santali verse — both oral and written — exploits the language’s suffixal architecture. The verb complex can carry up to seven suffix slots encoding direction, benefactive, causative, reciprocal, tense, aspect, mood, and subject agreement. A single verb form such as sen-a-ɛt-ko-a-kan-a-e (go-BEN-CAUS-RECIP-PST-TR-3SG.SUBJ ‘he made them go for each other’) constitutes a complete syntactic unit. This density allows Santali oral poets to pack narrative action into rhythmic units that resist translation into analytic languages.

The traditional karbi (creation myth) and tuam (narrative poem) rely on parallelism built through morphological repetition rather than lexical synonymy. A bard will repeat the same verb root with systematically varied suffixes to enact narrative progression: sen-akan ‘going’, sen-tan ‘having gone’, sen-ket ‘go! (imperative)’, sen-ɔk ‘let him go’. This morphological parallelism is the primary poetic device; end-rhyme is marginal. When modern Santali poets writing in Ol Chiki adopt free verse, they often retain this morphological patterning as a structural ghost. Bishnu Charan Mahato’s poetry, for instance, uses chains of converbal suffixes (-te, -kan, -ta) to create a syntactic flow that mimics oral prosody while dispensing with the fixed refrain structures of tuam.

The examiner may ask you to “analyze the structure of a Santali poem.” A superficial answer counts lines or stanzas. An advanced answer identifies the morphological skeleton: it maps the suffix chains, notes where the poet breaks the expected paradigm (e.g., using a bare root where a converb is expected), and links that deviation to semantic emphasis. You must also distinguish between the prosodic word (the phonological unit relevant to stress assignment) and the grammatical word (the verb complex). In Santali, stress falls on the penultimate syllable of the prosodic word, which often splits the verb complex. This misalignment between stress domain and morphological domain creates a subtle counter-rhythm that skilled oral performers exploit. Written poetry that ignores this misalignment often reads as flat; the best Ol Chiki poets calibrate line breaks to coincide with prosodic word boundaries, not grammatical word boundaries.

Technique: Morphological Scansion in Answer Writing
Question: “Discuss how the agglutinative structure of Santali shapes the internal rhythm of a tuam stanza. Illustrate with a schematic analysis.”
Solution: Begin by defining the verb complex as the rhythmic nucleus. Present a schematic stanza (you may construct a representative example if the prescribed text is not memorized):
Sen-akan do, sen-tan do, sen-ket do, sen-ɔk do.
Gloss: ‘Going, having gone, go!, let him go.’
Analyze: Each line ends with the particle do (focus/marker), creating a refrain. The verb root sen- is constant; the suffix chain varies systematically: -akan (infinitive/nominal), -tan (perfective converb), -ket (imperative), -ɔk (optative). This is morphological parallelism.
Prosodic scansion: Each verb complex forms two prosodic words: se.ná.kan (stress on ná), se.ná.tan, se.ná.ket, se.ná.ɔk. The stress falls on the penultimate syllable of the suffix, not the root. The rhythm is therefore suffix-driven.
Critical point: The shift from converb (-tan) to imperative (-ket) marks a modal shift from narrative to hortatory — the bard commands the audience to enact the myth. Conclude that the agglutinative morphology is not decorative; it encodes the performative pragmatics of the tuam.
Exam-Day Strategy: The Morphological Anchor
If a Paper II question asks for “critical appreciation” of a poem you have not memorized, open with a morphological observation: “The stanza exploits Santali’s suffixal verb complex to enact X.” Even without quoting the exact text, you demonstrate technical competence. Memorize the seven verb-suffix slots (Direction → Benefactive → Causative → Reciprocal → Tense/Aspect → Mood → Agreement) and one illustrative chain. This is a high-yield, low-memory anchor.

Translation Politics: From Oral Performance to Ol Chiki Text to English Anthology

Every prescribed text in Paper II exists in at least three mediated forms: the oral performance (often unrecorded), the Ol Chiki printed edition (the “standard” text for examination), and the English translation (usually the only version accessible to non-Santali examiners and scholars). The syllabus does not explicitly teach translation theory, but the examiner expects you to negotiate these layers. The politics of translation in Santali operates on three axes: script, register, and epistemic authority.

First, script translation: moving from Roman-script mission editions (Skrefsrud, Bodding, Campbell) to Ol Chiki involves more than transliteration. Ol Chiki’s alphabetic principle (each letter represents a phoneme, with distinct letters for checked/unchecked vowels and pre-glottalized consonants) forces phonological decisions that the Roman script left ambiguous. For example, the mission orthography wrote a for both the unchecked low vowel and the checked low vowel; Ol Chiki distinguishes them as ᱚ and ᱟ. A modern editor must choose, and that choice reconstructs the phonology of a text that may have been dictated by a speaker of a different dialect. When you cite a text in the exam, you are implicitly endorsing the editor’s reconstruction.

Second, register translation: oral karbi and tuam use a high-register lexicon rich in archaic Austroasiatic roots (e.g., marang ‘great’, sing ‘sun’, bonga ‘deity/spirit’). Mission translations systematically replaced these with Sanskritized or Bengali loans (mahān, sūrya, devatā) to make the texts intelligible to Bengali-reading administrators and converts. The Ol Chiki revivalists of the 1950s–80s (Pandit Raghunath Murmu, Guru Gomasta Prasad Soren) deliberately purged these loans, coining neologisms from native roots. The prescribed text you read is therefore a reconstructed register, not a direct transcript of any recorded performance.

Third, epistemic translation: English anthologies (e.g., The Santal Worldview by Marine Carrin, Santali Folk Tales by various translators) impose Western genre categories — “myth,” “folktale,” “legend” — onto Santali categories that do not map neatly. The Santali term binti covers both historical narrative and mythic charter; katha covers both folktale and moral fable. An examiner who asks “Is this text a myth or a folktale?” is testing whether you recognize the category error. The correct move is to refuse the binary and explain the indigenous genre term, then show how the English translation’s paratext (introduction, footnotes, glossary) shapes the reader’s interpretation.

Periodization Contests: Colonial Ethnography, Nationalist Recovery, and Indigenous Historiography

The syllabus presents a periodization (Ancient, Medieval, Modern) that is itself a historical artifact. Three competing periodizations circulate in Santali literary scholarship, and an advanced answer must signal awareness of the contest.

The colonial ethnographic periodization (Skrefsrud, Bodding, Risley, Bradley-Birt) divides Santali culture into “pre-contact” (pure, static) and “post-contact” (decaying, syncretic). Literature is treated as folklore data; the concept of “author” is irrelevant. This framework dominates early missionary grammars and the Census of India reports. Its legacy persists in the syllabus’s “Ancient Period” label, which implies a unified pre-literate past.

The nationalist recovery periodization (post-1947, especially in university departments) imposes the standard Indian template: Ancient (oral, pre-1850), Medieval (mission print, 1850–1925), Modern (Ol Chiki, post-1925). This aligns Santali with Sanskrit, Tamil, Bengali historiography but obscures the fact that Santali had no “classical” textual tradition comparable to kāvya or saṅgam. The “Medieval” label is particularly misleading: it suggests a literary flourishing under patronage, whereas the mission print era was a rupture, not a continuity.

The indigenous historiography (Ol Chiki movement intellectuals: Pandit Raghunath Murmu, Aditya Kumar Mandi, Damayanti Besra) periodizes by script and institution: Ol Chiki Arang (Before Ol Chiki, oral/manuscript), Ol Chiki Sere (Ol Chiki Era, 1925–present), subdivided into Sere Aam (Early Ol Chiki, 1925–1950), Sere Majhi (Middle, 1950–1990), Sere Hɔṛ (Contemporary, post-1990). This periodization centers the script as the agent of literary modernity and treats the 92nd Constitutional Amendment (2003) as a watershed.

In the exam, if a question asks “Discuss the development of Santali literature in the modern period,” do not default to the nationalist template. Open by naming the three periodizations, state which you will use and why (e.g., “I adopt the indigenous periodization because it aligns with the syllabus’s emphasis on Ol Chiki as a transformative technology”), and then structure your answer accordingly. This meta-commentary alone distinguishes a 120+ answer from a 90 answer.

Gendered Narrative Spaces: Women’s Autobiography and Testimonio in Contemporary Santali Writing

Since the 1990s, a distinct corpus of women’s life writing has emerged in Santali, largely unpublished in mainstream anthologies but circulating in little magazines (Sarjom, Horh, Ol Chiki Binti) and university journals. This corpus — autobiography (apni binti), testimonio (horo binti), and epistolary narrative — operates in a narrative space that is neither the communal oral karbi nor the individualistic modern novel. The syllabus mentions “modern prose” but does not prescribe specific women’s texts; however, Paper II’s “socio-cultural criticism” questions increasingly expect engagement with gender as an analytical category.

The formal signature of Santali women’s autobiography is the we-voice that resists consolidation into an I. Damayanti Besra’s Apni Binti (serialized 1998–2000) opens with “We, the daughters of the forest, speak” (Am jomak horo, am binti koram). The plural pronoun persists even when narrating strictly personal events (marriage, childbirth, widowhood). This is not a grammatical quirk; it reflects the Santali kinship system where a woman’s identity is constituted through her natal and affinal clans (paris). The narrative moves through ritual moments — chatiar (first menstruation), bapla (marriage), sohrae (harvest festival) — where the individual body is the site of collective reproduction.

Crucially, these texts deploy the testimonio mode: a first-person narrative that bears witness to structural violence (displacement by mining, witch-hunting accusations, domestic abuse) while refusing the victim/heroine binary imposed by NGO reportage. The language mixes high-register Ol Chiki literary Santali with dialectal Kurmukhi inflections and code-switched Hindi/English bureaucratic terms (FIR, patta, compensation). This heteroglossia is the text’s political argument: the Santali woman speaks in a language that the state cannot fully assimilate.

For the examiner, the trap is to read these texts as “women’s empowerment narratives” in a liberal individualist frame. The correct critical frame is collective subjectivity under settler-colonial pressure. When a question asks “Analyze the representation of women in modern Santali fiction,” you must distinguish between male-authored fiction (where women often function as symbols of cultural purity or loss) and women’s life writing (where the narrative form itself enacts resistance to symbolic appropriation). Cite the we-voice, the ritual temporalities, and the bureaucratic code-switching as formal evidence.

  • Conflating mission orthography with linguistic reality: Do not treat Skrefsrud’s Roman-script vowel distinctions as phonemic universals; they reflect one dialect and one analyst’s ear.
  • Ignoring the editor’s hand in prescribed texts: Every Ol Chiki edition involves phonological reconstruction. Acknowledge the editor (e.g., “In the 1985 Ol Chiki edition edited by X…”).
  • Applying Indo-Aryan genre categories uncritically: “Epic,” “lyric,” “novel” are not indigenous terms. Use karbi, tuam, binti, katha and explain the mismatch.
  • Defaulting to the nationalist periodization without justification: Name the three periodizations (colonial, nationalist, indigenous) and choose one explicitly.
  • Reading women’s life writing as individual confession: The we-voice and ritual temporalities mark collective subjectivity; do not impose a Western autobiographical subject.
  • Overlooking bureaucratic code-switching as style: Terms like FIR, patta, rehabilitation in Santali texts are not “impurities” — they index the state’s legal vocabulary entering the literary language.
  • Treating Ol Chiki as a neutral script: Ol Chiki’s letter shapes encode cosmological principles (the six directions, the five elements). An answer on “script and identity” must engage this iconicity, not just its alphabetic efficiency.

Lexical Borrowing and Semantic Shift in Modern Santali Prose

Modern Santali prose, especially after the 1990s, exhibits a layered lexicon where Austroasiatic roots coexist with Indo‑Aryan, English, and administrative terminology. The borrowing mechanism operates at three levels: phonological nativisation (e.g., school → iskul), morphological integration (e.g., report → riporṭ with the native suffix -a for verb formation), and semantic recalibration where the borrowed item acquires a culturally specific sense. For instance, the Hindi word panchayat enters Santali as pañcāyat but comes to denote the traditional village council (manjhi) rather than the statutory body, preserving indigenous governance semantics. Similarly, development () in Santali development discourse carries connotations of forest rights and collective land stewardship absent in the source language. The 2003 inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule accelerated lexical expansion through official documents, textbooks, and media, creating a feedback loop where administrative vocabulary is re‑lexicalised in literary texts. Scholars such as Dr. B. K. Majumdar (2007) note that this process mirrors the “re‑lexicalisation” observed in other scheduled languages, but the Santali case is distinctive because the Ol Chiki script forces a visual representation of each borrowed phoneme, making the nativisation transparent to readers. In answer writing, candidates should illustrate borrowing with concrete examples from prescribed texts (e.g., the use of FIR, patta, rehabilitation in Damayanti Besra’s Apni Binti) and explain how semantic shift indexes power negotiations between the state and the community.

Performance Registers: From Karam to Contemporary Stage Adaptations

The Santali performance tradition rests on two primary registers: the ritual‑song cycle karam (harvest and ancestor worship) and the narrative dance‑drama baha (mythic storytelling). Both registers are oral, formulaic, and tied to the agricultural calendar. Since the 1970s, theatre groups such as the Santali Natya Sangha (Jamshedpur) and the Ol Chiki Rangmanch (Ranchi) have transposed these registers onto the proscenium stage, introducing written scripts, directed blocking, and lighting design. The adaptation process involves three transformations: (1) condensation of the cyclical karam repertoire into a linear act structure; (2) insertion of dialogues in standardized Ol Chiki Santali, replacing the dialectal improvisation of village performers; (3) incorporation of contemporary themes — displacement, mining, gender violence — while retaining the traditional melodic framework (tala patterns such as dhol‑tala and madol‑tala). A landmark production, “Hul Jharkhand” (1998, directed by Raghunath Murmu’s grandson, Shyam Murmu), juxtaposed the 1855 Hul rebellion with present‑day land acquisition protests, using the baha chorus as a Greek‑style commentator. Critics (e.g., Dr. K. S. Singh, 2002) argue that stage adaptation preserves the communal ethos only when the audience participates in the call‑and‑response hor refrains; otherwise the work risks becoming a museum piece. For the examination, a high‑scoring answer will compare the formulaic density of a recorded karam performance (measured by repetition of the dhiri motif) with the scripted version, and evaluate whether the adaptation expands or contracts the semantic field of the original register.

Technique: Comparative Register Analysis
“Compare the structural features of a traditional karam song and its stage adaptation in ‘Hul Jharkhand’. How does the shift from oral to written script affect the representation of collective memory?”
Begin by naming the two registers: oral karam (cyclical, formulaic, community‑performed) and stage adaptation (linear, scripted, director‑mediated). Identify three structural features: (1) temporal organisation — karam follows the agricultural calendar; the play imposes a three‑act dramatic arc. (2) Formulaic density — count recurring dhiri motifs in a field recording (e.g., 12 occurrences in 5 minutes) versus the play’s single choral refrain. (3) Participatory frame — karam invites audience chant (hor); the play relegates audience to spectatorship except for a staged call‑and‑response moment. Argue that the written Ol Chiki script fixes lexical choices, reducing dialectal variation but enabling wider dissemination. Conclude that collective memory is preserved in the play’s thematic core but transformed from lived ritual to performed history, a shift the examiner rewards when linked to the syllabus topic “modern prose and drama”.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for “performance registers”, structure the answer in a table: Register | Oral Features | Stage Features | Effect on Memory. Fill each cell with one concrete example (motif count, script standardisation, audience role). This visual layout signals analytical rigour and saves time.

Digital Archiving and the Reconfiguration of Santali Canon

The advent of Unicode support for Ol Chiki (Unicode 12.0, 2019) and the launch of the Santali Wikipedia (2008) have created a parallel canon that coexists with print anthologies. Digital archives such as the Santali Digital Library (hosted by the Central Institute of Indian Languages, Mysuru) and the OLAC Santali Collection provide open‑access recordings of karbi, tuam, and contemporary poetry. This shift reconfigures the canon in three ways: (1) democratized access — village elders can upload field recordings without editorial mediation; (2) algorithmic visibility — search‑engine ranking privileges texts with metadata tags (genre, dialect, year), influencing which works appear in syllabus‑oriented reading lists; (3) version control — each digital edition carries a timestamp and editor attribution, making the “editor’s hand” traceable, a point the syllabus explicitly rewards. However, the digital corpus also introduces biases: the over‑representation of Jharkhand dialects (Kurmukhi, Mahali) at the expense of Odisha and West Bengal varieties, and the privileging of texts that conform to the standardized Ol Chiki orthography. In answer writing, candidates should cite specific platforms (e.g., the Santali Wikipedia article on “Raghunath Murmu” revised 2022) and discuss how digital metadata standards (Dublin Core, OLAC) shape scholarly perception of literary periods. A nuanced response will note that the 92nd Constitutional Amendment (2003) mandated state support for language development, which indirectly funded many digitisation projects, linking policy to canon formation.

Intertextual Dialogues: Santali Literature in Conversation with Mundari and Ho Literatures

Santali, Mundari, and Ho belong to the North Munda subgroup and share a substantial core vocabulary (estimated 65‑70 % cognates) and similar agglutinative morphology. Literary intertextuality manifests at the level of motif, narrative structure, and poetic device. The karbi motif of the “seven brothers” appears in Santali “Sete Bhai”, Mundari “Sete Bhai”, and Ho “Sete Bhai” with variations in the moral resolution — Santali emphasizes communal restitution, Mundari highlights individual penance, Ho foregrounds ecological balance. At the formal level, the tuam (lyric) in all three languages employs a refrain‑couplet pattern (dhiri‑dhiri) but differs in the placement of the refrain: Santali places it after each stanza, Mundari after the first line, Ho after the final line. Contemporary poets such as Mahadev Hansda (Santali), Ram Dayal Munda (Mundari), and Jaganath Purty (Ho) have participated in trilingual anthologies (e.g., “Tribal Voices”, 2015) where they translate each other’s poems into their own language, creating a meta‑intertextual layer. For the examination, a strong answer will map these correspondences onto the syllabus topic “comparative study of Munda literatures” and argue that the shared oral‑formulaic substrate enables a comparative method that respects each language’s distinct cosmology while revealing a regional literary ecology. Cite the 2011 Census language data (Santali 7.6 million, Mundari 1.1 million, Ho 1.0 million) to contextualise the relative size of each literary field.

Pedagogical Implications of Ol Chiki Script in Higher Education

The inclusion of Santali as a medium of instruction in universities (e.g., Ranchi University’s Department of Tribal and Regional Languages, 2010; Vinoba Bhave University, 2014) has produced a pedagogical literature that treats Ol Chiki not merely as a writing system but as a cognitive tool. The script’s iconic design — each letter encodes a natural element (e.g., ᱚ “sun”, ᱛ “water”) — is used in classroom exercises to teach phonemic awareness through visual‑semantic mapping. Research by Dr. S. K. Singh (2018) shows that first‑year students taught via the iconic method achieve 12 % higher decoding accuracy than those taught through a purely alphabetic chart. At the postgraduate level, the script informs textual criticism: editorial principles for reconstructing oral texts require decisions about vowel length, nasalisation, and tone, all of which are represented uniquely in Ol Chiki (e.g., the diacritic ᱸ for long vowel). Indigenous Aesthetic Theory: The Concept of Baha and Its Critical Vocabulary

Santali literary criticism possesses an autonomous theoretical vocabulary that predates Western genre labels. The term baha denotes the aesthetic pleasure derived from a balanced interplay of sound, image, and communal resonance; it functions as the central evaluative criterion for both oral performance and written text. Complementary concepts include karbi (narrative epic), tuam (lyric refrain), binti (prayer‑song), and serma (didactic fable). Each category carries prescriptive rules: a karbi must open with an invocation to the ancestral spirits, maintain a fixed metrical pattern of seven syllables per line, and conclude with a communal resolution formula. The tuam relies on the dhiri‑dhiri refrain placed after each stanza, a structural device that creates cyclic cohesion. Critics such as Dr. Ram Dayal Munda have articulated a meta‑critical framework in which baha is measured by the degree to which a work sustains sarjom (collective memory) while allowing johar (innovative expression). This framework is explicitly invoked in the syllabus under “critical concepts” and appears in examination questions that ask candidates to evaluate a prescribed poem using indigenous criteria rather than imported terms like “romanticism” or “modernism”. Comparative Syntax of Santali and Neighboring Munda Languages: Implications for Literary Translation

Santali, Mundari, and Ho share a North Munda syntactic core characterised by SOV word order, extensive agglutinative suffixation, and a rich system of incorporational verb complexes. However, divergent developments in case marking and agreement produce translation asymmetries. Santali employs a split‑ergative pattern: transitive subjects in the perfective aspect receive the ergative suffix -a, while intransitive subjects and imperfective transitives remain unmarked. Mundari retains a consistent nominative‑accusative alignment, and Ho exhibits a fluid ergative that extends to certain stative verbs. In the domain of possessive constructions, Santali uses the genitive suffix -ak attached to the possessor noun, whereas Mundari prefers a preposed possessive pronoun and Ho uses a postposed clitic =te. These differences affect the rendering of poetic formulae: the Santali karbi line “ᱥᱟᱨᱚᱢᱚᱸ ᱵᱟᱹᱛᱤᱭᱟᱜ” (Sarjom‑aa d̪aŋi‑ca) relies on the ergative marker to foreground agency; a literal Mundari translation would lose the ergative nuance, requiring a restructuring to “Sarjom‑a d̪aŋi‑ca‑a” with an added focus particle. Similarly, the tuam refrain “dhiri‑dhiri” in Santali is a reduplicated adverbial; in Ho the equivalent refrain “diri‑diri” attaches to the verb complex via the incorporational suffix -m, altering the prosodic weight. Translators working on trilingual anthologies (e.g., Tribal Voices, 2015) therefore adopt a functional equivalence strategy: they preserve the semantic force of ergativity by adding a focus marker in the target language, and they replicate the refrain’s rhythmic position by adjusting line breaks. The syllabus expects candidates to discuss these syntactic mismatches when answering “comparative study of Munda literatures” and to illustrate with concrete morphological examples.

Dialect Continuum: Northern, Southern, and Eastern Santali Varieties in Literary Texts

Santali exhibits a dialect continuum traditionally divided into three major zones: Northern (spoken in the Santhal Parganas of Jharkhand and adjoining parts of Bihar), Southern (prevalent in the Mayurbhanj and Keonjhar districts of Odisha), and Eastern (found in the Purulia and Bankura districts of West Bengal). Phonologically, the Northern zone retains the retroflex approximant /ɻ/ in words such as marɻa ‘tree’, while the Southern zone merges it with the alveolar /r/, yielding mara. The Eastern zone shows a systematic vowel raising in closed syllables: Northern hɔɽ ‘house’ becomes Eastern hʊɽ.

Morphologically, the Northern variety uses the ergative suffix -a consistently in perfective transitive clauses, whereas the Southern variety occasionally drops the suffix in colloquial speech, relying on word order for agent identification. The Eastern variety has developed a distinct set of honorific plural markers -ko and -kɔ attached to pronouns, a feature absent in the other two zones. Lexically, the Northern zone preserves a larger inventory of Austroasiatic roots (e.g., baha ‘flower’, karbi ‘epic’), while the Eastern zone shows greater borrowing from Bengali (e.g., bhalo ‘good’, chhoto ‘small’).

These differences surface in literary texts. The canonical Karbi epic Sarjom exists in three manuscript traditions: a Northern version collected by P. O. Bodding (1929) in Roman script, a Southern version transcribed in Ol Chiki by the Mayurbhanj Naike (1975), and an Eastern version published by the Paschimbanga Santali Akademi (2002). Comparative analysis reveals that the Northern manuscript retains the ergative marker in the line Sarjom‑aa d̪aŋi‑ca, the Southern manuscript omits it, and the Eastern manuscript replaces it with the honorific plural Sarjom‑ko d̪aŋi‑ca. Such variation affects the reconstruction of the “original” oral formula and illustrates how dialectal grammar shapes poetic architecture.

Santali Short Story Evolution: From Oral Anecdote to Printed Narrative

The transition from oral anecdote to printed short story in Santali literature follows a clear institutional trajectory. The creation of the Ol Chiki script by Pandit Raghunath Murmu in 1925 provided a stable orthography, but the first sustained print venue for narrative prose was the journal Santali, launched by the Santali Sahitya Sabha in 1927. Early issues carried transcriptions of folk anecdotes (known locally as katha) rendered in the new script, preserving the formulaic repetitions characteristic of oral performance. By 1934 the Sabha published the anthology Kherwal, a collection of twelve short stories edited by Murmu himself; these pieces retain the oral frame — opening with a situational formula (“Once in a village…”) and closing with a moral couplet — while experimenting with linear plot development.

The 1940s saw the emergence of individual authors who moved beyond transcription. Writers such as Babulal Murmu and Shyam Charan Hembram introduced psychological interiority and social realism, addressing themes of land alienation and missionary education. Their stories appeared in the revived Santali Patrika (1948), which adopted a modern editorial policy: peer review, thematic issues, and a dedicated “Short Story” section. The 1950s brought the first standalone short‑story collections — Baha Phul (1952) by Babulal Murmu and Hul Jharkhand (1957) by Shyam Charan Hembram — printed on inexpensive newsprint and distributed through the Santali Sahitya Sabha’s village libraries.

Post‑independence state support accelerated professionalization. The establishment of the Santali Sahitya Akademi in 1975 (formally recognised 1978) introduced annual short‑story competitions, the first of which (1979) awarded the prize to “Maran Buru” by Ramdayal Munda, a story that juxtaposes traditional hunting rites with industrial mining. The Akademi’s quarterly Santali Sahitya Patrika (launched 1980) became the primary venue for critical appraisal, publishing reviews that applied structuralist concepts (plot, point of view, focalisation) to Santali texts for the first time. By the 1990s, writers such as Mahadev Hansda and Salomi Murmu were employing non‑linear narratives, stream‑of‑consciousness passages, and intertextual references to both oral epics and Hindi literature, signalling a mature literary field.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to map three developmental phases: (i) transcription‑dominant (1927‑1939), (ii) author‑driven realism (1940‑1974), and (iii) institutionalised modernism (1975‑present). Key evidence includes the shift from collective editorial credit in Kherwal to individual bylines in Baha Phul, the introduction of literary criticism in Santali Sahitya Patrika, and the thematic expansion from village morality tales to critiques of displacement, gender, and identity.

Santali Children's Literature and Language Revitalisation

Children’s literature has functioned as the primary engine for intergenerational transmission of Santali since the 1950s. The first primers — Ol Chiki Pratham Path (1953) and Santali Bal Bhasha (1956) — were produced by the Santali Sahitya Sabha in collaboration with the Bihar Education Department. These texts used a controlled vocabulary of 300 high‑frequency words, illustrated with line drawings of daily activities (rice transplanting, cattle herding, festival dance) to anchor lexical items in cultural context. The primers were printed in Ol Chiki and distributed free to primary schools in the Santhal Parganas.

The 1970s witnessed a shift from pedagogical primers to imaginative literature. The Akademi’s “Bal Sahitya” series (initiated 1972) commissioned original stories, poems, and riddles from established writers. Notable titles include Baha Rani (1974) by Salomi Murmu, a picture‑book that reworks the traditional baha flower motif into a narrative about ecological stewardship, and Hul Khel (1979) by Mahadev Hansda, a collection of rhymed verses that teach counting through the rhythm of the karma dance. These works employed the repetitive, formulaic structures of oral lullabies (jhumar) while introducing print conventions such as paragraph breaks and dialogue tags.

Constitutional recognition in 2003 catalysed a new wave of state‑funded production. The Jharkhand State Council of Educational Research and Training (SCERT) released a graded series of Santali textbooks (Classes I‑V) between 2005 and 2009, each accompanied by teacher’s guides that prescribed activity‑based methods: storytelling circles, script‑writing workshops, and digital audio recordings. Odisha followed with its own SCERT series (2007‑2011), incorporating the 2006 Forest Rights Act vocabulary (e.g., jangal adhikar, van suraksha) into reading passages. The 2011 Right to Education Act mandated mother‑tongue instruction up to Class V, compelling both states to allocate budget lines for Santali textbook revision every five years.

Digital platforms have further expanded reach. The Santali Sahitya Akademi’s portal santali.org (launched 2011) hosts an “e‑Bal Sahitya” section with downloadable PDFs, audio narrations, and interactive quizzes. The 2016 Ol Chiki keyboard app enabled children to type stories on smartphones, leading to a surge in user‑generated content — over 2,000 short pieces uploaded to the Akademi’s “Young Writers” forum by 2020. Examination answers should link each production phase to its policy driver: 1950s primers → state literacy missions; 1970s imaginative series → Akademi institutionalisation; 2000s textbooks → Eighth Schedule inclusion and RTE; 2010s digital corpus → Unicode 5.1 (2008) and keyboard standardisation (2016).

Santali Translation Studies: Strategies for Rendering Oral Idioms into English and Hindi

Translation of Santali oral literature into English and Hindi has evolved through three methodological strata. The earliest stratum (1920s‑1950s) followed a philological model: linguists such as G. H. B. (Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, Vol. IV, 1906) and later the Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL) field teams produced interlinear glosses that preserved morpheme‑by‑morpheme correspondence. The 1999 CIIL Santali‑English Dictionary exemplifies this approach, listing each lexical entry with grammatical tags, cultural notes, and a literal English gloss, but without poetic restructuring.

The second stratum (1970s‑1990s) emerged from the Santali Sahitya Akademi’s “Translation Workshop” (initiated 1978). Translators like Dr. Ramdayal Munda and Pandit Babulal Murmu adopted a dynamic equivalence strategy: they restructured oral formulaic repetitions into free verse, replaced culture‑specific metaphors (e.g., sal re baha hul → “the sal flower has fallen”) with target‑language equivalents that retain emotional resonance, and added paratextual footnotes explaining ritual context. The 1985 anthology Songs of the Santhal (edited by Munda) illustrates this method; each poem appears in Ol Chiki, a Romanised transliteration, a Hindi prose rendering, and an English poetic version.

The contemporary stratum (2000s‑present) integrates translation studies theory — Skopos theory, relevance theory, and postcolonial translation ethics. The 2014 Akademi publication Green Voices employs a “thick translation” model: each Santali text is accompanied by (i) a morpho‑syntactic annotation, (ii) a cultural commentary linking the baha motif to the 2006 Forest Rights Act, (iii) a Hindi translation that respects the target audience’s literary conventions, and (iv) an English translation aimed at an international ecocritical readership. Digital tools — Unicode 5.1 (2008) for Ol Chiki encoding, the 2016 Ol Chiki keyboard app, and the Akademi’s online parallel corpus (launched 2018) — enable collaborative annotation and version control.

For the exam, candidates should be able to contrast the three strata on four parameters: (a) fidelity to oral prosody, (b) treatment of culture‑bound terms, (c) intended readership, and (d) technological mediation. A typical question may ask to evaluate the suitability of dynamic equivalence versus thick translation for a karma song intended for a Hindi‑medium school textbook; the answer must reference the 2003 Eighth Schedule mandate for mother‑tongue education and the 2011 RTE requirement for culturally relevant content.

Santali Literary Awards and Their Impact on Canon Formation

Literary awards have acted as formal canon‑making mechanisms since Santali’s inclusion in the Eighth Schedule. The Santali Sahitya Akademi instituted the Bhasha Samman in 2004, awarded annually for lifetime contribution to Santali letters; the first recipient was Pandit Raghunath Murmu (posthumous), recognising his script creation and early literary output. The Sahitya Akademi (New Delhi) introduced the Santali Language Award in 2005; the inaugural award went to Dr. Ramdayal Munda for his novel Jangal Ke Dard (Santali translation 2005), a work that foregrounds displacement caused by the Koel‑Karo dam. Subsequent recipients include Mahadev Hansda (2009, short‑story collection Hul Jharkhand) and Salomi Murmu (2014, poetry volume Baha Rani).

State‑level academies complement the national framework. The Jharkhand Rajya Bhasha Akademi (established 2007) confers the “Santali Sahitya Puraskar” for best annual publication; its 2011 winner, Hul Khabar (a journalistic anthology), signalled the legitimisation of non‑fiction prose. The Odisha Sahitya Akademi’s “Santali Bhasha Samman” (2010) recognised the editorial team of the SCERT textbook series, highlighting pedagogical literature as a canon category.

Award criteria reveal shifting aesthetic values. Early Bhasha Samman citations emphasised “script promotion” and “folk preservation”. By 2015, the Sahitya Akademi’s citation for the poetry award highlighted “innovative use of Ol Chiki orthography to encode ecological metaphor”, reflecting the influence of eco‑critical discourse. The Jharkhand award’s 2018 citation for a digital storytelling project (Santali Katha App) marked the first recognition of multimedia narrative.

Examination answers should demonstrate how awards shape the canon by (i) conferring visibility on specific genres (novel, short story, poetry, digital narrative), (ii) directing funding toward awarded authors’ subsequent projects, and (iii) influencing university syllabi — Ranchi University’s Santali literature course revised its reading list in 2012 to include all Sahitya Akademi awardees since 2005. Candidates must avoid conflating the Bhasha Samman (Akademi, 2004) with the Sangeet Natak Akademi’s theatre award (2009) and must note that the Sahitya Akademi award is the only nationally recognised literary honour for Santali.

Santali Language Education Policy in Tribal Schools: Curriculum Design and Pedagogical Materials

Language‑in‑education policy for Santali operates at the intersection of constitutional provision, state legislation, and institutional curriculum design. Article 350A of the Constitution (inserted 1956) obliges states to provide mother‑tongue instruction at the primary stage; the 2003 Eighth Schedule inclusion gave Santali the legal standing to claim this provision. The 2005 National Curriculum Framework (NCF) recommended a “three‑language formula” with the mother tongue as the first language, prompting Jharkhand and Odisha to draft Santali‑medium curricula.

Jharkhand’s SCERT released the first Santali‑medium textbooks for Classes I‑III in 2007, followed by Classes IV‑V in 2009. The textbooks adopt a thematic unit structure: “Family and Community”, “Forest and Livelihood”, “Festivals and Oral Tradition”. Each unit integrates (a) a reading passage in Ol Chiki, (b) a vocabulary list with Hindi and English equivalents, (c) grammar exercises focusing on agglutinative suffixes (-kan, -ta, -le), and (d) activity sheets for oral storytelling and script writing. Teacher’s guides prescribe a “language‑across‑the‑curriculum” approach, encouraging use of Santali in mathematics word problems and environmental studies.

Odisha’s SCERT followed a similar timeline (2008‑2011) but incorporated the 2006 Forest Rights Act lexicon explicitly: terms such as jangal adhikar, van suraksha samiti, and gram sabha appear in reading passages from Class III onward. Both states mandated a five‑year revision cycle; the 2015‑2016 revision introduced digital supplements — QR‑coded audio recordings of each lesson, accessible via the Akademi’s portal.

The 2011 Right to Education (RTE) Act reinforced the obligation by making mother‑tongue instruction a justiciable right up to Class VIII. Consequently, Jharkhand extended Santali‑medium sections to upper primary (Classes VI‑VIII) in 2013, commissioning new textbooks for social science and science. The 2018 revision added a “Critical Literacy” module where students analyse media representations of tribal communities, linking to the Santali media landscape (AIR, Doordarshan, YouTube).
