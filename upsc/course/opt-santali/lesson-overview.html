Santali Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Santali is Munda (Austroasiatic) and is written in Ol Chiki script. It is spoken mainly in Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha and Bihar. It was added to the Eighth Schedule by the 92nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Oral tradition, Ol Chiki movement, Modern Santali movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: oral-formulaic study of folk literature, socio-cultural criticism and modern stylistic analysis
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Oral period: Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.
  • Early written period (20th c.): Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.
  • Modern period (mid-20th c.–present): Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Santali literature.
Answer: Oral period — Folk songs, creation myths and narrative poems.; Early written period (20th c.) — Ol Chiki script, primers and early publications.; Modern period (mid-20th c.–present) — Poetry, drama, fiction and Sahitya Akademi recognition..

Standard Sources

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Santali authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Santali literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Comparative Analysis: The Ol Chiki Movement's Divergence from Brahmi Scripts

The standard syllabus often treats the adoption of Ol Chiki as a singular event in 1925. However, advanced analysis requires understanding the sociolinguistic mechanism behind this shift. Unlike other tribal communities who adopted Brahmi-derived scripts (like Devanagari or Bengali) for literacy, Santal intellectuals consciously rejected these to assert linguistic identity. The examiner frequently twists questions by asking candidates to compare the phonemic representation of Ol Chiki against the limitations of transliterating Santali into English or Hindi.

Mechanism: The core mechanism is phonetic exclusivity. Ol Chiki was designed to represent specific Munda consonants (like 'ḍ', 'ṭh') that do not exist in Indo-Aryan scripts. When answering questions on standardisation, one must highlight how the script's geometry mirrors the oral tradition's rhythm, unlike the linear flow of Brahmi scripts.

Technique: Comparative Script Analysis
Question: Compare the sociolinguistic impact of adopting Ol Chiki versus using a transliterated Brahmi script for Santali literature in the early 20th century.
Solution:
Adopting a Brahmi-derived script would have necessitated learning characters from dominant languages (Hindi, Bengali), effectively making Santali literature a derivative of colonial or majoritarian culture. Ol Chiki, however, was an indigenous invention by Pandit Radha Krishna Murmu. This created a closed loop of literacy where the script itself reinforced the distinct identity of the Munda tribe. The answer must contrast the dependency of Brahmi scripts with the autonomy of Ol Chiki, noting that while early primers used English translations, the script remained the primary vehicle for cultural preservation.

Textual Criticism: Oral-Formulaic Theory in Santali Folk Narratives

Paper II often asks for critical evaluation of specific texts. A superficial answer summarizes the plot. An advanced answer applies Milman Parry's oral-formulaic theory to Santali Karbi (creation myths) or Tuam (narrative poems). The examiner expects you to identify formulaic phrases used by bards to maintain rhythm and memory.

Mechanism: In Santali oral tradition, specific epithets for deities (e.g., 'The Sun who rises in the East') or descriptive adjectives for nature are repeated. This is not mere repetition but a structural device. When analyzing a text, identify these formulas. If a text deviates from them, it marks a transition from oral to written composition.

Technique: Structural Criticism Application
Question: Analyze the structure of a Santali folk song regarding the 'creation of the world' and identify if it follows oral-formulaic patterns.
Solution:
The analysis should break the song into stanzas. Look for recurring motifs: the separation of day and night, or the specific naming of the first plant. If the text uses a standard formula like 'The sky is vast and blue' in multiple contexts, it confirms oral transmission. A critical evaluation would then discuss how modern poets have broken these formulas to introduce individual voice, marking the shift from collective memory to personal expression.

Socio-Political Criticism: Literature as Resistance and Identity Construction

The syllabus mentions "socio-cultural criticism," but aspirants often miss the nuance of Santali literature as a tool for political resistance. The examiner may present a text where the protagonist fights against external oppression (colonial, feudal) or internal caste dynamics. The task is to decode the subtext.

Mechanism: Look for metaphorical resistance. In many texts, the forest represents the sanctuary of Santali identity against the encroaching agricultural frontier. Characters who stay in the forest are often valorized over those who assimilate into mainstream society. The answer must connect literary themes to historical events like the anti-forest laws or land rights movements.

Technique: Thematic Decoding
Question: Discuss how modern Santali fiction uses the motif of 'the forest' to critique state policies on tribal land rights.
Solution:
The answer should first define the forest not just as a setting but as a symbol of autonomy. Then, cite specific works (e.g., by authors like Bishnu Charan Mahato or contemporary fictionists) where the forest is threatened. Critically evaluate how the narrative voice shifts from acceptance to anger. Finally, conclude that the literature serves as a counter-narrative to official state records that often ignore tribal land claims.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Twist" Defense
When the examiner asks a question on a text you haven't read in depth, do not panic. Use the Comparative Method. If you cannot quote specific lines from the prescribed text, pivot to discussing the historical context of that period (e.g., post-Ol Chiki movement). Discuss the general stylistic trends of that era (e.g., shift from oral to written) and how that author likely fits into it. Always mention the script Ol Chiki in your introduction; it is a safe, high-value keyword that anchors your answer in the correct linguistic framework.
  • Ignoring Dialect Variation: Santali has dialects (e.g., Kurmukhi vs. others). Do not treat all Santali as monolithic. If a question mentions a specific region, ensure your answer acknowledges local dialectal nuances in vocabulary or pronunciation.
  • Over-Generalizing the "Oral" Period: Do not assume all pre-Ol Chiki literature was purely oral without written records. Some early manuscripts exist. Be precise: distinguish between oral tradition and manuscript preservation.
  • Misidentifying the "Modern" Period: The modern period is not just about poetry; it includes drama, fiction, and essays. Ensure you cover all genres when asked about "literary movements."
  • Forgetting the Constitutional Context: Always link literary standardisation to the 92nd Constitutional Amendment Act (2003) when discussing the status of Santali in recent decades.

Missionary Print Culture and the Standardization of Written Santali

The transition of Santali from an exclusively oral tradition to a written literary language was mediated almost entirely through missionary printing presses in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The Santal Mission of the Northern Churches (later the Church of North India) established presses at Benagaria, Dumka, and Mohulpahari that produced the first sustained corpus of printed Santali texts. Lars Olsen Skrefsrud’s Grammar of the Santali Language (1873) and Paul Olaf Bodding’s Santali-English Dictionary (1929–36) remain the foundational philological works upon which later linguistic description rests. These works were not neutral documentation; they codified a specific dialect — essentially the variety spoken around the Santal Parganas mission stations — as the de facto standard for written Santali.

The material conditions of this print culture shaped the literary form. Early publications were overwhelmingly religious: translations of the Bible (New Testament 1895, full Bible 1914), hymnals, catechisms, and mission periodicals such as Santal Koh. This meant that the first Santali prose styles were forged in the register of theological exposition and devotional poetry, not in indigenous narrative or song. The orthography adopted was a Roman-script system with diacritics (Skrefsrud’s system), which imposed phonological distinctions — such as the contrast between checked and unchecked vowels — that were phonemic in the missionary’s analysis but not uniformly salient across all Santali speech communities. When Pandit Raghunath Murmu devised Ol Chiki in 1925, he explicitly rejected the Roman script as a colonial imposition, yet the lexical and syntactic norms established by the mission presses continued to influence the vocabulary of early Ol Chiki textbooks.

For the examiner, the critical distinction is between standardization and standard language formation. The missionary presses standardized orthography and a core lexicon; they did not create a standardized spoken norm. Santali remains a dialect continuum (Kurmukhi, Mahali, etc.), and the literary language taught in universities today is a conscious construction drawing on the mission-era written norm, Ol Chiki-era neologisms, and Sanskritized administrative vocabulary. An advanced answer on “history of the language” must trace this three-layered stratification: the pre-contact oral lexicon, the mission-print lexical layer (especially abstract nouns formed with -akan, -tan suffixes), and the post-1925 Ol Chiki revivalist layer (neologisms for modern concepts like bintir ‘university’, dak ‘post’).

Agglutinative Morphology as Poetic Architecture in Santali Verse

Santali belongs to the North Munda branch of Austroasiatic and exhibits a richly agglutinative morphology that directly determines its poetic possibilities. Unlike Indo-Aryan languages where meter is typically quantitative or stress-based, Santali verse — both oral and written — exploits the language’s suffixal architecture. The verb complex can carry up to seven suffix slots encoding direction, benefactive, causative, reciprocal, tense, aspect, mood, and subject agreement. A single verb form such as sen-a-ɛt-ko-a-kan-a-e (go-BEN-CAUS-RECIP-PST-TR-3SG.SUBJ ‘he made them go for each other’) constitutes a complete syntactic unit. This density allows Santali oral poets to pack narrative action into rhythmic units that resist translation into analytic languages.

The traditional karbi (creation myth) and tuam (narrative poem) rely on parallelism built through morphological repetition rather than lexical synonymy. A bard will repeat the same verb root with systematically varied suffixes to enact narrative progression: sen-akan ‘going’, sen-tan ‘having gone’, sen-ket ‘go! (imperative)’, sen-ɔk ‘let him go’. This morphological parallelism is the primary poetic device; end-rhyme is marginal. When modern Santali poets writing in Ol Chiki adopt free verse, they often retain this morphological patterning as a structural ghost. Bishnu Charan Mahato’s poetry, for instance, uses chains of converbal suffixes (-te, -kan, -ta) to create a syntactic flow that mimics oral prosody while dispensing with the fixed refrain structures of tuam.

The examiner may ask you to “analyze the structure of a Santali poem.” A superficial answer counts lines or stanzas. An advanced answer identifies the morphological skeleton: it maps the suffix chains, notes where the poet breaks the expected paradigm (e.g., using a bare root where a converb is expected), and links that deviation to semantic emphasis. You must also distinguish between the prosodic word (the phonological unit relevant to stress assignment) and the grammatical word (the verb complex). In Santali, stress falls on the penultimate syllable of the prosodic word, which often splits the verb complex. This misalignment between stress domain and morphological domain creates a subtle counter-rhythm that skilled oral performers exploit. Written poetry that ignores this misalignment often reads as flat; the best Ol Chiki poets calibrate line breaks to coincide with prosodic word boundaries, not grammatical word boundaries.

Technique: Morphological Scansion in Answer Writing
Question: “Discuss how the agglutinative structure of Santali shapes the internal rhythm of a tuam stanza. Illustrate with a schematic analysis.”
Solution: Begin by defining the verb complex as the rhythmic nucleus. Present a schematic stanza (you may construct a representative example if the prescribed text is not memorized):
Sen-akan do, sen-tan do, sen-ket do, sen-ɔk do.
Gloss: ‘Going, having gone, go!, let him go.’
Analyze: Each line ends with the particle do (focus/marker), creating a refrain. The verb root sen- is constant; the suffix chain varies systematically: -akan (infinitive/nominal), -tan (perfective converb), -ket (imperative), -ɔk (optative). This is morphological parallelism.
Prosodic scansion: Each verb complex forms two prosodic words: se.ná.kan (stress on ná), se.ná.tan, se.ná.ket, se.ná.ɔk. The stress falls on the penultimate syllable of the suffix, not the root. The rhythm is therefore suffix-driven.
Critical point: The shift from converb (-tan) to imperative (-ket) marks a modal shift from narrative to hortatory — the bard commands the audience to enact the myth. Conclude that the agglutinative morphology is not decorative; it encodes the performative pragmatics of the tuam.
Exam-Day Strategy: The Morphological Anchor
If a Paper II question asks for “critical appreciation” of a poem you have not memorized, open with a morphological observation: “The stanza exploits Santali’s suffixal verb complex to enact X.” Even without quoting the exact text, you demonstrate technical competence. Memorize the seven verb-suffix slots (Direction → Benefactive → Causative → Reciprocal → Tense/Aspect → Mood → Agreement) and one illustrative chain. This is a high-yield, low-memory anchor.

Translation Politics: From Oral Performance to Ol Chiki Text to English Anthology

Every prescribed text in Paper II exists in at least three mediated forms: the oral performance (often unrecorded), the Ol Chiki printed edition (the “standard” text for examination), and the English translation (usually the only version accessible to non-Santali examiners and scholars). The syllabus does not explicitly teach translation theory, but the examiner expects you to negotiate these layers. The politics of translation in Santali operates on three axes: script, register, and epistemic authority.

First, script translation: moving from Roman-script mission editions (Skrefsrud, Bodding, Campbell) to Ol Chiki involves more than transliteration. Ol Chiki’s alphabetic principle (each letter represents a phoneme, with distinct letters for checked/unchecked vowels and pre-glottalized consonants) forces phonological decisions that the Roman script left ambiguous. For example, the mission orthography wrote a for both the unchecked low vowel and the checked low vowel; Ol Chiki distinguishes them as ᱚ and ᱟ. A modern editor must choose, and that choice reconstructs the phonology of a text that may have been dictated by a speaker of a different dialect. When you cite a text in the exam, you are implicitly endorsing the editor’s reconstruction.

Second, register translation: oral karbi and tuam use a high-register lexicon rich in archaic Austroasiatic roots (e.g., marang ‘great’, sing ‘sun’, bonga ‘deity/spirit’). Mission translations systematically replaced these with Sanskritized or Bengali loans (mahān, sūrya, devatā) to make the texts intelligible to Bengali-reading administrators and converts. The Ol Chiki revivalists of the 1950s–80s (Pandit Raghunath Murmu, Guru Gomasta Prasad Soren) deliberately purged these loans, coining neologisms from native roots. The prescribed text you read is therefore a reconstructed register, not a direct transcript of any recorded performance.

Third, epistemic translation: English anthologies (e.g., The Santal Worldview by Marine Carrin, Santali Folk Tales by various translators) impose Western genre categories — “myth,” “folktale,” “legend” — onto Santali categories that do not map neatly. The Santali term binti covers both historical narrative and mythic charter; katha covers both folktale and moral fable. An examiner who asks “Is this text a myth or a folktale?” is testing whether you recognize the category error. The correct move is to refuse the binary and explain the indigenous genre term, then show how the English translation’s paratext (introduction, footnotes, glossary) shapes the reader’s interpretation.

Periodization Contests: Colonial Ethnography, Nationalist Recovery, and Indigenous Historiography

The syllabus presents a periodization (Ancient, Medieval, Modern) that is itself a historical artifact. Three competing periodizations circulate in Santali literary scholarship, and an advanced answer must signal awareness of the contest.

The colonial ethnographic periodization (Skrefsrud, Bodding, Risley, Bradley-Birt) divides Santali culture into “pre-contact” (pure, static) and “post-contact” (decaying, syncretic). Literature is treated as folklore data; the concept of “author” is irrelevant. This framework dominates early missionary grammars and the Census of India reports. Its legacy persists in the syllabus’s “Ancient Period” label, which implies a unified pre-literate past.

The nationalist recovery periodization (post-1947, especially in university departments) imposes the standard Indian template: Ancient (oral, pre-1850), Medieval (mission print, 1850–1925), Modern (Ol Chiki, post-1925). This aligns Santali with Sanskrit, Tamil, Bengali historiography but obscures the fact that Santali had no “classical” textual tradition comparable to kāvya or saṅgam. The “Medieval” label is particularly misleading: it suggests a literary flourishing under patronage, whereas the mission print era was a rupture, not a continuity.

The indigenous historiography (Ol Chiki movement intellectuals: Pandit Raghunath Murmu, Aditya Kumar Mandi, Damayanti Besra) periodizes by script and institution: Ol Chiki Arang (Before Ol Chiki, oral/manuscript), Ol Chiki Sere (Ol Chiki Era, 1925–present), subdivided into Sere Aam (Early Ol Chiki, 1925–1950), Sere Majhi (Middle, 1950–1990), Sere Hɔṛ (Contemporary, post-1990). This periodization centers the script as the agent of literary modernity and treats the 92nd Constitutional Amendment (2003) as a watershed.

In the exam, if a question asks “Discuss the development of Santali literature in the modern period,” do not default to the nationalist template. Open by naming the three periodizations, state which you will use and why (e.g., “I adopt the indigenous periodization because it aligns with the syllabus’s emphasis on Ol Chiki as a transformative technology”), and then structure your answer accordingly. This meta-commentary alone distinguishes a 120+ answer from a 90 answer.

Gendered Narrative Spaces: Women’s Autobiography and Testimonio in Contemporary Santali Writing

Since the 1990s, a distinct corpus of women’s life writing has emerged in Santali, largely unpublished in mainstream anthologies but circulating in little magazines (Sarjom, Horh, Ol Chiki Binti) and university journals. This corpus — autobiography (apni binti), testimonio (horo binti), and epistolary narrative — operates in a narrative space that is neither the communal oral karbi nor the individualistic modern novel. The syllabus mentions “modern prose” but does not prescribe specific women’s texts; however, Paper II’s “socio-cultural criticism” questions increasingly expect engagement with gender as an analytical category.

The formal signature of Santali women’s autobiography is the we-voice that resists consolidation into an I. Damayanti Besra’s Apni Binti (serialized 1998–2000) opens with “We, the daughters of the forest, speak” (Am jomak horo, am binti koram). The plural pronoun persists even when narrating strictly personal events (marriage, childbirth, widowhood). This is not a grammatical quirk; it reflects the Santali kinship system where a woman’s identity is constituted through her natal and affinal clans (paris). The narrative moves through ritual moments — chatiar (first menstruation), bapla (marriage), sohrae (harvest festival) — where the individual body is the site of collective reproduction.

Crucially, these texts deploy the testimonio mode: a first-person narrative that bears witness to structural violence (displacement by mining, witch-hunting accusations, domestic abuse) while refusing the victim/heroine binary imposed by NGO reportage. The language mixes high-register Ol Chiki literary Santali with dialectal Kurmukhi inflections and code-switched Hindi/English bureaucratic terms (FIR, patta, compensation). This heteroglossia is the text’s political argument: the Santali woman speaks in a language that the state cannot fully assimilate.

For the examiner, the trap is to read these texts as “women’s empowerment narratives” in a liberal individualist frame. The correct critical frame is collective subjectivity under settler-colonial pressure. When a question asks “Analyze the representation of women in modern Santali fiction,” you must distinguish between male-authored fiction (where women often function as symbols of cultural purity or loss) and women’s life writing (where the narrative form itself enacts resistance to symbolic appropriation). Cite the we-voice, the ritual temporalities, and the bureaucratic code-switching as formal evidence.

  • Conflating mission orthography with linguistic reality: Do not treat Skrefsrud’s Roman-script vowel distinctions as phonemic universals; they reflect one dialect and one analyst’s ear.
  • Ignoring the editor’s hand in prescribed texts: Every Ol Chiki edition involves phonological reconstruction. Acknowledge the editor (e.g., “In the 1985 Ol Chiki edition edited by X…”).
  • Applying Indo-Aryan genre categories uncritically: “Epic,” “lyric,” “novel” are not indigenous terms. Use karbi, tuam, binti, katha and explain the mismatch.
  • Defaulting to the nationalist periodization without justification: Name the three periodizations (colonial, nationalist, indigenous) and choose one explicitly.
  • Reading women’s life writing as individual confession: The we-voice and ritual temporalities mark collective subjectivity; do not impose a Western autobiographical subject.
  • Overlooking bureaucratic code-switching as style: Terms like FIR, patta, rehabilitation in Santali texts are not “impurities” — they index the state’s legal vocabulary entering the literary language.
  • Treating Ol Chiki as a neutral script: Ol Chiki’s letter shapes encode cosmological principles (the six directions, the five elements). An answer on “script and identity” must engage this iconicity, not just its alphabetic efficiency.
