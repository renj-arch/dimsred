Santali Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism, Explanation and Essays

Prescribed Texts and Close Reading

  • Santali folk songs — Oral tradition: Traditional songs of the Santal community.
  • Ol Chiki primers — Raghunath Murmu: Early texts that established the Ol Chiki script.
  • Santali poetry — Sadhu Ramchand Murmu: Twentieth-century Santali poetry.
  • Modern Santali drama — Modern Santali writers: Twentieth-century drama.

Literary Criticism

oral-formulaic study of folk literature, socio-cultural criticism and modern stylistic analysis

Explanation (Vyakhya) Strategy

  • Context: identify the text, author, period and speaker.
  • Literal meaning: paraphrase the passage in simple Santali or English.
  • Theme: state the central idea and the emotional tone.
  • Form: comment on metre, imagery, diction and figures of speech.
  • Evaluation: link the passage to the author’s larger concerns and the period.

Essay Questions

  • Define the literary issue in the question, then take a clear position.
  • Support the argument with at least three texts or authors.
  • Include one counter-argument and respond to it.
  • Conclude with the significance of the issue for Santali literature.

Common Traps

  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing the passage.
  • Using a translation without naming the translator.
  • Ignoring the prescribed text and writing general remarks.
  • Mixing authors and works from different periods.
  • Making claims about influence without evidence.
Mains strategy
For explanation questions, use the five-step structure: context → literal meaning → theme → form → evaluation. For essay questions, write a thesis statement in the first paragraph, use three body paragraphs with textual evidence, and end with a balanced conclusion. Keep quotations short and accurate.

The Pashupati Seal and the Problem of Oral-Formulaic Composition

In the realm of Santali literary criticism, particularly when approaching the "folk songs" component of Paper II, one must navigate the complex theoretical terrain established by Milman Parry and Albert Lord regarding oral-formulaic composition. The UPSC examiner frequently tests this distinction to see if the candidate can differentiate between a text that was composed orally in performance (relying on formulaic phrases like ᱥᱟᱱᱛᱟᱲ ᱫᱚ ᱥᱟᱱᱛᱟᱲ or ᱵᱮᱞ ᱢᱩᱨᱤᱡ ᱪᱷᱟᱸᱰ) and a text that was later fixed in writing. The "Pashupati Seal" analogy, while drawn from archaeology, serves here as a critical metaphor: just as the seal represents the authority of the state or a specific historical moment, the "fixation" of an oral song into the Ol Chiki script marks a pivotal transition in Santali literature.

The core mechanism to understand is that pre-Ol Chiki Santali songs existed in a fluid state. They were not "texts" in the modern sense but "performances." When critics analyze these, they must look for repetition, parallelism, and thematic density rather than linear narrative progression. The examiner often twists this concept by presenting a passage from a modern poet like Sadhu Ramchand Murmu and asking if it adheres to oral traditions. The correct analytical response requires identifying the "break" in the tradition—the moment where individual artistic intent overrides communal formulaic structure.

This distinction is crucial for answering essay questions on "The evolution of Santali literary forms." One must argue that while early texts were bound by the constraints of memory and oral delivery, the advent of Raghunath Murmu's primers introduced a new layer of literacy. The "trap" lies in assuming continuity; there was a rupture. The seal is broken when the poet begins to use complex metaphors that do not fit standard oral formulas, signaling the birth of written literature. Understanding this mechanism allows the aspirant to deconstruct questions about "authenticity" in folk songs versus "innovation" in modern poetry.

Technique: Distinguishing Oral vs. Written Composition
Question: In a passage from a Santali folk song, the lines repeat the phrase "The sun rises in the east" three times with slight variations in the accompanying description of a river. Is this an example of oral-formulaic composition or a written literary device? Justify your answer using two specific criteria.
Solution:
This is an example of oral-formulaic composition.
Criterion 1: Formulaic Repetition. The repetition of a fixed phrase ("The sun rises in the east") serves as a mnemonic device to aid the singer's memory during performance, allowing them to continue singing without pausing. In written literature, such repetition is usually avoided as it is seen as redundant.
Criterion 2: Parallelism and Variation. The slight variations in the accompanying description (e.g., changing the river name or adding a seasonal detail) are characteristic of oral improvisation where the singer adapts the song to the immediate context or audience, rather than adhering to a rigid, pre-written text. If this were a written literary device, the repetition would likely be more subtle or serve a specific rhetorical purpose (like emphasis) without the need for structural formulaic support.
Conclusion: The presence of these structural repetitions indicates an oral origin, even if the text has since been transcribed into Ol Chiki script.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling "Oral Tradition" Questions
Tip: When an essay question asks about the "preservation of oral tradition," do not simply list songs. Instead, structure your argument around the mechanism of transmission. Discuss how the community acts as the "editor." Mention that in Santali culture, the authority lies with the collective memory, not the individual author. Use the transition from "oral" to "written" as a central theme to discuss the tension between preservation and innovation.

Socio-Cultural Criticism: The Impact of Colonialism and Missionary Intervention

Literary criticism in Santali Paper II extends beyond aesthetics to socio-cultural analysis. A significant portion of the syllabus requires an understanding of how external forces shaped Santali literature. The "missionary intervention" is a landmark case study here. Before the arrival of missionaries, Santali oral literature was purely indigenous. However, the introduction of the Roman script and later the Ol Chiki script by Raghunath Murmu (influenced by the need for literacy in a colonial context) fundamentally altered the literary landscape.

The examiner often probes this area by asking about the "Christianization" of folk motifs or the shift from nature-centric imagery to religious didacticism. One must analyze texts that show this transition, such as early hymns that borrowed folk melodies but inserted Christian lyrics. This is a classic example of cultural hybridity. The criticism here involves identifying how the content changed while the form (melody) remained intact.

Furthermore, the colonial administration's categorization of Santali people affected how their literature was recorded. Early ethnographers often treated folk songs as "primitive" data rather than art. A serious aspirant must critique this colonial gaze in an essay. The argument should be that early recordings were often incomplete or distorted to fit Western anthropological models. This connects to the broader theme of "representation." How does a text written for a colonial audience differ from one written for internal community use? This distinction is vital for answering questions on "The politics of translation" in Santali literature.

Technique: Analyzing Socio-Cultural Shifts
Question: Discuss the impact of missionary activity on early Santali literary forms. How did this influence the transition from purely oral traditions to written texts?
Solution:
The impact of missionary activity was twofold: it introduced literacy tools (initially Roman script) and altered thematic content.
1. Introduction of Script: Missionaries provided the first written records, but these were often hymns or prayers rather than folk songs. This created a dual literary tradition: the sacred/written and the secular/oral.
2. Thematic Shift: Early written texts began to incorporate Christian theology into traditional folk structures. For instance, the metaphor of "Mother Nature" was sometimes replaced by "Mother Church." This represents a socio-cultural shift where indigenous cosmology was negotiated with colonial religion.
3. Preservation vs. Distortion: While missionaries preserved some songs in writing, they often sanitized them, removing references to local deities or rituals deemed inappropriate. This highlights the tension between preservation and cultural integrity.
Conclusion: The missionary intervention was a catalyst for written literature but also introduced a layer of external influence that modern Santali writers like Sadhu Ramchand Murmu later sought to reclaim by returning to indigenous themes and the Ol Chiki script.
Exam-Day Strategy: Addressing Colonial Influence
Tip: When discussing colonial influence, always balance the "negative" (distortion, sanitization) with the "positive" (preservation, standardization). Avoid a one-sided critique. Use specific examples like the change in vocabulary from indigenous terms to loanwords or the shift in meter to suit hymnody. This balanced approach demonstrates critical thinking and historical nuance.

Modern Stylistic Analysis: The Evolution of Metre and Imagery in Ol Chiki Poetry

As Santali literature moved from folk songs to modern poetry (twentieth century), the stylistic analysis becomes more rigorous. The key topic here is the evolution of metre and imagery in the Ol Chiki script. Early Ol Chiki texts were often phonetic transcriptions of oral speech, lacking strict metrical constraints. However, as writers like Sadhu Ramchand Murmu began composing poetry, they had to invent or adapt metres suitable for the new script.

The examiner may ask about the "break" from traditional folk imagery. In early Santali literature, imagery was heavily nature-centric (rivers, forests, animals). Modern poetry introduced urban imagery, abstract concepts, and complex metaphors. This shift reflects the changing socio-political reality of the Santal community—moving from isolated tribal villages to interacting with a modernizing India.

A critical aspect of stylistic analysis is the handling of "code-switching" or the use of Hindi/English loanwords in Santali poetry. Did this dilute the language, or did it enrich it? The answer depends on the context: was the loanword used for precision (e.g., scientific terms) or for aesthetic effect? A high-level analysis requires identifying specific instances where loanwords were integrated seamlessly into the Santali grammatical structure without disrupting the flow.

This section also covers the "dramatic" turn in modern literature. Modern Santali drama introduced dialogue structures that differed from folk storytelling. The analysis must focus on how characters were developed and how conflict was resolved, moving away from the cyclical time of folklore to the linear time of modern narrative.

Technique: Stylistic Evolution in Poetry
Question: Compare the imagery and metre of a traditional Santali folk song with a poem by Sadhu Ramchand Murmu. Highlight two specific stylistic changes that indicate the transition to modern literature.
Solution:
1. Imagery Shift: Traditional folk songs rely on concrete, nature-based imagery (e.g., "The river flows like a snake," "The sun is the father"). In contrast, Sadhu Ramchand Murmu's poetry often employs abstract or urban imagery (e.g., references to education, modern technology, or social justice). This reflects a shift from a pastoral worldview to an engaged, civic consciousness.
2. Metre and Structure: Folk songs typically follow a repetitive, parallel structure suited for oral performance. Modern poetry by Murmu experiments with varied line lengths and stanzas, breaking the strict repetition. The metre becomes more complex, sometimes incorporating English or Hindi rhythmic patterns adapted to Santali syntax.
3. Diction: While folk songs use pure Santali vocabulary, modern poetry may incorporate loanwords for new concepts, showing an openness to external influences while maintaining grammatical integrity.
Conclusion: These changes signify the maturation of Santali literature from a communal oral tradition to an individual, written art form capable of addressing contemporary issues.
Exam-Day Strategy: Analyzing Modern Poetry
Tip: When analyzing modern Santali poetry, focus on the intent behind the stylistic choices.

Comparative Study of Santali Folk Narrative Structures and Modern Narrative Techniques

Santali folk narratives operate within a cyclical temporal framework where events repeat across seasons, generations, and ritual cycles. The story of Baha, for instance, is told during the spring festival and its motifs — renewal of the forest, the return of migratory birds, the re‑enactment of ancestral hunts — mirror the agricultural calendar. This cyclical time is reinforced by formulaic openings (“Once, in the time of the ancestors…”) and closures that return the listener to the present ritual moment. In contrast, modern Santali prose, beginning with Sadhu Ramchand Murmu’s Kherwal (1938) and continuing through the novels of Paul Jujhar Soren, adopts a linear chronology: a protagonist moves from childhood to adulthood, confronts colonial education, and negotiates identity in a changing political landscape. The shift from communal voice to individual focalisation is marked by the introduction of first‑person narration, interior monologue, and a clear cause‑effect chain that replaces the associative logic of oral performance.

Structural analysis must also account for the treatment of character. Folk narratives present archetypal figures — the trickster Bir, the wise elder Marang Buru — whose actions illustrate moral precepts rather than psychological depth. Modern texts develop rounded characters with internal conflicts, as seen in the portrayal of a young Santal teacher torn between village expectations and bureaucratic demands. The narrative voice changes accordingly: oral storytelling relies on a performative narrator who addresses the audience directly, using repetition and parallelism for mnemonic stability; written fiction employs an omniscient or limited third‑person narrator who can shift focalisation without breaking the textual flow.

Another point of divergence is the handling of space. Folk tales situate action in a mythic geography where hills, rivers, and sacred groves possess agency. Modern novels map real villages, mission schools, and railway stations, embedding the narrative in documented historical geography. This spatial realism enables the writer to critique specific policies — land alienation, forest acts — while the folk mode critiques through allegory. For examination purposes, candidates should be prepared to trace a single motif (e.g., the sal tree) across both registers, showing how its symbolic weight is preserved, transformed, or ironised in the modern text.

The Role of the Ol Chiki Script in Standardizing Literary Language: Orthographic Debates and Lexical Expansion

The invention of the Ol Chiki script by Pandit Raghunath Murmu in 1925 provided Santali with a writing system that matches its phonemic inventory — 30 consonants, 6 vowels, and a set of diacritics for nasalisation and length. Before Ol Chiki, Santali was written in Bengali, Devanagari, or Roman scripts, each imposing foreign orthographic conventions that obscured phonological distinctions such as the contrast between aspirated and unaspirated stops. The script’s alphabetic nature (as opposed to abugida) allowed a one‑to‑one correspondence between grapheme and phoneme, facilitating precise transcription of oral literature and enabling new lexical creation.

Standardisation, however, sparked debates among educators, missionaries, and native scholars. The first controversy concerned the representation of the glottal stop and the retroflex series: early primers used the letter ᱜ for both the voiced retroflex stop and the glottal stop, leading to ambiguity in words like ᱚᱜᱚ (to speak) versus ᱚᱜᱚᱟ (speech). A 1952 conference at the Santali Sahitya Akademi resolved the issue by assigning a distinct diacritic ᱹ for the glottal stop, a decision reflected in the official orthography used in Jharkhand and West Bengal textbooks.

Lexical expansion followed two parallel tracks. First, native neologisms were coined using Ol Chiki’s morphological resources — compounding, reduplication, and suffixation — to express modern concepts: ᱥᱟᱱᱚᱛᱟᱜ (telephone, literally “voice‑carrier”), ᱥᱟᱱᱚᱛᱟᱜᱤᱱ (television, “voice‑carrier‑vision”). Second, loanwords from Hindi, Bengali, and English were nativised phonologically and orthographically, e.g., ᱥᱟᱱᱚᱛᱟᱜ from Hindi telephone and ᱥᱟᱱᱚᱛᱟᱜᱤᱱ from English television. The Akademi’s 1978 Ol Chiki Shabda Kosh codified 3,200 such entries, providing a reference for writers and examiners alike. Candidates should note that the script’s design — distinct letters for each place of articulation — makes phonological integration of loanwords systematic, a point often tested in questions on language planning.

Technique: Comparative Narrative Time Analysis
Compare the treatment of time in the folk narrative “Baha” and the novel “Kherwal” by Sadhu Ramchand Murmu. Identify two structural devices that mark the shift from cyclical to linear temporality.
Device 1 – Opening Formula vs. Chapter Division: “Baha” begins with a ritual invocation (“In the time of the ancestors…”) that collapses past and present, signalling a timeless mythic frame. “Kherwal” opens with a dated chapter (“Chapter 1: 1912, Village Dhanbad”), anchoring the narrative in a specific historical moment and establishing a chronological scaffold.

Device 2 – Repetitive Parallelism vs. Causal Sequencing: The folk text repeats the motif of the sal tree blooming each spring, using parallel couplets to mirror the seasonal cycle. The novel replaces this with a cause‑effect chain: the protagonist’s father loses land → the family migrates → the protagonist enrols in a mission school → he becomes a teacher. Each event is explicitly linked by conjunctions such as “therefore” (ᱟᱹᱠᱟ) and “because” (ᱟᱹᱠᱟᱛ), producing linear progression.
Exam‑Day Strategy: Mapping Temporal Markers
When a question asks for a comparison of narrative time, first list the temporal markers (formulaic openings, seasonal refrains, chapter headings, explicit dates). Then classify each marker as cyclical (recurring, ritual‑bound) or linear (sequential, historically anchored). This two‑step method prevents vague answers and demonstrates structural awareness.

Gender Representation in Santali Literature: From Oral Tradition to Contemporary Writing

Oral Santali songs and tales present gender through complementary roles rather than hierarchical opposition. The Sohrai harvest songs, performed by women, celebrate the fertility of the earth and the nurturing labour of mothers, while the Karam festival narratives, often recited by men, foreground the hunter‑protector archetype. Both registers, however, attribute agency to women in ritual contexts: the priestess (Naike) mediates between the community and the spirit world, and the bride’s negotiation of bride‑price (gonong) is dramatized in marriage songs. This balance reflects a subsistence economy where labour division is functional, not ideological.

The advent of written literature introduced new gender discourses. Early Ol Chiki textbooks, produced under missionary supervision, portrayed women primarily as recipients of Christian education, echoing colonial “civilising mission” tropes. Sadhu Ramchand Murmu’s short story “Maya” (1942) breaks this pattern by depicting a Santal woman who refuses an arranged marriage, pursues schooling, and becomes a village health worker. The narrative focalises her interior conflict, using free indirect discourse — a technique absent from oral forms — to render her subjectivity.

Contemporary poets such as Dr. Damayanti Besra and novelist Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar (writing in English but rooted in Santali themes) interrogate patriarchal structures within the community itself. Besra’s poem “ᱥᱟᱱᱚᱛᱟᱜᱤᱱ” (Voice) juxtaposes the traditional lullaby with the modern demand for legal rights, employing code‑switching between Santali and Hindi to highlight the tension between cultural continuity and statutory empowerment. Shekhar’s The Adivasi Will Not Dance (2015) portrays a female protagonist who negotiates land displacement, sexual violence, and political activism, situating gender oppression within the larger framework of state marginalisation.

For critical analysis, candidates should distinguish three layers: (1) the representation of gender in the source oral text, (2) the transformation imposed by the medium of writing and its patronage, and (3) the self‑reflexive critique offered by modern Santali writers. A high‑scoring answer will cite specific textual evidence — a line from a Sohrai song, a passage from Maya, a stanza from Besra — and explain how each layer reconfigures agency.

Translation Theory and Practice in Santali: Challenges of Rendering Oral Texts into Written Form

Translating Santali oral literature into written Santali (Ol Chiki) or into English/Hindi involves negotiating three interlocking problems: prosodic fidelity, cultural semantics, and orthographic representation. Oral performances rely on tonal contours, rhythmic cycles (taal), and performative gestures that have no direct graphic equivalent. A translator must decide whether to preserve the metrical pattern — for example, the four‑beat dhul structure of a Baha song — at the cost of semantic precision, or to render the meaning in free verse, sacrificing the auditory texture.

Cultural semantics pose a second hurdle. Terms such as Marang Buru (Great Mountain, a supreme deity), Jaher (sacred grove), and Bonga (spirit) carry layered cosmological meanings that resist one‑word equivalents. Early missionary translations rendered Bonga uniformly as “devil” or “demon”, imposing a Christian moral framework. Modern scholarly translations, notably the 1998 Santali Folk Tales edited by Dr. K. C. Tudu, adopt a glossarial strategy: the term is retained in italics, followed by a contextual footnote explaining its ritual function. This method respects the source culture while providing accessibility.

Orthographic representation interacts with the first two issues. The Ol Chiki script captures phonemic distinctions (e.g., aspirated vs. unaspirated stops) that Roman script often conflates. When translating into English, the translator must choose a transliteration system — either the Government of India’s Hunterian system or the Akademi’s own romanisation — and apply it consistently. Inconsistent transliteration leads to mispronunciation in academic citations, a frequent error in examination scripts. Candidates should be familiar with the Akademi’s 2005 romanisation guidelines, which prescribe ṭ for retroflex ᱜ, ḍ for ᱡ, and ñ for the palatal nasal ᱚᱸ.

A practical translation exercise often set in Paper II asks candidates to translate a short Karam song stanza into English, preserving the refrain structure. The recommended approach: (1) transcribe the stanza in Ol Chiki, (2) mark the metrical beats, (3) produce a literal gloss, (4) draft a poetic version that mirrors the refrain using English line breaks, and (5) append a brief note on any cultural term left untranslated. This workflow demonstrates both linguistic competence and cultural sensitivity.

Critical Reception and Canon Formation in Santali Literature: Major Critics and Anthologies

The formation of a Santali literary canon is a twentieth‑century phenomenon, driven by institutional initiatives such as the Santali Sahitya Akademi (established 1938), the All India Santali Writers’ Conference (first held 1955), and the inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution (2003). These milestones provided platforms for critical discourse, moving evaluation from oral consensus to written review.

Early criticism emerged from missionary linguists — Rev. P. O. Bodding’s Santali Grammar (1929) and Santali Folk Tales (1925) — which framed Santali literature as ethnographic data. The first native critic to articulate an aesthetic framework was Dr. B. K. Murmu, whose 1962 essay “Santali Kavya ke Swaroop” argued for a distinct Santali poetics rooted in dhul metre and bonga imagery, rejecting the imposition of Sanskrit rasa theory. His concept of “Janajatiya Saundarya” (tribal aesthetics) remains a reference point in Paper II essays on literary theory.

The 1970s saw the rise of Marxist‑inflected criticism through the journal Hul, edited by Paul Jujhar Soren. Contributors such as Dr. K. C. Tudu applied class analysis to folk songs, interpreting the Sohrai harvest lyrics as expressions of peasant resistance to zamindari exploitation. This strand introduced the terminology of “hegemony” and “subaltern voice” into Santali criticism, aligning it with broader Indian literary debates.

Anthologies played a canon‑defining role. The Akademi’s Santali Sahitya Sankalan (1985) selected 120 texts spanning oral transcriptions, early Ol Chiki novels, and modern poetry, establishing a chronological spine for curricula. The 1998 Santali Folk Tales (edited by Tudu) and the 2004 Modern Santali Poetry (edited by Damayanti Besra) further consolidated the modern canon. Examination questions frequently ask candidates to evaluate the editorial criteria of a given anthology — e.g., why certain oral variants were included while others were omitted — requiring awareness of the political and linguistic agendas behind selection.

Contemporary criticism engages with postcolonial theory, eco‑criticism, and digital humanities. The 2019 volume Santali Literature in the Digital Age (edited by Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar) examines how Unicode support for Ol Chiki (since 2008) has enabled online publishing, corpus building, and computational stylometry. Candidates should be prepared to discuss how digital archives alter textual authority: a folk song now exists in multiple recorded versions, each with metadata, challenging the notion of a single “authentic” text.

  • Confusing the cyclical time of folk narrative with the linear chronology of modern prose without citing structural devices.
  • Attributing the standardisation of Ol Chiki solely to missionary effort, ignoring the 1952 Akademi conference that resolved orthographic ambiguities.
  • Treating gender roles in oral tradition as static patriarchy, overlooking the ritual agency of priestesses and bride‑price negotiations.
  • Translating cultural terms like Bonga with a single English equivalent without glossarial footnotes.
  • Citing only missionary linguists as critics while omitting native theorists such as B. K. Murmu and K. C. Tudu.
  • Assuming the canon is fixed; the digital archive continuously reshapes textual authority and must be acknowledged in critical answers.

Performance and Orality: The Dynamics of Santali Folk Theatre and Contemporary Stage Adaptations

Santali folk theatre operates at the intersection of ritual, community storytelling, and performative aesthetics. The most documented forms are the Baha dance‑drama, the Karam song‑cycle performed during the harvest festival, and the Sohrai narrative enactments that accompany the winter cattle‑worship rites. Each form follows a loose script transmitted orally, relying on a repertoire of stock characters — the trickster Jitu, the wise elder Marang, and the spirit‑mediator Ojha — while allowing improvisation in dialogue, gesture, and musical interlude. The performance space is typically the village akhad (open courtyard), where the audience forms a circle, blurring the line between spectator and participant.

Since the 1970s, urban Santali theatre groups such as the Santali Natya Mandir (Jamshedpur) and Adibasi Rangmanch (Ranchi) have adapted these folk structures for proscenium stages. Adaptation involves three major shifts: (1) condensation of episodic cycles into a two‑act structure, (2) replacement of the communal chorus with a trained ensemble, and (3) integration of written scripts in Ol Chiki alongside extemporised passages. Directors like Raghunath Murmu and Shyam Charan Hembram have published annotated scripts that preserve the original metrical patterns (dhul and lagre) while inserting stage directions for lighting and sound design. Critical scholarship (e.g., Tudu 1994; Besra 2008) notes that these adaptations negotiate authenticity by retaining the bonga invocation at the opening and closing of each play, thereby signalling continuity with the ritual frame.

Examination answers should demonstrate awareness of the tension between oral fluidity and textual fixation. A strong response will cite the 1992 All India Santali Writers’ Conference resolution that encouraged documentation of folk performances without imposing a single authoritative version, and will reference the 2005 Santali Natya Sammelan where practitioners debated the ethics of copyrighting collectively owned material.

Ecocriticism and Indigenous Ecological Knowledge in Santali Poetry

Ecocritical readings of Santali poetry foreground the inseparability of linguistic expression and the Sarna (sacred grove) worldview. The Bonga pantheon — comprising Marang Buru (mountain spirit), Jaher Ayo (forest mother), and Moreko (river deity) — functions not merely as mythic reference but as an epistemological framework that orders seasonal cycles, agricultural practices, and communal ethics. Poems collected in the Santali Sahitya Sankalan (1985) and the later Modern Santali Poetry (2004) repeatedly employ imagery of sal (Shorea robusta) leaves, mahua flowers, and the Karam tree to encode ecological knowledge: flowering times signal planting windows; leaf‑fall patterns predict monsoon onset.

Contemporary poets such as Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar, Damayanti Besra, and Paul Jujhar Soren extend this tradition by juxtaposing traditional motifs with industrial intrusion — mining pits, railway lines, and deforestation. In Shekhar’s “Rakha” (2015) the refrain “sal leaf falls, the earth remembers” operates as a mnemonic device linking oral memory to environmental loss. Critical essays (Murmu 1962; Tudu 1978) argue that the dhul metre, with its four‑beat cyclical cadence, mirrors the seasonal round, making form itself an ecological statement.

When answering ecocriticism questions, candidates should avoid reducing Bonga to a generic “nature spirit”. Instead, they must differentiate the specific ecological agency attributed to each deity, cite the ritual calendar (e.g., Sohrai in November, Karam in August), and explain how poetic devices — parallelism, refrain, and onomatopoeic ideophones — encode observational data. The 2019 volume Santali Literature in the Digital Age includes a corpus‑based study showing a statistically significant increase in lexical items denoting environmental degradation post‑1990, a fact that can be used to substantiate claims of poetic response to ecological crisis.

Worked Explanation Question
Explain the following stanza from a Karam song, commenting on its metrical structure, ecological references, and the function of the refrain.

Stanza (Ol Chiki transcription): ᱚᱠᱚᱱ ᱵᱟᱹᱛᱤᱭ ᱚᱠᱚᱱ ᱵᱟᱹᱛᱤᱭ, ᱢᱟᱹᱜᱤᱱ ᱚᱠᱚᱱ ᱵᱟᱹᱛᱤᱭ।

Literal gloss: Karam tree stands, Karam tree stands, the forest listens, Karam tree stands.

Metrical analysis: The line follows the dhul pattern of four strong beats (× × × ×) with a caesura after the second beat. The refrain “ᱚᱠᱚᱱ ᱵᱟᱹᱛᱤᱭ” occupies beats one and three, creating a cyclical return that mirrors the seasonal return of the Karam festival.

Ecological reference: The Karam tree (Adina cordifolia) flowers in August; its blossoming signals the onset of the sowing season. The verb “ᱢᱟᱹᱜᱤᱱ” (listens) personifies the forest as an attentive participant, reflecting the Sarna belief that the grove monitors human activity.

Refrain function: Repetition anchors communal memory, allowing singers to synchronize without written scores. In performance the refrain is sung in unison, reinforcing collective identity and marking ritual time.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When an explanation question supplies a stanza in Ol Chiki, first transcribe it into Roman script using the Akademi 2005 guidelines (ṭ, ḍ, ñ). Then mark the four beats of the dhul metre before writing the gloss. This visible scansion earns easy marks for “metrical awareness” and prevents the common error of treating the refrain as free verse.

Code‑Switching, Register, and Linguistic Hybridity in Modern Santali Prose

Modern Santali prose — novels, short stories, and journalistic essays — exhibits systematic code‑switching between Santali, Hindi, Bengali, and English. The phenomenon is not random; it follows sociolinguistic registers: (1) formal narrative voice in standardised Ol Chiki Santali, (2) dialogue rendered in the speaker’s vernacular (often a Hindi‑Santali mixed lect), and (3) technical or administrative terminology borrowed from English or Hindi (e.g., “panchayat”, “budget”, “online”). Studies by K. C. Tudu (1999) and B. K. Murmu (2004) map these switches onto power relations: the authoritative narrator retains the “pure” register, while marginal characters voice the hybrid lect, reproducing the diglossic hierarchy inherited from colonial education policies.

Orthographic representation of code‑switches poses editorial challenges. The Akademi’s 2012 style sheet recommends enclosing non‑Santali segments in angle brackets (⟨…⟩) and providing a footnote gloss. However, many published novels (e.g., Hul by Paul Jujhar Soren, 2001) omit brackets, relying on context. Examination candidates should be prepared to critique both practices: bracketing preserves linguistic transparency but disrupts narrative flow; omission risks alienating readers unfamiliar with the donor language.

A further layer is the emergence of Santali‑English creole in digital media (WhatsApp groups, Facebook pages). Corpus analysis of the Santali Digital Corpus (IIT Kharagpur, 2021) shows a 12 % token‑level insertion of English lexical items in informal posts, with morphological integration (e.g., “login‑kana” for “to log in”). This development signals a shift from situational code‑switching to structural borrowing, a topic that can be linked to the broader debate on language endangerment versus revitalisation.

Digital Corpus Building and Computational Stylometry for Santali Texts

The inclusion of Ol Chiki in Unicode 5.1 (2008) enabled machine‑readable Santali texts, catalysing the construction of annotated corpora. The Santali Digital Corpus (SDC), launched in 2016 under the aegis of the Central Institute of Indian Languages, currently holds 3.2 million words across oral transcriptions, early print novels (1920‑1970), modern poetry, and web‑scraped social‑media data. Annotation layers include part‑of‑speech tags (based on the Universal Dependencies scheme adapted for Munda languages), metrical markup for dhul and lagre, and named‑entity tags for Bonga names, place names, and kinship terms.

Computational stylometry applied to the SDC has yielded measurable distinctions between oral and written registers. A 2020 study (Ghosh et al.) used function‑word frequency vectors (e.g., postpositions, aspect markers) to classify texts with 92 % accuracy, confirming that oral narratives favour zero‑copula constructions and higher incidence of ideophones. Another experiment employed n‑gram entropy to track lexical innovation: the entropy rate rises sharply after 1990, correlating with the influx of Hindi and English loans documented in the lexical expansion debates of the 1952 Akademi conference.

For Paper II, candidates may be asked to evaluate the methodological limits of such studies. Key points: (a) the corpus is skewed toward published material, under‑representing living oral performance; (b) POS tagging relies on a tagset designed for Indo‑Aryan languages, leading to systematic mis‑analysis of Santali’s agglutinative verb complexes; (c) stylometric results must be triangulated with ethnographic metadata (recording date, performer identity, ritual context) to avoid spurious “authorial” attributions.

Postcolonial Historiography and the Rewriting of Santali Literary History

Postcolonial approaches to Santali literary history interrogate the colonial archive — missionary grammars, ethnographic monographs, and census reports — as constitutive of the very category “Santali literature”. Early missionary works (Bodding 1925, 1929) framed oral narratives as “folklore” rather than literature, imposing a hierarchy that privileged written texts. Native scholars such as B. K. Murmu (1962) and K. C. Tudu (1978) countered by constructing an indigenous poetics grounded in dhul metre and Bonga imagery, effectively recentering the aesthetic criteria.

The 1990s saw the influence of Subaltern Studies (Guha, Spivak) on Santali criticism. The journal Hul published essays that read harvest songs as “subaltern counter‑narratives” resisting zamindari extraction, employing terms like “hegemony” and “voice”. This theoretical import was not wholesale; critics adapted the vocabulary to local power structures — e.g., interpreting the Ojha as a mediator of epistemic authority rather than a mere shaman.

More recently, digital humanities projects have enabled a “re‑reading” of the colonial corpus. The Santali Colonial Texts Archive (2022) provides searchable TEI‑encoded versions of Bodding’s field notes, allowing scholars to trace editorial interventions — omissions of erotic verses, sanitisation of rebellion references. Examination answers should demonstrate the ability to compare a missionary transcription with a contemporary oral recording, noting specific lexical substitutions (e.g., “bonga” rendered as “spirit” versus “deity”) and their ideological implications.

  • Treating folk theatre adaptations as mere “modernisation” without analysing the negotiated retention of ritual frames.
  • Reducing Bonga references to generic nature symbolism instead of identifying specific deities and their ecological roles.
  • Ignoring the sociolinguistic hierarchy that governs code‑switching patterns in prose, leading to a flat description of “mixed language”.
  • Citing computational stylometry results without acknowledging corpus bias, tagset mismatch, or the need for ethnographic triangulation.
  • Applying postcolonial theory uncritically, overlooking the ways native critics have reshaped imported concepts for Santali contexts.
  • Assuming the digital archive provides a complete, neutral record of oral tradition, neglecting issues of access, consent, and metadata provenance.
  • Confusing the 1952 Akademi orthographic conference with later Unicode standardisation, thereby misdating the resolution of script ambiguities.

Intertextuality and the Santali Epic Tradition: The Baha and Hul Motifs

The Santali epic imagination is organised around two recurring narrative clusters — the Baha (the great hunt) and the Hul (the rebellion). Both motifs appear in oral epics and resurface in modern novels, poetry, and drama. Intertextual analysis suggests the Baha motif functions as a cosmological charter: the hunt of the primordial boar establishes the territorial boundaries of the Santal parha and legitimises the authority of the manjhi. The Hul motif, by contrast, encodes historical memory of the 1855‑56 Santal rebellion against zamindari and colonial exaction. In printed texts from the early twentieth century, writers deliberately re‑deploy these motifs to negotiate contemporary identity politics. For instance, a 1930s novel frames the hunt as a metaphor for the search for linguistic autonomy, while a 1970s play stages the rebellion as a critique of post‑independence land alienation.

Close reading reveals a systematic pattern of motif inversion. In oral performance the Baha ends with the communal feast that restores social equilibrium; in modern prose the feast is often deferred or disrupted, signalling unresolved tension between tradition and state‑driven development. Similarly, the Hul’s oral closure — the martyr’s song — is replaced in print by an open‑ended courtroom scene, foregrounding legal discourse over ritual lament. This shift mirrors the transition from a ritualised oral public sphere to a print‑mediated literary public sphere where the audience includes non‑Santali administrators and scholars. Candidates should be able to trace a single motif across at least three texts (one oral transcription, one early print novel, one post‑1970 poem) and explain how each re‑contextualisation alters the motif’s ideological load.

Methodological Note

When mapping intertextual links, use the motif‑index compiled by the Santali Digital Corpus (SDC) which tags occurrences of “baha”, “hul”, “manjhi”, and “bonga” with contextual metadata (performance date, recorder, dialect). Cross‑referencing the index with standard printed bibliographies prevents the common error of attributing a printed variation to an oral source that never contained it.

Narrative Voice and Focalization in Modern Santali Short Fiction

The short story emerged in Santali literature during the 1950s through journals such as Hul and Sarna. Unlike the oral tale, which relies on a communal narrator who shifts fluidly between characters, the printed short story experiments with fixed focalisation. Early practitioners adopted a third‑person limited perspective anchored in a single protagonist, often a young migrant labourer. This technique mirrors the influence of Bengali and Hindi modernist short fiction that was circulating in the same literary circles.

By the 1980s a second wave introduced free indirect discourse blended with Santali ideophones (dhul, lagre) to render interiority without abandoning oral texture. In a representative 1980s story the narrator slips into the protagonist’s sensory field using ideophones as focalisation markers, signalling moments when the narrative voice aligns with the character’s perception. Later writers push further, employing multiple focalisers within a single story to juxtapose village and urban viewpoints, thereby exposing the fracture caused by migration.

Examination answers should demonstrate the ability to identify the focalisation type (zero, internal, external) in a given passage, cite the specific linguistic cues (pronoun choice, verb aspect, ideophone density), and relate the choice to the story’s thematic concern — e.g., internal focalisation to convey alienation, external focalisation to preserve communal ethos. A comparative paragraph contrasting a 1950s story with a 1990s story, using the same thematic core (land loss), earns high marks for showing diachronic development of narrative technique.

Technique: Focalisation Mapping
Given the opening paragraph of a 1950s Santali short story and a 1990s story, identify the focalisation type in each and explain how the shift reflects changing sociolinguistic conditions.
In the 1950s passage the narrator uses third‑person pronouns and past‑perfect verb forms without ideophones; the perspective remains external, reporting actions from a distance — characteristic of zero focalisation. This mirrors the period’s editorial preference for an “objective” literary language modelled on regional realism. In the 1990s passage the narrator switches to present‑tense verbs, inserts ideophones aligned with the protagonist’s heartbeat, and employs free indirect discourse. These markers signal internal focalisation, granting direct access to the migrant’s sensory experience. The shift corresponds to the spread of Santali‑medium education and the influence of post‑colonial theory, which encouraged writers to centre subaltern subjectivity rather than conform to an external literary standard.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “narrative technique”, first label the focalisation (zero, internal, external, multiple). Then quote the smallest linguistic evidence (pronoun, tense, ideophone). Finally, link the technique to a concrete historical factor (journal policy, education spread, theoretical import). This three‑step template works for both explanation and essay questions.

Print Culture and the Transformation of Santali Poetic Forms (1920‑1970)

The arrival of the Ol Chiki script in 1925 and the establishment of a Santali press created a material substrate for poetic experimentation. Early printed poetry retained the oral dhul metre (four‑beat lines with a caesura after the second beat) but introduced line‑breaks and stanza divisions that had no counterpart in performance. This visual restructuring forced poets to conceive of the poem as a spatial object, leading to the emergence of fixed‑form poems such as the chauka (four‑line stanza) and the ashtaka (eight‑line stanza) modelled on Bengali payar and Hindi chaupai.

Simultaneously, missionary presses published hymnals that imposed Christian doctrinal content onto traditional metres. The resulting hybrid hymns preserve the lagre refrain but replace indigenous bonga names with biblical equivalents. This substitution illustrates how print mediated a semantic shift without altering the prosodic skeleton. By the 1950s, literary academies began sponsoring poetry competitions that rewarded “modern” imagery (industrial labour, railway, school) while still requiring adherence to dhul or lagre. Poets responded by expanding the lexical repertoire — coining compounds that fused traditional metres with new referents — thereby demonstrating that metre can accommodate neologisms without loss of rhythmic integrity.

For Paper II, a typical essay prompt asks candidates to evaluate whether print “standardised” or “ossified” Santali poetry. A balanced answer cites institutional records (which record debates on “metre flexibility”) and contrasts the pre‑print oral corpus (high variability in beat count) with the post‑1950 printed corpus (predominantly fixed four‑beat lines). The conclusion should acknowledge that standardisation enabled wider dissemination and pedagogical use, yet also marginalised regional dialectal metres such as the three‑beat forms found in some sub‑communities.

Santali Literary Journals and the Formation of a Public Sphere

Periodicals have been the primary venue for Santali literary debate since the launch of journals by the Santali Sahitya Akademi and university departments from the 1970s onward. These journals performed three interlocking functions: (1) they codified orthographic norms by publishing editorial style‑sheets that resolved ambiguities left by earlier script conferences; (2) they created a review culture — critical essays on new novels, poetry collections, and translations — that established a hierarchy of “canonical” authors; (3) they provided a forum for theoretical import, publishing translated excerpts of Subaltern Studies, postcolonial theory, and feminist criticism in Santali, thereby localising global critical vocabularies.

Editorial policies reveal the power dynamics of the public sphere. One major journal maintained a strict “Ol Chiki only” rule, rejecting submissions in Devanagari or Roman scripts, which reinforced script nationalism. Another accepted bilingual pieces, reflecting its base in regional movements where code‑switching was a political act. A third introduced a peer‑review system modelled on national journals, signalling an aspiration toward academic legitimacy. Candidates should be able to cite at least two editorials and explain how the journal’s material conditions (print run, distribution network, funding source) shaped the literary discourse it hosted.

A comparative table of key journals — circulation trends, language policy, and dominant critical orientation — is a high‑yield revision tool. Note that circulation data are drawn from Akademi annual reports and should be cited as “Akademi reports” rather than exact numbers, which vary across sources.

Comparative Munda Literatures: Santali, Mundari, and Ho in Dialogue

Santali does not exist in isolation; it shares a linguistic sub‑family (North Munda) and a cultural landscape with Mundari and Ho. Comparative literary study reveals both convergent and divergent trajectories. All three traditions possess a corpus of karma (ritual songs) performed during the Sarhul festival, yet the metrical templates differ: Santali favours the four‑beat dhul, Mundari employs a five‑beat lagre variant, and Ho uses a flexible three‑beat turi. The thematic overlap — invocation of Singbonga (supreme deity), agricultural cycles, ancestor veneration — allows a comparative reading of how each community negotiates the same ecological calendar through distinct prosodic lenses.

In the print era, the three literatures responded differently to missionary and state interventions. Mundari adopted the Roman script early under mission influence, producing a body of hymn literature that later influenced Mundari secular poetry. Ho, lacking a dedicated script until the mid‑twentieth century (when the Warang Chiti script was revived), relied on oral transmission longer, resulting in a richer oral archive but a thinner printed canon. Santali’s unique position — an indigenous script created by a community member — gave it a symbolic autonomy that Mundari and Ho lacked, shaping the tone of their respective literary manifestos. A 1978 Munda Literary Conference brought representatives of all three languages together; proceedings record a debate on “script politics” where Santali delegates argued for script‑based standardisation while Mundari and Ho delegates emphasised oral continuity.

For examination purposes, a comparative essay should structure the answer around three axes: (i) prosodic system, (ii) script history and its impact on canon formation, (iii) institutional support (Akademi, state academies, university departments). Cite standard secondary syntheses that collate primary texts from all three languages. Avoid the trap of treating the three literatures as a monolith; the examiner rewards precise differentiation of metrical terminology and script genealogy.

  • Confusing the dhul metre with the lagre refrain across languages — each has a distinct beat pattern and cultural association.
  • Attributing early orthographic resolutions to later Unicode inclusion — they are separate events with different stakeholders.
  • Assuming all Santali journals followed a single editorial policy; major journals had divergent script and language rules.
  • Overlooking the role of missionary hymnals in shaping early printed poetic vocabulary, leading to an incomplete account of lexical borrowing.
  • Treating focalisation shifts as purely aesthetic choices without linking them to sociolinguistic developments such as the spread of Santali‑medium schooling.
  • Neglecting the comparative dimension: writing about Santali poetry in isolation when the question explicitly asks for a Munda‑wide perspective.
  • Citing circulation figures as exact numbers without referencing the Akademi annual reports, which are the only authoritative source.

Santali Drama: From Traditional Jatra to Modern Stagecraft

The dramatic tradition of the Santal community originates in the jatra — open‑air performances that combine song, dance, and dialogue to enact mythic episodes such as the Baha (flower festival) and the Hul (rebellion). Early jatra troupes, noted by anthropologists including W. G. Archer in the early twentieth century, relied on a repertoire of karma songs and a narrator‑chorus structure that mirrors the oral epic form. The transition to scripted theatre gained momentum after the Santali Sahitya Akademi’s establishment (1990), which encouraged playwrights to adapt folk narratives for educational and stage use. Early scripts introduced a three‑act structure, stage directions, and a prose dialogue register distinct from the sung verses of jatra.

From the 1970s onward, institutional platforms such as the Santali Natya Parishad enabled experimental drama. Playwrights incorporated Brechtian alienation techniques while retaining the communal chorus. Representative works juxtapose the 1855 Santal rebellion with contemporary land‑rights struggles, using split staging to contrast archival projections with live oral testimony. Reception at regional theatre festivals demonstrated that modern stagecraft could accommodate indigenous aesthetics without diluting political urgency.

Contemporary Santali theatre operates in three overlapping circuits: (i) community‑based jatra troupes that tour villages during Sarhul and Sohrai; (ii) Akademi‑sponsored productions staged in Ol Chiki‑medium schools and at the annual Santali Natya Utsav; (iii) independent collectives that experiment with multimedia, projecting archival photographs onto backdrops while actors deliver lines in a hybrid register of Ol Chiki, Hindi, and English. Funding patterns reveal a shift: central and state tribal‑culture grants now cover a significant share of production costs for Akademi‑affiliated groups, whereas village troupes rely on community contributions and occasional NGO support.

Critical scholarship on Santali drama remains limited. The most cited monograph, Santali Theatre: Tradition and Modernity, argues that the persistence of the chorus functions as a “collective focaliser” that resists the individuation typical of Western realist drama. Examiners expect candidates to compare this collective focalisation with narrative voice shifts in modern short fiction, and to evaluate how script‑based standardisation (Ol Chiki) both enables and constrains theatrical innovation.

Linguistic Purism vs. Lexical Borrowing in Ol Chiki Standardisation

The Ol Chiki script, devised by Pandit Raghunath Murmu in 1925, was conceived as a phonologically transparent writing system for Santali. From its inception, two competing ideologies shaped lexical development: purist advocates, associated with the Santali Bhasha Parishad, insisted on coining neologisms from existing Santali roots (e.g., “horo‑dak” for “telephone”, literally “voice‑wire”), while pragmatist scholars, many affiliated with missionary presses, favoured direct borrowing from Hindi, Bengali, and English (e.g., “ṭeliphon”). A mid‑twentieth‑century orthography committee report records a compromise: a core vocabulary of root‑derived terms was ratified, and a supplementary list of loanwords was approved for technical, administrative, and scientific domains.

Subsequent revisions expanded the loanword list, reflecting the growing presence of Santali in higher education and mass media. A later revision introduced a morphological adaptation rule: borrowed nouns receive the Santali nominal suffix ‑a (e.g., “computer‑a”), and verbs adopt the infinitival marker ‑en (e.g., “download‑en”). This rule preserves the agglutinative morphology of Santali while allowing rapid lexical expansion. Critics argue that the rule masks a deeper syntactic convergence with Indo‑Aryan languages, evident in the increasing use of post‑positional case markers borrowed from Hindi.

The debate resurfaced during the Unicode inclusion of Ol Chiki (2008, Unicode 5.1). Technical committees required a stable character set; purists lobbied for the exclusion of diacritic marks used only for loanword transcription (e.g., the nukta for “z” in “zila”). The final Unicode chart retained the nukta, a decision cited in script‑studies literature as a pragmatic victory for digital usability over orthographic purity. Examination questions often ask candidates to evaluate how this tension influences contemporary poetic diction, citing poems from prescribed anthologies where poets deliberately alternate root‑derived and loanword registers for ironic effect.

Technique: Comparative Lexical Analysis

Explain how each choice reflects the poet’s stance on linguistic purism and ecological discourse.
A poem employing jhar‑bonga invokes the indigenous cosmology where the forest is a living deity. This lexical choice aligns with the purist agenda and signals an ecocritical perspective rooted in Santali ontology. In contrast, a poem using environment‑a, a direct adaptation of the English term, reflects pragmatist acceptance of global environmental vocabulary. The loanword situates the poem within transnational climate‑change debates, allowing intertextual reference to international documents. An effective answer notes the morphological integration (‑a suffix) and argues that the coexistence of both strategies demonstrates a dynamic lexicon rather than a binary opposition.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “critical evaluation of lexical policy”, structure the response in three moves: (1) cite the mid‑century committee compromise between root‑coinage and borrowing, (2) trace the later morphological adaptation rule and its syntactic consequences, (3) illustrate with a poetic example from the prescribed anthology. This three‑part template matches the marking rubric’s emphasis on historical grounding, linguistic analysis, and textual evidence.

The Role of Santali Language Movement in Shaping Literary Institutions

The Santali language movement, crystallising around the demand for constitutional recognition, directly moulded the institutional architecture that now governs literary production. The Santali Bhasa Andolan in the late 1970s mobilised large numbers, culminating in the inclusion of Santali in the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution via the 92nd Amendment (2003). This legal status triggered the creation of the Santali Sahitya Akademi (1990) under the Ministry of Culture, the establishment of Santali departments at Ranchi University and Vinoba Bhave University, and the allocation of dedicated plan funds for textbook development.

Institutional decisions reflect movement priorities. The Akademi’s first constitution mandated that all official publications use Ol Chiki exclusively, a rule traced to a Script Resolution passed at an All‑India Santali Conference. The resolution also required that a significant share of Akademi grants support oral‑tradition documentation, a quota still observed in recent annual reports. University curricula, designed by committees including movement veterans, embed comparative Munda literature (Santali, Mundari, Ho) as a core paper, ensuring that the movement’s pan‑Munda solidarity translates into academic practice.

Funding streams reveal the movement’s lasting influence. The Tribal Sub‑Plan allocates substantial annual funds for Santali language development; the bulk flows to the Akademi for manuscript publication, with significant portions to university research centres and community‑level language camps. A recent state‑commissioned Santali Language Policy Review recommended expanding digital corpus projects — a direct outcome of the movement’s “Digital Santali” campaign, which collected thousands of hours of oral recordings now housed in the Santali Digital Archive at the Central Institute of Indian Languages, Mysuru.

Examiners expect candidates to link these institutional outcomes to literary themes: the prevalence of “language‑rights” motifs in post‑1990 poetry, the emergence of academic criticism written in Santali, and the shift from missionary‑controlled presses to community‑owned publishing houses. A high‑scoring answer will cite the constitutional amendment, the Akademi charter, and at least two literary texts that foreground institutional agency.

Santali Children's Literature: Pedagogical Trends and Canonical Texts

Children’s literature in Santali emerged as a distinct genre after the 1990s, when Ol Chiki‑medium primary schools expanded under the District Primary Education Programme (DPEP). The first graded reader, compiled by the Akademi’s Textbook Committee, combined traditional karma verses with illustrated prose stories about village life. Its pedagogical design follows a “whole‑language” approach: each unit presents a thematic cluster with vocabulary lists, comprehension questions, and a creative writing prompt.

Subsequent series introduced progressive complexity: from monosyllabic root words to compound sentences employing the loan‑word morphology. Later series align with the National Curriculum Framework (2005) by integrating environmental studies, gender‑sensitive narratives, and multilingual awareness (Santali‑Hindi‑English parallel texts). Notably, one series includes a chapter on the 1855 rebellion written from a child’s perspective, a narrative strategy that mirrors the focalisation shifts discussed in modern short fiction.

Critical reception has been monitored through the Akademi’s biennial “Children’s Literature Review.” Reviews have highlighted two tensions: (i) the pressure to sanitise folk tales — removing references to spirit possession — to meet state textbook guidelines; (ii) the demand from parents for more “modern” stories featuring urban settings and technology. In response, a dedicated committee commissioned original works that blend coding terminology (loanwords) with traditional trickster motifs.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to trace the evolution from oral‑derived readers to curriculum‑aligned textbooks, cite representative titles with publication periods, and discuss how pedagogical policy (DPEP, NCF) shapes lexical choices and thematic content. A comparative note on the parallel development of Mundari and Ho children’s series (both delayed by script standardisation timelines) strengthens the answer.

Santali Literary Awards and Their Impact on Canon Formation

Formal literary awards constitute a relatively recent but decisive mechanism for canon formation in Santali. The Santali Sahitya Akademi Award (instituted 1995) and the Bhasha Samman (Sahitya Akademi, New Delhi, extended to Santali 2004) are the two most prestigious honours. Both require the work to be published in Ol Chiki within the preceding three years.

Analysis of award winners reveals patterns: poetry dominates, followed by short fiction and criticism. The first recipient, Pandit Raghunath Murmu (posthumous), for a linguistic scholarship, set a precedent for recognising language work. Subsequent winners cemented the status of drama and ecocritical poetry within the canon. A later Bhasha Samman for a work of vernacular literary theory signalled institutional validation of academic criticism in Santali.

Award juries, composed of senior Akademi members and university professors, publish brief “selection reports” in the Akademi’s annual bulletin. These reports explicitly reference criteria: (i) linguistic purity (adherence to established morphological norms), (ii) thematic relevance to Santali socio‑cultural concerns, (iii) formal innovation (e.g., use of free verse, intertextual allusion to Baha motifs). Recent reports note a shift toward “trans‑generic works” that blend poetry, essay, and oral testimony.

Critics argue that the award system reinforces a metropolitan bias: a disproportionate share of winners are based in urban centres, and women remain under‑represented. A women writers’ collective has petitioned the Akademi to introduce a dedicated category; the proposal is under review. Examination questions may ask candidates to evaluate how award structures shape thematic priorities, citing specific winners and selection‑report language.
