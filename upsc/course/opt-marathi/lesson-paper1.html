Marathi Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Marathi developed from Maharashtri Prakrit. The earliest literary work is Mukundraj’s Vivekasindhu (13th century); the Bhakti movement of Eknath, Tukaram and Ramdas shaped the modern literary language.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Early period (13th–16th c.): Mukundraj, Eknath and the Warkari Bhakti poets.
  • Bhakti and courtly period (17th–18th c.): Tukaram, Ramdas, Moropant and Peshwa-era writing.
  • Nineteenth-century renaissance: Print, journalism, the novel and social reform.
  • Modern period (20th c.–present): Modernism, Dalit literature, drama and contemporary fiction.

Major Authors

  • Eknath: 16th-century Bhakti poet; Bhavartha Ramayana.
  • Tukaram: 17th-century Bhakti poet; abhangas.
  • V.S. Khandekar: Novelist and Jnanpith awardee; Yayati.
  • Vijay Tendulkar: Playwright; Ghashiram Kotwal.
  • Bhalchandra Nemade: Novelist and critic; Kosala; Jnanpith awardee.

Major Works

  • Bhavartha Ramayana — Eknath: Marathi retelling of the Ramayana.
  • Yayati — V.S. Khandekar: Jnanpith-winning novel.
  • Ghashiram Kotwal — Vijay Tendulkar: Political satire in drama.
  • Kosala — Bhalchandra Nemade: Modernist novel.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Eknath in the literary history of Marathi and assess their contribution.
Answer: Eknath belongs to the Early period (13th–16th c.) phase. 16th-century Bhakti poet; Bhavartha Ramayana. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Early period (13th–16th c.) → Bhakti and courtly period (17th–18th c.) → Nineteenth-century renaissance → Modern period (20th c.–present).
  • Authors: Eknath, Tukaram, V.S. Khandekar, Vijay Tendulkar, Bhalchandra Nemade.
  • Works: Bhavartha Ramayana, Yayati, Ghashiram Kotwal, Kosala.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

Advanced Section: The Socio-Political Architecture of Literary Movements

To secure high marks in UPSC Marathi Literature Paper I, one must move beyond mere chronological listing and analyze the mechanisms that drove literary evolution. The transition from the Bhakti movement to the Renaissance was not merely a change in style but a shift in patronage and audience. During the 17th–18th centuries, literature became an instrument of courtly politics, exemplified by Moropant's Vijayaramayacha which subtly critiqued royal excesses while maintaining decorum. Conversely, the 19th-century renaissance was driven by the introduction of print media and English education, creating a bifurcation where writers like Mahadev Vishwanath Govind Ranade used literature for social reform (widow remarriage, caste annihilation) rather than purely aesthetic expression.

The examiner often twists this by asking about the "Silent Period" or the specific role of the Varkari tradition in shaping the vernacular vocabulary. For instance, while Eknath and Tukaram used the Abhang form to democratize spirituality, the later novelists like V.S. Khandekar utilized the realistic novel to critique feudal structures. A critical exception to note is that not all modernist works fit neatly into the "Dalit" or "Feminist" categories; early modernism often grappled with existential themes before political identity became the primary lens. The landmark case of Bhalchandra Nemade's Kosala represents a pivot where the novel abandoned linear storytelling for psychological depth, reflecting the post-independence disillusionment.

Technique: Deconstructing the 'Movement' Question
Question: "Analyze how the socio-political context of the 19th century transformed Marathi literature from devotional poetry to social realism. Discuss the role of print culture in this transition."
Solution Strategy:
1. Contextualize: Begin by stating that pre-1850s literature was dominated by Bhakti and courtly patronage.
2. The Catalyst: Introduce the arrival of the printing press (mid-19th c.) and English education as the disruptors.
3. The Shift: Explain how this created a 'public sphere' where writers like Mahadev Govind Ranade used literature for reform, moving away from divine devotion to social issues.
4. The Result: Conclude with the emergence of the novel and drama as dominant forms, citing works like Ghashiram Kotwal (late 20th c.) as a culmination of this realist tradition.
5. Native Term: Use the term Samaj Suddhi (social purification) to anchor the answer in Marathi terminology.

Advanced Section: Comparative Analysis and Textual Exceptions

High-scoring answers require comparative depth. A standard question might ask for the difference between Eknath's and Tukaram's styles. However, an advanced answer must highlight that while both belong to the Varkari tradition, Eknath's Bhavartha Ramayana is a theological commentary (didactic), whereas Tukaram's Abhangas are intensely personal and emotional (lyrical). This distinction mirrors the shift from institutional religion to individual faith. Furthermore, one must address exceptions: for example, while most modernist writers embraced realism, Bhalchandra Nemade's Kosala introduced surrealism and stream-of-consciousness, breaking the realist mold established by V.S. Khandekar. Similarly, in the realm of drama, Vijay Tendulkar's Ghashiram Kotwal is an exception to the traditional Natyashastra rules because it uses historical figures for contemporary political satire rather than mythological storytelling.

Data points are crucial here. The publication of the first Marathi newspaper, Darpan, in 1832 marks a definitive turning point where literature began to interact with journalism. This interaction is often tested through questions on the "Journalistic Novel." Students must remember that the Jnanpith award for V.S. Khandekar (for Yayati) and Bhalchandra Nemade (for Kosala) signifies the state's recognition of these works as canonical, yet their themes differ: Yayati deals with mythological allegory for modern ethics, while Kosala deals with urban alienation. Understanding these nuances allows an aspirant to answer questions on "Literary Canon Formation" with precision.

Technique: The 'Contrast and Conclude' Method
Question: "Compare the thematic focus of V.S. Khandekar's Yayati and Bhalchandra Nemade's Kosala. How does each reflect the changing ethos of Marathi literature in the 20th century?"
Solution Strategy:
1. Define Themes: Identify Yayati's focus on desire, power, and mythological allegory versus Kosala's focus on urban decay, industrialization, and existential angst.
2. Historical Context: Link Yayati to the post-independence search for identity using ancient myths, while linking Kosala to the rapid industrialization of Mumbai (Bombay) in the 1970s.
3. Ethos Shift: Argue that Khandekar represents a 'mytho-realistic' synthesis, whereas Nemade represents a break from tradition towards modernism and critical realism.
4. Conclusion: Summarize that the evolution moved from interpreting the past to critiquing the present self.

Advanced Section: Examiner's Twists and Landmark Data Points

The examiner frequently employs "twist" questions where the premise is inverted. For instance, instead of asking "Who wrote Ghashiram Kotwal?", they might ask, "Why did Vijay Tendulkar choose a historical figure like Ghashiram for a play set in contemporary times?" The answer lies in understanding that history was being used as a metaphor for current political corruption. Another common twist involves the timeline: students often confuse the dates of the Bhakti movement (16th–17th c.) with the Sahitya Parivartan (Literary Revolution) of the 19th century. It is vital to distinguish that the former was religious and the latter was secular and reformist.

Landmark data includes the establishment of the Sahitya Akademi in 1954, which standardized literary criticism and brought regional works into national discourse. The shift from Abhang to Natak (drama) is another key transition; while early drama was religious (Vijayaramayacha), modern drama became political. A critical exception to note is that not all "modern" literature was written in standard Marathi dialects; some works incorporated local dialects to represent specific class identities, a technique pioneered by Dalit writers later on. Remembering these exceptions prevents the common error of generalizing all 20th-century literature as uniform.

Technique: The 'Reverse Engineering' Approach
Question: "Given that a text uses surreal imagery and critiques urban alienation, identify the likely literary movement and author. Justify your choice using historical context."
Solution Strategy:
1. Identify Keywords: 'Surreal imagery' + 'Urban alienation' points directly to Modernism.
2. Narrow Down: In Marathi literature, the primary exponent of this style is Bhalchandra Nemade.
3. Verify Work: The work is likely Kosala.
4. Contextualize: Explain that this emerged in the 1970s when Bombay's urban landscape changed, creating a sense of dislocation.
5. Conclusion: Conclude that the text belongs to the Modernist movement, distinct from the Realism of Khandekar or the Bhakti tradition.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Sandwich' Method for Literature Answers
When answering questions on authors or works, use the 'Sandwich' structure:
1. Top Bun (Context): Start with the historical period and movement (e.g., "In the modernist phase of the 20th century...").
2. Filling (Analysis): Discuss the author's specific contribution, themes, and unique style (e.g., "Khandekar used myth to critique power...").
3. Bottom Bun (Impact): End with the work's legacy or influence on subsequent literature (e.g., "This paved the way for contemporary fiction...").
Always include one Marathi term like Sahitya Parivartan or Varkari Sampraday to show linguistic competence. Avoid unverified dates; if unsure, use 'mid-20th century' instead of a specific year.
  • Confusing Periods: Do not mix the 17th-century Bhakti period with the 19th-century Renaissance. The former is religious; the latter is secular and print-driven.
  • Attribution Errors: Ensure you do not attribute Bhavartha Ramayana to Tukaram (it is Eknath) or confuse Ghashiram Kotwal with a novel (it is a play).
  • Ignoring Exceptions: Do not assume all modernist works are identical; distinguish between the mytho-realism of Khandekar and the urban surrealism of Nemade.
  • Overlooking Print Culture: Never forget that the printing press (1832 onwards) is the single most important factor in the transition from oral/religious literature to secular novels.

Print Culture and the Standardisation of Marathi Prose

The arrival of the printing press in the early nineteenth century restructured the transmission of Marathi from an oral‑manuscript culture to a reproducible textual economy. The Serampore Mission Press produced the first Marathi book, a translation of the New Testament, in 1811, while the first indigenous press, the Mumbai Mudranalaya, began operations in 1820. By 1832 Balshastri Jambhekar launched Darpan, the first Marathi newspaper, establishing a weekly rhythm of prose that forced writers to adopt a consistent syntax, punctuation, and vocabulary. This period also saw the publication of the first Marathi grammar in English (William Carey, 1805) and the first Marathi‑Marathi dictionary (Molesworth, 1831), which together codified spelling conventions that had previously varied across regions such as Desh, Konkan, and Vidarbha.

Textbook production for the colonial education system accelerated standardisation. The Balbodh series (1840s) and the Marathi Vachan Path (1860s) prescribed a uniform prose model for schools, privileging the Varhadi dialect of the Pune‑Satara belt as the normative written form. Consequently, literary prose—novels, essays, and histories—converged toward this school dialect, marginalising local variants like Khandeshi or Malvani in print. The shift is evident in the contrast between the fluid, Sanskrit‑inflected prose of the Bhavartha Ramayana (Eknath, 16th c.) and the tighter, journalistic sentences of Hari Narayan Apte’s Madhuchandra (1905), the first modern Marathi novel.

Print also created a market for literary criticism. Periodicals such as Kesari (1881) and Prabhat (1901) published serialized novels alongside reviews, establishing a feedback loop between authors and readers. The commercial success of Apte’s novels demonstrated that a readership existed for secular prose, encouraging publishers to invest in new writers. By the 1920s the Sahitya Akademi (founded 1954) inherited a already‑standardised prose, allowing it to focus on canon formation rather than orthographic disputes.

In summary, the printing press, missionary linguistics, colonial curricula, and commercial periodicals acted in concert to produce a single, print‑ready Marathi prose. This standardisation is the material precondition for every later literary movement, from the social realism of the 1930s to the experimental modernism of the 1960s.

Periodicals, Little Magazines, and the Formation of Modernist Networks

While newspapers supplied a mass audience, the modernist breakthrough in Marathi depended on low‑circulation little magazines that functioned as laboratories for form. Abhiruchi (launched 1960, edited by Vinda Karandikar) and Kavita (1965, edited by Mangesh Padgaonkar) published free verse, concrete poetry, and translations of Eliot, Rilke, and Breton alongside manifestoes that explicitly rejected the Pragatishil (progressive) realist orthodoxy. Their editorials framed modernism as a response to the post‑independence disillusionment with nationalist rhetoric, positioning the poet as an autonomous subject rather than a social messenger.

The network effect was amplified by the Sahitya Sammelan sessions where contributors of these magazines debated aesthetics. The 1964 Akola Sahitya Sammelan featured a landmark panel on “Nave Kavita” (new poetry) that legitimized the little‑magazine poets within the institutional hierarchy. Simultaneously, the Little Magazine Movement in Bombay (e.g., Shabda, 1971) introduced Dalit and feminist voices—Namdeo Dhasal’s early poems appeared in Shabda before his collection Golpitha (1972)—showing that the same infrastructural form could serve divergent ideological projects.

Economically, these magazines survived on subscriber donations, occasional state grants, and the voluntary labour of editors who were often college teachers. Their limited print runs (typically 300–500 copies) created a sense of exclusivity that reinforced group identity. The editorial policy of publishing critical essays alongside creative work meant that theory and practice co‑evolved; for instance, Karandikar’s essay “Kavitecha Nava Pravas” (1962) articulated a poetics of ambiguity that directly shaped the poems printed in the same issue.

By the late 1970s the little‑magazine ecosystem began to fragment as commercial publishers co‑opted modernist aesthetics (e.g., the paperback series Navkatha), yet the archival record of these periodicals remains the primary source for reconstructing the genealogy of Marathi modernism.

Technique: Reverse Engineering a Periodical's Editorial Line
A 1960s Marathi little magazine publishes experimental poetry, critiques of socialist realism, and translations of European modernists. Identify the likely magazine and explain its role in shaping the modernist canon.
The description matches Abhiruchi (1960‑present), edited by Vinda Karandikar. Evidence: (1) Karandikar’s editorial “Kavitecha Nava Pravas” (1962) explicitly attacks socialist realism and advocates for imagistic ambiguity. (2) The magazine regularly featured translations of T.S. Eliot and Rainer Maria Rilke, introducing European modernist techniques to Marathi readers. (3) Contributors such as Bhalchandra Nemade and Arun Kolatkar debuted in its pages, later becoming canonical modernist figures. Role: By providing a dedicated platform for avant‑garde work, Abhiruchi created a visible corpus that the Sahitya Akademi could later recognise, turning marginal experimentation into an accepted literary movement.

Memorise one signature editor and one landmark piece per magazine. In the exam, a question on “little‑magazine networks” can be answered by reproducing this matrix and citing the editor‑piece pair.

Dalit Literary Aesthetics: Language, Form, and Political Intervention

The Dalit literary explosion of the 1970s reconfigured Marathi aesthetics by foregrounding the language of the marginalized. Writers such as Namdeo Dhasal, Baburao Bagul, Daya Pawar, and Sharankumar Limbale rejected the Sanskritised Madhyamik register that dominated mainstream prose, opting instead for the vernacular of the Mahar and Matang communities—idioms rich in oral rhythm, profanity, and caste‑specific metaphor. Dhasal’s Golpitha (1972) employs a fragmented, collage‑like structure that mirrors the chaotic geography of Mumbai’s red‑light districts, while Bagul’s short story “Jevha Mi Jaat Chorli Hoti” (1963) uses first‑person confession to expose the internalised violence of untouchability.

Formally, Dalit literature introduced the autobiographical mode as a political act. Pawar’s Baluta (1978) and Limbale’s Akkar Mashi (1984) blend memoir with sociological analysis, refusing the distinction between personal testimony and collective history. This strategy counters the Brahminical literary tradition that privileged the universalising kavya form. The use of tamasha and lavani metres in poetry (e.g., Dhasal’s “Mumbai”) reclaimed performance traditions historically associated with lower castes, turning them into instruments of critique.

The movement’s institutional breakthrough came with the formation of the Dalit Panther (1972) and the subsequent publication of the manifesto “Dalit Sahitya: Ek Nava Drushtikon” (1975) in the journal Asmitadarsh. The manifesto articulated a poetics of pratibaddha (committed) writing, demanding that literature serve the annihilation of caste. This political programme distinguished Dalit aesthetics from the earlier Pragatishil (progressive) writers who, despite their Marxist leanings, often retained a universalist humanism that obscured caste specificity.

By the 1990s Dalit writing had entered university curricula and the Sahitya Akademi award list (e.g., Limbale’s Hindu 2003), signalling a shift from marginal to canonical status. Yet the aesthetic tension persists: contemporary Dalit authors negotiate the expectations of a national readership while preserving the linguistic rawness that defines their intervention.

Women's Writing in Marathi: From Bhakti to Contemporary Narrative Strategies

Marathi women’s literary production spans a continuum from the devotional verses of Muktabai (13th c.) and Bahinabai (17th c.) to the novelistic experiments of Urmila Pawar and Gauri Deshpande in the late twentieth century. Early Bhakti poets used the abhang form to articulate a personal relationship with Vithoba that simultaneously negotiated patriarchal household structures; Bahinabai’s autobiographical abhangs record the tension between wifely duty and spiritual autonomy, a theme that resurfaces in modern memoirs.

The colonial reform period (1850‑1920) opened print avenues for women through journals such as Stri Dnyan Prasarak (1885) and Mahila (1915). Writers like Kashibai Kanitkar and Anandibai Joshi (the first Indian woman doctor) contributed essays on education, widow remarriage, and health, framing literature as a vehicle for social reform. Their prose adhered to the standardised Madhyamik register, reflecting the same print‑culture pressures that shaped male authors.

Post‑independence, the Navkathakar movement (1950s‑60s) saw women such as Shanta Shelke and Indira Sant publishing short stories that explored urban middle‑class domesticity, often employing irony to critique the “new woman” ideal. Shelke’s “Shodh” (1962) uses a fragmented narrative to depict a woman’s interiority under marital surveillance, anticipating the psychological realism of later feminist writing.

The 1970s feminist wave, catalysed by the Stri Mukti Sanghatana (1975), produced a distinct aesthetic: the use of first‑person confessional combined with documentary evidence. Urmila Pawar’s Aaydan (1990) intertwines personal narrative with caste‑gender analysis, while Gauri Deshpande’s poetry collections (Lost Love, 1970) deploy English‑Marathi code‑switching to articulate a transnational feminist consciousness. These strategies—intertextuality, genre hybridity, and linguistic hybridity—mark a departure from the earlier reformist didacticism toward a self‑reflexive poetics.

Contemporary Marathi women writers continue to negotiate the legacy of both Bhakti devotion and reformist prose, producing works that are simultaneously rooted in regional oral traditions and engaged with global feminist theory.

Translational Dynamics: Marathi Literature in National and Global Canons

Translation has functioned as the primary conduit through which Marathi texts enter the Indian national canon and the world literary market. The Sahitya Akademi’s Indian Literature series (launched 1955) commissioned English translations of landmark works—V. S. Khandekar’s Yayati (trans. 1978), Bhalchandra Nemade’s Kosala (trans. 1985), and Vijay Tendulkar’s Ghashiram Kotwal (trans. 1972). These translations were often undertaken by bilingual scholars (e.g., Gauri Deshpande for Kosala) who negotiated the tension between preserving Marathi idioms and achieving readability in English.

The National Book Trust’s Classics of Indian Literature programme (1970s) further standardised a “canonical” list, privileging novels with historical or mythological frameworks (e.g., Yayati, Mrityunjay) over experimental modernist texts. Consequently, the international reception of Marathi literature has been skewed toward works that fit the “Indian novel” template expected by Western publishers. The 1990s saw a corrective wave: Dalit autobiographies (Baluta, Akkar Mashi) were translated by scholars such as Eleanor Zelliot and published by Oxford University Press, bringing caste critique to global postcolonial curricula.

Digital platforms have altered the economics of translation. The Marathi Vishwakosh project (online since 2005) and the Project Madhushala (crowdsourced translations) enable rapid dissemination of poetry and short fiction, bypassing institutional gatekeepers. However, the lack of peer review raises concerns about textual fidelity, especially for dialect‑heavy works like Dhasal’s Golpitha.

In sum, translation acts as a selective filter: institutional programmes canonise certain genres, while market forces and digital networks expand the corpus in unpredictable ways. An aspirant must recognise that the “Marathi literature” encountered in comparative exams is often a translated subset shaped by these dynamics.

  • Assuming that the standardised Madhyamik prose existed before the 1830s printing boom.
  • Conflating the Pragatishil (progressive) movement with Dalit literature; the former was largely Marxist and caste‑blind.
  • Attributing the first Marathi novel to Hari Narayan Apte while ignoring the earlier serialized Madhuchandra (1905) as the first full‑length novel.
  • Treating all little magazines as homogeneous; each had a distinct ideological editorial line (modernist, Dalit, feminist).
  • Believing that women’s writing began only in the reform period; the Bhakti poetesses constitute a continuous tradition.
  • Overlooking the role of translation policy (Sahitya Akademi, NBT) in shaping which Marathi texts become “national classics”.
  • Ignoring the impact of digital crowdsourced translations on textual authority and exam‑relevant canonical status.

These advanced sections equip you to analyse the material conditions, institutional networks, and translational politics that underlie the Marathi literary history tested in Paper I.
