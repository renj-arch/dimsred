Marathi Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism, Explanation and Essays

Prescribed Texts and Close Reading

  • Bhavartha Ramayana — Eknath: Marathi retelling of the Ramayana.
  • Yayati — V.S. Khandekar: Jnanpith-winning novel.
  • Ghashiram Kotwal — Vijay Tendulkar: Political satire in drama.
  • Kosala — Bhalchandra Nemade: Modernist novel.

Literary Criticism

traditional rasa criticism, nationalist and Marxist criticism, modernist, Dalit and feminist approaches

Explanation (Vyakhya) Strategy

  • Context: identify the text, author, period and speaker.
  • Literal meaning: paraphrase the passage in simple Marathi or English.
  • Theme: state the central idea and the emotional tone.
  • Form: comment on metre, imagery, diction and figures of speech.
  • Evaluation: link the passage to the author’s larger concerns and the period.

Essay Questions

  • Define the literary issue in the question, then take a clear position.
  • Support the argument with at least three texts or authors.
  • Include one counter-argument and respond to it.
  • Conclude with the significance of the issue for Marathi literature.
Solved Example: Explanation
Question: Explain a prescribed passage from Bhavartha Ramayana.
Answer: Identify the speaker and situation, paraphrase the lines, state the theme, comment on imagery and form, and connect the passage to the work’s central concern. A good explanation moves from literal meaning to literary evaluation.

Common Traps

  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing the passage.
  • Using a translation without naming the translator.
  • Ignoring the prescribed text and writing general remarks.
  • Mixing authors and works from different periods.
  • Making claims about influence without evidence.
Mains strategy
For explanation questions, use the five-step structure: context → literal meaning → theme → form → evaluation. For essay questions, write a thesis statement in the first paragraph, use three body paragraphs with textual evidence, and end with a balanced conclusion. Keep quotations short and accurate.

Advanced Textual Dynamics: The Eknath-Bharat Dialogue as Critical Pivot

The examination board frequently tests the candidate's ability to distinguish between a mere summary and a critical engagement with the text. In Marathi Literature Paper II, the most sophisticated questions do not ask "What is the message of Bhavartha Ramayana?" but rather "How does Eknath's dialogue with Bharat reveal his own theological anxieties?" This requires moving beyond surface-level piety to analyze the dialogic structure of the text. The examiner often twists the concept by presenting a passage where Eknath seems to contradict standard Vaishnavite dogma, asking the candidate to identify if this is a rhetorical device or a genuine theological evolution.

Technique: Dialogic Analysis
Question: "Critically examine how Eknath uses the character of Bharat to externalize his own internal conflict regarding the nature of God in Bhavartha Ramayana."
Solution:

First, identify that Bharat is not just a protagonist but a mouthpiece for traditional orthodoxy. In passages where Eknath questions the efficacy of rituals, he often does so through Bharat's confusion or doubt.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Why' of the Quote
When quoting from prescribed texts like Yayati or Ghashiram Kotwal, never quote a line in isolation. Always preface your quote with the specific dramatic tension or narrative shift it represents. For instance, before quoting a line from Kosala, explicitly state whether it marks a transition from realism to modernist introspection. This contextual framing demonstrates higher-order thinking and prevents generic answers.

Critical Synthesis: Mapping Marathi Literary Movements Against National Discourse

Essay questions in Paper II often require synthesizing disparate literary movements. A common advanced trap is asking candidates to compare the "Nationalist" approach of early 20th-century writers with the "Marxist" critique of the mid-century, but specifically focusing on how they treated the peasant. Textbook facts state that Nationalists idealized the village, while Marxists deconstructed it. The examiner twists this by asking about exceptions: Did any Nationalist writer show peasant suffering realistically? (Answer: Yes, often in later works of Khandekar or Nemade). You must be able to draw a comparative matrix showing how the representation of 'Vidarbha' shifted from a pastoral idyll in early poetry to an industrialized dystopia in modern novels.

Furthermore, students often fail to distinguish between "Dalit Literature" as a genre and "Dalit Criticism" applied to non-Dalit texts. A landmark case for analysis is how the Dalit lens re-reads traditional epics like Ramayana or Mahabharata in Marathi. You must explain that this is not just about caste politics but a fundamental restructuring of narrative authority. For example, analyze how Eknath's Bhavartha Ramayana prefigures modern Dalit critiques by subverting the divine status of Rama to focus on human suffering.

Technique: Comparative Matrix Analysis
Question: "Compare and contrast the portrayal of the 'common man' in Ghashiram Kotwal (Tendulkar) and Kosala (Nemade). How does this reflect a shift in Marathi literary criticism from Nationalist to Modernist-Marxist paradigms?"
Solution:

Start by defining the 'common man' in Tendulkar's play: he is often complicit, morally ambiguous, and trapped in feudal power structures (Ghashiram himself). In Nemade's Kosala, the protagonist is an intellectual alienated from society, representing the crisis of modernity. The shift reflects a change in criticism from celebrating rural unity (Nationalist) to analyzing internal class contradictions and individual alienation (Modernist-Marxist). Use textual evidence: Ghashiram's dialogue reveals his fear of losing status, while Kosala's monologues reveal existential dread. Conclude that this evolution shows Marathi literature moving from collective identity narratives to fragmented, psychological realism.

The Examiner's Twist: Handling Ambiguity and Counter-Narratives

A serious aspirant must prepare for questions that present a counter-narrative. For instance, the standard view is that V.S. Khandekar's Yayati is a tragedy of desire. However, an advanced question might ask: "Is Yayati's downfall inevitable due to his nature, or is it a critique of the feudal system?" You must be ready to argue both sides using textual evidence. Another twist involves the treatment of women in Marathi literature; standard texts often focus on male protagonists, but questions may ask you to analyze female characters (like the queen in Ghashiram Kotwal or characters in Nemade's works) through a feminist lens without explicitly labeling them as 'feminist' if the text doesn't support it.

Be prepared to discuss "Landmark Cases" in literary criticism where a single line changes interpretation. For example, a specific verse in Bhavartha Ramayana that seems to praise karma might be read differently when placed against Eknath's later verses on grace. The examiner expects you to notice this tension and explain it as a feature of the text's complexity, not an error.

  • Ignoring Subtext: Failing to see that a character's polite dialogue in Ghashiram Kotwal hides deep political subversion.
  • Over-Generalizing Movements: Stating "Modernist writers were pessimistic" without citing specific works like Kosala or explaining the nuance of their modernism.
  • Misinterpreting 'Criticism': Treating literary criticism as a separate subject rather than an integrated tool for analyzing texts like Yayati or Bhavartha Ramayana.
  • Missing Historical Context: Analyzing a text from the 1950s without acknowledging the post-independence socio-political climate that shaped its themes.
  • Forgetting the 'Why': Providing a detailed plot summary of a passage in an explanation question instead of analyzing the literary devices and thematic implications.

The Pashupati Seal and What It Shows

To understand the trajectory of Marathi literary criticism, one must first grasp the socio-political soil from which it sprang. The late 19th century in Maharashtra was not merely a period of linguistic standardization but a crucible of identity formation. The 'indigenous past' metaphor refers to early nationalist attempts to root literature in indigenous history and mythology, distinct from British colonial narratives. However, the critical pivot occurred when writers realized that mere celebration of the past was insufficient for social reform. This realization led to the emergence of the 'Socialist Realism' school in the 1950s, which demanded that literature address immediate material conditions rather than abstract humanism.

The landmark case here is the transition from early 20th-century social novelists to V.S. Khandekar. While early works focused on the dignity of labor through a romanticized lens on the dignity of labor through a romanticized lens, Khandekar's *Yayati* deconstructed this by presenting the feudal system not as a backdrop for heroism but as a mechanism of exploitation. The critical distinction lies in how the 'common man' is portrayed: in early texts, he is a passive victim or a saintly figure; in mid-century criticism, he becomes an active agent whose rebellion is often crushed by structural forces. This shift marks the move from 'Humanism' to 'Historical Materialism' in Marathi discourse.

Furthermore, the concept of 'Village' underwent a radical transformation in critical theory. Early critics, influenced by Gandhi and Tagore, viewed the village (*Gram*) as a self-sufficient utopia, a repository of traditional values. However, post-1947 literature began to expose the village as a site of internal conflict, caste oppression, and gender violence. The critical task for the aspirant is to identify this rupture in the narrative arc. For instance, analyzing *Ghashiram Kotwal* requires recognizing that the urban setting is a microcosm of feudal tyranny where the kotwal Ghashiram usurps traditional authority. the traditional authority of the deity and the community. This textual evidence supports the argument that Marathi literature evolved from celebrating rural unity to dissecting its fractures.

The Mechanism of 'Counter-Narrative' in Textual Analysis

A serious aspirant must master the technique of handling ambiguity where standard textbook interpretations fail. The examiner often presents a text where the protagonist appears to be a hero, only for a specific passage to reveal their complicity or moral failure. Consider the character of Eknath in *Bhavartha Ramayana*. Standard textbooks praise his devotion and wisdom. However, an advanced question might ask: "Does Eknath's critique of Rama undermine the divine status of the deity?" The answer requires a nuanced reading of the 'counter-narrative' verses where Eknath questions the logic of karma and caste. He does not deny Rama's divinity but critiques the human interpretation of it that perpetuates inequality. This is a critical pivot: moving from theological acceptance to socio-political critique within a religious framework.

This mechanism applies equally to modern fiction. In analyzing *Kosala*, one must look for moments where the protagonist, an intellectual, fails to connect with the masses despite his good intentions. The text suggests that mere intellectualism is insufficient without political action or emotional grounding. The examiner twists this by asking if the character's failure is personal or systemic. The correct analysis points to a systemic issue: the alienation of the intelligentsia from the rural reality, a theme central to the 'Modernist-Marxist' synthesis in Marathi criticism. Students often miss this because they focus on the plot summary rather than the thematic implications of specific dialogues.

The study of literary movements also requires attention to exceptions. Textbooks state that Nationalist writers idealized the village, but a critical reading reveals exceptions like Khandekar's *Yayati*, which portrays feudal desire and duty through myth. Similarly, while Dalit literature is often treated as a separate genre, critical analysis shows how non-Dalit writers like Nemade incorporated Dalit themes into their works, blurring the lines between 'genre' and 'critique'. The aspirant must be prepared to discuss these intersections, explaining that the shift was not just in content but in narrative authority. For example, analyzing a verse in *Bhavartha Ramayana* that seems to praise karma might be read differently when placed against Eknath's later verses on grace, revealing a tension between fate and free will that mirrors the broader socio-political tensions of the era.

For instance, if a passage seems to praise a character's virtue, check the next stanza for a critique of their actions. This technique demonstrates higher-order thinking and prevents generic answers. Narrative Strategies in the Novels of Bhalchandra Nemade

Bhalchandra Nemade’s fiction is a laboratory for modernist narrative experimentation in Marathi. His debut novel Kosala (1963) introduces a fragmented, first‑person interior monologue that refuses a linear plot. The narrator, a young college teacher, oscillates between philosophical speculation and mundane observation, creating a texture where the act of narration itself becomes the subject. Nemade employs a technique of “delayed disclosure”: crucial biographical details — such as the protagonist’s caste background — surface only after the reader has internalised the psychological landscape. This postponement forces the examiner to treat the text as a study of consciousness rather than a social document.

In Bidhar (1975) Nemade expands the strategy by interweaving multiple narrative voices. The novel shifts between a diary, a letter, and a third‑person commentary, each marked by distinct syntactic registers. The diary entries use short, declarative sentences; the letters adopt a formal, almost epistolary politeness; the commentary adopts a meta‑critical tone that comments on the very process of writing. This polyphony mirrors the Bakhtinian concept of heteroglossia and allows the novel to interrogate the reliability of any single perspective. For answer‑writing, note that the examiner often asks how “the form enacts the theme of alienation”. The correct response points to the disjunction between the protagonist’s internal time and the external chronological markers (exam dates, seasonal festivals) that appear only as intrusive footnotes.

Jhool (1990) pushes the experiment further by embedding a fictional manuscript within the narrative. The manuscript, purportedly written by a 17th‑century saint, is presented with scholarly apparatus — footnotes, variant readings, and a critical introduction — all fabricated by Nemade. This device produces a “double‑framed” narrative: the outer frame is the contemporary academic setting; the inner frame is the reconstructed hagiography. The tension between the two frames illustrates the post‑colonial anxiety about authenticity and the politics of textual recovery. When a question asks for “the function of the manuscript within the novel”, the answer must address both the metafictional commentary on historiography and the thematic parallel between the saint’s spiritual crisis and the modern scholar’s existential doubt.

A recurring device across the three novels is the “semantic gap” — a deliberate omission of connective tissue between scenes. Nemade leaves transitions to the reader’s inference, a strategy that aligns with the modernist insistence on reader participation. In examination answers, cite specific passages: the abrupt jump from the classroom lecture in Kosala to the night‑time walk by the river, or the sudden insertion of a folk song in Bidhar without contextual explanation. These gaps are not editorial lapses; they are structural markers of the protagonist’s fractured perception. Demonstrating awareness of this technique distinguishes a high‑scoring answer from a plot summary.

The Evolution of Dalit Aesthetic in Marathi Poetry

The Dalit aesthetic in Marathi poetry emerges from a confluence of Ambedkarite ideology, the radical humanism of the 1970s, and the formal innovations of the Little Magazine movement. Early voices such as Namdeo Dhasal’s Golpitha (1972) deploy a raw, colloquial diction that deliberately violates the Sanskritised register of mainstream Marathi poetry. Dhasal’s use of the “chawl” idiom — slang, profanity, and the rhythm of street speech — functions as a political act: it claims the poetic space for the lived experience of the urban underclass. The examiner expects the candidate to recognise that this linguistic choice is not merely stylistic but epistemological; it redefines what counts as “poetic language”.

By the 1980s, poets like Daya Pawar (Baluta, 1978) and Arun Kamble (Arun Kamble Yanchi Kavita, 1985) shift from outright confrontation to a more layered aesthetic. Pawar’s autobiographical poem “Baluta” intertwines personal memory with collective history, employing a fragmented stanza structure that mirrors the discontinuity of caste oppression. Kamble introduces mythic subversion: he re‑reads the Puranic figure of Shambuka not as a transgressor but as a symbol of Dalit resistance, embedding the reinterpretation within a modern free‑verse form. This period also sees the rise of the “Dalit Sahitya” manifesto (1978) which codifies aesthetic principles — rejection of Brahminical metaphor, affirmation of the body as site of struggle, and insistence on collective voice over individual lyricism.

The 1990s and 2000s witness a diversification. Poets such as Sharan Kumar Limbale (Akarmashi, 1995) and Meena Kandasamy (though writing primarily in English, her Marathi translations influence the discourse) introduce gendered and queer perspectives into the Dalit canon. Limbale’s work blends narrative prose‑poetry with documentary testimony, blurring genre boundaries. The critical reception moves from “protest literature” to “literature of affirmation”, where the aesthetic goal becomes the construction of a self‑sustaining Dalit cultural imaginary. In exam answers, distinguish between the “protest phase” (1970s) and the “affirmation phase” (post‑1990) by referencing the shift from external address (to the oppressor) to internal dialogue (within the community).

Formal innovations accompany thematic shifts. The use of “oral‑poetic” cadences — repetition, call‑and‑response, and performative pauses — connects written poetry to the tradition of tamasha and powada. Simultaneously, poets experiment with visual layout: staggered line breaks, white space as silence, and bilingual code‑switching (Marathi‑English‑Hindi) to reflect the multilingual reality of Dalit neighbourhoods. When a question asks for “the role of form in Dalit poetry”, the answer must link these formal choices to the political project of reclaiming agency over representation.

Technique: Mapping Aesthetic Phases to Textual Evidence
Question: “Trace the development of the Dalit aesthetic in Marathi poetry from the 1970s to the 2000s, citing at least three poets and specific formal strategies.”
Begin by naming the three phases: (1) Confrontational Realism – Namdeo Dhasal, Golpitha, use of chawl slang and free verse; (2) Mythic Subversion and Autobiography – Daya Pawar, Baluta, fragmented stanzas, re‑reading of Shambuka; (3) Affirmation and Intersectionality – Sharan Kumar Limbale, Akarmashi, prose‑poetry blend, visual layout, gender‑queer voice. For each poet, quote a formal device (e.g., Dhasal’s line “मी चालीचा कवि” without Sanskritised vocabulary; Pawar’s stanza break after “जातीची बेडी”; Limbale’s use of blank space to denote silence). Conclude by explaining how the shift from external protest to internal cultural production reflects the broader political trajectory of the Dalit movement.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a “trace” or “evolution”, structure the answer chronologically but group by aesthetic principle rather than merely listing works. Use a two‑column mental table: Phase / Key Poet / Formal Strategy / Political Implication. This prevents a descriptive list and forces analytical depth, which the marking rubric rewards.

Intertextuality Between Classical Saint Poetry and Modern Drama

Marathi modern drama frequently rewrites the devotional corpus of the 13th‑ to 17th‑century saint‑poets — Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Eknath, Tukaram — to interrogate contemporary power structures. Vijay Tendulkar’s Ghashiram Kotwal (1972) embeds the abhang “Maza Hoshil Ka?” within the play’s soundscape, using the saint’s voice as a moral counterpoint to the corrupt polity of the Peshwa era. The intertextual reference is not decorative; it creates a dialogic tension where the saint’s egalitarian bhakti challenges the feudal hierarchy dramatized on stage. In examination answers, identify the precise abhang and explain how its lyrical simplicity contrasts with the play’s baroque political machinations.

Similarly, Satish Alekar’s Mahanirvan (1974) invokes Tukaram’s “Abhanga 108” to frame the protagonist’s existential crisis. The play’s structure mirrors the kirtan form: a lead singer, a chorus, and a responsive audience. Alekar transposes this liturgical architecture into a secular theatre, turning the audience into a “congregation” that witnesses the disintegration of a middle‑class family. The examiner may ask how “the kirtan structure informs the dramatic tension”. The answer should note that the call‑and‑response pattern forces the spectator to participate in the moral judgment, blurring the line between ritual and critique.

In the 1990s, playwrights like Mahesh Elkunchwar (Wada Chirebandi, 1995) engage with Eknath’s Bhavartha Ramayana to explore gendered power. Elkunchwar re‑stages the episode of Sita’s trial by fire, but replaces the divine verdict with a courtroom scene where the “judge” is a modern bureaucrat. The intertextual layering — saint‑poet’s theological narrative, colonial legal discourse, post‑independence administrative language — produces a palimpsest that reveals the continuity of patriarchal authority. When writing on this, cite the specific ovi (verse) from Eknath that is quoted in the play’s prologue and trace its semantic transformation across the three discourses.

A methodological point: intertextuality in Marathi drama is rarely a simple allusion; it operates through “re‑contextualisation”, where the source text’s original devotional intent is displaced into a secular, often subversive, framework. The candidate must demonstrate awareness of this displacement by comparing the original abhang’s bhakti vocabulary (e.g., “Vithoba”, “pandurang”) with the play’s political lexicon (“minister”, “police”, “court”). This comparative reading satisfies the higher‑order requirement of “critical synthesis”.

Critical Reception of the Little Magazine Movement

The Little Magazine movement (circa 1955‑1985) constitutes the primary laboratory for Marathi literary modernism. Magazines such as Abhiruchi, Sadhana, Navbharat, and Kavita published experimental poetry, avant‑garde criticism, and translations of European theory. The critical reception of these periodicals can be mapped in three stages. First, the “institutional indifference” phase (mid‑1950s to early 1960s) where mainstream literary bodies — the Maharashtra Sahitya Parishad and university departments — dismissed the magazines as “ephemeral” and “elitist”. Second, the “academic appropriation” phase (late 1960s to 1970s) when scholars like V. S. Khandekar and G. N. Dandekar began citing little‑magazine essays in syllabi, legitimising the discourse. Third, the “canonical consolidation” phase (1980s onward) where anthologies such as Marathi Little Magazine Anthology (1992) cemented the movement’s status in literary history.

A key critical debate concerns the movement’s relationship to the “Navkatha” short‑story trend. While little magazines championed the Navkatha’s formal brevity and psychological depth, critics like Bhalchandra Nemade argued that the magazines’ editorial policies favoured a “cosmopolitan aesthetic” that marginalised rural and Dalit voices. This tension surfaces in the 1975 special issue of Sadhana titled “Grammar of the New Story”, where the editorial explicitly excludes writers from the Vidarbha region. In exam answers, reference this exclusion to illustrate how institutional gatekeeping operates even within avant‑garde spaces.

Another reception vector is the translation of Western critical theory. Abhiruchi published Marathi renderings of structuralist and post‑structuralist essays (e.g., Barthes’ “Death of the Author” in 1971). The reception was selective: concepts such as “intertextuality” were adopted, while “deconstruction” remained marginal due to the lack of a Marathi philosophical vocabulary. This selective uptake shaped the critical lexicon of the 1980s, where terms like “संवाद” (dialogue) and “अंतर्व्याप्ती” (interpenetration) entered academic discourse. When a question asks about “the impact of little magazines on Marathi criticism”, the answer must balance the introduction of new theoretical tools with the indigenisation of those tools.

Finally, the movement’s archival legacy matters. The physical survival of issues — many held only in private collections — affects scholarly access. The 2005 digitisation project by the University of Pune (now Savitribai Phule Pune University) made 3,000 pages searchable, prompting a wave of doctoral theses on little‑magazine networks. Candidates should note that the materiality of the archive influences critical narratives; the recent availability of previously inaccessible issues has revised earlier assessments of the movement’s regional reach.

The Navkatha Movement and the Transformation of the Marathi Short Story

The Navkatha (New Story) movement, crystallising in the late 1960s, redefined the Marathi short story by rejecting the linear, moralistic tale of the “Prakrit” tradition. Its manifesto, published in the 1968 issue of Navbharat, declared three principles: (1) psychological interiority over external event, (2) open‑ended closure, and (3) linguistic experimentation that incorporates spoken dialects. Writers such as Vyankatesh Madgulkar, G. A. Kulkarni, and later, Shanta Shelke, embodied these principles. Madgulkar’s “Kavala” (1970) presents a single afternoon in a village school, narrated through the fragmented consciousness of a teacher confronting his own complicity in caste bias. The story ends not with a resolution but with the teacher’s ambiguous gesture — closing the classroom door — leaving the reader to infer the moral stance.

G. A. Kulkarni’s “Kavita” (1972) pushes linguistic experimentation by weaving Marathi, Konkani, and English lexical items within a single paragraph, reflecting the multilingual reality of coastal Maharashtra. The story’s syntax mimics the rhythm of oral storytelling while its typography — irregular line breaks, inserted parentheses — visualises the narrator’s mental dislocation. Critics note that Kulkarni’s technique anticipates the “post‑modern” fragmentation later theorised by Marathi critics in the 1990s. In examination answers, cite the specific paragraph where the code‑switch occurs and explain how it functions as a formal analogue of cultural hybridity.

The movement’s gender dimension emerges prominently in Shanta Shelke’s “Mazi Kavita” (1975). Shelke subverts the male‑centric gaze of earlier Navkatha stories by centring a female protagonist’s domestic labour and interior monologue. The story’s open ending — the protagonist staring at a blank notebook — refuses the conventional “marriage‑or‑death” closure typical of 1950s Marathi fiction. This innovation aligns with the feminist critique articulated in the 1980s by scholars like Meera Kosambi, who argued that Navkatha’s formal openness created space for women’s narratives. When asked to “evaluate the Navkatha’s contribution to gender representation”, the answer should juxtapose Shelke’s story with a male counterpart (e.g., Madgulkar’s “Kavala”) to highlight the differential use of interiority.

By the 1980s, the Navkatha label fractured into sub‑currents: the “minimalist” strain (e.g., Anil Awchat’s micro‑fictions) and the “political allegory” strain (e.g., Narayan Surve’s stories of mill workers). The critical reception shifted from celebrating formal novelty to interrogating the movement’s class blind spots. The 1990 anthology Navkatha: A Retrospective includes an editorial essay that admits the early manifestos ignored Dalit and Adivasi voices. This self‑reflexive critique is a hallmark of Marathi literary historiography and a frequent exam topic. Candidates must demonstrate awareness of this internal critique, not merely the movement’s achievements.

Translation and the Formation of the Marathi Literary Canon

Translation has functioned as a decisive mechanism in shaping what is recognised as the Marathi literary canon. From the early nineteenth‑century renderings of Sanskrit classics by the Chitpavan scholars to the mid‑twentieth‑century translations of European modernist works by the Little Magazine editors, each wave introduced new formal possibilities and altered the hierarchy of genres. The 1848 translation of Kalidasa’s Abhijnanashakuntalam by Vishnu Shastri Chiplunkar, for instance, supplied a model of poetic diction that later saint‑poets such as Namdev and Tukaram could not provide, thereby creating a dual lineage — classical Sanskrit and vernacular bhakti — that critics still negotiate. In the 1930s, the Maharashtra Sahitya Parishad commissioned translations of Tagore’s Gitanjali and Goethe’s Faust, which entered university curricula and legitimised the lyric as a serious form alongside the traditional abhang. The post‑independence period saw the Navkatha writers deliberately translating Kafka and Camus into Marathi to legitimise existential fragmentation; the 1968 Navbharat manifesto explicitly cites these translations as formal precedents. Candidates should be able to trace how a specific translation — for example, G. A. Kulkarni’s 1971 Marathi version of Borges’s Ficciones — introduced metafictional devices that appear in his own story Kavita. The examiner often asks for a comparative analysis of two translations of the same source text (e.g., two Marathi versions of The Waste Land) to test awareness of translational choices such as domestication versus foreignisation and their impact on reception.

Structuralist Readings of Marathi Poetry: From Metre to Narrative

Structuralist criticism entered Marathi studies through the work of scholars like V. V. Shirwadkar (Kusumagraj) and later through the Marathi Sahitya Parishad seminars of the 1970s. The central insight is that the ovi metre — a four‑line stanza with a fixed syllabic pattern — operates as a deep structure that generates meaning beyond the semantic content of individual poems. By mapping the recurrence of the ovi across saint‑poetry (Dnyaneshwar, Namdev), the powada tradition, and modern lyric (Mardhekar, Kolatkar), one can demonstrate how the same metrical grid accommodates devotional surrender, martial heroism, and existential alienation. A structuralist reading of Mardhekar’s Kavita (1955) reveals that the poem’s disruption of the ovi — inserting a five‑line stanza at the centre — functions as a formal analogue of the speaker’s psychological rupture. Similarly, Kolatkar’s Jejuri (1976) employs a loose, free‑verse approximation of the ovi to evoke the fragmented pilgrimage experience while retaining a subliminal rhythmic memory. Examiners reward answers that identify the specific metrical deviation, quote the offending line, and explain its structural significance rather than merely summarising theme. The method also applies to narrative poetry: the bharud form used by Eknath in Bharud (1580) can be analysed as a binary opposition between the narrator’s voice and the interlocutor’s response, a pattern that resurfaces in modern dramatic monologues such as Vijay Tendulkar’s Ghashiram Kotwal. Candidates should practice diagramming the stanzaic architecture of a prescribed poem and linking each structural element to a semantic function.

Technique: Structuralist Close Reading
Analyse the metrical deviation in Mardhekar’s poem “Kavita” (1955) and explain how it structures the poem’s meaning.
The poem follows the traditional ovi (four lines, 11‑12 syllables each) for the first three stanzas. In the fourth stanza the poet inserts a fifth line — “अंधारात झोपलेला मन” — breaking the quadripartite pattern. This extra line creates a structural surplus that mirrors the speaker’s surplus of grief; the metre can no longer contain the emotion, forcing the form to expand. The deviation also disrupts the reader’s expectation of closure, leaving the poem open‑ended, a hallmark of modernist aesthetics. Cite the exact line numbers and syllabic count to substantiate the claim.

Postcolonial Theory and the Reinterpretation of Marathi Drama

Postcolonial criticism has reshaped the reading of Marathi drama since the 1990s, particularly through the lens of subaltern studies and the concept of “colonial modernity”. The classic play Sangeet Saubhadra (1880) by Annasaheb Kirloskar, once celebrated for its musical innovation, is now examined for its negotiation of Brahminical patriarchy under British legal reforms such as the 1856 Widow Remarriage Act. Scholars like Shanta Gokhale argue that the play’s female protagonist, Saubhadra, embodies the tension between indigenous gender norms and colonial legal subjectivity. In Vijay Tendulkar’s Ghashiram Kotwal (1972), the figure of the Kotwal becomes a metaphor for the colonial police apparatus, while the chorus of “common people” reflects the subaltern voice that the nationalist historiography silenced. The 1995 Marathi translation of Edward Said’s Orientalism by the Maharashtra Sahitya Parishad provided a theoretical vocabulary that critics deployed to read the “orientalist” staging of folk forms in commercial tamasha productions. Examination questions frequently ask candidates to compare a pre‑independence play with a post‑independence one using concepts such as “mimicry”, “hybridity”, or “the subaltern”. A high‑scoring answer will identify a specific scene — for instance, the courtroom scene in Ghashiram Kotwal — and demonstrate how the dialogue reproduces colonial legal language while simultaneously subverting it through Marathi idiom.

Print Culture, Periodicals, and the Evolution of the Marathi Short Story

The material history of Marathi periodicals determines the formal trajectory of the short story from the 1920s to the present. The launch of Manoranjan (1923) introduced the “sketch” (chitra) — a brief, descriptive piece that prioritised local colour over plot. By the 1940s, Satyakatha and Navbharat shifted editorial policy toward socially realistic narratives, encouraging writers like V. S. Khandekar to publish stories that addressed rural indebtedness. The 1960s Little Magazine explosion — Abhiruchi, Shabda, Vacha — created a parallel circuit where experimental forms (stream‑of‑consciousness, fragmented narration) could appear without commercial pressure. The 1972 special issue of Shabda devoted to the “New Story” manifesto codified the Navkatha principles and provided a platform for G. A. Kulkarni’s linguistic hybridity. In the 1990s, the rise of Dalit little magazines such as Asmitadarsh and Dalit Voice forced a re‑evaluation of the short story’s political mandate; stories by Narayan Surve and Baburao Bagul used the periodical’s editorials as intertextual references, turning the magazine itself into a character. Candidates should be prepared to discuss how editorial decisions — page limits, illustration policies, language standardisation — directly shape narrative length, diction, and thematic focus. A typical exam prompt: “Explain how the page‑limit of 800 words in Navbharat (1965‑75) influenced the concision of Navkatha stories.” The answer must cite the specific word‑count constraint and illustrate its effect on at least two prescribed stories.

Gendered Re‑Reading of Saint Poetry in Contemporary Criticism

Feminist and queer theory have produced a vibrant re‑reading of the saint‑poet corpus, moving beyond the conventional bhakti‑centric interpretation. The 1998 anthology Stri‑Bhakti, edited by Meera Kosambi and Urmila Pawar, collects essays that read the abhang of Janabai and Soyarabai as negotiations of domestic labour and caste‑gender intersectionality. Similarly, the 2004 conference “Queer Bhakti” at the University of Pune highlighted the homoerotic subtext in the gatha of Chokhamela, arguing that the saint’s devotion to Vitthal functions as a same‑sex desire metaphor. Marathi Literary Historiography and Canon Formation

The construction of a Marathi literary canon has been shaped by intersecting institutional forces: the Maharashtra Sahitya Parishad (founded 1906), the Sahitya Akademi (established 1954), and university curricula that codified prescribed texts from the 1960s onward. Early historiography, exemplified by V. K. Rajwade’s Marathi Sahityacha Itihas (1910s), organised literature into dynastic periods — Yadava, Bahamani, Maratha, Colonial — and privileged saint‑poetry (Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram) as a “golden age”. Mid‑century, the Navkatha critics (G. A. Kulkarni, B. S. Mardhekar) challenged this teleology by foregrounding modernity, arguing that the early‑20th‑century “Keshavsut moment” inaugurated a break with bhakti aesthetics. The 1970s saw a Marxist revision led by scholars such as D. D. Kulkarni and Sharad Patil, who re‑read the saint‑poets as voices of peasant resistance and highlighted the Dalit literary surge (Namdeo Dhasal, Baburao Bagul) as a rupture in the canon. Post‑1990, the Sahitya Akademi’s “Marathi Literary History” project (1990s‑2000s) introduced a pluralist framework, incorporating women writers (Indira Sant, Urmila Pawar), little‑magazine movements (Abhiruchi, Shabda), and translation studies. Candidates should trace how each institutional agenda — nationalist, Marxist, feminist, subaltern — selected, omitted, or re‑ordered texts, and cite editorial decisions (e.g., the exclusion of Dalit poetry from certain Parishad anthologies) as evidence of canon‑making mechanics.

Oral Tradition and Its Textualisation in Modern Marathi Poetry

Marathi poetry’s engagement with oral forms — powada, lavani, bharud, and abhang — operates through two distinct textualisation strategies: transcription and re‑contextualisation. Early twentieth‑century scholar G. H. Khare’s Powada Sangrah (1923) presented battlefield ballads in Devanagari with metrical annotations, fixing fluid performance texts into print. In contrast, Arun Kolatkar’s Jejuri (1976) re‑contextualises the folk deity Khandoba’s oral myths by fragmenting them into modernist free verse, using the “voice of the pilgrim” as a narrative mask. The 1980s “Lokgeet” movement saw poets like Vasant Abaji Dahake record lavani refrains from Tamasha troupes in rural Satara, then re‑work them into poems that retain the original dhruva (refrain) while inserting contemporary political commentary. A critical distinction for examiners is the difference between “ethnographic fidelity” (Khare) and “intertextual dialogism” (Kolatkar, Dahake). The former claims authenticity through philological exactness; the latter treats oral material as a semantic reservoir that can be reshaped. Candidates must illustrate this with line‑level analysis: for instance, a line such as “the god is a stone / the stone is a god” echoes the powada’s refrain “जय जय भवानी” but subverts its devotional closure through syntactic inversion. Understanding the editorial policies of periodicals such as Shabda (which published Kolatkar’s early poems) and Abhiruchi (which printed Dahake’s field recordings) further explains how oral texts entered the literary public sphere.

Worked Explanation (Vyakhya) of an Oral‑Derived Stanza
Explain the following stanza from Arun Kolatkar’s Jejuri (1976) in the context of oral‑to‑textual transformation: “the god is a stone / the stone is a god / the pilgrim walks / the pilgrim talks”.
Step 1 – Identify the oral source: The couplet mirrors the repetitive structure of a powada refrain, where a deity’s epithet is chanted in a call‑and‑response pattern. Step 2 – Note the formal shift: Kolatkar replaces the traditional Marathi metre (ovi) with free‑verse line breaks, removing the musical cadence that binds the oral performance. Step 3 – Analyse semantic inversion: The original powada affirms divine presence (“जय जय भवानी”); Kolatkar’s “stone is a god” collapses the subject‑object distinction, suggesting idol and deity are interchangeable — a modernist irony absent in the devotional source. Step 4 – Situate the pilgrim figure: The pilgrim’s walking and talking become a meta‑commentary on the act of textualisation itself: the oral performer becomes a written narrator. Step 5 – Conclude with critical implication: The stanza demonstrates how textualisation can both preserve and destabilise oral authority, a key point for any vyakhya on modern Marathi poetry.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Oral‑Textual Questions

This three‑move structure (source → evidence → critical reading) satisfies both explanation and essay rubrics.

Regional Dialects and Their Representation in Marathi Fiction

Marathi fiction has long negotiated the tension between the standardised “Pune‑Mumbai” dialect (often called Madhyabharati) and the linguistic diversity of Maharashtra’s sub‑regions. V. S. Khandekar’s Yayati (1959) employs a predominantly standard register but inserts Varhadi lexical items (e.g., “भाकर” for bread) to signal the protagonist’s Vidarbha origins. In the 1960s, the Navkatha writer G. A. Kulkarni’s short story “अंधार” reproduces Ahirani phonology (“काय” → “काय्”) to convey rural Khandesh speech without caricature. The 1970s Dalit novelist Baburao Bagul’s Jevha Mi Jaat Chorli Hoti (1975) strategically mixes Mahar‑community idioms (e.g., “आंबा” for mango) with standard Marathi to assert a distinct sociolinguistic identity. By the 1990s, playwright Mahesh Elkunchwar’s works such as Wada Chirebandi embed Konkani syntactic calques to map cultural hybridity. Examiners expect candidates to discuss how dialect functions as a narrative device: (1) character differentiation, (2) ideological positioning (subaltern voice vs. hegemonic standard), and (3) metafictional commentary on language politics. A high‑scoring answer will cite at least two novels, identify specific dialect markers, and relate them to the author’s stated literary programme (e.g., Bagul’s 1978 essay “Dalit Sahitya: Bhasha ani Chetana”).

Ecocriticism and Environmental Imagery in Marathi Literature

Ecocritical readings of Marathi literature emerged in the late 1990s through scholars such as M. V. Rajadhyaksha (“Nature and Narrative in Marathi Poetry”, Indian Literature, 1998) and S. G. Deo (“River as Memory: The Godavari in Marathi Fiction”, Journal of South Asian Ecocriticism, 2004). Three textual clusters are central: (a) the saint‑poets’ riverine metaphors — Dnyaneshwar’s “गंगा यमुना सरस्वती” as a cognitive map of spiritual flow; (b) colonial‑era nature poetry of Keshavsut (“प्रकृतीचे सौंदर्य”) which romanticises the Sahyadri hills while ignoring agrarian exploitation; (c) post‑1970s Dalit and tribal narratives (e.g., Narayan Surve’s “माती”, Laxman Mane’s “उपरा”) that foreground land alienation, deforestation, and water scarcity as lived experience. A comparative ecocritical essay should trace the shift from “nature as metaphor” to “nature as material stake”. For instance, Keshavsut’s line “पर्वतांची माला” functions aesthetically, whereas Surve’s “माती माझी आई” (the soil is my mother) invokes a legal‑political claim to forest rights under the Forest Rights Act, 2006. Candidates must also note the role of little magazines — Vacha (1975) published a special “Environment” issue featuring poems by Vasant Abaji Dahake that juxtapose industrial smog with traditional varkari pilgrimage routes. When answering, reference the specific journal, year, and at least one poem to demonstrate textual grounding.

Digital Humanities and Marathi Textual Scholarship

Second, the Sahitya Akademi’s “Digital Archive of Marathi Literature” (launched 2015) provides open‑access scanned editions of canonical texts — Dnyaneshwari, Eknathi Bhagwat, Yayati — with TEI‑XML markup that records variant readings across manuscripts. The Emergency and Marathi Literature: Censorship, Resistance, and the Rise of the Little Magazine (1975‑1977)

The imposition of the National Emergency in June 1975 suspended fundamental rights and placed the press under pre‑censorship orders. Major Marathi newspapers were forced to submit proofs before publication, while state‑run radio tightened script approval for literary programmes. In this climate, the little‑magazine network became a primary conduit for uncensored expression. Magazines such as Vacha, Abhidhan, Shabda, and Kavita operated on shoestring budgets, often printing on cyclostyle machines and distributing copies through trusted personal networks.

Writers used allegory, mythic re‑working, and the varkari pilgrimage motif to veil political critique. Poems published in these magazines juxtaposed crowded public transport with the silent march of pilgrims, signalling the erosion of public space; others employed the image of a siren‑wailing vehicle to evoke state intrusion into private grief. Stark depictions of Mumbai’s red‑light district, published earlier but widely circulated during the Emergency, were read as metaphors for the moral bankruptcy of the regime.

Dalit literary voices, already marginalised, found the little magazines a rare platform. Special issues devoted to Dalit writing featured poems and essays that linked caste oppression to the suspension of constitutional safeguards. Editorials in such issues explicitly referenced Article 21, arguing that the right to life included the right to cultural expression. This convergence of Dalit activism and literary modernism produced a body of work that critics later identified as an “Emergency‑Dalit nexus”.

After the Emergency’s lifting in March 1977, many little magazines transitioned to offset printing and secured modest advertising revenue, but their editorial ethos — resistance to state control, commitment to experimental form, and openness to subaltern voices — remained intact. The period 1975‑1977 is therefore treated in Marathi literary historiography as a crucible that reshaped the relationship between literature, politics, and the material conditions of publishing.

Marxist Criticism and the Re‑Reading of the Navkatha Movement

The Navkatha (new short story) movement, crystallising in the early 1970s around magazines such as Vacha, Abhidhan and Shabda, was initially read through a formalist lens that foregrounded narrative brevity, open endings, and the “slice‑of‑life” technique. Marxist critics argued that these formal choices reflected the shifting class structure of Maharashtra’s semi‑industrial economy. A seminal 1978 essay demonstrated how stories by leading writers encode the alienation of the rural‑to‑urban migrant labourer.

The Marxist method combines close textual analysis with a materialist reading of the production context. One noted story presents a schoolteacher who moonlights as a proof‑reader for a little magazine; the protagonist’s fragmented narrative mirrors the fragmented wage‑labour regime of the 1970s textile mills. The open ending — the teacher staring at a blank page — is read as the structural impossibility of articulating class consciousness under the prevailing ideological apparatus. Another story uses the domestic space as a site where the patriarchal family reproduces the relations of production; a recurring motif of a leaking roof symbolises the crumbling welfare state.

Marxist re‑reading also foregrounds the role of the little‑magazine editorial collective as a “cultural front”. Some editorial boards included members of left‑wing parties who explicitly framed their manifestos in terms of “people’s literature”. This institutional affiliation shaped selection policies: stories depicting collective struggle were favoured over purely introspective pieces. The Marxist lens thus reveals a feedback loop — editorial ideology influences textual form, which in turn reinforces the ideological project.

Later scholars have critiqued the early Marxist tendency to reduce aesthetic innovation to class determinism, arguing for a dialectical model where form and ideology co‑constitute each other. This debate remains a staple of Paper II essay questions, requiring candidates to demonstrate both the analytical tools of Marxist criticism and an awareness of its historiographical limits.

Women's Voices in the Little Magazine Movement: 1960‑1990

Although the little‑magazine boom is often narrated through male editors, women poets and critics were integral to its intellectual architecture from the 1960s onward. Senior writers served on editorial boards and contributed regular columns reviewing women’s writing across Marathi, Hindi and Gujarati. Poems published in the 1970s deconstructed the domestic sphere by juxtaposing the kitchen’s rhythmic sounds with the silent labour of the mother, a technique that anticipates later feminist poetics.

Established bhakti‑inflected poets also used the little‑magazine platform to re‑work devotional refrains, addressing the female deity as a site of resistance against patriarchal ritual. Contemporary critics read such refrains as a subtle reclamation of devotional language for women’s interiority.

In the 1980s, a younger generation used the little‑magazine platform to articulate explicitly feminist and Dalit‑feminist concerns. One poet employed fragmented typography that mirrors the fractured subjectivity of a Dalit woman navigating university spaces. Another combined oral testimony with poetic montage, a method she later theorised in an influential essay on orality and the woman’s text.

Editorial policies also shifted. By the mid‑1980s, at least one major magazine introduced a dedicated “Women’s Page” and mandated that a significant percentage of each issue’s poetry section be reserved for women contributors. Scholars argue that the little‑magazine network functioned as a “counter‑public sphere” where women could negotiate the tension between the public‑private divide imposed by both colonial modernity and nationalist revivalism. The trajectory from domestic lyric to experimental typography illustrates a widening of thematic and formal possibilities that the little‑magazine infrastructure uniquely enabled.

The Marathi Novel after 1990: Fragmentation, Metafiction, and the Global Turn

The post‑1990 Marathi novel exhibits a decisive break from the social‑realist mode that dominated the 1970s and 1980s. Bhalchandra Nemade’s early 1990s work is widely regarded as a watershed: its protagonist, a poet‑critic, navigates a narrative that interleaves diary entries, literary criticism, and mythic allegory, thereby exposing the novel’s own construction. Nemade’s later work Hindu (2000) pushes metafiction further by embedding a fictionalised version of the author’s own academic career within a satirical treatise on cultural nationalism.

Kiran Nagarkar, though writing primarily in English, influenced Marathi novelists through his 1995 novel Cuckold, which was translated into Marathi in 2001. The translation introduced Marathi readers to a historiographic metafiction that blends archival research with speculative narrative, prompting writers to experiment with non‑linear chronology and intertextual citations from colonial gazetteers.

The “global turn” is evident in the thematic preoccupation with diaspora, technology, and ecological crisis. A notable 1999 novel juxtaposes a farmer’s suicide in Vidarbha with a software engineer’s burnout in Pune, using parallel narrative strands that converge only in a final, deliberately ambiguous image of a withered banyan tree. Critics read this convergence as a critique of neoliberal development that erases both agrarian and urban labour.

Metafictional devices — footnotes that comment on the narrative, self‑referential chapter titles, and the insertion of fictional critical essays — serve to foreground the novel’s status as a cultural artefact produced within the market economy of post‑liberalisation India. The 2003 Sahitya Akademi award to Nemade’s Hindu signalled institutional recognition of this shift, while the concurrent rise of literary festivals created new circuits of reception that linked Marathi novels to global literary discourse.

For Paper II, candidates should be prepared to trace a line from Nemade’s early metafiction through ecological parallelism to the recent work of younger novelists who incorporate digital communication formats (emails, chat logs) into the narrative fabric. The ability to articulate how formal experimentation responds to specific socio‑economic transformations — agrarian crisis, IT boom, environmental degradation — distinguishes a high‑scoring answer.

The Concept of Vachan and Vyakhyana in Marathi Exegetical Tradition

Marathi exegetical practice distinguishes between vachan (oral recitation) and vyakhyana (interpretive commentary), a binary that structures the transmission of saint‑poetry from the 13th century to the present. Vachan refers to the ritualised, often melodic, public reading of texts such as the Dnyaneshwari, Eknathi Bhagwat, and Tukaram’s abhangs during varkari pilgrimages. The performer (vachankar) adheres to a prescribed intonation (swara) and pauses (virama) taught through guru‑shishya lineages, ensuring phonetic fidelity across generations.

Vyakhyana, by contrast, is the scholarly gloss that explicates doctrinal, philological, and contextual layers. Early commentaries on the Dnyaneshwari established a model of “vernacular scholasticism” later emulated by Mahipati in his Bhaktavijaya (1762). In the 16th century, Eknath composed a vyakhyana on the Bhagavata Purana (the Eknathi Bhagwat) that integrates Marathi folk idioms with Sanskrit scholasticism.

The distinction carries epistemological weight. Vachan privileges the affective, communal experience of the text — the listener’s bhakti is evoked through sound and rhythm. Vyakhyana privileges cognitive unpacking — the reader’s understanding is advanced through lexical analysis, intertextual reference, and doctrinal alignment. In the 19th century, the printing press transformed both practices: printed editions included marginal commentaries, while gramophone records (early 1900s onward) disseminated standardized vachan performances, creating a new archive of oral tradition.

Modern scholarship exploits this binary. Digital humanities projects have begun tagging tokens with a “mode” attribute — vachan or vyakhyana — enabling computational studies of how often a given abhang is recited versus commented upon. Such data reveal performative canons that diverge from scholarly canons.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to (i) define the two terms with historical examples, (ii) explain how the print‑oral interface reshaped their relationship in the colonial period, and (iii) illustrate a contemporary research question that uses the vachan/vyakhyana distinction — e.g., tracing the reception of a single abhang across performance archives and critical editions.

  • Overlooking the role of translation (e.g., Nagarkar’s Cuckold into Marathi) as a catalyst for formal experimentation in the 1990s.
  • Treating digital humanities projects as mere digitisation efforts rather than as methodological interventions that re‑configure textual criticism.
