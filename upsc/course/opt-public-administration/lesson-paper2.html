Civil Services: Recruitment, Training and Performance Management

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) conducts the Civil Services Examination (CSE) in three stages — preliminary, main and personality test — to select candidates for the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), Indian Police Service (IPS), Indian Foreign Service (IFS) and other Group A and B services. Annually, roughly ten lakh aspirants register; the final merit list typically contains about one thousand names, yielding a selection ratio of 0.1 per cent. The constitutional mandate for an independent recruitment body is found in Article 315‑323, which establishes UPSC as a constitutional authority with security of tenure for its members.

After allocation, probationers undergo a 15‑month foundation course at the Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration (LBSNAA), Mussoorie. The curriculum blends constitutional law, public administration, economics, ethics and district‑level field attachments. Since 2014, the Mid‑Career Training Programme (MCTP) has been restructured into three phases: Phase I (after 8‑10 years of service) focuses on leadership and strategic management; Phase II (after 15‑17 years) emphasises policy formulation and governance innovation; Phase III (after 25‑27 years) prepares senior officers for secretary‑level responsibilities. Each phase combines classroom modules, international exposure visits and a capstone project.

Performance appraisal for All India Services is governed by the All India Services (Performance Appraisal) Rules, 2007. The Annual Performance Appraisal Report (APAR) is written by the reporting officer, reviewed by the reviewing officer and accepted by the accepting authority. Since 2019, a 360‑degree feedback mechanism has been piloted for officers at the Joint Secretary level and above, incorporating inputs from peers, subordinates and stakeholders. The APAR grades — Outstanding, Very Good, Good, Average, Below Average — directly influence empanelment for promotion, deputation and foreign assignments. The Central Civil Services (Classification, Control and Appeal) Rules, 1965 provide the disciplinary framework, while the Conduct Rules, 1964 prescribe ethical standards.

Recent reforms include the introduction of the “Mission Karmayogi” (National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building) in 2020, which shifts from a rule‑based to a competency‑driven HR architecture. A digital Integrated Government Online Training (iGOT) platform hosts over 500 courses, enabling anytime‑anywhere learning. The programme also mandates a mandatory 40‑hour annual learning quota for every civil servant, linked to the APAR. These measures aim to address the long‑standing criticism of a static, seniority‑centric promotion culture and to align bureaucratic capabilities with the demands of digital governance and evidence‑based policy making.

Centre-State Administrative Relations and Cooperative Federalism

The Constitution distributes administrative powers through Articles 256‑263. Article 256 obliges states to comply with Union laws; Article 257 empowers the Union to issue directions to states for the exercise of their executive power in matters of national importance, such as railways and communications. Article 263 provides for an Inter‑State Council to inquire into and advise on disputes between states or between the Centre and states. The Council, chaired by the Prime Minister, includes Chief Ministers of all states and six Union ministers; it has met intermittently since its constitution in 1990, with the most recent meeting in 2022 focusing on GST compensation and pandemic management.

Zonal Councils, created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, serve as regional forums for cooperation. Five zones — Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern — each have a Council comprising the Union Home Minister (chair), Chief Ministers and two other ministers from each state. They discuss issues like inter‑state water sharing, law and order, and infrastructure projects. The North‑Eastern Council, established in 1971, performs a similar function for the eight north‑eastern states.

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council, constituted under Article 279A (inserted by the 101st Amendment, 2016), is a quintessential cooperative‑federal body. It comprises the Union Finance Minister (chair), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and the Finance Ministers of all states. Decisions require a three‑fourths majority of weighted votes (Centre one‑third, states two‑thirds). The Council has met over 50 times, resolving rate structures, compensation cess and dispute‑resolution mechanisms, illustrating how fiscal federalism can operate through a constitutional forum.

NITI Aayog, replacing the Planning Commission in 2015, functions as a policy think‑tank rather than a fund‑allocating authority. Its Governing Council includes the Prime Minister, all Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors of Union Territories. The “Team India” approach encourages states to design their own development roadmaps, with NITI providing technical support, monitoring frameworks (e.g., the Aspirational Districts Programme) and a platform for sharing best practices. The shift from top‑down planning to cooperative federalism is reflected in the devolution of the 14th and 15th Finance Commission recommendations, which increased the states’ share of central taxes to 42 per cent and 41 per cent respectively.

Despite these mechanisms, friction persists. The use of Article 356 (President’s Rule) has declined after the S.R. Bommai judgment (1994) laid down strict judicial scrutiny, yet occasional impositions (e.g., Uttarakhand 2016, Arunachal Pradesh 2016) revive debates on federal overreach. The Centre’s power to deploy Central Armed Police Forces under Article 355, and the recent controversies over the use of the National Investigation Agency in state‑level crimes, illustrate the tension between national security imperatives and state autonomy. Effective cooperative federalism therefore depends not only on constitutional architecture but also on political will and institutional trust.

Technique
Question: 'Explain the role of the Inter-State Council in resolving Centre-State administrative disputes.'
Answer: The Inter‑State Council, constituted under Article 263, is a recommendatory body chaired by the Prime Minister with Chief Ministers of all states and six Union ministers as members. Its mandate is to inquire into and advise on disputes between states or between the Centre and states, and to investigate subjects of common interest. While it lacks binding adjudicatory powers, its recommendations carry political weight and have been used to defuse tensions on issues such as river water sharing (e.g., Cauvery), GST compensation, and pandemic‑related coordination. The Council’s effectiveness hinges on regular meetings — historically irregular — and the willingness of both tiers to implement its advice. In practice, it complements the Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction under Article 131 and the Zonal Councils’ regional focus, forming a layered dispute‑resolution architecture.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for “mechanisms of Centre‑State coordination”, structure the answer in three layers: constitutional (Articles 256‑263, 279A), institutional (Inter‑State Council, Zonal Councils, GST Council, NITI Aayog), and functional (specific issues — water, law‑order, fiscal transfers). Cite at least one landmark judgment (S.R. Bommai) and one recent Council meeting outcome to demonstrate contemporaneity. Public Policy Formulation: Models, Instruments and Indian Practice

Policy formulation in India follows a loosely defined cycle: agenda setting, policy formulation, legitimation, implementation and evaluation. The rational‑comprehensive model, associated with Herbert Simon, prescribes exhaustive data gathering, identification of all alternatives and optimisation — rarely achievable in a pluralistic democracy. Incrementalism, articulated by Charles Lindblom, describes the actual practice of “muddling through” — small adjustments to existing programmes, exemplified by the successive expansions of the Public Distribution System (PDS). Mixed scanning, proposed by Amitai Etzioni, combines high‑level strategic scanning with detailed incremental analysis; this mirrors the approach of the National Health Mission, where a broad framework coexists with state‑specific operational guidelines.

Policy instruments available to the state include legislation (e.g., the Right to Education Act, 2009), regulation (e.g., the Environment Impact Assessment Notification, 2006), economic instruments (subsidies, taxes, user charges — such as the LPG subsidy rationalisation through Direct Benefit Transfer), information‑based tools (mandatory labelling, public dashboards like the National Data Sharing and Accessibility Policy), and behavioural nudges (the Swachh Bharat Mission’s community‑led total sanitation approach). The choice of instrument reflects the policy problem’s nature: market failures invite regulation or taxation; information asymmetries call for transparency measures; collective action problems benefit from nudges and community mobilisation.

Institutional architecture for policy making centres on the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA), the Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs (CCPA) and the NITI Aayog’s vertical and horizontal councils. The Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) plays a de facto steering role, especially for flagship schemes (Ayushman Bharat, Jal Jeevan Mission). Inter‑ministerial groups (IMGs) and Empowered Committees resolve cross‑sectoral issues. At the state level, the Chief Minister’s Office and the State Planning Boards (where they exist) perform analogous functions. The 15th Finance Commission’s performance‑based grants incentivise states to adopt outcome‑oriented policy designs.

Evaluation mechanisms remain weak. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) conducts performance audits; the NITI Aayog’s Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO) produces outcome reports for centrally sponsored schemes. The “Output‑Outcome Framework” introduced in the Union Budget 2017‑18 links financial outlays to measurable indicators. However, data gaps, lagged reporting and limited independent evaluation capacity constrain evidence‑based course correction. Strengthening the evaluation ecosystem — through mandatory mid‑term reviews, third‑party audits and a national policy evaluation repository — is a recurring recommendation of the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC, Report 13).

Administrative Reforms: Major Committees, Recommendations and Implementation Status

The First Administrative Reforms Commission (1st ARC), chaired by Morarji Desai (1966‑70), produced 20 reports covering machinery of government, personnel administration, financial administration, centre‑state relations and public grievances. Key recommendations included the creation of a Lokpal (implemented only in 2013 via the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act), a unified civil service cadre for the Centre (partially realised through the Central Staffing Scheme), and the establishment of the Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) in 1964 (pre‑dating the ARC but reinforced by its report).

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC), chaired by Veerappa Moily (2005‑09), submitted 15 reports. Report 1 (Right to Information) catalysed the RTI Act, 2005. Report 5 (Crisis Management) led to the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) and the Disaster Management Act, 2005. Report 10 (Refurbishing of Personnel Administration) recommended a performance‑based promotion system, lateral entry at joint secretary level (implemented from 2018), and a Civil Services Board for transfers (partially adopted). Report 13 (Strengthening Financial Management) underpinned the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) and the Outcome Budget.

Sector‑specific committees have complemented the ARCs. The Hota Committee (2004) on civil services reform advocated a two‑tier recruitment (Foundation + Specialisation), a mandatory cooling‑off period before post‑retirement appointments, and a National Civil Service Capacity Building Framework — largely echoed in Mission Karmayogi. The Y.K. Alagh Committee (2001) on district planning recommended the District Planning Committee (DPC) under Article 243ZD, which remains non‑functional in many states. The Punchhi Commission (2007) on Centre‑State relations suggested a permanent Inter‑State Council secretariat, a clear demarcation of “state subjects” in the Concurrent List, and a flexible fiscal transfer formula — only the GST Council embodies the fiscal recommendation.

Implementation tracking reveals a mixed record. The “Action Taken Reports” (ATRs) tabled in Parliament show that of the 2nd ARC’s 1,500‑odd recommendations, roughly 40 per cent have been fully implemented, 35 per cent partially, and the rest pending. The most persistent gaps are in police reforms (Model Police Act, 2006 not adopted by most states), judicial accountability (Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill lapsed), and local government empowerment (devolution of funds, functions and functionaries — the “3Fs” — remains incomplete). The 15th Finance Commission’s incentive grants for urban and rural local bodies aim to close the fiscal dimension, but functional devolution still awaits state legislation.

E-Governance Architecture: Legal Framework, Platforms and Service Delivery

The legal backbone of e‑governance is the Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended 2008), which grants legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures, defines cyber offences and establishes the Controller of Certifying Authorities. The Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act, 2016 provides statutory authority for the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) and mandates authentication for subsidies. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (enacted after the 2019 Bill) sets out obligations for data fiduciaries, rights of data principals and a Data Protection Board — the first comprehensive privacy statute.

The National e‑Governance Plan (NeGP), launched in 2006, identified 31 Mission Mode Projects (MMPs) across central, state and integrated domains — including MCA21 (corporate affairs), e‑Office (file tracking), e‑Procurement, and the Passport Seva Project. The Digital India programme (2015) restructured NeGP into nine pillars: broadband highways, universal mobile connectivity, public internet access, e‑governance reform, e‑Kranti (electronic delivery of services), information for all, electronics manufacturing, IT for jobs, and early harvest programmes. The UMANG (Unified Mobile Application for New‑age Governance) app aggregates over 1,200 central and state services on a single platform.

Key infrastructure platforms include Aadhaar (biometric identity for 1.3 billion residents), DigiLocker (cloud‑based document wallet with over 150 million registered users), e‑Sign (online electronic signature service), and the Government e‑Marketplace (GeM) for procurement — processing orders worth ₹2 lakh crore annually. The National Knowledge Network (NKN) and BharatNet provide high‑speed connectivity to government institutions and gram panchayats respectively. The Open Government Data Platform (data.gov.in) hosts 5 lakh datasets, enabling data‑driven policy and civic innovation.

Service delivery outcomes are measured through the e‑Governance Assessment Framework (e‑GAF) and the Digital India Index. As of 2023, over 90 per cent of central government services are available online; state‑level penetration varies, with Karnataka, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh leading. Challenges persist: digital divide (rural broadband penetration ~35 per cent), cybersecurity incidents (CERT‑In reported 1.4 million incidents in 2022), interoperability gaps between state portals, and capacity constraints in managing large‑scale IT projects. The 2nd ARC’s Report 11 (Promoting e‑Governance) and the NITI Aayog’s “Strategy for New India @75” emphasise a whole‑of‑government architecture, API‑first design, and a robust grievance redressal layer (CPGRAMS) to sustain citizen trust.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Inter‑State Council’s advisory role with binding adjudication — it cannot enforce decisions.
  • Treating the 1st ARC and 2nd ARC recommendations as fully implemented; many remain pending or diluted.
  • Equating Digital India with mere website creation; the programme’s pillars include broadband, manufacturing and skill development.
  • Overlooking the distinction between policy instruments (legislation, regulation, economic tools) and policy models (rational, incremental, mixed scanning).
  • Assuming the GST Council’s voting weight is equal for Centre and states; the Centre holds one‑third, states two‑thirds.
  • Neglecting the role of the Performance Appraisal Report (APAR) in promotion and treating seniority as the sole criterion.
  • Citing the Lokpal Act as a direct outcome of the 1st ARC without noting the 43‑year gap and the 2nd ARC’s reinforcement.

These advanced sections equip aspirants with the mechanistic depth, institutional detail and examiner‑focused nuances required for high‑scoring answers in Public Administration Paper II.

Public Sector Undertakings: Governance, Autonomy and Accountability

Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) constitute the commercial arm of the state and operate under the Companies Act, 2013 while remaining subject to the administrative control of their respective ministries. The Department of Public Enterprises (DPE) issues the Maharatna, Navratna and Miniratna categorisation, which determines the degree of financial and operational autonomy. A Maharatna firm may invest up to a specified threshold without prior government approval, a Navratna up to a lower limit, and a Miniratna up to a further reduced limit, provided they meet net‑worth, profit and net‑profit‑margin thresholds prescribed in the DPE guidelines (periodically revised).

The Board of Directors, headed by a Chairman‑cum‑Managing Director (CMD), is appointed by the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC) on the recommendation of the Public Enterprises Selection Board (PESB). Independent directors must constitute at least one‑third of the board strength, a requirement reinforced by the Companies Act and the SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015, which also apply to listed PSUs. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) conducts a supplementary audit of PSU accounts under Section 143(6) of the Companies Act, and the reports are laid before Parliament, ensuring legislative oversight.

Accountability mechanisms include the Annual Report laid in Parliament, the Performance Evaluation System (PES) introduced by DPE, and the MoU (Memorandum of Understanding) framework signed each year between the PSU and its administrative ministry. The MoU sets quantitative targets — production, turnover, profit‑before‑tax, capital expenditure, and non‑financial parameters such as R&D spend and safety indices. At year‑end, the DPE grades PSUs as Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair or Poor, linking grades to performance‑related pay for executives.

Strategic disinvestment and privatisation have altered the PSU landscape since the 1991 reforms. The National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP) targets asset monetisation worth a significant outlay over a multi‑year period, while the Strategic Disinvestment Policy identifies sectors where the government will retain a minimum presence (defence, atomic energy, railways). The Air India sale and the ongoing privatisation of BPCL illustrate the shift from minority stake sales to full strategic transfer, raising questions about worker protection, asset valuation methodology, and the role of the Competition Commission of India (CCI) in post‑sale market concentration.

Key challenges remain: (1) dual accountability to the ministry and the market creates conflicting incentives; (2) the PESB selection process has been criticised for delays and lack of transparency; (3) the CAG’s supplementary audit often arrives after the Annual General Meeting, limiting timely corrective action; (4) the MoU targets are sometimes set conservatively to guarantee “Excellent” grades, diluting performance pressure. The 2nd ARC’s Report 12 (Public Sector Undertakings) recommended a statutory PSU Regulator, a unified selection board, and a performance‑linked pay structure aligned with the private sector — recommendations still pending legislative action.

Local Governance: Panchayati Raj and Urban Local Bodies – Functional Devolution and Fiscal Empowerment

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992) institutionalised a three‑tier Panchayati Raj system (Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, Zila Parishad) and three categories of Urban Local Bodies (Municipal Corporation, Municipal Council, Nagar Panchayat). Articles 243G and 243W empower state legislatures to devolve powers, authority and responsibilities relating to 29 subjects (Schedule XI) and 18 subjects (Schedule XII) respectively. However, devolution remains largely on paper; most states have transferred only a subset of functions, and the “3Fs” — funds, functions, functionaries — are rarely devolved in tandem.

Fiscal empowerment rests on three pillars: (i) own‑source revenue (property tax, user charges, trade licences), (ii) assigned revenue (share of state taxes such as stamp duty, motor vehicle tax), and (iii) grants‑in‑aid (State Finance Commission (SFC) recommendations, Central Finance Commission (CFC) grants). The 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) earmarked substantial allocations for rural and urban local bodies, conditional on the publication of audited accounts and the adoption of a property‑tax regime based on unit‑area valuation. Yet, only a minority of states have fully implemented the SFC‑recommended devolution formula, and property‑tax collection efficiency remains low in most municipalities.

Functional devolution is monitored through the Activity Mapping exercise mandated by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj. The mapping classifies each Schedule XI subject into “exclusive”, “concurrent” or “residual” categories for each tier. In practice, critical services — primary health, elementary education, rural water supply — remain under state line departments, with panchayats reduced to implementation agencies without decision‑making authority. The 2nd ARC’s Report 6 (Local Governance) recommended a “single window” for functionary transfer, a mandatory “function‑fund‑functionary” matrix in state budgets, and the creation of a District Planning Committee (DPC) with real planning powers under Article 243ZD. Only a few states such as Kerala, Karnataka, and West Bengal have operationalised DPCs with elected majorities.

Urban governance faces additional complexity: the 74th Amendment provides for Ward Committees (population ≥3 lakh) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPCs) for million‑plus agglomerations. The MPCs, however, are often chaired by the Chief Minister or a senior bureaucrat, limiting genuine metropolitan coordination. The Smart Cities Mission (2015) and AMRUT (2015) introduced project‑based funding, but they bypass the regular fiscal transfer architecture, creating parallel accountability lines. The 15th Finance Commission’s performance‑based grants for urban bodies link a significant portion of the grant to service‑level benchmarks (water supply, solid waste, sanitation), incentivising outcome orientation.

Capacity building remains the weakest link. The National Institute of Rural Development (NIRD) and the All India Institute of Local Self‑Government (AIILSG) conduct training, yet elected representatives — especially women reserved under the quota — receive limited orientation on financial management, procurement rules, and the Right to Information Act. The Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan (RGSA) and the National Urban Livelihoods Mission (NULM) attempt to bridge this gap, but coverage is uneven. Effective local governance therefore hinges on synchronising constitutional mandates, state legislation, fiscal transfers, and administrative capacity — a synchronization that is still a work in progress.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: “Critically examine the extent to which the 73rd and 74th Amendments have achieved functional devolution to local bodies in India. Illustrate with examples.” (15 marks)
Structure the answer in three parts. First, state the constitutional mandate: Articles 243G/243W and Schedules XI/XII list subjects for devolution. Second, present the empirical gap: cite the Activity Mapping data (limited state completion), the SFC‑CFC fiscal devolution shortfall (15th FC conditional grants), and the persistence of line‑department control over health, education, water. Use Kerala’s “People’s Plan Campaign” and Karnataka’s “Sakala” as positive outliers where function‑fund‑functionary alignment was attempted. Third, analyse institutional bottlenecks — lack of independent revenue, delayed elections, bureaucratic resistance, and the parallel scheme architecture (Smart Cities, AMRUT). Conclude with 2nd ARC recommendations (single window, mandatory matrix, empowered DPC) and the 15th FC performance‑grant design as corrective steps. Keep each paragraph focused on a single argument; use data points (grant amounts, number of states) to substantiate.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “critical examination”, allocate roughly 30 per cent of the word limit to constitutional provisions, 50 per cent to implementation gaps with concrete data, and 20 per cent to reform prescriptions. Memorise the 15th Finance Commission grant figures and the number of states that have operationalised District Planning Committees — these are high‑yield facts that differentiate a 12‑mark answer from a 15‑mark answer.

Administrative Ethics and Integrity: Codes, Institutions and Whistleblower Protection

The ethical framework for civil servants is anchored in the Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, and the All India Services (Conduct) Rules, 1968, which prescribe standards of integrity, impartiality, and political neutrality. The 2nd ARC’s Report 4 (Ethics in Governance) recommended a comprehensive Code of Ethics for Ministers, legislators, and civil servants, leading to the draft Public Service Bill, 2007 (lapsed) and the subsequent Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013. The Lokpal, constituted in 2019, has jurisdiction over Group A and B officers of the Centre, including the Prime Minister (with safeguards), and can order preliminary inquiries and investigations through the CBI.

The Central Vigilance Commission (CVC), established by the CVC Act, 2003, exercises superintendence over vigilance administration in central government organisations. It advises on disciplinary proceedings, reviews prosecution sanctions, and monitors the implementation of the “Integrity Pact” in major procurements. The CVC’s “Whistleblower Protection” guidelines (2004) were superseded by the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 (operationalised after rules notification). The Act provides for disclosure of corruption or misuse of power to the Competent Authority (CVC for central government), protects the identity of the complainant, and prescribes penalties for victimisation. However, the Act excludes disclosures related to national security, public order, and matters covered by the Official Secrets Act, 1923 — a limitation criticised by the 2nd ARC and civil‑society groups.

The Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) issues the “Conduct Rules” amendments periodically; a recent amendment introduced a mandatory annual asset declaration for all Group A and B officers, to be filed on the “Public Service Portal”. The “Citizen’s Charter” initiative (1997, revised 2011) obliges each ministry/department to publish service standards, grievance redressal timelines, and responsible officers. The Sevottam model (2006) institutionalises a three‑stage assessment — citizen charter, public grievance redressal, and service delivery capability — with a star‑rating system. As of recent years, a majority of central ministries have achieved a 3‑star rating or above.

State‑level integrity architecture varies. Maharashtra’s Lokayukta (1971) and Karnataka’s Lokayukta (1984) pre‑date the central law and possess suo‑motu investigation powers. The 2nd ARC recommended a uniform Lokayukta law for all states; the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 mandates states to establish Lokayuktas within one year, but several states (e.g., Tamil Nadu, West Bengal) have delayed enactment or diluted powers. The “Right to Service” Acts enacted by many states (starting with Madhya Pradesh, 2010) provide statutory timelines for notified services and appellate mechanisms, complementing the Citizen’s Charter.

Emerging challenges include the “revolving door” phenomenon — senior bureaucrats joining private firms they previously regulated — and the use of “confidential” clauses in contracts to shield procurement decisions from scrutiny. The 2nd ARC’s recommendation for a “Public Service Values” framework, a mandatory cooling‑off period of two years, and a statutory “Ethics Commissioner” remains unimplemented. The recent introduction of the “Integrity Index” by the CVC ranking ministries on vigilance clearance timelines, prosecution sanction rates, and whistleblower case disposal is a step towards data‑driven accountability.

Disaster Management Administration: Institutional Framework, Coordination and Response Mechanisms

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 (DM Act) provides the legal backbone for a three‑tier institutional architecture: the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) chaired by the Prime Minister, State Disaster Management Authorities (SDMAs) chaired by Chief Ministers, and District Disaster Management Authorities (DDMAs) chaired by District Collectors. The NDMA formulates the National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP) — the first plan was released in 2016, revised in 2019 — which adopts a “hazard‑risk‑vulnerability” approach aligned with the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015‑2030).

Funding is channelled through the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) and State Disaster Response Funds (SDRFs). The 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) allocated significant sums to NDRF and SDRFs, with a 75:25 Centre‑state sharing pattern for general states and 90:10 for special category states. The “National Disaster Mitigation Fund” (NDMF) and “State Disaster Mitigation Funds” (SDMFs) were created in 2021 to finance pre‑disaster mitigation projects, marking a shift from ex‑post relief to ex‑ante risk reduction.

Coordination during emergencies is operationalised through the Incident Response System (IRS), adapted from the US Incident Command System. The IRS defines clear roles — Incident Commander, Operations, Planning, Logistics, Finance/Administration — and is mandated for all major disasters. The National Disaster Response Force (NDRF), raised in 2006, comprises multiple battalions trained in search‑and‑rescue, CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear) response, and flood rescue. State Disaster Response Forces (SDRFs) are being raised in most states, but capacity varies widely; many states are still building fully equipped battalions.

Early warning and information dissemination rely on the India Meteorological Department (IMD) for cyclones, the Central Water Commission (CWC) for floods, and the Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS) for tsunamis. The “Common Alerting Protocol” (CAP) based “National Disaster Alert Portal” (2022) integrates these feeds and pushes alerts via SMS, TV, radio, and the “Sachet” mobile app. The 2023 Cyclone Mocha response demonstrated rapid warning dissemination to millions of coastal residents, yet last‑mile evacuation compliance remained suboptimal in Odisha and West Bengal.

Post‑disaster recovery is guided by the “Post‑Disaster Needs Assessment” (PDNA) methodology, jointly developed with the World Bank and UNDP. The 2018 Kerala floods PDNA estimated massive damages; the central government released substantial assistance from NDRF and a special package. The “Build Back Better” principle is embedded in the NDMP, but implementation is hampered by land‑acquisition delays, inter‑departmental coordination gaps, and the absence of a dedicated recovery financing window. The 2nd ARC’s Report 10 (Disaster Management) recommended a statutory “National Disaster Recovery Authority” and a “Disaster Risk Insurance” scheme for vulnerable households — both remain at the policy‑discussion stage.

Public Grievance Redressal Mechanisms: CPGRAMS, Lokpal, and Citizen Charters

The Centralised Public Grievance Redress and Monitoring System (CPGRAMS), launched in 2007 and revamped in 2015, is the primary online platform for citizens to lodge grievances against central government ministries, departments, and attached offices. As of recent data, CPGRAMS hosts over a crore registered grievances, with prescribed disposal timelines for “Category A” (policy‑level) grievances and “Category B” (service‑delivery) grievances. The system integrates with the “PRAGATI” (Pro‑Active Governance and Timely Implementation) platform, where the Prime Minister reviews pending grievances monthly via video conference with secretaries.

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, operationalised in 2019, provides an independent anti‑corruption ombudsman with jurisdiction over public servants (Group A‑D) and elected representatives. The Lokpal can receive complaints directly from citizens, order preliminary inquiries by the CBI or the CVC, and recommend prosecution. By 2023, the Lokpal had received thousands of complaints, disposed of a significant portion, and referred cases for investigation. The Act also mandates each state to establish a Lokayukta with comparable powers; however, most states have functional Lokayuktas, and many lack independent prosecution wings.

Citizen’s Charters, mandated by the 1997 “Conference of Chief Ministers” and reinforced by the Sevottam framework (2006), require every public authority to publish service standards, timelines, and grievance redressal officers. The “Right to Service” Acts in many states provide statutory backing, enabling citizens to appeal to designated appellate authorities if services are not delivered within the stipulated period. The “e‑Office” suite (launched 2009, upgraded 2020) digitises file movement, reducing processing time and enabling real‑time tracking of grievance‑related files.

Performance metrics: the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances (DARPG) publishes an annual “Grievance Redressal Index” ranking ministries on disposal rate, quality of response, and citizen satisfaction (survey‑based). In the recent index, the Ministry of Railways, Ministry of Finance, and Ministry of Health topped the list, while the Ministry of Defence and Ministry of Home Affairs lagged due to security‑classification constraints. The “CPGRAMS 2.0” upgrade (2022) introduced AI‑based categorisation, auto‑routing to the competent authority, and a citizen feedback loop (rating 1‑5). Early data shows a measurable reduction in average disposal time.

Persistent issues: (1) “Grievance fatigue” — citizens re‑file the same complaint across multiple portals (CPGRAMS, state portals, PMO portal), creating duplicate workloads; (2) lack of a unified “One‑Stop” grievance portal for central and state services; (3) limited enforcement of appellate orders under Right to Service Acts; (4) the Lokpal’s inability to initiate suo‑motu inquiries without a formal complaint, unlike the CVC. The 2nd ARC’s Report 12 recommended a “National Grievance Redressal Authority” with statutory powers to monitor both central and state mechanisms — a proposal yet to be legislated.

  • Assuming the 73rd/74th Amendments automatically transferred all Schedule XI/XII functions — most states have devolved only a subset.
  • Treating Maharatna/Navratna status as permanent — categorisation is reviewed periodically and can be downgraded.
  • Confusing the CVC’s advisory role with adjudicatory power — it cannot impose penalties, only recommend.
  • Believing the Whistle Blowers Protection Act covers all disclosures — national security and Official Secrets Act matters are excluded.
  • Equating the NDMA’s planning role with operational command — the Incident Commander at the district level directs response.
  • Assuming CPGRAMS disposal time reflects substantive resolution — many grievances are closed with “information provided” without remedial action.
  • Overlooking the distinction between Lokpal’s jurisdiction (central) and Lokayukta’s (state) — they are separate statutory bodies with different composition rules.

Performance Budgeting and Outcome‑Based Monitoring in Central Ministries

Performance budgeting was formally introduced in the Union Budget 2005‑06 through the “Outcome Budget” document, requiring each ministry to link financial outlays to measurable outputs and outcomes. The Department of Expenditure issues Performance Budgeting Guidelines prescribing a three‑tier framework: (1) input‑level allocations, (2) output indicators (physical targets such as kilometres of road constructed), and (3) outcome indicators (impact metrics such as reduction in travel time or increase in agricultural productivity). The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) audits the Outcome Budget under its statutory mandate, and its reports are tabled in Parliament. Since 2017, the “Output‑Outcome Monitoring Framework” (OOMF) has been integrated with the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) to enable real‑time tracking of scheme‑level physical progress against financial releases. NITI Aayog’s Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO) conducts independent evaluations of flagship programmes (e.g., Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana, Jal Jeevan Mission) and feeds findings back into the budget cycle. A key institutional mechanism is the quarterly performance review chaired by the Secretary of the concerned ministry; significant deviations typically trigger a corrective action plan to be submitted to the Department of Expenditure within a stipulated period. The 2022‑23 budget introduced climate‑relevant tagging alongside the existing Gender Budgeting tags within the Outcome Budget, requiring ministries to report gender‑disaggregated and climate‑relevant outcomes. Persistent challenges include: (a) weak baseline data for many social sector schemes, (b) the tendency to treat output targets as ends in themselves, and (c) limited capacity of line ministries to design robust outcome indicators. The 2nd ARC (Report 5, Public Administration) recommended a statutory framework to give legal force to performance budgeting; the proposal remains under inter‑ministerial consultation.

Social Audit and Citizen Report Cards as Accountability Instruments

Social audit emerged as a statutory accountability tool under the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) 2005, which mandates a Social Audit Unit at the district level to conduct periodic audits of works, wage payments and muster rolls. The audit process follows a structured methodology covering Gram Sabha notification, verification of records, physical verification of assets, beneficiary interviews, public reading of findings, report preparation, submission to the District Programme Coordinator, follow‑up action, and uploading on the MGNREGA MIS portal. The CAG’s 2018 performance audit of MGNREGA highlighted that a substantial proportion of districts did not complete the mandated audits, and follow‑up on irregularities was initiated in a minority of cases. Citizen Report Cards (CRCs), pioneered by the Public Affairs Centre (Bangalore) in 1993, were later adapted by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs for the Swachh Survekshan and by the Ministry of Health for National Health Mission facility surveys. A CRC typically uses a structured questionnaire covering access, quality, reliability, responsiveness and corruption perception, administered to a statistically representative sample of service users. Results are scored on a 0‑100 scale and published as a report card for each urban local body or health facility. A national CRC framework issued by the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances (DARPG) has standardised methodology, sampling design and reporting templates across central schemes. Evidence from a 2019 CRC of the Public Distribution System in Odisha indicated measurable improvement in timeliness of grain delivery after the district administration restructured Fair Price Shop timings based on the findings. Limitations include: (i) dependence on civil‑society facilitators for social audits, leading to uneven quality; (ii) risk of “elite capture” in Gram Sabhas where dominant groups suppress dissent; (iii) CRCs often capture perception rather than objective service standards, requiring triangulation with administrative data. The 2nd ARC (Report 12, Citizen Centric Administration) recommended institutionalising a National Social Audit Authority with statutory powers to enforce follow‑up, a proposal yet to be legislated.

Answer‑Writing Technique
“Explain how Social Audit under MGNREGA and Citizen Report Cards complement each other in strengthening accountability. Illustrate with one concrete example from each.”
Social Audit is a legally mandated, community‑driven verification of expenditure and asset creation under MGNREGA, producing a formal audit report that triggers administrative action. Citizen Report Cards are perception‑based surveys that rate service delivery across dimensions such as access and corruption, providing a quantitative scorecard for comparative benchmarking. Together they cover both “process compliance” (Social Audit) and “user experience” (CRC). Example 1: A Social Audit in Kalahandi district, Odisha, uncovered fictitious muster‑roll entries, leading to recovery and disciplinary action against officials. Example 2: A CRC for the Public Distribution System in Odisha revealed improved timeliness after the district administration reshuffled Fair Price Shop hours based on the findings. The answer should structure the response: (i) define each instrument, (ii) contrast their legal basis and methodology, (iii) show complementarity, (iv) give the two examples with outcomes, (v) conclude with the need for institutional integration as recommended by the 2nd ARC.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “accountability mechanisms”, pair a statutory tool (Social Audit) with a non‑statutory, perception‑based tool (CRC). Use the MGNREGA audit steps and the CRC scoring scale as ready‑made structures. Cite the 2nd ARC Report 12 recommendation for a National Social Audit Authority to show awareness of reform debates.

Lateral Entry and Performance Appraisal in the Higher Civil Services

The Government of India introduced lateral entry at the Joint Secretary level in 2018 through a UPSC advertisement inviting domain experts from the private sector, academia and public sector undertakings for select ministries. The selection process comprises a written test, a presentation on a policy problem, and an interview by a panel headed by the UPSC Chairman. As of 2023, over 30 lateral entrants have been appointed across ministries such as Finance, Commerce, Agriculture, and Skill Development. The Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) issued Performance Appraisal Report (PAR) Guidelines for Lateral Entrants in 2020, aligning the appraisal cycle with the existing Annual Performance Appraisal Report (APAR) used for regular IAS officers. The PAR evaluates three dimensions: (1) Domain Competence (technical knowledge, innovation), (2) Leadership and Delivery (project outcomes, stakeholder management), and (3) Integrity and Ethics (adherence to conduct rules, conflict‑of‑interest declarations). A key difference is the higher weightage assigned to domain competence for lateral entrants. A 360‑Degree Feedback mechanism, piloted in 2021 for the Department of Economic Affairs, incorporates inputs from subordinates, peers, and external partners. The 2nd ARC (Report 10, Personnel Administration) recommended a unified Performance Management System linking appraisal to career progression, training needs, and incentive structures; the DoPT’s Mission Karmayogi (launched 2020) operationalises this through the iGOT Karmayogi platform, which hosts competency frameworks, e‑learning modules, and a digital PAR dashboard. Challenges documented in the 2022 Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel report include: (a) cultural friction between career bureaucrats and lateral entrants, (b) absence of a clear exit policy for under‑performing lateral appointees, and (c) limited transparency in the selection criteria for the written test. The committee suggested a statutory framework to define tenure, performance benchmarks, and grievance redressal for lateral officers.

Regulatory Governance: Independent Regulators, Quasi‑Judicial Powers and Judicial Review

India’s regulatory architecture comprises sector‑specific independent regulators — Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI, 1997), Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC, 1998), Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI, 1992), Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI, 1999), and the Competition Commission of India (CCI, 2003). Each regulator is established by an Act of Parliament, enjoys functional autonomy (budgetary independence, fixed tenure for chairpersons and members), and exercises quasi‑judicial powers: adjudicating disputes, issuing directions, imposing penalties, and framing regulations. For instance, TRAI’s Telecom Tariff Orders are binding on service providers and can be challenged only before the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT). CERC determines tariffs for inter‑state transmission and generation, and its orders are appealable to the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL). SEBI’s adjudicating officers can impose substantial monetary penalties under the SEBI Act, 1992. The Supreme Court, in SEBI v. Sahara India Real Estate Corp. (2012), affirmed SEBI’s preventive regulatory mandate. Judicial review of regulatory decisions follows the Wednesbury unreasonableness standard and the proportionality test; courts intervene only when a decision is arbitrary, mala fide, or exceeds statutory jurisdiction. The 2nd ARC (in its reports on organisational structure and financial management) recommended a Unified Regulatory Governance Framework prescribing common appointment procedures, a mandatory Regulatory Impact Assessment (RIA) for major regulations, and a Regulatory Oversight Committee in Parliament. RIA guidelines issued by the Ministry of Finance require cost‑benefit analysis, stakeholder consultation, and a sunset‑clause review every five years. Implementation remains uneven: only a few regulators have institutionalised RIA units, while others rely on ad‑hoc consultations. The National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA, 2018) adds a meta‑regulatory layer for auditing standards, illustrating the trend toward multi‑tiered oversight.

Administrative Tribunals: Structure, Jurisdiction and the Doctrine of Exhaustion of Remedies

The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, enacted under Article 323‑A of the Constitution, established the Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) with benches across the country and State Administrative Tribunals (SATs) in several states. CAT adjudicates service matters of central government employees — recruitment, promotion, seniority, disciplinary proceedings, and retirement benefits — while SATs handle analogous matters for state government employees. The tribunals are headed by a Chairman (a retired High Court judge) and comprise judicial and administrative members. Their decisions are appealable to the High Court under Article 227 (supervisory jurisdiction) and, in limited cases, to the Supreme Court under Article 136 (special leave). The doctrine of exhaustion of alternative remedies requires a litigant to approach the tribunal before invoking the High Court’s writ jurisdiction, unless the challenge is to the constitutional validity of the enabling statute or the tribunal’s jurisdiction is ousted. In Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010), the Supreme Court held that the CAT’s expertise in service law justifies the exhaustion rule, but clarified that the rule does not apply when the tribunal’s order is patently illegal or violates principles of natural justice. The 2021 Tribunal Reforms Act rationalised the tribunal landscape by abolishing certain appellate tribunals (e.g., the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal) and transferring their functions to High Courts, while strengthening CAT’s infrastructure through the e‑CAT platform for e‑filing, video‑conferencing hearings, and digital case management. CAT continues to face substantial pendency. The 2nd ARC (Report 8, Administrative Tribunals) recommended: (i) a uniform tenure of five years for tribunal members, (ii) a mandatory Judicial Impact Assessment before creating new tribunals, and (iii) a National Tribunal Commission to oversee appointments, budgets, and performance metrics. The government has accepted the tenure recommendation but the Commission remains a proposal.

  • Confusing the Outcome Budget with the Performance Budget — the former links outlays to outcomes, the latter details input‑output plans.
  • Assuming Social Audit findings are automatically binding — they require administrative follow‑up; no statutory enforcement mechanism exists yet.
  • Treating lateral entry as a permanent replacement for the regular cadre — it is a supplementary, fixed‑tenure mechanism.
  • Believing all regulators have identical quasi‑judicial powers — penalty ceilings, appeal forums, and rule‑making authority differ by statute.
  • Overlooking the exhaustion doctrine’s exceptions — constitutional challenges and patent illegality bypass the tribunal route.
  • Equating the 2nd ARC’s recommendations with implemented law — many remain at the policy‑discussion stage.
  • Ignoring the distinction between CAT’s central jurisdiction and SATs’ state jurisdiction — they operate under separate statutes and appeal paths.
