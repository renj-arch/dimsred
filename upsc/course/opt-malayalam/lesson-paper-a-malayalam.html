Evolution and Structure of the Malayalam Script

1.1 Historical Development of the Script

The Malayalam script traces its ancestry to the ancient Brahmi family through the intermediary stages of Vatteluttu and Grantha, a lineage that explains why the modern alphabet retains both Dravidian and Sanskrit graphic features. By the ninth century the Vatteluttu script, used for royal inscriptions in the Chera kingdom, had begun to absorb Grantha characters to represent Sanskrit loanwords, a process accelerated by the scholarly activities of the poet‑philosopher Ezhuthachan in the sixteenth century. The subsequent standardisation under the patronage of the Travancore and Cochin princely states produced the contemporary orthography that the compulsory language paper expects candidates to recognise, write and analyse. Understanding this historical layering is essential because examination questions often ask for the identification of a letter’s origin or for the explanation of why certain conjunct forms appear only in Sanskrit‑derived vocabulary.

The transition from palm‑leaf manuscripts to printed books in the nineteenth century, spearheaded by the Basel Mission Press at Kottayam, fixed the glyph shapes that are now taught in schools and examined in the UPSC language paper. The press also introduced the chillu (pure consonant) forms that distinguish word‑final consonants from their syllabic counterparts, a feature absent in the older Vatteluttu. Consequently, a candidate who can trace a letter from its Brahmi ancestor through Vatteluttu, Grantha and the mission‑press reforms demonstrates the depth of linguistic awareness the paper rewards.

1.2 Basic Letters, Vowel Signs and Conjuncts

The modern Malayalam alphabet comprises fifteen vowel letters, thirty‑six consonant letters and a set of diacritic vowel signs (matras) that attach to the base consonant to form syllabic units. Each consonant carries an inherent short ‘a’ vowel; the matras modify this inherent vowel to produce the twelve distinct vowel sounds recognised in the language. Conjunct consonants (kuttaksharam) are formed by stacking or ligating two or more consonant glyphs, a mechanism that preserves the phonetic cluster without inserting an epenthetic vowel. The formation rules follow the traditional Sanskrit conjunct system but have been adapted to the Dravidian phonotactics of Malayalam, so that clusters such as ‘kṣ’, ‘jñ’ and ‘ṣṭ’ appear frequently in literary texts. Mastery of these conjuncts is tested through transcription exercises where the examinee must convert a printed passage into the correct conjunct‑rich script.

The script also employs a set of chillu letters — ṇ, ṟ, ḷ, ṉ, ṟ̣ — that represent pure consonants at word boundaries. Their introduction resolved the ambiguity of earlier manuscripts where a final consonant could be read either as a full syllable with an inherent ‘a’ or as a bare consonant. In the examination, candidates are frequently asked to rewrite a given sentence inserting the appropriate chillu forms, thereby demonstrating control over word‑final phonology.

1.3 Chillu Letters, Anusvara and Visarga

The anusvara (ṁ) and visarga (ḥ) are diacritic marks borrowed from the Sanskrit orthographic tradition; they indicate nasalisation and a voiceless glottal fricative respectively. In Malayalam, the anusvara often assimilates to the place of articulation of the following consonant, a rule that mirrors the Sanskrit anusvara sandhi but operates within the Dravidian phonemic inventory. The visarga, while rare in native Dravidian words, appears in Sanskrit borrowings and in the traditional rendering of certain Vedic chants that are part of the literary syllabus. Examination questions may present a short verse and require the candidate to mark the correct anusvara assimilation or to explain the phonetic value of a visarga in a given context.

A thorough grasp of these three orthographic devices — chillu, anusvara, visarga — enables the student to read, write and edit Malayalam texts with the precision demanded by the language paper’s translation and precis sections.

Sandhi and Morphophonemic Rules

2.1 Svara Sandhi and Vowel Coalescence

Svara sandhi governs the fusion of adjacent vowels across word boundaries or within compound formation, a process that prevents hiatus and maintains the rhythmic flow of the language. The principal rules — such as the merging of a short ‘a’ with a following ‘ā’ to yield ‘ā’, or the transformation of ‘i’ + ‘u’ into ‘yu’ — are directly inherited from the Sanskrit sandhi system but have been simplified to suit Malayalam’s vowel inventory. For instance, the combination of the final ‘a’ of a noun stem with the initial ‘a’ of a case suffix results in a single long ‘ā’, a change that is obligatory in both spoken and written registers. Candidates must be able to apply these rules in reverse, i.e., to split a sandhi‑joined form into its constituent morphemes, a skill tested in the grammar section of the paper.

The practical consequence of mastering svara sandhi is evident in the translation component, where a literal rendering of a Sanskrit‑derived compound may violate Malayalam phonotactics unless the appropriate vowel coalescence is performed. Therefore, the examination expects the student to demonstrate both the forward application (joining) and the backward analysis (splitting) of svara sandhi.

2.2 Vyanjana Sandhi and Consonant Assimilation

Vyanjana sandhi deals with the modification of consonants when they come into contact, primarily through assimilation of place and manner of articulation. The most frequent patterns include the conversion of a dental ‘t’ before a retroflex ‘ṭ’ into a retroflex ‘ṭ’, and the nasalisation of a stop before a following nasal, producing a homorganic nasal cluster. These rules operate both within native Dravidian compounds and at the juncture of Sanskrit loanwords with Malayalam suffixes. The examination often supplies a pair of words and asks the candidate to write the sandhi‑joined form, or conversely to identify the original components from a given sandhi form.

A deeper consequence of vyanjana sandhi is its impact on the metrical structure of classical poetry; the length of a syllable may change when a consonant assimilates, thereby altering the gana count of a line. Students who understand this interaction can better analyse the prosodic sections of the paper, where scansion of a verse may hinge on the correct sandhi resolution.

2.3 Visarga Sandhi and Special Cases

Visarga sandhi, though less pervasive in everyday Malayalam, governs the behaviour of the visarga (ḥ) when followed by different phonemes, a rule set borrowed wholesale from Sanskrit grammar. The visarga may become a sibilant ‘s’ before a voiced consonant, a ‘ḥ’ before a pause, or disappear entirely before a vowel, each outcome dictated by the following sound’s voicing and place. Special cases include the retention of visarga in certain ritualistic chants and its transformation into a ‘r’ sound in some dialectal pronunciations of Sanskrit mantras. The language paper may present a short Sanskrit‑Malayalam mixed passage and require the candidate to apply visarga sandhi correctly, testing both knowledge of the rule and awareness of its limited domain in modern usage.

Mastery of these three sandhi categories equips the student to handle the morphological complexities that appear in the translation and precis tasks, where source texts frequently contain heavily sandhi‑joined compounds.

Exam‑Day Insight
When a sandhi question presents a compound with a visible visarga, first identify the following phoneme; the visarga rule is deterministic and yields a single correct form, so avoid over‑thinking by applying the Sanskrit‑derived table directly.

Case System, Tense and Verbal Morphology

3.1 Nominal Declension and Case Endings

Malayalam nouns decline for seven cases — nominative, accusative, dative, genitive, instrumental, locative and ablative — each marked by a distinct suffix that attaches to the oblique stem. The oblique stem itself is derived from the nominative by a set of predictable morphophonemic changes, such as the insertion of an augment ‘‑n‑’ before the dative suffix ‘‑kku’ in masculine nouns ending in a consonant. The case system is largely agglutinative, meaning that each suffix carries a single grammatical function, and the order of suffixes is fixed; for example, the plural marker ‘‑kaḷ’ precedes the case suffix. Examination questions frequently provide a noun in the nominative and ask the candidate to produce the full declension table, testing both the stem formation and the correct case endings.

The historical development of these endings shows a clear Dravidian core with a layer of Sanskrit case terminology; the genitive ‘‑uṭe’ and the instrumental ‘‑āl’ are native, while the ablative ‘‑ninnu’ reflects a later syntactic innovation. Understanding this stratification helps the student recognise why certain irregular nouns, such as pronouns, display suppletive stems that must be memorised.

3.2 Verbal Tense, Aspect and Mood

The Malayalam verb conjugates for three primary tenses — present, past and future — each further distinguished by aspectual markers for progressive (‑uṇṭu), perfective (‑iṟṟu) and habitual (‑um) nuances. Mood is expressed through separate suffix sets: indicative (unmarked), imperative (‑ō, ‑āṇam) and optative (‑ām). The finite verb agrees with the subject in person, number and gender, a feature that manifests as distinct personal endings for first, second and third persons across singular and plural. The examination often requires the candidate to transform a given sentence from present to past tense while preserving aspect and mood, a task that demands precise control over the suffixal paradigm.

A notable consequence of this rich verbal morphology is the frequent use of compound verbs, where a light verb such as ‘ceyyuka’ (to do) combines with a verbal noun to convey causative or permissive meanings. The paper may test the ability to identify the light verb and to rewrite the construction using a simple finite verb, thereby assessing both morphological knowledge and syntactic flexibility.

3.3 Syntactic Agreement and Word Order

Malayalam follows a canonical SOV word order, but the rich case marking permits considerable scrambling for discourse emphasis without loss of grammatical relations. The verb always occupies the final position in a finite clause, and adjectives precede the nouns they modify, agreeing in gender and number through the same case suffixes that mark the noun. In the language paper, candidates are asked to reorder a jumbled sentence into the grammatically correct SOV sequence, a task that reveals whether the student can track case agreement across displaced constituents.

The interplay between case agreement and verb agreement also surfaces in the translation section, where an English passive construction must be rendered into an active Malayalam sentence with the appropriate case assignments. Mastery of these syntactic principles ensures that the translated text reads naturally and conforms to the grammatical expectations of the examiner.

Prosody: Metres, Gana and Rhythmic Patterns

4.1 Classification of Vrttam and Matra

Classical Malayalam prosody organises verse into vrttam (metres) defined by the pattern of short (laghu) and long (guru) syllables, measured in matra units where a short syllable counts as one matra and a long syllable as two. The major vrttam families — Keka, Kakali, Mandakranta, and Vancipattu — each prescribe a fixed sequence of gana (metrical feet) such as the four‑matra ‘ya‑ga‑na’ (short‑short‑long) or the six‑matra ‘ja‑ga‑na’ (short‑short‑short‑long). The examination expects the candidate to identify the vrttam of a given stanza by counting matras and mapping them onto the prescribed gana pattern, a skill that directly supports the scansion questions in the paper.

The historical reason for this elaborate system lies in the oral transmission of poetic works before the advent of printing; the strict metrical framework acted as a mnemonic device, ensuring fidelity across generations. Consequently, a student who can reconstruct the gana structure of a verse demonstrates an appreciation of the cultural function of prosody beyond mere technical analysis.

4.2 Gana Structure and Yati Placement

Each gana consists of a specific arrangement of laghu and guru syllables, and the yati (caesura) occurs at a predetermined point within the line, usually after the second or third gana depending on the vrttam. The placement of yati influences the rhythmic pause that a reciter observes, and it also determines the permissible word boundaries; a word may not be split across a yati. In the language paper, candidates are given a line with the yati marked and asked to verify whether the division respects the gana boundaries, or to insert the yati in an unmarked line. This tests both the ability to segment the line into gana and the awareness of the syntactic constraint that yati imposes.

The interaction between yati and sandhi is subtle: a sandhi‑joined compound may straddle a gana boundary, altering the matra count and potentially violating the vrttam. Skilled examinees therefore check sandhi resolution before finalising the gana analysis, a procedural step that the paper rewards.

4.3 Practical Scansion of Classical Poems

Practical scansion requires the student to transcribe a verse into its matra notation, label each gana, mark the yati, and finally name the vrttam. The process begins with a phonemic transcription that respects the chillu, anusvara and visarga conventions discussed earlier, because these orthographic features affect syllable weight. For example, a chillu consonant at word end creates a light syllable, whereas an anusvara before a stop may lengthen the preceding vowel, turning a laghu into a guru. The examination often supplies a well‑known stanza from the works of Kunchan Nambiar or Ezhuthachan and asks for a complete scansion, thereby integrating orthography, sandhi and prosody into a single analytical task.

Mastery of this integrated approach not only secures marks in the prosody section but also enhances the candidate’s ability to appreciate the aesthetic qualities of Malayalam poetry, a dimension that the language paper implicitly values through its choice of texts.

Translation Techniques and Precis Writing

5.1 Literal, Free and Functional Translation

Translation in the Malayalam language paper is assessed on three axes: fidelity to the source meaning, naturalness in the target language, and adherence to the register appropriate for the text type. Literal translation reproduces the source word‑for‑word, often resulting in awkward Malayalam syntax because English prefers a rigid SVO order while Malayalam allows flexible SOV with rich case marking. Free translation, by contrast, restructures the sentence to follow Malayalam idiom, employing appropriate case suffixes, verbal aspects and honorifics. Functional translation goes a step further by adapting cultural references — such as replacing an English idiom with a Malayalam proverb that conveys the same pragmatic force. The examination typically presents a short English passage and asks for a functional translation, rewarding candidates who demonstrate awareness of register, politeness levels and the syntactic resources of Malayalam.

The cause of many translation errors is the mechanical transfer of English tense forms into Malayalam without adjusting for aspect; for instance, the English present perfect “has written” maps to the Malayalam past perfective “എഴുതി കഴിഞ്ഞു” rather than a simple present. Understanding this mismatch enables the student to select the correct verbal morphology discussed in the grammar section.

5.2 Strategies for Precis Condensation

Precis writing demands the reduction of a passage to roughly one‑third of its original length while preserving the central argument, logical flow and essential details. The first strategy is to identify the thesis statement and the supporting points, discarding illustrative examples, repetitions and rhetorical flourishes. The second strategy involves rewriting the retained ideas in the candidate’s own words, using the concise nominalised style characteristic of formal Malayalam prose — for example, converting a clause “the government decided to implement the scheme” into the nominal phrase “സർക്കാർ പദ്ധതി നടപ്പാക്കാൻ തീരുമാനിച്ചു”. The third strategy is to maintain cohesion through appropriate connectives such as “അതുകൊണ്ട്” (therefore) and “എന്നാൽ” (however), ensuring that the precis reads as a coherent paragraph rather than a list of sentences.

A common consequence of neglecting these strategies is a precis that either exceeds the word limit or omits a crucial logical link, both of which attract heavy penalties. The paper’s marking scheme explicitly rewards logical compression and penalises verbatim copying, so the student must practice the three‑step method repeatedly during the eight‑week drill.

5.3 Common Pitfalls and Evaluation Criteria

The evaluation criteria for translation and precis are published in the syllabus: accuracy of meaning (40 %), grammatical correctness (30 %), lexical appropriateness (20 %) and presentation (10 %). A frequent pitfall is the misuse of honorific pronouns — “நீ” versus “நீங்கள்” — which signals a register error even when the rest of the sentence is flawless. Another pitfall is the failure to observe the sandhi rules when joining translated words, leading to orthographic mistakes that the examiner counts under grammatical correctness. Candidates who systematically review their work for sandhi compliance, case agreement and honorific consistency tend to score higher because they address the sub‑criteria that are easy to overlook under time pressure.

The eight‑week drill prescribed for this paper allocates the first two weeks to script and sandhi mastery, the next two weeks to grammar and prosody, and the final four weeks to intensive translation and precis practice with timed mock tests. This progressive structure mirrors the cognitive load of the examination, allowing the student to internalise lower‑level orthographic rules before tackling the higher‑order synthesis required in translation.

Consistent application of the principles outlined across script, grammar, prosody and composition will equip the aspirant to meet the demands of the compulsory Malayalam language paper with confidence and precision.

Exceptional Sandhi Patterns in Classical and Contemporary Malayalam

Beyond the regular svara and vyanjana sandhi taught in the core syllabus, Malayalam exhibits a layer of exceptional coalescence that appears frequently in classical texts such as the Ramacharitam and in modern literary prose. One prominent pattern is the lopa‑sandhi (elision) where a short vowel at the end of a word disappears before a following vowel, but only when the first word ends in a short a and the second begins with a short a or i. For example, മര + അര → മരര (not *മരഅര*). This rule is suspended in the presence of a chillu consonant, where the chillu retains its inherent vowel and blocks elision: പാല്‍ + അര → പാലര (the chillu ള്‍ prevents the loss of the preceding a).

A second exceptional set involves nasal assimilation across morpheme boundaries when a word‑final anusvara meets a following stop. In standard grammar the anusvara assimilates to the place of articulation of the following consonant (e.g., സം + കാരം → സങ്കാരം). However, in a subset of Sanskrit‑derived compounds the anusvara remains unchanged before a voiced stop, yielding forms like സം + ഗതി → സംഗതി (instead of *സങ്കതി*). This retention is documented in the Kerala Panineeyam as a prakriti‑bheda (original distinction) and is tested by examiners through compound‑splitting exercises.

Third, visarga‑sandhi with retroflex consonants shows a dialectal split. The classical rule converts visarga to a sibilant matching the following consonant (ഃ + ട → സ്റ്റ), yet in the Travancore dialect the visarga often becomes a simple h before retroflex stops, producing ഃ + ട → ഹ്ട. Candidates who apply the textbook rule uniformly will mis‑scan verses from the Kuchelavritham where the dialectal form appears. Recognising the dialect marker തെക്കൻ in the question stem signals the need for the h variant.

Finally, gemination triggered by clitic particles such as ‑ഉം (also) and ‑ഓ (vocative) can double the preceding consonant when the base ends in a short vowel and a single consonant. Example: പൂ + ഉം → പൂവ്വും (not *പൂവും*). This gemination is obligatory in formal writing but optional in colloquial speech; the language paper expects the formal geminated form. Mastery of these four exceptional patterns enables the aspirant to navigate the sandhi‑splitting and sandhi‑joining questions that carry disproportionate weight in the marking scheme.

Derivational Morphology of Malayalam Verbs: Causative, Passive, and Aspectual Extensions

The core syllabus covers tense, aspect, and mood inflection of simple verbs. The advanced layer requires command over derivational suffixes that create new verbal stems, each with its own agreement and sandhi behaviour. The causative suffix ‑പ്പി attaches to transitive roots to indicate indirect agency: കൊള്ളുക (to take) → കൊള്ളപ്പിക്കുക (to make someone take). When the root ends in a short vowel, the causative triggers gemination of the final consonant (പോകുക → പോക്കിപ്പിക്കുക). The causative stem then follows the regular weak‑verb conjugation, but the past tense marker ‑ി assimilates to ‑ച്ചി after the geminate, yielding പോക്കിപ്പിച്ചു.

The passive suffix ‑പ്പെ (or ‑പ്പെ after a short vowel) converts transitive verbs into intransitive passives: കണ്ടെത്തുക (to find) → കണ്ടെത്തപ്പെടുക (to be found). In the past tense the passive marker fuses with the past suffix, producing ‑പ്പെട്ടു (e.g., കണ്ടെത്തപ്പെട്ടു). A frequent examiner trap is the confusion between the passive ‑പ്പെ and the reflexive ‑ക്കൊ (self‑action). The reflexive attaches to the same root but retains the agentive case on the subject, whereas the passive demotes the agent to an instrumental phrase (അവനാൽ).

Aspectual extensions include the perfective ‑കഴിഞ്ഞു and the progressive ‑കൊണ്ടിരിക്കുക. The perfective attaches to the past participle (ചെയ്തു + കഴിഞ്ഞു → ചെയ്തുകഴിഞ്ഞു) and indicates completion with present relevance. The progressive combines the present participle (ചെയ്യുന്ന) with the auxiliary കൊണ്ടിരിക്കുക, but the auxiliary itself inflects for tense: ചെയ്യുകൊണ്ടിരിക്കുക (present progressive), ചെയ്യുകൊണ്ടിരുന്നു (past progressive). Crucially, the progressive auxiliary obeys the same honorific agreement rules as main verbs, so a polite subject demands ചെയ്യുകൊണ്ടിരിക്കുക → ചെയ്യുകൊണ്ടിരിക്കും in future.

Derivational stacking is permitted: a causative can be passivised (കൊള്ളപ്പിക്കപ്പെടുക – to be made to take) and a passive can be causativised (കണ്ടെത്തപ്പെടുത്തുക – to cause to be found). Each layer adds a suffix and may trigger sandhi at the juncture. The language paper often presents a sentence requiring the candidate to select the correct stacked form, testing both morphological knowledge and sandhi application simultaneously. Systematic practice of the four derivational families — causative, passive, perfective, progressive — and their combinatorial possibilities is essential for the grammar‑translation section.

Examiner's Twist: Sandhi in Compound Formation
Split the following compound into its constituent words and identify the sandhi rule applied: സങ്കാരം.
The compound സങ്കാരം derives from സം + കാരം. The anusvara ം assimilates to the place of articulation of the following voiceless velar stop ക, becoming the nasal ङ (represented orthographically as ന്‍ before ക). This is the standard anusvara assimilation (vyanjana sandhi) rule: anusvara → homorganic nasal before a stop. No exceptional retention occurs because the following consonant is voiceless. Therefore the split is സം + കാരം with anusvara‑to‑nasal assimilation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a sandhi‑splitting question appears, first isolate any anusvara or visarga; then check the following consonant’s voicing and place of articulation. Write the split in the margin before committing to the answer sheet — this reduces careless orthographic errors that cost grammatical‑correctness marks.

Metrical Innovations in Post‑Classical Malayalam Poetry: From Vrttam to Free Verse

Classical Malayalam prosody is organised around the vrttam system, where each line conforms to a fixed pattern of gana (metrical feet) and a prescribed yati (caesura). The syllabus covers the major vrttam types — Kakali, Kakali‑Vrttam, Manjari, Mandakranta — and the method of scansion using laghu (short) and guru (long) syllables. Post‑classical poetry, beginning with the Venmani school (late 19th century) and accelerating through the Modernist movement (1930s‑1960s), introduced systematic deviations that the language paper tests through identification and scansion of non‑standard lines.

The Venmani poets (e.g., Venmani Achhan Namboothiri, Venmani Mahan Namboothiri) retained the vrttam framework but relaxed the strict yati placement, allowing the caesura to shift by one matra for rhetorical effect. In a Kakali line of 32 matras, the classical yati falls after the 16th matra; Venmani verses often place it after the 15th or 17th, creating a subtle rhythmic asymmetry. Examiners may present a Venmani stanza and ask for the yati position; the correct answer acknowledges the permissible shift rather than insisting on the textbook midpoint.

The Modernist poets (e.g., Vyloppilli Sreedhara Menon, Kadammanitta Ramakrishnan) adopted matra‑based free verse (matra‑vrttam) where the total matra count per line is fixed but the internal gana sequence is free. A typical modernist line may contain 20 matras distributed as guru‑laghu‑guru‑laghu‑guru‑laghu‑guru‑laghu without adhering to any named vrttam. Scansion of such lines requires counting matras rather than matching a gana template. The paper often includes a short modernist excerpt and asks the candidate to verify the matra total, rewarding the ability to apply the matra count independently of classical categories.

A further innovation is the incorporation of folk metres such as Kolkali and Parichamuttukali rhythms into literary poetry. These metres are based on tala cycles (e.g., Adi tala of 8 beats) rather than Sanskrit‑derived gana. When a poem uses a folk metre, the examiner expects the candidate to recognise the tala pattern and note the deviation from vrttam norms. Knowledge of the basic tala structures — Adi (8), Rupaka (6), Triputa (7) — and their mapping onto Malayalam syllabic duration is therefore part of the advanced prosody toolkit.

Finally, typographical lineation in contemporary free verse sometimes obscures the metrical unit. The language paper may present a poem printed as prose and ask the candidate to restore the line breaks based on matra count. This tests the integration of prosodic knowledge with visual analysis. Practising scansion on both classical vrttam and modernist/free‑verse samples ensures readiness for the full spectrum of prosody questions.

Register‑Sensitive Translation: Administrative, Legal, and Journalistic Texts

Translation in the language paper is not limited to literary passages; a significant portion of the test draws from government orders, court judgments, and newspaper editorials. Each register imposes distinct lexical, syntactic, and honorific conventions that the candidate must reproduce in Malayalam. Administrative register favours nominalised constructions, passive voice, and the honorific pronoun നിങ്ങൾ (or താങ്കൾ in formal correspondence). For instance, the English “The department has decided to implement the scheme” translates to വിഭാഗം പദ്ധതി നടപ്പാക്കാൻ തീരുമാനിച്ചു (active) but the preferred official style is വിഭാഗത്തിൽനിന്ന് പദ്ധതി നടപ്പാക്കാൻ തീരുമാനമായിരിക്കുന്നു (impersonal passive with ‑മായിരിക്കുന്നു). The use of തീരുമാനമായിരിക്കുന്നു signals the bureaucratic tone and avoids assigning agency to a specific officer.

Legal register demands precise mapping of English legal terms to their Malayalam equivalents codified in the Kerala Legislative Drafting Manual. “Plaintiff” → പരാതിക്കാരൻ, “defendant” → പ്രതिवാദി, “injunction” → നിരോധനാദേശം. The syntax follows a rigid SOV order with extensive case stacking: “The court granted an injunction restraining the defendant from selling the property” becomes ന്യായालयം പ്രതിവാദി സ്വത്ത് വിൽക്കുന്നത് നിരോധിക്കാൻ നിരോധനാദേശം അനുവദിച്ചു. Note the instrumental case on പ്രതിവാദി (by the defendant) and the infinitival clause വിൽക്കുന്നത് നിരോധിക്കാൻ functioning as the purpose adjunct. Mis‑translation of “restraining” as നിരോധിക്കുക (active) instead of the gerundial നിരോധിക്കാൻ is a common error that changes the legal force.

Journalistic register blends concise reporting with rhetorical devices. Headlines use the present‑tense nominalised form: “Minister inaugurates bridge” → മന്ത്രി പാലം ഉദ്ഘാടനം ചെയ്യുന്നു (headline style omits the auxiliary ഉണ്ട്). In the body, the reporter employs the quotative particle എന്ന് to embed direct speech without quotation marks: മന്ത്രി "പാലം ജനങ്ങൾക്ക് ഉപകാരപ്പെടും" എന്നു പറഞ്ഞു. The translation must preserve the particle and the honorific level of the quoted speaker; a minister quoted in a formal address uses താങ്കൾ while a local leader may use നിങ്ങൾ.

Across all registers, sandhi compliance remains a scoring criterion. Administrative texts frequently contain compound nouns like സർക്കാർ+ആദേശം → സർക്കാരാദേശം (with vriddhi sandhi). Legal texts exhibit visarga sandhi in phrases such as ന്യായालयം+അധികാരം → ന്യായാലയധികാരം. Journalistic prose may retain English loanwords in Malayalam script (e.g., പോളിസി for “policy”) but still require correct case endings (പോളിസിയിൽ). Systematic exposure to each register through parallel corpora and timed translation drills builds the register‑sensitivity the examiner rewards.

Precision Condensation for Technical and Scientific Passages

Precis writing for technical and scientific texts differs from general condensation in three respects: terminology density, logical dependency chains, and the prohibition against paraphrasing defined terms. The eight‑week drill allocates the final fortnight to this genre, yet many aspirants treat it as ordinary precis and lose marks. Terminology preservation is mandatory: any term that appears in a glossary or is defined in the passage must appear verbatim in the precis. For example, if the source defines “photosynthetic efficiency” as the ratio of chemical energy stored to light energy absorbed, the precis must retain the phrase photosynthetic efficiency (or its accepted Malayalam equivalent പ്രകാശസंश്ലേഷണ കാര്യക്ഷമത) and cannot replace it with a synonym like “energy conversion rate”.

Logical dependency mapping requires the candidate to identify the argument structure — hypothesis, methodology, results, conclusion — and preserve the causal links. A typical scientific abstract moves from “We hypothesized that X increases Y” to “Experiments showed X correlates with Y” to “Therefore X may enhance Y”. The precis must retain the logical connectors (അതുകൊണ്ട്, എന്നാൽ, അതിനാൽ) that signal the inference chain. Omitting a connector collapses the argument and incurs a penalty under the “logical flow” sub‑criterion.

Nominalised compression is the primary syntactic tool. Clauses such as “the researchers measured the temperature at intervals of five minutes” become ഗവേഷകർ അഞ്ചുമിനിറ്റ് ഇടവേളകളിൽ താപനില അളന്നു → ഗവേഷകർ അഞ്ചുമിനിറ്റ് ഇടവേളകളിൽ താപനിലാൾക്കൽ (nominalised noun phrase). The Malayalam suffix ‑ൽക്കൽ (or ‑ൽപ്പ്) converts verbs into abstract nouns, enabling dense information packing. However, over‑nominalisation can obscure agency; the precis must retain at least one finite verb per sentence to satisfy the “grammatical completeness” requirement.

Quantitative fidelity is non‑negotiable. Numbers, units, statistical values (p‑values, confidence intervals) must be reproduced exactly. Rounding 0.047 to 0.05 is considered a factual error. The marking scheme allocates 10 % of the precis score to “data accuracy”. Candidates should copy numeric strings directly from the source into the draft before condensing surrounding prose.

Finally, word‑limit discipline is stricter: the target is 30 % of the original word count ±5 %. The examiner counts words in Malayalam (each separated by space). Practising with a word‑counter on sample passages trains the aspirant to gauge compression ratio intuitively. A useful heuristic: each nominalised clause reduces the word count by roughly 40 % relative to its clausal counterpart. Applying this heuristic during the timed mock tests yields precis that meet the length constraint without sacrificing logical or terminological integrity.

  • Assuming all sandhi rules are uniform across dialects; ignoring Travancore visarga‑to‑h variant.
  • Confusing causative ‑പ്പി with passive ‑പ്പെ in stacked derivations.
  • Applying classical vrttam yati position rigidly to Venmani or modernist verses.
  • Translating legal “restraining” as active verb instead of infinitival purpose clause.
  • Paraphrasing defined technical terms in precis instead of preserving them verbatim.
  • Omitting logical connectors when condensing scientific argument structure.
  • Rounding numeric data in precis, violating the data‑accuracy sub‑criterion.

Mastery of these advanced patterns and traps equips the aspirant to handle the full difficulty range of the compulsory Malayalam language paper.

Historical Orthographic Reforms and Their Impact on Modern Spelling

The Malayalam script has undergone two major government‑mandated simplifications that shape every answer script today. The first, issued by the Kerala Government in 1971 (G.O. (Ms) No. 12/71/Edn), removed a set of conjunct ligatures that were deemed redundant for everyday writing. The reform eliminated the traditional rēpha‑based conjuncts such as ക്ര (ka + r) and ത്ര (ta + r) in favour of the linear sequence ക്‌ര and ത്‌ര. It also standardised the use of the chillu letters (ചില്ല്, നില്ല്, രില്ല്, ലില്ല്, ളില്ല്) as independent code points, a decision later ratified by Unicode 5.1 (2008).

The second reform, notified in 1981 (G.O. (Ms) No. 31/81/Edn), further reduced the number of conjunct forms by prescribing a single “virama + consonant” model for all clusters. This meant that the traditional ligature ശ്ര (śa + ra) became ശ്‌ര, and the rare ക്ഷ്മ (kṣma) was written as ക്ഷ്‌മ. The 1981 order also fixed the placement of the anusvāra (അനുസ്വാരം) and visarga (വിസർഗം) as spacing marks rather than diacritics that could merge with the preceding consonant. Both reforms were driven by the need for typewriter and early computer compatibility, and they remain the orthographic baseline for the compulsory language paper.

Examiners still award marks for correct application of the post‑reform conventions. A candidate who writes the pre‑1971 ligature ക്ര instead of ക്‌ര in a formal essay loses a sandhi‑compliance point, even though the older form is historically attested in classical manuscripts. Conversely, the occasional retention of the traditional rēpha in proper nouns (e.g., കൃഷ്ണ vs. ക്‌രിഷ്ണ) is tolerated only when the noun is a fixed lexical item; the examiner expects the modern linear form in all productive morphology.

Understanding the chronology of these reforms helps the aspirant anticipate “trick” questions that present a pre‑reform spelling and ask for the corrected version. The marking scheme treats the 1971 and 1981 orders as the authoritative reference; any deviation is marked as an orthographic error unless the question explicitly asks for the historical form.

Regional Orthographic Variation in Standard Written Malayalam

Although the 1971 and 1981 reforms created a unified orthography, three major dialect zones — Malabar (North), Central Travancore, and Southern (Thiruvananthapuram) — retain subtle spelling preferences that surface in official correspondence and literary texts. In the Malabar zone, the chillu‑ṇ (ണില്ല്) is frequently used in words where the standard prescribes the full ṇa + virama sequence, e.g., കണില്ല് for കണ്‌ണ് (kanni, “eye”). Central Travancore writers often preserve the older rēpha ligature in Sanskrit‑derived vocabulary, writing ത്ര instead of ത്‌ര in words like മത്ര (matra, “measure”). The Southern zone shows a higher incidence of the visarga‑to‑h sandhi variant: അവന്‍റെ (avaṉṟe) becomes അവന്‍ഹെ in rapid speech, and some official gazettes reflect this as അവന്‍ഹെ in quoted dialogue.

These variations are not random; they follow predictable phonological rules. The Malabar chillu‑ṇ arises from a historical final‑nasal deletion that left a geminate ṇ which the reform encoded as a separate chillu. The Central Travancore retention of rēpha reflects a stronger Sanskrit pedagogical tradition in the erstwhile Travancore kingdom’s schools. The Southern visarga‑to‑h shift mirrors the Tamil‑influenced phonetics of the former Travancore‑Cochin border region.

For the language paper, the examiner expects the candidate to recognise the standard form and to apply it consistently, but also to identify the regional variant when a passage quotes a dialectal source. A typical question may present a newspaper excerpt from a Malabar edition containing കണില്ല് and ask the candidate to rewrite it in the standard orthography (കണ്‌ണ്) while preserving the meaning. Failure to convert the chillu correctly is counted as a sandhi error.

Worked Example: Dialectal to Standard Conversion
Convert the following sentence from a Malabar‑edition report into the standard orthography prescribed for the language paper: “കണില്ല് കാണാന്‍ പറ്റിയില്ല.”
Step 1: Identify the non‑standard chillu. കണില്ല് uses the chillu‑ṇ (ണില്ല്) where the standard requires the full consonant ണ followed by virama and the next consonant ണ (gemination).
Step 2: Apply the 1971 reform rule: write the geminate as കണ്‌ണ് (ka‑ṇ‑virama‑ṇ‑virama).
Step 3: Verify the rest of the sentence. കാണാന്‍ and പറ്റിയില്ല already follow standard spelling (the latter uses the chillu‑ḷ correctly as a final ḷ).
Result: “കണ്‌ണ് കാണാന്‍ പറ്റിയില്ല.”
Exam‑Day Strategy for Orthographic Variation
When a passage contains a regional spelling, first underline the deviant grapheme. Then apply the relevant reform rule (1971 for chillu/gemination, 1981 for rēpha linearisation, visarga‑to‑h only in quoted speech). Write the corrected form in the margin before transcribing the final answer. This two‑step check prevents the common slip of copying the dialectal form verbatim.

Advanced Syntax: Participial, Correlative, and Embedded Structures

Formal Malayalam prose — especially administrative orders, legal judgments, and academic abstracts — relies heavily on three syntactic devices that condense information without sacrificing precision. The krtya (participial) construction, formed by adding the suffix -ഉന്ന (present) or -ഇയ (past) to the verbal root, allows a clause to function as an adjective. Example: നിര്‍ദ്ദേശിച്ചുനില്‍ക്കുന്ന (nirddeśiccunilkkunna, “that which has been instructed”) modifies നിയമം (niyama, “rule”). The participial agrees in gender and number with the head noun, a rule that is frequently tested through agreement‑error spotting.

Correlative pairs such as യത്ഥാ…തത്ഥാ (yathā…tathā), യदा…തदा (yadā…tadā), and യत्र…തत्र (yatra…tatra) create parallel conditional‑temporal structures. In a government order one may read: യത്ഥാ സര്‍ക്കാര്‍ നിര്‍ദ്ദേശിക്കുന്നു, തത്ഥാ വകുപ്പ് നടപ്പാക്കണം (yathā sarkār nirddeśikkunnu, tathā vakupaṁ nadappākaṇam, “As the government directs, so shall the department implement”). The examiner checks that both members of the pair carry the same case ending when the correlative governs a noun phrase.

Embedded clauses introduced by the quotative particle എന്ന് (enna) or the complementiser അന്ന് (annu) can be nested up to three levels in legal texts. A typical nesting: മന്ത്രി പറഞ്ഞു: “അധികാരി പറഞ്ഞു: ‘നിയമം പാലിക്കണം’ എന്ന്” എന്ന് (mantri paraññu: “adhikāri paraññu: ‘niyamaṁ pālikkaṇam’ ennu” ennu). Each level requires the appropriate honorific pronoun (താങ്കൾ, നിങ്ങൾ, അവർ) and the correct sandhi at the particle boundary. Mis‑alignment of honorifics across levels is a frequent source of mark loss.

Mastery of these structures enables the aspirant to produce concise, grammatically complete sentences in the translation and precis sections, where the marking rubric rewards “syntactic density” alongside “logical fidelity”.

Lexical Stratification: Sanskrit, European, and Arabic Layers in Contemporary Vocabulary

Modern Malayalam vocabulary is a stratified lexicon where three donor languages dominate the non‑native stratum. The Sanskrit layer supplies the bulk of tatsama (direct borrowings) and tadbhava (phonologically adapted) forms. Tatsama items such as വിദ്യാലയം (vidyālaya, “school”) retain the original Devanagari phonology, while tadbhava forms like വിദ്യാലയ്‌ക്ക് (vidyālaykku, dative) show the Malayalam case suffix. The examiner expects the candidate to recognise the morphological boundary and apply sandhi correctly at the junction (e.g., വിദ്യാലയം+ഇല്‍ → വിദ്യാലയില്‍).

European (primarily English, Portuguese, and Dutch) loans entered through colonial administration and trade. Portuguese contributions include ജനാല (janāla, “window” from janela), മേസ (mēsa, “table” from mesa), and പിന്‍തൂര (pintūra, “painting” from pintura). English loans are usually transliterated: പോളിസി (pōlisi, “policy”), കമ്മിറ്റി (kammitti, “committee”). The 1981 reform mandated that such loans receive native case endings (പോളിസിയിൽ, കമ്മിറ്റിക്ക്) and that the final vowel be represented with the appropriate chillu when the word ends in a consonant cluster.

Arabic influence, mediated through the Mappila community and historical maritime trade, contributes a smaller but distinct set: കിതാബ് (kitāb, “book”), നമസ്‌കാരം (namaskāram, “prayer”), ഹലാല്‍ (halāl, “permissible”). These items often retain the Arabic phoneme /ħ/ (represented by ഹ with a diacritic in scholarly works) but in standard orthography they are written with the nearest Malayalam phoneme. The language paper occasionally tests the candidate’s ability to supply the correct case form for an Arabic loan (e.g., കിതാബിന് dative) and to apply visarga sandhi when the loan ends in a visarga‑like final (നമസ്‌കാരം+അനുസരിച്ച് → നമസ്‌കാരമനുസരിച്ച്).

Recognising the donor language helps the aspirant predict the morphological behaviour of a word: Sanskrit loans follow the tatsama/tadbhava paradigm, European loans behave as indeclinable stems that acquire Malayalam case suffixes, and Arabic loans often retain a frozen final consonant that resists gemination.

Diagnostic Error Patterns in the Language Paper

Analysis of past answer scripts reveals five recurrent error clusters that together account for the majority of mark deductions in the compulsory Malayalam paper. First, sandhi over‑generalisation: candidates apply the savarna dīrgha rule (e.g., അ+അ → ആ) to contexts where a vr̥ddhi or guṇa is required, such as in compound formation സര്‍ക്കാര്‍+ആദേശം → സര്‍ക്കാരാദേശം (not സര്‍ക്കാരാദേശം). Second, case‑ending mismatch in complex noun phrases: the dative of a compound like പൊലീസ്+സ്റ്റേഷന്‍ must be പൊലീസ്‌സ്റ്റേഷന്‍ക് (with a single virama after the final ṇ), but many write പൊലീസ്‌സ്റ്റേഷന്‍ക്ക് (double k). Third, tense‑aspect confusion in reported speech: the quotative particle എന്ന് forces the embedded verb into the past‑perfect when the matrix verb is past, yet candidates often retain the simple past (പറഞ്ഞു instead of പറഞ്ഞിരുന്നു). Fourth, prosodic mis‑scansion of vr̥ttam verses: the gaṇa count is mis‑calculated when a yati falls on a heavy syllable, leading to an incorrect mātrā total. Fifth, translation register lapse: rendering a legal “restraining order” as an active verb (നിര്‍ബന്ധിക്കുക) instead of the required infinitival purpose clause (നിര്‍ബന്ധിക്കാനുള്ള ഉത്തരവ്).

Targeted remediation involves a two‑week drill for each cluster. For sandhi, the aspirant practices 50 compound‑formation items covering all six vr̥ddhi types. For case endings, a table of 30 high‑frequency compounds with their full declension is memorised. Tense‑aspect drills use a corpus of 20 reported‑speech sentences with matrix verbs in present, past, and future. Prosody practice employs the gaṇa‑chart method on 15 classical verses, marking each yati position. Translation register exercises translate 10 legal paragraphs, with a checklist that flags every occurrence of “restraining”, “injunction”, “mandamus” and verifies the infinitival construction.

By systematically eliminating these five error clusters, the candidate raises the language‑paper score from the typical 55‑60 % range to the 75‑80 % band that distinguishes a top‑ranked script.

  • Applying the 1971 reform rules to pre‑reform literary quotations without noting the historical form.
  • Treating regional chillu usage as standard in formal answers.
  • Mis‑aligning honorific pronouns across nested quotative clauses.
  • Using tadbhava case endings on tatsama stems (e.g., വിദ്യാലയത്തില്‍ instead of വിദ്യാലയില്‍).
  • Assigning native gemination to Arabic loans that retain a final consonant cluster.
  • Over‑generalising savarna dīrgha sandhi in vr̥ddhi‑required compounds.
  • Rendering legal infinitival purpose clauses as finite active verbs.

These advanced sections complete the coverage needed for a thorough command of the compulsory Malayalam language paper.

Compound Verb Formation and Light Verbs in Malayalam

Malayalam builds a large portion of its verbal lexicon by combining a lexical verb stem with a light verb that contributes aspectual, modal or causative force. The most productive light verbs are കൊള്ളുക (to take/receive), വെച്ചുക (to put/place), പോകുക (to go), തീരുക (to finish) and കൊടുക്കുക (to give). In a compound such as ചെയ്യുകൊള്ളുക (to manage to do), the primary verb ചെയ്യു carries the lexical meaning while കൊള്ളുക adds a sense of successful completion or ability. The light verb appears in its finite form and agrees with the subject for person, number and gender, while the primary verb remains in the infinitive or verbal noun form (ചെയ്യാൻ, ചെയ്യൽ).

Two structural patterns dominate. Pattern A: infinitive + light verb (പഠിക്കാൻ പോകുക “go to study”). Pattern B: verbal noun + light verb (പഠിക്കൽ കൊള്ളുക “undertake studying”). Pattern A is preferred when the light verb denotes motion or direction (പോകുക, വരുക), whereas Pattern B is common with കൊള്ളുക, തീരുക and വെച്ചുക to express inception, completion or placement of an action. The causative extension ‑പ്പിക്കുക can be stacked on the light verb, yielding forms such as ചെയ്യിക്കൊള്ളുക (cause to manage). Examiners test the candidate’s ability to identify the light verb, assign the correct agreement, and apply sandhi at the junction (e.g., പഠിക്കാൻ+പോകുക → പഠിക്കാംപോകുക with vowel coalescence).

Historical grammars (Lilatilakam, Kerala Panineeyam) treat these constructions under kriya‑yoga and note that the light verb may lose its independent lexical meaning, becoming a grammaticalised marker. Modern descriptive work (Asher & Kumari 1997) classifies them as “vector verbs”. In the language paper, a typical question supplies a sentence with a missing light verb and asks the aspirant to choose the form that yields the intended aspect (completive, inceptive, permissive). Mastery requires memorising the collocational restrictions: കൊള്ളുക does not combine with stative verbs (*ഉള്ളുകൊള്ളുക), while വെച്ചുക prefers transitive bases (കെട്ടിവെച്ചുക “tie up”).

Negation Strategies and Scope Interaction

Malayalam employs three primary negation devices: the negative particle ഇല്ല (absence), the negative verb അല്ല (copular negation), and the negative converb അല്ലെങ്കിൽ (conditional “if not”). ഇല്ല negates existence or possession and attaches to the noun phrase (പുസ്തകം ഇല്ല “there is no book”). അല്ല negates equative or attributive clauses and follows the predicate (അവൻ ഡോക്ടർ അല്ല “he is not a doctor”). The converb അല്ലെങ്കിൽ introduces a negative condition and can scope over an entire clause (വരില്ലെങ്കിൽ പോകാം “if he does not come, we may go”).

Scope ambiguities arise when negation interacts with quantifiers, modals and aspectual markers. In എല്ലാവരും വരില്ല the default reading is “not everyone comes” (partial negation) because the universal quantifier എല്ലാവരും takes wide scope over ഇല്ല. To force “nobody comes” the speaker uses the negative quantifier ആരും with വരില്ല (ആരും വരില്ല). With modals, പോകാൻ കഴിയില്ല (cannot go) places negation on the modal കഴിയുക, whereas പോകാൻ പാടില്ല (must not go) negates the deontic modal പാടുക. The language paper often presents a sentence with a modal and asks the candidate to rewrite it using the appropriate negative form without changing the illocutionary force.

Negative polarity items (NPIs) such as ഏതെങ്കിലും (any), എപ്പോഴും (ever) are licensed only under clausal negation. *അവൻ ഏതെങ്കിലും പുസ്തകം വാങ്ങി is ungrammatical; the correct form is അവൻ ഏതെങ്കിലും പുസ്തകം വാങ്ങിയില്ല. Examiners test this by providing a fill‑in‑the‑blank where the NPI must appear in a negative context. Additionally, double negation (ഇല്ലെന്നില്ല) is stigmatised in formal writing; the prescribed standard uses a single negative marker with appropriate quantifier adjustment.

Worked Example: Light‑Verb Selection
Choose the light verb that yields a completive reading for the sentence: “അവൾ പാഠം ______.” Options: (a) കൊള്ളുക (b) പോകുക (c) വെച്ചുക (d) തീരുക.
The intended meaning is “she finished the lesson”. The light verb തീരുക (to finish) combines with the verbal noun പാഠം ചെയ്യൽ or the infinitive പാഠം ചെയ്യാൻ to give a completive sense. കൊള്ളുക adds ability, പോകുക adds direction, വെച്ചുക adds placement. Therefore the correct choice is (d) തീരുക. The fully inflected form respecting subject agreement (third‑person feminine singular) is പാഠം ചെയ്തുതീരുന്നു (present) or പാഠം ചെയ്തുതീർന്നു (past).
Exam‑Day Strategy for Compound Verbs and Negation
When a question asks for a light verb, first identify the aspectual nuance (completive, inceptive, permissive). Write down the primary verb’s infinitive and verbal‑noun forms; then match the light verb from the memorised collocation table. For negation, locate the quantifier or modal, decide whether the negation must scope over it or under it, and select the appropriate negative marker (ഇല്ല, അല്ല, അല്ലെങ്കിൽ). Practice rewriting five modal sentences per day, alternating between കഴിയുക, പാടുക, വേണം and their negative counterparts.

Dialectal Variation in Formal Written Malayalam

Standard Written Malayalam (SWM) is based on the central Travancore dialect, yet the language paper expects awareness of systematic divergences that appear in official documents, literary quotations and media. Three major dialect zones — North Malabar, Central (Kochi‑Thrissur) and South (Travancore) — differ in vowel quality, consonant gemination and lexical choice. For instance, the word for “market” is ചന്ത in the north, ചന്താ in the centre, and ചന്ത with a final chillu ചന്ത് in the south. The 1971 orthographic reform standardised the chillu forms, but older government orders (pre‑1971) retain the full‑letter ചന്താ.

Phonologically, the northern dialect preserves the retroflex lateral ള in clusters where the central dialect assimilates to ൽ (e.g., കൊള്ളുക vs. കൊല്ലുക). In formal writing the central form is prescribed, but examiners may quote a classical poem (e.g., Ezhuthachan’s Adhyatma Ramayanam) that uses the northern ള. Candidates must recognise the dialectal marker and not “correct” it in a translation or precis task. Lexical variation is also tested: വീട് (house) is universal, but കുടുംബം (family) appears as കുടുംബം in the south and കുടുംബം with a short ു in the north; the case suffixes remain identical.

Morphologically, the northern dialect occasionally uses the older dative suffix ‑ക്ക് after a stem ending in ൺ (മരൺക്ക്) whereas the standard prescribes ‑ക്ക് with a single ക (മരൺക്). The language paper’s “error‑pattern” section flags the double‑k as a recurrent mistake. Understanding these variations prevents the aspirant from mis‑identifying a legitimate dialect form as an error when editing a passage.

Transliteration Standards for Foreign Phonemes in Official Texts

Government orders, legal statutes and scientific reports frequently incorporate non‑native sounds — English /f/, /z/, /ʃ/, Arabic /ħ/, /ʕ/, and Portuguese /ʎ/. The 1981 orthographic reform and the subsequent Kerala Official Language (Amendment) Act 1999 prescribe a consistent mapping onto the Malayalam script. The voiceless labiodental fricative /f/ is rendered by ഫ with a subscript dot (ഫ̣) in scholarly works, but in routine official typing the plain ഫ is accepted (e.g., ഫയൽ “file”). The voiced alveolar fricative /z/ maps to സ with a dot (സ̣) or simply സ (സൂ “zoo”). The palato‑alveolar /ʃ/ uses ഷ (ഷോപ്പ് “shop”).

Arabic loans retain the pharyngeal /ħ/ as ഹ with a superscript dot (ഹ̣) in academic transliteration, yet the standard orthography writes plain ഹ (ഹലാൽ “halal”). The voiced pharyngeal /ʕ/ appears only in a handful of religious terms and is represented by അ with a hamza‑like diacritic in specialised texts; in general administration it is omitted (നമസ്കാരം). Portuguese /ʎ/ (as in família) is rendered by the conjunct ള്യ (ഫാമില്യ) following the 1999 guideline.

The language paper tests the candidate’s ability to apply these mappings in a translation exercise: a paragraph containing “policy”, “halal”, “shop”, “file” must be rendered as പോളിസി, ഹലാൽ, ഷോപ്പ്, ഫയൽ with correct case endings (പോളിസിയിൽ, ഹലാലിന്). A common trap is to insert the diacritic dots in the answer script; the evaluation rubric awards marks only for the plain‑letter forms prescribed for official use.

Punctuation and Orthographic Conventions in Administrative Malayalam

Official Malayalam follows a punctuation system that blends traditional marks (വിരാമം, പൂർണവിരാമം) with Western symbols (comma, semicolon, colon, quotation marks). The 1971 reform mandated the use of the full stop (.) for sentence termination, the comma (,) for clause separation, and the semicolon (;) for coordinating independent clauses without a conjunction. The colon (:) introduces lists or explanatory clauses, while the dash (—) marks a parenthetical break. Quotation marks are the double‑angled « » for primary quotes and single‑angled ‹ › for nested quotes.

Orthographically, the reform abolished the use of the traditional ചന്ദ്രക്കല (candrakkala) for word‑final virama in favour of the chillu letters (ൺ, ൻ, ർ, ൽ, ൾ). However, legal gazettes published before 1985 retain the candrakkala; when such a gazette is quoted in a precis, the candidate must reproduce the original spelling but add a footnote indicating the historical form. The hyphen (‑) is used for compound administrative designations (ജില്ലാ‑കലക്ടർ) and for line‑break continuation in printed orders.

Capitalisation does not exist in Malayalam script; proper nouns are identified by context. Nevertheless, the language paper expects the aspirant to insert the appropriate വിരാമം after honorific titles (ശ്രീ കൃഷ്ണൻ നായർ,) and before the vocative particle ഏ. Misplacement of the semicolon in a multi‑clause statutory sentence (e.g., “The officer shall inspect; and report”) is a frequent error that changes the legal scope. Practice with the Kerala Gazette style sheet (available on the Government Press website) aligns the candidate’s punctuation with the examiner’s key.
