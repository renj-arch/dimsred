1. Atomic Structure and Quantum Mechanical Foundations

1.1 The Quantum Mechanical Model of the Atom

The quantum mechanical model of the atom replaced the Bohr model because the latter could not explain the spectra of multi-electron atoms or the fine structure of spectral lines. Erwin Schrödinger formulated a wave equation in 1926 that treats the electron as a standing wave, described by a wave function ψ whose square ψ² gives the probability density of finding the electron in a given region of space. This probabilistic interpretation, proposed by Max Born, means that the electron does not follow a definite orbit but occupies an orbital, a three-dimensional region where the probability of finding the electron is high. The Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom yields exact solutions characterized by three quantum numbers: the principal quantum number n, the azimuthal quantum number l, and the magnetic quantum number mₗ. A fourth quantum number, the spin quantum number mₛ, was introduced by George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit in 1925 to account for the splitting of spectral lines in a magnetic field, known as the Zeeman effect. The Pauli exclusion principle, formulated by Wolfgang Pauli in 1925, states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers, which limits each orbital to a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins. This principle underpins the entire structure of the periodic table and the chemical behaviour of elements.

1.2 Quantum Numbers and Electronic Configuration

The principal quantum number n determines the main energy level or shell of an electron and takes positive integer values 1, 2, 3, and so on. The azimuthal quantum number l defines the shape of the orbital and ranges from 0 to n−1, with l = 0, 1, 2, 3 corresponding to s, p, d, and f orbitals respectively. The magnetic quantum number mₗ specifies the orientation of the orbital in space and takes integer values from −l to +l, giving 2l+1 orbitals per subshell. The spin quantum number mₛ can be +½ or −½. Electrons fill orbitals according to the Aufbau principle, which states that orbitals are filled in order of increasing (n+l) value, and for equal (n+l), the orbital with lower n fills first. This order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p. Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity requires that electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing begins, minimizing electron-electron repulsion. The electronic configuration of an element determines its position in the periodic table, its valency, and its chemical reactivity. For instance, the configuration of chromium is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² because a half-filled d subshell provides extra stability due to exchange energy, a quantum mechanical effect arising from the indistinguishability of electrons with parallel spins.

1.3 Periodic Classification and the Long Form Table

The modern periodic table, often called the long form, arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number Z, which is the number of protons in the nucleus. This arrangement, proposed by Henry Moseley in 1913 based on X-ray spectra, corrected the anomalies of Mendeleev's table which was based on atomic weights. The table consists of 18 vertical groups and 7 horizontal periods. Elements in the same group have similar valence shell electronic configurations and therefore exhibit similar chemical properties. The s-block comprises groups 1 and 2, the p-block groups 13 to 18, the d-block groups 3 to 12, and the f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides placed separately at the bottom. Period 1 has 2 elements, periods 2 and 3 have 8 each, periods 4 and 5 have 18 each, period 6 has 32 including the lanthanides, and period 7 is incomplete but would have 32 including the actinides. The block classification reflects the subshell being filled: ns for s-block, np for p-block, (n−1)d for d-block, and (n−2)f for f-block. This structure allows the prediction of trends in atomic radius, ionization enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, and electronegativity across periods and down groups, which are the foundation for understanding chemical periodicity.

2. Periodic Trends and the Chemistry of s-Block Elements

2.1 Periodic Trends in Atomic and Ionic Properties

Atomic radius generally decreases across a period from left to right because the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same principal shell, resulting in a greater effective nuclear charge experienced by the valence electrons. Down a group, atomic radius increases because a new principal shell is added, placing the valence electrons farther from the nucleus and increasing shielding. Ionic radius follows similar trends: cations are smaller than their parent atoms because the loss of electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and often removes an entire shell, while anions are larger because added electrons increase repulsion without increasing nuclear charge. Ionization enthalpy, the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from a gaseous atom, increases across a period due to increasing effective nuclear charge and decreases down a group due to increased atomic size and shielding. However, anomalies occur: the first ionization enthalpy of boron is lower than that of beryllium because the electron removed from boron comes from a 2p orbital which is higher in energy and more shielded than the 2s electron of beryllium. Similarly, oxygen has a lower first ionization enthalpy than nitrogen because the paired electrons in one 2p orbital of oxygen experience greater repulsion. Electron gain enthalpy becomes more negative across a period as the tendency to accept an electron increases, but the electron gain enthalpy of fluorine is less negative than that of chlorine because the small size of fluorine leads to high electron-electron repulsion in the compact 2p subshell. Electronegativity, a measure of an atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a covalent bond, follows the Pauling scale where fluorine is the most electronegative element at 4.0. These trends dictate the nature of bonding, the acid-base character of oxides, and the redox behaviour of elements.

2.2 Anomalous Behaviour of Lithium and Beryllium

Lithium and beryllium, the first elements of groups 1 and 2 respectively, show anomalous behaviour compared to the rest of their groups due to their exceptionally small atomic and ionic sizes, high charge densities, and the absence of low-lying d orbitals. Lithium is harder and has a higher melting point than other alkali metals; it forms a stable nitride Li₃N directly with nitrogen, whereas other alkali metals do not. Lithium carbonate Li₂CO₃ decomposes on heating to Li₂O and CO₂, while carbonates of other alkali metals are thermally stable. Lithium hydroxide is a weaker base than other alkali metal hydroxides, and lithium fluoride is sparingly soluble in water due to high lattice energy, unlike other alkali halides. Beryllium differs from other alkaline earth metals in forming predominantly covalent compounds, such as BeCl₂ which is a polymeric chain in the solid state but a linear monomer in the vapour phase. Beryllium oxide and hydroxide are amphoteric, dissolving in both acids and bases to give beryllates [Be(OH)₄]²⁻, whereas oxides and hydroxides of other group 2 elements are basic. Beryllium does not react with water even at high temperatures, and its carbide Be₂C yields methane on hydrolysis, unlike calcium carbide which yields ethyne. These anomalies arise because the high charge density of Li⁺ and Be²⁺ polarizes anion electron clouds strongly, introducing covalent character into nominally ionic compounds, a phenomenon described by Fajans' rules.

2.3 Diagonal Relationships and Hydrogen Chemistry

A diagonal relationship exists between certain pairs of elements in the second and third periods, notably lithium-magnesium, beryllium-aluminium, and boron-silicon. This similarity arises because moving down a group increases size while moving across a period decreases size; the two effects partially cancel diagonally, giving similar charge densities. Lithium and magnesium both form nitrides and oxides on heating in air, their carbonates decompose on heating, their hydroxides are sparingly soluble, and both form organometallic compounds with covalent character. Beryllium and aluminium both form amphoteric oxides and hydroxides, their carbides yield methane on hydrolysis, and both form complexes such as [BeF₄]²⁻ and [AlF₆]³⁻. Boron and silicon are both semiconductors, form covalent network solids, and their oxides B₂O₃ and SiO₂ are acidic. Hydrogen occupies a unique position in the periodic table; it resembles alkali metals in having one valence electron and forming H⁺, and halogens in needing one electron to complete its shell and forming H⁻. Its three isotopes—protium (¹H), deuterium (²H or D), and tritium (³H or T)—differ in neutron number. Deuterium oxide D₂O, heavy water, has a higher boiling point (374.4 K) and density (1.107 g cm⁻³) than H₂O and is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors. Hydrogen forms ionic hydrides with s-block elements (e.g., NaH), covalent hydrides with p-block elements (e.g., CH₄, NH₃), and metallic or interstitial hydrides with d-block elements (e.g., TiH₂). The hydrogen bond, a strong dipole-dipole interaction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, F) and another electronegative atom, is responsible for the anomalously high boiling points of water, ammonia, and hydrogen fluoride, and for the structure of DNA and proteins.

2.4 Chemistry of Group 1 and Group 2 Elements

The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) are highly reactive, low-density metals with a single ns¹ valence electron. Their reactivity increases down the group as ionization enthalpy decreases. They react vigorously with water to form hydroxides MOH and hydrogen gas, with the reaction becoming more violent down the group. Their oxides M₂O, peroxides M₂O₂, and superoxides MO₂ show a trend: lithium forms the oxide, sodium forms the peroxide, and potassium, rubidium, and caesium form superoxides, reflecting the stabilization of larger anions by larger cations. All alkali metal salts are generally soluble in water except lithium fluoride and some double salts. The alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) have ns² configuration and are less reactive than alkali metals. Their reactivity with water increases down the group: beryllium does not react, magnesium reacts with hot water, and calcium, strontium, and barium react readily with cold water. The solubility of their hydroxides increases down the group, while the solubility of their sulfates and carbonates decreases. This is because lattice energy decreases more slowly than hydration energy for larger anions like sulfate and carbonate, but decreases faster for the smaller hydroxide ion. Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases down the group due to decreasing polarizing power of the cation. Beryllium and magnesium are extracted by electrolysis of their fused chlorides, while calcium, strontium, and barium are obtained by electrolysis of fused chlorides or reduction of oxides with aluminium. The biological importance of these elements is profound: sodium and potassium maintain osmotic balance and nerve impulse transmission, magnesium is central to chlorophyll, and calcium is essential for bones, muscle contraction, and blood clotting.

Worked Example: Predicting Solubility Trends
Explain why the solubility of alkaline earth metal hydroxides increases down the group while the solubility of their sulfates decreases down the group. Use thermodynamic concepts of lattice energy and hydration energy.
The solubility of an ionic compound in water depends on the balance between lattice energy (U), the energy released when gaseous ions form the solid lattice, and hydration energy (ΔHₕyd), the energy released when gaseous ions are hydrated. For dissolution to be favourable, the hydration energy must exceed the lattice energy. Down group 2, the cationic radius increases from Be²⁺ to Ba²⁺. For hydroxides, the anion OH⁻ is relatively small. As the cation size increases, the lattice energy decreases significantly because the distance between cation and anion increases (U ∝ 1/(r₊ + r₋)). The hydration energy of the cation also decreases, but the decrease in lattice energy is proportionally larger than the decrease in hydration energy for the small OH⁻ anion. Consequently, the net enthalpy of solution becomes more negative, and solubility increases. For sulfates, the anion SO₄²⁻ is large. The lattice energy is less sensitive to cation size because the interionic distance is dominated by the large anion radius. The decrease in lattice energy down the group is therefore smaller, while the hydration energy of the cation still decreases substantially. The net result is that the enthalpy of solution becomes less negative (or more positive), and solubility decreases. This explains why BeSO₄ and MgSO₄ are highly soluble while BaSO₄ is insoluble, whereas Be(OH)₂ is insoluble and Ba(OH)₂ is soluble.
Exam Insight: Thermodynamic Reasoning in Inorganic Chemistry
UPSC examiners frequently test the ability to apply lattice energy and hydration energy arguments to explain solubility, thermal stability, and decomposition temperatures. Do not merely state trends; always derive them from the interplay of cationic charge density, anionic size, and the Kapustinskii equation for lattice energy. A concise answer comparing the relative rates of change of U and ΔHₕyd for a given anion series scores full marks.

3. Descriptive Chemistry of the p-Block Elements

3.1 Group 13: The Boron Family and Inert Pair Effect

Group 13 elements (B, Al, Ga, In, Tl) have the valence configuration ns² np¹. Boron is a non-metal with a high ionization enthalpy and forms exclusively covalent compounds, while aluminium and the heavier elements are metals. The inert pair effect, the reluctance of the ns² electrons to participate in bonding, becomes pronounced down the group due to poor shielding of the nucleus by intervening d and f electrons, which increases the effective nuclear charge on the ns electrons. Consequently, the +1 oxidation state becomes more stable than the +3 state for thallium; Tl⁺ is stable while Tl³⁺ is a strong oxidizing agent. Boron forms electron-deficient compounds such as BF₃ and BCl₃ which act as Lewis acids, accepting a pair of electrons to complete the octet. Borax (Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O) and boric acid (H₃BO₃) are important compounds; boric acid acts as a weak monobasic Lewis acid by accepting OH⁻ from water: B(OH)₃ + H₂O ⇌ [B(OH)₄]⁻ + H⁺. Aluminium forms amphoteric oxide Al₂O₃ and hydroxide Al(OH)₃, and its halide AlCl₃ is a dimer Al₂Cl₆ in the vapour phase and a Lewis acid catalyst in Friedel-Crafts reactions. Gallium, indium, and thallium show increasing metallic character. The alloy gallium arsenide (GaAs) is a crucial semiconductor for high-speed electronics. The standard electrode potential for Al³⁺/Al is −1.66 V, making aluminium a strong reducing agent, but a protective oxide layer renders it passive in air.

3.2 Group 14: Carbon Allotropes, Silicates, and Silicones

Group 14 elements (C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb) show a transition from non-metal to metal down the group. Carbon exhibits allotropy: diamond (sp³ hybridized, three-dimensional network, hardest known substance, insulator), graphite (sp² hybridized, layered structure with delocalized π electrons, conductor, lubricant), fullerenes (C₆₀, C₇₀, spherical cages), carbon nanotubes, and graphene (single layer of graphite, exceptional tensile strength and electron mobility). The catenation ability of carbon is maximum due to strong C−C bonds (348 kJ mol⁻¹) and small size. Silicon forms silicates based on the SiO₄⁴⁻ tetrahedron which polymerizes by sharing corners to give chains (pyroxenes), sheets (micas), and three-dimensional frameworks (zeolites, feldspars). Zeolites are aluminosilicates with porous structures used as ion-exchangers in water softening and as shape-selective catalysts in petroleum cracking. Silicones are polymeric compounds with −(R₂Si−O)ₙ− backbones, prepared by hydrolysis of dialkyldichlorosilanes (R₂SiCl₂). They are thermally stable, water-repellent, and used as lubricants, sealants, and in medical implants. Germanium is a semiconductor. Tin has two allotropes: white tin (metallic, stable above 286 K) and grey tin (diamond structure, stable below 286 K); the transformation causes "tin pest". Lead forms stable Pb²⁺ due to the inert pair effect; Pb⁴⁺ compounds like PbO₂ are strong oxidizing agents. Organolead compounds such as tetraethyllead were used as antiknock agents but are phased out due to toxicity.

3.3 Group 15: Nitrogen, Phosphorus, and Their Oxoacids

Group 15 elements (N, P, As, Sb, Bi) have ns² np³ configuration. Nitrogen differs from the rest due to its small size, high electronegativity, absence of d orbitals, and ability to form pπ−pπ multiple bonds. It exists as a diatomic gas N₂ with a triple bond (bond dissociation energy 945 kJ mol⁻¹), making it inert at room temperature. Phosphorus exists as P₄ tetrahedra in white phosphorus (highly reactive, toxic, stored under water) and polymeric red phosphorus (less reactive). The hydrides NH₃, PH₃, AsH₃, SbH₃, BiH₃ show decreasing thermal stability and basicity down the group. Ammonia is a Lewis base and a ligand; phosphine is a weaker base. Nitrogen forms a series of oxides: N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂/N₂O₄, N₂O₅. NO and NO₂ are odd-electron molecules; NO dimerizes to N₂O₄ at low temperatures. The oxoacids of nitrogen include hyponitrous acid (H₂N₂O₂), nitrous acid (HNO₂), and nitric acid (HNO₃). Nitric acid is a strong acid and a powerful oxidizing agent; its reaction with metals gives different products depending on concentration and metal reactivity (e.g., Cu + 4HNO₃(conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O; Cu + 8HNO₃(dil.) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O). Phosphorus forms oxoacids in +3 and +5 oxidation states: phosphorous acid (H₃PO₃, diprotic, structure HPO(OH)₂), phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄, triprotic), hypophosphorous acid (H₃PO₂, monoprotic), and condensed phosphates like pyrophosphoric acid (H₄P₂O₇) and metaphosphoric acid (HPO₃)ₙ. The P−O bond strength and the ability to form P=O double bonds using d orbitals (or pπ−dπ bonding) stabilize the +5 state.

3.4 Group 16: Oxygen, Sulfur, and Sulfur Oxoacids

Group 16 elements (O, S, Se, Te, Po) are the chalcogens. Oxygen is a diatomic gas with a double bond; it is the most electronegative element after fluorine. It shows −2 oxidation state predominantly, but −1 in peroxides (H₂O₂, Na₂O₂), −½ in superoxides (KO₂), and +2 in OF₂. Ozone (O₃) is a bent resonance hybrid with bond order 1.5, a powerful oxidizing agent used in water purification. Sulfur exhibits allotropy: rhombic sulfur (α-S₈, stable below 369 K) and monoclinic sulfur (β-S₈, stable above 369 K), both consisting of puckered S₈ rings. Plastic sulfur is a polymeric form. Sulfur forms a rich series of oxoacids. Sulfurous acid (H₂SO₃) exists only in solution; its salts are sulfites. Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) is the "king of chemicals", manufactured by the contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (V₂O₅ catalyst, 720 K, 2 bar), SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇ (oleum), H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄. It is a strong dehydrating agent (charring sugar) and oxidizing agent (with Cu, C, S). Peroxomonosulfuric acid (H₂SO₅, Caro's acid) and peroxodisulfuric acid (H₂S₂O₈, Marshall's acid) contain −O−O− linkages and are strong oxidizers. Thiosulfuric acid (H₂S₂O₃) gives thiosulfate S₂O₃²⁻ used in photography (fixing) and iodine-thiosulfate titrations. The oxoacids of selenium and tellurium are analogous but less stable. Polonium is radioactive; Po²⁺ and Po⁴⁺ exist in solution.

3.5 Group 17: Halogens, Interhalogens, and Pseudohalogens

Group 17 elements (F, Cl, Br, I, At) are the halogens, existing as diatomic molecules X₂. Fluorine is the most reactive non-metal, strongest oxidizing agent (E° = +2.87 V for F₂/F⁻), and shows only −1 oxidation state due to absence of d orbitals and highest electronegativity. It reacts with water explosively to give O₂ and OF₂. Chlorine, bromine, and iodine show positive oxidation states (+1, +3, +5, +7) in compounds with oxygen and fluorine. The oxidizing power decreases down the group: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Chlorine disproportionates in cold dilute alkali: Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O, and in hot concentrated alkali: 3Cl₂ + 6OH⁻ → 5Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + 3H₂O. The oxoacids of chlorine are hypochlorous (HClO), chlorous (HClO₂), chloric (HClO₃), and perchloric (HClO₄) acids; acid strength increases with oxidation state due to electron withdrawal by oxygen. Perchloric acid is a strong acid; its salts are oxidizing agents. Interhalogen compounds XYₙ (n = 1, 3, 5, 7) are formed between different halogens; the larger halogen is the central atom. Examples: ClF, BrF₃, IF₅, IF₇. They are covalent, diamagnetic, and hydrolyze to give halide and oxyhalide ions. Pseudohalogens are species that behave like halogens: cyanogen (CN)₂, thiocyanogen (SCN)₂, and their anions CN⁻, SCN⁻, OCN⁻. They form dimers, hydrogen acids (HCN), and salts with metals.

3.6 Group 18: Noble Gas Compounds and Clathrates

Group 18 elements (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) were considered inert until Neil Bartlett synthesized Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻ in 1962, inspired by the similar ionization enthalpy of xenon (1170 kJ mol⁻¹) and O₂ (1175 kJ mol⁻¹). Xenon forms fluorides XeF₂ (linear), XeF₄ (square planar), XeF₆ (distorted octahedral) by direct reaction with fluorine under controlled conditions. Hydrolysis of XeF₆ gives XeO₃ (explosive) and XeOF₄. Xenon oxides include XeO₃ and XeO₄. Krypton forms KrF₂ under extreme conditions. Helium, neon, and argon form no stable neutral compounds at room temperature. Noble gases form clathrates (inclusion compounds) where gas atoms are trapped in the cavities of a host lattice, typically water ice (gas hydrates) or quinol. The stability of clathrates depends on the size match between guest atom and host cavity. Argon, krypton, and xenon form hydrates; helium and neon do not. Clathrates are used for noble gas separation and storage. Radon is radioactive; its chemistry is limited to fluorides and complexes studied at tracer concentrations. The discovery of noble gas compounds revolutionized the concept of chemical inertness and demonstrated that high oxidation states can be stabilized by highly electronegative ligands like fluorine and oxygen.

4. Transition Metals, Coordination Chemistry, and f-Block Elements

4.1 General Properties of d-Block Elements

The d-block elements (groups 3 to 12) are characterized by the progressive filling of (n−1)d orbitals. Their general electronic configuration is (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns⁰⁻². The first transition series (Sc to Zn) involves filling of 3d orbitals, the second (Y to Cd) 4d, and the third (La, Hf to Hg) 5d. Transition metals exhibit typical metallic properties: high melting and boiling points, high enthalpies of atomization, good electrical and thermal conductivity, and mechanical strength. These arise from strong metallic bonding involving both ns and (n−1)d electrons. Atomic radii decrease across a series due to increasing nuclear charge, but the decrease is smaller than in s- and p-blocks because added d electrons shield each other poorly. A notable feature is the lanthanide contraction: the steady decrease in size of lanthanide ions (La³⁺ to Lu³⁺) due to poor shielding by 4f electrons causes the radii of 4d and 5d series elements to be very similar (e.g., Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm), leading to nearly identical chemistry for pairs like Zr-Hf, Nb-Ta, Mo-W. Ionization enthalpies increase across a series but irregularly due to exchange energy stabilization of half-filled (d⁵) and fully-filled (d¹⁰) configurations. Variable oxidation states are a hallmark: from +2 to +7 in the first series, with maximum oxidation state equal to the group number up to Mn (group 7), then decreasing.

4.2 Coordination Chemistry: Ligand Field Theory, Spectrochemical Series, and Magnetic Behaviour

Ligand Field Splitting in Octahedral and Tetrahedral Fields

In an octahedral complex the five d‑orbitals split into a lower‑energy t2g set (dxy, dxz, dyz) and a higher‑energy eg set (dz², dx²‑y²). The energy gap is denoted Δo. For a tetrahedral field the splitting is inverted and reduced: Δt ≈ 4/9 Δo. The magnitude of Δ depends on the metal oxidation state, the nature of the ligand, and the geometry. Higher oxidation states and ligands with strong σ‑donor/π‑acceptor ability increase Δ.

Spectrochemical Series and Its Origin

The empirical ordering of ligands by their field strength is the spectrochemical series: I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ (S) < Cl⁻ < NO₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ (N) < NH₃ < en < bipy < phen < NO₂⁻ < PPh₃ < CN⁻ < CO. Ligands at the right end are strong‑field (large Δ) and favour low‑spin configurations; those at the left are weak‑field (small Δ) and give high‑spin complexes. The series reflects a combination of σ‑donation, π‑donation, and π‑acceptance. π‑acceptor ligands (CO, CN⁻) lower the t2g orbitals by back‑bonding, enlarging Δ.

High‑Spin versus Low‑Spin Configurations

For d⁴–d⁷ metal ions in octahedral fields the electron distribution depends on the relative magnitude of Δo and the pairing energy P. If Δo > P the complex is low‑spin (electrons pair in t2g before occupying eg); if Δo < P it is high‑spin. Typical examples: [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (d⁶, low‑spin, t2g⁶ eg⁰, diamagnetic) versus [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (d⁶, high‑spin, t2g⁴ eg², four unpaired electrons). Magnetic moments measured by the spin‑only formula μso = √[n(n+2)] BM (n = number of unpaired electrons) provide experimental verification.

Jahn–Teller Distortions

Complexes with uneven eg occupancy (e.g. high‑spin d⁴, low‑spin d⁷, d⁹) undergo tetragonal elongation or compression to remove degeneracy. The most common is axial elongation in Cu²⁺ (d⁹) octahedral complexes, giving two long and four short M–L bonds. This distortion splits the eg set further and influences UV‑Vis spectra and reactivity.

4.3 f‑Block Elements: Lanthanide Contraction, Oxidation States, and Separation Techniques

Lanthanide Contraction and Its Consequences

The steady decrease in ionic radius from La³⁺ (103 pm) to Lu³⁺ (86 pm) arises because the 4f electrons shield the nuclear charge poorly. The contraction makes the radii of the 4d and 5d transition series almost identical (Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm; Nb 146 pm, Ta 146 pm). Consequently, Zr/Hf, Nb/Ta, Mo/W pairs exhibit nearly identical chemistry, complicating their separation. The contraction also increases the basicity of the later lanthanide hydroxides and influences the stability of complexes with oxygen‑donor ligands.

Oxidation States and Redox Chemistry

All lanthanides display a stable +3 oxidation state. Ce⁴⁺ (4f⁰) and Eu²⁺ (4f⁷) are the most accessible non‑+3 states because they achieve empty or half‑filled f‑subshells. Ce⁴⁺ is a strong oxidising agent (E° = +1.61 V) used in volumetric analysis; Eu²⁺ is a moderate reducing agent. Other lanthanides can be forced into +2 or +4 states in solid‑state matrices or with strongly π‑accepting ligands, but these are not stable in aqueous solution.

Separation: Ion‑Exchange and Solvent Extraction

Commercial separation exploits the slight differences in complex stability with chelating agents. In cation‑exchange chromatography using a resin such as Dowex‑50, the lanthanides are eluted with a gradient of α‑hydroxyisobutyrate (α‑HIBA) at pH 3.5–4.5; the heavier lanthanides elute first because their smaller radii give stronger complexation. Solvent extraction with organophosphorus extractants (e.g. TBP, HDEHP) in kerosene follows the same trend: distribution ratios increase across the series, allowing fractional extraction. Modern processes combine both steps for high‑purity individual oxides.

Worked Problem: Predicting Spin State and Magnetic Moment
A complex [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ is known to be low‑spin. Determine its d‑electron count, electron configuration in an octahedral field, number of unpaired electrons, and the spin‑only magnetic moment in Bohr magnetons.
Cobalt in +3 oxidation state: Co atomic number 27, electron configuration [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s². Co³⁺ loses three electrons → 3d⁶. In an octahedral strong‑field (NH₃ is a strong‑field ligand) the splitting Δ₀ > P, so the six electrons fill the lower t₂g set completely: t₂g⁶ e_g⁰. All electrons are paired → n = 0. Spin‑only magnetic moment μ_so = √[0(0+2)] = 0 BM. The complex is diamagnetic, consistent with experimental observation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for magnetic moment, first decide the oxidation state and d‑count, then assess ligand field strength from the spectrochemical series. Write the t₂g/e_g filling explicitly; this avoids the common mistake of assuming high‑spin for all 3d metals. Remember that 4d/5d metals are almost always low‑spin because Δ is larger.

5.1 Thermodynamics of Chemical Reactions: Gibbs Energy, Equilibrium, and Temperature Dependence

Fundamental Relations

The Gibbs free energy change for a reaction at constant temperature and pressure is ΔG = ΔH – TΔS. At equilibrium ΔG = 0, giving ΔG° = –RT ln K, where K is the equilibrium constant (dimensionless, based on activities). The standard reaction enthalpy ΔH° and entropy ΔS° are obtained from standard formation data. The temperature dependence of K follows the van’t Hoff equation: d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT²). Integration assuming ΔH° constant yields ln(K₂/K₁) = –ΔH°/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁).

Le Chatelier’s Principle Quantified

For an exothermic reaction (ΔH° < 0) increasing temperature shifts equilibrium toward reactants (K decreases). For an endothermic reaction (ΔH° > 0) K increases with temperature. Pressure changes affect only gas‑phase equilibria: increasing total pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas. The reaction quotient Q = Π a_i^ν_i (activities raised to stoichiometric coefficients) determines the direction: if Q < K the forward reaction proceeds; if Q > K the reverse reaction proceeds.

Coupled Reactions and Biochemical Relevance

In metabolic pathways an unfavourable reaction (ΔG° > 0) can be driven by coupling to a highly favourable one (e.g. ATP hydrolysis, ΔG°′ ≈ –30.5 kJ mol⁻¹). The overall ΔG° is the sum of the individual ΔG° values. This principle underlies the calculation of equilibrium constants for multi‑step sequences and the design of industrial processes where a product is continuously removed to shift equilibrium.

5.2 Electrochemistry: Electrode Potentials, Nernst Equation, and Applications in Corrosion and Batteries

Standard Electrode Potentials and the Electrochemical Series

The standard reduction potential E° of a half‑cell is measured versus the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) at 298 K, 1 bar, unit activity. The series arranges half‑reactions from most negative (strong reducing agents, e.g. Li⁺/Li –3.04 V) to most positive (strong oxidising agents, e.g. F₂/F⁻ +2.87 V). A spontaneous galvanic cell has E°_cell = E°_cathode – E°_anode > 0. The relationship ΔG° = –nFE°_cell links thermodynamics to electrochemistry (F = 96485 C mol⁻¹).

Nernst Equation for Non‑Standard Conditions

For a half‑reaction aA + ne⁻ ⇌ bB, the electrode potential is E = E° – (RT/nF) ln (a_B^b / a_A^a). At 298 K this simplifies to E = E° – (0.05916/n) log₁₀ Q. For a full cell, E_cell = E°_cell – (0.05916/n) log₁₀ Q_cell. The equation predicts how concentration, pressure, and pH shift potentials. In concentration cells E°_cell = 0 and the voltage arises solely from the activity ratio.

Corrosion as an Electrochemical Process

Corrosion of iron in aerated water involves anodic dissolution Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ (E° = –0.44 V) and cathodic oxygen reduction O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (E° = +0.40 V). The overall cell potential is positive, so corrosion proceeds spontaneously. Protective measures include cathodic protection (making the metal a cathode by attaching a more active sacrificial anode such as Zn or Mg) and applying coatings that block either the anodic or cathodic site.

Primary and Secondary Batteries

Primary cells (e.g. Zn–MnO₂ alkaline) are irreversible; secondary cells (e.g. Li‑ion, Ni‑MH, lead‑acid) are rechargeable. In a Li‑ion cell the anode reaction is LiₓC₆ → C₆ + xLi⁺ + xe⁻ and the cathode reaction is Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + xLi⁺ + xe⁻ → LiCoO₂. The cell voltage (~3.7 V) derives from the difference in chemical potentials of Li in the two hosts. Energy density, cycle life, and safety are governed by the choice of electrode materials, electrolyte stability, and solid‑electrolyte interphase formation.

6.1 Organic Reaction Mechanisms: Nucleophilic Substitution, Elimination, and Stereochemical Outcomes

SN1 versus SN2: Kinetic and Stereochemical Criteria

SN1 proceeds via a carbocation intermediate; rate = k[substrate]. It is favoured by tertiary substrates, polar protic solvents, and stable carbocations (allylic, benzylic). The product shows racemisation (partial inversion/retention) because the planar carbocation can be attacked from either face. SN2 is concerted; rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]. It requires a primary substrate, strong nucleophile, and polar aprotic solvent. The reaction proceeds with complete inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). The competition between the two pathways is a classic exam theme: changing solvent from ethanol to DMSO can switch a secondary alkyl halide from SN1 to SN2.

E1 and E2 Eliminations: Regioselectivity and Stereochemistry

E1 shares the carbocation intermediate with SN1; the rate‑determining step is loss of leaving group. The subsequent deprotonation yields alkenes following Zaitsev’s rule (more substituted alkene) unless a bulky base (e.g. t‑BuOK) favours the Hofmann product. E2 is concerted, requiring anti‑periplanar geometry of the leaving group and the β‑hydrogen. This stereoelectronic requirement explains why cyclohexyl halides with axial leaving groups eliminate faster than equatorial ones. The anti‑periplanar constraint also dictates the stereochemistry of the resulting double bond (E/Z).

Neighbouring Group Participation and Rearrangements

When a neighbouring lone pair or π‑bond can assist departure of a leaving group, the reaction rate accelerates and the stereochemical outcome may show retention (double inversion). Classic examples: solvolysis of 2‑bromo‑1‑phenylethane (phenyl participation) and the formation of a bridged bromonium ion in the solvolysis of 3‑bromo‑1‑propene. Carbocation rearrangements (hydride or alkyl shifts) occur to generate a more stable cation; they are common in SN1/E1 of secondary substrates and must be considered when predicting product mixtures.

  • Assuming all 3d metal complexes are high‑spin; 4d/5d metals are almost always low‑spin because Δ is larger.
  • Confusing the spectrochemical series order for π‑donor versus π‑acceptor ligands; CO and CN⁻ are the strongest field ligands, not halides.
  • Neglecting the temperature dependence of ΔG° when using ΔG° = –RT ln K; ΔH° and ΔS° must be evaluated at the same temperature.
  • Applying the Nernst equation with concentrations instead of activities for non‑ideal solutions; the exam often expects the activity correction.
  • Overlooking the anti‑periplanar requirement in E2 eliminations, leading to wrong prediction of major alkene geometry.
  • Treating SN1 racemisation as complete inversion; the product is a racemic mixture, not a single enantiomer.
  • Forgetting that lanthanide contraction makes Zr/Hf, Nb/Ta, Mo/W chemically almost identical, which is the basis for their difficult separation.

Organic Spectroscopy: UV‑Vis, IR, NMR, and Mass Spectrometry for Structural Elucidation

Ultraviolet‑visible spectroscopy probes π→π* and n→π* transitions. Conjugated dienes absorb near 220 nm; each additional double bond shifts λmax by ~30 nm (Woodward–Fieser rules). Carbonyl groups show n→π* bands at 280–300 nm (ε ≈ 10–100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹) and π→π* bands at 180–190 nm. Solvent polarity causes hypso‑ or bathochromic shifts that must be noted when comparing literature values.

Infrared spectroscopy identifies functional groups through characteristic stretching frequencies. The carbonyl stretch appears at 1700–1750 cm⁻¹; conjugation lowers it by 20–30 cm⁻¹, while electron‑withdrawing substituents raise it. O–H stretches of alcohols are broad (3200–3600 cm⁻¹) whereas carboxylic acids show a very broad band centred near 3000 cm⁻¹ due to hydrogen‑bonded dimers. N–H stretches (3300–3500 cm⁻¹) are sharper and often appear as doublets for primary amines.

Proton NMR (¹H NMR) provides chemical shift (δ), integration, multiplicity (n + 1 rule), and coupling constants (J). Typical δ ranges: alkyl 0.9–1.8 ppm, allylic 1.6–2.2 ppm, α‑to‑heteroatom 3.0–4.5 ppm, aromatic 6.5–8.0 ppm, aldehydic 9–10 ppm. Vicinal coupling in alkenes: Jtrans ≈ 15–18 Hz, Jcis ≈ 10–12 Hz, Jgem ≈ 2 Hz. Diastereotopic protons on a CH₂ adjacent to a chiral centre give separate signals (AB system).

Carbon‑13 NMR (¹³C NMR) is usually broadband decoupled, giving singlets for each distinct carbon. DEPT experiments differentiate CH₃, CH₂, CH, and quaternary carbons. Chemical shifts: alkyl 10–50 ppm, alkene 100–150 ppm, carbonyl 160–220 ppm. The number of signals equals the number of magnetically non‑equivalent carbons, a quick symmetry check.

Mass spectrometry (EI, ESI, MALDI) yields molecular ion (M⁺·) or protonated molecule [M+H]⁺. The nitrogen rule: odd nominal mass → odd number of nitrogens. Isotopic patterns (Cl ≈ 3:1, Br ≈ 1:1) confirm halogen presence. Fragmentation pathways (α‑cleavage, McLafferty rearrangement) help locate functional groups. High‑resolution MS provides exact mass to four decimal places, enabling elemental composition determination.

Integrated problem solving: combine UV‑Vis λmax (conjugation length), IR carbonyl frequency (conjugation vs. strain), ¹H NMR coupling pattern (alkene geometry), ¹³C count (symmetry), and HRMS formula to propose a unique structure. Practice with unknowns from past papers builds the required speed.

Chemical Kinetics: Rate Laws, Transition State Theory, and Catalysis

The rate law for a reaction aA + bB → products is experimentally determined: rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ. The overall order is x + y. Elementary steps have molecularity equal to stoichiometric coefficients; the rate‑determining step (RDS) controls the observed rate law. For a two‑step mechanism A ⇌ I (fast) → P (slow), the pre‑equilibrium approximation gives rate = k₂K₁[A] where K₁ = k₁/k₋₁.

Temperature dependence follows the Arrhenius equation k = A e^(–Ea/RT). A plot of ln k versus 1/T yields slope –Ea/R and intercept ln A. The Eyring equation from transition‑state theory: k = (kBT/h) e^(–ΔG‡/RT) where ΔG‡ = ΔH‡ – TΔS‡. A large negative ΔS‡ indicates a highly ordered transition state (e.g., bimolecular association).

Catalysis lowers Ea without altering ΔG of the overall reaction. Homogeneous catalysts (e.g., H⁺ in ester hydrolysis) operate in the same phase; heterogeneous catalysts (e.g., Pt in H₂ oxidation) provide surface sites. The Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism assumes both reactants adsorb on adjacent sites; the rate is proportional to surface coverages θAθB. The Eley–Rideal mechanism involves one adsorbed species reacting with a gas‑phase molecule.

Enzyme kinetics follow the Michaelis–Menten model: v₀ = (Vmax[S])/(KM + [S]). Lineweaver–Burk double‑reciprocal plots (1/v₀ vs 1/[S]) give straight lines; competitive inhibitors increase apparent KM (intercept unchanged), non‑competitive inhibitors decrease Vmax (slope unchanged), uncompetitive inhibitors decrease both. Turnover number kcat = Vmax/[E]₀.

Chain reactions (e.g., H₂ + Cl₂) involve initiation, propagation, and termination steps. The steady‑state approximation for radical intermediates yields rate laws with fractional orders. Photochemical reactions obey the Stark–Einstein law: one photon activates one molecule; quantum yield Φ = (number of events)/(photons absorbed) can exceed 1 for chain processes.

Diffusion‑controlled limits: in solution, the maximum second‑order rate constant kdiff ≈ 8RT/3η (η = viscosity). Reactions faster than this are activation‑controlled. Isotope effects (kH/kD ≈ 2–7 for C–H cleavage) reveal whether bond breaking occurs in the RDS.

Worked Example: Determining Mechanism from Kinetic Data
A reaction A + B → C shows rate = k[A]²[B]⁰. The proposed mechanism: (1) A + A ⇌ A₂ (fast equilibrium), (2) A₂ + B → C (slow). Derive the rate law and state the molecularity of the RDS.
Step 1 equilibrium constant K₁ = [A₂]/[A]² → [A₂] = K₁[A]². Step 2 is RDS, rate = k₂[A₂][B] = k₂K₁[A]²[B]. Since [B]⁰ experimentally, B must be in large excess or its concentration incorporated into k_obs. The RDS is bimolecular (A₂ + B), molecularity = 2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a rate law contains fractional orders, immediately consider a pre‑equilibrium or steady‑state approximation. Write the elementary steps, apply the approximation, and match the derived expression to the experimental law. This systematic approach earns full marks even if the final algebra is lengthy.

Solid State Chemistry: Crystal Defects, Non‑Stoichiometry, and Electrical Conductivity

Point defects in ionic crystals are described by Kröger‑Vink notation. A Schottky defect consists of a cation vacancy VM'' and an anion vacancy VX•• in a 1:1 ratio, preserving charge neutrality. A Frenkel defect pairs a cation vacancy VM'' with a cation interstitial Mi••. The equilibrium concentration follows n/N = exp(–ΔGf/2kT) for Schottky and exp(–ΔGf/2kT) for Frenkel (factor 2 because two defects form).

Non‑stoichiometric oxides such as Fe₁₋ₓO (wüstite) accommodate cation deficiency by creating Fe³⁺ ions and cation vacancies: 2Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + VFe''. The resulting p‑type conductivity arises from hole hopping between Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺. Conversely, TiO₂₋ₓ (oxygen deficiency) generates Ti³⁺ and electrons, giving n‑type conductivity. The defect chemistry is captured by mass‑action laws: [VFe''][Fe³⁺]² = KSchottkypO₂^(1/2).

Electrical conductivity σ = n e μ (n = carrier concentration, μ = mobility). In intrinsic semiconductors, n = p = ni = √(NCNV) exp(–Eg/2kT). Doping shifts the Fermi level: donors (group V in Si) raise EF toward the conduction band, acceptors (group III) lower it toward the valence band. The temperature dependence of σ distinguishes extrinsic (σ ∝ exp(–ED/kT)) from intrinsic regimes.

Superionic conductors (e.g., α‑AgI, β‑alumina) exhibit liquid‑like ionic mobility in a rigid framework. The conductivity jump at the transition temperature (≈ 147 °C for AgI) is 10³–10⁴ fold. Applications include solid‑state batteries and fuel cells. The Nernst–Einstein relation links diffusion coefficient D to conductivity: σ = n z²e²D/kT.

Band structure of transition‑metal oxides explains metal‑insulator transitions. In VO₂, a Peierls distortion paired with electron‑electron correlation (Mott‑Hubbard) opens a gap below 68 °C. The Hubbard U parameter versus bandwidth W determines whether a d‑electron system is metallic (U < W) or insulating (U > W). Perovskite manganites (La₁₋ₓSrₓMnO₃) show colossal magnetoresistance due to double‑exchange between Mn³⁺/Mn⁴⁺ and Jahn–Teller lattice coupling.

Defect engineering: aliovalent doping (e.g., Y³⁺ in ZrO₂) creates oxygen vacancies for oxide‑ion conduction (YSZ). Controlled reduction/oxidation tunes carrier density in transparent conducting oxides (SnO₂:F, In₂O₃:Sn). Understanding defect equilibria enables design of materials for catalysis, sensors, and energy storage.

Surface Chemistry: Adsorption Isotherms, Heterogeneous Catalysis, and Colloidal Stability

The Langmuir isotherm assumes monolayer coverage on identical sites with no lateral interactions: θ = (KP)/(1 + KP) where θ is fractional coverage, P pressure, K adsorption equilibrium constant. Linearised forms: P/θ = 1/K + P (Langmuir plot) or 1/θ = 1 + 1/(KP). The BET isotherm extends to multilayers: V/Vm = (C P/P₀)/[(1 – P/P₀)(1 – P/P₀ + C P/P₀)], yielding surface area from Vm (monolayer volume) and known cross‑sectional area of adsorbate (N₂ = 0.162 nm²).

Freundlich isotherm (θ = KP1/n) describes heterogeneous surfaces; n > 1 indicates favourable adsorption. Temkin isotherm accounts for linearly decreasing heat of adsorption with coverage. Choice of model is guided by linearity of transformed data and physical plausibility.

Heterogeneous catalysis proceeds via adsorption, surface reaction, and desorption. The rate‑determining step can be any of these. For a unimolecular surface reaction A* → products (where * denotes adsorbed species), rate = kθA. Substituting Langmuir θ gives rate = kKP/(1 + KP). At low pressure (KP ≪ 1) the reaction is first order in P; at high pressure (KP ≫ 1) it becomes zero order (surface saturated). Bimolecular Langmuir–Hinshelwood rate: rate = kθAθB = kKAKBPAPB/(1 + KAPA + KBPB)².

Promoters (e.g., K₂O in Fe/Haber‑Bosch) increase activity by modifying electronic structure or creating new active sites. Poisons (CO, S) bind irreversibly, blocking sites. Sintering reduces surface area at high temperature; supports (Al₂O₃, SiO₂) disperse metal particles and inhibit sintering.

Colloidal stability is governed by DLVO theory: total interaction energy VT = VA + VR, where VA (van der Waals attraction) ∝ –A/12πD² (A = Hamaker constant, D = separation) and VR (electrostatic repulsion) ∝ exp(–κD) (κ⁻¹ = Debye length). The energy barrier prevents aggregation; if barrier < ~ 15 kT, rapid coagulation occurs. Adding electrolyte compresses the double layer (increases κ), lowering the barrier (Schulze–Hardy rule: coagulation power ∝ z⁶). Steric stabilization uses adsorbed polymers to create a repulsive osmotic/elastic barrier independent of electrolyte.

Zeta potential (ζ) measured by electrophoresis indicates the potential at the slipping plane. |ζ| > 30 mV generally confers electrostatic stability. Isoelectric point (pH where ζ = 0) marks minimum stability. Applications: drug delivery (liposomes), water treatment (flocculation), and catalyst supports (controlled particle size).

Bioinorganic Chemistry: Metalloproteins, Oxygen Transport, and Enzyme Catalysis

Hemoglobin and myoglobin bind O₂ via a ferrous heme (Fe²⁺ protoporphyrin IX). In deoxyhemoglobin Fe²⁺ is high‑spin (S = 2) and lies ~0.6 Å out of the porphyrin plane; O₂ binding triggers spin‑state change to low‑spin (S = 0) and pulls Fe into the plane, initiating the T → R quaternary transition. Cooperative binding is quantified by the Hill equation: Y = pO₂ⁿ/(P₅₀ⁿ + pO₂ⁿ) with n ≈ 2.8 for human hemoglobin. The Bohr effect (H⁺/CO₂) stabilises the T state, lowering O₂ affinity; 2,3‑BPG binds the central cavity of the T state, further reducing affinity.

Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) reduces O₂ to H₂O using four electrons from cytochrome c and four protons from the matrix. The binuclear centre (heme a₃‑CuB) cycles through Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ and Cu⁺/Cu²⁺ states; a peroxide intermediate (Fe⁴⁺=O) is avoided by concerted proton‑coupled electron transfer. The enzyme pumps additional protons across the membrane, contributing to the proton motive force.

Nitrogenase reduces N₂ to 2NH₃ at the FeMo‑cofactor (Fe₇MoS₉C·homocitrate). The reaction requires 16 ATP per N₂, delivered by the Fe‑protein (Fe₄S₄ cluster) in a cyclic electron‑transfer process. The resting state is E₀; successive reductions generate E₁–E₄ states, with E₄ capable of binding N₂. CO inhibits by binding the FeMo‑cofactor; H₂ is an obligate by‑product.

Carbonic anhydrase (Zn²⁺ in a tetrahedral His₃OH⁻ site) catalyses CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ at near diffusion‑controlled rates (kcat/KM ≈ 10⁸ M⁻¹ s⁻¹). The mechanism: Zn‑bound hydroxide attacks CO₂, forming Zn‑bicarbonate, which is displaced by water. Proton transfer from the Zn‑bound water to a nearby His64 shuttle regenerates the hydroxide.

Superoxide dismutases (Cu/Zn‑SOD, Mn‑SOD, Fe‑SOD) disproportionate O₂•⁻ to O₂ and H₂O₂. The Cu/Zn enzyme uses a Cu²⁺/Cu⁺ redox cycle; Zn²⁺ provides structural stability. The active‑site geometry (square‑pyramidal Cu) and electrostatic guidance channel superoxide to the metal centre.

Model complexes replicate key features: picket‑fence porphyrins mimic O₂ binding without dimerisation; Fe‑S clusters in synthetic cubanes reproduce redox potentials of ferredoxins. Spectroscopic signatures (Mössbauer, EPR, XAS) correlate oxidation and spin states with enzymatic intermediates, guiding mechanistic proposals.

  • Assuming Langmuir isotherm applies when multilayer adsorption is evident; always check BET linearity first.
  • Confusing the Hill coefficient with the number of binding sites; n reflects cooperativity, not stoichiometry.
  • Neglecting the proton‑coupled electron transfer in cytochrome c oxidase and writing a simple electron‑only scheme.
  • Treating the FeMo‑cofactor as a simple Fe‑S cluster; the central carbide and homocitrate are essential for activity.
  • Overlooking the role of the His64 shuttle in carbonic anhydrase; the rate‑limiting step is proton transfer, not CO₂ hydration.
  • Using the Schulze–Hardy rule for non‑electrostatic (steric) stabilization; the rule applies only to DLVO‑type colloids.
  • Forgetting that the Bohr effect and 2,3‑BPG act synergistically; exam questions often ask for the combined effect on P₅₀.
