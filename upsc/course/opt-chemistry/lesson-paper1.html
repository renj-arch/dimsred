1. Atomic Structure and Quantum Mechanical Foundations

1.1 The Quantum Mechanical Model of the Atom

The quantum mechanical model of the atom replaced the Bohr model because the latter could not explain the spectra of multi-electron atoms or the fine structure of spectral lines. Erwin Schrödinger formulated a wave equation in 1926 that treats the electron as a standing wave, described by a wave function ψ whose square ψ² gives the probability density of finding the electron in a given region of space. This probabilistic interpretation, proposed by Max Born, means that the electron does not follow a definite orbit but occupies an orbital, a three-dimensional region where the probability of finding the electron is high. The Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom yields exact solutions characterized by three quantum numbers: the principal quantum number n, the azimuthal quantum number l, and the magnetic quantum number mₗ. A fourth quantum number, the spin quantum number mₛ, was introduced by George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit in 1925 to account for the splitting of spectral lines in a magnetic field, known as the Zeeman effect. The Pauli exclusion principle, formulated by Wolfgang Pauli in 1925, states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers, which limits each orbital to a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins. This principle underpins the entire structure of the periodic table and the chemical behaviour of elements.

1.2 Quantum Numbers and Electronic Configuration

The principal quantum number n determines the main energy level or shell of an electron and takes positive integer values 1, 2, 3, and so on. The azimuthal quantum number l defines the shape of the orbital and ranges from 0 to n−1, with l = 0, 1, 2, 3 corresponding to s, p, d, and f orbitals respectively. The magnetic quantum number mₗ specifies the orientation of the orbital in space and takes integer values from −l to +l, giving 2l+1 orbitals per subshell. The spin quantum number mₛ can be +½ or −½. Electrons fill orbitals according to the Aufbau principle, which states that orbitals are filled in order of increasing (n+l) value, and for equal (n+l), the orbital with lower n fills first. This order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p. Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity requires that electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing begins, minimizing electron-electron repulsion. The electronic configuration of an element determines its position in the periodic table, its valency, and its chemical reactivity. For instance, the configuration of chromium is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² because a half-filled d subshell provides extra stability due to exchange energy, a quantum mechanical effect arising from the indistinguishability of electrons with parallel spins.

1.3 Periodic Classification and the Long Form Table

The modern periodic table, often called the long form, arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number Z, which is the number of protons in the nucleus. This arrangement, proposed by Henry Moseley in 1913 based on X-ray spectra, corrected the anomalies of Mendeleev's table which was based on atomic weights. The table consists of 18 vertical groups and 7 horizontal periods. Elements in the same group have similar valence shell electronic configurations and therefore exhibit similar chemical properties. The s-block comprises groups 1 and 2, the p-block groups 13 to 18, the d-block groups 3 to 12, and the f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides placed separately at the bottom. Period 1 has 2 elements, periods 2 and 3 have 8 each, periods 4 and 5 have 18 each, period 6 has 32 including the lanthanides, and period 7 is incomplete but would have 32 including the actinides. The block classification reflects the subshell being filled: ns for s-block, np for p-block, (n−1)d for d-block, and (n−2)f for f-block. This structure allows the prediction of trends in atomic radius, ionization enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, and electronegativity across periods and down groups, which are the foundation for understanding chemical periodicity.

2. Periodic Trends and the Chemistry of s-Block Elements

2.1 Periodic Trends in Atomic and Ionic Properties

Atomic radius generally decreases across a period from left to right because the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same principal shell, resulting in a greater effective nuclear charge experienced by the valence electrons. Down a group, atomic radius increases because a new principal shell is added, placing the valence electrons farther from the nucleus and increasing shielding. Ionic radius follows similar trends: cations are smaller than their parent atoms because the loss of electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and often removes an entire shell, while anions are larger because added electrons increase repulsion without increasing nuclear charge. Ionization enthalpy, the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from a gaseous atom, increases across a period due to increasing effective nuclear charge and decreases down a group due to increased atomic size and shielding. However, anomalies occur: the first ionization enthalpy of boron is lower than that of beryllium because the electron removed from boron comes from a 2p orbital which is higher in energy and more shielded than the 2s electron of beryllium. Similarly, oxygen has a lower first ionization enthalpy than nitrogen because the paired electrons in one 2p orbital of oxygen experience greater repulsion. Electron gain enthalpy becomes more negative across a period as the tendency to accept an electron increases, but the electron gain enthalpy of fluorine is less negative than that of chlorine because the small size of fluorine leads to high electron-electron repulsion in the compact 2p subshell. Electronegativity, a measure of an atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a covalent bond, follows the Pauling scale where fluorine is the most electronegative element at 4.0. These trends dictate the nature of bonding, the acid-base character of oxides, and the redox behaviour of elements.

2.2 Anomalous Behaviour of Lithium and Beryllium

Lithium and beryllium, the first elements of groups 1 and 2 respectively, show anomalous behaviour compared to the rest of their groups due to their exceptionally small atomic and ionic sizes, high charge densities, and the absence of low-lying d orbitals. Lithium is harder and has a higher melting point than other alkali metals; it forms a stable nitride Li₃N directly with nitrogen, whereas other alkali metals do not. Lithium carbonate Li₂CO₃ decomposes on heating to Li₂O and CO₂, while carbonates of other alkali metals are thermally stable. Lithium hydroxide is a weaker base than other alkali metal hydroxides, and lithium fluoride is sparingly soluble in water due to high lattice energy, unlike other alkali halides. Beryllium differs from other alkaline earth metals in forming predominantly covalent compounds, such as BeCl₂ which is a polymeric chain in the solid state but a linear monomer in the vapour phase. Beryllium oxide and hydroxide are amphoteric, dissolving in both acids and bases to give beryllates [Be(OH)₄]²⁻, whereas oxides and hydroxides of other group 2 elements are basic. Beryllium does not react with water even at high temperatures, and its carbide Be₂C yields methane on hydrolysis, unlike calcium carbide which yields ethyne. These anomalies arise because the high charge density of Li⁺ and Be²⁺ polarizes anion electron clouds strongly, introducing covalent character into nominally ionic compounds, a phenomenon described by Fajans' rules.

2.3 Diagonal Relationships and Hydrogen Chemistry

A diagonal relationship exists between certain pairs of elements in the second and third periods, notably lithium-magnesium, beryllium-aluminium, and boron-silicon. This similarity arises because moving down a group increases size while moving across a period decreases size; the two effects partially cancel diagonally, giving similar charge densities. Lithium and magnesium both form nitrides and oxides on heating in air, their carbonates decompose on heating, their hydroxides are sparingly soluble, and both form organometallic compounds with covalent character. Beryllium and aluminium both form amphoteric oxides and hydroxides, their carbides yield methane on hydrolysis, and both form complexes such as [BeF₄]²⁻ and [AlF₆]³⁻. Boron and silicon are both semiconductors, form covalent network solids, and their oxides B₂O₃ and SiO₂ are acidic. Hydrogen occupies a unique position in the periodic table; it resembles alkali metals in having one valence electron and forming H⁺, and halogens in needing one electron to complete its shell and forming H⁻. Its three isotopes—protium (¹H), deuterium (²H or D), and tritium (³H or T)—differ in neutron number. Deuterium oxide D₂O, heavy water, has a higher boiling point (374.4 K) and density (1.107 g cm⁻³) than H₂O and is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors. Hydrogen forms ionic hydrides with s-block elements (e.g., NaH), covalent hydrides with p-block elements (e.g., CH₄, NH₃), and metallic or interstitial hydrides with d-block elements (e.g., TiH₂). The hydrogen bond, a strong dipole-dipole interaction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, F) and another electronegative atom, is responsible for the anomalously high boiling points of water, ammonia, and hydrogen fluoride, and for the structure of DNA and proteins.

2.4 Chemistry of Group 1 and Group 2 Elements

The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) are highly reactive, low-density metals with a single ns¹ valence electron. Their reactivity increases down the group as ionization enthalpy decreases. They react vigorously with water to form hydroxides MOH and hydrogen gas, with the reaction becoming more violent down the group. Their oxides M₂O, peroxides M₂O₂, and superoxides MO₂ show a trend: lithium forms the oxide, sodium forms the peroxide, and potassium, rubidium, and caesium form superoxides, reflecting the stabilization of larger anions by larger cations. All alkali metal salts are generally soluble in water except lithium fluoride and some double salts. The alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) have ns² configuration and are less reactive than alkali metals. Their reactivity with water increases down the group: beryllium does not react, magnesium reacts with hot water, and calcium, strontium, and barium react readily with cold water. The solubility of their hydroxides increases down the group, while the solubility of their sulfates and carbonates decreases. This is because lattice energy decreases more slowly than hydration energy for larger anions like sulfate and carbonate, but decreases faster for the smaller hydroxide ion. Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases down the group due to decreasing polarizing power of the cation. Beryllium and magnesium are extracted by electrolysis of their fused chlorides, while calcium, strontium, and barium are obtained by electrolysis of fused chlorides or reduction of oxides with aluminium. The biological importance of these elements is profound: sodium and potassium maintain osmotic balance and nerve impulse transmission, magnesium is central to chlorophyll, and calcium is essential for bones, muscle contraction, and blood clotting.

Worked Example: Predicting Solubility Trends
Explain why the solubility of alkaline earth metal hydroxides increases down the group while the solubility of their sulfates decreases down the group. Use thermodynamic concepts of lattice energy and hydration energy.
The solubility of an ionic compound in water depends on the balance between lattice energy (U), the energy released when gaseous ions form the solid lattice, and hydration energy (ΔHₕyd), the energy released when gaseous ions are hydrated. For dissolution to be favourable, the hydration energy must exceed the lattice energy. Down group 2, the cationic radius increases from Be²⁺ to Ba²⁺. For hydroxides, the anion OH⁻ is relatively small. As the cation size increases, the lattice energy decreases significantly because the distance between cation and anion increases (U ∝ 1/(r₊ + r₋)). The hydration energy of the cation also decreases, but the decrease in lattice energy is proportionally larger than the decrease in hydration energy for the small OH⁻ anion. Consequently, the net enthalpy of solution becomes more negative, and solubility increases. For sulfates, the anion SO₄²⁻ is large. The lattice energy is less sensitive to cation size because the interionic distance is dominated by the large anion radius. The decrease in lattice energy down the group is therefore smaller, while the hydration energy of the cation still decreases substantially. The net result is that the enthalpy of solution becomes less negative (or more positive), and solubility decreases. This explains why BeSO₄ and MgSO₄ are highly soluble while BaSO₄ is insoluble, whereas Be(OH)₂ is insoluble and Ba(OH)₂ is soluble.
Exam Insight: Thermodynamic Reasoning in Inorganic Chemistry
UPSC examiners frequently test the ability to apply lattice energy and hydration energy arguments to explain solubility, thermal stability, and decomposition temperatures. Do not merely state trends; always derive them from the interplay of cationic charge density, anionic size, and the Kapustinskii equation for lattice energy. A concise answer comparing the relative rates of change of U and ΔHₕyd for a given anion series scores full marks.

3. Descriptive Chemistry of the p-Block Elements

3.1 Group 13: The Boron Family and Inert Pair Effect

Group 13 elements (B, Al, Ga, In, Tl) have the valence configuration ns² np¹. Boron is a non-metal with a high ionization enthalpy and forms exclusively covalent compounds, while aluminium and the heavier elements are metals. The inert pair effect, the reluctance of the ns² electrons to participate in bonding, becomes pronounced down the group due to poor shielding of the nucleus by intervening d and f electrons, which increases the effective nuclear charge on the ns electrons. Consequently, the +1 oxidation state becomes more stable than the +3 state for thallium; Tl⁺ is stable while Tl³⁺ is a strong oxidizing agent. Boron forms electron-deficient compounds such as BF₃ and BCl₃ which act as Lewis acids, accepting a pair of electrons to complete the octet. Borax (Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O) and boric acid (H₃BO₃) are important compounds; boric acid acts as a weak monobasic Lewis acid by accepting OH⁻ from water: B(OH)₃ + H₂O ⇌ [B(OH)₄]⁻ + H⁺. Aluminium forms amphoteric oxide Al₂O₃ and hydroxide Al(OH)₃, and its halide AlCl₃ is a dimer Al₂Cl₆ in the vapour phase and a Lewis acid catalyst in Friedel-Crafts reactions. Gallium, indium, and thallium show increasing metallic character. The alloy gallium arsenide (GaAs) is a crucial semiconductor for high-speed electronics. The standard electrode potential for Al³⁺/Al is −1.66 V, making aluminium a strong reducing agent, but a protective oxide layer renders it passive in air.

3.2 Group 14: Carbon Allotropes, Silicates, and Silicones

Group 14 elements (C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb) show a transition from non-metal to metal down the group. Carbon exhibits allotropy: diamond (sp³ hybridized, three-dimensional network, hardest known substance, insulator), graphite (sp² hybridized, layered structure with delocalized π electrons, conductor, lubricant), fullerenes (C₆₀, C₇₀, spherical cages), carbon nanotubes, and graphene (single layer of graphite, exceptional tensile strength and electron mobility). The catenation ability of carbon is maximum due to strong C−C bonds (348 kJ mol⁻¹) and small size. Silicon forms silicates based on the SiO₄⁴⁻ tetrahedron which polymerizes by sharing corners to give chains (pyroxenes), sheets (micas), and three-dimensional frameworks (zeolites, feldspars). Zeolites are aluminosilicates with porous structures used as ion-exchangers in water softening and as shape-selective catalysts in petroleum cracking. Silicones are polymeric compounds with −(R₂Si−O)ₙ− backbones, prepared by hydrolysis of dialkyldichlorosilanes (R₂SiCl₂). They are thermally stable, water-repellent, and used as lubricants, sealants, and in medical implants. Germanium is a semiconductor. Tin has two allotropes: white tin (metallic, stable above 286 K) and grey tin (diamond structure, stable below 286 K); the transformation causes "tin pest". Lead forms stable Pb²⁺ due to the inert pair effect; Pb⁴⁺ compounds like PbO₂ are strong oxidizing agents. Organolead compounds such as tetraethyllead were used as antiknock agents but are phased out due to toxicity.

3.3 Group 15: Nitrogen, Phosphorus, and Their Oxoacids

Group 15 elements (N, P, As, Sb, Bi) have ns² np³ configuration. Nitrogen differs from the rest due to its small size, high electronegativity, absence of d orbitals, and ability to form pπ−pπ multiple bonds. It exists as a diatomic gas N₂ with a triple bond (bond dissociation energy 945 kJ mol⁻¹), making it inert at room temperature. Phosphorus exists as P₄ tetrahedra in white phosphorus (highly reactive, toxic, stored under water) and polymeric red phosphorus (less reactive). The hydrides NH₃, PH₃, AsH₃, SbH₃, BiH₃ show decreasing thermal stability and basicity down the group. Ammonia is a Lewis base and a ligand; phosphine is a weaker base. Nitrogen forms a series of oxides: N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂/N₂O₄, N₂O₅. NO and NO₂ are odd-electron molecules; NO dimerizes to N₂O₄ at low temperatures. The oxoacids of nitrogen include hyponitrous acid (H₂N₂O₂), nitrous acid (HNO₂), and nitric acid (HNO₃). Nitric acid is a strong acid and a powerful oxidizing agent; its reaction with metals gives different products depending on concentration and metal reactivity (e.g., Cu + 4HNO₃(conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O; Cu + 8HNO₃(dil.) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O). Phosphorus forms oxoacids in +3 and +5 oxidation states: phosphorous acid (H₃PO₃, diprotic, structure HPO(OH)₂), phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄, triprotic), hypophosphorous acid (H₃PO₂, monoprotic), and condensed phosphates like pyrophosphoric acid (H₄P₂O₇) and metaphosphoric acid (HPO₃)ₙ. The P−O bond strength and the ability to form P=O double bonds using d orbitals (or pπ−dπ bonding) stabilize the +5 state.

3.4 Group 16: Oxygen, Sulfur, and Sulfur Oxoacids

Group 16 elements (O, S, Se, Te, Po) are the chalcogens. Oxygen is a diatomic gas with a double bond; it is the most electronegative element after fluorine. It shows −2 oxidation state predominantly, but −1 in peroxides (H₂O₂, Na₂O₂), −½ in superoxides (KO₂), and +2 in OF₂. Ozone (O₃) is a bent resonance hybrid with bond order 1.5, a powerful oxidizing agent used in water purification. Sulfur exhibits allotropy: rhombic sulfur (α-S₈, stable below 369 K) and monoclinic sulfur (β-S₈, stable above 369 K), both consisting of puckered S₈ rings. Plastic sulfur is a polymeric form. Sulfur forms a rich series of oxoacids. Sulfurous acid (H₂SO₃) exists only in solution; its salts are sulfites. Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) is the "king of chemicals", manufactured by the contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (V₂O₅ catalyst, 720 K, 2 bar), SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇ (oleum), H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄. It is a strong dehydrating agent (charring sugar) and oxidizing agent (with Cu, C, S). Peroxomonosulfuric acid (H₂SO₅, Caro's acid) and peroxodisulfuric acid (H₂S₂O₈, Marshall's acid) contain −O−O− linkages and are strong oxidizers. Thiosulfuric acid (H₂S₂O₃) gives thiosulfate S₂O₃²⁻ used in photography (fixing) and iodine-thiosulfate titrations. The oxoacids of selenium and tellurium are analogous but less stable. Polonium is radioactive; Po²⁺ and Po⁴⁺ exist in solution.

3.5 Group 17: Halogens, Interhalogens, and Pseudohalogens

Group 17 elements (F, Cl, Br, I, At) are the halogens, existing as diatomic molecules X₂. Fluorine is the most reactive non-metal, strongest oxidizing agent (E° = +2.87 V for F₂/F⁻), and shows only −1 oxidation state due to absence of d orbitals and highest electronegativity. It reacts with water explosively to give O₂ and OF₂. Chlorine, bromine, and iodine show positive oxidation states (+1, +3, +5, +7) in compounds with oxygen and fluorine. The oxidizing power decreases down the group: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Chlorine disproportionates in cold dilute alkali: Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O, and in hot concentrated alkali: 3Cl₂ + 6OH⁻ → 5Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + 3H₂O. The oxoacids of chlorine are hypochlorous (HClO), chlorous (HClO₂), chloric (HClO₃), and perchloric (HClO₄) acids; acid strength increases with oxidation state due to electron withdrawal by oxygen. Perchloric acid is a strong acid; its salts are oxidizing agents. Interhalogen compounds XYₙ (n = 1, 3, 5, 7) are formed between different halogens; the larger halogen is the central atom. Examples: ClF, BrF₃, IF₅, IF₇. They are covalent, diamagnetic, and hydrolyze to give halide and oxyhalide ions. Pseudohalogens are species that behave like halogens: cyanogen (CN)₂, thiocyanogen (SCN)₂, and their anions CN⁻, SCN⁻, OCN⁻. They form dimers, hydrogen acids (HCN), and salts with metals.

3.6 Group 18: Noble Gas Compounds and Clathrates

Group 18 elements (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) were considered inert until Neil Bartlett synthesized Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻ in 1962, inspired by the similar ionization enthalpy of xenon (1170 kJ mol⁻¹) and O₂ (1175 kJ mol⁻¹). Xenon forms fluorides XeF₂ (linear), XeF₄ (square planar), XeF₆ (distorted octahedral) by direct reaction with fluorine under controlled conditions. Hydrolysis of XeF₆ gives XeO₃ (explosive) and XeOF₄. Xenon oxides include XeO₃ and XeO₄. Krypton forms KrF₂ under extreme conditions. Helium, neon, and argon form no stable neutral compounds at room temperature. Noble gases form clathrates (inclusion compounds) where gas atoms are trapped in the cavities of a host lattice, typically water ice (gas hydrates) or quinol. The stability of clathrates depends on the size match between guest atom and host cavity. Argon, krypton, and xenon form hydrates; helium and neon do not. Clathrates are used for noble gas separation and storage. Radon is radioactive; its chemistry is limited to fluorides and complexes studied at tracer concentrations. The discovery of noble gas compounds revolutionized the concept of chemical inertness and demonstrated that high oxidation states can be stabilized by highly electronegative ligands like fluorine and oxygen.

4. Transition Metals, Coordination Chemistry, and f-Block Elements

4.1 General Properties of d-Block Elements

The d-block elements (groups 3 to 12) are characterized by the progressive filling of (n−1)d orbitals. Their general electronic configuration is (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns⁰⁻². The first transition series (Sc to Zn) involves filling of 3d orbitals, the second (Y to Cd) 4d, and the third (La, Hf to Hg) 5d. Transition metals exhibit typical metallic properties: high melting and boiling points, high enthalpies of atomization, good electrical and thermal conductivity, and mechanical strength. These arise from strong metallic bonding involving both ns and (n−1)d electrons. Atomic radii decrease across a series due to increasing nuclear charge, but the decrease is smaller than in s- and p-blocks because added d electrons shield each other poorly. A notable feature is the lanthanide contraction: the steady decrease in size of lanthanide ions (La³⁺ to Lu³⁺) due to poor shielding by 4f electrons causes the radii of 4d and 5d series elements to be very similar (e.g., Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm), leading to nearly identical chemistry for pairs like Zr-Hf, Nb-Ta, Mo-W. Ionization enthalpies increase across a series but irregularly due to exchange energy stabilization of half-filled (d⁵) and fully-filled (d¹⁰) configurations. Variable oxidation states are a hallmark: from +2 to +7 in the first series, with maximum oxidation state equal to the group number up to Mn (group 7), then decreasing.
