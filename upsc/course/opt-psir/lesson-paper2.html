Key Concepts

PSIR Paper II — Comparative Politics and International Relations

Comparative Politics

  • Approaches: institutional, behavioural, structural-functional (Almond & Powell), systems approach (Easton), dependency and world-systems.
  • State in comparative perspective: liberal, Marxist, post-colonial and developmental state models.
  • Political economy of development: modernisation, dependency, import substitution, export-led growth, neo-liberalism.
  • Globalisation: economic, cultural and political dimensions; winners and losers; resistance movements.
  • Social movements and ethnicity: identity politics, federal accommodation, consociationalism.

International Relations

  • Theories: realism (Morgenthau, Waltz), neo-realism, liberalism, neo-liberal institutionalism, constructivism (Wendt), critical and feminist IR.
  • Cold War: bipolarity, containment, deterrence, proxy wars, détente, end of the Cold War.
  • Post-Cold War: unipolarity, multipolarity, rise of China, Russia–Ukraine war, US–China rivalry.
  • United Nations: Security Council reform, peacekeeping, specialised agencies, Sustainable Development Goals.
  • Regional organisations: EU, ASEAN, SAARC, BIMSTEC, African Union, Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, BRICS.
  • Global issues: terrorism, nuclear proliferation, arms control, climate change, human rights, humanitarian intervention.

India’s Foreign Policy

  • Foundations: non-alignment, Panchsheel, anti-colonialism, strategic autonomy.
  • Neighbourhood: Pakistan, China, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Afghanistan.
  • Major powers: India–US, India–Russia, India–EU, India–Japan, India–China.
  • Nuclear policy: No First Use, credible minimum deterrence, NSG and NPT issues.
  • Multilateralism: UN reform, G20, BRICS, SCO, Quad, Indo-Pacific strategy.

Area Studies

  • South Asia: SAARC, regional connectivity, China’s footprint.
  • West Asia: energy security, diaspora, Israel–Palestine, Iran nuclear deal.
  • Africa and Latin America: resource partnerships, South–South cooperation.
  • Indo-Pacific: freedom of navigation, QUAD, AUKUS, maritime security.
Solved Example: India’s Strategic Autonomy
Question: How is strategic autonomy different from non-alignment?
Answer: Non-alignment was a Cold War policy of not joining either bloc. Strategic autonomy is a broader post-Cold War posture: India retains freedom of decision-making, engages all major powers, and joins issue-based coalitions (Quad, SCO, BRICS) without formal alliance. It includes military purchases from Russia alongside deepening ties with the US — a balancing act rather than neutrality.

Common Traps

  • Confusing realism with neo-realism (Waltz’s systemic level).
  • Describing India’s foreign policy without doctrine names or dates.
  • Ignoring the difference between the UN General Assembly and Security Council powers.
  • Treating BRICS, SCO and Quad as identical groupings.
  • Writing current-affairs answers without theory.
Mains strategy
For IR answers: theory → event → India’s interest → options → evaluation. For comparative politics, use country examples and institutional comparisons. Keep a monthly current-affairs digest organised by region and institution, and revise India’s foreign-policy doctrines (Panchsheel, Gujral Doctrine, Look East/Act East, Neighbourhood First, Indo-Pacific).
