1. Comparative Politics: Foundations and Approaches

1.1 Institutional and Behavioural Approaches

The institutional approach treats formal structures such as constitutions, legislatures, executives and judiciaries as the primary units of analysis, arguing that rules and procedures shape political behaviour in predictable ways. Scholars working in this tradition compare the design of parliamentary versus presidential systems, the role of constitutional courts, and the impact of electoral laws on party systems across countries. The behavioural revolution of the 1950s and 1960s shifted attention from formal rules to the actual attitudes, beliefs and actions of political actors, introducing survey research, quantitative methods and a focus on political culture. Behaviouralists such as Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba demonstrated that civic culture — measured through trust, participation and competence — varies systematically between stable democracies and authoritarian regimes. By combining institutional design with empirical data on citizen orientations, contemporary comparative politics can explain why similar constitutional frameworks produce divergent outcomes in different societies.

In practice, a researcher might examine how the introduction of a mixed‑member proportional electoral system in New Zealand altered party fragmentation and coalition dynamics, while simultaneously measuring changes in voter turnout and partisan identification through longitudinal surveys. This dual lens reveals that institutions do not operate in a vacuum; they interact with pre‑existing social cleavages and political learning processes. Consequently, the institutional‑behavioural synthesis has become the dominant paradigm for explaining democratic consolidation, regime change and policy responsiveness across the globe.

1.2 Structural‑Functional and Systems Approaches

Structural‑functionalism, most famously articulated by Gabriel Almond and G. Bingham Powell, conceives the political system as a set of interrelated structures — interest articulation, interest aggregation, political communication, rule making, rule application and rule adjudication — each performing a function necessary for system maintenance. The approach assumes that all political systems, regardless of regime type, must perform these functions, and that the degree of specialization and differentiation of structures indicates the level of political development. David Easton’s systems theory complements this view by treating the polity as an open system that receives inputs (demands and supports) from the environment, processes them through a conversion mechanism, and produces outputs (decisions and actions) that feed back into the environment. The feedback loop is crucial: when outputs fail to satisfy demands, the system experiences stress that may lead to instability or regime change.

These frameworks have been applied to explain the resilience of the Indian democratic system despite deep social cleavages. The Indian party system, for instance, performs interest aggregation through a multiplicity of national and regional parties, while the federal structure provides a territorial channel for demand articulation. Easton’s feedback concept helps analysts understand how periodic elections, judicial review and civil‑society mobilisation act as corrective mechanisms that prevent systemic overload. Critics, however, argue that structural‑functionalism tends to be ahistorical and underestimates the role of power, coercion and ideological contestation in shaping political outcomes.

1.3 Dependency and World‑Systems Perspectives

Dependency theory emerged in Latin America in the 1960s as a critique of modernization theory, arguing that underdevelopment is not a stage but a structural condition produced by the integration of peripheral economies into a capitalist world system on unequal terms. Andre Gunder Frank’s “development of underdevelopment” thesis posits that surplus extraction from the periphery to the core creates a persistent development gap. Immanuel Wallerstein later expanded this insight into a world‑systems analysis that divides the globe into core, semi‑periphery and periphery zones, linked by a capitalist division of labour. In this view, political institutions in peripheral states are shaped by their position in the global economy; authoritarianism, weak state capacity and clientelism are seen as adaptations to external dependency rather than purely domestic failures.

Empirically, scholars have used world‑systems categories to compare the trajectories of East Asian developmental states — such as South Korea and Taiwan — with those of Sub‑Saharan African countries. The former managed to upgrade their position by pursuing export‑oriented industrialisation under strong state guidance, thereby moving from periphery toward semi‑periphery. The latter, constrained by commodity dependence and conditionalities imposed by international financial institutions, often remained locked in peripheral status. These perspectives remind students of comparative politics that domestic political phenomena cannot be fully understood without situating them in the broader architecture of global capitalism.

2. The State and Political Economy of Development

2.1 Liberal, Marxist and Post‑Colonial State Models

The liberal model of the state, rooted in the works of John Locke and later refined by Robert Nozick, envisions a neutral arbiter that protects property rights, enforces contracts and provides a minimal set of public goods. In this view, the state’s legitimacy derives from the consent of the governed and its limited interference in market transactions. The Marxist model, by contrast, treats the state as an instrument of class rule; following Lenin’s “State and Revolution”, the apparatus of coercion — police, military, bureaucracy — serves the interests of the bourgeoisie in capitalist societies and will be replaced by a dictatorship of the proletariat during the transition to socialism. Post‑colonial scholars such as Frantz Fanon and Partha Chatterjee argue that both liberal and Marxist templates are Eurocentric; they highlight how colonial rule produced a “derivative” state in the Global South, whose sovereignty is compromised by external economic pressures and whose legitimacy rests on a fragile synthesis of indigenous political traditions and imported institutional forms.

These three lenses generate distinct predictions about state behaviour in developing countries. A liberal analyst would emphasise the importance of rule‑of‑law reforms, anti‑corruption agencies and property‑rights enforcement to attract foreign investment. A Marxist analyst would focus on the ways in which land‑reform resistance, labour‑law dilution and privatisation serve elite accumulation. A post‑colonial analyst would examine how the rhetoric of “good governance” masks the continuation of unequal power relations inherited from colonial administration. In practice, most contemporary states exhibit hybrid characteristics, and comparative research increasingly adopts a pluralist methodological stance that draws on all three traditions.

2.2 Modernisation, Dependency and Neo‑Liberal Development Strategies

Modernisation theory, associated with Walt Rostow’s “Stages of Economic Growth”, posits that all societies progress through a linear sequence — traditional society, pre‑conditions for take‑off, take‑off, drive to maturity, and age of high mass consumption — driven by industrialisation, urbanisation and the spread of rational‑legal authority. The theory informed the development policies of the 1950s and 1960s, encouraging large‑scale infrastructure projects, import‑substitution industrialisation (ISI) and state‑led planning in newly independent countries. Dependency theorists challenged this optimism, arguing that ISI often deepened structural dependence on core economies by creating inefficient domestic industries protected behind high tariffs, while the terms of trade continued to deteriorate for primary‑commodity exporters.

The debt crises of the 1980s prompted a shift toward neo‑liberal prescriptions championed by the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank: fiscal austerity, trade liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation and the opening of capital accounts — collectively known as the Washington Consensus. Countries such as Chile under Pinochet and, later, India after 1991, adopted these reforms with varying outcomes. Chile experienced rapid export growth but also rising inequality; India’s liberalisation unleashed a services boom and lifted millions out of poverty, yet regional disparities and informal‑sector precarity persisted. The comparative lesson is that development strategies cannot be transplanted wholesale; their success depends on institutional capacity, social coalitions and the geopolitical context in which they are implemented.

2.3 Globalisation: Dimensions, Winners, Losers and Resistance

Globalisation operates along at least three intertwined dimensions: economic (cross‑border flows of goods, capital, technology and labour), cultural (diffusion of ideas, media, norms and identities) and political (the emergence of transnational governance institutions, norms and advocacy networks). The economic dimension has produced clear winners — multinational corporations, financial centres, skilled workers in export‑oriented sectors — and losers — unskilled workers in import‑competing industries, small farmers exposed to volatile commodity prices, and communities facing environmental degradation. Cultural globalisation has generated both homogenising pressures, evident in the spread of consumer brands and English as a lingua franca, and hybridising dynamics, as local traditions reinterpret global forms. Politically, globalisation has empowered non‑state actors such as NGOs, epistemic communities and terrorist networks, while challenging the Westphalian monopoly of the sovereign state.

Resistance to globalisation takes multiple forms: anti‑globalisation protests at World Trade Organization ministerials, the rise of populist nationalist parties advocating protectionism and border controls, and transnational movements for climate justice, labour rights and indigenous sovereignty. The 1999 Seattle protests, the 2011 Arab Spring uprisings, and the recent “Yellow Vest” movement in France illustrate how economic grievances intertwine with demands for political representation. Comparative scholars study these movements to understand the conditions under which globalisation triggers democratic deepening versus authoritarian retrenchment, noting that the presence of strong civil‑society institutions and inclusive political parties tends to channel discontent into reform rather than rupture.

Solved Example: Comparing Development Strategies
Question: Explain why import‑substitution industrialisation succeeded in Brazil during the 1950s‑1970s but faltered in many African countries in the same period.
Answer: Brazil possessed a relatively large domestic market, a nascent industrial bourgeoisie, and a state bureaucracy capable of directing credit to strategic sectors such as automotive and petrochemicals. The military regime after 1964 provided political stability and coordinated planning through agencies like BNDES, allowing economies of scale and technology transfer. In contrast, most African states inherited fragmented colonial economies, weak administrative capacity, and small internal markets; ISI projects there often resulted in high‑cost, low‑quality production dependent on imported inputs. External shocks — oil price hikes, commodity price collapses — exposed the fiscal fragility of African ISI, leading to balance‑of‑payments crises and eventual structural‑adjustment programmes. The comparison shows that the same policy framework yields divergent outcomes when underlying structural conditions differ.
Answer‑Writing Insight
When a question asks for a comparative evaluation of development models, structure the answer by first stating the theoretical logic of each model, then presenting two concise country cases that illustrate success and failure, and finally linking the outcomes to specific institutional variables such as state capacity, market size and elite cohesion. This theory‑case‑mechanism chain satisfies the examiner’s expectation for analytical depth.

3. International Relations: Theory and Historical Evolution

3.1 Classical Realism and Neo‑Realism

Classical realism, rooted in the writings of Thucydides, Machiavelli and Hans Morgenthau, argues that international politics is driven by an immutable human nature characterised by the will to power. Morgenthau’s “Politics Among Nations” (1948) distils this insight into six principles, the most famous being that politics is governed by objective laws rooted in human nature and that the concept of interest defined in terms of power is the key to understanding state behaviour. Neo‑realism, or structural realism, developed by Kenneth Waltz in “Theory of International Politics” (1979), shifts the explanatory variable from human nature to the anarchic structure of the international system. Waltz contends that because there is no overarching authority, states must rely on self‑help; the distribution of capabilities — polarity — determines the constraints and opportunities facing each unit. In a bipolar system, two great powers dominate and balance each other; in a multipolar system, fluid alliances and miscalculation increase the probability of war.

The distinction between the two variants matters for policy analysis. Classical realists advise statesmen to cultivate prudence, diplomacy and a nuanced appreciation of the adversary’s motives, while neo‑realists emphasise the structural imperatives that limit the scope for agency — for example, the security dilemma that makes arms races rational even when no state desires conflict. Empirically, the Cold War bipolar order is often cited as a validation of neo‑realist predictions: the United States and the Soviet Union maintained a stable balance of terror through nuclear deterrence and alliance systems (NATO and the Warsaw Pact). However, the peaceful end of the Cold War and the subsequent unipolar moment challenged the deterministic claim that polarity alone dictates outcomes, prompting scholars to incorporate domestic politics, norms and ideas into realist frameworks.

3.2 Liberalism, Neo‑Liberal Institutionalism and Constructivism

Liberal IR theory traces its lineage to Immanuel Kant’s “Perpetual Peace” (1795) and the post‑World War I idealism of Woodrow Wilson. Its core propositions are that democracies rarely fight each other (the democratic peace), that economic interdependence raises the cost of conflict, and that international institutions can mitigate anarchy by providing information, reducing transaction costs and facilitating cooperation. Neo‑liberal institutionalism, advanced by Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye in “Power and Interdependence” (1977), refines these claims by arguing that even in an anarchic system, regimes — sets of implicit or explicit principles, norms, rules and decision‑making procedures — enable states to achieve mutually beneficial outcomes without a central enforcer. The European Union’s single market and the World Trade Organization’s dispute‑settlement mechanism are frequently cited as empirical confirmations.

Constructivism, associated with Alexander Wendt’s seminal article “Anarchy is What States Make of It” (1992), challenges the materialist ontology of both realism and liberalism. Wendt argues that the structures of international politics are not given by the distribution of material capabilities alone but are constituted by shared ideas, identities and norms. For instance, the transformation of the US‑Soviet relationship from mortal enemies to partners in arms control was not merely a shift in power balances but a redefinition of each other’s identity from “enemy” to “rival” and eventually to “partner”. Constructivists therefore study how norms such as the prohibition of chemical weapons, the responsibility to protect, or the taboo against nuclear use emerge, spread and become internalised by states. This perspective has enriched IR by showing that material power and institutional design operate within a socially constructed context that can change over time.

3.3 Critical, Feminist and Post‑Structural IR

Critical theory in IR, drawing on the Frankfurt School and the work of Robert Cox, insists that all theories are “for someone and for some purpose”. Cox’s distinction between problem‑solving theory (which takes the world as it is) and critical theory (which asks how the world came to be and how it might be transformed) underpins a research agenda that interrogates the power‑knowledge nexus in global politics. Feminist IR, pioneered by scholars such as Cynthia Enloe and J. Ann Tickner, exposes the gendered assumptions embedded in concepts like sovereignty, security and the state. Enloe’s “Bananas, Beaches and Bases” (1990) demonstrates how the global economy relies on the invisible labour of women in plantations, factories and military bases, while Tickner redefines security to include structural violence, reproductive rights and the gendered impact of war. Post‑structuralists such as Richard Ashley and David Campbell deconstruct the binary oppositions — sovereign/anarchic, civilised/barbarian — that legitimise particular foreign‑policy practices. Together, these approaches broaden the analytical toolkit, reminding students that the categories we use to describe world politics are themselves sites of political contestation.

3.4 Cold War Bipolarity, Détente and the End of the Cold War

The Cold War (1947‑1991) instantiated a textbook bipolar system in which the United States and the Soviet Union commanded rival alliance networks, ideological blocs and nuclear arsenals. The doctrine of containment, articulated by George Kennan in the “Long Telegram” (1946) and later in the “X Article” (1947), guided US strategy of encircling Soviet influence through military alliances, economic aid (the Marshall Plan) and covert operations. The Soviet response combined the establishment of the Warsaw Pact (1955), support for communist movements in the Third World, and the development of a massive nuclear deterrent. The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) brought the world to the brink of nuclear war and subsequently spurred a period of détente characterised by arms‑control agreements such as the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I, 1972) and the Helsinki Accords (1975), which linked security, economic cooperation and human rights.

Détente unravelled in the late 1970s due to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979), the election of Ronald Reagan (1980) and the deployment of Pershing II missiles in Europe. The final phase of the Cold War saw Mikhail Gorbachev’s policies of glasnost and perestroika, which opened Soviet society to criticism and reduced the ideological rigidity that sustained the bipolar order. The fall of the Berlin Wall (1989), the reunification of Germany (1990) and the dissolution of the Soviet Union (1991) marked the transition to a unipolar moment dominated by the United States. Realist scholars debate whether the peaceful termination validates the stabilizing effect of nuclear deterrence or reflects the unique role of leadership ideas; liberal scholars emphasise the role of transnational civil‑society movements and the attractiveness of the Western normative order.

3.5 Post‑Cold War Unipolarity, Multipolarity and Emerging Rivalries

The immediate post‑Cold War era (1991‑2008) is often described as a unipolar system in which the United States enjoyed unrivalled military, economic and cultural preponderance. This period witnessed the expansion of NATO, the proliferation of humanitarian interventions (Somalia, Bosnia, Kosovo), and the consolidation of a liberal international order anchored in the United Nations, the World Trade Organization and the Bretton Woods institutions. However, the 2008 global financial crisis, the rise of China as an economic powerhouse, the resurgence of Russia under Vladimir Putin, and the diffusion of technology to non‑state actors have eroded unipolarity. Contemporary analysts speak of an emerging multipolar or “multiplex” order in which power is distributed among the United States, China, the European Union, India, Japan, Brazil and regional powers, while institutions such as the G20, BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation provide overlapping forums for coordination and contestation.

The US‑China rivalry now structures great‑power competition across trade, technology (5G, semiconductors, artificial intelligence), maritime security (South China Sea, Taiwan Strait) and normative influence (human rights, democracy promotion). Russia’s annexation of Crimea (2014) and the full‑scale invasion of Ukraine (2022) have re‑introduced conventional war in Europe, prompting NATO’s revitalisation and a re‑orientation of European defence policy. India’s strategic autonomy doctrine positions it as a swing state that engages both the Quad (with the US, Japan, Australia) and the SCO (with China, Russia), illustrating how middle powers navigate a fragmented order. For students, the key analytical task is to assess whether these trends point toward a new bipolarity (US vs China), a stable multipolar concert, or a chaotic “interregnum” where no single ordering principle prevails.

4. International Organisations and Global Issues

4.1 United Nations: Structure, Reform and Peacekeeping

The United Nations, established in 1945 by the San Francisco Conference, comprises six principal organs: the General Assembly, the Security Council, the Economic and Social Council, the Secretariat, the International Court of Justice and the Trusteeship Council (now inactive). The Security Council holds primary responsibility for international peace and security; its five permanent members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, the United States) possess veto power, a feature that has both preserved great‑power consensus and paralysed action in crises such as Syria (2011‑present) and Ukraine (2022‑present). Reform debates focus on expanding permanent membership to reflect contemporary power realities — candidates include India, Brazil, Germany, Japan (the G4) and African states — and on limiting the veto in cases of mass atrocities. Peacekeeping, though not mentioned in the Charter, evolved into a flagship activity; as of 2023 over 80,000 personnel serve in missions ranging from traditional ceasefire monitoring (UNMOGIP in Kashmir) to multidimensional operations with civilian protection mandates (MONUSCO in the Democratic Republic of the Congo). The effectiveness of peacekeeping depends on robust mandates, adequate resources, and the political will of troop‑contributing countries and the Security Council.

4.2 Regional Organisations: EU, ASEAN, SAARC, BIMSTEC, AU, SCO, BRICS

Regional organisations illustrate the diversity of institutional design and functional scope. The European Union represents the most advanced form of supranational integration, with a single market, a common currency (the euro) for 20 members, a directly elected parliament, and a legal order that supersedes national law in many domains. ASEAN, by contrast, operates on the “ASEAN Way” of consensus, non‑interference and informal diplomacy, which has facilitated dialogue in a heterogeneous region but limited the depth of economic integration. SAARC, founded in 1985, has been hamstrung by Indo‑Pakistani tensions; its summit meetings have frequently been postponed, and intra‑regional trade remains below 5 percent of total trade. BIMSTEC, linking South and Southeast Asia, offers an alternative platform that bypasses the Pakistan veto and focuses on connectivity, energy and counter‑terrorism. The African Union, successor to the OAU, has developed a Peace and Security Council with the authority to intervene in member states under Article 4(h) of its Constitutive Act, a notable departure from strict sovereignty norms. The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, originally a border‑demarcation forum, has expanded into counter‑terrorism, energy cooperation and, since 2017, includes India and Pakistan as full members. BRICS, an informal grouping of Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa, serves as a platform for South‑South coordination on financial reform (the New Development Bank) and multilateral diplomacy, though its members hold divergent strategic interests.

4.3 Transnational Challenges: Terrorism, Proliferation, Climate Change, Human Rights

Transnational challenges defy state‑centric solutions and demand coordinated multilateral responses. Terrorism, especially after the 11 September 2001 attacks, prompted the UN Security Council to adopt Resolution 1373 (2001), establishing the Counter‑Terrorism Committee and obligating states to criminalise terrorist financing, share intelligence and deny safe havens. The Global Counterterrorism Forum and the Financial Action Task Force complement these efforts. Nuclear proliferation is governed by the Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT, 1968), the International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards system, and export‑control regimes such as the Nuclear Suppliers Group. The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) illustrated how diplomacy can constrain a threshold state’s nuclear programme, though its subsequent unravelling underscores the fragility of such agreements. Climate change, addressed through the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement (2015), requires nationally determined contributions that are collectively insufficient to limit warming to 1.5 °C; the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities remains a flashpoint in negotiations. Human rights, codified in the Universal Declaration (1948) and subsequent covenants, are monitored by treaty bodies, the Human Rights Council and special rapporteurs, yet enforcement relies on state consent and political pressure, leading to accusations of selectivity and double standards.

5. India’s Foreign Policy: Doctrines, Neighbourhood and Major Powers

5.1 Foundations: Non‑Alignment, Panchsheel and Strategic Autonomy

India’s foreign‑policy identity was forged in the early years of independence under Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru. The principle of non‑alignment, articulated at the Bandung Conference (1955) and formalised through the Non‑Aligned Movement (1961), sought to preserve India’s sovereignty by refusing membership in either the Western or Eastern bloc during the Cold War. The Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence (Panchsheel), signed with China in 1954, enshrined mutual respect for territorial integrity, non‑aggression, non‑interference, equality and peaceful coexistence. These doctrines reflected a moral‑political stance rooted in anti‑colonial solidarity and a pragmatic calculation that alignment would constrain India’s developmental choices.
