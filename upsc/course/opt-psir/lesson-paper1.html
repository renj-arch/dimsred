1. Foundations of Political Theory

1.1 The Normative and Empirical Traditions

The study of political theory begins with a distinction between normative inquiry, which asks how politics ought to be organised, and empirical inquiry, which seeks to describe and explain political behaviour as it actually occurs. Normative traditions draw on the philosophical legacy of Plato and Aristotle, insisting that concepts such as justice, virtue and the common good provide standards against which real institutions can be judged. Empirical traditions, by contrast, emerged in the late nineteenth century with the rise of behaviouralism, which borrowed methods from the natural sciences to treat political actors as observable units whose choices could be measured, correlated and predicted. The behavioural revolution of the 1950s, led by scholars such as David Easton and Gabriel Almond, shifted the discipline toward hypothesis testing, survey research and quantitative analysis. Post‑behaviouralism, articulated by Easton himself in the 1960s, warned that an exclusive focus on measurable regularities risked ignoring the normative questions that give politics its meaning. Marxist, feminist and post‑colonial approaches further expanded the empirical agenda by insisting that power relations rooted in class, gender and empire shape both the production of knowledge and the lived experience of politics. Together these traditions constitute a pluralistic field in which normative aspirations and empirical findings constantly interrogate each other.

1.2 Theories of the State: Liberal, Marxist, Pluralist and Post‑colonial Perspectives

Liberal theory, rooted in the social‑contract writings of John Locke and John Stuart Mill, conceives the state as a neutral arbiter that protects individual rights, enforces contracts and provides a framework within which free citizens pursue their conceptions of the good. Neo‑liberal variants, associated with Friedrich Hayek and Robert Nozick, shrink the state’s role to the minimal functions of defence, police and courts, arguing that any redistribution beyond the protection of property rights violates individual liberty. Marxist theory, by contrast, treats the state as an instrument of class domination: the instrumentalist strand, following Lenin, sees the state as a direct tool of the capitalist class, while the structuralist strand, developed by Nicos Poulantzas, argues that the state’s relative autonomy serves the long‑term reproduction of capitalism. Pluralist theory, championed by Robert Dahl, depicts the state as an arena where multiple interest groups compete, with no single group able to dominate permanently. Post‑colonial scholars such as Partha Chatterjee and Frantz Fanon contend that the modern state form was imposed on colonised societies, creating a bifurcated political society in which elite institutions mimic Western models while the majority remain excluded from meaningful participation. Feminist theorists, including Carole Pateman and Iris Marion Young, add that the state’s public‑private distinction systematically marginalises women’s experiences and reproduces patriarchal power.

1.3 Justice, Equality and Rights: Rawls, Nozick, Sen and Communitarian Critiques

John Rawls’s A Theory of Justice (1971) revitalised normative political philosophy by proposing two principles chosen behind a veil of ignorance: first, each person has an equal right to the most extensive basic liberties compatible with similar liberties for others; second, social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least advantaged — the difference principle. This framework links justice to fairness and provides a benchmark for evaluating distributive policies such as progressive taxation or affirmative action. Robert Nozick’s Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) counters with an entitlement theory that treats justice as historical: a distribution is just if it arises from a just initial acquisition and voluntary transfers, rejecting patterned principles that require continual interference with holdings. Amartya Sen’s capability approach shifts the metric from primary goods to the real freedoms people enjoy to achieve valuable functionings, arguing that justice demands the expansion of capabilities such as health, education and political participation. Communitarian critics, notably Michael Sandel and Alasdair MacIntyre, contend that Rawls’s abstract individualism neglects the embeddedness of persons in shared traditions and that a conception of the good cannot be bracketed out of political deliberation. These debates shape contemporary Indian discourse on reservation, welfare schemes and the interpretation of fundamental rights.

1.4 Democracy, Power and Ideology: Procedural vs Substantive Models, Lukes’s Three Faces

Procedural definitions of democracy focus on the presence of competitive elections, universal suffrage, civil liberties and the rule of law, treating these institutions as sufficient for democratic legitimacy. Substantive models, advocated by theorists such as Carole Pateman and Joshua Cohen, argue that democracy also requires meaningful participation, deliberative equality and the redistribution of resources so that all citizens can effectively exercise their formal rights. Steven Lukes’s three faces of power deepen this analysis: the first face concerns observable decision‑making in open conflict; the second face involves agenda‑setting that prevents certain issues from reaching the decision stage; the third face shapes preferences and perceptions so that dominated groups may accept their subordination as natural. Ideologies provide the normative vocabularies through which these power relations are justified or contested. Liberalism champions individual autonomy and limited government; conservatism values tradition, hierarchy and organic community; socialism and Marxism prioritise collective ownership and class emancipation; fascism glorifies the nation‑state and authoritarian leadership; Gandhism emphasises non‑violent resistance, trusteeship and village self‑rule; feminism interrogates gendered power structures; ecologism extends moral consideration to the non‑human world. Understanding these ideologies is essential for analysing party manifestos, social movements and policy debates in India and beyond.

2. Major Traditions and Thinkers in Political Thought

2.1 Classical Foundations: Plato, Aristotle and Machiavelli

Plato’s Republic constructs an ideal city ruled by philosopher‑kings, arguing that justice consists in each class performing its proper function and that knowledge of the Forms qualifies rulers to govern. Aristotle, in the Politics, rejects the guardian class, classifying constitutions by the number of rulers and their aim, and famously defines man as a political animal whose flourishing requires participation in the polis. He distinguishes between correct constitutions — monarchy, aristocracy, polity — and their perversions — tyranny, oligarchy, democracy — and introduces the concept of the mixed regime as a stabilising blend. Niccolò Machiavelli’s The Prince and Discourses on Livy break with the normative tradition by analysing power as it is exercised, advising rulers to master fortuna through virtù, and praising republican institutions that channel conflict into constructive competition. These three thinkers establish the vocabulary of virtue, justice, regime classification and the realism‑idealism tension that continues to structure political theory.

2.2 Social Contract and Liberalism: Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Mill

Thomas Hobbes’s Leviathan (1651) posits a state of nature characterised by a war of all against all, leading rational individuals to surrender their natural rights to an absolute sovereign in exchange for security. John Locke’s Second Treatise of Government (1689) modifies this picture by asserting that natural rights to life, liberty and property pre‑exist the state, and that government’s legitimacy rests on the consent of the governed, with a right of revolution when trust is violated. Jean‑Jacques Rousseau’s The Social Contract (1762) replaces individual consent with the general will, arguing that true freedom is obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself, and that sovereignty resides in the collective body politic. John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty (1859) and Considerations on Representative Government (1861) defend individual liberty against both state tyranny and the tyranny of the majority, introducing the harm principle and advocating proportional representation, plural voting and the protection of minority opinions. Together these theorists lay the groundwork for modern constitutionalism, the doctrine of limited government and the liberal emphasis on rights, representation and deliberative democracy.

2.3 Critical Traditions: Marx, Gandhi, Ambedkar and Tagore

Karl Marx’s historical materialism, articulated in The Communist Manifesto (1848) and Capital (1867), analyses capitalism as a system of exploitation rooted in the appropriation of surplus value, predicts its crisis tendencies and envisions a communist society based on the free association of producers. Mahatma Gandhi’s political philosophy, expressed in Hind Swaraj (1909) and his practice of satyagraha, rejects the modern state’s centralised violence, proposing instead a decentralized order of self‑sufficient villages, trusteeship of wealth and non‑violent resistance as a method of moral conversion. B. R. Ambedkar, architect of the Indian Constitution, combines a radical critique of caste as a graded inequality with a liberal commitment to constitutional morality, insisting that social democracy must precede political democracy and advocating affirmative action, the abolition of untouchability and the protection of minority rights. Rabindranath Tagore, in essays such as Nationalism (1917), warns against the idolisation of the nation‑state, championing a universal humanism that transcends territorial borders and celebrates cultural pluralism. These thinkers provide distinct lenses — class, non‑violence, caste, cosmopolitanism — through which Indian politics can be interrogated.

2.4 Contemporary Debates: Feminism, Ecologism and Post‑colonial Theory

Feminist political theory, from the liberal feminism of Mary Wollstonecraft to the radical feminism of Catharine MacKinnon and the intersectional feminism of Kimberlé Crenshaw, exposes how the public‑private divide, reproductive labour and gendered citizenship sustain male dominance. Ecologism, represented by Arne Naess’s deep ecology and the political ecology of Ramachandra Guha, challenges anthropocentric conceptions of progress, arguing for the intrinsic value of ecosystems and the redistribution of ecological space. Post‑colonial theory, advanced by Edward Said, Gayatri Spivak and Homi Bhabha, deconstructs the epistemic violence of colonial knowledge production and examines how former colonies negotiate sovereignty, identity and development within a global order still shaped by imperial hierarchies. In the Indian context, these debates inform discussions on the Uniform Civil Code, forest rights legislation, climate justice and the politics of representation for Dalits, Adivasis, women and religious minorities.

Solved Example: Rawls’s Difference Principle
Question: Explain Rawls’s difference principle and illustrate how it would apply to a policy on progressive taxation.
Answer: Rawls argues that social and economic inequalities are permissible only when they improve the position of the least advantaged members of society. Behind the veil of ignorance rational agents would choose the difference principle together with the principle of equal basic liberties. In a progressive tax regime the higher rates on top incomes generate revenue that funds public goods such as health care and education, thereby raising the welfare of the poorest. Critics contend that the principle is difficult to operationalise because it requires a precise identification of the least advantaged group and may ignore individual desert.
Answer‑writing insight
When a question asks for a thinker’s thesis, first state the core proposition in one sentence, then unpack the supporting arguments, cite a key text, present a major criticism, and conclude with the concept’s relevance to contemporary Indian debates such as reservation policy or fiscal federalism.

3. Indian Constitutional Framework and Institutional Design

3.1 The Constituent Assembly and the Philosophy of the Constitution

The Constituent Assembly, convened in December 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan, comprised 299 members elected by provincial legislatures and representatives of princely states. Its deliberations, chaired by Dr. Rajendra Prasad and guided by the drafting committee led by B. R. Ambedkar, produced a document that came into force on 26 January 1950. The Preamble declares India a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic committed to justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. The Assembly’s debates reveal a synthesis of liberal constitutionalism, Gandhian moral politics and Ambedkarite social justice, reflected in the inclusion of both justiciable Fundamental Rights (Part III) and non‑justiciable Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV). The adoption of a parliamentary system with a ceremonial President and a responsible Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister reflects the British Westminster model, while the federal division of powers in the Seventh Schedule and the provision for a strong Centre draw on the Government of India Act 1935. The Constitution’s amendability under Article 368, balanced by the basic structure doctrine, ensures both flexibility and the preservation of core constitutional values.

3.2 Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Fundamental Rights guarantee civil liberties such as equality before law (Article 14), freedom of speech (Article 19), protection against arbitrary detention (Article 21) and the right to constitutional remedies (Article 32). The Directive Principles, though non‑enforceable, obligate the state to pursue social and economic goals — adequate livelihood, equal pay, education, public health — and have been used by courts to interpret the scope of Fundamental Rights. The basic structure doctrine, articulated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), holds that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 cannot alter the Constitution’s essential features: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism and the rule of law. Subsequent cases — Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) and I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) — have refined the doctrine, establishing judicial review as a guardian of constitutional identity. This jurisprudence shapes the limits of legislative action on issues ranging from land reform to the National Judicial Appointments Commission.

3.3 Parliamentary Executive: President, Prime Minister and Council of Ministers

The President, elected by an electoral college of Parliament and state legislatures, exercises executive power on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, as mandated by Article 74. While the President’s role is largely ceremonial, discretionary powers — such as appointing the Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority, dissolving the Lok Sabha, and granting pardons under Article 72 — become politically significant during hung parliaments or coalition crises. The Prime Minister, as the leader of the majority party or coalition in the Lok Sabha, drives the legislative agenda, chairs the Cabinet and represents India internationally. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, and a vote of no‑confidence can force its resignation. The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review and enhance executive authority, but the 44th Amendment (1978) restored many checks. The dynamics of prime‑ministerial power have varied from the dominant single‑party era under Nehru and Indira Gandhi to the coalition constraints of the 1990s and the renewed single‑party majority after 2014.

3.4 Judiciary: Supreme Court, Judicial Review and Landmark Cases

The Supreme Court, established under Article 124, is the apex judicial body with original, appellate and advisory jurisdiction. Its power of judicial review, though not explicitly enumerated, derives from Articles 13, 32, 136 and 142, enabling it to strike down laws and executive actions that violate Fundamental Rights or the basic structure. Landmark decisions illustrate the Court’s role as a constitutional arbiter: Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) held that Fundamental Rights cannot be amended; Kesavananda Bharati (1973) introduced the basic structure doctrine; Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded Article 21 to include due process; S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down guidelines for the imposition of President’s Rule under Article 356; Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) created guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace; Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations; and Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) recognised privacy as a fundamental right. These rulings demonstrate the Court’s capacity to shape public policy, protect minorities and mediate centre‑state disputes.

4.

Federalism and Centre‑State Relations: Legislative, Administrative and Financial Dimensions

The Indian Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal structure in which the Union enjoys a preponderant role while states retain significant autonomy in areas listed in the State List (List II) of the Seventh Schedule. Legislative competence is divided into three lists — Union (List I), State (List II) and Concurrent (List III) — with the Union Parliament empowered to legislate on Concurrent subjects under Article 246 and to override state laws on those subjects under Article 254. The residuary power vests in the Union (Article 248), a feature borrowed from the Government of India Act 1935 and distinct from the United States model where residuary powers belong to the states.

Administrative relations are governed by Articles 256‑263. The Union can issue directions to states for compliance with Union laws (Art. 256) and for the construction and maintenance of means of communication of national importance (Art. 257). The President may appoint a Governor who acts as the Union’s representative in the state (Art. 155) and can assume the functions of the state government under President’s Rule (Art. 356). The Inter‑State Council (Art. 263) and the National Development Council provide institutional forums for coordination, though their recommendations are non‑binding.

Financial federalism is anchored in the Finance Commission (Art. 280) which recommends the vertical and horizontal distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Centre and the states. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) introduced by the 101st Amendment (2016) created a dual GST model with a GST Council (Art. 279A) where the Union and states jointly decide rates, thresholds and exemptions. The Centre retains control over major revenue sources — income tax, corporate tax, customs and central excise — while states depend heavily on grants‑in‑aid (Art. 275) and centrally sponsored schemes, creating a fiscal asymmetry that fuels demands for greater devolution.

Judicial interpretation has shaped the balance. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court laid down strict guidelines for the imposition of President’s Rule, requiring a floor test and limiting the Centre’s discretion. In State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) the Court upheld the Union’s power to legislate on Concurrent subjects even when a state law occupies the field, provided the Union law is not repugnant. The Kesavananda Bharati basic‑structure doctrine also protects federalism as an essential feature, preventing constitutional amendments that would convert the Union into a unitary state.

Contemporary tensions arise from the use of Article 356, the allocation of GST compensation, and the Centre’s increasing reliance on centrally sponsored schemes that bypass state legislatures. The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended a 41 % vertical share for states, a modest increase over the 14th Commission’s 42 % after adjusting for the GST compensation cess, illustrating the ongoing negotiation of fiscal federalism.

Emergency Provisions: Article 352, Article 356 and Article 360 and Judicial Scrutiny

The Constitution provides three distinct emergency powers. Article 352 empowers the President to proclaim a National Emergency on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion. Such a proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority (two‑thirds of members present and voting, and a majority of total membership). During a National Emergency, the Union can legislate on State List subjects (Art. 250), extend the life of the Lok Sabha by one year at a time (Art. 83), and suspend the enforcement of Fundamental Rights under Article 19 (Art. 358) and the right to move courts for enforcement of other rights (Art. 359), subject to the 44th Amendment’s safeguard that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended.

Article 356 (President’s Rule) allows the President to assume the functions of a state government when the constitutional machinery fails. The proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months and can be extended for a maximum of three years with periodic parliamentary approval every six months. The 44th Amendment (1978) introduced the requirement that the proclamation be based on the Governor’s report or otherwise, and that the state legislative assembly not be dissolved without parliamentary approval. The Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai (1994) held that the proclamation is subject to judicial review, that the material facts must be placed before Parliament, and that the floor test is the decisive proof of majority.

Article 360 provides for a Financial Emergency when the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. It has never been invoked. If proclaimed, the Union can direct states to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of public servants, and require all money bills to be reserved for the President’s consideration. Parliamentary approval is required within two months, and the proclamation continues until revoked.

Judicial scrutiny has progressively constrained executive discretion. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) the Court struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that sought to make Emergency proclamations immune from judicial review. In Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) the Court reiterated that the satisfaction of the President under Article 356 is justiciable and that the Governor’s report must contain relevant material. The 44th Amendment also restored the pre‑1976 position that the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, limiting unilateral action.

These provisions illustrate the tension between central authority and federal autonomy. The historical misuse of Article 356 during the 1970s and 1980s, the 1975‑77 National Emergency, and the never‑used Financial Emergency together shape the constitutional discourse on emergency powers.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Explain why Article 356 is considered a 'dead letter' after the S. R. Bommai judgment." Provide a concise answer that references the judgment’s key holdings.
The S. R. Bommai (1994) judgment did not render Article 356 a dead letter; it subjected the proclamation to strict judicial review. The Court held that (1) the President’s satisfaction must be based on objective material, not merely the Governor’s report; (2) the state legislative assembly cannot be dissolved before Parliament approves the proclamation; (3) a floor test is the constitutional method to ascertain majority; and (4) the proclamation is justiciable on grounds of mala fides or irrelevance. Consequently, the Centre’s arbitrary dismissal of state governments has been sharply curtailed, but the provision remains operative for genuine breakdowns of constitutional machinery.
Exam‑day strategy
When answering questions on emergency provisions, structure the response in three layers: (i) constitutional text and procedural safeguards, (ii) landmark judicial pronouncements with case names and years, (iii) practical impact on centre‑state relations. Use a comparative table for Articles 352, 356, 360 to show differences in grounds, parliamentary approval, duration and judicial review. This fetches both knowledge and analytical marks.

Panchayati Raj and Urban Local Governance: The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments

The 73rd Amendment (1992) inserted Part IX (Articles 243‑243O) to constitutionally entrench rural local self‑government. It mandates a three‑tier Panchayati Raj system — Gram Panchayat at the village level, Panchayat Samiti at the intermediate (block) level, and Zilla Parishad at the district level — though states with a population below 20 lakhs may omit the intermediate tier. Elections are held every five years under the superintendence of a State Election Commission (Art. 243K). Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women (not less than one‑third) in direct elections and in the offices of chairpersons (Art. 243D). The State Finance Commission (Art. 243I) reviews the financial position of panchayats and recommends devolution of taxes, duties, tolls and grants‑in‑aid.

The 74th Amendment (1992) added Part IXA (Articles 243P‑243ZG) for urban local bodies — Municipal Corporations, Municipal Councils and Nagar Panchayats — based on population size and density. It mirrors the rural framework: regular elections, reservations, a State Election Commission, and a State Finance Commission. Additionally, it provides for District Planning Committees (Art. 243ZD) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (Art. 243ZE) to integrate spatial planning with economic development.

Implementation varies widely. Kerala and West Bengal devolved substantial functions (29 subjects under the Eleventh Schedule) and finances, enabling participatory planning through the People’s Plan Campaign. In contrast, many states retain control over key functions — water supply, sanitation, primary health — and rely on tied grants, limiting fiscal autonomy. The 14th Finance Commission (2015‑20) recommended a 4.3 % share of the divisible pool for local bodies, later raised to 4.5 % by the 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25), but the actual transfer depends on state compliance with audit and accounting standards.

Judicial interventions have reinforced the constitutional mandate. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (2005) the Supreme Court held that the State Election Commission’s independence is part of the basic structure. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2021) the Court directed timely elections to panchayats, rejecting the state’s plea of administrative inconvenience. The Court also ruled that the reservation rotation for women must follow the constitutional formula, not ad‑hoc executive orders.

Challenges remain: inadequate own‑source revenue, dependence on tied central schemes (e.g., MGNREGS, Swachh Bharat), limited capacity of elected representatives, and the persistence of parallel bureaucratic structures. The 2022 Model Panchayat Law drafted by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj seeks to standardise function devolution, but adoption is voluntary.

Comparative Political Regimes: Democracy, Authoritarianism and Hybrid Types

Comparative politics classifies regimes along a continuum from consolidated democracy to closed authoritarianism, with hybrid regimes occupying the middle ground. Robert Dahl’s polyarchy criteria — elected officials, free and fair elections, inclusive suffrage, right to run for office, freedom of expression, alternative information, associational autonomy — serve as the benchmark for liberal democracy. Countries such as Norway, Canada and India (despite deficits) meet most criteria.

Authoritarian regimes concentrate power in a single leader, a military junta or a dominant party, restrict political pluralism, and lack accountability mechanisms. Classic typologies include Linz’s “authoritarian” (limited pluralism, no guiding ideology) and “totalitarian” (comprehensive ideology, mass mobilisation, terror). Contemporary examples: China (single‑party authoritarianism with meritocratic recruitment), Saudi Arabia (monarchical authoritarianism), and North Korea (totalitarian).

Hybrid regimes — also termed “electoral authoritarian” or “competitive authoritarian” (Levitsky and Way) — hold regular elections but manipulate the playing field: media bias, harassment of opposition, control over election commissions, and misuse of state resources. Russia since the 2000s, Turkey post‑2016, and Hungary under Fidesz illustrate this type. They maintain a façade of legitimacy while systematically eroding checks and balances.

Institutional design influences regime trajectories. Parliamentary systems with proportional representation tend to produce multiparty coalitions, which can either stabilise democracy (Germany, Sweden) or fragment accountability (Israel, Italy). Presidential systems with winner‑take‑all elections risk “dual democratic legitimacy” conflicts between executive and legislature (Latin America). Semi‑presidential systems (France, Russia) combine a directly elected president with a prime minister responsible to parliament, creating variable power balances depending on party alignment.

Democratisation theories — modernisation (Lipset), structural (Moore), agency‑focused (O’Donnell & Schmitter) — explain transitions but not consolidation. The “democratic backsliding” literature (Bermeo, 2016) highlights incremental erosion: weakening judicial independence, curbing civil society, altering electoral laws. India’s experience — robust electoral competition coexisting with pressures on media, investigative agencies and federal tensions — is often cited as a case of “illiberal democracy” rather than full backsliding.

Electoral Systems and Party System Institutionalisation: First‑Past‑the‑Post, Proportional Representation and the Indian Experience

Electoral systems shape party systems through mechanical and psychological effects (Duverger’s Law). First‑Past‑the‑Post (FPTP) in single‑member districts tends to produce two‑party systems because smaller parties are mechanically under‑represented and voters strategically desert them. The United Kingdom and the United States exemplify this, though regional parties (SNP, Bloc Québécois) create multiparty dynamics within a two‑party national framework.

Proportional Representation (PR) — list PR, single transferable vote, mixed‑member proportional — allocates seats in proportion to vote shares, encouraging multiparty systems and coalition governments. Germany’s mixed‑member PR yields a stable coalition culture; the Netherlands’ pure list PR produces highly fragmented parliaments. The psychological effect reduces wasted‑vote anxiety, allowing niche parties (greens, radicals) to survive.

India adopts FPTP for the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies. Despite Duverger’s prediction, the Indian party system is multiparty at the national level (over 30 parties represented in the 17th Lok Sabha) and highly fragmented regionally. Explanations include: (i) social cleavages — caste, religion, language, ethnicity — that sustain distinct vote banks; (ii) the absence of a strong national two‑party cleavage; (iii) the role of state‑level parties that dominate regional politics (DMK, TMC, BJD, TRS); and (iv) the anti‑defection law (10th Schedule, 1985) which discourages party switching but does not prevent pre‑electoral alliances.

The effective number of parties (ENP) in the Lok Sabha rose from 2.5 in 1989 to 6.5 in 2019, reflecting coalition politics. The 2014 and 2019 elections saw a temporary consolidation around the BJP (ENP ≈ 3.5) due to a “presidentialised” campaign and a fragmented opposition. The 2024 election reverted to a higher ENP as regional parties regained ground.

Electoral reforms debated include: (a) introducing a mixed‑member PR tier to reduce disproportionality; (b) state funding of elections to curb money power; (c) simultaneous elections (One Nation, One Election) to reduce campaign costs and policy paralysis; (d) strengthening the Election Commission’s autonomy (already constitutionally protected under Art. 324). The Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) recommended a hybrid system but political consensus remains elusive.

Party institutionalisation — measured by organisational depth, ideological coherence, and intra‑party democracy — varies. The Indian National Congress historically displayed a catch‑all character with weak internal democracy; the BJP exhibits a cadre‑based structure with centralised decision‑making; regional parties often revolve around a charismatic leader. The Representation of the People Act, 1951, and the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, regulate registration and recognition but do not enforce internal democracy.

Public Policy Process and Implementation in India

The Indian policy cycle follows the classic stages of agenda‑setting, formulation, adoption, implementation and evaluation, but institutional realities reshape each stage. Agenda‑setting is driven by the Prime Minister’s Office (PMO), the Cabinet Secretariat and the NITI Aayog (successor to the Planning Commission since 2015). The NITI Aayog’s Governing Council, chaired by the Prime Minister and comprising Chief Ministers, provides a federal platform for national priorities such as the Aspirational Districts Programme (2018). Formulation involves line ministries, the Department of Expenditure (Ministry of Finance) and the Legislative Department for drafting bills. The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA) and the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) clear major proposals before they reach Parliament.

Adoption requires passage by both Houses; Money Bills need only Lok Sabha approval (Art. 110). The 2017 Goods and Services Tax (GST) Constitution Amendment (101st Amendment) illustrates a rare consensus‑building exercise involving the GST Council (Art. 279A) where Centre and States negotiate rates. Implementation is delegated to central ministries, state governments and local bodies. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA, 2005) demonstrates a rights‑based design with statutory social audits, yet the “implementation gap” persists due to delayed wage payments and capacity constraints at the Gram Panchayat level.

Monitoring and evaluation rely on the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) reports, the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Estimates Committee. The 2020 Atmanirbhar Bharat package introduced outcome‑based budgeting, linking allocations to measurable outputs (e.g., 10 million new jobs under the Production‑Linked Incentive scheme). Judicial review acts as a backstop: the Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment in Common Cause v. Union of India mandated real‑time disclosure of MGNREGA data, reinforcing accountability. The Right to Information Act (2005) further empowers citizens to track policy execution, though the 2019 amendments to the RTI Act raised concerns about exemption breadth.

Federal dynamics shape policy outcomes. The 14th Finance Commission (2015‑20) raised States’ share of central taxes to 42 %, enhancing fiscal autonomy but also creating coordination challenges for centrally sponsored schemes. The Inter‑State Council (Art. 263) remains under‑utilised; the GST Council is the most active federal bargaining forum. Recent reforms such as the 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Act illustrate the growing role of technology in policy design, while the 2022 National Education Policy (NEP) shows a shift toward multidisciplinary, outcome‑oriented frameworks.

Key take‑aways for analysis: (i) the PMO‑NITI Aayog axis sets the strategic agenda; (ii) the GST Council exemplifies cooperative federalism in tax policy; (iii) statutory social audits and RTI create bottom‑up accountability; (iv) CAG‑PAC scrutiny remains the principal ex‑post check; (v) judicial interventions increasingly define implementation standards.

Civil Society, Social Movements and Democratic Deepening

Civil society in India operates within a constitutional guarantee of freedom of association (Art. 19(1)(c)) and a dense regulatory thicket. The Societies Registration Act, 1860, the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, and the Companies Act, 2013 (Section 8) provide legal forms for non‑profit entities. The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act (FCRA), 2010, amended in 2020, mandates prior permission for foreign funding and caps administrative expenses at 20 %. As of 2022, the Ministry of Home Affairs reported approximately 3.1 million registered NGOs, though only a fraction are FCRA‑compliant.

Historical movements illustrate the interplay of protest, state response and institutional change. The Chipko movement (1973) in Uttarakhand pioneered eco‑feminist resistance, leading to a 15‑year ban on commercial felling in the Himalayan region. The Narmada Bachao Andolan (1985‑present) forced the Supreme Court to adjudicate displacement and rehabilitation norms, culminating in the 2000 judgment that mandated a comprehensive resettlement package. The Right to Food campaign (2001‑) leveraged Public Interest Litigation (PIL) to convert the Supreme Court’s 2001 interim order into the National Food Security Act (2013), guaranteeing subsidised grain to 67 % of the population.

Anti‑corruption mobilisation peaked with Anna Hazare’s 2011 fast, pressuring Parliament to pass the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act (2013). The 2020‑21 farmers’ protests against three farm laws demonstrated the capacity of coordinated farmer unions (Samyukta Kisan Morcha) to sustain a year‑long sit‑in at Delhi’s borders, resulting in the repeal of the laws in November 2021. Women’s collectives such as the Self‑Employed Women’s Association (SEWA, 1972) have institutionalised informal‑sector bargaining, while Dalit Panthers (1972) and the later Bahujan Samaj Party (1984) translated caste‑based assertion into electoral politics.

LGBTQ+ rights advanced through judicial activism: the 2009 Delhi High Court decision in Naz Foundation v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi read down Section 377, the 2013 Supreme Court reversal, and the 2018 Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India judgment that decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations. The 2019 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, despite criticism for its certification requirement, marked legislative recognition.

State‑civil society relations oscillate between partnership and containment. The 2005 National Policy on Voluntary Sector encouraged collaboration, yet the 2020 FCRA amendments and the 2021 Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA) amendments expanded the state’s ability to designate organisations as “unlawful”. Scholars note a shrinking “political opportunity structure” for dissent, even as digital platforms enable new mobilisation forms (e.g., #MeTooIndia, 2018). The Supreme Court’s 2022 judgment in Amnesty International India v. Union of India upheld the government’s power to freeze FCRA accounts, underscoring the legal vulnerability of foreign‑funded advocacy.

International Relations Theory and India’s Foreign Policy

India’s foreign policy cannot be reduced to a single theoretical lens; it draws on realism, liberalism, constructivism and post‑colonial thought. Classical realism (Morgenthau) explains the 1962 Sino‑Indian war and the 1998 Pokhran‑II nuclear tests as power‑maximising moves in an anarchic system. Structural realism (Waltz) underpins the “strategic autonomy” doctrine: India refuses formal alliances to preserve manoeuvre space, evident in the 2008 Indo‑US Civil Nuclear Agreement where India secured NSG waiver without signing the NPT.

Liberal institutionalism (Keohane, Nye) accounts for India’s deepening engagement with multilateral regimes. The 1991 economic liberalisation opened the door to WTO membership (1995) and the 2001‑2008 Doha Round negotiations where India championed the “Special and Differential Treatment” clause for developing countries. The 2015 Paris Agreement saw India pledge a 33‑35 % reduction in emissions intensity by 2030 (updated NDC 2022), reflecting a liberal belief in rule‑based climate governance.

Constructivism (Wendt) illuminates the normative dimension of the Non‑Alignment Movement (NAM) founded at Bandung (1955) and the Panchsheel principles (1954). India’s self‑identification as a “voice of the Global South” shapes its advocacy for UNSC reform (G4 bid) and the 2023 G20 presidency theme “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam”. Post‑colonial theory (Said, Chakrabarty) critiques the Eurocentric security architecture and informs India’s push for a “multipolar world” articulated in the 2017 “Act East” policy and the 2021 Quad revival (US, Japan, Australia, India).

Marxist‑world‑systems analysis (Wallerstein) highlights India’s position as a semi‑peripheral economy leveraging its demographic dividend and IT services to climb the value chain. The 2020‑21 Atmanirbhar Bharat (self‑reliant India) campaign mixes realist supply‑chain security with a developmental state narrative. The 2022 National Security Strategy (draft) and the 2020 Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) institutionalise indigenous defence production, reflecting a blend of realist deterrence and liberal industrial policy.

Key institutional actors: the Ministry of External Affairs (MEA), the National Security Council (NSC) chaired by the Prime Minister, the Defence Planning Committee (2021), and the Indian Council of World Affairs (ICWA) for track‑two diplomacy. The 2023 “Neighbourhood First” and “SAGAR” (Security and Growth for All in the Region) doctrines operationalise a concentric circles approach: immediate neighbours (Bhutan, Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Maldives), extended neighbourhood (ASEAN, Central Asia), and the Indo‑Pacific.

Global Governance: United Nations, WTO, Climate Regimes and India’s Role

The United Nations system remains the primary forum for legitimising India’s global aspirations. India has served eight terms as a non‑permanent Security Council member (latest 2021‑22) and contributes the largest troop contingent to UN peacekeeping (over 5,500 personnel as of 2023). The G4 (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan) pushes for permanent seats with veto restraint; the 2005 “Uniting for Consensus” bloc opposes expansion. India’s 2023 G20 presidency produced the New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration, emphasizing inclusive growth, digital public infrastructure and reformed multilateralism.

At the World Trade Organization, India has been a pivotal voice for the “development box”. The 2001 Doha Ministerial Declaration mandated negotiations on agriculture, services and TRIPS. India’s 2013 Bali Package compromise on the “peace clause” for food‑stockholding programmes protected its Minimum Support Price (MSP) regime. The 2017 Buenos Aires Ministerial saw India block the e‑commerce moratorium extension, arguing for policy space. The Appellate Body crisis (2019‑present) has left dispute settlement paralysed; India advocates a reformed two‑tier system with developing‑country representation.

Climate governance showcases India’s dual role as a major emitter and a champion of equity. The 1997 Kyoto Protocol imposed binding targets only on Annex I countries; India ratified in 2002. The 2015 Paris Agreement introduced Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). India’s 2022 updated NDC commits to 50 % cumulative electric power installed capacity from non‑fossil sources by 2030 and a net‑zero target by 2070. The International Solar Alliance (ISA), launched with France in 2015, now counts 110 signatories and aims to mobilise $1 trillion in solar investment by 2030. The Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI, 2019) extends climate adaptation to critical infrastructure.

Finance and technology transfer remain contentious. The $100 billion annual climate finance goal (Copenhagen 2009) is unmet; India insists on “new and additional” public finance, not re‑labelled ODA. The 2023 Loss and Damage fund agreement at COP28 (Dubai) marks a breakthrough, but operationalisation details are pending. India’s 2023 Green Credit Programme and the 2022 Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act create domestic carbon‑market mechanisms aligned with Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

Reform debates intersect: UNSC expansion requires Charter amendment (Art. 108) and P5 consent; WTO appellate reform needs consensus; climate finance demands credible pledges from developed economies. India’s diplomatic strategy leverages “plurilateral coalitions” (ISA, CDRI, Quad, BRICS) to advance its interests while preserving strategic autonomy.

The Evolving Contours of Indian Federalism: Beyond the Statutory Framework

India's federal system, often described as 'quasi-federal' or 'federal with a strong unitary bias' (K.C. Wheare), has evolved significantly beyond its initial constitutional design. While the Constitution delineates legislative, administrative, and financial relations between the Centre and States (Articles 245-293), the operational dynamics reflect a shift from purely cooperative to increasingly competitive federalism, particularly post-1991 economic reforms. Cooperative federalism, characterized by joint action and interdependence, was epitomized by institutions like the Planning Commission and the National Development Council. However, the abolition of the Planning Commission in 2014 and its replacement by NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) marked a strategic shift. NITI Aayog, a think-tank, emphasizes 'competitive federalism' through performance-based rankings and policy dialogues, encouraging states to vie for investment and development. This model, while promoting efficiency, can exacerbate regional disparities if not managed equitably.

A pivotal institution in contemporary Indian federalism is the Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council, established under Article 279A by the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016. It is a unique constitutional body comprising the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), Union Minister of State for Finance, and Finance Ministers of all States. Decisions are taken by a three-fourths majority of the weighted votes of members present and voting, with the Centre having one-third weightage and States two-thirds. This mechanism represents a significant pooling of fiscal sovereignty, where states collectively determine indirect tax rates and policies, making it a powerful forum for federal bargaining and consensus-building, unprecedented in India's fiscal history.

The office of the Governor (Article 153) remains a contentious aspect. While constitutionally the nominal head of the state executive, the Governor acts as a vital link between the Centre and the State. Controversies often arise concerning discretionary powers, especially in appointing Chief Ministers, dismissing state governments (leading to President's Rule under Article 356), and reserving bills for the President's consideration (Article 200). Landmark judgments like S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) significantly curtailed the arbitrary use of Article 356, mandating parliamentary approval and judicial review. However, debates persist regarding the Governor's role in hung assemblies or when state governments pass legislation contrary to central policy.

Inter-state water disputes (Article 262) are another critical dimension. The Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956, provides for the adjudication of such disputes by tribunals. However, the process is often protracted, and the implementation of tribunal awards remains challenging, highlighting the limits of legal mechanisms in resolving deeply entrenched political and economic conflicts (e.g., Cauvery Water Dispute). Financial federalism is shaped by the Finance Commission (Article 280), which recommends the vertical (Centre to States) and horizontal (among States) devolution of central taxes and grants-in-aid. The 15th Finance Commission (2020-2025) recommended a 41% share of the divisible pool to states, emphasizing performance-based grants, but also sparked debates over the use of 2011 population data for devolution, which penalizes states that have successfully controlled population growth. The increasing reliance on centrally sponsored schemes (CSS) also impacts state autonomy, as states are often compelled to align their priorities with central directives to access funds, leading to a 'top-down' approach in certain policy areas.

Judicial Review and Constitutional Interpretation: Doctrines and Dilemmas

Judicial review, enshrined in Articles 13, 32, and 226 of the Constitution, is a cornerstone of India's constitutional democracy, empowering the Supreme Court and High Courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive actions. This power ensures the supremacy of the Constitution and protects fundamental rights. However, the exercise of judicial review is not static; it is guided by evolving doctrines and faces inherent dilemmas regarding the separation of powers and judicial capacity.

The Basic Structure Doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), is the most significant contribution of Indian jurisprudence. It holds that while Parliament has the power to amend the Constitution (Article 368), it cannot alter its 'basic structure' or essential features. This doctrine was further elaborated in cases like Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), which included judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles as part of the basic structure. The I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) judgment affirmed that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after April 24, 1973 (the date of Kesavananda judgment) are open to judicial review if they violate the basic structure. This doctrine acts as a crucial check on parliamentary power, preventing constitutional amendments that could undermine the democratic and secular fabric of the nation.

Another increasingly applied doctrine is the Principle of Proportionality. While not explicitly mentioned in the Constitution, it has been invoked by the Supreme Court to test the validity of state actions, particularly those infringing upon fundamental rights. The principle requires that any restriction on a right must be for a legitimate aim, be rationally connected to that aim, be necessary (i.e., no less restrictive alternative available), and there must be a proper balance between the restriction and the public interest. The landmark judgment in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), which declared the right to privacy a fundamental right, extensively used the proportionality test to evaluate state intrusions. This doctrine provides a structured framework for balancing individual liberties with state interests.

The debate between Judicial Activism and Judicial Restraint is central to the dilemmas of judicial review. Judicial activism, often manifested through Public Interest Litigation (PIL) initiated by the Supreme Court in the early 1980s (e.g., S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1982), has expanded access to justice and addressed issues like environmental protection (M.C. Mehta cases), prison reforms, and child labor. While PIL has been instrumental in securing rights for marginalized sections, concerns about 'judicial overreach' arise when courts delve into policy-making or administrative functions traditionally reserved for the executive or legislature. Conversely, judicial restraint advocates for deference to elected branches, emphasizing the court's role as an interpreter, not a maker, of law.

The process of judicial appointments also presents a significant dilemma. The 'Collegium System,' evolved through the 'Three Judges Cases' (1993, 1998), grants judges the primary role in appointing judges to higher courts. This system, while ensuring judicial independence, has been criticized for a lack of transparency and accountability. The attempt to replace it with the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) through the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014, was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015, which held that the NJAC violated the basic structure by compromising judicial independence. This ongoing tension highlights the complex interplay between different organs of the state in upholding constitutional principles.

Examiner's trap
Question: "The Basic Structure Doctrine, while safeguarding the Constitution, has also been criticized for granting excessive power to the judiciary. Discuss this tension with reference to specific judicial pronouncements and constitutional principles."
Worked Answer: The Basic Structure Doctrine, established in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), asserts that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 is not absolute and cannot alter the fundamental features of the Constitution. This doctrine was a response to legislative attempts to dilute fundamental rights and has been hailed as a bulwark against majoritarianism, protecting core constitutional values like democracy, secularism, and judicial review. For instance, in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Supreme Court held that the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure, preventing Parliament from giving absolute primacy to DPSP over FRs. Similarly, I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) clarified that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 1973 are subject to basic structure review, reinforcing constitutional supremacy. However, the doctrine has faced criticism for potentially encroaching upon parliamentary sovereignty and democratic legitimacy. Critics argue that it introduces an element of judicial supremacy, as the judiciary, an unelected body, effectively defines the limits of legislative power. The 'basic structure' itself is not explicitly defined in the Constitution, leading to concerns about judicial subjectivity and the potential for judges to read their own philosophies into the Constitution. This tension is rooted in the principle of separation of powers, where each organ has its defined sphere. While the doctrine acts as a necessary check, ensuring that the 'spirit' of the Constitution is preserved, its indeterminate nature and the judiciary's ultimate authority in interpreting it remain a point of contention, balancing the need for constitutional stability against democratic accountability.

The Post-Colonial State and its Critiques: Development, Democracy, and Identity

The concept of the 'post-colonial state' refers to states that emerged from colonial rule, particularly after World War II, inheriting a complex legacy of institutions, economic structures, and social divisions. These states face the formidable dual challenge of nation-building and economic development, often within a global system shaped by their former colonizers. Unlike the European states that evolved organically, post-colonial states often have 'overdeveloped' state apparatuses (Hamza Alavi), meaning the state is disproportionately strong relative to the nascent civil society, a legacy of colonial administrative control. This can lead to authoritarian tendencies, clientelism, and a struggle for democratic consolidation.

Several theoretical perspectives offer critiques and analyses of the post-colonial state:

  • Dependency Theory: Pioneered by scholars like Andre Gunder Frank and Samir Amin, this theory argues that the underdevelopment of post-colonial states is not due to internal deficiencies but rather a direct consequence of their integration into the global capitalist system in a subordinate, 'peripheral' position. The 'core' developed nations exploit the 'periphery' through unequal exchange, resource extraction, and technological dependence, perpetuating a cycle of underdevelopment. This perspective challenges the modernization theory's assumption that all nations will follow a similar path to development.
  • State Autonomy Debate: Scholars like Myron Weiner and Atul Kohli have explored the degree of autonomy the post-colonial state possesses from dominant social classes, ethnic groups, or external pressures. Alavi's concept of the 'overdeveloped state' suggests that the state in post-colonial societies, having been created by colonial powers to serve their interests, often acts autonomously from its own society, leading to a disconnect between the state and its citizens. This autonomy can be a double-edged sword: it can enable developmental initiatives but also facilitate authoritarian rule.
  • Subaltern Studies: Originating in India with scholars like Ranajit Guha and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, this school of thought critiques elite-centric historiography and political analysis. It focuses on the agency, resistance, and consciousness of 'subaltern' groups – those marginalized by class, caste, gender, or ethnicity – whose voices and experiences are often excluded from mainstream narratives of nation-building and development. Subaltern studies highlight how post-colonial states, despite achieving independence, often perpetuate internal forms of domination and exclusion.

The challenges confronting post-colonial states are manifold. Economically, they grapple with dual economies (modern vs. traditional sectors), resource curses, persistent poverty, and often become entangled in debt traps or structural adjustment programs imposed by international financial institutions. Politically, the struggle for democratic consolidation is ongoing, marked by weak institutions, corruption, clientelistic networks, and the potential for military interventions or authoritarian reversals. India, for instance, has largely maintained democratic institutions but faces challenges from criminalization of politics and the influence of money power. Socially, identity politics based on caste, religion, language, or tribe often lead to fragmentation, demands for recognition, and sometimes violent conflicts, testing the state's capacity to forge a cohesive national identity while accommodating diversity. The Indian experience with caste-based reservations, linguistic reorganization of states, and the challenges to secularism exemplify these complex dynamics. In essence, the post-colonial state is a site of ongoing contestation over development models, democratic ideals, and the very definition of national identity, constantly navigating the legacies of its past and the pressures of the present global order.

Electoral Reforms and Democratic Deepening in India

India's electoral system, based on the First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) method, has ensured stable governments but also faces persistent challenges that necessitate continuous reform for democratic deepening. The Election Commission of India (ECI), an autonomous constitutional body (Article 324), plays a pivotal role in conducting free and fair elections, enforcing the Model Code of Conduct, and implementing reforms. Its initiatives, such as the introduction of Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) in the 1990s and Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trails (VVPATs) since 2013, have significantly enhanced transparency and efficiency, though debates about their infallibility persist.

Key areas of electoral reform include:

  • Criminalization of Politics: This remains a major concern. The Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) has consistently highlighted the increasing number of candidates with criminal backgrounds. While the Representation of the People Act (R.P. Act), 1951, disqualifies convicted individuals, the Supreme Court in Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013) struck down Section 8(4) of the R.P. Act, which allowed convicted legislators to continue in office if an appeal was filed within three months. This ruling aimed to immediately disqualify convicted lawmakers. However, the issue of candidates facing serious charges, but not yet convicted, remains. Proposed reforms include debarring individuals facing grave charges from contesting elections, which requires legislative action.
  • Campaign Finance: The funding of political parties and election campaigns has long been opaque. The introduction of Electoral Bonds in 2018 aimed to bring transparency by allowing individuals and corporations to donate anonymously to political parties through designated banks. However, critics argued it promoted opacity, as the donor's identity was hidden from the public, potentially leading to quid pro quo arrangements. The Supreme Court, in a landmark judgment in February 2024, struck down the Electoral Bond Scheme, declaring it unconstitutional for violating the right to information and free speech. Earlier reforms included ceilings on expenditure, but the lack of public funding and reliance on private donations continue to pose challenges.
  • Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule): Introduced by the 52nd Amendment in 1985 and strengthened by the 91st Amendment in 2003, this law aims to prevent political defections by disqualifying legislators who voluntarily give up party membership or vote against party directives. While it has brought some stability, issues persist regarding the Speaker's discretionary power in deciding defection cases, often leading to delays and politically motivated decisions. The 91st Amendment also raised the minimum number of defectors required for a 'split' (from one-third to two-thirds) to avoid disqualification, making individual defections harder.
  • Voter Participation and Representation: Efforts to enhance voter registration (e.g., online portals), facilitate voting for overseas citizens, and improve gender representation in legislatures are ongoing. The debate over the suitability of the FPTP system versus Proportional Representation (PR) for India continues. While FPTP ensures clear majorities and stable governments, PR is argued to offer better representation for smaller parties and diverse social groups. Committees like the Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990) and the Law Commission (2015) have suggested various reforms, including a partial shift to PR or a mixed system.

These reforms are crucial for democratic deepening by enhancing accountability, improving the quality of representation, strengthening political parties, and reducing the influence of money and muscle power. However, their implementation often faces resistance due to a lack of political consensus and the self-interest of political actors, highlighting the complex interplay between legal frameworks and political will in strengthening democratic institutions.

The Finance Commission and Fiscal Federalism: Allocation Criteria, Grants, and Emerging Challenges

The Finance Commission (FC) is a constitutional body constituted under Article 280 of the Constitution every five years to recommend the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States (vertical devolution) and the allocation among States (horizontal devolution). The Commission consists of a Chairman and four other members appointed by the President. Its terms of reference (ToR) are set by the President and typically include principles for sharing divisible pool taxes, grants-in-aid to States under Article 275, and measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of States for Panchayats and Municipalities.

The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑2025), chaired by N. K. Singh, introduced a revised horizontal formula that weights population (15 %), area (15 %), forest and ecology (10 %), income distance (45 %), demographic performance (12.5 %), and tax effort (2.5 %). The Commission recommended a vertical devolution of 41 % of the divisible pool to States, a reduction from the 42 % recommended by the 14th FC, citing the need to accommodate the Union’s fiscal obligations such as GST compensation and defence spending. It also earmarked ₹4.3 lakh crore as revenue deficit grants for 17 States and ₹1.2 lakh crore for local bodies, linking a portion of grants to performance‑based conditions like implementation of the 15th FC’s recommendations on property tax reforms.

Emerging challenges include the growing share of cesses and surcharges — which are not part of the divisible pool — thereby shrinking the resource base for devolution. The GST compensation cess, originally intended to be temporary, has become a permanent feature, creating uncertainty for State finances. Conditional grants tied to reforms (e.g., power sector losses, urban local body audits) have sparked debates over fiscal autonomy versus cooperative federalism. The 16th FC, to be constituted for 2026‑2031, will need to address the fiscal impact of the COVID‑19 pandemic, the transition to a green economy, and the demand for greater fiscal space for States to meet Sustainable Development Goals.

Parliamentary Committees: Types, Powers, and Their Role in Executive Accountability

Parliamentary committees are the engine rooms of legislative oversight. They are broadly classified into Standing Committees (permanent) and Ad Hoc Committees (temporary). The most influential Standing Committees are the Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), 24 in number, each shadowing a Union Ministry. They examine bills, annual reports, and demand for grants, and can summon ministers, officials, and experts. Financial committees — Public Accounts Committee (PAC), Estimates Committee, and Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) — operate under parliamentary rules to scrutinise CAG reports to scrutinise government expenditure and audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG).

The PAC, traditionally chaired by a senior Opposition member, examines the CAG’s audit reports on revenue receipts and expenditure. Its 2011 report on the 2G spectrum allocation exposed procedural lapses and led to the Supreme Court cancelling 122 licences. The Estimates Committee reviews the budgetary estimates of ministries and suggests economies; its 2018 report on defence procurement highlighted cost overruns in the Rafale deal. DRSCs, while lacking the power to enforce recommendations, shape legislative discourse; the Health DRSC’s 2020 report on COVID‑19 preparedness influenced the passage of the Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act, 2020.

Ad Hoc committees include Select Committees (to examine a specific bill) and Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs) formed by both Houses for matters of national importance, such as the JPC on the Personal Data Protection Bill (2019). Committees derive powers from the Rules of Procedure: they can call for papers, examine witnesses on oath, and submit reports to Parliament. However, their effectiveness is constrained by partisan composition, limited time, and the executive’s frequent non‑compliance with recommendations. The 2021 report of the Committee on Government Assurances noted that only 38 % of assurances given by ministers were fulfilled within the stipulated period.

Technique: Comparative Committee Analysis
Question: Compare the powers of the Public Accounts Committee and the Departmentally Related Standing Committees in holding the executive accountable.
Answer: The PAC derives its authority from Article 151 and the CAG’s audit reports; it examines past expenditure, can summon any official, and its reports are debated in Parliament, giving it a quasi‑judicial character. DRSCs, created under Rule 331 of Lok Sabha and Rule 269 of Rajya Sabha, scrutinise bills, policies, and budget demands of specific ministries; they can call for documents and witnesses but lack the statutory backing of the CAG. While PAC focuses on financial propriety after the fact, DRSCs engage in pre‑legislative and policy oversight. Both report to Parliament, but PAC’s recommendations often trigger immediate political debate, whereas DRSC reports influence legislative amendments over a longer horizon.

When a question asks about parliamentary committees, structure the answer by (1) constitutional or rule‑based origin, (2) composition and leadership conventions, (3) specific powers (summoning, report‑making), (4) landmark reports or outcomes, and (5) limitations. The Supreme Court Collegium System: Evolution, Critiques, and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Debate

The collegium system governs appointments and transfers of judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts. It originated in the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) — the Second Judges Case — which held that “consultation” with the Chief Justice of India (CJI) in Article 124(2) means “concurrence”. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the CJI plus the four senior‑most Supreme Court judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the CJI plus two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments. A Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) was finalised in 1999 and revised in 2017 to introduce timelines and a requirement to record reasons for recommendations.

Critics argue the collegium lacks transparency, operates without objective criteria, and is prone to nepotism. The Law Commission’s 230th Report (2009) and the Venkatachaliah Commission (2002) recommended a broader appointments commission. In response, Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014, and the 99th Constitutional Amendment, establishing a six‑member body comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court struck down both in the Fourth Judges Case (2015), holding that the NJAC violated the basic structure by compromising judicial independence.

Post‑2015, the collegium continues with incremental reforms: the 2017 MoP mandates publication of collegium resolutions on the Supreme Court website, disclosure of reasons for rejection of names, and a 30‑day timeline for the government to respond. However, vacancies persist — as of 2024, over 400 High Court posts remain unfilled — and the tension between executive delay and judicial primacy remains a central governance challenge.

India's Nuclear Doctrine: No First Use, Credible Minimum Deterrence, and Strategic Posture Shifts

India’s nuclear doctrine was formally articulated in the 1999 Draft Nuclear Doctrine and adopted officially in 2003 after the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) approval. Its core pillars are: (1) No First Use (NFU) — nuclear weapons will only be used in retaliation against a nuclear attack on Indian territory or forces; (2) Credible Minimum Deterrence — maintaining a sufficient, survivable arsenal to inflict unacceptable damage; (3) Massive Retaliation — any nuclear first strike against India will invite a massive, disproportionate response; (4) Civilian control through the Nuclear Command Authority (NCA), comprising a Political Council chaired by the Prime Minister and an Executive Council chaired by the National Security Adviser.

The doctrine was shaped by the 1998 Pokhran‑II tests (Operation Shakti) and the subsequent strategic dialogue with the United States culminating in the 2008 Civil Nuclear Agreement. India’s arsenal is estimated at 160‑170 warheads (2023 SIPRI data), delivered via land‑based Agni series, sea‑based K‑15/K‑4 SLBMs on Arihant‑class SSBNs, and air‑delivered gravity bombs. The 2019 Balakot airstrike and the 2020 Galwan clash reignited debate on NFU’s credibility. In 2020, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh stated that “circumstances” could dictate a review of NFU, signalling a possible shift toward “flexible response”. However, the official doctrine remains unchanged, and the NCA’s command‑and‑control architecture continues to emphasise civilian authorisation for any nuclear release.

Sub‑categorisation of Reservations: The 103rd Amendment, Creamy Layer, and the Debate on Equitable Representation

The Constitution (103rd Amendment) Act, 2019 inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6), enabling the State to provide up to 10 % reservation in education and public employment for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) not covered by existing SC/ST/OBC quotas. The Supreme Court, in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022), upheld the amendment by a 3:2 majority, ruling that economic criteria alone can be a basis for reservation and that the 50 % ceiling (Indra Sawhney, 1992) is not inviolable for EWS.

The creamy layer concept, introduced in Indra Sawhney, excludes socially advanced persons within OBCs from reservation benefits. The National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) and the Rohini Commission (constituted 2017) have recommended sub‑categorisation of OBCs into “backward”, “more backward”, and “most backward” to ensure equitable distribution. States like Maharashtra (2021) and Tamil Nadu (2022) have enacted legislation for sub‑categorisation, but these face judicial scrutiny for lack of empirical data. The 103rd Amendment’s EWS quota operates independently of the creamy layer test, leading to criticism that it benefits relatively affluent sections while OBC creamy layer exclusion remains rigid.

The debate centres on three issues: (i) the adequacy of quantifiable data to justify sub‑categorisation; (ii) the constitutional permissibility of exceeding the 50 % ceiling for EWS while maintaining it for caste‑based quotas; and (iii) the political economy of reservation — whether expanding quotas dilutes merit or addresses historic disadvantage. The Supreme Court’s forthcoming decision on the validity of state‑level sub‑categorisation laws will shape the next phase of affirmative action jurisprudence.

The Anti‑Defection Law: Tenth Schedule, Judicial Interpretation, and Reform Proposals

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the Constitution (Fifty‑second Amendment) Act, 1985 to curb political defections that destabilised legislatures. It disqualifies a member who voluntarily gives up membership of his party or votes contrary to the party whip, unless the defection occurs as part of a merger where at least two‑thirds of the legislature party agrees. The Constitution (Ninety‑first Amendment) Act, 2003 tightened the provision by removing the “split” exception and by barring disqualified members from holding ministerial office until re‑elected.

Judicial interpretation has shaped the law’s contours. In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) the Supreme Court upheld the Schedule’s constitutionality but held that the Speaker’s decision is subject to judicial review. Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994) clarified that “voluntarily giving up membership” includes conduct indicating abandonment of party allegiance. Jagjit Singh v. State of Haryana (2006) ruled that a member who joins another party after resignation attracts disqualification even if the resignation is later withdrawn. The 2020 Karnataka case (Karnataka Legislative Assembly v. Union of India) reinforced that the Speaker must decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable time, and that judicial review extends to procedural fairness.

Critics argue that the law concentrates power in the Speaker, who is often a party loyalist, and that the two‑thirds merger threshold is easily manipulated. The 2018 amendment (via the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty‑third Amendment) Bill, later withdrawn) attempted to raise the merger threshold to three‑fourths but lapsed. The Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) recommended an independent tribunal for disqualification decisions and a cooling‑off period before a disqualified member can contest. The 2022 Private Member’s Bill by MP Shashi Tharoor proposes a permanent Election Commission‑appointed adjudicatory body.

For answer‑writing, distinguish between the original 1985 framework, the 2003 amendment, and the judicial gloss. Cite the three landmark judgments and the specific constitutional articles (Articles 102(2) and 191(2)). Use a table to compare “split”, “merger”, and “voluntary resignation” exceptions.

The Role of the Governor: Constitutional Position, Discretionary Powers, and Judicial Review

The Governor is the constitutional head of a state under Articles 153‑162. While the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister aids and advises the Governor (Article 163), the Constitution reserves certain discretionary powers: appointment of the Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority (Article 164), reservation of bills for the President’s consideration (Article 200), and the power to recommend President’s Rule under Article 356. The Governor also exercises discretion in granting pardons (Article 161) and in summoning, proroguing, or dissolving the Legislative Assembly (Article 174).

Judicial scrutiny has defined the limits of this discretion. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court held that the Governor’s report recommending President’s Rule is subject to judicial review and must be based on objective material. Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) ruled that the Governor cannot act on political considerations when inviting a party to form government; the decision must be guided by the constitutional mandate to ensure a stable majority. The 2019 Maharashtra episode, where the Governor invited the BJP‑Shiv Sena combine despite the latter’s claim of a post‑poll alliance, triggered a Supreme Court directive for a floor test within 24 hours, reinforcing the principle that the Governor’s discretion is not unfettered.

Recent controversies — West Bengal (2021) where the Governor delayed assent to bills, and Kerala (2022) where the Governor publicly criticised the state government — have reignited the debate on whether the office should be made more accountable. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that the Governor be appointed in consultation with the Chief Minister and that a fixed tenure of five years be guaranteed. The Punchhi Commission (2010) went further, suggesting that the Governor’s discretionary powers be codified and that the office be made non‑partisan by requiring a cooling‑off period after active political service.

In examinations, contrast the Governor’s role with that of the President at the Centre. Highlight the “aid and advice” clause, the “discretionary” exceptions, and the judicial check‑points. A comparative table of Articles 163, 164, 200, 356 versus Articles 74, 75, 111, 356 (Centre) clarifies the federal symmetry.

Technique: Issue‑Rule‑Application‑Conclusion (IRAC) for Governor Discretion Questions
Question: “The Governor of State X refuses to assent to a Bill passed by the Legislative Assembly, citing policy disagreement. The State Government challenges the refusal in the High Court. Decide.”
Issue: Whether the Governor’s refusal to assent on policy grounds is constitutionally permissible.
Rule: Article 200 empowers the Governor to assent, withhold assent, or reserve a Bill for the President. The Supreme Court in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) held that withholding assent must be based on constitutional grounds, not policy disagreement. The Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Art. 163) except in the few discretionary spheres.
Application: The Governor’s stated reason — policy disagreement — falls outside the constitutional grounds (e.g., violation of fundamental rights, repugnancy to central law). The Council of Ministers’ advice to assent is binding. The refusal therefore exceeds the Governor’s discretionary space.
Conclusion: The High Court should direct the Governor to either assent or reserve the Bill for the President; a blanket refusal on policy grounds is unconstitutional.

The Election Commission of India: Independence, Powers, and Recent Controversies

Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction, and control of elections in the Election Commission of India (ECI). The Commission comprises a Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and up to two Election Commissioners, appointed by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Their tenure is six years or until age 65, whichever is earlier, and removal requires a process akin to that of a Supreme Court judge (Article 324(5)). This institutional design aims to insulate the ECI from executive pressure.

The ECI’s powers include delimitation of constituencies (in coordination with the Delimitation Commission), preparation and revision of electoral rolls, notification of election schedules, enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct (MCC), and adjudication of disputes relating to recognition of political parties and allotment of symbols. The Supreme Court in Election Commission v. State of Tamil Nadu (1993) affirmed that the ECI’s decisions on election matters are final and not subject to judicial review except on grounds of mala fides or jurisdictional error.

Recent controversies have tested this autonomy. The 2019 General Elections saw allegations of MCC violations by the ruling party, prompting the ECI to issue unprecedented directions to the Prime Minister’s Office. In 2021, the West Bengal Assembly elections witnessed a heated exchange between the ECI and the state government over deployment of central forces. The 2022 Gujarat elections raised questions about the ECI’s handling of the “VVPAT‑EVM” verification process after the Supreme Court in S. Subramaniam Balaji v. Election Commission (2021) mandated a minimum 5 % random VVPAT verification per constituency.

Reform proposals include the 2015 Law Commission Report (255th) recommending a collegium‑based appointment (Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, Chief Justice of India) to enhance perceived independence. The 2020 “Election Commission (Conditions of Service) Amendment Bill” sought to equalise the salary and status of Election Commissioners with that of the CEC, but lapsed. For answer‑writing, map the constitutional provisions (Articles 324‑329), the statutory framework (Representation of the People Act, 1950/1951), and the key judicial pronouncements. A flowchart showing the ECI’s decision‑making hierarchy — from notification to dispute resolution — aids clarity.

The Goods and Services Tax Council: Federal Fiscal Architecture, Decision‑Making, and Dispute Resolution

The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016 inserted Article 279A, establishing the Goods and Services Tax Council (GST Council) as a constitutional body for cooperative federalism in indirect taxation. The Council consists of the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and the Finance Ministers of all states. Decisions are taken by a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes: the Centre holds one‑third of the total weight, states collectively hold two‑thirds, with each state’s weight proportional to its share of the total GST revenue.

The Council’s mandate includes recommending GST rates, exemptions, threshold limits, and the principles of levy. It also decides on the compensation cess to protect state revenues for five years (till 2022). The 2022‑23 revenue shortfall triggered intense negotiations; the Council extended the compensation cess levy beyond the original period, a move challenged by several states in the Supreme Court. In Union of India v. State of Kerala (2023) the Court upheld the Council’s power to extend the cess, emphasising the Council’s role as a political‑fiscal forum rather than a purely legal one.

Dispute resolution under the GST regime is two‑tiered: the Council can refer differences to a “GST Dispute Resolution Mechanism” comprising a three‑member bench (one Centre nominee, one state nominee, one independent expert). If unresolved, the matter may be taken to the Supreme Court under Article 131 (original jurisdiction between Centre and states). The 2021 “GST Council (Procedure) Rules” formalised voting procedures, quorum (half the total members), and the requirement that any rate change be accompanied by a revenue impact assessment.

Key exam points: the constitutional basis (Art. 279A), the voting weight formula, the five‑year compensation guarantee, the distinction between “recommendatory” and “binding” decisions (the Council’s recommendations become law only after Parliament/State Legislatures enact them), and the interplay with the Finance Commission’s vertical devolution. A comparative table of the GST Council vs. the Finance Commission (constitutional vs. statutory, decision‑making vs. advisory) is a high‑yield revision tool.

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas: Anti‑Corruption Architecture, Appointment Process, and Effectiveness

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 (amended 2016) created a national anti‑corruption ombudsman (Lokpal) and mandated states to establish Lokayuktas. The Lokpal consists of a Chairperson and up to eight members, at least 50 % of whom must be judicial members. The selection committee comprises the Prime Minister (Chair), the Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha, the Chief Justice of India (or a Supreme Court judge nominated by him), and an eminent jurist nominated by the President. This composition seeks to balance executive, legislative, and judicial inputs.

The Lokpal’s jurisdiction covers the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, Members of Parliament, Group A‑D officers, and entities receiving foreign contributions above a threshold. It can inquire into allegations of corruption under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, and recommend prosecution. The Act provides for a “prosecution wing” and a “special court” for trial. The first Lokpal, Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, assumed office in March 2019 after a Supreme Court direction in Common Cause v. Union of India (2018) to appoint the body without further delay.

State Lokayuktas vary widely: Karnataka (1984), Maharashtra (1971), and Delhi (1995) have relatively strong institutions, while several states have yet to operationalise them. The 2016 amendment to the Lokpal Act modified certain provisions to include chairpersons of public sector undertakings and brought the Prime Minister’s Office under the Lokpal’s purview subject to a three‑member bench approval.

Effectiveness assessments (Transparency International India, 2022) note that the Lokpal has disposed of only a fraction of the complaints received, citing procedural delays, lack of investigative staff, and the requirement of prior sanction for prosecution of senior officials. The 2023 Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice recommended granting the Lokpal suo‑motu investigation powers and an independent budget. For examinations, focus on the appointment mechanism, the jurisdictional carve‑outs for the Prime Minister, the interplay with the Central Vigilance Commission, and the federal dimension — states’ obligation to enact Lokayukta laws under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013.
