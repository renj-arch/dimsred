1. Foundations of Political Theory

1.1 The Normative and Empirical Traditions

The study of political theory begins with a distinction between normative inquiry, which asks how politics ought to be organised, and empirical inquiry, which seeks to describe and explain political behaviour as it actually occurs. Normative traditions draw on the philosophical legacy of Plato and Aristotle, insisting that concepts such as justice, virtue and the common good provide standards against which real institutions can be judged. Empirical traditions, by contrast, emerged in the late nineteenth century with the rise of behaviouralism, which borrowed methods from the natural sciences to treat political actors as observable units whose choices could be measured, correlated and predicted. The behavioural revolution of the 1950s, led by scholars such as David Easton and Gabriel Almond, shifted the discipline toward hypothesis testing, survey research and quantitative analysis. Post‑behaviouralism, articulated by Easton himself in the 1960s, warned that an exclusive focus on measurable regularities risked ignoring the normative questions that give politics its meaning. Marxist, feminist and post‑colonial approaches further expanded the empirical agenda by insisting that power relations rooted in class, gender and empire shape both the production of knowledge and the lived experience of politics. Together these traditions constitute a pluralistic field in which normative aspirations and empirical findings constantly interrogate each other.

1.2 Theories of the State: Liberal, Marxist, Pluralist and Post‑colonial Perspectives

Liberal theory, rooted in the social‑contract writings of John Locke and John Stuart Mill, conceives the state as a neutral arbiter that protects individual rights, enforces contracts and provides a framework within which free citizens pursue their conceptions of the good. Neo‑liberal variants, associated with Friedrich Hayek and Robert Nozick, shrink the state’s role to the minimal functions of defence, police and courts, arguing that any redistribution beyond the protection of property rights violates individual liberty. Marxist theory, by contrast, treats the state as an instrument of class domination: the instrumentalist strand, following Lenin, sees the state as a direct tool of the capitalist class, while the structuralist strand, developed by Nicos Poulantzas, argues that the state’s relative autonomy serves the long‑term reproduction of capitalism. Pluralist theory, championed by Robert Dahl, depicts the state as an arena where multiple interest groups compete, with no single group able to dominate permanently. Post‑colonial scholars such as Partha Chatterjee and Frantz Fanon contend that the modern state form was imposed on colonised societies, creating a bifurcated political society in which elite institutions mimic Western models while the majority remain excluded from meaningful participation. Feminist theorists, including Carole Pateman and Iris Marion Young, add that the state’s public‑private distinction systematically marginalises women’s experiences and reproduces patriarchal power.

1.3 Justice, Equality and Rights: Rawls, Nozick, Sen and Communitarian Critiques

John Rawls’s A Theory of Justice (1971) revitalised normative political philosophy by proposing two principles chosen behind a veil of ignorance: first, each person has an equal right to the most extensive basic liberties compatible with similar liberties for others; second, social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least advantaged — the difference principle. This framework links justice to fairness and provides a benchmark for evaluating distributive policies such as progressive taxation or affirmative action. Robert Nozick’s Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) counters with an entitlement theory that treats justice as historical: a distribution is just if it arises from a just initial acquisition and voluntary transfers, rejecting patterned principles that require continual interference with holdings. Amartya Sen’s capability approach shifts the metric from primary goods to the real freedoms people enjoy to achieve valuable functionings, arguing that justice demands the expansion of capabilities such as health, education and political participation. Communitarian critics, notably Michael Sandel and Alasdair MacIntyre, contend that Rawls’s abstract individualism neglects the embeddedness of persons in shared traditions and that a conception of the good cannot be bracketed out of political deliberation. These debates shape contemporary Indian discourse on reservation, welfare schemes and the interpretation of fundamental rights.

1.4 Democracy, Power and Ideology: Procedural vs Substantive Models, Lukes’s Three Faces

Procedural definitions of democracy focus on the presence of competitive elections, universal suffrage, civil liberties and the rule of law, treating these institutions as sufficient for democratic legitimacy. Substantive models, advocated by theorists such as Carole Pateman and Joshua Cohen, argue that democracy also requires meaningful participation, deliberative equality and the redistribution of resources so that all citizens can effectively exercise their formal rights. Steven Lukes’s three faces of power deepen this analysis: the first face concerns observable decision‑making in open conflict; the second face involves agenda‑setting that prevents certain issues from reaching the decision stage; the third face shapes preferences and perceptions so that dominated groups may accept their subordination as natural. Ideologies provide the normative vocabularies through which these power relations are justified or contested. Liberalism champions individual autonomy and limited government; conservatism values tradition, hierarchy and organic community; socialism and Marxism prioritise collective ownership and class emancipation; fascism glorifies the nation‑state and authoritarian leadership; Gandhism emphasises non‑violent resistance, trusteeship and village self‑rule; feminism interrogates gendered power structures; ecologism extends moral consideration to the non‑human world. Understanding these ideologies is essential for analysing party manifestos, social movements and policy debates in India and beyond.

2. Major Traditions and Thinkers in Political Thought

2.1 Classical Foundations: Plato, Aristotle and Machiavelli

Plato’s Republic constructs an ideal city ruled by philosopher‑kings, arguing that justice consists in each class performing its proper function and that knowledge of the Forms qualifies rulers to govern. Aristotle, in the Politics, rejects the guardian class, classifying constitutions by the number of rulers and their aim, and famously defines man as a political animal whose flourishing requires participation in the polis. He distinguishes between correct constitutions — monarchy, aristocracy, polity — and their perversions — tyranny, oligarchy, democracy — and introduces the concept of the mixed regime as a stabilising blend. Niccolò Machiavelli’s The Prince and Discourses on Livy break with the normative tradition by analysing power as it is exercised, advising rulers to master fortuna through virtù, and praising republican institutions that channel conflict into constructive competition. These three thinkers establish the vocabulary of virtue, justice, regime classification and the realism‑idealism tension that continues to structure political theory.

2.2 Social Contract and Liberalism: Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Mill

Thomas Hobbes’s Leviathan (1651) posits a state of nature characterised by a war of all against all, leading rational individuals to surrender their natural rights to an absolute sovereign in exchange for security. John Locke’s Second Treatise of Government (1689) modifies this picture by asserting that natural rights to life, liberty and property pre‑exist the state, and that government’s legitimacy rests on the consent of the governed, with a right of revolution when trust is violated. Jean‑Jacques Rousseau’s The Social Contract (1762) replaces individual consent with the general will, arguing that true freedom is obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself, and that sovereignty resides in the collective body politic. John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty (1859) and Considerations on Representative Government (1861) defend individual liberty against both state tyranny and the tyranny of the majority, introducing the harm principle and advocating proportional representation, plural voting and the protection of minority opinions. Together these theorists lay the groundwork for modern constitutionalism, the doctrine of limited government and the liberal emphasis on rights, representation and deliberative democracy.

2.3 Critical Traditions: Marx, Gandhi, Ambedkar and Tagore

Karl Marx’s historical materialism, articulated in The Communist Manifesto (1848) and Capital (1867), analyses capitalism as a system of exploitation rooted in the appropriation of surplus value, predicts its crisis tendencies and envisions a communist society based on the free association of producers. Mahatma Gandhi’s political philosophy, expressed in Hind Swaraj (1909) and his practice of satyagraha, rejects the modern state’s centralised violence, proposing instead a decentralized order of self‑sufficient villages, trusteeship of wealth and non‑violent resistance as a method of moral conversion. B. R. Ambedkar, architect of the Indian Constitution, combines a radical critique of caste as a graded inequality with a liberal commitment to constitutional morality, insisting that social democracy must precede political democracy and advocating affirmative action, the abolition of untouchability and the protection of minority rights. Rabindranath Tagore, in essays such as Nationalism (1917), warns against the idolisation of the nation‑state, championing a universal humanism that transcends territorial borders and celebrates cultural pluralism. These thinkers provide distinct lenses — class, non‑violence, caste, cosmopolitanism — through which Indian politics can be interrogated.

2.4 Contemporary Debates: Feminism, Ecologism and Post‑colonial Theory

Feminist political theory, from the liberal feminism of Mary Wollstonecraft to the radical feminism of Catharine MacKinnon and the intersectional feminism of Kimberlé Crenshaw, exposes how the public‑private divide, reproductive labour and gendered citizenship sustain male dominance. Ecologism, represented by Arne Naess’s deep ecology and the political ecology of Ramachandra Guha, challenges anthropocentric conceptions of progress, arguing for the intrinsic value of ecosystems and the redistribution of ecological space. Post‑colonial theory, advanced by Edward Said, Gayatri Spivak and Homi Bhabha, deconstructs the epistemic violence of colonial knowledge production and examines how former colonies negotiate sovereignty, identity and development within a global order still shaped by imperial hierarchies. In the Indian context, these debates inform discussions on the Uniform Civil Code, forest rights legislation, climate justice and the politics of representation for Dalits, Adivasis, women and religious minorities.

Solved Example: Rawls’s Difference Principle
Question: Explain Rawls’s difference principle and illustrate how it would apply to a policy on progressive taxation.
Answer: Rawls argues that social and economic inequalities are permissible only when they improve the position of the least advantaged members of society. Behind the veil of ignorance rational agents would choose the difference principle together with the principle of equal basic liberties. In a progressive tax regime the higher rates on top incomes generate revenue that funds public goods such as health care and education, thereby raising the welfare of the poorest. Critics contend that the principle is difficult to operationalise because it requires a precise identification of the least advantaged group and may ignore individual desert.
Answer‑writing insight
When a question asks for a thinker’s thesis, first state the core proposition in one sentence, then unpack the supporting arguments, cite a key text, present a major criticism, and conclude with the concept’s relevance to contemporary Indian debates such as reservation policy or fiscal federalism.

3. Indian Constitutional Framework and Institutional Design

3.1 The Constituent Assembly and the Philosophy of the Constitution

The Constituent Assembly, convened in December 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan, comprised 299 members elected by provincial legislatures and representatives of princely states. Its deliberations, chaired by Dr. Rajendra Prasad and guided by the drafting committee led by B. R. Ambedkar, produced a document that came into force on 26 January 1950. The Preamble declares India a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic committed to justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. The Assembly’s debates reveal a synthesis of liberal constitutionalism, Gandhian moral politics and Ambedkarite social justice, reflected in the inclusion of both justiciable Fundamental Rights (Part III) and non‑justiciable Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV). The adoption of a parliamentary system with a ceremonial President and a responsible Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister reflects the British Westminster model, while the federal division of powers in the Seventh Schedule and the provision for a strong Centre draw on the Government of India Act 1935. The Constitution’s amendability under Article 368, balanced by the basic structure doctrine, ensures both flexibility and the preservation of core constitutional values.

3.2 Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Fundamental Rights guarantee civil liberties such as equality before law (Article 14), freedom of speech (Article 19), protection against arbitrary detention (Article 21) and the right to constitutional remedies (Article 32). The Directive Principles, though non‑enforceable, obligate the state to pursue social and economic goals — adequate livelihood, equal pay, education, public health — and have been used by courts to interpret the scope of Fundamental Rights. The basic structure doctrine, articulated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), holds that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 cannot alter the Constitution’s essential features: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism and the rule of law. Subsequent cases — Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) and I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) — have refined the doctrine, establishing judicial review as a guardian of constitutional identity. This jurisprudence shapes the limits of legislative action on issues ranging from land reform to the National Judicial Appointments Commission.

3.3 Parliamentary Executive: President, Prime Minister and Council of Ministers

The President, elected by an electoral college of Parliament and state legislatures, exercises executive power on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, as mandated by Article 74. While the President’s role is largely ceremonial, discretionary powers — such as appointing the Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority, dissolving the Lok Sabha, and granting pardons under Article 72 — become politically significant during hung parliaments or coalition crises. The Prime Minister, as the leader of the majority party or coalition in the Lok Sabha, drives the legislative agenda, chairs the Cabinet and represents India internationally. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, and a vote of no‑confidence can force its resignation. The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review and enhance executive authority, but the 44th Amendment (1978) restored many checks. The dynamics of prime‑ministerial power have varied from the dominant single‑party era under Nehru and Indira Gandhi to the coalition constraints of the 1990s and the renewed single‑party majority after 2014.

3.4 Judiciary: Supreme Court, Judicial Review and Landmark Cases

The Supreme Court, established under Article 124, is the apex judicial body with original, appellate and advisory jurisdiction. Its power of judicial review, though not explicitly enumerated, derives from Articles 13, 32, 136 and 142, enabling it to strike down laws and executive actions that violate Fundamental Rights or the basic structure. Landmark decisions illustrate the Court’s role as a constitutional arbiter: Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) held that Fundamental Rights cannot be amended; Kesavananda Bharati (1973) introduced the basic structure doctrine; Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded Article 21 to include due process; S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down guidelines for the imposition of President’s Rule under Article 356; Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) created guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace; Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations; and Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) recognised privacy as a fundamental right. These rulings demonstrate the Court’s capacity to shape public policy, protect minorities and mediate centre‑state disputes.

4.

Federalism and Centre‑State Relations: Legislative, Administrative and Financial Dimensions

The Indian Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal structure in which the Union enjoys a preponderant role while states retain significant autonomy in areas listed in the State List (List II) of the Seventh Schedule. Legislative competence is divided into three lists — Union (List I), State (List II) and Concurrent (List III) — with the Union Parliament empowered to legislate on Concurrent subjects under Article 246 and to override state laws on those subjects under Article 254. The residuary power vests in the Union (Article 248), a feature borrowed from the Government of India Act 1935 and distinct from the United States model where residuary powers belong to the states.

Administrative relations are governed by Articles 256‑263. The Union can issue directions to states for compliance with Union laws (Art. 256) and for the construction and maintenance of means of communication of national importance (Art. 257). The President may appoint a Governor who acts as the Union’s representative in the state (Art. 155) and can assume the functions of the state government under President’s Rule (Art. 356). The Inter‑State Council (Art. 263) and the National Development Council provide institutional forums for coordination, though their recommendations are non‑binding.

Financial federalism is anchored in the Finance Commission (Art. 280) which recommends the vertical and horizontal distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Centre and the states. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) introduced by the 101st Amendment (2016) created a dual GST model with a GST Council (Art. 279A) where the Union and states jointly decide rates, thresholds and exemptions. The Centre retains control over major revenue sources — income tax, corporate tax, customs and central excise — while states depend heavily on grants‑in‑aid (Art. 275) and centrally sponsored schemes, creating a fiscal asymmetry that fuels demands for greater devolution.

Judicial interpretation has shaped the balance. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court laid down strict guidelines for the imposition of President’s Rule, requiring a floor test and limiting the Centre’s discretion. In State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) the Court upheld the Union’s power to legislate on Concurrent subjects even when a state law occupies the field, provided the Union law is not repugnant. The Kesavananda Bharati basic‑structure doctrine also protects federalism as an essential feature, preventing constitutional amendments that would convert the Union into a unitary state.

Contemporary tensions arise from the use of Article 356, the allocation of GST compensation, and the Centre’s increasing reliance on centrally sponsored schemes that bypass state legislatures. The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended a 41 % vertical share for states, a modest increase over the 14th Commission’s 42 % after adjusting for the GST compensation cess, illustrating the ongoing negotiation of fiscal federalism.

Emergency Provisions: Article 352, Article 356 and Article 360 and Judicial Scrutiny

The Constitution provides three distinct emergency powers. Article 352 empowers the President to proclaim a National Emergency on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion. Such a proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority (two‑thirds of members present and voting, and a majority of total membership). During a National Emergency, the Union can legislate on State List subjects (Art. 250), extend the life of the Lok Sabha by one year at a time (Art. 83), and suspend the enforcement of Fundamental Rights under Article 19 (Art. 358) and the right to move courts for enforcement of other rights (Art. 359), subject to the 44th Amendment’s safeguard that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended.

Article 356 (President’s Rule) allows the President to assume the functions of a state government when the constitutional machinery fails. The proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months and can be extended for a maximum of three years with periodic parliamentary approval every six months. The 44th Amendment (1978) introduced the requirement that the proclamation be based on the Governor’s report or otherwise, and that the state legislative assembly not be dissolved without parliamentary approval. The Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai (1994) held that the proclamation is subject to judicial review, that the material facts must be placed before Parliament, and that the floor test is the decisive proof of majority.

Article 360 provides for a Financial Emergency when the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. It has never been invoked. If proclaimed, the Union can direct states to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of public servants, and require all money bills to be reserved for the President’s consideration. Parliamentary approval is required within two months, and the proclamation continues until revoked.

Judicial scrutiny has progressively constrained executive discretion. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) the Court struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that sought to make Emergency proclamations immune from judicial review. In Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) the Court reiterated that the satisfaction of the President under Article 356 is justiciable and that the Governor’s report must contain relevant material. The 44th Amendment also restored the pre‑1976 position that the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, limiting unilateral action.

These provisions illustrate the tension between central authority and federal autonomy. The historical misuse of Article 356 during the 1970s and 1980s, the 1975‑77 National Emergency, and the never‑used Financial Emergency together shape the constitutional discourse on emergency powers.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Explain why Article 356 is considered a 'dead letter' after the S. R. Bommai judgment." Provide a concise answer that references the judgment’s key holdings.
The S. R. Bommai (1994) judgment did not render Article 356 a dead letter; it subjected the proclamation to strict judicial review. The Court held that (1) the President’s satisfaction must be based on objective material, not merely the Governor’s report; (2) the state legislative assembly cannot be dissolved before Parliament approves the proclamation; (3) a floor test is the constitutional method to ascertain majority; and (4) the proclamation is justiciable on grounds of mala fides or irrelevance. Consequently, the Centre’s arbitrary dismissal of state governments has been sharply curtailed, but the provision remains operative for genuine breakdowns of constitutional machinery.
Exam‑day strategy
When answering questions on emergency provisions, structure the response in three layers: (i) constitutional text and procedural safeguards, (ii) landmark judicial pronouncements with case names and years, (iii) practical impact on centre‑state relations. Use a comparative table for Articles 352, 356, 360 to show differences in grounds, parliamentary approval, duration and judicial review. This fetches both knowledge and analytical marks.

Panchayati Raj and Urban Local Governance: The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments

The 73rd Amendment (1992) inserted Part IX (Articles 243‑243O) to constitutionally entrench rural local self‑government. It mandates a three‑tier Panchayati Raj system — Gram Panchayat at the village level, Panchayat Samiti at the intermediate (block) level, and Zilla Parishad at the district level — though states with a population below 20 lakhs may omit the intermediate tier. Elections are held every five years under the superintendence of a State Election Commission (Art. 243K). Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women (not less than one‑third) in direct elections and in the offices of chairpersons (Art. 243D). The State Finance Commission (Art. 243I) reviews the financial position of panchayats and recommends devolution of taxes, duties, tolls and grants‑in‑aid.

The 74th Amendment (1992) added Part IXA (Articles 243P‑243ZG) for urban local bodies — Municipal Corporations, Municipal Councils and Nagar Panchayats — based on population size and density. It mirrors the rural framework: regular elections, reservations, a State Election Commission, and a State Finance Commission. Additionally, it provides for District Planning Committees (Art. 243ZD) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (Art. 243ZE) to integrate spatial planning with economic development.

Implementation varies widely. Kerala and West Bengal devolved substantial functions (29 subjects under the Eleventh Schedule) and finances, enabling participatory planning through the People’s Plan Campaign. In contrast, many states retain control over key functions — water supply, sanitation, primary health — and rely on tied grants, limiting fiscal autonomy. The 14th Finance Commission (2015‑20) recommended a 4.3 % share of the divisible pool for local bodies, later raised to 4.5 % by the 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25), but the actual transfer depends on state compliance with audit and accounting standards.

Judicial interventions have reinforced the constitutional mandate. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (2005) the Supreme Court held that the State Election Commission’s independence is part of the basic structure. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2021) the Court directed timely elections to panchayats, rejecting the state’s plea of administrative inconvenience. The Court also ruled that the reservation rotation for women must follow the constitutional formula, not ad‑hoc executive orders.

Challenges remain: inadequate own‑source revenue, dependence on tied central schemes (e.g., MGNREGS, Swachh Bharat), limited capacity of elected representatives, and the persistence of parallel bureaucratic structures. The 2022 Model Panchayat Law drafted by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj seeks to standardise function devolution, but adoption is voluntary.

Comparative Political Regimes: Democracy, Authoritarianism and Hybrid Types

Comparative politics classifies regimes along a continuum from consolidated democracy to closed authoritarianism, with hybrid regimes occupying the middle ground. Robert Dahl’s polyarchy criteria — elected officials, free and fair elections, inclusive suffrage, right to run for office, freedom of expression, alternative information, associational autonomy — serve as the benchmark for liberal democracy. Countries such as Norway, Canada and India (despite deficits) meet most criteria.

Authoritarian regimes concentrate power in a single leader, a military junta or a dominant party, restrict political pluralism, and lack accountability mechanisms. Classic typologies include Linz’s “authoritarian” (limited pluralism, no guiding ideology) and “totalitarian” (comprehensive ideology, mass mobilisation, terror). Contemporary examples: China (single‑party authoritarianism with meritocratic recruitment), Saudi Arabia (monarchical authoritarianism), and North Korea (totalitarian).

Hybrid regimes — also termed “electoral authoritarian” or “competitive authoritarian” (Levitsky and Way) — hold regular elections but manipulate the playing field: media bias, harassment of opposition, control over election commissions, and misuse of state resources. Russia since the 2000s, Turkey post‑2016, and Hungary under Fidesz illustrate this type. They maintain a façade of legitimacy while systematically eroding checks and balances.

Institutional design influences regime trajectories. Parliamentary systems with proportional representation tend to produce multiparty coalitions, which can either stabilise democracy (Germany, Sweden) or fragment accountability (Israel, Italy). Presidential systems with winner‑take‑all elections risk “dual democratic legitimacy” conflicts between executive and legislature (Latin America). Semi‑presidential systems (France, Russia) combine a directly elected president with a prime minister responsible to parliament, creating variable power balances depending on party alignment.

Democratisation theories — modernisation (Lipset), structural (Moore), agency‑focused (O’Donnell & Schmitter) — explain transitions but not consolidation. The “democratic backsliding” literature (Bermeo, 2016) highlights incremental erosion: weakening judicial independence, curbing civil society, altering electoral laws. India’s experience — robust electoral competition coexisting with pressures on media, investigative agencies and federal tensions — is often cited as a case of “illiberal democracy” rather than full backsliding.

Electoral Systems and Party System Institutionalisation: First‑Past‑the‑Post, Proportional Representation and the Indian Experience

Electoral systems shape party systems through mechanical and psychological effects (Duverger’s Law). First‑Past‑the‑Post (FPTP) in single‑member districts tends to produce two‑party systems because smaller parties are mechanically under‑represented and voters strategically desert them. The United Kingdom and the United States exemplify this, though regional parties (SNP, Bloc Québécois) create multiparty dynamics within a two‑party national framework.

Proportional Representation (PR) — list PR, single transferable vote, mixed‑member proportional — allocates seats in proportion to vote shares, encouraging multiparty systems and coalition governments. Germany’s mixed‑member PR yields a stable coalition culture; the Netherlands’ pure list PR produces highly fragmented parliaments. The psychological effect reduces wasted‑vote anxiety, allowing niche parties (greens, radicals) to survive.

India adopts FPTP for the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies. Despite Duverger’s prediction, the Indian party system is multiparty at the national level (over 30 parties represented in the 17th Lok Sabha) and highly fragmented regionally. Explanations include: (i) social cleavages — caste, religion, language, ethnicity — that sustain distinct vote banks; (ii) the absence of a strong national two‑party cleavage; (iii) the role of state‑level parties that dominate regional politics (DMK, TMC, BJD, TRS); and (iv) the anti‑defection law (10th Schedule, 1985) which discourages party switching but does not prevent pre‑electoral alliances.

The effective number of parties (ENP) in the Lok Sabha rose from 2.5 in 1989 to 6.5 in 2019, reflecting coalition politics. The 2014 and 2019 elections saw a temporary consolidation around the BJP (ENP ≈ 3.5) due to a “presidentialised” campaign and a fragmented opposition. The 2024 election reverted to a higher ENP as regional parties regained ground.

Electoral reforms debated include: (a) introducing a mixed‑member PR tier to reduce disproportionality; (b) state funding of elections to curb money power; (c) simultaneous elections (One Nation, One Election) to reduce campaign costs and policy paralysis; (d) strengthening the Election Commission’s autonomy (already constitutionally protected under Art. 324). The Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) recommended a hybrid system but political consensus remains elusive.

Party institutionalisation — measured by organisational depth, ideological coherence, and intra‑party democracy — varies. The Indian National Congress historically displayed a catch‑all character with weak internal democracy; the BJP exhibits a cadre‑based structure with centralised decision‑making; regional parties often revolve around a charismatic leader. The Representation of the People Act, 1951, and the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, regulate registration and recognition but do not enforce internal democracy.

  • Confusing parliamentary sovereignty (UK) with constitutional sovereignty (India) — remember the basic‑structure doctrine limits Parliament’s amending power.
  • Assuming Article 356 is a dead letter after Bommai — it remains operative but subject to strict judicial review and procedural safeguards.
  • Treating the 73rd and 74th Amendments as identical — they differ in tier structure, planning committees and the list of devolved functions.
  • Equating hybrid regimes with illiberal democracies — hybrid regimes manipulate elections; illiberal democracies hold free elections but weaken liberal institutions.
  • Applying Duverger’s Law mechanically to India — social cleavages and regional parties produce a multiparty system despite FPTP.
  • Overlooking the Finance Commission’s role in fiscal federalism — it is the primary constitutional mechanism for vertical and horizontal tax devolution.
  • Neglecting the distinction between National Emergency (Art. 352) and President’s Rule (Art. 356) — they have different grounds, parliamentary approval thresholds and judicial scrutiny standards.
