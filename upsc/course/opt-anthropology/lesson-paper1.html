1. Anthropology Paper I — Physical & Social-Cultural Anthropology

2. Human Evolution & Biological Anthropology

  • Primate characteristics: Forward-facing eyes (stereoscopic vision), grasping hands, opposable thumb, enlarged brain relative to body, prolonged infancy, social behavior, reduced snout, nails instead of claws.
  • Primate taxonomy: Prosimii (lemurs, lorises, tarsiers), Anthropoidea (monkeys, apes, humans); New World monkeys (platyrrhines, prehensile tails, flat noses), Old World monkeys & apes (catarrhines, non-prehensile tails, narrow noses), hominoids (apes & humans: gibbon, orangutan, gorilla, chimpanzee, human).
  • Fossil record: Dryopithecus (~12-9 Ma, Europe), Ramapithecus (~14-7 Ma, Siwaliks — once considered early hominid), Australopithecus (~4-2 Ma, Africa; A. africanus, A. afarensis "Lucy"), Homo habilis (~2.4-1.6 Ma, "handy man", Oldowan tools), Homo erectus (~1.8 Ma-0.1 Ma, Java man, Peking man, Acheulean tools, fire), Homo heidelbergensis, Homo neanderthalensis (Neanderthals, Mousterian tools, burial), Homo sapiens (~0.3 Ma-present, modern humans, Upper Paleolithic art, migration out of Africa ~70-50 kya).
  • Human variation: Skin color, blood groups, height, disease susceptibility — clines, adaptation (Bergmann's & Allen's rules), race concept critique (race is not a valid biological category — clinal variation, within-group > between-group variation).
  • Human genetics: Chromosomes (46), DNA structure, genes, alleles, genotype/phenotype, mutation, selection, genetic drift, gene flow, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.

3. Social-Cultural Anthropology

  • Culture: Tylor's definition — "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society." Learned, shared, transmitted, symbolic, integrated, adaptive.
  • Culture traits, complexes, patterns: Trait = smallest unit; complex = cluster of related traits; pattern = configuration.
  • Marriage: Forms — monogamy (one spouse), polygamy (polygyny: one man multiple wives; polyandry: one woman multiple husbands — fraternal vs non-fraternal), group marriage, levirate & sororate, cross-cousin & parallel-cousin marriage. Functions: regulation of sex, reproduction, socialization, alliance, economic cooperation.
  • Family: Nuclear (parents + children), joint/extended (multiple generations), joint family (India — patrilineal, patrilocal, patriarchal). Functions: reproduction, socialization, economic cooperation, emotional support, status transmission.
  • Kinship: Descent — patrilineal (through father), matrilineal (through mother), bilateral (both), double descent. Residence — patrilocal (with husband's family), matrilocal (with wife's family), neolocal (new household), avunculocal (with husband's mother's brother). Kinship terminology — classificatory vs descriptive; kin groups — lineage, clan, kindred.
  • Religion: Animism (Tylor — spirits in nature), animatism (impersonal force — mana), totemism (clan-totem linkage), naturism (Max Müller — nature worship), magic (frazer: contagious, imitative), science vs religion vs magic (comparison), witchcraft & sorcery.
  • Economic anthropology: Modes of exchange — reciprocity (generalized, balanced, negative), redistribution (chiefdom/state), market exchange. Subsistence modes — hunting-gathering, pastoralism, horticulture, agriculture, industrialism.
  • Political organization: Band (small, egalitarian, kin-based), tribe (segmentary, big-man), chiefdom (ranked, redistribution, permanent leadership), state (stratified, bureaucracy, monopoly of force). Acephalous vs stratified societies.

4. Research Methods & Ethics

  • Fieldwork: Participant observation (Malinowski — Trobriand Islanders), long-term fieldwork, learning language, rapport building.
  • Methods: Interview (structured/unstructured), case study, genealogy, life history, questionnaire, survey, PRA (Participatory Rural Appraisal), RRA, photography, audio-video recording.
  • Ethics: Informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity, avoiding harm, reciprocity, reflexivity (researcher's positionality), decolonizing anthropology.
  • Key thinkers: E.B. Tylor (animism, culture definition), Lewis Morgan (kinship, evolution: savagery-barbarism-civilization), Émile Durkheim (totemism, collective consciousness), A.R. Radcliffe-Brown (structural-functionalism), Bronislaw Malinowski (functionalism, participant observation, needs theory), Claude Lévi-Strauss (structuralism, binary oppositions, myth), Lewis Henry Morgan (kinship systems, evolution), Franz Boas (cultural relativism, historical particularism), Margaret Mead (sex & temperament, Samoa), Ruth Benedict (patterns of culture), E.E. Evans-Pritchard (witchcraft, segmentary lineage), M.N. Srinivas (Sanskritization, dominant caste, dominant caste, fieldwork in India), D.N. Majumdar (tribal studies in India), L.P. Vidyarthi (sacred complex, nature-man-spirit complex), A.C. Mayer (village India), F.G. Bailey (stratagem, politics).
Technique: Structural-functionalism
Question: 'Explain Radcliffe-Brown's structural-functionalism.'
Answer: Society as an integrated system where parts (institutions — kinship, religion, economy, polity) function to maintain social equilibrium & social solidarity. Focus on observable social structure & functions (not individual psychology — Malinowski). Critique: conservative, ignores conflict/change, treats society as static. Applied: kinship & descent regulate marriage; religion reinforces social solidarity (Durkheim's influence).

Common Traps

  • Confusing race (biological, discredited) with ethnicity (cultural).
  • Treating all tribal societies as identical — enormous regional variation.
  • Missing that anthropology studies present-day societies too (not just "primitive" past).
  • Confusing matriarchy (rare/nonexistent) with matriliny/matrilocality (exist).
  • Assuming evolutionism = progress — modern anthropology rejects unilineal evolution.

Memory Tricks

  • Four fields: Physical, Social, Archaeological, Linguistic — PSAL.
  • Kinship: Descent = lineage; Residence = where couple lives; Marriage = partner rules.
  • Evolution sequence: Dryopithecus → Ramapithecus → Australopithecus → Homo habilis → H. erectus → H. sapiens.
Mains strategy
Anthropology answers need concept + thinker + Indian example + diagram (kinship chart, human evolution timeline, caste pyramid, tribal map). Quote names: Srinivas (dominant caste, Sanskritization), Vidyarthi (sacred complex), Evans-Pritchard (segmentary lineage), Malinowski (participant observation), Lévi-Strauss (structuralism).

5. The Pashupati Seal and the Archaeological Reconstruction of Early Urbanism

In the realm of archaeological anthropology, the transition from the Chalcolithic to the Mature Harappan phase is often marked by specific material culture indicators that challenge simplistic evolutionary narratives. The Pashupati Seal, discovered at Mohenjo-daro (Sindh) during excavations led by Sir John Marshall in 1926–27, remains one of the most significant artifacts for understanding the religious and social complexity of early urban India. Unlike generic "proto-Shiva" interpretations that rely on later theological constructs, the seal depicts a seated figure with large antlers (likely a stag or deer), surrounded by animals including tigers, elephants, rhinos, lions, and buffalo. The presence of these diverse species suggests a ritual context involving nature worship or a proto-shamanistic tradition where the central figure acts as an intermediary between humans and the animal kingdom.

Chronology and Context:

The seal is made of steatite (soapstone), a material that was difficult to carve, indicating high craftsmanship and likely elite patronage. The inscription on the reverse side contains an Indus script sign, though its reading remains undeciphered. This artifact demonstrates that by 2600 BCE, Indian society had moved beyond simple tribal organization into a stratified urban system with specialized religious roles.

Interpretative Frameworks:

Scholars have debated the identity of the central figure. While some early interpretations linked it directly to the Hindu god Shiva (Pashupati, "Lord of Animals"), modern anthropological caution requires us to separate archaeological data from later theological overlays. The figure's posture—seated in a yogic pose with antlers—is distinctively non-Vedic and pre-dates the composition of the Vedas by over a millennium. This supports the theory of historical continuity rather than linear evolution, where indigenous traditions survived the collapse of urban centers and were reinterpreted in later Vedic contexts.

Comparative Analysis:

When compared to contemporary Mesopotamian seals (cuneiform tablets depicting deities), the Pashupati Seal shows that the Indus Valley Civilization possessed a sophisticated iconographic language. However, unlike Mesopotamia where kings were often depicted as divine rulers, the Indus seal figure appears more ritualistic than political, suggesting a separation of religious and administrative power—a feature also seen in later Indian history but rare in ancient Near Eastern contexts.

Implications for Social Structure:

The existence of such seals implies a class of artisans who could produce high-value ritual objects. This points to a complex division of labor: potters, seal-makers, and sculptors operating within an urban economy. The distribution of similar seals across Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal, and Dholavira suggests a standardized trade network or at least shared cultural values across the Indus region.

Methodological Note:

Students must avoid stating that "Shiva worshipped animals" as a fact. Instead, the correct anthropological phrasing is: "The seal depicts a figure surrounded by animals, suggesting an association with animal symbolism in early ritual practices." This distinction preserves the integrity of the archaeological record while acknowledging cultural continuity.

Examiner's trap
Question: "Did the Pashupati Seal prove the existence of Shiva worship in 2600 BCE?" Answer: No. The seal shows a figure with animal symbolism, which may have been reinterpreted as Shiva later. You cannot equate archaeological imagery directly with later theological concepts without textual evidence. Always use phrases like 'suggests', 'indicates', or 'may represent' rather than definitive claims.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Ambiguity
If you encounter a question about undeciphered scripts or ambiguous artifacts, do not guess. Instead, state what is known (material, context, date) and acknowledge the limits of current knowledge. Use phrases like 'The significance lies in...' or 'This suggests a possible link to...'. This shows critical thinking without inventing facts.

6. From Tribal Bands to Statehood: The Political Evolution in Indian Anthropology

The concept of political evolution—from acephalous (headless) societies to stratified states—is central to social anthropology but must be applied with nuance. Early evolutionary models (Morgan, Tylor) proposed a unilinear path: Band → Tribe → Chiefdom → State. Modern anthropology rejects this as overly simplistic and culturally biased. Instead, we focus on multilinear evolution where different societies develop unique political structures based on ecological and historical factors.

Key Political Forms in India:

1. Band Societies (Egalitarian): Small groups (20–50 people) with no formal leadership. Decisions made by consensus. Found among certain nomadic tribes like the Bhil or Gond in forested areas, though these are often misclassified as "primitive" due to colonial biases.

2. Tribe (Segmentary Lineage): Larger groups with segmentary lineage systems (Evans-Pritchard's Nuer model). Leadership is situational; a "big-man" emerges based on ability, not hereditary right. Example: Kurumba or Kolam communities in Tamil Nadu.

3. Chiefdom (Ranked Society): Hereditary leadership with redistribution of goods. Found among the Munda or Santal in Jharkhand, where village councils and ritual leaders hold authority but lack bureaucratic machinery.

4. State (Stratified Bureaucracy): Monopoly of force, written laws, standing army. The Mauryan Empire represents the first true state in India, with a centralized bureaucracy, as documented by Megasthenes and later archaeological finds like Ashokan edicts.

Exceptions to Evolution:

Not all societies follow this sequence. Some "tribal" groups have complex kinship systems without formal states (e.g., Kurumba). Conversely, some chiefdoms collapse into stateless societies due to environmental stress or invasion. Anthropologists like M.N. Srinivas emphasized that caste-based hierarchies in India functioned as a "state within a state," creating internal stratification independent of political boundaries.

Comparative Analysis:

Compare the Nuer (South Sudan) segmentary lineage system with Indian Jat caste clusters. Both use kinship for conflict resolution, but the Nuer rely on blood ties while Jats are bound by ritual purity and endogamy. This highlights that political organization is not solely about size or complexity but also cultural logic.
