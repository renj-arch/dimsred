Anthropology Paper II — Archaeology & Applied Anthropology

Archaeological Anthropology

  • Methods: Survey (surface survey, aerial photography, remote sensing, GIS), excavation (stratigraphic excavation — Wheeler box grid, Wheeler-Kenyon method), dating methods.
  • Dating methods: Relative — stratigraphy (law of superposition), seriation, typology, fluorine dating. Absolute — radiocarbon (C-14, up to ~50,000 years), dendrochronology (tree rings), thermoluminescence (TL), potassium-argon (K-Ar, older sites), uranium-series, obsidian hydration.
  • Paleolithic (~2.5 Ma - 10,000 BP): Lower (Oldowan — Homo habilis; Acheulean — H. erectus hand axes), Middle (Mousterian — Neanderthals), Upper (blade tools, Homo sapiens, cave art — Lascaux, Chauvet, Bhimbetka in India).
  • Mesolithic (~10,000 - 8,000 BP): Microliths, bow-arrow, fishing, semi-sedentary, Mesolithic rock art (Bhimbetka, Bhimbetka shelters).
  • Neolithic (~8,000 - 4,000 BP): Agriculture & animal domestication (wheat, barley, rice, millets; sheep, goat, cattle, pig), pottery, polished stone axes, permanent villages (Jericho, Çatalhöyük, Mehrgarh in Pakistan), megaliths.
  • Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone, ~4,000-3,000 BP): Copper tools alongside stone, pottery, early urbanism (Mehrgarh phases, pre-Harappan).
  • Iron Age (~1,200 BP onwards): Iron technology, black-and-red ware, painted grey ware, megalithic burials (dolmens, cairns, menhirs, urn burials) in South India.
  • Harappan civilization (~2,600-1,900 BCE): Mature Harappan — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Lothal, Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi; urban planning (grid, drainage, great bath, granary, citadel & lower town), seals, script (undeciphered), trade (Mesopotamia), weights & measures, decline theories (climate change, river shift, invasion/migration theories debated).
  • Megalithic & post-megalithic South India: Dolmens, cairn circles, menhirs, urn burials, rock-cut caves, black-and-red ware, early Tamil sangam associations.

Applied Anthropology

  • Definition: Application of anthropological knowledge & methods to practical problems — development, health, education, environment, business, forensics.
  • Development anthropology: Critique of top-down development; participatory approaches; cultural appropriateness; displacement & resettlement (dams, mines, national parks — oustees & rehabilitation).
  • Tribal development in India: Constitutional safeguards — Fifth Schedule (scheduled areas, TAC), Sixth Schedule (autonomous districts NE), Article 342 (scheduled tribes list), Article 275(1) (grants), Article 338A (NCST); Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP), Integrated Tribal Development Projects (ITDP/Agencies), Forest Rights Act 2006 (individual & community forest rights, habitat rights for PVTGs), PESA 1996 (gram sabha powers in scheduled areas), National Policy on Tribals, Vanbandhu Kalyan Yojana.
  • Tribal problems: Land alienation, indebtedness, displacement (dams, mines, parks), migration, health (malnutrition, sickle cell, malaria, TB), education (dropout, language), nutrition, livelihood loss, Naxalism/Maoist insurgency links to deprivation & alienation.
  • Medical anthropology: Health & disease as cultural constructs; ethnomedicine (traditional healers — ojha, baiga, vaidy), pluralistic health systems (biomedicine + traditional), nutrition, reproductive health, mental health, disability, pandemics & stigma, COVID-19 & indigenous communities.
  • Environmental anthropology: Indigenous ecological knowledge (IEK/TEK), sacred groves, community forestry, biodiversity conservation & displacement (protected areas & tribal rights), climate change & vulnerable communities.
  • Business/corporate anthropology: Organizational culture, consumer behavior, user-centered design.
  • Forensic anthropology: Identification from skeletal remains (age, sex, ancestry, stature, trauma), mass disaster identification.

Tribal India: Issues & Movements

  • Constitutional safeguards: Fifth Schedule (scheduled areas in 10 states — TAC, governor's role), Sixth Schedule (autonomous districts in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram — district councils), Article 342 (ST list), Article 275(1) (special grants), Article 338A (National Commission for STs), Article 164(1) (tribal ministers in MP, Bihar, Odisha, etc.), Article 350 (instruction in mother tongue).
  • PESA 1996: Gram sabha powers in scheduled areas — land, minor forest produce, local plans, institutions, money lending, intoxicants.
  • FRA 2006: Individual forest rights (cultivation & habitation), community forest rights (community use), habitat rights for PVTGs, right to protect & manage forests, gram sabha as decision authority.
  • Tribal movements: Santhal (1855-56), Munda (Birsa Munda, 1899-1900), Bhil, Koli, Naga, Mizoram, Jharkhand movement (Jharkhand Mukti Morcha), Bodoland, contemporary identity & autonomy movements; Naxalism/Maoism — roots in tribal deprivation, land alienation, lack of development, state response (security + development).
Technique: FRA 2006 answer
Question: 'FRA 2006 empowers forest-dwelling communities but implementation remains weak.' Discuss.
Answer: FRA recognizes individual & community forest rights, habitat rights for PVTGs, gram sabha as authority. Achievements: millions of claims filed, lakhs of titles issued (esp. Odisha, MP, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra). Weaknesses: forest department resistance, lack of awareness, diluted guidelines (2012), conservation vs rights tension (tiger core zones), delayed recognition, implementation gaps. Way forward: gram sabha empowerment, time-bound disposal, convergence with MGNREGA/NRLM, monitoring cells.

  • FRA 2006: Individual rights + community rights + habitat rights (PVTGs) + gram sabha decides.

Archaeological Chronologies: From Microliths to Iron and the Chalcolithic Transition

The chronological framework of Indian prehistory is not merely a sequence of dates but a narrative of technological adaptation driven by environmental shifts. The Mesolithic period (c. 10,000–8,000 BCE) in India is defined by the transition from large-game hunting to small-game hunting and gathering, necessitating smaller, more efficient stone tools known as microliths. These are not isolated flakes but composite tools fitted into wooden or bone shafts. The Bombon culture of the Deccan plateau and the Kurnool culture in Andhra Pradesh exemplify this, showing a distinct adaptation to semi-arid environments where large animals were scarce. The shift from the Levallois technique (used in Upper Paleolithic) to the prepared core method is evident here, reflecting a move towards standardization and efficiency.

The Neolithic revolution in India is marked by the advent of polished stone tools and pottery, signifying sedentism and agriculture. The Narmada Valley culture (c. 6000 BCE) represents one of the earliest Neolithic cultures, characterized by microlithic industries and early pottery. However, the true agricultural breakthrough is seen in the Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone Age) period, which bridges the gap between stone-age technology and the Iron Age. The term 'Chalcolithic' implies the use of copper alongside stone, but archaeological evidence suggests that metallurgy was not widespread; rather, it refers to the technological sophistication of pottery and agriculture.

The Mahenagiri and Kurdukh cultures in central India showcase the transition from polished stone axes to copper ornaments. The most significant Chalcolithic assemblage is found at Ahar-Banas (Rajasthan) and Gandhara Grave Culture (Gujarat), though the latter is often debated. The Mahakala culture in Madhya Pradesh reveals a complex society with fortified settlements, indicating social stratification and warfare. The pottery styles—black-and-red ware, painted grey ware, and red ware—provide crucial dating markers. For instance, the presence of rice grains in potsherds at sites like Bhagwanpura confirms wet-rice cultivation techniques similar to modern Andhra Pradesh.

The Iron Age (c. 1200–600 BCE) introduces a paradigm shift with the introduction of iron tools, which allowed for deforestation and expansion into dense forests. The Kaikadi culture in Karnataka and the Hastinapura culture in Uttar Pradesh show the spread of iron technology. This period coincides with the Vedic age, but archaeological finds like the Pataliputra seals (Mauryan era) and the Ahichhat Prayag site provide material evidence for textual corroboration. The Sangam Age in Tamil Nadu (c. 300 BCE–300 CE) is archaeologically linked to the black-and-red ware found at sites like Tirumalai, which connects the literary tradition of the Sangam poets with material culture. The discovery of Roman coins and glass bangles in coastal Tamil Nadu sites like Korkai and Muziris (though Muziris's exact location is debated) highlights the maritime trade connections described in Tamil literature.

Examiner's trap: The "Copper Age" Misconception
A common error is assuming that because a site has copper beads, it belongs to the Chalcolithic period. Correct answer: The Chalcolithic term refers to a specific technological phase where copper was used for ornaments and small tools alongside stone, but widespread metallurgy (smelting iron) defines the Iron Age. Many Neolithic sites have copper finds but are still classified as Neolithic if polished stone axes dominate. Always prioritize the dominant tool kit (polished stone vs. iron) over minor metal artifacts.
Exam-Day Strategy: Chronology Mapping
When answering questions on prehistory, do not just list periods. Draw a mental timeline: Start with Paleolithic (hunting/gathering), move to Mesolithic (microliths/environmental change), then Neolithic (agriculture/pottery). Insert Chalcolithic as the bridge where copper ornaments appear but stone tools dominate. End with Iron Age and Vedic texts. Always link archaeological sites to specific regions (e.g., Ahar-Banas = Rajasthan, Mahakala = MP) to show spatial awareness. Use terms like 'sedentism', 'polished axes', and 'rice cultivation' to demonstrate technical vocabulary.

Tribal Constitutional Safeguards: The Architecture of Autonomy and Rights

The Indian Constitution provides a robust framework for tribal protection, distinct from general minority protections. The core lies in the Fifth Schedule (Articles 244(1) & 275(1)), applicable to states other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. It creates 'Scheduled Areas' where the Governor has special powers to make regulations regarding land transfer, money lending, and intoxicants without parliamentary interference. The Tribal Advisory Committee (TAC) assists the Governor in these matters, ensuring local tribal opinion is considered. This mechanism prevents external exploitation by landlords or moneylenders.

The Sixth Schedule applies exclusively to Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram, creating 'Autonomous District Councils' (ADCs). These councils possess legislative powers over land, forests, agriculture, and social customs within their districts. This is a form of devolution of power, allowing tribes to govern themselves according to customary laws. However, the ADCs are subject to parliamentary override under Article 200(3), ensuring national unity prevails over local autonomy in case of conflict.

The Forest Rights Act (FRA) 2006 is a statutory implementation of these constitutional mandates. It recognizes two types of rights: Individual Forest Rights (IFR) for cultivation and habitation, and Community Forest Rights (CFR) for common use resources like grazing lands, forests, and water bodies. The Act also grants 'habitat rights' to Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs), allowing them to protect their biodiversity and sacred groves. The Gram Sabha acts as the decision-making body, ensuring that forest management is community-led rather than state-imposed.

The PESA Act 1996 (Part IX of the 73rd Amendment) complements FRA by empowering Gram Sabhas in Scheduled Areas to manage local resources, including land, minor forest produce, and money lending. It mandates that no land transfer or lease can occur without the Gram Sabha's consent. This addresses the historical issue of land alienation where tribes were forced to sell land to outsiders due to lack of legal awareness.

The National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST), established under Article 338A, functions as a watchdog. Unlike the NITI Aayog, NCST has the power to investigate and recommend remedies for violations of tribal rights. It also monitors the implementation of the Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP) and Integrated Tribal Development Projects (ITDP).

Kinship Terminology and Descent Groups Among Central Indian Tribes

The Gond, Baiga, and Korku communities of the Satpura‑Maikal region illustrate the coexistence of patrilineal clan organization with matrifocal residence patterns. Gond society is divided into exogamous clans (gotra) such as Madhav, Koya, and Pardhan, each tracing descent through a mythical ancestor. Clan membership regulates marriage alliances, ritual participation, and access to forest resources. In contrast, the Baiga practice a form of uterine kinship where the mother’s brother (mama) holds authority over the nephew’s initiation into the Bhagoria hunting rites. Anthropological surveys (e.g., the 1971 Census of India ethnographic notes and the 1998 People of India series) record that the Korku maintain a dual descent system: patrilineal clans (kul) for land inheritance and matrilineal lineages (matri) for ritual priesthood. The terminology reflects these structures: bhai (brother) extends to all male clan mates, while didi (elder sister) covers female matrilineal relatives. Fieldwork by N. K. Bose (1960s) and later by the Anthropological Survey of India (2005) confirms that kinship idioms are deployed in dispute resolution — clan elders (panch) invoke gotra rules to mediate land conflicts, while the maternal uncle’s consent is required for a bride’s kanyadaan. Understanding these terminological nuances is essential for answering questions on “kinship as a mechanism of resource control” and for distinguishing between clan, lineage, and phratry in tribal contexts.

Tribal Development Planning: From Five‑Year Plans to the Aspirational Districts Programme

India’s planned approach to tribal welfare began with the First Five‑Year Plan (1951‑56) which earmarked a “Tribal Sub‑Plan” (TSP) within the overall outlay. The TSP mandated that a proportion of state plan funds — initially 8 % — be spent exclusively in Scheduled Areas. The Fourth Plan (1969‑74) introduced the Integrated Tribal Development Project (ITDP) model, concentrating resources in 194 blocks identified by the Dhebar Commission (1960‑61). The Fifth Plan (1974‑79) added the Modified Area Development Approach (MADA) for dispersed tribal pockets and the Cluster Approach for contiguous villages. The 1990s saw the launch of the Tribal Cooperative Marketing Development Federation (TRIFED) to institutionalize minor forest produce (MFP) procurement. In 2005 the National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (NREGA) was extended to Scheduled Areas, guaranteeing 100 days of wage employment. The 2018 Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP) shifted focus to outcome‑based monitoring across 112 districts, 38 of which are tribal‑dominant (e.g., Dantewada, Koraput, Gadchiroli). ADP uses 49 indicators — health, education, agriculture, financial inclusion — tracked through a real‑time dashboard. The programme’s “convergence” principle requires line ministries (Health, Education, Rural Development) to align schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana (PMGSY) and the National Health Mission (NHM) with tribal‑specific needs. Evaluation reports (NITI Aayog 2021) note that districts adopting community‑led micro‑planning under the Gram Sabha (empowered by PESA 1996) show faster improvement in maternal mortality and school enrolment. For answer‑writing, candidates should contrast the “top‑down allocation” of early plans with the “bottom‑up convergence” of ADP, citing the constitutional basis (Article 275(1) grants‑in‑aid) and the statutory backing (FRA 2006, PESA 1996).

Worked Answer: Contrasting Plan‑Era and Aspirational‑Era Tribal Strategies
Question: “Explain how the strategy for tribal development has shifted from the Five‑Year Plan period to the Aspirational Districts Programme. Illustrate with two concrete mechanisms.”
The Five‑Year Plans relied on earmarked financial allocations (Tribal Sub‑Plan, ITDP, MADA) disbursed through state departments, with limited community participation. Mechanisms: (1) Fixed percentage of plan outlay (8‑10 %) reserved for Scheduled Areas; (2) Block‑level ITDP offices implementing centrally designed schemes. The Aspirational Districts Programme replaces allocation‑centric logic with outcome‑centric convergence. Mechanisms: (1) Real‑time dashboard monitoring 49 indicators, forcing line ministries to align existing schemes (e.g., NHM, PMGSY) to district‑specific gaps; (2) Mandatory Gram Sabha micro‑plans under PESA, ensuring that tribal communities prioritize works such as forest‑rights recognition (FRA) and MFP value‑addition. The shift is from “funds follow plans” to “plans follow data and community voice”.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “evolution of tribal policy”, structure the answer in three phases: (i) Plan‑era earmarking (TSP, ITDP, MADA), (ii) Rights‑era legislation (PESA, FRA, NREGA), (iii) Outcome‑era convergence (ADP dashboard, Gram Sabha micro‑plans). Use the constitutional articles (244, 275, 338A) as anchors for each phase. This chronological‑thematic frame scores both content and analytical marks.

Participatory Rural Appraisal and Rapid Assessment Techniques in Applied Anthropology

Applied anthropologists routinely employ Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) tools to co‑produce knowledge with tribal communities. The core PRA toolkit — social mapping, seasonal calendars, wealth ranking, and Venn diagrams — was systematised by Robert Chambers (1994) and adapted for Indian tribal contexts by the Indian Council of Social Science Research (ICSSR) in the 1990s. Social mapping lets villagers depict resource distribution (water points, forest patches, sacred groves) on a ground‑drawn map, revealing tenure overlaps that official cadastral maps miss. Seasonal calendars capture labour peaks, migration windows, and MFP collection cycles (e.g., mahua flowering in March‑April, tendu leaf plucking in May). Wealth ranking, conducted through pairwise comparison of household assets, identifies the “poorest of the poor” for targeted entitlements under the Antyodaya Anna Yojana. Rapid Assessment Procedures (RAP) complement PRA when time is constrained; they use key‑informant interviews, focus‑group discussions, and transect walks to generate a “quick ethnography” within two weeks. The 2001 Tribal Health Survey (Ministry of Health & Family Welfare) combined RAP with biomedical screening to map malaria endemicity in Bastar. In applied projects, the “triangulation” principle — cross‑checking PRA outputs with secondary data (Census, NFHS, forest department records) — strengthens validity. Examiners expect candidates to name specific tools, explain the sequence (rapport building → tool application → validation), and discuss ethical safeguards: informed consent in the local language, data ownership by the Gram Sabha, and feedback sessions before report finalisation.

Global Value Chains and Non‑Timber Forest Produce: The Mahua and Tendu Leaf Trade

Non‑timber forest produce (NTFP) constitutes the primary cash income for an estimated 100 million forest‑dependent people, a figure cited in the 2011 State of Forest Report. Two commodities dominate the central Indian trade: mahua (Madhuca longifolia) flowers and tendu (Diospyros melanoxylon) leaves. Mahua flowers are collected between March and May, dried, and sold to local traders who supply distilleries in Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, and Maharashtra. The 2018 Mahua Price Monitoring Committee reported farm‑gate prices ranging from ₹12‑₹18 per kg, while wholesale rates in Indore exceeded ₹30 per kg, indicating a 70‑150 % margin captured by intermediaries. Tendu leaves, harvested in May‑June, are the raw material for beedi rolling. The Tendu Leaf (Control) Act, 1955 (Madhya Pradesh) and its counterparts in Odisha and Chhattisgarh nationalised collection, fixing a minimum support price (MSP) — ₹2,500 per standard bag (50 kg) in 2022. However, lease‑based contractors often under‑report yields, diverting surplus to the open market where prices can reach ₹4,000 per bag. The Forest Rights Act 2006 recognises Community Forest Rights (CFR) over NTFP, enabling Gram Sabhas to negotiate directly with processors. Pilot CFR‑based enterprises in Gadchiroli (Maharashtra) and Koraput (Odisha) have demonstrated 30‑40 % higher collector incomes by bypassing contractors. For answer‑writing, link the value‑chain analysis to policy instruments: MSP fixation, CFR recognition, TRIFED’s minimum support price operations, and the 2022 National Mission on Edible Oils which includes mahua oil as a potential bio‑fuel feedstock.

Customary Law and Formal Courts: The Role of Gram Nyayalayas in Tribal Justice

The Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 established village‑level courts to deliver inexpensive, speedy justice in rural areas, including Scheduled Areas. Each Gram Nyayalaya is presided over by a Nyayadhikari (a judicial magistrate of first class) and assisted by two lay assessors nominated by the Gram Sabha. The Act mandates that procedural laws (CPC, CrPC) apply only “as far as practicable”, allowing the court to adopt customary dispute‑resolution norms — such as the Gond panchayat practice of nyaya (restorative compensation) instead of punitive fines. In 2015 the Ministry of Law and Justice reported 395 functional Gram Nyayalayas, of which 112 operated in Fifth‑Schedule districts. Empirical studies (e.g., the 2019 Access to Justice Survey by the National Law University, Delhi) show that tribal litigants prefer Gram Nyayalayas for land‑boundary disputes, forest‑rights claims, and minor criminal matters because proceedings are conducted in the local dialect (Gondi, Halbi, Korku) and evidence can be oral. However, jurisdictional overlap with the District Court and the High Court’s supervisory powers under Article 227 creates forum‑shopping. The 2021 amendment to the Act empowered the State Government to notify “Special Gram Nyayalayas” for Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs), with assessors drawn from the community’s traditional headmen (mukhia, patel). For examination purposes, candidates should contrast the adversarial formal system (precedent‑bound, lawyer‑driven) with the consensual customary model (community‑validated, restorative), and cite the constitutional mandate of Article 39A (equal justice and free legal aid) as the policy rationale.

  • Confusing the Tribal Sub‑Plan (a budgetary earmark) with the Integrated Tribal Development Project (an implementation unit) — they operate at different administrative levels.
  • Assuming PRA tools are interchangeable; each tool (social map, seasonal calendar, wealth ranking) yields distinct data types and cannot substitute for another.
  • Overstating the Forest Rights Act’s automatic transfer of NTFP trade rights — CFR recognition requires a Gram Sabha resolution and state government notification.
  • Treating Gram Nyayalayas as a parallel judiciary; they are subordinate to the District Court and subject to High Court revision.
  • Neglecting the distinction between Fifth‑Schedule (Governor’s regulations) and Sixth‑Schedule (Autonomous District Councils) when discussing customary law enforcement.
  • Quoting outdated MSP figures for tendu leaf or mahua without referencing the latest state‑level notification.
  • Presenting “tribal development” as a linear progression; policy shifts are iterative, with overlapping plan‑era allocations persisting alongside rights‑based frameworks.

Scheduled Areas Governance: Fifth Schedule, PESA, and the Role of the Governor

The Fifth Schedule of the Constitution applies to the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in states other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Under Article 244(1), the President may declare an area as a Scheduled Area after consultation with the Governor of the concerned state. The Governor then exercises special responsibilities under the Fifth Schedule: the power to make regulations for the peace and good government of the area (Paragraph 5), the authority to direct that any particular Act of Parliament or the State Legislature shall not apply or shall apply with modifications, and the duty to submit an annual report to the President on the administration of Scheduled Areas (Paragraph 3).

The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) operationalises the constitutional mandate by extending Part IX to Scheduled Areas with crucial modifications. PESA recognises the Gram Sabha as the competent authority for safeguarding community resources, managing minor forest produce, controlling money‑lending, and regulating land alienation. Section 4(m) mandates that the Gram Sabha approve plans, programmes and projects for social and economic development before they are taken up for implementation by the village Panchayat. The Act also requires State Legislatures to endow Panchayats with powers necessary for self‑governance, including the power to enforce prohibition, manage village markets, and control local institutions.

Judicial interpretation has clarified the interplay between the Governor’s regulatory power and PESA. In Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) the Supreme Court held that the Governor’s regulation under the Fifth Schedule can prohibit the transfer of tribal land to non‑tribals, and that such regulation prevails over inconsistent State laws. In Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests (2013) the Court reiterated that the Gram Sabha’s consent under the Forest Rights Act, 2006 is a prerequisite for any diversion of forest land in Scheduled Areas, reinforcing the PESA principle of community consent.

Administratively, the Governor’s annual report is examined by the Ministry of Tribal Affairs and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes. Recent reports indicate that a minority of the ten States with Scheduled Areas have fully operationalised PESA provisions, and that devolution of funds to Gram Sabhas remains a small fraction of the Tribal Sub‑Plan allocation. For answer‑writing, candidates should link the constitutional architecture (Article 244, Fifth Schedule) to the statutory framework (PESA, FRA) and cite the Supreme Court’s “consent” jurisprudence as the binding legal standard.

Tribal Health and Nutrition: Epidemiological Transition and Policy Interventions

Tribal populations exhibit a distinct epidemiological profile characterised by a high burden of communicable diseases, rising non‑communicable disease (NCD) prevalence, and persistent under‑nutrition. The National Family Health Survey‑5 (2019‑21) reports that tribal children under five show higher rates of stunting, wasting, and anaemia among women compared to national averages. Simultaneously, the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes among tribal adults reflects an epidemiological transition driven by dietary shifts, reduced physical activity, and increased tobacco and alcohol consumption.

The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare has formulated tribal‑specific action plans under the National Health Mission. These prioritise: (1) strengthening sub‑centres and Primary Health Centres in Fifth‑Schedule districts through dedicated tribal health sub‑centres; (2) deploying Mobile Medical Units (MMUs) to reach Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) such as the Baiga, Saharia and Jarawa; (3) integrating traditional healers into the formal referral chain through structured collaboration frameworks; and (4) ensuring supply‑chain security for iron‑folic acid, albendazole, and anti‑malarial drugs.

Nutrition‑specific interventions include the tribal component of Poshan Abhiyaan, which provides take‑home rations fortified with millets (ragi, bajra) and locally sourced pulses. Evaluations by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) have shown reductions in severe acute malnutrition in districts where the Community‑Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) protocol was implemented with active Gram Sabha monitoring. However, gaps persist: recent Comptroller and Auditor General reports note that a significant proportion of sanctioned MMUs remain non‑functional, and data on tribal health indicators are often disaggregated only at the State level, masking intra‑district disparities.

For examination answers, candidates should contrast the “vertical disease‑control” approach (e.g., National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme) with the “horizontal health‑system strengthening” envisaged under tribal health action plans, and cite the constitutional mandate of Article 46 (promotion of educational and economic interests of Scheduled Tribes) as the policy rationale for targeted health financing.

Answer‑Writing Technique: Comparative Policy Analysis
Question: “Critically examine the shift from disease‑specific vertical programmes to the horizontal tribal health system strengthening under the National Tribal Health Action Plan.”
A high‑scoring answer structures the response in three layers. First, define vertical programmes (e.g., NVBDCP, Revised National Tuberculosis Control Programme) — centrally designed, disease‑specific, with dedicated funding and monitoring. Second, outline the horizontal framework — integration of tribal health into the general health system, emphasis on Gram Sabha governance, MMUs, and traditional healer inclusion. Third, evaluate outcomes using NFHS‑5 and CAG data: vertical programmes achieved reductions in malaria incidence but left systemic gaps (high staff vacancies in tribal PHCs). Horizontal measures improved antenatal care coverage in pilot districts, yet MMU functionality remains low. Conclude that a hybrid model — retaining disease‑specific surveillance while embedding service delivery in strengthened primary care — aligns with Article 46 and Sustainable Development Goal 3.8 (universal health coverage).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “critical examination”, allocate 30 % of the word limit to a concise description of each policy, 50 % to evidence‑based comparison (use NFHS, CAG, ICMR figures), and 20 % to a balanced judgement that references constitutional provisions (Art. 46, 244) and Supreme Court directions (Samatha, Orissa Mining). Keep a ready table of key health indicators for tribal vs. national averages for quick insertion.

Anthropology of Displacement: Development‑Induced Migration and Rehabilitation

Development‑induced displacement (DID) in India has affected tens of millions of people since independence, with tribal communities constituting a disproportionate share (40‑50 %) of the displaced despite representing only 8.6 % of the total population (Census 2011). Major drivers include large dams (e.g., Sardar Sarovar, Polavaram), mining projects (e.g., Niyamgiri bauxite, Jharia coal), industrial corridors (Delhi‑Mumbai Industrial Corridor), and wildlife conservation (tiger reserves under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972). The Land Acquisition Act, 1894 was replaced by the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR), which mandates a Social Impact Assessment (SIA), consent of 70‑80 % of affected families for private projects, and a Rehabilitation and Resettlement (R&R) package that includes land‑for‑land, housing, livelihood restoration, and a one‑time subsistence allowance.

Despite the statutory framework, implementation gaps are documented. CAG audits of major projects have found that a low proportion of displaced tribal families received the promised land‑for‑land compensation, and that many R&R colonies lack basic infrastructure (water supply, sanitation, schools). The Forest Rights Act, 2006 provides an additional layer: Section 4(5) requires that no forest rights be extinguished without the free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) of the Gram Sabha. In Vedanta Ltd. v. Union of India (2013) the Supreme Court upheld the Gram Sabha’s veto over the Niyamgiri mining lease, establishing FPIC as a justiciable right.

Anthropological studies highlight cultural dimensions of displacement: loss of sacred groves (sarna, devrai), disruption of shifting cultivation cycles, and erosion of oral knowledge systems. The “cultural displacement” concept, articulated by scholars such as Walter Fernandes and Amita Baviskar, argues that compensation measured solely in monetary terms fails to capture the non‑material losses that sustain tribal identity. Rehabilitation policies increasingly incorporate “cultural impact assessment” modules, but these remain advisory rather than mandatory.

For answer‑writing, candidates should juxtapose the legal safeguards (LARR 2013, FRA 2006, PESA 1996) with empirical outcomes (CAG, NITI Aayog evaluations), and reference the Supreme Court’s “FPIC” jurisprudence as the emerging standard for legitimate displacement.

Gender and Kinship Transformations Among Northeastern Tribes

The Northeastern region hosts over 200 distinct tribal groups, each with varied kinship organisations ranging from matrilineal (Khasi, Jaintia, Garo) to patrilineal (Naga, Mizo, Arunachal tribes) and bilateral systems (Bodo, Karbi). Matrilineal societies trace descent through the mother, with property and clan name transmitted through daughters; the youngest daughter (khadduh among Khasi) inherits the ancestral home and custodial responsibilities. Patrilineal Naga clans (e.g., Angami, Ao) organise around exogamous clans (khel) with strong male‑centred political institutions (village councils).

Post‑colonial state policies, missionary education, and market integration have triggered kinship transformations. The Khasi Hills Autonomous District Council (under the Sixth Schedule) enacted the Khasi Social Custom of Lineage Act, 1997, which codified matrilineal inheritance but also introduced provisions for male heirs to claim a share of parental property, reflecting pressure from gender‑equality advocates. Among the Mizo, the Mizo Marriage, Divorce and Inheritance of Property Act, 2014 replaced customary law with a uniform code granting equal inheritance rights to sons and daughters, a shift contested by traditional chiefs (lal).

Empirical work by the North Eastern Hill University (NEHU) indicates that female labour force participation in matrilineal societies remains higher than in patrilineal counterparts, yet decision‑making authority in public spheres (village councils, church bodies) is still male‑dominated. Reports by the National Commission for Women note rising incidence of domestic violence in both systems, linked to alcohol misuse and cash‑economy stressors.

For examination, candidates should compare the constitutional protection of customary law under Article 371A (Nagaland) and Article 371G (Mizoram) with the statutory reforms, and analyse how the Sixth Schedule’s Autonomous District Councils mediate between tradition and gender‑justice claims.

Digital Ethnography and Data Sovereignty in Tribal Research

The proliferation of mobile connectivity (high teledensity in tribal districts per TRAI data) and the COVID‑19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of digital ethnography — remote participant observation, mobile‑based surveys, and community‑generated multimedia. Anthropologists now use platforms such as Open Data Kit (ODK), KoBoToolbox, and WhatsApp groups for real‑time data collection on health‑seeking behaviour, NTFP market prices, and language documentation.

However, the shift raises data‑sovereignty concerns. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) Article 31 affirms the right of indigenous peoples to maintain, control, protect and develop their cultural heritage, traditional knowledge and traditional cultural expressions. In India, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 classifies biometric and genetic data as “special category data” requiring explicit consent. Tribal communities often lack the legal literacy to negotiate informed consent forms drafted in English or Hindi.

Collaborative models such as the “Community‑Owned Data Protocol” piloted in the Dangs district (Gujarat) require that raw data be stored on a community‑managed server, with researchers receiving only anonymised aggregates. The protocol also mandates benefit‑sharing: a percentage of any commercial revenue from derived products (e.g., herbal formulations) flows back to the Gram Sabha.

For answer‑writing, candidates should link methodological innovation (digital tools, participatory GIS) to ethical frameworks (UNDRIP, DPDP Act, FRA’s community forest rights) and cite the ethical guidelines for research with tribal communities issued by the Indian Council of Social Science Research (ICSSR) as the operative standard.

  • Confusing the Governor’s regulatory power under the Fifth Schedule with the legislative competence of the State Legislature — the Governor can modify central laws but cannot enact new statutes.
  • Treating PESA as a uniform law across all Scheduled Areas; State‑level PESA rules vary significantly in the devolution of powers to Gram Sabhas.
  • Citing outdated NFHS‑4 figures for tribal health indicators when NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) is the current reference.
  • Assuming LARR 2013 automatically guarantees land‑for‑land; the Act provides the entitlement but implementation depends on State‑level R&R policies and availability of cultivable land.
  • Equating matrilineal inheritance with gender equality; property transmission through women does not ensure women’s participation in political decision‑making.
  • Overlooking the requirement of Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) under FRA 2006 when discussing displacement — consent is a procedural right, not a mere consultation.
  • Neglecting the distinction between “special category data” and “personal data” under the DPDP Act 2023 when designing digital ethnography protocols for tribal communities.

Tribal Land Alienation and the Forest Rights Act: Implementation Gaps and Judicial Interpretations

The Forest Rights Act (FRA) 2006 recognises individual and community forest rights for Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers. Despite the statutory framework, land alienation persists through procedural delays, mis‑classification of forest land, and limited recognition of community forest resource (CFR) rights. Official data indicate that a small proportion of claimed CFR titles have been granted, with many claims pending at the Sub‑Divisional and District Level Committee stages. Judicial interventions have shaped the Act’s operational contours. The Supreme Court’s 2013 judgment in Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests (the Niyamgiri case) affirmed that Gram Sabhas must give free, prior and informed consent before any diversion of forest land for mining, reinforcing the procedural primacy of the FRA over the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980. Subsequent High Court orders in Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Odisha have directed state governments to expedite verification surveys, appoint independent forest rights committees, and prevent evictions pending final adjudication. A proposed 2019 amendment to the Indian Forest Act 1927, which would have empowered forest officers to summarily evict “encroachers”, was not enacted; however, the legal tension between summary eviction powers and FRA procedures remains a live issue in litigation. For answer‑writing, candidates should contrast the statutory mandate of the FRA (Sections 3, 4, 5) with the executive practice of “deemed forest” notifications, and cite the Niyamgiri precedent as the authoritative interpretation of Gram Sabha consent.

Urban Tribal Migration: Labour Markets, Identity Negotiation, and Policy Responses

Large‑scale migration of tribal youth to metropolitan centres — Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad — has accelerated since the 1990s, driven by agrarian distress, loss of forest access, and the expansion of informal construction, domestic work, and security‑guard sectors. Research by institutions such as the ICSSR and NIRD indicates that migrant tribal workers often occupy the lowest rungs of the urban labour market, facing wage discrimination, lack of social security, and housing precarity in informal settlements. Identity negotiation in the city is mediated by community networks, religious institutions, and ethnic associations that recreate kinship‑based support systems. The state’s policy response has been fragmented: the Inter‑State Migrant Workmen Act 1979 remains poorly enforced; the Unorganised Workers’ Social Security Act 2008 excludes many tribal migrants due to documentation barriers; and the e‑Shram portal (2021) aims to register unorganised workers but reports low tribal enrolment, attributed partly to digital access gaps. Field studies in Delhi’s tribal settlements show that remittance flows sustain rural households, yet the erosion of traditional authority structures leads to inter‑generational tension over marriage, education, and land decisions. For examination, aspirants should link push‑pull factors to the concept of “circular migration”, evaluate the adequacy of existing labour legislation, and discuss how urban tribal collectives negotiate citizenship claims through the FRA’s community forest resource provisions even while residing outside scheduled areas.

Technique
Explain how the Niyamgiri judgment operationalises the principle of Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) under the Forest Rights Act, and why this creates a conflict with the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980.
The Niyamgiri judgment (2013) held that the Gram Sabha’s consent is a prerequisite before any forest land can be diverted for non‑forest purposes, reading Section 4(5) of the FRA — which empowers Gram Sabhas to protect community forest resources — as a substantive veto. The Court ruled that the Ministry of Environment & Forests could not grant Stage‑II clearance under the Forest (Conservation) Act without first obtaining Gram Sabha resolutions, thereby elevating FRA’s procedural requirement above the earlier statutory regime. The conflict arises because the Forest (Conservation) Act vests diversion authority in the central government, while the FRA vests consent authority in the Gram Sabha. The judgment resolves the conflict by interpreting the FRA as a later, special law that prevails over the general Forest (Conservation) Act (lex specialis derogat legi generali). In answer‑writing, cite the judgment’s paragraph on “the Gram Sabha is the ultimate authority” and note that subsequent High Court orders have enforced this precedent by staying evictions pending Gram Sabha consent.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for “judicial interpretation of tribal land rights”, structure the answer in three layers: (1) statutory provision (FRA Sections 3‑5), (2) landmark judgment (Niyamgiri, 2013) with its legal reasoning, (3) subsequent executive or legislative friction (proposed 2019 Indian Forest Act amendment). This layered approach signals depth and avoids a mere narrative of events.

Traditional Ecological Knowledge and Biodiversity Conservation in Tribal Landscapes

Tribal communities manage vast tracts of biodiversity through customary practices that pre‑date formal protected‑area networks. Sacred groves (e.g., the “Devrai” of the Western Ghats, the “Sarna” of Jharkhand), shifting cultivation cycles (jhum in the Northeast, podu in Odisha), and community‑regulated hunting taboos function as de‑facto conservation regimes. The Biological Diversity Act 2002 recognises “traditional knowledge associated with biological resources” and mandates benefit‑sharing with local communities, yet the operationalisation of Access and Benefit Sharing (ABS) agreements remains limited. The National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) has notified several “Biodiversity Heritage Sites” that overlap with tribal territories, but the consent process has been criticised for inadequately involving Gram Sabhas, contravening the FRA’s community forest resource provisions. Ecological research documents that tribal fire‑management regimes maintain mosaic landscapes that support endemic flora and fauna, while state‑imposed fire suppression policies have led to fuel‑load accumulation and high‑intensity wildfires. The 2022 amendment to the Wildlife (Protection) Act, which expands the definition of “community reserves”, offers a legal pathway to recognise tribal stewardship, provided the governance structure respects customary decision‑making bodies. For examination, candidates should compare the “fortress conservation” model (exclusionary protected areas) with the “community‑based conservation” model, cite the role of the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) Article 8(j) in shaping domestic law, and analyse the tension between the Forest (Conservation) Act’s centralised clearance and the FRA’s decentralised consent.

Genetic Diversity, Population Structure, and Health Implications Among Indian Tribal Groups

Population‑genetic studies by institutions such as the CCMB, ISI, and NIBMG indicate that Indian tribal groups exhibit high levels of genetic drift, endogamy, and unique allele frequencies. A gradient of genetic affinity correlating with geography and language families (Austroasiatic, Dravidian, Tibeto‑Burman) has been mapped using genome‑wide SNP arrays. Specific health‑relevant variants include the high prevalence of the sickle‑cell trait (HbS) in central Indian tribes such as the Gond, Bhil, and Baiga, and G6PD deficiency alleles in certain Northeastern groups. These genetic profiles have direct policy relevance: the National Health Mission’s “Sickle Cell Disease Control Programme” targets tribal districts based on carrier‑frequency surveys, and the ICMR’s “Tribal Health Research Forum” coordinates community‑based screening. However, ethical concerns persist — informed consent procedures often lack linguistic appropriateness, and benefit‑sharing from commercial pharmacogenomic research is limited. The ICMR 2017 ethical guidelines for biomedical research emphasise community engagement, data sovereignty, and the return of results, with specific provisions for tribal populations. For answer‑writing, aspirants should link the concept of “genetic isolation” to the epidemiological transition observed in tribal areas (rising non‑communicable diseases alongside persistent infectious burden), and discuss how the FRA’s community forest rights can be leveraged to secure traditional medicinal plant knowledge that complements modern pharmacogenomics.

Anthropology of Tribal Education: Language Policy, Curriculum Design, and Pedagogical Challenges

Education policy for tribal children operates at the intersection of constitutional safeguards (Article 29, 350A), the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act 2009, and the National Education Policy 2020’s emphasis on mother‑tongue‑based multilingual education (MTB‑MLE). Ashram schools, Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS), and Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas (KGBV) constitute the primary institutional architecture. Despite increased enrolment, learning outcomes remain low; the Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) consistently shows tribal districts lagging in foundational literacy and numeracy. Pedagogical challenges include the absence of textbooks in tribal languages (e.g., Gondi, Santhali, Bodo, Mizo), teacher recruitment from non‑tribal backgrounds leading to cultural disconnect, and a curriculum that marginalises indigenous knowledge systems. The National Curriculum Framework for School Education (2023) proposes integration of local ecological knowledge, oral traditions, and community histories, but implementation depends on state‑level textbook bureaus. Digital initiatives such as DIKSHA and the “Tribal Language Atlas” (developed by the Central Institute of Indian Languages) aim to create open‑access resources, yet internet penetration in remote tribal blocks remains uneven. Evaluations by institutions such as TISS highlight that community‑managed school management committees (SMCs) improve attendance and relevance when they control curriculum adaptation. For examination, candidates should critically assess the tension between standardised assessment regimes and culturally responsive pedagogy, reference the Constitutional provision for instruction in mother tongue (Article 350A), and discuss how the FRA’s community forest rights can fund community‑run schools through forest‑produce revenue.
