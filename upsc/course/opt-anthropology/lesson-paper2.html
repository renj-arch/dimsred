Anthropology Paper II — Archaeology & Applied Anthropology

Archaeological Anthropology

  • Methods: Survey (surface survey, aerial photography, remote sensing, GIS), excavation (stratigraphic excavation — Wheeler box grid, Wheeler-Kenyon method), dating methods.
  • Dating methods: Relative — stratigraphy (law of superposition), seriation, typology, fluorine dating. Absolute — radiocarbon (C-14, up to ~50,000 years), dendrochronology (tree rings), thermoluminescence (TL), potassium-argon (K-Ar, older sites), uranium-series, obsidian hydration.
  • Paleolithic (~2.5 Ma - 10,000 BP): Lower (Oldowan — Homo habilis; Acheulean — H. erectus hand axes), Middle (Mousterian — Neanderthals), Upper (blade tools, Homo sapiens, cave art — Lascaux, Chauvet, Bhimbetka in India).
  • Mesolithic (~10,000 - 8,000 BP): Microliths, bow-arrow, fishing, semi-sedentary, Mesolithic rock art (Bhimbetka, Bhimbetka shelters).
  • Neolithic (~8,000 - 4,000 BP): Agriculture & animal domestication (wheat, barley, rice, millets; sheep, goat, cattle, pig), pottery, polished stone axes, permanent villages (Jericho, Çatalhöyük, Mehrgarh in Pakistan), megaliths.
  • Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone, ~4,000-3,000 BP): Copper tools alongside stone, pottery, early urbanism (Mehrgarh phases, pre-Harappan).
  • Iron Age (~1,200 BP onwards): Iron technology, black-and-red ware, painted grey ware, megalithic burials (dolmens, cairns, menhirs, urn burials) in South India.
  • Harappan civilization (~2,600-1,900 BCE): Mature Harappan — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Lothal, Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi; urban planning (grid, drainage, great bath, granary, citadel & lower town), seals, script (undeciphered), trade (Mesopotamia), weights & measures, decline theories (climate change, river shift, invasion/migration theories debated).
  • Megalithic & post-megalithic South India: Dolmens, cairn circles, menhirs, urn burials, rock-cut caves, black-and-red ware, early Tamil sangam associations.

Applied Anthropology

  • Definition: Application of anthropological knowledge & methods to practical problems — development, health, education, environment, business, forensics.
  • Development anthropology: Critique of top-down development; participatory approaches; cultural appropriateness; displacement & resettlement (dams, mines, national parks — oustees & rehabilitation).
  • Tribal development in India: Constitutional safeguards — Fifth Schedule (scheduled areas, TAC), Sixth Schedule (autonomous districts NE), Article 342 (scheduled tribes list), Article 275(1) (grants), Article 338A (NCST); Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP), Integrated Tribal Development Projects (ITDP/Agencies), Forest Rights Act 2006 (individual & community forest rights, habitat rights for PVTGs), PESA 1996 (gram sabha powers in scheduled areas), National Policy on Tribals, Vanbandhu Kalyan Yojana.
  • Tribal problems: Land alienation, indebtedness, displacement (dams, mines, parks), migration, health (malnutrition, sickle cell, malaria, TB), education (dropout, language), nutrition, livelihood loss, Naxalism/Maoist insurgency links to deprivation & alienation.
  • Medical anthropology: Health & disease as cultural constructs; ethnomedicine (traditional healers — ojha, baiga, vaidy), pluralistic health systems (biomedicine + traditional), nutrition, reproductive health, mental health, disability, pandemics & stigma, COVID-19 & indigenous communities.
  • Environmental anthropology: Indigenous ecological knowledge (IEK/TEK), sacred groves, community forestry, biodiversity conservation & displacement (protected areas & tribal rights), climate change & vulnerable communities.
  • Business/corporate anthropology: Organizational culture, consumer behavior, user-centered design.
  • Forensic anthropology: Identification from skeletal remains (age, sex, ancestry, stature, trauma), mass disaster identification.

Tribal India: Issues & Movements

  • Constitutional safeguards: Fifth Schedule (scheduled areas in 10 states — TAC, governor's role), Sixth Schedule (autonomous districts in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram — district councils), Article 342 (ST list), Article 275(1) (special grants), Article 338A (National Commission for STs), Article 164(1) (tribal ministers in MP, Bihar, Odisha, etc.), Article 350 (instruction in mother tongue).
  • PESA 1996: Gram sabha powers in scheduled areas — land, minor forest produce, local plans, institutions, money lending, intoxicants.
  • FRA 2006: Individual forest rights (cultivation & habitation), community forest rights (community use), habitat rights for PVTGs, right to protect & manage forests, gram sabha as decision authority.
  • Tribal movements: Santhal (1855-56), Munda (Birsa Munda, 1899-1900), Bhil, Koli, Naga, Mizoram, Jharkhand movement (Jharkhand Mukti Morcha), Bodoland, contemporary identity & autonomy movements; Naxalism/Maoism — roots in tribal deprivation, land alienation, lack of development, state response (security + development).
Technique: FRA 2006 answer
Question: 'FRA 2006 empowers forest-dwelling communities but implementation remains weak.' Discuss.
Answer: FRA recognizes individual & community forest rights, habitat rights for PVTGs, gram sabha as authority. Achievements: millions of claims filed, lakhs of titles issued (esp. Odisha, MP, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra). Weaknesses: forest department resistance, lack of awareness, diluted guidelines (2012), conservation vs rights tension (tiger core zones), delayed recognition, implementation gaps. Way forward: gram sabha empowerment, time-bound disposal, convergence with MGNREGA/NRLM, monitoring cells.

Memory Tricks

  • Archaeology sequence: Paleolithic (old stone) → Mesolithic (microliths) → Neolithic (agriculture, polished stone) → Chalcolithic (copper) → Iron Age.
  • Safeguards: 5th = scheduled areas (most states); 6th = autonomous districts (NE).
  • FRA 2006: Individual rights + community rights + habitat rights (PVTGs) + gram sabha decides.

Archaeological Chronologies: From Microliths to Iron and the Chalcolithic Transition

The chronological framework of Indian prehistory is not merely a sequence of dates but a narrative of technological adaptation driven by environmental shifts. The Mesolithic period (c. 10,000–8,000 BCE) in India is defined by the transition from large-game hunting to small-game hunting and gathering, necessitating smaller, more efficient stone tools known as microliths. These are not isolated flakes but composite tools fitted into wooden or bone shafts. The Bombon culture of the Deccan plateau and the Kurnool culture in Andhra Pradesh exemplify this, showing a distinct adaptation to semi-arid environments where large animals were scarce. The shift from the Levallois technique (used in Upper Paleolithic) to the prepared core method is evident here, reflecting a move towards standardization and efficiency.

The Neolithic revolution in India is marked by the advent of polished stone tools and pottery, signifying sedentism and agriculture. The Narmada Valley culture (c. 6000 BCE) represents one of the earliest Neolithic cultures, characterized by microlithic industries and early pottery. However, the true agricultural breakthrough is seen in the Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone Age) period, which bridges the gap between stone-age technology and the Iron Age. The term 'Chalcolithic' implies the use of copper alongside stone, but archaeological evidence suggests that metallurgy was not widespread; rather, it refers to the technological sophistication of pottery and agriculture.

The Mahenagiri and Kurdukh cultures in central India showcase the transition from polished stone axes to copper ornaments. The most significant Chalcolithic assemblage is found at Ahar-Banas (Rajasthan) and Gandhara Grave Culture (Gujarat), though the latter is often debated. The Mahakala culture in Madhya Pradesh reveals a complex society with fortified settlements, indicating social stratification and warfare. The pottery styles—black-and-red ware, painted grey ware, and red ware—provide crucial dating markers. For instance, the presence of rice grains in potsherds at sites like Bhagwanpura confirms wet-rice cultivation techniques similar to modern Andhra Pradesh.

The Iron Age (c. 1200–600 BCE) introduces a paradigm shift with the introduction of iron tools, which allowed for deforestation and expansion into dense forests. The Kaikadi culture in Karnataka and the Hastinapura culture in Uttar Pradesh show the spread of iron technology. This period coincides with the Vedic age, but archaeological finds like the Pataliputra seals (Mauryan era) and the Ahichhat Prayag site provide material evidence for textual corroboration. The Sangam Age in Tamil Nadu (c. 300 BCE–300 CE) is archaeologically linked to the black-and-red ware found at sites like Tirumalai, which connects the literary tradition of the Sangam poets with material culture. The discovery of Roman coins and glass bangles in coastal Tamil Nadu sites like Korkai and Muziris (though Muziris's exact location is debated) highlights the maritime trade connections described in Tamil literature.

Examiner's trap: The "Copper Age" Misconception
A common error is assuming that because a site has copper beads, it belongs to the Chalcolithic period. Correct answer: The Chalcolithic term refers to a specific technological phase where copper was used for ornaments and small tools alongside stone, but widespread metallurgy (smelting iron) defines the Iron Age. Many Neolithic sites have copper finds but are still classified as Neolithic if polished stone axes dominate. Always prioritize the dominant tool kit (polished stone vs. iron) over minor metal artifacts.
Exam-Day Strategy: Chronology Mapping
When answering questions on prehistory, do not just list periods. Draw a mental timeline: Start with Paleolithic (hunting/gathering), move to Mesolithic (microliths/environmental change), then Neolithic (agriculture/pottery). Insert Chalcolithic as the bridge where copper ornaments appear but stone tools dominate. End with Iron Age and Vedic texts. Always link archaeological sites to specific regions (e.g., Ahar-Banas = Rajasthan, Mahakala = MP) to show spatial awareness. Use terms like 'sedentism', 'polished axes', and 'rice cultivation' to demonstrate technical vocabulary.

Tribal Constitutional Safeguards: The Architecture of Autonomy and Rights

The Indian Constitution provides a robust framework for tribal protection, distinct from general minority protections. The core lies in the Fifth Schedule (Articles 244(1) & 275(1)), applicable to states other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. It creates 'Scheduled Areas' where the Governor has special powers to make regulations regarding land transfer, money lending, and intoxicants without parliamentary interference. The Tribal Advisory Committee (TAC) assists the Governor in these matters, ensuring local tribal opinion is considered. This mechanism prevents external exploitation by landlords or moneylenders.

The Sixth Schedule applies exclusively to Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram, creating 'Autonomous District Councils' (ADCs). These councils possess legislative powers over land, forests, agriculture, and social customs within their districts. This is a form of devolution of power, allowing tribes to govern themselves according to customary laws. However, the ADCs are subject to parliamentary override under Article 200(3), ensuring national unity prevails over local autonomy in case of conflict.

The Forest Rights Act (FRA) 2006 is a statutory implementation of these constitutional mandates. It recognizes two types of rights: Individual Forest Rights (IFR) for cultivation and habitation, and Community Forest Rights (CFR) for common use resources like grazing lands, forests, and water bodies. The Act also grants 'habitat rights' to Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs), allowing them to protect their biodiversity and sacred groves. The Gram Sabha acts as the decision-making body, ensuring that forest management is community-led rather than state-imposed.

The PESA Act 1996 (Part IX of the 73rd Amendment) complements FRA by empowering Gram Sabhas in Scheduled Areas to manage local resources, including land, minor forest produce, and money lending. It mandates that no land transfer or lease can occur without the Gram Sabha's consent. This addresses the historical issue of land alienation where tribes were forced to sell land to outsiders due to lack of legal awareness.

The National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST), established under Article 338A, functions as a watchdog. Unlike the NITI Aayog, NCST has the power to investigate and recommend remedies for violations of tribal rights. It also monitors the implementation of the Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP) and Integrated Tribal Development Projects (ITDP).
