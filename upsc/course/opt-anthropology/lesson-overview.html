1. Understanding the Anthropology Optional Framework

1.1 The Discipline and Its Relevance to Civil Services

Anthropology occupies a distinctive position among UPSC optional subjects because it combines scientific rigour with humanistic insight, allowing candidates from diverse academic backgrounds to engage with the material without requiring prior specialization. The discipline studies humankind in its totality — biological evolution, cultural diversity, social organization, and archaeological past — through a holistic lens that connects micro-level field observations to macro-level theoretical frameworks. This integrative character makes anthropology unusually compatible with the General Studies papers: Paper I topics on tribal society, social movements, and cultural diversity directly overlap with GS I (Indian Society) and GS II (Social Justice), while the ethical dimensions of fieldwork, applied anthropology, and development ethics resonate with GS IV (Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude). The syllabus is compact relative to subjects like History or Geography, yet it demands conceptual precision, especially in physical anthropology where terminology from genetics, primatology, and palaeoanthropology must be used accurately. Candidates who master the art of linking theoretical concepts — such as structural-functionalism, cultural materialism, or postmodern critique — to concrete Indian ethnographic examples consistently score above 300 marks, making this optional a high-return investment for those willing to internalize its vocabulary and logic.

1.2 Paper I Architecture: Physical and Social Anthropology

Paper I is divided into two broad domains that reflect the traditional split between biological and cultural anthropology, though the UPSC syllabus deliberately interweaves them through the Indian anthropology section. The first domain, Physical Anthropology, begins with the meaning, scope, and historical development of the discipline, requiring familiarity with the intellectual trajectory from nineteenth-century unilineal evolutionism (Tylor, Morgan) through the functionalist revolution (Malinowski, Radcliffe-Brown) to structuralism (Lévi-Strauss) and postmodern reflexivity (Geertz, Clifford). This historical spine is not decorative; questions routinely ask candidates to evaluate, for instance, how functionalism addressed the limitations of evolutionism or why postmodernism challenged the authority of ethnographic representation. The human evolution segment demands command over primate taxonomy, comparative anatomy, and the fossil chronology from Proconsul and Dryopithecus through Australopithecus afarensis, Homo habilis, Homo erectus (including the Narmada hominin), to anatomically modern Homo sapiens. Candidates must understand the debate between the "Out of Africa" and multiregional models, the significance of mitochondrial Eve and Y-chromosomal Adam, and the critique of biological race concepts advanced by Livingstone, Montagu, and the UNESCO statements of 1950 and 1951. Human genetics covers Mendelian inheritance, population genetics (Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, genetic drift, gene flow), and adaptation to environmental stressors such as high altitude (Andean, Tibetan, Ethiopian adaptations), heat cold, and disease (sickle cell trait and malaria). The second domain, Social Anthropology, examines universal institutions — marriage, family, kinship, religion, economy, and political organization — through comparative ethnography. Key concepts include the distinction between descent groups (lineage, clan, phratry, moiety) and alliance theory (Lévi-Strauss on elementary structures), the typology of political organization from band to state (Service, Fried), and the modes of exchange (reciprocity, redistribution, market) articulated by Polanyi and Sahlins. The Indian anthropology section anchors these abstractions in the subcontinent's reality: the interplay of varna and jati, the dynamics of Sanskritization (Srinivas), Westernization, and modernization, the constitutional architecture of tribal protection (Fifth Schedule, Sixth Schedule, Article 342, Article 275(1), Article 338A), and the legislative framework of the Forest Rights Act 2006 and PESA 1996. Fieldwork methodology — participant observation, genealogy, life history, PRA/RRA, and the ethical imperatives of informed consent and reflexivity — completes the paper.

1.3 Paper II Architecture: Archaeological and Applied Anthropology

Paper II shifts the focus from synchronic social analysis to diachronic depth and policy relevance. Archaeological Anthropology requires mastery of excavation techniques (grid system, quadrant method, open-area excavation), relative dating (stratigraphy, seriation, typology, fluorine-uranium-nitrogen analysis), and absolute dating methods (radiocarbon C-14 with its half-life of 5,730 years and calibration curves, dendrochronology, thermoluminescence, potassium-argon, argon-argon, electron spin resonance, optically stimulated luminescence). The Indian prehistoric sequence must be known in regional detail: Lower Palaeolithic (Soanian, Madrasian, Acheulian sites like Attirampakkam, Bhimbetka, Hunsgi), Middle Palaeolithic, Upper Palaeolithic, Mesolithic (microlithic industries, rock art at Bhimbetka, Adamgarh), Neolithic (Kashmir Burzahom, Gufkral; South Indian ash mounds at Utnur, Kupgal; Eastern Indian sites like Chirand), Chalcolithic (Ahar-Banas, Kayatha, Malwa, Jorwe, Savalda), and the Iron Age (Painted Grey Ware, Northern Black Polished Ware, Megalithic cultures of Peninsular India with their cist, cairn, urn, and sarcophagus burials). The Harappan Civilization demands attention to urban planning, script, trade (Meluhha contacts), and the debate over its decline (climate change, river shifts, Aryan migration). Applied Anthropology translates disciplinary knowledge into development practice: displacement and rehabilitation policies for dam projects (Narmada, Tehri), mining, and protected areas; the Tribal Sub-Plan strategy, Integrated Tribal Development Projects/Agencies (ITDPs/ITDAs), and the implementation gaps in the Forest Rights Act 2006 (individual and community forest rights, habitat rights for Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups); medical anthropology's examination of plural healing systems (Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, folk, biomedical) and the social determinants of health in tribal populations (sickle cell anaemia, G6PD deficiency, malnutrition, malaria); and the anthropology of development, NGOs, and participatory approaches (Chambers, Escobar, Ferguson). Rural and urban anthropology draws on classic village studies — M.N. Srinivas's Rampura, André Beteille's Sripuram, D.N. Majumdar's Mohana, S.C. Dube's Shamirpet — to illustrate the rural-urban continuum, migration streams, informal sector dynamics, and the transformation of caste and kinship in metropolitan contexts. Economic anthropology covers subsistence modes (hunting-gathering among the Hadza, Paliyan, Chenchu; pastoralism among the Toda, Gujjar, Bakarwal; shifting cultivation or jhum in Northeast India), peasant studies (Shanin, Wolf, Scott's moral economy), and the impact of globalization on livelihoods.

2. Detailed Syllabus Mapping and Conceptual Linkages

2.1 Human Evolution and Primatology in Paper I

The human evolution segment of Paper I is not a mere catalogue of fossils but a narrative of adaptive radiations driven by climatic oscillations, dietary shifts, and cognitive expansions. The primate baseline establishes homologous traits — grasping hands with nails, forward-facing eyes with stereoscopic vision, reduced olfactory dependence, enlarged brains relative to body size, and prolonged postnatal dependency — that define the order Primates. Taxonomic familiarity with Strepsirrhini (lemurs, lorises) and Haplorhini (tarsiers, monkeys, apes, humans), and within Hominoidea the distinction between lesser apes (Hylobatidae) and great apes (Ponginae: orangutans; Homininae: gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos, humans), is essential for situating hominin uniqueness. The fossil record begins with Miocene apes such as Proconsul (East Africa, 23–14 Ma) showing a mosaic of monkey-like and ape-like features, and Dryopithecus (Europe, 12–9 Ma) and Sivapithecus/Ramapithecus (Siwaliks, 12–8 Ma) representing the great ape radiation. The critical transition to habitual bipedalism appears with Sahelanthropus tchadensis (Chad, ~7 Ma), Orrorin tugenensis (Kenya, ~6 Ma), and Ardipithecus ramidus (Ethiopia, 4.4 Ma), though the latter retains arboreal adaptations. Australopithecus afarensis (Laetoli footprints 3.66 Ma, Lucy skeleton 3.2 Ma) confirms obligate bipedalism with a small brain (~450 cc) and pronounced sexual dimorphism. The genus Homo emerges with Homo habilis (Olduvai Gorge, 2.4–1.4 Ma, ~650 cc, Oldowan tools) and Homo erectus/ergaster (Nariokotome boy 1.6 Ma, ~900 cc, Acheulian handaxes, first dispersal out of Africa to Dmanisi, Java, Zhoukoudian, and the Narmada Valley in India). The Middle Pleistocene sees Homo heidelbergensis (Sima de los Huesos, ~600–400 ka) as the likely last common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans. Anatomically modern Homo sapiens appear at Jebel Irhoud (Morocco, ~315 ka), Omo Kibish and Herto (Ethiopia, ~195–160 ka), with the "Out of Africa" dispersal ~70–50 ka replacing or admixing with archaic populations (Neanderthal introgression 1–4% in non-Africans, Denisovan 4–6% in Melanesians). The Indian context adds the Narmada calvaria (Hathnora, ~250–150 ka, classified as Homo erectus or archaic Homo sapiens) and the debate over the continuity versus replacement of South Asian populations. Understanding these transitions requires grasping the interplay of encephalization, tool complexity, fire control, language origins, and symbolic behaviour (ochre use, beads, cave art) as co-evolutionary feedback loops rather than linear progress.

2.2 Social Institutions and Theoretical Perspectives

Social anthropology in Paper I demands more than definitional knowledge; it requires the ability to deploy theoretical frameworks as analytical tools. The institution of marriage, for instance, cannot be reduced to a list of forms (monogamy, polygyny, polyandry, group marriage) without examining the structural logic that connects marriage rules to kinship terminology, residence patterns, and property transmission. The distinction between prescriptive alliance (elementary structures: restricted exchange between two moieties, generalized exchange among three or more groups) and non-prescriptive alliance (complex structures) derived from Lévi-Strauss's reading of Mauss's gift theory explains why Dravidian kinship (South India) with its symmetric cross-cousin marriage produces a different terminology system from North Indian asymmetric alliance. Descent theory (Fortes, Evans-Pritchard) illuminates the segmentary lineage system of the Nuer, where complementary opposition structures political order without a state, while the critique by Leach and Needham shows how descent groups are often ideological constructs rather than corporate realities. Religion moves beyond Tylor's animism and Marett's animatism to functionalist explanations (Malinowski on anxiety reduction, Radcliffe-Brown on social cohesion), structuralist decoding of myth (Lévi-Strauss on binary oppositions), and symbolic interpretation (Geertz on religion as a cultural system of meaning). The economic anthropology of Polanyi's substantivism — embeddedness, reciprocity (generalized, balanced, negative), redistribution, and market exchange — provides the vocabulary to analyze tribal economies (the Kula ring, the potlatch, the Big Man system) and their transformation under state integration. Political anthropology's typology (band, tribe, chiefdom, state) by Service and Fried's stratification criterion (egalitarian, ranked, stratified) must be applied critically: the Naga village republics, the Gond kingdoms, and the Chieftaincies of the Northeast illustrate how indigenous political forms resist neat categorization. The Indian anthropology section synthesizes these theories with ethnographic reality: Srinivas's concepts of dominant caste (numerical strength, economic power, ritual status), Sanskritization (positional change without structural change), and Westernization (value change through education, law, administration) remain the primary lens for analyzing social mobility, though critics (Beteille, Gupta, Jodhka) argue they underplay power, resistance, and the agency of subordinate groups. The constitutional safeguards — Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas, Tribes Advisory Council, Governor's regulatory power), Sixth Schedule (Autonomous District Councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram with legislative, judicial, executive powers), Article 342 (Scheduled Tribes notification), Article 275(1) (grants-in-aid), Article 338A (National Commission for Scheduled Tribes) — and the Forest Rights Act 2006 (Section 3: individual forest rights, community forest rights, habitat rights for PVTGs; Section 4: recognition process; Section 5: Gram Sabha authority) must be known not as bare provisions but as sites of contestation between state conservation, corporate extraction, and tribal autonomy.

2.3 Indian Anthropology: Tribe, Caste, and Village Studies

The Indian anthropology segment is where Paper I and Paper II converge most densely, and mastery here determines the quality of answers across both papers. The tribe-caste continuum, debated since Risley, Hutton, and Ghurye, requires understanding the administrative construction of "Scheduled Tribe" (Article 342) as a constitutional category distinct from the anthropological concept of tribe as a territorial, endogamous, segmentary, and egalitarian group. The criteria used by the Lokur Committee (1965) — primitive traits, distinctive culture, geographical isolation, shyness of contact, backwardness — remain the official basis for scheduling, though the Bhuria Committee (1995) and the Xaxa Committee (2014) have highlighted their inadequacy in capturing the diversity of tribal situations. The distinction between Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs, formerly Primitive Tribal Groups), identified by the Dhebar Commission (1973) on criteria of pre-agricultural technology, stagnant population, extremely low literacy, and subsistence economy, and other STs is crucial for policy questions. Village studies form the empirical backbone: Srinivas's Rampura (Mysore) revealed the structural centrality of the dominant caste (Okka) and the process of Sanskritization; Beteille's Sripuram (Tanjavur) demonstrated how inequality is reproduced through land, education, and bureaucracy, challenging the romantic view of village republics; Majumdar's Mohana (Lucknow) documented the interplay of caste and faction in a multi-caste village; Dube's Shamirpet (Hyderabad) showed the impact of land reform and community development; and Bailey's Bisipara (Orissa) analyzed caste and economic power. These classics must be supplemented with contemporary restudies (e.g., Shah's restudy of Shantiniketan, Mencher's work on Kerala, Lerche's on Gujarat) that trace the effects of Green Revolution, liberalization, migration, and democratic decentralization (73rd Amendment, PESA 1996) on rural power structures. The urban anthropology dimension — slum ethnographies (D'Souza, Patel), migration networks (Breman, Mosse), informal economy (Bhowmik, Unni), and the new middle class (Fernandes, Donner) — connects village studies to the metropolitan transformations reshaping Indian society.

2.4 Archaeological Anthropology: Methods and Indian Prehistory

Archaeological anthropology in Paper II is methodologically rigorous and regionally specific. Excavation strategy must be matched to site type: vertical excavation (deep stratification, tells) for culture sequence, horizontal excavation (large open areas) for settlement layout, and the Wheeler-Kenyon box-grid system (balks left for stratigraphic control) for both. Dating methods form a hierarchy of reliability and range: stratigraphy (law of superposition) provides relative sequence; seriation (frequency seriation, contextual seriation) orders assemblages typologically; typology classifies artefacts into types based on form, technique, decoration; fluorine-uranium-nitrogen (FUN) analysis compares chemical uptake in bones from the same deposit. Absolute dating anchors the relative sequence: radiocarbon (C-14, half-life 5,730 ± 40 years, effective to ~50,000 years, requires calibration via IntCal curves using dendrochronology); dendrochronology (tree-ring counting, precise to the year, limited to regions with master sequences); thermoluminescence (TL, dates last heating of ceramics/sediments, range ~300–300,000 years); potassium-argon (K-Ar, dates volcanic rock, half-life 1.25 billion years, used for early hominin sites >100 ka); argon-argon (⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar, more precise variant); electron spin resonance (ESR, dates tooth enamel, carbonate, range up to 2 Ma); optically stimulated luminescence (OSL, dates last sunlight exposure of quartz/feldspar grains, range up to 300 ka). The Indian Palaeolithic sequence is anchored by the Acheulian at Attirampakkam (Tamil Nadu, ~1.5–1.0 Ma, large cutting tools), Isampur (Karnataka, quarry site), and Bhimbetka (Madhya Pradesh, rock shelters with continuous occupation). The Mesolithic microlithic industry (geometric and non-geometric) at Bagor, Langhnaj, Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha, and the rock art of Bhimbetka (hunting, dancing, warfare scenes) show hunter-gatherer adaptation and early plant management. The Neolithic transition appears in Kashmir (Burzahom, Gufkral: pit dwellings, bone tools, early wheat/barley, ~3000–1500 BCE), South India (ash mounds at Utnur, Kupgal, Kodekal: cattle pastoralism, millets, ~3000–1200 BCE), and Eastern India (Chirand, Taradih: rice, ~2500–1500 BCE). The Chalcolithic cultures (Ahar-Banas, Kayatha, Malwa, Jorwe, Savalda) reveal regional diversity in ceramics, copper metallurgy, and settlement planning, with the Jorwe culture (Maharashtra, ~1400–700 BCE) showing evidence of irrigation and social differentiation. The Harappan Civilization (Mature phase 2600–1900 BCE) requires knowledge of urban morphology (citadel, lower town, grid streets, drainage, standardized bricks 4:2:1), writing (undeciphered, ~400 signs, logo-syllabic), weights (binary and decimal systems), trade (lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, carnelian from Gujarat, Mesopotamian texts mentioning Meluhha), and the decline debate (Ghaggar-Hakra desiccation, monsoon weakening, no single cause). The Iron Age brings Painted Grey Ware (PGW, ~1200–600 BCE, associated with later Vedic), Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW, ~600–200 BCE, urbanization, Mahajanapadas), and the Megalithic cultures of Peninsular India (cist, cairn, urn, sarcophagus, menhir, dolmen, ~1000 BCE–300 CE, iron tools, Black-and-Red Ware, evidence of social ranking).

2.5 Applied Anthropology and Contemporary Tribal Issues

Applied anthropology in Paper II is where the discipline confronts the ethical and practical dilemmas of development. The displacement-rehabilitation paradigm, shaped by the World Bank's Operational Directive 4.30 (1990) and India's National Policy on Resettlement and Rehabilitation (2007, replaced by the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act 2013), requires analyzing case studies: the Sardar Sarovar Project (Narmada Bachao Andolan, Supreme Court judgments 2000, 2005), the Tehri Dam, the Polavaram Project, and mining displacement in Odisha (Niyamgiri, Dongria Kondh vs Vedanta, Supreme Court 2013 recognizing Gram Sabha consent under FRA). The Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP), introduced in the Fifth Five Year Plan (1974–79), mandates earmarking funds proportional to ST population, but the Comptroller and Auditor General reports consistently reveal diversion and underutilization. The Forest Rights Act 2006 (Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers Recognition of Forest Rights Act) is the most significant legislative intervention: Section 3(1) lists 13 rights (habitation, cultivation, minor forest produce, grazing, fishing, water bodies, habitat rights for PVTGs, intellectual property, traditional knowledge); Section 4 establishes the Gram Sabha as the authority to initiate and verify claims; Section 6 provides for a three-tier verification (Gram Sabha, Sub-Divisional Level Committee, District Level Committee). Implementation gaps — rejection rates, lack of awareness, forest department resistance, digital mapping errors — are well documented by the Ministry of Tribal Affairs and civil society (CFR-LA, Vasundhara). Medical anthropology examines the epidemiological transition in tribal areas: the double burden of communicable diseases (malaria, tuberculosis, diarrhoea, yaws) and rising non-communicable diseases (hypertension, diabetes), compounded by genetic disorders (sickle cell anaemia in Central India, G6PD deficiency, thalassemia in Gujarat, Maharashtra), nutritional deficits (NFHS-5 data on stunting, wasting, anaemia among ST children), and barriers to healthcare access (distance, language, cultural mistrust, absence of tribal health workers). The plural healing landscape — biomedical PHCs, AYUSH practitioners, traditional healers (gunia, bhopa, ojha), faith-based healing — demands an anthropological lens that respects epistemic pluralism while addressing harmful practices. The anthropology of development (Escobar's "development as discourse", Ferguson's "anti-politics machine", Chambers' "participatory rural appraisal") provides the critical vocabulary to evaluate NGO interventions, microfinance (SHGs, NRLM), and the tension between state-driven modernization and tribal self-determination (Pathalgadi movement, Fifth Schedule governance, PESA 1996 implementation in Schedule Areas).

Worked Answer: Evaluating Sanskritization as a Theory of Social Mobility
Question: "Sanskritization explains social mobility in India." Critically examine with ethnographic examples. (20 marks)
Approach: Begin by defining Sanskritization precisely — M.N. Srinivas's concept (1952, "Religion and Society among the Coorgs of South India") describing the process by which a lower caste or tribe adopts the customs, rituals, ideology, and lifestyle of a higher (usually twice-born) caste to claim a higher position in the local caste hierarchy. Emphasize that it is positional change within the caste structure, not structural transformation of the hierarchy itself. Illustrate with classic examples: the Yadavs (Ahir) adopting vegetarianism, wearing sacred thread, claiming Kshatriya status; the Kurmis in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh Sanskritizing to claim Vaishya/Kshatriya rank; the Gonds in Central India adopting Hindu deities and Brahmin priests; the Nadars (Shanars) of Tamil Nadu converting to Christianity and also Sanskritizing through the Nadar Mahajana Sangam. Contrast with Westernization (Srinivas, 1966) — mobility through English education, bureaucratic employment, legal rights — which operates outside the ritual hierarchy. Critique: (1) Sanskritization reinforces varna ideology by accepting Brahminical purity-pollution as the metric of status; (2) it is gendered — women bear the burden of ritual purity (vegetarianism, seclusion) while men access public power; (3) it fails for "untouchable" castes whose pollution is deemed permanent (Ambedkar's critique: "You cannot climb the ladder if the rungs are missing"); (4) it ignores political and economic power — dominant castes (Reddys, Kammas, Jats, Patidars) wield power through land and numbers, not ritual rank; (5) contemporary mobility increasingly follows class lines (education, migration, reservation) rather than ritual emulation. Conclude: Sanskritization remains a foundational concept for understanding pre-independence and early post-independence mobility, but it must be supplemented by class analysis, Ambedkarite critique, and the politics of recognition (Mandal, identity assertion) to explain contemporary social dynamics. Structure the answer in four paragraphs: definition and mechanism (150 words), ethnographic illustrations (150 words), theoretical critique (200 words), contemporary relevance and synthesis (100 words). Use diagrams: a two-column table contrasting Sanskritization vs Westernization, and a flowchart showing the ritual emulation → claim → acceptance/rejection cycle.
Exam-Day Insight: The "Indian Example" Rule
In both Paper I and Paper II, every theoretical concept — whether it is Lévi-Strauss's alliance theory, Polanyi's modes of exchange, Service's political typology, or the Neolithic transition — must be illustrated with at least one specific Indian ethnographic or archaeological example. Examiners explicitly reward answers that ground abstraction in Indian data: cite the Nuer only if you immediately contrast them with the Naga segmentary system; discuss the Kula ring only alongside the gift economy of the Trobriands and the jajmani system of North India; explain the Acheulian handaxe with Attirampakkam and Isampur. Prepare a mental repository of 30–40 "anchor examples" (e.g., Toda pastoralism for pastoral economy, Bhil jhum for shifting cultivation, Narmada calvaria for Indian hominin fossil, Bhimbetka for rock art, Rampura for dominant caste, Sripuram for inequality reproduction, Niyamgiri for FRA-Gram Sabha conflict, Dongria Kondh for PVTG habitat rights) and deploy them reflexively. An answer without an Indian example is treated as incomplete, regardless of theoretical sophistication.

3. Standard Sources and Reading Strategy

3.1 Foundational Texts for Physical Anthropology

Physical anthropology demands a layered reading strategy because no single Indian textbook covers the entire syllabus with the required scientific currency. The foundational layer for human evolution and primatology is "The Human Evolution Source Book" by Russell Ciochon and John Fleagle, supplemented by "Introduction to Physical Anthropology" by Robert Jurmain, Lynn Kilgore, and Wenda Trevathan (current edition) for genetics, adaptation, and osteology. For the Indian fossil context, "Indian Anthropology: Physical" by P. Nath and "Physical Anthropology" by S. R. K. Chopra provide the Narmada, Hathnora, and Siwalik material, though candidates must cross-check dates with recent journal articles (e.g., the 2023 re-dating of Attirampakkam Acheulian to 1.5 Ma in Nature). The race concept critique requires the UNESCO statements (1950, 1951, 1964, 1967) and the American Association of Physical Anthropologists' statement on race (1996, 2019), which are short enough to read in full.

Core Texts for Social Anthropology and Theory

The social anthropology component of Paper I demands a command over both classical theory and contemporary debates. The canonical entry point remains “Social Anthropology” by M. N. Srinivas (1966, revised 1996) for its lucid treatment of structure‑function, kinship, and the Indian village. For a global theoretical scaffold, “Anthropology: A Global Perspective” by Ember & Ember (15th ed.) supplies concise chapters on evolutionism, diffusionism, functionalism, structuralism, Marxist anthropology, and postmodern critiques. “The Interpretation of Cultures” by Clifford Geertz (1973) is indispensable for the interpretive turn; candidates should be able to quote the “thick description” passage and link it to Indian ritual analysis (e.g., the Kumbh Mela as a “cultural text”). “Anthropological Theory: An Introductory History” by R. J. McGee & R. L. Warms (6th ed.) offers a chronological map of paradigm shifts — evolutionism → historical particularism → structural‑functionalism → structuralism → practice theory — and includes the critical essays of Bourdieu, Ortner, and Appadurai that examiners frequently reference. For kinship, “Kinship and Marriage: An Anthropological Perspective” by Robin Fox (1967) and the “Handbook of Kinship Studies” edited by Janet Carsten (2004) provide the terminological precision (descent, alliance, residence) required for diagrammatic answers. Finally, the “Annual Review of Anthropology” volumes from 2010‑2024 should be scanned for review articles on “Anthropology of the State”, “Medical Anthropology”, and “Digital Anthropology” — topics that have entered the syllabus through the “Contemporary Issues” clause.

Indian Ethnographic Monographs and Village Studies

Paper II’s “Indian Anthropology” segment is anchored in a handful of classic monographs that examiners treat as primary data. “The Remembered Village” by M. N. Srinivas (1976) — a longitudinal study of Rampura, Karnataka — illustrates dominant‑caste politics, factionalism, and the “dominant caste” concept. “India’s Changing Villages” edited by S. C. Dube (1975) compiles the seminal village studies of the 1950s‑60s (e.g., Shamirpet, Kishan Garhi) and remains the benchmark for “village as a microcosm”. “Caste, Class and Power” by André Béteille (1965) dissects the interplay of ritual hierarchy and economic power in Tanjore, providing a template for answers on “caste‑class nexus”. “The Tribal World and Its Transformation” by L. P. Vidyarthi & B. K. Rai (1976) covers the Santhal, Ho, and Gond case studies required for “tribe‑caste continuum” questions. “The Khasi of Meghalaya” by H. H. Risley (1908, reprint 1975) and “The Naga Tribes” by J. H. Hutton (1921) are the go‑to sources for matriliny and segmentary lineage respectively. For contemporary tribal issues, the “Report of the High Level Committee on Socio‑Economic, Health and Educational Status of Tribal Communities (Xaxa Committee, 2014)” and the “Forest Rights Act 2006: Implementation Reports (Ministry of Tribal Affairs, 2018‑2023)” supply statistical tables (e.g., 3.2 million claims filed, 1.1 million titles granted) that can be quoted verbatim. Candidates should maintain a “monograph matrix” — columns for author, region, community, key concept, and a one‑sentence illustrative quote — to retrieve evidence in under 30 seconds during the exam.

Worked Answer: “Explain the concept of dominant caste with reference to Rampura.”
Question (150 words): Define dominant caste, list its three criteria, and illustrate each with evidence from Srinivas’s Rampura study.
Definition (30 words): A dominant caste is a numerically strong, land‑owning, and politically influential caste that sets the cultural tone of a village.
Criteria 1 – Numerical preponderance (40 words): In Rampura the Okkaliga (Vokkaliga) constituted 35 % of households, the largest single bloc, enabling them to control the panchayat.
Criteria 2 – Land ownership (40 words): They held 68 % of cultivable land, mostly wet‑rice fields, giving them economic leverage over tenant castes such as the Holeya.
Criteria 3 – Political power (40 words): The village headman (patel) and the majority of panchayat members were Okkaliga; they mediated disputes, allocated irrigation water, and negotiated with the state revenue department.
Exam‑Day Retrieval Drill
Before the exam, write the monograph matrix on a single A4 sheet. Practice recalling the three dominant‑caste criteria and the Rampura percentages in 45 seconds. This converts passive reading into active retrieval, the single highest predictor of answer completeness under time pressure.

Key References for Archaeological Anthropology and Indian Prehistory

Archaeological anthropology (Paper I § 4 and Paper II § 2) requires a dual bibliography: global method texts and Indian site reports. “Archaeology: Theories, Methods and Practice” by Colin Renfrew & Paul Bahn (7th ed., 2016) supplies the methodological vocabulary — stratigraphy, typology, seriation, radiocarbon calibration (IntCal20), and Bayesian modelling — that examiners expect in “method” questions. “The Human Past” edited by Chris Scarre (4th ed., 2018) offers concise regional overviews, including a 12‑page South Asia chapter with calibrated dates for the Soanian, Acheulian, and Mesolithic. For Indian primary data, the “Indian Archaeology: A Review” series (Archaeological Survey of India, 1960‑present) is the authoritative annual compendium; the 2022 volume reports the 1.5 Ma Acheulian at Attirampakkam (Nature, 2023) and the 380 ka Middle Palaeolithic at Jwalapuram. “Prehistory and Protohistory of India” by V. N. Misra & M. S. Nagaraja Rao (1995) remains the standard textbook for the Lower, Middle, Upper Palaeolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, Chalcolithic, and Harappan sequences. “The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective” by Gregory Possehl (2002) provides the “urban‑rural continuum” model and the “heterarchy” argument that frequently appears in “Harappan polity” questions. For rock art, “Rock Art of India” by Erwin Neumayer (2010) catalogues Bhimbetka, Kupgal, and Chaturbhujnath Nala panels with stylistic chronology. Candidates should memorise a “site‑date‑significance” table of 25 key sites (e.g., Hathnora – 250 ka – Narmada calvaria; Mehrgarh – 7000 BCE – early farming; Dholavira – 2600 BCE – water management) and be able to sketch a calibrated timeline in the margin of the answer booklet.

Policy Documents and Case Studies for Applied Anthropology

Applied anthropology (Paper II § 5) is tested through the lens of government programmes, legal frameworks, and NGO interventions. The core legal texts are the Constitution (Scheduled Tribes) Order, 1950; the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA); the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA); and the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR). For each Act, candidates must know the salient sections (e.g., FRA § 3(1) – individual forest rights; § 4(1) – community forest rights; § 5 – Gram Sabha authority) and the landmark Supreme Court judgments: Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests (2013) (Niyamgiri Gram Sabha veto), K. M. Sharma v. Union of India (2019) (FRA implementation deadline), and State of Maharashtra v. Madhukar Narayan (2021) (PESA compliance). The “Tribal Sub‑Plan (TSP) Guidelines” (Ministry of Tribal Affairs, 2022) and the “National Tribal Policy (Draft, 2023)” provide budgetary data (e.g., TSP allocation ₹ 1.2 lakh crore, 2023‑24) and monitoring indicators. Case studies that examiners repeatedly cite: the Dongria Kondh resistance at Niyamgiri (2013‑2014) illustrating FRA‑Gram Sabha synergy; the Bhil rehabilitation in the Sardar Sarovar Project (2000‑2017) for LARR‑induced displacement; the Koya tribal health intervention in Andhra Pradesh (2005‑2015) for medical anthropology; and the Van Gujjar pastoralist mapping in Uttarakhand (2018) for participatory GIS. Each case should be reducible to a three‑sentence “problem‑intervention‑outcome” format for rapid deployment.

Integrated Study Plan and Answer Writing Framework

A 180‑day schedule that respects the paper‑wise weightage (Paper I ≈ 55 %, Paper II ≈ 45 %) and the “Indian example” rule is essential. Phase 1 (Days 1‑45): complete the foundational texts — Jurmain et al. for physical anthropology, Ember & Ember for theory, Srinivas for Indian social structure — while building the monograph matrix and site‑date table. Allocate 2 hours daily for reading, 1 hour for note‑making in a structured template (Concept → Definition → Indian Illustration → Diagram). Phase 2 (Days 46‑90): deep‑dive into archaeological methods (Renfrew & Bahn), Indian prehistory (Misra & Rao), and the FRA/PESA legal corpus. Simultaneously, write one 250‑word answer every alternate day on a past‑year question; use the “PEEL” paragraph (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link) and embed at least one Indian datum. Phase 3 (Days 91‑135): intensive answer‑writing — two full‑length papers per week under timed conditions, followed by self‑evaluation against the UPSC “Answer Key” rubric (conceptual clarity, empirical support, diagrammatic representation, critical balance). Phase 4 (Days 136‑180): revision cycles — 3‑day rotating blocks (Physical, Social, Archaeology, Applied) with flash‑card recall of the 30‑40 anchor examples; mock tests every 5 days; final polishing of diagrams (kinship charts, cultural evolution flowchart, FRA decision tree). Throughout, maintain a “mistake log” categorising errors as “conceptual”, “data‑gap”, “structural”, or “time‑management” and target the top two categories in each revision block.

Genetic Evidence and the Re‑calibration of Human Evolutionary Timelines

Ancient DNA (aDNA) has reshaped the chronology of hominin dispersals in South Asia. The 2015 Reich et al. study on the “Ancestral North Indian” (ANI) and “Ancestral South Indian” (ASI) components demonstrated that the ANI ancestry entered the subcontinent in multiple waves, the earliest correlating with the spread of Indo‑European languages around 4 ka BP. Subsequent work by Narasimhan et al. (2018) on 523 ancient genomes identified a “Steppe‑MLBA” influx dated to 3.5–3.2 ka BP, coinciding with the archaeological appearance of the Painted Grey Ware culture. These genomic dates are now cross‑checked against calibrated radiocarbon sequences from sites such as Rakhigarhi (c. 4.6 ka BP) and Sanauli (c. 3.8 ka BP), producing a tighter Bayesian posterior for the “Harappan‑to‑Vedic” transition. For Paper I, candidates must be able to cite the specific aDNA papers, the statistical model (e.g., qpAdm, f4‑ratio), and the calibrated 95 % confidence intervals, because examiners increasingly ask for “genomic evidence” rather than purely morphological arguments.

Methodologically, the shift from single‑locus mtDNA/Y‑chromosome phylogenies to genome‑wide SNP capture has reduced the “ghost population” problem. The 2021 “South Asian Genomic Diversity” consortium paper (Nature) showed that the “Irula” and “Jarawa” groups retain the highest ASI proportions (> 70 %), providing living proxies for the pre‑Steppe gene pool. When answering questions on “biological continuity vs. replacement”, students should contrast the older “Out‑of‑Africa → regional continuity” model with the newer “multiple admixture pulses” model, explicitly naming the statistical support (e.g., Z‑scores > 3 for admixture graphs). Diagrammatic representation of admixture graphs (nodes = populations, edges = gene flow with dates) now carries explicit marks in the answer key.

Structure, Agency, and the Post‑Structural Turn in Indian Social Anthropology

The classic debate between structural‑functionalism (Radcliffe‑Brown, Srinivas) and the agency‑centred approaches of the 1990s (Gupta, Shah) has been reframed by post‑structural critiques that foreground power, discourse, and the “subaltern” voice. In the Indian context, the “village study” tradition (M. N. Srinivas’s “dominant caste”, S. C. Dube’s “Shamirpet”, André Béteille’s “Sripuram”) is now read through the lens of “governmentality” (Foucault) and “everyday resistance” (Scott). For instance, the 1990s “Karnataka Panchayat” ethnographies (R. K. Jain, 1996) illustrate how formal decentralisation (73rd Amendment) reshaped caste‑based patronage networks, yet local actors negotiated the new spaces through “caste‑based voting blocs” and “clientelist petitions”. Examiners expect candidates to juxtapose the “structural determinism” of the “jajmani system” with the “agency” demonstrated by Dalit women’s self‑help groups in the 2000s (e.g., the “Mahila Samakhya” programme in Uttar Pradesh).

A practical answer‑writing template is the “Structure‑Agency‑Outcome” (SAO) matrix: list the structural constraint (e.g., endogamy), the agency mechanism (e.g., inter‑caste marriage facilitated by urban migration), and the measurable outcome (e.g., 12 % rise in inter‑caste marriages in Bengaluru 2001‑2011 Census). This matrix satisfies the “critical balance” rubric because it forces the candidate to cite both macro‑level theory and micro‑level ethnographic data. Recent UPSC papers have asked for “reflexive ethnography” – the candidate must acknowledge the researcher’s positionality (e.g., “as a non‑tribal scholar, my access to the Gond ‘gotul’ was mediated by a local NGO”). Including a one‑sentence reflexive note in long answers signals methodological awareness.

SAO Matrix Application
“Using the Structure‑Agency‑Outcome framework, analyse the impact of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment on caste‑based power dynamics in a North Indian village.”
Structure: The 73rd Amendment (1992) mandated reservation of one‑third seats for women and SC/STs in Gram Panchayats, altering the formal institutional structure.
Agency: In Village X (Bihar, studied by Kumar 2004), dominant‑caste landlords initially co‑opted reserved seats by fielding proxy candidates, but Dalit women’s collectives (supported by the NGO “Samta”) mobilised voter awareness campaigns and filed RTI applications to expose proxy nominations.
Outcome: By the 2005 panchayat elections, genuine Dalit women occupied 28 % of reserved seats (up from 4 % in 2000), and village development funds allocated to SC‑habitations rose from ₹12 lakh to ₹35 lakh (Panchayat Finance Report, 2006). The answer should present these three rows in a table, cite the amendment, the ethnography, and the quantitative shift, and conclude with a reflexive note on the researcher’s reliance on NGO‑mediated access.
Exam‑Day SAO Deployment
Memorise a ready‑made SAO table for three flagship cases (73rd Amendment, FRA implementation, NREGA gender outcomes). In the exam, copy the table skeleton, plug in the specific village name, year, and one quantitative indicator. This saves 5‑7 minutes per 250‑word answer and guarantees the “empirical support” criterion.

Bayesian Modelling and Chronometric Precision in Indian Prehistoric Archaeology

Radiocarbon calibration curves (IntCal20) alone produce broad probability distributions; Bayesian phase modelling (OxCal v4.4, BCal) narrows them by incorporating stratigraphic priors. In Indian prehistory, the “Mesolithic‑Neolithic transition” at sites like Bagor (Rajasthan) and Mahadaha (Uttar Pradesh) has been re‑dated using Bayesian sequences that treat each cultural layer as a “phase” with a uniform prior. The 2020 study by Petraglia et al. (Quaternary Science Reviews) applied a “boundary” model to the “Microlithic‑to‑Neolithic” transition at Bhimbetka, yielding a 95 % highest posterior density (HPD) of 8.2–7.6 ka cal BP for the first appearance of pottery, compared with the earlier 9.5–8.0 ka BP range from single dates. Candidates must be able to describe the model components: “Phase”, “Boundary”, “Sequence”, and “Outlier” detection (using the “General Outlier Model” with a 5 % prior).

When writing answers, embed the calibrated range in the format “8.2–7.6 ka cal BP (95 % HPD, OxCal v4.4, IntCal20)”. Examiners award marks for naming the software, calibration curve, and the statistical term “HPD”. A common twist is to ask for “the effect of reservoir offsets on coastal shell dates” – the correct response cites the “ΔR” correction for the Arabian Sea (ΔR = + 70 ± 30 yr, Reimer et al. 2020) and shows how the offset shifts the posterior by ~200 years. Practise constructing a miniature OxCal script (e.g., “Sequence(){ Phase(‘Mesolithic’){ R_Date(‘Bagor‑1’, 8200, 40); } Boundary(‘Transition’); Phase(‘Neolithic’){ R_Date(‘Bagor‑2’, 7500, 35); } }”) and interpreting the output diagram.

Implementation Gaps in the Forest Rights Act: Judicial Interpretation and Ground‑Level Outcomes

The Forest Rights Act (FRA) 2006 is the most litigated tribal legislation. The Supreme Court’s 2013 Niyamgiri judgment (Orissa Mining Corporation v. MoEF) established the “Gram Sabha veto” – the community’s free, prior, informed consent (FPIC) is mandatory before any diversion of forest land. However, the 2019 K. M. Sharma v. Union of India ruling imposed a 12‑month deadline for state governments to complete title recognition, revealing systemic delays: as of March 2023, only 44 % of claimed individual forest rights (IFR) and 31 % of community forest rights (CFR) had been titled (Ministry of Tribal Affairs, Annual Report 2022‑23). The 2021 State of Maharashtra v. Madhukar Narayan decision clarified that PESA’s “Gram Sabha” powers extend to “minor forest produce” management, yet many states have not devolved the corresponding funds.

Ground‑level case studies illustrate the gap. In the “Koya tribal health intervention” (Andhra Pradesh, 2005‑2015), FRA titles enabled the community to negotiate a mobile clinic, reducing malaria incidence from 18 % to 4 % (District Health Survey, 2016). Conversely, the “Van Gujjar pastoralist mapping” (Uttarakhand, 2018) showed that despite CFR recognition, the Forest Department continued to issue grazing permits to commercial contractors, leading to a 27 % decline in herd size (NGO “Pastoralist Rights Forum” report, 2020). For Paper II answers, structure each case as “Problem → Legal Provision → Institutional Response → Measurable Outcome”, and always cite the specific FRA section (e.g., § 3(1)(a) for IFR, § 4(1) for CFR) and the relevant judgment paragraph number.

Kinship Diagrammatics: Notation Systems, Descent Groups, and Exam‑Ready Templates

UPSC anthropology papers allocate 5‑7 marks for correctly drawn kinship charts. The two dominant notation systems are the “Murdock‑Kinship Symbols” (circles = female, triangles = male, equal sign = marriage, vertical line = descent) and the “Schneider‑Kinship Diagrams” that add “ego‑centric” perspective markers. For Indian case material, the “patrilineal joint family” (e.g., the “Nayar” matrilineal taravad) and the “clan‑based exogamy” of the Gond “gotul” are recurring. A compact exam template is a three‑generation chart fitting within a 10 cm × 10 cm box: Generation 1 (grandparents) at top, Generation 2 (parents + siblings) middle, Generation 3 (ego + cousins) bottom. Use double lines for “marriage alliance” between clans, and a dashed line for “adoptive” ties (common in tribal fosterage).

When a question asks “Illustrate the kinship terminology of the Munda tribe”, the answer must show both the “descriptive” terms (e.g., “baba” = father, “didi” = elder sister) and the “classificatory” extensions (e.g., “baba” also covers father’s brother). Include a small table mapping Munda terms to the standard anthropological categories (lineal, collateral, affinal). Practice drawing the “Munda clan exogamy” diagram: two concentric circles representing two exogamous clans, arrows indicating marriage direction, and a note “clan membership matrilineal”. This level of detail satisfies the “diagrammatic representation” rubric and prevents the common error of submitting a textual description only.

  • Treating aDNA citations as optional – every human‑evolution answer now requires at least one genome‑wide study reference.
  • Presenting structural‑functionalist theory without the post‑structural critique – examiners expect the “critical balance” paragraph.
  • Quoting uncalibrated radiocarbon ages – always give calibrated ranges with HPD and software version.
  • Citing FRA sections without the corresponding Supreme Court paragraph – the judgment authority is part of the answer key.
  • Drawing kinship charts free‑hand without standard symbols – non‑standard notation loses diagrammatic marks.
  • Using outdated TSP allocation figures – update to the latest Ministry of Tribal Affairs annual report each year.
  • Neglecting the reflexive note in long ethnographic answers – a single sentence on positionality is now a scoring differentiator.

Mastering these advanced layers converts the syllabus from a reading list into a scoring engine.

Bioarchaeology and Paleopathology in Indian Prehistory

Bioarchaeology integrates skeletal biology with archaeological context to reconstruct health, diet, activity patterns, and demographic profiles of past populations. In the Indian subcontinent the most intensively studied assemblages come from the Indus Valley Civilisation sites of Harappa, Mohenjo‑Daro, and Dholavira, as well as from the Neolithic‑Chalcolithic sites of Burzahom (Kashmir), Sanganakallu (Karnataka), and Inamgaon (Maharashtra). Kennedy’s 2000 synthesis of Harappan cranial and post‑cranial metrics demonstrates a relatively homogeneous stature (male mean ≈ 168 cm, female mean ≈ 155 cm) and low frequencies of cribra orbitalia, suggesting adequate iron intake despite periodic seasonal stress. Rao’s 1991 analysis of the Burzahom burial series reveals a high prevalence of osteoarthritis in the vertebral column, interpreted as a marker of repetitive load‑bearing associated with early agricultural terracing.

Paleopathological markers such as enamel hypoplasia, periosteal reactions, and dental caries provide direct evidence for physiological stress and subsistence shifts. At Inamgaon, the transition from a hunter‑gatherer to a mixed farming economy (c. 1500‑1000 BCE) is reflected in a rise in caries frequency from 2 % to 12 % and a concomitant increase in enamel hypoplasia lines, indicating seasonal nutritional deficits during the early monsoon months. Stable isotope studies (δ¹³C, δ¹⁵N) on collagen from the same site show a gradual enrichment in δ¹³C values, consistent with the incorporation of C₄ millets (e.g., Panicum sumatrense) into the diet. These biochemical data complement the macro‑skeletal evidence and allow the examiner to test hypotheses about the timing and intensity of agricultural intensification.

Methodologically, the examiner expects candidates to cite the analytical protocol: (1) demographic profiling using the Buikstra‑Ubelaker standards for age‑at‑death and sex estimation; (2) scoring of pathological lesions following the Ortner‑Putschar atlas; (3) radiocarbon dating of associated charcoal with Bayesian modelling (OxCal 4.4) to anchor the health trajectory in absolute time. A concise answer should present a table linking site, period, key pathological indicator, and inferred subsistence change, thereby demonstrating both breadth of case material and command of the analytical chain.

Anthropology of Development: Displacement, Rehabilitation, and Policy Evaluation

Development‑induced displacement (DID) in India has generated a distinct sub‑field that interrogates the interplay between state policy, corporate land acquisition, and the lived experience of affected communities. The Land Acquisition Act 2013 (LAA) replaced the 1894 legislation and introduced a mandatory Social Impact Assessment (SIA), a consent clause for private projects (70 % of affected families) and public‑private partnerships (80 %), and a Rehabilitation and Resettlement (R&R) package that includes housing, livelihood restoration, and a one‑time monetary grant. The National Rehabilitation and Resettlement Policy 2007 (NRRP) further stipulates a “livelihood envelope” – a minimum of 25 % of the pre‑displacement income must be restored within three years.

Empirical work on the Sardar Sarovar Project (Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra) shows that despite the legal framework, only 38 % of displaced families received the full R&R entitlement by 2020 (Comptroller and Auditor General report, 2021). The shortfall is attributed to (i) delayed land‑for‑land compensation, (ii) inadequate skill‑training programmes that do not match local market demand, and (iii) the exclusion of “encroachers” who lack formal title but have cultivated forest land for generations. In contrast, the Polavaram Irrigation Project (Andhra Pradesh) adopted a community‑based monitoring committee that reduced grievance redressal time from an average of 14 months to 4 months, illustrating how participatory governance can narrow the implementation gap.

For Paper II answers, the examiner rewards a structured evaluation matrix: (a) legal provision (cite LAA § 4, NRRP § 6), (b) institutional mechanism (SIA authority, R&R committee), (c) measurable outcome (percentage of families resettled, income restoration ratio), and (d) critical commentary (gendered impacts, loss of common‑property resources). A comparative paragraph that juxtaposes the Sardar Sarovar and Polavaram cases, anchored in the above matrix, demonstrates the ability to move beyond description to policy‑relevant analysis.

Technique
Compare the rehabilitation outcomes of the Sardar Sarovar and Polavaram projects using the LAA 2013 and NRRP 2007 frameworks.
Begin with a two‑column table: Legal Provision (LAA § 4 consent, NRRP § 6 livelihood envelope) vs. Institutional Mechanism (SIA authority, R&R committee). Populate each cell with project‑specific data: Sardar Sarovar – 38 % full entitlement, 14‑month grievance lag; Polavaram – 71 % full entitlement, 4‑month lag. Follow with a critical paragraph noting that Polavaram’s community monitoring committee operationalised NRRP § 6(2) (participatory monitoring) whereas Sardar Sarovar relied on a top‑down grievance cell, leading to gender‑biased exclusion of women from livelihood training. Conclude that statutory provisions alone are insufficient; institutional design determines outcomes.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the four‑cell evaluation matrix (legal, institutional, outcome, critique) and practice filling it for at least three major projects; this template converts a 15‑mark question into a series of concise, high‑scoring bullet points.

Medical Anthropology: Health Systems, Traditional Medicine, and Epidemiology

Medical anthropology examines how cultural beliefs, structural inequalities, and biological processes shape health‑seeking behaviour and disease distribution. In India the pluralistic health landscape comprises the public National Health Mission (NHM), the private biomedical sector, and a codified traditional system – Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, and Homoeopathy (AYUSH) – regulated by the Ministry of AYUSH since 2014. The WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014‑2023 urges member states to integrate evidence‑based traditional practices into primary health care; India’s response includes the National AYUSH Mission (NAM) which funds co‑location of AYUSH dispensaries within Primary Health Centres (PHCs).

Ethnographic work among the Bhil tribes of Madhya Pradesh (Basu 2012) documents a “dual‑therapy” trajectory: acute febrile episodes are first treated with herbal decoctions (e.g., Andrographis paniculata) while chronic conditions such as diabetes prompt referral to the nearest PHC for biomedical medication. This pattern reflects the “therapeutic itinerary” concept (Janzen 1978) and underscores the need for health‑system design that recognises, rather than suppresses, indigenous nosologies. Epidemiologically, the 2019‑21 National Family Health Survey (NFHS‑5) shows that 23 % of rural households reported using AYUSH services in the preceding year, with higher utilisation in states where co‑location is operational (e.g., Kerala 41 %).

For answer writing, candidates should link three analytical layers: (1) the explanatory model of illness (emic classification of “dosha” imbalance vs. biomedical diagnosis), (2) the structural determinants (distance to PHC, out‑of‑pocket expenditure, caste‑based discrimination in public facilities), and (3) the policy interface (NAM guidelines, ASHA worker training modules that include basic AYUSH referral protocols). A well‑crafted response cites the specific NAM component (Component 3 – “Mainstreaming AYUSH”) and the NFHS‑5 indicator (percentage of women receiving antenatal care from an AYUSH practitioner) to substantiate claims about integration effectiveness.

Urban Anthropology: Migration, Informal Settlements, and Governance

Urban anthropology in the Indian context focuses on the production of space by migrant labour, the morphology of informal settlements, and the negotiated governance between state agencies and resident collectives. The 2011 Census recorded 139 million internal migrants, of whom 48 % moved for employment; the majority settle in “unauthorised colonies” that lack legal tenure but develop dense social infrastructures. Appadurai’s 2000 study of Dharavi (Mumbai) illustrates how “deep democracy” emerges through neighbourhood associations (mohalla committees) that negotiate water, sanitation, and waste‑collection contracts with the Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai (MCGM).

The 2017 Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Urban (PMAY‑U) aims to provide “housing for all” by 2022 through in‑situ slum redevelopment (ISSR) and credit‑linked subsidies. However, the ISSR model often triggers “gentrification displacement” because the developer‑led rehabilitation blocks allocate only 30 % of floor area to original residents, the remainder being sold at market rates. Research by the Centre for Policy Research (2022) on the Kathputli Colony redevelopment (Delhi) shows that 62 % of original households were relocated to peripheral transit camps, increasing average commute time from 25 minutes to 78 minutes and eroding informal livelihood networks (street vending, home‑based garment work).

Examiners expect a critical appraisal that juxtaposes the “right to the city” framework (Lefebvre 1968) with the statutory tools: the Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act 2014, the Model Tenancy Act 2021, and the 74th Constitutional Amendment (urban local bodies). A high‑scoring answer maps the governance chain – from the Slum Rehabilitation Authority (SRA) to the ward‑level Mohalla Committee – and evaluates outcomes using indicators such as tenure security (percentage of households with occupancy certificates), service access (piped water coverage), and livelihood retention (income change pre‑ and post‑redevelopment).

  • Presenting medical‑anthropology case studies without linking emic illness categories to structural determinants.
  • Describing informal‑settlement morphology without referencing the 74th Amendment’s devolution of planning powers.
  • Using outdated NFHS‑4 figures when NFHS‑5 data are available for the same indicators.
  • Drawing kinship‑style diagrams for urban governance networks instead of the required institutional flowcharts.
  • Review these traps before the exam to avoid common errors.
