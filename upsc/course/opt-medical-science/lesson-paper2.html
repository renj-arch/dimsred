1. Pharmacology: Core Principles and Clinical Applications

1.1 General Pharmacology: Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacokinetics describes the journey of a drug through the body, encompassing absorption from the site of administration, distribution to tissues, metabolic transformation primarily in the liver, and renal or biliary excretion; each phase determines the concentration‑time profile that ultimately drives therapeutic effect. Pharmacodynamics, in contrast, focuses on the biochemical and physiological actions of the drug at its molecular target, quantifying the relationship between drug concentration at the receptor and the magnitude of response through dose‑response curves, receptor occupancy theory, and concepts such as efficacy, potency, and therapeutic index. Understanding the interplay between these two domains allows the clinician to predict onset, duration, and intensity of action, to adjust dosing in renal or hepatic impairment, and to anticipate drug‑drug interactions that alter clearance or receptor sensitivity.

1.2 Autonomic Pharmacology: Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Modulation

The autonomic nervous system is a frequent therapeutic target because it regulates cardiovascular tone, bronchial calibre, gastrointestinal motility, and glandular secretion. Sympathomimetic agents such as adrenaline, noradrenaline, and selective β2‑agonists activate adrenergic receptors, producing bronchodilation, increased heart rate, and vasoconstriction depending on receptor subtype; conversely, sympatholytics including α‑blockers, β‑blockers, and centrally acting agents like clonidine blunt these responses and are mainstays in hypertension, angina, and heart failure. Parasympathomimetics (e.g., pilocarpine, neostigmine) enhance cholinergic transmission, finding use in glaucoma, postoperative ileus, and myasthenia gravis, while anticholinergics such as atropine, ipratropium, and glycopyrrolate block muscarinic receptors to reduce secretions, relieve bronchospasm, and treat bradyarrhythmias. The clinical selection among these agents hinges on receptor selectivity, organ distribution, and the side‑effect profile that follows from widespread autonomic innervation.

1.3 Central Nervous System Pharmacology: Anaesthetics, Sedatives, and Psychotropics

General anaesthetics achieve reversible loss of consciousness through modulation of GABA‑A receptors (volatile agents, propofol, barbiturates) or NMDA receptor antagonism (ketamine), and their potency correlates with lipid solubility as described by the Meyer‑Overton rule. Sedative‑hypnotics such as benzodiazepines enhance GABAergic inhibition, providing anxiolysis, muscle relaxant, and anticonvulsant properties, yet carry risks of respiratory depression and dependence. Antipsychotics block dopaminergic D2 receptors (typical) or combine serotonin‑dopamine antagonism (atypical) to mitigate positive symptoms of schizophrenia while metabolic side‑effects guide drug choice. Antidepressants elevate synaptic monoamines via selective serotonin reuptake inhibition, serotonin‑noradrenaline reuptake inhibition, or irreversible monoamine oxidase inhibition, with therapeutic latency reflecting downstream neuroplastic changes. Opioid analgesics activate µ, κ, and δ receptors to produce profound analgesia, euphoria, and respiratory depression, necessitating careful titration and monitoring for misuse.

1.4 Cardiovascular Pharmacology: Antihypertensives, Antianginals, and Anticoagulants

Hypertension management follows a stepwise algorithm that begins with lifestyle modification and proceeds through thiazide diuretics, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin‑II receptor blockers, calcium‑channel blockers, and β‑blockers, each class lowering blood pressure by distinct haemodynamic mechanisms such as volume reduction, renin‑angiotensin blockade, vascular smooth‑muscle relaxation, or reduced cardiac output. Antianginal therapy exploits the balance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand: nitrates venodilate to lower preload, β‑blockers diminish heart rate and contractility, and calcium‑channel blockers dilate coronary arteries while reducing afterload. Anticoagulation targets the coagulation cascade; vitamin‑K antagonists inhibit synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, and X, whereas direct oral anticoagulants selectively inhibit thrombin or factor Xa, offering predictable pharmacokinetics without routine monitoring. The choice among these agents depends on comorbid conditions, renal function, bleeding risk, and guideline‑directed indications such as atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, or mechanical heart valves.

1.5 Antimicrobial Pharmacology: Mechanisms, Resistance, and National Guidelines

Antibacterial agents are classified by their target: β‑lactams inhibit cell‑wall synthesis, aminoglycosides and tetracyclines bind the 30S ribosomal subunit, macrolides and chloramphenicol bind the 50S subunit, fluoroquinolones inhibit DNA gyrase, and sulfonamides block folate synthesis. Resistance emerges through enzymatic inactivation (β‑lactamases), target modification (altered penicillin‑binding proteins), efflux pumps, and reduced permeability, often encoded on mobile genetic elements that spread rapidly. Antitubercular therapy combines isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for an intensive phase followed by a continuation phase, adhering to directly observed treatment short‑course (DOTS) to prevent multidrug‑resistant tuberculosis. Antimalarial regimens now rely on artemisinin‑based combination therapy to delay resistance, while antiviral agents target viral entry, replication, or assembly, and antifungal drugs exploit differences in ergosterol synthesis or cell‑wall glucan formation. National programmes regularly update susceptibility breakpoints and treatment algorithms, and clinicians must align prescribing with these guidelines to preserve antimicrobial stewardship.

2. Medicine: Systemic Disease Management

2.1 Cardiology: Ischaemic Heart Disease, Heart Failure, and Arrhythmias

Ischaemic heart disease results from an imbalance between coronary oxygen supply and myocardial demand, most commonly due to atherosclerotic plaque rupture leading to acute coronary syndromes classified as ST‑elevation myocardial infarction, non‑ST‑elevation myocardial infarction, or unstable angina. Immediate reperfusion with primary percutaneous coronary intervention or fibrinolysis restores flow, while secondary prevention employs antiplatelet agents, statins, β‑blockers, ACE inhibitors, and lifestyle modification. Heart failure, whether with reduced or preserved ejection fraction, manifests as fluid overload and reduced cardiac output; guideline‑directed medical therapy now includes angiotensin‑receptor neprilysin inhibitors, sodium‑glucose cotransporter‑2 inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists alongside traditional diuretics and β‑blockers. Arrhythmias arise from abnormal impulse formation or conduction; atrial fibrillation requires rate or rhythm control plus anticoagulation based on CHA₂DS₂‑VASc score, while ventricular tachyarrhythmias may necessitate implantable cardioverter‑defibrillator placement.

2.2 Respiratory Medicine: Obstructive and Infectious Lung Diseases

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease share airflow limitation but differ in reversibility, inflammatory phenotype, and trigger profile; asthma is eosinophilic, often atopic, and highly responsive to inhaled corticosteroids, whereas COPD is neutrophilic, smoking‑related, and managed with long‑acting bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids only in frequent exacerbators. Community‑acquired pneumonia is stratified by severity scores such as CURB‑65 to decide outpatient versus inpatient therapy, with empirical antibiotics covering typical and atypical pathogens. Tuberculosis remains a public‑health priority; the standard six‑month regimen under DOTS achieves cure rates exceeding 85 % when adherence is ensured. Pleural effusions are classified as transudates or exudates using Light’s criteria, guiding further investigation for malignancy, infection, or heart failure.

2.3 Gastroenterology and Hepatology: Peptic Ulcer, Liver Disease, and Pancreatitis

Peptic ulcer disease is predominantly driven by Helicobacter pylori infection or non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drug use; eradication therapy combines a proton‑pump inhibitor with two antibiotics for 14 days, followed by acid suppression to promote healing. Chronic liver disease progresses from steatosis through steatohepatitis, fibrosis, to cirrhosis, with aetiologies including alcohol, viral hepatitis B and C, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease, and autoimmune conditions; complications such as portal hypertension, variceal bleeding, ascites, and hepatic encephalopathy dictate prognosis and the need for transplantation. Acute pancreatitis, most often gallstone‑ or alcohol‑induced, is diagnosed by elevated lipase and imaging; severity scoring (Ranson, APACHE II) guides aggressive fluid resuscitation, early enteral nutrition, and timely intervention for infected necrosis.

2.4 Nephrology and Endocrinology: Kidney Injury, Diabetes, and Thyroid Disorders

Acute kidney injury is staged by KDIGO criteria based on serum creatinine rise and urine output decline; management focuses on volume optimisation, avoidance of nephrotoxins, and renal replacement therapy for refractory acidosis, hyperkalaemia, or fluid overload. Chronic kidney disease classification by glomerular filtration rate and albuminuria informs cardiovascular risk stratification and preparation for dialysis or transplant. Diabetes mellitus type 2 is a metabolic syndrome characterised by insulin resistance and β‑cell dysfunction; treatment escalates from metformin through sulfonylureas, DPP‑4 inhibitors, GLP‑1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, to insulin, with cardiovascular and renal outcome trials shaping algorithmic choices. Thyroid disorders span hypothyroidism (often autoimmune Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) treated with levothyroxine, hyperthyroidism (Graves’ disease, toxic nodular goitre) managed by antithyroid drugs, radioiodine, or surgery, and thyroid nodules evaluated by ultrasound and fine‑needle aspiration using the Bethesda system.

2.5 Neurology and Infectious Diseases: Stroke, Epilepsy, and Tropical Infections

Acute ischaemic stroke demands rapid imaging to exclude haemorrhage and, within a therapeutic window, intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase or mechanical thrombectomy for large‑vessel occlusion; secondary prevention addresses hypertension, dyslipidaemia, atrial fibrillation, and lifestyle factors. Epilepsy classification guides antiseizure medication selection: focal seizures respond to carbamazepine, lamotrigine, or levetiracetam, while generalized tonic‑clonic seizures may require valproate (avoided in women of childbearing potential) or newer agents with favourable teratogenicity profiles. Tropical infections such as malaria (Plasmodium falciparum and vivax) are diagnosed by rapid antigen tests and microscopy, treated with artemisinin‑based combination therapy, and prevented by vector control; dengue management is supportive with careful fluid balance to avoid shock; HIV care follows WHO treat‑all guidelines using integrase‑inhibitor based regimens, with regular viral load monitoring to ensure suppression.

Solved Example: Positive Predictive Value Calculation
A screening test for a disease yields 90 true positives, 20 false positives, 10 false negatives, and 80 true negatives. Calculate the positive predictive value and interpret its clinical meaning.
Positive predictive value equals true positives divided by the sum of true positives and false positives. Substituting the given numbers, PPV = 90 ÷ (90 + 20) = 90 ÷ 110 ≈ 0.818, or 81.8 %. This indicates that among individuals who test positive, approximately 82 % truly have the disease, while the remaining 18 % are false alarms.
Time allocation for case‑based questions
In the written paper, allocate roughly 12 minutes per 10‑mark clinical case: spend the first two minutes structuring the answer into presentation, differential diagnosis, investigations, definitive diagnosis, management, and prevention; then devote the remaining time to concise, evidence‑based content. This disciplined pacing prevents over‑elaboration on any single component and ensures complete coverage of the marking scheme.

3. Surgery: Principles, Trauma, and Oncology

3.1 General Surgical Principles: Wound Healing, Shock, and Fluid Management

Wound healing proceeds through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling; factors such as infection, diabetes, malnutrition, and corticosteroid use impair collagen synthesis and tensile strength, predisposing to dehiscence or incisional hernia. Shock, whether hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, septic, or neurogenic, is recognised by hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria, and altered mental status; early goal‑directed resuscitation with crystalloids, blood products, and vasopressors restores tissue perfusion and prevents multi‑organ failure. Fluid and electrolyte management follows the principle of replacing ongoing losses, maintaining euvolaemia, and correcting derangements such as hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, and metabolic acidosis, guided by serial clinical assessment and laboratory monitoring.

3.2 Trauma Surgery: ATLS Protocol, Fracture Management, and Head Injury

The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) algorithm prioritises airway with cervical spine protection, breathing, circulation with haemorrhage control, disability (neurological assessment), and exposure/environment control; this systematic approach reduces missed injuries and mortality. Fracture management follows the principles of reduction, fixation, and rehabilitation; open fractures require urgent irrigation, debridement, and antibiotics (Gustilo‑Anderson classification guides severity), while definitive fixation may be internal (plates, intramedullary nails) or external depending on soft‑tissue condition. Head injury assessment uses the Glasgow Coma Scale; indications for computed tomography include GCS < 13, focal deficits, or clinical deterioration, and neurosurgical referral is mandated for intracranial haematomas exceeding volume thresholds or causing midline shift.

3.3 Surgical Oncology: Staging, Biopsy Techniques, and Operative Principles

Cancer staging employs the TNM system (tumour size, nodal involvement, metastasis) to stratify prognosis and guide multimodal therapy; accurate staging relies on imaging, endoscopic ultrasound, and pathological evaluation of resected specimens. Biopsy techniques—fine‑needle aspiration, core needle, incisional, and excisional—are selected to obtain adequate tissue for histology and molecular markers while minimising tumour seeding; sentinel lymph node biopsy in breast cancer and melanoma provides staging information with reduced morbidity compared with complete lymphadenectomy. Operative oncology adheres to the principles of en bloc resection with negative margins (R0), preservation of function when feasible, and integration with neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy as dictated by tumour biology and clinical trials.

3.4 Specialty Surgery: Urology, Orthopaedics, Neurosurgery, and Paediatric Basics

Urological emergencies such as acute urinary retention, obstructive uropathy, and testicular torsion demand prompt decompression or detorsion to preserve renal and gonadal function; benign prostatic hyperplasia is managed medically with α‑blockers or 5‑α‑reductase inhibitors, progressing to transurethral resection when refractory. Orthopaedic principles include fracture classification (AO/OTA), principles of internal fixation (absolute vs relative stability), and management of degenerative joint disease with arthroplasty. Neurosurgical conditions encompass raised intracranial pressure, spinal cord compression, and hydrocephalus, where timely decompression or shunting prevents irreversible neurological loss. Paediatric surgical basics cover congenital anomalies (hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, Hirschsprung disease, anorectal malformations) and the unique physiological considerations of fluid balance, thermoregulation, and drug dosing in neonates and infants.

4. Community Medicine: Epidemiology, Programmes, and Health Policy

4.1 Epidemiological Methods: Incidence, Prevalence, Study Designs, and Screening

Incidence measures the rate of new cases occurring in a defined population over a specified period, providing insight into disease aetiology and the effect of interventions, whereas prevalence captures the total burden of existing cases at a point or period, essential for resource allocation. Study designs range from descriptive cross‑sectional surveys to analytical case‑control and cohort studies; case‑control studies are efficient for rare diseases and estimate odds ratios, while cohort studies allow direct calculation of relative risk and incidence rates but require longer follow‑up. Randomised controlled trials remain the gold standard for therapeutic efficacy, and systematic reviews with meta‑analysis synthesize evidence across trials. Screening programmes apply tests to asymptomatic populations; the Wilson‑Jungner criteria guide selection of conditions where early detection improves outcomes, and test performance is evaluated by sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, all of which vary with disease prevalence.

4.2 National Health Programmes: Immunisation, Tuberculosis, Malaria, Maternal‑Child Health, and Non‑Communicable Diseases

The Universal Immunisation Programme delivers vaccines against diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, polio, measles, hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b, rotavirus, and pneumococcal disease, with recent inclusion of the human papillomavirus vaccine for adolescent girls; coverage targets aim for ≥90 % at the district level. The Revised National Tuberculosis Control Programme (now National TB Elimination Programme) implements the DOTS strategy, integrates drug‑resistant TB management with shorter all‑oral regimens, and engages private providers through notification incentives. The National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme coordinates malaria elimination through indoor residual spraying, long‑lasting insecticidal nets, rapid diagnostic tests, and artemisinin‑based combination therapy, while also addressing dengue, chikungunya, and Japanese encephalitis. Maternal and child health services under the Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health (RMNCH+A) continuum provide antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, postnatal visits, immunisation, nutrition supplementation, and adolescent health counselling. The National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS) promotes population‑level screening, lifestyle modification, and strengthening of secondary and tertiary care for non‑communicable diseases.

4.3 Biostatistics for Public Health: Central Tendency, Dispersion, Sampling, and Significance Testing

Descriptive statistics summarise data through measures of central tendency—mean, median, and mode—each appropriate for different distribution shapes; the mean is sensitive to outliers, while the median robustly represents skewed data such as income or length of stay. Dispersion is quantified by range, interquartile range, variance, and standard deviation, the latter enabling calculation of confidence intervals and standard errors for inferential purposes. Sampling methods—simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, and multistage—determine the representativeness of survey estimates and influence design effect calculations for sample size determination. Hypothesis testing employs parametric tests (t‑test, ANOVA) when normality and equal variance assumptions hold, and non‑parametric alternatives (Mann‑Whitney, Kruskal‑Wallis) otherwise; chi‑square tests assess associations between categorical variables, and logistic regression models adjust for confounders in binary outcomes. The p‑value threshold of 0.05 is conventional, yet confidence intervals convey precision and clinical relevance more informatively.

4.4 Environmental and Occupational Health: Water, Sanitation, Air Pollution, and Workplace Hazards

Safe water supply is monitored through bacteriological indicators (coliform count), chemical parameters (fluoride, arsenic, nitrate), and residual chlorine levels; the WHO Guidelines for Drinking‑Water Quality set health‑based targets that national standards adapt. Sanitation interventions—community‑led total sanitation, septic tanks, and sewerage networks—reduce faecal‑oral transmission of diarrhoeal diseases and helminths. Ambient air pollution, measured as particulate matter (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀), nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, and ozone, is linked to respiratory and cardiovascular morbidity; the National Ambient Air Quality Standards prescribe permissible limits, and the Air Quality Index communicates risk to the public. Occupational health addresses hazards such as silica dust (silicosis), asbestos (mesothelioma), noise (noise‑induced hearing loss), and ergonomic stressors; the Factories Act and the Employees’ State Insurance Act mandate periodic medical examinations, exposure limits, and compensation for occupational diseases.

4.5 Health Policy and Systems: National Health Policy, Ayushman Bharat, and Health System Strengthening

The National Health Policy 2017 articulates a vision of universal health coverage through increased public health expenditure (target 2.5 % of GDP), strengthening primary health care via Health and Wellness Centres, and strategic purchasing from both public and private providers. Ayushman Bharat comprises two pillars: the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, which provides cashless secondary and tertiary care coverage of ₹5 lakh per family per year for identified vulnerable groups, and the establishment of 150 000 Health and Wellness Centres delivering comprehensive primary care, including non‑communicable disease screening, mental health services, and essential medicines. Health system strengthening focuses on human resources (task‑shifting, specialist cadre), health information systems (integrated disease surveillance, electronic health records), supply chain management for drugs and diagnostics, and governance reforms such as clinical establishment regulation and patient rights charters. Monitoring progress relies on indicators like out‑of‑pocket expenditure, service coverage indices, and health‑adjusted life expectancy, ensuring accountability and mid‑course correction.

Mastery of these interconnected domains equips the candidate to construct coherent, evidence‑based answers that demonstrate both depth of knowledge and the ability to apply principles to clinical and public‑health scenarios.

Pharmacovigilance and Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Systems

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug‑related problem. In India the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) operates the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI) with the National Coordination Centre at the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad. The programme follows the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality assessment categories: certain, probable, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified and unassessable. Reporting is mandatory for marketing authorisation holders, healthcare professionals and consumers; the preferred tool is the Suspected Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) Reporting Form (Version 2.0) which captures patient demographics, drug details, reaction description, seriousness criteria (death, life‑threatening, hospitalisation, disability, congenital anomaly, medically significant) and outcome.

Signal detection employs disproportionality analysis — reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR) and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) — on the VigiBase database. A signal is considered credible when the lower bound of the 95 % confidence interval of the ROR exceeds 1 and at least three independent cases exist. Risk minimisation actions include label changes (boxed warnings, contraindications), restricted distribution programmes (e.g., thalidomide pregnancy prevention), and, in extreme cases, market withdrawal (rofecoxib, valdecoxib). Periodic Safety Update Reports (PSURs) are submitted at six‑monthly intervals for the first two years post‑approval and annually thereafter. The PvPI also conducts training workshops for medical colleges to improve reporting quality; the current reporting rate remains below 5 % of expected ADRs, indicating substantial under‑reporting.

Integration with the National Health Authority’s Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission enables real‑time ADR data exchange between hospitals and the PvPI, facilitating rapid regulatory action. The recent amendment to the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules (2022) mandates electronic submission of ADR data within 15 calendar days for serious reactions and 30 days for non‑serious ones. Understanding these timelines, causality algorithms and regulatory pathways is essential for answering questions on drug safety monitoring and for drafting policy‑level recommendations in the examination.

Critical Care Medicine: Sepsis, ARDS, and Ventilatory Strategies

Sepsis is defined as life‑threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection (Sepsis‑3, 2016). The quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) — respiratory rate ≥ 22/min, altered mentation, systolic blood pressure ≤ 100 mmHg — screens for high‑risk patients outside the ICU, while the full SOFA score quantifies organ dysfunction in six systems. Early goal‑directed therapy has been superseded by the “hour‑1 bundle”: measure lactate, obtain blood cultures before antibiotics, administer broad‑spectrum antibiotics within one hour, begin fluid resuscitation with 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥ 4 mmol/L, and apply vasopressors (norepinephrine first line) to maintain MAP ≥ 65 mmHg.

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) is diagnosed by the Berlin definition: acute onset ≤ 1 week, bilateral opacities on imaging not fully explained by effusions or atelectasis, respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure, and PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio ≤ 300 mmHg with PEEP ≥ 5 cmH₂O. Severity stratification: mild (200–300), moderate (100–200), severe (< 100). Lung‑protective ventilation targets tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure ≤ 30 cmH₂O, and driving pressure ≤ 15 cmH₂O. Prone positioning for ≥ 12 hours daily improves mortality in severe ARDS (PROSEVA trial). Neuromuscular blockade (cisatracurium infusion for 48 hours) is recommended early in severe ARDS (ACURASYS). Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is reserved for refractory hypoxaemia (PaO₂/FiO₂ < 80) despite optimal conventional management (EOLIA trial).

Ventilator‑associated pneumonia (VAP) prevention bundles include head‑of‑bed elevation 30–45°, daily sedation interruption with spontaneous breathing trials, subglottic secretion drainage, and selective oral decontamination with chlorhexidine. Antibiotic stewardship in ICU relies on procalcitonin‑guided de‑escalation and local antibiogram‑driven empiric therapy. Mastery of these protocols, scoring systems, and evidence‑based thresholds enables the candidate to construct concise, guideline‑aligned answers for both clinical vignettes and policy‑oriented questions.

Clinical Scenario Analysis
A 58‑year‑old male with community‑acquired pneumonia develops septic shock. Initial lactate 5.2 mmol/L, MAP 55 mmHg after 30 mL/kg crystalloid. Blood cultures drawn, broad‑spectrum antibiotics started. Which vasopressor is recommended as first line, and what is the target MAP according to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guidelines?
Norepinephrine is the first‑line vasopressor. The target mean arterial pressure (MAP) is ≥ 65 mmHg. If MAP remains below target despite norepinephrine, vasopressin (up to 0.03 U/min) may be added as a second agent to reduce norepinephrine dose.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the hour‑1 bundle components and the Berlin ARDS thresholds; they appear repeatedly in both short‑answer and case‑based questions. Write the bundle as a mnemonic (L‑C‑A‑F‑V) to recall Lactate, Cultures, Antibiotics, Fluids, Vasopressors under time pressure.

Minimally Invasive and Robotic Surgery: Principles and Outcomes

Laparoscopic surgery reduces surgical trauma through small ports, carbon dioxide pneumoperitoneum (12–15 mmHg), and magnified visualisation. Physiological effects include reduced postoperative pain, earlier return of bowel function, shorter hospital stay, and lower incisional hernia rates. However, pneumoperitoneum raises intra‑abdominal pressure, decreasing venous return and cardiac output, and can cause hypercapnia and subcutaneous emphysema. Contraindications include uncontrolled coagulopathy, severe cardiopulmonary disease, and extensive intra‑abdominal adhesions from prior open surgery.

Robotic‑assisted surgery (e.g., da Vinci Xi) adds wristed instruments with seven degrees of freedom, tremor filtration, and 3‑D high‑definition vision. The surgeon operates from a console, translating hand movements into precise intra‑corporeal actions. Established indications: radical prostatectomy, partial nephrectomy, rectal cancer resection, hysterectomy, and bariatric procedures. Comparative trials (e.g., ROLARR for rectal cancer) show similar oncological outcomes with lower conversion rates to open surgery but higher operative time and cost. Learning curve analysis indicates proficiency after 20–30 cases for basic procedures and 50–70 for complex pelvic surgery.

Single‑incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) and natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (NOTES) represent further minimisation but remain limited to selected centres. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols synergise with minimally invasive techniques: preoperative carbohydrate loading, multimodal analgesia (avoiding opioids), early mobilisation, and goal‑directed fluid therapy. Outcome metrics tracked in national registries (e.g., National Surgical Quality Improvement Program) include 30‑day mortality, surgical site infection, anastomotic leak, and readmission rates. Understanding the physiological impact of pneumoperitoneum, the ergonomic advantages of robotics, and the evidence base for each modality equips the aspirant to evaluate surgical innovations critically.

Health Economics, Financing, and Universal Health Coverage Metrics

Health economics applies scarcity principles to allocate resources efficiently across prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation. Key concepts: opportunity cost, cost‑effectiveness analysis (CEA) expressed as incremental cost‑effectiveness ratio (ICER = ΔCost/ΔQALY), and willingness‑to‑pay thresholds (often 1–3 × GDP per capita). In India, the Health Technology Assessment In India (HTAIn) under the Department of Health Research evaluates new drugs, devices, and programmes using CEA and budget impact analysis before inclusion in the National List of Essential Medicines or Ayushman Bharat benefit packages.

Financing mechanisms include general taxation, social health insurance (e.g., Employees’ State Insurance), community‑based health insurance, and out‑of‑pocket (OOP) payments. The National Health Accounts (2021‑22) show OOP expenditure at 48 % of total health spending, a major barrier to universal health coverage (UHC). The Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM‑JAY) aims to reduce catastrophic health expenditure by providing ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care. The Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs) deliver comprehensive primary care, financed through a mix of central and state grants under the National Health Mission.

UHC monitoring uses the Service Coverage Index (SCI) — tracer interventions across reproductive, maternal, newborn, child, infectious, non‑communicable, and service capacity domains — and financial protection indicators: incidence of catastrophic expenditure (> 10 % household income) and impoverishing expenditure (pushing households below poverty line). The WHO’s UHC cube visualises three dimensions: population coverage, service coverage, and financial protection. Recent data (2023) place India’s SCI at 61/100, with financial protection improving but still leaving 17 % of households facing catastrophic costs. Mastery of these metrics, financing flows, and HTA processes enables the candidate to answer policy‑analysis questions and to propose evidence‑based reforms.

Disaster Management and Public Health Emergency Response

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 establishes the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) chaired by the Prime Minister, State Disaster Management Authorities (SDMAs), and District Disaster Management Authorities (DDMAs). The National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) comprises 16 battalions trained in search‑and‑rescue, medical first response, and CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear) decontamination. Public health emergency preparedness follows the WHO International Health Regulations (IHR 2005) core capacities: surveillance, laboratory, risk communication, points of entry, and zoonotic event management.

Epidemic response uses the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) for weekly reporting of 33 priority diseases. The Early Warning Alert and Response System (EWARS) triggers outbreak investigation when the weekly case count exceeds the threshold (mean + 2 SD of previous three years). The National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) coordinates laboratory confirmation, contact tracing, and containment measures. For COVID‑19, the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) defined testing strategies (RT‑PCR, rapid antigen, TrueNat) and the Ministry of Health issued clinical management protocols (home isolation, mild, moderate, severe categories) aligned with WHO living guidelines.

Mass casualty management follows the triage sieve (START) and triage sort (Triage Revised Trauma Score) to prioritise patients into immediate, delayed, minimal, and expectant categories. Field hospitals (Type 1–3) are deployed per the WHO Emergency Medical Team (EMT) classification. Logistics rely on the National Health Mission’s supply chain for essential medicines, vaccines, and personal protective equipment. Post‑disaster mental health interventions use the Psychological First Aid (PFA) framework and the National Mental Health Programme’s district‑level counsellors. Understanding the legal framework, coordination structures, surveillance triggers, and triage algorithms is essential for answering both descriptive and case‑based questions on disaster health response.

Pharmacogenomics and Personalized Drug Therapy

Pharmacogenomics integrates germline genetic variation with drug disposition and response, enabling dose individualisation for high‑risk medications. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) publishes evidence‑based guidelines that translate genotype into prescribing actions. For clopidogrel, loss‑of‑function alleles of CYP2C19 (*2, *3) reduce active metabolite formation by 30–50 %, increasing stent thrombosis risk; CPIC recommends alternative P2Y12 inhibitors (prasugrel or ticagrelor) for *2/*2 or *2/*3 carriers. In contrast, CYP2C9 and VKORC1 variants explain up to 40 % of warfarin dose variability; genotype‑guided algorithms reduce time‑in‑therapeutic‑range deviation by 15 % compared with fixed‑dose initiation. The FDA label for abacavir mandates HLA‑B*57:01 screening because the allele confers a 50‑fold hypersensitivity risk (positive predictive value ≈ 55 %). For carbamazepine, HLA‑B*15:02 screening is recommended in populations of Asian ancestry where allele frequency reaches 10 %. Implementation barriers include turnaround time for genotyping, cost‑effectiveness thresholds (often quoted as ≤ $50 000 per quality‑adjusted life year), and clinician familiarity. Point‑of‑care platforms (e.g., TaqMan arrays) now deliver results within 2 hours, supporting pre‑emptive panel testing in tertiary centres. Ethical considerations involve incidental findings, data storage, and equitable access across socioeconomic strata. Examiners frequently test the distinction between pre‑emptive (genotype available before prescribing) and reactive (genotype ordered after adverse event) strategies, and the impact of allele frequency on number‑needed‑to‑screen calculations.

Advanced Cardiac Imaging and Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Strategies

Contemporary interventional cardiology relies on multimodal imaging to optimise lesion assessment, device selection, and procedural outcomes. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) provides quantitative lumen and plaque area measurements; a minimum lumen area (MLA) ≤ 4.0 mm² in non‑left‑main vessels and ≤ 6.0 mm² in left main correlates with fractional flow reserve (FFR) ≤ 0.80. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) offers 10‑µm resolution, enabling fibrous cap thickness measurement; caps < 65 µm identify thin‑cap fibroatheromas prone to rupture. FFR, measured with a pressure wire during maximal hyperaemia (adenosine 140 µg/kg/min), remains the physiological gold standard for ischaemia detection; the FAME‑2 trial demonstrated that FFR‑guided PCI reduced urgent revascularisation by 46 % versus medical therapy alone. Instantaneous wave‑free ratio (iFR) eliminates adenosine, showing non‑inferiority to FFR in the DEFINE‑FLAIR and iFR‑SWEDEHEART trials. For complex bifurcation lesions, the DK‑crush and culotte techniques achieve superior final kissing balloon expansion compared with provisional T‑stenting, as evidenced by the BBC‑ONE and NORDIC‑BIFURCATION studies. Drug‑coated balloons (DCB) are indicated for in‑stent restenosis (ISR) and small‑vessel disease (≤ 2.75 mm), with the PEPCAD‑China ISR trial reporting 9‑month target lesion revascularisation rates of 5.8 % versus 18.3 % for plain balloon angioplasty. Bioresorbable vascular scaffolds (BVS) were withdrawn after the ABSORB‑III trial showed higher device‑oriented composite endpoints (11.0 % vs 7.9 % at 3 years) driven by scaffold thrombosis. Current guidelines (ESC 2023, ACC/AHA 2024) endorse physiology‑guided revascularisation, IVUS/OCT optimisation for left main and complex lesions, and DCB for ISR. Examiners often ask for the sequence of imaging modalities in a left main PCI: coronary angiography → FFR/iFR → IVUS for sizing → OCT for edge dissection assessment → post‑PCI IVUS confirmation of MLA ≥ 5.5 mm² and stent expansion ≥ 90 %.

Technique: Integrated Imaging Decision Pathway
A 62‑year‑old male with stable angina undergoes coronary angiography showing a 70 % distal left main stenosis. FFR is 0.78. IVUS reveals MLA 4.2 mm², plaque burden 68 %, and minimal calcification. OCT shows a fibrous cap thickness of 55 µm at the proximal edge. Which stent strategy and post‑PCI optimisation criteria are most appropriate?
Because FFR ≤ 0.80 and IVUS MLA < 6.0 mm², revascularisation is indicated. The thin fibrous cap (< 65 µm) on OCT signals high plaque vulnerability, favouring a drug‑eluting stent (DES) with adequate coverage. Use IVUS to size the stent to the distal reference lumen (target stent diameter = 1.0 × mean reference diameter). Deploy a second‑generation DES (e.g., everolimus‑eluting) with overlapping technique if length > 28 mm. Post‑PCI IVUS criteria: MLA ≥ 5.5 mm², stent expansion ≥ 90 % of nominal area, symmetric expansion index ≥ 0.7, and no edge dissection > grade C. If MLA < 5.5 mm², perform high‑pressure non‑compliant balloon post‑dilatation (≤ 16 atm) and repeat IVUS. This pathway integrates physiology, plaque morphology, and quantitative stent optimisation per ESC 2023 recommendations.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three quantitative IVUS benchmarks (MLA, expansion, symmetry) and the OCT fibrous‑cap threshold (65 µm). When a vignette provides both FFR and IVUS, apply the “physiology first, anatomy second” rule: FFR decides *whether* to stent; IVUS decides *how* to stent. Practice writing the optimisation checklist in bullet form to save time.

Hemodynamic Monitoring and Vasoactive Titration in Septic Shock

Goal‑directed haemodynamic management in septic shock progresses from static surrogates to dynamic, flow‑based parameters. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) 2021 bundle recommends initial crystalloid resuscitation (30 mL/kg) followed by norepinephrine as first‑line vasopressor to target mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mmHg. Dynamic preload responsiveness is assessed by passive leg raise (PLR) with real‑time cardiac output (CO) monitoring (pulse contour analysis, echocardiography, or bioreactance); a ≥ 10 % increase in stroke volume predicts fluid responsiveness with 90 % sensitivity. Static indices (CVP, PAOP) are discouraged for guiding fluid therapy. After adequate preload, vasopressor titration follows a MAP‑guided algorithm: norepinephrine 0.05–0.5 µg/kg/min; if MAP target unmet at ≥ 0.5 µg/kg/min, add vasopressin 0.03 U/min (VASST trial showed reduced renal replacement therapy). Epinephrine (0.05–0.5 µg/kg/min) is the preferred second‑line agent when vasopressin unavailable; it increases CO but may worsen lactate clearance. Dobutamine (5–20 µg/kg/min) is added for persistent hypoperfusion (ScvO₂ < 70 % or lactate > 4 mmol/L) despite adequate MAP, targeting a cardiac index ≥ 2.5 L/min/m². The SSC advises against routine use of high‑dose corticosteroids; hydrocortisone 200 mg/day is reserved for shock refractory to ≥ 0.25 µg/kg/min norepinephrine. Lactate clearance ≥ 10 % per 2 hours correlates with 28‑day mortality reduction. Emerging technologies include transpulmonary thermodilution (PiCCO) providing extravascular lung water index (EVLWI) to guide fluid restriction (EVLWI > 10 mL/kg predicts pulmonary oedema). Examiners test the sequence: crystalloid → MAP target → dynamic preload assessment → norepinephrine → vasopressin/epinephrine → dobutamine for low CO, and the lactate clearance benchmark.

Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs and Resistance Containment in Hospital Settings

Effective antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) reduces inappropriate prescribing, curbs resistance, and improves patient outcomes. Core elements (CDC 2019) comprise leadership commitment, accountability (physician lead), pharmacy expertise (pharmacist lead), action (prospective audit with feedback and formulary restriction), tracking (days of therapy per 1 000 patient‑days, DDD), reporting, and education. The “Start Smart, Then Focus” approach mandates empirical therapy guided by local antibiograms, followed by de‑escalation at 48–72 hours based on culture data and clinical response. The WHO AWaRe classification (Access, Watch, Reserve) structures formulary decisions: Access agents (e.g., amoxicillin, ceftriaxone) should constitute ≥ 60 % of total antibiotic consumption; Watch agents (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam, meropenem) limited to specific indications; Reserve agents (e.g., colistin, tigecycline) restricted to multidrug‑resistant infections with infectious‑disease approval. Metrics include the defined daily dose (DDD) per 100 admissions, the proportion of broad‑spectrum prescriptions, and the 30‑day readmission rate for infection. The INCREMENT‑AMS trial demonstrated that a bundled intervention (rapid diagnostics, pharmacist‑led review, automatic stop orders at day 5) reduced carbapenem use by 28 % and carbapenem‑resistant Enterobacterales incidence by 15 % over 18 months. Rapid molecular panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray) deliver pathogen identification and resistance genes within 1 hour, enabling targeted therapy 24 hours earlier than conventional culture. Antifungal stewardship mirrors antibacterial principles, focusing on echinocandin de‑escalation to fluconazole for susceptible Candida spp. and limiting prophylactic fluconazole to high‑risk ICU patients (APACHE II > 20). Examiners often ask for the hierarchy of interventions: (1) prospective audit with feedback, (2) formulary restriction with pre‑authorisation, (3) rapid diagnostics, (4) automatic stop orders, (5) education. They also test the distinction between “days of therapy” (DOT) and “defined daily dose” (DDD) — DOT counts each calendar day a patient receives any dose, while DDD normalises to WHO standard dose, making DOT preferred for paediatric and renal‑adjusted populations.

Implementation Science for National Health Programme Evaluation

Implementation science bridges the gap between evidence‑based interventions and routine practice within health systems. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) organises determinants across five domains: intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and process. For India’s National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP), the RE-AIM model (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) quantifies programme penetration: Reach = proportion of eligible TB patients enrolled (target > 90 %); Effectiveness = treatment success rate (target ≥ 85 %); Adoption = proportion of public‑private facilities implementing Nikshay (target 100 %); Implementation fidelity = adherence to daily observed therapy (DOT) protocols (measured by pill‑count concordance > 95 %); Maintenance = sustained case notification rates over 5 years. Mixed‑methods evaluation combines quantitative indicators (cascade of care metrics, time to diagnosis) with qualitative barriers (stigma, drug stockouts, health‑worker motivation). The “Theory of Change” maps inputs (GeneXpert machines, human resources) to activities (active case finding), outputs (diagnosed cases), outcomes (treatment initiation), and impact (incidence reduction). Process evaluation uses the “fidelity‑dose‑reach” triad: fidelity = proportion of prescribed DOT visits completed; dose = number of patient‑contacts per month; reach = percentage of target population covered. Cost‑effectiveness analysis employs incremental cost‑effectiveness ratios (ICER) expressed in cost per disability‑adjusted life year (DALY) averted; the WHO threshold of 1–3 × GDP per capita guides adoption decisions. The Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn) applies these methods before scaling new diagnostics (e.g., ultra‑portable X‑ray with AI). Examiners test the ability to select the appropriate framework (CFIR for barrier analysis, RE‑AIM for programme‑level metrics) and to interpret a logic model linking inputs to impact. They also probe the difference between “implementation outcomes” (acceptability, feasibility) and “service outcomes” (efficiency, safety) as defined by Proctor et al.

Geriatric Pharmacology: Polypharmacy, Deprescribing, and Age‑Related Pharmacokinetic Changes

Physiological Basis of Altered Drug Disposition

Ageing reduces gastric acid secretion and intestinal blood flow, modestly delaying oral absorption but rarely altering bioavailability to a clinically significant degree. The most consistent changes are a 30‑40 % decline in hepatic phase‑I oxidative capacity (CYP3A4, CYP2D6) and a 20‑30 % reduction in renal glomerular filtration rate (GFR) per decade after 40 years, which together prolong half‑lives of renally cleared drugs (e.g., digoxin, gabapentin) and increase exposure to hepatically metabolised agents with narrow therapeutic indices (warfarin, phenytoin). Lean body mass falls while total body fat rises, expanding the volume of distribution for lipophilic drugs (diazepam, amitriptyline) and lengthening their terminal elimination phase. Plasma albumin declines 1‑2 g/L per decade, raising free fractions of highly protein‑bound acidic drugs (phenytoin, valproate) and necessitating free‑drug monitoring when toxicity is suspected.

Polypharmacy Definitions and Risk Stratification

Polypharmacy is conventionally defined as ≥5 concurrent chronic medications; hyper‑polypharmacy denotes ≥10. The Medication Appropriateness Index (MAI) and the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale quantify inappropriate prescribing. High‑risk drug classes in older adults include benzodiazepines (falls, delirium), anticholinergics (cognitive decline), non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (GI bleed, renal impairment), and long‑acting sulfonylureas (hypoglycaemia). The Beers Criteria (2023 update) and the STOPP/START criteria (version 3) provide explicit lists of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) and prescribing omissions, respectively. Examiners frequently test the distinction: STOPP flags a drug that should be stopped (e.g., dipyridamole as monotherapy for stroke prevention), while START flags an indicated drug that is missing (e.g., ACE inhibitor in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction).

Deprescribing Frameworks and Evidence

Deprescribing follows a stepwise algorithm: (1) ascertain all medications and indications, (2) assess overall benefit‑harm using life‑expectancy and goals‑of‑care, (3) prioritize drugs for withdrawal using STOPP/MAI, (4) taper slowly (e.g., benzodiazepine reduction by 10‑25 % of the dose every 1‑2 weeks), (5) monitor for withdrawal syndromes and disease recurrence. The D‑PRESCRIBE trial demonstrated that a pharmacist‑led deprescribing intervention reduced PIMs by 30 % and falls by 15 % over 12 months in community‑dwelling seniors. The OPERAM trial showed no mortality benefit but improved medication appropriateness scores. Examiners may ask for the “prescribing cascade” concept: a new drug is prescribed to treat an adverse effect of another drug (e.g., PPI for NSAID‑induced dyspepsia), and recognition of the cascade enables simultaneous withdrawal of both agents.

Clinical Scenarios and Examiner Twists

A typical vignette presents an 82‑year‑old woman on ten drugs including digoxin 0.25 mg daily, amitriptyline 25 mg nocte, ibuprofen 400 mg TDS, and glibenclamide 5 mg daily. The correct answer identifies four PIMs: digoxin dose >0.125 mg in renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min), amitriptyline (high anticholinergic burden), ibuprofen (NSAID in heart failure/CKD), and glibenclamide (long‑acting sulfonylurea). The examiner may ask which single intervention yields the greatest reduction in hospitalisation — evidence points to NSAID cessation. Another twist: “Which laboratory value best guides digoxin dose adjustment in this patient?” Answer: serum creatinine‑based eGFR (Cockcroft‑Gault) rather than serum digoxin level alone, because renal clearance drives accumulation.

Oncology Pharmacology: Targeted Therapies, Immunotherapy Toxicity Management, and Biomarker‑Guided Treatment

Molecular Targets and Representative Agents

Tyrosine‑kinase inhibitors (TKIs) dominate targeted therapy: imatinib (BCR‑ABL1), erlotinib/gefitinib/osimertinib (EGFR exon 19 deletion/L858R), crizotinib/alectinib/lorlatinib (ALK rearrangements), dabrafenib + trametinib (BRAF V600E), and sunitinib/pazopanib (VEGFR/PDGFR). Monoclonal antibodies include trastuzumab (HER2), cetuximab/panitumumab (EGFR), bevacizumab (VEGF‑A), and rituximab (CD20). Antibody‑drug conjugates (ADCs) such as trastuzumab‑emtansine (T‑DM1) and sacituzumab‑govitecan deliver cytotoxic payloads to HER2‑positive and Trop‑2‑expressing tumours respectively. Examiners test knowledge of resistance mechanisms: EGFR T790M (first‑generation TKI resistance) overcome by osimertinib; ALK G1202R (lorlatinib‑sensitive); MET amplification bypassing EGFR inhibition.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors and Immune‑Related Adverse Events (irAEs)

Anti‑PD‑1/PD‑L1 (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab) and anti‑CTLA‑4 (ipilimumab) antibodies unleash T‑cell activity. irAEs follow a distinct organ‑specific pattern: colitis (30‑40 % with CTLA‑4, 10‑20 % with PD‑1), hepatitis (AST/ALT elevation), pneumonitis (3‑5 %), endocrinopathies (hypophysitis, thyroiditis, adrenal insufficiency, type 1 diabetes), dermatologic (rash, vitiligo), and rare neurologic (myasthenia, encephalitis). Grading uses CTCAE v5.0; grade ≥3 requires corticosteroid 1‑2 mg/kg prednisolone equivalent, with taper over ≥4 weeks. Refractory cases receive infliximab (colitis) or mycophenolate (hepatitis). Examiners often ask: “Which irAE is an absolute contraindication to rechallenge?” Answer: grade 4 myocarditis, grade 4 neurologic toxicity, or grade 3‑4 pneumonitis requiring >10 mg prednisolone for >12 weeks.

Biomarker‑Guided Treatment Algorithms

PD‑L1 tumour proportion score (TPS) ≥50 % selects pembrolizumab monotherapy in first‑line metastatic NSCLC; TPS 1‑49 % permits pembrolizumab + chemotherapy. Tumour mutational burden (TMB) ≥10 mut/Mb (FoundationOne) supports pembrolizumab in previously treated solid tumours (KEYNOTE‑158). Microsatellite instability‑high (MSI‑H) or mismatch‑repair deficiency (dMMR) predicts response across histologies (pembrolizumab approved tissue‑agnostic). NTRK fusions (larotrectinib, entrectinib) and RET alterations (selpercatinib, pralsetinib) are actionable irrespective of tumour type. Examiners test the hierarchy: tissue‑agnostic approvals (MSI‑H/dMMR, NTRK, TMB‑high) supersede histology‑specific indications.

Clinical Vignette and Examiner Traps

A 55‑year‑old man with metastatic NSCLC, EGFR exon 19 deletion, progresses on osimertinib after 14 months. Re‑biopsy shows MET amplification without EGFR T790M. The guideline‑directed next step is osimertinib + savolitinib (MET inhibitor) per INSIGHT‑2 trial, not chemotherapy alone. A common trap: selecting a second‑generation EGFR TKI (afatinib) which lacks activity against MET‑mediated resistance. Another trap: assuming pembrolizumab can be added to osimertinib; the KEYNOTE‑789 trial showed increased interstitial lung disease without survival benefit.

Technique: Structured Clinical Reasoning for Deprescribing
An 84‑year‑old woman with hypertension, atrial fibrillation (CHA₂DS₂‑VASc = 4), osteoarthritis, and mild cognitive impairment takes: enalapril 10 mg, bisoprolol 5 mg, rivaroxaban 20 mg, ibuprofen 400 mg TDS, paracetamol 1 g QDS, donepezil 10 mg, and zolpidem 5 mg nocte. Her eGFR is 38 mL/min/1.73 m². Identify all potentially inappropriate medications per STOPP/START v3 and propose a deprescribing plan with monitoring parameters.
STOPP flags: (1) Rivaroxaban 20 mg in CrCl < 50 mL/min — reduce to 15 mg daily (START: anticoagulation indicated, dose adjustment required). (2) Ibuprofen NSAID in CKD stage 3b and hypertension — stop; replace with topical NSAID or paracetamol maximised. (3) Zolpidem — high fall risk, Beers PIM; taper off over 2 weeks, implement sleep hygiene. (4) Bisoprolol 5 mg may be excessive if heart rate < 50 bpm; assess and down‑titrate. START omissions: (1) Vitamin D 800 IU daily (osteoporosis prophylaxis). (2) Laxative if constipation from rivaroxaban/opioids (none here). Deprescribing sequence: Week 1‑2: stop ibuprofen, start topical diclofenac; reduce rivaroxaban to 15 mg. Week 2‑4: taper zolpidem 2.5 mg for 1 week then stop; monitor sleep diary and fall incidence. Week 4‑8: reassess bisoprolol dose via heart‑rate log; adjust if bradycardia. Monitoring: Renal function and haemoglobin at 4 weeks post‑NSAID cessation; INR not required for rivaroxaban but check anti‑Xa level if bleeding; cognitive screen (MoCA) at baseline and 3 months after zolpidem withdrawal; fall diary monthly.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a deprescribing question appears, first list every drug with its indication, then apply STOPP/START systematically. Write the STOPP criterion code (e.g., K1, J2) beside each flagged drug — this shows the examiner you know the framework. Prioritise interventions that reduce immediate harm (falls, bleeding, renal injury) over those addressing long‑term appropriateness. Always state the monitoring plan; a deprescribing answer without follow‑up is incomplete.

Critical Care Nephrology: Renal Replacement Therapy Modalities, Timing, and Dose Prescription

Modalities and Physiologic Principles

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) provides slow, sustained solute and fluid removal, haemodynamically better tolerated than intermittent haemodialysis (IHD) in vasopressor‑dependent shock. CRRT modalities: continuous venovenous haemofiltration (CVVH) — convection‑dominant, excellent middle‑molecule clearance; continuous venovenous haemodialysis (CVVHD) — diffusion‑dominant, superior small‑solute clearance (urea, potassium); continuous venovenous haemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) — combined, most versatile. Sustained low‑efficiency dialysis (SLED) bridges CRRT and IHD, using standard dialysis machines with extended sessions (6‑12 h) and lower blood flows (200‑250 mL/min). Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is rarely used in acute ICU settings due to catheter‑related infection risk and limited clearance in catabolic patients.

Timing of Initiation: Evidence Base

The AKIKI, ELAIN, IDEAL‑ICU, and STARRT‑AKI trials collectively inform timing. AKIKI (delayed vs early strategy) showed no 60‑day mortality difference but higher RRT‑free days in delayed arm. ELAIN (early vs delayed) reported lower 90‑day mortality with early initiation (KDIGO stage 2 within 8 h). IDEAL‑ICU found no benefit of early initiation in septic shock. STARRT‑AKI (largest, n = 3000) demonstrated that an accelerated strategy (initiation within 12 h of KDIGO stage 3) did not reduce 90‑day mortality versus standard strategy (watchful waiting with absolute indications). Current KDIGO 2024 guidance: initiate RRT for absolute indications (refractory hyperkalaemia > 6.5 mmol/L, severe acidosis pH < 7.15, pulmonary oedema unresponsive to diuretics, uraemic pericarditis/encephalopathy) and consider early initiation in patients with rapid trajectory (creatinine rise > 0.5 mg/dL/6 h) and high illness severity (SOFA > 10). Examiners test the nuance: “early” in trials refers to KDIGO stage 2, not stage 1.

Dose Prescription and Adequacy Metrics

CRRT dose is expressed as effluent volume per kg per hour (mL/kg/h). KDIGO recommends 20‑25 mL/kg/h for CVVHDF; the RENAL and ATN trials showed no mortality benefit of 35 mL/kg/h over 20 mL/kg/h. For CVVH, substitution fluid rate (pre‑ or post‑filter) plus ultrafiltration rate equals effluent dose. Filter life should exceed 24 h; clotting is mitigated by regional citrate anticoagulation (target post‑filter ionised calcium 0.25‑0.35 mmol/L) or systemic heparin (aPTT 1.5‑2× baseline). Citrate requires calcium replacement and monitoring of total/ionised calcium ratio (>2.5 suggests citrate accumulation). Adequacy is tracked by weekly urea kinetic modelling (stdKt/V ≥ 2.0 for CRRT) or by achieving a time‑averaged urea reduction ratio (URR) > 55 % per session in SLED. Examiners may ask: “Which parameter best reflects middle‑molecule clearance in CVVH?” Answer: β2‑microglobulin reduction ratio, not urea.

Complications and Special Populations

CRRT‑associated hypophosphataemia (phosphate loss in effluent) occurs in 30‑40 % of patients; protocolised phosphate replacement (0.5‑1 mmol/kg/day) prevents refeeding‑type weakness. Citrate‑induced metabolic alkalosis (high strong ion difference) is corrected by reducing citrate or increasing dialysis dose. In liver failure, citrate metabolism is impaired — prefer heparin or reduced citrate with close ionised calcium monitoring. In right‑heart failure, high venous pressures limit filter flow; consider femoral catheter with larger lumen or IHD/SLED. Paediatric dosing uses 2‑3 mL/kg/h higher effluent rates due to higher metabolic rate. Examiners often test the distinction between “dose” (effluent rate) and “intensity” (blood flow × filter surface area) and the impact of down‑time on delivered dose.

Advanced Trauma Resuscitation: Damage Control Surgery, Massive Transfusion Protocols, and Coagulopathy Management

Damage Control Surgery (DCS) Phases and Indications

DCS is a three‑phase strategy for exsanguinating trauma patients who cannot tolerate definitive repair. Phase 1 (abbreviated laparotomy/thoracotomy) achieves haemostasis (packing, vascular shunting, staple resection of contaminated bowel) and contamination control within 60‑90 minutes; the abdomen is left open with a temporary closure (Barker’s vacuum pack, Wittmann patch, or Bogotá bag). Phase 2 (ICU resuscitation) targets correction of the lethal triad: hypothermia (< 35 °C), acidosis (pH < 7.2), and coagulopathy (INR > 1.5, fibrinogen < 1.5 g/L). Phase 3 (definitive surgery) occurs 24‑48 h later when physiology is restored. Indications for DCS: penetrating torso trauma with > 6 units PRBC in 6 h, blunt trauma with base deficit > 6, lactate > 5 mmol/L, or intraoperative finding of “unsalvageable” physiology. Examiners test the “damage control resuscitation” (DCR) concept: permissive hypotension (SBP 80‑90 mmHg) until haemostasis, balanced transfusion, and early tranexamic acid (TXA) within 3 h (CRASH‑2).

Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP) Composition and Ratios

MTP delivers fixed ratios of red blood cells (RBC), fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelets (PLT) to approximate whole blood. The PROPPR trial compared 1:1:1 vs 1:1:2 (RBC:FFP:PLT) and found no 24‑h mortality difference but more patients achieved haemostasis and fewer exsanguination deaths in 1:1:1. Current guidelines (Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma, 2023) recommend 1:1:1 as default, with rapid availability of fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate (10 units) when fibrinogen < 1.5 g/L. Calcium replacement (1 g calcium chloride per 4 units RBC) prevents citrate‑induced hypocalcaemia. Tranexamic acid 1 g bolus over 10 min then 1 g over 8 h is standard. Examiners may ask: “What is the target platelet count during MTP?” Answer: ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L (or ≥ 100 × 10⁹/L if traumatic brain injury).

Trauma‑Induced Coagulopathy (TIC) Pathophysiology and Goal‑Directed Therapy

TIC is driven by endothelial activation, glycocalyx shedding, protein C pathway activation, and platelet dysfunction — distinct from dilutional coagulopathy. Viscoelastic testing (ROTEM/TEG) guides component therapy: EXTEM CT prolongation → FFP; FIBTEM A5 < 10 mm → fibrinogen concentrate (3‑4 g) or cryoprecipitate; EXTEM MCF low with normal FIBTEM → platelet transfusion. The ITACTIC trial showed ROTEM‑guided MTP reduced plasma and platelet use without increasing mortality. Fibrinogen is the first factor to fall critically; target ≥ 2.0 g/L in active bleeding. Factor XIII and von Willebrand factor are not routinely replaced. Examiners test the distinction: “Which ROTEM parameter reflects fibrinogen contribution to clot strength?” Answer: FIBTEM A5 (or MCF).

Adjuncts and Emerging Strategies

Whole blood (low‑titre group O) is increasingly used in pre‑hospital and early hospital phases; the SWIFT trial demonstrated feasibility and reduced component exposure. Recombinant factor VIIa remains restricted to refractory bleeding after surgical control and correction of physiology (pH > 7.2, temperature > 34 °C, fibrinogen > 1.5 g/L, platelet > 50 × 10⁹/L). Calcium monitoring: ionised calcium < 1.0 mmol/L triggers 10 mL 10 % calcium chloride. Hypothermia prevention: forced‑air warming, fluid warmers, and warmed blood products. Examiners may present a scenario where a patient receives 12 units RBC, 8 units FFP, 1 unit platelets, and remains oozing with ROTEM showing EXTEM CT normal, FIBTEM A5 = 6 mm — correct action: fibrinogen concentrate 4 g, not more FFP.

Health Technology Assessment and Decision‑Analytic Modelling for Policy Adoption

HTA Framework and Indian Institutional Landscape

Health Technology Assessment (HTA) evaluates clinical effectiveness, cost‑effectiveness, budget impact, ethical, legal, and social implications of health technologies (drugs, devices, programmes). In India, the Department of Health Research (DHR) established HTAIn (Health Technology Assessment in India) in 2017 as the nodal body. HTAIn follows the WHO‑recommended “core model” domains: health problem and current use, technology description, safety, clinical effectiveness, costs and economic evaluation, organisational aspects, patient and social aspects, and legal aspects. The National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM) revision and the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) pricing decisions increasingly rely on HTAIn outputs. Examiners test knowledge of the “reference case” for Indian economic evaluations: societal perspective, 3 % discount rate for both costs and health outcomes, lifetime horizon, cost per DALY averted threshold of 1‑3 × GDP per capita (≈ ₹2‑6 lakh per DALY in 2024).

Decision‑Analytic Modelling: Structures and Key Parameters

Common model structures: decision trees (short‑term, acute interventions), Markov cohort models (chronic diseases with health states), microsimulation (individual‑level heterogeneity), and dynamic transmission models (infectious diseases). A Markov model for hepatitis C treatment might have states: chronic HCV, compensated cirrhosis, decompensated cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, post‑transplant, death. Transition probabilities derived from meta‑analyses (e.g., annual progression 3‑5 % from compensated to decompensated cirrhosis). Utilities (quality‑of‑life weights) sourced from EQ‑5D Indian valuation set (e.g., chronic HCV 0.78, decompensated cirrhosis 0.55). Costs include drug acquisition (generic sofosbuvir/velpatasvir ₹12,000 per 12‑week course), monitoring, adverse‑event management, and productivity losses. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) with 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations yields cost‑effectiveness acceptability curves (CEAC). Examiners often ask: “Which parameter most influences the ICER in a hepatitis C model?” Answer: drug price (dominant driver) followed by sustained virologic response (SVR) rate.

Budget Impact Analysis (BIA) vs Cost‑Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)

BIA estimates the financial consequences of adopting a technology within a specific budget holder’s constraints (e.g., state health department) over a short horizon (3‑5 years), using current epidemiology and market uptake assumptions. CEA compares incremental cost per health gain (DALY/QALY) over a lifetime horizon from a societal perspective. A technology can be cost‑effective (ICER below threshold) but have unaffordable budget impact (e.g., CAR‑T cell therapy for relapsed ALL: ICER ₹25 lakh/DALY but 5‑year budget impact > ₹500 crore). HTAIn requires both analyses for reimbursement decisions. Examiners test the ability to interpret a BIA table: “If uptake rises from 10 % to 30 % in year 3, what is the incremental budget impact?” Calculation: (prevalence × uptake × per‑patient cost) difference.

Equity‑Focused HTA and Emerging Methods

Extended cost‑effectiveness analysis (ECEA) incorporates financial risk protection (FRP) and distributional consequences across income quintiles. The “distributional cost‑effectiveness analysis” (DCEA) uses concentration indices to quantify inequality aversion. For example, introducing rotavirus vaccine in the public programme yields greater DALYs averted in the poorest quintile (higher baseline mortality) and reduces out‑of‑pocket expenditure, producing a pro‑poor equity impact. Multi‑criteria decision analysis (MCDA) adds criteria such as feasibility, acceptability, and strategic alignment, weighted by stakeholder deliberation. Examiners may present a scenario: “A new dengue vaccine is cost‑effective (ICER ₹1.8 lakh/DALY) but requires a three‑dose schedule with cold‑chain at −20 °C. Using MCDA, which criteria would likely lower its overall score?” Answer: implementation complexity, cold‑chain requirement, and vaccine hesitancy.

  • Confusing the STOPP criterion for “drug without indication” with the START criterion for “indicated drug omitted” — they are opposite directions.
  • Assuming all immune‑related adverse events require permanent discontinuation; only grade 4 myocarditis, severe neurologic toxicity, and refractory grade 3‑4 pneumonitis mandate permanent stop.
  • Prescribing a fixed CRRT dose of 35 mL/kg/h based on outdated guidelines; current evidence supports 20‑25 mL/kg/h.
  • Using a 1:1:2 transfusion ratio as default in massive transfusion protocols; PROPPR and current guidelines endorse 1:1:1.
  • Equating budget impact analysis with cost‑effectiveness analysis; BIA is short‑term, payer‑perspective, while CEA is lifetime, societal.
  • Applying the WHO‑CHOICE threshold of 1‑3 × GDP per capita without adjusting for the Indian reference case discount rate of 3 % for both costs and outcomes.
  • These traps highlight the need for precise guideline‑based reasoning in the examination.
