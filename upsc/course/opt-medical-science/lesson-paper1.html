Anatomy

1. General Anatomy and Anatomical Terminology

The study of anatomy begins with a universally accepted reference frame known as the anatomical position, in which the body stands erect, faces forward, arms at the sides with palms directed anteriorly, and feet parallel. This convention, formalised in the Frankfurt plane, allows every description of location, movement, and relationship to be unambiguous across languages and centuries. The three cardinal planes — sagittal, coronal, and transverse — divide the body into right and left, anterior and posterior, superior and inferior portions respectively, and they are the foundation for radiological interpretation and surgical planning. Terms such as medial, lateral, proximal, distal, superficial, and deep describe relative positions along these planes, while directional adjectives like cranial and caudal retain their embryological significance. Mastery of this vocabulary is essential because every subsequent topic, from osteology to neuroanatomy, relies on precise spatial description to avoid confusion in both written answers and clinical communication.

2. Osteology, Arthrology and the Vertebral Column

The human skeleton comprises 206 bones classified by shape into long, short, flat, irregular, and sesamoid categories, each adapted to distinct mechanical functions. Long bones such as the femur and humerus grow by endochondral ossification at epiphyseal plates, a process regulated by growth hormone and thyroid hormone, and their diaphyses provide leverage for muscular action. Joints, or articulations, are classified structurally into fibrous, cartilaginous, and synovial types; synovial joints, exemplified by the knee and shoulder, permit the greatest range of motion because a fibrous capsule encloses a synovial cavity lubricated by hyaluronic acid‑rich fluid. The vertebral column, consisting of 33 vertebrae grouped into cervical (7), thoracic (12), lumbar (5), sacral (5 fused), and coccygeal (4 fused) regions, protects the spinal cord while supporting axial load and allowing flexion, extension, lateral flexion, and rotation. Intervertebral discs, composed of a peripheral annulus fibrosus and a central nucleus pulposus, act as hydraulic cushions; degeneration of the nucleus pulposus with age underlies disc herniation and radiculopathy, illustrating how structural change produces clinical consequence.

3. Embryology and Histology

Embryology traces the origin of all adult tissues from a single zygote through a series of tightly regulated events. Gametogenesis — spermatogenesis in the seminiferous tubules and oogenesis in the ovarian cortex — reduces the chromosome complement to haploid, and fertilisation restores diploidy in the fallopian tube. Cleavage yields a morula that compacts into a blastocyst, which implants into the endometrium around day six. Gastrulation establishes the three primary germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm. Ectoderm gives rise to the epidermis and the entire nervous system; mesoderm forms muscle, bone, blood, and the urogenital system; endoderm lines the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts and their associated glands. Organogenesis follows, with the heart beginning to beat by the fourth week and the neural tube closing by day 28. Histology, the microscopic counterpart of embryology, classifies tissues into four basic types — epithelium, connective tissue, muscle, and nervous tissue — each identifiable by characteristic cell shape, extracellular matrix, and specialised junctions. For instance, simple squamous epithelium lines alveoli to permit rapid gas diffusion, while dense regular connective tissue forms tendons that transmit muscular force to bone.

4. Neuroanatomy and Regional Anatomy

The central nervous system develops from the neural tube into the brain and spinal cord, while the peripheral nervous system arises from neural crest cells. The brain is organised into cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem, and cerebellum, each containing nuclei and tracts that mediate specific functions; the corticospinal tract, for example, descends from the primary motor cortex through the internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, and medullary pyramids to synapse on lower motor neurons in the spinal cord. Twelve pairs of cranial nerves emerge from the brainstem and forebrain, carrying sensory, motor, and autonomic fibres; the vagus nerve (CN X) uniquely provides parasympathetic innervation to thoracic and abdominal viscera. The autonomic nervous system is divided into sympathetic (thoracolumbar outflow) and parasympathetic (craniosacral outflow) divisions, which often exert opposing effects on target organs. Regional anatomy integrates these systems within body cavities: the thorax houses the heart, great vessels, and lungs within the mediastinum and pleural cavities; the abdomen contains the gastrointestinal tract, liver, pancreas, spleen, kidneys, and major vessels, all related to peritoneal reflections; the pelvis encloses the urinary bladder, rectum, and reproductive organs. Knowledge of these topographical relationships is indispensable for interpreting imaging, planning surgical approaches, and understanding the spread of pathology.

Physiology

5. Cellular and Neural Physiology

Cellular physiology rests on the principle that the plasma membrane maintains distinct intracellular and extracellular ionic compositions through selective permeability and active transport. The sodium‑potassium ATPase, discovered by Jens Christian Skou in 1957, hydrolyses one ATP to export three Na⁺ and import two K⁺, establishing the electrochemical gradients that underlie the resting membrane potential, typically –70 mV in neurons. When a stimulus depolarises the membrane to threshold, voltage‑gated Na⁺ channels open, producing a rapid influx of Na⁺ that generates the rising phase of the action potential; subsequent inactivation of Na⁺ channels and opening of voltage‑gated K⁺ channels repolarises the membrane. Synaptic transmission converts this electrical signal into a chemical one: arrival of an action potential at the presynaptic terminal triggers Ca²⁺ influx through voltage‑gated channels, prompting vesicle fusion and release of neurotransmitter into the cleft. Binding to postsynaptic receptors opens ligand‑gated ion channels, producing excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials that summate to determine whether the next neuron fires. This cascade illustrates how molecular events at the membrane scale up to integrated neural circuits.

6. Muscle Physiology

Muscle contraction is explained by the sliding filament theory, first articulated by Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke in 1954 and independently by Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson. In skeletal muscle, an action potential travels along the sarcolemma and down T‑tubules, triggering release of Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via ryanodine receptors. Calcium binds to troponin C, causing a conformational shift that moves tropomyosin away from the myosin‑binding sites on actin. Myosin heads, energised by ATP hydrolysis, attach to actin, undergo a power stroke, and detach upon binding a new ATP molecule, thereby sliding thin filaments past thick filaments and shortening the sarcomere. The force generated is proportional to the number of cross‑bridges cycling, which depends on the frequency of stimulation (temporal summation) and the number of motor units recruited (spatial summation). Cardiac muscle differs by having a prolonged action potential with a plateau phase due to L‑type Ca²⁺ channels, ensuring a refractory period longer than contraction and preventing tetanus. Smooth muscle relies on Ca²⁺‑calmodulin activation of myosin light‑chain kinase, a slower, more economical mechanism suited for sustained vascular tone.

7. Cardiovascular and Respiratory Physiology

The cardiovascular system functions as a closed hydraulic circuit driven by the heart, which operates as two serial pumps. The cardiac cycle consists of systole and diastole; during ventricular systole, pressure exceeds aortic and pulmonary pressures, opening the semilunar valves and ejecting stroke volume. Cardiac output (CO) equals stroke volume (SV) multiplied by heart rate (HR), a relationship first quantified by Adolf Fick in 1870 using the principle that oxygen consumption equals CO times arteriovenous oxygen difference. Blood pressure is regulated acutely by the baroreceptor reflex, which senses stretch in the carotid sinus and aortic arch and modulates sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow, and chronically by the renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system, which adjusts blood volume and vascular resistance. In the respiratory system, ventilation is achieved by cyclic changes in thoracic volume generated by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles; Boyle’s law dictates that an increase in volume lowers intrapulmonary pressure, drawing air in. Gas exchange across the alveolar‑capillary membrane follows Fick’s law of diffusion, where the rate is proportional to the surface area and partial pressure gradient and inversely proportional to membrane thickness. Oxygen is transported primarily bound to haemoglobin, whose sigmoidal dissociation curve reflects cooperative binding and is shifted by pH, CO₂, temperature, and 2,3‑bisphosphoglycerate (Bohr effect). Carbon dioxide is carried as bicarbonate (≈70 %), bound to haemoglobin (≈23 %), and dissolved in plasma (≈7 %). The respiratory centres in the medulla and pons integrate chemoreceptor input from central (CSF pH) and peripheral (carotid and aortic bodies) sensors to maintain arterial PO₂, PCO₂, and pH within narrow limits.

8. Renal, Endocrine and Reproductive Physiology

The kidneys filter approximately 180 L of plasma per day, a process governed by the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) equation GFR = Kf × (PGC – PBS – πGC), where Kf is the filtration coefficient, PGC the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, PBS the Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure, and πGC the glomerular capillary oncotic pressure. Autoregulation maintains GFR constant over a wide range of arterial pressures via the myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback. Tubular reabsorption reclaims the bulk of filtered water, Na⁺, glucose, amino acids, and bicarbonate, while secretion eliminates H⁺, K⁺, and organic acids. The counter‑current multiplier in the loop of Henle creates a medullary osmotic gradient that enables water reabsorption under antidiuretic hormone (ADH) control, concentrating urine up to 1200 mOsm/kg. Acid–base balance is defended by renal bicarbonate reabsorption and new bicarbonate generation via ammonium excretion, described quantitatively by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation pH = pKa + log([HCO₃⁻]/(0.03 × PaCO₂)). The endocrine system operates through hierarchical axes; the hypothalamic‑pituitary‑thyroid axis illustrates negative feedback: thyrotropin‑releasing hormone (TRH) stimulates thyroid‑stimulating hormone (TSH), which drives thyroid hormone (T₃/T₄) synthesis; rising T₃/T₄ then suppresses both TRH and TSH. The menstrual cycle exemplifies a coordinated endocrine rhythm: follicular phase estrogen rise triggers the LH surge, ovulation occurs, and the luteal phase progesterone prepares the endometrium; if implantation fails, corpus luteum regression withdraws steroid support, initiating menstruation. These integrated mechanisms demonstrate how homeostasis emerges from molecular, cellular, and organ‑level interactions.

Solved Example: Glomerular Filtration Rate Calculation
Question: A patient has a filtration coefficient (Kf) of 12.5 mL/min/mmHg, glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (PGC) of 45 mmHg, Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure (PBS) of 15 mmHg, and glomerular capillary oncotic pressure (πGC) of 30 mmHg. Calculate the glomerular filtration rate (GFR).
Solution: GFR = Kf × (PGC – PBS – πGC) = 12.5 × (45 – 15 – 30) = 12.5 × 0 = 0 mL/min. The net filtration pressure is zero, indicating that under these pressures filtration would cease; clinically this situation reflects severe afferent arteriolar constriction or elevated oncotic pressure, both of which reduce GFR dramatically.
Answer‑Writing Insight
When a physiology question asks for a quantitative relationship, write the governing equation first, define each variable with its units, substitute the given values, and state the physiological implication of the result. This structure shows the examiner that you understand both the mathematics and the underlying mechanism.

Biochemistry

9. Biomolecules and Enzymology

Biochemistry begins with the four major classes of biomolecules that constitute living matter. Carbohydrates, composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in a 1:2:1 ratio, serve as immediate energy sources (glucose), structural elements (cellulose, chitin), and recognition markers (glycoproteins). Lipids, defined by their hydrophobicity, include fatty acids, triglycerides, phospholipids, and steroids; the amphipathic nature of phospholipids drives spontaneous bilayer formation, the basis of all cellular membranes. Proteins are polymers of twenty α‑amino acids linked by peptide bonds; their primary sequence determines folding into secondary (α‑helix, β‑sheet), tertiary, and quaternary structures, which in turn dictate function as enzymes, transporters, receptors, and structural scaffolds. Nucleic acids — deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA) — store and transmit genetic information through complementary base pairing. Enzymes, mostly proteins, accelerate reactions by lowering activation energy; the Michaelis–Menten model describes the hyperbolic relationship between substrate concentration and reaction velocity, with Km reflecting substrate affinity and Vmax indicating catalytic capacity. Allosteric regulation, covalent modification (phosphorylation), and proteolytic activation provide rapid, reversible control of metabolic flux, exemplified by the activation of glycogen phosphorylase by phosphorylase kinase in response to glucagon.

10. Intermediary Metabolism

Central carbon metabolism channels glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids into a common pool of acetyl‑CoA, which enters the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) discovered by Hans Krebs in 1937. Glycolysis, occurring in the cytosol, converts one glucose into two pyruvate, yielding a net gain of two ATP and two NADH; the committed step is catalysed by phosphofructokinase‑1 (PFK‑1), allosterically activated by AMP and fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate enters mitochondria, is decarboxylated by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (regulated by phosphorylation), and the resulting acetyl‑CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate. The eight‑step citric acid cycle generates three NADH, one FADH₂, one GTP (or ATP), and two CO₂ per acetyl‑CoA. Electrons from NADH and FADH₂ flow through the electron transport chain (Complexes I–IV) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, creating a proton motive force that drives ATP synthase (Complex V) to produce approximately 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH₂. Gluconeogenesis, the synthesis of glucose from non‑carbohydrate precursors, bypasses three irreversible glycolytic steps via pyruvate carboxylase, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, fructose‑1,6‑bisphosphatase, and glucose‑6‑phosphatase, and is stimulated by glucagon and cortisol during fasting. Lipid metabolism includes β‑oxidation of fatty acids in the mitochondrial matrix, yielding acetyl‑CoA, NADH, and FADH₂, and ketogenesis in the liver when carbohydrate supply is low, producing acetoacetate and β‑hydroxybutyrate for extrahepatic tissues. Amino acid catabolism involves transamination, deamination, and entry of carbon skeletons into glycolytic or citric acid cycle intermediates, with nitrogen disposed of via the urea cycle in the liver, a pathway elucidated by Kurt Henseleit and Hans Krebs in 1932.

11. Molecular Biology and Gene Regulation

The flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA to protein, termed the central dogma, was formulated by Francis Crick in 1958. DNA replication is semi‑conservative, as demonstrated by Meselson and Stahl in 1958; the replication fork advances with leading‑strand synthesis continuous and lagging‑strand synthesis discontinuous, producing Okazaki fragments that are ligated by DNA ligase. Transcription in eukaryotes requires RNA polymerase II, general transcription factors, and promoter elements such as the TATA box; the primary transcript undergoes 5′ capping, 3′ polyadenylation, and splicing to remove introns, a process mediated by the spliceosome. Translation occurs on ribosomes, where messenger RNA codons are read in the 5′→3′ direction; initiator tRNAᵐᵉᵗ binds the start codon AUG in the P site, and elongation proceeds with aminoacyl‑tRNAs entering the A site, peptide bond formation catalysed by peptidyl‑transferase activity of the large ribosomal subunit, and translocation. Gene expression is regulated at multiple levels: chromatin remodeling and histone modifications (acetylation, methylation) alter DNA accessibility; transcription factors bind enhancers and silencers; microRNAs and long non‑coding RNAs post‑transcriptionally modulate mRNA stability and translation; and ubiquitin‑mediated proteolysis controls protein half‑life. The lac operon in Escherichia coli, described by François Jacob and Jacques Monod in 1961, remains the paradigm for inducible transcriptional control, illustrating how a repressor protein, an inducer (allolactose), and catabolite activator protein (CAP) integrate metabolic signals.

12. Nutrition and Clinical Biochemistry

Nutritional biochemistry links dietary intake to metabolic health. Vitamins function as coenzymes or antioxidants; for example, thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is essential for pyruvate dehydrogenase and α‑ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and its deficiency causes beriberi with cardiovascular and neurological manifestations. Niacin forms NAD⁺/NADP⁺, central to redox reactions; deficiency leads to pellagra characterised by dermatitis, diarrhoea, and dementia. Vitamin B₁₂ and folate are required for methionine synthase and thymidylate synthesis; deficiency produces megaloblastic anaemia and, in B₁₂ deficiency, subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord. Fat‑soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K have diverse roles: retinal in vision, calcitriol in calcium homeostasis, tocopherol as a lipid‑soluble antioxidant, and phylloquinone/menaquinone as cofactors for γ‑carboxylation of clotting factors. Minerals such as iron (heme synthesis), zinc (enzyme catalysis), and iodine (thyroid hormone) are required in trace amounts. Energy balance is governed by the first law of thermodynamics; basal metabolic rate (BMR) can be estimated by the Harris‑Benedict equation, and total energy expenditure adds activity thermogenesis and diet‑induced thermogenesis. Clinical biochemistry applies these principles: elevated serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) indicate hepatocellular injury; increased alkaline phosphatase and γ‑glutamyltransferase suggest cholestasis; serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) assess renal function; lipid profiles (total cholesterol, LDL‑C, HDL‑C, triglycerides) stratify cardiovascular risk; and glycated haemoglobin (HbA₁c) reflects average plasma glucose over the preceding 8–12 weeks, guiding diabetes management.

Pathology

13. Cell Injury, Adaptation and Death

Cell injury results when homeostatic mechanisms are overwhelmed by physical, chemical, infectious, or immunological insults. Reversible injury manifests as cellular swelling, fatty change, and loss of microvilli, reflecting ATP depletion and failure of ion pumps; if the stress persists, irreversible injury ensues, characterised by mitochondrial densities, nuclear pyknosis, karyorrhexis, and karyolysis, culminating in necrosis. Necrosis is an uncontrolled, inflammatory form of cell death with several morphological patterns: coagulative necrosis preserves tissue architecture (typical of ischemic infarction in heart, kidney, spleen), liquefactive necrosis digests tissue into pus (seen in bacterial abscesses and brain infarcts), caseous necrosis combines features of both (hallmark of tuberculosis), fat necrosis results from lipase action on adipose tissue (pancreatitis), and fibrinoid necrosis deposits immune complexes in vessel walls (immune vasculitis). Apoptosis, in contrast, is a genetically programmed, energy‑dependent process that eliminates unwanted cells without inflammation; it involves caspase activation, DNA fragmentation into nucleosomal ladders, membrane blebbing, and formation of apoptotic bodies that are phagocytosed. Cellular adaptation includes hypertrophy (increase in cell size, e.g., cardiac muscle in hypertension), hyperplasia (increase in cell number, e.g., endometrial proliferation under estrogen), atrophy (shrinkage due to disuse or denervation), and metaplasia (reversible change of one differentiated cell type to another, e.g., squamous metaplasia of bronchial epithelium in smokers). Understanding these patterns allows the pathologist to infer the aetiology, duration, and potential reversibility of tissue damage.

14. Inflammation, Repair and Neoplasia

Inflammation is the vascular and cellular response to injury, aimed at eliminating the cause and initiating repair. Acute inflammation develops within minutes to hours, characterised by vasodilation, increased permeability, and neutrophil emigration mediated by histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and chemokines such as IL‑8. The cardinal signs — rubor, calor, tumor, dolor, and functio laesa — reflect these vascular changes. Chronic inflammation persists for weeks to months, dominated by macrophages, lymphocytes, and plasma cells, and often results in granuloma formation when the inciting agent (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis) resists degradation. Repair follows inflammation and proceeds by regeneration (replacement by identical cells, possible in labile and stable tissues) or fibrosis (deposition of collagen by fibroblasts, forming a scar). Wound healing involves haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation (granulation tissue formation, angiogenesis, re‑epithelialisation), and remodelling (collagen cross‑linking, scar contraction). Neoplasia denotes abnormal, autonomous cell growth that persists after the stimulus ceases. Benign neoplasms are well‑differentiated, slow‑growing, encapsulated, and non‑invasive; malignant neoplasms exhibit anaplasia, rapid growth, invasion, and metastasis. Carcinogenesis is a multistep process involving initiation (irreversible DNA damage), promotion (clonal expansion of initiated cells), and progression (acquisition of additional mutations conferring invasiveness).
