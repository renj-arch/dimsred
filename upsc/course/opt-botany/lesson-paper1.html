Botany Paper I — Plant Diversity, Anatomy & Embryology

Microbiology

  • Bacteria: Structure (cell wall — peptidoglycan, capsule, pili, flagella, plasmid), nutrition (autotrophs/heterotrophs, photo/chemo), reproduction (binary fission, conjugation, transformation, transduction), economic importance (industrial — curd, cheese, antibiotics; agriculture — nitrogen fixation, decomposition; pathogens — tuberculosis, cholera, typhoid, cholera, tetanus).
  • Viruses: Structure (nucleic acid core, protein capsid, envelope in some), characteristics (obligate intracellular parasites, crystallizable), transmission, diseases (TMV, influenza, HIV, COVID-19, rabies, polio), bacteriophages (lytic & lysogenic cycles).
  • Mycoplasma: Smallest free-living organisms, no cell wall, pleomorphic, cause plant diseases (little leaf, witches' broom) & animal diseases.
  • Lichens: Symbiosis of fungus (mycobiont) & alga/cyanobacterium (photobiont), reproduction (soredia, isidia, apothecia), economic importance (pollution indicators, dye, litmus, perfume, medicinal — Usnea), ecological role (pioneer species in primary succession).
  • Fungi: Classification (based on thallus, reproduction, spore formation) — Phycomycetes (algal fungi, e.g., Rhizopus, Mucor), Ascomycetes (sac fungi, e.g., Aspergillus, Penicillium, Claviceps, morels, truffles), Basidiomycetes (club fungi, e.g., Agaricus, Ustilago, Puccinia), Deuteromycetes (fungi imperfecti — no sexual stage known, e.g., Alternaria, Colletotrichum). Economic importance — edible (Agaricus), medicinal (Penicillin from Penicillium), industrial (citric acid, enzymes), pathogens (wheat rust — Puccinia, late blight of potato — Phytophthora infestans, apple scab — Venturia), mycorrhiza (symbiotic association of fungi with roots — ectomycorrhiza & endomycorrhiza/VAM, benefits: nutrient uptake, drought resistance).

Plant Pathology

  • Symptoms: Necrosis, chlorosis, wilting, dwarfing, malformation, galls, cankers, rust, smut, blight, mildew, mosaic, leaf curl, damping off.
  • Disease cycle: Inoculum, penetration, infection, colonization, symptom production, dissemination, survival.
  • Major diseases: Rust of wheat (Puccinia graminis), smut of wheat (Ustilago), late blight of potato (Phytophthora infestans — Irish famine), early blight of potato (Alternaria), blast of rice (Magnaporthe oryzae), bacterial blight of rice (Xanthomonas oryzae), wilt of cotton/pigeonpea (Fusarium), root-knot (Meloidogyne nematode), mosaic viruses (tobacco mosaic virus — TMV, first virus discovered), little leaf of brinjal (mycoplasma).
  • Disease management: Cultural (crop rotation, sanitation, resistant varieties), biological (biocontrol agents — Trichoderma, Pseudomonas fluorescens), chemical (fungicides — protectants vs systemic, Bordeaux mixture, copper oxychloride, carbendazim, mancozeb), integrated disease management (IDM).

Cryptogams

  • Algae: Classification (based on pigments, flagella, reserve food) — Chlorophyceae (green — Chlamydomonas, Volvox, Spirogyra, Ulothrix, Chara), Phaeophyceae (brown — Ectocarpus, Laminaria, Fucus, Sargassum), Rhodophyceae (red — Polysiphonia, Gracilaria, Porphyra, Corallina), Xanthophyceae (yellow-green — Vaucheria), Bacillariophyceae (diatoms — Navicula, Nitzschia), Euglenophyceae (Euglena). Economic importance — food (Porphyra, Laminaria), fodder, biofuel, agar-agar (Gelidium, Gracilaria), carrageenan (Chondrus), alginates (Macrocystis, Laminaria), diatomite (diatom skeletons), sewage treatment, bioindicators.
  • Fungi: As above — economic importance & plant pathogens.
  • Bryophytes: Mosses (Funaria, Polytrichum, Sphagnum — peat moss), liverworts (Marchantia, Riccia, Anthoceros), hornworts. Life cycle — gametophyte dominant (haploid), sporophyte dependent. Economic importance — Sphagnum as peat & packing material, pollution indicators, soil formation (pioneers), Reaumuria. Economic importance of bryophytes: Sphagnum peat as fuel & horticulture, soil binding, indicators of pollution.
  • Pteridophytes: Classes — Psilopsida (Psilotum), Lycopsida (Selaginella, Lycopodium), Sphenopsida (Equisetum), Pteropsida (ferns — Pteris, Dryopteris, Adiantum, Marsilea). Life cycle — sporophyte dominant, heterospory (Selaginella, Salvinia, Marsilea) & origin of seed habit, alternation of generations, economic importance (Equisetum — scouring, Selaginella — resurrection plant, fossil fuels from ancient pteridophytes).

Phanerogams

  • Gymnosperms: Classification — Cycadopsida (Cycas), Coniferopsida (Pinus, Cedrus, Abies, Pinus, Ginkgo), Gnetopsida (Ephedra, Welwitschia, Gnetum). Structure & reproduction of Cycas, Pinus, Ephedra. Economic importance — timber (Pinus, Deodar, Cedar), resin & turpentine, paper, ornamental (Cycas), Ephedra (ephedrine — medicinal), Ginkgo (living fossil, medicine).
  • Angiosperms: Classification systems — Bentham & Hooker (natural system, 3 cohorts: Dicotyledones — Polypetalae, Gamopetalae, Monochlamydeae; Monocotyledones), Hutchinson (phylogenetic), APG (Angiosperm Phylogeny Group — modern, APG IV). Flower — structure (calyx, corolla, androecium, gynoecium), aestivation, placentation (marginal, axile, parietal, free central, basal, superficial), pollination (agents: wind, water, insects, birds, bats), fertilization — double fertilization, endosperm (nuclear, cellular, helobial types), fruit types (simple — drupe, berry, pome, hesperidium, caryopsis, achene, samara, nut; aggregate — etaerio of berries, drupes, follicles; multiple — sorosis of pineapple, mulberry, syconus of fig), seed structure (dicot — castor, mustard; monocot — maize, wheat, orchid), seed dormancy & germination (epigeal, hypogeal), viability.
  • Anatomy: Tissues — meristematic (apical, lateral, intercalary), permanent (simple — parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma — fibres & sclereids; complex — xylem: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, xylem parenchyma; phloem: sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem fibres, phloem parenchyma). Tissue systems — epidermal (epidermis, stomata, trichomes, cuticle), ground (cortex, pericycle, pith, medullary rays), vascular (conjoint — collateral, bicollateral, concentric; open vs closed). Anatomy of dicot & monocot stem & root (differences), dorsiventral & isobilateral leaf anatomy, C4 leaf anatomy (Kranz anatomy — bundle sheath cells with large chloroplasts, centripetal arrangement), secondary growth (cambium activity, annual rings, heartwood & sapwood, heartwood formation), anomalous secondary growth (Bignonia stem — phloem wedges, Dracaena stem — anomalous cambium, Boerhaavia/Dioscorea — anomalous root), anomalous structures (Bougainvillea stem — phloem wedges).
Technique: C4 Kranz anatomy
Question: Draw & label Kranz anatomy & state its function.
Answer: Kranz anatomy = cluster of bundle sheath cells surrounding vascular bundles, with large chloroplasts, centripetal arrangement, thick walls, no intercellular spaces; mesophyll cells between bundle sheaths. Function: concentrates CO2 around RuBisCO via PEP carboxylase in mesophyll → decarboxylation in bundle sheath → minimizes photorespiration in C4 plants (maize, sugarcane, sugarcane, sorghum, amaranthus).

Embryology

  • Microsporogenesis: Microsporocyte → meiosis → microspore tetrad → microspore → pollen grain (vegetative cell + generative cell → 2 male gametes). Pollen wall — exine (sporopollenin) & intine; apertures (apertures — colpate, porate, colporate); pollen viability & storage.
  • Megasporogenesis: Megaspore mother cell → meiosis → linear tetrad → functional megaspore (chalazal) → embryo sac development (Polygonum type — 7-celled, 8-nucleate: egg apparatus — egg cell + 2 synergids with filiform apparatus, central cell with 2 polar nuclei, 3 antipodals).
  • Fertilization: Pollen germination on stigma, pollen tube growth (chemotropic), entry into ovule (porogamy/chalazogamy/mesogamy), double fertilization (syngamy → zygote 2n; triple fusion → primary endosperm nucleus 3n), post-fertilization changes (ovule → seed, ovary → fruit, petals/stamens/sepal wither).
  • Endosperm: Types — nuclear (coconut liquid endosperm), cellular (Petunia), helobial (between); function — nourishment of embryo.
  • Embryo development: Dicot (Capsella type) — zygote → proembryo → globular → heart → torpedo → mature dicotyledonous embryo (2 cotyledons, plumule, radicle, hypocotyl); monocot (grass embryo — scutellum, coleoptile, plumule, radicle, coleorhiza). Polyembryony (Citrus — cleavage polyembryony, adventitious embryony), apomixis (agamospermy — adventitious embryony, diplospory, apospory, parthenogenesis), parthenocarpy (seedless fruit — banana, grapes, seedless watermelon — natural & induced by auxins/gibberellins).
Technique: Apomixis answer
Question: 'What is apomixis? Give its significance.'
Answer: Apomixis = seed formation without fertilization (diplospory, apospory, adventitious embryony). Significance: maintains hybrid vigour, rapid multiplication of elite genotypes, disease-free seed production (citrus, mango, Citrus), used in hybrid seed production (apomictic hybrids don't segregate), reduction in hybrid seed cost.

Palynology

  • Pollen morphology: Size, shape, symmetry (radial, bilateral), apertures (colpi — colporate, colpate, inaperturate/porate, sulcate), exine ornamentation (reticulate, foveolate, striate, psilate, echinate), pollen units (monad, dyad, tetrad, pollinia in orchids & Asclepiads), pollen viability, pollen banks.
  • Applications: Melissopalynology (pollen in honey — floral source), forensic palynology (crime scene linkage), palaeopalynology (vegetation & climate reconstruction), allergy & aerobiology (pollen allergy — Parthenium, Prosopis, grass pollen), honey quality & authentication.

Cellular Respiration: The Bioenergetic Logic of Metabolic Pathways

Understanding cellular respiration requires moving beyond the linear equation C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + Energy to appreciate the compartmentalised machinery of the cell. The process is strictly aerobic in higher plants and animals, occurring across three distinct stages: Glycolysis, the Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle), and the Electron Transport Chain (ETC) coupled with oxidative phosphorylation.

Glycolysis, the universal pathway found in all living organisms, occurs in the cytoplasm. It involves the breakdown of a six-carbon glucose molecule into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. This stage is an anaerobic investment requiring 2 ATP and yielding 4 ATP (net gain: 2 ATP) along with 2 NADH. The enzyme hexokinase initiates the process by phosphorylating glucose, trapping it within the cell. Under anaerobic conditions in plants, pyruvate is converted to ethanol and C2H5OH (alcohol fermentation) via alcohol dehydrogenase, a mechanism critical for survival during waterlogging (hypoxia). In contrast, lactic acid fermentation occurs in some plant tissues like roots or germinating seeds.

The subsequent stages require oxygen. Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion where it undergoes oxidative decarboxylation to form Acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2. The Krebs Cycle takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. Here, Acetyl-CoA is oxidised completely to CO2, generating 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 GTP (or ATP) per turn. Since one glucose yields two pyruvates, the cycle turns twice per glucose molecule.

The final stage is the Electron Transport Chain located in the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae). NADH and FADH2 donate high-energy electrons to Complex I and II respectively. These electrons flow through a series of protein complexes (I, III, IV) and mobile carriers (Ubiquinone, Cytochrome c), finally reducing oxygen to water at Complex IV. This electron flow pumps protons (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient known as the proton motive force. ATP Synthase (Complex V) allows protons to flow back into the matrix, driving the synthesis of ATP from ADP and Pi. This process is termed oxidative phosphorylation. The theoretical yield is approximately 38 ATP per glucose in older textbooks, though modern estimates based on P/O ratios suggest ~30-32 ATP due to transport costs.

Examiner's trap
Question: "Does glycolysis occur in the mitochondria?" Answer: No. This is a classic anatomical error. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm (cytosol). The link reaction and Krebs cycle occur in the matrix; ETC occurs on the inner membrane. Confusing these locations leads to loss of marks even if the chemical equations are correct.
Exam-Day Strategy: Diagrammatic Precision
When asked to "explain respiration," do not just write text. Draw a simplified diagram of the mitochondrion showing the matrix (Krebs), inner membrane folds (ETC), and cytoplasm (Glycolysis). Label NADH/FADH2 production points. This visualizes your understanding of compartmentalisation, which is a high-weightage concept in Paper I Physiology.

Photosynthesis: C3, C4, and CAM Strategies for Carbon Fixation

The core challenge in photosynthesis is the inefficiency of RuBisCO, the enzyme that fixes CO2. RuBisCO has an affinity for O2 as well as CO2, leading to photorespiration—a wasteful process where oxygen is fixed instead of carbon, releasing previously fixed CO2 and consuming ATP. To overcome this, plants have evolved distinct anatomical and biochemical strategies.

C3 Plants (e.g., wheat, rice, soybean) rely solely on the Calvin Cycle. RuBisCO operates directly in mesophyll cells. Photorespiration is high in C3 plants, reducing efficiency by up to 50% under hot, dry conditions where stomata close.

C4 Plants (e.g., maize, sugarcane, sorghum) evolved a spatial separation mechanism. They possess Kranz anatomy: bundle sheath cells are large, chloroplast-rich, and lack intercellular spaces; mesophyll cells surround them. Mesophyll cells fix CO2 into oxaloacetate (4C) using PEP carboxylase (which has high affinity for CO2 and no oxygenase activity). This is converted to malate or aspartate, transported to bundle sheath cells, decarboxylated to release CO2, which then enters the Calvin Cycle. This concentrates CO2 around RuBisCO, eliminating photorespiration. C4 plants are more efficient at high temperatures and low light but require more nitrogen for the additional enzymatic machinery.

CAM Plants (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) like cacti and pineapples use temporal separation. Stomata open at night to fix CO2 into organic acids (malic acid) stored in vacuoles. During the day, stomata close to conserve water; malic acid is decarboxylated to release CO2 for the Calvin Cycle. This is an adaptation to extreme arid environments.

Examiner's trap
Question: "Do C4 plants have Kranz anatomy?" Answer: Yes, this is a defining feature. However, the reverse is not always true; some monocots with scattered vascular bundles might show variations. The critical distinction to remember for exams is that only C4 plants among angiosperms exhibit Kranz anatomy.

Plant Anatomy: Secondary Growth and Wood Formation

Secondary growth in dicotyledonous stems and roots is driven by two lateral meristems: the vascular cambium and the cork cambium (phellogen). The vascular cambium originates from procambial strands within vascular bundles (fascicular cambium) and from interfascicular parenchyma (interfascicular cambium), forming a continuous cylinder. Its periclinal divisions produce secondary xylem (wood) toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside. In temperate species, the activity of the vascular cambium is seasonal, generating distinct growth rings. Early wood (spring wood) consists of wide‑lumened vessels and tracheids with thin walls, optimized for hydraulic conductivity. Late wood (summer wood) contains narrower, thick‑walled elements that provide mechanical support. The transition between early and late wood is often abrupt in ring‑porous species (e.g., Quercus) and gradual in diffuse‑porous species (e.g., Acer).

Heartwood formation results from the programmed death of older xylem cells and the deposition of extractives such as phenolics, terpenes, and tyloses that block vessels, conferring decay resistance and darker colour. Sapwood remains physiologically active in water transport. Reaction wood develops in response to gravitational stress: tension wood on the upper side of leaning angiosperm stems is rich in gelatinous (G‑) fibers with a cellulose‑rich inner layer, while compression wood on the lower side of gymnosperm stems shows rounded tracheids with high lignin content and a prominent S2 layer. The cork cambium arises in the cortex or secondary phloem and produces phellem (cork) outward and phelloderm inward. Cork cells are suberised, providing a protective barrier against water loss and pathogen entry. Lenticels, formed by loosely arranged complementary cells, permit gas exchange.

Anatomical markers such as the presence of storied (stratified) cambium, ray heterogeneity (uniseriate vs. multiseriate), and the type of perforation plates (simple vs. scalariform) are diagnostic in wood identification. The ratio of vessel diameter to frequency influences hydraulic efficiency versus safety trade‑offs, a concept frequently tested in applied physiology questions. Examiners often ask candidates to distinguish between ring‑porous and diffuse‑porous wood based on cross‑sectional diagrams, and to explain the functional significance of tyloses in heartwood durability.

Plant Physiology: Hormonal Regulation of Growth and Development

Plant hormones (phytohormones) operate at micromolar concentrations and exhibit extensive cross‑talk. Auxins (primarily indole‑3‑acetic acid, IAA) are synthesized in the shoot apex and young leaves, transported basipetally via PIN‑formed (PIN) efflux carriers. They promote cell elongation by activating plasma‑membrane H⁺‑ATPases, acidifying the apoplast, and inducing expansin‑mediated wall loosening. Auxin also regulates apical dominance, root initiation, and vascular differentiation. Gibberellins (GAs) are diterpenoid acids that stimulate stem elongation, seed germination (by inducing α‑amylase in the aleurone layer), and flowering in long‑day plants. The DELLA proteins act as repressors of GA signalling; GA binding to its receptor GID1 triggers DELLA degradation via the SCF^SLY1 ubiquitin ligase.

Cytokinins (e.g., zeatin) are adenine derivatives synthesized in roots and transported acropetally. They promote cell division (cytokinesis), delay senescence, and interact antagonistically with auxin in the control of shoot‑root balance. The two‑component histidine‑kinase receptor (AHK) initiates a phosphorelay that activates type‑B response regulators, which induce type‑A regulators as negative feedback. Abscisic acid (ABA) accumulates under water stress, inducing stomatal closure via guard‑cell anion channels (SLAC1) and activating stress‑responsive genes through the PYR/PYL/RCAR‑PP2C‑SnRK2 cascade. Ethylene, a gaseous hydrocarbon, is synthesized from ACC by ACC oxidase; its perception involves the ER‑localized receptors (ETR1, EIN4) that, in the absence of ethylene, activate CTR1 kinase to suppress the EIN2‑EIN3 transcription cascade.

Hormonal interactions are context‑dependent. For instance, auxin up‑regulates GA biosynthesis genes (GA3ox) while GA enhances auxin transport. Cytokinin and auxin ratios determine organogenesis in tissue culture: high auxin/cytokinin favours root formation, high cytokinin/auxin favours shoot formation. ABA and ethylene often synergise in stress responses but antagonise in seed dormancy release. The concept of “hormonal crosstalk” is a favourite examiner theme; questions may present a mutant phenotype (e.g., dwarfism, delayed senescence) and ask which signalling component is disrupted. Knowledge of the canonical pathways, key mutants (e.g., Arabidopsis pin1, gai, ctr1), and the direction of regulation is essential for high‑scoring answers.

Examiner's trap
A transgenic Arabidopsis line overexpresses a cytokinin oxidase gene, resulting in reduced cytokinin levels. Predict the phenotypic consequences on shoot apical meristem activity, leaf senescence, and root architecture, and explain the underlying hormonal cross‑talk.
Reduced cytokinin leads to a smaller shoot apical meristem because cytokinin promotes cell division via the WUSCHEL‑CLAVATA feedback loop; thus, fewer stem cells are maintained. Leaf senescence is accelerated because cytokinin normally delays senescence by up‑regulating chlorophyll‑binding proteins and inhibiting ethylene synthesis; its deficiency removes this brake. Root architecture shows increased primary root length and reduced lateral root density because auxin‑cytokinin antagonism shifts toward auxin dominance in the root, enhancing basipetal auxin flow that stimulates primary root elongation while suppressing lateral root initiation. The answer must reference the two‑component signalling cascade (AHK‑AHP‑ARR) and the auxin‑cytokinin ratio concept.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for “hormonal regulation” of a process, structure the answer as a table: Hormone → Biosynthesis site → Transport direction → Primary receptor → Key downstream component → Phenotypic output. This format forces you to include all mechanistic steps examiners reward and prevents omission of cross‑talk points.

Plant Taxonomy: Modern Phylogenetic Systems and Molecular Markers

The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) classification, now in its fourth iteration (APG IV, 2016), organizes flowering plants into orders and families based on robust molecular phylogenies. The backbone relies on concatenated chloroplast genes (rbcL, matK, ndhF) and nuclear ribosomal ITS (internal transcribed spacer) sequences. Chloroplast genomes are maternally inherited in most angiosperms, providing a clear maternal lineage signal, while nuclear markers capture biparental history and can reveal hybridisation events. The core eudicots are defined by the “pentapetalae” clade, characterized by a duplicated CYCLOIDEA‑like gene (CYC2) controlling floral symmetry. Monocots are distinguished by a single cotyledon, parallel venation, and a characteristic loss of the rpoA gene from the plastome.

DNA barcoding for plants uses a two‑locus standard: rbcL + matK (CBOL Plant Working Group, 2009). rbcL offers high universality and easy alignment but limited species‑level resolution; matK provides greater variability but can be difficult to amplify in some groups. Supplementary loci such as trnH‑psbA intergenic spacer and nuclear ITS are employed for problematic taxa (e.g., recently radiated genera). Phylogenomic approaches now routinely employ target enrichment (Hyb‑Seq) of hundreds of low‑copy nuclear genes, enabling resolution of deep nodes (e.g., the placement of Ceratophyllum) and detection of incomplete lineage sorting.

Taxonomic revisions driven by molecular data include the expansion of the family Amaranthaceae to include Chenopodiaceae, the recognition of the order Brassicales as monophyletic with the glucosinolate pathway as a synapomorphy, and the splitting of the polyphyletic “Liliaceae” into multiple families (e.g., Asparagaceae, Melanthiaceae). Examiners test the ability to interpret phylogenetic trees: identifying clades, recognizing bootstrap support values, and distinguishing between homology and homoplasy. A common twist is to present a tree with a known long‑branch attraction artefact and ask how taxon sampling or model selection could mitigate it.

Plant Genetics: Cytogenetics, Polyploidy and Chromosome Mapping

Cytogenetics integrates chromosome morphology with genetic analysis. Karyotyping reveals chromosome number, size, centromere position (metacentric, submetacentric, acrocentric, telocentric), and secondary constrictions (nucleolar organizing regions). Banding techniques (C‑banding for heterochromatin, Q‑banding for AT‑rich regions, G‑banding after trypsin‑Giemsa) allow precise identification of homologous chromosomes. Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) uses labelled DNA probes (e.g., 45S rDNA, 5S rDNA, centromeric repeats) to map specific loci onto chromosomes, revealing physical distances that complement genetic linkage maps.

Polyploidy is a major evolutionary force in plants. Autopolyploidy arises from genome duplication within a species (e.g., tetraploid potato, Solanum tuberosum, 2n=4x=48), leading to multivalent formation at meiosis and polysomic inheritance. Allopolyploidy combines divergent genomes from hybridisation followed by chromosome doubling (e.g., wheat, Triticum aestivum, 2n=6x=42, AABBDD). Allopolyploids typically show disomic inheritance because homologous chromosomes pair preferentially (preferential pairing). The “genome dominance” phenomenon, where one subgenome retains more genes and higher expression, is documented in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) and Brassica napus.

Chromosome mapping historically used three‑point test crosses to determine gene order and recombination frequencies (centiMorgans). Modern high‑density linkage maps employ SNP arrays or genotyping‑by‑sequencing (GBS) to place thousands of markers. Comparative mapping across related species (e.g., rice‑sorghum‑maize) reveals conserved synteny blocks and chromosomal rearrangements (inversions, translocations). The concept of “recombination hotspots” and “cold regions” (centromeric heterochromatin) is critical for marker‑assisted selection because markers in low‑recombination zones may not segregate independently of target genes. Examiners often ask to calculate map distances from progeny data, interpret FISH patterns for chromosome identification, or explain the meiotic behaviour of an allopolyploid with a known genome constitution.

Plant Breeding and Biotechnology: Marker‑Assisted Selection and Genome Editing

Marker‑assisted selection (MAS) accelerates breeding by using DNA markers tightly linked to target traits. The workflow: (1) identify quantitative trait loci (QTL) via biparental mapping or genome‑wide association studies (GWAS); (2) validate markers in diverse germplasm; (3) deploy diagnostic markers (e.g., KASP assays) for foreground selection; (4) combine with background selection using genome‑wide markers to recover the recurrent parent genome rapidly. Successful MAS examples include the Sub1A QTL for submergence tolerance in rice, the Rht‑B1b/Rht‑D1b dwarfing alleles in wheat, and the Xa21 bacterial blight resistance gene in rice.

Genome editing, particularly CRISPR‑Cas9, enables targeted mutagenesis, gene replacement, and transcriptional regulation. The system requires a guide RNA (gRNA) complementary to a target sequence adjacent to a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM, NGG for SpCas9). Off‑target effects are minimised by using high‑fidelity Cas9 variants (eSpCas9, HiFi Cas9) and careful gRNA design. Base editors (cytosine or adenine deaminases fused to dead Cas9) achieve single‑base conversions without double‑strand breaks. Prime editing extends versatility by using a pegRNA to direct insertions, deletions, or all twelve possible base‑to‑base changes.

Regulatory frameworks differ globally: some jurisdictions treat edited plants lacking foreign DNA as non‑GM, while others regulate the process. Breeders must document the absence of transgene integration (segregation of Cas9/gRNA cassette) and confirm edits by Sanger or amplicon sequencing. Trait stacking via multiplexed gRNA arrays allows simultaneous modification of multiple genes (e.g., knocking out three susceptibility genes for powdery mildew resistance in wheat). Examiners test understanding of the difference between MAS (selection) and genome editing (creation of novel alleles), the design of gRNA for specificity, and the interpretation of segregation ratios in edited lines (Mendelian 1:2:1 for heterozygous T0 plants).

Nitrogen Assimilation and Sulfur Metabolism in Higher Plants

Higher plants acquire nitrogen mainly as nitrate (NO₃⁻) or ammonium (NH₄⁺) from soil. Nitrate enters root cells via NRT1 (low‑affinity) and NRT2 (high‑affinity) transporters, then is reduced to nitrite by cytosolic nitrate reductase (NR) using NAD(P)H. Nitrite moves to plastids where ferredoxin‑dependent nitrite reductase (NiR) produces ammonium. Ammonium, from nitrate reduction or direct uptake via AMT transporters, is assimilated through the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase (GS/GOGAT) cycle: GS catalyses ATP‑dependent condensation of NH₄⁺ with glutamate to form glutamine; GOGAT (ferredoxin‑dependent in plastids, NADH‑dependent in cytosol) transfers the amide group to 2‑oxoglutarate, yielding two glutamate molecules. This cycle operates in photosynthetic and non‑photosynthetic tissues, regulated by light (ferredoxin), carbon status (2‑oxoglutarate), and feedback inhibition by glutamine.

Sulfur is taken up as sulfate (SO₄²⁻) by SULTR transporters (SULTR1;1/1;2 high‑affinity in roots, SULTR2;1 low‑affinity in vasculature). In plastids, ATP sulfurylase activates sulfate to adenosine‑5′‑phosphosulfate (APS). APS reductase reduces APS to sulfite using glutathione; sulfite reductase (SiR) reduces sulfite to sulfide with ferredoxin. Sulfide combines with O‑acetylserine (from serine acetyltransferase) via O‑acetylserine(thiol)lyase (OASTL) to form cysteine. Cysteine is precursor for methionine (via cystathionine γ‑synthase and β‑lyase) and glutathione (γ‑glutamylcysteine synthetase then glutathione synthetase). Glutathione acts as redox buffer, APS reductase cofactor, and phytochelatin substrate for heavy‑metal detoxification.

Cross‑talk between N and S metabolism is evident: glutathione links the pathways; sulfur deficiency down‑regulates NR expression, while nitrogen limitation reduces sulfate transporter transcription. The transcription factor SLIM1 activates SULTR genes under S‑deficiency; NIN‑LIKE PROTEINS (NLPs) mediate nitrate‑responsive expression. Understanding these regulatory nodes is essential for engineering nutrient‑use‑efficient crops.

Abiotic Stress Signaling: ROS, Calcium, and MAPK Cascades

Environmental stresses (salinity, drought, extreme temperature, heavy metals) trigger rapid ROS production — superoxide (O₂⁻·), H₂O₂, hydroxyl radical (·OH) — mainly in chloroplasts, mitochondria, peroxisomes, and apoplast. ROS act as secondary messengers; their spatiotemporal signatures are decoded by antioxidant systems (SOD, CAT, APX, GR) and redox‑sensitive proteins. Concurrently, a transient cytosolic Ca²⁺ elevation (“calcium signature”) arises via plasma‑membrane channels (CNGCs, GLRs) and internal release from vacuole/ER (TPC1, IP₃‑gated channels). Ca²⁺ sensors include calmodulin (CaM), calcineurin B‑like proteins (CBLs), and Ca²⁺‑dependent protein kinases (CDPKs). CBLs interact with CBL‑interacting protein kinases (CIPKs) forming the CBL‑CIPK network, regulating transporters like SOS1 (Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter) and AKT1 (K⁺ channel).

MAPK cascades amplify signals downstream of ROS and Ca²⁺. A typical module comprises a MAPKKK (e.g., MEKK1), MAPKK (e.g., MKK2), and MAPK (e.g., MPK4/MPK6). In salt stress, the SOS pathway exemplifies this: SOS3 (CBL) binds Ca²⁺, recruits SOS2 (CIPK), and the complex phosphorylates/activates SOS1 at the plasma membrane, extruding Na⁺. In drought, ABA‑activated SnRK2 kinases phosphorylate AREB/ABF transcription factors, inducing RD29A, RD29B, and LEA genes. Cold stress activates the ICE1‑CBF/DREB1 regulon; ICE1 is stabilized by SUMOylation and activates CBF promoters. MAPKs also phosphorylate transcription factors (e.g., WRKY33) and metabolic enzymes, linking perception to gene expression and metabolic reprogramming.

Worked Example: SOS Pathway Analysis
Explain how the SOS pathway mediates salt tolerance in Arabidopsis, and predict the phenotype of a sos2 loss‑of‑function mutant under 150 mM NaCl.
The SOS pathway is a Ca²⁺‑dependent signaling module. High external NaCl triggers a rapid cytosolic Ca²⁺ spike. The Ca²⁺ sensor SOS3 (a calcineurin B‑like protein) binds Ca²⁺ and recruits the serine/threonine kinase SOS2 (a CIPK). The SOS3‑SOS2 complex translocates to the plasma membrane where it phosphorylates the Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter SOS1 at its C‑terminal autoinhibitory domain, activating Na⁺ efflux. Simultaneously, SOS2 phosphorylates the vacuolar Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger NHX1, enhancing vacuolar sequestration. In a sos2 knockout, the kinase cannot be activated by SOS3, so SOS1 remains inactive and NHX1 is not phosphorylated. Consequently, Na⁺ accumulates in the cytosol, causing ion toxicity, growth arrest, chlorosis, and eventual death at 150 mM NaCl. The mutant shows a severe salt‑sensitive phenotype, whereas overexpression of SOS2 confers enhanced tolerance.

Meristem Identity Genes and Floral Organ Specification

The shoot apical meristem (SAM) maintains pluripotent stem cells via a feedback loop: WUSCHEL (WUS) in the organizing centre and the CLAVATA (CLV3/CLV1‑CLV2) peptide‑receptor system restricting the WUS domain. Photoperiod and vernalization pathways converge on FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), a mobile florigen, triggering reproductive transition. FT interacts with FD in the SAM to activate floral identity genes LEAFY (LFY) and APETALA1 (AP1). LFY and AP1 specify the floral meristem and repress TFL1, which maintains inflorescence indeterminacy.

Floral organ identity follows the ABCDE model, refined as a quartet model of MADS‑box complexes. Class A (AP1, AP2) specifies sepals; A+B (AP3, PI) specifies petals; B+C (AG) specifies stamens; C alone specifies carpels; D (STK, SHP) with C specifies ovules; E (SEP1‑4) proteins act as “glue” for tetrameric complexes in all organs. The quartet model predicts each whorl is defined by a distinct MADS‑box tetramer (e.g., AP1‑SEP3‑AP3‑PI for petals). SEP mutations convert all floral organs to leaf‑like structures, confirming their central role.

Regulatory layers include miRNA control: miR172 targets AP2‑like genes to promote flowering and organ identity; miR156/SPL regulates juvenile‑to‑adult transition. In rice, orthologs OsMADS14/15 (AP1), OsMADS16 (SEP), and OsMADS3 (AG) function similarly, but B‑class genes (e.g., OsMADS2, OsMADS4) show subfunctionalization. Understanding these networks enables flower architecture manipulation for hybrid seed production and ornamental breeding.

Chemotaxonomy and Numerical Taxonomy in Angiosperm Classification

Chemotaxonomy uses secondary metabolite distributions — alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolics, glucosinolates — as phylogenetic markers. These compounds are often lineage‑specific because their biosynthetic pathways involve enzyme families diversifying via gene duplication and neofunctionalization. For example, pyrrolizidine alkaloids characterize many Boraginaceae and Asteraceae (Senecioneae), while betalains replace anthocyanins in Caryophyllales (except Caryophyllaceae). Flavonoid profiles (e.g., flavone C‑glycosides in Poaceae, anthocyanidin 3‑O‑glycosides in Lamiaceae) resolved by HPLC‑DAD‑MS build chemical distance matrices.

Numerical taxonomy converts chemical data into similarity coefficients (Jaccard, Dice, Euclidean) and clusters taxa via UPGMA, neighbor‑joining, or Ward’s method. PCA and NMDS visualize chemical space. Combined with DNA data (total evidence), chemotaxonomic characters often resolve recent radiations where molecular markers show incomplete lineage sorting. In Lamiaceae, rosmarinic acid and diterpene profiles support Nepetoideae monophyly. In Solanaceae, steroidal alkaloid distributions (solasodine, tomatidine) support the Solanoideae/Cestroideae split.

Limitations include convergent metabolite evolution (e.g., caffeine in Coffea, Camellia, Paullinia) and environmental plasticity. Chemotaxonomic conclusions must be tested against robust molecular phylogenies. Modern workflows integrate untargeted metabolomics (LC‑HRMS) with phylogenomics, identifying biosynthetic gene clusters underlying chemotaxonomic markers.

Epigenetic Regulation of Gene Expression in Plant Development

Epigenetic mechanisms modulate gene activity without DNA sequence changes, providing plasticity in development and stress adaptation. Plant DNA methylation occurs in three contexts: CG, CHG, CHH (H = A/T/C). CG methylation is maintained by MET1 during replication; CHG methylation by CMT3 guided by H3K9me2; CHH methylation requires the RdDM pathway: 24‑nt siRNAs from DCL3 guide AGO4 to scaffold transcripts, recruiting DRM2 for de novo methylation.

Histone modifications form a second layer. Histone acetylation (H3K9ac, H3K27ac) by HATs correlates with activation; deacetylation by HDACs represses transcription. Histone methylation can be activating (H3K4me3 by ATX1/ATX2) or repressive (H3K27me3 by PRC2 components CLF, SWN, EMF2). Vernalization in Arabidopsis exemplifies epigenetic memory: prolonged cold induces PRC2‑mediated H3K27me3 at FLC, stably silencing it to permit flowering. This silencing is mitotically inherited but reset each generation.

Small RNAs (21‑24 nt) mediate post‑transcriptional and transcriptional silencing. miRNAs (e.g., miR156, miR172, miR396) regulate developmental transitions by targeting SPL, AP2‑like, and GRF transcription factors. Trans‑acting siRNAs (tasiRNAs) from TAS loci amplify silencing. Epigenetic reprogramming occurs during gametogenesis/early embryogenesis: DME‑mediated demethylation in the central cell establishes maternal imprinting; paternal genome retains methylation. Paramutation (heritable allele‑to‑allele epigenetic communication) is documented at maize b1 and r1 loci. CRISPR‑based epigenome editing (dCas9‑TET1, dCas9‑p300, dCas9‑KRAB) enables targeted methylation/acetylation for functional studies and crop improvement.
