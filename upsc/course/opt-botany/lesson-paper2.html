Botany Paper II — Plant Physiology, Biochemistry, Genetics & Ecology

Plant Physiology

  • Water relations: Water potential (Ψw = Ψs + Ψp), osmosis, diffusion, imbibition, plasmolysis & deplasmolysis, aquaporins, water absorption (active & passive), transpiration (mechanism — cohesion-tension theory, stomatal opening/closing — K+ pump theory, anti-transpirants), guttation.
  • Photosynthesis: Light reaction (PSI & PSII, non-cyclic & cyclic photophosphorylation, Z-scheme), dark reaction (C3 cycle — Calvin cycle: carboxylation by RuBisCO, reduction, regeneration), photorespiration (C2 cycle — wasteful), C4 pathway (Kranz anatomy, PEP carboxylase, mesophyll & bundle sheath cells), CAM plants (stomata open at night, succulents), factors affecting photosynthesis (light, CO2, temperature, water, mineral nutrition), limiting factors (law of limiting factors — Blackman).
  • Respiration: Glycolysis (cytoplasm, glucose → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH), Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix, pyruvate → CO2 + NADH + FADH2 + ATP/GTP), oxidative phosphorylation (ETC — oxidative phosphorylation, chemiosmosis, ATP synthase), fermentation (alcoholic & lactic acid), RQ (respiratory quotient — carb 1.0, fat & protein < 1), pentose phosphate pathway.
  • Mineral nutrition: Essential elements (17 essential: C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S — macronutrients; Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, Mo, B, Cl, Ni, Mo — micronutrients), functions & deficiency symptoms (chlorosis, necrosis), hydroponics, critical concentration, nitrogen cycle & biological nitrogen fixation (Rhizobium — symbiotic in legume root nodules, Azotobacter — free living, Azolla — cyanobacteria), nitrification (Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter), denitrification.
  • Plant growth & development: Phytohormones — auxin (IAA — cell elongation, apical dominance, tropisms), gibberellin (stem elongation, seed germination, bolting), cytokinin (cell division, delay senescence), ethylene (fruit ripening, abscission, triple response), abscisic acid (ABA — stress hormone, stomatal closure, seed dormancy), florigen (flowering signal), photoperiodism (short-day, long-day, day-neutral plants, critical day length), vernalization (cold treatment induces flowering — wheat, vernalization genes), plant movements (tropic — phototropism, geotropism, hydrotropism, thigmotropism; nastic — nyctinasty, seismonasty), senescence & abscission (role of ABA & ethylene).

Biochemistry

  • Carbohydrates: Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose), disaccharides (sucrose, maltose, lactose), polysaccharides (starch — amylose & amylopectin, glycogen, cellulose, chitin), functions (energy, structure).
  • Proteins: Amino acids (20, essential & non-essential), peptide bond, levels of structure (primary, secondary — α-helix & β-pleated sheet, tertiary, quaternary), denaturation, functions (enzymes, transport, structural, contractile, storage, hormonal, protective).
  • Lipids: Fatty acids (saturated & unsaturated), triglycerides, phospholipids (lecithin, cephalin), glycolipids, sterols (cholesterol, phytosterols), functions (energy storage, membrane structure, insulation, hormones).
  • Nucleic acids: DNA (double helix, Watson-Crick model, base pairing A=T, G≡C, semiconservative replication — Meselson-Stahl experiment), RNA (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA), genetic code (triplet, degenerate, unambiguous, universal, non-overlapping), transcription, translation.
  • Enzymes: Classification (6 major classes — oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases), mechanism (lock & key, induced fit), factors affecting enzyme activity (temperature, pH, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, inhibitors — competitive & non-competitive, activators), cofactors & coenzymes, allosteric regulation, feedback inhibition.
  • Secondary metabolites: Alkaloids (morphine, quinine, nicotine, caffeine — defense & medicinal), terpenoids (rubber, camphor, carotenoids — vision, pigment), flavonoids & phenolics (anthocyanins — color, tannins — astringency, UV protection), functions (defense against herbivores & pathogens, UV protection, signaling).

Genetics

  • Mendelian genetics: Monohybrid cross (3:1 F2), dihybrid cross (9:3:3:1 F2), law of segregation, law of independent assortment (exceptions — linkage). Test cross & back cross.
  • Linkage & crossing over: Linked genes (same chromosome), coupling & repulsion, crossing over during prophase I (chiasma formation), recombination frequency & genetic mapping (map units / morgan), Sturtevant's mapping.
  • Gene interaction: Epistasis (dominant — 12:3:1, recessive — 9:3:4, duplicate — 15:1, complementary — 9:7, supplementary — 9:3:4), polygenic inheritance (quantitative traits — skin color, height, yield — normal distribution, multiple genes + environment).
  • Multiple alleles: ABO blood groups (IA, IB, i), Rh factor.
  • Cytoplasmic inheritance: Mitochondrial & chloroplast DNA (maternal inheritance), rho (ρ) in Chlamydomonas, poky in Neurospora.
  • Mutation: Spontaneous & induced (mutagens — physical: X-rays, UV, gamma rays; chemical: EMS, mustard gas; chromosomal aberrations — deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation; aneuploidy — monosomy (2n-1), trisomy (2n+1, Down syndrome in humans), euploidy — haploidy, diploidy, polyploidy (autopolyploidy — colchicine induced, allopolyploidy — Triticale, Raphanobrassica).
  • Molecular genetics: DNA structure (Watson-Crick double helix, antiparallel, semiconservative replication — Meselson-Stahl), transcription (RNA polymerase, promoter, terminator), translation (ribosome, mRNA, tRNA, anticodon, initiation/elongation/termination), genetic code (triplet code, 64 codons, 3 stop codons, 1 start codon AUG, degenerate, unambiguous, universal, non-overlapping, commaless), gene regulation — lac operon (Jacob & Monod — structural genes lacZ, lacY, lacA, operator, promoter, repressor, inducer lactose), trp operon (repressible).
Technique: Lac operon answer
Question: 'Explain lac operon regulation in E. coli.'
Answer: lac operon = structural genes lacZ (β-galactosidase), lacY (permease), lacA (transacetylase), controlled by promoter (RNA polymerase binding), operator (repressor binding), regulator gene lacI (repressor protein). Without lactose: repressor binds operator → blocks transcription. With lactose (allolactose): inducer binds repressor → repressor released → transcription occurs. CAP-cAMP complex enhances transcription when glucose low (positive regulation).

Plant Breeding

  • Objectives: Higher yield, disease/pest resistance, drought/frost tolerance, quality (protein, oil, vitamin content), shorter duration, synchronous maturity, non-shattering, responsive to fertilizers.
  • Germplasm: Collection, evaluation, conservation (in-situ — national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves; ex-situ — seed banks, gene banks, field gene banks, cryopreservation, DNA banks), centers of origin (Vavilov's centers — 8 centers: Chinese, Indian, Central Asiatic, Near Eastern, Mediterranean, Abyssinian, South American Mexican & Chilean, South American Brazilian-Paraguayan). India is secondary center for rice, mango, citrus, banana, pigeonpea, black pepper, ginger, turmeric, cardamom.
  • Breeding methods: Self-pollinated crops — mass selection, pure line selection (Johannsen), pedigree method, bulk method, backcross method (transfer of single gene), pedigree vs bulk. Cross-pollinated crops — mass selection, recurrent selection, synthetic varieties, composite varieties, hybrid varieties (inbred lines, combining ability — GCA & SCA, heterosis, CMS — cytoplasmic male sterility for hybrid seed production), mutation breeding (mutagens — physical: X-rays, gamma rays, UV; chemical: EMS, mustard gas, colchicine for polyploidy), somaclonal variation, tissue culture (micropropagation, organogenesis, embryogenesis, protoplast culture & fusion — somatic hybridization), genetic engineering (Agrobacterium tumefaciens as vector, gene gun, Bt cotton, Bt brinjal, golden rice — vitamin A enriched), marker-assisted selection (MAS), DNA fingerprinting (RAPD, RFLP, AFLP, SSR/ISSR, DNA barcoding).
  • Seed technology: Seed quality (genetic, physical, physiological, health), seed certification (breeder, foundation, certified seed), seed treatment, storage.

Ecology

  • Ecosystem: Structure (abiotic — climate, soil, water, light, temperature; biotic — producers, consumers, decomposers), function (productivity — primary & secondary, decomposition, energy flow — food chain & food web, ecological pyramids — number, biomass, energy — upright in most, inverted pyramid of biomass in aquatic), nutrient cycling (gaseous — C, N, O; sedimentary — P, S), residence time.
  • Biogeochemical cycles: Carbon cycle (photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, fossil fuels, oceanic exchange), Nitrogen cycle (nitrogen fixation — biological & industrial, nitrification — Nitrosomonas & Nitrobacter, denitrification — Pseudomonas, ammonification, assimilation), Phosphorus cycle (sedimentary, rock weathering), Sulphur cycle.
  • Population ecology: Population attributes (density, natality, mortality, age distribution, sex ratio, dispersal), population growth (J-shaped — exponential, S-shaped — logistic with carrying capacity K), r & K strategies (r — opportunistic, short-lived, high fecundity; K — competitive, long-lived, low fecundity, parental care), life tables & survivorship curves.
  • Community ecology: Community structure (stratification, dominance, keystone species, ecotone & edge effect), ecological succession (hydrosere — hydrarch, xerosere — xerarch, stages: pioneer → seral stages → climax, facilitation, tolerance & inhibition models), biomes (tundra, taiga/coniferous forest, temperate deciduous forest, tropical rainforest, savanna, desert, freshwater, marine).
  • Biodiversity: Levels — genetic, species, ecosystem; measurement (alpha, beta, gamma); hotspots (criteria — endemism & threat, 36 global, India has 4: Western Ghats, Eastern Himalayas, Indo-Burma, Sundaland), threats (habitat loss & fragmentation, overexploitation, alien invasive species — Lantana, Parthenium, Eichhornia, pollution, climate change), conservation — in-situ (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves) & ex-situ (botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks, cryopreservation, tissue culture), IUCN Red List categories (Extinct, Extinct in Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern), India's biodiversity hotspots, Project Tiger, Project Elephant, CITES, CBD (Convention on Biological Diversity), Nagoya Protocol (access & benefit sharing), Ramsar Convention (wetlands), UNESCO MAB (Man & Biosphere Programme — biosphere reserves), National Biodiversity Authority (NBA), Biological Diversity Act 2002.
  • Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA): Process — screening, scoping, impact identification, prediction, mitigation, monitoring, public hearing, decision making; Environmental Management Plan (EMP).

Plant Biotechnology

  • Tissue culture: Totipotency, dedifferentiation & redifferentiation, explant types, callus culture, organogenesis (direct & indirect), embryogenesis (somatic embryogenesis), micropropagation (stages: establishment, multiplication, rooting, hardening), somaclonal variation, haploid production (anther & pollen culture), protoplast isolation & fusion (somatic hybridization — protoplast fusion, cybrids), embryo rescue.
  • Transgenic plants: Methods — Agrobacterium tumefaciens (Ti plasmid — T-DNA transfer), gene gun (biolistics), electroporation, microinjection; transgenic crops — Bt cotton (cry1Ac/cry2Ab against bollworm), Bt brinjal (approved but moratorium), Golden Rice (vitamin A enriched — β-carotene), Flavr Savr tomato, Bt brinjal (Bangladesh approved), regulatory framework in India — GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee), RCGM (Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation), IBSC (Institutional Biosafety Committee).
  • Molecular markers: RAPD (Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA), RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism), AFLP (Amplified Fragment Length Polymorphism), SSR (Simple Sequence Repeats / microsatellites), ISSR, DNA barcoding (rbcL, matK, trnH-psbA for plants), applications — germplasm characterization, diversity analysis, fingerprinting, marker-assisted selection (MAS).
  • Bioinformatics: Databases (GenBank, EMBL, DDBJ, PDB), BLAST, sequence alignment, phylogenetics.

Question: 'Discuss the status of Bt crops in India.'

Bt brinjal approved by GEAC 2009 but moratorium imposed (2010) — not commercially released. Golden Rice (vitamin A enriched) in pipeline but not approved. Regulatory: GEAC is apex body; IBSC at institution level; RCGM under DBT. Concerns: biosafety, biodiversity impact, farmer dependence on MNC seeds, farmer suicides debate, farmer suicides debate. Way forward: case-by-case evaluation, stringent biosafety, farmer education, public sector GM research (public good traits — nutrition, stress tolerance).

Memory Tricks

  • C3 vs C4: C3 — photorespiration losses (rice, wheat); C4 — Kranz anatomy, PEP carboxylase, no photorespiration (maize, sugarcane).
  • Double fertilization: Male gamete 1 + egg → zygote (2n); male gamete 2 + 2 polar nuclei → primary endosperm nucleus (3n) → endosperm.
  • Gymnosperm vs angiosperm: Gymnosperm — naked seeds (cones), no flowers, wind pollination mostly; Angiosperm — seeds enclosed in ovary (fruit), flowers, double fertilization.
  • Mitosis vs meiosis: Mitosis — equational (2n → 2n), somatic; Meiosis — reductional (2n → n), reproductive, crossing over in prophase I.

Plant Ecology: Succession, Community Dynamics and Climate‑Driven Shifts

Ecological succession describes the predictable, directional change in species composition after a disturbance. Primary succession begins on lifeless substrates — volcanic lava, glacial moraines, sand dunes — where pioneer organisms such as cyanobacteria, lichens (e.g., Cladonia spp.) and mosses (Polytrichum) initiate soil formation through weathering and organic matter accumulation. Secondary succession follows disturbances that leave soil intact (fire, logging, agriculture); it proceeds faster because a seed bank and residual nutrients persist. The classical Clementsian model views succession as a linear trajectory toward a single climax community dictated by regional climate (monoclimax). Modern theory recognises multiple stable states (polyclimax) and the role of stochastic events.

Three mechanistic models explain species replacement: facilitation (early species modify the environment to make it suitable for later species, e.g., nitrogen‑fixing Alnus enabling conifer establishment), tolerance (later species are simply more competitive under the modified conditions), and inhibition (early species suppress later ones until they are removed by disturbance). Empirical studies in temperate forests show facilitation dominates early stages, while tolerance and inhibition shape mid‑ to late‑successional transitions.

Disturbance regimes — frequency, intensity, size — interact with life‑history traits (seed dispersal, shade tolerance, resprouting ability) to determine community trajectories. Fire‑adapted ecosystems (e.g., Mediterranean chaparral, Australian eucalypt forests) exhibit serotiny and lignotubers, enabling rapid post‑fire regeneration. In contrast, tropical rainforests rely on gap dynamics; tree‑fall gaps create light heterogeneity that maintains high species diversity through niche partitioning.

Climate change adds a directional pressure that can decouple succession from historical climate envelopes. Species distribution models (SDMs) project upward elevational shifts of 100–300 m per decade for montane taxa, while lowland species face range contractions. Phenological mismatches — earlier flowering without corresponding pollinator emergence — reduce reproductive success. Assisted migration and climate‑smart restoration (using provenances pre‑adapted to future climates) are emerging management tools, but they raise ethical and genetic‑integrity concerns.

Key quantitative tools include chronosequence studies (space‑for‑time substitution), permanent plot networks (e.g., CTFS‑ForestGEO), and remote sensing of canopy dynamics (LiDAR, hyperspectral indices). These datasets feed into dynamic global vegetation models (DGVMs) that couple succession with carbon cycling, informing IPCC assessments.

Economic Botany: Medicinal Plants, Phytochemical Classes and Quality‑Control Standards

Medicinal plants constitute a multi‑billion‑dollar global trade and underpin primary health care for ~80 % of the world’s population (WHO estimate). Major pharmacopoeial species cultivated or wild‑harvested in India include Catharanthus roseus (vinca alkaloids vincristine, vinblastine for leukemia), Taxus wallichiana (paclitaxel precursor for ovarian and breast cancer), Artemisia annua (artemisinin for malaria), Withania somnifera (withanolides for adaptogenic formulations), and Bacopa monnieri (bacosides for cognitive enhancement). Each species illustrates a distinct biosynthetic pathway: terpenoid indole alkaloids (Catharanthus), diterpene taxanes (Taxus), sesquiterpene lactones (Artemisia), steroidal lactones (Withania), and triterpenoid saponins (Bacopa).

Phytochemical classification guides both bioprospecting and quality control. Alkaloids (nitrogen‑bearing, often basic) are extracted with acid‑base partitioning; terpenoids (isoprene‑derived, non‑polar) require organic solvents; phenolics (flavonoids, tannins) are polar and amenable to aqueous‑ethanol extraction. Standardisation follows pharmacopoeial monographs (Indian Pharmacopoeia, Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia, USP) that specify marker compounds, assay methods (HPLC‑UV, LC‑MS), and limits for contaminants (heavy metals ≤10 ppm Pb, ≤3 ppm Cd, ≤1 ppm Hg; pesticide residues per Codex Alimentarius; microbial load ≤10⁴ CFU/g total aerobic count).

Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP) and Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) are mandatory for export. GACP covers site selection (soil pH, altitude, absence of industrial pollution), propagation material authentication (voucher specimens, DNA barcoding with rbcL + matK), harvest timing (peak secondary‑metabolite accumulation), and post‑harvest drying (≤40 °C to avoid thermolabile loss). Traceability systems (batch numbers, QR‑linked certificates of analysis) enable recall and build buyer confidence.

Biotechnological interventions — hairy‑root cultures (Agrobacterium rhizogenes), elicitation (methyl jasmonate, salicylic acid), and metabolic engineering (overexpression of rate‑limiting enzymes such as strictosidine synthase in Catharanthus) — aim to stabilise supply and reduce pressure on wild populations. However, regulatory frameworks treat such products as “novel foods” or “herbal drugs” requiring safety dossiers (toxicity, genotoxicity, herb‑drug interaction studies).

Market dynamics: India exports ~US$ 300 million of herbal extracts annually; major destinations are the USA, EU, and Japan. The National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB) implements the “National Mission on Medicinal Plants” (NMMP) providing subsidies for cultivation, post‑harvest infrastructure, and market linkages. Emerging trends include nutraceutical formulations (standardised extracts in capsules), cosmeceuticals (anti‑aging creams with Centella asiatica asiaticoside), and phytopharmaceuticals undergoing clinical trials (e.g., Curcuma longa curcumin for osteoarthritis).

Answer‑Writing Technique: Structuring a 15‑Mark Question on Medicinal Plant Standardisation
“Discuss the steps involved in the standardisation of a medicinal plant raw material as per the Indian Pharmacopoeia, highlighting the role of marker compounds and contaminant limits.”
Introduction (2 marks): Define standardisation — ensuring identity, purity, strength, and reproducibility of herbal raw material. Mention regulatory basis: Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP) and Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API).
Body (10 marks): Enumerate sequential steps:
1. Botanical authentication — macroscopic & microscopic examination, voucher specimen deposition, DNA barcoding (rbcL + matK).
2. Collection & post‑harvest handling — GACP compliance, harvest at optimal phenophase, drying ≤40 °C.
3. Physicochemical parameters — foreign matter ≤2 %, total ash ≤10 %, acid‑insoluble ash ≤1 %, moisture ≤10 % (IP general chapter).
4. Phytochemical fingerprinting — TLC/HPTLC profile of marker compounds (e.g., vincristine for Catharanthus, artemisinin for Artemisia).
5. Quantitative assay — validated HPLC‑UV or LC‑MS method; acceptance criteria: marker content within ±10 % of label claim.
6. Contaminant testing — heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Hg, As) per IP limits; pesticide residues per Codex; microbial limits (total aerobic ≤10⁴ CFU/g, yeast/mould ≤10³ CFU/g, absence of E. coli, Salmonella).
7. Stability studies — accelerated (40 °C/75 % RH) and real‑time; monitor marker degradation.
8. Documentation & batch release — Certificate of Analysis (CoA) with batch number, test results, analyst signature.
Conclusion (3 marks): Emphasise that standardisation bridges traditional knowledge and modern regulatory science, enabling safe global trade and clinical credibility. Cite NMMP and NMPB initiatives for farmer‑level implementation.

Memorise a table of 8–10 flagship species with three columns: (1) Botanical name & family, (2) Major marker compound(s) & therapeutic use, (3) Key IP/API limits (marker % w/w, heavy‑metal ppm). Plant Pathology: Host‑Pathogen Interaction, Resistance Mechanisms and Integrated Disease Management

The disease triangle — susceptible host, virulent pathogen, conducive environment — remains the conceptual backbone. Molecular dialogue follows the gene‑for‑gene hypothesis (Flor, 1942): a pathogen avirulence (Avr) gene product is recognised by a corresponding host resistance (R) gene product, triggering effector‑triggered immunity (ETI). ETI typically culminates in a hypersensitive response (HR) — localized programmed cell death that restricts biotrophic pathogens (e.g., Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici causing stem rust). In contrast, pattern‑triggered immunity (PTI) is activated by conserved microbe‑associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) such as flagellin (flg22) or chitin, providing basal resistance against a broad spectrum.

Resistance durability differs: vertical (race‑specific) resistance conferred by single R genes (e.g., Sr31 against wheat stem rust) is often overcome by pathogen mutation; horizontal (partial) resistance involves multiple quantitative trait loci (QTL) with additive effects (e.g., Lr34 conferring adult‑plant resistance to leaf rust, stripe rust, and powdery mildew). Pyramiding several R genes (gene stacking) and deploying cultivar mixtures or multilines slow epidemic progress.

Systemic signalling amplifies local defence: systemic acquired resistance (SAR) depends on salicylic acid (SA) accumulation and NPR1‑mediated transcription of pathogenesis‑related (PR) proteins; induced systemic resistance (ISR) is jasmonic acid (JA)/ethylene (ET) dependent and often triggered by beneficial rhizobacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas fluorescens CHA0). Cross‑talk between SA and JA pathways can be antagonistic, influencing disease outcomes.

Integrated Disease Management (IDM) combines: (1) Cultural — crop rotation (break cycles of Fusarium wilt), sanitation (removal of infected debris), planting date adjustment (avoid peak inoculum), resistant varieties; (2) Biological — antagonistic microbes (Trichoderma spp. for soil‑borne pathogens, Bacillus subtilis for foliar diseases), phage therapy (emerging for bacterial blight); (3) Chemical — fungicides classified by FRAC codes (e.g., QoI – Group 11, SDHI – Group 7) with resistance‑management strategies (alternation, mixtures, dose optimisation); (4) Regulatory — quarantine (phytosanitary certificates, ISPM‑15 for wood packaging), seed certification, and disease‑free planting material.

Case study — Late blight of potato (Phytophthora infestans): The 1840s Irish famine illustrates epidemic potential. Modern IDM uses decision‑support systems (Blightcast) integrating weather data (temperature 10–20 °C, >90 % RH) to time fungicide sprays (e.g., mandipropamid, cyazofamid). Host resistance genes Rpi‑blb1, Rpi‑blb2 from wild Solanum bulbocastanum have been introgressed via marker‑assisted selection. Genome sequencing of P. infestans reveals effector repertoires (RXLR, CRN) guiding durable resistance breeding.

Biotechnology: Genome Editing, CRISPR‑Cas Systems and the Evolving Regulatory Landscape

CRISPR‑Cas (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats – CRISPR‑associated) provides programmable nucleases for targeted genome modification. The canonical SpCas9 (Streptococcus pyogenes) recognises a 5′‑NGG‑3′ protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) and generates a blunt double‑strand break (DSB). Repair pathways — non‑homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homology‑directed repair (HDR) — enable gene knockout, precise allele replacement, or transgene insertion. Variants such as SaCas9 (NNGRRT PAM), Cas12a (Cpf1) (TTTV PAM, staggered cut), and CasΦ (compact, broad PAM) expand targeting range.

Base editors (cytosine base editor — CBE, adenine base editor — ABE) fuse a deaminase domain to a nickase Cas9 (nCas9) enabling C→T or A→G conversions without DSBs, reducing indels. Prime editing couples a reverse‑transcriptase‑Cas9 fusion with a prime‑editing guide RNA (pegRNA) to install all 12 possible base changes, small insertions/deletions, and precise tag insertions. These tools have been demonstrated in rice (Wx gene for low‑amylose), wheat (TaMLO for powdery mildew resistance), tomato (Solyc08g075630 for parthenocarpy), and citrus (CsLOB1 for citrus canker resistance).

Regulatory classification hinges on the presence of foreign DNA. The SDN‑1 (site‑directed nuclease‑1) category — NHEJ‑mediated small indels without template — is treated as non‑GM in several jurisdictions (USA USDA‑APHIS “Am I Regulated?” process, Argentina, Brazil, Australia). SDN‑2 (template‑guided precise edit) and SDN‑3 (large DNA insertion) often fall under GM regulations. India’s 2022 “Guidelines for Safety Assessment of Genome‑Edited Plants” exempt SDN‑1 and SDN‑2 from the GEAC approval pathway, provided no foreign gene remains; SDN‑3 follows the conventional GM route (GEAC, RCGM, IBSC). The EU Court of Justice (2018) ruled that all genome‑edited organisms are GMOs, subjecting them to Directive 2001/18/EC.

Off‑target assessment employs whole‑genome sequencing (WGS) of edited lines, GUIDE‑seq, DISCOVER‑seq, and amplicon‑seq of predicted sites. Phenotypic screening for somaclonal variation remains essential. Intellectual property: Broad Institute (CRISPR‑Cas9), ToolGen (Cas9 variants), and public‑sector licences (e.g., “CRISPR‑Cas9 for humanitarian use”) shape technology access.

Future directions: multiplexed editing (tRNA‑gRNA arrays), epigenetic editing (dCas9‑TET1/dCas9‑DNMT3A for DNA methylation modulation), and gene drives (population‑level allele spread) — the latter confined to laboratory containment due to ecological risk.

Plant Systematics: Molecular Phylogenetics, DNA Barcoding and the APG IV Classification

Modern plant systematics integrates morphology, anatomy, cytology, and DNA sequence data to infer evolutionary relationships. The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) IV (2016) classification, based on multilocus plastid (rbcL, matK, ndhF), mitochondrial (matR), and nuclear (18S, 26S, PHYC) datasets, recognises 64 orders and 416 families. Key clades: Amborellales (basal), Nymphaeales, Austrobaileyales, then the massive Mesangiospermae split into Monocots (e.g., Poaceae, Orchidaceae) and Eudicots (core eudicots → rosids + asterids). Within rosids, the nitrogen‑fixing clade (Fabales, Rosales, Cucurbitales, Fagales) and the malvids (Malvales, Brassicales, Sapindales) are well supported.

DNA barcoding uses short, standardised loci for species identification. The plant barcode core — rbcL + matK — achieves ~70 % species discrimination; adding trnH‑psbA or ITS2 raises resolution to >90 % in many groups. Barcode libraries (BOLD, GenBank) enable rapid authentication of herbal raw materials, detection of adulterants (e.g., Swertia chirayita substituted by Swertia angustifolia), and forensic timber tracking.

Phylogenomic approaches — target‑capture (Hyb‑Seq), transcriptome sequencing, and whole‑plastome sequencing — resolve deep nodes previously ambiguous (e.g., placement of Ceratophyllum, relationships among basal angiosperms). Coalescent‑based species‑tree methods (ASTRAL, SVDquartets) accommodate incomplete lineage sorting. Divergence‑time estimation uses fossil calibrations (e.g., Archaefructus ~125 Ma for early angiosperms) in relaxed‑clock models (BEAST).

Taxonomic revisions driven by molecular evidence include the splitting of Acacia (retaining Acacia s.str. for Australian species, moving African species to Vachellia and Senegalia), the re‑circumscription of Leguminosae into six subfamilies (Caesalpinioideae, Dialioideae, Detarioideae, Duparquetioideae, Papilionoideae, Cercidoideae), and the recognition of Orchidaceae as the largest angiosperm family (~28 000 species).

Practical applications: (1) Conservation prioritisation — phylogenetic diversity (PD) metrics identify evolutionarily distinct lineages (e.g., Ginkgo biloba, Welwitschia mirabilis) for ex‑situ conservation; (2) Crop wild relative (CWR) mining — phylogeny guides trait introgression (e.g., drought tolerance from Oryza rufipogon into cultivated rice); (3) Biogeographic reconstruction — DEC models infer historical dispersal/vicariance, informing predictions of range shifts under climate change.

  • Confusing primary and secondary succession mechanisms — remember facilitation dominates early primary stages, while tolerance/inhibition shape later secondary stages.
  • Assuming all medicinal‑plant quality control relies only on marker‑compound quantification; contaminant limits (heavy metals, pesticides, microbes) are equally mandatory under IP/API.
  • Treating vertical resistance as durable; it is race‑specific and quickly overcome — horizontal resistance and gene pyramiding provide durability.
  • Believing CRISPR‑edited crops are universally exempt from GM regulation; regulatory status varies by country and by editing category (SDN‑1/2 vs SDN‑3).
  • Using only rbcL for DNA barcoding; the two‑locus core (rbcL + matK) plus a supplementary spacer (trnH‑psbA or ITS2) is the accepted standard.
  • Overlooking the role of SAR/ISR cross‑talk in disease management; SA‑JA antagonism can compromise resistance when both pathways are activated simultaneously.
  • Memorising APG IV family numbers without understanding the underlying clade hierarchy; examiners test ability to place a genus within the correct order and clade.
