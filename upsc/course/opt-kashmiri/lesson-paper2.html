Prescribed Texts and Close Reading

  • Lalla Vakyani — Lal Ded (Lalleshwari): Mystic verses (vakhs) attributed to Lal Ded.
  • Shruks — Nund Rishi: Devotional verses of the Rishi order.
  • Habba Khatoon’s lyrics — Habba Khatoon: Love lyrics of separation and longing.
  • Modern Kashmiri poetry — Ghulam Ahmad Mahjoor: Mahjoor pioneered modern Kashmiri poetry.

Literary Criticism

mystic and Sufi interpretation, traditional rasa criticism, modern stylistic and postcolonial approaches

Explanation (Vyakhya) Strategy

  • Context: identify the text, author, period and speaker.
  • Literal meaning: paraphrase the passage in simple Kashmiri or English.
  • Theme: state the central idea and the emotional tone.
  • Form: comment on metre, imagery, diction and figures of speech.
  • Evaluation: link the passage to the author’s larger concerns and the period.

Essay Questions

  • Define the literary issue in the question, then take a clear position.
  • Support the argument with at least three texts or authors.
  • Include one counter-argument and respond to it.
  • Conclude with the significance of the issue for Kashmiri literature.

Solved Example: Explanation
Question: Explain a prescribed passage from Lalla Vakyani.
Answer: Identify the speaker and situation, paraphrase the lines, state the theme, comment on imagery and form, and connect the passage to the work’s central concern. A good explanation moves from literal meaning to literary evaluation.

Common Traps

  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing the passage.
  • Using a translation without naming the translator.
  • Ignoring the prescribed text and writing general remarks.
  • Mixing authors and works from different periods.
  • Making claims about influence without evidence.
Mains strategy
For explanation questions, use the five-step structure: context → literal meaning → theme → form → evaluation. For essay questions, write a thesis statement in the first paragraph, use three body paragraphs with textual evidence, and end with a balanced conclusion. Keep quotations short and accurate.

The Evolution of the Sharada Script: From Kashmiri to Sanskrit and Beyond

A serious aspirant must understand that the "Perso-Arabic script" mentioned in the introductory passage is not the only historical reality for Kashmiri literature. The history of Kashmiri writing is bifurcated by a critical linguistic event: the shift from the indigenous Sharada script to the Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq) script. This transition, largely complete by the 19th century but rooted in earlier administrative shifts under Mughal rule, fundamentally altered the texture of literary criticism available to the examiner.

Chronology and Causality: The Sharada script, derived from Brahmi and flourishing between the 8th and 12th centuries, was primarily used for Sanskrit literature but also recorded early Kashmiri texts (like the Vakyani). However, with the advent of Muslim rule in the 14th century (Shah Mir dynasty), Persian became the court language. Consequently, literary production shifted to Nastaliq. The critical nuance for Paper II is that while Sharada manuscripts exist for Kashmiri (e.g., the Chandamoha or fragments of Lal Ded's verses in Sharada found later), the vast majority of "prescribed texts" for UPSC are printed in Persian script. A candidate who claims familiarity with Sharada without acknowledging its decline must be careful not to conflate the two scripts when discussing textual provenance.

Textual Evidence: The Lal Vakyani, attributed to Lal Ded (14th century), represents a pivotal moment. While her verses were originally composed in vernacular Kashmiri, they were recorded in Sharada script by scribes of the Rishi order before being transcribed into Persian script for wider dissemination. Similarly, Nund Rishi (Nun Nuni) and his disciples wrote in Sharada initially. The shift to Perso-Arabic is not merely a change of alphabet; it signifies a change in literary audience—from the monastic/elite Sanskrit-speaking circles to the broader populace and administrative bureaucracy.

The Case of Habba Khatoon: Habba Khatoon (16th century) represents the peak of this transition. She wrote in the vernacular but adopted the Persian script, aligning her work with the literary currents of the time. Her lyrics, found in manuscripts like Kahkashan-e-Khatoon, are exclusively in Perso-Arabic. The examiner often tests the candidate's ability to distinguish between "oral tradition" (which existed before writing) and "literary text" (fixed in script). The oral tradition of Lal Ded was preserved through memory and recitation, whereas Habba Khatoon's work required a written medium that had already standardized.

Modern Standardization: In the 20th century, with the influence of Mahjoor and later poets like Masroor Ahsan Qureshi, the standardization of Kashmiri in Perso-Arabic solidified. However, a subtle trap exists: some modern critics argue for reviving Sharada for literary authenticity. While academically interesting, this is not yet a standard practice in UPSC-prescribed texts. The examiner expects you to acknowledge the historical depth (Sharada) while analyzing the prescribed text (Perso-Arabic).

Technique: Script Identification and Provenance Analysis
Question: "The passage provided is written in Perso-Arabic script. However, historical records suggest that early Kashmiri literature was recorded in Sharada. Discuss the implications of this script shift on the preservation of Lal Ded's verses and how it affects literary criticism."
Answer Structure:

Step 1: Identify the Script. Acknowledge that the text is in Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq), which became dominant post-14th century due to Mughal influence and the rise of Persian as a court language.

Step 2: Historical Context. Explain that prior to this, Sharada script was used for both Sanskrit and early Kashmiri texts. The shift indicates a move from elite/monastic circles to a more administrative/bureaucratic context where Persian literacy was valued.

Step 3: Impact on Preservation. Note that while Sharada manuscripts of Lal Ded exist, they are rare and often fragmentary. The Perso-Arabic versions ensured wider dissemination but introduced orthographic variations (spelling differences) that critics must navigate. For instance, the pronunciation of 'k' vs 'kh' or specific vowel lengths might vary between scripts.

Step 4: Implications for Criticism. A critic using only Perso-Arabic texts might miss nuances present in the original Sharada manuscripts or the oral tradition. The shift also meant that some Sanskrit-influenced vocabulary was retained, while purely Persian loanwords entered the lexicon during this period.

Conclusion: The script shift is not just a technicality; it reflects the socio-political transformation of Kashmir from a Hindu-Buddhist/Brahmanical sphere to a Sufi-Persianate one, directly influencing the thematic content of the literature.

Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Script Ambiguity
If a question asks about "ancient Kashmiri texts" and you are unsure whether the examiner implies Sharada or Perso-Arabic, always default to mentioning both. State: "While early texts were in Sharada, the prescribed corpus is in Perso-Arabic." Never assume the examiner wants you to write in Sharada unless explicitly asked. Focus on the *content* and *themes* which transcend the script, but acknowledge the script as a marker of historical periodization.

The Rishi Order: Social Structure and Literary Patronage

To analyze Lal Vakyani or Shruks effectively, one must understand the social architecture of the Rishi order. This is not merely a literary movement but a socio-religious institution that functioned as an alternative hierarchy to the caste-based Brahmanical system.

Social Mechanism: The Rishi order was open to all castes, though predominantly composed of lower-caste individuals (often identified with the Chuhar or similar groups) who rejected caste purity rituals. Lal Ded's verses often critique the hypocrisy of Brahmins and the rigidities of social hierarchy. For example, in her famous verse "Kashmiri chhah jammu va kashmiras manz bolnu yun," she asserts the universality of the Kashmiri identity, transcending regional or caste boundaries.

Patronage and Textual Transmission: Unlike modern literature which relies on publishers, Rishi literature was transmitted through Wakhs (verses) recited in public gatherings. The "prescribed texts" are often collections of these recitations compiled by later disciples. The critical point for the examiner is that there is no single "authoritative edition" of Lal Ded's work in the same way as a Shakespeare play. Different manuscripts contain different verses, and some are attributed to her based on tradition rather than historical evidence.

Landmark Case: The Kahkashan-e-Khatoon: For Habba Khatoon, the text is a compilation of lyrics collected by Mirza Hassan Baksh (19th century). This is a crucial distinction. When analyzing Habba Khatoon, one must distinguish between her original oral performance and the compiled manuscript. The compiler's influence on the text cannot be ignored in literary criticism.

Nund Rishi's Shruks: Nund Rishi's verses are often more devotional and less polemical than Lal Ded's. His work focuses on the concept of Wahdat-ul-Wujud (Unity of Being), a core Sufi tenet. The literary criticism here shifts from social critique to metaphysical exploration. The examiner may ask you to compare the "social realism" of Lal Ded with the "metaphysical abstraction" of Nund Rishi.

Technique: Comparative Analysis of Rishi Themes
Question: "Compare the social critique in Lal Ded's Vakyani with the devotional focus in Nund Rishi's Shruks. How does this reflect the evolution of Kashmiri Sufism?"
Answer Structure:

Step 1: Define the Core Themes. Lal Ded's work is characterized by Vakhs that attack social hypocrisy, caste discrimination, and ritualism. Her imagery is often grounded in the harsh realities of Kashmiri life (snow, poverty, betrayal). Nund Rishi's Shruks, conversely, focus on inner purification, love for God, and the unity of existence.

Step 2: Analyze the Evolution. The shift from Lal Ded to Nund Rishi marks a transition from external social reform to internal spiritual realization. While Lal Ded fought against the *system* (caste), Nund Rishi sought to transcend the *self*. This evolution mirrors the broader trajectory of Indian Sufism, moving from the Silsila (order) structure to individual mystical experience.

Step 3: Textual Evidence. Cite a specific verse from Lal Ded where she addresses a Brahmin directly to challenge his authority. Contrast this with a Shruk by Nund Rishi where he addresses the soul directly, ignoring social labels entirely.

Step 4: Conclusion. The evolution reflects a maturation of Kashmiri thought: from rejecting external oppression to seeking internal liberation. Both are essential for a complete understanding of Kashmiri Sufism.

Exam-Day Strategy: Avoiding Anachronisms
Do not project modern concepts like "human rights" or "feminism" onto Lal Ded without qualification. While her work is proto-feminist in its rejection of gender roles, she operated within a medieval framework. Use terms like "social critique," "egalitarian impulses," or "rejection of ritualism" rather than modern political terminology unless the question specifically asks for a contemporary relevance analysis.

Mahjoor's Modernity: The Transition from Mysticism to Social Realism

The inclusion of Ghulam Ahmad Mahjoor in Paper II marks a definitive break from the medieval mystic tradition. Understanding this transition is vital for answering questions on "Modern Kashmiri Poetry."

The Shift in Imagery: Lal Ded and Nund Rishi used nature imagery (snow, pine, river) to symbolize divine states. Mahjoor, while using similar natural imagery, employed it to depict social conditions—poverty, displacement, and the impact of political turmoil. His poem "Kashmiri chhah..." is a declaration of linguistic pride but also a lament for the loss of cultural identity under colonial rule.

The Role of the Press: Unlike the Rishis who relied on oral transmission or manuscript circulation, Mahjoor was a journalist and editor. His work appeared in newspapers like Kashmir (published by him). This is a critical distinction for literary criticism: the text is mass-produced, edited, and intended for a literate public rather than a monastic audience.

Literary Criticism of Mahjoor: Critics often debate whether Mahjoor was a "modern" poet or a "traditional" one writing in modern forms.
