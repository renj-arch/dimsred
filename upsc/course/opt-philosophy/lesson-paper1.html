1. Western Philosophy: From the Pre‑Socratics to Kant

1.1 The Pre‑Socratic Foundations

The earliest Greek thinkers turned away from mythic explanation and asked what the world is made of, initiating a tradition of naturalistic inquiry that still shapes metaphysical debate. Thales of Miletus proposed water as the arche, Anaximander introduced the boundless (apeiron) as an indefinite principle, and Anaximenes identified air, each attempting to reduce the multiplicity of phenomena to a single material substrate. Heraclitus famously declared that everything flows, insisting that fire symbolises a universal process of becoming, while Parmenides countered that change is impossible because being is one, unchanging and eternal. Zeno’s paradoxes were crafted to defend Parmenides by showing that motion leads to logical absurdities, and Empedocles reconciled the two by positing four roots — earth, water, air, fire — moved by love and strife. Anaxagoras introduced nous (mind) as the ordering principle, and Democritus, with Leucippus, developed atomism, arguing that indivisible particles moving in void compose all objects. These doctrines established the agenda for later philosophy: the search for a fundamental principle, the problem of change versus permanence, and the relation between perception and reality.

1.2 Classical Greek Systematics: Socrates, Plato and Aristotle

Socrates shifted the focus from cosmology to ethics and epistemology, employing the elenchus to expose contradictions in his interlocutors’ beliefs and insisting that virtue is knowledge. His claim that the unexamined life is not worth living made self‑knowledge the cornerstone of moral philosophy. Plato, his most famous student, systematised Socratic insights into a metaphysical architecture of Forms — eternal, non‑material archetypes that particulars imitate. The allegory of the cave illustrates the ascent from opinion (doxa) to knowledge (episteme), while the tripartite soul (reason, spirit, appetite) grounds his political vision of the philosopher‑king. Aristotle, Plato’s pupil, rejected separate Forms and instead developed hylomorphism, analysing substances as composites of matter and form. His four causes — material, formal, efficient and final — provide a comprehensive explanatory framework, and his categories organise predicates into ten fundamental kinds. Aristotle’s syllogistic logic, especially the square of opposition, became the dominant formal system for two millennia, and his distinction between potentiality and actuality underpins his metaphysics of change.

1.3 Medieval Synthesis: Patristic Thought and Scholasticism

With the rise of Christianity, Greek philosophy was appropriated to articulate and defend doctrinal claims. Augustine fused Neoplatonism with scripture, treating evil as a privation of good and analysing time as a distension of the mind. Boethius, in The Consolation of Philosophy, reconciled divine foreknowledge with human freedom through the notion of eternal present. Anselm’s ontological argument defined God as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived” and deduced necessary existence from the concept itself. Abelard advanced conceptualism, mediating between realism and nominalism by locating universals in the mind’s concepts. The high scholastic period saw Thomas Aquinas harmonise Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology, producing the Five Ways as proofs of God’s existence, the analogy of being (analogia entis) to speak meaningfully about the divine, and the doctrine of actus essendi distinguishing essence from existence. Duns Scotus defended the univocity of being, allowing a single concept of being to apply to God and creatures, while William of Ockham championed nominalism and the principle of parsimony — Ockham’s razor — insisting that entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity.

1.4 The Modern Turn: Rationalism, Empiricism and Kantian Critical Philosophy

The seventeenth century witnessed a decisive break with scholastic authority. Rationalists such as Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz argued that reason alone can yield substantive knowledge. Descartes’ methodical doubt culminated in the cogito ergo sum, establishing the thinking self as the indubitable foundation; his substance dualism separated mind (res cogitans) from body (res extensa). Spinoza collapsed the dualism into a single substance — God or Nature — whose attributes are thought and extension, and whose deterministic order follows from the necessity of the divine nature. Leibniz introduced monads as simple, windowless substances coordinated by a pre‑established harmony, and he formulated the principle of sufficient reason and the optimism of the best of all possible worlds. Empiricists Locke, Berkeley and Hume insisted that all ideas originate in experience. Locke’s tabula rasa distinguished primary qualities (solidity, extension) from secondary qualities (colour, taste), while Berkeley denied material substance altogether, asserting esse est percipi. Hume radicalised empiricism by showing that causal necessity is not observed but inferred from constant conjunction, and he dissolved the self into a bundle of perceptions. Kant responded to the rationalist‑empiricist impasse with transcendental idealism: space and time are a priori forms of intuition, the categories of understanding (quantity, quality, relation, modality) structure experience, and synthetic a priori judgments are possible. His moral philosophy centres on the categorical imperative, demanding that maxims be universalizable and that humanity be treated as an end in itself.

2. Indian Philosophy: Orthodox and Heterodox Schools

2.1 The Vedic Foundations and the Six Orthodox Darshanas

Indian philosophy begins with the Vedic corpus, where ritual speculation gradually yields to systematic inquiry into the nature of reality (brahman), the self (atman) and the means of valid knowledge (pramana). The six orthodox (astika) schools — Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa and Vedanta — accept the authority of the Vedas while differing in metaphysics and methodology. Nyaya develops a rigorous logic and epistemology, identifying four pramanas (perception, inference, comparison, testimony) and a theory of causation that underpins its realist ontology. Vaisheshika, closely allied with Nyaya, enumerates seven categories (padarthas) — substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, inherence and non‑existence — and proposes an atomic theory of matter. Samkhya posits a dualism of purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (primordial matter) whose interaction produces the manifest world through the evolution of twenty‑five tattvas. Yoga, as systematised by Patanjali, adopts Samkhya metaphysics but adds an eight‑fold practical discipline (ashtanga) aimed at stilling the fluctuations of consciousness. Mimamsa focuses on dharma as ritual duty, defending the eternal validity of Vedic injunctions and developing a sophisticated theory of verbal testimony (shabda). Vedanta, in its various subschools — Advaita, Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita — interprets the Upanishadic equation of atman and brahman, ranging from non‑dual identity to qualified non‑dualism and strict dualism.

2.2 Heterodox Traditions: Buddhism, Jainism and Carvaka

The heterodox (nastika) schools reject Vedic authority and offer alternative soteriologies. Buddhism, founded by Siddhartha Gautama, teaches the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path, denying a permanent self (anatta) and analysing experience into five aggregates (skandhas) governed by dependent origination (pratityasamutpada). Its epistemology, especially in the Madhyamaka and Yogacara traditions, emphasises the emptiness (shunyata) of inherent existence and the mind‑only (vijnaptimatra) nature of phenomena. Jainism, attributed to Mahavira, asserts an eternal plurality of souls (jivas) bound by karma, and develops a sophisticated ontology of substances (dravyas) with the doctrine of anekantavada — the multiplicity of viewpoints — and syadvada, a seven‑fold conditional logic. The materialist Carvaka (Lokayata) school accepts only perception as a valid pramana, denies soul, afterlife and moral law, and holds that consciousness arises from the combination of material elements, anticipating later naturalistic philosophies.

2.3 Comparative Metaphysics: Atman, Brahman and Anatta

The central metaphysical disagreement between orthodox and heterodox systems concerns the status of the self. Advaita Vedanta, following Shankara, identifies atman with the non‑dual brahman, regarding individuality as an illusory superimposition (avidya) removable by knowledge (jnana). Ramanuja’s Vishishtadvaita qualifies this identity: individual souls and the world are real but exist as modes (prakaras) of a personal brahman. Madhva’s Dvaita maintains an absolute distinction between the supreme Vishnu and finite souls. In contrast, Buddhism’s anatta doctrine denies any enduring substrate, explaining continuity through causal chains without a metaphysical self. Jainism affirms an eternal jiva but distinguishes it from the body and karma, allowing for liberation through the purification of karmic matter. These positions shape each school’s ethics, epistemology and conception of liberation (moksha or nirvana).

2.4 Epistemology and Logic in Nyaya and Buddhist Thought

Nyaya’s contribution to Indian logic is the formalisation of inference (anumana) into a five‑membered syllogism (pratijna, hetu, udaharana, upanaya, nigamana) and the classification of fallacies (hetvabhasa). Its theory of perception (pratyaksha) distinguishes determinate (savikalpaka) from indeterminate (nirvikalpaka) cognition, and it defends the reliability of testimony (shabda) when sourced from a trustworthy person. Buddhist logicians, notably Dignaga and Dharmakirti, refined the notion of apoha (exclusion) to explain concept formation without universals, and they developed a two‑fold pramana scheme — perception and inference — with a rigorous analysis of the logical mark (linga) and the three characteristics of a valid reason (trairupya). The debate between Nyaya realists and Buddhist nominalists over the existence of universals (samanya) and the nature of the logical mark drove centuries of philosophical exchange, enriching both traditions.

Example Question
Explain how the Nyaya five‑membered syllogism differs from the Aristotelian categorical syllogism in structure and epistemic function.
The Nyaya syllogism (anumana) consists of five explicit steps: pratijna (thesis), hetu (reason), udaharana (example), upanaya (application) and nigamana (conclusion). Each step is articulated in natural language and serves a pedagogical and dialectical purpose, making the inferential move transparent to an audience. The Aristotelian categorical syllogism, by contrast, compresses inference into three propositions — major premise, minor premise and conclusion — each a categorical statement (A, E, I, O) governed by the rules of figure and mood. While Nyaya treats the example (udaharana) as an essential component that illustrates the invariable concomitance (vyapti) between reason and property, Aristotle’s system assumes the universal premise already encodes that relation. Epistemically, Nyaya regards inference as a pramana that yields new knowledge only when the hetu satisfies the three characteristics (trairupya): presence in the subject, presence in similar cases, absence in dissimilar cases. Aristotelian logic evaluates validity formally, independent of the epistemic status of the premises. Thus Nyaya integrates logical form with a theory of knowledge acquisition, whereas Aristotelian syllogistic focuses on formal validity.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for a comparison, structure the answer around a table‑like mental framework: identify the shared domain, list the divergent criteria, and illustrate each with a concrete doctrinal example; this prevents vague generalities and shows the examiner precise analytical control.

3. Ethics: Meta‑ethics, Normative Theories and Applied Issues

3.1 Meta‑ethical Foundations: Naturalism, Non‑naturalism and Expressivism

Meta‑ethics investigates the semantic, metaphysical and epistemological status of moral judgments. Ethical naturalists such as Mill and contemporary Cornell realists argue that moral properties are identical with or reducible to natural properties (e.g., pleasure, desire satisfaction), making moral truths discoverable by empirical science. G. E. Moore’s open‑question argument challenged this reduction, positing that “good” denotes a simple, non‑natural property known by intuition; his non‑naturalism insists that moral facts are sui generis and not amenable to naturalistic analysis. In the twentieth century, A. J. Ayer and C. L. Stevenson advanced emotivism, claiming that moral utterances express attitudes rather than propositions, while R. M. Hare’s prescriptivism treated them as universalizable imperatives. Later, Simon Blackburn’s quasi‑realism and Allan Gibbard’s norm‑expressivism attempted to preserve the realist‑like discourse of morality without committing to objective moral facts. These positions shape the debate over moral objectivity, the possibility of moral knowledge and the role of reason in ethics.

3.2 Normative Ethics: Deontology, Consequentialism and Virtue Ethics

Normative ethics supplies criteria for right action. Immanuel Kant’s deontology grounds duty in the categorical imperative: act only on maxims that can be willed as universal law, and treat humanity always as an end. This yields absolute prohibitions (e.g., against lying) irrespective of consequences. Consequentialism, epitomised by Jeremy Bentham’s and John Stuart Mill’s utilitarianism, evaluates actions by the net balance of happiness over suffering, demanding impartial maximisation of utility. Rule‑utilitarianism modifies the act‑focus by judging rules according to their long‑run consequences. Virtue ethics, revived by Elizabeth Anscombe, Philippa Foot and Alasdair MacIntyre, shifts attention from acts to character, arguing that eudaimonia (flourishing) is achieved through the cultivation of virtues such as courage, temperance and practical wisdom (phronesis). Each framework generates distinct verdicts in hard cases — for instance, whether to sacrifice one life to save five — and the comparative assessment of these verdicts remains a central pedagogical exercise.

3.3 Applied Ethics: Bioethics, Environmental Ethics and Professional Ethics

Applied ethics translates normative principles into concrete domains. In bioethics, the principles of autonomy, beneficence, non‑maleficence and justice (Beauchamp and Childress) guide decisions on informed consent, euthanasia, genetic editing and resource allocation. Environmental ethics extends moral consideration beyond humans: anthropocentric views value nature instrumentally, while biocentric (Paul Taylor) and ecocentric (Aldo Leopold’s land ethic) perspectives ascribe intrinsic value to living organisms or ecosystems. Deep ecology advocates a radical re‑orientation of self‑identity with the biosphere. Professional ethics addresses role‑specific duties — legal confidentiality, medical triage, engineering safety — often codified in codes of conduct that balance fiduciary obligations with public welfare. The interplay of theoretical commitments and contextual constraints makes applied ethics a testing ground for the coherence of normative theories.

4. Logic: Formal, Informal and Symbolic Systems

4.1 Classical Syllogistic and Propositional Logic

Logic studies the principles of correct reasoning. Aristotle’s syllogistic, the first formal system, analyses categorical propositions (A, E, I, O) and their combinations in three figures, providing rules for validity such as the distribution of the middle term and the avoidance of illicit major or minor. The square of opposition captures the logical relations of contradiction, contrariety, subcontrariety and subalternation among the four forms. In the nineteenth century, George Boole and Gottlob Frege revolutionised logic by introducing algebraic and quantificational methods. Propositional (sentential) logic treats whole statements as atoms connected by truth‑functional operators — negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, biconditional — and evaluates arguments via truth tables or natural deduction. The completeness and soundness of propositional calculus were proved by Bernays and Post, establishing that every tautology is derivable and every derivable formula is a tautology.

4.2 Predicate Logic, Modal Logic and Proof Theory

Predicate (first‑order) logic extends propositional logic with quantifiers (∀, ∃), variables, predicates and individual constants, allowing the formalisation of statements about objects and their relations. Frege’s Begriffsschrift and Russell‑Whitehead’s Principia Mathematica demonstrated that mathematics can be reduced to logic, though Gödel’s incompleteness theorems later showed inherent limits. Modal logic adds operators for necessity (□) and possibility (◇), interpreted via Kripke possible‑world semantics, enabling rigorous analysis of metaphysical, epistemic and deontic modalities. Proof theory investigates the structure of derivations: Gentzen’s sequent calculus and natural deduction provide normalisation and cut‑elimination theorems, revealing the constructive content of proofs. These developments underpin automated theorem proving, type theory and the foundations of computer science.

4.3 Informal Fallacies and Argument Evaluation

Beyond formal systems, informal logic catalogues patterns of reasoning that appear persuasive but are logically defective. Classical fallacies include ad hominem (attacking the person), straw man (misrepresenting the opponent), appeal to authority (illegitimate reliance on expertise), false dilemma (presenting only two extremes), slippery slope (unwarranted chain of consequences) and begging the question (circular reasoning). The pragma‑dialectical approach of van Eemeren and Grootendorst treats argumentation as a critical discussion governed by rules for confrontation, opening, argumentation and concluding stages. Evaluating real‑world arguments requires identifying the argument scheme, checking the acceptability, relevance and sufficiency of premises (the ARS criteria), and detecting hidden assumptions. Mastery of both formal and informal techniques equips the philosopher to construct rigorous proofs and to critique public discourse.

Post‑Kantian German Idealism: Hegel’s Dialectic and Marx’s Historical Materialism

After Kant’s critical philosophy limited knowledge to phenomena, German idealists sought to restore a systematic account of reality that could accommodate freedom, history and the absolute. Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit (1807) and Science of Logic (1812‑16) present a triadic dialectic — thesis, antithesis, synthesis — not as a formal rule but as the self‑development of Geist (Spirit). Each stage of consciousness (sense‑certainty, perception, understanding, self‑consciousness, reason, spirit, religion, absolute knowledge) negates its predecessor while preserving its truth content (Aufhebung). The culmination is the Absolute Idea, which knows itself as the totality of reality.

Hegel’s political philosophy in the Philosophy of Right (1820) articulates the modern state as the embodiment of ethical life (Sittlichkeit), integrating family, civil society and the state. Freedom is realized only within rational institutions; the constitutional monarchy, with a professional bureaucracy and estates assembly, mediates particular wills into universal law. Critics such as Schelling and the Young Hegelians (Feuerbach, Bauer) charged that Hegel’s system reifies the existing Prussian order.

Marx inverted Hegel’s idealist dialectic: “It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness.” In The German Ideology (1845‑46) and Capital (vol. 1, 1867) he replaces Geist with the mode of production. Historical materialism posits that the economic base (forces and relations of production) shapes the legal, political and ideological superstructure. Class struggle — between bourgeoisie and proletariat under capitalism — drives historical transitions from primitive communism through slavery, feudalism, capitalism to communism. The dialectic operates materially: contradictions within the capitalist mode (e.g., between socialised production and private appropriation) generate crises that propel revolutionary transformation.

Key distinctions for answer‑writing: Hegel’s dialectic is logical‑ontological, unfolding in the realm of ideas; Marx’s is socio‑economic, unfolding in concrete history. Hegel sees the state as the march of freedom; Marx sees the state as an instrument of class rule that will wither away. Examiners often ask to compare the concept of alienation: for Hegel alienation is the self‑externalisation of Spirit overcome in absolute knowledge; for Marx alienation is the worker’s estrangement from product, process, species‑being and fellow workers, removable only by abolishing private property.

Early Analytic Philosophy: Frege, Russell, and the Linguistic Turn

The analytic tradition originates in the late‑19th‑century revolt against British idealism and the quest for a logically perfect language. Gottlob Frege’s Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced a formal notation with quantifiers, variables and a rigorous distinction between sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung). His logicist programme aimed to derive arithmetic from pure logic; the paradox discovered by Russell (1901) — the set of all sets that do not contain themselves — forced a revision. Russell’s Principia Mathematica (1910‑13, with Whitehead) attempted to salvage logicism by a ramified theory of types, but Gödel’s incompleteness theorems (1931) later showed that any sufficiently strong formal system cannot be both complete and consistent.

Russell’s logical atomism (1918) held that the world consists of atomic facts mirrored by atomic propositions; molecular propositions are truth‑functions of these. His theory of descriptions (1905) solved puzzles about non‑referring terms (“The present King of France is bald”) by analysing definite descriptions as quantified expressions, eliminating the need for Meinongian objects. Ludwig Wittgenstein’s Tractatus Logico‑Philosophicus (1921) pushed the picture theory of language: propositions are pictures of possible states of affairs; the limits of language mark the limits of the world. The later Wittgenstein (Philosophical Investigations, 1953) abandoned the picture theory for the concept of language‑games, arguing that meaning is use within forms of life.

The Vienna Circle (Carnap, Schlick, Neurath) adopted the verification principle: a statement is cognitively meaningful only if empirically verifiable or tautological. This logical positivism dismissed metaphysics, ethics and theology as nonsensical. Quine’s “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951) attacked the analytic‑synthetic distinction and reductionism, ushering in a more holistic, naturalised epistemology. The linguistic turn thus shifted philosophy’s focus from metaphysical speculation to the analysis of language, logic and scientific methodology.

For examination purposes, distinguish three phases: (1) Frege’s formalisation and sense/reference; (2) Russell’s logical atomism and theory of descriptions; (3) Wittgenstein’s early picture theory versus later use‑theory. Typical questions ask to evaluate whether the verification principle is self‑refuting, or to explain how Russell’s theory of descriptions resolves the problem of negative existentials.

Worked Answer Technique
Explain how Russell’s theory of descriptions handles the sentence “The present King of France is bald” and why this avoids commitment to non‑existent entities.
Russell analyses the sentence as a conjunction of three quantified claims: (1) ∃x (KingOfFrance(x) ∧ ∀y (KingOfFrance(y) → y = x)) — there is exactly one King of France; (2) ∀x (KingOfFrance(x) → Bald(x)) — that unique individual is bald. The logical form is ∃x [KingOfFrance(x) ∧ ∀y (KingOfFrance(y) → y = x) ∧ Bald(x)]. Since the first conjunct is false (no such x exists), the whole conjunction is false, not meaningless. No reference to a non‑existent king is required; the description is eliminated in favour of quantifiers and predicates. This demonstrates how logical analysis dissolves the ontological puzzle.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a comparison, structure the answer in a two‑column table (e.g., Hegel vs Marx on dialectic, or early vs later Wittgenstein). Use the table to show contrast, then follow with a concise paragraph synthesising the philosophical significance. This format saves time and signals clear analytical thinking to the examiner.

Phenomenology and Existentialism: Husserl, Heidegger, Sartre

Edmund Husserl founded phenomenology as a rigorous science of consciousness, insisting on the epoché (bracketing) of the natural attitude to investigate the structures of intentional experience. In Logical Investigations (1900‑01) and Ideas I (1913) he distinguished noesis (act of consciousness) from noema (intentional content), arguing that every consciousness is consciousness of something. The transcendental reduction reveals the pure ego as the constitutive source of meaning.

Martin Heidegger, Husserl’s student, transformed phenomenology into fundamental ontology. Being and Time (1927) replaces the subject‑object schema with Dasein — the being for whom its own being is an issue. Dasein’s existential structures (being‑in‑the‑world, care, thrownness, projection, being‑towards‑death) disclose the meaning of Being (Sein) itself. The ontological difference between Being and beings (der ontologische Unterschied) is the central philosophical task. Heidegger’s later “turn” (die Kehre) emphasizes language as the house of Being and critiques technology’s enframing (Gestell).

Jean‑Paul Sartre radicalised existential phenomenology in Being and Nothingness (1943). He develops his own distinction between being‑in‑itself (en‑soi) and being‑for‑itself (pour‑soi), influenced by Heidegger but insists on radical freedom: consciousness is nothingness (néant) that nihilates the given. Bad faith (mauvaise foi) is the self‑deception of denying this freedom. Sartre’s ethics, sketched in Existentialism Is a Humanism (1946), holds that in choosing for oneself one chooses for all humanity, grounding responsibility in universalisability without appeal to a pre‑given essence.

Key exam points: Husserl’s epoché vs Heidegger’s hermeneutic phenomenology; the shift from consciousness to Dasein; Sartre’s claim “existence precedes essence” and its contrast with essentialist metaphysics. Questions often require evaluating whether Heidegger’s ontology successfully overcomes the subject‑object dichotomy, or whether Sartre’s notion of absolute freedom is compatible with social determinism.

Contemporary Indian Thought: Neo‑Vedanta, Gandhi’s Ethics, Ambedkar’s Social Philosophy

Neo‑Vedanta, articulated by Swami Vivekananda and later Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan, reinterprets Advaita for modern discourse. Vivekananda’s “practical Vedanta” stresses the divinity of every soul, service (seva) as worship, and religious universalism grounded in the Upanishadic equation Atman = Brahman. Radhakrishnan’s Indian Philosophy (1923) presents a comparative framework that positions Vedanta as the philosophical culmination of religious experience, emphasizing intuition (anubhava) over ritual.

M. K. Gandhi’s ethical philosophy integrates the Gita’s karma‑yoga with Tolstoyan non‑violence (ahimsa) and the Jain principle of anekantavada (many‑sidedness). Satyagraha is not passive resistance but active non‑violent non‑cooperation rooted in the conviction that means and ends are inseparable. The concept of trusteeship redefines property: wealth holders are trustees for society, not owners. Gandhi’s Hind Swaraj (1909) critiques modern civilisation’s mechanistic rationality and advocates self‑rule (swaraj) at individual and communal levels.

B. R. Ambedkar’s social philosophy, expressed in Annihilation of Caste (1936) and The Buddha and His Dhamma (1957), attacks the varna system as a graded inequality sanctioned by religious texts. He adopts a rationalist, democratic Buddhism that rejects metaphysical speculation in favour of social justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. Ambedkar’s constitutional vision (as chairman of the drafting committee) embeds these values in fundamental rights, directive principles and safeguards for depressed classes.

Comparative angles for answers: Neo‑Vedanta’s universalism vs Ambedkar’s particularist critique of caste; Gandhi’s moral purification of politics vs Ambedkar’s institutional‑legal approach; the role of scripture — Vivekananda re‑reads the Upanishads, Ambedkar rejects the Manusmriti. Examiners may ask to assess whether Gandhi’s trusteeship can function within a modern market economy, or to evaluate Ambedkar’s reinterpretation of Buddhism as a socially engaged ethics.

Philosophy of Science: Popper, Kuhn, and the Structure of Scientific Revolutions

Karl Popper’s The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934) proposes falsifiability as the demarcation criterion between science and non‑science. Scientific theories are conjectures that survive severe tests; they are never verified, only corroborated. The problem of induction is dissolved: science progresses by conjecture and refutation, not by enumerative induction. Popper’s critical rationalism extends to the open society, where piecemeal engineering replaces utopian planning.

Thomas Kuhn’s The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) challenges the cumulative view. Normal science operates within a paradigm — a shared set of exemplars, methods, and metaphysical commitments. Anomalies accumulate until a crisis precipitates a paradigm shift, a non‑cumulative, gestalt‑like transformation (e.g., Ptolemaic to Copernican, Newtonian to Einsteinian). Incommensurability means rival paradigms lack a common measure; theory choice involves values (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness) rather than pure logic.

Imre Lakatos reconciles Popper and Kuhn through research programmes: a hard core protected by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. Progressive programmes predict novel facts; degenerative ones only accommodate anomalies. Paul Feyerabend’s epistemological anarchism (“anything goes”) argues that methodological rules hinder scientific creativity, citing historical case studies where ad hoc moves advanced knowledge.

For answer‑writing, contrast Popper’s normative demarcation with Kuhn’s descriptive sociology of science. Note that Popper treats falsification as a logical asymmetry; Kuhn treats it as a historical process mediated by community consensus. Typical questions: “Is falsifiability a sufficient criterion for scientific status?” or “Explain Kuhn’s notion of incommensurability and its implications for scientific realism.”

  • Confusing Hegel’s logical dialectic with Marx’s materialist dialectic — always specify the ontological basis (Spirit vs mode of production).
  • Attributing the verification principle to Wittgenstein — it belongs to the Vienna Circle; Wittgenstein’s later work rejects it.
  • Equating Heidegger’s Dasein with a Cartesian subject — Dasein is being‑in‑the‑world, not a detached ego.
  • Presenting Gandhi’s ahimsa as mere pacifism — it is an active, strategic non‑violence linked to truth (satya).
  • Treating Ambedkar’s Buddhism as purely religious — his reinterpretation is explicitly political and ethical.
  • Stating that Popper’s falsifiability solves the problem of induction — Popper denies induction altogether; he does not “solve” it.
  • Claiming Kuhn’s paradigms are wholly incommensurable in a linguistic sense — Kuhn allows partial translation and shared observational language.

These advanced sections deepen the core syllabus by linking historical systems to their internal dynamics, critical receptions, and contemporary relevance, providing the analytical tools needed for high‑scoring answers.

Philosophy of Mind: Consciousness, Qualia, and the Mind‑Body Problem

The mind‑body problem asks how mental states relate to physical states. Classical dualism (Descartes) posits two substances: res cogitans (thinking) and res extensa (extended). Interactionism faces the causal closure of the physical: if every physical event has a physical cause, non‑physical mental events cannot affect the brain without violating conservation laws. Property dualism (e.g., Chalmers) retains a single substance but argues that phenomenal properties (qualia) are non‑reducible. The “hard problem” distinguishes explaining functions (easy problems) from explaining why there is something it is like to experience. Functionalism identifies mental states by causal roles; multiple realizability supports it, yet the inverted spectrum and absent qualia thought experiments challenge whether functional role captures phenomenal character. Physicalist responses include identity theory (type‑type or token‑token), eliminative materialism (Churchland), and non‑reductive physicalism (Davidson’s anomalous monism). The knowledge argument (Jackson’s Mary) claims that complete physical knowledge leaves out what it is like to see red; physicalists reply with the ability hypothesis (Lewis) or the phenomenal concept strategy (Loar). Contemporary debates involve integrated information theory, global workspace theory, and predictive processing as empirical frameworks for consciousness. In answer‑writing, distinguish ontological claims (what exists) from epistemic claims (what we can know), and specify which version of physicalism or dualism is under discussion.

Philosophy of Language: Meaning, Reference, and Speech Acts

Analytic philosophy of language investigates how linguistic expressions acquire meaning and how language performs actions. Frege’s sense‑reference distinction solves the identity puzzle: “Morning Star” and “Evening Star” share reference (Venus) but differ in sense (mode of presentation). Russell’s theory of descriptions treats definite descriptions as quantified phrases, eliminating reference to non‑existent entities. Kripke’s causal‑historical theory of reference argues that proper names are rigid designators fixed by an initial baptism and transmitted through a causal chain, opposing descriptivist accounts. Putnam’s Twin Earth thought experiment extends externalism to natural kind terms: meaning is not in the head. Speech act theory (Austin, Searle) classifies utterances into locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts; performatives (e.g., “I promise”) change normative status. Grice’s cooperative principle and conversational implicature explain how speakers convey more than literal meaning. The distinction between semantic content and pragmatic enrichment is crucial for questions on literal vs. speaker meaning. In examinations, be prepared to compare descriptivist vs. causal theories of reference, to analyse the force of an utterance using Searle’s taxonomy (assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, declarations), and to evaluate whether meaning is determined by internal mental states or external social practices.

Worked Answer Technique
Explain Kripke’s objection to the descriptivist theory of proper names and illustrate with the Gödel/Schmidt case.
Kripke argues that descriptivism fails because the description associated with a name may be false of the referent. In the Gödel/Schmidt scenario, speakers associate “Gödel” with “the prover of the incompleteness theorem”. If Schmidt actually proved it, the description picks out Schmidt, yet speakers still refer to Gödel. Kripke’s causal‑historical account explains this: the name “Gödel” was fixed at an initial baptism to the person Gödel, and the reference is transmitted through a causal chain regardless of any description. The answer should (1) state the descriptivist claim, (2) present the counterexample, (3) explain the rigid designator notion, and (4) conclude that reference is determined by historical chain, not by satisfying a description.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a comparison, structure the answer in a two‑column table (theory vs. theory) and then write a short synthesis paragraph. This saves time and makes the contrast visually clear for the examiner.

Political Philosophy: Justice, Liberty, and the State

Modern political philosophy centres on the justification of state authority and the distribution of rights and resources. Social contract theorists (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) differ on the state of nature and the terms of the contract: Hobbes argues for absolute sovereignty to avoid the war of all against all; Locke limits government to protection of life, liberty, and property, allowing revolution when trust is broken; Rousseau’s general will aims at collective self‑legislation, raising the problem of forcing people to be free. Rawls’ A Theory of Justice (1971) revives contractarianism behind a veil of ignorance, yielding two principles: equal basic liberties and the difference principle (inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged). Nozick’s entitlement theory (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974) defends a minimal state, arguing that any redistribution beyond voluntary exchange violates self‑ownership. Communitarians (Sandel, MacIntyre) critique the liberal “unencumbered self”, emphasizing embeddedness in communal traditions. The capability approach (Sen, Nussbaum) shifts focus from resources to substantive freedoms. Feminist political philosophy (Pateman, Okin) exposes how the public/private divide masks gendered power. In answers, distinguish procedural justice (fair processes) from distributive justice (fair outcomes), and clarify whether a theory is deontological (rights‑based) or consequentialist (outcome‑based).

Aesthetics: The Nature of Art and Aesthetic Judgment

Aesthetics examines the nature of beauty, art, and taste. Classical theories: Plato’s mimesis (art as imitation, twice removed from the Forms) leads to censorship; Aristotle’s catharsis defends tragedy’s emotional purification. Kant’s Critique of Judgment (1790) distinguishes the agreeable (subjective), the good (conceptual), and the beautiful (disinterested pleasure). The judgment of beauty claims universal validity without a concept, grounded in the free play of imagination and understanding. The sublime involves a feeling of overwhelming magnitude that reason can comprehend. Hegel historicises art as a stage of absolute spirit’s self‑knowledge, culminating in philosophy. Contemporary analytic aesthetics (Dickie’s institutional theory) defines art as whatever the artworld confers status upon; Danto’s “artworld” thesis argues that indiscernible objects can differ in art status due to theory‑laden interpretation. The definition of art remains contested: functional (art serves aesthetic experience), procedural (institutional), or historical (narrative). Aesthetic properties (e.g., elegance, grotesqueness) supervene on non‑aesthetic bases; the debate over aesthetic realism vs. anti‑realism mirrors meta‑ethical disputes. For examinations, be ready to contrast Kant’s disinterestedness with Bourdieu’s sociological critique, and to evaluate whether a definition of art can accommodate avant‑garde works that reject traditional aesthetic criteria.

Philosophy of Religion: Classical Arguments, the Problem of Evil, and Religious Language

Philosophy of religion analyses the rationality of religious belief. Classical theistic arguments: the cosmological argument (Aquinas’ Five Ways, Leibniz’s principle of sufficient reason) infers a necessary being; the teleological argument (Paley’s watchmaker, fine‑tuning) infers design; the ontological argument (Anselm, Descartes, Plantinga’s modal version) claims existence follows from the concept of a maximally great being. The problem of evil challenges the coexistence of an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent God with gratuitous suffering. Logical problem (Mackie) claims inconsistency; evidential problem (Rowe) claims improbability. Theodicies: free‑will defense (Plantinga), soul‑making (Hick), skeptical theism (we cannot know God’s reasons). Religious language: verificationism (Ayer) renders theological statements meaningless; Wittgensteinian language‑games treat religious utterances as expressions of form of life; analogical language (Aquinas) predicates terms of God analogically, not univocally or equivocally. The via negativa (negative theology) says God can only be described by what God is not. In answer‑writing, specify which version of an argument you discuss, distinguish logical from evidential problems of evil, and evaluate whether a theodicy preserves divine attributes without ad hoc moves.

  • Confusing property dualism with substance dualism — always clarify the ontological commitment.
  • Attributing the causal theory of reference to Frege — it belongs to Kripke and Putnam.
  • Treating Rawls’ difference principle as a utilitarian maximin — it is a lexical priority rule, not a simple maximisation.
  • Equating Kant’s disinterested pleasure with mere subjectivity — Kant claims universal communicability.
  • Presenting the ontological argument as a single proof — distinguish Anselm’s, Descartes’, and Plantinga’s modal versions.
  • Assuming the problem of evil is solved by any theodicy — each theodicy addresses a specific formulation and leaves others open.
  • Avoiding these pitfalls sharpens analytical precision in every answer.

Philosophy of Mathematics: Ontology, Epistemology, and the Limits of Formalisation

The philosophy of mathematics investigates the nature of mathematical objects, the status of mathematical truth, and the relationship between formal systems and informal practice. Three classical positions dominate the landscape. Platonism holds that numbers, sets, and functions exist independently of human thought in an abstract realm; mathematical statements are discovered, not invented. Formalism, associated with Hilbert, treats mathematics as the manipulation of symbols according to syntactic rules; truth is replaced by provability within a system. Intuitionism, championed by Brouwer, insists that mathematical objects are mental constructions and that the law of excluded middle fails for infinite totalities.

Logicism, pursued by Frege and Russell, attempted to reduce mathematics to logic. Gödel’s incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that any sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatizable system cannot be both complete and consistent, dealing a decisive blow to Hilbert’s programme and to a naïve logicist reduction. Structuralism (Benacerraf, Shapiro) shifts focus from objects to structures: mathematics is the study of patterns exemplified by any system satisfying the relevant axioms. This view sidesteps Benacerraf’s epistemological dilemma — how can we have knowledge of causally inert abstract entities? — by denying that mathematical objects have an identity beyond their structural roles.

Contemporary debates include the applicability of mathematics (Wigner’s “unreasonable effectiveness”), the status of large cardinal axioms in set theory, and the rise of experimental mathematics where computer-assisted proofs challenge traditional notions of rigor. In examinations, distinguish between ontological commitments (what exists) and epistemological access (how we know), and be ready to evaluate whether a given position can accommodate Gödel’s results without ad hoc restrictions.

Social Epistemology: Testimony, Injustice, and Collective Knowledge

Social epistemology examines how knowledge depends on interpersonal and institutional relations. Testimony is the primary vehicle for most of our beliefs; the reductionist view (Hume) demands independent positive reasons to trust a speaker, while the anti‑reductionist view (Coady, Burge) treats testimony as a basic source of justification unless defeated. Epistemic injustice, introduced by Miranda Fricker, identifies two forms: testimonial injustice (a speaker’s credibility is deflated due to prejudice) and hermeneutical injustice (a gap in collective interpretive resources prevents someone from understanding their own experience). Both reveal that epistemic practices are embedded in power structures.

Peer disagreement asks whether discovering that an epistemic peer holds a contrary belief should lead to conciliation (equal weight) or steadfastness (maintaining one’s view). The conciliationist (Elga, Christensen) argues for significant belief revision; the steadfast view (Kelly, Titelbaum) permits retaining confidence if one’s reasoning is transparent. Epistemic communities (Longino) and standpoint epistemology (Harding, Collins) stress that marginalized perspectives can yield epistemic advantages by exposing blind spots in dominant frameworks. Social epistemology of science (Kitcher, Solomon) analyses division of cognitive labour, peer review, and the role of values in theory choice.

In answer‑writing, specify which notion of “peer” you employ (evidential parity, reliability parity), distinguish testimonial from hermeneutical injustice, and evaluate whether a proposed solution to disagreement preserves both rationality and the social dimension of knowledge.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: Explain the distinction between testimonial injustice and hermeneutical injustice, and give a concrete example of each.
Testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer assigns a deflated credibility to a speaker because of identity prejudice (e.g., a woman’s expert testimony in a technical meeting is dismissed because of gender bias). Hermeneutical injustice occurs when a subject lacks the conceptual resources to make sense of a significant social experience because those resources are absent from the collective hermeneutical repertoire (e.g., before the term “sexual harassment” existed, victims could not articulate their experience as a recognisable wrong). The key difference: testimonial injustice is a credibility deficit in a specific exchange; hermeneutical injustice is a structural gap in shared interpretive tools that precedes any particular testimony.
Exam‑Day Strategy
For social epistemology questions, first map the conceptual terrain: identify the epistemic agent, the social relation, and the normative standard (justification, credibility, understanding). Then locate the specific theory (reductionism, anti‑reductionism, conciliationism, standpoint) and note its critics. Use a two‑column table in your rough notes to contrast positions on a single axis (e.g., source of justification vs. defeasibility). This prevents conflation of distinct debates and lets you write a structured answer under time pressure.

Philosophy of Action: Intentionality, Reasons, and Free Will

The philosophy of action analyses what it is to act intentionally, how reasons explain actions, and whether human agency is compatible with determinism. Davidson’s anomalous monism argues that mental events are identical with physical events (token‑identity) but that there are no strict psychophysical laws; reasons rationalise actions by making them intelligible, not by causally necessitating them in a law‑like way. The causal theory of action (Davidson, later refined by Smith) holds that an intentional action is a bodily movement caused by a belief‑desire pair in the right way. Deviant causal chains (e.g., a nervous climber’s desire to let go causes a slip that results in letting go) challenge the sufficiency of mere causation; solutions invoke “guidance” or “control” conditions.

Practical reason debates whether reasons for action are desire‑based (Humean) or can be grounded in objective values (Kantian, Scanlon). Weakness of will (akrasia) — acting against one’s better judgment — tests the unity of agency; Davidson’s “judgment‑sensitive” account treats akratic action as caused by a non‑dominant evaluative judgment. Free will positions: compatibilism (Hobbes, Frankfurt) defines freedom as acting in accordance with one’s higher‑order desires; libertarianism (Kane, O’Connor) requires indeterministic “self‑forming actions”; hard determinism denies free will altogether. Collective intentionality (Searle’s “we‑intentions”, Bratman’s shared cooperative activity) extends agency to groups, raising questions about moral responsibility of corporations and states.

In examinations, distinguish between the metaphysical question (is determinism true?) and the conceptual question (what counts as a free action?), and be prepared to evaluate whether a given compatibilist account can accommodate manipulation arguments (e.g., Frankfurt cases).

Philosophy of Technology: From Heidegger to Value‑Sensitive Design

Philosophy of technology interrogates the essence, normativity, and societal impact of technological artefacts and systems. Heidegger’s “The Question Concerning Technology” (1954) argues that modern technology reveals the world as standing‑reserve (Bestand), a mode of enframing (Gestell) that reduces entities to resources. Ellul’s The Technological Society (1954) posits technique as an autonomous, efficiency‑driven logic that subordinates ends to means. Feenberg’s critical theory of technology distinguishes instrumental, substantivist, and pluralist perspectives, advocating democratic rationalisation — redesigning technology to reflect diverse values.

Value‑Sensitive Design (VSD) (Friedman, Kahn) operationalises ethical analysis into iterative design cycles: conceptual (identify values), empirical (study stakeholders), technical (implement). Ethics of artificial intelligence addresses alignment, transparency, bias, and the moral status of autonomous systems; key frameworks include the Asilomar AI Principles and the EU’s AI Act risk‑based classification. Technological determinism vs. social constructivism: determinists (e.g., early Ellul) claim technology shapes society inevitably; constructivists (Pinch, Bijker) emphasise interpretive flexibility and the role of social groups in stabilising artefacts. Responsible innovation (Stilgoe, Owen) integrates anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion, and responsiveness into research governance.

When answering, clarify whether you are analysing a specific technology (e.g., facial recognition) or a philosophical thesis (e.g., enframing), and show how normative evaluation draws on both phenomenological insight and institutional analysis.

Comparative Philosophy: Method, Translation, and Cross‑Cultural Dialogue

Comparative philosophy seeks rigorous dialogue between traditions — primarily Indian, Chinese, and Western — without collapsing differences into a false unity. Methodological challenges include incommensurability (Kuhn, applied to conceptual schemes), translation indeterminacy (Quine), and the risk of orientalism (Said) or reverse orientalism. Scholars advocate hermeneutic charity (Gadamer) combined with critical reflexivity about one’s own presuppositions.

Concrete cross‑cultural engagements: Nyāya logic and analytic philosophy — both develop formal inference rules, but Nyāya’s anumāna includes a pragmatic “application” step absent in Fregean syllogisms. Madhyamaka emptiness (śūnyatā) and phenomenology — Nāgārjuna’s denial of intrinsic nature resonates with Husserl’s bracketing, yet Madhyamaka’s dialectical negation targets metaphysical reification, not epistemic suspension. Confucian role ethics and virtue ethics — both centre on character, but Confucianism grounds virtues in relational roles (filial piety) rather than individual flourishing. Global justice draws on ubuntu (African communalism), buen vivir (Andean), and capabilities approach to challenge liberal individualism.

In exam answers, avoid “parallelism” that merely lists similarities; instead, articulate a specific philosophical problem (e.g., the nature of self) and show how each tradition’s resources illuminate or obscure a solution, noting where translation choices affect the comparison.

  • Conflating Platonism in mathematics with metaphysical realism about universals — they are distinct debates.
  • Treating testimonial injustice as merely a credibility deficit without recognizing the structural hermeneutical dimension.
  • Equating Davidson’s anomalous monism with epiphenomenalism — Davidson insists mental events are causally efficacious as physical events.
  • Assuming technological determinism entails that all social change is caused by technology; determinism is a thesis about the direction of influence, not exclusivity.
  • Presenting comparative philosophy as a catalogue of “Eastern vs. Western” parallels without a shared problematic.
  • Ignoring the role of deviant causal chains when defending the causal theory of action.
  • Misidentifying the conciliationist position in peer disagreement as requiring full suspension of judgment rather than proportional revision.

These traps highlight the precision required when moving from textbook summaries to the analytical depth expected in advanced philosophy papers.

Philosophy of Law: Natural Law, Legal Positivism, and Dworkin’s Interpretivism

The central debate in jurisprudence concerns the relationship between law and morality. Natural‑law theorists (Aquinas, Finnis) argue that an unjust law lacks full legal validity; the binding force of a legal norm depends on its conformity to objective moral principles discoverable by reason. Legal positivists (Hart, Kelsen) separate the existence of law from its moral merit: a rule is law if it satisfies the system’s rule of recognition, regardless of its content. Hart’s “minimum content of natural law” concedes that any viable legal system must contain certain moral‑like provisions (e.g., prohibitions on violence) for pragmatic reasons, but he insists this does not collapse the separation thesis.

Hart’s Rule of Recognition and the Internal Point of View

Hart distinguishes primary rules (imposing duties) from secondary rules (conferring powers). The ultimate secondary rule — the rule of recognition — is a social practice among officials who accept it as a standard for identifying law. This “internal point of view” explains legal obligation without invoking moral authority. Critics (Fuller, Dworkin) argue that the rule of recognition cannot be identified without moral judgment, because officials must interpret what counts as a valid source.

Dworkin’s Interpretivism and the Right Answer Thesis

Dworkin rejects the positivist picture of law as a set of discrete rules. He proposes that law is an interpretive concept: judges construct the best moral justification for the community’s legal practices, yielding a unique “right answer” in hard cases. Principles, unlike rules, have weight and can conflict; the judge’s task is to fit and justify the legal scheme as a whole. This view blurs the law‑morality divide while preserving objectivity through the ideal of integrity.

Philosophy of History: Hegel, Marx, and Collingwood’s Re‑enactment Thesis

Philosophy of history asks whether human events exhibit a rational structure and how historians can know the past. Hegel conceives history as the progressive self‑realisation of Spirit (Geist) through a dialectical process of negation and sublation. The nation‑state embodies ethical life (Sittlichkeit); world‑historical individuals (Napoleon) are instruments of the World Spirit. Marx inverts Hegel: material production determines consciousness; history unfolds through class struggle, moving from primitive communism to capitalism and ultimately communism. Both treat history as a law‑governed, teleological process.

Collingwood’s Re‑enactment and the Autonomy of History

R. G. Collingwood rejects grand narratives. In The Idea of History (1946) he argues that historical knowledge consists in re‑enacting the thought of past agents. The historian does not merely collect facts but reconstructs the reasoning that led an agent to act. This makes history a science of mind, distinct from natural science, because its object — past thought — is accessible only through sympathetic understanding. Collingwood’s “logic of question and answer” holds that every historical statement answers a question the agent implicitly posed.

Methodological Implications

  • Hegel and Marx justify philosophical history as a priori; Collingwood insists on empirical reconstruction of thought.
  • Collingwood’s re‑enactment counters historicist relativism: the past is knowable because thought is universal.
  • Critics (Popper, Dray) charge that re‑enactment collapses into empathy and cannot guarantee objectivity.

When answering, contrast the deterministic teleology of Hegel/Marx with Collingwood’s emphasis on the historian’s active reconstruction, and note how each view shapes the criteria for historical explanation.

Worked Answer Technique
Explain how Collingwood’s re‑enactment thesis addresses the problem of historical objectivity, and evaluate whether it successfully avoids the relativism of historicism.

Collingwood argues that historical knowledge is not a mirror of past events but the historian’s re‑enactment of the agent’s thought. Because thought is governed by logical norms, the historian’s reconstruction is constrained by the requirement that the re‑enacted reasoning be coherent and responsive to the questions the agent faced. This provides a standard of correctness independent of the historian’s culture, thereby securing objectivity. However, critics contend that the historian’s own conceptual scheme inevitably shapes the questions asked, re‑introducing a perspectival element. A balanced answer acknowledges the internal logical constraint while noting the residual interpretive flexibility.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a comparison of “grand narrative” versus “re‑enactment” approaches, structure the answer in three moves: (1) state the metaphysical commitment of each (teleology vs. autonomy of mind), (2) show how each treats evidence (dialectical necessity vs. question‑answer logic), (3) evaluate the epistemic payoff (predictive power vs. interpretive rigor).

Philosophy of Economics: Rational Choice, Welfare Economics, and the Capability Approach

Philosophy of economics scrutinises the normative and methodological foundations of economic theory. Standard microeconomics adopts the rational‑choice model: agents have complete, transitive preferences and maximise expected utility. This framework underpins welfare economics, where Pareto efficiency and the Kaldor‑Hicks criterion evaluate allocations. However, the model’s descriptive adequacy and normative sufficiency are contested.

Critiques of Rational Choice

  • Allais and Ellsberg paradoxes demonstrate systematic violations of expected‑utility axioms.
  • Behavioural economics (Kahneman, Tversky) shows framing effects, loss aversion, and bounded rationality.
  • Sen argues that preference‑based welfare ignores the informational base of well‑being; preferences can be adaptive, conditioned by deprivation.

Sen’s Capability Approach

Amartya Sen replaces utility with capabilities — the real freedoms people have to achieve valuable functionings (being nourished, participating in community). The approach shifts evaluation from resources or satisfaction to substantive opportunities. It informs the Human Development Index and the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals. Nussbaum extends Sen by specifying a list of central capabilities as a threshold of justice.

Methodological Individualism vs. Structural Explanation

Methodological individualists (Mises, Popper) insist that social phenomena be explained by individual actions. Structuralists (Bourdieu, Giddens) argue that institutions and norms constrain and constitute agency. The debate bears on policy: if poverty is a capability failure, redistribution of resources alone may not expand freedoms without institutional reform.

Philosophy of Biology: Species, Teleology, and the Units of Selection Debate

Philosophy of biology examines conceptual issues arising from evolutionary theory, genetics, and ecology. Three enduring problems are the ontological status of species, the legitimacy of teleological language, and the level at which natural selection operates.

Species Concepts

  • Biological Species Concept (Mayr): species are groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups.
  • Phylogenetic Species Concept (Cracraft): species are the smallest diagnosable monophyletic groups.
  • Ecological Species Concept (Van Valen): species occupy distinct adaptive zones.

No single concept captures all taxa; pluralism is now standard, but it raises questions about the reality of species as natural kinds versus pragmatic categories.

Teleology and Function

Biologists routinely speak of “the function of the heart is to pump blood.” Philosophers distinguish etiological (selected‑effect) accounts (Wright, Millikan) — a trait’s function is what it was selected for — from systemic (causal‑role) accounts (Cummins) — a trait’s function is its contribution to the system’s capacity. The selected‑effect view naturalises teleology by grounding it in evolutionary history.

Units of Selection

The classic debate: does selection act on genes (Dawkins’ “selfish gene”), individuals (organisms), groups (Wynne‑Edwards, later multilevel selection), or species (Vrba, Gould)? A prominent view accepts multilevel selection: selection can operate at several hierarchical levels simultaneously, with the gene’s‑eye view providing a bookkeeping device. Price’s equation formalises the partitioning of evolutionary change across levels.
