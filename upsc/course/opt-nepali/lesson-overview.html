Key Concepts

Nepali Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Nepali is Indo-Aryan (Pahari group) and is written in Devanagari script. It is spoken mainly in Sikkim, Darjeeling and parts of Assam. It was added to the Eighth Schedule by the 71st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Bhanubhakta tradition, Modern Nepali movement, Sahitya Akademi phase.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: traditional rasa criticism, romantic and nationalist criticism, modernist and diaspora approaches
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Early period (18th–19th c.): Bhanubhakta Acharya and the beginnings of modern Nepali literature.
  • Early twentieth century: Laxmi Prasad Devkota, Balkrishna Sama and drama.
  • Modern period (mid-20th c.–present): Poetry, fiction and diaspora writing.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Nepali literature.
Answer: Early period (18th–19th c.) — Bhanubhakta Acharya and the beginnings of modern Nepali literature.; Early twentieth century — Laxmi Prasad Devkota, Balkrishna Sama and drama.; Modern period (mid-20th c.–present) — Poetry, fiction and diaspora writing..

Standard Sources

  • Sahitya Akademi histories of Nepali literature
  • Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature
  • University syllabi and prescribed texts
  • Standard Nepali literary histories

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Nepali authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Nepali literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Comparative Dynamics: Bhanubhakta vs. Contemporary Poetics

While the syllabus mandates the study of Bhanubhakta Acharya as the father of Nepali literature and the modern poets like Balkrishna Sama or Laxmi Prasad Devkota, a high-scoring answer requires moving beyond simple chronology to comparative poetics. The examiner often twists questions by asking for the "evolution of sentiment" rather than just listing names. You must understand the shift from devotional and didactic diction in the early period to psychological realism and existential angst in the modern era.

The Mechanism: The transition is not linear but dialectical. Bhanubhakta's use of Sanskritized vocabulary aimed at standardization, whereas contemporary poets like Bhupi Sherchan or Mukunda Rana reintroduced colloquialisms and local idioms to democratize language. A senior-level answer must highlight this tension between standardization (Paper I focus) and vernacular authenticity (Paper II focus).

Technique: The Comparative Bridge
Question: "How does the linguistic approach of Bhanubhakta Acharya differ from the 'New Poetry' movement of the 1970s, and what are the implications for national identity?"
Answer Structure:
1. Linguistic Basis: Bhanubhakta utilized a Sanskrit-heavy vocabulary to create a unified literary language (Kavi-Bhasha), aiming for pan-regional intelligibility.
2. The Shift: The New Poetry movement rejected this elitist lexicon, embracing the spoken dialects of Kathmandu and surrounding valleys to reflect urban reality.
3. Implication: This shift marks a move from a 'constructed' national identity based on religious texts to an 'organic' identity rooted in daily life and social struggles. Mentioning specific works like 'Ramayana' (Bhanubhakta) vs. collections by Bhupi Sherchan solidifies the argument.

Theoretical Criticism: Applying Western Frameworks to Nepali Context

Paper II often demands critical evaluation of texts using theories not explicitly named in the syllabus but implied through the study of literary criticism. The examiner expects you to apply concepts like Mimesis (imitation of reality) or Defamiliarization (making the familiar strange) to Nepali novels and poems.

The Mechanism: Do not merely define these terms; apply them. For instance, when analyzing Devkota's 'Nagarkarma', use Mimesis to discuss how he mirrored the decay of feudal society. When analyzing Sama's poetry, discuss Defamiliarization in how he broke traditional meter (Chhand) to create new rhythmic patterns.

Technique: Theoretical Application
Question: "Critically examine Laxmi Prasad Devkota's 'Nagarkarma' using the lens of Social Realism." (Note: While Social Realism is a movement, applying it theoretically is advanced).
Answer Structure:
1. Context: Post-1950s Nepal, where feudal structures were crumbling but urban poverty was rising.
2. Theoretical Lens: Social Realism demands the depiction of life 'as it is' without romanticizing suffering. Devkota's protagonist is not a hero but a victim of systemic failure.
3. Critical Evaluation: Unlike traditional Nepali literature which often ended with moral lessons, 'Nagarkarma' ends in ambiguity and despair, reflecting the modernist crisis. Quote the line regarding the 'city as a machine' to show your close reading.

Examiner's Twist: The Diaspora and Gender Dimensions

A common trap is ignoring the diaspora literature or treating gender issues only as a sub-topic. The examiner frequently asks about "Nepali Literature in English" or "Women Writers" to test your awareness of global Nepali identity. This requires knowledge of authors like Pushpa Thapa, Usha Rajbhandari, or poets writing in exile.

The Mechanism: These sections often appear as exceptions to the main canon. You must treat them not as outliers but as integral parts of the "Modern Period" (mid-20th c.–present). The strategy is to link local struggles with global themes like migration, displacement, and feminist critique.

Technique: Exception Handling
Question: "Discuss the role of women writers in reshaping Nepali literary narratives post-1990." (This tests knowledge outside the core male-dominated canon).
Answer Structure:
1. Historical Context: Pre-1990, women were largely peripheral figures or muses. Post-1990 democratization allowed female voices to emerge.
2. Key Figures: Mention Usha Rajbhandari (poetry) and Pushpa Thapa (fiction). Highlight their focus on domestic politics and the female body as a site of resistance.
3. Synthesis: Conclude that these writers expanded the definition of 'Nepali Literature' from a monolingual, male-centric discourse to a multilingual, gender-inclusive one.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Sandwich" Method for Theory Questions
When an examiner asks a theoretical question (e.g., about criticism or movements), do not start with the definition. Start with the Context (historical background of Nepal at that time). Place the Theory/Concept in the middle, applying it directly to a Nepali text. End with the Implication (how this changed the course of literature). This structure ensures you get marks for both content and critical thinking.
  • Ignoring the Diaspora: Failing to mention authors who write in English or about the Nepali experience abroad, which is a significant part of the modern syllabus.
  • Over-reliance on Plot Summaries: In Paper II, describing the story of a novel without analyzing its linguistic style or social critique will result in low marks.
  • Misidentifying Periods: Confusing the "Modern" period (post-1950s) with the "Early Modern" (Bhanubhakta era). Ensure you clearly demarcate the timeline in your answer headers.
  • Neglecting Script and Dialect: In Paper I, failing to discuss how dialect variation (e.g., Western vs. Eastern Nepal) influenced standardization is a critical oversight.
  • Generic Citations: Quoting English translations of Nepali poetry without acknowledging the translator or the nuance lost in translation.
