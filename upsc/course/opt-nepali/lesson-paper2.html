Nepali Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism, Explanation and Essays

Prescribed Texts and Close Reading

  • Bhanubhakta Ramayana — Bhanubhakta Acharya: Foundational Nepali retelling of the Ramayana.
  • Muna Madan — Laxmi Prasad Devkota: Narrative poem of love and migration.
  • Sirish Ko Phool — Parijat: Modern Nepali novel.
  • Modern Nepali poetry — Modern Nepali poets: Twentieth-century poetry.

Literary Criticism

traditional rasa criticism, romantic and nationalist criticism, modernist and diaspora approaches

Explanation (Vyakhya) Strategy

  • Context: identify the text, author, period and speaker.
  • Literal meaning: paraphrase the passage in simple Nepali or English.
  • Theme: state the central idea and the emotional tone.
  • Form: comment on metre, imagery, diction and figures of speech.
  • Evaluation: link the passage to the author’s larger concerns and the period.

Essay Questions

  • Define the literary issue in the question, then take a clear position.
  • Support the argument with at least three texts or authors.
  • Include one counter-argument and respond to it.
  • Conclude with the significance of the issue for Nepali literature.
Solved Example: Explanation
Question: Explain a prescribed passage from Bhanubhakta Ramayana.
Answer: Identify the speaker and situation, paraphrase the lines, state the theme, comment on imagery and form, and connect the passage to the work’s central concern. A good explanation moves from literal meaning to literary evaluation.

Common Traps

  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing the passage.
  • Using a translation without naming the translator.
  • Ignoring the prescribed text and writing general remarks.
  • Mixing authors and works from different periods.
  • Making claims about influence without evidence.
Mains strategy
For explanation questions, use the five-step structure: context → literal meaning → theme → form → evaluation. For essay questions, write a thesis statement in the first paragraph, use three body paragraphs with textual evidence, and end with a balanced conclusion. Keep quotations short and accurate.

Advanced Section: The Mechanics of Intertextuality and the 'Twist' in Examination Logic

While standard preparation focuses on summarizing prescribed texts, advanced mastery requires understanding the mechanisms of intertextuality—how Nepali literature constructs meaning through dialogue with itself. The examiner often twists concepts by asking candidates to identify a specific literary device not as an isolated feature, but as a response to a historical or social shift.

The Mechanism of 'Varna' (Color) and Symbolic Density:

In Muna Madan, the color red is not merely descriptive; it functions as a semantic operator linking blood, passion, and revolutionary violence. A serious aspirant must analyze how Devkota subverts traditional poetic imagery to serve a modernist agenda. Similarly, in Bhanubhakta Ramayana, the description of nature often mirrors the internal state of Ravana or Rama, a technique known as rasa-abhinaya (expression of sentiment) evolving into psychological realism.

The Examiner's Twist:

Candidates frequently fail when asked to compare two passages from different eras because they treat them chronologically rather than thematically. The examiner may present a quote from a 19th-century poet and ask for its relevance in the context of 20th-century modernism. The trap lies in assuming continuity where there is rupture. For instance, comparing the 'idealized love' of traditional Geet with the 'tragic migration' in Devkota requires recognizing the shift from communal identity to individual alienation.

Technique: Comparative Intertextual Analysis
Question: "Analyze how the concept of 'home' (ghar) evolves from Bhanubhakta's time to Devkota's era, citing specific textual evidence. Why does the examiner often reject answers that focus only on physical location?"
Solution:
The concept of 'home' shifts from a divine, immutable space in Bhanubhakta's Ramayana (where Ayodhya is the center of dharma) to a displaced, psychological space in Devkota's Muna Madan. In Bhanubhakta, home is synonymous with order and duty. In Devkota, 'home' becomes a site of trauma and migration.

Textual Evidence: Contrast the description of Ayodhya in the Ramayana with the 'foreign land' imagery in Muna Madan.

Examiner's Logic: Answers focusing only on physical location fail because they miss the socio-political critique. The examiner expects you to link the loss of 'home' to the colonial context and the struggle for national identity, not just geography.

Advanced Section: Critical Theory Application in Nepali Contexts

Standard criticism often lists schools (Romantic, Realist). Advanced mastery involves applying Western critical theories to local texts without forcing them. This is crucial for essay questions asking about "Post-colonial readings" or "Feminist perspectives."

Feminist Criticism in Nepali Literature:

When analyzing Sirish Ko Phool or works by Parijat, do not stop at describing the female protagonist's suffering. Apply feminist theory to examine how the narrative voice either silences or amplifies female agency. For example, in traditional texts, women are often objects of sringar (beauty); in modernist texts, they become subjects of existential crisis. The examiner twists this by asking: "Does the text reinforce patriarchy or subvert it?" A nuanced answer acknowledges the tension where a character might be oppressed yet articulates that oppression poetically.

The 'Diaspora' Lens:

Nepali diaspora literature often deals with displacement. The examiner may ask how this mirrors themes in Muna Madan. The key is to identify the "hybrid identity"—the character who belongs nowhere and everywhere. This connects to broader post-colonial theory where language itself becomes a site of conflict (Nepali vs. English).

Technique: Applying Feminist Theory to a Prescribed Text
Question: "Discuss the representation of women in Bhanubhakta's Ramayana versus modern Nepali novels. How does the shift reflect changing social values?"
Solution:
In Bhanubhakta, Sita is the ideal of purity and sacrifice; her voice is internalized through devotion. Her agency is limited to moral endurance.

In modern novels like Sirish Ko Phool, women are depicted with complex interiority, often challenging domestic norms. The shift reflects the transition from a caste-based, religious society to a secular, individualistic one.

Critical Twist: Do not simply say "women became stronger." Argue that while agency increased, new forms of social pressure emerged, creating a more complex, ambiguous representation.

Advanced Section: Structural Analysis and Metre Variations

Basic analysis notes the metre. Advanced analysis explains why a specific metre was chosen for a specific emotional effect. Nepali poetry relies heavily on matra-chhand (syllabic meter) and akhar-chhand (alphabetic meter).

The Mechanics of Metre:

In Muna Madan, Devkota often breaks strict classical metres to mimic the irregularity of human emotion. The examiner may ask: "Why does the poet switch from a slow, heavy metre to a rapid one in stanza X?" The answer lies in the rhythm matching the heartbeat of the speaker. In prose like Sirish Ko Phool, sentence structure mimics the flow of consciousness (stream of consciousness), which is a modernist technique distinct from traditional narrative.

Exceptions and Anomalies:

Textbooks often generalize that "modern poetry is free verse." However, many Nepali modernists still use structured metres but subvert the rhyme scheme. Recognizing this exception is vital. If a poem looks like free verse but has a hidden rhythmic pattern, identifying it demonstrates deep textual competence.

Technique: Structural Rhythm Analysis
Question: "Analyze the structural shift in a specific passage from a modern poem. How does the break in metre enhance the theme?"
Solution:
Identify the initial regularity (e.g., 10-syllable lines) which establishes order.

Locate the disruption (a line that is too short or too long, or a sudden silence).

Interpretation: The break signifies the collapse of social order or the intrusion of trauma. The visual layout on the page (if analyzing a printed text) might also show irregular spacing, reinforcing the theme of fragmentation.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling 'Unknown' Texts in Essays
If an essay question references a text you have not studied deeply, do not panic. Use the "Universal Archetype" strategy. Connect the specific text to a broader literary movement (e.g., Romanticism) or a universal theme (e.g., exile). Cite two other prescribed texts that share similar themes to build a robust argument even if your primary example is less familiar. Always ground abstract claims in concrete textual details, even if they are brief.
  • Over-Generalization: Avoid saying "All modern poets use free verse." Specificity wins marks.
  • Ignoring Metre: Never mention the metre of a poem without explaining its emotional function.
  • Chronological Fallacy: Do not assume that later writers automatically improved upon earlier ones; critique based on context, not timeline.
  • Lack of Evidence: Claims about "social change" must be backed by a specific line or scene from the text.
  • Ignoring the 'Twist': When asked about tradition vs. modernity, do not present it as a straight line; highlight the contradictions and hybridities.

Historical Materialism and the Class Struggle in Nepali Literature

To move beyond surface-level thematic analysis, one must apply Historical Materialism to Nepali literature. This framework posits that literary forms are not merely aesthetic choices but reflections of specific economic bases and class relations. In the transition from the Rana regime to the Panchayat era, the literary landscape shifted from feudal patronage (often supporting Sanskritized texts) to a bourgeois public sphere where vernacular novels emerged as tools for social critique. For instance, in analyzing Bhimsen Thapa or early modernist poetry, one must identify the "feudal residue" in the narrative structure versus the "bourgeois awakening" in the content. The examiner may ask: "How does the depiction of land ownership in a novel reflect the agrarian crisis of the 1950s?" A robust answer connects the text's portrayal of landlord oppression to the historical reality of the 1959 peasant movements. It is crucial not to conflate "feudalism" with "tradition"; rather, show how literature exposes the material conditions (land, labor, capital) that sustain power structures. The shift from Chhand-based poetry to free verse in the 1960s cannot be explained solely by aesthetic preference; it mirrors the breakdown of traditional caste hierarchies and the rise of a secular, urban intelligentsia seeking new modes of expression.

Examiner's trap
A common error is attributing social change in literature solely to "Western influence." Correct answer: Social change is endogenous; Western ideas were filtered through local material conditions (e.g., the failure of feudal land systems). Always ground abstract concepts like "liberation" in specific economic realities mentioned in the text.
Exam-Day Strategy
When analyzing a text for class struggle, look for the "economic undercurrent." Does the protagonist work on land? Do they own property? Is there a mention of rent or wages? These details anchor your Marxist analysis in textual evidence rather than theoretical abstraction.

The Role of Language Policy and Standardization in Literary Canon Formation

Literary criticism in Nepal must always account for the political economy of language. The standardization of Nepali (Khas Kura) as a national tongue was not an organic evolution but a deliberate state project intertwined with nation-building. In Paper II, questions often probe the tension between "pure" Nepali and dialectal influences. The examiner may ask: "How does the inclusion of Tharu or Tamang vocabulary in contemporary poetry challenge the hegemony of Khas Kura?" A nuanced response must reference the historical context of language policy (e.g., the 1950 Constitution vs. Panchayat era restrictions). It is vital to distinguish between "dialect" and "language" in this context; dialects were often suppressed or stigmatized, while standard Nepali became the vehicle for state authority. When discussing texts like Bhaktapur or works by contemporary writers who incorporate local idioms, one must analyze whether this is an act of resistance against linguistic homogenization or a stylistic flourish that inadvertently reinforces elite norms. The concept of "linguistic purism" must be critiqued: does the exclusion of dialects truly preserve purity, or does it erase valid cultural expressions? This connects to broader post-colonial debates where language becomes a site of identity politics.

Examiner's trap
A common error is treating "language policy" as a dry administrative topic. Correct answer: Always link it to literary production. For example, the ban on certain words in Panchayat times directly influenced the vocabulary and themes of that era's poetry.
Exam-Day Strategy
When discussing language, avoid vague statements like "language is important." Instead, specify: "The shift from Sanskritized Nepali to colloquial Nepali in the 1950s democratized literature by making it accessible to non-elite readers."

Gender, Caste, and Intersectionality in Contemporary Nepali Fiction

Traditional literary criticism often treats gender and caste as separate axes of analysis. However, contemporary Nepali fiction demands an intersectional approach where these categories overlap. In analyzing texts like Sirish Ko Phool or works by Parijat, one must examine how caste identity shapes the female experience beyond mere "patriarchy." For instance, a Dalit woman's oppression in a novel is not just gendered but also racialized within the Nepali context. The examiner may ask: "How does the intersection of caste and gender manifest in the protagonist's silence?" A superficial answer would blame only patriarchy; a sophisticated one identifies how caste hierarchies dictate who is allowed to speak or be heard. This requires familiarity with Dalit literature movements and their specific critiques of upper-caste feminism. The text must be read against the backdrop of Nepal's complex caste system, which is often invisible in Western feminist theory. When discussing "women's agency," one must specify whether it is an agency available only to upper-caste women or a contested space for marginalized groups. This level of detail prevents over-generalization and demonstrates deep textual competence.

Examiner's trap
A common error is applying Western feminist frameworks without considering caste. Correct answer: Always contextualize gender within Nepal's specific caste dynamics. For example, a character's "liberation" might be limited by her caste status.
Exam-Day Strategy
When analyzing intersectionality, use concrete examples from the text: "The protagonist's refusal to marry is not just feminist but also a rejection of Dalit marriage markets controlled by upper-caste men."

The Aesthetics of Memory and Trauma in Post-Conflict Nepali Literature

Nepali literature following the Maoist insurgency (1996–2006) is deeply marked by trauma. Criticism here must engage with theories of memory, forgetting, and narrative rupture. Authors like Pushpa Thapa or Bishnu Poudel often employ fragmented narratives to mimic the psychological impact of conflict. The examiner may ask: "How does the structure of a post-conflict novel reflect collective trauma?" A robust answer analyzes the use of non-linear timelines, gaps in narration, and symbolic imagery (e.g., ruins, scars) that signify unresolved pain. It is crucial not to romanticize this trauma or treat it as mere "backdrop"; instead, show how the text actively processes historical violence. The concept of "testimony" is vital here: whose voice is heard, and whose is silenced? This connects to global debates on transitional justice but must be grounded in Nepali specifics (e.g., the Truth and Reconciliation Commission). When discussing "healing," avoid clichés; instead, analyze how literature serves as a space for mourning and re-narrating history. The aesthetic of memory often involves repetition and variation, where key phrases recur with slight modifications to show the persistence of trauma.

Examiner's trap
A common error is treating post-conflict literature as purely political. Correct answer: Always analyze its aesthetic form (e.g., fragmented sentences, unreliable narrators) as a reflection of trauma.
Exam-Day Strategy
When discussing trauma, focus on specific textual techniques: "The repetition of the phrase 'I remember' signals an inability to move forward, contrasting with the protagonist's desire for closure."

The Mechanics of Intertextuality and the 'Twist' in Examination Logic

Advanced literary analysis requires recognizing how texts converse with each other across time and space. In Nepali literature, intertextuality often involves referencing classical epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata) within modern novels or poems.
