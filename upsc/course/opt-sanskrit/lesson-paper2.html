Sanskrit Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism, Explanation and Essays

Prescribed Texts and Close Reading

  • Abhijnanashakuntalam — Kalidasa: Classical drama of love and recognition.
  • Ashtadhyayi — Panini: Foundational grammar of Sanskrit.
  • Kadambari — Banabhatta: Classical prose romance.
  • Dhvanyaloka — Anandavardhana: Treatise on suggestion (dhvani) in poetry.

Literary Criticism

the rasa, dhvani, alamkara, guna and riti schools, beginning with Bharata’s Natyashastra

Explanation (Vyakhya) Strategy

  • Context: identify the text, author, period and speaker.
  • Literal meaning: paraphrase the passage in simple Sanskrit or English.
  • Theme: state the central idea and the emotional tone.
  • Form: comment on metre, imagery, diction and figures of speech.
  • Evaluation: link the passage to the author’s larger concerns and the period.

Essay Questions

  • Define the literary issue in the question, then take a clear position.
  • Support the argument with at least three texts or authors.
  • Include one counter-argument and respond to it.
  • Conclude with the significance of the issue for Sanskrit literature.
Solved Example: Explanation
Question: Explain a prescribed passage from Abhijnanashakuntalam.
Answer: Identify the speaker and situation, paraphrase the lines, state the theme, comment on imagery and form, and connect the passage to the work’s central concern. A good explanation moves from literal meaning to literary evaluation.

Common Traps

  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing the passage.
  • Using a translation without naming the translator.
  • Ignoring the prescribed text and writing general remarks.
  • Mixing authors and works from different periods.
  • Making claims about influence without evidence.
Mains strategy
For explanation questions, use the five-step structure: context → literal meaning → theme → form → evaluation. For essay questions, write a thesis statement in the first paragraph, use three body paragraphs with textual evidence, and end with a balanced conclusion. Keep quotations short and accurate.

Advanced Comparative Analysis: The Shift from Rasa to Dhvani

While standard texts outline the schools of Rasa, Dhvani, and Alamkara, the examiner often tests the nuanced transition between them. The critical mechanism here is understanding how Anandavardhana's theory of Suggestion (Dhvani) does not merely add to Bharata's Rasa but supersedes it as the ultimate aesthetic principle. In a high-level analysis, one must distinguish between Nirvachaka Dhvani (explicit suggestion) and Anuvyakti Dhvani (implicit suggestion). The latter is considered superior because it relies on the reader's imagination rather than direct description.

Landmark Case: The Critique of Alankara Shastra

A classic trap involves evaluating the Alamkara (ornament) schools. Textbooks often list them sequentially, but advanced criticism requires identifying the specific fault attributed to each by later critics like Vamana or Rudrata. For instance, the Gandharva Alankara Shastra is criticized for its rigid categorization of metaphors which can stifle natural flow. The examiner may present a passage from Kalidasa and ask you to identify which specific ornament it employs while simultaneously arguing why relying solely on ornaments without underlying Rasa or Dhvani results in "dead poetry." The solution lies in citing the hierarchy: Rasa (emotion) > Dhvani (suggestion) > Alamkara (decoration).

Technique: Comparative Textual Analysis
Question: Compare the treatment of 'love' in Kalidasa's Abhijnanashakuntalam versus Banabhatta's Kadambari. How does the shift from dramatic dialogue to prose narrative alter the application of Rasa and Dhvani? Provide textual evidence.
Solution:
1. Context: Kalidasa uses verse (Shlokas/Malacharikas) to evoke immediate emotional resonance, whereas Banabhatta uses prose to sustain a narrative arc.
2. Rasa in Drama: In Abhijnanashakuntalam, the climax relies on Anuvyakti Dhvani; the audience feels the recognition before the words are fully spoken (e.g., the ring scene). The emotion is instantaneous.
3. Dhvani in Prose: In Kadambari, the suggestion is often internalized through descriptive passages rather than dialogue. The prose allows for a slower build-up of Bhaava (emotion), which eventually culminates in Rasa.
4. Conclusion: While Kalidasa's drama relies on the 'now' of performance, Banabhatta's prose allows for a retrospective appreciation of suggestion. The shift moves the aesthetic experience from communal (drama) to individual contemplation (prose).

The Paninian Exception: Sandhi and Lakshana in Criticism

Panini's Ashtadhyayi is often treated as a static grammar book, but literary criticism requires understanding its dynamic application in poetry. The advanced concept here is the interplay between Lakshana (literal meaning) and Lakshya (implied meaning). A frequent examiner twist involves verses where Paninian's rules of Sandhi (euphonic combination) are intentionally broken to create a specific rhythmic or emotional effect, known as Vikara. The candidate must identify whether a deviation from Panini is an error or a deliberate aesthetic choice.

Applied Scenario: Analyzing Metre and Diction

In evaluating a passage from the Meghaduta, one must check if the metre (Anustubh) adheres to Paninian phonetics. If a word is elided or combined unusually, the candidate must explain it via the concept of Laghava (lightness) in poetry versus Gurutva (heaviness) in prose. The examiner may ask: "Does this violation of Sandhi enhance the Dhvani?" The answer requires citing specific sutras where flexibility is permitted for poetic license, distinguishing between grammatical error and artistic innovation.

Technique: Grammatical Aesthetics
Question: Explain the significance of a specific Sandhi violation in a verse from the Ramayana (Valmiki). Does it contradict Panini or enhance the aesthetic?
Solution:
1. Identify the Rule: Cite the relevant Paninian sutra that normally governs this combination.
2. Analyze the Deviation: Show how the standard rule is suspended for metrical reasons (Chandas) or emotional intensity.
3. Evaluate Aesthetically: Argue that the deviation creates a pause or emphasis that highlights the central word (Pradhana Shabda), thereby strengthening the Rasa.
4. Conclusion: In Sanskrit poetics, grammar serves art, not vice versa; the exception proves the rule of aesthetic priority.

Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Counter-Argument' Trap

A serious aspirant must master the "Counter-Argument" technique. When an essay question asks you to discuss a literary theory (e.g., Rasa), do not just praise it. The examiner expects you to acknowledge a limitation. For instance, while Rasa explains tragedy, it struggles to explain comedy or satire in later Sanskrit literature like the Vikramakar. A high-scoring answer admits this gap and proposes how Dhvani fills it.

Tip for Exam Day:

The "Bridge Sentence" Strategy
When moving from a literal explanation to literary evaluation, never jump abruptly. Use a "Bridge Sentence" that explicitly states: "While the literal meaning is X, the aesthetic experience transforms this into Y because of Z (metre/imagery)." This sentence signals to the examiner that you understand the difference between Vakya-artha (sentence meaning) and Sattva (essence).
  • The "All Texts" Trap: Do not list every text mentioned in the syllabus. Only use texts that directly support your argument about the specific literary issue.
  • The "Translation Overload" Trap: In explanation questions, providing a full English translation dilutes the Sanskrit aesthetic. Quote only key phrases and explain them.
  • The "Chronological Error" Trap: Ensure you do not attribute concepts from the Dhvanyaloka (10th century) to the Natyashastra (2nd-3rd century BCE) without noting the historical evolution of thought.
  • The "Vague Evaluation" Trap: Avoid saying "This is beautiful." Instead, say "The beauty arises from the juxtaposition of X and Y, creating a sense of Z."

The Pashupati Seal and What It Shows: The Archaeological Anchor of Sanskrit Literary Chronology

Sanskrit literature is often perceived as an exclusively textual tradition, yet its history is deeply rooted in material culture that predates the earliest surviving manuscripts. A critical area for advanced examination is the intersection of archaeology and literary criticism, specifically how physical artifacts validate or challenge the chronology of Sanskrit texts. The most significant case study here is the Pashupati Seal discovered at Mohenjo-daro (Sindh). This seal depicts a seated figure surrounded by animals, often interpreted as an early representation of Shiva, flanked by attendants that may be precursors to the Yakshas and Yaksinis found in later Vedic literature. The inscription on the seal is undeciphered, but its iconography provides a tangible link between the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2600 BCE) and the mythological universes described in the Vedas.

The literary implication of this artifact is profound. Texts like the Rigveda describe deities who are nature-worshippers, controlling elements such as thunder and rain. The Pashupati Seal suggests that these theological concepts were not merely poetic inventions but reflections of a lived reality where the divine was immanent in nature. For an examiner, the twist often lies in asking: "Does the existence of this seal prove the Vedic texts are older than previously thought?" The correct analytical response is nuanced: while the seal proves the *concept* existed by 2600 BCE, it does not date the specific verses of the Rigveda. The literary corpus evolved over centuries. Therefore, one must distinguish between the "archaeological horizon" and the "textual composition." A high-scoring answer would cite the seal to argue that the Vedic pantheon had a pre-Vedic lineage, thereby challenging the notion that Sanskrit literature began abruptly with the composition of the Samhitas. This distinction between material existence and textual formulation is a cornerstone of advanced literary history.

Furthermore, this artifact challenges the linear narrative often taught in introductory courses. It forces the candidate to consider "parallel development" theories where oral traditions existed alongside urban civilizations before being codified into written texts. The seal serves as a visual gloss on the concept of Rta (cosmic order) found in the Rigveda, suggesting that the moral universe described in literature was grounded in a physical, observable world. When analyzing questions about the "origin of Sanskrit Literature," one must never rely solely on the traditional dating of the Atharvaveda or the composition of the Mahabharata. Instead, one must integrate archaeological data to construct a more robust timeline. This approach demonstrates mastery over the subject by refusing to treat literature as an isolated intellectual exercise but rather as a reflection of historical consciousness.

The Evolution of Dhvani: From Anandavardhana's Theory to Contemporary Aesthetic Debates

Anandavardhana's Dhvanyaloka (10th century CE) revolutionized Sanskrit poetics by introducing Dhvani (suggestion) as the supreme aesthetic principle, surpassing even Rasa. However, a serious aspirant must understand that this was not the end of the debate but the beginning of a complex intellectual evolution. The concept of Dhvani posits that the literal meaning (Abhidha) and the suggestive meaning (Lakshana) are merely stepping stones to the ultimate aesthetic experience. Yet, later critics like Rudrata and Jagannatha expanded this framework, introducing sub-categories of suggestion such as Vipralambha Rasa (separated taste) and Sahopalabdhi (simultaneous perception). The advanced twist in examination questions often involves comparing Anandavardhana's static theory with the dynamic nature of performance.

Consider the case of Bhanudatta, a contemporary of Anandavardhana who offered a critique from a different angle. While Anandavardhana focused on the poet's intent and the audience's reception, Bhanudatta emphasized the role of the patron and the social context of poetry. This shift is crucial for understanding how Sanskrit literature functioned in courtly settings versus devotional contexts. For instance, the Dhvani theory explains why a poem about separation can evoke joy (in a tragic sense) without breaking aesthetic rules. However, later developments in Bhakti literature challenged this rigid framework. The intense personal devotion of poets like Jayadeva in the Gita Govinda often bypassed the need for elaborate suggestion, relying instead on direct emotional outpouring. This creates a tension between classical theory and devotional practice. An examiner might ask: "Does the rise of Bhakti literature invalidate Anandavardhana's theory?" The sophisticated answer is that it expands it; Dhvani becomes internalized as personal faith rather than an aesthetic construct for public performance.

The evolution continues into the medieval period with the emergence of Nava Rasa (nine tastes) and even Vibhuti Rasa (awe-inspiring taste) in later texts like the Rasamanjari. These additions reflect a changing cultural landscape where the aesthetic experience became more diverse. The examiner may present a verse from a medieval poet that uses imagery not covered in classical Dhvani theory, asking the candidate to explain how the theory adapts or fails. This requires a deep understanding of the historical trajectory of Sanskrit thought, moving from the rigid structures of the Mughal period to the fluidity of the Bhakti movement. The key takeaway is that literary criticism in Sanskrit is not a static set of rules but a living dialogue across centuries, where each generation reinterprets the classics to fit new spiritual and social realities.

Applied Scenario: Analyzing Metaphor and Suggestion
Question: In the following verse from a medieval Kavya, identify the type of Dhvani present. Does it follow Anandavardhana's strict definition or represent a deviation? "The moon is like a pearl dropped in the ocean of night." Solution: 1. Identify Literal Meaning (Abhidha): The moon resembles a pearl. 2. Identify Suggestive Meaning (Lakshana): The brightness and roundness of the moon are being highlighted. 3. Analyze Dhvani: Here, the suggestion is not merely emotional but visual; the Dhvani arises from the unexpected juxtaposition of a celestial body with a terrestrial gem. This creates a Vakrokti (twisted speech) effect. 4. Evaluate against Anandavardhana: While this fits the broad definition of suggestion, it leans towards Rupaka (metaphor) which was debated in later schools. The verse succeeds because the suggestion transcends the literal comparison to evoke a sense of purity and isolation. 5. Conclusion: This is a case of Sahopalabdhi Dhvani where the visual image and emotional response occur simultaneously, validating Anandavardhana's theory while showing its flexibility in later literature.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Historical Context' Bridge
When answering questions about literary evolution, never jump from a classical text to a medieval one without a "Bridge Sentence." For example: "While Anandavardhana's Dhvanyaloka established the primacy of suggestion in the 10th century, the later Bhakti movement transformed this aesthetic principle into a vehicle for direct divine communion. The shift from Rasa (public taste) to Bhava (personal emotion) demonstrates how literary theory adapts to changing spiritual needs." This sentence signals that you understand both the theoretical framework and its historical application.

The Sanskrit Grammarians as Literary Critics: Panini's Influence on Poetic Diction

Panini's Ashtadhyayi is primarily a grammar book, but its influence on literary criticism cannot be overstated. The advanced concept here is the distinction between Lakshana (grammatical meaning) and Lakshya (implied aesthetic meaning). Panini's rules were designed to standardize language, yet poets like Kalidasa and Bhavabhuti often bent these rules for aesthetic effect. This creates a fascinating tension between grammatical correctness and poetic license. For instance, the famous "Sandhi" violations in Sanskrit poetry are not errors but deliberate artistic choices known as Vikara. These deviations serve to create rhythm, emphasize specific words, or evoke particular emotions.

The examiner may ask: "Does Panini's grammar contradict the poetic usage of Sanskrit?" The answer requires a nuanced understanding of the relationship between language structure and aesthetic expression. One must argue that while Panini provides the foundation, poetry builds upon it by adding layers of meaning that go beyond literal definitions. This is where the concept of Alinga (mark) comes in; a word may not have a direct grammatical equivalent but carries an emotional weight that defines its usage in literature. The advanced analysis involves tracing how later poets used Paninian sutras as a framework for innovation rather than constraint.

Furthermore, the study of Sanskrit grammar reveals how language itself was viewed as a divine creation. The concept of Shabda Brahman (word as ultimate reality) suggests that every word carries spiritual significance. This theological dimension is often overlooked in standard literary criticism but is crucial for understanding texts like the Bhagavad Gita or the Maha Bharata. The examiner might present a verse where a grammatical rule is suspended to highlight a divine name, asking the candidate to explain the theological rationale.
