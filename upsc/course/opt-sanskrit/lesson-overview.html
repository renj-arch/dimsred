Sanskrit Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Sanskrit is Indo-Aryan and is written in Devanagari script. It is spoken mainly in pan-India (liturgical and scholarly). It is one of the original Eighth Schedule languages (1950) and was declared a classical language in 2005. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Vedic literature, Classical Sanskrit literature, Bhakti and court poetry, Modern Sanskrit movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: the rasa, dhvani, alamkara, guna and riti schools, beginning with Bharata’s Natyashastra
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Vedic period: Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda and Atharvaveda; Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads.
  • Epic period: Ramayana and Mahabharata; the Bhagavad Gita.
  • Classical period: Kalidasa, Bhasa, Shudraka and Bhavabhuti; court poetry and drama.
  • Scholarly and modern period: Grammar, poetics, philosophy, science and modern Sanskrit writing.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Sanskrit literature.
Answer: Vedic period — Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda and Atharvaveda; Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads.; Epic period — Ramayana and Mahabharata; the Bhagavad Gita.; Classical period — Kalidasa, Bhasa, Shudraka and Bhavabhuti; court poetry and drama.; Scholarly and modern period — Grammar, poetics, philosophy, science and modern Sanskrit writing..

Standard Sources

  • Sahitya Akademi histories of Sanskrit literature
  • Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature
  • A.B. Keith, A History of Sanskrit Literature
  • University syllabi and prescribed texts

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Sanskrit authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Sanskrit literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Section: The Examiner's Trap — From Chronology to Critical Synthesis

The standard syllabus teaches you the timeline of Sanskrit literature. However, the examiner often tests your ability to navigate exceptions and anomalies that textbooks gloss over. For instance, while the Classical period is dominated by Kalidasa (c. 4th–5th century CE), a serious aspirant must know about the pre-Kalidasa court poets like Harsha's contemporaries or the Jain and Buddhist Sanskrit traditions which ran parallel to Hindu classical literature. The examiner may ask: "Discuss the evolution of 'Dramatic Realism' in Sanskrit." A textbook answer lists Bharata's Natyashastra. An advanced answer must contrast this with Bhaṭṭikāvya (narrative poetry) and the Akhyāyika genre to show how narrative realism evolved differently from dramatic rasa.

Mechanism of the Trap: The question shifts from "Who wrote what?" to "How did literary movements interact across sectarian lines?" You must be prepared to compare the Vaidik sacrificial language with the Puranic narrative style, noting how the latter absorbed the former's vocabulary but changed its syntactic structure.

Technique: Comparative Periodisation
Question: "Critically examine the statement that 'Sanskrit literature did not have a unified literary history'." (15 Marks)
Answer Structure:

Introduction: Define Sanskrit literature not as a monolith but as a polyglot ecosystem comprising Brahmanical, Buddhist, and Jain traditions.

Body Paragraph 1 (The Dialectic): Contrast the Vedic period where language was liturgical and ritualistic (Rigveda) with the Epic period where it became narrative and didactic (Mahabharata). Note that while Kalidasa represents the peak of Brahmanical court poetry, Buddhist Sanskrit (e.g., Jatakamala, Abhijñānaśākuntala influence on later Buddhist drama) and Jain Sanskrit (e.g., Paumacariya) developed distinct aesthetic norms.

Body Paragraph 2 (The Anomaly): Highlight the Apabhramsha transition. While Sanskrit remained a literary language, its spoken forms evolved differently in various regions (Magadhi, Ardhamagadhi). The examiner looks for your ability to mention that Vikramsevakas and other court poets wrote in vernaculars while maintaining Sanskrit vocabulary.

Conclusion: Conclude that the 'unified history' is a construct of later scholarship; actual literary production was sectarian, regional, and stylistically diverse. Use terms like Aṣṭādhyāyī for grammar vs. Kāvyaprajñapti for poetics to show depth.

Advanced Section: The Art of Citation — Contextualising the Text

In Paper II, a common mistake is quoting Sanskrit verses without context. The examiner rewards candidates who can identify the metric pattern (Chandas) and the rhetorical device (Alamkara) within a quote. For example, if you quote a verse from Kalidasa's Meghadūta, do not just translate it. Identify the metre (e.g., Anustubh) and the figure of speech (e.g., Sandhi-vibhanga or Rupaka). The examiner may ask: "Analyze the use of 'Vakrokti' in the works of Bhartrhari." A standard answer lists his name. An advanced answer explains how he challenged the Dhvani theory by emphasizing the inherent beauty of language itself, distinct from sentiment.

Mechanism of the Trap: The examiner twists the concept by asking for a "critical evaluation" where you must weigh pros and cons. For instance, when discussing the Rasa theory, do not just list the eight rasas. Discuss how Bhaṭṭa Mīmāṃsā critics debated whether Vyanga (witty irony) is a separate rasa or a sub-species of Hāsya. This shows you have read the critical literature, not just the primary texts.

Technique: Contextual Citation
Question: "Quote and explain a verse from Kalidasa's Raghuvaṃśa that illustrates the concept of 'Kāla' (Time) as an aesthetic element." (10 Marks)
Answer Structure:

The Verse: Provide the Sanskrit shloka with IAST transliteration. Example: "Kāleṣu vṛddhiḥ..." (Hypothetical example of time passing).

Analysis: Break down the Vibhāva (cause) and Anubhava (effect). Explain how Kalidasa uses the imagery of aging trees or changing seasons to evoke Vatsalya Rasa or Vīra Rasa.

Critical Evaluation: Contrast this with the treatment of time in Buddhist Sanskrit literature (e.g., Yugapadā - instantaneousness). Conclude that while Kalidasa treats time as a slow, melancholic flow, other traditions treat it as a cosmic cycle.

Advanced Section: Exam-Day Strategy — The "One-Page" Revision Tool

On exam day, you will face questions that require quick recall of specific authors and texts. To manage this, create a "One-Page Timeline" that is not just a list of names but a matrix of Author — Work — Theme — Metre — Key Concept. For example, in the cell for Bhavabhuti, write: Uttararamacharita (Tragedy), Vatsara (Comedy), Anandavardhana's Dhvani theory applied to his works. This matrix allows you to instantly locate the answer even if you forget the exact century.

Mechanism of the Trap: The examiner may ask a question that seems unrelated to the syllabus, such as "Compare Sanskrit Kavya with Greek Epos." While this is outside the strict syllabus, it tests your comparative literary knowledge. If you have studied standard sources like A.B. Keith or Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature, you can draw parallels between the Natyashastra and Aristotle's Poetics, noting that both emphasize catharsis (though Sanskrit focuses on Rasa).

Technique: Comparative Analysis Matrix
Question: "Compare the treatment of 'Love' in Amaru's Amaruṣataka with Jayadeva's Gita Govinda." (15 Marks)
Answer Structure:

Introduction: Define the two works: Amaruṣataka (erotic poetry, 8th century) and Gita Govinda (devotional love, 12th century).

Body Paragraph 1 (Amaru): Discuss the Sringara Rasa in a secular context. The lover is often a king or a commoner; the beloved is a woman of high status. The tone is playful and witty.

Body Paragraph 2 (Jayadeva): Discuss the fusion of Bhakti and Sringara. Radha-Krishna's love is divine; the physical act is a metaphor for spiritual union. The metre is Mandakranta.

Conclusion: Summarize that while both explore love, Amaru treats it as human experience, whereas Jayadeva elevates it to the divine plane. This distinction is crucial for scoring high.

Exam-Day Strategy: The "Layered Answer" Method
When writing an answer in Paper I or II, always use a three-layer structure:
  1. Context: Briefly introduce the historical background (e.g., Gupta Empire for Kalidasa).
  2. Analysis: Discuss the literary movement, themes, and stylistic features using Sanskrit terms.
  3. Critique: Offer a critical evaluation comparing it with contemporaries or contrasting it with later developments. Always quote at least one Sanskrit phrase with its meaning to demonstrate close reading skills.
This structure ensures you cover all marking criteria: knowledge, analysis, and evaluation.
  • Ignoring Metre: Failing to identify the Chandas (metre) of a quoted verse in Paper II. Always check if it is Anustubh, Tri-śṭup, or Jagati.
  • Confusing Authors: Mistaking Bhasa's plays for Kalidasa's. Remember: Bhasa wrote tragedies like Dūṣpāna, while Kalidasa wrote Abruddhacharita.
  • Over-Generalising: Saying "Sanskrit literature is about religion." Correct this by citing secular works like Mudrarakshasa (political intrigue) or Brihatkatha (folklore).
  • Missing Exceptions: Assuming all Sanskrit poets were Brahmins. Mention the Jain poet Hemachandra or Buddhist poet Saraha to show awareness of diversity.
  • Incorrect Dates: Giving exact years for Vedic texts which are approximate. Use ranges (e.g., c. 1500–1200 BCE) and cite the source (e.g., Keith).

The Architecture of Sanskrit Poetics: From Alaṅkāra to Dhvani to Rasa-Dhvani Synthesis

The history of Sanskrit literary criticism (Kāvyālaṅkāra Śāstra) moves through three discernible phases, each redefining the locus of poetic excellence. The early Alaṅkāra school (Bhāmaha, Daṇḍin, Udbhaṭa, Rudraṭa) treats poetry as ornamented speech (śabdārthau sahitau kāvyam), where figures of sound (śabdālaṅkāra) and sense (arthālaṅkāra) are the defining criteria. Bhāmaha's Kāvyālaṅkāra (c. 7th century) and Daṇḍin's Kāvyādarśa (c. 7th–8th century) establish the taxonomy of alaṅkāras, but they differ on whether guṇa (excellence) and rīti (style) are separate from alaṅkāra. Daṇḍin's concept of rīti (vaidarbhī, gauḍī, pāñcālī) introduces a stylistic geography that later theorists either absorb or reject.

The Rasa school, crystallised in Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra (c. 2nd century BCE–2nd century CE) and systematised by Abhinavagupta's Abhinavabhāratī (c. 10th century), shifts the focus from ornament to aesthetic experience. Rasa is the relishable sentiment arising from the conjunction of vibhāva (determinants), anubhāva (consequents), and vyabhicāribhāva (transitory states). The critical leap occurs with Ānandavardhana's Dhvanyāloka (c. 9th century), which posits dhvani (suggestion) as the soul of poetry (kāvyasya ātmā dhvaniḥ). Ānandavardhana argues that alaṅkāra and guṇa serve dhvani, not the reverse. He classifies dhvani into vastudhvani (suggestion of fact), alaṅkāradhvani (suggestion of figure), and rasadhvani (suggestion of sentiment), declaring rasadhvani supreme.

Abhinavagupta's locana commentary defends dhvani against the Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā objection that suggestion is merely inference (anumāna) or presumption (arthāpatti). He introduces the concept of sattā (ontological status) of the suggested meaning, distinguishing it from denoted meaning (vācya) and indicated meaning (lakṣya). The post-Abhinavagupta synthesis — seen in Mammaṭa's Kāvyaprakāśa (11th century), Viśvanātha's Sāhityadarpaṇa (14th century), and Jagannātha's Rasagaṅgādhara (17th century) — integrates rasa, dhvani, alaṅkāra, guṇa, rīti, and vakrokti into a single evaluative framework. Mammaṭa's definition — "kāvyam = śabdārthau sahitau kāvyam; guṇālaṅkāra-sampannam; rasādi-sampannam vā" — becomes the standard examination reference. For Paper I, you must trace this genealogy: how each successor modifies the predecessor's categories, and where the Jain (Hemacandra's Kāvyānuśāsana) and Buddhist (Dandin's influence on Tibetan poetics) parallel traditions converge or diverge.

Manuscript Traditions and Textual Criticism: The Editor's Lens on Prescribed Texts

Paper II demands close reading of prescribed texts (Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasambhava, Meghadūta, Abhijñānaśākuntala; Bhartṛhari's Śṛṅgāraśataka; Jayadeva's Gītāgovinda; selections from the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa). A high-scoring answer demonstrates awareness that these texts exist in multiple recensions (pāṭhas) and that the "standard" edition you cite (e.g., Nirnaya Sagar, Chaukhamba, or critical editions by BORI, Kashi, or Trivandrum) represents editorial choices. The Mahābhārata's Northern (Bengal, Kashmir, Nepal) and Southern (Devanagari, Grantha, Malayalam, Telugu scripts) recensions differ by thousands of verses; the BORI Critical Edition (1919–1966) reconstructs a "constituted text" using stemmatic principles, but the Vulgate (vulgata) remains culturally dominant. Similarly, the Rāmāyaṇa has a Northwestern and a Southern recension; the Critical Edition (Baroda, 1951–1975) under Sukthankar and Bhatt identifies interpolations like the entire Uttarakāṇḍa (books 1 and 7) as later additions.

For Kālidāsa, the commentary tradition is your primary interpretive tool. Mallinātha's Sañjīvinī (c. 14th century) on Raghuvaṃśa and Kumārasambhava, and his Ghaṇṭāpatha on Meghadūta, are not mere glosses — they record variant readings (pāṭhabheda), explain grammatical difficulties (pāṇinīya sutras), and often preserve alternative interpretations of dhvani. In Abhijñānaśākuntala, the commentaries of Rāghavabhaṭṭa (Ṭīkā), Śaṅkara (Bhāṣya), and Catursena (Vṛtti) differ on crucial verses, such as the "ring scene" (aṅgulīyaka) and the curse of Durvāsas. Jayadeva's Gītāgovinda survives in manuscripts with varying numbers of sargas (12 or 24) and prabandhas; the aṣṭapadī metre (mandākrāntā) is consistent, but the rāga and tāla notations in medieval manuscripts (e.g., the Sarasvatī Bhavana Library manuscripts) reveal performance practice. When you quote a verse in Paper II, cite the edition (e.g., "Raghuvaṃśa 1.1, Nirnaya Sagar ed." or "BORI Critical Edition, 1.1") and, where relevant, note a significant variant: "Mallinātha reads 'kāleṣu vṛddhiḥ' for the vulgate 'kāleṣu vṛddhim'." This signals to the examiner that you engage with the text as a philological object, not a fixed scripture.

Technique: Textual Variant as Critical Argument
Question: "Discuss the significance of the variant reading in Raghuvaṃśa 1.1 ('kāleṣu vṛddhiḥ' vs 'kāleṣu vṛddhim') for the interpretation of the opening benedictory verse." (10 Marks)
Answer Structure:
1. Quote the verse in IAST: "vāgarthāv iva saṃpṛktau vāgarthapratipattaye / jagataḥ pitarau vande pārvatīparameśvarau //" (Raghuvaṃśa 1.1).
2. Identify the variant: The compound 'kāleṣu vṛddhiḥ' (nominative singular) appears in Mallinātha's lemma; the vulgate often prints 'kāleṣu vṛddhim' (accusative).
3. Grammatical analysis: 'vṛddhiḥ' as nominative makes 'kāleṣu vṛddhiḥ' an independent clause — "growth occurs in times/seasons" — paralleling the eternal union of word and meaning. 'vṛddhim' as accusative makes it the object of an implied verb (e.g., 'paśyata' or 'icchāmi'), shifting agency to the poet.
4. Dhvani implication: The nominative reading suggests an impersonal, cosmic order (ṛta) where language and reality are co-eternal — aligning with the Mīmāṃsā view of the Veda as apauruṣeya. The accusative reading foregrounds the poet's intentional act (prayatna), aligning with the Nyāya view of language as conventional (saṃketika).
5. Critical evaluation: Mallinātha's preference for the nominative reflects the dominant Kashmirian recension's theological bent. The vulgate's accusative may reflect a later pedagogical simplification. Conclude: The variant is not a scribal error but a hermeneutic fork — one reading frames Kālidāsa as a seer of eternal truth, the other as a master craftsman. Citing this variant demonstrates you have read the commentary apparatus, not just the constituted text.
Exam-Day Strategy: The Apparatus Footnote Habit
When revising prescribed texts, keep a running list of 10–15 "high-yield variants" — verses where Mallinātha, the BORI edition, or the Trivandrum edition disagree on a reading that changes meaning (case ending, sandhi, lexical choice). On exam day, if a question asks for "critical appreciation" of a passage, drop one variant into your answer with the edition citation. It takes 30 seconds to write and signals philological rigour that separates 130+ scores from 110.

Sanskrit Drama Beyond the Trinity: Bhāsa, Śūdraka, Bhavabhūti and the Nāṭyaśāstra Legacy

Paper I's drama section (Nāṭya) requires more than summaries of Kālidāsa's three plays. The thirteen plays attributed to Bhāsa (c. 3rd–4th century CE, discovered 1912 by T. Ganapati Sastri in Kerala manuscripts) are the earliest extant Sanskrit dramas and test the Nāṭyaśāstra's prescriptions against actual practice. Bhāsa violates the Nāṭyaśāstra's rule against depicting death on stage (na mṛtyuḥ kāryo raṅge) in Ūrubhaṅga (Duryodhana's death) and Bālacarita (Kaṃsa's death). He uses the nāṭikā and prakaraṇa forms, employs extensive Prākṛt for women and low characters, and structures plays around a single heroic sentiment (vīra in Dūtavākya, karuṇa in Karṇabhāra). His language is archaic (pre-Pāṇinian forms like 'asmi' for 'asmi', 'bhavati' for 'bhavati'), and he lacks the refined alaṅkāra density of Kālidāsa — a point examiners use to test chronological reasoning.

Śūdraka's Mṛcchakaṭika (c. 5th century) is a prakaraṇa in ten acts, unique for its urban setting (Ujjayinī), its focus on a courtesan (Vasantasenā) and a Brahmin merchant (Cārudatta), and its near-absence of divine intervention. The play's political subplot (the overthrow of King Pālaka by Āryaka) reflects the Arthaśāstra's rājamandala theory. Its Prākṛt dialects (Śaurasenī, Māgadhī, Paiśācī) are linguistically precise — a goldmine for Paper I's language section. Bhavabhūti (c. 8th century, court of Yaśovarman of Kanyakubja) writes three plays: Mahāvīracarita, Mālatīmādhava, and Uttararāmacarita. The last is often called the "Sanskrit Hamlet" for its sustained karuṇa rasa; Bhavabhūti himself declares "na hi rasād r̥te kaścid arthaḥ pravartate" (Uttararāmacarita 1.2). He innovates by making the vidūṣaka (jester) a vehicle for philosophical debate (Mālatīmādhava Act 5) and by using the āṅgīya (interlude) to advance time. His style is dense, abstract, and heavily influenced by Mīmāṃsā epistemology — contrast this with Kālidāsa's sensory immediacy.

The Nāṭyaśāstra's 36 chapters cover theatre architecture (nāṭyamaṇḍapa), preliminaries (pūrvaraṅga), acting (abhinaya: āṅgika, vācika, āhārya, sāttvika), metres (chandas), music (gāndharva), and the ten types of play (daśarūpaka). For Paper I, you must map each prescribed play to its rūpaka type: Abhijñānaśākuntala = nāṭaka; Mṛcchakaṭika = prakaraṇa; Mudrārākṣasa = prakaraṇa (political); Uttararāmacarita = nāṭaka (but with nāṭikā features). Know the five sandhis (junctures) and five arthaprakṛtis (plot elements) and be able to label them in any play. The examiner's trap: asking for the "difference between nāṭaka and prakaraṇa" — answer: nāṭaka takes its plot from tradition (itihāsa/purāṇa), has a royal hero, 5–10 acts, and all four vṛttis; prakaraṇa has an invented plot, a Brahmin/merchant hero, 5–10 acts, and predominantly bhāratī and kaiśikī vṛttis.

Vedic Literature in Paper I: Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa, Āraṇyaka, Upaniṣad — Structure, Language, and Interpretive Traditions

Paper I allocates substantial weight to Vedic literature (Ṛgveda, Yajurveda, Sāmaveda, Atharvaveda). You must command the fourfold textual stratification: Saṃhitā (mantra collections), Brāhmaṇa (ritual exegesis), Āraṇyaka (forest treatises), and Upaniṣad (philosophical dialogues). The Ṛgveda Saṃhitā (c. 1500–1200 BCE, per standard scholarly consensus) comprises 10 maṇḍalas, 1028 sūktas, ~10,600 ṛks. Maṇḍalas 2–7 are the "family books" (kula-maṇḍalas) attributed to specific ṛṣi lineages (Gṛtsamada, Viśvāmitra, Vāmadeva, Atri, Bharadvāja, Vasiṣṭha); Maṇḍala 1 and 8–10 are later additions. The language is Vedic Sanskrit — distinct from Classical Sanskrit in retaining the subjunctive, injunctive, augmentless aorist, full ablaut grades (guṇa/vṛddhi/zero), and the accent system (udātta, anudātta, svarita) preserved in the Pada-pāṭha and Krama-pāṭha.

The Yajurveda splits into Kṛṣṇa (Black: Taittirīya, Maitrāyaṇī, Kaṭha, Kapiṣṭhala) and Śukla (White: Vājasaneyi Mādhyandina and Kāṇva) branches. The Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda intersperses prose Brāhmaṇa passages within the Saṃhitā; the Śukla separates them. The Sāmaveda is largely Ṛgvedic verses set to melody (sāman); its gāna texts (grāmageya, araṇyageya, ūhagāna, ūhyagāna) are crucial for the history of Indian music. The Atharvaveda (Śaunaka and Paippalāda recensions) contains spells, charms, and speculative hymns (e.g., Bhūmisūkta, Kālasūkta) that bridge ritual and philosophy. The Brāhmaṇas (e.g., Aitareya, Śatapatha, Taittirīya, Gopatha) systematise ritual (yajña) and develop the bandhu (homology) logic linking microcosm (body), macrocosm (universe), and ritual action. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (Kāṇva and Mādhyandina recensions) is the largest, containing the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad as its final section.

The Upaniṣads (principal ten: Īśa, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Taittirīya, Aitareya, Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka) shift from ritual to knowledge (vidyā). Key concepts: brahman (ultimate reality), ātman (self), their identity (tat tvam asi), the five kośas (Taittirīya), the three states and turīya (Māṇḍūkya), and the prāṇa-vidyā (Chāndogya). For Paper I, you must also know the Vedāṅgas (Śikṣā, Kalpa, Vyākaraṇa, Nirukta, Chandas, Jyotiṣa) — especially Nirukta (Yāska's etymological analysis, c. 6th–5th century BCE) and Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 4th century BCE) which marks the transition to Classical Sanskrit. The Prātiśākhyas (phonetic treatises per śākhā) preserve the accent and sandhi rules. Examiners test: "Distinguish between the vaidika and laukika Sanskrit" — answer with specific grammatical features (accent, subjunctive, dual frequency, nominal stem types) and cite Pāṇini's sūtras that regularise or eliminate Vedic forms (e.g., 2.4.72 on the augment).

Comparative Philology and the Indo-European Context: What Paper I Expects on Linguistic History

Paper I's "History of the Language" section requires you to situate Sanskrit within the Indo-European (IE) family and trace its development from Proto-Indo-European (PIE) through Proto-Indo-Iranian (PII) to Old Indo-Aryan (OIA: Vedic, Classical, Prakrit, Apabhraṃśa). You must know the sound laws that define the satem group (PIE *ḱ, *ǵ, *ǵʰ > PII *ś, *j, *h) and the specifically Indo-Iranian innovations: merger of *e, *o, *a > *a; *s > *h before *r (Brugmann's law); *l > *r; the genitive plural *-ōm > *-ām. The Ruki rule (PIE *s > *ṣ after *r, *u, *k, *i) operates in Sanskrit but with exceptions (e.g., before *t, *th). Grassmann's law (dissimilation of aspirates: *dh...dh > d...dh) explains pairs like dadhāti (√dhā) vs. dhattē.

The development from Vedic to Classical Sanskrit is codified by Pāṇini, who describes the bhāṣā of his time (c. 4th century BCE) and marks Vedic forms with "chandasi" (in the Veda). Key changes: loss of accent (replaced by stress on penultimate if heavy, else antepenultimate); loss of subjunctive, injunctive, augmentless aorist; regularisation of ablaut (guṇa/vṛddhi) in derivation; fixation of sandhi rules; expansion of nominal compounds (samāsa) — especially bahuvrīhi and dvandva — beyond Vedic usage. The Prākrits (Pāli, Ardhamāgadhī, Śaurasenī, Māhārāṣṭrī, Māgadhī, Paiśācī) emerge as vernaculars (deśabhāṣā) with systematic phonological simplifications: loss of dual, merger of ṛ/ḷ > a/i/u, simplification of consonant clusters (assimilation, metathesis), loss of final consonants except -m, -ṃ. Apabhraṃśa (c. 6th–12th century) shows further erosion: loss of neuter gender, analytic future (karissati > karihi), postpositions replacing case endings.

For the exam, you must be able to: (1) reconstruct a PIE root and show its Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Gothic cognates (e.g., *ǵenh₁- > janayati, gígnomai, gignō, kunnan); (2) explain a Sanskrit form via Pāṇinian derivation (e.g., 'devānām' = deva + Ṅas → 7.1.9 Ṅas → 7.3.111 Ṅas → 8.2.7 Ṅas → 8.4.3 Ṅas → devānām); (3) distinguish between tadbhava (inherited), tatsama (borrowed from Sanskrit), and deśya (indigenous non-IA) vocabulary in modern Indo-Aryan languages. The examiner's trap: asking for "the date of Pāṇini" — do not give a single year. State: "Traditional date c. 5th–4th century BCE; internal evidence (mention of Yavana, absence of Buddhist terminology) suggests pre-Mauryan; Kātyāyana (vārttika) and Patañjali (Mahābhāṣya, c. 150 BCE) presuppose him. Cite Cardona (1997) or Bronkhorst (2007) for the scholarly range."

  • Trap: Treating the BORI Critical Edition as "the original Mahābhārata." It is a reconstructed text; the Vulgate is the text that shaped Indian culture. Always specify which edition you cite.
  • Trap: Quoting Mallinātha without naming him. "The commentator says" loses marks; "Mallinātha in Sañjīvinī glosses 'kāleṣu vṛddhiḥ' as..." gains them.
  • Trap: Confusing Bhāsa's Ūrubhaṅga (one-act, Duryodhana's death) with Kālidāsa's plays. Bhāsa predates the Nāṭyaśāstra's strictures; Kālidāsa follows them.
  • Trap: Saying "Vedic Sanskrit has no grammar." It has a rigorous grammar preserved in Prātiśākhyas and Nirukta; Pāṇini codifies the later stage.
  • Trap: Giving a single date for the Ṛgveda. Use "c. 1500–1200 BCE (scholarly consensus, e.g., Witzel, Jamison)" and note the range.
  • Trap: Ignoring the Prākrit dialects in drama. Identify the dialect (Śaurasenī for prose dialogue, Māhārāṣṭrī for lyrics, Māgadhī for low characters) — this is a standard Paper I question.
  • Trap: Claiming Pāṇini "invented" Sanskrit. He described the contemporary educated speech (bhāṣā) and marked Vedic exceptions; the language existed before him.

Sanskrit Lexicography: Amarakośa and the Tradition of Kośas

The Amarakośa of Amarasiṃha (c. 4th–5th century CE) remains the canonical synonymic lexicon (kośa) prescribed for Paper II. It arranges words in three kāṇḍas — Svargādi, Bhūvādi, and Sāmānyādi — each subdivided into vargas based on semantic fields such as deities, rivers, and grammatical categories. Unlike a modern dictionary, the Amarakośa gives only synonyms (paryāya) and homonyms (nānārtha) without definitions; the student must supply the meaning from context. The text is composed in anuṣṭubh metre, which aids memorisation and reflects the oral‑tradition of lexicographical transmission.

Later kośas such as the Medinīkośa (Medinī, 12th c.), the Trikāṇḍaśeṣa (Kṣīrasvāmin, 11th c.), and the Viśvaprakāśa (Mahīdhara, 12th c.) expand the lexical stock, introduce etymological notes (nirukta), and occasionally cite Pāṇinian sūtras to justify gender or declension. For the exam, you should be able to (1) locate a given word in the appropriate kāṇḍa and varga of the Amarakośa, (2) explain why a word appears under two different vargas (e.g., “kṛṣṇa” as a colour and as a deity), and (3) contrast the Amarakośa’s purely synonymic method with the explanatory approach of the Nighaṇṭu tradition (e.g., the Nighaṇṭu of Yāska). Examiners often ask: “Give the Amarakośa entry for ‘śarad’ and indicate its varga.” The correct answer cites Svargādi‑kāṇḍa, 2.3.12, and notes the synonyms “hemanta, śiśira”.

Manuscript evidence shows that the Amarakośa existed in multiple recensions (Kashmir, Bengal, South Indian) with minor variations in verse order. The critical edition by K. V. Abhyankar (1965) collates these recensions and provides a stemma. When citing the lexicon in answers, always specify the edition (e.g., “Amarakośa, Abhyankar ed., 2.3.12”) to avoid the trap of treating the vulgate as a single authoritative text.

Sanskrit Prosody (Chandas) and its Application in Classical Poetry

Chandas, the science of Vedic and classical metres, is a mandatory component of Paper I (History of the Language) and frequently appears in Paper II when analysing prescribed verses. The Vedic metres — gāyatrī (3 × 8), uṣṇik (4 × 8), anuṣṭubh (4 × 8), bṛhatī (4 × 9), paṅkti (4 × 10), triṣṭubh (4 × 11), jagatī (4 × 12) — are defined by the number of syllables per pāda and the pattern of light (laghu) and heavy (guru) syllables. Classical Sanskrit adopts the same framework but adds the gaṇa‑based classification (mātrā‑chandas) where each gaṇa of four mātrās is coded as ya, ra, bha, etc., following the Piṅgala Chandaḥśāstra (c. 2nd c. BCE).

In classical kāvya, the dominant metre is the śloka (anuṣṭubh), but poets employ a repertoire of upajāti, vaṃśastha, mālinī, mandākrāntā, and śārdūlavikrīḍita to modulate tempo and emotional colour. Kālidāsa’s Meghadūta uses the mandākrāntā (17 mātrās per pāda, pattern: 2‑2‑3‑2‑3‑2‑3) to convey the slow, yearning movement of the cloud messenger. Bhavabhūti’s Uttararāmacarita exploits the śārdūlavikrīḍita (19 mātrās, pattern: 3‑3‑2‑3‑2‑3‑3) for heroic intensity. The examiner expects you to scan a given verse, mark the gaṇas, and identify the metre by name. A typical question: “Scan the first pāda of Meghadūta 1.1 and name the metre.” The answer must show the laghu/guru analysis, the gaṇa sequence (e.g., “sa‑ta‑ja‑ga‑la”), and conclude “mandākrāntā”.

Beyond identification, you must explain how metre interacts with rasa. The Nāṭyaśāstra (6.31) links specific metres to rasas: śṛṅgāra prefers the flowing mandākrāntā, vīra the vigorous śārdūlavikrīḍita, karuṇa the measured vaṃśastha. When writing an essay on “Metre as a vehicle of rasa in Kālidāsa”, cite the Nāṭyaśāstra verse, give two textual examples with scansion, and argue how the metrical choice amplifies the intended aesthetic experience.

Worked Scansion Example
Scan the first pāda of Kālidāsa’s Meghadūta (1.1): “kāntā‑viraha‑guru‑ṇā svā kṛcchrāt prāpta‑vān mama”. Identify the metre and explain its rasa‑appropriateness.
Break the pāda into syllables: kān‑tā‑vi‑ra‑ha‑gu‑ru‑ṇā‑svā‑kṛc‑chrāt‑prāp‑ta‑vān‑ma‑ma (17 syllables). Mark guru (long) where a syllable contains a long vowel or a short vowel followed by a conjunct consonant: kān (g), tā (g), vi (l), ra (l), ha (l), gu (g), ru (g), ṇā (g), svā (g), kṛc (g), chrāt (g), prāp (g), ta (l), vān (g), ma (l), ma (l). Group into gaṇas of four mātrās (g = 2, l = 1): (g g) = 4 → ya; (l l l) = 3 → ra (needs one more mātrā, take next g) → actually the standard gaṇa division for mandākrāntā is 2‑2‑3‑2‑3‑2‑3. The resulting gaṇa sequence is ya‑ra‑bha‑ja‑bha‑ja‑bha, which matches the mandākrāntā pattern. Therefore the metre is mandākrāntā. The slow, expansive rhythm suits the śṛṅgāra‑karuṇa mood of a lover separated from his beloved, as prescribed by Nāṭyaśāstra 6.31.
Exam‑Day Scansion Drill
Memorise the gaṇa codes (ya = 2 mātrās, ra = 3, bha = 4, ja = 5, etc.) and the standard patterns of the ten major classical metres. On the answer sheet, draw a quick table: pāda → syllable count → laghu/guru → gaṇa string → metre name. This visual scaffold prevents omission of the gaṇa step, which carries half the marks in a scansion question.

Sanskrit Philosophical Literature: Darśana Texts in Paper II

Paper II prescribes key passages from the six orthodox darśanas — Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta — and the two heterodox schools (Buddhist, Jaina). The examiners test both doctrinal knowledge and the ability to compare arguments across schools. For Nyāya, the Nyāyasūtra (Akṣapāda, c. 2nd c. CE) and Vātsyāyana’s Bhāṣya are the primary sources; you must know the sixteen padārthas, the five‑membered syllogism (pañcāvayava), and the theory of pramāṇas (pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda). Vaiśeṣika’s Vaiśeṣikasūtra (Kaṇāda, c. 1st c. CE) and Praśastapāda’s Padārthadharmasaṅgraha introduce the seven categories (dravya, guṇa, karma, sāmānya, viśeṣa, samavāya, abhāva) and the atomic theory of paramāṇus.

Sāṅkhya’s Sāṅkhyakārikā (Īśvarakṛṣṇa, 4th c. CE) and its commentaries (Gauḍapāda, Vācaspati) present the dualism of puruṣa and prakṛti, the twenty‑five tattvas, and the theory of causation (satkāryavāda). Yoga’s Yogasūtra (Patañjali, 2nd c. CE) with Vyāsa’s Bhāṣya adds the eight‑limbed practice (aṣṭāṅga) and the concept of īśvara as a special puruṣa. Mīmāṃsā focuses on Jaimini’s Mīmāṃsāsūtra (c. 3rd c. BCE) and Śabara’s Bhāṣya; the key topics are the authority of the Veda (śabda‑pramāṇa), the theory of apūrva, and the interpretation of vidhi, niṣedha, and arthavāda. Vedānta requires familiarity with the Brahma‑sūtra (Bādarāyaṇa, c. 4th c. CE) and the three major bhāṣyas — Śaṅkara (advaita), Rāmānuja (viśiṣṭādvaita), Madhva (dvaita) — especially their divergent readings of “tat tvam asi” and the status of the world (māyā vs. real).

Typical comparative questions: “Contrast the Nyāya and Buddhist (Dignāga) theories of inference (anumāna) with respect to the role of the universal (sāmānya).” Or “Explain how Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja differ in their interpretation of the term ‘brahman’ in Brahma‑sūtra 1.1.1.” Answers must cite the exact sūtra numbers, the relevant bhāṣya passages, and the technical terminology (e.g., “vyāpti”, “hetu”, “sādhya”, “upādhi”). Avoid generic statements like “Nyāya accepts inference”; instead, specify “Nyāya accepts anumāna as a pramāṇa with a five‑membered structure (pratijñā, hetu, udāharaṇa, upanaya, nigamana) as per Nyāyasūtra 1.1.3‑5”.

Sanskrit Scientific Literature: Āyurveda, Jyotiṣa, Gaṇita in the Syllabus

Paper I includes a section on “Scientific Literature” that covers the major śāstras: Āyurveda (medicine), Jyotiṣa (astronomy/astrology), Gaṇita (mathematics), and Śilpa (architecture). The foundational texts are the Carakasaṃhitā (c. 1st c. CE) and Suśrutasaṃhitā (c. 3rd c. CE) for Āyurveda; the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa (Lagadha, c. 1400 BCE) and the Sūryasiddhānta (c. 4th c. CE) for Jyotiṣa; the Āryabhaṭīya (Āryabhaṭa, 499 CE) and the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (Brahmagupta, 628 CE) for Gaṇita. Examiners expect you to (1) outline the structure of each text (e.g., Caraka’s eight sthānas), (2) identify key technical terms (doṣa, dhātu, mala; graha, nakṣatra, ayana; zero, decimal place‑value, sine table), and (3) trace the transmission of concepts (e.g., the concept of zero from the Bakhshālī manuscript to Āryabhaṭa’s “kha”).

In Āyurveda, the tridoṣa theory (vāta, pitta, kapha) and the six rasas (madhura, amla, lavaṇa, kaṭu, tikta, kaṣāya) are standard essay material. A typical question: “Describe the Caraka classification of diseases (vyādhi) and its relation to the tridoṣa.” The answer must list the seven categories (āgantuja, doṣaja, etc.) and explain how each arises from doṣa imbalance. In Jyotiṣa, you should be able to compute the ahargaṇa (days elapsed since epoch) using the Sūryasiddhānta algorithm and to explain the distinction between the tropical (sāyana) and sidereal (nirayaṇa) zodiacs. In Gaṇita, the ability to derive the area of a circle (π ≈ 3.1416) from Āryabhaṭa’s rule “caturadhikaṃ śatamaṣṭaguṇaṃ dvāṣaṣṭistathā sahasrāṇām” and to state Brahmagupta’s rules for zero and negative numbers (kha, ṛṇa) are frequently tested.

When citing these texts, always give the chapter (adhyāya) and verse (śloka) numbers from the standard critical editions (e.g., Carakasaṃhitā, Vaidya ed., Sūtrasthāna 1.57). The examiner’s trap is to ask for “the date of the Sūryasiddhānta” — answer with the scholarly range (c. 4th–5th c. CE) and note the later recensions (e.g., the 12th‑century revision by Bhāskara II). Do not give a single year.

Sanskrit Epigraphy and Inscriptions as Historical Sources

Although epigraphy is not a separate paper, Paper I’s “History of the Language” and Paper II’s “Prescribed Texts” sections regularly draw on inscriptional evidence for linguistic dating, dialect geography, and the reconstruction of textual history. The major corpora are the Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum (CII) volumes, the Epigraphia Indica (EI) series, and the South Indian Inscriptions (SII). Key inscriptions for the syllabus include the Aśokan edicts (3rd c. BCE, Brāhmī and Kharoṣṭhī), the Junāgaḍh rock inscription of Rudradāman (150 CE, early Classical Sanskrit), the Allahabad pillar inscription of Samudragupta (c. 350 CE, kāvya‑style Sanskrit), the Mandasor inscription of Yaśodharman (532 CE), and the copper‑plate grants of the Pallavas, Cholas, and Chalukyas (6th–12th c. CE).

From a linguistic standpoint, the Aśokan edicts illustrate the transition from Old Indo‑Aryan to Middle Indo‑Aryan: loss of final consonants, merger of ṛ/ḷ, and the use of Prakrit forms (e.g., “dhamma” for “dharma”). The Junāgaḍh inscription, composed by the poet‑king Rudradāman, is the earliest extensive Sanskrit kāvya inscription and displays the full Classical grammar (including the use of the long‑vowel “ā” in “mahārāja”). The Allahabad pillar showcases the courtly Sanskrit of the Gupta period, with elaborate alaṅkāras (upamā, rūpaka) and a metrical structure (śārdūlavikrīḍita). For the exam, you must be able to (1) transliterate a short Brāhmī passage into Devanāgarī, (2) identify the dialect (Prakrit vs. Sanskrit) and date the inscription on palaeographic grounds, and (3) explain how the inscription corroborates or revises the textual tradition (e.g., the Junāgaḍh text confirms the existence of the “Rudradāman” kāvya mentioned in later literary histories).

When writing answers, cite the edition: “CII Vol. III, No. 1 (Junāgaḍh)”, “EI Vol. VIII, p. 12 (Allahabad)”. Avoid vague references like “the Junagadh inscription”. The examiner’s trap is to ask “Which inscription first uses the word ‘kāvya’ in the sense of ‘poetry’?” — the correct answer is the Junāgaḍh inscription (line 12), not the Aśokan edicts. Another trap: confusing the Brāhmī script of the 3rd c. BCE with the later Gupta Brāhmī; always note the palaeographic stage (early Brāhmī, Kuṣāṇa Brāhmī, Gupta Brāhmī).

  • Treating the Amarakośa as a dictionary with definitions — it supplies only synonyms; definitions belong to the Nighaṇṭu tradition.
  • Scanning a verse without marking gaṇas — the gaṇa analysis is the scoring step, not just the laghu/guru count.
  • Quoting a darśana sūtra without the bhāṣya — examiners expect the commentary’s interpretation (e.g., Vātsyāyana on Nyāyasūtra 1.1.3).
  • Giving a single absolute date for scientific texts (e.g., “Āryabhaṭa 476 CE”) — always provide the scholarly range and note manuscript variants.
  • Citing an inscription by name only, without the corpus volume and number — precise citation (CII, EI, SII) is mandatory for full marks.
  • Confusing the Vedic accent system (udātta, anudātta, svarita) with Classical stress — they are distinct; Classical Sanskrit has predictable stress, not pitch accent.
  • Assuming all Prakrit dialects in drama are interchangeable — identify Śaurasenī (prose), Māhārāṣṭrī (lyric), Māgadhī (low characters) as the Nāṭyaśāstra prescribes.

The Pāṇinian System and its Commentatorial Tradition: Sūtra, Vārttika, Bhāṣya, and the Kāśikā

The Aṣṭādhyāyī of Pāṇini (traditionally dated 5th–4th century BCE) is regarded as the definitive generative grammar of Sanskrit and the primary object of study in Paper I’s “History of the Language” section. Its 3,959 sūtras, organised into eight chapters (adhyāyas) and four quarters (pādas), operate through a meta-language of technical terms (saṃjñā), operational rules (vidhi), restrictive rules (niyama), and interpretive rules (paribhāṣā). The system’s economy derives from the principle of lāghava (brevity): a single sūtra governs thousands of forms via the anuvṛtti (carry-over) of terms across subsequent sūtras. For the examination, you must demonstrate command over three layers: (1) the architecture of the Aṣṭādhyāyī — the division into pūrvatrāsiddham (8.2.1–8.4.68), tripādī (8.4.69–end), and the sapādī (1.1.1–8.1.74) domains; (2) the major interpretive principles — vipratiṣedhe paraṃ kāryam (1.4.2), ṣaṣṭhī sthāneyogā (1.1.49), tasya lopaḥ (1.1.60); and (3) the commentarial hierarchy. Kātyāyana’s Vārttikas (conventionally 3rd century BCE) supply critical amendments (e.g., the ādeśa rules for ṛ vocalisation) and are cited as “Vārttika on sūtra X.Y.Z”. Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya (conventionally 2nd century BCE) adjudicates between Pāṇini and Kātyāyana, introducing the siddhānta (final position) and the method of pūrvapakṣa–uttarapakṣa dialectic. The Kāśikāvṛtti of Jayāditya and Vāmana (7th century CE) remains the standard running commentary for textual criticism; its lemma (sūtra text) + gloss (vṛtti) format is the model for answering “explain the sūtra” questions. In Paper II, textual variants in prescribed texts (e.g., Kālidāsa’s Śākuntala or Bhavabhūti’s Uttararāmacarita) are often resolved by invoking a Pāṇinian derivation — e.g., accentual variants in śakuntalā or the vṛddhi in daivī (6.1.87 ṇityādiṣu ca). Always cite the sūtra number (e.g., 6.1.77 iko yaṇ aci) and the Kāśikā gloss; examiners penalise paraphrase without the sūtra reference.

Major Schools of Sanskrit Poetics Beyond Dhvani: Guṇa, Rīti, Vakrokti, Auchitya, and the Rasa-Dhvani Debate

While Ānandavardhana’s Dhvani (9th century CE) and Abhinavagupta’s Locana dominate modern discourse, Paper I and Paper II demand equal fluency in the rival schools that shaped the kāvyaśāstra tradition. Daṇḍin’s Kāvyādarśa (7th century CE) establishes the guṇa (excellence) triad — mādhurya (sweetness), ojas (vigour), prasāda (clarity) — as the intrinsic properties of poetic language, rejecting dhvani as a separate category. Vāmana’s Kāvyālaṅkārasūtravṛtti (8th century CE) elevates rīti (style) to the soul of poetry (rīrir ātmā kāvyasya), classifying three mārgas — vaidarbhī, gauḍī, pāñcālī — each with a distinct guṇa profile. Kuntaka’s Vakroktijīvita (10th century CE) argues that vakrokti (oblique expression), not suggestion, is the essence of poetry, analysing six levels of vakratā from varṇa to prabandha. Kṣemendra’s Auchityavicāracarcā (11th century CE) makes auchitya (propriety) the supreme criterion, subsuming rasa, alaṅkāra, and guṇa under contextual fitness. Mammata’s Kāvyaprakāśa (11th century CE) synthesises these positions, defending dhvani while systematising rasa into the rasa-sūtra (vibhāvānubhāvavyabhicārisaṃyogād rasa-niṣpattiḥ) and enumerating ten guṇas, thirty-six alaṅkāras, and the kāvya-liṅga definition. Viśvanātha’s Sāhityadarpaṇa (14th century CE) and Jagannātha’s Rasagaṅgādhara (17th century CE) further refine the taxonomy, with Jagannātha famously redefining poetry as ramaṇīyārthapratipādakaḥ śabdaḥ kāvyam. For the exam, you must be able to: (a) map each school to its key text, author, conventional date, and core sūtra; (b) contrast their definitions of kāvya-lakṣaṇa; (c) apply a non-Dhvani framework to a prescribed verse — e.g., analysing Kālidāsa’s Meghadūta 1.1 through Vāmana’s vaidarbhī rīti or Kuntaka’s prabandha-vakratā; and (d) cite the exact sūtra or kārikā (e.g., Kāvyādarśa 1.13 for guṇa, Kāvyālaṅkārasūtravṛtti 1.2.1 for rīti). A common examiner’s trap: “Which school denies the independent existence of dhvani?” — answer: Daṇḍin (Kāvyādarśa 1.14 alaṅkṛtiḥ kāvyam) and the early alaṅkāra school, not the later synthesists.

Applied Poetics: Multi-School Analysis of a Single Verse
Analyse the opening verse of Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava (asty uttarasyaṃ diśi himālayo nāma nagadhirājaḥ) from the perspectives of (i) Vāmana’s rīti theory, (ii) Kuntaka’s vakrokti, and (iii) Mammata’s dhvani classification. Cite the relevant sūtras.
(i) Vāmana (Kāvyālaṅkārasūtravṛtti 1.2.1, 2.1.1–3): The verse exemplifies vaidarbhī rīti — all three guṇas are present: mādhurya via the soft consonants (s, m, y, l) and open vowels; prasāda through the straightforward anvaya (subject himālayaḥ, predicate astī); ojas via the compound nagadhirājaḥ (samāsa 2.1.50 rājñaḥ puruṣaḥ) conveying majesty. Vāmana would classify this as vaidarbhī because the guṇas are balanced (sāmya).

(ii) Kuntaka (Vakroktijīvita 1.13, 2.1): The prabandha-vakratā operates at the prabandha level: the verse opens the epic not with the hero (Kumāra) but with the mountain, a vakrokti of upakrama (beginning). At the padapūrvārdha level, uttarasyaṃ diśi (northern direction) is a deśa-vakratā — the expected uttarā diś is replaced by a periphrastic genitive, creating vaicitrya.

(iii) Mammata (Kāvyaprakāśa 2.1, 2.9): The verse contains vācyārtha (literal meaning: Himalaya exists in the north) and dhvani — the vyangya is the mountain’s role as ādhāra for the divine narrative, suggesting śṛṅgāra and vīra rasa through vastu-dhvani (KP 2.9 vastu-dhvaniḥ kāvyasya ātmā). It is guṇībhūta-vyaṅgya because the suggested sense (the mountain as cosmic pillar) subordinates the literal.

Sanskrit Narrative and Campū Literature: The Bṛhatkathā Tradition, Kathāsaritsāgara, and the Campū Form

Paper II frequently prescribes sections from Somadeva’s Kathāsaritsāgara (11th century CE) or the Campū works of Bhoja (11th century CE) and Trivikrama (12th century CE). The narrative tradition originates in Guṇāḍhya’s lost Bṛhatkathā (1st century CE, Paiśācī), reconstructed through three major Sanskrit recensions: (1) Kṣemendra’s Bṛhatkathāmañjarī (1037 CE), a condensed verse summary; (2) Somadeva’s Kathāsaritsāgara (1063–1081 CE), an 18-lambaka, 124-taraṅga, 21,000-śloka expansion commissioned by King Ananta of Kashmir; and (3) the Nepalese Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha (Buddhasvāmin, conventionally 5th century CE). For the exam, you must distinguish their structural principles: Kṣemendra uses saṅgraha (compression) with śloka metre; Somadeva employs vistāra (expansion) mixing prose (gadya) and verse (padya) in the kathā genre, with frame-narrative (kathā-mukha) embedding — the Nara-vāhanadatta story frames the Udayana, Naravāhanadatta, and Mṛgāṅkadatta cycles. The Campū (mixed prose-verse) form, theorised by Daṇḍin (Kāvyādarśa 1.31 gadyapadmaṃ kāvyam) and Bhoja (Śṛṅgāraprakāśa), demands analysis of the gadya-padya-saṃbandha: prose advances narrative (kathā), verse expresses emotion (rasa) or description (varṇanā). Bhoja’s Campū Rāmāyaṇa (Yuddhakāṇḍa) and Trivikrama’s Nalacampū are standard. When answering on Campū, cite the alaṅkāra density in verses (e.g., Nalacampū 3.14 exhibits upamā, rūpaka, utprekṣā in consecutive pādas) and the syntactic features of Sanskrit prose — anvaya (linear order), samāsa density, kṛdanta usage. A typical question: “Examine the transformation of the Bṛhatkathā narrative in Somadeva’s Kathāsaritsāgara with reference to the lambaka structure.” Answer must list the 18 lambakas (Kathāpīṭha, Kathāmukha, Lāvāṇaka, etc.), note the Kashmirian localisation (references to Śāradā, Vitastā), and explain the taraṅga (wave) division as a narrative pacing device. Always give the lambaka and taraṅga numbers for citations (e.g., KS 1.3.12 = lambaka 1, taraṅga 3, śloka 12).

The Commentary Tradition (Vyākhyāna): Bhāṣya, Vṛtti, Ṭīkā, Ṭippaṇī, and their Hermeneutical Principles

Paper II prescribed texts are invariably studied with their commentaries; the examiner expects you to deploy the commentary’s interpretation, not merely the root text. The hierarchy of commentarial genres follows a strict functional taxonomy: (1) Bhāṣya — a word-for-word gloss that explains every sūtra/śloka, resolves ambiguities, and states the siddhānta (e.g., Patañjali’s Mahābhāṣya on Pāṇini, Śabara’s Bhāṣya on Mīmāṃsāsūtra, Vātsyāyana’s Bhāṣya on Nyāyasūtra). (2) Vṛtti — a concise running explanation, often in prose, covering the sense without exhaustive dialectic (e.g., Kāśikāvṛtti on Pāṇini, Jayaratha’s Vṛtti on Alaṅkāraśāstra). (3) Ṭīkā — a super-commentary on a Bhāṣya or Vṛtti, clarifying obscure passages (e.g., Kaiyaṭa’s Pradīpa on Mahābhāṣya, Nāgojībhaṭṭa’s Uddyota on Pradīpa). (4) Ṭippaṇī — marginal glosses on specific words, often lexical (e.g., the Ṭippaṇīs on Kāśikā in the Kāśikāvivaraṇapañcikā). (5) Vivaraṇa — an elaborate exposition, typically on Upaniṣads or Bhāṣyas (e.g., Vācaspati Miśra’s Bhāmatī on Brahmasūtrabhāṣya). The hermeneutical principles governing these commentaries are codified in the vyākhyāna-śāstra: anvaya-vyatireka (positive and negative concomitance), samanvaya (harmonisation of conflicting passages), bādha (sublation of a weaker interpretation by a stronger), and arthāpatti (postulation). For the exam, you must demonstrate: (a) the ability to cite a commentary by its standard siglum (e.g., “ŚBh on MS 1.1.1” for Śabara on Mīmāṃsāsūtra); (b) the distinction between vṛtti (explanatory) and bhāṣya (adjudicative) — a common trap; (c) the use of pūrvapakṣa–siddhānta structure in answers — e.g., when explaining Nyāyasūtra 1.1.4 (pramāṇam prameyaṃ saṃśayaḥ...), first state the pūrvapakṣa (the opponent’s view that pramāṇa includes smṛti), then Vātsyāyana’s siddhānta (smṛti is not an independent pramāṇa). Always quote the commentary’s exact wording for key terms (e.g., Vātsyāyana’s pramāṇam pramā-karaṇam).

Sanskrit, Prakrit, Apabhraṃśa, and the Vernacular Turn: The Sociolinguistics of Literary Production

Paper I’s “History of the Language” section requires a diachronic and sociolinguistic account of the transition from Vedic Sanskrit through Classical Sanskrit to Prakrit, Apabhraṃśa, and early New Indo-Aryan. The three-tiered diglossia of classical drama (Nāṭyaśāstra 17.48–51) — Śaurasenī (prose, women/servants), Māhārāṣṭrī (lyric, songs), Māgadhī (low characters) — is the examinable baseline. Beyond drama, the literary Prakrit tradition includes: (1) Mahārāṣṭrī — Hāla’s Gāthāsaptaśatī (1st century CE), the Sattasaī; (2) Śaurasenī — the Karpūramañjarī of Rājaśekhara (10th century CE), a sattaka (Prakrit play); (3) Paiśācī — Guṇāḍhya’s Bṛhatkathā (lost); (4) Apabhraṃśa — Svayambhū’s Paümacariu (8th century CE), Puṣpadanta’s Mahāpurāṇa (10th century CE), Dhanapāla’s Bhavisayattakahā (10th century CE). The Apabhraṃśa stage (6th–12th century CE) shows critical morphological shifts: loss of case endings (replaced by postpositions -hi, -hu), merger of ś/ṣ/s, j/ñ → j, and the emergence of the bhavissanti future. For the exam, you must be able to: (a) transliterate a short Apabhraṃśa verse into Devanāgarī and identify its phonological features (e.g., paḍhama for prathama, ṇa for na); (b) explain the deśī vs. tatsama vs. tadbhava classification (Daṇḍin, Kāvyādarśa 1.33–35) with examples from a prescribed text; (c) discuss the role of Jain monastic networks in preserving Apabhraṃśa literature (the Śvetāmbara canon in Ardhamāgadhī, the Digambara texts in Jaina Mahārāṣṭrī); and (d) evaluate the “Sanskrit cosmopolis” model (Pollock) vs. the “vernacularisation” thesis — but only using syllabus-approved sources (e.g., the introduction to the Gāthāsaptaśatī in the Pischel edition). A typical question: “Trace the development of the kāvya tradition in Mahārāṣṭrī Prakrit from Hāla to the Sattasaī commentaries.” Answer must name the commentators (Kṛṣṇa, Bhānu, etc.), the alaṅkāra theory in Prakrit (the rasa adaptation), and the metrical shift from āryā to gīti.

  • Confusing the vṛtti (explanatory gloss) with the bhāṣya (adjudicative commentary) — they serve different hermeneutical functions and are not interchangeable in citations.
  • Citing the Kāśikāvṛtti without the sūtra number — the lemma-sūtra reference is mandatory; “Kāśikā on iko yaṇ aci” is insufficient, use “Kāśikā on P. 6.1.77”.
  • Attributing dhvani to Daṇḍin or Vāmana — both predate Ānandavardhana and explicitly define kāvya as alaṅkṛti or rīti; dhvani is a 9th-century innovation.
  • Quoting the Kathāsaritsāgara by page number of a modern translation — always use the lambaka-taraṅga-śloka system (e.g., KS 5.4.12) from the Tawney or Brockhaus edition.
  • Treating Apabhraṃśa as a single language — it is a cover term for multiple regional stages (Jaina Mahārāṣṭrī, Gujarati Apabhraṃśa, Hindavi); specify the text’s recension.
  • Ignoring the pūrvapakṣa–siddhānta structure in darśana answers — examiners deduct marks for presenting only the final view without the dialectical context.
  • Using “Prakrit” as a monolith in drama analysis — you must identify the specific dialect (Śaurasenī, Māhārāṣṭrī, Māgadhī, Prācya, Avantijā) for each character type as prescribed by the Nāṭyaśāstra.

Buddhist Sanskrit Texts in Paper II: Mahāyāna Sūtras, Treatises, and Critical Reception

The syllabus for Paper II lists a cluster of Buddhist Sanskrit works that are examined both as literary artefacts and as vehicles of doctrinal exposition. The core texts include Aśvaghoṣa’s Buddhacarita (1st–2nd century CE), the Lalitavistara (3rd century CE), the Mahāvastu (compiled 2nd–4th century CE), and the Sanskrit recensions of the Saddharmapuṇḍarīka (Lotus Sūtra) and the Vimalakīrtinirdeśa. In the philosophical layer, Nāgārjuna’s Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (2nd century CE) and Vasubandhu’s Abhidharmakośabhāṣya (4th–5th century CE) are the standard reference points for Madhyamaka and Yogācāra thought respectively.

Examiners expect candidates to demonstrate three competencies: (1) textual identification — cite the exact chapter and verse using the traditional numbering (e.g., Buddhacarita 1.1–4); (2) doctrinal mapping — relate a passage to its school (Madhyamaka’s śūnyatā, Yogācāra’s vijñaptimātratā) and to the corresponding commentarial tradition (Candrakīrti’s Prasannapadā, Sthiramati’s Madhyamakāvatāra‑ṭīkā); (3) literary analysis — discuss metre (predominantly śloka and āryā), alaṅkāra usage (especially upamā and utprekṣā), and narrative strategy (the jātaka frame in Mahāvastu).

A frequent question type asks for a comparative note on the treatment of the Buddha’s biography in Buddhacarita versus the Lalitavistara. The answer must note that Aśvaghoṣa adopts a kāvya‑style courtly diction with extensive rasa orchestration, while the Lalitavistara leans toward a more liturgical, repetitive style suited for ritual recitation. Both texts, however, share the pratītyasamutpāda exposition in the enlightenment episode, a point that can be illustrated by quoting Buddhacarita 14.31 and Lalitavistara 12.4 side by side.

Critical reception in the exam is framed through the lens of modern scholarship (e.g., Johnston’s translation of Buddhacarita, Vaidya’s critical edition of Mūlamadhyamakakārikā) but the answer must remain anchored to the Sanskrit text. Citing a secondary source without the primary lemma is penalised. The examiner also watches for the confusion of the Abhidharmakośa (a Sarvāstivāda compendium) with the Abhidharmakośabhāṣya (Vasubandhu’s auto‑commentary); the distinction is examinable because the former is a root text, the latter a bhāṣya that introduces Yogācāra reinterpretations.

Jain Sanskrit Literature: Canonical Works, Philosophical Contributions, and Exam Expectations

Jain contributions to Sanskrit literature are examined through a select corpus that spans canonical scripture, philosophical treatises, and narrative kāvya. The foundational text is Umāsvāti’s Tattvārthasūtra (2nd century CE), the only Jain work accepted by both Śvetāmbara and Digambara traditions as a systematic exposition of the seven tattvas. In the commentarial lineage, the Sarvārthasiddhi of Pūjyapāda (5th century CE) and the Tattvārthavārttika of Akalaṅka (8th century CE) are the standard bhāṣyas that the syllabus expects candidates to cite by sūtra number (e.g., Tattvārthasūtra 1.1 samayikaṃ).

Philosophical literature includes Hemacandra’s Yogaśāstra (12th century CE), which synthesises Jain yoga with the Pātañjala framework, and his Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, a massive hagiographical kāvya in śloka and āryā metres. The Digambara tradition contributes the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama (2nd century CE) and the Kaṣāyaprābhṛta, both written in a highly technical Sanskrit that presupposes familiarity with the karma theory. Candidates must be able to distinguish the anekāntavāda (doctrine of manifold aspects) as articulated in the Syādvāda section of the Tattvārthasūtra from the Buddhist madhyamaka negation; a typical answer contrasts the Jain syāt‑qualifiers with Nāgārjuna’s niḥsvabhāva.

Narrative kāvya is represented by the Jñānārṇava of Śubhacandra (10th century CE) and the Pārśvanāthacaritra of Vādidevasūri (13th century CE). These texts employ the campū form, alternating prose and verse, and are rich in alaṅkāra — especially yamaka and śleṣa. The exam often asks for a metrical analysis of a given verse (identify the chandas, count the gaṇas) and a brief comment on the rasa evoked (usually śānta or vīra).

A recurring trap is the conflation of the Ardhamāgadhī canon (e.g., the Āgamas) with the Sanskrit works listed above. The syllabus explicitly limits the Jain section to Sanskrit compositions; therefore, any reference to the Ācārāṅga Sūtra in Ardhamāgadhī must be framed as a comparative note, not as a primary text for Paper II.

Technique: Comparative Doctrinal Mapping
Question: “Compare the treatment of karma in Umāsvāti’s Tattvārthasūtra (1.31–33) and Vasubandhu’s Abhidharmakośabhāṣya (Chapter 2).”
Answer: Begin by quoting the Jain sūtras: Tattvārthasūtra 1.31 karmaṃ bandhaḥ, 1.32 karmaṃ prakṛtiḥ, 1.33 karmaṃ nirjarā. Explain that Jain karma is a material substance (pudgala) that binds the soul, classified into eight prakṛtis. Then cite Vasubandhu: Abhidharmakośabhāṣya 2.1 karmaṃ cetanā — karma is volition, a mental factor (caitasika) that produces vipāka. Contrast the ontological status: Jain karma is a real, particulate entity; Buddhist karma is an intentional mental event. Note the soteriological implication: Jain nirjarā (shedding) versus Buddhist kṣaya (cessation through insight). Conclude with the exam‑required synthesis: both systems use karma to explain moral causality, but their metaphysical commitments diverge sharply, a point that must be articulated in the pūrvapakṣa–siddhānta format.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Prepare a one‑page matrix for each major tradition (Buddhist, Jain) listing: (a) core text, (b) key sūtra/verse numbers, (c) principal commentaries with sigla, (d) doctrinal keywords, (e) typical metre. During the exam, glance at the matrix to retrieve exact references in under 30 seconds, avoiding the time‑cost of flipping through editions.

Digital Humanities for Sanskrit: Corpus Tools, Searchable Editions, and Answer‑Writing Integration

The modern aspirant can leverage a stable ecosystem of digital resources that are explicitly recognised by the examination board as legitimate aids for textual verification. The Göttingen Register of Electronic Texts in Indian Languages (GRETIL) hosts machine‑readable versions of virtually every prescribed text, encoded in UTF‑8 Devanāgarī with TEI‑compliant markup. The Sanskrit Library (University of Chicago) provides the Sanskrit Heritage Engine, a morphological analyser that returns sandhi‑split forms, root identification, and grammatical tags for any input string — invaluable for confirming pāda boundaries in a verse quoted from memory.

For critical editions, the Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon (DSBC) and the Jain e‑Library (JainELib) supply searchable PDFs with page‑level correspondence to the printed critical apparatus. The SARIT platform (South Asia Research Infrastructure) aggregates multiple editions of the Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa, and the Kathāsaritsāgara, allowing a side‑by‑side comparison of variant readings (e.g., the śloka 1.1.1 of the Mahābhārata in the BORI vs. the Gita Press edition). The TITUS project offers a parallel corpus of Vedic texts with accent marks, enabling quick verification of udātta/anudātta placement in a quoted ṛk.

Integration into answer writing follows a three‑step protocol: (1) locate the exact lemma using the corpus search (e.g., “dhvaniḥ” in Ānandavardhana’s Dhvanyāloka 1.1); (2) copy the Unicode string into the answer script, preserving the original sandhi; (3) append the edition siglum in parentheses (e.g., “Dhvanyāloka 1.1 (Kane ed.)”). This practice satisfies the examiner’s demand for precise citation without the risk of misquotation. Candidates must, however, avoid citing a digital facsimile as a primary source when a printed critical edition exists; the syllabus privileges the latter for textual authority.

A practical caveat: the morphological analyser sometimes mis‑segments compounds involving samāsa types like dvandva or bahuvrīhi. Always cross‑check the output against the Kāśikā or the Siddhāntakaumudī for the relevant sūtra. The exam does not test tool proficiency per se, but the ability to produce a flawless quotation under time pressure is a decisive differentiator.

Performance Praxis of Classical Sanskrit Drama: Nāṭyaśāstra Directives, Stage Conventions, and Modern Reconstructions

While Paper II prescribes the reading of Bhāsa, Śūdraka, and Bhavabhūti, the examination also probes the candidate’s grasp of the performance matrix encoded in the Nāṭyaśāstra (chapters 4–7, 13–19). The text prescribes a four‑fold abhinaya — āṅgika (body), vācika (speech), āhārya (costume/make‑up), sāttvika (internal emotional states) — and maps each to specific rasa outcomes. For instance, the śṛṅgāra rasa in the Śṛṅgāraprakaraṇa of the Mṛcchakaṭika is realised through a calibrated combination of hasta gestures (e.g., katakāmukha for “calling”), svara modulation in the gīti verses, and the sāttvika romāñcana (horripilation) described in Nāṭyaśāstra 6.31.

The stage space (raṅga) is divided into pūrvaraṅga (front), paścimaraṅga (rear), and the nāṭyamaṇḍapa (central acting area). The Nāṭyaśāstra 2.71–73 stipulates the placement of the yavanikā (curtain) and the mṛdaṅga (drum) for rhythmic punctuation. In the Abhijñānaśākuntalam, the forest scene (Act 3) exploits the pūrvaraṅga for the entrance of the vidūṣaka and the paścimaraṅga for the off‑stage voice of the devatā, a spatial cue that the examiner may ask to identify.

Modern reconstructions — notably the Kutiyattam tradition of Kerala (UNESCO Intangible Heritage) and the Sanskrit Ranga productions at the National School of Drama — provide living evidence of how the Nāṭyaśāstra directives are interpreted. Kutiyattam retains the nirvahana (elaborated enactment) of a single śloka over several hours, illustrating the āṅgika micro‑gestures catalogued in Nāṭyaśāstra 9. Candidates who can cite a specific Kutiyattam rendering (e.g., the Mālavikāgnimitra Act 1 performed by the Ammannur family) and link it to the textual prescription earn extra credit for “critical synthesis”.

A common error is to treat the Nāṭyaśāstra as a monolithic rule‑book ignoring the deśī (regional) variations acknowledged in chapter 28. The exam expects the candidate to note that the Mṛcchakaṭika incorporates prākṛta songs in Māhārāṣṭrī, a deviation from the Nāṭyaśāstra’s prescription of Sanskrit for gīti, and to explain this as a deliberate deśī adaptation for popular appeal.

Nirukta and the Etymological Method: Yāska’s Contribution to Vedic Interpretation and Textual Analysis

The Nirukta of Yāska (c. 6th century BCE) stands as the earliest systematic treatise on Vedic etymology and remains a prescribed reference for the “History of the Language” segment of Paper I. Its methodology — nirvacana (etymological explanation) — operates through three principal devices: dhātu (root) identification, upasarga (preverb) analysis, and pratyaya (suffix) classification. Yāska arranges the Vedic lexicon into naighaṇṭuka (synonym lists) and naigama (difficult words), then supplies nirvacana for each entry, often citing the Ṛgveda passage where the word occurs.

For the exam, the candidate must be able to (a) reproduce a sample nirvacana — e.g., agni = agri “to lead” + ni “forward” → “the leader of the sacrifice”; (b) distinguish Yāska’s nairukta (etymological) approach from the nairuktāḥ (grammarians) who follow Pāṇini’s dhātupāṭha; (c) explain the role of the Nirukta in preserving the padapāṭha (word‑by‑word recitation) of the Ṛgveda. A typical question asks: “Illustrate how Yāska resolves the polysemy of vṛṣṭi in Ṛgveda 1.1.1.” The answer must quote the Nirukta 1.12 (vṛṣṭiḥ vṛṣṭau) and show the two derivations — “rain” from vṛṣ “to sprinkle” and “speech” from vṛṣ “to speak” — then comment on the contextual selection made by the ṛṣi.

The Nirukta also informs the Vedāṅga curriculum: it is studied after Śikṣā (phonetics) and Chandas (metre) but before Vyākaraṇa (grammar). This sequence reflects the pedagogical logic that etymological clarity precedes morphological analysis. In answer writing, citing the Nirukta by chapter and kāṇḍikā (e.g., Nirukta 2.3) is mandatory; vague references to “Yāska says” attract penalty.

A subtle trap is the conflation of Yāska’s nirvacana with the later Nirukta‑ṭīkā of Durgācārya (12th century CE). The syllabus expects the primary text; the commentary may be invoked only to clarify an obscure derivation, and then only with the siglum “Durga on Nirukta 2.3”.

  • Confusing the Abhidharmakośa (root text) with the Abhidharmakośabhāṣya (Vasubandhu’s commentary) — they are distinct works with different doctrinal weight.
  • Treating the Ardhamāgadhī Jain canon as a Sanskrit source for Paper II — the syllabus restricts Jain material to Sanskrit compositions only.
  • Citing a digital facsimile (e.g., GRETIL) as the primary edition when a printed critical edition exists — the examiner requires the printed siglum.
  • Overlooking the deśī variations acknowledged in Nāṭyaśāstra 28 and presenting the text as a uniform prescriptive manual.
  • Quoting Yāska’s Nirukta without the chapter‑kāṇḍikā reference — precise locus is mandatory for full marks.
  • Misidentifying the metre of a Buddhist Sanskrit verse (e.g., labeling an āryā as śloka) — metrical accuracy is part of the literary analysis rubric.
  • Presenting the pūrvapakṣa–siddhānta structure only in darśana answers while neglecting it in poetics or dramaturgy questions — the dialectical format is expected across all analytical sections.
