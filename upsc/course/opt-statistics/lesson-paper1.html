1. Foundations of Probability Theory

1.1 The Axiomatic Framework of Kolmogorov

The modern theory of probability rests on the axiomatic system introduced by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933, which placed the subject on the same rigorous measure-theoretic footing as analysis. A probability space is defined as a triple consisting of a sample space Ω containing all elementary outcomes, a σ-algebra ℱ of subsets of Ω representing events, and a probability measure P mapping ℱ to the interval [0, 1]. The three axioms require that the probability of any event be non-negative, that the probability of the entire sample space equal unity, and that the probability of a countable union of mutually exclusive events equal the sum of their individual probabilities. This last axiom, countable additivity, distinguishes the modern theory from earlier finite-additivity approaches and enables the treatment of continuous sample spaces and limiting arguments essential for statistical inference. The axioms imply immediate consequences such as the probability of the empty set being zero, the complement rule P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A), and the monotonicity property that A ⊆ B implies P(A) ≤ P(B). These foundational results allow the construction of more complex probabilistic models while guaranteeing internal consistency.

1.2 Conditional Probability and the Multiplication Rule

Conditional probability quantifies how the occurrence of one event alters the likelihood of another. For events A and B with P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is defined as P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). This definition captures the intuitive idea of restricting the sample space to those outcomes where B has occurred and then measuring the relative frequency of A within that reduced space. The multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B)P(B) follows directly and extends to chains of events, yielding P(A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ) = P(A₁)P(A₂|A₁)⋯P(Aₙ|A₁∩…∩Aₙ₋₁). This factorization is indispensable in sequential experiments and in the derivation of likelihood functions for dependent data. The law of total probability partitions the sample space using a countable collection of mutually exclusive and exhaustive events {Bᵢ}, giving P(A) = Σᵢ P(A|Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ). This decomposition underpins Bayesian updating, where prior probabilities P(Bᵢ) are revised to posterior probabilities P(Bᵢ|A) upon observing evidence A.

1.3 Bayes' Theorem and Its Inferential Role

Bayes' theorem, P(Bᵢ|A) = P(A|Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ) / Σⱼ P(A|Bⱼ)P(Bⱼ), provides the mathematical mechanism for inverting conditional probabilities. The numerator combines the likelihood of the observed data under hypothesis Bᵢ with the prior probability of that hypothesis; the denominator acts as a normalizing constant ensuring the posterior probabilities sum to one. In statistical inference, the theorem formalizes the process of learning from data: prior beliefs about parameters are updated to posterior beliefs through the likelihood function. The theorem also reveals the base-rate fallacy, where neglect of the prior P(Bᵢ) leads to overestimation of rare-event probabilities. Applications range from diagnostic testing, where sensitivity and specificity must be combined with disease prevalence, to spam filtering, where word frequencies update the probability that a message is unwanted. The continuous analogue replaces sums with integrals and probability mass functions with densities, yielding the posterior density π(θ|x) ∝ f(x|θ)π(θ), which is the cornerstone of Bayesian statistics.

1.4 Independence and Its Implications

Two events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), which is equivalent to P(A|B) = P(A) whenever P(B) > 0. Independence means that knowledge of B provides no information about A, and vice versa. The concept extends to collections of events: a finite family {A₁, …, Aₙ} is mutually independent if the probability of any intersection equals the product of the individual probabilities. Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence, a subtle point illustrated by the classic example of two fair coin tosses where A = {first head}, B = {second head}, C = {same outcome} are pairwise independent but not mutually independent. For random variables, independence means the joint distribution factorizes into the product of marginals. This factorization simplifies the computation of expectations of products, since E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] for independent variables, and it underlies the additivity of variance for independent sums: Var(ΣXᵢ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ). Independence is a modeling assumption that must be justified by the physical mechanism of the experiment; its violation leads to biased standard errors and invalid hypothesis tests.

2. Random Variables and Mathematical Expectation

2.1 Definition and Classification of Random Variables

A random variable is a measurable function from the sample space Ω to the real line ℝ, assigning a numerical value to each elementary outcome. Measurability ensures that events of the form {X ≤ x} belong to the σ-algebra, so probabilities can be assigned to them. Random variables are classified by the nature of their range: discrete random variables take at most countably many values and are described by a probability mass function p(x) = P(X = x); continuous random variables possess a probability density function f(x) such that P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx; mixed random variables combine both behaviors, having a probability mass at some points and a density elsewhere. The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) unifies these cases, being non-decreasing, right-continuous, with limits 0 at −∞ and 1 at +∞. For discrete variables F is a step function; for continuous variables it is absolutely continuous with derivative f(x) almost everywhere. The quantile function F⁻¹(p) = inf{x : F(x) ≥ p} provides the inverse mapping used in simulation and in defining measures of spread such as the interquartile range.

2.2 Expectation, Variance, and Moment Generating Functions

The expectation of a random variable represents its long-run average value under repeated independent sampling. For a discrete variable, E[X] = Σ x p(x); for a continuous variable, E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx, provided the sum or integral converges absolutely. Expectation is linear: E[aX + bY + c] = aE[X] + bE[Y] + c for any constants a, b, c and random variables X, Y, regardless of dependence. The variance Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ² measures dispersion about the mean μ = E[X]. The standard deviation σ = √Var(X) restores the original units. Higher central moments characterize shape: the third standardized moment γ₁ = E[(X−μ)³]/σ³ measures skewness, indicating asymmetry; the fourth standardized moment γ₂ = E[(X−μ)⁴]/σ³ − 3 measures excess kurtosis, indicating tail heaviness relative to the normal distribution. The moment generating function M_X(t) = E[etX], when it exists in a neighborhood of zero, uniquely determines the distribution and generates moments via differentiation: E[Xᵏ] = M_X⁽ᵏ⁾(0). The characteristic function φ_X(t) = E[eitX] always exists and provides an alternative transform useful for proving limit theorems. The cumulant generating function K_X(t) = log M_X(t) yields cumulants, which simplify under addition of independent variables: the nth cumulant of a sum equals the sum of nth cumulants.

2.3 Conditional Expectation and Regression

Conditional expectation E[Y|X = x] generalizes the idea of an average to subpopulations defined by the value of another variable. For discrete X, E[Y|X = x] = Σ y p(y|x); for continuous X, it is ∫ y f(y|x) dy. The law of iterated expectations, E[Y] = E[E[Y|X]], states that the overall mean equals the average of the conditional means, a result used repeatedly in variance decomposition: Var(Y) = E[Var(Y|X)] + Var(E[Y|X]). The conditional expectation function m(x) = E[Y|X = x] is the best predictor of Y given X in the sense of minimizing mean squared error among all functions of X. The best linear predictor, obtained by restricting to functions of the form a + bX, yields the regression line Y = α + βX with β = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X) and α = E[Y] − βE[X]. The correlation coefficient ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) measures the strength of linear association, bounded between −1 and 1 by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. When (X,Y) is bivariate normal, the conditional expectation is exactly linear and the conditional variance is constant, making the regression line coincide with the conditional mean function.

Worked Example: Maximum Likelihood Estimation for the Poisson Distribution
Suppose X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ is a random sample from a Poisson distribution with unknown parameter λ > 0. The probability mass function is f(x|λ) = e−λ λˣ / x! for x = 0, 1, 2, … . Derive the maximum likelihood estimator of λ and show that it is unbiased and consistent.
The likelihood function is L(λ) = ∏i=1ⁿ e−λ λxᵢ / xᵢ! = e−nλ λΣxᵢ / ∏ xᵢ!. The log-likelihood is ℓ(λ) = −nλ + (Σxᵢ) log λ − Σ log(xᵢ!). Differentiating with respect to λ gives ℓ'(λ) = −n + (Σxᵢ)/λ. Setting the derivative to zero yields the maximum likelihood equation −n + (Σxᵢ)/λ̂ = 0, so λ̂ = (ΣXᵢ)/n = X̄. The second derivative ℓ''(λ) = −(Σxᵢ)/λ² is negative for λ > 0, confirming a maximum. Since E[Xᵢ] = λ for each Poisson observation, E[λ̂] = E[X̄] = λ, proving unbiasedness. By the weak law of large numbers, X̄ converges in probability to λ, so λ̂ is consistent. The Fisher information is I(λ) = −E[ℓ''(λ)] = n/λ, and the Cramér–Rao lower bound for unbiased estimators is λ/n, which equals Var(X̄) = λ/n, showing that the MLE attains the minimum variance bound asymptotically.
Exam Insight: Recognizing Exponential Family Structure
When a distribution belongs to the exponential family, the MLE of the natural parameter is simply the sample mean of the sufficient statistic. For the Poisson, the pmf can be written as exp{x log λ − λ − log x!}, revealing log λ as the natural parameter and X as the sufficient statistic. In the examination, identifying this structure allows you to write down the MLE immediately without re-deriving the likelihood equations, saving valuable time for the more demanding parts of the question.

3. Standard Probability Distributions

3.1 Discrete Distributions and Their Interrelationships

The Bernoulli distribution with parameter p models a single trial with two outcomes, success (1) and failure (0). The binomial distribution Bin(n, p) arises as the sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) variables, counting the number of successes in n trials. Its probability mass function is P(X = k) = C(n, k) pᵏ (1−p)n−k for k = 0, 1, …, n, with mean np and variance np(1−p). The Poisson distribution Pois(λ) models the number of rare events occurring in a fixed interval, with pmf P(X = k) = e−λ λᵏ / k! and equal mean and variance λ. It emerges as the limiting distribution of Bin(n, p) when n → ∞, p → 0 with np = λ fixed, providing a practical approximation when n is large and p is small. The geometric distribution Geo(p) counts the number of trials until the first success, with pmf P(X = k) = (1−p)k−1 p for k = 1, 2, … and mean 1/p. The negative binomial NB(r, p) generalizes this to the number of trials until the r-th success, with mean r/p. The hypergeometric distribution Hyp(N, K, n) describes sampling without replacement from a finite population of size N containing K successes, with pmf P(X = k) = C(K, k) C(N−K, n−k) / C(N, n). When N is large relative to n, the hypergeometric is well approximated by the binomial, reflecting the diminishing effect of sampling without replacement.

3.2 Continuous Distributions and Their Properties

The uniform distribution U(a, b) assigns constant density 1/(b−a) on the interval [a, b], serving as the baseline for generating other distributions via transformation methods. The exponential distribution Exp(λ) with density λe−λx for x ≥ 0 is the only continuous distribution possessing the memoryless property P(X > s+t | X > s) = P(X > t), making it the natural model for waiting times in a Poisson process. The gamma distribution Gamma(α, β) with density (β^α/Γ(α)) xα−1 e−βx for x > 0 generalizes the exponential (α = 1) and the chi-square (α = k/2, β = 1/2). Its mean is α/β and variance α/β². The beta distribution Beta(α, β) with density xα−1(1−x)β−1/B(α, β) on (0, 1) is the conjugate prior for the binomial proportion and models random probabilities. The normal distribution N(μ, σ²) with density (1/√(2πσ²)) e−(x−μ)²/(2σ²) occupies a central role due to the central limit theorem; its symmetry, bell shape, and the 68–95–99.7 rule make it the default approximation for sums of independent random variables. The chi-square distribution with k degrees of freedom is the distribution of the sum of k independent standard normal squares; it underlies goodness-of-fit tests and variance inference. The t-distribution with ν degrees of freedom arises as the ratio of a standard normal to the square root of an independent chi-square divided by its degrees of freedom; its heavier tails account for the uncertainty in estimating σ. The F-distribution with (d₁, d₂) degrees of freedom is the ratio of two independent chi-squares divided by their respective degrees of freedom; it is the reference distribution for analysis of variance and regression F-tests.

3.3 Approximations and Transformations Between Distributions

Relationships among distributions are not merely theoretical curiosities; they provide computational shortcuts and justify asymptotic procedures. The normal approximation to the binomial, with continuity correction, is accurate when np(1−p) > 5. The Poisson approximation to the binomial works when n is large and p is small. The gamma distribution with integer shape parameter α = r is the distribution of the sum of r independent exponential(β) variables, linking waiting times for the r-th event in a Poisson process to the gamma. The beta distribution with parameters (α, β) is the distribution of the α-th order statistic from a uniform sample of size α+β−1. The square of a standard normal is chi-square with one degree of freedom; the sum of independent chi-squares is chi-square with degrees of freedom added. The ratio of two independent chi-squares divided by their degrees of freedom follows the F-distribution. The t-statistic t = (X̄ − μ)/(S/√n) follows a t-distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom when sampling from a normal population, providing the basis for small-sample inference on the mean. These connections form a web that allows the statistician to move between exact and approximate distributions depending on sample size and modeling assumptions.

4. Joint Distributions and Correlation

4.1 Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Distributions

When two or more random variables are observed simultaneously, their joint behavior is described by a joint probability mass function p(x, y) for discrete variables or a joint probability density function f(x, y) for continuous variables. The marginal distribution of X is obtained by summing or integrating over the other variable: p_X(x) = Σ_y p(x, y) or f_X(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy. The conditional distribution of Y given X = x is defined as p(y|x) = p(x, y)/p_X(x) for discrete variables and f(y|x) = f(x, y)/f_X(x) for continuous variables, provided the denominator is positive. Independence is equivalent to the factorization p(x, y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y) or f(x, y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y) for all x, y. This factorization implies that conditional distributions equal marginal distributions, and that the joint cumulative distribution function factorizes as F(x, y) = F_X(x)F_Y(y). The support of the joint distribution reveals dependence: if the set of possible (x, y) pairs is not a Cartesian product, the variables cannot be independent. For example, the uniform distribution on the unit disc has circular support, so X and Y are dependent despite having symmetric marginals.

4.2 Covariance, Correlation, and Linear Relationships

The covariance Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)] = E[XY] − μ_Xμ_Y measures the average product of deviations from the means. Positive covariance indicates that large values of X tend to occur with large values of Y; negative covariance indicates the opposite. The correlation coefficient ρ = Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) standardizes covariance to the interval [−1, 1], removing the effect of scale. A correlation of ±1 implies a perfect linear relationship Y = a + bX almost surely, with b having the same sign as ρ. Zero correlation (uncorrelatedness) does not imply independence except in the special case of jointly normal variables. For bivariate normal (X, Y), zero correlation is equivalent to independence because the joint density factorizes when ρ = 0. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality guarantees |ρ| ≤ 1, with equality only when Y is a linear function of X. The coefficient of determination ρ² represents the proportion of variance in Y explained by the linear regression on X. In multivariate settings, the covariance matrix Σ = E[(X − μ)(X − μ)ᵀ] and the correlation matrix R with entries ρij summarize all pairwise linear associations.

4.3 Bivariate Normal Distribution and Its Properties

The bivariate normal distribution is the most important joint distribution in statistical practice. Its density is f(x, y) = 1/(2πσ_Xσ_Y√(1−ρ²)) exp{−1/(2(1−ρ²)) [ (x−μ_X)²/σ_X² − 2ρ(x−μ_X)(y−μ_Y)/(σ_Xσ_Y) + (y−μ_Y)²/σ_Y² ] }. Every linear combination aX + bY is normally distributed, and any subset of components is multivariate normal. The conditional distribution of Y given X = x is normal with mean μ_Y + ρ(σ_Y/σ_X)(x − μ_X) and variance σ_Y²(1 − ρ²). This linearity of conditional expectation and constancy of conditional variance are characteristic properties of the bivariate normal. The regression of Y on X is exactly linear, and the regression of X on Y is also linear, with slopes βY|X = ρσ_Y/σ_X and βX|Y = ρσ_X/σ_Y. The product of the two slopes equals ρ². The bivariate normal arises naturally as the limiting distribution of sample moments under mild conditions, and as the limiting distribution of sample moments under mild conditions.

5. Convergence Concepts and Limit Theorems

5.1 Modes of Convergence for Random Variables

Convergence of sequences of random variables is more nuanced than convergence of real numbers because random variables are functions on a probability space. Convergence in probability, denoted Xₙ →ₚ X, means that for every ε > 0, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞. This mode captures the idea that the probability of a large deviation vanishes. Almost sure convergence, Xₙ → X a.s., requires P(limn→∞ Xₙ = X) = 1, meaning the set of sample paths where convergence fails has probability zero. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, but the converse is false; a sequence can converge in probability without converging almost surely, as illustrated by the "typewriter" sequence of indicators on shrinking intervals. Convergence in distribution, Xₙ →_d X, means the cumulative distribution functions converge at all continuity points of the limit distribution: FXₙ(x) → F_X(x). This is the weakest mode, concerned only with the limiting distribution, not with the joint behavior of the sequence. Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution, but not vice versa. Convergence in L^p (mean of order p) means E[|Xₙ − X|^p] → 0; L^p convergence implies convergence in probability by Markov's inequality. These hierarchies are essential for understanding the strength of various limit theorems and for justifying asymptotic approximations in statistical inference.

5.2 Laws of Large Numbers

The weak law of large numbers (WLLN) states that for a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables with finite mean μ, the sample mean X̄ₙ converges in probability to μ. Chebyshev's inequality provides a simple proof when the variance is finite: P(|X̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(X)/(nε²) → 0. The strong law of large numbers (SLLN) asserts almost sure convergence of X̄ₙ to μ under the same i.i.d. assumption with finite mean. The SLLN is a deeper result, requiring measure-theoretic tools such as the Borel–Cantelli lemmas or martingale convergence. Both laws justify the frequentist interpretation of probability as long-run relative frequency. The WLLN suffices for most statistical applications because convergence in probability is preserved under continuous transformations (continuous mapping theorem), allowing the construction of consistent estimators. The SLLN guarantees that the sample mean eventually stays arbitrarily close to the population mean for almost every realization, a stronger assurance for simulation and ergodic theory.

5.3 Central Limit Theorem and Its Extensions

The central limit theorem (CLT) is the cornerstone of asymptotic statistics. For i.i.d. random variables with finite mean μ and finite variance σ², the standardized sum (Σi=1^n X_i − nμ)/(σ√n) = √n (X̄ₙ − μ)/σ converges in distribution to a standard normal N(0, 1). This remarkable result holds regardless of the shape of the underlying distribution, explaining the ubiquity of the normal approximation. The Lindeberg–Lévy CLT is the classical version; the Lindeberg–Feller CLT extends it to independent but not identically distributed variables under a Lindeberg condition that prevents any single variable from dominating the sum. The Lyapunov CLT provides a simpler sufficient condition involving moments of order 2+δ. The CLT justifies the use of normal critical values for t-statistics and z-statistics in large samples, the construction of asymptotic confidence intervals, and the derivation of the asymptotic distribution of maximum likelihood estimators. The delta method extends the CLT to smooth functions of the sample mean: if √n (X̄ₙ − μ) →_d N(0, σ²), then √n (g(X̄ₙ) − g(μ)) →_d N(0, [g'(μ)]²σ²) for differentiable g. This enables inference on transformed parameters such as log-odds or variance-stabilizing transformations.

6. Statistical Inference and Sampling Theory

6.1 Point Estimation: Principles and Criteria

Point estimation seeks a single value, derived from the sample, that approximates an unknown population parameter. An estimator is a statistic, a function of the sample, used to estimate the parameter. Unbiasedness requires E[θ̂] = θ for all θ in the parameter space; it ensures that the estimator is correct on average over repeated sampling. The mean squared error MSE(θ̂) = E[(θ̂ − θ)²] = Var(θ̂) + Bias(θ̂)² decomposes estimation error into variance and squared bias. Among unbiased estimators, the one with minimum variance is the minimum variance unbiased estimator (MVUE). The Cramér–Rao lower bound states that for any unbiased estimator of a differentiable parameter, Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/I(θ), where I(θ) = E[(∂/∂θ log f(X;θ))²] is the Fisher information. An unbiased estimator achieving this bound is efficient. The Rao–Blackwell theorem improves an unbiased estimator by conditioning on a sufficient statistic: if T is sufficient and U is unbiased, then E[U|T] is unbiased with variance no larger than U. The Lehmann–Scheffé theorem completes the picture: if T is complete and sufficient, then any unbiased function of T is the unique MVUE. These results provide a systematic pathway to optimal estimation: find a sufficient statistic, check completeness, and construct an unbiased function of it.

6.2 Maximum Likelihood Estimation and Its Properties

The method of maximum likelihood chooses the parameter value that maximizes the likelihood function L(θ) = f(x₁, …, xₙ; θ), or equivalently the log-likelihood ℓ(θ) = log L(θ). The MLE θ̂ satisfies the likelihood equation ∂ℓ/∂θ = 0, provided regularity conditions hold. Under standard regularity, the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information, and asymptotically efficient, achieving the Cramér–Rao bound in the limit. The invariance property states that if θ̂ is the MLE of θ, then g(θ̂) is the MLE of g(θ) for any function g. This simplifies estimation of transformed parameters. The observed Fisher information −∂²ℓ/∂θ² evaluated at θ̂ provides an estimate of the asymptotic variance, used to construct Wald confidence intervals. The likelihood ratio statistic λ = 2[ℓ(θ̂) − ℓ(θ₀)] converges in distribution to chi-square with degrees of freedom equal to the number of restrictions, providing the basis for likelihood ratio tests. When the likelihood is intractable, numerical optimization such as Newton–Raphson or EM algorithms is employed.

Interval Estimation: Confidence Intervals and Pivot Methods

Interval estimation replaces a single point estimate with a random interval that covers the unknown parameter with a prescribed probability. A confidence interval (CI) for a parameter θ at confidence level 1−α is a pair of statistics (L, U) such that Pθ(L ≤ θ ≤ U) = 1−α for all θ in the parameter space. The construction typically relies on a pivot — a function of the sample and the parameter whose distribution does not depend on θ. For a normal sample with known variance σ², the pivot Z = (X̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) follows a standard normal distribution, yielding the familiar symmetric interval X̄ ± zα/2·σ/√n. When σ² is unknown, the pivot T = (X̄ − μ)/(S/√n) follows a Student t distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom, giving the t‑interval X̄ ± tα/2,n−1·S/√n.

For non‑normal populations, large‑sample theory provides approximate pivots via the central limit theorem. The Wald interval uses the asymptotic normality of the MLE: θ̂ ± zα/2·√(I(θ̂)⁻¹). The score interval inverts the score test and often has better coverage for small samples, especially for binomial proportions. The likelihood‑ratio interval inverts the likelihood‑ratio test and is invariant under reparameterisation. Exact intervals exist for discrete distributions (Clopper–Pearson for binomial, Garwood for Poisson) but are conservative. Bootstrap percentile and bias‑corrected accelerated (BCa) intervals provide simulation‑based alternatives when analytic pivots are unavailable.

Interpretation is frequentist: in repeated sampling, a proportion 1−α of such intervals will contain the true parameter. The interval itself is random; the parameter is fixed. A common mistake is to state “there is a 95 % probability that μ lies in this interval”. The correct phrasing is “we are 95 % confident that the procedure that generated this interval produces intervals covering μ in 95 % of repeated samples”. Coverage probability can differ from nominal level for small samples or discrete parameters; checking actual coverage via simulation is good practice.

Hypothesis Testing: Neyman–Pearson Lemma, Likelihood Ratio Tests, and Power

Hypothesis testing formalises the decision between a null hypothesis H₀ and an alternative H₁. A test is a function φ(x) ∈ [0,1] giving the probability of rejecting H₀ when the data are x. The size (significance level) is supθ∈Θ₀ Eθ[φ(X)] ≤ α. The power function β(θ) = Eθ[φ(X)] for θ ∈ Θ₁ measures the probability of correctly rejecting H₀. The Neyman–Pearson lemma states that for simple hypotheses H₀: θ = θ₀ versus H₁: θ = θ₁, the most powerful test of size α rejects when the likelihood ratio L(θ₁)/L(θ₀) exceeds a constant k, with k chosen to achieve size α. This likelihood‑ratio test (LRT) is most powerful (MP) among all tests of size α.

For composite hypotheses, a UMP test rarely exists. The generalised likelihood‑ratio test (GLRT) rejects for large values of λ = 2[ℓ(θ̂) − ℓ(θ̂₀)], where θ̂ is the unrestricted MLE and θ̂₀ is the MLE under H₀. Under regularity conditions, λ converges in distribution to χ² with degrees of freedom equal to the difference in parameter dimensions (Wilks’ theorem). This provides an asymptotic χ² test for nested models. The Wald test uses (θ̂ − θ₀)ᵀ I(θ̂)(θ̂ − θ₀) and the score test uses U(θ₀)ᵀ I(θ₀)⁻¹ U(θ₀); all three are asymptotically equivalent.

Power analysis guides sample‑size determination. For a one‑sided normal mean test with known variance, power = 1 − Φ(zα − δ√n/σ) where δ = μ₁ − μ₀. The non‑centrality parameter δ√n/σ quantifies the distance between hypotheses. In practice, one specifies α, desired power (often 0.8 or 0.9), and a minimally important effect size to solve for n. Multiple testing adjustments (Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg) control family‑wise error rate or false discovery rate when many hypotheses are examined simultaneously.

Worked Example: Constructing a UMP Test for Exponential Rate
Let X₁,…,Xₙ be i.i.d. Exp(λ) with density f(x;λ)=λe−λx, x>0. Test H₀: λ = λ₀ versus H₁: λ = λ₁ where λ₁ > λ₀. Derive the most powerful test of size α and express the critical region in terms of the sample sum.
The likelihood ratio is L(λ₁)/L(λ₀) = (λ₁/λ₀)ⁿ exp{−(λ₁−λ₀)∑Xᵢ}. Since λ₁ > λ₀, the ratio is a decreasing function of T = ∑Xᵢ. By the Neyman–Pearson lemma, the MP test rejects H₀ for small values of T. Under H₀, 2λ₀T ∼ χ²2n. Choose c such that Pλ₀(T ≤ c) = α, i.e. c = χ²2n,α/(2λ₀). The rejection region is {∑Xᵢ ≤ χ²2n,α/(2λ₀)}. This test is UMP for the one‑sided composite alternative λ > λ₀ because the family has monotone likelihood ratio in T.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Testing Problems
When a question asks for “the most powerful test”, immediately check whether the hypotheses are simple. If they are, write down the likelihood ratio, simplify to a monotone function of a sufficient statistic, and identify the critical region using the exact null distribution of that statistic. For composite alternatives, state whether a UMP test exists (often it does not) and then derive the GLRT, Wald, or score test, citing Wilks’ theorem for the asymptotic χ² distribution. Always define the test function φ(x) explicitly and verify the size condition.

Sufficiency, Completeness, and Ancillarity: Factorization Theorem and Basu’s Theorem

A statistic T(X) is sufficient for θ if the conditional distribution of X given T does not depend on θ. The Fisher–Neyman factorisation theorem gives a practical criterion: f(x;θ) = g(T(x);θ) h(x) for some functions g and h. Minimal sufficiency means T is a function of every other sufficient statistic; it can be found by examining the ratio f(x;θ)/f(y;θ) and identifying the equivalence classes where the ratio is constant in θ. For exponential families in canonical form, the natural statistic is minimal sufficient.

Completeness strengthens sufficiency: a statistic T is complete if Eθ[g(T)] = 0 for all θ implies g(T) = 0 almost surely. In a full‑rank exponential family, the natural statistic is complete. The Lehmann–Scheffé theorem then guarantees that any unbiased function of a complete sufficient statistic is the unique MVUE. Ancillarity is the opposite notion: a statistic A is ancillary if its distribution does not depend on θ. Basu’s theorem states that any boundedly complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic. This independence underpins the separation of inference about θ from inference about nuisance parameters and justifies conditioning on ancillary statistics to obtain exact tests (e.g., Fisher’s exact test for 2×2 tables conditions on the marginal totals, which are ancillary for the odds ratio).

Practical identification: for a normal sample with unknown mean μ and known variance, X̄ is complete sufficient; the sample variance S² is ancillary for μ. For a uniform distribution on (0,θ), the maximum X_(n) is complete sufficient; the range is not ancillary. Recognising these structures allows rapid construction of MVUEs via Rao–Blackwellisation and Lehmann–Scheffé, and guides the choice of conditioning statistics for exact inference.

Bayesian Inference: Prior, Posterior, and Decision‑Theoretic Criteria

Bayesian inference treats the parameter θ as a random variable with a prior distribution π(θ) reflecting pre‑sample uncertainty. After observing data x, Bayes’ theorem updates the prior to the posterior π(θ|x) ∝ L(θ)π(θ). Conjugate priors yield posteriors in the same family: Beta–Binomial, Gamma–Poisson, Normal–Normal (known variance), Inverse‑Gamma–Normal (unknown variance). The posterior mean, median, or mode serve as point estimates; the posterior mean minimises posterior expected squared error loss, the median minimises absolute error loss, and the mode (MAP) minimises 0‑1 loss.

Credible intervals are direct probability statements: a 1−α credible set C satisfies ∫_C π(θ|x) dθ = 1−α. Highest posterior density (HPD) intervals have the shortest length for a given coverage. Unlike confidence intervals, credible intervals allow the interpretation “there is probability 1−α that θ lies in C”. Prior choice influences results, especially with small samples. Objective priors (Jeffreys prior π(θ) ∝ √I(θ), reference priors) aim to minimise prior information. Sensitivity analysis checks robustness to prior specification.

Decision theory formalises estimation and testing as minimising expected loss. For a loss function L(θ,δ), the Bayes rule δ*(x) minimises the posterior expected loss ∫ L(θ,δ) π(θ|x) dθ. Under squared error loss, δ* is the posterior mean; under absolute error loss, the posterior median. In hypothesis testing, the Bayes factor BF₁₀ = m₁(x)/m₀(x) compares marginal likelihoods under H₁ and H₀; posterior odds = prior odds × BF₁₀. Model selection uses posterior model probabilities or information criteria such as DIC, WAIC, or LOO‑CV that approximate out‑of‑sample predictive performance.

Sampling Theory: Design‑Based Estimation, Horvitz–Thompson Estimator, and Variance Estimation

Design‑based inference treats the population values as fixed and randomness arises solely from the sampling design. Let U = {1,…,N} be the finite population, y_i the study variable, and π_i = P(i ∈ s) the first‑order inclusion probability under a sampling design p(s). The Horvitz–Thompson (HT) estimator of the population total Y = ∑i∈U y_i is Ŷ_HT = ∑i∈s y_i/π_i. It is design‑unbiased: E_p[Ŷ_HT] = Y for any design with π_i > 0. The variance is Var_p(Ŷ_HT) = ∑i∈U∑j∈U (πij − π_iπ_j) (y_i/π_i)(y_j/π_j), where πij are second‑order inclusion probabilities.

For simple random sampling without replacement (SRSWOR) of size n, π_i = n/N, πij = n(n−1)/[N(N−1)], and the HT estimator reduces to the familiar expansion estimator Ŷ = (N/n)∑i∈s y_i with variance N²(1−f)S²/n, where f = n/N and S² is the population variance. For stratified sampling, the HT estimator applies within each stratum and the overall variance is the sum of stratum variances. Ratio and regression estimators improve efficiency by exploiting auxiliary variables x_i with known population totals X; the ratio estimator Ŷ_R = (∑i∈s y_i)/(∑i∈s x_i) · X is approximately unbiased and has smaller variance when y and x are highly correlated.

Variance estimation in complex designs uses linearisation (Taylor series), replication methods (balanced repeated replication, jackknife, bootstrap), or the generalised variance estimator (GVF). The Sen–Yates–Grundy form Var_p(Ŷ_HT) = −½ ∑i≠j (πij − π_iπ_j) (y_i/π_i − y_j/π_j)² is often preferred because it yields non‑negative estimates for many designs. Software packages (R survey, Stata svy) implement these estimators. Understanding the distinction between design‑based and model‑based (superpopulation) approaches is essential: design‑based properties hold for the realised sample under the known design, while model‑based properties rely on a stochastic model for the population values.

  • Confusing confidence level with posterior probability — confidence intervals are frequentist, credible intervals are Bayesian.
  • Assuming a UMP test exists for every composite alternative; often only GLRT or locally most powerful tests are available.
  • Forgetting to verify the regularity conditions (differentiability, support independence) before applying the Cramér–Rao bound or Wilks’ theorem.
  • Treating the Horvitz–Thompson estimator as model‑unbiased; it is design‑unbiased only under the sampling design.
  • Using the normal approximation for a binomial proportion without checking np and n(1−p) ≥ 5, leading to poor coverage.
  • Neglecting to condition on an ancillary statistic when an exact test is required, resulting in conservative or liberal size.
  • Misapplying the delta method by omitting the derivative g'(μ) or using it when g is not differentiable at the parameter value.

These advanced topics complete the core inferential toolkit required for rigorous statistical analysis and examination success.

Asymptotic Theory and Large Sample Inference

Asymptotic theory provides the backbone for approximating the sampling distributions of estimators and test statistics when exact finite‑sample results are unavailable. The central tools are the delta method, the central limit theorem for M‑estimators, and the asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) under standard regularity conditions. For a sequence of estimators Tn satisfying √n(Tn−θ) →d N(0, σ²), the delta method states that for a differentiable function g with g′(θ) ≠ 0, √n(g(Tn)−g(θ)) →d N(0, g′(θ)²σ²). This result extends to vector‑valued parameters via the multivariate delta method, where the asymptotic covariance matrix is ∇g(θ) Σ ∇g(θ)ᵀ.

The asymptotic distribution of the MLE follows from a Taylor expansion of the score function. Under identifiability, interior true parameter, twice continuous differentiability of the log‑likelihood, and non‑singular Fisher information I(θ), the MLE θ̂ satisfies √n(θ̂−θ) →d N(0, I(θ)⁻¹). The observed information Jn(θ̂) = −∂²ℓ/∂θ²|θ̂ provides a consistent estimator of I(θ), enabling Wald, score, and likelihood‑ratio tests that share the same χ² limiting distribution under the null. Wilks’ theorem formalises the likelihood‑ratio statistic: 2[ℓ(θ̂)−ℓ(θ̃)] →d χ²r, where r is the number of restrictions.

Higher‑order asymptotics refine these approximations. Edgeworth expansions incorporate skewness and kurtosis to improve coverage of confidence intervals, while saddlepoint approximations yield extremely accurate tail probabilities for statistics such as the sample mean of exponential families. Bootstrap methods (see later section) implement these ideas computationally. A practical caution: the regularity conditions must be verified — support of the distribution must not depend on the parameter, and the information matrix must be finite and invertible. Violations, as in the uniform distribution on [0, θ], lead to non‑standard limiting distributions (e.g., n(θ̂−θ) converges to an exponential law).

Nonparametric Inference and Rank‑Based Methods

Nonparametric inference avoids strong distributional assumptions by relying on the order structure of the data. The empirical distribution function F̂n(x) = n⁻¹∑i=1n I(Xi ≤ x) is the cornerstone; the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem guarantees uniform convergence, and Donsker’s theorem gives the weak convergence of √n(F̂n−F) to a Brownian bridge. This underpins the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for goodness‑of‑fit and the construction of confidence bands for F.

Rank‑based procedures replace observations by their ranks, yielding statistics invariant under monotone transformations. The Wilcoxon signed‑rank test for a symmetric location parameter uses the sum of signed ranks; its null distribution is known exactly for small n and asymptotically normal with mean 0 and variance n(n+1)(2n+1)/24. The Mann–Whitney U test (equivalently, the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test) compares two independent samples; under the null of identical continuous distributions, U has mean n1n2/2 and variance n1n2(n1+n2+1)/12. Both tests are consistent against a broad class of alternatives and achieve the Pitman asymptotic relative efficiency of 3/π ≈ 0.955 relative to the t‑test under normality, while outperforming it for heavy‑tailed distributions.

For more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test extends the rank‑sum idea; its statistic H = 12/(N(N+1)) ∑j=1k Rj²/nj − 3(N+1) follows χ²k−1 asymptotically. Rank correlation coefficients — Spearman’s ρS and Kendall’s τ — measure monotone association without assuming linearity or normality. Their asymptotic distributions are normal with variances that depend on the underlying copula; under independence, τ has variance 2(2n+5)/[9n(n−1)]. Nonparametric regression via kernel smoothing or splines estimates the conditional mean function m(x) = E[Y|X=x] with bandwidth selection governed by the bias‑variance trade‑off; the Nadaraya–Watson estimator achieves pointwise convergence rate n⁻²ᐟ⁵ under twice differentiability of m.

Technique: Deriving Asymptotic Distribution of Sample Median
Question: Let X1,...,Xn be i.i.d. with continuous density f positive at the population median θ. Show that √n (X̃_n - θ) converges in distribution to N(0, 1/(4 f(θ)2)).
Solution: The sample median X̃_n satisfies F̂_n(X̃_n) = 1/2. By Bahadur representation, X̃_n - θ = (1/2 - F̂_n(θ))/f(θ) + o_p(n-1/2). Since √n(F̂_n(θ) - 1/2) →_d N(0, 1/4) by the central limit theorem for the empirical process, Slutsky's theorem gives √n(X̃_n - θ) →_d N(0, 1/(4 f(θ)2)). Regularity requires f continuous and positive at θ.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for asymptotic distribution, first identify the estimator as a function of sample moments or quantiles, then apply the delta method or Bahadur representation. Write the regularity conditions explicitly; examiners award marks for stating them.

Multivariate Analysis: Inference for Mean Vectors and Covariance

Multivariate inference generalises univariate concepts to p‑dimensional random vectors. The multivariate normal distribution Np(μ, Σ) with density (2π)−p/2|Σ|−1/2 exp{−½(x−μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x−μ)} is the workhorse. For a random sample X1,…,Xn ∼ Np(μ, Σ), the sample mean X̄ and sample covariance S = (1/(n−1))∑(Xi−X̄)(Xi−X̄)ᵀ are independent, with X̄ ∼ Np(μ, Σ/n) and (n−1)S ∼ Wp(n−1, Σ) (Wishart distribution).

Testing H0: μ = μ0 uses Hotelling’s T² statistic T² = n(X̄−μ0)ᵀ S⁻¹ (X̄−μ0). Under H0, (n−p)/(p(n−1)) T² ∼ Fp, n−p. For two independent samples with common Σ, the two‑sample T² replaces S by the pooled covariance. Simultaneous confidence intervals for linear combinations aᵀμ are derived from the T² distribution: aᵀX̄ ± √(p(n−1)/(n−p) Fp, n−p; 1−α) √(aᵀS a/n).

Inference on Σ includes testing sphericity (Σ = σ²I) via the likelihood‑ratio statistic Λ = |S| / (tr(S)/p)p, which under normality yields −(n−1−(2p²+p+2)/(6p)) log Λ →d χ²p(p+1)/2−1. Testing equality of covariance matrices across groups uses Box’s M test, sensitive to non‑normality. Principal component analysis (PCA) diagonalises S = ΓΛΓᵀ; the eigenvalues λ1 ≥ … ≥ λp explain variance proportions. Asymptotic distributions of sample eigenvalues and eigenvectors follow from perturbation theory; for distinct population eigenvalues, √n(λ̂j−λj) →d N(0, 2λj²). Canonical correlation analysis finds linear combinations of two sets of variables with maximal correlation; the squared canonical correlations are eigenvalues of Σ11⁻¹ Σ12 Σ22⁻¹ Σ21.

Linear Models: Estimation, Diagnostics, and Model Selection

The general linear model Y = Xβ + ε, with Y (n×1), X (n×p) of full column rank, β (p×1), and ε ∼ Nn(0, σ²I), is the foundation of regression and ANOVA. The ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀY is BLUE (Gauss–Markov) and, under normality, the MLE. Its covariance is σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹, estimated by s²(XᵀX)⁻¹ where s² = RSS/(n−p) and RSS = Yᵀ(I−H)Y with hat matrix H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ.

Diagnostics assess model adequacy. Residuals e = (I−H)Y have covariance σ²(I−H); standardised residuals ri = ei/(s√(1−hii)) and studentised residuals ti = ei/(s(−i)√(1−hii)) (where s(−i) omits observation i) detect outliers. Leverage hii measures influence of Xi on its own fitted value; points with hii > 2

/n are high leverage. Cook’s distance Di = (β̂−β̂(−i))ᵀXᵀX(β̂−β̂(−i))/(p s²) combines leverage and residual size; values > 4/n warrant inspection. Multicollinearity inflates variances; the variance inflation factor VIFj = 1/(1−Rj²) (where Rj² is from regressing Xj on other predictors) exceeding 10 signals severe collinearity.

Model selection balances fit and parsimony. Mallows’ Cp = RSSp/s² − n + 2

estimates prediction error; models with Cp ≈ p are preferred. AIC = n log(RSS/n) + 2

and BIC = n log(RSS/n) + p log n penalise complexity differently; BIC is consistent for true model selection, AIC is asymptotically efficient for prediction. Cross‑validation (leave‑one‑out or k‑fold) directly estimates out‑of‑sample MSE. Stepwise procedures (forward, backward, bidirectional) automate search but can overfit; modern practice favours regularisation (ridge, lasso) which shrinks coefficients and performs variable selection via the L1 penalty.

Bootstrap and Resampling Methods

The bootstrap approximates the sampling distribution of a statistic by resampling from the empirical distribution. For an i.i.d. sample X1,…,Xn, the nonparametric bootstrap draws B bootstrap samples of size n with replacement, computes the statistic T* on each, and uses the empirical distribution of T* to estimate standard errors, bias, and confidence intervals. The percentile interval takes the α/2 and 1−α/2 quantiles of T*; the bias‑corrected and accelerated (BCa) interval adjusts for bias and skewness using the acceleration parameter a estimated via jackknife.

The parametric bootstrap assumes a parametric model, estimates parameters, and generates bootstrap samples from the fitted model. This is more efficient when the model is correct but can be misleading under misspecification. The wild bootstrap handles heteroscedasticity in regression by multiplying residuals by random weights with mean 0 and variance 1 (e.g., Rademacher or Mammen’s two‑point distribution). The block bootstrap preserves dependence in time series by resampling blocks of consecutive observations; the optimal block length grows as n¹ᐟ³.

Bootstrap consistency requires that the statistic be a smooth function of the empirical distribution (Hadamard differentiability). The sample median, for instance, is not Hadamard differentiable, so the naive bootstrap fails; the m‑out‑of‑n bootstrap (resampling m < n observations) restores consistency. The jackknife (leave‑one‑out) estimates bias and variance via pseudo‑values θ̂(−i) = nθ̂ − (n−1)θ̂(−i); it is less computationally intensive but can be unstable for non‑smooth statistics. The subsampling method draws without replacement smaller samples of size b = o(n) and works under minimal assumptions, providing valid confidence sets even when the bootstrap fails.

  • Assuming asymptotic normality holds for small samples without checking higher‑order terms or using bootstrap calibration.
  • Applying the delta method to a non‑differentiable function (e.g., indicator, absolute value) and ignoring the need for m‑out‑of‑n bootstrap.
  • Using the standard parametric bootstrap when the model is misspecified, leading to under‑coverage of confidence intervals.
  • Interpreting a non‑significant Hotelling’s T² as evidence of equality of mean vectors when the covariance matrices differ substantially.
  • Ignoring leverage points in regression diagnostics and reporting OLS estimates that are driven by a few high‑influence observations.
  • Selecting a model solely by minimising AIC without validating predictive performance on an independent test set or via cross‑validation.
  • Treating the percentile bootstrap interval as exact for skewed statistics; always consider BCa or studentised bootstrap intervals.

Edgeworth Expansions and Saddlepoint Approximations

Edgeworth expansions refine the central limit theorem by adding correction terms that involve cumulants of the underlying distribution. For a standardized sum Sn = (X̄n − μ)/(σ/√n) the expansion of its distribution function Fn(x) takes the form Φ(x) + φ(x)[ρ3(x²−1)/(6√n) + ρ4(x³−3x)/(24n) + ρ3²(x⁵−10x³+15x)/(72n) + …] where Φ and φ are the standard normal cdf and pdf, and ρk are standardized cumulants. The expansion is asymptotic; truncating after the n⁻¹ᐟ² term yields an error of order n⁻¹ under smoothness conditions (Cramér’s condition). Saddlepoint approximations, introduced by Daniels (1954), provide an alternative that is often more accurate in the tails. For a statistic with cumulant generating function K(t), the saddlepoint density approximation is f̂(x) = (2π)⁻¹ᐟ² |K''(t̂)|⁻¹ᐟ² exp[K(t̂) − t̂x] where t̂ solves K'(t̂) = x. The Lugannani–Rice formula extends this to tail probabilities: P(Sn ≥ x) ≈ 1 − Φ(w) + φ(w)(1/u − 1/w) with w = sign(t̂)√{2[t̂x − K(t̂)]} and u = t̂√{K''(t̂)}. Both methods require existence of the moment generating function in a neighbourhood of zero; heavy‑tailed distributions violate this and the approximations break down. In practice, Edgeworth expansions are used for constructing higher‑order confidence intervals (e.g., Hall’s bootstrap‑t intervals) while saddlepoint methods are preferred for likelihood‑based inference in exponential families, especially for small samples where the normal approximation is poor.

Robust Estimation: Influence Functions, Breakdown Point, and M‑Estimators

Robust statistics quantifies the sensitivity of an estimator to infinitesimal contamination via the influence function (IF). For a statistical functional T(F) the IF at point x is IF(x; T, F) = limε→0 [T((1−ε)F + εΔx) − T(F)]/ε, where Δx is a point mass at x. The gross‑error sensitivity γ* = supx |IF(x)| measures worst‑case local influence; the breakdown point ε* is the smallest contamination proportion that can drive the estimator arbitrarily far. The sample mean has IF(x) = x − μ, γ* = ∞, and ε* = 0, making it non‑robust. The median has bounded IF = sign(x − median)/(2f(median)), γ* = 1/(2f(median)), and ε* = 0.5, the maximum possible for location. M‑estimators generalize maximum likelihood by solving Σ ψ(Xi − θ) = 0 for a ψ‑function. Huber’s ψc(u) = max(−c, min(c, u)) yields a compromise: quadratic near zero (efficiency) and linear in the tails (bounded influence). The corresponding IF is proportional to ψ, so γ* = c. Tukey’s biweight ψc(u) = u[1 − (u/c)²]² for |u| ≤ c and 0 otherwise gives redescending influence, further reducing the effect of gross outliers. Asymptotic variance of an M‑estimator is V = E[ψ²]/(E[ψ'])² under regularity; choosing c to achieve 95 % efficiency at the normal model gives c ≈ 1.345 for Huber and c ≈ 4.685 for biweight. In regression, M‑estimators replace least squares by minimizing Σ ρ(ri) with ρ' = ψ; the resulting estimating equations are Σ ψ(ri)xi = 0. High‑breakdown alternatives such as the least median of squares (LMS) and least trimmed squares (LTS) achieve ε* ≈ 0.5 but have low efficiency and non‑standard asymptotics; modern practice often combines a high‑breakdown initial estimator (e.g., S‑estimator) with a one‑step M‑refinement to retain both robustness and efficiency.

Worked Example: Influence Function of Huber’s M‑Estimator
Let ψc(u) = max(−c, min(c, u)) with c = 1.345. For a location model X ∼ N(θ, 1), derive the influence function of the corresponding M‑estimator θ̂ and compute its gross‑error sensitivity.
The estimating equation is E[ψc(X − θ)] = 0. By symmetry of the normal distribution, the solution is θ = 0. The influence function for an M‑estimator of location is IF(x) = ψc(x − θ) / E[ψc'(X − θ)]. Here ψc'(u) = 1 for |u| ≤ c and 0 otherwise, so E[ψc'] = P(|X| ≤ c) = Φ(c) − Φ(−c) = 2Φ(c) − 1. With c = 1.345, Φ(1.345) ≈ 0.910, thus E[ψc'] ≈ 0.820. The IF is therefore ψc(x)/0.820. Since |ψc(x)| ≤ c, the gross‑error sensitivity γ* = c / 0.820 ≈ 1.345 / 0.820 ≈ 1.64. This bounded IF confirms the robustness of Huber’s estimator against infinitesimal contamination.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the influence function of an M‑estimator, write the general formula IF(x) = ψ(x − θ)/E[ψ'(X − θ)] first, then substitute the specific ψ. Remember that the denominator is the expectation of the derivative, not the derivative of the expectation. For gross‑error sensitivity, simply take the supremum of the absolute IF; for Huber it is c divided by that denominator. Keep a small table of common ψ‑functions (Huber, Tukey, Andrew’s sine) and their tuning constants for 95 % normal efficiency — this saves derivation time.

Sequential Probability Ratio Test and Optimal Stopping

The sequential probability ratio test (SPRT), developed by Wald (1945), provides an optimal sequential test for simple hypotheses H₀: θ = θ₀ versus H₁: θ = θ₁. At each stage n, the log‑likelihood ratio Ln = Σi=1ⁿ log[f(Xi; θ₁)/f(Xi; θ₀)] is computed. The test stops at the first n such that Ln ≥ log B (accept H₁) or Ln ≤ log A (accept H₀), where A = β/(1−α) and B = (1−β)/α for prescribed type‑I error α and type‑II error β. Wald’s approximations for the average sample number (ASN) under θ₀ and θ₁ are E₀[N] ≈ (α log A + (1−α) log B)/E₀[log Λ] and E₁[N] ≈ (β log B + (1−β) log A)/E₁[log Λ], where Λ is the single‑observation likelihood ratio. The SPRT minimizes the expected sample size among all tests with the same error probabilities (Wald–Wolfowitz optimality). For composite hypotheses, the generalized SPRT replaces the simple likelihood ratio by a ratio of maximized likelihoods or by a Bayes factor; optimality no longer holds exactly but the procedure remains widely used in quality control and clinical trials. The truncated SPRT imposes a maximum sample size Nmax to guarantee termination; the error probabilities then exceed the nominal levels slightly, and exact boundaries can be obtained by dynamic programming (backward induction) on the likelihood ratio process. In the Bayesian formulation, the posterior odds after n observations equal the prior odds times exp(Ln); stopping when the posterior probability of one hypothesis exceeds a threshold yields a Bayes rule with a linear loss for each observation. The optimal stopping problem for a random walk with drift leads to the same boundaries via the principle of smooth fit. Modern extensions include group‑sequential designs (Pocock, O’Brien–Fleming boundaries) and alpha‑spending functions (Lan–DeMets) that allow flexible interim analyses while controlling overall type‑I error.

Empirical Process Theory: Glivenko‑Cantelli and Donsker Classes

Empirical process theory studies the uniform convergence of the empirical measure Pn to the true distribution P over a class of functions ℱ. The empirical process is the map f ↦ √n(Pn − P)f. A class ℱ is Glivenko‑Cantelli (GC) if supf∈ℱ |Pnf − Pf| → 0 almost surely; this is the uniform law of large numbers. By the Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) theorem, a class of indicator functions of sets is GC iff its VC dimension is finite. For real‑valued classes, a sufficient condition is that the uniform entropy integral ∫₀¹ √(log N(ε, ℱ, L₂(P))) dε < ∞, where N(ε, ℱ, L₂(P)) is the covering number. A class is Donsker if the empirical process converges weakly in ℓ∞(ℱ) to a tight Gaussian process GP with covariance P(fg) − Pf·Pg. Donsker classes are necessarily GC, but the converse fails. The classical Donsker theorem states that the class of indicator functions of half‑lines (−∞, x] is Donsker, yielding the Brownian bridge limit. For parametric classes ℱ = {fθ : θ ∈ Θ ⊂ ℝd} with Lipschitz dependence on θ, the entropy condition holds and the class is Donsker. Non‑Donsker examples include the class of all monotone functions on [0,1] (entropy integral diverges) and the class of indicators of intervals with data‑dependent endpoints. The functional delta method extends Donsker results to smooth functionals: if φ is Hadamard differentiable at P tangentially to a Donsker class, then √n(φ(Pn) − φ(P)) converges to φ'P(GP). This underpins the asymptotic theory of M‑estimators, quantile processes, and the bootstrap. The bootstrap consistency for empirical processes requires that ℱ be Donsker and that the bootstrap empirical process Gn* = √n(Pn* − Pn) converges conditionally to the same Gaussian limit; this holds for Donsker classes but fails for non‑Donsker classes such as the sample median, where the m‑out‑of‑n bootstrap restores consistency.

High‑Dimensional Inference: Lasso, SCAD, and Post‑Selection Inference

In high‑dimensional linear models Y = Xβ + ε with p ≫ n, sparsity assumptions (||β||₀ ≤ s) enable consistent estimation. The Lasso (Tibshirani 1996) solves minβ ||Y − Xβ||₂² + λ||β||₁. The KKT conditions give Xjᵀ(Y − Xβ̂) = λ sign(β̂j) for active coordinates and |Xjᵀ(Y − Xβ̂)| ≤ λ for inactive ones. Under the restricted eigenvalue (RE) condition on X, the Lasso achieves prediction error ||X(β̂ − β)||₂²/n = OP(s log p / n) and estimation error ||β̂ − β||₁ = OP(s√(log p / n)). The tuning parameter λ ≍ σ√(log p / n) balances bias and variance. The Lasso’s ℓ₁ penalty introduces bias on large coefficients; the smoothly clipped absolute deviation (SCAD) penalty (Fan & Li 2001) and the minimax concave penalty (MCP) are non‑convex penalties that yield nearly unbiased estimates for large signals while retaining sparsity. SCAD’s derivative is p'λ(θ) = λ for |θ| ≤ λ, (aλ − |θ|)/(a−1) for λ < |θ| ≤ aλ, and 0 for |θ| > aλ, with a = 3.7. Oracle properties hold: with probability tending to one, the SCAD estimator identifies the true support and estimates non‑zero coefficients as efficiently as if the support were known. Post‑selection inference addresses the fact that standard confidence intervals after model selection have incorrect coverage because the selection event is ignored. The selective inference framework (Lee et al. 2016) conditions on the selection event {Â = A, sign(β̂A) = s} which is a polyhedral set in Y; the distribution of a linear contrast ηᵀβ̂ given this event is truncated normal, allowing exact p‑values and confidence intervals. The debiased (or desparsified) Lasso constructs β̂d = β̂ + ΘXᵀ(Y − Xβ̂)/n where Θ approximates (XᵀX/n)⁻¹; under sparsity of Θ’s rows, √n(β̂dj − βj) converges to N(0, σ²Θjj), enabling asymptotically valid inference without conditioning. Multiple testing in high dimensions uses the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure on debiased p‑values or the knockoff filter (Barber & Candès 2015) which constructs synthetic variables to control the false discovery rate exactly in finite samples.

  • Confusing the order of Edgeworth correction terms: the n⁻¹ᐟ² term involves the third cumulant, the n⁻¹ term involves both the fourth cumulant and the square of the third cumulant.
  • Assuming the influence function of an M‑estimator is simply ψ(x) without dividing by E[ψ'(X)]; this omits the scaling that ensures Fisher consistency.
  • Applying the SPRT to composite hypotheses without adjusting the boundaries; the simple‑hypothesis optimality does not extend, and error probabilities can be severely inflated.
  • Treating a non‑Donsker class (e.g., monotone functions) as if the empirical process converges weakly to a Gaussian limit; the bootstrap will then be inconsistent.
  • Using the standard Lasso confidence intervals (β̂ ± zα/2·se) without debiasing or selective inference; the ℓ₁ penalty induces bias and selection effects that invalidate coverage.
  • Ignoring the restricted eigenvalue condition when quoting Lasso error bounds; the bounds hold only under such design assumptions, not for arbitrary correlated predictors.
  • Believing that SCAD’s oracle property holds for any tuning parameter λ; it requires λ → 0 and √n λ → ∞ at appropriate rates, otherwise selection consistency fails.

These advanced topics deepen the theoretical toolkit required for rigorous statistical inference and are frequently examined through derivations, counter‑examples, and applied decision‑making scenarios.
