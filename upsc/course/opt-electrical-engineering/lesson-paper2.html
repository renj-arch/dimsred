Electrical Engineering Paper II — Control Systems, Electrical Machines & Power Systems

Control Systems

  • Control System Components: Open loop vs closed loop control systems, feedback and feedforward control, servo mechanisms, synchros, synchro transmitters and receivers, synchro torque differential systems, synchro capacitor, synchro applications.
  • Block Diagrams & Signal Flow Graphs: Block diagram reduction rules, block diagram algebra, Mason's gain formula, signal flow graphs, Mason's gain formula, Mason's gain formula applications.
  • Time Domain Analysis: Standard test signals (step, step, ramp, parabolic, impulse), transient response of first order and second order systems, rise time, peak time, peak overshoot, settling time, steady state error, steady state error for step, ramp, parabolic inputs, static error constants (Kp, Kv, Kv).
  • Stability Analysis: Routh-Hurwitz criterion, necessary and sufficient conditions for stability, special cases (zero row in Routh array, zero row in auxiliary polynomial), relative stability, root locus (root locus construction rules, angle condition, magnitude condition, angle of departure, angle of arrival, centroid, asymptotes, breakaway points, intersection with imaginary axis), root locus applications.
  • Frequency Response Analysis: Bode plots (magnitude plot, phase plot), corner frequencies, phase margin, gain margin, gain crossover frequency, phase crossover frequency, gain margin, phase margin, Bode plot stability criteria, Nyquist plot (Nyquist stability criterion, Nyquist stability criterion, encirclements of -1 point, Nyquist criterion), Nyquist plot construction.
  • Compensators: Lead compensator, lag compensator, lag-lead compensator, PID controller, PID tuning (Ziegler-Nichols rules), cascade control, feedforward control, cascade control.
  • State Space Analysis: State space representation, state space models (controllable canonical form, observable canonical form, diagonal form, Jordan canonical form), state transition matrix, properties of state transition matrix, solution of state equation, controllability (Kalman's test), observability (duality principle), pole placement (pole placement by state feedback), Ackermann's formula.

Electrical Machines

  • DC Machines: DC machine construction (stator, rotor, armature, field poles, interpoles, compensating windings), EMF equation (E = PNZ/(60A) × φN/60), torque equation (T = (PZ/2πa) × φIa), DC generator characteristics (open circuit characteristic, external characteristics of shunt, series, compound generators), DC motor characteristics (torque-speed characteristics, speed-torque characteristics), speed control of DC motors (armature control, field control, Ward-Leonard system), DC motor starters (3-point starter, 4-point starter).
  • Transformers: Transformer construction (core type, shell type), ideal transformer equations, practical transformer (equivalent circuit, equivalent circuit parameters - R1, X1, R2', X2', R0, X0), equivalent circuit parameters determination (open circuit test, short circuit test), voltage regulation, efficiency, all day efficiency, parallel operation of transformers (conditions for parallel operation, load sharing), autotransformer (advantages, disadvantages, applications), Scott connection (3-phase to 2-phase conversion), Scott connection applications.
  • Induction Motors: 3-phase induction motor construction (stator, rotor - squirrel cage, wound rotor), rotating magnetic field, principle of operation, slip (s = (Ns - N)/Ns), rotor EMF, rotor current, rotor frequency, torque equation (T ∝ φI₂cosφ₂), maximum torque condition (R₂ = X₂), maximum torque at starting (R₂ = X₂), torque-slip characteristics, speed control methods (stator voltage control, rotor resistance control, frequency control, pole changing, cascade control), induction generator, induction generator operation (self-excited induction generator, grid-connected induction generator).
  • Synchronous Machines: Synchronous machine construction (salient pole, cylindrical pole), EMF equation (E₄ = 4.44fNΦK_w), synchronous impedance method (open circuit test, short circuit test), voltage regulation (EMF method, MMF method, ZPF method, Potier method), synchronous motor (starting methods, V-curves, inverted V-curves, power factor control), synchronous condenser, hunting (hunting oscillations, damping windings), synchronous condenser applications.
Technique: Thevenin's Theorem
Question: Find Thevenin equivalent across terminals A-B of a circuit with 12V source, 4Ω resistor in series with parallel combination of 4Ω and 8Ω resistors.
Answer: V_th = open circuit voltage across A-B = voltage across 4Ω resistor = (4/(4+4))×12 = 6V. R_th = 4Ω || 4Ω = 2Ω. Thevenin equivalent: 6V source in series with 2Ω resistor.

Memory Tricks

  • Kirchhoff's Laws: KCL - algebraic sum of currents at node = 0 (charge conservation); KVL - algebraic sum of voltages around loop = 0 (energy conservation).
  • Thevenin's Theorem: V_th = V_oc (open circuit voltage), R_th = V_oc/I_sc (short circuit current), R_th = V_oc/I_sc.
  • Maximum Power Transfer: Maximum power transferred when load resistance equals Thevenin resistance (R_L = R_th), maximum power P_max = V_th²/(4R_th).
  • Resonance: Series resonance - impedance minimum, current maximum, power factor = 1; Parallel resonance - impedance maximum, current minimum, power factor = 1.
Mains strategy
Electrical Engineering answers: draw circuit diagrams, show step-by-step derivation of circuit equations, state assumptions clearly, quote IS codes (IS 456 for RCC, IS 800 for steel, IS 1893 for seismic design, IS 10262 for mix design), quote standard values (electrical resistivity of copper 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m, copper density 8.96 g/cm³, electrical resistivity of aluminium 2.83×10⁻⁸ Ω·m, aluminium density 2.7 g/cm³).

Power Electronics: The DC Chopper Circuit Family

The study of power electronics in Paper II shifts focus from linear control (amplifiers, analog circuits) to switching devices that convert energy at high frequencies. The core syllabus area here is the DC Chopper, which acts as a variable DC voltage source. Unlike a transformer which changes AC voltage levels, a chopper chops a fixed DC input into a varying average output using semiconductor switches (BJT, MOSFET, IGBT). The fundamental operation relies on the concept of duty cycle (δ = ton/T), where T is the switching period.

The standard reference for this domain is Bernard K. Bose, Power Electronics, which details the topologies extensively. A student must master four primary chopper configurations: Step-down (Buck), Step-up (Boost), Step-up-Step-down (Buck-Boost), and Cuk converters. Each has distinct voltage transfer ratios and polarity characteristics. For instance, in a Buck converter, the output voltage V_o is given by V_o = δ V_s, where V_s is the source voltage. Crucially, the load current must be continuous for these simple topologies to function as intended; if the load is highly resistive and frequency low, discontinuous conduction mode (DCM) occurs, altering the transfer ratio.

A critical mechanism often tested is the flyback diode (or freewheeling diode). When the switch opens, the inductive load tries to maintain current flow. The voltage across the inductor reverses polarity, forward-biasing the diode. Current then circulates through the load and diode, bypassing the switch. This prevents high-voltage spikes that would destroy the switch (arcing). In modern designs using IGBTs or MOSFETs, this diode is often an intrinsic body diode or a fast recovery Schottky diode to minimize switching losses.

The Inverter section complements this by converting DC to AC. The basic single-phase full-bridge inverter uses four switches (typically IGBTs) arranged in legs. By controlling the firing angles, one can generate square waves, quasi-square waves, or stepped waveforms. The harmonic content of these waveforms is a key analysis topic. A pure sine wave requires complex PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) control. The Stevedore's Inverter or specific industrial inverters often use phase-angle control to regulate output voltage by varying the conduction angle of thyristors, though this introduces significant harmonics.

Another advanced topic is the Cycloconverter, which converts fixed-frequency AC to variable-frequency AC directly without an intermediate DC link. This is used in large motor drives (like rolling mills). The control logic here involves overlapping firing sequences of thyristors to synthesize a lower frequency output from a higher frequency input. Efficiency drops significantly due to the lack of regenerative capability and high harmonic distortion, limiting its use to low-speed, high-torque applications.

Examiner's trap
Question: In a step-down chopper with continuous load current, if the duty cycle is 0.5 and input voltage is 100V, what is the output voltage? Answer: 50V. Trap: Students often forget that in discontinuous conduction mode (DCM), V_o depends on the load resistance and switching frequency, not just duty cycle. Another trap: Confusing the polarity of Buck-Boost (output opposite to input) with Cuk or SEPIC (output same polarity). Always check the diode orientation in the schematic.
Exam-day strategy
When solving numerical problems on choppers, always state your assumption regarding continuous vs. discontinuous conduction mode first. If the problem does not specify, assume Continuous Conduction Mode (CCM) for standard textbook answers, but mention DCM if the load is purely resistive and frequency is low. Always draw the voltage waveform across the switch and the diode to justify your calculation of average voltage.

Communication Systems: Modulation Indices and Bandwidth

The communication systems syllabus focuses on the transmission of information over a channel. The core concept is Modulation: varying a carrier parameter (amplitude, frequency, or phase) in accordance with the message signal. Amplitude Modulation (AM) varies the envelope amplitude; Frequency Modulation (FM) varies instantaneous frequency; Phase Modulation (PM) varies instantaneous phase.

A critical distinction lies in Analog vs. Digital modulation. Analog systems (AM, FM, PM) are susceptible to noise accumulation. Digital systems (ASK, FSK, PSK, QAM) offer better noise immunity but require higher bandwidth and complex coding. The Bandwidth requirement is a frequent calculation point. For AM, BW = 2f_m (twice the highest modulating frequency). For FM, Carson's rule applies: BW ≈ 2(Δ f + f_m), where Δ f is the peak frequency deviation. This explains why FM radio occupies more spectrum than AM but offers superior audio quality and noise rejection.

The Noise aspect is vital. Thermal noise (Johnson-Nyquist noise) is inherent in all resistive components. Shot noise arises from discrete charge carriers. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) determines the fidelity of transmission. In FM, the "capture effect" and "threshold effect" are unique phenomena where a stronger signal suppresses a weaker one on the same frequency, or where SNR degrades rapidly below a certain threshold level.

Digital Communication introduces concepts like Error Control Coding (Hamming codes, Reed-Solomon) and Synchronization. Without synchronization between transmitter and receiver, data bits cannot be aligned. Techniques like Manchester encoding ensure self-clocking in the signal. The Shannon-Hartley theorem (C = B log2(1 + S/N)) defines the theoretical maximum channel capacity. Students must understand that increasing bandwidth or SNR increases capacity, but practical constraints (filtering, non-ideal components) limit real-world performance.

The Satellite Communication section involves uplink/downlink frequencies, polarization (linear vs. circular), and path loss calculations involving free-space attenuation and atmospheric absorption. The concept of FDMA, TDMA, CDMA multiple access techniques is essential for understanding cellular networks. FDMA divides frequency spectrum; TDMA divides time slots; CDMA uses spreading codes to share the same frequency band simultaneously.

Examiner's trap
Question: If a carrier of 10 MHz is modulated by a 5 kHz sine wave, calculate bandwidth. Answer: 10 kHz (2 × 5 kHz). Trap: Students often add the carrier frequency to the bandwidth or confuse f_m with the carrier frequency. Another trap: In FM, calculating deviation ratio (β = Δ f / f_m) and forgetting that modulation index affects sideband amplitude distribution (Bessel functions), not just bandwidth.
Exam-day strategy
In communication questions, always define your variables (f_c, f_m, Δ f) clearly before substituting values. If asked about noise figure, recall that it is defined as the ratio of input SNR to output SNR (F = (SNR)in / (SNR)out). Remember that a lower noise figure is better.
