Constitutional Law of India

Constitutional Framework

  • Constitution of India: Adopted 26 Nov 1949, enforced 26 Jan 1950. Originally 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules; now over 450 Articles in 25 Parts and 12 Schedules.
  • Preamble: Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic; Justice (social, economic, political), Liberty (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), Equality (status and opportunity), Fraternity (assuring dignity of individual and unity and integrity of Nation).
  • Citizenship: Articles 5-11; Citizenship Act 1955; acquisition by birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory; termination by renunciation, termination, deprivation; CAA 2019 - accelerated citizenship for persecuted minorities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Pakistan.
  • Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12-35): Right to Equality (14-18), Right to Freedom (19-22), Right against Exploitation (23-24), Right to Freedom of Religion (25-28), Cultural and Educational Rights (29-30), Right to Constitutional Remedies (32). Right to Property deleted (44th Amendment 1978, Art 300A - legal right).
  • Directive Principles (Part IV, Articles 36-51): Non-justiciable; social, economic, political welfare; Uniform Civil Code (44), separation of judiciary from executive (50), organisation of village panchayats (40), promotion of educational and economic interests of SC/ST/weaker sections (46).
  • Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A): Added by 42nd Amendment 1976; 11 duties (originally 10, 11th added by 86th Amendment 2002 - parent/guardian to provide education to child 6-14 years).
  • Basic Structure Doctrine: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) - Parliament can amend any part of Constitution but cannot destroy/alter basic structure. Basic features: supremacy of Constitution, republican democratic republic, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, judicial review, freedom and dignity of individual, unity and integrity of nation.

Union Executive

  • President (Articles 52-62): Indirectly elected by electoral college (MPs + MLAs); 5-year term; re-election eligible; qualifications (citizen, 35+ years, qualified for Lok Sabha); qualifications, conditions (no office of profit), oath, impeachment (Article 61 - violation of Constitution).
  • Vice-President (Articles 63-71): Indirectly elected by electoral college (both Houses); ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha; 5-year term; impeachment (Rajya Sabha resolution passed by majority, agreed by Lok Sabha).
  • Prime Minister & Council of Ministers (Articles 74-75): PM appointed by President (leader of majority/coalition in Lok Sabha); other ministers appointed by President on advice of PM; collectively responsible to Lok Sabha (Art 75); individual responsibility; oath; salaries/allowances determined by Parliament.
  • Attorney General (Article 76): Appointed by President; must be qualified to be Supreme Court judge; holds office during pleasure of President; right to audience in all courts; right to speak in Parliament (but no voting); not a member of Cabinet.

Parliament

  • Parliament (Articles 79-122): Bicameral - President + Rajya Sabha (Council of States) + Lok Sabha (House of the People).
  • Rajya Sabha (245 members): 238 elected by State/UT Legislative Assemblies (proportional representation by single transferable vote), 12 nominated by President (special knowledge/practical experience in literature, science, art, social service). Permanent house - 1/3 retires every 2 years; 6-year term. Vice-President is ex-officio Chairman; Deputy Chairman elected from members.
  • Lok Sabha (545 members): 543 elected by direct election (single member constituencies), 2 nominated by President (Anglo-Indian - abolished by 104th Amendment 2019). 5-year term (can be dissolved earlier); Speaker (elected by Lok Sabha) presides; Deputy Speaker elected from members.
  • Qualifications for MP: Citizen of India; age 25 (LS) / 30 (RS); registered voter; not holding office of profit; not disqualified under 10th Schedule.
  • Disqualification (10th Schedule): Voluntarily giving up membership, voting against party whip, joining another party after election, independently elected member joining party, nominated member joining party after 6 months.
Technique: Article 32 - Right to Constitutional Remedies
Question: 'Article 32 is the heart and soul of the Constitution' - Dr. B.R. Ambedkar. Explain.
Answer: Article 32 provides right to approach Supreme Court directly for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. It makes Fundamental Rights justiciable/real. Supreme Court can issue writs: Habeas Corpus (produce person), Mandamus (command public official), Prohibition (stop lower court), Certiorari (transfer case), Quo Warranto (by what authority - challenge to public office). Without Art 32, Fundamental Rights would be mere declarations without enforcement mechanism.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Fundamental Rights (justiciable) with Directive Principles (non-justiciable).
  • Thinking President has executive power - President acts on aid & advice of Council of Ministers (Art 74).
  • Missing that Fundamental Duties are not legally enforceable (no penalty for violation).
  • Confusing Article 32 (Supreme Court) with Article 226 (High Court) - both issue writs, but SC jurisdiction is wider for FR enforcement while HC can enforce FR + other legal rights.
  • Overlooking that Emergency provisions (Articles 352, 356, 360) alter normal federal structure and can suspend Fundamental Rights (except Articles 20 & 21).

  • Emergency Types: Article 352 (National Emergency), 356 (President's Rule), 360 (Financial Emergency).
  • Writs: Habeas Corpus (produce person), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop lower court), Certiorari (transfer case), Quo Warranto (challenge to public office).

Basic Structure Doctrine: The Unamendable Core

In the realm of Indian Constitutional Law, no concept is more pivotal to judicial review than the Basic Structure Doctrine. While Article 368 grants Parliament the power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, it does not explicitly state that this power is unlimited. The Supreme Court, in a landmark shift from the *Berubari Union* case (1960) where it held that amendment power was plenary and unrestricted, evolved the doctrine that Parliament cannot alter the "basic structure" or essential features of the Constitution. This distinction separates mere legislative amendments from constitutional subversion.

The genesis of this doctrine lies in the Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) case. In a historic 7-6 majority judgment, Chief Justice H.R. Khanna articulated that while Parliament has wide powers of amendment, it cannot destroy or change the basic structure. The Court listed features such as secularism, democracy, rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review, and the unity and integrity of India as part of this inviolable core. Subsequently, in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Court struck down Part IV (Directive Principles) to the extent it conflicted with Part III (Fundamental Rights), reinforcing that there must be a "harmony" between them and that judicial review is itself part of the basic structure.

Examiner's trap
A frequent error is claiming that the Supreme Court can strike down any law passed by Parliament. Correction: The Court cannot strike down a law if it falls within the legislative competence and does not violate the basic structure. However, under I.R. Coelho, laws added to the Ninth Schedule after 1973 are NOT immune from judicial review regarding basic structure violations.
Exam Strategy: The "What If" Approach
When answering questions on Basic Structure, do not just list features. Instead, use a hypothetical scenario: "If Parliament were to abolish the office of the President, would it violate the basic structure? Yes, because the separation of powers and federalism are core features." This demonstrates applied understanding rather than rote memorization.

Article 32 vs Article 226: Jurisdictional Hierarchy

While both Article 32 (Right to Constitutional Remedies) and Article 226 (Powers of High Courts) empower courts to issue writs, their jurisdictional scope and nature differ significantly. Article 32 is the "heart and soul" of the Constitution, as Dr. B.R. Ambedkar noted. The Supreme Court's power under Article 32 is original and inherent; it can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights regardless of whether a corresponding remedy exists in ordinary law. Conversely, Article 226 grants High Courts wider powers: they can issue writs not only for Fundamental Rights but also for any "legal right" or to correct errors of jurisdiction by lower courts or tribunals.

A critical distinction lies in the scope of remedies. Under Article 32, the Supreme Court can grant relief even if no specific provision exists in the Constitution for that particular violation (though usually linked to FRs). High Courts under Article 226 are not limited by this and can intervene in administrative actions violating natural justice or statutory duties. However, once a matter falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 32, High Courts generally cannot interfere. The Supreme Court has held that it is not necessary to exhaust remedies under Article 226 before approaching the Supreme Court under Article 32, though in practice, High Courts are often approached first as they are geographically closer and have wider jurisdiction.

The Raj Narain v. State of UP (1975) case clarified that while Article 32 is the primary remedy for FR violations, Article 226 acts as a supplementary mechanism. Furthermore, during a National Emergency under Article 352, the Supreme Court's power under Article 32 remains intact, whereas High Courts' writ jurisdiction under Article 226 can be suspended if the President issues an order suspending the operation of Articles 19 and 226 (though this is rarely done). This hierarchy ensures that while local courts handle day-to-day grievances, the apex court stands ready to protect the constitutional conscience.

The Ninth Schedule: Immunity or Shield?

The Ninth Schedule was inserted into the Constitution by the First Amendment Act of 1951 specifically to protect land reform laws from judicial scrutiny. Its primary purpose was to prevent courts from striking down state legislation aimed at abolishing zamindari systems, which were often challenged on grounds of violating Fundamental Rights (like equality). For decades, it acted as a sanctuary for legislative enactments.

However, the immunity provided by the Ninth Schedule is not absolute. The Supreme Court ruled in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 1973 are subject to judicial review if they violate the basic structure of the Constitution. This judgment effectively neutralized the "immunity" myth, aligning with the Basic Structure Doctrine established in Kesavananda Bharati. The Court held that Parliament cannot place a law in the Ninth Schedule to shield it from scrutiny regarding fundamental features like secularism or equality.

This shift was crucial because earlier, during the Emergency era (1975-77), several laws were added to the Ninth Schedule to bypass judicial review. The Coelho judgment restored the balance between legislative power and judicial oversight. It is important to note that laws added before 1973 remain in the Ninth Schedule, but their status is not explicitly protected against basic structure challenges in the same way post-1973 entries are scrutinized. The practical effect is that no law, regardless of its location in the Constitution, can violate the basic structure.

Unlike the USA or UK with strict separations, India's model allows for checks and balances that prevent any single organ from becoming dominant. The judiciary has played a pivotal role in defining these boundaries.

Doctrine of Eclipse and Severability in Fundamental Rights Adjudication

The doctrine of eclipse operates on the premise that a pre‑Constitutional law which conflicts with a Fundamental Right does not become void ab initio; rather, it is rendered dormant — eclipsed — for the duration of the inconsistency. When the constitutional impediment is removed, either by amendment or by judicial interpretation narrowing the right, the law revives automatically. The Supreme Court first articulated this in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), holding that a law violating Article 19(1)(g) was merely eclipsed and would become operative again if the restriction were lifted. The doctrine applies only to laws enacted before the commencement of the Constitution; post‑Constitutional laws that contravene Fundamental Rights are void under Article 13(2) and cannot be revived by subsequent amendment unless the amendment itself validates them.

Severability, by contrast, permits a court to excise the offending portion of a statute while preserving the remainder, provided the valid part is capable of standing independently and the legislature would have enacted it without the invalid portion. The test was laid down in R.M.D.C. (Mysore) Ltd. v. Union of India (1957): the court must examine whether the unconstitutional provision is so intertwined with the rest of the Act that the legislative intent would be defeated if it were removed. In State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills Ltd. (1974), the Court severed the unconstitutional section of the Bombay Labour Welfare Fund Act, upholding the remaining provisions. A practical distinction for answer‑writing: eclipse concerns the temporal operation of an entire pre‑Constitutional law, whereas severability is a surgical tool applied to any statute, pre‑ or post‑Constitutional, to save its constitutional core.

Examiners often blend the two concepts. A typical twist presents a pre‑Constitutional statute partially inconsistent with a Fundamental Right and asks whether the whole law is eclipsed or only the offending part can be severed. The correct approach: first apply eclipse to the entire statute; if the inconsistency is confined to a severable segment, the court may sever that segment and allow the rest to operate, but the eclipse doctrine does not permit partial revival — either the whole law revives when the constitutional bar disappears, or the invalid part remains void while the valid part continues under severability. Remember that the 24th Amendment (1971) and the 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review of amendments, but the Basic Structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) restored the court’s power to examine whether an amendment itself violates the core, thereby indirectly affecting eclipse and severability analyses.

Judicial Review of Administrative Action: Wednesbury Unreasonableness, Proportionality, and Legitimate Expectation

Administrative law in India has evolved three principal standards of review. The Wednesbury unreasonableness test, borrowed from Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd. v. Wednesbury Corporation (1948), asks whether a decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have reached it. The Indian Supreme Court adopted this in State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2000) and reiterated it in Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000), emphasising that the court does not substitute its own judgment but only intervenes where the decision is perverse, irrational, or based on irrelevant considerations.

The proportionality standard, more rigorous, requires that the administrative measure be suitable, necessary, and balanced in relation to the objective pursued. The Court imported this from European jurisprudence in Union of India v. G. Ganayutham (1997) and refined it in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) (the privacy judgment), holding that any restriction on a Fundamental Right must satisfy the four‑fold test: legitimate aim, rational connection, necessity (least restrictive means), and proportionality stricto sensu. Proportionality now governs review of restrictions on Articles 19 and 21, displacing Wednesbury in rights‑based challenges.

Legitimate expectation arises when a public authority, by representation or consistent practice, creates an expectation in a person that a certain benefit or procedure will be afforded. The doctrine was recognised in Navjot Singh v. State of Punjab (1994) and expanded in Food Corporation of India v. M/s. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries (1993). It does not confer a substantive right but entitles the individual to procedural fairness — a hearing before the expectation is frustrated. The Court balances the expectation against public interest; a mere hope or desire does not suffice.

In practice, the hierarchy of review is: (i) illegality (acting beyond powers), (ii) procedural impropriety (breach of natural justice), (iii) irrationality (Wednesbury), and (iv) disproportionality (where Fundamental Rights are engaged). A decision may be struck down on any one ground. For answer‑writing, identify the nature of the challenge — statutory interpretation, procedural fairness, or rights restriction — and then select the appropriate standard. Cite the leading case that matches the standard to demonstrate doctrinal precision.

Issue‑Spotter: Selecting the Standard of Review
A municipal corporation issues an order cancelling a street‑vendor’s licence without any prior notice or hearing. The vendor challenges the order in the High Court under Article 226. Which standard(s) of review will the court apply, and what must the vendor demonstrate to succeed?
The court will primarily examine procedural impropriety — breach of the audi alteram partem rule — because the order was passed without hearing the affected party. This is a standalone ground; the vendor need not prove unreasonableness. If the corporation argues that the cancellation was based on policy, the court may also assess Wednesbury unreasonableness to see if the policy itself is irrational. Since a licence is a statutory entitlement, not a Fundamental Right, proportionality does not apply unless the vendor can link the cancellation to a violation of Article 19(1)(g). The vendor must show (a) a legitimate expectation of a hearing based on past practice or statutory requirement, and (b) that the absence of a hearing renders the order procedurally flawed. The remedy is usually quashing the order and directing a fresh decision after a fair hearing.
Exam‑Day Strategy: Map Review Standards to Fact Patterns
Create a mental matrix: (1) No hearing → procedural impropriety / legitimate expectation. (2) Decision based on irrelevant factors → Wednesbury. (3) Restriction on speech, movement, privacy → proportionality. (4) Ultra vires statute → illegality. Treaty‑Making Power and Domestic Implementation under Article 253

Article 253 empowers Parliament to make any law for the whole or any part of the territory of India for implementing any treaty, agreement, or convention with any other country or any decision made at any international conference, association, or other body. This provision bridges the dualist tradition — where international obligations do not automatically become part of domestic law — by giving Parliament the legislative competence to transform treaty obligations into enforceable municipal statutes. The executive alone cannot create domestic legal effects; a parliamentary enactment is indispensable unless the treaty is self‑executing, a concept the Indian judiciary has treated cautiously.

In Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India (1969), the Supreme Court held that the Indo‑Pak Agreement on the Rann of Kutch required parliamentary legislation under Article 253 before it could be enforced in Indian courts. The Court clarified that Article 253 confers exclusive legislative power on Parliament, overriding the distribution of legislative subjects in the Seventh Schedule. Consequently, even if a treaty touches a State List subject (e.g., public health), Parliament may legislate to implement it without requiring State consent.

However, the Court in Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980) recognised that certain treaty provisions, particularly those reflecting customary international law or jus cogens norms, may be directly applicable if they are of a self‑executing nature and do not require further legislative action. The distinction hinges on the treaty’s language: obligations phrased as “shall enact legislation” are non‑self‑executing; those stating “shall ensure” or “shall guarantee” may be self‑executing. In practice, Indian courts presume non‑self‑execution unless the treaty text and the legislative history indicate otherwise.

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969), though not ratified by India, is regarded as reflective of customary law. Article 26 (pacta sunt servanda) and Article 27 (internal law cannot justify failure to perform a treaty) reinforce the duty to implement. The Supreme Court in Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) used the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) — ratified but not legislated — to fill a legislative vacuum, treating the treaty as an interpretive aid for Fundamental Rights under Articles 14, 19, and 21. This demonstrates that even without an Article 253 enactment, ratified treaties can influence judicial reasoning, though they do not create directly enforceable rights.

For answer‑writing, distinguish three scenarios: (i) treaty requires implementing legislation → Parliament must act under Article 253; (ii) treaty is self‑executing → courts may apply it directly; (iii) treaty used as interpretive tool → courts may read domestic law consistently with the treaty. Cite Maganbhai for the legislative requirement, Jolly George for implementing legislation, and Vishaka for interpretive use.

State Responsibility and Diplomatic Protection in International Law

The law of state responsibility is codified in the International Law Commission’s Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA) (2001), which reflect customary international law. An internationally wrongful act requires two elements: (a) conduct attributable to the State under international law, and (b) a breach of an international obligation of the State. Attribution covers acts of State organs (Article 4), persons exercising governmental authority (Article 5), and conduct directed or controlled by the State (Article 8). The breach may arise from treaty obligations, customary law, or general principles.

Once responsibility is established, the responsible State is under an obligation to cease the wrongful act (Article 30) and to make full reparation (Article 31). Reparation takes three forms: restitution (re‑establishing the situation before the breach), compensation (monetary payment for financially assessable damage), and satisfaction (acknowledgement, apology, or other non‑monetary remedies for moral injury). The Chorzów Factory case (PCIJ, 1928) established the principle that reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all consequences of the illegal act.

Diplomatic protection is the means by which a State espouses the claim of its national against another State for an injury caused by an internationally wrongful act. The classic conditions, articulated in the Mavrommatis Palestine Concessions case (PCIJ, 1924) and reaffirmed in Barcelona Traction (ICJ, 1970), are: (i) the claimant must be a national of the protecting State at the time of the injury and continuously thereafter; (ii) the national must have exhausted local remedies in the respondent State, unless remedies are ineffective or futile; (iii) the claim must not be barred by the nationality of the claimant being the same as the respondent State (the “nationality of claims” rule). The Nottebohm case (ICJ, 1955) added the “genuine link” requirement for naturalised persons, though its scope remains contested.

In contemporary practice, diplomatic protection is increasingly supplemented by investor‑State arbitration under bilateral investment treaties (BITs) and the ICSID Convention, allowing nationals direct access to international tribunals. Nevertheless, the traditional route remains relevant for human rights violations, environmental damage, and denial of justice where no BIT applies. The ILC’s Draft Articles on Diplomatic Protection (2006) codify the customary rules and emphasise the discretionary nature of the protecting State’s decision to exercise protection.

For examination purposes, remember the sequence: attribution → breach → cessation and reparation → diplomatic protection (nationality, exhaustion of local remedies, genuine link). Cite ARSIWA articles, Chorzów Factory for reparation, Barcelona Traction for the nationality rule, and Nottebohm for the genuine link. Distinguish between the State’s primary responsibility and the secondary procedural step of diplomatic protection.

Emergency Provisions: Article 352, 356, and 360 – Scope, Judicial Review, and Safeguards

The Constitution envisages three distinct emergencies. Article 352 (National Emergency) may be proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) tightened the procedure: the proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority, and it can continue only with periodic parliamentary approval every six months. Crucially, Article 21 cannot be suspended during an emergency; Article 19 is automatically suspended only for war or external aggression. The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) held that the power to proclaim emergency is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fides or irrelevance.

Article 356 (President’s Rule) allows the President to assume the functions of a State government on receipt of a report from the Governor or otherwise, if satisfied that the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months and can last up to three years with periodic approvals. The landmark S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down stringent guidelines: the President’s satisfaction is justiciable; the proclamation cannot be used for political purposes; the State Legislative Assembly cannot be dissolved before parliamentary approval; and secularism, federalism, and democracy are basic features that cannot be undermined. The Court also mandated a floor test as the preferred method to ascertain majority.

Article 360 (Financial Emergency) empowers the President to declare a financial emergency if the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. It has never been invoked. During such an emergency, the Union can direct States to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the State, and require money bills to be reserved for the President’s consideration. The 44th Amendment removed the provision allowing the President to suspend the enforcement of Fundamental Rights during a financial emergency, reinforcing the principle that economic crises do not justify rights suspension.

Judicial review of all three emergencies focuses on the existence of the factual basis, the procedural compliance (parliamentary approval, special majority), and the proportionality of the measures taken. The Court in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) affirmed that the President’s subjective satisfaction is not immune from scrutiny. The 44th Amendment’s safeguards — mandatory parliamentary approval, time limits, and the non‑suspendability of Article 21 — collectively ensure that emergency powers remain exceptional and accountable.

In answer‑writing, structure the response by emergency type, cite the constitutional text, the 44th Amendment changes, and the controlling judgments (Minerva Mills for Article 352, S.R. Bommai for Article 356, and the constitutional text for Article 360). Highlight the distinction: Article 352 affects the entire Union, Article 356 targets a specific State, and Article 360 addresses financial stability. Emphasise that judicial review is available for all three, and that the basic structure doctrine limits the amplitude of emergency powers.

Doctrine of Colourable Legislation and Legislative Competence

The doctrine of colourable legislation addresses the situation where a legislature, lacking the constitutional competence to enact a law on a particular subject, attempts to achieve the same result indirectly by framing the legislation under a different entry. The Supreme Court in K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953) held that the court must look at the true nature and character of the legislation, not merely its form or label. If the substance of the law falls within a forbidden field, the law is void even though it is clothed in a permissible entry. The test is whether the legislature has, in reality, transgressed its constitutional limits by a colourable exercise of power. The Court examines the pith and substance of the enactment, the legislative history, and the practical effect of the provisions. In State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1959) the Court reiterated that the doctrine does not permit the court to question the motives of the legislature; it only scrutinises the competence. The doctrine operates alongside the principle of federal distribution of powers under the Seventh Schedule. A law enacted under the Union List cannot be saved by invoking a State List entry if its dominant purpose is to regulate a Union subject. Conversely, a State law that in substance regulates a Union subject is colourable. The doctrine also interacts with the doctrine of repugnancy under Article 254: a State law that is colourable may be struck down as repugnant to a central law even if the central law is later repealed, because the constitutional incompetence remains. In practice, the Court applies a two‑step analysis: first, identify the legislative field by the pith and substance; second, verify whether the legislature possesses the constitutional entry to legislate in that field. If the answer is negative, the law is declared ultra vires as colourable legislation.

Judicial Review of Delegated Legislation: Ultra Vires and the Principle of Legitimate Expectation

Delegated legislation, also known as subordinate legislation, derives its validity from the parent statute that confers rule‑making power on the executive. The Supreme Court in Hamidullah v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1972) established that delegated legislation must conform to the enabling statute in three respects: (i) it must not exceed the scope of the delegated power (substantive ultra vires); (ii) it must not be made in a manner not authorised by the statute (procedural ultra vires); and (iii) it must not be arbitrary, unreasonable, or discriminatory (the Wednesbury principle applied to rule‑making). The doctrine of legitimate expectation, originally developed in Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for the Civil Service (1985) and adopted in Indian jurisprudence in Navjot Singh v. Union of India (1995), protects a person who has been led to believe, by a consistent administrative practice or an express representation, that a particular benefit or procedure will continue. When a rule‑making authority alters a scheme without affording the affected party an opportunity to be heard, the courts may quash the amendment on the ground that it frustrates a legitimate expectation. However, the expectation must be reasonable, founded on a clear and unambiguous representation, and not contrary to law or public policy. In Union of India v. T. R. Verma (1999) the Court held that a mere hope or desire does not constitute a legitimate expectation. The principle operates as a procedural safeguard: the authority must either consult the affected persons or provide a reasoned decision for departing from the established practice. The interplay between ultra vires and legitimate expectation ensures that delegated legislation remains within the constitutional framework of accountability and fairness. Courts also examine whether the parent statute provides adequate guidance (the “policy and standards” test) to prevent excessive delegation, as emphasised in R. v. Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex parte Fire Brigades Union (1995) and followed in Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985).

Issue‑spotting in colourable legislation
A State enacts a law titled “Regulation of Inter‑State Trade in Agricultural Produce” under Entry 33 of the State List (trade and commerce within the State). The law imposes licensing requirements on traders who move agricultural produce across State borders. The Union challenges the law as colourable legislation. Identify the constitutional issue and the likely judicial outcome.
The pith and substance of the law is the regulation of inter‑State trade, which falls under Entry 41 of the Union List (inter‑State trade and commerce). Although the State invokes Entry 33, the dominant purpose is to control movement across State boundaries, a Union competence. Applying the test from K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo, the Court will hold the law colourable and therefore ultra vires the State legislature. The law is void irrespective of its label.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks you to distinguish between substantive and procedural ultra vires, first cite the enabling provision, then map each challenged rule to the three‑fold test (scope, procedure, reasonableness). For legitimate expectation, note the three prerequisites: clear representation, reliance, and reasonableness. Use a two‑column table in the answer to contrast the two doctrines; examiners reward structured comparison.

Customary International Law: Formation, Evidence, and the Persistent Objector Rule

Customary international law arises from a general and consistent practice of States accompanied by opinio juris — the belief that the practice is legally obligatory. The International Court of Justice in the North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969) articulated the two‑element test: (i) widespread and representative State practice, and (ii) acceptance of that practice as law. Practice need not be universal; it must be sufficiently extensive and virtually uniform. Evidence of practice includes diplomatic correspondence, official statements, treaties reflecting customary norms, resolutions of international organisations, and judicial decisions. The Lotus case (1927) demonstrated that silence or inaction may constitute practice when a State refrains from protesting a known practice. The persistent objector rule permits a State that has consistently objected to a nascent customary rule during its formation to be exempt from that rule. In Anglo‑Norwegian Fisheries (1951) the Court recognised that Norway’s persistent objection to the ten‑mile rule excluded it from the customary regime. However, the objection must be clear, consistent, and maintained from the inception of the practice; a State cannot become a persistent objector after the rule has crystallised. The rule does not apply to peremptory norms (jus cogens), from which no derogation is permitted. The Nicaragua v. United States (1986) judgment confirmed that the prohibition of the use of force is a jus cogens norm, binding on all States regardless of objection. In the Indian context, the Supreme Court in Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) treated the precautionary principle as part of customary international environmental law, illustrating domestic courts’ willingness to incorporate customary norms. The formation of custom is dynamic; new practices such as cyber operations are currently being assessed for customary status. Scholars emphasise the role of the International Law Commission’s Draft Conclusions on Identification of Customary International Law (2018) as a systematic restatement of the methodology.

Principle of Natural Justice in Administrative Proceedings: Audi Alteram Partem and the Rule Against Bias

The principles of natural justice constitute the procedural backbone of administrative law. The first principle, audi alteram partem (hear the other side), requires that no person be condemned unheard. The Supreme Court in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded the right to a fair hearing to include the right to know the case against oneself, the right to present evidence, and the right to cross‑examine witnesses where credibility is in issue. The Court held that the content of the hearing must be proportionate to the gravity of the decision. In Union of India v. T. R. Verma (1999) the Court clarified that a post‑decisional hearing may satisfy the requirement only when a pre‑decisional hearing is impracticable, and even then the affected party must be given a meaningful opportunity to rebut the findings. The second principle, the rule against bias (nemo judex in causa sua), disqualifies a decision‑maker who has a personal, pecuniary, or institutional interest in the outcome. The test is whether a reasonable, informed observer would apprehend a real likelihood of bias. In R. v. Gough (1993) the House of Lords adopted the “real likelihood” test, later refined in Porter v. Magill (2001) to the “appearance of bias” standard. Indian courts follow the “reasonable apprehension” test, as seen in K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1995). Institutional bias arises when the same authority performs investigative, prosecutorial, and adjudicative functions; the Court in State of Orissa v. Dr. (Miss) Binapani Dei (1967) held that such fusion violates natural justice unless the statute provides adequate safeguards. The principles are not absolute; they may be excluded by express statutory provision, by the nature of the function (e.g., legislative or policy‑making), or by urgent necessity. However, any exclusion must be strictly construed. The doctrine of legitimate expectation, discussed earlier, operates as an extension of audi alteram partem when an established practice creates an expectation of consultation.

Harmonious Construction and the Interpretation of Fundamental Rights

When two or more constitutional provisions appear to conflict, the doctrine of harmonious construction directs courts to interpret them in a manner that gives effect to both, avoiding a declaration of repugnancy unless absolutely necessary. The Supreme Court in State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951) held that the Constitution must be read as a whole and that provisions should be construed so as to preserve the maximum possible scope for each. In the context of Fundamental Rights, the Court applies harmonious construction to reconcile rights inter se and to balance rights against Directive Principles. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) the Court used harmonious construction to hold that the amending power under Article 368 cannot abrogate the basic structure, thereby reconciling the wide language of Article 368 with the implied limitations of the Preamble and the fundamental rights chapter. The doctrine also governs the relationship between Articles 19 and 21. In Maneka Gandhi the Court read Article 21 as incorporating the procedural safeguards of Article 19, ensuring that a law depriving personal liberty must also satisfy the reasonableness test of Article 19. Similarly, in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) the Court harmonised the directive to promote social welfare (Article 39) with the fundamental right to property (then Article 31) by holding that the basic structure includes the balance between Parts III and IV. The technique involves three steps: (i) identify the literal meaning of each provision; (ii) ascertain the legislative intent and constitutional scheme; (iii) adopt an interpretation that avoids conflict and advances the constitutional purpose. Where harmonious construction fails, the doctrine of implied repeal or the principle of federal supremacy (Article 254) may be invoked. The approach underscores the Constitution’s organic character and prevents a fragmented reading that would undermine the coherence of the fundamental rights regime.

  • Confusing colourable legislation with a mere misuse of legislative entry; the doctrine requires a substantive transgression of competence, not just an inconvenient label.
  • Assuming that any delegation of legislative power is automatically ultra vires; the parent statute must lack policy and standards for the delegation to be invalid.
  • Treating legitimate expectation as a substantive right; it is a procedural safeguard that can be overridden by a clear statutory exclusion or overriding public interest.
  • Believing that a State can become a persistent objector after a customary rule has crystallised; the objection must be continuous from the rule’s inception.
  • Applying the “real likelihood” bias test from English law without adapting it to the Indian “reasonable apprehension” standard used by the Supreme Court.
  • Ignoring that harmonious construction is a primary interpretive tool before resorting to implied repeal or federal supremacy; courts must first attempt to give effect to both provisions.
  • Overlooking that the basic structure doctrine limits not only constitutional amendments but also the scope of emergency powers and delegated legislation, ensuring constitutional coherence.

Doctrine of Pith and Substance: Determining Legislative Competence in a Federal Scheme

The doctrine of pith and substance is the principal tool used by Indian courts to ascertain whether a legislature has acted within its allotted field when a law appears to encroach on another legislature’s domain. The test requires the court to identify the true nature and character of the legislation — its “pith and substance” — by examining the statute as a whole, its object, scope, and the effect of its provisions. If the dominant purpose falls squarely within the legislative competence of the enacting legislature, incidental trenching on a subject assigned to the other legislature does not render the law ultra vires. The Supreme Court in State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) held that the Constitution distributes legislative powers on the basis of the dominant character of the law, not on the basis of incidental effects. The same principle was reiterated in Union of India v. Shah Goverdhan L. Kabra Teachers’ College (1965), where a state law on agricultural income tax was upheld despite its impact on central tax legislation because its pith and substance was taxation on agricultural income, a state subject under Entry 46 of List II.

The analysis proceeds in three stages. First, the court extracts the “true nature” of the enactment by reading the preamble, the statement of objects and reasons, and the operative provisions. Second, it classifies that nature under the appropriate entry in the Seventh Schedule. Third, it checks whether the incidental encroachment is merely ancillary or whether it constitutes a colourable exercise of power. The distinction between incidental encroachment and colourable legislation is crucial: the former is permissible, the latter is not. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1995) the Court clarified that a law whose dominant object is a state subject but which contains provisions that substantially regulate a union subject may be struck down as colourable, because the encroachment is not merely incidental but goes to the core of the union’s competence.

Practical application of the doctrine often involves the Concurrent List (List III). When both Parliament and a state legislature legislate on the same entry, the pith and substance test determines which law prevails under Article 254. If the state law’s dominant character is a matter in List II but it also touches a concurrent entry, the state law may still be valid unless Parliament has occupied the field. The doctrine thus preserves the federal balance by preventing a mechanical reading of entries that would paralyse legitimate legislation. It also underpins the “aspect theory” — a single subject may have multiple aspects, each falling under different entries, allowing both levels to legislate on different aspects without conflict.

Repugnancy under Article 254: Conflict Between Union and State Laws on Concurrent Subjects

Article 254 provides the constitutional mechanism for resolving inconsistencies between laws made by Parliament and state legislatures on matters enumerated in the Concurrent List. The article operates in two distinct situations. Under clause (1), if a state law is repugnant to a central law on a concurrent subject, the central law prevails and the state law becomes void to the extent of the repugnancy. Under clause (2), a state law that has been reserved for the President’s assent and received it can prevail over an earlier central law, but only until Parliament enacts a subsequent law that expressly or impliedly overrides it. The Supreme Court in M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979) laid down a three‑fold test for repugnancy: (i) there must be a direct conflict between the two provisions; (ii) the conflict must be such that the two cannot stand together; (iii) the central law must not have been enacted under a power that excludes state legislation altogether.

The concept of “occupied field” is central to the analysis. If Parliament has legislated comprehensively on a concurrent subject, leaving no room for state legislation, any state law on that subject is repugnant even without a direct textual clash. In Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983) the Court held that the Drugs (Control) Act, 1950, enacted by Parliament, occupied the field of drug price control, rendering a Bihar law on the same subject void. Conversely, in Zameer Ahmed Latifur Rehman v. State of Maharashtra (2010) the Court found no repugnancy because the central law (the Essential Commodities Act) and the state law (the Maharashtra Essential Commodities and Control of Prices Order) operated in distinct spheres — one regulating production and supply, the other fixing retail prices — and could coexist.

Clause (2) of Article 254 introduces a “saving” mechanism. A state law that has received presidential assent can override an earlier central law, but this protection is temporary. Once Parliament enacts a later law on the same subject, the state law again yields. The Supreme Court in State of Tamil Nadu v. State of Kerala (2014) emphasised that the President’s assent under Article 254(2) is not a mere formality; the President must be satisfied that the state law is not repugnant to any existing central law at the time of assent. The doctrine of repugnancy thus balances federal flexibility with the need for national uniformity, and its application requires a careful comparison of the operative provisions, the legislative intent, and the extent of field occupation.

Worked Illustration: Repugnancy Analysis
A state enacts the “State Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act, 2022” under Entry 28 of List III (markets and fairs). Parliament had earlier passed the “Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act, 2018” under the same entry. The state law provides for a lower market fee and a different licensing regime. The central law contains a clause stating that “no state law shall provide for a market fee lower than that prescribed by the Central Government.” Determine whether the state law is repugnant.
Step 1 – Identify the competing provisions. Central Act, Section 12 prescribes a minimum market fee. State Act, Section 5 fixes a lower fee. Step 2 – Apply the three‑fold test (Karunanidhi). (i) Direct conflict: the state fee is lower than the central minimum, so both cannot be obeyed simultaneously. (ii) The two provisions cannot stand together; compliance with one violates the other. (iii) The central law was enacted under Entry 28, List III, a concurrent power that does not exclude state legislation per se. Step 3 – Check for occupied field. The central Act does not expressly state that it occupies the entire field of market fees; it only sets a floor. However, the explicit prohibition on lower state fees indicates Parliament’s intent to set a uniform minimum, which constitutes an implied occupation of that aspect. Step 4 – Article 254(1) applies. Because the state law is repugnant to the central law, the central law prevails and the state provision is void to the extent of the lower fee. Conclusion: The state law’s fee provision is ultra vires; the remainder of the state Act may survive if severable.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a repugnancy question appears, first list the two statutes and the concurrent entry. Then run the Karunanidhi three‑fold test in order; do not jump to “occupied field” unless the direct conflict test is satisfied. Mention Article 254(2) only if the facts state that the state law was reserved and assented to. Cite Karunanidhi, Hoechst, and Zameer Ahmed as the authoritative trilogy. A concise table comparing the two provisions side‑by‑side earns easy marks.

Prospective Overruling: Judicial Technique to Limit Retroactive Effect of Constitutional Decisions

Prospective overruling is a judicial device whereby a court declares that its new interpretation of the Constitution will apply only to future cases, leaving past transactions and decisions governed by the earlier rule. The technique was introduced into Indian constitutional jurisprudence in Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967), where the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend fundamental rights, but limited the declaration’s effect to prospective application to avoid unsettling the vast body of legislation enacted under the previous understanding. The Court reasoned that a purely retroactive invalidation would create legal chaos, undermine the rule of law, and defeat the very purpose of constitutional adjudication.

The doctrine rests on three policy considerations. First, it protects vested rights and legitimate expectations that arose under the old rule. Second, it preserves the stability of the legal system by preventing a flood of litigation challenging past actions. Third, it respects the separation of powers by allowing the legislature time to amend the law in conformity with the new interpretation. In Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) the Court did not employ prospective overruling because the basic structure doctrine was declared as a continuing limitation on amending power, but later benches have used the technique in specific contexts. For instance, in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Court applied prospective overruling to the interpretation of Article 356, holding that the new standards for judicial review of President’s Rule would govern only future proclamations.

The Supreme Court has laid down guidelines for invoking prospective overruling in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1998): (i) the overruling must be necessitated by a change in constitutional interpretation, not merely a change in policy; (ii) the court must expressly state the temporal limit; (iii) the limitation must not defeat the constitutional right itself. The technique is not available for statutory interpretation unless the statute is constitutionally mandated. It is also distinct from the doctrine of “stare decisis” — prospective overruling is an exception to the normal rule that a judicial declaration states the law as it always was. In practice, the Court uses it sparingly, reserving it for cases where retroactive application would cause manifest injustice or administrative breakdown.

Jus Cogens and Peremptory Norms in International Law: Hierarchy and Enforcement

Jus cogens, or peremptory norms, are rules of international law accepted and recognised by the international community of states as a whole as norms from which no derogation is permitted. The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), 1969, codifies this concept in Articles 53 and 64. Article 53 provides that a treaty is void if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general international law. Article 64 stipulates that if a new peremptory norm emerges, any existing treaty that conflicts with it becomes void and terminates. The International Court of Justice (ICJ) in Nicaragua v. United States (1986) affirmed that the prohibition of the use of force, the principle of non‑intervention, and the prohibition of genocide are jus cogens norms. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) in Prosecutor v. Furundžija (1998) held that the prohibition of torture has attained peremptory status.

The hierarchy of norms places jus cogens above treaty law, customary law, and general principles. A treaty conflicting with a peremptory norm is void ab initio; a customary rule that conflicts with jus cogens cannot crystallise. The “persistent objector” rule does not apply to jus cogens — a state cannot opt out of a peremptory norm by persistent objection. Enforcement of jus cogens relies on several mechanisms. First, any state may invoke the responsibility of another state for breach of a peremptory norm under the Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (2001), because such breaches constitute “serious breaches of obligations under peremptory norms” (Article 40). Second, international courts and tribunals may refuse to apply treaties that violate jus cogens. Third, the UN Security Council may take enforcement action under Chapter VII when a peremptory norm is violated, as seen in resolutions concerning genocide in Rwanda and the former Yugoslavia.

Despite their elevated status, the precise content of jus cogens remains a subject of scholarly debate. The ILC’s 1966 draft articles listed a non‑exhaustive catalogue: prohibition of aggression, genocide, slavery, racial discrimination, crimes against humanity, and the right to self‑determination. Subsequent practice has added the prohibition of torture, piracy, and possibly the basic rules of international humanitarian law. The lack of a definitive list means that courts must determine peremptory status on a case‑by‑case basis, examining state practice, opinio juris, and the recognition by the international community as a whole. For UPSC purposes, candidates should be able to cite the VCLT articles, the Nicaragua and Furundžija decisions, and the ILC’s indicative list, and explain why the persistent objector rule is inapplicable.

Anti‑Defection Law and the Tenth Schedule: Judicial Review of Disqualification Proceedings

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment Act, 1985, seeks to curb political defections by providing for disqualification of legislators who voluntarily give up membership of their party or vote contrary to party directions. The Schedule vests the power to decide disqualification petitions in the Speaker (or Chairman) of the House, subject to judicial review. In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the Tenth Schedule but held that the Speaker’s decision is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, perversity, or violation of natural justice. The Court ruled that the finality clause in Paragraph 7 does not oust the jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 136.

Subsequent jurisprudence has refined the scope of review. In Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya (2007) the Court held that the Speaker must act as a quasi‑judicial authority, affording the affected member a fair hearing, and that delay in deciding a petition can be a ground for judicial intervention. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon’ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020) the Court directed the Speaker to decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable time, emphasising that indefinite pendency defeats the purpose of the anti‑defection law. The Court also clarified that “voluntarily giving up membership” is not limited to formal resignation; conduct such as publicly opposing the party, joining another party, or voting against the whip can constitute voluntary abandonment.

Recent developments include the 91st Amendment Act, 2003, which deleted the provision allowing a split of one‑third of the legislature party as a defence, and inserted a bar on holding ministerial office for disqualified members until re‑election. The Supreme Court in Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2023) reiterated that the Speaker’s role is not political but constitutional, and that courts may issue directions to ensure timely adjudication. For answer‑writing, candidates should distinguish between the Speaker’s quasi‑judicial function (subject to review) and the legislative function (immune from review), cite the Kihoto Hollohan, Rana, and Keisham Meghachandra trilogy, and note the 2003 amendment’s impact on the “split” defence.

  • Confusing pith and substance with colourable legislation; the former is a test of dominant purpose, the latter a finding of bad faith transgression.
  • Assuming Article 254(2) gives permanent immunity to a state law; it only protects until Parliament enacts a subsequent overriding law.
  • Treating prospective overruling as a routine tool; it is an exceptional judicial technique reserved for constitutional decisions with massive retroactive impact.
  • Believing a state can invoke the persistent objector rule against a jus cogens norm; peremptory norms admit no derogation or objection.
  • Thinking the Speaker’s decision under the Tenth Schedule is final and non‑reviewable; Kihoto Hollohan explicitly subjected it to judicial review on limited grounds.
  • Overlooking that the 2003 amendment removed the one‑third split defence; many candidates still cite the old provision.
  • Neglecting the distinction between “occupied field” and direct conflict in repugnancy analysis; both can lead to invalidity but require different evidentiary showings.

Doctrine of Constitutional Morality: Evolution, Scope, and Judicial Application

The concept of constitutional morality was first articulated by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly debates (1948) where he described it as “a paramount reverence for the forms of the Constitution”. The Supreme Court gave it doctrinal force in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), holding that constitutional morality requires the state to protect the dignity and autonomy of every individual, even when majoritarian sentiment opposes such protection. The Court distinguished constitutional morality from popular morality, emphasizing that the former is anchored in the Preamble, Fundamental Rights, and Directive Principles, while the latter reflects prevailing social norms.

Subsequent judgments have expanded the scope. In Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (Sabarimala, 2018) the Court held that the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the temple violated constitutional morality because it perpetuated gender‑based discrimination contrary to Articles 14, 15, and 25. In Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) the right to privacy was grounded in constitutional morality, linking dignity, autonomy, and the transformative potential of the Constitution. The doctrine also informs the Court’s approach to the basic structure test: any amendment that undermines constitutional morality — such as eroding secularism or judicial independence — is liable to be struck down.

Key elements of constitutional morality as crystallised by the Court include:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution over transient majoritarian preferences.
  • Protection of minority rights and marginalized communities.
  • Commitment to transformative justice — the Constitution as a charter for social revolution.
  • Judicial duty to enforce constitutional values even when the legislature is silent or complicit.

Critics argue that the doctrine grants judges excessive discretion, but the Court has repeatedly confined its application to cases where a constitutional provision is directly implicated, refusing to import abstract moral notions. For UPSC answer‑writing, candidates should cite the Ambedkar speech, the Navtej Johar and Sabarimala judgments, and explain how constitutional morality operates as an interpretive lens rather than an independent source of law.

The Doctrine of Waiver of Fundamental Rights: Limits and Judicial Pronouncements

The question whether a citizen can waive a fundamental right has been answered in the negative by the Supreme Court in a line of authorities beginning with Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax (1959). The Court held that fundamental rights are not mere private privileges but public guarantees; they embody constitutional values that cannot be surrendered by individual consent. The rationale rests on the public policy dimension of Part III: the rights exist to protect the democratic fabric, and allowing waiver would enable the state to coerce citizens into relinquishing protections.

In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) the Court reiterated that the right to livelihood under Article 21 cannot be waived, even if a slum‑dweller signs an undertaking not to claim rehabilitation. The Court distinguished between voluntary relinquishment of a benefit (e.g., refusing a government scholarship) and waiver of a constitutional guarantee. The former is permissible; the latter is not.

Exceptions are narrowly drawn. A person may choose not to exercise a right — for instance, a journalist may decide not to publish a story — but this is not a waiver; the right remains enforceable. The Court in State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kasab Jamat (2005) clarified that a legislative provision that conditions a benefit on the surrender of a fundamental right is unconstitutional unless the condition is reasonable, non‑discriminatory, and proportionate to a legitimate state aim.

Practical implications for answer‑writing:

  • Quote Basheshar Nath (1959) as the foundational precedent.
  • Distinguish “waiver” from “non‑exercise” and “contractual relinquishment of statutory benefits”.
  • Note that Article 13(2) renders any law that takes away a fundamental right void, and a private agreement cannot circumvent this.
  • Reference the 2020 judgment in K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (privacy) where the Court observed that consent to data processing does not amount to waiver of the right to privacy.
Examiner's Twist
A state enacts a law requiring all candidates for public employment to sign an undertaking that they will not invoke Article 19(1)(g) (freedom of trade) against any departmental transfer order. The law is challenged. Decide the validity of the undertaking clause.
The undertaking clause is unconstitutional. Under Basheshar Nath (1959) and Olga Tellis (1985), fundamental rights cannot be waived by private agreement or statutory condition. Article 19(1)(g) is a constitutional guarantee; any law that compels its surrender violates Article 13(2). The state may impose reasonable restrictions under Article 19(6), but it cannot extinguish the right through a mandatory undertaking. The Court would strike down the clause while upholding any reasonable transfer policy that satisfies the proportionality test.
Answer‑Writing Strategy
When a question asks about waiver, first state the general rule (no waiver of fundamental rights) with the leading case. Then examine whether the scenario involves a true waiver or merely a voluntary non‑exercise. Cite the distinction drawn in Olga Tellis and the proportionality requirement from Mirzapur Moti Kureshi. Conclude with the constitutional provision (Art. 13(2)) that renders any such waiver void.

Reservations to Treaties under the Vienna Convention: Validity, Objections, and Indian Practice

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), 1969, governs the formulation and effect of reservations. Articles 19–23 establish a three‑tier framework: (1) a reservation is permissible unless the treaty prohibits it, it is incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty, or it falls within a category of prohibited reservations; (2) other states may accept or object to the reservation; (3) an objection does not preclude the entry into force of the treaty between the reserving and objecting states unless the objecting state expressly declares otherwise.

The object‑and‑purpose test was authoritatively interpreted by the International Court of Justice in the Reservations to the Genocide Convention Advisory Opinion (1951) and later by the Human Rights Committee in General Comment No. 24 (1994). A reservation that defeats the essential obligations of a human‑rights treaty — for example, a reservation to the non‑derogable provisions of the ICCPR — is impermissible.

India’s practice illustrates both acceptance and objection. India ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) in 1979 with a reservation to Article 9 (detention) and Article 14 (fair trial) concerning preventive detention laws. Several states parties objected, arguing the reservation undermined the Covenant’s core. India maintained the reservation, asserting compatibility with its constitutional scheme. In contrast, India entered no reservation to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) upon ratification in 1993, but later declared an interpretative declaration on Article 16 (marriage and family) which the Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women treated as a reservation and urged withdrawal.

Key points for examination:

  • Article 19 VCLT — three grounds of impermissibility.
  • Article 20 — acceptance and objection regime; distinction between “objection” and “objection with a declaration of non‑entry into force”.
  • Article 21 — legal effects of a reservation and of an objection.
  • Human rights treaties: the severability principle — an impermissible reservation is severed, leaving the state bound by the treaty without the reservation (ICJ, Reservations to the Convention on the Rights of the Child, 1998).
  • India’s reservations to ICCPR, CEDAW, and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) — note the 2007 reservation to Article 12 (legal capacity) of CRPD and subsequent domestic amendments.

The Public Trust Doctrine in Environmental Jurisprudence: Constitutional Foundations and Landmark Cases

The public trust doctrine, rooted in Roman law and revived in the United States (Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois, 1892), was transplanted into Indian environmental law through judicial innovation. The Supreme Court in M. C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1997) held that the state holds natural resources — rivers, forests, air — as a trustee for the public, and any alienation that defeats the trust is subject to judicial review. The Court linked the doctrine to Articles 21, 48A, and 51A(g) of the Constitution, establishing a constitutional mandate for environmental protection.

In Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) the Court articulated the precautionary principle and the polluter‑pays principle as integral to the public trust, directing the closure of polluting tanneries in Tamil Nadu. The doctrine was further expanded in Intellectuals Forum v. State of A.P. (2006) where the Court prevented the conversion of a public park into a commercial complex, affirming that the trust extends to urban green spaces.

Recent developments include Maharashtra v. Union of India (2021) concerning the Western Ghats, where the Court invoked the public trust to restrain mining leases that threatened biodiversity corridors. The Court also applied the doctrine in Goa Foundation v. Union of India (2022) to invalidate a coastal regulation zone notification that diluted protection for mangroves.

For answer‑writing, candidates should:

  • Trace the doctrinal lineage: Roman law → US Supreme Court → Indian Supreme Court (M. C. Mehta).
  • Identify the constitutional anchors: Art. 21 (right to life includes clean environment), Art. 48A (state duty to protect environment), Art. 51A(g) (fundamental duty).
  • Distinguish the public trust from the doctrine of sustainable development — the former imposes a fiduciary duty on the state; the latter is a policy guideline.
  • Cite the trilogy: M. C. Mehta (1997), Vellore Citizens (1996), Intellectuals Forum (2006), and the 2021‑22 Western Ghats and Goa Foundation decisions.
  • Confusing constitutional morality with popular morality; the former is judicially enforceable, the latter is not.
  • Treating waiver of a fundamental right as permissible because a statute conditions a benefit on it; Basheshar Nath and Olga Tellis forbid this.
  • Assuming any reservation to a human‑rights treaty is valid if the reserving state declares it; the object‑and‑purpose test (Genocide Convention Advisory Opinion) invalidates reservations that defeat core obligations.
  • Believing an objection to a reservation automatically terminates treaty relations; under VCLT Art. 20(4)(b) the treaty remains in force unless the objecting state declares otherwise.
  • Equating the public trust doctrine with the precautionary principle; the trust imposes a fiduciary duty on the state, while the precautionary principle guides risk‑management decisions.
  • Overlooking that the public trust extends beyond natural resources to public spaces such as parks and urban commons (Intellectuals Forum, 2006).
  • Neglecting the severability principle for impermissible reservations; the state remains bound by the treaty without the offending reservation.

Doctrine of Federalism: Distribution of Legislative Powers and the Role of the Inter‑State Council

The constitutional architecture of Indian federalism rests on the Seventh Schedule, which distributes legislative competence between the Union and the States through three lists — Union List (List I), State List (List II) and Concurrent List (List III). Article 246 read with the Schedule establishes a rigid demarcation: Parliament has exclusive power over Union List subjects, State Legislatures over State List subjects, and both over Concurrent List subjects, with Union law prevailing in case of conflict under Article 254. The Supreme Court has consistently held that this distribution is a basic feature of the Constitution (S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, 1994), and any legislative encroachment is subject to judicial review under the doctrines of pith and substance and colourable legislation.

Beyond the textual division, the Constitution provides institutional mechanisms for cooperative federalism. Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter‑State Council to inquire into and advise upon disputes between States, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination of policy and action. The Council was first constituted in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission (1988), which emphasised the need for a permanent forum to mitigate inter‑governmental friction. The Punchhi Commission (2010) later recommended strengthening the Council’s mandate, including a role in monitoring the implementation of Central laws that affect State autonomy.

In practice, the Inter‑State Council has met intermittently; its recommendations are advisory and not binding. However, its existence signals the constitutional expectation of dialogue over confrontation. The Goods and Services Tax Council, created under Article 279A (inserted by the 101st Amendment, 2016), operates on a similar federal principle — decisions require a three‑fourths majority of weighted votes, giving States a decisive voice in indirect tax policy. This model of weighted voting is often cited as a template for future federal institutions.

Judicial pronouncements have reinforced the federal balance. In State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963), the Court upheld the Union’s power to acquire land for a central project, but stressed that the Union cannot legislate on State List subjects under the guise of a Union List entry. In Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018), the Constitution Bench clarified that the Lieutenant Governor’s concurrence is required only on matters outside the Legislative Assembly’s competence, preserving the elected government’s domain over State and Concurrent List subjects. These decisions illustrate the Court’s role as the arbiter of federal boundaries.

For answer‑writing, candidates should: (1) map the three lists with illustrative entries; (2) explain the interplay of Articles 246, 254 and 263; (3) cite the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commission recommendations; (4) contrast the advisory Inter‑State Council with the decision‑making GST Council; and (5) reference key judgments that define the limits of Union legislative power.

Judicial Review of Ordinances: Article 123 and 213 – Scope, Limits, and Landmark Cases

Articles 123 and 213 confer ordinance‑making power on the President and the Governors respectively, allowing the executive to legislate when Parliament or a State Legislature is not in session and immediate action is deemed necessary. The power is conditional: the President or Governor must be satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary to take immediate action. The ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of the legislature but must be laid before the legislature when it reassembles; it ceases to operate at the expiration of six weeks from reassembly unless approved earlier.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that ordinance‑making is not a parallel legislative power but an emergency measure. In R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970), the Court struck down the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969, on the ground that the President’s satisfaction was not justiciable but the ordinance could be challenged on constitutional grounds such as violation of fundamental rights. The 44th Amendment (1978) removed the clause making the President's satisfaction non-justiciable, restoring judicial review.

A critical development came in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017), where a seven‑judge Bench ruled that repromulgation of ordinances without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution. The Court held that the Governor’s power to repromulgate is not immune from judicial review and that repeated repromulgation bypasses the legislative mandate, violating the separation of powers. The judgment also clarified that the satisfaction of the President or Governor is subject to judicial scrutiny to the extent of mala fides or irrelevance.

Other notable cases include D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), where the Court condemned the practice of repromulgating ordinances over several years as a “constitutional fraud”, and T. Venkata Reddy v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1985), which held that an ordinance cannot be used to amend the Constitution. The Court has also examined the “immediate action” requirement in AK Roy v. Union of India (1982), upholding the National Security Ordinance, 1980, on the ground that the threat to public order justified immediate legislation.

For examination purposes, candidates should distinguish between the President’s and Governor’s ordinance powers, note the six‑week ceiling, explain the repromulgation bar established in Krishna Kumar Singh, and discuss the justiciability of the executive’s satisfaction. A comparative table of the two articles, the procedural safeguards, and the landmark judgments will fetch high marks.

Technique: Structuring a Comparative Answer on Ordinance Powers
Question: Compare the ordinance‑making powers of the President under Article 123 and the Governor under Article 213. Highlight the judicial checks on repromulgation.
Answer: Begin with a two‑column table: (1) Source — Article 123 (President) vs Article 213 (Governor); (2) Precondition — both require legislature not in session and immediate action; (3) Duration — both cease after six weeks of reassembly unless approved; (4) Repromulgation — barred by Krishna Kumar Singh (2017) for both; (5) Judicial review — satisfaction subject to mala fides test (R.C. Cooper); (6) Constitutional amendment — ordinance cannot amend Constitution (T. Venkata Reddy). Conclude with the principle that ordinance power is an emergency exception, not a substitute for legislative process.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “judicial review of ordinances”, do not merely list cases. Structure the answer around the constitutional text (Articles 123/213), the procedural safeguards (laying before legislature, six‑week limit), the repromulgation prohibition, and the two grounds of challenge — violation of fundamental rights and mala fide satisfaction. Use the Krishna Kumar Singh ratio as the authoritative statement on repromulgation.

Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties: Interpretation Rules (Articles 31‑33) and Indian Practice

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), adopted in 1969 and entered into force in 1980, codifies the customary rules of treaty interpretation. India became a party to the VCLT in 1990. Articles 31 to 33 constitute the interpretative framework: Article 31(1) establishes the general rule — a treaty shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose. The “context” includes the text, preamble, annexes, and any agreement or instrument made in connection with the treaty.

Article 31(3) directs interpreters to take into account any subsequent agreement between the parties regarding interpretation, any subsequent practice establishing the agreement of the parties, and any relevant rules of international law applicable in the relations between the parties. Article 32 permits recourse to supplementary means of interpretation — including the preparatory work (travaux préparatoires) and the circumstances of the treaty’s conclusion — to confirm the meaning resulting from Article 31 or to determine the meaning when the interpretation under Article 31 leaves the meaning ambiguous or obscure, or leads to a result which is manifestly absurd or unreasonable.

Article 33 addresses treaties authenticated in two or more languages. When a treaty is equally authentic in multiple languages, each text is equally authoritative. In case of a difference of meaning which the application of Articles 31 and 32 does not remove, the meaning which best reconciles the texts, having regard to the object and purpose, shall be adopted.

Indian courts have invoked these principles, albeit often without explicit citation of the VCLT. In Gramophone Company of India Ltd. v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984), the Supreme Court interpreted the Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works by reference to its object and purpose, aligning with Article 31(1). In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), the Court relied on the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) to fill a legislative vacuum, treating the treaty as an interpretative aid for domestic law under Article 31(3)(c). The Court in Apparel Export Promotion Council v. A.K. Chopra (1999) used the VCLT’s good‑faith principle to interpret India’s obligations under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

For answer‑writing, candidates should: (1) reproduce the three‑step hierarchy — Article 31 (primary), Article 32 (supplementary), Article 33 (multilingual treaties); (2) explain the role of “object and purpose” as a teleological anchor; (3) cite Indian cases where the Supreme Court applied these rules implicitly; and (4) note that India’s dualist system requires enabling legislation for treaty provisions to have direct domestic effect, but interpretative use of treaties is permissible even without incorporation.

Principle of Non‑Refoulement in International Refugee Law: Customary Status and Indian Judicial Approach

The principle of non‑refoulement prohibits the expulsion or return of a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on account of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion. It is enshrined in Article 33 of the 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol. Although India is not a party to either instrument, the principle has attained the status of customary international law, binding on all States regardless of treaty participation. The International Court of Justice, in the Case Concerning the Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Bosnia v. Serbia, 2007), affirmed that non‑refoulement reflects a norm of customary international law.

Indian jurisprudence has recognised non‑refoulement as an integral component of Article 21 (right to life and personal liberty). In National Human Rights Commission v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996), the Supreme Court directed the State to protect Chakma refugees from forced eviction, holding that the principle of non‑refoulement flows from the constitutional guarantee of life. In Ktaer Abbas Habib Al Qutaifi v. Union of India (1998), the Delhi High Court explicitly stated that non‑refoulement is part of the law of the land under Article 21, and that the government cannot deport a person to a country where they face persecution.

The Supreme Court in Louis De Raedt v. Union of India (1991) extended protection to foreign nationals detained under the Foreigners Act, 1946, emphasising that the power to deport is not absolute and must conform to the principles of natural justice and non‑refoulement. More recently, in Mohammad Salimullah v. Union of India (2021), the Court declined to stay the deportation of Rohingya refugees but reiterated that the principle of non‑refoulement is a constitutional value, leaving the matter to the executive’s discretion subject to judicial review.

For examination, candidates should: (1) define non‑refoulement and its treaty source; (2) explain its customary status and the ICJ’s endorsement; (3) trace the Indian judicial trajectory from NHRC v. Arunachal Pradesh to Mohammad Salimullah; (4) distinguish between the procedural safeguards under the Foreigners Act and the substantive bar of non‑refoulement; and (5) discuss the tension between national security concerns and the constitutional obligation under Article 21.

Doctrine of Parliamentary Privileges and Judicial Review: Article 105, 194 and the Contempt Power

Articles 105 and 194 of the Constitution confer on Members of Parliament and State Legislatures, respectively, freedom of speech in the House and immunity from court proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given. They also empower each House to define its privileges, punish for contempt, and regulate its internal proceedings. These privileges are modelled on the British parliamentary tradition but are subject to the written Constitution and judicial review.

The Supreme Court in Searchlight Case (M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha, 1959) held that parliamentary privileges are not absolute and must yield to fundamental rights. The Court ruled that the publication of expunged portions of a speech by a newspaper did not constitute contempt of the legislature, as the privilege of freedom of speech under Article 194(1) did not extend to protection against publication of proceedings that the House itself had ordered to be expunged.

In Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007), a Constitution Bench examined the expulsion of MPs for alleged “cash for questions”. The Court held that the power to expel is a privilege subject to judicial review for violation of natural justice, mala fides, or perversity. The judgment established that while the House is the sole judge of its privileges, the exercise of that power is justiciable when it affects the fundamental rights of members.

The contempt power has been scrutinised in Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly (1965), where the Allahabad High Court held that a legislative assembly cannot punish a non‑member for contempt committed outside the House unless the act directly obstructs the House’s functions. The Supreme Court later affirmed that the contempt jurisdiction must be exercised sparingly and in accordance with the principles of natural justice.

For answer‑writing, candidates should: (1) enumerate the privileges under Articles 105(1), (2) and 194(1), (2); (2) explain the distinction between collective privileges (e.g., power to punish contempt) and individual privileges (freedom of speech, immunity); (3) cite the Searchlight, Raja Ram Pal, and Keshav Singh judgments; (4) discuss the “harmonious construction” approach adopted by courts to balance privileges with fundamental rights; and (5) note that the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978, did not alter the privilege provisions, leaving the judicial review framework intact.
