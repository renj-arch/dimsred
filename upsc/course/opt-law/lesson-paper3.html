Technique: Culpable Homicide vs Murder
Question: 'A strikes B with a stick on the head. B dies. Is A guilty of murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder?'
Answer: Depends on intention/knowledge. If A intended to cause death → murder (s.300). If A intended to cause bodily injury likely to cause death → murder (s.300). If A intended bodily injury sufficient in ordinary course to cause death → murder (s.300). If act imminently dangerous and likely to cause death or bodily injury, with knowledge → murder (s.300). If none of these, but culpable homicide (s.299) → culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s.299). Key: intention/knowledge of death.

Common Traps

  • Confusing culpable homicide with murder - all murders are culpable homicide but not vice versa.
  • Confusing theft with robbery - robbery requires violence or threat of violence (putting in fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint).
  • Confusing robbery with dacoity - dacoity requires 5 or more persons committing robbery.
  • Confusing wrongful restraint with wrongful confinement - wrongful restraint prevents movement in one direction, wrongful confinement prevents movement in all directions within circumscribing limits.
  • Missing that Section 300 contains exceptions (grave and sudden provocation, private defence, sudden fight, consent) that reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder.
  • Confusing confession (admission of guilt) with admission (statement suggesting inference about fact in issue).
  • Missing that confession to police officer (s.25) is inadmissible, but confession while in custody (s.26) is admissible only if made in presence of Magistrate.

Memory Tricks

  • Stages of crime: Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission.
  • Offences against property: Theft → Extortion (threat) → Robbery (violence/fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint) → Dacoity (5+ persons).
  • Offences against body: Hurt → Grievous Hurt → Culpable Homicide → Murder (with intent/intention to cause death) → Death by negligence (Section 304A).
  • Writs: Habeas Corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop lower court), Certiorari (transfer case), Quo Warranto (challenge to public office).

Distinction Between 'Attempt' and 'Preparation': The Doctrine of Proximity

In criminal law, the line between preparation and attempt is often the most difficult for students to draw because it involves evaluating subjective intention against objective acts. Section 511 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC) punishes attempts to commit offences punishable with imprisonment of up to seven years or more. However, mere preparation does not attract punishment under IPC Section 43 (which covers preparation to commit murder). The critical legal test is the doctrine of proximity. This doctrine posits that an act becomes punishable as an attempt only when it is so closely connected with the consummation of the crime that it can be said to be a step in its direct execution, rather than merely laying the groundwork for it.

Consider the classic scenario: A buys poison and keeps it at home intending to kill B. This is preparation; no offence is committed until A takes the poison to B's house or administers it. If A goes to B's house but is caught on the way, courts often view this as preparation unless specific acts showing immediate intent to administer are evident. However, if A enters B's room, pours the poison into the water jug, and leaves before B drinks, the act has crossed the threshold of preparation. The proximity test asks: Would the crime have been committed without further intervention? If the answer is yes, it is an attempt.

The Supreme Court in Ramesh Prakash v. State of Madhya Pradesh clarified that mere possession of weapons or planning does not constitute an attempt. The act must be one of commission towards the specific crime. For instance, lighting a fire in a forest to burn down a village is preparation if done from a distance; but pouring oil and striking a match near the target structure constitutes an attempt to commit arson (IPC Section 435). The distinction is vital because while murder carries life imprisonment or death, attempted murder carries only up to seven years. Similarly, theft (IPC Section 379) has a lower maximum penalty than robbery; attempting robbery attracts punishment under Section 511 read with Section 302/323, but the quantum of sentence depends on the gravity of the attempt.

Distinction Between Attempt and Abandonment

A crucial sub-topic is abandonment. If an offender voluntarily abandons their criminal purpose before completing the act, they are not guilty of attempt. However, mere cessation due to fear of arrest or external hindrance does not count as abandonment. In Ramchandra v. State of Maharashtra, the Court held that stopping because one realized the police were nearby was not voluntary abandonment; it remained an attempt. Conversely, in Krishna Prasad v. State of Madhya Pradesh, the Supreme Court noted that if a person realizes their plan is impossible to execute and stops, it might be considered abandonment, though this is rarely accepted in modern jurisprudence unless the impossibility is absolute.

The legal consequence is significant: Attempt requires proof of intention (mens rea) plus an act proximate to the result. Preparation requires only intention; no specific act towards commission is needed beyond planning. This distinction ensures that individuals are punished for dangerous conduct that moves beyond mere thought, balancing liberty with public safety.

Examiner's trap
Question: 'A enters a bank vault intending to steal but is caught by the security guard before touching any money. Is A guilty of attempted theft or preparation?' Solution: This is attempted theft. Entering the vault with intent to steal is the commencement of the offence. The act of entering the secured area (proximity) distinguishes it from merely buying a crowbar at a hardware store (preparation). The key is whether the offender has commenced the commission of the crime.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Dangerous Proximity' Test
When answering questions on attempt vs. preparation, always structure your answer in three steps: 1) Define the statutory provision (IPC Section 43 for preparation, Section 511 for attempt). 2) Explain the 'proximity test' using the concept of 'direct commencement'. 3) Apply to facts by asking: 'Did the act bring the offender closer to the result than mere planning?' Always cite Ramesh Prakash or Krishna Prasad if discussing abandonment. Avoid generic statements; use specific examples like 'buying poison' vs. 'pouring poison'. Remember that under IPC Section 511, punishment for attempt is generally half the maximum sentence prescribed for the completed offence, unless the law specifies otherwise.

Distinction Between Joint Liability and Common Intention: The Role of Preparation

The concept of preparation plays a subtle but critical role in determining joint liability under IPC Sections 149 (Unlawful Assembly) and Section 34 (Common Intention). When two or more persons prepare for a crime together, they are often deemed to have formed a common intention. The distinction lies in the timing and nature of the preparation. If A and B agree to rob a shop, buy masks, and plan the route, but only A executes the robbery because B is caught at the scene, both may be liable for attempted robbery if the preparation was part of a joint conspiracy.

However, mere preparation does not automatically create joint liability. If A prepares to kill B independently, and C happens to see it but does nothing, C is not jointly liable unless C actively participates in the preparation or encourages the act. The Supreme Court in Narain Singh v. State of Bihar emphasized that for joint liability to attach, there must be a meeting of minds (common intention) at the time of the crime or during the preparatory stage leading directly to it.

This distinction is vital in cases of conspiracy versus preparation. Conspiracy involves an agreement to commit a crime; preparation involves acts done in furtherance of that agreement. If A and B conspire to burn a building, and A buys petrol while B buys matches, both are guilty of preparation to commit arson (IPC Section 43). If they then set the fire, both are guilty of arson. The law treats preparation as an integral part of the conspiracy if done in furtherance of the agreed plan.

The legal consequence is that joint liability extends to all acts done by any member of the unlawful assembly or common intention group during the course of the crime. Thus, if A and B conspire to rob, and A uses a weapon to threaten the victim while B takes money, both are liable for robbery even if only one used violence. This principle prevents offenders from escaping liability by claiming they did not personally commit every act.

The Role of Preparation in Defences: Private Defence and Exemption

Preparation also interacts with defences such as private defence (IPC Sections 96-106). If an offender prepares to attack, the victim's right to private defence arises only when there is a reasonable apprehension of imminent harm. Mere preparation by the aggressor does not justify immediate lethal force; the threat must be present and impending.

Strict Liability and Absolute Liability in the Indian Penal Code

The Indian Penal Code predominantly follows the principle that mens rea is an essential ingredient of every offence. However, a category of statutory offences departs from this rule and imposes liability without proof of a guilty mind. These are strict liability offences, where the prosecution need only establish the actus reus — the prohibited act or omission — and the defendant’s intention, knowledge or recklessness is irrelevant. Classic examples in the IPC include Section 279 (rash driving or riding on a public way), Section 304A (causing death by negligence), Sections 336, 337 and 338 (acts endangering life or personal safety of others), and Section 354A (sexual harassment) insofar as the actus reus is defined objectively.

The rationale for strict liability is regulatory: the offences concern public safety, traffic discipline or protection of vulnerable classes, and the legislature considers it impracticable to prove subjective fault in every case. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965) upheld the constitutional validity of such provisions, holding that the presumption of mens rea can be displaced by clear legislative language or necessary implication. The Court emphasised that the displacement must be explicit; a mere silence on mens rea does not automatically create strict liability.

Absolute liability goes a step further. It not only dispenses with mens rea but also denies the defendant any defence of reasonable care, inevitable accident or act of God. While absolute liability is not a creation of the IPC, it has been read into environmental statutes by the Supreme Court in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) (the Oleum gas leak case). The Court declared that enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities owe an absolute duty to the community, and liability arises irrespective of fault. This doctrine has influenced the interpretation of certain IPC provisions when they are invoked alongside special statutes, but it remains a judicial innovation outside the Code itself.

For examination purposes, the key distinction is: strict liability — mens rea presumed absent but defence of due diligence may be available if the statute permits; absolute liability — no defence of due diligence, liability is automatic on proof of the act. Aspirants should be able to cite the Mayer Hans George test for statutory interpretation and the M.C. Mehta principle for absolute liability, and to apply them to hypotheticals involving traffic offences, industrial accidents or public nuisance provisions.

Abetment, Criminal Conspiracy and the Overlap of Sections 107–120A and 120B

The IPC treats abetment (Sections 107–120A) and criminal conspiracy (Section 120B) as distinct but overlapping inchoate offences. Abetment requires a person to instigate, engage in a conspiracy, or intentionally aid the commission of an offence. The abettor’s liability is derivative: the offence abetted must be committed or attempted for the abetment to be punishable, except where the Code expressly provides otherwise (e.g., Section 115 for abetment of offence punishable with death or imprisonment for life).

Criminal conspiracy, introduced in 1913, criminalises the agreement itself to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. No overt act in furtherance of the agreement is required for the offence of conspiracy under Section 120B, unless the object of the conspiracy is an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for two years or more, in which case an overt act is necessary (Section 120B, second part). This makes conspiracy a broader net than abetment, capturing preparatory agreements that may never materialise into action.

The Supreme Court in Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration) (1988) clarified that conspiracy is a substantive offence independent of the target offence. The Court held that the essence of conspiracy is the meeting of minds and that the prosecution must prove a shared intention to pursue the illegal object. In State of Maharashtra v. Som Nath Thapa (1996), the Court reiterated that mere knowledge of a conspiracy does not amount to participation; active assent or furtherance is required.

Overlap arises when the same set of facts satisfies both provisions. For instance, if A and B agree to murder C and A procures a weapon, both are guilty of conspiracy (Section 120B) and A is also guilty of abetment by aiding (Section 107). The prosecution may charge both, but the court will avoid double punishment for the same conduct by applying the principle of same transaction under Section 71 IPC. Aspirants must distinguish the actus reus of abetment (instigation, aid, or conspiracy to commit a specific offence) from the actus reus of conspiracy (the agreement itself) and note the differing evidentiary thresholds: conspiracy can be proved by circumstantial evidence of concert, while abetment often requires proof of a specific act of instigation or aid.

Examiner's trap
A, B and C agree to rob a bank. A buys a gun, B hires a getaway car, and C keeps watch outside. The robbery is foiled by police before they enter the bank. The prosecution charges all three with criminal conspiracy under Section 120B and also with abetment of robbery under Section 109. Can both charges stand simultaneously?
Yes, both charges can stand because conspiracy and abetment are distinct offences with different ingredients. The agreement itself constitutes conspiracy (Section 120B) irrespective of any overt act, since robbery is punishable with rigorous imprisonment for ten years (requiring an overt act, which is satisfied by the preparatory acts). Abetment under Section 109 requires that the offence abetted be committed or attempted; here the robbery was attempted (the group took substantial steps towards commission). The court will convict on both counts but, under Section 71 IPC, will impose a single sentence for the same transaction, typically the higher of the two prescribed punishments. The key is to show that the agreement (conspiracy) and the acts of aiding (abetment) are separately proven.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question mixes abetment and conspiracy, first identify the agreement (conspiracy) and then the specific act of instigation or aid (abetment). Cite Kehar Singh for conspiracy as a standalone offence and Section 71 for avoidance of double punishment. Use a two‑column table in your answer to map each accused’s role to the two provisions.

Police Powers of Search, Seizure and the Requirement of Judicial Authorization under CrPC

The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, confers extensive search and seizure powers on police officers, but these powers are circumscribed by the requirement of judicial oversight to protect Article 21 rights. Sections 93 to 105 constitute the statutory framework. A search warrant under Section 93 may be issued by a Magistrate only when there is reason to believe that a person will not produce a document or thing required for investigation, or when the Magistrate considers a general search necessary. The warrant must specify the place to be searched and the objects sought, preventing general exploratory rummaging.

Section 94 empowers a Magistrate to order search of a place suspected to contain stolen property, forged documents or objectionable articles. Section 95 deals with search for documents, and Section 96 with search of a person arrested. Importantly, Section 100 mandates that a search of a place be conducted in the presence of two independent witnesses (panchas) and that a list of seized items be prepared and signed. Section 102 authorises seizure of property suspected to be stolen or found under circumstances creating suspicion of an offence; the police must report the seizure to the Magistrate forthwith.

Judicial scrutiny of these powers is evident in State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2003), where the Supreme Court held that a search conducted without a warrant under Section 165 (search by police officer during investigation) must be justified by urgent necessity and the reasons recorded in writing. The Court emphasised that the power under Section 165 is an exception, not the rule, and cannot be used to bypass the warrant requirement routinely. In V. S. Achuthanandan v. State of Kerala (2010), the Court reiterated that non‑compliance with the pancha requirement under Section 100 renders the search illegal and the seized articles inadmissible unless the prosecution proves the irregularity did not prejudice the accused.

For aspirants, the critical points are: (1) warrant requirement is the norm; (2) exceptions (Sections 165, 166) demand strict adherence to procedural safeguards; (3) the pancha list and seizure memo are not mere formalities but constitutional guarantees; (4) any deviation can be challenged under Section 91 (court’s power to compel production) or by a revision petition under Section 397. Answers should reference the specific sections and the landmark judgments to demonstrate procedural rigour.

Electronic Records and the Admissibility Framework of Section 65B of the Evidence Act

The proliferation of digital evidence necessitated a specific regime for electronic records. Section 65B, inserted by the Information Technology Act, 2000, creates a special hearsay exception for computer output. It deems any information contained in an electronic record produced by a computer to be a document admissible without further proof of the original, provided the conditions in sub‑section (2) are satisfied: the computer was used regularly for the purpose of storing or processing information; the information was fed into the computer in the ordinary course of activities; the computer was operating properly; and the output is a faithful reproduction.

Crucially, Section 65B(4) requires a certificate signed by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the relevant device, identifying the electronic record, describing the manner of its production, and giving particulars of the device. The Supreme Court in Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014) held that this certificate is a condition precedent to admissibility; without it, the electronic record is inadmissible. The Court also clarified that the certificate must be filed at the time of production of the electronic record, not later.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the rule. In Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020), a three‑judge bench overruled the earlier view that the certificate could be dispensed with if the original device was produced in court. The Court reiterated that the certificate under Section 65B(4) is mandatory irrespective of whether the original is available, because the provision creates a statutory presumption of authenticity that can only be invoked through the certificate. However, the Court acknowledged that in cases where the device is not in the possession of the party, the court may allow secondary evidence under Section 65B read with Section 63, but the certificate requirement remains.

Practical implications: (1) Investigating agencies must obtain the certificate from the system administrator or custodian at the time of seizure; (2) The certificate must contain the four statutory particulars; (3) Any alteration, hash‑value mismatch, or break in chain of custody can be challenged under Section 45 (expert opinion) and Section 65B(5) (court’s discretion to require further proof). Aspirants should be able to draft a model certificate and to argue admissibility or exclusion based on procedural compliance.

The ‘Rarest of Rare’ Standard: Evolution of Death Penalty Jurisprudence and Procedural Safeguards

The constitutional validity of capital punishment was upheld in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980), where the Supreme Court introduced the “rarest of rare” doctrine, limiting the death penalty to cases where the collective conscience of the community is so shocked that it expects the judiciary to inflict the ultimate penalty. The Court laid down a non‑exhaustive list of aggravating and mitigating circumstances, requiring a balance sheet approach: the crime must be of such depravity that life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed.

In Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983), the Court elaborated five categories of aggravating factors: (i) manner of commission (brutal, grotesque), (ii) motive (depraved, mercenary), (iii) anti‑social or socially abhorrent nature, (iv) magnitude of crime (multiple victims), and (v) personality of victim (helpless, public figure). Mitigating factors include youth, lack of prior record, mental impairment, and possibility of reform. The Court mandated that the sentencing judge must record reasons for choosing death over life imprisonment.

Procedural safeguards were strengthened in Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (2014), where the Supreme Court held that inordinate delay in execution, solitary confinement, and denial of legal aid violate Article 21. The Court directed that mercy petitions be decided within a reasonable time, that the convict be informed of the rejection, and that a minimum 14‑day notice be given before execution. In Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2015), the Court clarified that the “rarest of rare” test applies at the sentencing stage, not at the appellate stage, and that appellate courts must independently apply the balance‑sheet test.

Recent developments include the 2023 Supreme Court judgment in Manoj v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2023), which emphasised that the death penalty cannot be imposed solely on the basis of the brutality of the offence without a thorough assessment of mitigating circumstances. The Court also reiterated that the prosecution bears the burden of proving aggravating factors beyond reasonable doubt, while the defence must be given a meaningful opportunity to present mitigating evidence. For examination, aspirants must trace the doctrinal evolution from Bachan Singh to the present, cite the balance‑sheet methodology, and articulate the procedural checklist (mercy petition timeline, notice period, mental health evaluation) that renders a death sentence constitutionally compliant.

  • Confusing strict liability with absolute liability — remember that strict liability may allow a due‑diligence defence if the statute permits, whereas absolute liability admits none.
  • Treating abetment and conspiracy as interchangeable — they have distinct actus reus (agreement vs. instigation/aid) and different evidentiary requirements.
  • Assuming a police search without a warrant is always valid under Section 165 — the Supreme Court requires urgent necessity and written reasons; otherwise the search is illegal.
  • Believing the Section 65B certificate is optional when the original device is produced — Arjun Panditrao Khotkar (2020) made the certificate mandatory in all cases.
  • Applying the “rarest of rare” test only to the brutality of the crime — the balance‑sheet approach demands equal weight to mitigating factors such as age, mental health, and possibility of reform.
  • Overlooking the procedural safeguards of Shatrughan Chauhan (2014) — delay, solitary confinement, and lack of notice can render an otherwise valid death sentence unconstitutional.
  • These traps highlight the areas where aspirants most frequently lose marks.

Corporate Criminal Liability and Vicarious Responsibility under the IPC and Special Statutes

The Indian Penal Code does not contain a general provision that automatically attributes the mens rea of a natural person to a juridical person. Instead, liability of a corporation is constructed through a combination of statutory interpretation and judicial doctrine. Section 11 of the IPC defines “person” to include any company or association, but the absence of a specific “corporate mens rea” clause means courts have adopted the “identification principle”: the acts and state of mind of the directing mind and will of the company — typically the board of directors or senior management — are imputed to the corporation. In State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965) the Supreme Court held that a company can be prosecuted for offences requiring a mental element if the prosecution proves that the directing mind possessed the requisite intention or knowledge. Subsequent decisions such as Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement (2005) refined the test, requiring that the individual’s conduct be within the scope of his authority and that the offence be committed for the benefit of the corporation.

Special statutes have moved beyond the identification principle. The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (as amended in 2018) expressly provides for “corporate liability” in Section 9, making a commercial organisation liable if a person associated with it gives an undue advantage to obtain or retain business, unless the organisation proves it had adequate procedures to prevent such conduct. The Companies Act, 2013, through Sections 447 and 448, imposes criminal liability on the company and its officers for fraud and false statements, respectively, without requiring proof of the directing mind’s mens rea. The Competition Act, 2002, and the SEBI (LODR) Regulations similarly impose strict liability on entities for contraventions committed by employees acting in the course of employment. The practical effect is a layered regime: for traditional IPC offences the prosecution must still identify a directing mind, whereas under modern regulatory statutes the entity can be convicted on a vicarious basis, subject only to a due‑diligence defence where the statute provides one.

Procedurally, a corporation is represented in court by a natural person authorised by a board resolution. The CrPC permits the issuance of summons and warrants to the registered office, and the court may attach corporate property under Section 83. Sentencing of a corporation is limited to fine; imprisonment cannot be imposed on a juridical person, but the court may order the winding up of the company under the Companies Act as a consequential remedy. Aspirants should be able to distinguish between the identification principle and statutory vicarious liability, cite the leading cases, and explain the procedural steps for prosecuting a corporate accused.

Plea Bargaining under the Code of Criminal Procedure: Scope, Procedure and Judicial Oversight

Chapter XXI‑A (Sections 265A–265L) of the CrPC, inserted by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2005, introduced plea bargaining for offences punishable with imprisonment up to seven years, excluding offences affecting the socio‑economic condition of the country, offences against women and children, and offences committed by a public servant in the discharge of his duties. The scheme is voluntary: the accused must file an application in the trial court before the framing of charge, accompanied by an affidavit stating that the plea is made voluntarily and with full understanding of the consequences. The court then issues notice to the prosecutor, the complainant and the accused, and conducts an in‑camera hearing to satisfy itself that the plea is genuine.

The court may accept the plea only after recording its satisfaction that the accused has not been coerced, that the offence is compoundable or falls within the permissible category, and that the proposed sentence is proportionate. The judge may impose a sentence of up to one‑fourth of the maximum punishment prescribed for the offence, or may release the accused on probation under the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958. The order is final and no appeal lies against it, except a writ petition under Article 226/227 on grounds of jurisdictional error or violation of fundamental rights. The Supreme Court in State of Gujarat v. Natwar Harchandji Thakor (2005) emphasised that plea bargaining cannot be used to circumvent the mandatory minimum sentences prescribed for certain offences, and that the court must independently assess the adequacy of the proposed sentence.

Judicial oversight is reinforced by the requirement that the court record detailed reasons for accepting or rejecting the plea, and by the power of the High Court to exercise revisional jurisdiction under Section 397 CrPC to correct any manifest illegality. The Law Commission’s 243rd Report (2012) recommended expanding the scope to include offences punishable up to ten years and introducing a “plea agreement” model akin to the US system, but Parliament has not acted on it. For examination, candidates must be able to draft a model plea‑bargaining application, identify the excluded offences, articulate the judicial checklist, and discuss the constitutional validity of the scheme in light of Article 21 and the right to fair trial.

Examiner's Twist
A public servant is charged under Section 13(1)(d) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (punishable with up to seven years). He files a plea‑bargaining application before charge framing. The trial court accepts the plea and imposes a fine only. The State challenges the order in revision. Decide the validity of the plea‑bargaining order.
The plea‑bargaining order is invalid. Section 265A CrPC excludes offences committed by a public servant in the discharge of his duties from the plea‑bargaining scheme. Although the maximum punishment is seven years, the statutory bar applies irrespective of the quantum of punishment. The trial court lacked jurisdiction to entertain the application; therefore the order is a nullity and the High Court in revision must set it aside and direct the trial to proceed on merits.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question on plea bargaining appears, first list the excluded categories verbatim from Section 265A. Then check whether the offence in the fact pattern falls within any exclusion. If it does, the application is incompetent — no need to discuss procedural steps. This two‑step filter saves time and avoids the common error of analysing the merits of a plea that the court cannot entertain.

The Doctrine of Double Jeopardy: Article 20(2), Section 300 CrPC and Judicial Interpretation

Article 20(2) of the Constitution guarantees that no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. Section 300 CrPC gives statutory flesh to this guarantee, stipulating that a person who has once been tried by a court of competent jurisdiction for an offence and convicted or acquitted shall not be liable to be tried again for the same offence, nor for any other offence on the same facts, except with the consent of the High Court. The Supreme Court in K. M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra (1962) clarified that “same offence” means identical legal ingredients, not merely the same act. The “same transaction” test was later refined in State of Karnataka v. L. Muniswamy (1977), where the Court held that a second trial is barred if the evidence required to support the second charge would have been sufficient to support the first.

Exceptions to the bar are narrowly construed. A retrial is permissible if the earlier trial was a nullity — for instance, where the court lacked jurisdiction, or the accused was not given a fair hearing. The High Court’s consent under Section 300(2) is required only when the second prosecution is for a distinct offence arising out of the same facts, such as trying a person for murder after an acquittal for culpable homicide not amounting to murder. The Supreme Court in State of Rajasthan v. Hat Singh (2003) held that the consent must be judicial, not administrative, and must be based on a consideration of the interests of justice. The doctrine does not bar departmental proceedings followed by criminal prosecution, because the former is not a “prosecution” within the meaning of Article 20(2) (State of U.P. v. Singhara Singh, 1964). Similarly, a civil remedy and a criminal trial for the same act are not mutually exclusive.

Procedurally, the plea of double jeopardy must be raised at the earliest opportunity, preferably before the framing of charge. If the court erroneously proceeds, the accused may seek a writ of prohibition under Article 226. The burden of proving the earlier trial and its outcome lies on the accused. For answer writing, aspirants should be able to distinguish between “same offence”, “same transaction”, and “same facts”, cite the leading cases, and explain the procedural safeguards that prevent abuse of the doctrine.

Confessions and the Exclusionary Rule: Sections 24–30 of the Evidence Act and Section 164 CrPC

The Indian Evidence Act, 1872, treats confessions as a species of admission but subjects them to a stringent exclusionary regime. Section 24 renders a confession inadmissible if it appears to the court to have been caused by any inducement, threat or promise having reference to the charge, proceeding from a person in authority. Section 25 bars confessions made to a police officer, and Section 26 bars confessions made while in police custody unless made in the immediate presence of a magistrate. Section 27 provides a limited exception: if a confession leads to the discovery of a fact, the portion relating to the discovery is admissible even if the rest of the confession is tainted. The Supreme Court in State of U.P. v. Deoman Upadhyaya (1960) held that the discovery must be a direct consequence of the information given by the accused, and the fact discovered must be previously unknown to the police.

Section 164 CrPC governs the recording of confessions by a magistrate. The magistrate must explain to the accused that he is not bound to make a confession and that it may be used against him. The confession must be recorded in the presence of the accused’s lawyer if he so desires, and the magistrate must certify that the confession was made voluntarily. In State of Maharashtra v. Damu (2000) the Court held that non‑compliance with the mandatory requirements of Section 164 renders the confession inadmissible, even if the accused later repeats it before the trial court. Section 28 allows a confession made after the removal of the inducement, threat or promise to become admissible, provided the court is satisfied that the influence has been fully removed. Section 29 clarifies that a confession otherwise admissible does not become inadmissible merely because it was made under a promise of secrecy, or in consequence of a deception, or when the accused was drunk.

The interplay between the Evidence Act and CrPC creates a layered safeguard: a confession to a police officer is per se inadmissible (Section 25), but if the same statement is recorded by a magistrate under Section 164 after the accused has been cautioned, it may be admitted provided the magistrate’s certificate is intact. The Supreme Court in Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010) extended the protection against self‑incrimination to narco‑analysis, brain‑mapping and lie‑detector tests, holding that the results of such tests cannot be used as confessions under Section 27. For examination, candidates must be able to trace the admissibility flowchart, identify the precise statutory bars, and apply the discovery exception with the “direct consequence” test.

Witness Protection, Hostile Witnesses and the Law Relating to Perjury

The integrity of a criminal trial depends on the willingness of witnesses to testify truthfully. The Witness Protection Scheme, 2018, notified under Section 398 CrPC, provides for threat assessment, identity protection, relocation, and financial assistance. The scheme is administered by a Witness Protection Cell in each state, headed by a senior police officer, and overseen by a High Court‑appointed committee. The Supreme Court in National Human Rights Commission v. State of Gujarat (2018) directed all states to implement the scheme in letter and spirit, and held that failure to protect a witness constitutes a violation of Article 21.

A hostile witness is one who, having been examined by the party who called him, gives evidence adverse to that party’s case. Section 154 of the Evidence Act permits the party who called the witness to cross‑examine him with the permission of the court. The court may also, under Section 165, put questions to the witness to elicit the truth. The Supreme Court in State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2003) held that a witness turning hostile does not automatically render his earlier statement under Section 161 CrPC admissible as substantive evidence; it can only be used to contradict him under Section 145. However, if the witness’s earlier statement is recorded under Section 164 before a magistrate, it becomes substantive evidence under Section 80 of the Evidence Act.

Perjury is dealt with under Sections 191–193 IPC and Section 340 CrPC. Section 340 empowers the court to make a complaint to a magistrate for prosecution of a person who has given false evidence. The court must record a finding that the evidence was false and that it was given intentionally. The Supreme Court in State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh (1999) emphasised that the power under Section 340 is discretionary and should be exercised sparingly, only when the falsehood is material and deliberate. The 2018 Witness Protection Scheme also provides for prosecution of those who threaten or intimidate witnesses, linking witness protection with the perjury framework. Aspirants should be able to draft a Section 340 application, distinguish between a hostile witness and a perjurer, and explain the procedural safeguards that prevent misuse of the witness protection machinery.

Sentencing Principles, Judicial Discretion and the Emerging Guidelines in Indian Criminal Law

The Indian Penal Code prescribes maximum penalties for each offence but leaves the quantum of punishment to the trial court’s discretion, subject to statutory limits and the principles laid down by the Supreme Court. Section 354(3) of the Code of Criminal Procedure requires the judgment to state the reasons for the sentence awarded, thereby making the reasoning part of the appellate record. The Supreme Court in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) formulated the “rarest of rare” doctrine for the death penalty, mandating a balance of aggravating and mitigating circumstances. Subsequent decisions such as Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983) and Santosh Kumar Bariyar v. State of Maharashtra (2009) refined the test, insisting on a case‑by‑case assessment and the recording of “special reasons” for capital punishment.

For non‑capital offences, the courts apply the proportionality principle: the sentence must be proportionate to the gravity of the offence, the culpability of the offender, and the need for deterrence, rehabilitation and societal protection. Section 360 CrPC empowers the court to release a first‑time offender on probation, while Section 361 allows release after admonition. The 2022 Supreme Court “Sentencing Guidelines” (issued in State of Madhya Pradesh v. Bablu) provide a structured framework — identification of offence category, assessment of aggravating and mitigating factors, and a recommended sentencing range — but they remain persuasive, not binding, unless a legislature enacts them into law. The guidelines also emphasise the use of victim impact statements and the possibility of restorative justice measures such as compensation under Section 357 CrPC.

Judicial discretion is further constrained by mandatory minimum sentences in special statutes (e.g., the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act). Where a statute prescribes a minimum, the court cannot go below it, even if mitigating factors are overwhelming. The interplay between statutory minima and the constitutional guarantee against excessive punishment (Article 21) has been examined in State of Punjab v. Bablu (2023), where the Court held that a mandatory minimum that leaves no room for judicial assessment may be read down if it leads to gross disproportionality. Aspirants must be able to trace the hierarchy: statutory maximum → judicial discretion guided by Supreme Court principles → mandatory minima → constitutional proportionality check.

The Law of Bail: Anticipatory Bail, Regular Bail and the ‘Bail not Jail’ Doctrine

Bail is the rule and jail the exception, a principle reiterated in State of Rajasthan v. Balchand (1977) and reinforced by the Supreme Court in Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab (1980). The Code of Criminal Procedure distinguishes three principal regimes: regular bail under Sections 437 and 439, anticipatory bail under Section 438, and bail for persons already convicted pending appeal under Section 389. Section 437 applies to non‑bailable offences triable by a Magistrate; the court may grant bail if it is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing the accused is not guilty of a non‑bailable offence or that the offence is not punishable with death or life imprisonment. Section 439 confers wider powers on the Sessions Court and the High Court, allowing them to impose conditions deemed necessary.

Anticipatory bail under Section 438 is a pre‑arrest remedy available to a person who apprehends arrest on accusation of a non‑bailable offence. The Supreme Court in Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of Maharashtra (2011) held that the power is of an extraordinary character and must be exercised sparingly, with the court recording reasons for granting or refusing it. The 2014 judgment in Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar introduced safeguards against routine arrests, directing police to issue a notice under Section 41A CrPC before arrest in offences punishable up to seven years, thereby reducing the need for anticipatory bail in many cases. The “bail not jail” doctrine was further crystallised in the 2022 Supreme Court guidelines (in Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI), which mandated that bail applications be decided within a fixed time‑frame and that conditions should not be so onerous as to defeat the purpose of bail.

Conditions commonly imposed include surrender of passport, regular reporting to a police station, prohibition on tampering with evidence, and furnishing of surety. The court may also require the accused to cooperate with the investigation. Failure to comply entitles the prosecution to move for cancellation of bail under Section 437(5) or 439(2). The distinction between anticipatory and regular bail is crucial: anticipatory bail operates before arrest and can be granted even when the investigation is at a nascent stage, whereas regular bail presupposes custody. In examination answers, candidates should map the statutory provision, the judicial test (reasonable grounds, likelihood of tampering, flight risk), and the leading precedent that governs the discretion.

Compounding of Offences: Statutory Framework, Judicial Discretion and Limits

Section 320 of the Code of Criminal Procedure enumerates offences that may be compounded, dividing them into two tables. Table I lists offences compoundable without court permission (e.g., hurt under Section 323 IPC, criminal breach of trust under Section 406 IPC), while Table II lists offences compoundable only with the permission of the court (e.g., voluntarily causing grievous hurt under Section 325 IPC, theft under Section 379 IPC). The effect of a valid compounding is an acquittal of the accused, as held in State of Madhya Pradesh v. Madan Lal (1997). The Supreme Court in B. S. Joshi v. State of Haryana (2003) clarified that the power to permit compounding under Table II is discretionary; the court must satisfy itself that the compromise is genuine, voluntary, and not opposed to public policy.

Certain offences are expressly non‑compoundable irrespective of the tables: offences against the State (Chapter VI IPC), offences relating to the Army, Navy and Air Force, and heinous crimes such as murder (Section 302), rape (Section 376), and dacoity (Section 395). The 2013 Criminal Law (Amendment) Act added Section 326A (acid attack) and Section 326B (attempted acid attack) to the non‑compoundable list. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2003), the Court held that even where an offence is compoundable, the court may refuse permission if the compromise appears to be a device to shield a powerful accused from prosecution.

Recent jurisprudence has examined the compounding of offences under Section 498A IPC (cruelty by husband or relatives). In Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. Union of India (2018), the Supreme Court directed that Section 498A cases should not be compounded mechanically; the court must scrutinise the voluntariness of the settlement and the possibility of coercion. The 2021 judgment in K. Srinivas Rao v. D. A. Deepa reiterated that while the parties may settle matrimonial disputes, the court retains the gatekeeping function to prevent misuse. Aspirants should be able to distinguish between the two tables, articulate the court’s discretionary test, and identify the statutory bars that render certain offences absolutely non‑compoundable.

The Right to Speedy Trial and Fair Trial under Article 21 and the Code of Criminal Procedure

Article 21 of the Constitution guarantees the right to life and personal liberty, which the Supreme Court has expansively interpreted to include the right to a speedy trial. In Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979), the Court declared that “speedy trial is an essential part of the fundamental right to life and liberty” and issued directions for the release of undertrial prisoners who had been detained beyond the maximum possible sentence. The principle was reinforced in Abdul Rehman Antulay v. R.S. Nayak (1992), where the Court held that the right to speedy trial is not a mere procedural formality but a substantive guarantee that cannot be waived by the accused.

The Code of Criminal Procedure contains several provisions aimed at preventing undue delay. Section 309 empowers the court to postpone or adjourn proceedings only for sufficient cause, and mandates that the trial be conducted on a day‑to‑day basis as far as practicable. Section 311 allows the court to summon any person as a witness or examine any person in attendance, ensuring that evidence is not stalled. Section 317 permits the trial to proceed in the absence of the accused if he persistently absents himself, while Section 318 provides for trial in absentia under strict safeguards. Section 319 enables the court to proceed against other persons appearing to be guilty during the course of an inquiry or trial.

The Supreme Court’s 2018 directions in the “Speedy Trial” suo motu case (Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 1 of 2017) prescribed time‑frames for different categories of cases: six months for summary trials, one year for sessions trials, and two years for cases involving complex evidence. The Court also mandated the establishment of “Fast Track Courts” and the use of case‑management software to monitor progress. While these directions are not statutory, they have been treated as binding on the subordinate judiciary. In examination answers, candidates should link the constitutional mandate (Article 21) to the procedural tools (Sections 309, 311, 317‑319) and cite the landmark judgments that translate the abstract right into concrete judicial management.

Expert Evidence, Scientific Tests and the Admissibility Standards under Sections 45–51 of the Evidence Act

Sections 45 to 51 of the Indian Evidence Act govern the admissibility of expert opinion. Section 45 permits the court to rely on the opinion of persons specially skilled in foreign law, science, art, handwriting, or finger impressions. Section 45A extends this to electronic records. The expert must be qualified by education, training, or experience, and the opinion must be based on reliable principles and methods. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Damu (2000) laid down a three‑pronged test: (i) the field must be one where expert assistance is needed; (ii) the witness must possess sufficient expertise; (iii) the opinion must be based on a reliable methodology.

Scientific tests such as DNA profiling, narco‑analysis, brain‑mapping, and polygraph examination have been subjected to rigorous scrutiny. In Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010), the Court held that narco‑analysis, brain‑mapping and lie‑detector tests constitute “testimonial compulsion” and violate Article 20(3); their results cannot be used as confessions under Section 27, though information discovered as a direct consequence may be admitted. DNA evidence, by contrast, has been accepted as reliable when the chain of custody is intact and the laboratory follows accredited protocols, as affirmed in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2003) and later in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) regarding privacy safeguards.

The admissibility of brain‑mapping and functional MRI results remains unsettled. The 2020 judgment in State of Gujarat v. Kishanbhai observed that while such techniques may assist investigation, they do not meet the threshold of “general acceptance” in the scientific community required for judicial notice. Courts therefore treat them as corroborative rather than substantive evidence. Section 46 allows the court to call for the production of the expert’s report, and Section 47 permits the cross‑examination of the expert on the basis of his report. Section 48‑51 deal with the opinion of handwriting experts, electronic signatures, and the relevance of character evidence. Aspirants must be able to articulate the distinction between “scientific reliability” and “legal admissibility”, cite the controlling precedents, and apply the three‑pronged test to any novel forensic technique.
