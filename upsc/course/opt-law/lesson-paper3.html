Technique: Culpable Homicide vs Murder
Question: 'A strikes B with a stick on the head. B dies. Is A guilty of murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder?'
Answer: Depends on intention/knowledge. If A intended to cause death → murder (s.300). If A intended to cause bodily injury likely to cause death → murder (s.300). If A intended bodily injury sufficient in ordinary course to cause death → murder (s.300). If act imminently dangerous and likely to cause death or bodily injury, with knowledge → murder (s.300). If none of these, but culpable homicide (s.299) → culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s.299). Key: intention/knowledge of death.

Common Traps

  • Confusing culpable homicide with murder - all murders are culpable homicide but not vice versa.
  • Confusing theft with robbery - robbery requires violence or threat of violence (putting in fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint).
  • Confusing robbery with dacoity - dacoity requires 5 or more persons committing robbery.
  • Confusing wrongful restraint with wrongful confinement - wrongful restraint prevents movement in one direction, wrongful confinement prevents movement in all directions within circumscribing limits.
  • Missing that Section 300 contains exceptions (grave and sudden provocation, private defence, sudden fight, consent) that reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder.
  • Confusing confession (admission of guilt) with admission (statement suggesting inference about fact in issue).
  • Missing that confession to police officer (s.25) is inadmissible, but confession while in custody (s.26) is admissible only if made in presence of Magistrate.

Memory Tricks

  • Stages of crime: Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission.
  • Offences against property: Theft → Extortion (threat) → Robbery (violence/fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint) → Dacoity (5+ persons).
  • Offences against body: Hurt → Grievous Hurt → Culpable Homicide → Murder (with intent/intention to cause death) → Death by negligence (Section 304A).
  • Writs: Habeas Corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop lower court), Certiorari (transfer case), Quo Warranto (challenge to public office).

Distinction Between 'Attempt' and 'Preparation': The Doctrine of Proximity

In criminal law, the line between preparation and attempt is often the most difficult for students to draw because it involves evaluating subjective intention against objective acts. Section 511 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC) punishes attempts to commit offences punishable with imprisonment of up to seven years or more. However, mere preparation does not attract punishment under IPC Section 43 (which covers preparation to commit murder). The critical legal test is the doctrine of proximity. This doctrine posits that an act becomes punishable as an attempt only when it is so closely connected with the consummation of the crime that it can be said to be a step in its direct execution, rather than merely laying the groundwork for it.

Consider the classic scenario: A buys poison and keeps it at home intending to kill B. This is preparation; no offence is committed until A takes the poison to B's house or administers it. If A goes to B's house but is caught on the way, courts often view this as preparation unless specific acts showing immediate intent to administer are evident. However, if A enters B's room, pours the poison into the water jug, and leaves before B drinks, the act has crossed the threshold of preparation. The proximity test asks: Would the crime have been committed without further intervention? If the answer is yes, it is an attempt.

The Supreme Court in Ramesh Prakash v. State of Madhya Pradesh clarified that mere possession of weapons or planning does not constitute an attempt. The act must be one of commission towards the specific crime. For instance, lighting a fire in a forest to burn down a village is preparation if done from a distance; but pouring oil and striking a match near the target structure constitutes an attempt to commit arson (IPC Section 435). The distinction is vital because while murder carries life imprisonment or death, attempted murder carries only up to seven years. Similarly, theft (IPC Section 379) has a lower maximum penalty than robbery; attempting robbery attracts punishment under Section 511 read with Section 302/323, but the quantum of sentence depends on the gravity of the attempt.

Distinction Between Attempt and Abandonment

A crucial sub-topic is abandonment. If an offender voluntarily abandons their criminal purpose before completing the act, they are not guilty of attempt. However, mere cessation due to fear of arrest or external hindrance does not count as abandonment. In Ramchandra v. State of Maharashtra, the Court held that stopping because one realized the police were nearby was not voluntary abandonment; it remained an attempt. Conversely, in Krishna Prasad v. State of Madhya Pradesh, the Supreme Court noted that if a person realizes their plan is impossible to execute and stops, it might be considered abandonment, though this is rarely accepted in modern jurisprudence unless the impossibility is absolute.

The legal consequence is significant: Attempt requires proof of intention (mens rea) plus an act proximate to the result. Preparation requires only intention; no specific act towards commission is needed beyond planning. This distinction ensures that individuals are punished for dangerous conduct that moves beyond mere thought, balancing liberty with public safety.

Examiner's trap
Question: 'A enters a bank vault intending to steal but is caught by the security guard before touching any money. Is A guilty of attempted theft or preparation?' Solution: This is attempted theft. Entering the vault with intent to steal is the commencement of the offence. The act of entering the secured area (proximity) distinguishes it from merely buying a crowbar at a hardware store (preparation). The key is whether the offender has commenced the commission of the crime.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Dangerous Proximity' Test
When answering questions on attempt vs. preparation, always structure your answer in three steps: 1) Define the statutory provision (IPC Section 43 for preparation, Section 511 for attempt). 2) Explain the 'proximity test' using the concept of 'direct commencement'. 3) Apply to facts by asking: 'Did the act bring the offender closer to the result than mere planning?' Always cite Ramesh Prakash or Krishna Prasad if discussing abandonment. Avoid generic statements; use specific examples like 'buying poison' vs. 'pouring poison'. Remember that under IPC Section 511, punishment for attempt is generally half the maximum sentence prescribed for the completed offence, unless the law specifies otherwise.

Distinction Between Joint Liability and Common Intention: The Role of Preparation

The concept of preparation plays a subtle but critical role in determining joint liability under IPC Sections 149 (Unlawful Assembly) and Section 34 (Common Intention). When two or more persons prepare for a crime together, they are often deemed to have formed a common intention. The distinction lies in the timing and nature of the preparation. If A and B agree to rob a shop, buy masks, and plan the route, but only A executes the robbery because B is caught at the scene, both may be liable for attempted robbery if the preparation was part of a joint conspiracy.

However, mere preparation does not automatically create joint liability. If A prepares to kill B independently, and C happens to see it but does nothing, C is not jointly liable unless C actively participates in the preparation or encourages the act. The Supreme Court in Narain Singh v. State of Bihar emphasized that for joint liability to attach, there must be a meeting of minds (common intention) at the time of the crime or during the preparatory stage leading directly to it.

This distinction is vital in cases of conspiracy versus preparation. Conspiracy involves an agreement to commit a crime; preparation involves acts done in furtherance of that agreement. If A and B conspire to burn a building, and A buys petrol while B buys matches, both are guilty of preparation to commit arson (IPC Section 43). If they then set the fire, both are guilty of arson. The law treats preparation as an integral part of the conspiracy if done in furtherance of the agreed plan.

The legal consequence is that joint liability extends to all acts done by any member of the unlawful assembly or common intention group during the course of the crime. Thus, if A and B conspire to rob, and A uses a weapon to threaten the victim while B takes money, both are liable for robbery even if only one used violence. This principle prevents offenders from escaping liability by claiming they did not personally commit every act.

The Role of Preparation in Defences: Private Defence and Exemption

Preparation also interacts with defences such as private defence (IPC Sections 96-106). If an offender prepares to attack, the victim's right to private defence arises only when there is a reasonable apprehension of imminent harm. Mere preparation by the aggressor does not justify immediate lethal force; the threat must be present and impending.
