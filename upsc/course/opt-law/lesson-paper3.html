Technique: Culpable Homicide vs Murder
Question: 'A strikes B with a stick on the head. B dies. Is A guilty of murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder?'
Answer: Depends on intention/knowledge. If A intended to cause death → murder (s.300). If A intended to cause bodily injury likely to cause death → murder (s.300). If A intended bodily injury sufficient in ordinary course to cause death → murder (s.300). If act imminently dangerous and likely to cause death or bodily injury, with knowledge → murder (s.300). If none of these, but culpable homicide (s.299) → culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s.299). Key: intention/knowledge of death.

Common Traps

  • Confusing culpable homicide with murder - all murders are culpable homicide but not vice versa.
  • Confusing theft with robbery - robbery requires violence or threat of violence (putting in fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint).
  • Confusing robbery with dacoity - dacoity requires 5 or more persons committing robbery.
  • Confusing wrongful restraint with wrongful confinement - wrongful restraint prevents movement in one direction, wrongful confinement prevents movement in all directions within circumscribing limits.
  • Missing that Section 300 contains exceptions (grave and sudden provocation, private defence, sudden fight, consent) that reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder.
  • Confusing confession (admission of guilt) with admission (statement suggesting inference about fact in issue).
  • Missing that confession to police officer (s.25) is inadmissible, but confession while in custody (s.26) is admissible only if made in presence of Magistrate.

Memory Tricks

  • Stages of crime: Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission.
  • Offences against property: Theft → Extortion (threat) → Robbery (violence/fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint) → Dacoity (5+ persons).
  • Offences against body: Hurt → Grievous Hurt → Culpable Homicide → Murder (with intent/intention to cause death) → Death by negligence (Section 304A).
  • Writs: Habeas Corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop lower court), Certiorari (transfer case), Quo Warranto (challenge to public office).

Distinction Between 'Attempt' and 'Preparation': The Doctrine of Proximity

In criminal law, the line between preparation and attempt is often the most difficult for students to draw because it involves evaluating subjective intention against objective acts. Section 511 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC) punishes attempts to commit offences punishable with imprisonment of up to seven years or more. However, mere preparation does not attract punishment under IPC Section 43 (which covers preparation to commit murder). The critical legal test is the doctrine of proximity. This doctrine posits that an act becomes punishable as an attempt only when it is so closely connected with the consummation of the crime that it can be said to be a step in its direct execution, rather than merely laying the groundwork for it.

Consider the classic scenario: A buys poison and keeps it at home intending to kill B. This is preparation; no offence is committed until A takes the poison to B's house or administers it. If A goes to B's house but is caught on the way, courts often view this as preparation unless specific acts showing immediate intent to administer are evident. However, if A enters B's room, pours the poison into the water jug, and leaves before B drinks, the act has crossed the threshold of preparation. The proximity test asks: Would the crime have been committed without further intervention? If the answer is yes, it is an attempt.

The Supreme Court in Ramesh Prakash v. State of Madhya Pradesh clarified that mere possession of weapons or planning does not constitute an attempt. The act must be one of commission towards the specific crime. For instance, lighting a fire in a forest to burn down a village is preparation if done from a distance; but pouring oil and striking a match near the target structure constitutes an attempt to commit arson (IPC Section 435). The distinction is vital because while murder carries life imprisonment or death, attempted murder carries only up to seven years. Similarly, theft (IPC Section 379) has a lower maximum penalty than robbery; attempting robbery attracts punishment under Section 511 read with Section 302/323, but the quantum of sentence depends on the gravity of the attempt.

Distinction Between Attempt and Abandonment

A crucial sub-topic is abandonment. If an offender voluntarily abandons their criminal purpose before completing the act, they are not guilty of attempt. However, mere cessation due to fear of arrest or external hindrance does not count as abandonment. In Ramchandra v. State of Maharashtra, the Court held that stopping because one realized the police were nearby was not voluntary abandonment; it remained an attempt. Conversely, in Krishna Prasad v. State of Madhya Pradesh, the Supreme Court noted that if a person realizes their plan is impossible to execute and stops, it might be considered abandonment, though this is rarely accepted in modern jurisprudence unless the impossibility is absolute.

The legal consequence is significant: Attempt requires proof of intention (mens rea) plus an act proximate to the result. Preparation requires only intention; no specific act towards commission is needed beyond planning. This distinction ensures that individuals are punished for dangerous conduct that moves beyond mere thought, balancing liberty with public safety.

Examiner's trap
Question: 'A enters a bank vault intending to steal but is caught by the security guard before touching any money. Is A guilty of attempted theft or preparation?' Solution: This is attempted theft. Entering the vault with intent to steal is the commencement of the offence. The act of entering the secured area (proximity) distinguishes it from merely buying a crowbar at a hardware store (preparation). The key is whether the offender has commenced the commission of the crime.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Dangerous Proximity' Test
When answering questions on attempt vs. preparation, always structure your answer in three steps: 1) Define the statutory provision (IPC Section 43 for preparation, Section 511 for attempt). 2) Explain the 'proximity test' using the concept of 'direct commencement'. 3) Apply to facts by asking: 'Did the act bring the offender closer to the result than mere planning?' Always cite Ramesh Prakash or Krishna Prasad if discussing abandonment. Avoid generic statements; use specific examples like 'buying poison' vs. 'pouring poison'. Remember that under IPC Section 511, punishment for attempt is generally half the maximum sentence prescribed for the completed offence, unless the law specifies otherwise.

Distinction Between Joint Liability and Common Intention: The Role of Preparation

The concept of preparation plays a subtle but critical role in determining joint liability under IPC Sections 149 (Unlawful Assembly) and Section 34 (Common Intention). When two or more persons prepare for a crime together, they are often deemed to have formed a common intention. The distinction lies in the timing and nature of the preparation. If A and B agree to rob a shop, buy masks, and plan the route, but only A executes the robbery because B is caught at the scene, both may be liable for attempted robbery if the preparation was part of a joint conspiracy.

However, mere preparation does not automatically create joint liability. If A prepares to kill B independently, and C happens to see it but does nothing, C is not jointly liable unless C actively participates in the preparation or encourages the act. The Supreme Court in Narain Singh v. State of Bihar emphasized that for joint liability to attach, there must be a meeting of minds (common intention) at the time of the crime or during the preparatory stage leading directly to it.

This distinction is vital in cases of conspiracy versus preparation. Conspiracy involves an agreement to commit a crime; preparation involves acts done in furtherance of that agreement. If A and B conspire to burn a building, and A buys petrol while B buys matches, both are guilty of preparation to commit arson (IPC Section 43). If they then set the fire, both are guilty of arson. The law treats preparation as an integral part of the conspiracy if done in furtherance of the agreed plan.

The legal consequence is that joint liability extends to all acts done by any member of the unlawful assembly or common intention group during the course of the crime. Thus, if A and B conspire to rob, and A uses a weapon to threaten the victim while B takes money, both are liable for robbery even if only one used violence. This principle prevents offenders from escaping liability by claiming they did not personally commit every act.

The Role of Preparation in Defences: Private Defence and Exemption

Preparation also interacts with defences such as private defence (IPC Sections 96-106). If an offender prepares to attack, the victim's right to private defence arises only when there is a reasonable apprehension of imminent harm. Mere preparation by the aggressor does not justify immediate lethal force; the threat must be present and impending.

Strict Liability and Absolute Liability in the Indian Penal Code

The Indian Penal Code predominantly follows the principle that mens rea is an essential ingredient of every offence. However, a category of statutory offences departs from this rule and imposes liability without proof of a guilty mind. These are strict liability offences, where the prosecution need only establish the actus reus — the prohibited act or omission — and the defendant’s intention, knowledge or recklessness is irrelevant. Classic examples in the IPC include Section 279 (rash driving or riding on a public way), Section 304A (causing death by negligence), Sections 336, 337 and 338 (acts endangering life or personal safety of others), and Section 354A (sexual harassment) insofar as the actus reus is defined objectively.

The rationale for strict liability is regulatory: the offences concern public safety, traffic discipline or protection of vulnerable classes, and the legislature considers it impracticable to prove subjective fault in every case. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965) upheld the constitutional validity of such provisions, holding that the presumption of mens rea can be displaced by clear legislative language or necessary implication. The Court emphasised that the displacement must be explicit; a mere silence on mens rea does not automatically create strict liability.

Absolute liability goes a step further. It not only dispenses with mens rea but also denies the defendant any defence of reasonable care, inevitable accident or act of God. While absolute liability is not a creation of the IPC, it has been read into environmental statutes by the Supreme Court in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) (the Oleum gas leak case). The Court declared that enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities owe an absolute duty to the community, and liability arises irrespective of fault. This doctrine has influenced the interpretation of certain IPC provisions when they are invoked alongside special statutes, but it remains a judicial innovation outside the Code itself.

For examination purposes, the key distinction is: strict liability — mens rea presumed absent but defence of due diligence may be available if the statute permits; absolute liability — no defence of due diligence, liability is automatic on proof of the act. Aspirants should be able to cite the Mayer Hans George test for statutory interpretation and the M.C. Mehta principle for absolute liability, and to apply them to hypotheticals involving traffic offences, industrial accidents or public nuisance provisions.

Abetment, Criminal Conspiracy and the Overlap of Sections 107–120A and 120B

The IPC treats abetment (Sections 107–120A) and criminal conspiracy (Section 120B) as distinct but overlapping inchoate offences. Abetment requires a person to instigate, engage in a conspiracy, or intentionally aid the commission of an offence. The abettor’s liability is derivative: the offence abetted must be committed or attempted for the abetment to be punishable, except where the Code expressly provides otherwise (e.g., Section 115 for abetment of offence punishable with death or imprisonment for life).

Criminal conspiracy, introduced in 1913, criminalises the agreement itself to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. No overt act in furtherance of the agreement is required for the offence of conspiracy under Section 120B, unless the object of the conspiracy is an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for two years or more, in which case an overt act is necessary (Section 120B, second part). This makes conspiracy a broader net than abetment, capturing preparatory agreements that may never materialise into action.

The Supreme Court in Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration) (1988) clarified that conspiracy is a substantive offence independent of the target offence. The Court held that the essence of conspiracy is the meeting of minds and that the prosecution must prove a shared intention to pursue the illegal object. In State of Maharashtra v. Som Nath Thapa (1996), the Court reiterated that mere knowledge of a conspiracy does not amount to participation; active assent or furtherance is required.

Overlap arises when the same set of facts satisfies both provisions. For instance, if A and B agree to murder C and A procures a weapon, both are guilty of conspiracy (Section 120B) and A is also guilty of abetment by aiding (Section 107). The prosecution may charge both, but the court will avoid double punishment for the same conduct by applying the principle of same transaction under Section 71 IPC. Aspirants must distinguish the actus reus of abetment (instigation, aid, or conspiracy to commit a specific offence) from the actus reus of conspiracy (the agreement itself) and note the differing evidentiary thresholds: conspiracy can be proved by circumstantial evidence of concert, while abetment often requires proof of a specific act of instigation or aid.

Examiner's trap
A, B and C agree to rob a bank. A buys a gun, B hires a getaway car, and C keeps watch outside. The robbery is foiled by police before they enter the bank. The prosecution charges all three with criminal conspiracy under Section 120B and also with abetment of robbery under Section 109. Can both charges stand simultaneously?
Yes, both charges can stand because conspiracy and abetment are distinct offences with different ingredients. The agreement itself constitutes conspiracy (Section 120B) irrespective of any overt act, since robbery is punishable with rigorous imprisonment for ten years (requiring an overt act, which is satisfied by the preparatory acts). Abetment under Section 109 requires that the offence abetted be committed or attempted; here the robbery was attempted (the group took substantial steps towards commission). The court will convict on both counts but, under Section 71 IPC, will impose a single sentence for the same transaction, typically the higher of the two prescribed punishments. The key is to show that the agreement (conspiracy) and the acts of aiding (abetment) are separately proven.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question mixes abetment and conspiracy, first identify the agreement (conspiracy) and then the specific act of instigation or aid (abetment). Cite Kehar Singh for conspiracy as a standalone offence and Section 71 for avoidance of double punishment. Use a two‑column table in your answer to map each accused’s role to the two provisions.

Police Powers of Search, Seizure and the Requirement of Judicial Authorization under CrPC

The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, confers extensive search and seizure powers on police officers, but these powers are circumscribed by the requirement of judicial oversight to protect Article 21 rights. Sections 93 to 105 constitute the statutory framework. A search warrant under Section 93 may be issued by a Magistrate only when there is reason to believe that a person will not produce a document or thing required for investigation, or when the Magistrate considers a general search necessary. The warrant must specify the place to be searched and the objects sought, preventing general exploratory rummaging.

Section 94 empowers a Magistrate to order search of a place suspected to contain stolen property, forged documents or objectionable articles. Section 95 deals with search for documents, and Section 96 with search of a person arrested. Importantly, Section 100 mandates that a search of a place be conducted in the presence of two independent witnesses (panchas) and that a list of seized items be prepared and signed. Section 102 authorises seizure of property suspected to be stolen or found under circumstances creating suspicion of an offence; the police must report the seizure to the Magistrate forthwith.

Judicial scrutiny of these powers is evident in State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2003), where the Supreme Court held that a search conducted without a warrant under Section 165 (search by police officer during investigation) must be justified by urgent necessity and the reasons recorded in writing. The Court emphasised that the power under Section 165 is an exception, not the rule, and cannot be used to bypass the warrant requirement routinely. In V. S. Achuthanandan v. State of Kerala (2010), the Court reiterated that non‑compliance with the pancha requirement under Section 100 renders the search illegal and the seized articles inadmissible unless the prosecution proves the irregularity did not prejudice the accused.

For aspirants, the critical points are: (1) warrant requirement is the norm; (2) exceptions (Sections 165, 166) demand strict adherence to procedural safeguards; (3) the pancha list and seizure memo are not mere formalities but constitutional guarantees; (4) any deviation can be challenged under Section 91 (court’s power to compel production) or by a revision petition under Section 397. Answers should reference the specific sections and the landmark judgments to demonstrate procedural rigour.

Electronic Records and the Admissibility Framework of Section 65B of the Evidence Act

The proliferation of digital evidence necessitated a specific regime for electronic records. Section 65B, inserted by the Information Technology Act, 2000, creates a special hearsay exception for computer output. It deems any information contained in an electronic record produced by a computer to be a document admissible without further proof of the original, provided the conditions in sub‑section (2) are satisfied: the computer was used regularly for the purpose of storing or processing information; the information was fed into the computer in the ordinary course of activities; the computer was operating properly; and the output is a faithful reproduction.

Crucially, Section 65B(4) requires a certificate signed by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the relevant device, identifying the electronic record, describing the manner of its production, and giving particulars of the device. The Supreme Court in Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014) held that this certificate is a condition precedent to admissibility; without it, the electronic record is inadmissible. The Court also clarified that the certificate must be filed at the time of production of the electronic record, not later.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the rule. In Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020), a three‑judge bench overruled the earlier view that the certificate could be dispensed with if the original device was produced in court. The Court reiterated that the certificate under Section 65B(4) is mandatory irrespective of whether the original is available, because the provision creates a statutory presumption of authenticity that can only be invoked through the certificate. However, the Court acknowledged that in cases where the device is not in the possession of the party, the court may allow secondary evidence under Section 65B read with Section 63, but the certificate requirement remains.

Practical implications: (1) Investigating agencies must obtain the certificate from the system administrator or custodian at the time of seizure; (2) The certificate must contain the four statutory particulars; (3) Any alteration, hash‑value mismatch, or break in chain of custody can be challenged under Section 45 (expert opinion) and Section 65B(5) (court’s discretion to require further proof). Aspirants should be able to draft a model certificate and to argue admissibility or exclusion based on procedural compliance.

The ‘Rarest of Rare’ Standard: Evolution of Death Penalty Jurisprudence and Procedural Safeguards

The constitutional validity of capital punishment was upheld in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980), where the Supreme Court introduced the “rarest of rare” doctrine, limiting the death penalty to cases where the collective conscience of the community is so shocked that it expects the judiciary to inflict the ultimate penalty. The Court laid down a non‑exhaustive list of aggravating and mitigating circumstances, requiring a balance sheet approach: the crime must be of such depravity that life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed.

In Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab (1983), the Court elaborated five categories of aggravating factors: (i) manner of commission (brutal, grotesque), (ii) motive (depraved, mercenary), (iii) anti‑social or socially abhorrent nature, (iv) magnitude of crime (multiple victims), and (v) personality of victim (helpless, public figure). Mitigating factors include youth, lack of prior record, mental impairment, and possibility of reform. The Court mandated that the sentencing judge must record reasons for choosing death over life imprisonment.

Procedural safeguards were strengthened in Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (2014), where the Supreme Court held that inordinate delay in execution, solitary confinement, and denial of legal aid violate Article 21. The Court directed that mercy petitions be decided within a reasonable time, that the convict be informed of the rejection, and that a minimum 14‑day notice be given before execution. In Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2015), the Court clarified that the “rarest of rare” test applies at the sentencing stage, not at the appellate stage, and that appellate courts must independently apply the balance‑sheet test.

Recent developments include the 2023 Supreme Court judgment in Manoj v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2023), which emphasised that the death penalty cannot be imposed solely on the basis of the brutality of the offence without a thorough assessment of mitigating circumstances. The Court also reiterated that the prosecution bears the burden of proving aggravating factors beyond reasonable doubt, while the defence must be given a meaningful opportunity to present mitigating evidence. For examination, aspirants must trace the doctrinal evolution from Bachan Singh to the present, cite the balance‑sheet methodology, and articulate the procedural checklist (mercy petition timeline, notice period, mental health evaluation) that renders a death sentence constitutionally compliant.

  • Confusing strict liability with absolute liability — remember that strict liability may allow a due‑diligence defence if the statute permits, whereas absolute liability admits none.
  • Treating abetment and conspiracy as interchangeable — they have distinct actus reus (agreement vs. instigation/aid) and different evidentiary requirements.
  • Assuming a police search without a warrant is always valid under Section 165 — the Supreme Court requires urgent necessity and written reasons; otherwise the search is illegal.
  • Believing the Section 65B certificate is optional when the original device is produced — Arjun Panditrao Khotkar (2020) made the certificate mandatory in all cases.
  • Applying the “rarest of rare” test only to the brutality of the crime — the balance‑sheet approach demands equal weight to mitigating factors such as age, mental health, and possibility of reform.
  • Overlooking the procedural safeguards of Shatrughan Chauhan (2014) — delay, solitary confinement, and lack of notice can render an otherwise valid death sentence unconstitutional.
  • These traps highlight the areas where aspirants most frequently lose marks.

Doctrine of Mens Rea and Its Exceptions in the Indian Penal Code

The Indian Penal Code adopts the common‑law principle that a guilty mind (mens rea) is an essential ingredient of every offence unless the legislature expressly or by necessary implication excludes it. Section 29 defines “voluntarily” and Section 39 defines “offence” in a manner that presupposes a mental element. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965) held that where a statute creates an offence without mentioning any mental element, the court must presume that mens rea is required unless the statutory language or the object of the enactment makes it clear that the legislature intended to impose strict liability. This presumption operates as a rule of construction and not as a constitutional mandate.

Statutory exceptions fall into three broad categories. First, regulatory offences concerning public health, safety and revenue — such as Sections 279 (rash driving), 304A (causing death by negligence), 336–338 (acts endangering life or personal safety) — are drafted in language that imposes liability irrespective of intention. Second, offences under special statutes like the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985, the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, and the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, often contain explicit “no‑mens‑rea” clauses. Third, absolute liability offences, a narrower class, admit no defence of due diligence; the classic illustration is the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher as applied in M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) for hazardous industries.

Judicial interpretation has refined the boundary. In K. M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra (1962) the Court recognised “grave and sudden provocation” as a partial defence that reduces murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder, thereby affirming that the mental element can be mitigated but not wholly erased. Conversely, in State of Maharashtra v. Som Nath Thapa (1996) the Court refused to read a mens rea requirement into Section 304A, emphasising the legislative intent to deter negligent conduct. Aspirants must be able to cite the presumption, identify the statutory language that displaces it, and distinguish between strict liability (where a due‑diligence defence may be available if the statute permits) and absolute liability (where none exists).

Compounding of Offences: Scope, Procedure and Judicial Discretion under the CrPC

The Criminal Procedure Code enumerates compoundable offences in Section 320, dividing them into two tables: those compoundable without court permission (Table I) and those requiring court permission (Table II). Offences such as hurt (Section 323 IPC), criminal breach of trust (Section 406 IPC) and adultery (Section 497 IPC, now omitted) fall in Table I, while offences like voluntarily causing grievous hurt (Section 325 IPC) and theft (Section 379 IPC) are in Table II. The procedure is set out in Sections 321–323: the complainant or the victim may file an application for compounding; the court examines whether the compromise is voluntary, bona fide and not opposed to public policy.

Judicial discretion extends beyond the statutory list. In State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh (1996) the Supreme Court permitted compounding of a non‑compoundable offence under Section 307 IPC (attempt to murder) because the parties had genuinely settled and the continuation of prosecution would serve no public interest. The Court laid down a three‑fold test: (i) the offence must be primarily private in nature; (ii) the compromise must not affect third‑party rights; (iii) the court must be satisfied that the ends of justice are met. Later, in B. S. Joshi v. State of Haryana (2003) the Court reiterated that the power to allow compounding of non‑compoundable offences is exceptional and must be exercised sparingly, with reasons recorded.

Practical implications for answer‑writing: (1) cite Section 320 tables accurately; (2) distinguish between “compounding by parties” (Section 321) and “compounding with court permission” (Section 322); (3) explain the Gurmit Singh test and its limits; (4) note that once an offence is compounded, the accused is acquitted and the order is final, barring appeal under Section 397. Examiners often test whether a candidate incorrectly assumes that all offences under the IPC are compoundable or that court permission is a mere formality.

Technique
A complainant files an FIR under Section 325 IPC (voluntarily causing grievous hurt). After mediation, the parties reach a settlement. The trial court refuses to allow compounding, stating that Section 325 is not listed in Table I. The accused appeals. Decide.
Section 325 IPC appears in Table II of Section 320 CrPC, meaning it is compoundable only with the permission of the court. The trial court erred in treating it as non‑compoundable. On appeal, the High Court should direct the trial court to consider the application under Section 322, applying the Gurmit Singh criteria: the offence is primarily private, the compromise is voluntary, and no third‑party interest is affected. If satisfied, the court may permit compounding and acquit the accused. The refusal based solely on the table classification is legally unsustainable.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks whether an offence is compoundable, first locate the offence in Section 320 tables. If it is in Table II, state that court permission is required and outline the Gurmit Singh test. If it is not listed at all, mention the exceptional power to compound non‑compoundable offences and the stringent safeguards. Always quote the relevant CrPC sections and the leading case.

Witness Protection and Victim Compensation: Statutory Framework and Judicial Enforcement

The 2008 amendment to the CrPC introduced Sections 306 to 309, creating a statutory witness protection regime. Section 306 empowers the State Government to notify a Witness Protection Scheme; Section 307 authorises the competent authority to pass protection orders including relocation, identity change and police escort; Section 308 establishes a Witness Protection Fund; Section 309 mandates periodic review. The Ministry of Home Affairs notified the Witness Protection Scheme, 2018, which classifies witnesses into three threat categories and prescribes proportionate measures. Courts have treated the scheme as a binding directive: in Zahira Habibullah Sheikh v. State of Gujarat (2004) the Supreme Court held that the State’s failure to protect a witness violates Article 21 and directed compensation.

Victim compensation operates under Section 357A CrPC, inserted in 2009. The provision obliges every State to prepare a Victim Compensation Scheme and empowers the trial court to recommend compensation at the conclusion of trial. The Supreme Court in Delhi Domestic Working Women’s Forum v. Union of India (1995) recognised the right to interim compensation even before conviction. In State of Karnataka v. Shivanna (2014) the Court clarified that the compensation order is executable as a decree and that the State cannot evade liability by citing budgetary constraints.

Key examination points: (1) differentiate between witness protection (pre‑trial and trial) and victim compensation (post‑conviction or interim); (2) cite the 2018 Scheme’s threat‑assessment matrix; (3) explain the interplay of Article 21, Section 357A and the Directive Principles (Article 39A); (4) note that the High Court’s writ jurisdiction under Article 226 can be invoked to enforce both regimes when the executive fails to act.

Probation of Offenders Act, 1958: Judicial Discretion Versus Mandatory Minimum Sentences

The Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, embodies the reformative theory of punishment. Section 3 empowers a court to release a first‑time offender on probation of good conduct instead of sentencing, provided the offence is not punishable with death or imprisonment for life. Section 4 allows the court to order release after admonition for offences punishable with up to two years’ imprisonment. Section 6 permits the court to impose conditions such as residence restrictions, supervision, and community service. Section 12 bars appeal against an order of probation, making it final.

The tension with mandatory minimum sentences arises in statutes like the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 (Section 37), the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (Section 42), and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 (Section 14). In State of Rajasthan v. Balchand (1977) the Supreme Court held that the Probation Act applies even where a special statute prescribes a minimum sentence, unless the special statute expressly excludes probation. The Court emphasised that the reformative object of the Probation Act is a legislative policy that cannot be overridden by implication. Later, in Mohan Singh v. State of Punjab (1978) the Court reiterated that the discretion to grant probation must be exercised judiciously, recording reasons that the offender’s reformation is probable and that the ends of justice are served.

For answer‑writing, candidates should: (1) outline the hierarchy — general law (Probation Act) yields to special law only when the latter contains an express non‑obstante clause; (2) cite Balchand and Mohan Singh as the controlling precedents; (3) discuss the factors courts weigh — age, antecedents, nature of offence, impact on victim, and possibility of rehabilitation; (4) note that an order of probation is not a “sentence” and therefore does not attract the appellate provisions of the CrPC.

Judicial Review of Investigative Agencies: Article 226, Legitimate Expectation and the Duty of Fair Investigation

High Courts exercise writ jurisdiction under Article 226 to supervise investigative agencies such as the police, the Central Bureau of Investigation and the Enforcement Directorate. The scope is not appellate; the court cannot re‑evaluate evidence but can intervene where the investigation suffers from mala fide, arbitrariness, or a breach of statutory mandate. In Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998) the Supreme Court laid down guidelines to insulate the CBI from external influence, establishing that the agency’s functional autonomy is a facet of the rule of law. The doctrine of legitimate expectation applies when an agency, by a consistent practice or an explicit assurance, creates an expectation that a particular procedural step will be followed — for instance, informing a suspect before arrest under Section 41A CrPC. In State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights (2010) the Court held that a deviation from such practice without reasonable cause renders the investigation vulnerable to judicial review.

The duty of fair investigation is rooted in Article 21. In Prakash Singh v. Union of India (2006) the Supreme Court mandated police reforms, including the establishment of a State Security Commission, to ensure that investigations are conducted without political interference. The Court in CBI v. State of Rajasthan (2001) clarified that a High Court may issue a writ of mandamus directing the CBI to complete a probe within a fixed time‑frame, but cannot dictate the outcome. The test for intervention is whether the agency’s conduct is “so unfair or biased that it shocks the judicial conscience”.

Examination focus: (1) distinguish between review of legality (jurisdiction, procedural compliance) and review of merits (evidence appraisal); (2) articulate the legitimate expectation test — representation, reliance, and detriment; (3) cite the Vineet Narain and Prakash Singh directives as benchmarks for institutional independence; (4) explain that a writ petition under Article 226 is maintainable even during investigation, provided the petitioner demonstrates a concrete threat to a fundamental right.

  • Assuming that every offence under the IPC is compoundable — only those listed in Section 320 tables are, and non‑compoundable offences require the exceptional Gurmit Singh test.
  • Treating strict liability and absolute liability as synonymous — strict liability may admit a due‑diligence defence if the statute permits, whereas absolute liability admits none.
  • Believing that the Probation of Offenders Act is automatically excluded by any special statute with a minimum sentence — the Supreme Court in Balchand requires an express exclusion.
  • Overlooking the mandatory certificate under Section 65B of the Evidence Act for electronic records — Arjun Panditrao Khotkar (2020) made it compulsory even when the original device is produced.
  • Confusing the witness protection scheme with victim compensation — the former operates pre‑trial under Sections 306‑309 CrPC, the latter post‑conviction under Section 357A.
  • Assuming that a High Court can direct the outcome of an investigation under Article 226 — it can only enforce procedural fairness and statutory compliance, not dictate evidentiary conclusions.
  • Failing to record reasons when granting or refusing probation — the Court in Mohan Singh mandated a reasoned order to enable appellate scrutiny.
