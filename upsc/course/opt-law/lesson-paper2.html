Indian Penal Code & Criminal Procedure Code

  • Offences against the State: Waging war against India (121), waging war against any Asiatic power (123), sedition (124A), collecting arms (s.123), concealing design (s.124A).

Technique: IPC Sections answer
Question: 'What is the difference between culpable homicide and murder?'
Answer: Culpable homicide (Section 299): causing death by doing an act with intention of causing death, or with intention of causing bodily injury likely to cause death, or with knowledge that act is likely to cause death. Murder (Section 300): culpable homicide is murder if (a) intention to cause death, (b) intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death, (c) intention to cause bodily injury sufficient to cause death, (d) act imminently dangerous and likely to cause death, with knowledge. All murders are culpable homicide but all culpable homicides are not murder. Exceptions to murder: grave and sudden provocation, private defence, public servant acting in good faith, sudden fight, consent (death of person above 18 years who consents).

Common Traps

  • Confusing culpable homicide with murder - all murders are culpable homicide but not vice versa.
  • Confusing theft with robbery - robbery requires violence or threat of violence.
  • Confusing robbery with dacoity - dacoity requires 5 or more persons.
  • Confusing wrongful restraint with wrongful confinement - wrongful restraint prevents movement in one direction, wrongful confinement prevents movement in all directions.
  • Missing that Section 300 contains exceptions (grave and sudden provocation, private defence, etc.) that reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

Memory Tricks

  • Stages of crime: Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission.
  • Degrees of homicide: Culpable homicide → Murder (with intent/intention to cause death) → Death by negligence (Section 304A).
  • Offences against property: Theft → Extortion (threat) → Robbery (violence/fear) → Dacoity (5+ persons).
  • Writs: Habeas Corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop), Certiorari (transfer), Quo Warranto (authority).
Mains strategy
Criminal law answers: statutory provision (section number) → ingredients of offence → application to facts → case law. Quote landmark judgments: Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962 - Section 124A sedition), Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978 - Article 21), DK Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997 - arrest guidelines), DK Basu guidelines (arrest procedure), DK Basu guidelines (arrest memo), DK Basu (right to lawyer), Hussainara Khatoon (right to speedy trial), Hussainara Khatoon (right to legal aid), Hussainara Khatoon (right to free legal aid).

Constitutional Remedies: The Architecture of Rights Protection

The Indian Constitution provides specific mechanisms for the enforcement of rights and the adjudication of disputes between the State and its citizens, or between different organs of government. These are known as Constitutional Remedies, primarily housed in Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part V (Union Executive) alongside Articles 32 and 226. Article 32, often termed the "heart and soul" of the Constitution by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, empowers the Supreme Court to issue directions, orders, or writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. These writs are Habeas Corpus (Art. 32/226), Mandamus (Art. 32/226), Certiorari (Art. 32/226), Prohibition (Art. 32/226), and Quo Warranto (Art. 32).

Article 226 grants High Courts similar powers, though it is not limited strictly to Fundamental Rights; they can also enforce other legal rights or correct jurisdictional errors by lower courts. The distinction lies in the scope: while Article 32 is exclusive to the Supreme Court and mandatory for Fundamental Rights violations (unless Parliament has suspended it under Art. 32(4) - which it rarely does), Article 226 is a residual power available even when no Fundamental Right is infringed. This duality creates a robust two-tier judicial review system, ensuring that grievances are addressed at the most appropriate level of jurisdiction.

The evolution of these remedies has been shaped by landmark judgments. In Krishna Kanth Singh v. State of Bihar (1984), the Supreme Court clarified the scope of Article 226, stating that High Courts should not act as a substitute for the Supreme Court unless the latter is unable to provide relief. Conversely, in Rupa Ashok Hinduja v. Kanthi Chand Hinduja (2010), the Court held that Article 32 and 226 are complementary, not mutually exclusive; an aggrieved party can approach either forum. The concept of "Public Interest Litigation" (PIL) further expanded these remedies, allowing public-spirited individuals to file petitions on behalf of those unable to do so themselves, effectively democratizing access to justice.

The writ of Habeas Corpus is perhaps the most famous remedy, literally meaning "produce the body." It is a command to a person detaining another to bring the detainee before the court. In Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1979), the Supreme Court used this writ not just to secure physical liberty but also to enforce the right to a speedy trial and legal aid for undertrials detained for long periods without conviction. Similarly, the writ of Mandamus commands a public authority to perform its statutory duty; if they refuse, the court issues an order compelling compliance. Certiorari is used to quash the orders of lower courts or tribunals that have acted beyond their jurisdiction (ultra vires) or in violation of principles of natural justice. Prohibition prevents a lower court from proceeding with a case where it lacks jurisdiction, while Quo Warranto questions the legality of a person's claim to a public office.

Examiner's trap
A frequent error is assuming that Article 32 is available for any legal wrong. Correct analysis: Article 32 applies strictly to Fundamental Rights violations. For ordinary civil disputes or non-Fundamental Right issues, one must approach the High Court under Article 226 or a Civil Court. Furthermore, while Parliament can suspend Art. 32 (Art. 32(4)), it cannot do so for Art. 20-22 (Protection in respect of conviction for offences), making these rights absolute against legislative suspension.
Exam-Day Strategy
When answering questions on Constitutional Remedies, always structure your answer by first defining the specific writ, then citing the relevant Article (32 or 226), followed by a landmark case that expanded its scope (e.g., Hussainara Khatoon for speedy trial). Avoid generic definitions; instead, explain the "cause and effect" of the remedy. For instance, explain how the suspension of Habeas Corpus during an emergency (Art. 359) interacts with the non-suspendable rights under Art. 20-22. This demonstrates a deep understanding of the hierarchy of rights.

Separation of Powers: Functional Overlap and Checks

The doctrine of Separation of Powers, derived from Montesquieu's De l'esprit des lois, posits that legislative, executive, and judicial functions should be vested in distinct organs to prevent tyranny. However, the Indian Constitution adopts a system of "Functional Overlap" or "Cooperative Federalism," where these functions are not strictly segregated but interwoven. The President (Executive) is also Head of State with legislative functions; Parliament (Legislative) has executive oversight; and the Judiciary exercises judicial review over both. This design, often criticized as a violation of strict separation, was intentional to ensure checks and balances.

The most significant manifestation of this overlap is Judicial Review. Under Article 13(2), the Constitution explicitly states that any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights shall be void to the extent of such inconsistency. The Supreme Court, in Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), established the "Basic Structure Doctrine," holding that while Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter its basic features (e.g., secularism, democracy, rule of law). This effectively places the Judiciary as a guardian of the Constitution's core identity, acting as a check on legislative and executive excesses.

Conversely, the Executive can influence the Legislature through the Council of Ministers responsible to Parliament (Article 74), and the Legislature can control the Executive through budgetary approvals and questions. The Judiciary checks both by striking down unconstitutional laws or executive orders. However, this overlap is not without limits. In N.S. Sarkar v. Union of India (2013), the Supreme Court clarified that while functional overlap exists, the core functions must remain distinct; a judge cannot act as an administrator, nor can an executive officer adjudicate disputes. The distinction between "adjudicatory" and "administrative" functions remains crucial.

The concept of "Basic Structure" has evolved significantly. Initially debated in Keshavananda Bharati, it was reaffirmed in cases like S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), where the Court held that the federal character of the Constitution is part of its basic structure, preventing the Centre from arbitrarily dissolving State Assemblies under Article 356. This reinforced the idea that the separation of powers is not just about functional division but also about preserving the constitutional morality that binds all branches together.

General Defences under the Indian Penal Code: Mistake of Fact, Accident, and Necessity

The Indian Penal Code (IPC) recognises a limited set of general defences that negate the mental element required for criminal liability. Sections 76 to 106 enumerate these defences, the most frequently examined being mistake of fact (Section 76), accident (Section 80), and necessity (Section 81). A mistake of fact is a defence only when the mistake is honest, reasonable, and relates to a fact — not law — that, if true, would make the act lawful. In R v. Tolson (1889) the court held that a genuine belief in the existence of a marital relationship, though mistaken, excluded the mens rea for bigamy. The defence fails if the mistake is one of law (ignorantia juris non excusat) or if the accused’s belief was unreasonable in the circumstances.

Accident under Section 80 excuses an act done by misfortune without any criminal intention, provided the act is lawful, done in a lawful manner, and with proper care and caution. The classic illustration is a hunter who, while aiming at a lawful target, accidentally shoots a bystander; liability is excluded if the hunter exercised due care. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965) clarified that the burden of proving accident rests on the accused, but the standard is preponderance of probability, not beyond reasonable doubt.

Necessity (Section 81) applies when an act causing harm is done to prevent a greater harm, provided the harm caused is not disproportionate to the harm avoided. The defence is unavailable if the accused created the perilous situation. In R v. Dudley & Stephens (1884) the court rejected necessity as a defence to murder, establishing that the intentional killing of an innocent person cannot be justified even to save others. Indian courts follow this principle, limiting necessity to situations such as pulling down a house to stop a fire (Section 81 illustration) or a surgeon performing an emergency operation without consent.

Examiners often test the distinction between mistake of fact and mistake of law, and the requirement of proportionality in necessity. A precise answer must cite the relevant section, the leading case, and the factual matrix that satisfies each element of the defence.

Specific Offences against Property: Theft, Extortion, Robbery, and Dacoity

The IPC groups property offences in a hierarchy of aggravation. Theft (Section 378) is the dishonest removal of movable property out of the possession of another without consent. The essential ingredients are: (i) movable property, (ii) belonging to another, (iii) dishonest intention, (iv) removal from possession, and (v) without consent. In K. N. Mehra v. State of Rajasthan (1957) the Supreme Court held that a temporary removal with intent to return does not constitute theft if there is no dishonest intention to permanently deprive.

Extortion (Section 383) adds the element of putting a person in fear of injury to obtain property or valuable security. The fear may be of physical harm, reputational damage, or illegal restraint. The property need not be movable; it can be a document or a right. The landmark case R. S. Nayak v. A. R. Antulay (1984) clarified that the threat must be of a nature that would induce a person of ordinary firmness to part with the property.

Robbery (Section 390) is theft or extortion accompanied by the use or threat of instant death, hurt, or wrongful restraint. The presence of violence at the time of taking distinguishes robbery from mere theft. In State of Maharashtra v. Joseph Mingel Koli (1997) the court emphasized that the violence must be in furtherance of the taking, not merely incidental.

Dacoity (Section 391) is robbery committed by five or more persons acting conjointly. The numerical threshold is strict; if the group falls below five before the act, the offence reverts to robbery. The Supreme Court in State of U.P. v. Shiv Kumar (2000) held that the common intention of all participants must be proved, and mere presence is insufficient.

Understanding the gradation — theft → extortion → robbery → dacoity — is crucial for answering questions on charge framing, sentencing, and the applicability of Section 34 (common intention) versus Section 149 (unlawful assembly).

Worked Illustration
A, B, C, D, and E plan to rob a jewellery store. On the day, only A, B, and C enter the store, threaten the owner with a knife, and take the jewellery. D and E wait outside in a vehicle. The prosecution charges all five with dacoity under Section 391. Decide.
Dacoity requires five or more persons conjointly committing robbery. Here only three persons physically committed robbery; D and E were not present at the scene. The Supreme Court in State of U.P. v. Shiv Kumar held that conjoint participation demands actual presence and active involvement. Therefore, A, B, and C are liable for robbery (Section 390), while D and E may be charged with abetment under Section 109, not dacoity. The charge of dacoity against all five fails.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a property‑offence question lists multiple accused, first count the number who actually participated in the violent taking. If the count is below five, the highest offence is robbery; dacoity cannot be sustained. Always cite the numerical requirement of Section 391 and the Shiv Kumar precedent to secure full marks.

Formation and Vitiating Factors in Contract Law: Offer, Acceptance, Consideration, Coercion, Undue Influence, and Misrepresentation

A valid contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872 requires an offer, an unequivocal acceptance, lawful consideration, capacity of parties, free consent, and a lawful object. The offer must be definite, communicated, and capable of acceptance; an invitation to treat (e.g., display of goods) is not an offer (Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v. Boots Cash Chemists 1953). Acceptance must be absolute, communicated to the offeror, and in the prescribed mode; silence cannot amount to acceptance unless prior conduct creates a duty to speak.

Consideration need not be adequate but must be real and not illusory. Past consideration is valid in India (Section 2(d)) unlike English law, provided it was at the promisor’s desire. The doctrine of privity of contract prevents a stranger from enforcing a contract, though exceptions exist for trusts, family settlements, and agency.

Free consent is vitiated by coercion (Section 15), undue influence (Section 16), fraud (Section 17), misrepresentation (Section 18), and mistake (Sections 20‑22). Coercion involves committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the IPC, or unlawful detention of property. In Chikham Amiraju v. Chikham Seshamma (1917) a threat to commit suicide was held to be coercion. Undue influence arises where one party dominates the will of the other, typically in fiduciary relationships; the burden shifts to the dominant party to prove the transaction was fair.

Misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made innocently; it renders the contract voidable at the option of the aggrieved party. Fraud requires knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard. The distinction matters for the remedy: misrepresentation allows rescission, while fraud also permits damages. A common examiner trap is to conflate misrepresentation with mere puffery or opinion; only statements of existing fact qualify.

Remedies in Tort Law: Compensatory Damages, Exemplary Damages, Injunctions, and Specific Restitution

The law of torts aims to restore the claimant to the position before the wrong. Compensatory damages are the primary remedy, covering pecuniary loss (special damages) and non‑pecuniary loss such as pain and suffering (general damages). The principle of remoteness, articulated in The Wagon Mound (No. 1) (1961), limits recovery to damage that was reasonably foreseeable. In India, the Supreme Court in K. S. Ramaswamy v. State of Tamil Nadu (1995) applied the foreseeability test to medical negligence claims.

Exemplary (punitive) damages are awarded where the defendant’s conduct is oppressive, arbitrary, or outrageous, serving a deterrent function. The landmark case Rookes v. Barnard (1964) restricted exemplary damages to three categories: oppressive government conduct, calculated profit‑making wrongdoing, and statutory authorization. Indian courts follow this restrictive approach; in Bhim Singh v. State of Jammu & Kashmir (1985) exemplary damages were granted for illegal detention by police.

Injunctions are equitable remedies that restrain a party from committing or continuing a tort. A prohibitory injunction stops an act; a mandatory injunction compels an act. The court exercises discretion guided by the balance of convenience, adequacy of damages, and the plaintiff’s conduct. In American Cyanamid Co. v. Ethicon Ltd. (1975) the House of Lords set out the three‑stage test for interlocutory injunctions, which Indian courts adopt.

Specific restitution, though rare in tort, may be ordered where property is uniquely identifiable and damages are inadequate, such as the return of a stolen heirloom. The remedy is discretionary and subject to the defence of bona fide purchaser for value without notice.

Major Schools of Jurisprudence: Natural Law, Legal Positivism, Sociological Jurisprudence, and Critical Legal Studies

Jurisprudence examines the nature, sources, and limits of law. Natural law theorists (Aristotle, Aquinas, Finnis) argue that law derives its validity from moral principles inherent in human nature; an unjust law is not law (lex injusta non est lex). This school underpins constitutional review of legislation against fundamental rights.

Legal positivism, championed by Bentham, Austin, and Hart, separates law from morality. Law is a system of rules posited by a sovereign (Austin) or a union of primary and secondary rules (Hart). The “rule of recognition” identifies valid laws without reference to moral content. Positivism explains why a legally enacted but morally repugnant statute remains enforceable until repealed.

Sociological jurisprudence (Ehrlich, Pound, Llewellyn) studies law in action, emphasizing the social functions of legal rules and the gap between “law in books” and “law in practice”. It informs legislative reform and judicial policy‑making, especially in areas like labour law and environmental regulation.

Critical Legal Studies (CLS) emerged in the 1970s, arguing that law is indeterminate and serves to legitimize existing power structures. CLS scholars deconstruct legal doctrines to reveal hidden biases of class, gender, and race. While not a mainstream source for exam answers, awareness of CLS enriches critical analysis of judicial reasoning.

Examiners often ask to compare natural law and positivism on the validity of an unjust statute, or to explain how sociological jurisprudence influences the interpretation of “public interest” in administrative law. A concise answer must define each school, name its principal exponents, and illustrate with a concrete legal issue.

  • Confusing mistake of fact with mistake of law — only the former is a defence under Section 76 IPC.
  • Treating extortion as theft — extortion requires inducement by fear, not mere dishonest removal.
  • Assuming any threat constitutes coercion — the threatened act must be forbidden by the IPC or involve unlawful detention of property.
  • Believing exemplary damages are routinely awarded — they are exceptional and limited to the three Rookes v. Barnard categories.
  • Equating natural law with constitutional morality — natural law is a philosophical theory; constitutional morality is a judicial construct.
  • Overlooking the requirement of five persons for dacoity — mere abetment by outsiders does not satisfy Section 391.
  • Misapplying the rule of recognition — it identifies valid legal rules, not moral validity.
