Technique: IPC Sections answer
Question: 'What is the difference between culpable homicide and murder?'
Answer: Culpable homicide (Section 299): causing death by doing an act with intention of causing death, or with intention of causing bodily injury likely to cause death, or with knowledge that act is likely to cause death. Murder (Section 300): culpable homicide is murder if (a) intention to cause death, (b) intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death, (c) intention to cause bodily injury sufficient to cause death, (d) act imminently dangerous and likely to cause death, with knowledge. All murders are culpable homicide but all culpable homicides are not murder. Exceptions to murder: grave and sudden provocation, private defence, public servant acting in good faith, sudden fight, consent (death of person above 18 years who consents).

Common Traps

  • Confusing culpable homicide with murder - all murders are culpable homicide but not vice versa.
  • Confusing theft with robbery - robbery requires violence or threat of violence.
  • Confusing robbery with dacoity - dacoity requires 5 or more persons.
  • Confusing wrongful restraint with wrongful confinement - wrongful restraint prevents movement in one direction, wrongful confinement prevents movement in all directions.
  • Missing that Section 300 contains exceptions (grave and sudden provocation, private defence, etc.) that reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

Memory Tricks

  • Stages of crime: Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission.
  • Degrees of homicide: Culpable homicide → Murder (with intent/intention to cause death) → Death by negligence (Section 304A).
  • Offences against property: Theft → Extortion (threat) → Robbery (violence/fear) → Dacoity (5+ persons).
  • Writs: Habeas Corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop), Certiorari (transfer), Quo Warranto (authority).
Mains strategy
Criminal law answers: statutory provision (section number) → ingredients of offence → application to facts → case law. Quote landmark judgments: Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962 - Section 124A sedition), Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978 - Article 21), DK Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997 - arrest guidelines), DK Basu guidelines (arrest procedure), DK Basu guidelines (arrest memo), DK Basu (right to lawyer), Hussainara Khatoon (right to speedy trial), Hussainara Khatoon (right to legal aid), Hussainara Khatoon (right to free legal aid).

5. Constitutional Remedies: The Architecture of Rights Protection

The Indian Constitution provides specific mechanisms for the enforcement of rights and the adjudication of disputes between the State and its citizens, or between different organs of government. These are known as Constitutional Remedies, primarily housed in Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part V (Union Executive) alongside Articles 32 and 226. Article 32, often termed the "heart and soul" of the Constitution by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, empowers the Supreme Court to issue directions, orders, or writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. These writs are Habeas Corpus (Art. 32/226), Mandamus (Art. 32/226), Certiorari (Art. 32/226), Prohibition (Art. 32/226), and Quo Warranto (Art. 32).

Article 226 grants High Courts similar powers, though it is not limited strictly to Fundamental Rights; they can also enforce other legal rights or correct jurisdictional errors by lower courts. The distinction lies in the scope: while Article 32 is exclusive to the Supreme Court and mandatory for Fundamental Rights violations (unless Parliament has suspended it under Art. 32(4) - which it rarely does), Article 226 is a residual power available even when no Fundamental Right is infringed. This duality creates a robust two-tier judicial review system, ensuring that grievances are addressed at the most appropriate level of jurisdiction.

The evolution of these remedies has been shaped by landmark judgments. In Krishna Kanth Singh v. State of Bihar (1984), the Supreme Court clarified the scope of Article 226, stating that High Courts should not act as a substitute for the Supreme Court unless the latter is unable to provide relief. Conversely, in Rupa Ashok Hinduja v. Kanthi Chand Hinduja (2010), the Court held that Article 32 and 226 are complementary, not mutually exclusive; an aggrieved party can approach either forum. The concept of "Public Interest Litigation" (PIL) further expanded these remedies, allowing public-spirited individuals to file petitions on behalf of those unable to do so themselves, effectively democratizing access to justice.

The writ of Habeas Corpus is perhaps the most famous remedy, literally meaning "produce the body." It is a command to a person detaining another to bring the detainee before the court. In Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1979), the Supreme Court used this writ not just to secure physical liberty but also to enforce the right to a speedy trial and legal aid for undertrials detained for long periods without conviction. Similarly, the writ of Mandamus commands a public authority to perform its statutory duty; if they refuse, the court issues an order compelling compliance. Certiorari is used to quash the orders of lower courts or tribunals that have acted beyond their jurisdiction (ultra vires) or in violation of principles of natural justice. Prohibition prevents a lower court from proceeding with a case where it lacks jurisdiction, while Quo Warranto questions the legality of a person's claim to a public office.

Examiner's trap
A frequent error is assuming that Article 32 is available for any legal wrong. Correct analysis: Article 32 applies strictly to Fundamental Rights violations. For ordinary civil disputes or non-Fundamental Right issues, one must approach the High Court under Article 226 or a Civil Court. Furthermore, while Parliament can suspend Art. 32 (Art. 32(4)), it cannot do so for Art. 20-22 (Protection in respect of conviction for offences), making these rights absolute against legislative suspension.
Exam-Day Strategy
When answering questions on Constitutional Remedies, always structure your answer by first defining the specific writ, then citing the relevant Article (32 or 226), followed by a landmark case that expanded its scope (e.g., Hussainara Khatoon for speedy trial). Avoid generic definitions; instead, explain the "cause and effect" of the remedy. For instance, explain how the suspension of Habeas Corpus during an emergency (Art. 359) interacts with the non-suspendable rights under Art. 20-22. This demonstrates a deep understanding of the hierarchy of rights.

6. Separation of Powers: Functional Overlap and Checks

The doctrine of Separation of Powers, derived from Montesquieu's De l'esprit des lois, posits that legislative, executive, and judicial functions should be vested in distinct organs to prevent tyranny. However, the Indian Constitution adopts a system of "Functional Overlap" or "Cooperative Federalism," where these functions are not strictly segregated but interwoven. The President (Executive) is also Head of State with legislative functions; Parliament (Legislative) has executive oversight; and the Judiciary exercises judicial review over both. This design, often criticized as a violation of strict separation, was intentional to ensure checks and balances.

The most significant manifestation of this overlap is Judicial Review. Under Article 13(2), the Constitution explicitly states that any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights shall be void to the extent of such inconsistency. The Supreme Court, in Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), established the "Basic Structure Doctrine," holding that while Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter its basic features (e.g., secularism, democracy, rule of law). This effectively places the Judiciary as a guardian of the Constitution's core identity, acting as a check on legislative and executive excesses.

Conversely, the Executive can influence the Legislature through the Council of Ministers responsible to Parliament (Article 74), and the Legislature can control the Executive through budgetary approvals and questions. The Judiciary checks both by striking down unconstitutional laws or executive orders. However, this overlap is not without limits. In N.S. Sarkar v. Union of India (2013), the Supreme Court clarified that while functional overlap exists, the core functions must remain distinct; a judge cannot act as an administrator, nor can an executive officer adjudicate disputes. The distinction between "adjudicatory" and "administrative" functions remains crucial.

The concept of "Basic Structure" has evolved significantly. Initially debated in Keshavananda Bharati, it was reaffirmed in cases like S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), where the Court held that the federal character of the Constitution is part of its basic structure, preventing the Centre from arbitrarily dissolving State Assemblies under Article 356. This reinforced the idea that the separation of powers is not just about functional division but also about preserving the constitutional morality that binds all branches together.
