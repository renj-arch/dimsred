Structure of the Law Optional Papers

1.1 Paper I Composition and Weightage

The first paper of the law optional carries two hundred and fifty marks and is divided into three broad domains: constitutional law, administrative law and international law. Constitutional law forms the backbone of the paper because it supplies the fundamental principles that govern the relationship between the state and the individual, and it also provides the interpretative lens through which administrative and international questions are often examined. Administrative law builds on constitutional foundations by testing the legality of executive action, the scope of delegated legislation and the availability of judicial remedies such as writs and tribunals. International law, while distinct in subject matter, is linked to the domestic framework through the doctrine of incorporation and the role of treaties in shaping municipal legislation. The examination expects candidates to demonstrate not only a thorough knowledge of statutory provisions and landmark judgments but also an ability to interrelate these three areas when a question demands a comparative or integrated analysis.

1.2 Paper II Composition and Weightage

The second paper also carries two hundred and fifty marks and covers the private and penal branches of law. It is organised around six major clusters: the law of contracts together with mercantile statutes such as the Sale of Goods Act, the Partnership Act, the Negotiable Instruments Act and the Companies Act; the law of crimes centred on the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act; the law of torts focusing on negligence, nuisance, defamation, strict and vicarious liability and the quantification of damages; family law encompassing Hindu and Muslim personal laws on marriage, divorce, maintenance, adoption and succession; property law dealing with the Transfer of Property Act, easements, mortgages, leases and gifts; and industrial and labour law built around the Industrial Disputes Act, the Factories Act, minimum wages legislation, trade union regulation and workmen’s compensation. Although each cluster can be studied in isolation, the examination frequently frames problems that require a candidate to move across clusters, for instance by applying contract principles to a commercial dispute that also raises tortious liability or by invoking constitutional safeguards in a labour‑rights scenario.

1.3 Interrelationship Between the Two Papers

Understanding the interplay between the two papers is essential for a coherent preparation strategy. Constitutional provisions such as Article 14, Article 19 and Article 21 frequently surface in administrative law questions and also inform the interpretation of statutory rights in contract, tort and labour disputes. Similarly, principles of natural justice derived from administrative law are invoked in disciplinary proceedings under labour statutes and in the procedural fairness required by criminal courts. International law obligations, especially those concerning human rights and environmental protection, have been read into domestic legislation through judicial interpretation, thereby creating a bridge between Paper I and the private law topics of Paper II. A candidate who recognises these linkages can answer questions more holistically, citing constitutional jurisprudence to strengthen an argument in a contract or tort answer, and can therefore score higher marks across both papers.

Syllabus Map for Paper I

2.1 Constitutional Law Foundations

Constitutional law in this paper begins with the concept of constitutionalism and the distinction between a written constitution and constitutional conventions. The syllabus requires a detailed study of the Preamble, the classification of constitutional provisions into justiciable fundamental rights and non‑justiciable directive principles, and the enforceability of directive principles through judicial activism. The relationship among the three organs of government — executive, legislature and judiciary — is examined through the doctrine of separation of powers, the principle of checks and balances, and the scope of judicial review under Articles 32 and 226. Federalism is explored through the distribution of legislative powers in the Seventh Schedule, the role of the Finance Commission, and the dynamics of centre‑state relations during emergencies. The basic structure doctrine, originating in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, limits the amending power of Parliament and has been applied in subsequent cases such as Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain and Minerva Mills v. Union of India. Candidates must be able to articulate the evolution of each doctrine, cite the ratio decidendi of the leading judgments, and explain how the doctrine operates as a constitutional safeguard.

2.2 Administrative Law Principles

Administrative law focuses on the control of executive power through legal norms. The syllabus covers the nature and limits of delegated legislation, the requirement of legislative guidance and the judicial test of excessive delegation. Natural justice is examined through the twin pillars of audi alteram partem and nemo judex in causa sua, together with the expanding concept of fairness that includes the right to reasons and the duty to act without bias. Judicial review of administrative action is studied under the grounds of illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, and the remedies of certiorari, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and habeas corpus are analysed in the context of both statutory tribunals and constitutional writ jurisdiction. The role of the ombudsman, the Central Vigilance Commission and the Lokpal in providing alternative accountability mechanisms is also part of the syllabus. A thorough answer will link each principle to the constitutional provisions that empower or restrain administrative authorities, and will illustrate the application with recent Supreme Court decisions.

2.3 International Law Framework

International law is presented as a system of rules governing the conduct of states and international organisations. The sources of international law — treaties, custom, general principles, judicial decisions and scholarly writings — are studied with reference to Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice. The relationship between international law and municipal law is examined through the doctrines of monism and dualism, and the Indian practice of incorporating treaties through legislation or judicial interpretation. Recognition of states and governments, the principles of jurisdiction (territorial, nationality, protective and universal), and the law of state responsibility for internationally wrongful acts form the core of the syllabus. Specialised regimes such as the law of the sea (UNCLOS), air and space law, the law of armed conflict, neutrality, and the functioning of the United Nations and its specialised agencies are required. Human rights law, including the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, and international environmental law, particularly the principles of sustainable development and the precautionary principle, complete the map. Candidates should be prepared to discuss the interplay between these regimes and domestic law, citing relevant Indian judicial pronouncements.

Solved Example: Basic Structure Doctrine
Question: Explain the basic structure doctrine and its significance in constitutional amendment.
Answer: The basic structure doctrine was propounded by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). The Court held that while Parliament possesses wide amending power under Article 368, it cannot alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. The essential features identified include the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. The doctrine acts as a substantive limitation on constitutional amendments, ensuring that the core identity of the Constitution remains intact. It was subsequently applied in Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) to strike down the 39th Amendment clause that sought to immunise the election of the Prime Minister from judicial review, and in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) to invalidate provisions that curtailed judicial review and expanded Parliament’s amending power beyond the basic structure. The doctrine therefore remains the most potent judicial check on majoritarian amendment attempts.
Examination Insight
When a question asks for the “significance” of a doctrine, structure the answer by first stating the doctrine, then enumerating the constitutional values it protects, followed by two or three landmark applications, and finally a brief comment on its continuing relevance in recent amendments.

Syllabus Map for Paper II

3.1 Contract and Mercantile Law

The law of contracts forms the foundation of commercial transactions and is governed primarily by the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The syllabus demands a thorough understanding of offer and acceptance, consideration, capacity to contract, free consent, legality of object, and the various modes of discharge such as performance, breach, frustration and novation. Remedies for breach — damages, specific performance, injunction and quantum meruit — must be analysed with reference to statutory provisions and leading cases like Hadley v. Baxendale for remoteness of damage. Mercantile law extends the contract framework to specialised statutes: the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 deals with transfer of property, conditions and warranties, and the rights of unpaid sellers; the Partnership Act, 1932 covers the formation, rights and dissolution of firms; the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 regulates promissory notes, bills of exchange and cheques, with emphasis on holder in due course and dishonour; and the Companies Act, 2013 (as amended) addresses incorporation, corporate governance, share capital, meetings, and winding up. Candidates should be able to move seamlessly between the general contract principles and the specific statutory regimes, highlighting where the special statutes modify or supplement the general law.

3.2 Criminal Law and Procedure

Criminal law centres on the Indian Penal Code, 1860, and requires a clear distinction between the definitions of offences and the corresponding punishments. General exceptions such as mistake of fact, accident, necessity, infancy, insanity and intoxication must be studied with their judicial interpretations. Offences against the human body — culpable homicide (Section 299), murder (Section 300), hurt, grievous hurt, and kidnapping — are frequently tested through problem‑type questions that demand application of the relevant sections to a given fact pattern. Offences against property — theft, extortion, robbery, dacoity, criminal misappropriation and criminal breach of trust — require an understanding of the elements of dishonest intention and movable property. Offences against the state and public order, including sedition, waging war, and unlawful assembly, are examined in the light of constitutional freedoms under Article 19. The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, supplies the procedural framework: arrest, bail, investigation, charge, trial, appeal and revision. The Indian Evidence Act, 1872, governs the admissibility of evidence, the burden of proof, presumptions, and the examination of witnesses. A high‑scoring answer integrates the substantive offence with the procedural safeguards and cites the ratio of leading Supreme Court judgments such as State of Maharashtra v. Bachchu Lal for the test of culpable homicide versus murder.

3.3 Torts, Family, Property and Labour Law

The law of torts is built around the concepts of duty of care, breach, causation and remoteness. Negligence is illustrated by the neighbour principle from Donoghue v. Stevenson and the Indian adaptation in Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti. Nuisance, both public and private, defamation (libel and slander), strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher) and vicarious liability (master‑servant relationship) are essential. Remedies include damages (compensatory, exemplary, nominal), injunction and specific restitution. Family law requires a comparative study of Hindu law (codified in the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955; Hindu Succession Act, 1956; Hindu Adoption and Maintenance Act, 1956) and Muslim law (derived from the Shariat, the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986). Property law is anchored in the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, covering sale, mortgage, lease, gift and exchange, together with the law of easements under the Indian Easements Act, 1882. Labour law focuses on the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (definition of industry, workman, lay‑off, retrenchment, closure), the Factories Act, 1948 (health, safety, welfare), the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, the Trade Unions Act, 1926, and the Workmen’s Compensation Act, 1923. The examination often asks for a critical analysis of recent amendments, such as the 2020 labour codes, and their impact on the existing statutory framework.

Standard Sources and Reading Strategy

4.1 Core Textbooks for Constitutional and Administrative Law

A disciplined reading plan begins with the primary textbook for each subject, followed by the supplementary work for deeper analysis. For constitutional law, M. P. Jain provides a systematic article‑wise commentary that aligns with the syllabus order, while D. D. Basu offers a more discursive treatment of judicial doctrines and historical evolution. Reading Jain chapter by chapter, annotating the margins with the ratio of the cited cases, creates a ready reference for answer writing. Administrative law is best approached through C. K. Takwani’s lecture‑style exposition, which clarifies the conceptual underpinnings of delegated legislation and natural justice; I. P. Massey’s work then supplies a richer case‑law repository, especially on judicial review grounds and tribunal jurisprudence. The strategy is to complete one chapter of the primary text, solve the end‑of‑chapter questions, and then consult the supplementary text for the same topic to capture divergent judicial opinions.

4.2 Core Textbooks for International Law

International law requires a dual approach: a concise Indian‑authored textbook for the examination‑oriented coverage of topics such as state responsibility, law of the sea and human rights, and a comprehensive international treatise for the theoretical framework. S. K. Kapoor’s volume is tailored to the syllabus, presenting each topic with relevant Indian practice and case law. Malcolm Shaw’s International Law, while more extensive, is invaluable for understanding the sources, the law of treaties, and the functioning of international organisations at a conceptual level. The reading plan suggests allocating two weeks to Kapoor for a first pass, making detailed notes on the Indian position, and then using Shaw selectively to fill gaps in the theoretical understanding, especially for topics like the relationship between international and municipal law where Indian jurisprudence is still evolving.

4.3 Core Textbooks for Private and Penal Law

For contract and mercantile law, R. K. Bangia’s concise exposition of the Indian Contract Act, coupled with Avatar Singh’s detailed treatment of specific relief and the mercantile statutes, provides a balanced coverage. The candidate should read Bangia for the general principles and then move to Avatar Singh for the Sale of Goods, Partnership, Negotiable Instruments and Companies Acts, noting the statutory modifications of common law rules. Criminal law is anchored in Ratan Lal & Dhiraj Lal for the Indian Penal Code, which offers section‑wise commentary with illustrative cases; K. D. Gaur’s case‑materials book supplements this with edited judgments that demonstrate judicial reasoning on contentious issues such as the distinction between culpable homicide and murder. Torts, family, property and labour law each have a primary Indian textbook (Bangia for torts, Paras Diwan for family, R. K. Sinha for property, S. N. Misra for labour) and a classic supplementary work (Winfield for torts, Kusum for family, Mulla for property, P. L. Malik for labour). The reading rhythm is to finish the primary text, prepare a one‑page schematic for each chapter, and then use the supplementary text only for topics where the primary text is silent or where recent judicial developments have altered the position.

Answer Writing Technique and Study Plan

5.1 Structuring a Legal Answer

A well‑structured legal answer follows a logical sequence that mirrors judicial reasoning. The opening paragraph identifies the legal issue or issues raised by the question, framing them in precise legal terminology. The second paragraph states the applicable rule, citing the statutory provision, the relevant constitutional article, or the principle derived from a landmark judgment, and briefly explains the rationale behind the rule. The third paragraph applies the rule to the facts supplied in the problem, demonstrating how each element of the rule is satisfied or not satisfied, and draws analogies or distinctions with the cited authorities. The concluding paragraph delivers a clear conclusion, often phrased as “Therefore, the plaintiff will succeed” or “The amendment is ultra vires the basic structure”. Throughout the answer, the candidate must integrate at least two authoritative sources — a statute and a case, or two cases representing majority and minority views — to demonstrate breadth of reading. Headings such as “Issue”, “Rule”, “Application”, “Conclusion” are not written explicitly but the paragraph breaks serve the same function, guiding the examiner through the reasoning.

5.2 Integrating Statutes, Case Law and Commentary

Effective integration requires more than mere citation; it demands an explanation of why a particular authority supports the proposition. When a statute is quoted, the candidate should indicate the legislative intent, the language of the provision, and any judicial interpretation that has expanded or restricted its scope. When a case is cited, the ratio decidendi must be extracted and contrasted with any obiter dicta that may be relevant. Commentaries from standard textbooks can be used to summarise the academic consensus or to highlight a dissenting scholarly view, but they should never replace primary authorities. A practical technique is to maintain a personal “authority bank” — a notebook or digital file where each topic is linked to three to five key authorities with a one‑sentence summary of the holding. During revision, the candidate can quickly retrieve the appropriate authority and embed it in the answer without wasting time searching for the exact citation.

5.3 Time Allocation and Revision Cycles

A realistic study plan spans approximately nine months, divided into three phases. The first phase, lasting four months, is devoted to comprehensive reading of the primary textbooks across all subjects, with simultaneous preparation of concise notes and the authority bank. The second phase, of three months, focuses on answer writing practice: attempting at least two full‑length questions per day, timing each answer to twenty minutes, and then evaluating the answer against a model structure. The third phase, of two months, is reserved for intensive revision, mock tests under examination conditions, and targeted reading of recent judgments and legislative amendments. Weekly revision cycles should revisit the notes prepared in phase one, while monthly mock tests simulate the pressure of the actual examination. The candidate must also allocate time for current legal developments, especially Supreme Court decisions delivered in the preceding twelve months, as the examination frequently tests awareness of the latest jurisprudential trends. Consistent adherence to this phased approach ensures coverage of the vast syllabus while building the analytical speed required for high‑quality answers.

The lesson concludes with a reminder that disciplined reading, systematic note‑making and regular answer‑writing practice together form the foundation of success in the law optional.

Doctrine of Basic Structure and Its Evolution

The basic structure doctrine emerged from the Supreme Court’s decision in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) where a 7‑6 majority held that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 cannot alter the Constitution’s “basic structure”. The Court did not enumerate an exhaustive list but identified features such as supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, and federal character as part of the basic structure. Subsequent judgments refined the doctrine: Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) added free and fair elections; Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) declared that judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles are inviolable; and Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) introduced the “doctrine of prospective overruling” to protect amendments made before 24 April 1973. The evolution shows a gradual expansion from a narrow set of structural pillars to a broader constitutional ethos that includes rule of law, judicial independence, and the principle of limited government.

Critically, the doctrine operates as a substantive limitation on constituent power, not merely a procedural one. In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) the Court held that any law placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 is subject to basic‑structure scrutiny, thereby neutralising the legislative device of immune statutes. The judgment also clarified that the basic structure test is applied on a case‑by‑case basis, requiring the challenger to demonstrate that the amendment destroys a core constitutional feature. This jurisprudential trajectory makes the doctrine a frequent examination target: candidates must be able to trace the chronological development, cite the controlling ratios, and explain why certain features (e.g., parliamentary sovereignty) were rejected while others (e.g., judicial review) were entrenched.

A practical implication for answer writing is the need to distinguish between “basic structure” and “basic features”. The former is a judicial construct limiting amendment power; the latter refers to the essential characteristics of the Constitution that inform interpretation of statutes. When a question asks whether a particular amendment violates the basic structure, the answer must identify the specific feature alleged to be damaged, cite the relevant precedent, and apply the “destruction” test rather than a mere “alteration” test. Mastery of this nuance separates high‑scoring scripts from average ones.

Judicial Review of Administrative Action: Grounds and Remedies

Administrative law in India rests on the twin pillars of statutory authority and constitutional mandate. The Supreme Court has consistently held that any executive action must conform to the parent statute and the Constitution, and that judicial review is available on three broad grounds: illegality, irrationality (Wednesbury unreasonableness), and procedural impropriety. Illegality covers actions taken without jurisdiction, in excess of jurisdiction, or in breach of a mandatory statutory provision. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) the Court struck down a notification issued beyond the rule‑making power conferred by the enabling Act. Irrationality was imported from English law in Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd. v. Wednesbury Corporation (1948) and adopted in Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000), where a decision was quashed because no reasonable authority could have reached it. Procedural impropriety encompasses breach of natural justice — audi alteram partem and nemo judex in causa sua — as well as failure to follow statutory procedural requirements, illustrated by Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) where the passport impoundment without a hearing violated Article 21.

Remedies available under Articles 32 and 226 include writs of certiorari, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and habeas corpus. Certiorari is the principal remedy for quashing quasi‑judicial or administrative decisions tainted by the above grounds; mandamus compels performance of a statutory duty; prohibition prevents a tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) held that certiorari lies only against decisions of bodies exercising judicial or quasi‑judicial functions, a distinction that remains relevant when the challenged authority is a purely administrative officer. In recent years, the Court has expanded the scope of “legitimate expectation” as a facet of procedural fairness, notably in Food Corporation of India v. M/s. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries (1993), where a policy change without prior consultation was set aside. Understanding the interplay between statutory appeal mechanisms and constitutional writ jurisdiction is essential: where an efficacious alternative remedy exists, the Court may decline to entertain a writ petition, but this discretion does not apply to violations of fundamental rights.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to map a factual matrix to the appropriate ground, cite the controlling ratio, and articulate the remedy sought. A typical problem may describe a municipal order cancelling a licence without notice; the answer must identify procedural impropriety (breach of audi alteram partem), cite Maneka Gandhi and State of Orissa v. Dr. (Miss) Binapani Dei (1967), and pray for certiorari or a declaration of nullity. The ability to differentiate between “jurisdictional error” (reviewable by certiorari) and “error of law on the face of the record” (also certiorari) demonstrates depth of preparation.

Technique
A state government issues a notification under Section 5 of the Environmental Protection Act, 1986, banning all industrial activity within a 10 km radius of a wildlife sanctuary. The notification is challenged on the ground that the Minister did not consult the State Pollution Control Board as required by Section 5(2). Identify the ground of judicial review, the relevant precedent, and the appropriate writ.
The challenge falls under procedural impropriety — specifically, breach of a mandatory statutory consultation requirement. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) and State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) held that non‑compliance with a mandatory procedural provision renders the subordinate legislation void. The appropriate remedy is a writ of certiorari under Article 226 (or Article 32 if a fundamental right is implicated) to quash the notification, coupled with a declaration that the notification is ultra vires the enabling Act.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem question lists multiple possible defects, first classify each defect under illegality, irrationality, or procedural impropriety. Then select the strongest ground — usually a mandatory statutory breach — and cite the landmark case that squarely addresses that defect. Write the writ name in the prayer; examiners award marks for precise remedy identification.

Treaty Interpretation under the Vienna Convention

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), 1969, provides the authoritative framework for treaty interpretation, and Indian courts routinely invoke its Articles 31‑33. Article 31(1) establishes the general rule: a treaty shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose. The “context” includes the text, preamble, annexes, and any agreement or instrument made by the parties in connection with the treaty’s conclusion. Article 31(3) adds subsequent agreements, subsequent practice, and relevant rules of international law as supplementary means of interpretation. Article 32 permits recourse to preparatory work (travaux préparatoires) and the circumstances of conclusion only to confirm the meaning resulting from Article 31 or to resolve ambiguity or obscurity.

Indian jurisprudence illustrates the hierarchical application of these provisions. In National Thermal Power Corporation v. Singer Company (1992) the Supreme Court interpreted a bilateral investment treaty by first examining the ordinary meaning of “investment” in the treaty text, then considering the object and purpose of promoting foreign investment, and finally referring to the negotiating history only to confirm the conclusion. The Court emphasized that the VCLT reflects customary international law and is therefore binding even on non‑parties. In Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. v. Union of India (2018) the Court applied Article 31(3)(b) — subsequent practice — to interpret a power purchase agreement in light of the parties’ consistent conduct over a decade, demonstrating that subsequent practice can modify the effective meaning of treaty terms.

For answer writing, candidates must demonstrate the step‑by‑step methodology: (i) identify the treaty provision in dispute; (ii) extract the ordinary meaning of the key terms; (iii) situate the terms in the treaty’s context and object and purpose; (iv) assess any subsequent agreement or practice; (v) only if ambiguity persists, consult travaux préparatoires. Citing the VCLT articles by number and the Indian cases that have applied them shows both doctrinal knowledge and practical familiarity. A common examiner twist is to present a treaty clause with an ambiguous term and ask whether the preparatory work can be used; the correct answer is “only if the ambiguity remains after applying Article 31”.

Comparative Analysis of Contractual Frustration and Force Majeure

Section 56 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 codifies the doctrine of frustration: a contract becomes void when its performance becomes impossible or unlawful due to an event the promisor could not prevent. The Supreme Court in Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. (1954) laid down the test — the event must strike at the root of the contract, rendering performance radically different from what was undertaken. Force majeure, by contrast, is a contractual clause that allocates risk for specified supervening events (e.g., war, natural disaster, epidemic). The clause operates only if the event falls within its enumerated list or a catch‑all phrase such as “any other cause beyond the reasonable control of the parties”. In Energy Watchdog v. Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (2017) the Court held that a force‑majeure clause cannot be invoked for a mere rise in fuel prices because the clause enumerated only “acts of God” and “government action”.

The COVID‑19 pandemic generated a wave of litigation on both doctrines. In M/s. Standard Retail Pvt. Ltd. v. M/s. G.S. Global Corp. (2021) the Delhi High Court distinguished frustration from force majeure: where the contract contained a detailed force‑majeure clause covering “epidemics”, the parties’ remedy lay in the clause, not in Section 56. Conversely, in Ramanand v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2022) the Supreme Court allowed frustration where the contract lacked any force‑majeure provision and the lockdown rendered performance impossible. The Court reiterated that frustration discharges the contract automatically, whereas force majeure typically suspends obligations and may provide for termination after a prescribed period.

Examination questions often present a contract with a broadly worded force‑majeure clause and ask whether the party can also claim frustration. The answer must explain that the existence of a force‑majeure clause does not oust Section 56 unless the clause expressly covers the event and provides the exclusive remedy. The candidate should cite Satyabrata Ghose for the frustration test, Energy Watchdog for the interpretative approach to force‑majeure clauses, and the recent pandemic judgments for the practical interplay. A structured answer will first interpret the clause, then assess whether the event falls within it, and finally determine whether Section 56 remains available as a residual ground.

Emerging Trends in Labour Law: Gig Economy and Social Security

The rapid expansion of platform‑based work has outpaced the traditional labour‑law framework, which is built around the employer‑employee binary. The Code on Social Security, 2020 (not yet fully notified) defines “gig worker” and “platform worker” for the first time in Indian statute, extending social‑security benefits such as life and disability cover, health and maternity benefits, and old‑age protection. Section 2(35) of the Code includes “any person who performs work or participates in a work arrangement and earns from such activities outside of a traditional employer‑employee relationship”. The Code also establishes a Social Security Fund financed by contributions from aggregators (platform companies) at a rate of 1‑2 % of the transaction value, supplemented by central and state government contributions.

Judicial recognition of gig workers’ rights is evolving. In Indian Federation of App‑Based Transport Workers v. Union of India (2022) the Supreme Court issued notice to the Centre on a petition seeking classification of app‑based drivers as “workmen” under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, thereby entitling them to retrenchment compensation and collective bargaining. The Court observed that the “control test” and “integration test” must be adapted to algorithmic management, where the platform exercises control over fares, routes, ratings, and de‑activation. In Food Delivery Platforms case (2023) the Karnataka High Court directed a major aggregator to provide accident insurance and maternity benefits to its delivery partners, relying on the Directive Principles (Article 41) and the newly enacted Code provisions.

For the optional, candidates must be able to contrast the traditional “master‑servant” test with the emerging “algorithmic control” test, cite the statutory definitions in the Code on Social Security, and discuss the constitutional mandate under Articles 21, 23, 38, and 41. A typical essay question may ask whether the current legal regime adequately protects gig workers; a high‑scoring answer will evaluate the Code’s coverage gaps (e.g., absence of unemployment insurance), the enforcement challenges of aggregator contributions, and the judicial trend towards purposive interpretation of “workman”. Integrating the statutory scheme, case law, and constitutional principles demonstrates the synthesis expected at the optional level.

  • Treating every administrative error as a jurisdictional error; only errors that go to jurisdiction attract certiorari.
  • Invoking travaux préparatoires before exhausting Article 31 VCLT interpretation; the Convention permits supplementary means only after ambiguity persists.

  • Equating gig workers with traditional employees without analysing the algorithmic control test; the Supreme Court has not yet equated the two categories.

  • Failing to distinguish between suspension and termination under force‑majeure clauses; the contractual language determines the remedy.

Pith and Substance and Colourable Legislation in Centre‑State Relations

The doctrine of pith and substance determines whether a law falls within the legislative competence of Parliament or a State Legislature by examining the true nature and character of the enactment. The Supreme Court in State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951) held that incidental encroachment on a field assigned to another legislature does not invalidate the statute if its pith and substance lies within the enacting legislature’s domain. The test requires a two‑step inquiry: first, identify the dominant purpose of the law by reading the preamble, the statement of objects and reasons, and the operative provisions; second, ascertain whether that purpose corresponds to an entry in the Union List, State List or Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule.

Colourable legislation is a corollary that prevents a legislature from doing indirectly what it cannot do directly. In K. C. G. v. State of Tamil Nadu (1989) the Court struck down a state law that, under the guise of regulating “public health”, imposed a tax on the sale of liquor — a subject reserved for the Union under Entry 51 of the State List. The Court emphasised that the legislature’s motive is irrelevant; what matters is whether the law’s true character exceeds constitutional limits. The distinction between pith and substance and colourable legislation is subtle: the former is a jurisdictional test, the latter a doctrine of constitutional fraud. When a law is challenged on both grounds, the Court first applies pith and substance; only if the law survives that test does it examine whether the legislature has cloaked an ultra vires purpose in a seemingly valid entry.

Practical application in examinations often presents a statute that mixes regulatory and fiscal elements. A high‑scoring answer will (i) quote the relevant entries, (ii) dissect the statute’s dominant purpose using the “true nature” language from State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1959), (iii) discuss whether any incidental trenching is permissible under the “incidental or consequential” principle, and (iv) evaluate colourable legislation by asking whether the law’s real aim is to achieve a result forbidden to that legislature. The examiner may also test the “doctrine of harmonious construction” — reading Union and State entries together to avoid conflict — as illustrated in Union of India v. Shah Goverdhan L. Kabra (1975).

Legitimate Expectation and Promissory Estoppel in Administrative Decision‑Making

Legitimate expectation arises when a public authority, by an express promise or a regular practice, creates in a person a reasonable expectation of a particular benefit or procedural treatment. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. Hindustan Development Corporation (1993) recognised that a representation made by a minister in Parliament could generate a legitimate expectation, but clarified that such expectation does not confer a vested right; it merely entitles the individual to a fair hearing before the authority departs from the representation. The doctrine operates on two planes: procedural (right to be heard) and substantive (entitlement to the promised benefit). Substantive legitimate expectation is narrowly confined; the Court in State of Punjab v. Ram Singh (2000) held that a mere policy statement without a clear, unambiguous promise does not create a substantive expectation enforceable against the State.

Promissory estoppel, by contrast, prevents a government from reneging on a clear and unequivocal promise that has induced reliance, even in the absence of a formal contract. The landmark judgment in Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1979) established that the government cannot plead executive necessity to avoid a promise that has been acted upon, provided the promise is definite, the promisee has relied on it to his detriment, and enforcement would not be contrary to public policy. The Court later refined the doctrine in Century Spinning and Manufacturing Co. v. State of Gujarat (1992), stating that promissory estoppel cannot be invoked to compel the government to perform an act that is ultra vires the Constitution or a statute.

In answer‑writing, candidates must differentiate the two doctrines: legitimate expectation is rooted in fairness and procedural justice, while promissory estoppel is an equitable bar against unconscionable conduct. A typical problem may present a government scheme announced in a press release, followed by a policy reversal. The answer should (i) identify whether the announcement constitutes a “clear and unambiguous representation”, (ii) assess reliance and detriment, (iii) discuss the public‑policy exception (e.g., fiscal emergency), and (iv) conclude whether the aggrieved party is entitled to a hearing, continuation of the benefit, or merely compensation. Citation of Food Corporation of India v. M/s. K. S. R. Mills (2004) for the procedural dimension and State of Rajasthan v. Basant Singh (2005) for the substantive dimension strengthens the response.

Worked Illustration
A State Government issues a notification granting a 10 % subsidy on electricity tariffs for small‑scale industries for a period of five years. After two years, the Government withdraws the subsidy citing budgetary constraints. An affected industry challenges the withdrawal. Discuss the applicability of legitimate expectation and promissory estoppel.
Step 1 – Identify the representation. The notification is a formal, published act of the executive, creating a clear promise of a continuing benefit for a defined period.
Step 2 – Legitimate expectation. The industry has a procedural legitimate expectation to be heard before withdrawal (Hindustan Development Corp.). Since the withdrawal was abrupt without prior notice, the procedural fairness requirement is breached.
Step 3 – Substantive legitimate expectation. The promise was for a fixed five‑year term; the Court in Ram Singh held that a definite temporal commitment can ground a substantive expectation. However, the Government may invoke the public‑policy exception if it demonstrates an unforeseen fiscal crisis (Food Corporation of India).
Step 4 – Promissory estoppel. The industry relied on the subsidy for capital investment — detriment is established. The promise is unequivocal and not ultra vires. Under Motilal Padampat, the Government cannot resile merely on budgetary grounds unless it proves that continuation would violate a statutory or constitutional prohibition.
Conclusion. The industry is entitled to a hearing; if the Government fails to justify the withdrawal on compelling public‑policy grounds, the subsidy must be continued for the remaining period or compensation awarded.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes legitimate expectation and promissory estoppel, structure the answer in two clearly labelled parts. Use the “clear promise → reliance → detriment → public‑policy exception” checklist for promissory estoppel and the “representation → procedural/substantive expectation → fairness → exception” framework for legitimate expectation. Cite at least one Supreme Court authority for each limb; examiners reward precise case‑law mapping over generic statements.

Attribution of Conduct to the State under the Articles on State Responsibility

The International Law Commission’s Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA), adopted in 2001, codify the rules governing when conduct is attributable to a State. Article 4 establishes that the conduct of any State organ — legislative, executive, judicial or other — is attributable irrespective of its character as an organ of the central government or a territorial unit. Article 5 extends attribution to persons or entities exercising governmental authority under domestic law, provided they act in that capacity. The critical distinction lies in the “effective control” test for non‑State actors: the International Court of Justice in Nicaragua v. United States (1986) required “effective control” over specific operations, whereas the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia in Prosecutor v. Tadić (1999) adopted a lower “overall control” threshold. The ILC retained the stricter “effective control” standard in Article 8, reflecting State practice.

Attribution also covers conduct directed or controlled by a State (Article 8), conduct acknowledged and adopted by a State as its own (Article 11), and conduct of insurrectional movements that become the new government (Article 10). The “acknowledgment and adoption” principle was illustrated in the Tehran Hostages case (1980), where Iran’s endorsement of the militants’ seizure of the U.S. Embassy rendered the act attributable. Conversely, the Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (2005) judgment clarified that mere tolerance or failure to prevent private acts does not suffice for attribution absent effective control.

In examinations, candidates may be asked to analyse a scenario where a private security firm, contracted by a State to guard a border, commits human‑rights violations. The answer must (i) determine whether the firm qualifies as an entity exercising governmental authority under Article 5, (ii) assess the degree of State control — contractual oversight, operational directives, reporting mechanisms — against the “effective control” benchmark, and (iii) discuss the State’s due‑diligence obligation to prevent violations, citing Velásquez‑Rodríguez v. Honduras (1988) for the duty to investigate and punish. A nuanced response distinguishes attribution (which engages State responsibility directly) from the separate due‑diligence standard (which may give rise to responsibility for failure to act).

Right to Privacy, Data Protection and the Personal Data Protection Bill

The Supreme Court’s nine‑judge bench in (2017) unanimously declared the right to privacy a fundamental right under Articles 21, 19 and 14 of the Constitution. The judgment identified three facets: (i) bodily integrity, (ii) informational self‑determination, and (iii) decisional autonomy. Informational self‑determination directly underpins data‑protection jurisprudence, requiring that individuals control the collection, processing and dissemination of personal data. The Court laid down a triple test for any State intrusion: legality (existence of law), necessity (rational connection to a legitimate aim), and proportionality (least restrictive measure).

The Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019 (subsequently withdrawn and re‑introduced as the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023) operationalises these principles. Key provisions include: (a) definition of “personal data” and “sensitive personal data” (financial, health, biometric); (b) consent‑based processing with explicit consent for sensitive data; (c) data‑localisation requirements for critical personal data; (d) establishment of a Data Protection Authority (DPA) with adjudicatory and regulatory powers; (e) rights of data principals — access, correction, portability, and the “right to be forgotten” (Section 20). The Act also provides for significant penalties — up to ₹250 crore or 4 % of global turnover — for non‑compliance.

For the optional, a candidate must be able to (i) map the constitutional privacy doctrine onto the statutory framework, (ii) critique the consent model in the context of power asymmetries between data fiduciaries and principals, (iii) evaluate the localisation mandate against the proportionality test from Puttaswamy, and (iv) discuss the DPA’s independence — appointment by the Central Government, removal procedures, and judicial review of its orders. Comparative reference to the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) — lawful bases, data‑protection impact assessments, and supervisory authority independence — demonstrates the synthesis expected at the optional level.

Judicial Activism versus Judicial Restraint in Public Interest Litigation

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in India emerged in the late 1970s as a procedural innovation that relaxed locus standi, allowing any public‑spirited citizen to approach the Court on behalf of disadvantaged groups. The Supreme Court in S. P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) justified this expansion by invoking the constitutional mandate of access to justice under Article 39A. Judicial activism in PIL is characterised by the Court assuming quasi‑legislative and quasi‑executive roles — issuing continuing mandamus, framing guidelines, and monitoring implementation. Landmark instances include the Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) guidelines on sexual harassment, the M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1996) orders on vehicular pollution, and the People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2004) directions on custodial safeguards.

Critics argue that such activism encroaches on the separation of powers, transforms the judiciary into a “super‑legislature”, and risks policy‑making without democratic accountability. The Court itself has acknowledged limits: in Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass (2008) it cautioned against “judicial overreach” and emphasised that PIL should not become a substitute for governance. The doctrine of “judicial restraint” demands that courts defer to the executive and legislature on polycentric issues involving resource allocation, technical expertise, and political choices, intervening only when there is a clear constitutional violation or manifest arbitrariness.

In answer‑writing, a balanced analysis is essential. A high‑scoring response will (i) trace the historical evolution from Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) to the present, (ii) articulate the constitutional justification — Articles 32, 226, 39A, and the transformative vision of the Constitution, (iii) enumerate safeguards against overreach — the “continuing mandamus” must be time‑bound, the Court should avoid prescribing detailed policy, and it must respect the domain of the elected branches, and (iv) evaluate recent trends such as the Court’s reluctance to entertain PILs on policy matters like farm‑law repeal (2021) or the Citizenship Amendment Act challenges, signalling a shift toward restraint. Citation of scholarly commentary (e.g., Upendra Baxi’s “judicial activism as governance”) adds depth.

  • Confusing pith and substance with colourable legislation; the former is a jurisdictional test, the latter a fraud‑on‑the‑Constitution doctrine.
  • Assuming every government announcement creates a substantive legitimate expectation; only clear, definite promises with reliance qualify.
  • Applying the “overall control” test for attribution of non‑State actors; the ILC Articles require “effective control” per Nicaragua.
  • Treating the consent model in the Data Protection Act as sufficient for all processing contexts; sensitive data and power imbalances demand stricter safeguards.
  • Equating judicial activism with any expansive PIL order; activism must be distinguished from legitimate constitutional adjudication by the presence of policy‑making without textual mandate.
  • Overlooking the public‑policy exception in promissory estoppel; the government may resile if performance would violate a statute or the Constitution.
  • Failing to separate State responsibility for attribution from the due‑diligence obligation to prevent private violations; they engage different legal standards.

Doctrine of Eclipse and Its Application in Constitutional Law

The doctrine of eclipse holds that a pre‑Constitutional law which is inconsistent with a fundamental right does not become void ab initio; it is merely eclipsed or rendered dormant until the constitutional impediment is removed. The Supreme Court first articulated this principle in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), where a pre‑1950 Madhya Pradesh law regulating motor vehicles was held to be eclipsed by Article 19(1)(g) but revived when the Constitution (First Amendment) inserted Article 19(6) permitting reasonable restrictions. The doctrine rests on the premise that the Constitution does not retrospectively annul existing statutes; rather, it suspends their operation to the extent of inconsistency.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the scope. In State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills Ltd. (1974) the Court clarified that the doctrine applies only to laws enacted before the commencement of the Constitution (26 January 1950). Post‑Constitutional laws that violate fundamental rights are void under Article 13(2) and cannot be revived by subsequent amendment. The distinction is crucial for answer‑writing: a candidate must identify the temporal classification of the statute before invoking eclipse. The doctrine also interacts with the basic structure doctrine; in Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) the Court held that an amendment which seeks to revive an eclipsed law by removing the fundamental right itself would be subject to basic structure review.

Practical illustration: the Madhya Pradesh Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1947 was eclipsed by Article 19(1)(g) until the First Amendment inserted Article 19(6). When the amendment was challenged, the Court upheld the revival because the amendment did not alter the basic structure. Conversely, the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1947 provisions that conflicted with Article 21 were not eclipsed because they were re‑enacted after 1950, making them void under Article 13(2). Aspirants should be able to map the timeline, cite the relevant articles (13(1), 13(2), 19(1)(g), 19(6)), and explain the interplay with amendment power under Article 368.

Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Statutory Interpretation

Harmonious construction is a cardinal rule of interpretation that requires courts to construe seemingly conflicting provisions of a statute, or of two statutes, in a manner that gives effect to both, avoiding a declaration of repugnancy unless absolutely inevitable. The Supreme Court in Keshav Mills Co. Ltd. v. Union of India (1973) laid down the principle: “When two provisions of an enactment cannot be reconciled, they should be so interpreted that, if possible, effect may be given to both.” The doctrine operates at three levels: intra‑statutory (within the same Act), inter‑statutory (between two Acts), and constitutional (between a statute and the Constitution).

At the intra‑statutory level, the Court in State of Rajasthan v. Gopi Kishan (1992) reconciled Section 5(1) and Section 5(2) of the Rajasthan Land Revenue Act by reading the latter as an exception to the former, thereby preserving both. At the inter‑statutory level, M. S. M. Sharma v. Krishna Sinha (1959) harmonised the Parliamentary Privileges Act with Article 194 of the Constitution, holding that the Act could not abridge the constitutional privilege. At the constitutional level, the doctrine underpins the “pith and substance” test: in State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951) the Court read the Bombay Prohibition Act harmoniously with Entry 8 of List II, upholding the law as a valid exercise of state legislative competence.

The technique involves three steps: (i) identify the literal meaning of each provision; (ii) ascertain the legislative intent from the scheme, preamble, and history; (iii) adopt the interpretation that avoids conflict and advances the purpose of the enactment. A common examiner trap is to present two provisions that appear contradictory but are actually complementary — for example, Section 24 of the Code of Civil Procedure (transfer of suits) and Section 25 (power of the High Court to transfer). The correct answer demonstrates that Section 25 is a special provision that supplements, not overrides, Section 24. Mastery of harmonious construction is essential for both Paper I (Constitutional Law) and Paper II (Interpretation of Statutes) questions.

Illustrative Problem
A State legislature enacts a law regulating the use of loudspeakers in public places. The law contains two sections: Section 3 prohibits any use of loudspeakers between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m.; Section 4 empowers the District Magistrate to grant permission for “religious or cultural events” during the prohibited hours. A petitioner challenges Section 4 as violating the prohibition in Section 3 and Article 19(1)(a). Apply the doctrine of harmonious construction.
Step 1: Read Sections 3 and 4 together. Section 3 lays down a general rule; Section 4 creates a narrowly tailored exception for a specific category of events. Step 2: Determine legislative intent — the preamble states the law seeks to “balance public tranquillity with cultural expression”. Step 3: Apply harmonious construction: the exception in Section 4 does not nullify Section 3; it merely carves out a limited, discretionary window. The District Magistrate’s power is subject to guidelines (e.g., noise level limits) that preserve the core objective of Section 3. Step 4: Constitutional test — the exception is a reasonable restriction under Article 19(2) because it is content‑neutral, narrowly drawn, and serves the legitimate aim of public order. Therefore, Section 4 is valid and the challenge fails.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents two seemingly conflicting provisions, first quote the harmonious construction principle from Keshav Mills. Then structure the answer in three labelled steps: (1) Literal reading, (2) Legislative intent, (3) Harmonised interpretation. This template signals clarity to the examiner and saves time.

Principle of Proportionality in Administrative Law

The principle of proportionality requires that any administrative action restricting a fundamental right must be suitable, necessary, and balanced in the strict sense. The Indian Supreme Court adopted the four‑stage test from European jurisprudence in Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000): (i) the measure must be designated for a legitimate aim; (ii) it must be rationally connected to that aim; (iii) there must be no less restrictive alternative; (iv) the measure must not impose a disproportionate burden on the right‑holder. In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) the Court applied this test to the internet shutdown in Jammu & Kashmir, holding that the orders failed the “least restrictive means” prong because a complete shutdown was not necessary when targeted restrictions could achieve the same security objective.

Proportionality operates differently from the traditional Wednesbury unreasonableness test. Wednesbury asks whether a decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have made it; proportionality demands a structured balancing of rights and interests. The Court in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) affirmed that proportionality is an inherent component of the right to privacy under Article 21, thereby constitutionalising the doctrine. Consequently, any statutory delegation that permits disproportionate encroachment — for instance, Section 69A of the Information Technology Act allowing blocking of information — must be read down to satisfy the four‑stage test.

In answer‑writing, candidates should distinguish three contexts: (a) legislative proportionality (review of statutes under Article 13), (b) administrative proportionality (review of executive orders), and (c) judicial proportionality (review of court‑ordered remedies such as continuing mandamus). Cite the M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006) formulation for legislative review and the Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016) formulation for administrative review. A high‑scoring answer will also note the “margin of appreciation” accorded to the executive in matters of national security, as recognised in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2004), while insisting that the margin is not unlimited.

State Responsibility for Internationally Wrongful Acts: Defences and Countermeasures

The International Law Commission’s Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (2001) enumerate circumstances precluding wrongfulness in Articles 20‑25. These defences are: (1) consent (Art. 20) — a State may consent to an act that would otherwise breach an obligation; (2) self‑defence (Art. 21) — action taken in conformity with the UN Charter; (3) countermeasures (Art. 22) — proportionate, non‑forcible measures taken by an injured State to induce compliance; (4) force majeure (Art. 23) — an irresistible force or unforeseen event beyond the State’s control; (5) distress (Art. 24) — a situation where the State’s organ has no reasonable alternative to save lives; (6) necessity (Art. 25) — an essential interest threatened by a grave and imminent peril, provided the act does not seriously impair an essential interest of the other State.

The Gabcikovo‑Nagymaros Project (Hungary/Slovakia) (1997) illustrates necessity: Hungary invoked ecological necessity to suspend a treaty, but the ICJ held that the peril was not imminent and the suspension was not the only means, thus rejecting the defence. In LaGrand (Germany v. United States) (2001) the Court examined countermeasures, confirming that they must be reversible, proportionate, and preceded by a demand for compliance. The Oil Platforms (Iran v. United States) (2003) case clarified that self‑defence under Article 51 requires an armed attack; mere threats do not justify force.

For the optional, a candidate must be able to (i) identify the applicable defence from the factual matrix, (ii) articulate the procedural prerequisites — e.g., countermeasures require prior notification and an offer to negotiate, (iii) evaluate proportionality using the “necessary and proportionate” standard, and (iv) discuss the interplay with peremptory norms (jus cogens) which cannot be derogated even by necessity. A typical examiner twist presents a scenario where a State imposes economic sanctions claiming countermeasures, but the sanctions target the civilian population; the correct answer invokes the prohibition on collective punishment and the requirement that countermeasures not affect humanitarian obligations.

Judicial Review of Legislative Privileges and Parliamentary Privileges

Legislative privileges in India derive from Articles 105 and 194 of the Constitution, which confer on Parliament and State Legislatures the same privileges as the House of Commons enjoyed at the commencement of the Constitution. The Supreme Court in Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly (1965) held that the judiciary can review the exercise of privileges to the extent of determining whether a particular act falls within the scope of the privilege; however, the Court cannot question the wisdom or propriety of the legislative decision. In Raja Ram Pal v. Hon’ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007) the Court extended judicial review to the expulsion of members, ruling that the power to expel is not absolute and must conform to natural justice.

The tension between privilege and fundamental rights is resolved through the “harmonious construction” principle. In State of Kerala v. K. Ajith (2021) the Court balanced Article 194(3) (privilege of freedom of speech) against Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech of citizens), holding that legislative speech is protected only when it pertains to legislative functions. The Court also clarified that the privilege against arrest in civil cases (Art. 105(3)) does not extend to criminal proceedings, aligning with the Algaonkar v. State of Maharashtra (1975) precedent.

Answer‑writing should follow a three‑step framework: (i) identify the specific privilege invoked (speech, arrest, contempt, etc.); (ii) locate the constitutional source and any statutory codification (e.g., the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977); (iii) apply the judicial review standard — whether the exercise is “within the four corners” of the privilege and complies with procedural fairness. A common trap is to conflate “parliamentary sovereignty” (a UK concept) with Indian “constitutional sovereignty”; the correct answer emphasises that Indian legislatures are creatures of the Constitution and their privileges are subject to judicial review under Articles 32 and 226.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse with the doctrine of severability; eclipse applies only to pre‑Constitutional laws, severability to post‑Constitutional statutes.
  • Applying harmonious construction where the provisions are irreconcilably repugnant; in such cases the later or more specific provision prevails.
  • Treating proportionality as a mere reasonableness test; proportionality demands a structured four‑stage analysis, not a subjective Wednesbury review.
  • Invoking necessity as a defence for any emergency; necessity under ILC Article 25 requires an imminent peril and the absence of any alternative, and cannot justify violation of jus cogens.
  • Assuming legislative privileges are absolute; they are subject to judicial review for jurisdictional errors and compliance with natural justice.
  • Overlooking the procedural prerequisites for countermeasures — prior demand, proportionality, and reversibility — when analysing State responsibility scenarios.
  • Equating the margin of appreciation in national security with unfettered executive discretion; the margin is limited by the proportionality test and constitutional oversight.

These advanced topics deepen the aspirant’s ability to handle complex, multi‑layered questions that test both doctrinal precision and analytical synthesis.

International Humanitarian Law: Core Principles and Contemporary Challenges

International humanitarian law (IHL) governs the conduct of armed conflict and seeks to limit its effects on persons who are not or no longer participating in hostilities. The four Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their two Additional Protocols of 1977 constitute the treaty backbone, while customary rules fill gaps for non‑State actors and conflicts not covered by the Conventions. The cardinal principles — distinction, proportionality, necessity, and humanity — operate as interpretive lenses for every rule. Distinction obliges parties to differentiate at all times between combatants and civilians, and between military objectives and civilian objects; a breach occurs when an attack is directed at a civilian population or when the attacker fails to take feasible precautions. Proportionality prohibits attacks expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, or damage to civilian objects excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Necessity limits the use of force to that required to achieve a legitimate military aim, and humanity forbids the infliction of suffering beyond what is necessary for the accomplishment of a military purpose.

Contemporary challenges test these principles in asymmetrical warfare, urban combat, and the use of autonomous weapon systems. In non‑international armed conflicts, the threshold for applying Common Article 3 and Additional Protocol II is often contested; the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) maintains that the intensity of violence and the organization of non‑State groups are the decisive criteria. The 2016 ICRC Commentary on the Geneva Conventions clarifies that the “continuous combat function” test determines whether a person may be targeted as a fighter. Urban warfare amplifies proportionality analysis because military objectives are intermingled with dense civilian infrastructure; the 2014 Gaza conflict and the 2022 Ukraine war illustrate how the “feasible precautions” obligation is scrutinised through satellite imagery and open‑source intelligence. Autonomous weapons raise the question of whether a machine can satisfy the “human judgment” requirement embedded in the principles of distinction and proportionality; the 2023 UN Group of Governmental Experts report concludes that meaningful human control must be retained over critical functions of target selection and engagement.

Enforcement mechanisms remain fragmented. The International Criminal Court (ICC) exercises jurisdiction over war crimes when States are unwilling or unable to prosecute, but its reach is limited by the principle of complementarity and the non‑participation of major powers. The International Court of Justice (ICJ) has adjudicated State responsibility for IHL violations, notably in the Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (Democratic Republic of the Congo v. Uganda) (2005) and the Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (The Gambia v. Myanmar) (2022). National courts increasingly invoke universal jurisdiction; the German Federal Court of Justice’s 2021 conviction of a Syrian intelligence officer for crimes against humanity demonstrates the practical reach of the principle. For the optional, candidates must be able to (i) articulate the four core principles with treaty and customary citations, (ii) apply the proportionality test to a factual matrix involving collateral damage estimates, (iii) distinguish between international and non‑international armed conflict thresholds, and (iv) discuss the evolving role of technology in IHL compliance.

Law of Treaties: Reservations, Objections, and the Vienna Convention Regime

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) 1969 provides the comprehensive framework for the formation, interpretation, and termination of treaties. Articles 19‑23 regulate reservations — unilateral statements made by a State when signing, ratifying, accepting, approving or acceding to a treaty, whereby it purports to exclude or modify the legal effect of certain provisions. Article 19 permits a reservation unless (a) the treaty prohibits it, (b) the treaty provides that only specified reservations may be made, or (c) the reservation is incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty. The compatibility test, articulated by the International Court of Justice in the Reservations to the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (1951) Advisory Opinion, requires an assessment of whether the reservation undermines the essential obligations that constitute the treaty’s raison d’être. The Human Rights Committee’s General Comment No. 24 (1994) extends this reasoning to human rights treaties, holding that reservations to non‑derogable rights (e.g., prohibition of torture under Article 7 ICCPR) are per se incompatible.

Objections to reservations are governed by Articles 20‑21. A State may object to a reservation without necessarily rejecting the treaty; the legal effect depends on the intention expressed. If the objecting State declares that the reservation is incompatible with the object and purpose, the treaty does not enter into force between the two States unless the reserving State withdraws the reservation. The 1998 International Law Commission (ILC) Guide to Practice on Reservations to Treaties clarifies that a “silent” objection — mere notification without a statement of incompatibility — leaves the treaty in force, with the reserved provision not applying between the parties. The 2011 Case Concerning the Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Croatia v. Serbia) illustrates the ICJ’s approach: the Court examined whether Serbia’s reservation to Article IX (dispute settlement) was compatible with the Convention’s object and purpose, concluding that it was not, because the reservation would deprive the Court of its core jurisdictional function.

Interpretation of treaties follows Articles 31‑33 VCLT. The general rule (Art. 31) mandates a good‑faith reading of the text in context and in light of the treaty’s object and purpose. Supplementary means (Art. 32) — preparatory work, circumstances of conclusion — may be invoked when the text is ambiguous or leads to a manifestly absurd result. The 2018 Dispute concerning the Delimitation of the Maritime Boundary between Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire (ITLOS) demonstrates the interplay of textualism and teleology: the Tribunal gave primacy to the ordinary meaning of “equitable principles” while referencing the negotiating history to confirm the parties’ shared understanding. For the optional, aspirants must (i) identify the three‑step reservation compatibility test, (ii) differentiate between an objection that blocks entry into force and one that merely modifies inter‑party relations, (iii) apply Articles 31‑33 to a hypothetical treaty clause, and (iv) discuss the effect of subsequent practice under Article 31(3)(b) on the evolution of treaty obligations.

Technique: Assessing Reservation Validity under Article 19 VCLT
A State ratifies the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) but enters a reservation excluding the application of Article 12 (equal recognition before the law) to its domestic guardianship laws. The treaty contains no explicit prohibition on reservations. Determine whether the reservation is permissible.
Step 1: Check treaty prohibition — CRPD Article 46 allows reservations unless incompatible with object and purpose. Step 2: Identify object and purpose — CRPD aims to ensure full and equal enjoyment of all human rights by persons with disabilities; Article 12 is a cornerstone guaranteeing legal capacity. Step 3: Apply compatibility test — The reservation removes the core guarantee of legal capacity for a broad class of persons, thereby defeating the treaty’s essential obligation. The Human Rights Committee’s General Comment No. 24 and the CRPD Committee’s jurisprudence treat reservations to Article 12 as incompatible. Conclusion: The reservation is impermissible; the State remains bound by Article 12, and the reservation is severable, leaving the rest of the treaty in force.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a reservation question appears, first quote Article 19 VCLT verbatim, then run the three‑step test in a numbered list. Cite the Genocide Convention Advisory Opinion and General Comment No. 24 as authority. This structure signals doctrinal rigour and saves time.

Corporate Criminal Liability: Vicarious Liability, Mens Rea, and the Doctrine of Identification

Corporate criminal liability addresses the attribution of criminal conduct to a legal person. Two principal models dominate common‑law jurisdictions: the vicarious liability model and the identification (or “directing mind”) model. Under vicarious liability, a corporation is held responsible for offences committed by its employees or agents acting within the scope of their employment, irrespective of the corporation’s own fault. This model, rooted in the Salmond principle, is statutorily entrenched in many regulatory regimes (e.g., the UK Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, the US Federal Sentencing Guidelines). The identification model, articulated in Lennard’s Carrying Co Ltd v. Asiatic Petroleum Co Ltd (1915) and refined in Tesco Supermarkets Ltd v. Nattrass (1972), requires that the natural person who commits the actus reus and possesses the requisite mens rea be the “directing mind and will” of the corporation — typically a board member or senior manager whose decisions embody corporate policy.

The mens rea requirement varies with the offence. For strict liability offences (e.g., environmental pollution under the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974), no fault element is needed; the corporation is liable once the actus reus is proved. For offences requiring intention, knowledge, or recklessness, the prosecution must demonstrate that the identified individual possessed the mental element. The 2008 UK Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act introduced a new statutory test: an organisation is guilty if the way its activities are managed or organised causes a person’s death and amounts to a gross breach of a relevant duty of care. This shifts focus from a single “directing mind” to systemic management failures, aligning with the “failure of corporate culture” approach advocated in the 2010 R v. Cotswold Geotechnical Holdings Ltd decision.

In India, the Supreme Court in Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement (2005) recognised that a company can be prosecuted for offences under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002, applying the identification principle to the “alter ego” of the company. The 2013 Companies Act, Section 447, imposes liability for fraud on the company and its officers, while Section 212 enables the Serious Fraud Investigation Office to attribute knowledge to the corporation through its senior management. Comparative analysis shows a trend toward expanding corporate liability beyond the narrow identification model, especially in regulatory and economic offences. For the optional, candidates must (i) contrast vicarious liability with the identification doctrine, (ii) explain how mens rea is imputed under each model, (iii) evaluate the impact of the UK Corporate Manslaughter Act on the traditional directing‑mind test, and (iv) apply Indian statutory provisions to a scenario involving a multinational subsidiary’s bribery under the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988.

Environmental Law: Sustainable Development, Precautionary Principle, and the Polluter Pays Principle

Environmental law in the international and domestic spheres rests on three interlocking principles that shape judicial reasoning and legislative design. Sustainable development, articulated in the 1987 Brundtland Report and endorsed at the 1992 Rio Declaration (Principle 3), demands that present needs be met without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. The Indian Supreme Court in Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) elevated sustainable development to a constitutional mandate, reading it into Articles 21, 48A, and 51A(g). The Court held that economic development cannot be pursued at the cost of ecological degradation and that the “precautionary principle” and “polluter pays principle” are essential components of sustainable development.

The precautionary principle, formulated in Principle 15 of the Rio Declaration, provides that lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost‑effective measures to prevent environmental degradation. The European Court of Justice in Case C‑157/96, The Queen v. Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food, ex parte Federation of Seed Industry (1998) held that the principle authorises restrictive measures when there is a plausible risk, even if the probability cannot be quantified. In India, the National Green Tribunal (NGT) routinely invokes the principle to grant interim injunctions against projects lacking comprehensive Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs), as seen in Goa Foundation v. Union of India (2014) concerning mining leases in the Western Ghats.

The polluter pays principle, enshrined in Principle 16 of the Rio Declaration and the 1992 OECD Recommendation, requires that the polluter bear the cost of pollution prevention, control, and remediation. The Indian Supreme Court in Indian Council for Enviro‑Legal Action v. Union of India (1996) ordered the polluting industries in the Bichhri village case to pay for the cleanup of contaminated groundwater, establishing a precedent for absolute liability for hazardous activities. The 2010 Deepak Nitrite Ltd v. State of Gujarat decision extended the principle to require financial assurances (bank guarantees) before granting environmental clearances. Internationally, the 2010 Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit‑Sharing incorporates the principle by obligating users of genetic resources to share benefits with provider countries. For the optional, aspirants must (i) articulate the three principles with treaty and judicial citations, (ii) demonstrate how the NGT applies the precautionary principle in interim relief jurisprudence, (iii) analyse the shift from fault‑based liability to absolute liability in hazardous industry cases, and (iv) evaluate the tension between sustainable development and the “ease of doing business” narrative in recent amendments to the EIA Notification 2006.

Arbitration Law: Seat, Venue, and Enforcement of Foreign Awards under the New York Convention

The distinction between the seat (legal place) and the venue (physical location) of arbitration is pivotal for determining the supervisory jurisdiction of courts and the enforceability of awards. The seat determines the lex arbitri — the procedural law governing the arbitration — and confers exclusive jurisdiction on the courts of the seat to hear applications for setting aside the award under Article V(1)(e) of the New York Convention 1958. The venue merely denotes where hearings are held and does not affect the governing law. In Bharat Aluminium Co v. Kaiser Aluminium Technical Services (2012), the Indian Supreme Court held that Part I of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 applies only to arbitrations seated in India; for foreign‑seated arbitrations, only Part II (enforcement) is applicable, thereby ending the “Bhatia” era of judicial intervention in foreign arbitrations.

The New York Convention obliges Contracting States to recognise and enforce foreign arbitral awards, subject to the limited grounds in Article V. These grounds include incapacity of parties, invalid arbitration agreement, lack of proper notice, award beyond scope, composition irregularities, and the award not yet binding or set aside in the seat. The “public policy” exception (Art. V(2)(b)) is narrowly construed; the Supreme Court in ONGC v. Saw Pipes Ltd (2003) initially adopted a broad view, but the 2015 Amendment to the Arbitration Act and the 2017 Venture Global Engineering v. Satyam Computer Services decision restricted public policy to “fundamental policy of Indian law”, “interest of India”, and “justice or morality”. The 2020 Hindustan Construction Co v. Union of India ruling clarified that an award violating the “patent illegality” standard cannot be set aside under Section 34 unless it shocks the conscience of the court.

Enforcement proceedings under Section 48 of the Arbitration Act mirror Article V defences. The burden of proof lies on the party resisting enforcement. The 2021 Daimler AG v. Indian Oil Corporation case illustrated that a foreign award may be refused enforcement if the arbitration agreement is null and void under the law to which the parties subjected it (Art. V(1)(a)). The interplay between the seat’s supervisory jurisdiction and the enforcement court’s limited review creates a “dual control” regime; practitioners must ensure that the arbitration clause expressly designates the seat to avoid inadvertent application of Part I. For the optional, candidates must (i) differentiate seat and venue with statutory and case law authority, (ii) enumerate the Article V grounds and map them to Section 48 defences, (iii) analyse the evolution of the public policy exception post‑2015 Amendment, and (iv) draft an arbitration clause that secures a neutral seat while preserving the enforceability of the award in multiple jurisdictions.

  • Confusing the precautionary principle with the prevention principle; the former permits action despite scientific uncertainty, the latter requires known risk.
  • Treating the polluter pays principle as a mere cost‑recovery rule; it also imposes absolute liability for hazardous activities irrespective of fault.
  • Assuming the identification doctrine applies uniformly to all corporate offences; strict liability statutes bypass mens rea attribution.
  • Overlooking the distinction between seat and venue when drafting arbitration clauses, leading to unintended application of Part I of the Arbitration Act.
  • Applying the broad pre‑2015 “public policy” test to set aside foreign awards; the current standard is confined to fundamental policy, national interest, and justice or morality.
  • Failing to run the three‑step reservation compatibility test (prohibition, specified reservations, object‑purpose) before concluding permissibility.
  • Neglecting the procedural prerequisites for countermeasures — prior demand, proportionality, reversibility — when analysing State responsibility scenarios.

Doctrine of Constitutional Morality and Its Judicial Application

The concept of constitutional morality was first articulated by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly debates (1948) and later crystallised by the Supreme Court in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) where the Court held that constitutional morality requires adherence to the core values of the Constitution — liberty, equality, fraternity and dignity — even when they conflict with majoritarian social morality. The doctrine operates as an interpretive lens for judicial review of legislation and executive action, compelling courts to examine whether a law or policy respects the constitutional ethos rather than merely conforming to procedural validity. In Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2019) (Sabarimala case), the majority invoked constitutional morality to strike down the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the temple, emphasising that the Constitution’s transformative promise cannot be subordinated to customary practice. The doctrine also underpins the basic structure jurisprudence: in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) the Court linked the inviolability of the basic structure to the moral commitments of the Constitution. Critics argue that constitutional morality grants judges excessive discretion; however, the Court in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) clarified that the doctrine is anchored in textual provisions (Articles 14, 15, 19, 21) and the Preamble, limiting arbitrary invocation. For the optional, aspirants must (i) trace the historical evolution from Ambedkar’s speeches to contemporary judgments, (ii) distinguish constitutional morality from public morality and societal morality, (iii) analyse its role in expanding fundamental rights (privacy, sexual orientation, gender identity), and (iv) evaluate the tension between judicial activism and democratic legitimacy when the doctrine is used to invalidate popular legislation.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code: Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process and Recent Amendments

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) introduced a time‑bound Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) aimed at maximising asset value and promoting entrepreneurship. The CIRP is initiated by a financial creditor, operational creditor or the corporate debtor itself upon a default of at least ₹1 crore (raised from ₹1 lakh by the 2020 amendment). Once admitted, a moratorium under Section 14 halts all litigation and enforcement actions, and an Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) takes control of the debtor’s affairs. The Committee of Creditors (CoC), constituted under Section 21, holds the commercial wisdom to approve a resolution plan by a 66 % voting share (reduced from 75 % by the 2019 amendment). The 2020 amendment introduced a “pre‑packaged” insolvency framework for MSMEs, allowing a debtor‑in‑possession model with a 90‑day resolution window, and the 2021 amendment inserted Section 29A disqualifications to prevent wilful defaulters and related parties from submitting plans. The Supreme Court in Essar Steel India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta (2019) upheld the primacy of financial creditors and the 66 % threshold, while Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure Ltd. v. Union of India (2020) clarified that the moratorium does not extend to personal guarantors. The 2022 amendment (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2022) introduced a “cross‑border insolvency” framework aligned with the UNCITRAL Model Law, enabling cooperation with foreign courts. For the optional, candidates must (i) map the CIRP timeline (admission, IRP appointment, CoC formation, resolution plan submission, approval, implementation), (ii) compare the pre‑pack and regular CIRP regimes, (iii) analyse the constitutional validity of Section 29A in light of Article 14, and (iv) discuss the impact of the 2022 cross‑border provisions on multinational corporate groups.

Illustrative Problem
A financial creditor files a CIRP application against XYZ Ltd. for a default of ₹5 crore. The debtor proposes a resolution plan that offers 30 % recovery to financial creditors and 100 % to operational creditors. The CoC, comprising 70 % financial creditor voting share, rejects the plan. The debtor challenges the rejection before the NCLT, arguing that the plan treats operational creditors fairly. Decide the maintainability of the challenge.
The challenge is not maintainable. Under Section 30(4) of the IBC, the CoC’s commercial wisdom is paramount; a resolution plan requires approval of 66 % of the voting share of financial creditors. The CoC’s rejection, backed by 70 % voting share, is a valid exercise of its statutory discretion. The NCLT cannot substitute its judgment for that of the CoC unless the decision is mala fide, arbitrary or violates the Code (see Essar Steel). The differential treatment of operational creditors does not render the rejection illegal, as the Code prioritises financial creditors’ recovery. Therefore, the NCLT will dismiss the debtor’s application.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “CIRP timeline”, present a concise flowchart: Default → Application (Section 7/9/10) → Admission (Section 12) → Moratorium (Section 14) → IRP → CoC (Section 21) → Resolution Plan (Section 30) → Approval (66 % vote) → Implementation (Section 31). Cite the 2019 and 2020 amendments for voting threshold and pre‑pack provisions respectively. This visual aid fetches extra marks for clarity.

Intellectual Property Rights: Compulsory Licensing, Patent Evergreening and Access to Medicines

The Patents Act, 1970 (as amended in 2005 to comply with TRIPS) balances patent holder rights with public health imperatives. Section 84 empowers the Controller of Patents to grant a compulsory licence (CL) after three years from patent grant if the reasonable requirements of the public are not satisfied, the patented invention is not available at a reasonably affordable price, or the invention is not worked in India. The first CL in India was granted in 2012 to Natco Pharma for Bayer’s cancer drug Nexavar (sorafenib), setting a precedent that the “reasonable affordability” test includes the economic capacity of the target population. Patent evergreening — the practice of obtaining secondary patents on minor modifications to extend monopoly — is curtailed by Section 3(d), which excludes “mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy”. The Supreme Court in Novartis AG v. Union of India (2013) upheld the rejection of a patent for the beta‑crystalline form of imatinib mesylate (Glivec), affirming that enhanced therapeutic efficacy must be demonstrated. The Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health (2001) and the subsequent 2003 WTO General Council Decision permit export of medicines produced under CL to countries with insufficient manufacturing capacity; India’s 2005 amendment incorporated this via Section 92A. Recent debates centre on the COVID‑19 pandemic: India and South Africa’s 2020 proposal for a TRIPS waiver on vaccine patents was partially accommodated by the 2022 WTO Ministerial Decision allowing limited export of COVID‑19 vaccines under CL. For the optional, aspirants must (i) enumerate the statutory grounds for CL under Sections 84, 92, 92A, (ii) analyse the judicial interpretation of “enhancement of efficacy” in Section 3(d), (iii) evaluate the tension between evergreening prevention and incentivising incremental innovation, and (iv) discuss the interplay of CL regime with India’s obligations under the TRIPS Agreement and the Doha Declaration.

Law of Evidence: Hearsay Rule, Documentary Evidence and the Best Evidence Rule

The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 governs admissibility of evidence in civil and criminal proceedings. The hearsay rule, embodied in Section 60, requires that oral evidence be direct — the witness must have perceived the fact personally. Exceptions are enumerated in Sections 32 (dying declarations, statements in course of business), 33 (evidence of former proceedings), and 35 (entries in public records). The Supreme Court in State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2003) held that a dying declaration recorded by a magistrate under Section 164 CrPC is admissible even if the declarant later survives, provided the statement was made under a settled expectation of death. Documentary evidence is regulated by Sections 61‑90. The “best evidence rule” (Section 64) mandates production of the original document unless it is lost, destroyed, or in the possession of the adverse party. Secondary evidence (copies, oral accounts) is permissible under Section 65 only after satisfying the court of the original’s unavailability. The 2000 amendment introduced Sections 65A‑65B for electronic records, requiring a certificate under Section 65B(4) for admissibility; the Supreme Court in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020) clarified that the certificate is a condition precedent, not a mere formality. The Act also recognises presumptions: Section 79 presumes genuineness of certified copies of public documents; Section 90 presumes documents thirty years old are authentic if produced from proper custody. For the optional, candidates must (i) differentiate hearsay from admissible exceptions with case law, (ii) explain the procedural steps for admitting electronic records post‑2020, (iii) analyse the interplay between the best evidence rule and the secondary evidence provisions, and (iv) evaluate the impact of the 2020 Arjun Panditrao judgment on digital evidence in criminal trials.

Uniform Civil Code Debate: Personal Law Reforms and Constitutional Challenges

Article 44 of the Constitution directs the State to endeavour to secure a Uniform Civil Code (UCC) for all citizens. The debate intensified after the Supreme Court’s 1985 Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum judgment, which granted maintenance to a divorced Muslim woman under Section 125 CrPC, prompting the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 to nullify the ruling. In Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India (1995) the Court urged the legislature to enact a UCC to prevent fraudulent conversion for bigamy. The 2017 Shayara Bano v. Union of India (triple talaq) judgment declared instant triple talaq unconstitutional, reinforcing the argument that personal laws must conform to fundamental rights. The Law Commission’s 2018 consultation paper recommended a “progressive” UCC preserving cultural diversity while ensuring gender justice. Critics contend that a UCC would violate Article 25 (freedom of religion) and Article 29 (protection of minority culture). The Supreme Court in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2019) observed that the Constitution’s transformative vision permits reform of personal laws without a monolithic code. Recent legislative attempts include the 2019 Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act criminalising triple talaq, and the 2022 Uttarakhand UCC Bill (still pending). For the optional, aspirants must (i) trace the constitutional provisions (Articles 25, 26, 29, 44) and judicial pronouncements shaping the UCC discourse, (ii) analyse the tension between secular uniformity and pluralistic personal laws, (iii) evaluate the Law Commission’s “progressive UCC” model versus a “one‑size‑fits‑all” approach, and (iv) discuss the feasibility of a UCC in light of federalism (State List entries on marriage, succession) and the requirement of Presidential assent for State UCC legislation.

  • Assuming the 66 % voting threshold applies to operational creditors; it applies only to financial creditors in the CoC.
  • Treating Section 3(d) as a blanket bar on all pharmaceutical patents; it only blocks patents lacking enhanced therapeutic efficacy.
  • Believing the best evidence rule prohibits all copies; secondary evidence is admissible once the original’s unavailability is proved.
  • Arguing that Article 44 imposes a justiciable duty; it is a Directive Principle, non‑enforceable by courts.
  • Overlooking the requirement of a Section 65B certificate for every electronic record; the 2020 Supreme Court ruling makes it mandatory.
  • Neglecting the distinction between pre‑pack and regular CIRP timelines when answering procedural questions.
