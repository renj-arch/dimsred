Key Concepts

Urdu Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Urdu is Indo-Aryan and is written in Perso-Arabic script (Nastaliq). It is spoken mainly in pan-India. It is one of the original Eighth Schedule languages (1950). The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Deccani school, Delhi school, Lucknow school, Progressive Writers’ Movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: traditional rhetorics (ilm-e-bayan), progressive criticism, modernist and feminist approaches
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Early period (13th–17th c.): Amir Khusrau, Wali Dakani and the Deccan schools.
  • Classical period (18th–19th c.): Mir Taqi Mir, Mirza Ghalib and the Delhi and Lucknow schools.
  • Modern period (19th–20th c.): Aligarh movement, Iqbal and the Progressive Writers’ Movement.
  • Contemporary period: Partition literature, fiction and diaspora writing.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Urdu literature.
Answer: Early period (13th–17th c.) — Amir Khusrau, Wali Dakani and the Deccan schools.; Classical period (18th–19th c.) — Mir Taqi Mir, Mirza Ghalib and the Delhi and Lucknow schools.; Modern period (19th–20th c.) — Aligarh movement, Iqbal and the Progressive Writers’ Movement.; Contemporary period — Partition literature, fiction and diaspora writing..

Standard Sources

  • Sahitya Akademi histories of Urdu literature
  • Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature
  • University syllabi and prescribed texts
  • Standard Urdu literary histories

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Urdu authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Urdu literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

The Anatomy of the 'Tashkili' vs. 'Adabi' Shift in Exam Logic

While standard textbooks treat Urdu literature as a linear progression from Amir Khusrau to contemporary fiction, the examiner often tests the mechanism of literary change rather than just the chronology. The critical pivot point is understanding the distinction between Tashkili (structural/formal innovation) and Adabi (thematic/ideological shift). Most aspirants fail to distinguish that the 'Progressive Writers' Movement' was not merely a change in theme (social realism), but a radical break in form (rejecting ornate ghazal styles for direct narrative prose).

Advanced Mechanism: The examiner frequently asks about the "Silent Periods" or exceptions to the rule. For instance, while the 'Delhi School' is celebrated for Ghalib's philosophical depth, the exception lies in the simultaneous rise of the 'Deccan School' which prioritized emotional realism over metaphysical abstraction. A high-scoring answer must explicitly compare these two schools not just by name, but by analyzing their specific linguistic registers (Dakhni vs. Khari Boli) and how they influenced the standardization of Urdu.

Technique: The Comparative Matrix
Question: "Critically examine whether the transition from the Delhi School to the Progressive Writers' Movement was a natural evolution or a disruptive rupture in Urdu aesthetics."
Solution Strategy:
  1. Contextualize: Define 'Delhi School' (Ghalib) as focusing on 'Ishq-e-Majazi' (metaphorical love) and philosophical ambiguity.
  2. Analyze the Rupture: Contrast this with the Progressive Writers' Movement (P.W.M.), which demanded 'Ishq-e-Haqiqi' (social love) and explicit political messaging. Note that P.W.M. authors often rejected the ghazal form entirely for short stories and novels.
  3. Conclusion: Conclude it was a disruptive rupture, not an evolution, citing the shift from individual introspection to collective social consciousness as the defining metric of change.

Decoding the 'Ilm-e-Bayan' in Modern Criticism

Paper II often requires applying traditional rhetorical principles ('Ilm-e-Bayan') to modern texts. The advanced trap here is assuming that modern criticism has abandoned tradition entirely. In reality, many contemporary critics use classical terms like Tasawwur (conception), Akhlaq (characterization), and Majaz (metaphor) to critique modern fiction.

Applied Scenario: When analyzing a modern novel, do not just summarize the plot. Instead, identify where the author uses classical rhetorical devices. For example, in analyzing Saadat Hasan Manto's short stories, one must point out how he subverts the classical concept of Akhyar (imagery) by using stark, unadorned realism ('Sadaqaat') to create a new kind of emotional impact. The examiner looks for this synthesis of old terms and new contexts.

Technique: The Textual Micro-Analysis
Question: "Analyze the use of 'Majaz' (metaphor) in Ghalib's ghazal versus the 'Sadaqaat' (realism) in Manto's fiction. How does this reflect the changing literary consciousness?"
Solution Strategy:
  • Define Terms: Briefly define 'Majaz' as figurative language used to evoke emotion through ambiguity, and 'Sadaqaat' as literal description used for social critique.
  • Compare Usage: Show how Ghalib uses 'Majaz' to create a dream-like state (Khayali), whereas Manto uses 'Sadaqaat' to expose the brutal reality of Partition.
  • Evaluate Impact: Argue that this shift represents the movement from 'Adabi' (literary/aesthetic) concerns to 'Jazbati' (emotional/social) concerns, marking a definitive era in Urdu literature.

The Partition Narrative: Beyond Plot Summary

A common error is treating Partition literature as a single monolithic genre. The advanced strategy involves categorizing the 'Partition Narrative' into three distinct sub-genres based on linguistic and thematic focus:

  1. The Trauma Narrative: Focuses on immediate violence and loss (e.g., Manto, Qurratulain Hyder).
  2. The Diaspora/Exile Narrative: Focuses on the psychological displacement of the migrant (e.g., Bapsi Sidhwa's 'Cracking Grounds', though originally in English, is deeply rooted in Urdu cultural memory; or works by Faiz Ahmed Faiz).
  3. The Reconciliation/Myth-Making Narrative: Attempts to heal wounds through fiction (e.g., contemporary writers like Agha Shahid Ali's poetry).

The examiner may ask you to compare a specific text from this era with a pre-Partition classic. The key is to highlight how the language itself changes—from the ornate, courtly Urdu of the 19th century to the fractured, colloquial Urdu of the Partition era.

Technique: The Inter-textual Link
Question: "Trace the evolution of the concept of 'Maut' (Death) in Urdu literature from Ghalib to Manto."
Solution Strategy:

In Ghalib, 'Maut' is often personified as a lover or a philosophical constant ('Maut ka dost'). It is abstract and aesthetic. In Manto, 'Maut' becomes a physical, violent agent of destruction, often linked to the partition lines. The solution must explicitly contrast these two representations to show the shift from metaphysical death to historical trauma.

Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Keyword' Anchor
When writing answers for Paper I or II, always anchor your argument with one specific Urdu term (e.g., 'Ishq', 'Vatan', 'Jazba'). Start your paragraph by defining this term in the classical sense, then show how it evolved in the modern period. This demonstrates to the examiner that you understand both the history and the critical theory simultaneously.
  • Over-generalizing Movements: Do not say "The Progressive Writers' Movement was about socialism." Be specific: it was about "social realism rooted in Marxist ideology but expressed through indigenous Urdu forms."
  • Ignoring Dialects: Never ignore the role of 'Dakhni' or 'Braj Bhasha' influences on standard Urdu. Mentioning dialect variation adds depth to Paper I answers.
  • Plot Summaries in Paper II: Avoid retelling the story of a novel. Focus exclusively on themes, character development ('Akhlaq'), and linguistic style ('Sabaq').
  • Misquoting Poets: When quoting Ghalib or Iqbal, ensure the translation matches the original Urdu sentiment. A mistranslation of a key word like 'Jazba' (passion) vs 'Ishq' (divine love) can lose marks.
