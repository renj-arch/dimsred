Urdu Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Urdu developed from the Khari Boli dialect with Persian and Arabic influences in the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal periods. Amir Khusrau is an early figure; the ghazal became its signature form.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Early period (13th–17th c.): Amir Khusrau, Wali Dakani and the Deccan schools.
  • Classical period (18th–19th c.): Mir Taqi Mir, Mirza Ghalib and the Delhi and Lucknow schools.
  • Modern period (19th–20th c.): Aligarh movement, Iqbal and the Progressive Writers’ Movement.
  • Contemporary period: Partition literature, fiction and diaspora writing.

Major Authors

  • Mirza Ghalib: 19th-century poet; master of the ghazal.
  • Mir Taqi Mir: 18th-century poet; foundational Urdu ghazal poet.
  • Allama Iqbal: Poet and philosopher; Bang-e-Dra.
  • Faiz Ahmed Faiz: Progressive poet; Naqsh-e-Faryadi.
  • Ismat Chughtai: Fiction writer; Lifting the Veil.

Major Works

  • Diwan-e-Ghalib — Mirza Ghalib: Collection of Urdu ghazals.
  • Bang-e-Dra — Allama Iqbal: Poetic collection.
  • Naqsh-e-Faryadi — Faiz Ahmed Faiz: Progressive poetry collection.
  • Aag Ka Darya — Qurratulain Hyder: Novel of Indian history and identity.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Mirza Ghalib in the literary history of Urdu and assess their contribution.
Answer: Mirza Ghalib belongs to the Early period (13th–17th c.) phase. 19th-century poet; master of the ghazal. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Early period (13th–17th c.) → Classical period (18th–19th c.) → Modern period (19th–20th c.) → Contemporary period.
  • Authors: Mirza Ghalib, Mir Taqi Mir, Allama Iqbal, Faiz Ahmed Faiz, Ismat Chughtai.
  • Works: Diwan-e-Ghalib, Bang-e-Dra, Naqsh-e-Faryadi, Aag Ka Darya.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

The Mechanics of Literary Movements: From Ideology to Textual Manifestation

While standard texts list movements chronologically, a senior-level analysis requires understanding the internal mechanics that drove them. The Aligarh Movement, for instance, was not merely a literary trend but a socio-political project where literature served as a vehicle for modernization and Muslim identity formation. Similarly, the Progressive Writers' Movement in the 1930s was defined by its rejection of feudal themes and its adoption of socialist realism, which fundamentally altered the Urdu novel's structure.

Examiners often twist questions by asking for exceptions to these movements. For example, while the Progressive Writers were predominantly secular-nationalist in their later phase, early figures like Khwaja Mir Darwish or Muhammad Iqbal operated within a different ideological framework that the movement eventually rejected. Understanding these divergences is crucial for high-scoring answers.

Furthermore, one must analyze how specific literary forms evolved due to these movements. The transition from the classical Ghazal of Ghalib to the narrative-driven Nasr (prose) in the Progressive era represents a structural shift in Urdu literature. This shift was necessitated by the need to depict complex social realities like poverty and caste oppression, which the lyrical ghazal could not adequately capture.

Another critical dimension is the role of language standardization. The Urdu Zaban-e-Aam (common Urdu) movement sought to purify the language from excessive Persianisms while retaining its aesthetic core. This linguistic purification was a prerequisite for the rise of modern fiction and journalism in Urdu, marking a distinct break from the courtly literature of the Mughal era.

Technique: Comparative Analysis of Movements
Question: Compare and contrast the ideological foundations of the Aligarh Movement and the Progressive Writers' Movement in Urdu literature. How did their respective approaches to language and form differ?
Solution:
The Aligarh Movement (late 19th c.) was rooted in Islamic modernism and rationalism, championed by Sir Syed Ahmed Khan. Its literary output focused on translation of Western classics into Urdu to foster scientific temper, utilizing a formal, Persian-influenced style.
In contrast, the Progressive Writers' Movement (1930s) emerged from socialist and secular-nationalist ideologies. It rejected feudal themes and sought to give voice to the oppressed masses.
Difference in Form: Aligarh literature remained largely within the bounds of poetry and translation, whereas Progressive literature aggressively adopted the novel (Nasr) and short story to depict social realism.
Difference in Language: While Aligarhists used a dignified, semi-classical Urdu, Progressives advocated for Zaban-e-Aam (common language) to ensure accessibility, though they often struggled between purity and simplicity.

Landmark Texts: Analyzing the "Why" Behind Major Works

Standard syllabi list major works like Aag Ka Darya or Lifting the Veil, but a deep dive requires analyzing why these specific texts became landmarks. Qurratulain Hyder's Aag Ka Darya is not just a novel; it is a historiographical attempt to create a 'composite culture' narrative that challenged the binary of Hindu-Muslim divide prevalent in colonial historiography.

Similarly, Ismat Chughtai's Lifting the Veil was groundbreaking not merely for its subject matter but for its linguistic boldness. It broke the taboo of discussing women's sexuality and domestic oppression in Urdu prose, forcing a re-evaluation of what constituted 'respectable' literature. This text serves as a case study for how literature can drive social reform.

One must also consider the works of Mirza Ghalib not just as collections but as philosophical dialogues. His Diwan often functions as a critique of religious orthodoxy and political instability, using irony and ambiguity to navigate the sensitive socio-religious landscape of 19th-century Delhi.

Finally, contemporary works from the diaspora or post-Partition era introduce themes of displacement and hybrid identity. These texts challenge the reader to understand Urdu literature as a global phenomenon rather than one confined to the Indian subcontinent, expanding the scope of the syllabus significantly.

Exam-Day Strategy: The "Twist" Defense
When an examiner asks about a movement or author, they often look for the 'exception' or the 'counter-narrative'. For instance, if asked about Progressive Writers, mention how some members later drifted towards religious fundamentalism. Always include one native term (e.g., Zaban-e-Aam, Adabi Jazba) to show linguistic fluency. If a question asks for development, sketch a simple timeline in the margin connecting the Aligarh movement to the Progressive era via the partition.

Critical Evaluation: Assessing Impact and Legacy

Beyond listing facts, the advanced aspirant must be able to critically evaluate the impact of these movements. Did the Aligarh Movement succeed in creating a modern Muslim intellectual class? Yes, but did it fail to address internal communal tensions? A balanced answer weighs these pros and cons.

Similarly, assessing the legacy of the Progressive Writers requires acknowledging their success in popularizing socialist themes while critiquing their occasional dogmatism. Literature that is too preachy often fails artistically; a nuanced answer would point out this tension between art and activism.

Furthermore, one should evaluate the role of women writers like Ismat Chughtai and Qurratulain Hyder not just as authors but as agents of change who reshaped the literary canon. Their inclusion marks a shift from male-dominated narratives to a more inclusive literary history.

Finally, connecting historical movements to current issues in Urdu literature adds depth. For example, discussing how the themes of Partition in early 20th-century literature resonate with contemporary migration stories creates a bridge between history and relevance, which is highly valued in UPSC evaluations.

  • Trap 1: Confusing the dates of the Aligarh Movement's literary phase with its educational phase (University of Delhi vs. MAO College).
  • Trap 2: Assuming all Progressive Writers were secular; some had complex relationships with religion that need careful wording.
  • Trap 3: Overlooking the Deccan school's contribution (Wali Dakani) when focusing only on Delhi and Lucknow schools.
  • Trap 4: Misidentifying Aag Ka Darya as a collection of poems rather than a historical novel.
  • Trap 5: Forgetting to mention the influence of Persian on early Urdu when discussing Amir Khusrau or Wali Dakani.

The Deccan School and the Formation of Early Urdu Poetics

The Deccan (Dakani) school constitutes the earliest identifiable layer of Urdu literature, flourishing from the late sixteenth to the early eighteenth century in the sultanates of Bijapur, Golconda, and Ahmadnagar. Its poets — most notably Wali Dakani (1667‑1707), Ibn‑e‑Nishati, and Hashmi Bijapuri — wrote in a dialect that blended local Dakhini vocabulary with Persian syntactic structures, creating a literary idiom distinct from the later Delhi‑Lucknow continuum. Wali’s Diwan demonstrates a mature ghazal form that already employs the radif‑qafiya scheme, yet his imagery draws heavily on Deccani folk motifs, Sufi symbolism, and the Persianate masnavi tradition. The school’s significance lies in three mechanisms: (1) it proved that a vernacular could sustain the full apparatus of Persian poetic forms; (2) it supplied a corpus of rekhta (mixed) verse that later Delhi poets such as Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Ghalib would cite as a precedent for linguistic legitimacy; and (3) it established a manuscript culture — illustrated codices, marginal glosses, and patron‑driven anthologies — that prefigured the print culture of the nineteenth century. Scholars of the language‑history segment must note that the Deccan school’s orthography retained the nastaʿlīq script but introduced diacritical marks for retroflex consonants, a feature that later standardizers at Fort William College would codify.

Because the Deccan sultanates fell to Mughal expansion by 1687, the school’s direct patronage collapsed, yet its poetic vocabulary migrated northward with migrating scholars and merchants. This migration explains the presence of Dakhini lexical items — chhota, bazaar, dhobi — in early eighteenth‑century Delhi poetry. In examination answers, linking the Deccan school to the later “Rekhta” label demonstrates an understanding of continuity rather than rupture.

Print Culture, Fort William College, and the Standardization of Urdu Prose

The establishment of Fort William College in Calcutta (1800) under the East India Company marked the first systematic state‑driven effort to produce Urdu textbooks for administrative training. The college’s presses, notably the Matbaʿ‑e‑Mujtaba and later the Naval Kishore Press (Lucknow, 1858), transformed manuscript culture into a reproducible print culture. Two parallel processes emerged: (a) the compilation of grammar books — John Gilchrist’s Grammar of the Hindoostanee Language (1796) and later the Urdu‑English Dictionary (1818) — which fixed spelling conventions, standardized the izafat construction, and codified the use of the Persian‑derived zabar, zer, pesh diacritics; (b) the translation of European scientific, legal, and historical works into Urdu, creating a modern prose register (nasr) capable of expressing abstract concepts without resorting to Arabic‑Persian periphrasis. The college’s “Munshi” cadre — native scholars such as Mir Amman (author of Bagh‑o‑Bahar, 1801) and Hafizuddin Ahmad — produced the first Urdu novels and moral tales that used a deliberately simplified syntax (zaban‑e‑aam) while retaining Persian lexical prestige. This tension between accessibility and literary dignity became a defining feature of nineteenth‑century Urdu prose and resurfaced in the Progressive Writers’ debates of the 1930s. For the exam, candidates should be able to trace a direct line from Gilchrist’s orthographic tables to the 1937 Anjuman‑e‑Taraqqi‑e‑Urdu spelling reforms, noting that each reform was justified by pedagogical utility rather than aesthetic purity.

Worked Example: Tracing Orthographic Reform
Question: Explain how the orthographic choices made at Fort William College influenced the 1937 Anjuman‑e‑Taraqqi‑e‑Urdu spelling reform.
Answer: Fort William College standardized the representation of retroflex consonants (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ) by adopting distinct diacritics on the Persian script, a departure from the earlier Deccani practice of using the same letters for dental and retroflex sounds. Gilchrist’s grammar tables codified these marks, and the college’s textbooks disseminated them across North India. When the Anjuman convened in 1937, it retained the Fort William diacritics for retroflexes but eliminated the redundant zabar/zer/pesh variations for short vowels in non‑Qur’anic texts, arguing that the simplified system reduced printing costs and matched the phonology of spoken Urdu. The continuity lies in the principle that orthography should serve pedagogical clarity — a principle first articulated in the college’s 1801 prospectus.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “institutional factors” behind language standardization, name Fort William College, the Naval Kishore Press, and the Anjuman‑e‑Taraqqi‑e‑Urdu in chronological order. Pair each with one concrete output (grammar, dictionary, spelling reform) to show cause‑effect reasoning.

The Evolution of the Urdu Ghazal: Classical Foundations to Modernist Experiments

The ghazal remains the most resilient form in Urdu poetry, yet its internal architecture shifted dramatically across three phases. The classical phase (c. 1700‑1850) — represented by Mir, Sauda, and Ghalib — adhered to strict beher (meter) rules derived from Arabic ʿarūḍ, employed a fixed radif‑qafiya pattern, and cultivated a thematic repertoire of love, wine, and metaphysical longing. Ghalib’s innovation was philosophical: he turned the ghazal into a dialectical space where the speaker interrogates divine justice, using irony (tanz) and ambiguity (iham) to evade censorship. The reformist phase (1850‑1930) saw poets such as Altaf Hussain Hali and Muhammad Husain Azad advocating nazm (free verse) and a didactic ghazal that addressed social reform, yet they retained the traditional meter to preserve legitimacy. The modernist phase (post‑1930) — led by the Progressive Writers’ Association and later by the Halqa‑e‑Arbab‑e‑Zauq — broke the radif constraint, experimented with azad nazm (free verse), and introduced colloquial diction (bolchal ki zaban) to depict urban alienation. Faiz Ahmed Faiz’s Subh‑e‑Azadi (1947) exemplifies the hybrid: it keeps the ghazal’s couplet autonomy but replaces the radif with a recurring refrain of “hum dekhenge”, turning the form into a political chant. In the 1970s, poets like Nasir Kazmi and Ahmad Faraz pushed the ghazal toward surreal imagery and psychological interiority, while contemporary diaspora voices (e.g., Zeeshan Sahil) incorporate English code‑switching. For Paper I, the examiner expects a clear mapping of formal changes — meter, radif, diction — to ideological shifts: courtly patronage → colonial education → nationalist mobilization → post‑colonial identity negotiation.

Partition Narratives and the Transformation of the Urdu Short Story

The 1947 Partition generated a corpus of Urdu short stories that redefined the genre’s scope, technique, and moral compass. Pre‑Partition masters such as Premchand (writing in Urdu as Premchand) and Sajjad Zaheer had already moved the short story from anecdotal afsana to social realism. Partition intensified this trajectory in three ways. First, the event supplied a collective trauma that demanded fragmented narratives — non‑linear chronology, multiple focalizers, and elliptical endings — to mirror the disorientation of displacement. Saadat Hasan Manto’s Toba Tek Singh (1955) uses the asylum setting as a metaphor for the absurdity of borders, while his Khol Do (1952) employs a clinical, almost reportorial tone to convey horror without sentimentality. Second, women writers — Ismat Chughtai, Qurratulain Hyder, and later Khadija Mastur — introduced domestic interiors as sites of violence, expanding the short story’s gaze from public politics to private betrayal. Chughtai’s Lihaaf (1942) pre‑dated Partition but its frank treatment of female sexuality set a precedent for post‑1947 stories that explored gendered violence during migration. Third, the Progressive Writers’ ideological framework forced many stories into explicit allegory; however, the best works (e.g., Krishan Chander’s Jamun Ka Ped) resist didacticism by embedding political critique within sensory detail — the smell of burning wheat, the sound of a train whistle. In examinations, candidates should contrast the “event‑driven” story (Manto) with the “memory‑driven” novel (Hyder’s Aag Ka Darya) and note how the short story’s brevity made it the preferred medium for immediate testimony, while the novel accommodated historiographical reflection.

Post‑Independence Institutionalization: Akademis, Universities, and Canon Formation

After 1947, the Indian state created a network of institutions that shaped the production, dissemination, and evaluation of Urdu literature. The Sahitya Akademi (established 1954) instituted annual awards that conferred canonical status on works such as Aag Ka Darya (1962) and Khuda Ki Basti (1974). Simultaneously, the Urdu Akademi (Delhi, 1969) and its state‑level counterparts funded translations, dictionaries, and the Urdu‑Hindi Shabdkosh project, standardizing lexical equivalents for administrative terminology. University departments — Aligarh Muslim University, Jawaharlal Nehru University, and the University of Delhi — designed curricula that privileged the “three pillars” (Ghalib, Iqbal, Faiz) while marginalizing Deccani, folk, and diaspora voices until the 1990s. The 1970s saw the rise of Adabi Duniya and Naya Daur as critical journals that mediated between academic criticism and popular readership. In the 1990s, the University Grants Commission’s “Area Studies” grants enabled comparative projects linking Urdu literature with Punjabi, Sindhi, and Bengali Partition narratives, broadening the canon. For Paper I, aspirants must be able to explain how institutional patronage operates as a filter: funding decisions determine which manuscripts are edited, which authors receive fellowships, and which texts appear on syllabi. A nuanced answer will cite the 1992 Sahitya Akademi award to Intizar Husain (for Basti) as a moment when the canon expanded to include the muhajir experience, and will note the ongoing tension between state‑driven standardization (e.g., the 2001 Urdu Language Bill) and the pluralistic demands of digital publishing platforms.

  • Confusing the Deccan school’s timeline with the later Delhi school; the Deccan flourished before 1700, not concurrently with Mir.
  • Attributing the 1937 spelling reform solely to the Anjuman without acknowledging Fort William College’s earlier diacritic system.
  • Treating the ghazal’s radif as immutable; modernist poets deliberately abandoned it, and examiners test this distinction.
  • Assuming all Partition short stories are Progressive propaganda; Manto’s work resists ideological labeling.
  • Overlooking the role of the Sahitya Akademi awards in canon formation; they are a primary mechanism for textual legitimization.
  • Neglecting the impact of university curricula on the “three pillars” narrative; syllabi shape what is considered major.
  • Failing to connect digital publishing’s rise to the weakening of state‑controlled lexical standardization.
