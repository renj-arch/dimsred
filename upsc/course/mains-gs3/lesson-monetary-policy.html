Money and its Functions

1.1 Definition and Evolution of Money

Money is any asset that is widely accepted as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value and a standard of deferred payment; these four functions together distinguish money from other financial assets. In early economies commodity money such as cattle, grain or metallic coins performed the role because they possessed intrinsic value, but the inconvenience of transport and divisibility led to the adoption of representative money backed by precious metals. The transition to fiat money, which derives its value solely from the trust placed in the issuing authority, accelerated after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system in the early 1970s. In India the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, conferred the sole right of note issue on the central bank, and the Currency Ordinance of 1940 replaced the silver rupee with a managed paper currency. Since then the monetary system has evolved through decimalisation in 1957, the introduction of the Mahatma Gandhi series of banknotes in 1996, and the recent launch of the ₹2000 and ₹500 notes after the 2016 demonetisation, each step reflecting the changing needs of a growing economy.

1.2 Monetary Aggregates in India

The Reserve Bank publishes several monetary aggregates to capture the liquidity spectrum of the economy; the narrowest measure, reserve money (M0), comprises currency in circulation plus bankers’ deposits with the RBI and other deposits with the central bank. Broad money (M3) adds the time deposits of the banking system to the components of M1, which itself includes currency with the public, demand deposits with the banking system and other deposits with the RBI. The intermediate aggregates M1 and M2 are constructed by progressively adding savings deposits and short‑term time deposits, respectively, allowing analysts to trace how changes in policy rates percolate through different layers of liquidity. The choice of aggregate matters for policy because a rise in M0 may signal an expansion of the monetary base without an immediate increase in credit, whereas a surge in M3 typically reflects a broader expansion of purchasing power. The RBI monitors these aggregates weekly and uses them as intermediate targets when the operating framework shifts from a monetary targeting regime to the current flexible inflation targeting framework.

1.3 Role of Money in the Economy

Money lubricates the circular flow of income by reducing the transaction costs inherent in barter, enabling specialization and the division of labour that underpin modern productivity gains. As a unit of account it provides a common denominator for pricing goods, services and financial contracts, which makes economic calculation possible and allows firms to plan investment over multiple periods. The store‑of‑value function permits households to smooth consumption across time, while the standard of deferred payment underpins credit markets by allowing obligations to be settled in a universally accepted medium. When the supply of money grows faster than the economy’s capacity to produce goods and services, the excess liquidity bids up prices, leading to inflation; conversely, a contraction in money supply can depress aggregate demand and push the economy into recession. The central bank therefore seeks to calibrate money growth so that it is consistent with the inflation target while supporting sustainable output growth, a balancing act that lies at the heart of monetary policy.

Reserve Bank of India and Monetary Policy Framework

2.1 Institutional Structure of the RBI

The Reserve Bank of India was established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and was nationalised in 1949, making it a fully owned entity of the Government of India. Its central board, headed by the Governor, comprises up to four Deputy Governors and ten non‑official directors nominated by the government, ensuring a blend of operational expertise and public accountability. The RBI’s functional departments include the Department of Monetary Policy, the Department of Banking Regulation, the Department of Currency Management and the Department of Payment and Settlement Systems, each headed by a Deputy Governor. The bank also operates through a network of regional offices and a system of currency chests that manage the physical distribution of banknotes and coins across the country. This institutional architecture enables the RBI to perform its multiple mandates — monetary authority, regulator and supervisor of banks, manager of foreign exchange, and issuer of currency — while maintaining operational independence in day‑to‑day policy decisions.

2.2 Policy Rates and Instruments

The RBI’s monetary policy toolkit consists of a set of administered rates and quantitative instruments that together influence the cost and availability of credit. The repo rate, at which the central bank lends short‑term funds to commercial banks against government securities, is the primary signalling rate; a reduction lowers the marginal cost of funds for banks and typically leads to a decline in lending rates. The reverse repo rate, the rate at which the RBI borrows from banks, provides a floor for short‑term money market rates. The marginal standing facility (MSF) allows banks to borrow overnight at a penal rate above the repo, acting as a safety valve against liquidity shocks. The bank rate, historically the rate for long‑term accommodation, now aligns with the MSF rate. In addition to price‑based tools, the RBI employs the cash reserve ratio (CRR) and the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) as quantitative levers; raising the CRR drains primary liquidity from the banking system, while an increase in the SLR compels banks to hold a larger share of their net demand and time liabilities in approved government securities. Open market operations (OMOs), including outright purchases and sales of government securities and the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF) auctions, fine‑tune day‑to‑day liquidity conditions.

2.3 Monetary Policy Committee and Decision Making

The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) was constituted in 2016 under the amended RBI Act to bring transparency and collective decision‑making to the rate‑setting process. It comprises six members — three from the RBI, including the Governor who chairs the committee, and three external members appointed by the government for a four‑year term. The MPC meets at least four times a year, reviews macroeconomic data, and votes on the policy repo rate; decisions are taken by majority, with the Governor casting a deciding vote in case of a tie. The committee’s mandate is to maintain consumer price index (CPI) inflation within the target band of 4 ± 2 percent, while keeping in mind the objective of growth. Minutes of each meeting are published with a lag of two weeks, and the RBI releases a monetary policy report that explains the rationale behind the stance, the assessment of inflation dynamics, and the outlook for growth. This framework has shifted the RBI from a discretionary, opaque regime to a rule‑based, accountable one, enhancing credibility and anchoring inflation expectations.

Illustrative Question
Assume the RBI reduces the repo rate by 50 basis points while keeping the CRR unchanged. Explain the likely sequence of effects on the banking system’s lending rates, credit growth, and aggregate demand, highlighting any transmission lags.
A repo rate cut lowers the cost at which banks borrow short‑term funds from the RBI. Banks typically pass on a portion of this reduction to their marginal cost of funds based lending rate (MCLR) or external benchmark linked lending rates, but the pass‑through is incomplete and occurs with a lag of one to two quarters because banks first adjust deposit rates to protect margins. As lending rates decline, the cost of capital for firms and households falls, encouraging fresh borrowing for investment and consumption. Credit growth accelerates once the lower rates filter through to loan sanctions, which adds to aggregate demand. However, the transmission may be dampened if banks face high non‑performing assets, risk aversion, or if the demand for credit is weak due to uncertain business sentiment. The overall impact on output and inflation materialises over four to six quarters, reflecting the cumulative effect of the interest rate channel, the credit channel, and the expectations channel.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the transmission mechanism, structure the answer around the four canonical channels — interest rate, credit, exchange rate, and asset price — and explicitly mention the role of the MCLR or external benchmark in the Indian context; this demonstrates both conceptual clarity and country‑specific awareness.

Monetary Transmission Mechanism

3.1 Interest Rate Channel

The interest rate channel operates through the direct influence of the policy repo rate on short‑term money market rates, which then feed into the marginal cost of funds based lending rate (MCLR) or the external benchmark lending rate (EBLR) that banks use to price loans. A reduction in the repo rate lowers the interbank call money rate, the treasury bill yields and the commercial paper rates, thereby reducing the cost of wholesale funding for banks. Because the MCLR is computed from the marginal cost of deposits, borrowings and the return on net worth, a fall in wholesale rates pulls down the MCLR with a lag that depends on the reset frequency of the benchmark — typically monthly for EBLR and quarterly for MCLR. The lower lending rates stimulate investment by reducing the user cost of capital and encourage household borrowing for durable goods and housing, thereby expanding aggregate demand.

3.2 Credit and Balance Sheet Channels

The credit channel amplifies the interest rate effect by focusing on the supply side of credit; when policy rates fall, banks’ net worth improves because the market value of their asset portfolio rises, easing capital adequacy constraints and allowing them to expand lending. Simultaneously, the balance sheet channel works through borrowers’ net worth: lower interest rates increase the present value of collateral, improving firms’ and households’ creditworthiness and reducing the external finance premium. In the Indian context, the effectiveness of these channels is moderated by the level of non‑performing assets, which erode bank capital and make lenders cautious, and by the prevalence of directed lending norms that limit the discretionary allocation of credit. The RBI’s prompt corrective action framework and the recapitalisation of public sector banks aim to restore the potency of the credit channel.

3.3 Exchange Rate and Asset Price Channels

Changes in the policy rate influence the exchange rate through interest rate differentials; a rate cut tends to widen the negative carry on the rupee, prompting capital outflows and a depreciation that makes exports more competitive while raising the rupee cost of imports, thereby affecting the trade balance and domestic inflation. The asset price channel operates via the impact of lower discount rates on equity and real estate valuations; higher asset prices boost household wealth and collateral values, supporting consumption and investment. However, excessive asset price inflation can sow the seeds of financial instability, a risk the RBI monitors through macro‑prudential tools such as loan‑to‑value caps and sectoral exposure limits. The interplay of these channels determines the overall potency and speed of monetary transmission in an open economy like India.

Non-Performing Assets and Banking Sector Health

4.1 Classification and Recognition of NPAs

An asset becomes non‑performing when the borrower fails to pay interest or principal for a period of ninety days; the RBI’s prudential norms classify such assets into three categories — substandard, doubtful and loss — based on the duration of non‑performance and the realisable value of security. A substandard asset is one that has remained NPA for less than or equal to twelve months and has well‑defined credit weaknesses; a doubtful asset has remained in the substandard category for more than twelve months, and its recovery is highly uncertain; a loss asset is one where the bank has identified the loss but the amount has not been written off wholly. The recognition norms were tightened in 2015 with the introduction of the asset quality review, which forced banks to disclose hidden stress and led to a sharp rise in reported NPAs. Provisioning requirements increase progressively across categories, with loss assets requiring one hundred percent provisioning, thereby directly impacting bank profitability and capital adequacy.

4.2 Resolution Frameworks and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), enacted in 2016, created a time‑bound, creditor‑driven resolution process that replaced the earlier fragmented regime of the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act (SARFAESI), Debt Recovery Tribunals and Corporate Debt Restructuring. Under the IBC, a financial creditor can initiate a corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP) that must be completed within 180 days, extendable by 90 days, after which liquidation follows if no resolution plan is approved. The establishment of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) provides dedicated adjudicatory forums. Complementary measures such as the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, the creation of the National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL) and the India Debt Resolution Company Limited (IDRCL) aim to clean up legacy stressed assets and restore lending capacity. These reforms have improved recovery rates and reduced the stock of gross NPAs from a peak of over eleven percent of gross advances in 2018 to below six percent in recent reporting periods.

4.3 Impact on Credit Growth and Financial Stability

High levels of NPAs constrain credit growth by eroding bank capital, increasing risk aversion and raising the cost of funds as lenders demand higher risk premiums. When banks set aside large provisions, their net interest margins shrink, limiting the ability to lend to productive sectors, especially small and medium enterprises that rely heavily on bank finance. The resultant credit slowdown can dampen investment, reduce employment generation and weaken the monetary transmission mechanism because the interest rate channel loses potency when banks are unwilling or unable to pass on policy rate cuts. From a financial stability perspective, concentrated exposures to a few large borrowers or sectors create systemic risk; the RBI’s large exposure framework and sectoral deployment limits are designed to mitigate such concentration. The gradual improvement in asset quality, supported by the IBC and recapitalisation, is essential for reviving the credit cycle and ensuring that monetary policy impulses translate into real economic activity.

Financial Inclusion and Digital Payments

5.1 Evolution of Inclusion Initiatives

Financial inclusion in India has progressed through distinct phases, beginning with the nationalisation of banks in 1969 which mandated branch expansion into rural areas, followed by the Lead Bank Scheme in 1970 that assigned each district a lead bank responsible for credit planning. The launch of the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) in 2014 marked a paradigm shift by targeting universal access to a basic savings bank deposit account, a RuPay debit card and an overdraft facility, all linked to the Aadhaar biometric identity. By 2023 the scheme had opened over forty‑seven crore accounts, dramatically reducing the unbanked adult population. Complementary programmes such as the Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana (PMMY) for micro‑enterprise credit, the Stand‑Up India scheme for SC/ST and women entrepreneurs, and the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) architecture that routes subsidies through bank accounts have deepened the usage dimension of inclusion beyond mere account opening.

5.2 Role of Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and Mobile (JAM) Trinity

The JAM trinity — Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar authentication and mobile connectivity — forms the digital backbone that enables low‑cost, real‑time, paperless financial transactions. Aadhaar‑enabled payment systems (AePS) allow customers to perform cash withdrawals, balance enquiries and fund transfers using biometric authentication at business correspondents, overcoming the barrier of physical branch proximity. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), built on the Immediate Payment Service (IMPS) rails, leverages mobile numbers as virtual payment addresses, facilitating peer‑to‑peer and merchant payments at negligible cost. The Bharat Bill Payment System (BBPS) and the National Electronic Toll Collection (NETC) extend the digital payments ecosystem to recurring bill payments and highway tolls. Together, these platforms have driven a surge in digital transaction volumes, with UPI alone processing several billion transactions per month, fostering a culture of cashless payments even in semi‑urban and rural geographies.

5.3 Emerging Challenges and the Way Forward

Despite impressive quantitative gains, qualitative gaps persist: a significant share of Jan Dhan accounts remain dormant, digital literacy varies widely across states, and the business correspondent network faces viability challenges due to low transaction fees. Cybersecurity threats, data privacy concerns under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and the need for robust grievance redressal mechanisms require continuous regulatory attention. The RBI’s recent framework for offline digital payments and the pilot of the Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — the digital rupee — aim to address connectivity constraints and provide a sovereign digital alternative to private payment instruments. Strengthening the last‑mile infrastructure, incentivising active usage through targeted financial literacy campaigns, and ensuring interoperability across payment systems will determine whether the inclusion agenda translates into meaningful economic empowerment for the hitherto excluded segments.

The lesson concludes by integrating the monetary policy toolkit, transmission dynamics, banking sector resilience and inclusion architecture into a coherent framework that equips the student to analyse contemporary Indian macroeconomic issues with depth and precision.

Liquidity Management Framework of the Reserve Bank of India

The Reserve Bank manages day‑to‑day liquidity through a tiered corridor system that anchors the weighted average call money rate (WACR) close to the policy repo rate. The primary instruments are the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF), introduced in June 2000, which comprises a fixed‑rate repo window (the repo rate) and a fixed‑rate reverse repo window (the reverse repo rate). In 2011 the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) was added, allowing banks to borrow overnight up to 2 per cent of their net demand and time liabilities (NDTL) at a rate 25 basis points above the repo rate, providing a safety valve against unanticipated liquidity shocks.

Since 2020 the RBI has shifted toward variable‑rate operations to fine‑tune surplus liquidity. The Variable Rate Reverse Repo (VRRR) auctions, conducted at multiple tenors (2‑day, 7‑day, 14‑day), absorb excess funds at market‑determined rates, while Variable Rate Repo (VRR) auctions inject liquidity when the system turns deficit. The Standing Deposit Facility (SDF), launched in April 2022, replaced the fixed reverse repo as the floor of the corridor, offering banks an uncollateralised deposit window at a rate 25 bps below the repo rate. Together, the SDF (floor), repo (ceiling) and MSF (penalty ceiling) form a symmetric corridor of ±25 bps around the policy rate.

Open Market Operations (OMOs) — outright purchase or sale of government securities — remain the principal tool for durable liquidity management. The RBI also uses the Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS), introduced in 2004, to sterilise capital‑flow‑induced liquidity surges by issuing special treasury bills. In 2023 the central bank conducted a series of 14‑day VRRR auctions totalling ₹8.5 lakh crore to drain pandemic‑era surplus, demonstrating the flexibility of the new framework. The interplay of these instruments determines the operating target — the WACR — and influences the transmission of policy rates to money‑market, bond‑market and bank‑lending rates.

Evolution of Benchmark Lending Rates: From Base Rate to External Benchmark

Indian banks have migrated through three distinct benchmark regimes since 2010. The Base Rate system (July 2010) required each bank to declare a single minimum lending rate based on its average cost of funds, negative carry on CRR/SLR and a profit margin. While it improved transparency, the Base Rate was slow to reflect policy‑rate changes because banks could adjust the cost‑of‑funds component infrequently.

The Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate (MCLR), effective April 2016, linked the benchmark to the marginal cost of deposits, borrowings and return on net worth, with a mandatory monthly reset. MCLR accelerated transmission but retained bank‑specific spreads and allowed a choice of reset periods (overnight, one‑month, three‑month, six‑month, one‑year), creating heterogeneity across borrowers.

In October 2019 the RBI mandated that all new floating‑rate personal, retail and micro‑enterprise loans be linked to an external benchmark — either the policy repo rate, the 91‑day or 182‑day treasury bill yield, or any other market interest rate published by Financial Benchmarks India Ltd (FBIL). The External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR) framework requires a uniform spread over the chosen benchmark and a reset at least once every three months. As of March 2024, over 85 per cent of outstanding floating‑rate retail credit is priced off the repo rate, reducing the median transmission lag from 6‑9 months under MCLR to 2‑3 months.

The shift has also compressed net interest margins for banks that previously benefited from sticky Base Rate or MCLR spreads, prompting a strategic focus on fee‑based income and risk‑based pricing. The RBI’s 2023 review noted that while EBLR improved pass‑through, the effective transmission to small‑ticket borrowers remains uneven due to credit‑risk premia and operational rigidities in the business‑correspondent channel.

Examiner's Trap: Benchmark Identification
A question asks: "Which of the following statements about the External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR) is correct? (a) EBLR applies to all existing loans irrespective of origination date. (b) Banks may choose any external benchmark published by FBIL, but the spread must be uniform across all borrower categories. (c) The reset frequency under EBLR is at the bank's discretion. (d) EBLR was introduced in 2016 along with MCLR."
Correct answer: (b). EBLR applies only to new floating‑rate loans sanctioned after 1 October 2019; existing loans continue on Base Rate or MCLR unless the borrower opts for switching. The spread over the chosen benchmark must be constant for a given loan product, though it may differ across products. Reset frequency is mandated at least quarterly, not at the bank's discretion. EBLR was introduced in 2019, three years after MCLR.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question compares Base Rate, MCLR and EBLR, construct a three‑column mental table: (1) reference rate (average cost vs marginal cost vs external market rate), (2) reset frequency (no fixed rule vs monthly vs quarterly), (3) applicability (all loans vs new loans vs new floating‑rate retail loans). This eliminates confusion between "mandatory for all loans" and "mandatory for new loans only".

Macroprudential Regulation and the Financial Stability Architecture

Beyond micro‑prudential supervision of individual banks, the RBI has built a macroprudential toolkit to address systemic risk. The Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB), introduced in 2015 under Basel III guidelines, can be activated up to 2.5 per cent of risk‑weighted assets when credit‑to‑GDP gaps signal excessive credit growth; as of 2024 the buffer remains at zero, reflecting the RBI’s assessment that the credit cycle is not in an expansionary phase.

The Sectoral Capital Requirements — higher risk weights for commercial real estate (CRE) exposures (100 per cent vs 75 per cent for standard corporate) and for capital market exposures (125 per cent) — act as targeted macroprudential levers. The Large Exposure Framework (LEF), revised in 2019, caps a bank’s exposure to a single counterparty at 20 per cent of Tier I capital (25 per cent with board approval) and to a group of connected counterparties at 25 per cent, curbing concentration risk.

The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC), chaired by the Finance Minister and comprising the RBI Governor, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA and IBBI chiefs, serves as the apex coordination body. Its Sub‑Committee, headed by the RBI Governor, meets quarterly to assess systemic risks, review inter‑regulatory issues and recommend policy actions. The RBI’s half‑yearly Financial Stability Report (FSR) publishes stress‑test results for scheduled commercial banks, NBFCs and housing finance companies, covering scenarios such as a 300 bps rate shock, a 20 per cent GDP contraction and a 30 per cent fall in real‑estate prices.

In 2022 the RBI issued a framework for Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) for NBFCs, mirroring the bank PCA regime, with thresholds on capital adequacy, asset quality and leverage. The framework triggered restrictions on dividend distribution and branch expansion for three large NBFCs in 2023, illustrating the extension of macroprudential oversight beyond banks.

Central Bank Digital Currency: Design Choices and Pilot Outcomes

The RBI’s Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — the digital rupee (e₹) — follows a two‑tier, token‑based, account‑less model for the retail segment (CBDC‑R) and a wholesale segment (CBDC‑W) for interbank settlement. The retail pilot, launched in December 2022 in four cities (Mumbai, New Delhi, Bengaluru, Bhubaneswar) with four participating banks (SBI, ICICI, Yes Bank, IDFC First), uses a distributed ledger maintained by the RBI but does not employ a public blockchain. Transactions are settled instantly, finality is immediate, and the digital rupee is a direct liability of the central bank, bearing no interest.

Key design choices: (i) anonymity for low‑value transactions (up to ₹2 lakh) with KYC‑linked wallets for higher amounts, aligning with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023; (ii) offline capability via near‑field communication (NFC) for feature‑phone users, tested in a separate pilot in 2023; (iii) interoperability with UPI through QR‑code standards, allowing merchants to accept e₹ without new infrastructure. The wholesale pilot, commenced in November 2022, focuses on secondary‑market government‑securities settlement, reducing settlement risk from T+1 to T+0 and cutting collateral requirements.

By March 2024 the retail pilot had processed over 1.2 million transactions totalling ₹450 crore, with average ticket size below ₹500. The RBI’s 2023‑24 Annual Report highlighted that CBDC‑R did not displace UPI volumes but provided a sovereign alternative for programmable payments (e.g., targeted subsidies with expiry dates). Outstanding issues include the impact on bank deposits (disintermediation risk), the cost of maintaining the ledger at scale, and cross‑border interoperability — the RBI is participating in the BIS Innovation Hub’s Project mBridge to test multi‑CBDC corridors.

Account Aggregator Framework and Open Banking in India

The Account Aggregator (AA) framework, operational since September 2021 under the RBI’s Master Direction, creates a consent‑based data‑sharing layer that lets individuals and MSMEs share financial data (bank statements, tax returns, insurance policies, pension records) with regulated entities — lenders, wealth managers, insurers — without handing over login credentials. Eight licensed AAs (including CAMS, Finvu, OneMoney, Perfios) act as intermediaries; they cannot view, store or resell the data, only transmit encrypted packets between Financial Information Providers (FIPs) and Financial Information Users (FIUs).

The framework leverages the Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture (DEPA) standards, using the AA‑API specification for request‑response flows, digital signatures and audit trails. As of December 2023, over 1.1 billion consent artefacts had been created, and more than 300 FIUs (banks, NBFCs, fintechs) were live on the network. The RBI’s 2023 circular extended the AA scope to include GST returns, utility bill payments and telecom data, broadening the credit‑assessment dataset for thin‑file borrowers.

Open Banking in India is evolving through the Unified Lending Interface (ULI), a proposed common API layer for loan origination, underwriting and disbursement, currently in sandbox testing with select banks and fintechs. The ULI aims to standardise credit‑decision data formats, reduce turnaround time for MSME loans from weeks to hours, and enable embedded finance use‑cases (e.g., instant credit at point‑of‑sale). The RBI’s 2024 discussion paper on “Digital Lending — Regulatory Framework” proposes mandatory AA‑based data fetch for all digital lending apps, a cooling‑off period for loan‑cancellation, and a grievance‑redressal timeline of 30 days, signalling a move toward a fully interoperable, consent‑driven financial data ecosystem.

Prompt Corrective Action Framework and Its Effect on Bank Governance

The Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, first introduced by the Reserve Bank of India in 2002 and substantially revised in April 2017, acts as a supervisory early‑warning system that forces banks to correct deteriorating financial health before insolvency becomes inevitable. The framework monitors four key indicators: Capital to Risk‑Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR), Net Non‑Performing Assets (NNPA) ratio, Return on Assets (RoA) and Leverage Ratio. A breach of any threshold places the bank under one of three risk‑based categories — Risk Threshold 1 (mild), Risk Threshold 2 (moderate) and Risk Threshold 3 (severe) — each triggering a progressively stricter set of mandatory and discretionary corrective actions.

Trigger Points and Graduated Restrictions

  • CRAR: Below 9 % (Risk Threshold 1), below 7.75 % (Risk Threshold 2), below 6.25 % (Risk Threshold 3).
  • NNPA ratio: Above 6 % (RT1), above 9 % (RT2), above 12 % (RT3).
  • RoA: Negative for two consecutive years (RT1), negative for three consecutive years (RT2), negative for four consecutive years (RT3).
  • Leverage Ratio: Below 4 % (RT1), below 3.5 % (RT2), below 3 % (RT3).

Once a bank enters PCA, the RBI imposes mandatory curbs such as restriction on dividend distribution, branch expansion, and fresh lending to high‑risk sectors. Discretionary measures may include capping management compensation, requiring a board‑level turnaround plan, and limiting the bank’s ability to access liquidity windows. The framework also empowers the RBI to supersede the board in extreme cases, a power exercised only rarely.

Impact on Governance and Market Discipline

Empirical evidence from the 2017‑2020 cycle shows that PCA‑listed public sector banks (PSBs) undertook accelerated capital‑raising — through government recapitalisation, qualified institutional placements and AT‑1 bond issuance — and cleaned up balance sheets via aggressive NPA write‑offs and sale to asset reconstruction companies. The framework also sharpened board accountability: independent directors were mandated to review the turnaround plan quarterly, and the RBI’s supervisory colleges conducted deeper dive inspections. However, critics argue that PCA can create a stigma effect, raising the cost of wholesale funding for affected banks and potentially triggering deposit outflows, especially for smaller PSBs with limited franchise value.

Exit Criteria and Recent Developments

A bank exits PCA only after it meets all four indicators for four consecutive quarters and obtains RBI’s approval on a sustainable capital plan. As of March 2024, all but two PSBs that were under PCA in 2018 have exited, reflecting the combined effect of recapitalisation, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) resolutions and improved credit underwriting. The RBI’s 2023 review proposed a “PCA‑lite” for small finance banks and payment banks, recognising their distinct business models while preserving the core principle of early intervention.

Basel III Implementation in India: Capital, Leverage and Liquidity Standards

India adopted the Basel III reforms in a phased manner beginning 1 April 2013, with the final tranche of capital buffers fully effective from 1 October 2021. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) tailored the global standards to domestic conditions, preserving the three‑pillar structure — minimum capital requirements, supervisory review and market discipline — while introducing India‑specific calibrations such as a higher Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) minimum for Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D‑SIBs) and a staggered Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) rollout.

Leverage Ratio

A non‑risk‑based leverage ratio of 3.5 % was mandated from 1 April 2018, tightened to 4 % for D‑SIBs from 1 October 2019. The ratio is computed as Tier 1 capital divided by the exposure measure (on‑balance‑sheet assets plus off‑balance‑sheet items converted using credit conversion factors). It acts as a backstop against model risk and excessive balance‑sheet growth.

Liquidity Standards

  • LCR: Phased from 60 % (January 2015) to 100 % (January 2019). Banks must hold High‑Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) sufficient to survive a 30‑day stressed cash‑outflow scenario.
  • NSFR: Introduced at 100 % from 1 October 2021, requiring stable funding to cover required stable funding over a one‑year horizon.

Supervisory Review and Disclosure

Pillar 2 requires banks to conduct an Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) and submit it annually. The RBI’s Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP) may impose higher capital ratios on individual banks. Pillar 3 disclosures — quarterly for capital, monthly for LCR — enhance market discipline. The RBI’s 2023 circular aligned disclosure formats with the revised Basel Pillar 3 standards, mandating granular breakdown of HQLA composition and NSFR components.

Worked Example: Computing a Bank’s CET1 Requirement
A scheduled commercial bank (not a D‑SIB) reports CET1 capital of ₹12,000 crore and risk‑weighted assets of ₹150,000 crore. The bank’s current CCB is fully phased in. Determine whether the bank meets the minimum CET1 + CCB requirement and calculate the shortfall, if any.
Step 1: Compute CET1 ratio = CET1 capital ÷ RWA = 12,000 ÷ 150,000 = 8.0 %.
Step 2: Minimum CET1 + CCB = 5.5 % + 2.5 % = 8.0 %.
Step 3: The bank’s CET1 ratio equals the regulatory minimum exactly; there is no shortfall. However, any decline in CET1 capital or increase in RWA would breach the requirement, triggering supervisory action under Pillar 2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “current” Basel III minima, quote the fully phased‑in figures (CET1 5.5 %, CCB 2.5 %, Total 10.5 %). Remember that the CCyB is zero unless explicitly stated as activated. For leverage ratio, distinguish between the 3.5 % floor for all banks and the 4 % floor for D‑SIBs. A quick mental table of the three pillars and their Indian calibration earns full marks.

RBI's Government Debt Management and Market Borrowing Operations

As the banker to the central and state governments, the Reserve Bank of India manages the entire lifecycle of public debt — from primary issuance to secondary‑market liquidity support. The RBI’s Internal Debt Management Department (IDMD) conducts weekly auctions of dated securities (2‑ to 40‑year tenors) and treasury bills (91‑, 182‑ and 364‑day), while the Cash Management Bills (CMBs) address temporary mismatches. The annual borrowing calendar, published in the Union Budget, is split into “dated securities” and “treasury bills” tranches, with the RBI retaining the flexibility to adjust issuance size through “green‑shoe” options.

Ways and Means Advances (WMA)

The RBI provides two categories of WMA to the Centre: Normal WMA (unsecured, linked to the repo rate) and Special WMA (secured against government securities, at a rate 25 bps below the repo rate). The aggregate limit is revised quarterly; for FY 2023‑24 the normal WMA ceiling stood at ₹1.5 lakh crore. States access WMA through the RBI’s state‑level offices, with limits determined by the Finance Commission recommendations and the state’s fiscal deficit trajectory.

Open Market Operations and Market Stabilisation Scheme

  • OMO Purchase: Injects durable liquidity by buying government securities outright; used when systemic liquidity is in deficit.
  • OMO Sale: Absorbs surplus liquidity; often combined with the Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) which issues special securities to sterilise capital inflows.
  • MSS: Introduced in 2004, the MSS corpus is held in a separate account; interest on MSS securities is borne by the government, insulating the RBI’s balance sheet.

Secondary‑Market Support and Liquidity Facilities

The RBI operates the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) — repo and reverse repo — and the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) as the corridor floor. In addition, the RBI conducts Variable Rate Repo (VRR) and Variable Rate Reverse Repo (VRRR) auctions for fine‑tuning. The “Open Market Operations — Purchase of State Development Loans (SDLs)” window, launched in 2021, enables the RBI to buy SDLs directly, improving state‑level yield curves and reducing borrowing costs.

Debt Sustainability and Investor Base Diversification

The RBI monitors the debt‑to‑GDP ratio, interest‑to‑revenue ratio and the share of foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) in government securities. The “Fully Accessible Route” (FAR) introduced in 2020 allows non‑residents to invest in specified dated securities without investment limits, deepening the investor base. The RBI’s 2023‑24 Annual Report highlighted that the weighted average maturity of central government debt extended to 12.3 years, reducing rollover risk.

Scale‑Based Regulation of Non‑Banking Financial Companies and Housing Finance Companies

In October 2022 the RBI unveiled a scale‑based regulatory framework for NBFCs, replacing the earlier asset‑size classification with a four‑layer pyramid: Base Layer (NBFC‑BL), Middle Layer (NBFC‑ML), Upper Layer (NBFC‑UL) and Top Layer (NBFC‑TL). The classification hinges on a composite score comprising asset size, leverage, interconnectedness, complexity and supervisory inputs. Housing Finance Companies (HFCs), brought under RBI regulation in August 2019, are subjected to the same layering, with additional prudential norms for housing‑loan concentration.

The RBI’s 2023 guidelines on digital lending require that any First Loss Default Guarantee provided by a fintech partner to an NBFC or bank be capped at 5 % of the loan portfolio and be fully funded by the guarantor. These norms aim to curb predatory lending while preserving the innovation benefits of embedded finance.

  • Minimum 60 % of total assets in housing loans (down from 75 % pre‑2020).
  • Loan‑to‑Value (LTV) caps: 90 % for loans ≤₹30 lakh, 80 % for ₹30‑75 lakh, 75 % for >₹75 lakh.
  • Capital charge on commercial real estate exposure aligned with Basel III risk weights.

Forward Guidance and Monetary Policy Communication Strategy of the RBI

Since the adoption of the Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework in 2016, the RBI has progressively refined its communication toolkit to shape market expectations and enhance policy credibility. The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) publishes a bi‑monthly resolution statement, detailed minutes (released after a two‑week lag) and a quarterly Monetary Policy Report (MPR) that includes fan‑chart forecasts for inflation and growth. The “forward guidance” language — explicit statements on the expected path of the policy repo rate — was first used in the August 2020 MPC meeting (“accommodative stance as long as necessary to revive growth”) and has since become a regular feature.

Elements of the Communication Framework

  • Policy Stance: Categorised as accommodative, neutral, or hawkish; the stance signals the bias for future rate moves.
  • Forward Guidance Horizon: Typically 6‑12 months; the RBI avoids committing to a precise rate path, preserving flexibility.
  • MPR Fan Charts: Probabilistic projections for CPI inflation and real GDP growth, with confidence bands that widen over the forecast horizon.
  • Speeches and Interviews: Governor and Deputy Governors use public forums to reinforce the MPC’s collective view, reducing noise from individual dissent.

Impact on Financial Markets

Empirical studies (RBI Working Paper 2022‑03) show that forward‑guidance surprises — measured as the change in the 1‑year OIS rate on MPC announcement days — have a statistically significant effect on bond yields and the rupee‑dollar forward premium. The transparency of minutes has reduced the dispersion of professional forecasters’ inflation expectations by roughly 15 bps since 2018. However, the RBI’s reluctance to publish individual voting records (only the majority view is disclosed) limits the granularity of market pricing of dissent risk.

Recent Enhancements

The 2023‑24 MPR introduced a “scenario analysis” box that quantifies the impact of alternative oil‑price and global‑growth paths on domestic inflation. The RBI also launched a dedicated “Monetary Policy Communication” webpage hosting all statements, minutes, MPRs and FAQs in a searchable format, aligning with the BIS’s Central Bank Communication Good Practices.

  • Confusing the PCA trigger thresholds with Basel III capital minima — PCA uses CRAR, NNPA, RoA and leverage ratio, while Basel III focuses on CET1, CCB, CCyB and leverage ratio.
  • Assuming the Countercyclical Capital Buffer is permanently active — it has been at zero since inception and is only activated when the credit‑to‑GDP gap exceeds the threshold.
  • Treating Ways and Means Advances as a fiscal deficit financing tool — WMA are temporary, interest‑bearing advances with strict limits, not a substitute for market borrowing.
  • Believing all NBFCs are subject to LCR and NSFR — only Middle, Upper and Top Layer NBFCs face liquidity requirements; Base Layer NBFCs are exempt.
  • Equating forward guidance with a fixed rate‑path commitment — the RBI’s guidance is qualitative and conditional, not a binding promise.
  • Overlooking the distinction between the Market Stabilisation Scheme and regular OMO sales — MSS securities are issued specifically for sterilisation and carry a separate accounting treatment.
  • Assuming the digital rupee (CBDC‑R) pays interest like a savings deposit — it is a non‑interest‑bearing central‑bank liability.

Unconventional Monetary Policy Tools and Their Application in India

While conventional monetary policy primarily relies on adjusting the policy repo rate to influence short-term interest rates, central banks often deploy unconventional tools during periods of severe economic stress or when conventional tools become ineffective (e.g., at the Zero Lower Bound). These tools aim to directly influence long-term interest rates, credit flows, or market liquidity when the policy rate alone cannot achieve the desired stimulus or stability.

In India, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has selectively used unconventional measures, particularly in response to global financial crises and the COVID-19 pandemic, adapting them to the specific structural characteristics of the Indian economy. Key among these are:

  • Targeted Long-Term Refinancing Operations (TLTROs): Introduced in 2020 during the COVID-19 crisis, TLTROs involve the RBI providing long-term (up to three years) funds to banks at a fixed rate, usually linked to the prevailing repo rate. The crucial "targeted" aspect mandates banks to deploy these funds into specific sectors or instruments, such as corporate bonds, commercial papers, and non-convertible debentures issued by entities in designated sectors. The objective is to ensure liquidity reaches specific segments of the economy that might be facing funding constraints, thereby supporting credit flow and economic activity. For instance, TLTRO 1.0 and 2.0 aimed at supporting corporate bond markets and NBFCs/MFIs respectively.
  • Operation Twist: This involves simultaneous buying of long-term government securities (G-secs) and selling of short-term G-secs. The objective is to flatten the yield curve by bringing down long-term interest rates without expanding the overall monetary base significantly. Lower long-term rates reduce borrowing costs for businesses and individuals (e.g., for housing and capital expenditure), thereby stimulating investment and consumption. The RBI first conducted Operation Twist in December 2019, aiming to manage sovereign bond yields and provide an impetus to growth when inflation was within target.
  • Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) Enhancements: While LAF is a conventional tool, its scope and operational parameters have been enhanced during crises. This includes increasing the quantum of funds available under various windows, extending the tenor of repo operations, or introducing specific windows like the Special Long-Term Repo Operations (SLTROs) for specific sectors (e.g., small finance banks).
  • Forex Swaps: The RBI has used long-term foreign exchange buy/sell swaps to manage rupee liquidity and foreign currency liquidity in the banking system. For example, in 2019, the RBI conducted dollar-rupee buy/sell swaps to inject durable rupee liquidity into the system while absorbing foreign currency.

Unlike many developed economies, India has not adopted policies like Quantitative Easing (large-scale asset purchases across various asset classes) or Negative Interest Rate Policy (NIRP). The reasons include the relatively underdeveloped nature of India's bond market compared to developed economies, which limits the effectiveness of large-scale asset purchases, and concerns about the impact of negative rates on bank profitability and financial stability in a developing economy context.

India's Flexible Inflation Targeting Framework: Evolution, Challenges, and Review

India formally adopted a Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework in 2016, following an agreement between the Government of India and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). This marked a significant shift from a multiple-indicator approach to a more explicit and transparent monetary policy regime focused on price stability.

Evolution: The journey towards FIT began with the recommendations of the Expert Committee to Revise and Strengthen the Monetary Policy Framework, chaired by Dr. Urjit R. Patel (2014). The committee advocated for adopting CPI (Combined) inflation as the nominal anchor, with a target of 4% with a band of ±2% (i.e., 2% to 6%). The rationale was to provide a clear objective for monetary policy, enhance transparency, anchor inflation expectations, and improve policy credibility. The RBI Act, 1934, was amended in 2016 to provide a statutory basis for the FIT framework, establishing the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) as the decision-making body for setting the policy repo rate.

Key Features:

  • Inflation Target: The central government, in consultation with the RBI, sets the inflation target. The current target is 4% for CPI (Combined) inflation, with an upper tolerance limit of 6% and a lower tolerance limit of 2%.
  • Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): Comprising six members (three from the RBI, including the Governor as ex-officio Chairperson, and three external members appointed by the government), the MPC is entrusted with the task of determining the policy repo rate to achieve the inflation target. Decisions are taken by majority vote, with the Governor having a second or casting vote in case of a tie.
  • Accountability Mechanism: If the RBI fails to meet the inflation target for three consecutive quarters (i.e., average inflation is outside the 2-6% band for three consecutive quarters), it is required to submit a report to the government explaining the reasons for failure, proposed remedial actions, and an estimated timeframe for achieving the target.
  • Flexibility: The "flexible" aspect acknowledges that while price stability is the primary objective, monetary policy also considers the objective of growth, especially when inflation is within the tolerance band. This allows the MPC to respond to output gaps and growth concerns without compromising the long-term inflation objective.

Challenges:

  • Supply-Side Shocks: A significant challenge in India is the frequent occurrence of supply-side shocks (e.g., food price volatility due to monsoons, global oil price surges) which are largely beyond the control of monetary policy. While monetary policy can address demand-side inflation, its effectiveness against persistent supply shocks is limited, often leading to a trade-off with growth.
  • Monetary Transmission Lags: The time lag between policy rate changes and their full impact on inflation and output can be substantial and variable, making policy calibration complex.
  • Fiscal Dominance: High government borrowing can put upward pressure on interest rates and potentially lead to fiscal dominance, where monetary policy decisions are influenced by the need to finance government debt rather than solely by inflation objectives.
  • Data Quality and Forecasting: Accurate and timely data, along with robust forecasting models, are crucial for effective inflation targeting.

Review and Recent Developments: The government is mandated to review the inflation target every five years. The first review, conducted in 2021, affirmed the existing target of 4% with a ±2% tolerance band for the period April 1, 2021, to March 31, 2026. This continuity underscored the framework's effectiveness in anchoring inflation expectations and maintaining macroeconomic stability despite significant shocks like the pandemic. The review acknowledged the challenges posed by supply-side factors but reiterated the importance of a credible nominal anchor for sustainable growth.

Examiner's trap
Consider the following statement: "Quantitative Easing (QE) has been a standard tool of the Reserve Bank of India to manage long-term interest rates and inject liquidity into the economy, especially during periods of economic slowdown." Evaluate the accuracy of this statement.
The statement is inaccurate. While the RBI has indeed employed unconventional tools to manage long-term interest rates and inject liquidity, particularly during economic slowdowns (e.g., Operation Twist, TLTROs), it has not undertaken Quantitative Easing (QE) in the same comprehensive manner as central banks in developed economies. QE typically involves large-scale asset purchases across a broad range of government and sometimes private sector securities to significantly expand the monetary base. The RBI's interventions, while aimed at similar objectives, have been more targeted (like TLTROs for specific credit segments) or focused on specific segments of the government bond market (like Operation Twist). The term "standard tool" for QE in India is incorrect, as the RBI has generally preferred more calibrated and targeted liquidity operations.
Exam-day strategy
When encountering questions on monetary policy tools, distinguish carefully between conventional and unconventional measures. For unconventional tools, remember to identify the specific objective (e.g., yield curve control, targeted credit flow, liquidity injection) and the precise mechanism used by the RBI (e.g., TLTROs for sectoral credit, Operation Twist for yield curve flattening). Avoid generic statements; focus on the nuanced application in the Indian context.

Monetary-Fiscal Policy Coordination and Challenges in India

Monetary policy, managed by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), and fiscal policy, managed by the Government of India, are the two primary macroeconomic tools used to stabilize the economy. Their effective coordination is crucial for achieving objectives like price stability, sustainable growth, and external sector balance. However, their interaction often presents significant challenges, particularly in a developing economy like India.

Interaction Mechanisms:

  • Inflation Management: An expansionary fiscal policy (e.g., increased government spending, tax cuts) can boost aggregate demand, potentially leading to inflationary pressures. If the RBI is targeting inflation, it might respond with a contractionary monetary policy (e.g., raising interest rates), which can dampen the fiscal stimulus. Conversely, a tight fiscal policy can ease inflationary pressures, allowing the RBI more room for accommodative monetary policy to support growth.
  • Interest Rates and Debt Management: High government borrowing (fiscal deficit) increases the demand for funds in the market, potentially pushing up interest rates. This phenomenon is known as "crowding out," where private investment is reduced due to higher borrowing costs. The RBI, as the government's debt manager, has to balance its monetary policy objectives with the need to facilitate government borrowing at reasonable rates. Excessive reliance on the RBI to finance deficits (debt monetization) can lead to inflation.
  • External Sector: Fiscal policy can influence the current account balance (e.g., through import demand), while monetary policy affects capital flows and the exchange rate. A large fiscal deficit can lead to a current account deficit, requiring external financing and potentially putting pressure on the rupee.

Challenges to Coordination in India:

  • Fiscal Dominance: Historically, India has faced challenges of fiscal dominance, where the government's borrowing needs have often influenced the RBI's monetary policy decisions. While the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, aimed to impose fiscal discipline and reduce reliance on RBI financing, deviations have occurred, especially during crises.
  • Conflicting Objectives: The government's primary focus might be on growth and employment generation, often through fiscal expansion, while the RBI's primary mandate under FIT is price stability. These objectives can sometimes conflict, leading to policy friction. For instance, during a slowdown, the government might push for lower interest rates, but the RBI might be constrained by high inflation.
  • Information Asymmetry and Lags: Both policies operate with lags, and the full impact of one policy on the other might not be immediately apparent, making real-time coordination difficult.
  • Political Economy Factors: Fiscal policy decisions are often influenced by political cycles and electoral considerations, which may not always align with long-term macroeconomic stability goals.

Mechanisms for Coordination:

  • FRBM Act: Provides a statutory framework for fiscal discipline, aiming to reduce fiscal deficits and government debt, thereby creating space for independent monetary policy.
  • Consultations: Regular consultations between the Ministry of Finance and the RBI, though informal, are crucial for understanding each other's perspectives and policy stances.
  • Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): The establishment of the MPC has enhanced the independence of monetary policy, allowing it to focus on inflation targeting without direct government interference in rate-setting decisions. However, the fiscal environment remains a key input for MPC deliberations.

Effective coordination requires both entities to respect each other's mandates and work towards common macroeconomic goals, ensuring that policy actions are mutually reinforcing rather than contradictory.

Shadow Banking and Systemic Risk in India

Shadow banking refers to credit intermediation involving entities and activities outside the regular banking system, which are not subject to the same strict regulatory oversight as traditional banks. These entities typically perform bank-like functions, such as maturity transformation (borrowing short-term and lending long-term) and liquidity transformation, but without access to central bank liquidity support or deposit insurance. In India, the shadow banking sector is predominantly represented by Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs), but also includes housing finance companies (HFCs), mutual funds (especially debt funds), and peer-to-peer (P2P) lending platforms.

Growth Drivers in India:

  • Regulatory Arbitrage: NBFCs often face less stringent capital, liquidity, and lending norms compared to banks, allowing them to operate with greater flexibility and potentially higher returns.
  • Credit Gaps: Traditional banks may be reluctant to lend to certain segments (e.g., small businesses, unbanked populations, specific real estate projects) due to higher perceived risks or regulatory constraints. NBFCs step in to fill these gaps, catering to niche markets.
  • Ease of Operations: NBFCs often have faster decision-making processes and more flexible product offerings, appealing to borrowers seeking quick access to credit.
  • Digitalization: The rise of fintech has facilitated the growth of digital lending platforms, many of which are NBFCs or partner with them.

Systemic Risk Implications: The rapid growth and interconnectedness of the shadow banking sector can pose significant systemic risks to financial stability:

  • Liquidity Mismatch: Many shadow banking entities rely on short-term wholesale funding (e.g., commercial papers, market borrowings) to finance long-term assets. A sudden loss of market confidence can lead to funding dry-ups, triggering liquidity crises. The IL&FS crisis in 2018-19 highlighted this vulnerability, as its default on debt obligations led to a contagion fear across the NBFC sector and impacted mutual funds holding their papers.
  • Interconnectedness: Shadow banks are deeply interconnected with traditional banks (through lending, borrowing, and investment in each other's instruments) and other financial markets. A distress in the shadow banking sector can quickly spill over into the broader financial system.
  • Procyclicality: Shadow banking activities tend to be procyclical, expanding rapidly during booms and contracting sharply during downturns, thereby amplifying economic cycles.
  • Lack of Safety Nets: Unlike banks, shadow banking entities typically do not have access to central bank liquidity facilities as a lender of last resort or deposit insurance, making them more vulnerable to runs and panics.

Regulatory Responses in India: The RBI and other regulators have significantly enhanced oversight of the shadow banking sector, particularly after the IL&FS crisis:

  • Scale-Based Regulation (SBR): Introduced in 2021, SBR categorizes NBFCs into layers (Base, Middle, Upper, Top) based on their size, activity, and systemic importance, with progressively stricter regulations for higher layers (as discussed in the existing lesson). This includes enhanced capital requirements, governance norms, and liquidity management frameworks.
  • Prudential Norms: Tightening of asset classification, provisioning norms, and exposure limits for NBFCs.
  • Supervisory Oversight: Increased scrutiny through inspections, stress testing, and early warning systems.
  • Digital Lending Guidelines: Specific norms to address concerns related to predatory lending practices by digital lenders, many of whom are NBFCs or their partners.

Managing shadow banking risks involves striking a balance between fostering financial innovation and ensuring systemic stability, requiring continuous adaptation of regulatory frameworks.

Capital Account Convertibility and Exchange Rate Management in India

Capital account convertibility (CAC) refers to the freedom to convert local financial assets into foreign financial assets and vice versa at market-determined exchange rates. It implies the absence of restrictions on international capital flows. India has adopted a cautious and calibrated approach towards full CAC, largely guided by the recommendations of various committees, most notably the Tarapore Committee reports.

Understanding CAC:

  • Current Account Convertibility: India achieved full current account convertibility in 1994, meaning there are no restrictions on foreign exchange transactions related to trade in goods and services, income, and unilateral transfers.
  • Capital Account Convertibility: This involves transactions related to financial assets, such as foreign direct investment (FDI), foreign portfolio investment (FPI), external commercial borrowings (ECBs), and overseas investments by Indian residents. Full CAC would mean no restrictions on these inflows or outflows.

Arguments For and Against CAC:

  • Arguments For:
    • Increased Capital Inflows: Can attract more foreign investment, boosting economic growth and productivity.
    • Lower Cost of Capital: Access to global capital markets can reduce borrowing costs for domestic firms.
    • Enhanced Efficiency: Promotes efficient allocation of capital and integration into the global financial system.
    • Improved External Competitiveness: Can discipline domestic macroeconomic policies.
  • Arguments Against (Risks):
    • Financial Instability: Can lead to volatile capital flows, increasing vulnerability to currency crises and speculative attacks.
    • Loss of Monetary Policy Autonomy: With free capital flows and a fixed exchange rate, a central bank loses control over its monetary policy (impossible trinity).
    • Increased External Debt: Can lead to unsustainable accumulation of foreign debt.
    • Contagion Risk: Makes the economy more susceptible to global financial shocks.

India's Calibrated Approach and Tarapore Committee Reports:

  • Tarapore Committee I (1997): Recommended a phased approach to full CAC, conditional on certain macroeconomic prerequisites: a lower fiscal deficit (below 3.5% of GDP), a lower inflation rate (3-5%), a stronger financial system (lower NPAs, higher capital adequacy), and a robust exchange rate management framework. India made progress on some fronts but did not fully meet all conditions.
  • Tarapore Committee II (2006): Reaffirmed the phased approach, suggesting a roadmap for 2006-11, again conditional on fiscal consolidation, financial sector reforms, and adequate foreign exchange reserves. It emphasized the need for a strong regulatory and supervisory framework for the financial sector.

India currently operates under a regime of partial capital account convertibility, characterized by a managed float exchange rate regime. While significant liberalization has occurred for FDI, FPI, and ECBs, certain restrictions remain, particularly on resident individuals' overseas investments and some forms of short-term debt. The RBI's approach has been to liberalize capital flows gradually, ensuring that the domestic financial system and macroeconomic fundamentals are robust enough to absorb potential shocks.

RBI's Exchange Rate Management: The RBI follows a "managed float" exchange rate system. This means the exchange rate is primarily determined by market forces (demand and supply of foreign currency), but the RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to curb excessive volatility and prevent sharp appreciation or depreciation of the rupee. Interventions typically involve buying or selling foreign currency (usually US dollars) to influence the rupee's value. The objectives of intervention are to maintain orderly market conditions, prevent speculative attacks, and ensure that the exchange rate reflects underlying economic fundamentals without being subject to excessive short-term fluctuations that could harm trade or capital flows. The level of foreign exchange reserves is a key indicator of the RBI's capacity to intervene effectively.

Common Traps

  • Confusing QE with TLTRO/Operation Twist: Assuming RBI's targeted liquidity operations or yield curve management is the same as full-fledged Quantitative Easing, which it is not.
  • Misinterpreting "Flexible" in FIT: Believing "flexible" means the RBI can ignore inflation for growth, rather than balancing growth within the tolerance band of the inflation target.
  • Assuming full CAC in India: Stating that India has achieved full capital account convertibility, ignoring the calibrated and partial nature of its capital account liberalization.
  • Overlooking Fiscal Dominance: Ignoring the historical and ongoing challenges of fiscal policy influencing monetary policy, despite the statutory independence of the Monetary Policy Committee.
  • Underestimating Shadow Banking Risk: Treating NBFCs merely as alternative lenders without recognizing their systemic interconnectedness and potential for broader financial instability.
  • Misunderstanding RBI's Exchange Rate Stance: Believing the RBI targets a specific exchange rate level rather than managing volatility under a managed float regime.
  • Ignoring Prerequisites for CAC: Forgetting the crucial macroeconomic conditions (e.g., fiscal deficit reduction, financial sector health) recommended by committees like Tarapore for achieving full capital account convertibility.
