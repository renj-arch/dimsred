Money and its Functions

1.1 Definition and Evolution of Money

Money is any asset that is widely accepted as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value and a standard of deferred payment; these four functions together distinguish money from other financial assets. In early economies commodity money such as cattle, grain or metallic coins performed the role because they possessed intrinsic value, but the inconvenience of transport and divisibility led to the adoption of representative money backed by precious metals. The transition to fiat money, which derives its value solely from the trust placed in the issuing authority, accelerated after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system in the early 1970s. In India the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, conferred the sole right of note issue on the central bank, and the Currency Ordinance of 1940 replaced the silver rupee with a managed paper currency. Since then the monetary system has evolved through decimalisation in 1957, the introduction of the Mahatma Gandhi series of banknotes in 1996, and the recent launch of the ₹2000 and ₹500 notes after the 2016 demonetisation, each step reflecting the changing needs of a growing economy.

1.2 Monetary Aggregates in India

The Reserve Bank publishes several monetary aggregates to capture the liquidity spectrum of the economy; the narrowest measure, reserve money (M0), comprises currency in circulation plus bankers’ deposits with the RBI and other deposits with the central bank. Broad money (M3) adds the time deposits of the banking system to the components of M1, which itself includes currency with the public, demand deposits with the banking system and other deposits with the RBI. The intermediate aggregates M1 and M2 are constructed by progressively adding savings deposits and short‑term time deposits, respectively, allowing analysts to trace how changes in policy rates percolate through different layers of liquidity. The choice of aggregate matters for policy because a rise in M0 may signal an expansion of the monetary base without an immediate increase in credit, whereas a surge in M3 typically reflects a broader expansion of purchasing power. The RBI monitors these aggregates weekly and uses them as intermediate targets when the operating framework shifts from a monetary targeting regime to the current flexible inflation targeting framework.

1.3 Role of Money in the Economy

Money lubricates the circular flow of income by reducing the transaction costs inherent in barter, enabling specialization and the division of labour that underpin modern productivity gains. As a unit of account it provides a common denominator for pricing goods, services and financial contracts, which makes economic calculation possible and allows firms to plan investment over multiple periods. The store‑of‑value function permits households to smooth consumption across time, while the standard of deferred payment underpins credit markets by allowing obligations to be settled in a universally accepted medium. When the supply of money grows faster than the economy’s capacity to produce goods and services, the excess liquidity bids up prices, leading to inflation; conversely, a contraction in money supply can depress aggregate demand and push the economy into recession. The central bank therefore seeks to calibrate money growth so that it is consistent with the inflation target while supporting sustainable output growth, a balancing act that lies at the heart of monetary policy.

Reserve Bank of India and Monetary Policy Framework

2.1 Institutional Structure of the RBI

The Reserve Bank of India was established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and was nationalised in 1949, making it a fully owned entity of the Government of India. Its central board, headed by the Governor, comprises up to four Deputy Governors and ten non‑official directors nominated by the government, ensuring a blend of operational expertise and public accountability. The RBI’s functional departments include the Department of Monetary Policy, the Department of Banking Regulation, the Department of Currency Management and the Department of Payment and Settlement Systems, each headed by a Deputy Governor. The bank also operates through a network of regional offices and a system of currency chests that manage the physical distribution of banknotes and coins across the country. This institutional architecture enables the RBI to perform its multiple mandates — monetary authority, regulator and supervisor of banks, manager of foreign exchange, and issuer of currency — while maintaining operational independence in day‑to‑day policy decisions.

2.2 Policy Rates and Instruments

The RBI’s monetary policy toolkit consists of a set of administered rates and quantitative instruments that together influence the cost and availability of credit. The repo rate, at which the central bank lends short‑term funds to commercial banks against government securities, is the primary signalling rate; a reduction lowers the marginal cost of funds for banks and typically leads to a decline in lending rates. The reverse repo rate, the rate at which the RBI borrows from banks, provides a floor for short‑term money market rates. The marginal standing facility (MSF) allows banks to borrow overnight at a penal rate above the repo, acting as a safety valve against liquidity shocks. The bank rate, historically the rate for long‑term accommodation, now aligns with the MSF rate. In addition to price‑based tools, the RBI employs the cash reserve ratio (CRR) and the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) as quantitative levers; raising the CRR drains primary liquidity from the banking system, while an increase in the SLR compels banks to hold a larger share of their net demand and time liabilities in approved government securities. Open market operations (OMOs), including outright purchases and sales of government securities and the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF) auctions, fine‑tune day‑to‑day liquidity conditions.

2.3 Monetary Policy Committee and Decision Making

The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) was constituted in 2016 under the amended RBI Act to bring transparency and collective decision‑making to the rate‑setting process. It comprises six members — three from the RBI, including the Governor who chairs the committee, and three external members appointed by the government for a four‑year term. The MPC meets at least four times a year, reviews macroeconomic data, and votes on the policy repo rate; decisions are taken by majority, with the Governor casting a deciding vote in case of a tie. The committee’s mandate is to maintain consumer price index (CPI) inflation within the target band of 4 ± 2 percent, while keeping in mind the objective of growth. Minutes of each meeting are published with a lag of two weeks, and the RBI releases a monetary policy report that explains the rationale behind the stance, the assessment of inflation dynamics, and the outlook for growth. This framework has shifted the RBI from a discretionary, opaque regime to a rule‑based, accountable one, enhancing credibility and anchoring inflation expectations.

Illustrative Question
Assume the RBI reduces the repo rate by 50 basis points while keeping the CRR unchanged. Explain the likely sequence of effects on the banking system’s lending rates, credit growth, and aggregate demand, highlighting any transmission lags.
A repo rate cut lowers the cost at which banks borrow short‑term funds from the RBI. Banks typically pass on a portion of this reduction to their marginal cost of funds based lending rate (MCLR) or external benchmark linked lending rates, but the pass‑through is incomplete and occurs with a lag of one to two quarters because banks first adjust deposit rates to protect margins. As lending rates decline, the cost of capital for firms and households falls, encouraging fresh borrowing for investment and consumption. Credit growth accelerates once the lower rates filter through to loan sanctions, which adds to aggregate demand. However, the transmission may be dampened if banks face high non‑performing assets, risk aversion, or if the demand for credit is weak due to uncertain business sentiment. The overall impact on output and inflation materialises over four to six quarters, reflecting the cumulative effect of the interest rate channel, the credit channel, and the expectations channel.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the transmission mechanism, structure the answer around the four canonical channels — interest rate, credit, exchange rate, and asset price — and explicitly mention the role of the MCLR or external benchmark in the Indian context; this demonstrates both conceptual clarity and country‑specific awareness.

Monetary Transmission Mechanism

3.1 Interest Rate Channel

The interest rate channel operates through the direct influence of the policy repo rate on short‑term money market rates, which then feed into the marginal cost of funds based lending rate (MCLR) or the external benchmark lending rate (EBLR) that banks use to price loans. A reduction in the repo rate lowers the interbank call money rate, the treasury bill yields and the commercial paper rates, thereby reducing the cost of wholesale funding for banks. Because the MCLR is computed from the marginal cost of deposits, borrowings and the return on net worth, a fall in wholesale rates pulls down the MCLR with a lag that depends on the reset frequency of the benchmark — typically monthly for EBLR and quarterly for MCLR. The lower lending rates stimulate investment by reducing the user cost of capital and encourage household borrowing for durable goods and housing, thereby expanding aggregate demand.

3.2 Credit and Balance Sheet Channels

The credit channel amplifies the interest rate effect by focusing on the supply side of credit; when policy rates fall, banks’ net worth improves because the market value of their asset portfolio rises, easing capital adequacy constraints and allowing them to expand lending. Simultaneously, the balance sheet channel works through borrowers’ net worth: lower interest rates increase the present value of collateral, improving firms’ and households’ creditworthiness and reducing the external finance premium. In the Indian context, the effectiveness of these channels is moderated by the level of non‑performing assets, which erode bank capital and make lenders cautious, and by the prevalence of directed lending norms that limit the discretionary allocation of credit. The RBI’s prompt corrective action framework and the recapitalisation of public sector banks aim to restore the potency of the credit channel.

3.3 Exchange Rate and Asset Price Channels

Changes in the policy rate influence the exchange rate through interest rate differentials; a rate cut tends to widen the negative carry on the rupee, prompting capital outflows and a depreciation that makes exports more competitive while raising the rupee cost of imports, thereby affecting the trade balance and domestic inflation. The asset price channel operates via the impact of lower discount rates on equity and real estate valuations; higher asset prices boost household wealth and collateral values, supporting consumption and investment. However, excessive asset price inflation can sow the seeds of financial instability, a risk the RBI monitors through macro‑prudential tools such as loan‑to‑value caps and sectoral exposure limits. The interplay of these channels determines the overall potency and speed of monetary transmission in an open economy like India.

Non-Performing Assets and Banking Sector Health

4.1 Classification and Recognition of NPAs

An asset becomes non‑performing when the borrower fails to pay interest or principal for a period of ninety days; the RBI’s prudential norms classify such assets into three categories — substandard, doubtful and loss — based on the duration of non‑performance and the realisable value of security. A substandard asset is one that has remained NPA for less than or equal to twelve months and has well‑defined credit weaknesses; a doubtful asset has remained in the substandard category for more than twelve months, and its recovery is highly uncertain; a loss asset is one where the bank has identified the loss but the amount has not been written off wholly. The recognition norms were tightened in 2015 with the introduction of the asset quality review, which forced banks to disclose hidden stress and led to a sharp rise in reported NPAs. Provisioning requirements increase progressively across categories, with loss assets requiring one hundred percent provisioning, thereby directly impacting bank profitability and capital adequacy.

4.2 Resolution Frameworks and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), enacted in 2016, created a time‑bound, creditor‑driven resolution process that replaced the earlier fragmented regime of the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act (SARFAESI), Debt Recovery Tribunals and Corporate Debt Restructuring. Under the IBC, a financial creditor can initiate a corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP) that must be completed within 180 days, extendable by 90 days, after which liquidation follows if no resolution plan is approved. The establishment of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) provides dedicated adjudicatory forums. Complementary measures such as the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, the creation of the National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL) and the India Debt Resolution Company Limited (IDRCL) aim to clean up legacy stressed assets and restore lending capacity. These reforms have improved recovery rates and reduced the stock of gross NPAs from a peak of over eleven percent of gross advances in 2018 to below six percent in recent reporting periods.

4.3 Impact on Credit Growth and Financial Stability

High levels of NPAs constrain credit growth by eroding bank capital, increasing risk aversion and raising the cost of funds as lenders demand higher risk premiums. When banks set aside large provisions, their net interest margins shrink, limiting the ability to lend to productive sectors, especially small and medium enterprises that rely heavily on bank finance. The resultant credit slowdown can dampen investment, reduce employment generation and weaken the monetary transmission mechanism because the interest rate channel loses potency when banks are unwilling or unable to pass on policy rate cuts. From a financial stability perspective, concentrated exposures to a few large borrowers or sectors create systemic risk; the RBI’s large exposure framework and sectoral deployment limits are designed to mitigate such concentration. The gradual improvement in asset quality, supported by the IBC and recapitalisation, is essential for reviving the credit cycle and ensuring that monetary policy impulses translate into real economic activity.

Financial Inclusion and Digital Payments

5.1 Evolution of Inclusion Initiatives

Financial inclusion in India has progressed through distinct phases, beginning with the nationalisation of banks in 1969 which mandated branch expansion into rural areas, followed by the Lead Bank Scheme in 1970 that assigned each district a lead bank responsible for credit planning. The launch of the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) in 2014 marked a paradigm shift by targeting universal access to a basic savings bank deposit account, a RuPay debit card and an overdraft facility, all linked to the Aadhaar biometric identity. By 2023 the scheme had opened over forty‑seven crore accounts, dramatically reducing the unbanked adult population. Complementary programmes such as the Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana (PMMY) for micro‑enterprise credit, the Stand‑Up India scheme for SC/ST and women entrepreneurs, and the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) architecture that routes subsidies through bank accounts have deepened the usage dimension of inclusion beyond mere account opening.

5.2 Role of Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and Mobile (JAM) Trinity

The JAM trinity — Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar authentication and mobile connectivity — forms the digital backbone that enables low‑cost, real‑time, paperless financial transactions. Aadhaar‑enabled payment systems (AePS) allow customers to perform cash withdrawals, balance enquiries and fund transfers using biometric authentication at business correspondents, overcoming the barrier of physical branch proximity. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), built on the Immediate Payment Service (IMPS) rails, leverages mobile numbers as virtual payment addresses, facilitating peer‑to‑peer and merchant payments at negligible cost. The Bharat Bill Payment System (BBPS) and the National Electronic Toll Collection (NETC) extend the digital payments ecosystem to recurring bill payments and highway tolls. Together, these platforms have driven a surge in digital transaction volumes, with UPI alone processing several billion transactions per month, fostering a culture of cashless payments even in semi‑urban and rural geographies.

5.3 Emerging Challenges and the Way Forward

Despite impressive quantitative gains, qualitative gaps persist: a significant share of Jan Dhan accounts remain dormant, digital literacy varies widely across states, and the business correspondent network faces viability challenges due to low transaction fees. Cybersecurity threats, data privacy concerns under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and the need for robust grievance redressal mechanisms require continuous regulatory attention. The RBI’s recent framework for offline digital payments and the pilot of the Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — the digital rupee — aim to address connectivity constraints and provide a sovereign digital alternative to private payment instruments. Strengthening the last‑mile infrastructure, incentivising active usage through targeted financial literacy campaigns, and ensuring interoperability across payment systems will determine whether the inclusion agenda translates into meaningful economic empowerment for the hitherto excluded segments.

The lesson concludes by integrating the monetary policy toolkit, transmission dynamics, banking sector resilience and inclusion architecture into a coherent framework that equips the student to analyse contemporary Indian macroeconomic issues with depth and precision.
