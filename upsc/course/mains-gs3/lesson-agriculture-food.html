1. Indian Agriculture — Overview

Agriculture and allied sectors (crop husbandry, animal husbandry, fisheries, forestry) contributed 18.2% of GVA (FY24 1st AE, constant prices) while employing 45.8% of the workforce (PLFS 2022–23). This structural asymmetry — low productivity per worker — is the central challenge. The sector grew at 4.0% CAGR (FY13–FY24) vs 6.2% overall GVA, reflecting deceleration post-2013. (Source: MoSPI National Accounts 2023–24; PLFS 2022–23; Economic Survey 2023–24, Ch. 2)

Tenancy: Formal tenancy low (~10% operated area); oral/informal tenancy widespread (NSSO 70th Round: 35% of cultivator households lease in). PYQ Integration: Small Farmers (GS III 2022)
"Small and marginal farmers constitute the majority of India's agrarian population. Discuss the challenges they face and suggest measures to improve their income."
Model structure: (1) Data — 86% holdings <2 ha, operate 45% area (Agri Census 2015–16). (2) Challenges: (a) Scale diseconomies — input costs high, market access weak, mechanisation unviable; (b) Credit — collateral constraints, dependence on informal lenders (28% borrowing from non-institutional, AIDIS 2019); (c) Risk — no crop insurance reach (PMFBY coverage 30% farmers), price volatility; (d) Marketing — APMC monopsony, no storage, distress sale; (e) Extension — Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) understaffed. (3) Measures: (a) FPO/FPC promotion (10,000 FPOs target, ₹6,865 cr scheme) — collective input purchase, marketing, processing; (b) Custom hiring centres (CHCs) — mechanisation access; (c) PM-KISAN (₹6,000/yr) — income support; (d) KCC saturation — credit access; (e) Legalise tenancy (Model Act) — tenant security; (f) Crop diversification (millets, horticulture) — higher value/ha; (g) Post-harvest infrastructure (AIF ₹1 lakh cr) — reduce losses. (Source: PYQ 2022 GS III; Agri Census 2015–16; AIDIS 2019; FPO Scheme Guidelines)

1.4 Land Reforms: History, Status, and Unfinished Agenda

  • Phase I (1950s–70s): Abolition of intermediaries (zamindari, jagirdari) — completed in most states; tenancy reforms (security of tenure, fair rent, ownership rights for tenants) — partial; ceiling on landholdings — implemented with high ceilings, exemptions (plantations, trusts), benami transfers evaded. (Source: Planning Commission, Land Reforms Reports; M.L. Dantwala)
  • Phase II (1980s–2000s): Consolidation of holdings (Punjab, Haryana, UP — partial); Bhoodan/Gramdan (Vinoba Bhave) — voluntary, limited; updating land records — neglected.
  • Phase III (2008–present): Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP, 2008 — merged NLRMP + SRA) — conclusive titling, computerised records, cadastral maps, mutation automation. Progress: 28 states/UTs integrated with NLI (National Land Index); ~95% RoRs (Record of Rights) digitised; but conclusive titling (guaranteed title) not achieved — India has presumptive titling (registration of deeds, not title). (Source: DoLR, DILRMP Dashboard 2024; NCAER Land Records Index 2023)
  • Forest Rights Act (FRA) 2006: Recognises individual/community forest rights of STs and OTFDs. As of Mar 2024: 44 lakh claims filed, 23 lakh titles distributed (52%); implementation gaps — rejection without due process, lack of community rights recognition. (Source: MoTA, FRA Monthly Progress Report, Mar 2024)
Exam Trap: Presumptive vs Conclusive Titling
India follows presumptive titling — registration of sale deed creates a presumption of ownership, but title can be challenged in court. Conclusive titling (state-guaranteed title, Torrens system) is the DILRMP goal but requires legislative change (state subject). In answers, clarify: "DILRMP aims for conclusive titling but current legal framework remains presumptive; this creates litigation risk and credit constraints for farmers." (Source: NCAER Land Records and Services Index 2023)

Net irrigated area: 73 Mha (FY22); gross irrigated area: 112 Mha (cropping intensity 1.53). Irrigation coverage: 52% of net sown area (rainfed 48%). (Source: DAC&FW, Agricultural Statistics 2023; CWC Water Statistics)

2.2 Major Irrigation Programmes

  • Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY, 2015): "Har Khet Ko Pani" — components: (a) Accelerated Irrigation Benefit Programme (AIBP) — major/medium projects (99 priority projects identified, 48 completed as of Mar 2024); (b) Har Khet Ko Pani (HKKP) — command area development, surface minor irrigation, repair/renovation of water bodies; (c) Per Drop More Crop (PDMC) — micro-irrigation (drip/sprinkler); (d) Watershed Development — ridge-to-valley approach. (Source: PMKSY Guidelines 2015; DoWR Annual Report 2023–24)
  • Atal Bhujal Yojana (2020): World Bank-assisted (₹6,000 cr), community-led sustainable groundwater management in 7 states (Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, MP, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, UP) — 8,350 Gram Panchayats. (Source: Atal Bhujal Yojana Guidelines; World Bank Project Appraisal Document)
  • Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM, 2019): Har Ghar Jal — functional household tap connection (FHTC) by 2024. Rural coverage: 76% (Aug 2024) from 17% (2019). Reduces women's drudgery; water quality (arsenic, fluoride) remains challenge. (Source: JJM Dashboard; Ministry of Jal Shakti)
  • River Interlinking (ILR): National Perspective Plan (1980) — 30 links (14 Himalayan, 16 Peninsular). Ken-Betwa (Phase I) — first link under implementation (₹44,605 cr). Challenges: environmental clearance, inter-state disputes, rehabilitation, cost. (Source: NWDA, ILR Status 2024; Ken-Betwa DPR)

2.3 Water Use Efficiency and Demand Management

Irrigation Efficiency: Flood irrigation (dominant) — 30–35% efficiency; drip — 90%, sprinkler — 75%. "More crop per drop" requires: (a) micro-irrigation adoption (PDMC target: 10 Mha by 2025; achieved ~6 Mha); (b) pipe distribution (replacing open channels); (c) laser land levelling; (d) crop alignment — shift from paddy/sugarcane in water-scarce regions (Punjab paddy → maize/cotton; Maharashtra sugarcane → millets/pulses). (Source: PMKSY-PDMC Guidelines; ICAR Water Productivity Atlas)

Solved Mini-Example: Water Accounting
Question: "Punjab grows water-intensive paddy using free electricity for tube wells. Analyse the water-energy-food nexus."
Answer: (1) Punjab: 76% blocks over-exploited (CGWB 2022); paddy requires ~3,000–5,000 litres/kg; free power (₹7,000+ cr/yr subsidy) incentivises over-extraction. (2) Energy–water link: 14 lakh tubewells; 10,000+ GWh/yr agriculture consumption; groundwater decline 0.5–1 m/yr. (3) Food link: Punjab contributes 60%+ to central rice pool (PDS); procurement at MSP ensures farmer income but externalises water cost. (4) Solutions: (a) Direct Seeded Rice (DSR) — saves 15–20% water; (b) Crop diversification (Maize, Cotton, Oilseeds) — MSP + procurement assurance needed; (c) Metered power + DBT for electricity subsidy (Punjab pilot); (d) Solarisation (PM-KUSUM Component C) — grid-connected solar pumps. (Source: CGWB 2022; Punjab Economic Survey; PM-KUSUM Guidelines)

2.4 Water Governance: Legal and Institutional Framework

  • Water — State Subject (Entry 17, List II): States legislate on water supplies, irrigation, canals, drainage. Centre — Inter-state rivers (Entry 56, List I), River Boards Act 1956 (never used). (Source: Constitution, Seventh Schedule)
  • Inter-State Water Disputes Act 1956 (amended 2002, 2019): Tribunals (Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Mahanadi, Ravi-Beas, Vansadhara). 2019 amendment: single permanent tribunal, time-bound (3+1 yr), decision binding. Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA) 2018 — implementation body. (Source: ISWD Act 1956; 2019 Amendment; CWMA Notification)
  • Dam Safety Act 2021: National Committee on Dam Safety, State Dam Safety Organisations; surveillance, inspection, emergency action plans for 5,700+ large dams. (Source: Dam Safety Act 2021)
  • Model Groundwater Bill (2017/2020): Regulate extraction, protect recharge zones, community participation — not enacted by most states. (Source: MoJS, Model Groundwater (Sustainable Management) Bill 2020)

3. Agricultural Marketing and Reforms

3.1 APMC System: Structure, Issues, and State Acts

Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC) Acts — state legislations regulating first sale of notified agricultural produce. Issues: (a) Monopsony — licensed traders (arhatiyas) dominate; (b) Market fees (1–2%) + mandi fee + commission (2–6%) + rural development cess — cumulative 8–12%; (c) Restricted competition — entry barriers for private buyers; (d) Poor infrastructure — only 7,600 regulated markets vs 42,000 needed (National Commission on Farmers, 2006); (e) Fragmented markets — no inter-state trade. (Source: National Commission on Farmers Report, 2006; DAC&FW, State APMC Acts)

3.3 Minimum Support Price (MSP): Mechanism, Coverage, and Debates

  • CACP (Commission for Agricultural Costs & Prices): Recommends MSP for 23 mandated crops (7 cereals, 5 pulses, 7 oilseeds, 4 commercial). Formula: A2+FL (actual paid-out costs + imputed family labour) — used until 2017–18; since 2018–19, "C2+50%" announced (C2 = comprehensive cost including imputed rent on owned land, interest on owned capital). (Source: CACP Reports 2018–24; Budget 2018–19 Speech)
  • Procurement Reality: Effective only for wheat and rice (PDS-driven). FY24 procurement: Rice 56 Mt, Wheat 26 Mt. Pulses/oilseeds procurement minimal (Price Support Scheme — PSS, limited to 25% of production). Cotton — MSP operations by Cotton Corporation of India (CCI). Sugarcane — Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) by Centre; State Advised Price (SAP) by states (often higher, leads to arrears). (Source: FCI Annual Report 2023–24; CACP Kharif/Rabi Reports)
  • Legal Guarantee Debate: Farmers' demand: statutory MSP (C2+50%) for all 23 crops. Government position: fiscal burden (₹2–3 lakh cr/yr estimated), market distortion, WTO Amber Box limits (de minimis 10% of production value for developing countries), procurement infrastructure absent. Shanta Kumar Committee (2015): restrict FCI to non-procurement states; decentralised procurement (DCP). (Source: Shanta Kumar Committee Report, 2015; WTO Agreement on Agriculture; CACP Reports)
PYQ Integration: MSP and Farmers' Income (GS III 2020)
"The announcement of MSP at 1.5 times the cost of production has not translated into higher farmer incomes. Critically examine."
Structure: (1) MSP formula — Budget 2018 announced "C2+50%"; CACP calculates C2 but recommends A2+FL+50% (divergence ~15–20% lower). (2) Procurement limited to wheat/rice (PDS) in few states — 80% of procurement from Punjab, Haryana, MP, UP, Chhattisgarh. (3) Market prices for most crops (pulses, oilseeds, cotton, maize) rule below MSP — no procurement mechanism. (4) Input cost rise (fertiliser, diesel, labour) outpaces MSP hikes — real MSP stagnant. (5) Tenant farmers excluded (no land records). (6) Way forward: (a) Expand PSS/MIS with state partnership; (b) Market intelligence (AGMARKNET) + MSP as floor for private trade; (c) Decouple MSP from PDS — price deficiency payment (MP model — Bhavantar Bhugtan); (d) Legal guarantee for selected crops with fiscal cap. (Source: PYQ 2020 GS III; CACP Reports; Shanta Kumar Report; MP Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana)

4. Food Security and Public Distribution

4.1 Constitutional and Legal Framework

  • Article 21 (Right to Life) + Article 47 (Directive Principle): Supreme Court (PUCL v. Union of India, 2001) — right to food is fundamental. Interim orders: mid-day meals, ICDS universalisation, PDS reform. (Source: PUCL v. UoI, Writ Petition (Civil) 196/2001; Orders dated 2001–2013)
  • National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013: Legal entitlement for 75% rural / 50% urban population (priority households — 5 kg/person/month at ₹3/₹2/₹1; AAY — 35 kg/household/month). Coverage: ~81 cr beneficiaries (2011 Census base). Maternity benefit (≥₹6,000), children's meals (ICDS/MDM). (Source: NFSA 2013; Gazette Notification)
  • Coverage Updation: 2011 Census base frozen; actual coverage ~80 cr vs entitled ~90 cr (2024 pop.). 15th FC recommended updating Census base; not done. (Source: 15th FC Report, Vol. II, Ch. 4)

4.2 PDS: Architecture, Leakages, and Reforms

Architecture: FCI (procurement, central pool, buffer) → State agencies (transport, storage) → Fair Price Shops (FPS, 5.4 lakh) → Beneficiaries (ration cards: AAY, PHH, NPHH). (Source: FCI; NFSA Implementation Guidelines)

Leakages (NSSO 68th Round / IHDS / NIPFP studies): Estimated 25–40% diversion (ghost cards, under-weighing, open market sale). Reforms: (a) End-to-end computerisation (100% FPS automated); (b) Aadhaar seeding (99% ration cards); (c) e-PoS with biometric authentication (AePDS); (d) Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) pilots (Chandigarh, Puducherry, Dadra Nagar Haveli) — cash in lieu of foodgrains; (e) One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) — portability (37 states/UTs onboard, 100 cr+ transactions). (Source: DFPD Annual Reports; NIPFP PDS Studies; ONORC Dashboard)

Exam Trap: PDS Leakage vs Exclusion Errors
Leakage = grain diverted before reaching beneficiary (corruption). Exclusion error = eligible household not covered (outdated Census, migration, Aadhaar failure). Inclusion error = ineligible household covered. NFSA reduced inclusion errors but exclusion errors persist (migrants, homeless, Census lag). ONORC addresses migrant exclusion. In answers, distinguish: "While technology (e-PoS, Aadhaar) curbed leakage, exclusion errors remain due to frozen 2011 Census base and biometric failures." (Source: NFSA Evaluation Studies; IHDS 2019–21)

4.4 Buffer Stocking, Open Market Sale, and Food Corporation of India

  • Buffer Norms (FCI, 2023): Rice 10.25 Mt (Jul) / 7.6 Mt (Jan); Wheat 7.5 Mt (Jul) / 5.0 Mt (Jan). Actual stocks often 2–3× norms (cost: ₹2.5+ lakh cr economic cost). (Source: FCI Buffer Norms 2023; CAG Report on FCI 2022)
  • Economic Cost (FY24): Rice ₹3,700/qtl, Wheat ₹2,800/qtl (procurement + storage + interest + distribution). Issue price: ₹300/₹200/₹100. Subsidy = economic cost − issue price. (Source: FCI Annual Report 2023–24)
  • Open Market Sale Scheme (OMSS): FCI sells excess stocks to bulk consumers/flour mills at reserve price to moderate open market prices. FY24: 10+ Mt wheat sold. (Source: FCI OMSS Notifications)
  • Decentralised Procurement (DCP): States procure on behalf of FCI (Punjab, Haryana, MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Telangana, etc.) — reduces transport cost, incentivises states. (Source: FCI DCP Guidelines; Shanta Kumar Report)

5.2 Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY, 2016)

  • Structure: Yield-index insurance (area approach — Insurance Unit = village/GP for major crops). Premium: Kharif 2%, Rabi 1.5%, Commercial/Horticulture 5% (farmer share); balance shared 50:50 Centre:State (90:10 NE/hill). (Source: PMFBY Operational Guidelines 2016; Revised 2020, 2022)
  • Coverage (FY23): 4.5 cr farmer applications, 30% of GCA (gross cropped area), 55% loanee / 45% non-loanee. Sum insured ₹1.4 lakh cr. Claims paid ₹1.1 lakh cr (cumulative). (Source: PMFBY Dashboard; DAC&FW)
  • Critique: (a) Area approach — individual losses not captured; (b) Delayed claims — state premium share delay, yield data disputes; (c) Low non-loanee enrolment — awareness, documentation; (d) Crop cutting experiments (CCEs) — sample size small (4/block), delayed; (e) Insurer profitability — exit from loss-making clusters. (Source: CAG Report on PMFBY 2022; Parliamentary Standing Committee Report 2023)
  • Reforms (2020/2022 Guidelines): Voluntary for all (earlier mandatory for loanees); technology (smart sampling, drones, satellite — YES-TECH, WINDS); individual assessment for horticulture; timely yield data; premium subsidy cap (30% of sum insured for commercial crops). (Source: PMFBY Revised Guidelines 2020, 2022)
PYQ Integration: Crop Insurance (GS III 2019)
"Crop insurance schemes have failed to provide adequate risk cover to small farmers. Discuss."
Structure: (1) PMFBY design — area approach, subsidised premium, yield-index. (2) Gaps: (a) Area approach vs individual loss — small farmer with low yield but high village avg gets nothing; (b) CCEs — only 4 plots/block; not representative for fragmented holdings; (c) Delayed claims — states delay premium share (avg 6–12 months); (d) Low sum insured — based on scale of finance, not actual input cost; (e) Non-loanee exclusion — voluntary, low awareness, documentation burden; (f) Excluded risks — post-harvest (14 days), prevented sowing (limited), localized calamities (hailstorm) not fully covered. (3) Reforms needed: (a) Technology-driven individual assessment (drones, satellite, AI); (b) Faster CCEs (mobile app, photo evidence); (c) State premium subsidy — direct DBT to insurer; (d) Expand to price risk (price deficiency payment); (e) Risk pooling — national reinsurance pool. (Source: PYQ 2019 GS III; PMFBY Guidelines; CAG Report 2022; YES-TECH Framework)

5.3 Rural Indebtedness and the Informal Sector

AIDIS 2019 (All India Debt & Investment Survey): 50.2% agricultural households indebted; average debt ₹74,121; institutional share 66% (up from 61% in 2013), non-institutional 34% (moneylenders, traders, relatives). Interest rate: institutional 7–10%, non-institutional 24–36%+. (Source: NSSO 77th Round, AIDIS 2019)

Debt Waivers: State-level (UP 2017, Maharashtra 2017, Punjab 2018, MP 2019, Rajasthan 2019, Karnataka 2018) — fiscal cost ~₹2.5 lakh cr cumulative; moral hazard, credit discipline erosion, exclusion of non-institutional debt. Common Traps

  • Confusing MSP (minimum support price, govt announced) with market price (demand-supply determined).
  • Assuming all farmers benefit from PDS procurement — mostly wheat-rice growers in select states.
  • Missing that agriculture is a State subject (List II), Centre supports via Centrally Sponsored Schemes.
  • Overlooking post-harvest losses vs production losses — storage/transport gaps.
  • Thinking PMFBY covers all risks — it primarily covers yield loss, not price risk fully.
  • Confusing A2+FL (paid-out cost + family labour) with C2 (comprehensive cost including imputed rent and interest on own capital) — MSP formula divergence.
  • Assuming e-NAM replaced APMCs — it's a platform, not a market structure reform; APMC Acts still govern.
  • Mixing up Kharif/Rabi/Zaid seasons and crop-water requirements.
  • Thinking "doubling farmers' income" (2016 target, 2022 deadline) was achieved — it was not; Dalwai Committee (2017) recommended 10.4% nominal income growth/yr; actual ~3–4%.
  • Overlooking that fertiliser subsidy (urea) benefits large farmers disproportionately (higher usage/ha) and encourages imbalanced NPK use (N:P:K ~8:3:1 vs ideal 4:2:1).

Memory Tricks

  • Revolutions: Green—grain, White—milk, Blue—fish, Yellow—oilseeds, Golden—horticulture/fibres, Silver—wool/cotton.
  • Seasons: Kharif—rain (Jun–Oct), Rabi—winter (Oct–Mar), Zaid—summer (Mar–Jun).
  • Food security trinity: Availability (production), Access (purchasing power), Absorption (nutrition/health).
  • Credit: KCC = short-term production credit; NABARD = apex refinance; RIDF = rural infra fund.
  • MSP crops (23): 7 cereals (paddy, wheat, maize, jowar, bajra, ragi, barley), 5 pulses (gram, arhar, moong, urad, masur), 7 oilseeds (groundnut, soybean, sunflower, sesamum, niger, rapeseed/mustard, safflower), 4 commercial (cotton, jute, sugarcane, copra).
  • PMFBY Premium: Kharif 2%, Rabi 1.5%, Commercial 5% (farmer share).
  • NFSA Entitlement: PHH 5 kg/person/month (₹3/₹2/₹1); AAY 35 kg/household/month.
  • Land Holding: Marginal <1 ha (68%), Small 1–2 ha (18%) → 86% small & marginal.

For agriculture, link production → marketing → credit → insurance → food security. Cite specific schemes (PMFBY, e-NAM, PMKSY, NFSA, PM-KISAN, KCC, MIDH, PMMSY) and data (agriculture's GDP share 18%, workforce 46%, small/marginal 86%, irrigation coverage 52%, post-harvest losses ₹1.5 lakh cr). For water questions, discuss both supply (irrigation) and demand (water-guzzling crops like sugarcane/paddy in dry areas). For MSP, distinguish legal guarantee (statute) from policy instrument (Cabinet decision). Conclude with diversification (millets, horticulture, livestock), sustainable practices (natural farming, micro-irrigation), and farmer income focus (FPOs, value addition, market access).

7.3 Contract Farming: Legal Framework, Sponsor Obligations, and Dispute Resolution

State Contract Farming Acts (pre-2020): 20+ states enacted under Model APMC Act 2003 provisions. Issues: Sponsor registration cumbersome; dispute resolution via APMC (conflict of interest); no price discovery mechanism; farmer cannot exit easily. (Source: State Contract Farming Acts; NITI Aayog Review 2018)

Model Contract Farming Act (2018) — DAC&FW: Separate legislation (not under APMC); Contract Farming Authority (registers sponsors, approves contracts); Price determination — pre-agreed or reference to MSP/mandi price; Dispute resolution — Conciliation → Contract Farming Authority → Appellate Authority (30 days); Farmer protection — no land alienation, produce lien only, insurance mandate. Adoption: Tamil Nadu, Odisha, Punjab, Haryana, UP (partial); most states yet to enact. (Source: Model Contract Farming Act 2018; DAC&FW)

FAPAFS Act 2020 (Repealed): Central law — contract farming agreement (production support, price assurance); Sponsor obligations (input supply, technical advice, purchase at agreed price); Dispute: SDM → Collector → Appellate Authority; Electronic registry. Critique: Price formula not mandated (sponsor advantage); no MSP floor; registry not built. (Source: FAPAFS Act 2020; Gazette Notification)

PYQ Integration: Contract Farming and Smallholders (GS III 2021)
"Contract farming can integrate smallholders into value chains but requires robust legal safeguards. Discuss with reference to the Model Contract Farming Act 2018 and state-level experience."
Structure: (1) Potential — assured price, input/tech transfer, risk sharing, credit access (bankable contracts), export orientation (e.g., PepsiCo potatoes Punjab, Appachi cotton TN). (2) Model Act 2018 safeguards — (a) Separate authority (not APMC); (b) Price formula: max(MSP, mandi avg, pre-agreed); (c) No land alienation; (d) Conciliation-first dispute resolution (30 days); (e) Insurance mandatory; (f) FPO registration as "farmer" — collective bargaining. (3) State gaps — Tamil Nadu (2019 Act) operational, 3,000+ contracts; Punjab (2013 Act) — dispute resolution weak; Odisha — sponsor registration slow; most states not enacted. (4) Post-Farm Laws vacuum — no central law; states must act. (5) Way forward — Enact Model Act 2018 universally; digitise registry (AgriStack linkage); MSP as floor price in contracts; FPO-centric model (sponsor contracts with FPO, not individual); credit guarantee for contract crops. (Source: PYQ 2021 GS III; Model Contract Farming Act 2018; TN CF Act 2019; PepsiCo Case Study; NITI Aayog)

Exam Trap: C2 vs A2+FL — The MSP Formula Gap
Budget 2018 announced "MSP at 1.5 times cost of production" — interpreted as C2+50%. But CACP computes A2+FL+50% (paid-out cost + family labour), which is 15–20% lower than C2 (which includes imputed rent on own land and interest on own capital). In answers, always clarify: "The announced formula (C2+50%) diverges from the operational formula (A2+FL+50%) used by CACP, effectively reducing the MSP by 15–20% relative to the comprehensive cost benchmark." (Source: CACP Reports 2018–24; Budget Speech 2018–19; Parliamentary Q&A)

7.5 Price Deficiency Payment and Market Assurance Alternatives

  • Madhya Pradesh Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana (BBY, 2017–20): Price deficiency payment (PDP) for 8 crops (soybean, urad, moong, groundnut, til, ramtil, maize, onion). Farmer sells in mandi; state pays difference between MSP and modal mandi price (capped). Outcome: ₹5,000+ cr paid; fiscal stress; discontinued 2020. (Source: MP Govt BBY Guidelines; CAG Report 2020)
  • Haryana Bhavantar Bharpai Yojana (2018): Similar for vegetables/fruits; limited crops.
  • Telangana Rythu Bandhu (2018) / Odisha KALIA (2018): Per-acre investment support (₹10,000/yr, ₹5,000/season) — decoupled from price, not PDP.
  • NITI Aayog / 15th FC Recommendation: PDP for oilseeds/pulses (import substitution); MSP as floor for private trade (mandatory purchase at ≥MSP); decentralised procurement (DCP) expansion. (Source: NITI Aayog Discussion Paper 2020; 15th FC Vol. II)

8.2 PDS Reform Stack: Technology, Portability, and the Leakage-Exclusion Trade-off

Reform Layers (2013–present):

  1. End-to-end Computerisation: 100% FPS automated (5.4 lakh FPS); supply chain visibility (FCI → State → FPS).
  2. Aadhaar Seeding: 99% ration cards seeded; enables de-duplication (4.7 cr bogus cards removed 2013–23).
  3. e-PoS with Biometric Authentication (AePDS): Online transaction, real-time stock update; authentication success rate ~92% (network, biometric failure = exclusion).
  4. One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC, 2019): Portability across 37 states/UTs; 100+ cr transactions (2023–24); migrant access improved but inter-state coordination gaps (language, crop cycle mismatch).
  5. DBT Pilots (Cash in Lieu): Chandigarh, Puducherry, Dadra & Nagar Haveli — cash transfer replaces grain; mixed evidence (dietary diversity ↑, but food inflation risk, women's agency ↓).
  6. SMART PDS / PDS 2.0 (2024): Unified portal, AI-based anomaly detection, grievance redressal (1967 helpline), FPS viability (₹300/qtl margin + commission).
Source: DFPD Annual Reports; ONORC Dashboard; NIPFP PDS Studies; CAG PDS Audits; SMART PDS Guidelines 2024.
Exam Trap: Leakage vs Exclusion vs Inclusion Errors
Leakage = grain diverted before reaching beneficiary (corruption, open market sale). Exclusion error = eligible household not covered (frozen 2011 Census, migration, Aadhaar failure, homelessness). Inclusion error = ineligible household covered (ghost cards, outdated SECC). Key insight: Technology (e-PoS, Aadhaar, ONORC) reduced leakage (estimated 25–40% → 10–15%) but exclusion errors persist due to frozen Census base and biometric failures. In answers, distinguish: "While AePDS and ONORC curbed diversion, the 2011 Census freeze creates structural exclusion for 9–10 cr migrants/urban poor; biometric failure rates (8–10%) disproportionately affect elderly and manual workers." (Source: NFSA Evaluation; IHDS 2019–21; NIPFP Studies; DFPD Data)

Structure: (1) Production sufficiency — 330+ Mt foodgrains (FY24); buffer stocks 2–3× norms; FCI economic cost ₹2.5+ lakh cr. (3) Leakage-exclusion — 9–10 cr excluded (migrants, Census freeze); biometric failure 8–10%; ONORC helps but inter-state gaps. (4) POSHAN 2.0 — convergence attempt (ICDS + health + sanitation + drinking water); POSHAN Tracker (real-time growth monitoring); but — AWW workload (10+ registers), honorarium low (₹4,500–10,000), THR quality variable, last-mile convergence (health, WASH) weak. (5) Anaemia crisis — 67% children, 57% women; IFA compliance low; fortified rice rollout (112 aspirational districts, now universal) — evidence mixed. (6) Way forward — (a) Diversify PDS basket (millets, pulses, fortified oil); (b) Update Census base; (c) AWW as nutrition worker (delink from pre-school); (d) Community kitchens (urban migrants); (e) Dietary diversity metrics in POSHAN Tracker. (Source: PYQ 2022 GS III; NFHS-5; FCI Data; POSHAN 2.0 Guidelines; ICMR Dietary Guidelines)

8.5 Buffer Stocking, FCI Reform, and the Open Market Sale Dilemma

  • Buffer Norms (FCI, 2023): Rice 10.25 Mt (Jul) / 7.6 Mt (Jan); Wheat 7.5 Mt (Jul) / 5.0 Mt (Jan). Actual stocks often 2–3× norms (peak 2022: rice 45 Mt, wheat 30 Mt); carrying cost ₹2.5+ lakh cr/yr. (Source: FCI Buffer Norms 2023; CAG Report on FCI 2022)
  • Economic Cost (FY24): Rice ₹3,700/qtl, Wheat ₹2,800/qtl (procurement + storage + interest + distribution). Issue price: ₹0 (free). Subsidy = full economic cost.
  • Open Market Sale Scheme (OMSS): FCI sells excess stocks to bulk consumers/flour mills at reserve price to moderate open market prices. FY24: 10+ Mt wheat sold; FY23: 3 Mt rice. Critique: Ad hoc, distorts price signals, benefits large millers.
  • Decentralised Procurement (DCP): States procure on behalf of FCI (Punjab, Haryana, MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Telangana, etc.) — reduces transport cost, incentivises states. Shanta Kumar Committee (2015): Restrict FCI to non-DCP states; full DCP for wheat/rice; FCI as buffer manager only. (Source: FCI DCP Guidelines; Shanta Kumar Report; CAG FCI Audit)
  • FCI Reform Agenda (Pending): (a) Outsource storage (CWC/SWC/private); (b) Professionalise management; (c) Phase out physical handling (silo-based); (d) Dynamic buffer norms (linked to demand forecast); (e) Direct farmer payment (DBT for MSP) — piloted in Punjab/Haryana. (Source: Shanta Kumar Report; CAG; FCI Reform Committee 2023)
