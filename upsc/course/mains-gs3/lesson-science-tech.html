Science and Technology — Developments and Applications

GS III S&T covers Indian achievements in science and technology, indigenisation of technology, developing new technology, and applications in everyday life.

  • Space: ISRO (1969), INSAT (communication), IRS (remote sensing), PSLV (workhorse), GSLV (cryogenic, heavy lifts), Chandrayaan-1 (2008, water on moon), Chandrayaan-2 (2019, orbiter + lander attempt), Chandrayaan-3 (2023, soft landing near south pole — first country), Mangalyaan/Mars Orbiter Mission (2013, first Asian nation to Mars orbit, first in first attempt), Aditya-L1 (solar mission, 2023), Gaganyaan (human spaceflight, planned), NavIC (regional navigation satellite system), GSAT series, earth observation (EOS), small satellite launch vehicle (SSLV), commercial arm NSIL, IN-SPACe (private sector promotion).
  • Nuclear: Three-stage programme (Homi Bhabha) — Stage 1: PHWR (natural uranium, heavy water), Stage 2: FBR (plutonium, thorium breeding), Stage 3: thorium-based reactors (ABWR, AHWR); India has large thorium reserves (monazite sands), limited uranium. Civil nuclear cooperation — NSG waiver 2008 (India not NPT signatory), deals with US, France, Russia, Japan; Kudankulam (Russia), Tarapur, Rawatbhata, Kalpakkam, Kakrapar. Nuclear regulation — AERB; weapons — No First Use doctrine, voluntary moratorium on testing.
  • Defence: DRDO (1958), missiles — Agni (IRBM/ICBM), Prithvi (SRBM), Akash (SAM), BrahMos (supersonic cruise, India-Russia), Nirbhay (subsonic cruise), Astra (BVR AAM), Shaurya; LCA Tejas (fighter), Arjun (tank), Pinaka (MLRS), BrahMos exports; indigenous sonar, radar (Uttam AESA), electronic warfare; STRATEGIC — nuclear triad (land, air, sea — SINS, Arihant-class SSBN).
  • Biotechnology: Bt cotton (only GM crop approved commercially), Bt brinjal (moratorium), GM mustard (DHM-11, approval contested), Genome India Council, biofuels (ethanol blending — E20 target 2025), biopharma (vaccines — Covishield, Covaxin, India as vaccine hub), biotechnology parks, BIRAC, DBT.
  • Nanotechnology: National Mission on Nano Science and Technology (Nano Mission, 2007) — applications in medicine (drug delivery), electronics, energy, textiles, water purification; toxicity and ethical concerns.
  • IT and electronics: ISRO, MeitY, Digital India, Semiconductor Mission (India Semiconductor Mission, 2021, chip fabrication), supercomputers (Param series, Pratyush, Mihir), AI (National AI Strategy, NITI Aayog; AI for All), quantum computing (National Mission on Quantum Technologies and Applications — ₹8000 crore), blockchain, 5G (rollout 2022), IoT, cybersecurity (CERT-IN, Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre — I4C), data protection (Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023).
  • Health S&T: Telemedicine (e-Sanjeevani), genome sequencing (INSACOG — COVID variants), vaccines, diagnostics, medical devices (Ayushman Bharat digital health mission).

Awareness in IT, Space, Computers, Robotics

  • Robotics: Industrial automation, healthcare robots, defense robots (Daksh — bomb disposal), space robotics (rover Pragyan on Chandrayaan-3), humanoid (Vyommitra for Gaganyaan).
  • Computers: Supercomputing mission, quantum computing, edge computing, cloud, AI ethics (bias, transparency, job displacement).
  • Space applications: GPS-aided navigation (NavIC), telemedicine, tele-education, disaster management (flood/cyclone monitoring), agriculture (crop insurance, soil mapping), urban planning, climate monitoring.

Indigenisation of Technology

  • Policy: Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP 2020) — Buy Indian-IDDM priority, indigenously designed developed manufactured (IDDM); positive indigenisation lists (embargo on import of weapon systems); Atmanirbhar Bharat; Make in India; defence FDI limit raised (74% automatic, 100% government route).
  • Examples: Tejas, Arjun, Akash, BrahMos, INS Arihant, Tejas Mk1A, UAVs (Rustom, Heron), naval vessels (Mazagon Dock, GRSE), missiles.
Technique: ISRO achievements
Question: “India's space programme is application-oriented rather than prestige-driven.” Examine.
Answer: ISRO focuses on societal applications — remote sensing (agriculture, water, urban planning), communication (telemedicine, tele-education, DTH), navigation (NavIC), meteorology, disaster management (flood/cyclone forecasting), satellite-aided search and rescue (SAS&R), and space science (Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan, Aditya-L1). Cost-effective (Mangalyaan ~$74 million, Chandrayaan-3 ~$75 million). Commercial arm NSIL and IN-SPACe open space to private players.
Technique: Nuclear three-stage programme
Question: Why does India's nuclear power programme focus on thorium?
Answer: India has ~25% of world's thorium reserves (monazite sands in Kerala, Odisha) but limited uranium. Three-stage programme: Stage 1 PHWRs produce plutonium; Stage 2 FBRs breed more plutonium and convert thorium to U-233; Stage 3 uses U-233 and thorium for sustainable, long-term nuclear energy. NSG waiver 2008 enabled uranium imports for Stage 1 reactors under safeguards.

Common Traps

  • Confusing ISRO's launch vehicles — PSLV (polar, remote sensing), GSLV (geosynchronous, heavier, cryogenic), SSLV (small satellites, on-demand).
  • Missing that Chandrayaan-3's south pole landing (2023) made India the first country to soft-land near the lunar south pole.
  • Thinking Bt brinjal is commercially grown — it's under moratorium; only Bt cotton is approved.
  • Assuming India is an NSG member — waiver obtained (2008) but membership blocked by China (NPT condition).
  • Overlooking that India's No First Use nuclear doctrine is a declared policy, not a treaty obligation.

Memory Tricks

  • ISRO missions: Moon—Chandrayaan, Mars—Mangalyaan, Sun—Aditya-L1, Human—Gaganyaan, Navigation—NavIC.
  • Nuclear stages: Stage 1—uranium/PHWR; Stage 2—plutonium/FBR; Stage 3—thorium.
  • Defence missiles: Agni—surface-to-surface; Prithvi—short-range; Akash—surface-to-air; BrahMos—cruise.
Mains exam-day strategy
For S&T, connect technology to application (space → disaster management; biotech → vaccines; IT → governance). Mention Indian institutions (ISRO, DRDO, DBT, CSIR, ICMR, MeitY, NPCIL) and recent missions. For policy questions, discuss objectives, achievements, gaps, and the indigenisation angle (Atmanirbhar Bharat, Make in India, positive indigenisation lists). Conclude with ethical/safety concerns where relevant (AI bias, GM crops, nuclear safety, cybersecurity).

Strategic Autonomy: From Import Dependence to Indigenous Capability

The trajectory of India's science and technology policy has shifted from a phase of "import substitution" to one of "strategic autonomy." Historically, the Indian economy relied heavily on imported technologies, particularly in defence, pharmaceuticals, and electronics. This dependence created vulnerabilities: supply chain disruptions, high costs, and potential geopolitical leverage against India. The current paradigm, encapsulated in initiatives like Atmanirbhar Bharat (Self-Reliant India) and the Make in India campaign, seeks to reverse this trend not merely by manufacturing locally, but by developing indigenous intellectual property (IP). This distinction is crucial for UPSC analysis. Mere assembly (low value-addition) does not equate to strategic autonomy; true autonomy requires control over design, core components, and software ecosystems.

The concept of Positive Indigenisation is a specific policy mechanism where the government identifies critical technologies and places them on a "restricted import list." For these items, domestic production is prioritized even if foreign technology is superior in the short term. This approach acknowledges that long-term security and economic resilience depend on breaking the monopoly of foreign suppliers. However, this transition faces the "valley of death"—the gap between prototyping and mass manufacturing—where many Indian startups struggle due to lack of scale or access to raw materials.

In the realm of defence technology, the shift is evident in the Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020. This procedural overhaul moved from a "Buy" policy to a hierarchy: "Buy and Make," "Make," and finally "Indigenously Designed, Developed and Manufactured" (IDDM). The IDDM category is reserved for technologies where India has the capability to innovate. Examples include the Tejas Mk1A fighter jet, which integrates indigenous avionics and engines, and the BrahMos-II missile, developed with Russian collaboration but fully indigenized in production. Similarly, the Agni-V missile represents a leap in strategic deterrence capabilities, reducing reliance on foreign guidance systems or warhead technologies.

IT and Semiconductor Sovereignty represent the new frontier of this strategy. The India Semiconductor Mission (ISM), launched in 2021, aims to establish semiconductor design and fabrication units within India. This is not just about job creation; it is about securing the supply chain for critical electronics used in defence, healthcare, and infrastructure. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP) 2023 complements this by ensuring that data generated in India remains under Indian jurisdiction, preventing foreign tech giants from exploiting data asymmetries. Furthermore, the National Quantum Mission targets the development of quantum computers and sensors, areas where global competition is fierce. If India fails to enter this race now, it risks becoming a consumer of quantum technologies rather than a developer, losing strategic advantage in cryptography and material science.

Biotechnology and Biosecurity offer another dimension. While India has become a global hub for vaccine manufacturing (Covishield, Covaxin), the challenge lies in moving up the value chain to bioprocessing. The BIRAC (Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council) under DBT facilitates public-private partnerships to translate lab discoveries into marketable products. However, ethical concerns regarding GM crops and gene editing require robust regulatory frameworks. Strategic autonomy here means having a domestic seed research system that is not dependent on foreign intellectual property rights for basic genetic material.

Examiner's trap
A common error is conflating "Make in India" with "Indigenisation". Make in India encourages assembly of imported components (e.g., a car made in India with 90% foreign parts). Indigenisation requires domestic R&D and manufacturing of core technologies. Another trap is assuming all space missions are purely scientific; ISRO's primary mandate remains application-oriented (disaster management, agriculture), while other agencies often prioritize basic science exploration.
Exam-Day Strategy for S&T Questions
When answering questions on technology policy, always follow the structure: Context (Global context) → Indian Initiative (Mission/Act) → Impact (Economic/Social) → Challenge (Ethical/Technical). For instance, when discussing AI, mention the National AI Strategy, then discuss job displacement and bias, concluding with the need for an ethical framework. Always link technology to "Atmanirbhar Bharat" where relevant.

The Nuclear Three-Stage Programme: Physics, Geology, and Diplomacy

India's nuclear energy strategy is a masterclass in resource management under constraints. Unlike most nations that rely on imported uranium, India possesses abundant monazite sands rich in thorium but has limited uranium reserves. The Nuclear Three-Stage Programme (NTSP) was conceptualized to harness these resources sustainably over centuries.

Stage 1: Conventional Reactors. India currently operates Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) using natural uranium fuel. These reactors produce electricity and plutonium as a byproduct of fission. However, the plutonium generated is not sufficient to meet long-term energy needs if uranium imports are constrained.

Stage 2: Fast Breeder Reactors (FBR). To bridge the gap, India developed the Kalpakkam Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR). An FBR uses plutonium bred from uranium as fuel and converts fertile thorium or depleted uranium into fissile material. This process "breeds" more fuel than it consumes, effectively multiplying the energy yield. The PFBR is designed to generate 500 MWe of electricity. Once operational, it will provide the plutonium needed for Stage 3.

Stage 3: Thorium Cycle. This is the ultimate goal. In this stage, the FBRs will use thorium (abundant in India) as fuel to produce U-233, which is fissile. Thorium-based reactors are theoretically safer, produce less long-lived radioactive waste, and can burn transuranic elements from nuclear waste. This makes the cycle sustainable for thousands of years.

Diplomatic Context. The NTSP was enabled by a waiver from the Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG) in 2008. India, being a non-signatory to the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), faced restrictions on importing uranium. The waiver allowed imports specifically for Stage 1 reactors under safeguards, acknowledging India's peaceful nuclear intentions and its unique resource constraints. This diplomatic victory was crucial for initiating the programme.
