Evolution of India's Space Programme

1.1 Foundations and Early Vision

The Indian space programme began in the early 1960s when Dr Vikram Sarabhai articulated a vision that space technology should serve the developmental needs of a newly independent nation rather than pursue prestige alone. The establishment of the Indian National Committee for Space Research in 1962 and the subsequent formation of the Indian Space Research Organisation in 1969 institutionalised this vision. Early efforts focused on sounding rockets launched from the Thumba Equatorial Rocket Launching Station, which provided valuable data on the equatorial electrojet and demonstrated that a modest budget could yield scientific returns. The decision to build indigenous launch capability was driven by the recognition that reliance on foreign launch services would constrain both strategic autonomy and the ability to tailor missions to national priorities such as telecommunications, meteorology and remote sensing.

1.2 Development of Indigenous Launch Vehicles

The progression from the Satellite Launch Vehicle‑3 (SLV‑3) in the late 1970s to the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV) in the 1990s illustrates a deliberate strategy of incremental technology maturation. SLV‑3, though limited to a 40 kg payload to low Earth orbit, validated the end‑to‑end integration of solid propulsion, guidance and stage separation. The Augmented Satellite Launch Vehicle (ASLV) attempted to increase payload capacity but suffered repeated failures, prompting a redesign that culminated in the PSLV, which introduced a reliable four‑stage configuration combining solid and liquid propulsion. The PSLV’s first successful flight in 1994 placed the IRS‑1E satellite into orbit and established a track record of over 50 consecutive successful launches, making it the workhorse for both domestic and commercial missions. The subsequent development of the Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV) with an indigenous cryogenic upper stage addressed the requirement to place heavier communication satellites into geostationary transfer orbit, a capability that was finally demonstrated with the GSLV‑Mk II flight in 2014 after a decade of cryogenic engine development.

1.3 Institutional Expansion and International Partnerships

Parallel to launch vehicle development, ISRO expanded its ground infrastructure, establishing the Satish Dhawan Space Centre at Sriharikota as the primary launch port, the ISRO Telemetry, Tracking and Command Network (ISTRAC) for mission control, and the Indian Deep Space Network (IDSN) at Byalalu for interplanetary communication. The creation of the Antrix Corporation in 1992 commercialised launch services, enabling India to capture a share of the global small‑satellite market and generate revenue that could be reinvested in research. International cooperation has been selective; agreements with France for the Ariane‑5 launch of heavy satellites, with Russia for cryogenic engine technology transfer, and with the United States for the NASA‑ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) mission illustrate a pragmatic approach that balances technology acquisition with strategic autonomy. These partnerships have also facilitated capacity building in areas such as satellite navigation, where the Navigation with Indian Constellation (NavIC) system now provides regional positioning services independent of the Global Positioning System.

Major ISRO Missions and Their Strategic Significance

2.1 Remote Sensing and Earth Observation

The Indian Remote Sensing (IRS) series, beginning with IRS‑1A in 1988, created a continuous data stream for agriculture, forestry, water resources and urban planning. The launch of the Cartosat series introduced high‑resolution panchromatic and multispectral imaging, enabling cadastral mapping and infrastructure monitoring at sub‑meter resolution. The Resourcesat and Oceansat series added spectral bands tailored for vegetation health and ocean colour observations, respectively. These satellites feed the National Remote Sensing Centre, which distributes data through the Bhuvan geoportal, supporting both government decision‑making and commercial applications. The strategic value lies in the ability to monitor border areas, coastal zones and disaster‑prone regions without reliance on foreign imagery, thereby enhancing situational awareness for both civilian and defence agencies.

2.2 Communication, Navigation and Scientific Exploration

The INSAT/GSAT constellation provides transponder capacity for television broadcasting, tele‑education, tele‑medicine and strategic communication links for the armed forces. The introduction of the GSAT‑6 and GSAT‑7 series added dedicated military communication payloads, ensuring secure, jam‑resistant links for naval and air assets. NavIC, operational since 2018 with a constellation of seven satellites in geostationary and inclined geosynchronous orbits, delivers positioning accuracy better than 20 metres over the Indian region and 10 metres over the mainland, reducing dependence on GPS for critical infrastructure. On the scientific front, the Chandrayaan‑1 mission (2008) confirmed the presence of water molecules on the lunar surface, while the Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan, 2013) made India the first Asian nation to reach Martian orbit on a maiden attempt, demonstrating deep‑space navigation and autonomous operations. The Chandrayaan‑2 orbiter continues to map lunar mineralogy, and the upcoming Gaganyaan programme aims to place Indian astronauts in low Earth orbit, marking a transition from robotic to human spaceflight.

2.3 Commercial Launch Services and Space Diplomacy

Through Antrix and its successor NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), ISRO has launched over 300 foreign satellites from more than 30 countries, generating foreign exchange and establishing India as a reliable low‑cost launch provider. The PSLV’s multiple‑satellite deployment capability, demonstrated in the 2017 launch of 104 satellites in a single mission, showcased operational flexibility that attracts commercial customers. Space diplomacy is evident in the South Asia Satellite (GSAT‑9) gifted to neighbouring countries, which provides Ku‑band transponders for communication and disaster management, reinforcing regional goodwill. These activities also serve strategic objectives by embedding Indian space infrastructure in the security architectures of partner nations, creating interoperability that can be leveraged during crises.

Example Question
Explain how the progression from SLV‑3 to LVM3 reflects India’s approach to indigenous launch vehicle development and discuss the strategic implications of achieving a heavy‑lift capability.
The evolution from SLV‑3 (1979) through ASLV, PSLV, GSLV Mk II to LVM3 (2017) illustrates a stepwise maturation strategy where each vehicle addressed a specific payload and orbit requirement while reusing proven subsystems. SLV‑3 validated basic solid‑propulsion staging; ASLV attempted strap‑on augmentation but failed, prompting a redesign that yielded the reliable four‑stage PSLV with mixed solid‑liquid propulsion. The GSLV programme introduced an indigenous cryogenic upper stage (CE‑7.5) after technology denial regimes, culminating in the successful GSLV Mk II flight in 2014. LVM3 (formerly GSLV Mk III) integrates two massive solid boosters (S200), a liquid core stage (L110) and a high‑thrust cryogenic stage (C25), delivering 4 t to GTO and 10 t to LEO. Achieving heavy‑lift capability removes dependence on foreign launchers for heavy communication satellites (e.g., GSAT‑11) and enables future human‑rated missions (Gaganyaan) and deep‑space payloads, thereby enhancing strategic autonomy, reducing launch costs, and positioning India as a credible partner for international heavy‑payload contracts.
Exam Insight
When a question asks for “strategic implications”, link the technical milestone (e.g., heavy‑lift) to concrete policy outcomes: reduced import dependence, ability to launch military communication satellites on demand, leverage in diplomatic negotiations, and cost savings that can be redirected to R&D.

Nuclear Technology: Civilian and Strategic Dimensions

3.1 Three‑Stage Nuclear Power Programme

India’s nuclear energy roadmap, conceived by Homi Bhabha in the 1950s, is structured around three sequential stages to exploit the country’s modest uranium but abundant thorium reserves. The first stage deploys Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) that use natural uranium dioxide fuel and heavy water as both moderator and coolant, allowing operation without enrichment facilities. As of 2024, 22 PHWR units with a combined capacity of approximately 7.5 GW are in commercial operation, forming the backbone of the civilian nuclear fleet. The second stage introduces Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs) that breed plutonium‑239 from uranium‑238 in a sodium‑cooled core, thereby multiplying the fissile inventory. The Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) at Kalpakkam, with a design capacity of 500 MWe, is approaching criticality and will demonstrate the transition to a closed fuel cycle. The third stage envisions thorium‑based reactors, such as the Advanced Heavy Water Reactor (AHWR), which will utilise the bred uranium‑233 to achieve long‑term energy sustainability given India’s vast thorium deposits estimated at 846,000 tonnes.

3.2 Reactor Types, Fuel Cycle and Safety Architecture

The choice of PHWRs for the first stage was dictated by the ability to use natural uranium, avoiding the need for enrichment technology that was restricted by international export controls. Heavy water production, concentrated at the Heavy Water Board facilities in Kota, Baroda and Tuticorin, remains a strategic industrial capability. The fast breeder programme relies on reprocessing spent PHWR fuel to extract plutonium, a process carried out at the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) and the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR). Reprocessing also yields high‑level waste that is vitrified and stored in the Waste Immobilisation Plant at Tarapur. Safety oversight is exercised by the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB), which enforces the Atomic Energy Act, 1962, and the Radiation Protection Rules. Post‑Fukushima, India upgraded its safety standards, mandating passive decay heat removal systems for new reactors and conducting periodic safety reviews for operating units. The Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010, establishes a legal framework for compensation, channeling liability to the operator while providing a state‑backed insurance pool.

3.3 Strategic Programme and International Engagement

Parallel to the civilian programme, India maintains a strategic nuclear weapons capability that originated with the 1974 Pokhran‑I test and was expanded by the 1998 Pokhran‑II series, establishing a credible minimum deterrent. The weapons programme uses plutonium from dedicated production reactors (CIRUS, Dhruva) and highly enriched uranium from the Rare Materials Plant at Mysore. Delivery systems include land‑based ballistic missiles (Agni series), sea‑based K‑series submarine‑launched ballistic missiles, and air‑delivered gravity bombs, constituting a triad. India’s doctrine of “No First Use” and “Credible Minimum Deterrence” is articulated in the 2003 Cabinet Committee on Security statement. Internationally, the 2008 Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG) waiver and the subsequent civil nuclear cooperation agreements with the United States, France, Russia, Canada and Australia enabled import of uranium fuel and reactor components while preserving the strategic programme’s autonomy. India continues to seek NSG membership, arguing that its non‑proliferation record and adherence to IAEA safeguards on civilian facilities merit inclusion.

Defence Indigenisation: Policy, Institutions and Key Platforms

4.1 Policy Evolution and Institutional Framework

The drive for self‑reliance in defence production traces back to the 1962 Sino‑Indian conflict, which exposed the vulnerability of import dependence. The Defence Production and Procurement Policy (DPP) has been revised multiple times, with the 2020 Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) introducing the “Make‑I”, “Make‑II” and “Strategic Partnership” categories to incentivise private sector participation. The Department of Defence Production (DDP) under the Ministry of Defence oversees 41 Defence Public Sector Undertakings (DPSUs) and the Ordnance Factory Board (now corporatised into seven Defence PSUs). The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), with its network of over 50 laboratories, serves as the primary R&D engine, while the newly created Defence Innovation Organisation (DIO) and the Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX) scheme foster start‑up involvement. The Strategic Partnership model, launched in 2017, selects private firms to collaborate with foreign OEMs for manufacturing platforms such as submarines, fighter jets and helicopters, aiming to create domestic ecosystems with transfer of technology.

4.2 Flagship Indigenous Platforms

The Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) Tejas, developed by the Aeronautical Development Agency (ADA) and produced by Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL), exemplifies the challenges and successes of indigenisation. After a protracted development cycle, the Tejas Mk 1 achieved Final Operational Clearance in 2019, and the Mk 1A variant with an active electronically scanned array radar and beyond‑visual‑range missile capability is under production. The Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA) programme, currently in the design phase, aims for a fifth‑generation stealth fighter with indigenous engines (the Kaveri derivative) and sensor fusion. In the naval domain, the INS Arihant class of nuclear‑powered ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs) provides the sea‑based leg of the nuclear triad, with the first boat commissioned in 2016 and subsequent units under construction. The Project‑75I programme seeks to acquire six conventional submarines with air‑independent propulsion through the Strategic Partnership route. On land, the Arjun Main Battle Tank (Mk 1A) and the K9 Vajra‑T self‑propelled howitzer (licensed from South Korea but assembled in India) illustrate the mix of fully indigenous and co‑produced systems. The Akash surface‑to‑air missile system, with its indigenous Rajendra radar and command guidance, has been inducted in multiple regiments and exported to friendly nations.

4.3 Emerging Technologies and Private Sector Integration

Recent policy emphasis on emerging domains such as artificial intelligence, directed energy weapons, hypersonics and quantum communication has led to the establishment of the Defence AI Council and the Defence Space Agency. The iDEX programme has funded over 200 start‑ups working on swarm drones, secure communication waveforms and additive manufacturing of aerospace components. The Defence Investor Cell facilitates foreign direct investment up to 74 % under the automatic route, with higher percentages requiring government approval. The corporatisation of the Ordnance Factory Board into seven entities (e.g., Advanced Weapons and Equipment India Limited, Munitions India Limited) is intended to improve accountability, financial autonomy and competitiveness. However, challenges persist in the form of long procurement cycles, technology gaps in high‑thrust engines and active electronically scanned array radars, and the need for a robust intellectual property regime that protects indigenous innovations while enabling technology transfer.

Dual‑Use Technology: Governance, Export Controls and Future Trajectories

5.1 Concept and Regulatory Landscape

Dual‑use items are goods, software and technologies that have both civilian and military applications, ranging from high‑performance computing chips and encryption software to advanced materials and biotechnological agents. India’s regulatory framework is anchored in the Weapons of Mass Destruction and their Delivery Systems (Prohibition of Unlawful Activities) Act, 2005, and the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992, which empower the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) to maintain the Special Chemicals, Organisms, Materials, Equipment and Technologies (SCOMET) list. The SCOMET list is aligned with the control lists of the Nuclear Suppliers Group, the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR), the Wassenaar Arrangement and the Australia Group, reflecting India’s commitment to multilateral non‑proliferation norms despite not being a signatory to the Nuclear Non‑Proliferation Treaty. Licensing for export of SCOMET items requires an end‑use certificate and a no‑objection certificate from the Department of Defence Production, ensuring that transfers do not enhance the military capabilities of states of concern.

5.2 Implementation Challenges and Industry Impact

The dual‑use control regime creates compliance burdens for Indian high‑technology firms, particularly in the electronics, aerospace and biotechnology sectors. Companies must screen customers against denied‑party lists, obtain export licences for each transaction, and maintain detailed records for audit by the DGFT. The “deemed export” provision extends controls to technology transfers to foreign nationals within India, affecting research collaborations and academic exchanges. While these measures prevent inadvertent proliferation, they can also impede legitimate commercial activity and slow the integration of Indian firms into global supply chains. The government has responded by introducing the “Authorised Economic Operator” scheme, which grants trusted exporters faster clearance, and by publishing sector‑specific guidance documents that clarify classification thresholds for items such as field‑programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) and high‑precision machine tools.

5.3 Future Directions and Strategic Balancing

As India advances in areas like quantum computing, synthetic biology and autonomous systems, the dual‑use boundary will continue to blur. The National Security Council Secretariat is drafting a comprehensive Dual‑Use Technology Policy that aims to harmonise export controls with the “Atmanirbhar Bharat” (self‑reliant India) initiative, ensuring that indigenous innovation is not stifled by over‑regulation. International engagement will focus on shaping multilateral control regimes to reflect emerging technologies, advocating for criteria‑based rather than list‑based controls that can adapt faster to scientific breakthroughs. Domestically, the establishment of a Dual‑Use Technology Assessment Board, comprising representatives from DRDO, the Department of Space, the Department of Atomic Energy and industry, will provide real‑time classification advice. Balancing non‑proliferation obligations with the imperative to commercialise high‑value technologies will determine whether India can translate its scientific investments into economic and strategic advantage.

The integration of space, nuclear and defence capabilities under a coherent indigenisation strategy positions India to safeguard its sovereignty while contributing to global technological progress.

Space Situational Awareness and Debris Mitigation

India’s Space Situational Awareness (SSA) capability is anchored in the Network for Space Object Tracking and Analysis (NETRA) project, inaugurated in 2020 under the aegis of the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). NETRA integrates a ground‑based radar at the Satish Dhawan Space Centre, a telescope at the Mount Abu observatory, and a dedicated control centre at Bengaluru to catalogue objects larger than 10 cm in low‑Earth orbit (LEO) and larger than 1 m in geostationary orbit (GEO). The system feeds data to the Space Debris Monitoring and Mitigation (SDMM) cell, which issues collision‑avoidance advisories for operational satellites such as the Cartosat‑3 series and the NavIC constellation.

Mitigation measures follow the Inter‑Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) guidelines, which India adopted in 2007. Post‑mission disposal for LEO satellites mandates a controlled re‑entry within 25 years, while GEO platforms must be raised to a graveyard orbit at least 300 km above the operational belt. The 2022 launch of the PSLV‑C53 mission demonstrated a “passivation” protocol: residual propellant was vented and batteries discharged to reduce explosion risk. ISRO also participates in the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (UN‑COPUOS) working group on long‑term sustainability, contributing data from NETRA to the global Space‑Track catalogue.

Emerging challenges include the proliferation of mega‑constellations (e.g., Starlink, OneWeb) that increase conjunction frequency, and the lack of a legally binding international treaty on active debris removal. India’s draft Space Activities Bill, 2023, proposes a licensing regime that would require operators to submit end‑of‑life plans and to contribute to a national debris‑remediation fund. The Defence Space Agency (DSA) has been tasked with coordinating military SSA requirements, ensuring that strategic assets such as the GSAT‑7A communication satellite receive priority collision‑avoidance support.

Human Spaceflight Programme: Gaganyaan Architecture and Life Support

The Gaganyaan programme, approved by the Union Cabinet in 2018, aims to demonstrate indigenous human spaceflight capability by launching a crew of three to a 400 km circular orbit for up to seven days. The launch vehicle is the LVM3 (formerly GSLV Mk III), which has been human‑rated through a series of qualification tests including the Crew Escape System (CES) pad‑abort test in July 2022 and the high‑altitude abort test in 2023. The crew module, designated CM‑3, incorporates a carbon‑fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) pressure vessel, a thermal protection system (TPS) based on ablative silica‑phenolic tiles, and a redundant avionics suite compliant with the DO‑178C Level A standard.

Life‑support functions are provided by the Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS), developed jointly by ISRO’s Human Space Flight Centre (HSFC) and the Defence Bio‑Engineering and Electromedical Laboratory (DEBEL). The ECLSS manages atmosphere revitalisation (CO₂ removal via solid‑amine swing‑bed, O₂ generation by water electrolysis), temperature and humidity control, water recovery (urine‑to‑water distillation), and waste management. A closed‑loop water recovery rate of 85 % has been demonstrated in ground tests. Crew health monitoring relies on wearable biosensors transmitting real‑time telemetry to the Mission Control Centre (MCC) at Bengaluru, which is linked to the Indian Deep Space Network (IDSN) for continuous coverage.

Astronaut selection, conducted by the Institute of Aerospace Medicine (IAM) in 2020, shortlisted four Indian Air Force test pilots. Their training regimen includes microgravity exposure on parabolic flights in Russia, centrifuge runs at the Gagarin Cosmonaut Training Centre, and survival training in the Bay of Bengal. The first uncrewed test flight (G1) is scheduled for late 2024, followed by a second uncrewed mission (G2) carrying a humanoid payload “Vyommitra” to validate ECLSS performance. The crewed mission (H1) is targeted for 2025, contingent on successful completion of the abort and orbital‑flight test milestones.

UPSC‑style Applied Question
Explain how the Gaganyaan programme integrates indigenous launch‑vehicle human‑rating, crew‑escape system validation, and a closed‑loop life‑support architecture to meet the safety requirements for a crewed orbital mission. Illustrate with specific test milestones and subsystem redundancy features.
Answer Outline:
1. Launch‑vehicle human‑rating – LVM3 modifications: strengthened Vikas engines, additional health‑monitoring sensors, and a dedicated crew‑escape tower.
2. Crew‑Escape System (CES) validation – Pad‑abort test (July 2022) demonstrated 20 g acceleration and safe parachute deployment; high‑altitude abort test (2023) verified separation at Mach 1.2.
3. Closed‑loop ECLSS – CO₂ removal via solid‑amine swing‑bed (redundant beds), O₂ generation by water electrolysis (dual electrolyser units), water recovery 85 % (urine distillation + condensate).
4. Redundancy – Dual avionics strings (DO‑178C Level A), triple‑modular redundant (TMR) flight computers, backup battery packs for 30 min post‑separation.
5. Test milestones – G1 (uncrewed, 2024) validates orbital insertion and re‑entry; G2 (uncrewed with Vyommitra) validates ECLSS in orbit; H1 (crewed, 2025) confirms end‑to‑end crew safety.
Conclusion: The layered approach — vehicle, escape, life‑support — satisfies the “crew‑safety‑first” paradigm required for human rating.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “integration” of multiple subsystems, structure the answer by subsystem (launch vehicle, escape, life‑support) and then a final paragraph on cross‑system verification (e.g., abort‑to‑orbit scenarios). Use bullet‑style milestones for quick scanning; examiners reward clear mapping of test events to safety objectives.

Fast Breeder Reactor and Thorium Fuel Cycle Development

India’s three‑stage nuclear power programme envisions the Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR) as the bridge between the first‑stage Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) and the third‑stage thorium‑based reactors. The Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) at Kalpakkam, a 500 MWe sodium‑cooled, pool‑type reactor, achieved first criticality in September 2024 after a decade of construction and sodium‑system commissioning. The PFBR uses mixed oxide (MOX) fuel comprising plutonium‑239 recovered from PHWR spent fuel and depleted uranium, delivering a breeding ratio of approximately 1.2. Its core design incorporates 181 fuel sub‑assemblies and a passive decay‑heat removal system (DHRS) that relies on natural convection of sodium, eliminating the need for active pumps during a station‑blackout scenario.

The Fast Reactor Fuel Cycle Facility (FRFCF), co‑located at Kalpakkam, is being commissioned to close the fuel cycle: it will reprocess PFBR spent fuel, fabricate fresh MOX, and manage the separated minor actinides. The facility employs the advanced PUREX‑type aqueous process with enhanced safeguards, and its design capacity is 1.5 tHM yr⁻¹. Simultaneously, the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR) is developing the Metal Fuel Fast Breeder Reactor (MFFBR) concept, which promises higher breeding ratios and simpler fabrication using uranium‑plutonium‑zirconium alloy.

Thorium utilisation, the third stage, hinges on the conversion of thorium‑232 to fissile uranium‑233 in a thermal or fast neutron spectrum. The Advanced Heavy Water Reactor (AHWR‑300 LEU), a 300 MWe vertical pressure‑tube design, is the reference thorium‑fuelled reactor. It incorporates a passive core‑cooling system, a large gravity‑driven water pool for decay‑heat removal, and a fuel mix of (Th‑U‑233)O₂ and (Th‑Pu)O₂ pins. The AHWR design has completed pre‑project safety appraisal by the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB) and awaits government sanction for construction. Parallel research at Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) focuses on thorium‑based fuel fabrication, irradiation testing in the Dhruva reactor, and the development of a thorium‑uranium‑233 separation process that complies with IAEA safeguards.

Defence Procurement Procedure 2020: Offset, Indigenous Content and Procurement Timelines

The Defence Procurement Procedure (DPP) 2020, effective from 1 October 2020, replaced the 2016 edition and introduced a tiered categorisation to accelerate indigenisation. The principal categories are: Buy (Indian‑IDDM) – Indigenous Design, Development and Manufacture with a minimum 50 % indigenous content (IC) on cost basis; Buy (Indian) – Indian vendor with ≥ 50 % IC; Buy (Global – Manufacture in India) – foreign OEM must set up production line in India with ≥ 50 % IC; Buy (Global) – outright import when no Indian alternative exists. A new Make‑I (government‑funded) and Make‑II (industry‑funded) sub‑categories under “Make” encourage prototype development with milestone‑based payments.

Offset obligations apply to all contracts valued above ₹2,000 crore (≈ US$ 250 million). The offset percentage is 30 % of the contract value, dischargeable through eligible investments, technology transfer, or export of Indian‑made defence products. The DPP 2020 permits offset fulfilment via the “Offset Partner” route, where a foreign OEM partners with an Indian firm to co‑develop components, and through the “Offset Credit” mechanism for exports of defence items listed in the Positive Indigenisation List (PIL). The PIL, updated annually, currently contains 310 items (as of the 2023 revision) that must be procured only from indigenous sources after a stipulated timeline.

Procurement timelines have been compressed: the Acceptance of Necessity (AoN) to Request for Proposal (RFP) stage is capped at 12 months for “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)” and 18 months for “Buy (Global)”. The Contract Negotiation Committee (CNC) must conclude negotiations within 6 months of RFP response. The Defence Acquisition Council (DAC) now meets monthly, and the Defence Procurement Board (DPB) has been empowered to grant “Fast‑Track” approvals for critical capabilities (e.g., anti‑drone systems, high‑altitude pseudo‑satellites). The DPP also mandates a “Life‑Cycle Cost” (LCC) evaluation for all major platforms, shifting focus from lowest‑bid to total‑ownership cost.

Indigenous Aero‑Engine Development: Kaveri, HTFE‑25 and Future Programmes

The Gas Turbine Research Establishment (GTRE), a DRDO laboratory, has pursued indigenous aero‑engine development since the 1980s. The Kaveri (GTX‑35VS) afterburning turbofan, designed for 80 kN thrust with afterburner, was intended to power the Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) Tejas. After multiple redesigns — including a shift from a single‑spool to a two‑spool configuration and the incorporation of a full‑authority digital engine control (FADEC) — the engine completed 3,000 hours of ground testing by 2022 but has not yet cleared flight‑clearance milestones due to high‑pressure turbine blade life shortfalls. Consequently, the Tejas Mk 1A fleet is powered by the General Electric F404‑GE‑IN20 engine, while Kaveri continues as a technology demonstrator for future platforms.

In parallel, GTRE developed the HTFE‑25 (High‑Thrust Fan Engine‑25 kN), a non‑afterburning turbofan for unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) such as the Rustom‑II and the Tactical Advanced Platform for Aerial Surveillance (TAPAS). The HTFE‑25 achieved its first engine run in 2019 and completed altitude testing up to 30,000 ft in 2021, demonstrating a specific fuel consumption (SFC) of 0.65 kg kN⁻¹ h⁻¹. Productionisation is being transferred to Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) under a licence‑to‑manufacture agreement, with an initial order of 50 engines for the DRDO’s UAV programmes.

Looking ahead, the “Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft” (AMCA) programme requires a 110 kN class afterburning turbofan. DRDO has initiated the “Kaveri‑Derivative” (KD) programme, leveraging Kaveri’s core and integrating a new low‑pressure spool, advanced single‑crystal turbine blades, and a variable‑cycle architecture. A joint development agreement with Safran (France) for the “Joint Engine Development” (JED) was signed in 2023, focusing on high‑temperature materials and compressor aerodynamics. The target is a technology demonstrator by 2028 and series production by the early 2030s, aligning with the AMCA’s first‑flight schedule.

Small Satellite Launch Vehicle Development and Market Positioning

The Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (SSLV) programme was conceived to address the growing demand for dedicated rideshare‑free launches of satellites up to 500 kg into a 500 km sun‑synchronous orbit. The vehicle uses three solid‑propellant stages — SS1, SS2 and SS3 — and a liquid‑propellant velocity‑trimming module (VTM) for precise orbit insertion. The first developmental flight (SSLV‑D1) lifted off from the First Launch Pad at Satish Dhawan Space Centre on 7 August 2022; a sensor anomaly in the VTM caused the payloads to be placed in an elliptical orbit, resulting in mission loss. The second flight (SSLV‑D2) on 10 February 2023 successfully injected the EOS‑07, Janus‑1 and AzaadiSAT‑2 satellites into their intended orbits, validating the vehicle’s guidance, navigation and control algorithms.

Commercial operations are managed by NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), which markets SSLV at an estimated launch price of US $4–5 million — roughly one‑third the cost of a PSLV‑XL mission. The vehicle’s 72‑hour launch‑campaign timeline, enabled by a simplified integration flow and a mobile launch platform, positions it competitively for constellation deployment schedules that require rapid replenishment. As of the 2024‑25 launch manifest, NSIL has secured contracts for at least six SSLV missions, including dedicated rides for domestic start‑ups such as Pixxel and Dhruva Space, and a foreign customer mission for a European Earth‑observation microsatellite.

Technical upgrades under the SSLV‑D3 configuration include a higher‑performance SS1 motor with a 15 % increase in specific impulse, a redesigned VTM with redundant thrusters, and an upgraded avionics suite supporting autonomous fault detection. The programme also serves as a testbed for private‑sector participation: the motor cases for SS1 and SS2 are now fabricated by a consortium led by Larsen & Toubro, while the VTM propulsion system is supplied by a Bengaluru‑based start‑up under a Development‑cum‑Production Partner (DcPP) agreement. This industry‑led supply chain is intended to reduce recurring cost per launch to below US $3 million by 2027.

Strategically, SSLV complements the heavier PSLV and GSLV families by providing a responsive launch option for national security payloads — such as the Defence Research and Development Organisation’s (DRDO) experimental microsatellites — without waiting for a shared‑ride slot. The vehicle’s all‑solid first three stages also simplify storage and handling, an advantage for rapid‑deployment scenarios from mobile launch complexes that are under study for the Andaman and Nicobar Command.

Nuclear Liability Framework: Domestic Act and International Convention Alignment

The Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act (CLNDA), enacted in 2010, establishes a channelled liability regime in which the operator of a nuclear installation — currently the Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL) — is strictly liable for up to 300 million Special Drawing Rights (SDR) (≈ US $400 million) per incident. The Act permits the operator to seek recourse against suppliers for defects in design, construction or services, a provision that diverges from the international norm of exclusive operator liability. This supplier‑recourse clause (Section 17(b)) has been a focal point in negotiations with foreign vendors, notably for the Russian VVER‑1000 units at Kudankulam and the French EPR reactors proposed for Jaitapur.

India ratified the Convention on Supplementary Compensation for Nuclear Damage (CSC) in 2016, which entered into force for India on 4 May 2017. The CSC provides an additional international tier of compensation funded by contributions from contracting parties, raising the total available compensation to approximately 1.5 billion SDR. To reconcile the CLNDA with CSC obligations, the government issued the Nuclear Damage (Amendment) Rules, 2020, which clarified that the operator’s right of recourse does not diminish the international fund’s obligation to pay victims, and established a national insurance pool — the Nuclear Insurance Pool (NIP) — with a capacity of ₹1,500 crore (≈ US $180 million) backed by the General Insurance Corporation of India and private reinsurers.

In practice, the liability cap and the supplier‑recourse provision have influenced contract structures. For the Kudankulam Units 3‑4, the Inter‑Governmental Agreement (IGA) includes a side‑letter that limits supplier liability to the contract value and requires the Russian side to contribute to the NIP. For the proposed Jaitapur EPR project, the French consortium (EDF‑Areva) has sought a bilateral agreement that caps supplier liability at €300 million, citing the CLNDA’s open‑ended recourse as a commercial risk. The Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) has indicated that a legislative amendment to replace the supplier‑recourse clause with a defined financial limit is under consideration, but no bill has been tabled as of the 2024 winter session.

The liability framework also affects India’s participation in the International Atomic Energy Agency’s (IAEA) Action Plan on Nuclear Safety, where the country reports on the adequacy of its compensation mechanisms. The 2023 IAEA peer review commended the establishment of the NIP but recommended periodic stress‑testing of the pool’s solvency against a postulated severe accident scenario involving multiple units.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Explain how the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010 aligns with the Convention on Supplementary Compensation (CSC) and identify the remaining point of divergence that affects foreign reactor contracts.”
The CLNDA channels primary liability to the operator up to 300 million SDR and creates a national insurance pool, satisfying the CSC’s requirement for a minimum national compensation layer. India’s ratification of the CSC adds an international tier, raising total compensation to ~1.5 billion SDR. The key divergence is Section 17(b) of the CLNDA, which grants the operator a right of recourse against suppliers for defects. The CSC, like the Vienna and Paris conventions, envisages exclusive operator liability; therefore, foreign vendors view the open‑ended supplier recourse as a commercial risk, leading to side‑letters or demands for legislative amendment. The answer must cite the 300 million SDR cap, the NIP capacity, the CSC entry‑into‑force date (4 May 2017), and the specific clause (Section 17(b)) that remains contentious.

Then isolate the single clause that deviates from the international model — Section 17(b) supplier recourse — and link it to concrete contract negotiations (Kudankulam side‑letter, Jaitapur talks). Hypersonic Technology Demonstrator Vehicle and Scramjet Propulsion Progress

The Hypersonic Technology Demonstrator Vehicle (HSTDV) is an unmanned scramjet‑powered vehicle developed by the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) to validate air‑breathing propulsion at Mach 6. The first successful flight test occurred on 7 September 2020 from the Dr. A. P. J. Abdul Kalam Island, where the vehicle, boosted by a solid‑rocket motor to an altitude of 30 km and a speed of Mach 5.5, transitioned to scramjet mode and sustained combustion for approximately 20 seconds, covering a range of 450 km. The scramjet engine, designated the “Scramjet‑1”, uses indigenous high‑temperature nickel‑based superalloys for the combustor and a carbon‑carbon composite inlet, both qualified through ground tests at the Hypersonic Wind Tunnel (HWT) in Hyderabad.

A second flight on 27 October 2022 demonstrated repeatability, achieving a longer scramjet burn of 30 seconds and a peak Mach number of 6.2. Telemetry confirmed stable flame‑holding across a range of equivalence ratios, validating the fuel‑injection strategy that employs a staged‑injection of kerosene‑based JP‑7 fuel. The vehicle’s guidance system, derived from the Agni‑V re‑entry vehicle, executed a pre‑programmed trajectory with a terminal maneuver to simulate a hypersonic cruise missile profile.

The HSTDV programme feeds directly into the BrahMos‑II hypersonic cruise missile project, a joint venture with Russia’s NPO Mashinostroyeniya. BrahMos‑II aims for a range of 600 km at Mach 7–8, using a scramjet derived from the HSTDV core but with a larger combustor diameter (0.5 m vs. 0.35 m) and an advanced thermal‑protection system based on ultra‑high‑temperature ceramics (UHTCs). DRDO’s Gas Turbine Research Establishment (GTRE) is also exploring a dual‑mode ramjet/scramjet (DMRJ) that can operate from Mach 3 to Mach 8, which would enable a single propulsion package for both supersonic and hypersonic regimes.

Key challenges remaining include scaling the scramjet to full‑size missile dimensions, achieving reliable ignition at flight‑condition stagnation pressures above 1.5 MPa, and developing a reusable ground‑test facility capable of simulating continuous Mach 8 flow for durations exceeding 60 seconds. The Defence Acquisition Council (DAC) approved a ₹2,200 crore (≈ US $260 million) funding tranche in 2023 for the “Hypersonic Missile Technology Development” mission mode project, targeting a technology demonstrator flight of a full‑scale BrahMos‑II configuration by 2027.

Quantum Key Distribution Satellite Programme and Secure Communication Architecture

India’s Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) satellite initiative, led by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) in collaboration with the Raman Research Institute (RRI) and the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), aims to establish a space‑based quantum‑secure link for strategic communications. The first technology demonstrator, designated QKD‑Sat‑1, is a 300 kg microsatellite slated for launch on a PSLV‑C58 mission in the 2025‑26 timeframe. It carries a polarization‑entangled photon source based on spontaneous parametric down‑conversion (SPDC) in a periodically poled potassium titanyl phosphate (PPKTP) crystal, a fine‑pointing acquisition, tracking and pointing (ATP) subsystem with a 5 µrad pointing accuracy, and a single‑photon avalanche diode (SPAD) array cooled to ‑30 °C.

Ground segment development includes two optical ground stations (OGS) — one at the Space Applications Centre (SAC) in Ahmedabad and another at the Naval Physical and Oceanographic Laboratory (NPOL) in Kochi — each equipped with a 1.2 m aperture telescope, adaptive optics for turbulence compensation, and a time‑tagging module synchronized to a common‑view GPS disciplined oscillator. The protocol implemented is the decoy‑state BB84 scheme, chosen for its resilience to photon‑number‑splitting attacks and compatibility with existing classical encryption hardware.

In a 2023 laboratory demonstration, RRI achieved a secure key rate of 1.2 kbps over a 150 km free‑space link using a 10 cm telescope, validating the error‑correction and privacy‑amplification algorithms that will be ported to the flight software. The satellite’s mission profile includes a 6‑month commissioning phase during which key exchange sessions will be conducted with both OGSs, followed by an operational phase supporting the Indian Navy’s secure maritime domain awareness network and the Strategic Forces Command’s command‑and‑control links.

Internationally, the programme is benchmarked against China’s Micius satellite (launched 2016), which demonstrated intercontinental QKD over 7,600 km. India’s approach differs by emphasizing indigenous component development — especially the SPAD array and the ATP subsystem — and by integrating the quantum layer with the existing Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (IRNSS/NavIC) timing infrastructure, enabling sub‑nanosecond synchronization without reliance on foreign GNSS. The Department of Space has allocated ₹1,100 crore (≈ US $130 million) for the QKD‑Sat series through 2030, with a follow‑on constellation of three satellites planned to provide continuous coverage over the Indian Ocean Region.

Defence Artificial Intelligence: Project INDRA and Autonomous Weapon Systems

Project INDRA (Integrated Networked Decision‑making and Response Architecture) is DRDO’s flagship artificial‑intelligence programme aimed at embedding machine‑learning‑driven situational awareness, target recognition and decision support across the three services. Launched in 2019 under the aegis of the Centre for Artificial Intelligence and Robotics (CAIR), INDRA comprises three verticals: INDRA‑Surveil for multi‑sensor fusion (radar, electro‑optical, SIGINT), INDRA‑Strike for autonomous weapon‑engagement planning, and INDRA‑Log for predictive maintenance of platforms such as the Su‑30MKI fleet and the Arihant‑class submarines.

INDRA‑Surveil has been field‑tested on the Netra airborne early‑warning and control (AEW&C) platform, where a convolutional neural network (CNN) processes raw radar returns in real time to classify aerial targets with a reported 96 % accuracy for fighter‑size contacts at ranges up to 350 km. The system fuses data from the indigenous Uttam AESA radar, the DRDO‑developed electronic support measures (ESM) suite, and satellite‑linked track feeds, producing a unified tactical picture disseminated over the Software Defined Radio (SDR) backbone.

INDRA‑Strike explores the ethical and operational boundaries of lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS). A 2022 live‑fire trial at the Pokhran Test Range demonstrated a swarm of 12 loitering munitions (the “Nag‑ML” variant) executing a coordinated attack on a simulated armored column using a distributed consensus algorithm that allocated targets without human‑in‑the‑loop approval after a pre‑mission “rules‑of‑engagement” upload. The trial was conducted under the oversight of the Defence AI Ethics Committee, which mandated a hard‑coded “human‑on‑the‑loop” abort trigger that can be activated by a ground commander within 2 seconds of launch.

INDRA‑Log leverages digital‑twin models of gas‑turbine engines (including the Kaveri core) to predict remaining useful life with a mean absolute error of 45 hours, enabling condition‑based maintenance that has reduced unscheduled engine removals by 27 % in the Su‑30MKI fleet during the 2023‑24 fiscal year. The programme’s data pipeline ingests 1.2 TB of sensor data per sortie, processed on an edge‑computing cluster based on the indigenous “Shakti” RISC‑V processor.

Future milestones include the integration of INDRA‑Surveil with the Indian Navy’s P‑8I maritime patrol aircraft by 2025, the deployment of a fully autonomous “swarm‑as‑a‑service” capability for the Army’s Mechanised Infantry by 2027, and the establishment of a joint AI test‑bed with the United States under the Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies (iCET) to benchmark algorithmic robustness against adversarial attacks.

National Space Legislation and Liability Regime

India’s space governance has evolved from executive‑driven programme management to a layered legal architecture that now addresses private‑sector participation, international treaty obligations and domestic liability. The cornerstone remains the Outer Space Treaty (1967), the Rescue Agreement (1968), the Liability Convention (1972) and the Registration Convention (1975), all ratified by India between 1967 and 1982. Domestically, the Indian Space Policy 2023 supersedes the 2011 Satellite Communication Policy and the 2020 Spacecom Policy, providing a unified directive for both governmental and non‑governmental entities. It designates the Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre (IN‑SPACe) as the single‑window authorisation body for commercial launches, satellite operations and ground‑segment services.

The Draft Space Activities Bill, 2017 (still under parliamentary consideration) proposes a licensing regime, a national registry of space objects, and a liability framework that aligns operator responsibility with the 1972 Convention while retaining a sovereign “state‑as‑launching‑state” backstop. Key provisions include: (a) mandatory insurance or financial guarantee of at least ₹500 crore per launch; (b) a cap on operator liability at 300 million SDR (≈ US $400 million) for third‑party damage, mirroring the Convention; (c) a government‑backed indemnity fund for catastrophic losses exceeding the cap. The Bill also empowers the Central Government to prescribe technical standards for debris mitigation, end‑of‑life disposal and frequency coordination, thereby operationalising the Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines (2007) adopted by the UN COPUOS.

In practice, the Department of Space (DoS) issues Launch Authorisation Orders under the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 and the Wireless Telegraphy Act, 1933 for spectrum allocation, while the Ministry of Defence controls strategic payload clearances. The Space Situational Awareness (SSA) Control Centre at Bengaluru, operational since 2020, provides conjunction analysis for all Indian‑registered objects, feeding data into the International Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) database. Private firms such as Skyroot Aerospace and Agnikul Cosmos have secured launch licences under the interim “IN‑SPACe Authorisation Framework” (2022), which requires a Technical Safety Review and a Financial Viability Certificate before each mission.

Liability disputes are adjudicated under the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010 analogue — the Space Activities Bill proposes a dedicated Space Tribunal with appellate recourse to the High Courts. Until enactment, the Convention on International Liability for Damage Caused by Space Objects governs state‑to‑state claims, while private claims follow ordinary tort law. The 2023 policy explicitly encourages insurance pooling through the General Insurance Corporation of India (GIC Re) to meet the financial guarantee requirement, a model borrowed from the nuclear liability pool.

Nuclear Waste Management and Deep Geological Repository Development

India follows a closed fuel‑cycle strategy: spent fuel from Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) and Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs) is reprocessed at the Tarapur (Maharashtra), Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu) and Kota (Rajasthan) facilities to recover plutonium and uranium. The high‑level waste (HLW) generated — primarily fission products and minor actinides — is vitrified in borosilicate glass matrices at the Vitrification Plant, Tarapur (commissioned 1995) and the Integrated Waste Management Facility (IWMF), Kalpakkam (operational 2012). The current inventory stands at roughly 3,500 m³ of vitrified HLW canisters, stored in engineered near‑surface facilities designed for 50‑year institutional control.

The Atomic Energy (Safe Disposal of Radioactive Waste) Rules, 1987 (amended 2015) mandate a transition to a Deep Geological Repository (DGR) for permanent isolation. The Nuclear Waste Management Division (NWMD) of the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) initiated site‑characterisation studies in 2005 across three geological domains: (i) the Deccan Traps basalt in Maharashtra, (ii) the Granite‑Gneiss complex of the Southern Granulite Terrain (Tamil Nadu/Karnataka border), and (iii) the Sedimentary basins of Rajasthan. In 2021, the Site Selection Committee shortlisted the Bhandara‑Gondia region (Maharashtra) and the Kolar‑Chitradurga belt (Karnataka) for detailed hydro‑geological, geochemical and seismic investigations.

Engineered barrier concepts under evaluation combine a copper canister (corrosion allowance 1 mm/10⁴ yr), a bentonite buffer (dry density 1.6 g/cm³) and the host rock as the final barrier. Thermal modelling limits the canister surface temperature to 100 °C to preserve bentonite integrity, dictating a maximum areal loading of 0.5 MW/ha. The repository design targets a 10⁵‑year containment performance, consistent with IAEA Safety Standards Series No. SSG‑23.

Funding is routed through the Nuclear Waste Management Fund, capitalised by a levy of 0.5 % of gross electricity revenue from nuclear plants, yielding an annual corpus of approximately ₹1,200 crore. International cooperation includes participation in the OECD/NEA “IGSC” (Integration Group for the Safety Case) and a bilateral technical exchange with France’s ANDRA on clay‑host repository experience. Public outreach programmes, mandated by the 2015 amendment, have been conducted in the shortlisted districts since 2022 to address socio‑political acceptance — a prerequisite for the final licensing decision expected post‑2030.

Directed Energy Weapons: Laser and High‑Power Microwave Programmes

The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) pursues two complementary directed‑energy tracks: High‑Energy Lasers (HEL) for hard‑kill interception of drones, rockets and artillery shells, and High‑Power Microwaves (HPM) for soft‑kill disruption of electronics. The Laser Science and Technology Centre (LASTEC), Delhi, leads the HEL effort, while the Centre for High Energy Systems and Sciences (CHESS), Hyderabad, coordinates HPM development.

The KALI (Kilo‑Ampere Linear Injector) series constitutes the HPM backbone. KALI‑5000, commissioned in 2020, delivers a 40 GW, 1 µs electron beam pulse at 1 MeV, capable of generating a radiated microwave field exceeding 10 kV/m at 1 km. Ground tests in 2021 demonstrated functional degradation of a representative avionics suite at 500 m range. A mobile “KALI‑M” variant, mounted on a 8 × 8 Tatra chassis, completed mobility trials in 2023, targeting integration with the Army’s Integrated Air Command and Control System (IACCS).

On the laser front, LASTEC’s “Surya” 30 kW fibre‑laser demonstrator achieved continuous‑wave operation for 60 seconds in 2022, tracking a 2 kg quad‑copter at 3 km and neutralising it via thermal burn‑through. The system employs a beam‑combining architecture of six 5 kW single‑mode fibres, a fast‑steering mirror (bandwidth 2 kHz) and an adaptive optics module correcting atmospheric turbulence up to Cₙ² = 1×10⁻¹⁴ m⁻²ᐟ³. A naval version, “Varuna”, is slated for shipboard trials on the INS Vikramaditya by 2025, with a target output of 100 kW using spectral beam combining of 20 × 5 kW modules.

Both programmes share a common Power‑Conditioning Subsystem (PCS) based on indigenous SiC‑MOSFET switches, delivering > 95 % efficiency at 10 kV DC. The PCS is also the core of the “Shakti” pulsed‑power module used in the Electromagnetic Railgun (EMRG) demonstrator at the Terminal Ballistics Research Laboratory (TBRL), Chandigarh. Operational doctrine, drafted by the Defence Technology Council (DTC) in 2023, classifies DEWs as “Non‑Kinetic Effectors” under the Joint Doctrine for Air and Missile Defence, mandating a human‑on‑the‑loop authorisation for any hard‑kill engagement.

Defence Space Agency: Architecture, Mission Sets and Integration with ISRO

The Defence Space Agency (DSA) was raised on 28 September 2019 under the Integrated Defence Staff (IDS), headquartered in Bengaluru with a collocated Space Operations Centre (SpOC) at the ISRO Telemetry, Tracking and Command Network (ISTRAC) campus. The agency is headed by a three‑star officer (Air Vice Marshal / Rear Admiral / Major General) reporting directly to the Chief of Defence Staff (CDS). Its charter includes: (a) operational control of all military space assets — communication (GSAT‑7A, GSAT‑6A), navigation (NavIC‑augmented), reconnaissance (RISAT‑2BR1, Cartosat‑3), and early‑warning (GSAT‑7C); (b) space‑domain awareness (SDA) through the Space Situational Awareness Control Centre (SSACC); (c) coordination of launch requirements with NewSpace India Limited (NSIL) and IN‑SPACe for dedicated military missions.

The DSA’s Mission‑Set Matrix defines four core verticals: Secure Communications (SATCOM), Navigation & Timing (NAV), Intelligence, Surveillance & Reconnaissance (ISR), and Space Control (SC). SATCOM leverages the GSAT‑7A (Ku‑band) and GSAT‑6A (S‑band) transponders, providing beyond‑line‑of‑sight (BLOS) connectivity for the Army’s Tactical Communication System (TCS) and the Navy’s Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA) network. NAV exploits the NavIC L5/S‑band signals with military‑grade encryption (AES‑256) and anti‑spoofing modules developed by the Defence Electronics Research Laboratory (DLRL). ISR integrates synthetic‑aperture radar (SAR) data from RISAT‑2BR1 (X‑band, 1 m resolution) and electro‑optical imagery from Cartosat‑3 (0.25 m panchromatic) into the Integrated Intelligence Grid (IIG). SC encompasses both defensive (collision avoidance, jamming resilience) and offensive (kinetic/ non‑kinetic ASAT) capabilities; the latter remains under the Strategic Forces Command (SFC) with DSA providing targeting data.

Inter‑agency workflows are codified in the Joint Space Operations Directive (JSOD) 2021. A typical cycle: the Tri‑Service Space Requirements Board (TSSRB) submits a Capability Demand Document (CDD) to DSA; DSA validates against the Space Asset Catalogue and forwards a Launch Request to NSIL; NSIL contracts a launch vehicle (PSLV/GSLV/SSLV) and schedules the mission; post‑launch, DSA assumes on‑orbit operations via the SpOC, while ISRO provides telemetry support for the first 30 days. The Space Security Coordination Group (SSCG), chaired by the National Security Advisor (NSA), resolves policy conflicts, export‑control clearances and frequency‑spectrum disputes.

Recent milestones include the “Mission Shakti‑2” (2024) — a dedicated launch of a 350 kg ELINT satellite on an SSLV‑D2, achieving a 550 km sun‑synchronous orbit within 18 months of CDD approval, demonstrating the compressed procurement timeline targeted by the Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020. The DSA also participates in the Quad‑Space Working Group (India‑US‑Japan‑Australia) for joint SDA data‑sharing, and in the UN COPUOS “Long‑Term Sustainability” negotiations, representing India’s military space interests.
