Economic Growth and Development

Indian economy is the world's fifth-largest by nominal GDP and third-largest by PPP. GS III economy covers growth, development, planning, mobilisation of resources, and inclusive growth challenges.

  • GDP vs GVA: GDP = gross value added + product taxes − subsidies. GVA = value of output − intermediate consumption. GVA by sector shows sectoral performance; GDP includes taxes.
  • Growth vs development: Growth = increase in output/income; development = growth + improvement in health, education, equity, freedom (HDI, multidimensional poverty).
  • HDI: Human Development Index (UNDP) — health (life expectancy), education (schooling), standard of living (GNI per capita). India ranks ~134/193 (2022).
  • Planning in India: Planning Commission (1950–2014) replaced by NITI Aayog (2015) — think tank, cooperative federalism, Aspirational Districts Programme, no five-year plans since 12th Plan (2012–17).
  • Mobilisation of resources: Tax revenue (direct — income, corporate; indirect — GST), non-tax (dividends, disinvestment, spectrum), borrowing (market stabilisation scheme, ways and means), external (World Bank, ADB, bilateral, sovereign bonds).

Fiscal Policy and Budgeting

  • Union Budget: Article 112 — Annual Financial Statement. Revenue account (current income/expenditure) and capital account (assets/liabilities). Presented Feb 1 (since 2017), merged Railway Budget (2017).
  • Fiscal deficit: Total expenditure − total receipts (excluding borrowings). FRBM Act 2003 targets — eliminate revenue deficit, reduce fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP (targets relaxed post-pandemic).
  • Revenue deficit: Revenue expenditure exceeds revenue receipts — indicates government is borrowing for consumption, not investment.
  • Primary deficit: Fiscal deficit minus interest payments — shows current-year borrowing excluding past debt burden.
  • Deficit financing: Borrowing from RBI (monetised deficit — now limited), market borrowing, external debt.
  • GST: 101st Amendment 2016, launched July 1, 2017 — one nation one tax, subsumed ~17 central/state taxes, dual GST (CGST/SGST/IGST), GST Council (constitutional body, Article 279A), compensation cess (ends 2026).

Monetary Policy and Banking

  • RBI: Established 1935, nationalised 1949; monetary authority, banker to banks/government, currency issuer, regulator, manager of forex.
  • Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): 2016 (Finance Act), 6 members (3 RBI + 3 external), inflation targeting — CPI inflation 4% ± 2% band; repo rate is policy rate.
  • Policy rates: Repo (RBI lends to banks), reverse repo (RBI borrows), MSF (marginal standing facility — emergency overnight), bank rate (same as MSF), CRR (cash reserve ratio), SLR (statutory liquidity ratio).
  • Inflation: CPI (retail, headline), WPI (wholesale), core inflation (excluding food and fuel). Causes — demand-pull, cost-push, imported (oil).
  • Banking: Public sector banks (PSBs), private banks, small finance banks, payment banks, regional rural banks (RRBs), local area banks. NPAs (bad loans), Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016, NCLT, SARFAESI Act, one-time settlement, bad bank (NARCL).
  • Financial inclusion: PMJDY (zero-balance accounts), MUDRA, Stand-Up India, SHG-bank linkage, BC (business correspondent) model, Jan Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile (JAM) trinity, DBT.
  • Digital payments: UPI (NPCI), RTGS, NEFT, IMPS, BHIM, RuPay, AEPS, e-RUPI; RBI's regulatory sandbox.

External Sector

  • Balance of Payments (BoP): Current account (trade in goods/services, income, transfers) and capital account (FDI, FPI, ECB, NRI deposits, banking capital). BoP = current + capital + errors/omissions + reserve changes.
  • FDI vs FPI: FDI — long-term, controlling interest, brings technology/management (automatic route + government route sectors); FPI — portfolio investment, short-term, volatile (hot money).
  • Forex reserves: Foreign currency assets, gold, SDR, IMF reserve tranche; managed by RBI; import cover ~9–10 months.
  • Exchange rate: Managed float (RBI intervenes to curb volatility); real effective exchange rate (REER); rupee depreciation/appreciation effects on trade and inflation.
  • Trade agreements: FTAs (ASEAN, Japan, South Korea, UAE, Australia — ECTA/ECTA), bilateral investment treaties, RCEP (India opted out, 2019), WTO.

Inclusive Growth and Employment

  • Employment: PLFS (Periodic Labour Force Survey) — unemployment rate, labour force participation rate (LFPR), worker-population ratio; informal sector ~90%; gig/platform workers; Code on Wages 2019, occupational safety codes.
  • Poverty: NITI Aayog MPI, Tendulkar/Rangarajan lines; schemes — MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJAY, PMUY, PMAY, NSAP.
  • MSMEs: Definition (investment and turnover criteria, revised 2020), Udyam registration, Credit Guarantee Fund (CGTMSE), PMEGP, SFURTI, CHAMPIONS portal; contribution to GDP (~30%), exports (~45%), employment (~11 crore).
  • Startups: Startup India (2016), Fund of Funds (SIDBI), tax holidays, DPIIT recognition, unicorns; India is 3rd largest startup ecosystem.
  • Ease of Doing Business: India improved ranking (World Bank, discontinued 2021); reforms — single window, labour codes, GST, insolvency.

Investment Models

  • PPP (Public-Private Partnership): BOT (build-operate-transfer), BOOT, BOO, BLT, O&M; viability gap funding (VGF), Indian Infrastructure Finance Company (IIFCL), National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF).
  • Disinvestment/Privatisation: Strategic disinvestment (Air India, BPCL, Shipping Corporation), CPSE (central public sector enterprises), NIP (National Infrastructure Pipeline, ₹111 lakh crore).
Technique: Fiscal deficit framing
Question: “Fiscal deficit targets under FRBM are often breached — is the Act ineffective?”
Answer: FRBM Act 2003 mandates fiscal consolidation but escape clauses allow deviation (national security, calamity, structural reforms). Deviations occurred post-2008 crisis and during COVID (fiscal deficit ~9–10% in FY21). The Act institutionalised transparency (medium-term fiscal policy statement) but lacks enforcement teeth — targets are aspirational, and off-budget borrowings (FCI, NSSF) mask true deficit.
Technique: Monetary policy transmission
Question: “Repo rate cuts by RBI do not always reach borrowers.” Explain.
Answer: Transmission depends on banks' cost of funds, deposit rates, liquidity (surplus/deficit), NPA burden, and credit demand. If banks have excess liquidity or high NPAs, they may not pass on rate cuts. MPC's external benchmark linkage (since Oct 2019 — loans linked to repo/T-bill) improved transmission for new floating-rate loans.

Common Traps

  • Confusing fiscal deficit (total borrowing) with revenue deficit (borrowing for consumption).
  • Assuming FDI and FPI are similar — FDI is stable and long-term; FPI is volatile.
  • Missing that GST is a destination-based consumption tax, not origin-based.
  • Thinking RBI directly controls inflation — it influences via policy rates; supply-side inflation (food, oil) is harder to control.
  • Overlooking that NPA recognition and resolution (IBC) are different steps.

Memory Tricks

  • Deficits: Revenue < Fiscal < Primary (narrowest to widest concept).
  • RBI rates: Repo = RBI lends; Reverse repo = RBI borrows; MSF = emergency.
  • GST: One nation, one tax, one market.
  • External: Current account = trade + transfers; Capital account = investments + loans.
Mains exam-day strategy
For economy questions, always cite recent data (Budget, Economic Survey, RBI bulletin, PLFS, CPI/WPI). Use correct terminology (GVA, fiscal deficit, repo, CRR, FDI/FPI distinction). For policy questions, discuss objectives, achievements, gaps, and reforms. Conclude with a way forward — fiscal consolidation, financial inclusion, MSME support, infrastructure investment.

Structural Transformation: The Shift from Agriculture to Services

India's economic history is defined by the structural transformation of its workforce and output. Historically, the economy was agrarian, with a majority of the population dependent on agriculture. However, a distinct trend has emerged where the services sector has become the primary driver of GDP growth, while the manufacturing sector has grown at a slower pace relative to its potential. This phenomenon is often termed "premature de-industrialisation," where a country transitions to a service-led economy without passing through the heavy industrialisation phase typical of developed nations.

The structural shift is measured by the Kuznets Curve, which posits that as an economy develops, the share of agriculture in GDP and employment declines, while industry and services rise. In India, the agricultural share in GDP fell significantly from the 1970s to recent years, yet its share in total employment remains high relative to this decline. This divergence indicates a low-productivity trap where millions remain in subsistence farming despite productivity gains in other sectors. The services sector now contributes nearly half of GDP and employs a substantial portion of the workforce, dominated by IT-enabled services (ITES), telecommunications, finance, and retail.

The drivers of this shift include liberalisation reforms, globalisation, urbanisation, and a skilled English-speaking workforce. The rise of the IT sector in cities like Bangalore, Hyderabad, and Pune created a new engine for growth. However, this has led to regional imbalances, with growth concentrated in southern and western India, leaving eastern and central states behind. Furthermore, the service-led model relies heavily on consumption rather than investment, making the economy vulnerable to global shocks.

In contrast to China's strategy of building a robust manufacturing base before expanding services, India's path has been unique. The lack of large-scale infrastructure in the 1990s and rigid labour laws hindered manufacturing growth. While initiatives like "Make in India" aim to correct this, structural inertia remains significant. The economy is also characterised by a dualistic structure: a high-productivity formal sector (services) coexisting with a low-productivity informal sector (agriculture, small trade). This duality limits overall productivity growth and wage convergence.

The future trajectory depends on whether India can successfully industrialise or if it remains trapped in a service-led equilibrium. Policy incentives aim to encourage domestic manufacturing of electronics, pharmaceuticals, and automobiles. However, success requires not just policy incentives but also massive infrastructure investment, skill development, and labour law reforms to facilitate the movement of workers from low-productivity agriculture to high-productivity industry.

The Fiscal Federalism Architecture: Finance Commission and GST Council

Fiscal federalism in India is the mechanism by which financial resources are distributed between the Centre and States. It operates on two main pillars: the Finance Commission for grants-in-aid and tax devolution, and the GST Council for indirect tax policy. The 11th Finance Commission introduced a formula-based approach to distribute taxes, reducing political bargaining. Subsequent commissions have tweaked the weights of population, area, forest cover, and fiscal performance.

The Finance Commission is constituted under Article 280 of the Constitution. It recommends the distribution of tax revenues between the Union and States (vertical devolution) and the horizontal distribution among States. The key parameters include:

  • Population: Adjusted for demographic dividend.
  • Area: To account for geographical disadvantages.
  • Forest Cover: To incentivise conservation efforts.
  • Fiscal Performance: Based on tax effort and revenue mobilisation.

The GST Council, established under Article 279A, is a constitutional body chaired by the Union Finance Minister. It decides on GST rates, thresholds, and exemptions. The decision-making process follows a "dual majority" rule: at least two-thirds of the weighted votes must be in favour, and these votes must include at least one-third of the representatives from the States. This ensures that while the Centre holds significant weight (due to its larger tax base), States have veto power over major policy shifts.

The vertical devolution formula currently gives 42% of the net GST pool to States and Union Territories, with the remaining 58% retained by the Centre. The horizontal distribution among States is based on a formula that includes population (17%), distance from the National Capital Region (NCR) (31%), tax effort (7%), fiscal performance (8%), and special category status (21%). This formula aims to balance equity with incentives for growth.

Fiscal federalism also involves the concept of "grants-in-aid" under Article 275. These are non-repayable transfers from the Centre to States, often used for specific purposes like education or health. The Twelfth Finance Commission recommended a shift towards performance-based grants, but political considerations often override technical recommendations. The tension between fiscal autonomy and fiscal discipline is central to Indian federalism; States often demand more resources while resisting strict deficit limits imposed by the Centre.

Examiner's trap
Question: "Does the GST Council have equal voting power for the Centre and States?" Answer: No. The Centre has 1/3rd of the total votes, while States collectively hold 2/3rd. However, the decision rule requires a supermajority (2/3rd) including at least one-third from States. This means the Centre cannot pass a decision without State consent, but States cannot block a decision unilaterally if the Centre pushes hard enough. A common error is assuming simple majority rules apply.
Exam-day strategy
When answering questions on fiscal federalism, always mention the specific Articles (280 for FC, 279A for GST Council) and the "dual majority" rule. Avoid generic statements about "cooperation"; instead, cite the exact percentage of tax devolution (42%) and the weightage given to forest cover in the FC formula. This specificity distinguishes a high-scoring answer from a generic one.

The Banking Sector: From Nationalisation to Digitalisation

The Indian banking sector has undergone a radical transformation since independence. Initially dominated by private banks, the nationalisation of 14 major banks aimed at financial inclusion and directed credit to priority sectors like agriculture and small industries. This era saw the emergence of Public Sector Banks (PSBs) as the backbone of the economy. However, PSBs accumulated massive Non-Performing Assets (NPAs), leading to a crisis in the early 2000s.

The response was the Asset Quality Review (AQR) and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC). The IBC introduced a time-bound process for resolving corporate defaults, shifting power from creditors to debtors initially, but later refined to balance both interests. The sector has also seen the rise of Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) and Small Finance Banks (SFBs) to serve underserved segments.

Digitalisation has been the most recent revolution. The introduction of UPI (Unified Payments Interface) by NPCI has disrupted global payment systems, enabling instant, zero-cost transactions. This has reduced the need for physical branches and expanded banking to remote areas via Business Correspondents (BCs).
