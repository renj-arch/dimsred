1. Economic Growth and Development

India is the world's fifth-largest economy by nominal GDP (≈$3.7 trillion, IMF World Economic Outlook April 2024) and third-largest by purchasing power parity (≈$13 trillion). For GS III, "economic growth and development" encompasses measurement issues, structural transformation, planning architecture, resource mobilisation, and the distinction between growth (quantitative increase in output) and development (qualitative improvement in well-being). This section builds the conceptual foundation for every subsequent topic.

1.1 Measuring Growth: GDP, GVA, NDP, and the National Accounts Framework

The System of National Accounts (SNA 2008), adopted by India with base year 2011–12 (revised to 2017–18 in 2019), provides the accounting framework. Key aggregates:

  • Gross Domestic Product (GDP): Market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory in a period. GDP = GVA + Net Product Taxes (product taxes − product subsidies). (Source: MoSPI, National Accounts Statistics, 2017–18 series)
  • Gross Value Added (GVA): Value of output minus intermediate consumption. GVA by sector (agriculture, industry, services) reveals structural shifts better than GDP because it excludes the distorting effect of net indirect taxes. (Source: MoSPI, National Accounts Statistics, 2017–18 series)
  • Net Domestic Product (NDP): GDP minus consumption of fixed capital (depreciation). NDP better captures sustainable income because it accounts for capital stock maintenance. (Source: MoSPI, National Accounts Statistics)
  • Gross National Income (GNI): GDP + net primary income from abroad (compensation of employees, investment income). For India, GNI is marginally lower than GDP due to net outflow of investment income. (Source: RBI Handbook of Statistics on Indian Economy, 2023)
Solved Mini-Example: GDP vs GVA Gap
In FY23, India's nominal GDP was ₹272.4 lakh crore and GVA was ₹247.4 lakh crore. The difference (₹25 lakh crore) equals net product taxes. This gap widened post-GST because GST subsumed multiple indirect taxes, making the tax component more transparent. If net product taxes are negative (subsidies > taxes), GDP < GVA — as seen during COVID when fertiliser/food subsidies surged.
Answer: The GDP–GVA gap = net indirect taxes. A widening gap signals higher net tax buoyancy; a negative gap signals subsidy dominance. In answers, always specify which aggregate you are using and why.

1.2 Growth vs Development: Conceptual Distinction and Measurement

Economic growth is the rate of increase in real GDP (or GVA) — a quantitative, unidimensional metric. Economic development is multidimensional: growth plus structural, institutional, and human-capability improvements. Amartya Sen's capability approach (Development as Freedom, 1999) frames development as expansion of substantive freedoms — health, education, political participation, dignity. (Source: Sen, A., Development as Freedom, 1999)

Key development indices used in UPSC answers:

  • Human Development Index (HDI): UNDP, geometric mean of three normalised indices — life expectancy at birth (health), mean and expected years of schooling (education), GNI per capita PPP (standard of living). India's HDI 2022: 0.644 (rank 134/193); medium human development category. (Source: UNDP Human Development Report 2023–24)
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI): Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) + UNDP. India's national MPI (NITI Aayog, 2023): 14.96% population multidimensionally poor (down from 24.85% in 2015–16). Dimensions: health (nutrition, child mortality), education (years of schooling, school attendance), standard of living (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets, bank account). (Source: NITI Aayog, National Multidimensional Poverty Index: A Progress Review 2023)
  • SDG India Index (NITI Aayog): Composite score across 16 SDGs. India's overall score 2023–24: 71 (up from 66 in 2020–21). Top states: Kerala, Himachal Pradesh, Tamil Nadu. (Source: NITI Aayog, SDG India Index 2023–24)
  • Gender Development Index (GDI) / Gender Inequality Index (GII): India GII 2022: 0.490 (rank 108/166). (Source: UNDP HDR 2023–24)
Exam Trap: Conflating Growth with Development
A question asking "Has high GDP growth translated into human development?" requires you to discuss the linkage, not just list HDI numbers. Use the "growth-mediated" vs "support-led" pathways (Dreze & Sen, 2013): East Asia used growth-mediated (growth → jobs → public revenue → social spending); Kerala/Sri Lanka used support-led (direct public provisioning of health/education at low income levels). India's trajectory is mixed — growth has financed MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJAY, but delivery gaps persist. (Source: Drèze & Sen, An Uncertain Glory, 2013)

1.3 Structural Transformation: Sectoral Composition and Employment

A developing economy typically transitions: agriculture → industry → services. India's "premature deindustrialisation" (Rodrik, 2016) — services share rose before industry peaked — is a central GS III theme. (Source: Rodrik, D., Premature Deindustrialization, J. Econ. Growth, 2016)

Employment elasticity (percentage change in employment per 1% GDP growth) has declined: ~0.39 (1999–2005) → ~0.15 (2011–2018) → near zero/negative in manufacturing (PLFS). This "jobless growth" is attributed to capital intensity, labour law rigidity (pre-2020 codes), skill mismatch, and the missing middle (MSMEs). (Source: PLFS 2022–23; RBI Bulletin, Employment Elasticity in India, 2022)

PYQ Integration: Structural Transformation (GS III 2022)
"The increase in the share of the services sector in India's GDP has not been accompanied by a proportionate increase in employment. Explain."
Model structure: (1) State the fact — services 58% GVA, 29% employment (PLFS 2022–23). (2) Causes: skill-intensive sub-sectors (IT, finance, professional services) dominate GVA; low-end services (retail, transport, domestic work) absorb labour but add little value. (3) Missing manufacturing link — manufacturing employment elasticity near zero; labour-intensive sectors (textiles, leather, toys) shrank. (4) Policy gap — labour codes (2020) not fully implemented; PLI schemes capital-intensive. (5) Way forward — labour-intensive manufacturing (toys, footwear, apparel), gig/platform worker protections (Code on Social Security 2020), skill alignment (NEP 2020). (Source: PYQ 2022 GS III)

1.4 Planning Architecture: From Planning Commission to NITI Aayog

Planning Commission (1950–2014): Established by Cabinet resolution (not Constitution), chaired by PM. Five-Year Plans (FYPs) — 12th Plan (2012–17) was the last. Centralised resource allocation (Gadgil formula for state plan assistance), top-down model. (Source: Planning Commission Resolution, 1950; 12th Five Year Plan document)

NITI Aayog (2015–present): Established by Cabinet resolution (1 Jan 2015). Not a constitutional body. Mandate: cooperative federalism, think tank, monitoring & evaluation, Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP), Strategy for New India @75. No financial allocation powers — replaced plan/non-plan distinction with "scheme" and "non-scheme" expenditure in Union Budget 2017–18. (Source: Cabinet Resolution, 1 Jan 2015; NITI Aayog Annual Reports)

1.5 Mobilisation of Resources: Tax, Non-Tax, Borrowings, and External Flows

The Union government's resource envelope (Budget 2024–25): Gross Tax Revenue ₹38.3 lakh cr, Non-Tax Revenue ₹4.1 lakh cr, Capital Receipts (borrowings) ₹16.8 lakh cr, Disinvestment ₹50,000 cr. (Source: Union Budget 2024–25, Receipt Budget)

  • Direct Taxes (FY24 RE): Corporation Tax ₹9.2 lakh cr, Personal Income Tax ₹9.9 lakh cr. Direct tax/GDP ~6.1% (low vs OECD ~15%). Tax base narrow: ~7.4 cr PAN holders filed returns (AY 2023–24); effective taxpayers far fewer. (Source: Union Budget 2024–25; CBDT Time Series Data)
  • Indirect Taxes (FY24 RE): GST ₹9.7 lakh cr (CGST+IGST settlement), Customs ₹2.3 lakh cr, Excise ₹3.1 lakh cr (mainly petroleum). GST collections stabilised at ₹1.6–1.8 lakh cr/month post-FY22. (Source: Union Budget 2024–25; GST Council Reports)
  • Non-Tax Revenue: Dividends from PSUs/RBI (₹1.5–2 lakh cr), spectrum auctions (irregular), disinvestment (persistent shortfall — FY24 actual ₹10,000 cr vs target ₹51,000 cr). (Source: Union Budget 2024–25; DIPAM Reports)
  • Borrowings: Market loans (dated securities, T-bills), small savings (NSSF), external (World Bank, ADB, JICA, sovereign bonds). Gross market borrowing FY25: ₹14.1 lakh cr. (Source: Union Budget 2024–25; RBI Calendar of Market Borrowings)
  • External Sector Resources: Multilateral (IBRD, IDA, ADB), bilateral (Japan, Germany, France), sovereign green bonds (₹16,000 cr FY23, ₹20,000 cr FY24). (Source: DEA, Status Paper on External Debt, 2024)
Answer-Writing Framework: Resource Mobilisation Question
Question: "India's tax-to-GDP ratio remains low despite formalisation. Analyse constraints and suggest reforms." (GS III 2021)
Structure: (1) Data — tax/GDP ~11% (Centre+states), OECD avg 34%. Direct tax ~6%, indirect ~5%. (2) Constraints: narrow base (agri income exempt, informal sector), high threshold (₹2.5 lakh → ₹3 lakh → ₹7 lakh under new regime), compliance cost, litigation (₹12 lakh cr locked in disputes), GST rate complexity (4+ rates), petroleum/alcohol outside GST. (3) Reforms: rationalise GST to 2–3 rates, bring petroleum/real estate/electricity into GST, faceless assessment + appeals (Vivad se Vishwas), expand TDS/TCS net, property tax reform (15th FC incentive), formalise gig/platform income. (4) Fiscal federalism angle — states' own tax effort stagnant; 16th FC must incentivise effort. (Source: PYQ 2021 GS III; 15th FC Report)

1.6 Inclusive Growth: Concept, Measurement, and Policy Architecture

The Eleventh Plan (2007–12) coined "inclusive growth" as growth that "broadens the flow of benefits to the poor and marginalised." The 12th Plan (2012–17) added "faster, sustainable, and more inclusive growth." NITI Aayog's Strategy for New India @75 defines inclusion across: economic (jobs, income), social (health, education, gender), spatial (aspirational districts, NE, hills), and digital (JAM trinity). (Source: 11th & 12th Five Year Plan documents; NITI Aayog Strategy for New India @75, 2018)

Key inclusion metrics (PLFS 2022–23, NFHS-5, MPI):

  • LFPR (15+ years): 57.9% (male 78.5%, female 37.0%) — female LFPR rising but still low. (Source: PLFS 2022–23)
  • Unemployment rate (usual status): 3.2% overall; youth (15–29) 10.0%; graduate youth 29.1%. (Source: PLFS 2022–23)
  • Informal employment: ~90% (PLFS); no written contract, no social security. (Source: PLFS 2022–23)
  • Multidimensional poverty: 14.96% (NITI MPI 2023); rural 19.28%, urban 5.27%. (Source: NITI Aayog MPI 2023)

2. Fiscal Policy and Budgeting

Fiscal policy — government's taxation, expenditure, and borrowing decisions — is the primary instrument for macroeconomic stabilisation, redistribution, and public goods provision. The Constitutional framework (Articles 110–117, 265–293) and the FRBM Act (2003, amended 2012, 2015, 2018) structure India's fiscal architecture. This section covers the Budget process, deficit concepts, FRBM mechanics, GST, and Centre-state fiscal relations.

2.2 Union Budget: Structure, Presentation, and Documents

The Union Budget (Annual Financial Statement, Art. 112) is presented on 1 February (since FY18; previously last working day of Feb). Railway Budget merged in FY18. Budget documents (14+):

  • Annual Financial Statement (AFS): Receipts & expenditure — Revenue Account (current) + Capital Account (assets/liabilities).
  • Demands for Grants: Ministry-wise voted expenditure.
  • Finance Bill: Tax proposals (Money Bill).
  • Appropriation Bill: Authorises CFI withdrawal.
  • Fiscal Policy Statements (FRBM): Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement, Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement, Macro-Economic Framework Statement.
  • Expenditure Budget (Vol. I & II): Scheme-wise details.
  • Receipt Budget: Tax/non-tax, capital receipts, debt profile.
  • Outcome Budget: Output-outcome framework (since FY18).
  • Key Features of Budget / Budget at a Glance: Summary for public.
Solved Mini-Example: Budget Document Mapping
Question: "Which Budget document contains the Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement mandated under FRBM Act?"
Answer: The Fiscal Policy Statements laid under FRBM Act (Section 3) include: (1) Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement (MTFPS) — 3-year rolling targets for fiscal deficit, revenue deficit, debt/GDP, tax-GDP; (2) Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement — strategic priorities; (3) Macro-Economic Framework Statement — GDP growth, inflation, current account assumptions. These are separate documents tabled with the Budget. (Source: FRBM Act 2003, Sections 3–5; Budget 2024–25 documents)

Exam Trap: Off-Budget Borrowings Mask True Deficit
The headline fiscal deficit excludes borrowings by PSUs/agencies for government expenditure (FCI food subsidy bonds, NSSF loans, NHAI bonds, PFC/REC for power). CAG (2019) estimated off-budget borrowings ~1–1.5% GDP. The 15th FC recommended transparency: "All borrowings, including off-budget, should be consolidated." In answers, mention this — "The effective fiscal deficit, including off-budget borrowings, is estimated at 7–8% of GDP." (Source: CAG Report No. 20 of 2019; 15th FC Report, Vol. I, Ch. 4)

2.4 FRBM Act 2003: Architecture, Amendments, Escape Clauses, and Critique

Original FRBM Act 2003: Mandated — (a) eliminate revenue deficit by FY09, (b) reduce fiscal deficit to 3% GDP by FY09, (c) reduce debt/GDP to 60% (Centre+states) by FY25, (d) no RBI subscription to primary issues after FY06. (Source: FRBM Act 2003, Sections 4–5)

Amendments:

  • 2012: Introduced Effective Revenue Deficit; extended timelines.
  • 2015: NK Singh Committee (2016) recommended — debt anchor (60% general government, 40% Centre, 20% states), fiscal deficit 3% by FY20, escape clause (±0.5% for structural reforms/calamity), independent Fiscal Council. Not fully accepted.
  • 2018: Amended targets — fiscal deficit 3% by FY21 (later relaxed), central government debt 40% GDP by FY25. (Source: FRBM Amendment Act 2018; NK Singh Committee Report, 2017)

Escape Clause (Section 4(2)): Allows deviation up to 0.5% GDP for: (a) national security, (b) war, (c) national calamity, (d) agricultural collapse, (e) structural reforms with unanticipated fiscal implications. Invoked in FY20 (corporate tax cut), FY21–FY23 (COVID), FY24 (revenue shortfall). (Source: FRBM Act 2003, Section 4(2); Budget 2023–24, MTFPS)

PYQ Integration: FRBM Effectiveness (GS III 2020)
"The FRBM Act has been amended multiple times and its targets repeatedly missed. Critically examine."
Model answer: (1) Achievements — institutionalised fiscal transparency (MTFPS, quarterly reviews), debt/GDP declined pre-COVID (Centre 52% → 49%, states 24% → 22%), market discipline. (2) Failures — revenue deficit not eliminated (2.0% GDP FY24), fiscal deficit 3% target missed since FY09 (except FY08 2.7%), escape clause used routinely (COVID, corporate tax cut), no Fiscal Council established. (3) Structural gaps — off-budget borrowings excluded, no enforcement mechanism (no penalty for breach), Centre's cess/surcharge share rising (reduces divisible pool), state FRBMs not aligned. (4) NK Singh reforms pending — debt anchor, independent council, multi-year fiscal framework. (5) Way forward — 16th FC to design credible consolidation path; bring off-budget on-budget; legislative backing for Fiscal Council. (Source: PYQ 2020 GS III; NK Singh Committee Report, 2017; CAG Reports)

2.5 Goods and Services Tax (GST): Constitutional Design, Council, Rates, and Pending Issues

101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016: Inserted Articles 246A (concurrent GST power), 269A (IGST), 279A (GST Council). Launched 1 July 2017. Subsumed ~17 central/state taxes (excise, service tax, VAT, CST, entry tax, luxury tax, etc.). (Source: Constitution (101st Amendment) Act, 2016)

  • Dual GST: CGST (Centre), SGST/UTGST (states/UTs), IGST (inter-state = CGST+SGST). Destination-based consumption tax — revenue accrues to consuming state.
  • GST Council (Art. 279A): Constitutional body; Chair: FM; Members: FMs of all states; Quorum: 50%; Decisions: 3/4 weighted majority (Centre 1/3, states 2/3). Not a "federal" body — Centre has veto. (Source: Article 279A; GST Council Rules)
  • Rate Structure (4+ slabs): 0% (essentials), 5%, 12%, 18%, 28% + cess (sin/luxury). 15th FC: "GST rate structure is complex; rationalisation needed." (Source: GST Council Notifications; 15th FC Report)
  • Compensation Cess: 5-year guarantee (2017–22) for states' revenue loss (14% annual growth base). Extended to March 2026 for loan repayment. (Source: GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017; GST Council 50th meeting)
  • Excluded: Petroleum (crude, petrol, diesel, ATF, natural gas), Alcohol, Electricity, Real Estate (stamp duty + registration). (Source: Constitution 101st Amendment, Art. 246A, 279A)

Answer-Writing Framework: Fiscal Federalism Question
Question: "The increasing share of cesses and surcharges in the Centre's gross tax revenue undermines fiscal federalism. Discuss." (GS III 2019 / 2023)
Structure: (1) Data — cess/surcharge share rose from 10% (FY12) to 20%+ (FY24) of GTR. Major cesses: GST compensation cess, health/education cess (4%), road/infrastructure cess on fuel, agriculture infrastructure cess. (2) Impact — reduces divisible pool; states' share of GTR fell from ~35% to ~33%. Violates spirit of Art. 270 (taxes levied & distributed). (3) Legal — Art. 271 allows surcharge for "purposes of the Centre" but not routine expenditure. SC (T.M. Kanniyan 2002): surcharge must be for specific purpose, not permanent. (4) 15th FC view — "cesses/surcharges should be phased out"; recommended cap at 10% of GTR. (5) Reform — sunset clauses for cesses, merge into GST/base rates, 16th FC to enforce discipline. (6) Counter-argument — Centre needs flexibility for national priorities (defence, infrastructure, pandemic). Balance via time-bound, purpose-specific cesses. (Source: PYQ 2019/2023 GS III; 15th FC Report; SC Judgment T.M. Kanniyan vs Union of India, 2002)

3. Monetary Policy and Banking

3.1 RBI: Evolution, Mandate, and Governance

The Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 established RBI (1 April 1935); nationalised 1949. Preamble (amended 2016): "to operate the currency and credit system of the country to its advantage; to have a modern monetary policy framework to meet the challenge of an increasingly complex economy; to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth." (Source: RBI Act 1934, Preamble as amended 2016)

Functions: Monetary authority (MPC), banker to banks (liquidity window), banker to government (WMA, market borrowing manager), currency issuer (sole right, Art. 246+Entry 36 Union List), regulator/supervisor (banking, NBFCs, payment systems), forex manager (FEMA 1999), financial stability (FSDC), developmental (priority sector, financial inclusion).

3.2 Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) and Inflation Targeting

Finance Act 2016 amended RBI Act (Section 45Z) — statutory MPC, 6 members (Governor, Deputy Governor, one RBI officer, 3 external appointed by Centre). Quorum 4; Governor has casting vote. Minutes published after 14 days. (Source: RBI Act 1934, Sections 45Z–45ZK; MPC Minutes)

Inflation Targeting Framework (2016): CPI (Combined) headline inflation target 4% ± 2% band (2–6%). If breached for 3 consecutive quarters → RBI reports to Centre with reasons, remedial actions, timeline. First breach: Jan–Mar 2022 (CPI >6%); RBI explained supply-side (food, fuel). (Source: Gazette Notification, 2016; MPC Resolution 2022)

3.4 Inflation: Measurement, Drivers, and Policy Response

  • CPI (Combined): Retail inflation; base 2012=100; weights: food & beverages 45.9%, fuel & light 6.8%, core (excl. food/fuel) 47.3%. Headline target. (Source: MoSPI, CPI Technical Manual)
  • WPI: Wholesale inflation; base 2011–12=100; weights: manufactured products 64.2%, primary articles 22.6%, fuel & power 13.2%. Used for GDP deflator, escalation clauses. (Source: MoSPI, WPI Technical Manual)
  • Drivers: Demand-pull (output gap >0), cost-push (wages, input prices, supply shocks), imported (exchange rate, global commodity prices), inflation expectations (RBI survey of households/professional forecasters).
  • Supply-side inflation: Food (vegetables, cereals, proteins) — MPC cannot control; requires supply management (buffer stocks, trade policy, APMC reform). (Source: RBI Bulletin, State of the Economy, 2023–24)
PYQ Integration: Monetary Transmission (GS III 2021)
"Examine the reasons for weak monetary policy transmission in India. What measures have been taken to improve it?"
Structure: (1) Transmission = change in policy rate → change in bank lending/deposit rates → credit/GDP. India's transmission lag: 6–12 months; pass-through ~50–60% (RBI study 2020). (2) Reasons: high SLR/CRR (pre-empt resources), administered rates (small savings, PPF), high NPA burden (risk aversion), surplus liquidity (banks park in reverse repo/SDF), lack of external benchmark (pre-2019). (3) Measures: external benchmark linking (Oct 2019 — repo/T-bill), reduction in SLR (21.5%→18%), VRRR/VROR for liquidity management, MCLR→EBR shift, prompt corrective action (PCA) exit for cleaner banks. (4) Residual gaps — small savings rates still high, deposit stickiness, credit demand weak. (Source: PYQ 2021 GS III; RBI Study on Monetary Transmission, 2020; RBI Circular DBR.No.BP.BC.1/21.04.048/2019-20)

Common Traps

  • Confusing fiscal deficit (total borrowing) with revenue deficit (borrowing for consumption).
  • Assuming FDI and FPI are similar — FDI is stable and long-term; FPI is volatile.
  • Missing that GST is a destination-based consumption tax, not origin-based.
  • Thinking RBI directly controls inflation — it influences via policy rates; supply-side inflation (food, oil) is harder to control.
  • Overlooking that NPA recognition and resolution (IBC) are different steps.
  • Confusing primary deficit (excl. interest) with fiscal deficit — primary surplus possible even with fiscal deficit.
  • Assuming cess/surcharge are shareable with states — they are not (Art. 271).
  • Mixing up WPI (wholesale, producer prices) and CPI (retail, consumer prices) — different baskets, weights, uses.
  • Thinking "inclusive growth" = only poverty schemes — it includes spatial, digital, gender, and social inclusion.
  • Assuming FRBM targets are legally enforceable — no penalty for breach; escape clause is wide.

Memory Tricks

  • Deficits: Revenue < Fiscal < Primary (narrowest to widest concept).
  • RBI Rates: Repo = RBI lends; Reverse repo/SDF = RBI borrows; MSF = emergency.
  • GST: One nation, one tax, one market (destination-based).
  • External: Current account = trade + transfers; Capital account = investments + loans.
  • FRBM Escape: National security, war, calamity, farm collapse, structural reform — 0.5% GDP.
  • 15th FC Horizontal: Income 45, Population 15, Area 15, Forest 10, Demo 12.5, Tax effort 2.5.
  • PPP Models: BOT (toll roads), HAM (annuity), O&M (brownfield), VGF (gap funding).
  • NPA: 90-day overdue → Sub-standard (12m) → Doubtful → Loss.

PYQ Integration: IBC Effectiveness (GS III 2020 / 2023)
"The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code has changed the debtor-creditor relationship but resolution remains slow. Critically examine."
Structure: (1) Behavioural shift — debtors now repay pre-default (fear of losing control); promoters bid for own assets (Essar Steel, Bhushan Power) — "creditor in control" culture established. (2) Quantitative outcomes — 900+ resolutions, ₹3.2 lakh cr realisable value; but 32% recovery, 650 days, 45% liquidation. (3) Structural bottlenecks — (a) NCLT capacity (judicial vacancies, adjournments); (b) Litigation culture — CoC decisions challenged at NCLAT/SC (Essar Steel 2019: SC upheld CoC commercial wisdom); (c) Valuation disputes — two valuers, fair vs liquidation value; (d) Operational creditor marginalisation (secured financial creditors dominate CoC). (4) Reforms needed — (a) NCLT: more benches, specialised judges, strict timelines; (b) Pre-packs for all (not just MSMEs); (c) Group insolvency framework; (d) Cross-border protocol; (e) Secondary market for distressed debt (ARCs, AIFs) — reduce NCLT load. (5) Conclusion — IBC is a necessary but incomplete reform; the "resolution" pillar works; "recovery" and "speed" pillars need institutional investment. (Source: PYQ 2020/2023 GS III; IBBI Data; SC Judgment Essar Steel 2019; Parliamentary Committee 2023)

Answer-Writing Framework: Monetary Policy (GS III 2021/2023)
Question: "Inflation targeting has reduced inflation volatility but sacrificed growth. Evaluate."
Structure: (1) Achievements — CPI volatility ↓ (std dev 2.1% pre-FIT → 1.4% post-FIT); anchored expectations (RBI survey: 1Y ahead ~5%, 5Y ahead ~4.5%); credibility gains (foreign investor confidence). (2) Costs — (a) Supply-side blindness: MPC cannot control food (45.9% weight) / fuel; 2019–20 onion, 2022–24 cereals/vegetables — rate hikes hurt growth without fixing supply. (b) Asymmetric response: quick to hike, slow to cut (real rate >0 even when growth slows). (c) Fiscal-monetary coordination absent — FRBM escape clause used, but no formal coordination mechanism. (4) Conclusion — FIT improved credibility; next stage needs flexibility (growth weighting) and coordination (fiscal + supply). PYQ Integration: FDI vs FPI (GS III 2019 / 2022)
"Distinguish between FDI and FPI. Why does India prefer FDI? Assess recent FDI policy changes."
Structure: (1) Distinction — FDI: ≥10% equity + control/management influence (IMF/OECD); long-term, technology transfer, job creation. FPI: <10%, no control; portfolio equity/debt; volatile ("hot money"). (2) Why India prefers FDI — (a) Stability: FDI net inflows positive every year since FY12; FPI net negative in FY20, FY22. (b) Spillovers: R&D, skills, supply chains (e.g., Apple vendor ecosystem — Tata, Foxconn, Pegatron). (c) BOP quality: FDI finances CAD without reversal risk. (3) Recent Policy Shifts — (a) PLI-linked FDI: 14 sectors, ₹1.97 lakh cr outlay; conditionality on incremental sales/capex. (b) Single window (NSWS, 2021) — 32 ministries, 14 states integrated. (c) Space sector opened (2024) — 100% auto (components), 74% (launch), 49% (satellites). (d) Defence 74% auto (2021) — but procurement still DPSU-dominated. (4) Gaps — (a) FDI equity inflows flat (~$45–50 bn/yr); "China+1" capture limited (Vietnam, Mexico, Indonesia gaining more in electronics/textiles). (b) Greenfield announcements ≠ actual inflows (lag 2–3 years). (c) Treaty disputes (Vodafone, Cairn, Nissan) — BIT regime (2016 Model BIT) deters. (5) Way forward — BIT renegotiation (MFN clause), faster environmental/labour clearances, state-level competition (Investment Promotion Agencies), skilling for manufacturing jobs. (Source: PYQ 2019/2022 GS III; DPIIT FDI Data; UNCTAD WIR 2024; Model BIT 2016)
