1. Overview of Financial Markets in India

1.1 Evolution of Indian Financial Markets

The Indian financial system has undergone a profound transformation since the economic reforms of 1991, moving from a tightly controlled regime dominated by public sector institutions to a diversified marketplace where private banks, non‑banking financial companies, mutual funds, and foreign investors operate alongside a modern regulatory architecture. The liberalisation of interest rates, the introduction of electronic trading platforms, and the establishment of the Securities and Exchange Board of India as an autonomous regulator were pivotal in creating price discovery mechanisms that reflect genuine supply and demand conditions. This evolution has also been shaped by successive committees such as the Narasimham Committee on banking sector reforms and the Khan Committee on capital market development, which recommended structural changes that were later enacted through legislation.

As a consequence of these reforms, the depth and breadth of Indian markets have expanded dramatically. The equity market capitalisation rose from a modest fraction of GDP in the early 1990s to well over one hundred percent of GDP by the early 2020s, while the corporate bond market, though still smaller than equity, has grown steadily due to policy initiatives like the introduction of the corporate bond repo and the development of a unified settlement cycle. The integration of domestic markets with global capital flows has increased both the opportunities for financing and the exposure to external shocks, making the regulatory framework a critical determinant of macro‑financial stability.

1.2 Structure of the Financial System

The Indian financial system can be visualised as a layered structure in which the Reserve Bank of India sits at the apex as the monetary authority and regulator of the banking sector, while SEBI oversees the securities market, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India regulates insurance, and the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority supervises pension funds. Commercial banks, both public and private, remain the primary intermediaries for household savings, but their share of total financial assets has been declining as capital markets and non‑bank intermediaries gain prominence. Non‑banking financial companies, housing finance companies, microfinance institutions, and asset management companies now channel a significant portion of credit to sectors that are underserved by traditional banks, such as small enterprises, rural households, and infrastructure projects.

Market infrastructure institutions — stock exchanges, clearing corporations, depositories, and payment systems — provide the plumbing that enables seamless transfer of ownership and risk. The National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange dominate equity trading, while the National Securities Depository Limited and Central Depository Services Limited hold securities in dematerialised form, eliminating the risks associated with physical certificates. The introduction of the T+1 settlement cycle in 2023 further reduced counterparty risk and improved liquidity, aligning Indian practice with international standards.

1.3 Role of Financial Intermediaries

Financial intermediaries perform the essential functions of maturity transformation, risk pooling, and information production, thereby reducing transaction costs for savers and borrowers. Banks achieve maturity transformation by accepting short‑term deposits and extending long‑term loans, while mutual funds pool resources from a large number of investors to create diversified portfolios that individual investors could not replicate on their own. Non‑banking financial companies often specialise in niche segments such as vehicle finance, gold loans, or factoring, leveraging their operational flexibility and lighter regulatory burden relative to banks. However, the rapid growth of NBFCs has also raised concerns about systemic risk, especially when they rely heavily on short‑term wholesale funding from banks and mutual funds, creating interlinkages that can transmit stress across the financial system.

Regulatory oversight of these intermediaries is therefore calibrated to balance innovation with stability. The RBI’s scale‑based regulation for NBFCs, introduced in 2022, classifies them into base, middle, upper, and top layers based on asset size and systemic importance, prescribing proportionate capital, governance, and disclosure requirements. SEBI’s regulations for alternative investment funds, portfolio managers, and investment advisers aim to protect investors while allowing sophisticated products to develop. The coordination between regulators through the Financial Stability and Development Council ensures that regulatory arbitrage is minimised and that macro‑prudential policies are coherent.

2. Primary and Secondary Markets

2.1 Primary Market Mechanisms

The primary market is the arena where new securities are issued and sold for the first time, enabling issuers — governments, public sector undertakings, and private corporations — to raise fresh capital. In India, the main methods of primary issuance include public offers through a prospectus, rights issues to existing shareholders, preferential allotments to select investors, and qualified institutional placements that allow listed companies to place equity or convertible securities with qualified institutional buyers without a full public offer. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018, prescribe detailed eligibility criteria, pricing mechanisms, and disclosure norms to ensure that investors receive adequate information for informed decision‑making.

Book‑building, introduced in the late 1990s, has become the dominant price discovery mechanism for public offers. Under this process, the issuer and the lead merchant banker invite bids from institutional and non‑institutional investors within a price band, and the final issue price is determined based on the demand schedule. This method aligns the issue price more closely with market valuation than the fixed‑price method, reducing the incidence of under‑pricing and the associated wealth transfer from the issuer to early subscribers. The primary market also includes the issuance of government securities through auctions conducted by the Reserve Bank of India, where competitive and non‑competitive bidding determines the cut‑off yield.

2.2 Secondary Market Operations

Once securities are allotted in the primary market, they trade in the secondary market, where existing holders buy and sell among themselves without any fresh capital flowing to the issuer. The secondary market provides liquidity, price discovery, and a benchmark for valuing primary issuances. In India, the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange operate electronic order‑driven matching engines that support both cash and derivative segments. The introduction of the T+1 settlement cycle has shortened the time between trade execution and final settlement, reducing settlement risk and freeing up capital for market participants.

Market microstructure features such as market makers, high‑frequency traders, and algorithmic trading contribute to depth and narrow bid‑ask spreads, but they also raise concerns about fairness and stability. SEBI has responded with regulations on algorithmic trading, co‑location facilities, and the introduction of a market‑wide circuit breaker mechanism that halts trading when index movements exceed predefined thresholds. The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956, and the SEBI Act, 1992, provide the legal backbone for exchange governance, member registration, and investor protection, while the Investor Protection Fund administered by each exchange compensates investors in cases of broker default.

2.3 Market Infrastructure and Clearing

Robust market infrastructure is essential for the smooth functioning of both primary and secondary markets. The clearing corporations — National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited for the NSE and Indian Clearing Corporation Limited for the BSE — act as central counterparties, novating trades and guaranteeing settlement even if one party defaults. They manage margin requirements, monitor position limits, and operate a settlement guarantee fund that absorbs losses beyond individual margins. The depositories, NSDL and CDSL, hold securities in electronic form, enabling instantaneous transfer of ownership through book‑entry, which eliminates the risks of forged certificates and delayed delivery.

The integration of payment systems with securities settlement has been achieved through the Real Time Gross Settlement system and the Unified Payments Interface, allowing funds to move in real time and supporting the T+1 cycle. The introduction of the Legal Entity Identifier for all market participants enhances transparency and facilitates regulatory reporting. SEBI’s recent mandate for a common application form for all market intermediaries and the adoption of a unified KYC framework across the financial sector aim to reduce compliance duplication and improve the investor onboarding experience.

Worked Example: Impact of Book‑Building on Issue Pricing
A company plans to issue 10 million equity shares through a book‑built public offer. The price band is set at ₹150–₹180 per share. During the bidding period, qualified institutional buyers submit bids for 8 million shares at ₹175, high net‑worth individuals bid for 1.5 million shares at ₹170, and retail investors bid for 0.5 million shares at ₹160. The issue is oversubscribed. Determine the likely issue price and the amount of capital raised.
In a book‑building process, the issue price is typically set at the price at which the cumulative demand meets the issue size, often the cut‑off price where the issue is fully subscribed. The total demand at ₹175 is 8 million shares, at ₹170 it adds 1.5 million (total 9.5 million), and at ₹160 it adds 0.5 million (total 10 million). Since the issue size is 10 million shares, the cut‑off price is ₹160, the lowest price at which the issue is fully subscribed. However, SEBI guidelines allow the issuer to price at any price within the band that clears the issue; market practice often selects the highest price that still clears the issue, which would be ₹170 (since at ₹170 demand is 9.5 million, short of 10 million). To fully clear, the price must be ₹160. Assuming the issuer chooses the cut‑off price of ₹160, the capital raised equals 10 million × ₹160 = ₹1,600 million. If the issuer opts for a price of ₹170 and allocates proportionately, the capital raised would be 10 million × ₹170 = ₹1,700 million, but the issue would be under‑subscribed at that price. Therefore, the realistic issue price is ₹160, raising ₹1,600 million.
Exam Insight
When a question asks about the difference between primary and secondary markets, focus on the direction of capital flow and the role of the issuer. The primary market creates new financial claims; the secondary market merely transfers existing claims. Mention the regulatory statutes — SEBI (ICDR) Regulations for primary issues and the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act for secondary trading — to demonstrate statutory awareness.

3. SEBI: Mandate, Powers and Regulatory Framework

3.1 Statutory Mandate and Objectives

The Securities and Exchange Board of India was established in 1988 as a non‑statutory body and acquired statutory powers through the SEBI Act, 1992. Its preamble sets out three core objectives: protecting the interests of investors in securities, promoting the development of the securities market, and regulating the securities market. These objectives are not merely aspirational; they are enforceable through a comprehensive set of regulations that cover issuers, intermediaries, market infrastructure institutions, and collective investment schemes. The Act empowers SEBI to frame regulations, issue guidelines, conduct inspections, adjudicate violations, and impose penalties, including monetary fines, suspension of registration, and even criminal prosecution in coordination with law‑enforcement agencies.

SEBI’s mandate extends beyond traditional stock exchange regulation to encompass mutual funds, portfolio managers, investment advisers, credit rating agencies, custodians, and alternative investment funds. The regulator follows a principles‑based approach supplemented by detailed operational guidelines, allowing it to adapt to financial innovation while maintaining a consistent protective framework. The Securities Appellate Tribunal, established under the SEBI Act, provides an independent quasi‑judicial forum for appeals against SEBI orders, ensuring that regulatory actions are subject to judicial review.

3.2 Enforcement Powers and Adjudication

SEBI’s enforcement toolkit includes the power to summon persons, seize documents, conduct search and seizure operations, and freeze assets. The adjudication process is initiated by a designated adjudicating officer who can impose penalties up to ₹25 crore or three times the amount of gains made from the violation, whichever is higher. For serious offences such as insider trading, fraudulent and unfair trade practices, or market manipulation, SEBI can also refer cases for criminal prosecution under the SEBI Act and the Indian Penal Code. The regulator’s surveillance systems — including the Market Surveillance System, the Integrated Market Surveillance System, and the Corporate Governance Monitoring System — generate alerts on abnormal trading patterns, enabling timely intervention.

Recent high‑profile enforcement actions illustrate the breadth of SEBI’s reach. Cases involving algorithmic trading violations, front‑running by broker‑dealers, and mis‑selling of mutual fund schemes have resulted in substantial penalties and disgorgement orders. The introduction of the “Whistleblower” framework and the “Informant” reward scheme encourages market participants to report violations, enhancing the detection capability of the regulator. SEBI also collaborates with the Reserve Bank of India, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, and the Enforcement Directorate to address cross‑border financial crimes and money‑laundering risks.

3.3 Investor Protection and Market Development

Investor protection is operationalised through mandatory disclosure requirements, a robust grievance redressal mechanism, and the Investor Protection Fund maintained by each stock exchange. The SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015, mandate continuous disclosure of material events, financial results, shareholding patterns, and corporate governance reports, ensuring that investors have timely access to price‑sensitive information. The SCORES platform provides a centralized online complaint redressal system, allowing investors to track the status of their grievances against listed companies and intermediaries.

On the development side, SEBI has introduced measures to deepen the market, such as the framework for Real Estate Investment Trusts and Infrastructure Investment Trusts, the introduction of a regulatory sandbox for fintech innovations, and the promotion of a corporate bond repo market. The regulator’s emphasis on environmental, social, and governance disclosures — through the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report framework — aligns Indian markets with global sustainable finance trends. These initiatives collectively aim to broaden the investor base, improve market liquidity, and enhance the resilience of the financial system.

3.4 Recent Regulatory Initiatives

In the past few years, SEBI has undertaken several structural reforms. The shift to a T+1 settlement cycle for all equity trades, effective from January 2023, reduced settlement risk and capital requirements for market participants. The introduction of the “Unified Payments Interface” based block mechanism for application supported by blocked amount (ASBA) in public issues streamlined the subscription process and eliminated the need for physical cheques. SEBI also revised the framework for mutual fund categorisation and rationalised the total expense ratio limits, enhancing transparency for retail investors.

Another significant initiative is the “Framework for Issue of Equity Shares with Differential Voting Rights”, which allows promoters to retain control while raising equity capital, subject to stringent safeguards. The regulator has also tightened the norms for related party transactions, requiring prior approval of the audit committee and, in certain cases, shareholder approval, to prevent misuse of corporate resources. These reforms reflect SEBI’s proactive stance in balancing market innovation with the imperative of investor confidence and systemic stability.

4. Bond Market and Debt Instruments

4.1 Government Securities and Treasury Bills

Government securities (G‑secs) constitute the backbone of the Indian debt market, providing a risk‑free benchmark for pricing all other fixed‑income instruments. The Reserve Bank of India conducts primary auctions of dated securities and treasury bills on behalf of the central government, while state governments issue State Development Loans through a similar auction process. Treasury bills, with maturities of 91, 182, and 364 days, are issued at a discount and redeemed at par, serving as the primary instrument for short‑term liquidity management by banks and mutual funds. The yields on G‑secs influence the cost of borrowing across the economy, as they anchor the risk‑free rate used in valuation models for corporate bonds, loans, and derivatives.

The secondary market for G‑secs operates on the Negotiated Dealing System‑Order Matching platform and the Clearing Corporation of India Limited, which provides central counterparty clearing and settlement on a T+1 basis. The introduction of the “Uniform Price Auction” for dated securities and the “Multiple Price Auction” for treasury bills has improved price discovery and broadened participation. The RBI’s Open Market Operations, including outright purchases, sales, and long‑term repo operations, use G‑secs as the primary tool for liquidity management and monetary policy transmission.

4.2 Corporate Bonds and Debentures

Corporate bonds and non‑convertible debentures enable companies to diversify funding sources beyond bank credit, often at lower cost and longer tenors. The SEBI (Issue and Listing of Non‑Convertible Securities) Regulations, 2021, prescribe eligibility criteria, disclosure norms, and credit rating requirements for public issues and private placements. Credit rating agencies registered with SEBI assign ratings that signal credit quality to investors; a minimum investment‑grade rating is mandatory for public issues, while private placements may accept lower ratings subject to investor sophistication. The corporate bond market has historically been dominated by private placements to institutional investors such as banks, insurance companies, provident funds, and mutual funds, resulting in limited retail participation and secondary market liquidity.

To address liquidity constraints, SEBI and the RBI have introduced several measures: the corporate bond repo facility, the trade reporting platform on stock exchanges, the “Request for Quote” system for anonymous price discovery, and the “Basket of Bonds” exchange‑traded fund that provides retail investors with diversified exposure. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, has improved creditor recovery prospects, thereby enhancing the risk‑return profile of corporate debt. Nevertheless, the market remains shallow compared to equity, with outstanding corporate bonds representing a modest share of GDP, and the yield spread over G‑secs often reflecting liquidity premiums rather than pure credit risk.

4.3 Market Development and Liquidity Measures

Policy initiatives aimed at deepening the bond market include the development of a unified settlement cycle for all debt instruments, the introduction of a centralised trade repository for over‑the‑counter trades, and the promotion of market makers who provide two‑way quotes for selected securities. The RBI’s “On‑Tap” issuance of Floating Rate Savings Bonds and the “Retail Direct” platform allow individual investors to buy G‑secs directly, broadening the investor base. The Securities and Exchange Board of India has also permitted foreign portfolio investors to participate in the corporate bond repo market and to invest in securities issued by infrastructure investment trusts, subject to prescribed limits.

The establishment of the “National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited” and the “India Debt Resolution Company Limited” aims to resolve stressed assets and improve the quality of collateral in the bond market. The “Green Bond” framework, aligned with the International Capital Market Association’s Green Bond Principles, encourages issuance for environmentally sustainable projects, attracting a new class of ESG‑focused investors. These measures collectively seek to create a vibrant, liquid, and resilient bond market that can support long‑term infrastructure financing and reduce the economy’s reliance on bank intermediation.

5. Mutual Funds, Derivatives and Financial Market Reforms

5.1 Mutual Funds and Collective Investment

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it in a diversified portfolio under professional management. In India the industry is governed by the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, under which every fund is set up as a trust with a sponsor, a board of trustees, an asset management company that runs the schemes, and a custodian that holds the securities. Schemes are broadly open-ended or close-ended, and by asset class equity, debt, hybrid, index and exchange-traded funds; equity-linked savings schemes carry a tax benefit. Systematic investment plans have turned the industry's monthly inflows into a stable domestic counterweight to foreign portfolio flows, and the industry's assets under management have grown into the tens of lakh crore. For the examination, the useful points are the four-party structure, the difference between open- and close-ended schemes, the role of the Association of Mutual Funds in India, and the shift from commission-driven distribution to direct plans.

5.2 The Derivatives Market

Derivatives are contracts whose value is derived from an underlying asset. India's exchange-traded derivatives market began with index futures on the NSE in 2000 and expanded into index options, stock futures and options, currency derivatives on recognised exchanges, interest-rate futures and commodity derivatives regulated by SEBI. They allow firms to hedge price, interest-rate and currency risk, and they also attract speculative trading. The exceptional growth of retail index-option trading led SEBI to tighten the framework in 2024, raising contract sizes, limiting weekly expiries to one benchmark index and increasing margin requirements, on the view that a large share of retail traders were losing money and that the volumes were diverting savings from productive investment.

5.3 Deepening the Market: Reforms and Instruments

A series of reforms has widened and deepened India's financial markets. Dematerialised settlement through NSDL and CDSL, the shortening of the settlement cycle to T+1 in 2023, the demutualisation of stock exchanges and the corporatisation of brokers, the introduction of real estate investment trusts and infrastructure investment trusts, the creation of a social stock exchange for non-profit fund-raising, and measures to develop the corporate bond market are all part of the same project. The Securities and Exchange Board of India, established as a statutory regulator in 1992, is the central institution: it registers and regulates intermediaries, protects investors, prohibits fraudulent and unfair trade practices, and conducts investigations and adjudication. The persistent challenges are the shallowness of the corporate bond market relative to bank credit, the concentration of equity trading in a few exchanges, and the need to balance innovation in products against investor protection.
