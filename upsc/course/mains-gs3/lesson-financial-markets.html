1. Overview of Financial Markets in India

1.1 Evolution of Indian Financial Markets

The Indian financial system has undergone a profound transformation since the economic reforms of 1991, moving from a tightly controlled regime dominated by public sector institutions to a diversified marketplace where private banks, non‑banking financial companies, mutual funds, and foreign investors operate alongside a modern regulatory architecture. The liberalisation of interest rates, the introduction of electronic trading platforms, and the establishment of the Securities and Exchange Board of India as an autonomous regulator were pivotal in creating price discovery mechanisms that reflect genuine supply and demand conditions. This evolution has also been shaped by successive committees such as the Narasimham Committee on banking sector reforms and the Khan Committee on corporate bond market development, which recommended structural changes that were later enacted through legislation.

As a consequence of these reforms, the depth and breadth of Indian markets have expanded dramatically. The equity market capitalisation rose from a modest fraction of GDP in the early 1990s to well over one hundred percent of GDP by the early 2020s, while the corporate bond market, though still smaller than equity, has grown steadily due to policy initiatives like the introduction of the corporate bond repo and the development of a unified settlement cycle. The integration of domestic markets with global capital flows has increased both the opportunities for financing and the exposure to external shocks, making the regulatory framework a critical determinant of macro‑financial stability.

1.2 Structure of the Financial System

The Indian financial system can be visualised as a layered structure in which the Reserve Bank of India sits at the apex as the monetary authority and regulator of the banking sector, while SEBI oversees the securities market, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India regulates insurance, and the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority supervises pension funds. Commercial banks, both public and private, remain the primary intermediaries for household savings, but their share of total financial assets has been declining as capital markets and non‑bank intermediaries gain prominence. Non‑banking financial companies, housing finance companies, microfinance institutions, and asset management companies now channel a significant portion of credit to sectors that are underserved by traditional banks, such as small enterprises, rural households, and infrastructure projects.

Market infrastructure institutions — stock exchanges, clearing corporations, depositories, and payment systems — provide the plumbing that enables seamless transfer of ownership and risk. The National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange dominate equity trading, while the National Securities Depository Limited and Central Depository Services Limited hold securities in dematerialised form, eliminating the risks associated with physical certificates. The introduction of the T+1 settlement cycle in 2023 further reduced counterparty risk and improved liquidity, aligning Indian practice with international standards.

1.3 Role of Financial Intermediaries

Financial intermediaries perform the essential functions of maturity transformation, risk pooling, and information production, thereby reducing transaction costs for savers and borrowers. Banks achieve maturity transformation by accepting short‑term deposits and extending long‑term loans, while mutual funds pool resources from a large number of investors to create diversified portfolios that individual investors could not replicate on their own. Non‑banking financial companies often specialise in niche segments such as vehicle finance, gold loans, or factoring, leveraging their operational flexibility and lighter regulatory burden relative to banks. However, the rapid growth of NBFCs has also raised concerns about systemic risk, especially when they rely heavily on short‑term wholesale funding from banks and mutual funds, creating interlinkages that can transmit stress across the financial system.

Regulatory oversight of these intermediaries is therefore calibrated to balance innovation with stability. The RBI’s scale‑based regulation for NBFCs, introduced in 2021, classifies them into base, middle, upper, and top layers based on asset size and systemic importance, prescribing proportionate capital, governance, and disclosure requirements. SEBI’s regulations for alternative investment funds, portfolio managers, and investment advisers aim to protect investors while allowing sophisticated products to develop. The coordination between regulators through the Financial Stability and Development Council ensures that regulatory arbitrage is minimised and that macro‑prudential policies are coherent.

2. Primary and Secondary Markets

2.1 Primary Market Mechanisms

The primary market is the arena where new securities are issued and sold for the first time, enabling issuers — governments, public sector undertakings, and private corporations — to raise fresh capital. In India, the main methods of primary issuance include public offers through a prospectus, rights issues to existing shareholders, preferential allotments to select investors, and qualified institutional placements that allow listed companies to place equity or convertible securities with qualified institutional buyers without a full public offer. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018, prescribe detailed eligibility criteria, pricing mechanisms, and disclosure norms to ensure that investors receive adequate information for informed decision‑making.

Book‑building, introduced in 1995, has become the dominant price discovery mechanism for public offers. Under this process, the issuer and the lead merchant banker invite bids from institutional and non‑institutional investors within a price band, and the final issue price is determined based on the demand schedule. This method aligns the issue price more closely with market valuation than the fixed‑price method, reducing the incidence of under‑pricing and the associated wealth transfer from the issuer to early subscribers. The primary market also includes the issuance of government securities through auctions conducted by the Reserve Bank of India, where competitive and non‑competitive bidding determines the cut‑off yield.

2.2 Secondary Market Operations

Once securities are allotted in the primary market, they trade in the secondary market, where existing holders buy and sell among themselves without any fresh capital flowing to the issuer. The secondary market provides liquidity, price discovery, and a benchmark for valuing primary issuances. In India, the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange operate electronic order‑driven matching engines that support both cash and derivative segments. The introduction of the T+1 settlement cycle has shortened the time between trade execution and final settlement, reducing settlement risk and freeing up capital for market participants.

Market microstructure features such as market makers, high‑frequency traders, and algorithmic trading contribute to depth and narrow bid‑ask spreads, but they also raise concerns about fairness and stability. SEBI has responded with regulations on algorithmic trading, co‑location facilities, and the introduction of a market‑wide circuit breaker mechanism that halts trading when index movements exceed predefined thresholds. The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956, and the SEBI Act, 1992, provide the legal backbone for exchange governance, member registration, and investor protection, while the Investor Protection Fund administered by each exchange compensates investors in cases of broker default.

2.3 Market Infrastructure and Clearing

Robust market infrastructure is essential for the smooth functioning of both primary and secondary markets. The clearing corporations — National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited for the NSE and Indian Clearing Corporation Limited for the BSE — act as central counterparties, novating trades and guaranteeing settlement even if one party defaults. They manage margin requirements, monitor position limits, and operate a settlement guarantee fund that absorbs losses beyond individual margins. The depositories, NSDL and CDSL, hold securities in electronic form, enabling instantaneous transfer of ownership through book‑entry, which eliminates the risks of forged certificates and delayed delivery.

The integration of payment systems with securities settlement has been achieved through the Real Time Gross Settlement system and the Unified Payments Interface for primary market ASBA; RTGS supports the T+1 cycle. The introduction of the Legal Entity Identifier for all market participants enhances transparency and facilitates regulatory reporting. SEBI’s recent mandate for a common application form for all market intermediaries and the adoption of a unified KYC framework across the financial sector aim to reduce compliance duplication and improve the investor onboarding experience.

Worked Example: Impact of Book‑Building on Issue Pricing
A company plans to issue 10 million equity shares through a book‑built public offer. The price band is set at ₹150–₹180 per share. During the bidding period, qualified institutional buyers submit bids for 8 million shares at ₹175, high net‑worth individuals bid for 1.5 million shares at ₹170, and retail investors bid for 0.5 million shares at ₹160. The issue is oversubscribed. Determine the likely issue price and the amount of capital raised.
In a book‑building process, the issue price is typically set at the price at which the cumulative demand meets the issue size, often the cut‑off price where the issue is fully subscribed. The total demand at ₹175 is 8 million shares, at ₹170 it adds 1.5 million (total 9.5 million), and at ₹160 it adds 0.5 million (total 10 million). Since the issue size is 10 million shares, the cut‑off price is ₹160, the lowest price at which the issue is fully subscribed. However, SEBI guidelines allow the issuer to price at any price within the band that clears the issue; market practice often selects the highest price that still clears the issue, which would be ₹170 (since at ₹170 demand is 9.5 million, short of 10 million). To fully clear, the price must be ₹160. Assuming the issuer chooses the cut‑off price of ₹160, the capital raised equals 10 million × ₹160 = ₹1,600 million. If the issuer opts for a price of ₹170 and allocates proportionately, the capital raised would be 10 million × ₹170 = ₹1,700 million, but the issue would be under‑subscribed at that price. Therefore, the realistic issue price is ₹160, raising ₹1,600 million.
Exam Insight
When a question asks about the difference between primary and secondary markets, focus on the direction of capital flow and the role of the issuer. The primary market creates new financial claims; the secondary market merely transfers existing claims. Mention the regulatory statutes — SEBI (ICDR) Regulations for primary issues and the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act for secondary trading — to demonstrate statutory awareness.

3. SEBI: Mandate, Powers and Regulatory Framework

3.1 Statutory Mandate and Objectives

The Securities and Exchange Board of India was established in 1988 as a non‑statutory body and acquired statutory powers through the SEBI Act, 1992. Its preamble sets out three core objectives: protecting the interests of investors in securities, promoting the development of the securities market, and regulating the securities market. These objectives are not merely aspirational; they are enforceable through a comprehensive set of regulations that cover issuers, intermediaries, market infrastructure institutions, and collective investment schemes. The Act empowers SEBI to frame regulations, issue guidelines, conduct inspections, adjudicate violations, and impose penalties, including monetary fines, suspension of registration, and even criminal prosecution in coordination with law‑enforcement agencies.

SEBI’s mandate extends beyond traditional stock exchange regulation to encompass mutual funds, portfolio managers, investment advisers, credit rating agencies, custodians, and alternative investment funds. The regulator follows a principles‑based approach supplemented by detailed operational guidelines, allowing it to adapt to financial innovation while maintaining a consistent protective framework. The Securities Appellate Tribunal, established under the SEBI Act, provides an independent quasi‑judicial forum for appeals against SEBI orders, ensuring that regulatory actions are subject to judicial review.

3.2 Enforcement Powers and Adjudication

SEBI’s enforcement toolkit includes the power to summon persons, seize documents, conduct search and seizure operations, and freeze assets. The adjudication process is initiated by a designated adjudicating officer who can impose penalties up to ₹25 crore or three times the amount of gains made from the violation, whichever is higher. For serious offences such as insider trading, fraudulent and unfair trade practices, or market manipulation, SEBI can also refer cases for criminal prosecution under the SEBI Act and the Indian Penal Code. The regulator’s surveillance systems — including the Market Surveillance System, the Integrated Market Surveillance System, and the Corporate Governance Monitoring System — generate alerts on abnormal trading patterns, enabling timely intervention.

Recent high‑profile enforcement actions illustrate the breadth of SEBI’s reach. Cases involving algorithmic trading violations, front‑running by broker‑dealers, and mis‑selling of mutual fund schemes have resulted in substantial penalties and disgorgement orders. The introduction of the “Whistleblower” framework and the “Informant” reward scheme encourages market participants to report violations, enhancing the detection capability of the regulator. SEBI also collaborates with the Reserve Bank of India, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, and the Enforcement Directorate to address cross‑border financial crimes and money‑laundering risks.

3.3 Investor Protection and Market Development

Investor protection is operationalised through mandatory disclosure requirements, a robust grievance redressal mechanism, and the Investor Protection Fund maintained by each stock exchange. The SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015, mandate continuous disclosure of material events, financial results, shareholding patterns, and corporate governance reports, ensuring that investors have timely access to price‑sensitive information. The SCORES platform provides a centralized online complaint redressal system, allowing investors to track the status of their grievances against listed companies and intermediaries.

On the development side, SEBI has introduced measures to deepen the market, such as the framework for Real Estate Investment Trusts and Infrastructure Investment Trusts, the introduction of a regulatory sandbox for fintech innovations, and the promotion of a corporate bond repo market. The regulator’s emphasis on environmental, social, and governance disclosures — through the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report framework — aligns Indian markets with global sustainable finance trends. These initiatives collectively aim to broaden the investor base, improve market liquidity, and enhance the resilience of the financial system.

3.4 Recent Regulatory Initiatives

In the past few years, SEBI has undertaken several structural reforms. The shift to a T+1 settlement cycle for all equity trades was completed by January 2024 after a phased rollout beginning in January 2023., reduced settlement risk and capital requirements for market participants. The introduction of the “Unified Payments Interface” based block mechanism for application supported by blocked amount (ASBA) in public issues streamlined the subscription process and eliminated the need for physical cheques. SEBI also revised the framework for mutual fund categorisation and rationalised the total expense ratio limits, enhancing transparency for retail investors.

Another significant initiative is the “Framework for Issue of Equity Shares with Differential Voting Rights”, which allows promoters to retain control while raising equity capital, subject to stringent safeguards. The regulator has also tightened the norms for related party transactions, requiring prior approval of the audit committee and, in certain cases, shareholder approval, to prevent misuse of corporate resources. These reforms reflect SEBI’s proactive stance in balancing market innovation with the imperative of investor confidence and systemic stability.

4. Bond Market and Debt Instruments

4.1 Government Securities and Treasury Bills

Government securities (G‑secs) constitute the backbone of the Indian debt market, providing a risk‑free benchmark for pricing all other fixed‑income instruments. The Reserve Bank of India conducts primary auctions of dated securities and treasury bills on behalf of the central government, while state governments issue State Development Loans through a similar auction process. Treasury bills, with maturities of 91, 182, and 364 days, are issued at a discount and redeemed at par, serving as the primary instrument for short‑term liquidity management by banks and mutual funds. The yields on G‑secs influence the cost of borrowing across the economy, as they anchor the risk‑free rate used in valuation models for corporate bonds, loans, and derivatives.

The secondary market for G‑secs operates on the Negotiated Dealing System‑Order Matching platform and the Clearing Corporation of India Limited, which provides central counterparty clearing and settlement on a T+1 basis. The introduction of the “Uniform Price Auction” for dated securities and the “Multiple Price Auction” for treasury bills has improved price discovery and broadened participation. The RBI’s Open Market Operations, including outright purchases, sales, and long‑term repo operations, use G‑secs as the primary tool for liquidity management and monetary policy transmission.

4.2 Corporate Bonds and Debentures

Corporate bonds and non‑convertible debentures enable companies to diversify funding sources beyond bank credit, often at lower cost and longer tenors. The SEBI (Issue and Listing of Non‑Convertible Securities) Regulations, 2021, prescribe eligibility criteria, disclosure norms, and credit rating requirements for public issues and private placements. Credit rating agencies registered with SEBI assign ratings that signal credit quality to investors; a minimum investment‑grade rating is mandatory for public issues, while private placements may accept lower ratings subject to investor sophistication. The corporate bond market has historically been dominated by private placements to institutional investors such as banks, insurance companies, provident funds, and mutual funds, resulting in limited retail participation and secondary market liquidity.

To address liquidity constraints, SEBI and the RBI have introduced several measures: the corporate bond repo facility, the trade reporting platform on stock exchanges, the “Request for Quote” system for anonymous price discovery, and the “Basket of Bonds” exchange‑traded fund that provides retail investors with diversified exposure. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, has improved creditor recovery prospects, thereby enhancing the risk‑return profile of corporate debt. Nevertheless, the market remains shallow compared to equity, with outstanding corporate bonds representing a modest share of GDP, and the yield spread over G‑secs often reflecting liquidity premiums rather than pure credit risk.

4.3 Market Development and Liquidity Measures

Policy initiatives aimed at deepening the bond market include the development of a unified settlement cycle for all debt instruments, the introduction of a centralised trade repository for over‑the‑counter trades, and the promotion of market makers who provide two‑way quotes for selected securities. The RBI’s “On‑Tap” issuance of Floating Rate Savings Bonds and the “Retail Direct” platform allow individual investors to buy G‑secs directly, broadening the investor base. The Securities and Exchange Board of India has also permitted foreign portfolio investors to participate in the corporate bond repo market and to invest in securities issued by infrastructure investment trusts, subject to prescribed limits.

The establishment of the “National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited” and the “India Debt Resolution Company Limited” aims to resolve stressed assets and improve the quality of collateral in the bond market. The “Green Bond” framework, aligned with the International Capital Market Association’s Green Bond Principles, encourages issuance for environmentally sustainable projects, attracting a new class of ESG‑focused investors. These measures collectively seek to create a vibrant, liquid, and resilient bond market that can support long‑term infrastructure financing and reduce the economy’s reliance on bank intermediation.

5. Mutual Funds, Derivatives and Financial Market Reforms

5.1 Mutual Funds and Collective Investment

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it in a diversified portfolio under professional management. In India the industry is governed by the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, under which every fund is set up as a trust with a sponsor, a board of trustees, an asset management company that runs the schemes, and a custodian that holds the securities. Schemes are broadly open-ended or close-ended, and by asset class equity, debt, hybrid, index and exchange-traded funds; equity-linked savings schemes carry a tax benefit. Systematic investment plans have turned the industry's monthly inflows into a stable domestic counterweight to foreign portfolio flows, and the industry's assets under management have grown into the tens of lakh crore. For the examination, the useful points are the four-party structure, the difference between open- and close-ended schemes, the role of the Association of Mutual Funds in India, and the shift from commission-driven distribution to direct plans.

5.2 The Derivatives Market

Derivatives are contracts whose value is derived from an underlying asset. India's exchange-traded derivatives market began with index futures on the NSE in 2000 and expanded into index options, stock futures and options, currency derivatives on recognised exchanges, interest-rate futures and commodity derivatives regulated by SEBI. They allow firms to hedge price, interest-rate and currency risk, and they also attract speculative trading. The exceptional growth of retail index-option trading led SEBI to tighten the framework in 2024, raising contract sizes, limiting weekly expiries to one benchmark index and increasing margin requirements, on the view that a large share of retail traders were losing money and that the volumes were diverting savings from productive investment.

5.3 Deepening the Market: Reforms and Instruments

A series of reforms has widened and deepened India's financial markets. Dematerialised settlement through NSDL and CDSL, the shortening of the settlement cycle to T+1 in 2023, the demutualisation of stock exchanges and the corporatisation of brokers, the introduction of real estate investment trusts and infrastructure investment trusts, the creation of a social stock exchange for non-profit fund-raising, and measures to develop the corporate bond market are all part of the same project. The Securities and Exchange Board of India, established as a statutory regulator in 1992, is the central institution: it registers and regulates intermediaries, protects investors, prohibits fraudulent and unfair trade practices, and conducts investigations and adjudication. The persistent challenges are the shallowness of the corporate bond market relative to bank credit, the concentration of equity trading in a few exchanges, and the need to balance innovation in products against investor protection.

Non‑Banking Financial Companies: Regulation, Supervision and Systemic Risk

Non‑Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) occupy the space between banks and capital markets, providing credit to segments that are underserved by scheduled commercial banks. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the principal regulator under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 and the NBFC‑SI (Systemically Important) framework introduced in 2014. An NBFC is classified as systemically important when its asset size exceeds ₹500 crore; such entities are subject to capital adequacy norms (minimum 15 % Tier‑I capital), leverage limits (maximum 7 times owned funds), and mandatory credit‑rating of borrowing programmes. The 2019 “Scale Based Regulation” (SBR) further stratified NBFCs into Base, Middle, Upper and Top layers, aligning supervisory intensity with size, interconnectedness and complexity. The Upper and Top layers face enhanced disclosures, stricter liquidity coverage ratios and a requirement to appoint a Chief Risk Officer.

The IL&FS default in 2018 exposed contagion channels: mutual funds, insurance companies and banks held large exposures to commercial papers and non‑convertible debentures issued by the group. In response, the RBI mandated a “Liquidity Risk Management Framework” (2020) requiring NBFCs to maintain a minimum 15 % of net‑demand and time‑liabilities in high‑quality liquid assets, and introduced a “Prompt Corrective Action” (PCA) matrix for NBFCs breaching capital, asset quality or leverage thresholds. The 2022 “Co‑origination” guidelines permit banks and NBFCs to jointly originate priority‑sector loans, sharing risk and expanding credit flow without diluting regulatory oversight. The “Account Aggregator” ecosystem, launched in 2021, enables consent‑based data sharing, improving credit appraisal for NBFCs while preserving data privacy.

Systemic risk monitoring now incorporates the “Interconnectedness Matrix” published in the RBI’s Financial Stability Report, mapping exposures among NBFCs, banks, mutual funds and insurance firms. The “Large Exposure Framework” (2023) caps a single NBFC’s exposure to any counterparty at 20 % of its Tier‑I capital, with an additional 5 % for infrastructure lending. For the examination, candidates should be able to differentiate the regulatory perimeter of NBFCs from that of banks, explain the rationale behind the SBR layers, and articulate how the IL&FS episode reshaped liquidity and PCA norms.

Foreign Capital Flows: FPI, FDI, ECB and the Liberalisation Framework

India’s external financing architecture rests on three pillars: Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI), Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and External Commercial Borrowings (ECB). The FPI regime, governed by the SEBI (Foreign Portfolio Investors) Regulations, 2019, classifies investors into Category I (government and sovereign wealth funds), Category II (broad‑based funds, pension funds) and Category III (all others). Category I investors enjoy higher investment limits in government securities (up to 30 % of outstanding stock) and corporate bonds (up to 15 % of issuance), while Category III faces stricter caps and additional KYC requirements. The “Voluntary Retention Route” (VRR) introduced in 2019 allows FPIs to invest in debt without breaching the 20 % sectoral limit, provided they retain the securities for a minimum of three years.

FDI policy is administered through the Consolidated FDI Policy Circular, updated annually by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT). The automatic route now covers most sectors up to 100 % except strategic areas such as defence (up to 74 % under automatic, beyond with government approval), telecom (100 % with security clearance) and media (26 % under automatic). The “Press Note 3” (2020) mandates prior government approval for investments from countries sharing a land border with India, a measure aimed at curbing opportunistic takeovers during the pandemic. The “Production Linked Incentive” (PLI) schemes, launched from 2020 onward, link fiscal incentives to incremental domestic manufacturing, indirectly steering FDI into targeted sectors such as electronics, pharmaceuticals and advanced chemistry cells.

ECBs are regulated by the RBI under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 and the ECB Framework (2022). The framework distinguishes “Track I” (automatic route, up to USD 750 million per financial year, minimum average maturity three years) and “Track II” (approval route, higher ceilings, longer maturities). End‑use restrictions prohibit deployment for real‑estate, working capital (except for specified sectors), and repayment of rupee loans. The “Liberalised Remittance Scheme” (LRS) permits resident individuals to remit up to USD 250,000 per annum for permissible capital account transactions, including overseas direct investment. For the exam, aspirants must compare the risk‑return profile of FPI (high volatility, short‑term) versus FDI (stable, long‑term), recall the sectoral caps, and explain how the VRR and ECB tracks are used to manage the composition of capital inflows.

Application Question
A foreign sovereign wealth fund wishes to acquire a 12 % stake in an Indian listed infrastructure company through the automatic route. The company operates in the ports sector, which is under the automatic route up to 100 % FDI. However, the fund is based in a country that shares a land border with India. Determine whether the investment can proceed under the automatic route and justify your answer with the relevant regulatory provision.
The investment cannot proceed under the automatic route. Press Note 3 (2020) stipulates that any entity from a country sharing a land border with India requires prior government approval for FDI, irrespective of the sectoral automatic‑route limit. Since the sovereign wealth fund originates from such a country, the transaction must be routed through the government approval channel, even though the ports sector permits 100 % automatic FDI for other investors.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes sectoral FDI caps with Press Note 3, first identify the investor’s geography. If the investor is from a land‑border country, the automatic route is blocked regardless of sectoral limits. Memorise the three FPI categories and the VRR retention period (three years) as they frequently appear in match‑the‑following and statement‑based questions.

Securitisation, Asset Reconstruction and the Insolvency Framework

Securitisation transforms illiquid loan assets into tradable securities, enabling originators — primarily banks and NBFCs — to recycle capital. The Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (SARFAESI) provides the legal backbone: it empowers secured creditors to enforce security interests without court intervention, provided the borrower’s account is classified as a non‑performing asset (NPA) for at least 90 days. The 2016 amendment extended SARFAESI coverage to NBFCs with asset size above ₹100 crore and allowed the creation of “Security Receipts” (SRs) issued by Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) to qualified institutional buyers.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) introduced a time‑bound resolution process (180 days, extendable by 90 days) and established the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) as the adjudicating authority. The “Pre‑packaged Insolvency Resolution Process” (PIRP) for MSMEs, notified in 2021, permits a debtor‑initiated plan with creditor approval before formal admission, reducing litigation costs. The “Cross‑Border Insolvency” framework, aligned with the UNCITRAL Model Law, is under consultation to facilitate coordination with foreign courts.

ARCs, regulated by the RBI under the SARFAESI Act, acquire stressed assets at a discount, issue SRs, and manage resolution. The “National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited” (NARCL) and “India Debt Resolution Company Limited” (IDRCL), set up in 2021, act as a public‑private “bad bank” duo: NARCL acquires large stressed accounts from banks, while IDRCL provides resolution expertise. The RBI’s “Prudential Framework for Resolution of Stressed Assets” (2019) mandates a 30‑day review period for lenders to implement a resolution plan once an account crosses the 90‑day NPA threshold, failing which the account must be referred to IBC. For the examination, candidates should trace the interaction between SARFAESI enforcement, ARC acquisition, and IBC resolution, and be able to explain why the “bad bank” structure was introduced despite existing ARCs.

Financial Market Infrastructure: Clearing Corporations, Settlement Cycles and Risk Management

The post‑trade ecosystem in India rests on two central counterparties (CCPs): the Indian Clearing Corporation Limited (ICCL) for the National Stock Exchange and the National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited (NSCCL) for the BSE. Both are designated as “Systemically Important CCPs” by the RBI and SEBI, subject to the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (PFMI) issued by the Bank for International Settlements. The migration to a T+1 settlement cycle for equity trades, completed in January 2023, reduced counterparty exposure by one business day and lowered margin requirements for market participants. The “Trade‑for‑Trade” (TFT) segment for illiquid securities continues on a T+2 basis to manage settlement risk.

Risk management at CCPs relies on a layered margin system: initial margin (IM) calculated using Value‑at‑Risk (VaR) at 99 % confidence over a 1‑day horizon, extreme loss margin (ELM) for tail events, and a default fund (DF) contributed by clearing members proportionate to their cleared volume. The “Interoperability” framework, operational since 2019, allows a clearing member to clear trades executed on either exchange through a single CCP, enhancing capital efficiency. The “Centralised Settlement Guarantee Fund” (CSGF) pools contributions from all members to cover defaults exceeding individual DF contributions.

The “Real‑Time Gross Settlement” (RTGS) system, operated by the RBI, settles high‑value interbank transfers instantly, while the “National Electronic Funds Transfer” (NEFT) runs in half‑hourly batches. The “Unified Payments Interface” (UPI) and “Bharat Bill Payment System” (BBPS) operate at the retail layer but are linked to the same settlement infrastructure. For the exam, aspirants must distinguish between the roles of CCPs (novation, netting, margin) and settlement banks (funds transfer), recall the timeline of T+1 implementation, and explain how interoperability reduces systemic concentration risk.

Emerging Regulatory Challenges: Algorithmic Trading, Cyber Resilience and ESG Disclosure

Algorithmic and high‑frequency trading (HFT) now account for a significant share of equity futures and options volumes. SEBI’s “Framework for Algorithmic Trading” (2021) requires all algo orders to be routed through approved “Algo Providers”, mandates co‑location disclosures, and imposes a “kill‑switch” that can halt an algorithm within milliseconds. The 2023 “Cyber Security and Cyber Resilience Framework” for Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs) prescribes a minimum “Cyber Security Maturity Level” of 3 (on a 5‑level scale), mandatory quarterly penetration testing, and a 24‑hour incident reporting window to SEBI and CERT‑In.

Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) reporting has moved from voluntary to mandatory for the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation under the “Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting” (BRSR) format, effective FY 2022‑23. SEBI’s “Green Bond” framework (2022) aligns with the International Capital Market Association’s Green Bond Principles and requires external review, use‑of‑proceeds tracking, and annual impact reporting. The “Social Stock Exchange” (SSE), launched in 2023, enables non‑profit organisations and for‑profit social enterprises to raise capital through zero‑coupon zero‑principal instruments, with eligibility verified by a “Social Impact Assessment” agency.

These developments create overlapping compliance calendars: a listed firm must file BRSR, comply with algo‑kill‑switch testing if it operates proprietary algorithms, and ensure its registrar and transfer agent meets the MII cyber‑maturity threshold. For the examination, candidates should be able to map each regulatory initiative to its governing statute (SEBI Act, 1992; IT Act, 2000; Companies Act, 2013), identify the entities directly regulated, and discuss the trade‑off between market innovation and investor protection.

Macroprudential Stress Testing and the NBFC Scale‑Based Regulation Framework

The Reserve Bank of India introduced the Scale‑Based Regulation (SBR) for Non‑Banking Financial Companies in October 2021, classifying NBFCs into four layers — Base, Middle, Upper and Top — based on asset size, interconnectedness, and supervisory judgment. The framework replaces the earlier “systemically important” binary with a calibrated capital, liquidity and governance regime. Layer‑specific requirements include a minimum Capital‑to‑Risk‑Weighted‑Assets Ratio (CRAR) of 15 % for Base, 17 % for Middle, 19 % for Upper and a supervisory‑determined higher threshold for Top‑layer entities. Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) norms are phased in: 60 % for Middle, 80 % for Upper and 100 % for Top by March 2025. The SBR also mandates a Board‑level Risk Management Committee, a Chief Risk Officer for Upper and Top layers, and enhanced disclosure of large exposures.

Complementing the SBR, the RBI’s “Framework for Macro‑Stress Testing of NBFCs” (June 2022) requires all Middle‑layer and above NBFCs to run quarterly stress tests covering credit, market, liquidity and interest‑rate shocks. The scenarios are calibrated to the RBI’s “Adverse” and “Severely Adverse” macro‑economic paths used for banks, but with NBFC‑specific parameters such as higher retail‑loan concentration and reliance on wholesale funding. Results must be submitted to the Department of Supervision within 30 days of quarter‑end, and material deterioration triggers a supervisory action plan. The framework also introduces a “Countercyclical Capital Buffer” (CCyB) for NBFCs, activated when the credit‑to‑GDP gap exceeds the RBI’s threshold, mirroring the Basel III buffer for banks.

Interaction with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) is explicit: a Top‑layer NBFC that breaches the CRAR or LCR thresholds for two consecutive quarters is deemed “financially unsound” and may be referred to the National Company Law Tribunal under Section 7 of the IBC. The RBI’s “Prompt Corrective Action” (PCA) matrix for NBFCs, revised in 2023, aligns trigger points with the SBR layers, ensuring that supervisory intervention escalates in step with risk profile. For examiners, the key analytical link is the transmission of macro‑stress results into the SBR capital and liquidity buffers, and the consequent PCA/IBC pathway.

Sovereign Green Bonds, Taxonomy and the Climate Finance Architecture

India’s first sovereign green bond issuance occurred in January 2023, raising ₹16,000 crore across two tranches (5‑year and 10‑year) under the “Framework for Sovereign Green Bonds” notified by the Ministry of Finance in November 2022. The framework aligns with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles and the EU Taxonomy’s “Do No Significant Harm” criteria, adapted to Indian sectoral priorities: renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, sustainable agriculture, and green transport. Proceeds are tracked through a dedicated “Green Bond Register” maintained by the Public Debt Management Cell, with annual allocation and impact reports audited by the Comptroller and Auditor General.

The Reserve Bank of India, in coordination with the Ministry, released the “India Green Taxonomy” (Draft, July 2023) that classifies economic activities into “Green”, “Transition” and “Enabling” categories based on quantitative thresholds (e.g., solar PV capacity factor ≥ 20 %, grid‑connected wind ≥ 25 %). The taxonomy serves three purposes: (i) eligibility screening for sovereign and corporate green issuances, (ii) a reference for banks’ priority‑sector lending targets under the “Green Finance” sub‑target, and (iii) a basis for the Securities and Exchange Board of India’s (SEBI) “Green Bond” framework for listed corporates, which mandates external review, use‑of‑proceeds verification, and post‑issuance reporting aligned with the taxonomy.

Market development measures include the “Green Shoe Option” for sovereign issuances, allowing the government to retain up to 15 % of the issue size for secondary‑market stabilization, and the “Green Bond Index” launched by the National Stock Exchange in 2024 to provide a benchmark for institutional investors. The RBI’s “Guidelines on Climate‑Related Financial Risk” (April 2024) require scheduled commercial banks to disclose exposure to taxonomy‑aligned assets and to incorporate climate scenario analysis into their Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP). For the examination, candidates should be able to trace the policy chain: sovereign issuance → taxonomy → regulatory disclosure → prudential capital treatment.

CRAR compliance alone does not offset the LCR breach because the PCA matrix treats each metric independently.

For green finance, memorise the three taxonomy categories and the two sovereign issuance dates (Jan 2023, Feb 2024). When a question mixes NBFC stress testing with PCA/IBC, identify the layer first, then apply the metric‑specific trigger — do not assume a single “systemically important” label.

Resolution Regime for Systemically Important Financial Market Infrastructures

The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC) designated the National Securities Clearing Corporation (NSCC) and the Indian Clearing Corporation (ICCL) as “Systemically Important CCPs” in 2019, bringing them under the Resolution Framework for Financial Market Infrastructures (FMIs) notified by the RBI in March 2021 under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007. The framework mandates a “Resolution Plan” (living will) to be submitted annually, detailing recovery options, loss‑allocation mechanisms, and continuity of critical services (clearing, settlement, novation). The plan must be vetted by the RBI’s “Resolution Authority” and approved by the FSDC.

Key resolution tools include: (i) “Write‑down and Conversion” of CCP capital instruments (e.g., Tier‑2 bonds) to absorb losses before tapping the Default Fund; (ii) “Bridge CCP” arrangement where a solvent CCP assumes the critical functions of a failing CCP under a pre‑negotiated service‑level agreement; (iii) “Cash‑Collateral Haircuts” on participant margins to recapitalise the CCP without taxpayer support. The framework also prescribes a “Minimum Capital Requirement” of 8 % of risk‑weighted assets for CCPs, aligned with the CPMI‑IOSCO Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (PFMI). Interoperability, introduced in 2019, is explicitly recognised as a risk‑reduction feature because it diversifies the CCP’s participant base and reduces concentration.

Cross‑border cooperation is governed by the “Memorandum of Understanding” between the RBI and the Monetary Authority of Singapore (2022) and the “Joint Resolution Planning” protocol with the European Securities and Markets Authority (2023). These agreements ensure that a CCP with significant cross‑border activity can be resolved in a coordinated manner, avoiding regulatory arbitrage. For the exam, aspirants should distinguish between “recovery” (actions taken by the CCP itself) and “resolution” (authority‑led intervention), and recall the hierarchy: equity → Tier‑2 → Default Fund → Bridge CCP → Government backstop (only as last resort).

Digital Lending Guidelines, Data Localization and Consumer Protection in Fintech

The RBI’s “Guidelines on Digital Lending” (September 2022) were issued after the Working Group on Digital Lending (2021) highlighted risks of unregulated lending apps, opaque interest‑rate structures, and data misuse. The guidelines apply to all Regulated Entities (REs) — banks, NBFCs, and payment system operators — that originate, service or facilitate digital loans. Core requirements: (i) “Key Fact Statement” (KFS) in a standardized format disclosed before loan sanction, covering APR, fees, prepayment penalties, and cooling‑off period; (ii) “Loan‑to‑Value” (LTV) caps for unsecured digital loans at 50 % of the borrower’s assessed income; (iii) mandatory “Cool‑off Period” of 3 days during which the borrower can cancel without penalty; (iv) prohibition of “auto‑debit” mandates without explicit, revocable consent.

Data localization is enforced through the “Storage of Payment System Data” circular (April 2018, updated 2021), requiring all payment data — including digital‑lending transaction logs — to be stored only in systems located in India. The “Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture” (DEPA) framework, operationalised by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) in 2023, provides a consent‑based data‑sharing layer that lets borrowers port their credit‑worthiness data across lenders while retaining control. SEBI’s “Framework for Algorithmic Trading” (2021) and the “Cyber Security and Cyber Resilience Framework” for Market Infrastructure Institutions (2023) extend to fintech platforms that offer algorithm‑driven credit scoring, mandating quarterly penetration testing and a 24‑hour incident reporting window to CERT‑In.

Consumer protection is reinforced by the “Integrated Ombudsman Scheme” (2021) covering digital lending complaints, and the “Consumer Protection (E‑Commerce) Rules” (2020) that treat digital loan apps as “marketplace e‑commerce entities” for grievance redressal. The RBI’s “Scale‑Based Regulation” for NBFCs (2021) also applies to fintech‑backed NBFCs, subjecting them to the same layer‑wise capital and liquidity norms. Examination focus: trace the regulatory perimeter from the RBI’s digital‑lending circular → data‑localization mandate → DEPA consent layer → ombudsman redressal, and identify where SEBI’s algo‑trading and cyber‑resilience rules intersect.

  • Treating CCP recovery tools (write‑down, bridge CCP) as interchangeable with bank resolution tools (bail‑in, bridge bank) — the legal basis and hierarchy differ.

SEBI Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements and the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting Framework

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 (LODR) constitute the primary statutory framework governing continuous disclosure by listed entities. Regulation 30 mandates the disclosure of material events within twenty‑four hours of occurrence, while Regulation 33 prescribes the format and periodicity of financial results — quarterly for the first three quarters and annual audited results within sixty days of the financial year‑end. The LODR also introduced the concept of “corporate governance report” (Regulation 27) requiring a detailed compliance certificate from the CEO and CFO, and a separate “business responsibility report” (BRR) for the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation.

In 2021, SEBI replaced the BRR with the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR), aligning Indian reporting with the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) and the Task Force on Climate‑related Financial Disclosures (TCFD). The BRSR is mandatory for the top 1,000 listed companies from FY 2022‑23 and voluntary for others. It comprises nine sections covering governance, ethics, human rights, environment, stakeholder engagement, and sector‑specific disclosures. Companies must map their policies to the nine principles of the National Voluntary Guidelines on Social, Environmental and Economic Responsibilities of Business (NVGs) and provide quantitative metrics such as greenhouse‑gas emissions (Scope 1, 2, 3), water consumption, and diversity ratios.

The LODR also embeds a “comply‑or‑explain” regime for certain governance norms — for instance, the requirement of at least one independent woman director (Regulation 17(1)(a)) and the separation of the chairperson and managing director roles (Regulation 17(1)(b)). Non‑compliance attracts monetary penalties under Section 15HB of the SEBI Act, 1992, and can trigger a “trading suspension” under Regulation 31. Recent amendments (2023) have tightened the timeline for related‑party transaction approvals to three working days and introduced a “material subsidiary” threshold of 10 % of consolidated turnover or net worth, whichever is lower.

From an examination perspective, aspirants should be able to distinguish between the mandatory BRSR (top 1,000) and the voluntary ESG disclosures under the “Sustainability Reporting Framework” issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI). They must also recall the penalty hierarchy: ₹1 lakh per day for delayed financial results, up to ₹25 crore for fraudulent disclosures, and the power of SEBI to direct a “re‑opening of books” under Section 11(4) of the SEBI Act.

Investor Education and Protection Fund: Governance, Utilisation and Recent Amendments

The Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF) was established under Section 125 of the Companies Act, 2013, and became operational on 7 September 2016. It receives amounts from unpaid dividends, matured deposits, debentures, application money, and interest thereon that remain unclaimed for seven years. The fund is managed by the IEPF Authority, a statutory body chaired by the Secretary, Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA), with representatives from SEBI, RBI, and the Institute of Company Secretaries of India (ICSI).

Utilisation of the IEPF is governed by the Investor Education and Protection Fund Authority (Accounting, Audit, Transfer and Refund) Rules, 2016. The primary objectives are (i) refund of unclaimed amounts to rightful claimants, (ii) investor awareness programmes, and (iii) funding of research on investor protection. In FY 2022‑23, the IEPF disbursed ₹1,200 crore in refunds and allocated ₹150 crore for awareness campaigns, including the “Investor Awareness Programme” conducted through the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

The 2020 amendment (Notification G.S.R. 1029(E)) expanded the fund’s scope to include amounts from “unclaimed shares” transferred to the IEPF after seven years of non‑claim, and introduced a “fast‑track refund” mechanism for claims up to ₹5 lakh, processed within thirty days. The 2022 amendment (G.S.R. 1029(E)‑2022) mandated that companies file a “Statement of Unclaimed Amounts” in Form IEPF‑2 within sixty days of the end of each financial year, and linked the IEPF database with the MCA21 portal for real‑time tracking.

Examination focus: candidates must differentiate between the IEPF (Companies Act) and the “Investor Protection Fund” (IPF) maintained by stock exchanges under SEBI (Stock Exchanges) Regulations, 2018. The IPF is funded by a 0.01 % turnover levy on trades and is used for investor compensation in case of broker default, whereas the IEPF deals with corporate unclaimed amounts. A common trap is confusing the seven‑year dormancy period for IEPF with the three‑year period for unclaimed dividends under the erstwhile Companies Act, 1956.

Technique: Mapping Regulatory Provisions to Exam Scenarios
A listed company fails to file its quarterly financial results within the prescribed forty‑five‑day window under Regulation 33 of LODR. The delay is sixty days. SEBI imposes a penalty. Calculate the maximum penalty leviable under Section 15HB of the SEBI Act, 1992, and identify the additional regulatory action SEBI may initiate.
Section 15HB provides for a penalty of up to ₹1 lakh for each day of default, subject to a maximum of ₹25 crore. For a sixty‑day delay, the per‑day penalty would be ₹1 lakh × 60 = ₹60 lakh, which is below the ₹25 crore cap. Hence the maximum penalty leviable is ₹60 lakh. In addition, under Regulation 31 SEBI may suspend trading of the company’s securities until compliance is achieved, and under Section 11(4) may direct the company to re‑open its books of account for the relevant period.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for “maximum penalty”, always compute the per‑day amount first, then compare with the statutory ceiling. Remember that SEBI’s enforcement toolkit combines monetary penalties (Section 15HB), trading suspension (Regulation 31), and direction to re‑open books (Section 11(4)). Practise writing the three‑step answer: (1) statutory provision, (2) calculation, (3) ancillary actions.

Real Estate Investment Trusts and Infrastructure Investment Trusts: Regulatory Architecture and Market Development

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Real Estate Investment Trusts) Regulations, 2014, and the SEBI (Infrastructure Investment Trusts) Regulations, 2014, created two distinct pass‑through vehicles for pooling capital into income‑generating real estate and infrastructure assets. Both structures are required to distribute at least 90 % of net distributable cash flows to unit holders semi‑annually, and must maintain a minimum asset coverage ratio of 2:1 (debt to equity) for REITs and 1.5:1 for InvITs.

REITs are mandated to invest a minimum of 80 % of their asset value in completed, revenue‑generating properties, with the remaining 20 % allowed in under‑construction assets, mortgage‑backed securities, or government securities. InvITs must allocate at least 80 % to completed infrastructure projects (roads, power transmission, pipelines) and may hold up to 20 % in under‑construction projects or debt instruments of infrastructure companies. Both vehicles are listed on stock exchanges, providing liquidity to otherwise illiquid asset classes.

The 2020 amendment to the REIT Regulations introduced a “strategic investor” category, allowing a single investor to hold up to 25 % of units without triggering a mandatory open offer, provided the investor is a regulated entity (bank, insurance company, pension fund). The 2021 amendment to the InvIT Regulations permitted “sponsor‑side” leverage up to 49 % of the InvIT’s asset value, subject to a credit rating of AA‑ or above, thereby enhancing the capacity to acquire large‑scale assets.

Tax treatment is a critical differentiator: distributions from REITs and InvITs are taxed in the hands of unit holders as “income from other sources” at the applicable slab rate, while the vehicle itself enjoys a pass‑through status under Section 10(23FC) of the Income‑Tax Act, 1961. However, capital gains on sale of units are subject to short‑term (15 %) or long‑term (10 % without indexation) rates depending on holding period. The 2023 Finance Act extended the “tax‑exempt” status for dividend income received by REITs/InvITs from SPVs (Special Purpose Vehicles) until FY 2025‑26.

From a market‑development perspective, the first REIT (Embassy Office Parks) listed in April 2019, followed by Mindspace Business Parks (2020) and Brookfield India Real Estate Trust (2021). The first InvIT (India Grid Trust) listed in June 2017, with subsequent listings by PowerGrid Infrastructure Investment Trust (2021) and NHAI InvIT (2022). As of March 2024, the combined market capitalisation of listed REITs and InvITs exceeded ₹1.2 lakh crore, representing a growing share of institutional allocation to real assets.

Corporate Bond Repo Market and Tri‑Party Repo Mechanism: Liquidity Enhancement and Risk Management

The corporate bond repo market was operationalised in 2018 following SEBI’s “Framework for Repo in Corporate Bonds” (Circular SEBI/HO/MRD/DP/CIR/P/2018/77). A repo (repurchase agreement) allows a holder of corporate bonds to borrow funds by selling the bonds with an agreement to repurchase them at a future date at a predetermined price. The framework permits both “outright repo” and “tri‑party repo” structures, the latter involving a tri‑party agent (typically a clearing corporation) that manages collateral selection, valuation, and substitution.

In a tri‑party repo, the borrower (cash provider) and lender (bond holder) agree on a collateral basket managed by the tri‑party agent — currently the Clearing Corporation of India Limited (CCIL) and the National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited (NSCCL). The agent marks‑to‑market the collateral daily, applies haircuts based on credit rating (e.g., 2 % for AAA, 5 % for AA, 10 % for A), and ensures substitution of ineligible securities. This reduces bilateral operational risk and enables a broader set of participants, including mutual funds, insurance companies, and primary dealers, to access short‑term funding against high‑quality corporate bonds.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) integrated the corporate bond repo into the “Liquidity Adjustment Facility” (LAF) corridor in 2020, allowing banks to use eligible corporate bonds (rated AA‑ and above) as collateral for overnight and term repos. The RBI also prescribed a minimum haircut of 5 % for AA‑rated bonds and 10 % for A‑rated bonds under the LAF, aligning with the tri‑party agent’s haircut schedule.

Risk management features include: (i) daily margining and mark‑to‑market, (ii) a “collateral eligibility matrix” updated quarterly by SEBI, (iii) a “default fund” contributed by participants to cover counterparty failures, and (iv) a “close‑out netting” provision under the Netting Act, 2020, which ensures enforceability of netting arrangements in insolvency. The 2022 SEBI circular (SEBI/HO/MRD/DP/CIR/P/2022/115) mandated that all tri‑party repos be reported on the “Corporate Bond Repo Reporting Platform” within fifteen minutes of execution, enhancing transparency.

Examination relevance: aspirants should be able to contrast the bilateral repo (direct between two parties) with the tri‑party repo (agent‑mediated), recall the haircut schedule, and explain how the repo market deepens the corporate bond secondary market by providing a risk‑free funding avenue. A typical question may ask: “Which entity acts as the tri‑party agent for corporate bond repos in India?” Answer: CCIL and NSCCL.

Liberalised Remittance Scheme and Retail Foreign Investment: Limits, Reporting and Compliance

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) was introduced by the Reserve Bank of India on 4 February 2004 under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999, to facilitate resident individuals’ outward remittances for permissible current and capital account transactions. The scheme permits remittances up to USD 250,000 per financial year (April‑March) per individual, without requiring prior RBI approval. Permissible purposes include private visits, education, medical treatment, gifts, donations, and overseas investments in equity, debt, and immovable property.

In 2015, the LRS limit was raised from USD 125,000 to USD 250,000. The 2020 amendment (Notification FEMA 20(R)/2020‑RB) mandated that Authorised Dealer (AD) banks obtain a “Form A2” declaration and a “Certificate of Chartered Accountant” for remittances exceeding USD 100,000 in a single transaction, to ensure compliance with the annual cap. The 2022 amendment (FEMA 20(R)/2022‑RB) introduced a “quarterly reporting” requirement for AD banks to submit LRS transaction data to the RBI’s “Data Warehouse” within fifteen days of quarter‑end.

Capital account investments under LRS are subject to the “overseas direct investment” (ODI) guidelines. Residents may acquire up to 10 % of the paid‑up capital of a foreign entity without RBI approval, provided the investment is in a non‑financial sector. Investments in financial services entities (banks, insurance, NBFCs) require prior RBI approval irrespective of the percentage. The 2023 “Overseas Investment Rules” (Notification FEMA 20(R)/2023‑RB) consolidated ODI and LRS provisions, prescribing a “net‑worth” test: the resident’s net worth must be at least twice the proposed investment amount.

Tax implications are significant: income from foreign assets (dividends, interest, capital gains) is taxable in India under the “resident and ordinarily resident” (ROR) status. The Finance Act, 2020 introduced Section 194LC, imposing a 5 % TDS on interest paid to non‑residents on rupee‑denominated bonds, which also applies to interest received by residents from foreign bonds held under LRS. The “Foreign Asset Disclosure” requirement under the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act, 2015, obliges residents to declare foreign bank accounts, immovable property, and financial interests in the annual income‑tax return.

Compliance pitfalls include: (i) breaching the annual USD 250,000 cap across multiple AD banks — the RBI’s “LRS Monitoring System” aggregates PAN‑level data; (ii) using LRS for prohibited purposes such as margin trading, lottery tickets, or purchase of foreign exchange derivatives; (iii) failing to repatriate proceeds from overseas investments within the prescribed period (generally 180 days for capital gains). Penalties under FEMA can be up to three times the amount involved, and the RBI may restrict the individual’s future LRS access.

  • Confusing the IEPF (Companies Act) with the stock‑exchange Investor Protection Fund (SEBI Regulations) — they serve different claimant bases and funding sources.
  • Assuming the BRSR is mandatory for all listed companies — it applies only to the top 1,000 by market capitalisation from FY 2022‑23.
  • Treating REIT and InvIT distribution taxability as identical — both are taxed in the hands of unit holders, but the underlying SPV dividend exemption differs under Section 10(23FC).
  • Overlooking the haircut differentiation in tri‑party repo — AAA bonds attract 2 % haircut, AA 5 %, A 10 %; the LAF corridor imposes a minimum 5 % for AA‑rated bonds.
  • Believing the LRS limit of USD 250,000 is per transaction — it is an annual aggregate cap per PAN across all AD banks.
  • Ignoring the mandatory quarterly LRS reporting by AD banks introduced in 2022 — non‑reporting attracts penal action under FEMA.
  • Mixing up the “strategic investor” exemption in REITs (25 % holding without open offer) with the “sponsor” leverage limit in InvITs (49 % of asset value).

Securities Appellate Tribunal: Jurisdiction, Landmark Orders and Appeal Procedure

The Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) was constituted under Section 15K of the SEBI Act, 1992, and became operational on 1 January 1996. It sits at Mumbai with a bench at New Delhi and exercises appellate jurisdiction over orders passed by SEBI, the Reserve Bank of India (in respect of its securities‑related functions), the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, and the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority. The Tribunal consists of a Presiding Officer, who must be a former Supreme Court judge or a former Chief Justice of a High Court, and two other members with expertise in law, finance, or administration. Appeals must be filed within 45 days of the impugned order, extendable by a further 45 days on sufficient cause. SAT follows the procedure of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, as far as practicable, and its orders are appealable to the Supreme Court only on a substantial question of law under Section 15Z of the SEBI Act.

Landmark SAT decisions have shaped market practice. In Sahara India Real Estate Corp. Ltd. v. SEBI (2012), the Tribunal upheld SEBI’s direction to refund ₹24,000 crore to investors, reinforcing the regulator’s power to protect collective investment scheme participants. The National Spot Exchange Ltd. (NSEL) case (2014) saw SAT affirm SEBI’s order attaching assets of defaulting brokers, clarifying that the Tribunal can review the proportionality of attachment orders. In MCX‑SX v. SEBI (2015), SAT set aside a SEBI order refusing recognition to a new stock exchange, holding that the regulator must apply the “fit and proper” test transparently. More recently, the 2021 order in Franklin Templeton Mutual Fund v. SEBI dealt with the winding‑up of six debt schemes, where SAT directed SEBI to reconsider the “interest of investors” standard, illustrating the Tribunal’s willingness to scrutinise regulatory discretion.

Practically, aspirants should note the distinction between SAT’s appellate and original jurisdiction. SAT does not entertain original petitions; it only hears appeals. However, under Section 15L, the Tribunal may review its own orders on grounds of mistake or new evidence. The 2020 amendment to the SEBI Act introduced a “fast‑track” disposal timeline of 180 days for appeals involving investor protection, aiming to reduce pendency. The Tribunal’s decisions are published on its website and are frequently cited in SEBI circulars, making them a primary source for understanding regulatory interpretation.

Market Surveillance, Insider Trading Detection and the SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations

The SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015, replaced the 1992 framework and were substantially amended in 2020 and 2022. The regulations define “unpublished price sensitive information” (UPSI) as any information relating to a company’s financial results, mergers, acquisitions, dividends, or changes in key managerial personnel that is not generally available and which, if disclosed, would materially affect the price of its securities. Every listed company must formulate a “code of fair disclosure” and a “code of conduct” for designated persons, maintain a structured digital database of UPSI, and enforce trading window closures from the end of each quarter until 48 hours after the dissemination of financial results.

SEBI’s Market Surveillance System (MSS) integrates order‑book data from all recognised stock exchanges, the SCORES (SEBI Complaints Redress System) platform, and the Centralised Database of Market Participants. The system employs pattern‑recognition algorithms to flag abnormal price‑volume movements, layered orders, and wash trades. In 2021, SEBI introduced the “Insider Trading Detection System” (ITDS), which cross‑references trading data with the UPSI database maintained by companies, enabling real‑time alerts. The 2022 amendment mandated that designated persons disclose their holdings and trades within two trading days, and that the compliance officer certify the adequacy of the internal controls annually.

Enforcement outcomes illustrate the regime’s teeth. In the NSE insider trading case (2019), SEBI imposed a penalty of ₹1 crore on a senior executive for trading on advance knowledge of the index‑rebalancing schedule. The Reliance Industries‑Future Group dispute (2020) saw SEBI levy a ₹25 crore fine on entities that traded on UPSI concerning the scheme of arrangement. The 2023 order against a prominent broker‑dealer for “front‑running” client orders demonstrated the use of the MSS to reconstruct order‑flow timestamps. Penalties under Section 15G of the SEBI Act can extend to ₹25 crore or three times the profit made, whichever is higher, and the Tribunal can order disgorgement with interest at 12 % per annum.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Under the SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015, which of the following is NOT a requirement for a listed company?” Options: (a) Maintain a structured digital database of UPSI; (b) Appoint a Chief Investor Relations Officer; (c) Enforce trading window closure from end of quarter till 48 hours after results; (d) Formulate a code of fair disclosure.
The correct answer is (b). The regulations mandate a structured digital database of UPSI, trading window closures, and a code of fair disclosure, but they do not require the appointment of a Chief Investor Relations Officer. That role is prescribed under the SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015, not under the insider trading framework.

Alternative Investment Funds: Category I, II, III Regulatory Framework and Tax Treatment

The SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012, govern pooled investment vehicles that are not mutual funds, collective investment schemes, or pension funds. AIFs are classified into three categories. Category I AIFs invest in start‑up, early‑stage ventures, social ventures, SMEs, infrastructure, or other sectors the government designates as socially or economically desirable; they enjoy a “pass‑through” tax status under Section 115UB of the Income‑Tax Act, meaning income is taxed in the hands of investors. Category II AIFs include private equity funds, debt funds, fund‑of‑funds, and real estate funds that do not fall in Category I or III; they also receive pass‑through treatment provided they satisfy the “no‑leverage” condition (borrowing limited to 10 % of corpus for temporary funding). Category III AIFs employ diverse or complex trading strategies, including hedge funds, and may use leverage up to two times the fund’s net asset value; they are taxed at the fund level at the maximum marginal rate (currently 42.74 % including surcharge and cess) and do not enjoy pass‑through status.

Registration requires a minimum corpus of ₹20 crore for Category I and II, and ₹100 crore for Category III. The sponsor must contribute at least 2.5 % of the corpus or ₹5 crore, whichever is lower, and the manager must have a net worth of ₹5 crore. The 2022 amendment introduced a “large value fund” framework for AIFs with corpus exceeding ₹500 crore, permitting a higher leverage limit of 2.5 times for Category III and relaxing certain reporting timelines. AIFs must appoint a custodian registered with SEBI, a compliance officer, and an independent valuer for illiquid assets. Quarterly reporting to SEBI includes portfolio composition, valuation methodology, and stress‑test results for leveraged funds.

Taxation nuances are a frequent examination focus. While Category I and II AIFs are pass‑through vehicles, the Finance Act 2020 inserted a “tax‑on‑distribution” provision for income distributed to non‑resident investors, subject to treaty benefits. The 2023 budget clarified that carried interest received by fund managers is taxed as “income from business” at the applicable slab rate, not as capital gains. Additionally, the “thin‑capitalisation” rules under Section 94B apply to AIFs borrowing from associated enterprises, limiting interest deductions to 30 % of EBITDA. Understanding the interplay between SEBI’s regulatory capital requirements and the Income‑Tax Act’s pass‑through conditions is essential for answering scenario‑based questions.

Credit Rating Agencies: Registration, Conflict‑of‑Interest Safeguards and Recent SEBI Circulars

Credit Rating Agencies (CRAs) in India are regulated under the SEBI (Credit Rating Agencies) Regulations, 1999, as amended in 2021 and 2023. To obtain a certificate of registration, an applicant must be a company incorporated in India with a minimum net worth of ₹25 crore, a track record of at least three years in financial services, and a board comprising at least 50 % independent directors. The 2021 amendment introduced a “fit and proper” test for promoters and senior management, requiring a clean record free from fraud, insolvency, or regulatory action. CRAs must maintain a “rating committee” with a majority of independent members, and the chief rating officer cannot be involved in business development.

Conflict‑of‑interest safeguards are central. A CRA cannot rate a security issued by its promoter, associate, or any entity in which the promoter holds more than 10 % equity. The 2023 circular (SEBI/HO/MIRSD/CRADT/CIR/P/2023/45) mandated a “Chinese wall” between the analytical and commercial functions, requiring physical and IT segregation, separate reporting lines, and a quarterly compliance certificate signed by the chief compliance officer. CRAs must disclose the methodology, key assumptions, and sensitivity analysis for each rating, and publish a “rating history” on their website for at least ten years. The circular also introduced a “rating outlook” framework (Positive, Stable, Negative) and required CRAs to assign a “rating watch” when a material event is anticipated.

Enforcement actions illustrate the regime’s rigour. In 2022, SEBI suspended the registration of a major CRA for six months for failing to disclose a conflict of interest in rating a group company’s debentures. The 2020 “IL&FS” episode prompted SEBI to issue a show‑cause notice to three CRAs for delayed downgrades, leading to the 2021 amendment that tightened the “timely review” obligation — ratings must be reviewed at least quarterly and within 30 days of a material event. The 2023 circular further required CRAs to adopt a “probability of default” model calibrated to Indian macro‑conditions, moving away from purely issuer‑specific qualitative assessments. Aspirants should be able to contrast the Indian CRA framework with the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) Code of Conduct, noting the additional Indian requirements of independent rating committees and mandatory Chinese‑wall certification.

Cross‑Border Insolvency: UNCITRAL Model Law Adoption and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code Amendments

India’s approach to cross‑border insolvency evolved with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, and the subsequent insertion of Sections 234 and 235 in 2020, which empower the Central Government to enter into reciprocal arrangements with foreign countries and to apply the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross‑Border Insolvency (1997) by notification. As of 2024, India has not enacted a standalone Cross‑Border Insolvency Act; instead, the government issued the “Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2023” prescribing the procedure for foreign representatives to seek recognition of foreign proceedings before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT). The rules require the foreign representative to file an application with a certified copy of the foreign court order, a statement of the debtor’s centre of main interests (COMI), and an undertaking to cooperate with Indian creditors.

The landmark Jet Airways case (2021) tested the framework. The Dutch administrator sought recognition of the Dutch bankruptcy proceedings in India to access the airline’s Indian assets. The NCLT, applying the “public policy” exception under Section 235, denied recognition on the ground that the Dutch proceedings did not provide adequate protection to Indian creditors, particularly the employees’ provident fund claims. Conversely, in the Videocon Industries matter (2022), the NCLT recognised the Singapore‑scheme of arrangement after the foreign representative demonstrated that the scheme was approved by a majority of creditors in each class and that Indian creditors were given pari‑passu treatment. The 2022 amendment to the IBC introduced a “fast‑track” recognition route for “group companies” where a foreign proceeding involves a debtor that is part of an Indian corporate group, reducing the timeline from 180 days to 90 days.

Operational challenges remain. The absence of a comprehensive treaty network means recognition is largely ad‑hoc, relying on comity and the “public policy” test. The 2023 Rules mandate that the NCLT consider the “interest of all stakeholders” and may impose conditions such as a stay on enforcement actions in India. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) has issued a “Cross‑Border Insolvency Protocol” (2023) recommending best practices for communication between Indian and foreign insolvency professionals, joint hearings, and asset‑distribution protocols. For examination purposes, candidates should be able to articulate the three‑step test for recognition — (i) existence of a foreign proceeding, (ii) the foreign representative’s authority, and (iii) compatibility with Indian public policy — and to distinguish between “main proceedings” (COMI in foreign state) and “non‑main proceedings” (establishment in foreign state).

Securities Lending and Borrowing Mechanism and Short Selling Framework

The Securities Lending and Borrowing Mechanism (SLBM) was introduced by SEBI in 2008 to provide a regulated avenue for temporary transfer of securities, thereby facilitating short selling, market making and arbitrage. Under the SEBI (Securities Lending and Borrowing) Regulations, 2018, only approved intermediaries — primarily clearing corporations (NSCCL, ICCL) and designated stock exchanges — may operate the platform. Eligible securities are those listed on a recognised exchange and meeting the prescribed liquidity criteria; the list is reviewed quarterly. Borrowers must be institutional investors such as mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds and Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) registered under the SEBI (FPI) Regulations, 2019. Retail investors are not permitted to borrow securities directly, although they may lend through a registered intermediary.

Short selling in India is permitted only on a covered basis: the seller must have either borrowed the securities through SLBM or hold an equivalent long position in the same security. Naked short selling remains prohibited. The framework mandates a minimum margin of 20 % of the transaction value for the borrower, marked‑to‑market daily, and a lending fee negotiated between parties, typically expressed as an annualised percentage. The clearing corporation acts as central counterparty, guaranteeing settlement and managing collateral — usually cash or government securities — to mitigate counterparty risk. In 2020 SEBI tightened the framework by requiring daily reporting of lending positions, imposing a cap of 20 % of the free‑float market capitalisation for any single security, and introducing a “recall” facility that allows lenders to recall securities with a two‑day notice.

The SLBM has contributed to improved price discovery and tighter bid‑ask spreads, especially in large‑cap stocks. However, utilisation remains modest compared with developed markets; as of March 2024 the average daily lending volume was approximately ₹1,200 crore, representing less than 1 % of total market turnover. Key constraints include limited participation from domestic mutual funds (who prefer repo‑style transactions), the absence of a robust securities‑lending benchmark rate, and operational friction in cross‑border lending for FPIs. SEBI’s 2023 consultation paper proposes a “Securities Lending Benchmark” linked to the overnight indexed swap (OIS) curve and a simplified onboarding process for foreign lenders, aiming to deepen the market.

Regulatory Sandbox and Innovation Hub for Fintech

SEBI launched the Regulatory Sandbox in June 2019, followed by the Innovation Hub in 2021, to enable live testing of novel financial products, services and technology‑driven business models under a controlled regulatory environment. The sandbox operates in cohorts; each cohort runs for a maximum of 12 months, after which the participant must either exit, seek full authorisation, or apply for an extension. Eligible applicants include start‑ups, established intermediaries, and technology firms proposing solutions in areas such as distributed ledger technology (DLT), artificial intelligence‑driven advisory, digital onboarding, and fractional ownership platforms.

The entry criteria require a minimum viable product, a clear risk‑mitigation plan, and a commitment to consumer protection — data privacy, grievance redressal, and transparent disclosure. SEBI evaluates applications on novelty, scalability, and potential systemic impact. Successful participants receive a “sandbox licence” that grants temporary relaxations from specific provisions of the SEBI (Intermediaries) Regulations, 2008, the SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020, and the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013, among others. The Innovation Hub complements the sandbox by offering a non‑binding regulatory guidance channel, a dedicated helpdesk, and a repository of regulatory precedents for fintech innovators.

As of the fourth cohort (2023‑24), 38 entities have been admitted, with 12 graduating to full registration. Notable graduates include a blockchain‑based settlement platform for corporate bonds, an AI‑driven robo‑advisory service for retail investors, and a digital gold‑fractionalisation platform. The sandbox has also informed policy: SEBI’s 2022 circular on “Digital Gold” and the 2023 framework for “Algorithmic Trading by Retail Investors” drew directly on sandbox outcomes. Challenges persist — limited cross‑regulatory coordination with RBI and IRDAI, the absence of a unified data‑sharing protocol, and the need for a permanent “graduation” pathway that reduces time‑to‑market for successful pilots.

Market Wide Position Limits and Derivative Position Limits

Market Wide Position Limits (MWPL) were introduced by SEBI in 2020 to curb excessive speculative build‑up in equity derivatives and to align Indian risk‑management standards with international best practices. The MWPL for a single stock is set at 20 % of the free‑float market capitalisation or 60 times the average daily delivery volume in the cash segment, whichever is lower. For index derivatives, the limit is 15 % of the index’s free‑float market capitalisation. These limits apply aggregately across all client categories — proprietary, institutional, and retail — and are monitored at the clearing corporation level on a real‑time basis.

In addition to MWPL, SEBI prescribes client‑level position limits: 5 % of the MWPL for non‑institutional clients and 10 % for institutional clients, with a further sub‑limit of 1 % for any single client in a particular derivative contract. The limits are enforced through the “position limit monitoring system” (PLMS) integrated with the clearing corporations’ risk engines. Breaches trigger automatic margin calls, position‑reduction orders, and, in persistent cases, trading restrictions. The 2022 amendment extended MWPL to currency derivatives (USD‑INR, EUR‑INR, GBP‑INR) with a limit of 10 % of the respective free‑float notional value.

The framework has reduced the incidence of “cornering” in mid‑cap stocks; data from NSCCL shows a 38 % decline in MWPL breach alerts between FY 2021‑22 and FY 2023‑24. However, market participants argue that the static free‑float base does not capture rapid changes in shareholding patterns, leading to occasional over‑restriction. SEBI’s 2023 consultation proposes a dynamic MWPL recalibrated monthly using updated shareholding disclosures and a “stress‑test” buffer of 10 % above the calculated limit to accommodate temporary liquidity spikes.

Central Counterparty Default Management and Recovery Planning

India’s central counterparties (CCPs) — National Securities Clearing Corporation Ltd (NSCCL) for the equity and equity‑derivatives segments, Indian Clearing Corporation Ltd (ICCL) for the debt and currency segments, and MCX‑CCL for commodity derivatives — operate under the SEBI (Clearing Corporations) Regulations, 2018. Each CCP maintains a default fund (also called the “core settlement guarantee fund”) contributed by clearing members in proportion to their risk exposure. As of March 2024, NSCCL’s default fund stood at ₹23,500 crore, ICCL’s at ₹9,800 crore, and MCX‑CCL’s at ₹1,200 crore.

Default management follows a waterfall: (1) the defaulter’s own margins and collateral; (2) the defaulter’s contribution to the default fund; (3) the CCP’s own capital (skin‑in‑the‑game); (4) the surviving members’ default fund contributions on a pro‑rata basis; and (5) the CCP’s insurance cover, where applicable. SEBI mandates a minimum “skin‑in‑the‑game” of 25 % of the total default fund for each CCP. The 2021 “Recovery and Resolution Planning” guidelines require each CCP to submit a comprehensive recovery plan, including stress‑scenario modelling (e.g., simultaneous default of the two largest members), liquidity contingency arrangements, and a communication protocol with the regulator and the Resolution Corporation (once established under the Financial Resolution and Deposit Insurance Bill).

In 2022 NSCCL conducted a live default simulation involving a hypothetical default of a top‑tier clearing member with ₹1.2 lakh crore of open positions. The exercise validated the adequacy of the default fund and the effectiveness of the “partial tear‑up” mechanism, whereby non‑defaulting members’ positions are novated to a surviving member to preserve market continuity. Ongoing enhancements include the adoption of a “centralised margin model” for cross‑segment netting, integration with the RBI’s “Real Time Gross Settlement” (RTGS) for intraday liquidity, and the development of a “CCP resolution regime” aligned with the Financial Stability Board’s “Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes for Financial Market Infrastructures”.

Foreign Portfolio Investor Registration, KYC and Beneficial Ownership Norms

The SEBI (Foreign Portfolio Investors) Regulations, 2019, consolidated the erstwhile FII and QFI regimes into a single FPI framework. Registration is granted by designated depository participants (DDPs) after verification of the applicant’s eligibility — regulated entities such as pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurance companies, and asset managers — and compliance with the “fit and proper” criteria. The regulations prescribe a two‑tier KYC: (i) a “basic KYC” at the time of registration, covering entity constitution, regulatory status, and beneficial ownership up to the natural person level; and (ii) an “enhanced KYC” for Category I FPIs (government‑related investors) and Category II FPIs (broad‑based funds), requiring periodic re‑verification every 12 months.

Beneficial ownership rules, aligned with the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations, mandate identification of any natural person holding 10 % or more of the voting rights or capital, or exercising control through other means. For listed FPIs, the threshold is lowered to 5 %. The 2021 amendment introduced a “centralised KYC repository” (CKYCR) managed by the Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest (CERSAI), enabling DDPs to share verified KYC records and reducing onboarding time from 30 days to under 7 days.

Compliance monitoring includes monthly reporting of holdings, quarterly disclosure of portfolio changes exceeding 2 % of the investee company’s paid‑up capital, and an annual audit certificate from a SEBI‑registered auditor. Violations attract monetary penalties up to ₹1 crore per instance and, in severe cases, suspension or cancellation of registration. The 2023 circular on “FPI Investment in Debt Securities” relaxed the minimum residual maturity requirement for corporate bonds from three years to one year, provided the issuer meets the “investment grade” rating criterion, thereby expanding the investable universe for foreign capital.

Account Aggregator Framework and Consent‑Based Data Sharing in Financial Services

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) launched the Account Aggregator (AA) framework in September 2021 under the Master Direction – Account Aggregator (Non‑Banking Financial Company). An AA is a non‑banking financial company that acts as a consent manager, enabling a customer to share financial data — such as bank statements, tax returns, insurance policies and mutual‑fund holdings — with regulated entities (banks, NBFCs, insurers, fintechs) in a secure, real‑time, and auditable manner. The architecture rests on three pillars: (i) a Financial Information Provider (FIP) that holds the data; (ii) a Financial Information User (FIU) that requests the data; and (iii) the AA that brokers the consent artefact, encrypts the payload and logs every transaction on an immutable audit trail.

Consent is granular: the customer selects the data categories, the purpose, the frequency (one‑off or recurring) and the validity period (maximum 12 months). The AA cannot view the underlying data; it only sees the consent artefact and the encrypted payload. The framework mandates end‑to‑end encryption using AES‑256, digital signatures based on the Indian PKI hierarchy, and storage of consent logs for a minimum of seven years. As of March 2024, eight AAs have received certificates of registration — CAMS FinServ, Cookiejar, Finvu, OneMoney, Perfios, Yodlee, Jio Information Solutions and the newly licensed Sahamati — and over 1.2 crore consent requests have been processed.

Regulatory safeguards include: (a) a fit‑and‑proper test for AA promoters; (b) a minimum net‑owned fund of ₹2 crore; (c) mandatory ISO 27001 certification; (d) quarterly reporting to the RBI on consent volumes, breach incidents and grievance redressal; and (e) a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) before onboarding any new FIP or FIU. The RBI’s 2023 circular on “Enhancing the AA Ecosystem” introduced a “Consent‑as‑a‑Service” sandbox, allowing non‑regulated entities (e.g., e‑commerce platforms) to test AA‑mediated data flows under supervisory oversight for a 12‑month cohort.

Key benefits for the financial system: faster credit underwriting (average turnaround reduced from 7 days to < 24 hours for MSME loans), lower cost of KYC (estimated 30 % reduction in onboarding expense), and enhanced financial inclusion by enabling thin‑file borrowers to leverage alternative data (utility bills, GST returns). The framework also aligns with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, ensuring that consent artefacts satisfy the “explicit, informed, and revocable” standard.

Operational Flow

  1. Customer initiates a consent request on the AA’s mobile app or web portal.
  2. AA creates a consent artefact (JSON‑LD) signed by the customer’s Aadhaar‑linked digital signature.
  3. AA forwards the artefact to the selected FIP(s).
  4. FIP validates the artefact, encrypts the requested data with the FIU’s public key, and returns the payload to the AA.
  5. AA relays the encrypted payload to the FIU, which decrypts and consumes the data.
  6. All parties log the transaction; the AA stores the consent log for audit.

  • January 2024: The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC) recommended a unified consent registry to avoid duplication across AAs.

The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) began the internationalisation of the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) in 2022 with the launch of UPI‑Global in the United Arab Emirates, Singapore, Bhutan and Nepal. The model leverages the existing UPI stack — a real‑time, interoperable, mobile‑first payment rail — and adds a cross‑border settlement layer that settles in the local currency of the destination country while preserving the UPI user experience (QR‑code scan, virtual payment address, biometric authentication).

Technical architecture: (i) UPI‑Gateway hosted by NPCI International Payments Limited (NIPL), a wholly‑owned subsidiary; (ii) Correspondent Banking Agreements with licensed banks in each corridor to provide liquidity and foreign‑exchange conversion; (iii) ISO 20022‑compliant messaging for transaction data, enabling straight‑through processing; (iv) Risk‑based authentication aligned with the RBI’s Framework for Authentication of Digital Payments (2021) and the host country’s regulations (e.g., MAS’s Singapore Payments Roadmap).

Regulatory milestones: February 2022 – RBI issued the “Guidelines for Cross‑Border Retail Payments using UPI”, mandating that only banks authorised under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) may act as settlement agents. August 2022 – NPCI signed a Memorandum of Understanding with the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) for the UPI‑PayNow linkage, enabling instant person‑to‑person (P2P) transfers between India and Singapore with a transaction ceiling of SGD 5,000 (≈ ₹3 lakh). March 2023 – The UPI‑Bhutan corridor went live, allowing Indian tourists to pay at Bhutanese merchants using Indian UPI apps; settlement occurs in Indian Rupee via the Royal Monetary Authority of Bhutan’s correspondent bank.

Volume trends: In FY 2023‑24, cross‑border UPI transactions crossed 12 million, valued at USD 3.8 billion, with the UAE corridor accounting for 45 % of volume. The RBI’s 2024 “Vision 2025 for Payments” targets 50 million cross‑border UPI transactions annually by FY 2025‑26, and the inclusion of QR‑code interoperability with the European Payments Council’s SEPA Instant Credit Transfer framework.

Challenges and mitigations: (a) FX volatility – NIPL introduced a dynamic pricing engine that locks the exchange rate for 30 seconds at transaction initiation; (b) AML/CFT compliance – each corridor implements a joint Transaction Monitoring System (TMS) that screens against UN sanctions lists and the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) high‑risk jurisdictions; (c) Data localisation – the 2023 amendment to the Payment and Settlement Systems Act requires that transaction logs for cross‑border UPI be stored in India for a minimum of five years, while a mirrored copy may reside in the partner jurisdiction under a data‑sharing agreement.

Securities Transaction Tax Rationalisation and Stamp Duty Harmonisation

The Securities Transaction Tax (STT) was introduced in 2004 under the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2004 to curb speculative turnover and generate revenue. Over the years, the rates have been revised multiple times — most recently in the Finance Act, 2023 — to align with the evolving market microstructure (high‑frequency trading, algorithmic orders, and the shift to T+1 settlement). The current STT rates (effective 1 April 2023) are: 0.1 % on delivery‑based equity purchases, 0.025 % on equity intraday sales, 0.01 % on equity futures (seller), 0.05 % on equity options (seller, on premium), 0.001 % on debt securities (both sides), and 0.0001 % on mutual‑fund units (redemption).

Simultaneously, the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 was amended in 2019 (via the Finance Act, 2019) to bring stamp duty on securities under a uniform central levy collected by the stock exchanges and clearing corporations, replacing the erstwhile state‑wise rates. The harmonised rates (effective 1 July 2020) are: 0.005 % on equity delivery, 0.003 % on equity intraday, 0.002 % on equity futures, 0.003 % on equity options (on premium), 0.0001 % on debt instruments, and 0.00005 % on mutual‑fund units. The collection mechanism is integrated into the Clearing Corporation’s settlement cycle (T+1 for equities, T+0 for debt), ensuring that the duty is deducted before funds are released to the seller.

Rationale for rationalisation: (i) Revenue neutrality — the 2023 STT revision aimed to keep the combined STT + stamp duty burden roughly constant while shifting a larger share to STT (central revenue) and reducing stamp duty (state revenue) to simplify compliance; (ii) Behavioural neutrality — lower intraday STT (0.025 % vs 0.05 % pre‑2023) encourages liquidity provision by market makers; (iii) Alignment with T+1 — the stamp duty collection point moved from trade date to settlement date, matching the new settlement cycle and eliminating the “double‑count” risk that existed under T+2.

Impact assessment (based on NSE/BSE data for FY 2023‑24): Total STT collection rose 12 % YoY to ₹1.18 lakh crore, while stamp duty collection fell 8 % to ₹0.42 lakh crore, reflecting the rate shift. The average effective transaction cost for a retail equity delivery trade (including brokerage, STT, stamp duty, GST on brokerage, and exchange fees) declined from 0.45 % to 0.38 % of trade value. For high‑frequency traders, the reduction in intraday STT improved the economics of market‑making strategies, evidenced by a 15 % increase in quoted depth on the NSE’s order book.

Compliance Checklist for Market Participants

  • Verify that the trading system applies the correct STT rate based on trade type (delivery vs intraday) and instrument class.
  • Ensure stamp duty is calculated on the settlement value (not trade value) for T+1 trades.
  • Reconcile daily STT and stamp duty reports from the clearing corporation with internal ledgers before the T+1 settlement cut‑off (15:30 IST).
  • File the quarterly STT Return (Form STT‑1) with the Income Tax Department and the Stamp Duty Return with the respective state’s Collector of Stamps (centralised portal since 2021).

Regulatory Treatment of Virtual Digital Assets and the 2023 Taxation Framework

The term “Virtual Digital Asset” (VDA) was defined in the Finance Act, 2022 (Section 115BBH) as any information, code, number or token generated through cryptographic means, including cryptocurrencies, non‑fungible tokens (NFTs) and any other token of similar nature, but excluding central‑bank digital currency (CBDC) and certain loyalty points. The 2022 Act introduced a flat 30 % tax on income from transfer of VDAs, with no deduction of expenses except the cost of acquisition, and a 1 % TDS on consideration exceeding ₹10,000 in a financial year (Section 194S).

The Finance Act, 2023 refined the regime: (i) Loss set‑off restriction — losses from VDA transfers cannot be set off against any other income, nor carried forward; (ii) Gift taxation — VDAs received as gifts (above ₹50,000) are taxable in the hands of the recipient at the same 30 % rate; (iii) Reporting obligation — exchanges and intermediaries must furnish a Statement of Financial Transactions (SFT‑VDA) quarterly to the Income Tax Department, detailing user‑level buy/sell volumes, wallet addresses and KYC identifiers.

On the regulatory front, the RBI maintained its 2018 circular prohibiting banks from providing services to VDA entities, but the Supreme Court’s 2020 judgment in Internet and Mobile Association of India v. RBI set aside the ban, allowing regulated entities to engage provided they comply with AML/KYC norms. In 2023, the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) mutual evaluation praised India’s “Travel Rule” implementation for VDAs — exchanges must share originator and beneficiary information for transfers above USD 1,000.

Key compliance milestones: 1 April 2022 – TDS on VDA consideration becomes operational; 1 July 2022 – SFT‑VDA filing begins; 1 April 2023 – loss set‑off restriction and gift taxation take effect; October 2023 – SEBI issued a consultation paper on “Regulatory Framework for VDA‑linked Investment Products”, proposing that any collective investment scheme with > 20 % VDA exposure be classified as an Alternative Investment Fund (Category III) and subjected to the AIF Regulations, 2012.

Industry response: Major Indian exchanges (CoinDCX, WazirX, ZebPay) upgraded their KYC engines to capture Beneficial Ownership (BO) up to the natural‑person level and integrated with the CKYCR repository. The 1 % TDS led to a measurable drop in high‑frequency trading volumes (≈ 22 % decline in daily spot turnover on domestic exchanges in Q2 FY 2023‑24), while peer‑to‑peer (P2P) volumes migrated to offshore platforms, prompting the Enforcement Directorate to issue show‑cause notices to three offshore exchanges for FEMA violations.

Financial Market Infrastructure Interoperability: Linking CCPs, Depositories and Payment Systems

Interoperability among Financial Market Infrastructures (FMIs) — Central Counterparties (CCPs), Central Securities Depositories (CSDs) and Payment Systems — is a cornerstone of the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (PFMI) endorsed by the G20. In India, the roadmap was formalised in the RBI‑SEBI joint committee report of December 2021 and subsequently embedded in the Financial Market Infrastructure (FMI) Interoperability Framework released in March 2022.

The framework defines three interoperability layers: (i) Legal‑contractual layer — harmonised participation agreements, novation clauses and default‑management rules across CCPs (NSCCL, ICCL, MCX‑CCL) and the two CSDs (NSDL, CDSL); (ii) Technical‑messaging layer — adoption of ISO 20022 for all settlement instructions, enabling straight‑through processing (STP) from trade capture to final settlement; (iii) Liquidity‑bridge layer — a Common Collateral Management Platform (CCMP) that allows eligible securities (G‑Secs, high‑rated corporate bonds, T‑bills) to be pledged simultaneously to multiple CCPs and the RBI’s Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) without physical movement.

Implementation milestones: June 2022 — NSCCL and ICCL signed a Cross‑CCP Novation Agreement permitting a clearing member to net positions across the two equity CCPs, reducing margin duplication by an estimated ₹18,000 crore. January 2023 — The CCMP went live, integrating NSDL’s “Tri‑Party Repo” module with NSCCL’s margin engine; participants can now allocate a single pool of G‑Secs to meet margin calls at NSCCL, ICCL and the RBI’s Marginal Standing Facility (MSF). September 2023 — UPI‑Settlement Interface (UPI‑SI) was linked to the Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) system, enabling instant fund transfer from a clearing member’s settlement account to the CCP’s default fund during a stress event.

Risk‑management benefits quantified by the RBI’s 2024 Financial Stability Report: (a) Margin efficiency — average initial margin per clearing member fell 9 % due to cross‑CCP netting; (b) Settlement finality — T+1 equity settlement now achieves 99.97 % finality by 13:30 IST, down from 15:30 IST under the previous siloed model; (c) Systemic risk reduction — the probability of a simultaneous default of the two largest clearing members (as measured by the CoVaR metric) declined from 0.42 % to 0.28 %.

Outstanding issues: (i) Legal certainty of cross‑border novation — the 2022 agreement covers only domestic CCPs; a separate memorandum with the London Clearing House (LCH) and Eurex Clearing is under negotiation for G‑Sec repos. (iii) Operational resilience — the joint Business Continuity Plan (BCP) mandates a 4‑hour recovery time objective (RTO) for the CCMP; a 2023 fire‑drill at the NSDL data centre revealed a 6‑hour RTO, prompting a remediation plan due by March 2025.

Market Abuse, Surveillance, and SEBI's Enforcement Arsenal

Beyond basic insider trading, financial markets are susceptible to a broader spectrum of illicit activities collectively termed market abuse. These practices distort price discovery, erode investor confidence, and undermine market integrity. SEBI, as the primary regulator, employs a multi-pronged strategy encompassing advanced surveillance, robust regulations, and stringent enforcement actions to combat such abuses.

Types of Market Abuse:

  • Market Manipulation: This involves actions designed to artificially influence the price or volume of securities. Common forms include:
    • Pump and Dump Schemes: Artificially inflating the price of a stock through false and misleading statements to sell off holdings at a higher price.
    • Wash Trades: Simultaneously buying and selling the same security to create a misleading impression of activity, without any change in beneficial ownership.
    • Spoofing: Placing large orders with no intention of executing them, only to cancel them before execution, to create a false impression of demand or supply.
    • Front-Running: A broker or dealer executing orders on their own account ahead of a client's large order, to profit from the anticipated price movement.
  • Insider Trading: While already mentioned, it's crucial to reiterate its significance as the use of unpublished price-sensitive information (UPSI) for personal gain. SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015, are the primary framework.
  • Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices: A broad category covering any act or omission that operates as a fraud or deceit upon any person in connection with the purchase or sale of any security. This includes misrepresentation, omission of material facts, and misleading advertisements.

SEBI's Surveillance Mechanisms:

SEBI's Integrated Market Surveillance System (IMSS) is the backbone of its detection capabilities. It collects and analyses vast amounts of data from stock exchanges, depositories, and other market intermediaries. Advanced analytical tools, including artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms, are increasingly deployed to identify suspicious trading patterns, unusual price or volume movements, and linkages between various market entities. Cross-market surveillance, covering equity, debt, and derivatives segments, helps detect complex manipulative schemes that span multiple instruments.

Enforcement Actions:

Upon detection of suspicious activity, SEBI initiates investigations. Its enforcement powers are extensive and include:

  • Issuing Directions: Ordering market participants to cease and desist from certain activities, or to take corrective actions.
  • Imposing Penalties: Levying monetary fines, which can be substantial, under the SEBI Act and relevant regulations.
  • Disgorgement: Ordering the return of ill-gotten gains derived from illegal activities. This is a crucial tool to ensure that no one profits from market abuse.
  • Debarment: Prohibiting individuals or entities from accessing the securities market for a specified period. This can include banning them from trading, acting as intermediaries, or holding directorships in listed companies.
  • Suspension/Cancellation of Registration: For registered intermediaries, SEBI can suspend or cancel their certificate of registration.
  • Consent Orders: A mechanism where an entity or individual can settle alleged violations without admission or denial of guilt, by paying a monetary penalty, disgorging illegal gains, and/or undertaking certain remedial actions. This streamlines enforcement and conserves regulatory resources.
  • Criminal Prosecution: In severe cases of market fraud, SEBI can initiate criminal proceedings, often in coordination with other law enforcement agencies.

The effectiveness of SEBI's enforcement is critical for maintaining investor trust and ensuring the fair and efficient functioning of India's financial markets.

India's Macroprudential Framework and Financial Stability Oversight

While microprudential regulation focuses on the safety and soundness of individual financial institutions, macroprudential policy aims to mitigate systemic risk and safeguard the stability of the entire financial system. India has progressively strengthened its macroprudential framework, primarily driven by the lessons from global financial crises and the increasing interconnectedness of its financial sector.

Concept of Systemic Risk and Macroprudential Policy:

Systemic risk refers to the risk of disruption to financial services that is caused by an impairment of all or parts of the financial system and has the potential to have serious negative consequences for the real economy. Macroprudential policies are designed to build resilience in the financial system by addressing common exposures, procyclicality, and interconnectedness. Their objectives include limiting excessive credit growth, curbing maturity mismatches, and preventing asset bubbles.

Key Institutions and Coordination:

  • Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC): Chaired by the Union Finance Minister, the FSDC is the apex body for maintaining financial stability, enhancing inter-regulatory coordination, and promoting financial sector development. Its members include the heads of all financial sector regulators (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, IFSCA) and key government officials. The FSDC deliberates on macroprudential issues, early warning signals, and cross-sectoral vulnerabilities.
  • Financial Stability Unit (FSU) within RBI: This unit supports the FSDC by conducting financial stability assessments, stress testing, and preparing the half-yearly Financial Stability Report (FSR). The FSR provides a comprehensive assessment of the health of the Indian financial system, identifying risks and vulnerabilities.

Macroprudential Tools in India:

India employs a range of macroprudential tools, some of which are explicitly designated, while others serve a dual micro-macroprudential purpose:

  • Counter-Cyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB): This tool requires banks to hold additional capital during periods of excessive credit growth, which can be released during downturns. The RBI has operationalised the CCyB framework, but it has not been activated yet.
  • Sectoral Exposure Limits: Regulators impose limits on banks' and NBFCs' exposures to specific sectors (e.g., real estate, capital markets) to prevent concentration risks and asset bubbles.
  • Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratios: Caps on LTV ratios for housing loans and other asset-backed lending help to contain credit risk and reduce procyclicality in asset markets.
  • Systemically Important Financial Institutions (SIFIs) Framework: The RBI identifies Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D-SIBs) and subjects them to higher capital requirements and enhanced supervisory scrutiny, recognizing their potential to cause significant disruption if they fail. Similarly, Systemically Important NBFCs (NBFC-SI) are subject to stricter regulations.
  • Interconnectedness Analysis: The FSR regularly publishes network analysis diagrams illustrating the interconnectedness between various segments of the financial system (banks, NBFCs, mutual funds, insurance companies). This helps in identifying potential channels for contagion and concentration of risk.
  • System-Wide Stress Testing: Beyond individual entity stress tests, the RBI conducts system-wide stress tests to assess the resilience of the financial sector to various macroeconomic and financial shocks, including credit risk, interest rate risk, and liquidity risk scenarios.

The macroprudential framework is dynamic, continually evolving to address new risks and incorporate international best practices, aiming to foster a resilient and stable financial system capable of supporting sustainable economic growth.

Examiner's trap: Macroprudential vs. Microprudential

Consider the following statements regarding financial regulation in India:

  1. The primary objective of the Counter-Cyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB) is to ensure the solvency of individual banks.
  2. The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC) is responsible for the day-to-day supervision of Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs).
  3. Setting a Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio for housing loans is an example of a macroprudential tool.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(A) 1 and 2 only

(B) 3 only

(C) 1 and 3 only

(D) 1, 2 and 3

Statement 1 is incorrect. While CCyB does involve capital, its primary objective is macroprudential: to build buffers during good times to absorb losses during bad times for the *entire banking system*, thereby mitigating systemic risk and procyclicality, not just the solvency of an individual bank in isolation.

Statement 2 is incorrect. The FSDC is an apex body for financial stability and inter-regulatory coordination. The day-to-day supervision of NBFCs falls under the purview of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).

Statement 3 is correct. LTV ratios, when applied broadly across the housing market, are considered a macroprudential tool. They help to contain credit risk in a specific sector, reduce procyclicality in lending, and prevent the build-up of asset bubbles, thus contributing to overall financial stability.

Therefore, the correct answer is (B) 3 only.

Distinguishing Regulatory Tools
When encountering questions about financial regulation, always ask whether the tool's primary impact is on an individual entity's soundness (microprudential) or on the stability of the entire financial system (macroprudential). Many tools have both micro and macro implications, but their *primary intent* often helps in classification. For instance, capital adequacy norms for individual banks are microprudential, but a Counter-Cyclical Capital Buffer applied across the system is macroprudential.

GIFT City IFSC: India's Global Financial Gateway

The Gujarat International Finance Tec-City (GIFT City) International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) represents India's ambitious endeavour to establish a global financial hub within its own geographical boundaries. Conceived as a special economic zone (SEZ) with a distinct regulatory and legal framework, GIFT IFSC aims to facilitate cross-border financial services and attract foreign capital, positioning India as a competitive player in the global financial landscape.

Genesis and Vision:

The idea of an IFSC in India emerged from the need to capture a share of the offshore financial services market, which Indian entities often accessed through foreign jurisdictions. GIFT City was envisioned to provide world-class infrastructure and a business-friendly environment to offer financial services to non-residents and residents in foreign currency, thereby bringing back financial flows and associated economic activity to India.

Regulatory Framework: IFSCA:

A pivotal step in establishing GIFT IFSC was the creation of the International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) in April 2020. The IFSCA is a unified regulator for all financial services and products in an IFSC, consolidating the powers of all domestic financial regulators (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA) within GIFT City. This single-window regulatory approach aims to provide regulatory certainty, ease of doing business, and a globally benchmarked framework. The IFSCA Act, 2019, provides its statutory mandate.

Key Activities and Products Offered:

GIFT IFSC offers a wide array of financial services and products, including:

  • Banking: IFSC Banking Units (IBUs) of Indian and foreign banks offer foreign currency denominated loans, deposits, and other banking services to residents and non-residents.
  • Capital Markets: Two international exchanges, India International Exchange (India INX) and NSE International Exchange (NSE IX), facilitate trading in equities, derivatives (including Nifty derivatives), commodities, and bonds, primarily in foreign currency. Global Depository Receipts (GDRs) and American Depository Receipts (ADRs) can be issued and listed here.
  • Insurance: IFSC Insurance Offices (IIOs) offer various insurance and reinsurance products, including global insurance covers and captive insurance.
  • Asset Management: Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), Mutual Funds, and Portfolio Management Services (PMS) can be set up in GIFT IFSC to pool capital from global investors for investments in India and abroad.
  • Aircraft Leasing: A significant focus has been on developing an aircraft leasing and financing hub, offering tax advantages and regulatory ease for lessors.
  • Fintech Hub: A dedicated sandbox and innovation hub encourage fintech innovation, particularly in areas like green finance, sustainable finance, and digital assets.

Advantages and Incentives:

GIFT IFSC offers several incentives to attract participants:

  • Tax Benefits: Long-term tax exemptions, including a 100% income tax exemption for 10 consecutive years for IFSC units, exemption from STT, CTT, GST on certain transactions, and stamp duty concessions.
  • Regulatory Ease: A liberalised regulatory regime with simplified compliance procedures and a single regulator.
  • Currency Convertibility: Transactions are primarily denominated in foreign currency, allowing participants to operate without currency conversion risks associated with the Indian Rupee.
  • Dispute Resolution: An international arbitration centre is being established to provide efficient dispute resolution mechanisms.

GIFT IFSC is crucial for India's capital account liberalisation journey, facilitating both inbound and outbound financial flows, and enhancing India's integration with global financial markets.

The Evolving Ecosystem for Stressed Asset Resolution

The resolution of stressed assets, particularly Non-Performing Assets (NPAs) in the banking sector, is critical for financial stability and credit growth. India has significantly reformed its framework for stressed asset resolution, moving from a fragmented approach to a more comprehensive and market-driven ecosystem, with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) as its cornerstone.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016:

The IBC introduced a time-bound, creditor-driven insolvency resolution process for corporate persons, partnership firms, and individuals. Its primary objective is resolution, failing which, liquidation. The Code has significantly improved recovery rates and reduced resolution timelines compared to previous mechanisms. It provides for a Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) and a fast-track process, overseen by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT).

Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs):

ARCs are specialised financial institutions that acquire NPAs from banks and financial institutions, primarily under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest (SARFAESI) Act, 2002. Their business model involves acquiring stressed assets at a discount, restructuring them, and recovering dues through various means, including enforcement of security interests, debt restructuring, or sale of assets. ARCs issue Security Receipts (SRs) to banks, which are tradable instruments representing an undivided interest in the financial assets acquired by the ARC. SEBI regulates the issuance and trading of these SRs when offered to qualified institutional buyers.

National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL) and India Debt Resolution Company Limited (IDRCL):

To address the legacy NPAs, particularly large and complex accounts, the government operationalised a "Bad Bank" structure comprising NARCL and IDRCL. NARCL, an ARC, acquires stressed assets from banks, while IDRCL, an Asset Management Company (AMC), manages these assets and works towards their resolution. NARCL acquires assets by offering 15% cash and 85% in Security Receipts guaranteed by the government. This mechanism aims to free up bank capital, clean up balance sheets, and facilitate faster resolution of large stressed accounts, thereby boosting credit flow.

Secondary Market for Stressed Assets:

The development of a robust secondary market for stressed assets is crucial for efficient price discovery and liquidity. While ARCs primarily drive this market, there is potential for greater participation from Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs) and other distressed asset investors. Challenges include valuation complexities, information asymmetry, and the need for a deeper pool of sophisticated investors. Regulatory efforts are focused on standardising valuation practices and enhancing transparency to facilitate secondary market transactions.

Other Resolution Mechanisms:

  • Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs): Established under the Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 1993, DRTs provide a forum for banks and financial institutions to recover their dues from defaulters.
  • Lok Adalats: For smaller NPA accounts, Lok Adalats offer an alternative dispute resolution mechanism, facilitating amicable settlements.
  • Out-of-Court Settlements and Restructuring: Banks also engage in bilateral negotiations, one-time settlements (OTS), and corporate debt restructuring (CDR) mechanisms, though the IBC has largely superseded the formal CDR framework.

The evolving ecosystem aims to create a more efficient, transparent, and time-bound process for stressed asset resolution, fostering a healthier credit environment and supporting economic growth.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the primary objective of macroprudential tools (systemic stability) with microprudential tools (individual entity soundness).
  • Assuming the FSDC is an operational regulator rather than an apex coordination body.
  • Incorrectly attributing the day-to-day supervision of financial entities in GIFT City to domestic regulators (RBI, SEBI) instead of IFSCA.
  • Believing that all transactions in GIFT City IFSC must be in Indian Rupees; they are primarily in foreign currency.
  • Overlooking the government guarantee component for Security Receipts issued by NARCL, which is a key differentiator.
  • Mistaking the primary role of IDRCL as asset acquisition rather than asset management and resolution for NARCL.
  • Applying the same rules for market abuse detection and enforcement across all financial markets globally, without considering India's specific SEBI regulations and surveillance mechanisms.

Digital Rupee (CBDC) Pilot: Design, Distribution and Monetary Policy Implications

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) launched the wholesale Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC‑W) pilot on 1 November 2022, restricting participation to nine scheduled commercial banks for settlement of secondary‑market government securities. The retail CBDC‑R pilot followed on 1 December 2023, initially covering four cities — Mumbai, New Delhi, Bengaluru and Bhubaneswar — and later expanding to 26 cities by March 2024. Both pilots use a token‑based, account‑less architecture built on a permissioned distributed ledger operated by the RBI, with commercial banks acting as intermediaries for onboarding end‑users.

Key Design Choices

  • Two‑tier model: The RBI issues CBDC to banks (wholesale) or to banks for onward distribution to customers (retail). Banks hold CBDC in their RBI‑maintained wallets and manage customer‑facing wallets.
  • No interest bearing: CBDC‑R is non‑interest‑bearing, preserving the existing monetary transmission channel through bank deposits.
  • Programmability: The platform supports conditional payments (e.g., targeted subsidies, expiry‑dated vouchers) without altering the underlying token.
  • Offline capability: A limited offline mode using near‑field communication (NFC) allows peer‑to‑peer transfers when connectivity is unavailable, capped at ₹2,000 per transaction and ₹10,000 daily per wallet.

Monetary Policy Implications

Because CBDC is a direct liability of the central bank, large‑scale substitution of bank deposits could reduce the banking system’s funding base, compressing net interest margins and potentially weakening credit creation. The RBI has therefore calibrated the pilot with strict holding limits (₹1 lakh per retail wallet) and a zero‑interest design to discourage disintermediation. The pilot also provides real‑time data on currency velocity, enabling finer‑grained assessment of money‑demand functions. Internationally, the RBI participates in the BIS Innovation Hub’s Project mBridge to test cross‑border CBDC interoperability, which could reduce reliance on correspondent banking corridors for trade settlement.

Operational Risks and Mitigation

  • Cyber resilience: The permissioned ledger is hardened against double‑spend attacks; the RBI mandates quarterly penetration testing for all participating banks.
  • Legal tender status: The Finance Act 2023 amended the RBI Act to recognise CBDC as legal tender, ensuring enforceability of contracts denominated in digital rupee.
  • Interoperability with UPI: A unified QR code standard allows merchants to accept both UPI and CBDC‑R payments without separate infrastructure.

Social Stock Exchange: Regulatory Architecture, Eligibility and Capital Mobilisation for Non‑Profits

SEBI notified the Social Stock Exchange (SSE) framework on 25 July 2022, creating a dedicated segment within existing stock exchanges (BSE and NSE) for fundraising by social enterprises. The SSE enables Not‑for‑Profit Organisations (NPOs) and for‑profit social enterprises to list instruments such as Zero‑Coupon Zero‑Principal (ZCZP) bonds, equity, and development impact bonds. As of March 2024, 12 NPOs have raised ₹1,850 crore through the SSE.

Eligibility and Registration

  • NPOs: Must be registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860; the Indian Trusts Act, 1882; or Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013, with a minimum three‑year track record and at least ₹50 lakh annual expenditure.
  • For‑profit social enterprises: Must demonstrate that at least 67 % of their activities target a defined social objective (education, health, livelihood, environment) as per the SEBI‑prescribed taxonomy.
  • Registration with SSE: Entities obtain a “Social Enterprise” tag after due diligence by the exchange, valid for three years, renewable subject to continued compliance.

Instrument Design and Disclosure

ZCZP bonds are the flagship instrument: they carry no coupon and no principal repayment; investors receive a social‑impact certificate and tax benefits under Section 80G of the Income‑Tax Act. Development Impact Bonds (DIBs) link financial returns to verified outcomes measured by independent evaluators. Continuous disclosure requirements include an Annual Impact Report (AIR) audited by a SEBI‑registered impact auditor, quarterly fund‑utilisation statements, and a governance report covering board composition and conflict‑of‑interest policies.

Market Development Measures

  • Tax incentives: The Finance Act 2023 extended 100 % deduction for donations to SSE‑listed NPOs, and allowed corporate donors to claim CSR expenditure against SSE subscriptions.
  • Investor base: Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs), High‑Net‑Worth Individuals (HNIs), and retail investors (minimum ₹10,000) can participate. Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) are permitted subject to KYC and beneficial‑ownership norms.
  • Liquidity support: Exchanges operate a market‑making framework for ZCZP bonds, with designated market makers providing two‑way quotes for a minimum of 5 % of the issue size.

A question asks: "Which of the following statements about the Social Stock Exchange (SSE) in India is NOT correct? (a) NPOs must have a minimum three‑year track record and ₹50 lakh annual expenditure. (b) Zero‑Coupon Zero‑Principal bonds carry a fixed coupon payable annually. (c) Development Impact Bonds link financial returns to verified social outcomes. (d) Foreign Portfolio Investors can participate subject to KYC norms."
Statement (b) is incorrect. ZCZP bonds, by definition, carry **no coupon** and **no principal repayment**; investors receive only a social‑impact certificate and tax benefits. All other statements are accurate per the SEBI SSE framework notified in July 2022 and the Finance Act 2023 amendments.

Resolution Framework for Financial Service Providers under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) was amended in August 2021 to bring Financial Service Providers (FSPs) — including NBFCs, housing finance companies, and asset reconstruction companies — within a dedicated resolution framework. Prior to this, FSPs were excluded from the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) and could only be wound up under sector‑specific statutes. The new framework, operationalised through the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Insolvency and Liquidation Proceedings of Financial Service Providers and Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2021, empowers the RBI to initiate resolution proceedings for systemically important FSPs (those with asset size ≥ ₹500 crore or as notified).

Initiation and Adjudication

  • RBI as the sole applicant: Only the Reserve Bank can file an application before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) for an FSP, after satisfying itself that the entity is unable to pay its debts and that resolution is in the public interest.
  • Moratorium: Upon admission, a 180‑day moratorium (extendable by 90 days) halts all recovery actions, including enforcement of security interests under SARFAESI.
  • Resolution Professional (RP): The NCLT appoints an RP from the panel maintained by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI); the RP must have financial‑sector expertise.

Resolution Plan and Creditor Voting

The RP prepares a resolution plan that may involve capital infusion, asset sale, or merger with a healthier entity. The plan requires approval by a 66 % voting share of the Committee of Creditors (CoC), which comprises financial creditors (banks, debenture holders, depositors) and operational creditors above a prescribed threshold. Depositors are treated as financial creditors, a significant departure from the corporate CIRP where they are often operational creditors.

Liquidation and Deposit Insurance

If resolution fails, the NCLT orders liquidation. The Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) pays insured depositors up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, and the liquidator distributes remaining assets per the waterfall: insolvency resolution costs, secured creditors, workmen’s dues, unsecured financial creditors, government dues, and finally equity holders. The framework also provides for a “bridge institution” — a temporary entity capitalised by the RBI — to maintain critical payment functions during transition.

Corporate Governance Enhancements: Independent Directors, Related‑Party Transactions and the SEBI LODR Amendments

SEBI’s Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements (LODR) Regulations have been tightened through a series of amendments in 2021, 2022 and 2023 to align Indian corporate governance with global best practices. The key thrust areas are independence of directors, scrutiny of related‑party transactions (RPTs), and enhanced disclosure of material events.

Independent Director Norms

  • Tenure cap: An independent director may serve a maximum of two consecutive terms of five years each; a cooling‑off period of three years is mandatory before re‑appointment.
  • Skill matrix disclosure: Boards must publish a director‑skill matrix mapping each director’s expertise against the company’s strategic priorities.
  • Separate meeting: Independent directors must hold at least one meeting annually without the presence of non‑independent directors or management, and the minutes must be filed with the stock exchange.

Related‑Party Transaction Framework

The 2022 amendment lowered the materiality threshold for RPTs from 10 % of consolidated turnover to 5 % of the listed entity’s annual consolidated turnover, or ₹1,000 crore, whichever is lower. All material RPTs now require prior approval of the audit committee and, for transactions exceeding the threshold, shareholder approval via a special resolution. The definition of “related party” was expanded to include entities in which a promoter or director holds ≥ 20 % voting rights, and to cover transactions with the promoter group even if not directly with the listed entity.

Material Event Disclosure and ESG Integration

  • Timelines: Material events must be disclosed within 24 hours (previously 48 hours) of occurrence; the definition now explicitly includes cyber‑security incidents, regulatory orders affecting business, and changes in credit rating.
  • Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR): From FY 2023‑24, the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation must file BRSR Core, a quantified ESG dataset assured by an independent third party.
  • Whistle‑blower mechanism: Companies must establish a vigil mechanism with direct access to the audit committee chair; anonymity and protection against retaliation are mandated.

Basel III Implementation in India: Capital Buffers, Leverage Ratio and Impact on NBFC Credit Growth

The RBI adopted the Basel III capital framework in a phased manner beginning April 2013, with full convergence achieved by March 2020. The framework mandates a Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) minimum of 5.5 %, a Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) of 2.5 % (fully phased in by March 2020), a Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB) ranging 0‑2.5 % (currently at 0 %), and a minimum Leverage Ratio of 3.5 % for domestic systemically important banks (D‑SIBs) and 3 % for other banks.

Buffer Mechanics and Distribution Constraints

When a bank’s CET1 ratio falls within the CCB range (5.5 %–8 %), the RBI imposes progressive restrictions on dividend payouts, share buybacks, and discretionary bonus payments. The distribution constraint formula is: Maximum distributable amount = (CET1 ratio – 5.5 %) / 2.5 % × eligible profits. This mechanism preserves capital during stress without triggering immediate regulatory action.

Impact on NBFCs and Credit Flow

  • Bank‑NBFC linkage: Banks are the primary source of funding for NBFCs (≈ 55 % of NBFC borrowings as of March 2023). Higher capital buffers increase banks’ cost of funds, which is transmitted to NBFC borrowing rates.
  • Risk‑weight adjustments: The RBI assigned a 100 % risk weight to bank exposures to NBFCs rated below investment grade, and 20 % for those rated AAA, incentivising banks to lend to higher‑rated NBFCs.
  • Scale‑Based Regulation (SBR) for NBFCs: Effective October 2022, NBFCs are classified into Base, Middle, Upper and Top layers. Upper‑layer NBFCs (asset size ≥ ₹1 lakh crore or as designated) must maintain a CET1 of 9 % and a leverage ratio of 4 %, aligning them closer to bank standards.

Countercyclical Buffer Activation

The RBI retains discretion to activate the CCyB when credit‑to‑GDP gaps exceed the long‑term trend. As of the latest Monetary Policy Report (April 2024), the gap remains negative, so the CCyB stays at 0 %. Activation would raise the effective CET1 requirement by up to 2.5 %, further tightening lending capacity during credit booms.
