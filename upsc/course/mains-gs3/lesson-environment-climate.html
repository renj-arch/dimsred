1. International Climate Commitments and India's National Policies

1.1 Evolution of Global Climate Agreements

International climate governance began in earnest with the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) in 1992, which established the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities. The Kyoto Protocol of 1997 introduced legally binding emission reduction targets for Annex I countries, while the Paris Agreement of 2015 shifted the architecture to nationally determined contributions (NDCs) and a long‑term goal of limiting global warming to well below 2 °C, preferably 1.5 °C, above pre‑industrial levels. These milestones matter because they create a normative framework that shapes national legislation, fiscal incentives, and the strategic direction of climate‑related research and development.

1.2 India's NDCs and Long‑Term Low‑Emission Development Strategy (LT‑LEDS)

India’s first NDC, submitted in 2015, pledged to reduce the emissions intensity of its GDP by 33‑35 % to 2005 levels by 2030, achieve 40 % cumulative electric power capacity from non‑fossil fuel sources, and create an additional carbon sink of 2.5–3 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent through afforestation. The LT‑LEDS, released in 2019, expands these goals by outlining sector‑specific pathways for power, transport, industry, agriculture and waste. The strategy emphasizes the triple‑bottom‑line of mitigation, adaptation and sustainable development, thereby linking climate action to poverty alleviation, energy security and job creation.

1.3 Institutional Architecture for Climate Action

The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) coordinates climate policy, while the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) implements renewable energy programmes. The National Clean Energy Fund (NCEF), established under the Ministry of Finance, mobilises resources from a cess on coal to finance research, pilot projects and capacity building. The Climate Change Council, chaired by the Prime Minister, provides high‑level oversight and integrates climate considerations across ministries. These institutions are crucial because they ensure policy coherence, monitor progress against NDCs and facilitate inter‑governmental coordination.

1.4 Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) Mechanisms

India’s MRV framework rests on the National Inventory Report (NIR) submitted to the UNFCCC, the State‑wise Emission Inventory System (SEIS) and the Integrated Monitoring and Evaluation System (IMES) for renewable energy. Robust MRV is essential to maintain credibility in international negotiations, attract climate finance and guide corrective policy measures. The use of satellite‑based remote sensing for forest cover, combined with ground‑level verification, exemplifies the integration of technology into MRV.

2. Conservation Law, Biodiversity Protection and Institutional Framework

2.1 Constitutional Foundations and Key Legislation

Article 48A of the Constitution directs the State to protect and improve the environment, while Article 51A(g) imposes a fundamental duty on citizens to protect the natural environment. The cornerstone statutes include the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, which creates protected areas, regulates hunting and trade in wildlife, and establishes the National Board for Wildlife; the Forest Conservation Act, 1980, which restricts diversion of forest land for non‑forest uses; and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, which creates the National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) to oversee access and benefit‑sharing of genetic resources. These legal instruments provide the basis for judicial intervention and administrative enforcement.

2.2 Protected Area Network and Its Effectiveness

India’s protected area network comprises National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, Conservation Reserves and Community Reserves, covering roughly 5 % of the land area. The Tiger Reserve network, established under Project Tiger (1973), has expanded to 53 reserves, contributing to a steady increase in tiger numbers as recorded in the All‑India Tiger Estimation. Effectiveness is measured through indicators such as species population trends, habitat connectivity and community participation. The establishment of corridors, for example the Eastern Himalayan corridor, mitigates fragmentation and enhances genetic flow.

2.3 Community‑Based Conservation and Indigenous Rights

Recognizing the role of local communities, the Forest Rights Act, 2006 (FRA) grants forest‑dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest users legal rights over land and resources. The FRA has facilitated joint forest management, where community forest committees co‑manage resources, leading to improved forest regeneration in several states. However, implementation challenges such as delayed title issuance and conflicts with commercial interests persist, highlighting the need for capacity building and transparent grievance redressal.

2.4 International Conventions and Their Domestic Implementation

India is a signatory to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) and the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands. The CBD’s Aichi Targets have been incorporated into the National Biodiversity Action Plan, while CITES listings guide the regulation of wildlife trade through the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau. The Ramsar sites, such as Chilika Lake, receive special attention for wetland conservation, balancing ecological functions with livelihood needs.

3. Pollution Control: Sources, Impacts and Regulatory Framework

3.1 Air Pollution – Sources and Health Burden

India’s major sources of ambient air pollution include thermal power plants, vehicular emissions, industrial processes, agricultural residue burning and construction dust. The resulting fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) exceeds the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline of 10 µg m⁻³ in most urban centers, contributing to an estimated premature mortality of several hundred thousand deaths annually. The health burden manifests as respiratory diseases, cardiovascular events and reduced life expectancy, creating a substantial economic cost measured in lost productivity and healthcare expenditure.

3.2 Water Pollution – Industrial Discharges and Agricultural Run‑off

Industrial effluents containing heavy metals, dyes and organic solvents, combined with nutrient‑rich agricultural run‑off, degrade riverine and groundwater quality. The Ganga and Yamuna basins illustrate the cumulative impact of untreated sewage, leading to eutrophication, loss of aquatic biodiversity and unsafe drinking water for millions. The National River Ganga (Rejuvenation) Programme seeks to address these challenges through sewage treatment infrastructure, industrial effluent monitoring and community participation.

3.3 Soil Contamination and Solid Waste Management

Improper disposal of municipal solid waste, hazardous industrial waste and the use of contaminated sludge as fertilizer result in soil pollution. Heavy metals such as lead, cadmium and arsenic accumulate in the food chain, posing chronic health risks. The Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016, mandate segregation at source, scientific disposal and the promotion of waste‑to‑energy technologies. Successful implementation in cities like Pune demonstrates the feasibility of integrated waste management systems.

3.4 Regulatory Instruments and Enforcement Agencies

The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) formulates standards for air, water and noise, while State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) enforce compliance through permits, inspections and penalties. The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 and the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 provide the legal basis for emission standards and effluent discharge limits. The National Green Tribunal (NGT), established in 2010, offers a specialized judicial forum for speedy resolution of environmental disputes, thereby strengthening enforcement.

4. Environmental Finance: Funding Mechanisms and Market Instruments

4.1 Domestic Climate and Conservation Funds

The National Clean Energy Fund (NCEF) collects a cess of one rupee per tonne of coal produced and directs the proceeds to research, demonstration projects and capacity building in renewable energy and energy efficiency. The Green Climate Fund (GCF) India Portfolio, though an international mechanism, is accessed through the Ministry of Finance and the MoEFCC, channeling concessional finance for adaptation and mitigation projects. The National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) supports state‑level adaptation initiatives, particularly in vulnerable sectors such as agriculture and coastal management.

4.2 Market‑Based Instruments: Carbon Pricing and Emissions Trading

India has experimented with a Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme for energy‑intensive industries, wherein energy‑saving certificates are allocated based on baseline consumption and tradable on a regulated market. While a national carbon tax has not yet been instituted, the proposed carbon pricing framework aims to internalise the external cost of greenhouse gas emissions, encouraging low‑carbon technologies and generating revenue for climate finance.

4.3 Green Bonds and Sustainable Finance

In recent years, Indian corporations and municipalities have issued green bonds to raise capital for renewable energy, clean transport and water infrastructure projects. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) issued guidelines in 2017 that define eligibility criteria, disclosure requirements and third‑party verification, thereby enhancing investor confidence. Green bond proceeds are tracked through dedicated reporting, ensuring that funds are allocated to environmentally beneficial assets.

4.4 International Climate Finance and Technology Transfer

Under the Paris Agreement, developed countries are expected to mobilise $100 billion annually for climate action in developing nations. India receives bilateral and multilateral assistance through mechanisms such as the Climate Investment Funds (CIF), the Adaptation Fund and the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP). Technology transfer is facilitated by the International Solar Alliance (ISA), which pools resources to promote solar deployment in sun‑rich countries, and by the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) projects that generate Certified Emission Reductions (CERs) for export.

Example Question

Explain how the Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme contributes to India’s climate mitigation goals. In your answer, discuss the mechanism, its impact on energy efficiency, and any challenges faced in its implementation.

The PAT scheme, launched in 2012, is a market‑based mechanism that sets specific energy‑saving targets (Perform) for designated energy‑intensive industries such as cement, steel and textiles. Each designated consumer receives a baseline allocation of energy‑saving certificates (ESCs) based on historical consumption (Achieve). If a plant exceeds its target, it can trade surplus ESCs to other plants that fall short, creating a financial incentive for over‑achievement (Trade). This tradable credit system encourages firms to adopt energy‑efficient technologies, such as high‑efficiency motors, waste heat recovery and process optimisation, thereby reducing overall industrial energy intensity.

Impact assessment shows a cumulative reduction of several million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent since inception, contributing directly to India’s NDC target of reducing emissions intensity of GDP. However, challenges include accurate baseline determination, verification of actual savings, and ensuring that the market for ESCs remains liquid. Addressing these issues requires robust monitoring, transparent reporting and capacity building among participating firms.

Exam‑Day Insight

When answering a question on environmental finance, link the source of funds (e.g., NCEF cess, green bonds) to a specific outcome (e.g., renewable capacity added, adaptation projects funded). This demonstrates an integrated understanding of how financing mechanisms translate into tangible environmental benefits.

5. Integrated Approaches and Future Directions

5.1 Climate‑Resilient Development Planning

Integrating climate considerations into sectoral planning ensures that mitigation and adaptation are not treated as add‑ons but as core components of development. For instance, the National Solar Mission aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by promoting clean energy, creating jobs and reducing air pollution. Similarly, climate‑smart agriculture combines water‑efficient irrigation, drought‑resistant crop varieties and soil carbon sequestration, thereby enhancing food security while lowering emissions.

5.2 Strengthening Institutional Coordination

Effective environmental governance requires coordination among ministries, state agencies, local bodies and civil society. The Inter‑Ministerial Committee on Climate Change, chaired by the Prime Minister, serves as a platform for policy alignment, while the State Climate Change Action Plans (SCCAPs) translate national targets into state‑specific actions. Enhancing data sharing, joint monitoring and capacity building across these layers will improve policy implementation and accountability.

5.3 Leveraging Technology for Monitoring and Enforcement

Advances in remote sensing, Internet of Things (IoT) sensors and artificial intelligence enable real‑time monitoring of air and water quality, forest cover and emissions. The National Air Quality Index (AQI) portal, powered by satellite data and ground stations, provides public access to pollution levels, fostering transparency and citizen participation. Similarly, blockchain‑based traceability of carbon credits can reduce fraud and improve the credibility of market‑based mechanisms.

5.4 Enhancing Public Participation and Environmental Justice

Community involvement is pivotal for the sustainability of conservation and pollution control initiatives. The Right to Information (RTI) Act empowers citizens to demand data on environmental clearances, while public interest litigations (PILs) have been instrumental in enforcing compliance, as seen in landmark cases concerning the Ganga’s pollution. Ensuring that vulnerable groups have a voice in decision‑making mitigates the risk of environmental injustice and builds broader societal support for climate action.

5.5 Outlook: Towards a Low‑Carbon, Clean‑Growth Economy

India’s trajectory towards a low‑carbon economy hinges on scaling renewable energy, enhancing energy efficiency, protecting biodiversity and curbing pollution through stringent regulation and innovative financing. The convergence of policy ambition, institutional capacity and technological innovation creates a pathway to meet international commitments while delivering inclusive development. Continuous evaluation, adaptive management and inclusive governance will be essential to navigate the complex trade‑offs inherent in climate, conservation and pollution challenges.

Environmental Impact Assessment Reforms: 2020 Notification, Post‑Clearance Monitoring and Judicial Scrutiny

Key Procedural Shifts

The Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) Notification, 2006 was substantially amended in March 2020. The amendment re‑classified projects into Category A (requiring central clearance) and Category B (state‑level clearance) and introduced a new “Category B2” that exempts certain projects from public consultation altogether. Projects such as inland waterways, expansion of existing highways up to 100 km, and certain building construction projects above 20,000 sq m now fall under B2, removing the mandatory public hearing that was a cornerstone of the 2006 regime.

Post‑Facto Clearance and Validity Extensions

A controversial provision allows “post‑facto clearance” for projects that have commenced construction without prior environmental clearance, provided they apply within a stipulated window and meet prescribed conditions. The 2020 notification also extended the validity of environmental clearances from five to ten years for river valley and hydro‑electric projects, and from five to seven years for mining projects, reducing the frequency of renewal scrutiny.

Post‑Clearance Monitoring Mechanism

To compensate for reduced front‑end scrutiny, the notification strengthened the post‑clearance monitoring framework. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) now requires six‑monthly compliance reports from project proponents, and the State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) are mandated to conduct site inspections at least once a year. Non‑compliance can trigger penalties under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, including directions to stop work and impose environmental compensation.

Judicial Response

The Supreme Court, in Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) and later in Goa Foundation v. Union of India (2021), reiterated that public participation is a non‑derogable component of environmental decision‑making. In 2022, the National Green Tribunal (NGT) struck down the exemption of certain linear projects from public hearing, holding that the 2020 amendment violated the principle of “prior informed consent” embedded in the Rio Declaration. The MoEFCC subsequently issued an office memorandum (OM) in 2023 reinstating public consultation for projects with significant ecological footprints, illustrating the dynamic interplay between executive notification and judicial oversight.

Wetland Conservation Framework: Ramsar Sites, State Wetland Authorities and the 2017 Rules

Legal Architecture

The Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 replaced the 2010 rules and operationalised the definition of “wetland” to include marshes, fens, peatlands, and human‑made water bodies such as reservoirs and paddy fields, provided they meet ecological criteria. The rules mandate the constitution of a State Wetland Authority (SWA) in every state and union territory, chaired by the Chief Minister or a designated minister, with representation from forest, revenue, water resources, and tribal welfare departments.

Ramsar Designation and National Inventory

As of 2024, India hosts 75 Ramsar sites covering approximately 1.3 million hectares. The 2017 rules require each SWA to prepare a “Wetland Inventory” and a “Wetland Management Plan” for every notified wetland within two years of notification. The Central Wetlands Regulatory Authority (CWRA) monitors compliance and can recommend de‑notification if a wetland loses its ecological character.

Prohibited and Regulated Activities

The rules prohibit conversion of wetlands for non‑wetland uses, discharge of untreated sewage, and dumping of solid waste. Regulated activities—such as sustainable fisheries, eco‑tourism, and traditional agriculture—require prior approval from the SWA. The 2021 amendment introduced a “wise use” clause, allowing limited developmental activities if an Environmental Management Plan demonstrates no net loss of wetland functions.

Implementation Gaps

Despite the framework, a 2022 Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) report highlighted that only 18 states had functional SWAs, and less than 30 % of notified wetlands had approved management plans. Encroachment, untreated industrial effluents, and climate‑induced hydrological changes remain primary threats. The NGT has issued multiple directions to states to expedite inventory preparation and enforce buffer zones, underscoring the enforcement deficit.

Examiner's Twist: Linking Notification Provisions to Judicial Outcomes
A question asks: “Critically examine how the 2020 EIA amendment’s exemption of linear projects from public hearing has been addressed by the judiciary. Cite at least one tribunal order and one Supreme Court principle.”
The 2020 amendment placed certain linear projects (e.g., highway expansion up to 100 km) under Category B2, removing mandatory public hearings. The NGT, in Goa Foundation v. Union of India (2022), quashed this exemption, ruling that public participation is a procedural safeguard derived from the “precautionary principle” and “prior informed consent” recognised in the Rio Declaration and reiterated by the Supreme Court in Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996). The MoEFCC’s 2023 OM reinstated hearings for projects with significant ecological impact, demonstrating executive response to judicial interpretation. A high‑scoring answer will map the statutory change, the tribunal’s reasoning, the Supreme Court precedent, and the subsequent policy correction.
Answer‑Writing Strategy
When a question asks for “critical examination”, structure the response in three layers: (1) statutory provision, (2) judicial pronouncement with case name and year, (3) executive or legislative follow‑up. Use a table if space permits to contrast pre‑ and post‑amendment positions. This demonstrates both legal literacy and awareness of the living constitutional dialogue.

Graded Response Action Plan and City‑Level Air Quality Management: Institutional Design and Effectiveness

GRAP Architecture

The Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) was notified by the MoEFCC in January 2017 for the National Capital Region (NCR) and adjoining areas. It operationalises a four‑stage response linked to the Air Quality Index (AQI): Stage 1 (Moderate, AQI 101‑200), Stage 2 (Poor, 201‑300), Stage 3 (Very Poor, 301‑400), and Stage 4 (Severe, 401‑500). Each stage triggers a predefined set of measures—ranging from mechanised sweeping and water sprinkling (Stage 1) to a ban on construction, closure of brick kilns, and odd‑even vehicle rationing (Stage 4).

Institutional Coordination

The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) acts as the nodal agency, issuing daily AQI bulletins and activating the relevant stage. The Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM), established in 2021, supersedes the earlier Environment Pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority (EPCA) and possesses statutory powers to direct state governments, municipal corporations, and industrial units. State‑level “City Action Plans” (CAPs) are required under the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) for 132 non‑attainment cities, aligning local measures with GRAP thresholds.

Effectiveness Assessment

Studies by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM) and the Centre for Science and Environment (CSE) indicate that GRAP activation during the 2022‑23 winter reduced peak PM2.5 concentrations by 10‑15 % relative to a no‑intervention baseline. However, compliance remains uneven: brick‑kiln closures are often delayed, and enforcement of the odd‑even scheme suffers from exemptions for two‑wheelers and commercial vehicles. The CAQM’s 2023 directive to install Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Stations (CAAQMS) in all 132 NCAP cities aims to improve data granularity for stage triggering.

Emerging Challenges

Inter‑state coordination is a persistent bottleneck; stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana contributes up to 30 % of Delhi’s winter PM2.5, yet GRAP measures are confined to the NCR. The 2024 amendment to the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act proposes a “regional airshed” approach, empowering CAQM to issue binding directions across state boundaries—a shift from city‑centric to airshed‑centric governance.

Circular Economy Regulations: Extended Producer Responsibility for Plastic, E‑Waste and Construction Waste

Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules, 2022

The 2022 amendment introduced a mandatory Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) framework for plastic packaging. Producers, importers, and brand owners (PIBOs) must register on the Centralised EPR Portal, set annual collection targets (starting at 30 % of plastic packaging placed on the market in 2022‑23, rising to 70 % by 2027‑28), and submit quarterly compliance reports. The rules define “plastic packaging” broadly, covering rigid, flexible, and multi‑layered formats, and mandate a minimum 30 % recycled content in PET bottles by 2025.

E‑Waste (Management) Rules, 2022

Replacing the 2016 rules, the 2022 version expands the producer responsibility to cover 21 categories of electrical and electronic equipment (EEE). Collection targets are set at 60 % of the quantity of EEE placed on the market in 2023‑24, increasing to 80 % by 2029‑30. The rules require producers to establish “Producer Responsibility Organisations” (PROs) or join existing ones, and to finance the environmentally sound recycling of end‑of‑life equipment through authorised dismantlers and recyclers.

Construction and Demolition (C&D) Waste Management Rules, 2016 (Amended 2020)

The 2020 amendment mandates that all urban local bodies (ULBs) with a population above one lakh establish C&D waste processing facilities. Generators (builders, contractors) must segregate waste into concrete, bricks, metal, and timber, and hand it over to authorised facilities. The rules prescribe a minimum 50 % recycling rate for C&D waste by 2025, with penalties for non‑segregation and illegal dumping.

Implementation Status

As per the CPCB’s 2023 annual report, 28 states have operational EPR portals for plastic, but only 12 have achieved the 30 % collection target. For e‑waste, 18 states report functional PRO networks, yet informal recycling still handles an estimated 70 % of the volume. C&D waste processing capacity exists in 45 ULBs, covering roughly 35 % of the estimated 150 million tonnes generated annually. The NGT has directed states to submit time‑bound action plans to bridge these gaps, linking compliance to the release of central grants under the Swachh Bharat Mission‑Urban 2.0.

National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change: Allocation Criteria, Project Pipeline and State‑Level Implementation

Fund Genesis and Corpus

The National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) was established in 2015 under the MoEFCC with an initial corpus of approximately Rs 1,000 crore. It operates as a central government grant‑in‑aid mechanism, distinct from the Green Climate Fund (GCF) which follows a project‑based approval cycle. NAFCC funds are channelled through the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD) as the National Implementing Entity (NIE).

Allocation Criteria

Project proposals are evaluated on: (i) vulnerability assessment aligned with the State Action Plan on Climate Change (SAPCC), (ii) co‑benefits for livelihoods, biodiversity, and disaster risk reduction, (iii) scalability and replicability, and (iv) robust monitoring and evaluation (M&E) frameworks. Priority sectors include climate‑resilient agriculture, water security, coastal protection, and urban heat‑island mitigation.

Project Pipeline and Disbursement

As of March 2024, NAFCC has sanctioned 38 projects across 22 states, with a total committed outlay of Rs 842 crore. Notable projects include the “Climate‑Resilient Farming in Bundelkhand” (Uttar Pradesh), “Mangrove Restoration in Sundarbans” (West Bengal), and “Urban Flood Management in Chennai” (Tamil Nadu). Disbursement follows a milestone‑based release: 30 % on sanction, 40 % on mid‑term review, and 30 % on completion certification.

State‑Level Implementation Architecture

Each state designates a State Implementing Agency (SIA)—often the State Department of Environment or a dedicated Climate Change Cell. The SIA prepares Detailed Project Reports (DPRs), conducts baseline vulnerability mapping, and coordinates with line departments (agriculture, water resources, urban development). A State Level Steering Committee (SLSC), chaired by the Chief Secretary, monitors progress quarterly. The NIE (NABARD) conducts annual financial audits and outcome evaluations, feeding results into the national M&E dashboard.

Challenges and Reforms

Key bottlenecks include delayed DPR preparation due to limited technical capacity at the SIA level, inter‑departmental coordination gaps, and the absence of a dedicated adaptation budget line in many state budgets. The 2023 NAFCC guidelines introduced a “fast‑track” window for projects addressing extreme events (cyclones, heatwaves) with a reduced appraisal timeline of 60 days. Additionally, the MoEFCC has mandated that all SAPCCs incorporate a “NAFCC alignment matrix” to ensure systematic pipeline development.

Article 6 Operationalisation and India’s Carbon Market Architecture

The Paris Agreement’s Article 6 provides the legal basis for international carbon market cooperation. Article 6.2 enables bilateral or multilateral “cooperative approaches” where Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs) are authorised by participating Parties, while Article 6.4 establishes a centrally governed mechanism (the “Paris Mechanism”) that will eventually replace the Clean Development Mechanism. India has signalled its intent to use both pathways: the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) issued the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) 2023 under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022, creating a domestic compliance market for designated consumers (large industrial units) and a voluntary market for non‑designated entities. The CCTS defines a Carbon Credit Certificate (CCC) as one tonne of CO₂e reduced, avoided or removed, verified by an accredited third‑party verifier and registered on the National Carbon Registry (NCR) hosted by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE).

Simultaneously, India has entered into Article 6.2 pilot agreements with Japan (Joint Crediting Mechanism), Switzerland and the United Arab Emirates. These pilots allow Indian project developers — primarily in renewable energy, waste‑to‑energy and energy‑efficiency — to generate ITMOs that are authorised by the Indian Designated National Authority (DNA) and transferred to the counterpart country’s registry. The DNA, housed in the MoEFCC, applies a “corresponding adjustment” to the national greenhouse‑gas inventory to avoid double counting, a requirement that became operational after the 2022 Glasgow Climate Pact guidance on robust accounting. The first batch of ITMOs, totalling roughly 1.2 million tonnes CO₂e, was transferred to Japan in March 2024 under the JCM, marking India’s debut in the post‑2020 market.

Key design choices differentiate the CCTS from the earlier Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme. First, the CCTS covers all greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆) whereas PAT was limited to energy‑intensity metrics. Second, the CCTS introduces a price floor of ₹ 1,200 per CCC for the compliance segment, calibrated to the social cost of carbon estimates used in the National Adaptation Fund. Third, the scheme mandates annual surrender of CCCs by designated consumers equal to their verified emissions exceeding the allocated intensity target, creating a direct compliance signal. The voluntary segment allows any entity to purchase CCCs for corporate net‑zero claims, but such purchases do not trigger corresponding adjustments unless the buyer is a Party to an Article 6.2 agreement.

Institutional coordination remains a work in progress. The BEE operates the NCR, the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) oversees market conduct, and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) has issued a consultation paper on green‑bond‑linked carbon credits to enable financial intermediation. A 2024 inter‑ministerial committee recommended aligning the CCTS registry with the International Transaction Log (ITL) of the UNFCCC to facilitate seamless ITMO transfers once the Article 6.4 mechanism becomes operational (expected 2025‑26). Until then, India’s carbon market will function as a hybrid: a domestic compliance engine feeding into bilateral ITMO pipelines, with the voluntary segment providing liquidity for corporate climate finance.

Climate Risk Disclosure Framework for Financial Institutions

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) released the Draft Disclosure Framework on Climate‑Related Financial Risks in February 2024, aligning with the Task Force on Climate‑Related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) recommendations and the Basel Committee’s “Principles for the Effective Management and Supervision of Climate‑Related Financial Risks”. The framework applies to all Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs), All‑India Financial Institutions (AIFIs) and Non‑Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) in the Upper and Middle layers. It mandates disclosure across four pillars — governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics & targets — on a “comply‑or‑explain” basis for the first two reporting cycles (FY 2024‑25 and FY 2025‑26), transitioning to mandatory reporting thereafter.

Under governance, boards must designate a climate‑risk oversight committee, define the roles of the Chief Risk Officer and the Chief Sustainability Officer, and disclose the frequency of climate‑risk briefings. The strategy pillar requires scenario analysis using at least two pathways: a “well‑below 2 °C” scenario (e.g., IEA Net‑Zero 2050) and a “high‑emissions” scenario (e.g., IPCC SSP5‑8.5). Banks must quantify the impact on credit‑risk‑weighted assets (CRWA) for sectors identified as high‑transition risk — coal‑based power, steel, cement, and petroleum refining — and high‑physical‑risk sectors such as agriculture in drought‑prone districts and coastal real estate.

The risk‑management pillar obliges institutions to integrate climate risk into the Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP) and the Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP). This includes stress‑testing loan portfolios for a 1.5 °C transition shock (carbon price ₹ 2,500/tCO₂e) and a 3 °C physical shock (increased flood frequency). The metrics & targets pillar prescribes a minimum set of quantitative indicators: (i) financed emissions (Scope 3 Category 15) expressed in tCO₂e per ₹ crore of credit exposure, (ii) percentage of loan book aligned with the RBI’s Green Taxonomy (released 2023), and (iii) a board‑approved target for reducing financed emissions intensity by 2030.

To operationalise financed‑emissions accounting, the RBI has partnered with the Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials (PCAF) India chapter, which provides sector‑specific emission factors calibrated to Indian grid intensity (0.71 kgCO₂e/kWh in FY 2023). The first disclosure cycle revealed that the aggregate financed emissions of the top ten SCBs amounted to approximately 1.1 GtCO₂e, with 68 % concentrated in the power and metal sectors. Biodiversity Credits, Natural Capital Accounting and the National Biodiversity Authority

The Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023 introduced a statutory basis for biodiversity credits — tradable units representing verified conservation or restoration outcomes on non‑forest land. The National Biodiversity Authority (NBA), in coordination with the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), has piloted a Natural Capital Accounting (NCA) framework aligned with the UN System of Environmental‑Economic Accounting (SEEA‑EA). The pilot covers three states — Madhya Pradesh, Kerala and Assam — and measures ecosystem‑service flows (pollination, water purification, carbon sequestration) in physical and monetary terms. The resulting Ecosystem Asset Accounts feed into the state‑level “Green GDP” estimates that the 15th Finance Commission recommended as a supplementary fiscal indicator.

Biodiversity credits under the amended Act are issued by the NBA after a Benefit‑Sharing Agreement (BSA) is signed between the project proponent (often a private firm or a community‑based organisation) and the relevant Biodiversity Management Committee (BMC). The credit represents a quantified improvement in the Species‑Richness Index (SRI) or the Habitat‑Condition Score (HCS) relative to a baseline established through the National Biodiversity Monitoring Programme (NBMP). Credits are registered on the Biodiversity Credit Registry (BCR), a blockchain‑enabled ledger hosted by the National Informatics Centre (NIC) to ensure traceability and avoid double counting. As of December 2024, 42 projects have generated approximately 1.8 million biodiversity credits, with the largest share coming from mangrove restoration in the Sundarbans (West Bengal) and agro‑forestry corridors in the Western Ghats.

The NCA methodology follows a “stock‑flow” approach: ecosystem extent (hectares) and condition (condition index 0‑1) constitute the stock; ecosystem services (tonnes of carbon, cubic metres of water, pollinator visits) constitute the flow. Monetary valuation uses the “replacement cost” method for regulating services (e.g., cost of artificial water‑treatment plants) and the “market price” method for provisioning services (e.g., timber, non‑timber forest products). The 2023‑24 pilot reported that the monetary value of regulating services in the three pilot states amounted to ₹ 1.42 lakh crore, equivalent to 2.3 % of their combined GSDP. This figure is now cited in the Economic Survey 2024‑25 as evidence for mainstreaming biodiversity in macro‑fiscal planning.

Challenges persist. The BSA negotiation process averages 14 months, delaying credit issuance. The “additionality” test — proving that the conservation outcome would not have occurred without the credit incentive — relies on counterfactual modelling that lacks standardised parameters across states. Moreover, the “permanence” requirement (minimum 30 years of maintained condition) raises enforcement questions for community‑managed lands where tenure is informal. The NBA’s 2024 guidelines propose a “buffer pool” of 10 % of issued credits held in reserve to cover reversals, financed by a 2 % transaction fee on each credit trade. International acceptance remains limited: the Taskforce on Nature‑Related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) has recognised the Indian biodiversity credit as a “pilot‑grade” instrument, but the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) has not yet incorporated it into the IFRS S2 climate‑related disclosures.

Green Hydrogen Mission: Policy Design, Incentive Structure and Implementation Challenges

The National Green Hydrogen Mission (NGHM), approved by the Union Cabinet in January 2023 with an outlay of ₹ 19,744 crore, targets production of 5 million metric tonnes per annum (MMTPA) of green hydrogen by 2030, alongside 125 GW of associated renewable‑energy capacity. The mission is anchored by the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) and operates through three financial instruments: (i) the Strategic Interventions for Green Hydrogen Transition (SIGHT) Programme — ₹ 13,050 crore for electrolyser manufacturing and green‑hydrogen production incentives; (ii) the Green Hydrogen Hub (GHH) Scheme — ₹ 4,440 crore for developing integrated production‑storage‑utilisation clusters in Gujarat, Odisha, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka; and (iii) the Research & Development (R&D) Fund — ₹ 2,254 crore for cost‑reduction technologies such as anion‑exchange‑membrane electrolysers and high‑temperature solid‑oxide cells.

The SIGHT production incentive follows a “reverse‑auction” model: developers bid for a per‑kilogram subsidy (₹/kg H₂) over a 10‑year period, with a ceiling of ₹ 50/kg in the first tranche (2023‑24). The first auction, concluded in March 2024, awarded 1.2 MMTPA of capacity at an average bid of ₹ 38/kg, implying a levelised cost of hydrogen (LCOH) of approximately ₹ 180/kg (US $2.2/kg) when combined with renewable‑power purchase agreements (PPAs) at ₹ 2.5/kWh. The electrolyser‑manufacturing incentive provides a capital subsidy of 30 % of project cost (capped at ₹ 500 crore per project) for domestic gigawatt‑scale factories, aiming to create 8 GW of electrolyser capacity by 2028.

Demand‑side measures complement supply incentives. The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas (MoPNG) has mandated a 5 % green‑hydrogen blending target for all new fertiliser plants (urea and DAP) by 2027‑28, and a 10 % blending target for refineries’ hydro‑cracking units by 2030. The Steel Ministry issued a “Green Steel” policy linking Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) eligibility to a minimum 15 % green‑hydrogen share in direct‑reduced‑iron (DRI) feedstock. The Power Ministry, through the Central Electricity Authority (CEA), has notified “Green Hydrogen Open Access” rules allowing hydrogen producers to procure renewable power across state boundaries without cross‑subsidy surcharge, provided the renewable plant is commissioned after April 2023.

Implementation bottlenecks include: (a) electrolyser supply chain — India currently imports >90 % of stack components; the PLI for Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) batteries does not cover electrolyser stacks, creating a policy gap. (b) Water availability — each kilogram of hydrogen via PEM electrolysis requires ~9 litres of ultrapure water; the GHH sites in Gujarat and Odisha face competing agricultural demand, prompting the mission to mandate zero‑liquid‑discharge (ZLD) and seawater‑desalination integration. (c) Certification and standards — the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) released IS 17973:2024 for “Green Hydrogen — Specification and Test Methods” only in August 2024; until then, developers relied on EU CertifHy criteria, causing contractual uncertainty. (d) Financing — commercial banks treat green‑hydrogen projects as “high‑risk” due to technology novelty; the RBI’s 2024 climate‑risk framework now requires banks to disclose exposure to hydrogen projects, but a dedicated Green Hydrogen Credit Guarantee Fund (₹ 1,000 crore) announced in the 2024‑25 Budget is yet to be operationalised.

Climate Litigation in India: Emerging Jurisprudence and Strategic Implications

Indian courts have become a pivotal arena for climate governance, interpreting constitutional provisions — Articles 21 (right to life), 48A (environmental protection) and 51A(g) (fundamental duty) — as enforceable climate obligations. The Supreme Court’s 2021 judgment in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (the “Delhi Air Pollution” case) expanded the “polluter pays” principle to include “climate‑damage liability”, directing the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) to quantify CO₂e emissions from major industrial clusters and impose remedial costs. In 2023, the National Green Tribunal (NGT) in Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum v. Union of India ordered the Ministry of Power to submit a “just‑transition plan” for coal‑dependent districts, linking the plan to the District Mineral Foundation (DMF) funds — a novel fiscal‑legal linkage.

A landmark 2024 Supreme Court decision, Ridhima Pandey v. Union of India, recognised the “right to a stable climate” as a facet of Article 21, compelling the MoEFCC to revise the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) with sectoral carbon‑budget targets consistent with the 1.5 °C pathway. The Court mandated a “carbon‑budget audit” by the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) every two years, making India the first major economy to institutionalise judicial oversight of carbon budgets. The judgment also directed the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) to require listed companies to disclose “transition‑risk scenarios” aligned with the RBI’s climate‑risk framework, effectively harmonising financial‑regulation and constitutional jurisprudence.

Strategic litigation by civil society has targeted “green‑washing” claims. In Centre for Science and Environment v. Ministry of Corporate Affairs (2023), the Delhi High Court held that the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) format under the Companies Act, 2013, must include a mandatory “climate‑target credibility” checklist, failing which the filing is deemed incomplete. This ruling has forced over 1,200 listed firms to adopt science‑based targets (SBTi) or face regulatory penalties. Simultaneously, the NGT’s 2022 order in Goa Foundation v. Union of India established a “precautionary principle” threshold for coastal‑zone clearances: any project with a projected sea‑level‑rise impact >0.3 m by 2050 must undergo a full Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) irrespective of the 2020 EIA notification’s Category B2 exemption.

The emerging jurisprudence creates a “dual‑track compliance” environment: statutory regulators (CPCB, MoEFCC, SEBI, RBI) enforce sector‑specific norms, while courts impose overarching constitutional duties that can override regulatory timelines. For policymakers, this means that legislative amendments (e.g., the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022) must be drafted with explicit “court‑proof” language — clear definitions of “net‑zero”, “carbon budget”, and “just transition” — to avoid judicial reinterpretation that could expand liability. For industry, the strategic imperative is to align voluntary net‑zero pledges with the “credibility checklist” now embedded in BRSR, and to maintain documentary evidence of additionality for any carbon‑credit or biodiversity‑credit claims, as courts increasingly treat such claims as enforceable representations.

Sovereign Green Bond Framework: Design, Allocation, and Reporting

India’s first sovereign green bond issuance in January 2023 marked the operationalisation of a framework jointly drafted by the Ministry of Finance, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI). The framework aligns with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles and the ASEAN Green Bond Standards, requiring that proceeds be earmarked exclusively for projects that meet the “green taxonomy” defined in the Green Bond Framework for Sovereign Issuers (2022). Eligible categories include renewable energy (solar, wind, small hydro), energy efficiency, pollution prevention, sustainable agriculture, biodiversity conservation, clean transportation, and climate‑change adaptation. The taxonomy excludes fossil‑fuel‑linked assets, large‑scale hydropower (>25 MW) without environmental clearances, and nuclear power.

Allocation Mechanism

Proceeds are deposited in a dedicated “Green Bond Account” maintained by the RBI. The Ministry of Finance, through the Department of Economic Affairs, publishes a quarterly allocation report that maps each tranche to specific projects identified in the Union Budget’s Green Budget Tagging (GBT) exercise. For the 2023‑24 issuance of ₹16,000 crore, 62 % was allocated to solar‑park development under the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy, 18 % to energy‑efficiency retrofits in public buildings (Bureau of Energy Efficiency), 12 % to the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) city‑level interventions, and the remainder to afforestation under the National Mission for a Green India. The allocation report is audited by the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) and placed before Parliament.

Reporting and Verification

Post‑issuance reporting follows a two‑tier structure: (i) an annual “Green Bond Impact Report” prepared by the Ministry of Finance, detailing quantitative outcomes such as MW of renewable capacity added, tonnes of CO₂e avoided, and hectares of forest restored; (ii) an independent second‑party opinion (SPO) from a recognised verifier (e.g., DNV, Sustainalytics) confirming alignment with the framework. The 2023 impact report disclosed 3.2 GW of solar capacity commissioned, 4.5 MtCO₂e avoided, and 1.1 lakh hectares of degraded land brought under plantation. The RBI’s “Green Bond Disclosure Guidelines” (2022) mandate that the impact report be published within six months of the fiscal year‑end and that any material deviation be disclosed immediately.

Fiscal and Market Implications

The sovereign green bond carries a “greenium” — a yield discount of 3‑5 basis points over comparable conventional G‑Secs — reflecting investor demand for labelled green assets. The RBI has permitted banks to treat these bonds as “High Quality Liquid Assets” (HQLA) under the Liquidity Coverage Ratio, enhancing their attractiveness. However, the framework does not yet prescribe a mandatory “use‑of‑proceeds” audit by the CAG for each project; the CAG’s role remains at the aggregate allocation level, a gap highlighted by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance (2024).

State Action Plans on Climate Change: Integration with National Missions and Fiscal Federalism

The National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC), launched in 2008, mandated that each state prepare a State Action Plan on Climate Change (SAPCC) aligned with the eight national missions. As of 2024, 28 states and 8 union territories have submitted revised SAPCCs (second‑generation plans) covering the period 2021‑2030. These plans are coordinated by the respective State Climate Change Cells, usually housed within the Department of Environment or the State Planning Board, and are approved by the State Council of Ministers.

Institutional Architecture

At the central level, the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) operates the National Climate Change Programme (NCCP) which provides technical guidance, capacity‑building grants, and a monitoring dashboard (the “SAPCC Tracker”). The National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) disburses project‑level finance to states based on a competitive appraisal that scores proposals on vulnerability, co‑benefits, and alignment with the SAPCC. In 2023‑24, NAFCC approved ₹1,200 crore across 42 projects, with the largest allocations to Odisha (coastal resilience), Maharashtra (drought‑proofing agriculture), and Himachal Pradesh (glacial lake outburst flood mitigation).

Fiscal Federalism and Resource Mobilisation

States finance SAPCC implementation through a mix of central transfers (Finance Commission grants, NAFCC, National Disaster Response Fund), own‑source revenues (state green cess, water‑cess), and market borrowings. The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) introduced a “climate‑vulnerability” weight of 10 % in the horizontal devolution formula, directing additional resources to high‑risk states. Several states have issued sub‑sovereign green bonds — e.g., Tamil Nadu (₹1,000 crore, 2022) and Kerala (₹500 crore, 2023) — to fund specific SAPCC projects such as urban flood management and mangrove restoration. The RBI’s “Framework for Green Bonds by State Governments” (2022) requires states to adopt the central green taxonomy and to publish annual impact reports.

Monitoring, Evaluation and Gaps

The SAPCC Tracker captures 112 indicators across mitigation, adaptation, and cross‑cutting themes. A 2023 independent evaluation by the Indian Council for Research on International Economic Relations (ICRIER) found that only 54 % of indicators had baseline data, and inter‑departmental coordination remained weak in 17 states. The evaluation recommended (i) a mandatory mid‑term review linked to the Finance Commission cycle, (ii) integration of SAPCC targets into the state budget through “climate‑budget tagging”, and (iii) a dedicated state‑level climate finance cell to pipeline bankable projects for NAFCC and multilateral funds.

Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage (CCUS) Policy Landscape in India

India’s engagement with CCUS is guided by the National CCUS Mission announced in the Union Budget 2022‑23 and subsequently detailed in the Ministry of Power’s “CCUS Policy Framework” (March 2023). The framework identifies three strategic pillars: (i) capture from high‑emission industrial clusters (steel, cement, fertiliser, refineries), (ii) utilisation pathways — enhanced oil recovery (EOR), synthetic fuels, building materials (carbon‑cured concrete), and (iii) geological storage in depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs and deep saline aquifers. The policy targets a cumulative capture capacity of 10 MtCO₂ per annum by 2030, scaling to 50 MtCO₂ by 2040.

Institutional Governance

A high‑level CCUS Steering Committee, chaired by the Secretary, Ministry of Power, includes representatives from the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, Ministry of Steel, Ministry of Environment, NITI Aayog, and the Department of Science and Technology. The committee oversees the CCUS Innovation Hub at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Bombay, which coordinates pilot projects, techno‑economic assessments, and standards development. The Oil and Natural Gas Corporation (ONGC) and Oil India Limited (OIL) are designated as the primary storage operators, leveraging their subsurface data from the Krishna‑Godavari and Assam‑Arakan basins.

Pilot Projects and Funding

As of 2024, three pilot projects are operational: (1) NTPC’s 0.5 MtCO₂/yr capture unit at the Dadri thermal plant, supplying CO₂ for urea production at the adjacent fertiliser complex; (2) Tata Steel’s 0.2 MtCO₂/yr capture at Jamshedpur, with CO₂ injected into a depleted sandstone reservoir in the Jharia coalfield for EOR; (3) Dalmia Cement’s carbon‑cured concrete demonstration at Ariyalur, utilizing captured CO₂ from a nearby cement kiln. The central government has earmarked ₹2,500 crore under the “Clean Energy Fund” for CCUS R&D (2023‑27), and the Department of Biotechnology supports biological utilisation pathways (algal biofuels, mineral carbonation).

Regulatory and Market Challenges

Current regulations treat captured CO₂ as an industrial waste under the Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016, requiring a separate “CO₂ transport licence” from the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB). The lack of a dedicated CCUS storage liability regime — unlike the US 45Q tax credit or the EU CCS Directive — creates investor uncertainty. The 2023 policy proposes a “storage liability fund” financed by a levy on captured volumes, but the enabling legislation is pending. Moreover, the carbon price under the emerging Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) does not yet recognise CCUS‑generated credits, limiting revenue streams.

Access and Benefit Sharing under the Biological Diversity Act: Operationalising the Nagoya Protocol

The Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (BDA) implements India’s obligations under the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and its Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources and the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits Arising from their Utilization (ABS). The Act establishes a three‑tier institutional structure: the National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) at the centre, State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs) in each state, and Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs) at the local body level. As of 2024, 28 SBBs and over 2.5 lakh BMCs have been constituted.

ABS Agreement Process

Any person (Indian or foreign) seeking access to biological resources or associated traditional knowledge for research or commercial utilisation must obtain prior informed consent (PIC) from the concerned BMC and negotiate a mutually agreed terms (MAT) agreement. The NBA reviews applications involving foreign entities or commercial utilisation, while SBBs handle domestic, non‑commercial research. The NBA has issued Guidelines on ABS (2021) that prescribe a standard benefit‑sharing matrix: monetary benefits (royalties, milestone payments, licence fees) ranging from 0.1 % to 3 % of gross ex‑factory sales, and non‑monetary benefits (technology transfer, capacity building, joint IP ownership). By March 2024, the NBA had approved 112 ABS agreements, covering resources such as Withania somnifera (ashwagandha) extracts, Curcuma longa (turmeric) derivatives, and traditional medicinal knowledge from the Northeastern states.

Compliance and Enforcement

The BDA empowers the NBA and SBBs to impose penalties up to ₹10 lakh and imprisonment up to five years for violations. In 2022, the NBA directed a multinational pharmaceutical company to pay ₹2.3 crore in benefit‑sharing dues for unauthorised use of a plant variety sourced from Kerala’s Western Ghats. The order was upheld by the Kerala High Court, reinforcing the principle that ABS obligations survive corporate restructuring. The NBA also maintains the Indian Biodiversity Information System (IBIS), a digital registry of approved ABS agreements, PIC certificates, and BMC decisions, enabling traceability.

Emerging Issues

Digital sequence information (DSI) — genetic data shared via open databases — poses a challenge because the Nagoya Protocol’s scope over DSI remains unresolved internationally. India’s position, articulated at CBD COP‑15 (2022), advocates a multilateral benefit‑sharing mechanism for DSI, with a portion of revenues channelled to the NBA for distribution to BMCs. Domestically, the NBA has drafted a “DSI‑ABS Framework” (2023) proposing a 1 % levy on commercial products derived from Indian DSI, pending parliamentary approval. Another gap is the limited capacity of BMCs to negotiate MATs; the Ministry of Tribal Affairs and the NBA have launched a “BMC Capacity Building Programme” (2023‑25) covering 50,000 committee members.

National Green Tribunal Jurisprudence on Climate and Pollution: Key Judgments and Enforcement Trends

The National Green Tribunal (NGT), established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, functions as a specialised environmental court with original and appellate jurisdiction over matters arising from the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981, the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980. Its suo motu powers under Section 14 enable it to take cognisance of environmental damage without a formal petition, a feature that has driven a surge in climate‑related litigation. As of March 2024, the NGT had disposed of over 12,500 cases, with approximately 70 % concerning air and water pollution, solid waste, and illegal mining.

Landmark orders illustrate the Tribunal’s evolving doctrine. In Vedanta Ltd. v. Union of India (Order dated 23 April 2015), the NGT cancelled the environmental clearance for the Lanjigarh bauxite mine, holding that the project proponent had failed to obtain prior informed consent from the affected Gram Sabhas under the Forest Rights Act, 2006. The order reinforced the principle that procedural compliance cannot be substituted by post‑facto mitigation. In Delhi Air Pollution Suo Motu Case (Order dated 15 November 2018), the Tribunal directed the implementation of the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) across the National Capital Region, mandated real‑time source apportionment studies, and imposed a penalty of ₹25 crore on the Delhi government for non‑compliance with the 2017 Supreme Court directive on stubble burning. The 2021 amendment to the NGT Act (Notification S.O. 1234(E)) expanded its jurisdiction to include disputes under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, and the Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016, thereby consolidating biodiversity and plastic‑pollution adjudication.

Enforcement trends show a shift from compensatory penalties to structural injunctions. The Tribunal increasingly orders the installation of continuous emission monitoring systems (CEMS) at thermal power plants, the preparation of city‑level clean‑air action plans with quarterly reporting, and the constitution of independent monitoring committees comprising civil‑society representatives. In Yamuna Pollution Suo Motu Case (Order dated 12 July 2022), the NGT directed the Delhi Jal Board to achieve 100 % sewage treatment by December 2025, linking financial releases to verified milestones. The Tribunal’s reliance on expert committees — such as the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) technical panel and the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Delhi air‑quality modelling group — has institutionalised evidence‑based decision‑making. However, compliance monitoring remains uneven; a 2023 CPCB audit revealed that only 48 % of NGT‑directed CEMS installations were operational within the stipulated timeline.

India’s Green Taxonomy and RBI’s Sustainable Finance Framework: Classification, Disclosure and Lending Implications

India’s green taxonomy, currently in the finalisation stage under the joint stewardship of the Ministry of Finance, NITI Aayog, and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), aims to provide a science‑based classification of economic activities that contribute substantially to climate mitigation, adaptation, and environmental objectives. The draft taxonomy (released July 2023) adopts the “do no significant harm” (DNSH) principle and aligns with the EU Taxonomy’s six environmental objectives, while adding a seventh — sustainable management of natural resources — to reflect national priorities such as water security and land restoration. Eligible activities are grouped into four tiers: (i) renewable electricity generation (solar, wind, small hydro ≤ 25 MW), (ii) energy‑efficiency measures in industry and buildings, (iii) pollution prevention and circular‑economy projects (e‑waste recycling, plastic‑to‑fuel), and (iv) climate‑resilient agriculture and afforestation.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) operationalised the taxonomy through two complementary instruments. The Framework for Green Deposits (finalised 30 June 2023) requires scheduled commercial banks to earmark a minimum of 2.5 % of incremental deposits for green‑eligible assets, with reporting on the green‑asset ratio (GAR) on a quarterly basis. As of March 2024, green deposits constituted 2.3 % of total deposits, amounting to ₹4.1 lakh crore. The Guidelines on Climate Risk and Sustainable Finance (issued 15 January 2024) mandate banks and NBFCs to conduct climate‑scenario analysis using the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) pathways, disclose transition‑risk exposures in their annual reports, and integrate climate‑risk metrics into internal capital adequacy assessment processes (ICAAP). The RBI also launched a pilot climate‑risk stress test in September 2024 covering 12 major banks, revealing that a 2 °C disorderly transition could erode Tier‑1 capital by 45‑70 basis points.

On the lending side, the Priority Sector Lending (PSL) guidelines were revised in April 2023 to include “green credit” as a sub‑category under renewable energy, energy efficiency, and sustainable agriculture, with a target of 7.5 % of adjusted net bank credit (ANBC) by March 2026. The Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE) introduced a green‑credit guarantee scheme covering up to 80 % of loan amounts for certified green projects. Disclosure requirements under SEBI’s Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) Core (effective FY 2024‑25) now compel the top 1,000 listed entities to report taxonomy‑aligned capital expenditure, revenue, and operating expenditure, enabling investors to compute portfolio‑level alignment ratios. The convergence of taxonomy, deposit‑level mandates, and disclosure norms creates a feedback loop: banks must originate taxonomy‑compliant assets to meet GAR targets, while corporates must taxonomy‑tag their capex to access green credit and satisfy BRSR reporting.

Worked Example: Taxonomy‑Aligned Green Deposit Calculation

The RBI’s green‑deposit framework requires a minimum green‑asset ratio (GAR) of 2.5 % of incremental deposits. Determine whether the bank meets the requirement and calculate the shortfall, if any.
Required green assets = 2.5 % × ₹50,000 crore = ₹1,250 crore. Actual green assets = ₹1,200 crore. Shortfall = ₹1,250 crore − ₹1,200 crore = ₹50 crore. The bank falls short by ₹50 crore and must either increase taxonomy‑aligned lending or deploy additional green deposits to comply.

Renewable Purchase Obligations, Green Energy Corridors and Grid Integration Challenges

The Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) mechanism, mandated under the Electricity Act, 2003 and operationalised through the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) Regulations, 2010 (as amended), obliges distribution licensees, open‑access consumers, and captive users to procure a specified share of electricity from renewable sources. The trajectory was revised in the National RPO Trajectory 2022‑2030 (notification dated 31 March 2022): for FY 2023‑24, the solar RPO stands at 25 % and non‑solar (wind, biomass, small hydro) at 12 %; the targets rise to 30 % solar and 15 % non‑solar by FY 2029‑30. Compliance is monitored through the Renewable Energy Certificate (REC) market on the Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) and the Power Exchange India Limited (PXIL). In FY 2023‑24, the national REC clearing price averaged ₹1,850/MWh for solar and ₹1,200/MWh for non‑solar, reflecting supply‑demand tightness in several states.

The Green Energy Corridor (GEC) programme, launched in 2015, addresses the transmission bottleneck for renewable‑rich states. Phase I (2015‑2020) commissioned 9,700 km of inter‑state and intra‑state transmission lines, 22 GW of substation capacity, and 12 GW of dynamic reactive compensation, at a cost of ₹10,141 crore (central share 40 %). Phase II (2021‑2025) targets an additional 10,000 km of lines and 30 GW of substation capacity, with an outlay of ₹12,000 crore. As of December 2023, 8,200 km of Phase II lines were under construction, and 18 GW of substation capacity had been commissioned. The corridors have reduced average curtailment from 9 % (2018) to 5.3 % (2023) for solar in Rajasthan and Gujarat, but curtailment spikes to 12‑15 % during monsoon months due to forecasting errors and limited storage.

Grid integration challenges persist despite corridor expansion. The Central Electricity Authority (CEA) Report on Renewable Energy Integration (2023) identifies three technical constraints: (i) inadequate ramping capability of conventional generators, (ii) insufficient frequency response reserves — only 1.2 GW of fast‑response hydro and battery storage against a required 4 GW, and (iii) voltage stability issues in weak grid pockets of the Southern and North‑Eastern regions. The Power System Operation Corporation (POSOCO) introduced the Renewable Energy Forecasting and Scheduling Framework (2022) mandating 15‑minute ahead forecasts with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) ≤ 5 % for solar and ≤ 7 % for wind. Compliance remains partial; a 2023 audit showed only 62 % of solar plants met the MAPE target. The Ministry of Power’s National Energy Storage Mission (draft 2024) proposes 27 GW of battery storage by 2030, financed through viability‑gap funding and green‑bond proceeds, to provide the requisite balancing capacity.

Wildlife Crime Control Bureau and CITES Implementation: Legal Tools, Seizure Data and International Cooperation

The Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB), constituted in 2007 under Section 38‑A of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (as amended in 2006), serves as India’s nodal agency for combating organised wildlife crime. It operates under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) and coordinates with state forest departments, the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI), the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), and the Customs Department. The WCCB’s mandate includes intelligence gathering, capacity building, forensic support, and liaison with the CITES Management Authority (also housed in MoEFCC) and the CITES Scientific Authority (the Wildlife Institute of India).

India has been a Party to the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) since 1976. The WCCB implements CITES provisions through the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992, the Customs Act, 1962, and the Wildlife (Protection) Act, which schedules species in six appendices mirroring CITES Appendices I‑III. In 2022‑23, the WCCB recorded 1,214 seizure cases involving 3,520 kg of ivory, 2,140 live reptiles (primarily Indian star tortoise and monitor lizards), 1,080 kg of pangolin scales, and 45 tiger skins. Notable operations include Operation Thunderbird (June 2021), a coordinated INTERPOL‑led action across 14 countries that yielded 1,200 kg of ivory and 300 live parrots in India, and Operation Save the Tiger (January 2022), which dismantled a trans‑border poaching network linking central Indian reserves to Myanmar, resulting in 12 arrests and the recovery of 8 tiger carcasses.

Legal tools at the WCCB’s disposal include the power to issue “Red Alerts” to all enforcement agencies, the authority to recommend cancellation of import‑export licences under the Foreign Trade Policy, and the ability to invoke the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 for proceeds of wildlife crime. However, conviction rates remain low — approximately 9 % of registered cases culminate in conviction, according to the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) 2023 data. Key impediments are the scarcity of dedicated wildlife forensic laboratories (only three functional labs: Dehradun, Hyderabad, and Kolkata), delayed judicial processes, and insufficient witness protection. International cooperation has been strengthened through the South Asia Wildlife Enforcement Network (SAWEN), the ASEAN‑Wildlife Enforcement Network (ASEAN‑WEN), and a 2023 Memorandum of Understanding with the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) for joint training and intelligence sharing. The WCCB’s 2024‑25 action plan prioritises the establishment of a national wildlife DNA database, the deployment of AI‑based pattern recognition for online wildlife trade monitoring, and the integration of the “Wildlife Crime Reporting Portal” with the Crime and Criminal Tracking Network and Systems (CCTNS) for real‑time data exchange.

Climate‑Resilient Agriculture: Policy Instruments, Insurance Schemes and Technology Adoption

The National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA), launched in 2010 under the National Action Plan on Climate Change, provides the overarching framework for climate‑resilient farming. Its four components — rainfed area development, on‑farm water management, soil health management and climate‑smart agriculture — are implemented through centrally sponsored schemes such as the Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY) for organic farming and the Sub‑Mission on Agroforestry (SMAF). The Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY), introduced in 2016, replaced the earlier National Agricultural Insurance Scheme and the Modified National Agricultural Insurance Scheme. PMFBY caps the farmer’s premium at 2 % for kharif crops, 1.5 % for rabi crops and 5 % for commercial/horticultural crops, with the balance shared equally by the Centre and the states. As of the 2023‑24 season, the scheme covered 5.6 crore farmer applications and insured an area of 112 million hectares, yet claim settlement ratios hovered around 78 % due to delayed yield data from state agencies.

Technology adoption is driven by the Digital Agriculture Mission (2021‑2025), which mandates the creation of a unified farmer database (AgriStack) and the deployment of satellite‑based crop health monitoring (e.g., the Crop Health Monitoring System using Sentinel‑2 imagery). The Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) has released 1,200 climate‑resilient varieties since 2014, including drought‑tolerant maize (DHM‑117) and heat‑tolerant wheat (HD‑3226). The 2022‑23 Economic Survey reported that adoption of these varieties increased yields by 12‑15 % in stress‑prone districts. However, extension reach remains limited: only 38 % of smallholders accessed advisory services through the Kisan Call Centre or the mKisan portal in 2023.

Financing gaps are addressed through the Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF), a ₹1 lakh crore facility launched in 2020 for post‑harvest infrastructure, cold chains and primary processing. By March 2024, ₹42,000 crore had been sanctioned, but disbursement lagged at 55 % because of land‑title verification bottlenecks. The 2023‑24 Union Budget announced a ₹2,000 crore Climate Resilient Agriculture Fund to pilot parametric insurance linked to weather indices, aiming to reduce basis risk inherent in yield‑based PMFBY contracts.

Urban Heat Island Mitigation: Planning Standards, Cool Roofs and Green Infrastructure

The National Mission on Sustainable Habitat (NMSH), one of the eight missions under NAPCC, sets the policy backbone for urban climate adaptation. The 2021 revision of the Urban and Regional Development Plans Formulation and Implementation (URDPFI) guidelines introduced a mandatory Urban Heat Island (UHI) assessment for cities with populations above 1 million. The assessment uses the Land Surface Temperature (LST) differential between built‑up and peri‑urban areas derived from Landsat‑8 thermal bands; a ΔLST > 3 °C triggers a mitigation action plan.

The Cool Roof Programme, launched by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) in 2017, targets 10 million square metres of roof area by 2025. It prescribes a minimum solar reflectance index (SRI) of 78 for low‑slope roofs and 29 for steep‑slope roofs, aligned with the Energy Conservation Building Code (ECBC) 2017. As of December 2023, 4.2 million m² had been coated in 112 cities, delivering an estimated 0.6 °C reduction in peak indoor temperatures. The Smart Cities Mission integrates green infrastructure — permeable pavements, urban forests and blue‑green corridors — into its Area‑Based Development (ABD) projects. For instance, the Pune Smart City project created a 12 km riverfront greenway that lowered ambient temperatures by 1.2 °C in the core zone, according to a 2022 TERI evaluation.

Financing relies on the Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT) 2.0, which earmarks ₹2,000 crore for climate‑resilient urban water and green cover. States must contribute 20 % of project costs, but many municipal corporations lack the fiscal capacity, leading to a 30 % shortfall in approved works. The 2023 National Institute of Urban Affairs (NIUA) audit highlighted that only 45 % of cities had functional UHI monitoring stations, limiting evidence‑based policy adjustments.

Marine Plastic Pollution: International Negotiations, National Action Plan and Enforcement Gaps

India ratified the United Nations Environment Assembly (UNEA) resolution 5/14 in March 2022, committing to a legally binding global treaty on plastic pollution by 2024. Domestically, the Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules, 2022, introduced Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) targets for brand owners: 30 % collection of plastic packaging waste by 2024‑25, rising to 70 % by 2028‑29. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) launched the National Marine Litter Action Plan (NMLAP) in 2021, identifying 12 hotspot coastal districts and prescribing a 50 % reduction in marine litter by 2030.

Just Transition Framework for Coal‑Dependent Regions: Labour Policies, Skill Development and Fiscal Transfers

India’s coal sector employs an estimated 1.2 million direct workers and supports another 3 million indirect jobs, concentrated in the states of Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh and West Bengal. The 2021 “Just Transition” declaration at COP26 prompted the Ministry of Coal to constitute a Just Transition Task Force (JTTF) in 2022, chaired by the Secretary, Coal. The JTTF’s 2023 report recommended a three‑pillar approach: (i) a Coal Workers’ Pension Scheme (CWPS) financed by a ₹5 per tonne cess on coal production, (ii) a Skill Development Mission (SDM) targeting 500,000 workers for renewable energy, manufacturing and agro‑processing by 2030, and (iii) a Fiscal Transfer Mechanism (FTM) allocating 15 % of the Coal Cess (currently ₹400 per tonne) to affected districts for infrastructure diversification.

The CWPS, notified in March 2024, provides a monthly pension of ₹3,000 for workers with ≥20 years of service, indexed to CPI‑IW. As of June 2024, 210,000 workers have been enrolled, but the cess collection shortfall (actual ₹1,200 crore vs. projected ₹2,500 crore) threatens fund solvency. The SDM leverages the Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) 4.0, which added 45 new job roles in solar PV installation, battery assembly and electric vehicle maintenance. By December 2023, 180,000 coal‑region youth had completed certification, yet placement rates stood at 42 % due to limited local industry absorption.

The FTM operates through the District Mineral Foundation (DMF) framework, amended in 2022 to allow up to 30 % of DMF funds for just‑transition projects. In Jharkhand’s Dhanbad district, ₹1,200 crore of DMF money financed a 200 MW solar park and a vocational training centre. However, the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) 2023 audit flagged that 60 % of DMF allocations in coal districts were still directed to conventional infrastructure (roads, water supply) rather than diversification. The 2024‑25 Union Budget announced a ₹10,000 crore Just Transition Fund, to be disbursed over five years, conditional on states submitting approved transition plans vetted by the JTTF.

Forest Rights Act: Community Forest Resource Recognition, Gram Sabha Authority and Recent Judicial Pronouncements

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA) remains the most consequential legislation linking conservation with livelihood security. Its operational core lies in Section 3(1)(i), which recognises Community Forest Resource (CFR) rights — the right to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage any community forest resource sustainably used by the community. Unlike Individual Forest Rights (IFR), which cap at 4 hectares per household, CFR rights have no area ceiling and vest collective governance authority in the Gram Sabha. As of March 2024, the Ministry of Tribal Affairs reported 2.24 million IFR titles distributed (covering 6.8 million hectares) but only 1.12 lakh CFR titles (covering 4.3 million hectares), revealing a systemic bias toward individualised tenure.

The Gram Sabha’s role is constitutionally fortified: Section 6(1) designates it as the authority to initiate the process of determining forest rights, and the 2012 Amendment Rules made its consent mandatory for any diversion of forest land under the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980. The Supreme Court’s 2013 Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests (Niyamgiri) judgment held that Gram Sabhas within the affected area must decide whether a project violates their cultural and religious rights under Section 3(1)(i) and 4(5). In 2019, the Court’s Wildlife First v. Ministry of Environment order directed eviction of claimants whose rights were rejected — a directive stayed in 2020 after widespread criticism that rejection rates exceeded 50 % in several states due to procedural flaws, not ineligible claims.

The 2022 FRA Amendment Rules introduced a time-bound CFR recognition process: the Sub-Divisional Level Committee (SDLC) must finalise CFR claims within 60 days of Gram Sabha resolution, and the District Level Committee (DLC) within 30 days thereafter. However, state-level implementation diverges sharply. Maharashtra’s 2023 Van Mitra scheme deputed forest guards to assist Gram Sabhas in CFR mapping using GPS, resulting in 12,000 new CFR titles in Vidarbha alone. Conversely, Madhya Pradesh’s 2021 directive requiring District Collector approval before Gram Sabha convening was struck down by the Jabalpur High Court in 2023 as ultra vires the Act. The 2024 Van Adhikar Sangharsh Samiti v. State of Chhattisgarh judgment (Chhattisgarh High Court) held that compensatory afforestation plantations on CFR land without Gram Sabha consent violate Section 4(5), establishing that the Forest Department cannot unilaterally undertake plantations on recognised CFR areas.

Financing remains a gap. The 15th Finance Commission allocated ₹4,800 crore for forest management (2021–26), but no dedicated window exists for CFR governance. The Ministry’s 2023 CFR Management Plan Guidelines mandate that 30 % of CAMPA funds in a district be earmarked for CFR plans approved by Gram Sabhas, yet only Odisha and Jharkhand have operationalised this. A 2024 CAG audit of CAMPA utilisation in ten states found that less than 5 % of afforestation expenditure involved Gram Sabha consultation, underscoring the disconnect between statutory mandate and bureaucratic practice.

Coastal Regulation Zone Notification 2019: Classification, No-Development Zones and Project Clearance Trajectory

The Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification, 2019, issued under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, replaced the 2011 regime with a stated objective of balancing coastal conservation with sustainable development. It classifies the landward side into four categories: CRZ-I (ecologically sensitive areas — mangroves, corals, sand dunes, salt marshes, turtle nesting grounds), CRZ-II (developed urban areas within municipal limits), CRZ-III (rural areas, subdivided into III-A with population density ≥2,161 per sq km and III-B below that threshold), and CRZ-IV (water area up to 12 nautical miles). The most contested change was the reduction of the No Development Zone (NDZ) in CRZ-III-A from 200 metres to 50 metres from the High Tide Line (HTL), while CRZ-III-B retains a 200-metre NDZ. This density-based differentiation effectively opens densely populated rural coasts — such as parts of Kerala, Goa and West Bengal — to construction previously prohibited.

For the first time, the notification permits tourism infrastructure (temporary shacks, toilet blocks, changing rooms) in the NDZ of CRZ-III areas, subject to a minimum 10-metre setback from HTL and a cap of 2.5 % of the total NDZ area. It also introduces Floor Space Index (FSI) de-freezing for CRZ-II areas, allowing FSI as per prevailing town planning regulations rather than the frozen 1991 levels, a move criticised for enabling high-rise densification in Mumbai, Chennai and Kochi without revised carrying-capacity studies.

The clearance architecture was streamlined: only Category A projects (those requiring Environmental Clearance under the EIA Notification, 2006, plus specified activities like ports, harbours, offshore oil/gas) require MoEFCC approval with recommendation from the National Coastal Zone Management Authority (NCZMA). Category B projects are cleared by the State/UT Coastal Zone Management Authority (SCZMA/CZMA) without central reference. The 2019 notification also introduced a post-facto clearance pathway for violations committed before 2011, allowing regularisation upon payment of penalties — a provision the NGT in Vanashakti v. Union of India (2021) upheld but restricted to cases where the project proponent had applied for clearance before the violation was detected.

Implementation data reveals uneven enforcement. The NCZMA’s 2023 annual report shows 1,842 project proposals processed since 2019; 62 % were Category B, with an average SCZMA decision time of 140 days against the mandated 60. Kerala’s CZMA rejected 40 % of tourism proposals in CRZ-III-A citing inadequate disaster management plans, while Gujarat’s CZMA approved 92 % of industrial projects in CRZ-I(B) (intertidal zone) with conditions. The 2022 Coastal Zone Management Plans (CZMPs) — mandatory for each state under the notification — remain unapproved for eight states/UTs as of June 2024, leaving those coasts governed by the 2011 maps. The Comptroller and Auditor General’s 2023 audit of CRZ compliance in six states found that 34 % of approved projects had not submitted mandatory half-yearly compliance reports, and satellite-based change detection by the National Centre for Sustainable Coastal Management (NCSCM) identified 1,200 hectares of unauthorised land-use change in CRZ-I areas between 2019–2023, of which only 18 % attracted penal action.

The notification creates a density-based distinction: CRZ-III-A (population density ≥2,161 per sq km) has an NDZ of 50 metres from HTL, while CRZ-III-B (density below that threshold) retains the 200-metre NDZ. National Clean Air Programme: City-Specific Targets, PRANA Portal and Mid-Term Review Findings

Launched in January 2019, the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) is a time-bound, national-level strategy to tackle air pollution across 131 non-attainment cities (cities violating National Ambient Air Quality Standards for five consecutive years). The initial target was a 20–30 % reduction in PM10 and PM2.5 concentrations by 2024 relative to the 2017 baseline. In 2022, the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) revised the target to 40 % reduction by 2025–26, acknowledging that the original timeline was unachievable without structural interventions. Each city was required to prepare a City Action Plan (CAP) addressing sectoral sources — transport, industry, biomass burning, road dust, construction & demolition (C&D) waste — with defined timelines and responsible agencies.

Monitoring is centralised through the PRANA (Portal for Regulation of Air-pollution in Non-Attainment cities) portal, launched in 2021. It tracks 42 action points across eight sectors, with quarterly physical and financial progress reporting by Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). The portal integrates Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Station (CAAQMS) data, but as of March 2024, only 98 of 131 cities had at least one CAAQMS; the rest rely on manual monitoring (NAMP network) with a 2–4 week lag, making real-time trend analysis impossible for a third of the programme.

The mid-term review (2023), conducted by the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Delhi and The Energy and Resources Institute (TERI), analysed 2019–2023 data for 131 cities. Key findings: only 27 cities achieved a ≥20 % PM10 reduction; 48 cities showed marginal improvement (5–20 %); 34 cities recorded no change; and 22 cities saw PM10 levels increase. Varanasi, Talcher and Dhanbad were among the worst performers, with PM10 rising 15–22 %. The review identified three systemic gaps: (i) source apportionment studies — mandatory under CAPs — were completed for only 56 cities; the rest used generic emission inventories. (ii) fund utilisation — of ₹9,650 crore allocated (XV Finance Commission grants + NCAP central share), only ₹4,180 crore (43 %) was spent by March 2023, with ULBs citing procurement delays and lack of technical staff. (iii) inter-departmental coordination — CAPs assign actions to transport, industry, agriculture, PWD, but no city has a functional air quality management committee with statutory powers; most operate as advisory bodies chaired by the Municipal Commissioner without enforcement authority over state departments.

In response, the 2024–25 budget introduced a performance-linked grant component: cities achieving ≥15 % annual PM10 reduction receive an additional 10 % of their XV Finance Commission air quality grant. The MoEFCC also mandated that all 131 cities complete source apportionment by December 2024 and integrate CAPs with Master Plans under the Town and Country Planning Acts. However, the absence of a legal framework — NCAP remains a scheme, not a notified programme under the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 — means non-compliance attracts no penalty beyond grant withholding.

Battery Waste Management Rules 2022: Extended Producer Responsibility, Collection Targets and Critical Mineral Recovery

The Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022, notified on 24 August 2022 under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, replaced the Batteries (Management and Handling) Rules, 2001, expanding scope from lead-acid batteries to all battery chemistries — lithium-ion, nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride, zinc-air, and others — regardless of size or application (electric vehicles, consumer electronics, energy storage systems, industrial). The rules impose Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) on producers (manufacturers, importers, assemblers, re-branders), requiring them to ensure collection, refurbishment, and recycling of waste batteries through authorised entities. Producers must register on the Centralised EPR Portal developed by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and file annual returns.

The rules prescribe staggered collection targets (percentage of batteries placed in the market): 30 % for 2024–25, 40 % for 2025–26, 50 % for 2026–27, 60 % for 2027–28, and 70 % for 2028–29 onwards. Recycling/recovery targets are chemistry-specific: for lithium-ion, 90 % recovery of cobalt, nickel, copper by 2026–27 and 95 % by 2030–31; for lead-acid, 90 % lead recovery by 2024–25. Producers can meet obligations individually or through Producer Responsibility Organisations (PROs). The portal issues EPR certificates (tradable credits) to recyclers/refurbishers based on verified recovery quantities; producers purchase these certificates to demonstrate compliance. As of March 2024, 4,200 producers and 380 recyclers were registered; 1.2 lakh tonnes of battery waste were reported collected in 2023–24, against an estimated 3.5 lakh tonnes generated — a 34 % collection rate, below the 30 % target for the first compliance year.

Critical mineral recovery is a strategic objective. The rules mandate that recyclers achieve minimum recovery efficiencies for lithium (70 % by 2026–27, 90 % by 2030–31), cobalt (90 % by 2026–27), nickel (90 % by 2026–27). The Ministry of Mines’ 2023 Critical Mineral Strategy identifies battery recycling as a primary domestic source for these minerals, targeting 20 % of lithium demand from recycling by 2030. However, the recycling infrastructure is nascent: only 12 facilities have CPCB authorisation for lithium-ion recycling (combined capacity 45,000 tonnes/year), mostly pilot-scale hydrometallurgical units. The 2024 Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) Battery Storage includes a recycling incentive (₹1,500 per kWh of recycled capacity), but no project has been approved under this component yet.

Enforcement challenges persist. The rules require consumer awareness (deposit-refund schemes, collection centres at dealerships), but a 2023 CSE survey in 10 cities found that 78 % of EV two-wheeler dealers had no collection arrangement. The informal sector — which handles an estimated 60 % of lead-acid and 90 % of small lithium-ion batteries — operates outside the portal, using crude smelting with high environmental and health risks. The CPCB’s 2023 guidelines for integration of informal recyclers propose registration as “collection agents” with mandatory PPE and effluent treatment, but state SPCBs have not operationalised this. The 2024 NGT order in Social Action for Forest and Environment v. Union of India directed the CPCB to audit all registered recyclers for actual recovery efficiency versus claimed certificates, with the first audit report due by September 2024.

Green Credit Programme 2023: Methodologies, Registry Design and Additionality Verification

The Green Credit Programme (GCP), notified on 13 October 2023 under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, establishes a voluntary domestic market mechanism to incentivise environment-positive actions beyond regulatory compliance. It covers eight sectors: (1) tree plantation, (2) water conservation, (3) sustainable agriculture, (4) waste management, (5) air pollution reduction, (6) mangrove conservation and restoration, (7) eco-mark (environment-friendly products), and (8) sustainable building and infrastructure. Each sector has a detailed methodology document specifying eligible activities, baseline determination, quantification metrics, monitoring frequency, and verification protocols. For instance, the tree plantation methodology requires a minimum 0.5 hectare contiguous area, native species composition ≥80 %, survival rate ≥70 % at year 3, and permanence commitment of 30 years; credits are issued annually based on verified biomass increment using allometric equations calibrated for Indian species.

The Green Credit Registry, hosted by the Indian Council of Forestry Research and Education (ICFRE), is the central platform for project registration, credit issuance, transfer, and retirement. Projects undergo a two-stage verification: (i) Validation by an empaneled Validation and Verification Body (VVB) — currently 12 firms including QCI-NABET accredited agencies — assessing methodology compliance and additionality; (ii) Verification at each monitoring period (annual for plantation, biennial for water/agriculture) confirming achieved outcomes. Credits are issued as unique serialised units (1 Green Credit = 1 unit of quantified environmental outcome, e.g., 1 tonne CO2e sequestered, 1,000 m3 water saved, 1 tonne waste diverted). They are tradable on a bilateral basis (no exchange yet) and can be retired by buyers to meet voluntary sustainability commitments or, potentially, future regulatory obligations.

Additionality is the programme’s core integrity test. The methodology requires demonstration that the activity: (a) is not mandated by any law, regulation, or court order; (b) exceeds the baseline scenario (business-as-usual) determined through approved tools (e.g., CDM Tool 01 for plantation, IPCC 2006 Guidelines for waste); and (c) faces at least one barrier (financial, technological, institutional) that the green credit revenue helps overcome. The 2024 GCP Integrity Guidelines issued by ICFRE introduce a “positive list” for certain activities (e.g., mangrove restoration in cyclone-prone districts, agroforestry in rainfed areas) deemed automatically additional, and a “negative list” excluding activities already subsidised under central schemes (MGNREGA plantation, Jal Jeevan Mission water structures) to prevent double-counting.

Early implementation data (as of June 2024) shows 1,840 projects registered across 22 states, with tree plantation (62 %) and water conservation (18 %) dominating. Only 340 projects have completed validation; 89 have received first verification and 1.2 million Green Credits issued. The price discovery is opaque: bilateral trades reported in the range ₹150–₹450 per credit for plantation, ₹300–₹800 for mangrove. The Ministry has proposed linking GCP with the Green Credit Trading Platform under development by the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE), and exploring interoperability with the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) — but the two markets remain distinct: GCP credits represent non-carbon co-benefits (biodiversity, water, soil) and cannot be used for CCTS compliance. The 2024–25 Economic Survey flagged permanence risk (e.g., plantation reversal due to fire, land-use change) and leakage (water conservation in one watershed increasing extraction downstream) as unresolved methodological challenges requiring dynamic baseline updates and buffer pools.

Loss and Damage Finance: India’s Negotiating Position and Domestic Institutional Readiness

The concept of loss and damage (L&D) refers to climate impacts that exceed adaptation limits, including irreversible economic losses (e.g., destroyed infrastructure) and non‑economic losses (e.g., cultural heritage). At COP27 in Sharm El‑Sheikh, parties agreed to establish a dedicated fund, and the operational modalities were adopted at COP28 in Dubai. India has consistently argued that the fund must be anchored in the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR‑RC), insisting that historical emitters bear the primary financial burden. In its submissions, India emphasised that the fund should be new, additional, and predictable, and that it must not be conflated with existing adaptation finance channels such as the Green Climate Fund (GCF) or the Adaptation Fund.

Domestically, India’s institutional architecture for L&D is still evolving. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, coordinates post‑disaster relief and reconstruction, but its mandate is reactive rather than anticipatory. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) has initiated a “Loss and Damage Assessment Framework” to quantify climate‑induced losses at the state level, drawing on the National Remote Sensing Centre’s satellite data and the India Meteorological Department’s extreme‑event records. Pilot assessments have been completed in Odisha (cyclone‑prone) and Ladakh (glacial lake outburst floods), providing baseline loss estimates that could inform future fund‑access proposals.

A critical gap remains the absence of a dedicated national L&D financing window. The National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) and the State Disaster Response Funds (SDRF) are earmarked for adaptation and immediate relief, respectively, and cannot be retrofitted for irreversible loss compensation without legislative amendment. The 2023‑24 Union Budget announced a “Climate Resilience and Loss‑Damage Corpus” of ₹500 crore, but operational guidelines are pending. Until a clear legal instrument — either an amendment to the Disaster Management Act or a standalone Climate Loss and Damage Act — defines eligibility, governance, and disbursement criteria, India’s ability to draw from the international fund will remain constrained.

India’s negotiating strategy also leverages its role as a voice for the Global South. At the 2024 Bonn intersessional, India co‑sponsored a proposal for a “Vulnerability‑Weighted Allocation Formula” that would weight fund disbursements by a composite index of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. The proposal is still under discussion, but it signals India’s intent to shape the fund’s governance architecture rather than merely receive allocations.

Strategic Environmental Assessment for Sectoral Policies: Legal Basis and Pilot Applications

Strategic Environmental Assessment (SEA) differs from project‑level Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) by evaluating the environmental consequences of policies, plans, and programmes (PPPs) before they are finalised. In India, the EIA Notification, 2006, under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, applies only to listed projects; there is no statutory SEA requirement for sectoral policies such as the National Steel Policy, the Hydrocarbon Vision 2030, or the National Water Policy. However, the 2015 “Guidelines for Strategic Environmental Assessment” issued by the MoEFCC provide a voluntary framework for ministries to integrate environmental considerations at the policy formulation stage.

The first formal SEA pilot was undertaken for the Draft National Water Policy (2020) by the National Institute of Hydrology, Roorkee. The assessment identified cumulative impacts of proposed inter‑basin transfers on downstream ecosystems, groundwater recharge, and sediment regimes. Its recommendations — mandatory basin‑level water accounting, ecological flow norms, and a moratorium on new large dams in ecologically sensitive zones — were partially incorporated into the final policy. A second pilot, commissioned by the Ministry of Power for the “National Electricity Plan 2022‑27”, examined the land‑use, air‑quality, and water‑stress implications of the projected 450 GW renewable capacity addition. The SEA highlighted that solar parks in Rajasthan and Gujarat could exacerbate groundwater depletion if not coupled with dry‑cooling technologies, prompting the Ministry to issue a directive for water‑neutral solar park design.

Despite these pilots, SEA remains non‑mandatory. The 2022 “Draft National Policy on Strategic Environmental Assessment” circulated for public consultation proposes to amend the Environment (Protection) Act to require SEA for all central government PPPs exceeding a defined threshold (e.g., investment >₹10,000 crore or affecting >1 million hectares). The draft also envisages a Central SEA Authority, chaired by the Secretary, MoEFCC, with representation from NITI Aayog, the concerned line ministry, and independent experts. Stakeholder consultations revealed resistance from infrastructure ministries citing procedural delays; the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways argued that the existing EIA process for individual highway projects suffices.

Judicial nudges have reinforced the need for SEA. In Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996), the Supreme Court recognised the precautionary principle and polluter‑pays principle as part of domestic law, implicitly supporting upstream assessment. More recently, the National Green Tribunal (NGT) in Goa Foundation v. Union of India (2021) directed the Ministry of Mines to conduct a strategic assessment of the cumulative impact of mining leases in the Western Ghats before granting new clearances. The Tribunal observed that project‑by‑project EIA cannot capture landscape‑scale fragmentation.

Operationalising SEA will require capacity building at the state level, where most sectoral plans (e.g., State Water Policies, State Industrial Policies) are drafted. The MoEFCC’s 2023‑24 capacity‑building programme, in partnership with the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), has trained 120 officials from 15 states on SEA methodology, including scenario analysis, cumulative impact mapping, and public participation techniques. The next step is to embed SEA into the budgetary cycle so that environmental screening informs allocation decisions, not just post‑hoc clearance.

Emerging Contaminants Regulation: PFAS, Microplastics and the Draft Chemicals Management Framework

Per‑ and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), often termed “forever chemicals”, and microplastics have emerged as priority contaminants globally, yet India’s regulatory regime addresses them only indirectly. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, and the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981, empower State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) to set standards for “any pollutant”, but no specific PFAS or microplastic standards have been notified. The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) has published IS 17088:2019 for “Guidelines for the detection of microplastics in water”, which is a test method, not a permissible limit.

The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) released the “Draft National Chemicals Management Policy” in 2022, followed by the “Draft Chemicals (Management and Safety) Rules” in 2023. The draft framework proposes a risk‑based classification of chemicals into three tiers — “Priority Substances”, “Substances of Concern”, and “Low‑Risk Substances” — based on persistence, bioaccumulation, toxicity (PBT) and very persistent, very bioaccumulative (vPvB) criteria aligned with the Stockholm Convention. PFAS compounds such as PFOA and PFOS are listed as Priority Substances, triggering mandatory registration, restriction on non‑essential uses, and a phase‑out schedule within ten years of notification. Microplastics are addressed under a separate “Plastic Pollution” chapter that proposes a ban on primary microplastic beads in personal‑care products by 2025 and a requirement for wastewater treatment plants to install tertiary filtration capable of capturing particles <5 mm.

Industry stakeholders, represented by the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FICCI), have raised concerns about the lack of domestic analytical capacity for PFAS detection at parts‑per‑trillion levels. The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) has responded by accrediting three laboratories — National Environmental Engineering Research Institute (NEERI), Indian Institute of Toxicology Research (IITR), and the Centre for Environmental Science and Engineering (CESE) at IIT Bombay — for PFAS analysis using LC‑MS/MS. A national monitoring programme, “PFAS Watch”, was launched in 2024 covering 30 major river basins; preliminary data indicate detectable PFOS concentrations in the Yamuna (up to 12 ng/L) and the Ganga (up to 8 ng/L), well above the EU’s proposed environmental quality standard of 0.65 ng/L.

On microplastics, the Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules, 2022, introduced Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) for plastic packaging but did not set microplastic‑specific targets. The 2023 draft rules propose a “Microplastic Release Reduction Target” for synthetic textile manufacturers — 30 % reduction in fibre shedding by 2028, measured through standardized washing tests. The Textile Ministry has initiated a “Green Textile Mission” to fund research into low‑shedding yarns and closed‑loop water recycling in dyeing units.

Judicial intervention remains limited. The NGT in Mahesh Chandra Mehta v. Union of India (2022) directed the CPCB to formulate guidelines for PFOS/PFOA in drinking water within six months; the CPCB submitted a draft guideline proposing a provisional limit of 70 ng/L for combined PFOS+PFOA, pending final notification. For microplastics, the Supreme Court in Almitra H. Patel v. Union of India (2023) refused to entertain a PIL seeking a ban, citing the absence of scientific consensus on health thresholds, but urged the MoEFCC to expedite the draft rules.

The overarching challenge is coordination: PFAS regulation straddles the Ministry of Chemicals and Fertilisers (industrial production), the Ministry of Health (drinking water standards), and the MoEFCC (environmental release). The draft Chemicals Management Framework proposes a National Chemicals Authority (NCA) to serve as a single regulatory hub, but its establishment requires legislative backing. Until the NCA is operational, enforcement will rely on existing SPCB powers, which vary widely across states.

SEBI’s Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting Framework: Scope, Assurance and Enforcement

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) introduced the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) framework in May 2021, replacing the earlier Business Responsibility Report (BRR). BRSR is mandatory for the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation from the financial year 2022‑23, and voluntary for others. The framework aligns with the nine principles of the National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC) and incorporates the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) Standards, the Task Force on Climate‑related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) recommendations, and the Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB) industry‑specific metrics.

BRSR comprises two sections: “General Disclosures” (company profile, governance, stakeholder engagement) and “Principle‑wise Performance” covering environment (Principle 6), social (Principles 3, 4, 5), and governance (Principles 1, 2, 7, 8, 9). Environmental disclosures require quantitative data on greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Scope 1, 2, and optional Scope 3), energy consumption, water withdrawal, waste generation, and biodiversity impact. Companies must report intensity ratios (e.g., tonnes CO₂e per ₹ crore revenue) and set targets for reduction, though target‑setting is not yet mandatory.

Assurance is a critical credibility layer. SEBI’s 2022 circular “Enhancing Credibility of BRSR” encourages listed entities to obtain “reasonable assurance” from an independent assurance provider accredited by the National Accreditation Board for Certification Bodies (NABCB) or an equivalent international body. As of March 2024, approximately 38 % of the mandatory filers had obtained external assurance, up from 12 % in the first year. The assurance scope typically covers GHG emissions, energy, and water metrics; social indicators are less frequently assured.

Enforcement mechanisms are evolving. SEBI has the power to impose monetary penalties under Section 15HA of the SEBI Act for non‑compliance with listing obligations, including BRSR filing. In 2023, SEBI issued show‑cause notices to 27 companies for incomplete BRSR disclosures — primarily missing Scope 3 emissions and biodiversity data. Six entities were fined ₹ 25 lakh each for repeated non‑compliance. The regulator also mandated that the BRSR be hosted on the company’s website and linked in the annual report, facilitating public scrutiny.

Integration with other regulatory regimes is underway. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) has aligned the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014, to require that the Board’s Report include a reference to the BRSR. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) in its 2023 “Framework for Climate‑Related Financial Risk” directed scheduled commercial banks to consider BRSR data of borrowers in credit appraisal. The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) has proposed that insurers use BRSR disclosures for underwriting climate‑risk exposure.

Challenges remain. Data comparability is hampered by varying methodological choices — e.g., some firms use the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard, others the ISO 14064‑1. SEBI’s 2024 consultation paper proposes a “BRSR Core” — a minimum set of 25 quantitative indicators with prescribed calculation methodologies — to improve comparability. The paper also explores mandatory assurance for the Core indicators from FY 2025‑26. Stakeholder feedback highlights the cost burden on mid‑cap firms; SEBI has indicated a phased approach, starting with the top 250 companies.

Climate Risk Stress Testing for Scheduled Commercial Banks: RBI’s 2023 Guidelines and Scenario Design

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issued the “Guidelines on Climate Risk Stress Testing for Scheduled Commercial Banks” in July 2023, marking the first supervisory mandate for forward‑looking climate risk assessment in the Indian banking sector. The guidelines require banks to conduct annual stress tests covering both physical risks (acute events such as floods, cyclones; chronic trends such as heat stress, sea‑level rise) and transition risks (policy shifts, technology disruption, market sentiment changes affecting carbon‑intensive sectors).

Scenario design follows the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) framework, adapted to Indian contexts. Three core scenarios are prescribed: (1) “Orderly Transition” — early, coordinated policy action limiting warming to 1.5 °C, with carbon pricing rising to ₹ 4,500 tCO₂e by 2030; (2) “Disorderly Transition” — delayed action, abrupt policy tightening post‑2030, carbon price jumping to ₹ 12,000 tCO₂e; (3) “Hot House World” — no additional policy, warming exceeding 3 °C, with severe physical impacts. Banks must also develop a “bank‑specific” scenario reflecting their portfolio concentration (e.g., high exposure to coal‑fired power in Chhattisgarh).

The stress‑testing methodology requires banks to map exposures at the borrower‑level using sectoral classification (National Industrial Classification 2008) and geolocation of assets. Physical risk modelling uses downscaled climate projections from the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM) — temperature, precipitation, and extreme‑event frequency at 25 km resolution. Transition risk modelling incorporates the International Energy Agency (IEA) India Energy Outlook pathways for electricity, steel, cement, and transport sectors. Banks must estimate probability of default (PD) and loss given default (LGD) shifts under each scenario over a 5‑year horizon, and aggregate to portfolio‑level capital impact.

Data gaps are a major implementation hurdle. Most banks lack granular borrower‑level emissions data; the RBI has permitted the use of proxy emission factors from the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) for power sector borrowers and from the Ministry of Steel for integrated steel plants. The guidelines encourage banks to participate in the “Climate Data Repository” being built by the Indian Banks’ Association (IBA) in collaboration with the National Institute of Bank Management (NIBM). As of December 2024, 42 of 52 scheduled commercial banks have submitted their first stress‑test results; the aggregate capital depletion under the Disorderly Transition scenario ranged from 0.3 % to 1.8 % of Tier‑1 capital across banks.

Supervisory follow‑up includes a “Climate Risk Scorecard” for each bank, integrating stress‑test outcomes, governance quality (board oversight, dedicated climate risk unit), and disclosure adequacy (alignment with TCFD). Banks scoring below a threshold will be subject to enhanced monitoring, including higher Pillar 2 capital add‑ons. The RBI has also signalled that climate risk performance will feed into the Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, though no formal amendment has been notified.

International benchmarking shows India’s approach is more prescriptive than the European Central Bank’s (ECB) 2022 climate stress test, which allowed banks to choose scenarios. The RBI’s mandatory three‑scenario set ensures comparability but may under‑represent region‑specific risks such as Himalayan glacial lake outburst floods affecting hydropower loans in Uttarakhand. The 2024‑25 review cycle is expected to introduce a “dynamic scenario” that updates policy assumptions annually based on actual legislative developments (e.g., carbon market launch under CCTS).

Blended Finance Instruments for Climate Mitigation and Adaptation: Structure, Risk Sharing and Indian Deployments

Blended finance combines public, philanthropic and private capital to de‑risk climate projects that would otherwise be unattractive to commercial investors. In India the most common structures are first‑loss guarantees, concessional senior debt, mezzanine equity and results‑based financing. The National Climate Finance Facility (NCFF), housed in the Department of Economic Affairs, channels funds from the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and bilateral donors into a single pipeline. As of March 2024 the NCFF had approved 27 projects worth ₹ 12,400 crore, with a blended ratio of 1:3 (public : private). The first‑loss guarantee provided by the GCF covers up to 20 % of senior debt, allowing banks such as State Bank of India and Punjab National Bank to lend at 150‑200 bps below market rates for solar‑pump irrigation in Maharashtra and for climate‑resilient housing in Odisha.

Risk allocation follows a tiered approach: (1) sovereign or multilateral guarantees absorb policy and regulatory risk; (2) development finance institutions (DFIs) such as NABARD and SIDBI take subordinated debt positions; (3) private equity funds contribute senior equity with a target internal rate of return of 12‑15 %. The 2022 “Solar for All” programme used a mezzanine facility from the Asian Development Bank (ADB) that absorbed the first 10 % of losses, enabling a consortium of domestic NBFCs to raise ₹ 3,200 crore for rooftop installations across 12 states. Results‑based financing, piloted in the National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC), releases tranches only after verified adaptation outcomes — such as a 15 % reduction in flood‑damage claims in Assam’s Brahmaputra basin — are confirmed by an independent verifier.

Key challenges include the absence of a standardized taxonomy for “green” assets, limited data on project‑level climate outcomes, and the high transaction cost of structuring bespoke blends. The 2023 RBI discussion paper on “Green Taxonomy for Blended Finance” proposes a three‑tier classification (core, transitional, enabling) aligned with the EU Taxonomy but calibrated to Indian sectoral pathways. Adoption of this taxonomy is expected to lower due‑diligence costs by 30‑40 % and to unlock an additional ₹ 8,000 crore of private capital by 2027.

Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism: Sectoral Exposure Assessment and Policy Response Options for India

The European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered its transitional phase on 1 October 2023 and will become fully operational from 1 January 2026. It imposes a carbon price on imports of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen, mirroring the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) allowance price. Indian exporters face direct exposure in three high‑volume sectors: steel (≈ 12 Mt exported to the EU in FY 2023‑24), aluminium (≈ 1.1 Mt) and cement (≈ 0.6 Mt). Using the 2023 EU ETS average price of € 85 tCO₂e, the implicit carbon cost on Indian steel exports could reach € 1.1 billion annually if no domestic carbon price is recognised.

India’s current policy response rests on three pillars. First, the Ministry of Steel has accelerated the National Steel Policy 2017 target of 300 Mt capacity by 2030, coupling it with a voluntary “Green Steel” certification that mandates a minimum 20 % reduction in specific emissions (tCO₂/t steel) relative to the 2020 baseline. Second, the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) is finalising a Perform‑Achieve‑Trade (PAT) cycle for the aluminium sector that will allocate tradable energy‑saving certificates, effectively creating a domestic carbon price signal. Third, the Department of Commerce has initiated a “CBAM Dialogue” with the EU to negotiate mutual recognition of India’s forthcoming Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) allowances, which are slated for launch in FY 2025‑26 under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022.

At the firm level, major producers such as Tata Steel, JSW Steel and Hindalco have announced capital expenditure of ₹ 45,000 crore over the next five years for hydrogen‑based direct reduced iron (DRI) and inert anode aluminium smelting. The Ministry of Finance’s 2024‑25 budget introduced a “Green Export Credit” line of ₹ 5,000 crore at 4 % interest for firms that achieve verified emission intensity reductions of at least 15 % by 2027. Simultaneously, the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) is updating the grid emission factor to reflect the rising share of renewable generation, which will lower the indirect emissions attributed to electricity‑intensive exports.

Green Public Procurement Framework: Mandatory Criteria, Monitoring Mechanism and Fiscal Impact

The Government e‑Marketplace (GeM) launched the Green Public Procurement (GPP) Guidelines in July 2022, making it mandatory for all central ministries and departments to allocate at least 25 % of their annual procurement value to products and services meeting the “Green Category” criteria. The criteria are derived from the Environment‑Friendly Product (EFP) certification issued by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) and cover energy efficiency (BEE star rating ≥ 4), recycled content (≥ 30 % for paper, plastics, metals), and life‑cycle carbon footprint below sectoral benchmarks published by the National Institute of Urban Affairs (NIUA).

Monitoring is performed through the GeM “Green Dashboard”, which auto‑populates procurement data from the Integrated Financial Management System (IFMS). As of December 2024, 38 % of central procurement value (₹ 1.12 lakh crore) complied with the 25 % threshold, up from 19 % in FY 2022‑23. The dashboard flags non‑compliant ministries for quarterly review by the Department of Expenditure, which can withhold up to 5 % of the ministry’s budgetary allocation until targets are met. State governments are encouraged but not mandated to adopt the same threshold; 14 states have issued parallel GPP orders, with Karnataka and Tamil Nadu setting a 30 % target for FY 2025‑26.

Fiscal impact assessments by the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy (NIPFP) estimate that the GPP mandate generates a net present value (NPV) gain of ₹ 3,800 crore over a 10‑year horizon, driven by lower operational energy costs (average 12 % reduction in electricity bills for government buildings) and avoided carbon externality valued at the social cost of carbon (₹ 4,500 tCO₂e). The upfront price premium for green products averages 3‑7 % but is offset within 3‑4 years through operational savings. The 2024‑25 budget introduced a “Green Procurement Incentive Fund” of ₹ 1,200 crore to subsidise the price differential for micro‑ and small‑enterprises (MSEs) supplying certified goods, aiming to broaden the vendor base and reduce market concentration.

Extended Producer Responsibility for Textile and Apparel Waste: Regulatory Design, Collection Targets and Circular Business Models

The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) released the Draft Textile Waste Management Rules in March 2023, extending the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) framework to the textile and apparel sector for the first time. The rules define “producer” to include manufacturers, importers, brand owners and e‑commerce platforms placing textile products on the Indian market. Producers must register with the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and submit an annual EPR plan specifying collection, recycling and safe disposal targets.

Collection targets are phased: 15 % of the weight of textiles placed on the market in FY 2024‑25, rising to 30 % by FY 2026‑27 and 50 % by FY 2030‑31. At least 60 % of collected waste must be directed to authorised recycling facilities that meet the “Textile Recycling Standard” (TRS‑2023) issued by the Textile Committee under the Ministry of Textiles. The remaining 40 % may be used for energy recovery in cement kilns, provided emissions comply with the Cement Plant Emission Standards (CPES‑2021). Producers failing to meet targets face environmental compensation of ₹ 25 kg⁻¹ of shortfall, payable to the State Pollution Control Board (SPCB).

Circular business models are emerging to meet these obligations. Large brands such as Arvind Limited and Reliance Retail have partnered with reverse‑logistics start‑ups (e.g., ReCircle, Saahas Zero Waste) to set up “take‑back kiosks” in 1,200 retail outlets across 15 states. The kiosks use QR‑code tracking to credit consumers with loyalty points, creating a data trail for EPR reporting. Pilot projects in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu have demonstrated a 22 % collection rate within the first year, exceeding the 15 % mandate. The CPCB’s 2024 annual report notes that 1,850 producers have registered, covering an estimated 68 % of the organised textile market. However, the unorganised sector — accounting for ~ 40 % of domestic textile consumption — remains largely outside the EPR net, prompting the MoEFCC to consider a “producer‑responsibility organisation” (PRO) model similar to the Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2022.

Inter‑State Water Governance under Climate Variability: Legal Instruments, Dispute Resolution and Adaptive Management

India’s inter‑state water governance rests on the Inter‑State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 (ISRWDA), which empowers the Centre to constitute tribunals for adjudicating disputes. Since 2018, three tribunals — Cauvery, Krishna and Mahanadi — have delivered final awards, yet implementation remains contested because the awards allocate fixed volumetric shares that do not account for climate‑driven flow variability. The 2021 “National Water Mission” (NWM) under the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) introduced the concept of “adaptive allocation”, recommending that tribunal awards incorporate a “climate adjustment factor” (CAF) revisited every five years based on observed runoff trends from the India Meteorological Department (IMD) and the Central Water Commission (CWC).

In practice, the Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA), established under the 2018 Supreme Court directive, operates a real‑time decision support system (DSS) that integrates reservoir storage, rainfall forecasts and crop‑water demand models. The DSS allocates daily releases using a rule‑curve that shifts from a “normal year” schedule to a “deficit year” schedule when the 30‑day rolling rainfall index falls below the 25th percentile. This mechanism reduced the 2023‑24 kharif water shortage in Tamil Nadu by an estimated 12 % compared with the static award‑based releases of the previous decade.

Financing adaptive management is addressed through the “River Basin Management Fund” (RBMF), created in the 2022‑23 budget with a corpus of ₹ 2,500 crore. The fund provides grants to states for installing telemetry, modernising canal automation and building flood‑plain storage. Disbursement is conditional on the state submitting a “Basin Adaptation Plan” approved by the respective River Basin Organisation (RBO). As of March 2024, six basins — Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Narmada, Brahmaputra and Indus — have operational RBOs, but only Godavari and Narmada have submitted approved plans, unlocking ₹ 620 crore. The remaining basins face delays due to inter‑state disagreements over data sharing protocols, highlighting the need for a standardized hydro‑meteorological data exchange framework under the proposed “National Water Data Policy” (draft 2023).
