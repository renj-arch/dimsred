1. Structure and Presentation of the Union Budget

1.1 Constitutional Basis and Annual Financial Statement

The Union Budget derives its legitimacy from Article 112 of the Constitution, which obliges the President to cause a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure to be laid before each House of Parliament every financial year. This statement, known as the Annual Financial Statement, separates the Consolidated Fund, the Contingency Fund and the Public Account, thereby establishing a clear legal hierarchy for government money. The Consolidated Fund receives all revenues, loans and recoveries, and no money can be withdrawn without parliamentary authorisation, which makes the budget a binding legislative instrument rather than a mere policy document.

1.2 Components of the Budget Documents

In practice the budget is presented as a bundle of documents that together give a complete picture of fiscal intent. The Finance Bill carries the tax proposals, the Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawals from the Consolidated Fund, the Macro‑Economic Framework Statement sets out the growth, inflation and fiscal assumptions, and the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement projects the fiscal trajectory for the next three years. Each document serves a distinct purpose: the Finance Bill translates policy into law, the Appropriation Bill provides the legal sanction for spending, while the macro‑economic and medium‑term statements enable Parliament and the public to assess the credibility of the numbers.

1.3 Classification of Expenditure and Receipts

Expenditure is classified into revenue and capital categories, and further into plan and non‑plan heads until the planning nomenclature was replaced by the scheme‑based classification in 2017‑18. Revenue expenditure covers interest payments, subsidies, salaries and pensions, which are recurring in nature, whereas capital expenditure creates assets or reduces liabilities. On the receipts side, the budget distinguishes between revenue receipts — tax and non‑tax revenues — and capital receipts, which include market borrowings, recoveries of loans and disinvestment proceeds. This classification is essential because the fiscal deficit, the most watched indicator, is defined as the excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings.

2. Deficit Concepts and Their Interrelationships

2.1 Revenue Deficit and Its Significance

The revenue deficit measures the shortfall of revenue receipts over revenue expenditure, indicating whether the government is able to meet its day‑to‑day obligations from current income. A persistent revenue deficit signals that the state is borrowing to finance consumption, which erodes the quality of public finances and limits the space for productive capital outlays. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003, originally targeted the elimination of the revenue deficit by 2008‑09, a goal that has been repeatedly postponed, reflecting the structural rigidity of subsidies and interest commitments.

2.2 Fiscal Deficit as the Core Indicator

The fiscal deficit, defined as total expenditure minus total receipts other than borrowings, captures the overall borrowing requirement of the government. It is the primary gauge used by rating agencies, the Reserve Bank of India and international investors to assess sovereign risk. A high fiscal deficit can crowd out private investment by pushing up interest rates, but a moderate deficit financed by productive capital spending may raise the economy’s potential output. The FRBM framework therefore sets a fiscal deficit target as a percentage of GDP, currently 3.5 per cent for the centre, with a glide path that allows temporary deviations during crises such as the COVID‑19 pandemic.

2.3 Primary Deficit and Debt Dynamics

The primary deficit strips out interest payments from the fiscal deficit, revealing the borrowing needed to finance current operations excluding the cost of past debt. When the primary deficit is positive, the government must borrow even to pay interest, setting off a debt spiral. The relationship between the primary deficit, the nominal GDP growth rate and the average interest rate on debt determines whether the debt‑to‑GDP ratio stabilises, rises or falls. If nominal GDP growth exceeds the effective interest rate, a modest primary deficit can still be consistent with a declining debt ratio, a condition that underpins the sustainability analysis in the medium‑term fiscal policy statement.

Worked Example: Deriving Primary Deficit from Budget Data
Assume the Union Budget for a given year reports the following figures (in lakh crore rupees): Revenue Receipts 20, Revenue Expenditure 24, Capital Expenditure 6, Interest Payments 5, Non‑debt Capital Receipts 2. Calculate the revenue deficit, fiscal deficit and primary deficit.
Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts = 24 − 20 = 4 lakh crore. Total Expenditure = Revenue Expenditure + Capital Expenditure = 24 + 6 = 30 lakh crore. Total Receipts excluding borrowings = Revenue Receipts + Non‑debt Capital Receipts = 20 + 2 = 22 lakh crore. Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding borrowings = 30 − 22 = 8 lakh crore. Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments = 8 − 5 = 3 lakh crore. The primary deficit of 3 lakh crore shows that even after removing interest obligations, the government still needs to borrow to finance its operations.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the “primary deficit” but provides only the fiscal deficit and interest payments, remember the simple subtraction; however, if the data gives revenue and capital figures separately, first reconstruct the fiscal deficit using the definition before isolating the primary component.

3. Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Framework

3.1 Legislative Genesis and Objectives

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, enacted in 2003 and amended in 2012 and 2018, was a legislative response to the persistent fiscal imbalances of the 1990s and early 2000s. Its core objectives are to ensure inter‑generational equity in fiscal management, to provide flexibility to the central government in counter‑cyclical fiscal policy, and to enhance transparency through mandatory disclosure statements. The Act binds the centre to quantified targets for revenue deficit, fiscal deficit and total outstanding liabilities, while allowing an escape clause for national security, calamity or severe economic downturn.

3.2 Target Architecture and Escape Clauses

The original FRBM trajectory aimed at a revenue deficit of zero and a fiscal deficit of 3 per cent of GDP by 2008‑09. Subsequent amendments shifted the fiscal deficit target to 3.5 per cent for 2020‑21, with a glide path to 3 per cent by 2025‑26. The 2018 amendment introduced a “debt‑to‑GDP” anchor of 40 per cent for the centre and 20 per cent for states, and a “fiscal deficit‑to‑GDP” anchor of 3 per cent. The escape clause permits a deviation of up to 0.5 percentage points in any year, provided the finance minister tables a statement explaining the reasons and a roadmap for returning to the path. This clause was invoked in 2020‑21 and 2021‑22 to accommodate pandemic‑related spending.

3.3 Monitoring, Reporting and the Role of the Comptroller and Auditor General

The Act mandates the presentation of a Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement, a Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement and a Macro‑Economic Framework Statement alongside the budget. The Comptroller and Auditor General of India audits the accounts and certifies whether the fiscal targets have been met, reporting any deviations to Parliament. The Finance Commission also uses the FRBM framework as a reference when recommending the vertical and horizontal distribution of tax revenues, linking fiscal discipline to the devolution formula. This institutional architecture creates a feedback loop where fiscal rules, audit scrutiny and inter‑governmental transfers jointly shape the fiscal stance.

4. Taxation Reforms and the Goods and Services Tax

4.1 Evolution from a Fragmented Indirect Tax Regime

Before 2017 India’s indirect tax landscape was a mosaic of central excise, service tax, state value‑added tax, central sales tax, entry tax and a host of cesses. This fragmentation created cascading taxes, compliance multiplicity and barriers to inter‑state trade, reducing the efficiency of resource allocation. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, empowered Parliament and state legislatures to make laws on goods and services tax, paving the way for a unified national market. The GST Council, a federal body chaired by the Union Finance Minister and comprising state finance ministers, was constituted to recommend rates, exemptions and threshold limits, embodying cooperative federalism in tax policy.

4.2 Structure of the GST and Revenue Implications

The GST is a destination‑based, multi‑stage tax levied on the supply of goods and services, with input tax credit available across the value chain. It subsumes most central and state indirect taxes, except a few items such as petroleum products, alcoholic liquor and electricity, which remain outside the GST net. The tax is administered through a dual model: Central GST (CGST) collected by the centre, State GST (SGST) collected by the states, and Integrated GST (IGST) on inter‑state supplies, which is apportioned between the centre and the destination state. The revenue neutrality principle guided the initial rate structure, but the multiple slabs — 5, 12, 18 and 28 per cent — plus cesses on luxury and sin goods have complicated compliance and invited criticism for deviating from a single‑rate ideal.

4.3 Compensation Mechanism and Fiscal Federalism

To secure state consent, the GST legislation guaranteed compensation for any revenue loss for five years, financed through a compensation cess on luxury and demerit goods. The COVID‑19 shock caused a sharp shortfall in cess collections, leading to a dispute over the centre’s obligation to borrow on behalf of states. The centre eventually borrowed and passed on the funds as back‑to‑back loans, preserving the states’ fiscal space without violating the FRBM borrowing limits. This episode highlighted the tension between a rigid compensation guarantee and the centre’s own fiscal rules, and it spurred discussions on extending the compensation period or restructuring the cess.

5. Subsidies, Public Debt and Sustainability

5.1 Classification and Economic Rationale of Subsidies

Subsidies in India are broadly classified into explicit budgetary subsidies — such as food, fertilizer and petroleum — and implicit subsidies arising from under‑pricing of public goods like electricity and water. The economic rationale for explicit subsidies is to protect vulnerable populations, ensure food security and support strategic sectors, but they often suffer from leakages, mis‑targeting and fiscal opacity. The Direct Benefit Transfer platform, leveraging Aadhaar‑linked bank accounts, has reduced leakages in the LPG and fertilizer subsidies, yet the food subsidy bill remains large because of the open‑ended procurement policy under the National Food Security Act, 2013.

5.2 Public Debt Composition and Cost Dynamics

The central government’s debt portfolio consists of dated securities, treasury bills, special securities issued to the National Small Savings Fund, and external borrowings. The weighted average interest cost has declined from around 8.5 per cent in the early 2000s to below 6.5 per cent in recent years, reflecting monetary easing and a shift toward market‑based borrowing. However, the share of short‑term treasury bills has risen, increasing rollover risk. The debt‑to‑GDP ratio, which stood near 50 per cent before the pandemic, surged above 60 per cent in 2020‑21, prompting the FRBM review committee to recommend a medium‑term anchor of 40 per cent for the centre and 20 per cent for states, with a clear glide path.

5.3 Sustainability Assessment and Policy Levers

Debt sustainability hinges on the differential between the nominal GDP growth rate and the effective interest rate on debt, the primary deficit trajectory, and the maturity profile of liabilities. A favourable growth‑interest differential allows a higher primary deficit without destabilising the debt ratio. Policy levers include improving tax buoyancy through base broadening, rationalising subsidies by better targeting, extending the average maturity of borrowings, and developing a deep corporate bond market to diversify funding sources. The Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement explicitly models these variables, providing Parliament with a quantitative basis for judging whether the fiscal stance is sustainable over the next five years.

The integration of fiscal rules, tax reform, subsidy rationalisation and debt management creates a coherent framework that aims to balance developmental aspirations with macroeconomic stability.

Off‑Budget Borrowings and Extra‑Budgetary Resources

Off‑budget borrowings refer to liabilities incurred by public sector entities or special purpose vehicles that are not recorded in the Union Budget’s fiscal deficit but are ultimately serviced by the central government. The most prominent instruments have been the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) loans to the Food Corporation of India (FCI) for food‑subsidy operations, the issuance of oil bonds to oil‑marketing companies, and the recapitalisation bonds issued to public sector banks. Because these obligations do not appear in the headline fiscal deficit, they create a wedge between the reported deficit and the true fiscal stance, complicating the FRBM monitoring framework.

The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑20) flagged the growing stock of off‑budget liabilities, estimating them at roughly 1.5 % of GDP in 2016‑17. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) reiterated the need for transparency and recommended that all such borrowings be brought on‑budget within a defined time‑frame. In response, the 2021‑22 Budget announced the conversion of NSSF loans to FCI into regular budgetary support, and the 2022‑23 Budget provided for the phased redemption of oil bonds. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has consistently qualified its audit opinion on the Union Government’s accounts on the ground that off‑budget borrowings obscure the actual debt position.

From a macro‑economic perspective, off‑budget borrowings affect the debt‑to‑GDP ratio, the interest‑payment burden, and the crowding‑out of private investment. They also undermine the credibility of the fiscal consolidation path because the escape‑clause triggers in the FRBM Act are calibrated to the on‑budget fiscal deficit. Analysts therefore track the “augmented fiscal deficit”, which adds the net increase in off‑budget liabilities to the reported fiscal deficit, to assess the true fiscal impulse.

Key Vehicles and Their Fiscal Impact

  • NSSF‑FCI Loans: Used to finance the food subsidy bill without raising the budgetary food‑subsidy allocation. The interest cost is borne by the centre, and the principal is repaid through budgetary transfers.
  • Oil Bonds: Issued between 2005 and 2010 to compensate oil‑marketing companies for under‑recoveries. The bonds carried coupon rates of 7‑9 % and were redeemed from 2015‑16 onward, adding to the centre’s interest outgo.
  • Bank Recapitalisation Bonds: Issued in 2017‑18 and 2019‑20 to infuse capital into public sector banks. These are accounted as capital expenditure in the year of issuance but the interest servicing continues for the bond tenor.

Bringing these items on‑budget improves fiscal transparency, enables Parliament to scrutinise the full resource envelope, and aligns the fiscal deficit with the FRBM targets. However, it also raises the headline deficit in the transition years, requiring a calibrated communication strategy to avoid market over‑reaction.

Finance Commission Transfers and the Horizontal Distribution Formula

The Finance Commission (FC) constitutes the constitutional mechanism for vertical and horizontal fiscal transfers between the Union and the States. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25), chaired by N. K. Singh, recommended a vertical share of 41 % of the divisible pool for States, unchanged from the Fourteenth FC, but introduced a revised horizontal distribution formula that weights multiple criteria to balance equity, efficiency, and fiscal discipline.

The horizontal formula assigns the following weights: income distance (45 %), population (15 % based on the 2011 Census), area (15 %), forest and ecology (10 %), demographic performance (12.5 %), and tax effort (2.5 %). Income distance measures the per‑capita GSDP gap between a State and the highest‑income State, ensuring that poorer States receive a larger share. The demographic performance criterion rewards States that have achieved lower total fertility rates, incentivising population stabilisation. Tax effort captures the ratio of a State’s own‑tax revenue to its GSDP, encouraging revenue mobilisation.

The Commission also introduced a “grants‑in‑aid” component for specific purposes: revenue deficit grants for 17 States, grants for local bodies (rural and urban), disaster risk management, and sector‑specific grants for health, education, and agriculture. The revenue‑deficit grants are calibrated to close the post‑devolution revenue gap, ensuring that no State’s revenue expenditure exceeds its revenue receipts after tax devolution.

A notable innovation of the Fifteenth FC is the inclusion of a “performance‑based incentive” for States that undertake reforms in power‑sector finances, ease of doing business, and GST compliance. The incentive pool is modest (0.5 % of the divisible pool) but signals a shift toward outcome‑linked transfers. The Commission also recommended the creation of a “Fiscal Consolidation Fund” at the State level, to be monitored by an independent fiscal council, to enforce the FRBM‑type rules at the sub‑national level.

The horizontal formula has been contested by richer States, which argue that the high weight on income distance penalises fiscal prudence. The Centre, however, has accepted the formula in full, and the devolution for 2021‑22 to 2025‑26 is being implemented accordingly. The next Finance Commission (Sixteenth) will reassess the weights, especially in light of the 2021 Census data and the evolving GST revenue trajectory.

Technique: Deconstructing the Horizontal Formula
A question asks: “If a State’s per‑capita GSDP is 60 % of the highest‑income State, its population share is 5 % of the national total, and it scores average on all other criteria, which criterion contributes the largest share to its horizontal allocation?”
The horizontal allocation is a weighted sum of the six criteria. The weight for income distance is 45 %, the highest among all criteria. Even though the State’s income distance is only 60 % of the maximum, the 45 % weight ensures that this criterion contributes the largest portion of its total horizontal share. Population carries 15 %, area 15 %, forest and ecology 10 %, demographic performance 12.5 %, and tax effort 2.5 %. Therefore, income distance is the dominant driver.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the exact weightage of each horizontal criterion (45‑15‑15‑10‑12.5‑2.5). In MCQs, the examiner often swaps two weights (e.g., 12.5 % for tax effort). Write the formula on a rough sheet before attempting the question; a quick visual check prevents careless errors.

Outcome Budgeting, Gender Budgeting and Performance‑Based Allocation

Outcome budgeting was introduced in India in 2005‑06 on the recommendation of the Second Administrative Reforms Commission. It requires each ministry to link financial outlays to measurable outputs and outcomes, moving beyond the traditional input‑based line‑item budget. The Outcome Budget document presents a matrix of schemes, physical targets (e.g., kilometres of road constructed, number of households electrified) and the corresponding financial allocation, enabling Parliament to assess cost‑effectiveness.

Gender budgeting, also launched in 2005‑06, mandates that every ministry report the gender‑differentiated impact of its schemes. The Gender Budget Statement (Statement 13) classifies expenditures into three categories: women‑specific schemes (100 % women beneficiaries), pro‑women schemes (at least 30 % women beneficiaries), and gender‑neutral schemes. The Ministry of Women and Child Development monitors compliance, and the Finance Ministry issues guidelines for integrating gender analysis into the appraisal of new schemes.

Performance‑based allocation gained traction with the introduction of the “Performance Budget” for central ministries in 2017‑18, which ties a portion of the budgetary increment to the achievement of pre‑defined key performance indicators (KPIs). The NITI Aayog’s “Output‑Outcome Monitoring Framework” (OOMF) tracks quarterly progress against these KPIs, and the data feed into the Mid‑Year Review and the Annual Report of the Ministry of Finance.

Despite these reforms, implementation gaps persist. Many ministries treat outcome budgeting as a compliance exercise, recycling previous year’s targets without rigorous baseline surveys. Gender budgeting often remains confined to the Statement 13 annexure, with limited influence on scheme design. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended that outcome and gender budgeting be made mandatory for State budgets as a condition for receiving performance‑based grants, a suggestion that several States have begun to adopt.

Institutional Architecture

  • Department of Expenditure (DoE): Nodal agency for outcome budgeting; issues the “Outcome Budget Guidelines” annually.
  • Ministry of Women and Child Development: Oversees gender budgeting; conducts capacity‑building workshops for line ministries.
  • NITI Aayog: Develops the OOMF dashboard; publishes the “Performance Review of Central Ministries”.
  • Public Financial Management System (PFMS): Integrates financial and physical data for real‑time monitoring.

The convergence of these three budgeting streams — outcome, gender, and performance — represents a shift toward evidence‑based fiscal policy. Their effectiveness hinges on the quality of baseline data, the independence of monitoring agencies, and the willingness of political leadership to link fiscal rewards to verified results.

Sovereign Debt Management Strategy: Issuance, Buybacks and Cash Management

India’s sovereign debt management is coordinated by the Public Debt Management Cell (PDMC) under the Department of Economic Affairs, with the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) acting as the debt manager and banker to the government. The strategy aims to minimise the cost of borrowing over the medium term while containing rollover risk and maintaining a liquid secondary market for government securities (G‑secs).

The issuance calendar, published at the start of each fiscal year, specifies the weekly auction amounts for dated securities (2‑year to 40‑year tenors) and treasury bills (91‑day, 182‑day, 364‑day). The weighted average maturity of dated securities has been progressively extended — from about 9.5 years in 2010‑11 to over 12 years in 2022‑23 — to reduce refinancing pressure. The RBI conducts Open Market Operations (OMOs) and the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) to manage systemic liquidity, which indirectly influences G‑sec yields.

Buyback (or “switch”) operations allow the government to repurchase high‑coupon, near‑maturity securities and replace them with lower‑coupon, longer‑dated paper. The first large‑scale buyback was executed in 2015‑16, and subsequent operations have been used to smooth the redemption profile. In 2021‑22, the government bought back securities worth ₹1.2 lakh crore, lowering the average coupon on the portfolio by roughly 15 basis points.

Cash management bills (CMBs), introduced in 2010, are short‑dated instruments (typically 14‑91 days) issued to meet temporary cash mismatches. They are not part of the regular auction calendar and are deployed when the government’s cash balances with the RBI fall below the prescribed minimum. The use of CMBs has increased during periods of large tax‑refund outflows (e.g., March 2020) and during the COVID‑19 fiscal stimulus.

The proposed independent Debt Management Office (DMO), recommended by the FRBM Review Committee (2017) and the Kelkar Committee (2012), has not yet been legislated. Until its establishment, the PDMC‑RBI arrangement continues, with the RBI’s Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) retaining the final say on interest‑rate policy, which creates a potential conflict between debt‑cost minimisation and inflation targeting.

Green Budgeting and Climate Finance Integration

Green budgeting refers to the systematic tagging, tracking, and reporting of budgetary allocations that contribute to climate mitigation, adaptation, and environmental sustainability. India’s first formal green budgeting exercise was presented in the 2021‑22 Union Budget, where the Finance Minister announced a “Green Budget” component and the Ministry of Finance issued a “Climate Budget Tagging” methodology in 2022‑23.

The tagging methodology classifies each scheme into four categories: (1) climate‑relevant mitigation, (2) climate‑relevant adaptation, (3) biodiversity and ecosystem services, and (4) pollution control. Schemes are tagged at the programme level using a keyword‑based algorithm supplemented by ministry‑level validation. The tagged amounts are aggregated in the “Statement on Climate Finance” (Statement 14) and presented to Parliament alongside the regular Budget documents.

In 2022‑23, the tagged climate‑relevant expenditure amounted to approximately ₹3.5 lakh crore, representing about 2.5 % of total budgetary outlay. Major contributors included the National Solar Mission, the Green India Mission, the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture, and the Urban Mass Transit programmes. The tagging also captures fiscal incentives such as the Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for advanced chemistry cell batteries and the FAME‑II scheme for electric vehicles.

On the financing side, India has accessed international climate finance through the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Global Environment Facility (GEF), and bilateral channels (e.g., the Indo‑German Climate Partnership). The 2023‑24 Budget announced the issuance of sovereign green bonds, with the first tranche of ₹16,000 crore raised in January 2023. Proceeds are earmarked for projects meeting the “Green Bond Framework” criteria, which align with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles.

Challenges remain in ensuring additionality (that tagged funds represent new climate action rather than re‑labelled existing spending), avoiding double‑counting across mitigation and adaptation tags, and building capacity at the State level for sub‑national green budgeting. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended that States adopt a uniform climate‑budget tagging template to enable aggregation at the national level, a process currently being piloted in five States.

Institutional Arrangements

  • Ministry of Finance (Department of Economic Affairs): Nodal authority for green budgeting methodology and sovereign green bond issuance.
  • Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change: Provides technical validation of climate tags and monitors project‑level outcomes.
  • Reserve Bank of India: Acts as the issuing agent for sovereign green bonds and monitors the use‑of‑proceeds reporting.
  • NITI Aayog: Coordinates the “Climate Finance Tracking” framework across ministries and States.

Integrating green budgeting with the mainstream fiscal process enhances transparency, facilitates international reporting under the Paris Agreement, and signals policy credibility to domestic and foreign investors. Its evolution from a tagging exercise to a decision‑making tool will depend on the rigour of ex‑post impact evaluation and the alignment of fiscal incentives with long‑term decarbonisation pathways.

Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement and Fiscal Strategy Statement: Linking Targets to Annual Budgets

The Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement (MTFPS) is a statutory document mandated under Section 4 of the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, as amended in 2012 and 2018. It requires the Central Government to lay before Parliament a rolling three‑year fiscal framework that specifies targets for the fiscal deficit, revenue deficit, and the debt‑to‑GDP ratio. The MTFPS is presented alongside the Union Budget each year, usually in the “Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement” (FPSS) that forms part of the Budget documents (Statement 1). The Fiscal Strategy Statement (FSS), distinct from the MTFPS, translates the medium‑term targets into concrete annual policy actions — such as expenditure prioritisation, revenue measures, and borrowing plans — for the forthcoming financial year.

The 2023‑24 MTFPS, for instance, set a fiscal deficit target of 5.9 % of GDP for 2023‑24, 5.1 % for 2024‑25, and 4.5 % for 2025‑26, with a corresponding revenue deficit glide path of 2.9 %, 2.3 % and 1.5 % respectively. The debt‑to‑GDP ratio was projected to decline from 58.7 % in 2023‑24 to 56.5 % by 2025‑26. These numbers are derived from the macro‑fiscal model maintained by the Department of Economic Affairs (DEA) and are calibrated against the nominal GDP growth assumptions (10.5 % for 2023‑24) and the revenue buoyancy estimates discussed later.

The linkage mechanism works through three channels. First, the MTFPS fixes the ceiling for the gross fiscal deficit (GFD) which directly determines the net market borrowing requirement (NMBR) after accounting for small savings, provident funds, and other non‑market sources. Second, the FSS allocates the NMBR across dated securities, treasury bills, and cash management bills, guiding the Public Debt Management Cell (PDMC)‑RBI auction calendar. Third, the expenditure side is constrained by the “expenditure ceiling” derived from the revenue projections; any breach triggers the escape clause under Section 4(2) of the FRBM Act, which permits a deviation of up to 0.5 % of GDP in a year of national calamity or severe economic slowdown, subject to Parliamentary approval.

Empirically, the MTFPS has improved predictability. Since the 2017‑18 cycle, the average absolute deviation of the actual fiscal deficit from the MTFPS target has narrowed to about 0.2 % of GDP, compared with 0.6 % in the pre‑MTFPS period (2004‑05 to 2016‑17). However, the revenue deficit target has been missed in five of the last seven years, mainly due to shortfalls in tax collections and higher‑than‑budgeted subsidies. The 2022‑23 MTFPS introduced a “revenue deficit reduction path” that explicitly ties the revenue deficit to the tax‑to‑GDP ratio, making the target conditional on achieving a tax buoyancy of at least 1.1.

For aspirants, the key analytical point is that the MTFPS is not a mere declaration; it is the anchor for the entire fiscal architecture — borrowing calendar, cash management, and the design of the Fiscal Consolidation Plan that the Finance Commission uses to assess the Centre’s fiscal space for vertical transfers.

Tax Buoyancy, Elasticity and Revenue Forecasting: Methodologies and Empirical Evidence

Tax buoyancy measures the responsiveness of tax revenue to changes in nominal GDP, holding tax policy constant. Formally, buoyancy (B) = (ΔTax Revenue / Tax Revenue) ÷ (ΔNominal GDP / Nominal GDP). Elasticity, by contrast, isolates the automatic response of the tax base to income changes, excluding discretionary rate or base changes. In practice, the Ministry of Finance estimates both using the “proportional adjustment” method: actual collections are adjusted for known policy changes (rate cuts, base broadening, cess introductions) to derive a “policy‑neutral” series, which is then regressed on nominal GDP.

The Revenue Forecasting Committee (RFC), constituted in 2015 under the Department of Revenue, produces the official tax forecasts that feed into the Budget. The RFC employs a suite of econometric models — error‑correction models (ECM) for direct taxes, vector autoregression (VAR) for indirect taxes, and time‑series decomposition for non‑tax revenue. The models incorporate leading indicators such as the Index of Industrial Production (IIP), services PMI, corporate profitability (from the Ministry of Corporate Affairs), and customs clearance data. The forecasts are vetted by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) and the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC) before finalisation.

Empirical estimates from the Economic Survey 2022‑23 show a direct tax buoyancy of 1.22 (personal income tax 1.15, corporate tax 1.30) and an indirect tax buoyancy of 0.94 (GST 0.98, customs 0.85). The overall tax buoyancy for 2021‑22 was 1.08, implying that a 1 % rise in nominal GDP yielded a 1.08 % rise in tax revenue. The elasticity of the GST base, after adjusting for the 2017‑18 rate rationalisation, is estimated at 0.85, reflecting the still‑evolving compliance trajectory.

Forecast errors have narrowed over time. The mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for gross tax revenue fell from 6.3 % in 2014‑15 to 2.7 % in 2022‑23. The largest errors historically arise from corporate tax, where advance tax instalments and the “minimum alternate tax” (MAT) create timing mismatches. The RFC now uses a “nowcasting” framework that updates the forecast monthly using high‑frequency data (e‑way bills, GSTN returns, TDS collections), reducing the in‑year revision magnitude.

A critical nuance for exam questions: buoyancy is a *policy‑neutral* concept, whereas the observed revenue‑to‑GDP ratio conflates policy changes. A question may present a scenario where the government cuts the corporate tax rate from 30 % to 22 % (as in 2019) and ask for the impact on buoyancy versus elasticity. The correct answer: buoyancy falls temporarily because the policy change reduces collections, but elasticity of the underlying base remains unchanged; the RFC’s policy‑neutral adjustment restores the buoyancy estimate to its structural level.

Worked Example: Disentangling Buoyancy and Elasticity
In 2019‑20, nominal GDP grew by 7.5 %. The government reduced the corporate tax rate from 30 % to 22 % (effective September 2019). Actual corporate tax collections fell by 4.2 % year‑on‑year. Assuming the corporate tax base (profits) grew in line with nominal GDP, calculate the observed buoyancy, the policy‑adjusted buoyancy, and the implied elasticity of the corporate tax base.
Observed buoyancy = (‑4.2 % / 7.5 %) = ‑0.56. The rate cut of 8 percentage points on a 30 % base is a 26.7 % reduction in the effective rate. If the base grew 7.5 %, the policy‑neutral collection change would be +7.5 % (base growth) × (1 ‑ 0.267) ≈ ‑19.5 %? Wait — policy‑neutral means we remove the rate effect: the base grew 7.5 %, so collections would have risen 7.5 % without the rate cut. The actual fall of 4.2 % reflects base growth (+7.5 %) offset by rate cut (‑26.7 % of base). Policy‑adjusted buoyancy = (+7.5 % / 7.5 %) = 1.0. Elasticity of the base = 1.0 (by assumption). Thus observed buoyancy (‑0.56) is distorted by the discretionary rate change; policy‑adjusted buoyancy restores the structural relationship.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “buoyancy” without qualification, assume the policy‑neutral (structural) measure used by the RFC. If the stem mentions a specific tax rate change, explicitly state that observed buoyancy will be biased and that the RFC adjusts for it. Remember the formula: Buoyancy = (ΔRevenue/Revenue) ÷ (ΔGDP/GDP). Keep the distinction between buoyancy (includes policy) and elasticity (excludes policy) sharp — it is a favourite trap in both GS‑III and optional economics papers.

Fiscal Risks from State Guarantees, Contingent Liabilities and the Role of the Fiscal Council

State government guarantees constitute the largest category of contingent liabilities for the general government. As per the “Report on State Guarantees” laid before Parliament in March 2023, the outstanding guarantees of all States and Union Territories stood at ₹7.6 lakh crore at end‑March 2022, equivalent to 3.2 % of GDP. These guarantees cover borrowings by State‑owned power distribution companies (DISCOMs), road transport corporations, and urban development authorities. The FRBM Act, 2003 (as amended in 2018) requires the Centre to disclose the aggregate guarantees in the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement, but it does not impose a statutory ceiling on State guarantees.

The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended a “Guarantee Redemption Fund” (GRF) at the State level, financed by a guarantee commission of 0.5 % of the guaranteed amount, to create a buffer against invocation. Only a handful of States (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka) have operationalised such funds. The Commission also urged the Centre to set up an independent Fiscal Council to monitor both Central and State fiscal risks, including guarantees, off‑budget borrowings, and public‑private partnership (PPP) contingent liabilities. The NK Singh Committee (2017) on FRBM review had proposed a Fiscal Council with a mandate to publish an annual “Fiscal Risk Statement” and to advise on the invocation of the escape clause.

In practice, the Fiscal Council remains a proposal. The 2021‑22 Budget announced the formation of a “Fiscal Council” under the Department of Economic Affairs, but the enabling legislation has not been passed. Until then, the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) and the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) perform ex‑post scrutiny. The CAG’s 2022 report on “Guarantees and Contingent Liabilities” highlighted that 12 States had guarantee exposure exceeding 5 % of their GSDP, with Punjab and Rajasthan above 8 %. The report also flagged the lack of a uniform accounting standard for guarantees across States, making aggregation unreliable.

From a debt‑sustainability perspective, the IMF’s “Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) for Low‑Income Countries” framework, adapted for Indian States, treats guarantees as “implicit debt” when the probability of invocation exceeds 50 %. The 2023‑24 Union Budget’s “Statement on Contingent Liabilities” (Statement 13) lists Central guarantees at ₹1.2 lakh crore, but the State‑level data is presented only in aggregate. Analysts often use the “guarantee‑to‑GSDP” ratio as a leading indicator of fiscal stress; a ratio above 4 % has historically preceded rating downgrades by domestic agencies (CRISIL, ICRA).

For answer writing, remember three layers: (1) the legal framework — FRBM Act mandates disclosure but not limits; (2) the institutional gap — no operational Fiscal Council, so monitoring is fragmented; (3) the quantitative threshold — the 15th FC’s 5 % GSDP benchmark and the IMF’s 50 % invocation probability rule. A question may ask “Which of the following is a recommendation of the 15th Finance Commission regarding State guarantees?” The correct option is the creation of a Guarantee Redemption Fund financed by a guarantee commission.

Public Financial Management Reforms: PFMS, IFMS, Treasury Single Account and the Expenditure Management Commission

The Public Financial Management System (PFMS), launched in 2009 as the Central Plan Scheme Monitoring System (CPSMS) and rebranded in 2013, is the Government of India’s end‑to‑end digital platform for fund flow tracking, payment processing, and accounting integration. PFMS connects the Consolidated Fund of India, the Contingency Fund, and the Public Account with over 1.2 lakh implementing agencies, enabling real‑time visibility of scheme‑level expenditures. As of March 2023, PFMS processed ₹1.8 lakh crore of direct benefit transfers (DBT) across 300‑plus schemes, reducing leakage by an estimated 15‑20 % according to the DBT Mission’s impact assessment.

At the State level, the Integrated Financial Management System (IFMS) replicates PFMS functionality for State treasuries. The 14th Finance Commission incentivised IFMS adoption by linking a portion of the performance grant to the completion of treasury computerisation. By 2022, 28 States had operational IFMS modules covering budget preparation, bill submission, and pension disbursement. The Centre’s “Model IFMS” framework, issued by the Department of Expenditure in 2017, standardises the chart of accounts, the budget classification (major/minor/sub‑minor heads), and the interface with the RBI’s e‑Kuber core banking solution.

The Treasury Single Account (TSA) concept, adopted by the Union Government in 2015‑16, consolidates all government cash balances into a single account at the RBI, eliminating idle balances in multiple departmental bank accounts. The TSA covers the Consolidated Fund, the Contingency Fund, and the Public Account, and is operated through the RBI’s e‑Kuber platform. States have been encouraged to adopt a State‑level TSA; as of 2023, 19 States have migrated to a unified treasury account, yielding an estimated annual interest saving of ₹3,500 crore on idle cash.

The Expenditure Management Commission (EMC), constituted in 2014 under the chairmanship of a former Finance Secretary, submitted its report in 2016. Its key recommendations included: (i) a shift from input‑based to outcome‑based budgeting, (ii) adoption of zero‑based budgeting for all Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) on a pilot basis, (iii) strengthening the “Output‑Outcome Monitoring Framework” (OOMF) with quarterly physical‑financial reporting, and (iv) institutionalising a “Spending Review” mechanism akin to the UK’s Comprehensive Spending Review. The Government accepted the recommendation to integrate the OOMF with PFMS, and the 2022‑23 Budget introduced the “Scheme‑Level Output‑Outcome Dashboard” accessible to Parliament.

These reforms collectively address the classic PFM triangle: *aggregate fiscal discipline* (via TSA and FRBM targets), *allocative efficiency* (via outcome budgeting and the EMC’s spending reviews), and *operational efficiency* (via PFMS/IFMS automation). A typical exam question may ask: “Which of the following is NOT a component of the Treasury Single Account?” The correct answer would exclude “State‑level provident fund accounts” because they remain outside the Consolidated Fund. Another common twist: “The Expenditure Management Commission recommended the adoption of zero‑based budgeting for all CSS.” This is true only for the pilot phase; the Commission did not mandate universal ZBB.

  • Treating observed tax buoyancy as structural buoyancy without adjusting for discretionary rate or base changes; the RFC’s policy‑neutral series is the exam‑standard measure.
  • Assuming the Fiscal Council is already functional; it remains a proposal despite Budget announcements, and monitoring of State guarantees is currently done by the CAG and PAC.
  • Believing the Treasury Single Account covers all public sector bank accounts; it only consolidates Central Government cash balances held at the RBI.
  • Overstating the Expenditure Management Commission’s mandate — it recommended pilot zero‑based budgeting for CSS, not a blanket rollout.
  • Mixing up the guarantee commission rate (0.5 % recommended by the 15th FC) with the guarantee redemption fund corpus; the fund is financed by the commission, not the other way round.
  • Master these nuances to avoid common pitfalls in fiscal policy questions.

Countercyclical Fiscal Policy and Automatic Stabilisers

Countercyclical fiscal policy refers to deliberate changes in government spending and taxation that move opposite to the business cycle. In India the framework rests on two pillars: automatic stabilisers that operate without fresh legislative action, and discretionary stimulus packages that require parliamentary approval. The most potent automatic stabilisers are the progressive personal income tax structure and the network of targeted transfer programmes such as the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (MGNREGS) and the Public Distribution System (PDS). When incomes fall, tax liabilities shrink automatically, while enrolment in MGNREGS rises, injecting demand without a new budget law.

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, as amended in 2018, introduced an explicit escape clause (Section 4(2)) that permits the central government to breach the fiscal deficit target by up to 0.5 percentage points of GDP in the event of a national calamity, severe agricultural distress, or a sharp decline in real output growth. The clause was invoked for the first time in the 2020‑21 Budget after the COVID‑19 lockdown. The government announced a fiscal stimulus of ₹20.97 lakh crore (≈10 % of GDP) under the Atmanirbhar Bharat package, comprising credit guarantees, liquidity support for NBFCs, and additional allocation for MGNREGS. The fiscal deficit for 2020‑21 was recorded at 9.5 % of GDP, well above the 3.5 % target, but within the escape‑clause bandwidth.

Empirical work by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy (NIPFP) estimates that automatic stabilisers offset roughly 0.3‑0.4 percentage points of GDP for every 1 % decline in real GDP growth. Discretionary measures, by contrast, have a multiplier in the range of 0.8‑1.2 depending on the composition (capital expenditure versus revenue expenditure). The 2021‑22 Budget shifted the emphasis toward capital outlay (₹5.54 lakh crore) to raise the quality of the deficit, targeting a fiscal deficit of 6.8 % of GDP. The Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement (MTFPS) 2022‑23 projects a glide path to 4.5 % by 2025‑26, assuming a nominal GDP growth of 10.5 % and a tax buoyancy of 1.2.

A key analytical distinction for examiners is between the structural deficit (the deficit that would prevail at potential output) and the cyclical deficit (the portion attributable to the output gap). The FRBM Review Committee (2017) recommended that the fiscal deficit target be expressed in structural terms, but the government retained the headline nominal target. Consequently, a year of high growth can mask a deteriorating structural position, while a recession can make the headline deficit appear worse than the underlying stance. Candidates should be able to compute the cyclical component using the formula: Cyclical Deficit = (Actual Deficit − Structural Deficit) = ε × Output Gap, where ε is the semi‑elasticity of the budget balance to the output gap (estimated at 0.35 for India).

Monetary‑Fiscal Coordination: RBI’s Role in Government Borrowing and Market Stabilisation

The Reserve Bank of India acts as the banker and debt manager for the central government under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and the Government Securities Act, 2006. Coordination occurs through three main channels: Ways and Means Advances (WMA), the Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS), and the annual government securities (G‑Sec) issuance calendar prepared by the Public Debt Management Cell (PDMC) in consultation with the RBI.

WMA are temporary advances to bridge mismatches between receipts and payments. The normal WMA limit for the Centre is ₹1.5 lakh crore (revised upward to ₹2 lakh crore in April 2020). If the government exceeds this limit, an overdraft facility at the repo rate plus 2 percentage points is available for up to 10 consecutive working days. In 2020‑21 the Centre utilised WMA for 120 days, the highest since the scheme’s inception, reflecting the cash‑flow stress from the lockdown.

The MSS, introduced in 2004, enables the RBI to sterilise surplus liquidity arising from large capital inflows or from the government’s own cash balances. Under MSS, the RBI issues Treasury Bills and dated securities on behalf of the government, and the proceeds are held in a separate MSS account, not available for fiscal expenditure. In 2021‑22 the RBI conducted MSS issuances of ₹1.2 lakh crore to absorb excess liquidity after the pandemic‑era monetary easing. The interest cost on MSS securities is borne by the government, adding to the fiscal deficit.

The G‑Sec issuance calendar is announced quarterly. For 2023‑24 the Centre planned gross market borrowing of ₹15.4 lakh crore, with a net borrowing target of ₹11.8 lakh crore after buybacks. The PDMC, headed by the Finance Secretary, coordinates with the RBI’s Market Operations Department to schedule auctions, decide on tenors (2‑year, 5‑year, 10‑year, 30‑year), and manage the green shoe option. Buyback operations, introduced in 2022, allow the government to repurchase high‑cost securities, reducing the weighted average cost of borrowing. In the first half of 2023‑24 buybacks amounted to ₹0.6 lakh crore, lowering the average coupon by 12 basis points.

Monetary‑fiscal coordination also extends to the RBI’s Open Market Operations (OMO) and the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF). When the RBI conducts OMO purchases of G‑Secs, it effectively monetises a portion of the deficit, influencing the yield curve. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act mandates that the central government shall not borrow from the RBI except through WMA, preserving the autonomy of monetary policy. However, the 2020‑21 pandemic saw a temporary relaxation where the RBI subscribed to primary issuances under the “Special Liquidity Facility for Mutual Funds” and the “Targeted Long‑Term Repo Operations” (TLTRO), indirectly supporting government borrowing costs.

Technique: Identifying the Correct Instrument
Question: Which of the following instruments is used by the RBI to sterilise surplus liquidity arising from government cash balances, and the proceeds of which are kept in a separate account not available for fiscal expenditure? (a) Ways and Means Advances (b) Market Stabilisation Scheme (c) Open Market Operations (d) Treasury Bills issued under the regular auction calendar
Solution: The Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) is specifically designed for sterilisation of surplus liquidity, with proceeds held in a distinct MSS account. Ways and Means Advances are temporary loans to the government, not sterilisation tools. Open Market Operations involve outright purchase or sale of securities for monetary policy, not a separate account. Regular Treasury Bill auctions finance the deficit directly. Hence the correct answer is (b).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to match a fiscal‑monetary instrument with its purpose, first classify the instrument as either a financing tool (WMA, regular G‑Sec auction), a sterilisation tool (MSS), or a monetary policy tool (OMO, SDF). The presence of a “separate account” or “not available for expenditure” is a hallmark of MSS. Memorise the WMA limit (₹1.5 lakh crore normal, ₹2 lakh crore enhanced) and the overdraft rate (repo + 2 %). These numbers appear frequently in both prelims and mains.

Fiscal Risks from Public Sector Enterprises: Guarantees, Dividends and Disinvestment

Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) constitute a significant source of contingent liability for the Union Government. As of March 2023, the total outstanding government guarantees stood at ₹6.3 lakh crore, equivalent to 2.8 % of GDP. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a guarantee commission of 0.5 % per annum on the guaranteed amount, to be credited to a Guarantee Redemption Fund (GRF) managed by the Ministry of Finance. The GRF corpus reached ₹0.45 lakh crore by end‑2022, providing a buffer against potential defaults.

Dividend policy for CPSEs is governed by the Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) guidelines, which mandate a minimum payout of 30 % of profit after tax (PAT) or 5 % of net worth, whichever is higher. In 2022‑23, CPSEs declared dividends of ₹0.68 lakh crore, of which the Centre received ₹0.55 lakh crore. However, the dividend yield has been volatile; several oil‑marketing CPSEs deferred payouts during the 2020‑21 price crash, creating a shortfall in non‑tax revenue projections.

Disinvestment proceeds are treated as non‑debt capital receipts and directly reduce the fiscal deficit. The Union Budget 2023‑24 set a disinvestment target of ₹0.51 lakh crore, comprising strategic disinvestment (e.g., Air India, completed in January 2022; BPCL, pending) and minority stake sales through Offer for Sale (OFS) and Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs). The National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP), launched in August 2021, aims to unlock ₹6 lakh crore by leasing core assets (roads, railways, power transmission) over four years. As of March 2023, contracts worth ₹1.1 lakh crore had been awarded.

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) in its 2022 report on “Guarantees and Contingent Liabilities” highlighted that 12 CPSEs had guarantee utilisation exceeding 80 % of the sanctioned limit, raising the probability of invocation. The report also flagged that the GRF investment policy (predominantly in government securities) yields a return lower than the guarantee commission, creating a negative carry. For mains, candidates should be able to explain the interplay between guarantee commissions, GRF adequacy, and the impact on the fiscal deficit if a guarantee is invoked (the amount is booked as a capital expenditure, widening the deficit).

Parliamentary Financial Committees and Budgetary Oversight

Three standing committees of Parliament provide ex‑post scrutiny of government finances: the Public Accounts Committee (PAC), the Estimates Committee, and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). The PAC, constituted under Rule 308 of the Lok Sabha Rules, examines the Appropriation Accounts and the CAG’s audit reports. It comprises 22 members (15 from Lok Sabha, 7 from Rajya Sabha) and is traditionally chaired by a senior opposition leader. In its 2022 report on Direct Benefit Transfers, the PAC flagged a 12 % leakage in the PM‑KISAN scheme due to duplicate beneficiaries, prompting the Ministry of Agriculture to integrate Aadhaar‑based verification.

The Estimates Committee, with 30 members (all from Lok Sabha), scrutinises the budget estimates of ministries before they are voted. It suggests economies in expenditure and examines whether the estimates are realistic. The 2021‑22 report on the Ministry of Health highlighted that the revised estimate for the National Health Mission was 18 % higher than the budget estimate, indicating poor forecasting. The committee recommended a shift to zero‑based budgeting for centrally sponsored schemes, echoing the Expenditure Management Commission’s pilot proposal.

The Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU), also 22 members (15 Lok Sabha, 7 Rajya Sabha), reviews the reports of the CAG on CPSEs and examines their autonomy, efficiency, and pricing policies. Its 2020 report on the Steel Authority of India Ltd. (SAIL) recommended rationalising the product mix and accelerating the disinvestment of non‑core assets. COPU’s recommendations are advisory but carry weight because they are debated in the House.

All three committees rely on the CAG’s audit reports, which are tabled in Parliament under Article 151. The CAG’s 2023 report on “Off‑Budget Borrowings” revealed that the Centre had raised ₹1.2 lakh crore through extra‑budgetary resources (EBRs) such as the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) and the Food Corporation of India (FCI) bonds, which were not reflected in the fiscal deficit. The PAC subsequently summoned the Finance Secretary and recommended that all EBRs be brought on‑budget within two years.

Tax Administration Reforms: CBDT, CBIC and the Taxpayer Charter

The Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) and the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC) are the apex bodies for direct and indirect tax administration, respectively. Since 2019, both boards have implemented a “faceless” ecosystem: faceless assessment (2019), faceless appeal (2020), and faceless penalty (2021). The faceless assessment scheme uses a centralised National e‑Assessment Centre (NeAC) that randomly assigns cases to assessment units across the country, eliminating territorial jurisdiction. By March 2023, 98 % of scrutiny cases were processed under the faceless regime, reducing the average disposal time from 420 days to 180 days.

The Vivad se Vishwas (VsV) scheme, launched in March 2020, offered a one‑time settlement of disputed tax liabilities by waiving interest and penalty. As of December 2022, 1.1 lakh cases involving ₹0.97 lakh crore of disputed tax were settled, yielding ₹0.54 lakh crore in revenue. The scheme was extended twice, reflecting its success in unlocking locked‑up revenue.

The Taxpayer Charter, notified in August 2020, enumerates 14 rights and 14 obligations, including the right to fair and courteous treatment, the right to privacy, and the obligation to provide accurate information. The Charter is legally enforceable through the Income‑Tax Act, 1961 (Section 119A) and the CGST Act, 2017 (Section 171A). The CBDT’s “Project Insight” leverages big data analytics, social media profiling, and third‑party data (bank deposits, property registrations) to identify non‑filers and under‑reporters. In 2022‑23, Project Insight added 1.3 crore new taxpayers to the net.

On the indirect tax side, the CBIC rolled out e‑invoicing for B2B transactions (mandatory for turnover >₹50 crore from October 2020, extended to >₹5 crore from January 2023) and the e‑way bill system for inter‑state movement of goods. The GST Network (GSTN) processes over 1.2 billion invoices monthly. The introduction of the “Quarterly Return Monthly Payment” (QRMP) scheme for small taxpayers (turnover ≤₹5 crore) reduced compliance frequency. Revenue buoyancy for GST improved from 0.9 in 2018‑19 to 1.15 in 2022‑23, indicating a more responsive tax base.

  • Treating Ways and Means Advances as a fiscal deficit financing tool; they are a cash‑management facility and do not alter the deficit unless overdrawn.

Debt Sustainability Analysis: IMF‑World Bank Framework and India’s Debt‑to‑GDP Trajectory

The IMF‑World Bank Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) provides a standardized template for assessing whether a sovereign’s public debt can be serviced without resorting to exceptional financing or default. The framework evaluates three core indicators: the debt‑to‑GDP ratio, the debt‑service‑to‑revenue ratio, and the gross financing need as a share of GDP. Thresholds differ by country classification; for emerging market economies the benchmark debt‑to‑GDP ceiling is 70 % and the debt‑service‑to‑revenue threshold is 30 %. India’s general government debt stood at approximately 90 % of GDP in 2023‑24, well above the emerging‑market benchmark, while the interest‑payment‑to‑revenue ratio hovered near 28 %, approaching the critical line. The DSA also incorporates a stochastic fan‑chart that models macro‑shocks — growth slowdown, interest‑rate spikes, exchange‑rate depreciation — to test the resilience of the debt path. India’s medium‑term fiscal strategy, articulated in the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement, targets a gradual reduction of the central government debt‑to‑GDP ratio to 60 % by 2027‑28, relying on nominal GDP growth outpacing the effective interest rate (the “r < g” condition) and a primary surplus of 0.5 % of GDP. The 15th Finance Commission reinforced this trajectory by recommending a consolidated fiscal deficit glide path for the Centre and states combined, and by linking performance‑based grants to adherence to the FRBM targets. A key policy lever is the Public Debt Management Cell’s (PDMC) annual borrowing calendar, which front‑loads issuance in the first half of the fiscal year to exploit favourable liquidity conditions and to keep the weighted average cost of borrowing below 7 %. The RBI’s role as debt manager — conducting open market operations, managing the cash‑management bill programme, and providing Ways and Means Advances — complements the PDMC’s strategy by smoothing short‑term cash mismatches without altering the fiscal deficit. Together, these mechanisms form the operational backbone of India’s debt‑sustainability framework.

Tax Expenditure Reporting: Estimating Revenue Foregone and Policy Implications

Tax expenditures — revenue losses arising from exemptions, deductions, credits, deferrals and preferential rates — are the fiscal counterpart of direct spending. Since the 2019‑20 Union Budget, the Ministry of Finance has published a Tax Expenditure Statement (TES) that quantifies the revenue foregone under each major provision of the Income‑Tax Act, the GST law and customs tariffs. The 2022‑23 TES estimated total tax expenditures at roughly ₹2.5 lakh crore, equivalent to about 1.2 % of GDP. The largest components are the standard deduction for salaried taxpayers, the exemption of agricultural income, the reduced GST rates on essential goods, and the export‑oriented incentives under the Remission of Duties and Taxes on Exported Products (RoDTEP) scheme. The TES follows the OECD methodology: each provision is benchmarked against a “normal” tax structure, the revenue loss is calculated using the current tax base, and the estimate is presented both in absolute terms and as a share of total tax revenue. This transparency enables Parliament and the public to evaluate whether a given concession delivers its intended social or economic objective. For instance, the 2021‑22 TES highlighted that the exemption on long‑term capital gains from listed equity (up to ₹1 lakh) cost ₹12,000 crore annually, prompting a debate on its distributional impact. The Finance Commission also uses tax‑expenditure data to assess the revenue capacity of states, because many concessions erode the divisible pool. A growing practice is to embed a “sunset clause” in new tax expenditures, requiring a periodic review — typically every three years — before automatic renewal. The 2023‑24 budget introduced a sunset review for the accelerated depreciation benefit for manufacturing, aiming to align the incentive with the Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme’s outcomes. By treating tax expenditures on par with explicit outlays, the TES strengthens fiscal discipline and improves the quality of budgetary decision‑making.

Identifying the Correct Component of the Tax Expenditure Statement
In the 2022‑23 Tax Expenditure Statement, which of the following items is classified as a tax expenditure under the Income‑Tax Act? (a) Standard deduction for salaried employees (b) Corporate tax rate reduction from 30 % to 22 % for new manufacturing units (c) GST compensation cess on luxury cars (d) Customs duty exemption on imported capital goods for SEZs
The Tax Expenditure Statement covers revenue foregone under direct tax laws (Income‑Tax Act) and indirect tax laws (GST, customs). The standard deduction for salaried employees is a explicit deduction under Section 16 of the Income‑Tax Act and is therefore listed as a tax expenditure. The corporate tax rate reduction for new manufacturing units is a rate change, not a deduction or exemption, and is captured in the revenue impact of the rate change rather than as a separate tax expenditure line. The GST compensation cess is a separate levy, not a tax expenditure. The customs duty exemption for SEZ capital goods is a customs concession and appears in the customs section of the TES, but the question asks specifically under the Income‑Tax Act. Hence the correct answer is (a).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks you to pick a tax‑expenditure item, first identify the governing statute (Income‑Tax, GST, Customs). Then recall that only exemptions, deductions, credits, deferrals or preferential rates qualify; pure rate cuts or new cesses do not. The TES table in the budget document is the definitive source — memorise the top three direct‑tax expenditures (standard deduction, agricultural income exemption, long‑term capital gains exemption) and the top two indirect‑tax expenditures (GST reduced rates on essentials, export incentives).

State‑Level Fiscal Rules: FRBM Acts, 15th Finance Commission Incentives and Compliance Monitoring

Each Indian state enacts its own Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, mirroring the central legislation but calibrated to state‑specific macro‑conditions. The typical state FRBM targets a fiscal deficit of 3 % of Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) and a total outstanding liability ceiling of 25 % of GSDP. However, the 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) introduced a differentiated incentive framework: states that meet the deficit and debt targets become eligible for an additional 0.5 % of GSDP as performance‑based grants, while those that breach the limits face a proportional reduction in the revenue‑deficit grant component. The Commission also mandated the creation of an independent Fiscal Council at the state level — currently operational in Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka — to monitor compliance, publish mid‑year fiscal reviews, and advise on corrective measures. The Reserve Bank of India’s “State Finances: A Study of Budgets” report (2023) shows that 19 states stayed within the 3 % fiscal‑deficit threshold in 2022‑23, while 9 states exceeded it, primarily due to elevated revenue expenditure on subsidies and pension commitments. The Centre’s “State Government Borrowing Programme” coordinates market borrowings through the RBI’s auction calendar, ensuring that aggregate state borrowing does not crowd out central government issuance. A notable innovation is the “Debt‑Swap” facility introduced in 2022, allowing states with high‑cost legacy loans to refinance at the prevailing weighted average cost of central government securities, thereby reducing interest outgo. The Comptroller and Auditor General’s (CAG) annual audit of state accounts now includes a specific “FRBM Compliance” chapter, flagging off‑budget borrowings, guarantees extended to state‑owned enterprises, and the use of the Consolidated Sinking Fund for debt redemption. This multi‑layered oversight — statutory targets, Finance Commission incentives, independent fiscal councils, RBI coordination, and CAG audit — constitutes the institutional architecture that seeks to align sub‑national fiscal behaviour with the national consolidation path.

Fiscal Transparency and Open Budget Initiatives: CAG Audit, Fiscal Data Portal and International Benchmarks

Fiscal transparency has become a prerequisite for credible macro‑management and for attracting long‑term capital. India’s primary transparency instruments are the Comptroller and Auditor General’s (CAG) audit reports, the Ministry of Finance’s Fiscal Data Portal (launched in 2022), and participation in the International Budget Partnership’s Open Budget Survey (OBS). The CAG tables three major reports each year: the Report on Union Government Accounts, the Report on State Finances, and the Report on Public Sector Enterprises. These reports are placed before Parliament under Article 151 and are examined by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). The 2023 CAG report on “Off‑Budget Borrowings” quantified extra‑budgetary resources at ₹1.2 lakh crore, prompting the PAC to recommend full on‑budget disclosure within two years. The Fiscal Data Portal (https://fiscaldata.gov.in) provides machine‑readable, time‑series data on receipts, expenditure, deficits, debt, guarantees and tax expenditures from 1990‑91 onward, enabling researchers and civil‑society organisations to conduct independent fiscal analysis. India’s OBS score improved from 41 in 2019 to 49 in 2023 (out of 100), reflecting better budget documentation, public participation mechanisms and legislative oversight, yet it remains below the global average of 55. The survey’s “Budget Transparency” sub‑index highlights gaps in the publication of a pre‑budget statement, a citizens’ budget, and a mid‑year review. In response, the 2024‑25 budget introduced a “Citizen’s Budget Summary” in 12 languages and mandated a mid‑year fiscal review to be tabled in Parliament by 31 December. The Ministry also adopted the IMF’s Fiscal Transparency Code (2019) as a benchmark, aligning disclosure practices on contingent liabilities, quasi‑fiscal activities and natural‑resource revenues. These initiatives collectively raise the information asymmetry between the executive and the legislature, empowering evidence‑based scrutiny of fiscal policy.

Climate‑Responsive Fiscal Policy: Green Bonds, Adaptation Funding and the National Adaptation Fund

Integrating climate considerations into fiscal planning marks a paradigm shift from reactive disaster relief to proactive resilience investment. In January 2023 the Government of India issued its first sovereign green bonds, raising ₹16,000 crore (approximately US $2 billion) in two tranches with ten‑year and thirty‑year maturities. The proceeds are earmarked for projects classified under the Green Bond Framework — renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, sustainable agriculture, and climate‑resilient infrastructure. The framework follows the International Capital Market Association’s Green Bond Principles and is verified by an external reviewer. Simultaneously, the National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC), established in 2015 with a corpus of ₹1,000 crore, finances state‑level adaptation projects such as coastal protection, drought‑proofing of agriculture, and urban heat‑island mitigation. As of March 2024, the NAFCC has sanctioned 112 projects across 27 states, with a total outlay of ₹2,300 crore, leveraging additional co‑financing from multilateral development banks. The 15th Finance Commission recognised climate vulnerability as a criterion for horizontal devolution, assigning a 10 % weight to “forest and ecology” and a 5 % weight to “disaster management” in the distribution formula. The Union Budget 2024‑25 introduced a “Green Budgeting” tag in the expenditure classification, requiring ministries to report the climate‑relevance of each scheme. The Public Financial Management System (PFMS) now captures a climate‑tag field, enabling the Ministry of Finance to aggregate green expenditure and report it in the Annual Financial Statement. On the revenue side, the GST Council has maintained a zero‑rate on solar photovoltaic modules and a reduced 5 % rate on electric vehicles, while the compensation cess on coal continues to fund the Clean Energy Fund. These coordinated measures — sovereign green issuance, dedicated adaptation fund, climate‑weighted devolution, green budgeting tags, and tax incentives — embed climate resilience into the core fiscal architecture, aligning public finance with India’s Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement.

Fiscal Federalism and the GST Council Decision‑Making Process

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) introduced by the 101st Constitution Amendment Act, 2016 created a dual‑structure tax where the Centre and the States concurrently levy CGST and SGST on intra‑state supplies, while IGST applies to inter‑state transactions. The GST Council, constituted under Article 279A, is the apex decision‑making body comprising the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and the Finance Ministers of all States and Union Territories with legislatures. Decisions are taken by a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes, where the Centre holds one‑third of the total voting weight and the States collectively hold two‑thirds, each State’s weight being proportional to its share of the total GST revenue. This voting architecture ensures that no single jurisdiction can unilaterally impose rate changes, while still allowing the Centre a decisive voice.

The Council’s mandate covers tax rates, exemptions, threshold limits, place‑of‑supply rules, and the resolution of disputes between the Centre and States or among States. Since its first meeting in September 2016, the Council has met over 50 times, adopting a consensus‑driven approach for most rate rationalisations. Notable outcomes include the reduction of the standard rate from 28 % to 18 % for a large basket of goods in 2017‑18, the introduction of a composition scheme for small taxpayers (turnover up to ₹1.5 crore), and the phased rollout of e‑invoicing and the GST‑N (Network) platform. The Council also oversees the compensation cess levied on sin goods and luxury items, which funds the five‑year compensation guarantee to States for revenue loss arising from GST implementation. The guarantee, originally slated to end in June 2022, was extended by a year through a Council resolution after the COVID‑19 pandemic severely impacted State revenues.

Dispute resolution under the Council follows a two‑tier mechanism: the Council itself attempts conciliation; if unresolved, the matter can be referred to the GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT) once it becomes operational. Until the Tribunal’s constitution, the Council’s decisions are final and binding. The Council’s secretariat, housed in the Department of Revenue, prepares agenda papers, conducts revenue impact analyses using the GST‑N data warehouse, and circulates minutes to all members. The transparency of this process is reinforced by the publication of Council meeting outcomes on the GST portal, enabling stakeholders to track rate changes and compliance requirements in real time.

From a fiscal‑federalism perspective, the GST Council represents a unique institutional innovation: a constitutional body that operationalises cooperative federalism in tax policy. Its weighted‑voting design balances the Centre’s fiscal responsibility with States’ autonomy, while the compensation mechanism mitigates the risk of revenue volatility during the transition. However, critics argue that the Centre’s veto‑like weight on critical issues such as the inclusion of petroleum products and real estate under GST limits true fiscal decentralisation. The ongoing debate over the inclusion of electricity, alcohol for human consumption, and petroleum in the GST base illustrates the tension between revenue certainty for States and the Centre’s macro‑economic policy space.

Public Expenditure Management: Zero‑Based Budgeting and Expenditure Review Committees

Zero‑Based Budgeting (ZBB) requires every ministry to justify its entire budget request from a “zero base” each financial year, rather than incrementally adjusting the previous year’s allocation. India first experimented with ZBB in the 1970s under the Planning Commission, but the exercise was abandoned due to the administrative burden and the dominance of plan‑non‑plan classification. A renewed push came with the 2016‑17 budget when the Finance Minister announced a pilot ZBB for select centrally sponsored schemes (CSS) and the establishment of the Expenditure Review Committee (ERC) under the Department of Expenditure. The ERC, chaired by the Secretary (Expenditure), evaluates the relevance, efficiency, and outcome‑orientation of existing programmes, recommending continuation, restructuring, or closure.

The ERC’s methodology combines performance data from the Outcome Budget, the Public Financial Management System (PFMS), and independent evaluations by the Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO). In its first cycle (2017‑18), the ERC reviewed 12 CSS, leading to the merger of the National Rural Livelihoods Mission with the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana and the rationalisation of the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana’s funding pattern. Subsequent cycles expanded coverage to 30 schemes, with the 2020‑21 review recommending the sunset of the National Food Security Mission’s wheat component after achieving self‑sufficiency. The committee also introduced a “sunset clause” template, requiring each new scheme to specify a review date not later than five years from launch.

ZBB’s practical implementation in India faces three structural constraints. First, the sheer number of line items — over 1.5 lakh across the Union Budget — makes a full‑scale zero‑base exercise infeasible without substantial IT support. The PFMS‑IFMS integration, completed in 2022, now allows real‑time tracking of expenditure down to the object head level, providing the data granularity needed for ZBB. Second, the political economy of entrenched subsidies and employment‑generating schemes creates resistance to zero‑base scrutiny; the ERC mitigates this by focusing on “low‑impact, high‑cost” programmes identified through cost‑benefit thresholds (benefit‑cost ratio < 1.2). Third, the capacity of line ministries to prepare zero‑base proposals varies; the Department of Expenditure conducts annual capacity‑building workshops and issues a ZBB manual prescribing standardised formats for activity‑based costing, outcome indicators, and alternative delivery models.

The synergy between ZBB and the Outcome Budget strengthens performance‑based allocation. Since 2017‑18, the Outcome Budget has been presented alongside the Annual Financial Statement, linking each major scheme to measurable outputs (e.g., kilometres of rural roads constructed, number of households electrified) and outcomes (e.g., reduction in rural poverty incidence). The ERC uses these outcome metrics as the primary evidence base for its recommendations, creating a feedback loop where budget formulation, execution monitoring, and ex‑post evaluation inform each other. This loop is further reinforced by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance, which examines ERC reports and can summon ministry officials for oral evidence, thereby embedding legislative oversight into the expenditure management cycle.

Fiscal Implications of Demographic Transition and Pension Liabilities

India’s demographic profile is shifting rapidly: the share of the population aged 60 years and above is projected to rise from 8.6 % in 2011 to 12.5 % by 2030 and 19.5 % by 2050 (UN World Population Prospects 2022). This transition expands the fiscal burden through two channels — higher pension outlays for government employees and increased demand for social‑security schemes such as the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) and the Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan‑Dhan (PM‑SYM). The Central Government’s defined‑benefit pension liability for civil servants (pre‑2004 recruits) is estimated at ₹23 lakh crore (≈ 12 % of GDP) as of March 2023, with annual pension payments exceeding ₹2 lakh crore. The New Pension System (NPS), introduced for post‑2004 recruits, shifts risk to the employee but still requires the government to contribute 14 % of basic pay, adding a predictable but growing expenditure line.

State governments face an even steeper trajectory because a larger proportion of their workforce remains under the old pension scheme. The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) recognised this by assigning a 12.5 % weight to “demographic performance” in the horizontal devolution formula, rewarding States that achieve lower fertility rates and better health outcomes. However, the Commission also flagged the risk of “pension crowding out” — where rising committed expenditure on pensions and salaries reduces the fiscal space for capital outlay. Several States (e.g., Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab) now allocate more than 20 % of their revenue receipts to pension payments, limiting their ability to meet the FRBM‑mandated capital‑expenditure‑to‑GSDP ratio of 3 %.

Policy responses have focused on three levers. First, the Central Government has encouraged States to migrate to the NPS through the “Model Pension Scheme” guidelines issued by the Department of Financial Services in 2021, offering a one‑time fiscal incentive of 0.5 % of GSDP for each year of transition. As of March 2024, 18 States have notified NPS adoption for new recruits. Second, the Atal Pension Yojana (APY) and PM‑SYM target the unorganised sector, aiming to cover 10 crore subscribers by 2025; the fiscal cost is borne through a co‑contribution mechanism where the Central Government matches 50 % of the subscriber’s contribution up to ₹1,000 per annum. Third, the Public Financial Management System now tags pension‑related expenditure with a “committed‑expenditure” flag, enabling the Finance Ministry to monitor the share of committed outlays in real time and to trigger early‑warning alerts when the ratio exceeds 35 % of revenue receipts.

Long‑term sustainability hinges on actuarial valuation of pension funds, investment returns on NPS corpus (managed by Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority), and the fiscal space created by revenue buoyancy. The 2023‑24 Economic Survey highlighted that a 1 % increase in the effective retirement age (from 60 to 61) could reduce the present value of pension liabilities by approximately ₹1.2 lakh crore. Such parametric reforms, combined with a gradual shift to contribution‑based systems, are essential to prevent pension obligations from destabilising the fiscal deficit trajectory mandated by the FRBM Act.

Revenue Mobilisation through Non‑Tax Receipts: Disinvestment, Spectrum Auctions and Asset Monetisation

Non‑tax receipts have become a critical pillar of the Union Government’s resource envelope, especially when tax buoyancy falters. The three dominant streams are disinvestment proceeds, spectrum auction revenues, and the National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP) launched in August 2021. Disinvestment targets are set annually in the Budget; the 2023‑24 target of ₹51,000 crore was revised to ₹30,000 crore after market volatility delayed the strategic sale of Air India (completed in January 2022) and the partial stake sale in LIC (May 2022). The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) follows a “strategic disinvestment” framework where management control is transferred to a private entity, complemented by minority stake sales through Offer for Sale (OFS) on stock exchanges. The proceeds are credited to the Consolidated Fund of India and used to finance the fiscal deficit, thereby reducing market borrowing.

Spectrum auctions, conducted by the Department of Telecommunications under the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 (as amended), generate lump‑sum revenues that are treated as non‑tax capital receipts. The 2022 auction of 5G spectrum (3.3‑3.67 GHz and 26 GHz bands) fetched ₹1.5 lakh crore, the highest ever, with a deferred payment schedule allowing successful bidders to pay in 20 annual instalments after a two‑year moratorium. The auction design includes a “floor price” set by the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) and a “spectrum usage charge” (SUC) of 3 % of adjusted gross revenue, which continues as a recurring non‑tax receipt. The 2023‑24 budget accounted for ₹60,000 crore from spectrum instalments, illustrating the growing predictability of this stream.

The NMP aims to unlock value from brownfield infrastructure assets — roads, railways, power transmission, pipelines, and warehousing — by leasing them to private operators for a defined concession period (typically 25‑30 years) while retaining ownership. The pipeline, valued at ₹6 lakh crore over FY 2022‑25, is coordinated by the Ministry of Finance with NITI Aayog as the knowledge partner. As of March 2024, 12 road‑asset bundles worth ₹1.1 lakh crore and 4 railway station redevelopment projects worth ₹18,000 crore have been awarded. The monetisation proceeds are classified as “capital receipts” and are earmarked for new capital expenditure under the “Asset Monetisation Fund” created in the Public Account, ensuring that the funds are not diverted to revenue expenditure.

Fiscal discipline requires that non‑tax receipts be treated as financing items rather than recurring revenue. The FRBM Act’s “effective revenue deficit” concept excludes one‑off capital receipts from the revenue deficit calculation, preventing the masking of structural imbalances. However, over‑reliance on disinvestment to meet deficit targets can create a “sale‑of‑assets” bias, reducing the government’s future revenue base (dividends, surplus from PSUs). The 15th Finance Commission recommended a ceiling of 1 % of GDP on disinvestment‑financed deficit, a guideline the Centre has broadly observed. Transparent reporting through the “Statement of Assets and Liabilities” in the Budget and the quarterly “Cash Management Report” of the Controller General of Accounts enables Parliament to scrutinise the composition and sustainability of non‑tax mobilisation.

Fiscal Policy Response to External Shocks: COVID‑19, Oil Price Volatility and Supply‑Chain Disruptions

External shocks test the flexibility of fiscal frameworks and the coordination between monetary and fiscal authorities. The COVID‑19 pandemic (2020‑21) triggered a 23.9 % contraction in Q1 FY 2020‑21 GDP, prompting the Government to announce the Atmanirbhar Bharat package totalling ₹29.87 lakh crore (≈ 15 % of GDP). The package combined direct fiscal measures (₹4.5 lakh crore for food security, cash transfers, and MGNREGA wage enhancements) with credit‑guarantee schemes (Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme — ECLGS) and structural reforms (agriculture marketing, labour codes). The fiscal deficit widened to 9.2 % of GDP in FY 2020‑21, well above the FRBM target of 3 %, invoking the “escape clause” under Section 4(2) of the FRBM Act, which permits a deviation of up to 0.5 % of GDP for national calamity; the Government invoked a 2.5 % deviation, justified by the unprecedented health‑economic crisis.

Oil price volatility exerts a dual fiscal impact: on the revenue side through excise duties on petroleum products (which contribute ~ ₹3.5 lakh crore annually) and on the expenditure side via fertiliser and fuel subsidies. The 2022‑23 spike in crude prices (Brent averaging US $105/barrel) led to a ₹1.2 lakh crore increase in fertiliser subsidy outlay, while the Centre raised excise duties on petrol and diesel by ₹13 and ₹16 per litre respectively in March 2022, partially offsetting the revenue loss from the earlier duty cuts of November 2021. The dynamic duty adjustment illustrates the use of “tax‑rate flexibility” as a fiscal stabiliser, a tool that is more immediate than legislative changes to the GST rate structure.

Supply‑chain disruptions, especially in semiconductors and critical minerals, have prompted targeted fiscal incentives. The Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, launched in 2020‑21 for 14 sectors with a total outlay of ₹1.97 lakh crore, provides output‑linked subsidies (4‑6 % of incremental sales) to attract domestic manufacturing. The semiconductor PLI (₹76,000 crore) and the critical minerals PLI (₹18,000 crore) are designed to reduce import dependence, thereby improving the current‑account balance and reducing future fiscal risk from exchange‑rate‑driven subsidy escalation. The scheme’s fiscal cost is front‑loaded; the 2023‑24 budget allocated ₹12,000 crore for PLI disbursements, with a built‑in sunset clause after five years.

Monetary‑fiscal coordination during these shocks was institutionalised through the “Monetary Policy Framework Agreement” (2016) and the regular meetings of the Fiscal‑Monetary Coordination Committee (FMCC) chaired by the Finance Secretary with the RBI Deputy Governor as co‑chair. During the pandemic, the RBI’s Open Market Operations (OMOs) and the Government’s “Ways and Means Advances” (WMA) limit enhancement (from ₹75,000 crore to ₹1.5 lakh crore) ensured that the borrowing programme (₹12 lakh crore in FY 2020‑21) was absorbed without excessive yield spikes. The 2022‑23 “Market Stabilisation Scheme” (MSS) bonds, issued to sterilise surplus liquidity from foreign portfolio inflows, further illustrate the joint toolkit. The experience underscores that a credible fiscal consolidation path (the glide‑path to 4.5 % fiscal deficit by FY 2025‑26 announced in the 2023‑24 budget) must be anchored in realistic revenue forecasts, flexible tax instruments, and a transparent off‑budget borrowing framework to preserve macro‑economic stability.

Fiscal Council and Independent Fiscal Institutions: Design, Mandate and International Experience

The idea of an independent fiscal council in India has been debated since the 13th Finance Commission (2010) recommended a “Fiscal Council” to monitor compliance with the FRBM Act and to provide macro‑fiscal forecasts. The 14th Finance Commission (2015) reiterated the need for a statutory body with a clear mandate, independent appointments, and direct reporting to Parliament. The 15th Finance Commission (2020) went further, proposing a Fiscal Council with a five‑year term, headed by a person of eminence in public finance, and equipped with powers to access all government data. Despite these recommendations, the FRBM Amendment Act, 2018 only inserted an enabling provision (Section 6A) for a Fiscal Council; the council has not been constituted as of the latest budget cycle.

International experience offers three broad design templates. The United Kingdom’s Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR), created in 2010, operates at arm’s length from the Treasury, publishes five‑year fiscal forecasts alongside the Budget, and evaluates the government’s compliance with its fiscal mandate. The United States Congressional Budget Office (CBO), established in 1974, provides non‑partisan analysis of budgetary and economic issues to Congress, including long‑term budget projections and cost estimates for legislation. Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO), created in 2006, reports to both Houses of Parliament and focuses on fiscal sustainability, costing of proposals, and economic forecasting. Common features across these institutions include: (i) statutory independence with fixed tenures; (ii) a mandate covering macro‑economic forecasting, fiscal rule monitoring, and long‑term sustainability analysis; (iii) transparent methodology and public release of reports; and (iv) accountability to the legislature rather than the executive.

For India, key design choices remain unresolved. Appointment mechanisms — whether by a collegium of the Finance Minister, the Leader of Opposition, and the CAG, or by a parliamentary committee — affect perceived independence. The council’s access to real‑time data from the Controller General of Accounts, the RBI, and the GST Network is essential for credible nowcasting. Reporting frequency (quarterly vs. annual) and the power to trigger a “fiscal correction” recommendation (as the OBR can) are also debated. The 2021‑22 Budget speech announced the government’s intention to set up a Fiscal Council, but the absence of enabling legislation means the institution remains a proposal. Until enacted, the CAG, the Finance Commission, and the RBI’s Monetary Policy Committee collectively perform fragmented oversight, leaving gaps in systematic, forward‑looking fiscal scrutiny.

Direct Benefit Transfer, JAM Trinity and Subsidy Leakage Reduction

The Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) programme, launched in January 2013 for LPG subsidies, was extended to over 300 schemes by 2023, covering food, fertiliser, scholarships, pensions, and wages under MGNREGA. The architectural backbone of DBT is the JAM trinity: Jan Dhan Yojana (universal bank accounts), Aadhaar (biometric unique identification), and Mobile (digital payment infrastructure). As of March 2024, more than 48 crore Jan Dhan accounts had been opened, Aadhaar coverage exceeded 135 crore residents, and the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) processed over 10 billion transactions per month, providing the rails for real‑time credit to beneficiaries.

Leakage reduction is the primary fiscal rationale. Pre‑DBT estimates for the Public Distribution System (PDS) suggested diversion rates of 35‑45 % of food grains. Post‑DBT evaluations by the Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO) and independent studies indicate that Aadhaar‑seeded PDS in states such as Andhra Pradesh and Telangana brought diversion down to single digits. In the fertiliser sector, the Neem‑coated urea policy combined with DBT reduced the subsidy bill by an estimated ₹15,000‑20,000 crore annually by curbing black‑market diversion. The 2022‑23 Union Budget allocated ₹3.5 lakh crore for DBT‑linked schemes, and the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) tracks each transaction from sanction to credit, enabling real‑time reconciliation.

Operational challenges persist. Exclusion errors — where eligible households fail authentication due to biometric mismatches or connectivity gaps — have been documented in tribal and remote regions. The Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India upheld Aadhaar’s constitutionality but mandated that no beneficiary be denied entitlements solely for lack of Aadhaar, prompting the government to allow alternative identification. The “last‑mile” delivery still relies on banking correspondents and post offices, whose coverage remains uneven. The 2023‑24 Economic Survey highlighted that while DBT has improved targeting efficiency, the fiscal savings are partially offset by the administrative cost of maintaining the JAM infrastructure, estimated at 0.2‑0.3 % of GDP annually.

Fiscal Risks from Public‑Private Partnerships: Contingent Liabilities and Viability Gap Funding

Public‑Private Partnerships (PPPs) have been the primary vehicle for infrastructure financing since the 2000s, with models such as Build‑Operate‑Transfer (BOT), Build‑Own‑Operate‑Transfer (BOOT), Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM), and Viability Gap Funding (VGF). While PPPs keep upfront capital off the government’s balance sheet, they generate contingent liabilities — explicit guarantees (e.g., revenue shortfall guarantees, termination payments) and implicit obligations (e.g., political pressure to bail out a failing project). The 2015 PPP Guidelines issued by the Department of Economic Affairs require that all contingent liabilities be disclosed in the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement, yet the Comptroller and Auditor General’s 2020 report noted that several state‑level PPPs omitted termination‑payment clauses from their disclosures.

Viability Gap Funding, introduced in 2004 and revamped in 2020 for social infrastructure (health, education, water), provides a capital grant of up to 20 % of project cost (40 % for projects in the North‑East and hill states). The VGF is disbursed through the India Infrastructure Finance Company Limited (IIFCL) and is accounted as a capital expenditure in the year of release. However, the fiscal risk arises when revenue projections underpinning the VGF prove optimistic; the government may face additional outlays if the concessionaire invokes the revenue‑shortfall guarantee. The 2021‑22 Budget announced a “National Monetisation Pipeline” to unlock value from brownfield assets, but the pipeline’s success hinges on robust risk‑sharing frameworks that limit open‑ended fiscal exposure.

A systematic PPP fiscal risk register — modelled on the IMF’s Public‑Private Partnership Fiscal Risk Assessment Model (PFRAM) — has been recommended by the 15th Finance Commission. Such a register would capture: (i) the stock of guaranteed debt, (ii) the probability‑weighted expected payout of termination clauses, (iii) the sensitivity of project cash flows to traffic or demand shocks, and (iv) the fiscal space available for contingent payouts. Until a centralized register is operational, the Union and state budgets rely on ad‑hoc disclosures, making it difficult for Parliament and rating agencies to assess the true extent of off‑balance‑sheet obligations.

Natural Resource Revenue Management: Mineral Royalties, Hydrocarbon Sharing and the District Mineral Foundation

India’s natural resource fiscal regime rests on two pillars: mineral royalties under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 (amended 2015, 2021) and hydrocarbon revenue sharing under the Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy (HELP), 2016. Royalty rates for major minerals are set by the Central Government and shared with states on a 50:50 basis; for minor minerals, states have full authority. The 2015 amendment introduced the District Mineral Foundation (DMF), funded by a 10 % contribution of royalty for major minerals and 30 % for minor minerals, earmarked for the welfare of mining‑affected communities. As of March 2024, DMFs across 600 districts had accumulated over ₹55,000 crore, with utilisation rates improving from 35 % (2018) to 68 % (2023) after the Ministry of Mines issued utilisation guidelines.

Hydrocarbon policy shifted from a production‑sharing contract (PSC) regime under the New Exploration Licensing Policy (NELP) to a revenue‑sharing model under HELP, where the government’s share is a percentage of gross revenue, bid by the contractor. This aligns fiscal returns with market prices and reduces administrative discretion. The 2022‑23 Budget created the National Mineral Exploration Trust (NMET) with a corpus of ₹1,000 crore to fund regional exploration, aiming to reduce import dependence on critical minerals such as lithium, cobalt, and rare earths.

Fiscal volatility is inherent: royalty and revenue‑sharing receipts swing with global commodity cycles. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a Natural Resource Fund (NRF) to smooth expenditures, but the proposal has not been legislated. In the interim, the Centre uses the “cess” route (e.g., the Clean Energy Cess on coal) to capture a portion of resource rents for specific purposes. Effective management requires transparent auction data, real‑time production monitoring (via the Integrated Mines and Minerals Management System), and a clear rule‑based transfer mechanism to avoid ad‑hoc allocations that undermine fiscal predictability.

Fiscal Policy, Exchange Rate Dynamics and Sovereign Credit Ratings

The interaction between fiscal policy and the external sector operates through the twin‑deficits channel: a widening fiscal deficit tends to increase the current‑account deficit by boosting domestic demand and imports, while also influencing capital‑flow expectations. India’s sovereign credit ratings — S&P (BBB‑, stable), Moody’s (Baa3, stable), Fitch (BBB‑, negative outlook as of 2023) — explicitly weight the general‑government debt‑to‑GDP trajectory, revenue buoyancy, and the credibility of the consolidation path. The 2023‑24 Budget’s glide‑path targeting a fiscal deficit of 4.5 % of GDP by 2025‑26 was calibrated to signal commitment to rating agencies, which view the FRBM Act’s statutory targets as a commitment device.

Exchange‑rate management by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) interacts with fiscal space. When capital inflows surge, the RBI intervenes to prevent excessive rupee appreciation, sterilising the liquidity through Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) bonds — a fiscal‑monetary coordination tool. Conversely, during outflow episodes (e.g., 2013 taper tantrum, 2020 COVID shock), the RBI draws down foreign‑exchange reserves and may allow depreciation, which raises the rupee‑denominated cost of external debt and imported subsidies (fertiliser, oil). The 2022‑23 spike in Brent crude to US $105/barrel increased the fertiliser subsidy by ₹1.2 lakh crore, while the Centre raised excise duties on petrol and diesel by ₹13 and ₹16 per litre respectively, illustrating the use of tax‑rate flexibility as a fiscal stabiliser.

Rating agencies also monitor the composition of debt: the share of foreign‑currency debt remains low (<5 % of total), but the rising proportion of marketable securities held by foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) introduces rollover risk. The 2021‑22 introduction of the “Fully Accessible Route” (FAR) for government securities aimed to broaden the investor base, yet FPI holdings are sensitive to global risk appetite. A credible medium‑term fiscal framework, transparent off‑budget borrowing disclosures, and a predictable tax‑policy environment (especially GST rate stability) are the primary levers to maintain rating stability and preserve low‑cost market access.

Fiscal Implications of Digital Services Taxation: Equalisation Levy, Significant Economic Presence and the OECD Pillar One Framework

The Equalisation Levy (EL) was introduced through the Finance Act, 2016 as a 6 % levy on consideration received by non‑resident e‑commerce operators for online advertising services. The scope was widened by the Finance Act, 2020 to a 2 % levy on the gross consideration received by non‑resident e‑commerce operators for the supply of goods and services facilitated through a digital platform, effective 1 April 2020. The levy applies only when the aggregate consideration exceeds ₹2 crore in a financial year, and the operator does not have a permanent establishment (PE) in India. Collections rose from ₹1,100 crore in FY 2017‑18 to ₹4,800 crore in FY 2022‑23, reflecting the rapid growth of digital advertising and marketplace revenues.

Parallel to the EL, the concept of Significant Economic Presence (SEP) was inserted in the Income‑Tax Act by the Finance Act, 2018 (effective 1 April 2019). SEP deems a non‑resident to have a business connection in India if it engages in systematic and continuous solicitation of business through digital means, or enters into contracts for the supply of goods or services with Indian residents, provided the aggregate payments exceed ₹2 crore. The SEP provision enables the taxation of business profits attributable to the Indian market even in the absence of a physical PE, aligning domestic law with the OECD’s “digital presence” nexus.

At the multilateral level, the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework finalised the Two‑Pillar solution in October 2021. Pillar One reallocates a portion of the residual profits of the largest and most profitable multinational enterprises (MNEs) to market jurisdictions based on a revenue‑based allocation key. India signed the Multilateral Convention (MLC) on 10 July 2023, committing to implement Pillar One once the convention enters into force. The convention provides for a safe‑harbour mechanism that would replace unilateral measures such as the EL and SEP for covered MNEs, but the transition rules allow India to retain the EL until the MLC is ratified by a critical mass of jurisdictions. The 2023‑24 Budget announced a review of the EL rate and threshold in light of the Pillar One timeline, signalling a possible reduction to 1 % once the multilateral regime becomes operational.

The coexistence of three overlapping regimes creates compliance complexity: a non‑resident digital platform may simultaneously be liable for EL on advertising revenue, SEP‑based business profit taxation on marketplace commissions, and, once Pillar One is effective, a top‑up tax on residual profits. The Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) issued Circular No. 12/2022 clarifying the order of application — EL is a final tax on gross consideration, SEP profits are computed under normal provisions after allowing deductions, and Pillar One top‑up would be a separate liability. Revenue forecasting for the EL uses a buoyancy estimate of 1.3 relative to nominal GDP growth, while SEP collections are modelled on the growth of cross‑border digital transactions reported through the Equalisation Levy Return (Form EL‑1) and the SEP disclosure in the Income‑Tax Return (ITR‑6).

Disaster Risk Financing: National Disaster Response Fund, State Disaster Response Funds and the Contingency Fund of India

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 established a three‑tier financing architecture. The National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) is financed primarily through the National Calamity Contingent Duty (NCCD) levied on tobacco products, pan masala and certain petroleum products. The NCCD rate was revised in the 2021‑22 Budget to ₹1.5 per kg on unmanufactured tobacco and ₹0.5 per kg on manufactured tobacco, generating an estimated ₹7,200 crore annually. The NDRF corpus stood at ₹23,000 crore as of 31 March 2023, with an average annual utilisation of ₹12,500 crore over the preceding five years.

State Disaster Response Funds (SDRFs) operate under a 75:25 Centre‑State sharing formula for general category states and 90:10 for special category states. The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended an enhanced allocation of ₹1.6 lakh crore for the SDRF cycle, a 30 % increase over the 14th FC award. The Centre releases its share in two instalments — 50 % in April and the balance after the state submits utilisation certificates. States are required to maintain a minimum balance of 10 % of the annual allocation; shortfalls trigger a “top‑up” from the NDRF.

The Contingency Fund of India, constituted under Article 267 of the Constitution, holds a corpus of ₹500 crore (raised from ₹50 crore in 2005). It is placed at the disposal of the President to meet unforeseen expenditure pending parliamentary approval. In practice, the fund is used for urgent disaster relief when the NDRF/SDRF release procedures cannot meet the immediacy of the response. The 2022‑23 Supplementary Demands for Grants included a ₹1,200 crore drawdown from the Contingency Fund for flood relief in Assam and Bihar, later regularised through the Appropriation Act.

Coordination among the three funds is governed by the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) guidelines issued in 2019. The guidelines mandate a “single window” release mechanism: the state submits a consolidated request to the Ministry of Home Affairs, which simultaneously triggers NDRF and SDRF releases. The 2023‑24 Budget introduced a “Disaster Resilience Fund” of ₹5,000 crore, earmarked for pre‑disaster mitigation projects, to be financed from the NDRF surplus and the National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF) green bond proceeds.

Fiscal Management of Public Sector Bank Recapitalisation: Recapitalisation Bonds, Capital Adequacy and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code

Following the asset‑quality review of 2015‑16, the Government of India embarked on a multi‑year recapitalisation programme for public sector banks (PSBs). The first tranche, announced in the 2017‑18 Budget, amounted to ₹2.11 lakh crore, of which ₹1.35 lakh crore was raised through recapitalisation bonds (non‑SLR, 10‑year tenor, coupon 7.5‑8 %). A second tranche of ₹1.06 lakh crore was approved in 2019‑20, again predominantly via bonds. The 2020‑21 Budget added ₹20,000 crore to address COVID‑related stress, bringing cumulative bond issuance to ₹2.61 lakh crore by March 2021.

These bonds are classified as “capital receipts” and do not increase the fiscal deficit because they are offset by an equivalent increase in the government’s equity holdings in PSBs. However, the interest outgo on the bonds (≈₹18,000 crore annually) is accounted for in the revenue expenditure, marginally widening the revenue deficit. The 2022‑23 Budget introduced a “capital infusion through non‑bond instruments” — direct budgetary support of ₹15,000 crore — to reduce the interest burden.

Capital adequacy is governed by the Basel III framework as adopted by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). PSBs are required to maintain a Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio of 8 % plus a capital conservation buffer of 2.5 % (total 10.5 %). As of March 2023, the aggregate CET1 of PSBs stood at 11.2 %, up from 9.1 % in March 2018, reflecting the recapitalisation impact. The RBI’s Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, revised in 2017, uses CET1, net NPA ratio and leverage ratio as triggers; by 2023 only two PSBs remained under PCA.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, complemented recapitalisation by providing a time‑bound resolution mechanism for stressed assets. As of December 2023, the IBC had facilitated the resolution of 1,200 corporate debtors with an aggregate admitted claim of ₹8.5 lakh crore, recovering ₹3.2 lakh crore (38 % recovery rate). The 2021 amendment introduced a “pre‑packaged insolvency resolution process” for MSMEs, accelerating resolution for smaller borrowers that form a large share of PSB loan books. The combined effect of recapitalisation, Basel III compliance and IBC resolutions has reduced the gross NPA ratio of PSBs from 11.5 % (March 2018) to 4.8 % (March 2023).

Fiscal Policy and Income Inequality: Progressive Taxation, Wealth Tax Debates and the Role of the Direct Tax Code

India’s personal income‑tax structure has become progressively steeper since the 2017‑18 Budget, which introduced a 10 % surcharge on income above ₹50 lakh and a 15 % surcharge above ₹1 crore. The 2020‑21 Budget added a new 25 % surcharge on income above ₹2 crore and a 37 % surcharge above ₹5 crore, raising the effective marginal rate for the top bracket to 42.74 % (including health and education cess). The 2023‑24 Budget retained these rates but increased the rebate under Section 87A to ₹7 lakh, effectively exempting incomes up to that threshold.

The Wealth Tax Act, 1957, was abolished effective 1 April 2016 (Finance Act, 2015) on the grounds of high collection cost (≈0.03 % of GDP) and low yield (₹1,000 crore in FY 2013‑14). Since then, several committees — the 2018 “Direct Tax Code” (DTC) Task Force chaired by Arbind Modi, and the 2021 “Tax Reforms Committee” under the 15th Finance Commission — have recommended a re‑introduction of a net‑wealth tax on assets above ₹10 crore at 1‑2 % to address wealth concentration. The DTC draft (2019) proposed a unified tax code merging income‑tax, wealth‑tax and gift‑tax, with a simplified slab structure and a general anti‑avoidance rule (GAAR) aligned with the OECD BEPS Action 6. The DTC has not been enacted; the government has preferred incremental amendments to the Income‑Tax Act.

Empirical studies using the World Inequality Database (2022) show that the top 1 % of earners captured 21 % of national income in 2021, up from 15 % in 2000. The Gini coefficient for post‑tax income fell marginally from 0.49 (2015) to 0.47 (2021) due to expanded direct benefit transfers (DBT) and higher surcharge collections. However, the tax‑to‑GDP ratio remains low at 10.8 % (2022‑23), limiting redistributive capacity. The 15th Finance Commission’s “horizontal distribution formula” assigns a 12.5 % weight to “tax effort”, incentivising states to broaden their tax bases, but does not directly address vertical inequality.

Policy options under discussion include: (i) a “millionaire surcharge” of 5 % on income above ₹10 crore, (ii) a modest annual wealth tax of 0.5 % on net assets above ₹50 crore with a valuation safe‑harbour, and (iii) expanding the scope of the Securities Transaction Tax (STT) to cover derivative notional values. The 2024‑25 Budget speech indicated a “comprehensive review of direct tax architecture” but stopped short of announcing a wealth tax, citing administrative complexity and the risk of capital flight.

Fiscal Federalism and Inter‑State Trade Barriers: The Impact of Entry Taxes, Octroi Abolition and the GST Compensation Cess on State Revenues

Prior to GST, states levied entry taxes on goods entering their territory and octroi on goods entering municipal limits. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, and the subsequent GST Acts (2017) subsumed these levies. The Central Government compensated states for the loss of entry‑tax and octroi revenue through the GST Compensation Cess, levied on sin goods (tobacco, aerated drinks, luxury cars) and on coal. The cess was designed to guarantee a 14 % year‑on‑year growth in protected revenue for five years (2017‑22).

Actual collections of the compensation cess fell short of the guaranteed amount from FY 2019‑20 onward due to lower-than‑expected consumption of cess‑able goods and the COVID‑19 demand shock. The shortfall peaked at ₹1.1 lakh crore in FY 2020‑21. The Centre met the gap by borrowing ₹1.1 lakh crore from the market in 2020‑21 and ₹1.59 lakh crore in 2021‑22, on‑lending to states as back‑to‑back loans with a 40‑year tenor and a 0.5 % interest subvention. The 15th Finance Commission recommended extending the compensation period by two years (till 2024) and restructuring the cess base to include a broader set of goods, but the GST Council has not yet adopted the extension.

The abolition of entry taxes and octroi removed a major source of own‑tax revenue for states such as Maharashtra, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu, which previously collected ₹30,000‑₹40,000 crore annually from these levies. While GST subsumed the tax base, the revenue neutrality assumption relied on a higher GST rate structure (28 % on many goods) that has been progressively reduced. The net effect has been a shift from buoyant, destination‑based entry taxes to a shared GST pool where the state’s share depends on the Integrated GST (IGST) settlement formula and the devolution formula of the Finance Commission.

Inter‑state trade barriers persist in non‑GST areas: stamp duty on property transactions, motor‑vehicle taxes, and electricity duties. The 2022‑23 Economic Survey highlighted that the “effective inter‑state trade cost” remains 1.5‑2 % of the value of goods due to compliance frictions (e‑way bill mismatches, differing HSN code interpretations). The GST Council’s 2023 decision to introduce a “single national e‑way bill” and to harmonise HSN codes at the 6‑digit level aims to reduce this friction. Fiscal federalism analysis suggests that a permanent compensation mechanism — either a restructured cess or a dedicated “state revenue equalisation fund” — is essential to prevent fiscal stress in high‑consumption states that lost entry‑tax buoyancy.

Fiscal Implications of the National Small Savings Fund on Government Borrowing and Interest Cost

The National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) aggregates deposits from schemes such as the Public Provident Fund, National Savings Certificate, Senior Citizens Savings Scheme and the Sukanya Samriddhi Account. These collections constitute a captive source of low‑cost financing for the Centre because the Government of India issues special securities to the NSSF at rates linked to the prevailing market yield on dated government securities, typically with a small spread. The fund’s net accretions are transferred to the Consolidated Fund of India and used to meet the fiscal deficit, thereby reducing the volume of market borrowings that the Centre must raise through dated securities and treasury bills.

Because the NSSF’s interest obligations are accounted for under the revenue expenditure head “Interest Payments”, any increase in the fund’s corpus directly raises the revenue deficit. In recent years the government has lowered the administered rates on small‑savings instruments to align them with market rates, which compresses the spread but also slows net inflows. The 2022‑23 Budget introduced a quarterly reset of small‑savings rates based on the average yield of the 10‑year benchmark G‑sec, a move intended to prevent arbitrage and to make the NSSF a more market‑aligned financing window.

The NSSF also influences the maturity profile of central government debt. Securities issued to the fund carry long tenors (10‑30 years), extending the average maturity of the debt portfolio and lowering rollover risk. However, the fund’s large holdings create a concentration risk: a sudden surge in withdrawals — triggered by a sharp rise in market rates or a policy change — could force the Centre to replace NSSF financing with market borrowing at higher yields, widening the fiscal deficit. The Public Debt Management Cell monitors the NSSF’s share of total outstanding liabilities (around 15‑18 % in recent years) and coordinates with the Department of Economic Affairs to calibrate issuance calendars.

From a fiscal‑policy perspective, the NSSF acts as an automatic stabiliser during periods of high market volatility because small‑savings inflows tend to be sticky. Yet the government’s ability to use the fund for deficit financing is constrained by the statutory ceiling on the fund’s net accretions and by the requirement that the fund’s investments remain in government securities. Any proposal to diversify NSSF assets into infrastructure or equity would require legislative amendment and carries implications for the fund’s risk profile and for the transparency of off‑budget financing.

Fiscal Federalism and the Divisible Pool: Cess, Surcharge and the Shrinking Share of States

The Constitution mandates that the net proceeds of certain central taxes — primarily income tax, corporation tax, customs duties, excise duties (excluding those on petroleum and tobacco) and the central goods and services tax — form the divisible pool that is shared between the Centre and the states according to the Finance Commission’s vertical distribution formula. However, cesses and surcharges levied by the Centre are explicitly excluded from the divisible pool under Article 270 and Article 271. Over the past decade the Centre has increasingly relied on cesses (e.g., the Health and Education Cess, the Road and Infrastructure Cess, the Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess) and surcharges (e.g., the surcharge on high‑income earners) to raise revenue without enlarging the states’ share.

Because cesses and surcharges are earmarked for specific purposes, they are not subject to the vertical sharing formula. The 15th Finance Commission noted that the share of cesses and surcharges in gross tax revenue rose from about 9 % in 2014‑15 to over 15 % in 2021‑22, effectively reducing the divisible pool by a corresponding margin. This structural shift has two consequences: first, states receive a smaller proportion of total central tax collections, tightening their fiscal space; second, the earmarked nature of cess revenue limits the Centre’s flexibility to reallocate resources across sectors, creating rigidities in expenditure planning.

The GST regime introduced a compensation cess on sin goods and coal to guarantee states a 14 % year‑on‑year growth in protected revenue for five years. While the compensation cess is technically a cess, its proceeds are transferred to states as compensation, partially offsetting the loss of entry‑tax and octroi revenue. However, the compensation cess is also excluded from the divisible pool, and its collections have been volatile, leading to back‑to‑back loans from the Centre to states. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a gradual phasing out of the compensation cess and its replacement with a broader-based revenue source, but the GST Council has not yet adopted a permanent mechanism.

Policy debates centre on whether the Constitution should be amended to bring a defined portion of cess and surcharge revenue into the divisible pool, or whether the Finance Commission should be empowered to recommend a “cess‑adjusted” vertical share. The 16th Finance Commission, when constituted, will need to address the growing wedge between the Centre’s gross tax revenue and the divisible pool, and to assess the impact on horizontal equity among states with differing capacities to generate own‑tax revenue.

Fiscal Rules for Capital Expenditure: The Capex Multiplier, Crowding‑In and the Golden Rule

The “golden rule” of fiscal policy — borrowed funds should finance only capital expenditure that yields future income streams — has been a guiding principle for the Union Budget since the FRBM Act’s inception. In practice, the Centre has increasingly front‑loaded capital outlays (roads, railways, defence, water supply) while containing revenue expenditure growth. Empirical studies by the Reserve Bank of India and the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy estimate the short‑run fiscal multiplier for central capital expenditure in the range of 1.5 to 2.5, meaning each rupee of capex generates 1.5‑2.5 rupees of additional GDP within a year. The multiplier is higher during periods of slack capacity and when projects have strong forward linkages (e.g., highway construction stimulating cement, steel and logistics).

Crowding‑in occurs when public capital formation improves the productivity of private investment — for instance, a new freight corridor reduces logistics costs, raising the marginal return on private warehousing and manufacturing. The 2023‑24 Economic Survey highlighted that the ratio of gross fixed capital formation to GDP rose from 28.6 % in 2019‑20 to 31.2 % in 2022‑23, partly driven by the Centre’s capex push. However, the survey also cautioned that the quality of spending matters: projects with long gestation, land‑acquisition delays or weak contract enforcement deliver lower multipliers and can exacerbate the revenue deficit if financed through borrowing.

The FRBM framework does not prescribe a separate capex ceiling; instead, the fiscal deficit target (3 % of GDP by 2025‑26 as per the 2023 amendment) indirectly limits borrowing for capital outlays. The Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement projects a declining revenue deficit while allowing the primary deficit to accommodate capex. Some states have adopted a “capital expenditure rule” that caps revenue expenditure growth at a percentage of GSDP while protecting capex from cuts. The 15th Finance Commission incentivised states that maintain a capex‑to‑GSDP ratio above a threshold by granting additional borrowing space.

From a debt‑sustainability perspective, capex financed by borrowing is sustainable only if the incremental output growth raises the nominal GDP denominator faster than the debt stock. The debt‑to‑GDP trajectory therefore hinges on the realised multiplier. If the multiplier falls below unity — due to implementation bottlenecks or misallocation — the debt ratio worsens, violating the golden rule in effect. Continuous monitoring through the Public Expenditure Management System (PFMS) and the Output‑Outcome Monitoring Framework is essential to ensure that borrowed funds translate into productive assets.

Fiscal Transparency and the Open Budget Survey: India's Scores, Methodology and Reform Agenda

The Open Budget Survey (OBS), conducted biennially by the International Budget Partnership, evaluates countries on three pillars: budget transparency (availability and comprehensiveness of eight key budget documents), public participation (opportunities for citizens and civil society to engage in the budget process), and oversight (strength of legislative and audit institutions). In the 2023 round India scored 45 out of 100, ranking 53rd among 120 countries. The transparency sub‑score was 48, participation 13, and oversight 71. The low participation score reflects limited formal mechanisms for public input during budget formulation and execution, while the oversight score benefits from an active Public Accounts Committee and a constitutionally empowered Comptroller and Auditor General.

The OBS methodology assigns points for the timely publication of the Pre‑Budget Statement, Executive’s Budget Proposal, Enacted Budget, Citizens Budget, In‑Year Reports, Mid‑Year Review, Year‑End Report and Audit Report. India publishes all eight documents, but the Citizens Budget — a non‑technical summary for the general public — has historically been released late and lacks disaggregated data on scheme‑level allocations. The 2022‑23 Budget introduced a “Budget at a Glance” booklet and an interactive dashboard on the India Budget Portal, steps aimed at improving the transparency score.

Reform proposals under discussion include: (i) institutionalising a pre‑budget consultation framework with state governments, industry associations and civil‑society organisations, with a mandatory response document; (ii) legislating a “Budget Transparency Act” that fixes publication deadlines for each document and mandates machine‑readable data formats; (iii) strengthening the role of the Parliamentary Budget Office (proposed but not yet established) to provide independent fiscal analysis to legislators; and (iv) expanding the scope of the Citizens Budget to include gender‑responsive and climate‑responsive budgeting tags.

Improving the OBS score is not an end in itself; higher transparency correlates with better credit ratings, lower borrowing costs and more effective allocation of resources. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that the Centre and states adopt the OBS framework as a benchmark for fiscal transparency reforms, and the Ministry of Finance has constituted a working group to map the survey’s indicators to the Public Financial Management System’s reporting modules.

Fiscal Management of Public Sector Undertakings' Dividend Policy and Its Budgetary Impact

Dividends from Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) constitute a non‑tax revenue receipt that directly reduces the Centre’s revenue deficit. The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) issues guidelines requiring profitable CPSEs to pay a minimum dividend of 30 % of profit after tax or 5 % of net worth, whichever is higher, unless exempted by the administrative ministry. In 2022‑23 the Centre received approximately ₹0.48 lakh crore as dividend receipts, accounting for roughly 12 % of total non‑tax revenue.

The dividend policy interacts with the disinvestment programme. When the government sells equity stakes, the dividend stream from the residual holding declines, creating a trade‑off between one‑off disinvestment proceeds and recurring dividend income. The 2021‑22 Budget introduced a “dividend‑linked disinvestment” framework: CPSEs that declare higher dividends are given priority for strategic sale, on the rationale that a strong dividend track record signals robust cash flows and governance.

From a fiscal‑sustainability angle, dividend receipts are volatile — they depend on commodity cycles (for oil, mining and steel CPSEs), regulatory tariff orders (for power utilities) and global demand. The revenue budget therefore treats dividend income as a “non‑recurring” receipt for the purpose of the fiscal deficit calculation, even though it appears under revenue receipts. This classification prevents the Centre from using dividend windfalls to finance permanent revenue expenditure.

Recent reforms aim to make dividend flows more predictable. The 2023‑24 guidelines allow CPSEs to set aside a “dividend stabilisation reserve” up to 10 % of net profit, which can be drawn upon in lean years to maintain the minimum payout. Additionally, the Centre has encouraged CPSEs to adopt a “total shareholder return” approach, combining dividends with share buybacks, to align capital allocation with market expectations. The impact of these measures on the revenue deficit will be monitored through the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement and the Outcome Budget.

Ways and Means Advances and the RBI Overdraft Facility: Mechanics, Limits and Fiscal Implications

The Ways and Means Advances (WMA) scheme is the primary short‑term liquidity window that the Reserve Bank of India extends to the Central Government. Under the scheme the Centre can draw up to a pre‑approved limit — currently ₹50,000 crore — without providing collateral. The limit is reviewed periodically by the RBI in consultation with the Ministry of Finance and is calibrated to the Centre’s cash‑flow profile, especially the timing of tax inflows and large expenditure outlays such as subsidy releases and capital transfers.

When the WMA limit is exhausted, the Centre may access an overdraft facility. The overdraft is capped at 10 % of the WMA limit (i.e. ₹5,000 crore) and can be used for a maximum of ten consecutive working days. Interest on WMA is charged at the repo rate, while the overdraft carries a penal rate of repo + 2 percentage points. Both rates are reset in line with the monetary policy stance, making the cost of short‑term borrowing directly sensitive to the RBI’s policy corridor.

From a fiscal‑management perspective, WMA and overdraft drawings are recorded as “cash management” operations and do not alter the fiscal deficit. However, persistent reliance on the overdraft signals a mismatch between receipts and payments within the financial year and can trigger a review of the borrowing calendar. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) flags repeated overdraft usage in its reports on the Union Government’s accounts, and the Public Accounts Committee often seeks explanations from the Finance Ministry.

The scheme also has a state‑level counterpart. Each state government has a separate WMA limit determined by the RBI based on its fiscal position and revenue buoyancy. States that breach their limits repeatedly may face restrictions on market borrowings under the FRBM framework. The Centre’s WMA limit is typically larger because of its broader revenue base and the need to manage large, lumpy outflows such as the release of GST compensation cess and the National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme wages.

In practice, the Centre aims to minimise WMA usage by front‑loading tax collections through advance tax payments and by aligning the borrowing calendar with expected cash deficits. The 2023‑24 borrowing calendar, for instance, scheduled a higher volume of dated‑security issuances in the first half of the year to reduce the need for WMA drawdowns during the lean months of July‑September.

Sovereign External Borrowing: Composition, Cost and Risk Management Framework

India’s sovereign external debt comprises loans from multilateral institutions (World Bank, Asian Development Bank, International Monetary Fund), bilateral creditors (Japan, Germany, France), and market‑based instruments such as sovereign bonds denominated in foreign currencies. As of end‑March 2023 the outstanding external debt of the Central Government was around US$ 600 billion, accounting for roughly 20 % of total public debt. The share of market‑based borrowing has risen steadily since the 1990s, reflecting the government’s strategy to diversify funding sources and lengthen maturities.

External loans from multilaterals are typically concessional, carrying interest rates of 0.5‑1.5 % with long grace periods and maturities of 20‑30 years. Bilateral loans, especially those under the Japanese Official Development Assistance programme, are also low‑cost but may be tied to specific projects. Market‑issued sovereign bonds, on the other hand, are priced at a spread over US Treasury yields; the spread reflects India’s sovereign credit rating, global risk appetite, and the currency denomination (mostly US dollars, with smaller issuances in yen and euros).

The Ministry of Finance, through the Department of Economic Affairs, manages external borrowing within a medium‑term debt management strategy approved by the Cabinet. The strategy sets ceilings on the share of external debt in total debt (currently 25 %), limits on short‑term external liabilities, and targets for the average maturity of new issuances (typically above 10 years). The RBI acts as the fiscal agent, executing issuances, managing the redemption profile, and hedging currency risk through forward contracts where appropriate.

Risk management focuses on three dimensions: currency risk, rollover risk, and interest‑rate risk. Currency risk is mitigated by maintaining a diversified currency basket and by using natural hedges — export earnings and remittance inflows — to service dollar‑denominated debt. Rollover risk is addressed by smoothing the redemption schedule; the government avoids clustering large repayments in any single year. Interest‑rate risk on floating‑rate external loans is limited because the bulk of external debt is fixed‑rate.

Contingent liabilities arising from sovereign guarantees to public sector enterprises for external borrowing are monitored through the Guarantee Redemption Fund. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that the Centre disclose the full stock of guaranteed external debt in the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement, a practice that has been adopted from the 2022‑23 Budget onward.

Government Securities Issuance Calendar: Design, Communication and Market Impact

The Reserve Bank of India publishes a half‑yearly issuance calendar for Central Government dated securities and treasury bills. The calendar specifies the aggregate amount to be raised, the tenor buckets (e.g., 2‑year, 5‑year, 10‑year, 30‑year), and the indicative timing of each auction. It is released at least two weeks before the start of the half‑year, giving primary dealers, institutional investors, and foreign portfolio investors a forward view of supply.

Design of the calendar balances three objectives: meeting the gross borrowing requirement approved by Parliament, maintaining a smooth maturity profile to avoid redemption spikes, and supporting the development of the yield curve. The Ministry of Finance provides the gross borrowing target; the RBI translates it into a calendar after consulting the Primary Dealer Association and assessing market liquidity conditions. In years of high fiscal deficit, the calendar may front‑load issuances in the first half to exploit favourable market sentiment.

Communication is reinforced through the RBI’s “Press Release – Government Securities Auction Calendar” and the “Weekly Statistical Supplement” which tracks actual issuance against the calendar. Deviations are explained in the subsequent Monetary Policy Report. Transparency in the calendar reduces uncertainty premiums in bond yields; empirical studies by the RBI show that a well‑telegraphed calendar lowers the bid‑to‑cover ratio volatility by 15‑20 %.

Market impact is observed through the behaviour of the yield curve on auction days. A larger‑than‑expected supply in a particular tenor typically steepens that segment of the curve, while a shortfall can cause a rally. The RBI uses the “green‑shoe” option (additional issuance up to 10 % of the notified amount) to manage oversubscription without surprising the market. The calendar also influences the RBI’s open market operations (OMOs); when the calendar indicates a heavy supply week, the RBI may conduct purchase OMOs to absorb excess liquidity and keep short‑term rates aligned with the policy corridor.

Recent reforms have introduced a “switch auction” facility, allowing the government to exchange near‑maturity securities for longer‑dated ones, thereby extending the average maturity without increasing gross borrowing. The switch auctions are announced alongside the regular calendar and have been used extensively since 2021‑22 to manage the redemption profile of the 2024‑25 and 2025‑26 maturity walls.

Fiscal Deficit Targeting under Nominal GDP Growth: The Denominator Effect and Policy Trade‑offs

The fiscal deficit is expressed as a percentage of nominal GDP, making the ratio sensitive to both the numerator (absolute deficit) and the denominator (nominal GDP). When nominal GDP grows rapidly — driven by real growth and inflation — the deficit‑to‑GDP ratio can fall even if the absolute deficit remains unchanged. This “denominator effect” was evident in 2021‑22 when nominal GDP expanded by over 19 % and the fiscal deficit ratio improved from 9.5 % to 6.7 % despite a higher absolute borrowing.

Policy makers exploit the denominator effect by setting deficit targets in nominal terms rather than real terms. The FRBM Act mandates a fiscal deficit target of 3 % of GDP by 2025‑26, but the glide path is calibrated on projected nominal GDP growth. If growth undershoots projections, the same absolute deficit yields a higher ratio, potentially breaching the target. Conversely, an inflation‑driven nominal GDP surge can create fiscal space without any expenditure compression.

The trade‑off arises because relying on the denominator effect may mask structural revenue weaknesses. A high nominal GDP growth fueled by inflation does not necessarily improve the government’s ability to service debt in real terms, especially if interest payments are indexed to inflation. Moreover, the revenue buoyancy of major taxes (income tax, GST) may not keep pace with nominal GDP if the tax base is narrow or compliance lags.

To mitigate this, the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement includes a “structural deficit” estimate that strips out cyclical and inflationary components. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that the Centre and states publish a “deficit‑adjusted‑for‑nominal‑GDP‑growth” metric alongside the headline ratio. This practice helps Parliament assess whether deficit reduction is driven by genuine fiscal consolidation or merely by a favourable denominator.

In the annual budget, the Finance Minister often presents two scenarios: a baseline with the official nominal GDP projection and a stress scenario with 1‑2 percentage‑points lower growth. The stress scenario shows the required additional revenue measures or expenditure cuts to stay within the FRBM glide path, making the denominator effect explicit for legislative scrutiny.

Revenue Forecasting Errors and Their Impact on Fiscal Deficit Outcomes: Methodology, Empirical Evidence and Corrective Measures

Revenue forecasting is the cornerstone of budget credibility. The Ministry of Finance uses a combination of econometric models (tax buoyancy and elasticity estimates), time‑series extrapolation, and sectoral inputs from the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) and the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC). Buoyancy measures the percentage change in tax revenue for a 1 % change in nominal GDP; elasticity isolates the policy‑induced component by holding the tax base constant.

Empirical analysis of the past two decades shows that the Centre’s gross tax revenue forecast error (budget estimate minus actual) has averaged around ±4 % of the estimate, with a systematic upward bias in the pre‑2014 period and a downward bias after the GST rollout. The largest single‑year deviation occurred in 2020‑21 when the COVID‑19 shock caused a 15 % shortfall relative to the budget estimate, pushing the fiscal deficit to 9.5 % of GDP.

Forecast errors feed directly into the fiscal deficit because the expenditure side is largely committed (interest, pensions, salaries). A revenue shortfall forces the government to either increase borrowing or compress capital expenditure. The 2022‑23 Budget introduced a “Revenue Forecast Review” at the mid‑year stage, where the Finance Ministry revises the full‑year revenue projection based on actual collections up to September. This review informs the supplementary demands for grants and the revised borrowing calendar.

Corrective measures include: (i) improving the granularity of tax‑base data through the GST Network and the Income Tax Department’s “Project Insight”; (ii) adopting a “nowcasting” framework that uses high‑frequency indicators (e‑way bills, PMI, electricity consumption) to update quarterly revenue projections; (iii) institutionalising an independent Fiscal Council (as recommended by the 14th and 15th Finance Commissions) to audit the forecasting methodology and publish an annual “Forecast Evaluation Report”.

No FRBM (Amendment) Bill was introduced in 2023; the last amendment was in 2018. the fiscal year‑end, quantifying the error and explaining the drivers. This transparency mechanism is expected to reduce systematic bias and improve the reliability of the fiscal deficit path presented in the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement.
