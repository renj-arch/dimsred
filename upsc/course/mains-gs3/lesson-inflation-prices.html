1. Understanding Inflation: Concepts and Causes

1.1 Definition and Core Drivers

Inflation is the sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time, which erodes the purchasing power of money. It is measured as the percentage change in a price index from one period to the next. The core drivers of inflation can be grouped into demand‑side forces, supply‑side forces, and the role of expectations. When aggregate demand grows faster than the economy’s productive capacity, prices are bid up, creating demand‑pull inflation. Conversely, when production costs rise because of higher wages, raw‑material prices, or supply disruptions, firms pass these costs on to consumers, generating cost‑push inflation. Expectations about future inflation influence wage‑setting and price‑setting behaviour, embedding a self‑fulfilling component that can persist even after the original shock has faded.

1.2 Demand‑Pull Inflation

Demand‑pull inflation arises when total spending in the economy — comprising consumption, investment, government expenditure, and net exports — exceeds the level of output that can be produced at full employment. In such a situation, firms face excess demand and respond by raising prices because they cannot immediately expand production. The phenomenon is often associated with expansionary fiscal policy, such as large budget deficits financed by borrowing, or with loose monetary policy that lowers interest rates and stimulates credit growth. Historical episodes, such as the post‑World War II boom in the United States, illustrate how rapid demand growth can outpace supply, leading to a persistent upward drift in the price level.

1.3 Cost‑Push Inflation

Cost‑push inflation originates from the supply side when the cost of production rises independently of demand. Sharp increases in the price of oil, as experienced during the 1973 and 1979 oil crises, raise transportation and input costs across a wide range of industries. Similarly, wage‑push inflation can occur when strong labour unions negotiate nominal wage increases that exceed productivity growth, forcing firms to raise prices to maintain profit margins. Unlike demand‑pull inflation, cost‑push inflation can coexist with stagnant or falling output, a situation described as stagflation, which poses a particular challenge for policymakers because conventional demand‑management tools may worsen unemployment.

1.4 Built‑In Inflation and Expectations

Built‑in inflation, sometimes called inertial inflation, reflects the tendency of past inflation to feed into current price‑setting through adaptive expectations. Workers and firms observe recent inflation rates and incorporate them into wage contracts and pricing decisions, creating a feedback loop. The expectations‑augmented Phillips curve formalises this mechanism by showing that the short‑run trade‑off between inflation and unemployment depends on expected inflation. When expectations become unanchored — for example, after a prolonged period of high inflation — the economy can experience a wage‑price spiral that is difficult to break without a credible commitment to low inflation, often delivered through an independent central bank with an explicit inflation target.

2. Measurement of Inflation: Price Indices

2.1 Wholesale Price Index (WPI)

The Wholesale Price Index tracks the average change in prices of goods at the wholesale level, that is, at the first commercial transaction after production. In India, the WPI basket comprises three major groups: primary articles, fuel and power, and manufactured products, with weights derived from the value of production in the base year. Because it captures price movements before they reach the retail consumer, the WPI is often used as a leading indicator of inflationary pressures in the pipeline. However, the WPI excludes services, which constitute a large and growing share of the economy, and its weighting structure may not reflect current consumption patterns.

2.2 Consumer Price Index (CPI)

The Consumer Price Index measures the change in the price of a fixed basket of goods and services typically purchased by households. India publishes several CPI series — CPI‑Combined, CPI‑Urban, CPI‑Rural — each with its own weighting diagram based on consumer expenditure surveys. The CPI includes food, fuel, housing, clothing, transport, education, and medical care, making it a more comprehensive gauge of the cost of living. The Reserve Bank of India adopted the CPI‑Combined as the nominal anchor for its flexible inflation‑targeting framework in 2016, targeting a 4 % inflation rate with a tolerance band of ±2 %.

2.3 GDP Deflator and Other Measures

The GDP deflator is a broad measure of price change for all domestically produced final goods and services, calculated as the ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP multiplied by 100. Unlike the WPI and CPI, the GDP deflator’s basket changes each year to reflect the current composition of output, so it automatically incorporates new products and structural shifts. Other specialised indices include the Producer Price Index (PPI), which tracks prices received by producers, and the Core Inflation index, which excludes volatile food and energy components to reveal underlying trends.

2.4 Comparing WPI and CPI

While both indices aim to capture inflation, they differ in coverage, weighting, and usage. The WPI is producer‑oriented, excludes services, and uses a fixed base‑year weighting scheme, making it more sensitive to commodity price swings. The CPI is consumer‑oriented, includes services, and is updated more frequently to reflect changing consumption patterns, making it a better proxy for the cost of living. Policymakers in India now rely primarily on the CPI for monetary policy, whereas the WPI continues to be monitored for early signals of input‑cost pressures. Worked Example: Computing Inflation Rate from CPI
Suppose the CPI‑Combined for January 2023 is 176.5 and for January 2024 is 184.2. Calculate the year‑on‑year inflation rate for January 2024.
The inflation rate is calculated as ((CPIcurrent − CPIprevious) ÷ CPIprevious) × 100. Substituting the values: ((184.2 − 176.5) ÷ 176.5) × 100 = (7.7 ÷ 176.5) × 100 ≈ 4.36 %. Therefore, the year‑on‑year CPI inflation for January 2024 is approximately 4.4 %.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the “headline inflation” versus “core inflation”, remember that headline inflation refers to the full CPI (or WPI) number, while core inflation excludes food and fuel. The RBI’s inflation‑targeting framework explicitly targets headline CPI, but analysts often track core CPI to assess the persistence of price pressures.

3. The Phillips Curve, Output Gap and Policy Implications

3.1 Original Phillips Curve and Its Evolution

The original Phillips curve, published by A. W. Phillips in 1958, documented an inverse empirical relationship between wage inflation and unemployment in the United Kingdom over the period 1861‑1957. Subsequent work by Samuelson and Solow extended the concept to price inflation, suggesting a stable trade‑off that policymakers could exploit: lower unemployment could be achieved at the cost of higher inflation. However, the stagflation of the 1970s — simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment — demonstrated that the simple curve broke down when expectations adjusted. This led to the development of the expectations‑augmented Phillips curve, which incorporates expected inflation as a shift parameter.

3.2 Expectations‑Augmented Phillips Curve

In the expectations‑augmented formulation, the short‑run Phillips curve is expressed as π = πᵉ − β(u − uⁿ) + ε, where π is actual inflation, πᵉ is expected inflation, u is the unemployment rate, uⁿ is the natural rate of unemployment (NAIRU), β > 0 captures the sensitivity of inflation to unemployment gaps, and ε represents supply shocks. When expectations are adaptive, πᵉ equals last period’s inflation, so a persistent expansionary policy that pushes unemployment below uⁿ raises inflation, which in turn raises expectations, shifting the curve upward. If expectations are rational and the central bank is credible, πᵉ anchors to the target, flattening the short‑run trade‑off and making disinflation less costly.

3.3 Output Gap as a Policy Guide

The output gap measures the difference between actual real GDP and potential GDP, expressed as a percentage of potential GDP. A positive output gap (actual > potential) signals overheating and upward pressure on inflation, while a negative gap indicates slack and disinflationary pressure. Central banks use the output gap, together with inflation forecasts, to set the policy interest rate via a Taylor‑type rule: i = r* + π* + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5(y − y*), where i is the nominal policy rate, r* the equilibrium real rate, π* the inflation target, and y − y* the output gap. Accurate estimation of potential output is therefore crucial; over‑estimation can lead to overly accommodative policy and entrenched inflation, while under‑estimation can cause unnecessary tightening and higher unemployment.

4. Business Cycle: Phases, Indicators and Interaction with Inflation

4.1 Expansion, Peak, Contraction and Trough

The business cycle describes the recurrent fluctuations in aggregate economic activity around its long‑term growth trend. An expansion phase is characterised by rising output, employment, and income, often accompanied by increasing capacity utilisation. The peak marks the turning point where growth reaches its maximum before decelerating. A contraction (or recession) follows, with falling output, rising unemployment, and declining investment. The trough is the lowest point, after which a new expansion begins. The duration and amplitude of each phase vary across cycles; for instance, the post‑2008 global financial crisis produced a deep, prolonged contraction in many advanced economies, whereas the COVID‑19 pandemic triggered a sharp but brief contraction followed by a rapid, policy‑driven recovery.

4.2 Leading, Coincident and Lagging Indicators

Economists monitor a suite of indicators to gauge the cycle’s current phase and anticipate turning points. Leading indicators — such as stock market indices, new orders for capital goods, building permits, and the yield curve slope — tend to move ahead of the overall economy. Coincident indicators — including industrial production, retail sales, and non‑farm payrolls — move roughly in step with the cycle. Lagging indicators — such as the unemployment rate, corporate profits, and the consumer price index for services — confirm trends after they have been established. In India, the Index of Industrial Production (IIP), the Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) for manufacturing and services, and the RBI’s composite leading indicator are widely tracked.

4.3 Inflation Dynamics Across the Cycle

Inflation typically behaves pro‑cyclically: it rises during expansions as demand pressures build and falls during contractions as slack emerges. However, the relationship is not mechanical. Supply shocks — such as a sudden spike in crude oil prices — can push inflation up even when the output gap is negative, creating a stagflationary environment. Conversely, credible inflation‑targeting regimes can decouple inflation from the cycle, keeping expectations anchored even when the output gap widens. 5. Contemporary Issues and Indian Context

5.1 Inflation Targeting Framework in India

India formally adopted a flexible inflation‑targeting framework in February 2015 through an amendment to the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934. The framework mandates the RBI to set the policy repo rate to achieve the inflation target of 4 % measured by the CPI‑Combined, with an upper tolerance of 6 % and a lower tolerance of 2 %. The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), comprising six members — three from the RBI and three external experts — meets at least four times a year to decide the policy stance. The framework has contributed to a notable decline in inflation volatility since its inception, although episodes such as the 2020‑21 pandemic‑induced supply disruptions and the 2022‑23 commodity price surge have tested its credibility.

5.2 Supply‑Side Shocks and Policy Responses

Supply‑side shocks, particularly in food and energy, pose a persistent challenge for Indian monetary policy because they affect the headline CPI directly and are less amenable to demand‑management tools. The RBI often relies on “look‑through” guidance, signalling that it will not react to transient supply‑driven spikes provided they do not feed into core inflation or inflation expectations. Fiscal measures — such as targeted subsidies, strategic reserves, and trade policy adjustments — complement monetary policy by addressing the root cause of the shock. The coordination between the Ministry of Finance and the RBI during the 2022‑23 global commodity surge illustrates the importance of a combined policy toolkit.

5.3 Global Linkages and Exchange Rate Pass‑Through

In an increasingly integrated world, domestic inflation is influenced by global commodity prices, foreign monetary policies, and exchange‑rate movements. A depreciation of the rupee raises the domestic currency price of imported goods, especially crude oil, fertilisers, and edible oils, feeding into both WPI and CPI. The pass‑through coefficient — the proportion of exchange‑rate change reflected in domestic prices — varies over time and across product categories; empirical studies for India estimate a pass‑through of roughly 0.2‑0.3 for the overall CPI but higher for fuel and edible oils. The RBI’s foreign‑exchange interventions and the maintenance of adequate reserves aim to limit excessive volatility, thereby containing imported inflation.

The interplay of inflation measurement, the Phillips curve, the output gap, and the business cycle forms the analytical backbone for understanding macroeconomic stability and for designing policy responses that balance growth and price stability.
