1. Trade Policy Framework

1.1 Evolution of Trade Policy

India’s trade policy has moved from a highly restrictive import‑substitution regime in the early decades after independence to a progressively liberalised framework that began in earnest with the 1991 economic reforms. The shift was driven by a balance‑of‑payments crisis that forced the government to seek International Monetary Fund assistance, which in turn required the dismantling of quantitative restrictions, reduction of tariff peaks, and the introduction of a market‑determined exchange rate. Subsequent policy documents such as the Foreign Trade Policy (FTP) 2004‑09, 2009‑14, 2015‑20 and the latest 2023‑28 have institutionalised a rule‑based, transparent system that aligns with World Trade Organization commitments while providing strategic space for sectors deemed critical for national security and employment.

The policy evolution reflects a deliberate sequencing: first, removal of licensing for most goods; second, rationalisation of tariff structures through the Harmonised System of Nomenclature; third, introduction of export promotion schemes such as the Merchandise Exports from India Scheme (MEIS) and the later Remission of Duties and Taxes on Exported Products (RoDTEP). Each step was calibrated to avoid sudden exposure of vulnerable domestic industries, and the government retained the ability to impose safeguard measures under the WTO Agreement on Safeguards when import surges threatened serious injury.

1.2 Instruments of Trade Policy

The principal instruments available to the government include tariffs, non‑tariff measures, export incentives, and trade facilitation measures. Tariffs remain the most visible tool; the applied Most‑Favoured‑Nation (MFN) rates have been progressively lowered, with the average bound tariff for industrial goods remains above 30 per cent, while agricultural tariffs remain higher to protect food security. Non‑tariff measures such as technical standards, sanitary and phytosanitary requirements, and compulsory certification are used to regulate quality and protect domestic consumers, but they also serve as de‑facto barriers when applied discriminately.

Export incentives have transitioned from direct subsidies, which are prohibited under the WTO Subsidies and Countervailing Measures Agreement, to duty remission schemes that neutralise the incidence of domestic taxes on exported goods. The RoDTEP scheme, for instance, refunds embedded central, state and local taxes that are not otherwise refunded, thereby improving the price competitiveness of Indian exports without violating multilateral rules. Trade facilitation measures, including the implementation of the Single Window Interface for Facilitating Trade (SWIFT) and the adoption of the Authorized Economic Operator programme, aim to reduce transaction costs and dwell time at ports, directly enhancing export efficiency.

1.3 Recent Reforms and Strategic Orientation

The 2023‑28 FTP introduces a “district as export hub” concept, encouraging each district to identify products with comparative advantage and build supporting infrastructure, skill development, and market linkages. This decentralised approach is complemented by the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes targeting sectors such as electronics, pharmaceuticals, and advanced chemistry cells, which aim to integrate India into global value chains. The policy also emphasises services exports, especially information technology, business process management, and healthcare, recognising that services now constitute a smaller share of India’s gross exports than merchandise.

Strategically, the government has pursued bilateral and regional trade agreements to secure market access, notably the Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement with the United Arab Emirates, the Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement with Australia, and ongoing negotiations with the United Kingdom and the European Union. These agreements incorporate provisions on rules of origin, services liberalisation, and digital trade, reflecting the evolving nature of international commerce. At the same time, India maintains a cautious stance on multilateral negotiations at the WTO, advocating for special and differential treatment for developing countries while resisting premature commitments on issues such as e‑commerce moratoriums and fisheries subsidies.

2. Balance of Payments Structure

2.1 Current Account Components

The current account records the flow of goods, services, primary income, and secondary income between residents and non‑residents. In India, the merchandise trade deficit has historically been the dominant component, driven by high imports of crude oil, gold, and electronic components. However, the services surplus, led by software exports, business services, and travel receipts, partially offsets the goods deficit. Primary income, which includes investment income such as interest and dividends, has remained a net outflow because India’s external liabilities exceed its foreign assets. Secondary income, dominated by private remittances from the large Indian diaspora, has consistently provided a sizeable net inflow, often exceeding the current account deficit in magnitude.

Recent data show that the current account deficit (CAD) narrowed to around 0.7 per cent of GDP in 2023‑24, reflecting a moderation in oil prices, robust services exports, and resilient remittance flows. The composition of the CAD matters for policy: a deficit financed by stable, non‑debt‑creating flows such as remittances and foreign direct investment (FDI) is less risky than one financed by volatile portfolio inflows or short‑term external commercial borrowings. Consequently, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) monitors the financing pattern closely, using the “net international investment position” as a gauge of external vulnerability.

2.2 Capital Account Dynamics

The capital account captures capital transfers and the acquisition or disposal of non‑produced, non‑financial assets, but the financially significant part is the financial account, which records foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, external commercial borrowings, trade credit, and reserve assets. FDI inflows have been relatively stable, supported by liberalised sectoral caps, the automatic route for most sectors, and the PLI schemes that incentivise manufacturing. Portfolio flows, comprising foreign portfolio investment (FPI) in equity and debt, are more sensitive to global risk appetite, US monetary policy, and domestic macroeconomic indicators.

External commercial borrowings (ECBs) provide an alternative source of long‑term funding for corporates, but the RBI imposes end‑use restrictions, maturity ceilings, and all‑in‑cost ceilings to prevent excessive leverage and currency mismatches. Trade credit, both buyer’s and supplier’s credit, finances import‑export transactions and is subject to prudential limits. The net outcome of these flows determines the change in foreign exchange reserves; a surplus in the financial account typically leads to reserve accumulation, while a deficit draws down reserves unless offset by valuation gains.

2.3 Errors and Omissions and Statistical Discrepancies

The “errors and omissions” (E&O) item balances the BoP identity, ensuring that the sum of the current account, capital account, and financial account equals the change in reserve assets. In practice, E&O captures unrecorded or mis‑reported transactions, timing differences, and valuation changes. Persistent large positive E&O may indicate under‑reporting of capital outflows, such as illicit financial flows or unreported trade credit, while large negative E&O could signal under‑recorded inflows. The RBI publishes quarterly BoP data with a lag, and analysts use the E&O trend as an early warning indicator of data quality and potential hidden vulnerabilities.

Statistical discrepancies also arise from the treatment of gold imports, which are recorded in the merchandise trade account but may be partially offset by changes in the monetary gold component of reserves. The RBI’s shift to the “import cover” metric, which expresses reserves in months of projected imports, helps contextualise the adequacy of reserves irrespective of composition. A rising import cover, combined with a declining E&O magnitude, generally signals improving external sector transparency and resilience.

Example Question
Assume India’s current account deficit is 1.5 per cent of GDP, net FDI inflows are 2.0 per cent of GDP, net portfolio outflows are 0.8 per cent of GDP, and net external commercial borrowings are 0.3 per cent of GDP. Calculate the change in foreign exchange reserves as a percentage of GDP, ignoring valuation effects and errors and omissions.
The balance of payments identity states that the current account balance plus the financial account balance equals the change in reserve assets (with opposite sign). The financial account balance is the sum of net FDI (+2.0), net portfolio (‑0.8), and net ECB (+0.3) = +1.5 per cent of GDP. The current account balance is ‑1.5 per cent of GDP. Adding them: (‑1.5) + (+1.5) = 0. Therefore, the change in foreign exchange reserves is zero per cent of GDP. This result indicates that the capital inflows exactly finance the current account deficit, leaving reserves unchanged.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a BoP numerical question appears, first write the identity: Current Account + Financial Account = –ΔReserves. Then list each component with its sign (inflows positive, outflows negative). Summation errors are the most common cause of lost marks, so double‑check the arithmetic before concluding.

3. Foreign Exchange Reserves Management

3.1 Composition and Adequacy

India’s foreign exchange reserves comprise foreign currency assets (FCA), gold, special drawing rights (SDRs), and the reserve position in the International Monetary Fund. As of March 2024, FCA accounted for roughly 90 per cent of total reserves, gold about 7 per cent, with SDRs and the IMF reserve position making up the remainder. The RBI follows a diversified investment strategy for FCA, placing funds in sovereign bonds of major reserve currencies, deposits with highly rated central banks, and short‑term money‑market instruments, while adhering to the principles of safety, liquidity, and return.

Adequacy is assessed through multiple metrics: import cover (reserves divided by average monthly imports), the ratio of reserves to short‑term external debt (the “Guidotti‑Greenspan” rule), and the reserve‑to‑broad‑money ratio. An import cover of 10‑12 months is generally considered comfortable for an emerging market with a managed float. The RBI’s internal benchmarks also incorporate stress‑testing scenarios that simulate sudden stops in capital flows, sharp currency depreciation, and commodity price shocks, ensuring that the reserve buffer can withstand tail‑risk events without resorting to disruptive policy measures.

3.2 Reserve Accumulation Strategies

Reserve accumulation in India has been driven largely by intervention in the foreign exchange market to prevent excessive rupee appreciation during periods of strong capital inflows. The RBI purchases dollars (or other reserve currencies) in the spot market, sterilising the resultant rupee liquidity through open market operations such as the issuance of government securities under the Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) or the conduct of variable rate reverse repos. Sterilisation prevents the monetary expansion that would otherwise fuel domestic inflation and asset bubbles.

In addition to intervention, the RBI encourages reserve‑building through policy measures that attract stable inflows: liberalising FDI limits, issuing sovereign bonds in foreign markets (the “Masala bonds” and later USD-denominated sovereign bonds), and facilitating external commercial borrowings with longer maturities. The central bank also participates in bilateral currency swap arrangements with major central banks, which provide contingent liquidity lines that enhance reserve adequacy without immediate balance‑sheet expansion.

3.3 Role of Sovereign Wealth Funds and Strategic Reserves

While India does not operate a classic sovereign wealth fund (SWF) like Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global, the National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF) and the India Infrastructure Finance Company Limited (IIFCL) perform quasi‑SWF functions by channeling domestic and foreign capital into long‑term infrastructure assets. These entities are funded partly by government contributions and partly by raising capital from global investors, thereby indirectly augmenting the country’s net foreign asset position.

Strategic reserves, particularly of crude oil and petroleum products, are managed by the Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Limited (ISPRL). These physical stockpiles provide a buffer against supply disruptions and reduce the need for large foreign currency outlays during price spikes. The coordination between financial reserves (held by the RBI) and strategic commodity reserves (held by ISPRL) exemplifies a holistic approach to external sector resilience, where financial and real buffers complement each other.

4. Exchange Rate Regimes and Policy

4.1 Fixed vs Floating Regimes

The choice of exchange rate regime reflects a trade‑off between monetary policy independence, exchange rate stability, and capital mobility, famously captured by the impossible trinity. A fixed regime, such as a currency board or a hard peg, sacrifices monetary autonomy to anchor inflation expectations and reduce transaction costs for trade and investment. However, it requires the central bank to hold substantial foreign reserves and to align domestic interest rates with those of the anchor currency, limiting the ability to respond to domestic shocks.

A freely floating regime, by contrast, grants full monetary policy autonomy and allows the exchange rate to act as a shock absorber. When a negative terms‑of‑trade shock hits, depreciation improves export competitiveness and dampens import demand, facilitating external adjustment without the need for painful internal devaluation. The downside is higher exchange rate volatility, which can increase hedging costs for firms and create uncertainty for cross‑border investment. Most emerging markets, including India, adopt an intermediate “managed float” that seeks to capture the benefits of flexibility while limiting excessive volatility.

4.2 Managed Float in India

India transitioned to a market‑determined exchange rate system in 1993 with the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS), moving to a unified exchange rate in 1994. Since then, the rupee has operated under a managed float: the RBI intervenes occasionally in the spot and forward markets to smooth excessive short‑term movements, but does not target a specific level or band. The intervention policy is guided by the “orderly market conditions” principle, aiming to prevent disorderly overshooting that could destabilise inflation expectations or corporate balance sheets.

The RBI’s toolkit includes spot purchases or sales of dollars, forward dollar‑rupee swaps, and the use of the Market Stabilisation Scheme to sterilise liquidity. The central bank also communicates its stance through the Monetary Policy Report and the Financial Stability Report, providing forward guidance that reduces speculative pressure. Empirical studies suggest that India’s intervention has been asymmetric – more frequent when the rupee appreciates sharply – reflecting a preference for avoiding loss of export competitiveness.

4.3 Intervention Mechanisms and Effectiveness

Effectiveness of intervention is debated. In the short run, sterilised intervention can influence the exchange rate by altering the relative supply of domestic and foreign assets, especially when capital mobility is imperfect. However, the “portfolio balance” channel weakens as financial markets deepen and investors can easily substitute between domestic and foreign assets. The RBI therefore complements intervention with macroprudential measures such as limits on unhedged foreign currency exposure for corporates and banks, and with capital flow management measures (CFMs) like the temporary increase in the withholding tax on interest payments to non‑residents during periods of excessive inflow.

Transparency has improved with the publication of daily foreign exchange intervention data (with a lag) and the adoption of the “Foreign Exchange Management Act” (FEMA) framework that codifies permissible transactions. The RBI’s shift to a flexible inflation targeting framework in 2016 further anchors expectations, reducing the need for exchange rate targeting as a nominal anchor. Overall, the managed float has delivered a relatively stable real effective exchange rate (REER) trajectory, supporting export growth while preserving monetary policy credibility.

5. Capital Account Management and Liberalisation

5.1 Evolution of Capital Controls

India’s capital account liberalisation has followed a sequenced, cautious approach, consistent with the “sequencing hypothesis” that recommends opening the current account first, then long‑term capital flows (FDI), followed by portfolio flows, and finally short‑term debt. In the 1990s, the government liberalised FDI in a phased manner, introducing the automatic route for most sectors and raising sectoral caps. The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA) was replaced by the more liberal Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) in 1999, shifting the regulatory philosophy from control to management.

Portfolio investment limits for foreign institutional investors (FIIs) were gradually raised, and the introduction of the Qualified Foreign Investor (QFI) scheme in 2011 allowed smaller investors to access Indian markets without full FII registration. The debt market saw the creation of the “Fully Accessible Route” (FAR) for specified government securities, enabling non‑residents to invest without investment limits. Short‑term external commercial borrowings remain subject to strict end‑use, maturity, and all‑in‑cost ceilings, reflecting the higher risk of sudden stops.

5.2 Foreign Direct Investment Policy

FDI policy is articulated through the Consolidated FDI Policy Circular, updated annually. The policy distinguishes between the automatic route (no prior government approval) and the government route (requiring approval from the concerned ministry). Sectors such as defence, telecom, media, and multi‑brand retail have caps and conditions, often linked to security considerations or domestic industry protection. The “Press Note 3” (2020) introduced a requirement for prior government approval for investments from countries sharing a land border with India, a measure aimed at safeguarding strategic sectors.

The PLI schemes have become a major driver of FDI in manufacturing, offering financial incentives linked to incremental production and sales. These schemes target sectors with high import dependence and export potential, such as mobile phones, pharmaceuticals, and specialty steel. The government also launched the “National Single Window System” (NSWS) to streamline approvals, reducing the time and cost of establishing foreign‑funded projects. The net effect has been a steady rise in FDI equity inflows, crossing the US$ 80 billion mark in 2022‑23.

5.3 Portfolio Flows, Volatility, and Macroprudential Safeguards

Portfolio flows, while providing depth to domestic capital markets, are prone to sharp reversals driven by global risk‑off episodes, US Federal Reserve policy shifts, or domestic political uncertainty. The RBI monitors the “net portfolio investment” series and employs macroprudential tools such as the “volatility cap” on foreign portfolio investment in government securities, which limits the share of a single security that can be held by non‑residents. Additionally, the “short‑term debt limit” for banks and corporates curtails excessive reliance on volatile external funding.

The introduction of the “Foreign Portfolio Investor (FPI) regime” in 2019 consolidated multiple investor categories into a single framework with simplified KYC, common application, and a unified investment limit of 24 per cent of the paid‑up capital of an Indian company (subject to sectoral caps). The regime also permits off‑shore derivative instruments (ODIs) under strict reporting requirements, balancing market access with transparency. During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the RBI temporarily relaxed certain limits and provided a US$ 2 billion dollar‑rupee swap window to ease dollar funding pressures, demonstrating the flexibility of the macroprudential toolkit.

The external sector framework described above equips policymakers with a coherent set of instruments to manage trade, balance‑of‑payments dynamics, reserve adequacy, exchange‑rate stability, and capital‑account openness in a mutually reinforcing manner.

Real Effective Exchange Rate and Terms of Trade Dynamics

The Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) is the trade‑weighted average of the nominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price levels, usually measured by consumer price indices. India’s REER is published monthly by the RBI and covers 36 partner currencies with weights derived from the 2015‑16 trade basket. A rising REER signals an appreciation in real terms, which tends to erode export competitiveness and improve import affordability, while a falling REER has the opposite effect. The Terms of Trade (ToT) index, computed as the ratio of export unit values to import unit values, moves broadly with the REER but also reflects commodity‑specific price shocks. For instance, the 2022‑23 surge in crude oil prices pushed India’s import unit values up sharply year‑on‑year, dragging the ToT down even as the REER remained relatively stable (base 2015‑16 = 100). RBI studies on exchange‑rate pass‑through suggest that a 1 % REER appreciation reduces export volumes by 0.3‑0.4 % within two quarters, whereas import volumes respond with a lag of three to four quarters. Policymakers therefore monitor the REER‑ToT gap: a persistent divergence (REER appreciating while ToT deteriorates) often precedes a current‑account widening, as seen in the 2010‑11 episode when the REER rose but the ToT fell due to rising gold and oil imports. The 2023‑24 Economic Survey noted that the REER had moderated by March 2024, aided by nominal depreciation against the US dollar and softer domestic inflation, helping narrow the trade deficit.

Transmission Channels

  • Price channel: REER changes alter relative prices of tradables, influencing firm‑level pricing decisions and export order books.
  • Income channel: Appreciation raises real incomes of import‑dependent households, boosting demand for non‑tradables and widening the non‑oil trade gap.
  • Balance‑sheet channel: Firms with foreign‑currency debt face higher rupee‑denominated servicing costs when the REER depreciates, affecting investment and credit quality.

Policy Implications

The RBI’s “managed float” framework allows intervention to smooth excessive REER volatility without targeting a specific level. The 2022‑23 intervention (net sales) was calibrated to prevent a disorderly REER overshoot during the Fed tightening cycle. The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) also factors REER movements into its inflation forecasts, given the estimated pass‑through to headline CPI per 1 % REER change. Analysts use the “REER‑ToT gap” as a leading indicator: a gap exceeding a threshold for consecutive quarters has historically preceded a current‑account deficit widening within the next six months.

External Debt Sustainability Metrics and Debt Service Ratios

India’s external debt stood at US$ 620 billion at end‑March 2024, representing 19.8 % of GDP, down from a peak of 23.5 % in March 2021, per official data. The debt composition is split between sovereign (central government) and non‑sovereign (corporate, banks, and NBFCs) borrowers. Sovereign external debt, largely in the form of market‑issued bonds and multilateral loans, accounted for about 38 % of the total, while commercial borrowings (ECBs) and short‑term trade credit made up the remainder. The key sustainability indicators tracked by the Ministry of Finance and the RBI are the debt‑to‑GDP ratio, the debt‑service‑to‑exports ratio, and the short‑term‑debt‑to‑reserves ratio. As of March 2024, the debt‑service‑to‑exports ratio was 7.2 %, well below the IMF’s prudential threshold, and the short‑term‑debt‑to‑reserves ratio stood at 0.18, comfortably under the 0.5 benchmark. The “original sin” problem—borrowing in foreign currency while earning in rupees—has been mitigated by the gradual shift toward rupee‑denominated offshore bonds (masala bonds) and the issuance of sovereign green bonds in 2023. The 2013 “taper tantrum” episode illustrated the vulnerability of high‑yield corporate ECBs: a sharp rise in US Treasury yields triggered a spike in Indian corporate bond spreads, prompting the RBI to activate the FCNR‑B swap window. The government’s “External Debt Management Strategy” (2022‑27) targets a gradual reduction of the debt‑to‑GDP ratio to 18 % by 2027, primarily through fiscal consolidation, longer‑maturity issuance, and diversification of the investor base.

Debt Service Profile

  • Principal repayments: Concentrated in 2025‑27 due to the maturity of 2015‑17 sovereign issuances.
  • Interest payments: Average coupon 3.8 % on sovereign bonds; corporate ECBs carry 4.5‑5.5 % all‑in cost.
  • Currency composition: 78 % USD, 12 % EUR, 5 % JPY, 5 % others.

Stress‑Testing Framework

The RBI conducts quarterly stress tests assuming a 300‑basis‑point rise in global rates, a 10 % rupee depreciation, and a 15 % decline in export earnings. Under the adverse scenario, the debt‑service‑to‑exports ratio and the short‑term‑debt‑to‑reserves ratio remain within comfort zones. The framework also incorporates contingent liabilities from state‑owned enterprises and off‑balance‑sheet guarantees, which added to the effective external debt stock in 2023.

Memorise the three IMF prudential thresholds (40 %, 15 %, 0.5) and practice quick mental arithmetic for ratio conversion. In the mains, a two‑line calculation followed by a one‑sentence assessment earns full marks; avoid lengthy derivations.

Trade Agreements, Rules of Origin, and Global Value Chain Integration

India’s trade agreement architecture has expanded from a handful of bilateral FTAs in the early 2000s to a network covering multiple comprehensive agreements and preferential arrangements as of 2024. The most consequential recent accords are the India‑UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA, 2022), the India‑Australia Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA, 2022), and the ongoing negotiations for the India‑UK FTA and the India‑EU Broad‑Based Trade and Investment Agreement (BTIA). A critical technical layer in each agreement is the Rules of Origin (RoO) regime, which determines whether a product qualifies for preferential tariffs. India follows the “wholly obtained” and “substantial transformation” criteria, typically requiring a minimum value addition (VA) in the exporting party, complemented by a change‑in‑tariff‑heading (CTH) rule for manufactured goods. The 2022 CEPA with UAE introduced a “product‑specific rule” (PSR) schedule covering most tariff lines, with VA thresholds varying by sector. Compliance costs are significant: a 2023 World Bank study estimated that Indian exporters incur an average share of export value in RoO documentation and verification, rising for SMEs in the electronics sector. The “cumulation” provision—allowing inputs from partner countries to count as originating—has been limited to bilateral cumulation in most Indian FTAs, unlike the diagonal cumulation available under the ASEAN‑India FTA (AIFTA) and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) framework, which India opted out of in 2019. The absence of diagonal cumulation restricts India’s ability to integrate into regional value chains, especially in electronics and auto parts where cross‑border production sharing is intensive. The 2024 Economic Survey highlighted that sectors with high foreign value‑added content face the steepest RoO barriers, limiting the export elasticity of these sectors to FTA tariff cuts. To address this, the Department of Commerce launched the “RoO Simplification Initiative” in 2023, introducing a digital certificate‑of‑origin platform (e‑CoO) and a “self‑certification” pilot for authorised exporters, reducing processing time significantly.

Global Value Chain (GVC) Positioning

  • Forward participation: India’s share of foreign value added in its exports rose from 18 % (2000) to 24 % (2022), driven by IT services and refined petroleum.
  • Backward participation: Domestic value added embedded in foreign exports increased from 12 % to 16 % over the same period, reflecting growing upstream supply of raw materials and intermediate goods.
  • Key GVC hubs: Gujarat (petrochemicals), Tamil Nadu (automotive), Karnataka (electronics assembly), and Maharashtra (pharma APIs).

Policy Levers

The Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes for mobile phones, IT hardware, and specialty steel are explicitly designed to raise domestic value addition above RoO thresholds, enabling Indian firms to claim originating status under FTAs. The 2023‑24 PLI disbursement is projected to lift the domestic VA share in mobile phone exports by 2026, unlocking preferential access under the India‑UAE and India‑Australia agreements.

Capital Flow Management Measures under the IMF Institutional View

The IMF’s Institutional View (IV) on capital flows, adopted in 2012 and refined in 2020, provides a framework for member countries to use Capital Flow Management Measures (CFMs) as part of a broader macroeconomic policy toolkit. India’s approach aligns with the IV by treating CFMs as temporary, targeted, and transparent instruments complementing monetary, fiscal, and exchange‑rate policies. The RBI’s macroprudential toolkit includes: (i) a volatility cap on foreign portfolio investment (FPI) in government securities, limiting non‑resident holdings of any single security; (ii) a short‑term debt limit for banks and corporates, capping external commercial borrowings (ECBs) with original maturity below three years; (iii) the “FPI‑ODI reporting regime” introduced in 2019, requiring offshore derivative instrument (ODI) positions to be disclosed weekly to the designated depository participant; and (iv) the “dollar‑rupee swap window” deployed during the COVID‑19 shock (March 2020) and again during the 2022 Fed tightening cycle (June 2022). The IV stresses that CFMs should not substitute for necessary macroeconomic adjustment; accordingly, the RBI’s 2023‑24 Monetary Policy Report noted that the volatility cap was relaxed after the Fed signaled a pause, illustrating the “temporary” nature of the measure. IMF empirical work on India’s CFMs found that the volatility cap reduced the sensitivity of portfolio inflows to US Treasury yield shocks, while the short‑term debt limit lowered the probability of a sudden stop in stress‑test simulations. The RBI publishes quarterly data detailing the stock of each measure, its utilisation rate, and the rationale for any modification, satisfying the IV’s transparency requirement. In 2024, the government announced a review of the ECB framework to allow “green ECBs” with longer maturity and concessional cost, aimed at financing climate‑resilient infrastructure without breaching the short‑term debt ceiling.

Interaction with Monetary Policy

  • Interest‑rate differential: CFMs reduce the need for aggressive rate hikes to defend the rupee, preserving domestic credit growth.
  • Reserve accumulation: By dampening volatile inflows, CFMs smooth the path of reserve accretion, avoiding sterilisation costs.
  • Financial stability: Limits on short‑term external debt curb currency‑mismatch risk in bank balance sheets.

Future Directions

The 2024‑25 Union Budget signaled a gradual liberalisation of the FPI volatility cap by March 2026, contingent on the establishment of a domestic “central counterparty” for government securities to enhance market depth. Simultaneously, the RBI is exploring a “dynamic CFM” framework where caps adjust automatically based on a composite indicator of global risk appetite, capital‑flow volatility, and domestic credit growth, moving closer to the IV’s recommendation of a rules‑based, state‑contingent approach.

Trade Remedies: Anti‑Dumping, Countervailing and Safeguard Measures

India’s trade‑remedy architecture operates under the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 (as amended) and the WTO agreements on Anti‑Dumping, Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (SCM), and Safeguards. The Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR), attached to the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, conducts investigations and recommends duties. An anti‑dumping duty is imposed when the normal value of a product in the exporting country exceeds its export price to India and the dumping causes material injury to the domestic industry. The normal value is usually the comparable price in the ordinary course of trade in the exporting country; if that is unavailable, a constructed value based on cost of production plus a reasonable profit margin is used. Countervailing duties (CVD) target specific subsidies contingent upon export performance or the use of domestic over imported goods. The SCM Agreement distinguishes prohibited subsidies (export subsidies, import‑substitution subsidies) from actionable subsidies (which cause adverse effects). Safeguard measures are temporary, non‑discriminatory restrictions (tariff‑rate quotas or tariff increases) applied when a surge in imports causes or threatens serious injury to a domestic industry, irrespective of unfair trade practice. India has been an active user of these instruments; in recent years the DGTR has initiated numerous anti‑dumping, CVD and safeguard cases, with the steel, chemicals and textiles sectors accounting for a large share. WTO dispute panels have examined several Indian measures, including a notable report on steel products that upheld certain anti‑dumping duties while recommending a review of injury‑margin calculations. Recent amendments to the Customs Tariff Act introduced a sunset‑review clause requiring duties to terminate after five years unless a review shows expiry would lead to continuation or recurrence of dumping and injury. The amendments also tightened de‑minimis thresholds for dumping and subsidy margins, aligning domestic law with WTO ministerial decisions. In practice, the DGTR’s final findings are subject to a public comment period before the Ministry of Finance issues a notification; the process from initiation to imposition typically spans 12‑18 months.

Services Trade, Mode 4 Mobility and the GATS Framework

The General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) classifies services supply into four modes: cross‑border supply (Mode 1), consumption abroad (Mode 2), commercial presence (Mode 3) and presence of natural persons (Mode 4). India’s offensive interests lie primarily in Mode 1 (IT‑enabled services, business process outsourcing) and Mode 4 (movement of skilled professionals such as software engineers, doctors and accountants). Various bilateral agreements and joint action plans contain specific Mode 4 commitments, including annual quotas for ICT professionals and fast‑track visa categories subject to labour‑market tests. The WTO’s Services Waiver for Least‑Developed Countries does not apply to India, but the Joint Statement Initiative on Services Domestic Regulation — joined by many members including India — aims to reduce regulatory barriers such as licensing, qualification recognition and visa procedures. Domestically, the Ministry of Commerce coordinates with the Ministry of Home Affairs and the Ministry of External Affairs to align visa policy with trade commitments. Recent visa liberalisation measures allowed multiple‑entry visas for up to five years for professionals in sectors covered by FTAs, reducing average processing times. However, the labour‑market‑test requirement — obliging employers to demonstrate that no suitable Indian candidate exists — remains a binding condition in most agreements, limiting practical utilisation of Mode 4 quotas. Official surveys have highlighted low fill‑rates for certain bilateral quotas, attributing shortfalls to recognition‑of‑qualifications delays and mandatory language‑proficiency tests. Ongoing FTA negotiations propose mutual recognition agreements for professional qualifications in accounting, architecture and engineering, which would be a first for India’s services schedule. India’s GATS MFN exemption list includes Mode 4 commitments under regional services agreements, allowing differential treatment for partner professionals. Upcoming WTO ministerial discussions are expected to address a services trade facilitation work programme that could further streamline Mode 4 mobility.

Worked Example: Calculating the Dumping Margin
A domestic producer of steel pipes alleges dumping by Country X. The normal value in Country X is USD 850 per tonne (ex‑factory). The export price to India, after adjusting for freight, insurance and commission, is USD 720 per tonne. The DGTR also finds a 5 % profit margin on cost of production in Country X. Compute the dumping margin as a percentage of the export price.
Dumping margin = (Normal Value – Export Price) ÷ Export Price × 100 = (850 – 720) ÷ 720 × 100 = 130 ÷ 720 × 100 ≈ 18.06 %. The 5 % profit margin is already embedded in the normal value; no further adjustment is required. Since the margin exceeds the de‑minimis threshold (currently 1 % post‑amendment), the investigation proceeds to injury determination.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the sequence of a trade‑remedy investigation, recall the statutory timeline: initiation → preliminary findings (within 60‑90 days) → provisional duty (if affirmative) → final findings → sunset review after five years. Memorise the de‑minimis thresholds (1 % dumping, 0.5 % subsidy) and the distinction between prohibited and actionable subsidies under the SCM Agreement; these are frequent MCQ pivots.

Digital Trade, Data Localization and Cross‑Border Data Flows

The rise of digital services has introduced a new regulatory frontier for the external sector. The WTO’s moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions has been renewed periodically; India has consistently supported its continuation, arguing that imposing duties would hinder the growth of its IT‑enabled services exports. However, domestic legislation increasingly conditions market access on data‑localisation requirements. The Reserve Bank of India’s circular on storage of payment‑system data mandates that all payment‑system operators store transaction data exclusively in India, affecting global card networks and fintech firms. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act introduces a “significant data fiduciary” classification and permits cross‑border transfers only to jurisdictions notified as providing adequate protection, or under approved standard contractual clauses. The Telecommunications Act empowers the government to direct licensees to localise critical telecom data for national security. These measures intersect with trade commitments: certain FTAs include free‑flow‑of‑information clauses, but India has invoked security exceptions to justify data‑localisation rules. Joint initiatives with major partners commit both sides to develop trusted data‑flow frameworks allowing certified entities to transfer personal data without localisation, mirroring adequacy‑decision processes. Recent policy forums have proposed data‑free‑flow‑with‑trust certification schemes, aligning with OECD recommendations on cross‑border data flows. From a balance‑of‑payments perspective, restrictive data policies can reduce Mode 1 exports by raising compliance costs for foreign clients; studies estimate a measurable contraction in IT‑services export growth if full localisation were enforced across all sectors. Conversely, adequacy mechanisms could unlock new markets for Indian cloud providers if major jurisdictions grant favourable decisions, potentially adding significantly to services exports over the medium term.

External Sector Stress Testing: Scenario Design and Policy Response

Stress testing of the external sector has become a core function of the Reserve Bank’s Financial Stability Unit and the Ministry of Finance’s macro‑economic framework. The framework follows the IMF’s External Sector Assessment methodology, combining a baseline macro‑projection with adverse scenarios calibrated to historical tail events. Three canonical scenarios are routinely modelled: (1) a sudden stop of portfolio inflows triggered by a sharp rise in US Treasury yields; (2) a commodity‑price shock — a significant drop in crude oil prices combined with a fall in metal prices — reflecting India’s net‑import status for energy and net‑export status for refined petroleum products; (3) a geopolitical disruption — a prolonged closure of a critical shipping chokepoint — raising freight rates sharply and cutting remittance inflows from the Gulf. The stress‑test engine integrates the current‑account financing gap, the short‑term external debt to reserves ratio, and the import‑cover metric. Recent stress‑test reports showed that under the first scenario, the short‑term‑debt‑to‑reserves ratio would breach prudential thresholds within two quarters, prompting pre‑emptive activation of dollar‑rupee swap windows and temporary adjustments to FPI volatility caps. Under the second scenario, the current‑account deficit widens but terms‑of‑trade gains from cheaper oil partially offset financing needs; the model recommends a calibrated drawdown of reserves rather than policy‑rate hikes. The third scenario produces a simultaneous current‑account and capital‑account shock, pushing import cover below comfort levels; the prescribed response combines a policy‑rate increase, temporary relaxation of external‑commercial‑borrowing maturity restrictions for infrastructure, and coordinated fiscal stimulus targeting export‑oriented sectors. The RBI publishes key assumptions, shock magnitudes and policy‑reaction functions in its half‑yearly Financial Stability Report. Recent legislative amendments formalised the external‑sector stress‑testing framework as a statutory obligation, requiring results to be presented to the Central Board within a defined period after each half‑year end.

Sovereign Credit Ratings, External Debt and Market Access

India’s sovereign credit ratings sit at the lowest investment‑grade notch, making the external sector highly sensitive to rating actions. Rating agencies weight the external‑sector assessment substantially in their sovereign scorecards, focusing on the current‑account balance, external‑debt‑to‑GDP, reserve adequacy (import cover, reserve‑to‑short‑term‑debt), and the composition of capital flows (FDI vs. portfolio). Recent methodology updates introduced a foreign‑currency liquidity sub‑factor that explicitly incorporates the RBI’s swap‑line network. Updated sovereign criteria added a contingent‑liability metric capturing off‑budget borrowings of state‑owned enterprises guaranteed by the central government; guaranteed debt at a notable share of GDP could trigger a negative outlook if it crosses a threshold. External debt sustainability analysis uses debt‑service‑to‑exports and debt‑service‑to‑revenue ratios, both currently within comfort zones. However, the currency composition of external debt — predominantly USD with significant EUR, JPY and other exposures — exposes the sovereign to valuation effects; a meaningful rupee depreciation against the dollar raises the rupee‑value of external debt substantially, widening the fiscal deficit. Market access is reflected in the sovereign spread on USD bonds, which has narrowed following reserve accumulation and fiscal‑consolidation commitments. Recent budget announcements included green sovereign bond issuance earmarked for climate‑resilient infrastructure, viewed positively for long‑term debt sustainability. A downgrade to sub‑investment grade would raise borrowing costs, increase the cost of external commercial borrowings for corporates, and potentially trigger capital‑outflow clauses in several bilateral investment treaties.

  • Assuming Mode 4 commitments automatically grant visa‑free entry; most FTAs retain labour‑market tests and qualification‑recognition hurdles.
  • Treating data‑localisation rules as pure domestic regulation — they can constitute a market‑access barrier under GATS Article XVI and trigger dispute settlement.
  • Using only the import‑cover ratio to judge reserve adequacy; the short‑term‑debt‑to‑reserves and currency‑composition metrics are equally critical.
  • Believing a sovereign rating upgrade instantly lowers external borrowing costs — market spreads also reflect global risk appetite and liquidity conditions.
  • Overlooking the “sunset review” requirement for trade‑remedy duties; duties lapse after five years unless a review proves continued injury.
  • Ignoring the distinction between prohibited and actionable subsidies under the SCM Agreement — only prohibited subsidies are per se illegal.

Exchange Rate Pass‑Through and Inflation Dynamics

The degree to which movements in the nominal exchange rate translate into domestic price changes — known as exchange‑rate pass‑through (ERPT) — is a core determinant of monetary policy effectiveness in an open economy. In India, empirical work by the RBI and academic researchers consistently shows a declining long‑run pass‑through coefficient, from roughly 0.6 in the early 1990s to below 0.2 after the adoption of flexible inflation targeting in 2016. The decline reflects three structural shifts: (i) a larger share of imported intermediate goods priced in domestic currency through invoicing practices, (ii) deeper domestic financial markets that allow firms to hedge currency risk, and (iii) the anchoring of inflation expectations by the Monetary Policy Framework Agreement (MPFA) of 2015.

Short‑run pass‑through remains higher for energy and food items because these categories are heavily import‑dependent and have limited domestic substitutes. A 10 per cent depreciation of the rupee typically raises the wholesale price index (WPI) for fuel by 3–4 per cent within one quarter, while the consumer price index (CPI) for food shows a lagged response of 1.5–2 per cent over two quarters. The RBI’s quarterly “Monetary Policy Report” decomposes the contribution of exchange‑rate movements to headline inflation using a structural vector autoregression (SVAR) that identifies supply‑side shocks, demand‑side shocks, and exchange‑rate shocks separately.

Policy implications are asymmetric. When depreciation is driven by a terms‑of‑trade shock (e.g., a surge in crude oil prices), the RBI tolerates a temporary rise in inflation because the shock is largely supply‑side and monetary tightening would exacerbate output loss. Conversely, when depreciation stems from capital‑outflow pressures, the central bank may raise the policy repo rate to curb imported inflation and stabilise the currency, as observed during the 2013 “taper tantrum” and the 2020 COVID‑induced portfolio reversal. The MPFA mandates that the RBI explain any deviation of inflation from the 4 per cent target (±2 per cent band) in its bi‑monthly resolution, explicitly referencing the exchange‑rate channel.

Measurement issues persist. The choice of price index (CPI‑Combined vs. CPI‑Industrial Workers), the treatment of administered prices (petrol, diesel, electricity), and the lag structure all affect estimated coefficients. The RBI now publishes a “pass‑through dashboard” that updates rolling‑window estimates monthly, allowing analysts to track regime changes in real time.

Current Account Adjustment: Expenditure Switching versus Expenditure Reducing

A current‑account deficit (CAD) can be corrected through two broad mechanisms: expenditure switching (changing the composition of demand between domestic and foreign goods) and expenditure reducing (lowering aggregate absorption). In the Indian context, the 1991 balance‑of‑payments crisis illustrated a classic expenditure‑reducing adjustment — a sharp fiscal contraction, import compression via licensing, and a 19 per cent devaluation that simultaneously switched expenditure. Post‑1991 reforms shifted the policy mix toward expenditure switching by liberalising trade, moving to a market‑determined exchange rate, and allowing the rupee to act as a shock absorber.

The expenditure‑switching channel operates primarily through the real effective exchange rate (REER). A depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, improving the trade balance provided the Marshall‑Lerner condition holds (sum of export and import elasticities > 1). Empirical estimates for India place the long‑run export elasticity at 0.9 and import elasticity at 0.7, satisfying the condition. However, the short‑run elasticities are lower (0.3 and 0.2 respectively), implying a J‑curve effect: the CAD initially widens after depreciation before improving after 4–6 quarters.

Expenditure‑reducing policies — fiscal consolidation, monetary tightening, or structural reforms that raise savings — lower domestic absorption (C + I + G) and thus reduce import demand. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 (amended 2018) institutionalised a medium‑term fiscal deficit target of 3 per cent of GDP, directly supporting expenditure reduction. The 2020‑21 pandemic response temporarily suspended the FRBM escape clause, leading to a fiscal deficit of 9.5 per cent of GDP and a CAD of 1.9 per cent of GDP, financed largely by capital inflows and reserve drawdown.

The interaction between the two channels is critical. A depreciation that improves competitiveness can be offset if the resulting higher import prices raise inflation, prompting monetary tightening that depresses domestic demand — an expenditure‑reducing side effect. The RBI’s “flexible inflation targeting” framework explicitly models this trade‑off: the policy reaction function includes the output gap, inflation gap, and the REER gap. During 2018‑19, a modest rupee depreciation (≈5 per cent) combined with a 25‑basis‑point repo‑rate hike achieved a 0.4 percentage‑point CAD reduction without a significant output loss, illustrating a calibrated mix.

Structural factors — labour‑market rigidity, infrastructure bottlenecks, and the share of non‑tradable services — limit the potency of expenditure switching. The Economic Survey 2022‑23 highlighted that a 1 per cent REER depreciation raises manufacturing export volumes by only 0.3 per cent, whereas the same depreciation boosts services exports (IT, business process outsourcing) by 0.7 per cent because pricing is often in foreign currency. Consequently, policy emphasis has shifted toward improving supply‑side competitiveness (production‑linked incentive schemes, logistics cost reduction) rather than relying solely on exchange‑rate movements.

Examiner's technique: Identifying the adjustment channel in a scenario
A question describes a country with a persistent CAD of 3 per cent of GDP, a floating exchange rate, and an inflation target of 4 per cent. The central bank raises the policy rate by 50 basis points while the currency depreciates 6 per cent over the next year. The CAD narrows to 2 per cent. Which channel — expenditure switching or expenditure reducing — played the dominant role? Justify using elasticities and the policy reaction function.
The dominant channel is expenditure switching. The depreciation of 6 per cent, given long‑run export elasticity ≈0.9 and import elasticity ≈0.7, implies a trade‑balance improvement of roughly (0.9 + 0.7) × 6 ≈ 9.6 per cent of the trade deficit, sufficient to cut the CAD by 1 percentage point. The 50‑basis‑point rate hike would reduce domestic absorption modestly (estimated interest‑elasticity of investment ≈ ‑0.2), contributing at most 0.2 percentage points. The policy reaction function under flexible inflation targeting assigns a higher weight to the REER gap than to the output gap when inflation is near target, confirming that the central bank relied on the exchange‑rate channel. Therefore, the observed CAD reduction is primarily expenditure switching.
Exam‑day strategy
When a case study mixes exchange‑rate moves and interest‑rate changes, first compute the implied trade‑balance effect using the Marshall‑Lerner condition (export + import elasticities). Then estimate the demand‑compression effect from the rate hike using the investment‑interest elasticity. Compare magnitudes; the larger one signals the dominant adjustment channel. Always cite the relevant elasticities (RBI’s latest Monetary Policy Report) and the policy reaction function specification.

Foreign Exchange Intervention Sterilisation and Monetary Policy Independence

Sterilised foreign‑exchange intervention (SFXI) refers to the central bank’s purchase or sale of foreign currency accompanied by an offsetting domestic‑currency operation (open‑market operations, repo/reverse repo, or cash‑reserve‑ratio adjustments) that leaves the monetary base unchanged. The RBI has employed SFXI extensively since the 1993‑94 shift to a market‑determined exchange rate, notably during the 2008‑09 global financial crisis (USD ≈ 30 billion purchased, sterilised via Market Stabilisation Scheme bonds) and the 2020‑21 pandemic surge (USD ≈ 100 billion accumulated, sterilised through variable‑rate reverse repos and the Standing Deposit Facility introduced in April 2022).

The effectiveness of sterilisation hinges on the interest‑rate differential between domestic and foreign assets, the depth of the domestic money market, and the credibility of the inflation‑targeting framework. When domestic interest rates exceed foreign rates (as in India’s positive carry environment), sterilisation is costly: the RBI issues short‑term government securities or conducts reverse repos at the repo rate, paying a higher yield than the return on foreign‑currency assets (typically US Treasury yields). The quasi‑fiscal cost averaged 0.15 per cent of GDP during 2015‑2020, rising to 0.25 per cent in 2021‑22 as the repo rate was cut to 4 per cent while US rates remained near zero.

Empirical studies (RBI Working Paper 2021‑03; BIS Papers No 115) find that sterilised intervention in India has a statistically significant but modest impact on the rupee — about 0.3‑0.5 per cent depreciation per USD 10 billion of net purchases — and does not materially affect domestic short‑term rates because the offsetting operations are fully absorbed by the banking system’s excess liquidity. However, persistent large‑scale sterilisation can signal a “fear of floating” and undermine the credibility of the flexible exchange‑rate regime, prompting the IMF’s 2022 Article IV consultation to recommend greater exchange‑rate flexibility and a gradual reduction of the intervention‑to‑reserves ratio.

The introduction of the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) at 3.75 per cent (25 bps below the repo rate) in 2022 provided a lower‑cost sterilisation tool, reducing the quasi‑fiscal burden. The RBI’s “Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) 1999” amendments in 2023 clarified that sterilisation operations are monetary‑policy actions, not capital‑account measures, reinforcing the legal separation between exchange‑rate management and capital‑flow management.

Monetary policy independence is preserved as long as the central bank can set the policy rate without being constrained by the need to maintain a specific exchange‑rate level. The MPFA (2015) and the subsequent inflation‑targeting framework institutionalised this independence. Nevertheless, when intervention volumes exceed 5 per cent of GDP in a quarter, the RBI’s balance‑sheet expansion can transmit to broad money growth, complicating inflation forecasting. The 2023‑24 Monetary Policy Report noted that the M3 growth rate remained within the 10‑12 per cent corridor despite record reserve accumulation, indicating effective sterilisation.

Geopolitical Sanctions, Trade Diversion and Capital Flow Re‑routing

Geopolitical sanctions — such as the 2014‑2022 restrictions on Russia, the 2018‑2021 US‑China trade tensions, and the 2022‑23 measures against Iran — create abrupt shifts in trade and capital‑flow patterns that test the resilience of an emerging‑market external sector. India’s response illustrates three mechanisms: (i) trade diversion through alternative sourcing, (ii) payment‑system workarounds, and (iii) capital‑flow re‑routing via third‑country financial centres.

Following the 2022 sanctions on Russian energy, India increased crude imports from Russia from 2 per cent of total oil imports (FY 2021‑22) to 22 per cent (FY 2023‑24), settling payments in a mix of rupee‑rupee Vostro accounts, UAE dirham, and Chinese yuan. The RBI authorised special Vostro accounts for designated Russian banks under FEMA Notification No. FEMA.2022‑RBI‑03, allowing rupee‑denominated settlement without accessing the SWIFT network. This arrangement preserved foreign‑exchange reserves (saving an estimated USD 7 billion in FY 2023‑24) but introduced currency‑mismatch risk on the rupee‑rupee leg, monitored through the RBI’s “Currency Composition of Reserves” dashboard.

Trade diversion also appeared in the electronics and pharmaceutical sectors. The Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes for mobile‑phone manufacturing (2020) and active‑pharmaceutical‑ingredients (2021) attracted investment redirected from China, raising India’s share of global smartphone exports from 3 per cent (2020) to 9 per cent (2023). The WTO’s Trade Policy Review 2023 noted that India’s average applied MFN tariff on electronic components fell from 10 per cent to 0 per cent under the Information Technology Agreement (ITA) expansion, facilitating the diversion.

Capital‑flow re‑routing emerged as portfolio investors shifted allocations from sanctioned jurisdictions to India via Mauritius, Singapore, and the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) at GIFT City. The RBI’s “Foreign Portfolio Investor (FPI) Regulations, 2019” were amended in 2022 to allow “grandfathered” Mauritius‑route investments to retain treaty benefits until 2025, while new investments must comply with the India‑Mauritius DTAA’s Limitation‑of‑Benefits clause. The IFSC’s tax‑neutral regime (zero capital‑gains tax on listed securities) attracted USD 12 billion of net FPI inflows in FY 2023‑24, partially offsetting outflows from other emerging markets.

These adaptations have macro‑prudential implications. The RBI’s “Capital Flow Management Measures (CFMs) under the IMF Institutional View” framework classifies the Vostro‑account mechanism as a targeted, temporary CFM consistent with the “macro‑prudential” pillar, provided it is phased out once normal payment channels resume. The 2024 Financial Stability Report stressed that prolonged reliance on alternative payment currencies could fragment the international monetary system and increase the cost of reserve management.

Reserve Currency Diversification, Gold Accumulation and the Role of SDRs

India’s foreign‑exchange reserves, which stood at USD 642 billion (end‑March 2024), are predominantly held in US dollars (≈ 55 per cent), euros (≈ 20 per cent), Japanese yen (≈ 5 per cent), and pound sterling (≈ 4 per cent). Since 2018 the RBI has pursued a deliberate diversification strategy: (i) incremental gold purchases, (ii) allocation to Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), and (iii) selective acquisition of non‑traditional reserve currencies such as the Australian dollar and Canadian dollar.

Gold holdings rose from 558 tonnes (March 2018) to 822 tonnes (March 2024), raising the gold share of reserves from 5.5 per cent to 8.3 per cent. Purchases are conducted through the “Gold Monetisation Scheme” and direct market operations, with the RBI acting as a price‑taker on the London Bullion Market Association (LBMA) platform. The 2022‑23 Annual Report highlighted that gold’s negative correlation with the dollar (≈ ‑0.4 over the past decade) improves the risk‑adjusted return of the reserve portfolio, especially during periods of dollar strength.

SDR allocations, governed by the IMF’s Articles of Agreement, provide an unconditional, interest‑bearing reserve asset. India’s cumulative SDR holdings increased from SDR 3.2 billion (2016) to SDR 13.5 billion (2024) following the 2021 general allocation (SDR 456 billion globally) and the 2023 special allocation for low‑income countries (which India voluntarily contributed to). The SDR interest rate (SDRi) is a weighted average of short‑term rates in the five basket currencies; in 2023‑24 it averaged 3.2 per cent, higher than the yield on US Treasury bills, enhancing reserve income.

Diversification also serves a strategic purpose. The RBI’s “Reserve Management Guidelines” (revised 2022) set a maximum single‑currency exposure of 60 per cent and a minimum gold share of 5 per cent. The guidelines require quarterly stress‑testing of the reserve portfolio against a 20 per cent dollar appreciation scenario, a 30 per cent equity‑market drawdown, and a sovereign‑default shock on a major reserve‑currency issuer. Results are reported to the Central Board and inform the annual “Reserve Adequacy Assessment” presented to Parliament.

Operationally, the RBI uses a “liquidity‑first” hierarchy: (1) cash and short‑term sovereign securities in the top‑four reserve currencies, (2) gold held in allocated accounts at the Bank of England and the Bank for International Settlements, (3) SDRs held in the IMF’s SDR Department, and (4) a small tactical allocation to high‑quality corporate bonds in non‑reserve currencies (≤ 1 per cent of total reserves). This structure ensures that the RBI can meet any sudden balance‑of‑payments financing need — such as the 2020‑21 COVID‑related drawdown of USD 30 billion — without disrupting market functioning.

  • Confusing the short‑run J‑curve effect with a permanent improvement in the trade balance after depreciation.
  • Assuming sterilised intervention has no fiscal cost; the quasi‑fiscal loss from interest‑rate differentials can be material.
  • Treating Vostro‑account rupee settlements as a permanent substitute for convertible‑currency payments; they are temporary CFMs under IMF guidance.
  • Overstating the impact of gold purchases on reserve adequacy without adjusting for valuation volatility and storage costs.
  • Neglecting the SDR interest‑rate mechanism and assuming SDRs are a static, non‑yielding asset.
  • Believing that a higher REER automatically improves the current account; the Marshall‑Lerner condition must hold and supply‑side constraints matter.
  • Ignoring the legal distinction between FEMA‑authorised payment mechanisms and capital‑account liberalisation measures when analysing sanctions‑driven flow re‑routing.

Currency Composition of Trade Invoicing and Its Implications for Exchange Rate Pass‑Through

The currency in which cross‑border transactions are invoiced shapes how exchange‑rate movements transmit to domestic prices and trade volumes. Globally, the US dollar dominates invoicing: studies estimate roughly 80 per cent of world exports and 70 per cent of imports are denominated in dollars, a pattern entrenched since the Bretton Woods era. In India, the share is higher still — around 86 per cent of merchandise imports and 78 per cent of merchandise exports are invoiced in dollars, per the RBI’s 2023 Survey on Invoicing Currency. This heavy dollar invoicing means a rupee depreciation against the dollar raises the rupee cost of imported inputs nearly one‑for‑one, while export revenues in rupee terms rise only if foreign demand is price‑elastic. Research estimates India’s short‑run import‑price pass‑through coefficient at 0.6‑0.7, reflecting both invoicing structure and pricing‑to‑market by foreign exporters. Export‑price pass‑through is lower, near 0.3, because many Indian exporters price in dollars and absorb rupee appreciation to retain market share. The asymmetry creates a “currency‑invoicing channel” that amplifies the impact of dollar fluctuations on the trade balance and domestic inflation — a mechanism standard Mundell‑Fleming models often understate.

Policy responses have focused on diversifying invoicing currencies. The RBI’s 2022 “Framework for Promoting Use of Local Currencies in Cross‑Border Transactions” encourages rupee invoicing for trade with partners that have bilateral swap lines (e.g., UAE, Sri Lanka, Mauritius). The 2023 India‑UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement includes a clause to settle a portion of bilateral trade in rupees and dirhams. Early data from the rupee‑dirham mechanism suggest a modest shift: rupee‑denominated exports to the UAE rose from about 2 per cent to 5 per cent of total bilateral exports in the first year. However, network effects and the depth of dollar‑denominated trade‑finance markets limit the speed of substitution. Recent firm‑level analysis using customs data (2021‑2024) indicates that firms with existing dollar‑denominated credit lines are significantly less likely to switch invoicing currency, even when offered a discount on trade‑finance costs. This suggests any policy aiming to reduce dollar invoicing must address complementary financial infrastructure — hedging products, correspondent banking relationships, and benchmark interest rates — in the alternative currency.

Offshore Rupee Markets, Non‑Deliverable Forwards and the Limits of Capital Controls

The offshore rupee market, centred in financial hubs such as Singapore, Dubai, and London, operates through non‑deliverable forwards (NDFs) and offshore rupee deposits. NDFs are cash‑settled contracts that allow participants to hedge or speculate on the INR/USD rate without delivering rupees, thereby circumventing onshore capital‑account restrictions. Daily turnover in the offshore NDF market is estimated at around USD 30‑35 billion in 2023, roughly twice the onshore forward market volume reported by the RBI. This offshore depth creates a price‑discovery venue that often leads the onshore spot rate by a few basis points, especially during periods of heightened global risk aversion. The RBI monitors the NDF‑onshore basis as an early indicator of capital‑flow pressure; a widening basis (offshore forward premium exceeding onshore) signals that non‑residents are demanding a higher premium to hold rupee exposure, often preceding portfolio outflows.

India’s capital‑control architecture — primarily the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) and the RBI’s master directions on external commercial borrowings (ECBs) — limits resident participation in offshore derivatives. Residents may only hedge bona‑fide trade exposures through authorised dealer banks, and speculative positions in NDFs are prohibited. Nevertheless, the offshore market remains accessible to non‑residents, foreign portfolio investors, and multinational corporates with Indian operations. The 2020‑21 COVID‑19 shock illustrated the limits of these controls: as global dollar funding dried up, the offshore NDF premium rose sharply to around 4‑5 per cent annualised, while the onshore forward premium stayed near 2 per cent. The RBI responded by expanding the onshore forward window, allowing banks to offer longer‑tenor forwards up to one year, and by conducting USD/INR swap auctions to inject dollar liquidity. These measures narrowed the basis but did not eliminate it, underscoring that as long as a sizable offshore market exists, the rupee’s external value is partially determined beyond the direct reach of domestic regulation. The IMF’s 2023 Article IV consultation observed that the offshore NDF market acts as a “pressure valve” but also transmits global financial cycles into domestic monetary conditions, complicating the RBI’s inflation‑targeting framework.

Worked Example: Interpreting the NDF‑Onshore Basis
A candidate is asked: “In April 2024 the 3‑month offshore NDF implied USD/INR forward rate is 83.50, while the onshore 3‑month forward rate quoted by authorised dealers is 83.20. The spot rate is 83.00. Explain what the basis indicates about market expectations and possible RBI policy response.”
The offshore forward premium is (83.50‑83.00)/83.00 × (12/3) ≈ 2.4 per cent annualised; the onshore premium is (83.20‑83.00)/83.00 × (12/3) ≈ 0.96 per cent. The positive basis of roughly 1.4 percentage points shows non‑residents demand a higher rupee‑depreciation compensation than onshore participants, signalling anticipated capital outflows or heightened risk aversion. The RBI would typically (i) conduct USD/INR sell‑buy swaps to supply dollars and compress the offshore premium, (ii) allow banks to extend onshore forward tenors to improve hedging capacity, and (iii) communicate readiness to intervene in the spot market if the rupee depreciates beyond a comfort band. The answer should link the basis to the interest‑rate differential, capital‑flow outlook, and the RBI’s toolkit.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question cites NDF and onshore forward rates, compute both annualised premiums quickly; the difference is the basis. Remember that a widening basis precedes portfolio outflows and often triggers RBI swap auctions. Quote the 2020‑21 episode and the IMF 2023 observation to demonstrate current‑affairs awareness.

Balance of Payments Crisis Early‑Warning Indicators and the IMF’s Reserve Adequacy Metric

Policymakers and analysts rely on a set of quantitative early‑warning indicators (EWIs) to assess the probability of a balance‑of‑payments (BoP) crisis. The IMF’s Vulnerability Exercise for Emerging Markets tracks variables such as the current‑account deficit (CAD) as a share of GDP, short‑term external debt to reserves, the ratio of gross external financing needs to reserves, and the real effective exchange rate (REER) deviation from its long‑run average. For India, the CAD widened to around 2.1 per cent of GDP in FY 2022‑23 before narrowing to about 1.2 per cent in FY 2023‑24, staying below the 3 per cent level the IMF often flags as elevated risk. Short‑term external debt (original maturity ≤ 1 year) stood at approximately USD 85 billion at end‑March 2024, while foreign‑exchange reserves were USD 642 billion, giving a short‑term‑debt‑to‑reserves ratio of 0.13 — well under the 1.0 benchmark. Gross external financing needs (current‑account deficit plus amortisation of medium‑ and long‑term debt) amounted to roughly USD 120 billion, yielding a financing‑needs‑to‑reserves ratio of 0.19, again comfortably below the 0.5‑0.6 warning zone.

The IMF’s Assessing Reserve Adequacy (ARA) metric combines these variables into a single composite score. The ARA weight for emerging markets with flexible exchange rates is 10 per cent of GDP, 30 per cent of broad money, 20 per cent of short‑term debt, and 40 per cent of other portfolio liabilities. Applying 2024 data, India’s ARA‑implied adequate reserve level is estimated at approximately USD 530 billion; actual reserves of USD 642 billion imply a coverage ratio of 1.21, indicating a comfortable buffer. However, the ARA framework assumes normal market access. During the 2020‑21 pandemic shock, India’s reserves fell to around USD 580 billion while financing needs surged, temporarily pushing the coverage ratio below 1.0. The RBI’s pre‑emptive swap lines with the Federal Reserve (USD 60 billion) and the Bank of Japan (USD 75 billion) were activated, illustrating how contingent liquidity arrangements complement static reserve adequacy measures. Analysts should therefore monitor both the level of reserves relative to the ARA benchmark and the availability of swap facilities, especially when global risk appetite deteriorates.

Sectoral Export Elasticities and the Role of Intermediate Goods in GVC Upgrading

Understanding how export volumes respond to exchange‑rate changes requires sector‑specific elasticity estimates, because aggregate elasticities mask wide heterogeneity. An RBI study (2022) using firm‑level data (2005‑2020) suggested short‑run price elasticities of export volume: engineering goods ‑0.45, pharmaceuticals ‑0.30, textiles ‑0.55, and electronics ‑0.70. The higher elasticity for electronics reflects its deep integration into global value chains (GVCs) where final assembly is highly sensitive to cost differentials. Importantly, a large share of India’s electronics exports consists of imported components (semiconductors, displays) that are re‑exported after minimal value addition. The imported‑content share in electronics exports exceeds 70 per cent, implying that a rupee depreciation raises input costs almost as much as it boosts revenue, dampening the net volume response. By contrast, pharmaceuticals have a lower imported‑content share (≈ 30 per cent) and benefit from strong non‑price competitiveness (regulatory approvals, brand loyalty), resulting in a more muted elasticity.

Policy efforts to upgrade GVC positioning — such as the Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes for semiconductors, telecom equipment, and white goods — aim to raise domestic value addition and reduce imported‑content intensity. Early outcomes from the PLI for mobile phones (launched 2020) show domestic value addition reportedly rising from 15 per cent to 22 per cent by FY 2023‑24, while export volumes grew at a compound annual rate of 28 per cent. However, the elasticity of these higher‑value‑added exports to the exchange rate remains elevated because final assembly still relies on imported chips. A sustained reduction in exchange‑rate sensitivity will require deeper backward linkages — domestic wafer fabrication, display manufacturing, and component ecosystems — which are long‑gestation investments. The 2024 Economic Survey suggests that moving from “assembly‑only” to “design‑and‑manufacture” could lower the electronics export elasticity to below ‑0.4, aligning it with the engineering‑goods benchmark and improving the stability of export earnings under exchange‑rate volatility.

Macro‑Prudential Regulation of External Commercial Borrowings and the Role of the ECB Framework

External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) constitute a major channel for Indian corporates to access foreign currency funding. The RBI’s ECB framework, last comprehensively revised in 2018 and fine‑tuned in 2021, classifies borrowings by maturity, end‑use, and all‑in‑cost ceiling. Minimum average maturity is three years for most categories, with a five‑year floor for infrastructure and green projects. The all‑in‑cost ceiling is benchmarked to the 6‑month SOFR (or its predecessor LIBOR) plus a spread of 450 basis points for corporates and 350 basis points for eligible infrastructure borrowers. End‑use restrictions prohibit deployment for real‑estate, capital‑market speculation, and working‑capital purposes (except for specific COVID‑19 relief windows). As of March 2024, outstanding ECBs stood at around USD 210 billion, with the corporate sector accounting for roughly 68 per cent and the remainder split between NBFCs and infrastructure SPVs.

Macro‑prudential safeguards include a prudential limit on the ratio of ECB‑denominated debt to a borrower’s net worth (generally 4:1) and a requirement that at least 70 per cent of ECB proceeds be hedged for currency risk if the borrowing exceeds USD 20 million. The RBI also monitors the aggregate ECB‑to‑GDP ratio; it remained moderate in FY 2023‑24, well below the threshold that would trigger tighter oversight. During the 2022‑23 tightening cycle, the RBI raised the all‑in‑cost ceiling by 50 basis points and shortened the minimum maturity for green bonds to three years to encourage climate‑finance inflows. These adjustments illustrate the framework’s flexibility: it can be calibrated to attract specific flow types while preserving overall external‑debt sustainability. The 2023 Financial Stability Report highlighted that the hedging compliance rate among large corporates improved from 62 per cent (2019) to 84 per cent (2023), reducing unhedged currency exposure that could amplify balance‑sheet stress during rupee depreciation episodes.

  • Treating the IMF’s ARA metric as a hard ceiling; it is a guideline that must be read alongside contingent swap lines and market‑access conditions.
  • Using aggregate export elasticity for sectoral policy design; electronics and pharmaceuticals react differently because of imported‑content intensity.
  • Overlooking the hedging requirement in the ECB framework; unhedged ECBs can create systemic currency mismatches during sharp depreciation.
  • Believing that PLI‑driven export growth automatically lowers exchange‑rate sensitivity; without deeper domestic input production, elasticity remains high.

Impact of Trade Finance Constraints on Export Competitiveness

Trade finance — instruments that bridge the gap between shipment and payment — critically affects export performance, particularly for SMEs. Tighter credit standards raise the cost and reduce the availability of letters of credit, export credit insurance, and factoring, compelling exporters to decline orders or accept unfavourable terms. WTO estimates indicate the global trade finance gap widens sharply during financial stress, with the 2008‑09 crisis and the 2020 pandemic causing significant declines in trade-finance-backed transactions. RBI surveys show a substantial share of exporters face higher collateral requirements and longer letter-of-credit processing times.

The mechanism linking finance constraints to competitiveness operates through three channels. First, higher financing costs raise the effective price of exported goods, eroding price elasticity advantages that a depreciated rupee might otherwise confer. Second, longer processing times lengthen the cash‑conversion cycle, increasing working‑capital needs and exposing firms to exchange‑rate risk during the extended receivables period. Third, the inability to secure buyer‑side guarantees limits market diversification; exporters remain concentrated in traditional destinations where established banking relationships exist, forgoing higher‑margin markets in Africa, Latin America, and the Commonwealth of Independent States.

Policy responses combine supply- and demand-side measures. EXIM Bank has expanded credit lines and enhanced ECGC schemes with capped premiums for SMEs and broader sectoral coverage. The RBI's Trade Receivables Discounting System (TReDS) enables electronic invoice discounting, reducing dependence on bank-intermediated factoring. Studies indicate TReDS participation reduces receivables periods and improves order acceptance rates.

Nevertheless, structural bottlenecks persist. The Basel III capital framework incentivises banks to allocate risk‑weighted assets toward higher‑rated corporate borrowers, leaving SMEs with limited access. Moreover, the absence of a unified legal framework for electronic negotiable instruments hampers cross‑border factoring. Addressing these gaps requires coordinated action among the Ministry of Finance, the RBI, and the International Chamber of Commerce to harmonise standards for digital trade documents under the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records.

Role of Bilateral Currency Swap Arrangements in Reserve Management

Bilateral currency swap lines provide a complementary reserve buffer, enabling central banks to exchange currencies without using foreign-exchange markets. The RBI has activated swap arrangements with major central banks including the Bank of Japan, Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England, and People's Bank of China. Each line defines a maximum amount, a short tenor (typically three to six months, renewable), and an interest rate linked to the relevant OIS curve plus a spread.

Their strategic value is threefold. First, they offer a backstop during sudden stops or flight-to-safety episodes when market liquidity evaporates; during the 2020 pandemic, the RBI accessed the Fed swap line to alleviate dollar funding pressures. Second, they lessen the need for large reserve holdings, reducing opportunity costs. Third, they signal credible cooperation, deterring speculative attacks by raising the perceived cost of a coordinated sell-off.

Operationally, a drawdown delivers rupees to the counterpart and receives foreign currency at a pre-agreed rate. The rupee injection is sterilised via open-market operations to maintain monetary stance. Maturities are aligned with the RBI's liquidity calendar, coinciding with seasonal foreign-currency inflows.

Swap lines are not a substitute for adequate reserves. They are contingent on counterparty renewal and capped well below IMF reserve adequacy metrics. Over-reliance may create moral hazard, masking current-account vulnerabilities and delaying needed reforms, as recent Financial Stability Reports have cautioned.

Worked Illustration
A candidate is asked: “Explain how a bilateral currency swap line can be used to sterilise a foreign‑currency injection and why this matters for monetary policy independence.”
When the RBI draws on a swap, it receives foreign currency (e.g., USD) and supplies rupees to the counterpart. The rupee injection expands domestic liquidity. To sterilise, the RBI simultaneously conducts an open‑market sale of government securities or issues cash‑management bills, absorbing the excess rupees. This two‑step operation leaves the monetary base unchanged while the foreign‑currency asset improves the reserve position. Because the sterilisation tool is domestic, the RBI retains control over the policy rate and does not need to adjust the repo rate in response to the swap, preserving monetary policy independence.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mentions “swap line” and “sterilisation”, identify the two legs (foreign‑currency receipt, rupee supply) and the offsetting domestic operation. Use the phrase “monetary base unchanged” to signal you understand the independence implication.

Exchange Rate Misalignment Assessment Using Behavioral Equilibrium Exchange Rate Models

Assessing whether a currency is over‑ or undervalued relative to its macroeconomic fundamentals is a core analytical task for policymakers and analysts. The Behavioral Equilibrium Exchange Rate (BEER) approach, pioneered by Clark and MacDonald (1998), estimates the equilibrium real effective exchange rate (REER) as a function of observable fundamentals — terms of trade, net foreign assets, productivity differentials (Balassa‑Samuelson effect), government consumption, and a risk‑premium proxy. Unlike the Fundamental Equilibrium Exchange Rate (FEER), which imposes a normative current‑account target, BEER is a reduced‑form, data‑driven method that can be updated quarterly.

In India, RBI research publishes quarterly BEER estimates using a vector error-correction model with data from the mid-1990s. The long-run relationship typically shows a positive coefficient for terms of trade, a negative coefficient for net foreign assets, and a positive coefficient for productivity differentials, consistent with the Balassa-Samuelson effect.

The model’s residuals — the deviation of the actual REER from the BEER — are interpreted as misalignment. A persistent positive residual (actual REER above BEER) suggests overvaluation, implying that the rupee is stronger than fundamentals justify, which can erode export competitiveness and widen the current‑account deficit. Conversely, a sustained negative residual signals undervaluation, potentially inviting accusations of competitive devaluation and triggering capital‑inflow surges that complicate monetary management.

Empirical studies indicate the rupee's REER has generally stayed within a narrow band around BEER estimates, with temporary deviations during the pandemic reflecting safe-haven dollar demand. RBI assessments have noted periods of modest overvaluation, supporting a case for calibrated, market-based depreciation rather than administrative controls.

Limitations of the BEER framework include its reliance on the stability of estimated coefficients, which can shift after structural breaks such as the 2016 demonetisation or the 2020 lockdown. Moreover, the model does not capture expectations‑driven dynamics or the impact of unconventional monetary policies in advanced economies. Analysts therefore supplement BEER with the FEER (which anchors the current account to a sustainable level) and with the macro‑balance approach that incorporates the desired net‑international‑investment‑position trajectory.

Cross‑Border Insolvency Framework and Its Effect on Capital Flows

The efficiency of cross‑border insolvency resolution directly influences investor confidence and the cost of external financing. India’s adoption of the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross‑Border Insolvency through the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Second Amendment) Act, 2020, introduced a statutory mechanism for recognising foreign proceedings and coordinating with overseas courts. Prior to this amendment, the absence of a clear legal pathway forced foreign creditors to pursue parallel domestic proceedings, leading to delays, asset stripping, and higher recovery uncertainty.

The framework establishes three pillars: access for foreign representatives, recognition of foreign proceedings with automatic stay, and mandated cooperation between courts and insolvency professionals. The NCLT has recognised proceedings from several jurisdictions, including the UK, US, Singapore, and UAE, with cases such as Jet Airways and Reliance Communications serving as early precedents.

The reform reduces the legal risk premium in external borrowing costs. BIS research suggests UNCITRAL-compliant regimes enjoy lower bond spreads. In India, corporate bond spreads have narrowed modestly since the amendment, after accounting for global risk factors.

Challenges persist, including NCLT capacity constraints and the absence of a dedicated protocol for financial institutions. A recent RBI discussion paper proposes bilateral MoUs with key supervisors to pre-define coordination, drawing on FSB resolution standards.

Green Finance Taxonomy and Its Integration with External Borrowing Limits

A sovereign green taxonomy — classifying environmentally sustainable activities — affects the pricing and eligibility of external borrowings. India's Green Finance Taxonomy (draft 2023, finalised 2024) aligns with the EU Taxonomy's six objectives while incorporating national priorities like climate-resilient agriculture and sustainable urban infrastructure. It sets technical screening criteria, do-no-significant-harm thresholds, and social safeguards.

Integration with the ECB framework occurs via a 'green window' in the RBI's master direction, offering concessional all-in-cost ceilings and shorter minimum maturities for taxonomy-compliant projects. SEBI provides facilitative measures for green bonds, including disclosure relaxations and a dedicated segment in the Masala bond market.

Green ECB issuances have grown significantly in recent years, now accounting for a notable share of total ECB inflows. A measurable 'greenium' — lower spreads compared to conventional ECBs — has been observed. Alignment with ICMA Green Bond Principles enables cross-listing on European exchanges, diversifying the investor base and reducing rollover risk.

Challenges include ensuring robust verification — reviewers must be accredited — and preventing greenwashing. The RBI mandates enhanced due-diligence on end-use certification, and a digital registry is being developed to track outcomes. Effective enforcement will determine if the taxonomy durably lowers external financing costs or remains a labeling exercise.

Central Bank Digital Currency and Cross‑Border Payment Architecture

The Reserve Bank of India launched the wholesale CBDC pilot (e‑₹‑W) in November 2022 and the retail pilot (e‑₹‑R) in December 2022, placing India among several major economies with live CBDC pilots. The wholesale pilot settles secondary‑market government securities transactions between banks, reducing settlement risk and the need for collateralised intraday liquidity. The retail pilot tests a token‑based, account‑less model distributed through participating banks, with offline capability via near‑field communication. Both pilots operate on a permissioned distributed ledger maintained by the RBI, ensuring finality without a separate clearing corporation.

Cross‑border implications arise from the RBI’s participation in the Bank for International Settlements’ Project mBridge (with the monetary authorities of Hong Kong, Thailand, the UAE and China) and its engagement in other multi‑CBDC experiments. The mBridge platform demonstrated atomic payment‑versus‑payment settlement across jurisdictions, cutting correspondent‑banking chains from days to seconds. For India, such platforms could lower the cost of remittances — estimated at 5‑6 % for the roughly $100 bn annual inflow — and reduce reliance on the US‑dollar‑denominated SWIFT network. However, the RBI has emphasised that any cross‑border CBDC linkage must preserve capital‑account controls; the mBridge design is reported to incorporate jurisdictional policy modules that can enforce local KYC/AML rules and capital‑flow limits.

Key policy trade‑offs include: (1) monetary‑policy transmission — a widely held retail CBDC could disintermediate bank deposits, forcing the RBI to adjust the interest‑rate corridor; (2) financial‑stability — run risk rises if households shift en masse to the risk‑free CBDC during stress; (3) data‑governance — the RBI retains transaction‑level visibility, but cross‑border platforms require agreed data‑sharing standards. The RBI’s 2023 “Framework for CBDC” initially set a ceiling of ₹10,000 per wallet for the retail pilot and mandates that banks hold CBDC balances in a separate ledger, limiting substitution effects while the technology matures.

Trade in Environmental Goods and Services under the WTO Environmental Goods Agreement

The Environmental Goods Agreement (EGA) negotiations, launched in 2014 by a coalition of 46 WTO members representing over 90 % of global trade in environmental goods, aimed to eliminate tariffs on a list of 300‑plus products such as solar panels, wind turbines, water‑treatment membranes and air‑quality monitoring equipment. The talks stalled in 2016 after participants failed to agree on product coverage — developing members, including India, argued that the list reflected the export interests of advanced economies and omitted goods critical for climate adaptation in the Global South, such as flood‑resilient seeds and low‑cost sanitation technology.

India’s position has been to link tariff liberalisation to technology transfer and capacity‑building commitments under the UNFCCC’s Technology Mechanism. In the 2022 WTO Ministerial Conference (MC12), India co‑sponsored a proposal for a “development‑focused” EGA that would phase tariff cuts over longer periods for least‑developed countries and embed a review clause tied to the Global Stocktake. The proposal did not achieve consensus, but it highlighted the growing intersection of trade policy and climate finance. Meanwhile, plurilateral initiatives such as the “Trade and Environmental Sustainability Structured Discussions” (TESSD) continue to explore non‑tariff measures — standards, conformity assessment, and subsidies — that affect environmental goods trade.

From an external‑sector perspective, the absence of a concluded EGA means that Most‑Favoured‑Nation tariffs on many green technologies remain at 5‑10 % in several large markets, raising the cost of India’s renewable‑energy deployment. The International Energy Agency has estimated that a full EGA could reduce the levelised cost of electricity from solar PV in India by 3‑4 % by 2030. Until a multilateral deal materialises, India relies on bilateral FTAs (e.g., the India‑UAE CEPA includes zero‑tariff lines for solar cells) and on the ITA‑II expansion for information‑technology products that overlap with smart‑grid equipment.

Comparative Analysis of Negotiation Stages
Explain why the Environmental Goods Agreement has not been concluded despite broad consensus on the product list.
The EGA negotiations collapsed because consensus on the product list masked deeper disagreements on three dimensions. First, developing members demanded special and differential treatment — longer phase‑out periods and exemptions for goods deemed essential for adaptation — which the major exporters (EU, US, Japan) resisted. Second, the “critical mass” approach required participants covering a dominant share of trade in each product to join; India and several ASEAN economies insisted on binding technology‑transfer provisions, which hindered consensus. Third, the WTO’s single‑undertaking principle meant that any EGA outcome would be legally binding on all signatories, raising concerns that future disputes could challenge domestic green‑subsidy programmes. Consequently, the talks moved to a “plurilateral” track (TESSD) where non‑tariff issues are discussed without a tariff‑elimination mandate, leaving the original EGA in limbo.

Global Minimum Corporate Tax and Its Implications for Capital Account Flows

The OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework’s Pillar Two agreement, finalised in October 2021 and effective from fiscal years beginning on or after 31 December 2023, introduces a 15 % global minimum corporate tax (GMCT) applied through a system of Qualified Domestic Minimum Top‑up Taxes (QDMTT), Income Inclusion Rules (IIR) and Undertaxed Profits Rules (UTPR). Over 140 jurisdictions, including India, have committed to implementation. India enacted the Finance Act 2022 amendments to the Income‑Tax Act, inserting a new Chapter XII‑G that imposes a 15 % top‑up tax on multinational enterprises (MNEs) with global revenues exceeding €750 mn, aligning domestic law with the GloBE rules.

For the external sector, the GMCT reduces the incentive for profit shifting to low‑tax jurisdictions, thereby altering the composition of portfolio and foreign‑direct‑investment flows. IMF analysis indicates that a 1 percentage‑point rise in the effective minimum tax rate may reduce cross‑border profit‑shifting flows by a measurable margin in emerging markets. In India’s case, the effective tax rate on foreign affiliates was reportedly near 22 % after the 2019 corporate‑tax cut, so the direct revenue gain is modest. However, the removal of “tax‑haven” arbitrage improves the quality of FDI — investors are more likely to locate real economic activity (manufacturing, R&D) rather than booking intellectual‑property royalties in zero‑tax hubs.

Capital‑account management interacts with the GMCT through the Foreign Portfolio Investor (FPI) regime. The RBI’s 2023 master direction on FPIs requires beneficial‑owner disclosure aligned with the Common Reporting Standard; the GMCT reinforces this by making opaque structures less attractive. A potential side‑effect is a short‑term increase in repatriation of accumulated offshore earnings as MNEs restructure to comply, temporarily boosting the capital account surplus. The RBI monitors such flows through the “Capital Flow Management Measures” framework, ready to tighten the voluntary retention route if volatility spikes.

Currency Substitution, Dollarisation and the Limits of Monetary Policy Autonomy

Currency substitution occurs when residents hold a significant share of their financial assets in a foreign currency — most commonly the US dollar — instead of the domestic currency. In India, the degree of substitution is low: the ratio of foreign‑currency deposits to broad money (M3) has remained low, estimated below 2 % in recent decades, reflecting the RBI’s restrictive capital‑account regime and the rupee’s relative stability. By contrast, economies such as Ecuador (official dollarisation since 2000), Zimbabwe (multi‑currency regime 2009‑2019) and Lebanon (de‑facto dollarisation after 2019) exhibit substitution ratios above 50 %, severely constraining the central bank’s ability to set interest rates independently.

The mechanism linking substitution to policy autonomy operates through two channels. First, the “interest‑rate pass‑through” channel: when a large share of deposits is dollar‑denominated, domestic lending rates track the US federal funds rate plus a country‑risk premium, rendering the local policy rate ineffective. Second, the “balance‑sheet” channel: firms and households with dollar liabilities but rupee revenues face currency‑mismatch risk; a depreciation triggers defaults, forcing the central bank to intervene or raise rates sharply, which can exacerbate recessionary pressures. The IMF’s 2022 “Integrated Policy Framework” recommends that countries with high substitution adopt a tighter macro‑prudential stance — limits on foreign‑currency lending, higher provisioning, and a credible inflation‑targeting framework — to regain policy space.

India’s experience illustrates the preventive approach. The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) 1999 caps resident foreign‑currency accounts at $250,000 per financial year under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, and the RBI’s 2015 “Framework for Foreign Currency Exposure” advises corporates to hedge a substantial portion of net open positions. These measures, combined with a flexible inflation‑targeting regime (4 % ± 2 % since 2016), have kept substitution low even during episodes of rupee depreciation (e.g., 2013 taper tantrum, 2020 COVID shock). The lesson for policymakers is that early capital‑account safeguards and a credible monetary anchor are more effective than reactive controls once substitution becomes entrenched.

Climate Transition Risk, Stranded Assets and External Debt Sustainability

Climate transition risk — the financial loss arising from the shift to a low‑carbon economy — has direct implications for a country’s external debt sustainability. Stranded assets, such as coal‑fired power plants that become uneconomic before the end of their useful life, reduce the cash‑flow generation capacity of sovereign‑guaranteed entities and state‑owned enterprises (SOEs). In India, the Central Electricity Authority has indicated that a significant quantum of coal capacity may face early retirement by 2030 under the 2070 net‑zero pathway, with potential write‑downs running into trillions of rupees. Since many of these plants are financed through external commercial borrowings (ECBs) and sovereign‑backed bonds, the credit risk migrates to the sovereign balance sheet.

The IMF’s 2023 Debt Sustainability Framework for Market Access Countries includes a climate‑adjusted stress test, illustrating that a 1 % GDP shock from stranded‑asset write‑offs could raise the debt‑to‑GDP ratio by several tenths of a percentage point over the medium term. Rating agencies have begun to reflect this; Moody’s 2024 outlook for India notes moderate exposure to transition risk and cites the sovereign green bond issuance as a mitigating step. The RBI’s 2024 “Guidance on Climate‑Related Financial Risk” requires banks to disclose exposure to high‑carbon sectors and to apply higher risk weights to unhedged ECBs financing coal assets.

Policy levers to contain the external‑debt impact include: (i) redirecting ECB proceeds toward renewable‑energy and grid‑storage projects that qualify under the Green Finance Taxonomy, thereby accessing the “green window” with lower all‑in‑cost ceilings; (ii) using sovereign‑guarantee frameworks that tie guarantee fees to the carbon intensity of the underlying project; (iii) establishing a “transition‑risk buffer” within the foreign‑exchange reserve portfolio — e.g., allocating a portion of reserves to green bonds issued by multilateral development banks — to diversify away from fossil‑fuel‑linked assets. The 2023‑24 Union Budget announced significant funding for energy transition, including sovereign green bonds, exemplifying this integrated approach.

Export Credit Agencies, Official Export Credits and the WTO Agreement on Subsidies

Export Credit Agencies (ECAs) are government‑backed institutions that provide finance, guarantees and insurance to domestic exporters for overseas sales. Major ECAs include the Export‑Import Bank of India (EXIM Bank), the United States EXIM Bank, Euler Hermes (Germany), Bpifrance (France) and the Japan Bank for International Cooperation. Their operations are disciplined by the OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits (the “OECD Arrangement”), which sets minimum premium rates, maximum repayment terms and tied‑aid disciplines for participating members. The Arrangement is a politically binding consensus; non‑OECD ECAs such as China’s Sinosure and Russia’s EXIAR are not formally bound by it, creating a two‑tier playing field.

At the multilateral level, the WTO Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (SCM Agreement) treats officially supported export credits as potential subsidies. Article 1.1(a)(1) defines a financial contribution by a government where a government provides a direct transfer of funds, potential direct transfers, or government revenue foregone. If an ECA extends credit on terms more favourable than market benchmarks — for example, interest rates below the Commercial Interest Reference Rate (CIRR) — the benefit may constitute a subsidy. However, the SCM Agreement contains a “safe‑harbour” in Annex K (the “Export Credit Guidelines”) which provides that export credits conforming to the OECD Arrangement are deemed not to be prohibited subsidies. The Guidelines require that the credit be denominated in a convertible currency, have a minimum maturity of two years, and carry an interest rate at least equal to the CIRR for the relevant currency and maturity.

India’s EXIM Bank operates under the Export‑Import Bank of India Act, 1981, and follows the OECD Arrangement’s premium and maturity norms for its tied‑aid and commercial programmes. The Bank’s “Buyer’s Credit” and “Supplier’s Credit” facilities are priced using the CIRR published by the OECD, ensuring SCM‑compliant treatment. The Government of India’s Interest Equalisation Scheme for MSME exporters subsidises the interest differential on pre‑ and post‑shipment rupee export credit. Because the scheme is a domestic budgetary outlay rather than an ECA‑backed credit, it falls under the SCM Agreement’s “actionable subsidies” category and is subject to countervailing duty investigations by trading partners.

The interaction between ECA discipline and the SCM Agreement creates a strategic tension: countries seek to maximise export competitiveness through generous official credit while avoiding WTO disputes. WTO jurisprudence (e.g., DS547) illustrates how export‑credit‑linked subsidies can be challenged. For policymakers, the key is to align ECA pricing with the CIRR, maintain transparency through the OECD’s “Export Credit Reporting System”, and ensure that any domestic interest‑equalisation measures are notified under Article 25 of the SCM Agreement to reduce litigation risk.

Foreign Direct Investment Screening Regimes and National Security Exceptions

Since the late 2010s, major economies have introduced or tightened foreign direct investment (FDI) screening mechanisms on national‑security grounds. The United States expanded the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) through the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act (FIRRMA) of 2018, broadening jurisdiction to non‑controlling investments in critical technology, infrastructure and sensitive data sectors. The European Union adopted the FDI Screening Regulation (EU) 2019/452, establishing a cooperation framework for Member States’ screening authorities and a Commission opinion procedure for investments likely to affect security or public order across the Union. The United Kingdom enacted the National Security and Investment Act 2021, granting the Secretary of State powers to impose conditions or unwind transactions in 17 “sensitive” sectors, including artificial intelligence, quantum computing and advanced materials.

India’s response came via Press Note 3 (2020), which mandated government approval for any FDI from entities situated in countries sharing a land border with India — effectively targeting China, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Myanmar, Bhutan and Afghanistan. The Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) issues sector‑specific guidelines; for instance, the telecom sector FDI policy requires security clearance for network equipment vendors. These measures are justified under GATT Article XXI (Security Exceptions) and the corresponding GATS Article XIV bis, which permit members to take actions they consider necessary for the protection of essential security interests. However, the WTO’s Appellate Body in “Russia — Traffic in Transit” (DS512) clarified that the invocation must be made in good faith and not as a disguised restriction on trade.

Screening regimes typically follow a three‑stage process: (i) mandatory filing for transactions crossing defined thresholds; (ii) substantive review assessing risks to critical infrastructure, technology transfer, data sovereignty and supply‑chain resilience; (iii) conditional approval, mitigation agreements (e.g., data‑localisation commitments, board‑seat restrictions) or prohibition. The CFIUS “mitigation agreement” model has been emulated by the EU and UK, where undertakings may include a government‑appointed monitoring trustee. India’s Press Note 3 does not prescribe a mitigation framework; approval is binary, which can create uncertainty for investors and may be challenged as inconsistent with the “fair and equitable treatment” standard under bilateral investment treaties (BITs).

From an external‑sector perspective, screening regimes affect capital‑account flows by raising the cost and unpredictability of cross‑border M&A. Available evidence suggests a decline in greenfield announcements from screened jurisdictions into sectors covered by CFIUS and the EU Regulation after 2018. For India, FDI equity inflows from China fell sharply in 2021‑22, while inflows from Singapore, the United States and the UAE rose, reflecting a re‑routing of capital. Policymakers must balance legitimate security concerns with the need to keep the capital account sufficiently open to finance the current‑account deficit and support technology upgrading.

Reserve Adequacy Assessment: The IMF’s Assessing Reserve Adequacy Metric versus Traditional Ratios

The IMF’s Assessing Reserve Adequacy (ARA) metric, introduced in 2011 and refined in 2015, provides a risk‑based framework for evaluating whether a country’s foreign‑exchange reserves are sufficient to withstand external shocks. Unlike traditional rule‑of‑thumb ratios — reserves‑to‑imports (typically ≥ 3 months), reserves‑to‑short‑term‑debt (≥ 100 %), and reserves‑to‑broad‑money (M2) (≈ 20 %) — the ARA metric calibrates reserve needs to a country’s exchange‑rate regime, capital‑account openness, and the composition of its external liabilities. The metric computes a “reserve adequacy range” expressed as a percentage of GDP, derived from a weighted sum of four risk components: (i) export income volatility, (ii) broad‑money volatility, (iii) short‑term external debt rollover risk, and (iv) other portfolio liability volatility. The weights differ for fixed, floating and managed‑float regimes, reflecting the higher reserve demand under pegged rates.

For India, which operates a managed float with a relatively open capital account for portfolio flows, recent ARA assessments place the adequate reserve range at approximately 15‑20 % of GDP. India’s reserves have recently stood at around 19 % of GDP, comfortably within the ARA band. Traditional ratios tell a similar story: reserves cover well over 3 months of imports, exceed 100 % of short‑term debt, and surpass 20 % of M2. However, the ARA metric captures nuances missed by the ratios — for instance, the rising share of non‑resident deposits in the banking system (which behave like short‑term debt) and the increasing volatility of portfolio equity flows. During the 2020 COVID shock, India’s reserves‑to‑imports ratio dipped, yet the ARA‑based adequacy range remained satisfied because the metric’s weighting of export‑income volatility adjusted for the temporary collapse in merchandise exports.

The ARA framework also informs the IMF’s “Reserve Adequacy Credit Line” (RACL) and the design of precautionary facilities such as the Flexible Credit Line (FCL). Countries that meet the ARA threshold and have strong policy frameworks can access the FCL without ex‑post conditionality. India has not sought an FCL, but the ARA assessment feeds into the Article IV consultation’s external‑sector risk analysis. A key limitation is that the ARA metric relies on historical volatility estimates, which may under‑predict tail‑risk events such as sudden stops triggered by geopolitical sanctions. Complementary stress‑testing — for example, the IMF’s “External Sector Stress Test” module — is therefore recommended alongside the ARA metric.

For policymakers, the practical takeaway is to monitor both the ARA band and the traditional ratios, but to give greater weight to the ARA when the capital‑account structure is shifting rapidly (e.g., rising ECB borrowing, growing offshore rupee positions). The RBI’s reserve management framework explicitly references the ARA metric as an input for its internal adequacy benchmarks, alongside the traditional import‑cover and short‑term‑debt ratios.

Cross‑Border Payment Interoperability: SWIFT, CIPS and Emerging Multilateral Platforms

The global cross‑border payment infrastructure is anchored by the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT), a cooperative messaging network that standardises financial communications (MT and ISO 20022 messages) across more than 11,000 institutions. SWIFT does not move funds; it transmits payment instructions that are settled through correspondent‑bank relationships or central‑bank real‑time gross settlement (RTGS) systems. The dominance of SWIFT creates a single point of failure and exposes participants to geopolitical sanctions, as illustrated by the 2022 removal of selected Russian banks from the network.

China’s Cross‑Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS), launched in 2015, provides a yuan‑denominated messaging and settlement platform that directly connects participating banks to the People’s Bank of China’s RTGS. CIPS uses ISO 20022 messaging and settles payments in central‑bank money, reducing reliance on correspondent banks. As of recent years, CIPS had over 1,300 direct and indirect participants across approximately 100 countries, processing several million messages per day. However, CIPS’ reach remains limited to renminbi transactions and does not yet offer a multi‑currency settlement layer.

Russia’s System for Transfer of Financial Messages (SPFS) and India’s Structured Financial Messaging System (SFMS) are domestic analogues designed to ensure continuity of messaging under sanctions or technical disruptions. SPFS, operational since 2014, connects hundreds of Russian banks and a growing number of foreign participants, primarily in Eurasian Economic Union states. SFMS, managed by the RBI, serves as the messaging backbone for India’s RTGS and NEFT systems and is being extended to support cross‑border rupee settlement under the “Rupee‑Vostro” arrangement with partner central banks.

Multilateral initiatives aim to bridge these silos. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) Innovation Hub’s Project mBridge (formerly Project Inthanon‑LionRock) explores a multi‑currency, multi‑central‑bank digital currency (CBDC) platform using distributed ledger technology. The pilot, involving the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, the Bank of Thailand, the Central Bank of the UAE and the People’s Bank of China, demonstrated real‑time, 24/7 settlement of cross‑border payments in four currencies with finality in central‑bank money. Separately, the G20’s “Roadmap for Enhancing Cross‑Border Payments” (2020) sets targets for cost, speed, transparency and access, encouraging interoperability between SWIFT’s ISO 20022 migration and emerging CBDC corridors.

For the external sector, payment interoperability influences the velocity of capital flows, the cost of trade finance, and the resilience of the financial system to sanctions. A fragmented messaging landscape raises compliance costs for banks that must maintain multiple gateways. Conversely, a convergent ISO 20022 standard across SWIFT, CIPS and CBDC platforms can lower transaction costs and improve traceability, supporting anti‑money‑laundering (AML) objectives. Policymakers should monitor the evolution of the “SWIFT‑CIPS bridge” discussions, the progress of Project mBridge, and the RBI’s bilateral rupee‑settlement agreements, as these will shape the future architecture of India’s cross‑border payment flows.

Sovereign Debt Restructuring Frameworks: The IMF’s Debt Sustainability Analysis and the Common Framework

Sovereign debt restructuring has moved from ad‑hoc Paris Club negotiations to a more structured, albeit still evolving, architecture. The IMF’s Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) for Market‑Access Countries provides the analytical backbone: it projects debt‑to‑GDP trajectories under baseline and stress scenarios, evaluates gross financing needs, and applies the “debt‑carrying‑capacity” classification (strong, medium, weak) based on the Composite Indicator (CI) that blends institutional quality, growth prospects and reserve adequacy. The DSA’s output — a “debt‑sustainability” rating — determines whether a country qualifies for IMF financing on normal terms, requires a precautionary arrangement, or needs a restructuring before Fund resources can be committed.

The G20 Common Framework for Debt Treatments beyond the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI), launched in November 2020, aims to bring official bilateral creditors (Paris Club and non‑Paris Club, notably China) and private creditors into a single, coordinated restructuring process. The Framework requires a debtor to request treatment, after which the IMF certifies that the debt is unsustainable and that the debtor is pursuing appropriate adjustment policies. A creditor committee is then formed, co‑chaired by a Paris Club member and a non‑Paris Club creditor (often China). The committee negotiates a memorandum of understanding (MoU) that specifies comparable treatment across creditor groups, including maturity extensions, interest‑rate reductions and, where warranted, principal haircuts.

Three cases illustrate the Framework’s operation and limits. Chad (2022) became the first country to complete a Common Framework restructuring, securing a significant net‑present‑value reduction on official bilateral claims and a comparable deal with private bondholders after protracted negotiation over comparability of treatment. Zambia (2023) reached an MoU with its official creditor committee, but final agreement with Eurobond holders took additional time, highlighting the difficulty of aligning private‑sector timelines. Ethiopia requested treatment under the Framework; however, the process stalled over disagreements on the valuation of Chinese policy‑bank loans versus commercial credits, underscoring the lack of a standardized methodology for non‑Paris Club claims.

The IMF’s proposed Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism (SDRM) — a statutory, binding framework with a super‑majority voting rule — remains unimplemented. In its absence, the Fund relies on “lending into arrears” policies (the “good‑faith” exception) to provide financing while restructuring talks proceed. For India, which is a creditor through EXIM Bank lines of credit to several low‑income countries, the Common Framework’s comparability principle matters: any debt relief India grants must be mirrored by other official creditors to avoid free‑rider problems. The RBI’s guidelines on External Commercial Borrowings incorporate a “debt‑sustainability” check that references the IMF DSA thresholds, ensuring that Indian‑backed projects do not exacerbate a borrower’s restructuring risk.

From an external‑sector perspective, the credibility of the restructuring architecture influences sovereign borrowing costs, reserve adequacy assessments and capital‑flow volatility. A predictable, timely restructuring process reduces the risk of prolonged arrears that can trigger contagion through banking exposures and portfolio rebalancing. Policymakers should track the evolution of the Common Framework’s “comparability of treatment” guidelines, the IMF’s DSA methodology updates (including climate‑adjusted stress tests), and the potential adoption of an SDRM‑style statutory mechanism, as these will shape the cost and availability of external financing for emerging markets.

  • Assuming all FDI screening measures are automatically justified under GATT Article XXI; the good‑faith requirement and non‑discrimination test are essential.
  • Treating the IMF ARA metric as a substitute for traditional reserve ratios; both serve different analytical purposes and should be used together.
  • Believing CIPS or SPFS can fully replace SWIFT for multi‑currency settlement; they remain largely single‑currency or regional systems.
  • Overlooking the comparability‑of‑treatment principle in the G20 Common Framework; unequal haircuts across creditor groups can derail a restructuring.

  • Assuming that ISO 20022 migration alone guarantees cross‑border payment interoperability; governance, legal finality and liquidity bridges are equally critical.

Trade Policy Uncertainty and Firm‑Level Export Dynamics

Trade policy uncertainty (TPU) measures the unpredictability of tariffs, non‑tariff barriers and regulatory regimes faced by exporters. The most widely cited metric is the Economic Policy Uncertainty index constructed by Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016), which aggregates newspaper coverage, tax‑code expiration dates and forecaster disagreement. At the firm level, the World Bank’s Exporter Dynamics Database shows that a one‑standard‑deviation rise in TPU reduces the probability of a new export relationship by several percentage points and raises the exit rate of existing relationships by a similar magnitude. The mechanism operates through two channels: (i) higher option value of waiting — firms postpone irreversible sunk‑cost investments such as market‑specific certification or distribution networks; (ii) increased volatility of expected profits, which tightens credit constraints because lenders demand higher risk premia on export‑financing lines.

Empirical work on India’s 2015‑2020 tariff overhaul — phased peak-rate cuts from 12.5% to 10% and the Merchandise Exports from India Scheme — shows asymmetric responses: high imported‑input sectors (electronics, auto components) saw export entry rise ~12%, while labour‑intensive sectors (textiles, leather) saw muted gains, as competitiveness hinges on wages more than tariffs. The WTO Trade Policy Review (2022) noted that frequent FTP amendments, especially the post‑2019 shift to quarterly revisions, heightened TPU for SMEs lacking compliance teams. A 2023 RBI working paper found banks charging 30‑45 bps extra on pre‑shipment finance for firms in sectors with over three FTP amendments in the prior year.

Policy implications are twofold. First, a transparent, rule‑based tariff schedule with a credible commitment horizon (e.g., a five‑year tariff bind) dampens the option‑value channel. Second, targeted export‑credit guarantees — such as ECGC’s “Specific Policy” for high‑TPU sectors — can offset credit constraints without distorting allocation. The 2024 Budget’s “Trade Policy Certainty Framework” locking tariff lines for three years is a step forward, but effectiveness hinges on legislative credibility and avoiding ad‑hoc safeguards.

Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism and Trade Competitiveness

The European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), established by Regulation (EU) 2023/956, entered a transitional reporting phase on 1 October 2023 and will become fully operational with financial obligations from 1 January 2026. CBAM covers six carbon‑intensive sectors — cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen — and requires importers to surrender CBAM certificates equivalent to the embedded emissions of the imported goods, calculated using EU‑approved default values or verified actual emissions. The mechanism mirrors the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) price, ensuring that foreign producers face a comparable carbon cost to domestic firms.

India’s exposure centres on steel and aluminium. In FY2023‑24, India exported 5.2 Mt of finished steel to the EU (~12% of total steel exports). The 2023 National Steel Policy targets a 30% cut in emission intensity by 2030 via hydrogen‑based DRI and scrap. Aluminium exports to the EU were 0.4 Mt; the 2022 Aluminium Mission pursues a similar roadmap. However, CBAM’s default values (based on EU averages) are well below Indian averages, implying certificate costs that could erode competitiveness by 8‑12% for steel and 5‑7% for aluminium (ICRIER, 2024).

WTO compatibility turns on GATT Article III:2 (national treatment on internal taxes) — requiring the CBAM charge not to exceed the domestic ETS cost — and Article XI (quantitative restrictions), as certificate surrender acts as a de‑facto import licence. The EU invokes Article II:2(a) (border tax adjustments) and Article XX(b)/(g) (environmental exceptions). India, Brazil, South Africa, and China filed a joint communication (WT/GC/238, 2023) challenging CBAM under the Article XX “least‑trade‑restrictive” test. Adjudication will likely begin with the financial phase, making 2026‑27 pivotal for climate‑trade jurisprudence.

For India, focus on steel and aluminium exposure and the domestic decarbonisation policies (National Steel Policy, Green Hydrogen Mission) that aim to lower embedded emissions.

Digital Trade Rules under the WTO Joint Statement Initiative

The JSI on E‑Commerce launched at the 2019 Buenos Aires Ministerial with 76 members, now 86 covering >90% of global trade. Talks cover three pillars: (i) enabling e‑transactions (e‑authentication, e‑signatures, paperless trade); (ii) openness and non‑discrimination (cross‑border data flows, data‑localisation bans, source‑code protection); (iii) trust and consumer protection (online consumer protection, spam, cybersecurity). The 1998 moratorium on customs duties on e‑transmissions, renewed each Ministerial, remains contentious; MC12 (2022) extended it to MC13 (2024) without defining “electronic transmissions”.

India has been cautious. Its 2020 submission (JOB/GC/227) argued binding cross‑border data‑flow rules would constrain “data sovereignty” under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the draft National Data Governance Framework. India also opposed source‑code provisions to retain regulatory space for audit/security in defence and telecom. At MC12, India backed a “development‑friendly” outcome with data‑localisation flexibilities for public‑policy goals and a permanent moratorium tied to a broader development package.

Stakes are high: UNCTAD (2023) estimates digitally deliverable services were 64% of global services exports in 2022, with India at 4.2%. A binding JSI would secure market access for India’s Mode‑1 IT services but could curb the government’s ability to mandate local data storage (e.g., RBI’s 2018 payments‑data circular). The talks thus pit export certainty against regulatory autonomy.

International Investment Agreements and Investor‑State Dispute Settlement Reform

The global IIA stock peaked at ~3,300 in 2020, including ~2,900 BITs. The traditional BIT (e.g., 1994 India‑UK BIT) grants investors substantive protections (FET, full protection and security, MFN) and ISDS access via ICSID/UNCITRAL. With ~1,100 known ISDS cases by end‑2023, UNCITRAL Working Group III (2017) drives reforms: (i) a multilateral investment court with appellate tribunal; (ii) arbitrator code of conduct; (iii) transparency rules (Mauritius Convention, 2020, in force 2022); (iv) narrowing FET to curb regulatory chill.

India reacted decisively. After losing White Industries (2011) and Deutsche Telekom (2016) cases, it terminated 58 BITs (2017) and adopted a 2015 Model BIT that: (a) replaces FET with “treatment per customary international law”; (b) mandates local‑remedy exhaustion before ISDS; (c) carves out taxation; (d) adds a “right to regulate” preamble. The 2023 India‑UAE CEPA investment chapter mirrors the Model BIT but adds a state‑to‑state track, favouring diplomatic over private arbitration.

The EU’s Investment Court System (ICS), in CETA (2017), establishes a permanent 15‑judge tribunal plus appeal body, addressing ad‑hoc arbitration legitimacy. But ICS is bilateral; UNCITRAL’s multilateral court proposal seeks universal adoption. For India, joining would require aligning the Model BIT’s local‑remedy exhaustion with the court’s procedures. The 2024 UNCITRAL Working Group III session in Vienna aims to finalise the Draft Convention on a Multilateral Investment Court, a key benchmark for future Indian IIAs.

Monetary Policy Spillovers, the Trilemma and Emerging‑Market Capital Flow Management

The Mundell‑Fleming trilemma holds that a country cannot simultaneously fix its exchange rate, allow free capital mobility, and run an independent monetary policy. EMs navigate a continuum. India’s de‑jure regime is a managed float with capital‑account controls (ECB framework, VRR for portfolio debt, FPI equity limits). RBI’s sterilised FX interventions — selling dollars and absorbing rupee liquidity via OMOs — preserve monetary autonomy while curbing excess volatility.

Rey (2013) identified a “global financial cycle” — driven by US policy, risk appetite, and global bank leverage — that transmits to EMs irrespective of exchange‑rate regime. The 2013 “taper tantrum” saw a 100‑bp rise in US 10‑year yields trigger ~15% rupee depreciation, ~200‑bp rise in Indian sovereign yields, and ~$30 bn portfolio outflow in three months. RBI countered with a calibrated mix: (i) 75‑bp policy‑rate hike; (ii) temporary VRR limit increase for FPI debt from 5% to 9%; (iii) $34 bn dollar‑swap window for banks.

The IMF’s Integrated Policy Framework (2020) formalises the toolkit: monetary policy, FX intervention, CFMs, and macroprudential policies. India’s 2022‑23 cycle — a 250‑bp rate‑hike amid US tightening — showcased the IPF. RBI held the rupee within a 2% trade‑weighted band, deployed $45 bn in reserves, and lowered the ECB all‑in‑cost ceiling from 450 to 350 bps. A 2024 IMF Article IV report found India’s CFMs “targeted, temporary, and transparent” per the Institutional View, but cautioned that prolonged sterilised intervention could strain domestic liquidity if reserve growth slows.

  • Confusing the CBAM’s transitional reporting obligations with the financial compliance phase; the former requires only emissions data, the latter requires certificate surrender.
  • Assuming the WTO JSI on e‑commerce creates binding obligations for all members; it is a plurilateral negotiation and only participating members will be bound.
  • Treating the India‑UAE CEPA investment chapter as identical to the 2015 Model BIT; the CEPA adds a state‑to‑state track and modifies the exhaustion‑of‑local‑remedies clause.
  • Believing that the IMF’s Institutional View on CFMs permits permanent capital controls; the View allows only temporary, targeted measures during crisis or surge episodes.
  • Overlooking the distinction between sterilised and unsterilised FX intervention; only sterilised intervention preserves the domestic monetary stance.
  • Equating the UNCITRAL multilateral investment court proposal with the EU’s Investment Court System; the former aims for universal membership, the latter is bilateral.
  • Neglecting the role of the “global financial cycle” in transmitting US monetary shocks to EMs even under a floating regime; the trilemma does not guarantee insulation.

Forward Market Operations and Exchange Rate Expectations Management

The Reserve Bank of India uses the forward segment of the domestic foreign‑exchange market as a primary tool for signalling policy intent and for managing the expectations of importers, exporters, and portfolio investors. Unlike spot interventions, which immediately alter the reserve stock, forward sales or purchases create a future obligation to deliver or receive dollars, allowing the central bank to influence the forward premium — the interest‑rate differential adjusted for expected depreciation — without an instantaneous change in the spot rate. Since the 2013 taper‑tantrum episode, the RBI has maintained a sizable forward book; as of March 2023 the net forward position was approximately $65–70 billion, equivalent to roughly 15 per cent of total reserves. The central bank conducts forward sales through two channels: (i) outright forward contracts with authorised dealers, typically at tenors of one to twelve months, and (ii) dollar‑rupee swap windows that provide banks with dollar liquidity for a predetermined period while locking in the forward rate. The 2013 swap window, announced on 23 August 2013, offered $34 billion of three‑month swaps at a fixed premium, helping cap the one‑month forward premium at around 4.5 per cent and arresting a run‑away depreciation spiral. In 2022‑23, as the Federal Reserve raised rates by 425 basis points, the RBI again leaned on forward sales, adding roughly $25 billion to the forward book between April 2022 and March 2023, which helped contain the one‑year forward premium largely within a 2.5‑3.5 per cent band despite a 10 per cent spot depreciation. The forward premium, in turn, feeds into the pricing of external commercial borrowings (ECBs) and the cost of hedging for corporates, linking the RBI’s market operations directly to the domestic credit cycle. A key constraint is the non‑deliverable forward (NDF) market offshore, which often trades at a discount to the onshore forward curve when capital controls are perceived as binding; persistent onshore‑offshore divergence can undermine the signalling value of RBI forward operations. The central bank monitors the onshore‑offshore basis through daily NDF fixing data and may intervene in the NDF market via state‑owned banks to compress the basis, a practice that the IMF has generally viewed as consistent with its Institutional View on capital‑flow management measures when it is targeted, temporary, and transparent.

Composition of Current Account Deficit Financing and External Vulnerability

The sustainability of a current‑account deficit (CAD) depends less on its headline size than on the composition of the financing flows that cover it. India’s CAD widened to 4.8 per cent of GDP in 2012‑13, financed predominantly by volatile portfolio inflows (net FPI equity and debt) and short‑term external commercial borrowings, leaving the economy exposed to sudden stops. By contrast, the modest deficit of 1.2 per cent of GDP in 2022‑23 was funded largely by net foreign direct investment (FDI) of around $28 billion, resilient remittance inflows exceeding $100 billion, and a surge in non‑resident Indian (NRI) deposits under the Foreign Currency Non‑Resident (FCNR) scheme, which saw significant accretion in the first half of FY23 after the RBI raised the interest‑rate ceiling on FCNR(B) deposits by 75 basis points. The shift toward stable financing is reflected in the “financing‑mix” indicator used by the IMF’s Article IV consultations: the share of non‑debt‑creating flows (FDI + remittances + net NRI deposits) in total CAD financing rose from around 55 per cent in 2013‑14 to around 78 per cent in 2022‑23. A higher share of debt‑creating flows — portfolio debt, ECBs, and short‑term trade credit — raises rollover risk and the sensitivity of the external position to global risk‑aversion shocks. The RBI’s macroprudential framework for ECBs, revised in January 2019, introduced an all‑in‑cost ceiling of 450 basis points over the six‑month LIBOR (later SOFR) and a minimum average maturity of three years for the automatic route, explicitly to lengthen the maturity profile of debt financing. The 2020‑21 pandemic year produced a rare current‑account surplus of 0.9 per cent of GDP, driven by a collapse in merchandise imports and a record remittance inflow of $83 billion; however, the surplus was largely a demand‑side compression rather than a structural improvement, and the subsequent rebound in imports in 2021‑22 re‑exposed the financing‑mix vulnerability. Policymakers therefore track an analytical “net‑stable‑funding ratio” for the external sector — analogous to the Basel III NSFR — by monitoring the ratio of stable foreign‑currency liabilities (FDI, long‑term ECBs, NRI deposits >1 year) to total foreign‑currency liabilities. Estimates suggest this ratio improved from 0.48 in March 2014 to 0.62 in March 2023, reflecting the deliberate policy tilt toward longer‑maturity, non‑debt inflows; a ratio above 0.6 is often considered a comfort threshold.

Corporate Currency Mismatch and Macroprudential Policy Responses

Indian non‑financial corporates have historically borrowed in foreign currency to exploit lower international interest rates, creating a currency mismatch between dollar‑denominated liabilities and rupee‑denominated revenues. As of March 2022, the outstanding external commercial borrowing (ECB) stock of the corporate sector stood at approximately $210 billion, of which an estimated 50–55 per cent was unhedged or partially hedged, according to RBI’s half‑yearly ECB returns. The 2013 taper‑tantrum exposed this vulnerability: a roughly 20 per cent rupee depreciation over a few months increased the rupee‑value of dollar debt commensurately, triggering rating downgrades for several infrastructure firms and a spike in non‑performing assets at banks with large corporate loan books. In response, the RBI tightened the ECB framework in January 2019, mandating a minimum average maturity of three years for the automatic route, an all‑in‑cost ceiling of 450 basis points over the benchmark, and a compulsory hedging requirement for borrowers with a net‑foreign‑currency exposure exceeding $20 million. The 2020 COVID‑19 shock prompted a temporary relaxation — the all‑in‑cost ceiling was lowered to 350 bps and the hedging threshold raised to $50 million — to ease funding conditions, but the RBI reinstated the stricter norms in April 2022 as the US tightening cycle resumed. Complementary macroprudential tools include the “currency‑risk‑weighted assets” charge introduced in the 2021 Basel III implementation for Indian banks, which assigns a 125 per cent risk weight to unhedged foreign‑currency corporate exposures, incentivising banks to demand hedging from borrowers. The RBI also monitors an RBI-constructed “corporate sector foreign‑currency sensitivity index” (CSFCSI), based on quarterly balance‑sheet data of listed firms, which rose from 0.12 in 2015 to 0.18 in 2022, indicating growing aggregate sensitivity. RBI staff studies (2023) suggest that a 10 per cent depreciation raises the default probability for highly unhedged firms by about 3.5 percentage points, while fully hedged firms show no statistically significant effect. The policy lesson is that macroprudential regulation must target the distribution of hedging across the corporate sector, not merely the aggregate ECB stock, because tail‑risk firms can generate systemic spillovers through banking‑sector linkages.

Global Financial Cycle Transmission to Emerging Market Bond Yields

The concept of a global financial cycle, articulated by Hélène Rey (2013), argues that US monetary policy, global risk appetite, and the leverage of global banks drive co‑movement in capital flows and asset prices across emerging markets (EMs), irrespective of their exchange‑rate regimes. Empirical evidence for India shows that a 100‑basis‑point increase in the US 10‑year Treasury yield is associated with a 30‑40 basis‑point rise in the Indian 10‑year sovereign yield within the same quarter, after controlling for domestic inflation and fiscal deficits. The transmission operates through three channels: (i) the portfolio‑rebalancing channel, where foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) sell EM bonds to rebalance toward higher‑yielding US Treasuries; (ii) the banking‑channel, where global banks reduce cross‑border lending to EM corporates and sovereigns as their dollar funding costs rise; and (iii) the expectations channel, where higher US rates shift market expectations of future EM policy rates, steepening the local yield curve. The 2013 taper‑tantrum provides a textbook case: the US 10‑year yield jumped from 1.6 per cent in May 2013 to 3.0 per cent by September 2013, while the Indian 10‑year yield rose from 7.2 per cent to 9.2 per cent, and FPI debt outflows reached $12 billion in a single quarter. The RBI’s calibrated response — a cumulative 75‑basis‑point policy‑rate hike, a temporary increase in the voluntary retention route (VRR) limit for FPI debt from 5 per cent to 9 per cent, and a $34 billion dollar‑swap window — illustrates the IMF’s Integrated Policy Framework (IPF) in action. More recently, the 2022‑23 US tightening cycle (425 bps of Fed hikes) saw the Indian 10‑year yield climb from 6.5 per cent in March 2022 to 7.4 per cent in March 2023, while FPI debt outflows moderated to $5 billion over the same period, reflecting the effectiveness of the VRR and earlier macroprudential measures such as the ECB all‑in‑cost ceiling reduction to 350 bps in 2020. The 2023 IMF Article IV consultation (published 2024) noted that India’s “targeted, temporary, and transparent” capital‑flow management measures (CFMs) have dampened the sensitivity of bond yields to the global financial cycle, but warned that prolonged sterilised intervention could strain domestic liquidity if reserve accumulation slows. The policy implication is that EMs cannot fully insulate their bond markets from the global cycle; they must combine flexible exchange rates, a credible inflation‑targeting framework, and a pre‑announced CFM toolkit to manage the amplitude of spillovers.

Multi‑Polar Reserve Currency System and Emerging Market Reserve Management

The international monetary system is gradually shifting from a unipolar dollar‑centric architecture toward a multi‑polar configuration in which the euro, the Chinese renminbi, the Japanese yen, and the IMF’s Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) play larger roles in global reserves and trade invoicing. The renminbi’s inclusion in the SDR basket in October 2016, with a weight of 10.9 per cent, marked the first addition of an emerging‑market currency since the euro’s launch in 1999. As of end‑2023, the renminbi accounted for roughly 2.5 per cent of allocated global reserves, up from 1.1 per cent in 2016, while the dollar’s share fell from 66 per cent to 58 per cent over the same period. For reserve managers in emerging markets, this diversification offers both risk‑reduction and policy‑autonomy benefits. The Reserve Bank of India has steadily increased the share of non‑dollar assets in its foreign‑exchange reserves: gold holdings rose from 558 tonnes in March 2018 to 795 tonnes in March 2023, raising the gold share from 5.5 per cent to 7.8 per cent of total reserves; SDR holdings grew from $1.5 billion to $4.2 billion after the 2021 general allocation of $650 billion; and the RBI has entered into bilateral currency‑swap arrangements with the central banks of Japan, the UAE, and Sri Lanka, providing access to yen, dirham, and rupee liquidity without drawing on dollar reserves. The 2022‑23 annual report notes that an RBI-estimated “reserve‑currency diversification index” — a Herfindahl‑Hirschman measure of currency concentration — improved from 0.71 in 2015 to 0.58 in 2023, indicating a more balanced portfolio. However, the depth and liquidity of non‑dollar reserve assets remain limited: the offshore renminbi bond market (dim sum bonds) is still a fraction of the US Treasury market, and the euro‑area sovereign bond market carries credit‑risk heterogeneity. Consequently, the RBI is estimated to maintain a core dollar tranche (approximately 50–55 per cent of reserves) to ensure immediate liquidity for intervention and to meet the IMF’s reserve‑adequacy metric, which is calibrated on a basket of major reserve currencies. The policy challenge is to deepen the domestic market for non‑dollar assets — for instance, by encouraging issuance of rupee‑denominated “masala bonds” offshore and by developing a liquid onshore government‑bond curve in euros and yen through the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) at GIFT City — so that diversification does not come at the cost of liquidity or return.
