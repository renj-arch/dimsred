1. Internal Security Challenges — Organised Crime, Terrorism, Cyber, Money Laundering

GS III syllabus (UPSC notification) covers: linkages between organised crime and terrorism; role of external state and non-state actors; challenges to internal security through communication networks, media and social networking sites; basics of cyber security; money-laundering and its prevention; security forces and agencies and their mandate. UPSC GS III Syllabus 2024

1.2 External State and Non-State Actors

Pakistan (ISI): Cross-border terrorism in J&K — infiltration, launch pads, terrorism financing, radicalisation. China: Historical support to Northeast insurgents (NSCN, ULFA — 1980s–90s); current — cyber espionage (APT groups), infrastructure dual-use on LAC, strategic support to Pakistan. Non-state: Daesh/ISIS-K (Afghanistan-Pakistan), Al-Qaeda (AQIS), Khalistan diaspora networks (SFJ, BKI — designated terrorists UAPA 2019–2024). MHA Annual Report 2023; NIA 2024

PYQ 2023 (15 marks): "The nexus between organised crime and terrorism poses a serious threat to national security. Discuss with examples."
Model: (1) Define nexus — convergence of motivation (ideology vs profit), methods (logistics, financing), and actors. (2) Channels — Drugs (Golden Crescent/Triangle → Punjab/Northeast), FICN (Pakistan → Nepal/Bangladesh), Arms (drones, sea), Hawala, Kidnapping. (3) Case studies — Punjab drone drops (2020–24, 500+ drones), Northeast meth/Yaba funding NSCN/ULFA, J&K over-ground workers (OGWs) as logistics bridge. (4) Responses — NIA (UAPA scheduling), NCB (drugs), ED (PMLA), NATGRID, CIBMS, drone detection, international cooperation (FATF, UNSC 1267). (5) Gaps — attribution difficulty, encrypted comms, jurisdictional overlaps, diaspora financing.

1.3 Communication Networks, Social Media, and Radicalisation

Terrorist Use: Encrypted apps (Telegram, Signal, Threema), dark web, social media (propaganda, recruitment, coordination — e.g., ISIS modules Kerala 2016–2022, LeT virtual handlers). Fake News / Rumour Mongering: Lynching incidents (2018–2020), communal mobilisation. Legal Framework: IT Act 2000 — Sec 69A (blocking powers, 14,000+ URLs blocked 2014–2023), Sec 69 (interception), Sec 79 (intermediary liability); IT Rules 2021 — traceability mandate (first originator), grievance officer, compliance reports. MeitY 2021; MHA 2023

Critique: Encryption vs Lawful Access
(1) Traceability mandate (IT Rules 2021 Rule 4(2)) — breaks end-to-end encryption; challenged in WhatsApp v. Union of India (Delhi HC 2021, pending SC). (2) Sec 69A blocking — opaque process; no judicial oversight; RTI exemptions. (3) Data localisation — DPDP Act 2023 allows cross-border transfer (adequacy assessment), but CERT-IN 2022 directions require local logs. (4) Attribution — VPN, Tor, proxy chains; state-sponsored APTs (APT41, Lazarus, SideWinder) use false flags.

1.5 Money Laundering — PMLA, ED, FATF

PMLA 2002 (amended 2005, 2009, 2012, 2015, 2018, 2019) — offence (Sec 3), attachment (Sec 5), prosecution (Sec 45 — twin bail conditions: prima facie not guilty + not likely to commit offence). PMLA 2002; SC Vijay Madanlal 2022 ED (Enforcement Directorate) — investigation, attachment, prosecution; FIU-IND — financial intelligence (STR, CTR); Scheduled Offences — Part A (domestic), Part B (cross-border), Part C (transnational).

FATF (Financial Action Task Force): India member since 2010; Mutual Evaluation Report (2024) — "compliant/largely compliant" on 37/40 recommendations; grey list risk averted. Key Gaps Identified: DNFBP (Designated Non-Financial Businesses and Professions) supervision — real estate, gems/jewellery, lawyers; beneficial ownership registry (Companies Act 2013 Sec 89/90, but enforcement weak); NPO (non-profit) risk-based monitoring. FATF 2024

PYQ 2021 (10 marks): "Money laundering poses a serious threat to economic sovereignty. Discuss the legislative framework and enforcement challenges."
Model: (1) Legislative — PMLA 2002 (Sec 3 offence, Sec 5 attachment, Sec 45 bail), PMLA Rules, FEMA 1999 (cross-border), Black Money Act 2015, Benami Act 1988 (amended 2016), Fugitive Economic Offenders Act 2018. (2) Institutional — ED, FIU-IND, NIA (terror financing), RBI (banking AML), SEBI (securities), IRDAI (insurance). (3) Challenges — Twin bail (SC upheld Vijay Madanlal 2022 but criticism on presumption of guilt), attachment without conviction, ED's expanding mandate (non-scheduled offences via "proceeds of crime" interpretation), federal friction (state police vs ED), FATF DNFBP gap, crypto/VASP regulation nascent. (4) Data — ₹1.1L cr attached (2014–23), conviction rate <5%.

2.2 Coastal and Maritime Security

Post-26/11 architecture: Indian Coast Guard (maritime law enforcement, SAR, pollution); Coastal Police (state, 12 nm); Sagar Prahari Bal (Navy, force protection); National Committee for Strengthening Maritime and Coastal Security (NCSMCS) — Cabinet Secretary chaired. Technical: Coastal Radar Chain (46 stations), VTS (Vessel Traffic Surveillance), AIS (Automatic Identification System), biometric ID for 2.5M+ fishermen, space-based AIS (ISRO). Island Territories: Andaman & Nicobar Command (ANC) — only tri-service theatre command; Lakshadweep — naval detachment. MHA 2023; ICG 2023

Critique: Border Management Gaps
(1) LAC — no delineation; infrastructure deficit vs China (G219 highway, dual-use villages, 5G); BTTM/WMCC are confidence-building, not dispute resolution. (2) Myanmar — FMR review stalled; insurgent sanctuaries persist; fencing 10% only; Assam Rifles dual control (MHA ops, Army admin) creates friction. (3) Nepal — open border treaty (1950) limits hardening; FICN, trafficking exploit trust. (4) Coastal — marine police understaffed (sanctioned 20,000, filled ~60%); inter-agency coordination (ICG, Navy, Customs, State) ad hoc; island security (ANC) under-resourced. (5) Technology — CIBMS pilot only; drone detection limited; satellite surveillance gaps.
PYQ 2022 (15 marks): "Border management is a complex task due to difficult terrain and hostile neighbours. Discuss the challenges and strategies for effective border management."
Answer: (1) Challenges — Terrain (Himalayas, deserts, riverine, jungles); Hostile neighbours (Pakistan proxy war, China salami-slicing); Porous borders (Nepal open, Myanmar FMR, Bangladesh riverine); Non-state actors (insurgents, smugglers, traffickers); Technology asymmetry (drones, tunnels, cyber). (2) Strategies — Institutional (BSF/ITBP/SSB/Assam Rifles mandates clarified); Infrastructure (roads, tunnels, fencing, floodlights, BOPs); Technology (CIBMS, radar, satellite, drones, biometrics); Diplomatic (BTTM/WMCC, LBA, boundary commissions, joint patrolling); Development (Vibrant Villages, Border Area Development Programme, border haats); Legal (Anti-trafficking, NDPS, Customs, UAPA). (3) Whole-of-government — NCSMCS, NATGRID, MHA coordination.
Source: UPSC Mains 2022 GS III Q5; MHA Annual Reports PYQ 2022

4. Regional Security Challenges — J&K, LWE, Northeast

4.1 Jammu & Kashmir

Post-2019 Architecture: Article 370 abrogation (Aug 2019), Reorganisation Act 2019 — UTs J&K (with legislature) and Ladakh (without). Security Dynamics: Infiltration (launch pads PoK), OGW networks, targeted killings (minorities, migrant workers, local politicians), hybrid terrorism (pistols, IEDs, drones), stone-pelting (declined), AFSPA (disturbed areas — entire J&K except few districts). Political Process: Delimitation Commission (2022), elections 2024 (first since 2014), statehood demand. MHA 2024; J&K Reorganisation Act 2019

Critique: J&K Security-Paradigm
(1) Security gains — infiltration down (141 in 2018 → ~30 in 2023), local recruitment down, OGW network disrupted. (2) Alienation — democratic vacuum (no elected govt 2018–2024), media restrictions, PSA detentions, youth radicalisation (hybrid terrorism). (3) Strategic — Pakistan proxy war persists (drones, tunnels, LoC ceasefire 2021 fragile); China factor (Ladakh). (4) Way forward — Statehood restoration, political engagement, economic package (industrial policy, tourism), de-radicalisation, police modernisation (J&K Police led CT).

Answer-Writing Framework for Internal Security (10/15 marks)
Deconstruct: Identify theme (terrorism/LWE/border/cyber/legal) and directive (analyse/discuss/examine/critically evaluate).
Thesis: One-sentence position — e.g., "India's internal security architecture has achieved tactical successes but structural gaps in coordination, legal safeguards, and federal balance persist."
Threat Matrix: Categorise — External (Pakistan/China proxy), Internal (LWE, Northeast, communal), Transnational (cyber, drugs, terror financing), Emerging (drone, AI, crypto).
Institutional-Legal Layer: Cite specific Act, Section, Agency, Force (UAPA Sec 43D, AFSPA Sec 4/6, PMLA Sec 45, NIA Act, BSF Act, IT Act Sec 69A).
Data Anchors: 2–3 verifiable numbers — incidents, surrenders, border length, conviction rate, budget, FATF rating.
Critique: 2–3 structural gaps — federalism (police State List), human rights (AFSPA/NSA/UAPA), coordination (MAC/NATGRID/NCORD overlap), capacity (police vacancies 20%, cyber skills).
Way Forward: Specific — not "strengthen coordination" but "operationalise NATGRID with 21 databases; legislate IB/RAW charter; amend UAPA bail to allow constitutional court discretion; implement Prakash Singh police reforms."
Conclusion: Tie to constitutional mandate (Article 355, 356), national security doctrine, democratic legitimacy.

Common Traps

  • Confusing LoC (Pakistan, J&K, 740 km, militarised) with LAC (China, 3,488 km, undemarcated, differing perceptions). MHA 2023
  • Assuming CAPFs and Army share ministry — CAPFs (CRPF, BSF, ITBP, SSB, CISF, NSG) under MHA; Army/Navy/Air Force under MoD; Coast Guard under MoD; Assam Rifles dual (MHA ops, Army admin). MHA 2023
  • Thinking UAPA allows indefinite detention — max pre-charge 180 days (Sec 43D(2)); bail barred but not absolute (SC K.A. Najeeb 2021 — Article 21 override). SC 2021
  • Missing AFSPA applies only to "disturbed areas" declared by Centre/Governor (not whole state); currently in parts of Nagaland, Manipur, Assam, Arunachal, J&K. MHA 2024
  • Overlooking coastal security involves Navy (maritime defence), ICG (constabulary, 200 nm), State Marine Police (12 nm), Customs, Fisheries — coordination via NCSMCS. MHA 2023
  • Confusing NIA (terror investigation, UAPA) with CBI (corruption, special crimes, consent required) — NIA has suo motu jurisdiction for scheduled offences; CBI needs state consent (Sec 6 DSPE Act). NIA Act 2008
  • Thinking PMLA conviction rate is high — <5% (ED data 2014–23); attachment ≠ conviction. ED 2023

5.2 LWE — Operational Architecture of CPI(Maoist)

Organisational Structure: Central Committee (CC) — Politburo, Central Military Commission (CMC); Regional Bureaus — Dandakaranya (DK), Bihar-Jharkhand (BJ), Odisha (O), Maharashtra-Madhya Pradesh-Chhattisgarh (MMC), West Bengal; Divisional/Sub-divisional Committees; PLGA (People's Liberation Guerrilla Army) — Companies, Platoons, Squads; Front Organisations — Revolutionary Democratic Front (RDF), CPI(Maoist) cultural troupes, student/youth/women fronts. Communication: HF/VHF radio sets, satellite phones, courier network, coded messages.

8.2 LWE — Operational Case Studies: Successes, Failures, and Lessons

Case Study 1: Operation Prahaar (Bastar, 2017–2024) — Sustained Offensive in Core
Context: Dandakaranya (DK) — CPI(Maoist) heartland; 40% of national LWE incidents. Strategy: CRPF CoBRA (10 Bns) + State SF (DRG, STF, Bastar Fighters) — forward operating bases (FOBs) deep in jungle (e.g., Tekalgudiam, Minpa, Chintagufa); UAV/ISR + DAS perimeter; humint from surrenderees; targeted leadership strikes. Results: 2017–2024: 300+ neutralised, 500+ surrendered, 15 FOBs established, 500+ km roads (RRP-II), 300+ mobile towers. Costs: 150+ SF martyred (IEDs 60%); Minpa ambush (2021, 22 CoBRA); Tekalgudiam (2023, 10 DRG). Lesson: FOBs deny sanctuary but require sustained logistics, casualty evacuation (helicopter), and political will. Development must follow security within 6 months (PESA, FRA, banking, health) or alienation recurs.
Source: MHA 2024; CRPF Annual Reports; "Operation Prahaar" internal assessments CRPF 2024
Case Study 2: Greyhounds Model (Andhra/Telangana, 1989–Present) — The Gold Standard
Genesis: Raised 1989 (undivided AP) for anti-Naxal ops; 2,500 personnel; voluntary, rigorous selection (endurance, jungle craft, firing). Doctrine: "Go to the enemy" — long-range patrols (15–20 days), no static posts, humint-centric (tribal trackers), zero collateral damage culture. Achievements: Undivided AP: LWE incidents 500+ (1990s) → <20 (2020); top leadership neutralised (Cherukuri Rajkumar "Azad" 2010, Narmada 2012); surrenders 5,000+. Replicable Elements: (1) Autonomous command (DIG Greyhounds reports to DG); (2) Career incentives (promotion, allowances); (3) Tribal recruitment (Chenchu, Koya) — cultural fluency; (4) Intelligence fusion (SIB + Greyhounds own humint); (5) No politicised transfers. Gap: Telangana/AP bifurcation (2014) split force; Chhattisgarh/Odisha attempted replication (DRG, STF, SOG) but lack autonomy, career path, tribal integration.

Case Study 3: Saranda (Jharkhand) — Development-First Experiment (2011–2016) and Relapse
Experiment: Saranda (West Singhbhum) — "Saranda Action Plan" (2011): 7,000+ FRA titles, 500 km roads, 50 schools, 30 health centres, banking, livelihoods (lac, tasar); security: CRPF camps + Jharkhand Jaguar. Question (15 marks): "The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 has been amended multiple times to strengthen counter-terrorism. Critically examine the tension between security imperatives and civil liberties under UAPA, with reference to judicial interventions and conviction data."
Model Structure:
Introduction (40 words): UAPA 1967 (amended 2004, 2008, 2012, 2019) is India's primary anti-terror law. 2019 amendment — individual terrorist designation (Sec 35), 180-day pre-charge detention (Sec 43D), stringent bail (Sec 43D(5)). Core tension: preventive security vs due process.

Body Paragraph 1 — Security Rationale (80 words): Cross-border terrorism (J&K), urban modules (ISIS, LeT), terror financing, OGW networks. Legislative intent — "effective prevention" (Statement of Objects 2019); NIA empowerment (suo motu, inter-state). Data — 5,000+ arrests (2019–23), 40+ organisations/individuals designated.

Body Paragraph 2 — Civil Liberties Concerns (100 words): (a) Individual designation without trial — violates presumption of innocence, no hearing (Sec 35); (b) 180-day detention without charge sheet — CrPC max 90 days; (c) Bail bar (Sec 43D(5)) — "not prima facie guilty" reverses burden; (d) Vague definitions — "terrorist act" (Sec 15) covers property damage, disruption; (e) Misuse — Bhima Koregaon (activists), Delhi riots (students), Kashmir journalists (Sajad Gul, Fahad Shah), tribal leaders (Stan Swamy death in custody 2021).

Body Paragraph 3 — Judicial Interventions (100 words): (1) Watali (SC 2019) — upheld strict bail; "prima facie" on prosecution case. (2) K.A. Najeeb (SC 2021) — constitutional courts can grant bail under Article 21 despite Sec 43D(5) if trial delayed; "constitutional courts not powerless." (3) Thwai Thwai (SC 2023) — bail for elderly/ill despite UAPA. (4) High Courts — increasing bail grants (Delhi HC 2022–24, Bombay HC 2023) citing Najeeb. (5) Gap — trial delay average 5+ years; SC guidelines (2023) for speedy trial not enforced.

Body Paragraph 4 — Conviction Data and Structural Gaps (80 words): Conviction rate ~2% (NCRB 2022); 98% undertrials. Causes — evidence quality (Sec 50 statement admissibility challenged), witness protection absent, NIA capacity (300+ cases, ~800 officers), federal friction (state police vs NIA). Compare — TADA (1985–95) conviction 1%, repealed; POTA (2002–04) similar.

Body Paragraph 5 — Roadmap (60 words): (1) Amend Sec 35 — designate only post-charge sheet or judicial order; (2) Cap pre-charge detention at 90 days (align CrPC); (3) Bail reform — replace "prima facie" with "reasonable grounds" + Article 21 discretion codified; (4) Sunset clause — periodic legislative review; (5) Witness protection law; (6) Fast-track NIA courts (dedicated, time-bound).

Conclusion (40 words): UAPA is necessary but current form tilts toward executive overreach. Security without liberty is fragile; liberty without security is illusory. The Najeeb jurisprudence offers a constitutional bridge — Parliament must now legislate it.
