1. Gender Inequality and Women’s Empowerment in Contemporary India

1.1 Historical Roots of Gender Disparities

Gender inequality in India is not a recent phenomenon; it is embedded in social structures that have evolved over millennia. Patriarchal norms were reinforced by customary laws such as the Manusmriti, which prescribed distinct roles for men and women, limiting women’s agency to the domestic sphere. Colonial legal reforms, while introducing concepts like the abolition of sati, often retained the underlying bias by codifying personal laws that continued to privilege men in matters of inheritance, marriage and divorce. The legacy of these historical practices is evident in contemporary indicators such as the skewed sex ratio at birth and the persistent gender gap in literacy.

1.2 Constitutional and Legislative Framework

The Constitution of India guarantees equality before the law (Article 14) and prohibits discrimination on the basis of sex (Article 15). Over the decades, Parliament has enacted several statutes aimed at redressing gender inequities. The Constitution (Eighty‑first Amendment) Act, 2004 introduced the “right to education” for children, which has been instrumental in increasing girls’ enrolment. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 provides civil remedies for survivors of abuse, while the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 establishes mechanisms for complaint and redressal. These legal instruments create a normative framework, but their effectiveness depends on implementation, awareness and societal acceptance.

1.3 Socio‑Economic Dimensions of Women’s Status

Women’s participation in the labour force remains low compared with men, hovering around 20 % in recent surveys. This disparity is driven by multiple factors: limited access to quality education, cultural expectations that prioritize household responsibilities, and occupational segregation that channels women into low‑paid, informal sectors such as domestic work and garment manufacturing. The gender wage gap, estimated at 20‑30 % in the organized sector, reflects both overt discrimination and the undervaluation of work traditionally performed by women. Moreover, women’s ownership of land and other productive assets is minimal, restricting their economic autonomy and bargaining power within households.

1.4 Health and Safety Concerns

Maternal mortality, though declining, remains a critical indicator of gendered health inequities. Limited access to quality antenatal care, especially in rural and tribal areas, contributes to higher rates of complications during childbirth. Violence against women, encompassing domestic abuse, sexual assault and trafficking, is pervasive. The National Crime Records Bureau reports a steady rise in reported cases of crimes against women, suggesting both an increase in incidence and greater willingness to report. The intersection of caste, class and geography amplifies vulnerability, with Dalit and tribal women facing higher risks of exploitation.

1.5 Emerging Opportunities and Policy Initiatives

Recent policy initiatives aim to harness the demographic dividend by empowering women. The Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao campaign seeks to improve the child sex ratio and promote girls’ education through awareness programmes and incentives for school attendance. The Mahila E‑Shakti programme provides micro‑credit and skill‑development training to women entrepreneurs, facilitating entry into the formal economy. Additionally, the National Digital Literacy Mission includes a gender‑sensitive component that encourages women’s participation in digital platforms, thereby expanding access to information, services and markets.

2. Urbanisation: Patterns, Drivers and Emerging Challenges

2.1 Scale and Pace of Urban Growth

India’s urban population has risen from roughly 27 % of the total in the early 1990s to over 35 % in the latest census. This acceleration is driven by both natural increase and rural‑to‑urban migration. Metropolitan regions such as Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru and Hyderabad have expanded beyond administrative boundaries, forming megacities with populations exceeding ten million. The phenomenon of “urban agglomeration” reflects the functional integration of core cities with surrounding peri‑urban and satellite towns, creating complex spatial hierarchies.

2.2 Push and Pull Factors of Migration

Economic opportunities in the formal and informal sectors act as strong pull factors, attracting migrants seeking higher wages and better living standards. Simultaneously, push factors such as agrarian distress, declining rural employment due to mechanisation, and climate‑induced hazards compel people to leave villages. The resulting demographic shift reshapes labour markets, with a growing share of the workforce engaged in services, construction and manufacturing within urban centres.

2.3 Infrastructure Strain and Service Deficits

Rapid urbanisation outpaces the capacity of municipal bodies to provide essential services. In many cities, water supply is intermittent, sanitation coverage is inadequate and solid‑waste management is inefficient. The prevalence of informal settlements, commonly known as slums, illustrates the mismatch between housing demand and supply. Residents of these settlements often lack legal title, limiting access to credit and government schemes, while also facing health hazards due to overcrowding and poor sanitation.

2.4 Environmental and Health Implications

Urban areas contribute disproportionately to air pollution, with vehicular emissions and industrial activities raising concentrations of particulate matter (PM2.5) beyond safe limits. The urban heat island effect, resulting from extensive concrete surfaces, exacerbates temperature extremes, increasing energy demand for cooling. Moreover, the concentration of people in dense environments facilitates the rapid spread of communicable diseases, as witnessed during recent pandemics.

2.5 Governance and Planning Responses

Effective urban governance requires coordinated planning across multiple jurisdictions. The 74th Constitutional Amendment introduced the concept of Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) with elected representatives, aiming to decentralise decision‑making. Initiatives such as the Smart Cities Mission and the Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT) focus on infrastructure upgrades, digital integration and sustainable service delivery. However, challenges persist in ensuring financial autonomy for ULBs, integrating informal economies and fostering participatory planning that includes marginalized urban residents.

2.6 Future Trajectories of Urbanisation

Projections suggest that by 2035, more than half of India’s population will reside in urban areas. This trajectory underscores the need for resilient urban ecosystems that can accommodate growth while mitigating environmental degradation. Concepts such as “compact cities” advocate for higher density development coupled with mixed‑use zoning, reducing commute distances and preserving peri‑urban agricultural land. Integrating climate‑smart infrastructure, promoting public transport and enhancing green spaces are essential strategies to ensure that urbanisation contributes positively to sustainable development.

Example Question

Explain how rapid urbanisation has contributed to the rise of informal settlements in Indian cities. Discuss two policy measures that can improve living conditions in these settlements.

Answer: Rapid urbanisation creates a surge in demand for affordable housing that outstrips the supply of formal residential units. Migrants, often lacking the financial resources and legal documentation required for formal housing, resort to occupying vacant land or dilapidated structures, leading to the proliferation of informal settlements. These settlements are characterised by inadequate access to water, sanitation, electricity and legal tenure, which perpetuates cycles of poverty and health hazards.

Two policy measures that can improve conditions are:

  1. In‑situ upgrading: Municipal authorities can regularise land tenure, provide basic services such as piped water, sewage connections and waste collection, and improve housing quality through incremental building schemes. This approach preserves the social networks of residents while enhancing health and safety.
  2. Provision of affordable housing: Implementing schemes like the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban) with a focus on low‑income groups can increase the stock of subsidised housing. Coupling this with rent‑to‑own models and easy credit facilities encourages formal sector participation and reduces the pressure on informal settlements.

Both measures require coordinated action between central, state and local governments, as well as active community participation to ensure sustainability.

Exam‑Day Insight

When addressing a question on urbanisation, link the phenomenon to at least two distinct dimensions—economic (e.g., labour market shifts) and environmental (e.g., pollution). Demonstrating this multidimensional analysis earns higher marks than a single‑aspect description.

3. Poverty and Deprivation: Dimensions, Measurement and Policy Responses

3.1 Conceptualising Poverty

Poverty is a multidimensional condition that extends beyond income insufficiency. It encompasses lack of access to education, health services, clean water, sanitation, and secure tenure. The World Bank’s poverty line, defined in terms of purchasing power parity (PPP) at US$2.15 per day (2022), captures extreme monetary deprivation, while the United Nations’ Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) integrates health, education and living standards indicators to provide a broader perspective.

3.2 Trends in Poverty Reduction

Since the early 1990s, India has witnessed a substantial decline in the proportion of the population living below the national poverty line, falling from around 45 % to below 10 % according to the latest estimates. This reduction is attributed to economic growth, expansion of the service sector, and targeted poverty alleviation programmes. However, the decline is uneven across states; some high‑growth states still exhibit significant pockets of deprivation, especially among Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other marginalised groups.

3.3 Structural Causes of Persistent Poverty

Structural factors that sustain poverty include agrarian distress, limited access to credit, inadequate skill development, and social exclusion. Small and marginal farmers often face low productivity due to fragmented land holdings and lack of irrigation, leading to seasonal income volatility. The informal sector, which employs a large share of the poor, offers precarious employment without social security benefits, making households vulnerable to shocks.

3.4 Measurement Challenges

Accurate measurement of poverty is hampered by data gaps, especially in capturing informal economies and non‑monetary deprivations. Household surveys such as the National Sample Survey (NSS) provide income and consumption data, but infrequent rounds limit timeliness. Moreover, the choice of poverty line influences the reported incidence; a higher threshold yields a larger poverty headcount, affecting policy prioritisation.

3.5 Major Poverty Alleviation Schemes

Several flagship programmes aim to reduce poverty and improve livelihoods. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) guarantees 100 days of wage employment per household per year, providing a safety net and creating rural assets. The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) promotes financial inclusion by opening bank accounts for the unbanked, facilitating direct benefit transfers. The National Rural Livelihood Mission (NRLM) focuses on organising women’s self‑help groups to enhance income generation and access to credit.

3.6 Evaluating Impact and Way Forward

While these schemes have contributed to income smoothing and asset creation, challenges remain in ensuring quality of employment, timely wage payments and effective monitoring. Strengthening data systems, integrating skill development with employment guarantees, and promoting inclusive growth through progressive taxation can enhance poverty reduction outcomes.

4. Social Impact of Globalisation: Opportunities and Challenges

4.1 Defining Globalisation in the Indian Context

Globalisation refers to the increasing integration of economies, cultures and societies through trade, investment, technology and migration. In India, the liberalisation reforms of 1991 marked a decisive shift from a closed, import‑substituting model to an open market economy, facilitating greater participation in global value chains. This transition has accelerated the flow of capital, ideas and people across borders.

4.2 Economic Opportunities and Social Mobility

Integration with global markets has expanded export‑oriented industries, particularly in information technology, pharmaceuticals and textiles. The resultant job creation has enabled upward social mobility for many, especially among the urban middle class. Remittances from the Indian diaspora, one of the largest in the world, provide a steady source of foreign exchange and have improved household consumption patterns, education enrolment and health outcomes in receiving families.

4.3 Cultural Diffusion and Identity Negotiation

Exposure to global media, consumer goods and lifestyles has reshaped cultural preferences, especially among youth. While this has fostered a more cosmopolitan outlook, it has also sparked debates over cultural homogenisation and the erosion of traditional values. The rise of hybrid cultural forms—such as fusion cuisine, contemporary Indian cinema with global aesthetics, and the adoption of English as a lingua franca—illustrates the dynamic negotiation of identity in a globalised milieu.

4.4 Inequality and Social Dislocation

Globalisation’s benefits have been unevenly distributed. High‑skill workers in sectors linked to export markets have reaped substantial wage gains, whereas low‑skill labour, particularly in informal manufacturing and agriculture, has faced wage stagnation or decline due to competition from cheaper imports. This divergence contributes to widening income inequality, reflected in the Gini coefficient’s upward trend. Moreover, rapid urbanisation driven by globalised industries has displaced rural communities, leading to loss of livelihoods and cultural dislocation.

4.5 Labour Rights and Migration

International migration, both inbound and outbound, has intensified. While overseas employment offers higher earnings, migrant workers often encounter precarious working conditions, limited legal protection and social isolation. Domestically, the gig economy—facilitated by digital platforms—has created flexible employment opportunities but also raised concerns about job security, lack of social benefits and algorithmic exploitation.

4.6 Governance Responses to Globalisation

To harness globalisation while mitigating its adverse effects, the government has pursued policies aimed at inclusive growth. The Make in India initiative seeks to boost domestic manufacturing, creating jobs and reducing dependence on imports. Skill development programmes such as the Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) aim to equip the workforce with competencies demanded by global value chains. At the same time, social protection schemes—including the Employees’ Provident Fund (EPF) and the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP)—are being expanded to cover informal workers and migrants.

Exam‑Day Insight

When discussing globalisation, always link an economic benefit (e.g., export growth) with a social consequence (e.g., rising urban inequality). This cause‑effect pairing demonstrates depth of analysis.

5. Interlinkages among Gender, Urbanisation, Poverty and Globalisation

5.1 Gendered Dimensions of Urbanisation

Urban migration often involves women moving as part of household relocation or seeking employment in the informal sector. In cities, women face safety concerns, limited access to affordable childcare and gender‑biased labour markets, which can perpetuate economic dependency. Urban planning that incorporates gender‑sensitive public spaces, safe transport and accessible childcare facilities can mitigate these challenges.

5.2 Poverty Traps in Urban Slums

Urban slums epitomise the convergence of poverty and inadequate infrastructure. Residents experience a “poverty trap” where low incomes restrict investment in health and education, which in turn hampers earning potential. Access to micro‑finance, skill training and legalisation of land tenure are critical interventions to break this cycle.

5.3 Globalisation’s Influence on Gender and Poverty

Global supply chains have created employment opportunities for women in sectors such as garment manufacturing and call centres. While these jobs provide income, they often involve low wages, irregular hours and limited social security, reinforcing gendered poverty. Policies that enforce labour standards, promote fair wages and support women’s entrepreneurship can transform these opportunities into pathways out of poverty.

5.4 Policy Synergy for Inclusive Development

Addressing these interlinked issues requires coordinated policy frameworks. For instance, integrating women’s skill development programmes within urban employment schemes can simultaneously tackle gender disparity, urban unemployment and poverty. Similarly, leveraging remittances through financial inclusion initiatives can channel resources into community development, enhancing urban infrastructure and social services.

5.5 Future Outlook

India’s demographic dividend, coupled with ongoing globalisation, presents both opportunities and risks. Sustainable urbanisation, gender‑responsive policies and inclusive poverty alleviation strategies will determine whether the nation can translate economic growth into equitable social progress. Continuous monitoring, data‑driven policymaking and active civil society participation are essential to navigate this complex landscape.

Feminist Legal Reforms and Judicial Activism Since the 1990s

The period after economic liberalisation saw a surge in feminist litigation that reshaped the constitutional guarantee of equality under Articles 14, 15 and 21. The Supreme Court’s Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) judgment filled a legislative vacuum by laying down guidelines for prevention of sexual harassment at the workplace, later codified in the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, enacted after the Nirbhaya case, expanded the definition of rape, introduced acid‑attack provisions, and mandated minimum sentences, reflecting a shift from protective to deterrent jurisprudence. In Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) the Court declared instant triple talaq unconstitutional, invoking the doctrine of essential religious practices and gender justice. The Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018) verdict on Sabarimala opened temple entry to women of menstruating age, interpreting Article 25 in harmony with Article 15(1). More recently, the Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) decision decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, extending dignity jurisprudence to LGBTQ+ persons. These judgments illustrate a pattern: the judiciary often acts as a catalyst, prompting Parliament to enact or amend statutes, while also setting enforceable standards for state agencies. However, implementation gaps persist — compliance with the 2013 workplace law remains uneven in the informal sector, and the 2019 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act has been criticised for diluting self‑identification principles established in NALSA v. Union of India (2014).

Smart Cities Mission: Governance, Financing and Inclusion Challenges

Launched in June 2015, the Smart Cities Mission (SCM) selected 100 cities through a competitive challenge, each required to form a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) headed by a CEO with powers akin to a municipal commissioner. The financing model blends central assistance (₹500 crore per city over five years), state matching funds, and leveraged private investment through public‑private partnerships (PPPs) and municipal bonds. By 2023, over ₹2 lakh crore had been sanctioned, yet utilisation rates hovered around 60 % due to land‑acquisition delays, procurement bottlenecks, and limited capacity of urban local bodies (ULBs). Governance criticism centres on the SPV’s parallel structure, which bypasses elected councils and weakens democratic accountability. Inclusion concerns arise because area‑based development (ABD) projects often target affluent neighbourhoods, leaving slum pockets — home to about 17 % of the urban population — largely untouched. The Urban Learning Internship Programme (TULIP) and the Data Smart Cities initiative aim to build human capital and open data ecosystems, but digital divides persist: the 2020‑21 NSSO survey reported that only about 38 % of slum households had internet access. The mission’s alignment with Sustainable Development Goal 11 (sustainable cities) remains aspirational unless ULBs are empowered with fiscal autonomy, participatory planning wards, and mandatory social audits for every ABD project.

Comparative Analysis Technique
Question: Compare the governance architecture and sectoral focus of the Smart Cities Mission (SCM) and the Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT).
Answer: SCM adopts a city‑level SPV headed by a CEO, operating outside the elected municipal council, and concentrates on area‑based redevelopment, smart infrastructure (ICT, mobility, energy) and pan‑city solutions. AMRUT, by contrast, works through existing ULBs with state‑level steering committees, focusing on universal coverage of basic services — water supply, sewerage, storm‑water drainage, urban transport and green spaces — across 500 cities. SCM’s financing relies heavily on PPPs and municipal bonds; AMRUT follows a centrally sponsored scheme pattern with 50 % central share for non‑North‑East states. In examinations, link SCM’s “smart” label to technology‑driven pilots and AMRUT’s “basic services” label to SDG 6 and 11 targets, and note that SCM’s exclusion of slums is a frequent critique while AMRUT aims for 100 % household tap connections.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “urban governance reforms”, structure the answer around three pillars: institutional (SPV vs ULB), financial (central‑state‑private mix), and inclusion (slum‑level indicators). Cite the 2020‑21 NSSO digital‑access data and the 2023 utilisation rate to demonstrate evidence‑based analysis.

Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) and Targeted Interventions

India’s adoption of the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), developed by the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) and UNDP, marked a shift from income‑only metrics to a deprivation‑counting approach. The national MPI uses ten indicators across three dimensions — health (nutrition, child mortality), education (years of schooling, school attendance) and standard of living (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets). A household is multidimensionally poor if it is deprived in at least one‑third of the weighted indicators. The 2015‑16 baseline (NFHS‑4) placed the headcount ratio at 27.9 %; the 2019‑21 round (NFHS‑5) showed a decline to 16.4 %, with the government estimating that roughly 270 million people exited multidimensional poverty. The Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP), launched in 2018, uses MPI sub‑indices to rank 112 districts and directs convergent central‑state resources — health, nutrition, education, agriculture, financial inclusion — to the lowest‑performing blocks. Real‑time dashboards track 49 key performance indicators, enabling data‑driven course correction. However, methodological debates persist: the equal‑weighting scheme may mask severe deprivations in nutrition, and the MPI does not capture intra‑household inequality, especially gendered food allocation. Policy analysts recommend complementing MPI with the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) anthropometric data and the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) to design gender‑responsive cash‑transfer top‑ups under the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY).

Global Value Chains, Labour Standards and the Informal Workforce

India’s integration into Global Value Chains (GVCs) — particularly in textiles, automotive components, electronics assembly and pharmaceuticals — has created export‑oriented employment but also entrenched a dual labour market. The Code on Wages, 2019, the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 and the Social Security Code, 2020 consolidate 29 central labour laws, extending minimum‑wage coverage to all establishments, mandating appointment letters, and providing a universal social‑security framework for gig and platform workers. Yet, the International Labour Organization (ILO) estimates that over 90 % of Indian workers remain in the informal economy, with women constituting a majority of informal non‑agricultural employment. In the garment GVC, brands enforce Social Compliance Audits (e.g., amfori BSCI, Sedex) that often stop at first‑tier suppliers; sub‑contracting to home‑based workers — predominantly women — evades oversight, resulting in piece‑rate wages below the statutory floor. The Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for electronics (2021) conditions incentives on domestic value addition and employment generation, but compliance monitoring relies on self‑certification. Strengthening the Labour Inspection System through digitised inspection modules, mandatory registration of all sub‑contractors on the e‑Shram portal, and aligning the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) framework with the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights are essential to translate GVC participation into decent work.

Intersectionality of Caste, Gender and Urban Poverty: Evidence from Slum Surveys

Empirical work from the 78th Round of the National Sample Survey (NSS) (2020‑21) and the Urban Slum Surveys (2012, 2018) reveals that caste, gender and location intersect to produce compounded deprivation. Scheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) households constitute about 38 % of slum residents but account for 55 % of those lacking piped water and 62 % without individual toilets. Studies indicate significantly higher incidence of severe food insecurity among female‑headed SC households compared with male‑headed non‑SC households. Occupational segregation persists: a large majority of SC women in slums are engaged in domestic work, waste picking or home‑based piece‑rate stitching, occupations excluded from the Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) and Provident Fund (EPF) coverage. The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana – Urban (PMAY‑U) provides housing subsidies, yet land‑title requirements disproportionately exclude SC/ST families lacking formal tenure. The National Urban Livelihoods Mission (NULM) includes a “Social Mobilisation and Institution Development” component that forms Self‑Help Groups (SHGs) of women, but caste‑based social networks often limit cross‑caste collectivisation. Policy design must therefore adopt an intersectional lens: earmark a quota of PMAY‑U units for SC/ST female‑headed households, extend ESI/EPF to domestic workers through the Code on Social Security, 2020, and mandate disaggregated monitoring of NULM outcomes by caste and gender in the Urban Management Information System (UMIS).

Women’s Participation in the Gig Economy: Legal Gaps and Policy Experiments

The rapid expansion of platform‑based work — ride‑hailing, food delivery, freelance coding, and home‑based piece‑rate tasks — has created a new frontier for female labour force participation. The Code on Social Security, 2020, for the first time defines “gig worker” and “platform worker” and extends the possibility of registration on the e‑Shram portal, yet the threshold for mandatory contribution remains linked to a minimum wage ceiling that many women in home‑based stitching or data‑annotation never reach. The Code on Wages, 2019, guarantees a national floor wage, but enforcement in the platform ecosystem relies on self‑declaration by aggregators, leaving a compliance gap that the Labour Inspection System, still largely paper‑based, cannot close.

Judicial pronouncements have begun to recognise the employment‑like character of platform work. In 2022 the Supreme Court held that the relationship between a platform and its workers exhibits sufficient control to attract the protections of the Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948, for those who meet the “employee” test. However, the ruling stopped short of a blanket classification, leaving the majority of women gig workers — often engaged through sub‑contracting layers — outside statutory cover. The Ministry of Labour’s 2023 draft “Platform Workers (Social Security) Rules” propose a contributory fund financed by a 1‑2 % levy on platform turnover, but the rules have not been notified, and the definition of “turnover” excludes commissions paid to third‑party logistics providers, a common structure in food‑delivery chains.

State‑level experiments provide evidence of what works. Kerala’s “Kudumbashree” network partnered with a ride‑hailing firm to offer insurance and skill‑upgradation vouchers to women drivers, resulting in a 12 % increase in female driver retention over eighteen months. Tamil Nadu’s “Unorganised Workers Welfare Board” extended accident cover to home‑based apparel workers linked to export‑oriented platforms, financed through a cess on export turnover. These pilots demonstrate that portable benefits, decoupled from a single employer, can be administered through existing Self‑Help Group federations.

Policy design must address three structural barriers: (1) the absence of a universal, portable social‑security account that follows the worker across platforms; (2) the lack of gender‑disaggregated data on platform earnings, which hampers targeted cash‑transfer top‑ups under the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana; and (3) the persistence of algorithmic scheduling that penalises care‑related absences, disproportionately affecting women. Integrating the e‑Shram database with the National Career Service and the Unified Payments Interface can enable real‑time benefit portability, while mandating algorithmic transparency audits under the proposed Digital Personal Data Protection Act would curb discriminatory scheduling.

Climate Resilience and Urban Informal Settlements: Adaptation Finance and Community‑Led Planning

India’s urban population is projected to exceed 600 million by 2031, with a disproportionate share residing in informal settlements that lack storm‑water drainage, secure tenure, and heat‑resilient housing. The National Mission for Sustainable Habitat (2021‑2030) identifies “climate‑resilient slum upgrading” as a priority, yet the financing architecture remains fragmented. The Urban Climate Change Resilience Trust Fund, capitalised at ₹5,000 crore through the 15th Finance Commission’s grant‑in‑aid, channels resources to State‑level Climate Change Action Plans, but disbursement is conditional on the preparation of City‑Level Climate Resilience Strategies — a requirement many Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) cannot meet due to limited technical capacity.

Community‑led planning has proven effective where formal processes stall. In Ahmedabad’s “Mahila Housing SEWA Trust” model, women’s collectives co‑design flood‑resilient housing using locally sourced fly‑ash bricks and elevated plinths, reducing flood‑damage claims by 38 % during the 2022 monsoon. The model leverages the Community Development Fund under the National Urban Livelihoods Mission (NULM) and accesses micro‑credit from the National Housing Bank’s “Affordable Housing Fund”. Similarly, the “Slum Rehabilitation Authority” in Mumbai piloted a “Participatory Climate Budgeting” exercise in 2023, allocating ₹120 crore for drainage upgrades based on vulnerability maps prepared by resident committees.

Adaptation finance must move beyond project‑based grants to outcome‑linked instruments. The Green Climate Fund’s “Results‑Based Payments” pilot in Odisha ties disbursement to measurable reductions in heat‑stress mortality in slum clusters, verified through the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme. Domestic instruments such as the “Municipal Green Bonds” issued by Pune and Ghaziabad (2022‑24) earmark proceeds for permeable pavements and rooftop rainwater harvesting in low‑income neighbourhoods, with credit enhancement from the India Infrastructure Finance Company Limited.

Key policy levers include: (1) mandating that all Smart Cities Mission Area‑Based Development (ABD) plans incorporate a slum‑level climate vulnerability assessment; (2) extending the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Urban (PMAY‑U) “Technology Sub‑Mission” to finance heat‑reflective roofing and passive cooling for informal housing; and (3) institutionalising a “Slum Climate Resilience Officer” within each ULB, funded through the 15th Finance Commission’s performance‑based grants, to coordinate across water, health, and housing departments.

Technique: Interlinkage Mapping in Answer Writing
Question: “Explain how climate‑resilient upgrading of informal settlements can simultaneously address gender inequality, urban poverty, and the informal labour market.”
Structure the answer in three linked paragraphs. First, describe the physical upgrade (elevated plinths, drainage, cool roofs) and cite the Ahmedabad SEWA model as evidence of reduced flood loss and heat stress. Second, connect the upgrade to gender outcomes: women’s collectives gain tenure security, enabling access to credit (NULM SHG linkage) and reducing time spent on water collection, which raises female labour‑force participation in home‑based gig work. Third, show the labour‑market effect: resilient housing lowers asset loss for informal workers, stabilising income streams and making them eligible for portable social‑security registration on e‑Shram. Conclude with a policy recommendation — integrate climate budgeting into PMAY‑U and NULM convergence guidelines — to demonstrate synthesis across the four themes.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “interlinkages”, draw a quick mental matrix: rows = themes (gender, urbanisation, poverty, globalisation), columns = mechanisms (infrastructure, finance, law, data). Fiscal Federalism and Urban Local Body Revenue Autonomy: Implications for Service Delivery

The 74th Constitutional Amendment (1992) devolved 18 functions to Urban Local Bodies (ULBs), yet own‑source revenue remains below 15 % of total municipal expenditure in most states. Property tax, the principal own‑source instrument, suffers from outdated cadastral maps, low coverage (only 60 % of assessable properties in the 2011 Census were on the tax roll), and political resistance to periodic revision. The 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) recommended a “performance‑based grant” of ₹1.2 lakh crore conditional on ULBs achieving a 20 % increase in property‑tax collection efficiency and publishing audited accounts within nine months of the financial year‑end. As of 2024, only 12 states have operationalised the required GIS‑based property‑tax information systems.

Goods and Services Tax (GST) compensation cess, originally a temporary measure, has become a de‑facto revenue share for ULBs in several states (e.g., Maharashtra’s “Local Body Grant” linked to SGST collections). However, the cess is scheduled to sunset in 2026, creating a fiscal cliff. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs’ “Municipal Finance Improvement Programme” (2022) pilots a “Revenue Mobilisation Dashboard” that integrates property‑tax, advertisement‑tax, and user‑charge data with the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) to enable real‑time monitoring by the State Finance Commissions.

User charges for water, sanitation, and solid‑waste management remain poorly calibrated. The “Service Level Benchmarks” (SLBs) issued by the Ministry in 2019 prescribe cost‑recovery ratios of 80 % for water supply, yet the average recovery ratio across 500 ULBs surveyed in the 2022‑23 “Municipal Performance Index” was 38 %. Cross‑subsidisation through property‑tax surcharges is legally permissible under Article 243X but rarely implemented because of the political cost of raising tariffs for low‑income households.

Innovative financing instruments are emerging. Pune Municipal Corporation issued India’s first “Municipal Green Bond” (₹200 crore, 2022) rated AA by CRISIL, earmarked for energy‑efficient street lighting and sewage treatment. Similarly, the “Land Value Capture” framework piloted in Hyderabad’s “Transit‑Oriented Development” zones leverages betterment levies on land appreciation near metro corridors, generating ₹1,500 crore for last‑mile connectivity in low‑income neighbourhoods.

For sustainable service delivery, three reforms are essential: (1) a statutory mandate for quinquennial property‑tax revaluation linked to a transparent GIS cadastre; (2) a constitutionally protected “Urban Consolidated Fund” at the state level that pools a fixed share of SGST and central grants, insulated from annual budgetary vagaries; and (3) capacity‑building grants tied to the “Municipal Cadre” recruitment rules, ensuring that financial management posts are filled by qualified professionals rather than political appointees.

Social Protection Portability for Migrant Workers: One Nation One Ration Card and Beyond

Internal migration in India exceeds 450 million (Census 2011), with circular and seasonal flows dominating the construction, brick‑kiln, and agricultural sectors. The “One Nation One Ration Card” (ONORC) scheme, launched nationally in 2020, enables portability of Public Distribution System (PDS) entitlements across states through Aadhaar‑seeded ration cards and the “Integrated Management of Public Distribution System” (IM‑PDS) portal. By March 2024, 35 states/UTs had onboarded, covering 98 % of NFSA beneficiaries. However, portability of non‑food benefits — health insurance under Ayushman Bharat, maternity benefits under PMMVY, and pension under the National Social Assistance Programme — remains fragmented.

Critical gaps persist: (1) the absence of a unified “Beneficiary Registry” that reconciles Aadhaar, ration‑card, e‑Shram, and health‑ID (ABHA) identifiers; (2) limited interoperability between the IM‑PDS and the “National Health Authority” platform, causing duplicate verification at point‑of‑service; and (3) the lack of a legal framework for “portable contributory pensions” that accrue across employers and state boundaries. The 15th Finance Commission’s recommendation to create a “National Migrant Welfare Fund” financed by a 0.2 % levy on inter‑state goods movement remains unimplemented.

A roadmap for universal portability should include: (a) mandatory Aadhaar‑based seeding of all central‑scheme databases by 2025; (b) a “Single Window Portability API” developed by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, allowing real‑time entitlement verification at fair‑price shops, hospitals, and pension offices; and (c) a legislative amendment to the Code on Social Security, 2020, reducing the vesting period for migrant workers to five years and recognising “split‑contribution” histories across multiple employers.

The care economy — comprising unpaid household work, community‑based caregiving, and paid domestic and health‑care services — accounts for a substantial share of India’s gross domestic product yet remains invisible in conventional national accounts. Urban Heat Islands and Gendered Vulnerability

Satellite‑derived land‑surface temperature analyses by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM) show that megacities such as Delhi, Mumbai, and Chennai experience significant peak UHI intensities during pre‑monsoon months. The effect is amplified in low‑income settlements where roofing materials have high thermal conductivity and green cover is minimal. Studies mapping slum clusters across multiple cities find that a majority of households lack access to passive cooling and rely on electricity‑intensive fans or coolers, pushing monthly energy expenditure to a high share of household income.

Gendered vulnerability emerges from intersecting physiological, occupational, and social factors. Women in informal home‑based work spend long hours daily in poorly ventilated rooms, increasing heat‑stress risk. Health survey data report higher prevalence of heat‑related morbidity among women in the hottest urban wards compared with men. Pregnant and lactating women face additional thermoregulatory strain; cohort studies have linked rises in ambient temperature during pregnancy to adverse birth outcomes. Moreover, women’s responsibility for water collection intensifies during heat waves when municipal supply becomes erratic, extending exposure to outdoor heat.

Adaptation strategies must therefore be gender‑responsive. The “Cool Roofs” programme launched under the National Mission on Sustainable Habitat subsidises high‑albedo coatings for low‑income roofs, with a quota for women‑headed households. The “Urban Greening” component of AMRUT 2.0 earmarks funds for pocket parks and tree‑lined streets in slum‑adjacent wards, prioritising locations identified by the “Heat Vulnerability Index” (HVI) developed by the National Institute of Urban Affairs (NIUA). Community‑level early‑warning systems, piloted in city heat action plans, disseminate vernacular alerts and deploy “cooling centres” in anganwadi premises, reducing heat‑related emergency visits.

Financing remains a bottleneck. Finance Commission performance‑based grants for urban local bodies link a portion of the allocation to climate‑resilience milestones, yet many ULBs have not submitted approved heat‑action plans. Private‑sector participation through “cool‑roof” energy‑service companies (ESCOs) is nascent; pilots have demonstrated reductions in indoor temperature and electricity bills, but scale‑up requires standardised measurement‑and‑verification protocols. Integrating gender‑disaggregated heat‑health indicators into the Health Management Information System (HMIS) and linking them to the “National Programme on Climate Change and Human Health” (NPCCHH) would enable targeted resource allocation and monitor progress toward the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 11.7 target of universal access to safe, inclusive green spaces by 2030.

Gender‑responsive budgeting (GRB) entered India’s fiscal architecture through the Union Budget of 2005‑06 when a separate Gender Budget Statement (GBS) was presented for the first time. Metropolitan Planning Committees and the 74th Constitutional Amendment

The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, inserted Part IXA into the Constitution, mandating the constitution of Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPCs) in every metropolitan area with a population of ten lakh or more (Article 243ZE). An MPC is tasked with preparing a draft development plan for the metropolitan region, integrating spatial, economic, and environmental planning across municipal corporations, municipalities, and panchayats within the metropolitan boundary. The committee’s composition — two‑thirds elected members from local bodies and one‑third nominated experts — was designed to balance democratic legitimacy with technical competence.

Despite the constitutional mandate, functional MPCs exist in only a minority of eligible metros. Official reviews indicate that only a fraction of metropolitan areas have constituted MPCs, and even fewer have approved a metropolitan development plan. Common impediments include: (i) ambiguity over the “metropolitan area” delineation, leading to jurisdictional disputes between the state government and urban local bodies (ULBs); (ii) lack of devolved planning functions and financial resources to the MPC, rendering it advisory rather than executive; and (iii) political reluctance to cede planning authority to a multi‑jurisdictional body.

The “Model Metropolitan Planning Committee Rules” issued by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) attempted to standardise procedures: a fixed tenure, a mandatory technical secretariat, and a requirement to align the metropolitan plan with the State’s Master Plan and the National Urban Transport Policy. States such as Gujarat and Tamil Nadu have adopted these rules and operationalised MPCs with dedicated GIS‑based planning cells. In contrast, the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority (MMRDA) continues to function as a parallel planning agency, sidelining the MPC.

Judicial interventions have reinforced the constitutional obligation. High Courts have directed state governments to constitute MPCs within stipulated timeframes, citing the violation of Article 243ZE. The Supreme Court has upheld the principle that MPCs cannot be rendered defunct by executive inaction, and ordered state governments to notify MPCs for major metropolitan regions.

Effective metropolitan planning also demands fiscal empowerment. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a “Metropolitan Grant” conditional on the existence of a functional MPC and an approved metropolitan plan. The grant is intended to finance cross‑boundary infrastructure — regional transport corridors, integrated solid‑waste management, and climate‑resilient drainage — that no single ULB can fund alone. Early evidence from operational MPCs shows that such grants can leverage additional state and private investment for regional projects.

Chronic and Transient Poverty: Measurement, Dynamics and Policy Design

Poverty dynamics distinguish between chronic poverty — households that remain below the poverty line across multiple survey rounds — and transient poverty — households that move in and out of poverty due to shocks such as illness, crop failure, or job loss. The National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) consumption expenditure surveys and the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) provide the primary longitudinal evidence for India. Studies using the “synthetic panel” technique on these cross‑sections estimate that a substantial share of the poor are chronically poor, while the remainder experience at least one spell of transient poverty over a multi‑year horizon.

Measurement challenges arise because consumption data are collected at intervals of five to seven years, obscuring short‑duration spells. The World Bank’s “poverty dynamics” framework recommends supplementing consumption panels with high‑frequency phone surveys and administrative data (e.g., MGNREGA job‑card records, PDS off‑take) to capture seasonal fluctuations. Recent survey pilots have introduced quarterly modules on food insecurity, offering a more granular view of transient episodes.

Policy design must therefore be dual‑track. For chronic poverty, structural interventions — land reform, universal basic services (health, education, water), and asset‑building programmes such as the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana‑National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY‑NRLM) — address deep‑rooted capability deficits. Recent rural transformation projects explicitly target ultra‑poor households identified through participatory “poverty‑scorecards” that incorporate chronic‑poverty indicators (e.g., female‑headed household, disability, lack of productive assets).

For transient poverty, shock‑responsive safety nets are essential. The Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Yojana (PMGKY) during the COVID‑19 pandemic demonstrated the feasibility of rapid cash transfers using the Jan‑Dhan‑Aadhaar‑Mobile (JAM) trinity, reaching a vast number of beneficiaries within weeks. However, exclusion errors persisted: assessments documented that a notable share of eligible migrant workers lacked linked bank accounts or faced biometric authentication failures. Integrating the e‑Shram database with the JAM platform and allowing “proxy authentication” for shared‑phone households can reduce such gaps.

Insurance‑based mechanisms complement cash transfers. The Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) and the Ayushman Bharat‑Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB‑PMJAY) target agricultural and health shocks respectively. Evaluations show that claim settlement ratios improved after guideline revisions mandating timely yield‑data sharing by states. For urban informal workers, the “Unorganised Workers’ Social Security Act, 2008” provides a legal framework for portable benefits, but implementation remains fragmented across states.

Globalisation, Intangible Cultural Heritage and Language Endangerment

Globalisation accelerates the diffusion of dominant cultural forms — media, fashion, digital platforms — while simultaneously creating spaces for the revitalisation of intangible cultural heritage (ICH). India ratified the UNESCO 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2005, obligating the state to inventory, protect, and transmit practices such as oral traditions, performing arts, rituals, and traditional craftsmanship. The Ministry of Culture’s “National List of Intangible Cultural Heritage” enumerates several elements, including Koodiyattam (Sanskrit theatre), Chhau dance, and the traditional brass‑craft of Pembarthi.

Language endangerment is a direct consequence of globalised communication networks. UNESCO’s “Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger” classifies a large number of Indian languages across categories ranging from “vulnerable” to “critically endangered”. The 2011 Census recorded 121 languages with more than 10,000 speakers; however, many minor languages — such as Toda (Nilgiris), Sentinelese (Andaman), and several Tibeto‑Burman languages of the Northeast — have very few speakers and lack institutional support.

The “Scheduled Languages” framework (Eighth Schedule) provides constitutional recognition to 22 languages, granting them official status, educational provisions, and eligibility for central funding. Yet, languages outside the Schedule rely on state‑level initiatives. The “National Education Policy 2020” recommends mother‑tongue instruction up to Grade 5 and the establishment of “Language Academies” for endangered languages. Pilot academies for several tribal languages have been set up, with outcomes constrained by insufficient teacher recruitment.

Digital globalisation offers both threat and opportunity. Streaming platforms and social media marginalise minority languages by privileging Hindi and English content. Conversely, community‑led digital archives — such as the “Endangered Languages Project” hosted by the Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL) — enable crowdsourced documentation of oral narratives, songs, and lexical data. The “Bhashini” mission aims to build open‑source language models for scheduled languages and additional languages, potentially extending machine‑translation services to under‑resourced tongues.

Policy coherence requires aligning cultural safeguarding with economic globalisation. The “Geographical Indications (GI) Act, 1999” protects traditional crafts (e.g., Pochampally Ikat, Madhubani painting) from misappropriation in global markets, providing collective branding that can raise artisan incomes. However, GI registration alone does not guarantee transmission of skills; the “Guru‑Shishya Parampara” scheme under the Ministry of Culture funds master‑apprentice stipends, yet coverage remains limited relative to the number of identified practitioners.

Circular Migration, Remittances and Urban Informal Labour Markets

Circular migration — the repeated movement of workers between rural homes and urban job sites — characterises a large share of India’s internal migration. NSSO surveys have estimated tens of millions of circular migrants, predominantly male, engaged in construction, brick‑kilns, and seasonal agriculture. Employment‑unemployment surveys confirm that a significant share of urban informal workers report a rural origin and maintain a household in the village, confirming the circular pattern.

Remittances from circular migrants constitute a vital income stream for rural households. The World Bank’s “Migration and Development Brief” places India’s inward remittances among the highest globally, with a substantial portion originating from domestic circular migrants rather than international migrants. Micro‑studies in several states reveal that remittances finance a major share of household consumption, fund agricultural inputs, and enable investment in children’s education.

Urban informal labour markets absorb circular migrants through dense networks of labour contractors (thekedars) and informal recruitment at “nakas” (street corners). These markets operate without written contracts, social security, or minimum‑wage enforcement. The “Inter‑State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1979” mandates registration of contractors and provision of basic amenities, but compliance remains low: inspections have found that only a small fraction of construction sites maintain the required registers.

Portable social protection is the policy frontier. The “One Nation One Ration Card” (ONORC) scheme, rolled out nationally, allows migrants to access Public Distribution System (PDS) grain at any fair‑price shop using Aadhaar authentication. Uptake data indicate millions of portability transactions, yet coverage gaps persist for migrants lacking Aadhaar‑linked ration cards or residing in states yet to integrate their PDS databases.

The “e‑Shram” portal registers unorganised workers, including circular migrants, and issues a Universal Account Number (UAN) linked to accident insurance and pension schemes. Tens of millions of workers are registered, but only a minority have completed the “skill‑mapping” module that could facilitate job matching. Enhancing the portal with real‑time labour‑demand data from urban municipal corporations and construction industry associations could transform it from a static registry into a dynamic labour‑market intermediary.

Land Tenure Security and Informal Settlements: Legal Frameworks and Eviction Risks

The constitutional guarantee of the right to life under Article 21 has been interpreted by the Supreme Court to include the right to shelter, making forced evictions without due process a potential violation of fundamental rights. The landmark judgment in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) held that pavement dwellers cannot be evicted without providing alternative accommodation, establishing a procedural safeguard that later judgments have reinforced. The Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1956 empowers state governments to declare slum areas and undertake improvement or clearance, but it requires a prior survey, a rehabilitation plan, and a hearing for affected residents. Many states have enacted their own slum rehabilitation statutes — for example, the Maharashtra Slum Areas (Improvement, Clearance and Redevelopment) Act, 1971 and the Delhi Urban Shelter Improvement Board Act, 2010 — which create dedicated authorities for in‑situ redevelopment and allotment of tenement rights.

The 74th Constitutional Amendment (1992) devolved planning powers to Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) and mandated the constitution of Ward Committees, giving slum dwellers a formal platform to participate in land‑use decisions. However, the amendment does not confer proprietary rights; tenure security remains contingent on state‑level policies. The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban) (PMAY‑U) seeks to provide “Housing for All” by 2024 through four verticals, of which the “In‑situ Slum Redevelopment” (ISSR) component uses land as a resource, allowing private developers to build on slum land in exchange for providing pucca houses to eligible residents. The Model Tenancy Act, 2021 — circulated by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs — proposes a regulatory framework for rental housing, including a Rent Authority, rent courts, and a provision that tenants cannot be evicted without a court order, aiming to reduce informal tenancy insecurity.

Despite these instruments, eviction drives continue in major metros. The 2020‑21 “anti‑encroachment” campaigns in Mumbai, Delhi, and Bengaluru displaced thousands of families, often without the mandated rehabilitation. Courts have repeatedly intervened: in Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation v. Nawab Khan Gulab Khan (1997) the Supreme Court ordered the provision of alternative sites before demolition, and in Sudama Singh v. Government of Delhi (2010) the Delhi High Court directed the Delhi Urban Shelter Improvement Board to follow due process. The persistent gap between statutory safeguards and on‑ground practice stems from weak enforcement capacity of ULBs, lack of updated land records, and the political economy of high‑value urban land. A robust tenure‑security regime therefore requires (i) integration of slum surveys with the National Urban Housing and Habitat Policy, (ii) mandatory social impact assessments before any clearance, and (iii) a grievance‑redressal mechanism linked to the e‑Courts platform for time‑bound adjudication of eviction disputes.

Public Health Infrastructure and Urban Poor: Lessons from COVID‑19

The COVID‑19 pandemic exposed structural deficits in urban public health delivery, especially for over 65 million slum residents (Census 2011). The National Urban Health Mission (NUHM), launched in 2013 as a sub‑mission of the National Health Mission, aimed to strengthen primary health care through Urban Primary Health Centres (U‑PHCs) and Urban Community Health Centres (U‑CHCs). By 2020, only about 3,500 U‑PHCs were functional against a target of one per 50,000 urban population, leaving a significant coverage gap in million‑plus cities. The Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB‑PMJAY) provides cashless secondary and tertiary care up to ₹5 lakh per family per year, but enrolment among slum households remained low due to documentation barriers and lack of awareness.

During the first wave, sero‑surveillance in Mumbai’s Dharavi showed a prevalence of around 57 percent, yet mortality rates were lower than in non‑slum wards, attributed to community‑led containment, aggressive testing, and the deployment of Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and Anganwadi workers for door‑to‑door surveillance. The Pradhan Mantri Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission (PM‑ABHIM), announced in 2021, allocated ₹64,180 crore for critical care blocks, integrated public health labs, and urban wellness centres, explicitly targeting underserved urban populations. The mission also mandated the creation of Urban Health and Wellness Centres (U‑HWCs) to deliver comprehensive primary care, including non‑communicable disease screening, mental health services, and tele‑consultation.

Key policy lessons include: (i) the necessity of real‑time disease surveillance integrated with the Integrated Health Information Platform (IHIP) to capture slum‑level data; (ii) the value of community health volunteers who enjoy trust and can bridge the “last‑mile” gap; (iii) the importance of portable health entitlements — the One Nation One Health Card pilot under AB‑PMJAY aims to allow migrants to access services across state boundaries; and (iv) the need for financial protection against catastrophic out‑of‑pocket expenditure, which pushes many urban poor deeper into poverty. Strengthening urban health governance therefore requires ULBs to assume statutory responsibility for health planning under the 74th Amendment, earmarked budgetary allocations for slum‑specific interventions, and a robust accountability framework linking U‑PHC performance to the National Health Systems Resource Centre (NHSRC) monitoring dashboards.

Digital Financial Inclusion and Women's Economic Agency

The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY), launched in August 2014, has opened over 48 crore basic savings accounts, of which women constitute roughly 55 percent. The scheme’s zero‑balance feature, RuPay debit card, and accidental insurance cover (₹2 lakh) lowered entry barriers for low‑income women. However, the Global Findex data indicate a persistent gender gap in active usage, with women account holders less likely than men to have performed a transaction in the preceding year. The Aadhaar‑enabled Payment System (AePS) and the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) have expanded digital transaction channels, yet women’s share of UPI transactions remains low, reflecting lower smartphone ownership and digital literacy.

Targeted interventions aim to close this gap. The Business Correspondent (BC) Sakhi programme, rolled out by the Ministry of Rural Development in partnership with banks, recruits women from Self‑Help Groups (SHGs) as banking correspondents, providing them with a micro‑ATM device and a commission‑based income. As of March 2023, over 1 lakh BC Sakhis were operational, facilitating cash‑in/cash‑out, balance enquiry, and credit linkage for SHG members. The Mahila e‑Haats platform, launched by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, enables women artisans and entrepreneurs to list products on a government‑backed e‑marketplace, integrating UPI for payments. Early evaluations indicate a significant increase in average monthly sales for registered sellers.

Policy coherence requires addressing three structural constraints: (i) device and connectivity access — the BharatNet project’s last‑mile fibre rollout to gram panchayats must be complemented by affordable data plans for women; (ii) financial literacy — the National Centre for Financial Education (NCFE) runs the “Financial Literacy Week” and develops gender‑sensitive modules for SHG federations; (iii) credit scoring for thin‑file borrowers — the Account Aggregator (AA) framework under the Reserve Bank of India allows consent‑based sharing of transaction data, enabling lenders to assess women’s cash‑flow without traditional collateral. Integrating these elements into the Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) stack — Aadhaar, UPI, AA, and DigiLocker — can transform women’s economic agency from account ownership to active participation in formal credit markets and digital commerce.

Social Audit Mechanisms in Rural Employment Guarantee Schemes

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005, institutionalised social audit as a statutory accountability tool under Section 17. The Act mandates that every Gram Panchayat conduct a social audit at least once in six months, facilitated by an independent Social Audit Unit (SAU) at the state level. The SAU, funded centrally under the National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (NREGS), recruits and trains Social Audit Facilitators (SAFs) who guide the Gram Sabha through verification of muster rolls, measurement books, wage payments, and asset creation. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has recognised social audit as a “citizen‑centric audit” that complements formal audit.

Evidence from social audit reports across states suggests that regular audits have reduced leakages in wage payments and improved the proportion of works completed as per technical specifications. The Andhra Pradesh model, where the SAU operates as an autonomous society with a large dedicated cadre of SAFs, is frequently cited for its near‑universal coverage of Gram Panchayats audited annually and the use of a Mobile‑based Social Audit Management System (SAMS) for real‑time data capture. In contrast, states like Bihar and Uttar Pradesh have struggled with irregular audits, insufficient SAF capacity, and political interference in Gram Sabha proceedings.

The Ministry of Rural Development issued the Social Audit Guidelines, 2021, which standardise the audit calendar, define the role of the Independent Social Audit Committee (ISAC) at the block level, and prescribe a grievance‑redressal timeline of 30 days for findings. The guidelines also encourage convergence with the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) to flag discrepancies between fund releases and expenditure reported in social audits. Extending the social‑audit framework to urban employment programmes — such as the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana‑National Urban Livelihoods Mission (DAY‑NULM) — has been piloted in Tamil Nadu and Odisha, where urban ward committees conduct “social audits” of skill‑training and micro‑enterprise components. Scaling this model requires legal backing (amending the respective mission guidelines), capacity building of urban community organisations, and integration with the Urban Local Body (ULB) accountability architecture under the 74th Amendment.

Gendered Impact of Climate Change on Rural Livelihoods

Climate change amplifies existing gender inequalities in rural India because women constitute a large share of the agricultural labour force and are primary managers of household water, fuel and food security. Census and Agriculture Census data indicate that women account for a substantial portion of the agricultural workforce, yet hold a small fraction of operational landholdings. This tenure gap limits their access to credit, insurance and extension services essential for adopting climate‑resilient practices. The National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC), launched in 2008, includes the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA) which promotes climate‑smart agriculture, but its implementation guidelines do not mandate gender‑disaggregated targeting. Consequently, schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY) and the Sub‑Mission on Agroforestry (SMAF) tend to reach male‑headed households first.

Studies from the Indo‑Gangetic plains and the Deccan plateau indicate that women’s workload increases during droughts and erratic monsoons because they must travel longer distances for water and fodder. A 2021 NITI Aayog vulnerability assessment highlights districts in Maharashtra, Karnataka and Odisha where female‑headed households face sharper declines in crop income compared with male‑headed counterparts. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) provides a safety net, yet surveys suggest women’s participation in MGNREGA works drops in drought‑affected areas, partly because work‑site facilities (creches, sanitation) remain inadequate.

Policy responses are beginning to integrate gender. The Ministry of Agriculture’s 2023 “Gender‑Responsive Climate Resilient Agriculture” framework recommends (i) mandatory inclusion of women in Village Climate Risk Management Committees, (ii) earmarking a share of NMSA funds for women‑led farmer producer organisations, and (iii) linking the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) payouts to joint bank accounts. The framework also calls for integrating the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) self‑help groups with climate information services such as the Indian Meteorological Department’s “Agromet Advisory Services” delivered via mobile apps. However, the digital divide — discussed in the next section — limits the reach of these advisories to women who lack smartphones or data connectivity.

Digital Divide and Educational Outcomes for Marginalised Girls

The COVID‑19 pandemic exposed a stark digital divide that disproportionately affects girls from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and economically weaker sections. The Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) 2022 found that a significantly lower proportion of rural girls in the 14‑18 age group had access to a smartphone for learning compared with boys. In tribal districts of Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, the gap is even wider. The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 envisions a “Digital Infrastructure for Knowledge Sharing” (DIKSHA) platform and the PM eVIDYA programme, which aggregates TV channels, radio, and the DIKSHA app. Yet UDISE+ data shows that only about half of government schools have functional internet connectivity, and fewer than a third have dedicated computer labs.

Gendered barriers compound the infrastructure gap. Household surveys by the Centre for Policy Research (2021) reveal that when a single device is shared, boys are prioritised for online classes while girls are assigned domestic chores. The “Beti Bachao Beti Padhao” (BBBP) scheme, expanded to include a “Digital Literacy for Girls” component, provides community‑level digital centres in aspirational districts. Early evaluations indicate improved enrolment in online remedial classes where centres are operational, but sustainability remains a concern because funding is tied to the Centrally Sponsored Scheme cycle.

Policy levers that can close the gap include (i) the BharatNet project’s last‑mile connectivity to gram panchayats, which must be paired with affordable data plans for women — a recommendation of the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) 2023 consultation paper; (ii) device‑sharing models such as community‑managed laptop pilots in Kerala’s Kudumbashree network, which showed improvements in girls’ learning outcomes; and (iii) gender‑sensitive content design on DIKSHA, ensuring that pedagogical materials address girls’ lived realities (menstrual health, safety, career aspirations). The 2024 Budget announced a “Digital Gender Inclusion Fund” to subsidise smartphones for girls in Classes 9‑12, but the implementation guidelines are awaited.

Social Security Code 2020 and Informal Workers

The Code on Social Security, 2020, passed by Parliament in September 2020 and assented to by the President on 28 September 2020, consolidates nine central labour enactments — including the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952; the Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948; the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961; and the Unorganised Workers’ Social Security Act, 2008 — into a single statute. The Code extends the definition of “employee” to include gig workers, platform workers and inter‑state migrant workers, thereby bringing a vast number of informal workers under a statutory social‑security umbrella for the first time. However, the Code’s operationalisation depends on the framing of central rules and state‑level notifications, which remain partially notified.

Key provisions affecting women and marginalised groups include: (i) a universal maternity benefit of 26 weeks for all women workers, including those in the gig economy, contingent on a contribution‑based funding model to be shared between the central government, state governments and aggregators; (ii) a National Social Security Board for unorganised workers, chaired by the Minister for Labour, with representation from trade unions, employers and civil society; (iii) a Social Security Fund financed through a cess on aggregators and contributions from workers (optional, with a matching government contribution for those below the poverty line). The Code also mandates the registration of all unorganised workers on the e‑Shram portal, which has enrolled over 28 crore workers, of whom a majority are women.

Implementation challenges are significant. First, the contributory architecture assumes a formal employer‑employee relationship; for platform workers the “aggregator” is often a technology intermediary that disputes employer status. The Supreme Court’s 2023 judgment on app‑based transport workers upheld the Code’s applicability to platform workers but left the determination of “employer” to the central rules. Second, the cess collection mechanism requires robust digital integration between the Goods and Services Tax Network (GSTN) and the e‑Shram database — a linkage still under pilot in some states. Third, portability of benefits across states remains limited; the “One Nation One Social Security” framework envisaged under the Code is yet to be operationalised, leaving migrant workers vulnerable to loss of entitlements when they move.

Civil society organisations such as the Self‑Employed Women’s Association (SEWA) and the Centre for Workers’ Management have advocated for (a) a universal, non‑contributory basic pension for all informal workers aged 60 plus, (b) inclusion of domestic workers under the Employees’ State Insurance Corporation (ESIC) network, and (c) a grievance‑redressal timeline of 30 days for benefit claims, aligned with the Social Audit Guidelines, 2021. The 2024‑25 Union Budget allocated funds for the Social Security Fund, but the disbursement schedule is tied to the finalisation of state rules, creating a fiscal‑federal bottleneck.

Land Rights, Forest Rights Act and Tribal Women

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA) recognises individual and community forest rights, including the right to cultivate, collect minor forest produce, and manage community forest resources. While the Act is gender‑neutral in language, its implementation has historically marginalised tribal women because land titles are issued in the name of the “head of household,” typically a male. The 2013 Supreme Court judgment in *Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests* (the Niyamgiri case) affirmed the Gram Sabha’s authority to decide on forest diversion, yet the subsequent Forest Rights Committee (FRC) guidelines issued by the Ministry of Tribal Affairs in 2014 did not mandate women’s representation in FRCs.

Data from the Ministry of Tribal Affairs’ implementation reports shows that a small fraction of individual forest rights titles are in women’s names, and community forest resource (CFR) rights have been recognised in a limited number of villages, with women’s participation in CFR management committees remaining low. The 2019 amendment to the FRA Rules introduced a provision that “at least one‑third of the members of the Forest Rights Committee shall be women,” but compliance remains uneven. In states like Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, civil society networks have facilitated women‑led CFR claims, resulting in increased women’s title holdings in pilot areas.

Secure forest rights have direct livelihood implications. Research indicates that women’s control over non‑timber forest produce (NTFP) collection and marketing raises household income and improves nutrition outcomes for children. The Mahua, Tendu and Sal seed value chains, traditionally managed by women, benefit from collective bargaining when CFR rights are recognised. However, the 2021 Forest Conservation Rules, which streamline forest clearance for infrastructure projects, have been criticised for weakening Gram Sabha consent requirements, potentially undermining women’s newly secured rights.

Policy pathways to strengthen tribal women’s land tenure include: (i) mandatory joint titling of individual forest rights in the names of both spouses, as piloted in Odisha’s “Joint Forest Rights” scheme; (ii) capacity‑building of women’s collectives for CFR management plans, supported by the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) and the Tribal Cooperative Marketing Development Federation of India (TRIFED); (iii) integration of FRA implementation data with the Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP) to enable real‑time monitoring of gender‑disaggregated title distribution. The 2023 “Tribal Women’s Empowerment” sub‑scheme under the Ministry of Tribal Affairs allocates funds for these interventions, but its effectiveness hinges on state‑level political will and the functional autonomy of Gram Sabhas.

  • Treating e‑Shram enrolment numbers as proof of benefit delivery; registration does not equal actual receipt of pension, insurance or maternity entitlements.
  • Overlooking the distinction between individual forest rights and community forest resource rights when answering questions on tribal livelihoods.

Disability, Gender and Urban Poverty: Intersectional Vulnerabilities

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 (RPwD Act) expanded the definition of disability to 21 conditions and mandated 4 % reservation in government jobs and 5 % in higher education. Yet the 2011 Census records only 2.21 % of the urban population as disabled, a figure widely regarded as an undercount because the census schedule relies on self‑reporting and excludes many invisible impairments. The 78th round of the National Sample Survey (2020‑21) indicates that urban women with disabilities have a labour‑force participation rate of around 9 % compared with roughly 24 % for urban men with disabilities and 32 % for urban women without disabilities. This gap reflects three compounding barriers: inaccessible public transport (low‑floor buses remain a small minority in major metros), lack of gender‑sensitive assistive technology, and the absence of disability‑disaggregated data in urban poverty programmes such as the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana‑National Urban Livelihoods Mission (DAY‑NULM).

The Supreme Court in National Federation of Blind v. Union of India (2021) directed state governments to conduct accessibility audits of public buildings within twelve months, but compliance remains uneven. In Mumbai, the Accessible India Campaign (Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan) retrofitted a large number of railway stations, yet a 2023 audit by the Centre for Disability Studies found that only a minority of these stations provide tactile paving and audio announcements functional for women commuters who also face safety concerns after dark. The National Urban Health Mission (NUHM) includes a component for disability‑inclusive primary health centres, but recent Health Management Information System data indicates that only a small fraction of urban PHCs have a trained rehabilitation worker.

Policy convergence is possible through the “Inclusive City” framework proposed by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) in 2022, which calls for (i) mandatory universal design in all new municipal housing under the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Urban (PMAY‑U), (ii) gender‑disaggregated disability indicators in the Urban Local Body (ULB) performance dashboard, and (iii) a dedicated fund line in the Fifteenth Finance Commission grants for assistive‑device distribution. Pilot projects in Bhubaneswar and Kochi, where self‑help groups of women with disabilities manage community‑level mobility aids, have reported improved school attendance for girls with disabilities. Scaling such models requires amending the RPwD Rules to make accessibility audits a condition for central assistance under the Smart Cities Mission.

Artificial Intelligence, Automation and the Future of Informal Work

The National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (NSAI), released by NITI Aayog in 2018, identifies “AI for All” as a pillar but does not address the gendered impact of algorithmic management on platform workers. The 2022‑23 Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) estimates that several million workers are engaged in digital platform work, with women constituting a substantial share, predominantly in domestic‑care, beauty‑services and data‑annotation tasks. Algorithmic rating systems on platforms such as Urban Company and Swiggy allocate tasks based on historical acceptance rates, creating a feedback loop where women—who often have higher rejection rates due to safety‑related cancellations—receive fewer high‑pay assignments. A 2023 study by the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad found that women platform workers earn significantly less per hour than male counterparts after controlling for task type and hours worked.

The Code on Social Security, 2020 extends coverage to gig and platform workers through a contributory Social Security Fund, but the fund’s capitalisation depends on a cess on aggregator turnover that has not yet been notified. The Ministry of Labour and Employment’s 2024 draft “Platform Workers (Conditions of Work) Rules” propose a transparency obligation: platforms must disclose the logic of task allocation algorithms and provide a grievance redressal mechanism within 48 hours. However, the rules stop short of mandating algorithmic audits by an independent body, a gap highlighted by the International Labour Organization’s 2023 “Global Commission on the Future of Work” report.

Automation in traditional informal sectors is also accelerating. The textile cluster in Tiruppur has introduced AI‑driven fabric‑inspection machines, displacing a large number of low‑skill workers, a majority of whom are women. The Ministry of Textiles’ “Scheme for Integrated Textile Parks” (SITP) includes a reskilling component, yet enrolment of displaced workers in the 2022‑23 training cycle remained very low. The National Skill Development Corporation (NSDC) launched the “Future Skills Prime” platform in 2021, offering micro‑credentials in data‑labelling and AI‑assisted design, but uptake among informal women workers remains minimal due to digital‑literacy barriers and lack of childcare support at training centres.

Policy pathways to mitigate adverse effects include: (i) a mandatory algorithmic impact assessment for platforms employing more than 10,000 workers, modelled on the EU’s AI Act risk‑classification; (ii) a gender‑budgeted allocation within the Social Security Fund for portable maternity and childcare benefits; (iii) integration of AI‑literacy modules into the Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Grameen Kaushalya Yojana (DDU‑GKY) curriculum for urban migrants; and (iv) establishment of a tripartite “Platform Work Council” at the state level, comprising worker representatives, platform firms, and labour departments, to negotiate fair‑pay standards and data‑ownership rights.

Climate‑Induced Migration and Urban Governance Challenges

The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC) recorded several million new disaster‑related displacements in India during 2022, with a majority occurring in coastal and deltaic states—West Bengal, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat. The NITI Aayog’s “Report on Climate Resilient Development” (2021) projects that by 2050 tens of millions of people could migrate internally due to sea‑level rise, cyclonic intensification and agrarian distress. Urban local bodies (ULBs) in receiving cities such as Kolkata, Bhubaneswar, Visakhapatnam and Surat lack a statutory framework to integrate climate migrants into housing, livelihood and social‑protection systems.

The 74th Constitutional Amendment empowers municipalities to plan for “economic and social development” but does not explicitly mention climate‑induced migration. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs’ “Climate Smart Cities Assessment Framework” (CSCAF, 2022) includes an indicator on “inclusive housing for vulnerable groups”, yet only a small minority of the 100 Smart Cities have submitted baseline data on migrant settlements. The National Urban Policy Framework (draft 2023) proposes a “Migrant Integration Cell” in each ULB, tasked with (i) issuing portable identity credentials linked to Aadhaar, (ii) mapping informal settlements for tenure regularisation under the “Slum Free City” scheme, and (iii) coordinating with the State Disaster Management Authority (SDMA) for pre‑emptive evacuation planning.

Evidence from the 2020‑21 “Sundarbans Migration Study” (conducted by the Centre for Policy Research) shows that a large majority of migrant households in Kolkata’s Rajarhat‑New Town corridor lack access to the Public Distribution System (PDS) because ration cards remain tied to the place of origin. The One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) scheme, rolled out nationally in 2020, reduces this barrier but requires biometric authentication at fair‑price shops, which many recent migrants cannot complete without a permanent address. The 2022‑23 Economic Survey notes that only about a third of urban migrants have successfully ported their ration entitlements.

Governance innovations include: (a) the “Migrant Worker Registry” piloted in Kerala’s Kudumbashree network, which links skill‑mapping to the National Career Service portal; (b) the “Urban Flood Resilience Plan” of Surat Municipal Corporation, which earmarks a share of the annual capital budget for upgrading drainage in migrant‑dense wards; (c) the “Climate Adaptation Fund” under the Fifteenth Finance Commission, which allows states to claim performance‑based grants for migrant‑inclusive infrastructure. Scaling these pilots demands a central‑level “Climate Migration Act” that defines the legal status of climate migrants, mandates ULB‑level integration plans, and ties central assistance to measurable inclusion indicators.

Participatory Budgeting and Gender‑Responsive Urban Planning

Participatory budgeting (PB) was first institutionalised in India in 2001 when the Pune Municipal Corporation allocated 5 % of its capital budget to ward‑level citizen assemblies. By 2023, over two dozen cities—including Bengaluru, Coimbatore, and Thiruvananthapuram—had adopted PB mechanisms, but only a minority earmarked a gender‑budget sub‑component. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj’s “Guidelines for Gender‑Responsive Budgeting in Local Governments” (2019) recommend that a significant share of PB funds be directed to projects addressing women’s practical needs (street lighting, safe toilets, childcare centres) and strategic interests (skill‑training hubs, market‑space for women vendors).

A 2022 evaluation by the Centre for Budget and Governance Accountability (CBGA) of Bengaluru’s PB cycle found that women’s participation in ward assemblies averaged around 38 %, yet only a small fraction of approved projects explicitly targeted gender gaps. The study attributes the shortfall to (i) lack of gender‑disaggregated baseline data in the ward‑level “Citizen’s Charter”, (ii) absence of a mandatory gender‑impact appraisal template, and (iii) limited capacity of women’s self‑help groups (SHGs) to prepare technical project proposals. In contrast, the “Kudumbashree” model in Kerala integrates SHGs as formal “Neighbourhood Groups” with voting rights in the PB process, resulting in a substantial share of PB allocations funding women‑led micro‑enterprise clusters and community kitchens.

The 74th Amendment’s District Planning Committees (DPCs) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPCs) provide a statutory platform for scaling gender‑responsive PB. The Fifteenth Finance Commission’s “Performance‑Based Grant” for ULBs includes a “Gender Equality Index” (GEI) that rewards cities achieving a meaningful gender‑budget share. However, the GEI relies on self‑reported expenditure data, which the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) 2023 audit found to be inconsistent across states. A robust framework would require: (a) mandatory gender‑disaggregated budgeting templates in the Municipal Accounting System (MAS); (b) an independent “Gender Audit Cell” at the state level to verify PB outcomes; (c) capacity‑building grants for women’s collectives to engage in technical project design, financed through the National Urban Livelihoods Mission (NULM) “Social Mobilisation” component.

Community Land Trusts and Tenure Security for Urban Poor

The Community Land Trust (CLT) model separates land ownership from housing ownership: a non‑profit trust holds the land in perpetuity while residents own or lease the dwelling units under long‑term ground leases that preserve affordability. In India, an early formal CLT was registered as the “Mahila Housing Trust” (MHT) in Ahmedabad, supported by the Self‑Employed Women’s Association (SEWA) and the Gujarat Urban Development Mission. MHT acquired vacant municipal land through a long‑term lease at a nominal rent, enabling over a thousand low‑income families—predominantly headed by women—to secure incremental housing with tenure guarantees.

The legal basis for CLTs draws on the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (for leasehold creation), the Registration Act, 1908 (for trust registration), and the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (RFCTLARR), which mandates “consent of 70 % of affected families” for private acquisition but does not explicitly recognise community‑held land trusts. The 2020 “Model Tenancy Act” circulated by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs encourages states to adopt “community land banks” but leaves the governance structure to state legislatures. Maharashtra’s “Slum Rehabilitation Authority” (SRA) scheme, while providing free‑sale housing, retains land ownership with the developer, leading to resale restrictions that erode long‑term affordability.

Evidence from the “Dharavi Community Land Trust” pilot (launched 2021 by the Tata Trusts and the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority) shows that CLT‑managed plots significantly reduced eviction risk compared with adjacent SRA sites, and enabled residents to access formal credit using the ground‑lease as collateral. The pilot also integrated a “Women’s Land Rights Committee” that monitors lease compliance and mediates disputes, resulting in a notable increase in women’s name‑registration on lease deeds.

Scaling CLTs requires: (i) a central “Community Land Trust Act” that defines the trust’s legal personality, tax‑exempt status, and mandatory women representation on the board; (ii) inclusion of CLTs as eligible entities under the “Affordable Housing Fund” of the National Housing Bank; (iii) integration of CLT land records with the Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme (DILRMP) to ensure real‑time transparency; and (iv) a dedicated “CLT Capacity‑Building Window” under the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana‑NULM to finance legal registration, community mobilisation, and technical design of incremental housing.

Urban Informal Economy and the Role of Street Vendor Acts in Livelihood Protection

The Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014, enacted after the Supreme Court’s 2010 directive in National Association of Street Vendors of India v. Union of India, provides a statutory framework for the recognition, regulation and protection of street vendors. The Act mandates the constitution of Town Vending Committees (TVCs) with a minimum 40 % representation of women vendors, the preparation of a Street Vending Plan, and the issuance of vending certificates that cannot be arbitrarily revoked. Despite the legal mandate, implementation remains uneven: as of the 2022‑23 Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) review, only about 18 states had notified TVCs and fewer than 30 % of identified vendors held valid certificates.

Data from the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) 2011‑12 round estimated that street vendors constituted roughly 2.5 % of the urban workforce, a figure that the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2022‑23 suggests has risen to about 3 % owing to pandemic‑induced livelihood shifts. The Act’s “no‑eviction without rehabilitation” clause mirrors the principle established in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), where the Court held that the right to livelihood is an integral facet of the right to life under Article 21. However, municipal corporations often invoke “public nuisance” or “traffic obstruction” provisions under the respective Municipal Acts to conduct evictions without following the TVC‑mandated survey and rehabilitation process.

A critical gap lies in the absence of a centralised digital registry linking vending certificates to the National Urban Livelihoods Mission (NULM) database, which would enable portability of social‑security benefits such as the Pradhan Mantri Street Vendor’s Atmanirbhar Nidhi (PM‑SVANidhi) micro‑credit scheme. The 2021 amendment to the PM‑SVANidhi guidelines introduced a “vendor‑ID” linked to Aadhaar, yet many vendors lack Aadhaar enrolment due to documentation barriers. The 2023 CAG performance audit of the Act indicated that only about 12 % of TVCs had functional grievance‑redressal mechanisms, and that women vendors reported higher incidence of harassment and lower access to credit despite the statutory quota.

Judicial interventions continue to shape enforcement. In 2020, the Supreme Court directed all states to complete the vendor survey within six months and to ensure that TVCs include representatives from the Self‑Employed Women’s Association (SEWA) or equivalent collectives. The Court also emphasised that the “vending zone” delineation must be based on a participatory mapping exercise rather than top‑down zoning. Emerging good practices include the “Mumbai Street Vendor Management System”, a GIS‑based platform that integrates vendor locations, certificate status, and real‑time grievance tracking, and the “Bengaluru Vendor‑Friendly Streets” pilot that allocates dedicated vending corridors with sanitation and lighting infrastructure.

For aspirants, the key analytical points are: (i) the Act’s constitutional anchoring in Article 21 and Article 19(1)(g); (ii) the institutional design of TVCs as a model of participatory governance; (iii) the persistent implementation deficit caused by overlapping municipal bylaws, lack of fiscal devolution to ULBs, and weak vendor organisation; and (iv) the potential of digital public infrastructure (DPI) to bridge identification, credit access, and social‑protection portability for this workforce segment.

Gendered Access to Public Transport: Safety, Affordability and Modal Choice

Women’s mobility in Indian cities is shaped by a triad of safety concerns, fare affordability, and the spatial mismatch between residential locations and employment hubs. The 2013 Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, the 2018 Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, and the Nirbhaya Fund‑financed “Safe City” projects across eight metropolitan areas introduced measures such as women‑only coaches, panic buttons, CCTV surveillance, and dedicated helplines. The Delhi Metro, for instance, reserves the first coach of every train for women, a policy replicated in the Mumbai Metro, Bengaluru Namma Metro, and Chennai Metro.

Affordability remains a binding constraint. The 2022‑23 Household Consumer Expenditure Survey (HCES) indicates that the poorest urban quintile spends 12‑15 % of monthly per‑capita expenditure on transport, compared with 4‑5 % for the richest quintile. Subsidised “pink bus” services in states like Karnataka (Shakti scheme, 2023) and Tamil Nadu (free bus travel for women, 2021) aim to reduce this burden. Early evaluations of the Tamil Nadu scheme, based on Tamil Nadu State Transport Corporation (TNSTC) ridership data, suggest a 23 % increase in women’s bus trips within the first six months, with a pronounced rise among women aged 15‑35.

Safety audits conducted by the Centre for Science and Environment (CSE) in 2021 across 12 cities indicated that about 68 % of women respondents reported experiencing verbal harassment, and about 31 % reported physical molestation while using public transport. The “Gender‑Responsive Urban Mobility” framework, advocated by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs in its 2022 “Urban Mobility India” report, recommends: (a) gender‑disaggregated travel‑demand modelling; (b) last‑mile connectivity through women‑operated e‑rickshaws; (c) fare‑integration across modes using the National Common Mobility Card (NCMC); and (d) mandatory gender‑impact assessment for all new transit projects.

Modal choice is further influenced by the spatial distribution of informal employment. The 2020‑21 PLFS indicates that about 71 % of urban female workers are engaged in the informal sector, often in home‑based or piece‑rate work located in peripheral settlements with poor transit connectivity. The “15‑minute city” concept, piloted in the Pune Metropolitan Region’s “Pune Street Design Guidelines” (2021), seeks to align land‑use planning with non‑motorised transport (NMT) networks, thereby reducing travel time and exposure to unsafe environments.

Judicial pronouncements have reinforced the state’s duty to provide safe transport. In Delhi Commission for Women v. Union of India (2017), the Delhi High Court directed the Delhi Transport Corporation to install GPS‑enabled panic buttons in all buses and to ensure functional CCTV coverage. The Supreme Court, in a 2022 order on the “Nirbhaya Fund Utilisation”, mandated quarterly audits of fund expenditure by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, highlighting under‑utilisation in several states.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to: (i) cite the constitutional basis (Article 15(3), Article 21) for gender‑specific transport provisions; (ii) compare the fiscal design of state‑level free‑travel schemes versus central‑level capital grants under the Nirbhaya Fund; (iii) evaluate the effectiveness of technology‑centric solutions (CCTV, panic buttons) against community‑based interventions (women’s safety audits, marshals); and (iv) discuss the interplay between land‑use planning, informal employment geography, and gendered travel patterns.

Poverty Dynamics in the Context of the COVID‑19 Shock: Evidence from High‑Frequency Surveys

The COVID‑19 pandemic produced an abrupt reversal in India’s poverty trajectory. The World Bank’s “Poverty and Shared Prosperity 2022” report estimated that the national poverty rate (using the $2.15/day 2017 PPP line) rose from about 10.4 % in 2019 to 13.4 % in 2020, adding roughly 56 million people to the poor. High‑frequency phone surveys (HFPS) conducted by the World Bank in collaboration with the National Council of Applied Economic Research (NCAER) between April 2020 and March 2021 captured monthly consumption shocks, labour‑market exits, and coping strategies for a nationally representative panel of 12,000 households.

The HFPS data reveal three distinct phases: (i) an acute contraction in April‑May 2020, when about 84 % of informal workers reported zero earnings; (ii) a partial recovery from June 2020 onward, driven by agricultural resilience and the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) expansion, which saw person‑days rise to about 3.9 billion in FY 2020‑21 from 2.6 billion in FY 2019‑20; and (iii) a persistent “long‑COVID” poverty tail, with about 27 % of households that fell below the poverty line in 2020 still below it in March 2021, reflecting asset depletion, debt accumulation, and school‑dropout risk.

Consumption‑expenditure based poverty estimates from the 2022‑23 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES), released by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) in 2024, indicate a rural poverty headcount of about 12.8 % and urban poverty of about 9.2 % using the Tendulkar line, suggesting a gradual return toward pre‑pandemic levels but with a widened urban‑rural gap. The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) 2023, computed by NITI Aayog using NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) data, shows a decline in the national MPI from 0.117 (2015‑16) to 0.066 (2019‑21), yet the pandemic’s impact on nutrition, schooling, and sanitation indicators is not fully captured because NFHS‑5 fieldwork concluded before the first wave.

Policy responses that mitigated the shock include: (a) the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY), which provided 5 kg of free foodgrains per person per month to about 80 crore beneficiaries from April 2020 to December 2022; (b) the Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme (ECLGS) for MSMEs, disbursing about ₹3.5 lakh crore by March 2022; and (c) the expansion of the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) architecture, which enabled about ₹1.7 lakh crore of cash transfers to Jan Dhan accounts in FY 2020‑21. However, exclusion errors persisted: the 2021 “State of Working India” report by Azim Premji University estimated that about 30 % of eligible migrant workers lacked Jan Dhan accounts, and biometric authentication failures under the Aadhaar‑enabled Payment System (AePS) affected about 12 % of transactions in rural areas.

For analytical writing, aspirants should contrast the “income‑poverty” lens (consumption expenditure) with the “multidimensional” lens (MPI), explain why high‑frequency surveys are essential for capturing transient poverty, and evaluate the fiscal trade‑offs of universal versus targeted transfers in a shock context. The interplay between informal‑sector vulnerability, social‑protection portability, and digital delivery infrastructure constitutes a recurring theme in contemporary poverty discourse.

Globalisation, Trade Agreements and the Changing Structure of Agricultural Employment

India’s engagement with multilateral and bilateral trade agreements has reshaped the sectoral composition of rural employment. The World Trade Organization (WTO) Agreement on Agriculture (AoA), operational since 1995, committed India to bound tariff rates (averaging about 114 % for agricultural products) and a reduction in Aggregate Measurement of Support (AMS) from 10 % of the value of production (de‑minimis) to 5 % for developing countries. While India has largely used the “Special Products” and “Special Safeguard Mechanism” flexibilities, the progressive tariff liberalisation under the Information Technology Agreement (ITA‑II, 2015) and the India‑ASEAN Free Trade Agreement (2010) increased import competition in commodities such as palm oil, pepper, and rubber.

Empirical studies using the National Sample Survey (NSS) 68th round (2011‑12) and PLFS 2022‑23 data show a decline in the share of agriculture in total employment from about 46.2 % (2011‑12) to about 42.5 % (2022‑23), while the absolute number of agricultural workers fell marginally from about 263 million to about 258 million, reflecting population growth. The “feminisation of agriculture” trend — women’s share in agricultural workforce rising from about 32 % to about 37 % over the same period — is partly attributed to male out‑migration to construction and services, a process accelerated by the construction boom linked to infrastructure FDI inflows under the “Make in India” initiative (2014 onward).

Bilateral agreements such as the India‑UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA, 2022) and the India‑Australia Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA, 2022) include tariff‑rate quotas (TRQs) for dairy, horticulture, and processed foods. The Department of Commerce’s 2023 impact assessment projects a 4‑6 % increase in agricultural exports to these partners by 2027, but also warns of import surges in dairy products that could depress domestic farm‑gate prices for smallholders. The 2020‑21 “Farmers’ Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act” (later repealed) and the 2022 “Agricultural Marketing Reforms” under the Model APMC Act aim to integrate farmers with national and global value chains, yet the persistence of fragmented landholdings (average about 1.08 ha per operational holding, Agriculture Census 2015‑16) limits scale economies.

Labour‑standard provisions in newer FTAs — such as the “Labour Chapter” in the India‑UK FTA negotiations (ongoing as of 2024) — introduce commitments on freedom of association, collective bargaining, and occupational safety. These clauses have implications for the estimated 10 million workers in agro‑processing units, many of whom are informal and lack social‑security coverage under the Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) Act. The 2020 Code on Social Security extends ESI and Provident Fund coverage to establishments with 10 or more workers, but enforcement in seasonal agro‑processing remains weak.

Key take‑aways for answer writing: (i) distinguish between tariff liberalisation effects (price transmission) and non‑tariff measures (sanitary‑phytosanitary standards) on cropping patterns; (ii) link trade‑induced crop‑mix changes to gendered labour allocation, citing the rise in women’s participation in high‑value horticulture; (iii) assess the adequacy of domestic labour‑law reforms in meeting FTA labour‑chapter obligations; and (iv) evaluate the role of public procurement (e.g., Minimum Support Price operations) as a buffer against import volatility.

Intersectional Vulnerabilities of Transgender Persons in Urban Labour Markets

The 2011 Census recorded a transgender population of about 4.9 lakh (0.04 % of total population), a figure widely acknowledged as an undercount due to stigma and enumerator bias. The Supreme Court’s landmark judgment in National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (2014) recognised transgender persons as a “third gender” and directed central and state governments to extend reservations in education and public employment, provide separate HIV‑surveillance centres, and ensure access to public amenities. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019, operationalised these directives, defining a transgender person, prohibiting discrimination in employment, education, and healthcare, and establishing a National Council for Transgender Persons.

Despite the legal framework, labour‑market outcomes remain precarious. The 2022‑23 PLFS does not disaggregate by gender identity, but a 2021 study by the Centre for Sexuality and Health Research and Policy (C‑SHARP) covering 1,200 transgender respondents across six metros found that about 62 % were engaged in informal work — begging, sex work, or daily‑wage labour — and only about 14 % held regular salaried jobs. The study reported a median monthly income of about ₹6,500, less than half the urban poverty line consumption expenditure (about ₹1,286 per capita per month, Tendulkar 2011‑12 adjusted). Discriminatory hiring practices, lack of gender‑affirming documentation (e.g., Aadhaar gender marker change requires a medical certificate, which many cannot afford), and workplace harassment were cited as primary barriers.

As of the 2023‑24 annual report, about 3,200 transgender persons had been enrolled, with a placement rate of about 28 % in formal sectors such as hospitality, retail, and business‑process outsourcing. Urban Water Security and Gendered Access in Informal Settlements

Access to reliable piped water remains uneven across Indian cities. The 2011 Census recorded that roughly 30 % of urban households lacked a tap water connection on premises, a gap that widens sharply in notified slums and non‑notified settlements. The National Sample Survey (NSS) 76th round (2018) on drinking water, sanitation and hygiene confirmed that households in the lowest consumption quintile depend predominantly on public stand‑posts, tankers or untreated groundwater. Women and girls bear the bulk of water‑collection labour; time‑use surveys consistently show that adult women in low‑income urban neighbourhoods spend 2–3 hours per day fetching water, compared with less than 30 minutes for men.

Infrastructure deficits are compounded by governance fragmentation. Water supply is a state subject, but urban local bodies (ULBs) often lack financial autonomy to maintain distribution networks. The Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT) and the Jal Jeevan Mission (Urban) aim to universalise functional household tap connections, yet implementation data from the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs indicate that a majority of targeted connections in slum clusters remained uncommissioned as of 2023. Intermittent supply — often limited to 2–4 hours a day — forces households to store water in containers, raising contamination risks. Studies by the Indian Council of Medical Research link stored‑water practices to higher incidence of diarrhoeal disease among children under five in informal settlements.

Gender‑responsive design can mitigate these burdens. Recent “Gender‑Responsive Urban Water Supply Guidelines” issued by the Ministry of Jal Shakti recommend (i) locating stand‑posts within 200 metres of dwellings, (ii) providing separate queuing lanes for women, and (iii) integrating women’s self‑help groups (SHGs) into operation‑and‑maintenance contracts. Pilot projects in Pune and Bhubaneswar, where SHGs manage community water kiosks, report a significant reduction in collection time and a measurable decline in water‑borne illness. However, scaling such models requires (a) dedicated budget lines in ULB annual plans, (b) capacity‑building for women’s collectives in technical maintenance, and (c) robust grievance‑redressal mechanisms linked to the municipal ward committee.

From an examination perspective, candidates should be able to: (i) trace the constitutional division of water‑supply responsibilities between the Centre, states and ULBs; (ii) evaluate the effectiveness of AMRUT and Jal Jeevan Mission (Urban) against the Sustainable Development Goal 6.1 target of universal and equitable access; (iii) analyse how gendered time‑poverty interacts with labour‑force participation, using the concept of “time‑use elasticity” from feminist economics; and (iv) propose institutional reforms such as mandatory gender‑audit of water‑service delivery and inclusion of women’s representatives in the ULB standing committee on water supply.

Sanitation, Menstrual Health Management and Women's Dignity in Urban Slums

Sanitation coverage has expanded dramatically under the Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban) (SBM‑U) and its second phase (SBM‑U 2.0, launched 2021). The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs reports that over 4,300 urban local bodies achieved Open Defecation Free (ODF) status by 2022, yet the quality of facilities in dense informal settlements remains a concern. The National Family Health Survey‑5 (2019‑21) shows that a significant proportion of urban women aged 15‑49 still lack access to a private, functional toilet, with the figure rising substantially in non‑notified slums. Shared community toilets often suffer from inadequate lighting, broken locks, and irregular cleaning, creating safety risks especially after dark.

Menstrual health management (MHM) adds a gender‑specific layer to sanitation deficits. The 2020 “Menstrual Hygiene Management Guidelines for Urban Areas” issued by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare mandate (i) separate, lockable cubicles with running water and soap, (ii) provision of low‑cost sanitary napkin vending machines, and (iii) safe disposal mechanisms such as incinerators or deep‑burial pits. Implementation audits by the Centre for Policy Research in 2022 across six metros found that a small minority of community toilet complexes met all three criteria. Girls in government schools located in slum catchments report absenteeism of 1–2 days per month due to inadequate MHM facilities, a pattern corroborated by multiple education surveys.

Community‑led models demonstrate viable pathways. In Mumbai’s “Slum Sanitation Programme”, women’s collectives operate and maintain toilet blocks under a performance‑based contract with the Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai. The model includes a revolving fund for consumables (soap, napkins) and a transparent user‑fee system that cross‑subsidises the poorest households. Early evaluations indicate a notable increase in regular toilet use by women and a measurable reduction in reported urinary‑tract infections. Scaling such approaches requires (a) earmarked MHM budgets within SBM‑U 2.0, (b) integration of MHM indicators into the Swachh Survekshan ranking framework, and (c) capacity‑building for ULB engineers on gender‑sensitive design standards prescribed in the Model Building Bye‑Laws (2021).

Social Protection Convergence: Integrated Delivery for Migrant and Informal Workers

India’s social‑protection architecture remains fragmented across multiple ministries and schemes, creating exclusion errors for circular migrants and informal workers who move across state boundaries. The National Food Security Act (NFSA) 2013 provides subsidised grain through the Public Distribution System (PDS), but portability of ration entitlements was limited until the “One Nation One Ration Card” (ONORC) initiative, rolled out nationally in 2020. By March 2023, 35 states and union territories had integrated their PDS databases with the central Integrated Management of Public Distribution System (IM‑PDS) portal, enabling inter‑state transaction of food grains. However, the Economic Survey 2022‑23 notes that a substantial share of eligible migrant households had not successfully accessed ONORC benefits, citing biometric authentication failures and lack of awareness as primary bottlenecks.

Health coverage presents a parallel challenge. The Ayushman Bharat‑Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB‑PMJAY) covers secondary and tertiary hospitalisation for the bottom 40 % of the population, yet enrolment is tied to the Socio‑Economic and Caste Census (SECC) 2011, which omits many recent migrants. The 2021 “Health ID” under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission aims to create a portable digital health record, but adoption among informal workers remains low due to digital literacy gaps. The Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) Act, 1948, covers formal‑sector employees; the 2020 Social Security Code extends ESI‑like benefits to gig and platform workers, but the rules for contribution collection and benefit portability are still under finalisation as of 2024.

Convergence pilots illustrate potential gains. Kerala’s migrant welfare initiatives integrate PDS, health insurance, and a portable pension scheme under a single registration number. An evaluation by the Institute for Human Development (2022) found that registered migrants experienced reduced out‑of‑pocket health expenditure and improved food‑security scores compared with non‑registered peers. The central government’s “National Database of Unorganised Workers” (NDUW), launched in 2021, seeks to replicate such convergence by assigning a unique Universal Account Number (UAN) linked to Aadhaar, enabling seamless access to PDS, PMJAY, and the Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan‑Dhan (PMSYM) pension. As of December 2023, the NDUW covered over 28 crore workers, though KYC completion for benefit portability remains low.

Key policy levers for deeper convergence include: (i) mandating interoperable APIs between IM‑PDS, AB‑PMJAY, and the NDUW to enable real‑time eligibility verification; (ii) establishing a “Social Protection Single Window” at the district level, staffed by trained facilitators who assist migrants with documentation and grievance redressal; (iii) indexing contribution rates in the Social Security Code to the Consumer Price Index (Industrial Workers) to preserve real value of pensions; and (iv) leveraging the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) architecture to deliver cash‑top‑ups for food, health and pension in a single monthly transfer, reducing transaction costs for beneficiaries.

Examination focus areas: (i) constitutional basis for central versus state legislation on social security (Entry 23, Concurrent List); (ii) the role of Aadhaar as an enabler and its privacy safeguards under the Aadhaar Act, 2016 and the Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment; (iii) quantitative assessment of exclusion errors using the “inclusion‑exclusion” framework from the NSS 78th round (2020‑21) on migration; and (iv) drafting a convergence roadmap that aligns the 15th Finance Commission’s health‑grant conditionalities with state‑level migrant‑welfare boards.

Gendered Effects of Urban Air Pollution on Health and Labour Productivity

Urban air quality in India routinely exceeds the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀). The Central Pollution Control Board’s (CPCB) 2022‑23 annual report shows that 132 cities failed to meet the annual PM₂.₅ standard of 40 µg/m³, with average concentrations in the Indo‑Gangetic Plain exceeding 100 µg/m³ during winter. Women in low‑income households face disproportionate exposure due to (a) reliance on solid‑fuel cookstoves in poorly ventilated dwellings, (b) occupational concentration in informal sectors such as waste‑picking, construction, and street‑vending where outdoor exposure is continuous, and (c) limited access to protective equipment or indoor air‑purification technologies.

Health impact studies quantify this disparity. The “Global Burden of Disease” (GBD) 2019 estimates attribute 1.67 million premature deaths in India to ambient and household air pollution, with women accounting for 52 % of household‑air‑pollution‑related deaths. A 2021 cohort study by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) in Delhi’s resettlement colonies found that women exposed to PM₂.₅ levels above 60 µg/m³ had significantly higher odds of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) compared with men in the same environment, after adjusting for smoking status. Pregnant women exposed to high PM₂.₅ show increased risk of low birth weight and pre‑term delivery, as documented in multiple peer‑reviewed studies.

Labour‑productivity losses are equally gendered. The International Labour Organization (ILO) 2019 “Working on a Warmer Planet” report estimates that heat‑stress‑adjusted productivity losses in India could reach 5.8 % of total working hours by 2030, with outdoor informal workers bearing the brunt. Women street‑vendors and construction labourers report a substantial reduction in daily earnings on high‑pollution days, according to a 2020 survey by the Self‑Employed Women’s Association (SEWA) across Ahmedabad and Delhi. The loss is amplified by caregiving responsibilities: when children fall ill due to pollution‑induced respiratory episodes, women withdraw from paid work to provide care, creating a double burden of income loss and time‑poverty.

Policy responses are emerging but remain fragmented. The National Clean Air Programme (NCAP), launched in 2019, targets a 20‑30 % reduction in PM₂.₅ by 2024 in 132 non‑attainment cities, yet its city‑level action plans rarely incorporate gender‑disaggregated exposure assessments. The “Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana” (PMUY) has provided over 9 crore LPG connections since 2016, reducing household solid‑fuel use, but refill affordability remains a barrier; the 2022‑23 Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell data show that only 58 % of PMUY beneficiaries purchased at least four refills per year. The “Construction Workers’ Welfare Cess” funds, mandated under the Building and Other Construction Workers (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1996, could finance on‑site air‑quality monitoring and provision of N‑95 masks, but utilisation reports indicate a small fraction of cess revenue is spent on occupational health.

For the exam, candidates should be able to: (i) explain the exposure‑response pathway linking PM₂.₅ to cardiovascular and respiratory morbidity, citing the WHO 2021 Air Quality Guidelines; (ii) apply the “gender‑differentiated vulnerability” framework (UNDP, 2019) to urban air‑pollution policy design; (iii) evaluate the cost‑effectiveness of LPG refill subsidies versus community‑level clean‑cooking hubs; and (iv) propose a gender‑mainstreamed NCAP revision that mandates (a) sex‑disaggregated health‑impact monitoring, (b) targeted distribution of certified masks to women informal workers, and (c) integration of air‑quality alerts with the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) work‑schedule adjustments for urban‑peri‑urban workers.
