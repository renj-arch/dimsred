1. Geomorphology: Landforms, Processes & Tectonics

1.1 Earth's Interior & Plate Tectonics

  • Internal Structure: Crust (continental 30–70 km, oceanic 5–10 km), Moho discontinuity, Mantle (upper mantle + asthenosphere 100–350 km, transition zone 410–660 km, lower mantle), Gutenberg discontinuity, Outer Core (liquid Fe-Ni, 2,900–5,100 km), Lehmann discontinuity, Inner Core (solid Fe-Ni, 5,100–6,371 km). Seismic waves (P, S, surface) reveal boundaries. (Source: PREM Model, Dziewonski & Anderson 1981; Stein & Wysession, An Introduction to Seismology, 2003)
  • Plate Tectonics (Wilson, Morgan, McKenzie, Parker 1960s): Lithosphere divided into 7 major + 8 minor plates moving on asthenosphere (2–15 cm/yr). Driving forces — mantle convection (ridge push, slab pull), gravitational sliding. (Source: Morgan, W.J., Plate Tectonics, 1968; McKenzie & Parker, 1967; Wilson, J.T., 1965)
  • Plate Boundaries: Divergent (mid-ocean ridges — seafloor spreading, basaltic volcanism, shallow earthquakes); Convergent (ocean-ocean — island arcs, trenches; ocean-continent — Andean-type mountains; continent-continent — Himalayas); Transform (strike-slip faults — San Andreas, shallow earthquakes). (Source: Bird, P., Plate Boundaries, 2003; USGS Plate Boundary Data)
  • Hotspots: Mantle plumes (Hawaii, Yellowstone, Réunion, Iceland) — fixed relative to moving plates, create volcanic chains. Age progression confirms plate motion. (Source: Wilson, J.T., Hotspot Theory, 1963; Sleep, M., Hotspot Volcanism, 1990)

1.2 Geomorphic Processes & Landforms

  • Endogenic (Internal): Diastrophism — orogeny (mountain building: folding, faulting, thrusting), epeirogeny (continental uplift/warping). Volcanism — extrusive (lava flows, pyroclasts, shield/strato volcanoes), intrusive (batholiths, laccoliths, dykes, sills). Earthquakes — focus, epicentre, body/surface waves, magnitude (Richter/Moment), intensity (Mercalli). (Source: Summerfield, M.A., Global Geomorphology, 1991; Bloom, A.L., Geomorphology, 1998)
  • Exogenic (External): Weathering — physical (frost wedging, thermal expansion, exfoliation), chemical (solution, oxidation, hydrolysis, carbonation), biological. Mass wasting — creep, slide, flow, fall. Erosion/Deposition agents — running water (fluvial), wind (aeolian), glaciers, groundwater (karst), waves/currents (coastal). (Source: Ritter, D.F., Process Geomorphology, 2011; Goudie, A., Encyclopaedia of Geomorphology, 2004)

1.4 Drainage Patterns & River Systems

  • Drainage Patterns: Dendritic (homogeneous rock — most common), Trellis (folded strata — Appalachians, Satpura), Radial (domes/volcanoes — Amarkantak), Rectangular (jointed/faulted rock — Canadian Shield), Parallel (steep slopes — Western Ghats), Annular (structural domes — Black Hills). (Source: Howard, A.D., Drainage Analysis, 1967; Strahler, A.N., Quantitative Geomorphology, 1964)
  • River Regimes: Himalayan (perennial — snow + monsoon: Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra), Peninsular (monsoon-fed: Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Kaveri, Narmada, Tapti). (Source: Rao, K.L., India's Water Wealth, 1979; CWC River Basin Atlas)

1.5 Earthquakes & Volcanoes — Distribution & Hazard

  • Circum-Pacific Ring of Fire: ~75% world's volcanoes, ~90% earthquakes — convergent boundaries (Japan, Philippines, Indonesia, Andes, Cascades, Aleutians). (Source: USGS; Simkin & Siebert, Volcanoes of the World, 1994)
  • Alpine-Himalayan Belt: ~15% earthquakes — continent-continent collision (Mediterranean, Iran, Himalayas, Burma). (Source: UNESCO Seismic Hazard Maps; ISC-GEM Catalogue)
  • Mid-Ocean Ridges: Shallow earthquakes, basaltic volcanism (Iceland, Azores, East Pacific Rise). (Source: NOAA Ocean Explorer; InterRidge Database)
  • India Seismic Zoning (BIS 1893:2016): Zone V (very high — NE, Kutch, Uttarakhand, HP, J&K), Zone IV (high — Delhi, Bihar, WB, Maharashtra), Zone III (moderate), Zone II (low). (Source: IS 1893:2016; NDMA Seismic Zoning Map; BMTPC)
PYQ 2021 GS I
"Explain the formation of the Himalayas and its impact on the climate of the Indian subcontinent." — Answer: Formation — Indian plate northward drift (15 cm/yr post-Gondwana breakup), collision with Eurasian plate ~50 Ma, Tethys closure, crustal shortening (Main Central Thrust, Main Boundary Thrust, Himalayan Frontal Thrust), ongoing uplift (5 mm/yr). Climate impact — orographic barrier to SW monsoon (rainfall on windward slopes), rain shadow (Tibetan plateau cold desert), winter western disturbances, temperature gradient drives monsoon, glaciers feed perennial rivers. (Source: PYQ 2021 GS I; Valdiya, K., Dynamics of Himalaya, 1998; Bookhagen & Burbank, 2010)
Technique: Plate boundary identification from landforms
Question: "Identify the plate boundary type from the following: (a) Mid-Atlantic Ridge, (b) Andes, (c) San Andreas Fault, (d) Himalayas, (e) Mariana Trench."
Answer: (a) Divergent — seafloor spreading, basaltic volcanism, shallow EQs. (b) Ocean-continent convergent — subduction, Andean volcanism, trench (Peru-Chile), deep EQs (Benioff zone). (c) Transform — strike-slip, shallow EQs, no volcanism. (d) Continent-continent convergent — crustal shortening, fold mountains, no subduction, shallow-intermediate EQs. (e) Ocean-ocean convergent — subduction, island arc (Mariana Islands), deep trench (deepest point), deep EQs.

2. Climatology: Atmosphere, Circulation & Climate Systems

2.1 Atmosphere — Composition, Structure & Heat Budget

  • Composition (dry air): N₂ 78.08%, O₂ 20.95%, Ar 0.93%, CO₂ ~420 ppm (2024, Mauna Loa), trace gases (Ne, He, CH₄, Kr, H₂, N₂O, O₃). Variable — H₂O (0–4%), aerosols, pollutants. (Source: NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory; IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 2)
  • Vertical Structure (temperature gradient): Troposphere (0–12 km, -6.5°C/km lapse rate, weather), Tropopause, Stratosphere (12–50 km, +ve gradient — ozone absorption), Stratopause, Mesosphere (50–85 km, -ve gradient), Mesopause, Thermosphere (85–600 km, +ve — solar radiation), Exosphere (>600 km). (Source: US Standard Atmosphere 1976; WMO)
  • Heat Budget: Insolation 100 units → 30 reflected (albedo — clouds 20, surface 6, atmosphere 4), 20 absorbed by atmosphere (ozone, H₂O, CO₂, clouds), 50 absorbed by surface. Surface re-radiates longwave → 34 trapped by GHGs (greenhouse effect), 16 direct to space. Net radiation +ve equator to 35°N/S, -ve poles → meridional heat transport. (Source: Kiehl & Trenberth, Earth's Energy Budget, 1997; Wild et al., 2019; IPCC AR6)

2.2 Pressure Belts & Planetary Winds

  • Pressure Belts (thermally/rotationally induced): Equatorial Low (ITCZ, 5°N–5°S, convection), Subtropical Highs (30°N/S, subsidence — Horse Latitudes), Subpolar Lows (60°N/S, convergence of westerlies/polar easterlies), Polar Highs (90°N/S, cold subsidence). Seasonal shift ~5–10° latitude with solar declination. (Source: Peixoto & Oort, Physics of Climate, 1992; Hartmann, Global Physical Climatology, 1994)
  • Planetary Winds: Trade Winds (subtropical high → equatorial low, NE in NH, SE in SH — Coriolis), Westerlies (subtropical high → subpolar low, SW in NH, NW in SH — variable, stormy), Polar Easterlies (polar high → subpolar low, NE in NH, SE in SH). (Source: Wallace & Hobbs, Atmospheric Science, 2006)
  • Monsoons (seasonal reversal): Thermal (land-sea differential heating) + dynamic (ITCZ shift, upper tropospheric westerly jet, Tibetan Plateau heating). Indian Monsoon — SW (June–Sep, 75% rainfall), NE (Oct–Dec, TN coast). (Source: Webster et al., Monsoons, 1998; Gadgil, Indian Monsoon, 2003; IMD Monsoon Reports)

2.3 Air Masses, Fronts & Cyclones

  • Air Masses: Source regions (polar, tropical, continental, maritime) — cP, mP, cT, mT. Modification during movement. (Source: Bergeron, Air Mass Analysis, 1930; Petterssen, Weather Analysis, 1956)
  • Fronts: Cold front (cold displaces warm, steep, thunderstorms), Warm front (warm overrides cold, gentle, stratiform rain), Occluded front (cold overtakes warm), Stationary front. Mid-latitude cyclones (temperate) — polar front theory (Bjerknes), wave cyclone, life cycle. (Source: Bjerknes, J., Cyclone Model, 1922; Carlson, Mid-Latitude Weather Systems, 1998)
  • Tropical Cyclones: Warm core, ocean-born (>26.5°C SST, >50m depth), low Coriolis (≥5° lat), low shear, upper divergence. Stages — TD, TS, Hurricane/Typhoon/Cyclone (Saffir-Simpson). Eye, eyewall, spiral rainbands. Names — Atlantic/E Pacific: Hurricane; W Pacific: Typhoon; N Indian Ocean: Cyclone. (Source: Emanuel, K., Tropical Cyclones, 1987; WMO Tropical Cyclone Programme; IMD Cyclone Tracks)

2.4 Climate Classification (Köppen-Geiger)

  • Five Major Groups: A — Tropical (Af, Am, Aw/As); B — Dry (BW — desert, BS — steppe, h/k — hot/cold); C — Temperate (Cf — no dry season, Cw — winter dry, Cs — summer dry); D — Continental (Df, Dw); E — Polar (ET — tundra, EF — ice cap). (Source: Köppen, Climatology, 1936; Kottek et al., World Map of Köppen-Geiger, 2006; Peel et al., 2007)
  • Key Climate Regions: Equatorial (Amazon, Congo, SE Asia — Af), Monsoon (India, SE Asia, N Australia — Am), Savannah (Aw — India interior, Africa, Brazil), Hot Desert (BWh — Sahara, Thar, Arabian, Atacama), Mediterranean (Cs — California, Chile, Cape Town, SW Australia), Mid-latitude Desert (BWk — Gobi, Patagonia, Ladakh), Steppe (BS — Prairie, Pampas, Veldt, Downs), Taiga (Df/Dw — Siberia, Canada), Tundra (ET — Arctic coast). (Source: Peel et al., Updated Köppen-Geiger Map, 2007; Critchfield, General Climatology, 1983)

2.5 Climate Variability & Change

  • ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation): El Niño — warming of E/C Pacific (weakens trade winds, reduces upwelling, shifts convection eastward → weak Indian monsoon, wet Peru, dry Australia/Indonesia); La Niña — cooling (stronger trades, enhanced upwelling → strong Indian monsoon, dry Peru, wet Australia). ENSO cycle 2–7 years. IOD (Indian Ocean Dipole) — positive IOD enhances Indian monsoon. (Source: Philander, El Niño, La Niña, 1990; NOAA CPC ENSO Diagnostics; IMD Monsoon-ENSO Reports)
  • Global Warming: +1.1°C above pre-industrial (IPCC AR6 2021), CO₂ 420 ppm, CH₄ 1,900 ppb. Impacts — sea level rise (3.7 mm/yr), extreme events (heatwaves, floods, cyclones intensification), cryosphere loss (Arctic ice -13%/decade, glaciers), ecosystem shifts. (Source: IPCC AR6 WGI 2021; WMO State of Climate 2023; NOAA Climate.gov)
PYQ 2019 GS I
"How do the Indian monsoons influence the agricultural calendar and economy of the country?" — Answer: SW monsoon (June–Sep) — kharif sowing (rice, cotton, soybean, maize, pulses), 50% net sown area rainfed; NE monsoon (Oct–Dec) — rabi in TN/AP (rice), kharif late. Variability — early/late onset, breaks, excess/deficit → drought/flood, GDP impact (agri 16% GVA, 42% employment). IMD long-range forecasts, MSP, crop insurance (PMFBY), irrigation (PMKSY), contingency plans. (Source: PYQ 2019 GS I; IMD Monsoon Reports; Economic Survey Agriculture Chapter)
Technique: Monsoon mechanism diagram
Question: "Explain the role of the Tibetan Plateau in the Indian monsoon."
Answer: Tibetan Plateau (avg 4,500m) — elevated heat source in summer (sensible heating), creates massive low pressure (thermal low), draws ITCZ northward to 25–30°N, intensifies cross-equatorial flow (Findlater Jet/Somali Jet) bringing moisture. Acts as "monsoon engine". Winter — elevated cold high, strengthens NE monsoon. Upper-level easterly jet (TEJ) over Peninsula — divergence aloft, convergence below. (Source: Yanai et al., Tibetan Plateau Heating, 1992; Gadgil, Indian Monsoon, 2003)

    • 3.3 Ocean Currents — Drivers, Patterns & Climate Impact

    • Drivers: Wind (Ekman transport, Sverdrup balance), Coriolis, Density (thermohaline — temperature/salinity), Topography (bathymetry). (Source: Vallis, Atmospheric and Oceanic Fluid Dynamics, 2017; Talley et al., Descriptive Physical Oceanography, 2011)
    • Surface Gyres (subtropical): N/S Pacific, N/S Atlantic, Indian Ocean — anticyclonic in NH, cyclonic in SH. Western boundary currents (intensified) — Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, Agulhas, East Australian (warm, narrow, fast). Eastern boundary currents — California, Canary, Peru/Humboldt, Benguela, West Australian (cold, broad, slow, upwelling). (Source: Stommel, Gulf Stream, 1948; Munk, Wind-Driven Circulation, 1950; Schott et al., 2009)
    • Equatorial Currents: North/South Equatorial (westerly), Equatorial Countercurrent (easterly), Equatorial Undercurrent (Cromwell, easterly subsurface). (Source: Knauss, Introduction to Physical Oceanography, 1997)
    • Thermohaline Circulation (Global Conveyor Belt): NADW formation (N Atlantic — cooling, sinking), AABW (Antarctic — brine rejection), upwelling (N Pacific, Southern Ocean). Timescale ~1,000 years. AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation) — weakening observed (Caesar et al. 2018). (Source: Broecker, Great Ocean Conveyor, 1991; Rahmstorf, AMOC, 2002; Caesar et al., Nature 2018)
    • Climate Impact: Warm currents → mild winters, high rainfall (NW Europe — Gulf Stream/North Atlantic Drift); Cold currents → aridity, fog (Atacama — Peru Current, Namib — Benguela, California); Upwelling zones → high productivity (Peru, California, NW Africa, Somalia, Benguela) → major fisheries. (Source: Reid, Ocean Circulation Climate, 1961; Pauly & Christensen, Fisheries Oceanography, 1995)

    3.4 Tides, Waves & Coastal Processes

    • Tides: Gravitational (moon 68%, sun 32%), centrifugal. Spring (syzygy — new/full moon, higher range), Neap (quadrature — 1st/3rd quarter, lower range). Semi-diurnal (2 high/low), Diurnal (1), Mixed. Amphidromic points. Tidal energy — La Rance (France), Sihwa (Korea), Gulf of Kutch/Khambhat potential. (Source: Proudman & Doodson, Tides, 1953; NOAA Tides & Currents; NIOT Tidal Energy Reports)
    • Waves: Wind-generated (fetch, duration, velocity), Swell. Wave refraction, diffraction, reflection. Coastal erosion — hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution. Deposition — beaches, spits, bars, tombolos, barrier islands. (Source: Komar, Beach Processes, 1998; Davis & Fitzgerald, Beaches and Coasts, 2004)
    • Coral Reefs: Fringing (shore-attached), Barrier (lagoon), Atoll (lagoon, no island — Darwin's subsidence theory). Conditions: 20–30°C, saline (35–38 ppt), clear, shallow (<50m), sunlight. Symbiosis — zooxanthellae. Threats — bleaching (thermal stress), acidification, pollution, crown-of-thorns. (Source: Darwin, Coral Reefs, 1842; Veron, Corals of the World, 2000; IPCC AR6 Ch. 3)

    3.5 Marine Resources & Law of the Sea

    • Living Resources: Fisheries — 178 Mt (2022, FAO), 90% stocks fully/over-exploited. Aquaculture 130 Mt (57%). Major grounds — NW Pacific, SE Pacific, NE Atlantic, W Central Pacific. (Source: FAO SOFIA 2024; World Bank Fisheries Data)
    • Non-Living: Oil/Gas (continental shelves — Persian Gulf, Gulf of Mexico, North Sea, Bombay High), Polymetallic Nodules (Clarion-Clipperton Zone — Mn, Ni, Cu, Co), Cobalt-rich Crusts (seamounts), Hydrothermal Vents (Cu, Zn, Au, Ag), Methane Hydrates (continental slopes). (Source: ISA Exploration Contracts; USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries; NIO India Deep Sea Mission)
    • UNCLOS 1982: Territorial Sea (12 nm), Contiguous Zone (24 nm), EEZ (200 nm — sovereign rights over resources), Continental Shelf (200 nm or natural prolongation), High Seas (freedom), The Area (seabed beyond — common heritage, ISA). India — 15 EEZ claims, CLCS submissions, ISA pioneer investor (CCZ, Indian Ocean). (Source: UNCLOS 1982; MEA India UNCLOS Submissions; ISA Council Reports)
    PYQ 2018 GS I
    "Explain the factors responsible for the origin of ocean currents. How do they influence regional climates?" — Answer: Factors — planetary winds (Ekman), Coriolis, density differences (thermohaline), bottom topography. Influence — warm currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, North Atlantic Drift) warm western coasts, increase rainfall; cold currents (Labrador, Peru, Benguela, California) create deserts, fog, upwelling fisheries. Examples — NW Europe mild winters (NAD), Atacama/Namib deserts (cold currents), Grand Banks fishing (Gulf Stream + Labrador convergence). (Source: PYQ 2018 GS I; Reid 1961; Pauly & Christensen 1995)
    Technique: Current-climate linkage
    Question: "Why is the climate of Western Europe milder than eastern Canada at the same latitude?"
    Answer: North Atlantic Drift (warm, extension of Gulf Stream) flows past Western Europe → warm moist winds → mild winters, ice-free ports. Eastern Canada receives cold Labrador Current → cold winds, sea ice, harsher winters. Prevailing westerlies transport maritime influence inland. Same latitude (45–60°N), different oceanic heat advection. (Source: Reid, 1961; IPCC AR6 Atlas)

    4. Biogeography: Soils, Vegetation, Biodiversity & Conservation

    4.1 Soil Formation, Classification & Distribution

    • Soil Forming Factors (Jenny, 1941): Climate (precipitation, temperature — pedogenic threshold), Parent Material (rock type), Topography (slope, aspect, drainage), Organisms (vegetation, microbes, fauna), Time (maturity). (Source: Jenny, H., Factors of Soil Formation, 1941; Brady & Weil, Nature and Properties of Soils, 2016)
    • Soil Horizons: O (organic), A (topsoil — humus + mineral), E (eluviation — leached), B (subsoil — illuviation, accumulation), C (parent material), R (bedrock). (Source: USDA Soil Taxonomy, 12th Ed. 2014; WRB 2022)
    • Major Soil Orders (USDA) / Reference Groups (WRB) — Global Distribution: Alfisols/Luvisols (temperate deciduous forests), Mollisols/Chernozems (grasslands — prairie, steppe, pampas, veldt, downs — high fertility), Aridisols/Calcisols (deserts — low organic, calcic/gypsic horizons), Oxisols/Ferralsols (tropical rainforest — laterite, deep weathering, low fertility), Ultisols/Acrisols (subtropical humid — acidic, clay accumulation), Spodosols/Podzols (boreal coniferous — Fe/Al-humus illuviation), Histosols (wetlands — peat), Vertisols (smectite clay — shrink-swell, Deccan black cotton), Andisols (volcanic ash — high P retention), Gelisols (permafrost). (Source: USDA Keys to Soil Taxonomy 2014; IUSS WRB 2022; FAO Soil Map of the World)
    • Indian Soils (ICAR): Alluvial (Indo-Gangetic, Brahmaputra — 43%), Black/Regur (Deccan Traps — 17%, cotton), Red (Peninsular gneiss — 10%), Laterite (Western Ghats, NE hills — 5%), Desert/Arid (Thar), Saline/Alkaline (Indus, Ganga delta), Peaty/Marshy (Kerala, Sunderbans), Mountain (Himalayas). (Source: ICAR Soil Atlas of India; NBSS&LUP Publications)

    4.2 Natural Vegetation & Biomes

    • Forest Biomes: Tropical Rainforest (Af — Amazon, Congo, Western Ghats, Andamans — multilayered, evergreen, high diversity), Tropical Deciduous (Aw — Monsoon forests: Teak, Sal, Sandalwood — dry season leaf shed), Subtropical (Cs — Mediterranean: sclerophyllous — oak, olive, pine), Temperate Broadleaf (Cf/Df — oak, maple, beech), Temperate Coniferous (Df — Taiga: spruce, fir, pine, larch — largest biome), Boreal (Dw — larch), Montane (vertical zonation). (Source: Whittaker, Communities and Ecosystems, 1975; Olson et al., Terrestrial Ecoregions, 2001; WWF Global 200)
    • Grassland Biomes: Tropical Savanna (Aw — acacia, grasses, fire-maintained — Africa, Cerrado, N Australia), Temperate Grassland (BS — Prairie, Steppe, Pampas, Veldt, Downs — deep Mollisols), Flooded Grasslands (Pantanal, Okavango). (Source: Archer, Grassland Ecology, 2001; White, Grasslands of the World, 2000)
    • Desert & Tundra: Hot Desert (BWh — xerophytes: cacti, succulents, ephemerals), Cold Desert (BWk — Ladakh, Gobi, Patagonia — sparse shrubs), Tundra (ET — mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs, permafrost). (Source: Cody, Desert Ecology, 1986; Bliss, Tundra Ecosystems, 1981)

    4.3 Biodiversity — Patterns, Hotspots & Conservation

    • Latitudinal Gradient: Species richness peaks at equator, declines poleward — hypotheses: energy (solar), stability, area, evolutionary time, niche conservatism. (Source: Willig et al., Latitudinal Gradient, 2003; Mittelbach et al., 2007)
    • Biodiversity Hotspots (Myers et al. 2000): 36 global hotspots — ≥1,500 endemic vascular plants, ≤30% original habitat. India's neighbourhood — Western Ghats & Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma (NE India, Myanmar, Thailand), Himalaya (Eastern Himalaya), Sundaland (Nicobar). (Source: Myers et al., Nature 2000; Mittermeier et al., Hotspots Revisited, 2004; CEPF Hotspot Profiles)
    • India's Biodiversity: 2.4% land area, 8% species (91,000 animal, 45,000 plant). Endemism — Western Ghats (amphibians 78%, reptiles 62%), Eastern Himalaya. Megadiverse country (17). (Source: ZSI & BSI Faunal/Floral Databases; NBAP 2014; MoEFCC India's Biodiversity Profile)
    • Conservation Framework: In-situ — Protected Areas (NP 106, WLS 573, Conservation Reserves 115, Community Reserves 220 — 5.3% land), Tiger Reserves 54 (Project Tiger 1973), Elephant Reserves 33, Biosphere Reserves 18 (12 in UNESCO MAB). Ex-situ — Botanical Gardens, Zoos, Seed Banks (NBAGR), Cryopreservation. (Source: Wildlife Institute of India PA Database 2023; NTCA Tiger Reports; UNESCO MAB Network)
    • International: CBD (1992 — Nagoya Protocol 2010, Kunming-Montreal GBF 2022 — 30×30), CITES (trade), Ramsar (wetlands — 85 sites India), CMS (migratory species), IUCN Red List. (Source: CBD COP15 2022; Ramsar Sites India; CITES Appendices)

    5. Natural Resources: Distribution, Critical Minerals & Energy

    5.1 Mineral Resources — Global Distribution & Geopolitics

    • Ferrous: Iron Ore — Australia (Pilbara), Brazil (Carajás), China, India (Odisha-Jharkhand, Bailadila, Ballari), Russia, Ukraine, South Africa. Manganese — South Africa (Kalahari), Gabon, Australia, Brazil, India (MOIL). Chromite — South Africa (Bushveld), Kazakhstan, India (Sukinda). (Source: USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2024; IBM Indian Minerals Yearbook)
    • Non-Ferrous (Base): Copper — Chile (Escondida), Peru, China, DRC, USA, Indonesia, India (Khetri, Malanjkhand). Aluminium (Bauxite) — Guinea, Australia, China, Brazil, India (Odisha, Jharkhand). Lead/Zinc — China, Australia, Peru, USA, India (Rampura Agucha). Nickel — Indonesia, Philippines, New Caledonia, Russia, Australia. (Source: USGS MCS 2024; LME; IBM Yearbook)

    6. Human Geography: Population, Settlement, Migration & Urbanisation

    6.1 World Population — Distribution, Growth & Transition

    • Distribution (2024): 8.1 billion. Asia 60% (China 1.4B, India 1.44B), Africa 18%, Europe 9%, Latin America 8%, North America 5%, Oceania 0.5%. Density — physiological (per arable land) vs arithmetic. (Source: UN WPP 2024; World Bank Population Data; PRB World Population Data Sheet)

(Source: UN World Population Policies Database; China NBS; India NPP 2000)

    6.2 Migration — Theories, Patterns & Contemporary Issues

    • Theories: Ravenstein's Laws (1885) — short distance, step migration, urban-rural differential, counter-streams; Lee's Push-Pull (1966) — origin/destination factors, intervening obstacles; Harris-Todaro (1970) — expected urban wage > rural wage; World Systems (Wallerstein) — core-periphery labour migration; Transnationalism — sustained cross-border ties. (Source: Ravenstein, Laws of Migration, 1885; Lee, Theory of Migration, 1966; Harris & Todaro, 1970; Massey et al., Worlds in Motion, 1998)
    • Global Patterns: International migrants 281 million (3.6% pop, 2020 UN DESA). Top corridors — Mexico→USA, India→UAE, India→USA, Russia→Ukraine, Bangladesh→India. South-South > South-North. Remittances $802B (2022) — India $111B, Mexico $61B, China $51B. (Source: UN DESA International Migrant Stock 2020; World Bank Migration and Development Brief 2023)
    • Forced Displacement: 117 million (UNHCR 2023) — refugees 36M, IDPs 62M, asylum-seekers 5.4M, Venezuelans displaced 7M. Top origins — Syria, Ukraine, Afghanistan, South Sudan, Myanmar. (Source: UNHCR Global Trends 2023; IDMC Global Report on Internal Displacement)

    • Urban Settlements: Definitions vary — administrative, functional (OECD: density >1,500/km², 50,000 pop), morphological (built-up continuity). Urbanisation — 57% (2023), projected 68% (2050).     Technique: Weber's least-cost location
    Question: "A weight-losing industry uses 4 tonnes of raw material to produce 1 tonne of product. Raw material is ubiquitous but one source is 50 km closer. Market is 200 km away. Where should the plant locate?"
    Answer: Material index = weight of localised raw material / weight of product = 4/1 = 4 > 1 → weight-losing. Plant locates at raw material source to minimise transport cost (4 tonnes moved shorter distance vs 1 tonne longer). If ubiquitous material, location indifferent; but if one source closer, locate there to save 4 × 50 km haul. Market distance irrelevant for weight-losing. (Source: Weber, 1909; Chisholm, 1979)

    Common Traps

    • Confusing weather (short-term) with climate (long-term average — 30 years WMO). (Source: WMO Technical Regulations)
    • Assuming all deserts are hot — cold deserts exist (Ladakh, Gobi, Patagonia, Antarctica). (Source: Cody, Desert Ecology, 1986; Wadia, Geology of India, 1975)
    • Missing that El Niño weakens the Indian monsoon, while La Niña often strengthens it (but not always — 1997 strong El Niño, normal monsoon; 2002 moderate El Niño, severe drought). (Source: IMD Monsoon-ENSO Reports; Gadgil, 2003)
    • Confusing tropical cyclones (warm-core, ocean-born, low latitude) with temperate cyclones (front-based, mid-latitude, baroclinic). (Source: Emanuel, 1987; Carlson, 1998)
    • Thinking coral reefs grow in cold/deep water — they need warm (20–30°C), shallow (<50m), saline, clear, sunlit water. (Source: Veron, 2000; IPCC AR6 Ch. 3)
    • Overlooking that the Green Revolution was regionally concentrated (Punjab, Haryana, Western UP) and crop-specific (wheat, rice). (Source: Pingali, 2012; Bhalla & Singh, Indian Agriculture, 2010)
    • Treating urbanisation as only megacities — Census Towns (3,894 in 2011) are "hidden urbanisation". (Source: Census 2011; Pradhan, 2013)

    9. Climate Change, Hazards & Disaster Geography

    9.1 Anthropogenic Climate Change — Evidence, Attribution & Projections

    • Observed Warming: Global mean surface temperature +1.1°C above 1850–1900 (IPCC AR6 2021); 2023 was the warmest year on record (+1.45°C). Land warms faster than oceans (+1.6°C vs +0.9°C). Arctic amplification — 3–4x global average. (Source: IPCC AR6 WGI 2021; WMO State of Global Climate 2023; NASA GISS; HadCRUT5)
    • Greenhouse Gas Concentrations (2023): CO₂ 420 ppm (50% above pre-industrial), CH₄ 1,920 ppb (165%), N₂O 336 ppb (24%). Cumulative CO₂ emissions 1850–2022: ~2,550 GtCO₂; remaining carbon budget for 1.5°C (50% likelihood): ~250 GtCO₂ (~6 years at current rates). (Source: NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory; IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 5; Global Carbon Project 2023)
    • Attribution: Human influence "unequivocally" warmed the climate system (AR6). Fingerprinting — stratospheric cooling + tropospheric warming, ocean heat content (90% excess energy), patterns of precipitation change, extreme event attribution (World Weather Attribution). (Source: IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 3; Bindoff et al., Detection and Attribution, 2013; WWA Rapid Attribution Studies)
    • Projections (SSP Scenarios): SSP1-1.9 (net zero ~2050) → +1.4°C by 2100; SSP2-4.5 (intermediate) → +2.7°C; SSP5-8.5 (high) → +4.4°C. Sea level rise 2081–2100 vs 1995–2014: 0.28–0.55 m (SSP1-1.9) to 0.63–1.01 m (SSP5-8.5); low-likelihood high-impact >2 m cannot be ruled out (ice sheet collapse). (Source: IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 4; O'Neill et al., SSPs, 2016; IPCC AR6 WGII Cross-Chapter Box on Sea Level)

    9.2 Cryosphere, Hydrosphere & Biosphere Impacts

    • Cryosphere: Arctic sea ice minimum -13%/decade (1979–2023); Greenland ice loss 269 Gt/yr (2002–2022), Antarctica 146 Gt/yr; glaciers -267 Gt/yr (Hugonnet et al. 2021); permafrost thaw releasing CH₄/CO₂; Northern Hemisphere snow cover -2.7%/decade (spring). (Source: Hugonnet et al., Nature 2021; IMBIE Team, Nature 2020; NSIDC Arctic Sea Ice News; IPCC AR6 Ch. 9)
    • Oceans: Heat content record high (2023); acidification — surface pH -0.017 to -0.027 pH units since 1980s (30% increase in H⁺); deoxygenation — 2% loss since 1960; marine heatwaves doubled in frequency (1982–2016); coral reefs — 70–90% loss at 1.5°C, >99% at 2°C. (Source: IPCC AR6 Ch. 9; Cheng et al., Ocean Heat Content, 2023; Hughes et al., Coral Reefs, 2018; IOC-UNESCO Ocean State Report)
    • Hydrological Cycle: Intensification — +7% atmospheric moisture per °C (Clausius-Clapeyron); wet regions wetter, dry regions drier; extreme precipitation +5–10% per °C; soil moisture decline in Mediterranean, SW North America, SW South America, Southern Africa, Australia; glacier-fed rivers — peak water passed in many basins (Himalaya, Andes). (Source: Held & Soden, 2006; IPCC AR6 Ch. 8; WGI Atlas; Immerzeel et al., Nature 2020)
    • Biosphere: Phenological shifts (earlier spring), range shifts poleward/upward (avg 17 km/decade, 11 m/decade elevation); extinction risk — 14% species at 1.5°C, 29% at 3°C; Amazon dieback risk (tipping point ~20–25% deforestation + warming); boreal forest northward expansion, tundra loss.     9.3 Natural Hazards & Disaster Geography

    • Hazard Classification (UNDRR): Geophysical (earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunamis, landslides), Hydrometeorological (floods, cyclones, droughts, heatwaves, storms), Climatological (glacial lake outbursts, wildfires), Biological (epidemics, locusts), Extraterrestrial (impacts). Compound/cascading hazards — e.g., earthquake → landslide → river damming → outburst flood. (Source: UNDRR Terminology 2017; Sendai Framework 2015–2030; IPCC SREX 2012)
    • Global Disaster Trends (EM-DAT 1980–2023): Reported disasters 5x increase (better reporting + climate); economic losses $4.2 trillion (2023 $); deaths declining (early warning) but 91% in developing countries. Floods (44%), storms (28%), earthquakes (8%), droughts (7%), extreme temperature (5%). (Source: EM-DAT, CRED/UCLouvain; UNDRR Global Assessment Report 2023; WMO Atlas of Mortality and Economic Losses 2023)
    • Climate Change & Hazard Intensification: Tropical cyclones — higher intensity (Category 4–5 proportion +10–15% per °C), slower translation (more rainfall), poleward migration; Heatwaves — frequency +2.8x at 1.5°C, +5.6x at 3°C; Droughts — agricultural/ecological drought increase in Mediterranean, SW North America, SW South America, Southern Africa; Floods — extreme precipitation + pluvial/flash flood risk; Wildfires — fire weather season length +18% (1979–2019), area burned increasing in boreal, temperate, Mediterranean. (Source: IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 11 & WGII Ch. 16; Knutson et al., Tropical Cyclones, 2020; Jolly et al., Fire Weather, 2015; WWA Heatwave Attribution Studies)
    • Disaster Risk Equation: Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability / Capacity. Vulnerability drivers — poverty, unplanned urbanisation, environmental degradation, weak governance, gender/age/disability inequality. Capacity — early warning (MHEWS), building codes, land-use planning, ecosystem-based DRR (mangroves, wetlands), social protection. (Source: UNDRR; IPCC SREX Ch. 2; Wisner et al., At Risk, 2004)
