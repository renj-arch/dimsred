1. Enlightenment and Revolutions (18th–19th Century)

The Enlightenment (Age of Reason) emphasised reason, science, individual rights, and secularism, challenging monarchical and church authority. It shaped modern political revolutions.

  • Key thinkers: John Locke (natural rights, social contract), Montesquieu (separation of powers), Voltaire (freedom of speech, religious tolerance), Rousseau (general will, popular sovereignty), Adam Smith (capitalism, invisible hand), Immanuel Kant (reason, enlightenment).
  • American Revolution (1775–83): "No taxation without representation"; Declaration of Independence (1776); Articles of Confederation; US Constitution (1787) — federalism, checks and balances, Bill of Rights (1791). First anti-colonial revolution.
  • French Revolution (1789–99): Causes — financial crisis, inequality (Estates-General), bread shortages, Enlightenment ideas. Events — storming of Bastille (1789), Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789), Reign of Terror (Robespierre), rise of Napoleon. Ideals — Liberty, Equality, Fraternity. Spread nationalism and republicanism across Europe.
  • Industrial Revolution (late 18th–19th c.): Began in Britain; steam engine (James Watt), textile machinery, railways, coal, iron. Shift from agrarian to industrial economy; urbanisation; factory system; child labour; rise of capitalism and working class; colonial markets for raw materials and finished goods.

2. Ideologies and Unifications (19th Century)

  • Nationalism: Loyalty to nation over dynasty/religion; language, shared history, territory as unifying factors; led to unification and independence movements.
  • Liberalism: Constitutional government, individual rights, free markets, limited state.
  • Socialism: Utopian (Owen, Saint-Simon) and scientific (Marx, Engels); critique of capitalism; class struggle; Communist Manifesto (1848).
  • Italian unification (Risorgimento, 1861): Mazzini (Young Italy), Cavour (diplomacy), Garibaldi (Redshirts), Victor Emmanuel II (first king). Role of realpolitik.
  • German unification (1871): Otto von Bismarck's "blood and iron"; Danish War (1864), Austro-Prussian War (1866), Franco-Prussian War (1870–71); proclamation of German Empire at Versailles.
  • Russian emancipation (1861): Serfdom abolished by Alexander II; limited land for peasants; revolutionary ferment continued.

3. Imperialism and Colonialism (19th–Early 20th Century)

  • Old vs new imperialism: Old (trade, settlements) vs new (political control, economic exploitation, "civilising mission," social Darwinism).
  • Scramble for Africa (1880s–1900): Berlin Conference (1884–85) divided Africa among European powers without African consent; colonisation of Congo, South Africa, Egypt, etc.
  • Asia: British in India, Burma, Malaya; French in Indochina; Dutch in Indonesia; US in Philippines; Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) — modernisation and imperial expansion (Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria).
  • Consequences: Economic drain, cultural imposition, plantation economies, famines, resistance movements, and seeds of 20th-century decolonisation.

4. World Wars and the Interwar Period

  • WWI (1914–18): Causes — militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism (M-A-I-N); assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand (Sarajevo, 1914); Central Powers vs Allies; trench warfare; Treaty of Versailles (1919) — war guilt clause, reparations, League of Nations (weakened by US non-membership).
  • Russian Revolution (1917): February Revolution (Tsar abdicated, Provisional Government); October Revolution (Bolsheviks, Lenin); Treaty of Brest-Litovsk; Russian Civil War; USSR formed (1922). First communist state.
  • Interwar (1919–39): Weimar Germany (hyperinflation, 1923); Great Depression (1929, US stock market crash, global impact); rise of fascism (Mussolini, Italy, 1922) and nazism (Hitler, Germany, 1933); appeasement; Spanish Civil War (1936–39).
  • WWII (1939–45): Invasion of Poland (1939); Axis (Germany, Italy, Japan) vs Allies (UK, USSR, US); Pearl Harbor (1941); Stalingrad (turning point, 1942–43); D-Day (1944); atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (1945); Holocaust (6 million Jews); UN formed (1945); Nuremberg trials.

5. Cold War and Decolonisation (1945–1991)

  • Cold War: US-led capitalist bloc vs USSR-led communist bloc; Truman Doctrine (1947), Marshall Plan (1948), NATO (1949), Warsaw Pact (1955); Korean War (1950–53), Vietnam War, Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), Space Race; détente; fall of Berlin Wall (1989); USSR dissolved (1991).
  • Decolonisation: India (1947), Indonesia (1949), Algeria (1962), African nations (1950s–70s); Non-Aligned Movement (1961, Belgrade; Nehru, Nasser, Tito, Sukarno, Nkrumah); struggles against apartheid (South Africa, Nelson Mandela).
  • Middle East: Israel-Palestine conflict (1948 creation of Israel, wars 1956, 1967, 1973); oil politics; Iranian Revolution (1979); Gulf Wars.

6. Post-Cold War World

  • Unipolar moment: US as sole superpower; Gulf War (1991); intervention in Balkans.
  • Globalisation: WTO (1995), regional blocs (EU, NAFTA/USMCA, ASEAN, SAARC), information technology revolution, outsourcing, rise of China (economic reforms from 1978, WTO entry 2001), resurgence of Russia.
  • 21st century challenges: 9/11 and War on Terror (Afghanistan 2001, Iraq 2003), climate change, pandemics, rise of multipolarity, great-power competition (US-China), Brexit, regional conflicts.
Technique: Comparing revolutions
Question: Compare the American and French Revolutions.
Answer: Both challenged monarchy and drew on Enlightenment ideas. American Revolution was anti-colonial (independence from Britain), produced a stable federal republic, and preserved property relations. French Revolution was internal (against the ancien régime), more radical (regicide, Reign of Terror), abolished feudal privileges, and exported revolutionary ideals across Europe through Napoleonic wars. Both inspired later nationalist and democratic movements.
Technique: Cold War blocs
Question: What led to the end of the Cold War?
Answer: Economic stagnation in USSR, costly arms race, Afghanistan war (1979–89), Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms (glasnost — openness, perestroika — restructuring), rise of nationalist movements in Soviet republics, fall of Berlin Wall (1989), and dissolution of USSR (1991) into 15 independent states.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the February and October Revolutions of 1917.
  • Thinking the Treaty of Versailles punished Germany alone — Austria-Hungary and Ottoman Empire were also dismantled (Treaties of Saint-Germain, Sèvres/Lausanne).
  • Assuming decolonisation was peaceful everywhere — Algeria, Vietnam, Kenya (Mau Mau), and Congo saw violent struggles.
  • Missing that NAM was not neutrality but active independence from both blocs.

Memory Tricks

  • M-A-I-N: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism — causes of WWI.
  • Three unifications: Italy (1861), Germany (1871) — both used realpolitik and wars.
  • Cold War acronyms: NATO (West), Warsaw Pact (East), NAM (independent).
  • UN = 1945: Successor to the failed League of Nations.
Mains exam-day strategy
For world history, connect events to ideas (Enlightenment → revolutions → ideologies). Use maps mentally — locate events geographically. For comparative questions, use a framework: causes, course, leadership, outcome, global impact. Mention treaties and their terms precisely (Versailles, Vienna, Berlin). Avoid Eurocentrism — include Asian, African, and Latin American perspectives where relevant.

7. The Industrial Revolution: From Steam to Steel

The Industrial Revolution, broadly spanning the late 18th to mid-19th century, marks the pivotal shift from agrarian, handicraft-based economies to machine-driven industrial systems. While often associated with Britain, it was a global process with distinct phases and regional variations. The first phase (c. 1760–1840) was characterised by the mechanisation of textiles and iron production in Britain. Key inventions included James Watt's improved steam engine (patented 1769), which decoupled industrial power from water sources, enabling factories to be located near coal deposits rather than rivers. The spinning jenny (Hargreaves, 1764) and the power loom (Cartwright, 1785) multiplied textile output exponentially. This phase also saw the rise of the factory system, which concentrated labour under strict discipline, contrasting with the earlier domestic or put-out system.

The second phase (c. 1830–1870) introduced heavy industry and railways. The railway age began in earnest with the Stockton and Darlington Railway (1825) and expanded rapidly across Europe and North America, creating a national market for goods and labour. Iron production shifted from charcoal to coke (Abraham Darby's earlier innovation scaled up), reducing costs. Steel production remained primitive until Henry Bessemer's process (1856) allowed mass manufacturing. This era also saw the expansion of mining in coal and iron ore, fueling urbanisation. The social cost was immense: child labour, long hours, and hazardous conditions led to movements for reform, such as the Factory Acts in Britain (1833, 1844). In India, British colonial policy actively promoted cotton textiles from Bengal to Lancashire, dismantling indigenous spinning and weaving industries to serve the global market, a dynamic that reshaped South Asian economic geography.

The third phase (late 19th century onwards) involved electricity, chemicals, and oil. Thomas Edison's light bulb and the development of alternating current (AC) systems by Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse electrified cities, transforming work patterns and leisure. The Bessemer process evolved into the open-hearth furnace, improving steel quality. This period also saw the rise of multinational corporations and trusts (e.g., Standard Oil), leading to antitrust legislation in the US. In India, this phase coincided with the Swadeshi movement (1905 onwards), where indigenous entrepreneurs like G.D. Birla and Jamsetji Tata began establishing modern industries despite colonial restrictions, laying the foundation for post-independence industrialisation.

Examiner's trap
Students often assume the Industrial Revolution started in Europe and spread linearly. A more nuanced view recognises that while Britain led, other regions like Japan (Meiji Restoration) and India (colonial extraction) experienced industrialisation under different political pressures. Also, do not confuse the "Industrial Revolution" with the "Green Revolution" (agricultural mechanisation), which occurred later in the 20th century.
Exam-Day Strategy: Industrial Cycles
When answering questions on industrial history, always link technology to social change. For instance, mention how the steam engine reduced reliance on river transport, enabling inland trade networks. Connect the Bessemer process to the rise of skyscrapers and bridges. Always include a regional perspective: if discussing global industrialisation, briefly note India's role as a raw material supplier or its late start due to colonial de-industrialisation.

8. The World Wars: Total War and Geopolitical Realignment

World War I (1914–1918) was the first "total war," where entire societies were mobilised for military effort. The conflict began with the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo, triggering a chain reaction among alliance systems. Trench warfare defined the Western Front, leading to stalemates and massive casualties (e.g., Battle of the Somme). The war ended with the Treaty of Versailles (1919), which imposed heavy reparations on Germany, sowing seeds for future conflict. The League of Nations was established but lacked enforcement power due to the absence of major powers like the US.

World War II (1939–1945) was even more destructive, involving atomic weapons and genocide. Key turning points included the Battle of Stalingrad (1942–43), which halted German advances, and D-Day (1944). The Holocaust resulted in the systematic murder of six million Jews and millions of others by Nazi Germany. The war led to the formation of the United Nations (1945) to prevent future conflicts. In Asia, Japan's invasion of China and Pearl Harbor brought the US into the war. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki forced Japan's surrender in 1945. Post-war geopolitics shifted dramatically: Europe was devastated, while the US and USSR emerged as superpowers. This set the stage for the Cold War.

In India, both wars had profound impacts. During WWII, India contributed millions of soldiers to Allied forces, yet colonial rule continued until 1947. The war weakened British economic capacity, accelerating decolonisation. In Europe, the war led to the Holocaust and the Nuremberg Trials, establishing principles of international criminal law. The Yalta and Potsdam conferences (1945) divided Europe into spheres of influence between the US and USSR, creating the bipolar world order.

Examiner's trap
Do not confuse the League of Nations (post-WWI) with the United Nations (post-WWII). The League failed due to lack of universal membership and enforcement mechanisms; the UN succeeded by including all major powers in its Security Council. Also, avoid attributing WWII solely to Hitler's aggression; mention structural causes like economic instability from WWI and the Great Depression.
Exam-Day Strategy: War and Memory
When discussing wars, always mention their human cost and long-term geopolitical consequences. For example, link the Treaty of Versailles to the rise of Nazism and WWII. When writing about decolonisation, connect it to wartime weakening of European empires. Use specific dates (e.g., 1945 for UN formation) but focus on implications rather than rote memorisation.

9. The Cold War: Ideological Blocs and Proxy Conflicts

The Cold War (1947–1991) was a period of geopolitical tension between the US-led capitalist bloc and the USSR-led communist bloc, without direct large-scale conflict. It was defined by an arms race, espionage, and proxy wars in regions such as Korea, Vietnam, and Afghanistan. The ideological divide manifested in competing economic models: planned economies in the East versus market capitalism in the West. Key flashpoints included the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), which brought the world to the brink of nuclear war, and the Space Race, symbolised by the Soviet launch of Sputnik and the US landing on the moon. The era concluded with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.

Examiner's trap
Students often conflate the Cold War with World War II or assume it involved direct US-Soviet combat. It was a struggle of influence, not direct warfare between superpowers. Also, distinguish between "proxy wars" (where allies fight) and direct intervention. Do not attribute all Cold War tensions to ideology alone; economic competition and national security concerns were equally significant drivers.
Exam-Day Strategy: Bloc Dynamics
When analysing Cold War events, always identify the primary superpower interests involved. For instance, link US intervention in Latin America to containment policy and Soviet support for anti-colonial movements in Asia/Africa to ideological expansion. Mention India's non-aligned stance as a strategic alternative to both blocs. Focus on how proxy conflicts shaped modern borders and ethnic conflicts.
