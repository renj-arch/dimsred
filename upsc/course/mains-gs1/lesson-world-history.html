1. Enlightenment, Revolutions & Industrialisation

1.1 The Enlightenment: Ideas, Thinkers, and Political Impact

The Enlightenment (c. 1685–1815) was an intellectual movement emphasising reason, science, individualism, scepticism of tradition, and progress. It provided the ideological foundation for modern liberal democracy, constitutionalism, and revolutionary movements [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Enlightenment," 2023].

1.2 The American Revolution (1765–1789): Anti-Colonial Prototype

  • Causes: "No taxation without representation" — Sugar Act (1764), Stamp Act (1765), Townshend Acts (1767), Tea Act (1773); Enlightenment ideas (Locke, Paine's Common Sense, 1776); British debt from Seven Years' War (1756–63).
  • Course: Continental Congresses (1774, 1775); Lexington & Concord (Apr 1775); Declaration of Independence (4 Jul 1776, Jefferson); Saratoga (1777) — French entry; Yorktown (1781); Treaty of Paris (1783) — recognition of US independence to Mississippi River.
  • Constitutional Settlement: Articles of Confederation (1781) — weak centre; Constitutional Convention (1787, Philadelphia) — federalism, separation of powers, checks and balances, Electoral College, 3/5 compromise; Ratification (1788); Bill of Rights (1791, first 10 amendments).
  • Significance: First successful anti-colonial revolution; written constitution; federal republic; inspired French and Latin American revolutions; but excluded slavery (abolished 1865) and women (suffrage 1920).
Technique: Comparative revolution framework
"Compare the American and French Revolutions." (UPSC 2018, 10 marks)
Answer: Similarities: Both drew on Enlightenment (Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau); challenged monarchical legitimacy; produced written constitutions; declared rights (US Bill of Rights 1791, French Declaration 1789); inspired global republicanism. Differences: (1) Nature: American = anti-colonial (external ruler); French = internal (ancien régime). (2) Radicalism: American preserved property/slavery; French abolished feudalism (Aug 1789), executed king (1793), Reign of Terror (1793–94), dechristianisation. (3) Outcome: Stable federal republic (US); French cycled through Republic → Directory → Consulate → Empire (Napoleon) → Restoration. (4) Global diffusion: US model = constitutional federalism; French model = nationalism, secularism, rights-based citizenship exported via Napoleonic Code. Conclude: American = conservative revolution (preserved social order); French = transformative revolution (upturned social hierarchy).

1.3 The French Revolution (1789–1799): Radical Rupture

  • Structural causes: Fiscal crisis (debt from American War, lavish court); inequitable taxation (nobility/clergy exempt); poor harvests (1788–89); rising bread prices.
  • Phases: (1) Moderate (1789–91): Estates-General → National Assembly (Jun 1789); Tennis Court Oath; Storming of Bastille (14 Jul 1789); August Decrees (abolish feudalism); Declaration of Rights of Man (Aug 1789); Constitution of 1791 (constitutional monarchy). (2) Radical (1792–94): War with Austria/Prussia (Apr 1792); August 10 insurrection → monarchy abolished; Republic proclaimed (Sep 1792); Louis XVI executed (Jan 1793); Committee of Public Safety (Robespierre); Reign of Terror (1793–94, ~16,000 executed); Law of Maximum (price controls). (3) Thermidorian Reaction (1794–99): Robespierre executed (Jul 1794); Constitution of 1795 (Directory, 5-man executive); reliance on army; Napoleon's coup (18 Brumaire, Nov 1799).
  • Legacies: Nationalism (levée en masse), secularism (Civil Constitution of Clergy 1790), metric system, meritocracy, Napoleonic Code (1804) — exported across Europe.

Mains Framing: Industrialisation & inequality
For "Industrial Revolution and social consequences" questions: Use Piketty-style framework — (1) Productivity vs wages: Engels' pause (1790–1840, real wages stagnant despite productivity rise); (2) Urban mortality: life expectancy fell in early industrial cities (Manchester 1841: 24 years); (3) Gender/child labour: factory acts (1833, 1844, 1847) — state response to social pressure; (4) Global divergence: India's deindustrialisation (textile exports £1.3M 1780 → imports £40M 1850) [Broadberry & Gupta, Indian Economic History, 2010]; (5) Long-run: Kuznets curve — inequality rose then fell (post-1914 wars, progressive tax, welfare state). Cite Mokyr (2009), Allen (2009), O'Brien (2018).

2. Colonialism & Imperialism

2.1 Old vs New Imperialism: Continuity and Rupture

  • Old Imperialism (15th–18th c.): Trade monopolies (Portuguese Estado da Índia, VOC, EIC), coastal forts, settlement colonies (Americas, Siberia), mercantilism, limited territorial control.
  • New Imperialism (1870s–1914): Political control (formal empire), economic exploitation (investment, raw materials, markets), "civilising mission" (white man's burden, social Darwinism), technology gap (quinine, steamship, telegraph, machine gun, railway). Trigger: Long Depression (1873–96) → protectionism → scramble for secure markets.

2.4 Colonial Economies: Extraction, Integration, and Underdevelopment

  • Drain of Wealth (India): Naoroji Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901) — home charges, military expenditure, trade surplus remitted; estimated £30–40M/yr (1858–1947) [Naoroji, 1901; Patnaik, Columbia UP, 2018].
  • Plantation/Export economies: Monoculture (tea Assam/Ceylon, rubber Malaya/Indochina, cotton Egypt, cocoa Gold Coast, sisal Tanganyika); labour coercion (indenture — 1.3M Indians to Caribbean/Fiji/Mauritius 1834–1917; kuli system).
  • Infrastructure as extraction: Railways — India 65,000 km (1947) but radial (ports to interior), not integrative; gauge diversity; freight rates favoured exports.
  • Deindustrialisation: India's textile share of world manufacturing: 25% (1750) → 2% (1900); per capita industrialisation reversed [Bairoch, Industrialisation Levels 1750–1980, 1982].
Technique: Colonialism & nationalism linkage
"Trace the link between colonial economic policies and the rise of nationalism in India." (UPSC 2020, 15 marks)
Answer: Land revenue — Permanent Settlement (zamindars) → rural indebtedness → peasant uprisings (Pabna 1873, Deccan 1875); Ryotwari → state as landlord → no occupancy rights → Champaran (1917), Kheda (1918). Trade/Deindustrialisation — Lancashire mills vs Indian handlooms; tariff discrimination (1894–1925: free entry British goods, 3.5% duty Indian goods); Swadeshi (1905) → boycott, indigenous enterprise (Tata Steel 1907, Bengal Chemicals). Famine policy — 1876–78, 1896–97, 1899–1900, 1943 (Bengal, 3M deaths); Famine Codes ignored; nationalist critique (Naoroji, Dutt, RC Dutt). Labour migration — Indenture (girmitiyas) → diaspora consciousness (Ghadar 1913, Singapore mutiny 1915). Education/Western ideas — Macaulay's minute (1835) → English-educated intelligentsia (Dadabhai, Gokhale, Tilak, Gandhi, Nehru) — appropriated liberal discourse (self-determination, representation) against colonial practice. Conclude: Colonialism produced its own gravediggers by creating the material conditions (poverty, dislocation) and ideological tools (rights, representation) for nationalist mobilisation.

The Cold War was a bipolar ideological, geopolitical, and economic confrontation between the US-led capitalist bloc and the USSR-led communist bloc, characterised by nuclear deterrence, proxy wars, and competition for the "Third World" [Gaddis, The Cold War: A New History, 2005; Westad, The Global Cold War, 2005].

(3) Case studies: Congo (Lumumba assassinated 1961, Mobutu installed); Angola (MPLA vs UNITA/FNLA, Cuban troops 1975); Vietnam (nationalism > communism). Conclude: Decolonisation was not a byproduct but a theatre of Cold War; the "Global South" emerged as a structural category.

6.3 Answer-Writing Frameworks for World History

Framework A: "Trace / Discuss / Examine" (10–15 marks) — Chronological-Thematic
Structure: Introduction (15%) — Define scope, period, thesis (e.g., "Cold War evolved through four phases driven by nuclear deterrence and decolonisation"). Body (70%) — Chronological phases as main headings; within each: Ideological (doctrine), Geopolitical (alliances, crises), Economic (aid, trade, arms), Third World (proxy, NAM). Use signposting: "The first phase (1945–53) was defined by..." "A shift occurred in 1953 with..." "Détente (1969–79) represented..." Conclusion (15%) — Synthesis: what changed, what persisted; one-sentence takeaway.
Example (Cold War evolution): Intro — 1945–91: bipolar confrontation, no direct war, global reach. Body — Phase 1 (1945–53): Containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO, Korea, nuclear monopoly lost. Phase 2 (1953–62): Thaw, peaceful coexistence, Warsaw Pact, Hungary, Suez, Sputnik, Berlin Wall, Cuba brink. Phase 3 (1962–79): MAD stabilisation, hotline, PTBT, NPT, détente, SALT, Helsinki, triangular diplomacy (Nixon-China). Phase 4 (1979–91): Second Cold War (Afghanistan, Euromissiles, SDI), Gorbachev (glasnost/perestroika), INF, 1989 revolutions, German unity, USSR dissolution. Conclusion — From ideological crusade to managed competition to systemic transformation; nuclear deterrence prevented WWIII but proxy wars devastated Global South.
Framework B: "Compare / Contrast" (10–12.5 marks) — Dimension-Based
Structure: Introduction — Identify the two phenomena, state overall similarity/difference thesis. Body — 4–5 comparative dimensions as sub-headings: (1) Causes (structural vs contingent), (2) Leadership (mass vs vanguard), (3) Violence (restrained vs terror), (4) Outcome (stable vs cyclical), (5) Global Diffusion (model type). Each paragraph: similarity → difference → significance. Conclusion — Net assessment: which was more transformative for modernity?
Example (American vs French Revolution): Intro — Both Enlightenment-inspired anti-monarchical; but American = conservative/separatist, French = radical/universalist. Body — Causes: American = colonial grievance (tax, representation); French = fiscal crisis + social inequality (estates). Leadership: American = propertied elites (Washington, Jefferson); French = lawyers/journalists (Robespierre, Danton) + sans-culottes. Violence: American = conventional war; French = Reign of Terror (16k guillotined), dechristianisation. Outcome: American = stable federal republic (survives); French = Republic → Empire → Restoration → Republic (1870). Diffusion: American = constitutional federalism (Latin America, post-1945); French = nationalism, secularism, rights-based citizenship (Europe 1848, 1917, decolonisation). Conclusion — French Revolution invented modern politics (left/right, terror, nationalism); American Revolution invented modern constitutionalism.
Framework C: "Analyse / Critically Examine" (15 marks) — Dialectical
Structure: Introduction — Proposition to be analysed (e.g., "Globalisation has reduced poverty but increased inequality"). Body — Thesis (argument for), Antithesis (argument against/counter-evidence), Synthesis (nuanced resolution). Use 3–4 thematic clusters: Economic (growth, trade, FDI, inequality), Social (poverty, labour, migration, culture), Political (sovereignty, democracy, regulation, backlash), Environmental (carbon, biodiversity, climate justice). Each cluster: data → mechanism → caveat. Conclusion — Conditional verdict: "Globalisation is necessary but not sufficient; requires domestic complementarities (education, safety nets, carbon pricing) and global governance reform (WTO, tax, climate)."
Example (Globalisation impact on India): Intro — 1991 reforms integrated India; growth accelerated (6%→7%+), poverty fell (45%→22%), but inequality rose (Gini 0.32→0.35), agriculture distressed. Thesis — Services/IT boom (export-led, $194B), demographic dividend utilised, FDI ($83B FY23), consumer choice, technology transfer. Antithesis — Premature deindustrialisation (manufacturing 16% GDP), agrarian crisis (MSP, fragmentation), informalisation (90%), regional divergence (coastal vs hinterland), capital intensity, IPR/TRIPS constraints. Synthesis — 2nd gen reforms needed: land/labour/power, PLI for manufacturing, skill mission, carbon border readiness, digital public goods export. Conclusion — Globalisation enabled India's rise but did not guarantee inclusive transformation; agency matters.

7.2 February Revolution (March 1917): Spontaneous Uprising and Dual Power

  • Trigger: International Women's Day (Feb 23 OS / Mar 8 NS) — women textile workers in Petrograd strike for bread; joined by metalworkers (Putilov); 200k+ on streets by Feb 25.
  • Mutiny: Garrison (180k) refuses to fire; Feb 27 — Volhynian, Preobrazhensky regiments join; Tsar's train diverted; abdication Mar 2 (Mar 15 NS) at Pskov.
  • Dual Power (Dvlastie): Provisional Government (Prince Lvov, then Kerensky Jul–Oct) — Duma Committee origin, liberal/constitutional democratic, committed to war, postponed land reform; Petrograd Soviet (Chkheidze, then Trotsky Sep) — workers/soldiers delegates, radicalising, Order No. 1 (Mar 1) — military obedience only to Soviet, elected committees. [Rabinowitch, The Bolsheviks Come to Power, 1976; Hasegawa, The February Revolution, 1981]
Mains Framing: Why Dual Power Failed
For "Explain the concept of Dual Power and its collapse": Dual Power was structurally unstable — two claimants to sovereignty (state vs class). PG derived authority from legal continuity (Duma); Soviet from revolutionary legitimacy (workers/soldiers). PG's fatal errors: (1) War continuation — Milyukov Note (Apr 1917) promised Allied war aims; (2) Land postponement — "Constituent Assembly will decide" while peasants seized estates (summer 1917); (3) Repression of July Days — disarmed Bolsheviks but legitimised Soviet radicalism; (4) Kornilov Affair (Aug 1917) — Kerensky armed Bolsheviks to stop right-wing coup → Bolshevik credibility surged. Dual Power resolved only by one side destroying the other — Oct 1917. [Lenin, April Theses, 1917; Trotsky, History of the Russian Revolution, 1930]

7.4 October Revolution (November 1917): Insurrection and Consolidation

  • Military Revolutionary Committee (MRC) — Trotsky (Petrograd Soviet chair, Oct 8) creates MRC (Oct 16); garrison committees pledge loyalty.
  • Seizure (Oct 25 / Nov 7): Near-bloodless — MRC takes bridges, telegraph, telephone, state bank, railway stations; Winter Palace stormed (Oct 26) — PG ministers arrested; Kerensky flees.
  • Second Congress of Soviets (Oct 26): 670 delegates (Bolshevik 390, Left SR 100); SR/Menshevik right walk out; Congress endorses: Decree on Peace (immediate armistice, no annexations), Decree on Land (peasant seizure of gentry land — Social Revolutionary programme adopted), Workers' Control decree. [Decrees on Peace and Land, Oct 1917; Proceedings of the Second Congress of Soviets]
Technique: "Was October a Coup or a Revolution?" (UPSC 2019, 15 marks)
Structure: Thesis (Coup arguments) — Small numbers (25k Red Guards vs 2M city); MRC planning; key points seized by force; PG undefended; Congress of Soviets manipulated (walkout). Antithesis (Revolution arguments) — Mass base (soldiers, workers, Kronstadt); Soviet legitimacy (elected delegates); Decrees enacted popular demands (peace, land); PG had no social support (Kerensky fled alone); February was the revolution, October its completion. Synthesis — Insurrectionary revolution: organised vanguard (Bolshevik party, 240k members Oct 1917) acted in the name of mass organs (Soviets) to realise popular demands that the PG betrayed. Not a palace coup (no elite replacement) but a class transfer of power. Historiography: Pipes (1990) = coup; Figes (1996), Rabinowitch (1976/2004) = revolution with popular support; McMeekin (2017) = German-financed putsch (debated). Conclude: For Mains, use dual character — form = insurrection, substance = social revolution. [Pipes 1990; Figes 1996; Rabinowitch 1976/2004; McMeekin 2017; Smith 2017]

Mains Framing: Partition as Structural Violence
For "Critically examine the role of Partition in decolonisation": Partition was not an accident but a colonial strategy — divide and quit. Patterns: (1) Demographic engineering — lines drawn by outsiders (Radcliffe, UNSCOP, 38th parallel) ignoring local realities; (2) Minority insecurity — new states define nationhood ethnoreligiously (Pakistan = Muslim, Israel = Jewish, Northern Ireland = Protestant); (3) Frozen conflicts — Kashmir, Palestine, Korea, Cyprus, Cyprus — UN peacekeeping but no resolution; (4) Nuclearisation — India-Pakistan, Korea — partition created security dilemmas driving proliferation. Counter-argument: In some cases (India, Ireland), partition prevented larger civil war; but the long-term costs (militarisation, minority persecution, democratic deficit) outweigh. 8.6 Neo-Colonialism, NIEO, and the Unfinished Decolonisation Agenda

  • Kwame Nkrumah, Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism (1965): Political independence without economic sovereignty — foreign capital controls mining, banking, trade; "the essence of neo-colonialism is that the State which is subject to it is, in theory, independent and has all the outward trappings of international sovereignty but in reality its economic system and thus its political policy is directed from outside." [Nkrumah, Neo-Colonialism, 1965]
  • New International Economic Order (NIEO, 1974): UNGA Res 3201/3202 (S-UNGA); demands: sovereign control over resources, technology transfer, preferential market access, reform of Bretton Woods, commodity price stabilisation. Outcome: Blocked by Global North (US veto, G7); 1980s debt crisis → structural adjustment (IMF/World Bank) reversed gains. [UNGA Res 3201/3202, 1974; South-North Dialogue (Krasner, 1985)]
  • Contemporary Forms: (1) Debt trap — Zambia (2020), Sri Lanka (2022), Ghana (2022) — sovereign default, asset seizures; (2) Investor-State Dispute Settlement (ISDS) — Philip Morris v. Australia (plain packaging), Vodafone v. India (retro tax) — regulatory chill; (3) Climate colonialism — Global North historic emissions (79% cumulative), Global South adaptation burden; CBAM (EU) as green protectionism; (4) Digital colonialism — data extraction (US/China platforms), no local tax/base erosion; (5) Vaccine apartheid (COVID) — hoarding, TRIPS waiver blocked. [Oxfam Vaccine Apartheid 2021; UNCTAD Trade and Development Report 2023; IPCC AR6 2023]
