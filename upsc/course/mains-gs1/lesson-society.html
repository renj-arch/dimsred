1. Indian Society — Salient Features & Diversity

Indian society is characterised by "unity in diversity" — immense regional, linguistic, religious, and cultural variation within a shared civilisational framework. The phrase, attributed to Jawaharlal Nehru in The Discovery of India (1946), captures the coexistence of centrifugal diversities and centripetal unities.

  • Linguistic diversity: 22 scheduled languages (Eighth Schedule), 99 non-scheduled languages with ≥10,000 speakers, and hundreds of dialects (Census 2011: 121 languages, 19,569 mother tongues rationalised). Language reorganised states in 1956 (States Reorganisation Act), creating a federal linguistic compact. (Source: Census 2011, Language Data; States Reorganisation Act 1956)
  • Religious diversity: Six major religions — Hinduism (79.8%), Islam (14.2%), Christianity (2.3%), Sikhism (1.7%), Buddhism (0.7%), Jainism (0.4%) per Census 2011. Constitutional secularism (Articles 25–28) guarantees freedom of conscience, profession, practice, and propagation, subject to public order, morality, health. (Source: Census 2011, Religion Data; Constitution Articles 25–28)
  • Racial/ethnic composition: Anthropological Survey of India (B.S. Guha, 1930s) identified six racial types — Negrito, Proto-Australoid, Mongoloid, Mediterranean, Western Brachycephalic, Nordic. Modern genetics (Basu et al., 2016; Narasimhan et al., 2019) shows four ancestral components: Ancestral North Indian (ANI), Ancestral South Indian (ASI), Austro-Asiatic, Tibeto-Burman. (Source: Guha, B.S., Racial Elements in the Population, 1935; Basu et al., PNAS 2016; Narasimhan et al., Science 2019)
  • Cultural unity factors: Constitutional framework (single citizenship, fundamental rights, federal structure), shared epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata — transmitted across languages/regions), pilgrimage circuits (Char Dham, Jyotirlingas, Shakti Peethas), festivals (Diwali, Eid, Christmas, Vaisakhi, Buddha Purnima), and the civilisational idea of Bharatvarsha. (Source: Nehru, J., The Discovery of India, 1946; Constitution of India)
  • Family and marriage: Joint family (traditional, coparcenary property under Hindu law), nuclear family (growing — 52% urban, 48% rural per NFHS-5), arranged marriages (endogamy within caste/community), rising age at marriage (female 22.1, male 25.5 per NFHS-5), increasing divorce (0.5% → 1.1% over two decades), live-in relationships recognised (D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal, 2010; Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma, 2013), LGBTQ+ recognition (Navtej Singh Johar v. UoI, 2018 — Section 377 read down; Supriyo v. UoI, 2023 — no right to marry but civil union protection). (Source: NFHS-5 2019–21; Hindu Succession Act 1956 (amended 2005); SC Judgments: Navtej Johar 2018, Supriyo 2023)
PYQ 2017 GS I
"The spirit of tolerance and love is not only an interesting feature of Indian society from very early times, but it is also playing an important part at the present. Elaborate." — Answer framework: Define tolerance in Indian context (Ashoka's edicts, Sufi-Bhakti syncretism, Constitutional secularism). Historical evidence — Ashoka's Rock Edict XII (respect for all sects), Akbar's Sulh-i-kul, Bhakti-Sufi traditions. Contemporary role — Article 25–28, composite culture (Article 51A(f)), judicial guardianship (S.R. Bommai 1994: secularism basic structure). Challenges — communal violence, hate speech, majoritarianism. Way forward — interfaith dialogue, education, strict law enforcement.
Technique: Diversity analysis matrix
Question: "Diversity in India is both a strength and a challenge for nation-building." Analyse with examples.
Answer structure: (1) Strengths — linguistic reorganisation prevented secessionism (vs Pakistan, Sri Lanka); religious pluralism enabled democratic survival; cultural richness fuels soft power. (2) Challenges — linguistic chauvinism (anti-Hindi agitations 1965, 2019); communal faultlines (Partition legacy, 1984, 2002, 2020); regionalism (Gorkhaland, Bodoland, Vidarbha); caste polarisation (reservation politics). (3) Management mechanisms — Eighth Schedule, Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, Finance Commission, Article 371 special provisions. (4) Conclusion — diversity managed through constitutional accommodation, not suppression.

2. Caste System: Structure, Change & Constitutional Response

The caste system is a closed, hereditary, endogamous stratification system combining ritual purity/pollution hierarchy (varna) with thousands of occupational birth-groups (jati). It is distinct from class (economic) and estate (legal) systems.

2.1 Classical Structure & Sociological Theories

  • Varna (four-fold): Brahmin (priest/teacher), Kshatriya (warrior/ruler), Vaishya (merchant/producer), Shudra (service). Untouchables (Avarna/Dalits) outside varna. Textual basis: Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90), Manusmriti. (Source: Rig Veda 10.90; Manusmriti)
  • Jati (thousands): Regional, occupation-linked, endogamous groups with commensality rules. Jati varies by region — same varna rank, different jati hierarchy. (Source: Srinivas, M.N., Social Change in Modern India, 1962)
  • Key theories: Louis Dumont — Homo Hierarchicus (1970): caste as religious ideology (pure/impure opposition); M.N. Srinivas — Sanskritisation (lower castes emulate upper-caste rituals), Dominant Caste (numerical/economic/political power); André Béteille — inequality vs hierarchy, class-caste convergence; Gail Omvedt — Dalit Bahujan perspective, anti-caste movements. (Source: Dumont, L., Homo Hierarchicus, 1970; Srinivas, M.N., Social Change in Modern India, 1962; Béteille, A., Inequality and Social Change, 1983)

2.2 Constitutional & Legal Abolition

  • Article 17: Abolishes untouchability; practice in any form forbidden; enforcement law (Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955, amended 1976). (Source: Constitution Article 17; Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955)
  • Article 15(4) & 16(4): Enable reservations for SEBCs/SCs/STs in education and public employment. (Source: Constitution Articles 15(4), 16(4))
  • SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 (amended 2015, 2018): Stringent penalties, special courts, victim compensation. 2018 amendment restored original provisions after SC dilution (Subhash Kashinath Mahajan v. State of Maharashtra, 2018). (Source: SC/ST PoA Act 1989; Amendments 2015, 2018; SC Judgment Subhash Kashinath 2018)
  • Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers Act 2013: Bans manual scavenging, mandates rehabilitation. Still persists — 58,098 manual scavengers identified (2018 survey); NAMASTE scheme (2022) for mechanised sanitation. (Source: Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers Act 2013; NAMASTE Scheme 2022; Safai Karmachari Andolan data)

2.3 Judicial Interpretation — Reservation Jurisprudence

  • Indra Sawhney v. UoI (1992) — Mandal Commission: 50% ceiling on reservations (except extraordinary circumstances); creamy layer exclusion for OBCs; no reservation in promotions (overruled by 77th/85th Amendments); carry-forward rule for unfilled vacancies. (Source: Indra Sawhney v. UoI, 1992, 3 SCC 217)
  • M. Nagaraj v. UoI (2006): For SC/ST promotion reservations — state must show backwardness, inadequacy of representation, administrative efficiency. (Source: M. Nagaraj v. UoI, 2006)
  • Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain (2018): Creamy layer applies to SC/ST promotions; Nagaraj's "quantifiable data" requirement diluted. (Source: Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain, 2018)
  • Janhit Abhiyan v. UoI (2022) — EWS Quota: 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (general category) upheld; 50% ceiling not inviolable; economic criterion valid. (Source: Janhit Abhiyan v. UoI, 2022)
  • State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024): Sub-classification within SC/ST for reservation benefits permitted (overruling E.V. Chinnaiah 2004). (Source: State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh, 2024)

2.4 Contemporary Dynamics

  • Caste in politics: Mandal (1990) → OBC mobilisation; Dalit assertion (BSP, Dalit Panthers); dominant castes (Jats, Marathas, Patidars) demanding OBC status. (Source: Jaffrelot, C., India's Silent Revolution, 2003)
  • Caste among non-Hindus: Dalit Christians/Muslims (Pasmanda) demand SC status (Ranganath Misra Commission 2007 recommended delinking SC from religion; 2024 Commission under K.G. Balakrishnan examining). Caste exists among Sikhs (Mazhabi, Ravidasia), Muslims (Ashraf/Ajlaf/Arzal). (Source: Ranganath Misra Commission Report 2007; Sachar Committee 2006; K.G. Balakrishnan Commission 2024)
  • Urban anonymity vs rural visibility: Caste persists in marriage (95%+ endogamy per IHDS), housing, politics; weakened in workplace, dining. (Source: India Human Development Survey 2011–12; NSSO 75th Round)
PYQ 2022 GS I
"Caste system is assuming new identities and associational forms. Hence, the caste system cannot be eradicated in India." Comment. — Answer: New forms — caste associations (sangathans), political parties, matrimonial websites, social media groups, professional networks. Eradication vs annihilation (Ambedkar: annihilation requires inter-caste marriage, dining, priesthood democratisation). Constitutional route (Article 17, reservations) addresses disability, not identity. Identity persists for political mobilisation, social capital, marriage markets. Way forward — inter-caste marriage incentives (Dr. Ambedkar Scheme), education, urbanisation, economic mobility.
Answer Framework: Caste Questions
1. Define caste — varna/jati, purity/pollution, endogamy, heredity.
2. Constitutional provisions — Art 17, 15(4), 16(4), 330/332, 338/338A/338B.
3. Key judgments — Indra Sawhney (50%, creamy layer), Nagaraj, Jarnail Singh, Janhit, Davinder Singh.
4. Data — Census SC/ST %, NFHS caste-wise indicators, NCRB atrocity data.
5. Contemporary trends — political mobilisation, sub-classification, non-Hindu castes, urban persistence.
6. Critique — reservation ceiling, creamy layer efficacy, exclusion errors, merit debate.
7. Way forward — Ambedkar's annihilation agenda, inter-caste marriage, land reform, quality education.

3. Tribal Societies: Identity, Rights & Development

Scheduled Tribes (STs) constitute 8.6% of India's population (104 million, Census 2011) across 705 notified groups. Concentrated in central India (Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, Maharashtra), Northeast, and Andaman & Nicobar.

3.1 Constitutional & Legal Framework

  • Fifth Schedule (Article 244(1)): Scheduled Areas in 10 states (AP, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, HP, Jharkhand, MP, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan). Governor's special powers — Tribes Advisory Council, regulation of land transfer, money-lending. (Source: Constitution Article 244(1), Fifth Schedule)
  • Sixth Schedule (Article 244(2)): Autonomous District/Regional Councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram — legislative, executive, judicial powers over land, forests, inheritance, marriage, social customs. (Source: Constitution Article 244(2), Sixth Schedule)
  • Article 275(1): Grants-in-aid for ST welfare and administration of Scheduled Areas. (Source: Constitution Article 275(1))
  • PESA Act 1996 (Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas): Gram Sabha as core — ownership of minor forest produce, land acquisition consent, control over money-lending, village markets, minor minerals. Implementation gaps — state laws not aligned, Gram Sabha bypassed. (Source: PESA Act 1996; MoPR Implementation Reports; Xaxa Committee 2014)
  • FRA 2006 (Forest Rights Act): Individual forest rights (IFR), Community forest rights (CFR), habitat rights for PVTGs. As of 2024: ~23 lakh IFR titles, ~1 lakh CFR titles; rejection rate high (~50%). (Source: FRA 2006; MoTA Monthly Progress Reports; CFR-LA Database)
  • PVTGs (Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups): 75 groups (earlier PTGs) — pre-agricultural tech, stagnant/declining population, extremely low literacy, subsistence economy. Dhebar Commission (1973) identified criteria. Dedicated schemes (PM-JANMAN 2023 — ₹24,000 cr for housing, water, health, connectivity). (Source: Dhebar Commission 1973; MoTA PVTG List; PM-JANMAN 2023)

3.2 Development Challenges

  • Displacement: ~40% of displaced by development projects (dams, mines, industries) are STs (Fernandes 1991; Planning Commission). LARR Act 2013 (consent 70–80%, social impact assessment) — 2015 ordinance diluted consent; 2024 rules pending. (Source: Fernandes, W., Development-Induced Displacement, 1991; LARR Act 2013; Xaxa Committee 2014)
  • Health & Nutrition: Stunting 40.9% (NFHS-5 ST vs 35.5% national), anaemia 59.5% women, malaria/tuberculosis burden high. (Source: NFHS-5 2019–21, ST disaggregated data)
  • Education: Literacy 59% (Census 2011) vs 73% national; dropout high at secondary; medium of instruction barrier. Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS) — 740 sanctioned (2024). (Source: Census 2011; MoTA EMRS Dashboard)
  • Northeast specificity: Sixth Schedule councils, AFSPA (Armed Forces Special Powers Act) areas, insurgency-development nexus, Inner Line Permit (ILP) regime, Citizenship Amendment Act 2019 concerns. (Source: Xaxa Committee Report 2014; MHA Northeast Reports)
PYQ 2018 GS I
"The Forest Rights Act, 2006 is a landmark legislation for tribal empowerment. Examine its implementation challenges." — Answer: FRA provisions — IFR, CFR, habitat rights, Gram Sabha authority. Achievements — titles distributed, CFR potential for conservation. Challenges — procedural complexity (13 forms), forest department resistance, lack of awareness, GPS mapping disputes, compensatory afforestation (CAMPA) vs CFR conflict, rejection without reasons, no time-bound disposal. Way forward — simplified process, Gram Sabha capacity building, CFR convergence with MGNREGA/CAMPA, judicial monitoring.
Technique: Tribal policy matrix
Question: "PESA and FRA are complementary but implementation diverges." Analyse.
Answer: PESA — Gram Sabha ownership of MFP, land consent, local governance (Fifth Schedule areas). FRA — forest land rights (individual/community), conservation rights (all forest areas). Complementarity — both empower Gram Sabha; FRA operationalises PESA's MFP ownership. Divergence — PESA only in Fifth Schedule; FRA nationwide. State PESA rules often omit MFP/minor minerals; FRA implementation by forest dept (conflict of interest). Convergence strategy — joint Gram Sabha meetings, single-window clearance, revenue-forest dept coordination.

4. Gender, Women's Organisations & Social Empowerment

4.1 Constitutional & Legal Architecture

  • Fundamental Rights: Article 14 (equality), Article 15(1) (non-discrimination), Article 15(3) (protective discrimination for women/children), Article 16 (equal opportunity), Article 21 (dignity, bodily autonomy — Suchita Srivastava 2009; Justice K.S. Puttaswamy 2017). (Source: Constitution Articles 14, 15, 16, 21; SC Judgments: Suchita Srivastava 2009, Puttaswamy 2017)
  • Directive Principles: Article 39(a) (equal right to livelihood), 39(d) (equal pay), 42 (maternity relief), 44 (uniform civil code), 46 (protection of weaker sections). (Source: Constitution Articles 39, 42, 44, 46)
  • Political Reservations: Article 243D (Panchayats — 33%, 50% in 21 states), Article 243T (Municipalities). Women's Reservation Bill (106th Amendment, 2023) — 33% Lok Sabha/State Assemblies, linked to delimitation post-Census (not yet implemented). (Source: Constitution 106th Amendment Act 2023; Articles 243D, 243T)
  • Key Legislations: Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 (equal coparcenary rights — Vineeta Sharma 2020 retrospective); Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 (civil remedy, residence order); Sexual Harassment at Workplace Act 2013 (Vishaka guidelines 1997 codified); Dowry Prohibition Act 1961 (amended 1984, 1986); MTP Act 1971 (amended 2021 — 24 weeks, unmarried women); PCPNDT Act 1994 (sex selection ban); Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006 (age 18/21). (Source: Respective Acts; SC Judgments: Vineeta Sharma 2020, Vishaka 1997, Independent Thought 2017)

4.2 Women's Organisations & Movements

  • Pre-Independence: AIWC (1927, Margaret Cousins), WIA (Women's Indian Association, 1917, Annie Besant), NFIW (1954, post-independence merger). Focus — suffrage, education, social reform (Sarda Act 1929 — child marriage). (Source: Forbes, G., Women in Modern India, 1996; AIWC Archives)
  • Post-Independence — Autonomous: SEWA (1972, Ela Bhatt) — informal women workers, union+cooperative model; AIDWA (1981, CPI(M)); NFIW (CPI); PUDR/PUCL women's wings. (Source: Bhatt, E., We Are Poor But So Many, 2006; SEWA Annual Reports)
  • Issue-based Movements: Chipko (1973, Gaura Devi — women's forest rights); Narmada Bachao Andolan (Medha Patkar — displacement, rehabilitation); Anti-Arrack (Andhra 1990s — women's self-help groups); Nirbhaya (2012 — criminal law amendment 2013); #MeToo India (2018 — workplace harassment); Pinjra Tod (2015 — hostel curfews). (Source: Shiva, V., Staying Alive, 1988; NBA Archives; Criminal Law Amendment 2013)
  • SHGs & NRLM: Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana-NRLM — 8.9 cr women in 81 lakh SHGs (2024); bank linkage ₹7.7 lakh cr; federation into Village Organisations, Cluster Level Federations. (Source: NRLM MIS Dashboard 2024; World Bank NRLM Evaluation)

4.3 Status Indicators & Gaps

  • Sex Ratio: 1020 females/1000 males (NFHS-5, first time >1000); Child Sex Ratio (0–6) 929 (NFHS-5) vs 918 (Census 2011) — improvement but 12 states/UTs below 900. (Source: NFHS-5 2019–21; Census 2011)
  • Female LFPR: 37.0% (PLFS 2022–23, usual status, 15+), up from 23.3% (2017–18) — driven by rural self-employment in agriculture; urban FLFPR 25.4%. (Source: PLFS 2022–23; ILOSTAT)
  • Violence: NCRB 2022 — 4.45 lakh crimes against women; cruelty by husband/relatives 31.4%, assault 19.2%, kidnapping 18.7%, rape 7.1%. Conviction rate ~27%. (Source: NCRB Crime in India 2022)
  • Political Representation: Lok Sabha 14.4% (2024), Rajya Sabha 13%, State Assemblies avg 9% (ADR). Panchayats — 46% elected women (1.3 million). (Source: ADR Election Watch 2024; MoPR Panchayat Data)
PYQ 2021 GS I
"Though women have made significant strides in various fields, the gender gap in political representation remains a concern." Discuss. — Answer: Data — Panchayats 46% (reservation), Assemblies 9%, Parliament 14%. Causes — patriarchal party structures (ticket distribution), money/muscle power, double burden (domestic + public), violence/harassment, delimitation-linked WRB delay. Solutions — WRB implementation, intra-party quotas (voluntary), state funding of elections, capacity building (NIRD), addressing violence (ECI guidelines).
Answer Framework: Gender Questions
1. Constitutional articles — 14, 15(3), 16, 39, 42, 243D/T, 106th Amendment.
2. Key laws — DV Act 2005, POSH 2013, MTP 2021, PCPNDT, Hindu Succession 2005.
3. Landmark judgments — Vishaka, Vineeta Sharma, Navtej Johar, Suchita Srivastava, Independent Thought.
4. Data — NFHS-5 (sex ratio, anaemia, DV), PLFS (FLFPR), NCRB (crimes), ADR (political).
5. Organisations — SEWA, AIWC, SHGs/NRLM, issue movements (Chipko, NBA, Nirbhaya, Pinjra Tod).
6. Gaps — implementation, patriarchy, intersectionality (caste+gender), unpaid care work.
7. Way forward — WRB, care economy recognition, gender budgeting, behavioural change.

5. Population Dynamics: Demographic Transition, Dividend & Ageing

5.1 Demographic Profile (2023–24 Estimates)

  • Population: ~1.44 billion (UN World Population Prospects 2024) — surpassed China (April 2023). (Source: UN World Population Prospects 2024)
  • Fertility: TFR 2.0 (NFHS-5) — below replacement (2.1); 31 states/UTs at/below replacement; Bihar (3.0), UP (2.4), Meghalaya (2.9) above. (Source: NFHS-5 2019–21; SRS Statistical Report 2021)
  • Mortality: IMR 28 (SRS 2021), U5MR 36, MMR 97 (2018–20, SRS); Life expectancy 70.1 (M) / 72.7 (F). (Source: SRS Bulletins 2021; Sample Registration System)
  • Age Structure: 0–14: 25%, 15–64: 68%, 65+: 7% (2023). Median age 28.7. (Source: UN WPP 2024; Census 2011 projections)
  • Urbanisation: 35.9% (World Bank 2023); Census 2011: 31.1%; projected 50% by 2050. (Source: World Bank Data; Census 2011; UN Urbanization Prospects)

5.2 Demographic Transition Theory & India's Trajectory

  • Stages: Stage 1 (high stationary) — pre-1920; Stage 2 (early expanding) — 1921–1951 (mortality decline, fertility high); Stage 3 (late expanding) — 1951–1981 (fertility decline begins); Stage 4 (low stationary) — 1981–present (TFR near replacement); Stage 5 (declining) — projected post-2050. (Source: Davis, K., The Population of India and Pakistan, 1951; Census Reports)
  • Regional divergence: South/West (Kerala, TN, Karnataka, Maharashtra, AP) — Stage 4/5 (TFR <1.8, ageing); North/Central/East (Bihar, UP, MP, Rajasthan, Jharkhand) — Stage 3 (TFR 2.3–3.0, youth bulge). (Source: SRS Statistical Reports; NFHS-5 State Factsheets)

5.3 Demographic Dividend — Opportunity & Risk

  • Window: 2005–2055 (peak 2020–2040). Working-age share (15–64) peaks ~69% (2030). (Source: UN WPP 2024; Economic Survey 2018–19 Ch. 7)
  • Realisation conditions: Education (NEP 2020 — GER 50% by 2035), Skill (PMKVY, NAPS — 40 cr trained by 2022 target), Health (Ayushman Bharat, NFHS-5 anaemia 57% women), Jobs (PLFS 2022–23: youth UR 10%, graduate UR 29.1%), Female LFPR (37% — East Asia 60%+ at peak). (Source: Economic Survey 2018–19; PLFS 2022–23; NEP 2020; PMKVY Dashboard)
  • East Asian precedent: South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore — export-led manufacturing absorbed labour; high female participation; high savings → investment. India — services-led, premature deindustrialisation (Rodrik 2016), low female LFPR. (Source: Rodrik, D., Premature Deindustrialization, 2016; World Bank East Asian Miracle 1993)

5.4 Ageing — Emerging Challenge

  • Elderly (60+): 104 million (2011) → 194 million (2031) → 319 million (2050) — 10% → 19% share. (Source: UN WPP 2024; LASI Wave 1 2017–18)
  • Feminisation of ageing: Women outlive men; 54% of 60+ are women; widowed 51% (60+) vs 12% men (LASI). (Source: Longitudinal Ageing Study in India Wave 1, 2017–18)
  • Policy: National Policy for Older Persons (1999, revised 2011), Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007 (amended 2019 — children/relatives definition expanded, tribunal), NPHCE, Atal Vayo Abhyuday Yojana, PMVVY (pension). (Source: MWPSC Act 2007 (amended 2019); MoSJE Schemes)
  • Challenges: Erosion of joint family, inadequate pensions (IGNOAPS ₹200–500/month), healthcare access, loneliness, elder abuse (HelpAge 2023: 5–10% prevalence). (Source: HelpAge India Report 2023; LASI 2018)
PYQ 2020 GS I
"Examine the linkages between demographic dividend and women's empowerment in India." — Answer: Dividend requires female LFPR rise (currently 37% vs male 78%). Education — female GER higher at secondary but drops at tertiary (marriage, care). Skill — PMKVY female participation 40% but placement in low-wage sectors. Health — anaemia 57%, maternal mortality 97. Unpaid care — women spend 5 hrs/day vs men 1 hr (NSSO Time Use 2019). Solutions — care infrastructure (creches, Maternity Benefit Act 2017), flexible work, gender-responsive skilling, safe transport, behavioural change (Beti Bachao Beti Padhao beyond survival).
Technique: Dividend accounting
Question: "India's demographic dividend is a time-bound opportunity." Quantify the window and necessary conditions.
Answer: Window — dependency ratio <67% till 2045 (UN). Peak working-age 68.9% (2030). Conditions: (1) Education — secondary completion 50% → 80%, tertiary GER 27% → 50% (NEP). (2) Jobs — 90 million non-farm jobs needed by 2030 (McKinsey); manufacturing share 17% → 25% GDP. (3) Female LFPR — 37% → 50% (East Asia benchmark). (4) Health — stunting 35% → <20%, anaemia 57% → <30%. (5) Skill — 500 million certified by 2030. Failure → demographic burden (unemployment, social unrest, fiscal stress).

6. Poverty, Inequality & Inclusive Development

6.1 Poverty Measurement & Estimates

  • Official poverty line: Tendulkar Committee (2009) — ₹33/day (urban), ₹27/day (rural) at 2011–12 prices → 21.9% poor (2011–12). Rangarajan Committee (2014) — ₹47/₹32 → 29.5%. No official estimate since 2011–12 (NSSO 68th Round). (Source: Tendulkar Committee Report 2009; Rangarajan Committee Report 2014; NSSO 68th Round)
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI): NITI Aayog National MPI (Alkire-Foster method) — 3 dimensions (health, education, living standards), 12 indicators. 2015–16: 24.85% multidimensionally poor; 2019–21: 14.96% (NFHS-4/5). Rural 19.28%, Urban 5.27%. Bihar 33.7%, Jharkhand 28.8%, UP 22.9% (highest). (Source: NITI Aayog National MPI Progress Review 2023; NFHS-4/5)
  • International: World Bank $2.15/day (2017 PPP) — ~10% (2021); $3.65/day (LMIC line) — ~40%. (Source: World Bank Poverty and Inequality Platform 2023)

6.2 Inequality — Income, Wealth, Consumption

  • World Inequality Database (2022): Top 1% income share 22.6%, wealth share 40.1%; Bottom 50% income share 15%, wealth share 6.4%. Top 10% income 57%, wealth 65%. (Source: World Inequality Database, India 2022 Update; Chancel & Piketty 2019)
  • Consumption Gini (NSSO): ~0.35 (rural), ~0.38 (urban) — lower than income/wealth Gini due to social transfers, PDS. (Source: NSSO 68th/75th/78th Rounds; NCAER IHDS)
  • Regional inequality: Per capita NSDP (2022–23) — Goa ₹4.7 lakh, Bihar ₹47,000 (10x gap). Southern/Western states 2–3x Northern/Eastern. (Source: MoSPI State-wise GDP 2022–23; 15th FC Report)
  • Social group inequality: ST/SC/ OBC poverty 2–3x general category (MPI); land ownership — SC 9%, ST 11% operational holdings (Agri Census 2015–16). (Source: Agri Census 2015–16; NITI MPI 2023)

6.3 Inclusive Development Frameworks

  • Eleventh Plan (2007–12): "Inclusive growth" — broadens benefit flow to poor/marginalised. Twelfth Plan (2012–17) — "faster, sustainable, more inclusive". (Source: 11th & 12th Five Year Plan Documents)
  • NITI Aayog Strategy @75: Inclusion across economic (jobs, income), social (health, education, gender), spatial (Aspirational Districts, NE, hills), digital (JAM trinity). (Source: NITI Aayog Strategy for New India @75, 2018)
  • Core Schemes: MGNREGA (Art. 21 — right to work; 260 cr person-days FY24, ₹97,000 cr), NFSA (Art. 21 — right to food; 81 cr beneficiaries, 5 kg/month), PMJAY (50 cr beneficiaries, ₹5 lakh cover), PMUY (10 cr LPG), PMAY-G/U (3 cr houses target), NSAP (pension), Jal Jeevan Mission (tap water — 14.5 cr rural connections, 78% coverage). (Source: Respective Ministry Dashboards 2024; Economic Survey 2023–24)
  • Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT): 315 schemes, ₹3.8 lakh cr FY24; estimated gain ₹2.7 lakh cr (leakage plug). Aadhaar-enabled, JAM trinity. (Source: DBT Bharat Portal; Economic Survey 2023–24)
  • Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP): 112 districts, 49 indicators (health, education, agriculture, financial inclusion, skill). Delta ranking drives competition. NITI Aayog 2023 — significant improvement in health/education; agriculture/financial inclusion lag. (Source: NITI Aayog ADP Reports 2023; UNDP Independent Evaluation 2022)
PYQ 2023 GS I
"Multidimensional Poverty Index is a better measure of poverty than income-based measures." Critically examine. — Answer: MPI advantages — captures deprivations (nutrition, schooling, sanitation, cooking fuel, assets), allows decomposition by indicator/region/group, policy-actionable (indicator-specific), comparable globally (OPHI/UNDP). Limitations — data lag (NFHS 3–5 years), indicator weights arbitrary, misses income volatility, intra-household inequality, does not capture vulnerability/shocks. Income measures — consumption-based (NSSO) captures current welfare, allows poverty line debate, but misses non-monetary deprivations. Complementary use — MPI for targeting, consumption for monitoring, both for SDG 1.2.
Answer Framework: Poverty/Inequality Questions
1. Measurement — Tendulkar/Rangarajan, NITI MPI (12 indicators), World Bank $2.15/$3.65.
2. Data — MPI 14.96% (2019–21), WID top 1% wealth 40%, consumption Gini 0.35, regional 10x gap.
3. Drivers — jobless growth, low female LFPR, landlessness, caste/gender/regional overlap, weak social protection.
4. Policy — MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJAY, PMUY, PMAY, DBT, ADP, NRLM, skill.
5. Gaps — exclusion errors, fiscal constraints, delivery leaks, urban poverty invisible, climate vulnerability.
6. Way forward — universal basic services, urban employment guarantee, wealth tax debate, 16th FC equity weights.

7. Urbanisation: Patterns, Problems & Governance

7.1 Urbanisation Trends

  • Level & Pace: 35.9% (World Bank 2023), up from 31.1% (Census 2011), 27.8% (2001). Absolute urban population: 377 million (2011) → ~500 million (2023) → 800 million (2050). (Source: World Bank Urbanization Data; Census 2011; UN World Urbanization Prospects 2018)
  • Census vs Dynamic Definition: Census towns (CTs) — 3,894 (2011) meeting criteria (population >5,000, density >400/sq km, 75% male non-agri) but governed as rural. Statutory towns: 4,041. CTs = "hidden urbanisation". (Source: Census 2011; Pradhan, K., India's Urban Transition, 2013)
  • Metropolisation: 53 million-plus cities (2011); 3 megacities (Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata >10 million). Top 10 = 15% urban population. Peripheral growth > core (Delhi NCR, Mumbai MMR, Bangalore). (Source: Census 2011 Urban Agglomerations; NITI Aayog Urban Reports)
  • State variation: Tamil Nadu (48.4%), Kerala (47.7%), Maharashtra (45.2%), Gujarat (42.6%) vs Bihar (11.3%), Assam (14.1%), Odisha (16.7%). (Source: Census 2011 State Urbanisation Data)

7.2 Urban Problems — Structural & Emergent

  • Housing & Slums: 65 million slum dwellers (Census 2011); housing shortage 18.78 million (2018, mostly EWS/LIG). Dharavi (Mumbai) — 1 million in 2.4 sq km. PMAY-U — 1.18 cr sanctioned, 80 lakh completed (2024). (Source: Census 2011 Slum Data; PMAY-U Dashboard 2024; NSSO 76th Round)
  • Water & Sanitation: 135 lpcd norm; actual 69 lpcd avg; non-revenue water 30–50%. Jal Jeevan Mission (Urban) — universal tap connections. Swachh Bharat Mission-U — ODF+ (1.2 lakh), ODF++ (15,000), Water+ (500) cities. (Source: MoHUA Service Level Benchmarks 2023; SBM-U Dashboard; CPHEEO Manual)
  • Mobility & Congestion: 400+ million registered vehicles (2023); public transport share declining (bus 30% → 15% trips). Metro — 900+ km operational (2024), 1000+ km under construction. NUTP 2014 — TOD, NMT, parking management. (Source: MoRTH Road Transport Yearbook 2023; Metro Rail Dashboard; NUTP 2014)
  • Air Pollution: 131 non-attainment cities (NCAP); Delhi PM2.5 annual avg 90–100 μg/m³ (WHO 5). Transport, industry, dust, biomass, stubble. GRAP (Delhi-NCR), NCAP 40% reduction target by 2026. (Source: CPCB NCAP 2024; SAFAR-IITM; IQAir World Air Quality Report)
  • Waste: 160,000 TPD MSW; 75% collected, 30% processed. SWM Rules 2016 — segregation, processing, landfill <20%. Legacy dumpsites — 3,000+ (Swachh Bharat 2.0 remediation). (Source: CPCB Annual Report 2023; SWM Rules 2016; SBM 2.0)
  • Climate Vulnerability: Urban heat islands (Delhi +4–6°C), flooding (Chennai 2015, Mumbai 2005/2017, Bangalore 2022), water scarcity (Chennai 2019, Bangalore 2024). Master plans ignore climate adaptation. (Source: IPCC AR6 WGII Ch. 6; NIDM Urban Flood Reports; IMD Heatwave Data)

7.3 Urban Governance — 74th Amendment & Beyond

  • 74th CAA 1992: Three-tier municipalities (Municipal Corporation, Municipal Council, Nagar Panchayat), Ward Committees (population >3 lakh), DPC (District Planning Committee), MPC (Metropolitan Planning Committee >10 lakh), State Election Commission, State Finance Commission. (Source: Constitution 74th Amendment Act 1992; Articles 243P–243ZG)
  • Implementation Gaps: Ward Committees not formed/defunct in most cities; MPCs non-existent (only 9 constituted); parastatals (development authorities, water boards, metro rail) undermine elected mayors; Mayor tenure 1 year (rotational), no executive powers; SFC recommendations not binding; own revenue <15% of expenditure (property tax, user charges). (Source: Praja Foundation Urban Governance Reports 2023; 15th FC Ch. 8; Janaagraha ASICS 2023)
  • Reforms: RERA 2016 (real estate), Model Municipal Law 2021 (directly elected mayor, 5-year term, ward committees mandatory), 15th FC — performance grants linked to property tax growth, audited accounts, publication of service levels. Smart Cities Mission (100 cities, ₹2 lakh cr) — ICCC, area-based development; critique — top-down, limited citizen participation. (Source: Smart Cities Mission Dashboard; Model Municipal Law 2021; 15th FC Report Vol. II)
PYQ 2019 GS I
"Urbanisation in India is not just a demographic phenomenon but a structural transformation." Discuss. — Answer: Demographic — rural-urban migration, natural increase, reclassification (CTs). Structural — sectoral shift (agri 42% employment → 14% GDP), spatial restructuring (peri-urban, corridors), informality (90% workforce), governance deficit (74th CAA unimplemented), environmental stress. Policy — NUTP, Smart Cities, AMRUT, PMAY-U, Metro, RERA, 74th CAA full implementation, metropolitan governance (MPCs), own revenue augmentation, climate-resilient planning.
Technique: Urban governance diagnosis
Question: "The 74th Amendment remains a half-finished agenda." Evaluate with evidence.
Answer: Mandatory provisions — 3-tier municipalities, reservations (SC/ST/women), SEC, SFC, 5-year term. Discretionary — Ward Committees, MPCs, DPCs. Status: Ward Committees — 12 states formed, 6 functional; MPCs — 9/53 metros; Mayors — 7 states direct election, 1-year term in 15 states; Own revenue — property tax 0.15% GDP (OECD 1%). Parastatals control water, transport, planning. 15th FC conditions — property tax reform, audited accounts, service level benchmarks. Model Municipal Law 2021 pending adoption. Conclusion — devolution of funds, functions, functionaries (3Fs) incomplete.

8. Migration: Internal, International & Diaspora

8.1 Internal Migration — Scale, Patterns, Drivers

  • Census 2011: 450 million internal migrants (37% of population) — inter-district 140M, inter-state 54M, intra-district 256M. (Source: Census 2011 Migration Tables; Srivastava, R., Internal Migration in India, 2020)
  • NSSO 64th Round (2007–08) / PLFS: Short-term/seasonal migrants ~100 million (Deshingkar 2019); construction, brick kilns, agriculture, domestic work. (Source: NSSO 64th Round; Deshingkar, P., Circular Migration, 2019; PLFS 2020–21 migration module)
  • Drivers: Push — agrarian distress, landlessness, drought, caste oppression, conflict; Pull — employment, education, marriage (women 67% migrants), urban amenities. (Source: Srivastava, R., Migration and Development, 2020; UNESCO Social Inclusion of Migrants 2013)
  • Inter-state corridors: Hindi belt → Maharashtra, Gujarat, Delhi, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu; Northeast → Bangalore, Chennai, Kerala; Odisha/Jharkhand/Chhattisgarh → brick kilns, construction in metros. (Source: Census 2011 Inter-state Migration Matrix; World Bank Migration and Development Brief 2020)
  • Covid-19 reverse migration: 10–12 million returned (2020); exposed portability gaps (PDS, health, social security), lack of inter-state coordination. One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) — 35 states/UTs onboarded (2024). (Source: Stranded Workers Action Network 2020; MoCA ONORC Dashboard; Economic Survey 2020–21 Ch. 5)

8.2 Policy & Legal Framework

  • Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979 (subsumed in OSH Code 2020): Registration of contractors/establishments, journey allowance, displacement allowance, protective conditions. Poor enforcement — registration <10%. (Source: ISMW Act 1979; OSH Code 2020; Parliamentary Standing Committee on Labour 2021)
  • Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008 (subsumed in Code on Social Security 2020): Social security boards, schemes for migrants. e-SHRAM portal — 29 cr registered (2024), portability via Aadhaar. (Source: e-SHRAM Dashboard 2024; Code on Social Security 2020)
  • Portability Initiatives: ONORC (ration), Ayushman Bharat (health — portability across states), PMAY-U (housing), PM-SVANidhi (street vendors). (Source: MoCA/DoFPD/NHA Dashboards 2024)
  • Urban Policy: Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (ARHC) under PMAY-U; Migrant Worker Hostels; NULM (shelters, skill). (Source: MoHUA ARHC Guidelines 2021; NULM Framework)

8.3 International Migration & Diaspora

  • Emigration: 18 million Indian-born abroad (UN DESA 2020); top destinations — UAE (3.5M), USA (2.8M), Saudi Arabia (2.5M), Malaysia, Qatar, UK, Canada, Oman. (Source: UN DESA International Migrant Stock 2020; MEA Annual Reports)
  • Remittances: $125 billion (2023, World Bank) — world's highest; 3.3% GDP; Kerala, Punjab, Tamil Nadu, UP top recipients. (Source: World Bank Migration and Development Brief 2023; RBI Remittances Survey 2021)
  • Emigration categories: ECR (Emigration Check Required) — low-skilled (Gulf); ECNR — professionals, students. ECR countries 18 (Gulf, SE Asia). eMigrate portal — registration, grievance. (Source: MEA eMigrate Portal; Emigration Act 1983)
  • Diaspora Engagement: Pravasi Bharatiya Divas, OCI/PIO merger (2015), Vande Bharat Mission (Covid repatriation), KNOW India Programme, SPARSH (pension), voting rights (proxy — Representation of People Amendment Bill lapsed). (Source: MEA Diaspora Division; Pravasi Bharatiya Divas Reports; RoP Amendment Bill 2018)
  • Brain Drain vs Brain Circulation: IIT/IIM graduates — 60–70% emigrate (historically); reverse flow — startups, R&D centres (Google, Microsoft, Tesla India), GIAN, VAJRA, SERB-POWER. (Source: Khadria, B., The Changing Face of Brain Drain, 2019; DST GIAN/VAJRA Reports)

Outside — Gulf boom (1970s–2000s) low-skilled; 1990s+ IT/professionals to USA/UK/Canada; student migration surge (7.5 lakh 2023); remittances $125B; policy — eMigrate, ONORC, OSH Code, bilateral MoUs (Gulf).

1. Data — Census 450M internal, 54M inter-state; NSSO 100M seasonal; UN 18M diaspora, $125B remittances.
2. Typology — rural-urban, inter-state, seasonal/circular, marriage, distress, skill-based.
3. Drivers — push (agrarian, caste, climate), pull (jobs, education, marriage), networks.
4. Vulnerabilities — informality, no portability, housing, health, education of children, contractor exploitation.
5. Legal — ISMW 1979→OSH Code, UWSS 2008→Code on Social Security, e-SHRAM, ONORC, Ayushman portability.
6. International — Gulf low-skilled, West high-skilled/students, remittances, diaspora engagement.
7. Way forward — universal portability, urban rental housing, migrant registration, skill certification, climate migration planning.

9. Globalisation's Impact on Indian Society

9.1 Economic & Labour Market Transformation

  • Post-1991 Structural Shift: Services 58% GVA (2023), Industry 26%, Agriculture 16%. IT/ITeS exports $194B (FY24), 5.4 million direct employment. (Source: MeitY Annual Report 2023–24; RBI BoP Data; PLFS 2022–23)
  • Female Workforce Entry: BPO/IT — 34% women (NASSCOM 2023); garments, electronics assembly (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka). But FLFP 37% — concentrated in low-wage, informal, home-based work. (Source: NASSCOM Strategic Review 2023; PLFS 2022–23; ILO Women and Men in Informal Economy 2018)
  • Gig/Platform Economy: NITI Aayog 2022 — 7.7 million (2020–21) → 23.5 million by 2029–30. Code on Social Security 2020 — platform workers eligible for PF, ESI, maternity; rules pending. Swiggy/Zomato/Uber/Ola/Urban Company — algorithmic control, no employer-employee relationship. (Source: NITI Aayog, India's Booming Gig Economy, 2022; Code on Social Security 2020; IFATU Reports)
  • Agricultural Distress: WTO AoA — import liberalisation, price volatility; farmer suicides 10,000+/year (NCRB); MSP procurement skewed (rice/wheat, Punjab/Haryana); PM-KISAN ₹6,000/year. (Source: NCRB Accidental Deaths & Suicides 2022; WTO AoA; GoI Agricultural Statistics 2023)

9.2 Social & Cultural Changes

  • Family & Kinship: Nuclear families 52% urban (NFHS-5); delayed marriage, declining fertility, rising divorce (1.1%), live-in recognition, single-person households rising (elderly, migrants). (Source: NFHS-5 2019–21; Census 2011 Household Data)
  • Consumption & Lifestyle: Middle class 300–400 million (NCAER); consumer credit (credit cards 9 cr, BNPL), e-commerce (Flipkart/Amazon/JioMart), OTT, food delivery, travel. Cultural homogenisation — English medium, global brands, festivals (Valentine's, Halloween). (Source: NCAER India Consumer Economy 2019; RBI Household Finance; TRAI OTT Data)
  • Digital Divide: Internet 900M subscribers (TRAI 2024), rural 38% penetration vs urban 67%; gender gap — 33% women vs 57% men (NFHS-5). Digital Public Infrastructure (Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, ONDC) — inclusion enabler but access gap persists. (Source: TRAI Telecom Subscription Data 2024; NFHS-5 Internet Use; MeitY DPI Reports)
  • Mental Health: NMHS 2016 — 10.6% prevalence; treatment gap 70–92%; youth anxiety, loneliness, social media pressure. Tele-MANAS (2022) — 1,500+ calls/day. (Source: National Mental Health Survey 2015–16; Tele-MANAS Dashboard 2024; Lancet Psychiatry India 2022)

9.3 Social Movements in Globalised India

  • Anti-corruption (2011): Anna Hazare, India Against Corruption → Lokpal Act 2013. (Source: Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013; IAC Archives)
  • Environmental: Chipko (1973), Narmada (1985–), Save Aarey (2019), Hasdeo Aranya (2022–). (Source: Guha, R., Environmentalism: A Global History, 2000; NBA Archives)
  • Gender: Nirbhaya (2012) → Criminal Law Amendment 2013; #MeToo India (2018) → POSH enforcement; Pinjra Tod (2015). (Source: Criminal Law Amendment Act 2013; POSH Act 2013; Pinjra Tod Archives)
  • Caste: Rohith Vemula (2016) → campus caste discrimination; Dalit assertion (Una 2016, Bhima Koregaon 2018). (Source: Jodhka, S., Caste in Contemporary India, 2018; NCSC Reports)
  • Farmers: 2020–21 protest (3 farm laws repealed 2021) — MSP legal guarantee, APMC mandate. (Source: Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021; SKM Archives; Parliamentary Debates)

10. Communalism, Regionalism & Secularism

10.1 Communalism — Concept, Stages, Dynamics

  • Definition: Bipan Chandra — ideology with three elements: (1) people of same religion have common secular interests; (2) interests of different religions are dissimilar/divergent; (3) interests are mutually antagonistic. (Source: Chandra, B., Communalism in Modern India, 1984; Engineer, A., Communalism in India, 1985)
  • Stages: Communal consciousness (religious identity salient) → Liberal communalism (separate interests, negotiable) → Extremist communalism (antagonistic, zero-sum, violence). (Source: Chandra, B., Communalism in Modern India, 1984)
  • Colonial Roots: Separate electorates (Morley-Minto 1909, Communal Award 1932), Partition (1947), two-nation theory. Post-independence — Congress secularism vs Hindu Mahasabha/RSS (Golwalkar's "We, or Our Nationhood Defined" 1939). (Source: Golwalkar, M.S., We, or Our Nationhood Defined, 1939; Chandra, B., India's Struggle for Independence, 1989)
  • Post-Independence Trajectory: 1960s — relative calm; 1980s — Shah Bano (1985), Ayodhya mobilisation (1986–92), Mandal-Mandal (1990), Babri demolition (1992), Gujarat 2002, Muzaffarnagar 2013, Delhi 2020. (Source: Engineer, A., Communal Riots in Post-Independence India, 1985; Varshney, A., Ethnic Conflict and Civic Life, 2002)
  • Institutional Responses: Article 25–28 (freedom of religion), Article 29–30 (cultural/educational rights), Section 153A/295A IPC (hate speech, religious insult), Places of Worship Act 1991 (status quo 15 Aug 1947), Communal Violence Bill (lapsed 2011, 2013), NCM (National Commission for Minorities 1992). (Source: Places of Worship Act 1991; NCM Act 1992; IPC Sections 153A, 295A)

10.2 Secularism — Indian Model & Judicial Interpretation

  • Indian vs Western: Western — separation (wall of separation, Jefferson); Indian — principled distance (Rajeev Bhargava), equal respect (Sarva Dharma Samabhava), state intervenes for reform (abolition of untouchability, temple entry, triple talaq ban). (Source: Bhargava, R., Secularism and its Critics, 1998; SC Judgments below)
  • S.R. Bommai v. UoI (1994): Secularism = basic structure; state neutrality; President's Rule valid if state government pursues anti-secular policies. (Source: S.R. Bommai v. UoI, 1994)
  • Ismail Faruqui v. UoI (1994): Mosque not essential to Islam (namaz anywhere); acquisition upheld. (Source: Ismail Faruqui v. UoI, 1994)
  • Shayara Bano v. UoI (2017) — Triple Talaq: Instant talaq (talaq-e-biddat) unconstitutional (3:2); violation of Article 14, 21, 25. Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019 criminalised. (Source: Shayara Bano v. UoI, 2017; Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019)
  • Sabrimala (2018) — Women's Entry: 4:1 — exclusion violates Article 25(1), 14, 15; essential religious practice test. Review pending (7-judge bench on essential practice test). (Source: Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala, 2018; Review Petition 2020)
  • Ayodhya (2019): 5:0 — title to Hindus (trust), alternative 5 acres to Muslims; Places of Worship Act 1991 upheld. (Source: M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das, 2019)

10.3 Regionalism — Forms, Causes, Management

  • Forms: Secessionism (Khalistan, Nagaland pre-1997, ULFA), Statehood (Telangana 2014, Gorkhaland, Bodoland, Vidarbha, Bundelkhand), Autonomy (Sixth Schedule, Article 371), Inter-state disputes (water — Cauvery, Krishna, Mahanadi; border — Maharashtra-Karnataka, Assam-Meghalaya), Sons-of-soil (Maharashtra 1960s, Assam 1980s, Haryana 2020s). (Source: Brass, P., Language, Religion and Politics in North India, 1974; Tillin, L., Indian Federalism, 2019)
  • Causes: Uneven development (regional disparity), cultural/linguistic distinctiveness, political mobilisation (regional parties), centralisation (Article 356 misuse), resource disputes (water, minerals). (Source: Tillin, L., Indian Federalism, 2019; 15th FC Regional Disparity Analysis)
  • Constitutional Management: Article 3 (parliamentary power), Article 4 (no amendment needed), Article 371 (special provisions — Maharashtra/Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal, Goa, Karnataka), Fifth/Sixth Schedules, Inter-State Council (Article 263), Zonal Councils, River Boards Act 1956, ISRWD Act 1956. (Source: Constitution Articles 3, 4, 263, 371; ISRWD Act 1956; ISC Reports)
  • Telangana Case Study: 1969/1972/2009 agitations; Sri Krishna Committee (2010); Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014 — water sharing, Hyderabad capital (10 years), Polavaram national project. (Source: AP Reorganisation Act 2014; Sri Krishna Committee Report 2010)
PYQ 2013 GS I
"Communalism arises either due to power struggle or relative deprivation." Argue with illustrations. — Answer: Power struggle — elite competition for state resources (electoral mobilisation, Ayodhya 1990s, Gujarat 2002). Relative deprivation — economic backwardness of community (Sachar Committee 2006: Muslims 8.5% population, 4.9% govt jobs, 3.7% IAS); perception of unfairness fuels mobilisation. Interaction — elites exploit deprivation (Jinnah's two-nation, 1980s Shah Bano/Ayodhya). Counter — civic engagement (Varshney: integrated associational life prevents riots — Aligarh vs Calicut).

But also — economic (water disputes, job reservation for locals — Haryana 75%, Andhra 75%), political (regional parties — DMK, TDP, TRS, AAP, TMC), administrative (statehood demands — Vidarbha, Bodaland). Cultural assertiveness is trigger; political economy sustains. Management — Article 371, Sixth Schedule, ISC, Zonal Councils, NITI ADP.

3. Key Judgments — Bommai (basic structure), Faruqui, Shayara Bano, Sabrimala, Ayodhya.
4. Data — Sachar 2006, NCRB communal riots, Varshney civic engagement, regional disparity (15th FC).
5. Regionalism forms — secession, statehood, autonomy, sons-of-soil, water/border disputes.

Mains Exam-Day Strategy — Society
For society questions, blend constitutional provisions, data (Census, NFHS, NSSO, NITI MPI, PLFS, NCRB), schemes, and critical analysis. Avoid generalisations — regional, caste, class, gender intersections matter. Use Supreme Court judgments (Navtej Johar, Joseph Shine, Puttaswamy, Bommai, Shayara Bano, Sabrimala, Ayodhya, Davinder Singh) where relevant. Structure: Concept → Constitutional/Legal → Data/Evidence → Judicial → Critique/Gaps → Way Forward. Conclude with behavioural change, law enforcement, education, economic opportunity, and federal cooperation. Always cite specific sources: "As per NFHS-5...", "SC in Bommai held...", "NITI MPI 2023 shows...". Practice 150-word and 250-word answers for each sub-topic.

Common Traps

  • Assuming caste is only a Hindu phenomenon — it exists among Christians, Muslims (Pasmanda), and Sikhs too. (Source: Sachar Committee Report 2006; Ranganath Misra Commission 2007)
  • Thinking urbanisation is only about cities — it includes peri-urban, census towns, and rural-to-urban migration dynamics. (Source: Pradhan, K., India's Urban Transition, 2013)
  • Missing that the Women's Reservation Bill (106th Amendment 2023) is linked to delimitation and Census — not yet implemented. (Source: Constitution 106th Amendment Act 2023)
  • Confusing demographic dividend with population explosion — both coexist (large population + young age structure). (Source: Economic Survey 2018–19 Ch. 7)
  • Overlooking that tribal societies are not homogeneous — distinct languages, economies, and integration levels across regions. (Source: Xaxa Committee Report 2014)
  • Treating secularism as "equidistance" — Indian model is principled distance, not strict separation. (Source: Bhargava, R., Secularism and its Critics, 1998)
  • Ignoring that regionalism has economic roots (water, jobs, resources) alongside cultural ones. (Source: Tillin, L., Indian Federalism, 2019)
