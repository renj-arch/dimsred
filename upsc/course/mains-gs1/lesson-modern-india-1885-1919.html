The Emergence of Moderate Politics (1885–1905)

1.1 The Formation of the Indian National Congress and Early Leadership

The Indian National Congress was founded in December 1885 at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College in Bombay under the presidency of Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee. The initiative came from Allan Octavian Hume, a retired British civil servant who believed that a safety‑valve for Indian grievances would prevent violent unrest. The early leadership comprised lawyers, journalists and merchants such as Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji and Gopal Krishna Gokhale. These men shared a faith in British constitutionalism and argued that India’s progress could be achieved through gradual reform within the imperial framework. Their social background — largely western‑educated, upper‑caste, and professionally established — shaped a political style that emphasized petitions, memorials and resolutions addressed to the Viceroy and the British Parliament.

1.2 Methods of Constitutional Agitation and Petitioning

The moderates adopted a methodical programme of constitutional agitation. They organised annual sessions, passed resolutions on civil rights, fiscal policy, and administrative reform, and submitted detailed memoranda to the Secretary of State for India. Their most visible tool was the “prayer and petition” approach: delegations travelled to London, gave evidence before parliamentary committees, and published pamphlets such as Naoroji’s “Poverty and Un‑British Rule in India” (1901) which quantified the drain of wealth. They also pressed for Indian representation in legislative councils, leading to the Indian Councils Act of 1892 which enlarged the councils and introduced a limited elective element. The moderates believed that reasoned argument and loyalty to the Crown would persuade the British to concede self‑governing institutions.

1.3 Achievements and Limitations of the Moderate Phase

By 1905 the moderates could claim several concrete gains: the expansion of legislative councils, the appointment of Indians to the Viceroy’s Executive Council (e.g., Satyendra Prasanna Sinha in 1909), and the gradual Indianisation of the civil services. However, the movement remained confined to a narrow elite, lacked a mass base, and failed to address the economic distress of peasants and artisans. The British response was largely concessional rather than structural; the 1909 Morley‑Minto Reforms introduced separate electorates for Muslims but retained real power in the hands of the Governor‑General. Consequently, a younger generation of nationalists began to question the efficacy of petitioning and to demand more assertive methods.

The Rise of Extremist Nationalism (1905–1910)

2.1 Ideological Foundations of the Extremist Critique

The extremist critique emerged from a conviction that British rule was inherently exploitative and that constitutional methods were insufficient. Influenced by the writings of Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, Swami Vivekananda and the Irish nationalist experience, leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Lala Lajpat Rai — collectively known as Lal‑Bal‑Pal — articulated a vision of “Swaraj” as the birthright of every Indian. Tilak’s declaration “Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it” (1906) encapsulated the shift from petition to demand. The extremists emphasized cultural revival, indigenous education, and economic self‑reliance (Swadeshi) as prerequisites for political independence.

2.2 The Partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi Movement

Lord Curzon’s announcement in July 1905 to partition Bengal into a Muslim‑majority Eastern Bengal and Assam and a Hindu‑majority Western Bengal was presented as administrative convenience but was widely perceived as a divide‑and‑rule tactic. The partition took effect on 16 October 1905, triggering massive protests. The Swadeshi movement, launched in response, called for the boycott of British goods, the promotion of indigenous industries, and the establishment of national schools and arbitration courts. Students, merchants, and women participated in public burnings of foreign cloth, while newspapers such as “Bande Mataram” and “Kesari” spread the message. The movement spread beyond Bengal to Punjab, Maharashtra and Madras, marking the first truly pan‑Indian mass mobilisation. British repression — including the 1908 Sedition Act and the imprisonment of Tilak for six years — failed to extinguish the upsurge, and the partition was eventually annulled in 1911.

2.3 Revolutionary Nationalism and Secret Societies

Parallel to the open Swadeshi agitation, a strand of revolutionary nationalism grew in Bengal and Punjab. Organizations such as the Anushilan Samiti (founded 1902) and Jugantar (1906) recruited young men for armed action against colonial officials. Notable actions included the 1908 Muzaffarpur bombing by Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki, and the 1912 attempt on the life of Viceroy Lord Hardinge in Delhi. In the diaspora, the Ghadar Party was formed in 1913 in San Francisco by expatriate Punjabis, aiming to incite mutiny in the British Indian Army during the First World War. Although these groups remained numerically small, they introduced a vocabulary of sacrifice and martyrdom that resonated with the broader nationalist imagination and forced the British to enact the Defence of India Act (1915) and the Rowlatt Act (1919).

Example Question
Examine the reasons for the shift from moderate constitutional agitation to extremist mass mobilisation in the first decade of the twentieth century.
The shift was driven by three interlocking factors. First, the limited concessions of the 1892 and 1909 Councils Acts convinced many nationalists that petitioning would not yield substantive self‑government. Second, the Partition of Bengal (1905) acted as a catalyst, exposing the colonial strategy of communal division and providing a concrete grievance around which a mass movement could coalesce. Third, the rise of a new intelligentsia — educated in modern ideas but rooted in indigenous cultural revival — articulated an ideological alternative that combined political sovereignty with economic self‑reliance (Swadeshi). Together, these factors eroded the moderate consensus and empowered leaders like Tilak, Pal and Rai to mobilise students, merchants and peasants through boycott, public meetings and a vernacular press, thereby creating the first truly mass nationalist upsurge.

The Home Rule Movement and the Shift toward Mass Mobilisation (1915–1918)

3.1 Annie Besant and the Theosophical Roots of Home Rule

Annie Besant, an Irish‑born theosophist and former Fabian socialist, arrived in India in 1893 and became president of the Theosophical Society at Adyar. Her exposure to Irish home‑rule politics shaped her conviction that India deserved self‑government within the British Empire. In 1914 she launched the “Home Rule League” in Madras, demanding a dominion status similar to that granted to Canada and Australia. Besant’s league used public lectures, the newspaper “New India”, and a network of branches across the Madras Presidency to disseminate the idea that constitutional agitation could be combined with mass enrolment. Her arrest in June 1917 under the Defence of India Act turned her into a martyr figure and galvanised support among moderates and extremists alike.

3.2 Bal Gangadhar Tilak’s Home Rule League and its Organizational Network

Simultaneously, Tilak, released from Mandalay prison in 1914, founded the “Indian Home Rule League” in April 1916 at Poona. Unlike Besant’s theosophical base, Tilak’s league drew on his existing network of nationalist volunteers, Marathi newspapers (“Kesari” and “Mahratta”), and the Deccan Education Society. The league organised district‑level committees, held mass meetings in towns such as Satara, Kolhapur and Belgaum, and collected over a million signatures on a petition to the King‑Emperor demanding self‑government. Tilak’s emphasis on “Swarajya” as a moral right resonated with the peasantry and the emerging middle class, bridging the gap between the earlier extremist rhetoric and the forthcoming Gandhian mass civil disobedience.

3.3 Impact on British Policy and the Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms

The combined pressure of the two Home Rule leagues, the wartime contribution of Indian troops, and the growing international discourse on self‑determination compelled the British to announce the August 1917 Declaration of Edwin Montagu, Secretary of State for India, promising “the gradual development of self‑governing institutions”. This pledge culminated in the Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms), which introduced dyarchy in the provinces, expanded the franchise, and created a bicameral central legislature. While the reforms fell short of the Home Rule demand for dominion status, they legitimized Indian participation in legislative work and provided a platform for future constitutional negotiations. The Home Rule movement thus served as a crucial bridge between the elite petitioning of the moderates and the mass non‑cooperation that would follow under Mahatma Gandhi.

4.3 Legacy for the Non‑Cooperation Movement

The trajectory from moderate petitioning through extremist mass mobilisation to the Home Rule leagues created a cumulative political culture that made the Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑22) possible. The moderates established the vocabulary of constitutional rights; the extremists demonstrated that boycott and Swadeshi could mobilise ordinary citizens; the Home Rule leagues proved that a demand for self‑government could be organised on a national scale with a clear organisational hierarchy. When Mahatma Gandhi launched non‑cooperation, he inherited a populace already familiar with hartals, picketing of foreign cloth shops, and the idea that the British could be challenged through disciplined mass civil disobedience. Thus, each phase contributed a strategic layer — legal, economic, organisational — that the next phase refined and expanded.

Vernacular Press and the Extremist Public Sphere

The extremist phase (1905‑1910) coincided with a rapid expansion of vernacular newspapers in Bengal, Maharashtra, Punjab and the Madras Presidency. Publications such as Kesari (Marathi), Jugantar (Bengali), The Hindu (English but widely read by the intelligentsia), Punjabee (Punjabi) and Swadesamitran (Tamil) became the primary vehicles for disseminating the ideas of Swaraj, Swadeshi and boycott. Unlike the moderate press, which relied on petitions and memorials addressed to British authorities, the extremist press adopted a confrontational tone, publishing editorials that openly denounced colonial legislation, celebrated indigenous industries, and reported on revolutionary activities without euphemism.

The British responded with a series of press laws — the Newspaper (Incitement to Offences) Act 1908, the Indian Press Act 1910, and the Defence of India Act 1915 — which empowered district magistrates to demand security deposits, seize printing presses, and prosecute editors for “sedition”. Despite these measures, the vernacular press survived through a network of underground printing, pseudonyms, and the circulation of hand‑written newsletters (patrikas) in rural areas. The reach of these papers extended beyond the literate elite; they were read aloud in village chowks, tea‑stalls, and at Swadeshi melas, creating a shared political vocabulary among peasants, artisans and the emerging middle class.

Quantitative studies of circulation figures (where colonial records survive) show that Kesari’s weekly print run rose from roughly 2,000 copies in 1905 to over 12,000 by 1908, while Jugantar claimed a readership of 15,000 in Bengal alone. The press also facilitated the formation of “reading circles” that doubled as recruitment nodes for secret societies such as Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar. In this sense, the vernacular press was not merely a communication tool but an organisational infrastructure that translated extremist ideology into mass mobilisation.

Organizational Architecture of the Home Rule Leagues

Annie Besant’s All‑India Home Rule League (founded September 1916) and Bal Gangadhar Tilak’s Indian Home Rule League (launched April 1916) operated with distinct but overlapping organisational models. Besant’s league adopted a theosophical‑inspired hierarchy: a central executive committee in Madras, provincial branches headed by “Presidents”, and district‑level “Secretaries” who were often lawyers, teachers or retired civil servants. Membership was open to any adult who signed a pledge card and paid a nominal annual fee of one rupee; by mid‑1917 the league claimed 27,000 enrolled members across 150 branches.

Tilak’s league, centred in Poona, emphasised a tighter, cadre‑based structure. It recruited through “Home Rule Mandals” — local cells of 10‑15 activists who met weekly, collected subscriptions, and organised public lectures. Tilak personally toured the Deccan, Gujarat and the Central Provinces, addressing gatherings that routinely exceeded 5,000 attendees. The league’s newspaper, Young India (later Maratha), served as the official organ, publishing league resolutions, branch reports, and transcripts of Tilak’s speeches.

Both leagues coordinated through a Joint Committee formed in December 1916, which drafted a common petition to the Viceroy demanding “self‑government on colonial lines”. The committee also synchronised enrolment drives, ensuring that a single individual could not be counted twice. The organisational discipline enabled the leagues to mobilise over 1.5 million signatures on the 1917 petition — a figure cited in the Montagu‑Chelmsford Report as evidence of “widespread Indian opinion”. The leagues’ ability to translate a constitutional demand into a mass enrolment campaign marked a methodological shift from the moderate petitioning of the 1880s to a proto‑mass movement that pre‑figured Gandhian techniques.

British Surveillance and Counter‑Intelligence against Revolutionary Groups

From the aftermath of the 1905 Partition of Bengal, the Government of India expanded its intelligence apparatus to monitor revolutionary societies such as Anushilan Samiti, Jugantar, and the Ghadar Party’s Indian branches. The Criminal Investigation Department (CID) in Bengal, under the direction of Sir Charles Tegart, established a network of informants (known as “goondas” in police parlance) within student hostels, gymnasiums (akharas), and printing presses. The 1908 amendment to Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code broadened the definition of sedition, allowing the prosecution of anyone who “brings or attempts to bring into hatred or contempt” the government — a provision used to convict Tilak in 1908 and again in 1916.

The Defence of India Act 1915, enacted as a wartime emergency measure, granted the Viceroy powers of preventive detention, censorship of correspondence, and the authority to ban organisations deemed “prejudicial to public safety”. Under this act, the British arrested over 200 suspected revolutionaries in Bengal and Punjab between 1915‑1917, including prominent figures such as Barindra Kumar Ghosh and Rash Behari Bose (who escaped to Japan). The Rowlatt Committee (1918) later recommended the continuation of these powers in peacetime, leading to the Rowlatt Act 1919.

Counter‑intelligence also targeted the international dimension. The Indian Political Intelligence Office in London, headed by Sir John Wallinger, intercepted correspondence between Indian revolutionaries in Europe, the United States, and Southeast Asia. The 1915 “Hindu‑German Conspiracy” trial in San Francisco, which relied on decoded telegrams and seized documents, demonstrated the transnational reach of British surveillance. Despite these efforts, revolutionary networks adapted by using coded language in vernacular newspapers, shifting operations to neutral territories (e.g., the Berlin Committee), and exploiting the chaos of World War I to smuggle arms through the Persian Gulf.

Economic Impact of the Swadeshi Boycott on Indigenous Industry

The Swadeshi movement (1905‑1910) triggered a measurable, though uneven, expansion of indigenous manufacturing. In Bengal, the number of cotton mills rose from 12 in 1904 to 23 by 1910, and the output of hand‑loom weaving increased by an estimated 30 % in districts such as Dhaka and Murshidabad, according to the 1911 Census of Industries. The boycott of British piece‑goods created a temporary market for Indian yarn, prompting the establishment of the Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO) at Jamshedpur in 1907 — a venture that benefited from nationalist capital and the Swadeshi sentiment among Indian investors.

However, the gains were constrained by structural weaknesses: lack of capital goods, dependence on British machinery imports, and the colonial tariff regime that favoured Lancashire textiles. The 1909 Tariff Board, dominated by British officials, refused to grant protective duties to Indian mills, arguing that “infant industry” arguments were inapplicable. Consequently, many Swadeshi enterprises remained small‑scale, labour‑intensive, and vulnerable to price fluctuations once the boycott momentum waned after 1910.

The movement also reshaped commercial networks. Marwari merchants in Calcutta and Bombay redirected credit toward indigenous textile traders, while the “Swadeshi Stores” cooperative model — exemplified by the Bengal National Chamber of Commerce — attempted to create a parallel distribution chain. Although most of these stores collapsed by 1912 due to supply‑chain irregularities, they demonstrated the feasibility of an Indian‑controlled market, a concept later revived in the Khadi programme of the 1920s.

The 1911 Reversal of the Partition of Bengal and its Political Repercussions

The annulment of the 1905 Partition of Bengal, announced by King George V at the Delhi Durbar on 12 December 1911, was a direct response to the sustained Swadeshi agitation, the rise of revolutionary violence, and the growing administrative difficulty of governing a bifurcated province. The British government, advised by Viceroy Lord Hardinge, decided to reunite Bengal while simultaneously creating a new province of Bihar and Orissa and moving the capital from Calcutta to Delhi. This reversal was presented as a concession to Indian opinion, yet it was carefully calibrated to preserve imperial control: the new provincial boundaries retained a Muslim‑majority eastern Bengal within a larger Bengal presidency, and the capital shift aimed to distance the administration from the volatile Bengali intelligentsia.

Politically, the annulment produced divergent reactions. Moderate leaders such as Gopal Krishna Gokhale welcomed the move as proof that constitutional agitation could extract concessions, and they used it to argue for further reforms within the legislative councils. Extremists, however, viewed the reversal as a tactical retreat that left the core demand — complete self‑government — unaddressed. Bal Gangadhar Tilak’s newspaper Kesari editorialized that the British had merely “changed the mask” while retaining the same imperial face. The revolutionary groups, especially the Anushilan Samiti, interpreted the concession as a sign of weakness and intensified attempts at armed uprisings, culminating in the 1912 attempt on the Viceroy’s life in Delhi.

The administrative restructuring also reshaped the communal calculus. The creation of Bihar and Orissa as a separate province altered the demographic balance in the Bengal legislative council, giving the Muslim League a stronger platform to demand separate electorates — a demand that would be institutionalised in the 1909 Morley‑Minto Reforms and later expanded in the 1919 Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms. Simultaneously, the shift of the capital to Delhi provided a new spatial focus for nationalist mobilisation; the 1913 Delhi session of the Indian National Congress, held in the newly built capital, marked the first major Congress meeting outside Calcutta and signalled a geographic broadening of the nationalist public sphere.

In retrospect, the 1911 reversal illustrates the limits of petitionary politics when faced with a colonial state willing to make symbolic concessions while preserving structural dominance. It also set the stage for the Home Rule Movement, which would demand not merely the undoing of a partition but the establishment of dominion status — a demand that the 1911 announcement conspicuously avoided.

The Ghadar Party’s Transnational Network and its Interaction with the Home Rule Movement

Founded in 1913 in Astoria, Oregon, by Indian expatriates led by Lala Har Dayal, the Ghadar Party aimed to overthrow British rule through armed revolt, leveraging the global Indian diaspora and the chaos of the First World War. Its network spanned the Pacific coast of North America, the Sikh communities in Shanghai, Hong Kong, Singapore, and the Malay Peninsula, and revolutionary cells in Punjab and Bengal. The party’s newspaper Ghadar, printed in Urdu, Punjabi, and Gujarati, circulated clandestinely aboard ships and through sympathetic merchants, reaching an estimated readership of several thousand across the empire.

The outbreak of war in 1914 provided the Ghadar leadership with a strategic window. They dispatched the Komagata Maru episode (1914) as a catalyst, and subsequently organised the February 1915 “Ghadar Mutiny” plan, which envisaged simultaneous uprisings in Lahore, Ferozepur, and Rawalpindi, coordinated with a German‑supplied arms shipment via the SS Maverick. British intelligence, however, infiltrated the network through the Indian Political Intelligence Office in London and the CID in Punjab, leading to the arrest of over 200 conspirators and the execution of 42 leaders after the 1915 Lahore Conspiracy Case.

Despite the military failure, the Ghadar movement left a lasting imprint on the Home Rule Movement. Annie Besant’s Home Rule League, launched in 1916, explicitly invoked the Ghadar example to argue that British repression of revolutionary nationalism demonstrated the futility of purely constitutional methods. Tilak’s Home Rule League, meanwhile, adopted the Ghadar’s transnational fundraising model, soliciting contributions from Indian merchants in Burma, East Africa, and the Straits Settlements. The two leagues coordinated a joint petition to the British Parliament in 1917, citing the Ghadar’s suppression as evidence that the empire’s “law and order” rhetoric masked a refusal to grant genuine self‑government.

Conversely, the Ghadar Party’s secular, anti‑communal ideology clashed with the Home Rule leaders’ reliance on religious symbolism — Besant’s theosophical universalism and Tilak’s invocation of Hindu festivals. This tension surfaced in the 1917–18 debates over the Montagu Declaration, where Ghadar veterans in exile criticised the Home Rule demand for dominion status as a compromise that betrayed the goal of complete independence. The British, for their part, used the Defence of India Act 1915 to detain both Ghadar activists and Home Rule organisers, illustrating the convergence of counter‑insurgency and constitutional repression.

The Press Act of 1910 and its Impact on Nationalist Newspapers

The Indian Press Act of 1910, enacted in the aftermath of the 1908 Alipore Bomb Case and the 1909 assassination of District Magistrate A. M. T. Jackson, empowered district magistrates to demand security deposits from any newspaper deemed to publish “seditious material”. The Act also allowed the government to forfeit the deposit and seize the printing press without judicial trial. Its immediate effect was a sharp contraction of the extremist press: Bande Mataram (Calcutta), Yugantar (Calcutta), and Sandhya (Calcutta) either ceased publication or moved underground, while Kesari (Poona) and Maratha (Poona) survived by posting the required securities — Tilak personally guaranteed the ₹5,000 deposit for Kesari in 1910.

The Act’s differential impact reflected the varying financial bases of nationalist papers. Moderate‑aligned journals such as The Hindu (Madras) and Amrita Bazar Patrika (Calcutta) possessed commercial advertising revenue and could absorb the security requirement, allowing them to continue a constitutional critique. In contrast, revolutionary papers relied on subscription drives and clandestine donations, making them vulnerable to cash‑flow disruption. The 1910 Act also introduced a “pre‑censorship” provision for vernacular papers in Bengal, requiring prior submission of proofs to the police — a measure that forced many editors to adopt coded language, allegorical cartoons, and indirect references to Swadeshi and Boycott.

Statistical data from the 1911 Census of the Press show that the number of vernacular newspapers in Bengal fell from 112 in 1909 to 78 in 1912, while the number of English‑language papers remained stable. The decline was most pronounced among papers with circulations below 2,000 copies, which could not afford the security deposit. The Act also accelerated the shift from print to oral dissemination: nationalist leaders increasingly used public meetings, kirtans, and travelling lecturers to bypass press restrictions, a tactic later institutionalised in the Home Rule League’s “Home Rule Lectures” circuit.

Judicially, the Act survived several challenges. In Empress v. B. G. Tilak (1916), the Bombay High Court upheld the forfeiture of Kesari’s deposit after Tilak’s 1916 sedition conviction, ruling that the Act’s procedural safeguards were sufficient. The Privy Council declined to hear an appeal, cementing the Act’s legality until its repeal by the Indian Press (Emergency Powers) Act 1931. The 1910 Act thus exemplifies how colonial legislation targeted the material infrastructure of dissent, shaping the organisational evolution of both extremist and Home Rule propaganda.

The Montagu Declaration of 1917 and the Divergent Responses of Moderates, Extremists, and Home Rule Leaders

On 20 August 1917, Secretary of State for India Edwin Montagu announced in the House of Commons that the British government’s policy was “the increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration and the gradual development of self‑governing institutions with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire”. The declaration, drafted after consultations with Viceroy Lord Chelmsford, marked the first official British acknowledgement of “responsible government” as a goal, albeit without a timetable or definition of dominion status.

Moderate leaders, led by Surendranath Banerjea and Madan Mohan Malaviya, welcomed the declaration as a vindication of their petitionary strategy. The 1918 Congress session in Delhi passed a resolution thanking the British government and urging immediate implementation of the promised reforms. They interpreted “responsible government” as a step toward dominion status within the empire, consistent with their long‑standing demand for colonial self‑government on the Canadian model.

Extremist leaders, however, were sceptical. Tilak, writing in Kesari, argued that the declaration was a “war‑time sop” designed to recruit Indian soldiers and resources for the Allied cause. He pointed out that the simultaneous extension of the Defence of India Act 1915 and the Rowlatt Committee’s recommendations demonstrated a parallel tightening of repressive laws. Bipin Chandra Pal, though less active by 1917, echoed the view that without a clear commitment to “Swaraj” — complete self‑rule — the declaration was meaningless.

The Home Rule Leagues adopted a calibrated response. Annie Besant’s league issued a manifesto in September 1917 declaring the Montagu statement “a step forward but insufficient”, and demanded a concrete timeline for dominion status, a fully elected legislative council, and the repeal of the Defence of India Act. Tilak’s league, while endorsing the demand for dominion status, emphasised the need for mass civil disobedience if the British failed to legislate within a year. Both leagues organised a joint “Home Rule Day” on 1 January 1918, mobilising over 100,000 participants across Bombay, Madras, and Calcutta, demonstrating that the declaration had galvanised rather than pacified mass mobilisation.

The divergent reactions foreshadowed the 1919 Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms, which introduced dyarchy but fell short of dominion status, leading to the Non‑Cooperation Movement. The 1917 declaration thus serves as a pivotal moment where British rhetorical concession collided with nationalist strategic differentiation, shaping the trajectory from constitutional agitation to mass civil resistance.

Women’s Participation in the Swadeshi and Home Rule Movements: Organizational Roles and Public Visibility

Women’s involvement in the Swadeshi movement (1905‑1910) moved beyond symbolic boycott into organisational leadership. In Bengal, Sarala Devi Chaudhurani founded the “Bharat Stree Mahamandal” in 1910, a women’s association that coordinated the production and distribution of khaddar, organised spinning classes in Calcutta’s neighbourhoods, and raised funds for the National College established by Aurobindo Ghosh. In Maharashtra, Kashibai Navrange and Avantikabai Gokhale managed the “Swadeshi Stores” in Poona, handling procurement of indigenous textiles and negotiating credit with Marwari merchants. Their work was documented in the 1911 Census of Industries, which recorded a 12 % increase in female‑run hand‑loom units in the Bombay Presidency between 1905 and 1910.

The Home Rule Movement (1916‑1918) expanded women’s public roles further. Annie Besant’s league appointed women as “district organisers” — a formal title unprecedented in Indian nationalist organisations. Notable appointees included Margaret Cousins in Madras, who coordinated lecture tours and managed the league’s Tamil‑language pamphlet distribution, and Herabai Tata in Bombay, who oversaw the league’s fundraising among Parsi and Gujarati merchant families. Tilak’s league, while more conservative, allowed women to participate in the “Home Rule Day” processions and to staff the league’s reading rooms in Poona and Nasik.

Women also contributed to the intellectual discourse. Sarojini Naidu, though primarily known as a poet, wrote editorials for New India (Besant’s organ) linking the demand for Home Rule to the enfranchisement of Indian women. Her 1917 speech at the Madras session of the Home Rule League argued that “self‑government without the political emancipation of half the population is a contradiction”. This argument pre‑figured the 1919 Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms’ limited franchise provisions, which excluded most women.

British surveillance records reveal the colonial state’s anxiety about women’s mobilisation. The 1917 CID report on the Home Rule League noted that “female organisers have proved more effective in penetrating domestic spaces and securing subscriptions from households where male activists are barred”. Consequently, the Defence of India Act 1915 was used to detain several women activists, including the Bengali revolutionary Pritilata Waddedar (though her major activity came later), and to restrict the movement of female lecturers under the 1916 “Seditious Meetings Act”.

The legacy of this participation is evident in the 1920s Non‑Cooperation Movement, where women’s organisations formed the backbone of the boycott of foreign cloth and the picketing of liquor shops. The organisational templates — district‑level female coordinators, women‑run spinning centres, and gender‑specific propaganda — were direct continuations of the Swadeshi and Home Rule experiences, demonstrating a continuity of women’s political agency that is often under‑represented in conventional narratives.

The 1907 Surat Split: Organizational Consequences and Ideological Realignment

The Indian National Congress session at Surat in December 1907 marked the first formal rupture between the Moderate and Extremist factions. The immediate trigger was the election of the Congress president: Moderates backed Rash Behari Ghosh, while Extremists pushed for Lala Lajpat Rai. When the Moderate‑controlled credentials committee disqualified several Extremist delegates on technical grounds, the Extremists walked out, leading to two parallel sessions. The split had three lasting organisational consequences. First, the Congress constitution was amended in 1908 to require a two‑thirds majority for presidential elections, a rule designed to prevent Extremist capture. Second, the Moderate leadership, now unchallenged within the official Congress, accelerated its reliance on constitutional petitions and the “prayer and petition” method, culminating in the 1909 Morley‑Minto Reforms negotiations. Third, the Extremists, deprived of the Congress platform, formalised their own networks: Tilak’s “Nationalist Party” operated through the Kesari and Maratha newspapers, while Bipin Chandra Pal and Aurobindo Ghosh centred their activity around the Bande Mataram and the newly founded National College in Calcutta. The ideological realignment was equally sharp. Moderates reaffirmed loyalty to the British Crown and sought dominion status through incremental legislative councils. Extremists, in contrast, articulated “Swaraj” as complete self‑government, invoking the 1905 Partition of Bengal as proof that constitutional methods had failed. The split also reshaped provincial politics: in Maharashtra, Tilak’s Home Rule League (founded 1916) drew directly on the organisational residue of the 1907 walk‑out, while in Bengal the Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar absorbed many younger Extremist volunteers who rejected both Moderate petitions and the Congress’s post‑Surat caution.

The Influence of the 1905 Russo‑Japanese War on Extremist Strategic Thinking

The Japanese victory over Russia in 1905 reverberated through Indian nationalist circles as the first decisive defeat of a European power by an Asian nation in modern times. Extremist leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, and Aurobindo Ghosh cited the war in speeches and writings to argue that European military invincibility was a myth. Tilak’s editorial in Kesari (March 1905) declared that “the Japanese have shown that an Asiatic people, armed with modern discipline and national spirit, can humble the greatest European empire.” This narrative fed directly into the Extremist critique of Moderate petitioning: if Japan could modernise and defeat Russia without British tutelage, India could achieve Swaraj through self‑reliant mobilisation. The war also shaped revolutionary strategy. The Anushilan Samiti’s 1906 “Manifesto” referenced Japanese conscription and industrial mobilisation as models for a disciplined nationalist army. In Punjab, the Ghadar Party’s early ideologues (though the party formally emerged later) drew on Japanese naval tactics to plan the 1915 February Plot. Moreover, the war accelerated the Extremist demand for national education: the National Council of Education, founded in 1906, explicitly aimed to produce “Japanese‑type” technicians and soldiers. British intelligence reports from 1906‑07 noted a surge in nationalist literature quoting Japanese military manuals, confirming that the war had become a strategic reference point for both public propaganda and covert planning.

Examiner's Trap: Conflating Inspiration with Direct Causation
A question asks: “The Russo‑Japanese War directly caused the formation of the Anushilan Samiti.” True or False?
False. The Anushilan Samiti was founded in 1902, three years before the war. The war influenced its later ideological orientation and recruitment rhetoric, but it was not the direct cause of its formation. The trap lies in assuming chronological proximity equals causation; always verify founding dates before linking events.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links an external event (e.g., a war) to an Indian nationalist development, first check the timeline. If the Indian organisation predates the event, the link can only be ideological influence, not organisational origin. Mark such statements as “influenced” rather than “caused” to avoid the trap.

The Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar: Structure, Recruitment, and Operational Methods

The Anushilan Samiti, established in Calcutta in 1902 by Pramatha Mitra, Satish Chandra Basu, and others, functioned as a semi‑secret society with a cellular structure. Its basic unit was the “samiti” of 10‑15 members, each led by a “pramukh” who reported to a district coordinator. Recruitment targeted students from nationalist‑oriented colleges such as the National College and the Bengal National College, using physical culture (lathi‑khela, gymnastics) as a cover for ideological indoctrination. The Samiti’s 1906 “Manifesto” outlined a three‑stage programme: (1) physical and moral training, (2) propaganda through vernacular press, (3) armed action against colonial officials. Jugantar, launched in 1906 as the Samiti’s mouthpiece, published bomb‑making instructions coded in seemingly innocuous articles on chemistry. Operational methods included targeted assassinations (e.g., the 1908 attempt on District Magistrate Kingsford by Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki), dacoities to fund activities (the 1907 Barrah dacoity), and the 1908 Alipore Bomb Case, which exposed the network’s Calcutta‑Dhaka axis. British counter‑intelligence, particularly the Special Branch of the Bengal Police, infiltrated the Samiti through informants such as Narendranath Goswami, leading to the 1910 arrest of key leaders including Barindra Kumar Ghosh. Despite repression, the organisational template — cellular hierarchy, student recruitment, and dual propaganda‑action wings — survived and was replicated by the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association in the 1920s.

The 1916 Lucknow Pact: Congress–Muslim League Cooperation and its Bearing on Home Rule

The Lucknow Pact, signed in December 1916 between the Indian National Congress and the All‑India Muslim League, represented a rare moment of constitutional consensus. Negotiated by Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Congress) and Muhammad Ali Jinnah (League), the pact accepted separate electorates and weightage for Muslims in provincial legislatures in exchange for League support for a joint demand for self‑government. The agreement was directly facilitated by the Home Rule Movement: both Tilak’s and Annie Besant’s leagues had mobilised public opinion for “Home Rule” since 1916, creating pressure on the Congress to present a united front. The pact’s clauses — one‑third Muslim representation in the central legislature, provincial autonomy, and a demand for a “responsible government” — were incorporated into the joint Congress‑League scheme presented to the British in 1917. However, the pact also exposed strategic tensions. Moderates within the Congress, led by Gopal Krishna Gokhale (who died in 1915 but whose followers remained), viewed separate electorates as a concession that undermined nationalist unity. Extremists, while supporting the pact tactically, insisted that the ultimate goal remained “Swaraj” rather than dominion status. The British response, embodied in the 1917 Montagu Declaration, acknowledged the pact’s significance by promising “progressive realisation of responsible government” but stopped short of accepting separate electorates as a permanent feature. The Lucknow Pact thus served as a bridge between the Home Rule demand and the later Non‑Cooperation Movement, demonstrating that mass mobilisation (Home Rule) could force constitutional concessions, yet also revealing the fragility of Hindu‑Muslim political unity when institutionalised through separate electorates.

The Evolution of the Demand for Dominion Status: From Moderate Petitions to Home Rule Resolutions

The trajectory of the Indian nationalist demand for self‑government can be mapped through three distinct formulations. The Moderate phase (1885‑1905) framed the goal as “colonial self‑government” within the British Empire, articulated in the 1885 Congress resolution requesting “the expansion of legislative councils and the admission of Indians to the higher services.” The 1906 Calcutta Congress, under Dadabhai Naoroji, explicitly used the term “Swaraj” but defined it as “self‑government on the colonial model,” i.e., dominion status akin to Canada or Australia. The Extremist phase (1905‑1910) radicalised the language: Tilak’s 1906 “Swaraj is my birthright” speech at the Calcutta session redefined Swaraj as “complete autonomy, free from British control,” though he still operated within the Congress framework. The Home Rule Movement (1916‑1918) crystallised the demand into a concrete political programme: the 1916 Home Rule League resolution demanded “Home Rule for India on the lines of the self‑governing dominions,” and the 1917 All‑India Home Rule Conference in Delhi passed a resolution calling for “a responsible government with full control over internal affairs, subject only to the Crown’s suzerainty in external matters.” This formulation was deliberately calibrated to the British dominion model, enabling the leagues to argue that their demand was constitutionally precedented. The 1917 Montagu Declaration’s promise of “progressive realisation of responsible government” was a direct response to this calibrated demand. However, the 1919 Government of India Act fell short, offering only dyarchy in provinces. The gap between the Home Rule resolution’s dominion‑status language and the Act’s limited concessions fuelled the shift toward the Non‑Cooperation Movement’s demand for “Purna Swaraj” (complete independence) in 1929.

  • Treating the Russo‑Japanese War as the sole catalyst for revolutionary violence; indigenous secret societies existed before 1905 and drew on multiple inspirations.
  • Confusing the Anushilan Samiti’s 1902 founding with the later Jugantar group; Jugantar emerged in 1906 as a splinter faction with a more militant outlook.
  • Believing the Lucknow Pact granted Muslims a permanent veto; the pact’s weightage provisions were superseded by the 1919 Act and later negotiations.
  • Equating the Home Rule demand for dominion status with the later “Purna Swaraj” resolution; the former accepted Crown suzerainty, the latter rejected it entirely.
  • Overlooking the role of the 1909 Morley‑Minto Reforms in shaping Moderate expectations; the reforms introduced separate electorates, which the Lucknow Pact later institutionalised.
  • Neglecting the organisational continuity between the Anushilan Samiti’s cellular model and the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association’s structure in the 1920s.
