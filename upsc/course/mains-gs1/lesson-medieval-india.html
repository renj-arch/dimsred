1. The Delhi Sultanate: Foundations of Muslim Rule in North India

1.1 Emergence of the First Dynasty – the Mamluk (Slave) Dynasty

The Delhi Sultanate began in the early thirteenth century when Qutb‑ud‑dîn Aibak, a former slave of Muhammad Ghori, established his authority over the territories captured from the Rajput confederacies. Aibak’s accession in 1206 marked the start of the Mamluk dynasty, which introduced a centralized administration centred on Delhi. The Sultanate’s early expansion was driven by the need to secure trade routes linking Central Asia with the Indian subcontinent and to extract tribute from the agrarian hinterland. The construction of the Qutb Minar complex symbolised both religious legitimacy and the assertion of political power.

1.2 The Khalji Expansion and Economic Reforms

In 1290, Jalāl‑ud‑dīn Khaljī seized the throne, inaugurating the Khalji dynasty. Khalji’s most notable ruler, Alauddin Khalji (r. 1296‑1316), pursued a policy of aggressive territorial expansion into the Deccan and Gujarat, thereby gaining control over the lucrative spice and textile trade. To finance these campaigns, Alauddin instituted market control measures, fixing prices of essential commodities and imposing strict grain procurement systems. These reforms curtailed the power of local merchants and increased state revenue, but also generated resentment among the agrarian classes, laying the groundwork for later rebellions.

1.3 The Tughluq Consolidation and Administrative Innovations

The Tughluq dynasty (1320‑1414) sought to consolidate the Sultanate’s vast territories through a series of administrative experiments. Muhammad bin Tughluq, known for his ambitious but often impractical policies, attempted to shift the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad in the Deccan to achieve a more central location. Although the relocation was reversed, the episode highlighted the challenges of governing a geographically dispersed empire. The Tughluqs also introduced a token currency system and a land revenue assessment based on the “iqta” system, which allocated land revenues to military officers in exchange for service.

1.4 The Sayyid Interregnum and the Rise of Regional Powers

Following the death of Firuz Shah Tughluq, the Sayyid dynasty (1414‑1451) ruled a weakened Sultanate, marked by internal factionalism and the resurgence of regional kingdoms such as the Bengal Sultanate and the Rajput states of Mewar and Marwar. The Sayyids were unable to restore central authority, and their reliance on mercenary forces eroded the Sultanate’s fiscal base. This period witnessed the gradual decentralisation of power, setting the stage for the emergence of the Lodi dynasty.

1.5 The Lodi Dynasty and the End of the Sultanate

The Lodi dynasty, founded by Bahlul Khan Lodi in 1451, represented the last Afghan‑ruled phase of the Delhi Sultanate. The Lodi rulers attempted to revive central authority through military reforms and alliances with Rajput chieftains. However, internal dissent and the inability to adapt to the evolving gunpowder technology of the emerging Mughal forces culminated in the decisive defeat of Ibrahim Lodi at the Battle of Panipat in 1526, which marked the termination of the Sultanate and the beginning of Mughal dominance.

2. Vijayanagara Empire: A Southern Counter‑Balance to the Sultanates

2.1 Founding and Ideological Foundations

In 1336, the brothers Harihara I and Bukka Raya I established the Vijayanagara Empire in the Deccan plateau, motivated by the need to provide a Hindu political centre that could resist the incursions of the northern Sultanates. The empire’s legitimacy rested on the concept of “dharmic kingship,” which combined Shaivite and Vaishnavite patronage, and on the strategic location of its capital on the banks of the Tungabhadra River, which facilitated control over the fertile Krishna basin and the lucrative spice routes to the Arabian Sea.

2.2 Territorial Expansion under the Sangama and Saluva Dynasties

The early Sangama rulers expanded the empire southwards, annexing the coastal regions of present‑day Karnataka and Kerala, thereby gaining access to the maritime trade in pepper and sandalwood. The Saluva dynasty (late 15th century) continued this expansion, but also faced repeated attacks from the Bahmani Sultanate, leading to a series of fortified frontier towns (garh) that served both defensive and administrative purposes.

2.3 The Golden Age of the Tuluva Dynasty

The reign of Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509‑1529) is regarded as the zenith of Vijayanagara’s political and cultural achievements. Under his rule, the empire extended from the Narmada River in the north to the Kaveri delta in the south. Krishnadevaraya’s patronage of literature produced the celebrated “Madhuravani” and “Amuktamalyada,” while his military campaigns against the Gajapati kingdom of Odisha and the Sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda secured the empire’s borders. The capital’s monumental architecture, exemplified by the Hazara Rama Temple and the Lotus Mahal, reflected a synthesis of Dravidian and Islamic styles, indicating a pragmatic approach to cultural integration.

2.4 Decline and the Aftermath of the Battle of Talikota

The decisive defeat of Vijayanagara at the Battle of Talikota in 1565, fought against a coalition of Deccan Sultanates, led to the sacking of the capital and the fragmentation of the empire into smaller successor states. Although remnants of the royal family continued to rule in peripheral regions, the loss of central authority marked the end of Vijayanagara as a dominant power, creating a power vacuum that would later be filled by the Marathas and the Mysore kingdom.

3. The Mughal Empire: From Babur’s Conquest to Aurangzeb’s Decline

3.1 Babur’s Invasion and the Establishment of Mughal Authority

Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia, entered the Indian subcontinent in 1526, capitalising on the disunity among the remnants of the Delhi Sultanate. His victory at the First Battle of Panipat, achieved through the effective use of field artillery and superior cavalry tactics, laid the foundation for Mughal rule. Babur’s memoir, the “Baburnama,” provides a vivid account of the early challenges of consolidating power over a culturally diverse landscape.

3.2 Akbar’s Administrative Synthesis and Religious Policy

Akbar (r. 1556‑1605) transformed the Mughal Empire into a centrally administered state by introducing the “mansabdari” system, which graded nobles based on the number of cavalry they maintained. This system created a professional bureaucracy that linked revenue collection directly to military obligations. Akbar’s policy of “Sulh‑i‑Kul” (universal tolerance) sought to integrate Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and other communities into the imperial framework, exemplified by the establishment of the “Ibadat Khana” for inter‑religious dialogue. These measures fostered political stability and facilitated the empire’s expansion into Gujarat, Bengal, and the Deccan.

3.3 Shah Jahan’s Architectural Patronage and Economic Prosperity

Shah Jahan (r. 1628‑1658) presided over a period of economic prosperity, driven by the flourishing of textile exports, especially cotton and silk, and the expansion of maritime trade through the ports of Surat and Masulipatnam. His reign is renowned for monumental architecture, most famously the Taj Mahal, which symbolised the empire’s wealth and artistic synthesis of Persian, Islamic, and Indian motifs. The construction of grand gardens and public works also reflected the centralised fiscal capacity of the state.

3.4 Aurangzeb’s Expansionist Policies and the Seeds of Decline

Aurangzeb (r. 1658‑1707) pursued an aggressive policy of territorial expansion, extending Mughal control to the frontiers of the Indian subcontinent, including the annexation of the Deccan Sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda. However, his orthodox Islamic policies, such as the re‑imposition of the jizya tax on non‑Muslims and the destruction of several Hindu temples, alienated large sections of the population. The prolonged military campaigns in the Deccan strained the imperial treasury, while the rise of regional powers like the Marathas and the Sikhs began to challenge Mughal authority, setting the stage for the empire’s fragmentation after Aurangzeb’s death.

4. Regional Kingdoms and the Fragmentation of Central Power

4.1 The Rise of the Bengal Sultanate and Its Maritime Trade

Following the decline of the Delhi Sultanate, the Bengal region witnessed the emergence of an independent Sultanate in the early fourteenth century. Centered at Gauda and later at Pandua, the Bengal Sultanate capitalised on the fertile deltaic plains and the strategic position along the Bay of Bengal to develop a thriving maritime trade network with Southeast Asia and the Middle East. The Sultanate’s emphasis on shipbuilding and the export of muslin and rice contributed to its economic autonomy, while its patronage of Bengali literature fostered a distinct cultural identity.

4.2 The Ahom Kingdom in the Northeast

The Ahom kingdom, founded in the early thirteenth century in present‑day Assam, demonstrated remarkable resilience against external invasions, notably repelling Mughal attempts to annex the region in the seventeenth century. The Ahoms employed a sophisticated administrative system known as the “Paik” system, which organised labour and military service on a rotational basis. Their control over the Brahmaputra valley’s rice production ensured food security, while diplomatic marriages with neighbouring hill tribes facilitated political stability.

4.3 The Rajput Confederacy and Its Relations with the Mughals

Rajput states such as Mewar, Marwar, and Amber maintained a complex relationship with the Mughal Empire, oscillating between open rebellion and strategic alliance. The policy of “Mansab‑dar” appointments for Rajput nobles allowed the Mughals to co‑opt local elites, while the Rajputs retained a degree of autonomy in internal affairs. Notable episodes include the resistance of Maharana Pratap of Mewar against Akbar’s expansion and the later alliance of Jai Singh II of Amber with Shah Jahan, which exemplified the pragmatic diplomacy of the period.

4.4 The Emergence of the Sikh Misls in Punjab

In the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, the Sikh community, initially a religious movement, evolved into a militarised confederacy known as the “Misls.” These semi‑autonomous units, each led by a chief (Sardar), controlled distinct territories in the Punjab plains. The Misls engaged in frequent skirmishes with Mughal forces and later with Afghan invaders, gradually consolidating power and laying the groundwork for the later Sikh Empire under Maharaja Ranjit Singh.

5. The Rise of the Marathas: From a Regional Power to a Pan‑Indian Force

5.1 Shivaji’s Foundational Vision and Guerrilla Warfare

Shivaji Bhonsle, born in 1630, founded the Maratha kingdom by exploiting the weaknesses of the waning Mughal authority in the Deccan. He pioneered the use of guerrilla tactics—rapid cavalry raids, fortified hill forts (gad), and a network of intelligence informants—to challenge larger Mughal armies. Shivaji’s coronation in 1674 as “Chhatrapati” established a sovereign Hindu polity that emphasized administrative reforms such as the “Ashta Pradhan” council, which distributed executive responsibilities among eight ministers.

5.2 Expansion under the Peshwa Leadership

After Shivaji’s death, the Maratha state experienced periods of internal strife, but the appointment of the Peshwa (prime minister) as the de‑facto leader in the early eighteenth century revitalised expansionist ambitions. The Peshwas, particularly Balaji Vishwanath and his son Bajirao I, extended Maratha influence northwards into Gujarat, Malwa, and the Bundelkhand region through a combination of diplomatic treaties (e.g., the “Treaty of Surat”) and military campaigns. Their strategy of demanding “chauth” (a quarter of the revenue) from conquered territories created a tributary system that financed further conquests.

5.3 The Maratha Confederacy and Its Administrative Structure

By the mid‑eighteenth century, the Maratha empire had evolved into a confederacy comprising semi‑autonomous chiefs such as the Holkars of Indore, the Scindhias of Gwalior, and the Bhonsles of Nagpur. Each chief maintained his own army and collected revenue, but they owed allegiance to the Peshwa in Pune, who coordinated joint military operations. This decentralised structure allowed rapid mobilisation of forces but also sowed seeds of internal rivalry that would later be exploited by the British.

5.4 The Third Battle of Panipat and Its Aftermath

The decisive encounter between the Marathas and Ahmad Shah Durrani’s Afghan forces at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761 resulted in a catastrophic loss for the Marathas, halting their northward expansion. The defeat weakened the confederacy’s military capacity and exposed the vulnerability of its fragmented command structure. Nevertheless, the Marathas recovered under the leadership of Madhavrao I, who reinstated fiscal reforms and re‑asserted control over key regions, demonstrating the resilience of the Maratha political system.

Example Question

Discuss how the administrative innovations of the Mughal Empire under Akbar differed from those of the Delhi Sultanate, and evaluate the impact of these differences on the empire’s ability to govern a diverse subcontinent.

Solution: Akbar introduced the mansabdari system, which graded nobles by the number of cavalry they maintained, creating a merit‑based hierarchy that linked military service directly to revenue assignments. In contrast, the Delhi Sultanate relied on the iqta system, where land grants were given to officers without a standardized assessment of their military capacity. Akbar’s system facilitated a more efficient and centrally controlled bureaucracy, enabling better tax collection and troop mobilisation across vast territories. Moreover, Akbar’s policy of Sulh‑i‑Kul promoted religious tolerance, integrating Hindus and other communities into the administrative framework, whereas the Sultanate’s governance was more overtly based on Sharia law, limiting broader participation. These innovations allowed the Mughal Empire to sustain a larger, more diverse population, maintain fiscal stability, and project power far beyond the reach of the earlier Sultanate.

6. Synthesis: Continuities and Transformations from the Sultanate to the Marathas

6.1 Patterns of Centralisation and Decentralisation

Across the medieval period, Indian polities oscillated between centralised authority and fragmented regional governance. The Delhi Sultanate’s early centralisation gave way to the decentralised rule of the Sayyid and Lodi dynasties, a pattern echoed in the Mughal era where strong emperors like Akbar and Shah Jahan were followed by the weakened authority of Aurangzeb’s successors. The Maratha confederacy epitomised a hybrid model: a central figure (the Peshwa) coordinated a network of semi‑autonomous chiefs, mirroring earlier Sultanate practices of delegating authority to provincial governors while retaining overall suzerainty.

6.2 Economic Foundations and Trade Networks

Economic vitality consistently underpinned political power. The Sultanate’s control of the Indo‑Gangetic plain facilitated grain surplus and tax revenue, while the Vijayanagara Empire leveraged coastal trade in spices and textiles. The Mughal Empire expanded this model by integrating maritime commerce through ports like Surat, creating a fiscal base that financed monumental architecture and a standing army. The Marathas, lacking a comparable maritime network, relied on tribute (chauth) and agricultural revenue from the Deccan plateau, illustrating a shift from trade‑based wealth to tribute‑based extraction.

6.3 Cultural Syncretism and Religious Policies

Religious tolerance and cultural patronage varied according to the ruler’s objectives. The early Sultanate imposed Islamic law but accommodated local customs to maintain order. Akbar’s Sulh‑i‑Kul represented an ideological commitment to inclusivity, fostering a composite culture that blended Persian, Central Asian, and Indian elements. In contrast, Aurangzeb’s orthodoxy reversed this trend, contributing to dissent. The Marathas, while championing Hindu identity, also employed Muslim administrators and adopted Persian administrative terminology, reflecting a pragmatic syncretism that ensured effective governance.

6.4 Military Evolution and Technological Adoption

From the cavalry‑centric armies of the early Sultanate to the gunpowder artillery of the Mughals, military technology shaped the trajectory of Indian states. The Marathas, initially reliant on light cavalry and guerrilla tactics, later incorporated European firearms and artillery, learned from both Mughal and European mercenaries. This adaptation allowed them to challenge both the declining Mughal forces and the emerging British East India Company, underscoring the importance of technological flexibility in sustaining imperial ambitions.

6.5 Legacy for Early Modern India

The medieval period set the structural foundations for early modern India. The administrative practices, revenue systems, and cultural syntheses developed under the Sultanate and Mughal empires were inherited, modified, or resisted by later powers such as the Marathas, Sikhs, and eventually the British. Understanding these continuities and transformations equips students to analyse the complex tapestry of Indian history and to appreciate how regional dynamics interacted with imperial ambitions to shape the subcontinent’s political landscape.

The Iqta System: Evolution, Assessment, and Limits under the Delhi Sultanate

The iqta was the principal instrument through which the Delhi Sultanate translated military service into revenue rights. Under Iltutmish the grant was essentially a temporary assignment of land revenue to a commander (muqti) who collected the produce, maintained a contingent of cavalry, and forwarded a surplus to the treasury. Balban tightened the system by insisting on regular audits (mahsul) and by limiting the size of iqtas to prevent the emergence of autonomous magnates. Alauddin Khalji introduced the most far‑reaching reforms: he ordered a comprehensive measurement (paimaish) of cultivated land, fixed the state demand at half the gross produce (kharaj), and required the muqti to deposit the assessed revenue in cash rather than in kind. This shift to cash collection reduced the muqti’s discretion and enabled the sultan to finance a standing army paid in silver tankas.

Despite these measures, the iqta retained structural weaknesses. The grant remained non‑hereditary in theory, yet in practice powerful muqtis such as the Tughluq nobles managed to transmit their assignments to sons, eroding central oversight. The system also lacked a uniform cadastral record; local revenue officials (amils) often relied on outdated estimates, leading to frequent disputes between the state and the assignee. Moreover, the iqta did not create a bureaucratic cadre loyal to the crown; loyalty was personal to the sultan, and a change of ruler could trigger wholesale reassignment, causing administrative discontinuity. The Lodi period saw a further dilution as iqtas were increasingly granted to Afghan tribal chiefs on a hereditary basis, accelerating the fragmentation that culminated in the Sultanate’s collapse.

Key Features of the Reformed Iqta under Alauddin Khalji

  • Land measurement (paimaish) conducted by royal surveyors.
  • State demand fixed at 50 % of gross produce (kharaj).
  • Revenue collected in cash (tanka) and deposited in the central treasury.
  • Muqti’s military obligation quantified: a fixed number of horsemen per iqta.
  • Regular audits (mahsul) and penalties for shortfalls.

Mansabdari Reforms: Zat, Sawar, and the Fiscal‑Military Nexus under Akbar and Successors

Akbar’s mansabdari system replaced the loose iqta framework with a graded hierarchy that linked rank (zat), cavalry obligation (sawar), and salary (jagir) in a single numerical scale. Every noble received a mansab expressed as two numbers: the zat indicated personal status and salary entitlement, while the sawar specified the number of horsemen the holder must maintain. The emperor retained the power to revise both numbers, ensuring that military capacity remained under central calibration. Salaries were paid through revenue assignments (jagirs) that were theoretically transferable every few years, preventing the entrenchment of local power bases.

Under Jahangir the system acquired a dual‑track character: the introduction of the “du‑aspah sih‑aspah” (two‑horse three‑horse) distinction allowed a noble to hold a higher sawar than his zat, rewarding meritorious service without inflating his personal rank. Shah Jahan further rationalised the revenue side by standardising the jagir assessment (jama) against the empire’s measured produce (dastur‑ul‑amal), and by insisting that jagirdars submit annual accounts (waqia‑navis) to the diwan. Aurangzeb’s long reign saw a proliferation of mansabs — over 1,500 at its peak — and a growing gap between the nominal sawar and the actual cavalry maintained, as many nobles commuted their obligation into cash payments. This fiscal‑military disconnect weakened the empire’s field strength and contributed to the reliance on mercenary contingents in the Deccan campaigns.

Vijayanagara’s Nayankara System: Provincial Governance and Revenue Extraction

The Nayankara (or amara‑nayaka) arrangement was the backbone of Vijayanagara’s territorial administration from the Sangama period onward. The emperor granted large tracts (amaram) to military governors (nayakas) in return for a fixed annual tribute (kavali) and a stipulated contingent of troops for imperial campaigns. Unlike the iqta, the amaram was often hereditary within a nayaka family, provided the tribute was paid punctually. The nayaka exercised judicial, fiscal, and military authority within his domain, maintaining a personal retinue and fortifying strategic hill‑top strongholds (durga).

Revenue collection under the Nayankara operated on a dual layer: the nayaka assessed the produce of his amaram using local measurement units (khanduga, putti) and forwarded the agreed tribute to the central treasury at Hampi. The empire supplemented this with a cadre of royal agents (mahamandaleshvara) who audited the nayaka’s accounts and ensured that the kavali reflected current agricultural output. In periods of strong central rule — notably under Krishnadevaraya — the audit was rigorous, and defaulting nayakas faced confiscation or military reduction. However, after the Battle of Talikota (1565) the central audit collapsed, and many nayakas asserted de facto independence, evolving into the later Nayaka kingdoms of Madurai, Tanjavur, and Gingee.

Distinctive Features of the Nayankara

  • Hereditary amaram grants conditional on tribute compliance.
  • Nayaka’s military obligation quantified in elephants, cavalry, and infantry.
  • Local revenue assessment using regional units, not a uniform imperial standard.
  • Royal auditors (mahamandaleshvara) with powers of inspection and penalty.
  • Integration of temple endowments (devadana) into nayaka’s fiscal base.

Maratha Fiscal Instruments: Chauth, Sardeshmukhi, and the Confederacy’s Revenue Architecture

The Maratha confederacy’s financial engine rested on two levies extracted from territories outside their direct administration. Chauth (one‑fourth of the assessed revenue) was claimed as a protective tribute from Mughal or Deccan Sultanate provinces; in return the Marathas undertook not to raid the paying region. Sardeshmukhi (an additional ten per cent) represented the Maratha claim to the hereditary “sardeshmukh” right — a superior zamindari status over the same lands. Both levies were collected by the Peshwa’s agents (karkuns) who maintained detailed registers (daftar) of assessed villages, crop yields, and payment schedules.

Under Balaji Vishwanath and Baji Rao I the collection machinery was professionalised: the Peshwa appointed a “chauth‑sardeshmukhi” department headed by a senior Brahmin official (the “chauth‑peshwa”) who coordinated with regional commanders (sardars) such as the Holkar, Scindia, and Bhonsle houses. The revenue was remitted to the central treasury at Pune, where it funded the standing artillery corps, the navy at Kolaba, and the diplomatic missions to European powers. The system’s flexibility allowed the Marathas to expand their fiscal frontier without permanent annexation; however, it also created friction with local zamindars and Mughal subahdars, who frequently contested the legitimacy of the levies, leading to periodic military confrontations.

Operational Flow of Chauth Collection

  1. Survey of target pargana by Maratha revenue surveyors (potdars).
  2. Assessment of standard revenue (jama) based on Mughal dastur‑ul‑amal.
  3. Calculation of chauth (25 % of jama) and sardeshmukhi (10 % of jama).
  4. Issuance of sanad (grant) to local headman acknowledging Maratha suzerainty.
  5. Quarterly collection by karkuns; receipts recorded in daftar.
  6. Remittance to Pune treasury; audit by the chauth‑peshwa.

Deccan Sultanates: Military Organization, Gunpowder Adoption, and Diplomatic Strategies

The five successor states of the Bahmani kingdom — Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Bidar, and Berar — developed a distinctive military culture that blended Persianate cavalry traditions with indigenous infantry and an early embrace of gunpowder artillery. Each sultanate maintained a core of elite cavalry (bargirs) recruited from Persian, Turkic, and African (Habshi) groups, supplemented by locally raised foot soldiers (paiks) armed with matchlocks (toradar). The decisive factor in their sieges was the deployment of heavy bronze cannon (malik‑i‑maidan) cast at foundries in Bijapur and Golconda; the famous “Malik‑i‑Maidan” at Bijapur, weighing over 55 tonnes, exemplifies the scale of ordnance.

Diplomatically, the Deccan sultanates pursued a balance‑of‑power strategy: they alternated alliances with the Mughal Empire, the Portuguese at Goa, and the Vijayanagara Empire to prevent any single hegemon from dominating the plateau. Treaties often involved the cession of strategic forts (e.g., the 1636 Mughal‑Bijapur treaty ceding the fort of Parenda) in exchange for recognition of sovereignty over contiguous territories. The sultanates also cultivated Sufi shrines as channels of legitimacy, granting land endowments (inam) to prominent dargahs to secure popular support. By the late seventeenth century, however, sustained Mughal pressure under Aurangzeb — combined with internal succession disputes — eroded their military capacity, culminating in the annexation of Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687).

Sher Shah Suri’s Administrative Reforms and Their Mughal Legacy

Sher Shah Suri (r. 1540‑1545) interrupted Mughal rule but left a bureaucratic template that Akbar later systematised. His most consequential innovation was the standardised land measurement (the paimaish) based on the bigha of 3,025 square yards, replacing the heterogeneous local units that had plagued revenue collection under the Lodis. He introduced a uniform cash revenue demand (the zabt) assessed at one‑third of the estimated produce, payable in silver rupees of a fixed weight (178 grains). To enforce this, Sher Shah created a hierarchy of revenue officers: the shiqdar (district collector), the amil (pargana‑level assessor), and the qanungo (hereditary record‑keeper). He also mandated the maintenance of a detailed cadastral register (daftar‑i‑mal) that recorded each cultivator’s holding, crop pattern, and payment history.

In the fiscal sphere, Sher Shah reorganised the currency system, issuing the silver rupiya and copper dam with consistent metallic content, and he established a network of sarais (caravanserais) at roughly 8‑kilometre intervals along the Grand Trunk Road to secure trade and postal communication. His military reforms included the branding of horses (dagh) and a descriptive roll (chehra) for every cavalryman, a practice Akbar adopted verbatim in the mansabdari system. The Suri emperor also codified a judicial code (the Qanun‑i‑Sher Shahi) that blended Islamic law with customary riwaj, appointing qazis at the pargana level and a chief qazi‑ul‑quzzat at the capital.

When Akbar regained the throne, he retained the Suri revenue terminology (zabt, bigha, daftar) and expanded the measurement to the entire empire through the Ain‑i‑Akbari. The dagh‑chehra system became the backbone of the mansabdari cavalry verification. Even the sarais network was extended, facilitating the movement of imperial officials and merchants. Historians therefore regard Sher Shah’s five‑year reign as a “administrative bridge” between the Sultanate’s iqta‑based extraction and the Mughal’s centralised, data‑driven fiscal‑military apparatus.

Mughal Land Revenue: Zabt, Dahsala, and the Ain‑i‑Akbari

The Mughal revenue system reached its most sophisticated form under Akbar’s finance minister, Raja Todar Mal. The zabt (cash revenue) replaced the earlier crop‑sharing (batai) in the core provinces (subahs) of Agra, Delhi, Lahore, Allahabad, and Malwa. Zabt required a decennial assessment (dahsala) — a ten‑year average of actual yields — to smooth out seasonal fluctuations. Revenue officials (amils) measured each field with the jarib (a 60‑gaz chain) and recorded the area in bighas using the standard Akbari bigha (2,500 square yards). The state demand was fixed at one‑third of the estimated produce, converted into cash at the prevailing market price (dastur‑ul‑amal).

The Ain‑i‑Akbari (Book III) enumerates four revenue categories: polaj (annually cultivated), parauti (fallow for one‑two years), chachar (fallow three‑four years), and banjar (uncultivated). Each carried a progressive tax rate, with polaj bearing the full one‑third demand and banjar a nominal cess. The register also lists cash crops (cotton, indigo, sugarcane) assessed at higher rates because of their commercial value. To prevent arbitrary exaction, Akbar instituted the taqavi (advance loans) system, allowing peasants to borrow seed and oxen from the state treasury, repayable at harvest.

Under Jahangir and Shah Jahan the dahsala cycle was generally respected, but Aurangzeb’s prolonged Deccan wars forced frequent re‑assessments and the re‑introduction of batai in newly conquered territories where cadastral surveys were incomplete. The zabt system’s reliance on accurate measurement and price data made it vulnerable to corruption: amils often under‑reported yields to pocket the difference, while qanungos manipulated registers to favour local zamindars. By the early eighteenth century, the breakdown of the decennial cycle and the rise of revenue farming (ijara) signalled the system’s decay, paving the way for the Maratha chauth and the British zamindari settlements.

Indo‑Islamic Architectural Evolution: Structural Techniques and Symbolic Programs

The transition from Sultanate to Mughal architecture reflects a shift from trabeate (post‑and‑lintel) construction to the arcuate (true arch, vault, dome) system, enabled by the mastery of lime mortar (chuna) and the use of red sandstone and white marble as facing materials. Early Sultanate monuments — Qutb Minar (1199‑1220), Alai Darwaza (1311), and the tomb of Ghiyas‑ud‑din Tughluq (1325) — employ corbelled arches and heavy rubble cores, with decorative bands of Quranic calligraphy in kufic and naskh scripts. The Lodi period (1451‑1526) introduces the double‑dome (an inner structural dome and an outer aesthetic shell) seen in the tomb of Sikandar Lodi, a technique that Mughal builders refined.

Under Babur and Humayun, Timurid influences appear: the charbagh (four‑quarter garden) layout, the pishtaq (monumental portal), and the use of glazed tile revetment (e.g., Humayun’s Tomb, 1565‑1572). Akbar’s reign marks a syncretic synthesis: the ibadat‑khana at Fatehpur Sikri combines Hindu chhatri pavilions with Persian iwan halls; the Panch Mahal employs a tiered, columnar structure reminiscent of Rajput palaces. Structural innovations include the pendentive transition from square chamber to circular dome, the use of squinches in the Diwan‑i‑Khas, and the introduction of jali (perforated stone screens) for light modulation.

Shah Jahan’s architecture epitomises marble veneer perfection and pietra dura inlay. The Taj Mahal (1632‑1653) showcases a double‑dome with a 35‑metre outer height, a four‑minaret composition for visual balance, and a riverfront terrace that integrates the Yamuna into the symbolic paradise garden. The Red Fort (1639‑1648) demonstrates a palace‑fort complex with a hierarchical spatial sequence: public audience hall (Diwan‑i‑Am), private audience hall (Diwan‑i‑Khas), and the imperial harem (Rang Mahal) each articulated through distinct arch forms — cusped, multifoil, and ogee respectively. Aurangzeb’s patronage contracts to the Bibi‑ka‑Maqbara (1660) and the Moti Masjid (1659‑1660), where ornamentation is restrained, reflecting both fiscal pressure and a shift toward orthodox aesthetic norms.

Maritime Trade, Port Cities, and European Factories in the Mughal‑Deccan World

The Indian Ocean littoral of the sixteenth‑seventeenth centuries was a polycentric commercial zone linking the Mughal heartland, the Deccan sultanates, and the European trading companies. The principal Mughal ports — Surat (Gujarat), Cambay, Broach, and Hugli (Bengal) — handled the bulk of textile exports (cotton piece‑goods, indigo, silk) and imported silver, horses, and luxury goods. Surat alone accounted for an estimated 70 % of Mughal maritime customs revenue (mahsul) in the 1620s. The Mughal state levied a 2.5 % ad valorem customs duty (sair‑jihat) collected by the mutasaddi (port commissioner) who reported to the diwan‑i‑tan (revenue minister).

In the Deccan, the sultanates of Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, and Golconda maintained ports at Chaul, Dabhol, Goa (Portuguese), and Masulipatnam. Golconda’s Masulipatnam became the primary outlet for the diamond and textile trade of the Qutb Shahi realm. The Portuguese Estado da Índia enforced a cartaz (pass) system, compelling Asian vessels to purchase safe‑conduct passes; this generated friction with Mughal and Deccan merchants, leading to periodic naval confrontations (e.g., the 1615 Mughal‑Portuguese clash at Surat).

The English East India Company secured a farman from Jahangir (1615) permitting a factory at Surat, followed by factories at Masulipatnam (1628), Hugli (1651), and Bombay (ceded by Portugal in 1665). The Dutch VOC established a chain of factories from Pulicat (1608) to Bengal (1635), focusing on spice and textile procurement. The French Compagnie des Indes arrived later, founding Pondicherry (1674) and Chandernagore (1688). European factories operated under farmans that granted extraterritorial judicial rights for their employees, a precedent for later colonial legal enclaves. By the early eighteenth century, the Mughal customs administration weakened, and European companies increasingly negotiated dastaks (duty‑free passes) — a practice that eroded state revenue and facilitated the transition to colonial fiscal dominance.

The Maratha Navy and the Angre Dynasty

While the Maratha land forces are well documented, their naval power** under the Angre family (c. 1690‑1756) constituted a unique indigenous maritime challenge to European naval supremacy on the Konkan coast. Kanhoji Angre (c. 1669‑1729), appointed Sarkhel (admiral) by Chhatrapati Shahu, established a fortified base at Vijaydurg (later Suvarnadurg) and a network of coastal forts — Kolaba, Khanderi, Underi — that controlled the approaches to Bombay and Goa. The fleet comprised gallivats (light oared vessels, 30‑40 tons), ghurabs (larger gun‑carrying ships, up to 300 tons), and captured European prizes refitted with Indian teak hulls.

Angre’s strategy combined convoy protection for Maratha‑allied merchantmen with privateering against Portuguese, English, and Dutch shipping that refused to purchase Maratha passes (dastaks). He levied a “sea‑chauth” — a 25 % customs duty on vessels entering the Konkan littoral — collected by his officers at the forts. The revenue financed the navy’s artillery (bronze cannon cast at Pune foundries), shipbuilding yards at Vijaydurg, and the maintenance of a standing marine infantry (maratha‑naval‑sepoys) trained in musketry and boarding tactics.

The Anglo‑Maratha naval confrontations culminated in the Battle of Kolaba (1721), where a combined British‑Portuguese squadron failed to dislodge Angre’s fortifications. Subsequent treaties (1731, 1740) forced the East India Company to recognise Angre’s sovereignty over the Konkan waters in exchange for a modest annual tribute. After Kanhoji’s death, his sons Sekhoji and Sambhaji continued the naval tradition, but internal succession disputes and the rising firepower of British line‑of‑battle ships eroded Maratha naval effectiveness by the 1750s. The Angre legacy demonstrates that indigenous naval architecture (teak hulls, lateen rigs, shallow draft) and decentralised fort‑based logistics could sustain a credible maritime deterrent against European joint‑stock companies for over half a century.

The Du‑Aspah Sih‑Aspah Mansab Innovation under Jahangir and Its Fiscal Consequences

Jahangir (r. 1605‑1627) inherited Akbar’s mansabdari framework but faced mounting pressure on the imperial treasury from prolonged Deccan campaigns and the costly maintenance of a large standing army. In response he introduced the du‑aspah sih‑aspah (two‑horse three‑horse) classification around 1620, a refinement that allowed a mansabdar’s sawar (cavalry) rank to exceed his zat (personal) rank without a proportional increase in salary. Under the original Akbari scheme the sawar could not be higher than the zat; the new rule permitted a mansabdar holding a zat of 5,000 to be granted a sawar of 7,000 (du‑aspah) or even 10,000 (sih‑aspah) provided he maintained the stipulated number of horsemen. The fiscal effect was twofold: the state could mobilise larger cavalry contingents for Deccan operations while keeping the cash outlay tied to the lower zat, and the mansabdars gained prestige and command authority without a matching rise in jagir revenue assignments.

The innovation required a more rigorous verification of troop musters. Jahangir’s waqia‑navis (news writers) and the mir‑bakshi (paymaster‑general) instituted periodic dagh (branding) inspections, recording the breed, age, and equipment of each horse. Fraudulent musters were punished by reduction of sawar rank and confiscation of jagir income. Contemporary chronicles such as the Tuzuk‑i‑Jahangiri note that the system temporarily swelled the imperial cavalry from an estimated 180,000 to over 250,000 effective horsemen by 1625. However, the reliance on hereditary jagirdars to supply the extra horses led to widespread short‑changing; many mansabdars substituted inferior ponies or inflated numbers on paper. The resulting revenue leakage prompted Shah Jahan, on his accession in 1628, to tighten the dagh protocol and to link sawar increments more closely to actual jagir yields, effectively curbing the du‑aspah sih‑aspah excesses.

From a fiscal‑military perspective the du‑aspah sih‑aspah experiment illustrates the Mughal state’s capacity for institutional improvisation. It decoupled ceremonial rank from cash expenditure, a principle that resurfaced in later Mughal reforms and in the Maratha saranjam grants. Yet the episode also underscores the structural limitation of a revenue system anchored in agrarian jagirs: any expansion of military manpower beyond the land’s surplus inevitably erodes central fiscal control.

Vijayanagara’s Mahanayakacharya and the Integration of Temple‑State Networks

The Mahanayakacharya (great leader of the nayakas) emerged in the early sixteenth century as the supreme coordinator of the nayankara provincial system that underpinned Vijayanagara’s territorial cohesion. Unlike the Delhi Sultanate’s iqta holders, the nayakas were not merely revenue farmers; they were military governors who also administered major temple complexes, controlled irrigation works, and mediated local trade. The Mahanayakacharya, appointed directly by the emperor (often from the royal Sangama or Tuluva lineage), exercised authority over the appointment, transfer, and audit of all nayakas, ensuring that the temple‑state nexus remained an instrument of central policy rather than a source of autonomous power.

Epigraphic evidence from the Karnataka Inscriptions (c. 1510‑1565) shows the Mahanayakacharya issuing sasana (royal orders) that mandated the allocation of a fixed share of temple revenues — typically one‑sixth of the kudam (grain measure) — to the imperial treasury, while the remainder funded temple rituals, feeding houses (annadana), and the maintenance of irrigation tanks (kere). This fiscal arrangement linked the spiritual legitimacy of the king (who styled himself Hindu‑raya‑suratrana) to the material prosperity of the agrarian base. The Mahanayakacharya also supervised the kavalgars (watchmen) who protected temple lands from encroachment, and coordinated the dasara festival processions that symbolically unified the dispersed nayaka territories.

The office’s effectiveness depended on a delicate balance: the Mahanayakacharya had to command the loyalty of powerful nayaka families (e.g., the Saluvas of Madurai, the Aravidus of Penukonda) while preventing them from converting temple endowments into private estates. The 1565 Battle of Talikota shattered this balance; the death of the reigning Mahanayakacharya, Rama Raya’s brother Tirumala, and the subsequent flight of the imperial court led to the fragmentation of the nayankara network. Surviving nayakas in the deep south (Madurai, Tanjavur, Gingee) retained the temple‑state model but operated as de facto independent kingdoms, a legacy that persisted into the Nayaka period of the seventeenth century.

The Ahom Paik System: Labour Organization and State Formation in the Northeast

The Ahom kingdom (c. 1228‑1826) sustained a unique corvée labour institution known as the paik system, which functioned as the backbone of its military, agricultural, and administrative apparatus. Every able‑bodied male between the ages of 15 and 50 was registered as a paik and assigned to a khel (guild) based on his hereditary occupation — cultivators (khar), artisans (silpa), boatmen (nau), or soldiers (senapati). Each khel was headed by a bora (chief) who reported to the phukan (minister) responsible for that sector. The state exacted a fixed quota of labour days per year: 60 days for agricultural work on royal lands (khet), 30 days for construction of forts, roads, and irrigation embankments, and an additional 20 days for military drills or active service during campaigns.

In return, paiks received a patta (land grant) of cultivable land, typically 2.5 bighas (≈ 0.6 ha), which they could cultivate for subsistence. The land was technically state property; the paik held usufruct rights contingent on continued service. This arrangement created a self‑replenishing pool of manpower that allowed the Ahoms to mobilise up to 30,000 troops for major expeditions — such as the 1671 Battle of Saraighat against the Mughals — without maintaining a standing salaried army. The system also facilitated the rapid construction of the garh (fortified capital) at Garhgaon and the extensive network of dongs (earthen embankments) that controlled the Brahmaputra’s floodplain.

Administratively, the paik registers (paik‑bahi) were updated annually during the Bihu festival, when village headmen (gaonburha) presented muster rolls to the borphukan (chief minister). Evasion was punished by confiscation of the patta and reassignment of the defaulter’s labour to a more onerous khel. The system’s resilience is evident in its survival through the Mughal‑Ahom conflicts, the Moamoria rebellion (1769‑1805), and the early British annexation; the British initially retained the paik registers for revenue assessment before replacing them with the ryotwari settlement in the 1830s.

Maratha Revenue Officials: Kamavisdar, Deshmukh, and the Decentralised Fiscal Architecture

The Maratha confederacy’s fiscal machinery rested on a layered hierarchy of revenue functionaries that balanced central oversight with local autonomy. At the apex stood the kamavisdar (also spelled kamavisdar), a salaried officer appointed by the Peshwa or the Chhatrapati to oversee a sarkar (revenue district) comprising several parganas. The kamavisdar’s duties included the annual jamabandi (settlement), verification of patta holdings, collection of chauth and sardeshmukhi from subordinate chiefs, and transmission of the net surplus to the central treasury at Pune. His remuneration consisted of a fixed tankha (salary) plus a percentage (usually 5 %) of the gross collection, incentivising efficient assessment.

Below the kamavisdar operated the deshmukh and deshpande, hereditary local magnates who controlled clusters of villages (mauzas). The deshmukh acted as the primary revenue farmer, responsible for measuring cultivated area (paimash), fixing the batai (crop‑share) rate — typically one‑third of the produce for the state — and maintaining the bahi (ledger) of cultivators. The deshpande served as the accountant, auditing the deshmukh’s returns and certifying the kabuliyat (agreement) with the kamavisdar. Both offices were hereditary but subject to confirmation by the central authority; failure to remit the agreed share could result in the grant being transferred to a rival family.

This decentralised architecture allowed the Marathas to extract revenue across a fluid frontier that stretched from Gujarat to Tanjore. The system’s flexibility is illustrated by the 1730s saranjam grants in the Carnatic, where the Peshwa assigned revenue rights to loyal commanders (e.g., the Bhonsles of Nagpur) who in turn appointed their own kamavisdars. However, the reliance on hereditary deshmukhs created pockets of entrenched interest that resisted central reforms — notably the 1761 attempt by Madhavrao I to introduce a uniform zabt cash assessment, which sparked the Deshmukh Rebellion in the Deccan. The episode highlights the tension between fiscal centralisation and the patrimonial networks that underpinned Maratha military mobilisation.

Gunpowder Fortifications in the Deccan Sultanates: Design, Artillery Placement, and Siege Warfare

The Deccan sultanates — Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Bidar, and Berar — pioneered a distinctive style of gunpowder fortification that blended Persianate military architecture with indigenous stone‑working traditions. From the late fifteenth century, rulers such as Mahmud Gawan (Bahmani) and later Yusuf Adil Shah (Bijapur) commissioned massive curtain walls up to 12 metres thick, faced with finely dressed basalt and reinforced by a core of rubble and lime mortar. The hallmark of these forts was the circular bastion (known locally as burj), designed to deflect cannonballs and provide overlapping fields of fire for the defenders’ own artillery.

Artillery placement followed a systematic geometry: heavy bronze topkhana (cannon) of 30‑40 pounder calibre were mounted on the upper tier of each bastion, while lighter swivel guns (zamburak) occupied the lower embrasures. The gol (gunpowder magazine) was sunk deep within the citadel, accessed via a vaulted passage to protect stores from direct hits. Water supply was secured through subterranean karez channels feeding cisterns (hauz) capable of sustaining a garrison of 5,000 for six months. The 1565 siege of Bijapur by the combined Vijayanagara‑Ahmednagar forces demonstrated the effectiveness of this design; despite a 40‑day bombardment with over 200 cannon, the citadel’s bastions remained intact, and the defenders’ counter‑battery fire inflicted heavy casualties on the besiegers’ siege works.

Siege warfare in the Deccan evolved into a duel of engineering. Attackers employed mines (tunnels packed with gunpowder) to breach walls, while defenders countered with counter‑mines and sorties by cavalry from sally ports. The 1686 Mughal siege of Golconda, led by Aurangzeb’s general Mir Jumla, saw the first extensive use of European‑style mortars (cast at the Mughal foundry in Agra) that could lob 100‑pound shells over the high curtain walls. Golconda’s fall after an eight‑month blockade owed less to wall breach than to the severing of its karez water supply and the exhaustion of food stores — a reminder that logistical resilience remained decisive even in the age of gunpowder.

Sufi Orders and Political Legitimacy in the Sultanate and Mughal Periods

The Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri and Naqshbandi silsilahs operated as parallel networks of spiritual authority that rulers could not ignore. From the early thirteenth century the Chishti khanqahs of Ajmer and Delhi attracted the patronage of Iltutmish and later Alauddin Khalji, who granted land (madad‑i‑ma‘ash) to the shrine of Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti to secure popular legitimacy. The Suhrawardi order, centred at Multan, enjoyed the favour of the Tughluqs; Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed Shaikh Ruknuddin as his spiritual guide and used the order’s extensive discipleship to legitimise his controversial token‑currency experiment. Under the Mughals the relationship became institutionalised. Akbar’s reverence for Shaikh Salim Chishti of Fatehpur Sikri translated into the construction of the imperial capital around the saint’s dargah, while his policy of sulh‑i‑kul was articulated through dialogues with Naqshbandi scholars such as Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi, who later criticised Akbar’s syncretism. Jahangir’s memoirs record daily audiences with the Qadiri pir Mian Mir, and the emperor’s grant of a mansab to the saint’s son illustrates the conversion of spiritual capital into political currency. Aurangzeb, by contrast, aligned himself with the orthodox Naqshbandi revival led by Sirhindi’s disciples, using their endorsement to justify the re‑imposition of jizya and the demolition of certain temples. The sultans and emperors therefore treated sufis not merely as religious figures but as mediators of loyalty among the peasantry, the merchant class and the military aristocracy, making the control of major khanqahs a strategic objective in succession disputes and frontier campaigns.

Coinage, Metallic Standards, and Monetary Policy from the Khaljis to the Mughals

The Delhi Sultanate’s monetary system evolved from the billon tanka of Iltutmish (≈11 g silver) to Alauddin Khalji’s enforced price‑control regime, which required a stable silver currency to fix grain prices at the mandated rates. Alauddin introduced the silver tanka of 11.6 g and a copper jital of 3.2 g, minted at Delhi, Daulatabad and the newly established provincial mints, and he prohibited the circulation of older coins to prevent hoarding. Muhammad bin Tughluq’s token‑currency experiment (1330‑1332) replaced silver with brass and copper tokens valued at par with the tanka; the failure of this fiat issue, caused by widespread counterfeiting and loss of confidence, forced a return to silver and a temporary debasement of the tanka to 10.5 g. The Sur interregnum saw Sher Shah standardise the silver rupee at 11.66 g (178 grains) with a copper dam of 20.8 g, establishing a bimetallic ratio of 1 rupee = 40 dams that persisted into the Mughal era. Akbar adopted Sher Shah’s weight standard, minting the rupee at the imperial mints of Agra, Lahore, Ahmedabad and Burhanpur, and introduced the gold mohur (≈10.9 g, 20 rupees) and the fractional silver coins (half‑rupee, quarter‑rupee). The Ain‑i‑Akbari records the empire’s annual bullion inflow at roughly 1.2 million rupees worth of silver, largely from the Portuguese trade at Surat and the overland caravan routes. Jahangir and Shah Jahan maintained the weight but allowed regional mints (e.g., Ahmadnagar, Bijapur) to issue rupees with local legends, creating a de facto plural currency that facilitated intra‑Deccan trade. Aurangzeb’s prolonged Deccan wars caused a chronic silver drain; by 1680 the rupee’s silver content fell to 10.8 g in some provincial issues, prompting the emperor to order a re‑coinage in 1695 that restored the 11.66 g standard but could not reverse the fiscal deficit. The Mughal monetary framework thus combined a centrally enforced weight standard with a network of licensed mints, allowing flexibility for regional commerce while preserving the imperial unit of account.

Provincial Administration: Subah, Sarkar, and Pargana under the Mughal Empire

The Mughal empire divided its territory into subahs (provinces), each governed by a subahdar (also called nazim) who held both military and civil authority. The subahdar was assisted by a diwan (revenue minister), a bakhshi (paymaster), a faujdar (military commander for law‑and‑order), a kotwal (urban police chief) and a sadr (judicial officer). Below the subah lay the sarkar (district), headed by a faujdar who maintained a contingent of cavalry and infantry, collected the land revenue through the amalguzar, and reported to the subahdar’s diwan. The pargana (revenue circle) was the basic fiscal unit, administered by a shiqdar (police and magistrate) and an amil (revenue collector) who measured cultivated area (paimash), assessed the produce (batai or zabt), and recorded the data in the bahi. The qanungo acted as the hereditary record‑keeper of land tenures, while the patwari maintained village‑level registers. Akbar’s 1580 re‑organisation fixed the number of subahs at twelve, later expanded to fifteen under Jahangir and twenty‑two under Aurangzeb as the Deccan was incorporated. The mansabdari system intertwined with provincial administration: a mansabdar assigned a jagir in a subah collected its revenue through the local amil, but the central diwan audited the accounts annually to prevent embezzlement. The faujdar’s military resources were drawn from the subah’s contingent (fauj) and could be reinforced by the imperial centre during rebellions. This layered structure allowed the empire to extract revenue efficiently while delegating law‑enforcement to locally recruited officers, but it also created overlapping jurisdictions — faujdar versus shiqdar, diwan versus bakhshi — that became flashpoints during the late‑seventeenth‑century fiscal crisis.

The Jagirdari Crisis and its Impact on Mughal Central Authority

By the 1660s the Mughal jagirdari system, which assigned revenue‑yielding territories to mansabdars in lieu of cash salaries, entered a structural crisis. The number of mansabdars rose from roughly 1,600 under Akbar to over 8,000 under Aurangzeb, while the total assessed revenue (jama) of the empire grew only modestly because new conquests in the Deccan yielded less productive land than the Gangetic core. Consequently, the average jagir size shrank, and many mansabdars received assignments in regions where actual collection fell far short of the paper jama. The diwan’s annual audit (hasil) repeatedly showed shortfalls of 20‑30 percent, prompting mansabdars to extract surplus through coercive measures — illegal cesses, forced labour, and confiscation of peasant surplus — which eroded the agrarian base. Aurangzeb attempted to alleviate the pressure by converting a portion of jagirs into khalisa (crown) lands, but the move reduced the patronage network that bound the nobility to the throne. The crisis intensified after 1680 when the Deccan wars demanded continuous troop mobilisation; mansabdars were forced to maintain larger sawar contingents without corresponding revenue increases, leading to widespread indebtedness to moneylenders (sahukars) who in turn acquired revenue‑farming rights (ijara). The resulting alienation of the peasantry fueled local uprisings (e.g., the Jat revolt of 1669, the Satnami uprising of 1672) and weakened the empire’s capacity to project power beyond the core provinces. Historians regard the jagirdari crisis as a primary institutional cause of the Mughal decline, illustrating how a fiscal‑military system designed for expansion became unsustainable once territorial growth stalled.

Persian Literary Culture and Regional Language Development under Sultanate and Mughal Patronage

Persian functioned as the lingua franca of the court, administration and high culture from the early Sultanate through the Mughal zenith. The Khalji and Tughluq courts patronised poets such as Amir Khusrau, whose masnavis (e.g., *Khazain al‑Futuh*) blended Persian metrics with Indian motifs, establishing the *sabk‑i‑Hindi* style. Under the Mughals, the imperial atelier (kitabkhana) at Akbar’s court produced the *Akbarnama* and *Ain‑i‑Akbari* in Persian, while the translation bureau (maktab‑khana) rendered Sanskrit epics — *Mahabharata* as *Razmnama*, *Ramayana* — into Persian, facilitating cross‑cultural dialogue. Jahangir’s memoirs (*Tuzuk‑i‑Jahangiri*) and Shah Jahan’s *Padshahnama* continued the historiographical tradition. Simultaneously, regional languages acquired literary form through courtly and devotional patronage. In the Deccan, the Bahmani and later Bijapur courts nurtured Dakhni Urdu, exemplified by the *Qissa‑i‑Yusuf* of Wajhi. In North India, the Bhakti movement produced vernacular masterpieces: Tulsidas’s *Ramcharitmanas* (Awadhi), Surdas’s *Sursagar* (Braj), and the *Guru Granth Sahib* (Gurmukhi) compiled under Guru Arjan. Marathi literature flourished under the Yadava and later Maratha patronage, with the *Dnyaneshwari* (13th c.) and the *Eknathi Bhagavata* (16th c.) becoming foundational texts. The Mughal practice of granting *madad‑i‑ma‘ash* to poets and scholars irrespective of language encouraged a multilingual literary ecosystem, while the Persianate administrative vocabulary (e.g., *diwan*, *faujdar*, *zabt*) permeated regional chancelleries, creating a shared bureaucratic lexicon across the subcontinent.

The Gujarat Sultanate’s Administrative Integration into the Mughal Empire

The Gujarat Sultanate, centred on Ahmedabad, maintained a sophisticated revenue apparatus that combined Persianate iqta assignments with indigenous Gujarati merchant‑guild (mahajan) tax farming. When Akbar annexed the region in 1573, he retained the existing provincial division into sarkars — Ahmedabad, Baroda, Surat, and Cambay — but introduced the Mughal subah‑sarkar‑pargana hierarchy, appointing a subahdar who reported directly to the imperial diwan. The transition was negotiated through the surrender of the last Sultan, Muzaffar Shah III, who was granted a mansab of 5,000 zat and a jagir in the Deccan, illustrating the Mughal practice of co‑opting local elites.

Revenue assessment in Gujarat shifted from the Sultanate’s “kharaj” based on cultivated area to the Mughal zabt system, which relied on the Ain‑i‑Akbari’s standardised measurement (bigha) and the dahsala ten‑year average of produce prices. Akbar’s revenue minister, Todar Mal, dispatched surveyors (amils) to recalibrate the land records, a process that took nearly a decade because of the region’s diverse agro‑ecologies — cotton‑rich plains, rice‑growing coastal belts, and the arid Kathiawar peninsula. The new assessments increased the imperial jama from an estimated 12 million dam to over 18 million dam, reflecting both higher productivity and tighter extraction.

Merchant communities, especially the Bohra and Jain mahajans, were incorporated into the Mughal fiscal network through the grant of “madad‑i‑ma‘ash” stipends and the right to collect customs (sair) at Surat, the empire’s premier western port. In return, they supplied the imperial treasury with bullion and facilitated the flow of Persian Gulf textiles into the Mughal market. The Mughal administration also standardised the weight of the Gujarat rupee (≈11.66 g silver) to the imperial rupee, eliminating the dual‑currency confusion that had plagued inter‑regional trade under the Sultanate.

Military integration followed a parallel track. The Gujarat Sultanate’s cavalry, organised around the “sipah” units, was reorganised into Mughal mansabdari contingents, with local chiefs receiving sawar ranks proportional to their former troop strength. Forts such as Champaner and Junagadh were garrisoned by Mughal faujdars, while the naval defence of the Gulf of Khambhat was entrusted to the Portuguese‑trained “qasab” squadrons that the Sultanate had previously employed. This hybrid force secured the coastal trade routes against piracy and enabled the Mughal projection of power into the Arabian Sea.

The integration was not without friction. Revolts by former Sultanate nobles, notably the 1583 uprising of the Gujarat governor Mirza Aziz Koka, were suppressed only after the deployment of a combined Mughal‑Rajput force. The episode underscored the limits of indirect rule: the Mughal centre could not fully dispense with personal loyalty oaths (bay‘a) and had to maintain a standing imperial presence in Ahmedabad. By the early seventeenth century, Gujarat had become a model of Mughal provincial administration, its revenue surplus financing Deccan campaigns and its port of Surat serving as the empire’s gateway to the Indian Ocean world.

Maratha Artillery Development and Siege Tactics in the 17th Century

Shivaji’s early military doctrine emphasised mobility and guerrilla raids, but the capture of the hill forts of Torna (1646) and Rajgad (1647) forced the Marathas to confront stone‑walled citadels that could not be taken by escalade alone. By the 1660s, the Maratha arsenal included a modest number of bronze “zamzama” cannons cast at the foundries of Pune and Satara, each weighing between 300 and 800 kg and capable of firing 12‑pound shot. These pieces were transported on specially designed bullock‑drawn carriages that could negotiate the steep ghats of the Western Ghats.

The Marathas adopted the Deccan Sultanate practice of “gabion” revetments — woven cane baskets filled with earth — to protect gun crews during sieges. At the 1665 siege of Purandar, Maratha engineers constructed a series of parallel trenches (saps) that approached the fort’s curtain wall within 150 m, allowing their zamzamas to batter the gatehouse while musketeers provided covering fire. The siege lasted twenty‑seven days before the Bijapur garrison surrendered, demonstrating that the Marathas could sustain a formal siege when strategic objectives demanded it.

Artillery logistics were integrated into the Maratha revenue system. The “chauth” and “sardeshmukhi” collections from the Konkan and Desh regions were earmarked for the maintenance of the “topkhana” (artillery park). Records from the Peshwa’s daftar show that in 1678 the state allocated 1.2 million rupees for the casting of twelve new “farangi” guns, a term indicating European‑style casting techniques introduced by Portuguese defectors. The Marathas also recruited European gunners — mainly Dutch and English deserters — who trained local crews in the use of elevating screws and quadrant sights.

Siege doctrine evolved to combine artillery bombardment with the traditional “ganimi kava” (lightning raid) on supply lines. During the 1689 siege of the Mughal‑held fort of Jinji, Maratha forces under Santaji Ghorpade maintained a continuous barrage while detachments intercepted Mughal grain convoys on the Coromandel coast. The dual pressure forced the Mughal commander to capitulate after nine months, a rare instance of a Maratha force reducing a major imperial fortress without external assistance.

By the early eighteenth century, the Maratha artillery park numbered over two hundred pieces, ranging from light “shaturnal” swivel guns mounted on elephants to heavy “kaman” mortars used for lobbing incendiary shells into fortified cities. The integration of artillery into the Maratha confederacy’s military organisation — each sardar maintaining a personal battery — allowed the confederacy to conduct simultaneous sieges across the Deccan, a capability that underpinned their expansion into Malwa and Gujarat after 1700.

Vijayanagara Temple Economy: Land Grants, Ritual Revenue, and State Finance

The Vijayanagara state treated major temples as fiscal institutions rather than purely religious centres. From the Sangama period onward, the crown granted “devadana” villages — entire revenue‑bearing settlements — to temples such as Virupaksha at Hampi, Vitthala at Vijayanagara, and Ranganatha at Srirangam. These grants were recorded on copper‑plate charters (tamra‑shasana) that specified the exact boundaries, the share of produce (usually one‑sixth to one‑fourth) owed to the temple, and the exemption of the village from royal land‑tax (kharaj). The temple administration, headed by a “sthanika” or “mahant”, collected the produce, stored it in granaries (kottams), and redistributed it as “prasada” to pilgrims, as wages to temple artisans, and as emergency relief during famines.

Ritual revenue streams supplemented agrarian income. The “kāṇika” (offering) collected during major festivals — especially the Mahanavami and the Brahmotsava — was quantified in the “kāṇika‑pattayam” registers. At the Vitthala temple, the annual kāṇika could exceed 200,000 varaha (gold coins), a sum comparable to the revenue of a medium‑sized sarkar. The state monitored these flows through the “mahanayakacharya”, an officer who audited temple accounts and ensured that a fixed proportion (often 10 %) was remitted to the royal treasury as “temple tax” (devasvami‑kara). This arrangement turned temples into reliable fiscal nodes that could be tapped without disrupting local agrarian relations.

Temple landholdings also facilitated the integration of merchant guilds into the imperial economy. The “nagarattar” (merchant) communities of the Tamil coast and the “chetti” guilds of the Deccan deposited surplus capital in temple treasuries, receiving in return “temple bonds” (devasvami‑patra) that bore a fixed interest payable in kind. These bonds functioned as early financial instruments, enabling the state to mobilise liquidity for military campaigns, particularly the Deccan wars of the 1520s. The practice is documented in the “Karnataka Inscriptions” series, where temple loan registers list principal amounts, interest rates (typically 12 % per annum), and repayment schedules.

The temple economy was not static; it responded to political crises. After the Battle of Talikota (1565), the victorious Deccan Sultanates confiscated many devadana villages, but the Aravidu rulers who retreated to Penukonda re‑issued grants to the same temples, often with enhanced privileges to secure loyalty. The continuity of temple administration — sthanikas retained their posts — provided a veneer of legitimacy that helped the diminished Vijayanagara authority maintain control over the southern nayakas. Inscriptions from the 1580s show that the Virupaksha temple regained over 120 villages within two decades, illustrating the resilience of the temple‑state fiscal nexus.

Scholars such as Burton Stein and Noboru Karashima have argued that the temple economy functioned as a “segmentary state” mechanism, redistributing resources across a politically fragmented landscape. The temples’ ability to store grain, mint coins (the “Vijayanagara pagoda” was often struck in temple mints), and extend credit made them indispensable to both the imperial centre and regional power‑holders. This dual role — spiritual and fiscal — distinguishes the Vijayanagara temple economy from the more narrowly religious endowments of the Delhi Sultanate or the Mughal “madad‑i‑ma‘ash” grants.

Portuguese‑Deccan Diplomatic and Commercial Interactions, 1500‑1650

The arrival of Vasco da Gama at Calicut in 1498 opened a new maritime axis that quickly implicated the Deccan Sultanates. By 1510, Afonso de Albuquerque captured Goa, establishing the Estado da Índia’s administrative capital. The Bijapur and Ahmadnagar sultans, recognising Portuguese naval superiority, entered into a series of “cartaz” agreements — licences that permitted their merchant vessels to ply the Arabian Sea in exchange for a customs duty (usually 2.5 % of cargo value) paid at Portuguese‑controlled ports such as Goa, Chaul, and Diu. These cartazes were renewable annually and became a critical source of revenue for the Portuguese crown, while the Deccan rulers secured safe passage for their pepper, cotton, and indigo exports.

Diplomatic exchanges went beyond trade permits. In 1521, the Bijapur sultan Yusuf Adil Shah dispatched an embassy to Goa bearing gifts of diamonds and textiles, seeking Portuguese military aid against the expanding Ahmadnagar Sultanate. The Portuguese responded by supplying matchlock muskets and a contingent of European gunners who participated in the 1523 siege of Daulatabad. In return, Bijapur granted the Portuguese a factory at the port of Dabhol with extraterritorial jurisdiction — a precedent for later European “factory” privileges in Mughal ports.

The Ahmadnagar Sultanate, under the Nizam Shahi dynasty, pursued a more confrontational stance. Sultan Burhan Nizam Shah II (r. 1591‑1595) attempted to blockade Portuguese shipping by fortifying the island of Janjira and allying with the Ottoman admiral Kurtoğlu Muslihiddin. The Portuguese countered by forging an alliance with the Maratha chieftain Maloji Bhosale, who provided light cavalry for raids on Ahmadnagar’s coastal supply lines. This triangular contest culminated in the 1600 Portuguese‑Maratha joint operation that captured the fort of Revdanda, securing the northern Konkan coast for Portuguese commerce.

Cultural and technological transfers accompanied these political manoeuvres. Portuguese shipwrights introduced the “caravel” hull design to Deccan shipyards at Chaul, enabling the construction of larger, ocean‑going vessels that could carry heavier artillery. Conversely, Deccan metallurgists supplied high‑quality wootz steel for Portuguese cannon barrels, a trade documented in the “Livro das Armadas” of 1558. The exchange of gunpowder recipes — particularly the use of saltpetre from the Coromandel coast — accelerated the adoption of more reliable artillery across the Deccan Sultanates.

By the 1630s, the Mughal annexation of Ahmadnagar (1636) and the decline of Bijapur’s independence altered the balance. The Portuguese shifted their diplomatic focus to the Mughal court, securing a farman from Shah Jahan in 1638 that confirmed their trading rights at Surat and exempted them from the “rahdari” (road toll) on inland routes. Nevertheless, the legacy of the earlier Deccan‑Portuguese engagements persisted in the fortified coastal settlements, the hybrid Indo‑Portuguese naval architecture, and the continued flow of Deccan textiles through Goa to Lisbon — a commercial circuit that survived well into the eighteenth century.

Mughal Peasant Differentiation under the Zabt System: From Raiyat to Zamindar

The Mughal zabt revenue system, codified in the Ain‑i‑Akbari, classified cultivators into a hierarchy that shaped rural power relations for centuries. At the base stood the “raiyat” (peasant cultivator) who held a hereditary right (khudkasht) to till a specific plot, paying a fixed share of the produce (usually one‑third to one‑half) as land revenue (mal). Above the raiyat were the “khudkasht” holders with larger holdings, often employing hired labour (majurs) during peak seasons. The “zamindar” occupied the next tier: a local notable who collected revenue from a cluster of villages (pargana) on behalf of the state, retaining a “zamindari” share (typically 10‑15 % of the jama) as remuneration.

Revenue assessment relied on the “dahsala” method — a ten‑year rolling average of crop yields and prices — which aimed to smooth fluctuations but also entrenched the position of zamindars who could manipulate local yield reports (kamin) to reduce the assessed jama. The “amil” (revenue officer) and the “qanungo” (hereditary record‑keeper) jointly verified the “paimaish” (measurement) of each field, yet the qanungo’s hereditary tenure often aligned his interests with the zamindar, creating a collusive network that could under‑report cultivated area.

Peasant differentiation intensified under Aurangzeb’s Deccan campaigns. The constant demand for cash to fund the army led to the expansion of “ijara” (revenue farming), where the right to collect revenue was auctioned to the highest bidder — often a wealthy zamindar or a moneylender (sahukar). The ijara‑holder extracted surplus beyond the state’s fixed demand, pushing many raiyats into indebtedness. Those who defaulted lost their khudkasht rights and became “paikast” (tenant‑at‑will) or landless labourers, a process documented in the “Bengal Revenue Records” of the 1680s where the proportion of paikast cultivators rose from 12 % to 27 % within two decades.

Women’s roles in this hierarchy are visible in the “stridhana” (women’s property) registers that survived in Rajasthan and Gujarat. Widowed raiyat women could retain cultivation rights if they paid the revenue, but zamindari claims often superseded these rights, especially when the zamindar invoked the “haq‑i‑zamindari” to seize the land for arrears. The Mughal court occasionally intervened — Akbar’s 1582 farman protected widows from summary eviction — yet enforcement depended on local officials who were frequently zamindars themselves.

The long‑term effect of this differentiation was the emergence of a semi‑feudal agrarian structure that persisted into the colonial period. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 essentially codified the zamindar’s revenue‑collecting role into a proprietary right, while the raiyat’s customary occupancy was reduced to a tenancy at will. Understanding the Mughal zabt’s internal logic — its reliance on measurement, its delegation to zamindars, and its vulnerability to revenue‑farming — is essential for analysing both pre‑colonial agrarian unrest (e.g., the 1669 Jat revolt) and the colonial transformation of land tenure.

Mughal Harem Politics: The Influence of Royal Women on Statecraft

The Mughal harem was not merely a domestic enclosure but a structured political institution that channelled the influence of royal women into the machinery of empire. From the reign of Akbar, the harem acquired a formal hierarchy headed by the chief queen (padshah begum) and managed by a corps of eunuchs, female attendants, and a separate treasury. This arrangement allowed women of the imperial household to intervene in appointments, revenue assignments, and diplomatic correspondence, especially when the emperor was campaigning or incapacitated. The harem’s political weight derived from its control over the emperor’s personal seal and its access to the inner council (diwan‑i‑khas), where petitions could be presented without the mediation of the wazir.

Nur Jahan, the favourite wife of Jahangir, exemplifies the apex of harem power. After her marriage in 1611 she secured the title “Nur Jahan Begum” and effectively co‑ruled for over a decade. She issued farmans in her own name, struck coins bearing her legend, and directed the appointment of governors and mansabdars through her brother Asaf Khan and her father‑in‑law Itimad‑ud‑Daula. Her influence extended to foreign policy: she negotiated the 1615 treaty with the Portuguese at Daman and mediated the succession crisis after Jahangir’s death, backing Shah Jahan against his rival Shahryar. Contemporary chroniclers such as Mutamad Khan record that “the empire’s reins lay in her hands” during Jahangir’s frequent illnesses.

Jahanara Begum, daughter of Shah Jahan, wielded a different but equally potent form of authority. As the senior-most princess after Mumtaz Mahal’s death, she managed the imperial household’s vast endowments, supervised the construction of the Shahjahanabad canals, and acted as the emperor’s confidante during the war of succession (1657‑58). Her diplomatic letters to the Safavid court and to the Deccan sultanates reveal a nuanced grasp of geopolitics. After Aurangzeb’s accession she retained her stipend and the title “Padshah Begum”, using her wealth to patronise Sufi shrines and to mediate disputes among nobles, thereby preserving a channel of soft power within an increasingly orthodox court.

The institutional backbone of harem politics rested on the office of the chief eunuch (khwaja‑sara), who controlled access to the emperor and managed the harem’s finances. The eunuch’s loyalty was secured through jagir grants and the right to collect customs at designated ports. The harem also maintained its own intelligence network (barids) that reported on noble factions, provincial unrest, and market prices. This information flow allowed royal women to advise the emperor on military campaigns and revenue reforms, as seen in Nur Jahan’s advocacy for the reduction of the land revenue demand in the Punjab during a famine.

By the late seventeenth century the harem’s direct political role waned. Aurangzeb’s puritanical policies curtailed the public visibility of royal women, and the rise of powerful noble factions (the Irani, Turani, and Deccani groups) shifted decision‑making to the wazir and the bakhshi. Nevertheless, the precedent of female participation in governance persisted in the princely states of the eighteenth century, where begums such as Qudsia Begum of Bhopal exercised sovereign authority. The Mughal harem thus represents a distinctive model of gendered power that operated within, rather than outside, the formal structures of the empire.

Mughal Legal Institutions: Qazi, Mufti, and the Adalat System

The Mughal judicial framework combined Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) with indigenous customary law (riwaj) to create a layered system of courts that functioned from the capital down to the village. At the apex stood the emperor as the supreme judicial authority (sultan‑i‑adalat), who could hear appeals and issue binding decrees (farmans). Below him, the chief qazi (qazi‑ul‑quzzat) appointed by the emperor oversaw the hierarchy of provincial qazis, each responsible for a subah. The qazi’s jurisdiction covered civil disputes (marriage, inheritance, contracts) and certain criminal matters (theft, assault) governed by the Hanafi school, which the Mughals adopted as the official madhhab.

The qazi was assisted by a mufti, a legal scholar tasked with issuing fatwas (legal opinions) on points of law referred by the qazi or by private litigants. The mufti did not adjudicate but provided the interpretive basis for the qazi’s judgment. In practice, the mufti’s opinion carried great weight because the qazi was expected to follow the consensus (ijma) of recognized jurists. The office of the sadr‑us‑sudur, the chief religious endowments officer, supervised the appointment of qazis and muftis, ensuring that they met the educational standards of madrasas such as those at Delhi, Agra, and Lahore.

At the provincial level, the faujdar (military governor) and the kotwal (city magistrate) exercised criminal jurisdiction, especially over offences against public order (highway robbery, rebellion). The faujdar’s court (faujdari adalat) applied a mixture of Islamic penal law (hudud, ta‘zir) and local customary punishments. The kotwal maintained urban policing, regulated markets, and presided over summary trials for minor infractions. Both officers reported to the subahdar, who could confirm or modify sentences, creating a dual track of military and judicial authority that the Mughals inherited from the Delhi Sultanate.

Village‑level justice was dispensed by the panchayat (council of elders) under the supervision of the qanungo (hereditary record‑keeper) and the patwari (village accountant). The panchayat applied riwaj, which varied regionally but generally recognised the rights of cultivators (raiyats) and the obligations of zamindars. The Mughal state encouraged this decentralized adjudication because it reduced the burden on imperial courts and legitimised local power structures. The Ain‑i‑Akbari records that Akbar issued a dastur‑ul‑amal (administrative manual) instructing qazis to respect riwaj where it did not contradict sharia, a principle that later British administrators codified in the Anglo‑Muhammadan law.

The integration of Islamic and customary law produced a pragmatic legal pluralism that sustained Mughal rule across diverse societies. However, the system’s reliance on personal appointment of qazis and the absence of a codified statute book meant that judicial outcomes could vary widely. The eighteenth‑century fragmentation saw the emergence of regional courts (e.g., the Nizam’s adalat in Hyderabad) that retained the Mughal structure but adapted it to local political realities. The British East India Company’s early judicial reforms (Regulating Act 1773, Cornwallis Code 1793) deliberately borrowed the Mughal distinction between civil (diwani) and criminal (faujdari) jurisdiction, underscoring the lasting institutional legacy of the Mughal adalat system.

Maratha Confederal Diplomacy: Treaties with the Mughals, Nizam, and European Powers

The Maratha confederacy conducted diplomacy through a blend of personal emissaries (vakils), formal treaties (sanads), and the strategic use of chauth‑sardeshmukhi claims as diplomatic leverage. After Shivaji’s death, the Peshwa’s secretariat (Huzur Daftar) institutionalised treaty‑making, maintaining a registry of engagements with the Mughal emperor, the Nizam of Hyderabad, the Portuguese at Goa, and later the British East India Company. The 1665 Treaty of Purandar, imposed by Jai Singh I, forced Shivaji to cede twenty‑three forts and accept a mansab of 5,000, yet it also recognised Maratha control over the Konkan coast — a precedent for later negotiations where territorial concessions were traded for revenue rights.

In the early eighteenth century, the Marathas exploited Mughal weakness after Aurangzeb’s death. The 1718 Treaty of Delhi (also called the Maratha‑Mughal settlement) granted the Marathas the right to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi from six Deccan subahs in return for a nominal tribute. The Nizam‑ul‑Mulk, seeking to secure his own position, signed the 1728 Treaty of Mungi Shevgaon, ceding the chauth of the Carnatic to the Marathas while retaining administrative control. These agreements were recorded in Persian and Marathi, sealed with the Peshwa’s signet, and enforced by Maratha cavalry stationed at strategic forts such as Pune, Satara, and Gwalior.

European powers entered Maratha diplomacy as both rivals and allies. The 1739 Treaty of Bassein with the Portuguese confirmed Maratha possession of Salsette and Bassein in exchange for a yearly payment, while the 1775 Treaty of Surat with the British recognised the Peshwa’s claim to the revenues of Surat and Broach. The 1782 Treaty of Salbai ended the First Anglo‑Maratha War, restoring the status quo ante bellum and guaranteeing Maratha control over territories north of the Narmada. The decisive 1802 Treaty of Bassein (with the British) turned the Peshwa into a subsidiary ally, allowing British troops to be stationed in Poona and effectively ending the confederacy’s independent foreign policy.

Internal confederacy diplomacy was equally critical. The Peshwa acted as primus inter pares among the great houses — Scindia of Gwalior, Holkar of Indore, Bhonsle of Nagpur, and Gaekwad of Baroda. The 1795 Treaty of Pune attempted to regulate the distribution of chauth among these chiefs, but rivalries over the control of the Mughal emperor (the “emperor‑making” policy) led to the 1795‑96 civil war between Scindia and Holkar. The Peshwa’s inability to enforce a unified diplomatic line weakened the confederacy’s bargaining position vis‑à‑vis the British, who exploited these divisions through the subsidiary alliance system.

By the 1818 Treaty of Poona, the Maratha confederacy was dissolved, its territories annexed or placed under princely states. The diplomatic corpus — over two hundred sanads, farmans, and correspondence — survives in the Peshwa Daftar at Pune and the British India Office Records. These documents illustrate a sophisticated diplomatic culture that combined Mughal ceremonial forms (khilat, nazar) with Maratha fiscal concepts (chauth, sardeshmukhi) and adapted to the treaty‑based international law introduced by European powers.

Vijayanagara's Overseas Connections: Trade and Cultural Exchange with Southeast Asia

Vijayanagara’s maritime outreach was anchored in a network of west‑coast ports — Bhatkal, Honavar, Mangalore, and the inland riverine emporium of Goa — that linked the empire’s agrarian surplus and textile production to the Indian Ocean trading system. The state maintained a modest navy (the “kadal‑pattam”) primarily for anti‑piracy patrols and for escorting merchant convoys to the Malabar coast. Revenue from port duties (sungam) and the royal monopoly on pepper and cardamom financed the navy and contributed to the imperial treasury, as recorded in the inscriptions of the Tuluva period.

Trade with Southeast Asia flourished from the late fifteenth to the mid‑sixteenth century. Vijayanagara exported cotton textiles (especially the famed “calico” from the Coromandel), indigo, and iron ore to ports such as Malacca, Aceh, and Ayutthaya. In return, the empire imported aromatic woods (sandalwood, eaglewood), Chinese ceramics, and Persian horses trans‑shipped through Malacca. The Portuguese capture of Goa in 1510 initially disrupted this circuit, but the Vijayanagara rulers negotiated a 1518 agreement allowing Portuguese ships to load pepper at Bhatkal in exchange for a fixed customs share, thereby preserving a degree of commercial autonomy.

Cultural exchange accompanied commerce. Sanskrit inscriptions dated to the reign of Krishnadevaraya have been found in Java and Bali, attesting to the movement of Brahmins and scribes. The architectural vocabulary of Vijayanagara’s temples — particularly the use of the gopura and the mandapa — influenced the temple complexes of the Majapahit successor states. Conversely, Southeast Asian motifs such as the kala‑makara doorway appear in the sculptural programme of the Vitthala temple at Hampi, suggesting a two‑way flow of artistic ideas mediated by merchant guilds (nanadesis) operating across the Bay of Bengal.

The Portuguese presence introduced new naval technology (carracks, lateen rigs) and cartographic knowledge that Vijayanagara’s shipwrights adopted for their own coastal vessels. However, the 1565 Battle of Talikota and the subsequent sack of Hampi severed the empire’s central coordination of maritime policy. The successor Nayaka states (Madurai, Tanjavur, Ikkeri) continued individual trade relations with the Portuguese and the Dutch, but the unified imperial naval strategy collapsed. By the early seventeenth century, the English and Dutch East India Companies had established factories at Masulipatnam and Pulicat, redirecting the bulk of Coromandel textile exports away from the traditional Vijayanagara‑controlled routes.

Despite the political fragmentation, the legacy of Vijayanagara’s overseas connections persisted in the continued use of the “Vijayanagara peso” (a gold coin standard) in the Malabar‑Southeast Asian trade well into the eighteenth century. The empire’s integration of agrarian revenue, port customs, and a state‑supported merchant marine represents an early example of a South Indian polity actively shaping Indian Ocean commerce rather than merely reacting to it.

Delhi Sultanate Urban Planning: The Foundation and Layout of Sultanate Capitals

The Delhi Sultanate’s capital cities were deliberately engineered to project sovereign authority, accommodate a growing military‑administrative elite, and control strategic trade routes. Qutb al‑Din Aibak (r. 1206‑10) established the first sultanate capital at Mehrauli, centred on the Qutb Minar complex. The layout followed a citadel‑mosque‑market triad: the fortified citadel (Qila‑i‑Kuhna) housed the royal palace and treasury; the Quwwat‑ul‑Islam mosque symbolised Islamic legitimacy; and the surrounding bazaar (the “chahar‑suq”) linked the city to the Grand Trunk Road, facilitating revenue collection from the Doab.

Alauddin Khalji (r. 1296‑1316) responded to Mongol pressure and population growth by founding Siri, a new fortified city northeast of Mehrauli. Siri’s plan incorporated a massive rectangular wall with seven gates, a central palace complex (the “Hazar Sutun”), and a dedicated garrison area for the standing army (the “lashkar”). The city’s water supply was secured by the Hauz‑i‑Alai reservoir, fed by a canal from the Yamuna, demonstrating an early integration of hydraulic engineering into urban design. The market reforms of Alauddin were enforced through the “shahna‑i‑mandi” (market superintendent) stationed in Siri’s main square.

Muhammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325‑51) undertook the most ambitious — and controversial — urban experiment by ordering the transfer of the capital to Daulatabad (Deogir) in the Deccan. The new capital, renamed “Tughluqabad”, was laid out on a grid with a massive stone fort, a congregational mosque, and a planned residential sector for the transplanted Delhi elite. The forced migration (the “Daulatabad experiment”) aimed to consolidate control over the newly conquered southern territories but resulted in massive demographic loss and economic disruption, leading to the capital’s return to Delhi within a decade.

Firuz Shah Tughluq (r. 1351‑88) shifted focus to public works and welfare architecture. He founded Firuzabad on the banks of the Yamuna, incorporating a network of canals (the “Yamuna‑Firuz” canal) that irrigated the surrounding countryside and supplied the city’s numerous gardens (bagh), hospitals (dar‑ul‑shifa), and madrasas. The city’s layout emphasized axial symmetry: the royal palace faced the Jami Masjid, while the “Kushk‑i‑Firuz” (pavilion) overlooked a large artificial lake. Firuz Shah’s building inscriptions claim the construction of 1,200 gardens, 200 towns, and 30 reservoirs, underscoring the sultanate’s capacity for large‑scale urban infrastructure.

The Lodi period (1451‑1526) saw a return to a more modest urban scale. Sikandar Lodi established Agra as a secondary capital, laying out the “Lodi Gardens” as a funerary complex that doubled as a public space. The Lodi sultans fortified the existing Delhi walls, added the “Bara‑Gumbad” and “Shish‑Gumbad” tombs, and reorganised the city’s wards (mohallas) around craft guilds. By the time Babur captured Delhi in 1526, the sultanate’s urban legacy — citadel‑mosque‑market complexes, hydraulic works, and planned residential wards — provided a template that the early Mughals adapted and expanded in their own capital projects at Agra, Fatehpur Sikri, and Shahjahanabad.

Mughal Military Logistics in the Deccan: Banjara Caravans, Supply Depots, and the Campaigns of Aurangzeb

The protracted Deccan campaigns of Aurangzeb (1681‑1707) exposed the limits of a centrally‑controlled supply chain. The Mughal army, often exceeding 200,000 combatants plus camp followers, depended on a hybrid logistics network that combined state‑run depots (faujdari‑khana) with the private enterprise of Banjara grain‑carriers. Banjaras, organised into tanda (caravan) units of 500‑1,000 bullock‑carts, contracted directly with the imperial commissariat (mir‑i‑saman) to move wheat, barley, and fodder from the fertile Doab and Malwa to forward bases at Burhanpur, Aurangabad, and Jinji. Each tanda carried a sealed manifest (parwana) specifying quantity, destination, and a fixed price (zabt‑rate) that insulated the state from market spikes.

State depots were positioned at strategic river crossings — notably at the Tapti near Burhanpur and the Godavari near Paithan — where grain was stored in mud‑brick silos (kothi) protected by a garrison of 200‑300 matchlockmen. The depots operated on a “first‑in‑first‑out” rotation to minimise spoilage, and their accounts were audited quarterly by the diwan‑i‑tan (revenue auditor). When the monsoon disrupted river transport, the Banjaras shifted to overland routes through the Satpura passes, a maneuver that added 15‑20 days to delivery but kept the army fed during the 1689‑90 siege of Bijapur.

The system’s fragility lay in its reliance on a single commodity corridor. Maratha light cavalry (bargirs) repeatedly intercepted Banjara convoys, forcing the Mughals to allocate 10‑15 percent of their cavalry to escort duty. Aurangzeb’s 1695 order to fortify the “Banjara Road” with a series of watch‑towers (burj) and to impose a toll (rahdari) on all non‑imperial traffic reduced losses by an estimated one‑third, yet the overall cost of logistics consumed nearly 40 percent of the Deccan expedition’s annual budget, contributing to the fiscal strain that precipitated the jagirdari crisis of the 1700s.

The Maratha Dak System: Postal Relays, Intelligence Gathering, and Administrative Communication

Shivaji’s creation of a dedicated postal network (dak) in the 1660s gave the Maratha state a communication speed unmatched by contemporary Indian powers. The system rested on a chain of relay stations (dak‑chowki) spaced roughly 12‑15 km apart along the main arteries linking Raigad, Pune, Satara, and the coastal forts of Kolaba and Suvarnadurg. Each chowki maintained a complement of two mounted couriers (dak‑sawar) and a foot‑messenger (paik) who could traverse the ghats during monsoon when horses were unusable. Messages were written on palm‑leaf folios (patra) sealed with the royal mudra, and a time‑stamp (ghadi) recorded at every handover, allowing the Peshwa’s secretariat to calculate transit times with an accuracy of ±30 minutes over a 300 km stretch.

Beyond official orders, the dak carried intelligence reports (khabar) from village headmen (patil) and spies (gupte) embedded in Mughal and Bijapur camps. The Peshwa’s “Khabar‑Navis” office at Pune compiled these into daily digests (roz‑nama) that informed strategic decisions such as the timing of the 1674 raid on Surat. The network also facilitated revenue administration: assessment registers (mali‑patra) travelled from the district (pargana) to the central treasury (raj‑khazana) within a fortnight, enabling the swift adjustment of chauth demands.

Under the Peshwas (1713‑1761) the dak was professionalised. A salaried cadre of “dak‑adhyaksha” oversaw station masters, standardized the breed of Maratha horses (the “Deccani” stallion), and introduced a coded cipher (sanket) for sensitive diplomatic correspondence with the Nizam and the English at Bombay. The system’s reach extended to the Carnatic, where Maratha agents at Tanjore and Madurai used the same relay logic to coordinate the 1740‑41 campaigns. By the 1750s the dak comprised over 1,200 stations, a density that allowed a message from Pune to reach Delhi in 12‑14 days — a speed the Mughal imperial post (barid) could not match after the 1739 Nadir Shah invasion disrupted its northern routes.

Technique: Comparative Speed Analysis
A question asks: “Compare the average transmission speed of the Maratha dak (c. 1750) with the Mughal barid (c. 1650) for a 1,200 km distance. Which system was faster and by how many days?”
The Maratha dak covered ~1,200 km in 12‑14 days (average 13 days). The Mughal barid, using horse‑relay stations every 8‑10 km, averaged 20‑22 days for the same distance in the mid‑17th century. Therefore the Maratha dak was faster by roughly 7‑9 days. The answer should cite the relay spacing, the use of foot‑messengers in ghats, and the coded cipher that reduced handling time.

Ahom‑Mughal Diplomatic Encounters: The Treaty of Ghilajharighat and Its Aftermath

The Ahom kingdom’s resistance to Mughal expansion in the Brahmaputra valley culminated in the 1663 Treaty of Ghilajharighat, negotiated between Ahom king Jayadhwaj Singha and Mughal governor Mir Jumla. The treaty obliged the Ahoms to cede the western territories up to the Barnadi River, pay an indemnity of 300,000 rupees and 20 elephants, and accept a Mughal thana (garrison) at Guwahati. Crucially, the Ahoms retained internal sovereignty, their own paiks (militia) and the hereditary office of the Borphukan, which the Mughals recognised as a “zamindari‑type” authority.

Mir Jumla’s subsequent death (1663) and the Mughal preoccupation with the Deccan wars allowed the Ahoms to regroup. Under king Chakradhwaj Singha (r. 1663‑1670) the Ahoms launched a counter‑offensive, recapturing Guwahati in 1667 and expelling the Mughal thana. The Mughal response, led by Raja Ram Singh, ended in the decisive Ahom victory at Saraighat (1671), where the Ahom navy — a fleet of 2,000 war‑boats (bachari) armed with swivel‑guns — exploited the river’s narrow channels to neutralise Mughal artillery.

The diplomatic fallout reshaped Mughal frontier policy. Emperor Aurangzeb, recognising the impossibility of a permanent garrison in the malaria‑prone valley, shifted to a “tribute‑only” model: the Ahoms sent annual presents (nazrana) of gold, musk, and elephants to Delhi, while the Mughals refrained from stationing troops east of the Barnadi. This arrangement persisted until the 1760s, when the Burmese invasions forced the Ahoms to seek British assistance, ending the unique Mughal‑Ahom diplomatic equilibrium.

Mughal Bengal Administration: The Diwan‑i‑Bengal, Zamindari Settlements, and Revenue Farming

Bengal’s integration into the Mughal fiscal apparatus after Akbar’s 1576 conquest created a distinctive administrative template that blended the imperial diwan’s oversight with the pre‑existing zamindari hierarchy. The Diwan‑i‑Bengal, headquartered at Dhaka (later Murshidabad), supervised a two‑tier revenue collection: the “zabt” cash assessment on directly‑managed khalisa lands, and the “ijara” (revenue‑farming) contracts granted to zamindars and taluqdars for the remaining parganas. Zamindars, often former Afghan or Bengali chieftains, received a sanad (patent) confirming hereditary rights in return for a fixed annual peshkash (tribute) calibrated to the Ain‑i‑Akbari’s dahsala averages.

The revenue‑farming system incentivised zamindars to expand cultivation; they advanced seed‑loans (taqavi) to peasants (raiyats) and constructed irrigation bunds (bandh) that increased the measured area (bigha) by up to 15 percent in the 1620s. However, the system also generated friction: the imperial diwan periodically revised the peshkash upward (the “re‑assessment” of 1658‑59 under Shah Shuja), prompting zamindar revolts such as the 1660 uprising of the Bara Bhuiyans. The Mughal response combined military suppression with the appointment of loyal faujdars who oversaw the collection of the “mal‑guzari” (land‑tax) directly from villages, bypassing recalcitrant intermediaries.

By the late 17th century, Bengal’s revenue yield — estimated at 12‑13 million rupees annually — financed a significant portion of the imperial army’s Deccan expenditures. The province’s monetary surplus also attracted European factories; the English East India Company’s 1698 farman granted them duty‑free trade in return for a fixed annual payment of 3,000 rupees, a precedent that later underpinned the 1765 Diwani grant. The administrative continuity from Akbar’s zabt to the Nawabi “mal‑guzari” illustrates how Mughal fiscal institutions adapted to regional agrarian structures while preserving central revenue claims.

The Mughal Imperial Household Treasury: The Khazana, the Diwan‑i‑Khas, and the Mir Bakhshi’s Fiscal Role

Beyond the public exchequer (khazana‑i‑amm), the Mughal emperors maintained a private treasury (khazana‑i‑khas) that financed the imperial household, the harem, the royal workshops (karkhana), and personal military retinues (ahadis). The Diwan‑i‑Khas, a senior finance minister distinct from the Diwan‑i‑Aam (revenue minister), administered this private purse. He received daily cash inflows from the “sair‑jihat” (customs on luxury imports), the “zar‑i‑zamin” (gold‑mine royalties from Kolar and Hutti), and the “nazrana” (tribute) presented by provincial governors on accession and festivals.

The Mir Bakhshi, traditionally the head of the military establishment (mir‑i‑arzi), also exercised fiscal authority over the household cavalry (ahadis) and the imperial guard (khass‑daran). His office audited the pay‑rolls (tankhwa) of these troops, which were drawn from the khazana‑i‑khas rather than the jagirdari assignments. This dual control created a checks‑and‑balances mechanism: the Diwan‑i‑Khas could not disburse funds without the Mir Bakhshi’s certification of troop strength, while the Mir Bakhshi could not recruit beyond the budget sanctioned by the Diwan‑i‑Khas.

Under Jahangir (1605‑27) the khazana‑i‑khas financed the construction of the Shalimar Gardens and the patronage of European painters, while Shah Jahan (1628‑58) expanded it to fund the Taj Mahal’s marble procurement from Makrana. Aurangzeb, however, curtailed household expenditure after 1680, redirecting surplus to the Deccan war chest; the Diwan‑i‑Khas’s annual accounts (bahi‑khata) show a 30 percent decline in “karkhana” outlays between 1680 and 1700. The household treasury thus functioned as a flexible fiscal reservoir, enabling emperors to bypass the rigid jagirdari system for prestige projects and emergency military needs.
