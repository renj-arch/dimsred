1. The Delhi Sultanate: Foundations of Muslim Rule in North India

1.1 Emergence of the First Dynasty – the Mamluk (Slave) Dynasty

The Delhi Sultanate began in the early thirteenth century when Qutb‑ud‑dîn Aibak, a former slave of Muhammad Ghori, established his authority over the territories captured from the Rajput confederacies. Aibak’s accession in 1206 marked the start of the Mamluk dynasty, which introduced a centralized administration centred on Delhi. The Sultanate’s early expansion was driven by the need to secure trade routes linking Central Asia with the Indian subcontinent and to extract tribute from the agrarian hinterland. The construction of the Qutb Minar complex symbolised both religious legitimacy and the assertion of political power.

1.2 The Khalji Expansion and Economic Reforms

In 1290, Jalāl‑ud‑dīn Khaljī seized the throne, inaugurating the Khalji dynasty. Khalji’s most notable ruler, Alauddin Khalji (r. 1296‑1316), pursued a policy of aggressive territorial expansion into the Deccan and Gujarat, thereby gaining control over the lucrative spice and textile trade. To finance these campaigns, Alauddin instituted market control measures, fixing prices of essential commodities and imposing strict grain procurement systems. These reforms curtailed the power of local merchants and increased state revenue, but also generated resentment among the agrarian classes, laying the groundwork for later rebellions.

1.3 The Tughluq Consolidation and Administrative Innovations

The Tughluq dynasty (1320‑1414) sought to consolidate the Sultanate’s vast territories through a series of administrative experiments. Muhammad bin Tughluq, known for his ambitious but often impractical policies, attempted to shift the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad in the Deccan to achieve a more central location. Although the relocation was reversed, the episode highlighted the challenges of governing a geographically dispersed empire. The Tughluqs also introduced a token currency system and a land revenue assessment based on the “iqta” system, which allocated land revenues to military officers in exchange for service.

1.4 The Sayyid Interregnum and the Rise of Regional Powers

Following the death of Firuz Shah Tughluq, the Sayyid dynasty (1414‑1451) ruled a weakened Sultanate, marked by internal factionalism and the resurgence of regional kingdoms such as the Bengal Sultanate and the Rajput states of Mewar and Marwar. The Sayyids were unable to restore central authority, and their reliance on mercenary forces eroded the Sultanate’s fiscal base. This period witnessed the gradual decentralisation of power, setting the stage for the emergence of the Lodi dynasty.

1.5 The Lodi Dynasty and the End of the Sultanate

The Lodi dynasty, founded by Bahlul Khan Lodi in 1451, represented the last Afghan‑ruled phase of the Delhi Sultanate. The Lodi rulers attempted to revive central authority through military reforms and alliances with Rajput chieftains. However, internal dissent and the inability to adapt to the evolving gunpowder technology of the emerging Mughal forces culminated in the decisive defeat of Ibrahim Lodi at the Battle of Panipat in 1526, which marked the termination of the Sultanate and the beginning of Mughal dominance.

2. Vijayanagara Empire: A Southern Counter‑Balance to the Sultanates

2.1 Founding and Ideological Foundations

In 1336, the brothers Harihara I and Bukka Raya I established the Vijayanagara Empire in the Deccan plateau, motivated by the need to provide a Hindu political centre that could resist the incursions of the northern Sultanates. The empire’s legitimacy rested on the concept of “dharmic kingship,” which combined Shaivite and Vaishnavite patronage, and on the strategic location of its capital on the banks of the Tungabhadra River, which facilitated control over the fertile Krishna basin and the lucrative spice routes to the Arabian Sea.

2.2 Territorial Expansion under the Sangama and Saluva Dynasties

The early Sangama rulers expanded the empire southwards, annexing the coastal regions of present‑day Karnataka and Kerala, thereby gaining access to the maritime trade in pepper and sandalwood. The Saluva dynasty (late 15th century) continued this expansion, but also faced repeated attacks from the Bahmani Sultanate, leading to a series of fortified frontier towns (garh) that served both defensive and administrative purposes.

2.3 The Golden Age of the Tuluva Dynasty

The reign of Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509‑1529) is regarded as the zenith of Vijayanagara’s political and cultural achievements. Under his rule, the empire extended from the Narmada River in the north to the Kaveri delta in the south. Krishnadevaraya’s patronage of literature produced the celebrated “Madhuravani” and “Amuktamalyada,” while his military campaigns against the Gajapati kingdom of Odisha and the Sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda secured the empire’s borders. The capital’s monumental architecture, exemplified by the Hazara Rama Temple and the Lotus Mahal, reflected a synthesis of Dravidian and Islamic styles, indicating a pragmatic approach to cultural integration.

2.4 Decline and the Aftermath of the Battle of Talikota

The decisive defeat of Vijayanagara at the Battle of Talikota in 1565, fought against a coalition of Deccan Sultanates, led to the sacking of the capital and the fragmentation of the empire into smaller successor states. Although remnants of the royal family continued to rule in peripheral regions, the loss of central authority marked the end of Vijayanagara as a dominant power, creating a power vacuum that would later be filled by the Marathas and the Mysore kingdom.

3. The Mughal Empire: From Babur’s Conquest to Aurangzeb’s Decline

3.1 Babur’s Invasion and the Establishment of Mughal Authority

Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia, entered the Indian subcontinent in 1526, capitalising on the disunity among the remnants of the Delhi Sultanate. His victory at the First Battle of Panipat, achieved through the effective use of field artillery and superior cavalry tactics, laid the foundation for Mughal rule. Babur’s memoir, the “Baburnama,” provides a vivid account of the early challenges of consolidating power over a culturally diverse landscape.

3.2 Akbar’s Administrative Synthesis and Religious Policy

Akbar (r. 1556‑1605) transformed the Mughal Empire into a centrally administered state by introducing the “mansabdari” system, which graded nobles based on the number of cavalry they maintained. This system created a professional bureaucracy that linked revenue collection directly to military obligations. Akbar’s policy of “Sulh‑i‑Kul” (universal tolerance) sought to integrate Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and other communities into the imperial framework, exemplified by the establishment of the “Ibadat Khana” for inter‑religious dialogue. These measures fostered political stability and facilitated the empire’s expansion into Gujarat, Bengal, and the Deccan.

3.3 Shah Jahan’s Architectural Patronage and Economic Prosperity

Shah Jahan (r. 1628‑1658) presided over a period of economic prosperity, driven by the flourishing of textile exports, especially cotton and silk, and the expansion of maritime trade through the ports of Surat and Masulipatnam. His reign is renowned for monumental architecture, most famously the Taj Mahal, which symbolised the empire’s wealth and artistic synthesis of Persian, Islamic, and Indian motifs. The construction of grand gardens and public works also reflected the centralised fiscal capacity of the state.

3.4 Aurangzeb’s Expansionist Policies and the Seeds of Decline

Aurangzeb (r. 1658‑1707) pursued an aggressive policy of territorial expansion, extending Mughal control to the frontiers of the Indian subcontinent, including the annexation of the Deccan Sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda. However, his orthodox Islamic policies, such as the re‑imposition of the jizya tax on non‑Muslims and the destruction of several Hindu temples, alienated large sections of the population. The prolonged military campaigns in the Deccan strained the imperial treasury, while the rise of regional powers like the Marathas and the Sikhs began to challenge Mughal authority, setting the stage for the empire’s fragmentation after Aurangzeb’s death.

4. Regional Kingdoms and the Fragmentation of Central Power

4.1 The Rise of the Bengal Sultanate and Its Maritime Trade

Following the decline of the Delhi Sultanate, the Bengal region witnessed the emergence of an independent Sultanate in the early fourteenth century. Centered at Gauda and later at Pandua, the Bengal Sultanate capitalised on the fertile deltaic plains and the strategic position along the Bay of Bengal to develop a thriving maritime trade network with Southeast Asia and the Middle East. The Sultanate’s emphasis on shipbuilding and the export of muslin and rice contributed to its economic autonomy, while its patronage of Bengali literature fostered a distinct cultural identity.

4.2 The Ahom Kingdom in the Northeast

The Ahom kingdom, founded in the early thirteenth century in present‑day Assam, demonstrated remarkable resilience against external invasions, notably repelling Mughal attempts to annex the region in the seventeenth century. The Ahoms employed a sophisticated administrative system known as the “Paik” system, which organised labour and military service on a rotational basis. Their control over the Brahmaputra valley’s rice production ensured food security, while diplomatic marriages with neighbouring hill tribes facilitated political stability.

4.3 The Rajput Confederacy and Its Relations with the Mughals

Rajput states such as Mewar, Marwar, and Amber maintained a complex relationship with the Mughal Empire, oscillating between open rebellion and strategic alliance. The policy of “Mansab‑dar” appointments for Rajput nobles allowed the Mughals to co‑opt local elites, while the Rajputs retained a degree of autonomy in internal affairs. Notable episodes include the resistance of Maharana Pratap of Mewar against Akbar’s expansion and the later alliance of Jai Singh II of Amber with Shah Jahan, which exemplified the pragmatic diplomacy of the period.

4.4 The Emergence of the Sikh Misls in Punjab

In the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, the Sikh community, initially a religious movement, evolved into a militarised confederacy known as the “Misls.” These semi‑autonomous units, each led by a chief (Sardar), controlled distinct territories in the Punjab plains. The Misls engaged in frequent skirmishes with Mughal forces and later with Afghan invaders, gradually consolidating power and laying the groundwork for the later Sikh Empire under Maharaja Ranjit Singh.

5. The Rise of the Marathas: From a Regional Power to a Pan‑Indian Force

5.1 Shivaji’s Foundational Vision and Guerrilla Warfare

Shivaji Bhonsle, born in 1630, founded the Maratha kingdom by exploiting the weaknesses of the waning Mughal authority in the Deccan. He pioneered the use of guerrilla tactics—rapid cavalry raids, fortified hill forts (gad), and a network of intelligence informants—to challenge larger Mughal armies. Shivaji’s coronation in 1674 as “Chhatrapati” established a sovereign Hindu polity that emphasized administrative reforms such as the “Ashta Pradhan” council, which distributed executive responsibilities among eight ministers.

5.2 Expansion under the Peshwa Leadership

After Shivaji’s death, the Maratha state experienced periods of internal strife, but the appointment of the Peshwa (prime minister) as the de‑facto leader in the early eighteenth century revitalised expansionist ambitions. The Peshwas, particularly Balaji Vishwanath and his son Bajirao I, extended Maratha influence northwards into Gujarat, Malwa, and the Bundelkhand region through a combination of diplomatic treaties (e.g., the “Treaty of Surat”) and military campaigns. Their strategy of demanding “chauth” (a quarter of the revenue) from conquered territories created a tributary system that financed further conquests.

5.3 The Maratha Confederacy and Its Administrative Structure

By the mid‑eighteenth century, the Maratha empire had evolved into a confederacy comprising semi‑autonomous chiefs such as the Holkars of Indore, the Scindhias of Gwalior, and the Bhonsles of Nagpur. Each chief maintained his own army and collected revenue, but they owed allegiance to the Peshwa in Pune, who coordinated joint military operations. This decentralised structure allowed rapid mobilisation of forces but also sowed seeds of internal rivalry that would later be exploited by the British.

5.4 The Third Battle of Panipat and Its Aftermath

The decisive encounter between the Marathas and Ahmad Shah Durrani’s Afghan forces at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761 resulted in a catastrophic loss for the Marathas, halting their northward expansion. The defeat weakened the confederacy’s military capacity and exposed the vulnerability of its fragmented command structure. Nevertheless, the Marathas recovered under the leadership of Madhavrao I, who reinstated fiscal reforms and re‑asserted control over key regions, demonstrating the resilience of the Maratha political system.

Example Question

Discuss how the administrative innovations of the Mughal Empire under Akbar differed from those of the Delhi Sultanate, and evaluate the impact of these differences on the empire’s ability to govern a diverse subcontinent.

Solution: Akbar introduced the mansabdari system, which graded nobles by the number of cavalry they maintained, creating a merit‑based hierarchy that linked military service directly to revenue assignments. In contrast, the Delhi Sultanate relied on the iqta system, where land grants were given to officers without a standardized assessment of their military capacity. Akbar’s system facilitated a more efficient and centrally controlled bureaucracy, enabling better tax collection and troop mobilisation across vast territories. Moreover, Akbar’s policy of Sulh‑i‑Kul promoted religious tolerance, integrating Hindus and other communities into the administrative framework, whereas the Sultanate’s governance was more overtly based on Sharia law, limiting broader participation. These innovations allowed the Mughal Empire to sustain a larger, more diverse population, maintain fiscal stability, and project power far beyond the reach of the earlier Sultanate.

Exam‑Day Insight

When answering a question on the rise of the Marathas, begin by linking Shivaji’s guerrilla tactics to the geographical advantage of the Western Ghats, then trace how the Peshwa’s fiscal policy of “chauth” transformed a regional power into a pan‑Indian confederacy; this logical progression demonstrates both cause and consequence, which is rewarded in answer‑writing.

6. Synthesis: Continuities and Transformations from the Sultanate to the Marathas

6.1 Patterns of Centralisation and Decentralisation

Across the medieval period, Indian polities oscillated between centralised authority and fragmented regional governance. The Delhi Sultanate’s early centralisation gave way to the decentralised rule of the Sayyid and Lodi dynasties, a pattern echoed in the Mughal era where strong emperors like Akbar and Shah Jahan were followed by the weakened authority of Aurangzeb’s successors. The Maratha confederacy epitomised a hybrid model: a central figure (the Peshwa) coordinated a network of semi‑autonomous chiefs, mirroring earlier Sultanate practices of delegating authority to provincial governors while retaining overall suzerainty.

6.2 Economic Foundations and Trade Networks

Economic vitality consistently underpinned political power. The Sultanate’s control of the Indo‑Gangetic plain facilitated grain surplus and tax revenue, while the Vijayanagara Empire leveraged coastal trade in spices and textiles. The Mughal Empire expanded this model by integrating maritime commerce through ports like Surat, creating a fiscal base that financed monumental architecture and a standing army. The Marathas, lacking a comparable maritime network, relied on tribute (chauth) and agricultural revenue from the Deccan plateau, illustrating a shift from trade‑based wealth to tribute‑based extraction.

6.3 Cultural Syncretism and Religious Policies

Religious tolerance and cultural patronage varied according to the ruler’s objectives. The early Sultanate imposed Islamic law but accommodated local customs to maintain order. Akbar’s Sulh‑i‑Kul represented an ideological commitment to inclusivity, fostering a composite culture that blended Persian, Central Asian, and Indian elements. In contrast, Aurangzeb’s orthodoxy reversed this trend, contributing to dissent. The Marathas, while championing Hindu identity, also employed Muslim administrators and adopted Persian administrative terminology, reflecting a pragmatic syncretism that ensured effective governance.

6.4 Military Evolution and Technological Adoption

From the cavalry‑centric armies of the early Sultanate to the gunpowder artillery of the Mughals, military technology shaped the trajectory of Indian states. The Marathas, initially reliant on light cavalry and guerrilla tactics, later incorporated European firearms and artillery, learned from both Mughal and European mercenaries. This adaptation allowed them to challenge both the declining Mughal forces and the emerging British East India Company, underscoring the importance of technological flexibility in sustaining imperial ambitions.

6.5 Legacy for Early Modern India

The medieval period set the structural foundations for early modern India. The administrative practices, revenue systems, and cultural syntheses developed under the Sultanate and Mughal empires were inherited, modified, or resisted by later powers such as the Marathas, Sikhs, and eventually the British. Understanding these continuities and transformations equips students to analyse the complex tapestry of Indian history and to appreciate how regional dynamics interacted with imperial ambitions to shape the subcontinent’s political landscape.

The Iqta System: Evolution, Assessment, and Limits under the Delhi Sultanate

The iqta was the principal instrument through which the Delhi Sultanate translated military service into revenue rights. Under Iltutmish the grant was essentially a temporary assignment of land revenue to a commander (muqti) who collected the produce, maintained a contingent of cavalry, and forwarded a surplus to the treasury. Balban tightened the system by insisting on regular audits (mahsul) and by limiting the size of iqtas to prevent the emergence of autonomous magnates. Alauddin Khalji introduced the most far‑reaching reforms: he ordered a comprehensive measurement (paimaish) of cultivated land, fixed the state demand at half the gross produce (kharaj), and required the muqti to deposit the assessed revenue in cash rather than in kind. This shift to cash collection reduced the muqti’s discretion and enabled the sultan to finance a standing army paid in silver tankas.

Despite these measures, the iqta retained structural weaknesses. The grant remained non‑hereditary in theory, yet in practice powerful muqtis such as the Tughluq nobles managed to transmit their assignments to sons, eroding central oversight. The system also lacked a uniform cadastral record; local revenue officials (amils) often relied on outdated estimates, leading to frequent disputes between the state and the assignee. Moreover, the iqta did not create a bureaucratic cadre loyal to the crown; loyalty was personal to the sultan, and a change of ruler could trigger wholesale reassignment, causing administrative discontinuity. The Lodi period saw a further dilution as iqtas were increasingly granted to Afghan tribal chiefs on a hereditary basis, accelerating the fragmentation that culminated in the Sultanate’s collapse.

Key Features of the Reformed Iqta under Alauddin Khalji

  • Land measurement (paimaish) conducted by royal surveyors.
  • State demand fixed at 50 % of gross produce (kharaj).
  • Revenue collected in cash (tanka) and deposited in the central treasury.
  • Muqti’s military obligation quantified: a fixed number of horsemen per iqta.
  • Regular audits (mahsul) and penalties for shortfalls.

Mansabdari Reforms: Zat, Sawar, and the Fiscal‑Military Nexus under Akbar and Successors

Akbar’s mansabdari system replaced the loose iqta framework with a graded hierarchy that linked rank (zat), cavalry obligation (sawar), and salary (jagir) in a single numerical scale. Every noble received a mansab expressed as two numbers: the zat indicated personal status and salary entitlement, while the sawar specified the number of horsemen the holder must maintain. The emperor retained the power to revise both numbers, ensuring that military capacity remained under central calibration. Salaries were paid through revenue assignments (jagirs) that were theoretically transferable every few years, preventing the entrenchment of local power bases.

Under Jahangir the system acquired a dual‑track character: the introduction of the “du‑aspah sih‑aspah” (two‑horse three‑horse) distinction allowed a noble to hold a higher sawar than his zat, rewarding meritorious service without inflating his personal rank. Shah Jahan further rationalised the revenue side by standardising the jagir assessment (jama) against the empire’s measured produce (dastur‑ul‑amal), and by insisting that jagirdars submit annual accounts (waqia‑navis) to the diwan. Aurangzeb’s long reign saw a proliferation of mansabs — over 1,500 at its peak — and a growing gap between the nominal sawar and the actual cavalry maintained, as many nobles commuted their obligation into cash payments. This fiscal‑military disconnect weakened the empire’s field strength and contributed to the reliance on mercenary contingents in the Deccan campaigns.

Vijayanagara’s Nayankara System: Provincial Governance and Revenue Extraction

The Nayankara (or amara‑nayaka) arrangement was the backbone of Vijayanagara’s territorial administration from the Sangama period onward. The emperor granted large tracts (amaram) to military governors (nayakas) in return for a fixed annual tribute (kavali) and a stipulated contingent of troops for imperial campaigns. Unlike the iqta, the amaram was often hereditary within a nayaka family, provided the tribute was paid punctually. The nayaka exercised judicial, fiscal, and military authority within his domain, maintaining a personal retinue and fortifying strategic hill‑top strongholds (durga).

Revenue collection under the Nayankara operated on a dual layer: the nayaka assessed the produce of his amaram using local measurement units (khanduga, putti) and forwarded the agreed tribute to the central treasury at Hampi. The empire supplemented this with a cadre of royal agents (mahamandaleshvara) who audited the nayaka’s accounts and ensured that the kavali reflected current agricultural output. In periods of strong central rule — notably under Krishnadevaraya — the audit was rigorous, and defaulting nayakas faced confiscation or military reduction. However, after the Battle of Talikota (1565) the central audit collapsed, and many nayakas asserted de facto independence, evolving into the later Nayaka kingdoms of Madurai, Tanjavur, and Gingee.

Distinctive Features of the Nayankara

  • Hereditary amaram grants conditional on tribute compliance.
  • Nayaka’s military obligation quantified in elephants, cavalry, and infantry.
  • Local revenue assessment using regional units, not a uniform imperial standard.
  • Royal auditors (mahamandaleshvara) with powers of inspection and penalty.
  • Integration of temple endowments (devadana) into nayaka’s fiscal base.

Maratha Fiscal Instruments: Chauth, Sardeshmukhi, and the Confederacy’s Revenue Architecture

The Maratha confederacy’s financial engine rested on two levies extracted from territories outside their direct administration. Chauth (one‑fourth of the assessed revenue) was claimed as a protective tribute from Mughal or Deccan Sultanate provinces; in return the Marathas undertook not to raid the paying region. Sardeshmukhi (an additional ten per cent) represented the Maratha claim to the hereditary “sardeshmukh” right — a superior zamindari status over the same lands. Both levies were collected by the Peshwa’s agents (karkuns) who maintained detailed registers (daftar) of assessed villages, crop yields, and payment schedules.

Under Balaji Vishwanath and Baji Rao I the collection machinery was professionalised: the Peshwa appointed a “chauth‑sardeshmukhi” department headed by a senior Brahmin official (the “chauth‑peshwa”) who coordinated with regional commanders (sardars) such as the Holkar, Scindia, and Bhonsle houses. The revenue was remitted to the central treasury at Pune, where it funded the standing artillery corps, the navy at Kolaba, and the diplomatic missions to European powers. The system’s flexibility allowed the Marathas to expand their fiscal frontier without permanent annexation; however, it also created friction with local zamindars and Mughal subahdars, who frequently contested the legitimacy of the levies, leading to periodic military confrontations.

Operational Flow of Chauth Collection

  1. Survey of target pargana by Maratha revenue surveyors (potdars).
  2. Assessment of standard revenue (jama) based on Mughal dastur‑ul‑amal.
  3. Calculation of chauth (25 % of jama) and sardeshmukhi (10 % of jama).
  4. Issuance of sanad (grant) to local headman acknowledging Maratha suzerainty.
  5. Quarterly collection by karkuns; receipts recorded in daftar.
  6. Remittance to Pune treasury; audit by the chauth‑peshwa.

Deccan Sultanates: Military Organization, Gunpowder Adoption, and Diplomatic Strategies

The five successor states of the Bahmani kingdom — Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Bidar, and Berar — developed a distinctive military culture that blended Persianate cavalry traditions with indigenous infantry and an early embrace of gunpowder artillery. Each sultanate maintained a core of elite cavalry (bargirs) recruited from Persian, Turkic, and African (Habshi) groups, supplemented by locally raised foot soldiers (paiks) armed with matchlocks (toradar). The decisive factor in their sieges was the deployment of heavy bronze cannon (malik‑i‑maidan) cast at foundries in Bijapur and Golconda; the famous “Malik‑i‑Maidan” at Bijapur, weighing over 55 tonnes, exemplifies the scale of ordnance.

Diplomatically, the Deccan sultanates pursued a balance‑of‑power strategy: they alternated alliances with the Mughal Empire, the Portuguese at Goa, and the Vijayanagara Empire to prevent any single hegemon from dominating the plateau. Treaties often involved the cession of strategic forts (e.g., the 1636 Mughal‑Bijapur treaty ceding the fort of Parenda) in exchange for recognition of sovereignty over contiguous territories. The sultanates also cultivated Sufi shrines as channels of legitimacy, granting land endowments (inam) to prominent dargahs to secure popular support. By the late seventeenth century, however, sustained Mughal pressure under Aurangzeb — combined with internal succession disputes — eroded their military capacity, culminating in the annexation of Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687).
