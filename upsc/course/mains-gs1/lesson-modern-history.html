Key Concepts

Advent of Europeans and Consolidation of British Power

European arrival in India began with Vasco da Gama (1498, Portuguese). The Portuguese established Goa (1510), followed by the Dutch, French, Danes, and finally the British East India Company (EIC), which received a Royal Charter in 1600 and established its first factory at Surat (1608).

  • Battle of Plassey (1757): Robert Clive defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah with Mir Jafar's betrayal — marked the beginning of British political power.
  • Battle of Buxar (1764): Company defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah, and Shah Alam II — granted Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa (1765).
  • Dual Government (1765–72): Company had Diwani (revenue) but Nawab had Nizamat (administration) — led to administrative paralysis and exploitation.
  • Wars: Three Anglo-Mysore Wars (Haider Ali, Tipu Sultan), three Anglo-Maratha Wars, Anglo-Sikh Wars, Anglo-Nepal War, Anglo-Burmese Wars, Anglo-Afghan Wars.
  • Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie): Annexed Satara (1848), Sambalpur (1849), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854), Awadh (1856 on grounds of misrule).

Company Rule: Administration and Policies

  • Regulating Act 1773: First parliamentary control; Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings); Supreme Court at Calcutta.
  • Pitt's India Act 1784: Dual control — Board of Control (political) and Court of Directors (commercial).
  • Charter Acts 1813, 1833, 1853: Ended trade monopoly (except tea), centralised legislation, introduced open competition for civil services, and began the Legislative Council.
  • Land revenue: Permanent Settlement (1793, Cornwallis — Bengal, Bihar, Orissa), Ryotwari (Madras, Bombay — direct with cultivators), Mahalwari (North-Western Provinces — village-based).
  • Commercial policies: De-industrialisation, drain of wealth (Dadabhai Naoroji's theory), forced commercialisation of crops (indigo, opium, cotton).

Socio-Religious Reform Movements

  • Brahmo Samaj (1828, Raja Ram Mohan Roy): Abolition of sati (1829), monotheism, opposition to idol worship and caste.
  • Arya Samaj (1875, Dayananda Saraswati): "Back to the Vedas," shuddhi (reconversion), opposition to cow slaughter and untouchability.
  • Prarthana Samaj (1867, Atmaram Pandurang): Western India, social reform (widow remarriage, women's education).
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897, Swami Vivekananda): Service to humanity, Vedanta, Chicago Parliament of Religions (1893).
  • Theosophical Society (1875, H.P. Blavatsky and H.S. Olcott; Annie Besant later): Adyar headquarters, revival of ancient religions.
  • Aligarh Movement (Sir Syed Ahmad Khan): Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College (1875), modern education for Muslims, loyal to British initially.
  • Singh Sabha (1873, Amritsar): Sikh reform, Gurdwara reforms, Punjabi language.
  • Satya Shodhak Samaj (Jyotiba Phule): Anti-caste, education for lower castes and women, Satyashodhak marriage.
  • Self-Respect Movement (E.V. Ramasamy 'Periyar'): Anti-Brahminism, rationalism, women's rights, Tamil pride.

Revolt of 1857

  • Causes: Political (Doctrine of Lapse, annexation of Awadh), economic (land revenue, de-industrialisation), military (greased cartridges, disparity in pay/promotion), social (interference in customs, Christian missionary activity), and immediate (Enfield rifle cartridges, March 1857, Meerut outbreak May 10).
  • Centres and leaders: Delhi (Bahadur Shah II), Kanpur (Nana Sahib, Tantia Tope), Lucknow (Begum Hazrat Mahal), Jhansi (Rani Lakshmibai), Bihar (Kunwar Singh), Faizabad (Maulvi Ahmadullah).
  • Suppression: Delhi recaptured (September 1857), Lucknow (Campbell), Jhansi (Hughes), Kunwar Singh defeated.
  • Nature: Debated — sepoy mutiny (British view), first war of independence (Savarkar, nationalist view), or a combination of grievances without unified nationalism.
  • Consequences: End of Company rule (1858), Crown takeover, Queen's Proclamation (1858), end of Doctrine of Lapse, reorganisation of army, rise of racial antagonism.

Indian National Movement — Moderate Phase (1885–1905)

  • Indian National Congress (1885): Founded by A.O. Hume; first session Bombay (W.C. Bonnerjee, president). Early leaders — Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjee, Dinshaw Wacha.
  • Methods: Petitions, prayers, protests, constitutional agitation, educating public opinion.
  • Economic critique: Dadabhai Naoroji's "Poverty and Un-British Rule in India" (drain of wealth), R.C. Dutt's economic history.
  • Demands: Indianisation of services, separation of judiciary from executive, reduction of military expenditure, civil rights, legislative councils with elected members.

Extremist Phase and Swadeshi (1905–1918)

  • Partition of Bengal (1905, Lord Curzon): Divided Bengal into East Bengal and Assam (Muslim-majority) and West Bengal (Hindu-majority) — seen as "divide and rule."
  • Swadeshi and Boycott: Boycott of British goods, promotion of Indian industries (Bengal Chemical, Tata Steel), national education (Bengal National College), Swadeshi steamship company (V.O. Chidambaram Pillai).
  • Extremist leaders: Bal Gangadhar Tilak ("Swaraj is my birthright"), Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal (Lal-Bal-Pal), Aurobindo Ghosh.
  • Surat Split (1907): Congress split into Moderates and Extremists over methods and goal (self-government vs constitutional reforms).
  • Muslim League (1906, Dacca): Founded by Nawab Salimullah; supported Partition of Bengal; demanded separate electorates (Morley-Minto Reforms 1909).
  • Home Rule Movement (1916): Annie Besant and Tilak — demanded self-government within the British Empire on lines of Irish Home Rule.
  • Lucknow Pact (1916): Congress-Muslim League agreement on separate electorates and weightage; joint demand for self-government.

Gandhian Era (1919–1947)

  • Rowlatt Act (1919) and Jallianwala Bagh (April 13, 1919): General Dyer fired on unarmed crowd in Amritsar — turning point in Indian politics.
  • Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22): Launched after Khilafat issue and Jallianwala Bagh; boycott of schools, courts, councils, foreign goods; Chauri Chaura (February 1922) led Gandhi to withdraw it.
  • Swaraj Party (1923, Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das): Council entry to obstruct government from within.
  • Simon Commission (1927): All-British commission — boycotted ("Simon Go Back"); led to Nehru Report (1928) demanding dominion status.
  • Purna Swaraj (1929, Lahore session): Jawaharlal Nehru as president; January 26 declared Independence Day.
  • Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–34): Dandi March (March 12–April 6, 1930), salt law violation; Round Table Conferences (1930–32); Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931); Poona Pact (1932) — reserved seats for depressed classes within Hindu electorate.
  • Quit India Movement (1942): "Do or Die" (August 8, 1942); mass arrests, underground leadership, parallel governments (Ballia, Tamluk, Satara).
  • INA (Subhas Chandra Bose): Azad Hind Fauj, Provisional Government of Free India (Singapore, 1943), women's regiment (Rani of Jhansi Regiment, Lakshmi Sahgal).
  • Cabinet Mission (1946): Three-tier federal plan, interim government; rejected by League and Congress in parts; led to Direct Action Day (August 16, 1946) and communal violence.
  • Mountbatten Plan / Indian Independence Act 1947: Partition into India and Pakistan; August 15, 1947; princely states given option to join either.

Post-Independence Consolidation

  • Integration of princely states (Sardar Patel): Hyderabad (Operation Polo, 1948), Junagadh (plebiscite), Kashmir (accession, 1947, Article 370), Goa (Operation Vijay, 1961), Pondicherry (French, 1954).
  • States Reorganisation Act 1956: Linguistic states — Andhra (1953, first linguistic state), then reorganisation on language basis.
  • Constitution adopted (1950): Republic declared.
Technique: Movement chronology
Question: Arrange in chronological order: Swadeshi, Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, Quit India.
Answer: Swadeshi (1905) → Non-Cooperation (1920) → Civil Disobedience (1930) → Quit India (1942). Each escalated in mass participation and British repression.
Technique: Assessing the Moderates
Question: “The Moderates failed because they lacked mass base.” Comment.
Answer: Moderates (1885–1905) relied on educated elite, petitions, and British goodwill. Their achievements — economic critique, Council Act 1892, Indianisation of services — were real but limited. Lack of mass mobilisation, faith in British justice, and no direct action made them vulnerable to the Extremist critique after Partition of Bengal (1905).

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Regulating Act (1773) with Pitt's India Act (1784).
  • Thinking the 1857 Revolt had a unified nationalist ideology — it was a collection of regional grievances.
  • Missing that Swadeshi was both economic (boycott British goods) and political (swaraj).
  • Assuming Gandhi withdrew Non-Cooperation only due to Chauri Chaura — also due to fear of losing control and British repression.
  • Confusing Poona Pact (1932, Gandhi-Ambedkar) with Communal Award (1932, Ramsay MacDonald).

Memory Tricks

  • 1857 → 1947: Revolt → Crown rule → INC (1885) → Swadeshi (1905) → Non-Cooperation (1920) → Civil Disobedience (1930) → Quit India (1942) → Independence (1947).
  • Lal-Bal-Pal: Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal — Extremist trio.
  • Three settlements: Gandhi-Irwin (1931), Poona Pact (1932), Cabinet Mission (1946).
Mains exam-day strategy
For modern history, always anchor events with dates, viceroys, and key leaders. Distinguish between causes, course, and consequences. For movement-based questions, discuss leadership, methods, participation, British response, and impact. Avoid hero-worship — critically assess limitations (e.g., Moderates' elite base, Gandhian withdrawals).
