Section 1: Advent of Europeans to Consolidation — 1498–1857

The standard narrative — "trade to territory" — obscures more than it reveals. A Mains-quality answer must demonstrate that the East India Company's (EIC) transformation was neither linear nor inevitable. It was shaped by three intersecting logics: (1) Corporate-fiscal — a joint-stock company accountable to shareholders and Parliament, needing surplus revenue; (2) Geopolitical — Anglo-French global rivalry (Carnatic Wars, Seven Years' War) projecting onto Indian soil; (3) Indigenous — the fragmentation of Mughal successor states, military-fiscal competition among them, and the agency of Indian merchants, bankers, and soldiers who enabled or resisted Company expansion.

1.1 The European Factories and the Mughal Decline Context

Vasco da Gama's arrival (1498) inaugurated Portuguese Estado da Índia — a crown monopoly, not a company. The Dutch VOC (1602), English EIC (1600), French Compagnie (1664) were chartered companies with sovereign powers (forts, armies, coinage, treaty-making). Their initial constraint was Mughal sovereignty: farman-secured trade privileges, no territorial ambition. The Mughal decline post-1707 (Aurangzeb's death) changed this. Successor states (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, Mysore, Marathas) inherited Mughal institutions but lacked imperial authority to enforce monopoly over European competition.

Mains Answer Hook: Plassey as "Financial Coup"
Do not describe Plassey as a battle. Frame it: (1) Siraj's conflict was with EIC's dastak abuse (private trade duty-free), not sovereignty per se. (2) The conspiracy (Mir Jafar, Jagat Seths, Rai Durlabh) was a Bengali merchant-military alliance betting on a pliant nawab. (3) Clive's "victory" was buying the nawab's army. (4) After Plassey, EIC servants' private trade exploded — from ₹30 lakh (1757) to ₹2 crore (1765). (5) The Diwani (1765) converted military control into fiscal sovereignty — revenue funded EIC's China tea trade (opium) and exports to Britain. Cite: P.J. Marshall, East Indian Fortunes (1976); S. Chaudhuri, From Protest to Challenge (2016).

Parliament's intervention was driven by three crises: (1) EIC servants' corruption (Clive, Hastings impeachments), (2) Famine and revenue collapse (1770 Bengal famine), (3) Geopolitical wars (Anglo-French, Anglo-Mysore, Anglo-Maratha) requiring state oversight. 1.4 Land Revenue Systems: The Fiscal Architecture of Colonial Rule

Land revenue was the single largest source of colonial revenue (~50% throughout Company rule). The three systems were not "policies" but property-rights regimes shaped by:
(1) Pre-colonial precedent — Mughal zabt (measurement), zamindari (revenue farming);
(2) British ideological priors — Cornwallis (Whig, property rights), Munro (Scottish Enlightenment, peasant proprietorship), Holt Mackenzie (utilitarian, village community);
(3) Information constraints — cadastral survey capacity, local intermediaries.

PYQ Model: Land Revenue as State Formation (2018, 15 marks)
"The land revenue systems of the British were not merely fiscal devices but instruments of social engineering." Critically examine.
Thesis: Each system created a distinct property regime and class structure. (1) Permanent Settlement: Created a loyal zamindar class (political insurance), but froze demand → zamindars became rentiers, subinfeudation created jotedars (rich peasants) as real power. Peasants reduced to tenants-at-will. (2) Ryotwari: Made state the direct landlord; cadastral survey created individual property titles (pre-colonial: collective/village rights); enabled market in land → indebtedness, alienation to moneylenders (sahukars). (3) Mahalwari: Reinforced village hierarchy (lambardar, patwari); joint liability allowed state to coerce through community. (4) Common thread: All three converted revenue rights into property rights — commodification of land. (5) Historiography: Marxist (Dutt, Habib) = feudalism/imperialism; Revisionist (Guha, Stokes) = pragmatic adaptation; Subaltern (Ludden) = peasant agency. Conclusion: Fiscal necessity drove design; social consequences were structural, not incidental.

1.5 Commercialisation, De-industrialisation, and Drain of Wealth

The colonial economy is often reduced to "drain of wealth" (Dadabhai Naoroji, 1867). A nuanced answer distinguishes three processes:

  • De-industrialisation: Decline of handicrafts (textiles, shipbuilding, metallurgy) due to: (a) British industrial goods (machine-made, cheaper) — post-1813 Charter Act; (b) Loss of princely patronage (Mughal courts, nawabs); (c) Railway freight policy favouring port-to-hinterland (raw cotton out, Manchester cloth in); (d) Tariff policy — free entry for British goods, duties on Indian exports (1879 cotton duties removed only after Lancashire pressure). source
  • Forced Commercialisation: Shift from foodgrains to cash crops (indigo, opium, cotton, jute, tea) — driven by revenue demand (cash payment), export market, and planter pressure. Indigo (Bengal/Bihar, tinkathia system), Opium (Benares/Malwa agencies, China trade), Cotton (Deccan, American Civil War boom 1861–65), Jute (Bengal, Dundee mills). Result: food insecurity, famines (1876–78, 1896–97, 1899–1900). source
  • Drain of Wealth: Naoroji's Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901) quantified: Home Charges (interest on debt, pensions, civil/military charges, India Office expenses) + private remittances + profits on trade = ~₹30–40 crore/year (late 19th c.). Counter-argument (Morris, 1963; Roy, 2000): Drain financed India's rail/irrigation imports; net resource transfer debated. Mains requires presenting both with evidence. source

Section 2: Socio-Religious Reform Movements and the Making of Modern Indian Identities

Reform movements (19th–early 20th c.) are not a monolithic "Indian Renaissance." They operated on three contested axes: (1) Scriptural vs. Rational — authority of Vedas/Quran vs. reason/humanism; (2) Assimilationist vs. Separatist — engage colonial modernity on its terms vs. retrieve "pure" tradition; (3) Elite vs. Subaltern — Brahmin/male-led vs. lower-caste/women-led. The colonial state enabled reform (law, print, education) but also shaped it (codifying personal law, census categories, communal electorates).

2.1 The Bengal Renaissance: Brahmo Samaj and Its Fractures

Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833) is the "Father of Modern India" — but his project was selective: Vedantic monotheism (Upanishads), abolition of sati (1829, Regulation XVII), women's property rights, English education. He founded Brahmo Sabha (1828) → Brahmo Samaj. Post-Roy, the Samaj fractured along the three axes:

2.2 Arya Samaj: Vedic Fundamentalism as Modernisation

Dayananda Saraswati (1824–83), a Gujarati sanyasi, founded Arya Samaj (1875, Bombay). His Satyarth Prakash (1875) is the manifesto: "Back to the Vedas" — Vedas as infallible, scientific, containing all knowledge. Key positions:

  • Shuddhi (reconversion): Ritual to "purify" converts to Islam/Christianity — first organised Hindu proselytisation. source
  • Cow Protection: Gau Raksha movement (1882) — first mass Hindu mobilisation on religious symbol; 1893 riots (Azamgarh).
  • Anti-Untouchability: Inter-dining, temple entry — but within varna framework (not annihilation).
  • Education: DAV College (Lahore, 1886) — network of schools/colleges; Lala Lajpat Rai product.
  • Women: Widow remarriage, female education (Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Jalandhar), but patriarchal family ideal.
Mains Trap: Arya Samaj as "Reform" vs. "Revival"
Do not label Arya Samaj simply "reformist." It was restorationist — claiming Vedic society was ideal (no caste by birth, no idolatry, women educated). But its method was modern: print (Satyarth Prakash in Hindi), organisation (samajes), shuddhi (institutionalised conversion), political mobilisation (cow protection). It fed Hindu nationalism (Hindu Mahasabha, RSS). Contrast with Brahmo Samaj: Brahmo = reason + scripture; Arya = scripture AS reason. Both were elite, male, upper-caste projects. Subaltern critique (Jotirao Phule): Arya Samaj defended varna; Phule attacked it.

2.4 South India: Self-Respect, Dravidian, and Anti-Caste Modernity

The Madras Presidency produced the most radical anti-caste trajectory — moving from reform to political capture.

  • Justice Party (1916): Non-Brahmin manifesto (T.M. Nair, P. Theagaraya Chetty); communal representation (1921, first in India); seized power under dyarchy (1920–37). source
  • Self-Respect Movement (1925): E.V. Ramasamy 'Periyar' (1879–1973) — broke from Congress (1925) over Brahmin dominance; Kudi Arasu journal; anti-god, anti-religion, anti-Brahmin, pro-women (self-respect marriage, contraception, property rights), pro-Tamil (anti-Hindi, 1937–39). source
  • Dravidar Kazhagam (1944): Periyar + C.N. Annadurai split (1949) → DMK; captured Tamil Nadu (1967) — reform became state policy.
  • Temple Entry Movements: Vaikom (1924–25, Gandhi-Periyar tension), Guruvayur (1931–32), Madras Temple Entry Act (1939, under Congress ministry).

2.6 Sikh Reform: Singh Sabha and the Definition of Sikh Identity

Singh Sabha (1873, Amritsar; 1879, Lahore) responded to three threats: Christian missions, Arya Samaj shuddhi, and Brahminical rituals in gurdwaras. Two streams:

  • Amritsar (conservative): Aristocrats, custodians of Golden Temple; sought British patronage.
  • Lahore (radical): Bhai Vir Singh, Kahn Singh Nabha (Hum Hindu Nahin, 1898 — "We are not Hindus"); Khalsa identity (5 Ks, rehat maryada); Gurdwara Reform Movement (1920–25) → SGPC (1925), Akali Dal. source

2.7 The Colonial State as Reform Arbiter: Law, Census, and Communal Categories

Reform was not purely indigenous. The colonial state codified religion through:

  • Personal Law: Hindu Law (Bengal, 1772 — Halhed's Code; Mitakshara/Dayabhaga), Muslim Law (Hidayah, Fatwa Alamgiri) — administered by British judges with pandits/maulvis. Result: Anglo-Hindu Law — fixed, textual, Brahminical (women's rights reduced vs. customary). source
  • Census (1871 onward): Rigid categories (Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, Christian, Tribal/Animist) — forced fluid identities into boxes. "Caste" enumerated (1881, 1901, 1911, 1931) — created all-India caste hierarchy where none existed. Risley's Nasial Index (1901) — racialised caste. source
  • Legislative Reform: Sati (1829), Widow Remarriage (1856), Age of Consent (1891, 1929 — Sarda Act), Child Marriage Restraint (1929), Hindu Women's Rights to Property (1937) — each provoked "orthodox" vs. "reformist" mobilisation, communalised debate.
PYQ Model: Colonial State and Social Reform (2019, 15 marks)
"The colonial state was both the enabler and the constraint of social reform in 19th century India." Discuss.
Structure: (1) Enabler: Legal prohibition (Sati 1829, Widow Remarriage 1856); print/censorship regime allowed reformist journals (Tattvabodhini, Satyarth Prakash, Gulamgiri); English education created reformist intelligentsia; police power enforced reform (Age of Consent). (2) Constraint: Anglo-Hindu Law fossilised "Hindu law" as Brahminical text (Mitakshara), erasing customary diversity; Census created rigid religious/caste categories (Hindu/Muslim/Sikh) — reformers had to claim "authentic" tradition within colonial frames; Communal electorates (1909, 1919, 1935) made religious identity political currency — reform became communal competition (shuddhi vs. tabligh, cow protection vs. khilafat). (3) Case studies: Age of Consent (1891) — Tilak opposed as "interference"; Sarda Act (1929) — opposed by Muslim League as "Hindu law". (4) Conclusion: Reform was negotiated in a colonial public sphere where the state set the terms.

Section 3: The National Movement — Phases, Leadership, and Strategic Debates

The national movement (1885–1947) is not a single narrative but a succession of strategic experiments — each phase defined by its theory of change: how to convert grievance into sovereignty. Mains answers must evaluate each phase on: Leadership social base, Methods, Mass participation, British response, Internal tensions, Legacy.

3.1 The Moderate Phase (1885–1905): Constitutionalism and the "Safety Valve" Debate

The Indian National Congress (INC, 1885) was founded by A.O. Hume (retired ICS) with official blessing — the "safety valve" theory (Lajpat Rai, 1916; Dutt, 1920). Moderates (Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gokhale, Surendranath Banerjee) were Western-educated professionals (lawyers, journalists, teachers) who believed in British justice and India's "political infancy."

3.2 The Extremist Phase (1905–1918): Swadeshi, Boycott, and the Split

The Partition of Bengal (1905, Curzon) — ostensibly administrative (Bengal too large), actually divide-and-rule (Muslim-majority East Bengal). The response: Swadeshi Movement — first mass mobilisation beyond elite.

  • Methods: Boycott (British cloth, salt, sugar), Swadeshi enterprises (Bengal Chemicals, Tata Steel, Swadeshi Steam Navigation — V.O. Chidambaram Pillai), National Education (Bengal National College, Aurobindo Ghosh), Samitis (volunteer corps), Rakhi-bandhan (communal unity). source
  • Leadership: Lal-Bal-Pal (Lajpat Rai, Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal) + Aurobindo Ghosh. Tilak: "Swaraj is my birthright" — first explicit political goal (not just reform).
  • Surat Split (1907): Over presidency (Rash Behari Ghosh vs. Tilak) and goal (self-government vs. constitutional reforms). Extremists expelled; Tilak imprisoned (1908–14, Mandalay).
  • Revolutionary Terrorism: Anushilan Samiti (Barin Ghosh), Jugantar, Abhinav Bharat (Savarkar), Ghadar Party (1913, US/Canada — Sohan Singh Bhakna, Kartar Singh Sarabha). Assassinations (Jackson, 1909; Curzon Wyllie, 1909), arms smuggling. source
  • Home Rule (1916): Besant + Tilak (two leagues); demanded self-government on Irish model; mass membership (60,000+); forced Montagu Declaration (1917).
  • Lucknow Pact (1916): Congress-League unity; accepted separate electorates + weightage; joint demand for self-government. Tilak's pragmatism; Jinnah as "ambassador of Hindu-Muslim unity."
Mains Differentiator: Swadeshi as Economic + Political
Swadeshi was not just boycott. It was a constructive programme — building indigenous institutions (banks, insurance, textiles, education, arbitration courts). Sumit Sarkar: "Swadeshi was the first attempt to create a 'national economy'." But it had limits: (a) Upper-caste Hindu leadership (Muslims wary — Nawab Salimullah supported Partition); (b) Landlord-peasant tension (Swadeshi leaders often zamindars); (c) Repression (CID, sedition, press laws) decimated it by 1908. For Mains: "Swadeshi invented the repertoire of mass politics — boycott, swadeshi enterprises, national education, volunteer corps — that Gandhi later scaled nationally."

Section 4: The Gandhian Phase — 1915–1947: Movements, Ideology, Leadership

Gandhi's return from South Africa (1915) inaugurated the mass phase of the national movement. This section analyses his theory of change (satyagraha), the four major movements, leadership tensions, and ideological critiques — the depth UPSC Mains demands beyond textbook chronology.

4.1 Satyagraha: Theory, Method, and Ethical Architecture

Satyagraha (satya + agraha — "holding onto truth") is not passive resistance; it is active non-violent non-cooperation grounded in three claims: (1) Ontological: Truth is the ultimate reality; injustice is untruth. (2) Epistemological: The opponent's conscience can be awakened through self-suffering (tapasya). (3) Strategic: Non-violence weaponises the moral asymmetry between ruler and ruled — the British could not claim legitimacy while massacring unarmed civilians. source

4.5 Constructive Programme — The Unexamined Dimension

The Constructive Programme (1941, Harijan) was Gandhi's prefigurative politics — building the post-colonial society in the shell of the old. 18 items: communal unity, removal of untouchability, prohibition, khadi, village sanitation, Nai Talim (basic education), adult education, women's equality, kisans, labour, Adivasis, lepers, students, cow protection, village industries, sanitation, health, propagation of Hindi/Hindustani.

Why Constructive Programme Matters for Mains

5.2 The Critical Junctures — Three Negotiations That Shaped Partition

5.2.1 The Communal Award (1932) and Poona Pact

Ramsay MacDonald's Communal Award (Aug 1932) granted separate electorates to Depressed Classes, Sikhs, Europeans, Anglo-Indians, in addition to Muslims. Gandhi's fast (Yerwada, Sept 1932) forced Ambedkar to accept Poona Pact (Sept 24, 1932): reserved seats in joint electorates (148 seats, doubled from 71), primary panel of Dalit voters to choose four candidates for general electorate. Consequence: Dalit political representation became dependent on Hindu majority votes — structural constraint persisting today (Art. 330/332). source

5.2.2 The 1937 Elections and Congress Ministries — The Federal Moment Lost

The 1937 elections under GOI Act 1935 were the first mass electoral test. Congress won 711/1,585 seats (45%), formed ministries in 7 provinces (Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, UP, Bihar, Orissa, NWFP). Key actions: (1) Civil liberties — released political prisoners, lifted press bans; (2) Agrarian — tenancy reform (UP, Bihar), debt relief (Madras); (3) Labour — Trade Disputes Act (Bombay), minimum wages; (4) Education — Wardha Scheme (Basic Education); (5) Communal — Muslim mass contact campaign (failed; League portrayed as Hindu raj).

Why it matters: (a) Demonstrated Congress administrative capacity; (b) Exposed limits of provincial autonomy (finance controlled by Centre); (c) League's poor performance (4.4% Muslim vote) → Jinnah's "Muslims in danger" campaign; (d) Resignation (Nov 1939) over war declaration without consultation — handed political initiative to League. source

5.2.3 The Cabinet Mission (1946) — The Last Chance for Unity

Cabinet Mission (Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, Alexander) arrived March 1946. Plan (May 16, 1946): Three-tier federation — Group A (Hindu-majority: Madras, Bombay, UP, Bihar, Central Provinces, Orissa), Group B (Muslim-majority NW: Punjab, NWFP, Sind, Baluchistan), Group C (Muslim-majority East: Bengal, Assam). Centre: Defence, External Affairs, Communications. Provinces: all other subjects + option to review grouping after 10 years. Constituent Assembly elected by provincial legislatures.

PYQ Model: Cabinet Mission (2020, 15 marks)
"The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) was the last viable framework for a united India. Its failure was not inevitable." Critically examine.

Thesis: The Plan's architecture — federal grouping with voluntary review — contained the seeds of both unity and partition. Failure resulted from mutual distrust and British haste, not structural impossibility.

1. The Plan's Ingenuity: It avoided "Pakistan" while conceding Muslim-majority autonomy (Groups B, C). The 10-year review clause allowed renegotiation. Centre limited to three subjects — maximum decentralisation compatible with unity. Sikh concerns (Punjab split) were acknowledged but not resolved.

2. Congress's "Voluntary Grouping" Interpretation: Nehru's press conference (July 10, 1946): "Congress is free to change the Plan in the Constituent Assembly." Jinnah saw this as repudiation. Congress feared Group B/C would become de facto Pakistan; League feared Congress would dominate Centre and prevent grouping.

3. League's Intransigence: Jinnah demanded explicit compulsory grouping. When Mission clarified (June 16) that grouping was compulsory but sections could opt out after first election, League accepted then rejected (July 29). Direct Action Day (Aug 16) made violence the political currency.

4. British Complicity: Wavell (Viceroy) allowed League into Interim Govt (Oct 1946) despite Direct Action — legitimised violence as leverage. Attlee's Feb 1947 deadline ("June 1948") forced Mountbatten's accelerated timetable — no time for Plan to mature.

5. Counterfactual: Had Congress accepted compulsory grouping in writing (as Mission proposed June 16), League might have stayed. Had League accepted voluntary grouping (as Congress insisted), Constituent Assembly would have functioned. Both sides treated the Plan as tactical, not final.

Conclusion: The Plan was viable if political will existed. Its failure was the failure of elite negotiation under British-imposed deadline. The 1947 Partition was not the Plan's child — it was the orphan of its abortion.

5.3 The Mountbatten Plan (June 3 Plan) and Independence Act

Mountbatten arrived March 1947. Plan Balkan (May 1947) — provincial option to join either dominion or stay independent — rejected by Nehru (balkanisation). June 3 Plan (announced June 3, 1947):

  • Partition of Punjab and Bengal — legislative assemblies of West/East Punjab and West/East Bengal meet separately (Muslim vs non-Muslim majorities) to decide partition.
  • Referendum in NWFP and Sylhet — NWFP chose Pakistan (boycotted by Khudai Khidmatgar); Sylhet (Assam) chose East Pakistan.
  • Boundary Commission (Radcliffe) — two commissions (Punjab, Bengal); Radcliffe line announced Aug 17 (after independence).
  • Princely States — paramountcy lapses; free to accede to India or Pakistan (geographical contiguity, people's wishes).
  • Date — 15 August 1947 (advanced from June 1948).
Mains Differentiator: Mountbatten's Agency vs. Structural Forces
Question: "Mountbatten accelerated Partition; he did not cause it." Discuss.
Answer: (1) Structural forces: 1937 elections → League's "Muslims in danger"; 1940 Lahore Resolution; 1946 Direct Action; communal violence made coexistence untenable in Punjab/Bengal. (2) Mountbatten's choices: (a) Advanced date (June 1948 → Aug 1947) — 10 months less for preparation; (b) Radcliffe line delayed until Aug 17 — boundaries unknown on Independence Day; (c) No joint boundary force — British troops withdrawn, leaving vacuum; (d) Princely state accession — "geographical contiguity" formula but Kashmir/Junagadh/Hyderabad left ambiguous. (3) Evaluation: Mountbatten managed the timetable of an inevitable Partition; his haste exacerbated violence but did not create the demand. Cite: A. Jalal, The Sole Spokesman (1985); Y. Khan, The Great Partition (2007); L. Collins & D. Lapierre, Freedom at Midnight (1975); PYQ 2022.

PYQ Model: Constitutional Continuity (2019, 10 marks)
"The Government of India Act 1935 was the blueprint for the Constitution of India, but with critical departures." Discuss.

Answer: (1) Blueprint elements: Federal structure (Union/State/Concurrent lists — Seventh Schedule from 1935); Parliamentary system (Governor/Governor-General on advice); Provincial autonomy → State autonomy; Federal Court → Supreme Court; PSC → UPSC/SPSCs; Emergency provisions (Section 93 → Art. 356); RBI, Federal Railway Authority → statutory bodies. (2) Critical departures: (a) Sovereignty: 1935 Act — British Parliament supreme; Constitution — "We the People" (Preamble), popular sovereignty. (b) Communal electorates abolished: 1935 entrenched them; Constitution — joint electorates, SC/ST reservations only. (c) Fundamental Rights (Part III): 1935 had none; Constitution — justiciable rights (Art. 12–35), judicial review (Art. 13). (d) Directive Principles (Part IV): 1935 had "Instruments of Instructions" (non-justiciable); Constitution — DPSP (Art. 36–51), social-economic democracy. (e) Adult Franchise (Art. 326): 1935 — ~14% franchise, property/education qualifications; Constitution — universal adult suffrage. (f) Amendment procedure (Art. 368): 1935 — British Parliament; Constitution — self-amending (with state ratification for federal provisions). (3) Conclusion: The 1935 Act provided the administrative architecture; the Constitution provided the democratic soul. The continuity was institutional; the break was philosophical.
