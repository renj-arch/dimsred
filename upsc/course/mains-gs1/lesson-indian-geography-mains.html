1. Physiography & Drainage

1.1 Geological Evolution and Major Physiographic Divisions

India's physical geography is the product of three major geological episodes: the Precambrian shield formation (Peninsular India), the Himalayan orogeny (Tertiary), and the Quaternary alluvial deposition (Indo-Gangetic plains). The Survey of India recognises six physiographic divisions [SoI, Physiographic Divisions of India, 2021]:

On an outline map, draw the Aravalli–Delhi ridge separating Thar from the Punjab plains; the Western Ghats escarpment (not a true mountain range but a faulted edge of the Deccan plateau); the Satpura–Maikal range as the traditional north–south divider of the peninsula. These are frequent Mains map-location items.

1.2 The Himalayas: Structure, Passes, and Seismicity

The Himalayas formed by the ongoing collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate at ~5 cm/yr convergence [USGS, Himalayan Tectonics, 2023]. From south to north:

  • Shiwaliks (Outer Himalaya): 600–1,500 m; conglomerates, sandstones; Doons (Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun) between Shiwaliks and Lesser Himalaya. High erosion, landslide-prone.
  • Lesser Himalaya (Himachal): 1,500–5,000 m; metamorphic rocks (schists, gneisses); major hill stations (Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital); thrust zones — Main Boundary Thrust (MBT), Main Central Thrust (MCT).
  • Greater Himalaya (Himadri): >6,000 m; crystalline core (granites, gneisses); highest peaks — Kanchenjunga (8,586 m), Nanda Devi (7,816 m), Kamet (7,756 m); perpetual snow line ~4,500–5,500 m.
  • Trans-Himalaya / Tibetan Himalaya: North of the Indus–Tsangpo Suture Zone; Karakoram, Ladakh, Zanskar ranges; cold desert.
Mains Technique: Pass-based questions
"Examine the strategic and economic significance of Himalayan passes with examples." (UPSC 2018, 10 marks)
Answer structure: (1) Define passes as natural gaps enabling movement across watersheds. (2) Categorise: Trade — Nathu La (Sikkim–Tibet, reopened 2006), Shipki La (Himachal–Tibet), Lipulekh (Uttarakhand–Tibet, Kailash Mansarovar route); Defence — Zoji La (Srinagar–Leh), Bum La (Arunachal–Tibet), Rezang La (Chushul sector); Tourism/Pilgrimage — Rohtang, Mana, Jelep La. (3) Link to Border Roads Organisation (BRO) infra push — all-weather tunnels (Atal Tunnel at Rohtang, 9.02 km), strategic roads. (4) Challenges: glacial retreat, seismic risk, China's border infra. Conclude: Passes are geographical determinants of India's northern security calculus.

1.3 Northern Plains: Alluvial Architecture and Flood Dynamics

The Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra plain spans ~700,000 km², formed by sediment flux of ~1.6–2.0 billion tonnes/yr from the Himalaya [CWC, Sediment Yield of Indian Rivers, 2022]. Four longitudinal zones:

  • Bhabar: 8–16 km wide piedmont zone; coarse alluvium (pebbles, boulders); streams disappear underground; high porosity, low agriculture.
  • Terai: 15–30 km wide; streams re-emerge; marshy, high water table; historically malarial, now cleared for agriculture (Punjab, UP, Bihar, West Bengal).
  • Bhangar: Older alluvium, higher elevation; kankar (calcareous nodules); less fertile, less flood-prone.
  • Khadar: Newer alluvium in active floodplains; annually renewed silt; most fertile; rice–wheat belt.
Answer Framing: Flood management
For questions on flood management, use the structural vs non-structural framework: Structural — embankments (Kosi, Gandak), dams (Tehri, Bhakra), channel improvement; Non-structural — flood plain zoning (NDMA 2008 guidelines), forecasting (CWC/IMD), insurance (PMFBY), community-based early warning. Cite National Disaster Management Plan 2019 and Kosi River Treaty (1954, renewed 2024) for transboundary dimension.

1.4 Peninsular Plateau: Sub-divisions, Mineral Belts, and Rift Valleys

The peninsula comprises two macro-units separated by the Narmada–Son lineament (a deep crustal fracture):

  • Central Highlands (north of Narmada): Aravallis (oldest fold mountains, ~1.8 Ga), Malwa Plateau (basaltic), Bundelkhand (granite–gneiss), Baghelkhand, Chotanagpur Plateau (Gondwana coal, iron ore — Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro, Dhanbad).
  • Deccan Plateau (south of Narmada): Maharashtra Plateau (Deccan Traps — flood basalts, 66 Ma), Karnataka Plateau (Dharwar craton — gold, iron), Telangana Plateau, Tamil Nadu uplands. Black cotton soil (regur) from basalt weathering.
Technique: Rift valley rivers
"Why do Narmada and Tapi flow west while other peninsular rivers flow east?" (UPSC 2013, 10 marks)
Answer: Narmada and Tapi occupy rift valleys (graben) formed by faulting along the Narmada–Son and Tapi lineaments during the Cretaceous Deccan volcanism. Their gradients are steep, courses short, and they form estuaries (not deltas) due to high tidal energy and low sediment load. Other peninsular rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi) follow the regional eastward tilt of the peninsula, forming large deltas. Use a sketch map showing the Western Ghats watershed close to the coast (~50 km) forcing short west-flowing streams.

1.6 Drainage Patterns and Geomorphic Significance

  • Dendritic: Indo-Gangetic plains (uniform alluvium) — resembles tree branches.
  • Radial: Amarkantak (Narmada, Son, Mahanadi originate here) — flow outward from a dome.
  • Trellis: Himalayas (fold structure) — parallel consequent streams with right-angle tributaries.
  • Rectangular: Vindhyas, Chotanagpur (jointed/faulted bedrock) — right-angle bends follow joints.
  • Centripetal: Ladakh, Sambhar Lake — streams converge into inland basins.
PYQ Pattern: River linking
UPSC 2020 (15 marks): "Discuss the feasibility of inter-basin water transfer in India." Framework: (1) Need — spatial/temporal mismatch (70% rain in 4 months, 12% area water-surplus, 38% deficit). (2) National Water Development Agency (NWDA) plan — 30 links (14 Himalayan, 16 peninsular); Ken–Betwa (Phase I, approved 2021), Damanganga–Pinjal, Par–Tapi–Narmada. (3) Challenges — ecological (submergence, fisheries), inter-state disputes, cost (~₹5.6 lakh cr), climate uncertainty, transboundary (Bangladesh, Nepal). (4) Alternatives — demand management, micro-irrigation, groundwater recharge, basin-level governance. Conclude: Linking is necessary but not sufficient; integrate with Atal Bhujal Yojana and Jal Jeevan Mission.

2. Climate & Monsoon

2.1 Climatic Controls and Köppen Classification

India's climate is governed by latitude (8°4'N–37°6'N), altitude (Himalayan barrier), pressure/wind systems, continentality, and ocean currents. The Tropic of Cancer (23.5°N) passes through 8 states — Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura, Mizoram [SoI, India Reference Map, 2022]. 2.2 Seasonal Cycle and Monsoon Mechanism

Four seasons (IMD classification) [IMD, Seasonal Classification, 2023]:

  • Cold Weather (Dec–Feb): NE monsoon (retreating), clear skies, low humidity; western disturbances (mid-latitude westerlies) bring winter rain to NW India (wheat, mustard); frost in Punjab, Haryana, UP.
  • Hot Weather (Mar–May): Rising temps (40–48°C in Rajasthan, Vidarbha); heat waves (IMD: >45°C or >4.5°C above normal); pre-monsoon thunderstorms — Nor'westers/Kal Baisakhi (Bengal, Bihar, Odisha), Mango showers (Kerala, Karnataka), Cherry blossoms (coffee, Kodagu).
  • Advancing SW Monsoon (Jun–Sep): Onset over Kerala (~1 June); northward progression in two branches — Arabian Sea branch (Mumbai ~10 Jun, Delhi ~29 Jun) and Bay of Bengal branch (Kolkata ~7 Jun); mechanism: differential heating → ITCZ shifts to 25°N over Tibet → cross-equatorial flow deflects to SW (Coriolis) → Somali Jet (Findlater Jet, 15–20 m/s at 850 hPa) → moisture transport; Tibetan Plateau heating creates upper-level anticyclone → Tropical Easterly Jet (TEJ) at 150 hPa → monsoon maintenance.
  • Retreating Monsoon (Oct–Nov): ITCZ shifts south; NE trades re-establish; cyclones in Bay of Bengal (post-monsoon peak); Tamil Nadu gets 50–60% annual rain from NE monsoon.
Technique: Monsoon onset/withdrawal dates
"Explain the role of the Somali Jet and Tibetan Plateau in the Indian monsoon." (UPSC 2015, 12.5 marks)
Answer: (1) Somali Jet (Findlater Jet): Cross-equatorial low-level jet (850 hPa, ~15 m/s) along East African coast; transports ~40% of moisture for Indian monsoon; strength correlates with monsoon intensity (stronger jet = above-normal rain). (2) Tibetan Plateau: Elevated heat source (2.5 km mean elevation); spring heating creates upper-level anticyclone (Bolton High) at 200 hPa; drives TEJ (150 hPa, easterly 30–40 m/s) over peninsular India; TEJ enhances divergence aloft → ascent → convection. (3) Coupling: Plateau heating precedes onset by ~1 month; snow cover on Tibet (Eurasian snow hypothesis) inversely correlates with monsoon strength. Cite Rao (1976), Webster et al. (1998), IMD Monsoon Reports.

2.4 Climate Variability: ENSO, IOD, and Intra-seasonal Oscillations

  • El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO): Warm phase (El Niño) weakens Walker Circulation → reduced monsoon rainfall (correlation ~−0.6); but 40% of El Niño years have normal/excess rain (1997, 2009 exceptions) [IMD, ENSO–Monsoon Relationship, 2023].
  • Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD): Positive IOD (warm western IO, cool eastern IO) enhances monsoon (1994, 1997, 2019); can offset El Niño (1997). Negative IOD suppresses rain.
  • Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO): Eastward-moving convective envelope (30–60 day cycle); active phase over Indian Ocean → enhanced rainfall (active spells); suppressed phase → breaks. Critical for extended-range prediction (IMD ERP, 2–3 week lead).
  • Monsoon breaks: Mid-season dry spells (1–2 weeks) due to northward shift of monsoon trough to Himalaya; cause kharif stress in central India.
Mains Framing: Climate resilience
For questions on climate change impacts on agriculture: Use IPCC AR6 regional projections for South Asia — +1.5–2°C by 2050, increased extreme rainfall (+10–20%), more heatwaves, glacial retreat. Link to National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA), PM-KISAN, PMFBY 2.0 (tech-driven yield estimation), ICAR NICRA (climate-resilient varieties). Cite Economic Survey 2023–24 Ch. on Climate Finance and NAPCC 2008/2021 update.

2.5 Extreme Events: Cyclones, Heatwaves, and Western Disturbances

  • Cyclones: Bay of Bengal (4:1 ratio vs Arabian Sea); pre-monsoon (Apr–May) and post-monsoon (Oct–Nov) peaks; recent intensification (Amphan 2020, Yaas 2021, Mocha 2023) linked to warmer SSTs [IMD, Cyclone Annual Report, 2023]. Odisha, WB, AP, TN most vulnerable.
  • Heatwaves: IMD criteria: plains >45°C, hills >30°C, coastal >37°C (departure >4.5°C); increasing frequency/duration (1980–2020: +2.5 days/decade) [IMD, Heatwave Atlas, 2022]. Impact: mortality, labour productivity, power demand, wheat yield loss.
  • Western Disturbances: Mid-latitude westerlies (Dec–Mar); origin: Mediterranean/Caspian/Black Sea; bring winter rain/snow to J&K, Himachal, Uttarakhand, Punjab, Haryana; critical for rabi crops and glacier mass balance. Declining trend in recent decades.

3.2 Soil Degradation and Conservation

  • Water erosion: 68% of degraded land (147 Mha); severe in Shiwaliks (150–200 t/ha/yr), Chambal ravines (badlands), Western Ghats, NE hills (jhum/shifting cultivation) [ICAR, Land Degradation Atlas, 2021].
  • Wind erosion: 10% (Thar, western Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana); dust storms.
  • Chemical degradation: Salinisation (6.7 Mha), alkalinity (3.8 Mha), acidity (18 Mha), nutrient mining (N:P:K imbalance — ideal 4:2:1, actual ~8:3:1).
  • Physical degradation: Waterlogging (4.1 Mha, canal commands), compaction, subsidence.
  • Conservation programmes: PMKSY–Watershed Development (ridge-to-valley), Soil Health Card Scheme (2015, 23 cr cards issued), National Mission on Sustainable Agriculture, Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (per drop more crop).

Technique: Groundwater governance
"Critically examine the groundwater crisis in India and suggest a governance framework." (UPSC 2019, 15 marks)
Answer: (1) Crisis dimensions — 256/734 blocks over-exploited (CGWB 2023); Punjab (79% blocks critical), Rajasthan, Haryana, Delhi, TN; annual extraction 245 BCM (62% for irrigation); water table decline >1 m/yr in NW India (GRACE satellite). (2) Drivers — free/subsidised electricity → over-pumping; water-intensive cropping (paddy in Punjab, sugarcane in Maharashtra); weak regulation (Easement Act 1882 — landowner owns groundwater); climate variability. (3) Governance framework — Supply side: Atal Bhujal Yojana (participatory management, 7 states), aquifer mapping (NAQUIM), MAR (managed aquifer recharge), Jal Shakti Abhiyan. Demand side: Crop diversification (Maize, pulses, oilseeds via MSP), micro-irrigation (PMKSY), direct seeding of rice (DSR), solar pumps (PM-KUSUM). Institutional: Model Groundwater Bill (2017/2020) — community-based management, registration, pricing; state acts (Maharashtra 2009, Karnataka 2011, UP 2019). Conclude: Shift from resource development to resource governance; align energy–water–food nexus.

Technique: Regional disparity answer
"Examine the persistence of regional disparities in India despite decades of planned development." (UPSC 2016, 12.5 marks)
Answer: (1) Historical legacies — colonial port-centric development (Bombay, Calcutta, Madras), princely state variations, Partition disruption (Punjab, Bengal), freight equalisation policy (1952–93) eroded mineral advantage of eastern India. (2) Structural factors — Green Revolution bypassed east/central (rainfed, small holdings); industrial licensing (pre-1991) favoured west/south; infrastructure deficit (rail, road, power density). (3) Governance — state capacity variation (Kerala/TN vs Bihar/UP); political instability; leakages in CSS. (4) Post-1991 divergence — FDI/exports clustered in Gujarat, Maharashtra, TN, Karnataka; services/IT in Bangalore, Hyderabad, Pune; agriculture-dependent states (Bihar, UP, MP) grew slower. (5) Convergence evidence — 15th FC income distance weight (45%) helps; Aspirational Districts show improvement in health/education; but per capita income divergence persists (CV rising). Conclude: Need place-based policies (not one-size-fits-all), investment in human capital, decentralised governance, and breaking the low-skill trap in lagging regions.

6.3 Answer-Writing Frameworks for Indian Geography

Framework A: "Examine / Discuss / Critically Examine" (10–15 marks)
Structure: Introduction (15–20%) — Define key term, cite data/ranking, state thesis. Body (65–70%) — 3–4 dimensions (Physical, Economic, Social, Political/Institutional) with sub-headings; each paragraph = one argument + evidence (data, scheme, case study, map reference). Conclusion (15%) — Synthesis, forward-looking (policy/tech/behaviour), one-line punch.
Example (River disputes): Intro — Water=State subject (Entry 17), Centre=inter-state (Entry 56), Art 262 bars SC jurisdiction; 9 tribunals, delays. Body — (1) Constitutional/federal: asymmetry, tribunal awards not binding (Cauvery), Centre's role limited. (2) Hydrological: basin as unit vs state boundaries; data opacity; climate change altering flows. (3) Socio-economic: agrarian distress, urban demand, industry vs agriculture. (4) Solutions: Basin authorities (Damodar, Brahmaputra Board), data sharing (NWIC), mediation, water pricing, demand management. Conclusion — Move from adjudication to basin governance; amend ISRWD Act for time-bound, enforceable awards.
Framework B: "Account for / Explain the reasons" (10–12.5 marks)
Structure: Introduction — Phenomenon statement with spatial reference (map mental image). Body — Group causes into Physical (geology, climate, relief), Historical (colonial, policy), Economic (market, labour, infrastructure), Contemporary (technology, policy, globalisation). Use connectors: "Furthermore," "Conversely," "In contrast," "This is reinforced by." Conclusion — Summary of dominant factor + emerging trend.
Example (Cotton textile distribution): Intro — 2nd largest employer, 12% exports; clusters: Maharashtra–Gujarat (60%), Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Madhya Pradesh, WB. Body — Physical: Black soil (cotton), humid climate (spinning), water (processing). Historical: Mumbai (port, capital, humidity), Ahmedabad (entrepreneurship), Coimbatore (hydro power). Economic: Market (domestic + export), labour (skilled, low-cost), power (captive), policy (TUF, Amended TUFS, PLI). Contemporary: Technical textiles, MMF shift, cluster decay (Mumbai), new hubs (Perundurai, Dindigul). Conclusion — From resource-based to skill/policy-driven; need fibre-neutral policy.
Framework C: Map-based / Location questions (10 marks)
Mental map checklist: (1) Locate — State, district, river, mountain, coordinate (lat/long if possible). (2) Geological/Geomorphic context — Rock type, structure, process. (3) Climatic context — Rainfall, temperature, wind. (4) Resource/Economic context — Mineral, crop, industry, port. (5) Strategic/Current context — Border, corridor, project, dispute. Draw — Sketch map with 4–5 annotations (arrow for flow, shade for region, dot for point).
Example (Western Ghats): Locate — 1,600 km, Gujarat (Dangs) to Tamil Nadu (Kanyakumari); 3 states (MH, KA, TN) + Kerala (windward). Geomorphic — Faulted edge of Deccan plateau (not true mountains); escarpment 1,000–2,500 m; Anaimudi (2,695 m) highest. Climatic — Orographic barrier; windward >5,000 mm, leeward <600 mm; biodiversity hotspot (325 endemic species). Resource — Laterite/bauxite, hydro (Sharavathy, Koyna), spices/tea/coffee. Strategic — Nilgiri biosphere, Periyar tiger reserve; Gap (Palghat) — only break; UNESCO WHS (39 serial sites).
