Unity in Diversity: The Fabric of Indian Society

1.1 Conceptual Foundations of Unity in Diversity

The phrase “unity in diversity” captures the paradox that a single political entity, the Republic of India, encompasses a multitude of languages, religions, ethnic groups, and cultural practices while maintaining a shared constitutional identity. The Constitution of India, adopted on 26 January 1950, enshrines this principle through the Preamble’s commitment to “fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation”. The linguistic reorganisation of states in 1956, guided by the States Reorganisation Act, gave concrete administrative expression to linguistic diversity, allowing each major language community to govern its own affairs within the federal framework. Religious pluralism is protected by Articles 25 to 28, which guarantee freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practice and propagate religion, while Article 29 safeguards the cultural and educational rights of minorities. These constitutional provisions create a legal architecture that translates abstract diversity into enforceable rights, thereby preventing the fragmentation that unchecked pluralism could cause.

1.2 Linguistic and Religious Mosaic

India recognises 22 languages in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution, and the Census of 2011 recorded more than 19,500 mother tongues, of which 121 are spoken by more than 10,000 people each. This linguistic richness is mirrored by religious diversity: Hindus constitute roughly 79.8 % of the population, Muslims 14.2 %, Christians 2.3 %, Sikhs 1.7 %, Buddhists 0.7 %, Jains 0.4 % and other faiths the remainder. The coexistence of these groups is not merely demographic; it shapes public policy, for instance the provision of personal laws for marriage, divorce and inheritance that differ across religious communities, and the reservation of seats for linguistic minorities in legislative bodies under Article 330 and Article 332. The interplay of language and religion also influences regional political mobilisation, as seen in the demand for separate statehood on linguistic lines (e.g., Telangana in 2014) and the articulation of religious identity in electoral politics.

1.3 Regional Cultural Expressions and National Integration

Cultural expressions such as classical dance forms (Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Odissi), folk traditions (Bhangra, Garba, Bihu), and festivals (Diwali, Eid, Christmas, Pongal) operate simultaneously at the local and national levels. The Ministry of Culture’s “Ek Bharat Shreshtha Bharat” programme, launched in 2015, institutionalises cultural exchange by pairing states and union territories for year‑long people‑to‑people interactions, thereby converting diversity into a tool for integration. Moreover, the National Education Policy 2020 emphasises multilingual education, recommending mother‑tongue instruction up to Grade 5 and the teaching of at least two Indian languages, which reinforces linguistic diversity while fostering a common civic vocabulary. These policies illustrate how the state actively manages diversity, turning potential centrifugal forces into centripetal bonds.

Family and Marriage: Structures, Changes and Legal Framework

2.1 Evolution of Family Forms from Traditional to Modern

The traditional Indian family has been predominantly patrilineal, patrilocal and joint, with the eldest male (karta) exercising authority over property and ritual matters. Colonial legislation such as the Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act 1856 and the Hindu Marriage Act 1955 began to alter these norms by recognising women’s rights to remarry and by codifying conditions for a valid Hindu marriage, including monogamy and minimum age. Post‑independence, the Hindu Succession Act 1956, amended in 2005, granted daughters equal coparcenary rights in ancestral property, fundamentally reshaping intra‑family power dynamics. Simultaneously, urbanisation, migration for employment, and the spread of education have accelerated the transition toward nuclear families, especially in metropolitan areas where housing constraints and labour mobility make large co‑residential units impractical. The National Family Health Survey‑5 (2019‑21) records that the average household size has fallen from 5.3 in 1992‑93 to 4.2, reflecting this structural shift.

2.2 Marriage Practices: Continuity and Reform

Marriage in India remains a socially sanctioned union that often involves families rather than merely individuals. Arranged marriages, mediated by parents, community elders or matrimonial platforms, continue to dominate, though the criteria for matching have expanded from caste and horoscope compatibility to education, profession and lifestyle preferences. The Special Marriage Act 1954 provides a civil framework for inter‑religious and inter‑caste unions, allowing couples to marry without converting to each other’s religion. Judicial interventions have further liberalised marriage norms: the Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, and the 2021 judgment in the “Hadiya” case affirmed an adult woman’s right to choose her spouse irrespective of parental consent. Despite these advances, child marriage persists in certain regions; the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006 sets the minimum age at 18 for women and 21 for men, yet enforcement remains uneven, with the 2021 Census indicating that 23 % of women aged 20‑24 were married before 18.

2.3 Legal Safeguards for Women and Children within the Family

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 (PWDVA) defines domestic violence broadly to include physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and economic abuse, and provides for protection orders, residence orders and monetary relief. The Dowry Prohibition Act 1961, amended in 1984 and 1986, criminalises the giving and receiving of dowry, yet the National Crime Records Bureau reports over 7,000 dowry deaths annually, underscoring implementation gaps. For children, the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act 2015, aligned with the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, establishes a child‑centric justice system and mandates the establishment of Child Welfare Committees in every district. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act 2009 (RTE) guarantees elementary education as a fundamental right, indirectly strengthening the bargaining position of girls within families by delaying marriage and enhancing economic prospects.

Example Question
Explain how the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 transformed property rights of women in a joint Hindu family and discuss its impact on the prevalence of joint family structures in urban India.
The 2005 amendment made daughters coparceners by birth, granting them the same rights and liabilities as sons in ancestral property, including the right to demand partition. This legal equality weakened the economic rationale for maintaining a joint family, because daughters could now claim an independent share, reducing the incentive for families to pool property under a single karta. Empirical studies using NFHS‑5 data show a faster decline in joint‑family households in states with higher female literacy, suggesting that the amendment, combined with education and urban employment, accelerates nuclearisation. However, cultural attachment to joint living persists in many regions, so the structural shift is gradual rather than abrupt.

When a question asks for “impact on family structure”, link the legal change to measurable demographic indicators (household size, joint‑family percentage) and cite the relevant survey (NFHS‑5) to demonstrate evidence‑based reasoning.

Caste System: Historical Evolution, Contemporary Dynamics and Constitutional Safeguards

3.1 Origins and Classical Textual Foundations

The varna framework, articulated in the Rigveda (Purusha Sukta) and later codified in the Dharmashastras such as the Manusmriti (circa 200 BCE–200 CE), divides society into four hierarchical varnas — Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas and Shudras — with a fifth category, the “untouchables” (later termed Dalits), placed outside the varna order. The jati system, comprising thousands of endogamous occupational groups, operationalised varna at the local level, creating a dense lattice of social stratification that regulated marriage, commensality, and ritual purity. Colonial ethnography, notably the 1901 Census under Herbert Risley, attempted to map jatis onto varna categories, thereby rigidifying fluid identities for administrative convenience. This census‑driven classification became the basis for later affirmative‑action policies.

3.2 Constitutional Provisions and Affirmative Action

The Constitution addresses caste‑based inequality through a suite of articles. Article 15(1) prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth, while Article 15(4) and Article 16(4) enable the State to make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs), Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs). The President, under Article 341 and Article 342, notifies the lists of SCs and STs for each state. The Mandal Commission (1979) recommended 27 % reservation for Other Backward Classes (OBCs) in central government jobs, implemented in 1992 after the Supreme Court’s Indra Sawhney v. Union of India judgment, which also introduced the “creamy layer” exclusion for OBCs. The 103rd Constitutional Amendment (2019) added a 10 % reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) among the general category, expanding the affirmative‑action matrix beyond caste.

3.3 Contemporary Caste Dynamics: Mobility, Politics and Identity

Post‑independence land reforms, the Green Revolution (1960s‑70s) and the expansion of public sector employment created avenues for upward mobility among certain OBC groups, notably the Yadavs, Kurmis and Vokkaligas, who leveraged numerical strength to capture political power in states such as Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Karnataka. The rise of caste‑based parties — Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP) in Uttar Pradesh, Rashtriya Janata Dal (RJD) in Bihar, and the Dravidian parties in Tamil Nadu — transformed caste from a ritual hierarchy into a political resource. Simultaneously, Dalit assertion, inspired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar’s vision and the Dalit Panther movement (1970s), has led to the emergence of a vibrant Dalit middle class, though atrocities against Dalits persist; the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989, amended in 2015, provides stringent penalties but conviction rates remain low. The intersection of caste with gender and class produces compounded disadvantage, evident in the lower literacy and higher maternal mortality among Dalit women compared with upper‑caste women.

Tribal Societies: Distribution, Culture and Development Challenges

4.1 Geographic Distribution and Demographic Profile

According to the 2011 Census, Scheduled Tribes constitute 8.6 % of India’s population, numbering approximately 104 million. They are concentrated in the “tribal belt” stretching across central India — Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh — and in the northeastern states where they form a majority in several districts (e.g., Mizoram 94.4 %, Nagaland 86.5 %). The Forest Rights Act 2006 recognises the traditional rights of forest‑dwelling STs over land and minor forest produce, aiming to rectify historical dispossession caused by colonial forest laws (Indian Forest Act 1927) and post‑independence conservation policies.

4.2 Cultural Specificities and Social Organisation

Tribal societies exhibit diverse kinship systems: many central Indian tribes (Gond, Bhil, Santhal) follow clan‑based exogamy with a dormitory system (ghotul) for youth socialisation, while northeastern tribes (Naga, Mizo, Khasi) often practise matrilineal descent (Khasi, Garo) or segmentary lineage organisation. Language families include Austroasiatic (Santali, Mundari), Dravidian (Gondi, Kui), Tibeto‑Burman (Naga languages, Mizo) and Indo‑Aryan (Bhil languages). Traditional governance structures such as the “village council” (panchayat) or “chiefdom” (raja) coexist with the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act 1996 (PESA), which mandates Gram Sabha authority over natural resources, minor forest produce and local dispute resolution in Fifth Schedule areas.

4.3 Development Interventions and Persistent Gaps

Despite targeted schemes — Tribal Sub‑Plan (TSP) since 1974, Vanbandhu Kalyan Yojana (2014), and the Pradhan Mantri Adi Adarsh Gram Yojana (2021) — tribal human development indicators lag behind national averages. The 2019‑21 NFHS‑5 shows tribal literacy at 59 % versus 77 % nationally, infant mortality at 44 per 1,000 live births versus 30, and anaemia prevalence among tribal women at 65 % versus 53 %. Displacement due to mining, dam projects (e.g., Sardar Sarovar, Polavaram) and infrastructure corridors often proceeds without free, prior and informed consent (FPIC), violating both PESA and the Forest Rights Act. The COVID‑19 pandemic exacerbated these vulnerabilities, as tribal communities faced barriers to digital education, healthcare access and livelihood loss, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive, community‑led development planning.

Globalisation and Indian Society: Impacts on Diversity, Family, Caste and Tribe

5.1 Economic Liberalisation and Social Stratification

The 1991 economic reforms opened India to foreign direct investment, multinational corporations and global value chains, creating new occupational categories in information technology, finance and services. This shift generated a professional middle class largely drawn from historically privileged castes (Brahmins, Kayasthas, Banias) who possessed the cultural capital — English fluency, urban networks — to capture high‑skill jobs. Conversely, the informal sector, which employs over 80 % of the workforce, expanded with precarious contract labour, disproportionately absorbing Dalits, Adivasis and women. The resulting “dual economy” deepens caste‑class overlap, as evidenced by the Periodic Labour Force Survey (2022‑23) showing that 45 % of SC workers remain in casual labour compared with 22 % of general‑category workers.

5.2 Media, Technology and Cultural Homogenisation versus Revitalisation

Satellite television, streaming platforms and social media have introduced global cultural norms — individualistic lifestyles, consumerist aspirations, nuclear‑family ideals — into Indian households, accelerating the decline of joint families and altering marriage expectations (e.g., preference for love marriages, delayed childbearing). At the same time, digital platforms enable marginalized communities to archive and disseminate oral traditions, languages and music; the “Adivasi Lives Matter” campaign on Twitter and YouTube channels teaching Santali script illustrate how technology can reinforce identity. The government’s “Digital India” programme, launched in 2015, aims to bridge the digital divide, yet the 2021 Internet and Mobile Association of India report notes that only 34 % of rural ST households have internet access, limiting participatory benefits.

5.3 Policy Responses: Inclusive Growth and Social Protection

Recognising the uneven impact of globalisation, the state has expanded social protection: the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 (MGNREGA) provides 100 days of wage employment, disproportionately benefiting SC/ST households; the National Food Security Act 2013 guarantees subsidised grains to 67 % of the population; and the Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (2018) offers health insurance up to ₹5 lakh per family per year. The 2020‑21 Economic Survey emphasises “inclusive growth” through skill development (Skill India Mission), entrepreneurship (Stand‑Up India for SC/ST/women) and formalisation of the gig economy (Code on Social Security 2020). However, implementation gaps — leakages in MGNREGA, low enrolment in Ayushman Bharat among tribal districts — persist, requiring stronger local governance, community monitoring and data‑driven targeting to translate policy intent into equitable outcomes.

The lesson concludes by emphasising that understanding Indian society demands an integrated lens that connects constitutional design, historical legacies, demographic realities and the forces of globalisation, enabling aspirants to analyse social issues with depth and nuance.

Intersectionality of Caste, Gender and Class in Contemporary Mobility

Social mobility in India cannot be understood through a single axis; the interplay of caste, gender and class creates layered barriers that differ across regions and generations. Empirical work using the India Human Development Survey (IHDS) 2011‑12 and the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2022‑23 shows that Dalit women in rural Bihar experience a 38 percentage‑point gap in regular salaried employment compared with upper‑caste men, while the same gap narrows to 12 points for Dalit men in urban Tamil Nadu. The mechanism operates through three channels: (1) occupational segregation — Dalit women are over‑represented in informal sanitation, domestic work and agricultural wage labour; (2) educational attainment — female literacy among Scheduled Castes (SC) remains 15 points below the national female average, limiting entry into high‑skill sectors; (3) social capital — marriage networks and kinship ties that facilitate job referrals are caste‑endogamous, excluding women from dominant‑caste professional circles. Policy interventions such as the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) and the Mahila Kisan Sashaktikaran Pariyojana (MKSP) target women’s collectives, yet evaluation studies (e.g., World Bank 2020) find that without explicit caste‑disaggregated targeting, benefits accrue disproportionately to relatively better‑off SC households. The 2021 amendment to the Maternity Benefit Act, extending paid leave to 26 weeks for all women, improves formal‑sector retention but does not reach the 89 % of SC women workers in the informal economy. A nuanced answer in the mains must therefore link constitutional guarantees (Art. 15(3), Art. 16(4)) with ground‑level data, showing how intersecting identities produce non‑additive disadvantages.

Legal Evolution of Tribal Land Rights: From Forest Act to FRA and PESA

The trajectory of tribal land tenure reflects a shift from colonial extraction to constitutional recognition. The Indian Forest Act 1927 vested forest land in the state, criminalising traditional shifting cultivation (jhum) and declaring vast tracts “reserved” or “protected”. Post‑independence, the Fifth and Sixth Schedules provided administrative autonomy but left substantive land rights ambiguous. The 1996 Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act (PESA) mandated Gram Sabha consent for land acquisition, minor forest produce (MFP) management and local dispute resolution in Fifth Schedule areas. However, PESA’s implementation remained patchy; many states delayed framing rules, and the Forest Department retained de facto control over MFP. The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act 2006 (FRA) marked a paradigm shift by recognising individual and community forest rights (CFR) over both cultivated and uncultivated land, including rights to protect, regenerate and manage community forest resources. As of March 2024, the Ministry of Tribal Affairs reports 4.2 million titles distributed, covering 13 million hectares, yet 38 % of claims remain pending, with rejection rates highest in Madhya Pradesh and Odisha due to “lack of evidence” and “encroachment” classifications. The Supreme Court’s 2019 order in Wildlife Trust of India v. Union of India upheld the FRA’s primacy over the Forest Conservation Act 1980, clarifying that CFR titles cannot be overridden by wildlife sanctuary notifications without Gram Sabha consent. Nevertheless, the 2022 amendment to the Forest Conservation Rules, allowing “deemed” clearance for strategic projects, re‑introduces tension. Aspirants should be able to trace this chronology, cite the key statutory provisions (Sec. 3(1)(i) FRA, Sec. 4 PESA), and explain how judicial interpretation mediates the conflict between conservation imperatives and tribal autonomy.

Applied Scenario
A question asks: “Explain how the intersection of caste and gender shapes access to formal credit for rural women entrepreneurs. Use data and constitutional provisions.”
Begin by stating the constitutional basis: Art. 15(3) permits special provisions for women; Art. 16(4) enables reservation for backward classes. Cite the 2022‑23 PLFS showing only 9 % of SC women own a bank account linked to a formal credit line versus 27 % of upper‑caste women. Explain the supply‑side barrier: banks use collateral norms that disadvantage women lacking land titles (most SC women are landless). Demand‑side barrier: social norms restrict women’s mobility to bank branches. Mention the Stand‑Up India scheme (2016) which mandates 10 % of loans to SC/ST women, but the 2021 RBI review notes only 3 % utilisation due to awareness gaps. Conclude with a policy prescription: gender‑caste disaggregated credit targets, financial literacy modules in Self‑Help Groups, and legal aid for land titling under the FRA.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question combines multiple social axes, structure the answer in three layers: (1) constitutional/legal framework, (2) quantitative evidence (survey, census, NFHS), (3) mechanism linking the two. Use a table only if the prompt explicitly asks for comparison; otherwise, a concise paragraph with data points scores higher.

Changing Patterns of Kinship and Household Decision‑Making in Urban India

Urbanisation has reconfigured kinship from extended joint families to nuclear and “stem” households, but the transition is neither linear nor uniform. The National Family Health Survey‑5 (2019‑21) records that 68 % of urban households are nuclear, up from 55 % in NFHS‑3 (2005‑06). Yet, “functional jointness” persists: 42 % of nuclear households report daily financial or caregiving exchanges with parental households within the same city. Decision‑making authority has shifted from patriarchal heads to negotiated spousal partnerships, especially among couples where both partners hold salaried jobs. The 2020 Time Use Survey shows urban women spending 4.5 hours daily on unpaid care work versus 1.2 hours for men, a gap that narrows in dual‑earner households where outsourcing (domestic help, crèches) is affordable. Caste continues to mediate kinship norms; dominant‑caste families more frequently maintain joint property (ancestral homes, agricultural land) as a status marker, while SC/ST families, historically landless, rely on wage income and thus adopt nuclear forms earlier. The 2017 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act, granting daughters equal coparcenary rights, has accelerated property partition in urban settings, but implementation surveys (Centre for Social Research 2022) reveal that only 31 % of eligible women have filed mutation claims. For mains, candidates should contrast the “ideal type” of joint family in Dharmashastra texts with the empirically observed “modified extended family” and link changes to labour market structure, housing policy (e.g., Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Urban), and legal reforms.

Impact of Digital Labour Platforms on Informal Caste‑Based Occupations

The rise of platform economy — ride‑hailing, food delivery, gig‑based domestic work — has created new entry points for historically marginalised castes, yet it also reproduces caste hierarchies in algorithmic form. A 2023 study by the Centre for Internet and Society found that 62 % of delivery partners in Bengaluru belong to SC/ST/OBC categories, reflecting low barriers to entry (vehicle ownership, smartphone). However, platform rating systems penalise workers for “customer complaints” that correlate with caste‑based bias; Dalit drivers report 1.8× higher deactivation rates than upper‑caste peers for comparable performance metrics. In domestic‑work platforms (e.g., Urban Company), caste‑segregated task allocation persists: “cleaning” and “sanitation” gigs are disproportionately assigned to Valmiki and Musahar workers, while “beauty” and “wellness” services go to dominant‑caste women. The Code on Social Security 2020 extends gig‑worker registration and accident insurance, but enrolment among SC/ST gig workers remains below 15 % due to documentation hurdles (Aadhaar‑linked bank accounts, PAN). Moreover, algorithmic opacity prevents collective bargaining; the 2021 Supreme Court judgment in Indian Federation of App‑Based Transport Workers v. Union of India recognised gig workers as “unorganised workers” but stopped short of mandating platform transparency. Aspirants must evaluate whether digital platforms constitute a “new informal sector” that deepens caste‑class overlap or a potential avenue for upward mobility, citing both quantitative participation data and qualitative evidence of algorithmic discrimination.

Constitutional Jurisprudence on Affirmative Action: Key Supreme Court Judgments

The Supreme Court has calibrated the scope of reservations through a series of landmark rulings that balance equality (Art. 14) with compensatory justice (Art. 15(4), Art. 16(4)). In State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) the Court struck down caste‑based quotas in educational institutions, prompting the First Amendment (1951) inserting Art. 15(4). Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) upheld 27 % OBC reservation in central services, introduced the “creamy layer” exclusion (income > ₹8 lakh per annum, later revised), and capped total reservations at 50 %. M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006) laid down three conditions for SC/ST promotion quotas: backwardness, inadequacy of representation, and maintenance of administrative efficiency. The 2018 Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta judgment removed the requirement to prove “backwardness” for SC/STs in promotions, affirming that presidential lists under Art. 341/342 are conclusive. In Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) the Court upheld the 10 % Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) quota, ruling that economic criteria alone can justify reservation without violating the 50 % ceiling, provided it does not erode SC/ST/OBC quotas. The 2023 National Commission for Backward Classes v. Union of India case reinforced the NCBC’s constitutional status (Art. 338B) and its advisory role in creamy‑layer revisions. For mains, candidates should be able to trace the doctrinal evolution, quote the operative ratios (e.g., “creamy layer”, “50 % ceiling”, “administrative efficiency”), and apply them to hypothetical policy proposals such as sub‑categorisation of OBCs or extension of reservations to private sector.

  • Confusing the creamy‑layer test for OBCs with the income threshold for EWS; they operate under different constitutional provisions.
  • Assuming PESA automatically guarantees Gram Sabha veto over all mining projects; the Forest Conservation Act can override without consent in “strategic” projects.
  • Treating nuclear family prevalence as evidence of joint family disappearance; functional jointness and financial interdependence often persist.
  • Citing FRA title distribution figures without mentioning the high rejection rate and pending claims, which distorts the implementation picture.
  • Attributing gig‑worker caste disparities solely to worker choice, ignoring algorithmic rating bias documented in independent audits.
  • Overlooking the 2022 EWS judgment’s caveat that the 50 % ceiling applies only to caste‑based quotas, not to economic quotas.

Sub‑categorisation of OBCs: Rohini Commission Findings and State Implementation

The Rohini Commission was constituted in October 2017 under Article 340 to examine the feasibility of sub‑categorising the Other Backward Classes (OBC) central list into narrower groups based on relative backwardness. The Commission submitted its report in July 2023 after analysing data from the Socio‑Economic and Caste Census (SECC) 2011, the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) employment‑unemployment rounds, and state‑level backwardness indices. Its key recommendation was a three‑tier classification — “Most Backward”, “Backward”, and “Less Backward” — with a proposed redistribution of the 27 % central OBC quota in proportion to each tier’s population share and deprivation indicators.

At the state level, several governments have already experimented with sub‑categorisation. Bihar introduced a two‑category split (Annexure‑I and Annexure‑II) in 2018, allocating 18 % of the state OBC quota to the more deprived Annexure‑I castes. Tamil Nadu’s 69 % reservation framework (protected by the Ninth Schedule) internally divides OBCs into “Backward Classes” and “Most Backward Classes” with separate quotas since the 1990s. Karnataka’s 2021 order created a “Category‑2A” for the most backward OBC groups, earmarking 4 % of the state quota. These experiments demonstrate administrative feasibility but also reveal political contestation: dominant OBC castes often resist dilution of their share, leading to litigation in state high courts.

The Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) held that sub‑categorisation is constitutionally permissible provided it is based on “adequate data” and does not breach the 50 % ceiling. The 2022 Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India judgment, while upholding the 10 % EWS quota, reiterated that any further fragmentation of OBC quotas must satisfy the “creamy layer” test and the “quantifiable data” standard. As of 2024, the central government has not enacted a legislative framework for national sub‑categorisation; the matter remains under inter‑ministerial consultation, with the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) tasked to validate the Commission’s methodology.

For mains, aspirants should be able to contrast the Rohini Commission’s data‑driven approach with the politically negotiated state models, cite the constitutional provisions (Art. 15(4), Art. 16(4), Art. 340), and evaluate the tension between “equality of opportunity” and “equitable redistribution” within the OBC category.

Caste and the Formal Labour Market: Public Sector Recruitment, Private Sector Diversity Charters, and the Merit Debate

Public sector employment remains the primary arena where constitutional reservation operates at scale. As per the Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) annual report 2022‑23, Scheduled Castes (SC) constitute 15.2 % of central government posts, Scheduled Tribes (ST) 7.4 %, and OBCs 27.0 % — closely matching the mandated percentages. However, representation thins sharply at higher pay‑levels (Group A and B), where SC/ST share drops below 8 % and 3 % respectively, prompting the 2006 M. Nagaraj v. Union of India conditions for promotion quotas: backwardness, inadequacy of representation, and administrative efficiency.

In the private sector, no statutory reservation exists. The 2019 “Merit vs Reservation” debate intensified after the Confederation of Indian Industry (CII) launched the “India Inclusion Charter”, a voluntary pledge by over 150 firms to adopt diversity targets, conduct caste‑sensitisation workshops, and publish annual inclusion reports. NASSCOM’s “Diversity and Inclusion Framework” (2021) recommends a 10 % hiring goal for SC/ST/OBC candidates in technology roles, but compliance is self‑reported and unverified. A 2022 study by the Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy (CMIE) found that SC/ST professionals accounted for only 4.3 % of the organised private‑sector workforce, compared with 22 % in the public sector.

The “merit” argument frequently invokes Article 16(2) — “no citizen shall be ineligible for any office under the State on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence” — to claim that caste‑based preferences violate formal equality. The Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney clarified that “merit” must be understood in the context of substantive equality, allowing compensatory measures. The 2022 Janhit Abhiyan judgment extended this logic to the EWS quota, affirming that economic criteria can constitute a legitimate classification without breaching the 50 % ceiling, provided it does not erode existing SC/ST/OBC quotas.

Policy analysts argue that a hybrid model — mandatory reservation in public employment combined with incentivised diversity reporting, tax benefits, and procurement preferences for inclusive private firms — could bridge the representation gap without legislative compulsion. The 2023 “National Commission for Backward Classes v. Union of India” ruling reinforced NCBC’s advisory role in revising the creamy‑layer threshold, which indirectly influences private‑sector eligibility for government‑linked contracts.

Technique: Comparative Table for Reservation Regimes
Compare the constitutional basis, coverage, and enforcement mechanisms of reservation in public sector employment versus voluntary diversity initiatives in the private sector.
Public Sector: Constitutional mandate under Art. 15(4) and Art. 16(4); fixed percentages (SC 15 %, ST 7.5 %, OBC 27 %); enforced through DoPT recruitment rules, roster system, and judicial review (e.g., M. Nagaraj conditions for promotions). Private Sector: No constitutional mandate; voluntary charters (CII Inclusion Charter, NASSCOM D&I Framework); targets are aspirational (e.g., 10 % SC/ST/OBC hiring); enforcement relies on self‑reporting, ESG ratings, and procurement preferences; no legal sanction for non‑compliance. Key Distinction: Public sector reservation is justiciable and quota‑based; private sector diversity is market‑driven and metric‑based.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to “critically examine” reservation in the private sector, structure the answer in three layers: (1) constitutional silence and the Indra Sawhney “merit” interpretation, (2) current voluntary frameworks with quantitative gaps, (3) policy options — legislative mandate, incentive‑based compliance, or status‑quo — each evaluated against administrative feasibility and political acceptability.

Tribal Health Systems: Epidemiological Profile, Access Barriers, and the National Health Mission’s Tribal Sub‑Plan

India’s tribal population, recorded at 104 million in Census 2011 (8.6 % of total), bears a disproportionate disease burden. The National Family Health Survey‑5 (2019‑21) reports stunting at 40.9 % among tribal children under five, compared with 35.5 % nationally; under‑five mortality stands at 57 per 1,000 live births versus 42 nationally. Endemic malaria accounts for over 50 % of national cases despite tribal districts covering only 8 % of land area. Sickle cell disease prevalence reaches 20‑30 % in certain central Indian belts (e.g., Gond, Bhil communities), and tuberculosis notification rates are 1.5 times the national average.

Access barriers are structural: 38 % of tribal habitations lack a functional Primary Health Centre (PHC) within 5 km; language divergence limits health‑communication effectiveness; cultural practices such as home births and traditional healers delay formal care-seeking. The National Health Mission (NHM) launched the Tribal Sub‑Plan (TSP) in 2013, earmarking 8.6 % of the NHM budget — proportional to tribal population share — for dedicated infrastructure, mobile medical units, and tribal‑specific health‑worker cadres. As of 2022‑23, 12,000 Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) were deployed in tribal blocks, achieving a ratio of 1 ASHA per 1,000 tribal population, yet vacancy rates exceed 25 % in states like Odisha and Chhattisgarh.

The 2021 “Tribal Health Action Plan” introduced community‑based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) and integrated vector‑control strategies co‑designed with Gram Sabhas. The 2023 “National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission” targets screening of 7 crore tribal individuals by 2026, leveraging point‑of‑care diagnostics at Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness Centres. Early evaluations from pilot districts in Madhya Pradesh show a 12 % reduction in severe anaemia prevalence after two rounds of iron‑folic acid supplementation combined with dietary counselling.

For answer writing, candidates should link epidemiological data to governance gaps, cite the TSP’s budgetary architecture, and assess the effectiveness of community‑owned models (Gram Sabha‑led health planning) versus top‑down vertical programmes.

Marriage Market Digitalisation: Matrimonial Platforms, Caste Filters, and Changing Dowry Practices

Online matrimonial platforms — Shaadi.com (launched 1996), BharatMatrimony (2000), Jeevansathi (2004) — now mediate an estimated 60 % of urban arranged marriages. A 2021 Centre for Social Research survey of 4,500 users across six metros found that 68 % actively used caste‑based filters, and 42 % reported that “caste compatibility” was the primary shortlisting criterion, ahead of education (31 %) and profession (19 %). Platform algorithms default to caste‑sorted results unless the user explicitly disables the filter, reinforcing endogamy at scale.

Dowry, prohibited under the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961, persists in digital negotiations. The same survey recorded that 27 % of female respondents experienced explicit dowry demands communicated through platform chat features, while 15 % reported “gift expectations” framed as “voluntary contributions”. The 2020 “Matrimonial Sites (Regulation) Bill” proposed mandatory verification of user identities, prohibition of caste‑based filtering, and a grievance redressal mechanism; it lapsed with the dissolution of the 17th Lok Sabha. Meanwhile, the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 and Section 498A IPC remain the primary legal recourses, though conviction rates remain below 10 %.

NFHS‑5 data indicate that 27.3 % of ever‑married women aged 18‑49 reported having faced dowry demand, with higher incidence among OBC (30.1 %) and SC (28.7 %) groups. Digital platforms have also enabled “hyper‑gamy” — women marrying into higher‑status castes — which sometimes reduces dowry pressure but introduces new power asymmetries. Emerging “caste‑neutral” niche platforms (e.g., “LoveVivah”, “Betterhalf”) claim algorithmic matching based on values and personality, yet their market share remains under 3 %.

Examiners expect candidates to discuss the dual role of technology: as a reinforcer of traditional caste endogamy through default filters, and as a potential disruptor if regulatory frameworks mandate transparency, caste‑blind design, and dowry‑reporting mechanisms.

Internal Migration and Caste Network Reconfiguration in Mega‑Cities: Remittances, Housing, and Political Mobilisation

Census 2011 recorded 450 million internal migrants, with the dominant corridors being Uttar Pradesh and Bihar to Maharashtra, Delhi, Gujarat, and Karnataka. The 2022 World Bank Migration and Development Brief estimated intra‑India remittances at USD 87 billion, of which a substantial share originates from short‑term circular migrants in construction, domestic work, and textile clusters. Caste networks operate as labour‑market intermediaries: “jati‑based” contractors recruit workers from their own sub‑castes, ensuring trust but also reproducing occupational segregation — Valmiki and Musahar migrants dominate sanitation and waste‑picking in Delhi and Mumbai, while Yadav and Kurmi networks supply dairy and transport labour in Gujarat.

Housing outcomes reflect caste clustering. The 2017‑18 National Sample Survey (NSS) 76th round shows that 42 % of migrant households in Mumbai’s slums belong to a single dominant caste group, facilitating collective bargaining for water and electricity but also limiting spatial mobility. The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Urban (PMAY‑U) has sanctioned 1.12 crore houses as of March 2024; however, only 18 % of beneficiaries in metro slums are from SC/ST categories, partly because land‑title documentation — often absent in informal settlements — is a prerequisite.

Political mobilisation leverages these networks. The Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP) established “migrant cells” in Delhi and Mumbai after 2014, targeting Dalit voters from Uttar Pradesh. The Aam Aadmi Party (AAP) introduced “Mohalla Clinics” and free water schemes that disproportionately benefit migrant‑dense colonies, translating into electoral gains in the 2020 Delhi assembly elections. The Inter‑State Migrant Workmen Act 1979, subsumed under the Code on Social Security 2020, now mandates registration of inter‑state migrants for portability of provident fund and insurance benefits, yet enrolment remains below 30 % due to employer non‑compliance and lack of awareness.

For mains, aspirants should integrate migration data, caste‑based labour intermediation, housing policy gaps, and emerging political strategies, illustrating how internal migration reshapes both the economic and democratic landscape of Indian mega‑cities.

  • Assuming sub‑categorisation of OBCs automatically reduces the creamy‑layer threshold; the two operate under separate constitutional provisions.
  • Equating voluntary private‑sector diversity charters with legally enforceable reservation; they lack justiciability and sanction mechanisms.
  • Citing tribal health improvements solely from NHM budget allocations without referencing persistent ASHA vacancies and cultural access barriers.
  • Treating matrimonial platform caste filters as a neutral technological feature; they are default design choices that reinforce endogamy.
  • Overlooking the requirement of land‑title documentation in PMAY‑U, which excludes most migrant slum dwellers from housing benefits.

  • Attributing political mobilisation of migrants exclusively to caste identity, ignoring class‑based issues such as wage theft and housing insecurity.
