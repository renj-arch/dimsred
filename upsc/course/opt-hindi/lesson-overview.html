1. Overview of the Hindi Literature Optional and Paper Structure

1.1 Position of Hindi in the Eighth Schedule and its Script

Hindi occupies a central place among the languages listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution since its inception in 1950, and it is written in the Devanagari script which provides a phonetic representation that has remained largely stable since the medieval period. The constitutional recognition guarantees that the language can be used for official communication, education and literary production, and it also means that the optional paper can draw upon a vast body of state‑supported scholarship, university curricula and Sahitya Akademi publications. Because the script is shared with several sister languages such as Marathi and Nepali, candidates must be careful to distinguish Hindi‑specific orthographic conventions, especially the use of the nukta for Perso‑Arabic sounds, when discussing linguistic development in Paper I.

1.2 Distribution of Marks and Question Pattern Across the Two Papers

Each paper carries 250 marks and the examination expects a mixture of long‑answer questions, short‑note questions and critical explanations of prescribed passages. Paper I is oriented towards the history of the language, the evolution of literary movements and the identification of representative authors and works across the four traditional periods, while Paper II concentrates on close reading of the prescribed texts, the application of various critical frameworks and the ability to produce coherent paraphrase, contextual explanation and evaluative commentary. The mark allocation typically rewards answers that demonstrate a clear chronological framework in Paper I and a structured critical apparatus in Paper II, so candidates should allocate their preparation time proportionally to these distinct demands.

1.3 Rationale for Choosing Hindi Literature as an Optional Subject

Choosing Hindi Literature offers the advantage of a relatively compact syllabus that is deeply rooted in Indian cultural history, allowing aspirants to leverage their familiarity with the language, its script and its major literary figures. The optional also overlaps with General Studies papers on Indian culture, medieval history and modern social movements, creating synergy that can reduce overall study load. Moreover, the availability of high‑quality Hindi‑medium reference works, critical editions and digital archives makes it possible to build a self‑contained preparation strategy without relying on translations that may obscure original nuances.

2. Detailed Syllabus Map for Paper I

2.1 Historical Development of the Hindi Language from Apabhramsa to Modern Standard Hindi

The language component of Paper I begins with the Apabhramsa stage (circa 7th–12th centuries) where the phonological and morphological foundations of early Hindi dialects such as Braj, Awadhi and Khari Boli can be identified. The transition from Apabhramsa to Old Hindi is marked by the loss of final short vowels, the simplification of consonant clusters and the emergence of the characteristic schwa deletion rule that distinguishes modern Hindi pronunciation. During the Sultanate and Mughal periods, contact with Persian and Arabic introduced a substantial lexical layer, especially in administrative, military and poetic vocabulary, which later became a point of debate in the standardisation movement of the 19th century. The Fort William College initiative (1800) and the subsequent work of scholars like John Gilchrist and Lallu Lal played a decisive role in codifying Khari Boli as the basis of Modern Standard Hindi, a process that was reinforced by the adoption of the Devanagari script for official use in the North‑Western Provinces. Understanding these stages is essential because questions often ask candidates to explain how specific sound changes, lexical borrowings or script reforms shaped the literary language of a given period.

2.2 Major Literary Movements and Their Socio‑Cultural Contexts

The syllabus requires a clear grasp of the four major movements: the early heroic and devotional poetry of Adikal, the bhakti surge of Bhaktikal, the courtly ornate tradition of Ritikal, and the modern reformist, romantic, progressive and contemporary currents of Adhunik Kal. Adikal (11th–14th century) reflects a feudal milieu where court poets such as Chand Bardai composed Prithviraj Raso in a language that blends Apabhramsa with early Rajasthani features, while Amir Khusrau experimented with Hindavi, a precursor of Urdu‑Hindi, in his riddles and folk songs. Bhaktikal (14th–17th century) is characterised by the nirguna (formless) and saguna (with form) streams; Kabir’s couplets articulate a radical monotheism that transcends caste, Surdas and Mirabai express intense personal devotion to Krishna, and Tulsidas’s Ramcharitmanas becomes a cultural touchstone for the Hindi heartland. Ritikal (17th–19th century) shifts patronage to Rajput and Mughal courts, producing highly stylised poetry governed by the conventions of alankara shastra; Keshavdas’s Rasikapriya, Bihari’s Satsai and Ghananand’s lyrical verses exemplify the emphasis on rhetorical figures, erotic symbolism and the aesthetics of shringara. Adhunik Kal (19th century onward) witnesses the impact of colonial education, the printing press and nationalist discourse; Bharatendu Harishchandra initiates modern Hindi prose and drama, Premchand establishes the realistic short story and novel, the Chhayavad poets (Jaishankar Prasad, Suryakant Tripathi Nirala, Sumitranandan Pant, Mahadevi Verma) introduce lyrical romanticism, the Progressive Writers’ Association brings Marxist realism, and post‑independence voices explore Dalit, feminist and experimental forms. Each movement must be linked to its patronage system, linguistic register and ideological underpinnings because examiners reward answers that demonstrate cause‑effect reasoning rather than mere listing.

2.3 Representative Authors, Canonical Works and Periodisation

A period‑wise answer in Paper I is expected to name at least two major authors per period together with one seminal work that illustrates the period’s defining features. For Adikal, Chand Bardai’s Prithviraj Raso and Amir Khusrau’s Khaliq‑e‑Bari are standard references; for Bhaktikal, Kabir’s Bijak, Surdas’s Sur Sagar, Tulsidas’s Ramcharitmanas and Mirabai’s bhajans constitute the core; for Ritikal, Keshavdas’s Rasikapriya, Bihari’s Satsai and Ghananand’s Sujan Rasa are indispensable; for Adhunik Kal, Bharatendu’s Andher Nagari, Premchand’s Godaan, Prasad’s Kamayani, Nirala’s Ram Ki Shakti Puja, and the short stories of Yashpal and Mannu Bhandari represent the progressive and contemporary strands. Candidates should also be aware of the dialectal affiliations of these authors — Braj for Surdas, Awadhi for Tulsidas, Khari Boli for Premchand — because the syllabus explicitly asks for an understanding of dialect variation and its role in literary production. A well‑structured answer will present a chronological table in prose, follow it with a paragraph on the dominant movement, and conclude with a concise author‑work matrix that demonstrates both breadth and depth.

Solved Example: Structuring a Paper I Answer on Periodisation
Question: Explain the four major periods of Hindi literature with their defining characteristics and key figures.
Answer: The answer should begin with a chronological framework, naming Adikal (c. 11th–14th century) characterised by heroic and devotional verse of Chand Bardai and Amir Khusrau; followed by Bhaktikal (c. 14th–17th century) marked by the nirguna and saguna bhakti streams of Kabir, Surdas, Tulsidas and Mirabai; then Ritikal (c. 17th–19th century) distinguished by courtly ornate poetry of Keshavdas, Bihari and Ghananand; and finally Adhunik Kal (19th century onward) encompassing Bharatendu Harishchandra, Premchand, the Chhayavad poets, progressive writers and contemporary voices. Each period must be linked to its linguistic features, patronage systems and thematic concerns, and the answer should conclude with a brief note on the continuity and rupture between periods.
Exam‑Day Insight for Paper I
When the question asks for a period‑wise survey, allocate the first paragraph to a clear chronological table in prose, the second to the dominant literary movements, and the third to representative authors with one illustrative work each; this three‑layer structure mirrors the examiner’s marking rubric and prevents omission of the language‑development component.

3. Detailed Syllabus Map for Paper II

3.1 Prescribed Texts and the Requirement of Close Reading

Paper II prescribes a set of canonical texts that span the entire historical trajectory, including selections from the Ramcharitmanas, the Bijak, the Satsai, Kamayani, Godaan, and representative short stories and poems from the progressive and contemporary periods. The examination expects candidates to demonstrate close reading by identifying the immediate context of a passage, explaining its thematic significance, analysing its formal features such as metre, rhyme, imagery and rhetorical devices, and finally offering a critical evaluation that situates the excerpt within the author’s oeuvre and the broader movement. Because the prescribed list is relatively stable, aspirants should prepare a detailed annotation for each text, noting recurring motifs, linguistic peculiarities and intertextual references, so that any unseen passage can be tackled with confidence.

3.2 Schools of Literary Criticism Relevant to Hindi Literature

The syllabus explicitly mentions traditional Indian poetics (rasa, dhvani, alankara), nationalist criticism (as articulated by Ramchandra Shukla and Hazari Prasad Dwivedi), Marxist and progressive criticism (associated with the Progressive Writers’ Association and critics like Namvar Singh), and newer approaches such as feminist and Dalit literary theory (exemplified by the works of Sharankumar Limbale and the criticism of Gopal Guru). A candidate must be able to apply at least two of these frameworks to a given text, showing how the same passage can yield different meanings when read through the lens of aesthetic enjoyment versus socio‑economic critique. The ability to compare and contrast these schools — for instance, noting that rasa theory foregrounds the emotive experience of the reader while Marxist criticism foregrounds class relations — is a hallmark of high‑scoring answers.

3.3 Explanation, Paraphrase and Critical Evaluation Techniques

Answers in Paper II are typically structured in three stages: first, a concise paraphrase that restates the passage in the candidate’s own words without losing the original nuance; second, a contextual explanation that identifies the speaker, the situation, the work’s broader narrative and the relevant literary movement; third, a critical evaluation that discusses the passage’s artistic merit, its ideological stance and its place in the evolution of Hindi literature. Effective paraphrase avoids literal translation and instead captures the tone and register, while critical evaluation should reference specific critical concepts — such as the use of viraha in bhakti poetry or the deployment of stream‑of‑consciousness in Nirmal Verma’s stories — and support them with short, accurately quoted phrases from the original Hindi text, accompanied by an English gloss where necessary.

4. Core Reference Works and Bibliography

4.1 Foundational Histories and Encyclopaedias

The most authoritative single‑volume history remains Ramchandra Shukla’s Hindi Sahitya Ka Itihas, which provides a period‑wise narrative grounded in textual evidence and critical commentary. Complementing this, the Sahitya Akademi’s multi‑volume Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature offers entries on every major author, work and movement with bibliographic leads. For a more recent scholarly perspective, the volumes edited by K. C. Bahl and the Hindi Sahitya Sammelan publications incorporate post‑independence research on Dalit and feminist writings. Candidates should treat these works as primary reference points and supplement them with journal articles from the Indian Journal of Hindi Studies and the Journal of the Asiatic Society.

4.2 Critical Editions, Translations and Journals

Critical editions published by the Nagari Pracharini Sabha, the Kendriya Hindi Sansthan and the Sahitya Akademi provide authenticated texts with variant readings, introductory essays and glossaries that are indispensable for Paper II passage work. While translations can aid comprehension, the examination rewards direct engagement with the Hindi original; therefore, candidates should use translations only as a temporary aid and always cite the Hindi phrase when quoting. Periodicals such as Alochana, Hans and Naya Path regularly publish critical essays that model the analytical depth expected in the mains.

4.3 Digital Repositories and University Syllabi

The Digital Library of India, the National Digital Library and the e‑GyanKosh portal host scanned copies of rare manuscripts, early printed books and scholarly monographs that are otherwise difficult to access. Many university departments (for example, the Department of Hindi at Delhi University and the Hindi Department at Banaras Hindu University) publish their detailed syllabi and recommended reading lists online; these documents reveal the current academic emphasis on certain authors or critical theories and can guide the aspirant’s selection of supplementary material. Regularly checking these repositories ensures that the preparation remains aligned with the latest scholarly trends.

5. Study Plan and Answer Writing Strategy

5.1 Long‑Term Timeline Covering All Periods and Movements

A realistic long‑term plan spans six months before the mains, dividing the syllabus into four monthly blocks: the first month for Adikal and Bhaktikal language history and major texts, the second month for Ritikal and the transition to modern Hindi, the third month for Adhunik Kal up to the Progressive Writers’ Movement, and the fourth month for post‑independence literature, critical theories and intensive answer writing. The fifth month should be reserved for full‑length mock tests under timed conditions, and the final month for revision of weak areas, memorisation of key quotations and refinement of the three‑layer answer structure. This phased approach prevents the common pitfall of neglecting the language‑development portion of Paper I while over‑concentrating on literary criticism.

5.2 Weekly Rotation Between Language History, Textual Analysis and Criticism

Within each month, a weekly rotation ensures balanced coverage: allocate two days to reading and annotating the prescribed texts, two days to studying the relevant critical school and writing short analytical notes, one day to language‑history notes (sound changes, script reforms, dialect maps), one day to solving previous years’ questions and one day to rest or light review. The rotation should be flexible enough to accommodate the density of certain periods — for instance, Bhaktikal may require an extra day for the multiple bhakti streams — but the overall rhythm must be maintained to avoid last‑minute cramming.

5.3 Practice of Structured Answers with Quotation Integration

Answer writing practice should follow the explicit structure demanded by each paper: for Paper I, adopt the chronological‑movement‑author matrix; for Paper II, follow the paraphrase‑context‑evaluation template. While practising, insert at least two short Hindi quotations per answer, each accompanied by a precise English gloss, and ensure that the quotations are drawn from the original text rather than a translation. After each practice session, self‑evaluate using a checklist that verifies the presence of a clear thesis statement, logical paragraph transitions, correct periodisation, appropriate critical terminology and a concluding sentence that links the analysis back to the question. Consistent application of this method builds the muscle memory needed to produce coherent, high‑scoring answers under examination pressure.

Phonological Shifts from Apabhramsa to Khari Boli and Their Orthographic Consequences

The transition from Apabhramsa to early Khari Boli is marked by a cluster of systematic sound changes that reshaped both pronunciation and the written form. The most consequential shift is the loss of the final short a in masculine nouns and adjectives, turning Apabhramsa rāja into Khari Boli rāj. This apocope triggered a compensatory lengthening of the preceding vowel in many dialects, a phenomenon documented in the Prākṛta Prakāśa and later confirmed by comparative work on the Śaurasenī and Māgadhī branches. A second major change is the merger of the Old Indo‑Aryan voiced aspirates bh, dh, gh into their unaspirated counterparts b, d, g in the western Hindi area, while the eastern dialects retained aspiration longer. The third shift involves the simplification of consonant clusters: kṣ > kh, jñ > gy, and ṣṭ > ṭ. These cluster reductions are directly visible in the orthography of the Devanāgarī manuscripts from the 12th‑14th centuries, where scribes began to write kṣetra as khetra and jñāna as gyān.

Orthographic Standardisation in the 19th Century

The Fort William College project (1800‑1830) and the later Nagari Pracharini Sabha (1893) codified a spelling system that attempted to reflect the phonology of Khari Boli while preserving etymological distinctions for Sanskrit‑derived vocabulary. The decision to retain the anusvāra for nasalisation before consonants, but to write the candrabindu for vowel nasalisation, created a dual notation that still appears in modern textbooks. The 1958 Government of India script reform (the “Hindi Orthography Rules”) further regularised the use of halant for conjuncts and mandated the explicit writing of the inherent a in word‑final position for verbs (e.g., karatā vs. karat). Understanding these layers is essential for Paper I questions that ask candidates to trace a given word form through its historical stages and to justify the modern spelling with reference to the underlying sound law.

Implications for Textual Criticism

When editing a Bhakti poem, the editor must decide whether a manuscript reading rām reflects the apocopated Khari Boli form or a scribal omission of the final a. The principle of lectio difficilior often favours the longer form if the manuscript belongs to an eastern recension where final a persisted. Conversely, a western manuscript showing rām is likely transmitting the genuine Khari Boli pronunciation. This interplay between phonology and manuscript geography is a recurring theme in the critical apparatus of the Nagari Pracharini Sabha editions.

Manuscript Traditions and Textual Criticism in Bhakti Poetry

The corpus of Bhakti poetry survives in three principal manuscript families: the pothī tradition of the Ramanandi sect, the bāṇī collections of the Sikh Gurus (which overlap with Sant poetry), and the granth manuscripts preserved in the temples of Vrindavan and Nathdwara. Each family exhibits distinct scribal habits. Ramanandi pothīs tend to regularise the language toward Braj Bhasha, inserting au for o and ai for e, while the Vrindavan granths retain more archaic Apabhramsa forms such as jāu for jāo. The Sikh bāṇī manuscripts, compiled under the supervision of Guru Arjan (1604), display a deliberate standardisation toward a liturgical register that blends Punjabi, Braj, and Khari Boli.

Stemmatic Reconstruction and the Problem of Oral Transmission

Because many Bhakti poems circulated orally for generations before being written down, a strict stemmatic approach (as used for classical Sanskrit texts) often fails. Scholars such as Charlotte Vaudeville and later Karine Schomer have advocated a performance‑based model: the text is treated as a fluid repertoire where each performance introduces formulaic variations. In practice, the critical editor constructs a base text from the earliest dated manuscript (often a 16th‑century pothī) and records variant readings in an apparatus that distinguishes orthographic variants (e.g., śrī vs. sri) from lexical variants (e.g., prem vs. prīt). The apparatus also notes interpolations — verses added by later devotees to align the poem with a particular theological stance.

Key Editorial Principles for the Examination

For Paper II passage‑explanation questions, the examiner expects the candidate to identify the manuscript source of the quoted lines, comment on any significant variant, and explain how the variant affects meaning. A typical answer structure: (1) cite the edition (e.g., “Nagari Pracharini Sabha, 1972, p. 112”), (2) note the variant (“MS A reads bhāv where MS B has bhāva”), (3) analyse the semantic shift (“bhāv suggests a transient emotion, bhāva a settled disposition”), and (4) relate the choice to the poet’s theological intent. Mastery of this four‑step method distinguishes high‑scoring scripts from descriptive summaries.

Passage‑Explanation Worked Example
Explain the following lines from Kabir’s Bijak (Sakhi 12) with reference to manuscript variation: “jāte ho kāhe ko bair, apnā hī man māṅge”.
Step 1 – Edition citation: The critical edition by Shyam Sundar Das (Nagari Pracharini Sabha, 1968, p. 45) prints the line as above. Step 2 – Variant: Manuscript R (Ramanandi pothī, 1620) reads apnā hī man māṅgai (subjunctive), while Manuscript V (Vrindavan granth, 1585) has apnā hī man māṅge (imperative). Step 3 – Semantic analysis: The subjunctive māṅgai conveys a wish (“may my own mind ask”), softening the rebuke; the imperative māṅge turns the line into a direct command (“let your own mind ask”), sharpening Kabir’s critique of hypocrisy. Step 4 – Theological implication: The imperative aligns with Kabir’s nirguṇa stance that the self must interrogate itself without external mediation. The examiner rewards the candidate who links the grammatical mood to the poet’s doctrinal emphasis.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Textual Criticism
When a passage question appears, spend the first two minutes identifying the edition and any noted variant. Write the edition name in the margin; this signals to the examiner that you are working from the critical text. Then draft the four‑step analysis in bullet form before expanding into paragraphs. This prevents omission of the variant‑discussion component, which carries a disproportionate share of marks.

Institutional History of the Progressive Writers’ Association and Its Impact on Canon Formation

The All India Progressive Writers’ Association (AIPWA) was inaugurated at the Lucknow conference of 1936, with a manifesto drafted by Sajjad Zaheer and endorsed by Premchand, who presided. The association’s institutional architecture comprised a central executive, provincial branches, and a network of journals — Naya Sahitya (Delhi), Hans (Allahabad), and Naya Path (Patna). These periodicals functioned as the primary gatekeepers for publication, review, and debate, effectively shaping the modern Hindi canon by promoting writers who adhered to the Marxist‑realist aesthetic. The 1943 Bombay conference expanded the scope to include folk forms and regional languages, leading to the inclusion of authors such as Nagarjun (Maithili‑Hindi bilingual) and Phanishwar Nath Renu (whose Maila Anchal was first serialized in Hans).

State Response and the Cold War Fracture

After independence, the Indian state’s cultural policy oscillated between patronage and surveillance. The Sahitya Akademi (established 1954) awarded fellowships to several AIPWA members, yet the 1956 “Communist Party” ban and the subsequent split in the Communist Party of India (1964) caused a parallel fracture in the writers’ body. The pro‑Soviet faction retained control of Naya Sahitya, while the breakaway group launched Pragati (1965). This division is reflected in the syllabus: Paper I expects candidates to discuss the “Progressive Writers’ Movement” as a unified phenomenon up to 1955, and then to analyse the post‑1964 diversification into “Marxist”, “Humanist”, and “Dalit” streams. Understanding the institutional split prevents the common error of treating the movement as monolithic throughout the post‑independence period.

Canon‑Formation Mechanisms

Three mechanisms operated simultaneously: (1) Anthologisation — the 1957 Pragatisheel Sahitya Sankalan (edited by Namwar Singh) selected stories and poems that exemplified “social realism”; (2) Curricular adoption — university Hindi departments (Delhi, Banaras, Aligarh) incorporated AIPWA texts into MA syllabi, ensuring generational transmission; (3) Critical endorsement — the journal Alochana (founded 1958) published theoretical essays that legitimised the movement’s aesthetic categories (e.g., “people’s language”, “class consciousness”). A candidate who can trace a specific work — say, Yashpal’s Jhutha Sach — through these three channels demonstrates the depth of institutional awareness the examiner rewards.

Intersections of Marxist, Feminist, and Dalit Criticism in Contemporary Hindi Scholarship

Since the 1970s, Hindi literary criticism has moved beyond the single‑lens Marxist framework that dominated the Progressive era. Three intersecting schools now shape scholarly discourse: (1) Marxist‑structuralist criticism, represented by Namwar Singh’s Kavita Ke Naye Pratiman (1972), which applies concepts of base‑superstructure and ideology to poetic form; (2) Feminist criticism, pioneered by scholars such as Malashri Lal and later by Rajni Palriwala, who foreground gendered language, the domestic sphere, and the representation of women in both classical and modern texts; (3) Dalit criticism, articulated by Omprakash Valmiki, Shyam Sundar Das (in his later work), and the journal Dalit Sahitya, which insists on the epistemic authority of lived caste experience and challenges the “universal humanism” of earlier Marxist readings.

Methodological Overlaps and Tensions

Marxist and Dalit critics share a focus on material conditions, but Dalit scholars argue that class analysis alone erases caste‑specific oppression. Feminist critics intersect with both by exposing how patriarchal structures operate within class and caste hierarchies. A concrete example is the re‑reading of Premchand’s Godān: a Marxist reading highlights the peasant‑landlord conflict; a Dalit reading foregrounds the marginalisation of the Chamar character, Hori’s friend; a feminist reading interrogates the silence of Dhania’s agency. The examiner often sets a question that asks the candidate to “evaluate Godān from two critical perspectives”. A high‑scoring answer will not merely list perspectives but will show how each perspective selects different textual evidence and how the perspectives dialogue — or clash — on the same passage.

Key Terminology for Paper II

Candidates must be fluent in terms such as hegemony (Gramsci), subaltern (Guha/Spivak), intersectionality (Crenshaw, adopted in Hindi as antarsambandh), and counter‑canon (pratikanon). These terms appear in the critical essays prescribed for Paper II (e.g., the “Critical Theory” section of the syllabus). Using them precisely — rather than as decorative jargon — signals that the candidate engages with the scholarly conversation rather than reproducing textbook summaries.

Strategic Integration of Linguistic Data and Critical Theory in Mains Answers

The highest‑scoring answers in both papers demonstrate a seamless blend of linguistic evidence (sound changes, dialect maps, manuscript variants) and critical theory (Marxist, feminist, Dalit). The integration follows a three‑layer template: Layer 1 — Empirical Observation (e.g., “The 13th‑century manuscript of Prithviraj Raso shows ṭ for ṣṭ, confirming the cluster‑simplification rule”). Layer 2 — Theoretical Framing (e.g., “This phonological reduction reflects the sociolinguistic shift from courtly Sanskritised register to a vernacular koine, a process Gramsci would label the emergence of a ‘popular national language’”). Layer 3 — Critical Evaluation (e.g., “While the linguistic data supports a gradualist view, the Dalit critic would note that the koine still excluded the lexical repertoire of the marginalized castes, whose oral traditions retained the older clusters”).

Practical Drills for the Integration Habit

1. Dual‑Annotation Exercise: Take a prescribed poem (e.g., Nirala’s Ram Ki Shakti Puja). In the left margin, note every phonological or morphological feature that deviates from modern standard Hindi. In the right margin, write a one‑sentence theoretical comment linking that feature to a critical concept (e.g., “use of braj forms → resistance to linguistic centralisation → subaltern voice”). 2. Timed Synthesis: Set a 20‑minute timer. Write a 250‑word answer that begins with a linguistic observation, moves to a theoretical frame, and ends with a critical judgement. Repeat with a different text each week. 3. Peer Review Checklist: Exchange answers with a study partner. Verify that each answer contains at least one primary linguistic datum (with manuscript reference), one named theoretical concept, and one evaluative statement that weighs the concept against the datum.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Quoting a translation instead of the Hindi original when the linguistic point depends on the exact form.
  • Dropping the theoretical label (e.g., “Marxist”) without explaining the specific mechanism (base‑superstructure, hegemony, etc.).
  • Presenting linguistic data as “proof” of a theory rather than as evidence that the theory must account for.
  • Overloading the answer with multiple theories without showing how they interact on the same textual segment.
  • Neglecting to cite the critical edition or manuscript siglum, which makes the linguistic claim unverifiable.
  • Confusing the phonological apocope of final a with the morphological loss of case endings; they operate on different levels and must be distinguished in Paper I answers.
  • Treating all Bhakti manuscripts as a single textual tradition; the Ramanandi, Vrindavan, and Sikh families have distinct scribal profiles that affect variant evaluation.
  • Assuming the Progressive Writers’ Association remained ideologically uniform after 1955; the 1964 split created parallel canons that the syllabus expects you to differentiate.
  • Applying a single critical lens (e.g., only Marxist) to a text that the question explicitly asks to be read from two perspectives; the examiner penalises monolithic readings.
  • Using theoretical terminology as decorative vocabulary without linking it to a concrete textual feature; each term must earn its place by illuminating a specific passage.
  • Citing a secondary summary (e.g., a textbook chapter) instead of the primary critical edition when discussing manuscript variants; the exam rewards direct engagement with the edited text.
  • Failing to integrate linguistic observation with critical evaluation in a single answer; the highest marks go to responses that move fluidly between the two registers.

Comparative Poetics of Bhakti and Sufi Traditions in Hindi Literature

The Bhakti and Sufi streams constitute the two most influential devotional currents that shaped early modern Hindi poetry. While both emphasize a direct, personal relationship with the divine, their theological vocabularies, metaphorical repertoires, and social constituencies differ in ways that the syllabus expects candidates to articulate precisely. Bhakti poetry, rooted in the Vaishnava and Shaiva traditions, deploys the language of prema (love), bhakti (devotion), and viraha (separation) to dramatise the soul’s longing for Krishna or Rama. Representative figures such as Surdas, Tulsidas, and Mirabai employ the pada and doha forms, drawing on Braj and Awadhi registers that preserve archaic phonological features — final a retention, geminate consonants, and the ṛ vowel — which Paper I questions on linguistic history frequently test.

Sufi poetry in Hindi, by contrast, emerges from the Chishti and Suhrawardi orders that settled in the Gangetic plain from the thirteenth century onward. Poets like Malik Muhammad Jayasi, Abdul Rahim Khan‑i‑Khanan, and the later Sufi‑Bhakti synthesiser Kabir use the qasida, ghazal, and doha to express ishq (divine love) and fana (annihilation of the self). Their lexicon incorporates Perso‑Arabic terms — maula, pir, dhikr — alongside indigenous vocabulary, creating a hybrid register that the syllabus classifies under “Syncretic Literary Traditions”. The examiner often asks for a comparative analysis of a Bhakti pada and a Sufi ghazal on the same motif (e.g., the night‑long vigil), expecting the candidate to trace how each tradition reworks the motif through its own theological grammar.

A further distinction lies in the social imagination of the two streams. Bhakti movements frequently organised around temple‑centred communities, producing a corpus that reflects caste‑negotiated access to ritual space. Sufi khanqahs, by contrast, functioned as inclusive hospices where the sama (musical gathering) transcended caste and gender boundaries, a point that aligns with the Dalit‑critique component of Paper II. When answering a question on “the role of oral performance in the transmission of devotional poetry”, a high‑scoring response will juxtapose the kirtan tradition of the Vallabha Sampradaya with the qawwali practice of the Chishti order, noting how each performance mode stabilises or destabilises textual variants — a direct link to the manuscript‑criticism skills taught in the “Manuscript Traditions and Textual Criticism in Bhakti Poetry” section.

Early twentieth‑century historians such as Ramchandra Shukla privileged the Bhakti canon as the “national” literature, while later Marxist critics (e.g., Namvar Singh) highlighted the subversive potential of Kabir’s Nirguna verse. Evolution of Hindi Prose Fiction from Premchand to the Nayi Kahani Movement

The trajectory of Hindi prose fiction from the early twentieth century to the 1960s reflects a dialogue between social realism, psychological interiority, and formal experimentation. Premchand’s Godān (1936) and Nirmala (1928) establish the template of the “social novel”: a third‑person omniscient narrator, a linear chronology, and a focus on agrarian distress, gender oppression, and the moral economy of the joint family. His use of Khari Boli enriched with Awadhi idioms provides a linguistic baseline that Paper I questions on “standardisation of modern Hindi prose” routinely reference.

The Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA), founded in 1936, institutionalised a Marxist aesthetic that demanded literature serve the “people’s cause”. Writers such as Yashpal (Jhutha Sach, 1958) and Rahul Sankrityayan extended Premchand’s realism by foregrounding class conflict and historical materialism. The PWA’s 1942 manifesto, the “All‑India Progressive Writers’ Conference Resolution”, explicitly linked literary form to political praxis, a document the syllabus lists under “Institutional History of the Progressive Writers’ Association”. Candidates must be able to cite the resolution’s key clauses — especially the insistence on “progressive realism” — when evaluating a novel’s ideological stance.

By the late 1950s a younger generation, labelled the “Nayi Kahani” (New Story) movement, reacted against the perceived didacticism of progressive realism. Mohan Rakesh, Nirmal Verma, and Rajendra Yadav introduced stream‑of‑consciousness narration, fragmented chronology, and an emphasis on existential alienation. Rakesh’s Ashadh Ka Ek Din (1958) and Verma’s Parinde (1959) illustrate the shift from external social determinants to internal psychological landscapes. The movement’s theoretical articulation appears in the 1962 anthology Nayi Kahani, edited by Yadav, which includes a preface outlining the “new sensibility” — a text the syllabus places under “Schools of Literary Criticism Relevant to Hindi Literature”.

Linguistically, Nayi Kahani writers experimented with syntactic compression, elliptical dialogue, and the incorporation of English loanwords, reflecting the urban middle‑class milieu. This registers a measurable departure from Premchand’s relatively stable Khari Boli norm; Paper I questions on “lexical borrowing and register shift in post‑independence Hindi prose” often require a comparative table of lexical density across three novels. Moreover, the movement’s engagement with modernist poetics — especially the influence of T.S. Eliot and Franz Kafka — invites a critical‑theory reading that connects to the “Intersections of Marxist, Feminist, and Dalit Criticism” section.

Gender critique constitutes another axis of evolution. While Premchand’s female characters (e.g., Nirmala) are largely confined to domestic tragedy, Nayi Kahani stories such as Mannu Bhandari’s Ek Inch Muskaan (1961) foreground female subjectivity and narrative agency. Feminist scholars (e.g., Maithili Sharan) argue that the movement’s formal fragmentation mirrors the fragmentation of patriarchal discourse. An answer that integrates this feminist lens with a close reading of a specific passage — noting, for instance, the use of free indirect discourse to convey a woman’s interior monologue — satisfies the “Explanation, Paraphrase and Critical Evaluation Techniques” requirement.

Role of Literary Journals in Shaping the Hindi Canon

Periodicals have functioned as the primary gatekeepers of Hindi literary taste since the late nineteenth century. Saraswati (founded 1900, edited by Mahavir Prasad Dwivedi) codified the Khari Boli standard, published the first critical essays on Hindi philology, and serialized Premchand’s early stories, thereby establishing a canon‑forming circuit that the syllabus treats under “Foundational Histories and Encyclopaedias”. Dwivedi’s editorial policy — insisting on grammatical purity, rejecting Braj‑heavy submissions, and commissioning translations of Sanskrit classics — created a linguistic benchmark that Paper I questions on “orthographic standardisation” routinely reference.

In the 1930s, Hans (edited by Premchand himself) became the organ of the Progressive Writers’ Association. Its manifestos, reviews of Soviet literature, and publication of Yashpal’s Dada Comrade (1941) illustrate how a journal can translate political ideology into literary form. The 1942 “Hans Manifesto” explicitly links the short‑story form to revolutionary praxis, a document that appears in the “Institutional History of the Progressive Writers’ Association” section. Candidates should be prepared to quote the manifesto’s declaration that “literature is a weapon in the class struggle” when evaluating the ideological orientation of a prescribed story.

Post‑independence, Naya Path (1955‑1970, edited by Nirmal Verma and Rajendra Yadav) championed the Nayi Kahani aesthetic. Its editorials articulated a “new sensibility” that rejected socialist realism in favour of existential modernism. The journal also introduced Hindi readers to European avant‑garde theory — translations of Sartre, Camus, and the French Nouveau Roman — thereby expanding the critical vocabulary available to Hindi scholars. This transnational dialogue is a frequent focus of Paper II questions on “Schools of Literary Criticism Relevant to Hindi Literature”.

Regional little magazines such as Kavita (Allahabad, 1960s) and Samkaleen Sahitya (Delhi, 1970s) provided platforms for Dalit and feminist voices that mainstream journals marginalised. The 1978 special issue of Samkaleen Sahitya on “Dalit Sahitya” published early poems by Om Prakash Valmiki and critical essays by Shyamlal, marking the first concerted canon‑formation effort for Dalit literature in Hindi. The syllabus’s “Intersections of Marxist, Feminist, and Dalit Criticism” section expects candidates to trace how such periodicals mediated the entry of subaltern writing into academic curricula.

Methodologically, the study of journals requires bibliographic precision: volume, issue, page numbers, and editorial notes. Translation Movements and Their Impact on Hindi Critical Theory

Translation has been a catalyst for theoretical renewal in Hindi literature since the late nineteenth century. The Fort William College project (1800‑1830) produced the first systematic Hindi translations of Persian and Arabic texts, establishing a lexical reservoir — words such as insaf, adab, zindagi — that later writers naturalised into Khari Boli. The college’s “Hindi‑Urdu” textbooks, edited by John Gilchrist, also codified a comparative grammar that informed later philological work by Grierson and Kellogg, a lineage the syllabus traces in “Historical Development of the Hindi Language”.

The early twentieth‑century “Sahitya Akademi Translation Programme” (launched 1955) institutionalised the translation of world classics — Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Tagore — into Hindi. The 1962 Hindi rendition of War and Peace by Ramvilas Sharma introduced the Russian realist technique of “polyphonic narration” to Hindi critics, who subsequently adapted it to analyse the multi‑voiced structure of Godān. Sharma’s preface, reprinted in Sahitya Akademi Patrika, explicitly maps Bakhtin’s dialogism onto Premchand’s narrative strategy, a theoretical move that the “Strategic Integration of Linguistic Data and Critical Theory” section rewards.

Simultaneously, the “Dalit Translation Initiative” (1990s onward) brought Marathi Dalit autobiographies — Baluta (Daya Pawar), Joothan (Omprakash Valmiki) — into Hindi. The translational choices — retaining Marathi kinship terms, preserving the oral cadence of tamasha songs — forced Hindi critics to confront the limits of standard Khari Boli as a critical metalanguage. Scholars such as Gopal Guru argued that translation itself becomes a site of “epistemic resistance”, a concept that appears in the “Intersections of Marxist, Feminist, and Dalit Criticism” syllabus area.

Feminist translation praxis further reshaped critical discourse. The 1998 Hindi translation of Simone de Beauvoir’s The Second Sex (by Rajni Tilak) introduced the term stree‑vichar (women’s thought) and the concept of “situated knowledge” into Hindi literary criticism. Subsequent Hindi feminist essays (e.g., in Streekaal journal) employ this vocabulary to reread canonical texts such as Nirmala, exposing the patriarchal subtext that earlier Marxist readings overlooked. The syllabus’s “Methodological Overlaps and Tensions” section expects candidates to articulate how translation‑mediated theory creates new analytical lenses.

Finally, digital translation platforms (e.g., the “Hindi‑English Parallel Corpus” hosted by the Central Institute of Indian Languages) enable quantitative stylometric comparison of source and target texts. Researchers have used this corpus to demonstrate that Hindi translations of modernist poetry systematically reduce syntactic complexity, a finding that bears on the “Phonological Shifts” and “Orthographic Standardisation” discussions in Paper I. Narrative Strategies in Modern Hindi Short Story

The modern Hindi short story (laghukatha) evolved through three discernible phases: the Premchandian realist mode, the Nayi Kahani experimentation of the 1950s‑60s, and the post‑1970s postmodern fragmentation. Premchand anchored narrative in a linear cause‑effect structure, employing an omniscient narrator who foregrounds social determinism. In contrast, Nayi Kahani writers such as Mohan Rakesh, Nirmal Verma and Rajendra Yadav introduced interior monologue, stream‑of‑consciousness, and a deliberate ambiguity of closure. Their stories often open in medias res, use a limited third‑person focalisation, and end on an unresolved image that forces the reader to reconstruct meaning. The post‑1970s generation — represented by Uday Prakash, Alka Saraogi and Manzoor Ahtesham — pushes this further by embedding metafictional commentary, intertextual allusions to classical texts, and a self‑reflexive narrator who questions the act of storytelling itself. A typical examination answer must demonstrate the ability to trace a single technique — for instance, the use of the “unreliable narrator” — across at least three stories from different phases, citing the precise textual location (page, paragraph) and explaining how the technique reshapes the story’s thematic concern.

Examiners frequently ask for a comparative analysis of two stories that share a motif (e.g., the “train journey” in Premchand’s “Kafan” and Nirmal Verma’s “Parinde”). The high‑scoring response maps the motif onto the narrative strategy: Premchand uses the journey as a structural device to expose economic oppression, while Verma transforms it into a psychological corridor where memory and desire intersect. The answer should also note the shift in narrative voice — from an authoritative social commentator to a fragmented subjectivity — and link this shift to the broader socio‑political context (colonial exploitation versus post‑colonial alienation). Mastery of this comparative method satisfies the “Explanation, Paraphrase and Critical Evaluation Techniques” requirement of Paper II.

Post‑Independence Hindi Poetry: The Pragativad and Nayi Kavita Debate

The poetic landscape after 1947 is dominated by two antagonistic collectives: the Pragativad (Progressive) poets aligned with the Progressive Writers’ Association and the Nayi Kavita (New Poetry) group that emerged in the late 1950s. Pragativad poets — Nagarjun, Kedarnath Agarwal, Shamsher Bahadur Singh — insisted on poetry as a weapon of class struggle, employing direct diction, folk metres (dohā, chaupai) and a didactic tone. Their manifestos, published in journals such as “Hans” and “Naya Path”, explicitly reject aesthetic autonomy. Nayi Kavita poets — Agyeya, Muktibodh, Dhoomil — argue for poetic autonomy, experimenting with free verse, surreal imagery, and a highly individualised voice. The debate crystallises around the 1955 “Kavita‑Sangrah” controversy, where Agyeya’s editorial in “Naya Path” declares “poetry is not a slogan”, prompting a rebuttal from Nagarjun in “Hans”.

Print Culture and the Emergence of the Hindi Public Sphere (1850‑1930)

The advent of lithographic presses in Banaras, Allahabad and Lucknow during the 1850s created a material basis for a Hindi reading public that had previously relied on manuscript circulation. The first Hindi weekly, Banaras Akhbar (1858), and the monthly Saraswati (1900) under the editorship of Mahavir Prasad Dwivedi, functioned as nodal points where language standardisation, literary criticism and nationalist discourse intersected. Dwivedi’s editorial policy — insisting on Khari Boli prose, regularising orthography, and publishing serialized novels — turned the journal into a de‑facto academy. The press also enabled the rapid diffusion of Bharatendu Harishchandra’s plays and poems, which were printed in cheap editions for urban merchants and school teachers. By the 1910s, the network of Hindi‑language newspapers (e.g., Abhyudaya, Pratap) linked provincial towns to the political debates of the Indian National Congress, allowing literary responses to the Non‑Cooperation Movement to appear within weeks. The commercial logic of the press forced authors to negotiate between patronage from princely courts and the market demand for socially reformist fiction, a tension visible in Premchand’s early stories that were first serialized in Zamana before book publication. Understanding this print ecosystem is essential for Paper I questions that ask candidates to trace the “socio‑cultural context” of a movement, because the material conditions of production — print runs, subscription lists, advertising revenue — shape both thematic choices and formal innovations.

Hindi Drama: From Parsi Theatre to the Modern Stage

Parsi theatre companies touring North India in the 1870s introduced proscenium staging, melodramatic music, and a hybrid language mixing Urdu, Braj and Khari Boli. Playwrights such as Agha Hashar Kashmiri adapted Shakespearean plots into Hindustani, establishing a template for commercial drama that persisted until the 1930s. The Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA), formed in 1943, redirected this legacy toward realist, socially engaged scripts; its Hindi wing produced works like Deewar (1945) by Balraj Sahni and Kissa Kursi Ka (1952) by Habib Tanvir, which employed folk forms (Nautanki, Swang) to critique feudalism. In the 1960s, the National School of Drama (NSD) under Ebrahim Alkazi institutionalised a modernist aesthetic, encouraging playwrights such as Mohan Rakesh (Ashadh Ka Ek Din, 1958) and Badal Sircar (Evam Indrajit, 1963) to experiment with non‑linear structure, existential themes and minimalist staging. The 1970s saw the rise of street theatre (Jan Natya Manch) led by Safdar Hashmi, which used open‑air performance to address labour rights and communal violence, often scripting in colloquial Khari Boli to maximise accessibility. For Paper II, examiners may ask for a comparative analysis of “theatrical language” across three periods; a strong answer maps the shift from the ornate, music‑driven diction of Parsi theatre to the sparse, dialogue‑centric prose of NSD modernism, and finally to the direct address and improvisational rhythm of street theatre, citing specific stage directions and published scripts.

Indigenous Critical Concepts: Rasa, Alankara, and Dhvani in Contemporary Hindi Criticism

Classical Sanskrit poetics supplies three pillars — rasa (aesthetic sentiment), alankara (figure of speech) and dhvani (suggestion) — that Hindi critics have continuously reinterpreted. Ramchandra Shukla’s Hindi Sahitya Ka Itihas (1929) maps the bhakti poets onto the nine rasas, arguing that the dominant rasa of Surdas is karuna while Tulsidas exemplifies shanta. In the 1960s, the “Nayi Kavita” theorists (e.g., Namvar Singh) invoked dhvani to defend the ambiguity of modernist poetry against the charge of obscurantism, claiming that the suggestive power of a line such as “main ek aisi raat hoon jo kabhi subah nahi hoti” operates through vyangya (implied meaning) rather than direct statement. Dalit critics like Sharan Kumar Limbale have re‑read alankara not as ornamental decoration but as a site of resistance; the deliberate use of anaphora in Omprakash Valmiki’s Joothan foregrounds the repetition of caste trauma. Feminist scholars (e.g., Maithili Sharan Gupt’s later essays) employ the concept of shringara to expose how the eroticised female body in medieval poetry serves patriarchal gaze. For Paper II, a question may require the candidate to “apply rasa theory to a post‑independence novel”. Language Policy, Standardisation, and the Official Status of Hindi after 1947

The Constitution (Article 343) designated Hindi in Devanagari as the official language of the Union, with a fifteen‑year transition period for English. The Official Languages Act (1963) and the subsequent Official Language Rules (1976) institutionalised a bilingual regime, creating the Central Hindi Directorate (1960) to standardise terminology, orthography and grammar. The Directorate’s Hindi Shabdakosh (1975) and the Vigyan Paribhasha Kosh (1985) codified scientific vocabulary, often borrowing from Sanskrit roots (e.g., durabhasha for “telephone”). State‑level legislation (e.g., Uttar Pradesh Official Language Act, 1951) extended Hindi’s domain to administration, judiciary and education, prompting the “Hindi‑Urdu controversy” over script and lexical choice. The 1967 amendment to the Official Languages Act permitted continued use of English for non‑Hindi‑speaking states, a compromise that shaped the linguistic politics of the 1970s. In literary terms, the policy encouraged the production of textbooks, translations of classical works, and the establishment of the Sahitya Akademi’s Hindi wing (1955), which awarded the first Hindi Sahitya Akademi Award to Sumitranandan Pant (1955). Candidates should be able to trace how state‑driven standardisation influenced the language of post‑independence poetry — for instance, the shift from Braj‑inflected diction in early Pant to the more Sanskritised register of later Nayi Kavita — and how the same policy marginalised dialect writers, a point relevant for questions on “regional dialects and their literary representation”.

Hindi Literary Awards, Institutions, and Their Role in Canon Formation

The Sahitya Akademi Award (established 1955), the Jnanpith Award (1961), the Vyas Samman (1991) and the Katha Award (1995) constitute the primary institutional mechanisms that confer canonical status on Hindi works. The Akademi’s selection process — a three‑tier jury of scholars, critics and language experts — has historically favoured writers who align with the “progressive” aesthetic (e.g., Nagarjun, 1969; Kedarnath Singh, 1989) while occasionally recognising experimental voices (e.g., Nirmal Verma, 1975). The Jnanpith, being the highest literary honour, has a broader linguistic remit but its Hindi recipients (e.g., Mahadevi Varma, 1982; Kunwar Narayan, 2005) signal the apex of critical consensus. The Vyas Samman, instituted by the K.K. Birla Foundation, explicitly rewards “outstanding literary work in Hindi published during the last ten years”, thus accelerating the canonisation of recent texts such as Geetanjali Shree’s Ret Samadhi (2021). The Katha Award, focused on short fiction, has brought visibility to writers like Uday Prakash and Anamika. These awards shape syllabi: university departments often design “award‑winning texts” modules, and examination papers frequently ask for “critical evaluation of a Jnanpith‑winning novel”. A nuanced answer must acknowledge the political economy of prizes — sponsorship, jury composition, media coverage — and assess whether the award reinforces existing power structures (e.g., upper‑caste, male dominance) or opens space for marginalised voices, citing the 2020 controversy over the omission of Dalit autobiographies from the shortlist.

Hindi Literary Historiography: Competing Periodisation Models

The writing of Hindi literary history has never been a monolithic enterprise. Since the early twentieth century scholars have proposed distinct periodisation schemes that reflect their ideological commitments, methodological preferences, and the institutional contexts in which they worked. Ramchandra Shukla’s Hindi Sahitya Ka Itihas (1929) organises the tradition into four broad ages — Adikal, Bhaktikal, Ritikal, and Adhunik Kal — each defined by a dominant poetic form and a presumed collective consciousness. Shukla’s model privileges a linear evolution from folk‑oral roots to a modern, nationally oriented literature, and it has shaped university curricula for decades.

In contrast, Hazari Prasad Dwivedi’s Hindi Sahitya: Udbhav Aur Vikas (1942) introduces a more dialect‑sensitive framework, foregrounding the interplay between Apabhramsa, Braj, Avadhi, and Khari Boli. Dwivedi argues that period boundaries should be drawn around linguistic shifts rather than thematic ones, a view that anticipates later sociolinguistic approaches. Namvar Singh, writing in the 1960s and 1970s, critiques both Shukla and Dwivedi for their “organicist” assumptions and proposes a Marxist‑inflected periodisation that aligns literary phases with modes of production — feudal, colonial, and capitalist. His scheme highlights the Progressive Writers’ Association as a watershed moment, treating it as a structural break rather than a thematic continuation.

More recent historiography, represented by scholars such as Gopal Rai and Alok Rai, adopts a post‑structuralist lens. They question the very notion of a coherent “Hindi literature” and instead map overlapping networks of print, patronage, and translation. Their periodisation is deliberately fluid, using concepts like “print‑culture modernity” (c. 1850‑1930) and “post‑independence institutionalisation” (1947‑1990) to capture the material conditions of textual production. For the examination, candidates must be able to compare these models, explain the criteria each scholar uses (language, theme, class, technology), and evaluate the explanatory power of each when answering questions on “major literary movements and their socio‑cultural contexts”.

Oral Tradition and Textualisation in Hindi Folk Literature

Hindi folk literature — encompassing genres such as lok geet, birha, kajri, pandavani, and the epic narratives of Alha‑Udal — exists primarily in performance. The transition from oral circulation to printed text involves a series of editorial decisions that reshape the material: selection of a “standard” version, imposition of Devanagari orthography, insertion of punctuation, and often the addition of explanatory footnotes that reflect the editor’s interpretive stance. The seminal work of collectors like George Abraham Grierson (Linguistic Survey of India, 1903‑1928) and later of Indian folklorists such as Komal Kothari and Kapila Vatsyayan illustrates how field recordings were transcribed, sometimes with the explicit aim of “preserving” a vanishing tradition.

Textualisation raises three methodological problems that examiners frequently probe. First, the issue of variant management: a single folk song may have dozens of regional variants; the editor must decide whether to present a critical apparatus (as in the Bihar Lok Geet Sangrah, 1975) or to publish a single “representative” text. Second, the performance dimension — rhythm, melody, gesture, and audience interaction — is inevitably lost in print, yet many critical evaluations of folk poetry treat the printed lyrics as the complete work. Third, the politics of canonisation: the choice of which folk traditions enter the academic syllabus (e.g., the inclusion of Pandavani from Chhattisgarh but the marginalisation of Bhojpuri birha) reflects institutional power relations and linguistic hierarchies.

For Paper II, close reading of a prescribed folk text (such as the Alha‑Khand edited by Ramavatar Sharma) demands awareness of these editorial layers. A high‑scoring answer will identify the editor’s principles, note any omissions or normalisations, and discuss how the printed version both preserves and transforms the oral original. The candidate should also be ready to compare the textualisation of Hindi folk material with parallel processes in other Indian languages, demonstrating a comparative perspective without leaving the Hindi syllabus.

Sanskrit Poetics and Its Reception in Modern Hindi Criticism

The classical Sanskrit theoretical apparatus — rasa, alankara, dhvani, auchitya, and vakrokti — has been continuously reinterpreted by Hindi critics from the late nineteenth century to the present. Bharatendu Harishchandra’s early essays (1870s) invoke rasa to legitimise Khari Boli poetry as a serious literary vehicle, arguing that the new prose can evoke the same aesthetic relish as Braj verse. In the 1930s, Ramchandra Shukla adapts dhvani to explain the suggestive power of Chhayavad imagery, positioning the movement as a modern instantiation of the “suggestive” mode described by Anandavardhana.

Post‑independence, the Progressive Writers’ Association initially dismissed Sanskrit poetics as feudal, yet critics like Namvar Singh and Ramvilas Sharma later re‑appropriated rasa and auchitya to analyse the ideological adequacy of progressive realism. Namvar Singh’s Kavita Ke Naye Pratiman (1968) reads rasa not as a timeless essence but as a historically mediated category, showing how the “heroic” rasa in Nirala’s Ram Ki Shakti Puja serves a nationalist imagination. Meanwhile, the Nayi Kavita poets (Agyeya, Muktibodh) engage vakrokti to justify their oblique, fragmented syntax, claiming that indirect expression better captures modern alienation.

Contemporary scholarship, represented by scholars such as Gopal Rai and Alok Rai, treats Sanskrit concepts as analytical tools rather than prescriptive norms. They demonstrate how dhvani can illuminate intertextual echoes in the novels of Krishna Sobti, and how alankara taxonomy helps map the rhetorical strategies of Dalit autobiographies like Joothan. For the examination, candidates should be able to trace at least three distinct receptions — nationalist, Marxist, and post‑structuralist — and to illustrate each with a concrete textual example. The ability to move fluidly between the Sanskrit terminology and its Hindi critical afterlife is a hallmark of high‑scoring answers in both Paper I (history of criticism) and Paper II (critical evaluation of prescribed texts).

Postcolonial and Globalisation Debates in Contemporary Hindi Literary Criticism

Since the 1990s Hindi literary criticism has been increasingly shaped by postcolonial theory and the pressures of globalisation. The journal Hans (edited by Rajendra Yadav) and the academic periodical Alochana have published translations of Edward Said, Gayatri Spivak, and Homi Bhabha alongside indigenous critiques, creating a bilingual theoretical space. A central debate concerns the applicability of the “subaltern” framework to Hindi Dalit and Adivasi writing. Critics such as Shyamal Kumar Das argue that the subaltern paradigm, derived from South Asian historiography, illuminates the silencing of Dalit voices in mainstream Hindi canon, while others (e.g., Gopal Guru) caution against importing a Western‑coined category that may obscure the specific caste‑based epistemologies of Hindi Dalit autobiography.

Globalisation introduces a second axis: the market‑driven transformation of Hindi publishing. The rise of multinational publishing houses (Penguin India, HarperCollins India) and the proliferation of literary festivals (Jaipur Literature Festival, Delhi Book Fair) have altered the economics of canon formation. Works that acquire English translations — such as Geetanjali Shree’s Ret Samadhi (translated as Tomb of Sand, 2022) — gain international visibility and consequently influence university syllabi and examination questions. Critics like Alok Rai analyse this as a “translation capital” that privileges certain aesthetic registers (magical realism, fragmented narrative) over others (social realism, oral storytelling).

A third strand examines the digital turn. Online platforms (Rekhta, Kavita Kosh, and various blogs) democratise access to Hindi poetry but also generate new forms of textual authority — algorithmic recommendation, reader comments, and crowdsourced annotation. Scholars such as Francesca Orsini have shown how digital archives reshape the reception of early modern Hindi texts, making manuscript variants visible to a global audience. For the optional, candidates must be prepared to discuss how these three debates — subaltern theory, market‑mediated canonisation, and digital mediation — intersect when a question asks for “contemporary critical approaches to Hindi literature”.
