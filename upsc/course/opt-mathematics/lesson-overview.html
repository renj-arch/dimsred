Mathematics Optional — Overview & Syllabus

Mathematics is a popular optional for mathematics/engineering graduates with a highly scoring, objective syllabus. Paper I covers Linear Algebra, Calculus, Real Analysis, Ordinary Differential Equations. Paper II covers Algebra, Complex Analysis, Ordinary & Partial Differential Equations, Statics & Dynamics, Vector Analysis.

Paper I — Linear Algebra, Calculus, Real Analysis, ODE

  • Vector Spaces: Vector spaces over R/C, subspaces, linear independence, basis & dimension, dimension theorem, quotient spaces, direct sum, dual space, dual basis.
  • Linear Transformations: Linear transformations, kernel & image, rank & nullity, rank-nullity theorem, matrix representation, change of basis, eigenvalues & eigenvectors, characteristic polynomial, minimal polynomial, Cayley-Hamilton theorem, diagonalization, Jordan canonical form.
  • Inner Product Spaces: Inner product, norm, angle between vectors, orthogonality, orthonormal sets, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, Bessel's inequality, Parseval's identity, projection theorem, adjoint of linear operator, self-adjoint, normal, unitary/unitary operators.
  • Canonical Forms: Jordan canonical form, rational canonical form, elementary divisors, invariant factors.
  • Real Analysis: Real number system (completeness, Archimedean property), sequences & series (convergence, Cauchy sequences, monotone convergence, Bolzano-Weierstrass, Cauchy criterion), series of positive terms (comparison test, ratio test, root test, integral test), absolute & conditional convergence, rearrangements.
  • Continuity & Differentiability: Continuity & discontinuity, uniform continuity, intermediate value theorem, extreme value theorem, differentiation, mean value theorem (Rolle's, Lagrange's, Cauchy's), Taylor's theorem with remainder, L'Hôpital's rule, Taylor series, Taylor series expansion, analytic functions.
  • Riemann Integration: Riemann integral, Darboux sums, integrability criteria, fundamental theorem of calculus, mean value theorem for integrals, improper integrals (absolute & conditional convergence, comparison test, beta & gamma functions).
  • Sequences & Series of Functions: Pointwise & uniform convergence, uniform convergence & continuity/differentiability/integration, Weierstrass M-test, uniform convergence & differentiation/integration interchange, power series (radius of convergence, Abel's theorem), Taylor series, Fourier series (Fourier coefficients, convergence, Gibbs phenomenon).
  • Functions of Several Variables: Limits & continuity in R^n, partial derivatives, differentiability, chain rule, directional derivatives, gradient, implicit function theorem, inverse function theorem, maxima & minima (Lagrange multipliers), multiple integrals (Fubini's theorem, change of variables), improper integrals (beta & gamma functions).

Paper II — Algebra, Complex Analysis, ODE & PDE, Mechanics

  • Algebra: Groups (definition, examples, subgroups, cyclic groups, permutation groups, Lagrange's theorem, cosets, normal subgroups, quotient groups, homomorphisms & isomorphisms, isomorphism theorems), Rings (definition, examples, ideals, quotient rings, prime & maximal ideals, Euclidean domains, PID, UFD), Fields (definition, examples, field extensions, algebraic & transcendental extensions, finite fields, Galois theory basics), Matrices (rank, inverse, elementary operations, canonical forms, Smith normal form).
  • Complex Analysis: Complex numbers, complex functions, analyticity, Cauchy-Riemann equations, harmonic functions, contour integration, Cauchy's theorem, Cauchy's integral formula, Liouville's theorem, maximum modulus principle, Taylor series, Laurent series, singularities (isolated singularities, poles, essential singularities), residue theorem, residue calculus, evaluation of real integrals, argument principle, Rouché's theorem, conformal mapping.
  • Ordinary Differential Equations: First order ODE (separable, linear, exact, integrating factors, Bernoulli, Riccati), higher order linear ODE (homogeneous & non-homogeneous, method of undetermined coefficients, variation of parameters), systems of linear ODEs, power series solutions (Frobenius method), special functions (Bessel, Legendre, Hermite, Laguerre), Sturm-Liouville theory.
  • Partial Differential Equations: First order PDE (Lagrange's method, Charpit's method), second order linear PDE (classification: elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic), wave equation, heat equation, Laplace equation, separation of variables, Fourier series solutions, D'Alembert's solution, Duhamel's principle, Green's functions.
  • Mechanics: Particle kinematics (rectilinear & curvilinear motion, velocity, acceleration), dynamics (Newton's laws, work-energy theorem, impulse-momentum), work-energy theorem, conservation laws (energy, momentum, angular momentum), central force motion (Kepler's laws, orbital motion), simple harmonic motion, damped & forced oscillations, rigid body dynamics (moment of inertia, angular momentum, Euler's equations), friction (dry friction, rolling friction).
  • Vector Analysis: Vector algebra (dot product, cross product, scalar triple product, vector triple product), vector differentiation (gradient, divergence, curl), vector integration (line integrals, surface integrals, volume integrals), Green's theorem, Gauss divergence theorem, Stokes' theorem, curvilinear coordinates.
Technique: Cauchy-Riemann equations
Question: 'Show that f(z) = z^2 is analytic everywhere.'
Answer: f(z) = z^2 = (x+iy)^2 = x^2 - y^2 + 2ixy. So u(x,y) = x^2 - y^2, v(x,y) = 2xy. Compute partial derivatives: u_x = 2x, u_y = -2y, v_x = 2y, v_y = 2x. Check Cauchy-Riemann: u_x = v_y (2x = 2x) ✓, u_y = -v_x (-2y = -2y) ✓. Since u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y are continuous and C-R equations hold everywhere, f(z) = z^2 is analytic everywhere (entire function).

Memory Tricks

  • CR equations: u_x = v_y, u_y = -v_x (u_x = v_y, u_y = -v_x — "x with x, y with -y").
  • Integration by parts: ∫u dv = uv - ∫v du (LIATE rule for choosing u: Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential).
  • Convergence tests: p-series (∑1/n^p converges if p>1), geometric series (∑ar^n converges if |r|<1), ratio test, root test, comparison test, integral test.
  • Laplace transforms: L{f'(t)} = sF(s) - f(0), L{f''(t)} = s^2F(s) - sf(0) - f'(0).

Paper I vs Paper II: The Strategic Choice of Structure

The decision to choose Mathematics Optional hinges on a deep understanding of the structural differences between Paper I and Paper II, not merely their content overlap. While both papers cover Linear Algebra, Calculus, Real Analysis, ODEs, Complex Analysis, and Mechanics, the weightage and depth vary significantly. In Paper I, topics like Linear Algebra (approx. 20 marks), Calculus (approx. 15 marks), and Real Analysis (approx. 20 marks) form the core. The remaining marks are distributed among ODEs, Vector Analysis, and Probability & Statistics (which is a distinct module in many years). Paper II typically mirrors this with Algebra taking a larger chunk (Groups, Rings, Fields, Matrices), followed by Complex Analysis, PDEs, Mechanics, and sometimes Differential Geometry or Numerical Analysis depending on the year's syllabus revision.

A critical strategic insight is that Paper I often tests foundational rigor more heavily, whereas Paper II allows for a broader range of applications. For instance, the 'Algebra' section in Paper II demands precise definitions and proofs regarding abstract structures like groups and fields, which can be intimidating but rewards rote conceptual clarity. Conversely, the 'Complex Analysis' section in both papers is notoriously dense with theorems (Cauchy's Integral Formula, Residue Theorem) that require rigorous proof writing rather than just calculation. Students often underestimate the volume of theory required for Real Analysis in Paper I; limits, continuity, compactness, and connectedness are not just definitions but require constructing proofs using ε-δ arguments. This distinction means a student must tailor their preparation: if one is strong in computation but weak in abstract proof, Paper II's Algebra might be a safer bet provided the Complex Analysis section is mastered.

The syllabus map also reveals hidden connections. The 'Ordinary Differential Equations' in Paper I often focuses on existence and uniqueness theorems (Picard-Lindelöf) alongside solution methods, while Paper II's ODE section frequently integrates with PDEs to form a cohesive unit on physical modeling. Similarly, Vector Analysis in Paper I is purely analytical (divergence, curl, Stokes' theorem), whereas Mechanics in Paper II applies these concepts to rigid body dynamics and Lagrangian/Hamiltonian formalisms (though the latter might be optional or reduced in some years). Understanding this flow allows an aspirant to see Mathematics not as a collection of disjointed subjects but as a unified language where Linear Algebra provides the matrix machinery for Mechanics, and Calculus provides the analytical engine for Complex Analysis.

Examiner's trap: The "Easy" Question
Question: 'Evaluate ∫_C (e^z)/((z-1)2) dz where C is the unit circle.' A student might jump straight to the Residue formula. Trap: The question asks for evaluation, but in a rigorous exam context, you must first state Cauchy's Integral Formula for derivatives, verify that f(z)=e^z is analytic inside and on C, identify the singularity at z=1 (inside C), calculate the residue as (d)/(dz)(e^z)|z=1, and then apply the formula. Skipping the 'show that' steps leads to zero marks even if the final number is correct.
Solution: Let f(z) = e^z, which is entire. The integrand has a pole of order 2 at z=1. By Cauchy's Integral Formula for derivatives, ∫_C (f(z))/((z-z0)n+1) dz = (2π i)/(n!) f(n)(z0). Here n=1 and z0=1. Thus, integral = 2π i · f'(1) = 2π i · e1 = 2π i e.
Exam-Day Strategy: Pacing the Paper
In a 3-hour exam, spend the first 15 minutes scanning and marking questions. Mathematics exams are time-intensive due to proof writing. If you get stuck on a complex analysis proof for 20 minutes, move on. Do not leave blank spaces; write 'Let f(z) be analytic...' and proceed with known results if you forget a specific theorem name, but always try to recall the statement of Cauchy's Theorem or Green's Theorem as it often carries partial marks. Allocate roughly 45-50 minutes per major question (e.g., a proof in Real Analysis) and 30 minutes for calculation-heavy problems (ODEs/Matrix algebra).

The Anatomy of a Perfect Proof: From Statement to Conclusion

In Mathematics Optional, the quality of your answer is judged not just by the final result but by the logical flow from hypothesis to conclusion. A perfect proof follows a strict architecture: start with the statement of the theorem or definition being used, list the given conditions explicitly, define any auxiliary functions or sets introduced, and then proceed step-by-step with clear transitions. For example, in proving that a continuous function on a closed interval attains its maximum (Extreme Value Theorem), one must first invoke the Heine-Borel theorem to establish compactness of [a,b], then use the fact that the image of a compact set under a continuous map is compact (and thus bounded and closed in R), implying the existence of supremum and infimum which are attained. Skipping the invocation of Heine-Borel or failing to distinguish between open and closed intervals is a fatal error.

Consider the proof of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: |⟨ u, v ⟩|2 ≤ ⟨ u, u ⟩ ⟨ v, v ⟩. A common mistake is to assume equality holds for all vectors. The correct approach involves considering the quadratic polynomial P(t) = ⟨ u + tv, u + tv ⟩ which must be non-negative for all real t (since it is an inner product). Expanding this gives A t2 + B t + C ≥ 0, where A = ⟨ v, v ⟩, B = 2Re⟨ u, v ⟩, and C = ⟨ u, u ⟩. Since the discriminant of a non-negative quadratic must be non-positive (B2 - 4AC ≤ 0), substituting back yields the inequality. This logical chain—defining the polynomial, establishing its non-negativity, expanding, and analyzing the discriminant—is what examiners look for. Merely writing the inequality without derivation loses marks.

Similarly, in Linear Algebra, when asked to find the Smith Normal Form of a matrix over a PID, one must explicitly state the algorithm: perform elementary row and column operations (swapping rows/cols, adding multiples, multiplying by units) to reduce the diagonal entries such that each divides the next.

Linear Algebra as the Backbone of Mechanics and PDEs

Linear algebra supplies the language in which both the mechanics portion of Paper II and the partial differential equations of Paper II are expressed. The eigenvalue problem for a real symmetric matrix appears directly in the analysis of small oscillations of a rigid body: the inertia tensor is a symmetric positive‑definite matrix, its principal axes are the eigenvectors, and the principal moments are the eigenvalues. In the same way, the separation of variables for the heat equation or wave equation reduces the PDE to a Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem, where the differential operator is self‑adjoint on a suitable Hilbert space. Understanding the spectral theorem for compact self‑adjoint operators therefore gives a unified view of normal modes in mechanics and Fourier series solutions of PDEs. Aspirants should practice moving between the matrix formulation (finite‑dimensional) and the operator formulation (infinite‑dimensional) because examiners frequently ask for a proof that the eigenfunctions of a regular Sturm–Liouville problem form an orthogonal basis, mirroring the finite‑dimensional orthogonal diagonalisation theorem.

A second bridge is the use of matrix exponentials to solve systems of linear ODEs, which is a core topic in Paper I. The same exponential map appears in the solution of linear PDEs with constant coefficients via the Fourier transform: the transformed PDE becomes an ODE in the transform variable, and its solution involves the exponential of a matrix symbol. When a question asks for the fundamental solution of the heat equation, writing the solution as the convolution of the initial data with the Gaussian kernel is equivalent to applying the matrix exponential of the Laplacian operator discretised on a grid. Recognising this parallel allows a candidate to reuse the computation of exp(At) for a constant matrix A, a skill honed in the Linear Algebra section, to answer a PDE question without re‑deriving the kernel from scratch.

Finally, the concept of a quadratic form unifies the kinetic energy in Lagrangian mechanics and the energy functional associated with an elliptic PDE. In mechanics the kinetic energy T = ½ q̇ᵀ M q̇ is a quadratic form in the generalized velocities with the mass matrix M. In the calculus of variations, the Dirichlet integral ∫|∇u|² is a quadratic functional on the Sobolev space H¹₀. The Euler–Lagrange equation derived from the latter is the Poisson equation, while the former yields the equations of motion M q̈ + K q = 0 after adding a potential energy quadratic form ½ qᵀ K q. Mastery of the theory of quadratic forms — Sylvester’s law of inertia, simultaneous diagonalisation, and the min‑max principle — therefore pays dividends across three distinct syllabus blocks.

Contour Integration and Residue Theory in Complex Analysis

Contour integration is the computational engine of the Complex Analysis section of Paper II. The syllabus expects fluency with Cauchy’s integral theorem, Cauchy’s integral formula, the residue theorem, and the evaluation of real integrals by closing contours in the complex plane. A typical examiner’s twist is to present an integral that looks elementary but requires a careful choice of branch cut for a multivalued function such as z^α or log z. For instance, ∫₀^∞ xs‑1/(1+x) dx with 0 < Re(s) < 1 is evaluated by integrating f(z)=zs‑1/(1+z) around a keyhole contour that avoids the branch cut along the positive real axis. The candidate must state the branch of the logarithm, verify that the contributions from the large and small circles vanish, and then apply the residue at z = ‑1. Skipping the justification of the vanishing arcs or the branch choice leads to loss of marks even if the final Beta‑function value is correct.

Another frequent pattern is the use of Jordan’s lemma to handle integrals of the form ∫‑∞∞ ei a x f(x) dx where f is a rational function decaying sufficiently fast. The lemma requires that the maximum of |f(z)| on the semicircular arc tends to zero as the radius grows. A common trap is to apply the lemma when f has poles on the real axis; in that case the contour must be indented and the contribution of the small semicircles gives half‑residues. The examiner may also ask for the principal value of an integral with a simple pole on the real line, testing whether the candidate knows the indentation rule and the factor of π i versus 2π i.

Higher‑order poles appear in problems that ask for the coefficient of a Laurent expansion or for the evaluation of integrals involving derivatives of analytic functions. The formula Res(f, z₀) = 1/(m‑1)! limz→z₀ dm‑1/dzm‑1 [(z‑z₀)^m f(z)] must be quoted and applied correctly. A subtle point: when the integrand contains a factor like (z‑z₀)^k in the numerator, the order of the pole reduces, and the candidate must recompute the order before applying the formula. Practising a variety of pole orders, branch cuts, and indented contours builds the pattern recognition needed to finish a 20‑mark question within the allotted time.

Worked Example: Keyhole Contour for a Real Integral
Evaluate I = ∫₀^∞ x‑1/2/(1+x) dx.

1. Define the branch of the logarithm: log z = ln|z| + i arg z with arg z ∈ (0, 2π). Then z‑1/2 = e‑½ log z. The branch cut is the positive real axis.

2. Consider the keyhole contour Γ consisting of: (a) the upper edge of the cut from ε to R, (b) a large circle C_R of radius R, (c) the lower edge of the cut from R back to ε, (d) a small circle C_ε of radius ε.

3. On the upper edge, arg z = 0, so z‑1/2 = x‑1/2. On the lower edge, arg z = 2π, so z‑1/2 = e‑iπ x‑1/2 = –x‑1/2.

4. The integral over Γ of f(z)=z‑1/2/(1+z) equals 2π i Res(f, –1). The only pole inside is at z = –1 (arg = π). Residue = (–1)‑1/2 = e‑iπ/2 = –i.

5. Hence ∮_Γ f(z) dz = 2π i (–i) = 2π.

6. Show that the integrals over C_R and C_ε vanish as R→∞, ε→0 (standard estimates using |z‑1/2| = R‑1/2 and ε‑1/2).

7. The contributions from the two edges give ∫_ε^R x‑1/2/(1+x) dx + ∫_R^ε (–x‑1/2)/(1+x) dx = 2 ∫0^∞ x‑1/2/(1+x) dx = 2I.

8. Equating 2I = 2π gives I = π.

Exam‑Day Pacing for Complex Analysis
Allocate the first 10 minutes of the Complex Analysis block to read all sub‑questions and identify which ones involve standard contours (keyhole, semicircle, rectangle). Start with the question that uses a contour you have practised most; this secures easy marks early. If a question asks for a branch cut, write the branch definition explicitly before drawing the contour — examiners award marks for the definition alone. Keep a mental checklist: (i) state theorem, (ii) verify hypotheses, (iii) compute residues, (iv) justify vanishing arcs. Missing any step costs 2–3 marks per step.

Measure Theory and Lebesgue Integration in Real Analysis

The Real Analysis portion of Paper I has shifted from a purely Riemann‑integral focus to a substantial measure‑theoretic component. The syllabus now includes σ‑algebras, outer measure, Carathéodory’s extension theorem, Lebesgue measure on ℝⁿ, measurable functions, and the convergence theorems (Monotone Convergence, Dominated Convergence, Fatou’s Lemma). A typical question asks to prove that a given set is Lebesgue measurable by exhibiting it as a countable union of intervals up to a null set, or to apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem to interchange limit and integral for a sequence of functions fₙ(x) = n x e‑n x on [0,∞). The examiner expects the candidate to identify a dominating integrable function — here g(x) = C e‑x/2 works — and to verify pointwise convergence. Merely stating the theorem without checking the domination condition receives no credit.

Another recurring theme is the relationship between Riemann and Lebesgue integrals. A classic problem: show that a bounded function on [a,b] is Riemann integrable iff its set of discontinuities has Lebesgue measure zero (Lebesgue’s criterion). The proof requires constructing upper and lower Darboux sums and linking them to the outer measure of the discontinuity set. Candidates often forget to prove the “only if” direction, which uses the fact that the oscillation function is lower semicontinuous and its superlevel sets are closed. Practising this equivalence sharpens the ability to move between the two integration theories, a skill that also appears in the evaluation of improper Riemann integrals via Lebesgue theory.

Product measures and Fubini–Tonelli theorems form the third pillar. Questions may present a double integral ∫₀¹∫₀¹ (x‑y)/(x+y)³ dy dx and ask whether the iterated integrals are equal. The integrand is not absolutely integrable, so Tonelli does not apply; one must check the conditions of Fubini. Computing the inner integral explicitly shows the iterated integrals differ, demonstrating the failure of absolute integrability. Such counter‑examples test whether the candidate can distinguish between the hypotheses of Tonelli (non‑negative) and Fubini (integrable). Mastery of these theorems, together with the ability to construct σ‑finite measure spaces, is essential for the 20‑mark proof questions that appear regularly.

Variational Principles in Classical Mechanics

The Mechanics section of Paper II is built on the calculus of variations. The action functional S[q] = ∫t₁t₂ L(q, q̇, t) dt is stationary for the true trajectory, leading to the Euler–Lagrange equations d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) – ∂L/∂q = 0. Examiners often ask for the derivation of these equations from Hamilton’s principle, requiring the candidate to integrate by parts and argue that the boundary term vanishes because the variations δq(t₁)=δq(t₂)=0. A common twist is to impose a non‑holonomic constraint of the form f(q, q̇, t)=0 that cannot be integrated to a relation among coordinates. The correct treatment uses Lagrange multipliers: the modified Lagrangian L' = L + λ f yields the equations d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) – ∂L/∂q = λ ∂f/∂q̇. Candidates who attempt to eliminate the constraint by substitution lose marks because the constraint is non‑integrable.

Hamiltonian mechanics follows from the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian. The Hamiltonian H(q, p, t) = p q̇ – L(q, q̇, t) with p = ∂L/∂q̇ satisfies Hamilton’s equations q̇ = ∂H/∂p, ṗ = –∂H/∂q. A typical question asks to show that the Poisson bracket {f, g} = ∂f/∂q ∂g/∂p – ∂f/∂p ∂g/∂q satisfies the Jacobi identity. The proof is a straightforward but lengthy computation; the examiner awards partial marks for correctly writing the definition and expanding the cyclic sum. Another favourite is the canonical transformation generated by a function F₂(q, P, t): p = ∂F₂/∂q, Q = ∂F₂/∂P. The candidate must verify that the new variables satisfy Hamilton’s equations with a new Hamiltonian K = H + ∂F₂/∂t. Practising the four types of generating functions (F₁, F₂, F₃, F₄) and their associated transformation rules is essential because the exam often includes a short‑answer question asking to identify the generating function for a given transformation.

Small oscillations about equilibrium provide a direct link to Linear Algebra. Expanding the potential V(q) to second order gives V ≈ V₀ + ½ (q‑q₀)ᵀ K (q‑q₀) where K is the Hessian matrix of V at equilibrium. The kinetic energy is T = ½ q̇ᵀ M q̇. The normal mode frequencies are the square roots of the eigenvalues of M⁻¹K. A question may ask to find the normal modes of a double pendulum or a coupled spring‑mass system. The solution requires setting up the mass and stiffness matrices, solving the generalized eigenvalue problem K a = ω² M a, and interpreting the eigenvectors as mode shapes. This is precisely the simultaneous diagonalisation of two quadratic forms, a topic covered in the Linear Algebra section, illustrating the syllabus interconnectivity.

Answer Structuring and Presentation for Mathematics Optional

Scoring high in Mathematics Optional depends as much on presentation as on mathematical correctness. The examiner reads dozens of scripts in a sitting; a well‑structured answer that signals the logical flow at a glance earns goodwill marks. Begin each answer with a clear statement of the theorem, definition, or principle you intend to use. For a proof, write “We shall prove that …” followed by “Proof:” on a new line. Number the major steps (1), (2), (3) when the argument has distinct phases — for example, (1) show the set is measurable, (2) apply Monotone Convergence, (3) conclude the limit equals the integral. This numbering helps the examiner award partial marks even if a later step contains an algebraic slip.

When a computation is required, separate the algebraic manipulation from the conceptual justification. Write the formula you are applying, substitute the given data, and then simplify. Avoid cramming multiple equalities on a single line; instead, align equal signs vertically or use line breaks. For instance, when evaluating a contour integral, first write the residue theorem, then list the poles inside the contour, compute each residue on separate lines, and finally sum them multiplied by 2π i. If you need to invoke a lemma (e.g., Jordan’s lemma), state it explicitly and verify its hypotheses in a short paragraph before using it. This discipline prevents the common error of applying a theorem without checking conditions, which the marking scheme penalises heavily.

Diagrams and sketches, though not mandatory, can convey geometric insight quickly. In Complex Analysis, a small contour diagram with the branch cut, poles, and orientation arrows can replace a paragraph of description. In Mechanics, a free‑body diagram or a configuration sketch for a constrained system shows that you understand the physical setup. Draw diagrams with a ruler, label all relevant quantities, and refer to them in the text (“as shown in Figure 1”). Finally, end every answer with a concluding sentence that restates the result in the language of the question (“Hence the integral equals π”). This closure signals completeness and prevents the examiner from wondering whether you omitted the final step.

  • Ignoring branch cuts in contour integrals and treating multivalued functions as single‑valued.
  • Confusing the conditions of Tonelli and Fubini, leading to incorrect interchange of integrals.
  • Applying Lagrange multipliers to non‑holonomic constraints as if they were holonomic.
  • Skipping the justification that boundary terms vanish in variational derivations.
  • Writing a proof as a single dense paragraph without logical signposting, causing the examiner to miss key steps.
  • Failing to restate the final answer in the exact form requested by the question.

Spectral Theory of Linear Operators and Its Role in PDEs

The spectral theorem for compact self‑adjoint operators on a Hilbert space provides the analytical backbone for solving linear boundary‑value problems. In the context of the syllabus, the operator A = −Δ with Dirichlet boundary conditions on a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ is a positive, self‑adjoint operator with compact resolvent. Its eigenfunctions {φₖ} form an orthonormal basis of L²(Ω) and the eigenvalues λₖ satisfy 0 < λ₁ ≤ λ₂ ≤ … → ∞. This discrete spectrum allows any f ∈ L²(Ω) to be expanded as f = Σ ⟨f, φₖ⟩ φₖ, and the solution of the Poisson equation −Δu = f with u|∂Ω = 0 is given by u = Σ λₖ⁻¹ ⟨f, φₖ⟩ φₖ. The same framework extends to the heat equation uₜ = Δu and the wave equation uₜₜ = Δu, where the time evolution is expressed through the semigroup etΔ and the cosine family cos(t√{−Δ}) respectively. A typical examiner twist asks to prove that the eigenfunctions of a Sturm–Liouville problem are complete in L²([a,b]; w(x)dx) by invoking the Hilbert–Schmidt theorem for the associated integral operator. Another common variant requires estimating the spectral gap λ₂ − λ₁ for a given domain, which connects to the Rayleigh quotient and the min‑max principle. Mastery of the spectral mapping theorem, functional calculus for self‑adjoint operators, and the distinction between point, continuous, and residual spectrum is essential for answering questions that blend Real Analysis, Linear Algebra, and PDEs.

Distribution Theory and Weak Solutions of Partial Differential Equations

Distribution theory, introduced by Laurent Schwartz, replaces pointwise differentiation by the action of linear functionals on the space of test functions 𝒟(Ω) = C_c^∞(Ω). A distribution T is a continuous linear map T: 𝒟(Ω) → ℝ (or ℂ). The derivative ∂^α T is defined by ⟨∂^α T, φ⟩ = (−1)|α| ⟨T, ∂^α φ⟩ for all φ ∈ 𝒟(Ω). This definition makes every locally integrable function a distribution and allows differentiation of functions that are not classically differentiable. The fundamental solution of the Laplacian in ℝⁿ, E(x) = cₙ |x|2−n (n ≥ 3) and E(x) = (1/2π) log|x| (n = 2), satisfies −ΔE = δ₀ in the sense of distributions. Using convolution, a weak solution of −Δu = f with f ∈ L²(Ω) is obtained as u = E * f, which belongs to the Sobolev space H¹₀(Ω). The Lax–Milgram theorem then guarantees existence and uniqueness of the weak solution in H¹₀(Ω) for the bilinear form a(u,v) = ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx. Examiners frequently ask to verify that a given function is a weak solution by testing against smooth compactly supported functions, or to prove that the weak formulation is equivalent to the classical one when the solution is sufficiently regular. Another favourite is the construction of the Green’s function for a bounded domain via the method of images, expressed as a distribution. Understanding the dual pairing between H¹₀(Ω) and its dual H⁻¹(Ω), the density of 𝒟(Ω) in L²(Ω), and the Sobolev embedding theorems (e.g., H¹(ℝⁿ) ↪ L2n/(n−2) for n > 2) is crucial for tackling questions that mix Functional Analysis with PDEs.

Worked Example: Weak Formulation of Poisson Equation
Let Ω = (0,1)² and consider −Δu = f in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω with f ∈ L²(Ω). Derive the weak formulation and show that the bilinear form a(u,v) = ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx is coercive on H¹₀(Ω).
Step 1: Multiply by a test function. Take any v ∈ C_c^∞(Ω) ⊂ H¹₀(Ω). Multiply the PDE by v and integrate over Ω: ∫_Ω (−Δu) v dx = ∫_Ω f v dx. Step 2: Integration by parts. Using Green’s identity and the fact that v vanishes on ∂Ω, ∫_Ω (−Δu) v dx = ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx. Hence the weak formulation reads: find u ∈ H¹₀(Ω) such that a(u,v) = ∫_Ω f v dx for all v ∈ H¹₀(Ω). Step 3: Coercivity. By the Poincaré inequality, there exists C_P > 0 such that ‖u‖L² ≤ C_P ‖∇u‖L² for all u ∈ H¹₀(Ω). Then a(u,u) = ‖∇u‖²L² ≥ (1/(1+C_P²)) (‖∇u‖²L² + ‖u‖²L²) = α ‖u‖²H¹ with α = 1/(1+C_P²) > 0. Thus a is coercive. Step 4: Boundedness. |a(u,v)| ≤ ‖∇u‖L² ‖∇v‖L² ≤ ‖u‖H¹ ‖v‖H¹. Lax–Milgram now yields a unique weak solution u ∈ H¹₀(Ω).
Exam‑Day Strategy for Distribution Questions
When a question asks to “show that u is a weak solution”, write the definition of the weak derivative explicitly, then perform integration by parts on a single line, citing the vanishing of boundary terms because the test function has compact support. Keep the chain of equalities aligned vertically; this makes the logical flow visible and secures partial marks even if a later algebraic step slips.

Asymptotic Methods for Ordinary Differential Equations

Asymptotic analysis provides systematic approximations to solutions of ODEs when a small or large parameter is present. The method of matched asymptotic expansions handles boundary layers: an outer expansion valid away from the layer and an inner expansion stretched by the layer thickness δ(ε) are matched via the intermediate limit. For the singularly perturbed problem ε y'' + y' − y = 0, y(0)=0, y(1)=1 with 0 < ε ≪ 1, the outer solution satisfies y' − y = 0 giving y_out = C e^x. The boundary layer at x=0 requires the stretched variable ξ = x/ε, leading to the inner equation Y'' + Y' = 0 with Y(0)=0, Y(∞)=C. Matching determines C = 1/(1−e−1/ε) ≈ 1. The composite approximation y_comp = y_out + y_in − common part yields a uniformly valid approximation. The WKB method applies to linear second‑order equations y'' + Q(x) y = 0 with a large parameter λ: set y = exp(λ S₀ + S₁ + …) and collect powers of λ. The leading order gives S₀'² = Q(x), so S₀ = ±∫ √Q dx. The next order yields the amplitude factor Q−1/4. Examiners often ask to derive the leading‑order WKB approximation for the Airy equation y'' − x y = 0 as x → +∞, or to locate turning points where Q(x)=0 and construct uniform approximations using Airy functions. Another staple is the method of steepest descent for integrals representing solutions, e.g., the integral representation of the Bessel function J_ν(x) = (1/2πi) ∮ e(x/2)(t−1/t) t−ν−1 dt. Identifying saddle points, deforming contours, and evaluating Gaussian integrals are tested regularly. Mastery of gauge functions, asymptotic sequences, and the distinction between asymptotic and convergent series is essential for full marks.

Geometric Mechanics: Symplectic Structure and Reduction

Geometric mechanics reformulates Hamiltonian dynamics on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) where ω is a closed, non‑degenerate 2‑form. In canonical coordinates (q^i, p_i), ω = dp_i ∧ dq^i. A Hamiltonian vector field X_H is defined by ιX_H ω = dH, yielding Hamilton’s equations q̇^i = ∂H/∂p_i, ṗ_i = −∂H/∂q^i. The flow of X_H preserves ω (Liouville’s theorem) and the Hamiltonian (energy conservation). When a Lie group G acts on M by symplectomorphisms with an equivariant momentum map J: M → 𝔤*, the Marsden–Weinstein reduction theorem constructs the reduced phase space M_μ = J−1(μ)/G_μ, which inherits a symplectic form ω_μ. For the free rigid body, G = SO(3) acts on T*SO(3) ≅ SO(3) × 𝔰𝔬(3)*; the momentum map is the body angular momentum Π. Reduction at a regular value μ yields the coadjoint orbit O_μ ≅ S²‖μ‖ with the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau symplectic form. The reduced Hamiltonian is the kinetic energy expressed in terms of Π, leading to Euler’s equations Π̇ = Π × Ω. Examiners frequently ask to verify that a given map is a momentum map, to compute the reduced space for a simple system like the spherical pendulum, or to prove that the Poisson bracket on the reduced space satisfies the Jacobi identity. Understanding the distinction between symplectic and Poisson reduction, the role of the isotropy subgroup G_μ, and the reconstruction of dynamics on the original phase space from the reduced flow are key for high‑scoring answers.

Functional Analysis Tools for Integral Equations

Integral equations of the second kind, φ(x) − λ ∫_a^b K(x,y) φ(y) dy = f(x), are solved by viewing the integral operator (Kφ)(x) = ∫_a^b K(x,y) φ(y) dy as a compact operator on L²([a,b]) when K ∈ L²([a,b]×[a,b]). The Fredholm alternative states that either (I − λK) is invertible, giving a unique solution for every f, or λ⁻¹ is an eigenvalue of K, in which case a solution exists iff f is orthogonal to the kernel of (I − λK)*. The resolvent (I − λK)−1 = I + λR_λ, where R_λ is the resolvent kernel, can be constructed via the Neumann series ∑n=1^∞ λn−1 K^n when |λ| ‖K‖ < 1. For symmetric kernels K(x,y)=K(y,x), the spectral theorem yields an orthonormal eigenbasis {φ_n} with real eigenvalues λ_n → 0, and the solution expands as φ = f + ∑ (λ λ_n/(1−λ λ_n)) ⟨f, φ_n⟩ φ_n. Examiners often ask to prove compactness of the Volterra operator (Kφ)(x) = ∫_a^x K(x,y) φ(y) dy, to compute the eigenvalues of a degenerate (separable) kernel K(x,y)=∑i=1^n a_i(x) b_i(y) by reducing to a finite matrix problem, or to apply the Hilbert–Schmidt theorem to show that the eigenfunctions of a symmetric kernel form a complete set in L². The connection with the Green’s function of a differential operator is a favourite: the inverse of a Sturm–Liouville operator is an integral operator with a symmetric Green’s kernel, linking the spectral theory of ODEs to the Fredholm theory. Proficiency in estimating operator norms, distinguishing between Hilbert–Schmidt and trace‑class operators, and using the analytic Fredholm theorem for meromorphic dependence on λ is expected for the toughest questions.

Numerical Analysis: Convergence, Stability, and Error Propagation

Numerical analysis forms the computational backbone of Paper II. The syllabus expects mastery of iterative methods for linear systems, root‑finding for nonlinear equations, and the stability theory of ordinary differential equation solvers. For a linear system Ax = b with a nonsingular matrix A, the classical stationary iterations — Jacobi, Gauss‑Seidel, and Successive Over‑Relaxation (SOR) — converge iff the spectral radius ρ(T) < 1 where T is the iteration matrix. The optimal relaxation parameter ω for SOR can be estimated from the eigenvalues of the Jacobi matrix when A is consistently ordered. In practice, examiners ask to prove convergence of Gauss‑Seidel for strictly diagonally dominant or symmetric positive‑definite matrices, and to derive the SOR iteration formula x(k+1) = (D − ωL)−1[(1−ω)D + ωU]x(k) + ω(D − ωL)−1b.

For nonlinear equations f(x)=0, the Newton–Raphson method xn+1=x_n−f(x_n)/f'(x_n) enjoys quadratic convergence near a simple root, provided f' does not vanish. The secant method replaces the derivative by a finite difference, achieving superlinear order (1+√5)/2. Questions often require a comparison of orders, an error‑recurrence derivation, or a demonstration that the method fails when the initial guess lies near a point where f'≈0.

When solving initial‑value problems y' = f(t,y), the local truncation error of the explicit Euler scheme is O(h²) while the global error is O(h). Runge‑Kutta methods of order four reduce the global error to O(h⁴) with four function evaluations per step. Stiff problems — where eigenvalues of the Jacobian have widely separated magnitudes — demand A‑stable methods such as the implicit trapezoidal rule or backward differentiation formulas. A typical examiner twist is to ask for the region of absolute stability of a given method and to decide whether it can integrate y' = −λy with λ≫1 using a reasonable step size.

Error propagation is analysed through forward and backward error concepts. The condition number κ(A)=‖A‖‖A−1‖ quantifies sensitivity of the solution to perturbations in A or b. For eigenvalue problems, the Bauer–Fike theorem bounds the displacement of eigenvalues under matrix perturbations. Candidates should be comfortable deriving the condition number for the linear system arising from a finite‑difference discretisation of a boundary‑value problem and explaining how mesh refinement affects both discretisation error and round‑off error.

Probability Theory: Measure‑Theoretic Foundations and Limit Theorems

Paper II treats probability on a rigorous measure‑theoretic footing. A probability space is a triple (Ω, ℱ, P) where Ω is the sample space, ℱ a σ‑algebra of events, and P a countably additive measure with P(Ω)=1. Random variables are measurable functions X:Ω→ℝ; their distribution functions F_X(x)=P(X≤x) and densities (when they exist) are derived from the push‑forward measure P∘X−1. Expectation is the Lebesgue integral E[X]=∫_Ω X dP, and the L^p spaces provide the natural setting for moments and convergence.

Independence of σ‑algebras (or random variables) is defined by factorisation of probabilities. The Kolmogorov zero‑one law and the Borel–Cantelli lemmas are standard tools for almost‑sure convergence. Modes of convergence — almost sure, in probability, in L^p, and in distribution — form a hierarchy: almost sure ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution; L^p convergence implies convergence in probability. The characteristic function φ_X(t)=E[eitX] uniquely determines the distribution and converts convolution into multiplication, making it the principal instrument for limit theorems.

The Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) states that for i.i.d. variables with finite mean μ, the sample mean converges in probability to μ. The Strong Law (SLLN) upgrades this to almost‑sure convergence under the same moment condition. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) asserts that the normalised sum (S_n−nμ)/√(nσ²) converges in distribution to a standard normal, provided the variance σ² is finite. The Lindeberg–Feller condition extends the CLT to non‑identically distributed summands. Examiners frequently ask for a proof sketch of the CLT using characteristic functions, including the Taylor expansion φX(t)=1+itμ−t²σ²/2+o(t²) and the limit φS_n(t/√n)^n → e−t²/2.

Applications include the De Moivre–Laplace approximation to the binomial, the delta method for functions of sample means, and the construction of confidence intervals via asymptotic normality. A thorough grasp of the distinction between convergence in distribution and convergence of moments (which requires uniform integrability) is essential for full marks.

Characteristic Function Proof of the Central Limit Theorem
Let X₁, X₂, … be i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ²>0. Define S_n = ∑k=1^n X_k. Show that (S_n−nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to N(0,1) using characteristic functions.
  1. Let Y_k = (X_k−μ)/σ. Then E[Y_k]=0, Var(Y_k)=1, and the characteristic function of Y_k is φ(t)=E[eitY1].
  2. By Taylor’s theorem, φ(t)=1−t²/2+o(t²) as t→0 because the first moment vanishes and the second moment equals 1.
  3. The characteristic function of the normalised sum Z_n = (∑k=1^n Y_k)/√n is φZ_n(t)=φ(t/√n)^n.
  4. Take logarithms: log φZ_n(t)= n log φ(t/√n). Using log(1+u)=u+o(u) and the expansion of φ, we get log φZ_n(t)= n[−t²/(2n)+o(1/n)] = −t²/2+o(1).
  5. Hence φZ_n(t) → e−t²/2 for every t, which is the characteristic function of N(0,1). By Lévy’s continuity theorem, Z_n converges in distribution to the standard normal.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Limit Theorems
Memorise the characteristic‑function expansion up to the quadratic term and the logarithmic approximation log(1+u)=u+o(u). In the exam, write the four‑step outline (centre, expand, log, limit) — it earns the bulk of the marks even if you skip the rigorous justification of the o(t²) remainder.

Statistical Inference: Point Estimation, Confidence Intervals, and Hypothesis Testing

Statistical inference occupies a substantial portion of Paper II. Point estimation begins with the concepts of unbiasedness E[θ̂]=θ, consistency θ̂_n → θ in probability, and efficiency measured by the Cramér–Rao lower bound Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/I(θ) where I(θ)=E[(∂/∂θ log f(X;θ))²] is the Fisher information. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) solves the likelihood equation ∂/∂θ log L(θ)=0 and, under regularity conditions, is asymptotically normal with variance I(θ)−1. The method of moments equates sample moments to theoretical moments; it is simpler but generally less efficient.

Confidence intervals are constructed from pivotal quantities — functions of the data and the parameter whose distribution does not depend on the parameter. For a normal sample with known variance, (X̄−μ)/(σ/√n) ∼ N(0,1) yields the exact interval X̄ ± zα/2 σ/√n. When the variance is unknown, the pivotal (X̄−μ)/(S/√n) ∼ tn−1 gives the Student‑t interval. For large samples, the asymptotic normality of the MLE provides approximate intervals θ̂ ± zα/2 √(I(θ̂)−1/n).

Hypothesis testing is framed by the Neyman–Pearson lemma: the most powerful test of size α for simple versus simple hypotheses rejects when the likelihood ratio Λ(x)=f₁(x)/f₀(x) exceeds a constant k chosen to achieve the desired size. For composite hypotheses, the likelihood‑ratio test statistic λ = supθ∈Θ₀ L(θ) / supθ∈Θ L(θ) leads, under regularity, to −2 log λ ∼ χ²dim(Θ)−dim(Θ₀) asymptotically (Wilks’ theorem). The power function β(θ)=P_θ(reject H₀) and the concept of uniformly most powerful (UMP) tests are examined through the Karlin–Rubin theorem for one‑parameter exponential families.

Examiners often combine estimation and testing: derive the MLE, compute its asymptotic variance, construct a confidence interval, and then formulate the corresponding likelihood‑ratio test. Familiarity with the exponential family form f(x;θ)=h(x)exp{η(θ)T(x)−A(θ)} simplifies the derivation of sufficient statistics, Fisher information, and UMP tests.

Linear Programming: Duality Theory, Sensitivity Analysis, and the Simplex Method

Linear programming (LP) is a core optimisation topic in Paper II. The primal problem in standard form is maximise cᵀx subject to Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0. Its dual is minimise bᵀy subject to Aᵀy ≥ c, y ≥ 0. Weak duality states cᵀx ≤ bᵀy for any feasible primal‑dual pair; strong duality asserts equality at optimality provided both problems are feasible. Complementary slackness x_j (cᵀ−yᵀA)_j = 0 and y_i (b−Ax)_i = 0 characterises optimal solutions and provides a certificate of optimality.

The simplex algorithm moves from one basic feasible solution to an adjacent one by pivoting on a non‑basic variable with positive reduced cost c_j − yᵀA_j > 0. Degeneracy (basic variable at zero) can cause cycling; Bland’s rule (choose the smallest index) guarantees finite termination. The revised simplex method maintains the basis inverse B−1 explicitly, reducing computational effort for large sparse problems.

Sensitivity analysis examines how the optimal solution changes when c, b, or A are perturbed. The shadow price of a constraint equals the optimal dual variable y_i and indicates the rate of change of the objective with respect to b_i within the allowable range. The range of optimality for a cost coefficient c_j is the interval where the current basis remains optimal; it is derived from the reduced‑cost signs. Adding a new variable or constraint is handled by the dual simplex method, which restores feasibility while preserving optimality.

Typical exam questions: formulate the dual of a given primal, prove strong duality using Farkas’ lemma, perform one simplex iteration by hand, compute shadow prices from the final tableau, and determine the effect of increasing a resource by a given amount. A clear tabular presentation of the simplex tableau with labelled rows and columns is essential for full credit.

Integral Transforms: Laplace and Fourier Techniques for Partial Differential Equations

Integral transforms convert differential equations into algebraic equations, a technique heavily tested in the PDE portion of Paper II. The Laplace transform ℒ{f(t)} = F(s) = ∫0^∞ e−st f(t) dt is defined for functions of exponential order. Its key properties — linearity, differentiation ℒ{f'(t)} = sF(s)−f(0), integration, convolution ℒ{f*g} = F(s)G(s), and the initial/final value theorems — enable the solution of linear ODEs with constant coefficients and of PDEs on semi‑infinite domains.

The Fourier transform ℱ{f(x)} = F(ω) = ∫−∞^∞ e−iωx f(x) dx and its inverse are used for problems on the whole line. The transform of a derivative is ℱ{f'(x)} = iω F(ω), turning differential operators into multiplication by polynomials. For the heat equation u_t = α uxx on ℝ with initial data u(x,0)=φ(x), the Fourier transform yields û_t = −α ω² û, giving û(ω,t)=φ̂(ω) e−α ω² t. Inverting produces the Gaussian convolution u(x,t)= (4παt)−1/2 ∫ e−(x−y)²/(4αt) φ(y) dy.

On finite intervals, Fourier series replace the transform. The method of separation of variables leads to eigenfunction expansions; the coefficients are determined by orthogonality of sines and cosines. For the wave equation utt=c² uxx with Dirichlet boundary conditions, the solution is a superposition of standing waves ∑ (A_n cos(nπct/L)+B_n sin(nπct/L)) sin(nπx/L). The Laplace transform in time combined with Fourier series in space handles non‑homogeneous boundary conditions and forcing terms.

Examiners expect candidates to choose the appropriate transform, justify the interchange of integration and differentiation (via dominated convergence or uniform convergence), and correctly invert the transform using tables or the Bromwich integral. Common pitfalls include forgetting the region of convergence for the Laplace transform, misapplying the convolution theorem when the limits are not 0 to ∞, and neglecting the Gibbs phenomenon when representing discontinuous initial data by Fourier series.

  • Assuming convergence of an iterative method without verifying the spectral radius condition.
  • Applying the Central Limit Theorem to heavy‑tailed distributions without checking the finite‑variance hypothesis.
  • Confusing the pivotal quantity for a confidence interval with the test statistic for a hypothesis test.
  • Neglecting complementary slackness when certifying optimality in linear programming.
  • Using the Fourier transform on a finite interval instead of a Fourier series, leading to an incorrect solution form.
  • Omitting the justification for differentiating under the integral sign when applying Laplace or Fourier transforms.
  • Forgetting to state the regularity conditions required for the asymptotic normality of the MLE.

Three-Dimensional Analytic Geometry: Classification of Central Conicoids

The general second-degree equation in three variables F(x,y,z) = ax² + by² + cz² + 2fyz + 2gzx + 2hxy + 2ux + 2vy + 2wz + d = 0 represents a quadric surface. The discriminant Δ = det A where A is the 4×4 matrix of coefficients, and the minor Δ₃ = det of the 3×3 quadratic-form matrix Q = [[a,h,g],[h,b,f],[g,f,c]] determine the nature. For central conicoids (ellipsoid, hyperboloid of one sheet, hyperboloid of two sheets), the centre (x₀,y₀,z₀) satisfies ∇F = 0, i.e. Qx₀ = −u where u = (u,v,w)ᵀ. Translating to the centre eliminates linear terms, yielding XᵀQX + F(x₀) = 0. Diagonalising Q by an orthogonal transformation X = PX′ gives the canonical form λ₁x′² + λ₂y′² + λ₃z′² + F(x₀) = 0 where λᵢ are eigenvalues of Q. The signs of λᵢ and the constant term classify the surface: all λᵢ same sign and constant opposite sign → ellipsoid; two λᵢ same sign, one opposite, constant non-zero → hyperboloid of one sheet; one λᵢ sign different from other two, constant same sign as the single one → hyperboloid of two sheets; Δ₃ = 0 → paraboloids or cylinders. The principal planes are the eigenspaces of Q. The lengths of semi-axes of a central conicoid are √(−F(x₀)/λᵢ). The tangent plane at (x₁,y₁,z₁) is F₁(x,y,z) = 0 where F₁ is obtained by x²→xx₁, xy→(xy₁+x₁y)/2, etc. The polar plane of an external point (α,β,γ) is F₁ = 0. The enveloping cone from (α,β,γ) has equation F(x,y,z)F(α,β,γ) = F₁². Examiners frequently ask for the condition that a plane lx+my+nz = p touches a given conicoid; the answer is the discriminant of the quadratic obtained by eliminating one variable vanishes, equivalently the perpendicular distance from the centre to the plane equals the length of the semi-axis in the normal direction.

Vector Analysis: Differential Operators and Integral Theorems in Curvilinear Coordinates

In orthogonal curvilinear coordinates (u₁,u₂,u₃) with scale factors h₁,h₂,h₃, the gradient, divergence, curl, and Laplacian take the forms ∇φ = Σ (1/hᵢ)(∂φ/∂uᵢ) eᵢ, ∇·F = (1/h₁h₂h₃) Σ ∂/∂uᵢ (h₁h₂h₃/hᵢ Fᵢ), ∇×F = (1/h₁h₂h₃) det [[h₁e₁, h₂e₂, h₃e₃], [∂/∂u₁, ∂/∂u₂, ∂/∂u₃], [h₁F₁, h₂F₂, h₃F₃]], and ∇²φ = (1/h₁h₂h₃) Σ ∂/∂uᵢ (h₁h₂h₃/hᵢ² ∂φ/∂uᵢ). For spherical coordinates (r,θ,φ) with h₁=1, h₂=r, h₃=r sin θ, these reduce to the standard expressions. The divergence theorem ∫V ∇·F dV = ∮S F·n dS and Stokes' theorem ∮C F·dr = ∫S (∇×F)·n dS hold in any coordinate system provided the fields are sufficiently smooth. Green's identities follow from the divergence theorem with F = φ∇ψ and F = ψ∇φ: ∫V (φ∇²ψ + ∇φ·∇ψ) dV = ∮S φ ∂ψ/∂n dS and ∫V (φ∇²ψ − ψ∇²φ) dV = ∮S (φ ∂ψ/∂n − ψ ∂φ/∂n) dS. These are foundational for uniqueness theorems in potential theory and for deriving integral representations of solutions to Laplace's and Poisson's equations. The vector Laplacian ∇²F = ∇(∇·F) − ∇×(∇×F) appears in the Navier-Stokes equations and in the wave equation for the vector potential. In cylindrical coordinates (ρ,φ,z), the Laplacian of a vector field has non-trivial coupling between components due to the curvature of the coordinate lines; the φ-component of ∇²F contains terms −Fφ/ρ² − (2/ρ²) ∂Fρ/∂φ. A common examination question asks to verify the divergence theorem for a given vector field over a specified volume (e.g., a hemisphere or a parallelepiped) by computing both sides explicitly. Another standard problem: given a vector field F = Pi + Qj + Rk, find a scalar potential φ such that F = ∇φ if ∇×F = 0, or a vector potential A such that F = ∇×A if ∇·F = 0. The Poincaré lemma guarantees local existence; global existence requires topological conditions (simply connected domain for scalar potential, second de Rham cohomology vanishing for vector potential).

Reduce the quadric 5x² + 5y² + 5z² − 2yz − 2zx − 2xy − 2x − 4y − 6z + 3 = 0 to canonical form and identify the surface.

Exam-Day Strategy
For quadric classification, always compute the eigenvalues of the 3×3 quadratic-form matrix first — they are invariant under rotation and immediately tell you the type (sign pattern). The centre calculation is a linear system; use Cramer's rule or row reduction rather than matrix inversion to avoid arithmetic errors. If the constant term after translation has the same sign as all eigenvalues, the quadric has no real points (imaginary surface) — state this explicitly; examiners award marks for recognising imaginary loci. For vector analysis problems, write the scale factors for the coordinate system at the start; this prevents missing factors of r or sin θ in divergence and curl. When verifying integral theorems, choose the side (volume or surface integral) that is simpler to compute and use the theorem to find the other — this is often the intended shortcut.

Fluid Dynamics: Inviscid Flow, Vorticity Dynamics, and Potential Theory

The Euler equations for an inviscid, incompressible fluid are ∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u = −(1/ρ)∇p + f, ∇·u = 0. Taking the curl gives the vorticity equation ∂ω/∂t + (u·∇)ω = (ω·∇)u where ω = ∇×u. The term (ω·∇)u represents vortex stretching and tilting; in two-dimensional flow it vanishes, so vorticity is materially conserved: Dω/Dt = 0. Kelvin's circulation theorem states that for barotropic flow with conservative body forces, the circulation Γ = ∮C u·dr around a closed material curve remains constant with time. This follows from ∮C (Du/Dt)·dr = 0 when the acceleration is a gradient. Helmholtz's vortex theorems: (1) vortex lines move with the fluid (vortex lines are material lines), (2) the strength of a vortex tube (flux of vorticity through a cross-section) is constant along its length, (3) a vortex tube cannot end in the fluid — it must extend to boundaries or form a closed loop. For irrotational flow (ω = 0), a velocity potential φ exists with u = ∇φ, and incompressibility gives Laplace's equation ∇²φ = 0. The Bernoulli equation for unsteady potential flow is ∂φ/∂t + ½|∇φ|² + p/ρ + χ = F(t) where χ is the potential of body forces. For steady flow, this reduces to the familiar p + ½ρ|u|² + ρχ = constant along streamlines (and everywhere for irrotational flow). The method of images and conformal mapping solve Laplace's equation in simple geometries. For a source of strength m at the origin in 3D, φ = −m/(4πr), u = (m/(4πr²)) er. A dipole (doublet) of moment μ has φ = −μ·r/(4πr³). The flow past a sphere of radius a with uniform stream U is φ = U(r + a³/(2r²)) cos θ. The pressure distribution from Bernoulli gives d'Alembert's paradox: zero drag in steady inviscid flow. In two dimensions, complex potential w(z) = φ + iψ where ψ is the stream function. The flow past a cylinder with circulation Γ is w = U(z + a²/z) − (iΓ/(2π)) log z. The Kutta-Joukowski theorem states lift per unit span L = ρUΓ. The Blasius-Chaplygin formulas give force and moment from contour integrals of w'². Boundary layer theory (Prandtl) resolves d'Alembert's paradox by introducing viscosity in a thin layer near the body; the displacement thickness δ* and momentum thickness θ characterise the layer's effect on the outer inviscid flow.

Tensor Calculus: Covariant Differentiation, Curvature, and Geodesics

In a Riemannian manifold with metric tensor gij, the Christoffel symbols of the second kind are Γkij = ½gkl(∂igjl + ∂jgil − ∂lgij). The covariant derivative of a vector vi is ∇jvi = ∂jvi + Γijkvk; for a covector wi, ∇jwi = ∂jwi − Γkijwk. The metric is covariantly constant: ∇kgij = 0. The Riemann curvature tensor is Rlijk = ∂jΓlik − ∂kΓlij + ΓljmΓmik − ΓlkmΓmij. Its symmetries: Rlijk = −Rlikj, Rlijk = −Rljik, Rlijk = Rklij, and the first Bianchi identity Rl[ijk] = 0. The Ricci tensor is Rij = Rkikj; the scalar curvature is R = gijRij. The Einstein tensor Gij = Rij − ½Rgij is divergence-free (∇iGij = 0) by the contracted Bianchi identity. Geodesics are curves xi(s) that parallel-transport their tangent vector: d²xi/ds² + Γijk (dxj/ds)(dxk/ds) = 0. They extremise the arc length ∫ √(gij dxi dxj). The geodesic deviation equation D²ξi/Ds² = Rijkl uj ξk ul describes how neighbouring geodesics accelerate relative to each other, where ξ is the separation vector and u the tangent. For a 2D surface with Gaussian curvature K, the Riemann tensor has only one independent component: R1212 = K det(g). The Gauss-Bonnet theorem relates total curvature to topology: ∫M K dA + ∫∂M kg ds = 2π χ(M). In general relativity, the field equations are Gij = 8πGTij/c⁴. For UPSC, the key computational skills are: computing Christoffel symbols for a given metric (e.g., spherical, cylindrical, or a 2D surface metric), writing the geodesic equations, and computing the Riemann and Ricci tensors for simple metrics. A typical problem: given the metric ds² = dr² + f(r)² dθ², find the Gaussian curvature K = −f''/f and the geodesic equations.

Numerical Methods for Partial Differential Equations: Finite Difference and Finite Element Discretisation

The numerical solution of PDEs is a core component of the Numerical Analysis section of Paper II. For the heat equation ut = αuxx on [0,L] with Dirichlet conditions, the explicit FTCS scheme is uin+1 = uin + r(ui+1n − 2uin + ui−1n) where r = αΔt/Δx². Von Neumann stability analysis substitutes uin = ξn eikiΔx giving amplification factor ξ = 1 − 4r sin²(kΔx/2). Stability requires |ξ| ≤ 1 for all k, yielding r ≤ ½. The implicit BTCS scheme uin+1 − r(ui+1n+1 − 2uin+1 + ui−1n+1) = uin is unconditionally stable but requires solving a tridiagonal system at each step. The Crank-Nicolson scheme averages the two: uin+1 − uin = ½r(δ²xuin+1 + δ²xuin), giving second-order accuracy in both time and space and unconditional stability. For the wave equation utt = c²uxx, the explicit central-difference scheme uin+1 = 2uin − uin−1 + λ²(ui+1n − 2uin + ui−1n) with λ = cΔt/Δx is stable iff λ ≤ 1 (CFL condition). For elliptic equations like Poisson's equation ∇²u = f, the standard five-point stencil on a uniform grid gives a sparse linear system Au = b. Iterative methods (Gauss-Seidel, SOR) are preferred for large systems; the optimal SOR parameter for a square grid is ωopt ≈ 2/(1 + sin(πΔx)). The finite element method (FEM) uses a variational formulation: find u ∈ H¹₀ such that a(u,v) = L(v) for all v ∈ H¹₀, where a(u,v) = ∫ ∇u·∇v and L(v) = ∫ fv. The domain is partitioned into elements (triangles in 2D, tetrahedra in 3D), and the solution is approximated by piecewise polynomials (typically linear or quadratic). The stiffness matrix Kij = a(φj,φi) and load vector Fi = L(φi) are assembled element-wise. The Galerkin orthogonality property a(u−uh,vh) = 0 implies the FEM solution is the best approximation in the energy norm. Error estimates: ||u−uh||H¹ ≤ Ch|u|H² for linear elements. Examiners test: deriving finite difference stencils from Taylor expansions, performing stability analysis, setting up the tridiagonal matrix for implicit schemes, and writing the weak form for a given BVP. A common question: "Derive the Crank-Nicolson scheme for the heat equation and prove it is unconditionally stable using von Neumann analysis." Another: "Formulate the finite element equations for −u'' = f on (0,1) with u(0)=u(1)=0 using linear hat functions."
