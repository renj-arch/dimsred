1. Essence, Determinants & Dimensions of Ethics

1.1 What is Ethics?

Ethics is the branch of philosophy that systematises, defends, and recommends concepts of right and wrong conduct. The word comes from the Greek ethos (character, custom). In the GS IV context, ethics is not abstract moralising — it is the study of how a public servant should act when rules are silent, when two duties conflict, and when personal interest clashes with public duty.

Ethics vs morality: Morality is the social expression of values — what a society or community holds to be right at a given time. Ethics is the reasoned, critical examination of those values. A practice can be moral in a society (e.g., untouchability was once morally sanctioned) but unethical because it fails the test of reason and human dignity. GS IV rewards answers that distinguish the two: morality describes what people believe; ethics asks what they ought to do.

Ethics vs law: Law is the minimum standard of conduct enforced by the state. Ethics is the maximum standard a person sets for herself. Law punishes after the act; ethics guides before the act. A civil servant can be legally compliant and still be ethically bankrupt — obeying an immoral order is lawful but unethical. Conversely, civil disobedience may be illegal but ethical (e.g., Gandhi's Salt March broke the law to uphold a higher ethical principle).

Ethics vs conscience: Conscience is the inner voice that approves or disapproves one's actions. It is a psychological faculty; ethics is the rational framework that trains that faculty. Conscience can be misinformed (a corrupt officer may feel no guilt). Ethics refines conscience through reason, constitutional values, and stakeholder impact.

Distinction that wins marks
In a 200-word GS IV answer, opening with the ethics–law distinction signals conceptual clarity: "Law enforces minimum compliance; ethics demands maximum responsibility. A civil servant may stay within the law and still violate ethics." This frames every subsequent argument.

1.2 Determinants of Ethics

Ethical behaviour is shaped by multiple sources. UPSC frequently asks candidates to list and explain these:

  • Family — the first school of values. Honesty, compassion, respect, and fairness are first learned at home. A child who sees parents pay bribes internalises corruption as normal; a child who sees truth spoken at cost internalises integrity.
  • Education. Schools and colleges build critical thinking, constitutional literacy, and exposure to diverse viewpoints — the intellectual basis for ethical judgment beyond custom.
  • Society. Norms, customs, peer pressure, and reference groups shape conduct. Public opinion can reinforce virtue (honouring a whistleblower) or vice (social boycott of an honest officer).
  • Religion. Religious traditions provide moral codes (ahimsa, compassion, zakat, service). Ethics is secular and reason-based, but religious values often converge with ethical ideals. UPSC expects candidates to respect religious roots while grounding answers in constitutional morality.
  • Law. Law codifies society's minimum enforceable standards. It cannot cover every grey situation, which is where ethics operates.
  • Conscience. The internal moral monitor. Ethics strengthens conscience so it aligns with reason rather than self-interest.
  • Experience. Life events — failure, success, loss, public service — refine ethical judgment. A young officer's first posting often tests whether training values survive field reality.

1.3 Dimensions of Ethics

Three dimensions structure ethical analysis — know them, because questions often frame themselves around one:

  • Meta-ethics: The nature of moral judgments. Are ethical statements factual or expressions of feeling? Where does moral authority come from? This is theoretical and rarely asked directly, but it underpins the other two.
  • Normative ethics: Standards for right action. Three schools dominate Indian public administration:
    • Virtue ethics (Aristotle): act as a virtuous person would — courage, temperance, justice, prudence.
    • Deontology (Kant): act from duty; certain acts (lying, killing, corruption) are wrong regardless of consequences. The categorical imperative — act only on rules you could universalise.
    • Consequentialism (Bentham, Mill): judge acts by outcomes — greatest good for the greatest number.
    UPSC answers gain depth when they name the school being applied.
  • Applied ethics: Ethics in specific domains — bioethics, business ethics, environmental ethics, media ethics, and for GS IV, administrative ethics. This is where case studies live.

2.1 Extracting a Value (Exam Method)

When a question cites a leader, do not narrate their life. Follow this sequence:

  1. Name the value — one word or phrase (e.g., integrity, compassion, impartiality).
  2. Cite the evidence — one specific act or decision, dated and located.
  3. State the principle — what the act teaches about right conduct.
  4. Apply to administration — how a civil servant should act in a comparable situation.

Example: Question — "What does the life of Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel teach a civil servant about national integration?"

  • Value: Unity and firmness.
  • Evidence: Integration of 565 princely states into the Indian Union (1947–49), using persuasion, privy purses, and where necessary police action (Hyderabad).
  • Principle: National interest overrides regional or princely sentiment; integration requires both negotiation and resolve.
  • Application: A district officer facing separatist or caste-based mobilisation engages all stakeholders firmly, upholds constitutional unity, and does not appease vested interests.

3. Ethics in Private & Public Relationships

The syllabus: Ethics in private and public relationships. This section tests whether a candidate understands that private conduct and public duty are connected, but not identical.

3.1 Private Relationships

Private relationships — family, marriage, friendship, love — rest on trust, loyalty, empathy, care, reciprocity, and boundaries. The ethical foundation is personal integrity: keeping promises even when no one is watching, treating dependents with dignity, and not exploiting closeness for advantage.

Why UPSC tests this: A civil servant who lies to family, betrays a friend, or abuses a dependent has demonstrated a pattern of putting self-interest above duty. That pattern transfers to office. As Kautilya observed in the Arthashastra, a person's conduct in small matters predicts conduct in great ones.

3.2 Public Relationships

Public relationships — citizen-officer, politician-bureaucrat, superior-subordinate, inter-departmental — rest on impartiality, accountability, transparency, and service. The ethical foundation is constitutional morality: serving the Constitution and the public, not a person, party, or private interest.

A public relationship is governed by office, not affection. A citizen is not a client to be favoured; a subordinate is not a servant to be bullied; a politician is not a patron to be obeyed uncritically.

3.3 The Private–Public Link (and its Limits)

The standard proposition
Private ethics informs public ethics — but does not determine it. A person who is honest at home is more likely to be honest in office; a person who is cruel in private relations is likely to abuse power publicly. However, the reverse is not automatically true: a person with a flawed private life can still discharge public duty impartially, and a spotless private life does not guarantee ethical administration. UPSC rewards nuance: acknowledge the link, then judge each case on its public conduct.

3.4 Common Exam Traps

  • Moral equivalence: Treating a private lie (e.g., missing a family dinner) as equivalent to a public lie (e.g., falsifying a land record). Public duty carries greater weight because it affects citizens' rights.
  • Confidentiality confusion: A civil servant may know private information about citizens through office. Using it for personal advantage is a breach of both law and ethics (cf. data privacy, official secrets).
  • Nepotism as "family values": Helping a relative get a post is not "loyalty to family" — it is misuse of public office and violates impartiality.

4. Ethics in Governance & Probity

The syllabus: Probity in governance: philosophical basis of governance and probity; information sharing and transparency in government, Right to Information, Codes of Ethics, Codes of Conduct, Citizen's Charters, Work culture, Quality of service delivery, Utilisation of public funds, challenges of corruption.

4.1 Probity

Probity is complete integrity — uprightness, honesty, and strict adherence to ethical principles, especially in handling public money and public trust. It is not merely the absence of corruption; it is the active presence of ethical diligence. A probation officer with probity does not wait to be caught not taking a bribe; she refuses the bribe, reports the offer, and documents it.

4.2 Transparency and the Right to Information

The Right to Information Act, 2005 is the transparency backbone of Indian governance. It lets any citizen request information from public authorities, with replies due within 30 days (48 hours for life/liberty cases). Section 8 lists exemptions (sovereignty, security, privacy, cabinet papers), but the default is disclosure. For GS IV, transparency is an ethical duty: citizens own the information their taxes generate, and secrecy requires justification, not disclosure.

4.3 Citizen's Charter

A Citizen's Charter is a public declaration of service standards — what a citizen can expect, within what time, at what cost, and with what grievance redress mechanism. The six principles: published standards, openness, choice and consultation, courtesy and helpfulness, grievance redress, and value for money. A charter is ethical only if it is published, monitored, and enforceable — otherwise it is a paper promise.

4.4 Codes of Ethics vs Codes of Conduct

Aspect Code of Ethics Code of Conduct
Nature Values and principles (aspirational) Specific rules and prohibitions (prescriptive)
Enforceability Not directly enforceable; guides judgment Enforceable; breach invites disciplinary action
Example "Civil servants shall serve the public interest" "No gift above Rs. 5,000 shall be accepted"

India's civil services operate under Conduct Rules (1964 for All India Services). A Code of Ethics has been recommended (2nd ARC) but is not yet universally codified.

4.5 Utilisation of Public Funds

Public money is a trust, not a resource to be spent. Ethical utilisation means: spending only for authorised purposes, avoiding waste and duplication, preferring economy without compromising quality, and documenting every decision. Misuse need not be embezzlement — buying an unnecessarily expensive vehicle, or sponsoring an event for publicity, are ethical breaches even if legal.

4.6 Challenges of Corruption

Corruption thrives where discretion is high, accountability is low, and transparency is absent. Anti-corruption ethics is not just punishment — it is system design: simplify rules so discretion shrinks, digitise services so bribes are harder to demand, protect whistleblowers, and rotate officers in corruption-prone posts. The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (amended 2018) criminalises bribery and, crucially, also penalises the bribe-giver — recognising that corruption is a transaction, not a one-sided extortion.

5. Thinkers & Their Contributions

UPSC frequently asks: "What do you understand by 'X'? Explain with reference to the thoughts of [thinker]." Know each thinker's core idea and one administrative application:

Immanuel Kant Deontology — act from duty; the categorical imperative (universalise your rule); treat humanity as an end, never merely as a means. Do not use citizens as mere means to meet targets; an act is wrong even if its consequences appear good.

6. PYQ Trends & Answer-Writing Framework

6.1 What UPSC Actually Asks in GS IV

  • Conceptual definition questions — "What is meant by 'crisis of conscience'? How does it manifest in public service?"
  • Thinkers/application questions — "Bring out the relevance of 'nishkama karma' from the Gita to a civil servant."
  • Case studies — a 250-word administrative dilemma with 3–4 sub-questions asking for stakeholders, options, and a course of action.
  • Distinction questions — "Distinguish between code of ethics and code of conduct."

6.2 The Answer-Writing Framework (150–250 words)

Every GS IV answer benefits from a repeatable structure:

  1. Define (30 words): State the core term with precision, cite the thinker/act if relevant.
  2. Stakeholders & conflict (60 words): Who is affected? What values are in tension? (e.g., compassion vs rule-following)
  3. Options (60 words): List 2–3 courses of action with pros and cons.
  4. Course of action (60 words): Choose one, justify it with constitutional/ethical principle, and state implementation steps.
  5. Conclusion (30 words): One-line synthesis linking back to the value upheld.
Solved mini-example
Q: You discover a senior officer has approved a contract to a relative's firm. Stakeholders: you, the officer, the firm, the public exchequer, citizens. Conflict: loyalty to senior vs integrity vs public funds. Options: (1) stay silent — complicity, corruption continues; (2) confront privately — may stop it but no record, retaliation risk; (3) report to vigilance/CVC with evidence — lawful, creates record, protects you under whistleblower provisions. Course of action: gather documentary evidence, report through official channel, cooperate with inquiry. Conclusion: Integrity outweighs hierarchical loyalty when public funds are at stake.

6.3 Case-Study Method

  1. Identify the core dilemma in one sentence (e.g., "compassion for a beneficiary vs strict rule application").
  2. List stakeholders — primary and secondary, with their interests.
  3. Identify ethical principles — impartiality, accountability, compassion, rule of law, public trust.
  4. Generate options — never just one; include a "do nothing" option to show you've considered it and rejected it.
  5. Evaluate each option against legality, fairness, long-term impact, and precedent.
  6. Decide and justify — pick the option that best upholds constitutional values, explain why others were rejected.
  7. Implementation — concrete steps, timeline, whom to inform, documentation.
1. Which of the following is NOT a determinant of ethics?
a. Family
b. Education
c. Personal wealth
d. Conscience
Correct: c. Personal wealth
Determinants of ethics are family, education, society, religion, law, conscience, and experience. Personal wealth is not a determinant; it may influence choices but does not shape ethical foundations.
2. The categorical imperative is associated with which thinker?
a. Aristotle
b. Kant
c. Mill
d. Rawls
Correct: b. Kant
The categorical imperative — "act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law" — is Immanuel Kant's central deontological principle.
3. A Citizen's Charter is ethical only when it is:
a. Printed and displayed
b. Published, monitored, and enforceable
c. Approved by the minister
d. Updated annually
Correct: b. Published, monitored, and enforceable
A charter without monitoring or enforcement is a paper promise. Ethics requires that standards be real, measurable, and accountable.

7. Emotional Intelligence & Attitude — Integrated View

The syllabus treats Emotional Intelligence and Attitude as separate topics, but in administration they operate as a unified system. Attitude is the cognitive-affective-behavioural disposition toward an object, person, or situation. Emotional Intelligence (EI) is the capacity to perceive, understand, regulate, and use emotions — both one's own and others'. Together, they determine how a civil servant processes a situation and acts on it.

7.1 The Attitude–EI Feedback Loop

Attitude structure (ABC model): Affect (feeling/emotion) → Behavioural intention (predisposition to act) → Cognition (beliefs, knowledge). EI operates at the Affect level (perceiving/regulating emotion) and the Behavioural level (choosing response). A civil servant with high EI but negative attitude (cynicism, prejudice) will use EI to manipulate; one with positive attitude but low EI will misread situations and react poorly. Integration is the goal.

7.3 Attitude Formation & Change in Public Service

Sources of attitude formation: Direct experience (posting in Naxal area shapes attitude on security), social learning (mentor modelling integrity), conditioning (rewards for compliance), cognitive dissonance reduction (justifying a compromise). Change strategies: Persuasion (central route — evidence, logic; peripheral route — credibility, liking), cognitive dissonance induction (highlight gap between values and action), role-playing (simulated dilemmas), institutional culture (code of ethics, peer norms).

Attitude change in administration
A junior officer believes "rules are obstacles." Senior uses central route persuasion: presents data on how rule-bending caused a bridge collapse (evidence), explains how procedural integrity protects the officer (logic). Over 6 months, officer's attitude shifts to "rules are safeguards." This is lasting change — not compliance, but internalisation.

7.4 Moral & Political Attitudes — Administrative Relevance

Moral attitudes (justice, honesty, compassion) are the bedrock — they are less mutable, rooted in conscience and constitutional values. Political attitudes (views on policy, ideology, governance philosophy) are legitimate but must not override moral attitudes in decision-making. A civil servant may personally oppose a government scheme (political attitude) but must implement it faithfully (moral attitude: duty, integrity) while using feedback channels to suggest improvements.

Exam trap: Political vs Moral Attitude
Question: "A civil servant disagrees with a government policy. What should they do?"
Wrong: "Resist implementation" or "Resign."
Right: "Implement faithfully (duty/integrity), document concerns through file notings, use internal review mechanisms, suggest evidence-based modifications. Political neutrality ≠ moral silence."

7.5 Social Influence & Persuasion in Governance

Cialdini's 6 principles applied ethically in administration: (1) Reciprocity — deliver service first, citizens trust back; (2) Commitment/Consistency — public pledges (Swachh Bharat) drive behaviour; (3) Social Proof — showcase compliant panchayats; (4) Authority — expert committees, not political bosses; (5) Liking — approachable officers build cooperation; (6) Scarcity — "limited seats, apply now" for welfare camps. Ethical boundary: Persuasion informs choice; manipulation bypasses consent. Nudge units (like India's NITI Aayog Behavioural Insights Unit) must disclose defaults and preserve opt-out.

7.6 Integrated Case: The Protesting Farmers

Case
You are a District Magistrate. Farmers protest against land acquisition for a highway. They block the highway. Political pressure mounts to "clear the road by evening." Your police force is fatigued. Some farmers are elderly; women and children are present. Media is live.
Integrated Attitude–EI Analysis
Attitude appraisal: Your attitude toward protesters — "law-breakers" (negative, cognitive) vs "citizens with grievance" (constitutional, moral). The latter aligns with constitutional morality (Ambedkar).

EI deployment: Self-awareness: recognise your stress, fatigue, irritation at political pressure. Self-regulation: tactical breathing, 2-min pause before orders. Social awareness: read crowd mood — fear, anger, resolve; identify informal leaders. Relationship management: negotiate with leaders, not mob.

Integrated decision: Refuse "clear by evening" deadline. Order: (1) No force unless life threatened. (2) DM + SP meet farmer leaders at protest site (empathy + authority). (3) Commit to written response on rehabilitation within 7 days (commitment principle). (4) Provide water/medical aid (reciprocity). (5) Document everything for accountability.

Outcome: Protest de-escalates; trust built; legal process respected; political pressure absorbed through documented process.

8. Ethics in Public vs Private Life — Case Method

Section 3 established the theoretical distinction. This section applies it through case-based reasoning — the method UPSC uses. The core principle: Public office carries fiduciary duty; private life carries personal duty. They intersect but are not identical. The test is: does the private conduct impair public trust or institutional integrity?

8.1 Decision Framework for Public–Private Ethics Cases

  1. Identify the domain: Is the conduct purely private (family, friendship, personal finance) or does it use public office/resources/information?
  2. Test for impairment: Does it create conflict of interest? Undermine impartiality? Erode public trust? Violate law/Conduct Rules?
  3. Apply proportionality: Is the response proportionate to the harm? (e.g., counselling vs suspension vs prosecution)
  4. Distinguish person from office: Judge the act in office, not the private character — unless private act directly impairs public duty.

8.2 Worked Case Studies

Case 8.1: The Officer's Spouse in Business

Case
You are a Secretary in the Commerce Ministry. Your spouse runs an export consultancy that advises firms on government schemes. The consultancy does not bid for contracts but "facilitates" access. No rule explicitly bars this. Media alleges "conflict of interest."
Analysis
Domain: Spouse's private business — but uses Ministerial access/knowledge (public resource).
Impairment test: Conduct Rules (AIS 1968) Rule 18: "Avoid conflict of interest." Rule 20: "Declare interest." Even without explicit ban, appearance of access-selling impairs public trust. Facilitation = using insider knowledge = misuse of office.
Proportionality: Not criminal (no bribe proven). But administrative action required: (1) Mandatory declaration. (2) Recusal from files where spouse's clients appear. (3) Spouse's firm barred from "facilitating" Ministry schemes. (4) Cooling-off: spouse exits business or officer seeks transfer.
Person vs Office: Officer's personal integrity may be intact. But the institutional integrity of the Ministry is compromised by the structure. The office demands the structure be fixed.

Case 8.2: Private Social Media Conduct

Case
An IAS officer posts on personal Instagram: a reel mocking a government scheme ("another jumla"), wearing uniform in background. No official handle used. Complaint filed under Conduct Rules.
Analysis
Domain: Private speech — but uniform in background blurs domain. Conduct Rules: "No criticism of government policy in public." Supreme Court (Lipika Paul, 2020): Public servants have free speech but subject to "reasonable restrictions" — discipline, neutrality, public trust.
Impairment: Uniform + mockery = undermines neutrality, signals partisan bias, erodes trust. Citizens see the officer, not the private person.
Proportionality: Not dismissal. Warning + counselling + social media guidelines training. Repeat offence = departmental inquiry.
Principle: Private speech is protected; speech that appears official or uses official symbols is not. The officer represents the State 24/7 in public perception.

Case 8.3: The Honest Officer with a Corrupt Relative

Case
You are an SP. Your brother (private businessman) is arrested for bribing a different department's officer. Media links you: "SP's brother corrupt." You had no knowledge, no involvement. Pressure to transfer you.
Analysis
Domain: Brother's private crime. No use of your office, no shared proceeds, no quid pro quo.
Impairment test: Zero direct impairment. But perception risk: public may suspect nexus. Conduct Rules don't punish relatives' acts unless officer abets/benefits.
Proportionality: Transfer is punishment without fault — violates natural justice. Right response: (1) Voluntary declaration to DGP. (2) Request independent inquiry into any nexus. (3) Continue duties; transparency is the shield. (4) If inquiry clears you, public statement by DGP.
Person vs Office: Your private family life includes a criminal relative — that is not your moral failing. Your public duty is unimpaired. The test is your conduct, not your kinship.

Case 8.4: Post-Retirement Employment

Case
A Secretary (Defence) retires. Six months later, joins the board of a major defence contractor that bid on contracts she cleared. No cooling-off period violated (rules require 1 year for some posts, but not all).
Analysis
Domain: Post-retirement private employment — but directly linked to official decisions.
Impairment: "Revolving door" creates ex ante incentive to favour future employer. Even if no quid pro quo occurred, the appearance corrupts institutional trust. 2nd ARC recommended 2-year cooling-off for all commercial posts related to former portfolio.
Proportionality: Legal? Possibly. Ethical? No. Gandhi's trusteeship: office held in trust, not for future private gain. Constitutional morality (Ambedkar): public office must not be a stepping stone to private profit.
Remedy: Voluntary resignation from board; advocate for stricter cooling-off law; disclose all post-retirement offers for 5 years.
Case-method template for Public vs Private
In the exam, use this 4-step structure for any public-private case:
1. Domain classification — private / public / blurred (cite Conduct Rule).
2. Impairment test — conflict? trust erosion? impartiality hit?
3. Proportional response — declare, recuse, transfer, inquiry, law change.
4. Principle statement — "Office is trust (Gandhi); constitutional morality > personal convenience (Ambedkar); perception matters as much as fact (Nolan)."

9. Applied Ethics — Environment, Bioethics, Business & Media Ethics

The syllabus: Applied ethics in specific domains. GS IV tests whether candidates can transfer ethical frameworks (virtue, deontology, consequentialism, Gandhian, constitutional) to concrete policy domains. Four domains recur: Environment, Bioethics, Business, Media.

9.1 Environmental Ethics

Core tension: Anthropocentrism (human welfare first) vs. Ecocentrism (intrinsic value of nature) vs. Sustainable development (intergenerational equity).

Framework Environmental Principle Administrative Application
Consequentialism Cost-benefit analysis of ecosystem services; discount rate for future EIA quantification; Net Present Value of forest vs project
Deontology Nature has rights; duties to future generations (Kant: treat humanity as end — includes future humans) Absolute bans: no mining in Protected Areas; Species Protection Act
Virtue Ethics Stewardship, humility, temperance (consumption) Officer as trustee of commons; lifestyle audit for environment posts
Gandhian Trusteeship — earth held in trust; antodaya includes ecological poor Gram Sabha consent (FRA/PESA); community forest management
Constitutional Article 48A (State shall protect environment); Article 51A(g) (fundamental duty) SC: Article 21 includes right to clean environment (Subhash Kumar, 1991)
Key doctrines for GS IV
Precautionary Principle: Where threats of serious/irreversible damage exist, lack of full scientific certainty shall not postpone cost-effective measures. (Rio Declaration 1992; adopted in Indian law via SC in Vellore Citizens Forum, 1996).
Polluter Pays Principle: Polluter bears cost of remediation, not public. (SC: Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action, 1996).
Intergenerational Equity: Present generation holds earth in trust for future. (SC: Goa Foundation, 2014 — mining lease renewal denied).
Public Trust Doctrine: Natural resources (air, water, forests) are held by State in trust for public; cannot be alienated. (SC: M.C. Mehta, 1997).

9.3 Business Ethics

Core tension: Shareholder primacy (Friedman: profit maximisation within law) vs. Stakeholder capitalism (Freeman: value for all stakeholders) vs. Trusteeship (Gandhi: wealth held in trust).

CSR in India — Legal & Ethical Framework
Legal: Companies Act 2013, Section 135 — 2% of average net profits for companies meeting threshold (net worth ₹500Cr / turnover ₹1000Cr / net profit ₹5Cr). Schedule VII lists eligible activities. Ethical critique: 2% is a floor, not a ceiling. "Compliance CSR" (box-ticking) vs "Strategic CSR" (shared value). Gandhi's trusteeship: business is an organ of society; profit is a byproduct of service. Governance angle: MCA monitors; CSR Committee of Board; impact assessment for large projects. Officer's role: District-level CSR convergence — align corporate CSR with district development plans (aspirational districts), avoid duplication, ensure community ownership.

9.4 Media Ethics

Core tension: Freedom of press (Article 19(1)(a)) vs. Responsibility (accuracy, fairness, privacy, national security).

Principle Ethical Standard Indian Mechanism Officer's Interface
Accuracy & Verification Report facts, not allegations; right of reply PCI Norms of Journalistic Conduct; News Broadcasters Standards Authority Proactive disclosure (RTI S.4) reduces speculation; timely press briefings
Privacy vs Public Interest Private life unless public relevance; no trial by media Article 21 (Privacy, Puttaswamy 2017); Contempt of Court Act Protect victim/witness identity (POCSO, rape cases); no media leaks from investigation
Communal Harmony No hate speech; verify before reporting communal incidents IPC 153A, 295A; Cable TV Networks Rules; PCI guidelines District-level media coordination; counter rumours fast; no custodial leaks
Paid News / Native Advertising Editorial independence; disclosure of sponsored content PCI guidelines; ECI paid news monitoring during elections Government ads via DAVP only; no quid pro quo for coverage
Digital / Social Media Virality vs verification; algorithmic amplification; deepfakes IT Rules 2021 (intermediary liability); Fact-check units Official handles for verified info; cyber cell coordination; media literacy
Media ethics case template
Case: A viral video shows a police officer allegedly beating a protestor. The video is edited; context missing. Media runs it prime time. Officer suspended. Later, full video shows protestor attacked first.
Answer: (1) Officer: due process — inquiry before suspension (natural justice). (2) Media: failed verification duty (PCI norms); should have sought police version. (3) Administration: proactive release of full CCTV/body-cam footage (transparency). (4) Systemic: mandatory body-cams; 24-hr media cell for incident response; media literacy for officers.

10. Probity in Governance — RTI, Citizens Charter, Lokpal, Codes

Section 4 introduced probity. This section deep-dives into the four institutional pillars with legal precision, case law, and administrative application — the level UPSC expects in 150/250-word answers.

10.1 Right to Information Act, 2005 — The Transparency Engine

Key Provisions (Must-Know for GS IV)

Section Provision GS IV Relevance
S. 2(h) "Public authority" — includes NGOs substantially financed by govt Broad coverage; not just departments
S. 4 Proactive disclosure (suo motu) — publish 17 categories of info Ethical duty: transparency by default, not on request
S. 6 Application process — no reason needed; fee ₹10; BPL exempt Low barrier = citizen empowerment
S. 7 Time limit: 30 days (48 hrs for life/liberty); deemed refusal = appeal Time-bound = accountability
S. 8 Exemptions: sovereignty, security, strategic, commercial confidence, fiduciary, personal privacy, safety, cabinet papers (post-decision) Exemptions are qualified — public interest override (S. 8(2))
S. 11 Third-party notice — opportunity to object before disclosure Procedural fairness
S. 18–20 CIC powers: impose penalty (₹250/day, max ₹25,000); recommend disciplinary action Penalty on PIO = personal accountability
S. 24 Exempted organisations: IB, RAW, BSF, CRPF, etc. (but corruption/human rights not exempt) Even security agencies accountable for corruption/rights

Landmark Judgments

  • CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011): Answer sheets are "information" — must be disclosed. Redefines "information" broadly.
  • Giriraj Singh v. CIC (2012): File notings are "information" — disclosure unless exempt. Crucial: File notings are not exempt per se; they reflect decision-making.
  • Namit Sharma v. Union of India (2013): CIC cannot be a rubber stamp; must apply judicial mind.
  • Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India (2019): Vacancies in CIC violate RTI Act; SC directed time-bound appointments.

10.2 Citizen's Charter — From Promise to Performance

Six Principles (Sevottam Model): (1) Published Standards — specific, measurable, time-bound. (2) Openness & Information — charter accessible, multilingual. (3) Choice & Consultation — citizens consulted in design. (4) Courtesy & Helpfulness — staff behaviour standards. (5) Grievance Redress — time-bound, multi-channel, escalation. (6) Value for Money — efficiency, no hidden costs.

Making Charters Ethical (Not Paper Promises)
Defects in practice: Generic standards ("prompt service"), no monitoring, no penalty for breach, no citizen awareness.
Ethical design: (1) Co-create with citizen groups (consultation). (2) Standards = "Birth certificate in 3 working days" not "promptly." (3) Public dashboard: real-time % compliance per service. (4) Grievance: online + offline; auto-escalation at 50% time; compensation for delay (like service guarantee laws in MP, J&K). (5) Annual social audit by independent agency. (6) Link to officer's ACR — charter compliance = performance metric.
Sevottam Framework: DARPG's 3 modules: Charter, Grievance, Capability Building. ISO 10002 certification for grievance systems.

10.3 Lokpal & Lokayuktas — The Anti-Corruption Architecture

Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 — Key Provisions

Aspect Provision GS IV Application
Composition Chairperson + max 8 members (50% judicial); SC Judge / CJ of HC / eminent person Independence via selection committee (PM, LoP, CJI, Speaker, eminent jurist)
Jurisdiction PM (with safeguards), Ministers, MPs (Art 105), Group A-D officers, NGOs receiving govt funds Wide coverage; PM inclusion with in-camera procedure
Powers Preliminary inquiry → Investigation (CBI/own wing) → Prosecution (special court); attachment of property Investigation + prosecution under one roof
Whistleblower Protection S. 63: Identity protection; no victimisation; penalise false complaints Integrated with Whistleblower Protection Act 2014
Time Limits Inquiry 60 days (extendable 6 months); Investigation 6 months (extendable 6); Trial 1 year (extendable 1) Time-bound = deterrence
Lokayuktas States must establish within 1 year; consistent with Central Act Federal anti-corruption architecture

Critical Gaps (For "Critically Examine" Answers)

  • Selection Committee imbalance: Government majority (PM, Speaker, LoP if recognised) vs judicial minority.
  • No suo motu power: Lokpal acts only on complaints; cannot initiate on credible information.
  • Anonymous complaints barred: S. 14 requires complainant identity — deters whistleblowers.
  • CBI dependence: Investigation wing under Lokpal but CBI still under govt control for non-Lokpal cases.
  • States' dilution: Many Lokayuktas weak (no prosecution wing, no time limits, CM excluded).

10.4 Codes of Ethics vs Codes of Conduct — Operationalising the Distinction

Code of Ethics (Aspirational, Values-based): "Civil servants shall uphold the Constitution," "Serve with integrity and compassion." Not directly enforceable; guides judgment in grey areas. 2nd ARC recommended a statutory Code of Ethics for all civil services.

Code of Conduct (Prescriptive, Rule-based): "No gifts above ₹5,000," "No political activity," "Declare assets annually." Enforceable; breach → disciplinary action (CCA Rules). Current: All India Services Conduct Rules 1968; Central Civil Services Conduct Rules 1964.

Why Both Are Needed (Exam Answer)
Rules cannot cover every situation — e.g., "Is it ethical to accept hospitality from a contractor's wife at a wedding?" Code of Conduct may be silent. Code of Ethics (integrity, impartiality) guides: "Does this create obligation? Does it impair perception?" Conversely, Ethics without Conduct is toothless — "uphold integrity" is unactionable without "no gifts above ₹5,000." Integration: Conduct Rules operationalise Ethics; Ethics interprets Conduct Rules in grey zones. 2nd ARC: Statutory Code of Ethics + existing Conduct Rules = complete framework. Officer's duty: Know both; when in doubt, apply the stricter standard.

10.5 Integrated Probity Framework — The Officer's Checklist

Probity Decision Checklist (Memorise for Case Studies)
When a case involves probity, run this mental checklist:
☐ RTI: Is info being proactively disclosed (S.4)? Is PIO responding in 30 days? Is S.8 exemption justified?
☐ Citizen's Charter: Are standards specific, published, monitored? Is grievance redress time-bound with escalation?
☐ Lokpal: Does the allegation fall under Lokpal jurisdiction? Is there a whistleblower? Is identity protected?
☐ Codes: Does Conduct Rule explicitly cover this? If not, what does Code of Ethics (integrity, impartiality) demand?
☐ Systemic: Is this a one-off or pattern? What institutional fix prevents recurrence (e-procurement, rotation, audit, transparency)?
