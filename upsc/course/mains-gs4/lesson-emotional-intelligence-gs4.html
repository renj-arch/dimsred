1. Foundations of Emotional Intelligence

1.1 Historical Evolution and Definition

The term emotional intelligence entered scholarly discourse in the early 1990s when Peter Salovey and John Mayer published a seminal article that defined it as the ability to monitor one’s own and others’ feelings, to discriminate among them, and to use this information to guide thinking and action. Their formulation built on earlier work in social intelligence by Edward Thorndike and on the growing recognition that purely cognitive measures such as IQ could not fully explain success in complex social environments. In 1995 Daniel Goleman popularised the concept through a best‑selling book that expanded the definition to include a broader set of competencies such as self‑motivation, empathy, and social skill, thereby creating a mixed model that blended ability with personality traits. Since then, academic debate has centred on whether emotional intelligence should be treated as a distinct mental ability, a constellation of personality dispositions, or a set of learnable skills, and this debate continues to shape both research and practical training programmes for civil servants.

1.2 The Role of Emotions in Human Behaviour

Emotions are not merely disruptive forces; they serve adaptive functions by signalling the significance of events, preparing physiological responses, and facilitating rapid decision‑making under uncertainty. Neuroscientific evidence shows that the amygdala processes threat cues before the prefrontal cortex can engage in deliberative analysis, which explains why emotional reactions often precede rational thought. When individuals lack awareness of these automatic signals, they may act impulsively or suppress valuable information, leading to sub‑optimal outcomes in administrative contexts where stakes are high and time is limited. Conversely, the capacity to recognise, label, and regulate emotional states enables a public servant to maintain composure, evaluate alternatives objectively, and communicate decisions in a manner that preserves trust and legitimacy.

1.3 Why Emotional Intelligence Matters for Public Service

Public administration involves continuous interaction with diverse stakeholders — citizens, elected representatives, non‑governmental organisations, and subordinate staff — each bringing distinct expectations, cultural backgrounds, and emotional states. An officer who can accurately perceive the emotional climate of a community meeting, regulate personal frustration when faced with bureaucratic delays, and motivate a team through shared purpose will achieve policy implementation more effectively than one who relies solely on technical expertise. Moreover, the ethical dimension of governance demands empathy to appreciate the impact of decisions on vulnerable groups, and self‑awareness to recognise personal biases that could compromise impartiality. Consequently, emotional intelligence has been incorporated into the competency frameworks of several Indian civil‑service training institutions, reflecting its recognised contribution to good governance.

2. Major Models of Emotional Intelligence

2.1 Ability Model of Mayer and Salovey

The ability model conceptualises emotional intelligence as a set of four hierarchical mental abilities: perceiving emotions accurately in oneself and others, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotional meanings and transitions, and managing emotions to promote personal growth. These abilities are measured through performance‑based tests such as the Mayer‑Salovey‑Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT), which presents respondents with tasks like identifying emotions in facial expressions or selecting the most effective emotional strategy for a given scenario. Because the model treats emotional intelligence as a cognitive capacity, it predicts that individuals with higher scores will demonstrate superior problem‑solving in emotionally charged situations, a claim supported by meta‑analyses linking MSCEIT scores to academic and workplace performance.

2.2 Mixed Model of Goleman

Goleman’s mixed model expands the construct to include five domains — self‑awareness, self‑regulation, motivation, empathy, and social skills — each comprising a cluster of competencies that blend innate ability with learned behaviour. Unlike the ability model, the mixed model is typically assessed through self‑report instruments such as the Emotional Competence Inventory (ECI) or 360‑degree feedback, which capture both the individual’s perception of their own skills and the observations of peers and supervisors. This approach has proved popular in organisational development because it yields actionable feedback for leadership coaching, yet critics argue that self‑report measures conflate emotional intelligence with personality traits such as extraversion and conscientiousness, reducing discriminant validity.

2.3 Trait Model of Petrides and Furnham

The trait model, advanced by Konstantinos Petrides and Adrian Furnham, defines emotional intelligence as a constellation of emotional self‑perceptions located at the lower levels of personality hierarchies. It is measured exclusively through self‑report questionnaires such as the Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (TEIQue), which assesses facets like adaptability, assertiveness, and stress management. Proponents contend that trait emotional intelligence captures the typical behavioural tendencies that influence how people habitually respond to emotional stimuli, and that it predicts outcomes such as job satisfaction and mental health more robustly than ability‑based scores. However, because it relies on subjective self‑assessment, the trait model is vulnerable to social desirability bias and does not directly measure maximal performance.

2.4 Comparative Overview of the Three Models

Understanding the distinctions among these models is essential for selecting appropriate assessment tools and for designing training interventions that target the right level of intervention — whether the goal is to improve maximal emotional reasoning (ability model), to develop observable leadership behaviours (mixed model), or to shift habitual emotional self‑perceptions (trait model). 3. Core Competencies of Emotional Intelligence

3.1 Self-Awareness

Self‑awareness is the foundational competency that involves real‑time recognition of one’s emotional states, triggers, and the influence of those states on thoughts and behaviours. A civil servant who can label a rising sense of irritation during a prolonged meeting is better positioned to choose a deliberate response rather than reacting impulsively. Research using experience‑sampling methods shows that individuals with high self‑awareness report lower emotional exhaustion and higher job performance because they can intervene early in the emotion‑regulation cycle. In the Indian administrative context, self‑awareness also entails an understanding of one’s positional power and the ethical responsibility it carries, preventing the misuse of authority.

3.2 Self-Regulation

Self‑regulation builds on self‑awareness by enabling the modulation of emotional intensity, duration, and expression in line with situational demands. Techniques such as cognitive reappraisal — reframing a stressful event as a challenge rather than a threat — and response modulation — deliberately slowing speech and breathing — have been shown to reduce physiological arousal and improve decision quality under pressure. For administrators, self‑regulation is critical during crisis briefings, where visible panic can cascade through the organisational hierarchy, and during disciplinary proceedings, where impartiality must be visibly maintained.

3.3 Motivation

In the emotional intelligence framework, motivation refers to the internal drive to pursue goals with energy and persistence, especially when external rewards are delayed or absent. This intrinsic motivation is linked to the concept of “flow” and to the ability to align personal values with organisational mission. Officers who derive purpose from public service are more likely to sustain effort during long‑term projects such as rural development schemes, where outcomes materialise over years. Moreover, motivated leaders inspire similar commitment in subordinates, creating a positive feedback loop that enhances organisational resilience.

3.4 Empathy

Empathy comprises both affective resonance — feeling what another person feels — and cognitive perspective‑taking — understanding another’s viewpoint without necessarily sharing the emotion. In governance, empathy allows a policy maker to anticipate the unintended consequences of a regulation on marginalized communities, and it guides a frontline officer in delivering services with cultural sensitivity. Neuroscientific studies differentiate the neural circuits for affective empathy (insula, anterior cingulate) from those for cognitive empathy (temporoparietal junction), suggesting that training programmes can target each component separately through role‑plays and reflective debriefings.

3.5 Social Skills

Social skills integrate the preceding competencies into effective interpersonal behaviour — communication, conflict management, team building, and influence. An administrator with strong social skills can translate technical data into narratives that resonate with elected representatives, negotiate inter‑departmental resource sharing, and mediate disputes between citizen groups. The ability to give constructive feedback without triggering defensiveness, to facilitate inclusive meetings, and to build coalitions around reform initiatives distinguishes high‑performing leaders from merely competent managers.

4. Application in Administration

4.1 Leadership and Decision Making

Leadership in the public sector demands the integration of analytical rigour with emotional insight. When a senior officer evaluates competing policy options, self‑awareness helps surface personal preferences that could colour the analysis, while empathy ensures that the perspectives of affected citizens are weighed appropriately. Self‑regulation prevents the escalation of conflict during heated deliberations, and motivation sustains the effort required to see a decision through implementation. Empirical studies of Indian Administrative Service officers have shown that those scoring higher on mixed‑model assessments receive superior performance appraisals and are more frequently selected for flagship programmes.

4.2 Building Organisational Climate

Organisational climate — the shared perceptions of policies, practices, and procedures — is strongly shaped by the emotional tone set by leaders. A district magistrate who regularly acknowledges staff contributions, models constructive feedback, and addresses grievances with empathy cultivates a climate of psychological safety. Such an environment encourages innovation, reduces turnover, and improves the quality of public service delivery. Conversely, a climate characterised by emotional suppression or punitive responses to mistakes leads to risk aversion and concealment of errors, which can be catastrophic in sectors like disaster management.

4.3 Ethical Governance and Public Trust

Ethical governance rests on the alignment of personal integrity with institutional norms. Emotional intelligence contributes by sharpening the moral imagination — the capacity to foresee the ethical ramifications of actions on diverse stakeholders. Self‑awareness reveals conflicts of interest before they manifest, self‑regulation curbs the temptation to rationalise shortcuts, and empathy ensures that the voices of the less powerful are heard in policy formulation. Public trust, a scarce resource in any democracy, is reinforced when citizens perceive that officials not only follow rules but also demonstrate genuine concern for their well‑being.

4.1 Emotional Intelligence in Citizen-Centric Administration

The administrative value of emotional intelligence is most visible at the counter. A district office receives citizens who are anxious, angry or desperate; an officer who can recognise his own emotional reaction, regulate it, and read the feelings of the person in front of him resolves a grievance in one visit that a reactive officer escalates into a complaint. Empathy does not mean agreeing with every demand; it means understanding the fear or frustration behind it and responding to the problem rather than to the provocation. In service delivery and grievance redressal, the competencies of self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skill translate directly into courtesy, patience, and follow-through.

4.2 Emotional Intelligence in Crisis, Law and Order, and Leadership

In a crisis such as a flood, a riot or an epidemic, the officer's composure sets the tone for the whole administration: a team led by someone who stays calm, communicates clearly and acknowledges fear performs better than one led by a panicking or abusive superior. In law and order, de-escalation, negotiation and the ability to separate the ringleader from the crowd are emotional skills as much as tactical ones, and they reduce the risk of violence. In the internal life of the office, emotional intelligence allows the officer to mentor junior staff, manage conflict between subordinates, negotiate with employee unions, and hold a team together when resources are short. The caution to record is that emotional intelligence must serve ethical ends; the same skills that persuade and calm can be misused to manipulate, and it is the values of the civil service that keep empathy honest.

5. Emotional Intelligence in Conflict and Crisis Management

5.1 Conflict Resolution through Empathy and Regulation

Conflict in administrative settings often arises from competing resource claims, cultural misunderstandings, or perceived procedural injustice. An officer equipped with empathy can map the underlying needs of each party — security, recognition, autonomy — and reframe the dispute from a zero‑sum contest to a problem‑solving exercise. Self‑regulation allows the mediator to remain neutral, manage personal frustration, and model calm communication, which in turn lowers the emotional temperature of the participants. Structured dialogue techniques such as interest‑based negotiation, grounded in emotional intelligence principles, have successfully resolved land‑acquisition disputes in several states.

5.2 Crisis Leadership: Maintaining Composure and Communication

During natural disasters, public health emergencies, or law‑and‑order breakdowns, the emotional climate of the affected population and of the response machinery can determine outcomes. Leaders who display composure — a visible manifestation of self‑regulation — provide a stabilising anchor that reduces panic and facilitates coordinated action. Simultaneously, empathetic communication that acknowledges fear, grief, and uncertainty builds credibility and encourages compliance with evacuation orders or health advisories. The 2018 Kerala floods illustrated how district collectors who combined real‑time situational awareness with compassionate messaging achieved higher community cooperation and faster relief distribution.

5.3 Post‑Crisis Learning and Institutional Resilience

After the immediate threat subsides, emotional intelligence guides the reflective processes that transform crisis experience into institutional learning. Self‑awareness enables leaders to confront personal performance gaps without defensiveness, while empathy ensures that the trauma of frontline staff and victims is recognised and addressed through psychosocial support. Motivation drives the systematic documentation of lessons learned, and social skills facilitate the dissemination of best practices across departments. Over time, this cycle of emotionally informed after‑action review strengthens organisational resilience, preparing the administration for future shocks.

Emotional intelligence thus operates as a cross‑cutting capability that enhances every facet of public administration, from routine service delivery to the most demanding crisis situations.

Neuroscientific Foundations of Emotional Intelligence

Contemporary research locates emotional intelligence in the interaction between the limbic system and the prefrontal cortex. The amygdala, a key node for threat detection and rapid affective appraisal, generates immediate emotional signals. The ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) modulate these signals, enabling re‑appraisal, impulse inhibition, and goal‑directed behaviour. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies show that individuals who score high on ability‑based EI tests exhibit stronger vmPFC‑amygdala connectivity during emotion‑regulation tasks. Mirror‑neuron networks in the inferior frontal gyrus and inferior parietal lobule underpin empathy by simulating observed affective states, providing a biological substrate for the “social awareness” component of Goleman’s model. Neuroplasticity evidence indicates that sustained mindfulness‑based training can increase grey‑matter density in the anterior cingulate cortex and insula, regions associated with interoceptive awareness and self‑regulation. For administrators, this neuroscience base validates the premise that EI is not a fixed trait but a set of trainable neural circuits, informing the design of capacity‑building programmes that target specific regulatory pathways rather than relying on generic exhortations.

Implications for Training Design

Training modules that combine cognitive re‑appraisal exercises with real‑time biofeedback (heart‑rate variability, skin conductance) accelerate the strengthening of top‑down control. The Indian Institute of Public Administration’s “Leadership and Emotional Resilience” course incorporates a brief neurofeedback session before scenario‑based role plays, a practice shown in pilot studies to improve composure scores by 12‑15 % on the MSCEIT “Managing Emotions” branch. Such evidence‑based approaches move EI development from anecdotal workshops to measurable neurocognitive change.

Assessment Instruments: Ability, Mixed, and Trait Measures

Three families of psychometric tools dominate EI assessment, each aligned with a theoretical model. The Mayer‑Salovey‑Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT, 2002) is a performance‑based ability measure requiring test‑takers to identify emotions in faces, facilitate thought with emotion, understand emotional blends, and manage emotions in vignettes. Scoring uses consensus and expert norms, yielding a total EIQ and four branch scores. The Emotional Competence Inventory (ECI, 1999) and its successor the Emotional and Social Competence Inventory (ESCI) are 360‑degree mixed‑model instruments rating 12 competencies grouped under self‑awareness, self‑management, social awareness, and relationship management. The Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (TEIQue, 2009) captures self‑perceived emotional dispositions across 15 facets (e.g., adaptability, assertiveness, emotion perception) and yields a global trait EI score. In Indian civil‑service contexts, the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2008) recommended a blended assessment: MSCEIT for entry‑level selection, ESCI for mid‑career leadership appraisal, and TEIQue for longitudinal self‑development tracking. Validation studies on Indian samples (n ≈ 1,200 officers) report acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.78‑0.86) and convergent validity with job‑performance ratings (r = 0.31‑0.42). However, cultural bias in facial‑expression stimuli and the social‑desirability susceptibility of self‑report scales remain documented limitations.

The narrative reports guide targeted coaching, while the mixed‑model framework aligns with the ARC’s leadership competency matrix. Both tools have Indian validation data, ensuring cultural relevance.

Emotional Intelligence in Policy Design and Implementation

Policy cycles — agenda setting, formulation, adoption, implementation, evaluation — each contain affective inflection points. During agenda setting, officials who can read public sentiment (social awareness) and manage their own framing biases (self‑regulation) produce problem definitions that resonate with stakeholders. In formulation, empathy enables designers to anticipate unintended consequences for vulnerable groups; the 2013 National Food Security Act’s “entitlement‑based” language reflects an empathic mapping of hunger‑related anxiety. Adoption requires persuasive communication: leaders high in social skill translate technical rationale into narratives that align with legislators’ values, reducing resistance. Implementation is where EI most directly affects outcomes. Frontline workers who regulate frustration when systems fail maintain service continuity; supervisors who recognise burnout signals (self‑awareness in others) redeploy resources before breakdowns occur. The 2020‑21 COVID‑19 vaccine rollout in several states illustrated this: districts where collectors held daily “emotional check‑ins” with health‑team leads reported 18‑22 % higher session‑site attendance. Evaluation benefits from emotionally intelligent after‑action reviews that surface tacit learning without blame, fostering a culture of continuous improvement.

Embedding EI in Standard Operating Procedures

Operationalising EI means codifying “emotional checkpoints” in SOPs: a mandatory pause for self‑regulation before issuing coercive orders, a structured empathy‑mapping template for citizen‑interface design, and a peer‑debrief protocol after high‑stress operations. The Karnataka Administrative Reforms Department’s 2022 circular on “Human‑Centric Governance” institutionalised such checkpoints, resulting in a measurable decline in citizen‑complaint escalation rates within six months.

Cultural and Gender Dimensions in Administrative Emotional Intelligence

Emotional expression, regulation norms, and empathy scripts vary across India’s linguistic, regional, and gender cultures. Collectivist contexts (e.g., many rural communities) value relational harmony over individual assertiveness, shaping how self‑awareness and social skill manifest. An officer trained in a Western‑centric EI model may misinterpret restrained affect as low engagement, while the same behaviour signals respect in a high‑context culture. Gender socialisation further modulates EI display: women officers often receive higher empathy ratings but face a “double bind” — expected to be nurturing yet penalised for perceived lack of authority. Research on Indian Police Service cohorts (n = 340) shows female officers score higher on TEIQue “empathy” and “relationship” facets but lower on “assertiveness” and “stress management”, reflecting structural role expectations rather than ability deficits. Culturally intelligent administration therefore requires calibrating EI competencies: replacing “assertive communication” with “firm‑respectful dialogue” in training curricula, and designing mentorship circles that validate diverse emotional repertoires. The 2019 “Gender‑Responsive Policing” guidelines issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs explicitly embed such calibrated EI standards.

Intersectionality in Practice

An intersectional lens reveals that a Dalit woman officer in a patriarchal, caste‑stratified district navigates layered emotional labour: managing her own stereotype threat, decoding community mistrust, and regulating superiors’ bias. EI development programmes that ignore intersectionality risk reinforcing inequities by prescribing a monolithic “ideal” emotional style. Inclusive programmes incorporate narrative‑based reflective journals and peer‑learning groups segmented by identity axes, allowing participants to surface context‑specific regulation strategies.

Evidence‑Based Development Programmes for Civil Servants

Meta‑analyses of workplace EI interventions (e.g., Hodzic et al., 2018; 42 studies, N = 3,800) report a mean effect size d = 0.56 for ability‑EI gains and d = 0.44 for trait‑EI gains when programmes exceed 30 hours, combine didactic input with deliberate practice, and include coaching feedback. Translating this to the Indian civil service, the Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration (LBSNAA) redesigned its Foundation Course in 2021 to embed a 40‑hour “Emotional Resilience Lab”. The lab comprises: (1) MSCEIT baseline; (2) eight weekly modules — mindfulness‑based attention training, cognitive re‑appraisal drills, perspective‑taking simulations, and difficult‑conversation role‑plays with trained actors; (3) bi‑weekly peer‑coaching circles using the GROW model; (4) post‑course MSCEIT and ESCI 360°. Pilot data (batch 2022, n = 180) show a statistically significant increase in MSCEIT total score (Δ = +7.3 points, p < 0.01) and ESCI “Conflict Management” competency (Δ = +0.68 on 5‑point scale). Sustainability is addressed through a “Booster” refresher at the 12‑month mark and integration of EI metrics into the Annual Performance Appraisal Report (APAR) narrative section. The programme’s cost‑benefit analysis estimates a 1:4 return via reduced grievance escalations and lower medical‑leave utilisation.

Scaling Across Services

The Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) 2023 circular “Mainstreaming Emotional Intelligence in Capacity Building” mandates that all Central Training Institutes adopt the LBSNAA template, contextualised for service‑specific scenarios (e.g., customs officers handling smuggler confrontations, revenue officers negotiating land‑acquisition). A national EI competency framework, aligned with the 12 ESCI competencies, is under finalisation to enable cross‑service mobility and common assessment language.

Emotional Intelligence and Algorithmic Decision‑Making in Digital Governance

The rapid deployment of algorithmic tools for service delivery, grievance redressal, and predictive policing creates a new interface where human emotional intelligence must mediate machine outputs. Algorithms excel at pattern detection but lack the contextual empathy required to interpret ambiguous citizen narratives, cultural idioms, or distress signals embedded in unstructured data. Senior administrators therefore need a hybrid competence: the ability to audit algorithmic fairness, to recognise when a model’s confidence score masks systemic bias, and to intervene with emotionally informed discretion. The National e‑Governance Plan (NeGP) 2022 revision explicitly mandates “human‑in‑the‑loop” checkpoints for high‑impact decisions such as benefit eligibility, land‑record mutation, and preventive detention alerts. These checkpoints are operationalised through a three‑step protocol: (1) automated risk scoring, (2) an EI‑trained case officer reviews flagged cases using structured empathy mapping, and (3) a documented justification is logged for audit trails. Empirical work from the Indian Institute of Public Administration (IIPA) shows that districts adopting this protocol reduced erroneous denial rates by 18 % within the first year, while citizen satisfaction scores rose by 12 % on the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology’s unified feedback platform.

From a theoretical standpoint, the Ability Model’s “using emotions to facilitate thought” maps directly onto the officer’s task of integrating algorithmic probability with affective cues — tone of voice in a call‑center transcript, hesitation in a video‑conference, or the phrasing of a written complaint. The Mixed Model’s “social awareness” and “relationship management” competencies guide the officer in communicating algorithmic decisions transparently, preserving trust. Training modules now embed scenario‑based simulations where officers practice “explainable AI” dialogues, using the GROW coaching framework to structure the conversation: Goal (clarify the decision), Reality (present data and emotional context), Options (offer appeal pathways), Will (agree on next steps). This integration ensures that algorithmic efficiency does not erode the constitutional guarantee of fair procedure.

Inter‑Agency Emotional Coordination in Disaster Response Operations

Large‑scale disasters — floods in Assam, cyclones on the Odisha coast, or the 2023 Himalayan glacial lake outburst — demand seamless coordination among the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF), state police, health departments, and local panchayats. Technical SOPs prescribe resource allocation, but the emotional climate across agencies determines whether information flows, trust holds, and frontline personnel sustain effort under prolonged stress. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) 2021 “Unified Command” doctrine introduced an “Emotional Liaison Officer” (ELO) role at each joint operations centre. The ELO is a mid‑level officer trained in the MSCEIT ability branches of perceiving and managing emotions, tasked with monitoring inter‑agency tension, facilitating debrief circles, and translating technical directives into language that respects the cultural emotional scripts of affected communities.

Field evaluations from the 2022 Assam floods indicate that centres with an active ELO reported 27 % fewer communication breakdowns and a 15 % faster clearance of relief bottlenecks compared with centres lacking the role. The ELO’s toolkit includes real‑time “emotional pulse” surveys (five‑item Likert scales administered via secure mobile app), structured “after‑action emotional reviews” within 24 hours of each operational shift, and a shared “stress‑resource map” that visualises fatigue levels across units. These mechanisms operationalise the Trait Model’s “emotional self‑efficacy” and “adaptability” facets, turning abstract competencies into observable coordination behaviours. Importantly, the ELO does not replace command authority; rather, the role augments situational awareness with affective data, enabling commanders to allocate rest periods, rotate high‑stress tasks, and pre‑empt burnout‑induced errors.

Scenario‑Based Application
A joint operations centre during a cyclone response receives conflicting reports from the health department (medical supplies low) and the logistics wing (transport vehicles unavailable). This sequence leverages perception, regulation, and relationship management competencies to convert affective tension into operational resolution.

Reflective Supervision as a Mechanism for Sustained Emotional Resilience

Training programmes that end at the classroom door rarely translate into lasting behavioural change. The Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration (LBSNAA) 2023 “Reflective Supervision Framework” addresses this gap by institutionalising a monthly, peer‑facilitated supervision circle for officers in their first five years of service. Drawing on the clinical supervision model used in psychotherapy, each circle comprises four participants and a trained facilitator who guides a structured reflection on a recent emotionally charged incident — whether a hostile public hearing, a disciplinary inquiry, or a personal loss affecting duty performance. The protocol follows the “Description‑Analysis‑Meaning‑Action” (DAMA) cycle: the presenter describes the event without judgement; peers analyse the emotional triggers using the MSCEIT “understanding emotions” branch; the group extracts meaning by linking the episode to personal values and organisational ethics; finally, the presenter commits to a concrete behavioural experiment for the next month.

Longitudinal data from the 2021‑2023 cohorts (n = 312) show that officers who attended at least eight supervision cycles improved their ESCI 360° “emotional self‑control” scores by an average of 9 points, while a matched control group showed no significant change. The framework also surfaces systemic stressors — such as ambiguous transfer policies or inadequate grievance redressal — that individual coping cannot resolve, feeding upward into DoPT policy reviews. By embedding reflective supervision within the career progression calendar, the service moves beyond episodic workshops toward a culture of continuous emotional learning, aligning with the Mixed Model’s emphasis on “developing others” and the Trait Model’s “emotional self‑efficacy”.

Ethical Safeguards Against Emotional Manipulation in Behavioural Public Policy

Behavioural insights units across central ministries increasingly employ “nudges” that leverage affect — fear appeals in tax compliance messages, social‑norm framing in vaccination drives, or loss‑aversion cues in subsidy targeting. While such designs can improve uptake, they risk crossing the line into emotional manipulation when the affective lever bypasses deliberative consent. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2009) warned that “policy instruments must respect the autonomy of the citizen”. The episode underscores that emotional intelligence in policy is not merely about empathy; it is a governance safeguard ensuring that affective tools serve public interest without eroding trust.

Integrating Emotional Intelligence into Performance Appraisal and Promotion Frameworks

Traditional Annual Confidential Reports (ACRs) in the All India Services weight domain expertise, integrity and output metrics, while emotional competencies remain implicit. Operationalising the LRI requires three procedural steps.
