1. Decoding Ethics Case Studies & Directive Words

1.1 Why Directive Words Decide Your Score

UPSC does not ask "what do you think?" — it asks you to analyse, critically examine, evaluate, comment, discuss, distinguish, explain, illustrate. Each verb carries a distinct structural demand. Candidates who ignore the verb write generic essays; candidates who obey it write structured arguments that examiners can tick-mark.

Directive Word What It Demands Answer Structure Common Trap
Analyse Break into components, show relationships, explain how parts interact Define → Component 1 → Component 2 → Interaction/Synthesis → Conclusion Listing parts without showing how they affect each other
Critically Examine Analyse + judge strengths/weaknesses, give a reasoned verdict Define → Arguments for → Arguments against → Weighed judgment → Conclusion Only listing pros/cons without a final position
Evaluate Assess value, significance, or effectiveness against criteria State criteria → Apply to subject → Score each criterion → Overall verdict Describing instead of judging; no explicit criteria
Comment Give an informed opinion grounded in evidence/reasoning Context → Your position → Evidence/Reasoning → Counter-view → Rebuttal → Conclusion Unsubstantiated personal opinion
Discuss Present multiple perspectives, explore dimensions Define → Perspective A → Perspective B → Perspective C → Synthesis → Conclusion One-sided treatment; missing synthesis
Distinguish Highlight differences on specific bases Table with columns: Basis / Concept 1 / Concept 2 → 3–5 rows of differences Writing two separate paragraphs instead of a comparative table
Explain Make clear the mechanism, cause, or reasoning Definition → Step-by-step mechanism/reasoning → Example → Significance Stating what without how/why
Illustrate Clarify with concrete examples Concept → Example 1 (detail) → Example 2 (contrast) → What the examples prove Abstract explanation with no named, specific examples

1.2 Directive Words in Case-Study Sub-Questions

Section B case studies typically carry 3–4 sub-questions. Each sub-question uses a directive word that tells you what to deliver for that specific sub-part:

Typical case-study sub-question pattern
Q(a): Identify the ethical dilemmas involved. → Directive: Identify. List 2–3 dilemmas in one sentence each. No analysis needed.
Q(b): What are the options available to you? Evaluate each. → Directive: Evaluate. List 3–4 options. For each: Pros (ethical/legal/administrative) + Cons + Net assessment.
Q(c): Which option would you choose and why? → Directive: Choose + Justify. State ONE option clearly. Justify with constitutional values, stakeholder impact, and long-term precedent.
Q(d): Suggest measures to prevent such situations. → Directive: Suggest. Systemic solutions (institutional, procedural, cultural), not personal pledges.

1.3 Case-Study Archetypes (Know the Pattern, Not Just the Story)

UPSC recycles dilemma archetypes with different surface facts. Recognise the archetype, and you pre-load the ethical framework:

Archetype Core Tension Key Values in Play Typical Directive
Corruption / Bribe Offer Personal gain / career safety vs. integrity / public trust Integrity, probity, accountability, legal duty Evaluate options, choose course of action
Compassion vs. Rules Helping a deserving individual vs. following procedure/law Compassion, empathy, rule of law, impartiality, equity Critically examine, comment on decision
Whistleblowing / Loyalty Conflict Loyalty to senior/team vs. duty to Constitution/public Integrity, constitutional morality, courage, legal protection Analyse dilemmas, justify decision
Conflict of Interest Personal/private interest vs. public duty Impartiality, transparency, probity, recusal Identify, evaluate, suggest safeguards
Political Pressure Obeying illegitimate political directive vs. constitutional duty Non-partisanship, constitutional morality, courage, rule of law Critically examine, comment
Resource Allocation / Triage Competing legitimate claims, insufficient resources Justice, equity, utilitarianism, procedural fairness Analyse, evaluate, suggest framework
Data Privacy / Surveillance Security/efficiency vs. individual privacy/rights Right to privacy, proportionality, accountability, transparency Discuss, critically examine

1.4 The "Hidden" Directive: Ethical Theory Application

No UPSC question explicitly says "apply Kant." But every critically examine, evaluate, justify implicitly demands a normative framework. The candidate who writes "Option A respects the dignity of the citizen as an end in itself (Kant)" scores higher than the one who writes "Option A is good because it protects rights." Name the theory, state its principle, apply it to the option.

Directive-word cheat sheet for the exam hall
Print this table on one A5 card. Before writing any sub-question, tick the directive word. If it says critically examine, your answer MUST have a final weighed judgment. If it says evaluate, you MUST state criteria first. If it says distinguish, you MUST use a table. No exceptions.

2. The 7-Step Case-Study Answer Framework

This framework is not a suggestion — it is the minimum viable structure for a 20-mark case study. Every step corresponds to what the examiner is trained to look for. Skip a step, lose marks. The framework works for 150-word and 250-word limits; you compress, not omit.

Step 1 — Facts & Core Dilemma (15–20 words)
What to write: One sentence summarising the situation. One sentence naming the ethical dilemma in standard form: "Value X vs. Value Y" or "Duty A vs. Duty B."
Example: "A District Magistrate discovers a senior officer has awarded a contract to a relative's firm. Core dilemma: Hierarchical loyalty vs. Integrity and probity in public office."
Step 2 — Stakeholder Mapping (30–40 words)
What to write: List 5–7 stakeholders in a table or bullet list. For each: (a) who they are, (b) their legitimate interest, (c) how they are affected. Include the institution and public trust as stakeholders — not just people.
Example: "1) You (DM) — career, conscience, legal duty. 2) Senior officer — reputation, career. 3) Relative's firm — financial gain. 4) Competing bidders — fair opportunity lost. 5) Public exchequer — potential loss. 6) Citizens — trust in administration. 7) Institution of civil service — precedent."
Step 3 — Ethical Principles & Legal Framework (30–40 words)
What to write: Name 3–4 constitutional/ethical/legal principles that govern this situation. Cite the specific article, rule, or thinker. This is where you show the normative scaffolding.
Example: "Article 14 (equality before law), Article 16 (equality of opportunity), Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (amended 2018) — criminalises both bribe-giver and taker, Whistleblower Protection Act 2014, Conduct Rules (AIS 1968) — impartiality, integrity. Kant: treat public office as duty, not means to private end. Rawls: decision behind veil of ignorance would reject nepotism."
Step 4 — Options Generation (50–60 words)
What to write: 3–4 distinct, mutually exclusive courses of action. Include a "do nothing / status quo" option to show you considered and rejected it. Label them Option A, B, C, D. Each option must be actionable (not "be ethical").
Example: "A) Stay silent — complicity. B) Confront senior privately — may stop it but no record, retaliation risk. C) Anonymous complaint to CVC — unverified, no protection. D) Documented complaint to CVC/Lokpal with evidence under Whistleblower Protection Act — lawful, recorded, protected."
Step 5 — Option Evaluation (60–80 words)
What to write: For each option, assess against: (a) Legality, (b) Fairness to stakeholders, (c) Long-term institutional impact, (d) Precedent value, (e) Personal risk. Use a mini-table or structured bullets. This is where critically examine lives.
Example: "Option A: Illegal (PCA), unfair to bidders/citizens, corrodes institution, sets corrupt precedent, personal risk if exposed. Option B: Legal but ineffective, no institutional fix, senior may retaliate. Option C: Legal but weak — anonymity reduces credibility, no whistleblower protection. Option D: Legal (PCA, Whistleblower Act), fair (due process), strengthens institution (deterrence), sets clean precedent, protected by law."
Step 6 — Decision & Justification (40–50 words)
What to write: State ONE option clearly. Bold it. Justify with the strongest principle, not a laundry list. Address the strongest counter-argument and rebut it. This is the evaluate and comment moment.
Example: "Decision: Option D — File documented complaint with CVC/Lokpal under Whistleblower Protection Act. Justification: Constitutional morality (Ambedkar) and probity demand that public office serve the public, not private kin. Counter: 'Loyalty to senior.' Rebuttal: Loyalty to a person who corrupts the system betrays the Constitution. The oath is to the Constitution, not to a senior."
Step 7 — Implementation & Way Forward (30–40 words)
What to write: Concrete steps, timeline, responsible actors, documentation, and systemic safeguards. This is the suggest moment. Move from the individual case to institutional design.
Example: "Immediate: Gather contract files, bid documents, communication records (Day 1–2). File complaint with CVC/Lokpal with indexed evidence (Day 3). Request transfer if retaliation feared (Day 3). Systemic: Advocate e-procurement with auto-disqualification of related-party bids; mandatory annual asset declarations for contract-approving officers; rotate officers in procurement posts every 2 years; strengthen Whistleblower Act with witness protection."

2.2 Formatting Conventions That Earn Marks

  • Bold the dilemma statement and the final decision — examiner scans for these.
  • Number options (Option 1, 2, 3) — never bullet points for options.
  • Use a table for evaluation — columns: Option / Legality / Fairness / Institutional Impact / Verdict.
  • Cite thinkers by name in the principles step — "Kant's categorical imperative," "Rawls's veil of ignorance," "Gandhi's ends-means purity."
  • Leave white space between steps — visual separation helps the examiner tick each component.
  • No flowery language — "I will act with utmost integrity" scores zero. "I will file a complaint under Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act" scores marks.
The "No Fence-Sitting" Rule
UPSC explicitly penalises answers that say "a middle path" or "balance both sides" without choosing. In a dilemma between integrity and loyalty, you MUST choose integrity and justify why loyalty is subordinate. The only exception: when the question explicitly asks "Discuss the merits and demerits of each option" without asking for a final choice — then present all sides equally. But 95% of case-study sub-questions demand a decision.

3. Ethical Theories Applied to Answers

Ethical theories are not decoration. They are reasoning engines that convert a vague "this feels wrong" into a structured argument the examiner can verify. In GS IV, you apply three dominant normative frameworks — Virtue Ethics (Aristotle), Deontology (Kant), and Consequentialism (Bentham/Mill) — plus two Indian frameworks: Gandhian Ethics and Constitutional Morality (Ambedkar). Each theory answers a different question:

Theory Core Question It Answers When to Deploy in GS IV
Virtue Ethics "What would a virtuous officer do?" Questions on character, foundational values, leadership, "be the change"
Deontology "Is this act right regardless of outcome?" Corruption, lying, rights violations, whistleblowing, categorical duties
Consequentialism "Which option produces the best overall outcome?" Resource allocation, triage, policy trade-offs, utilitarian calculus
Gandhian Ethics "Are the means as pure as the ends?" Ends-justify-means dilemmas, non-violent resistance, trusteeship
Constitutional Morality "Does this conform to constitutional values?" Majoritarian pressure vs. minority rights, political pressure, rule of law

3.1 Virtue Ethics — Aristotle & The Civil Servant's Character

Core principle: Right action flows from a virtuous character (hexis). Virtues are stable dispositions acquired by habituation — courage, temperance, justice, prudence (phronesis). The golden mean: each virtue lies between excess and deficiency (courage between rashness and cowardice).

GS IV application template: "A virtuous civil servant, possessing the virtue of courage (mean between reckless defiance and cowardly compliance), would refuse an illegal political directive not because of consequences but because courage is constitutive of the office. Phronesis (practical wisdom) discerns the right mean in this specific context — here, respectful refusal with written reasons, not public grandstanding."

Virtue ethics in a 150-word answer
Q: "Courage is the most important virtue for a civil servant." Discuss.
Answer snippet: Aristotle defines courage as the mean between rashness and cowardice. In public service, rashness is public confrontation that destroys institutional channels; cowardice is silent compliance with illegality. A virtuous officer exercises phronesis — practical wisdom — to choose the right mean: documented dissent through proper channels (file noting, RTI, CVC complaint). This is not mere bravery; it is courage informed by justice (fairness to citizens) and temperance (restraint from self-promotion). Example: Ashok Khemka's 50+ transfers reflect courage sustained by phronesis — each transfer order was met with documented reasoning, not resignation or rebellion. Virtue ethics thus demands character formation through training, not just rule compliance.

3.2 Deontology — Kant & The Categorical Imperative

Core principle: Act only according to maxims you can will as universal law. Treat humanity (yourself and others) always as an end, never merely as a means. Duties are categorical — they hold regardless of consequences. Lying, coercion, corruption, using people as tools — these are intrinsically wrong.

GS IV application template: "Option X treats the citizen as a mere means to achieve a target (e.g., inflating vaccination numbers). This violates Kant's second formulation — humanity as an end. Even if the outcome appears beneficial (higher reported coverage), the act is ethically impermissible because it undermines the citizen's autonomy and trust. The categorical imperative test: 'Could I will that every officer falsifies data when targets are missed?' No — the system would collapse. Therefore, Option X is ruled out ab initio, irrespective of consequences."

Deontology in a case study
Case: You are pressured to approve a contract to a firm that doesn't meet specs, because "it will create jobs."
Deontological reasoning: Approving a non-compliant contract uses the public (taxpayers, competing firms) as mere means to a political end (job creation). The maxim "approve substandard contracts when politically pressured" cannot be universalised — if every officer did it, procurement would lose all credibility. The duty to follow procedure (Conduct Rules, GFR) is categorical. Consequences (jobs, political goodwill) do not alter the wrongness of the act. Decision: Refuse approval. Document reasons. Escalate if pressured.

3.3 Consequentialism — Bentham, Mill & The Greatest Good

Core principle: The rightness of an act is determined by its consequences — specifically, maximising overall well-being (utility). Mill refines Bentham: higher pleasures (intellectual, moral) outweigh lower; liberty principle — no interference unless harm to others. Rule utilitarianism: follow rules that generally maximise utility.

GS IV application template: "When allocating scarce ICU beds during a pandemic, a consequentialist framework evaluates options by projected lives saved, quality-adjusted life years, and systemic trust. Option A (first-come-first-served) seems fair but may waste beds on low-survival patients. Option B (clinical triage score) maximises lives saved but risks public perception of 'playing God.' A rule-utilitarian approach: adopt a transparent, published triage protocol developed by experts — this maximises long-term trust AND immediate outcomes. The liberty principle requires that no patient is denied care arbitrarily; the protocol must be appealable."

Consequentialism trap in GS IV
Never use consequentialism to justify corruption, rights violations, or illegality. "The ends justify the means" is not Mill — it's a caricature. Mill's harm principle and rule utilitarianism constrain consequentialism with rights and rules. In GS IV, use consequentialism for policy design, resource allocation, and trade-offs between legitimate goods — never for excusing individual wrongdoing.

3.4 Gandhian Ethics — Ends-Means Purity & Trusteeship

Core principles: (1) Satya (truth) and Ahimsa (non-violence) are non-negotiable. (2) Ends and means must be congruent — impure means corrupt the end. (3) Trusteeship — power, wealth, talent are held in trust for society; the last person (antodaya) is the test of every decision.

GS IV application template: "A proposal to use coercive land acquisition for a 'development' project violates Gandhian ethics because the means (force, displacement) contradict the claimed end (public welfare). Trusteeship demands that the officer ask: 'Would the poorest displaced family consent to this if they fully understood?' If not, the officer holds power in trust for them, not for the project proponent. The Salt March exemplifies: breaking an unjust law (illegal) to uphold a higher moral law (ethical) — but the means (non-violent, open, accepting punishment) remained pure."

3.5 Constitutional Morality — Ambedkar & The Veil of Governance

Core principle: Constitutional morality is not mere legal compliance; it is the spirit of the Constitution — justice (social, economic, political), liberty, equality, fraternity, dignity of the individual. Ambedkar warned: "Constitutional morality is not a natural sentiment. It has to be cultivated." It requires that administrative action conform to constitutional values even when popular sentiment, political pressure, or custom demands otherwise.

GS IV application template: "When a dominant community demands denial of a scheme benefit to a marginalised group, constitutional morality requires the officer to uphold the scheme's universal eligibility (Article 14, 15, 16). The officer's duty is to the Constitution, not to the majority. This is not 'anti-majoritarian' — it is the precondition for democracy. Rawls's veil of ignorance reinforces this: behind the veil, you would not design a system where your rights depend on your community's numerical strength."

3.6 Integrated Theory Application — The "Three-Lens" Method

In a 250-word case study, you don't have space for full essays on each theory. Use the Three-Lens Method in the Evaluation step (Step 5):

Lens 1 — Deontological (Rights/Duties): Does this option violate a categorical duty (honesty, impartiality, legal compliance)? If yes, reject or heavily penalise.
Lens 2 — Consequentialist (Outcomes): Among the remaining options, which produces the best net outcome for stakeholders and institutional health? Weight long-term precedent higher than short-term convenience.
Lens 3 — Virtue/Constitutional (Character/Precedent): Does this option build the character of the service and uphold constitutional morality? Does it pass the "veil of ignorance" and "trusteeship" tests?

Your final decision should survive all three lenses. If it fails Lens 1, it's a non-starter. If it passes Lens 1 but fails Lens 2, reconsider options. If it passes 1 & 2 but fails Lens 3, the decision is legally correct but institutionally corrosive — find a way to strengthen it.

Model Integrated Evaluation (template)
Option D (Documented complaint to CVC):
Lens 1 (Deontology): PASS. Fulfils duty under PCA, Whistleblower Act, Conduct Rules. Treats public office as duty, not means.
Lens 2 (Consequentialism): PASS. Creates deterrence, protects future bidders, strengthens institutional credibility. Short-term personal risk mitigated by legal protection.
Lens 3 (Virtue/Constitutional): PASS. Embodies courage (mean), integrity (consistency), constitutional morality (Ambedkar), trusteeship (Gandhi). Sets precedent that probity > loyalty.
Verdict: Option D survives all three lenses. Selected.
Name the theory, don't just gesture
Writing "this respects human dignity" is vague. Writing "this respects human dignity as an end in itself per Kant's second formulation" is precise. The examiner awards marks for correct application of a named framework. Practice writing one-sentence theory applications for each archetype until they are automatic.

4. Model Answers — 5 Solved Case Studies (250 Words Each)

Each model answer follows the 7-step framework. Word counts are calibrated for the 250-word limit. Study the structure, not just the content — the skeleton transfers to any case.

4.1 Case Study 1: Corruption / Bribe Offer — The Procurement Kickback

Case
You are a Deputy Secretary handling a ₹50 crore IT procurement. A vendor's representative offers you ₹10 lakh to "expedite" the technical evaluation in their favour. The vendor's product is technically compliant but not the best. You have a child's medical emergency requiring ₹8 lakh.
Model Answer (248 words)
Facts & Dilemma: Vendor offers ₹10 lakh bribe to influence a ₹50 cr procurement. Core dilemma: Personal financial distress vs. Integrity, probity, and legal duty.

Stakeholders: 1) You (DS) — medical need, career, conscience. 2) Vendor — contract, profit. 3) Competing vendors — fair competition denied. 4) Public exchequer — potential overpayment/suboptimal product. 5) Citizens — degraded service. 6) Institution — corruption precedent. 7) Family — medical crisis.

Principles & Law: Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (amended 2018) — S. 7 (bribe-taking), S. 8 (bribe-giving), S. 17 (investigation). Conduct Rules (AIS 1968) — integrity, impartiality. Article 14/16 — equality in public procurement. Kant: bribe treats office as means, not duty. Gandhi: impure means corrupt the end.

Options: A) Accept bribe, favour vendor — illegal, corrupts institution, personal risk if caught. B) Accept bribe, still evaluate fairly — contradiction; bribe creates obligation. C) Reject bribe, evaluate on merit, report attempt — legal, ethical, creates record. D) Resign citing medical emergency — avoids corruption but abandons duty; no guarantee next officer resists.

Evaluation: Option C passes all three lenses. Deontology: fulfils categorical duty to reject corruption. Consequentialism: deters vendor, protects competition, maintains institutional trust. Virtue: embodies courage (mean between cowardice and martyrdom), integrity (consistency). Medical need addressed separately: apply for medical advance (Conduct Rules), CGHS, PMNRF — legitimate channels exist.

Decision: Option C — Reject bribe outright, document attempt in file noting, report to CVC/vigilance immediately, evaluate bids strictly on merit.

Implementation: Day 1: Written refusal to vendor, file noting with timestamp. Day 1: Complaint to CVC under PCA S. 17. Day 2: Medical advance application + PMNRF request. Day 3: Transparent evaluation committee minutes published. Systemic: Advocate e-procurement with auto-audit trails; mandatory cooling-off for procurement officers.

4.2 Case Study 2: Compassion vs. Rules — The Widow's Pension

Case
You are a BDO. A 68-year-old widow's pension application lacks her husband's death certificate (lost in floods). She has no other documents. The scheme rules mandate the certificate. She is destitute. Your subordinate suggests "adjusting" the record. The widow has two malnourished grandchildren.
Model Answer (249 words)
Facts & Dilemma: Destitute widow lacks mandatory death certificate for pension. Core dilemma: Compassion/empathy for a vulnerable citizen vs. Rule of law, procedural integrity, impartiality.

Stakeholders: 1) Widow & grandchildren — survival, dignity. 2) You (BDO) — discretion, accountability, precedent. 3) Subordinate — pressure to "help." 4) Scheme integrity — rule compliance, audit. 5) Other beneficiaries — equity (if exception made). 6) Administration — public trust in rule-based governance.

Principles & Law: Article 21 — right to life with dignity. NFSA 2013 — food security as legal entitlement. Conduct Rules — impartiality, accountability. Mill: harm principle — denying pension causes severe harm. Rawls: veil of ignorance — would you design a system with no exception for documented loss? Gandhi: antodaya — last person test. But Kant: rules as universalisable duty; exceptions without criteria = arbitrariness.

Options: A) Grant pension without certificate — compassionate but illegal, creates precedent, audit objection, unfair to others who followed rules. B) Reject application strictly — legal but causes destitution, violates Article 21 spirit. C) Use alternative verification (panchayat verification, neighbour affidavits, flood records, bank/death register cross-check), process within 7 days — legal, compassionate, preserves rule integrity. D) Refer to District Collector for ex-gratia — passes buck, delays relief.

Evaluation: Option C survives all lenses. Deontology: fulfils duty to follow procedure AND duty to protect life (Article 21) — not a violation but a permissible administrative verification. Consequentialism: widow gets pension fast; scheme integrity intact; precedent is "alternative verification protocol" not "rule waiver." Virtue: phronesis — practical wisdom finds the mean between rigidity and arbitrariness.

Decision: Option C — Initiate alternative verification: Gram Sabha resolution + two neighbour affidavits + flood damage report + cross-check with Registrar of Deaths. Process pension within 7 working days.

Implementation: Day 1: Direct subordinate to collect alternative evidence. Day 2: Gram Sabha emergency meeting. Day 3: Compile verification dossier. Day 4: Forward to Treasury with verification certificate. Day 5–7: Pension credited. Systemic: Issue circular defining "alternative verification protocol" for disaster-affected applicants; digitise death records; link pension to Aadhaar/Jan Dhan for auto-verification.

4.3 Case Study 3: Whistleblowing / Loyalty Conflict — The Environmental Clearance

Case
You are a Project Director. Your mentor and senior (whom you respect deeply) has manipulated an EIA report to clear a mining project in a Schedule V tribal area. The manipulation hides the impact on a sacred grove and water source. Tribal protests are rising. Your senior says "the project brings development; don't rock the boat."
Model Answer (250 words)
Facts & Dilemma: Senior manipulates EIA for mining in Schedule V area, hiding sacred grove/water impact. Core dilemma: Loyalty to mentor/senior vs. Constitutional duty to tribals, environmental law, probity.

Stakeholders: 1) Tribal communities — survival, cultural rights, FRA/PESA rights. 2) You (PD) — conscience, career, legal liability. 3) Senior/mentor — reputation, project completion. 4) Mining company — profit. 5) State — revenue, "development" narrative. 6) Environment — irreversible damage. 7) Institution — credibility of EIA process.

Principles & Law: Forest Rights Act 2006 (S. 4(1) — Gram Sabha consent mandatory in Schedule V). PESA 1996. EIA Notification 2006 — fraudulent EIA = void clearance. PCA 1988 — S. 13 (criminal misconduct). Whistleblower Protection Act 2014. Ambedkar: constitutional morality > majoritarian/development narrative. Kant: tribals as ends, not means. Gandhi: Gram Swaraj — consent is non-negotiable.

Options: A) Stay silent — complicity in fraud, legal liability under PCA, tribal harm continues. B) Confront senior privately — may not work; no record; retaliation risk. C) Leak anonymously to media/NGO — unverified, no legal protection, may harm tribals if project stalls without remedy. D) Document evidence, file complaint with MoEFCC/NGT/CVC under Whistleblower Act, inform Gram Sabha — lawful, protected, creates institutional record.

Evaluation: Option D passes all lenses. Deontology: duty to report fraud (PCA), protect FRA rights. Consequentialism: triggers legal review, protects tribals' water/culture, deters future EIA fraud. Virtue: courage (mean), integrity, constitutional morality. Senior's "development" argument fails veil of ignorance — would you accept this if you were the tribal?

Decision: Option D — Compile documentary evidence (original vs. manipulated EIA, Gram Sabha records, hydrology reports). File signed complaint with MoEFCC, NGT, and CVC under Whistleblower Protection Act. Simultaneously share evidence with Gram Sabha for their own legal action.

Implementation: Day 1–2: Secure evidence (screenshots, emails, original reports). Day 3: File complaints with MoEFCC (EIA fraud), NGT (environmental damage), CVC (PCA). Invoke Whistleblower Act for protection. Day 4: Meet Gram Sabha, share evidence, assist in filing FRA claim. Systemic: Mandate third-party EIA audit for Schedule V projects; Gram Sabha veto power in clearance; rotate EIA consultants; strengthen Whistleblower Act with witness protection.

4.4 Case Study 4: Conflict of Interest — The Relative's Contract

Case
You are an Executive Engineer. Your brother-in-law's construction firm bids for a ₹12 crore road contract. The firm is technically qualified and the lowest bidder. The tender process was fair. Your wife urges you to "not create trouble." The firm has a good track record.
Model Answer (247 words)
Facts & Dilemma: Brother-in-law's firm is L1 in fair tender for ₹12 cr road contract. Core dilemma: Personal/family interest vs. Impartiality, institutional integrity, appearance of bias.

Stakeholders: 1) You (EE) — family pressure, career, conscience. 2) Brother-in-law's firm — legitimate business, reputation. 3) Competing bidders — fair process perception. 4) Public — quality road, trust. 5) Administration — integrity of procurement. 6) Family — relational harmony.

Principles & Law: Conduct Rules (AIS 1968) — S. 18 (avoid conflict of interest), S. 20 (declare interest). GFR 2017 — Rule 160 (transparency), Rule 175 (fair competition). PCA 1988 — S. 13(1)(d) (pecuniary advantage to relative). Nolan: integrity, objectivity, accountability. Kant: universalise "approve relative's bid if qualified" → procurement collapses. Rawls: behind veil, you wouldn't want contracts decided by kinship.

Options: A) Approve contract — technically correct but creates perception of nepotism; audit objection; PCA risk. B) Reject firm arbitrarily — unfair to firm, legally challengeable. C) Recuse yourself, declare interest in writing, transfer file to next senior officer — legal, ethical, preserves fairness. D) Delay decision citing "review" — passive, creates suspicion.

Evaluation: Option C is the only one passing all lenses. Deontology: fulfils duty to recuse (Conduct Rules). Consequentialism: firm wins on merit if truly L1 (new officer evaluates); competitors trust process; institution gains credibility. Virtue: integrity (consistency), impartiality (objectivity), humility (recusal admits human bias). Family pressure addressed: explain that recusal protects the firm from future allegations.

Decision: Option C — Immediate written declaration of interest to Head of Department. Recuse from evaluation/award. Request transfer of file to next senior officer. Inform family that recusal is mandatory under Conduct Rules and protects all parties.

Implementation: Day 1: Written declaration (proforma: name, relationship, nature of interest, date). Day 1: File transfer to HOD with note. Day 2: HOD assigns to another EE. Day 3: New evaluation — if firm remains L1, award stands clean. Systemic: Mandatory annual conflict-of-interest declarations for all procurement officers; auto-flagging of related-party bids in e-procurement; cooling-off period (2 years) for officers whose relatives bid.

4.5 Case Study 5: Public Trust — The Fake Encounter Pressure

Case
You are an SP. A dreaded gangster is killed in a genuine encounter. Political leadership and media pressure you to "frame it as a clean encounter" even though procedural lapses occurred (no magistrate inquiry initiated, body not handed to family per NHRC guidelines). "The public wants justice; don't give ammunition to critics."
Model Answer (250 words)
Facts & Dilemma: Genuine encounter but procedural lapses (no magisterial inquiry, NHRC guideline violations). Pressure to "clean up" narrative. Core dilemma: Political/media pressure for convenient narrative vs. Rule of law, institutional accountability, public trust through transparency.

Stakeholders: 1) Victim's family — right to truth, due process. 2) You (SP) — career, pressure, conscience. 3) Police force — morale, legitimacy. 4) Political leadership — narrative control. 5) Media/public — trust in justice system. 6) Judiciary/NHRC — oversight mandate. 7) Rule of law — precedent.

Principles & Law: NHRC Guidelines 2010 — mandatory magisterial inquiry, videography, family notification. SC in PUCL v. State (2014) — 16-point guidelines for encounter investigations. PCA — framing false narrative = fabrication of evidence. Article 21 — right to fair procedure even for accused. Ambedkar: constitutional morality demands institutional honesty. Kant: truth as categorical duty; lying to public treats citizens as means.

Options: A) Comply with pressure, suppress lapses — short-term political relief, long-term institutional rot, legal liability if exposed. B) Resign in protest — principled but abandons responsibility to fix it. C) Initiate magisterial inquiry voluntarily, disclose lapses transparently, correct record — legal, builds trust, sets precedent. D) Leak to media — uncontrolled, damages investigation.

Evaluation: Option C passes all lenses. Deontology: fulfils legal duty (NHRC, SC guidelines), treats truth as duty. Consequentialism: voluntary transparency > forced exposure; builds long-term police legitimacy; deters future lapses. Virtue: courage (mean), integrity, constitutional morality. "Public wants justice" argument — real justice requires procedural justice, not just outcome.

Decision: Option C — Immediately write to District Magistrate requesting magisterial inquiry under CrPC S. 176. Submit factual report to NHRC within 48 hours. Issue press statement: "Encounter was genuine; procedural lapses identified; inquiry initiated; accountability will follow."

Implementation: Day 1: Letter to DM for magisterial inquiry. Day 1: NHRC report with full facts. Day 2: Press briefing — transparent admission. Day 3–30: Inquiry proceeds; guilty officers charged. Systemic: Mandatory body cameras for all operations; automatic magisterial inquiry trigger for every encounter; NHRC compliance audit quarterly; witness protection for police whistleblowers.
Pattern recognition across the 5 models
Notice the constants: (1) Every answer names the dilemma in Value vs. Value form. (2) Stakeholders always include the institution and public trust. (3) Principles cite specific Articles, Sections, Rules — not vague "law." (4) Options include a "do nothing" and a "pass the buck" to show rejection. (5) Evaluation uses the three-lens method explicitly. (6) Decision is bolded and names the option letter. (7) Implementation is time-bound, specific, systemic. Memorise this skeleton.
