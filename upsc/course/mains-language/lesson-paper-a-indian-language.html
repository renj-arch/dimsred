What Paper A is

Paper A is one Indian language from the Eighth Schedule. It is 300 marks and three hours. It is qualifying: you need 25% (75/300), and the marks are not added to the merit list. Failing it ends the attempt, so it deserves a planned place in the timetable.

The 25% rule
The Commission evaluates Paper A and Paper B alongside the scored papers, but only candidates who reach 25% in each qualifying paper are considered for ranking. Treat 75/300 as a floor, not a target.

What the paper tests

  • Comprehension: read a passage and answer questions on its meaning, inference and vocabulary.
  • Precis: compress a passage to about one-third without losing its argument or tone.
  • Usage and vocabulary: grammar, sentence construction, idioms and word choice.
  • Short essays: organised writing on a general topic.
  • Translation: English to the chosen language and the chosen language to English.

The notification does not publish a fixed section-wise mark split. Prepare all five areas rather than optimising for a guessed weighting.

5. Exam Strategy & Time Allocation

Paper A is 300 marks, three hours, qualifying at 25% (75 marks) and not counted in the merit list. That changes the optimal strategy: you are not chasing marks, you are clearing a floor without spending study time better used on GS. The practical target is 120–150 marks, comfortably above the floor, with a disciplined time budget.

Order of attempt
Do comprehension and précis first — they are technique-bound and reward a fresh mind. Leave translation last: it is the most time-elastic and the easiest to rush badly. Never leave a question blank; a partial answer scores, an empty one does not.

6. Comprehension Technique

Comprehension rewards method, not vocabulary. Read the passage twice: once for the argument (what is the author claiming?), once for the questions (what exactly is asked?). Most lost marks come from answering a question the examiner did not ask.

  1. Skim the questions first — know what you are hunting for.
  2. Read for structure — identify the topic sentence of each paragraph and the author's stance.
  3. Answer in the passage's words — paraphrase only when the question demands it. Direct lift is safe; invention is not.
  4. Handle inference carefully — an inference must follow necessarily from the text, not from your general knowledge.
  5. Vocabulary-in-context — the meaning is the one the passage uses, not the dictionary's first sense.
Worked example
Passage: "The committee's report was thorough but its recommendations were timid; it mapped the problem with precision and then declined to prescribe the remedy."
Q: What does the author imply about the committee?
Answer: That it diagnosed the problem accurately but lacked the courage to recommend action. The words "timid" and "declined to prescribe" signal a gap between analysis and remedy — not incompetence in analysis.

For a full method and practice passages, see Précis & Comprehension.

7. Précis Method

A précis compresses a passage to about one-third of its length while keeping its argument, tone and emphasis. It is not a summary of facts; it is a faithful compression of the author's point.

  1. Read and mark — underline the thesis and the supporting reasons; strike examples and repetition.
  2. Draft in your own words — never copy sentences verbatim; the examiner tests whether you understood.
  3. Keep the order of ideas — a précis follows the passage's logic, not your preferred arrangement.
  4. Cut examples, keep claims — illustrations go; the propositions they support stay.
  5. Count words — aim for one-third, within ±10%. Add a title and, if asked, a rough scale (number of words).
The one-third trap
Writing far under one-third usually means you dropped a key reason; writing far over means you kept examples. Both lose marks. The exact proportion is the examiner's quick check that you can compress without distortion.

8. Short Essays & Letter Writing

Paper A typically asks short essays and, in some languages, letters or notices. The standard is correctness and organisation, not literary flourish. A three-paragraph structure works: (1) define and state the issue, (2) two or three reasoned points with examples, (3) a balanced conclusion with a way forward.

  • Use the language's register — formal but plain; avoid slang and heavy ornamentation.
  • One idea per paragraph — the examiner reads for structure and coherence.
  • Keep sentences short — long sentences in a second-language paper invite grammar errors.
  • For letters: match the prescribed format (formal/informal), state purpose in the first line, close correctly.

9. Translation & Grammar

Translation is judged on fidelity, grammar and naturalness — in that order. Translate meaning, not words: an idiomatic rendering that preserves sense beats a literal one that reads awkwardly.

  • English → Indian language: unpack long English sentences into shorter clauses; convert passive to active where the language prefers it; render idioms by sense.
  • Indian language → English: preserve tense and number carefully; avoid literal word-order transfer; keep technical terms consistent.
  • Officialese: learn the standard equivalents for administrative terms (e.g., "notwithstanding", "hereinafter", "shall be deemed") in your chosen language.
  • Grammar: focus on the high-frequency error types — agreement, case, tense consistency, postpositions/particles, and script-specific spelling rules.
