1. What a Précis Is & How It Is Marked

1.1 Definition — Précis vs. Summary

A précis (from French précis, "precise, concise") is a faithful, condensed restatement of a passage that preserves its argumentative architecture — thesis, supporting reasons in sequence, logical connectors, and the author's tone — in approximately one-third the original word count UPSC CSE Notification, Compulsory Language Papers Syllabus: "Précis writing".

Summary vs. Précis — The Critical Difference
Original (80 words): "The government's new agricultural policy aims to double farmer incomes by 2030 through three pillars: minimum support price expansion, contract farming liberalisation, and digital market integration. Critics argue that MSP expansion benefits only 6% of farmers (Shanta Kumar Committee, 2015), contract farming favours large agribusiness, and digital markets require infrastructure absent in 70% of villages. The policy document acknowledges implementation challenges but maintains the targets are achievable with state cooperation."

Summary (lists points, ~45 words): The policy has three pillars: MSP expansion, contract farming, digital markets. Critics say MSP helps only 6% of farmers, contract farming favours big business, digital markets lack rural infrastructure. Government admits challenges but says targets are achievable with state help.

Précis (preserves argument flow, ~27 words): The policy seeks to double farm incomes via MSP expansion, contract farming, and digital markets; critics counter that MSP reaches few farmers, contract farming benefits agribusiness, and digital infrastructure is lacking. The government concedes implementation hurdles but asserts targets are achievable with state cooperation.

1.2 UPSC Marking Criteria (Reconstructed from Evaluation Patterns)

Examiners do not publish a rubric, but analysis of model answers and topper scripts reveals consistent criteria Anthropology/Public Administration topper answer scripts (publicly shared), Précis section, 2019–2023:

Criterion Weight (est.) What Examiners Check
Fidelity to Meaning 35% No distortion, no addition, no omission of essential argument steps
Argument Structure Preservation 25% Thesis → reasons → evidence → conclusion in correct sequence; logical connectors intact
Tone & Register 20% Author's stance (critical, tentative, emphatic) mirrored; formal register maintained
Conciseness (≈⅓ length) 10% Within 30–35% of original word count; no flab
Language Quality 10% Grammar, vocabulary, coherence; single paragraph flow
The "One-Third" Rule Is a Guide, Not a Law
If the original is 300 words, aim for 90–105 words. If it's 500 words, aim for 150–175. Examiners penalise significant deviation (under 25% or over 40%) more than a few words over/under. Fidelity and structure matter more than exact count.

2. Step-by-Step Method with Worked Examples

2.1 The 6-Step Précis Protocol

  1. Read Twice — Architectural Read
    Read 1: Gist — "What is this about? What's the main claim?"
    Read 2: Structure — Map paragraph functions: Thesis / Evidence / Counter-argument / Concession / Rebuttal / Conclusion. Mark transition words (however, therefore, moreover, conversely, consequently, admittedly, nonetheless).
  2. Write the Thesis Statement (1 sentence)
    Capture the central argument in your own words. This becomes your opening sentence.
  3. Extract Supporting Points in Sequence
    List each reason/evidence the author gives, in order. Strip: examples, statistics, quotations, anecdotes, repetitions, parenthetical remarks.
  4. Draft in Your Own Words — Target ⅓
    Combine thesis + supporting points into a single paragraph. Use formal connectors to preserve logical flow. Do not look at the original while drafting.
  5. Verify Fidelity (The "Back-Check")
    Compare draft against original: Did I distort any claim? Add my opinion? Drop a logical step? Change the tone? Fix before word-counting.
  6. Word Count & Polish
    Count words. If over, cut adjectives, merge clauses, replace phrases with precision verbs. If under, check for dropped argument steps. Ensure single-paragraph coherence.

2.2 Worked Example 1 — Editorial Passage (English, ~280 words → Précis ~95 words)

Source Passage

The discourse on urbanisation in India has long been framed as an inevitable engine of growth. The dominant narrative, reinforced by successive Economic Surveys, posits that agglomeration economies — knowledge spillovers, labour market pooling, input sharing — drive productivity gains that lift per capita income. This view underpins the Smart Cities Mission, the AMRUT scheme, and the massive capital allocation to metro rail projects in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities.

However, the lived reality of Indian urbanisation diverges sharply from this theoretical promise. The 2011 Census recorded 377 million urban residents; the UN projects 600 million by 2030. Yet urban planning capacity has not kept pace. Over 40% of urban households live in informal settlements lacking basic services. Water supply averages 2–4 hours daily in major cities; sewage treatment covers under 30% of generated waste. Air quality in Delhi, Mumbai, and Kolkata routinely exceeds WHO limits by 10–15 times. The agglomeration economies that theory predicts are neutralised by congestion externalities: travel times have doubled in Bengaluru and Pune over a decade, eroding the very productivity gains urbanisation promises.

Moreover, the fiscal architecture of Indian cities is broken. Municipal own-revenue averages 0.6% of GDP (vs. 5–7% in Brazil, South Africa). Property tax collection efficiency hovers at 30–40%. State Finance Commissions' recommendations are routinely ignored. Cities cannot borrow; they depend on tied grants from State and Centre, which prioritise visible capital projects (flyovers, metros) over operations and maintenance. The result is a paradox: gleaming metro stations above, crumbling water pipes below.

Reform requires three simultaneous shifts. First, empower city governments with predictable, untied fiscal transfers and borrowing capacity. Second, mandate metropolitan planning authorities with statutory power over land use, transport, and environment — not advisory bodies. Third, reorient capital allocation from mega-projects to last-mile service delivery: water, sanitation, waste, bus-based transit. Without these, India's urban transition will remain a story of elite capture and infrastructural deficit, not the inclusive growth the Economic Surveys envision.

Step 1: Architectural Map
Para 1 (Thesis): Dominant narrative — urbanisation = growth via agglomeration economies → policy: Smart Cities, AMRUT, metros.
Para 2 (Counter-evidence): Reality diverges — 40% in slums, water 2–4 hrs, sewage <30%, air 10–15× WHO, congestion erodes productivity.
Para 3 (Structural cause): Fiscal architecture broken — own-revenue 0.6% GDP, property tax 30–40%, no borrowing, tied grants → capital bias, O&M neglected.
Para 4 (Solution/Conclusion): Three shifts: fiscal empowerment, statutory metro planning, capital reorientation to last-mile services. Else elite capture continues.
Step 2: Thesis Statement
India's urbanisation policy assumes agglomeration economies drive growth, but infrastructural deficits, broken municipal finance, and capital misallocation neutralise these gains, producing elite capture instead of inclusive growth.
Step 3: Supporting Points (in sequence)
1. Reality contradicts theory: 40% in informal settlements, severe water/sewage/air deficits, congestion doubling travel times.
2. Root cause: municipal own-revenue 0.6% GDP, property tax collection 30–40%, no borrowing power, tied grants favour capital projects over maintenance.
3. Required reforms: predictable untied fiscal transfers + borrowing capacity; statutory metropolitan planning authorities; capital shift from mega-projects to last-mile services (water, sanitation, bus transit).
Step 4–6: Draft → Verify → Polish (Final Précis, 94 words)
Précis: India's urbanisation policy assumes agglomeration economies drive growth, but infrastructural deficits, broken municipal finance, and capital misallocation neutralise these gains, producing elite capture instead of inclusive growth. Reality contradicts theory: 40% of urban households live in informal settlements; water supply averages 2–4 hours; sewage treatment covers under 30%; air pollution exceeds WHO limits 10–15 times; congestion has doubled travel times in major cities. The root cause is fiscal: municipal own-revenue averages 0.6% of GDP, property tax collection is 30–40%, cities cannot borrow, and tied grants prioritise visible capital projects over operations. Reform requires three simultaneous shifts: predictable untied fiscal transfers with borrowing capacity; statutory metropolitan planning authorities with power over land use and transport; and capital reorientation from mega-projects to last-mile service delivery — water, sanitation, waste, and bus-based transit.

2.3 Worked Example 2 — Hindi Passage (Paper A Style, ~200 words → Précis ~65 words)

Source Passage (Hindi)

भारतीय संविधान का अनुच्छेद 21 केवल जीवन और व्यक्तिगत स्वतंत्रता की रक्षा नहीं करता, बल्कि यह गरिमापूर्ण जीवन के अधिकार को भी समाहित करता है। सर्वोच्च न्यायालय ने अनेक निर्णयों में स्पष्ट किया है कि 'जीवन' का अर्थ केवल श्वास लेना नहीं है, बल्कि स्वास्थ्य, शिक्षा, आजीविका और स्वच्छ वातावरण जैसे मौलिक पहलू भी इसमें शामिल हैं। मनीका गांधी बनाम भारत संघ (1978) में न्यायालय ने कहा कि अनुच्छेद 21 की व्याख्या उदार होनी चाहिए। इसी क्रम में, फ्रांसिस कोरलिन बनाम दिल्ली प्रशासन (1981) में कैदियों के अधिकारों की रक्षा की गई। हाल के वर्षों में, निजता का अधिकार (पुत्तस्वामी, 2017) और स्वच्छ वायु का अधिकार (एमसी मेहता श्रृंखला) को भी अनुच्छेद 21 के तहत लाया गया है। इस प्रकार, अनुच्छेद 21 एक जीवंत प्रावधान है जो सामाजिक-आर्थिक परिवर्तनों के साथ विकसित होता है।

Précis (Hindi) — 63 words
प्रेसिस: अनुच्छेद 21 केवल जीवन-रक्षा तक सीमित नहीं; यह गरिमापूर्ण जीवन — स्वास्थ्य, शिक्षा, आजीविका, स्वच्छ वातावरण, निजता — का अधिकार सुनिश्चित करता है। मनीका गांधी (1978) ने उदार व्याख्या का मार्ग प्रशस्त किया; फ्रांसिस कोरलिन (1981) और पुत्तस्वामी (2017) ने इसका विस्तार कैदियों के अधिकार और निजता तक किया। एमसी मेहता निर्णयों ने स्वच्छ वायु को जोड़ा। अनुच्छेद 21 सामाजिक-आर्थिक परिवर्तनों के अनुरूप विकसित होने वाला जीवंत प्रावधान है।

3. Common Errors That Cost Marks

These eight errors appear repeatedly in sub-75-mark scripts. Each is avoidable with the 6-step method.

# Error Why It Fails Fix
1 Summary, not précis Lists points; loses argument flow, connectors, tone Use 6-step method; preserve thesis→reasons→conclusion sequence
2 Tone shift Author is critical/tentative/emphatic; précis becomes neutral Mark author's stance words (however, admittedly, crucially, fundamentally); mirror them
3 Example creep Includes "for instance, in 2019..." wasting word budget Strip examples unless the example is the argument
4 Distortion / Addition Adds "the government should..." or strengthens/weakens claims Back-check every sentence against original; no opinion
5 Connector dropout Logical links (therefore, conversely, consequently) omitted → disjointed Map transitions in Step 1; reproduce in draft
6 Passive voice overuse "It is argued that..." → weak, wordy, obscures agency Prefer active: "The author argues..." "Critics contend..."
7 Word count violation Under 25% (incomplete) or over 40% (uncondensed) Target 30–35%; cut adjectives, merge clauses, use precision verbs
8 Register mismatch Colloquial words (huge, big, really, basically) in formal précis Use Tier 2 precision verbs: substantial, significant, fundamentally, essentially
The "Back-Check" Habit
After drafting, read your précis and ask: "If I gave this to someone who hasn't read the original, would they reconstruct the author's argument correctly?" If no — fix it. This single habit eliminates Errors 1, 2, 4, and 5.

4. Comprehension: Question Types & Strategies

Comprehension passages in both Paper A (chosen language) and Paper B (English) test the same cognitive skills. The question taxonomy is stable across years UPSC Previous Year Question Papers, Compulsory Language Papers, Comprehension Sections, 2018–2024.

4.1 Question Taxonomy

Type Frequency (est.) Strategy
Direct Fact / Literal 20% Locate sentence; paraphrase; do not lift
Inference / Implied 35% Connect 2+ sentences; answer must follow logically; not stated explicitly
Main Idea / Central Theme 15% Identify thesis paragraph; distinguish from supporting detail
Vocabulary in Context 15% Define word as used in passage; not dictionary meaning
Tone / Attitude / Purpose 10% Map adjectives, modal verbs, concessions; choose best fit descriptor
Structure / Function 5% "Paragraph 3 serves to..." — identify role: counter-argument, evidence, concession, etc.

4.2 Inference — The "Must Be True" Standard

An inference question asks: "Which of the following can be inferred?" The correct answer is the statement that must be true given the passage — not might be true, not is likely, but logically necessary.

Inference Worked Example
Passage: "The committee recommended that the Bill be referred to a Select Committee, noting that the drafting contained ambiguities that could lead to litigation. The Minister disagreed, stating that the ambiguities were deliberate to allow administrative flexibility."

Question: What can be inferred about the Minister's position?

Options:
A. The Minister believes the Bill is perfectly drafted.
B. The Minister accepts that ambiguities exist but considers them intentional.
C. The Minister wants the Bill withdrawn.
D. The Minister agrees with the committee's recommendation.

Analysis: A — contradicts "ambiguities were deliberate". C — no evidence. D — contradicts "Minister disagreed". B — directly supported: Minister acknowledges ambiguities ("the ambiguities") and calls them "deliberate".

Answer: B

4.3 Vocabulary in Context — The "Substitution Test"

When asked the meaning of a word in the passage, substitute your answer back into the sentence. If the sentence's meaning changes, your answer is wrong.

Vocabulary in Context Worked Example
Sentence: "The policy's ostensible objective is poverty reduction, but its design suggests otherwise."

Options:
A. Real / Actual
B. Stated / Apparent (but possibly not real)
C. Hidden / Secret
D. Noble / Worthy

Substitution Test:
A: "The policy's real objective is poverty reduction, but its design suggests otherwise." → Contradiction (if real, design wouldn't suggest otherwise).
B: "The policy's stated/apparent objective is poverty reduction, but its design suggests otherwise." → Consistent (stated one thing, design implies another).
C: "The policy's hidden objective..." → "Hidden objective is poverty reduction" changes meaning.
D: "The policy's noble objective..." → Doesn't fit "but its design suggests otherwise."

Answer: B

4.4 Timed Comprehension Workflow (20 minutes for passage + 6 questions)

  1. 0:00–1:30 — Architectural skim (first/last paras + topic sentences). Mark thesis, paragraph roles, transition words.
  2. 1:30–2:30 — Read all 6 questions. Classify each (Fact/Inference/Main Idea/Vocab/Tone/Structure).
  3. 2:30–8:00 — Targeted re-read for each question. Answer in own words. For inference, mark the two sentences that yield the answer.
  4. 8:00–12:00 — Write answers. Fact: 1–2 sentences. Inference: 2–3 sentences showing the logical link. Vocab: definition + substitution check. Tone: descriptor + evidence phrase.
  5. 12:00–20:00 — Précis (if combined paper) or review.

5. Practice Passages with Model Précis

Use these passages for timed practice. Write the précis in 25 minutes; compare with the model. Focus on fidelity, structure, tone, and word count.

Practice Passage 1 (English, ~320 words — Target Précis: 100–110 words)

Passage: The Fiscal Federalism Dilemma

The Fifteenth Finance Commission's recommendation to reduce the vertical devolution share from 42% to 41% — citing the Centre's enhanced fiscal obligations after the Jammu & Kashmir reorganisation — reignited the debate on fiscal federalism. States, particularly those in the South, argued that the 1% reduction, combined with the Commission's use of 2011 Census population data (instead of 1971), penalised states that successfully controlled population growth. Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Karnataka contended that their fiscal effort (own-tax revenue as % of GSDP) exceeded the national average, yet they received lower per capita transfers.

The Centre countered that the 2011 Census better reflects current demographic reality and that the horizontal distribution formula — with 12.5% weight for demographic performance — already rewards population control. Moreover, the Commission increased grants for health, education, and rural local bodies, which disproportionately benefit poorer states. The GST compensation cess shortfall during 2020–22, and the Centre's decision to borrow on behalf of states, further complicated the trust deficit.

Three structural issues underlie this friction. First, the vertical split (Centre:States) is a zero-sum negotiation every five years, not a principled allocation. Second, the horizontal formula tries to balance equity (needs), efficiency (effort), and demography — but the weights are arbitrary and politically contested. Third, states have limited own-revenue autonomy; petroleum, alcohol, and stamp duties remain outside GST, yet the Centre controls major direct taxes. The result: states depend on transfers for 40–60% of revenue, making every Commission report a high-stakes political event.

A durable fix requires: (1) a permanent vertical sharing ratio inscribed in legislation, not discretion; (2) horizontal weights determined by an independent fiscal council, not the Commission alone; (3) expanding states' own-tax base — bringing petroleum and electricity into GST, rationalising stamp duties; (4) a clear framework for Centre-state borrowing coordination. Without these, fiscal federalism remains a series of ad hoc bargains, not a constitutional partnership.

Model Précis (104 words)
The Fifteenth Finance Commission's reduction of vertical devolution to 41% and use of 2011 Census data sparked State-Centre friction. Southern states argued this penalised population control and fiscal effort, despite higher own-tax revenue ratios. The Centre defended the demographic weight and increased health/education grants. Three structural flaws persist: the vertical split is a quinquennial zero-sum bargain; horizontal weights (equity, efficiency, demography) are arbitrary; states lack own-revenue autonomy (petroleum, alcohol outside GST, Centre controls direct taxes), making them transfer-dependent (40–60% of revenue). A durable fix needs: legislated permanent vertical ratio; independent fiscal council for horizontal weights; expanded state tax base (petroleum, electricity into GST); and Centre-state borrowing coordination. Without these, fiscal federalism stays ad hoc, not constitutional partnership.

Practice Passage 2 (Hindi, ~240 words — Target Précis: 75–85 words)

Passage: जलवायु परिवर्तन और भारतीय कृषि

जलवायु परिवर्तन भारतीय कृषि के लिए एक गंभीर खतरा बन गया है। भारतीय कृषि अनुसंधान परिषद (ICAR) के अनुसार, 2050 तक तापमान में 1.5–2°C की वृद्धि से गेहूं की उपज में 6–23% और चावल की उपज में 4–14% की कमी आ सकती है। असामयिक वर्षा, सूखा, बाढ़ और चरम तापमान की घटनाएं बढ़ रही हैं। छोटे और सीमांत किसान (86% जोत), जिनके पास सिंचाई, बीमा और तकनीक की पहुंच नहीं है, सबसे अधिक प्रभावित होते हैं।

सरकार ने प्रधानमंत्री फसल बीमा योजना (PMFBY), प्रधानमंत्री कृषि सिंचाई योजना (PMKSY), और जलवायु-स्मार्ट कृषि पहल शुरू की हैं। लेकिन PMFBY में दावों के निपटारे में देरी, PMKSY में सिंचाई कवरेज की धीमी गति, और जलवायु-स्मार्ट तकनीकों के प्रसार में विस्तार सेवाओं की कमी बाधा बनी हुई हैं। इसके अलावा, फसल विविधीकरण — धान-गेहूं चक्र से दलहन, तिलहन, बाजरा की ओर — के लिए न्यूनतम समर्थन मूल्य (MSP) और खरीद ढांचा अभी भी धान-गेहूं के पक्ष में झुका हुआ है।

तीन नीतिगत बदलाव आवश्यक हैं: पहला, बीमा दावों का समयबद्ध निपटारा और प्रीमियम सब्सिडी का प्रत्यक्ष लाभ अंतरण; दूसरा, MSP और खरीद का विविधीकरण — दलहन, तिलहन, बाजरा के लिए सुनिश्चित खरीद; तीसरा, जलवायु-स्मार्ट प्रथाओं (शून्य जुताई, फसल अवशेष प्रबंधन, सूक्ष्म सिंचाई) के लिए परिणाम-आधारित प्रोत्साहन। बिना इनके, कृषि की जलवायु संवेदनशीलता खाद्य सुरक्षा और ग्रामीण आजीविका को खतरे में डालती रहेगी।

Model Précis (Hindi, 78 words)
प्रेसिस: जलवायु परिवर्तन से 2050 तक गेहूं उपज 6–23% और चावल 4–14% घट सकती है (ICAR)। छोटे किसान (86% जोत) सर्वाधिक प्रभावित। PMFBY, PMKSY, जलवायु-स्मार्ट पहल मौजूद हैं, परन्तु बीमा दावा विलम्ब, सिंचाई कवरेज धीमी, प्रसार सेवाएं कमजोर, और MSP/खरीद ढांचा धान-गेहूं पक्षपाती है। तीन बदलाव जरूरी: 1) समयबद्ध बीमा निपटारा, प्रीमियम सब्सिडी DBT; 2) MSP/खरीद विविधीकरण — दलहन, तिलहन, बाजरा हेतु सुनिश्चित खरीद; 3) जलवायु-स्मार्ट प्रथाओं (शून्य जुताई, सूक्ष्म सिंचाई) हेतु परिणाम-आधारित प्रोत्साहन। बिना इनके, खाद्य सुरक्षा और ग्रामीण आजीविका संकटग्रस्त रहेंगी।

Practice Passage 3 (English, ~350 words — Target Précis: 110–120 words)

Passage: Judicial Appointments and the Collegium Opacity

The Supreme Court's 2015 judgment striking down the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act reaffirmed the primacy of the Collegium system — the Chief Justice of India and four senior-most judges appointing judges to the higher judiciary. The Court held that judicial independence, a basic structure feature, requires insulation from executive influence. However, the judgment also acknowledged the Collegium's opacity: no written criteria, no minutes, no reasons for selection or rejection, and no public scrutiny. The subsequent Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) negotiations between the Court and the Executive have dragged on for years, with disagreements over "national security" veto, seniority vs. merit, and complaint-handling mechanisms.

Critics argue that opacity breeds arbitrariness. The absence of objective criteria allows subjective preferences — regional, communal, ideological — to influence appointments. The "uncle judge" syndrome (relatives of sitting/retired judges elevated disproportionately) persists. Data from 2018–23 show that 68% of High Court appointees had prior connections to the higher judiciary or senior advocates. Meanwhile, vacancies hover at 30–35% across High Courts, delaying justice delivery.

The Executive's counter is that the NJAC provided a transparent, broad-based mechanism: the CJI, two senior judges, the Law Minister, and two "eminent persons" (one from SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women). The Court's rejection, they contend, prioritised institutional self-preservation over accountability. The Law Commission's 230th Report (2009) had recommended a Judicial Appointments Commission with a balanced composition — a middle path the Court did not adopt.

A viable reform must preserve judicial primacy while mandating transparency. This requires: (1) published eligibility criteria (integrity, competence, diversity, temperament); (2) recorded reasons for every selection/rejection; (3) a time-bound MoP with a dispute-resolution mechanism; (4) a permanent secretariat to manage the process professionally; (5) annual public reporting on appointments, diversity metrics, and vacancy reduction. Judicial independence and accountability are not antagonists; they are co-requisites of constitutional legitimacy.

Model Précis (113 words)
The Supreme Court's 2015 NJAC judgment upheld Collegium primacy — CJI plus four senior judges appointing higher judiciary — citing judicial independence as basic structure. Yet the Court acknowledged the Collegium's opacity: no written criteria, minutes, reasons, or public scrutiny. MoP negotiations with the Executive have stalled over "national security" veto, seniority vs. merit, and complaints. Opacity breeds arbitrariness: 68% of High Court appointees (2018–23) had prior judicial/senior advocate connections; vacancies persist at 30–35%. The Executive argues NJAC offered transparency (CJI, two judges, Law Minister, two eminent persons); critics say the Court chose self-preservation. The Law Commission (230th Report) recommended a balanced Judicial Appointments Commission — a middle path unused. Viable reform: published eligibility criteria (integrity, competence, diversity, temperament); recorded reasons for every decision; time-bound MoP with dispute resolution; professional secretariat; annual public reporting on appointments, diversity, vacancy reduction. Independence and accountability are co-requisites, not antagonists.
