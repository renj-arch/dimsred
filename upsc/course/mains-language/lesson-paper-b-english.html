1. Exam Structure & Syllabus

Paper B is the English qualifying paper in the UPSC Civil Services Main Examination. It carries 300 marks over 3 hours. The qualifying threshold is 25% (75 marks out of 300) UPSC CSE Notification 2024, Section III, Plan of Examination. Marks are not added to the final merit list. The paper is set at approximately matriculation (Class X) standard, but this means careful, formal written English — not conversational fluency.

The 25% Floor — Non-Negotiable
Every year, 2–4% of candidates who clear Prelims and score well in GS/Essay/Optional fail Paper B because they treated it as trivial. "Matriculation standard" does not mean "easy" — it means the level of grammar and vocabulary expected, not the effort required. A candidate writing informally, making basic agreement errors, or leaving sections blank will fall below 75/300.

1.1 Syllabus Components (Official Notification)

The UPSC notification lists four components for Paper B. Unlike Paper A, translation is not listed for Paper B. Do not assume it will appear; prepare only the listed sections.

Component What is Tested Typical Weight (est.)
Comprehension Reading a passage (400–600 words) and answering questions on meaning, inference, vocabulary, tone, structure ~75 marks
Précis Writing Condensing a passage to ~⅓ length preserving argument, tone, logical flow ~75 marks
Usage & Vocabulary Grammar, sentence correction, sentence improvement, idioms, phrasal verbs, word choice ~75 marks
Short Essay One or two essays of ~150–200 words each on general/topical subjects ~75 marks

Estimated weights based on analysis of UPSC CSE Mains question papers 2018–2024 UPSC Previous Year Question Papers, Paper B (English), 2018–2024.

2. Comprehension & Précis

Both comprehension and précis passages in Paper B are in English. The passages are typically drawn from editorials, analytical essays, or government reports — formal, structured, argumentative prose.

2.1 Comprehension Strategy (Exam-Hall Workflow)

  1. Skim for architecture (90 sec): Read first and last paragraphs fully; read first sentence of each middle paragraph. Identify: central thesis, paragraph functions (claim, evidence, counter-argument, concession, conclusion), author's stance.
  2. Read questions (60 sec): Classify each: Direct fact (explicit in text), Inference (must connect two+ pieces), Vocabulary in context, Tone/Attitude, Main idea.
  3. Targeted re-read (3–4 min): Locate answers. For inference questions, mark the two sentences that jointly yield the answer.
  4. Answer in own words: Do not lift sentences. Paraphrase. For vocabulary, explain the meaning as used in the passage, not the dictionary definition.
Inference Question — Worked Example
Passage excerpt: "The reform reduced procedural delays, yet the backlog grew. Officials attributed this to a surge in filings, but the data show filings rose only 3% while disposals fell 12%."

Question: What can be inferred about the officials' explanation?

Wrong approach: "Officials are lying." (Too strong; not in text)
Correct inference: "The officials' explanation (surge in filings) is not supported by the data, since filings rose only marginally while disposals fell significantly, suggesting the backlog growth stems from reduced disposals, not increased filings."

2.2 Précis Writing — The 6-Step Method

The précis in Paper B must be written in your own words, approximately one-third the length of the original, preserving the argument structure and author's tone.

  1. Read twice — first for gist, second for logical skeleton (thesis → reasons → evidence → conclusion).
  2. Write the thesis statement — one sentence capturing the central argument.
  3. List supporting points in sequence — strip examples, statistics, quotations, repetitions.
  4. Draft in your own words — target ⅓ length; use formal connectors (however, consequently, moreover, conversely, therefore).
  5. Verify fidelity — check: no distortion, no added opinion, logical flow intact, tone preserved.
  6. Word count & polish — count words; ensure single coherent paragraph.
Précis Worked Example (Original ~210 words → Précis ~70 words)
Original: "The notion that economic growth automatically reduces poverty has been challenged by recent evidence. While India's GDP grew at an average of 7% between 2004 and 2019, the pace of poverty reduction slowed after 2011. The reason is structural: growth has been concentrated in skill-intensive services and capital-intensive manufacturing, which generate relatively few jobs for the low-skilled workforce. Meanwhile, agriculture — still employing over 40% of workers — has seen stagnant productivity. Inequality has widened; the top 10% now hold 57% of national income (World Inequality Lab, 2022). Redistributive policies — progressive taxation, universal basic services, land reform — are necessary complements to growth if poverty is to be durably reduced. Growth is necessary but not sufficient."

Précis: High GDP growth (7%, 2004–19) did not proportionally reduce poverty because growth concentrated in skill- and capital-intensive sectors, creating few low-skilled jobs, while agriculture — employing 40%+ — stagnated. Inequality widened (top 10% hold 57% income). Durable poverty reduction requires redistributive policies — progressive taxation, universal basic services, land reform — alongside growth. Growth is necessary but not sufficient.
Précis Traps in Paper B
1) Summary ≠ Précis: A summary lists points; a précis preserves the argumentative architecture. 2) Tone shift: If the author is sceptical, your précis must be sceptical — not neutral. 3) Example creep: Do not include "for example, in 2019..." unless the example is the argument. 4) Connector overload: Use transitions only where the logic shifts. 5) Passive voice overuse: Prefer active ("The data show..." not "It is shown by the data...").

3. Grammar & Usage (Error Correction, Sentence Improvement)

This section tests formal written English — the grammar of government reports, judicial opinions, and quality editorials. It is not a test of spoken English or "rules memorisation." Questions appear as: Error Spotting (which part has an error), Sentence Improvement (choose the best version), and Fill in the Blanks (grammar/preposition/verb form).

3.1 High-Yield Grammar Areas

Area What UPSC Tests Example Trap
Subject-Verb Agreement Intervening phrases, collective nouns, each/every/none, either...or/neither...nor "The committee, along with its members, were divided." → was
Tense Consistency Sequence of tenses in reported speech, conditional structures, perfect aspects "He said he will come." → would (reported speech)
Modals & Conditionals Should/would/could/might in formal recommendations; mixed conditionals "The report recommended that he should be appointed." → be (subjunctive)
Prepositions & Phrasal Verbs Dependent prepositions (adhere to, consonant with, precedent for); formal phrasal verbs "The policy is aimed at reducing poverty." (correct); not "aimed for"
Parallelism Lists, correlative conjunctions (not only...but also, either...or) "The scheme aims to provide housing, creating jobs, and improve health." → create
Modifiers (Dangling/Misplaced) Participial phrases, reduced clauses at sentence start "Having reviewed the evidence, the decision was made." → The committee made the decision
Pronoun Reference & Case Ambiguous antecedents; who/whom, who/whomever in formal prose "The officer who I met yesterday..." → whom (object of met)
Articles & Determiners Definite/indefinite in generic vs. specific reference; each/every/both/neither "Each of the candidates have submitted." → has

3.2 Sentence Improvement — Decision Framework

When choosing the "best" version, apply this hierarchy:

  1. Grammatical correctness — eliminate options with errors.
  2. Formal register — prefer objective, concise, impersonal style.
  3. Conciseness — fewer words, same meaning (but not at cost of clarity).
  4. Parallelism & flow — balanced structures, logical connectors.
Sentence Improvement — Worked Example
Original: "The reason for the delay in the project completion is because the contractor failed to supply the materials on time."

Options:
A. The reason for the delay in the project completion is because the contractor failed to supply the materials on time.
B. The reason for the delay in project completion is that the contractor failed to supply materials on time.
C. The project was delayed because the contractor failed to supply materials on time.
D. Due to the contractor failing to supply materials on time, the project completion was delayed.

Analysis: A — "reason...is because" is redundant (reason = cause). B — corrects redundancy, formal. C — most concise, active, formal. D — "due to" modifies nouns, not verbs; "failing" is a gerund, less formal.

Best: C

4. Letter & Report Writing

Paper B tests formal official correspondence and report writing. Both follow structured formats. The topics are administrative, civic, or professional — not personal.

4.1 Formal Letter Format (Block Style)

Element Specification
Sender's Address Top left (no name). Pin code on last line.
Date Below address. Format: 14 November 2026.
Receiver's Designation & Address Below date. Designation first, then office, then address.
Subject Line "Subject:" followed by concise phrase (6–10 words).
Salutation "Sir," or "Madam," (for officials); "Dear Sir/Madam," (general).
Body — Para 1 Context: why you are writing (reference, event, problem).
Body — Para 2 Details: facts, request, proposal, complaint specifics.
Body — Para 3 Call to action: what you expect, by when, next steps.
Complimentary Close "Yours faithfully," (if "Sir/Madam"); "Yours sincerely," (if named).
Signature Block Name (printed), designation if applicable, contact.

Sir,

I write to bring to your notice the deteriorating condition of Park Street between Mayo Road and Lindsay Street. Over the past three months, multiple deep potholes have developed, causing daily traffic congestion and posing a safety risk to two-wheeler riders and pedestrians. Despite two written complaints lodged via the KMC portal (Ref: KMC/GRV/2026/04412, KMC/GRV/2026/05187), no remedial action has been taken.

The monsoon has widened the potholes further. Waterlogging obscures them at night, increasing accident risk. The stretch serves three schools and a hospital; morning and evening peak hours see heavy pedestrian movement.

4.2 Formal Report Format

Reports in Paper B are typically event/incident reports or inspection/inquiry reports. Structure:

  1. Title — Descriptive, specific (e.g., "Report on Waterlogging in Ward 14 After Cyclone Dana")
  2. To / From / Date / Reference — Header block.
  3. Introduction — Purpose, scope, authority, methodology (site visit, records reviewed, interviews).
  4. Findings — Factual, numbered, objective. Use sub-headings.
  5. Analysis / Discussion — Causes, implications, comparison with norms.
  6. Recommendations — Numbered, actionable, prioritised (immediate/short-term/long-term).
  7. Conclusion — One-paragraph summary.
  8. Annexures — Photographs, data tables, correspondence (referenced).

5. Vocabulary & Practice Protocol

5.1 Vocabulary Tiers for Paper B

  • Tier 1 — Administrative/Legal Register (150 words): adjudicate, promulgate, erstwhile, pursuant to, notwithstanding, herein, therefrom, consequent upon, in furtherance of, vide, qua, inter alia, prima facie, suo motu, locus standi, caveat, mandamus, certiorari, habeas corpus, quo warranto, prohibition.
  • Tier 2 — Argument Connectors (50 phrases): in light of, by virtue of, consequent upon, notwithstanding, conversely, moreover, furthermore, nevertheless, accordingly, thereby, herein, wherein, whereby, whereupon, insofar as, be that as it may.
  • Tier 3 — Precision Verbs (replace weak verb + adverb): mitigate (not reduce), exacerbate (not make worse), underscore (not highlight), corroborate (not support), substantiate (not prove), elucidate (not explain), delineate (not describe), circumvent (not avoid), promulgate (not announce), adjudicate (not decide).
  • Tier 4 — Idioms & Phrasal Verbs (formal register only): bring to bear, call into question, give rise to, in the wake of, by dint of, at variance with, in furtherance of, for want of, notwithstanding that, be that as it may.

5.2 Weekly Practice Protocol (10-Week Cycle)

Day Activity Duration Output
Mon Comprehension: 1 editorial (The Hindu/Indian Express) + 6 questions 45 min Marked answers; inference log
Tue Précis: 1 passage (400–500 words) → précis + word count 45 min Draft; fidelity check vs 6-step method
Wed Grammar: 20 questions (error spotting + sentence improvement) 35 min Error log by category (agreement, tense, modifier...)
Thu Letter/Report: 1 task, timed (25 min) 30 min Formatted output; structure checklist
Fri Vocabulary: 10 Tier 1/2 words + 5 idioms; write 3 sentences each 20 min Anki deck / flashcards; usage sentences
Sat Mock Section: Rotate full comprehension/précis/grammar (timed) 60 min Timed attempt; review every error
Sun Review: Error log consolidation; re-write 3 worst items 30 min Clean error log; rule summaries

5.3 Error Log Template

Maintain a spreadsheet: Date | Section | Question Type | Error Category | Original Sentence | Correction | Rule/Principle | Source. Review weekly. After 4 weeks, patterns emerge — e.g., "consistent modifier errors in sentence improvement" or "tense shifts in reported speech." Target those specifically.

5.4 Final 4-Week Intensification

  • Weeks 1–2: Full 3-hour timed mocks (previous year papers 2018–2024) — one per week. No dictionary, no breaks.
  • Week 3: Targeted remediation — re-do every error from mocks; re-write letters/reports; re-precise passages.
  • Week 4: Light review — flashcards, essay outlines, grammar rule summaries. No new material. Sleep well.
