What Paper B is

Paper B is the English qualifying paper: 300 marks, three hours, and a 25% pass mark (75/300). The marks are not counted in the merit list. It is set at matriculation standard, but that means careful, formal English, not a literature paper and not a grammar quiz.

The 25% rule
A candidate who fails Paper B is not ranked, however well the scored papers went. Treat 75/300 as a floor.

What the syllabus lists

  • Comprehension of a given passage.
  • Precis writing.
  • Usage and vocabulary.
  • Short essays.

The syllabus does not list a separate translation section for Paper B. Check the current notification and the printed paper before you assume a section will appear.

Where marks are lost

  • A summary instead of a precis. A precis keeps the argument and the tone in about one-third of the words. Cutting sentences is not compression.
  • Informal register. Contractions, chatty asides and rhetorical questions belong in a blog, not in a qualifying paper.
  • Vocabulary guessed from sound. Use the context of the passage. If a word is not clear, say what the sentence is doing rather than inventing a definition.
  • An essay with no structure. A short essay still needs a beginning, two or three developed points and a close.
  • Leaving a section blank. Attempt every section, even briefly. An unanswered section is a guaranteed loss.

A four-week routine

  1. Week 1 — Comprehension and precis. One editorial a day. Read once for the claim, once for the structure. Write the precis without looking back.
  2. Week 2 — Usage and vocabulary. Twenty minutes a day on sentence correction, idioms and word choice. Keep one error log.
  3. Week 3 — Essays. Two 250-word essays. Underline the thesis and the topic sentence of each paragraph before you write.
  4. Week 4 — Full paper. Sit one full Paper B under three hours. Fix the section that ran over time.
Answer discipline
Write the answer, not the working. The examiner is checking control of English, so a clear short sentence beats a tangled long one.

1. Exam Structure & Syllabus

Paper B (English) is a qualifying paper of 300 marks and three hours. The pass mark is 25% (75/300), and the marks are not added to the merit list. It tests functional English, not literature: comprehension, précis, grammar and usage, vocabulary, and letter/report writing. Prepare it as a skills paper, not a content paper.

2. Comprehension & Précis

Comprehension is the highest-yield section: the answers are in the passage, so method decides the marks. Read the questions first, then the passage; answer in the passage's own words; treat inference as "what must follow", not "what could be true".

For précis, compress to about one-third, keep the argument and tone, cut examples and repetition, and write in your own words. A faithful compression that follows the passage's logic scores; a fact-dump does not.

Worked précis
Passage (78 words): "Urban flooding is no longer a freak event but a predictable consequence of paving over wetlands, choking drains, and building on floodplains. Each monsoon the same localities flood, the same complaints are filed, and the same repairs are promised. What is missing is not money but a plan that treats drainage as infrastructure rather than as an afterthought to roads and real estate."
Précis (~25 words): Urban flooding is a predictable result of unplanned construction and neglected drainage; the deficit is planning, not funding.

3. Grammar & Usage

Grammar questions test a small set of recurring rules. Learn the rule, then the trap:

RuleWrongRight
Subject–verb agreementThe list of items are long.The list of items is long.
Pronoun referenceEvery student must bring their book.Every student must bring his or her book.
ComparativeHe is more taller.He is taller.
Tense consistencyHe said he is busy.He said he was busy.
PrepositionDiscuss about the issue.Discuss the issue.
Dangling modifierWalking home, the rain began.Walking home, I was caught in the rain.
Sentence improvement
In "improve the sentence" questions, change only what is wrong. If none of the options is better than the original, "no improvement" is a legitimate and often correct answer.

4. Letter & Report Writing

Formal letters follow a fixed skeleton: sender's address, date, receiver's designation and address, subject line, salutation, body (purpose in the first sentence, details in one or two paragraphs, action requested), and formal closing. Reports add a title, a byline, and a factual, third-person tone.

  • State purpose immediately — the examiner should know why you are writing by the first line.
  • One topic per letter — do not bundle unrelated requests.
  • Match register — formal for officials, semi-formal for acquaintances; never slang.
  • Reports: lead with the finding, then evidence, then recommendation.

5. Vocabulary & Practice Plan

Vocabulary is learned in context, not from lists. Read one editorial a day, note five new words with their sentence, and revise them weekly. For one-word substitution and idioms, group by theme (government, law, character, finance).
