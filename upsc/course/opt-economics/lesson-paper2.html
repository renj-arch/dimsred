Macroeconomics & International Economics

National Income & Aggregates

  • National Income Accounting: GDP, GNP, NNP, Personal Income, Disposable Income. Methods: Product, Income, Expenditure.
  • GDP vs GNP: GDP = domestic production; GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad.
  • Real vs Nominal GDP: GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100.
  • Green GDP: GDP adjusted for environmental degradation.
  • Limitations of GDP: Does not measure welfare, excludes non-market transactions, ignores income distribution, environmental degradation.

National Income Determination

  • Classical Theory: Say's Law - supply creates its own demand; full employment equilibrium.
  • Keynesian Theory: Effective demand (aggregate demand = aggregate supply), equilibrium level of output & employment.
  • Consumption Function: C = a + bY (autonomous consumption + MPC × income), APC & MPC.
  • Investment Function: Investment depends on interest rate & Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC).
  • Investment Multiplier: k = 1/(1-MPC) = 1/MPS.
  • Foreign Trade Multiplier: k_f = 1/(1 - MPC + MPM) where MPM = marginal propensity to import.
  • Foreign Trade Multiplier: k_f = 1/(1 - MPC + MPM) where MPM = marginal propensity to import.

Technique: Investment Multiplier
Question: 'If MPC = 0.8, what is the investment multiplier?'
Answer: Investment multiplier k = 1/(1-MPC) = 1/(1-0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5. A ₹1 crore increase in investment increases national income by ₹5 crore through the multiplier process.

Five‑Year Plans and the Transition to NITI Aayog

The planning era in India began with the First Five‑Year Plan (1951‑56) which prioritised agriculture, irrigation and power to rebuild a war‑torn economy. Subsequent plans shifted emphasis: the Second Plan (1956‑61) adopted the Mahalanobis model, concentrating on heavy industry and capital goods; the Third Plan (1961‑66) attempted self‑reliance but was disrupted by wars and droughts. The Sixth Plan (1980‑85) introduced the “growth with social justice” slogan, while the Fifth Plan (1974‑79) focused on poverty alleviation through the Minimum Needs Programme. The Sixth (1980‑85) and Seventh (1985‑90) plans emphasised modernisation, employment generation and technological upgrading. The Eighth Plan (1992‑97) was launched after the 1991 balance‑of‑payments crisis and marked the beginning of liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. The Ninth (1997‑2002) and Tenth (2002‑07) plans targeted higher growth rates (6‑8 %) and sectoral reforms. The Eleventh Plan (2007‑12) aimed at “faster, sustainable and more inclusive growth” with a 9 % average GDP target. The Twelfth Plan (2012‑17) set a 8 % growth goal and introduced monitorable targets for health, education and infrastructure.

In 2015 the Planning Commission was replaced by the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog). Unlike the Commission, NITI Aayog does not allocate plan funds; it functions as a policy think‑tank, promoting cooperative federalism through the Governing Council of Chief Ministers. It prepares the Three‑Year Action Agenda, the Seven‑Year Strategy and the Fifteen‑Year Vision documents. The shift reflects a move from centralised directive planning to indicative, evidence‑based policy formulation. Key differences include: (i) no mandatory plan outlays; (ii) greater role for states in designing schemes; (iii) emphasis on outcomes measured by the Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO). The transition also altered the fiscal architecture — central assistance now flows through Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) and the Finance Commission’s devolution formula rather than plan grants.

Monetary Policy Framework: Inflation Targeting and the Monetary Policy Committee

The Reserve Bank of India Act was amended in 2016 to establish a statutory Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) tasked with maintaining price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth. The MPC comprises six members — three from the RBI (Governor, Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and one officer) and three external members appointed by the Government for a four‑year term. Decisions are taken by majority vote; the Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie. The inflation target is set by the Central Government in consultation with the RBI every five years. The current target (2021‑26) is 4 % Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation with a tolerance band of ±2 % (i.e., 2 %‑6 %). If inflation breaches the band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI must publish a report explaining the reasons and remedial actions.

The policy repo rate is the key signalling instrument. Changes in the repo rate transmit through the short‑term money market (call/notice money, tri‑party repo), then to the banking system’s lending and deposit rates, influencing aggregate demand. The RBI also uses the standing deposit facility (SDF) and marginal standing facility (MSF) as corridor bounds. Open market operations (OMOs), variable rate reverse repo auctions, and the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF) manage durable liquidity. The framework replaced the earlier multiple‑indicator approach (money supply, exchange rate, credit growth) with a single nominal anchor, improving transparency and accountability. Empirical studies show that after 2016 inflation expectations have become better anchored, and the pass‑through from policy rate to bank lending rates has improved, though transmission to small‑borrower segments remains incomplete.

Technique: Repo Rate Transmission Calculation
Assume the policy repo rate is increased by 50 basis points. The banking system’s weighted average lending rate (WALR) responds with a pass‑through coefficient of 0.6 in the first quarter and 0.85 over four quarters. Compute the expected change in WALR after one quarter and after one year.
First‑quarter change = 0.50 % × 0.60 = 0.30 % (30 basis points). Four‑quarter change = 0.50 % × 0.85 = 0.425 % (42.5 basis points). The answer shows the lagged and incomplete nature of transmission, a point examiners often test.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “transmission mechanism”, structure the answer in three layers: (1) policy rate change → (2) money‑market rates (call, repo, T‑bill) → (3) bank lending/deposit rates → (4) aggregate demand components (consumption, investment, net exports). Cite the 2016 MPC Act, the current inflation target (4 % ±2 %), and the latest RBI Monetary Policy Report for data credibility.

Balance of Payments Structure and External Sector Reforms

The Balance of Payments (BoP) records all economic transactions between residents and non‑residents in a given period. It comprises the Current Account (goods, services, primary income, secondary income), the Capital Account (capital transfers, acquisition/disposal of non‑produced non‑financial assets) and the Financial Account (direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment, reserve assets). India’s merchandise trade deficit has historically been offset by net services exports (especially IT‑enabled services) and private remittances, keeping the current account deficit (CAD) moderate — typically 1‑2 % of GDP. The capital account has been liberalised in phases: the 1991 reforms introduced the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS), later replaced by a market‑determined exchange rate (1993). Foreign Institutional Investor (FII) limits were raised, External Commercial Borrowing (ECB) frameworks were rationalised, and the Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) policy moved to the automatic route for most sectors.

Key reforms after 2015 include: (i) the introduction of the Fully Accessible Route (FAR) for government securities to attract non‑resident investors; (ii) the increase in the FPI investment limit in corporate bonds to 15 % of outstanding stock; (iii) the liberalisation of ECB norms allowing start‑ups to raise up to USD 3 million; (iv) the launch of the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) at GIFT City with tax incentives for offshore banking. The RBI’s reserve management policy targets a comfortable import cover (currently around 11 months) and a diversified currency composition. The BoP identity — Current Account + Capital Account + Financial Account + Net Errors & Omissions = 0 — is frequently tested; candidates must be able to derive the impact of a surge in portfolio inflows on the exchange rate and reserve accumulation.

Fiscal Federalism: Finance Commissions and the GST Architecture

India’s fiscal federalism is anchored by the Finance Commission (FC) constituted under Article 280 every five years. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended a vertical devolution of 41 % of the divisible pool to states, down from 42 % in the Fourteenth FC, reflecting the inclusion of the GST compensation cess in the pool. Horizontal distribution uses criteria: population (15 % weight, 2011 Census), area (15 %), forest & ecology (10 %), income distance (45 %), demographic performance (12.5 %) and tax effort (2.5 %). The Commission also recommended revenue deficit grants for 17 states and sector‑specific grants for health, education and agriculture.

The Goods and Services Tax (GST), introduced in July 2017, subsumed multiple central and state indirect taxes into a dual GST (CGST, SGST/UTGST, IGST). The GST Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister with state finance ministers as members, decides rates, exemptions and threshold limits. The compensation mechanism guaranteed states a 14 % annual growth in GST revenue for five years (2017‑22), funded by a cess on sin and luxury goods. Post‑compensation, the Council has moved to a “revenue neutral rate” approach and rationalised the rate structure to four main slabs (5 %, 12 %, 18 %, 28 %). The Centre’s share of GST (CGST) and the states’ share (SGST) are collected concurrently; IGST on inter‑state supplies is apportioned by the Centre to the destination state. The GST Network (GSTN) provides the IT backbone for return filing, invoice matching and e‑way bills. Issues frequently examined include: (i) the impact of rate changes on the revenue neutrality condition; (ii) the fiscal autonomy of states under the GST Council’s voting structure (three‑fourths majority required); (iii) the treatment of petroleum, alcohol and real estate outside GST.

Industrial Policy Evolution and the Production‑Linked Incentive Scheme

India’s industrial policy has moved from the licence‑permit raj of the 1950s‑80s to a liberalised regime after 1991, and most recently to a targeted incentive architecture centred on the Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme. The 1991 reforms abolished industrial licensing for all but a handful of strategic sectors, introduced automatic approval for foreign direct investment up to 51 % in most industries, and replaced the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act with the Competition Act, 2002. The National Manufacturing Policy (2011) set a goal of raising manufacturing’s share of GDP to 25 % and creating 100 million jobs by 2022, but the target was missed, prompting a shift toward sector‑specific incentives.

The PLI scheme, announced in 2020 and subsequently expanded, covers 14 sectors including mobile phones, pharmaceuticals, automobiles, textiles, specialty steel, and electronics. The central outlay is ₹1.97 lakh crore (≈ USD 26 billion) over five years, disbursed as a percentage of incremental sales over a base year. For example, the mobile‑phone PLI offers 4‑6 % of incremental revenue for five years, subject to a minimum investment threshold and domestic value‑addition norms. The scheme is administered by the concerned line ministries with a monitoring framework that includes quarterly production reports and third‑party verification.

Key Design Features

  • Performance‑linked payouts – incentives are paid only after the beneficiary achieves the stipulated production or sales milestone, reducing fiscal risk.
  • Domestic value‑addition (DVA) requirements – most PLI categories mandate a minimum DVA of 30‑40 % to prevent mere assembly operations.
  • Sunset clauses – each sector’s incentive window is limited to five years, after which the scheme lapses unless extended.
  • Export orientation – several PLI categories (e.g., electronics, pharma) require a minimum export share, aligning the scheme with the “Make in India for the world” narrative.

Early Outcomes and Critiques

By the end of FY 2023‑24, the mobile‑phone PLI had attracted committed investments of over ₹30,000 crore and generated incremental production worth ₹1.2 lakh crore, with exports crossing ₹70,000 crore. However, the scheme has faced criticism for (i) concentrating benefits in a few large firms, (ii) insufficient backward‑linkage development, and (iii) the administrative burden of compliance monitoring. The 2024‑25 budget outlined plans for a “PLI 2.0” focusing on emerging sectors such as drones, robotics, and green hydrogen, with a stronger emphasis on MSME participation through a dedicated window.

Agricultural Price Policy: Minimum Support Prices, Procurement and Market Reforms

India’s agricultural price policy rests on three pillars: the Minimum Support Price (MSP) announced by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP), physical procurement by the Food Corporation of India (FCI) and state agencies, and a series of market‑reform legislations aimed at improving price discovery. The MSP is calculated using the “A2+FL” cost concept (actual paid‑out costs plus imputed value of family labour) plus a 50 % margin, a formula adopted in the 2018‑19 budget; the Swaminathan Commission had recommended a higher “C2+50%” benchmark. For the 2023‑24 marketing season, MSPs were fixed for 23 crops; paddy (common) was set at ₹2,183 per quintal, while wheat for the 2024‑25 season was fixed at ₹2,275 per quintal.

Procurement Mechanism

Procurement is concentrated in a few states — Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh account for over 80 % of wheat and rice procurement. FCI operates through a network of over 2,000 procurement centres, while state agencies such as the Punjab State Civil Supplies Corporation (PUNSUP) and Haryana State Cooperative Supply and Marketing Federation (HAFED) handle the bulk of operations. The central government reimburses the economic cost (procurement price plus incidentals) to FCI, which is financed through the food subsidy budget. In FY 2023‑24, the food subsidy bill exceeded ₹2.1 lakh crore, reflecting both higher MSPs and expanded coverage under the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013.

Market Reform Acts

  • Farmers’ Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020 – allows intra‑state and inter‑state trade outside APMC mandis, enabling electronic trading platforms.
  • Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, 2020 – provides a legal framework for contract farming with price assurance clauses.
  • Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020 – removes stock‑holding limits on cereals, pulses, oilseeds, edible oils, onion and potatoes except under extraordinary circumstances.

These Acts were repealed in November 2021 after widespread farmer protests, but the underlying policy direction — promoting alternative marketing channels, warehouse receipt financing, and the electronic National Agriculture Market (e‑NAM) — continues. As of March 2024, e‑NAM integrates 1,260 mandis across 23 states and 4 union territories, with a cumulative trade volume of ₹2.3 lakh crore.

Examiner’s Twist: MSP Calculation vs. Market Price
A question asks: “If the market price of wheat falls 10 % below the announced MSP, what is the fiscal implication for the central government assuming procurement remains unchanged?”
The MSP is a floor price; the government is not obliged to procure at MSP unless it chooses to. If procurement volume stays the same, the economic cost to FCI (procurement price + incidentals) does not change because the procurement price is the MSP. However, the market price decline widens the gap between MSP and open‑market price, increasing the subsidy burden when FCI releases stocks under the NFSA at the Central Issue Price (CIP), which is lower than MSP. The fiscal impact is therefore a higher food subsidy outlay, not a direct loss on procurement. Candidates must distinguish between procurement cost (fixed at MSP) and distribution subsidy (difference between economic cost and CIP).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes MSP, procurement, and subsidy numbers, first identify which leg of the policy triangle is being tested: (1) price‑setting (CACP formula), (2) physical procurement (FCI/state agency operations), or (3) fiscal transfer (food subsidy budget). Write the relevant equation — e.g., Food Subsidy = Economic Cost − CIP × Quantity Distributed — and plug in the given data. This structured approach prevents conflating procurement cost with distribution subsidy.

Financial Sector Regulation: Basel Norms, Prompt Corrective Action and Resolution Framework

India’s banking regulation has converged with global standards through phased adoption of Basel III capital and liquidity norms, a domestic Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, which created a time‑bound resolution mechanism. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) mandated a minimum Common Equity Tier‑1 (CET1) ratio of 5.5 % plus a Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) of 2.5 % by March 2020, bringing the total CET1 requirement to 8 %. The Leverage Ratio was set at 3.5 % and the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) at 100 % from January 2019, with the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) at 100 % from October 2021.

Prompt Corrective Action (PCA)

The PCA framework, revised in 2017, triggers supervisory actions when a bank breaches any of three risk thresholds: (i) CET1 below 5.5 % (or 4.5 % for small finance banks), (ii) Net NPA ratio above 6 % (or 9 % for small finance banks), and (iii) Return on Assets (RoA) below 0.25 % for two consecutive years. Once triggered, the RBI imposes mandatory restrictions — dividend distribution ban, branch expansion freeze, and limits on management compensation — and discretionary measures such as recapitalisation directives or merger recommendations. As of March 2024, only two public sector banks remained under PCA, down from eleven in 2018, reflecting recapitalisation and asset‑quality improvement.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) Resolution

The IBC establishes a 180‑day (extendable by 90 days) Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) under the oversight of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT). The Committee of Creditors (CoC), comprising financial creditors, approves a resolution plan by a 66 % voting share. Since inception, the IBC has resolved over 500 cases with a recovery rate averaging around 30‑40 % of admitted claims, significantly higher than the pre‑IBC regime. The 2020 amendment introduced a pre‑packaged insolvency route for MSMEs, and the 2022 amendment allowed cross‑border insolvency cooperation under the UNCITRAL Model Law.

Recent Developments

  • Scale‑Based Regulation (SBR) – effective October 2022, classifies NBFCs into Base, Middle, Upper and Top layers with proportionate capital, governance and disclosure requirements.
  • Digital Lending Guidelines – issued September 2022, mandate a Key Fact Statement, cooling‑off period, and direct disbursement to borrower bank accounts to curb predatory practices.
  • Green Deposit Framework – launched June 2023, enables banks to raise earmarked deposits for climate‑friendly projects, with disclosure aligned to the RBI’s taxonomy.

Trade Policy Architecture: Tariff Structure, Free Trade Agreements and WTO Dispute Settlement

India’s applied Most‑Favoured‑Nation (MFN) tariff schedule averages 14.8 % (simple average) across all products, with agricultural tariffs averaging 32 % and non‑agricultural tariffs around 10 %. The tariff profile exhibits high dispersion — peaks above 100 % on certain agricultural items (e.g., coffee, tea) and low rates on capital goods. Since 2014, India has pursued a “strategic” FTA agenda, concluding comprehensive agreements with the United Arab Emirates (2022), Australia (2022), and the United Kingdom (ongoing), while reviewing older pacts such as the ASEAN‑India FTA (2010) and the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA).

Key FTA Provisions

  • Rules of Origin (RoO) – typically require 35‑40 % regional value content or a change in tariff classification; the UAE‑India CEPA introduces a “product‑specific” RoO schedule.
  • Services Liberalisation – Mode 1 (cross‑border) and Mode 4 (movement of natural persons) commitments are deeper in newer FTAs, with mutual recognition agreements for professional qualifications.
  • Investment Chapters – include Investor‑State Dispute Settlement (ISDS) provisions; India’s 2017 Model BIT replaces the earlier BITs and limits ISDS to exhaustion of local remedies.

WTO Engagement

India has been an active litigant at the WTO Dispute Settlement Body (DSB). Notable cases include: (i) India — Solar Cells (DS456), where the Appellate Body ruled against domestic content requirements; (ii) United States — Countervailing Measures on Certain Hot‑Rolled Carbon Steel Flat Products from India (DS536), where India secured a favourable ruling on subsidy calculations; and (iii) India — Export Subsidies on Certain Products (DS541), leading to the phased withdrawal of the Merchandise Exports from India Scheme (MEIS) and its replacement by the Remission of Duties and Taxes on Exported Products (RoDTEP) scheme, which is WTO‑compatible as a duty‑drawback mechanism.

Current Negotiating Priorities

At the 13th Ministerial Conference (MC13, 2024), India pushed for a permanent solution on public stockholding for food security, a special safeguard mechanism for developing countries, and discipline on fisheries subsidies that preserves policy space for artisanal fishers. The ongoing Joint Statement Initiative on E‑Commerce sees India advocating data‑localisation carve‑outs and source‑code protection clauses.

Social Protection Architecture: Targeted Subsidies, Direct Benefit Transfer and Universal Basic Income Debates

India’s social protection system combines contributory schemes (Employees’ Provident Fund, Employees’ State Insurance), non‑contributory targeted transfers, and a growing direct‑benefit‑transfer (DBT) infrastructure. The DBT platform, launched in 2013, leverages the Aadhaar biometric ID and the Jan‑Dhan‑Aadhaar‑Mobile (JAM) trinity to route subsidies directly into beneficiaries’ bank accounts. By March 2024, over 1,300 schemes across 54 ministries were on DBT, covering 1.2 billion transactions annually and estimated fiscal savings of ₹2.7 lakh crore through leak‑reduction.

Major Targeted Programmes

  • Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM‑KISAN) – ₹6,000 per year in three equal instalments to land‑holding farmer families; coverage expanded to 11 crore beneficiaries in 2023‑24.
  • National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013 – entitles 75 % of rural and 50 % of urban population to 5 kg of foodgrains per person per month at ₹3/₹2/₹1 per kg for rice/wheat/coarse grains.
  • Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PM‑GKAY) – additional 5 kg free foodgrains per month during COVID‑19, extended multiple times; cost ₹3.9 lakh crore cumulatively.
  • Ayushman Bharat‑Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM‑JAY) – health cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary/tertiary care; 12 crore families enrolled as of 2024.

Universal Basic Income (UBI) Debate

The 2017‑18 Economic Survey devoted a chapter to UBI, estimating a quasi‑universal transfer of ₹7,620 per year (≈ 0.5 % of GDP) could replace multiple subsidies with lower administrative cost. Critics argue that (i) fiscal space is constrained — a full UBI at the poverty line would cost 4‑5 % of GDP, (ii) targeting efficiency of existing schemes has improved via DBT, and (iii) political economy risks of withdrawing in‑kind transfers (food, fertilizer) are high. The 2023‑24 budget did not announce a UBI pilot but increased allocation for PM‑KISAN; the government separately launched the “PM‑VISHWAKARMA” scheme for artisans in 2023, reflecting a preference for occupational targeting over universal cash.

  • Confusing the PLI scheme’s incremental‑sales basis with a simple investment subsidy — the payout is triggered only after production milestones are met.
  • Treating MSP as a guaranteed procurement price for all farmers; procurement is limited to designated crops, states and agencies.
  • Mixing up the PCA trigger thresholds for CET1 (5.5 %) and Net NPA (6 %) — each breach independently invokes the framework.
  • Assuming all FTAs contain identical Rules of Origin; product‑specific RoO schedules differ markedly across agreements.
  • Equating DBT with UBI — DBT is a delivery mechanism for existing targeted schemes, not an unconditional universal transfer.
  • Overlooking the distinction between the food subsidy (economic cost minus CIP) and the procurement cost (MSP plus incidentals) when calculating fiscal impact.
  • Citing the 2017 Model BIT as the governing treaty for all Indian BITs; many older BITs remain in force until terminated or replaced.

Public Debt Management and Debt Sustainability Framework

India’s public debt management is anchored in the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, amended in 2018 to embed a medium‑term debt‑to‑GDP target of 40 % for the Centre and 20 % for states by 2024‑25. The Department of Economic Affairs (DEA) formulates the annual borrowing calendar, while the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) acts as the debt manager, conducting primary auctions of dated securities, treasury bills and cash management bills. The debt portfolio is split into marketable (dated securities, T‑bills) and non‑marketable (small savings, provident funds, special securities issued to the RBI). As of March 2024, the Centre’s gross debt stood at around ₹155 lakh crore, with a weighted average maturity of approximately 10.2 years and an average coupon near 7.1 %.

Debt sustainability analysis (DSA) follows the IMF‑World Bank framework, evaluating solvency (debt‑to‑GDP, interest‑to‑revenue) and liquidity (gross financing needs, rollover risk). The 2023‑24 DSA indicated that the Centre’s interest‑to‑revenue ratio remained above 30 %, signalling limited fiscal space for counter‑cyclical spending. The RBI’s “Medium‑Term Debt Management Strategy” (MTDMS) 2022‑27 emphasises lengthening maturity, increasing the share of floating‑rate bonds, and developing a liquid secondary market through the “Negotiated Dealing System‑Order Matching” (NDS‑OM) platform. The introduction of the “Sovereign Green Bond” framework in 2022‑23, with around ₹16,000 crore issued by March 2024, diversifies the investor base and links borrowing to climate‑finance commitments.

State‑level debt is monitored through the “State Debt Sustainability Report” published by the RBI. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a consolidated debt‑to‑GDP ceiling of 20 % for states, with a glide path for high‑debt states (Punjab, Rajasthan, West Bengal). The Centre’s “Special Assistance to States for Capital Expenditure” scheme (2023‑24) provides interest‑free 50‑year loans conditional on reform milestones, linking debt relief to capital formation. The “Cash Management Bill” (CMB) window, expanded in 2020, allows the Centre to meet temporary mismatches without disturbing the dated‑security calendar, improving cash‑flow predictability.

Key risks include: (i) a sharp rise in global yields transmitting to domestic rates via the “interest‑rate‑parity” channel; (ii) contingent liabilities from guarantees to public‑sector enterprises (estimated at ₹5 lakh crore as of 2023); and (iii) “off‑budget” borrowings through the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) and extra‑budgetary resources (EBRs) that obscure the true fiscal stance. The 2024‑25 budget announced a “Debt Management Office” (DMO) under the DEA to professionalise issuance, enhance transparency, and align borrowing with the MTDMS.

Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments and the Unified Payments Interface

The Jan‑Dhan‑Aadhaar‑Mobile (JAM) trinity, launched in 2014, created the backbone for direct‑benefit transfers and digital payments. By March 2024, over 50 crore Jan‑Dhan accounts were operational, with a deposit base exceeding ₹2 lakh crore. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), developed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) and launched in April 2016, processes real‑time inter‑bank transfers using a virtual payment address. UPI transaction volume crossed 12 billion in FY 2023‑24, valued at around ₹200 lakh crore, making it the world’s largest retail payment system by volume.

Regulatory architecture: the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 empowers the RBI to authorise payment system operators. The “Payments Vision 2025” (released 2022) sets targets for 3 billion daily digital transactions, 100 % merchant acceptance of QR‑code payments, and a “Unified Lending Interface” (ULI) for credit‑assessment data sharing. The RBI’s “Digital Lending Guidelines” (September 2022) mandate a “Key Fact Statement”, cooling‑off period, and direct disbursement to borrower accounts, curbing predatory practices by fintech lenders.

Interoperability milestones: (i) UPI‑QR code interoperability (2021) allows any UPI app to scan any merchant QR; (ii) “UPI‑Lite” (2022) enables offline low‑value transactions up to ₹200 without internet; (iii) “UPI‑123PAY” (2023) extends UPI to feature phones via IVR, missed‑call and sound‑based protocols. The “Bharat Bill Payment System” (BBPS), operational since 2018, aggregates recurring bill payments across 200+ billers, processing an estimated ₹5 lakh crore annually.

Financial inclusion metrics: the “Financial Inclusion Index” (FI‑Index) constructed by the RBI rose from 43.4 (2017) to 60.1 (2023), driven by account ownership, digital transaction intensity and credit‑to‑GDP ratio for micro‑enterprises. The “Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana” (PMMY) leveraged the digital stack to sanction around ₹23 lakh crore to 38 crore micro‑units by March 2024. The “Account Aggregator” framework (2021) enables consent‑based sharing of financial data across regulated entities, reducing information asymmetry for MSME credit.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Explain why the UPI transaction volume is not a reliable indicator of the depth of financial inclusion.”
UPI volume captures the number of digital payment instructions, which can be inflated by high‑frequency low‑value transactions (e.g., peer‑to‑peer transfers, merchant QR scans) that do not necessarily reflect access to credit, savings or insurance. Depth of inclusion is better measured by the share of adults with at least one formal savings account, the credit‑to‑GDP ratio for micro‑enterprises, and the usage of insurance or pension products. The FI‑Index combines these dimensions; a rise in UPI volume alone may mask stagnant credit penetration or dormant accounts. Therefore, citing UPI volume as proof of deep inclusion is a conceptual error.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question links a digital‑payment metric to inclusion, first identify the metric’s scope (transaction count vs. stock of accounts vs. credit flow). Then map each metric to the three pillars of inclusion — access, usage, quality — and explicitly state which pillar the metric covers and which it omits. This structured comparison earns full marks for analytical depth.

Labour Market Reforms: Code on Wages, Industrial Relations and Social Security

Parliament passed four labour codes between 2019 and 2020 — the Code on Wages, 2019; the Industrial Relations Code, 2020; the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020; and the Code on Social Security, 2020 — consolidating 29 central labour statutes. The Codes aim to simplify compliance, extend coverage to the unorganised sector (≈ 90 % of workforce), and introduce a universal minimum wage floor. As of March 2024, over 25 states have pre‑published draft rules; the Centre notified the Code on Wages and the Code on Social Security for central sphere establishments in 2022, while the remaining two await state‑level rule finalisation.

Code on Wages: defines “floor wage” to be fixed by the Centre after tripartite consultation; states may set higher minimum wages. It mandates timely payment (within 7 days of wage period), prohibits gender‑based wage discrimination, and introduces a “wage ceiling” for bonus eligibility (₹21,000/month). The “National Floor Level Minimum Wage” (NFLMW) was revised to ₹178/day in 2022, but states retain discretion, leading to wide inter‑state dispersion (₹160–₹423/day).

Industrial Relations Code: raises the threshold for standing orders from 100 to 300 workers, easing hiring‑firing flexibility for medium firms. It introduces a “fixed‑term employment” category with pro‑rata benefits, and a “re‑skilling fund” financed by employer contributions (0.5 % of wages) for retrenched workers. The code also mandates a 60‑day notice period for strikes/lockouts in public utility services and establishes a two‑tier adjudication mechanism (Industrial Tribunal and National Industrial Tribunal).

Code on Social Security: extends Employees’ Provident Fund (EPF) and Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) to gig and platform workers via a “Social Security Fund” financed by aggregator contributions (1–2 % of transaction value). It provides for maternity benefit (26 weeks), gratuity (after 5 years continuous service) and a “National Career Service” portal for job matching. The “Unorganised Workers’ Social Security Act, 2008” is subsumed, with the Centre bearing 75 % of the fund for the first three years.

Implementation challenges: (i) state‑level rule divergence creates compliance complexity for multi‑state firms; (ii) the “floor wage” lacks a binding enforcement mechanism, leaving de‑facto minima to market forces; (iii) gig‑worker registration on the “e‑Shram” portal reached around 28 crore by 2023, but portability of benefits across platforms remains untested; (iv) the “fixed‑term” provision raises concerns about job security and unionisation rights. The 2023‑24 Economic Survey noted that formal‑sector employment share rose from 9.7 % (2017‑18) to 11.2 % (2022‑23), attributing part of the gain to code‑induced formalisation.

Energy Sector Reforms: Power Distribution, Renewable Integration and Tariff Rationalisation

The Ujwal DISCOM Assurance Yojana (UDAY), launched in 2015, restructured around ₹2.5 lakh crore of state‑owned distribution company (DISCOM) debt by transferring 75 % to state governments via bonds, conditional on operational reforms (smart metering, feeder separation, AT&C loss reduction). By 2022, aggregate AT&C losses fell from 23.7 % (2015‑16) to 17.3 %, but revenue gaps persisted due to inadequate tariff revisions. The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS), approved in 2021 with a ₹3.03 lakh crore outlay (2021‑26), links central assistance to pre‑paid smart metering (targeting 25 crore meters by 2025), loss‑reduction trajectories and financial turnaround plans.

Renewable integration: India’s installed renewable capacity reached around 180 GW (including approximately 70 GW solar, 45 GW wind) by March 2024, targeting 500 GW non‑fossil capacity by 2030. The “Green Energy Corridor” (GEC) phases I and II, with around ₹12,000 crore central funding, strengthen inter‑state transmission for renewable evacuation. The “Renewable Purchase Obligation” (RPO) trajectory mandates 21 % solar and 10 % non‑solar RPO for 2023‑24, with penalties for non‑compliance. The “Deviation Settlement Mechanism” (DSM) and “Ancillary Services” regulations (2022) enable frequency regulation and ramping support from battery storage and hydro.

Tariff rationalisation: The Tariff Policy, 2016 (amended 2022) prescribes cost‑reflective tariffs, gradual elimination of cross‑subsidies (±20 % of average cost of supply), and time‑of‑day (ToD) tariffs for commercial/industrial consumers. The “Open Access” regulations (2020) allow consumers with ≥1 MW load to procure power directly from generators, subject to wheeling charges and cross‑subsidy surcharge. As of 2023, around 18 states have notified ToD tariffs; however, political resistance to agricultural subsidy removal keeps the cross‑subsidy burden high (estimated at ₹1.2 lakh crore annually).

Emerging issues: (i) “Stranded assets” risk for coal‑based plants as plant load factors fall below 55 %; (ii) “Grid stability” with high variable renewable share — the “National Electricity Plan” (2023) projects 30 % storage capacity (around 120 GW) by 2030; (iii) “Hydrogen Mission” (2023) allocates ₹19,744 crore for green hydrogen hubs, linking power sector decarbonisation to industrial demand. The 2024‑25 budget announced a “Power Finance Corporation” (PFC) green bond issuance of ₹10,000 crore to fund RDSS and storage projects.

Urban Infrastructure Financing: Municipal Bonds, Smart Cities Mission and Land Value Capture

India’s urban local bodies (ULBs) face an estimated infrastructure investment gap of ₹4.5 lakh crore (2020‑30). Municipal bonds have emerged as a market‑based financing tool; the first credit‑rated municipal bond was issued by Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation in 1998. By March 2024, around 12 ULBs had raised approximately ₹12,500 crore through bonds, with credit ratings ranging from AA‑ to A+. The “Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation” (AMRUT) and the “Smart Cities Mission” (SCM, launched 2015) provide central grants (₹48,000 crore for SCM) contingent on reform milestones — double‑entry accounting, GIS‑based property tax, and credit‑rating acquisition.

Smart Cities Mission: 100 cities selected through a competitive challenge; each receives ₹500 crore central assistance over five years, matched by state/ULB funds. As of December 2023, over 5,000 projects worth around ₹1.1 lakh crore were grounded, with over 3,800 completed. Core components include integrated command‑and‑control centres, intelligent traffic management, and public‑private partnership (PPP) models for waste‑to‑energy and affordable housing. The “City Investment Plan” (CIP) framework aligns project pipelines with credit‑rating improvement, enabling bond issuance.

Land Value Capture (LVC) mechanisms: (i) “Betterment Levy” — levied on land value increment due to public infrastructure (e.g., metro corridors); (ii) “Transfer of Development Rights” (TDR) — allows landowners to sell unused floor‑area‑ratio (FAR) to developers in designated receiving zones; (iii) “Impact Fees” — one‑time charges on new developments for trunk infrastructure. The “National Urban Policy Framework” (2021) recommends a uniform LVC legislation; Maharashtra and Gujarat have enacted TDR policies, while Delhi Metro Rail Corporation uses “Station Area Development” contracts capturing land value via commercial development rights.

PPP models: The “Viability Gap Funding (VGF) is used for metro, bus rapid transit and water supply projects; the Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM) is primarily used for highway projects. The “Infrastructure Investment Trust” (InvIT) structure, regulated by SEBI (2014), enables monetisation of operational assets (e.g., toll roads, transmission lines) to recycle capital. The 2023‑24 budget announced a “Urban Infrastructure Development Fund” (UIDF) of ₹10,000 crore, managed by the National Housing Bank (NHB), to provide long‑tenure debt to ULBs with investment‑grade ratings.

Key constraints: (i) weak own‑source revenue — property tax collection efficiency averages around 45 %; (ii) limited credit‑rating coverage — only around 30 % of ULBs rated; (iii) regulatory delays in LVC enactment; (iv) capacity gaps in project preparation and financial management. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a “Performance‑Based Grant” linking 25 % of ULB grants to revenue‑mobilisation and service‑delivery benchmarks, aiming to incentivise fiscal discipline.

  • Confusing the FRBM debt‑to‑GDP target (40 % for Centre) with the 15th Finance Commission’s consolidated state target (20 %) — they apply to different government tiers.
  • Treating UPI transaction volume as a proxy for credit inclusion — volume reflects payment activity, not access to formal credit or savings products.
  • Assuming the four labour codes are uniformly enforced across all states — rule‑making is a state subject, and implementation timelines differ widely.
  • Equating the Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) compliance penalty with a carbon price — RPO penalties are regulatory fines, not market‑based carbon pricing instruments.
  • Believing municipal bond issuance is open to all ULBs — only ULBs with investment‑grade credit ratings and audited accounts can access the bond market.
  • Overlooking that the “floor wage” under the Code on Wages is advisory — states may set higher minima, but the Centre cannot enforce a uniform national minimum.
  • These traps highlight the need for precise conceptual clarity when linking policy design to measurable outcomes.
