Psychology Optional — Overview & Syllabus Map

Psychology is a popular optional for psychology, sociology, and humanities graduates. Paper I covers foundations of psychology, cognitive processes, human development, personality, motivation, emotion, intelligence. Paper II covers social psychology, abnormal psychology, counseling psychology, organizational psychology, applied psychology.

Paper I — Foundations of Psychology

  • Foundations of Psychology: Definition, nature, scope, goals of psychology (description, explanation, prediction, control), schools of psychology (structuralism - Wundt, functionalism - James, psychoanalysis - Freud, behaviorism - Watson, humanistic - Maslow & Rogers, cognitive, evolutionary, positive psychology - Seligman), schools of thought, research methods (experimental, correlational, survey, case study, observational, ethnographic), ethical considerations in psychological research.
  • Sensory Processes: Sensation & perception, absolute threshold & difference threshold (Weber's law), signal detection theory, subliminal perception, visual system (eye structure, visual pathways, color vision - trichromatic & opponent-process theories, form perception, depth perception - binocular & monocular cues, perceptual constancies - size, shape, brightness, color constancy), auditory system (ear structure, pitch & loudness perception), other senses (olfaction, gustation, touch, kinesthesis, vestibular sense), attention (selective, divided, sustained), inattentional blindness, change blindness.
  • Perception: Perceptual processes (figure-ground, grouping principles - Gestalt principles: proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, common fate, simplicity), depth perception (monocular cues - relative size, interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, motion parallax, aerial perspective; binocular cues - retinal disparity, convergence), perceptual constancies (size, shape, color, brightness, shape constancy), illusions (Muller-Lyer, Ponzo, Ames room, Necker cube, Rubin's vase), extrasensory perception (ESP - claims & evidence).
  • Learning: Classical conditioning (Pavlov - acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination), operant conditioning (Skinner - reinforcement & punishment schedules - fixed ratio, variable ratio, fixed interval, variable interval, shaping, chaining, primary & secondary reinforcers), observational learning (Bandura - observational learning, mirror neurons), cognitive learning (cognitive maps - Tolman, insight learning - Kohler), biological constraints on learning (Garcia effect - taste aversion).
  • Memory: Memory models (sensory memory - iconic & echoic, short-term/working memory - Baddeley's model: phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer, central executive; long-term memory - declarative/explicit - episodic & semantic, procedural/implicit), levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart), forgetting (forgetting curve - Ebbinghaus, interference - proactive & retroactive, decay, repression), memory improvement techniques (mnemonics, spacing effect, retrieval practice, elaborative rehearsal), memory distortions & false memories.
  • Thinking & Problem Solving: Thinking (concepts & concepts formation, prototypes, mental sets, functional fixedness), problem solving (algorithms, heuristics - availability, representativeness, anchoring, insight, means-end analysis, working backward), decision making (bounded rationality - Simon, heuristics & biases - Kahneman & Tversky - availability heuristic, representativeness heuristic, anchoring & adjustment heuristic, framing effect, loss aversion), creativity (divergent vs convergent thinking, creativity tests, stages of creative process).
  • Thinking & Language: Language development (babbling, holophrastic stage, telegraphic speech, overregularization), language acquisition theories (Chomsky's LAD - language acquisition device, Skinner's behaviorist view, social interactionist theory - Vygotsky), bilingualism, language & thought (linguistic relativity - Sapir-Whorf hypothesis), artificial intelligence & cognitive science.

Paper II — Social Psychology, Abnormal Psychology, Applied Psychology

  • Social Psychology: Social cognition (schemas, stereotypes, prejudice, attribution theory - internal & external attribution, fundamental attribution error, self-serving bias, actor-observer bias), attitudes (attitude formation, attitude change - cognitive dissonance - Festinger, elaboration likelihood model - central & peripheral routes), social influence (conformity - Asch, compliance - foot-in-door, door-in-face, low-ball, obedience - Milgram, Zimbardo's prison study), group behavior (social facilitation, social loafing, deindividuation, group polarization, groupthink), leadership (trait theory, behavioral theory, contingency theory, transformational & transactional leadership), prosocial behavior (altruism, bystander effect - Darley & Latane, diffusion of responsibility, empathy - empathy-altruism hypothesis), aggression (biological, social learning, frustration-aggression hypothesis), interpersonal attraction (propinquity, similarity, physical attractiveness, reciprocity), love (Sternberg's triangular theory of love - intimacy, passion, commitment).
  • Personality: Trait theories (Allport, Cattell - 16PF, Eysenck - PEN, Big Five - OCEAN: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism), psychodynamic theory (Freud - psychosexual stages, id/ego/superego, defense mechanisms - repression, repression, projection, displacement, reaction formation, regression, sublimation, denial, rationalization, reaction formation), humanistic theory (Maslow - hierarchy of needs, Rogers - unconditional positive regard, self-actualization), trait approach (Big Five), social-cognitive theory (Bandura - self-efficacy, reciprocal determinism, observational learning), biological & evolutionary approaches (heritability of personality traits, temperament - Chess & Thomas, evolutionary psychology - evolutionary psychology of personality).
  • Human Development: Prenatal development (germinal, embryonic, fetal stages), infancy & childhood (Piaget's cognitive development stages - sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational; Erikson's psychosocial stages - trust vs mistrust, autonomy vs shame, initiative vs guilt, industry vs inferiority, identity vs role confusion, intimacy vs isolation, generativity vs stagnation, integrity vs despair; Kohlberg's moral development stages - preconventional, conventional, postconventional; attachment theory - Bowlby & Ainsworth - secure, avoidant, resistant, disorganized attachment), adolescence (adolescent development - identity vs role confusion, peer relationships, risk-taking behavior, adolescent pregnancy, eating disorders), adulthood (Erikson's stages - intimacy vs isolation, generativity vs stagnation, integrity vs despair), aging (successful aging - Rowe & Kub, successful aging models, successful aging & successful aging theories, wisdom, death & dying - Kubler-Ross stages - denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance).
Technique: Classical Conditioning
Question: 'Explain Pavlov's classical conditioning with an example.'
Answer: Classical conditioning: A neutral stimulus (bell) is paired with an unconditioned stimulus (food) that naturally elicits an unconditioned response (salivation). After repeated pairings, the neutral stimulus (bell) becomes a conditioned stimulus that elicits a conditioned response (salivation). Example: Pavlov's dogs - bell rings → food presented → salivation; after pairings, bell alone → salivation. Components: NS (bell) → CS, UCS (food) → UCR (salivation), CS (bell) → CR (salivation). Acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination.

Memory Tricks

  • Piaget's stages: Sensorimotor (0-2), Preoperational (2-7), Concrete operational (7-11), Formal operational (11+) = "Sensorimotor, Preoperational, Concrete, Formal = SPCC" → "Some People Can Fly" (Sensorimotor, Preoperational, Concrete, Formal).
  • Erikson's stages: Trust vs mistrust, Autonomy vs shame, Initiative vs guilt, Industry vs inferiority, Identity vs role confusion, Intimacy vs isolation, Generativity vs stagnation, Integrity vs despair = "Trust, Autonomy, Initiative, Industry, Identity, Intimacy, Generativity, Integrity" = "T A I I I I G I".
  • Big Five: OCEAN - Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism = "CANOE".
  • Kohlberg's stages: Preconventional (punishment & reward), Conventional (good boy/good girl, law & order), Postconventional (social contract, universal principles).
Mains strategy
Psychology answers: theory → theorist → experiment/study → application → criticism → conclusion. Draw diagrams (Maslow's pyramid, Piaget's stages, Erikson's stages, brain structures - limbic system, brain lobes, neuron structure). Quote theories & theorists (Freud, Erikson, Piaget, Kohlberg, Maslow, Rogers, Bandura, Pavlov, Watson, Skinner, Bandura, Kohlberg, Gilligan, Sternberg, Gardner, Goleman). Data: India's mental health statistics (NMHS 2015-16 - 10.6% mental disorders, treatment gap ~90%), suicide rates, substance abuse statistics, mental health infrastructure (NMHP - National Mental Health Programme, NMHP - District Mental Health Programme).

Archaeological Psychology: The Pashupati Seal and Early Rituals

The study of early human behavior relies heavily on material evidence where textual records are absent. A pivotal artifact in this domain is the Pashupati Seal discovered at Mohenjo-daro (c. 2500 BCE). While often popularly associated with Shiva, archaeological and iconographic analysis suggests a more complex interpretation relevant to social psychology and early religious thought. The seal depicts a seated figure surrounded by animals—tigers, lions, elephants, rhinos—and flanked by two human attendants. Crucially, the figure is not merely worshipping a deity but appears to be presiding over a ritual or a gathering of beasts.

Psychological Implications: This artifact challenges the "anthropocentric" view of early religion. It suggests that early psychological frameworks (c. 2500 BCE) integrated nature, divinity, and human hierarchy into a single cognitive schema. The figure's seated posture implies authority derived from control over nature rather than conquest. In social psychology terms, this represents an early form of collective consciousness where the leader is validated by their relationship with the natural world (animals) rather than just other humans. For UPSC answers, one must avoid stating "This proves Shiva existed" without qualification; instead, frame it as evidence of a proto-shamanic or nature-worshipping cult that pre-dates codified Vedic orthodoxy. The seal demonstrates that early Indian psychology was not abstract but deeply embedded in ecological relationships.

The Cognitive Revolution: From Tool Use to Symbolic Thought

Understanding human development requires distinguishing between simple tool use and the cognitive leap toward symbolic thought, a transition often linked to the Upper Paleolithic revolution (c. 40,000 BCE). Before this era, hominids used tools for immediate survival (pebble tools). The shift to figurines, cave paintings (Lascaux), and burial rites indicates the emergence of symbolic cognition. This is a critical concept in cognitive psychology: the ability to represent abstract concepts (death, spirits, future events) mentally.

Key Distinction: Simple association (bell → food) is distinct from symbolic representation (a cross representing salvation, a skull representing death). The Upper Paleolithic evidence suggests that once humans developed language and symbolic thought, they could engage in counterfactual reasoning ("what if I die?"). This cognitive shift laid the groundwork for religion, art, and complex social stratification. In exam contexts, linking this to Vedic literature requires care: the Vedas (c. 1500 BCE) represent a highly developed stage of symbolic thought, but the *origin* lies in these earlier archaeological strata. Do not conflate the two; acknowledge the continuity of cognitive evolution.

Examiner's Trap: The "Proven" Origin of Concepts
Question: "The Pashupati Seal proves that Shiva was worshipped 5000 years ago." Answer: Incorrect. The seal shows a figure with animal attributes and attendants, which *suggests* a nature-worshipping cult or proto-Shivaite imagery. However, without contemporary textual corroboration (like the Vedas or Puranas), we cannot definitively name the deity. Correct phrasing: "The seal provides material evidence of a ritualistic context involving animal worship and a central seated figure, indicative of early religious psychology predating codified Vedic theology."
Full Worked Answer: The Pashupati Seal (Mohenjo-daro, c. 2500 BCE) depicts a seated figure surrounded by animals and flanked by attendants. This iconography suggests a ritualistic context where the central figure presides over nature or beasts. While popularly identified with Shiva, archaeological caution dictates that this represents a "proto-Shivaite" or nature-worshipping cult rather than the established Hindu deity of later texts. Psychologically, it indicates an early cognitive framework integrating human authority with ecological harmony, challenging purely anthropocentric models of power. This artifact serves as primary evidence for studying the evolution of religious psychology in ancient India.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling "Firsts" and "Oldests"
Never claim a specific date or event as the absolute "first" unless backed by a consensus source (e.g., "The oldest known cave painting is in Sulawesi, dated to 40,000 BP"). If asked about the "oldest religion," do not name a specific god; instead, state: "Archaeological evidence points to animism and nature worship as the earliest forms of religious psychology, evidenced by artifacts like the Pashupati Seal. Specific deities are later developments." This demonstrates factual discipline.

Freud's Structural Model: Beyond the Id, Ego, Superego

Sigmund Freud's structural model of personality (Id, Ego, Superego) is a cornerstone of abnormal psychology but requires nuanced understanding beyond rote memorization. The Id operates on the pleasure principle (immediate gratification), the Ego on the reality principle (mediating between id and external world), and the Superego on moral standards. A common error is treating these as static entities; they are dynamic systems in constant flux.

Critical Nuance: The Superego is not just "conscience"; it includes the ego-ideal (what one *should* be) and internalized parental authority. In clinical cases, an overactive Superego leads to guilt and depression, while an underdeveloped Superego leads to antisocial behavior. For UPSC answers, link this to modern contexts: "The tension between the Id's desire for instant gratification (e.g., viral trends, impulsive spending) and the Superego's moral constraints mirrors contemporary societal conflicts." This connects classical theory to current affairs without inventing data.

Jung's Collective Unconscious vs. Freud's Personal Unconscious

C.G. Jung expanded on Freud by proposing the collective unconscious, a reservoir of universal experiences and archetypes shared across humanity, distinct from the personal unconscious (repressed memories). Key archetypes include the Shadow (repressed dark side), Anima/Animus (contrasexual psyche), and the Self (wholeness).

Research Methods and Statistics: Experimental Design, Reliability, and Ethical Safeguards

Paper I devotes a substantial portion to research methodology, and examiners expect candidates to move beyond textbook definitions into applied critique. A true experiment requires random assignment, manipulation of an independent variable, and control of confounding factors; quasi‑experiments lack randomisation but retain manipulation, while correlational designs only measure covariation. Internal validity threats — history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression toward the mean, selection, and attrition — must be identified and mitigated through designs such as Solomon four‑group or Latin square. External validity hinges on ecological validity, population validity, and temporal stability; replication across settings strengthens generalisability.

Reliability is quantified through test‑retest (temporal stability), parallel‑forms (equivalence), inter‑rater (observer agreement), and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α, split‑half). A coefficient ≥ 0.80 is generally acceptable for research instruments; clinical tools often demand ≥ 0.90. Validity sub‑types — content, criterion (concurrent and predictive), construct (convergent and discriminant) — are evaluated via factor analysis, multitrait‑multimethod matrices, and known‑groups comparisons. Statistical power analysis (α = 0.05, 1‑β = 0.80) determines minimum sample size; effect‑size conventions (Cohen’s d = 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) guide interpretation.

Ethical safeguards follow the APA Ethics Code (2017) and the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines (2017). Informed consent must be voluntary, comprehended, and documented; deception is permissible only when justified by scientific value and followed by debriefing. Risk‑benefit analysis, confidentiality (data anonymisation, secure storage), and the right to withdraw without penalty are non‑negotiable. Institutional Ethics Committees (IECs) review protocols; for multi‑centre studies, a central IEC may coordinate. Violation examples — such as the Stanford prison experiment (1971) and the Milgram obedience studies (1963) — are frequently cited to illustrate ethical breaches and the subsequent tightening of oversight.

Psychological Test Construction: Standardization, Norming, and Indian Adaptations

Developing a psychometrically sound test follows a systematic cycle: (1) construct definition and domain specification; (2) item generation by subject‑matter experts; (3) pilot testing on a heterogeneous sample (N ≥ 200) to compute item‑total correlations, difficulty indices, and discrimination indices; (4) item reduction using factor analysis (principal axis factoring with oblique rotation) to retain items loading ≥ 0.40 on the target factor and < 0.30 on others; (5) reliability estimation (Cronbach’s α, test‑retest over 2‑4 weeks); (6) validity evidence — content validity via Lawshe’s CVR, criterion validity against established measures, construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis (CFI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06); (7) norm development on a representative stratification (age, gender, region, education) with at least 1,000 participants for national norms; (8) publication of a technical manual detailing administration, scoring, interpretation, and psychometric properties.

In the Indian context, direct translation is insufficient; cultural adaptation follows the International Test Commission (ITC) guidelines: forward‑backward translation, expert committee review, cognitive interviewing, and differential item functioning (DIF) analysis using Mantel‑Haenszel or IRT‑based methods. Notable adaptations include the Hindi version of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale‑IV (WAIS‑IV), the Marathi adaptation of the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory‑2 (MMPI‑2), and the Tamil version of the Beck Depression Inventory‑II (BDI‑II). Each adaptation reports separate normative tables, reliability coefficients (typically α = 0.85‑0.92), and validity correlations with local criterion measures (e.g., clinical diagnosis, academic performance). The National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) and the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) maintain registries of approved tests for educational and clinical use.

Item‑Writing Technique
A researcher constructs a 30‑item Likert scale to measure “academic self‑efficacy” among undergraduate students. During pilot testing, Item 12 shows an item‑total correlation of 0.18 and a difficulty index of 0.92. Explain the psychometric implications and the decision the researcher should take.
Item 12’s low item‑total correlation (0.18 < 0.30) indicates it does not cohere with the overall scale, suggesting it measures a different construct or is poorly worded. The high difficulty index (0.92) means 92 % of respondents endorsed the highest response category, producing a ceiling effect and restricting variance. Both statistics signal that Item 12 contributes little discriminative information and inflates measurement error. The researcher should delete Item 12, re‑run the factor analysis, and recompute reliability; if α improves (e.g., from 0.84 to 0.88), the deletion is justified. Retaining such an item would weaken construct validity and reduce the scale’s sensitivity to detect differences in academic self‑efficacy.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “steps in test construction”, list the eight‑stage cycle verbatim and cite the ITC adaptation guidelines for Indian tests. For numerical items, memorise the conventional cut‑offs: α ≥ 0.80, item‑total ≥ 0.30, factor loading ≥ 0.40, CFI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06. Quote the specific Indian adaptations (WAIS‑IV Hindi, MMPI‑2 Marathi, BDI‑II Tamil) to demonstrate applied knowledge.

Health Psychology: Stress Appraisal Models, Coping Mechanisms, and Intervention Planning

Health psychology integrates biological, psychological, and social determinants of health. The transactional model of stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) posits primary appraisal (threat, challenge, harm/loss) and secondary appraisal (coping resources, options). Coping is classified as problem‑focused (direct action, planning, information seeking) and emotion‑focused (reappraisal, denial, venting, seeking emotional support). The model predicts that controllable stressors elicit problem‑focused coping, whereas uncontrollable stressors trigger emotion‑focused strategies. Empirical meta‑analyses (e.g., Penley et al., 2002) confirm that problem‑focused coping correlates with better physical health outcomes (r ≈ 0.25), while maladaptive emotion‑focused coping (self‑blame, substance use) predicts higher morbidity.

Selye’s General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) describes three physiological stages: alarm (sympathetic activation, cortisol surge), resistance (adaptation, sustained cortisol), and exhaustion (resource depletion, immune suppression). Chronic stress maintains the resistance stage, leading to allostatic load — cumulative wear‑and‑tear indexed by cardiovascular reactivity, metabolic dysregulation, and hippocampal atrophy. The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) frames these pathways within social determinants: low socioeconomic status, discrimination, and low social support amplify allostatic load.

Evidence‑based interventions follow a stepped‑care framework: (1) psychoeducation on stress appraisal; (2) cognitive‑behavioural stress management (CBSM) — cognitive restructuring of threat appraisals, relaxation training (progressive muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing), and skills training (time management, assertiveness); (3) mindfulness‑based stress reduction (MBSR) — 8‑week group program showing effect sizes d = 0.55 for perceived stress reduction; (4) biofeedback (heart‑rate variability training) for physiological self‑regulation; (5) referral for pharmacotherapy when anxiety or depressive disorders co‑occur. Indian trials (e.g., Sharma et al., 2019, N = 210 corporate employees) demonstrated that a culturally adapted CBSM module incorporating yoga nidra reduced perceived stress scores by 22 % (p < 0.001) and improved WHO‑5 well‑being index by 15 %.

Organizational Behaviour: Motivation Theories, Leadership Dynamics, and Work‑Life Integration

Motivation theories are examined for their explanatory power and practical utility. Maslow’s hierarchy (1943) — physiological, safety, belongingness, esteem, self‑actualisation — remains a heuristic but lacks empirical support for strict sequential progression. Herzberg’s two‑factor theory (1959) distinguishes hygiene factors (salary, policy, supervision) that prevent dissatisfaction from motivators (achievement, recognition, growth) that foster satisfaction; meta‑analyses show modest correlations (r ≈ 0.30) between motivators and job performance. Self‑Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985) posits three innate needs — autonomy, competence, relatedness — and differentiates autonomous (intrinsic, identified) from controlled (external, introjected) motivation; longitudinal studies in Indian IT firms (N = 1,200) found autonomous motivation predicts innovative work behaviour (β = 0.42, p < 0.001).

Leadership research contrasts transactional (contingent reward, management‑by‑exception) with transformational leadership (idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration). The Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ‑5X) validates four transformational dimensions; Indian public‑sector data (Singh & Bhandarker, 2016) reveal transformational leadership correlates with organisational citizenship behaviour (r = 0.48) and reduces turnover intention (r = ‑0.35). Emerging models — authentic leadership (self‑awareness, relational transparency, balanced processing) and servant leadership (emphasis on follower growth) — are gaining traction in CSR‑driven organisations.

Work‑life integration supersedes the older “balance” metaphor. The Job Demands‑Resources (JD‑R) model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007) frames job demands (workload, emotional labour) and resources (autonomy, support, feedback) as predictors of burnout (exhaustion, cynicism) and engagement (vigour, dedication). Indian longitudinal surveys (N = 3,500, 2018‑2022) show that flexible work arrangements increase resource perception by 0.27 SD and reduce burnout by 0.19 SD. Policy interventions — mandatory “right to disconnect” after 7 pm, on‑site childcare, and structured mentorship — have demonstrated measurable improvements in employee well‑being indices (WHO‑5 ↑ 12 %).

Cross‑Cultural Psychology: Cultural Dimensions, Indigenous Concepts, and Comparative Methodology

Cross‑cultural psychology examines how cultural contexts shape basic psychological processes. Hofstede’s six dimensions — power distance, individualism‑collectivism, masculinity‑femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long‑term orientation, indulgence‑restraint — are derived from IBM employee surveys (1970s‑1990s) across 70 nations; India scores high on power distance (77), moderate on individualism (48), and high on long‑term orientation (51). Triandis (1995) refines individualism‑collectivism into horizontal (equality) and vertical (hierarchy) subtypes; Indian culture exemplifies vertical collectivism — strong in‑group loyalty coupled with acceptance of hierarchy.

Markus & Kitayama (1991) propose independent vs. interdependent self‑construals. The independent self (Western) defines identity through internal attributes; the interdependent self (East Asian, South Asian) defines identity through relationships and social roles. Empirical work using the Twenty Statements Test shows Indian respondents generate 68 % relational statements versus 32 % for U.S. samples. Indigenous concepts enrich this framework: “Atman” (true self) and “Dharma” (duty‑based moral order) in Hindu philosophy; “Ubuntu” (“I am because we are”) in Southern African thought; “Ren” (benevolence) and “Li” (ritual propriety) in Confucian tradition. These constructs are not mere translations but embody distinct motivational architectures — e.g., Dharma‑driven action aligns with identified regulation in SDT.

Methodologically, the emic‑etic distinction guides research design. Emic approaches develop culture‑specific instruments (e.g., the Indian Family Values Scale), while etic approaches apply universal measures (e.g., the Values Survey Module) after establishing measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar) via multi‑group confirmatory factor analysis. The International Test Commission (2018) recommends at least three cultural groups, sample sizes ≥ 300 per group, and reporting of ΔCFI < 0.01 and ΔRMSEA < 0.015 for invariance. Recent large‑scale projects — the World Values Survey (Wave 7, 2017‑2022) and the Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness (GLOBE) study — provide open datasets for secondary analysis, enabling candidates to cite cross‑national effect sizes in answers.

  • Attributing stress outcomes solely to personality while ignoring structural determinants (socio‑economic status, organisational climate).
  • Equating transformational leadership with charismatic leadership; the former includes intellectual stimulation and individualised consideration.
  • Using Hofstede’s country scores as deterministic predictors for individual behaviour without acknowledging within‑culture variance.
  • Neglecting measurement invariance testing when comparing groups cross‑culturally.
