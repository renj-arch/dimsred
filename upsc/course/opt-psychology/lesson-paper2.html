Technique: Cognitive Dissonance
Question: "Explain cognitive dissonance with an example."
Answer: Cognitive dissonance (Festinger) - psychological discomfort from holding conflicting cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, behaviors). Example: A smoker who knows smoking causes cancer experiences dissonance between "I smoke" & "smoking causes cancer." Resolution: change behavior (quit smoking), change belief (smoking isn't that bad), or add consonant cognition (my grandfather smoked & lived to 90). Dissonance reduction strategies: change behavior, change belief, add consonant cognition, trivialization, selective exposure to information.

Abnormal Psychology: The Diagnostic Landscape and Etiological Models

The classification of psychological disorders has undergone a radical transformation from the descriptive, often speculative nosologies of the 19th century to the operationalized criteria systems of today. The current paradigm relies heavily on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), currently in its fifth edition (DSM-5), which prioritizes reliability in diagnosis over strict etiological theories. This shift reflects a move towards dimensional models, acknowledging that psychopathology exists on a continuum rather than as discrete categories. A critical understanding of this landscape requires distinguishing between the medical model, which views disorders as diseases with biological causes (genetics, neurochemistry), and the psychosocial model, which emphasizes environmental stressors, learning history, and cognitive distortions. The tension between these models is evident in the debate over whether conditions like schizophrenia or depression are primarily genetic vulnerabilities triggered by environmental stressors (the diathesis-stress model) or solely products of social context.

Historically, the definition of abnormality relied on four criteria: deviance (statistical rarity or violation of norms), suffering (distress to the individual), dysfunction (impairment in daily functioning), and danger (risk to self or others). However, these criteria have significant limitations. Deviance is culturally relative; behaviors considered pathological in one society may be normative in another. Suffering is subjective and can be reported by individuals who are not actually impaired. The concept of the "medical model" itself has been criticized for reducing complex human experiences to biological malfunctions, often neglecting the role of trauma, poverty, and social injustice. Consequently, modern psychology integrates these perspectives, leading to biopsychosocial models that consider genetic predispositions, psychological processes, and social environments simultaneously.

A crucial distinction in this field is between normal personality traits and personality disorders. While high levels of neuroticism or low agreeableness are normal variations, these become pathological when they are rigid, pervasive, and maladaptive across time and situations. The DSM-5 introduced the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) to address this nuance, focusing on impairments in personality functioning (self and interpersonal) and pathological personality traits, moving away from the purely categorical approach of previous editions.

Examiner's trap
A common error is equating "abnormality" strictly with "illness." Correct answer: Abnormality encompasses a spectrum including statistical deviance, cultural non-conformity, and distress without necessarily implying a biological disease entity. Furthermore, the DSM-5 removed Asperger's Disorder as a distinct diagnosis, subsuming it under Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) to reflect the continuum of symptoms.
Exam-day strategy: The "Why" over the "What"
When answering questions on abnormal psychology, do not merely list symptoms. Always link the diagnosis to its theoretical underpinnings (e.g., explain how a phobia might be explained by classical conditioning vs. evolutionary preparedness). Use the biopsychosocial framework as a concluding synthesis to demonstrate comprehensive understanding.

Social Psychology: The Architecture of Group Behavior and Conformity

Group behavior is not merely the sum of individual behaviors but emerges from complex interactions governed by specific psychological mechanisms. Conformity, the adjustment of one's attitudes or behaviors to match group norms, is best illustrated by Solomon Asch's line judgment experiments, where participants conformed to incorrect majority opinions due to normative social influence (desire to fit in) and informational social influence (belief that the group knows better). However, conformity is not universal; it depends on factors such as group size (optimal at 3-5 members), unanimity (a dissenter drastically reduces conformity), and task difficulty. Groupthink, identified by Irving Janis, describes a mode of thinking where the desire for harmony or conformity in a group results in irrational or dysfunctional decision-making outcomes. This occurs in cohesive groups under stress where dissenting views are suppressed.

Another critical phenomenon is deindividuation, a state of reduced self-awareness and weakened adherence to personal values, often occurring in crowds or online environments. Stanley Milgram's obedience studies complement this by showing how ordinary individuals can commit harmful acts when instructed by an authority figure, highlighting the power of situational forces over dispositional traits (the fundamental attribution error). Social loafing, where individual effort decreases in a group setting, is explained by diffusion of responsibility and reduced identifiability. Conversely, social facilitation occurs when the presence of others enhances performance on simple or well-learned tasks but impairs it on complex ones (Zajonc's drive theory).

The dynamics of leadership also play a pivotal role. Transformational leadership, which inspires followers to transcend self-interest for the good of the group, contrasts with transactional leadership, based on exchanges of rewards and punishments. The bystander effect, where individuals are less likely to offer help in the presence of others, is attributed to diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance (assuming others know what to do). Understanding these mechanisms allows for interventions that reduce violence, increase cooperation, and improve organizational effectiveness.

Applied Psychology: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy and Evidence-Based Practice

Applied psychology bridges theoretical knowledge with practical intervention, most notably through Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). Developed from the work of Aaron Beck and Albert Ellis, CBT posits that emotional distress is not caused directly by events but by one's interpretations of those events (cognitive distortions). Techniques include cognitive restructuring (identifying and challenging automatic negative thoughts), behavioral activation, and exposure therapy for anxiety disorders. The efficacy of CBT is supported by a vast body of empirical evidence, making it the gold standard for treating depression, anxiety, PTSD, and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Industrial-Organizational (I-O) psychology applies psychological principles to workplace settings. Key areas include employee selection (using validity studies to predict job performance), training and development, organizational culture, and stress management. The concept of job satisfaction is often analyzed using the Job Demands-Resources model, which suggests that high demands without sufficient resources lead to burnout, whereas a balance promotes engagement. In health psychology, the focus is on behavior change (smoking cessation, diet, exercise) using motivational interviewing and self-efficacy theories (Bandura). These applications require rigorous evaluation of outcomes to ensure interventions are not only theoretically sound but practically effective.

Critical Analysis: Limitations of Current Paradigms and Future Directions

While the current frameworks in psychology are robust, they face significant criticisms. The DSM's categorical approach has been criticized for poor reliability across cultures and for pathologizing normal human variation (e.g., gender dysphoria debates). There is a growing movement towards precision psychiatry, which uses biomarkers and genetic data to tailor treatments, though this raises ethical concerns regarding privacy and determinism. Furthermore, the over-reliance on Western samples in research limits the generalizability of findings to non-Western populations, necessitating more cross-cultural studies. The rise of neuroimaging has led to a "neuro-essentialist" view that some disorders are purely brain-based, potentially neglecting social determinants of health.

The future of psychology lies in integrating these diverse perspectives. Digital mental health interventions (apps, teletherapy) offer scalable solutions but require validation for efficacy and safety. The integration of artificial intelligence in diagnosing and treating mental health conditions holds promise but also risks algorithmic bias if training data is not representative. Ethical considerations regarding the use of psychological data, informed consent in online settings, and the potential for manipulation via social media algorithms are becoming central to the curriculum. Psychology must remain a humanistic science that respects individual agency while acknowledging structural constraints.

A common error is attributing all group behavior to conformity.
