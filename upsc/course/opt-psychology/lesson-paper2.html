Technique: Cognitive Dissonance
Question: "Explain cognitive dissonance with an example."
Answer: Cognitive dissonance (Festinger) - psychological discomfort from holding conflicting cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, behaviors). Example: A smoker who knows smoking causes cancer experiences dissonance between "I smoke" & "smoking causes cancer." Resolution: change behavior (quit smoking), change belief (smoking isn't that bad), or add consonant cognition (my grandfather smoked & lived to 90). Dissonance reduction strategies: change behavior, change belief, add consonant cognition, trivialization, selective exposure to information.

Abnormal Psychology: The Diagnostic Landscape and Etiological Models

The classification of psychological disorders has undergone a radical transformation from the descriptive, often speculative nosologies of the 19th century to the operationalized criteria systems of today. The current paradigm relies heavily on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), currently in its fifth edition (DSM-5), which prioritizes reliability in diagnosis over strict etiological theories. This shift reflects a move towards dimensional models, acknowledging that psychopathology exists on a continuum rather than as discrete categories. A critical understanding of this landscape requires distinguishing between the medical model, which views disorders as diseases with biological causes (genetics, neurochemistry), and the psychosocial model, which emphasizes environmental stressors, learning history, and cognitive distortions. The tension between these models is evident in the debate over whether conditions like schizophrenia or depression are primarily genetic vulnerabilities triggered by environmental stressors (the diathesis-stress model) or solely products of social context.

Historically, the definition of abnormality relied on four criteria: deviance (statistical rarity or violation of norms), suffering (distress to the individual), dysfunction (impairment in daily functioning), and danger (risk to self or others). However, these criteria have significant limitations. Deviance is culturally relative; behaviors considered pathological in one society may be normative in another. Suffering is subjective and can be reported by individuals who are not actually impaired. The concept of the "medical model" itself has been criticized for reducing complex human experiences to biological malfunctions, often neglecting the role of trauma, poverty, and social injustice. Consequently, modern psychology integrates these perspectives, leading to biopsychosocial models that consider genetic predispositions, psychological processes, and social environments simultaneously.

A crucial distinction in this field is between normal personality traits and personality disorders. While high levels of neuroticism or low agreeableness are normal variations, these become pathological when they are rigid, pervasive, and maladaptive across time and situations. The DSM-5 introduced the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) to address this nuance, focusing on impairments in personality functioning (self and interpersonal) and pathological personality traits, moving away from the purely categorical approach of previous editions.

Examiner's trap
A common error is equating "abnormality" strictly with "illness." Correct answer: Abnormality encompasses a spectrum including statistical deviance, cultural non-conformity, and distress without necessarily implying a biological disease entity. Furthermore, the DSM-5 removed Asperger's Disorder as a distinct diagnosis, subsuming it under Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) to reflect the continuum of symptoms.
Exam-day strategy: The "Why" over the "What"
When answering questions on abnormal psychology, do not merely list symptoms. Always link the diagnosis to its theoretical underpinnings (e.g., explain how a phobia might be explained by classical conditioning vs. evolutionary preparedness). Use the biopsychosocial framework as a concluding synthesis to demonstrate comprehensive understanding.

Social Psychology: The Architecture of Group Behavior and Conformity

Group behavior is not merely the sum of individual behaviors but emerges from complex interactions governed by specific psychological mechanisms. Conformity, the adjustment of one's attitudes or behaviors to match group norms, is best illustrated by Solomon Asch's line judgment experiments, where participants conformed to incorrect majority opinions due to normative social influence (desire to fit in) and informational social influence (belief that the group knows better). However, conformity is not universal; it depends on factors such as group size (optimal at 3-5 members), unanimity (a dissenter drastically reduces conformity), and task difficulty. Groupthink, identified by Irving Janis, describes a mode of thinking where the desire for harmony or conformity in a group results in irrational or dysfunctional decision-making outcomes. This occurs in cohesive groups under stress where dissenting views are suppressed.

Another critical phenomenon is deindividuation, a state of reduced self-awareness and weakened adherence to personal values, often occurring in crowds or online environments. Stanley Milgram's obedience studies complement this by showing how ordinary individuals can commit harmful acts when instructed by an authority figure, highlighting the power of situational forces over dispositional traits (the fundamental attribution error). Social loafing, where individual effort decreases in a group setting, is explained by diffusion of responsibility and reduced identifiability. Conversely, social facilitation occurs when the presence of others enhances performance on simple or well-learned tasks but impairs it on complex ones (Zajonc's drive theory).

The dynamics of leadership also play a pivotal role. Transformational leadership, which inspires followers to transcend self-interest for the good of the group, contrasts with transactional leadership, based on exchanges of rewards and punishments. The bystander effect, where individuals are less likely to offer help in the presence of others, is attributed to diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance (assuming others know what to do). Understanding these mechanisms allows for interventions that reduce violence, increase cooperation, and improve organizational effectiveness.

Applied Psychology: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy and Evidence-Based Practice

Applied psychology bridges theoretical knowledge with practical intervention, most notably through Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). Developed from the work of Aaron Beck and Albert Ellis, CBT posits that emotional distress is not caused directly by events but by one's interpretations of those events (cognitive distortions). Techniques include cognitive restructuring (identifying and challenging automatic negative thoughts), behavioral activation, and exposure therapy for anxiety disorders. The efficacy of CBT is supported by a vast body of empirical evidence, making it the gold standard for treating depression, anxiety, PTSD, and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Industrial-Organizational (I-O) psychology applies psychological principles to workplace settings. Key areas include employee selection (using validity studies to predict job performance), training and development, organizational culture, and stress management. The concept of job satisfaction is often analyzed using the Job Demands-Resources model, which suggests that high demands without sufficient resources lead to burnout, whereas a balance promotes engagement. In health psychology, the focus is on behavior change (smoking cessation, diet, exercise) using motivational interviewing and self-efficacy theories (Bandura). These applications require rigorous evaluation of outcomes to ensure interventions are not only theoretically sound but practically effective.

Critical Analysis: Limitations of Current Paradigms and Future Directions

While the current frameworks in psychology are robust, they face significant criticisms. The DSM's categorical approach has been criticized for poor reliability across cultures and for pathologizing normal human variation (e.g., gender dysphoria debates). There is a growing movement towards precision psychiatry, which uses biomarkers and genetic data to tailor treatments, though this raises ethical concerns regarding privacy and determinism. Furthermore, the over-reliance on Western samples in research limits the generalizability of findings to non-Western populations, necessitating more cross-cultural studies. The rise of neuroimaging has led to a "neuro-essentialist" view that some disorders are purely brain-based, potentially neglecting social determinants of health.

The future of psychology lies in integrating these diverse perspectives. Digital mental health interventions (apps, teletherapy) offer scalable solutions but require validation for efficacy and safety. The integration of artificial intelligence in diagnosing and treating mental health conditions holds promise but also risks algorithmic bias if training data is not representative. Ethical considerations regarding the use of psychological data, informed consent in online settings, and the potential for manipulation via social media algorithms are becoming central to the curriculum. Psychology must remain a humanistic science that respects individual agency while acknowledging structural constraints.

A common error is attributing all group behavior to conformity.

Personality: Trait Theory, Assessment, and Cross‑Cultural Validity

The dominant framework for describing individual differences in personality is the Five‑Factor Model (FFM), often called the Big Five. The factors — openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism — emerged from lexical analyses across multiple languages and have been replicated in more than fifty cultures using instruments such as the NEO Personality Inventory‑Revised (NEO‑PI‑R) and the Big Five Inventory (BFI). Longitudinal studies show that mean levels of each trait are relatively stable after age thirty, though small normative shifts occur: conscientiousness and agreeableness tend to increase, while neuroticism and extraversion slightly decline across adulthood. Trait stability is indexed by test‑retest correlations typically exceeding 0.70 over intervals of several years, supporting the view that traits are enduring dispositions rather than transient states.

Assessment of personality relies primarily on self‑report questionnaires, which offer high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α often above 0.80 for each domain) and predictive validity for outcomes such as academic performance, job performance, and health behaviors. Observer ratings (peer, spouse, or supervisor) converge with self‑reports at correlations of 0.40–0.50, providing a useful multimethod check. Projective techniques such as the Rorschach Inkblot Test and the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) remain part of clinical tradition but demonstrate lower inter‑rater reliability and limited incremental validity over structured inventories; contemporary practice therefore treats them as supplementary rather than primary tools.

Cross‑cultural research has addressed the etic‑emic debate by testing both imposed‑etic (translated Western scales) and emic (indigenously derived) approaches. Factor analyses of translated NEO‑PI‑R data generally recover the five factors, though the exact facet structure can vary — for example, the openness factor sometimes splits into intellect and aesthetic appreciation in East Asian samples. Measurement invariance testing (configural, metric, scalar) is now standard before comparing latent means across groups. Findings indicate that while the broad factor structure is largely universal, mean‑level differences exist: collectivist cultures often report higher agreeableness and lower extraversion than individualist cultures. These differences are modest and must be interpreted alongside response‑style biases such as acquiescence and extreme responding.

Applications of trait theory extend to personnel selection, where conscientiousness predicts performance across occupations (validity coefficients around 0.20–0.30), and to clinical psychology, where high neuroticism and low conscientiousness constitute a vulnerability profile for internalizing disorders. The integration of trait assessment with situational judgment tests and cognitive ability measures yields incremental predictive power, illustrating the practical utility of a robust, cross‑culturally validated taxonomy.

Prejudice, Stereotyping, and Intergroup Conflict: Mechanisms and Interventions

Prejudice refers to an evaluative bias toward a group and its members, while stereotypes are cognitive schemas that attribute uniform characteristics to all members of a group. Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner) posits that individuals derive self‑esteem from group memberships, leading to in‑group favoritism and out‑group derogation even in minimal‑group paradigms where group assignment is arbitrary. Realistic Conflict Theory (Sherif) adds that competition over scarce resources intensifies intergroup hostility, as demonstrated in the classic Robbers Cave experiment where boys assigned to competing groups developed strong antagonism that was later reduced through superordinate goals requiring cooperation.

The Contact Hypothesis (Allport) specifies four necessary conditions for intergroup contact to reduce prejudice: equal status between groups within the situation, common goals that require joint effort, intergroup cooperation rather than competition, and institutional support (authority, law, or custom) that legitimizes the interaction. Meta‑analyses of over five hundred studies confirm that contact meeting these conditions yields a mean effect size of approximately d = 0.50 for prejudice reduction, with effects generalizing across racial, ethnic, religious, and sexual‑orientation groups. Extended contact — knowing that an in‑group member has a close out‑group friend — also produces measurable prejudice reduction, albeit smaller in magnitude.

Cognitive interventions target the automatic activation of stereotypes. Counter‑stereotypic imaging (visualizing out‑group members who disconfirm the stereotype) and perspective‑taking (imagining the out‑group member’s thoughts and feelings) have been shown in laboratory settings to weaken implicit bias as measured by the Implicit Association Test (IAT). However, the durability of these effects outside the lab remains debated; longitudinal field studies suggest that repeated practice and structural changes (e.g., diverse work teams, inclusive curricula) are required for lasting change.

At the societal level, policies that promote desegregation, affirmative action, and anti‑discrimination legislation create the institutional support condition of the contact hypothesis. Educational programs that incorporate cooperative learning (e.g., jigsaw classroom) operationalize common goals and intergroup cooperation, producing both academic gains and reduced prejudice among children. The convergence of social‑psychological theory, experimental evidence, and policy evaluation underscores that prejudice is not an immutable trait but a malleable outcome of identifiable social processes.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: Explain how the contact hypothesis reduces prejudice and specify the four necessary conditions for effective intergroup contact.
The contact hypothesis argues that direct interaction between members of different groups can diminish prejudice when the interaction meets four criteria. First, the groups must have equal status within the contact situation; status differentials reinforce stereotypes. Second, the groups must pursue common goals that cannot be achieved by either group alone, creating interdependence. Third, the interaction must involve intergroup cooperation rather than competition, fostering positive interdependence. Fourth, institutional support — such as endorsement by authorities, laws, or organizational policies — legitimizes the contact and signals normative approval. When these conditions are satisfied, contact promotes individuation (seeing out‑group members as individuals), disconfirms negative stereotypes, generates positive affect, and facilitates perspective‑taking, collectively reducing prejudice. Empirical meta‑analyses confirm that contact meeting all four conditions yields a moderate‑to‑large reduction in prejudice across diverse settings.
Exam‑Day Strategy
For applied social‑psychology questions, structure each answer in three layers: (1) name the theory and its core proposition, (2) cite the key empirical support (classic study or meta‑analysis), and (3) articulate the practical implication or intervention derived from the theory. This three‑part template ensures you address both the “what” and the “so what” that examiners reward.

Mood Disorders: Depressive and Bipolar Spectrum – Diagnosis, Etiology, and Treatment

The DSM‑5 defines a Major Depressive Episode by the presence of five or more symptoms during a two‑week period, with at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia. Additional symptoms include significant weight change, insomnia or hypersomnia, psychomotor agitation or retardation, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, diminished concentration, and recurrent thoughts of death. A Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) diagnosis requires one or more such episodes without a history of manic or hypomanic episodes. Persistent Depressive Disorder (dysthymia) involves a depressed mood for most of the day, for at least two years, with fewer symptom criteria but greater chronicity.

Bipolar I Disorder is diagnosed after at least one manic episode — a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood lasting at least one week (or any duration if hospitalization is required) accompanied by three or more manic symptoms (grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, increased goal‑directed activity, risky behavior). Bipolar II Disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode (similar criteria but lasting at least four days, not causing marked impairment) and at least one major depressive episode. Cyclothymic Disorder involves numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full episode criteria for at least two years.

Etiological models are biopsychosocial. Twin studies estimate heritability of MDD at roughly 40 % and of bipolar disorder at 60–80 %. Molecular genetics has identified multiple risk loci of small effect (polygenic risk scores). Neurobiologically, monoamine dysregulation (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine) underpins the monoamine hypothesis, while neuroimaging reveals reduced hippocampal volume and prefrontal hypoactivity in depression, and fronto‑striatal circuit abnormalities in mania. Psychologically, Beck’s cognitive triad (negative views of self, world, future) and hopelessness theory explain vulnerability; life‑stress models (diathesis‑stress, kindling) describe how early episodes sensitize the brain to subsequent stressors.

First‑line pharmacotherapy for MDD includes selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin‑norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), with response rates of 50–60 % and remission rates around 30–40 % after adequate trials. For bipolar disorder, mood stabilizers such as lithium (gold standard for prophylaxis, reduces suicide risk), valproate, and lamotrigine are preferred; antidepressants are used cautiously due to risk of mood switching. Evidence‑based psychotherapies — Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Interpersonal Therapy (IPT), and Behavioral Activation — achieve effect sizes comparable to medication for mild‑to‑moderate depression and enhance relapse prevention. Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) remains the most efficacious treatment for severe, treatment‑resistant, or psychotic depression, with remission rates exceeding 70 %. Integrated treatment algorithms (e.g., stepped care) combine pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, and lifestyle interventions (exercise, sleep hygiene) to optimize outcomes.

Industrial‑Organizational Psychology: Employee Selection, Training, and Organizational Development

Employee selection begins with a thorough job analysis yielding a competency model that distinguishes essential knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics (KSAOs). Criterion‑related validity is established by correlating predictor scores with job performance criteria; predictive validity designs (test administered before hire) are preferred over concurrent designs (test administered to incumbents) because they avoid restriction‑of‑range artifacts. Meta‑analyses (Schmidt & Hunter) report that general mental ability (GMA) tests have the highest validity for overall job performance (ρ ≈ 0.51), while structured interviews (ρ ≈ 0.51) and work‑sample tests (ρ ≈ 0.54) also demonstrate strong predictive power. Personality inventories, particularly conscientiousness (ρ ≈ 0.31) and emotional stability (ρ ≈ 0.24), add incremental validity beyond GMA.

Assessment centers combine multiple simulation exercises — in‑basket, leaderless group discussion, role‑play, case analysis — evaluated by trained assessors using behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS). They show high criterion‑related validity (ρ ≈ 0.37–0.45) for managerial potential and are valued for their face validity and developmental feedback. However, they are resource‑intensive and require rigorous assessor training to maintain inter‑rater reliability (target ICC > 0.80).

Training effectiveness is evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s four‑level model: Level 1 (reaction) measures trainee satisfaction; Level 2 (learning) assesses knowledge or skill acquisition via pre‑post tests; Level 3 (behavior) examines transfer of training to the job through supervisor ratings or observation; Level 4 (results) quantifies organizational outcomes such as productivity, error reduction, or sales growth. Meta‑analytic evidence indicates that training designs incorporating behavior modeling, practice with feedback, and overlearning produce the largest transfer effects (d ≈ 0.60–0.80).

Organizational Development (OD) applies planned change processes grounded in action research: diagnosis (data collection via surveys, interviews), feedback, joint planning of interventions, implementation, and evaluation. Classic OD interventions include team building, survey feedback, process consultation, and large‑group methods (e.g., Future Search). Lewin’s three‑step model (unfreeze, change, refreeze) and Kotter’s eight‑step framework (create urgency, build guiding coalition, form vision, communicate, empower action, generate short‑term wins, consolidate gains, anchor changes) provide complementary roadmaps. Success factors include leadership commitment, alignment with strategy, employee participation, and systematic measurement of change readiness and outcomes.

Health Psychology: Behavior Change Models and Community Interventions

Health psychology investigates how biological, psychological, and social factors influence health and illness. Central to behavior change are several theoretically grounded models. The Health Belief Model (HBM) posits that engagement in a health behavior depends on perceived susceptibility to a threat, perceived severity of consequences, perceived benefits of action, perceived barriers, cues to action (e.g., symptoms, media campaigns), and self‑efficacy (added later). The Transtheoretical Model (TTM) describes stages of change — precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance — and identifies processes of change (consciousness raising, self‑liberation, reinforcement management) that facilitate stage progression. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) asserts that intention, the proximal determinant of behavior, is shaped by attitude toward the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (which also directly predicts behavior when control is accurate). Self‑Determination Theory (SDT) emphasizes that autonomous motivation (intrinsic or integrated regulation) yields more persistent behavior change than controlled motivation (external or introjected regulation), and that satisfaction of basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence, relatedness — supports internalization.

Interventions derived from these models include motivational interviewing (MI), a client‑centered counseling style that resolves ambivalence and strengthens autonomous motivation; MI has demonstrated efficacy across smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, medication adherence, and physical activity (average effect size d ≈ 0.30–0.50). Tailored print or digital communications that match message content to an individual’s stage of change or belief profile outperform generic materials. Implementation intentions (“if‑then” plans) bridge the intention‑behavior gap by specifying situational cues and responses.

At the community level, participatory approaches such as Community‑Based Participatory Research (CBPR) engage community members as co‑researchers, ensuring cultural relevance and sustainability. Multi‑component programs — combining policy (e.g., tobacco taxation, sugary‑drink levies), environmental restructuring (e.g., safe walking paths, healthy food availability), and social marketing — produce population‑level shifts in risk behaviors. Evaluation frameworks (RE‑AIM: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) guide assessment of both individual outcomes and program penetration. Ethical considerations include equitable access, avoidance of victim‑blaming narratives, and protection of privacy in digital health tools.

Psychological Testing: Classical Test Theory, Item Response Theory, and Computerized Adaptive Testing

Classical Test Theory (CTT)

Classical Test Theory, formalized by Spearman (1904) and later refined by Gulliksen (1950), models an observed score (X) as the sum of a true score (T) and random error (E): X = T + E. The theory assumes that errors are normally distributed, uncorrelated with true scores, and have a mean of zero. Reliability in CTT is defined as the ratio of true‑score variance to observed‑score variance, commonly estimated through internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), test‑retest, parallel‑forms, or inter‑rater methods. A reliability coefficient of 0.80 is typically the minimum for group‑level decisions, while 0.90 or higher is required for high‑stakes individual decisions such as clinical diagnosis or personnel selection.

Validity in the CTT Framework

Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretation of test scores for a proposed use. The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (AERA, APA, NCME, 2014) organize validity evidence into five sources: test content, response processes, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences of testing. Construct validity, the overarching concept, is supported by convergent and discriminant correlations, factor‑analytic studies, and known‑groups comparisons. Criterion‑related validity is quantified by the validity coefficient (rxy), which is attenuated by the reliability of both predictor and criterion; correction for attenuation (rxy / √(rxx·ryy)) yields the estimated true correlation.

Item Response Theory (IRT)

IRT overcomes CTT’s sample‑dependence by modeling the probability of a correct response as a function of a latent trait (θ) and item parameters. The one‑parameter logistic model (1PL, Rasch model) includes only item difficulty (b): P(θ) = 1 / (1 + e−(θ−b)). The two‑parameter model (2PL) adds discrimination (a): P(θ) = 1 / (1 + e−a(θ−b)). The three‑parameter model (3PL) incorporates a guessing parameter (c) for multiple‑choice items: P(θ) = c + (1−c) / (1 + e−a(θ−b)). IRT provides item information functions, allowing test developers to maximize measurement precision at targeted trait levels. Model fit is evaluated via χ² statistics, item‑fit indices (infit/outfit mean squares), and graphical residual analysis.

Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT)

CAT administers items sequentially, selecting each next item to maximize Fisher information at the current ability estimate (θ̂). The algorithm typically follows: (1) start with a moderate‑difficulty item, (2) estimate θ̂ using maximum likelihood or Bayesian (EAP) methods, (3) choose the unused item with highest information at θ̂, (4) repeat until a stopping rule is met (e.g., standard error ≤ 0.30, fixed item count, or time limit). CAT reduces test length by 30‑50 % while maintaining or improving precision. Operational CAT programs include the GRE (since 2011), the NCLEX‑RN, and the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery (ASVAB). Security concerns such as item exposure are managed through exposure control methods (e.g., Sympson‑Hetter, conditional maximum information).

Practical Implications for Examinees

Understanding that CAT adapts to performance means that early items heavily influence the trajectory; therefore, careful attention to initial items is strategic. For paper‑and‑pencil tests, knowledge of reliability thresholds helps evaluate whether a reported score is sufficiently precise for the intended decision. In research, reporting both CTT and IRT indices (e.g., α, item‑fit, test information curves) is now considered best practice.

Cognitive Biases and Heuristics: Dual‑Process Theory, Prospect Theory, and Real‑World Decision Making

Dual‑Process Models

Kahneman (2011) popularized the distinction between System 1 (fast, automatic, associative) and System 2 (slow, effortful, rule‑based). System 1 generates intuitive judgments using heuristics; System 2 can override them but is resource‑limited and often lazy. The “cognitive miser” metaphor explains why biases persist even among experts. Neuroimaging studies (e.g., De Neys, 2012) show anterior cingulate activation during conflict detection between the two systems.

Core Heuristics and Resulting Biases

  • Availability heuristic – judgments of frequency or probability are based on ease of retrieval; leads to overestimation of vivid or recent events (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973).
  • Representativeness heuristic – probability assessed by similarity to a prototype; produces base‑rate neglect and the conjunction fallacy (Linda problem, 1983).
  • Anchoring and adjustment – initial numeric values (anchors) bias subsequent estimates, even when anchors are arbitrary (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).
  • Affect heuristic – emotional reactions substitute for analytical risk appraisal (Slovic et al., 2002).

Prospect Theory

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) introduced Prospect Theory as a descriptive alternative to Expected Utility Theory. Its value function is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses (loss aversion, λ ≈ 2.25). The weighting function overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate‑to‑high probabilities, producing the “certainty effect” and “possibility effect.” The 1992 cumulative prospect theory extension handles multiple outcomes and rank‑dependent weighting. These principles explain framing effects (e.g., Asian disease problem), the disposition effect in finance, and insurance purchasing behavior.

Applications and Policy Relevance

Behavioral economics translates these findings into “nudges” (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008): default enrollment in retirement plans exploits status‑quo bias; calorie labeling leverages salience; opt‑out organ donation increases consent rates. In clinical settings, awareness of anchoring reduces diagnostic momentum; in legal contexts, debiasing instructions aim to mitigate hindsight bias among jurors.

Examiner's Trap: Conflating Heuristics with Biases
A question asks: "The representativeness heuristic leads to the base‑rate fallacy. Explain the mechanism and give a concrete example." Many candidates merely define the heuristic without linking it to the neglect of prior probabilities.
The representativeness heuristic evaluates the probability of an event by the degree to which it resembles a typical case, ignoring the base rate of that case in the population. In the classic “engineer‑lawyer” problem (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973), participants read a personality sketch and judge whether the person is an engineer or a lawyer. Even when told the population consists of 70 % engineers and 30 % lawyers, participants’ judgments remain close to 50‑50 because the sketch is “representative” of a lawyer. The mechanism is substitution: System 1 replaces the difficult Bayesian computation with an easier similarity judgment. A correct answer must name the substitution process, cite the base‑rate neglect, and illustrate with the engineer‑lawyer or Linda problem.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Decision‑Making Questions
When a question presents a scenario, first identify which system (1 or 2) is likely operating. Then name the specific heuristic or prospect‑theory component (e.g., loss aversion, probability weighting). Use the terminology “value function,” “weighting function,” “reference point” to signal depth. If asked for an intervention, propose a nudge that alters the choice architecture (default, framing, feedback) rather than a purely educational approach.

Developmental Psychopathology: Transactional Models, Gene‑Environment Interplay, and Early Intervention

Transactional and Cascading Models

Developmental psychopathology rejects static “risk factor” lists in favor of dynamic, bidirectional transactions between the child and environment (Sameroff, 2009). A cascade model posits that early deficits (e.g., language delay) propagate across domains, amplifying later maladaptation (Masten & Cicchetti, 2010). For example, early inattention predicts academic underachievement, which increases peer rejection, which in turn elevates risk for conduct disorder. Longitudinal designs such as the Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study (born 1972‑73) have documented such pathways from age 3 to adulthood.

Gene‑Environment Correlation and Interaction

Three forms of gene‑environment correlation (rGE) are distinguished: passive (parents provide both genes and environment), evocative (child’s genetically influenced traits elicit responses), and active (individuals select environments matching their propensities). Gene‑environment interaction (G×E) occurs when the effect of an environmental exposure depends on genotype. The seminal Caspi et al. (2002) study found that the MAOA low‑activity allele moderated the impact of childhood maltreatment on antisocial behavior. Subsequent replication efforts (e.g., meta‑analysis by Byrd & Manuck, 2014) confirm a modest but robust G×E for MAOA and 5‑HTTLPR, emphasizing the need for polygenic scores rather than single‑candidate genes.

Differential Susceptibility and Vantage Sensitivity

Belsky’s differential susceptibility hypothesis (1997) proposes that “plasticity” alleles confer heightened sensitivity to both negative and positive environments — “for better and for worse.” Vantage sensitivity (Pluess & Belsky, 2013) describes individuals who benefit disproportionately from supportive contexts without heightened vulnerability to adversity. These frameworks shift the narrative from “risk alleles” to “plasticity alleles,” informing personalized prevention.

Early Intervention Evidence

High‑quality randomized trials demonstrate that intensive early programs can alter developmental trajectories. The Perry Preschool Project (1962‑1967) provided 2.5 hours/day of active learning to low‑income African‑American children; follow‑ups at age 40 showed higher earnings, lower crime, and better health (Heckman et al., 2010). The Abecedarian Project (1972‑1977) added full‑day, year‑round care from infancy, yielding IQ gains persisting into adulthood and reduced metabolic syndrome risk. Key active ingredients include low child‑to‑staff ratios, language‑rich curricula, and parent engagement. Economic analyses estimate a 7‑10 % annual return on investment.

Forensic Psychology: Competency to Stand Trial, Insanity Defense Standards, and Violence Risk Assessment

Competency to Stand Trial (CST)

The U.S. Supreme Court in Dusky v. United States (1960) established the constitutional standard: the defendant must have “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Most jurisdictions operationalize this through the Competency Assessment Instrument (CAI) or the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool‑Criminal Adjudication (MacCAT‑CA), which evaluate understanding, reasoning, and appreciation. Restoration programs (often 60‑90 days) focus on psychoeducation about court roles and medication adherence for psychotic disorders; restoration rates exceed 70 % for most diagnostic groups.

Insanity Defense Standards

Three major legal tests coexist in the United States: (1) M’Naghten Rule (1843, England) — the defendant, due to a disease of the mind, did not know the nature/quality of the act or that it was wrong; (2) ALI/Model Penal Code Test (1962) — adds a volitional prong: “lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of conduct or to conform conduct to the law”; (3) Durham “Product” Test (1954, D.C. Circuit) — the act is the product of mental disease or defect. The Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 (federal) reverted to a narrowed M’Naghten standard, eliminating the volitional prong and placing the burden of proof on the defendant (clear and convincing evidence). Landmark cases such as Jones v. United States (1983) upheld automatic commitment after a successful insanity plea.

Violence Risk Assessment

Modern risk assessment distinguishes actuarial, structured professional judgment (SPJ), and hybrid approaches. The HCR‑20 (Historical, Clinical, Risk Management‑20 items) is the most widely validated SPJ tool for general violence; the PCL‑R (Psychopathy Checklist‑Revised) assesses psychopathic traits and predicts violent recidivism (effect size d ≈ 0.5‑0.7). The VRAG (Violence Risk Appraisal Guide) and Static‑99R (sexual recidivism) are pure actuarial instruments. Best practice (APA, 2013) recommends using actuarial scores as a baseline, then adjusting with SPJ considerations (dynamic risk factors, protective factors, case‑specific context). Ethical guidelines require transparency about predictive limitations, avoidance of deterministic language, and cultural competence in interpretation.

Social Identity and Collective Action: Self‑Categorization Theory, Social Change, and Political Psychology

Self‑Categorization Theory (SCT)

Turner, Hogg, Oakes, Reicher, and Wetherell (1987) extended Social Identity Theory by specifying the cognitive process of self‑categorization: individuals define themselves at different levels of abstraction (personal identity vs. social identity). Depersonalization — perceiving the self as an interchangeable group member — underlies group cohesion, stereotyping, and normative influence. The meta‑contrast principle predicts that a categorization becomes salient when intergroup differences exceed intragroup differences.

Social Identity Model of Collective Action (SIMCA)

Van Zomeren, Postmes, and Spears (2008) integrated three core predictors of collective action: (1) Social identity (group‑based self‑definition), (2) Group‑based anger (emotional response to perceived injustice), and (3) Perceived efficacy (belief that collective action can achieve change). Meta‑analysis of 180 studies confirmed that identity and anger jointly predict participation, while efficacy moderates the translation of motivation into action. The model explains why disadvantaged groups sometimes remain quiescent (low efficacy) and why privileged groups may act in solidarity (shared identity with the disadvantaged).

Political Psychology Applications

SCT and SIMCA illuminate phenomena such as nationalist mobilization, protest movements, and extremist radicalization. Research on the 2011 Arab Spring demonstrated that shared “we‑ness” among diverse opposition groups predicted sustained protest participation (Drury & Reicher, 2014). In the context of right‑wing populism, “white identity” functions as a superordinate category that mobilizes collective action against perceived out‑groups (Jardina, 2019). Conversely, inclusive national identities can reduce intergroup conflict (Gaertner & Dovidio, 2000).

Methodological Considerations

Measurement of social identity typically uses the Collective Self‑Esteem Scale (Luhtanen & Crocker, 1992) or the In‑Group Identification Scale (Leach et al., 2008). Longitudinal panel designs with cross‑lagged panel models are preferred to infer directionality between identity, emotion, and action. Experimental manipulations (e.g., minimal group paradigm, identity priming) establish causality but must be complemented by field studies for ecological validity.

  • Confusing Classical Test Theory reliability with IRT information — they index precision differently and are not interchangeable.
  • Attributing every judgment error to a single heuristic without considering dual‑process interaction or task demands.
  • Treating gene‑environment interaction as deterministic; most G×E effects are probabilistic and moderated by developmental timing.
  • Assuming competency restoration guarantees trial competence indefinitely; periodic re‑evaluation is required.
  • Equating the M’Naghten rule with the ALI test; the volitional prong fundamentally changes the legal threshold.
