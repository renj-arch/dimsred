Mechanical Engineering Paper I — Thermodynamics, Fluid Mechanics and Mechanics of Materials

Thermodynamics

  • Zeroth law: thermal equilibrium and temperature.
  • First law: energy conservation; closed-system and steady-flow energy equations.
  • Second law: Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements; entropy; reversible and irreversible processes; availability and irreversibility.
  • Cycles: Carnot, Rankine, Brayton, Otto, Diesel, dual; mean effective pressure; air-standard assumptions.
  • Steam and psychrometry: properties, Mollier chart, dry and wet bulb temperature, humidity ratio.
  • Refrigeration: vapour-compression cycle, COP, refrigerants.

Fluid Mechanics

  • Properties: density, viscosity, surface tension, capillarity, compressibility.
  • Statics: pressure distribution, manometers, forces on submerged surfaces, buoyancy.
  • Dynamics: continuity, Bernoulli, momentum equation, flow measurement (orifice, venturi, notches).
  • Viscous flow: laminar and turbulent pipe flow, Darcy–Weisbach friction factor, minor losses, boundary layer, separation, drag and lift.
  • Turbomachinery: pumps, turbines, specific speed, cavitation.

Mechanics of Materials

  • Stress–strain: elastic constants, Hooke’s law, Poisson’s ratio, thermal stresses.
  • Shear force and bending moment: diagrams for beams under point, uniform and varying loads.
  • Bending and shear stresses: flexure formula, shear formula, section modulus.
  • Torsion: circular shafts, power transmission, combined bending and torsion.
  • Columns: Euler and Rankine–Gordon formulas, slenderness ratio.
  • Failure theories: maximum principal stress, maximum shear stress, von Mises, strain energy.
Solved Example: Thermal Stress
Question: A steel rod of length 2 m, area 500 mm², E = 200 GPa is heated by 50°C with both ends fixed. Find the thermal stress (α = 12 × 10⁻⁶/°C).
Answer: Free expansion = αLΔT; prevented strain = αΔT = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 50 = 6 × 10⁻⁴. Stress = E × strain = 200 × 10⁹ × 6 × 10⁻⁴ = 120 × 10⁶ Pa = 120 MPa (compressive).

Common Traps

  • Mixing gauge and absolute pressures.
  • Using Bernoulli across a machine without including work or head terms.
  • Drawing bending moment diagrams with wrong sign conventions.
  • Forgetting stress concentration in combined loading.
  • Using Euler’s formula beyond the elastic limit.
Mains strategy
Always draw the system, free-body diagram and T–s or p–v sketch. State assumptions explicitly (ideal gas, steady flow, neglect losses). Show unit conversions in a separate line. For derivation questions, define every symbol before using it.

Advanced Syllabus Integration: Mechanisms, Exceptions, and Examiner Twists

Moving beyond standard textbook definitions requires understanding how concepts interact in complex systems and where real-world physics deviates from idealized models. For instance, while the Rankine cycle is the standard for steam power plants, a senior examiner often asks about the Reheat-Rankine or Regenerative cycles to test efficiency improvements under practical constraints. Similarly, in Fluid Mechanics, knowing Bernoulli's equation is insufficient; one must understand its limitations regarding viscous losses and compressibility effects at high Mach numbers.

A critical area of depth lies in Turbomachinery. The standard text defines specific speed (N_s) for pump selection. However, the advanced understanding involves recognizing that N_s alone does not dictate efficiency; the shape of the impeller (radial vs. mixed flow) and the cavitation index (σ) are equally vital for high-head applications. The examiner frequently presents a scenario where a pump operates near its Best Efficiency Point (BEP) but suffers from recirculation vortices, leading to noise and vibration—a phenomenon not covered in basic syllabi.

In Mechanics of Materials, the transition from Euler's column formula to Rankine-Gordon is standard. The advanced twist involves analyzing columns with initial crookedness or eccentric loading (Eccentrically loaded columns), where the stress distribution is non-uniform. Furthermore, understanding the distinction between ductile and brittle failure modes in terms of fracture toughness (KIC) versus yield strength is essential for modern structural analysis, which often appears in optional papers focusing on design safety.

Technique: Comparative Analysis with Real-World Constraints
Question: Compare the Otto and Diesel cycles. Derive the expression for thermal efficiency of both. Now, explain why modern heavy-duty engines prefer the Diesel cycle despite its lower theoretical efficiency at low compression ratios. Include a discussion on knocking in SI engines versus diesel knock.
Solution:
Theoretical Efficiency:
Otto Cycle: etaotto = 1 - (1)/(rγ-1)
Diesel Cycle: etadiesel = 1 - (1)/(rγ-1) · (ρ^γ - 1)/((ρ-1)(γ-1))

Practical Preference:
The Diesel cycle operates with a higher compression ratio (r) compared to the Otto cycle. Since efficiency increases significantly with r, and the Diesel cycle allows much higher r values (up to 20:1 or more) without the risk of auto-ignition (knocking), it yields superior thermal efficiency for heavy loads. In contrast, SI engines are limited to r ≈ 9:1 due to detonation limits.
Knocking:
Knocking in SI engines is caused by premature ignition of the end-gas before the flame front reaches it, leading to explosive pressure spikes. In Diesel engines, "diesel knock" occurs due to the rapid heat release during the combustion phase when fuel injection timing is incorrect or air-fuel ratio is too lean. The former is destructive; the latter is often audible but less catastrophic if not severe.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Multi-Part Derivations
When asked to derive a formula (e.g., Bernoulli's equation) and apply it, do not skip the intermediate steps. Explicitly state the assumptions made at every stage (inviscid, incompressible, steady flow). If the question asks for a comparison, use a small table to contrast parameters like work output, heat input, and efficiency. This saves time and demonstrates structured thinking.

Applied Scenarios: Energy Recovery and Non-Standard Cycles

The core syllabus focuses on the Carnot cycle as the benchmark for efficiency. However, modern engineering challenges involve Organic Rankine Cycles (ORC) for waste heat recovery or Supercritical CO2 cycles for next-generation power plants. These systems operate far from the ideal assumptions of air-standard cycles. A serious aspirant must understand that in ORC, the working fluid undergoes phase change at lower temperatures, requiring careful selection of organic fluids with appropriate boiling points and environmental safety profiles.

In Fluid Mechanics, the study of open channel flow often stops at the Froude number and specific energy diagrams. The advanced application involves analyzing hydraulic jumps in spillways or stilling basins, where energy dissipation is critical to prevent scouring downstream. Understanding the momentum equation applied to a control volume with sudden expansion or contraction (Borda-Carnot loss) is crucial for designing pipe networks.

Mechanics of Materials extends into fatigue analysis. While static failure theories (Von Mises, Tresca) are standard, real components fail due to cyclic loading. The S-N curve (Wöhler curve) and the concept of endurance limit are vital. The Goodman diagram or Gerber parabola is used to predict failure under combined mean and alternating stresses. This is a frequent topic in design-oriented questions.

Exceptions and Landmark Data in Mechanical Systems

Textbooks often present linear relationships, but nature is non-linear. For example, the viscosity of liquids decreases with temperature, whereas gases show an increase. This fundamental difference affects lubrication regimes (hydrodynamic vs. elastohydrodynamic). In thermodynamics, the ideal gas law fails at high pressures and low temperatures; the Van der Waals equation or Redlich-Kwong equation must be used. The critical point of water (374^circC, 22.1 MPa) is a landmark datum where liquid and vapor phases become indistinguishable (supercritical fluid), utilized in supercritical power cycles.

In Mechanics of Materials, the assumption of small deformations breaks down in large displacement problems (geometric non-linearity). This is relevant in rubber components or thin shells under high pressure. The Poisson's ratio for auxetic materials (negative Poisson's ratio) is a modern exception to the standard 0 < nu < 0.5 range, offering unique expansion properties.

Technique: Identifying Non-Linear Behavior in Standard Problems
Question: Discuss why the standard Bernoulli equation cannot be applied directly to water flow through a long pipe with rough walls, and how the Moody chart corrects this.
Solution:
The Bernoulli equation assumes inviscid (zero viscosity) flow. In real pipes, wall friction causes head loss (h_f). The term (P)/(ρ g) + (V2)/(2g) + z is constant only in ideal flow.
Correction: Add the Darcy-Weisbach loss term:
h_L = f · (L)/(D) · (V2)/(2g)
The friction factor f is not constant; it depends on Reynolds number (Re) and relative roughness (ε/D). The Moody chart provides the relationship between these variables. At high Reynolds numbers (turbulent flow), f becomes dependent only on roughness, leading to fully rough pipe behavior where head loss is proportional to V1.75 or V2 depending on the regime.
  • Ignoring Fluid Compressibility: Applying Bernoulli to high-speed gas flow (Mach > 0.3) without using compressible flow relations leads to significant errors in pressure drop calculations.
  • Misapplying Euler's Formula: Using Euler's buckling load for short, stocky columns where the failure mode is crushing (yielding) rather than elastic instability results in unsafe designs. Always check slenderness ratio (L/k).
  • Neglecting Stress Concentration: Calculating stress at a notch or hole using nominal stress formulas without applying K_t leads to underestimating fatigue life by orders of magnitude.
  • Confusing COP and EER: In refrigeration cycles, confusing Coefficient of Performance (COP) with Energy Efficiency Ratio (EER) can lead to incorrect efficiency comparisons. Remember EER is essentially COP multiplied by a conversion factor (usually 3.412).

Entropy Generation and Exergy Analysis in Real Power Cycles

Entropy generation quantifies the irreversibility present in every real thermodynamic process. For a control volume operating at steady state, the rate of entropy generation Ṡ_gen equals the net entropy outflow minus inflow plus entropy transfer with heat. The Gouy‑Stodola theorem links this generation to exergy destruction: Ė_D = T₀ Ṡ_gen, where T₀ is the reference environment temperature. In a combined‑cycle power plant the gas‑turbine combustor, the high‑pressure turbine, the heat‑recovery steam generator (HRSG) and the steam turbine each contribute to Ṡ_gen. The second‑law efficiency of a component is defined as the ratio of its exergy output to exergy input; for a turbine it becomes η_II = (Ė_out − Ė_in) / (Ė_fuel − Ė_loss). Chemical exergy of the fuel, often approximated as 1.04 times its higher heating value for hydrocarbons, sets the ultimate exergy supply. When performing an exergy audit, the reference environment is usually taken as 298.15 K and 1 atm with a composition of dry air; any deviation must be stated explicitly. A common examiner twist is to ask for the “minimum work input” to a compressor when the inlet temperature is raised by a regenerator — here the exergy balance must include the regenerator’s own destruction term. The exergy destruction in the HRSG is dominated by the large temperature difference between the gas‑turbine exhaust (≈ 800 K) and the water/steam side (≈ 550 K); reducing this pinch point improves the overall second‑law efficiency more than a comparable increase in turbine inlet temperature.

  • Gouy‑Stodola relation: Ė_D = T₀ Ṡ_gen
  • Second‑law efficiency: η_II = Exergy_out / Exergy_in
  • Chemical exergy of typical hydrocarbon fuel ≈ 1.04 × HHV
  • Reference environment: 298.15 K, 1 atm, dry‑air composition
  • Pinch‑point analysis in HRSG directly reveals major exergy destruction zones

Compressible Flow Through Convergent‑Divergent Nozzles

Isentropic flow of a perfect gas through a convergent‑divergent (C‑D) nozzle is governed by the area‑Mach number relation A/A* = (1/M) [(2/(γ+1)) (1 + (γ−1)/2 M²)]^((γ+1)/(2(γ−1))). At the throat the Mach number reaches unity when the pressure ratio across the nozzle exceeds the critical value (P₀/P*) = ((γ+1)/2)^(γ/(γ−1)); this condition is called choking. Downstream of the throat the flow can expand supersonically (M > 1) if the back pressure is low enough, or a normal shock can appear inside the divergent section when the back pressure lies between the design pressure and the pressure required for isentropic supersonic expansion. The shock position is determined by matching the mass flow rate and the downstream static pressure. Overexpanded nozzles (exit pressure < ambient) produce expansion fans at the lip, while underexpanded nozzles (exit pressure > ambient) generate compression waves. Real nozzles experience friction and heat transfer; Fanno flow models adiabatic flow with friction in constant‑area ducts, and Rayleigh flow models frictionless flow with heat addition. In rocket nozzles the high‑temperature gas often deviates from perfect‑gas behaviour, requiring temperature‑dependent specific heats or equilibrium chemistry tables. The method of characteristics is used to design the divergent contour for uniform exit flow, minimizing losses due to non‑axial velocity components.

  • Critical pressure ratio for air (γ = 1.4): P₀/P* ≈ 1.893
  • Choked mass flow: ṁ = (A* P₀ / √(T₀)) √(γ/R) ((γ+1)/2)^(-(γ+1)/(2(γ−1)))
  • Normal‑shock relations: M₂² = (1 + (γ−1)/2 M₁²) / (γ M₁² − (γ−1)/2)
  • Fanno line: constant stagnation enthalpy, entropy increases with friction
  • Rayleigh line: constant stagnation pressure, entropy changes with heat addition
Technique: Exergy Balance for a Combined Cycle
Question: A gas turbine exhaust at 800 K supplies heat to a bottoming steam cycle. The ambient temperature is 300 K. If the gas turbine produces 100 MW of work and the exhaust heat rate is 150 MW, calculate the maximum possible work from the bottoming cycle using exergy analysis.
Solution: The exergy of the exhaust heat stream is Q_ex × (1 − T₀/T_ex) where Q_ex = 150 MW, T_ex = 800 K, T₀ = 300 K. Exergy = 150 × (1 − 300/800) = 150 × (1 − 0.375) = 150 × 0.625 = 93.75 MW. This is the theoretical maximum work the bottoming cycle can produce. Actual work will be lower due to irreversibilities in the heat recovery steam generator and steam turbine.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a problem asks for “maximum possible work” or “minimum work input”, immediately identify the reference environment (T₀, P₀) and apply the Gouy‑Stodola relation. Write the exergy balance in a single line before substituting numbers; this shows the examiner you understand the concept and avoids arithmetic slips.

Plastic Hinge Formation and Limit Analysis of Beams

When a beam section reaches its plastic moment M_p = Z σ_y (where Z is the plastic section modulus and σ_y the yield stress), a plastic hinge forms. The length of the hinge over which the moment remains at M_p depends on the moment gradient; for a rectangular section under a point load the hinge length is approximately L_p = L (1 − M_y/M_p) where M_y is the yield moment. In limit analysis the upper‑bound (kinematic) theorem states that any compatible collapse mechanism gives an upper bound to the true collapse load, while the lower‑bound (static) theorem states that any statically admissible moment distribution not exceeding M_p gives a lower bound. For a simply supported beam with a central point load the exact collapse load is P_c = 4 M_p / L. In frames, plastic hinges typically form at beam ends, column bases, and points of maximum moment; the collapse mechanism is identified by the number of hinges required to turn the structure into a mechanism (usually n + 1 hinges for a frame with n degrees of indeterminacy). Axial force reduces the plastic moment capacity according to the interaction equation (M/M_p) + (N/N_p) ≤ 1 for rectangular sections, where N_p = A σ_y. Moment redistribution in continuous beams is permitted up to 30 % of the elastic peak moment in many design codes, provided sufficient rotation capacity exists at the hinge locations.

  • Plastic moment: M_p = Z σ_y
  • Shape factor for rectangle: Z/S = 1.5
  • Upper‑bound theorem: collapse load ≤ load from any kinematic mechanism
  • Lower‑bound theorem: collapse load ≥ load from any static moment field ≤ M_p
  • Interaction equation: (M/M_p) + (N/N_p) ≤ 1

Thermoelastic Coupling in High‑Speed Rotating Machinery

In gas‑turbine rotors the temperature field is highly non‑uniform: the disk rim can be several hundred kelvin hotter than the bore. This gradient induces thermal stresses that superimpose on the centrifugal stresses. The radial thermal stress in a thin rotating disk with a linear temperature distribution T(r) = T_i + (T_o − T_i) (r − r_i)/(r_o − r_i) is given by σ_th = (E α / (1 − ν)) (T_avg − T(r)) where E is Young’s modulus, α the coefficient of thermal expansion, ν Poisson’s ratio, and T_avg the area‑averaged temperature. The combined stress alters the effective stiffness and therefore the critical speeds. A Campbell diagram plots the natural frequencies (both forward and backward whirl) against rotational speed; intersections with the synchronous excitation line (1× Ω) indicate critical speeds. Thermoelastic instability can arise when the thermal expansion causes a change in bearing clearance, leading to a shift in the rotor’s centre of mass and a self‑excited vibration known as “thermal bow”. Materials such as nickel‑based superalloys retain high E and low α at 1000 K, but their creep behaviour must be accounted for in long‑term operation. Design practice includes a “thermal‑mechanical” finite‑element analysis where the temperature field from a conjugate heat‑transfer solution is mapped as a thermal load onto the structural model, and the resulting stress field is checked against both yield and low‑cycle fatigue limits.

  • Thermal stress in rotating disk: σ_th = (E α / (1 − ν)) (T_avg − T(r))
  • Campbell diagram: natural frequency vs. rotational speed, identifies critical speeds
  • Thermal bow: self‑excited vibration due to asymmetric thermal expansion
  • Superalloy properties at 1000 K: high E, low α, significant creep
  • Coupled analysis workflow: CFD → conjugate heat transfer → thermal load mapping → structural FEM
  • Assuming constant specific heats in exergy calculations for high‑temperature combustion products.
  • Neglecting the kinetic‑energy term in the steady‑flow energy equation for nozzle throat conditions.
  • Using the elastic section modulus instead of the plastic modulus when checking plastic hinge capacity.
  • Applying the simple Euler buckling formula to short columns without checking the slenderness ratio.
  • Ignoring the effect of axial force on plastic moment capacity in frame limit analysis.
  • Treating thermal stresses as purely static when the rotor passes through critical speeds.
  • Using a single reference temperature for exergy destruction in components that operate over a wide temperature range.

These advanced topics integrate second‑law analysis, compressible‑flow theory, plastic‑limit methods, and thermoelastic coupling to give a complete picture of real‑world mechanical‑engineering systems.

Second‑Law Analysis of Real‑Gas Power Cycles

Ideal‑gas assumptions simplify the first and second laws but become inaccurate above 800 K where specific heats vary strongly and intermolecular forces matter. A rigorous second‑law analysis for real gases uses a cubic equation of state — most commonly Peng‑Robinson or Soave‑Redlich‑Kwong — to obtain enthalpy, entropy and fugacity coefficients as functions of temperature and pressure. The specific exergy of a stream is then

ψ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀) + ½ V² + g z

where the reference state (h₀, s₀) is taken at the dead‑state temperature T₀ and pressure p₀. For a closed‑cycle gas turbine the total exergy destruction Σ I = T₀ Σ ΔS_gen is distributed among the compressor, combustor, turbine and heat‑exchangers. Because the real‑gas entropy departure Δs_dep = s^ig − s^real is a function of the compressibility factor Z, the exergy destruction in the compressor can be significantly larger than the ideal‑gas prediction at pressure ratios above 30. The combustor exergy loss is dominated by chemical irreversibility; using the real‑gas enthalpy of formation for the fuel‑air mixture shifts the adiabatic flame temperature, which directly changes the turbine inlet exergy. In combined‑cycle plants the bottoming steam cycle receives a lower‑temperature heat source when real‑gas effects are accounted for, reducing the overall second‑law efficiency. Practising engineers therefore map the entire cycle on a T‑s diagram generated from the EOS, compute component‑wise exergy destruction, and identify the compressor and combustor as primary targets for improvement — for example by intercooling or by staged combustion with exhaust‑gas recirculation.

Key Equations and Data

  • Peng‑Robinson EOS: p = RT/(v−b) − aα/(v²+2bv−b²) with a = 0.45724 R²T_c²/p_c, b = 0.07780 RT_c/p_c, α = [1+κ(1−√(T/T_c))]².
  • Entropy departure: Δs_dep = R ln(Z−B) + (aα/(2√2 bRT)) ln[(Z+(1+√2)B)/(Z+(1−√2)B)], B = bp/RT.
  • Typical exergy destruction fractions (real gas, 30 bar, 1500 K): compressor 0.38, combustor 0.42, turbine 0.15, heat‑exchangers 0.05.

When the cycle is modelled in a process simulator (e.g., Aspen Plus, gPROMS) the second‑law efficiency η_II = 1 − Σ I/Ė_fuel can be reported directly. For a 400 MW class gas turbine the real‑gas η_II is typically lower than the ideal‑gas model, a difference that is decisive in competitive bidding.

Boundary‑Layer Transition and Separation Control in Compressible Flow

In high‑speed internal and external flows the laminar‑to‑turbulent transition point determines skin‑friction drag, heat‑transfer rates and the onset of shock‑induced separation. The classical eⁿ method correlates the amplification factor N of the most unstable Tollmien‑Schlichting wave with the local Reynolds number Re_θ based on momentum thickness. For compressible flow the Van Driest transformation maps the compressible velocity profile to an equivalent incompressible one, allowing the use of the same N‑factor correlations (typically N = 9–11 for natural transition). At Mach numbers above 2.5 the second‑mode (Mack) instability dominates; its growth rate scales with the edge Mach number M_e and the wall‑to‑recovery temperature ratio T_w/T_r. Transition prediction therefore requires a coupled solution of the linear stability equations (Orr‑Sommerfeld) with the mean flow from a Reynolds‑averaged Navier‑Stokes (RANS) solver.

Separation control aims to delay or eliminate the adverse‑pressure‑gradient induced reversal of the near‑wall flow. Passive devices — vortex generators (VGs), surface roughness strips, and saw‑tooth leading edges — introduce streamwise vortices that transport high‑momentum fluid toward the wall. For a typical transonic compressor cascade, a row of rectangular VGs of height h = 0.5 δ (δ = local boundary‑layer thickness) spaced at 4 h can reduce the separation bubble length substantially and raise the stall margin by several percent in terms of pressure ratio. Active control — steady or pulsed blowing/suction through slots — can achieve larger gains but at the cost of system complexity. The momentum coefficient C_μ = (ṁ_jet V_jet)/(½ ρ_∞ U_∞² A_slot) is the key nondimensional parameter; values of C_μ ≈ 0.001–0.005 are sufficient for attached‑flow maintenance in Mach 1.5–2.0 flows. Recent experiments on a Mach 2.5 ramp show that plasma actuators operating at 10 kHz produce a body‑force distribution equivalent to C_μ ≈ 0.003, suppressing separation without moving parts.

Design Guidelines

  • Compute the local Re_θ and apply the eⁿ criterion with Van Driest‑transformed profiles for M < 2.5.
  • For M ≥ 2.5 include second‑mode growth rates from linear stability codes (e.g., STAB, LASTRAC).
  • Select VG height h ≈ 0.4–0.6 δ and spacing 3–5 h; verify with CFD that the generated vortices remain coherent to the separation point.
  • For active blowing, size the slot to deliver C_μ ≥ 0.001 at the design Mach number; ensure the total pressure ratio of the supply system exceeds 1.2.

Integrating transition and separation models into the design loop (RANS → stability → geometry update) can significantly reduce the number of wind‑tunnel tests for modern high‑pressure turbine blades.

Worked Example: Exergy Destruction in a Real‑Gas Compressor Stage
A single‑stage axial compressor operates with inlet total conditions T₀₁ = 300 K, p₀₁ = 1 bar and exit total pressure p₀₂ = 30 bar. The working fluid is methane modeled with the Peng‑Robinson EOS (T_c = 190.6 K, p_c = 46.0 bar, ω = 0.011). The isentropic efficiency is η_c = 0.88. Compute the specific exergy destruction in the compressor assuming a dead state of T₀ = 298 K, p₀ = 1 bar.

1. Determine the isentropic exit temperature T₀₂s from the real‑gas isentropic relation s(T₀₂s, p₀₂) = s(T₀₁, p₀₁). Using the Peng‑Robinson departure functions, an iterative solution gives T₀₂s ≈ 620 K.

2. Actual exit temperature from η_c = (h₀₂s − h₀₁)/(h₀₂ − h₀₁). With real‑gas enthalpies h₀₁ = 520 kJ/kg, h₀₂s = 1150 kJ/kg, the actual enthalpy h₀₂ = h₀₁ + (h₀₂s − h₀₁)/η_c ≈ 1240 kJ/kg, corresponding to T₀₂ ≈ 660 K.

3. Specific entropy at inlet s₁ = 9.85 kJ/(kg·K) (from EOS). At actual exit s₂ = 10.30 kJ/(kg·K). Entropy generation Δs_gen = s₂ − s₁ = 0.45 kJ/(kg·K).

4. Exergy destruction i = T₀ Δs_gen = 298 K × 0.45 kJ/(kg·K) = 134 kJ/kg.

5. For comparison, the ideal‑gas model (c_p = 2.2 kJ/(kg·K), γ = 1.31) yields i_ideal ≈ 108 kJ/kg, under‑predicting destruction by ~19 %.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Advanced Thermodynamics and Fluid Mechanics
When a question asks for a second‑law efficiency or exergy destruction, first state the reference environment (T₀, p₀) and the property model (ideal gas, Peng‑Robinson, etc.). Write the general exergy balance before substituting numbers; this shows the examiner you understand the framework. For compressible‑flow transition problems, quote the eⁿ criterion and the Van Driest transformation, then indicate whether first‑ or second‑mode instability is expected based on the given Mach number. In fracture‑mechanics or fatigue parts, define the relevant parameter (J‑integral, ΔK, critical plane) and the applicable code (ASME VIII, ASTM E1820). Always carry units through the calculation and round only at the final step.

Elastic‑Plastic Fracture Mechanics for Pressure Vessel Design

When a pressure vessel operates in the ductile‑to‑brittle transition regime or contains a flaw that grows under cyclic loading, linear‑elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM) based on the stress‑intensity factor K_I becomes non‑conservative because the plastic zone size r_p = (1/2π)(K_I/σ_y)² can be a significant fraction of the wall thickness. Elastic‑plastic fracture mechanics (EPFM) replaces K_I with the J‑integral, defined as the energy release rate for a nonlinear material: J = ∫_Γ (W dy − T_i ∂u_i/∂x ds), where W is the strain‑energy density, T_i the traction vector, and u_i the displacement field. For a power‑law hardening material σ = K εⁿ the HRR singularity gives J = α σ_y ε_y r_p, with α a geometry factor. The crack‑tip opening displacement (CTOD) δ is related to J by δ = d_n J/σ_y, where d_n depends on the hardening exponent n (d_n ≈ 0.4 for n = 0.2). ASME Section VIII Division 2 provides the failure assessment diagram (FAD) that plots the load ratio L_r = σ_ref/σ_y against the toughness ratio K_r = K_I/K_mat (or J_r = J/J_mat). The assessment point must lie below the FAD curve, which for a typical ferritic steel follows the Option 1 curve: (L_r)² + (K_r)² = 1 for L_r ≤ 1, and L_r + K_r = 1 for L_r > 1.

In practice the engineer computes the reference stress σ_ref from a limit‑load solution (e.g., σ_ref = p R/t for a thin cylinder) and evaluates K_I from the stress distribution using weight‑function methods or finite‑element J‑integral extraction. The material toughness K_mat is taken as the lower bound of the K_IC or J_IC data at the operating temperature, often expressed as a master curve per ASTM E1921. For a 200 mm thick reactor pressure vessel steel (SA‑508 Cl.3) at 288 °C, typical values are K_IC ≈ 150 MPa√m, J_IC ≈ 200 kJ/m². A semi‑elliptical surface crack of depth a = 30 mm and aspect ratio a/c = 0.5 yields K_I ≈ 120 MPa√m under design pressure, giving K_r = 0.80. With σ_ref = 250 MPa and σ_y = 350 MPa, L_r = 0.71. The point (0.71, 0.80) lies inside the FAD, so the vessel passes the EPFM assessment. If the same crack were subjected to a thermal shock that raises the local stress to 400 MPa, L_r = 1.14 and the assessment point moves outside the curve, indicating a need for repair or reduced pressure.

Creep‑Fatigue Interaction in High‑Temperature Alloys

Components in gas turbines, steam headers and nuclear reactors experience simultaneous cyclic loading and sustained high temperature, leading to a combined damage mode that is not captured by separate creep‑rupture or fatigue curves. The most widely used interaction model is the linear damage rule (LDR) based on the time‑fraction and cycle‑fraction approach: D = Σ (Δt_i / t_r,i) + Σ (N_i / N_f,i) ≤ 1, where t_r,i is the creep‑rupture life at the stress and temperature of the i‑th hold period, and N_f,i is the fatigue life at the corresponding strain range. For more accurate prediction the strain‑range partitioning (SRP) method divides the total inelastic strain range Δε_in into four components — plastic‑plastic (PP), creep‑plastic (CP), plastic‑creep (PC) and creep‑creep (CC) — each with its own damage coefficient. The total damage per cycle is D = Σ (Δε_j / ε_f,j) where ε_f,j are the ductility exhaustion values obtained from creep‑fatigue tests at the relevant temperature.

The Larson‑Miller parameter (LMP) P = T (C + log t_r) with C ≈ 20 for many nickel‑based superalloys correlates creep‑rupture data across temperatures. For a typical IN‑738LC turbine blade alloy at 950 °C, a stress of 250 MPa gives P ≈ 30 000, corresponding to t_r ≈ 10⁴ h. If the same blade experiences a thermal cycle of Δε = 0.6 % every 30 min, the fatigue life from the Coffin‑Manson relation Δε_p/2 = ε_f' (2N_f)^c (with ε_f' = 0.5, c = –0.5) is N_f ≈ 2 × 10⁴ cycles. The LDR then yields D_cycle = (0.5 h / 10⁴ h) + (1 / 2 × 10⁴) ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁵ + 5 × 10⁻⁵ = 1 × 10⁻⁴ per cycle, giving a predicted life of about 10⁴ cycles — far shorter than either pure creep or pure fatigue life. The SRP method often predicts a longer life because it accounts for the reduced damage of the CP and PC components relative to the fully plastic PP component.

Design Recommendations

  • Obtain creep‑fatigue data at the exact temperature and stress ratio; extrapolating from a single temperature using LMP alone can introduce large errors.
  • Use the SRP method for components with hold times >10 % of the cycle period; otherwise the LDR is acceptable.
  • Apply a safety factor of 1.5 on the predicted life for critical rotating parts, as recommended by ASME Section III, Subsection NH.
  • Consider environmental effects (oxidation, carburization) that reduce ε_f' by up to 40 % in air at >900 °C.

Finite‑element creep‑fatigue analysis (e.g., using the Chaboche viscoplastic model with isotropic/kinematic hardening) is now standard for turbine blade root fir‑tree attachments, where stress concentrations and multiaxial states make analytical partitioning impractical.

Multiaxial Fatigue Criteria and Critical Plane Methods

Real machine elements — crankshafts, gear teeth, turbine disks — rarely experience proportional uniaxial loading. Non‑proportional multiaxial histories (e.g., combined bending and torsion with a phase shift) produce additional hardening and a different crack‑initiation plane than predicted by equivalent‑stress approaches. The von Mises equivalent strain amplitude Δε_eq = √(2/3 Δε_ij Δε_ij) works for proportional loading but underestimates damage for 90° out‑of‑phase bending‑torsion by up to a factor of 3. Critical‑plane methods evaluate the fatigue damage on the plane where the crack is most likely to nucleate, typically the plane of maximum shear strain amplitude or maximum normal stress. The Fatemi‑Socie parameter FS = (Δγ_max/2) (1 + k σ_n,max/σ_y) combines the maximum shear strain range Δγ_max on the critical plane with the maximum normal stress σ_n,max acting on that plane; k is a material constant (≈0.3 for steels). The Smith‑Watson‑Topper (SWT) parameter SWT = σ_n,max Δε_n/2 uses the normal strain range Δε_n on the plane of maximum normal stress. For non‑proportional loading the critical plane orientation changes during the cycle; the Wang‑Brown method searches all possible plane orientations at each time increment and accumulates damage using a cycle‑counting algorithm (e.g., rainflow) on the resolved shear and normal histories.

Studies on SAE 4340 steel show that for a 90° out‑of‑phase bending‑torsion history with strain amplitudes ε_a = 0.4 % and γ_a = 0.7 %, the Fatemi‑Socie model predicts N_f ≈ 1.2 × 10⁴ cycles, while the von Mises equivalent strain gives N_f ≈ 3.5 × 10⁴ cycles; the measured life is 1.0 × 10⁴ cycles. The critical‑plane approach also captures the observed crack orientation at 45° to the shaft axis, which the equivalent‑stress method cannot. In design codes such as ASME Section VIII‑2 Appendix 4, the Fatemi‑Socie parameter is recommended for high‑cycle fatigue of pressure‑vessel nozzles under combined pressure and thermal gradients.

Implementation Steps

  1. Obtain the full stress/strain tensor history at the critical location from a nonlinear FEM analysis (including plasticity).
  2. For each time increment, compute the resolved shear strain range Δγ(θ,φ) and normal stress σ_n(θ,φ) on a dense set of plane orientations (θ,φ).
  3. Apply a multiaxial rainflow counting algorithm to the resolved histories to extract cycles.
  4. Evaluate the chosen critical‑plane parameter (FS, SWT, or a combined metric) for each counted cycle.
  5. Sum damage using Miner’s rule D = Σ (1/N_f,i) where N_f,i is obtained from the material’s uniaxial fatigue curve expressed in terms of the critical‑plane parameter.

Modern fatigue solvers (e.g., nCode DesignLife, fe‑safe) automate steps 2‑5 and can handle variable‑amplitude spectra with non‑proportional loading, reducing the analyst’s effort to a single FEM run and a material‑parameter calibration.

Common Traps

  • Assuming ideal‑gas properties for exergy calculations above 800 K, which underestimates compressor exergy destruction.
  • Applying the eⁿ transition criterion without the Van Driest transformation for Mach numbers above 1.5, leading to premature transition prediction.
  • Using K_IC from room‑temperature tests for EPFM assessments at operating temperature, ignoring the master‑curve shift.
  • Applying the linear damage rule to creep‑fatigue problems with hold times exceeding 10 % of the cycle period, where strain‑range partitioning is required.
  • Employing von Mises equivalent strain for non‑proportional multiaxial fatigue, which can over‑predict life by a factor of 2‑3.
  • Neglecting the effect of oxidation on fatigue ductility exponent ε_f' in high‑temperature creep‑fatigue life estimates.
  • Using a single reference temperature for exergy destruction in components that experience a wide temperature gradient, such as a turbine blade cooling passage.

Real‑Gas Thermodynamics in High‑Pressure Turbine Expansion

At turbine inlet temperatures above 1200 K and pressures exceeding 30 bar, the ideal‑gas assumption can introduce errors exceeding 5 % in specific work and exergy destruction. Real‑gas behavior is captured by cubic equations of state (Peng–Robinson, Soave‑Redlich‑Kwong) or by the NASA‑GLASSES database, which provides temperature‑dependent specific heats, enthalpy, and entropy for combustion products. The departure functions for enthalpy and entropy are evaluated from residual properties: hR = ∫[T(∂Z/∂T)P − (Z − 1)] R dT and sR = ∫[(∂Z/∂T)P − (Z − 1)/T] R dT. In a typical high‑pressure turbine stage, the isentropic expansion line on a T‑s diagram deviates from the ideal‑gas line; the real‑gas isentropic exponent k may vary from approximately 1.30 at inlet to 1.22 at exit. This variation can reduce predicted stage work by 3–4 % and increase entropy generation by a comparable amount. For design‑point calculations, real‑gas tables are interpolated at each station; for off‑design maps, a polynomial fit of cp(T,P) and γ(T,P) is stored in the performance code. The impact on cycle efficiency is pronounced in combined‑cycle plants where the bottoming‑cycle heat‑recovery steam generator receives exhaust around 800 K; a 2 % error in turbine exhaust enthalpy may propagate to approximately 0.3 % error in overall plant efficiency. Examiner questions often ask for the quantitative difference between ideal‑gas and real‑gas work for a given pressure ratio, requiring the candidate to set up the integral of v dP using the real‑gas specific volume from the equation of state.

Non‑Newtonian Lubrication Theory for Journal Bearings

Heavy‑duty journal bearings in marine diesel engines and high‑speed compressors frequently operate with shear‑thinning lubricants (e.g., polymer‑thickened oils) or with contaminated oil that exhibits a yield stress. The classical Reynolds equation assumes a Newtonian viscosity μ constant across the film. For a power‑law fluid τ = K γ̇n (0 < n < 1), the effective viscosity becomes μeff = K γ̇n‑1. Substituting this into the momentum balance and integrating across the film yields a modified Reynolds equation: ∂/∂x (h2n+1 ∂p/∂x) + ∂/∂z (h2n+1 ∂p/∂z) = 6 U ∂h/∂x, where U is the journal surface speed. The pressure gradient is therefore amplified in the converging wedge because the apparent viscosity drops with increasing shear rate. For a bearing with n = 0.7, peak pressure may rise by 12–15 % compared with the Newtonian prediction, while minimum film thickness may decrease by 8–10 %. When a yield stress τy is present (Bingham plastic), a plug‑flow region forms where |τ| < τy; the Reynolds equation is solved piecewise, and load‑capacity reduction can reach 20 % for τy = 0.5 MPa. Thermal coupling is essential: the viscosity‑temperature coefficient for non‑Newtonian oils is often larger than for base oils, so the energy equation must be solved simultaneously with the modified Reynolds equation. In practice, bearing designers use the dimensionless Sommerfeld number S = (μ N L D)/W corrected by a factor f(n, τy) obtained from numerical lookup tables. Examination problems may provide a viscosity‑shear‑rate curve and ask for the percentage change in load capacity or the new minimum film thickness, requiring the candidate to set up the integral form of the modified Reynolds equation and evaluate it for a given eccentricity ratio.

Worked Example: Power‑Law Bearing Load Capacity
A 100 mm diameter, 100 mm long journal bearing runs at 3000 rpm with a radial clearance of 0.05 mm. The lubricant follows a power‑law model with K = 0.12 Pa·sn and n = 0.65. The applied load is 5 kN. Estimate the percentage change in load capacity relative to a Newtonian oil of viscosity μ = 0.08 Pa·s at the same operating conditions.
1. Compute the Sommerfeld number for the Newtonian case: SN = (μ N L D)/W. N = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s. μ = 0.08 Pa·s, L = D = 0.1 m, W = 5000 N. SN = (0.08 × 50 × 0.1 × 0.1)/5000 = 8 × 10‑6. 2. For a short bearing (L/D = 1) the load capacity scales with S‑0.5. The non‑Newtonian correction factor for n = 0.65 is f ≈ 1.13 (from standard tables). 3. Effective Sommerfeld number SNN = SN/f = 7.08 × 10‑6. 4. Load capacity ratio WNN/WN = (SN/SNN)0.5 = f0.5 ≈ 1.063. 5. Percentage increase = (1.063 − 1) × 100 % ≈ 6.3 %. Thus, in this example, the power‑law fluid raises the load capacity by about 6 % compared with the Newtonian oil of the same zero‑shear viscosity.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a non‑Newtonian bearing problem appears, first identify the rheological model (power‑law, Bingham, Carreau). Write the modified Reynolds equation in its dimensionless form; the exponent on film thickness (2n+1) is the key. Use the supplied Sommerfeld number or compute it from given data. If a correction factor table is not provided, remember that for shear‑thinning fluids (n < 1) the load capacity increases roughly as f0.5 where f ≈ 1 + 0.2(1‑n) (a common approximation). Show the scaling steps clearly; examiners may award marks for the correct exponent manipulation even if the final numeric integration is omitted.

Fracture Mechanics of Welded Pressure Vessel Joints

Welded joints in pressure vessels introduce geometric discontinuities (toe radius, undercut) and residual stress fields that dominate fracture‑mechanics assessments. The stress‑intensity factor for a surface crack at a weld toe is expressed as KI = Y σ √(π a), where the geometry factor Y depends on the weld profile angle θ, the toe radius ρ, and the crack aspect ratio a/c. For a double‑V butt weld with a 30° reinforcement angle and a toe radius of 1 mm, finite‑element calibration suggests Y ≈ 1.12 (1 + 0.3 a/ρ) for shallow cracks (a < 2 mm). Residual stresses from welding can approach 0.8 σy in the heat‑affected zone; they are incorporated by superposition: Ktotal = Kapplied + Kresidual. The residual‑stress intensity factor is obtained from the weight‑function method: Kresidual = ∫0a σres(x) m(x,a) dx, where m(x,a) is the weight function for a surface crack in a plate. In ASME Section VIII‑2, the allowable flaw size is determined by the failure assessment diagram (FAD) using the ratio Kr = Ktotal/Kmat and the load ratio Lr = σref/σy. For a 20 mm thick SA‑516‑Gr70 vessel operating at 15 MPa, a 3 mm deep toe crack may yield Kr ≈ 0.68 and Lr ≈ 0.45, placing the point inside the Level 2 acceptance curve. However, if the weld toe radius is reduced to 0.3 mm by grinding, Y may rise to 1.35 and Kr may exceed 0.85, potentially requiring repair. Examiner questions often provide a weld geometry, a residual‑stress distribution (e.g., σres = 0.6 σy exp(‑x/2 mm)), and ask for the critical crack depth using the FAD. The candidate must set up the weight‑function integral, evaluate it analytically or numerically, and then plot the assessment point.

Thermo‑Mechanical Fatigue of Gas‑Turbine Blades

Gas‑turbine blades experience simultaneous high‑temperature creep and cyclic thermal gradients, leading to thermo‑mechanical fatigue (TMF) that cannot be captured by isothermal fatigue curves alone. The strain range partitioning (SRP) method divides the total inelastic strain range Δεin into four components: plastic (PP), creep‑tensile (CP), creep‑compressive (CC), and creep‑relaxation (CR). Each component is assigned a damage exponent: Nf = (ΔεPP/εf′)‑1/c + (ΔεCP/εf′)‑1/cc + …. For a typical Ni‑base superalloy (IN‑738) at 950 °C, the creep‑tensile exponent cc is approximately 0.6, while the plastic exponent c is approximately 0.5. The phase angle φ between the mechanical strain and temperature cycles critically influences the partitioning: in‑phase (φ = 0°) maximizes CP damage, out‑of‑phase (φ = 180°) maximizes CC damage. Experimental data on IN‑738 indicate that for a total strain range of 0.8 % and φ = 0°, predicted life is approximately 1.2 × 104 cycles; for φ = 180° life may drop to around 6.5 × 103 cycles because tensile creep occurs at the highest temperature. Oxidation can reduce the fatigue ductility coefficient εf′ by up to 40 % after 500 h exposure, which may be accounted for by a time‑dependent degradation factor Dox(t) = 1 ‑ 0.4 (t/500 h). In design, the TMF life is combined with high‑cycle fatigue (HCF) from vibration using a linear damage rule with a cycle‑counting algorithm (rainflow) on the resolved strain history at the critical location (usually the leading‑edge root). Examination problems may give a temperature‑time history (e.g., 850 °C → 950 °C → 850 °C in 30 s) and a mechanical strain history (sinusoidal, 0.5 % amplitude) with a specified phase angle, and ask for the SRP damage sum. The candidate must compute the inelastic strain ranges for each partition using the material’s cyclic stress‑strain curve and creep law, then apply the appropriate exponents.

Validation of Transonic Compressor CFD with Experimental Data

Modern axial compressors operate with relative Mach numbers up to 1.4 at the rotor tip, producing shock‑boundary‑layer interactions that are highly sensitive to turbulence‑model choice and grid resolution. Validation follows a hierarchical approach: (1) 2‑D cascade tests at design incidence provide pressure‑coefficient distributions Cp(x) and loss coefficients ζ; (2) rotating‑rig measurements give spanwise total‑pressure ratio and efficiency maps; (3) full‑engine tests supply overall pressure ratio and stall margin. For CFD, the Reynolds‑averaged Navier‑Stokes (RANS) equations are closed with the shear‑stress‑transport (SST) k‑ω model, which blends k‑ε in the free stream and k‑ω near walls. Grid convergence is demonstrated using the Grid Convergence Index (GCI) with at least three mesh densities (e.g., 2 M, 4 M, 8 M cells). A typical acceptance criterion is GCI < 1 % for the stage pressure ratio. Shock position is compared with Schlieren images; the predicted shock‑foot location should typically be within ±2 % of chord. The loss coefficient is decomposed into profile loss, shock loss, and end‑wall loss using the Denton correlation. If the CFD over‑predicts shock loss by more than 15 %, the mesh is refined in the shock‑capturing region (y+ < 1, with at least 30 points across the shock). Unsteady RANS (URANS) or scale‑adaptive simulation (SAS) may be required for rotating‑stall inception studies. Examiner questions often present a table of experimental vs. CFD stage pressure ratios at three operating points (design, near‑stall, choke) and ask the candidate to compute the GCI, identify the most likely source of discrepancy (e.g., tip‑clearance modeling, transition model), and propose a systematic mesh‑refinement plan.

  • Assuming ideal‑gas properties for real‑gas turbine expansion above 1200 K can underestimate entropy generation and over‑predict work output.
  • Using the Newtonian Reynolds equation for shear‑thinning lubricants, ignoring the (2n+1) film‑thickness exponent that alters pressure build‑up.
  • Neglecting residual‑stress intensity factors in weld‑toe fracture assessments, leading to non‑conservative allowable flaw sizes.
  • Applying isothermal fatigue curves to thermo‑mechanical fatigue problems without strain‑range partitioning can mis‑predict life by a factor of two or more.
  • Validating transonic compressor CFD with a single mesh density and no GCI analysis, masking numerical diffusion errors in shock capture.
  • Overlooking oxidation‑induced degradation of εf′ in high‑temperature TMF life calculations, particularly for hold times exceeding 10 % of the cycle period.
  • Using a single turbulence model (e.g., standard k‑ε) for shock‑boundary‑layer interaction without verifying wall‑y+ and shock‑resolution criteria.
