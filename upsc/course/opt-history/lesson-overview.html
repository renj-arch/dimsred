1. History Optional — Overview

History Optional covers ancient, medieval, modern India and world history. It has significant overlap with GS I (History, Art & Culture) and Essay. The subject is factual, chronological, and analytical — ideal for aspirants with a humanities background.

2. Paper I — Ancient & Medieval India

  • Prehistoric & Ancient India: Paleolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, Chalcolithic cultures; Indus Valley Civilization (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Lothal, Dholavira — town planning, drainage, trade, decline); Vedic period (Early & Later Vedic — society, polity, religion, economy); Jainism & Buddhism (Buddha, Mahavira, teachings, councils, spread); Mauryan Empire (Chandragupta, Bindusara, Ashoka — edicts, dhamma, administration); Post-Mauryan (Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians, Kushanas — Kanishka, Kanishka's council); Gupta Empire (Golden Age — Samudragupta, Chandragupta II, administration, art, literature, science); Post-Gupta (Vardhana dynasty — Harsha, Nalanda University).
  • Early Medieval India (750-1200 CE): Regional kingdoms (Palas, Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas, Cholas, Cheras, Pandyas); South Indian polities (Chola administration, local self-government); Arab conquest of Sindh (Muhammad bin Qasim 712 CE); Turko-Afghan invasions (Mahmud of Ghazni, Muhammad Ghori); Delhi Sultanate (Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi dynasties); Vijayanagara & Bahmani kingdoms.
  • Bhakti & Sufi movements: Bhakti saints (Ramanuja, Kabir, Nanak, Chaitanya, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Eknath, Tukaram); Sufi saints (Moinduddin Chishti, Nizamuddin Auliya, Fariduddin Ganjshakar); impact on society & culture.

3. Paper II — Modern India & World History

  • Modern India (1750-1947): European advent (Portuguese, Dutch, French, British); East India Company rule (Battle of Plassey 1757, Battle of Buxar 1764, Diwani rights 1765); Company administration (Regulating Act 1773, Pitt's India Act 1784, Charter Acts); land revenue settlements (Permanent Settlement 1793, Ryotwari, Mahalwari); Revolt of 1857 (causes, course, consequences); Indian National Congress (1885) & early phase; Swadeshi & Boycott (1905); Extremist phase; Home Rule Movement; Lucknow Pact (1916); Gandhian era (Non-Cooperation 1920-22, Civil Disobedience 1930-34, Quit India 1942); Constitutional developments (Morley-Minto 1909, Montagu-Chelmsford 1919, Simon Commission, Nehru Report, Round Table Conferences, Government of India Act 1935); Provincial autonomy & provincial elections (1937); Cripps Mission (1942); Wavell Plan (1945); Cabinet Mission (1946); Mountbatten Plan (1947); Indian Independence Act 1947; Partition & Partition violence; Integration of princely states (Sardar Patel); Constitution making (Constituent Assembly, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constitution adopted 26 Nov 1949, enforced 26 Jan 1950).
  • World History: Renaissance & Reformation (15th-16th centuries); American Revolution (1776); French Revolution (1789) — causes, phases, impact; Napoleonic Wars; Industrial Revolution (18th-19th century — causes, impact, spread); American Civil War (1861-65); Unification of Italy (1861) & Germany (1871); Russian Revolution (1917); World War I (1914-18) — causes, course, consequences, Treaty of Versailles; League of Nations; Russian Revolution (1917) — Bolshevik Revolution, Lenin, Stalin; Great Depression (1929); Rise of Fascism (Mussolini, Italy 1922) & Nazism (Hitler, Germany 1933); World War II (1939-45) — causes, course, consequences, Holocaust, atomic bombing; UNO (1945); Cold War (1947-1991) — Cold War origins, Cold War blocs (NATO, Warsaw Pact), Cold War conflicts (Korean War 1950-53, Vietnam War, Cuban Missile Crisis 1962), decolonization (India 1947, Indonesia 1949, Algeria 1962, African countries 1960s), Non-Aligned Movement (1961), end of Cold War (1989-91), collapse of USSR (1991), globalization.
Technique: Revolt of 1857 answer
Question: 'Examine the causes & nature of the Revolt of 1857.'
Answer: Causes: Political (Doctrine of Lapse, annexation of Awadh, disrespect to Mughal emperor), Economic (land revenue policies, destruction of handicrafts, drain of wealth), Military (greased cartridges, disparity in pay & promotion, discrimination), Social (interference in customs & traditions, Christian missionaries), Immediate cause (greased cartridges of Enfield rifle). Nature of Revolt: Debated — Sepoy Mutiny (British view), First War of Independence (Savarkar & nationalist view), combination of grievances without unified leadership, localized revolts without coordinated leadership. Consequences: End of Company rule (1858), Crown rule (Queen's Proclamation 1858), end of Doctrine of Lapse, reorganization of army (proportion of British troops increased), racial antagonism increased.

Mains strategy
History answers: chronology → causes → course → consequences → significance. Draw maps (extent of empires, battle sites, trade routes). Quote dates, rulers, battles, treaties, acts, commissions. Use source material (travelers' accounts — Megasthenes, Fa-Hien, Xuanzang, Al-Biruni, Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo; foreign travelers' accounts). Quote archaeological evidence (Harappan sites, Ashokan edicts, temple inscriptions, copper plates, coins). Quote literary sources (Vedas, Puranas, epics, Buddhist Jataka tales, Jain texts, court poet accounts — Banabhuta's Harshacharita, Kalhana's Rajatarangini).

4. Historiography: From Colonial Narrative to Subaltern Perspectives

The construction of historical narratives has never been neutral; it is deeply embedded in the political and social contexts of the time. For much of the 19th and early 20th centuries, Indian history was written largely by British administrators and Orientalists who viewed India through a lens of "decline" or as a static civilization waiting for European intervention. This colonial historiography often relied on official records—court cases, revenue reports, and military logs—which inherently marginalized the voices of the peasantry, women, and lower castes. The shift to modern historical consciousness began with the nationalist historians like R.C. Dutt, B.N. Mukherjee, and M.G. Ranade in the late 19th century, who sought to reclaim agency by highlighting indigenous economic strength and political sophistication before British annexation.

The post-independence era introduced Marxist historiography, emphasizing class struggle, modes of production, and the peasantry's role in agrarian revolts. Scholars like G.S. Sardesai and R.N. Dhar focused on regional histories to counter the centralizing narrative of Delhi-centric history. The most significant paradigm shift occurred with the Subaltern Studies group in the 1980s, led by Ranajit Guha, Dipesh Chakrabarty, and Gayatri Spivak. They argued that elite narratives (whether colonial or nationalist) obscured the agency of marginalized groups. This approach moved history from "great men" to social movements, gender relations, and everyday resistance.

Contemporary historiography now embraces global interconnectedness (connected histories), environmental history, and digital humanities. It questions the very sources used—asking how oral traditions, material culture, and non-textual artifacts can rewrite established timelines. For instance, the "history from below" approach utilizes village panchayat records, land deeds, and folk songs to reconstruct local power dynamics that official gazetteers ignored. A student must understand not just *what* happened, but *who wrote it*, *why they wrote it*, and *whose voices were silenced*. This critical lens is essential for answering questions on historiography in the UPSC exam, where one might be asked to compare colonial vs. nationalist interpretations of a specific event like the 1857 Revolt or the nature of the "Village Republic."

Examiner's trap
A common error is stating that all pre-independence historians were biased against India. Correct answer: Early Orientalists like James Prinsep and John Marshall were often anti-colonial in sentiment (e.g., Prinsep deciphering Brahmi to prove Indian antiquity), while later colonial officials wrote from an administrative, not necessarily hostile, perspective. Bias is structural, not just personal.
Exam-day strategy
When asked about sources, do not just list names; explain the *limitation* of each source. For example, when citing British records, mention that they are "official documents" which may lack local nuance or reflect imperial bias. When citing nationalist historians, note their potential romanticization of the past. Always balance the answer with a mention of newer methodologies like oral history or archaeology.

5. The Archaeological Record: Decoding Material Culture and Epigraphy

Ancient Indian history is primarily reconstructed through material remains and inscriptions, as written records are sparse before the 3rd century BCE. Epigraphy—the study of inscriptions—provides the most reliable chronological anchors. Ashokan edicts (c. 268–232 BCE) offer a standardized dating system based on regnal years from his accession in 273 BCE. These edicts, found across South Asia and beyond, use Brahmi script and Kharosthi characters, revealing the linguistic diversity of the Mauryan empire. Later dynasties like the Guptas (c. 320–550 CE) left inscriptions on copper plates and stone pillars that detail land grants (*agrahara*), temple construction, and royal patronage.

Archaeological sites provide physical evidence of urbanization, trade, and social stratification. The Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300–1300 BCE) is known through seals, pottery, and bricks found at Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, and Kalibangan. These sites reveal a planned urban layout with drainage systems, granaries, and standardized weights, suggesting a complex administrative system. However, the lack of deciphered long texts means we rely on iconography (e.g., the Pashupati seal) and comparative linguistics to interpret their culture. Similarly, the Nalanda and Taxila sites provide evidence of Buddhist monastic universities, while Ajanta and Ellora offer insights into artistic evolution and religious syncretism.

Coinage serves as another crucial source for economic and political history. Punch-marked coins (c. 600–400 BCE) indicate early trade networks, while the Mauryan punch-marked coins and later Gupta gold dinars reflect monetary systems and state control over currency. The transition from punch-marked to struck coins under the Kushanas and Guptas marks a shift in numismatic technology and statecraft. Artifacts like terracotta figurines, pottery shards (Kamarajar's pottery), and tools found at excavation sites help reconstruct daily life, diet, and craft specialization. For instance, the discovery of iron slag at various sites confirms the widespread use of iron during the Iron Age, challenging earlier assumptions about bronze dominance.

7. The Pashupati Seal: Iconography, Linguistics, and the Limits of Decipherment

The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) presents a unique historiographical challenge: a highly urbanized society with standardized weights, bricks, and script that remains largely "mute" regarding its own self-identification. The primary iconographic evidence comes from seals, particularly the one known as the "Pashupati Seal" found at Mohenjo-daro. This artifact is critical because it challenges early colonial interpretations of IVC as a purely proto-Aryan or non-religious culture.

The seal depicts a seated figure in a yogic posture, surrounded by animals including tigers, rhinoceroses, elephants, and buffalo. The central figure wears a horned headdress (often interpreted as a triple crescent or horns) and is flanked by what appear to be attendants. For decades, the consensus was that this represented a proto-Shiva figure, linking the Indus Valley religion directly to later Hinduism. However, modern scholarship emphasizes caution. The "Pashupati" interpretation relies heavily on comparative iconography with later Vedic and Puranic texts (like the *Shiva Purana*), which were composed centuries after the IVC period. While the seal is consistent with a pan-regional cult involving animal worship and asceticism, asserting it as a definitive proof of "proto-Shiva" requires bridging a chronological gap that textual evidence cannot fill.

Linguistically, the script remains undeciphered. The seal bears an inscription in the Indus script (Brahmi precursor). Unlike Ashokan edicts where the language is Sanskrit Prakrit and the script is Brahmi, IVC seals lack long texts that allow for grammatical analysis. We have only short inscriptions (2–5 symbols) on seals and pottery. This limits our ability to confirm whether the language was Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, or a distinct isolate like Elamite. The presence of Harappan seals in Mesopotamia suggests trade, but without decipherment, we cannot read the diplomatic correspondence or commercial contracts that might have been exchanged. Thus, the Pashupati Seal serves not as a definitive theological statement, but as a testament to a complex, syncretic religious landscape that predates the Vedic pantheon.

The Chronological Anchor: From Mohenjo-daro to Rakhigarhi

Understanding the timeline of IVC is crucial for Paper I questions. The civilization flourished roughly between 3300 BCE and 1300 BCE, with a decline phase starting around 1900 BCE. Recent excavations at Rakhigarhi (Haryana) have revealed that the site covers an area of over 50 hectares, suggesting it was one of the largest settlements in the region, comparable to Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. The discovery of cremation burials at Rakhigarhi challenges earlier assumptions based on burial practices at Harappa, indicating regional variation in funerary rites.

The decline of IVC is often attributed to environmental changes rather than Aryan invasion. Evidence suggests a shift in the Ghaggar-Hakra river system (often identified with the Saraswati River in texts), leading to desertification and forcing populations to migrate eastward towards the Ganges plain, where the later Vedic culture emerged. This "migration theory" is supported by archaeological data showing a gradual cultural transition rather than a sudden destruction layer.

Examiner's trap
Question: "Shiva was definitely worshipped in the Indus Valley because of the Pashupati Seal."
Solution: This is an overstatement. While the seal depicts a figure in yogic posture surrounded by animals, direct textual evidence linking it to the Vedic god Shiva is absent. The correct answer should qualify the statement: "The Pashupati Seal provides iconographic evidence of a proto-Shiva-like deity or ascetic worship, suggesting continuity with later Hindu traditions, though definitive identification as 'Shiva' relies on later theological texts and comparative analysis." Always distinguish between material evidence (the seal) and interpretative frameworks (Vedic theology).
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Undeciphered Scripts
When a question asks about the IVC script, do not claim it is "Sanskrit" or "Dravidian." Instead, state: "The Indus script remains undeciphered. While some scholars propose Dravidian links based on place names (e.g., Harappa, Lothal), others suggest Indo-Aryan connections. The consensus is that we cannot confirm the language family without a bilingual text like Rosetta Stone. Focus on the *function* of the script: likely administrative, used for trade labels or seals, rather than literary composition."

8. Epigraphic Chronology: From Ashokan Edicts to Gupta Copper Plates

For Medieval India (Paper I), inscriptions are the primary source for dating and verifying dynastic claims. The Ashokan edicts (c. 268–232 BCE) provide the first standardized chronological system in South Asia, using regnal years from Ashoka's accession in 273 BCE. These edicts were issued in Prakrit languages (using Brahmi script in the east and Kharosthi in the northwest), reflecting linguistic diversity within the Mauryan empire.

The transition to later dynasties reveals a shift in medium and content. The Guptas (c. 320–550 CE) left inscriptions on copper plates (*prashasti*) detailing land grants (*agrahara*), temple construction, and royal patronage. These records are vital for understanding the emergence of the *Brahmadeya* system (land grants to Brahmins), which laid the groundwork for feudalism in India. Unlike Ashokan edicts which were proclamations of policy, Gupta inscriptions often served as legal deeds, recording disputes, donations, and genealogies.

A critical distinction in epigraphy is between *royal* inscriptions (commissioned by kings to glorify their reign) and *donor* inscriptions (by commoners or local elites). The latter, found in temple complexes like those at Ellora or the Deccan, reveal the agency of non-royal groups. For instance, the *Chaturvedins* or local chieftains often commissioned smaller shrines, indicating a decentralized religious economy even under imperial rule.

The Role of Numismatics: Coins as Historical Sources

Coinage serves as another crucial source for economic and political history. Punch-marked coins (c. 600–400 BCE) indicate early trade networks, while the Mauryan punch-marked coins and later Gupta gold dinars reflect monetary systems and state control over currency. The transition from punch-marked to struck coins under the Kushanas and Guptas marks a shift in numismatic technology and statecraft.

Gupta coins are particularly valuable because they bear legends in Sanskrit (e.g., *Lichchhavi*, *Gupta*) alongside Greek and Kharosthi scripts, suggesting a multilingual administrative apparatus. The reverse side often features symbols like the lion (Sanskrit: *Simha*), bull (*Vrishabha*), or Garuda (*Garuda*), which serve as dynastic emblems. These symbols are not merely decorative; they functioned as brand marks for state authority.

Examiner's trap
Question: "Did the Gupta Empire issue silver coins?"
Solution: No, the Guptas primarily issued gold *dinars* and copper *pannasa*. Silver was rare in their circulation. This is a common misconception because later Mughal and British coins were largely silver. Correct answer: The Gupta economy relied heavily on gold for high-value transactions and copper for small change; silver coins are not characteristic of the Gupta period.

Historiographical Schools and Their Methodological Implications for UPSC Answers

9. Major Schools and Core Premises

Understanding the historiographical lineage is essential because examiners often test the ability to identify the methodological lens behind a given interpretation. The principal schools relevant to Ancient and Medieval India are:

  • Colonial/Imperialist – Emphasised political unity under British rule, portrayed Indian history as a succession of invasions, and relied heavily on Persian chronicles and European travelogues. Key figures: James Mill, Vincent Smith.
  • Nationalist – Reacted against colonial narratives by stressing indigenous cultural continuity, the “golden age” of the Guptas, and the role of dynastic legitimacy. Representative works: R. C. Majumdar’s Ancient India, K. A. Nilakanta Sastri’s A History of South India.
  • Marxist/Structuralist – Focused on mode of production, class formation, and feudalism debates (e.g., D. D. Kosambi, R. S. Sharma). Introduced the concept of “Indian feudalism” and analysed land grants (brahmadeya, devadana) as economic instruments.
  • Subaltern Studies – Shifted attention to peasant uprisings, tribal movements, and the agency of non‑elite groups (Ranajit Guha, Shahid Amin). In medieval context, highlighted the 1857 revolt as a subaltern rebellion rather than a sepoy mutiny.
  • Revisionist/Post‑colonial – Critiqued both nationalist and Marxist meta‑narratives, stressing regional diversity, gender, and environmental history (Romila Thapar, Upinder Singh).

10. Examiner Traps: Historiographical Schools

Trap: Questions may present a statement such as “The Gupta period represents a uniform golden age across the subcontinent.” The correct answer requires naming the nationalist school that propagated this view and contrasting it with Marxist and revisionist critiques that point to regional variations and socio‑economic stratification.

11. Worked Example: Identifying the Lens

Prompt: “The land‑grant inscriptions of the early medieval period reflect a feudal mode of production.”

Step‑by‑step:

  1. Recognise the keyword “feudal mode of production” → Marxist terminology (R. S. Sharma, Indian Feudalism).
  2. Note the source type: copper‑plate grants (brahmadeya, agrahara).
  3. Contrast with nationalist view (land grants as religious merit) and subaltern view (grants as tools of peasant subordination).
  4. Answer: The statement reflects the Marxist/structuralist school, specifically the “Indian feudalism” thesis.

12. Key References for Answer Writing

  • Romila Thapar, The Past Before Us – historiography overview.
  • R. S. Sharma, Indian Feudalism – Marxist framework.
  • Ranajit Guha (ed.), Subaltern Studies – subaltern methodology.
  • Upinder Singh, A History of Ancient and Early Medieval India – revisionist synthesis.

Primary Archaeological Sources: Stratigraphy, Typology, and Chronology Building

13. Stratigraphic Principles in Indian Context

Archaeological excavation in India follows the Harris Matrix for context recording. The principle of superposition allows relative dating: lower layers are older unless disturbed. Key sites illustrating clean stratigraphy include:

  • Harappa (Mature Harappan, c. 2600–1900 BCE) – distinct citadel‑lower town division.
  • Dholavira (Gujarat) – water‑management reservoirs sealed by successive occupational layers.
  • Kaushambi (Uttar Pradesh) – continuous sequence from Painted Grey Ware (PGW) to Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) to early historic.

15. Absolute Dating Techniques

  • Radiocarbon (C‑14) – applied to charcoal from hearths at Rakhigarhi (c. 2500 BCE). Calibration using IntCal20 curve.
  • Thermoluminescence (TL) – used for fired bricks at Lothal dockyard.
  • Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL) – dates sediment layers at Dholavira reservoirs.

16. Examiner Traps: Relative Chronology

Trap: A question may ask “Which ware marks the transition from the Vedic to the Mahajanapada period?” The correct answer is NBPW, not PGW. PGW is associated with the later Vedic; NBPW coincides with the rise of urban centres and the Mahajanapadas.

17. Worked Example: Building a Relative Chronology

Scenario: An excavation at a site in the Ganga‑Yamuna doab yields three successive layers: (1) PGW sherds, (2) NBPW sherds with iron slag, (3) RPW sherds with Roman amphora fragments.

Interpretation:

  1. Layer 1 → Late Vedic (c. 1000–600 BCE).
  2. Layer 2 → Early historic, Mahajanapada/early Mauryan (c. 600–200 BCE); iron indicates technological shift.
  3. Layer 3 → Early historic trade contact (c. 200 BCE–300 CE); Roman amphorae confirm Indo‑Roman trade.

Thus the site provides a micro‑stratigraphic sequence mirroring the macro‑regional cultural transition.

19. Language Evolution and Decipherment

  • Brahmi → Nagari: The transition is traceable through the “Kushan” and “Gupta” Brahmi variants. James Prinsep’s 1837 breakthrough used the bilingual (Greek‑Brahmi) coins of the Indo‑Greeks.
  • Kharosthi: Derived from Aramaic; limited to north‑western regions. Deciphered by Christian Lassen and Prinsep using the bilingual coins of the Indo‑Scythians.
  • Tamil‑Brahmi: Early Tamil inscriptions (2nd c. BCE–3rd c. CE) show a distinct vowel‑marker system. Decipherment aided by the Tolkāppiyam grammatical tradition.
  • Grantha & Vatteluttu: Used for Sanskrit and Tamil respectively in the deep south; crucial for Chola and Pandya epigraphy.

20. Examiner Traps: Epigraphic Dating

Trap: “The Junagadh rock inscription of Rudradaman is written in Sanskrit using the Brahmi script.” The inscription is in Sanskrit but the script is Western Kshatrapa Brahmi, a regional variant. Examiners may test awareness of script variants versus a monolithic “Brahmi”.

21. Worked Example: Decoding a Gupta Copper Plate

Plate: Banskhera Copper Plate of Harsha (c. 630 CE).

  1. Identify script: Early Nagari (post‑Gupta).
  2. Language: Classical Sanskrit, prose‑verse (prashasti + grant).
  3. Structure: Invocation → Genealogy → Eulogy (prashasti) → Grant details (village, boundaries, donee) → Imprecatory verse.
  4. Key data extracted: Donor: Harshavardhana; Donee: Brahmin Bhatta; Village: Kanyakubja; Date: Harsha era year 22 (corresponds to 630 CE using the Harsha epoch of 606 CE).

Such step‑wise decoding is a frequent “source‑based” question in Paper I.

23. Methodologies for Numismatic Analysis

  • Die‑study – establishes mint output, chronology, and possible co‑circulation.
  • Metallurgical assay (XRF, LA‑ICP‑MS) – reveals bullion sources (e.g., Roman gold in Kushan dinaras).
  • Hoard analysis – spatial distribution of hoards maps trade routes (e.g., Roman coin hoards in South India).
  • Iconographic decoding – identifies religious policy (e.g., Kanishka’s Buddha vs. Shiva on reverse).

24. Examiner Traps: Numismatic Interpretation

Trap: “The Gupta gold coinage was the first in India to carry a legend in Sanskrit.” Earlier, the Kushan coins carried legends in Bactrian (Greek script) and the Satavahana lead coins used Prakrit in Brahmi. The Gupta series standardised Sanskrit in Brahmi/Nagari, and it was the first to carry Sanskrit legends.

25. Worked Example: Interpreting a Hoard

Hoard: Kottayam (Kerala) Roman coin hoard, 1990 – 112 aurei of Augustus to Septimius Severus.

  1. Date range of coins → 27 BCE–211 CE.
  2. Absence of later Roman issues → deposition likely early 3rd c. CE.
  3. Context: proximity to Muziris (identified with Pattanam) → confirms Indo‑Roman maritime trade described in Periplus of the Erythraean Sea.
  4. Economic inference: high‑value gold indicates elite exchange, not daily market currency.

26. Indigenous Literary Corpus

  • Vedic Samhitas, Brahmanas, Upanishads – oral composition, fixed by śruti tradition; dating via internal astronomical references (e.g., Rigveda 1.161.13 – winter solstice in Magha).
  • Epics (Mahabharata, Ramayana) – layered texts; critical edition (Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute) separates core from later accretions.
  • Puranas – dynastic lists (Vamshanucharita) useful for genealogies but contain mythic material; Matsya Purana and Vayu Purana relatively early.
  • Dharmashastras (Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya) – prescriptive, reflect Brahmanical norms; date via commentarial tradition (Medhatithi, 9th c. CE).
  • Sangam Literature (Tamil) – anthologies (Ettuthokai, Pattuppattu); internal references to Roman trade (Yavana) and Chera/Chola/Pandya chiefs.
  • Jain and Buddhist Canons (Pali Tipitaka, Ardhamagadhi Agamas) – provide socio‑economic data (e.g., Vinaya Pitaka on monastic economy).
