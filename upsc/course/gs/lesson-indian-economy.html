Key Concepts

Indian Economy for UPSC

Indian Economy is a vital subject for UPSC Prelims (GS Paper I) and Mains (GS Paper III). The syllabus covers macroeconomics (GDP, inflation, monetary/fiscal policy), microeconomics (markets, pricing), Indian economic development (planning, reforms, poverty, inequality), banking and finance, taxation, external sector, and contemporary issues (digital economy, financial inclusion, climate finance). UPSC increasingly asks application-based questions linking economic concepts with current policy debates.

Key Concepts

National Income Accounting: GDP (Gross Domestic Product) — total value of final goods and services produced within India's territory. GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA). NNP = GNP - Depreciation. GDP at Market Price vs Factor Cost — MP includes net indirect taxes (subsidies - indirect taxes). GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP/Real GDP) × 100. GVA (Gross Value Added) at basic prices — since 2015, India uses GVA as primary measure. Base year: 2011-12 (revised from 2004-05). Methods of measurement: Product/Output method (value of output), Income method (factor payments — rent, wages, interest, profit), Expenditure method (C+I+G+X-M). CSO (Central Statistics Office) under MoSPI compiles national accounts. Recent trends: India became 5th largest economy (overtook UK in 2022, now 5th behind US, China, Germany, Japan). Growth projections: IMF World Economic Outlook 2024 projects India at ~7% growth in FY25. Key challenges — low manufacturing share (~17% of GDP), stagnant agricultural growth, employment elasticity of growth declining.

Inflation: Sustained increase in general price level. Measured by WPI (Wholesale Price Index — base 2011-12, Commodity basket: Primary 22.6%, Fuel 13.2%, Manufactured 64.2%) and CPI (Consumer Price Index — base 2012=100, three series: CPI-IW for industrial workers, CPI-AL for agricultural labour, CPI-RL for rural labour; combined CPI for headline inflation since 2014). CPI is used by RBI for Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) inflation targeting. Inflation target: 4% ± 2% (i.e., 2-6% range) as per the amended RBI Act 1934 (2016 amendment). Types: Demand-pull (excess aggregate demand), Cost-push (supply shocks — oil prices, food prices), Structural (supply bottlenecks, hoarding), Core inflation (excluding food & fuel — measures underlying trend). Stagflation (high inflation + low growth) — 1970s oil crisis, risk for India post-Ukraine war. Phillips Curve — inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment (short run); long-run vertical at natural rate of unemployment. India's inflation management — RBI uses repo rate (6.50% as of Dec 2024), CRR (4.5%), SLR (18%). Open Market Operations (OMOs).

Banking & Financial System: RBI — central bank (est. 1935, nationalised 1949). Functions: monetary authority, issuer of currency, banker to government and banks, regulator of payment systems, foreign exchange management (FEMA 1999). Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) — 6 members (3 from RBI + 3 external), Governor has casting vote. Repo Rate (RBI lends to banks), Reverse Repo Rate (RBI borrows from banks), Standing Deposit Facility (SDF — 2018, uncollateralised), MSF (Marginal Standing Facility — banks borrow overnight above SLR). CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio — % of deposits kept with RBI, no interest). SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio — % kept in approved securities). Bank Rate — rate at which RBI gives long-term loans. Commercial Banks — Public Sector (SBI, PNB, etc.), Private Sector (HDFC, ICICI, Axis), Foreign. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs). Cooperative Banks. Payment Banks (Airtel, Paytm, India Post). Small Finance Banks (AU, Equitas, Ujjivan, Jana). Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) — shadow banking, regulated by RBI. IBC (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016) — resolution of stressed assets, NCLT process, 330-day timeline. Bad loans/GNPA — peak 2019 (~11%), reduced to ~3% (Sep 2024) through IBC, ARC, recapitalisation. Basel-III norms — capital adequacy (CAR ≥ 11.5%), CRAR, LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio). Financial inclusion — PM Jan Dhan Yojana (over 50 crore accounts), Aadhaar-enabled payments, UPI (Payments Corporation of India), digital lending, Account Aggregator framework. RBI's Digital Rupee (CBDC — e₹, retail pilot 2022).

Fiscal Policy & Budget: Government budget — Art 112 (Annual Financial Statement). Revenue Budget (Revenue Receipts — tax + non-tax revenue vs Revenue Expenditure — interest payments, subsidies, salaries). Capital Budget (Capital Receipts — borrowings, disinvestment, loan recoveries vs Capital Expenditure — infrastructure, investment, loans). Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure - Total Receipts excluding borrowings (gap met by borrowing). Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure - Revenue Receipts (indicates consumption vs saving). Effective Revenue Deficit (since 2011 — excludes grants for capital creation). Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments (indicates borrowing beyond interest). FRBM Act 2003 — targets: fiscal deficit ≤ 3% of GDP, revenue deficit zero, effective revenue deficit zero, debt-GDP ≤ 40% centre, 20% states. Escape clause (FRBM 2018 amendment) allows 0.5% extra during national security / calamity / structural reforms. Recent fiscal trends: deficit expanded during COVID (~9.3% of GDP in FY21), consolidation path — target 4.5% in FY26. 15th Finance Commission (2021-26). GST (Goods & Services Tax 2017) — One Nation One Tax (CGST+SGST+IGST). GST Council (Art 279A). Tax revenue: Direct (Income Tax, Corporate Tax) ~53%, Indirect (GST 23%, Customs, Excise) ~47%. Direct tax GDP ratio ~6%, low by international standards. Tax devolution to states — 41% as per 15th FC. Disinvestment — CPSEs, LIC IPO (2022, largest IPO in India). National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP 2022-25) — ₹6 lakh crore from assets.

External Sector: Balance of Payments (BoP) — Current Account (trade balance + invisibles) and Capital Account (FDI, FPI, loans, banking capital, NRI deposits). India's current account deficit (CAD) — typically 1-3% of GDP. Exports — petroleum products, gems & jewellery, pharma, textiles, software services, agri products. Imports — crude oil, electronic goods, gold, machinery, chemicals. Trade deficit with China (~$85 billion in 2024). Foreign exchange reserves ~$650 billion (Dec 2024) — gives 11+ months of import cover. Capital flows: FDI (Foreign Direct Investment — stable, long-term) — top sources: Singapore, Mauritius, USA, UAE. Major sectors: Services, Computer, Trading, Construction, Automobiles. FPI (Foreign Portfolio Investment — volatile, debt + equity). External debt — ~19% of GDP (low risk). Exchange rate management — RBI intervenes in forex market to manage volatility (managed float). INR vs USD — depreciated from ~₹45 (2007) to ~₹84 (2024). Rupee internationalisation — task force report, bilateral trade in INR (with Russia, UAE, Sri Lanka, Mauritius).

Economic Reforms (LPG 1991): Background — BoP crisis 1991, foreign reserves ~$1 billion, inflation ~17%. Narasimha Rao govt + Manmohan Singh (FM). Liberalisation — delicensing of industries (industrial licensing abolished except for 5 sectors), deregulation, removal of MRTP Act restrictions. Privatisation — disinvestment of PSUs, strategic sale, Navratna/Mini-Ratna status, private entry in banking/insurance/telecom/aviation. Globalisation — tariff reduction (peak rate from 300% to ~10%), removal of quantitative restrictions, current account convertibility (1994), capital account liberalisation (gradual), WTO membership (1995). Post-reforms growth — GDP growth doubled from ~3.5% (Hindu growth rate) to 6-7% average. Services sector grew fastest (IT, finance, telecom). Infrastructure — Golden Quadrilateral (2001-13), NHDP, Sagarmala, Bharatmala, Dedicated Freight Corridors. Make in India (2014), Production Linked Incentive (PLI) 2020 — 14 sectors, ₹1.97 lakh crore outlay. Gati Shakti (2021) — National Master Plan for multimodal connectivity. National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP) — ₹111 lakh crore. India's demographic dividend — median age 28, 65% below 35, working age population rising until 2055. However — labour force participation rate low (~40% for women, ~76% for men). Employment challenge — 1 crore/year new entrants, formal sector only ~10% of total employment. Gig economy, platform work growing.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Prelims 2023)
Consider the following statements about the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC):
1. It is chaired by the Finance Minister.
2. It determines the policy repo rate.
3. It has 6 members.

Which of the above statements are correct?

a) 1 and 2 only b) 2 and 3 only c) 1 and 3 only d) 1, 2 and 3
Solution: b) 2 and 3 only. The MPC (established 2016, amended RBI Act 1934) has 6 members: 3 from RBI (Governor — chairperson, Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, one officer nominated by RBI Board) and 3 external members appointed by the central government. The Finance Minister does NOT chair it — the RBI Governor chairs. The MPC determines the policy repo rate to achieve the inflation target (4% ± 2%). Decisions are by majority vote, with Governor having a casting vote in case of a tie. So statement 1 is wrong, 2 and 3 are correct.
Example 2 (Mains 2022)
Explain the concept of 'Fiscal Deficit' and discuss its implications for economic growth and inflation. What measures has the government taken to control the fiscal deficit in recent years?
Solution: Fiscal deficit = Total expenditure minus total receipts excluding borrowings. It represents the government's borrowing requirement to bridge the gap between spending and revenue. Implications for economic growth: (1) Positive — borrowing for capital expenditure (infrastructure, education, health) boosts aggregate demand, creates productive assets, and generates future growth (productive debt). (2) Negative — excessive borrowing crowds out private investment (increases interest rates, reduces availability of credit for private sector); revenue deficit (borrowing for consumption) does not create future repayment capacity; high debt-GDP ratio leads to intergenerational burden and reduces fiscal space for future crises. Implications for inflation: excessive fiscal deficit leads to monetisation (RBI printing money to finance deficit) → demand-pull inflation. Government borrowing also creates upward pressure on interest rates, which can be inflationary if it raises cost of capital. Recent measures: FRBM Act 2003 with fiscal consolidation roadmap; 2018 amendment allowed flexibility with escape clause; GST implementation improved tax compliance and revenue; DBT (direct benefit transfer) reduced leakages in subsidies; rationalisation of subsidies (PAHAL for LPG, Ujjwala); disinvestment and monetisation (NMP); nominal GDP growth exceeding 10% helped debt-GDP ratio moderate; target of fiscal deficit 4.5% of GDP by FY26 under the revised consolidation plan announced in Budget 2021. The government's emphasis on capex (50% increase in FY24 budget, ₹11.11 lakh crore capex in FY25) aims to balance growth with consolidation.
Example 3 (Prelims 2021)
Consider the following statements about the Balance of Payments (BoP):
1. The Current Account records exports and imports of goods and services, and transfer payments.
2. The Capital Account records foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, and loans.
3. A BoP surplus necessarily means a Current Account surplus.

Which of the above are correct?

a) 1 and 2 only b) 1 and 3 only c) 2 and 3 only d) 1, 2 and 3
Solution: a) 1 and 2 only. The Current Account includes merchandise trade (exports/imports), invisibles (services, transfers, income), and unilateral transfers (remittances, gifts). The Capital Account includes FDI, FPI, ECB, external assistance, NRI deposits, banking capital — all cross-border capital transactions. Statement 3 is incorrect: a BoP surplus can arise from a Capital Account surplus offsetting a Current Account deficit (CAD). India typically runs a CAD but has a capital account surplus from FDI, FPI, and NRI inflows, resulting in an overall BoP surplus (or deficit depending on net capital flows). For example, in many years India has CAD of 1-3% of GDP but overall BoP surplus due to strong capital inflows.
