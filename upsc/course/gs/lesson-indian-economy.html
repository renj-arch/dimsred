1. National Income Accounting

1.1 Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within the geographical boundaries of a country during a given period (usually a year). It is the most widely used measure of economic activity. GDP was first conceptualised by Simon Kuznets in 1934 for a US Congress report. In India, the Central Statistics Office (CSO) under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) is responsible for compiling national accounts.

GDP can be measured at Market Prices or Factor Cost. GDP at Market Price includes net indirect taxes (indirect taxes minus subsidies). GDP at Factor Cost measures the income earned by factors of production (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship). The relationship is:

Formula
GDP at Market Price = GDP at Factor Cost + Net Indirect Taxes
Net Indirect Taxes = Indirect Taxes − Subsidies

1.2 Gross National Product (GNP) and Net National Product (NNP)

GNP measures the total value of goods and services produced by the residents of a country, regardless of where the production takes place. The difference between GDP and GNP arises from Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA):

Formula
GNP = GDP + NFIA
Where NFIA = Factor income earned by residents from abroad − Factor income earned by non-residents within the country

NNP is obtained by subtracting depreciation (capital consumption allowance) from GNP:

Formula
NNP = GNP − Depreciation
NNP at Factor Cost = National Income
Key Concept: UPSC Angle
NNP at Factor Cost is also called National Income. It represents the income that factors of production actually earn. India switched from GNP to GDP as the primary measure in line with international norms under SNA 2008.

1.3 Gross Value Added (GVA) — India's Primary Measure Since 2015

Since January 2015, India uses Gross Value Added (GVA) at Basic Prices as the primary measure of economic output. GVA measures the value of output minus intermediate consumption, representing the contribution of each sector to the economy.

Formula
GVA at Basic Prices = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption
GDP at Market Prices = Sum of GVA of All Sectors + Product Taxes − Product Subsidies

GVA is sector-specific. The three broad sectors are:

  • Agriculture, Forestry & Fishing (~17.7% of GVA)
  • Industry (~27.6% of GVA) — includes mining, manufacturing, construction, electricity
  • Services (~54.7% of GVA) — includes trade, hotels, transport, communication, financial services, real estate, public administration

1.4 Methods of Measurement

Three approaches yield the same GDP figure theoretically:

Product (Output) Method

Sum of value added across all sectors of the economy. GDP = Gross Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption. This is the most direct method. India primarily uses the product method for the manufacturing sector and the income method for the services sector.

Income Method

Sum of factor incomes: GDP = Compensation of Employees + Gross Operating Surplus + Gross Mixed Income + Net Taxes on Production and Imports. Factor incomes include rent (land), wages (labour), interest (capital), and profit (entrepreneurship).

Expenditure Method

Sum of all expenditures on final goods and services:

Formula
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
Where:
C = Private Final Consumption Expenditure (~56.3% of GDP)
I = Gross Fixed Capital Formation + Change in Stocks (~33.5% of GDP)
G = Government Final Consumption Expenditure (~9.3% of GDP)
X − M = Net Exports (Exports − Imports)

1.5 GDP Deflator

The GDP Deflator is the broadest measure of inflation in the economy. It reflects changes in prices of all domestically produced goods and services (unlike CPI which covers only consumer goods).

Formula
GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100

Nominal GDP is valued at current market prices. Real GDP is valued at constant base-year prices. India's current base year is 2011-12 (revised from 2004-05 in January 2015). The base year is periodically updated to reflect structural changes in the economy.

1.6 Base Year 2011-12 and CSO/MoSPI

The base year of 2011-12 was adopted for GDP and IIP (Index of Industrial Production) to align with the changes in the economy. The CSO (Central Statistics Office), headed by the Chief Statistician of India, compiles national accounts under the oversight of MoSPI. The CSO classifies economic activities according to the National Industrial Classification (NIC).

1.7 Personal, Private, and Disposable Income

Personal Income is the total income received by individuals and households from all sources, including factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit), transfer payments (pensions, scholarships, social security benefits), and other current transfers. It excludes corporate retained earnings and includes income from property and entrepreneurship. Private Income is the sum of factor income from domestic product accruing to the private sector plus net factor income from abroad plus all current transfers from the government and rest of the world. Personal Disposable Income (PDI) is personal income minus direct taxes (income tax, wealth tax) and miscellaneous government fees. PDI represents the income available for consumption and saving. National Disposable Income (NDI) is NNP at market prices plus net current transfers from abroad (including remittances, grants, gifts). India's NDI is higher than NNP due to large inward remittances (~$120B).

1.8 Circular Flow of Income

The circular flow of income is a model showing the flow of payments between households and firms in a closed economy. In the two-sector model, households supply factor services to firms and receive factor payments (rent, wages, interest, profit), which they spend on goods and services produced by firms. In the four-sector model (households, firms, government, external sector), the flows include government spending, taxation, exports, and imports. The circular flow demonstrates that one person's expenditure is another's income — the basis for the three methods of GDP measurement (product, income, expenditure). Leakages (savings, taxes, imports) and injections (investment, government spending, exports) must balance for equilibrium. The multiplier effect describes how an initial change in spending leads to a larger change in national income.

1.9 GDP Revisions and Measurement Challenges

India's GDP series underwent a major revision in January 2015 with the shift from GDP at Factor Cost to GDP at Market Prices as the primary measure, adoption of the 2011-12 base year, and the introduction of GVA at Basic Prices. The National Statistical Commission (NSC) had recommended these changes to align with international standards (UN SNA 2008). The revision also changed the data sources and methodology — manufacturing estimates shifted from the Annual Survey of Industries (ASI) to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA21) database, which captured a larger set of companies. This led to a sharp upward revision of GDP growth for 2013-14 from 4.7% (old series) to 6.9% (new series), sparking controversy. Critics argued the new methodology overstated growth by including more formal sector data while the informal sector — still a large part of the economy — was inadequately captured. The informal sector accounts for ~45% of GDP and ~90% of employment. The CSO uses the National Sample Survey (NSS) and the Economic Census to estimate the unorganised sector, but data lags reduce accuracy. In November 2025, the IMF gave India's economic data a 'C' grade, citing that national accounts and inflation data do not accurately record the informal sector and consumption patterns, leading to concerns that GDP growth may be overstated by 0.5–1.0 percentage points annually.

1.10 Green Accounting and Alternative Measures

Green GDP adjusts traditional GDP for environmental costs — deducting the value of natural resource depletion (minerals, forests, water) and environmental degradation (pollution, biodiversity loss). The UN System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) provides the framework. India has piloted green GDP estimates through the TERI (The Energy and Resources Institute) and the Ministry of Statistics. Other alternative welfare measures include: Human Development Index (HDI) — combines income, health, and education (India ranked 134th in 2024); Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI) — adjusts GDP for income distribution, environmental costs, and unpaid work; Gross National Happiness (GNH) — pioneered by Bhutan, measuring psychological wellbeing, health, education, cultural diversity, ecological resilience, and living standards; Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) — captures deprivations in health, education, and living standards. India's MPI fell from 55% (2005-06) to 25% (2019-21), reflecting significant improvements in basic amenities.

1.8 Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) vs Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP uses current market exchange rates for international comparison. PPP GDP adjusts for differences in cost of living across countries. India's PPP GDP is significantly higher because the same rupee buys more goods and services domestically than its exchange-rate value would suggest. PPP is better for comparing living standards; nominal GDP is better for comparing the size of economies in international markets.

1.9 Growth Trends

India's GDP growth averaged ~7% in the 2000s. Post-COVID, India recovered strongly with growth rates of 9.1% (FY22), 7.2% (FY23), 8.0% (FY24), and estimated 7.6% (FY25). The IMF World Economic Outlook (April 2025) projected India's growth at ~6.5% for 2026. Key drivers: domestic consumption (70% of GDP), services exports, digital economy (~11.7% of GDP), and manufacturing under PLI schemes.

1.10 Limitations of GDP as a Welfare Measure

  • Non-market transactions: Unpaid domestic work, volunteer services, and barter are excluded.
  • Environmental degradation: GDP counts spending on cleaning up pollution as positive output but does not deduct the cost of environmental damage.
  • Quality of life: GDP does not measure leisure, health, education quality, or income distribution.
  • Black economy: Illegal activities (drugs, smuggling) and unreported income are not captured.
  • Gini coefficient blind: GDP per capita can rise while the majority of citizens see no improvement if income is skewed.
  • Broken window fallacy: Spending on disaster recovery adds to GDP but does not represent net welfare gain.

2. Inflation and Price Indices

2.1 Definition and Types of Inflation

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services over a period of time. It erodes the real purchasing power of money. In India, inflation is measured primarily through the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and the Wholesale Price Index (WPI).

Types by Rate of Inflation

  • Walking Inflation: 2-4% per year — mild and manageable.
  • Galloping Inflation: 10-20% or higher — severe economic distortions.
  • Hyperinflation: >50% per month — complete loss of confidence in currency (e.g., Zimbabwe 2008, Germany 1923).

Types by Cause

  • Demand-Pull Inflation: Excess aggregate demand — "too much money chasing too few goods." Caused by rising consumption, government spending, or money supply expansion.
  • Cost-Push Inflation: Rising input costs (wages, raw materials, energy) passed on to consumers. The 1970s oil crisis is a classic example.
  • Structural Inflation: Arises from supply-side bottlenecks, inadequate infrastructure, inefficient distribution systems.
  • Imported Inflation: Rise in prices of imported goods, especially crude oil, due to global price increases or rupee depreciation.

2.2 Wholesale Price Index (WPI)

The WPI measures the changes in prices at the wholesale level — prices of goods traded between businesses. It is compiled by the Office of the Economic Adviser, Ministry of Commerce and Industry. The current base year is 2011-12.

2.3 Producer Price Index (PPI) — The Missing Index

Unlike most developed economies that use the Producer Price Index (PPI) as a key inflation measure, India does not yet have an official PPI. PPI measures price changes from the perspective of domestic producers, covering both goods and services at the first commercial transaction point. It differs from WPI in that it excludes inter-firm transactions, excludes indirect taxes, and includes services. The Ministry of Statistics has been working on a PPI since 2017 but its release has been delayed due to data gaps in services pricing and the need for a comprehensive sampling frame. A PPI would help India better track input cost pressures and provide a more complete picture of inflation along the supply chain. The IMF has recommended India develop a PPI to align with international statistical standards.

2.4 Index Number Theory — Construction of Price Indices

Price indices are constructed using different mathematical formulas. The Laspeyres Index uses a fixed base-period quantity basket and measures the change in cost of purchasing that same basket in the current period. Both CPI and WPI in India are Laspeyres-type indices. The Paasche Index uses current-period quantities, reflecting changes in consumption patterns but requiring more up-to-date data. The Fisher Ideal Index is the geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche indices and is considered the "ideal" index by economists as it accounts for both base and current period quantities. India uses Laspeyres indices because they are easier to compute with fixed weights, but they tend to overstate inflation (substitution bias — consumers shift away from goods whose prices rise fastest). The base year is periodically updated (most recently to 2012 for CPI and 2011-12 for WPI) to refresh the consumption basket and weights. The CPI basket (2012 base) comprises 448 items: Food & Beverages (45.9%), Pan, Tobacco & Intoxicants (2.4%), Clothing & Footwear (6.5%), Housing (10.1%), Fuel & Light (6.8%), Miscellaneous (28.3% — health, education, transport, communication, recreation). Rural and urban CPI baskets differ in weights: food has higher weight in rural (54.2%) than urban (36.3%), while housing is only in urban (21.7%).

2.5 Consumer Price Index (CPI)

CPI measures changes in prices of goods and services consumed by households. Since April 2014, CPI (Combined) is the official measure of inflation for monetary policy purposes in India. It is compiled by the CSO/MoSPI. The base year is 2012.

Series of CPI in India

  • CPI-IW (Industrial Workers): Base 2001, revised 2016. Used for calculating Dearness Allowance (DA) for government employees and wage indexation.
  • CPI-AL (Agricultural Labourers): Base 1986-87.
  • CPI-RL (Rural Labourers): Base 1986-87.
  • CPI-C (Combined): Base 2012 — covers both rural and urban areas. Used for inflation targeting by the Monetary Policy Committee.

2.4 GDP Deflator — The Broadest Measure

The GDP Deflator is the ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP multiplied by 100. Unlike WPI and CPI, it covers all domestically produced goods and services, including capital goods and government services. It does not rely on a fixed basket; the basket automatically changes as the composition of GDP changes. Hence, it is the most comprehensive inflation measure but is available only quarterly with a lag.

2.5 Core Inflation vs Headline Inflation

Headline Inflation includes all items in the CPI basket, including volatile food and fuel prices. Core Inflation excludes food and energy prices to capture the underlying trend. The RBI monitors core inflation to gauge demand-side pressures. Food has a large weight (~46% in CPI) in India, making headline volatile.

2.6 Causes of Inflation in India

  • Food Prices: Food has the highest weight in CPI. Erratic monsoons, hoarding, inefficient supply chains, and MSP hikes drive food inflation.
  • Minimum Support Price (MSP): Government-mandated increases in MSP for crops create a price floor and push up food prices.
  • Crude Oil: India imports ~85% of its oil needs. Global crude price shocks directly impact WPI and CPI (through transportation and fuel costs).
  • Supply Chain Bottlenecks: Inadequate storage, transport, and logistics lead to wastage and price spikes.
  • Fiscal Deficit: Large fiscal deficits monetised by the RBI increase money supply, fueling demand-pull inflation.
  • Money Supply: Excessive growth in M3 (broad money) relative to output growth is inflationary.

2.7 Effects of Inflation

  • Savers lose: Fixed deposits and cash holdings lose real value.
  • Borrowers gain: Real value of debt decreases (if wages rise with inflation).
  • Fixed-income groups suffer: Pensioners, wage earners with sticky nominal wages.
  • Exports become competitive: Moderate inflation can make exports cheaper in foreign markets (if exchange rate adjusts).
  • Uncertainty: High inflation discourages long-term investment.
  • Menu costs: Cost of changing prices frequently.
  • Shoe-leather costs: People hold less cash, incurring transaction costs.

2.8 Phillips Curve

The Phillips Curve depicts an inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment in the short run. In the long run, the curve is vertical at the NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment), implying no trade-off. In India, the Phillips curve is relatively flat due to structural factors — changes in demand have limited impact on prices given supply-side constraints.

2.9 Stagflation, Disinflation, and Deflation

  • Stagflation: High inflation + high unemployment + stagnant growth. India experienced stagflation-like conditions in the 1970s.
  • Disinflation: A decrease in the rate of inflation (prices still rising, but slower).
  • Deflation: Negative inflation — prices falling. Deflation is dangerous because it leads to deferred consumption, falling output, and rising real debt burdens.

2.10 Inflation Trends (2022-24)

India's CPI inflation peaked at 7.79% in April 2022, remaining above the RBI's upper tolerance band of 6% for much of 2022-23. It moderated through 2023, averaging ~5.4% in FY24. WPI inflation turned negative in April-July 2023 (disinflation/deflation in wholesale prices), while CPI remained sticky due to food prices. By early 2024, CPI eased to ~4.85%. Food inflation remains volatile due to vegetable price spikes, cereal prices, and pulses.

2.11 Inflation Targeting Framework

India adopted Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) in 2016 through the amendment of the RBI Act, 1934. Key features:

  • Target: CPI inflation of 4% with a tolerance band of ±2% (i.e., 2% to 6%).
  • Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): 6 members — 3 from RBI (Governor + 2 others) + 3 external members appointed by the government. Meets bi-monthly. Decisions by majority; Governor has casting vote.
  • Accountability: If inflation exceeds 6% or falls below 2% for three consecutive quarters, the RBI must submit a report to the government explaining reasons, remedial actions, and timeline.
  • The framework is valid until 31 March 2026.

3. Monetary Policy

3.1 Reserve Bank of India — Establishment and History

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) was established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, based on the recommendations of the Hilton Young Commission (1926). It was originally a privately-owned shareholders' bank with a paid-up capital of ₹5 crore. The RBI was nationalised on 1 January 1949 and has since been fully owned by the Government of India. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar's book "The Problem of the Rupee" influenced the establishment of the central bank.

3.2 Functions of the RBI

Monetary Authority

The RBI formulates, implements, and monitors monetary policy with the objective of maintaining price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth (as per the amended RBI Act, 1934).

Regulator of the Banking System

The RBI regulates and supervises commercial banks, cooperative banks, NBFCs, and financial institutions under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 and the RBI Act, 1934. It prescribes capital adequacy norms (Basel III), conducts inspections, and enforces prudential norms.

Issuer of Currency

Under Section 22 of the RBI Act, the RBI has the sole right to issue banknotes in India. The Government of India issues the ₹1 note and all coins. The RBI follows the Minimum Reserve System — it must maintain a minimum reserve of ₹200 crore (₹115 crore in gold + ₹85 crore in foreign securities) against the total currency issued.

Banker to Government

The RBI acts as banker, debt manager, and financial advisor to the central and state governments. It manages their accounts, floats loans, and provides Ways and Means Advances (WMA) — temporary loans to bridge revenue-expenditure mismatches.

Banker to Banks (Lender of Last Resort)

The RBI maintains accounts of all scheduled commercial banks and provides them emergency advances against government securities when they face liquidity shortages. This function gives the RBI its character as the "lender of last resort."

Foreign Exchange Management

The RBI manages foreign exchange reserves and regulates foreign exchange transactions under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999. It intervenes in the forex market to prevent excessive volatility in the rupee exchange rate.

Developmental Role

The RBI promotes financial inclusion, priority sector lending (agriculture, MSMEs, education, housing), digital payments, and the development of financial markets. It established institutions like NABARD, NHB, SIDBI, and DFHI.

3.3 Instruments of Monetary Policy

Quantitative Instruments

Bank Rate: The rate at which the RBI lends to commercial banks for long-term (typically 90 days or more). It is also used as the penal rate for default in CRR/SLR maintenance. Currently 6.25%.

Repo Rate: The rate at which the RBI lends short-term funds (overnight) to commercial banks against government securities. When banks need funds, they sell securities to RBI with an agreement to repurchase them. Repo rate is the key policy rate. Currently 6.0%.

Reverse Repo Rate: The rate at which the RBI borrows from commercial banks by selling securities with a repurchase agreement. It is the absorption rate — the floor of the LAF corridor. Currently 3.35%.

Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): The portion of a bank's Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that must be held with the RBI as cash reserves. Currently 4%. No interest is paid on CRR balances since 2007. CRR restricts the lending capacity of banks.

Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR): The portion of NDTL that banks must maintain as liquid assets (gold, cash, government securities). Currently 18%. SLR ensures solvency and provides funds for government borrowing.

Marginal Standing Facility (MSF): A penal facility introduced in 2011 under which banks can borrow overnight up to 2% of their NDTL at a rate 25 bps above the repo rate. It provides a safety valve for unanticipated liquidity shocks. Currently 6.25%.

Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF): The framework through which the RBI manages day-to-day liquidity. It operates through repo (injection) and reverse repo (absorption) auctions. The LAF corridor is defined by MSF (upper bound) and Reverse Repo (lower bound), with the Repo Rate at the centre.

Open Market Operations (OMO): The buying and selling of government securities by the RBI in the open market to inject or absorb liquidity. OMOs are used for permanent liquidity management, unlike LAF which is for short-term.

Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS): Introduced in 2004, MSS allows the RBI to issue additional government securities to absorb excess liquidity (especially from forex inflows) without affecting the government's borrowing programme. The funds raised are held in a separate account.

Priority Sector Lending (PSL)

The RBI mandates that all scheduled commercial banks lend a specified portion of their Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) or Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure (CEOBE) to priority sectors. Current targets (as of 2024): Total PSL target: 40% of ANBC. Sub-targets: Agriculture — 18% (with 8% to small and marginal farmers); Micro Enterprises — 7.5% (under 60% of MSME target); Weaker Sections — 12% (including small/marginal farmers, SC/ST, women, minorities, PDS shops). Education loans up to ₹20 lakh and housing loans up to ₹35 lakh (rural) / ₹50 lakh (urban) qualify. PSL certificates allow banks to trade priority sector obligations. Priority Sector Lending (PSL) has been the cornerstone of directed credit in India since bank nationalisation (1969). It has expanded credit access but also contributed to NPA accumulation due to political pressure and inadequate credit assessment. Foreign banks with 20+ branches must meet the 40% target. The priority sector classification has been periodically revised — the Nayak Committee (2015) recommended changes, and recent additions include renewable energy, startups, and social infrastructure.

BASEL III Implementation Timeline in India

India adopted BASEL III norms from 1 April 2019, with full implementation by 2022. Key milestones in India's BASEL journey:

  • 1992-93: Narasimham Committee I recommendations implemented — India adopted BASEL I norms with a CRAR of 8%.
  • 1998-99: Narasimham Committee II recommendations — CRAR raised to 9% (India maintained this higher floor beyond BASEL's 8%).
  • 2008-09: BASEL II implemented in India with a phased approach (Pillar 1 by March 2008, Pillars 2 and 3 by March 2009). India introduced Standardised Approach for credit risk and Basic Indicator Approach for operational risk.
  • 2014-15: RBI issued draft guidelines for BASEL III implementation with a phased schedule from 2014 to 2019. Implementation was staggered to avoid disrupting credit growth.
  • 2019-20: BASEL III fully implemented — capital conservation buffer of 2.5% phased in. Countercyclical capital buffer kept at 0%.
  • 2023-24: RBI issued guidelines for BASEL III final reforms (output floor, credit valuation adjustment, operational risk standardised approach) with implementation from April 2024 onwards. The output floor ensures banks' risk-weighted assets calculated under internal models do not fall below 72.5% of those under the standardised approach.

Qualitative (Selective) Instruments

  • Margin Requirements: Minimum margin for loans against specific securities.
  • Credit Rationing: Ceilings on the amount of credit for particular purposes.
  • Moral Suasion: Persuasion through meetings, letters, and advisories.
  • Direct Action: RBI can issue directions to banks, impose penalties, or supersede management.
  • Credit Authorization Scheme (CAS): Mandatory approval before extending large loans (phased out in post-1991 reforms).

3.4 Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

The MPC was constituted in 2016 by amending the RBI Act, 1934 through the Finance Act, 2016. The MPC determines the policy repo rate to achieve the inflation target.

Composition (6 members):

  • Governor of RBI — Chairperson, ex-officio (Sanjay Malhotra)
  • Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy (Poonam Gupta)
  • Executive Director in charge of monetary policy (Indranil Bhattacharyya)
  • 3 external members appointed by the central government (Ram Singh, Saugata Bhattacharya, Nagesh Kumar)

Key features:

  • Meets bi-monthly (at least 4 times a year)
  • Decisions by majority vote; Governor has casting vote in case of a tie
  • 7-day "silent period" before and after decisions
  • Minutes published after each meeting
  • Target: 4% CPI inflation ± 2%

3.6 Repo Rate Transmission and History

Transmission mechanism: Repo rate change → short-term money market rates → bank deposit and lending rates → aggregate demand → inflation. Transmission has historically been weak in India due to rigid deposit rates, high NPAs, and administered small savings rates. The introduction of External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR) in 2019 has improved transmission.

Recent movements (2022-24):

  • Repo rate was 4.00% during COVID (May 2020 to May 2022).
  • RBI hiked repo rate by 250 bps from May 2022 to Feb 2023 (4.00% → 6.50%) to combat inflation.
  • Repo rate remained at 6.50% through 2023-24.
  • First cut of 25 bps in April 2025 to 6.25%, followed by another 25 bps cut (now at 6.00% as of mid-2025).

3.7 RBI's Balance Sheet

The RBI's balance sheet includes assets (foreign exchange reserves, government securities, gold) and liabilities (currency issued, deposits of banks and government). As of March 2026, total assets exceeded ₹80 lakh crore. The RBI transfers its surplus (profits) to the government as dividend — a significant non-tax revenue source.

3.8 Liquidity Management

The RBI manages systemic liquidity through LAF operations. When there is a liquidity surplus, banks park funds with RBI via reverse repo. When there is a deficit, banks borrow via repo. The RBI uses OMOs and MSS for durable liquidity management. The Weighted Average Call Rate (WACR) is the operating target, kept close to the repo rate.

4. Fiscal Policy and Budget

4.1 Union Budget Structure

The Union Budget (presented by the Finance Minister in February) is a statement of the government's estimated receipts and expenditures for the financial year (1 April to 31 March). It is divided into Revenue Budget and Capital Budget.

Revenue Budget

Revenue Receipts: Tax Revenue (income tax, corporate tax, GST, customs, excise) + Non-Tax Revenue (dividends from PSUs, RBI surplus, fees, licences). These are recurring and do not create liabilities.

Revenue Expenditure: Expenditure that does not create assets — salaries, subsidies, interest payments, defence revenue expenditure, grants to states.

Capital Budget

Capital Receipts: Loans raised from the market (borrowings), disinvestment proceeds, recovery of loans, small savings. These create liabilities or reduce assets.

Capital Expenditure: Expenditure that creates assets — infrastructure, machinery, investment in PSUs, loans to states and UTs.

4.2 Key Deficits

Revenue Deficit

Formula
Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts

Indicates the gap that must be covered by borrowing or capital receipts for consumption purposes. A high revenue deficit suggests the government is borrowing to fund current consumption rather than capital formation.

Fiscal Deficit

Formula
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts (excluding borrowings)
Or: Fiscal Deficit = Borrowings + Other Liabilities

This is the most watched fiscal indicator. It represents the total borrowing requirement of the government. The fiscal deficit must be financed by market borrowings, external loans, or drawing down cash balances.

Primary Deficit

Formula
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Net Interest Payments

Indicates the government's borrowing requirement excluding interest payments on past debt. A primary surplus means the government is reducing its debt burden.

Effective Revenue Deficit (ERD)

Formula
ERD = Revenue Deficit − Grants for Creation of Capital Assets

Introduced in the 2011-12 budget to better capture the revenue deficit by excluding grants that go towards capital creation.

4.3 FRBM Act, 2003

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 was enacted to ensure fiscal discipline and inter-generational equity. Key provisions:

  • Original targets: Reduce fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP by 2008 (missed due to 2008 global financial crisis).
  • Eliminate revenue deficit by 2008.
  • Prohibits the RBI from buying government bonds in the primary market (ending automatic monetisation of deficit).

NK Singh Committee (2017)

The FRBM Review Committee under NK Singh recommended:

  • Fiscal deficit target of 3% of GDP by FY20 and 2.5% by FY23.
  • General government debt (Centre + States) to be brought down to 60% of GDP (Centre: 40%, States: 20%).
  • Escape clause: 0.5% deviation allowed for national security, national calamity, or structural reforms.
  • A Fiscal Council to be established for independent oversight.
Current Status
India's fiscal deficit has exceeded FRBM targets post-COVID. FY25-26 fiscal deficit target is 4.5% of GDP, with the government aiming to reach 3.0% by FY28-29. The FRBM targets have been relaxed multiple times.

4.4 Government Accounts

Consolidated Fund of India: All government revenues, loans raised, and recoveries of loans go into this fund. All government expenditure (except for certain specific items) is incurred from this fund. Parliamentary approval is mandatory for withdrawal.

Contingency Fund of India: A standing fund of ₹500 crore at the disposal of the President to meet unforeseen expenditure. Parliament subsequently approves the expenditure through a supplementary grant.

Public Account of India: Holds funds where the government is a banker — provident funds, small savings, security deposits, etc. These are returnable to the depositors. Withdrawal does not require parliamentary approval.

4.5 Taxation

Direct Taxes

  • Income Tax: Levied on individual and corporate incomes under the Income Tax Act, 1961. Progressive structure.
  • Corporate Tax: Levied on company profits. Reduced from 30% to 22% (15% for new manufacturing units) in 2019.
  • Capital Gains Tax: Levied on profits from sale of capital assets. Short-term vs long-term classification based on holding period.
  • Wealth Tax: Abolished in 2015, replaced by a surcharge on the super-rich.

Indirect Taxes

  • Customs Duty: Levied on imports under the Customs Act, 1962.
  • Excise Duty: Levied on manufacture of goods (most subsumed under GST).
  • GST: Goods and Services Tax — a unified indirect tax.
  • Service Tax: Merged into GST.

Tax Concepts

  • Progressive Tax: Higher tax rate for higher income (e.g., income tax).
  • Regressive Tax: Lower burden on higher income groups (e.g., flat-rate sales tax).
  • Proportional Tax: Same rate regardless of income.

Cannons of Taxation (Adam Smith)

  • Equity: Taxes should be fair and based on ability to pay.
  • Certainty: Time, manner, and amount of payment should be clear.
  • Convenience: Method and timing should be convenient for the taxpayer.
  • Economy: Cost of collection should be low relative to yield.

Laffer Curve

The Laffer Curve illustrates the relationship between tax rates and tax revenue. At very high tax rates, economic activity declines and revenue falls. There exists an optimal tax rate that maximises revenue without disincentivising production.

4.6 Tax-to-GDP Ratio and Buoyancy

India's tax-to-GDP ratio (combined Centre + States) is approximately 11-12%, which is low compared to the OECD average of ~34% and the developing country average of ~18%. The central government's direct tax-to-GDP ratio is ~6.5% and indirect tax-to-GDP is ~5%. Tax buoyancy measures the responsiveness of tax revenue to GDP growth. A buoyancy >1 implies tax revenue grows faster than GDP. India's tax buoyancy has improved post-GST (estimated at ~1.2-1.3 for GST revenue). Tax elasticity measures the automatic response of tax revenue to GDP changes (excluding discretionary policy changes). India's tax-GDP ratio has remained stagnant despite high GDP growth, indicating a narrow tax base — only ~6-7% of the population pays income tax. The government's focus on widening the tax base includes the faceless assessment scheme, e-invoicing, and tighter reporting requirements. The direct tax code (proposed in 2010 but not enacted) sought to simplify and consolidate direct tax laws. Corporate tax reforms in 2019 — reduction from 30% to 22% (with 15% for new manufacturing) — temporarily reduced the corporate tax-to-GDP ratio but aimed to boost investment and long-term revenue.

4.7 Goods and Services Tax (GST)

GST was introduced through the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 and implemented on 1 July 2017 at a historic midnight session of Parliament. It subsumed 17 central and state taxes (including excise duty, service tax, VAT, octroi, entry tax, luxury tax, entertainment tax) into a unified system, creating a common national market for the first time. The GST is a destination-based consumption tax, unlike the earlier origin-based system — revenue accrues to the state where goods/services are consumed, not produced.

Structure:

  • CGST: Central GST — levied by the Centre on intra-state supplies.
  • SGST: State GST — levied by the State on intra-state supplies.
  • IGST: Integrated GST — levied on inter-state supplies, collected by the Centre and apportioned to the destination state. IGST ensures seamless inter-state trade through a mechanism of input tax credit transfer across states.

GST Council: Constitutional body (Article 279A) chaired by the Union Finance Minister with State Finance Ministers as members. It makes recommendations on GST rates, exemptions, model laws, and special provisions. Decisions require 3/4th majority (Centre has 1/3 vote, States 2/3). The GST Council has met over 50 times since its inception, demonstrating cooperative federalism in action. Key decisions include rate revisions, composition scheme thresholds, e-way bill rules, and the launch of e-invoicing.

Rate Structure: 0% (essentials — food grains, fresh vegetables, milk, education), 5% (mass consumption — packaged food, medicines, coal), 12% (standard rate — processed food, computers, mobile phones), 18% (general rate — most goods and services), 28% (luxury/demerit goods — cars, tobacco, aerated drinks, sin goods). A compensation cess (levied on luxury and sin goods) was originally guaranteed to compensate states for revenue loss for 5 years (2017-2022). Due to the pandemic, compensation was extended through March 2026 via a special borrowing mechanism — the central government borrowed ₹2.69 lakh crore from the market and passed it to states as back-to-back loans. The cess is also used to repay the borrowing. The compensation mechanism ensures that state revenues grow at 14% per annum from the base year (2015-16).

GST Network (GSTN): GSTN is a non-government, private limited company (51% held by the central and state governments, 49% by financial institutions — HDFC, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, NSE, LIC Housing Finance). It provides the IT backbone for GST — registration, return filing, e-invoicing, e-way bill generation, and taxpayer analytics. GSTN processes ~100 lakh returns per month. The introduction of e-invoicing (2020) for businesses with turnover above ₹5 crore (now ₹5 crore threshold from August 2023) enables real-time reporting to the GST portal and has improved tax compliance. The GST Composition Scheme allows small businesses (turnover up to ₹1.5 crore, ₹75 lakh for special category states) to pay tax at a flat rate (1-6%) with minimal compliance. The reverse charge mechanism shifts the tax payment liability from supplier to receiver in specified cases (e.g., unregistered dealers supplying to registered entities).

4.7 Budget Documents and Process

Key Budget Documents:

  • Annual Financial Statement: Article 112 — shows estimated receipts and expenditure for the year.
  • Demands for Grants: Ministry-wise expenditure proposals; voted by Lok Sabha.
  • Finance Bill: Contains tax proposals (direct and indirect). A Money Bill (Article 110).
  • Appropriation Bill: Authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund.
  • Macro-Economic Framework Statement: Explains prospects and outlook.
  • Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement: Explains fiscal stance and rationale.
  • Medium-Term Expenditure Framework Statement: 3-year rolling expenditure plan.
  • Outcome Budget: Tracks physical outcomes of budget schemes.

Economic Survey: Presented a day before the Budget (since 1964). Prepared by the Chief Economic Adviser (CEA) in the Ministry of Finance. It reviews economic performance and provides the macroeconomic outlook.

Finance Bill vs Money Bill: A Money Bill (Article 110) deals only with taxation, government expenditure, and borrowing. It can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha; the Rajya Sabha can only recommend amendments within 14 days. The Finance Bill may contain provisions that are not exclusively money matters.

Types of Grants

  • Supplementary Grant: When additional expenditure is needed beyond the budgeted amount.
  • Excess Grant: For expenditure incurred in excess of the amount granted (regularised by Parliament).
  • Additional Grant: For new services not contemplated in the budget.
  • Vote on Account: Interim approval for expenditure when the full budget is delayed. Typically for 2-4 months before general elections.

Budget-Making Process

  1. September-October: Budget circular issued to ministries.
  2. November-December: Pre-budget consultations with stakeholders.
  3. January: Finalisation of receipts and expenditure estimates.
  4. Late January: Economic Survey tabled.
  5. 1 February: Finance Minister presents the Budget in Lok Sabha.
  6. February-March: Parliamentary discussions and voting on Demands for Grants.
  7. 31 March: Appropriation Bill and Finance Bill passed — budget enacted.

5. Banking and Financial System

Commercial Banks in India

India's banking system is broadly classified into scheduled and non-scheduled banks. Scheduled banks are those included under the 2nd Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934. They are further classified into Public Sector Banks (PSBs), Private Sector Banks, Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), Foreign Banks, and Co-operative Banks.

History of Banking in India

Banking in India has ancient roots — references to money lending and deposits appear in Vedic texts and Kautilya's Arthashastra (4th century BCE). The Hundi and Chitti instruments were used for trade finance in medieval India. The modern banking system began with the establishment of the Bank of Hindustan (1770, dissolved 1832) and the General Bank of India (1786). The Presidency Banks — Bank of Bengal (1809), Bank of Bombay (1840), and Bank of Madras (1843) — were established under charters from the British East India Company and served as quasi-central banks. They were amalgamated in 1921 to form the Imperial Bank of India, which was nationalised in 1955 to become the State Bank of India (SBI). The Allahabad Bank (est. 1865) is the oldest surviving joint stock bank in India. The Punjab National Bank (est. 1894) was the first bank wholly managed by Indians. The Swadeshi Movement (1905-1911) led to the establishment of many Indian-owned banks, including Bank of India, Central Bank of India, Canara Bank, Indian Bank, and Bank of Baroda. The Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 established India's central bank, which began operations on 1 April 1935. The Banking Regulation Act, 1949 empowered the RBI to regulate all banks. The landmark nationalisation of 14 major banks in 1969 (followed by 6 more in 1980) brought ~91% of banking under government control, prioritizing rural credit and priority sector lending. The post-1991 reforms under Narasimham Committees I and II deregulated interest rates, reduced SLR/CRR, allowed private sector banks (HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank), and introduced prudential norms aligned with BASEL standards.

Public Sector Banks (PSBs)

After the mega-merger of 10 PSBs into 4 in 2019, the number of PSBs reduced from 27 to 12. The mergers were:

  • Punjab National Bank (anchor) + Oriental Bank of Commerce + United Bank of India — now the 2nd largest PSB
  • Canara Bank (anchor) + Syndicate Bank — 4th largest PSB
  • Union Bank of India (anchor) + Andhra Bank + Corporation Bank — 5th largest PSB
  • Indian Bank (anchor) + Allahabad Bank — 7th largest PSB
  • Bank of Baroda + Dena Bank + Vijaya Bank (2019) — 3rd largest PSB
  • SBI merged with its 5 associate banks + Bharatiya Mahila Bank (2017) — now the single largest PSB

The remaining PSBs include: Bank of Maharashtra, Indian Overseas Bank, UCO Bank, Punjab & Sind Bank, and IDBI Bank (now classified as a private sector bank after LIC's majority stake).

Private Sector Banks

Major private sector banks include: HDFC Bank (merged with parent HDFC Ltd in 2023), ICICI Bank, Axis Bank (formerly UTI Bank), Kotak Mahindra Bank, Yes Bank (rescued by RBI in 2020 via SBI-led consortium), IDFC First Bank, IndusInd Bank, Federal Bank, South Indian Bank, Bandhan Bank, and DCB Bank.

Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)

RRBs were established under the RRB Act 1976 to serve rural areas. They are jointly owned by the Central Government (50%), the concerned State Government (15%), and the sponsor bank (35%). With the 2010 policy revamp, RRBs were merged into state-level entities, effectively becoming subsidiary banks of their sponsor PSBs. As of 2024, there are 43 RRBs in India.

Co-operative Banks

Co-operative banks are classified into Urban Co-operative Banks (UCBs) and Rural Co-operative Banks. Rural cooperatives have a three-tier structure: State Co-operative Banks (StCBs) at the apex, District Central Co-operative Banks (DCCBs) at the district level, and Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) at the village level. UCBs are regulated by RBI, while rural cooperatives are under dual regulation (RBI + State Governments via Registrar of Co-operative Societies). In 2020, the Banking Regulation Act was amended to bring all co-operative banks under RBI's direct supervision.

Key Issue
The PMC Bank crisis (2019) involving a ₹4,355 crore fraud led to depositor distress, prompting the RBI to tighten co-operative bank regulation through the 2020 amendments.

Payment Banks

The RBI conceptualised Payment Banks in 2014 to further financial inclusion. They can accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh per customer, offer current/savings accounts, issue ATM/debit cards, and provide net-banking and mobile-banking, but cannot lend or issue credit cards. Operating payment banks include: Airtel Payments Bank, India Post Payments Bank, Fino Payments Bank, and Jio Payments Bank.

Small Finance Banks (SFBs)

SFBs were licensed in 2016 to serve the unbanked and under-banked. They must open 25% of branches in unbanked rural areas, hold 75% of net credit in priority sector lending, and 50% of loans must be below ₹25 lakh. SFBs include: AU Small Finance Bank (received in-principle approval for universal bank status in 2025), Equitas SFB, Ujjivan SFB, ESAF SFB, Suryoday SFB, Utkarsh SFB, and Jana SFB. In 2024, AU SFB merged with Fincare SFB and NE SFB was acquired by Slice (digital banking unit).

Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs)

NBFCs are financial institutions registered under the Companies Act, 1956/2013, engaged in lending, acquisition of shares/stocks/bonds, and similar activities. They cannot accept demand deposits (with some exceptions) and are not part of the payment and settlement system. Types include:

  • Asset Finance Companies (AFC): e.g., Shriram Transport Finance
  • Investment Companies (IC): Primarily deal in securities
  • Loan Companies (LC): e.g., Bajaj Finance, Muthoot Finance
  • Infrastructure Finance Companies (IFC): e.g., IDFC, PFC
  • Systemically Important Core Investment Companies (CIC-ND-SI)
  • Housing Finance Companies (HFCs): Regulated by NHB, e.g., HDFC (now merged with HDFC Bank), LIC Housing Finance, DHFL (now Piramal Capital)
  • Microfinance Institutions (MFIs)
UPSC Tip
The distinction between NBFCs and banks: NBFCs cannot accept demand deposits, do not form part of the payment and settlement system, and deposit insurance (DICGC) does not apply to them.

Banking Regulation

The Banking Regulation Act, 1949 is the primary legislation governing banking in India. It empowers the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to regulate, control, and inspect banks. The RBI was established in 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, and nationalised in 1949. The Narasimham Committee I (1991) and Narasimham Committee II (1998) recommended major financial sector reforms including reduction of SLR/CRR, deregulation of interest rates, and strengthening of prudential norms.

BASEL Norms

BASEL norms are international banking supervision standards issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) under the Bank for International Settlements (BIS).

India's Adoption of BASEL III

India adopted BASEL III norms from 1 April 2019 (fully phased in by 2022). Current requirements:

  • Minimum CRAR (Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio): 9% (higher than BASEL's 8%)
  • Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB): 2.5%
  • Total CRAR + CCB: 11.5%
  • CET1: 5.5% (plus 2.5% CCB = 8%)
  • Leverage Ratio: Minimum 4%
  • LCR: 100% (as of April 2020)
  • NSFR: 100% (from April 2021)

Non-Performing Asset (NPA) Crisis

An NPA is a loan or advance where the borrower has stopped making interest or principal payments for 90 days. Classification:

  • Substandard Assets: NPA for up to 12 months
  • Doubtful Assets: NPA for more than 12 months
  • Loss Assets: Identified as uncollectible by RBI or the bank's auditors

GNPA ratio peaked at 11.2% in March 2018 (the "twin balance sheet" problem) and has since declined to ~3% (March 2024) due to IBC resolutions, improved recovery, and provisioning. PCR (Provision Coverage Ratio) has improved to over 75%.

Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework

The RBI's PCA framework imposes restrictions on banks breaching certain thresholds for CRAR, NPA ratio, and ROA. Restrictions include limits on branching, dividends, management compensation, and lending. Banks placed under PCA include IDBI Bank, Indian Overseas Bank, UCO Bank, and Bank of Maharashtra at various times.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016

The IBC consolidated fragmented insolvency laws into a single time-bound resolution framework. Key features:

  • Time-bound resolution: 330 days maximum (including litigation) for Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)
  • Adjudicating Authority: National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) for companies; Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT) for individuals/partnerships
  • Committee of Creditors (CoC): Financial creditors vote on resolution plans (requires 66% approval)
  • Resolution Professional (RP): Manages the corporate debtor during CIRP; board of directors suspended
  • Moratorium: Imposed on admission — bars suits, asset transfers, enforcement of security interests
  • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI): Regulator overseeing insolvency professionals and processes
  • Liquidation Waterfall: Proceeds distributed: (1) insolvency resolution costs, (2) secured creditors, (3) employee dues (up to 12 months), (4) unsecured creditors, (5) government dues, (6) remaining shareholders
Landmark IBC Cases
Essar Steel (2019): ₹49,000 crore debt; ArcelorMittal-Nippon JV acquired for ₹42,000 crore. SC upheld the 330-day timeline and the primacy of CoC.

Bhushan Steel (2018): First of the 12 major NPA accounts (RBI's first list); acquired by Tata Steel for ₹36,400 crore.

Jet Airways (2020): Resolution plan by Jalan-Kalrock consortium remained mired in litigation.

DHFL (2021): First HFC referred to IBC by RBI; acquired by Piramal Group for ₹34,250 crore.

DICGC (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation)

DICGC provides insurance cover on bank deposits. In 2020, the cover was increased from ₹1 lakh to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank (covering 98% of accounts). Premium paid by banks. Insurance applies to all commercial banks, RRBs, local area banks, and co-operative banks (since 2020 amendments).

Financial Inclusion

Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY)

Launched on 28 August 2014, PMJDY aims to provide universal access to banking. Key achievements:

  • Over 50 crore accounts opened (as of August 2023)
  • Total deposits exceed ₹2 lakh crore
  • RuPay debit cards issued to all account holders with in-built accident insurance cover of ₹2 lakh
  • Overdraft facility of ₹10,000 available after satisfactory operation of account for 6 months

  • PM Mudra Yojana (2015): Loans up to ₹10 lakh to non-corporate, non-farm small/micro enterprises under three categories: Shishu (up to ₹50,000), Kishor (₹50,000–₹5 lakh), Tarun (₹5–10 lakh)

Digital Payments

UPI (Unified Payments Interface): Launched in 2016 by NPCI, UPI has revolutionised digital payments in India. As of 2024, monthly transactions exceed 11 billion. Key platforms include BHIM (Bharat Interface for Money), PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm, CRED, Amazon Pay, and BharatPe.

Payment Systems

  • NEFT (National Electronic Funds Transfer): Now operates 24×7, settles in half-hourly batches
  • RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement): Real-time settlement for large-value transactions (no upper limit), 24×7 since 2020
  • IMPS (Immediate Payment Service): Instant 24×7 interbank electronic funds transfer
  • NACH (National Automated Clearing House): Bulk transactions for DBT, dividends, etc.

It has enabled fintech growth and financial inclusion at unprecedented scale.

6. Indian Economic Development

Pre-Colonial and Colonial Era

India's share of world GDP was approximately 32% in 1700 (pre-colonial Mughal era), which declined to just 4% by 1947. This decline is attributed to colonial exploitation including:

  • Drain of Wealth: Systematic transfer of wealth from India to Britain via tribute, home charges, and remittances (Dadabhai Naoroji's theory)
  • De-industrialisation: Destruction of India's textile and handicraft industry due to Britain's industrial revolution and discriminatory tariffs
  • Commercialisation of Agriculture: Shift from food crops to cash crops (indigo, opium, cotton, jute) for export, leading to famines (over 30 major famines between 1770-1943)
  • India's per capita income stagnated or declined from 1900-1947

Post-Independence (1947-1990): The Nehru-Mahalanobis Model

Independent India adopted a mixed economy framework with centralised planning. The Industrial Policy Resolution, 1948 and 1956 reserved key industries for the public sector.

NITI Aayog was established under the chairmanship of the Prime Minister with the aim of fostering cooperative federalism through a bottom-up approach, replacing the top-down planning model. Key differences from the Planning Commission: (i) NITI Aayog is not a statutory body — it was created by executive resolution and acts as a think tank, unlike the Planning Commission which derived its powers from the Constitution (Article 282 for plan grants); (ii) states are equal partners in policy formulation through the Governing Council (Chief Ministers of all states and Lt. Governors of UTs); (iii) no power to allocate funds — the Finance Commission and Union Ministries handle resource allocation; (iv) focus on long-term strategic vision rather than detailed five-year plans.

Structure: Chairperson (Prime Minister); Vice-Chairperson (appointed by PM — currently Suman Bery); Chief Executive Officer (CEO — a senior IAS officer); full-time and part-time members; and ex-officio members (Union Ministers nominated by PM). The Governing Council includes all Chief Ministers and Lt. Governors — meets annually to discuss policy direction. The Regional Councils address specific regional issues. Expert committees and domain-specific verticals coordinate sectoral policies.

Key initiatives of NITI Aayog: (i) SDG India Index — tracks states' progress on Sustainable Development Goals; (ii) India Innovation Index — ranks states on innovation ecosystem; (iii) School Education Quality Index and Health Index — performance-based ranking; (iv) Atal Innovation Mission (AIM) — establishing Tinkering Labs in schools and incubation centres; (v) Aspirational Districts Programme (2018) — transformative acceleration in 112 most backward districts across 5 themes (health, education, agriculture, infrastructure, financial inclusion) with real-time monitoring; (vi) Women Entrepreneurship Platform (WEP); (vii) National Data and Analytics Platform (NDAP) — democratising access to government data; (viii) Battery Swapping Policy and Green Hydrogen Mission drafting; (ix) Export Preparedness Index; (x) State Energy and Climate Index. NITI Aayog has also prepared Vision Documents — the 15-year Vision document, 7-year Strategy document, and 3-year Action Agenda (2017-18 to 2019-20) replacing the Five-Year Plan framework.

Gadgil Formula and Plan Transfers

Prior to NITI Aayog, the Gadgil Formula (1969) governed the allocation of Central Plan assistance to states. Named after economist D.R. Gadgil, the formula allocated 60% based on population, 10% on per capita income (deviation from national average), 10% on tax effort, 10% on special problems, and 10% on irrigation and power projects. The formula was revised in 1980 (as Gadgil-Mukherjee formula) and 1990, with changing weightage. Post-NITI Aayog, the distinction between Plan and Non-Plan expenditure was abolished (2017), and the Finance Commission's recommendations (based on the 15-point devolution formula) now determine the vertical and horizontal distribution of central tax revenues to states. The Finance Commission's horizontal formula (15th FC, 2021-26) uses: 45% population (2011 Census), 15% area, 10% forest & ecology, 10% income distance, 5% tax effort, 2.5% demographic performance, 2.5% infrastructure, 10% — based on specific criteria including SC/ST population.

Green Revolution (1960s-1970s)

The Green Revolution was initiated under the leadership of Norman Borlaug (Nobel Peace Prize 1970) and M.S. Swaminathan (Father of the Green Revolution in India). It involved:

  • High-Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds — wheat (Kalyan Sona, Sonalika) and rice (IR8, Jaya)
  • Large-scale use of chemical fertilisers, pesticides, and irrigation
  • Focus on Punjab, Haryana, and Western Uttar Pradesh — India's "breadbasket"
  • Result: Food self-sufficiency achieved by the 1970s; India went from importer to exporter of food grains
  • Wheat yield rose from 0.8 tonnes/ha (1948) to 4.7 tonnes/ha (1975)
Criticism
The Green Revolution led to regional disparities, groundwater depletion, soil degradation, and increased farmer debt in states like Punjab.

Bank Nationalisation

Indira Gandhi nationalised 14 major banks on 19 July 1969 (each with deposits > ₹50 crore), capturing 85% of bank deposits. 6 more banks were nationalised in 1980, bringing 91% of banking under government control. The stated goal was to align credit delivery with planned development priorities (priority sector lending).

1991 Economic Crisis

The 1991 crisis was a severe balance of payments crisis. Causes included:

  • Large fiscal deficits and foreign debt accumulation in the 1980s
  • Gulf War (1990-91): Oil import bill swelled, remittances from Gulf fell
  • Dissolution of the Soviet Union — India's largest trading partner
  • Foreign exchange reserves fell to $1.2 billion (barely 3 weeks of imports)
  • Moody's downgraded India's bond ratings; IMF and World Bank suspended assistance

Response: India pledged 67 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England and Union Bank of Switzerland to raise $600 million as collateral for an IMF loan of $7 billion. The Chandra Shekhar government authorised the airlift, and the Narasimha Rao government with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister launched comprehensive reforms.

1991 Reforms (LPG)

Liberalisation

  • Industrial licensing abolished for all but 18 strategic industries (New Industrial Policy, 1991)
  • MRTP restrictions removed: Requirement for government approval for asset expansion scrapped
  • Trade liberalisation: Tariffs reduced, import licensing dismantled
  • Exchange rate devaluation: Rupee devalued by 19% in two doses (1 July and 3 July 1991)
  • Financial sector reform: As per Narasimham Committee; SLR and CRR reduced, interest rates deregulated

Privatisation

  • Disinvestment of government stakes in PSUs (started cautiously in the 1990s, accelerated under Vajpayee and Modi)
  • Navratna (1997): 9 top-performing PSUs given greater autonomy — later extended to Maharatna (2010), Miniratna categories
  • Strategic sale of assets (e.g., Modern Foods, VSNL, BALCO, CMC, Hindustan Zinc)

Globalisation

  • Foreign investment liberalised — up to 51% FDI in many sectors automatically approved
  • India joined WTO on 1 January 1995
  • Current account convertibility introduced (1994) — rupee convertible for trade purposes
  • Capital account convertibility remains partially restricted (Tarapore Committee reports)

Post-Reform Growth Trajectory

  • Average ~6-7% growth in the 1990s-2000s
  • Peak growth of 9%+ during 2005-2007
  • Slowdown after 2008 global financial crisis
  • Temporary boost 2014-16, then slowdown (demonetisation Nov 2016, GST July 2017, NBFC crisis 2018)
  • COVID-19 contraction: GDP fell by 5.8% in FY2020-21
  • V-shaped recovery: 9.1% growth in FY2021-22, 7.2% in FY2022-23, ~7% in FY2023-24

Current Economic Profile

  • Nominal GDP: ~$3.7 trillion (5th largest economy)
  • PPP GDP: ~$14.5 trillion (3rd largest after US and China)
  • Demographic dividend: Median age ~28 years; ~68% working-age population (15-64)
  • Labour force participation rate: ~46% (low by global standards)
  • Unemployment: ~6-7% (CMIE data); informal sector employs ~90%+ of workers

Key Government Initiatives

  • Make in India (2014): Boost manufacturing, attract FDI, create jobs
  • Startup India (2016): Promote entrepreneurship, tax benefits, easier compliance
  • Skill India (2015): Train 400 million people in various skills by 2022
  • Digital India (2015): Transform India into a digitally empowered society
  • PM GatiShakti (2021): National Master Plan for multimodal infrastructure connectivity
  • National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP): ₹111 lakh crore investment in infrastructure (2020-25)
7. Poverty, Inequality and Employment

Poverty Estimation in India

Tendulkar Committee (2009)

Defined poverty line based on per capita consumption expenditure: ₹27/day rural, ₹33/day urban (2011-12 prices). This translates to ₹816/month rural and ₹1,000/month urban. Based on this, poverty was 29.6% in 2009-10 and 21.9% in 2011-12.

Rangarajan Committee (2014)

Revised the poverty line upward: ₹32/day rural and ₹47/day urban (₹972/month rural, ₹1,407/month urban). Based on this methodology, poverty was 38.2% in 2009-10 and 29.5% in 2011-12.

Current Official Poverty Estimates
The Government of India does not have an official poverty line post-2014. Based on the Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) 2022-23, the SBI estimates rural poverty at 7.2% and urban poverty at 4.6% using a revised Tendulkar methodology-based poverty line of ₹1,622/month (rural) and ₹1,929/month (urban).

Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

Developed by OPHI and UNDP, the MPI uses 10 indicators across 3 dimensions (Health, Education, Standard of Living). India's MPI (NITI Aayog, 2021):

  • 25.01% of India's population is multi-dimensionally poor
  • MPI reduced from 55% to 25% between 2005-06 and 2019-21 (a halving in 15 years)
  • Around 415 million people moved out of multidimensional poverty in this period
  • States with highest MPI: Bihar, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Chhattisgarh
  • States with lowest MPI: Kerala, Goa, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Tamil Nadu

Inequality

  • Gini coefficient (consumption): ~0.49 (moderately high income inequality)
  • Gini coefficient (wealth): ~0.82 (one of the highest in the world)
  • Oxfam report: Top 10% owns 77% of national wealth; top 1% owns 73% of wealth generated in 2017
  • World Inequality Report 2022: Top 1% income share in India is 21.7%
  • Bottom 50% of population owns just 2-3% of wealth

Poverty Alleviation Programmes

Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005

MGNREGA guarantees 100 days of wage employment per household per year to adult members who volunteer for unskilled manual work. Key features:

  • Legal right to work — employment must be provided within 15 days of application, failing which unemployment allowance must be paid
  • Wages vary by state: ₹149-₹374/day (2022 rates: Haryana highest ₹331, Chhattisgarh lowest ₹204)
  • At least 33% participation of women (actual ~47%)
  • Implemented by Gram Panchayats (primary authority)
  • Works include water conservation, drought proofing, irrigation, land development, rural connectivity
  • 60:40 wage-to-material ratio
  • As of 2021, 99.7% of payments through DBT (electronic fund transfer)
  • Repealed in December 2025 and replaced by VB-G RAM G (Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission Gramin)
Global Recognition
The World Bank's World Development Report 2014 called MGNREGA a "stellar example of rural development". SC/ST households account for 51% of person-days generated.

National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013

  • Entitlement: 5 kg of food grain per person per month at subsidised rates (₹3/kg rice, ₹2/kg wheat, ₹1/kg coarse grains)
  • Covers 67% of population (75% rural, 50% urban)
  • Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY) households get 35 kg per family per month at even lower prices
  • Implemented through Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS)

Other Key Social Welfare Schemes

  • PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY): Free food grains during COVID-19 (5 kg/person/month free); extended until December 2023 and then subsumed under NFSA for 5 years
  • PM Awas Yojana (PMAY): Housing for all by 2024 — credit-linked subsidy for construction/improvement of houses
  • Saubhagya (2017): Last-mile electricity connections — achieved 100% household electrification
  • Jal Jeevan Mission (2019): Tap water connection to every rural household — 12+ crore connections provided by 2024
  • Swachh Bharat Mission (2014): Declared Open Defecation Free (ODF) in 2019; 10+ crore toilets built
  • PM Poshan Abhiyaan (Mid-Day Meal): Hot cooked meals to school children — world's largest school feeding programme
  • Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS): Anganwadi centres for nutrition, health, and pre-school education

Employment

Labour Codes, 2020

Four labour codes consolidated 29 central labour laws:

  • Code on Wages, 2019: Universal minimum wage, floor wage by central government
  • Industrial Relations Code, 2020: Standing orders, retrenchment norms, fixed-term employment
  • Social Security Code, 2020: Universal social security coverage for organised and unorganised workers
  • Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020: Workplace safety, health standards

Employment Trends

  • PLFS (Periodic Labour Force Survey): Annual survey by MoSPI tracking LPR, WPR, and unemployment rates. The PLFS was launched in 2017 to provide annual and quarterly estimates of key labour market indicators. It replaced the earlier quinquennial Employment-Unemployment Surveys (EUS) of NSSO. The PLFS uses a two-stage stratified sampling design and covers both usual status (CWS — Current Weekly Status) and current daily status (CDS) estimates. Quarterly bulletins provide urban unemployment rates, while annual reports cover both rural and urban areas. The PLFS defines: (i) Usual Principal Activity Status (UPSS) — activity status for the majority of the year; (ii) Subsidiary Economic Activity Status (SS) — activity in addition to principal; (iii) Current Weekly Status (CWS) — activity during the reference week. The CMIE (Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy) provides more frequent (monthly) unemployment estimates using a different methodology (CPHS — Consumer Pyramids Household Survey), often showing higher unemployment rates than PLFS due to its sample composition and definitional differences.
  • Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR): ~46% (low; global average ~60%)
  • Worker-Population Ratio (WPR): ~45%
  • Unemployment Rate (UR): ~6-7% (CMIE data shows higher at ~8-9% for youth)
  • Female Labour Force Participation (FLFPR): ~25% — among the lowest in South Asia. Reasons: cultural norms, lack of safe transport, gender wage gap (women earn ~50-60% of men for same work), unpaid care work burden, lack of flexible work, and measurement issues (women's work in household enterprises often uncounted). Recent improvements in FLFPR are partly attributed to increased self-employment and MGNREGA participation.
  • Informal/unorganised sector: ~90%+ of workers. The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector (NCEUS) defines informal workers as those without social security, job security, or regular employment contracts. The Social Security Code, 2020 aims to extend social security coverage to gig, platform, and informal workers through a universal registration system (e-Shram portal — 30+ crore registered unorganised workers as of 2024).
  • Gig economy: Growing platform-based work (Zomato, Swiggy, Uber, Ola, Urban Company) — estimated 7-8 million gig workers by 2024. The gig economy provides flexibility but lacks social security, minimum wage guarantees, and collective bargaining rights. The NITI Aayog report on gig economy (2022) estimates 7.7M gig workers (2.6% of non-agricultural workforce) and projects 23.5M by 2029-30.

Key Employment Schemes

  • PM Rozgar Mela (2022): 10 lakh government jobs filled in mission mode; regular recruitment drives across ministries.
  • Apprenticeship Act, 1961 (amended 2014): Mandates apprenticeship training in designated trades; recent reforms relax compliance for MSMEs and increase stipend support.
  • National Career Service (NCS) Portal: Online job matching platform connecting job seekers and employers; also provides career counselling, vocational guidance, and skill development information.
  • Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana — National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM): Promotes self-help groups (SHGs) for rural women; provides bank linkage, skill training, and market access. Over 8 crore women are part of SHGs under DAY-NRLM.
  • Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana — National Urban Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NULM): Extension of SHG model to urban areas; supports street vendors, homeless, and urban poor through skill training and self-employment.
  • PM Employment Generation Programme (PMEGP): Credit-linked subsidy for setting up micro-enterprises in non-farm sector; implemented by Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC).

Youth Employment Challenge

India's demographic dividend (~68% working age) is also a challenge: about 10-12 million youth enter the labour force annually against limited formal job creation. The employment elasticity of growth has been declining — the services-led growth has been "jobless" in nature, with manufacturing still at ~11-12% of GDP and unable to absorb surplus agricultural labour.

8. Agriculture and Food Security

Agriculture in the Indian Economy

Agriculture contributes approximately 18% of GDP and employs about 45% of the workforce (down from 60% in 1990 but still high for a developing economy at India's income level). India has the 2nd largest arable land in the world (159.7 million hectares).

Land Holdings and Fragmentation

  • Average land holding size: 1.08 hectares (down from 2.3 ha in 1970-71)
  • Small and marginal farmers (holdings <2 ha): 86% of total operational holdings
  • Land fragmentation due to inheritance laws and land ceiling acts remains a major productivity constraint

Cropping Seasons

  • Rabi (Winter): Sown October-December, harvested April-June. Major crops: wheat, mustard, gram, barley, peas
  • Kharif (Monsoon): Sown June-July, harvested September-October. Major crops: rice, cotton, sugarcane, jowar, bajra, maize, groundnut
  • Zaid (Summer): Sown April-May, harvested July-August. Crops: watermelons, muskmelons, cucumbers, vegetables, fodder

Food Grain Production

  • Total food grain production: ~330 MT (2023-24) — record level
  • Rice: ~135 MT; Wheat: ~110 MT; Coarse grains: ~50 MT; Pulses: ~27 MT
  • India is the world's largest producer of milk, pulses, spices, ginger, okra, bananas, mangoes, buffalo meat
  • India is the world's 2nd largest producer of rice, wheat, sugarcane, cotton, tea, fruits and vegetables, groundnuts
  • India has the highest cotton acreage in the world and is the 2nd largest cotton producer

Revolutions in Indian Agriculture

  • Green Revolution: HYV seeds, fertilisers, irrigation — wheat and rice self-sufficiency (1960s-70s)
  • Evergreen Revolution: M.S. Swaminathan's concept of sustainable agricultural productivity growth
  • White Revolution: Operation Flood (1970-96) — world's largest dairy development program; Amul model under Verghese Kurien and NDDB; made India the largest milk producer
  • Blue Revolution: Fisheries — India is the 2nd largest fish producer (aquaculture + capture)
  • Golden Revolution: Horticulture — fruits, vegetables, flowers, spices; India is the 2nd largest producer
  • Rainbow Revolution: Integrated approach combining all agricultural revolutions

Land Reforms

Post-independence land reforms included:

  • Zamindari Abolition — elimination of intermediaries between state and cultivator
  • Tenancy Reforms — regulation of rent, security of tenure, conferment of ownership
  • Land Ceiling — fixing maximum land holding size and redistribution of surplus land
  • Consolidation of Holdings — reducing fragmentation through exchange of scattered plots

Implementation has been uneven across states, with West Bengal and Kerala achieving greater success.

Agricultural Credit and Insurance

Kisan Credit Card (KCC)

Introduced in 1998 to provide farmers with timely and adequate credit for cultivation and other needs. Over 7.5 crore KCCs have been issued as of 2023. Interest subvention of 4% is provided on prompt repayment (effective rate ~4% per annum).

Priority Sector Lending (PSL)

Banks must lend 18% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to agriculture, with a target of 8% for small and marginal farmers. NABARD is the apex institution for rural credit.

PM Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY), 2016

Crop insurance scheme replacing earlier schemes (NAIS, MNAIS, WBCIS). Features:

  • Uniform premium: 2% for kharif, 1.5% for rabi, 5% for commercial/horticultural crops
  • Balance premium shared 50:50 by Centre and State (90:10 for NE states)
  • Covers prevented sowing, post-harvest losses, localised calamities
  • Cluster approach for empanelment of insurance companies
  • Use of technology: YES-TECH (remote sensing for yield estimation), DBT for claim payments

Input Subsidies

  • Fertiliser subsidy: ₹2.4 lakh crore in FY2025 — includes Urea (controlled under NBS — Nutrient Based Subsidy), DAP, MOP, SSP under decontrolled category
  • Power subsidy: Free/subsidised electricity for agriculture in states like Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu (Rajasthan's Kisan Urja Yojana)
  • Irrigation subsidy: Under PMKSY (Per Drop More Crop) — focus on micro-irrigation (drip, sprinkler)

Minimum Support Price (MSP)

MSP was introduced in 1965 based on the recommendations of the Agricultural Prices Commission (now CACP — Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices). MSP is announced for 23 crops (14 kharif, 6 rabi, 3 commercial).

CategoryCrops under MSP
KharifPaddy, jowar, bajra, maize, ragi, tur, moong, urad, cotton, groundnut, sunflower, soybean (yellow+black), sesamum, niger seed
RabiWheat, barley, gram, masur (lentil), rapeseed/mustard, safflower
CommercialSugarcane (Fair and Remunerative Price), copra, raw jute

CACP recommends MSP based on cost of production (A2+FL), demand-supply, price trends, input prices, and terms of trade. The Union Cabinet takes the final decision. Since the 2018 Budget, MSP is set at 1.5 times A2+FL cost (Swaminathan formula).

Challenges with MSP

  • Only 20-25% of wheat and paddy sold at MSP; procurement heavily skewed towards Punjab, Haryana, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana
  • Only 23% of farmers are aware of MSP (NSSO 2013 data)
  • Leads to overproduction of rice and wheat, depletion of groundwater, and stubble burning
  • WTO dispute: India's MSP-based procurement subsidies breach the 10% de minimis limit (India invoked the Peace Clause 5 times)
  • Fiscal burden: food + fertiliser subsidy ~1/8th of total government budget
2020-21 Farmer Protests
Three farm laws (Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce Act, Farmers Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, Essential Commodities Amendment Act) were passed in 2020 but repealed in November 2021 after year-long protests, primarily demanding a legal guarantee for MSP.

Agricultural Marketing

APMC Act and e-NAM

The Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC) Act established regulated markets. Reforms include:

  • e-NAM (Electronic National Agriculture Market): Launched 2016 — online trading platform integrating APMC mandis; 1,360+ mandis integrated across 25 states and 3 UTs
  • Allows farmers to discover prices online and trade across state borders
  • Private mandis and direct marketing allowed under model APMC Act, 2003

Agricultural Exports

  • India accounts for ~2.5% of global agricultural trade
  • Major exports: rice (world's largest exporter — basmati + non-basmati), spices, marine products, meat, tea, coffee, cotton
  • Export restrictions: wheat ban (May 2022), rice ban (July 2023 on non-basmati white rice, partially eased December 2024)
  • Total agri exports: ~$50+ billion in 2023-24

Key Government Schemes in Agriculture

  • PM-KISAN (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi): Launched Feb 2019 — ₹6,000/year to all farmer families (irrespective of landholding size) in 3 equal instalments of ₹2,000 via DBT. Over 9.5 crore beneficiaries. Budget: ₹75,000 crore/annum. Excludes income tax payers and institutional landholders.
  • Soil Health Card Scheme (2015): Soil testing every 3 years for all farmers; 24 crore+ cards issued
  • PM Krishi Sinchai Yojana (PMKSY): "Per Drop More Crop" — focus on micro-irrigation, end-to-end water solutions
  • Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY): Promotion of organic farming in clusters
  • PM Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY): Crop insurance at uniform low premiums
  • PM Kisan Maan Dhan Yojana (PM-KMY): Pension of ₹3,000/month to small and marginal farmers after 60 years (contributory scheme)
  • Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (RKVY): Incentivising states to increase agriculture investment

Nutrition and Food Security

  • NFSA, 2013: National Food Security Act — provides legal entitlement to subsidised food grains
  • Mid-Day Meal Scheme (PM Poshan Shakti Nirman): Hot cooked meals to 12 crore+ school children
  • ICDS / Anganwadi: Nutrition, health, and pre-school education for children under 6 and pregnant/lactating mothers
  • Poshan Abhiyaan (2018): Reduce stunting, undernutrition, anaemia; focus on the first 1000 days of life
  • Doubling Farmer Income target: Set for 2022 (not achieved); CACP recommended strategy of productivity increase, cost reduction, MSP realisation, diversification, and livestock

Allied Agricultural Activities

Livestock and Dairying: India is the world's largest milk producer (~230 MT in 2023-24), accounting for ~23% of global milk production. Operation Flood (1970-96), spearheaded by Verghese Kurien and the National Dairy Development Board (NDDB), transformed India from a milk-deficient nation to the largest producer. The Amul cooperative model (Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation) is the most successful dairy cooperative globally, with 3.6M farmer members. Livestock contributes ~4.5% of total GDP and ~25% of agricultural GVA. Key schemes: Rashtriya Gokul Mission (indigenous cattle breeding), National Programme for Dairy Development (NPDD), Dairy Processing and Infrastructure Development Fund (DIDF).

Fisheries (Blue Revolution): India is the 2nd largest fish producer globally (~17.5 MT) after China, and the 2nd largest aquaculture producer. The sector contributes ~1% of GDP and ~5.4% of agricultural GVA. It employs ~2.8 crore fishers and fish farmers. The Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY), launched in 2020 with an outlay of ₹20,050 crore, aims to enhance fish production to 22 MT by 2024-25 (extended to 2027-28). Key schemes: Blue Revolution: Integrated Development and Management of Fisheries, Fisheries and Aquaculture Infrastructure Development Fund (FIDF). Marine exports: ~$8B annually.

Horticulture (Golden Revolution): India is the 2nd largest producer of fruits and vegetables globally (~350 MT). The sector has surpassed food grains in output value and contributes ~33% of agricultural GVA. Key crops: mango (largest producer), banana (largest), papaya (largest), potato (2nd largest), onion (2nd largest), cauliflower (largest). The Mission for Integrated Development of Horticulture (MIDH) provides financial assistance for nursery, production, and post-harvest management. The National Horticulture Board (NHB) promotes commercial horticulture.

Sericulture and Apiculture: India is the 2nd largest silk producer (after China), producing all four commercial varieties (mulberry, eri, tasar, muga — muga is exclusive to Assam). The Central Silk Board (CSB) promotes sericulture under the Silk Samagra scheme. Apiculture (honey production) is promoted under the National Beekeeping and Honey Mission (NBHM) with the goal of doubling honey production to 2 lakh MT by 2027.

Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs)

FPOs are collectives of small and marginal farmers formed to improve their bargaining power, reduce transaction costs, and access better markets, credit, and technology. They are registered as Farmer Producer Companies (FPCs) under the Companies Act, 2013. The government launched a scheme in 2020 to establish 10,000 new FPOs over 5 years with a budget of ₹6,865 crore. Each FPO receives financial support of up to ₹18 lakh for 3 years. The Small Farmers' Agri-Business Consortium (SFAC) and NABARD are implementing agencies. FPOs help farmers with: (i) bulk procurement of inputs (seeds, fertilisers) at lower cost, (ii) collective marketing and price negotiation, (iii) access to warehouse and cold storage, (iv) processing and value addition, (v) credit linkage through banks, (vi) technology transfer. As of 2024, over 8,000 FPOs have been registered under the scheme. Successful FPO models include Dairy cooperatives (Amul, Mother Dairy), Sahaja Samrudha (Karnataka organic), and Mahagrapes (grape export consortium).

Organic Farming

India has the largest number of organic producers globally (2.9M registered farmers) and ranks 7th in organic agricultural area (~3.7M hectares). The total organic production is ~3.5 MT. Key organic-producing states: Madhya Pradesh (highest area), Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Uttarakhand, and Sikkim (first fully organic state, achieved 2016). The Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY) promotes organic farming in clusters of 50 hectares or contiguous patches, offering ₹50,000 per hectare over 3 years. The Mission Organic Value Chain Development for North Eastern Region (MOVCD-NER) supports organic value chains in NE states. India's organic export market is ~$1B annually, with key destinations: USA, EU, Canada, and Japan. Certification is through APEDA (Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority)-accredited certifying bodies under NPOP (National Programme for Organic Production).

National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA)

NMSA is one of the eight missions under the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC). It aims to make agriculture more resilient to climate change through: (i) Rainfed Area Development (RAD) — integrated farming systems for rainfed areas; (ii) On-Farm Water Management (OFWM) — water-use efficiency; (iii) Soil Health Management — organic farming, soil conservation; (iv) Climate Change Adaptation — contingency planning, stress-tolerant varieties. The mission promotes Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) practices including conservation agriculture, agroforestry, integrated nutrient management, and weather-based crop advisories. India has also launched the National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) which funds state-level adaptation projects in agriculture, water, and allied sectors.

9. External Sector and Balance of Payments

9.1 Balance of Payments (BoP) – Conceptual Framework

The Balance of Payments is a systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world during a given period. It follows the double-entry bookkeeping system and is divided into two main accounts: the Current Account and the Capital Account (including the Financial Account under IMF methodology). By accounting identity, the BoP always balances: a surplus or deficit in one account is offset by a corresponding deficit or surplus in the other, with changes in foreign exchange reserves acting as the balancing item.

Current Account

The current account records transactions in goods, services, primary income, and secondary income.

  • Merchandise Trade (Visible Trade): Exports and imports of physical goods. India's merchandise exports were ~$437B in FY24 and FY25, while imports were ~$678B (FY24) and ~$720B (FY25), yielding a merchandise trade deficit of ~$241B and ~$283B respectively.
  • Services Trade (Invisible Trade): India is a net exporter of services. Service exports reached ~$388B in FY25, driven by IT-BPM, software, and business services. Service imports were ~$199B, giving a services surplus of ~$189B.
  • Primary Income: Covers investment income (interest, dividends, profits) and compensation of employees. India typically runs a deficit here due to repatriation of profits by foreign companies.
  • Secondary Income: Largely consists of remittances. India is the world's largest recipient of remittances (~$120B in 2024), mainly from the UAE, USA, Saudi Arabia, and other GCC countries.

Capital Account

The capital account records capital transfers and acquisition/disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets. Under the broader IMF definition, the Financial Account captures: Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI), External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs), NRI deposits, banking capital, and other capital flows.

Current Account Deficit (CAD)

India typically runs a Current Account Deficit because merchandise imports exceed exports, only partially offset by the services surplus and remittances. The CAD peaked at 4.8% of GDP in FY2013 (the "taper tantrum" period), narrowed to ~0.7% in FY2021, and widened to ~2% of GDP in FY2024. The CAD is financed by surplus on the capital account — principally FDI, FPI, and external borrowing. A manageable CAD (below 2.5–3% of GDP) is considered sustainable if financed by stable capital flows.

Key Insight for UPSC
A CAD is not inherently bad if it finances productive investment (capital goods imports) rather than consumption. The "twin deficit" problem arises when the fiscal deficit also widens, putting pressure on the external account.

9.3 Rupee Convertibility and Exchange Rate Regimes

Rupee Convertibility

Current Account Convertibility: India achieved full current account convertibility in 1994 under the IMF's Article VIII obligations, meaning there are no restrictions on payments for current account transactions (trade, travel, remittances, etc.).

Capital Account Convertibility: India has partial capital account convertibility. The Tarapore Committee (1997) recommended a phased roadmap to full capital account convertibility over 3 years, but the Asian Financial Crisis (1997) led to a more cautious approach. A second Tarapore Committee (2006) revisited the issue, recommending a 5-phase liberalisation, but full convertibility has not been implemented due to concerns about financial stability, inflation targeting, and fiscal discipline. Currently, FDI, FPI, and ECBs are largely free but with prudential limits.

Exchange Rate Regimes in India

  • Fixed Exchange Rate (1947–1971): Rupee was pegged to the British pound sterling under the Bretton Woods system. Devaluation in 1966 (from ₹4.76 to ₹7.50 per USD).
  • Pegged to a Basket (1975–1992): Rupee was linked to a basket of major trading partner currencies.
  • Dual Exchange Rate (1992–1993): Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS) — 40% at official rate, 60% at market rate.
  • Managed Float (1993–present): Market-determined exchange rate with RBI intervention to manage volatility. RBI does not target a specific level but intervenes to prevent "disorderly conditions."

NEER and REER

The RBI publishes Nominal Effective Exchange Rate (NEER) and Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) indices for a 6-currency basket (major trading partners) and a 36-currency basket (broader trade coverage). REER adjusts NEER for inflation differentials and indicates competitiveness. A REER above 100 implies the rupee is overvalued in real terms, hurting export competitiveness.

Rupee Depreciation Trends

  • 1947: ₹1 = $1 (parity)
  • 1966: ₹7.50 per USD (post-devaluation)
  • 1991: ₹25 per USD (BoP crisis)
  • 2000: ₹45 per USD
  • 2010: ₹45 per USD
  • 2013: ₹68 per USD (taper tantrum)
  • 2020: ₹75 per USD (COVID-19)
  • 2024: ~₹83–84 per USD
Important Distinction
Depreciation is a market-driven fall in value (managed float); devaluation is a deliberate official reduction in par value (fixed regime). India has experienced depreciation since 1993, not devaluation.

9.4 India's External Debt

India's external debt stood at ~$650 billion as of FY2024, approximately 18–20% of GDP. The composition: Commercial borrowings (ECBs) ~38%, NRI deposits ~25%, short-term debt ~20%, multilateral/bilateral loans ~10%. India's external debt is considered sustainable due to: (i) low share of short-term debt, (ii) high forex reserves covering >100% of short-term debt, (iii) debt denominated largely in rupees (NRI deposits). Key metrics: External Debt to GDP ratio (~19%), Debt Service Ratio (~5-6% of current receipts), Short-term debt to reserves ratio (~20%).

10. Industry, Services and Infrastructure

10.1 Industrial Sector – Structure and Policy

The industrial sector accounts for ~26% of India's GDP and employs ~24% of the workforce. It includes manufacturing, mining & quarrying, electricity, gas & water supply, and construction. Manufacturing alone contributes ~17% of GDP (stagnant for two decades), well below the government's target of 25%.

Industrial Policy Evolution

  • Industrial Policy Resolution (IPR) 1956: Based on the socialist pattern of society; reserved 17 industries for the public sector; categorised industries into Schedule A (exclusive state), Schedule B (state + private), Schedule C (private).
  • IPR 1991: Abolished industrial licensing for all except 18 industries (now only 4: liquor, tobacco, defence, explosives); removed MRTP (Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices) asset limits; opened most sectors to private investment; permitted FDI up to 51% in high-priority sectors.
  • National Manufacturing Policy (2011): Target of raising manufacturing share to 25% of GDP; creation of National Investment and Manufacturing Zones (NIMZs); emphasis on skill development, technology acquisition, and green manufacturing.
  • Make in India (2014): 25 sectors identified; FDI liberalisation; ease of doing business; "Zero Defect Zero Effect" slogan.
  • Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme (2020): 14 sectors, outlay of ₹1.97 lakh crore (~$24B).

Ease of Doing Business

India's rank in the World Bank's Ease of Doing Business Index improved dramatically from 142 in 2014 to 63 in 2019, a jump of 79 places. Key reforms: (i) Goods and Services Tax (GST), (ii) Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016, (iii) corporate tax reduction to 22% (plus 15% for new manufacturing), (iv) online single-window clearance, (v) abolished 1,500+ obsolete laws. The World Bank discontinued the index in 2021 after data irregularities were found. India's own Business Reform Action Plan (BRAP) now ranks states based on implementation of reforms.

Industrial Corridors

Five major industrial corridors are being developed:

  • Delhi-Mumbai Industrial Corridor (DMIC): 1,500 km, 24 nodes (e.g., Dholera, Shendra-Bidkin, Greater Noida); funded by Japan and World Bank.
  • Chennai-Bengaluru Industrial Corridor (CBIC): 560 km; nodes at Krishnagiri, Tumakuru, Ponneri.
  • Vizag-Chennai Industrial Corridor (VCIC): 800 km; nodes at Visakhapatnam, Kakinada, Yerraguntla.
  • Amritsar-Kolkata Industrial Corridor (AKIC): 1,800 km; nodes at Ludhiana, Panipat, Aligarh, Patna.
  • Bengaluru-Mumbai Industrial Corridor (BMIC): 1,000 km; nodes at Dharwad, Solapur, Satara.

MSME Sector — Backbone of Indian Manufacturing

The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSME) sector is the backbone of India's industrial economy, contributing ~30% of GDP, ~45% of manufacturing output, and ~48% of exports. It employs ~11 crore workers (second largest employer after agriculture) across 6.4 crore MSMEs (as per Udyam registration data). The government classifies MSMEs based on investment in plant & machinery and annual turnover (revised in 2020): Micro — investment ≤₹1 crore and turnover ≤₹5 crore; Small — investment ≤₹10 crore and turnover ≤₹50 crore; Medium — investment ≤₹50 crore and turnover ≤₹250 crore. Key challenges: (i) credit access — only ~16% of MSMEs have access to formal credit, (ii) technology obsolescence, (iii) marketing constraints, (iv) compliance burden, (v) delayed payments — the MSME Development Act (2006) mandates 45-day payment but non-compliance is widespread. The government's response includes: PM Mudra Yojana (₹25+ lakh crore sanctioned since 2015), CGTMSE (Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises — collateral-free loans up to ₹5 crore), Udyam Registration Portal (online registration since 2020, replacing Udyog Aadhaar), PSB Loans in 59 Minutes (digital loan approval platform), MSME Samadhaan (delayed payment grievance portal), GeM Portal (Government e-Marketplace — public procurement from MSMEs, with 25% mandatory sourcing from MSEs and 3% from women-owned MSEs), RAMP (Raising and Accelerating MSME Performance programme — World Bank-assisted, ₹6,000 crore), and ZED Certification (Zero Defect Zero Effect — quality certification for MSMEs with financial incentives). The Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC) promotes traditional village industries, providing employment to rural artisans. The Cluster Development Programme establishes Common Facility Centres (CFCs) for MSME clusters to improve competitiveness through shared infrastructure.

Industrial Parks and Special Economic Zones (SEZs)

India has 300+ Special Economic Zones (SEZs) under the SEZ Act, 2005, designed to promote exports with tax incentives (income tax exemption, customs duty exemption) and single-window clearance. However, the SEZ policy has been diluted after the introduction of DTC (Direct Taxes Code) provisions and the sunset clause (March 2020) for tax benefits. The National Industrial Corridor Development Programme (NICDP) coordinates industrial corridor development. The Delhi-Mumbai Industrial Corridor Development Corporation (DMICDC) oversees DMIC development with Japanese collaboration. Other notable industrial parks: Dholera Special Investment Region (Gujarat — India's first greenfield smart industrial city), Tumakuru Industrial Park (Karnataka), Vikram Udyogpuri (MP), and Krishnapatnam Industrial Area (Andhra Pradesh). The National Manufacturing Policy (2011) proposed NIMZs (National Investment and Manufacturing Zones) as self-governing industrial townships with environmental clearances and labour law flexibilities — limited progress made.

Construction Sector

The construction sector is the second largest employer after agriculture, employing ~5.5 crore workers and contributing ~8-9% of GDP. It is a key driver of growth with strong backward linkages (steel, cement, sand, bricks) and forward linkages (real estate, infrastructure). The sector faces challenges: (i) high informal employment (~85% of workers are unorganised), (ii) low mechanisation, (iii) regulatory bottlenecks (environment clearance, land acquisition — the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, or LARR Act), (iv) delayed payments under the RERA (Real Estate Regulatory Authority) Act, 2016 — mandatory registration of real estate projects with state RERA authorities; (v) skilled labour shortage. The Building and Other Construction Workers (BOCW) Act, 1996 provides for welfare boards and cess funds for construction workers. The government's infrastructure push (NIP, GatiShakti, PMAY-Urban and Rural) is expected to boost construction growth. The National Urban Housing Fund (NUHF) and Affordable Housing Partnership aim to facilitate housing for all.

10.3 Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs)

Classification of CPSEs

  • Maharatna (12): ONGC, Coal India, NTPC, IOC, GAIL, SAIL, BHEL, HPCL, BPCL, PGCIL, HAL, Power Finance Corp — can invest up to ₹5,000 Cr without government approval.
  • Navratna (17): BEL, HAL, HCL, MTNL, NMDC, OIL, NBCC, CONCOR, Engineers India, etc. — can invest up to ₹1,000 Cr.
  • Miniratna (75+): Category I (investment up to ₹500 Cr), Category II (up to ₹300 Cr).

Disinvestment Policy

Strategic Disinvestment: Transfer of majority stake (≥51%) along with management control to a private entity. Examples: Air India (2021), LIC (IPO — 2022, not strategic), BPCL (ongoing).

Non-Strategic/Minority Sale: Dilution of government stake through OFS, buyback, ETF route (CPSE ETF, Bharat 22 ETF) while retaining management control.

Public Sector Enterprise Policy (2021): Identified 4 strategic sectors: (i) Atomic energy, space, defence, (ii) Transport & energy (coal, oil, gas, railways, shipping), (iii) Banking, insurance, financial services, (iv) Others. In strategic sectors, minimum 1 and maximum 4 CPSEs will remain; others will be privatised/merged/consolidated. All non-strategic PSUs will be privatised on a case-by-case basis.

Key Agency: DIPAM (Department of Investment and Public Asset Management) under Ministry of Finance handles disinvestment. The NITI Aayog identifies CPSEs for strategic disinvestment.

Example: CPSE ETF
The Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSE) Exchange Traded Fund is a diversified portfolio of Maharatna, Navratna, and Miniratna stocks. The government uses the ETF route to raise capital by selling small portions of its holdings without diluting management control. The CPSE ETF has been followed by Bharat 22 ETF (22 CPSEs and PSU banks) and Nifty CPSE Index Fund.

10.4 Services Sector

The services sector is the dominant pillar of the Indian economy, contributing ~55% of GDP and growing at 7-9% annually. It is the fastest-growing sector and the largest FDI recipient.

IT-BPM Industry

India's IT and Business Process Management (BPM) sector is a global powerhouse, with revenues exceeding $250B in FY24 (including domestic). IT exports alone are ~$200B+. Top IT firms: TCS (largest, $29B revenue), Infosys, Wipro, HCL Tech, Tech Mahindra. India commands ~55% of the global offshoring market. Emerging sub-sectors: AI/ML services, cloud computing, cybersecurity, digital engineering.

Startup Ecosystem

India has the 3rd largest startup ecosystem globally after the US and China, with 100,000+ DPITT-recognised startups and 100+ unicorns ($1B+ valuation). Key hubs: Bangalore (Silicon Valley of India), Hyderabad, Gurugram, Mumbai, Pune, Delhi-NCR. The government's Startup India initiative (2016) provides tax benefits, patent support, and a ₹10,000 Cr Fund of Funds.

Tourism

Tourism's direct + indirect contribution to GDP is ~9%. India is ranked 54th in WTTC Travel & Tourism Competitiveness Index. Foreign tourist arrivals (FTAs) pre-COVID were ~11M; post-COVID recovery is underway. Key drivers: medical tourism (India is a top destination), spiritual tourism, eco-tourism, adventure tourism.

Other Services

Banking & Insurance: Robust financial services growth; insurance penetration ~4% of GDP (low by global standards). Real Estate: RERA (2016) brought transparency. Trade, Hotels, Transport & Communication: collectively ~20% of GDP. E-commerce: $100B+ market (expected $350B by 2030).

Mining and Quarrying

The mining and quarrying sector contributes ~2-2.5% of GDP and is essential for industrial raw materials (coal, iron ore, bauxite, limestone, copper, gold, diamonds). India has the 4th largest coal reserves, the 7th largest iron ore reserves, and significant bauxite, chromite, and manganese deposits. The sector was significantly reformed with the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2021, which introduced: (i) commercial coal mining — private sector allowed to mine coal without end-use restrictions; (ii) auction of mineral blocks on a revenue-sharing basis (replacing the earlier profit-sharing model); (iii) rationalised royalty rates; (iv) exploration of deep-seated minerals. The Coal India Limited (CIL) is the world's largest coal miner, accounting for ~80% of India's coal production (~900 MT). The National Mineral Policy 2019 promotes private sector participation in mineral exploration (through the National Mineral Exploration Trust — NMET) and reduces import dependence for critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, rare earths). India has discovered lithium reserves in Jammu & Kashmir (5.9 MT) and Rajasthan — though extraction viability is under study. The Mining Surveillance System (MSS) uses satellite imagery to detect illegal mining. India is also a member of the Critical Minerals Group (with USA, Australia, other allies) to secure supply chains for energy transition minerals.

Space Economy

India's space economy is estimated at ~$8-9B, with the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) as the central player. ISRO has achieved global recognition for cost-effective space missions: Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan, 2014) — first Asian nation to reach Mars orbit; Chandrayaan-3 (2023) — first nation to land on the lunar south pole; Gaganyaan — India's first crewed space mission (planned 2025-26); Aditya-L1 — first solar mission (2023). The government's Indian Space Policy 2023 opened the sector to private participation, with NewSpace India Limited (NSIL) as the commercial arm. The Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre (IN-SPACe) acts as a single-window regulator for private space entities. Private space startups (Skyroot Aerospace, Agnikul Cosmos, Pixxel, Dhruva Space) have developed launch vehicles, satellite systems, and earth observation solutions. India has launched 430+ foreign satellites for 40+ countries on commercial terms. The space sector contributes to remote sensing, communications (GSAT series), navigation (NavIC — India's regional satellite navigation system, with 8 satellites), weather forecasting, and disaster management. The Space Applications Centre (SAC) in Ahmedabad develops application-specific payloads. The global space economy is ~$570B; India's share (~1.5%) is expected to grow to $40B+ by 2040 under the new space policy framework.

Telecommunication Sector

India has the 2nd largest telecom network globally (~1.2B subscribers), after China. The sector underwent radical transformation with the National Telecom Policy 2012 and the entry of Reliance Jio in September 2016, which disrupted the market with free voice calls and ultra-low data prices, leading to the consolidation of the industry from 14 players to 3 major private operators (Jio, Airtel, Vi-Vodafone Idea) plus state-owned BSNL/MTNL. Key metrics: wireless subscribers ~1.17B; broadband subscribers ~900M+; average data consumption per user ~25 GB/month (highest globally); ARPU (Average Revenue Per User) ~₹180-200/month (among the lowest globally). The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) regulates tariffs, quality of service, and interconnection. Universal Service Obligation Fund (USOF) subsidises rural telecom connectivity. The government's BharatNet project aims to connect all 2.5 lakh gram panchayats with optical fibre — 2.15 lakh connected as of 2024. The launch of 5G services (October 2022) by Jio and Airtel has expanded to 750+ districts. The Telecom Sector Reforms (2021) package offered a 4-year moratorium on spectrum payments, rationalisation of Adjusted Gross Revenue (AGR) dues, 100% FDI (automatic route), and spectrum auction reforms. The Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 remains the primary legislation, though the Telecommunication Act, 2023 (passed to replace the 1885 Act) modernises the legal framework, addressing spectrum management, right of way, consumer protection, and national security provisions. The sector also includes OTT (Over-the-Top) services (WhatsApp, Skype, Zoom) — the regulatory treatment of OTT communication services (whether they should be licensed like telecom operators) remains a debated issue. India's telecom equipment manufacturing is promoted under the PLI scheme with a focus on indigenous 5G gear and core network products. The Centre for Development of Telematics (C-DOT) drives indigenous R&D in telecom technologies including 4G/5G stack.

10.5 Infrastructure

National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP) 2019-25

The NIP is a ₹111 lakh crore ($1.4T) investment plan covering energy (24%), roads (18%), railways (12%), urban infrastructure (16%), irrigation (8%), social infrastructure (7%), etc. The NIP was launched in 2019 with an initial outlay of ₹102 lakh crore, later revised to ₹111 lakh crore. Projects are classified as under-construction, conceptual, or under-development. The NIP aims to improve infrastructure quality and boost economic growth.

PM GatiShakti (2021)

The National Master Plan for Multimodal Connectivity (PM GatiShakti) is a GIS-based digital platform integrating 16 ministries (railways, roads, ports, aviation, telecom, pipelines, etc.). Key objectives: (i) synchronised infrastructure planning, (ii) reduced logistics costs (target: reduce from 14% to 8% of GDP), (iii) multimodal connectivity for industrial clusters, (iv) real-time project monitoring through PM GatiShakti portal. It replaced the earlier NIP with a more integrated approach.

National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP) 2022-25

The NMP aims to raise ₹6 lakh crore ($73B) through monetisation of completed public assets across: roads (₹1.6L Cr via TOT — Toll Operate Transfer), railways (₹1.5L Cr — stations, trains, tracks), power (₹0.9L Cr — transmission lines), telecom (₹0.6L Cr — fibre ducts, towers), airports (₹0.4L Cr — etc.), pipelines (₹0.5L Cr), warehouses & stadiums. Asset recycling: core assets remain in government ownership while revenue rights are transferred to private bidders.

Roads and Highways

  • National Highway (NH) length: ~1.5 lakh km (5th largest globally).
  • Bharatmala Pariyojana: Phase 1: 34,800 km of NH construction, ₹10.6 lakh crore investment. Focus on border roads, coastal roads, expressways, and corridor-based development.
  • Key expressways: Delhi-Mumbai (1,350 km), Dwarka (Delhi-Gurugram), Purvanchal (UP), Agra-Lucknow, Bengaluru-Chennai (planned).
  • Vehicle scrappage policy (2021): phase out old polluting vehicles; fitness tests.

Railways

  • Dedicated Freight Corridors (DFCs): Western DFC (1,500 km, approved), Eastern DFC (1,856 km, operational) — speeds up to 100 km/h, double-stack container trains.
  • Vande Bharat Trains: India's first indigenously manufactured semi-high-speed train (160 km/h), 80+ trains operational.
  • Kavach: Indigenous Automatic Train Protection (ATP) system developed by RDSO; deployed on thousands of route km.
  • Railway Ministry oversees Indian Railways, the largest employer in India (~1.2M employees).
  • 100% FDI allowed in railway infrastructure (automatic route).

Ports and Shipping

  • 12 Major Ports under central government (Kandla, Mumbai, JNPT, Mormugao, New Mangalore, Cochin, Chennai, VOC, Kamarajar, V.O. Chidambaranar, Visakhapatnam, Paradip, Kolkata).
  • 200+ Non-Major Ports under state governments.
  • Sagarmala Programme: port modernization, port connectivity, port-led industrialization, coastal community development. Target: reduce logistics costs by 10%.
  • JNPT (Nhava Sheva): India's largest container port (5.5M TEU capacity).

Airports

  • 140+ operational airports (up from 74 in 2014).
  • UDAN-RCS Scheme (Ude Desh ka Aam Nagrik — Regional Connectivity Scheme): aims to connect unserved/underserved airports with subsidised air fares (₹2,500 for 1-hr flights). 80+ airports operationalised under UDAN.
  • Airport privatisation: Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Cochin (PPP). New ones: Nagpur, Lucknow, Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Mangaluru, Guwahati.
  • Noida International Airport (Jewar): under construction; will be Asia's largest airport when completed.

Power Sector

  • Installed Capacity: ~430 GW (as of 2024). Thermal: ~240 GW (coal 208 GW, gas 25 GW, lignite 6 GW); Renewables: ~180 GW (solar 70+ GW, wind 45 GW, hydro 47 GW, biomass 10 GW); Nuclear: ~8 GW.
  • 24x7 Power for All: Achieved near-universal electrification under Saubhagya scheme (2017).
  • UDAY Scheme (Ujwal DISCOM Assurance Yojana, 2015): financial turnaround of state power distribution companies (DISCOMs). Reduced AT&C losses, revised tariffs, state takeover of DISCOM debt.
  • Green Energy Target: 500 GW non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030 (Panchamrit target); national hydrogen mission; renewable purchase obligations (RPO).
  • One Nation One Grid One Frequency: National grid synchronised.
UPSC Key Linkage
Infrastructure investment has a high multiplier effect on GDP. The NIP, GatiShakti, and NMP are complementary: NIP identifies projects, GatiShakti provides integrated planning, NMP generates revenue for new investment. Together they target the logistics cost reduction from 14% to 8% of GDP, boosting manufacturing competitiveness under Make in India.
11. Contemporary Issues and Reforms

11.1 Digital Economy and Fintech Revolution

Unified Payments Interface (UPI) Revolution

The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), launched in April 2016 by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), has transformed India's digital payment landscape. UPI is an open-source API-based instant payment system facilitating inter-bank P2P and P2M transactions via mobile devices.

  • Scale: As of 2025, India accounts for 50% of the world's digital transactions, with UPI processing 250+ billion annual transactions worth $3.4T. Monthly transactions exceed $300B.
  • Daily throughput: UPI handles 640M+ transactions daily — surpassing Visa's global daily volume of ~639M.
  • Market share: UPI accounts for 84% of India's digital payments. Leading apps: PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm.
  • UPI 2.0 (2018): Overdraft accounts, AutoPay for recurring payments, invoice viewing.
  • UPI 123PAY (2022): Voice-based UPI for feature phone users (400M users).
  • UPI Lite: On-device wallet for offline low-value transactions (₹200 limit per transaction, ₹2,000 wallet cap).
  • UPI Circle (2024): Delegated payments — primary user authorizes secondary users (up to 5) with transaction limits.
  • Internationalisation: UPI linked with Singapore PayNow, UAE (UPI-One World), France, Bhutan, Malaysia, etc. 7+ countries exploring UPI-like systems.
Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)
UPI is part of India's broader DPI stack: Aadhaar (digital identity), UPI (payments), DigiLocker (documents), Account Aggregator (financial data sharing), OCEN (credit enablement). India's DPI model has been showcased globally, including at the G20 under India's presidency (DPI Repository).

Digital Rupee (CBDC)

The Digital Rupee (e₹) is India's Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC), launched in December 2022. Two variants: e₹-W (Wholesale) — for interbank settlement, and e₹-R (Retail) — for consumer transactions. The RBI is piloting the CBDC across 26 cities, integrated with UPI QR codes. Features: offline capability, programmability, non-anonymous (unlike cryptocurrency). As of 2024, 5M+ retail users. The RBI plans a phased rollout, with legal backing from the RBI Act amendment.

Account Aggregator Framework (2021)

RBI's Account Aggregator (AA) framework allows individuals to securely share financial data (bank accounts, mutual funds, insurance, pensions, taxes) across regulated entities with explicit consent. It is built on the Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture (DEPA). As of 2024, 15+ AAs are operational, with 100+ Financial Information Providers (FIPs) and 50+ Financial Information Users (FIUs). Benefits: paperless loan applications, better credit assessment, personalised financial advice.

ONDC (2022)

The Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) is a government-backed initiative to democratise e-commerce by unbundling the buyer-seller-platform model. Unlike platform-centric models (Amazon, Flipkart), ONDC is an open protocol enabling any seller app to connect with any buyer app. It aims to increase e-commerce penetration (currently ~5% of retail), reduce platform monopolies, and include small retailers. As of 2024, ONDC covers 800+ cities and 200K+ sellers.

Data Protection and Privacy

Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act) 2023 is India's first comprehensive data protection law. Key provisions: (i) applies to both government and private entities, (ii) consent-based data processing, (iii) rights of individuals (access, correction, erasure, grievance), (iv) Data Protection Board as enforcement body, (v) cross-border data transfer restrictions (whitelisted countries), (vi) penalties up to ₹250 Cr for violations. Regulation of cryptocurrency: India imposes a 30% tax on crypto income + 1% TDS on transfers; RBI has opposed legalising private cryptocurrencies (citing financial stability risks); the government is considering a consultation paper on crypto regulation.

Fintech Regulation

  • RBI Regulatory Sandbox: framework for fintech firms to test innovative products in a controlled environment.
  • P2P Lending Regulation: RBI notified NBFC-P2P rules (2017) — platforms must be registered, maximum lending ₹50L, maximum borrowing ₹50L per person, funds routed via escrow accounts.
  • Digital lending guidelines (2022): ban on unauthorised lending apps; transparent interest rates; no harassment for recovery.

11.2 Climate Finance and Green Initiatives

Climate Finance – Definition and Scope

Climate finance refers to local, national, or transnational financing — drawn from public, private, and alternative sources — that supports climate change mitigation and adaptation. The UNFCCC defines it as finance that aims at reducing emissions and enhancing sinks of greenhouse gases, and reducing vulnerability to climate impacts. The Paris Agreement commits developed countries to mobilise $100B per year by 2020 (target missed — reached only $89.6B in 2021).

Green Bonds

Green bonds are fixed-income instruments whose proceeds are exclusively used to finance or refinance eligible green projects. India's SEBI framework for green bonds (2017) defines eligible categories: renewable energy, clean transportation, energy efficiency, sustainable water, climate adaptation, etc.

  • Sovereign Green Bonds: India issued its first sovereign green bond in January 2023, raising ₹16,000 Cr at competitive yields.
  • Green Bond Principles (ICMA): voluntary guidelines ensuring transparency, use-of-proceeds tracking, and impact reporting.
  • Greenwashing risk: bonds may be labelled "green" without sufficient environmental additionality; third-party certification under Climate Bonds Standard addresses this.

National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC)

NAFCC was established in 2015 with an initial corpus of ₹350 Cr to support adaptation projects in vulnerable sectors (water, agriculture, health, forests, coastal zones). It is implemented by the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD). State-level projects are approved under the State Action Plans on Climate Change (SAPCCs).

Loss and Damage Fund (COP28)

At COP28 in Dubai (2023), nations agreed to establish a Loss and Damage Fund to support developing countries experiencing irreversible climate impacts (sea-level rise, extreme weather, desertification). The fund is hosted by the World Bank. India has advocated for developed countries to contribute "new and additional" resources (not repurposed aid). Operationalisation is ongoing with initial pledges of ~$700M.

11.3 India's Climate Commitments and Green Transition

Panchamrit Targets (COP26, Glasgow 2021)

Prime Minister Modi announced five (Panchamrit) targets at COP26:

  • 500 GW non-fossil fuel energy capacity by 2030
  • 50% of energy requirements from renewables by 2030
  • 1 billion tonnes reduction in projected carbon emissions by 2030
  • 45% reduction in emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 (from 2005 level)
  • Net‑zero emissions by 2070

LiFE Mission (Lifestyle for Environment)

Launched by India at COP26, LiFE promotes sustainable lifestyles through collective individual action (reduce waste, use public transport, conserve energy). The LiFE Global Movement was formally launched by the UN and India in 2022. NITI Aayog's Behavioural Insights Unit works on LiFE-related nudges.

Sovereign Green Bond Framework (2022)

India's Sovereign Green Bond Framework follows ICMA's Green Bond Principles. Eligible sectors: renewable energy, green hydrogen, energy efficiency, clean transportation, climate adaptation, sustainable water, waste management, afforestation. The framework includes: (i) use-of-proceeds tracking via a dedicated account, (ii) annual allocation report, (iii) independent third-party assurance, (iv) post-issuance impact reporting. Rating agencies and climate bond certification bodies provide assurance.

National Green Hydrogen Mission (2023)

Outlay: ₹19,744 Cr. Target: 5 Million Metric Tonnes (MMT) of green hydrogen production by 2030. Key components: (i) SIGHT (Strategic Interventions for Green Hydrogen Transition) programme, (ii) electrolyser manufacturing PLI, (iii) green hydrogen hubs (pilot), (iv) R&D, (v) export promotion. India aims to become a global hub for green hydrogen production and export.

Coal Phase-Down (Not Phase-Out)

India successfully advocated for "phase-down" rather than "phase-out" of coal at COP26 and COP28. Coal accounts for ~50% of India's installed capacity and 70%+ of electricity generation. India's position: (i) developed countries have historical responsibility, (ii) India's per capita emissions are low (2.5 tCO2 vs global avg 4.7, US ~15), (iii) coal is essential for energy security and baseload power, (iv) transition must be "just" with funding for just transition, (v) India is expanding renewable capacity aggressively while coal remains the bridge fuel.

Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)

India submitted its first NDC in 2015, updated in 2022. Key updated targets: (i) reduce emissions intensity by 45% by 2030 (vs 2005), (ii) achieve 50% cumulative electric power from non-fossil sources, (iii) create additional carbon sink of 2.5-3 billion tonnes through forest cover. India's NDC is "conditional" on international climate finance and technology transfer.

11.4 National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP) and Asset Recycling

The NMP is a ₹6 lakh crore monetisation plan for FY22-25 across sectors: roads (TOT model), railways (dedicated freight corridors, stations, trains), power (transmission assets), telecommunication (fibre, towers), mining (coal blocks), airports (existing terminals), natural gas pipelines, oil & gas pipelines, warehousing assets, and stadiums. Asset recycling: the government maintains ownership of core assets while transferring revenue rights to private entities. Revenue generated is used for new infrastructure creation. Progress: ~₹3.5 lakh crore achieved by end of FY24 (58% of target).

11.5 National Asset Reconstruction Company (NARCL — Bad Bank)

Set up in 2021, NARCL (also called the "bad bank") acquires stressed assets from banks and undertakes their resolution. It is backed by the government (51% ownership) and banks (49%). India Debt Resolution Company Ltd (IDRCL) manages the assets. NARCL has acquired ~₹2 lakh crore in bad loans from PSBs. The IBC (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code) 2016 provides the legal framework for resolution. The "bad bank" complements IBC by taking over large, complex NPAs for specialised resolution.

11.6 Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) and JAM Trinity

Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT): transfers government subsidies/welfare benefits directly into beneficiaries' bank accounts, eliminating intermediaries. ₹6.3 lakh crore transferred in FY23; saved ₹2.5 lakh crore via elimination of duplicate/fake beneficiaries (Aadhaar-based de-duplication).

JAM Trinity: Jan Dhan (financial inclusion accounts, 500M+ accounts) + Aadhaar (unique ID, 1.4B enrolments) + Mobile (1.2B phones). Together they enable: (i) Aadhaar-based e-KYC, (ii) Aadhaar Payment Bridge (APB) for DBT, (iii) Aadhaar Enabled Payment System (AEPS), (iv) UPI payments. PAHAL (DBT for LPG subsidy) saved ₹50,000 Cr through de-duplication. Other DBT schemes: PM-KISAN (farmers), MGNREGA wage payments, scholarship disbursements, fertiliser subsidies.

11.7 G20 Presidency (2023) and Key Outcomes

India held the G20 Presidency from December 2022 to November 2023, hosting the summit in New Delhi in September 2023 under the theme "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — One Earth, One Family, One Future."

Key Outcomes

  • New Delhi Leaders' Declaration: adopted unanimously with 122 paragraphs; included consensus language on the Ukraine-Russia war (refrained from blaming Russia, focused on human suffering and need for peace).
  • India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEEC): launched as a connectivity project linking India, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Israel, Greece, and Europe via rail, maritime, digitised customs, and green hydrogen infrastructure. Seen as a counter to China's Belt and Road Initiative.
  • Global Biofuels Alliance (GBA): launched by India, USA, Brazil, and others to promote sustainable biofuels (ethanol, biodiesel, sustainable aviation fuel).
  • One Earth One Health: focus on pandemic preparedness, One Health approach (human-animal-environment interface), digital health.
  • Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) Repository: India's UPI, Aadhaar, DigiLocker shared as global public goods; G20 agreed on a voluntary DPI repository.
  • TechFin and CBDC: G20 agreed on a roadmap for regulating cryptocurrencies (Financial Stability Board framework) and promoting CBDCs.
  • Gender Equality: establishment of a G20 Working Group on Women's Empowerment.
  • Green Development Pact: climate finance, sustainable development goals, loss and damage fund.
  • African Union: became permanent member of G20 — India strongly advocated for this.
Critical Analysis
While the G20 presidency enhanced India's global stature, outcomes like the IMEEC face implementation challenges (Gaza war delaying the Israel component, funding gaps). The leaders' declaration's Ukraine language was a diplomatic success (balanced between Russia and the West) but was criticised by some Western allies as too weak.

11.8 India Semiconductor Mission (ISM)

Launched in 2022 with an outlay of ₹76,000 crore (~$10B), the ISM aims to establish a comprehensive semiconductor ecosystem in India, reducing import dependence (India imports ~$40B of electronics chips annually). The mission is implemented by the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) Directorate under the Ministry of Electronics and IT. Key components: (i) Scheme for Setting up Semiconductor Fabs — financial support of up to 50% of project cost for establishing silicon CMOS fabrication plants; (ii) Scheme for Setting up Display Fabs — similar support for TFT LCD/AMOLED display manufacturing units; (iii) Scheme for Compound Semiconductors / Silicon Photonics / MEMS Fabs; (iv) Scheme for Semiconductor ATMP (Assembly, Testing, Marking, Packaging) units — support of up to 50% of capital expenditure. Approved projects: (i) Tata Electronics-PSMC joint venture — first commercial fab in Dholera (Gujarat), 50,000 wafer starts per month, targeting 2026; (ii) CG Power-Renesas ATMP unit in Gujarat; (iii) Kaynes Semicon ATMP unit; (iv) Micron's ATMP facility in Sanand (Gujarat) — $2.75B investment. The SemiconIndia conference and Semicon India Future Skills programme aim to develop the talent pipeline. India's strategy focuses on the semiconductor supply chain diversification — positioning India as an alternative to the current concentration in Taiwan (TSMC) and South Korea (Samsung), in line with the global "China Plus One" strategy. The US-India iCET (Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies) includes semiconductor cooperation as a key pillar.

11.9 Defence Manufacturing and Exports

India is the world's 2nd largest arms importer (after Saudi Arabia) but has aggressively pushed domestic production under the Atmanirbhar Bharat (Self-Reliant India) initiative. The defence production target is ₹1.75 lakh crore ($21B) by 2025-26. Key reforms: (i) Positive Indigenisation Lists — 5,000+ items banned from import (phased timelines); (ii) Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020 — preference to Indian vendors, longer lease periods, 50% R&D budget to private sector; (iii) FDI in defence raised to 74% (automatic route) and up to 100% through government route; (iv) Innovation for Defence Excellence (iDEX) — startup engagement platform for defence innovation; (v) Defence India Startup Challenge (DISC) — funding for dual-use technologies. Major defence export successes: (i) BrahMos supersonic cruise missile — exported to Philippines ($375M deal, first export); (ii) Akash missile system — interest from multiple countries; (iii) Tejas light combat aircraft (LCA) — having received Final Operational Clearance (FOC), export interest from Malaysia, Egypt, Argentina; (iv) Advanced Towed Artillery Gun System (ATAGS) — exported to Armenia; (v) WhAP (Wheeled Armoured Platform) by Tata Advanced Systems. Defence exports grew from ₹1,900 crore (FY14) to ₹21,083 crore (FY24), targeting ₹35,000 crore by FY26. The Defence Testing Infrastructure Scheme (DTIS) supports setting up testing facilities for defence products. The Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL), Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL), Bharat Dynamics Limited (BDL), Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders, and Garden Reach Shipbuilders are the major defence PSUs. Private sector players (Tata, Mahindra, L&T, Kalyani, Adani) are increasingly important in the defence ecosystem. The DRDO (Defence Research and Development Organisation) is the R&D backbone, with laboratories across domains (aerospace, armaments, electronics, naval systems, life sciences).

11.10 National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 and Human Capital

The National Education Policy 2020 replaced the 34-year-old National Policy on Education (NPE) 1986 and aims to transform India's education system to meet 21st-century needs. Key structural reforms: (i) 5+3+3+4 school curriculum structure — replacing the 10+2 system, with Foundational (ages 3-8), Preparatory (8-11), Middle (11-14), and Secondary (14-18) stages; (ii) multidisciplinary higher education — flexible undergraduate programmes with multiple entry/exit options (certificate after 1 year, diploma after 2, degree after 3/4 years); (iii) Academic Bank of Credits (ABC) — digital storage of earned credits for seamless transfer across institutions; (iv) National Research Foundation (NRF) — apex body for research funding and promotion; (v) Higher Education Commission of India (HECI) — single regulator replacing UGC, AICTE, and NCTE; (vi) National Testing Agency (NTA) — single-window common entrance test (CUET) for university admissions; (vii) vocational education integration — internships and vocational courses from Class 6 onwards, targeting 50% of learners exposed to vocational education by 2025; (viii) mother tongue/regional language instruction until Class 5 (ideally Class 8); (ix) technology integration — DIKSHA platform for e-content, SWAYAM for MOOCs, NDL (National Digital Library). The NEP targets 100% Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER) in school education by 2030 and 50% GER in higher education by 2035 (from ~27% in 2021). Implementation progress: 7 years of NEP will have been completed by 2027; challenges include state-level adoption (education is in the Concurrent List), teacher training (6 million teachers need upskilling), funding (target of 6% of GDP for education — currently ~3-4% of GDP), digital divide in rural areas, and resistance to structural changes. The NEP 2020 is critical for India's ability to reap its demographic dividend — a skilled and educated workforce is essential for transitioning from a lower-middle-income to a high-income economy.

11.11 India's Demographic Dividend

Demographic dividend is the economic growth potential resulting from a shift in the age structure when the working-age population (15-64) is larger than the dependent population (children + elderly). India is in the midst of this dividend.

Key Statistics

  • Median age: 28 years (vs China 38, Japan 48, USA 38).
  • Working-age population (15-64): ~68% of total population.
  • Peak dividend: India's demographic dividend is expected to peak around 2041, after which the dependency ratio will start rising.
  • IMF estimate: changing demographics contributed ~2% per annum to India's per capita GDP growth in the 2000s.

Challenges in Reaping the Dividend

  • Job creation: India needs 8-10 million new jobs annually just to keep pace with new entrants; current formal job creation is inadequate.
  • Skill gap: only ~5% of the Indian workforce has formal vocational training (vs 52% in USA, 80% in Japan). Low employability of graduates.
  • Low Female Labour Force Participation Rate (FLFP): ~37% (low by global standards; China ~60%, USA ~56%). Cultural barriers, safety concerns, lack of flexible work hours, wage gap.
  • Ageing before becoming rich: India is "growing old before growing rich." By 2050, 20% of India's population will be 60+ while still being a lower-middle-income country (unlike East Asian economies that grew rich before ageing).
  • Informal sector dominance: ~90% of workers are in the informal sector with low productivity, no social security, and limited upward mobility.
  • Regional disparities: southern states are ageing faster (Kerala — already approaching East Asian dependency ratios), while northern states (Bihar, UP) have higher fertility but lower human capital.
Policy Response
National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 aims to improve skill outcomes; PLI schemes target manufacturing job creation; production-linked employment schemes (like the proposed Employment Linked Incentive in FY25 budget); expanding MUDRA loans, Start-up India, and the gig/platform economy are partial responses to the jobs challenge.

11.9 India as a Developing Economy – Characteristics

  • Low per capita income: ~$2,500 nominal, ~$10,000 PPP (vs global avg $12,700). India is still a lower-middle-income country (World Bank classification).
  • High informal sector: ~90% of employment is informal; ~93% of firms are unorganised.
  • Infrastructure gaps: Ranked 50th in Global Competitiveness Index (infrastructure pillar); logistics cost ~14% of GDP (target 8%).
  • Inequality: Top 1% holds ~40% of wealth (Oxfam); Gini coefficient ~0.35 (moderate but rising).
  • Regional disparities: GSDP per capita of Goa ~$7,000 vs Bihar ~$1,000 (7:1 ratio).
  • Demographic duality: young population but skill deficit; aging workforce in some states.
  • External vulnerability: oil import dependence, FPI volatility, global demand sensitivity.
12. International Organisations and India

12.1 International Monetary Fund (IMF)

India is a founding member of the IMF (1944). India's quota is SDR 13.1 billion (~2.75% of total), making it the 8th largest shareholder (after US, Japan, China, Germany, UK, France, Italy). Key reforms:

  • 14th General Review of Quotas (2010): doubled total quotas from SDR 238B to SDR 477B; shifted 6% of quota shares to emerging economies. India's share rose from 2.44% to 2.75%.
  • 15th Review (2019): maintained existing quotas (no increase).
  • 16th Review (2023): 50% quota increase for all members (from SDR 477B to SDR 716B); no change in relative shares. New quota formula expected in future reviews to better reflect GDP of developing countries.
  • Special Drawing Rights (SDRs): India received an allocation of SDR 9.2B (~$12.6B) in August 2021 (part of the $650B global allocation to address COVID-19 liquidity needs). India's total SDR holdings are ~$18.5B.
  • IMF Quota Formula: based on GDP (50%), openness (30%), variability (15%), reserves (5%). Emerging economies argue the formula overweights GDP at market exchange rates (favouring developed countries) and should use PPP-based GDP.

India's Role in the IMF

  • India has an Executive Director (ED) representing India, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Sri Lanka.
  • India has been a consistent advocate for: (i) greater voice and representation for emerging economies, (ii) concessional financing for LICs (Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust), (iii) climate finance through the Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST), (iv) coordinated global response to debt crises.
  • IMF's Article IV Consultations with India provide policy recommendations on fiscal consolidation, inflation targeting, financial stability, and structural reforms.

12.2 World Bank Group

The World Bank Group comprises five institutions: IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development), IDA (International Development Association), IFC (International Finance Corporation), MIGA (Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency), ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes). India is a founder member.

India and IDA

  • India was the largest recipient of IDA concessional loans for decades.
  • India graduated from IDA in 2015 (crossed the per capita income threshold of $1,215).
  • India now accesses a blend of IBRD and IDA: IBRD (near-market rates) + IDA (concessional, limited volume).
  • India's current portfolio with the World Bank: ~$25B in 100+ projects (as of 2024).

India's World Bank Positions

  • India is the largest recipient of IBRD loans among all countries.
  • India has an Executive Director (ED) representing India, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Sri Lanka.
  • India hosts the South Asia Regional Office of the World Bank in New Delhi.
  • India advocated for the Pandemic Fund (2022) hosted by the World Bank.

12.3 World Trade Organization (WTO)

The WTO was established in 1995, succeeding the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) 1947. India is a founding member and a key voice for developing countries.

India's Key WTO Positions

  • Public Stockholding for Food Security: India demands a permanent solution to allow developing countries to stockpile grains at administered prices for food security without violating WTO subsidy limits (currently protected by a "peace clause" till 2035, extended at MC13 in Abu Dhabi 2024).
  • TRIPS Waiver for COVID Vaccines: India co-sponsored the proposal; resulted in a partial waiver (June 2022) for vaccine patents, limited to 5 years.
  • Fisheries Subsidies: India opposes disciplines that would restrict subsidies for small-scale and artisanal fisheries (the "development box"). At MC13, members reached an agreement banning IUU fishing subsidies but deferred the broader deal.
  • E-commerce Moratorium: India opposes extension of the moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions (says it denies developing countries revenue and prevents digital industrialisation). The moratorium was extended at MC13 until MC14 (2026).
  • Agriculture Special Safeguard Mechanism (SSM): India demands a new SSM enabling developing countries to raise tariffs temporarily when import surges hurt farmers.
  • Trade Disputes: India has been involved in many WTO disputes. Notable: (i) India lost the solar cells case (USA challenged India's domestic content requirement in National Solar Mission — found inconsistent with WTO rules), (ii) India lost the export subsidies case (USA challenged India's export incentive schemes — found to violate SCM Agreement), (iii) India won against USA on steel/aluminium tariffs (2023 panel).
  • Anti-Dumping: India is the highest user of anti-dumping measures among WTO members (protecting domestic industry from alleged underpriced imports).

India's WTO Accession and Commitments

India's commitments under the Agreement on Agriculture (AoA): (i) no minimum market access commitments, (ii) bound tariffs on agricultural products average ~50% (applied ~30%), (iii) domestic support is low (de minimis exemptions allow MSP operations). Under GATS, India committed to liberalisation of many services, especially IT, telecom, and financial services.

OECD?
India is not a member of the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) because membership requires high standards of economic freedom, transparency, and democratic governance. However, India is a member of the OECD Development Centre (since 2001) and participates as a Key Partner in many OECD committees.

12.4 Regional Development Banks

Asian Development Bank (ADB)

India is a founding member (1966). ADB's current portfolio in India is ~$25B across 70+ projects. India is the second largest borrower after Pakistan. Key sectors: transport (35%), energy (20%), urban development (15%), water/sanitation (10%), finance (10%). ADB's India Resident Mission is in New Delhi.

New Development Bank (NDB)

Founded by the BRICS nations in 2014 with initial authorised capital of $100B ($50B initial subscribed capital). India holds ~20% share. K.V. Kamath (India) served as the first President (2015-2020). The NDB's authorised capital was increased to ~$185B in 2023; subscribed capital stands at ~$41B. The NDB funds sustainable infrastructure and renewable energy projects in BRICS and other emerging economies. It has expanded membership to include UAE, Bangladesh, Egypt, Uruguay. As of 2024, NDB has approved ~$35B in projects.

Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)

Founded in 2016 on China's initiative; initial capital $100B. India is the second largest shareholder (after China) with ~8% voting share. AIIB has 100+ members. India is the largest recipient of AIIB financing (~$5B in 20+ projects). Key projects: Andhra Pradesh rural roads, Mumbai Metro, Delhi-Meerut RRTS, energy projects.

12.5 BRICS+

BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) was formally established in 2009. India is a founding member. Key institutions: (i) New Development Bank (NDB), (ii) Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA) — $100B fund for liquidity support during BoP crises (China $41B, India/Russia/Brazil $18B each, South Africa $5B). At the 2024 Johannesburg Summit, BRICS expanded to BRICS+ with 5 new full members: Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE, Saudi Arabia (also admission of Indonesia in 2025). The bloc now represents ~40% of global GDP (PPP) and ~45% of world population.

12.6 Other Multilateral Forums

  • Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO): India became a full member in 2017 (observer since 2005). Focus: security, counter-terrorism, trade, energy, connectivity. India hosted the SCO Summit in 2026 (virtual).
  • Quad (Quadrilateral Security Dialogue): India, USA, Japan, Australia. Focus: Indo-Pacific security, maritime domain awareness, critical technology, climate action, vaccine cooperation. QUAD+ format under consideration.
  • IPEF (Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity): launched 2022 by USA; India is a member. Pillars: trade, supply chains, clean economy, fair economy. India participates in 3 pillars (opted out of trade pillar due to labour/environment standards concerns).
  • BIMSTEC (Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation): India, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, Sri Lanka. Focus: connectivity, counter-terrorism, trade.
  • IOR-ARC (Indian Ocean Rim Association): 23 member states; India is a founding member and vice chair.
  • Commonwealth: India is a founding member (1947); largest member by population.
  • Global South Voice: India hosted Voice of Global South Summits (January 2023, November 2024) to articulate priorities of developing countries on debt, climate finance, UN reforms, SDGs, and G20 agenda.

12.7 India's Development Assistance (ODA)

India is a net donor of development assistance, particularly to its neighbours and Africa. India's annual ODA is ~$2.8B (soft loans and grants). Key instruments:

  • Lines of Credit (LoCs): extended through EXIM Bank of India to developing countries for infrastructure, industrial projects, railways, power plants, solar parks, hospitals. Major LoCs: Bangladesh ($8B), Sri Lanka ($4B), Nepal ($1B), Maldives, Myanmar, Africa ($10B+ under Team-9 initiative).
  • Grant Assistance: through Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) for health, education, capacity building, disaster relief, ITEC (Indian Technical and Economic Cooperation) training, Pan-African e-Network, and DNA (Development Partnership Administration).
  • ITEC Programme: 10,000+ slots annually for training in agriculture, IT, banking, management, defence, and entrepreneurship.
  • Neighbourhood First Policy: India's development assistance prioritises neighbours (SAARC members, Myanmar, Maldives, Afghanistan). Specific projects: Nepal hydropower, Bangladesh connectivity (rail, power), Sri Lanka housing and hospitals, Maldives social infrastructure, Myanmar border roads.

South-South Cooperation

India positions its development assistance as South-South Cooperation (SSC), distinct from traditional North-South aid. Principles: (i) demand-driven and concessional (not grants), (ii) non-conditional, (iii) respect for sovereignty, (iv) mutual benefit, (v) use of Indian expertise and products. India is a founding member of the UN Office for South-South Cooperation (UNOSSC) and actively participates in the BAPA+40 agenda.

12.8 Remittances to India

India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, with inflows of ~$120 billion in 2024 (World Bank estimate). This accounts for ~3.5% of India's GDP. Top sources: UAE (~$20B), USA (~$18B), Saudi Arabia (~$12B), Kuwait, Qatar, UK, Singapore, Australia. Remittances are a stable component of India's BoP (secondary income in current account), offsetting part of the trade deficit. The NRI deposit schemes (NRE, NRO, FCNR-B) also contribute to capital account inflows.

UPSC Exam Relevance
Questions on India's external sector frequently appear in GS Paper III (Indian Economy) and GS Paper II (International Relations). Key themes: (i) BoP dynamics — CAD financing, reserves adequacy, (ii) FDI vs FPI — stability, (iii) rupee volatility and RBI intervention, (iv) WTO disputes and India's negotiating strategy, (v) India's role in multilateral institutions — IMF quota reform, BRICS+ expansion, (vi) climate finance — developed vs developing country responsibilities. The inter-connectivity between these topics — e.g., how climate finance affects India's external debt, or how WTO agreements impact trade deficit — is a hallmark of UPSC's application-based approach.
