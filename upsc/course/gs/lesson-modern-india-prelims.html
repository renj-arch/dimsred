1. Foundations of Company Rule (1757‑1857)

1.1 The Battle of Plassey and the Grant of Diwani

The Battle of Plassey in 1757 marked the decisive military victory of the British East India Company over the Nawab of Bengal, Siraj‑ud‑Daula, and it opened the door for the Company to acquire the diwani, or revenue‑collecting rights, of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765. This grant transformed the Company from a commercial enterprise into a territorial power because it now controlled the fiscal resources of one of the richest provinces in the subcontinent. The revenue extracted from Bengal financed further military expansion, subsidised the Company’s trade deficits with China, and enabled the recruitment of Indian sepoys on a large scale. The political consequence was the gradual erosion of indigenous authority, as the Company began to intervene in succession disputes and to install compliant rulers, thereby establishing a pattern of indirect rule that would be replicated across India.

The diwani also created a new class of Indian intermediaries — zamindars, revenue farmers and merchants — who collaborated with the Company to maximise collections. Their cooperation was secured through the promise of hereditary rights and judicial privileges, which later became the basis of the Permanent Settlement. At the same time, the Company’s reliance on land revenue forced it to devise systematic assessment methods, leading to the first large‑scale cadastral surveys in Indian history. These surveys not only increased revenue certainty but also produced detailed maps that later aided military planning and railway construction.

1.2 The Regulating Act and the Establishment of Supreme Court

Parliament’s Regulating Act of 1773 was the first legislative attempt to bring the Company’s administration under parliamentary oversight. It created the office of Governor‑General of Bengal, vested in Warren Hastings, and established a Supreme Court at Calcutta with jurisdiction over British subjects and, controversially, over Indians in certain civil matters. The Act aimed to curb corruption by requiring the Company to submit annual accounts to Parliament and by leaving the Governor‑General’s tenure at the Crown’s pleasure. However, the vague definition of the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction led to frequent clashes between the executive council and the judiciary, most famously in the case of Raja Nand Kumar, whose execution for forgery highlighted the tension between English law and local customs.

The Regulating Act also introduced a Council of Four to assist the Governor‑General, but the Council’s decisions could be overruled by the Governor‑General’s casting vote, concentrating power in a single individual. This centralisation facilitated rapid decision‑making during military crises, yet it also meant that policy errors — such as the mismanagement of the Bengal famine of 1770 — were not addressed by the Act by collective deliberation. The Act’s legacy was a hybrid governance structure in which parliamentary sovereignty coexisted with the Company’s commercial autonomy, a tension that would persist until the Crown assumed direct control in 1858.

1.3 The Permanent Settlement and its Agrarian Consequences

In 1793 Lord Cornwallis introduced the Permanent Settlement in Bengal, fixing the land revenue demand permanently with the zamindars, who were recognised as proprietors of the soil. The intention was to create a loyal landed aristocracy that would invest in agricultural improvement, thereby guaranteeing a stable revenue stream for the Company. In practice, the settlement froze the revenue demand at a level that often exceeded the productive capacity of the land, compelling zamindars to extract maximal rents from cultivators. The result was widespread indebtedness, loss of occupancy rights for peasants, and the emergence of a class of absentee landlords who had little incentive to improve irrigation or soil fertility.

The Permanent Settlement also distorted the development of a market for land, because the revenue demand was immutable and could not be adjusted to reflect changes in productivity or price levels. Consequently, when the Company later introduced the Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems in Madras, Bombay and the North‑Western Provinces, those systems attempted to settle revenue directly with the cultivator or the village community, respectively, allowing periodic revisions. The contrast between the three systems illustrates how differing revenue philosophies shaped agrarian relations, social stratification, and the pace of commercialisation in each region.

2. Early Resistance and the Revolt of 1857

2.1 Precursors: Tribal and Peasant Uprisings

Long before the 1857 Revolt, the Company faced a series of localized rebellions that revealed the fragility of its authority. The Santhal Hul of 1855‑56 in the Rajmahal hills was a massive tribal uprising against exploitative moneylenders, zamindars and the colonial legal system that favoured them. Similarly, the Kol Rebellion of 1831‑32 in Chotanagpur and the Mappila outbreaks in Malabar reflected grievances over land alienation, forest restrictions and religious interference. These movements, though suppressed, demonstrated that resistance was not confined to the elite; it emerged from the very communities most affected by the Company’s revenue and forest policies.

The Company’s response combined military force with legal reforms such as the Forest Acts of 1865 and 1878, which sought to regulate tribal access to forest produce. However, the underlying causes — loss of customary rights, heavy taxation, and cultural insensitivity — remained unaddressed, creating a reservoir of discontent that fueled later tribal uprisings.

2.2 The Sepoy Mutiny: Causes and Outbreak

The immediate spark for the 1857 Revolt was the introduction of the new Enfield rifle cartridge, rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat, offending both Hindu and Muslim sepoys. Yet the deeper causes lay in the cumulative impact of the Doctrine of Lapse, which annexed princely states such as Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur; the abolition of the Mughal emperor’s pension; and the widespread perception that the Company was deliberately undermining Indian religions and social customs. The annexation of Awadh in 1856, justified on grounds of misgovernance, displaced a large number of nobles, soldiers and artisans, adding a political dimension to the military grievance.

On 10 May 1857, sepoys at Meerut refused to use the cartridges, were court‑martialed, and then broke open the jail, marching to Delhi where they proclaimed the aged Bahadur Shah Zafar as the symbolic emperor of Hindustan. The rebellion quickly spread to Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Gwalior and Bihar, drawing in disaffected zamindars, peasants and local rulers such as Nana Sahib, Rani Lakshmibai and Kunwar Singh. The geographic breadth of the uprising — covering the Gangetic plain, central India and parts of the Deccan — indicated that it was more than a mere mutiny; it was a concerted, albeit loosely coordinated, challenge to Company sovereignty.

2.3 Course of the Rebellion and British Suppression

The Company’s initial response was hampered by the dispersal of its European troops across the subcontinent and by the logistical difficulty of moving reinforcements from Britain. However, the arrival of the Punjab Movable Column under John Nicholson, the decisive victory at the Siege of Delhi in September 1857, and the subsequent relief of Lucknow in November turned the tide. The British employed a combination of superior artillery, telegraphic communication, and a policy of brutal reprisals — including the execution of captured rebels and the destruction of villages suspected of supporting the uprising — to crush resistance.

By mid‑1858 the major centres of rebellion had been recaptured, and the last significant leader, Tantia Tope, was captured and executed in April 1859. The suppression was marked by a deliberate policy of collective punishment, which left deep scars in the collective memory of the affected regions. The human cost, while never precisely quantified, was enormous, with entire communities displaced and agricultural production severely disrupted for years.

2.4 Consequences: End of Company Rule and the Proclamation of 1858

The Revolt of 1857 compelled the British Parliament to pass the Government of India Act 1858, which transferred sovereignty from the East India Company to the Crown. Queen Victoria’s Proclamation of 1 November 1858 promised respect for the rights, dignity and religious practices of Indian princes and peoples, and it declared that the British government would not interfere in the internal affairs of the native states. In practice, the proclamation inaugurated a new administrative architecture: a Secretary of State for India in London, a Viceroy in Calcutta, and an Indian Civil Service recruited through competitive examinations.

The Act also reorganised the army, reducing the proportion of Indian sepoys and increasing the number of British troops, while ensuring that artillery and key strategic posts remained under European control. The experience of 1857 thus reshaped the colonial state into a more centralized, militarised and bureaucratically controlled entity, setting the stage for the next phase of Indian nationalism.

Example Question
Explain how the Permanent Settlement of 1793 altered the relationship between the state, zamindars and peasants in Bengal, and why it led to long‑term agrarian distress.
The Permanent Settlement fixed the land revenue demand permanently with the zamindars, making them the de‑facto proprietors of the soil. The state thus transferred its fiscal risk to the zamindars, who were guaranteed a hereditary right to collect revenue provided they paid the fixed sum to the Company. Because the revenue demand was set at a high level and could not be revised, zamindars maximised short‑term extraction from cultivators, often through coercive rent‑collection and the imposition of illegal cesses. Peasants lost customary occupancy rights, became tenants‑at‑will, and fell into chronic indebtedness to moneylenders who were frequently the same zamindars. The absence of a mechanism to adjust revenue to agricultural productivity meant that any decline in output — due to floods, droughts or market fluctuations — translated directly into peasant impoverishment without relief to the state. Over time this created a rigid agrarian hierarchy, stunted capital formation in agriculture, and contributed to the recurrent famines of the late nineteenth century.
Exam Insight
When a question asks for the “impact” of a revenue settlement, structure the answer by tracing the chain: state policy → zamindar incentives → peasant outcomes → long‑term structural change. Examiners reward answers that link the fixed revenue demand to the emergence of a rentier class and to the stagnation of agricultural investment.

3. Rise of Organized Nationalism (1885‑1919)

3.1 Formation of the Indian National Congress and Early Moderates

The Indian National Congress was founded in December 1885 at Bombay on the initiative of Allan Octavian Hume, a retired civil servant who believed that a platform for educated Indians to petition the government would prevent more radical unrest. The early Congress was dominated by moderates such as Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta and Gopal Krishna Gokhale, who advocated constitutional agitation — petitions, resolutions, and deputations to the British Parliament — to secure incremental reforms. Their economic critique, epitomised by Naoroji’s “drain theory”, argued that Britain siphoned India’s wealth through home charges, railways built with Indian capital, and unequal trade, thereby impoverishing the peasantry.

The moderates achieved modest successes: the Indian Councils Act 1892 expanded legislative councils and introduced limited indirect elections; the establishment of the Royal Commission on Decentralisation (1907) reflected their pressure for administrative reform. However, their reliance on the goodwill of the British authorities and their exclusion of the masses limited the Congress’s political reach. The partition of Bengal in 1905, justified on administrative grounds but widely perceived as a divide‑and‑rule tactic, galvanised a more assertive nationalist response and marked the beginning of the extremist phase.

3.2 The Extremist Phase and the Swadeshi Movement

Leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Lala Lajpat Rai — collectively known as the Lal‑Bal‑Pal trio — rejected the moderates’ petitioning in favour of mass mobilisation, boycott of British goods (swadeshi), and the promotion of indigenous education and industry. The Swadeshi Movement, centred in Bengal but resonating across the country, saw the establishment of national schools, the revival of hand‑spinning, and the organisation of volunteer corps (samitis) that enforced the boycott. The movement also produced a vibrant cultural renaissance, with Rabindranath Tagore’s songs and Bankim Chandra Chatterjee’s “Vande Mataram” becoming rallying symbols.

The British responded with the repressive measures of the 1908 Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act and the 1910 Indian Press Act, while simultaneously offering the Morley‑Minto Reforms of 1909, which introduced separate electorates for Muslims. The reforms were intended to split the nationalist movement along communal lines, but they also institutionalised communal representation, a legacy that would shape future constitutional negotiations. The extremist phase waned after the 1908 deportation of Tilak to Mandalay and the 1911 annulment of the Bengal partition, yet it left a lasting template for mass civil resistance.

3.3 The Home Rule Movement and the Lucknow Pact

During the First World War, Annie Besant and Tilak launched parallel Home Rule Leagues in 1916, demanding self‑government within the British Empire on the model of Irish home rule. The leagues mobilised students, professionals and women, and they forced the British to confront the contradiction of fighting for democracy abroad while denying it at home. The war also created an opportunity for the Congress and the All‑India Muslim League to cooperate, culminating in the Lucknow Pact of 1916, where the Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims in exchange for the League’s support for a joint demand for self‑government.

The Pact represented a high point of Hindu‑Muslim political unity, but it also entrenched the principle of communal electorates, which later became a structural obstacle to a unified nationalist front. The British response was the Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms of 1919, which introduced dyarchy in the provinces — a limited transfer of certain departments to elected Indian ministers while retaining control over finance, law and order at the centre. The reforms fell short of the Home Rule demand, leading to widespread disappointment and setting the stage for the next wave of mass agitation.

3.4 The Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh and the Emergence of Gandhi

The Rowlatt Act of 1919, officially the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, extended wartime emergency powers — detention without trial, censorship, and restrictions on public gatherings — into peacetime. Its passage provoked nationwide protests, and on 13 April 1919 General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, killing hundreds. The massacre shocked Indian public opinion and turned many former moderates toward more radical methods. It was in this climate that Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, who had returned from South Africa in 1915, launched his first all‑India satyagraha against the Rowlatt Act, calling for a nationwide hartal on 6 April 1919.

Gandhi’s strategy combined non‑violent non‑cooperation with a moral appeal to the conscience of the oppressor, a method he had refined in South Africa. The Rowlatt satyagraha, though partially suppressed, demonstrated the potential of mass civil disobedience to paralyse colonial administration. It also marked Gandhi’s emergence as the undisputed leader of the nationalist movement, a position he would consolidate through the Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑22), the Salt March (1930) and the Quit India Movement (1942).

4. Gandhian Mass Movements (1919‑1942)

4.1 Non‑Cooperation Movement and the Khilafat Alliance

In 1920 Gandhi called for the Non‑Cooperation Movement, urging Indians to boycott government schools, courts, legislative councils and foreign cloth, and to resign from government service. The movement was uniquely broadened by an alliance with the Khilafat Movement, led by the Ali brothers, which sought to protect the Ottoman Caliphate after the First World War. This Hindu‑Muslim unity brought massive participation from peasants, artisans, students and women, and it transformed the Congress from an elite debating society into a mass organisation with a network of volunteers (satyagrahis) across the country.

The movement reached its peak with the boycott of the Prince of Wales’s visit in 1921 and the widespread adoption of khadi as a symbol of self‑reliance. However, the violent incident at Chauri Chaura in February 1922, where a police station was burnt and twenty‑two policemen killed, prompted Gandhi to suspend the movement unilaterally, arguing that the moral purity of non‑violence had been compromised. The suspension disappointed many radicals, but it reinforced Gandhi’s conviction that the means must be consistent with the ends, a principle that would guide all subsequent campaigns.

4.2 Civil Disobedience and the Salt March

After a period of constructive work — promotion of khadi, village sanitation, and Hindu‑Muslim harmony — Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1930 with the iconic Salt March from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi, a 240‑mile walk to produce salt in defiance of the British monopoly. The march captured global attention, drawing journalists and sympathisers, and it sparked parallel acts of defiance across India: illegal salt production, refusal to pay land revenue, boycott of foreign cloth, and the picketing of liquor shops. The British responded with mass arrests — over 60,000 satyagrahis were imprisoned — and with the Gandhi‑Irwin Pact of 1931, which secured the release of political prisoners and the right to make salt for personal use in exchange for the suspension of civil disobedience and participation in the Second Round Table Conference in London.

The conference, however, failed to produce a substantive constitutional advance because the British insisted on safeguards for minorities and princely states, while Gandhi demanded full responsible government. The deadlock led to the renewal of civil disobedience in 1932, which was met with harsher repression, including the Communal Award of 1932 that granted separate electorates to the Depressed Classes. Gandhi’s fast unto death in Yerwada Jail forced the Poona Pact, which replaced separate electorates with reserved seats within a joint electorate, a compromise that shaped the representation of Dalits in subsequent legislatures.

4.3 The Government of India Act 1935 and Provincial Elections

The Government of India Act 1935 was the most extensive constitutional measure before independence. It introduced provincial autonomy, a federal structure (never fully implemented), and a greatly expanded franchise — about 30 million voters, roughly one‑sixth of the adult population. The Act also retained the dyarchy at the centre, kept defence and foreign affairs under the Viceroy, and provided for separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Europeans, Anglo‑Indians and the Depressed Classes. The Congress contested the 1937 provincial elections under the Act and won absolute majorities in five provinces and formed coalition governments in two others, demonstrating its claim to represent the Indian masses.

Congress ministries undertook social reforms — tenant protection, debt relief, primary education expansion — but their tenure was cut short by the outbreak of the Second World War in 1939. The Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, declared India a belligerent without consulting Indian leaders, prompting the Congress to resign its ministries in protest. The resignation created a political vacuum that the Muslim League, under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, filled by observing a “Day of Deliverance” and by advancing the demand for a separate Muslim state, articulated in the Lahore Resolution of 1940.

4.4 Quit India Movement and the Final Phase of Mass Struggle

The failure of the Cripps Mission in 1942, which offered dominion status after the war but retained British control over defence, convinced Gandhi that only an immediate British withdrawal could prevent India from being drawn deeper into the war. On 8 August 1942 the All‑India Congress Committee passed the Quit India Resolution, calling for a mass non‑violent struggle to compel the British to “Quit India”. Gandhi’s “Do or Die” speech ignited spontaneous uprisings across the country — strikes, sabotage of railways and telegraph lines, attacks on police stations — despite the immediate arrest of the entire Congress leadership.

The British responded with overwhelming force, deploying over 100,000 troops, imposing press censorship, and banning the Congress. The movement was largely crushed by the end of 1943, but it demonstrated that the colonial state could no longer rely on the loyalty of the Indian army or the bureaucracy. The war’s end in 1945, the Labour Party’s victory in Britain, and the mounting global and domestic pressures forced the British to negotiate a transfer of power, culminating in the Cabinet Mission of 1946 and, ultimately, the Mountbatten Plan of 1947.

5. Partition and Transfer of Power (1942‑1947)

5.1 The Cabinet Mission and the Failure of a United India

The Cabinet Mission of March 1946, comprising three British ministers, proposed a three‑tier constitutional framework: a weak centre handling defence, foreign affairs and communications; groups of provinces (Group A – Hindu‑majority, Group B – Muslim‑majority in the northwest, Group C – Muslim‑majority in the east) with the option to secede after ten years; and a constituent assembly to draft the constitution.

The Morley‑Minto Reforms of 1909 and the Introduction of Separate Electorates

The Indian Councils Act 1909, commonly known as the Morley‑Minto Reforms, marked the first statutory attempt to associate Indians with the legislative process at the provincial and central levels. The Act increased the number of additional members of the Imperial Legislative Council from 16 to 60, of whom 27 were to be elected by special constituencies representing landholders, chambers of commerce, universities and Muslim communities. For the first time, the principle of separate electorates was institutionalised: Muslim voters could elect their own representatives, a concession granted after the Simla Deputation (1906) led by the Aga Khan. The reforms also enlarged provincial legislative councils and granted them the right to discuss the budget and ask questions, though they retained no power to vote on supply or to initiate legislation. The franchise remained extremely narrow — roughly one percent of the adult male population — and the electorate was weighted heavily toward propertied and educated elites. While the Act was welcomed by moderates such as Gopal Krishna Gokhale as a step toward responsible government, nationalists criticised it for entrenching communal divisions and for preserving real executive authority in the hands of the Viceroy and his Executive Council. The separate electorate provision became a structural feature of subsequent constitutional schemes and is widely regarded as a constitutional antecedent of the two‑nation theory that culminated in Partition.

  • Expanded central council to 60 additional members with 27 elected seats.
  • Introduced separate Muslim electorates — first statutory communal representation.
  • Provincial councils enlarged; limited budget discussion rights.
  • Franchise restricted to property, education and tax‑paying criteria.
  • Executive remained unaccountable to the legislature.

The Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms and the Government of India Act 1919: Dyarchy in Practice

The Government of India Act 1919, drafted on the basis of the Montagu‑Chelmsford Report, introduced the system of dyarchy in the provinces — a dual form of government in which certain subjects were transferred to Indian ministers responsible to the legislative council, while others remained reserved under the direct control of the Governor and his Executive Council. Transferred subjects included education, public health, agriculture, and local self‑government; reserved subjects comprised law and order, finance, land revenue, and irrigation. The Act also created a bicameral central legislature: the Council of State (60 members) and the Legislative Assembly (145 members), both with a majority of elected members, though the franchise was still limited to about ten percent of the adult male population. The Viceroy retained overriding powers, including the ability to certify bills, issue ordinances, and control the budget. The reforms were a response to the Home Rule Movement, the Lucknow Pact, and the pressure generated by India’s contribution to the First World War. In practice, dyarchy proved cumbersome: ministers lacked control over finance, and the Governor’s reserved powers often paralysed transferred departments. The 1923 and 1926 elections saw the Swaraj Party enter the councils to obstruct the system from within, while the Simon Commission (1927) was appointed to review the Act’s working, leading eventually to the Government of India Act 1935.

  • Dyarchy: transferred vs. reserved subjects at provincial level.
  • Bicameral central legislature with expanded elected majority.
  • Franchise extended to roughly 10 % of adult males.
  • Viceroy’s overriding powers (certification, ordinances, budget control).
  • Dyarchy’s operational failures prompted the 1935 Act.
Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes the 1909 and 1919 reforms?” Options: (a) 1909 introduced dyarchy; 1919 introduced separate electorates. (b) 1909 introduced separate electorates; 1919 introduced dyarchy. (c) Both introduced dyarchy. (d) Both introduced separate electorates.
The correct answer is (b). The 1909 Act introduced separate electorates for Muslims, while the 1919 Act introduced provincial dyarchy. Dyarchy did not exist in 1909, and separate electorates were not a feature of the 1919 Act (they were retained from 1909). Recognising the chronological order of these constitutional innovations is a frequent test of precise knowledge.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question compares the 1909 and 1919 Acts, first identify the unique feature of each: separate electorates (1909) and dyarchy (1919). Then eliminate options that attribute the wrong feature to the wrong year. A quick mental table — Year | Key Innovation — saves time and avoids the common confusion between communal representation and provincial autonomy.

Revolutionary Nationalism: The Hindustan Republican Association, the HSRA, the Kakori Conspiracy and the Chittagong Armoury Raid

While the mainstream Congress pursued constitutional agitation, a parallel stream of revolutionary nationalism emerged, convinced that armed struggle was indispensable for overthrowing colonial rule. The Hindustan Republican Association (HRA), formed in 1924, carried out the Kakori Train Robbery of 9 August 1925, in which a group of revolutionaries looted the government treasury being transported on the Saharanpur‑Lucknow passenger train. The raid yielded modest funds but generated enormous propaganda value; the subsequent trial and the execution of four leaders — Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Roshan Singh and Rajendra Lahiri — turned them into martyrs and inspired a new generation of radicals. In 1928, the HRA was reorganised as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) by Chandrasekhar Azad, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev Thapar and others, adding a socialist orientation. The HSRA conducted the Lahore Conspiracy Case (1929‑30) following the assassination of British police officer John Saunders, which led to the trial and execution of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru in March 1931. Separately, the Chittagong Armoury Raid of 1930, led by Surya Sen (Masterda), aimed to seize arms from police and auxiliary depots, declare a provisional government, and spark a wider uprising. Although the raiders captured the armoury, they failed to secure the ammunition store and were eventually surrounded; Surya Sen was captured in 1933 and hanged in 1934. These actions, while militarily limited, forced the British to confront the depth of anti‑colonial sentiment among youth and contributed to the hardening of repressive legislation such as the Public Safety Bill and the Trade Disputes Act.

  • HRA formed 1924; Kakori robbery 1925 — propaganda coup, four executions.
  • HSRA founded 1928; socialist‑revolutionary synthesis.
  • Lahore Conspiracy Case 1929‑30 — Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Rajguru executed 1931.
  • Chittagong Armoury Raid 1930 — Surya Sen, provisional government attempt.
  • Revolutionary actions prompted harsher colonial laws.

The Royal Indian Navy Mutiny of 1946 and the Acceleration of British Withdrawal

In February 1946, ratings of the Royal Indian Navy (RIN) stationed at Bombay (now Mumbai) launched a strike that rapidly spread to naval establishments in Karachi, Calcutta, Madras and other ports, involving over 20,000 sailors. The immediate grievances were racial discrimination, poor pay, substandard food, and the continued use of Indian personnel in a war that had ended. The mutineers hoisted the flags of the Congress, the Muslim League and the Communist Party, signalling a rare moment of cross‑communal solidarity. The British responded with a combination of negotiations and force: Admiral Godfrey offered concessions on pay and conditions, while the Royal Navy and British troops were placed on standby. The Congress leadership, wary of uncontrolled violence, urged the ratings to surrender, and the Muslim League similarly called for a return to duty. By 23 February the strike collapsed after the ratings accepted a settlement that promised an inquiry into their demands. Although the mutiny was short‑lived, it demonstrated that the loyalty of the Indian armed forces — long considered the bedrock of British rule — could no longer be taken for granted. The episode, together with the simultaneous INA trials and the widespread unrest in the countryside, convinced the Labour government in London that a negotiated transfer of power was the only viable exit strategy, hastening the dispatch of the Cabinet Mission in March 1946.

  • RIN mutiny began 18 February 1946 at Bombay; spread to major ports.
  • Over 20,000 ratings participated; cross‑communal flag hoisting.
  • Grievances: racial discrimination, pay, food, post‑war demobilisation.
  • British response: limited concessions, threat of force.
  • Congress and League urged surrender; strike ended 23 February.
  • Mutiny signalled erosion of military loyalty, accelerated Cabinet Mission.

The Integration of Princely States: The Instrument of Accession, the Role of Sardar Patel and the States' Ministry

At the time of independence, British India comprised 562 princely states covering roughly 40 % of the subcontinent’s territory and 23 % of its population. The Indian Independence Act 1947 gave each ruler the option to accede to either India or Pakistan; with the lapse of paramountcy, some rulers considered remaining independent. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as the first Home Minister and Minister of States, headed the newly created States' Ministry, assisted by V. P. Menon as Secretary. The ministry employed a combination of diplomacy, political pressure and, where necessary, military action to secure accession. The Instrument of Accession, a legal document, transferred control of defence, external affairs and communications to the Dominion of India while preserving the ruler’s internal autonomy. Most states acceded peacefully by 15 August 1947. Three major exceptions required forceful integration: Junagadh, where the Nawab acceded to Pakistan despite a Hindu‑majority population; a plebiscite in February 1948 confirmed the people’s desire to join India. Hyderabad, the largest and richest state, resisted under the Nizam; the Indian Army launched Operation Polo in September 1948, leading to the Nizam’s surrender and the state’s merger. Jammu and Kashmir’s Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession in October 1947 after tribal incursions from Pakistan, triggering the first Indo‑Pak war and a UN‑mediated ceasefire that left the region divided. By 1950, the States' Ministry had merged the remaining states into the Indian Union, reorganising them into Part A, B, C and D categories before the States Reorganisation Act 1956 created linguistic states.

  • 562 princely states at independence; option to accede to India or Pakistan (independence a de facto possibility after lapse of paramountcy).
  • Instrument of Accession transferred defence, external affairs, communications.
  • Sardar Patel and V. P. Menon led the States' Ministry.

The Communal Award, the Poona Pact and the Reshaping of Electoral Politics (1932‑1935)

In August 1932 British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald announced the Communal Award, a unilateral decision that extended separate electorates to the “Depressed Classes” (later termed Scheduled Castes) in addition to the existing Muslim, Sikh, European and Anglo‑Indian constituencies. The Award allocated 71 seats for the Depressed Classes in provincial legislatures, to be filled through separate electorates. Mahatma Gandhi, then imprisoned in Yerwada, viewed the measure as a British attempt to fracture Hindu unity and began a fast‑unto‑death on 20 September 1932. The fast forced a negotiated settlement: on 24 September 1932 B. R. Ambedkar and Gandhi signed the Poona Pact, which replaced separate electorates with a system of reserved seats filled by a joint electorate. The Pact increased the number of reserved seats to 148 in the provincial legislatures and 18 in the Central Legislative Assembly, while retaining the principle of reservation for the Depressed Classes.

The Poona Pact had immediate constitutional consequences. The Government of India Act 1935 incorporated the reserved‑seat formula, embedding it in the provincial autonomy framework. It also shaped the political trajectory of Ambedkar, who later founded the Independent Labour Party (1936) and the Scheduled Castes Federation (1942), arguing that reserved seats alone could not guarantee genuine representation without separate electorates. For the Congress, the Pact was a tactical victory that preserved a united Hindu front, yet it sowed the seeds of a distinct Dalit political identity that would re‑emerge forcefully in the 1940s and after independence. The Award‑Pact episode illustrates how British constitutional engineering, nationalist pressure and intra‑community negotiation intertwined to produce a durable template for affirmative action in Indian legislatures.

The Cripps Mission, the Failure of Negotiated Settlement and the Road to Quit India (1942)

In March 1942 Sir Stafford Cripps, a senior member of the British War Cabinet, arrived in New Delhi with a proposal designed to secure Indian cooperation in the Second World War. The Cripps Offer promised dominion status after the war, a constituent assembly elected by provincial legislatures to frame a new constitution, and the right of any province to opt out of the future union — a concession aimed at the Muslim League’s demand for Pakistan. The Congress Working Committee, meeting at Wardha, rejected the offer on 7 April 1942, citing the absence of an immediate transfer of power, the retention of the Viceroy’s veto, and the opt‑out clause which they viewed as a blueprint for partition. The Muslim League, under Jinnah, also dismissed the plan because it did not explicitly concede a sovereign Pakistan. The failure of the mission left a political vacuum that the Congress filled by launching the Quit India Movement on 8 August 1942, demanding an immediate British withdrawal. The British responded with mass arrests — including Gandhi, Nehru and the entire Congress leadership — and severe repression, which radicalised large sections of the peasantry and student bodies. The Cripps Mission thus stands as the last serious British attempt at a negotiated settlement before the final phase of mass civil disobedience.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about the Cripps Mission is correct? (a) It offered immediate independence. (b) It proposed a constituent assembly elected by universal adult franchise. (c) It allowed provinces to opt out of the future Indian union. (d) It was accepted by the Congress.”
The correct answer is (c). The Cripps Offer explicitly permitted any province to opt out of the proposed union, a provision intended to accommodate the Muslim League’s demand for a separate state. It did not grant immediate independence (a), the constituent assembly was to be elected by provincial legislatures, not by universal adult franchise (b), and the Congress rejected the proposal (d).

Memorise the chronological chain: Cripps Mission (March 1942) → Quit India Movement (August 1942) → Cabinet Mission (March 1946) → Mountbatten Plan (June 1947). Linking each event to its immediate predecessor prevents confusion between similar‑sounding offers.

Subhas Chandra Bose, the Indian National Army and the Azad Hind Government (1942‑1945)

After escaping house arrest in Calcutta in January 1941, Subhas Chandra Bose travelled via Afghanistan and the Soviet Union to Berlin, where he sought Axis support for an armed liberation struggle. In 1943 he moved to Japanese‑occupied Southeast Asia, assumed leadership of the Indian Independence League, and reorganised the Indian National Army (INA) — originally formed by Captain Mohan Singh from Indian prisoners of war captured in Malaya. On 21 October 1943 Bose proclaimed the Provisional Government of Azad Hind in Singapore, which was recognised by Japan, Germany, Italy and several other Axis states. The INA, numbering around 40,000 at its peak, included the Rani of Jhansi Regiment, an all‑women combat unit led by Captain Lakshmi Sahgal. The INA participated in the Japanese offensives against British positions in Arakan and the Imphal‑Kohima sector (March‑July 1944). Although the military campaign ended in retreat, the INA’s existence and the subsequent Red Fort trials (November 1945‑May 1946) galvanised public opinion across India, forcing the British to confront the untenability of their rule. The trials sparked massive demonstrations, mutinies in the Royal Indian Navy and a surge in nationalist sentiment that accelerated the transfer of power.

The Role of the Vernacular Press and Literature in Mobilising Mass Nationalism (1870‑1947)

From the late nineteenth century, newspapers published in Indian languages became the primary vehicle for disseminating nationalist ideas beyond the English‑educated elite. Bal Gangadhar Tilak’s Marathi weekly Kesari (1881) and its English counterpart The Mahratta turned the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals into mass political mobilisation tools. In Bengal, Amrita Bazar Patrika (1868) and Bengalee (1882) carried the writings of Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, whose novel Anandamath (1882) supplied the hymn “Vande Mataram”. The Tamil poet Subramania Bharati used India and Swadesamitran to fuse Tamil cultural pride with anti‑colonial resistance. In the Hindi belt, Premchand’s stories in Madhuri and Hans highlighted peasant suffering, while the Urdu press — notably Zamindar and Al‑Hilal — articulated Muslim nationalist and pan‑Islamic perspectives. British attempts to curb this influence culminated in the Vernacular Press Act 1878, the Indian Press Act 1910 and the Press (Emergency Powers) Act 1931, each met with widespread defiance, underground printing and the emergence of cyclostyled newsletters. The press thus functioned as a parallel public sphere, translating elite political discourse into idioms accessible to peasants, artisans and women, and sustaining a continuous narrative of resistance across linguistic regions.

The Impact of the Great Depression on Indian Agrarian Economy and Nationalist Strategy (1929‑1939)

The global price collapse after 1929 halved the export value of Indian cotton, wheat and jute, while land revenue demands remained fixed in rupees. Peasants in the Deccan, United Provinces and Bengal faced mounting indebtedness, leading to a wave of no‑rent and no‑tax campaigns — such as the Kisan Sabha agitations in Bihar (1930‑31); the earlier Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) had already demonstrated the potential of peasant resistance. The economic distress radicalised the Congress left wing; at the 1936 Faizpur session the All‑India Kisan Sabha was formed under Sahajanand Saraswati, demanding debt relief, tenancy reform and a moratorium on revenue collection. The 1937 provincial elections, fought on a platform that combined civil liberties with agrarian relief, brought Congress ministries to power in seven provinces, where they enacted tenancy legislation (e.g., the Bihar Tenancy Act 1938) and reduced land revenue in some areas. However, the outbreak of the Second World War in 1939 forced the resignation of Congress ministries, and the subsequent “Deliverance Day” declared by the Muslim League highlighted the growing communal divide. The Depression thus reshaped nationalist strategy from a primarily constitutional agitation to a mass‑based movement that linked political freedom with economic justice.

  • Overlooking the Vernacular Press Act 1878 as a temporary measure — it remained in force until 1921 and shaped press‑freedom jurisprudence.

  • Mixing the dates of the Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) with the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930) — they are distinct campaigns with different leadership and demands.
  • Believing the 1937 Congress ministries fully implemented Kisan Sabha demands — most reforms were limited and reversed after the ministries resigned in 1939.

The Economic Drain Theory and Deindustrialisation (1858‑1947)

The “drain of wealth” argument was first systematised by Dadabhai Naoroji in Poverty and Un‑British Rule in India (1901). He identified the annual outflow of British‑controlled revenues — home charges, interest on public debt, pensions of British officials, military expenditure incurred in India but charged to the Indian treasury, and the surplus of exports over imports — as a transfer of real resources from India to Britain without equivalent return. Naoroji estimated the drain at tens of millions of pounds annually in the early twentieth century, a figure later refined by R. C. Dutt in The Economic History of India (1901‑03). The drain was institutionalised through the “home charges” voted by the British Parliament and the fixed land‑revenue demands that left peasants with little surplus for reinvestment.

Simultaneously, colonial trade policy turned India into a supplier of raw cotton, jute, opium and wheat while flooding its markets with machine‑made British textiles. The hand‑loom weaving sector, which employed millions in Bengal, the Deccan and the Punjab, declined sharply between 1850 and 1900; the number of hand‑looms fell from millions to a few hundred thousand. The decline was accelerated by the removal of import duties on British cloth (1882) and the imposition of excise duties on Indian yarn (1894). The resulting deindustrialisation forced artisans into agricultural labour, swelling the landless proletariat and deepening rural indebtedness.

Land‑revenue systems amplified the drain. The Permanent Settlement (1793) fixed zamindari demands in perpetuity, encouraging rack‑renting and absentee landlordism. The Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems, though theoretically based on periodic settlement, were revised upward whenever prices rose, leaving cultivators with a shrinking share of the produce. The combination of fixed revenue, export‑oriented cropping patterns and the drain of surplus capital stunted capital formation in agriculture and industry alike.

By the 1930s the drain had become a central plank of nationalist economic critique. The Congress’s 1931 Karachi Resolution demanded “the abolition of the drain” and the nationalisation of key industries. The Great Depression intensified the crisis: export values fell sharply while revenue demands remained unchanged, triggering no‑rent campaigns and the rise of the All‑India Kisan Sabha (1936). The drain thesis thus linked political subjection to material impoverishment, providing a quantitative backbone for the demand for Purna Swaraj.

The Impact of the First World War on Indian Nationalism and the Montagu Declaration (1914‑1919)

India’s contribution to the First World War was massive: over a million men served in the British Indian Army, and the Indian treasury supplied a war loan of around £100 million in 1917 alone. Wartime inflation, driven by the suspension of the gold standard and the printing of currency notes, roughly doubled prices between 1914 and 1918. The burden fell disproportionately on peasants and urban workers, while the colonial government increased land revenue and introduced a new income tax (1917) to finance the war effort.

The war also reshaped political expectations. The 1916 Lucknow Pact between the Congress and the Muslim League had already signalled a united demand for self‑government. In August 1917 Edwin Montagu, Secretary of State for India, announced in the House of Commons the “progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire”. The Montagu Declaration promised “increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration” and laid the groundwork for the Montagu‑Chelmsford Reforms of 1919.

Nationalist leaders seized the moment. Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Annie Besant launched the Home Rule League (1916) demanding dominion status on the Irish model. The 1918‑1919 Rowlatt Acts, which extended wartime emergency powers into peacetime, provoked widespread protests culminating in the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (April 1919). The massacre, together with the perceived betrayal of the Montagu promise, radicalised a generation and propelled Mohandas Gandhi into the leadership of the Congress.

Economically, the war accelerated the growth of Indian-owned industries — cotton mills in Bombay and Ahmedabad, jute mills in Calcutta, and the Tata Iron and Steel Company at Jamshedpur — because British imports were disrupted. This nascent industrial class began to finance nationalist activities, creating a material base for mass mobilisation. The war thus acted as a catalyst: it expanded the fiscal-military extraction that fuelled the drain, while simultaneously generating the political rhetoric and organisational capacity that made the post‑war nationalist upsurge possible.

Source‑Based Analysis
A 1918 British parliamentary paper acknowledged India's unprecedented contribution of men, money, and material to the war effort. Using this acknowledgment, explain how the war altered the relationship between the colonial state and Indian nationalist leaders.
The statement acknowledges the scale of Indian sacrifice, which nationalist leaders cited to demand political reciprocity. The Montagu Declaration (1917) was a direct response, promising responsible government. However, the simultaneous enactment of the Rowlatt Acts (1919) showed the state’s reluctance to relinquish coercive power. This contradiction — recognition of contribution versus denial of rights — fuelled the Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑22) and shifted the Congress from petitioning to mass civil disobedience. The war therefore transformed the nationalist discourse from loyalist cooperation to a claim of entitlement based on shared sacrifice.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links war‑time contributions to constitutional reforms, structure the answer in three steps: (1) quantify the contribution (men, money, material), (2) identify the British response (Montagu Declaration, 1919 Act), and (3) evaluate the nationalist reaction (Home Rule, Rowlatt protests, Gandhi’s rise). This causal chain satisfies both “cause‑effect” and “change‑continuity” rubrics.

The Communist Party of India, Workers' and Peasants' Movements (1920‑1947)

The Communist Party of India (CPI) was formally founded at the Kanpur Conference in December 1925, though Marxist circles had existed since the 1917 October Revolution inspired figures such as M. N. Roy, S. A. Dange and Muzaffar Ahmed. The party’s early work focused on organising industrial labour in Bombay, Calcutta and Kanpur. The 1928 Bombay textile strike, led by the Girni Kamgar Union under Communist influence, involved over a hundred thousand workers and lasted several months, securing significant wage concessions.

In the agrarian sphere, the CPI played a pivotal role in the formation of the All‑India Kisan Sabha (AIKS) at the 1936 Lucknow session of the Congress. Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, a peasant leader from Bihar, became its first president, but Communist cadres such as N. G. Ranga and E. M. S. Namboodiripad provided organisational backbone. The AIKS demanded abolition of zamindari, reduction of rent, debt relief and security of tenure. The 1938‑39 Bakasht movement in Bihar and the 1946‑47 Tebhaga movement in Bengal — where sharecroppers demanded two‑thirds of the produce — were spearheaded by Communist‑led kisan sabhas.

The party’s relationship with the Congress was ambivalent. During the 1930s the CPI supported the Congress’s anti‑imperialist line but criticised its “bourgeois leadership”. In 1942 the CPI initially opposed the Quit India Movement, arguing that the war against fascism took precedence; this stance led to a temporary ban and the arrest of many leaders. By 1944, however, the CPI shifted to “People’s War” and backed the British war effort, a move that alienated it from the mainstream nationalist movement but allowed it to operate legally and expand trade‑union work.

Post‑war, the CPI was instrumental in the 1946 Royal Indian Navy mutiny, providing political education to ratings through the All‑India Students Federation and the Communist‑led Naval Central Strike Committee. The party also organised the 1946‑47 Telangana peasant uprising (though its major phase extended beyond 1947). By 1947 the CPI had a membership of tens of thousands, a network of trade unions in major industrial centres, and a peasant base in Bengal, Bihar, Andhra and Kerala, making it the most organised left‑wing force in the subcontinent.

The Government of India Act 1935: Federal Scheme, Provincial Autonomy and the Aborted All‑India Federation

The Government of India Act 1935 was the longest statute enacted by the British Parliament for India, containing 321 sections and 10 schedules. Its centrepiece was a proposed All‑India Federation comprising British Indian provinces and princely states. The federation never materialised because the princely states, required to accede voluntarily, refused to join; the Act therefore operated only its provincial provisions from 1 April 1937.

At the centre the Act retained dyarchy: “reserved” subjects (defence, external affairs, ecclesiastical affairs, tribal areas) remained under the Governor‑General’s exclusive control, while “transferred” subjects were placed under a Council of Ministers responsible to the Federal Legislature. The Federal Legislature was bicameral — a Council of State (260 members) and a Federal Assembly (375 members) — but its powers were heavily circumscribed by the Governor‑General’s veto and the British Parliament’s supremacy.

Provincial autonomy was the Act’s most effective innovation. Eleven provinces received elected legislative assemblies and responsible ministries. Governors retained “special responsibilities” (minority protection, law and order, financial stability) and could override ministers through Section 93 proclamations. The 1937 elections brought Congress ministries to power in seven provinces (Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, United Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, Assam). These ministries enacted tenancy reforms (e.g., Bihar Tenancy Act 1938), reduced land revenue in some areas, and released political prisoners.

The Act also established a Federal Court (precursor to the Supreme Court) with original, appellate and advisory jurisdiction. Its judgments on centre‑state disputes and fundamental rights (though the Act did not enumerate a bill of rights) laid early precedents for judicial review. When Congress ministries resigned in November 1939 in protest against India’s unilateral entry into the Second World War, the Act’s emergency provisions allowed Governors to assume direct administration, demonstrating the fragility of provincial autonomy under imperial oversight. The 1935 Act’s federal design, though stillborn, provided the structural template — three‑list distribution, bicameralism, and a supreme court — that the 1950 Constitution would adapt.

The Interim Government, the Constituent Assembly and the Mountbatten Plan (1946‑1947)

Following the Cabinet Mission’s failure (June 1946), Viceroy Lord Wavell invited the Indian National Congress to form an Interim Government on 2 September 1946. Jawaharlal Nehru became Vice‑President of the Executive Council (de facto prime minister), with Sardar Patel heading the Home Department and Rajendra Prasad the Food and Agriculture portfolio. The Muslim League initially refused to join but entered on 26 October 1946 after securing the Finance portfolio for Liaquat Ali Khan. The League’s participation was short‑lived; it withdrew in February 1947, citing the Congress’s refusal to accept the “grouping” formula for provinces.

The Constituent Assembly, elected by the provincial legislatures in July‑August 1946, convened for its first session on 9 December 1946. Of its approximately 296 members (later 299 after the accession of princely states), over 200 were from the Congress, around 70 from the Muslim League (which boycotted the first session), and the remainder from smaller parties and independents. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected President; the Assembly’s Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, chaired by Sardar Patel, and the Drafting Committee, chaired by B. R. Ambedkar, began work even before the transfer of power.

In March 1947 Lord Mountbatten replaced Wavell as Viceroy with a mandate to effect a swift transfer. On 3 June 1947 he announced the Mountbatten Plan: partition of British India into two dominions — India and Pakistan — based on the “principal of demarcation” of Muslim‑majority contiguous areas. The plan provided for a Boundary Commission under Sir Cyril Radcliffe, the division of assets and armed forces, and a deadline of 15 August 1947 for independence. The Indian Independence Act 1947, passed by the British Parliament on 18 July 1947, gave legal effect to the plan, abolishing the Crown’s suzerainty over princely states and empowering the new dominions to accede or remain independent.

The Interim Government continued to function until 15 August, overseeing the partition of Punjab and Bengal, the movement of millions of refugees, and the communal violence that claimed hundreds of thousands to over a million lives. On 14‑15 August the Constituent Assembly met as the sovereign legislature of the Dominion of India; Nehru delivered the “Tryst with Destiny” speech. The Assembly’s work culminated in the adoption of the Constitution on 26 November 1949, which came into force on 26 January 1950, completing the constitutional transition that began with the 1935 Act and the 1946 elections.

  • Attributing the Montagu Declaration solely to Indian pressure — it was also driven by British imperial strategic needs during the war.
  • Assuming the CPI uniformly opposed the Quit India Movement — the party’s line shifted from opposition (1942) to support (1944) after the “People’s War” turn.
  • Believing the 1935 Act’s federal scheme was implemented — the federation never came into existence because princely states did not accede.
  • Treating the Mountbatten Plan as a negotiated settlement between Congress and League — it was a unilateral British decision imposed after the failure of the Cabinet Mission.
  • Overlooking the role of the Governor’s “special responsibilities” in the 1935 Act — they allowed the colonial executive to suspend provincial autonomy at will.
