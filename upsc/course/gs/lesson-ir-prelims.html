India’s Neighbourhood: Geopolitical Context

1.1 Historical Foundations of South Asian Relations

The modern map of South Asia emerged from the partition of British India in 1947, an event that created two sovereign states, India and Pakistan, and left a legacy of contested borders, refugee flows, and competing national narratives. The princely states that acceded to either side added further complexity, especially in Jammu and Kashmir, where the Line of Control continues to shape bilateral discourse. The early decades were marked by wars in 1947‑48, 1965, and 1971, each reinforcing a security dilemma that still informs defence postures and diplomatic signalling across the region.

1.2 Bilateral Dynamics with Pakistan

India’s relationship with Pakistan remains the most consequential bilateral axis in the neighbourhood. The core disputes centre on Kashmir, cross‑border terrorism, and water sharing under the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960. Periodic attempts at confidence‑building — such as the Lahore Declaration of 1999, the Agra Summit of 2001, and the Composite Dialogue process — have been interrupted by major attacks, notably the 2001 Parliament attack, the 2008 Mumbai attacks, and the 2019 Pulwama incident. Each rupture has led to a tightening of military readiness, diplomatic isolation strategies, and a recalibration of India’s “Neighbourhood First” policy to prioritize security over economic integration with Islamabad.

1.3 Engagement with Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and Maldives

Beyond Pakistan, India has cultivated a web of cooperative frameworks with the smaller South Asian states. The 1971 Liberation War forged a deep strategic bond with Bangladesh, reflected in the 2015 Land Boundary Agreement that resolved enclaves and the 2019 Teesta water‑sharing discussions. With Nepal, the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship provides open borders, yet periodic tensions over the 2015 Constitution and the 2020 border map dispute illustrate the sensitivity of sovereignty narratives. Bhutan’s unique relationship is anchored in the 2007 Friendship Treaty, which guides Indian assistance in hydropower and infrastructure while respecting Thimphu’s foreign‑policy autonomy. Sri Lanka’s post‑civil‑war reconstruction has seen Indian development aid, the 1987 Indo‑Sri Lanka Accord, and recent cooperation on maritime security. The Maldives, after a period of political turbulence, has returned to a defence cooperation agreement with India in 2021, underscoring the strategic importance of the Indian Ocean’s southern approaches.

1.4 Regional Cooperation Mechanisms: SAARC and BIMSTEC

The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC), launched in 1985, was envisioned as a platform for economic integration, but its progress has been stalled by Indo‑Pakistani tensions; the 2016 summit was cancelled after the Uri attack, and subsequent meetings have been informal. In response, India has championed the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi‑Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation (BIMSTEC), which excludes Pakistan and links South Asia with Southeast Asia. BIMSTEC’s 2018 Kathmandu Declaration and the 2022 Colombo Summit have advanced connectivity projects such as the India‑Myanmar‑Thailand Trilateral Highway and the BBIN (Bangladesh‑Bhutan‑India‑Nepal) motor‑vehicles agreement, offering an alternative pathway for regional economic cooperation.

Major Powers and India’s Strategic Partnerships

2.1 United States: From Estrangement to Comprehensive Global Strategic Partnership

The trajectory of India‑US relations shifted dramatically after the 1998 nuclear tests, when sanctions gave way to the 2005 Civil Nuclear Agreement, which recognized India as a responsible nuclear power and opened the door for technology transfers. The 2016 Logistics Exchange Memorandum of Agreement (LEMOA), the 2018 Communications Compatibility and Security Agreement (COMCASA), and the 2020 Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement (BECA) have institutionalised defence interoperability. The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad), revived in 2017 with Japan and Australia, reflects a convergence on a free and open Indo‑Pacific, while the 2022 Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies (iCET) deepens cooperation in semiconductors, artificial intelligence, and quantum computing.

2.2 Russia: Legacy Ties and Contemporary Defence Cooperation

India’s defence inventory remains heavily reliant on Russian platforms — Su‑30MKI fighters, T‑90 tanks, S‑400 air‑defence systems, and the INS Vikramaditya aircraft carrier. The 2018 S‑400 deal, despite US CAATSA sanctions risk, underscores the strategic autonomy India seeks. Annual summits, the 2021 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue, and the 2022 “Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership” declaration have expanded cooperation into nuclear energy (Kudankulam reactors), space (Gaganyaan crew‑module training), and Arctic research. However, the Ukraine conflict has introduced complexities, compelling India to balance its historic partnership with Moscow against growing Western pressure.

2.3 China: Border Disputes, Economic Interdependence, and Strategic Competition

The 3,488‑kilometre Line of Actual Control (LAC) remains unsettled, with flashpoints at Galwan Valley (2020), Pangong Tso (2021), and Depsang Plains. The 1993 and 1996 border agreements, the 2005 Political Parameters Guiding Principles, and the 2013 Border Defence Cooperation Agreement have not prevented periodic standoffs. Simultaneously, bilateral trade crossed US$100 billion in 2022, with India importing electronics, pharmaceuticals, and machinery while exporting iron ore, cotton, and chemicals. The 2020 “Atmanirbhar Bharat” push and the 2021 Production‑Linked Incentive schemes aim to reduce critical‑sector dependency, while the Quad and the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) provide diplomatic counterweights.

2.4 European Union and Key European States

The EU‑India Strategic Partnership, launched in 2004, was upgraded to a “Strategic Partnership for the 21st Century” in 2020, targeting trade, climate, digital, and security cooperation. The long‑stalled Broad‑Based Trade and Investment Agreement (BTIA) negotiations resumed in 2022, focusing on market access, geographical indications, and sustainable development. Bilateral ties with France (Rafale jets, Scorpène submarines, joint naval exercises), Germany (green hydrogen, vocational training), and the United Kingdom (2021 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, defence logistics) add depth to the European dimension.

2.5 Japan and Australia: Quad and Indo‑Pacific Alignment

Japan’s “Free and Open Indo‑Pacific” vision aligns with India’s Act East Policy, producing the 2015 Special Strategic and Global Partnership, the 2020 Acquisition and Cross‑Servicing Agreement (ACSA), and joint infrastructure projects in the Andaman‑Nicobar Islands and the Northeast. Australia’s 2020 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, the 2022 Mutual Logistics Support Agreement (MLSA), and the 2023 Australia‑India Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA) expand cooperation in critical minerals, cyber security, and maritime domain awareness. The Quad’s 2023 Hiroshima Summit reaffirmed commitments to vaccine delivery, climate finance, and technology standards.

Example Question
Explain how the 2020 Galwan Valley clash reshaped India’s strategic posture toward China and its engagement with the Quad. In your answer, refer to specific policy shifts, defence acquisitions, and diplomatic initiatives undertaken after the incident.
The Galwan clash in June 2020, which resulted in the first fatal confrontation on the LAC since 1975, triggered a comprehensive recalibration of India’s China policy. First, the government accelerated the “Atmanirbhar Bharat” drive, imposing restrictions on Chinese investments in sensitive sectors and banning over 200 Chinese mobile applications citing data‑security concerns. Second, defence procurement was fast‑tracked: the emergency purchase of 36 Rafale jets from France was complemented by the induction of the indigenous Light Combat Aircraft Tejas Mk‑1A and the acceleration of the S‑400 delivery schedule from Russia. Third, the border infrastructure programme received a massive budgetary boost, with the Border Roads Organisation completing the Darbuk‑Shyok‑DBO road and advancing the construction of all‑weather tunnels such as the Sela Tunnel. Diplomatically, India deepened Quad engagement — the 2021 Quad Leaders’ Summit in Washington produced a joint statement on a “free, open, inclusive, and resilient Indo‑Pacific,” and the 2022 Quad Foreign Ministers’ Meeting in Tokyo launched the Quad Vaccine Partnership and the Quad Critical and Emerging Technology Working Group. Simultaneously, India revived the 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue with the United States, signed the Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement (BECA) in October 2020, and intensified naval exercises such as Malabar 2020, which included Australia for the first time since 2007. Collectively, these steps signal a shift from strategic ambiguity to explicit balancing, embedding the Quad as a central pillar of India’s Indo‑Pacific architecture while maintaining a calibrated dialogue channel with Beijing through the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination (WMCC) and the Special Representatives’ talks.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “policy shifts after a specific event,” structure the answer chronologically: immediate security measures, medium‑term defence procurement, long‑term diplomatic realignments. Cite the exact agreements (e.g., BECA, ACSA, MLSA) and institutional mechanisms (WMCC, Quad Working Groups) to demonstrate precision.

Multilateral Groupings and India’s Role

3.1 United Nations and Reform Aspirations

India has been a founding member of the United Nations and has contributed over 250,000 personnel to peacekeeping missions since 1950, making it one of the largest troop contributors. The quest for a permanent seat on the Security Council is anchored in the G4 (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan) framework, which advocates expansion in both permanent and non‑permanent categories. India’s 2021‑22 Presidency of the UN Security Council highlighted themes of maritime security, counter‑terrorism, and technology‑driven peacekeeping, reinforcing its claim to greater responsibility.

3.2 BRICS: Economic Cooperation and Political Coordination

The BRICS grouping (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) provides a platform for emerging‑market coordination on financial architecture, exemplified by the New Development Bank (NDB) established in 2015 with an initial capital of US$100 billion. India has leveraged the NDB to fund sustainable infrastructure projects such as the Mumbai Metro and the Rajasthan solar parks. The 2023 Johannesburg Summit expanded BRICS to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates, signalling a shift toward a broader “Global South” coalition, while India continues to push for reform of the IMF quota system and greater use of local currencies in intra‑BRICS trade.

3.3 G20: Global Economic Governance

India’s G20 Presidency in 2023, themed “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — One Earth, One Family, One Future,” prioritized inclusive growth, digital public infrastructure, and climate finance. The New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration secured consensus on the “G20 Action Plan on Sustainable Development Goals,” the “Global Biofuels Alliance,” and a commitment to triple renewable energy capacity by 2030. India’s advocacy for the “Digital Public Goods” framework, illustrated by the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) and the CoWIN platform, positioned it as a model for scalable, open‑source digital solutions.

3.4 SCO: Security and Regional Connectivity

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO), joined by India and Pakistan in 2017, focuses on counter‑terrorism, regional stability, and connectivity. India participates in the Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure (RATS) and has hosted the 2023 SCO Summit in New Delhi, emphasizing the “SCO Development Strategy 2030” and the “SCO Economic Development Strategy.” The International North‑South Transport Corridor (INSTC) and the Chabahar Port project are highlighted as SCO‑compatible connectivity initiatives linking India with Central Asia and Russia.

3.5 IORA and Indian Ocean Rim Initiatives

The Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA), with 23 member states, serves as India’s primary multilateral forum for maritime cooperation. The 2022 IORA Council of Ministers Meeting in Dhaka adopted the “IORA Action Plan 2022‑2026,” focusing on maritime safety, blue economy, and disaster risk reduction. India’s “Security and Growth for All in the Region” (SAGAR) doctrine, articulated in 2015, aligns with IORA objectives, and the Indian Navy’s Information Fusion Centre‑Indian Ocean Region (IFC‑IOR) in Gurugram operationalises shared maritime domain awareness.

International Institutions and Normative Frameworks

4.1 World Trade Organization and Trade Dispute Settlement

India has been an active participant in the WTO since its inception in 1995, championing the Doha Development Agenda and defending special and differential treatment for developing countries. The 2013 Bali Package on trade facilitation and the 2017 Nairobi Ministerial Decision on public stockholding for food security reflect India’s negotiating priorities. In recent years, India has filed disputes against the United States on steel and aluminium tariffs (DS547) and against the European Union on the carbon border adjustment mechanism, while also defending its own agricultural subsidies in the DS541 case brought by the US.

4.2 International Monetary Fund and Financial Stability

India’s quota in the IMF was increased to 2.75 % in the 2010 reforms, raising its voting share to 2.63 %. The 2021 Special Drawing Rights (SDR) allocation of US$650 billion provided India with an additional SDR 12.5 billion, bolstering foreign‑exchange reserves. India advocates for a more representative governance structure, pushing for a third chair for emerging markets on the Executive Board and for the inclusion of the Chinese renminbi in the SDR basket, which was realised in 2016.

4.3 Climate Change Regime: UNFCCC, Paris Agreement, and India’s Commitments

Under the UNFCCC, India submitted its first Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) in 2015, pledging a 33‑35 % reduction in emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 from 2005 levels, 40 % cumulative electric power installed capacity from non‑fossil sources, and creation of an additional carbon sink of 2.5‑3 billion tonnes CO₂ equivalent. The 2021 Glasgow Climate Pact saw India announce a net‑zero target by 2070, a 500 GW renewable capacity goal by 2030, and the launch of the International Solar Alliance (ISA) with France, now comprising 110 signatory countries. The 2023 G20 New Delhi Declaration reinforced the “phase‑down” of unabated coal power and the “tripling” of renewable energy capacity globally.

4.4 Nuclear Non‑Proliferation Regime: NPT, CTBT, and India’s Position

India remains outside the Nuclear Non‑Proliferation Treaty (NPT) and the Comprehensive Nuclear‑Test‑Ban Treaty (CTBT), arguing that these instruments create a discriminatory hierarchy between nuclear‑weapon states and others. The 2008 NSG waiver, secured through the US‑India Civil Nuclear Agreement, allowed India to engage in civilian nuclear commerce while maintaining its strategic arsenal. India adheres to a voluntary moratorium on nuclear testing since 1998, supports the Fissile Material Cut‑off Treaty (FMCT) negotiations, and participates in the Nuclear Security Summits, emphasizing responsible stewardship.

4.5 Human Rights Mechanisms and India’s Engagement

India is a party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), and it engages with the Universal Periodic Review (UPR) process at the Human Rights Council. The 2022 UPR cycle saw India accept 152 of 250 recommendations, focusing on women’s empowerment, child protection, and the rights of persons with disabilities. India’s domestic legislation — such as the 2013 Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, the 2019 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, and the 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Act — reflects an evolving alignment with international norms, though debates persist on issues like the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act and the Citizenship (Amendment) Act.

The Indian Diaspora: Soft Power and Policy

5.1 Historical Migration Waves and Demographic Profile

The Indian diaspora, estimated at over 32 million persons of Indian origin (PIOs) and non‑resident Indians (NRIs) as of 2023, traces its roots to nineteenth‑century indentured labour migration to Mauritius, Trinidad, Fiji, and South Africa, followed by post‑1960s professional migration to the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states. The Gulf hosts the largest concentration, with approximately 9 million Indian workers contributing to construction, healthcare, and services sectors, while the United States and the United Kingdom each host communities exceeding 4 million, distinguished by high educational attainment and income levels.

5.2 Remittances, Investment, and Knowledge Transfer

Remittances to India reached a record US$111 billion in 2022, representing roughly 3 % of GDP and surpassing foreign direct investment inflows. The Reserve Bank of India’s “Remittance Survey” shows that the GCC corridor accounts for over 30 % of total flows, followed by North America and Europe. Beyond capital, diaspora networks facilitate technology transfer — exemplified by the “India‑US Science and Technology Forum” and the “VAJRA” faculty scheme — and stimulate entrepreneurship through initiatives like the “Startup India” diaspora engagement chapter.

5.3 Political Influence in Host Countries

Indian‑origin politicians have attained prominent positions: Kamala Harris as Vice President of the United States, Rishi Sunak as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, and numerous ministers and legislators in Canada, Australia, Singapore, and the Gulf. These leaders often advocate for deeper bilateral ties, as seen in the 2023 US‑India Strategic Clean Energy Partnership and the 2022 UK‑India Comprehensive Strategic Partnership. Diaspora organisations such as the US‑India Business Council (USIBC) and the Overseas Friends of BJP (OFBJP) mobilise political support for trade agreements, defence deals, and visa liberalisation.

5.4 Government Policies: OCI, PIO, and Pravasi Bharatiya Divas

The Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) scheme, introduced in 2005 and merged with the Persons of Indian Origin (PIO) card in 2015, grants lifelong visa‑free travel, parity with NRIs in economic and educational fields, and exemption from registration for stays exceeding 180 days. The annual Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (PBD), celebrated on 9 January since 2003, serves as a platform for policy dialogue, investment pitching, and cultural exchange. The 2023 PBD in Indore focused on “Diaspora: Reliable Partners for India’s Progress in Amrit Kaal,” launching the “Global Indian Talent Pool” portal to match skilled diaspora professionals with domestic opportunities.

5.5 Challenges: Dual Citizenship, Consular Protection, and Integration

India’s Constitution prohibits dual citizenship, compelling many diaspora members to choose between Indian nationality and the citizenship of their country of residence, which can limit political participation and property rights in India. Consular protection remains a concern, especially for low‑skilled workers in the Gulf facing contractual disputes, passport confiscation, and repatriation challenges during crises such as the COVID‑19 pandemic. Integration issues — ranging from cultural preservation to second‑generation identity — are addressed through community organisations, language schools, and the “Know India Programme” for youth, yet the scale and diversity of the diaspora demand continuous policy refinement.

India’s Maritime Security Architecture in the Indian Ocean

India’s maritime security architecture rests on three pillars: a credible naval force, a layered domain‑awareness network, and a diplomatic framework that translates capability into regional influence. The Indian Navy operates a fleet of over 130 ships and 200+ aircraft, organised into three commands — Western, Eastern, and Southern — each responsible for a distinct maritime zone. The 2015 “Security and Growth for All in the Region” (SAGAR) doctrine articulated the strategic intent: a free, open, and inclusive Indian Ocean backed by cooperative security mechanisms rather than unilateral dominance.

Domain awareness is anchored by the Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (IFC‑IOR), inaugurated in Gurugram in 2018. The centre fuses Automatic Identification System (AIS) feeds, coastal radar chains, satellite imagery, and white‑shipping agreements with over 20 partner nations to produce a near‑real‑time maritime picture. Complementing IFC‑IOR, the Navy’s Mission‑Based Deployments (MBD) maintain a persistent presence at choke points such as the Strait of Hormuz, the Bab‑el‑Mandeb, and the Malacca Strait, enabling rapid response to piracy, illegal fishing, and humanitarian crises.

The Coast Guard, with a fleet of over 150 surface platforms and 70 aircraft, handles law‑enforcement tasks within the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) and coordinates search‑and‑rescue (SAR) operations. Joint exercises — MALABAR (with the United States, Japan, and Australia), VARUNA (France), and SIMBEX (Singapore) — sharpen interoperability and signal commitment to a rules‑based order. Recent editions of the multilateral “Milan” naval exercise have hosted over 40 navies, underscoring India’s role as a net security provider.

Diplomatically, India has signed white‑shipping agreements with over 30 countries, enabling automatic exchange of merchant‑vessel data. The Indian Ocean Naval Symposium (IONS), chaired by India in 2021‑22, provides a forum for littoral navies to discuss capacity building, HADR (Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief), and maritime domain awareness. The Indo‑Pacific Oceans Initiative (IPOI), launched in 2019, further institutionalises cooperation across seven pillars: maritime security, ecology, resources, capacity building, disaster risk reduction, science & technology, and trade connectivity.

Challenges remain. The growing People’s Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) presence — regular deployments of Type 055 destroyers and a base at Djibouti — tests India’s ability to sustain MBDs without overstretch. Budgetary constraints limit the pace of indigenous carrier construction (the second Indigenous Aircraft Carrier, IAC‑II, is still under consideration) and the acquisition of additional long‑range maritime patrol aircraft. Nevertheless, the convergence of naval modernisation, fused intelligence, and a widening diplomatic web positions India as a principal architect of Indian Ocean security for the coming decade.

Strategic Autonomy and Multi‑Alignment in Great Power Competition

Strategic autonomy — the capacity to pursue national interests without external coercion — has been the lodestar of Indian foreign policy since the Non‑Aligned Movement (NAM) of the 1950s. In the contemporary multipolar order, autonomy is operationalised through “multi‑alignment”: simultaneous deepening of ties with competing powers while avoiding binding alliances that could constrain decision‑making. This approach is evident in India’s concurrent membership of the Quad (with the United States, Japan, Australia), BRICS (with Brazil, Russia, China, South Africa), and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO).

The 2020 Galwan clash accelerated defence cooperation with the United States, adding the Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement (BECA) for geospatial intelligence to the earlier Logistics Exchange Memorandum of Agreement (LEMOA, 2016) and Communications Compatibility and Security Agreement (COMCASA, 2018). Yet India preserved its defence partnership with Russia, proceeding with the S‑400 Triumf air‑defence system delivery despite the threat of CAATSA sanctions. The 2022‑23 “2+2” ministerial dialogues with both Washington and Moscow illustrate the balancing act: each side receives high‑level attention, but neither obtains a veto over India’s other engagements.

Economic multi‑alignment mirrors the security dimension. India participates in the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) for supply‑chain resilience, clean energy, and fair‑economy (tax/anti‑corruption) pillars, while simultaneously negotiating a Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) with the European Union and a Free Trade Agreement (FTA) with the United Kingdom. In the energy sphere, India continues to import discounted Russian crude (accounting for over one‑third of total oil imports in 2023) while expanding strategic petroleum reserves with the United Arab Emirates and the United States.

Doctrinal articulations define strategic autonomy as “the ability to choose partners, platforms, and pathways based on sovereign assessment of national interest.” Critics argue that multi‑alignment risks dilution of credibility; proponents contend it maximises leverage — each major power competes to offer better technology, market access, or diplomatic support. The 2023 G20 presidency showcased this leverage: India secured consensus on the “New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration” despite deep divisions over the Ukraine conflict, demonstrating that a non‑aligned convener can still shape outcomes.

Operationalising autonomy demands robust institutional capacity — inter‑ministerial coordination cells, a professional foreign‑service cadre, and a defence‑industrial base capable of absorbing technology transfers. The 2020 Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) and the 2021 “Atmanirbhar Bharat” defence corridors aim to reduce import dependence, thereby expanding the decision space that autonomy requires. As great‑power rivalry intensifies, India’s ability to sustain multi‑alignment without slipping into strategic ambiguity will be the definitive test of its autonomous foreign policy.

Examiner's technique: Comparative framing
Question: “India’s strategic autonomy is best described as ‘multi‑alignment’ rather than ‘non‑alignment’. Critically examine this statement with reference to India’s engagements in the Quad, BRICS, and SCO.”
Answer: Begin by defining strategic autonomy (sovereign decision‑making) and contrasting non‑alignment (Cold War era equidistance) with multi‑alignment (simultaneous deep partnerships). Cite Quad (security‑technology cooperation with US, Japan, Australia), BRICS (economic‑political coordination with Russia, China), and SCO (counter‑terrorism, connectivity with Central Asia, Russia, China). Highlight that India signs foundational agreements with the US (BECA, LEMOA, COMCASA) while proceeding with S‑400 purchase from Russia, illustrating concurrent engagement. Note that multi‑alignment expands leverage but requires institutional bandwidth to manage divergent expectations. Conclude that the shift reflects a pragmatic adaptation to multipolarity, not an abandonment of autonomy.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks to “critically examine” a foreign‑policy concept, structure the answer in three layers: (1) conceptual definition, (2) empirical evidence across at least three groupings, (3) assessment of trade‑offs. Use a two‑column table in the rough sheet to map each grouping against the autonomy criteria (decision independence, technology access, risk of entrapment). This visual aid speeds up retrieval and ensures balanced coverage.

Critical Minerals and Technology Diplomacy

Access to critical minerals — lithium, cobalt, rare‑earth elements (REEs), graphite, and nickel — has become a core determinant of India’s clean‑energy transition and defence modernisation. The 2019 establishment of Khanij Bidesh India Ltd (KABIL), a joint venture of three public‑sector undertakings, marked the first institutionalised overseas mineral‑acquisition vehicle. KABIL has signed memoranda of understanding with Argentina, Australia, and Chile for lithium, cobalt, REE, and copper exploration projects.

Domestically, the 2023 amendment to the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act introduced a “critical mineral” schedule, empowering the central government to auction composite licences for exploration and mining of 24 identified minerals. The Geological Survey of India (GSI) has intensified reconnaissance in the Rajasthan‑Gujarat belt for lithium and in the Karnataka‑Andhra region for REEs, though commercial production remains years away. The National Critical Minerals Strategy targets meeting a significant portion of domestic demand through indigenous sources by 2030.

Technology diplomacy runs in parallel. The India‑United States Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies (iCET), launched in January 2023, creates a joint working group on semiconductor supply chains, quantum computing, and advanced telecommunications. The 2022 “Semicon India” programme, with a fiscal outlay of ₹76,000 crore, aims to establish fabrication units (fabs) and assembly‑test‑mark‑pack (ATMP) facilities, leveraging partnerships with global majors such as GlobalFoundries and Renesas, and engagement with TSMC. The 2023 India‑Japan “Digital Partnership” adds cooperation on 5G/6G test‑beds and trusted‑source certification for telecom equipment.

Strategic stockpiling complements acquisition. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve model is being adapted for critical minerals: a proposed “National Mineral Reserve” would hold 30‑day consumption buffers for lithium, cobalt, and REEs, financed through a sovereign wealth fund. India’s emerging green finance taxonomy includes critical‑mineral intensity as a metric for sustainable finance, directing capital toward low‑carbon mining projects.

Geopolitical risk management shapes partner selection. India avoids over‑reliance on any single jurisdiction; the 2022 Australia‑India Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA) includes a critical‑minerals annex guaranteeing non‑discriminatory access. Simultaneously, the 2023 India‑EU Trade and Technology Council establishes a working group on raw‑material supply chains, reducing exposure to Chinese dominance in REE processing (China controls ~60 % of global separation capacity). The Quad Critical Minerals Partnership — announced at the 2023 Hiroshima summit — commits the four nations to joint mapping, recycling R&D, and standards harmonisation.

Challenges persist: environmental clearances for domestic mines face litigation; overseas assets are vulnerable to resource‑nationalism; and recycling infrastructure is nascent. Nevertheless, the convergence of legislative reform, dedicated acquisition vehicles, and multi‑lateral technology pacts positions India to mitigate supply‑chain fragility in the decade ahead.

Global Health Governance and Vaccine Diplomacy

India’s engagement with global health governance has evolved from a recipient of aid to a rule‑shaper and supplier of affordable medicines. The 2001 Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health, championed by India, affirmed the right of developing countries to issue compulsory licences for essential drugs — a precedent that underpins current debates on pandemic‑related intellectual‑property waivers.

The COVID‑19 pandemic catalysed “Vaccine Maitri”, a diplomatic initiative that dispatched tens of millions of vaccine doses as grants and facilitated commercial exports to over 100 countries between January 2021 and March 2022. Supplies prioritised neighbours (Bhutan, Maldives, Nepal, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka) and extended to Africa, Latin America, and the Pacific. The programme leveraged two domestically produced vaccines — Covishield (Oxford‑AstraZeneca, manufactured by Serum Institute of India) and Covaxin (Bharat Biotech) — and was coordinated through the Ministry of External Affairs’ “Vaccine Supply Chain Management System”.

India’s role in COVAX, the global vaccine‑sharing facility co‑led by Gavi, CEPI, and WHO, was pivotal: the Serum Institute committed up to 1.1 billion doses to COVAX, though export restrictions during the domestic second wave (April‑June 2021) temporarily halted shipments. The 2022 “TRIPS Waiver” proposal at the WTO, co‑sponsored by India and South Africa, sought temporary suspension of patent protections for COVID‑19 vaccines, diagnostics, and therapeutics. After protracted negotiations, a limited waiver covering vaccines was adopted in June 2022, reflecting India’s ability to mobilise the Global South.

Beyond vaccines, India contributes to the WHO’s “Access to COVID‑19 Tools Accelerator” (ACT‑A) and the “Pandemic Fund” hosted by the World Bank. The 2023 “Global Initiative on Digital Health” (GIDH), launched under India’s G20 presidency, aims to standardise health‑data interoperability, drawing on the CoWIN platform’s architecture. India also chairs the “Global Health Security Agenda” (GHSA) steering group for 2024‑25, focusing on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance and zoonotic disease early warning.

Domestic legal frameworks reinforce international commitments. The 2020 “Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act” and revisions to the National Health Mission align with the International Health Regulations (IHR) 2005 core capacities. The 2022 “National Policy for Rare Diseases” integrates WHO orphan‑drug guidelines, while the 2023 “National Medical Devices Policy” adopts the IMDRF (International Medical Device Regulators Forum) classification.

Challenges include sustaining vaccine‑manufacturing capacity post‑pandemic, addressing vaccine‑hesitancy in partner countries, and navigating the tension between compulsory‑licence advocacy and the need to attract foreign R&D investment. The ongoing “WHO Pandemic Agreement” negotiations will test India’s ability to balance equity demands with the pharmaceutical industry’s innovation incentives. India’s track record — affordable generics, large‑scale production, and diplomatic distribution — positions it as a decisive voice in shaping a more resilient global health architecture.

Emerging Minilateral Groupings: I2U2, Quad, and the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework

Minilateralism — issue‑based coalitions of like‑minded states — has become India’s preferred vehicle for translating strategic autonomy into tangible outcomes. Three groupings illustrate this trend: the I2U2 (India, Israel, United Arab Emirates, United States), the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad), and the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF).

I2U2, launched in 2021, focuses on six sectors: water, energy, transportation, space, health, and food security. The 2022 “I2U2 Business Forum” in Abu Dhabi announced a $2 billion investment pipeline for food‑park corridors in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh, leveraging Israeli drip‑irrigation technology, Emirati capital, and US market access. The 2023 “Hybrid Renewable Energy Project” in Rajasthan — combining solar, wind, and battery storage — exemplifies the grouping’s capacity to mobilise private capital for climate‑resilient infrastructure.

The Quad, revived in 2017 and elevated to leader‑level summits from 2021, has expanded beyond maritime security into critical‑technology supply chains, humanitarian assistance, and climate finance. The 2022 “Quad Fellowship” sponsors 100 STEM graduate students annually from the four nations. The 2023 “Quad Infrastructure Coordination Group” aligns standards for transparent, high‑quality infrastructure, directly countering opaque financing models. The Quad Cancer Moonshot initiative pools research funding for cervical‑cancer elimination in the Indo‑Pacific.

IPEF, launched by the United States in May 2022 with 14 initial partners (including India), comprises four pillars: trade, supply chains, clean economy, and fair economy. India joined the supply‑chain and clean‑economy pillars but opted out of the trade pillar, citing concerns over labour and environmental standards that could constrain policy space. The 2023 “IPEF Supply Chain Resilience Agreement” establishes a crisis‑response network for critical‑mineral disruptions, a direct complement to India’s KABIL strategy. The 2024 “IPEF Clean Economy Investor Forum” in Singapore mobilised significant capital for renewable‑energy projects across member states.

These minilaterals share design features: variable geometry (members opt‑in per pillar), non‑binding commitments, and a focus on deliverables rather than treaty obligations. This flexibility suits India’s multi‑alignment posture — it can deepen cooperation with the United States and its allies on technology and supply chains while preserving strategic space with Russia and China in BRICS and SCO. However, overlapping memberships risk duplication; the 2023 “Quad‑IPEF Coordination Mechanism” was created to harmonise work‑streams on semiconductors and clean energy.

Effectiveness hinges on institutionalising secretariats, establishing clear metrics, and ensuring domestic bureaucratic buy‑in. A recent review commissioned by the Ministry of External Affairs recommended a single “Minilateral Coordination Cell” within the National Security Council Secretariat to track deliverables across I2U2, Quad, and IPEF. If implemented, this cell could transform ad‑hoc cooperation into a sustained strategic asset, reinforcing India’s role as a pivotal node in the emerging Indo‑Pacific order.

  • Confusing strategic autonomy with isolationism — autonomy means freedom to choose partners, not refusal to engage.
  • Treating the Quad as a formal military alliance — it remains a consultative framework without mutual‑defence obligations.
  • Overstating India’s critical‑mineral self‑sufficiency — domestic production meets only a fraction of demand; overseas acquisition remains essential.
  • Assuming Vaccine Maitri was purely altruistic — it combined humanitarian goals with diplomatic signalling and commercial interests of Indian manufacturers.
  • Neglecting the opt‑out clause in IPEF — India’s selective pillar participation is a deliberate sovereignty safeguard, not a lack of commitment.
  • Equating I2U2 with a security pact — its mandate is economic and technological, not defence‑oriented.

India’s Engagement with Central Asia: Connectivity, Energy, and Security

India’s “Connect Central Asia” policy, unveiled in 2012, marked the first comprehensive diplomatic framework targeting the five Central Asian republics — Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan. The policy identified three pillars: political dialogue, economic cooperation, and people‑to‑people exchanges. Institutionalisation advanced with the India‑Central Asia Dialogue at foreign‑minister level (first held in Samarkand, 2019), followed by the first India‑Central Asia Summit in virtual format on 27 January 2022, where Prime Minister Narendra Modi and the five Central Asian presidents adopted a joint declaration emphasising the International North‑South Transport Corridor (INSTC), the Chabahar port route, and the Turkmenistan‑Afghanistan‑Pakistan‑India (TAPI) gas pipeline. The second summit, held in person in New Delhi on 14‑15 June 2023, produced a “Roadmap for Cooperation 2023‑2027” that added a digital connectivity corridor, a joint working group on counter‑terrorism under the SCO Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure, and a commitment to operationalise the INSTC’s eastern branch through the Iranian port of Chabahar. Energy cooperation has centred on the TAPI pipeline, for which an intergovernmental agreement was signed in 2015; despite security challenges in Afghanistan, India continues to fund feasibility studies and has extended substantial credit lines for Central Asian energy projects. Trade, still modest at around $2–3 billion in 2023, is targeted to reach $10 billion by 2030 through the removal of non‑tariff barriers and the launch of a “Central Asia‑India Business Council”. Cultural ties are reinforced by regular “India‑Central Asia Cultural Festivals” and the expansion of Indian Council for Cultural Relations scholarships. The strategic dimension is anchored in India’s SCO membership (since 2017), which provides a multilateral platform for counter‑terrorism coordination and regional stability, especially after the Taliban’s return to power in August 2021.

India–Africa Partnership: Development Cooperation, Trade, and Peacekeeping

The India‑Africa Forum Summit (IAFS) process, launched in 2008 in New Delhi, has become the principal driver of India’s engagement with the continent. The first summit (2008) established the “India‑Africa Framework for Cooperation” and pledged $5 billion in lines of credit (LoCs). The second summit (Addis Ababa, 2011) doubled the LoC envelope to $10 billion and introduced the Pan‑African e‑Network project, linking 53 African countries with Indian universities and hospitals via satellite. The third summit (New Delhi, 2015) announced an additional $10 billion in LoCs, a $600 million grant for the “India‑Africa Health Sciences Collaborative Platform”, and the creation of the “India‑Africa Defence Ministers’ Conclave” (first held in 2018). The fourth summit, held virtually in 2021 and in person in New Delhi in October 2023, focused on the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), digital public infrastructure transfer (UPI, CoWIN, Aadhaar‑stack), and climate‑resilient agriculture. As of March 2024, cumulative LoCs to Africa exceed $12 billion, financing projects in railways (Ethiopia‑Djibouti line), power (Kenya’s Olkaria geothermal expansion), and water (Tanzania’s Lake Victoria sanitation). Trade grew from $5.2 billion in 2005 to nearly $100 billion in 2023, with pharmaceuticals, refined petroleum, and machinery dominating exports. India is the largest cumulative troop contributor to UN peacekeeping, with a significant share deployed in Africa; currently, Indian battalions serve in MONUSCO (DRC) and UNMISS (South Sudan). The 2022 India‑Africa Defence Ministers’ Conclave in New Delhi produced a “Joint Declaration on Maritime Security” and agreed to co‑develop a coastal surveillance network for the Western Indian Ocean. Capacity‑building under the Indian Technical and Economic Cooperation (ITEC) programme now offers over 1,000 annual slots for African officials, while the “India‑Africa Science and Technology Initiative” funds joint research in tropical diseases and renewable energy.

Comparative Analysis of Summit Deliverables
Compare the deliverables of the 2015 India‑Africa Forum Summit with the 2022 India‑Central Asia Summit in terms of connectivity projects and institutional mechanisms.
The 2015 IAFS emphasised hard infrastructure: $10 billion LoCs for railways, power, and water; the Pan‑African e‑Network for digital connectivity; and the establishment of the India‑Africa Defence Ministers’ Conclave as a new institutional dialogue. The 2022 India‑Central Asia Summit, by contrast, focused on multimodal corridors — INSTC eastern branch via Chabahar, the TAPI pipeline, and a digital connectivity corridor — and created a joint working group on counter‑terrorism under the SCO RATS. While both summits launched business councils, the African summit’s outcomes were grant‑heavy and health‑oriented, whereas the Central Asian summit’s outcomes were pipeline‑ and corridor‑centric with a stronger security architecture. The examiner expects you to map each summit to its sectoral focus (health vs energy), financing mode (grant vs credit), and institutional novelty (defence conclave vs SCO‑linked working group).
Exam‑Day Mapping Technique
Create a two‑column table for each major summit: left column — sector (connectivity, energy, health, security); right column — specific deliverable (LoC amount, corridor name, institutional body). Memorise the table headers; the details become retrieval cues under time pressure.

India’s Arctic Policy: Scientific Research, Climate Governance, and Strategic Interests

India acquired observer status in the Arctic Council in May 2013, joining China, Japan, South Korea, and Singapore. The 2022 “India’s Arctic Policy: Building a Partnership for Sustainable Development” articulates five pillars: scientific research, climate and environmental protection, economic and human development, transportation and connectivity, and governance and international cooperation. India operates two permanent research facilities: “Himadri” at Ny‑Ålesund, Svalbard (inaugurated 2008) and the moored observatory “IndARC” in the Kongsfjorden fjord (deployed 2014, upgraded 2021). Indian scientists have participated in every Arctic Science Ministerial (2016 Washington, 2018 Berlin, 2021 Tokyo) and contribute to the Arctic Monitoring and Assessment Programme (AMAP), the Conservation of Arctic Flora and Fauna (CAFF), and the Protection of the Arctic Marine Environment (PAME) working groups. The policy highlights the Northern Sea Route (NSR) as a potential transit corridor reducing shipping time between Europe and East Asia by up to 40 %; India’s Shipping Corporation has conducted feasibility studies for NSR‑compatible vessels. On resource governance, India supports the UNCLOS framework and advocates for equitable benefit‑sharing of mineral resources. Climate diplomacy is central: India has committed to reducing black‑carbon emissions in the Arctic under the Arctic Council’s “Black Carbon and Methane” framework. The policy also commits to capacity‑building for Arctic indigenous communities through knowledge‑exchange initiatives. Strategic interest stems from the Arctic’s impact on the Indian monsoon system; studies from Himadri have linked Arctic sea‑ice loss to altered monsoon onset, reinforcing the case for sustained Indian scientific presence.

India’s Approach to Cyber Governance and Digital Public Infrastructure Exports

India’s domestic cyber architecture rests on the 2013 National Cyber Security Policy (NCSP), which established the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC) and mandated sectoral Computer Emergency Response Teams (CERT‑In). A draft National Cyber Security Strategy (NCSS) released in 2020 proposes a Cyber Security Board, mandatory breach reporting within six hours, and a “Zero Trust” architecture for government networks. The “Cyber Surakshit Bharat” initiative (2021) trains chief information security officers across ministries. On the export front, the India Stack — comprising Aadhaar (biometric ID), Unified Payments Interface (UPI), DigiLocker (document wallet), and the CoWIN vaccination platform — has been offered as open‑source digital public goods. Bhutan adopted UPI in July 2021, Nepal in February 2022, and Sri Lanka in March 2023; the “UPI‑Global” pilot with France and UAE began in 2024. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) signed a Memorandum of Understanding with the African Union in 2022 to deploy the CoWIN architecture for immunisation tracking. In multilateral forums, India participated in the UN Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) 2019‑2021 and the Open‑Ended Working Group (OEWG) 2021‑2025, advocating a multi‑stakeholder model, opposing the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (which India has not signed), and pushing for a universal norm against supply‑chain attacks. The 2023 “Global Partnership on Artificial Intelligence” (GPAI) summit in New Delhi saw India champion “AI for All” and propose a “Digital Public Infrastructure Repository” under the UN’s Digital Cooperation Roadmap. Domestically, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, establishes a Data Protection Board and cross‑border data flow safeguards, aligning with the G20’s “Data Free Flow with Trust” principles endorsed at the 2023 New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration.

India’s Defence Diplomacy: Military Exercises, Capacity Building, and Export Controls

Defence diplomacy is operationalised through a dense calendar of bilateral and multilateral exercises. The Malabar series, initiated in 1992 as a India‑US bilateral drill, expanded to include Japan (2015) and Australia (2020), evolving into a quadrilateral maritime exercise focused on anti‑submarine warfare and interoperability. Yudh Abhyas (India‑US, annual since 2004) emphasises high‑altitude joint operations; Indra (India‑Russia, since 2003) covers air, land, and sea domains; Garuda (India‑France, since 2003) centres on air‑force integration; Sampriti (India‑Bangladesh, since 2011) and Surya Kiran (India‑Nepal, since 2011) build counter‑insurgency and humanitarian assistance capacities. India’s defence exports have accelerated under the Defence Production and Export Promotion Policy (DPEPP) 2020 and the Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020. The landmark BrahMos supersonic cruise missile deal with the Philippines, signed in January 2022 for $375 million, marked India’s first major missile export. The Akash surface‑to‑air missile system was exported to Armenia in 2022 (estimated $250 million). Platform exports include the Dornier‑228 maritime patrol aircraft to Sri Lanka (2021) and the Advanced Light Helicopter (ALH) Mk III to Mauritius (2022). The Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX) framework, launched in 2018, has funded over 300 start‑ups, producing indigenous systems across surveillance, communication, and AI domains. Two Defence Industrial Corridors — in Uttar Pradesh (Aligarh, Kanpur, Lucknow) and Tamil Nadu (Chennai, Coimbatore, Salem) — were notified in 2018 to attract private investment. Capacity‑building in the Indian Ocean Region includes the “Indian Ocean Naval Symposium” (IONS) chaired by India in 2021‑2022, the “Coastal Surveillance Radar” network gifted to Mauritius, Seychelles, and Maldives (2019‑2022), and training‑team deployments to Tanzania and Mozambique for counter‑piracy. India remains the largest cumulative troop contributor to UN peacekeeping, with over 250,000 personnel deployed since 1950; as of 2024, Indian battalions serve in MONUSCO, UNMISS, and UNDOF, reinforcing the diplomatic weight of its defence outreach.

  • Confusing the Connect Central Asia policy (2012) with the later India‑Central Asia Summit declarations — they are distinct phases, not a single document.
  • Assuming the Pan‑African e‑Network is still operational in its original satellite form; it was migrated to a fibre‑based platform in 2020.
  • Treating India’s Arctic observer status as full membership; observers cannot vote in Arctic Council decisions.
  • Overstating UPI’s global adoption — only Bhutan, Nepal, Sri Lanka, France (pilot), and UAE (pilot) have live integrations as of 2024.
  • Equating the BrahMos export to the Philippines with a government‑to‑government loan; it was a commercial contract financed through a Philippine defence budget allocation.
  • Believing the iDEX programme directly funds production; it provides prototype grants, not serial‑production financing.
  • Remember that each summit, policy, and export deal has a specific year, financing mechanism, and institutional home — mixing them up is the most common trap.
