India’s Neighbourhood: Geopolitical Context

1.1 Historical Foundations of South Asian Relations

The modern map of South Asia emerged from the partition of British India in 1947, an event that created two sovereign states, India and Pakistan, and left a legacy of contested borders, refugee flows, and competing national narratives. The princely states that acceded to either side added further complexity, especially in Jammu and Kashmir, where the Line of Control continues to shape bilateral discourse. The early decades were marked by wars in 1947‑48, 1965, and 1971, each reinforcing a security dilemma that still informs defence postures and diplomatic signalling across the region.

1.2 Bilateral Dynamics with Pakistan

India’s relationship with Pakistan remains the most consequential bilateral axis in the neighbourhood. The core disputes centre on Kashmir, cross‑border terrorism, and water sharing under the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960. Periodic attempts at confidence‑building — such as the Lahore Declaration of 1999, the Agra Summit of 2001, and the Composite Dialogue process — have been interrupted by major attacks, notably the 2001 Parliament attack, the 2008 Mumbai attacks, and the 2019 Pulwama incident. Each rupture has led to a tightening of military readiness, diplomatic isolation strategies, and a recalibration of India’s “Neighbourhood First” policy to prioritize security over economic integration with Islamabad.

1.3 Engagement with Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and Maldives

Beyond Pakistan, India has cultivated a web of cooperative frameworks with the smaller South Asian states. The 1971 Liberation War forged a deep strategic bond with Bangladesh, reflected in the 2015 Land Boundary Agreement that resolved enclaves and the 2019 Teesta water‑sharing discussions. With Nepal, the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship provides open borders, yet periodic tensions over the 2015 Constitution and the 2020 border map dispute illustrate the sensitivity of sovereignty narratives. Bhutan’s unique relationship is anchored in the 2007 Friendship Treaty, which guides Indian assistance in hydropower and infrastructure while respecting Thimphu’s foreign‑policy autonomy. Sri Lanka’s post‑civil‑war reconstruction has seen Indian development aid, the 1987 Indo‑Sri Lanka Accord, and recent cooperation on maritime security. The Maldives, after a period of political turbulence, has returned to a defence cooperation agreement with India in 2021, underscoring the strategic importance of the Indian Ocean’s southern approaches.

1.4 Regional Cooperation Mechanisms: SAARC and BIMSTEC

The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC), launched in 1985, was envisioned as a platform for economic integration, but its progress has been stalled by Indo‑Pakistani tensions; the 2016 summit was cancelled after the Uri attack, and subsequent meetings have been informal. In response, India has championed the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi‑Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation (BIMSTEC), which excludes Pakistan and links South Asia with Southeast Asia. BIMSTEC’s 2018 Kathmandu Declaration and the 2022 Colombo Summit have advanced connectivity projects such as the India‑Myanmar‑Thailand Trilateral Highway and the BBIN (Bangladesh‑Bhutan‑India‑Nepal) motor‑vehicles agreement, offering an alternative pathway for regional economic cooperation.

Major Powers and India’s Strategic Partnerships

2.1 United States: From Estrangement to Comprehensive Global Strategic Partnership

The trajectory of India‑US relations shifted dramatically after the 1998 nuclear tests, when sanctions gave way to the 2005 Civil Nuclear Agreement, which recognized India as a responsible nuclear power and opened the door for technology transfers. The 2016 Logistics Exchange Memorandum of Agreement (LEMOA), the 2018 Communications Compatibility and Security Agreement (COMCASA), and the 2020 Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement (BECA) have institutionalised defence interoperability. The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad), revived in 2017 with Japan and Australia, reflects a convergence on a free and open Indo‑Pacific, while the 2022 Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies (iCET) deepens cooperation in semiconductors, artificial intelligence, and quantum computing.

2.2 Russia: Legacy Ties and Contemporary Defence Cooperation

India’s defence inventory remains heavily reliant on Russian platforms — Su‑30MKI fighters, T‑90 tanks, S‑400 air‑defence systems, and the INS Vikramaditya aircraft carrier. The 2018 S‑400 deal, despite US CAATSA sanctions risk, underscores the strategic autonomy India seeks. Annual summits, the 2021 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue, and the 2022 “Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership” declaration have expanded cooperation into nuclear energy (Kudankulam reactors), space (Gaganyaan crew‑module training), and Arctic research. However, the Ukraine conflict has introduced complexities, compelling India to balance its historic partnership with Moscow against growing Western pressure.

2.3 China: Border Disputes, Economic Interdependence, and Strategic Competition

The 3,488‑kilometre Line of Actual Control (LAC) remains unsettled, with flashpoints at Galwan Valley (2020), Pangong Tso (2021), and Depsang Plains. The 1993 and 1996 border agreements, the 2005 Political Parameters Guiding Principles, and the 2013 Border Defence Cooperation Agreement have not prevented periodic standoffs. Simultaneously, bilateral trade crossed US$100 billion in 2022, with India importing electronics, pharmaceuticals, and machinery while exporting iron ore, cotton, and chemicals. The 2020 “Atmanirbhar Bharat” push and the 2021 Production‑Linked Incentive schemes aim to reduce critical‑sector dependency, while the Quad and the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) provide diplomatic counterweights.

2.4 European Union and Key European States

The EU‑India Strategic Partnership, launched in 2004, was upgraded to a “Strategic Partnership for the 21st Century” in 2020, targeting trade, climate, digital, and security cooperation. The long‑stalled Broad‑Based Trade and Investment Agreement (BTIA) negotiations resumed in 2022, focusing on market access, geographical indications, and sustainable development. Bilateral ties with France (Rafale jets, Scorpène submarines, joint naval exercises), Germany (green hydrogen, vocational training), and the United Kingdom (2021 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, defence logistics) add depth to the European dimension.

2.5 Japan and Australia: Quad and Indo‑Pacific Alignment

Japan’s “Free and Open Indo‑Pacific” vision aligns with India’s Act East Policy, producing the 2015 Special Strategic and Global Partnership, the 2020 Acquisition and Cross‑Servicing Agreement (ACSA), and joint infrastructure projects in the Andaman‑Nicobar Islands and the Northeast. Australia’s 2020 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, the 2022 Mutual Logistics Support Agreement (MLSA), and the 2023 Australia‑India Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA) expand cooperation in critical minerals, cyber security, and maritime domain awareness. The Quad’s 2023 Hiroshima Summit reaffirmed commitments to vaccine delivery, climate finance, and technology standards.

Example Question
Explain how the 2020 Galwan Valley clash reshaped India’s strategic posture toward China and its engagement with the Quad. In your answer, refer to specific policy shifts, defence acquisitions, and diplomatic initiatives undertaken after the incident.
The Galwan clash in June 2020, which resulted in the first fatal confrontation on the LAC since 1975, triggered a comprehensive recalibration of India’s China policy. First, the government accelerated the “Atmanirbhar Bharat” drive, imposing restrictions on Chinese investments in sensitive sectors and banning over 200 Chinese mobile applications citing data‑security concerns. Second, defence procurement was fast‑tracked: the emergency purchase of 36 Rafale jets from France was complemented by the induction of the indigenous Light Combat Aircraft Tejas Mk‑1A and the acceleration of the S‑400 delivery schedule from Russia. Third, the border infrastructure programme received a massive budgetary boost, with the Border Roads Organisation completing the Darbuk‑Shyok‑DBO road and advancing the construction of all‑weather tunnels such as the Sela Tunnel. Diplomatically, India deepened Quad engagement — the 2021 Quad Leaders’ Summit in Washington produced a joint statement on a “free, open, inclusive, and resilient Indo‑Pacific,” and the 2022 Quad Foreign Ministers’ Meeting in Tokyo launched the Quad Vaccine Partnership and the Quad Critical and Emerging Technology Working Group. Simultaneously, India revived the 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue with the United States, signed the Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement (BECA) in October 2020, and intensified naval exercises such as Malabar 2020, which included Australia for the first time since 2007. Collectively, these steps signal a shift from strategic ambiguity to explicit balancing, embedding the Quad as a central pillar of India’s Indo‑Pacific architecture while maintaining a calibrated dialogue channel with Beijing through the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination (WMCC) and the Special Representatives’ talks.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “policy shifts after a specific event,” structure the answer chronologically: immediate security measures, medium‑term defence procurement, long‑term diplomatic realignments. Cite the exact agreements (e.g., BECA, ACSA, MLSA) and institutional mechanisms (WMCC, Quad Working Groups) to demonstrate precision.

Multilateral Groupings and India’s Role

3.1 United Nations and Reform Aspirations

India has been a founding member of the United Nations and has contributed over 250,000 personnel to peacekeeping missions since 1950, making it one of the largest troop contributors. The quest for a permanent seat on the Security Council is anchored in the G4 (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan) framework, which advocates expansion in both permanent and non‑permanent categories. India’s 2021‑22 Presidency of the UN Security Council highlighted themes of maritime security, counter‑terrorism, and technology‑driven peacekeeping, reinforcing its claim to greater responsibility.

3.2 BRICS: Economic Cooperation and Political Coordination

The BRICS grouping (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) provides a platform for emerging‑market coordination on financial architecture, exemplified by the New Development Bank (NDB) established in 2015 with an initial capital of US$100 billion. India has leveraged the NDB to fund sustainable infrastructure projects such as the Mumbai Metro and the Rajasthan solar parks. The 2023 Johannesburg Summit expanded BRICS to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates, signalling a shift toward a broader “Global South” coalition, while India continues to push for reform of the IMF quota system and greater use of local currencies in intra‑BRICS trade.

3.3 G20: Global Economic Governance

India’s G20 Presidency in 2023, themed “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — One Earth, One Family, One Future,” prioritized inclusive growth, digital public infrastructure, and climate finance. The New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration secured consensus on the “G20 Action Plan on Sustainable Development Goals,” the “Global Biofuels Alliance,” and a commitment to triple renewable energy capacity by 2030. India’s advocacy for the “Digital Public Goods” framework, illustrated by the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) and the CoWIN platform, positioned it as a model for scalable, open‑source digital solutions.

3.4 SCO: Security and Regional Connectivity

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO), joined by India and Pakistan in 2017, focuses on counter‑terrorism, regional stability, and connectivity. India participates in the Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure (RATS) and has hosted the 2023 SCO Summit in New Delhi, emphasizing the “SCO Development Strategy 2030” and the “SCO Economic Development Strategy.” The International North‑South Transport Corridor (INSTC) and the Chabahar Port project are highlighted as SCO‑compatible connectivity initiatives linking India with Central Asia and Russia.

3.5 IORA and Indian Ocean Rim Initiatives

The Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA), with 23 member states, serves as India’s primary multilateral forum for maritime cooperation. The 2022 IORA Council of Ministers Meeting in Dhaka adopted the “IORA Action Plan 2022‑2026,” focusing on maritime safety, blue economy, and disaster risk reduction. India’s “Security and Growth for All in the Region” (SAGAR) doctrine, articulated in 2015, aligns with IORA objectives, and the Indian Navy’s Information Fusion Centre‑Indian Ocean Region (IFC‑IOR) in Gurugram operationalises shared maritime domain awareness.

International Institutions and Normative Frameworks

4.1 World Trade Organization and Trade Dispute Settlement

India has been an active participant in the WTO since its inception in 1995, championing the Doha Development Agenda and defending special and differential treatment for developing countries. The 2013 Bali Package on trade facilitation and the 2017 Nairobi Ministerial Decision on public stockholding for food security reflect India’s negotiating priorities. In recent years, India has filed disputes against the United States on steel and aluminium tariffs (DS547) and against the European Union on the carbon border adjustment mechanism, while also defending its own agricultural subsidies in the DS541 case brought by the US.

4.2 International Monetary Fund and Financial Stability

India’s quota in the IMF was increased to 2.75 % in the 2010 reforms, raising its voting share to 2.63 %. The 2021 Special Drawing Rights (SDR) allocation of US$650 billion provided India with an additional SDR 12.5 billion, bolstering foreign‑exchange reserves. India advocates for a more representative governance structure, pushing for a third chair for emerging markets on the Executive Board and for the inclusion of the Chinese renminbi in the SDR basket, which was realised in 2016.

4.3 Climate Change Regime: UNFCCC, Paris Agreement, and India’s Commitments

Under the UNFCCC, India submitted its first Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) in 2015, pledging a 33‑35 % reduction in emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 from 2005 levels, 40 % cumulative electric power installed capacity from non‑fossil sources, and creation of an additional carbon sink of 2.5‑3 billion tonnes CO₂ equivalent. The 2021 Glasgow Climate Pact saw India announce a net‑zero target by 2070, a 500 GW renewable capacity goal by 2030, and the launch of the International Solar Alliance (ISA) with France, now comprising 110 signatory countries. The 2023 G20 New Delhi Declaration reinforced the “phase‑down” of unabated coal power and the “tripling” of renewable energy capacity globally.

4.4 Nuclear Non‑Proliferation Regime: NPT, CTBT, and India’s Position

India remains outside the Nuclear Non‑Proliferation Treaty (NPT) and the Comprehensive Nuclear‑Test‑Ban Treaty (CTBT), arguing that these instruments create a discriminatory hierarchy between nuclear‑weapon states and others. The 2008 NSG waiver, secured through the US‑India Civil Nuclear Agreement, allowed India to engage in civilian nuclear commerce while maintaining its strategic arsenal. India adheres to a voluntary moratorium on nuclear testing since 1998, supports the Fissile Material Cut‑off Treaty (FMCT) negotiations, and participates in the Nuclear Security Summits, emphasizing responsible stewardship.

4.5 Human Rights Mechanisms and India’s Engagement

India is a party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), and it engages with the Universal Periodic Review (UPR) process at the Human Rights Council. The 2022 UPR cycle saw India accept 152 of 250 recommendations, focusing on women’s empowerment, child protection, and the rights of persons with disabilities. India’s domestic legislation — such as the 2013 Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, the 2019 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, and the 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Act — reflects an evolving alignment with international norms, though debates persist on issues like the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act and the Citizenship (Amendment) Act.

The Indian Diaspora: Soft Power and Policy

5.1 Historical Migration Waves and Demographic Profile

The Indian diaspora, estimated at over 32 million persons of Indian origin (PIOs) and non‑resident Indians (NRIs) as of 2023, traces its roots to nineteenth‑century indentured labour migration to Mauritius, Trinidad, Fiji, and South Africa, followed by post‑1960s professional migration to the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states. The Gulf hosts the largest concentration, with approximately 9 million Indian workers contributing to construction, healthcare, and services sectors, while the United States and the United Kingdom each host communities exceeding 4 million, distinguished by high educational attainment and income levels.

5.2 Remittances, Investment, and Knowledge Transfer

Remittances to India reached a record US$111 billion in 2022, representing roughly 3 % of GDP and surpassing foreign direct investment inflows. The Reserve Bank of India’s “Remittance Survey” shows that the GCC corridor accounts for over 30 % of total flows, followed by North America and Europe. Beyond capital, diaspora networks facilitate technology transfer — exemplified by the “India‑US Science and Technology Forum” and the “VAJRA” faculty scheme — and stimulate entrepreneurship through initiatives like the “Startup India” diaspora engagement chapter.

5.3 Political Influence in Host Countries

Indian‑origin politicians have attained prominent positions: Kamala Harris as Vice President of the United States, Rishi Sunak as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, and numerous ministers and legislators in Canada, Australia, Singapore, and the Gulf. These leaders often advocate for deeper bilateral ties, as seen in the 2023 US‑India Strategic Clean Energy Partnership and the 2022 UK‑India Comprehensive Strategic Partnership. Diaspora organisations such as the US‑India Business Council (USIBC) and the Overseas Friends of BJP (OFBJP) mobilise political support for trade agreements, defence deals, and visa liberalisation.

5.4 Government Policies: OCI, PIO, and Pravasi Bharatiya Divas

The Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) scheme, introduced in 2005 and merged with the Persons of Indian Origin (PIO) card in 2015, grants lifelong visa‑free travel, parity with NRIs in economic and educational fields, and exemption from registration for stays exceeding 180 days. The annual Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (PBD), celebrated on 9 January since 2003, serves as a platform for policy dialogue, investment pitching, and cultural exchange. The 2023 PBD in Indore focused on “Diaspora: Reliable Partners for India’s Progress in Amrit Kaal,” launching the “Global Indian Talent Pool” portal to match skilled diaspora professionals with domestic opportunities.

5.5 Challenges: Dual Citizenship, Consular Protection, and Integration

India’s Constitution prohibits dual citizenship, compelling many diaspora members to choose between Indian nationality and the citizenship of their country of residence, which can limit political participation and property rights in India. Consular protection remains a concern, especially for low‑skilled workers in the Gulf facing contractual disputes, passport confiscation, and repatriation challenges during crises such as the COVID‑19 pandemic. Integration issues — ranging from cultural preservation to second‑generation identity — are addressed through community organisations, language schools, and the “Know India Programme” for youth, yet the scale and diversity of the diaspora demand continuous policy refinement.
