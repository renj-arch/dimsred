1. Government Schemes and Programmes

1.1 Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB PM-JAY)

Launched on 23 September 2018 by PM Narendra Modi from Ranchi, Jharkhand, AB PM-JAY is the world’s largest government-funded healthcare programme. It provides a health cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care hospitalization to ~10.7 crore poor and vulnerable families (~50 crore beneficiaries). The scheme is cashless and paperless at empanelled hospitals — over 27,000 public and private hospitals are empanelled nationwide. As of 2026, over 11.69 crore hospital admissions worth ₹1.73 lakh crore have been authorized.

Key Fact: In September 2024, the scheme was extended to cover all citizens aged 70 years and above, irrespective of economic status — each senior gets a separate ₹5 lakh top-up cover.

Background and Genesis

The concept of universal health coverage (UHC) in India dates back to the Bhore Committee (1946), which first recommended comprehensive healthcare for all. In 2017-18, out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) on health pushed ~5.5 crore Indians into poverty annually. NITI Aayog’s Three-Year Action Agenda (2017-20) recommended merging existing health insurance schemes (RSBY, SHIS) into a single comprehensive programme. The National Health Policy 2017 set a target of increasing public health expenditure to 2.5% of GDP. Against this backdrop, AB PM-JAY was announced in the Union Budget 2018-19 and formally launched later that year, subsuming the existing Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana (RSBY, 2008) and the Senior Citizen Health Insurance Scheme (SCHIS, 2016).

Objectives

  • Reduce out-of-pocket health expenditure and catastrophic health spending among poor and vulnerable families
  • Increase access to quality secondary and tertiary healthcare
  • Create a culture of formal healthcare-seeking behaviour among the poor
  • Leverage public-private partnership to expand healthcare delivery infrastructure
  • Generate health data for evidence-based policy making under the National Digital Health Mission (NDHM)

Funding and Implementation Model

The scheme is funded by the Government of India and respective state governments in a 60:40 ratio (60:40 for states and UTs with legislature; 90:10 for NE and Himalayan states; 100% Centre for UTs without legislature). The National Health Authority (NHA) is the apex body for implementation at the national level, with State Health Agencies (SHAs) at the state level. Premiums are paid to insurance companies or Trust/TPA models are used. The benefit package covers 1,949 medical procedures across 27 specialties, including cardiology, oncology, neurology, orthopaedics, and renal care. Pre-authorisation for procedures is computer-driven with a target of 1-hour turnaround.

Milestones

  • 2018: Launch by PM Modi; 13 states join initially
  • 2019: Coverage expanded to ~10.7 crore families; 1 crore hospital admissions crossed
  • 2020: COVID-19 testing and treatment included; Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) launched
  • 2021: PM-JAY for health workers fighting COVID; 2.5 crore hospital admissions crossed
  • 2022: Extension to all frontline workers; Ayushman Arogya Mandir network expanded
  • 2024: Senior citizen top-up cover for 70+ age group announced
  • 2026: Over 11.69 crore hospital admissions authorized; network of 27,000+ empanelled hospitals

Criticism and Challenges

  • Underutilisation: Despite coverage, hospital utilisation rates remain below projections — only ~30% of eligible families have used the scheme
  • Provider Empanelment: Private hospitals in rural areas reluctant to join due to low reimbursement rates compared to CGHS/ESIS rates
  • Administrative Delays: Pre-authorisation and claims processing delays in some states
  • Fraud and Abuse: Reports of fraudulent claims, unnecessary procedures, and billing irregularities — NHA has implemented AI-based fraud detection
  • Awareness Gap: Many beneficiaries unaware of their coverage, particularly in remote areas
  • Quality Concerns: No robust quality assurance framework; health outcomes not systematically tracked
  • Fiscal Sustainability: Rising premium costs and expanding coverage raise concerns about long-term fiscal burden on states

State Participation

Most states have joined, though some like Odisha initially opted out and later joined in April 2025 after BJP won state elections. Delhi implemented the scheme in April 2025 after the BJP government assumed office. West Bengal implemented it in May 2026 under CM Suvendu Adhikari.

1.2 Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN)

Launched in February 2019, PM-KISAN provides income support of ₹6,000 per year to all landholding farmer families, paid in three equal installments of ₹2,000 each directly into the bank accounts via DBT. The scheme covers ~11.8 crore beneficiaries with an annual budget of ₹75,000 crore. The 18th installment was released in October 2024. Uttar Pradesh has the highest number of beneficiaries (~2.25 crore), followed by Maharashtra (~91 lakh) and Madhya Pradesh (~81 lakh).

Background

The scheme was announced in the Interim Budget 2019-20 by then-FM Piyush Goyal. It was retroactively made effective from December 2018. The scheme emerged from the context of agrarian distress — falling farm incomes, rising input costs, and the demand for a universal MSP guarantee. Several states had already launched their own income support schemes: Telangana’s Rythu Bandhu (2018), Odisha’s KALIA (2018), and Andhra’s Annadatha Sukhibhava. PM-KISAN was positioned as the central government’s response to the agrarian crisis.

Eligibility and Exclusions

Initially limited to small and marginal farmers (up to 2 hectares), the scheme was expanded to all landholding farmers in June 2019. Exclusions include: income tax payers, government employees (excluding Class IV/Group D), pensioners with pension >₹10,000/month, professionals (doctors, engineers, lawyers, architects, chartered accountants), and those who paid income tax in the previous assessment year. Institutional landholders are also excluded.

Criticism

  • Exclusion of Tenant Farmers: The scheme is landowner-based — India’s ~27% agricultural households who are tenant farmers sharecroppers do not benefit
  • Inadequate Amount: ₹6,000/year works out to ₹500/month — critics argue this is insufficient to meaningfully address agrarian distress; farmer organisations demand ₹12,000-18,000/year
  • Inflation Erosion: No indexation or increase since 2019 — real value has declined due to inflation
  • Targeting Issues: Rich farmers with large landholdings also receive benefits — many states have not updated land records, leading to inclusion errors
  • MSP Demands: Farmer protestors argue that income support should complement, not replace, a legal guarantee of MSP
Comparison: PM-KISAN is similar to Telangana’s Rythu Bandhu (₹10,000/acre/year), Andhra’s Annadatha Sukhibhava (₹9,000-15,000/family), and Odisha’s KALIA (₹5,000/family/season). However, PM-KISAN is landowner-based and excludes tenant farmers.

1.3 Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY)

PMAY is a flagship housing scheme with two components: PMAY-Urban (PMAY-U) and PMAY-Gramin (PMAY-G). The combined target is 2.95 crore houses. PMAY-U (2015) provides assistance of ₹1-2.5 lakh per house under various verticals — Beneficiary-Led Construction (BLC), Credit-Linked Subsidy Scheme (CLSS), In-Situ Slum Redevelopment (ISSR), and Affordable Housing in Partnership (AHP). PMAY-G (2016) provides ₹1.20 lakh in plain areas and ₹1.30 lakh in hilly/difficult areas for the construction of pucca houses. Over 2.68 crore houses have been completed under both schemes as of 2024.

Background and Evolution

PMAY subsumed the earlier Indira Awaas Yojana (IAY, 1985) and Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (JNNURM) housing components. The scheme was launched to fulfill the government’s commitment of “Housing for All by 2022.” PMAY-U was approved in June 2015 with an initial investment of ₹4.83 lakh crore, and PMAY-G was launched in November 2016. The target date was extended multiple times — PMAY-U to 2024 and PMAY-G to 2026. In Budget 2024-25, the government announced a new PMAY-U 2.0 with an additional 1 crore houses under construction over the next 5 years.

PMAY-U Verticals

  • Beneficiary-Led Construction (BLC): Financial assistance of ₹1.5 lakh to individual households for constructing/reconstruction of houses on their own land
  • Credit-Linked Subsidy Scheme (CLSS): Interest subsidy on home loans — 6.5% for EWS (up to ₹6L loan), 4% for LIG (up to ₹9L), 3% for MIG-I (up to ₹12L), and MIG-II (up to ₹12L) — though CLSS for MIG was discontinued in 2022
  • In-Situ Slum Redevelopment (ISSR): Using land as a resource — private developers redevelop slums with cross-subsidisation; FSI/TDR incentives provided
  • Affordable Housing in Partnership (AHP): ₹1.5 lakh per house for affordable housing projects where 35% of houses are for EWS

Milestones

  • 2015: PMAY-U launched with 4 verticals
  • 2016: PMAY-G launched replacing IAY; 1 crore houses sanctioned
  • 2020: CLSS extended to MIG category (later discontinued March 2022)
  • 2022: 2 crore houses completed across both schemes
  • 2024: 2.68 crore houses completed; PMAY-U 2.0 announced for additional 1 crore houses

1.4 Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY)

Launched in May 2016, PMUY provides free LPG connections to women from BPL households. Over 10.3 crore connections have been released. Under Ujjwala 2.0 (launched August 2021), the scheme was expanded to include migrant families and those missed in the SECC data. The subsidy for refills is ₹200 per cylinder (upto 12 refills/year). The scheme aims to reduce indoor air pollution and improve women’s health.

Background and Health Impact

Indoor air pollution from traditional cooking fuels (firewood, dung cakes, kerosene) was causing an estimated 5 lakh premature deaths annually in India (WHO data). Women and young children were disproportionately affected. The World Health Organization identified indoor smoke as a leading risk factor for COPD, lung cancer, and pneumonia in India. PMUY was designed to address this public health crisis while also reducing the time burden on women who spent 2-4 hours daily collecting firewood.

Evolution

  • PMUY 1.0 (2016): Target of 5 crore connections to BPL households — achieved in 2018, 8 months ahead of schedule
  • PMUY 2.0 (2021): Expanded to include migrant families (who lacked proof of address) and those missed in SECC data; target of 1 crore additional connections; also covered households from 7 priority categories (SC/ST, islands, forest dwellers, etc.)
  • Current Status: Over 10.3 crore total connections released; budget allocation ₹2,400 crore (FY25) for PAHAL subsidy

Criticism

  • Sustained Usage: CAG report (2021) found that ~20% of beneficiaries did not refill cylinders after the initial free connection — inability to afford refills at market price (₹800-1,000/cylinder)
  • Subsidy Cuts: Government has periodically reduced LPG subsidy — in 2020, the ₹200/cylinder subsidy was stopped for most consumers and restored for Ujjwala beneficiaries only in 2022
  • Price Volatility: Global LPG price volatility affects refill affordability — Ujjwala beneficiaries highly sensitive to price changes
  • Distribution Gaps: Remote rural areas lack last-mile delivery infrastructure — distributors often charge extra for delivery

1.5 Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM)

Launched in August 2019 under the Ministry of Jal Shakti, JJM aims to provide Functional Household Tap Connection (FHTC) to every rural household by 2024. Target: 15.3 crore rural households. Coverage increased from ~18% (3.23 crore) in August 2019 to ~78% (15.11 crore) by August 2024. Budget allocation: ₹60,000+ crore annually. Goa and Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu became the first ’Har Ghar Jal’ certified state and UT respectively in August 2022. The WHO estimates ~4 lakh diarrhoeal deaths prevented due to safe drinking water access. JJM 2.0 approved in March 2026 with a target of 100% coverage by 2028.

Background

Despite decades of investment, by 2019 only 18% of rural households had piped water connections. The National Rural Drinking Water Programme (NRDWP, 2009) and earlier Accelerated Rural Water Supply Programme (ARWSP, 1972-73) had made limited progress. The JJM was created by merging NRDWP into a new mission-oriented approach with full central assistance for capital expenditure to states. The formation of the Ministry of Jal Shakti (May 2019) — merging the Ministry of Water Resources and Ministry of Drinking Water and Sanitation — provided unified administrative oversight.

Funding and Implementation Model

Total outlay of ₹3.6 lakh crore (2020-2024). Funding ratio: 50:50 for Himalayan and NE states, 90:10 for other states, 100% for UTs. States prepare State Action Plans (SAPs) with Gram Panchayats as key implementing units. Community contribution of 5% (10% in NE/Himalayan states) for in-village infrastructure. The mission emphasises source sustainability — through rainwater harvesting, greywater treatment, recharge structures. The sensor-based IoT platform for real-time monitoring of water quality and quantity is under development.

Challenges

  • Water Quality: Even with tap connections, many sources have contamination — arsenic in West Bengal/Bihar/Jharkhand, fluoride in Rajasthan/Gujarat, salinity in coastal areas
  • Source Sustainability: In water-stressed regions (Marathwada, Bundelkhand, rain-shadow areas), groundwater depletion threatens long-term functionality
  • Operation & Maintenance: Gram Panchayats lack technical capacity and financial resources to maintain piped water schemes after construction
  • Greywater Management: Increased water consumption without corresponding wastewater treatment infrastructure creates new sanitation challenges
  • Behavioural Change: Communities accustomed to traditional sources may not adopt tap water for drinking

1.6 Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM)

Launched on 2 October 2014 at Rajghat, New Delhi. Phase 1 (2014-2019): Achieved Open Defecation Free (ODF) status for over 600,000 villages. Over 10.9 crore individual household latrines (IHHLs) constructed in rural areas. Phase 2 (2020-2025): Focus on ODF+ (sustaining ODF) and ODF++ (solid and liquid waste management). The mission operates under two wings: SBM-Gramin (Ministry of Jal Shakti) and SBM-Urban (Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs). SBM-U 2.0 (2021-2026) budget is ₹30,980 crore. India’s open defecation rate declined from 73% in 2000 to ~7% in 2024.

Background and Historical Context

India’s sanitation crisis was among the world’s most acute. The Total Sanitation Campaign (1999), later renamed Nirmal Bharat Abhiyan (2012), had limited success — constructing toilets but failing to achieve behavioural change. By 2014, ~60% of rural India still practised open defecation (UNICEF/WHO JMP data). SBM was launched with unprecedented political commitment, brand visibility, and budget allocation — ₹2.04 lakh crore total outlay (2014-2019). The mission drew inspiration from successful sanitation programmes in Bangladesh (which achieved ODF status by 2003) and the community-led total sanitation (CLTS) approach pioneered by Kamal Kar.

Phase-wise Evolution

SBM Phase I (2014-2019): Focus on toilet construction and behaviour change. Over 10.9 crore IHHLs constructed. 4.4 lakh villages declared ODF. 698 districts ODF. The National Annual Rural Sanitation Survey (NARSS) found 96.5% of rural households had access to toilets and 95.6% were using them. SBM Phase II (2020-2025): ODF+ (sustaining ODF and managing solid waste), ODF++ (liquid waste management also), and Water+ (greywater treatment). The phase focuses on SLWM (Solid and Liquid Waste Management) infrastructure — 2.3 lakh community compost pits, 1.1 lakh wastewater management systems. SBM-U 2.0 (2021-2026): Focus on faecal sludge management, wastewater treatment, source segregation of garbage, remediation of legacy dumpsites, and establishing waste-to-energy plants.

Criticism

  • Behaviour vs Infrastructure: Studies show that toilet construction does not automatically translate to usage — the SQUAT study (2014-15) found that even when toilets were available, many households continued open defecation due to habit, convenience, and cultural norms
  • Quality of Construction: CAG (2020) reported poor quality toilets, missing superstructures, and lack of water supply — many toilets abandoned or used for storage
  • Verification Concerns: ODF claims relied on self-reporting and third-party verification audits that faced methodological criticism — WHO/UNICEF JMP data shows slower improvement than government ODF claims
  • Caste and Labour: Manual scavenging, though legally prohibited, persists — sanitation workers face social stigma and occupational hazards
  • Urban-Rural Gap: Urban sanitation — particularly in slums — remains inadequate; untreated sewage discharge continues into rivers

1.7 National Education Policy (NEP) 2020

Approved by Union Cabinet on 29 July 2020, replacing the 1986 policy. Key reforms include the 5+3+3+4 curricular structure: Foundational (3-7 yrs), Preparatory (8-10), Middle (11-13), Secondary (14-18). Vocational education from Class 6 with internships. Multiple entry and exit options in higher education through the Academic Bank of Credits (ABC). The National Testing Agency (NTA) conducts common entrance exams. PARAKH (Performance Assessment, Review and Analysis of Knowledge for Holistic Development) sets board exam standards. DIKSHA and SWAYAM are digital platforms for learning. The policy aims to increase public spending on education to 6% of GDP.

Historical Background

India’s education policy framework has evolved through several landmark documents: the Wood’s Dispatch (1854 — modern education), the University Education Commission (Radhakrishnan, 1948), the Secondary Education Commission (Mudaliar, 1952), and the Education Commission (Kothari, 1964-66) which first recommended the 10+2 structure and 6% GDP investment in education. The National Policy on Education 1968 was the first national policy, followed by NPE 1986 (revised 1992). The NEP 2020 is the first comprehensive policy of the 21st century. The draft was prepared by a committee led by former ISRO chief K. Kasturirangan, submitted in 2019 after extensive consultation.

Key Reforms — School Education

  • 5+3+3+4 Structure: Replaces the earlier 10+2 system — Foundational (3 years pre-school + 2 years primary), Preparatory (3 years), Middle (3 years), Secondary (4 years)
  • Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE): Universal access to quality early childhood education by 2030 — integration of anganwadis with schools
  • National Curricular Framework (NCF): NCF for Foundational Stage (2022) emphasises play-based learning, mother tongue as medium of instruction (at least till Grade 5)
  • Assessment Reform: Shifting from summative board exams to competency-based assessment; PARAKH as national assessment regulator
  • Vocational Education: From Class 6 onwards with internships; target of 50% of students exposed to vocational education by 2025
  • Flexible Subject Choices: No rigid stream separation — students can choose subjects across arts, science, and commerce

Key Reforms — Higher Education

  • Multiple Entry and Exit: Certificate after 1 year, Diploma after 2 years, Degree after 3 years, Honours after 4 years
  • Academic Bank of Credits (ABC): Digital storehouse of credits earned — transferable across universities
  • Higher Education Commission of India (HECI): Single regulatory body replacing UGC, AICTE, NCTE, and others — with 4 independent verticals
  • Multidisciplinary Universities: All HEIs to become multidisciplinary by 2040 — target of 20 million seats for higher education
  • Internationalisation: Top 100 foreign universities allowed to set up campuses in India
  • Research: National Research Foundation (NRF) established 2023 — ₹50,000 crore funding over 5 years

Implementation Challenges

  • Funding Gap: Public spending on education remains ~3% of GDP, far from the 6% target; states spend varying proportions from their budgets
  • Teacher Shortage: 1 million+ teacher vacancies across government schools; quality of teacher education remains poor
  • Language Debate: Three-language formula contentious in Tamil Nadu (which follows two-language policy); mother-tongue medium faces implementation hurdles
  • Digital Divide: Online learning (DIKSHA, SWAYAM) requires internet access — only ~45% rural households have internet connectivity
  • NTA Controversies: Exam paper leaks, NEET-UG 2024 irregularities raised questions about NTA’s capacity
  • Teacher Deployment: Single-teacher schools (1,12,000+ in 2022) cannot implement the Foundational Stage curriculum

Detailed Notes on Selected Schemes

PM Mudra Yojana (2015): Launched on 8 April 2015, MUDRA (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency) provides loans up to ₹10 lakh to non-corporate, non-farm small/micro enterprises. Three products: Shishu (loans up to ₹50,000), Kishor (₹50,001-₹5 lakh), Tarun (₹5 lakh-₹10 lakh). As of 2024, over ₹30.5 lakh crore sanctioned cumulatively. Women entrepreneurs constitute ~70% of beneficiaries. Interest rates range from 8-14% depending on the lending institution. Criticism: high NPAs (~12% in Kishor/Tarun categories), lack of financial literacy among borrowers, and limited formal credit penetration in rural areas.

PM SVANidhi (2020): Launched on 1 June 2020 as a COVID-relief measure for street vendors. Provides collateral-free working capital loans of ₹10,000 (first tranche), ₹20,000 (second), and ₹50,000 (third) with 7% interest subsidy if repaid on time. Over 66 lakh beneficiaries as of 2024. Linked to the SVANidhi Seva platform for digital payments. Criticism: limited loan amounts, high rejection rates in some states, and difficulties in reaching vendors without vending certificates.

PM Vishwakarma Yojana (2023): Launched on 17 September 2023 (Vishwakarma Jayanti) with an outlay of ₹13,000 crore. Covers 18 traditional trades including carpenter, blacksmith, potter, tailor, barber, washerman, mason, goldsmith, etc. Benefits: skill training (5-7 days with ₹500/day stipend), toolkit kit (₹15,000), collateral-free loan up to ₹3 lakh (1st tranche ₹1 lakh, 2nd tranche ₹2 lakh) at 5% interest, and digital literacy. Target of 20 lakh beneficiaries in FY 2023-24. The scheme fills the gap for traditional artisans who were not covered by PM Mudra or MUDRA.

PM GatiShakti National Master Plan (2021): Launched on 13 October 2021, a GIS-based digital platform integrating infrastructure planning across 16 ministries — roads, railways, ports, airports, telecom, and pipelines. Enables synchronised project planning, real-time monitoring, and multimodal connectivity. Over 1,400 critical infrastructure projects mapped. The plan addresses the historical problem of siloed infrastructure planning — e.g., a highway constructed without coordinating with railway overbridges or telecom duct laying. NIP (National Infrastructure Pipeline) launched in 2019 identified ₹111 lakh crore worth of projects (2019-25).

FAME India (2015): Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Electric Vehicles — Phase I (2015-19) outlay ₹895 crore; Phase II (2019-24) outlay ₹11,500 crore (later extended). Provides demand incentives for EV buyers (₹10,000-20,000 per kWh battery capacity). Phase II also supports charging infrastructure (2,877 charging stations sanctioned). Criticism: incentives primarily benefit electric 2- and 3-wheelers; electric car penetration remains low; domestic battery manufacturing ecosystem underdeveloped. The ₹26,058 crore PLI scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) battery storage aims to address domestic manufacturing. In 2024, 6.4 lakh EVs were sold in India — 6% of total vehicle sales. The government targets 30% EV penetration by 2030.

Aspirational Districts Programme (2018): Launched in January 2018 by PM Modi, based on the “Transformation of Aspirational Districts” initiative. 112 districts across 28 states identified based on composite indicators covering health & nutrition, education, agriculture & water resources, financial inclusion & skill development, and basic infrastructure. The programme uses 49 indicators across 5 themes. Results: significant improvements in basic infrastructure (electrification, road connectivity), health indicators (institutional births, immunisation), and education (school enrolment, learning outcomes). The delta ranking methodology incentivises competition among districts. The programme has been recognised globally by the World Bank and UNDP as a model for sub-national development administration.

PM-JANMAN (2023): Pradhan Mantri Janjati Adivasi Nyaya Maha Abhiyan — launched on 15 November 2023 (Janjatiya Gaurav Divas) with a ₹24,000 crore outlay. Focus on Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) — 75 communities across 18 states. The scheme provides comprehensive coverage: pucca housing (PMAY), road connectivity (PMGSY), piped water (JJM), electricity, solar lighting, education infrastructure, health outreach, and skill development. The PVTGs represent the most marginalised among tribal communities — with declining populations, pre-agricultural technology levels, and extremely low literacy rates (e.g., Jarawas, Shompens, Sentinelese in Andaman; Birhor in Jharkhand; Chenchu in Telangana-AP; Kathodi in Gujarat). The government had earlier constituted a PVTG Development Mission in 2019.

Digital India (2015): Launched on 1 July 2015 with the vision to transform India into a digitally empowered society. Key pillars: digital infrastructure (BharatNet — fibre-to-the-gram-panchayat, NOFN), digital governance (e-Kranti, e-Governance initiatives), digital literacy (Pradhan Mantri Gramin Digital Saksharta Abhiyan), and digital delivery (Direct Benefit Transfer, Aadhaar, DigiLocker, UMANG app, eSign). India’s UPI system processed ₹200+ lakh crore in FY24 — world’s largest real-time payment system. Over 1.3 billion Aadhaar cards issued. CoWIN platform delivered 2.2 billion COVID-19 vaccine doses. The India Stack (Aadhaar-Payments-DigiLocker-Consent) is now being exported to other developing countries. The ₹1.39 lakh crore BharatNet Phase III aims to connect all 2.5 lakh gram panchayats with high-speed broadband.

Fertiliser Subsidy and Nano Urea: The fertiliser subsidy bill peaked at ₹2.55 lakh crore in FY23 due to higher global prices post-Ukraine war, declining to ₹1.64 lakh crore in FY25. The government promotes nano-urea (liquid, produced by IFFCO) to reduce dependence on imported urea. A 500 ml bottle of nano-urea is equivalent to a 45 kg bag of conventional urea and is priced at ₹240 (vs ₹266 per bag of conventional urea). The world’s first nano urea plant was inaugurated in Gandhinagar, Gujarat in 2022. PM-PRANAM (PM Programme for Restoration, Awareness, Nourishment and Amelioration of Mother Earth) launched in 2023 to incentivise states to reduce chemical fertiliser use by promoting alternative fertilisers.

2. Union Budget and Economic Survey

2.1 Union Budget 2024-25

The Interim Budget was presented on 1 February 2024 by FM Nirmala Sitharaman (her 6th consecutive budget). The Full Budget was presented on 22 July 2024 after the general elections. Key highlights: Capex ₹11.11 lakh crore (+11.1% YoY), fiscal deficit 4.9% of GDP (revised from 5.1% in interim budget). Total expenditure: ~₹48.2 lakh crore. Gross tax receipts: ~₹38.3 lakh crore. Net tax to Centre: ~₹25.8 lakh crore.

Historical Context of the Budget Process

The first Union Budget of independent India was presented by R.K. Shanmukham Chetty on 26 November 1947. Prior to 2017, the Railway Budget and General Budget were presented separately (a colonial legacy since 1924 when the Acworth Committee recommended separating railway finances). FM Arun Jaitley merged the two in 2017. The budget presentation date was advanced from the last day of February to 1 February in 2017 to allow departments to spend the full fiscal year. The Finance Bill and Appropriation Bill are debated in Parliament under Articles 112-117 of the Constitution. The budget is prepared by the Department of Economic Affairs under the Ministry of Finance through an elaborate 5-month process starting in September.

Economic Rationale Behind 2024-25 Budget

The 2024-25 Budget was framed in a context of global economic uncertainty — post-pandemic recovery, Russia-Ukraine war inflation, Red Sea supply chain disruptions, and tight global monetary policy. India’s real GDP growth for FY24 was 8.2% — the fastest among major economies. The budget aimed to: (a) maintain fiscal consolidation (target 4.9% deficit, reducing to 4.5% by FY26) despite coalition constraints; (b) continue infrastructure capex push to crowd-in private investment; (c) provide consumption support to rural and lower-income groups through tax relief and scheme allocations; and (d) signal fiscal credibility to global rating agencies and bond markets. India’s inclusion in the JP Morgan Emerging Market Bond Index (June 2024) was a key milestone enabled by fiscal discipline — expected to attract $20-30 billion in foreign portfolio flows.

Detailed Tax Proposals

  • Income Tax — New Regime: nil (0-3L), 5% (3-7L), 10% (7-10L), 15% (10-12L), 20% (12-15L), 30% (15L+). Standard deduction hiked to ₹75,000 from ₹50,000. Family pension standard deduction raised to ₹25,000. The new tax regime is now the default — taxpayers must actively opt for the old regime
  • Capital Gains: LTCG rate for all assets reduced to 12.5% (from 10% for some and 20% for others). STCG for certain financial assets increased to 20% (from 15%). Exemption limit for LTCG on equity raised to ₹1.25 lakh (from ₹1 lakh). Holding period for assets unified to 24 months (from 36 months for most assets)
  • Angel Tax: Abolished for all investors — the controversial Section 56(2)(viib) removed for startups and investors
  • Customs Duties: Reduced on mobile phone components, critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, nickel), and cancer drugs (three new drugs exempted). Basic customs duty on gold reduced to 6%
  • STT: Securities Transaction Tax increased on Futures & Options to curb retail speculative trading — F&O turnover crossed ₹500 lakh crore in FY24
  • Vivad Se Vishwas 2.0: Income tax dispute resolution scheme for pending appeals — reduced interest and penalty for early settlement

Subsidies — Detailed Analysis

  • Food Subsidy (₹2.05 lakh crore): Covers NFSA (National Food Security Act) beneficiaries — 81.35 crore people at 5 kg foodgrains/month at ₹2-3/kg. PMGKAY (PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana) extended periodically post-COVID providing free additional 5 kg foodgrains. Food Corporation of India (FCI) manages procurement and distribution. Central Issue Price (CIP) for wheat at ₹2/kg and rice at ₹3/kg has not changed since 2013 — increasing subsidy burden. Open market sales (OMSS) used for market intervention. The food subsidy bill has tripled from ₹72,000 crore (FY14) to ~₹2.05 lakh crore
  • Fertiliser Subsidy (₹1.64 lakh crore): Down from ₹2.55 lakh crore peak (FY23) due to moderation in global energy prices. The government imports ~30% of urea requirement (domestic production via IFFCO, NFL, FACT). Nano urea promotion aims to reduce physical urea consumption. The Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS) regime covers P&K fertilisers, while urea remains under MRP control with the difference as subsidy. Nutrient Use Efficiency (NUE) low — 30-40% for nitrogen vs global 50-60%. PM-PRANAM incentivises states to promote alternative fertilisers. One Nation One Fertiliser (PMBharat) brand for all fertiliser companies
  • Petroleum Subsidy (~₹11,000 crore): Primarily for LPG (Ujjwala) and kerosene (PDS kerosene under phase-out). Petrol and diesel are generally at market-determined prices, though the government reduced excise duty in November 2021 and has not restored it. The under-recovery on LPG is shared between OMCs and government

Fiscal Consolidation Roadmap

The government targets reducing fiscal deficit from 4.9% (FY25) to 4.5% (FY26) and below 4.5% by FY27-28, consistent with the FRBM target of 3% by FY28-29. Revenue deficit target for FY25 is 2.1% of GDP. The consolidation is driven by: nominal GDP growth (projected 10.5%), tax buoyancy (GST collections crossing ₹2 lakh crore/month mark, direct tax growth ~15%), and rationalisation of subsidies. The Central government’s debt-to-GDP ratio is ~58% — below the fiscal responsibility target of 60% but above the recommended FRBM medium-term target. IMF’s Debt Sustainability Analysis for India (2024) found debt on a declining trajectory under the baseline scenario but vulnerable to growth and interest rate shocks.

2.2 Economic Survey 2023-24

Tabled in Parliament on 22 July 2024 (one day before the Budget) by Chief Economic Advisor V. Anantha Nageswaran. Key themes:

GDP Growth Projection: FY25 real GDP growth projected at 6.5-7%. RBI MPC repo rate maintained at 6.5% with CPI inflation within 4-4.5% target band.

Structural Themes of the Survey

  • Inflation: CPI inflation declined from 6.7% (2022-23) to 5.4% (2023-24). Core inflation at multi-year low — indicating demand-side moderation despite supply disruptions. Food inflation remains a concern — vegetable and pulses price spikes. RBI MPC maintained repo rate at 6.5% for 8th consecutive meeting (status quo since February 2023). Monetary policy transmission to lending rates complete — average lending rates increased by ~250 bps since rate hike cycle began
  • Current Account Deficit: Estimated at 1-1.5% of GDP, comfortable by historical standards. Services exports (particularly IT and business services) remained strong. Remittances crossed $125 billion — India is the world’s largest remittance recipient (17% of global remittances). Goods trade deficit moderated due to lower commodity prices and export diversification (engineering goods, electronics, pharma)
  • Fiscal Consolidation: Path to reduce fiscal deficit to 4.5% of GDP by FY26. The Survey emphasised quality of fiscal consolidation — shifting from revenue to capital expenditure. Capital expenditure has tripled from ₹2.9 lakh crore (FY19) to ₹11.11 lakh crore (FY25). Revenue deficit declined from 3.4% of GDP (FY21) to 2.1% (FY25)
  • Private Capex Cycle: Revival driven by corporate balance sheet repair (corporate debt-to-equity at decadal low), capacity utilisation crossing 75%, and Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes. Banks and corporates de-leveraged — bank credit growth at ~15%. Gross Fixed Capital Formation (GFCF) as % of GDP recovered from low of 26% (FY20) to 31% (FY24)
  • Employment: PLFS data shows formalisation increasing, EPFO net additions rising — 14 million net additions in FY24. Unemployment rate declined to 6.6% (2023-24) from 8.3% (2019-20). Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) improved to 57.2% (urban) — driven by rural women’s participation. However, female LFPR at ~25% remains lowest in South Asia — the Survey dedicated an entire chapter to this challenge
  • Agriculture: Sector resilient — 3.5% growth despite monsoon variability (El Niño year). Rabi crop production robust — record wheat production of 114 million tonnes. Horticulture output consistently exceeding food grains — 355 million tonnes (2023-24). Agriculture sector employs 45% of workforce but contributes only 18% to GVA — indicating low labour productivity. MSP increased for all major crops (50% above A2+FL cost recommended by CACP)
  • Manufacturing: PLI schemes boosting production in electronics, pharma, auto. 14 sectors notified under PLI — electronics manufacturing doubled to $30 billion+, mobile phone exports crossing ₹1.2 lakh crore. Manufacturing PMI in expansion zone for 3+ years. However, manufacturing share in GVA stagnant at ~17% — well below the target of 25% (National Manufacturing Policy 2011)
  • Services: Services PMI in expansion territory, digital services exports surging — IT services exports crossed $200 billion. Services contribute ~55% to GVA. India’s share in global services exports doubled from 3% (2010) to 6% (2023). Global Capability Centres (GCCs) crossed 1,600 in India — employing 1.9 million professionals with $60 billion revenue
  • Green Transition: India committed to net-zero by 2070, panchamrit targets. Renewable energy capacity crossed 190 GW (excluding large hydro). Solar tariffs declined to ₹2.50/kWh — competitive with thermal power. Green hydrogen production target of 5 MMT by 2030 under National Green Hydrogen Mission
  • Demographic Dividend: Working-age population bulge — need for skill development and job creation. Median age 28 years. However, the Survey warned that the “demographic dividend” is not automatic — it requires investment in education, skill development, and job creation. The Economic Advisory Council to the PM (EAC-PM) has noted that India’s working-age population will peak at ~69% by 2047

Inflation Analysis — RBI Perspective

The Survey devoted significant attention to inflation management. Retail inflation averaged 5.4% in FY24, down from 6.7% in FY23. Core inflation (excluding food and fuel) declined to ~3.5% — indicating stable demand conditions. However, food inflation remained volatile at 8.7% on average, driven by vegetable price spikes (onion, tomato) and pulses. The Survey noted that India’s inflation management was better than most emerging markets, attributing this to the proactive monetary policy stance, effective supply-side measures (release of buffer stocks, open market operations, import duty reductions), and Administrative Price Mechanism (APM) for petrol/diesel. The flexible inflation targeting framework (CPI target 4% ± 2%) has enhanced credibility since its adoption in 2016.

External Sector Analysis

India’s external position in FY24 was characterised by: Current Account Deficit of ~$30 billion (1% of GDP) — comfortable by historical standards; Foreign Exchange Reserves crossing $670 billion (June 2024) — covering ~11 months of imports, 2nd highest among emerging markets (after China); Net International Investment Position (NIIP) at -$130 billion; External Debt at 18.7% of GDP — one of the lowest among Emerging Market Economies; Rupee stability — average depreciation ~2% against USD in FY24 (one of the most stable EM currencies). India’s inclusion in JP Morgan GBI-EM bond index (June 2024) is expected to attract $20-30 billion in passive flows. Sovereign external debt ratings remain below investment grade (BBB- by S&P/Fitch, Baa3 by Moody’s) — citing high fiscal deficit and debt-to-GDP.

2.3 Budget Making Process

The Budget is prepared by the Budget Division, Department of Economic Affairs, Ministry of Finance. Process (September to February): pre-budget consultations with stakeholders, inputs from ministries, finalisation of receipts and expenditures. The Halwa Ceremony marks the start of the lock-in period for printing. Budget documents presented under Article 112 of the Constitution (Annual Financial Statement). The Finance Bill and Appropriation Bill must be passed by Lok Sabha before April 1.

Key Budget Terms

  • Revenue Budget: Revenue receipts + Revenue expenditure (no asset creation)
  • Capital Budget: Capital receipts + Capital expenditure (asset-creating)
  • Fiscal Deficit: Total expenditure minus total receipts (excluding borrowings) — FRBM target: 3% of GDP. The government targets 4.5% by FY26 and 3% by FY28-29
  • Revenue Deficit: Revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts — indicates dis-savings of government; target 2.1% of GDP (FY25)
  • Primary Deficit: Fiscal deficit minus interest payments — measures borrowing excluding past debt burden. Interest payments in FY24 were ₹10.6 lakh crore — 22% of revenue receipts. Primary surplus (i.e., deficit excluding interest) of ~1.6% of GDP in FY25
  • Effective Revenue Deficit: Revenue deficit minus grants for creation of capital assets — introduced by FRBM Amendment Act 2012
  • Monetised Deficit: Government borrowing from RBI (seigniorage) — effectively zero after the 1997 agreement not to issue ad-hoc treasury bills

Special Budgets

  • Outcome Budget: Tracks physical outcomes of budget allocations (since 2005). Each ministry presents measurable outcomes, output indicators, and deliverables linked to budget outlays. Replaces earlier Performance Budget
  • Gender Budget: Allocation for women-specific and pro-women schemes (~₹3.3 lakh crore in FY25). Part A (100% women-specific: ₹1.5 lakh crore) and Part B (at least 30% pro-women: ₹1.8 lakh crore). In FY25, Gender Budget allocation increased to 6.8% of total budget (from 5.6% in FY22). Criticised for covering existing schemes rather than new gender-specific initiatives
  • Green Budget: Tracks environment-related expenditure across ministries. Covers renewable energy, pollution control, afforestation, clean technology. The government launched the Green Budgeting framework in FY24 to tag all green expenditures. In FY25, green budget estimated at ~₹4.1 lakh crore (8.5% of total expenditure). Ministry of Environment, Forests and Climate Change leads the tagging exercise

FRBM Act and Fiscal Responsibility

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 mandates the government to maintain fiscal discipline. Key provisions: fiscal deficit ceiling of 3% of GDP by 2008 (amended multiple times — 3.2% in 2017, 3.5% in 2020 temporarily). Revenue deficit to be brought down to zero. The NK Singh Committee (2017) recommended: fiscal deficit target of 2.5% by FY23, 3% debt-to-GDP target for central and 20% for states combined, and escape clauses for national security, national calamity, severe economic downturn. The government invoked escape clauses during COVID-19 (fiscal deficit allowed to go to 9.2% in FY21). The 15th Finance Commission (2021-26) recommended a glide path for fiscal consolidation. The Fiscal Council (recommended by NK Singh Committee) has not been established yet. State governments are also bound by FRBM targets under the States’ Fiscal Responsibility Acts — average state fiscal deficit is ~3.1% of GSDP as of FY24.

3. Awards, Sports and Honours

3.1 Bharat Ratna — India’s Highest Civilian Award

Instituted on 2 January 1954, conferred for exceptional service/performance of the highest order in any field of human endeavour. The award comprises a peepal leaf-shaped medallion and a Sanad (certificate) signed by the President. The Prime Minister recommends names to the President. As of 2026, 53 individuals have received the award, including 18 posthumously. 2024 Recipients: LK Advani (politician), PV Narasimha Rao (former PM — posthumous), Chaudhary Charan Singh (former PM — posthumous), MS Swaminathan (agricultural scientist — posthumous), Karpoori Thakur (former Bihar CM — posthumous).

History and Evolution

The Bharat Ratna was instituted by the President of India through a Presidential Notification on 2 January 1954. The original specifications (circular gold medal, 35mm diameter) were revised in 1957 to the current design — peepal leaf shape (5¼ cm × 2.75 cm), toned bronze, with a sunburst design on the obverse and the national emblem on the reverse. The award was originally limited to “arts, literature, science, and public services.” In 2011, the criteria were expanded to include “any field of human endeavour.” There is no constitutional provision — the award is created under Article 18(1) and is not a title under Article 18 (though it may be used as a prefix/suffix by recipients). The award does not carry monetary grant — only a certificate (Sanad) and a medallion.

The first recipients (1954) were C. Rajagopalachari (last Governor-General of India), Dr Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (philosopher-President), and Dr C.V. Raman (Nobel laureate physicist). The award was temporarily suspended from 1977 to 1980 (Morarji Desai government introduced a resolution against conferment). It was revived in 1980 by Indira Gandhi. Mother Teresa (1980) was the first naturalised citizen recipient. Sachin Tendulkar (2014, aged 40) — the first sportsperson and the youngest recipient. Non-Indians who have received the award: Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan (Pakistan, 1987) and Nelson Mandela (South Africa, 1990). Notable omissions: B.R. Ambedkar (posthumous recommendation made in 1990 but not conferred).

3.2 Padma Awards 2024 & 2025

The Padma Awards are announced on Republic Day (26 January) annually. Categories: Padma Vibhushan (2nd highest), Padma Bhushan (3rd), Padma Shri (4th). Padma Awards 2024: 5 Padma Vibhushan, 17 Padma Bhushan, 110 Padma Shri — total 132 awardees. Padma Awards 2025: Announced on 25 January 2025 — 31 pairs (62+ individuals): 7 Padma Vibhushan including Venugopala R, Chiranjeevi, Suman Lata, Ratan Thiyam; 19 Padma Bhushan including eminent figures from arts, social work, public affairs; 113 Padma Shri awardees across diverse fields including unsung heroes, tribal artists, and grassroots innovators.

History of the Padma Awards

Instituted on 2 January 1954, the same day as the Bharat Ratna, the Padma awards are classified into three categories: Padma Vibhushan (“Exceptional and Distinguished Service” — highest of the three), Padma Bhushan (“Distinguished Service of a High Order”), and Padma Shri (“Distinguished Service”). The awards were suspended from 1977 to 1980 (Morarji Desai government) and then revived. The award criteria originally restricted to “any field of activity” including art, literature, education, science, sports, medicine, social work, public affairs, and civil service. The awards have faced criticism for political favouritism — particularly the high number of awards given to government officials and political figures. The Supreme Court (2015) upheld the constitutional validity of the awards, ruling they do not violate the prohibition on titles under Article 18. The total number of Padma awards in a year is capped at 120 (excluding posthumous and foreigners/NRI/PIO cases), though this was exceeded in 2024.

Notable Padma Shri 2024 Recipients

  • Unsung Heroes: Chami Murmu (90-year-old Santhal artist from Jharkhand), Radha Mohan and Sabarmatee (organic farming in Odisha), Sathyanarayana Mundayoor (book distribution in Arunachal for 40+ years)
  • Arts & Culture: Vyjayantimala Bali (Bharatanatyam), actor Chiranjeevi (Telugu cinema, conferred Padma Vibhushan 2025), M. Night Shyamalan (film director, Padma Shri 2024)
  • Environment: Tulsi Gowda (75-year-old “tree goddess” from Karnataka — planted 30,000+ trees)
  • Sports: Satpal Singh (wrestling coach), MS Dhoni (Padma Bhushan 2019), PT Usha (Padma Shri 1984 — not repeated)
  • Social Work: Jamuna Tudu (environmental activist from Jharkhand — “Lady Tarzan”)

3.3 National Sports Awards

Major Dhyan Chand Khel Ratna Award (India’s Highest Sporting Honour)

Instituted in 1991-92, formerly the Rajiv Gandhi Khel Ratna Award, renamed in August 2021 after hockey legend Major Dhyan Chand. Includes ₹25 lakh cash prize, a medallion, and certificate. 2024 Khel Ratna awardees: Manu Bhaker (shooting), D Gukesh (chess — youngest world chess champion 2024), Harmanpreet Singh (hockey), Praveen Kumar (para athletics). Total 62 recipients across 16 sports disciplines. First recipient: Viswanathan Anand (chess, 1991-92).

History and Evolution of the Award

Established in 1991-92 as the Rajiv Gandhi Khel Ratna Award by the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports. The first recipient was chess Grandmaster Viswanathan Anand (1991-92). The award was renamed “Major Dhyan Chand Khel Ratna Award” in August 2021 to honour the hockey legend who won 3 Olympic gold medals (1928, 1932, 1936). The award carries ₹25 lakh cash prize (increased from ₹7.5 lakh in 2016), a medallion, and a certificate. The criteria: consistently outstanding performance over the last 4 years at the international level. The selection committee is headed by a retired Supreme Court/HC judge with members from the sports ministry and eminent sportspersons. Only one recipient per year was the norm, but in recent years multiple athletes have been selected. The most recipients in a single year: 4 (2024). Notable recipients: Viswanathan Anand (1991-92), Sachin Tendulkar (1997-98), P.T. Usha (1999-2000 — first female), Leander Paes (1996-97), Abhinav Bindra (2006-07), Mary Kom (2009-10), M.S. Dhoni (2007-08), P.V. Sindhu (2016), Neeraj Chopra (2021).

Other National Sports Awards

  • Arjuna Award (2024): 32 sportspersons for consistent outstanding performance over 4 years. Instituted in 1961. Cash prize: ₹15 lakh (enhanced from ₹5 lakh in 2016). Notable 2024 recipients: Lakshya Sen (badminton), Chirag Shetty (badminton), Satwiksairaj Rankireddy (badminton), Mohammed Shami (cricket), Poonam Yadav (cricket). First recipient: Salil Kumar Dutta (football, 1961). The Arjuna Award is one of the oldest continuing sports awards in India — over 900 athletes have received it
  • Dronacharya Award (2024): For eminent coaches who produce medal winners. Instituted in 1985. Cash prize: ₹25 lakh (lifetime category) and ₹15 lakh (regular). Categories: Regular (active coaches) and Lifetime (retired coaches). 2024 recipients: coach of Manu Bhaker (Jaspal Rana — shooting), coach of D Gukesh (R.B. Ramesh — chess)
  • Dhyan Chand Award (2024): Lifetime achievement/contribution to sports. Instituted in 2002. Cash prize: ₹10 lakh. 2024 recipients: former hockey players, athletics coaches, wrestling officials
  • Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (MAKA) Trophy: For overall top-performing university in inter-university sports — awarded annually since 1957
  • Rashtriya Khel Protsahan Puruskar: For corporate/individual contributions to sports development

3.4 Major Sports Achievements (2022-2025)

Paris Olympics 2024 — Detailed Analysis

India won 6 medals (0 Gold, 1 Silver, 5 Bronze) — ranked 71st in medal tally. This was India’s second-best medal haul after Tokyo 2020 (7 medals). However, the 0 Gold performance was a disappointment given the pre-Games expectations of 10+ medals. Medalists: Neeraj Chopra (Silver — Javelin Throw, 2nd Olympic medal — Gold in Tokyo 2020, Silver in Paris 2024), Manu Bhaker (2 Bronze — Women’s 10m Air Pistol & Mixed Team 10m Air Pistol with Sarabjot Singh — first Indian since independence to win two medals in a single Olympics, also the first Indian woman shooter to win an Olympic medal), Swapnil Kusale (Bronze — Men’s 50m Rifle 3 Positions — first Indian male shooter to win a medal in this event), Aman Sehrawat (Bronze — Men’s Freestyle 57kg Wrestling — youngest Indian Olympic medalist at 21), Indian Men’s Hockey Team (Bronze — 2nd consecutive Olympic bronze after Tokyo 2020, beating Spain 2-1 in bronze medal match).

Performance Analysis: India’s medal tally (6 medals) was below the pre-Games target of 10-12. The government’s Target Olympic Podium Scheme (TOPS) had supported 150+ athletes at a cost of ₹1,000+ crore for Paris cycle. India’s population-to-medal ratio (240 million per medal) remains among the worst in Olympics. Weaknesses: track and field (except javelin), swimming (no medal since 2012), gymnastics (no medal ever), cycling (no medal ever), and team sports (hockey only bronze). Positive developments: first medals in shooting for women, youngest wrestling medalist, hockey continuity. India’s Olympic performance follows a consistent upward trend: 3 medals (2008 Beijing), 6 (2012 London), 1 (2016 Rio — worst in a decade), 7 (2020 Tokyo — best), 6 (2024 Paris).

Asian Games 2022 (Hangzhou, held Sep-Oct 2023)

India’s best-ever haul: 107 medals (28 Gold, 38 Silver, 41 Bronze) — 4th in medal tally. Breakthrough performances in athletics, shooting, archery, and cricket (women’s gold). Previous best was 70 medals (2018 Jakarta). Key highlights: athletics — 8 gold (including Neeraj Chopra in javelin, Murali Sreeshankar in long jump, Avinash Sable in 3000m steeplechase); shooting — 7 gold, 9 silver, 6 bronze (dominated rifle/pistol events); archery — 5 gold (compound teams); badminton — Men’s doubles gold (Satwik-Chirag), women’s singles silver (PV Sindhu); lawn bowls — 4 medals. India finished above traditional Asian powers Japan (4th with 107) behind China (383), Japan (188), and South Korea (190).

Commonwealth Games 2022 (Birmingham)

India won 61 medals (22 Gold, 16 Silver, 23 Bronze) — 4th position. Top performers: shooting excluded from CWG after 2022 — India had historically won ~30% of its gold in shooting events at CWG. Despite this, India managed 61 medals through wrestling (12 medals), boxing (7 medals), weightlifting (10 medals), badminton (3 medals), and athletics (3 medals).

PM Narendra Modi inaugurated the new Parliament building as part of the Central Vista redevelopment project. Origins of the Sengol

The Sengol (Tamil: அல்னவவ்ன், meaning sceptre) has a deeply symbolic history dating back to the Chola dynasty, where it represented the transfer of royal power. On the eve of India’s independence (August 14, 1947), Lord Mountbatten asked Pt. Jawaharlal Nehru: “What symbol will represent the transfer of power?” On C. Rajagopalachari’s advice, a Sengol was specially commissioned from the Thiruvavaduthurai Adheenam (a Shaiva monastery in Tamil Nadu, established 14th century). The Sengol is made of gold and silver, 5 feet long, with a Nandi (bull) on top symbolising justice (Dharmic rule). It was consecrated in a ceremony at the Adheenam before being brought to Delhi. The Sengol was handed to Nehru by the Adheenam seer on the night of August 14, 1947, and kept at the Nehru Memorial Museum. In May 2023, it was retrieved, re-consecrated, and installed in the new Parliament building near the Speaker’s chair — following extensive research about its historical significance by the Nehru Memorial Museum and the Ministry of Culture.

G20 New Delhi Summit (September 9-10, 2023)

India hosted the G20 Leaders’ Summit at Pragati Maidan, New Delhi. Key outcomes: New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration adopted unanimously (overcoming differences on Ukraine war). Launch of India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEEC) — a rail+sea connectivity initiative (India-UAE-Saudi Arabia-Jordan-Israel-Europe). Launch of Global Biofuels Alliance. India proposed inclusion of African Union as permanent G20 member (achieved).

Diplomatic Significance of the G20 Summit

India’s G20 Presidency (December 2022-November 2023) was a landmark in its global diplomatic outreach. The theme “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — One Earth, One Family, One Future” encapsulated India’s civilisational ethos. Over 60 meetings were held across 55+ Indian cities — showcasing India’s logistical capacity and cultural diversity to the world. The primary diplomatic challenge was the Ukraine-Russia conflict — Western nations demanded strong condemnation of Russia in the leaders’ declaration, while Russia and China opposed this. India’s phrasing — “condemned the use or threat of use of nuclear weapons,” “a just and lasting peace,” and referencing the UN Charter and territorial integrity — was carefully crafted to secure consensus from all 20 members plus the African Union. The virtual summit format (India also hosted a virtual G20 Summit on 22 November 2023 as the closing event) allowed for continuous engagement.

Key Outcomes in Detail

New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration (NDLD) — adopted by consensus with 83 paragraphs covering: (1) economic recovery — commitment to macro-financial stability, price stability, food and energy security; (2) digital public infrastructure — India’s UPI, Aadhaar, CoWIN highlighted as DPI for inclusive growth; (3) climate finance — commitment to mobilise $100 billion per year by 2020 (still unmet); (4) multilateral reform — UNSC reform, WTO reform; (5) health — pandemic preparedness, One Health approach, AMR; (6) gender equality — women-led development. India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEEC) — MoU signed by India, USA, Saudi Arabia, UAE, EU, France, Germany, Italy. The corridor includes a rail network from India to the Gulf (via sea), then through Saudi Arabia and Jordan to Israel and Europe. Also includes power cables, hydrogen pipelines, and fibre-optic cables. Seen as a strategic counter to China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). Global Biofuels Alliance (GBA) — launched by India, USA, Brazil as founding members. 25+ countries joined. Aims to promote sustainable biofuels, set global standards, facilitate technology transfer, and increase ethanol blending. Voice of Global South Summits — India hosted two Voice of Global South Summits (January and November 2023) to amplify developing country voices before G20 and COP meetings. This consolidated India’s positioning as the “voice of the Global South.”

Ram Mandir Pran Pratishtha (January 22, 2024)

PM Modi performed the consecration ceremony of the Ram Mandir in Ayodhya, Uttar Pradesh. The temple is built on the site of the demolished Babri Masjid (1992). The Supreme Court verdict (November 2019) awarded the disputed land to the Ram Janmabhoomi trust and allotted alternate land for a mosque.

Historical and Legal Background

The Ram Janmabhoomi-Babri Masjid dispute is one of the longest-running and most consequential legal-political disputes in Indian history. The Babri Masjid was constructed in 1528 by Mir Baqi, a commander of Mughal emperor Babur, on what Hindus believe was the birthplace of Lord Ram. The first recorded legal dispute dates to 1853 — communal clashes at the site. In 1859, the British administration erected a fence — Muslims prayed inside, Hindus outside. In 1949, idols of Ram Lalla appeared inside the mosque (allegedly placed by Hindu activists), leading to a lockout. The mosque was demolished on December 6, 1992, by a mob of kar sevaks — triggering nationwide riots and India’s worst communal violence since independence. The Supreme Court (5-judge bench) heard the case from 2018 to 2019 and delivered judgment on November 9, 2019 — unanimously (5-0) awarding the 2.77-acre disputed land to the Ram Janmabhoomi Trust for temple construction, while directing the central government to grant 5 acres of alternate land to the Sunni Waqf Board for a mosque. The Ram Mandir was constructed by the Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra Trust. The temple (built in Nagara style) is 380 feet long, 250 feet wide, and 161 feet high, with 392 pillars and 44 doors. The consecration ceremony was attended by 7,000+ guests, including 60+ countries’ diplomats. Opposition parties (Congress, Trinamool, AAP) boycotted the ceremony, calling it a political event ahead of the 2024 elections.

Article 370 — Supreme Court Judgment (December 2023)

A 5-judge Constitution Bench unanimously upheld the abrogation of Article 370 (August 5, 2019) which revoked the special status of Jammu & Kashmir. The Court directed that Jammu & Kashmir statehood must be restored at the earliest. Assembly elections were held in Sep-Oct 2024 (first since abrogation). The NC-led alliance under Omar Abdullah formed the government.

Detailed Background

Article 370 was a “temporary, transitional, and special” provision in Part XXI of the Constitution. It was drafted by N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar (constituent assembly) and incorporated into the Constitution in 1949 via a Presidential Order. It granted special autonomy to Jammu & Kashmir — the state had its own constitution (1957), a Sadar-e-Riyasat (head of state elected by the legislature), and Parliament’s legislative jurisdiction was limited to defence, foreign affairs, and communications (extended later via Presidential Orders). Article 35A (added through Presidential Order 1954) allowed the state legislature to define “permanent residents” and grant them special rights in public employment, property, and settlement. On August 5, 2019, the Home Minister moved a Presidential Order (CO 272) under Article 370(1) — replacing the 1954 Order — which effectively abrogated Article 370. Parliament also passed the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, bifurcating the state into two Union Territories: Jammu & Kashmir (with legislature) and Ladakh (without legislature). Over 400 petitions were filed challenging the abrogation. The Supreme Court, in a unanimous verdict (December 11, 2023), upheld the abrogation but directed restoration of statehood at the earliest. The Court held that Article 370 was intended to be a temporary provision (given the context of accession during Partition), the President’s power to issue orders under Article 370(1)(d) was not limited, and the “doctrine of basic structure” did not apply to the abrogation (since Article 370 is a “situational provision,” not a “basic feature”). The Court also upheld the reorganisation into UTs. J&K assembly elections were held in three phases (Sep 18, Sep 25, Oct 1, 2024) — voter turnout 63.88%. The NC-Congress alliance won 48/90 seats — Omar Abdullah became Chief Minister on October 16, 2024, after the state’s first assembly session post-abrogation.

CAA Implementation (March 2024)

The Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA) 2019 was notified on 11 March 2024, four years after its passage in Parliament. The rules under the Act were published, enabling persecuted non-Muslim migrants (Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists, Parsis, Christians) from Pakistan, Afghanistan, and Bangladesh who arrived in India before 31 December 2014 to apply for Indian citizenship. The Act faced massive protests in 2019-20 across India, particularly in the Northeast and Delhi (Shaheen Bagh protests). The Supreme Court is hearing multiple petitions challenging the constitutional validity of the CAA. The notification triggered the implementation of the Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024, which introduced an online application portal. Opposition parties called the notification politically timed ahead of the 2024 general elections. The Act relaxes the residence requirement for naturalisation from 11 years to 5 years for these migrants. The Home Ministry clarified that CAA does not affect the citizenship rights of any Indian citizen, regardless of religion. As of 2026, over 8,000 applications have been granted citizenship under CAA provisions.

Waqf Amendment Act 2025

In April 2025, Parliament passed the Waqf Amendment Act, significantly reforming the Waqf Board administration. Key provisions: mandatory inclusion of women and non-Muslim members in Waqf Boards, digitisation of all Waqf properties (over 6 lakh properties spanning 9.5 lakh acres), enhanced audit and transparency mechanisms, and removal of the Waqf Tribunal’s exclusive jurisdiction. The Act aims to address encroachment on Waqf properties (estimated 30% encroached) and improve revenue generation (current annual revenue of ~₹200 crore from Waqf properties, against potential of ₹12,000 crore). The opposition criticised the Act as an encroachment on religious freedom, while the government defended it as a measure of transparency. The Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) examined the bill and received 1.2 crore+ submissions — the highest for any bill in Indian parliamentary history.

One Nation One Election (ONOE)

The High-Level Committee on One Nation One Election, chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind, submitted its report in March 2024. The committee recommended synchronised elections for Lok Sabha and state assemblies in two phases: first elections (Lok Sabha and all assemblies together) followed by local body elections within 100 days. The committee suggested 18 constitutional amendments (mostly requiring state ratification) to implement the proposal, including amendments to Articles 83, 85, 172, 174, and 356. Estimated cost: ~₹10,000 crore once-off but saving ~₹5,000 crore per cycle compared to the current staggered schedule (2019-24 cycle cost ~₹1.5 lakh crore across all elections). The government introduced two bills in the 2025 Winter Session to implement ONOE, referred to JPC for wider consultation. Supporters argue reduced election expenditure and focused governance; critics cite federalism concerns and logistical challenges.

Uniform Civil Code (UCC) — Uttarakhand Leads

Uttarakhand became the first state in independent India to implement a UCC, with the Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code Act, 2024 receiving Presidential assent on 12 March 2024. It applies to all residents except tribals (customary law protections under Article 371F). Key provisions: minimum marriage age 18 for women and 21 for men (uniform across religions), ban on polygamy, compulsory registration of marriages and divorces, equal inheritance rights for sons and daughters, and simplified adoption procedures. The Act came into effect in 2025. Article 44 of the Constitution (Directive Principle) mandates the state to endeavour to secure a UCC. Gujarat, Assam, and Himachal Pradesh have announced intentions to introduce similar legislation.

New Criminal Laws (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita)

Three new criminal codes came into effect on 1 July 2024, replacing the colonial-era laws: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaced IPC (1860), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) replaced CrPC (1973), and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) replaced Indian Evidence Act (1872). Key changes: sedition (Section 124A IPC) replaced by treason (Section 152 BNS) with stricter provisions; organised crime and terrorism defined for the first time in ordinary law; death penalty expanded to include mob lynching and rape of minors under 12; zero FIR and electronic FIR mandatory; videography of crime scenes mandatory; timelines for investigation (60-90 days) and trial (1-2 years) prescribed; community service introduced as a punishment for petty offences; trials in absentia permitted for organised crime. The new laws emphasise victim-centric justice, use of technology (digital evidence, videoconferencing for witnesses), and speedy trials. Criticism: concerns about police brutality, lack of judicial infrastructure, and potential misuse of expanded police powers. The laws were drafted by the Law Commission and the Home Ministry after extensive consultation but opposition parties criticised the lack of parliamentary committee scrutiny.

5. International Relations

5.1 India-United States Relations

India-US relations have reached their most comprehensive and strategic level under the framework of a Global Comprehensive Strategic Partnership. The relationship spans defence, technology, trade, people-to-people ties, and multilateral coordination. The Indian diaspora in the US has grown to 4.8 million — the second-largest immigrant group. Bilateral trade crossed $190 billion in 2023-24. Key defence agreements: LEMOA (2016, logistics exchange), COMCASA (2018, communications security), BECA (2020, geospatial intelligence). The US has become India’s largest defence supplier with cumulative defence deals exceeding $25 billion.

Key Developments

  • iCET (Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies): Launched in January 2023, co-led by NSA Ajit Doval and US NSA Jake Sullivan. Focus areas: AI, quantum computing, semiconductors, biotechnology, space collaboration, 5G/6G telecom. Under iCET, GE Aerospace signed a landmark MoU with Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd (HAL) to co-produce GE F414-INS6 jet engines (80% transfer of technology) — the first such jet engine technology transfer to India. The engines will power the Tejas Mk2 and AMCA (Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft). The deal is valued at $1.06 billion and will create a dedicated production facility in India
  • Defence Cooperation: Major US-origin platforms in Indian service: C-17 Globemaster (11), C-130J Hercules (12), P-8I Poseidon (12, maritime patrol), Apache helicopters (22), Chinook helicopters (15), MQ-9B Predator drones ($3.99 billion deal for 31 drones, 2024). Joint exercises: Yudh Abhyas (army), Malabar (naval, with Japan and Australia), Vajra Prahar (special forces), Cope India (air force). The US designated India as a “Major Defence Partner” in 2016 — the highest level of defence partnership short of a treaty ally
  • Trade and Investment: Bilateral goods and services trade ~$190 billion. US is India’s largest export destination. Over 200 US Fortune 500 companies have operations in India. The US-India CEO Forum meets regularly. Key trade issues: US concerns about Indian tariff barriers, intellectual property protection, data localisation; Indian concerns about H-1B visa restrictions, Section 232 tariffs on steel/aluminium. Trade Policy Forum (TPF) working on a limited trade deal. India restored GSP beneficiary status demand active. FDI from US into India accumulated ~$60 billion (8% of total FDI equity inflow)
  • Technology and Semiconductors: Under iCET, Applied Materials opened a $400 million semiconductor commercialisation centre in Bengaluru. Micron Technology announced $2.75 billion investment for a semiconductor assembly and test facility in Gujarat (total project cost $825 million with government support). Google and Amazon have committed billions in cloud infrastructure and AI research in India. The US-India Quantum Coordination Mechanism launched
  • People-to-People Ties: Indian students in US crossed 460,000 in 2025 — largest foreign student group. STEM Optional Practical Training (OPT) extension continues to benefit Indian graduates. The US opened new consulates in Bengaluru and Ahmedabad. India opened new consulates in Seattle and Atlanta

5.2 India-Russia Relations

India-Russia relations are built on a Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership dating back to the Cold War era. Despite Western pressure post-Ukraine invasion (2022), India has maintained its strategic autonomy — continuing defence purchases, oil imports, and diplomatic engagement.

Key Developments

  • Defence Cooperation: Russia remains India’s largest defence supplier historically (~60% of defence equipment is Russian-origin). Key systems: S-400 Triumf air defence system (5 regiments delivered, deliveries delayed due to Ukraine war), T-90S Bhishma tanks, Su-30MKI fighter jets (licensed production at HAL), Kamov helicopters, AK-203 assault rifles (joint production at Korwa, UP under the India-Russia Rifles Private Limited). INS Tushil — first of the upgraded Talwar-class frigates — commissioned in December 2024
  • Oil Imports: India became the largest buyer of Russian crude oil after China. Russian oil share in India’s crude imports rose from less than 2% (pre-2022) to ~35% in 2024. Discounted oil saved India an estimated $8-10 billion annually. Payments are in non-dollar currencies — rupee-rouble trade mechanism, dirham, yuan. This has significantly altered global oil trade flows
  • Trade and Investment: Bilateral trade ~$65 billion (2024), heavily skewed by oil imports. Target of $100 billion by 2030. Non-oil trade stagnant at ~$10 billion. Key sectors: fertilisers (Russia is largest supplier to India), diamonds (ALROSA), nuclear energy (Kudankulam, TN — 6 reactors planned, 2 operational). The India-Russia Inter-Governmental Commission (IRIGC) is the principal dialogue mechanism. Issues: trade imbalance, payment difficulties due to sanctions, logistics (INSTC — International North-South Transport Corridor)
  • Multilateral Cooperation: Russia is a key partner in SCO, BRICS, RIC (Russia-India-China trilateral). India abstained from UN votes condemning Russia’s annexation of Ukrainian territories. The Russia-Ukraine war has tested India’s strategic autonomy — India has repeatedly called for “dialogue and diplomacy” and respect for UN Charter without directly condemning Russia. PM Modi told President Putin in Astana (July 2024) that “war cannot solve problems” and “children are dying” in Ukraine — his most direct criticism
  • Eastern Military District and Vladivostok: India-Russia Annual Bilateral Summits were not held in 2022, 2023, and 2024 (the first breaks in the 20+ year tradition) due to the Ukraine war. PM Modi visited Russia in July 2024 for the 22nd Annual Summit, where he also visited the Rosatom pavilion. India-Russia joint military exercise INDRA continued annually

5.3 India-China Relations

India-China relations remain at their lowest point since the 1962 war, following the Galwan Valley clash (June 2020) in which 20 Indian soldiers were killed. The border standoff in Eastern Ladakh continues with no disengagement in most friction points (Depsang, Demchok, Hot Springs, Gogra). Relations are governed by the three mutual agreements: Panchsheel (1954), Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity (1993), and Agreement on Confidence-Building Measures (1996, 2005).

Key Developments

  • Border Standoff (2020-2026): After Galwan (June 2020), India and China held over 30 rounds of Corps Commander-level talks. Disengagement completed at Patrolling Point 15 (Gogra) and PP17A (Hot Springs) in 2022. Friction points at Depsang Plains and Demchok remain unresolved. China continues to build infrastructure (roads, barracks, helipads) on its side of the LAC. India has responded with enhanced infrastructure development (border roads, tunnels like Sela, Nechiphu, Daporijo) and increased troop deployment (~1,00,000 troops in Eastern Ladakh). India has operationalised the new integrated theatre commands (notably the recently approved theatre commands for maritime and air domains). India has significantly upgraded airfields in Ladakh (Nyoma, Daulat Beg Oldie, Fukche) and deployed Rafale, Su-30MKI, and Apache helicopters
  • Trade and Economic Decoupling: Bilateral trade peaked at $136 billion (2022) before declining to $118 billion (2024). India’s trade deficit with China is ~$85 billion — India’s largest bilateral trade deficit. Major Indian imports: electronics (mobile phones, telecom equipment, laptops), machinery, active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), fertilisers, steel, coal. India has taken several measures: PLI schemes to reduce import dependence, Quality Control Orders (QCOs) restricting Chinese imports, anti-dumping duties on Chinese steel and chemicals, stricter FDI norms from land-bordering countries (2020). India blocked 230+ Chinese mobile apps (TikTok, UC Browser, Shein, PUBG) under Section 69A of IT Act
  • Diplomatic Engagement: Bilateral meetings at BRICS, SCO, G20 continue but no annual summits since 2019. Foreign Minister S. Jaishankar and Chinese counterpart Wang Yi met on the sidelines of multilateral meetings. PM Modi and Xi Jinping have not held a bilateral summit since the Mamallapuram informal summit (October 2019). India insists on full disengagement and de-escalation as precondition for normalisation of ties. China continues to raise concerns about India’s sheltering of Tibetan leaders (Dalai Lama) and the so-called “Arunachal Pradesh dispute” (China claims 90,000 sq km in Arunachal Pradesh as “South Tibet”). India responded by refusing China’s proposal to revive the Special Representatives dialogue on the boundary
  • Competition in the Neighbourhood: China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), especially the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) passing through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir, is a major irritant. China has increased its presence in the Indian Ocean region (string of pearls — Gwadar, Hambantota, Djibouti, Sittwe). India has countered with infrastructure development in friendly countries (Iran’s Chabahar port, Maldives’ development projects, Sri Lanka’s Colombo terminal, Nepal hydro projects), and through Quad (with US, Japan, Australia)
  • PLA and Indian Army Comparison: China has ~2 million active troops (largest army in the world) vs India’s 1.4 million. China’s defence budget (~$230 billion) is nearly 3 times India’s (~$75 billion). China has 250+ nuclear warheads vs India’s estimated 160. India has focused on niche capabilities: Rafale and S-400 for air superiority, BrahMos for precision strikes, naval modernisation (INS Vikrant aircraft carrier, nuclear submarines INS Arihant, INS Arighat), and cyber warfare capabilities

5.4 India-Pakistan Relations

India-Pakistan relations remain frozen after India’s abrogation of Article 370 (August 2019) and Pakistan’s downgrading of diplomatic relations. No high-level dialogue since 2015 (NSA talks cancelled). Cross-border terrorism continues, though the number of infiltration attempts has declined due to India’s active counter-infiltration grid and the improved security situation in Jammu region. The Indus Water Commission continues to function as a technical body under the 1960 Indus Water Treaty (brokered by World Bank). Pakistan has not complied with its obligation under the treaty to construct the Kishanganga and Ratle hydroelectric projects as per the neutral expert’s decision. In 2024, India issued a notice to Pakistan seeking modification of the treaty, citing changes in demographics, environmental requirements, and the need for renewable energy. The UN Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan (UNMOGIP) continues to monitor the ceasefire along the Line of Control (LoC). The 2003 ceasefire understanding has largely held since February 2021 when both armies recommitted to it. Trade between the two countries is negligible (~$500 million). Pakistan has not granted Most Favoured Nation (MFN) status to India. India has revoked Pakistan’s MFN status after the Pulwama attack (2019). The Kartarpur Corridor (opened 2019) remains the only functional people-to-people link, allowing Indian Sikh pilgrims to visit Gurdwara Darbar Sahib in Pakistan visa-free.

5.5 India-Neighbourhood Relations

India-Nepal Relations

Nepal is a priority partner under India’s “Neighbourhood First” policy. The relationship was strained by the Kalapani-Lipulekh border dispute (2020) and Nepal’s new political map depicting Indian territory. Bilateral ties improved after PM Deuba’s visit to India (2022) and PM Pushpa Kamal Dahal “Prachanda’s” visits (2023, 2024). India is Nepal’s largest trade partner (bilateral trade ~$10 billion) and largest development partner with ~$5 billion committed in infrastructure (B P Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, cross-border rail links, integrated check posts, Arun-3 hydroelectric project (900 MW), Upper Karnali, West Seti). India completed the 145-km Motihari-Amlekhgunj petroleum pipeline (first cross-border pipeline in South Asia). India also completed the 90 MW Arun-3 project's transmission line. The Nepal-India Boundary Working Group (BWG) continues to discuss outstanding border issues. 2025-26: PM Prachanda visited India and signed several MoUs on energy cooperation, water resources, and cultural exchange. Nepal’s political instability (frequent changes of government) remains a concern. India-Tibet border issues involving Nepal (Lipulekh, Kalapani) remain under diplomatic discussion.

India-Bhutan Relations

Bhutan remains India’s closest neighbour under the “special relationship” framework of the 1949 Treaty (revised in 2007). The India-Bhutan Friendship Treaty (2007) does not require Bhutan to seek India’s guidance on foreign policy. India is Bhutan’s largest development partner (~$5 billion in Five-Year Plans). Hydropower cooperation is the cornerstone: four operational projects (Chukha 336 MW, Kurichhu 60 MW, Tala 1020 MW, Mangdechhu 720 MW) supply electricity to India. The 1020 MW Punatsangchhu-I and 600 MW Punatsangchhu-II projects are under construction with delays. Under the 118th Constitution Amendment, Bhutan’s monarchy transferred full executive powers to the elected government post-2023 elections. PM Tshering Tobgay (People’s Democratic Party) visited India in 2024 as his first foreign trip and committed to further deepen ties. The India-Bhutan border is peaceful and demarcated. Joint mechanisms: Bhutan-India Interstate Border Committee (IBC), India-Bhutan Foundation for cultural exchanges, and the annual Foreign Office Consultations.

India-Bangladesh Relations

India-Bangladesh relations are at a historic high under PM Sheikh Hasina (in power since 2009). The relationship is anchored in the “Shonali Adhyay” (Golden Chapter) vision. The Land Boundary Agreement (LBA, 2015) resolved 68-year-old border enclave issues — India exchanged 111 enclaves (17,160 acres) with Bangladesh. The Ganga Waters Treaty (1996) shares dry-season water at Farakka. Bilateral trade crossed $18 billion (2024). India is Bangladesh’s largest trading partner in Asia. Key connectivity initiatives: Akhaura-Agartala rail link (operational 2023), Maitree Express (Kolkata-Dhaka), Mitali Express (New Jalpaiguri-Dhaka), Dhaka-Guwahati bus service, Banglabandha integrated check post. Energy cooperation: India supplies 1,600 MW electricity to Bangladesh through cross-border transmission lines. The Maitree Super Thermal Power Project (1320 MW) in Rampal is a joint venture. Border management: the India-Bangladesh border (~4,096 km) is one of the most complex in the world. The Border Haat programme has 24 operational border markets. The India-Bangladesh Joint Boundary Working Group continues border demarcation. Bangladesh granted India transit rights to the Northeast (Chittagong, Mongla ports) under the 2015 Protocol on Inland Water Transit and Trade. The Teesta water sharing agreement remains pending due to West Bengal’s objections. The relationship faced challenges in 2024-25 related to Bangladesh’s national election legitimacy concerns and the killing of Bangladeshi nationals at the border by BSF. Nevertheless, in 2025, Sheikh Hasina again won her 5th consecutive term and further consolidated the partnership with India, signing the comprehensive bilateral trade agreement.

India-Myanmar Relations

India-Myanmar relations have been severely strained since the military coup of February 2021 (removing Aung San Suu Kyi’s NLD government). India has adopted a cautious approach: it condemned the use of violence but continued engagement with the junta (SAC — State Administration Council) for strategic reasons. The border with Myanmar (1,643 km across four states — Arunachal, Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram) is porous and affected by insurgency. In 2024, the government announced the scrapping of the Free Movement Regime (FMR) along the border and initiated fencing of the entire border (ongoing). The Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project (India to Mizoram via Sittwe port in Myanmar) is near completion. The India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway (1,360 km) connecting Moreh (India) to Mae Sot (Thailand) via Myanmar is delayed due to the internal conflict in Myanmar. India has provided humanitarian assistance (cyclone relief) and announced development projects (~$2 billion line of credit). However, the junta’s loss of territorial control to ethnic armed organisations (EAOs) and the pro-democracy People’s Defence Forces (PDFs) has complicated India’s engagement. India evacuated its citizens from conflict zones in 2024. The border has seen increased influx of refugees into Mizoram and Manipur (estimated 50,000+). India maintains a policy of non-interference while advocating for the return of democratic processes. China’s growing influence in Myanmar (BRI projects, military cooperation) is a countervailing factor for India’s strategic calculus.

India-Maldives Relations

India-Maldives relations suffered a downturn under President Mohamed Muizzu (elected November 2023), who campaigned on an “India Out” platform. Muizzu demanded the removal of Indian military personnel operating 2 helicopters and 1 aircraft (given for humanitarian missions). India withdrew all military personnel by May 2024, replacing them with civilian technicians. The “India Out” campaign subsided after Maldives faced severe economic challenges (foreign exchange reserves depleting, debt servicing issues, tourism slowdown). China granted $75 million budget support to Maldives. However, by early 2025, Muizzu recalibrated his foreign policy: he visited India for the swearing-in, participated in the Voice of Global South Summit, and India agreed to restructure Maldives’ debt ($50 million loan repayments deferred). India remains Maldives’ largest development partner (cumulative assistance ~$2.5 billion). The Greater Malé Connectivity Project (bridge connecting Malé, Villingili, Gulhifalhu, and Thilafushi) is India’s flagship project in Maldives, funded by ₹400 crore grant. India also extended a $100 million budget support line and a $400 million currency swap line. In 2025, the diplomatic relationship stabilised with both countries focusing on economic cooperation, counter-terrorism, and maritime security under the Colombo Security Conclave and IONS (Indian Ocean Naval Symposium).

India-Sri Lanka Relations

India-Sri Lanka relations have strengthened significantly following Sri Lanka’s economic crisis (2022). India provided unprecedented assistance of ~$4 billion (including $1 billion credit line for essential imports, $400 million currency swap, $500 million for fuel imports, and $50 million for fertiliser). India was the first country to extend assistance under its “Neighbourhood First” policy. President Ranil Wickremesinghe (elected 2022 by Parliament) visited India frequently. Key projects: India-assisted housing project (14,000 houses for war-affected Tamils completed), Jaffna cultural centre (Kochchikade), solar power projects in the Northern Province, and the Sampur power project. The fishermen issue — Indian fishermen crossing the International Maritime Boundary Line (IMBL) and being arrested by Sri Lankan Navy — remains a recurring bilateral irritant (over 200 arrests annually). India has pressed for a political solution to the Tamil question including implementation of the 13th Amendment (provincial autonomy) and full devolution of powers. The 13th Amendment was part of the India-Sri Lanka Accord (1987). In 2024, Sri Lanka held presidential elections (first post-crisis) and President Anura Kumara Dissanayake was elected. He visited India soon after and assured full implementation of bilateral agreements. Sri Lanka’s debt restructuring was completed in late 2024 with India playing a supportive role in the Paris Club and IMF processes.

India-Afghanistan Relations

India does not recognise the Taliban regime that returned to power in August 2021. India closed its embassy in Kabul and operates through a “Technical Team” based in the Indian mission. India has provided humanitarian assistance to Afghanistan: 50,000 MT of wheat, 500 tonnes of medicines, COVID vaccines, 60,000 units of school supplies. India also provided 20,000 litres of pesticides and 3,000 MT of pulses under bilateral trade. India has consistently raised concerns about terrorism emanating from Afghan soil (Lashkar-e-Taiba, Jaish-e-Mohammed) and the protection of minority rights (Sikhs and Hindus). India has not opened diplomatic relations with the Taliban and maintains a policy of waiting for an inclusive and representative government in Kabul. India participated in the “Moscow Format” talks (2023) and Regional Security Dialogue (Dushanbe format) to coordinate on Afghanistan issues. The Taliban has asked India to resume development projects (Salma Dam, Shahtoot Dam, Afghan-India Friendship Dam, Parliament building, Zaranj-Delaram highway) but India has not agreed without formal recognition. India evacuated 168 people from Kabul in August 2021 (Operation Devi Shakti). The UNSC Resolution 2593 (2021) on Afghanistan, which India sponsored, demands that Afghan territory should not be used for terrorism. As of 2026, no country has formally recognised the Taliban regime.

5.6 Major Multilateral Engagements

G20

India held the G20 Presidency from December 2022 to November 2023, organising over 60 meetings across 55+ Indian cities. Key success: unanimous adoption of the New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration (September 2023) with 83 paragraphs covering economic recovery, digital public infrastructure, climate finance, multilateral reform, health, and gender equality. India proposed and secured the inclusion of the African Union as a permanent G20 member — the first expansion since the G20’s inception in 1999. India hosted a virtual G20 Summit in November 2023 as the closing event. The theme “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — One Earth, One Family, One Future” reflected India’s civilisational ethos. Key initiatives launched: India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEEC), Global Biofuels Alliance. The India-mooted “Voice of Global South” Summits (January and November 2023) amplified developing country perspectives. The G20 troika (past, present, future presidency) included India, Indonesia (2022), and Brazil (2024). Brazil hosted G20 in 2024 under the theme “Building a Just World and a Sustainable Planet.” Key outcomes in Rio de Janeiro (November 2024): Global Alliance Against Hunger and Poverty launched, Tax cooperation on ultra-high-net-worth individuals (2% minimum tax proposal), expanded climate finance commitments, and continued efforts to reform international financial institutions. South Africa will host G20 in 2025.

BRICS

BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) expanded in August 2023 to BRICS+ with the inclusion of Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE (effective January 2024). Argentina was invited but declined after the change of government. The 16th BRICS Summit was held in Kazan, Russia (October 2024). Key outcomes: emphasis on de-dollarisation and use of local currencies in trade, BRICS New Development Bank (NDB) expansion, creation of a BRICS Grain Exchange, BRICS Space Alliance, and common position on UN reforms. India has supported BRICS expansion cautiously, ensuring it does not become a China-dominated forum. The BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA, $100 billion) provides currency swap lines for balance of payments crises. The NDB has approved ~$35 billion in infrastructure projects across members. India emphasised the need for BRICS to address terrorism, climate finance, and SDG financing. The BRICS+ expansion brings together 11 countries representing ~46% of world population and ~37% of global GDP (PPP). Divergences within BRICS: India-China border tensions, Saudi-Iran rivalry, and Egypt-Ethiopia disagreements over the Renaissance Dam. Russia’s chairmanship in 2024 was overshadowed by the Ukraine war and Western sanctions.

SCO (Shanghai Cooperation Organisation)

India is a full member of SCO since 2017. The 24th SCO Summit was held in Astana, Kazakhstan (July 2024). Key outcomes: adoption of the Astana Declaration on unity and regional security, consensus on counter-terrorism framework, expansion of SCO (Belarus admitted as 10th full member). India emphasised the need for SCO to address terrorism (state-sponsored, cross-border), radicalisation, drug trafficking, and infrastructure connectivity. India advocated for the creation of an SCO Development Bank and the SCO Joint Credit Rating Agency. India’s position on the SCO has been complicated by Pakistan’s membership and China’s dominance in the organisation. India boycotted the SCO defence ministers’ meeting in Pakistan (2024) as a protest against cross-border terrorism. India has actively participated in SCO RATS (Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure) exercises and joint military exercises (Peace Mission). The SCO, originally formed as the Shanghai Five (1996) with China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and expanded to include Uzbekistan (2001), is seen as a counterweight to Western-dominated multilateral institutions.

Quad (Quadrilateral Security Dialogue)

The Quad (India, US, Japan, Australia) has evolved from a 2004 tsunami-response coordination group to a core strategic partnership for a “Free and Open Indo-Pacific” (FOIP). The Quad Leaders’ Summit in Tokyo (May 2022), Hiroshima (May 2023), Wilmington, Delaware (September 2024), and virtual meetings continued. Key initiatives: Quad Fellowship (100 STEM students annually), Quad Cancer Moonshot (HPV vaccine access in Indo-Pacific), Quad Infrastructure Fellows Programme, Quad Maritime Domain Awareness initiative (satellite data for Indo-Pacific maritime surveillance), Quad Ports and Logistics Network, and Quad Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief (HADR) coordination. The Quad is not a military alliance but conducts joint naval exercises (Exercise Malabar) and has enhanced maritime domain awareness. In 2025, the Quad expanded its focus to include critical technologies (5G, AI, quantum computing, semiconductors under the Quad Critical and Emerging Technology Working Group). The Quad+ format with South Korea, New Zealand, Vietnam (Quad Plus) has been explored. India sees Quad as complementing ASEAN centrality and not as a containment mechanism against China. China has criticised Quad as “mini-NATO.” In 2024, the Quad leaders announced the Maritime Domain Awareness partnership to track illegal fishing, drug trafficking, and piracy. The Quad Vaccine Partnership delivered 80 million doses of COVID-19 vaccines across the Indo-Pacific.

I2U2 (India-Israel-UAE-USA)

I2U2 was formalised in July 2022 as a quadrilateral economic grouping. Focus areas: water, energy, transportation, space, health, and food security. Key projects: a $2 billion integrated food park in Gujarat (collaboration between UAE and Israel, US technical support). I2U2 complements the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEEC) announced at G20 2023. The grouping leverages Israel’s agri-tech and water-tech, UAE’s capital and logistics, US technology and market access, and India’s scale and manufacturing. In 2024, I2U2 expanded its scope to include climate tech, fintech, and space collaboration. The grouping is seen as a response to China’s BRI and a vehicle for regional integration.

UNSC Reform and India’s Podium

India has intensified its demand for UN Security Council reform to reflect contemporary geopolitical realities. The G4 (India, Japan, Germany, Brazil) continues to push for expansion in both permanent and non-permanent categories. India has secured support from the US, UK (both supporting permanent membership for India with veto option), and France. Russia supports India’s permanent membership but opposes expansion of veto. China remains non-committal on India’s bid. The Inter-Governmental Negotiations (IGN) at UNGA have produced the Common African Position (Ezulwini Consensus) supporting expansion. India’s 8th term as a non-permanent UNSC member (2021-22) was marked by its presidency of the Council (August 2021, December 2022). India continues to advocate for a text-based negotiation framework for UNSC reform at the UNGA. The 2024 UNGA session saw 120+ countries supporting India’s candidature in principle. The Summit of the Future (September 2024) in New York adopted the Pact for the Future which reaffirmed the need for UNSC reform but did not set a timeline. India also pushed for reform of the Bretton Woods institutions (IMF and World Bank) and the WTO.

Key outcomes: iCET progress review, GE F414 engine deal implementation, semiconductor partnership advances, and defence cooperation MoU on MQ-9B drone acquisition
  • PM Modi’s Russia Visit (July 2024): 22nd India-Russia Annual Summit in Moscow. PM Modi awarded Russia’s highest state decoration (Order of St Andrew). Focus: defence supplies, oil trade, nuclear energy, and consular issues of Indians in Russian army
  • PM Modi’s France Visit (2024): Key outcomes: defence industrial roadmap, Scorpene submarine construction for Indian Navy, civil nuclear cooperation (Jaitapur EPR reactors), and space collaboration (Indian astronauts training at CNES). India-France strategic partnership completed 25 years in 2023
  • PM Modi’s Japan Visit (2024): Discussed India-Japan Act East Forum, India-Japan Industrial Competitiveness Partnership, Mumbai-Ahmedabad High Speed Rail (Shinkansen technology), and defence equipment cooperation (UNICORN counter-drone system).

UAE committed $75 billion for India’s infrastructure. The RuPay card was launched in UAE. The Indian diaspora in UAE (~3.5 million) is the largest expatriate community
  • President Droupadi Murmu’s State Visits: Visited Indonesia, Timor-Leste (2024), South Korea (2025), and Ghana (2025). These visits focused on deepening India’s ties with Southeast Asia and Africa (Global South outreach)
  • President of India’s address at COP29 (Baku, November 2024): India emphasised equity and climate justice, LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment) mission, and the gap in climate finance commitments

5.8 India and the Indian Diaspora

The Indian diaspora (estimated 36 million) is the largest in the world. Major concentrations: USA (4.8 million), UAE (3.5 million), Saudi Arabia (2.6 million), Malaysia (2.0 million), UK (1.8 million), Canada (1.6 million), Australia (975,000), South Africa (1.5 million). Remittances received by India crossed $125 billion in 2024 — the highest in the world (17% of global remittances). The government conducts annual Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (PBD, 9th January) conventions — the 18th PBD was held in Bhubaneswar (2025) with the theme “Diaspora’s Contribution to a Viksit Bharat.” The government introduced the Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) card in 2005 (replacing PIO card), and the Know India Programme (KIP) for diaspora youth. In 2024, the government announced OCI lifetime visa and parity with NRIs in most economic spheres. The e-Migrate system for emigration clearance was revamped. The evacuation of Indian citizens from Sudan (Operation Kaveri, 2023, evacuating 4,000+), Israel (2023), and Ukraine (2022) demonstrated India’s rapid-response diplomatic capability. In 2024-25, India evacuated 800+ citizens from conflict zones in Myanmar and 300+ from Lebanon. The MEA launched the MADAD portal for consular grievances. India signed Migration and Mobility Partnership Agreements (MMPA) with Italy, Germany, Israel, and Australia. The India-UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) includes labour mobility provisions.

6. Science, Technology and Environment

6.1 ISRO Missions and Space Achievements

  • Chandrayaan-3 (August 2023): India became the 4th country to soft-land on the Moon and the 1st to land near the lunar south pole (69.37°S). The mission launched on 14 July 2023 from SDSC-SHAR, Sriharikota, onboard LVM3-M4. The Vikram lander and Pragyan rover landed on 23 August 2023. The rover operated for 1 lunar day (14 Earth days), covering 103 metres, and performed in-situ experiments: APXS (Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer) and LIBS (Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscope) confirmed the presence of sulphur, aluminium, calcium, iron, titanium, manganese, oxygen, and silicon on the lunar surface. The lander carried ChaSTE (Chandra’s Surface Thermophysical Experiment), ILSA (Instrument for Lunar Seismic Activity), and Laser Retroreflector Array (NASA). This was India’s second lunar landing attempt after Chandrayaan-2’s lander crash in September 2019
  • Aditya-L1 (September 2023): India’s first solar mission, launched on 2 September 2023 onboard PSLV-C57. The spacecraft reached the Sun-Earth Lagrange Point L1 (1.5 million km from Earth) on 6 January 2024. Seven payloads study the solar corona, chromosphere, photosphere, and solar wind. Key instruments: VELC (Visible Emission Line Coronagraph — for corona imaging), SUIT (Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope), ASPEX (Aditya Solar Wind Particle Experiment), PAPA (Plasma Analyser Package for Aditya), SoLEXS (Solar Low Energy X-ray Spectrometer), HEL1OS (High Energy L1 Orbiting X-ray Spectrometer), and Magnetometer. Initial results: detection of solar flares and their impact on space weather, imaging of the solar corona below 1.05 solar radii. Aditya-L1 is expected to operate for 5+ years
  • Gaganyaan Programme: India’s first human spaceflight mission. TV-D1 (Test Vehicle Development Flight 1) was successfully conducted on 28 October 2023, demonstrating crew escape system performance at Mach 1.2. The TV-D2 test was conducted in 2024. The uncrewed orbital test (G1) is expected in 2026 (crew module with Vyommitra, a humanoid robot). The crewed mission (H1) will send 3 Indian astronauts (Group Captain Prashanth Nair, Group Captain Ajit Krishnan, Group Captain Angad Pratap, and Wing Commander Shubhanshu Shukla — trained at Yuri Gagarin Cosmonaut Training Centre, Russia) to 400 km orbit for 3-7 days. India aims to start its own space station (Bharatiya Antariksha Station) by 2035 and a crewed lunar mission by 2040
  • XPoSat (January 2024): India’s first polarimetry mission (X-ray Polarimeter Satellite) launched on 1 January 2024 to study X-ray emissions from celestial sources (black holes, neutron stars, pulsars). It carries the POLIX (Indian) and XSPECT payloads
  • SSLV (Small Satellite Launch Vehicle): After the failed maiden flight (August 2022), the SSLV-D2 and SSLV-D3 were successfully launched in 2023 and 2024, demonstrating India’s capability for on-demand, low-cost (₹35 crore) satellite launch. The SSLV was transferred to private industry (consortium led by Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd and L&T) for commercial production. India also launched 50+ foreign satellites under NewSpace India Ltd (NSIL, established 2019, commercial arm of ISRO)
  • NAVIC: India’s regional satellite navigation system (IRNSS) with 7 operational satellites. The L1 band signal for civilian use was approved by the US FCC in 2024. India is planning NVS-02 and NVS-03 satellites to augment NAVIC. Work towards making NAVIC interoperable with GPS, Glonass, and Galileo is ongoing
  • SpaDeX (Space Docking Experiment, January 2025): ISRO demonstrated autonomous space docking capability (two small satellites docked in orbit) — a critical technology for future space station assembly, crew transfer, and satellite servicing. India became the 4th country to achieve in-orbit docking

6.2 Defence Technology and DRDO

  • Tejas Mk1A: Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd (HAL) received a ₹48,000 crore order for 83 Tejas Mk1A fighter jets for the Indian Air Force (IAF). The Mk1A features an AESA radar (ELTA-2052), advanced electronic warfare suite (DRDO’s DARE), Beyond Visual Range (BVR) missile integration (Derby, I-Derby ER), and mid-air refuelling capability. First deliveries expected 2026. The Tejas programme has achieved Final Operational Clearance (FOC) and completed 5,000+ sorties since induction. The Tejas Mk2 (27% larger, GE F414 engine) is under development with first flight expected 2027. The AMCA (Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft), a 5th-generation stealth fighter, is under design phase with DRDO’s Aeronautical Development Agency (ADA). The TEDBF (Twin Engine Deck-Based Fighter) for the Indian Navy is under development
  • BrahMos Missile: India-Russia joint venture (BrahMos Aerospace). BrahMos supersonic cruise missile (Mach 2.8) has been inducted by all three services. The BrahMos-NG (New Generation) variant, lighter and smaller (1.5 tonne vs 3 tonne), is under testing for aircraft, submarine, and surface launches. The BrahMos-ER (Extended Range, 800 km) variant is under development, critical for the IAF’s Su-30MKI fleet. Export orders from Philippines (₹18,900 crore for 3 batteries) were delivered starting 2024. Other interested countries: Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, Egypt
  • Akash and QRSAM: Akash surface-to-air missile (25 km range) is the indigenous air defence system for the IAF. The Akash-NG (New Generation, 30 km range) is under final testing. QRSAM (Quick Reaction Surface-to-Air Missile, 15-30 km range) was successfully tested in 2024 for mobile air defence. India also test-fired the VL-SRSAM (Vertical Launch Short-Range SAM) for naval use and extended-range MRSAM (Barak-8 derivatives) for both IAF and Navy
  • Nuclear Triad and Ballistic Missiles: INS Arihant (SSBN, 6,000 tonnes, 4 Silos) completed India’s nuclear triad in 2018. INS Arighat (2nd Arihant-class) was commissioned in 2024, fitted with K-4 missiles (3,500 km range). The 3rd SSBN (INS Aridhaman) is under sea trials. India also operates the Agni series of ICBMs: Agni-5 (5,000-8,000 km, MIRV-capable) — successfully tested in 2024 with MIRV (Multiple Independently-Targetable Re-entry Vehicles) for the first time. Agni-6 (8,000-12,000 km) is under development. The K-6 submarine-launched ballistic missile (6,000 km) is under development. India maintains a No First Use (NFU) doctrine but reserved the right to retaliate massively if attacked with WMDs. India also tested the Prithvi, Prahaar, Pinaka (guided rockets), Pralay (short-range ballistic, 150-500 km), and Zorawar (light tank for high-altitude operations) systems
  • Naval Modernisation: INS Vikrant (IAC-1, 45,000 tonnes) — India’s first indigenously built aircraft carrier — commissioned in September 2022. The IAC-2 (65,000 tonnes, CATOBAR configuration) is under planning. 4 Scorpene-class submarines (Kalvari class) under construction at Mazagon Dock with French technology transfer. 6 P-75I submarines (Project 75 India) under construction. The Indian Navy has 132 ships in active service with 52 under construction. Modernisation of naval air arm with MiG-29K (45), Rafale-M (26 ordered), and naval Tejas. INS Nilgiri (first of 7 stealth frigates under Project 17A) commissioned 2024. India also commissioned INS Mormugao (Visakhapatnam-class destroyer), INS Imphal (3rd Visakhapatnam-class), and INS Surat (4th). India’s naval doctrine emphasises Indian Ocean Region (IOR) dominance and anti-piracy operations
  • DRDO’s Next-Gen Technologies: Directed Energy Weapons (DEWs) — KALI (Kilo Ampere Linear Injector) particle beam weapon, laser weapons (1 kW to 20 kW for anti-drone). Hypersonic Technology Demonstrator Vehicle (HSTDV) — scramjet engine tested (Mach 6, 20 seconds burn). India working on hypersonic cruise missiles (with Russia’s help) and the HGV (Hypersonic Glide Vehicle). Quantum computing — DRDO’s quantum key distribution (QKD) and quantum radar (detecting stealth aircraft). The Defence Space Agency (DSA) was established for space-based surveillance. DRDO is working on AI-enabled drones (combat), exoskeletons for soldiers, and advanced body armour (Bhabha Kavach)

6.3 Nuclear Energy

India’s installed nuclear capacity is ~7,500 MW (22 reactors, 8 under construction). The target is to reach 22,480 MW by 2031-32 under the 3-stage nuclear programme. Stage I (Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors — PHWRs), Stage II (Fast Breeder Reactors — FBRs), Stage III (Thorium-based reactors). The 500 MW Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) at Kalpakkam, the world’s first FBR to use a unique combination of mixed uranium-plutonium oxide fuel and liquid sodium coolant, is nearing criticality (expected 2026). India is the world leader in thorium-based reactor research. The Advanced Heavy Water Reactor (AHWR-300) under design at BARC. India also operates the BHAVINI (Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Ltd) for fast breeder reactors. The Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant (KKNPP, 2x1000 MW VVER reactors operational, 2 more under construction) is India’s largest nuclear station. India and France are pursuing the Jaitapur Nuclear Power Project (6x1650 MW EPR reactors) — negotiations ongoing for cost and liability issues. India also signed civil nuclear agreements with USA (2008 waiver, 123 Agreement enabling nuclear trade after 34-year isolation), Russia (Kudankulam), France (Jaitapur), and Kazakhstan (uranium supply). The Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act (CLNDA, 2010) has been a barrier to foreign investment due to its operator liability clause (right of recourse against suppliers). India is working on small modular reactors (SMRs, 300 MW each) to repurpose retiring coal-fired power plants. The Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) and the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) govern nuclear energy policy. India is not a signatory to the NPT (Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty) but has a voluntary IAEA safeguards agreement for civilian facilities under the India-specific safeguards agreement (2009).

6.4 Semiconductors and Electronics

India launched the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) with a ₹76,000 crore outlay under the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme. Key developments: Micron Technology (US) invested $2.75 billion for a semiconductor assembly, testing, marking, and packaging (ATMP) facility in Sanand, Gujarat (operational by 2025). Tata Group and Taiwan’s Powerchip Semiconductor Manufacturing Corp (PSMC) are setting up India’s first semiconductor fabrication plant (fab) in Dholera, Gujarat (₹91,000 crore investment, 28-65 nm node, 30,000 wafers per month capacity, expected 2027). Tata also has an ATMP plant in Assam. CG Power (with Renesas, Japan) and Kaynes Technology are setting up ATMP units. Foxconn and Vedanta signed a JV (later dissolved for semiconductor, Foxconn pursuing solo). India’s electronics manufacturing crossed $100 billion in FY24 (target $300 billion by 2026). Mobile phone exports crossed ₹1.2 lakh crore (Apple’s iPhones being the largest component). India is positioning itself as a trusted global supply chain partner in semiconductors and electronics.

6.5 Artificial Intelligence and Digital Technology

  • IndiaAI Mission (2024): Cabinet approved the IndiaAI Mission with ₹10,372 crore outlay (2024-2028). Key pillars: IndiaAI Compute (10,000+ GPUs under PPP), IndiaAI Innovation Centre (IIC, developing foundational AI models), IndiaAI Datasets Platform (anonymised non-personal datasets), IndiaAI Application Development Initiative (AI solutions for agriculture, healthcare, education, climate, and smart cities), IndiaAI FutureSkills (training 5 lakh+ AI professionals), and IndiaAI Startup Financing (₹2,000 crore fund). The government is developing a National Data Governance Framework and has released a draft AI regulation — preferring a risk-based, innovation-friendly approach rather than EC’s-style regulation
  • India Stack: Aadhaar (1.4 billion biometric IDs), UPI (processed ₹200+ lakh crore in FY24, dominating retail payments with 85%+ market share), DigiLocker (700+ million documents stored, 1.5 billion issuances), Account Aggregator framework (financial data sharing), ONDC (Open Network for Digital Commerce, democratising e-commerce), CoWIN (delivered 2.2 billion COVID vaccine doses), Ayushman Bharat Digital Health Mission (ABDM), and DEPA (Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture). India is exporting the India Stack model to developing countries: UPI now accepted in UAE, Singapore, Bhutan, Nepal, Mauritius, France, Sri Lanka, and several other countries. Aadhaar-based authentication (e-KYC) has saved banks ~₹40,000 crore in compliance costs
  • MeitY Initiatives: National Supercomputing Mission (Param series — Param Siddhi AI, 5.26 Petaflops). National Quantum Mission (2023-2031, ₹6,003 crore) aims for quantum computers (50-100 qubits by 2028, 1,000 qubits by 2031), quantum communication (2,000 km networks), and quantum sensors. Bhashini (National Language Translation Mission) for AI-based translation across 22 Scheduled Languages. Digital India Bhashini Divas celebrated with the launch of real-time translation tools
  • Cyber Security: National Cyber Security Strategy 2024 released. The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) reported 18+ lakh cyber security incidents in 2024. India has introduced the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act, 2023) replacing various provisions of IT Act 2000. The DPDP Act provides for: personal data protection, data fiduciaries’ obligations, Data Protection Board of India (DPBI), and significant penalties (up to ₹250 crore). The Act has been criticised for exemptions for government processing and the right to erasure not being absolute. India is also working on the National Data Governance Framework Policy, the Digital India Act (to replace IT Act 2000), and the Indian Telecommunication Bill 2023 (replacing archaic Indian Telegraph Act 1885)

6.6 Biotechnology and Health

  • Genome India Project: Completed sequencing of 10,000 Indian genomes under the Genome India Initiative (led by Department of Biotechnology, 20 institutions). The initiative aims to create a reference Indian genome (since the Indian population has unique genetic diversity not fully represented in global databases). Applications: personalised medicine, rare disease diagnosis, population-specific drug discovery, and understanding genetic predisposition to diseases (diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular)
  • Mission COVID Suraksha: India developed Covaxin (Bharat Biotech), ZyCoV-D (Zydus — world’s first DNA vaccine, needle-free), and Corbevax (Biological E — protein subunit). India administered 2.2 billion doses under the world’s largest vaccination programme. India’s vaccine manufacturing capacity (Serum Institute — world’s largest vaccine manufacturer by volume, Bharat Biotech, Biological E) is being leveraged for global health security. India launched the Pradhan Mantri Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission (PM-ABHIM) with ₹64,000 crore outlay for pandemic preparedness
  • mRNA Vaccine Technology: India is developing indigenous mRNA vaccine platforms (Gennova’s HGCO19 was India’s first mRNA vaccine candidate, approved for emergency use). The Pune-based National Institute of Virology (NIV) and Translational Health Science and Technology Institute (THSTI) are advancing mRNA research. India aims to create a self-reliant vaccine ecosystem against future pandemics
  • Pharmaceuticals: India is the “pharmacy of the world” — accounting for 20% of global generic medicine supply and 60% of global vaccine supply. Indian pharma exports crossed $25 billion in FY24. The PLI scheme for pharmaceuticals (₹15,000 crore) targets 53 critical bulk drugs for domestic production (reducing API import dependence on China). India also launched the Promotion of Research and Innovation in Pharma MedTech Sector (PRIP) scheme with ₹5,000 crore. India became the 1st country to develop a WHO-prequalified Typhoid Conjugate Vaccine (Vi-DT by Biological E)

6.7 Climate Change and Environment

  • COP28 (Dubai, December 2023): India actively participated. Key outcomes: Global Stocktake adopted (first-ever assessment of Paris Agreement progress), operationalisation of the Loss and Damage Fund (pledged ~$700 million, India not a contributor), Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA) framework, and UAE Consensus calling for “transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems.” India emphasised equity, CBDR-RC (Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities), and climate finance commitments of $100 billion per year still unmet. India highlighted its performance: renewable energy capacity 190+ GW, fastest growth in renewable capacity among G20 countries, and achievement of its NDC target (40% non-fossil fuel capacity) 9 years ahead of schedule
  • COP29 (Baku, November 2024): India submitted its 3rd National Communication and first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR). The conference agreed on a New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) on climate finance: $300 billion per year by 2035 (from developed to developing countries) — criticised as inadequate by India and developing countries (original demand was $1.3 trillion). Full operationalisation of Article 6 (international carbon markets) agreed. India launched the “We Lead” initiative for women-led climate action and the “Infrastructure for Resilient Island States” (IRIS) for SIDS. India also launched the Global Green Credit Initiative (GGCI)
  • Panchamrit Targets (COP26, Glasgow 2021): India’s 5 commitments: 1) 500 GW non-fossil fuel energy capacity by 2030; 2) 50% energy needs from renewables by 2030; 3) Reduction of 1 billion tonnes of projected carbon emissions by 2030; 4) Reduce carbon intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 (from 2005 levels); 5) Net-zero emissions by 2070. Progress: non-fossil capacity crossed 190 GW (including large hydro) — on track for 500 GW target. Renewable energy capacity (excluding large hydro) crossed 75 GW solar, 45 GW wind. Solar tariff declined to ₹2.50/kWh (competitive with coal power). The government launched the PM-KUSUM scheme for solar pumps (target 30.8 GW), PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana (free solar power for 1 crore households, ₹75,000 crore), and Green Hydrogen Mission
  • National Green Hydrogen Mission (2023): ₹19,744 crore outlay. Target: 5 MMT (million metric tonnes) green hydrogen production by 2030, 125 GW renewable energy capacity for hydrogen production, ₹8 lakh crore investment, creation of 6 lakh jobs, and reduction of ₹1 lakh crore in fossil fuel imports. Strategic interventions for Green Hydrogen (SIGHT) programme provides incentives for electrolyser manufacturing (target 15 GW capacity) and green hydrogen production. Green Hydrogen Hubs identified: Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, Rajasthan. India also launched the pilot projects for Green Steel, Green Shipping, and Green Ammonia. India’s green hydrogen cost target: $1/kg by 2030 (currently ~$4-5/kg). The world’s largest green hydrogen plant was commissioned in 2025 in Gujarat (AM Green, 450 KTPA ammonia)
  • LiFE Mission (Lifestyle for Environment): Launched by PM Modi at COP26, LiFE promotes individual and community action for climate change through sustainable lifestyles. India adopted a Mission LiFE framework with global outreach. The UNEP and India developed a LiFE Scorecard. The concept promotes mindful consumption, waste reduction, energy conservation, and sustainable food choices. India also launched the Global Alliance for a Sustainable Planet (GASP) focusing on sustainable consumption
  • Forest and Biodiversity: India’s forest and tree cover is ~25% of geographical area (8,27,396 sq km as per ISFR 2023). India submitted its 4th National Biodiversity Action Plan (NBAP) aligned with the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF, 2022). Targets: protect 30% of land and sea by 2030 (30x30 target), restore 30% of degraded ecosystems, and halve food waste. India has 106 national parks, 573 wildlife sanctuaries, 220 conservation reserves, and 18 biosphere reserves. India is home to 4 of 36 global biodiversity hotspots (Western Ghats, Eastern Himalayas, Indo-Burma, Sundaland) and 7 World Heritage Sites (natural). India’s Project Tiger (1973) now covers 54 tiger reserves with 3,682 tigers (2022 estimate — 75% of global tiger population). India’s Project Elephant covers 33 elephant reserves. The Great Indian Bustard (GIB) conservation programme uses ex-situ breeding and habitat restoration. The Cheetah reintroduction project (Project Cheetah, 2022) at Kuno National Park, Madhya Pradesh, saw 20+ cheetahs translocated from Namibia and South Africa (8 died, rest adapting well). The Gangetic dolphin conservation programme under Project Dolphin (launched 2020)

6.8 Space and Defence: Recent Policy Developments

  • India Space Policy (2023): Released in April 2023, this policy opens up the space sector to private players. The Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Centre (IN-SPACe, established 2020) is the single-window regulator for private space activities. NewSpace India Ltd (NSIL, the commercial arm of ISRO) will handle satellite and launch services commercially. Startups like Skyroot Aerospace (Vikram-S, India’s first private rocket launch, 2022), Agnikul Cosmos (world’s first 3D-printed rocket engine, Agnibaan, 2023-24), Dhruva Space, Pixxel (hyperspectral satellite constellation), and Bellatrix Aerospace have emerged as private space players. India has 200+ space-tech startups with cumulative funding of $500+ million
  • Defence Reforms: The Chief of Defence Staff (CDS) post was created in 2019 to bring tri-service synergy. The Department of Military Affairs (DMA) under CDS was established in 2020. CDS works on theatre commands: India is planning to create integrated theatre commands (maritime, air defence, and possibly western/northern commands). The Defence Acquisition Council (DAC) approved several landmark Make-in-India projects: 156 combat helicopters (Prachand, Rudra), 200+ K9 Vajra-T howitzers, and 250+ Arjun Mk1A tanks. The iDEX (Innovations for Defence Excellence) scheme, under the Defence Innovation Organisation (DIO), supports 400+ defence start-ups and 30 innovations. The Defence India Startup Challenges attracted 2000+ applicants. The positive indigenisation list (4 lists with 5,000 items progressively banned for import) under Atmanirbhar Bharat has accelerated domestic defence manufacturing. Defence exports crossed ₹21,000 crore (FY24) — a 30-fold increase from 2014. Export destinations include Philippines (BrahMos), UAE, Maldives, Sri Lanka, and African nations. India targets defence exports of ₹35,000 crore by 2026

7.2 Key Economic Reports

IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2024)

India is projected to grow at 7.0% in FY25 and 6.5% in FY26 — the fastest among major economies. Global growth projected at 3.2% (2024) and 3.1% (2025). Key risks: geopolitical tensions (Middle East, Ukraine-Russia), commodity price volatility, persistent inflation in advanced economies (services inflation), and China’s property market slowdown. The IMF praised India’s digital public infrastructure, formalisation of the economy, and GST collections. The IMF Article IV Consultation (2024) recommended: fiscal consolidation with a medium-term target of 3% GDP deficit by FY29, GST simplification (rate rationalisation, inclusion of petroleum products), financial sector reforms to reduce NPAs, labour market reforms to boost formal employment, and continued investment in education and health. The IMF also urged India to reduce trade barriers and improve ease of doing business. India’s share in global GDP (PPP) is ~8%, projected to reach 10% by 2030.

World Bank India Development Update (2024)

The World Bank projected India’s GDP growth at 6.8% for FY25. Key themes: India needs to add 8-10 million formal jobs annually to absorb its young population, female labour force participation (FLFP) needs to double from ~25% to 50% to achieve 8% growth, export diversification (electronics, pharma, engineering goods) needs acceleration, and the logistics sector requires reforms (Logistics Ease Across Different States — LEADS index). The World Bank’s India Country Partnership Framework (CPF 2024-28) focuses on: promoting green growth, accelerating human capital development, and enabling sustainable and inclusive economic growth. The World Bank approved $3 billion in loans for India in FY24 across health, education, agriculture, and energy projects.

RBI Annual Report (2023-24)

Key highlights: GDP growth 8.2% (FY24), CPI inflation 5.4% (within RBI’s 2-6% tolerance band), current account deficit 1% of GDP, foreign exchange reserves $670+ billion (June 2024), banking sector NPAs at 12-year low of 2.8% (gross), capital adequacy ratio 16.8% (well above minimum 9%), credit growth 15% (driven by personal loans, NBFCs, and services), deposit growth 12.5%. The RBI maintained repo rate at 6.50% throughout FY24 (status quo since February 2023). The MPC voted 5:1 to maintain the rate. The RBI introduced the Digital Rupee (e-Rupee) in pilot mode since December 2022 — 1.2 million retail users and 300,000 merchants onboarded by 2024. The RBI’s Financial Stability Report (FSR, June 2024) noted that banks’ gross NPAs declined to a 12-year low of 2.8% and macro-stress tests indicated banks are well-capitalised even under adverse scenarios. The RBI also tightened norms for NBFCs and unsecured lending in 2024 to prevent systemic risk. The RBI raised the UPI transaction limit for tax payments to ₹5 lakh. India’s external debt was 18.7% of GDP (one of the lowest among emerging economies).

Economic Survey (2023-24) — Detailed

Tabled by CEA V. Anantha Nageswaran on 22 July 2024. Key highlights: GDP growth projection for FY25 at 6.5-7%. The Survey recommended continued focus on fiscal consolidation (target 4.5% deficit by FY26), highlighted the need for regulatory simplification to spur private investment (India has the highest number of regulations in the world per economic activity), noted that Indian manufacturing is at an inflection point with PLI schemes showing results (especially electronics and mobile phones), called for a “regulatory impact assessment” system for new regulations, expressed concern about rising household financial liabilities (household debt as % of GDP increased from 35% in 2019 to ~40% in 2024), observed that rural demand is recovering with improved agricultural prospects (normal monsoon, record wheat and rice production), noted that formalisation of the economy accelerated (EPFO net additions 14 million, GST registrations crossed 1.4 crore), warned about AI’s potential impact on India’s services sector (IT/BPM exports employing 5.4 million), and dedicated a chapter to labour and employment stressing the need for employment-linked incentives and female labour force participation. The Survey’s theme: “A Conservative Approach to Risk Management.”

  • Air Quality: National Clean Air Programme (NCAP): Target: 40% reduction in PM2.5 concentration by 2026 (from 2017 baseline) in 131 non-attainment cities. As of 2024, average PM2.5 reduction of ~30% achieved. Cities showing significant improvement: Varanasi, Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, and Ahmedabad. Yet, 46 of the world’s 50 most polluted cities are in India (IQAir World Air Quality Report 2024). Delhi remains the world’s most polluted capital. The Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM) in NCR has implemented a graded response action plan (GRAP). Winter air quality crisis persists due to stubble burning (Punjab, Haryana), vehicular emissions, construction dust, and industrial pollution. India launched the National Air Quality Management System (NAQMS) for real-time monitoring. The National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) are being revised. The World Bank estimated India loses $95 billion (3% of GDP) annually due to air pollution

Other Notable Reports

  • State of the World Population Report (UNFPA, 2024): India is the most populous country (1.45 billion as of 2024). Median age 28 years. Working-age population (15-64) ~69%. The report highlighted India’s demographic dividend potential but warned of the need for job creation and skill development
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) 2024 (UNDP & OPHI): India’s MPI value declined from 0.117 (2015-16) to 0.069 (2019-21). 14.96% of the population is multidimensionally poor — a significant reduction from 24.85% (2015-16). 13.5 crore people exited poverty between 2015-16 and 2019-21. Improvements in all 10 indicators: nutrition, child mortality, years of schooling, school attendance, cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, and assets. Indicators that showed fastest improvement: banking access, electricity, sanitation. India is on track to achieve SDG 1 (No Poverty) target by 2030. States with highest MPI: Bihar (33.7%), Jharkhand (30.1%), Madhya Pradesh (30.0%), Uttar Pradesh (27.8%). Lowest: Kerala (0.8%), Goa (1.0%), Tamil Nadu (2.2%)
  • NITI Aayog’s Export Preparedness Index (EPI 2024): Top states: Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana. EPI evaluates export ecosystem across 4 pillars: policy, business ecosystem, export ecosystem, and export performance. India’s total exports (goods + services) crossed $800 billion (FY24) — target $2 trillion by 2030
  • TRIPS & Global Innovation Scoreboard: India granted 100,000+ patents in 2024 — a 25% increase over 2023. India’s patent filing rate growing at 15% CAGR. India moved from 81st (2015) to 42nd (2024) in the Global Innovation Index. India became the 6th largest filer of PCT (Patent Cooperation Treaty) applications
  • Liveability Index (EIU 2024): India cities: New Delhi ranked 137th, Mumbai 139th, Chennai 140th, Bengaluru 144th. Global Liveability Index factors: stability, healthcare, culture & environment, education, infrastructure. Top cities: Vienna, Copenhagen, Zurich, Melbourne, Calgary. Ease of Living Index (Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs): top Indian cities: Bengaluru, Pune, Ahmedabad, Chennai, Surat, Navi Mumbai, Coimbatore, Vadodara, Indore, Greater Mumbai
  • Annual Survey of Industries (ASI) 2022-23: Growth in manufacturing GVA at current prices: 7.3% (2022-23). Number of factories: 2.6 lakh. Employment in manufacturing: 1.8 crore. Gross value added: ₹36.8 lakh crore. The ASI noted rising contract labour usage (36% of manufacturing workforce) and low female share in factory employment (12%)
  • India Employment Report (ILO & IHD, 2024): India needs to create 8-10 million non-farm jobs annually until 2030 to absorb the young population. The report highlighted: educated youth unemployment (15-29 years) at 18%, worsening quality of employment (rising casualisation, informalisation), and the need to boost female labour force participation. The report recommended: employment-linked incentives for formal job creation, enhanced social protection for gig and platform workers, and a National Employment Policy
