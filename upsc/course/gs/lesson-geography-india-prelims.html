Physiography of India

1.1 The Northern Mountains

The northern mountains of India are dominated by the Himalayan range, a young fold mountain system that continues to rise as the Indian plate pushes northward into the Eurasian plate. This tectonic collision creates a series of parallel ranges — the Greater Himalaya, the Lesser Himalaya, and the Siwalik foothills — each with distinct lithology and relief. The Greater Himalaya hosts the highest peaks such as Kangchenjunga and Nanda Devi, while the Siwaliks are composed of unconsolidated sediments that erode rapidly, feeding the great alluvial fans of the Indo‑Gangetic plain. The orographic barrier forces the southwest monsoon to shed most of its moisture on the windward slopes, producing dense temperate forests and permanent snowfields that act as natural reservoirs for the major river systems.

1.2 The Indo‑Gangetic Plain

Stretching from the Punjab in the west to the Brahmaputra valley in the east, the Indo‑Gangetic plain is a vast alluvial tract formed by the deposition of sediments carried by the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra rivers and their tributaries. The plain is divided into the Upper, Middle, and Lower sections based on gradient and sediment size; the Upper plain near the Himalaya receives coarse boulders and gravels, while the Lower plain in Bengal and Bangladesh is dominated by fine silt and clay. This gradation controls soil fertility, groundwater recharge, and the pattern of human settlement, making the plain one of the most densely populated agricultural regions in the world.

1.3 The Peninsular Plateau

The Peninsular plateau, also known as the Deccan plateau, forms the stable crystalline core of the Indian subcontinent. It is composed largely of Archean gneisses, granites, and the extensive Deccan Traps — flood basalts erupted around 66 million years ago. The plateau is dissected by a series of river valleys such as the Narmada, Tapti, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri, which flow eastward to the Bay of Bengal or westward to the Arabian Sea. The western edge rises sharply as the Western Ghats, a biodiversity hotspot, while the eastern edge slopes gently into the Eastern Ghats, creating a rain‑shadow effect that influences the climate of the interior.

1.4 The Coastal Plains and Islands

India’s coastline extends over 7,500 kilometres, flanked by narrow coastal plains that differ markedly on the western and eastern sides. The western coastal plain, sandwiched between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea, is subdivided into the Konkan, Kanara, and Malabar coasts and is characterized by estuaries, lagoons, and a high density of ports. The eastern coastal plain, broader and more fertile, includes the Coromandel and Utkal coasts and hosts large deltas such as those of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri. Offshore, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal and the Lakshadweep Islands in the Arabian Sea represent volcanic and coral origins respectively, adding strategic and ecological dimensions to India’s physiography.

Drainage Systems of India

2.1 The Himalayan River System

The Himalayan rivers — Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra — are antecedent streams that originated before the uplift of the Himalaya and have maintained their courses by cutting down through the rising mountains. Their upper reaches are characterized by steep gradients, V‑shaped valleys, and high erosive power, which generate enormous sediment loads. As they enter the plains, the gradient drops sharply, causing the rivers to braid, meander, and deposit vast alluvial fans. The seasonal meltwater from glaciers and the summer monsoon combine to produce a pronounced flood pulse that sustains agriculture but also poses recurrent flood hazards in the densely populated floodplains.

2.2 The Peninsular River System

Peninsular rivers such as the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri are largely rain‑fed and exhibit a more mature dendritic drainage pattern on the stable plateau. Their courses are controlled by the regional slope toward the east, resulting in long, relatively straight channels that develop broad deltas at the coast. The Narmada and Tapti are exceptions, flowing westward through rift valleys formed by the same tectonic forces that created the Deccan Traps. Because these rivers depend on the southwest monsoon, their discharge is highly seasonal, with low flows in the dry months and sharp peaks during June‑September.

2.3 Inland Drainage and Lakes

Parts of the Thar Desert and the Rann of Kutch exhibit inland drainage where streams terminate in salt flats or ephemeral lakes rather than reaching the sea. The Sambhar Lake in Rajasthan and the Chilika Lake in Odisha are notable examples of brackish water bodies that support unique ecosystems and salt production. These closed basins accumulate evaporites, influencing local soil salinity and limiting agricultural potential, while also serving as important habitats for migratory birds.

Example Question
Explain why the Ganga‑Brahmaputra delta is one of the most fertile and densely populated regions in the world, referring to the interplay of river dynamics, sediment supply, and monsoon climate.
The Ganga‑Brahmaputra delta receives an enormous sediment load — estimated at over one billion tonnes annually — from two of the world’s largest Himalayan rivers. The high sediment supply is a direct consequence of intense physical weathering in the young Himalaya and the strong monsoonal runoff that transports material downstream. When the rivers reach the low‑gradient delta plain, their velocity drops, causing massive deposition of fine silt and clay that builds new land and renews soil fertility each flood season. The tropical monsoon climate ensures a year‑round growing season with abundant rainfall, while the flat topography allows extensive irrigation and rice cultivation. Together, these factors create a highly productive agricultural base that supports a population density exceeding 1,000 persons per square kilometre.
Exam Insight
Remember that antecedent rivers like the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra cut across the Himalaya because they pre‑date the uplift; this concept frequently appears in questions on river evolution and drainage patterns.

Soils of India

3.1 Alluvial Soils

Alluvial soils dominate the Indo‑Gangetic plain, the Brahmaputra valley, and the coastal deltas. They are formed by the deposition of sediments carried by rivers and are classified into khadar (new alluvium) and bhangar (old alluvium) based on age and texture. Khadar is light‑coloured, sandy‑loam, and highly fertile, found in active floodplains, while bhangar is darker, clayey, and contains kankar nodules, occurring on higher terraces. The periodic replenishment by floods maintains nutrient levels, making these soils the backbone of India’s food grain production, especially wheat, rice, and sugarcane.

3.2 Black Cotton Soils

Black soils, locally known as regur, cover the Deccan plateau, particularly in Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, and parts of Telangana. They originate from the weathering of basaltic Deccan Traps and are rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, and iron oxides, giving them a characteristic dark colour. Their high clay content (montmorillonite) causes pronounced swelling when wet and cracking when dry, a property that aids aeration but makes tillage difficult. These soils are ideally suited for cotton, soybean, and pulses, and their moisture‑retention capacity supports rain‑fed agriculture in semi‑arid zones.

3.3 Red and Laterite Soils

Red soils develop on crystalline granitic and gneissic rocks in the southern and eastern parts of the peninsula, especially in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Odisha. Their reddish hue comes from iron oxide coatings on sand and silt particles. They are generally low in nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic matter, requiring fertilizer inputs for sustained cropping. Laterite soils form under intense leaching in high‑rainfall areas such as the Western Ghats, the northeastern hills, and parts of the Eastern Ghats. They are highly acidic, deficient in bases, and harden irreversibly on exposure, limiting their agricultural use to plantation crops like tea, coffee, and cashew.

3.4 Arid and Desert Soils

Arid soils occupy the Thar Desert and the Rann of Kutch, where evaporation far exceeds precipitation. They are sandy, low in organic matter, and often saline or alkaline due to capillary rise of groundwater and subsequent evaporation. The presence of gypsum and calcium carbonate layers can impede root penetration. Despite these constraints, irrigation projects such as the Indira Gandhi Canal have transformed parts of the desert into productive agricultural land for wheat, mustard, and fodder crops.

Natural Vegetation and Climate

4.1 Tropical Evergreen and Deciduous Forests

The Western Ghats, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and the northeastern states receive over 2,000 millimetres of annual rainfall, supporting tropical evergreen forests with a multi‑layered canopy, rich epiphytic flora, and high species endemism. Adjacent areas with a distinct dry season host tropical moist deciduous forests dominated by sal, teak, and bamboo, where trees shed leaves during the dry months to conserve water. These forests are crucial for biodiversity, carbon sequestration, and the livelihoods of tribal communities dependent on non‑timber forest products.

4.2 Subtropical and Temperate Forests

In the Himalayan foothills and the higher reaches of the Western Ghats, the climate transitions to subtropical and temperate regimes. Subtropical pine forests (chir pine) occupy the lower Himalaya, while temperate broadleaf forests of oak, rhododendron, and maple appear between 1,500 and 3,000 metres. Above the treeline, alpine meadows and scrub give way to permanent snowfields. The altitudinal zonation creates a compressed latitudinal gradient, allowing a wide variety of flora and fauna within a relatively short horizontal distance.

4.3 Grasslands and Desert Vegetation

The Terai‑Duars region at the base of the Himalaya supports tall grasslands (phumdis) that are maintained by seasonal flooding and grazing. Central India’s Deccan plateau hosts savanna‑type grasslands with scattered trees such as acacia and babul, adapted to a pronounced dry season. In the Thar Desert, vegetation is sparse and xerophytic, comprising species like Prosopis cineraria, Calligonum polygonoides, and various grasses that complete their life cycle during the brief monsoon. These ecosystems are highly sensitive to overgrazing and climate variability.

Agriculture, Minerals and Industries

5.1 Cropping Patterns and Major Crops

India’s cropping pattern is dictated by the interplay of soil, climate, and irrigation availability. The kharif season (June‑October) is dominated by rice, maize, millets, cotton, and soybean in the rain‑fed regions, while the rabi season (November‑April) sees wheat, barley, mustard, and chickpea in the irrigated north‑western plains. The introduction of high‑yielding varieties during the Green Revolution transformed the Punjab‑Haryana‑Western Uttar Pradesh belt into a wheat‑rice bowl, but also led to groundwater depletion and soil salinity. In the peninsular region, a double‑cropping system of rice‑rice or rice‑pulses is common where assured irrigation exists.

5.2 Irrigation and Water Management

Irrigation covers roughly 48 percent of the net sown area, with major sources being canals (especially the Indira Gandhi Canal, the Bhakra‑Nangal system, and the extensive canal network of the Ganga basin), tanks in the southern peninsula, and a rapidly expanding groundwater regime powered by electric and diesel pumps. The over‑exploitation of aquifers in Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, and Tamil Nadu has caused water tables to fall at rates exceeding one metre per year, prompting policy shifts toward micro‑irrigation (drip and sprinkler) and participatory groundwater management. River‑linking proposals aim to transfer surplus water from the Brahmaputra and Ganga basins to deficit areas, though ecological and inter‑state concerns remain unresolved.

5.3 Mineral Resources Distribution

India’s mineral wealth is concentrated in the peninsular shield. The Chotanagpur plateau (Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Chhattisgarh) holds the bulk of iron ore, manganese, coal, and bauxite reserves, making it the core of the country’s heavy industry. The Cuddapah and Vindhyan basins host limestone, dolomite, and cement‑grade minerals. The coastal tracts of Gujarat and Rajasthan are rich in lignite, gypsum, and rock phosphate. Offshore, the Bombay High and Krishna‑Godavari basins produce the majority of crude oil and natural gas. The strategic distribution of these resources has shaped the location of steel plants, aluminium smelters, and fertilizer factories.

5.4 Industrial Regions and Growth Corridors

Industrial activity clusters around resource bases, transport nodes, and market access. The Hooghly‑Kolkata belt leverages jute, tea, and port facilities; the Mumbai‑Pune corridor benefits from cotton, petrochemicals, and a deep‑water port; the Delhi‑NCR region thrives on automotive, electronics, and services; the Chennai‑Bengaluru‑Hyderabad triangle exploits automotive, IT, and pharmaceutical clusters; and the Gujarat‑Maharashtra corridor hosts refineries, chemicals, and textile parks. Recent policy initiatives such as the National Industrial Corridor Development Programme aim to create new growth poles along the Dedicated Freight Corridors, integrating logistics, urbanization, and skill development to reduce regional disparities.

The comprehensive understanding of India’s physical geography, from the towering Himalaya to the mineral‑rich plateaus, provides the essential context for analyzing the country’s economic activities, environmental challenges, and developmental planning.

River Interlinking Projects and Inter‑State Water Disputes

The National River Linking Project (NRLP) envisages 30 links — 14 in the Himalayan component and 16 in the peninsular component — to transfer surplus water from the Brahmaputra, Ganga and Mahanadi basins to deficit areas in western and southern India. The Ken‑Betwa link, the first to receive clearance, aims to irrigate 6.35 lakh hectares in Bundelkhand and supply drinking water to 1.3 crore people. Other priority links include the Damanganga‑Pinjal, Par‑Tapi‑Narmada and the Godavari‑Cauvery (Grand Anicut) proposals. Each link requires a Detailed Project Report, environmental clearance, and consensus among the riparian states, making inter‑state agreement the principal bottleneck.

Constitutionally, water is a State subject (Entry 17, List II) while regulation of inter‑state rivers falls under Union jurisdiction (Entry 56, List I). Article 262 empowers Parliament to adjudicate disputes, leading to the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act, 1956. Under this Act, tribunals have been constituted for the Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Mahanadi, Ravi‑Beas, and Vansadhara disputes. The Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1990) delivered its final award in 2007, later modified by the Supreme Court in 2018 to allocate 404.25 tmcft to Tamil Nadu, 284.75 tmcft to Karnataka, 30 tmcft to Kerala and 7 tmcft to Puducherry. The Krishna tribunal (2004) allocated 811 tmcft among Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, with a later bifurcation for Telangana. The Mahanadi tribunal, constituted in 2018, is still deliberating on Odisha‑Chhattisgarh claims.

Recent policy shifts emphasise cooperative federalism: the 2019 amendment to the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act introduces a single permanent tribunal with a three‑year mandate, and the Centre has proposed a National Water Framework Bill to institutionalise basin‑level management. However, political resistance, ecological concerns over downstream flow reduction, and the high capital cost (estimated ₹5.6 lakh crore for the full NRLP) continue to delay implementation.

Groundwater Governance, Aquifer Mapping and the Atal Bhujal Yojana

India extracts roughly 250 billion cubic metres (bcm) of groundwater annually, accounting for 60 % of irrigation and 85 % of rural drinking water supply. The Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) has completed aquifer mapping at 1:250 000 scale for 24 lakh km², identifying 1 200 assessment units, of which 1 071 are over‑exploited, 217 critical and 697 semi‑critical. Contamination hotspots include arsenic in the Ganga‑Brahmaputra floodplains (West Bengal, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh), fluoride in Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and salinity in coastal Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.

The Atal Bhujal Yojana (ABY), launched in 2020 with World Bank assistance (₹6 000 crore), targets 7 states — Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh — covering 8 350 gram panchayats. The scheme incentivises community‑led water budgeting, construction of recharge structures, and adoption of micro‑irrigation. Performance is measured through a Disbursement Linked Indicator (DLI) framework: states receive funds only after achieving predefined outcomes such as a 10 % reduction in groundwater extraction or a 15 % increase in recharge.

At the legislative level, the Model Bill for Groundwater Management (2022) proposes a State Groundwater Authority, mandatory registration of wells, and a pricing mechanism for commercial extraction. Several states (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Punjab) have enacted their own groundwater acts, but enforcement remains uneven. The Jal Shakti Ministry’s “National Aquifer Mapping and Management Programme” (NAMMP) aims to upscale mapping to 1:50 000 scale and integrate real‑time monitoring via the India‑WRIS portal.

Examiner's trap
Which of the following statements about the Atal Bhujal Yojana is correct?
1. It is a centrally sponsored scheme covering all 28 states.
2. It uses a Disbursement Linked Indicator framework tied to community‑level outcomes.
3. It focuses exclusively on construction of large dams for groundwater recharge.
4. It is funded entirely by the Government of India without external assistance.
Statement 2 is correct. ABY is a World Bank‑assisted central scheme limited to seven water‑stressed states. Its fund release is conditioned on achieving measurable outcomes (DLIs) such as reduced extraction and enhanced recharge, verified at the gram‑panchayat level. Statements 1, 3 and 4 are factually inaccurate: the scheme is not pan‑India, does not build large dams, and receives external financing.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links a scheme to a funding pattern or implementation mechanism, first identify the financing source (central, state, external) and the conditionalities (DLIs, outcome‑based). Eliminate options that claim universal coverage or exclusive reliance on large infrastructure. The phrase “community‑led water budgeting” is a hallmark of ABY and often appears in correct statements.

Climate Change Impacts on the Indian Monsoon and Agro‑Ecological Zones

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) notes a 0.7 °C rise in India’s mean temperature since 1901, with the most pronounced warming in the Himalaya and the Indo‑Gangetic Plain. Monsoon onset over Kerala has delayed by 5‑7 days on average since the 1970s, while the frequency of extreme rainfall events (>150 mm/day) has increased by 30 % over central India. Simultaneously, the number of dry spells during the core monsoon months (June‑September) has risen, amplifying intra‑seasonal variability.

These shifts are reshaping agro‑ecological zones (AEZs). The wheat‑dominant north‑western AEZ (Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh) is experiencing terminal heat stress during grain filling, reducing yields by 4‑6 % per °C above 30 °C. The rice‑wheat system in the eastern Indo‑Gangetic Plain faces increased submergence risk from flash floods. In the Deccan plateau, the semi‑arid AEZ is expanding southward, pushing the cotton‑sorghum belt into marginally suitable soils. The National Innovations on Climate Resilient Agriculture (NICRA) programme has documented a 10‑15 % decline in kharif soybean yields in Madhya Pradesh under delayed monsoon scenarios.

Policy responses are embedded in the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC, 2008) and its eight missions, notably the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA) and the National Water Mission. The Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) now incorporates weather‑index triggers based on satellite‑derived rainfall anomalies. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) has released 1 200 climate‑resilient varieties since 2014, including drought‑tolerant maize (DHM‑117) and heat‑tolerant wheat (HD‑3226).

Disaster Management: Floods, Droughts and Cyclones in the Indian Context

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 established the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) chaired by the Prime Minister, State Disaster Management Authorities (SDMAs) and the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) comprising 16 battalions. The Sendai Framework (2015‑2030) guides India’s shift from relief‑centric to risk‑reduction approaches.

Floods affect 40 million hectares annually. The Brahmaputra basin experiences recurrent erosion and embankment breaches; the 2017 Assam floods displaced 2.5 million people. The Ganga basin’s 2013 Uttarakhand flash floods highlighted the role of glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs). Kerala’s 2018 deluge (483 mm in 24 hours) underscored the need for reservoir operation rules and urban drainage planning.

Droughts are declared under the Manual for Drought Management (2016) using the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) and the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). The 2015‑16 El Niño‑linked drought impacted 266 districts across 11 states, prompting the Drought Prone Areas Programme (DPAP) and the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (MGNREGS) for water‑conservation works.

Cyclones originate in the Bay of Bengal (post‑monsoon) and the Arabian Sea (pre‑monsoon). The India Meteorological Department (IMD) follows the World Meteorological Organization naming convention (e.g., Cyclone Amphan 2020, Cyclone Tauktae 2021). The National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project (NCRMP) has installed 1 200 early‑warning dissemination towers and constructed 3 500 multipurpose cyclone shelters in Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal and Gujarat.

Renewable Energy Potential, Grid Integration and the National Green Hydrogen Mission

India’s renewable energy potential is estimated at 750 GW solar, 300 GW wind (onshore), 140 GW wind (offshore), 20 GW small hydro and 25 GW biomass. As of March 2024, installed capacity stands at 73 GW solar, 44 GW wind, 10 GW biomass and 4.5 GW small hydro, totalling 131 GW (excluding large hydro). The 2030 target of 450 GW non‑fossil capacity requires an annual addition of ~35 GW.

Grid integration challenges include variability, forecasting errors, and the need for balancing reserves. The Green Energy Corridors (GEC) programme — Intra‑State (₹10 000 crore) and Inter‑State (₹12 000 crore) — aims to evacuate renewable power from resource‑rich states (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu) to load centres. Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) of 4 GW/16 GWh have been approved under the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cells.

The National Green Hydrogen Mission (NGHM), launched in January 2023 with an outlay of ₹19 744 crore, targets 5 million metric tonnes (MMT) per annum of green hydrogen production by 2030. The mission incentivises electrolyser manufacturing (PLI of ₹4 500 crore), creates demand through blending mandates in refineries and fertilizer plants, and supports pilot projects for steel decarbonisation. The Inter‑State Transmission System (ISTS) waiver for green hydrogen projects until 2030 reduces wheeling charges, enhancing economic viability.
