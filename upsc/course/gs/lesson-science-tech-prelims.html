Space Technology

The Indian space programme began with the launch of Aryabhata in 1975 and has since evolved into a comprehensive capability that spans launch vehicle development, satellite manufacturing, and deep‑space exploration. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) coordinates these activities through a network of centres such as the Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre for launch vehicles, the U R Rao Satellite Centre for spacecraft, and the Laboratory for Electro‑Optics Systems for payloads. This institutional architecture enables a systematic progression from sounding rockets to heavy‑lift vehicles and from experimental satellites to operational constellations that serve communication, navigation, earth observation, and scientific research.

1. Launch Vehicles and Propulsion

The Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV) emerged as the workhorse of the programme after its first successful flight in 1994. Its four‑stage configuration combines solid‑propellant strap‑on motors with liquid‑propellant core stages, giving it the flexibility to place multiple payloads into a variety of low‑earth and sun‑synchronous orbits. The Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV) programme, particularly the Mk III variant, introduced an indigenous cryogenic upper stage that uses liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, allowing payloads of up to ten tonnes to be delivered to geostationary transfer orbit. The development of the semi‑cryogenic engine for the future Unified Launch Vehicle aims to reduce cost per kilogram by employing a kerosene‑liquid oxygen combination that is easier to handle than fully cryogenic propellants.

Propulsion research also extends to electric thrusters for station‑keeping and orbit raising, which provide higher specific impulse than chemical systems and therefore extend satellite lifetimes. The Indian National Committee for Space Research has sponsored the development of Hall‑effect thrusters and gridded ion engines, with flight qualification planned on upcoming communication satellites. These advances illustrate a deliberate strategy to master both high‑thrust chemical propulsion for launch and low‑thrust electric propulsion for in‑space manoeuvres.

2. Satellite Systems and Applications

Satellite design in India follows a modular bus approach that standardises structure, thermal control, power, and telemetry across missions, thereby reducing development time and cost. The INSAT and GSAT series provide telecommunication, broadcasting, and meteorological services, while the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (IRNSS), branded as NavIC, delivers positioning signals over the Indian region and a surrounding area of 1,500 kilometres. Earth observation satellites such as the Resourcesat, Cartosat, and RISAT families carry multispectral, panchromatic, and synthetic‑aperture radar payloads that support agriculture, urban planning, disaster management, and strategic surveillance.

The Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan) launched in 2013 demonstrated interplanetary navigation, autonomous fault management, and deep‑space communication with a modest budget, earning India a place among the few nations to reach Martian orbit. The Chandrayaan missions have progressively added an orbiter, a lander, and a rover, with Chandrayaan‑3 achieving a soft landing near the lunar south pole in 2023. These missions generate scientific data on lunar mineralogy, water‑ice distribution, and exosphere dynamics, while also testing technologies required for future human missions.

3. Human Spaceflight and Exploration

The Gaganyaan programme, announced in 2018, aims to send Indian astronauts to low‑earth orbit aboard an indigenously developed crew module launched on the GSLV Mk III. The crew module incorporates a launch‑escape system, environmental control and life‑support subsystems, and a heat‑shield capable of withstanding re‑entry temperatures exceeding 1,600 °C. Astronaut selection, training in Russia and India, and the establishment of a human‑spaceflight centre in Bengaluru reflect a whole‑of‑government approach that involves the Department of Space, the Indian Air Force, and academic institutions.

Beyond low‑earth orbit, ISRO has outlined a roadmap that includes a lunar base concept, a Venus orbiter, and participation in the International Lunar Research Station led by China and Russia. These long‑term goals require advances in in‑situ resource utilisation, closed‑loop life support, and high‑power nuclear electric propulsion, all of which are being pursued through technology demonstration missions and collaborative research with national laboratories.

Defence Technology

India’s defence research establishment, anchored by the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), pursues self‑reliance in critical weapon systems through a network of laboratories specialising in missiles, electronics, materials, and naval architecture. The Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme, launched in the early 1980s, laid the foundation for a family of strategic and tactical missiles that now form the backbone of the country’s deterrence posture. Parallel efforts in electronic warfare, radar, and cyber capabilities address the evolving spectrum of modern conflict, while indigenous shipbuilding programmes aim to transform the Indian Navy into a blue‑water force.

4. Missile Systems and Strategic Deterrence

The Agni series of ballistic missiles, ranging from the short‑range Agni‑I to the intercontinental Agni‑V, provides a credible minimum deterrent with road‑mobile launchers that enhance survivability. The development of multiple independently targetable re‑entry vehicle (MIRV) technology for Agni‑V and the ongoing work on Agni‑VI reflect a focus on penetrative capability against advanced missile defence systems. The Prithvi and Prahaar tactical missiles complement the strategic arsenal by offering quick‑reaction options for battlefield scenarios, while the BrahMos supersonic cruise missile, jointly developed with Russia, adds a versatile anti‑ship and land‑attack capability deployable from land, sea, and air platforms.

Ballistic missile defence (BMD) is being realised through a two‑layered architecture comprising the Prithvi Air Defence (PAD) for exo‑atmospheric interception and the Advanced Air Defence (AAD) for endo‑atmospheric engagement. Successful flight tests have demonstrated hit‑to‑kill kinetics at altitudes above 100 kilometres, and the integration of long‑range tracking radars such as the Swordfish and the Multi‑Function Radar strengthens the sensor grid. The BMD programme also drives advances in real‑time data fusion, high‑speed computing, and secure communication links that have spin‑off applications in civilian air traffic management.

5. Electronic Warfare and Cyber Defence

Electronic warfare suites such as the Samyukta and the Sangraha provide the Indian Army with capabilities for signal interception, direction finding, and jamming across the HF to SHF bands. The development of the DRDO’s Netra airborne early‑warning and control platform, mounted on an Embraer ERJ‑145 airframe, extends surveillance and command‑control functions over land and maritime domains. In the cyber domain, the Defence Cyber Agency, established in 2019, coordinates offensive and defensive operations, while the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre safeguards strategic assets against advanced persistent threats.

Research into quantum key distribution (QKD) and post‑quantum cryptography is underway at the Centre for Artificial Intelligence and Robotics, aiming to future‑proof military communications against emerging quantum computing attacks. The integration of artificial intelligence into signal intelligence platforms enables automated threat classification and adaptive waveform generation, reducing the cognitive load on operators and accelerating the decision cycle in contested electromagnetic environments.

6. Naval Platforms and Indigenous Shipbuilding

The Indigenous Aircraft Carrier (IAC‑1) Vikrant, commissioned in 2022, marks a milestone in warship design, incorporating a ski‑jump launch system, integrated electric propulsion, and a combat management system developed by the Naval Physical and Oceanographic Laboratory. The follow‑on IAC‑2 design envisions a larger displacement, catapult‑assisted launch, and enhanced stealth features, aligning with the Navy’s goal of operating three carrier battle groups. Submarine construction under Project 75 and Project 75I leverages technology transfer from France and Germany while progressively increasing indigenous content, culminating in the nuclear‑powered ballistic missile submarine (SSBN) Arihant class that completes the nuclear triad.

Surface combatants such as the Kolkata‑class destroyers and the Nilgiri‑class frigates incorporate multi‑function radars, vertical launch systems for BrahMos and Barak missiles, and advanced sonar suites for anti‑submarine warfare. The adoption of modular construction techniques at shipyards in Mumbai, Kochi, and Visakhapatnam reduces build time and facilitates technology insertion. These platforms collectively enhance power projection, sea‑lane security, and humanitarian assistance capabilities across the Indian Ocean Region.

Example Question
Explain how the development of the indigenous cryogenic upper stage for GSLV Mk III contributes to India's strategic autonomy in space launch capabilities.
The cryogenic upper stage uses liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, which provide a higher specific impulse than the hypergolic propellants used in earlier GSLV versions. Mastering the complex thermodynamics, materials, and manufacturing processes for cryogenic engines eliminates dependence on foreign suppliers who previously denied technology transfer under export control regimes. With an operational cryogenic stage, ISRO can launch heavier communication satellites and interplanetary payloads on its own schedule, negotiate commercial launch contracts from a position of strength, and allocate launch slots for national security missions without external constraints. This self‑reliance directly supports strategic autonomy by ensuring assured access to space for both civilian and defence requirements.
Exam Insight
When a question asks for the strategic significance of a technology, link the technical performance metric (e.g., specific impulse, payload capacity) to the policy outcome (e.g., reduced import dependence, enhanced deterrence). Examiners reward answers that connect engineering achievements to national security or economic objectives.

Biotechnology and Health Technology

India’s biotechnology sector has grown from a modest research base in the 1980s to a vibrant ecosystem that contributes to vaccines, biosimilars, agricultural genomics, and emerging gene‑editing therapies. The Department of Biotechnology (DBT) and the Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council (BIRAC) provide funding, regulatory guidance, and incubation support, while the National Biotechnology Development Strategy outlines priority areas such as affordable healthcare, food security, and environmental sustainability. The COVID‑19 pandemic accelerated public‑private partnerships, leading to the rapid development and deployment of indigenous vaccines and diagnostic kits.

7. Genome Editing and Therapeutic Applications

Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)‑Cas9 has become the dominant genome‑editing tool in Indian laboratories because of its simplicity, multiplexing capability, and relatively low cost. Researchers at the Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology have demonstrated correction of the sickle‑cell mutation in patient‑derived hematopoietic stem cells, paving the way for autologous transplantation trials. Parallel work on base editors and prime editors aims to reduce off‑target effects, a critical requirement for clinical translation. The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) has issued guidelines for somatic gene editing, emphasizing ethical oversight, informed consent, and long‑term follow‑up.

Transcription Activator‑Like Effector Nucleases (TALENs) and Zinc‑Finger Nucleases (ZFNs) remain relevant for applications where larger DNA recognition domains or higher specificity are needed, such as in certain plant‑breeding programmes. The choice between CRISPR and TALEN often depends on the target sequence context, delivery vehicle constraints, and intellectual‑property considerations. Indian start‑ups are licensing both platforms to develop disease‑resistant crop varieties and to engineer microbial strains for bio‑manufacturing of high‑value chemicals.

8. Vaccines, Diagnostics and Public Health

The success of Covaxin, an inactivated whole‑virus vaccine developed by Bharat Biotech in collaboration with the National Institute of Virology, demonstrated the capacity to move from virus isolation to emergency use authorisation within twelve months. The parallel development of ZyCoV‑D, a plasmid DNA vaccine by Zydus Cadila, introduced a novel platform that can be rapidly adapted to emerging variants. Both vaccines were manufactured at scale using existing Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) facilities, and the government’s advance market commitment ensured supply for the national immunisation programme.

Diagnostics have shifted from centralized RT‑PCR laboratories to point‑of‑care antigen tests and CRISPR‑based nucleic acid detection assays such as FELUDA, which delivers results in under an hour with minimal equipment. The Indian SARS‑CoV‑2 Genomics Consortium (INSACOG) coordinates sequencing across a network of laboratories, enabling real‑time surveillance of variants of concern. These capabilities are now being repurposed for tuberculosis, antimicrobial resistance, and vector‑borne disease monitoring under the National Health Mission.

9. Bioprocess Engineering and Industrial Biotechnology

Bioprocess engineering focuses on scaling up microbial fermentation, mammalian cell culture, and enzyme catalysis to produce biologics, biofuels, and specialty chemicals. The National Institute of Pharmaceutical Education and Research (NIPER) has established a centre of excellence for continuous manufacturing, which reduces footprint, improves product consistency, and lowers cost of goods for monoclonal antibodies. Metabolic engineering of yeast and bacterial chassis, guided by systems‑biology models, has enabled the production of artemisinic acid, a precursor of the antimalarial drug artemisinin, at commercially viable titers.

Industrial biotechnology also addresses waste valorisation through anaerobic digestion and microbial electrolysis cells that convert agricultural residues into biogas and hydrogen. The Department of Biotechnology’s Bio‑energy Mission supports pilot plants that integrate lignocellulosic pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, and fermentation to produce second‑generation ethanol. These initiatives align with the National Policy on Biofuels and contribute to the target of blending twenty percent ethanol in petrol by 2025.

Information Technology and Artificial Intelligence

The Digital India programme, launched in 2015, has expanded broadband connectivity to over six hundred thousand villages, created a unified payments interface that processes billions of transactions monthly, and established a framework for electronic governance through the National e‑Governance Plan. The Information Technology Act 2000, amended in 2008, provides legal recognition for electronic records and digital signatures, while the forthcoming Personal Data Protection Bill seeks to align India’s data‑privacy regime with global standards such as the EU General Data Protection Regulation. These policy instruments create the regulatory substrate upon which emerging technologies are built.

10. Computing Infrastructure and Cloud Services

India’s supercomputing capability has been consolidated under the National Supercomputing Mission, which has deployed systems such as PARAM Siddhi‑AI and Pratyush, delivering petaflop‑scale performance for weather modelling, computational fluid dynamics, and artificial‑intelligence training. The MeghRaj cloud initiative offers government departments a sovereign cloud platform that hosts applications ranging from tax administration to health records, ensuring data residency and compliance with the IT Act. Private providers have also established large‑scale data centres in Mumbai, Chennai, and Hyderabad, attracting hyperscale investments and fostering a competitive market for Infrastructure‑as‑a‑Service (IaaS) and Platform‑as‑a‑Service (PaaS).

Edge computing is gaining traction to support latency‑sensitive applications such as real‑time video analytics for smart cities, industrial IoT in manufacturing, and autonomous vehicle perception. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) has released recommendations for a regulatory framework that addresses spectrum allocation for 5G, network slicing, and the deployment of Multi‑Access Edge Computing (MEC) nodes. These developments reduce backhaul congestion and enable distributed AI inference closer to data sources.

11. Machine Learning, Deep Learning and Explainable AI

Indian research institutes, notably the Indian Institute of Science, the Indian Institutes of Technology, and the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing, have contributed to foundational work in deep neural networks, reinforcement learning, and natural language processing for Indic languages. The National AI Strategy, released by NITI Aayog in 2018, identifies healthcare, agriculture, education, smart cities, and mobility as priority sectors for AI deployment. Projects such as the AI‑based diabetic retinopathy screening tool, the crop‑yield prediction model using satellite imagery, and the multilingual chatbot for citizen services illustrate the translational pipeline from research to public‑good applications.

Explainable AI (XAI) is receiving attention because regulatory bodies and domain experts require transparency in high‑stakes decisions such as credit scoring, medical diagnosis, and judicial risk assessment. Techniques like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), LIME (Local Interpretable Model‑agnostic Explanations), and counterfactual explanations are being integrated into model‑deployment pipelines. The Bureau of Indian Standards is drafting standards for AI model documentation, bias assessment, and lifecycle management, which will shape procurement guidelines for government agencies.

12. Data Governance, Privacy and Ethical Frameworks

The Personal Data Protection Bill proposes a Data Protection Authority, classification of personal data into sensitive and critical categories, and obligations for data fiduciaries to conduct Data Protection Impact Assessments. The bill also introduces the concept of data localisation for critical personal data, requiring storage on servers located within India. These provisions aim to balance the facilitation of cross‑border data flows for trade with the protection of citizens’ informational autonomy.

Ethical AI guidelines issued by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology emphasize fairness, accountability, transparency, and human‑centric design. They recommend the establishment of Institutional Ethics Committees for AI projects, periodic algorithmic audits, and the inclusion of diverse stakeholder voices during model development. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, once enacted, will provide a legal backbone for these principles, enabling enforcement through penalties and compensation mechanisms.

Energy Technology

India’s energy transition is guided by the Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement, targeting forty‑five percent of installed capacity from non‑fossil sources by 2030 and net‑zero emissions by 2070. The International Solar Alliance, co‑founded by India and France, mobilises finance and technology transfer for solar deployment across the tropics. Simultaneously, the National Hydrogen Mission, launched in 2021, aims to produce five million tonnes of green hydrogen annually by 2030, leveraging low‑cost renewable electricity and electrolyser manufacturing incentives.

13. Renewable Energy Generation and Grid Integration

Solar photovoltaic capacity has surpassed seventy gigawatts, driven by competitive bidding under the Solar Energy Corporation of India and state‑level auctions that have discovered tariffs below two rupees per kilowatt‑hour. Wind power, concentrated in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, and Maharashtra, contributes over forty gigawatts, with newer offshore wind zones being demarcated along the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coasts. The Green Energy Corridor project strengthens inter‑state transmission, deploys dynamic line rating, and integrates renewable‑energy‑rich regions with load centres, reducing curtailment.

Grid integration challenges such as variability, frequency regulation, and voltage stability are addressed through ancillary service markets, automatic generation control, and the deployment of grid‑scale battery storage. The Central Electricity Regulatory Commission has issued regulations for frequency response ancillary services, enabling fast‑responding resources like batteries and hydro to be compensated. Forecasting tools powered by machine learning improve scheduling accuracy, while virtual power plants aggregate distributed rooftop solar and demand‑response loads to provide firm capacity.

14. Nuclear Power, Fusion Research and Waste Management

India’s three‑stage nuclear programme, conceived by Homi Bhabha, progresses from pressurised heavy‑water reactors (PHWRs) using natural uranium, to fast breeder reactors (FBRs) that breed plutonium, and finally to thorium‑based reactors that exploit the vast domestic thorium reserves. The Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor at Kalpakkam, with a capacity of five hundred megawatts electric, is approaching criticality and will demonstrate the closed‑fuel‑cycle technology essential for the second stage. The Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant, built with Russian VVER‑1000 units, adds two gigawatts of low‑carbon baseload power.

India participates in the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) project, contributing the cryostat, in‑vessel components, and diagnostic systems. Domestic fusion research at the Institute for Plasma Research focuses on steady‑state tokamak operation, plasma‑wall interaction materials, and tritium breeding blanket concepts. Radioactive waste management follows a closed‑cycle approach: high‑level waste is vitrified and stored in engineered near‑surface facilities, while low‑ and intermediate‑level waste is disposed of in near‑surface repositories designed for institutional control periods of three hundred years.

15. Energy Storage, Hydrogen Economy and Smart Grids

Lithium‑ion battery manufacturing is being incentivised through the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cells, targeting a domestic capacity of fifty gigawatt‑hours by 2030. Alternative chemistries such as sodium‑ion, solid‑state, and flow batteries are under development at the Centre for Battery Engineering and Electric Vehicles, aiming to reduce reliance on imported cobalt and lithium. Second‑life applications repurpose electric‑vehicle batteries for stationary storage, extending asset utilisation and improving economics.

Green hydrogen production couples alkaline or proton‑exchange‑membrane electrolyisers with dedicated solar‑wind hybrid plants. The National Hydrogen Mission provides viability gap funding, mandates hydrogen purchase obligations for refineries and fertiliser plants, and supports the creation of hydrogen hubs at ports such as Kandla and Paradip for export. Smart grid technologies, including advanced metering infrastructure, distribution automation, and blockchain‑based peer‑to‑peer energy trading pilots, enable real‑time balancing, reduce technical losses, and empower prosumers. These integrated advances position India to meet its climate commitments while enhancing energy security and industrial competitiveness.

The comprehensive interplay of space, defence, biotechnology, information technology, and energy technologies illustrates how strategic investments in science and technology translate into national capability, economic growth, and societal well‑being.

Space Situational Awareness, Debris Mitigation and On‑Orbit Servicing

India’s Space Situational Awareness (SSA) capability rests on the Network for Space Object Tracking and Analysis (NETRA), a ground‑based radar‑optical system operated by ISRO’s Telemetry, Tracking and Command Network (ISTRAC). NETRA’s primary radar at Sriharikota and the optical telescope at Mount Abu provide cataloguing of objects down to 10 cm in low Earth orbit (LEO) and 1 m in geostationary orbit (GEO). The data feed the Indian Space Debris Monitoring and Risk Assessment (ISDMRA) software, which generates conjunction alerts for operational satellites such as the Cartosat and GSAT series. In 2022 ISRO signed a memorandum of understanding with the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) to share SSA data under the “Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines” framework.

Debris mitigation follows the Inter‑Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) guidelines: end‑of‑life passivation, controlled re‑entry for LEO platforms, and graveyard orbit disposal for GEO assets. The PSLV‑C53 mission demonstrated a “passive de‑orbit” experiment using a drag‑augmentation sail on the POEM‑2 platform, reducing orbital lifetime from years to months. Active debris removal (ADR) remains at technology‑demonstration stage; the upcoming SpaDeX (Space Docking Experiment) mission, slated for 2025, will test autonomous rendezvous, capture and de‑orbit of a defunct microsatellite using a robotic arm and a net‑capture mechanism.

On‑orbit servicing (OOS) extends satellite life and enables in‑space assembly. ISRO’s “Satellite Life Extension” concept envisions a servicer spacecraft equipped with a modular payload bay, electric propulsion for orbit raising, and a standardised mechanical‑electrical interface (the Indian Standard Docking Interface, ISDI). The 2023‑24 budget allocated ₹1,200 crore for OOS technology development, including fluid‑transfer demonstrations for propellant replenishment. International cooperation with the European Space Agency’s “Space Rider” and NASA’s “OSAM‑1” informs the design of a common berthing standard, reducing duplication and fostering a commercial servicing market.

Policy‑level, the Draft Space Activities Bill (2023) proposes a licensing regime that mandates SSA data sharing, debris mitigation plans, and insurance coverage for collision liability. The bill also empowers the Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Center (IN‑SPACe) to certify private OOS operators, aligning domestic regulation with the Artemis Accords’ “due regard” principle. These measures collectively aim to preserve the long‑term sustainability of the orbital environment while unlocking new revenue streams for Indian industry.

Hypersonic Glide Vehicles, Directed‑Energy Weapons and Strategic Stability

Hypersonic glide vehicles (HGVs) combine a rocket boost phase with a manoeuvrable glide trajectory at speeds above Mach 5, compressing decision‑making windows for missile defence. India’s Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) successfully flight‑tested the Hypersonic Technology Demonstrator Vehicle (HSTDV) in September 2020, achieving a sustained scramjet combustion for 20 seconds at Mach 6. The follow‑on programme, designated “Hypersonic Cruise Missile” (HCM), targets a range of 1,500 km with a conventional payload, leveraging the BrahMos‑II joint venture with Russia for a scramjet‑powered cruise stage.

Directed‑energy weapons (DEWs) offer a complementary hard‑kill layer. DRDO’s KALI (Kilo Ampere Linear Injector) series — KALI‑5000 and KALI‑10000 — generate high‑power microwave pulses capable of disabling electronic subsystems at kilometre ranges. In 2022 a ground‑based laser weapon demonstrator, the “DURGA‑II” 100 kW solid‑state laser, intercepted a low‑altitude UAV at 3 km, validating beam‑control and atmospheric‑compensation algorithms. The Navy’s “Laser Weapon System” (LaWS) integration on the INS Vikramaditya is under evaluation for close‑in defence against swarming drones.

The strategic stability implications are profound. HGVs’ unpredictable flight paths undermine traditional ballistic‑missile‑defence (BMD) interceptors that rely on predictable trajectories. DEWs, while offering low cost‑per‑shot, suffer from weather dependence and power‑generation constraints on mobile platforms. The 2023 “Joint Doctrine for Space and Cyber Operations” explicitly links hypersonic and DEW capabilities to the “no‑first‑use” nuclear posture, stating that conventional hypersonic strikes shall not be used to degrade an adversary’s nuclear command‑and‑control. This doctrinal clarity aims to prevent inadvertent escalation while preserving deterrence credibility.

Internationally, the United States’ Conventional Prompt Strike (CPS), China’s DF‑17 HGV, and Russia’s Avangard create a tri‑polar hypersonic race. India participates in the “Hypersonic Weapons Track” of the United Nations Institute for Disarmament Research (UNIDIR) to advocate for transparency measures such as flight‑test notifications and payload declarations. Domestically, the Defence Acquisition Council (DAC) approved a ₹9,500 crore “Hypersonic and Directed Energy Capability Development” programme in 2024, earmarking funds for scramjet materials, high‑temperature ceramics, and compact pulsed‑power modules.

Examiner's twist
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about India’s hypersonic and directed‑energy programmes is correct? (a) HSTDV demonstrated a sustained scramjet flight at Mach 8 for 30 seconds. (b) KALI‑5000 is a high‑energy laser weapon deployed on INS Vikramaditya. (c) The 2023 Joint Doctrine links conventional hypersonic strikes to the no‑first‑use nuclear posture. (d) BrahMos‑II is a purely ballistic missile with a range of 3,000 km.”
Correct answer: (c). HSTDV achieved Mach 6 for ~20 seconds, not Mach 8. KALI‑5000 is a high‑power microwave system, not a laser, and has not been ship‑deployed. BrahMos‑II is a scramjet‑powered cruise missile, not a ballistic missile, with an expected range around 1,500 km. The Joint Doctrine explicitly states conventional hypersonic strikes shall not target nuclear command‑and‑control, reinforcing the no‑first‑use commitment.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mixes multiple emerging weapon systems, isolate each programme’s demonstrated performance parameters (speed, range, propulsion type) and match them to the statement. Eliminate options that conflate microwave and laser DEWs or that assign ballistic characteristics to cruise‑type hypersonics. A quick mental table of “Programme – Propulsion – Demonstrated Speed – Platform” saves time and reduces guesswork.

Synthetic Biology, Gene Drives and Biosafety Governance

Synthetic biology in India is coordinated by the Department of Biotechnology (DBT) through the “National Mission on Synthetic Biology” launched in 2021. The mission funds genome‑scale design platforms, cell‑free protein synthesis, and standardized biological parts (BioBricks) compliant with the iGEM registry. Indian teams have consistently ranked in the top ten at the International Genetically Engineered Machine (iGEM) competition, with the 2023 IIT‑Madras project engineering a chassis for aromatic compound production from lignocellulosic hydrolysate.

Gene‑drive research targets vector‑borne diseases. The “Gene Drive for Malaria Control” consortium, involving the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), the National Institute of Malaria Research, and the Tata Institute of Genetics, has constructed a CRISPR‑based homing drive targeting the doublesex gene in Anopheles stephensi. Laboratory cage trials in 2022 demonstrated >95 % drive conversion efficiency with no detectable resistance alleles after ten generations. Field‑release protocols follow the WHO “Guidance Framework for Testing of Genetically Modified Mosquitoes” and require clearance from the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

Biosafety governance integrates the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (ratified 2003) and the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit‑Sharing (ratified 2014). The “Recombinant DNA Safety Guidelines” (2017) classify activities into four risk categories (RC‑I to RC‑IV) and mandate Institutional Biosafety Committees (IBSCs) for any work involving synthetic nucleic acids, gene drives, or chassis organisms with altered host range. The 2022 amendment introduced a “Synthetic Biology Risk Assessment Matrix” that scores projects on novelty, environmental persistence, horizontal gene transfer potential, and dual‑use concern.

Dual‑use oversight is coordinated by the National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS) through the “Biosecurity Working Group”. The 2023 “National Biosecurity Policy” proposes a licensing regime for synthesis of pathogens listed in the Australia Group control lists, mandatory sequence‑screening for commercial DNA synthesis providers, and a national registry of high‑containment (BSL‑3/4) facilities. India’s participation in the “Global Health Security Agenda” (GHSA) ensures alignment with international best practices for laboratory biosafety and incident reporting.

Quantum Computing, Post‑Quantum Cryptography and National Mission

The National Mission on Quantum Technologies and Applications (NM‑QTA), approved in 2020 with an outlay of ₹8,000 crore over five years, anchors India’s quantum roadmap. The mission funds three pillars: (i) quantum computing hardware — superconducting qubits at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) and trapped‑ion platforms at the Raman Research Institute; (ii) quantum communication — satellite‑based quantum key distribution (QKD) demonstrated by the “QKD‑Sat” payload on the PSLV‑C54 mission (2022), achieving a secure key rate of 1 kbps over 600 km; (iii) quantum sensing — atomic interferometer gravimeters for navigation‑grade inertial sensing.

On the algorithmic front, the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C‑DAC) released “QSim” in 2021, an open‑source quantum simulator supporting up to 30 qubits on classical HPC clusters. QSim integrates with the “Quantum Software Development Kit” (QSDK) enabling variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) and quantum approximate optimisation algorithm (QAOA) workflows for chemistry and logistics optimisation. The 2023 “Quantum‑Ready Cryptography” white paper from the Office of the Principal Scientific Adviser recommends migration to lattice‑based schemes (CRYSTALS‑Kyber for key encapsulation, CRYSTALS‑Dilithium for signatures) aligned with NIST’s post‑quantum cryptography (PQC) standardisation round‑3 selections.

Implementation timelines: the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) mandated that all payment‑system operators adopt PQC‑compatible TLS 1.3 with Kyber‑768 by March 2026. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) issued a “Quantum‑Safe Migration Guide” for government e‑services, requiring inventory of RSA/ECC certificates and a phased replacement schedule. The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) is developing a “Quantum Key Distribution Network” linking major command centres, with a target of 200 km fibre‑based trusted‑node links by 2027.

International collaboration includes the “Quantum Communication Network” under the Indo‑French “Joint Working Group on Quantum Technologies” and participation in the “Quantum Internet Alliance” testbed. India’s contribution to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) Focus Group on Quantum Key Distribution ensures that domestic standards influence global interoperability. The mission’s mid‑term review (2024) reported 12 patents filed, 45 PhD scholars trained, and a 20‑qubit superconducting processor achieving 99.5 % two‑qubit gate fidelity.

Small Modular Reactors, Advanced Fuel Cycles and Nuclear Waste Transmutation

India’s Small Modular Reactor (SMR) programme is anchored at the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) with the “300 MWe Pressurised Heavy Water SMR” (PHWR‑SMR) design. The reactor retains the proven PHWR physics — natural uranium fuel, heavy‑water moderator and coolant — but integrates passive safety systems: gravity‑driven emergency core cooling, a compact containment with a 48‑hour coping period, and a factory‑fabricated pressure vessel transportable by rail. The first‑of‑a‑kind unit is slated for construction at the Gorakhpur site, with a target commercial operation date of 2030.

Advanced fuel cycles aim to close the plutonium loop. The “Advanced Heavy Water Reactor” (AHWR‑300LEU) uses a thorium‑plutonium mixed oxide (MOX) fuel bundle, achieving a conversion ratio >0.9 and producing 233U in‑situ. The AHWR design incorporates a low‑void‑coefficient core and a passive decay‑heat removal system based on natural circulation of light water. A technology demonstrator, the “AHWR Critical Facility”, achieved criticality in 2021 at the Reactor Research Centre, Kalpakkam, validating neutronic predictions.

Waste transmutation research focuses on partitioning and transmutation (P&T) of minor actinides (neptunium, americium, curium) and long‑lived fission products (technetium‑99, iodine‑129). The “Fast Reactor Fuel Cycle Facility” (FRFCF) at Kalpakkam houses a pyro‑processing line for metallic fuel reprocessing, demonstrating >99.9 % recovery of plutonium and >95 % separation of minor actinides. The separated actinides are fabricated into “transmutation targets” for irradiation in the Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) and future metal‑fuel fast reactors, reducing the radiotoxicity half‑life of high‑level waste from ~10⁵ years to <300 years.

Regulatory oversight follows the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB) “SMR Licensing Guide” (2022), which adopts a graded approach: design certification, site‑specific safety analysis, and a combined construction‑operation licence. The guide references IAEA Safety Standards Series No. SSR‑2/1 (Rev. 1) and mandates a “Digital Twin” of the reactor for real‑time safety monitoring. Waste management conforms to the “Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010” and the “Radioactive Waste Management Rules, 2020”, which require a deep geological repository (DGR) site selection by 2035. The “National Nuclear Waste Management Strategy” (2023) earmarks the Bhandari‑Khetri region in Rajasthan for preliminary site characterisation, leveraging the stable granitic basement and low groundwater flux.
