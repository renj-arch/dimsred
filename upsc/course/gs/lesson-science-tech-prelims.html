1. Space Technology

1.1 Evolution of Launch Vehicles

The Indian space programme began with the launch of Aryabhata in 1975 aboard a Soviet Kosmos‑3M rocket, marking the country’s entry into orbital operations. Indigenous launch capability was achieved with the Satellite Launch Vehicle‑3 (SLV‑3) in 1980, which placed the Rohini series into low Earth orbit and demonstrated the feasibility of solid‑propellant stages. The subsequent Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV) made its maiden flight in 1993 and, after a series of incremental improvements, became the workhorse for both domestic remote‑sensing missions and commercial payloads, earning a reputation for reliability with over fifty consecutive successful flights by 2023. The Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV) programme, initiated in the late 1990s, aimed to master cryogenic upper‑stage technology; the first successful flight with an indigenous cryogenic engine occurred in 2014, enabling India to launch heavier communication satellites into geostationary transfer orbit without foreign assistance. The development of the GSLV Mk III, later renamed LVM‑3, further expanded payload capacity to four tonnes, supporting the Chandrayaan‑2 lunar mission and the upcoming Gaganyaan human‑spaceflight programme.

Each successive vehicle addressed a specific strategic gap: SLV‑3 proved basic orbital insertion, PSLV provided cost‑effective access to polar orbits for earth observation, and GSLV closed the gap for heavy geostationary payloads. The progression also stimulated domestic industry, as private firms began supplying subsystems such as composite structures, avionics, and propulsion components, creating a supply chain that now contributes to export‑oriented launch services through NewSpace India Limited (NSIL).

1.2 Major Scientific Missions

Chandrayaan‑1, launched in October 2008 aboard a PSLV‑C11, carried eleven scientific instruments, including the Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3) provided by NASA, which confirmed the presence of water‑ice in permanently shadowed craters near the lunar poles. The mission’s success established India as a credible lunar explorer and paved the way for Chandrayaan‑2 in July 2019, which comprised an orbiter, the Vikram lander, and the Pragyan rover. Although the lander experienced a hard landing, the orbiter continues to return high‑resolution data on lunar topography, mineralogy, and exospheric composition. The follow‑on Chandrayaan‑3, launched in July 2023 on an LVM‑3, achieved a soft landing near the south pole in August 2023, making India the fourth nation to accomplish a controlled lunar landing and the first to do so in the high‑latitude region.

The Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan), launched in November 2013 on a PSLV‑C25, entered Martian orbit in September 2014 on its first attempt, a feat achieved by only a handful of space agencies. Carrying five scientific payloads, it studied surface features, mineralogy, and atmospheric dynamics, demonstrating deep‑space navigation and autonomous operations at a fraction of the cost of comparable missions. The success of Mangalyaan reinforced India’s capability for interplanetary missions and informed the design of future planetary explorers such as the proposed Venus orbiter (Shukrayaan) and the Aditya‑L1 solar observatory, launched in September 2023 to study the solar corona from the Sun‑Earth L1 point.

1.3 Human Spaceflight and Future Directions

The Gaganyaan programme, announced in 2018, aims to demonstrate indigenous human spaceflight capability by launching a crew of three into a 400 km low Earth orbit for up to seven days. The programme leverages the LVM‑3 launch vehicle, a crew module with an integrated escape system, and a service module derived from the GSLV Mk III upper stage. Critical technologies such as environmental control and life support (ECLS), crew escape system (CES), and re‑entry thermal protection have been qualified through a series of pad‑abort tests (2022) and high‑altitude abort tests (2023). The first uncrewed test flight (G1) is scheduled for 2024, followed by a second uncrewed mission (G2) and the inaugural crewed flight (H1) targeted for 2025.

Beyond Gaganyaan, the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) has outlined a roadmap that includes a modular space station (Bharatiya Antariksha Station) by the mid‑2030s, lunar base concepts in collaboration with international partners, and participation in the Artemis Accords framework for sustainable lunar exploration. The establishment of the Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Centre (IN‑SPACe) in 2020 and the creation of NSIL have opened the sector to private enterprises, enabling start‑ups to develop small‑satellite launch vehicles, satellite constellations for broadband, and on‑orbit servicing technologies, thereby transforming the space ecosystem from a government‑centric model to a collaborative innovation platform.

2. Defence Technology

2.1 Missile Development Programme

The Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (IGMDP), launched in 1983 under the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), produced a family of strategic and tactical missiles that form the backbone of India’s deterrence posture. The Prithvi series, beginning with Prithvi‑I (150 km range) in 1988, provided the army with a short‑range ballistic missile capable of conventional and nuclear delivery. The Agni series progressed from Agni‑I (700 km) in 1989 to Agni‑V (over 5,000 km) tested successfully in 2012, giving India an intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) capability with multiple independently targetable re‑entry vehicle (MIRV) potential. The submarine‑launched ballistic missile (SLBM) K‑15 (Sagarika), tested from the INS Arihant in 2018, completed the nuclear triad by providing a sea‑based second‑strike option.

In the tactical domain, the Akash surface‑to‑air missile system, inducted in 2014, employs a phased‑array radar (Rajendra) and can engage multiple aerial targets at ranges up to 25 km. The BrahMos supersonic cruise missile, a joint venture with Russia’s NPO Mashinostroyenia, entered service in 2005 with a speed of Mach 2.8 and a range initially limited to 290 km; subsequent variants have extended the range beyond 400 km following India’s accession to the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR) in 2016. The development of the hypersonic technology demonstrator vehicle (HSTDV) in 2020 marked India’s entry into scramjet propulsion research, a prerequisite for future hypersonic cruise missiles.

2.2 Naval and Aerospace Platforms

The indigenous aircraft carrier INS Vikrant (IAC‑1), commissioned in September 2022, displaces approximately 45,000 tonnes and operates a complement of MiG‑29K fighters and MH‑60R helicopters, representing a major milestone in naval shipbuilding. The Project‑75 Scorpène‑class submarines, built under technology transfer from France’s Naval Group, have delivered six conventional attack submarines (INS Kalvari to INS Vagsheer) equipped with air‑independent propulsion (AIP) modules developed by DRDO, enhancing underwater endurance. On the aerospace front, the Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) Tejas, inducted into the Indian Air Force in 2016, integrates a digital fly‑by‑wire control system, composite airframe, and an indigenous AESA radar (Uttam) in its Mk 1A variant, reducing reliance on foreign fighter imports.

The development of the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA), a fifth‑generation stealth fighter, is underway with a target first flight in the late 2020s. The programme emphasizes indigenous engine development (the Kaveri afterburning turbofan), low‑observable shaping, and network‑centric warfare capabilities. Simultaneously, the DRDO’s Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) portfolio includes the Rustom‑II medium‑altitude long‑endurance (MALE) drone, which completed its first flight in 2021 and is intended for intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) missions along contested borders.

2.3 Strategic Systems and Electronic Warfare

India’s nuclear command and control architecture rests on the Nuclear Command Authority (NCA) established in 2003, comprising a Political Council chaired by the Prime Minister and an Executive Council headed by the National Security Adviser. The Strategic Forces Command (SFC) operationalises the land, air, and sea legs of the triad, with the Agni‑V and K‑4 SLBM (3,500 km range) providing credible minimum deterrence. The 2019 Mission Shakti anti‑satellite (ASAT) test, which destroyed a live microsatellite at 300 km altitude using a modified ballistic missile interceptor, demonstrated kinetic kill capability in low Earth orbit and signalled India’s ability to protect space assets.

Electronic warfare (EW) capabilities have been consolidated under the Defence Electronics Research Laboratory (DLRL) and the Defence Avionics Research Establishment (DARE). The Samyukta integrated EW system, deployed on frontline platforms, provides radar warning, jamming, and direction‑finding functions. The development of the DRDO’s Netra airborne early warning and control (AEW&C) platform, mounted on an Embraer ERJ‑145 airframe, enhances situational awareness over land and maritime domains. These systems collectively enable spectrum dominance, a prerequisite for modern network‑centric operations.

Example Question
Explain how the progression from SLV‑3 to LVM‑3 reflects the evolution of India’s launch vehicle technology and its impact on strategic autonomy in space.
The SLV‑3 (1980) established India’s ability to place a lightweight payload into low Earth orbit using all‑solid propulsion, proving basic launch competence. The PSLV (1993) introduced liquid‑stage technology and a modular strap‑on configuration, enabling reliable polar launches for remote‑sensing satellites and commercial rideshares, which generated revenue and reduced dependence on foreign launch services. The GSLV programme (first flight 2001) targeted geostationary payloads but initially relied on Russian cryogenic stages; the successful indigenous cryogenic upper stage flight in 2014 marked mastery of high‑energy liquid hydrogen‑liquid oxygen propulsion. The LVM‑3 (formerly GSLV Mk III), with a 4‑tonne GTO capacity, consolidated these advances — solid boosters, a high‑thrust liquid core, and an indigenous cryogenic stage — allowing heavy communication satellites, lunar missions (Chandrayaan‑2/3), and the Gaganyaan crewed programme to be launched domestically. This trajectory eliminated reliance on external launch providers for strategic payloads, secured sovereign access to space, and created a commercial launch ecosystem through NSIL, thereby enhancing both technological self‑reliance and geopolitical leverage.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “strategic autonomy” in space, link specific vehicle milestones (SLV‑3, PSLV, GSLV cryogenic success, LVM‑3) to concrete outcomes: domestic heavy‑satellite launch, human‑spaceflight capability, and commercial launch revenue. Cite the year of the first indigenous cryogenic flight (2014) and the LVM‑3 payload figure (4 t to GTO) to earn full marks.

3. Biotechnology

3.1 Foundations and Genomic Milestones

Modern biotechnology in India traces its institutional roots to the establishment of the Department of Biotechnology (DBT) in 1986 under the Ministry of Science and Technology. The DBT spearheaded the Indian participation in the Human Genome Project, contributing to the sequencing of chromosome 2 and the annotation of disease‑associated genes. The completion of the first Indian human genome sequence in 2009 by the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR)‑Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology (IGIB) demonstrated the country’s capacity for large‑scale genomics. Subsequently, the Genome India Project, launched in 2020, aims to catalogue genetic variation across 10,000 individuals representing diverse ethnic groups, providing a reference panel for precision medicine and population‑level disease association studies.

Recombinant DNA technology entered the Indian regulatory framework with the adoption of the “Rules for the Manufacture, Use, Import, Export and Storage of Hazardous Microorganisms/Genetically Engineered Organisms or Cells” in 1989, later superseded by the 1998 “Guidelines for Research in Transgenic Plants” and the 2006 “National Biotechnology Development Strategy”. These instruments established the Genetic Engineering Approval Committee (GEAC) as the apex body for environmental release of genetically modified organisms (GMOs). The commercial cultivation of Bt cotton, approved in 2002, became the first transgenic crop in India, leading to a rapid increase in cotton yield and a reduction in pesticide use, though it also sparked debates on farmer indebtedness and resistance management.

3.2 Gene Editing and Therapeutic Applications

The discovery of the CRISPR‑Cas9 system in 2012 revolutionised genome editing by providing a programmable, RNA‑guided nuclease that can target any DNA sequence with high specificity. Indian laboratories at the National Centre for Biological Sciences (NCBS), the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), and the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (CCMB) quickly adopted CRISPR for functional genomics, creating knockout models for diseases such as sickle‑cell anaemia and beta‑thalassaemia. In 2020, the DBT‑funded “Genome Editing for Crop Improvement” network initiated projects to develop disease‑resistant rice and wheat lines using base‑editing variants that avoid double‑strand breaks, thereby reducing off‑target effects.

Therapeutically, the first Indian clinical trial employing CRISPR‑based therapy was approved in 2022 for the treatment of transfusion‑dependent beta‑thalassaemia, using autologous haematopoietic stem cells edited ex‑vivo to reactivate fetal haemoglobin production. Parallel efforts in CAR‑T cell therapy have led to the development of an indigenous CD19‑targeted product (NexCAR19) by ImmunoACT, a spin‑off from IIT Bombay, which received regulatory clearance for a phase‑I/II trial in 2023. These advances illustrate the transition from basic genome editing research to clinically translatable platforms within a decade.

3.3 Biomanufacturing, Vaccines and Bio‑economy

The COVID‑19 pandemic accelerated India’s biomanufacturing capacity, with the Serum Institute of India (SII) producing the Oxford‑AstraZeneca vaccine (Covishield) and Bharat Biotech developing the inactivated whole‑virion vaccine (Covaxin) under a public‑private partnership with the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR). By mid‑2022, India had supplied over 200 million doses domestically and exported vaccines to more than 90 countries under the “Vaccine Maitri” initiative, underscoring the strategic role of vaccine diplomacy. The National Biopharma Mission (NBM), launched in 2017 with World Bank support, aims to strengthen the translational pipeline from academic discovery to commercial product by funding shared infrastructure such as Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) facilities, bio‑incubators, and clinical trial networks.

The bio‑economy target set by the DBT envisions a valuation of US $150 billion by 2025, driven by sectors including bio‑pharmaceuticals, bio‑agriculture, bio‑industrial enzymes, and bio‑services. The Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for pharmaceuticals and medical devices, announced in 2021, provides financial incentives for domestic manufacturing of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), key starting materials, and high‑end medical devices, reducing import dependence on China for critical intermediates. Together, these policies create a feedback loop where regulatory reforms, infrastructure investment, and market incentives reinforce each other to expand the biotechnology footprint.

4. Information Technology and Artificial Intelligence

4.1 Digital Infrastructure and Governance Platforms

The National Informatics Centre (NIC), established in 1976, laid the backbone for government‑wide networking, culminating in the National Knowledge Network (NKN) in 2010, a multi‑gigabit pan‑India network connecting research institutions, universities, and government agencies. The Aadhaar programme, launched in 2009 by the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI), enrolled over 1.3 billion residents by 2023, providing a biometric identity platform that underpins direct benefit transfers (DBT), e‑KYC, and authentication services across banking, telecom, and welfare schemes. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), developed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) and launched in 2016, transformed retail payments by enabling instant, interoperable, mobile‑first transactions; by March 2024 UPI processed over 10 billion transactions per month, making it the world’s largest real‑time payment system by volume.

The Digital India programme (2015) integrated these pillars with initiatives such as BharatNet, which aims to provide optical‑fiber connectivity to all 250,000 gram panchayats, and the Government e‑Marketplace (GeM), an online procurement portal that has reduced procurement cycle times and increased transparency. The National Digital Health Mission (NDHM), renamed Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) in 2021, creates a federated health‑record architecture using unique health IDs, consent‑based data exchange, and interoperable standards (FHIR), enabling portable health records and telemedicine at scale.

4.2 Artificial Intelligence Strategy and Research Ecosystem

India’s National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (NSAI), released by NITI Aayog in 2018, identified five sectors — healthcare, agriculture, education, smart cities, and smart mobility — as priority domains for AI deployment. The strategy emphasised the creation of a National AI Marketplace, a data‑sharing framework, and a skilling mission targeting one million AI professionals by 2025. In 2020, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) launched the “Responsible AI for Youth” programme to introduce AI curricula in schools, while the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C‑DAC) established the PARAM Siddhi‑AI supercomputer, delivering 5.26 petaflops of AI‑optimised compute for research and start‑ups.

The AI research ecosystem is anchored by centres of excellence at IITs, IISc, IIITs, and the newly formed Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Hyderabad’s AI‑ML hub. The “AI for All” initiative, a collaboration between MeitY and industry partners, provides open datasets (e.g., Indian language speech corpora, agricultural pest images) and benchmark challenges to stimulate indigenous model development. In 2022, the government approved the National Mission on Interdisciplinary Cyber‑Physical Systems (NM‑ICPS), which funds testbeds for AI‑enabled robotics, autonomous vehicles, and industrial IoT, linking academic research with defence and manufacturing requirements.

4.3 Cybersecurity, Data Governance and Emerging Technologies

The Information Technology Act, 2000, amended in 2008, provides the legal framework for electronic commerce, digital signatures, and cyber offences. The Computer Emergency Response Team‑India (CERT‑In), operational since 2004, coordinates incident response, issues advisories, and conducts security audits for critical information infrastructure. The Personal Data Protection Bill, introduced in 2019 and subsequently withdrawn in 2022, was replaced by the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, which establishes a Data Protection Board, defines consent‑based processing, and imposes penalties up to ₹250 crore for non‑compliance. The Act also recognises the concept of “significant data fiduciaries” subject to additional obligations such as data‑localisation for certain categories.

Emerging technologies such as blockchain, quantum computing, and 5G are being integrated into the national roadmap. The National Blockchain Framework (2021) pilots use‑cases in land records, pharmaceutical supply chains, and academic credential verification. The National Quantum Mission, approved in 2023 with a budget of ₹6,000 crore, targets development of quantum computers (50‑100 qubits), quantum communication networks (satellite‑based QKD), and quantum sensors for navigation. The 5G spectrum auction in 2022 allocated mid‑band (3.3‑3.67 GHz) and millimetre‑wave bands to telecom operators, enabling low‑latency connectivity essential for AI‑driven edge applications, industrial automation, and remote surgery.

5. Energy and Health Technology

5.1 Renewable Energy Expansion and Grid Integration

The Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission (JNNSM), launched in 2010, set an initial target of 20 GW of solar capacity by 2022, later revised to 100 GW as part of the 175 GW renewable energy goal (100 GW solar, 60 GW wind, 10 GW biomass, 5 GW small hydro). By March 2024, installed solar capacity exceeded 70 GW, driven by competitive reverse auctions that pushed tariffs below ₹2.5 per kWh. Wind energy, concentrated in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, and Maharashtra, crossed 45 GW, with the introduction of hybrid wind‑solar parks improving capacity utilisation. The Green Energy Corridor project, funded by the Power Grid Corporation and multilateral agencies, is constructing inter‑state transmission lines and substations to evacuate renewable power from resource‑rich states to load centres, addressing curtailment that peaked at 10 % in 2020.

Grid integration challenges are being mitigated through forecasting tools developed by the National Institute of Wind Energy (NIWE) and the Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI), which use satellite irradiance data and machine‑learning models to predict generation with a mean absolute percentage error below 5 %. Battery energy storage systems (BESS) are being piloted at 100 MW scale in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, while pumped hydro storage projects (e.g., 1,000 MW at Tehri) provide long‑duration balancing. The National Hydrogen Mission, launched in 2023, aims to produce 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen annually by 2030, leveraging electrolyser manufacturing incentives under the PLI scheme and creating demand in refineries, fertiliser plants, and steel mills.

5.2 Nuclear Power and Advanced Reactor Programmes

India’s three‑stage nuclear power programme, conceived by Homi Bhabha in the 1950s, progresses from pressurised heavy‑water reactors (PHWRs) using natural uranium, to fast breeder reactors (FBRs) breeding plutonium, and finally to thorium‑based reactors exploiting the vast thorium reserves. The first stage operates 22 PHWRs with a combined capacity of 7.5 GW, including the 700 MWe units at Kakrapar and Rawatbhata. The second stage is represented by the 500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) at Kalpakkam, which achieved first criticality in 2023 and is expected to reach commercial operation by 2025, demonstrating sodium‑cooled fast‑reactor technology and closed‑fuel‑cycle capabilities.

The third stage focuses on the Advanced Heavy Water Reactor (AHWR‑300LEU), a 300 MWe thorium‑fuelled design with passive safety features, currently in the detailed engineering phase. Simultaneously, India is a partner in the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) project, contributing the cryostat, in‑vessel components, and diagnostic systems, with the aim of mastering fusion technology for long‑term energy security. The Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010, and the subsequent amendment in 2015, provide a legal framework for operator liability and supplier recourse, facilitating foreign collaboration on reactor projects such as the six VVER‑1200 units at Kudankulam (units 3‑6 under construction).

5.3 Health Technology, Digital Health and Pandemic Preparedness

The Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM‑JAY), launched in 2018, provides health insurance coverage of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care to over 100 million vulnerable households. The scheme’s IT backbone, the Beneficiary Identification System (BIS) and the Transaction Management System (TMS), leverages Aadhaar‑based authentication and the ABDM health‑ID framework to enable cashless, paperless claims across empaneled hospitals. The National Digital Health Blueprint (2019) defined the architecture for a federated health information exchange, specifying standards for electronic health records (EHR), consent management, and interoperability (HL7 FHIR R4).

During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the CoWIN platform, built on the ABDM infrastructure, managed vaccine registration, scheduling, certification, and adverse‑event reporting for over 2 billion doses administered by 2023. The platform’s open APIs allowed integration with state‑level dashboards, private hospital systems, and third‑party analytics tools, demonstrating the scalability of a national digital health stack. The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) established the National Institute of Virology (NIV) as a WHO‑designated reference laboratory, which sequenced the SARS‑CoV‑2 genome early in 2020 and contributed to global variant surveillance. The pandemic also accelerated the adoption of telemedicine guidelines (2020), AI‑based radiology triage (e.g., qXR for chest X‑ray screening), and indigenous ventilator development (e.g., the “Prana” low‑cost ventilator by DRDO‑IIT collaboration). These innovations have been institutionalised into the National Health Stack, ensuring that the digital health ecosystem remains resilient for future public‑health emergencies.

The lesson concludes by emphasising that sustained investment in research institutions, regulatory clarity, and public‑private partnerships will determine whether India translates its scientific capabilities into inclusive economic growth and strategic resilience.

Space Situational Awareness and Debris Mitigation

India’s Space Situational Awareness (SSA) capability has moved from a research‑oriented activity to an operational service that protects both national assets and the global orbital environment. The ISRO‑led Network for Space Object Tracking and Analysis (NETRA) became fully functional in 2022, integrating a 0.5 m optical telescope at Mount Abu, a 1.2 m radar at Sriharikota, and a data‑fusion centre at Bengaluru. NETRA now catalogues objects down to 10 cm in low‑Earth orbit (LEO) and 1 m in geostationary orbit (GEO), providing conjunction‑assessment alerts to satellite operators with a lead time of 24–48 hours.

The 2020 Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines, issued by the Department of Space, mandate end‑of‑life disposal for all Indian spacecraft: controlled re‑entry for LEO platforms and graveyard‑orbit raising for GEO platforms. Compliance is verified through the Spacecraft Control Centre’s post‑mission disposal reports, which are submitted to the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (UN‑COPUOS) as part of India’s voluntary transparency measures. In 2023, the PSLV‑C55 mission demonstrated a passive de‑orbit sail on a 100 kg microsatellite, reducing its orbital lifetime from 25 years to under 5 years.

International cooperation is formalised through the Inter‑Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) and the Space Data Association (SDA). India contributes orbital data to the SDA’s pooled catalogue, enabling cross‑verification of conjunction predictions. The 2021 Memorandum of Understanding with the European Space Agency (ESA) established a joint working group on collision‑avoidance manoeuvre planning, leveraging ESA’s Space Debris Office expertise and ISRO’s real‑time tracking feeds.

Emerging challenges include the proliferation of mega‑constellations, which increase the conjunction rate by an order of magnitude. ISRO’s 2024 roadmap proposes a dedicated SSA satellite constellation — three 200 kg platforms in sun‑synchronous orbit — equipped with wide‑field optical sensors and on‑board machine‑learning filters to autonomously detect and characterise debris fragments down to 5 cm. Funding for the first demonstrator was approved under the 2023‑24 Space Technology Development Programme.

Key Policy Instruments

  • Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines (2020) – mandatory disposal timelines.
  • National Space Policy (2023) – SSA as a sovereign capability.
  • UN‑COPUOS voluntary reporting template (annual).

Hypersonic Weapon Development and Test Infrastructure

India’s hypersonic programme centres on the Hypersonic Technology Demonstrator Vehicle (HSTDV), an unmanned scramjet‑powered platform designed to validate sustained flight at Mach 6–7. The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) conducted the first successful flight test on 7 September 2020 from the Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam Island, achieving a 20‑second scramjet burn at 32 km altitude. A second test on 12 July 2022 extended the burn to 30 seconds and demonstrated autonomous guidance using an indigenous inertial navigation system coupled with a terrain‑referenced navigation module.

The test infrastructure includes the Hypersonic Wind Tunnel (HWT) at the Defence Research and Development Laboratory (DRDL), Hyderabad, capable of simulating Mach 8–12 flows for 100 ms durations, and the High Enthalpy Shock Tunnel (HEST) at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru, which provides 1 ms test windows at stagnation enthalpies up to 30 MJ/kg. These facilities support aerodynamic database generation for the BrahMos‑II cruise missile, a joint Indo‑Russian venture targeting Mach 7–8 with a range of 600 km. The BrahMos‑II design review in 2023 confirmed the use of a dual‑mode ramjet/scramjet combustor, with flight‑test articles slated for 2025.

Strategic implications are framed within the 2022 “No‑First‑Use” nuclear doctrine review, which acknowledges hypersonic delivery systems as a destabilising factor for regional deterrence. The National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS) commissioned a 2023 study on hypersonic threat perception, recommending the establishment of a dedicated Hypersonic Test Range (HTR) at the Integrated Test Range (ITR), Chandipur, to consolidate launch, tracking, and recovery operations. The HTR will feature a 300 km down‑range corridor with telemetry stations spaced at 50 km intervals, enabling full‑trajectory data capture for both scramjet and boost‑glide vehicles.

International benchmarking shows that India’s scramjet flight‑test success rate (2 out of 3 attempts) is comparable to early US X‑51A and Chinese DF‑17 programmes. However, the lack of a reusable test vehicle limits iterative design cycles. The 2024 DRDO roadmap proposes a reusable “Hypersonic Testbed” (HTB) based on a modified sounding rocket first stage, aiming for a 10‑flight campaign by 2028 to accelerate thermal‑protection‑system qualification.

Critical Milestones

  • HSTDV first flight – 7 Sep 2020 (Mach 6, 20 s burn).
  • HSTDV second flight – 12 Jul 2022 (Mach 6.5, 30 s burn).
  • BrahMos‑II CDR completion – 2023.
  • HTR sanction – 2024 (construction start 2025).
Examiner's Twist
A question asks: “Which Indian facility provides the longest continuous hypersonic flow simulation for scramjet testing?” Options include the Hypersonic Wind Tunnel (DRDL), High Enthalpy Shock Tunnel (IISc), and the proposed Hypersonic Test Range (Chandipur). Identify the correct answer and justify.
The High Enthalpy Shock Tunnel (HEST) at IISc, Bengaluru, offers the highest stagnation enthalpy (up to 30 MJ/kg) but only 1 ms test time. The Hypersonic Wind Tunnel (HWT) at DRDL provides longer duration (100 ms) at lower enthalpy (Mach 8–12). The Hypersonic Test Range is a flight‑test corridor, not a ground‑based flow simulator. Therefore, for “longest continuous hypersonic flow simulation” the HWT at DRDL is the correct choice because it delivers the longest steady‑state test window among existing ground facilities.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes ground‑test facilities with flight‑test ranges, first classify each option as “ground‑based simulator” or “flight corridor”. The examiner often expects you to pick the facility that matches the specific performance metric (duration, enthalpy, Mach number). Memorise the key parameters: HWT – 100 ms, Mach 8–12; HEST – 1 ms, 30 MJ/kg; HTR – 300 km corridor, telemetry every 50 km.

Synthetic Biology and Biosafety Governance

Synthetic biology in India is governed by a layered regulatory architecture that combines the 1989 Environment (Protection) Act, the 2013 Guidelines for Genetically Engineered Organisms (GEOs), and the 2021 “National Guidelines for Gene Editing and Synthetic Biology” issued by the Department of Biotechnology (DBT). The 2021 guidelines introduce a risk‑based classification: Level 1 (contained laboratory work with non‑pathogenic chassis), Level 2 (environmental release of engineered microbes for bioremediation), and Level 3 (human‑therapeutic applications). Each level requires escalating oversight from Institutional Biosafety Committees (IBSCs) to the Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation (RCGM) and, for Level 3, the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC).

The DBT’s 2022 “Synthetic Biology Mission” allocated ₹1,200 crore over five years to establish three Synthetic Biology Foundries — at the National Centre for Biological Sciences (NCBS) Bengaluru, the Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology (IGIB) Delhi, and the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (CCMB) Hyderabad. These foundries provide standardized BioBrick parts, automated DNA assembly platforms, and high‑throughput phenotyping pipelines. The mission also funds the “iGEM India” consortium, which has produced 12 gold‑medal projects since 2018, focusing on arsenic biosensors, nitrogen‑fixing cereals, and biodegradable plastic precursors.

Biosafety incidents have shaped policy revisions. The 2019 accidental release of a genetically modified *E. coli* strain from a university lab in Pune prompted the DBT to mandate electronic laboratory notebooks (eLNs) with immutable audit trails for all Level 2 and Level 3 work. The 2020 amendment to the Rules for the Manufacture, Use, Import, Export and Storage of Hazardous Microorganisms/Genetically Engineered Organisms (1989) introduced a “dual‑use” review clause, requiring principal investigators to declare potential misuse pathways for any engineered organism with pathogenicity‑enhancing traits.

International alignment is pursued through the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Guidance Framework for Human Genome Editing (2021) and the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) Cartagena Protocol. India’s 2023 submission to the CBD’s Ad Hoc Technical Expert Group on Synthetic Biology highlighted the need for a globally harmonised “sequence‑of‑concern” database, a proposal that was adopted in the 2024 meeting of the Parties.

Regulatory Decision Flow

  • Project proposal → IBSC risk assessment → RCGM (Level 1/2) or GEAC (Level 3).
  • Dual‑use declaration → National Biosecurity Committee (NBC) review.
  • Environmental release → State Biodiversity Board concurrence.

Quantum Communication and Computing Roadmap

The National Quantum Mission (NQM), approved by the Union Cabinet in April 2023 with a budget of ₹6,003 crore for 2023‑2031, structures India’s quantum technology development across four verticals: quantum communication, quantum computing, quantum sensing & metrology, and quantum materials & devices. The mission is steered by the Department of Science and Technology (DST) with implementation partners including the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), the Raman Research Institute (RRI), the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR), and the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C‑DAC).

In quantum communication, the 2022 demonstration of a 300 km fibre‑based Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) link between Delhi and Hisar, using decoy‑state BB84 protocol and trusted‑node relays, established a national testbed. The 2023 satellite‑based QKD experiment, conducted jointly with the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) on the GSAT‑30 platform, achieved a secure key rate of 1 kbps over a 1,200 km free‑space link, validating the feasibility of a future Quantum Satellite Constellation (QSC) for pan‑India coverage. The NQM targets a 2,000 km trusted‑node network by 2026 and a fully satellite‑based QKD backbone by 2030.

Quantum computing efforts focus on superconducting transmon qubits and trapped‑ion platforms. The 2024 milestone of a 27‑qubit superconducting processor at TIFR, with a two‑qubit gate fidelity of 99.2 % and coherence times exceeding 80 µs, positions India among the few nations with a >20‑qubit device. Parallelly, the RRI trapped‑ion system demonstrated a 12‑qubit register with 99.9 % single‑qubit fidelity. The mission’s software stack, “QSim”, developed by C‑DAC, provides a hardware‑agnostic simulation environment and a compiler targeting both architectures.

Quantum sensing applications include a chip‑scale atomic magnetometer for defence navigation (DRDO‑IIT Delhi collaboration, 2023) and a gravity gradiometer for underground resource mapping (NGRI‑IISc, 2024). The NQM also funds a Quantum Materials Foundry at the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science (IACS), Kolkata, to grow high‑purity 2D materials (hexagonal boron nitride, transition‑metal dichalcogenides) essential for low‑loss Josephson junctions.

Mission Targets (2023‑2031)

  • Quantum communication: 2,000 km trusted‑node network (2026); satellite QKD backbone (2030).
  • Quantum computing: 50‑qubit processor with error‑corrected logical qubit (2028); 100‑qubit fault‑tolerant system (2031).
  • Quantum sensing: Deployable atomic clock (10⁻¹⁵ stability) for strategic use (2027).
  • Human resource: 2,500 PhDs and 5,000 trained professionals by 2031.

Advanced Nuclear Fuel Cycle and Waste Management

India’s closed‑fuel‑cycle strategy relies on reprocessing spent fuel from Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) to extract plutonium for Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs) and, eventually, thorium‑based reactors. The Nuclear Fuel Complex (NFC), Hyderabad, fabricates PHWR fuel bundles, while the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) operates the Power Reactor Fuel Reprocessing Plant (PREFRE) at Tarapur (capacity 100 tHM/yr) and the Fast Reactor Fuel Reprocessing Plant (FRFRP) at Kalpakkam (capacity 30 tHM/yr). The 2023 commissioning of the Integrated Nuclear Recycle Plant (INRP) at Tarapur, designed for 200 tHM/yr, marks a shift toward co‑processing of PHWR and FBR spent fuel streams.

High‑level waste (HLW) vitrification is performed at the Waste Immobilisation Plant (WIP), Trombay, using a borosilicate glass matrix. The plant has immobilised over 1,200 m³ of HLW since 1995, with a current throughput of 30 m³/yr. The 2021 “National Policy on Radioactive Waste Management” mandates a deep geological repository (DGR) for HLW and long‑lived intermediate‑level waste (ILW). Site characterisation studies are underway in the granitic formations of the Chhattisgarh‑Odisha border, with a target repository licence application by 2028 and operational readiness by 2035.

Advanced fuel development includes the Mixed Oxide (MOX) fuel for the Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) and the Thorium‑Plutonium MOX for the Advanced Heavy Water Reactor (AHWR‑300LEU). The 2022 irradiation test of (Th,Pu)O₂ pins in the Dhruva research reactor achieved a burn‑up of 60 GWd/tHM, confirming the feasibility of thorium utilisation in a thermal spectrum. The AHWR design incorporates passive safety systems — gravity‑driven water injection, passive decay heat removal, and a negative void coefficient — meeting Generation‑III+ safety criteria.

Regulatory oversight is exercised by the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB) under the Atomic Energy Act, 1962, and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010 (amended 2015). The 2023 amendment to the AERB Safety Code for Fuel Cycle Facilities introduced a requirement for periodic probabilistic safety assessment (PSA) Level 2 for reprocessing plants, aligning with IAEA SSG‑44 guidance. International cooperation includes the 2020 agreement with France’s Orano for technical exchange on advanced reprocessing flowsheets and the 2022 joint working group with Russia’s Rosatom on MOX fuel qualification for VVER‑1200 reactors at Kudankulam.

Fuel Cycle Flow Summary

  • Uranium mining (UCIL) → NFC fuel fabrication → PHWR operation → PREFRE/FRFRP reprocessing → Pu extraction.
  • Pu + depleted U → MOX fuel → PFBR (FBR) → FRFRP reprocessing → Pu recycle.
  • Thorium blanket in AHWR → U‑233 breeding → Th‑Pu MOX → AHWR closed cycle.
  • HLW → WIP vitrification → Interim storage → DGR (future).
