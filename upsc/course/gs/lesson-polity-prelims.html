1. Constitutional Framework and Historical Evolution

1.1 The Making of the Constitution

The Constitution of India emerged from a prolonged process of deliberation that began with the demand for a sovereign constituent assembly during the freedom struggle and culminated in the adoption of the document on 26 November 1949. The Constituent Assembly, composed of representatives elected by provincial legislatures, met for the first time on 9 December 1946 and functioned through eleven sessions spread over almost three years. Its debates reflected a synthesis of indigenous political thought, British parliamentary practice, and the constitutional experiences of the United States, Canada, and Ireland. The drafting committee, chaired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar, prepared the final text after incorporating more than two thousand amendments moved by members. The Assembly’s decision to adopt a written, rigid constitution with a preamble declaring India a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic set the normative foundation for the entire legal order. The historical context of partition, communal violence, and the integration of princely states compelled the framers to embed strong centralising features while simultaneously guaranteeing fundamental rights to protect minorities.

1.2 The Preamble and Its Significance

The Preamble serves as the philosophical key to the Constitution, encapsulating the objectives of justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity. It declares the source of authority as the people of India, thereby establishing popular sovereignty as the bedrock of the polity. The words “socialist” and “secular” were inserted by the Forty‑second Amendment in 1976, reflecting the evolving ideological orientation of the state. Courts have repeatedly held that the Preamble is not merely a decorative preface but an interpretive aid; in the Kesavananda Bharati case the Supreme Court ruled that the basic structure of the Constitution cannot be altered even by a constitutional amendment, and the Preamble’s ideals form part of that basic structure. Consequently, any legislative or executive action that undermines the declared objectives can be struck down as unconstitutional. The Preamble also guides the interpretation of fundamental rights and directive principles, ensuring that the Constitution remains a living document responsive to the aspirations of the citizenry.

1.3 Salient Features of the Constitution

The Indian Constitution is distinguished by its length, detail, and blend of rigidity and flexibility. It contains 395 articles in 22 parts and 12 schedules, making it one of the longest written constitutions in the world. The document establishes a parliamentary system of government at both the centre and the states, with a ceremonial President and a real executive headed by the Prime Minister. Federalism is enshrined through a clear division of legislative powers in the Seventh Schedule, yet the centre possesses overriding authority in matters of national importance, emergency provisions, and the power to reorganise states. The Constitution guarantees six categories of fundamental rights, enforceable by the judiciary, and enumerates directive principles that, while non‑justiciable, direct state policy. An independent judiciary with the power of judicial review, a comprehensive amendment procedure under Article 368, and provisions for emergency rule further characterize the framework. These features collectively create a system that balances unity with diversity, stability with adaptability.

1.4 The Doctrine of Basic Structure

The basic structure doctrine, articulated in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati judgment of 1973, limits the amending power of Parliament by declaring that certain core features of the Constitution cannot be altered. The Supreme Court identified elements such as the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law as constituting the basic structure. Subsequent cases, including Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and Minerva Mills (1980), refined the doctrine by adding judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles. The doctrine operates as a constitutional safeguard against majoritarian excesses, ensuring that amendments do not destroy the identity of the Constitution. It also empowers the judiciary to review constitutional amendments, a power that has been exercised sparingly but decisively. The basic structure doctrine thus functions as a constitutional “immune system,” preserving the foundational values against transient political pressures.

2. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

2.1 Classification of Fundamental Rights

The Constitution groups fundamental rights into six categories: right to equality (Articles 14‑18), right to freedom (Articles 19‑22), right against exploitation (Articles 23‑24), right to freedom of religion (Articles 25‑28), cultural and educational rights (Articles 29‑30), and right to constitutional remedies (Article 32). Each category addresses a distinct dimension of individual liberty and collective dignity. The right to equality prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth and empowers the state to make special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes. The right to freedom guarantees six freedoms, including speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession, subject to reasonable restrictions imposed in the interest of sovereignty, public order, and morality. The right against exploitation abolishes traffic in human beings, forced labour, and child labour in hazardous occupations. Freedom of religion protects the conscience of individuals and the autonomy of religious denominations. Cultural and educational rights safeguard the interests of minorities to conserve language, script, and culture and to establish educational institutions. Finally, the right to constitutional remedies empowers the Supreme Court and High Courts to issue writs for the enforcement of all other fundamental rights, making the judiciary the ultimate guardian of the charter.

2.2 Judicial Review and Landmark Cases

Judicial review is the mechanism through which courts examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive actions. The power derives implicitly from Article 13, which declares that any law inconsistent with fundamental rights shall be void, and explicitly from Article 32 and 226, which confer writ jurisdiction on the Supreme Court and High Courts. In A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), the Court initially adopted a narrow, literal interpretation of “procedure established by law,” but this was overturned in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court held that the procedure must be fair, just, and reasonable, thereby importing due‑process principles into Article 21. The Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) established the basic structure doctrine, limiting Parliament’s amending power. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court laid down guidelines for the imposition of President’s rule under Article 356, reinforcing federalism. More recently, the Navtej Singh Johar judgment (2018) decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, expanding the scope of dignity and privacy under Article 21. These cases illustrate how judicial review dynamically shapes the contours of fundamental rights in response to evolving societal values.

2.3 Directive Principles of State Policy

Part IV of the Constitution (Articles 36‑51) contains the Directive Principles of State Policy, which embody the socio‑economic aspirations of the framers. Unlike fundamental rights, these principles are not enforceable by any court, yet Article 37 declares them fundamental in the governance of the country and obliges the state to apply them in making laws. They are classified into three broad groups: social and economic principles (e.g., adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, right to work, education, and public assistance), Gandhian principles (e.g., promotion of cottage industries, prohibition of intoxicating drinks, organisation of village panchayats), and liberal‑intellectual principles (e.g., uniform civil code, separation of judiciary from executive, promotion of international peace). The principles have guided landmark legislation such as the Minimum Wages Act, the Right to Education Act, and the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act. Although non‑justiciable, courts have occasionally read them into fundamental rights to expand the scope of Article 21, as seen in the Unni Krishnan case (1993) where the right to education was derived from the directive principle in Article 41.

2.4 Relationship Between Rights and Principles

The interplay between fundamental rights and directive principles has been a central theme in constitutional jurisprudence. Initially, the Supreme Court in the Champakam Dorairajan case (1951) held that directive principles cannot override fundamental rights. However, the Twenty‑fifth Amendment (1971) inserted Article 31C, giving primacy to laws implementing certain directive principles over Articles 14 and 19. The Kesavananda Bharati judgment upheld this amendment only to the extent that it did not damage the basic structure. In Minerva Mills (1980), the Court struck down the clause that gave blanket precedence to all directive principles, reaffirming that a harmonious construction must be sought. The current position is that fundamental rights and directive principles are complementary; the state must strive to achieve the goals of the directives while respecting the inviolable core of fundamental rights. This balance ensures that socio‑economic reforms do not come at the cost of civil liberties, and that the Constitution remains a charter of both liberty and justice.

Example Question
A state legislature passes a law that provides for compulsory acquisition of private agricultural land for a private industrial project, offering compensation determined by the state government without any judicial review. A group of affected farmers challenges the law under Article 300A (property right) and Article 21 (right to life). Discuss the constitutional validity of the law, referring to relevant provisions and case law.
The law must be examined under the framework of Article 300A, which provides that no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. The phrase “authority of law” requires a validly enacted statute that follows a fair, just, and reasonable procedure, as established in the Maneka Gandhi case. Since the compensation is fixed by the executive without any independent assessment or judicial oversight, the procedure lacks fairness and violates the requirement of due process implicit in Article 21, which has been expanded to include the right to livelihood and shelter (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation). Moreover, the acquisition for a private industrial project raises the question of “public purpose” under the Land Acquisition Act, 2013, which mandates that acquisition must serve a public purpose and that consent of 70% of affected families is required for private projects. The absence of such safeguards renders the law constitutionally suspect. The Supreme Court in the K. T. Plantation case (2011) held that arbitrary fixation of compensation without reference to market value violates Article 300A. Therefore, the law is likely to be struck down as unconstitutional for violating both Article 300A and Article 21.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks you to evaluate the validity of a law affecting property, always check whether the statute provides for a fair procedure, independent valuation, and a genuine public purpose; the combination of Article 300A and the expanded Article 21 jurisprudence makes procedural fairness the decisive factor.

3. Union Executive and Legislature

3.1 The President and the Council of Ministers

The President of India is the constitutional head of the Union, elected by an electoral college consisting of elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the Legislative Assemblies of the States. Although the President exercises formal executive power, the real executive authority rests with the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, as mandated by Article 74. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council, which is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. The President’s discretionary powers are limited to situations such as the appointment of the Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority, the dissolution of the Lok Sabha on the advice of a defeated government, and the referral of bills for reconsideration. The President also possesses veto powers: absolute veto over money bills (which cannot be returned), suspensive veto over ordinary bills, and pocket veto by simply withholding assent indefinitely. The office thus embodies a ceremonial symbol of unity while the parliamentary executive ensures democratic accountability.

3.2 Parliament: Composition and Powers

Parliament consists of the President and two Houses: the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha). The Rajya Sabha represents the states and union territories, with members elected by the legislative assemblies of the states through proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote. The Lok Sabha is directly elected by adult suffrage from territorial constituencies. Parliament’s legislative powers extend to all subjects in the Union List and the Concurrent List, and, under certain circumstances, to the State List. It controls the executive through questions, motions, and committees, and holds the power of the purse by approving the annual budget and supplementary grants. The Parliament also has constituent functions, including the power to amend the Constitution under Article 368, and judicial functions such as the impeachment of the President and removal of judges. Its role as a forum for national debate makes it the central institution of representative democracy.

3.3 Legislative Process and Money Bills

A bill may originate in either House, except a Money Bill which can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha on the recommendation of the President. After passage by one House, the bill is transmitted to the other House, which may pass, reject, or amend it. In case of disagreement, a joint sitting may be summoned by the President under Article 108, except for Money Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills. A Money Bill, defined in Article 110, deals exclusively with taxation, borrowing, expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, and related financial matters. The Rajya Sabha cannot amend a Money Bill; it may only make recommendations, which the Lok Sabha may accept or reject. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies whether a bill is a Money Bill, and this certification is final. The President’s assent is required for all bills, but for Money Bills the President cannot withhold assent. This procedure ensures that the directly elected House retains primacy over financial legislation, reflecting the principle of fiscal accountability.

3.4 Parliamentary Committees and Accountability

Parliamentary committees are the engine of detailed scrutiny, enabling Parliament to examine legislation, expenditure, and administration beyond the constraints of floor debates. Standing committees such as the Public Accounts Committee, the Estimates Committee, and the Committee on Public Undertakings examine government accounts and audit reports, ensuring financial accountability. Department‑related standing committees, reconstituted annually, scrutinise the demands for grants, bills, and policy documents of specific ministries. Ad hoc committees are constituted for specific inquiries, such as the Joint Parliamentary Committee on the 2G spectrum allocation. Committee reports, though not binding, carry significant moral and political weight; the government is expected to respond to recommendations. The committee system also provides a platform for opposition members to influence policy, thereby strengthening the checks‑and‑balances architecture of the parliamentary system.

4. State Executive and Legislature

4.1 The Governor and the State Council of Ministers

The Governor is the constitutional head of a state, appointed by the President on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers. The Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President and acts as the representative of the centre in the state. Executive power of the state is vested in the Governor, but it is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister, mirroring the Union pattern. The Governor’s discretionary powers include the appointment of the Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority, the power to reserve certain bills for the President’s consideration, and the authority to report to the President on the failure of constitutional machinery under Article 356. The Governor also summons, prorogues, and dissolves the state legislature. While the office is intended to be a neutral constitutional authority, political controversies have arisen over the use of discretionary powers, prompting judicial guidelines in cases such as S. R. Bommai to prevent misuse.

4.2 State Legislature: Structure and Functions

Most states have a unicameral legislature consisting of the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha), while a few possess a bicameral structure with a Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad) in addition to the Assembly. Members of the Assembly are directly elected from territorial constituencies, whereas Council members are elected by local bodies, graduates, teachers, and members of the Assembly, with some nominated by the Governor. The state legislature exercises legislative power over subjects in the State List and the Concurrent List, subject to the supremacy of Union law in case of conflict. It controls the state executive through questions, motions, and the passage of the budget. The Assembly alone can pass Money Bills, and the Council can only delay non‑Money Bills for a maximum of three months. The legislature also participates in the amendment of the Constitution by ratifying certain amendments under Article 368.

4.3 Centre‑State Legislative Relations

The distribution of legislative powers is governed by the Seventh Schedule, which delineates the Union List, State List, and Concurrent List. Parliament has exclusive authority over the Union List, while state legislatures have exclusive authority over the State List. Both can legislate on Concurrent List subjects, but in case of repugnancy, the Union law prevails unless the state law has been reserved for the President’s assent and received it. Parliament can also legislate on State List subjects under five extraordinary circumstances: national interest (Article 249), emergency (Article 250), request by two or more states (Article 252), implementation of international treaties (Article 253), and President’s rule (Article 356). These provisions create a strong centre, justified by the need for national integrity, economic unity, and effective implementation of international obligations. The Sarkaria Commission and the Punchhi Commission have recommended safeguards to prevent excessive centralisation, emphasizing consultation and cooperative federalism.

4.4 Administrative Relations and All India Services

Administrative relations between the centre and the states are structured to ensure compliance with Union laws while preserving state autonomy in day‑to‑day governance. The Union can issue directions to states under Articles 256 and 257 for the implementation of Union laws and for the maintenance of means of communication and railway protection. The All India Services — Indian Administrative Service, Indian Police Service, and Indian Forest Service — are recruited and trained by the Union but serve under state governments, providing a cadre of officers with a national perspective. The Union also controls the appointment and transfer of High Court judges, the Comptroller and Auditor General, and the Election Commission, reinforcing institutional uniformity. The Inter‑State Council, established under Article 263, serves as a forum for discussion of common interests, though its recommendations are advisory. These mechanisms aim to balance the need for a cohesive administrative framework with the federal principle of state self‑governance.

5. Federalism, Distribution of Powers and Amendments

5.1 Distribution of Legislative Powers: Union, State and Concurrent Lists

The Seventh Schedule is the constitutional blueprint for legislative federalism. The Union List contains 97 entries covering subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, citizenship, and interstate trade. The State List comprises 66 entries dealing with local matters like police, public health, agriculture, and local government. The Concurrent List includes 47 entries where both Parliament and state legislatures may legislate, including criminal law, marriage, bankruptcy, and economic planning. In the event of a conflict between a Union law and a state law on a Concurrent subject, the Union law prevails under Article 254, unless the state law has been reserved for the President’s assent and received it. The residuary power of legislation, covering any subject not enumerated in the three lists, vests in Parliament under Article 248. This distribution reflects a quasi‑federal design: a strong centre with sufficient flexibility for states to address regional needs, while the Concurrent List enables cooperative legislation on matters of common concern.

5.2 Financial Relations and the Finance Commission

Financial federalism is mediated through the distribution of tax revenues and grants‑in‑aid. The Constitution divides taxing powers: the Union levies taxes on income (except agricultural income), customs, excise on goods manufactured or produced, and corporation tax; states levy taxes on land, agricultural income, sales tax (now subsumed under GST), and state excise. The Goods and Services Tax (GST), introduced by the One Hundred and First Amendment in 2016, created a unified indirect tax regime with a dual structure — Central GST and State GST — and an Integrated GST for inter‑state supplies. The Finance Commission, constituted every five years under Article 280, recommends the vertical share of Union taxes to states and the horizontal distribution among states based on criteria such as population, area, fiscal capacity, and fiscal discipline. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended a 41% vertical devolution and introduced performance‑based incentives. Grants‑in‑aid under Article 275 supplement the resources of states with special needs. This architecture seeks to balance equity, efficiency, and autonomy in fiscal matters.

5.3 Constitutional Amendments: Procedure and Major Amendments

Article 368 prescribes the procedure for amending the Constitution. An amendment bill must be passed by a special majority — a majority of the total membership of each House and a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting — in both Houses of Parliament. Certain amendments affecting the federal structure, such as changes to the representation of states in Parliament, the extent of executive power, or the provisions of the Seventh Schedule, additionally require ratification by at least half of the state legislatures. The Constitution has been amended 105 times as of 2023. Major amendments include the First Amendment (1951) placing reasonable restrictions on freedom of speech, the Twenty‑fourth Amendment (1971) affirming Parliament’s power to amend any part of the Constitution, the Forty‑second Amendment (1976) — known as the “Mini‑Constitution” — which altered the Preamble, curtailed judicial review, and extended the term of Lok Sabha, and the Forty‑fourth Amendment (1978) which restored many pre‑Emergency provisions. The Seventy‑third and Seventy‑fourth Amendments (1992) constitutionalised Panchayati Raj institutions and urban local bodies. The One Hundred and First Amendment (2016) introduced GST. Each amendment reflects the evolving political consensus and the tension between flexibility and the preservation of the basic structure.

5.4 Emerging Issues in Federal Balance

Contemporary federal dynamics are shaped by several emerging issues. The implementation of GST has altered the fiscal autonomy of states, prompting demands for a larger share of the GST compensation cess and greater representation in the GST Council. The use of Article 356 for President’s rule has declined after the S. R. Bommai guidelines, but political pressures persist in states with fractured mandates. The creation of new states, such as Telangana in 2014, raises questions about the criteria for reorganisation and the impact on administrative efficiency. The National Education Policy 2020 and the proposed National Medical Commission illustrate the centre’s increasing role in sectors traditionally under state control, justified by the Concurrent List. The Inter‑State Water Disputes Act and the River Boards Act highlight the need for cooperative mechanisms to manage shared natural resources. Finally, the debate over a uniform civil code under Article 44 intersects with personal law, a state subject, testing the limits of legislative competence. These issues underscore that federalism in India is a living negotiation rather than a static division of powers.

The Constitution thus provides a comprehensive framework that balances unity with diversity, rigidity with flexibility, and central authority with regional autonomy, enabling the world’s largest democracy to adapt while preserving its foundational values.

Standards of Judicial Review: Wednesbury, Proportionality and Legitimate Expectation

Indian courts have moved beyond the traditional Wednesbury unreasonableness test imported from English administrative law. The Supreme Court in Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000) held that the Wednesbury standard — whether a decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have made it — is insufficient for reviewing executive action that affects fundamental rights. Instead, the Court adopted a proportionality analysis requiring that the measure be suitable, necessary and balanced in its impact on the right concerned. This three‑limbed test was refined in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) where the Court linked proportionality to the reasonableness requirement under Article 14 and the due process dimension of Article 21.

A parallel doctrine, legitimate expectation, protects individuals who have been led by a consistent administrative practice or an explicit promise to expect a particular benefit. In State of Punjab v. Ram Singh (1992) the Court ruled that a legitimate expectation arises when a public authority makes a clear representation, the claimant relies on it, and it would be unfair to frustrate that reliance. The doctrine does not create a substantive right but imposes a procedural fairness obligation: the authority must hear the affected person before withdrawing the benefit.

Emergency Provisions: National Emergency, State Emergency and Financial Emergency

The Constitution envisages three distinct emergency regimes, each with its own triggering conditions, parliamentary safeguards and judicial oversight mechanisms.

National Emergency (Article 352)

A proclamation of national emergency can be issued only on the grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion. The President must act on the written advice of the Union Cabinet. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority (majority of total membership and two‑thirds of members present and voting). Once approved, it remains in force for six months and can be extended indefinitely with repeated parliamentary approvals every six months. During a national emergency, the Centre acquires the power to legislate on State List subjects (Article 250), the life of the Lok Sabha can be extended by one year at a time (Article 83), and fundamental rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended (Article 358). The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) held that the proclamation is subject to judicial review on the ground of mala fides or irrelevance, but the Court refrains from reviewing the subjective satisfaction of the President.

State Emergency / President’s Rule (Article 356)

President’s Rule may be imposed when the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor or otherwise, is satisfied that the government of a state cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months by a simple majority. It remains in force for six months and can be extended up to a maximum of three years, provided that after the first year the extension requires a certification by the Election Commission that elections cannot be held. The landmark S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) judgment laid down stringent guidelines: the proclamation is justiciable; the material on which the President acted must be placed before Parliament; the State Legislative Assembly cannot be dissolved before parliamentary approval; and secularism being a basic feature, a government pursuing anti‑secular policies can be dismissed. The Court also held that the imposition of President’s Rule for political reasons constitutes a fraud on the Constitution.

Financial Emergency (Article 360)

A financial emergency can be proclaimed if the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India or any part thereof is threatened. It must be approved by Parliament within two months by a simple majority. During its operation, the Union can direct states to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the state, and require all money bills to be reserved for the President’s consideration. To date, Article 360 has never been invoked.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which emergency provision allows the Centre to legislate on State List subjects without the State’s consent?” Many aspirants answer “President’s Rule (Art. 356)”. The correct answer is “National Emergency (Art. 352)”, because under Art. 250 Parliament gains concurrent legislative power over State List matters only during a national emergency.
National Emergency under Article 352 triggers Article 250, empowering Parliament to legislate on State List subjects. President’s Rule under Article 356 merely enables the President to assume the functions of the state government and the powers of the state legislature to be exercised by Parliament, but it does not automatically confer legislative competence over State List subjects in the same manner.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the three emergencies in a table: triggering ground, parliamentary approval majority, maximum duration, and key judicial precedent (Minerva Mills for Art. 352; S. R. Bommai for Art. 356; none for Art. 360). A quick recall of this matrix eliminates confusion between “special majority” and “simple majority” requirements.

Cooperative Federalism in Practice: Inter‑State Council, Zonal Councils and the GST Council

Beyond the formal distribution of legislative and financial powers, the Constitution provides institutional mechanisms for routine Centre‑State dialogue. Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter‑State Council to inquire into and advise upon disputes between states, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination. The Council, chaired by the Prime Minister and comprising Chief Ministers of all states and Union Territories with legislatures, plus six Union Cabinet Ministers, meets periodically; its recommendations are advisory but carry political weight. The Zonal Councils, created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, group states into five zones (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern) to foster regional cooperation on issues such as law and order, water sharing, and infrastructure. Each Zonal Council is chaired by the Union Home Minister and includes the Chief Ministers of the constituent states.

The Goods and Services Tax Council (Article 279A), introduced by the 101st Amendment, is the most consequential cooperative body in recent times. It comprises the Union Finance Minister (Chair), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and the Finance Ministers of all states. Decisions are taken by a three‑fourths weighted majority, with the Centre holding one‑third of the vote and states collectively two‑thirds. The Council determines tax rates, exemptions, threshold limits, and the model GST law. Its consensus‑based functioning has been tested by disputes over compensation cess, rate rationalisation, and the inclusion of petroleum products. The Fifteenth Finance Commission’s recommendation to link a portion of vertical devolution to GST performance further intertwines fiscal federalism with cooperative decision‑making.

These bodies illustrate a shift from “competitive federalism” — where states vie for central resources — to “cooperative federalism” where institutionalised negotiation replaces ad‑hoc bargaining. However, the effectiveness of the Inter‑State Council has been limited by irregular meetings (only 12 meetings since 1990) and the non‑binding nature of its advice. The Zonal Councils, while more frequent, often focus on law‑and‑order rather than developmental issues. The GST Council, by contrast, meets monthly and its decisions have direct revenue impact, making it the most operational forum for federal bargaining today.

Independent Constitutional Bodies: Appointment, Tenure, Removal and Functional Autonomy

The Constitution insulates several key institutions from executive interference through specific appointment procedures, fixed tenures, and removal processes that require parliamentary super‑majorities or judicial inquiry.

Election Commission (Article 324)

The Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and Election Commissioners are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister‑led Council of Ministers. Since the 2023 amendment to the Election Commission (Conditions of Service) Act, the CEC and Commissioners hold office for a term of six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier. Removal of the CEC requires a motion passed by both Houses of Parliament by a special majority on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity — the same standard as a Supreme Court judge. The other Commissioners can be removed only on the recommendation of the CEC.

Comptroller and Auditor General (Article 148)

The CAG is appointed by the President for a term of six years or until age 65. The CAG can be removed only by an address of both Houses of Parliament supported by a special majority on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The CAG’s reports are laid before Parliament and state legislatures, ensuring legislative oversight of executive expenditure.

Union Public Service Commission (Article 315)

The Chairman and members of the UPSC are appointed by the President. They hold office for a term of six years or until age 62. Removal follows the same special‑majority parliamentary address procedure as the CAG. The UPSC’s recommendations on recruitment, promotions and disciplinary matters are advisory but the government is constitutionally bound to consult it.

National Commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes (Articles 338, 338A, 338B)

Each Commission consists of a Chairperson, Vice‑Chairperson and three other members appointed by the President. They serve for a term of three years. Removal requires a presidential order after a Supreme Court inquiry on grounds of misbehaviour or incapacity. These Commissions investigate safeguards, monitor implementation of protective legislation, and advise on policy formulation.

Functional autonomy is reinforced by budgetary independence: the expenses of the Election Commission, CAG and UPSC are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, not subject to annual parliamentary voting. Nevertheless, concerns persist about the executive’s influence in appointments, especially for the Election Commission, where the lack of a collegium‑style selection mechanism has been flagged by the Supreme Court in Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023) as a potential threat to institutional independence.

Parliamentary Privileges, Contempt of Parliament and Judicial Oversight

Articles 105 (for Parliament) and 194 (for State Legislatures) confer two broad categories of privileges: freedom of speech in the House and immunity from legal proceedings for anything said or any vote cast; and immunity from arrest in civil cases during the session and 40 days before and after it. The Houses also possess the power to punish for contempt — any act that obstructs or impedes the performance of their functions — which includes publishing false reports of proceedings, assaulting members, or refusing to attend as a witness.

The scope of these privileges is not codified; they are derived from the powers of the British House of Commons as they stood on 26 January 1950, subject to constitutional limitations. The Supreme Court in Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly (1965) held that the judiciary can examine whether a privilege claimed by the House exists under Article 105, but cannot review the House’s internal disciplinary decisions. In Raja Ram Pal v. Hon’ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007) the Court asserted that parliamentary privilege is subject to fundamental rights; a member’s expulsion for “cash‑for‑questions” was upheld because the House’s power to expel is a part of its privilege, yet the Court retained the authority to test the procedural fairness of the expulsion.

Key distinctions:

  • Privilege vs. Contempt: Privilege is a right or immunity enjoyed by members; contempt is an offence against the authority of the House.
  • Judicial Review: Courts may determine the existence and extent of a privilege (justiciability) but generally refrain from reviewing the merits of a contempt punishment.
  • Codification: The Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959 and the Rules of Procedure provide procedural frameworks, but a comprehensive codification has been repeatedly recommended (e.g., by the 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission) and remains pending.

These principles ensure that legislatures can function without external intimidation while preventing the misuse of privilege to shield corruption or suppress dissent.

  • Confusing the special‑majority requirement for national emergency (Art. 352) with the simple majority for President’s Rule (Art. 356).
  • Assuming the GST Council’s decisions require unanimity; the Constitution mandates a three‑fourths weighted majority.
  • Treating the Election Commission’s removal procedure as identical to that of the CAG; the CEC needs a special‑majority address, while other Commissioners are removed on the CEC’s recommendation.
  • Believing that legitimate expectation creates a substantive right enforceable as a fundamental right; it only imposes a procedural fairness duty.
  • Thinking that parliamentary privilege is absolute; the Supreme Court has held it subject to fundamental rights and justiciable on the question of existence.
  • Overlooking that the Inter‑State Council’s recommendations are advisory and not binding on the Centre or states.
  • Misstating that Financial Emergency (Art. 360) has been invoked; it has never been proclaimed since the Constitution came into force.

Judicial Appointments: The Collegium System, the NJAC Experiment and Judicial Independence

The Constitution vests the power to appoint Supreme Court and High Court judges in the President acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers (Articles 124, 217, 222). In practice, the Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to require a consultative process that safeguards judicial independence. The First Judges Case (S. P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981) held that “consultation” did not mean “concurrence”, allowing the executive a dominant role. The Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 1993) reversed this, declaring that the Chief Justice of India (CJI) must have primacy and that a collegium of the CJI and the two senior‑most judges would recommend appointments. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the CJI plus four senior‑most judges for Supreme Court appointments and the CJI plus two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments.

In 2014 Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act and the 99th Constitutional Amendment, seeking to replace the collegium with a six‑member body comprising the CJI, two senior judges, the Law Minister and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court struck down both the Amendment and the Act in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015), holding that the NJAC violated the basic structure by compromising judicial primacy and the independence of the judiciary. The Court reiterated that the collegium, despite its opacity, remains the constitutionally mandated mechanism. Subsequent reforms have focused on transparency: the collegium now publishes its resolutions and reasons for recommendation, and a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) was finalised in 2017 after negotiations between the judiciary and the executive.

Key procedural points for aspirants: (1) The President’s formal appointment power is ceremonial; the real decision rests with the collegium. (2) The MoP prescribes a time‑frame — the government must respond within 30 days for Supreme Court appointments, and if it returns a name, the collegium may reiterate, after which appointment becomes obligatory. (3) The collegium’s recommendations are not subject to judicial review on merits, but the Court can examine whether the process followed the MoP. (4) The NJAC judgment clarified that any future amendment altering the appointment mechanism must preserve judicial primacy to survive basic‑structure scrutiny.

Anti‑Defection Law: The Tenth Schedule, Disqualification Mechanics and Judicial Review

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985) and substantially amended by the 91st Amendment (2003), aims to curb political defections. A member of Parliament or a State Legislature incurs disqualification if he voluntarily gives up membership of his party, votes or abstains contrary to a party whip without prior permission, or joins another party after election. The 2003 amendment removed the “split” exception (one‑third of a legislature party) and retained only the “merger” exception: a merger is recognised only if at least two‑thirds of the legislature party agrees to merge with another party.

The Speaker/Chairman of the House is the sole authority to decide disqualification petitions. In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the Speaker’s adjudicatory role but held that the decision is subject to judicial review on grounds of jurisdictional error, mala fide, perversity or violation of natural justice. The Court also ruled that the Speaker’s order is not immune from review merely because it is a “political question”. Subsequent cases have refined the standard: Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994) clarified that a member’s resignation from the party must be “voluntary and genuine”; a mere letter of resignation submitted under duress does not attract disqualification. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Court directed the Speaker to decide pending petitions within a reasonable time, emphasising that inordinate delay defeats the purpose of the Tenth Schedule.

Procedural nuances: (1) The whip must be issued by the authorised party functionary; a whip issued by an unauthorised person is invalid. (2) A member who abstains from voting on a confidence motion without permission may be treated as having acted against the party line. (3) The merger test is strictly numerical — two‑thirds of the total membership of the legislature party, not merely those present. (4) The 91st Amendment also capped the size of the Council of Ministers at 15 % of the total strength of the House, linking anti‑defection to executive accountability.

Identifying the correct ground for disqualification under the Tenth Schedule
A member of a State Legislative Party submits a resignation letter to the party president, but the party president refuses to accept it. The member then votes against the party whip on a money bill. The Speaker disqualifies the member. Which ground under the Tenth Schedule is correctly invoked?
The correct ground is voluntarily giving up membership of the party (Paragraph 2(1)(a) of the Tenth Schedule). The resignation letter, even if not accepted, demonstrates the member’s intention to relinquish party membership. Voting against the whip (Paragraph 2(1)(b)) is an alternative ground, but the primary and more direct ground is the voluntary resignation. The Speaker’s order will be upheld if the resignation is found genuine and voluntary, as per Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994).
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mixes resignation and whip‑violation, first check whether the resignation was voluntary and accepted or rejected. The Tenth Schedule treats voluntary resignation as an independent ground; the whip violation is secondary. Remember the 2003 amendment removed the one‑third split defence — only a two‑thirds merger survives. Memorise the Kihoto Hollohan principle: Speaker’s decision is reviewable for jurisdictional error, mala fide, perversity or breach of natural justice.

Special Provisions for States: Articles 371 to 371J and Asymmetric Federalism

The Constitution contains a series of special provisions (Articles 371, 371A‑371J) that grant certain states legislative, administrative or fiscal autonomy beyond the normal Union‑State distribution. These provisions embody asymmetric federalism, recognising historical, cultural and developmental diversities. Article 371 (originally for Maharashtra and Gujarat) empowers the President to establish separate development boards for Vidarbha, Marathwada and the rest of Maharashtra, and for Saurashtra and Kutch in Gujarat, with equitable allocation of funds. Article 371A (Nagaland) protects Naga customary law, religious practices and ownership of land and resources; Acts of Parliament do not apply to these matters unless the Nagaland Legislative Assembly so decides. Article 371B (Assam) provides for a committee of the Legislative Assembly consisting of members from tribal areas to oversee legislation affecting those areas. Article 371C (Manipur) creates a similar Hill Areas Committee. Article 371D (Andhra Pradesh) and 371E (Telangana) guarantee equitable opportunities in public employment and education through local cadres and a Central University respectively. Article 371F (Sikkim) preserves the pre‑merger laws and the special status of the Sikkim Legislative Assembly. Article 371G (Mizoram) and 371H (Arunachal Pradesh) mirror the Nagaland model for customary law and land ownership. Article 371I (Goa) fixes the Legislative Assembly at 40 members. Article 371J (Karnataka) establishes a separate development board for the Hyderabad‑Karnataka region with reservation in education and public employment.

These provisions have been invoked in litigation. In State of Nagaland v. Union of India, the Supreme Court upheld the validity of Article 371A, affirming that Parliament cannot legislate on Naga customary law without the State Assembly’s consent. Aspirants should note that these articles are entrenched — they cannot be amended by a simple majority; any amendment affecting them requires the special majority under Article 368 and, where the provision expressly so provides, ratification by the concerned state legislature.

Local Self‑Government: The 73rd and 74th Amendments, Panchayats, Municipalities and Fiscal Devolution

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992) inserted Part IX (Articles 243‑243O) establishing a three‑tier Panchayati Raj system — Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (block) and Zilla Parishad (district). The 74th Amendment (1992) inserted Part IXA (Articles 243P‑243ZG) for urban local bodies — Municipal Corporations, Municipal Councils and Nagar Panchayats. Both amendments mandate regular elections every five years, reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women (not less than one‑third), and the constitution of a State Election Commission (Article 243K) and a State Finance Commission (Article 243I) every five years to review the financial position of local bodies and recommend devolution of taxes, duties, tolls and grants‑in‑aid.

Key functional aspects: (1) The Eleventh Schedule lists 29 subjects (e.g., agriculture, minor irrigation, rural housing) that may be devolved to Panchayats; the Twelfth Schedule lists 18 subjects (e.g., urban planning, water supply, public health) for municipalities. Devolution is not automatic — state legislatures must enact conformity legislation. (2) The Gram Sabha (village assembly) is the foundation of direct democracy; it approves plans, budgets and selects beneficiaries for welfare schemes. (3) The District Planning Committee (Article 243ZD) consolidates plans of Panchayats and municipalities into a district development plan. (4) The State Finance Commission recommendations are advisory; however, successive Finance Commissions have recommended enhanced devolution to local bodies, a benchmark many states have adopted. (5) The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) operationalises the Panchayat’s role as the principal implementing agency, linking constitutional mandate with flagship welfare policy.

Judicial interventions have reinforced autonomy: the Supreme Court has held that the State Election Commission’s independence is part of the basic structure, and has directed timely constitution of State Finance Commissions. Aspirants should distinguish between the constitutional mandate (Articles 243‑243ZG) and the state‑level conformity laws which vary widely — a frequent source of exam questions on “which subject is devolved to Panchayats under the Eleventh Schedule?”.

Constitutional Interpretation: Doctrines of Eclipse, Severability, Pith and Substance, Colourable Legislation and Harmonious Construction

The Supreme Court has evolved a toolkit of interpretive doctrines to reconcile legislative competence with fundamental rights and federal distribution. The Doctrine of Eclipse (Article 13(1)) holds that a pre‑Constitution law inconsistent with a fundamental right is not dead but eclipsed; it revives if the right is amended or removed. In State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951), the Court applied eclipse to a pre‑1950 prohibition law after the 1st Amendment inserted Article 19(6). The Doctrine of Severability (Article 13(2)) permits the Court to strike down only the offending portion of a statute, preserving the remainder if it is capable of independent operation. R. M. D. C. v. Union of India (1957) established the test: the valid part must be severable without altering the legislative intent.

The Doctrine of Pith and Substance determines the true nature of a law to decide whether it falls within Union or State legislative competence (Seventh Schedule). If the pith and substance of a law falls within the competence of the enacting legislature, incidental encroachment on the other list is permissible. K. C. Gajapati v. State of Orissa (1954) and State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) are leading illustrations. The Doctrine of Colourable Legislation prevents a legislature from doing indirectly what it cannot do directly; the Court examines the real purpose behind the law. In K. C. Gajapati the Court struck down a tax on motor vehicles disguised as a fee because the pith and substance was taxation, a Union subject.

Harmonious Construction requires that when two constitutional provisions appear to conflict, they be interpreted so that both have effect, avoiding a declaration of repugnancy. The Supreme Court in Re: Kerala Education Bill (1958) applied harmonious construction between Article 30(1) (minority rights) and Article 29(2) (non‑discrimination). These doctrines are not mutually exclusive; a single case may invoke several. For instance, Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) used basic‑structure, severability and harmonious construction to invalidate clauses of the 42nd Amendment. Mastery of these doctrines enables aspirants to answer “which doctrine will the Court apply to test the validity of a State law that incidentally touches a Union subject?” — the answer is pith and substance, supplemented by colourable legislation if the law’s true aim is to usurp Union power.

  • Confusing the collegium’s primacy with the executive’s appointment power; the President’s role is formal, not discretionary.
  • Assuming the NJAC judgment permits any future commission; the Court insisted judicial primacy is non‑negotiable.
  • Treating the Speaker’s disqualification order as final; it is reviewable for jurisdictional error, mala fide, perversity or breach of natural justice.
  • Believing the one‑third split defence still exists under the Tenth Schedule; it was removed by the 91st Amendment (2003).
  • Thinking Articles 371‑371J can be amended by simple majority; they require special majority and, where specified, state ratification.
  • Assuming the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules automatically devolve functions; state conformity legislation is mandatory.
  • Applying the doctrine of eclipse to post‑Constitution laws; it applies only to pre‑Constitution statutes.

Judicial Review of Legislative Competence: The Doctrine of Repugnancy and Article 254

Article 254 of the Constitution governs conflicts between Union and State laws on subjects enumerated in the Concurrent List (List III of the Seventh Schedule). When a State law is repugnant to a Union law, the Union law prevails unless the State law has been reserved for the President’s consideration and has received his assent under Article 254(2). The Supreme Court has articulated a test for repugnancy requiring: (1) both laws to operate on the same field; (2) directly contradictory provisions such that obedience to one entails disobedience to the other; and (3) that the Union law was not enacted under a power that excludes State legislation altogether (the “occupied field” doctrine). In M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979) the Court held that a State law on prevention of corruption was not repugnant to the Central Prevention of Corruption Act because the two statutes addressed distinct aspects of the same subject. Conversely, in Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983) a State law fixing drug prices was struck down as repugnant to the Central Drugs (Prices Control) Order, since both regulated the identical price‑fixing mechanism.

The “occupied field” principle, articulated in State of Maharashtra v. Bharat Shanti Lal (1995), bars State legislation when Parliament has legislated comprehensively on a Concurrent subject, even if the State law is not directly contradictory. The Court examines legislative intent, the scope of the Union statute, and whether the State law would frustrate the Union scheme. A further nuance arises when a State law receives Presidential assent under Article 254(2). In Tika Ramji v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1956) the Court clarified that such assent renders the State law operative only in that State and does not affect the Union law’s operation elsewhere. However, if Parliament subsequently enacts a law on the same subject with a non‑obstante clause, the later Union law prevails over the earlier assented State law (see Gujarat University v. Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar, 1963).

The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review of repugnancy by inserting Article 254A, but the provision was never brought into force, and the basic‑structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) preserves the Court’s power to adjudicate federal legislative conflicts. Aspirants should note that repugnancy does not arise for subjects in the Union List or State List; it is exclusive to the Concurrent List. The doctrine of pith and substance (Article 246) is applied first to determine whether a law genuinely falls under the Concurrent List before the repugnancy test is invoked.

The Constitutional Scheme for Union Territories: Delhi, Puducherry and the Former State of Jammu & Kashmir

Part VIII (Articles 239–242) provides a graduated framework for the administration of Union Territories (UTs). Article 239 empowers the President to administer a UT through an Administrator appointed by him. Article 239A (inserted by the 14th Amendment, 1962) enables Parliament to create legislatures for certain UTs; Puducherry is the principal example, governed by the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963, which grants a Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister. The Lieutenant Governor (LG) of Puducherry acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in matters where the LG is required to act in his discretion under the Act.

Delhi enjoys a unique status under Article 239AA, introduced by the 69th Amendment (1991). It provides for a Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers, but the LG retains discretionary powers over “public order”, “police” and “land” (Entries 1, 2 and 18 of the State List as applied to Delhi). The Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi Act, 1991 operationalises this scheme. The Supreme Court in Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) held that the LG’s discretion is limited to the three enumerated subjects and that the Council of Ministers’ aid and advice binds the LG on all other matters. A subsequent Constitution Bench (2023) reinforced that the LG cannot stall routine governance by referring every decision to the President.

Jammu & Kashmir was a State with special autonomy under Article 370 and Article 35A until the Presidential Order of 5 August 2019 and the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, which abrogated Article 370 and bifurcated the State into two UTs: Jammu & Kashmir (with a legislature under Article 239A) and Ladakh (without a legislature, administered directly under Article 239). The Reorganisation Act extends the bulk of central laws to the new UTs and provides for a Lieutenant Governor for each. The Supreme Court, in a series of petitions (2020‑2023), upheld the constitutional validity of the abrogation, emphasizing that Article 370 was a temporary provision and that the President’s power under Article 370(3) could be exercised with the concurrence of the Governor (acting as the State government).

Other UTs — Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, Lakshadweep — lack legislatures and are administered by Administrators appointed by the President. The constitutional design thus reflects a spectrum: full Statehood, UT with legislature (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir), and UT without legislature. Aspirants must distinguish the legislative competence of each category, the role of the LG/Administrator, and the extent of central control, as questions frequently test the distinction between “aid and advice” and “discretionary” functions.

Identifying Repugnancy
A State enacts a law on “prevention of adulteration of food” (a Concurrent List entry). The Union has already enacted the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, which comprehensively regulates food standards, licensing, and penalties. The State law provides stricter penalties for certain offences and has received Presidential assent under Article 254(2). Which law prevails in that State?
The Union law prevails. Although the State law received Presidential assent, the Supreme Court in Hoechst Pharmaceuticals v. State of Bihar (1983) held that assent under Article 254(2) only saves the State law from being void ab initio; it does not immunise it from subsequent Union legislation that occupies the field. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, enacted after the State law, contains a non‑obstante clause and covers the entire field of food safety. Therefore, the Union law overrides the State law even in the State where assent was given.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks which law prevails in a Concurrent List conflict, first check whether the State law has Presidential assent. If yes, ask whether Parliament later enacted a comprehensive law with a non‑obstante clause. The later Union law wins. Remember the repugnancy test: same field, direct contradiction, and no occupied‑field bar. Practise with the landmark cases Karunanidhi, Hoechst, and Bharat Shanti Lal to recognise the pattern.

Administration of Scheduled and Tribal Areas: The Fifth and Sixth Schedules and the PESA Act

The Constitution provides two distinct regimes for the administration of areas inhabited predominantly by Scheduled Tribes. The Fifth Schedule (Article 244(1)) applies to “Scheduled Areas” in States other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. The Governor of each such State has special responsibilities: he may direct that any particular Act of Parliament or of the State Legislature shall not apply to a Scheduled Area or shall apply with modifications; he may make regulations for the peace and good government of the Area after consulting the Tribes Advisory Council (TAC) constituted under the Fifth Schedule. The TAC consists of not more than twenty members, three‑fourths of whom are representatives of the Scheduled Tribes in the State Legislative Assembly.

The Sixth Schedule (Article 244(2)) governs “Tribal Areas” in the four northeastern States. It provides for Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) and Autonomous Regional Councils (ARCs) with legislative, executive and judicial powers over subjects such as land, forests, inheritance, marriage and social customs. The Councils can make laws on these subjects subject to the assent of the Governor. The Sixth Schedule also provides for a Regional Council for each autonomous region. The Governor may annul or suspend any law made by a Council if it is repugnant to central or State law.

The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) extends the provisions of Part IX (Panchayats) to the Fifth Schedule Areas with crucial modifications. PESA mandates that the Gram Sabha (village assembly) shall be competent to safeguard and preserve the traditions, customs, cultural identity, community resources and dispute resolution mechanisms of the tribal community. It requires that the Gram Sabha’s prior consent be obtained for land acquisition, mining leases and the grant of prospecting licences in Scheduled Areas. Ownership of minor forest produce is vested in the Gram Sabha. The Act also reserves the chairperson’s post in Panchayats at all levels for Scheduled Tribes.

Judicial interpretation has reinforced tribal autonomy. In Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) the Supreme Court held that the transfer of tribal land to non‑tribals for mining violated the Fifth Schedule and the Andhra Pradesh Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation, 1959. In Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests (2013) the Court upheld the Gram Sabha’s right to reject a mining proposal under the Forest Rights Act, 2006, read with PESA. The Court has also ruled that the 73rd Amendment does not automatically apply to Scheduled Areas; State conformity legislation is mandatory, and any State law that dilutes PESA’s mandatory provisions is ultra vires.

Aspirants should distinguish the Fifth Schedule’s Governor‑centric model from the Sixth Schedule’s Council‑centric model, remember that PESA applies only to Fifth Schedule Areas, and note that the Sixth Schedule Areas are excluded from PESA. Questions often test the composition of the Tribes Advisory Council, the requirement of Gram Sabha consent for land acquisition, and the hierarchy of laws when a Central Act, a State Act, a Council regulation and a PESA provision conflict.

Official Language Provisions and the Eighth Schedule: Evolution, Judicial Interpretation and Language Policy

Part XVII (Articles 343–351) lays down the official language framework of the Union. Article 343 declares Hindi in the Devanagari script as the official language of the Union, with English continuing for official purposes for a period of fifteen years from the commencement of the Constitution (i.e., until 26 January 1965). The Official Languages Act, 1963, enacted under Article 343(3), provided for the continued use of English alongside Hindi for all official purposes of the Union, effectively removing the fifteen‑year limit.

Article 344 mandates the constitution of a Commission (the Official Language Commission) and a Committee of Parliament to review the progress of Hindi and recommend measures for its propagation. The first Commission (1955) under B. G. Kher recommended a phased transition; subsequent Committees have recommended continued bilingualism. Article 345 empowers a State Legislature to adopt any one or more languages in use in the State as its official language(s). Article 346 provides for the language of communication between the Union and a State or between States. Article 347 allows the President to recognise a language spoken by a substantial proportion of a State’s population as an official language of that State.

Article 348 stipulates that the language of the Supreme Court and High Courts shall be English until Parliament provides otherwise. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977) held that the use of Hindi in High Court proceedings cannot be mandated without parliamentary legislation. Article 349 prescribes a special procedure during the initial fifteen-year period: no Bill making provision for the language to be used for purposes mentioned in Article 348(1) can be introduced in Parliament without the President’s prior sanction. Article 350 guarantees the right to submit representations in any language used in the Union or the State. Article 350A obligates the State to provide facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage. Article 350B creates the office of the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities to investigate safeguards for linguistic minorities.

The Eighth Schedule originally listed fourteen languages. The 21st Amendment (1967) added Sindhi. The 71st Amendment (1992) added Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali. The 92nd Amendment (2003) added Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali, bringing the total to twenty‑two. The 96th Amendment (2011) changed the spelling of “Oriya” to “Odia”. The Schedule does not confer any legal right; it serves as a reference for the development of languages under Article 351, which directs the Union to promote the spread of Hindi and to enrich it by assimilating forms, style and expressions from the languages in the Eighth Schedule.

Judicial pronouncements have clarified that the Eighth Schedule is not exhaustive of India’s linguistic diversity and that the State’s power under Article 345 is not limited to scheduled languages. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) the Court upheld the State’s decision to adopt Kannada as the sole official language, rejecting a challenge based on the Eighth Schedule. The Official Languages (Use for Official Purposes) Rules, 1976, prescribe the languages to be used in communications between the Union and States. Aspirants must be able to trace the amendment history of the Eighth Schedule, distinguish between the constitutional mandate (Article 343) and the statutory regime (Official Languages Act, 1963), and recall the special procedure under Article 349 for language legislation during the initial period.

Inter‑State Trade and Commerce: Article 301, the Freedom of Trade Doctrine and Judicial Tests

Part XIII (Articles 301–307) guarantees freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India. Article 301 declares that trade, commerce and intercourse shall be free, subject to the other provisions of Part XIII. Article 302 empowers Parliament to impose restrictions on this freedom in the public interest. Article 303(1) prohibits Parliament and State Legislatures from giving preference to one State over another or from making laws that discriminate between States in matters of trade and commerce. Article 303(2) permits Parliament to make such discriminatory laws if a declaration is made that it is necessary for dealing with a scarcity of goods. Article 304(a) allows a State to impose taxes on goods imported from other States if similar goods produced in the State are subject to the same tax; Article 304(b) permits a State to impose reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade in the public interest, subject to the President’s assent.

The Supreme Court has evolved a “free flow” test: any State measure that directly or indirectly impedes the movement of goods across State borders is prima facie violative of Article 301 unless saved by Article 302 or 304. In Atiabari Tea Co. v. State of Assam (1961) a State tax on the movement of tea through Assam was struck down because it was a tax on the movement itself, not a compensatory tax for services rendered. In Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) Ltd. v. State of Rajasthan (1962) the Court upheld a motor vehicles tax that was levied on all vehicles using State roads, holding it to be a compensatory tax for road maintenance and therefore not a restriction on inter‑State trade.

The “compensatory tax” doctrine was refined in State of Karnataka v. Hansa Corporation (1995), where an entry tax on goods entering the State was upheld because the tax was earmarked for the development of market infrastructure used by the imported goods. However, in Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana (2016) a five‑judge Bench struck down Haryana’s entry tax on the ground that the tax was not demonstrably linked to any service provided to the goods and thus amounted to a restriction on inter‑State trade. The Court reiterated that the burden of proving the compensatory nature of a tax lies on the State.

Article 305 saves existing laws and laws providing for the imposition of taxes on the sale or purchase of goods (other than newspapers) from the operation of Articles 301 and 303. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime, introduced by the 101st Amendment (2016), subsumes most indirect taxes on goods and services, including entry taxes, and establishes a unified market. Nevertheless, Article 301 remains relevant for non‑GST goods (e.g., petroleum products, alcohol) and for any State measure that creates a barrier to the free flow of such goods. Aspirants should be able to apply the three‑step analysis: (1) does the measure restrict inter‑State movement? (2) is it a compensatory tax or a regulatory measure saved by Article 304(b) with Presidential assent? (3) does it fall within the GST exemption? Mastery of the Atiabari, Automobile Transport, Hansa Corporation and Jindal Stainless line of cases is essential for answering scenario‑based questions on inter‑State trade barriers.

  • Confusing the repugnancy test (Article 254) with the pith‑and‑substance test (Article 246); repugnancy applies only after both laws are validly enacted under the Concurrent List.
  • Assuming Presidential assent under Article 254(2) makes a State law immune from later Union legislation; a subsequent Union law with a non‑obstante clause prevails.
  • Treating the Lieutenant Governor of Delhi as having the same discretionary powers as the Governor of a State; the LG’s discretion is confined to public order, police and land under Article 239AA.
  • Believing the Sixth Schedule applies to all tribal areas in India; it is limited to Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.
  • Thinking PESA extends to Sixth Schedule Areas; PESA applies only to Fifth Schedule Areas.
  • Assuming the Eighth Schedule languages are the only languages a State may adopt as official language; Article 345 permits any language in use in the State.
  • Overlooking that Article 301 continues to govern non‑GST goods such as petroleum and alcohol; the GST regime does not extinguish the constitutional guarantee of free trade for those items.

Doctrine of Constitutional Morality and Its Judicial Application

The phrase constitutional morality was prominently invoked by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly debates (1948) to denote a commitment to the values embedded in the Constitution — liberty, equality, fraternity, and the rule of law — that must guide both state action and judicial interpretation, even when they conflict with prevailing social morality. (The term has earlier provenance in constitutional theory, but Ambedkar anchored it in the Indian constitutional project.) Unlike public morality, which reflects majoritarian sentiment, constitutional morality is anchored in the text and the transformative vision of the Constitution. The Supreme Court has invoked this doctrine to strike down laws and practices that, though socially accepted, violate fundamental rights.

In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), a five‑judge Bench decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations under Section 377 IPC, holding that constitutional morality requires the protection of dignity and autonomy of LGBTQ+ individuals, overriding majoritarian moral disapproval. The Court emphasised that the Constitution’s guarantee of equality (Art. 14) and non‑discrimination (Art. 15) cannot be subordinated to societal notions of morality. Similarly, in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018) (the Sabarimala case), the majority ruled that the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the temple violated constitutional morality, because the practice discriminated on the basis of gender and was not an essential religious practice protected under Art. 25.

The doctrine also operates in the domain of federalism and governance. In NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018), the Court held that the Lieutenant Governor must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in matters of land, police, and public order, because constitutional morality demands respect for the democratic mandate of the elected government. The Court warned against a “constitutional morality of convenience” where the Centre uses discretionary powers to undermine elected state governments.

Critics argue that constitutional morality risks judicial overreach, allowing unelected judges to impose their own moral preferences under the guise of constitutional values. The Court has responded by insisting that the doctrine is not a free‑floating moral compass but is tethered to specific constitutional provisions — fundamental rights, directive principles, and the preamble. The test applied is whether the impugned action is consistent with the core constitutional commitments of justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity, as interpreted through the text and precedent.

For aspirants, the key take‑aways are: (1) constitutional morality is a textual‑and‑structural concept, not a synonym for popular morality; (2) it has been deployed to expand the scope of Arts. 14, 15, 19, 21, and 25; (3) it serves as a check on majoritarian legislative and executive actions; and (4) the Court balances it against the doctrine of essential religious practices and federal distribution of powers.

Separation of Powers in the Indian Constitutional Scheme: Institutional Checks and Judicial Enforcement

The Indian Constitution does not adopt a rigid separation of powers akin to the United States model; instead it establishes a system of checks and balances where the three organs — legislature, executive, and judiciary — are inter‑linked yet subject to constitutional limits. Article 50 directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services. Articles 121 and 211 prohibit parliamentary and state legislative discussion of judicial conduct, while Articles 122 and 212 bar courts from inquiring into legislative proceedings. Article 361 grants the President and Governors immunity from judicial process for official acts.

Functional overlap is deliberate: the executive is drawn from the legislature (Art. 75, 164), the legislature exercises control over the executive through confidence motions, and the judiciary reviews both legislative and executive actions (Arts. 13, 32, 226). The Supreme Court in Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955) observed that the Constitution “does not recognise a strict separation of powers” but distributes functions to prevent concentration of authority. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), the Court upheld the validity of the 39th Amendment but struck down the clause that ousted judicial review of the Prime Minister’s election, affirming that judicial review is a basic feature.

The most significant modern confrontation came with the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015), a four‑to‑one majority held that the NJAC violated the independence of the judiciary, a component of the basic structure, because it gave the executive a decisive role in appointments. The Court reiterated that while the Constitution permits legislative regulation of appointments, it cannot alter the primacy of the judiciary in the collegium system.

Legislative privileges (Arts. 105, 194) also illustrate the balance. In Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly (1965), the Court held that parliamentary privilege is subject to fundamental rights, and the judiciary can examine whether a privilege claim is a colourable exercise of power. In subsequent rulings, the Court has recognised that the legislature’s power to punish for contempt is not absolute and must conform to due process.

Thus, the Indian model is best described as “separation of functions with overlapping personnel”. The judiciary acts as the ultimate arbiter of constitutional limits, ensuring that neither the legislature nor the executive transgresses its domain. Aspirants should be able to cite the relevant articles, the landmark judgments, and the principle that the basic structure doctrine safeguards the institutional equilibrium.

Identify the Constitutional Provision
A state government passes a law that authorises the Chief Minister to appoint district judges without consulting the High Court. The High Court challenges the law, arguing it violates the separation of powers. Which constitutional provision most directly supports the High Court’s contention?
Article 235 vests control over the subordinate judiciary in the High Court, and Article 50 obliges the State to separate the judiciary from the executive. The law allowing the Chief Minister (executive) to appoint district judges encroaches on the High Court’s administrative authority and breaches the directive in Art. 50. Therefore, the High Court can rely on Arts. 235 and 50 read with the basic structure principle of judicial independence (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 2015).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question tests separation of powers, first locate the specific article that allocates the function in dispute (e.g., Art. 50 for judiciary‑executive separation, Art. 121/211 for legislative‑judicial immunity, Art. 75/164 for executive‑legislative link). Then map the factual scenario to the article and cite the leading case that interpreted it. This two‑step method — article → precedent — yields a concise, high‑scoring answer.

Inter‑State Water Disputes: Article 262, the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act, and Supreme Court Jurisprudence

Article 262 empowers Parliament to provide for the adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution, or control of waters of inter‑state rivers and river valleys. Crucially, it also bars the Supreme Court and any other court from exercising jurisdiction over such disputes once Parliament has enacted a law. Pursuant to this mandate, Parliament enacted the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act, 1956, which establishes a tribunal for each dispute referred by the Central Government. The tribunal’s award is final and binding, subject only to a limited review by the Supreme Court under Article 136 (special leave) on grounds of jurisdictional error or violation of natural justice.

Major tribunals constituted under the Act include the Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1990), the Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal (2004), the Godavari Water Disputes Tribunal (1969), and the Narmada Water Disputes Tribunal (1969). The Cauvery dispute illustrates the procedural trajectory: after the tribunal’s 2007 final award, the Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu (2018) upheld the award with modifications, emphasising that the tribunal’s expertise in hydrology and equitable apportionment deserves deference, but the Court retained the power to correct manifest errors.

The 2019 amendment to the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act introduced a single standing tribunal with multiple benches, fixed timelines for adjudication (three years, extendable by two), and a mechanism for the Central Government to notify the award, making it enforceable without further legislative ratification. This amendment aims to reduce the decades‑long delays that plagued earlier tribunals.

Despite the statutory bar, the Supreme Court has occasionally entertained writ petitions under Article 32 when a state alleges that the tribunal’s composition or procedure violates fundamental rights. In State of Haryana v. State of Punjab (2002), the Court held that a challenge to the constitution of a tribunal is justiciable because it implicates the rule of law. However, the Court has consistently refused to re‑evaluate the substantive water allocation, respecting the legislative intent of Article 262.

For examination purposes, aspirants must remember: (1) Article 262 + Inter‑State Water Disputes Act = exclusive tribunal jurisdiction; (2) Supreme Court review is confined to jurisdictional or procedural flaws, not merits; (3) the 2019 amendment creates a permanent tribunal with strict timelines; (4) key disputes — Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Narmada — serve as illustrative case studies for questions on federal water governance.

The Office of the Attorney General of India: Constitutional Mandate, Functions, and Accountability Mechanisms

Article 76 establishes the Attorney General for India as the highest law officer of the Union. The President appoints a person qualified to be a Supreme Court judge. Unlike the Advocate General of a state (Art. 165), the Attorney General holds office during the pleasure of the President, with no fixed term, and is not a member of the Cabinet. The Attorney General has the right of audience in all courts in India (Art. 76(2)) and may participate in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament and their committees (Art. 88), but cannot vote.

The core functions are: (a) to give advice to the Government of India on legal matters referred by the President; (b) to perform duties of a legal character assigned by the President; (c) to represent the Union in the Supreme Court and any High Court in cases where the Government is a party; and (d) to discharge functions conferred by any other law. The Attorney General is assisted by the Solicitor General and Additional Solicitors General, who are appointed on a contractual basis and do not enjoy constitutional status.

Accountability mechanisms are largely political and conventional. The Attorney General can be removed by the President at any time; there is no impeachment process. In practice, the office changes with a change of government, raising concerns about independence. The Supreme Court has observed that the Attorney General must render impartial legal advice, not merely defend government policy. The Law Commission’s 14th Report (1958) recommended a fixed tenure and a statutory removal procedure akin to that of the Comptroller and Auditor General, but this has not been enacted.

Recent debates have focused on the Attorney General’s role in high‑profile litigation — such as the Ayodhya title dispute (2019), the Article 370 abrogation (2019), and the farm laws challenge (2021) — where the office’s advice shaped the Union’s legal strategy. Critics argue that the lack of security of tenure compromises the ability to give candid advice, while defenders maintain that the pleasure doctrine ensures democratic accountability.

Key points for aspirants: (1) Article 76 — appointment, qualifications, tenure, functions; (2) Right of audience in all courts; (3) Participation in Parliament without vote; (4) Distinction from Solicitor General (statutory, not constitutional); (5) Absence of fixed tenure and formal removal safeguards; (6) Judicial emphasis on impartiality of advice.

  • Confusing constitutional morality with public morality; the former is anchored in constitutional text, the latter in societal norms.
  • Assuming a strict separation of powers exists in India; the Constitution provides for functional overlap with checks and balances.
  • Believing the Supreme Court can review the merits of an inter‑state water tribunal award; its review is limited to jurisdictional or procedural errors.
  • Thinking the Attorney General has a fixed term like the CAG; the office is held during the President’s pleasure.
  • Overlooking that the 2019 amendment to the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act created a permanent tribunal with binding timelines.
  • Treating the Solicitor General as a constitutional authority; the post is created by statute and lacks the constitutional protections of the Attorney General.
