1. Physics — Core Principles

Physics, from the Greek physikos meaning "of nature," is the most fundamental of the natural sciences. It seeks to understand the behaviour of the universe from the smallest subatomic particles to the largest cosmic structures. The scientific revolution of the 16th–17th centuries, led by figures such as Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), and Isaac Newton (1643–1727), established the mathematical foundations of classical physics. The 20th century brought two revolutionary frameworks: quantum mechanics (governing the microscopic world) and general relativity (governing gravity and cosmology). For UPSC, a conceptual grasp of core principles, their technological applications, and key historical discoveries is essential.

Newton's Laws of Motion

Published in Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), these three laws form the bedrock of classical mechanics. First Law (Law of Inertia): A body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion stays in uniform straight-line motion unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force. This concept was anticipated by Galileo (experiments with inclined planes) and refined by René Descartes. Inertia is directly proportional to mass. Second Law: F = ma (force equals mass times acceleration). More fundamentally, Newton expressed it as F = dp/dt — force is the rate of change of momentum. This law quantifies the effect of forces. Applications range from calculating rocket thrust to designing automotive safety systems. Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. This is the principle behind rocket propulsion — exhaust gases pushed downward produce an upward thrust on the rocket. Everyday examples include walking (feet push ground backward, ground pushes forward) and recoil of a gun.

UPSC Application: Rocket PropulsionNewton's third law is the operating principle of all launch vehicles. The PSLV and LVM3 generate thrust by expelling high-velocity exhaust gases downward through a nozzle. The net thrust is given by F = ṁvₑ + (pₑ − pₐ)Aₑ, where ṁ is the mass flow rate, vₑ is exhaust velocity, pₑ and pₐ are exit and ambient pressures, and Aₑ is the nozzle exit area.

Gravitation

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation: F = G m₁m₂/r², where G = 6.67430×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² (the gravitational constant, first measured by Henry Cavendish in 1798 using a torsion balance). Acceleration due to gravity: g = GM/R² = 9.80665 m/s² at sea level. Variation of g: (a) with altitude: g' = g(1 − 2h/R) for h ≪ R; (b) with depth: g' = g(1 − d/R) — linear decrease; (c) latitude: g is minimum at the equator (9.78 m/s²) and maximum at the poles (9.83 m/s²) due to Earth's rotation and oblate shape. Escape velocity: vₑ = √(2GM/R) = 11.2 km/s for Earth, 2.38 km/s for the Moon, 617.7 km/s for the Sun. Orbital velocity: vₒ = √(GM/r). Satellites: Geostationary (~35,786 km altitude, 24-hr period, equatorial orbit — used for communication and broadcasting, pioneered by Arthur C. Clarke in 1945), Polar orbits (~600–900 km, Sun-synchronous for remote sensing and weather monitoring), Low Earth Orbit (LEO) (~200–2,000 km, used for ISS, Earth observation, and LEO communication constellations like Starlink and OneWeb). Gravitational potential energy: U = −GMm/r; defined as zero at infinity. Kepler's Laws: (1) Elliptical orbits with Sun at one focus, (2) Equal area in equal time (areal velocity constant — conservation of angular momentum), (3) T² ∝ a³ (square of period proportional to cube of semi-major axis).

Work, Energy & Power

Kinetic Energy: KE = ½mv². Potential Energy: PE = mgh (gravitational near Earth's surface). Elastic Potential Energy: PE = ½kx² (Hooke's law, Robert Hooke, 1660). Conservation of Mechanical Energy: KEᵢ + PEᵢ = KE_f + PE_f (in conservative systems — no non-conservative forces like friction). Work-Energy Theorem: Net work = change in KE. Power: P = W/t = F·v. Unit: watt (W = J/s). Horsepower: 1 hp = 746 W. Collisions: Elastic (KE conserved — ideal gas molecules), Inelastic (KE not conserved, objects may stick — coefficient of restitution e < 1), Perfectly Inelastic (e = 0, objects stick together).

Fluid Mechanics

Study of fluids (liquids and gases) at rest and in motion. Archimedes' Principle (c. 250 BCE): A body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid. Archimedes of Syracuse discovered this while stepping into a bath — leading to his "Eureka" moment for determining whether a gold crown was pure. Buoyancy: An object floats if its average density is less than the fluid. Applications: ships (steel hulls are hollow → average density less than water), submarines (ballast tanks adjust buoyancy for diving/surfacing), hydrometers (measure liquid density), hot-air balloons. Pascal's Law (1653): Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and container walls. Applications: hydraulic brakes, hydraulic lifts, car jacks — F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂, a small force over small area produces large force over large area. Bernoulli's Principle (1738): In steady, incompressible, inviscid flow, an increase in velocity occurs simultaneously with decrease in pressure. Mathematically: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant. Derived by Daniel Bernoulli (Hydrodynamica, 1738). Applications: Aerofoil lift — air moves faster over curved upper surface → lower pressure → net upward force enables flight. Atomiser/sprayer: fast air reduces pressure, fluid rises and atomises. Venturi meter: measures flow rate. Bunsen burner: gas draws air through side openings. Pitot tube: measures aircraft airspeed. Viscosity: A fluid's resistance to flow (internal friction). Newtonian fluids: constant viscosity at given temperature (water, air, thin oils). Non-Newtonian fluids: ketchup (shear-thinning), cornstarch in water (shear-thickening/dilatant), blood, toothpaste, paint. Poiseuille's Law: Flow rate ∝ r⁴/η — crucial in blood flow; small reductions in artery radius drastically reduce flow (explains impact of atherosclerosis). Surface Tension: Liquid surface behaves like stretched elastic membrane due to molecular cohesion. Water has high surface tension (72 mN/m at 20°C). Allows water striders to walk on water, causes capillary action, droplet formation. Capillary Action: Rise/fall of liquid in narrow tubes due to adhesion and cohesion. Plants transport water from roots to leaves via xylem capillaries. Soap/detergents reduce surface tension for better wetting and cleaning. Applications: Oil spills — surfactants disperse oil into droplets; detergents penetrate fabrics; capillary tubes in laboratory (blood collection, microfluidics).

Heat & Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics studies heat, work, temperature, and energy. Specific Heat Capacity: Q = mcΔT (heat required to raise temperature of 1 kg by 1 K). Water has unusually high specific heat (4186 J/kg·K) — moderates climate. Latent Heat: Q = mL — heat absorbed/released during phase change without temperature change. Latent heat of fusion of ice: 334 kJ/kg; vaporisation of water: 2260 kJ/kg. Zeroth Law: If A = B and B = C in thermal equilibrium, then A = C — basis of thermometry (temperature measurement). First Law: ΔU = Q − W — conservation of energy for thermodynamic systems. Second Law: Clausius statement: Heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot body. Kelvin-Planck statement: No heat engine can convert all heat into work. Entropy (S) of an isolated system always increases — ΔS ≥ 0. This law dictates the direction of natural processes. Third Law: Absolute zero (0 K = −273.15°C) is unattainable in a finite number of steps. At 0 K, all molecular motion ceases (except zero-point energy). Carnot Engine: Most efficient reversible heat engine operating between two temperatures. Efficiency η = 1 − T_C/T_H (always < 1). The Carnot cycle consists of two isothermal and two adiabatic processes. Heat engines: Internal combustion (Otto cycle — petrol, Diesel cycle), external combustion (Stirling, Rankine — steam turbines). Heat pumps and refrigerators: COP (Coefficient of Performance) = Q_C/W. Conduction (Fourier's law), Convection (Newton's law of cooling), Radiation (Stefan-Boltzmann law: P = εσAT⁴, Wien's displacement law: λ_maxT = constant = 2.898×10⁻³ m·K).

Exam TipEntropy (S) is a measure of microscopic disorder. ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings ≥ 0 for any spontaneous process. For UPSC, remember the connection between the Second Law and the arrow of time — entropy always increases, giving time a direction.

Sound

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave that requires a medium (solid, liquid, or gas). It propagates via compression and rarefaction of the medium. Speed in air at STP: ~340 m/s (more precisely, 331 m/s at 0°C, increasing by 0.6 m/s per °C rise). Speed in water: ~1,500 m/s; in steel: ~5,000 m/s. Properties: Amplitude → loudness (intensity, measured in decibels — dB; logarithmic scale: 0 dB = threshold of hearing, 120 dB = threshold of pain). Frequency → pitch (Hz). Audible range: 20 Hz – 20,000 Hz; infrasound (< 20 Hz — elephants, earthquakes); ultrasound (> 20 kHz — bats, SONAR, medical imaging). Quality (Timbre): Distinguishes same pitch/amplitude from different instruments — due to harmonics/overtones. Doppler Effect: Apparent frequency change due to relative motion between source and observer — f' = f(v ± vₒ)/(v ∓ vₛ). First demonstrated by Christian Doppler (1842) for sound. Applications: radar (radio waves), astronomy (redshift = receding source, blueshift = approaching source — used by Edwin Hubble to discover the expanding universe), medical ultrasound (blood flow velocity measurement), speed guns. Resonance: When driving frequency matches natural frequency, amplitude increases dramatically (Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse, 1940). Standing waves in strings and pipes → musical instruments. Echo and SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging) for underwater object detection. Ultrasonic waves in cleaning, welding, medical imaging (sonography), and breaking kidney stones (lithotripsy).

Historical Context: Doppler Effect & CosmologyThe Doppler effect applied to light waves gives us the cosmological redshift. When Edwin Hubble (1929) observed that light from distant galaxies was redshifted (wavelength stretched), he concluded that galaxies are moving away from us. The recession velocity v = H₀d (Hubble's law), where H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc is the Hubble constant. This provided the first observational evidence for the Big Bang theory.

Light & Optics

Light exhibits wave-particle duality — it behaves as an electromagnetic wave (wave theory of Christiaan Huygens, 1678) and as a stream of photons (particle theory of Albert Einstein, 1905 — photoelectric effect, Nobel Prize 1921). Speed in vacuum: c = 299,792,458 m/s (exact, by definition).

Reflection

Angle of incidence = angle of reflection. Mirrors: Plane (virtual, erect, same size image), Concave (converging — real/inverted/virtual/erect depending on object position; used in torches, telescopes, shaving mirrors, dentist mirrors, solar concentrators), Convex (diverging — always virtual, erect, diminished; used in rear-view mirrors for wider field of view). Mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u. Magnification: m = −v/u.

Refraction

Bending of light when passing from one medium to another due to change in speed. Snell's Law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Refractive index n = c/v (speed in vacuum/speed in medium). Absolute refractive indices: air ≈ 1.0003, water = 1.33, crown glass = 1.52, diamond = 2.42 (highest common — causes total internal reflection and brilliance). Lenses: Convex (converging, +f) and Concave (diverging, −f). Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u. Power of lens: P = 1/f (in dioptres, D). Lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). Combination of lenses: P = P₁ + P₂.

Dispersion & Scattering

Dispersion: Splitting of white light into constituent colours (VIBGYOR: Violet 380–450 nm, Indigo 450–485 nm, Blue 485–500 nm, Green 500–565 nm, Yellow 565–590 nm, Orange 590–625 nm, Red 625–740 nm) by a prism — discovered by Isaac Newton (1666). Rainbow formation: dispersion + total internal reflection + refraction in water droplets. Scattering of Light (Rayleigh scattering): Intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴ — blue light scattered most → sky appears blue (Lord Rayleigh, 1871). At sunrise/sunset, longer path → blue scattered away → red/orange sky. Tyndall Effect: Scattering by colloidal particles (blue eye colour, visibility of smoke/clouds). Total Internal Reflection (TIR): Occurs when light travels from denser to rarer medium at angle > critical angle (θ_c = sin⁻¹(n₂/n₁)). Applications: optical fibres (telecom backbone, endoscopy, fibre optic sensors), mirage (hot air near ground acts as rarer medium, light bends upward creating illusion of water), diamond sparkle (high n = 2.42, small critical angle ~24°, light trapped internally reflects multiple times before exiting).

Wave Optics

Huygens' Principle: Every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets. Interference: Young's double-slit experiment (Thomas Young, 1801) — proved wave nature of light. Constructive (path difference = nλ) and destructive (path difference = (2n+1)λ/2) interference. Diffraction: Bending of light around obstacles (Fresnel and Fraunhofer diffraction). Single-slit diffraction pattern. Polarization: Restriction of light waves to a single plane of vibration — discovered by Étienne-Louis Malus (1808). Applications: 3D movies (polarised glasses), LCD screens, glare reduction (Polaroid sunglasses), optical stress analysis.

Human Eye & Vision Defects

The human eye is a natural optical instrument. Light enters through the cornea (most refraction occurs here), passes through the pupil (iris controls aperture), is focused by the crystalline lens (accommodation — changes shape via ciliary muscles) onto the retina (contains rods for dim light vision and cones for colour vision — three types for RGB). The fovea centralis provides sharpest vision. Defects: Myopia (near-sightedness — image formed before retina, corrected by concave lens), Hypermetropia (far-sightedness — image formed behind retina, corrected by convex lens), Astigmatism (irregular corneal curvature — corrected by cylindrical lens), Presbyopia (age-related loss of accommodation — bifocals or progressive lenses). Cataract: Lens opacification — most common cause of blindness globally; treated by Phacoemulsification (ultrasonic lens fragmentation) with IOL (intraocular lens) implantation. LASIK: Laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis — reshaping cornea using excimer laser for permanent vision correction.

Electromagnetism

Coulomb's Law: F = k q₁q₂/r², where k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C². Unit of charge: Coulomb (C). Ohm's Law: V = IR (Georg Ohm, 1827). Resistivity: R = ρL/A. Conductivity: σ = 1/ρ. Series: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + ... Parallel: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... Magnetic Effect of Current: Oersted's experiment (1820) — current-carrying wire deflects compass needle. Biot-Savart law and Ampere's law quantify magnetic field due to current. Electromagnet: soft iron core wrapped with coil — magnetic field when current passes. Applications: MRI machines, maglev trains, particle accelerators, electric bells. Electric Motor: Current-carrying conductor in magnetic field experiences force (F = qv × B, Lorentz force) — converts electrical to mechanical energy. Electromagnetic Induction (Faraday, 1831): e = −dΦ/dt (Lenz's law: induced current opposes change). Basis of generator (mechanical → electrical) and transformer. AC vs DC: AC (alternating current, sinusoidal, frequency 50 Hz in India, 60 Hz in USA) — easily transformed; DC (direct current, constant polarity) — used in electronics, batteries. Transformer: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p = I_p/I_s. Core: laminated silicon steel to minimise eddy currents. Step-up (N_s > N_p) increases voltage, decreases current — used in power transmission (high voltage reduces I²R losses). Step-down (N_s < N_p) for domestic use. Eddy currents: Induced circulating currents in conductors — cause heating (used in induction cooking) and energy loss (minimised by laminating cores). Maxwell's Equations: The four equations (James Clerk Maxwell, 1865) that unified electricity and magnetism, predicted electromagnetic waves, and showed light is an EM wave.

Important Electrical Devices & Concepts

  • Rectifier: Converts AC to DC (using diodes — p-n junction). Half-wave and full-wave rectification.
  • Capacitor: Stores electrical energy (C = Q/V). Dielectric increases capacitance. Used in power supplies, filters, timing circuits.
  • Inductor: Stores energy in magnetic field. Opposes changes in current. Used in transformers, chokes, filters.
  • Semiconductor: Conductivity between conductor and insulator. Doping (adding impurities) creates n-type (excess electrons) and p-type (excess holes) materials. p-n junction → diode (allows current in one direction).
  • Transistor: Three-layer semiconductor device (Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley, 1947 — Nobel Prize 1956). Acts as amplifier and switch. Revolutionised electronics → integrated circuits → modern computing.
  • Photovoltaic Cell: Converts light energy to electrical energy (solar cell). p-n junction generates voltage when illuminated. India's solar capacity: ~90 GW (2026).

All EM waves travel at c = 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum. Increasing frequency → decreasing wavelength. Heinrich Hertz (1887) first generated and detected radio waves. Nuclear Physics

Atomic Nucleus: Discovered by Ernest Rutherford (1911, gold foil experiment). Contains protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral) — collectively called nucleons. Nuclear force: Short-range strong force binding nucleons (range ~1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m). Binding Energy: Energy required to split nucleus into constituent nucleons. Binding energy per nucleon vs mass number curve — maximum at Fe-56 (most stable). Fusion of light elements and fission of heavy elements both release energy because products have higher binding energy per nucleon. Radioactivity: Spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei. Discovered by Henri Becquerel (1896); further studied by Marie and Pierre Curie (discovered Po and Ra; Marie Curie — first person to win two Nobel Prizes). Alpha (α): ²⁴He nucleus (2p+2n), charge +2, low penetration (5 cm in air, stopped by paper), high ionising power. Beta (β⁻): electron emitted when neutron → proton + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (antineutrino). Moderate penetration (5 mm Al). Beta⁺ (β⁺): positron emission. Gamma (γ): high-energy EM photon released after α/β decay (nucleus de-excites), high penetration (cm of Pb, m of concrete), low ionising power. Half-life (t½): Time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay. Exponential decay: N = N₀(½)^(t/t½). Range: nanoseconds (Po-214: 164 μs) to billions of years (U-238: 4.47×10⁹ yr, C-14: 5,730 yr). Carbon Dating: Uses C-14 half-life for dating organic remains up to ~50,000 years (Willard Libby, 1949, Nobel 1960).

Nuclear Fission: Heavy nucleus (U-235, Pu-239) absorbs neutron → splits into two lighter nuclei + 2–3 neutrons + ~200 MeV energy. Discovered by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann (1938); explained by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch. Chain reaction: Each fission produces neutrons that trigger further fissions. Critical mass: Minimum mass for self-sustaining chain reaction. Used in: Atom bomb (uncontrolled chain reaction — Little Boy (U-235) over Hiroshima 6 Aug 1945, Fat Man (Pu-239) over Nagasaki 9 Aug 1945); Nuclear reactor (controlled chain reaction — control rods absorb excess neutrons, moderator slows neutrons to thermal energies). Nuclear Fusion: Light nuclei combine to form heavier nucleus + energy. Requires extremely high temperature (~10⁷ K) to overcome Coulomb barrier. Powers the Sun (proton-proton chain: 4H → He + 2e⁺ + 2νₑ + 26.7 MeV). Used in Hydrogen bomb (thermonuclear — uses fission trigger to achieve fusion conditions). Controlled fusion: Tokamak (magnetic confinement — ITER in France, India is partner) and inertial confinement (NIF in USA). In 2022, NIF achieved first net energy gain (Q ~ 1.5).

India's Nuclear ProgrammePHWR (Pressurised Heavy Water Reactor) — mainstay of Indian programme, uses natural uranium (0.7% U-235) as fuel and D₂O as moderator/coolant. FBR (Fast Breeder Reactor) — produces more fissile fuel than it consumes (converts U-238 to Pu-239). LWR (Light Water Reactor) — imported (Kudankulam, VVER type from Russia). AHWR (Advanced Heavy Water Reactor) — thorium-based, under development. India's three-stage nuclear programme, conceived by Dr. Homi J. Bhabha, aims to leverage India's abundant thorium reserves.

Particle Physics

Standard Model of Particle Physics: The theoretical framework describing fundamental particles and their interactions (except gravity). Developed in the 1960s–70s, finalised with the discovery of the Higgs boson at CERN (2012, Nobel Prize 2013 — Peter Higgs, François Englert). Fundamental particles: Quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom — constituents of protons: uud, neutrons: udd). Quarks have fractional charge (+2/3 for up/charm/top, −1/3 for down/strange/bottom). Leptons (electron, muon, tau, and their associated neutrinos: νₑ, ν_μ, ν_τ). Bosons (force carriers): photon (γ) — electromagnetic force; gluon (g) — strong nuclear force, binds quarks; W⁺/W⁻/Z⁰ — weak nuclear force, responsible for beta decay; Higgs (H) — gives mass to particles via Higgs mechanism. Antimatter: Each particle has an antiparticle (same mass, opposite charge). Dirac predicted positron (1931); Anderson discovered it (1932). Matter-antimatter annihilation releases pure energy (E = mc²). CERN: European Organization for Nuclear Research — operates the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), 27 km circumference, proton-proton collisions at 13.6 TeV. India is an Associate Member of CERN.

Superconductivity

Superconductivity: Zero electrical resistance below critical temperature (T_c). Discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes (1911, Nobel 1913) in mercury at 4.2 K. Meissner Effect: Expulsion of magnetic field — superconductors are perfect diamagnets (magnetic levitation). Type I vs Type II: Type I (pure metals, T_c < 30 K); Type II (alloys and compounds, higher T_c, allow partial flux penetration — used for high-field magnets). BCS Theory (Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer, 1957, Nobel 1972): electron pairs (Cooper pairs) mediated by lattice vibrations (phonons). High-T_c Superconductors: Cuprate ceramics (e.g., YBa₂Cu₃O₇ — YBCO, T_c = 92 K) discovered by Georg Bednorz and K. Alex Müller (1986, Nobel 1987). Operate above 77 K (liquid N₂ boiling point). Applications: MRI magnets, particle accelerators (LHC), maglev trains (SCMaglev — 603 km/h record), quantum computing (superconducting qubits), SQUIDs (extremely sensitive magnetometers).

Relativity: Special and General

Special Theory of Relativity (1905): Proposed by Albert Einstein while working at the Swiss Patent Office, published in the journal Annalen der Physik (his annus mirabilis year). Based on two postulates: (1) The laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames. (2) The speed of light in vacuum is constant for all observers, independent of the motion of the source. Consequences: (a) Time Dilation: Moving clocks run slow — Δt = γΔt₀, where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) is the Lorentz factor. Experimental confirmation: muons created by cosmic rays in the upper atmosphere (travel at ~0.998c) reach Earth's surface due to time dilation; Hafele–Keating experiment (1971) — atomic clocks flown on commercial aircraft showed time difference relative to ground clocks. GPS satellites orbit at ~14,000 km/h (~0.000013c) and must correct for both special relativistic (time dilation due to orbital speed: −7 μs/day) and general relativistic (gravitational time dilation: +45 μs/day) effects — net correction ~38 μs/day; without correction, GPS would accumulate ~10 km/day positioning error. (b) Length Contraction: Moving objects contract along direction of motion: L = L₀/γ. (c) Relativistic Mass Increase: m = m₀γ. As v → c, m → ∞, making c an unattainable speed limit for massive objects. (d) Mass-Energy Equivalence: E = mc² — small mass converts to enormous energy. 1 kg of mass completely converted yields ~9×10¹⁶ J, equivalent to ~21 megatons of TNT. Powers the Sun (nuclear fusion) and nuclear weapons/reactors. (e) Relativity of Simultaneity: Events simultaneous in one frame may not be simultaneous in another moving relative to it. (f) Relativistic Velocity Addition: u' = (u + v)/(1 + uv/c²) — ensures no observed speed exceeds c. Lorentz Transformations: Mathematical framework connecting space and time coordinates between inertial frames (Hendrik Lorentz, 1904; Henri Poincaré, 1905). The interval (Δs)² = (cΔt)² − (Δx)² − (Δy)² − (Δz)² is invariant — all observers agree on it.

General Theory of Relativity (1915): Einstein's geometric theory of gravitation. Core idea: Gravity is not a force but curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. Einstein field equations: G_μν + Λg_μν = (8πG/c⁴)T_μν — relate spacetime curvature (left side) to matter-energy distribution (right side). Equivalence Principle: An observer in a uniformly accelerating reference frame cannot distinguish it from a uniform gravitational field — locally, gravity and acceleration are equivalent. Predictions and Confirmations: (1) Bending of Light: Starlight passing near the Sun is deflected — confirmed by Arthur Eddington's 1919 solar eclipse expedition (measurements at Principe, West Africa, and Sobral, Brazil), which made Einstein a global celebrity. (2) Gravitational Time Dilation: Clocks run slower in stronger gravity — confirmed by Pound–Rebka experiment (1959) using Mössbauer spectroscopy at Harvard's Jefferson Tower (22.6 m height difference, measured ~1 part in 10¹⁵). (3) Mercury's Perihelion Precession: Newtonian gravity could not account for 43 arcseconds/century anomaly in Mercury's orbit; GR explained it exactly — one of its first triumphs. (4) Gravitational Lensing: Massive objects bend spacetime, creating multiple images, arcs, or Einstein rings of distant galaxies — used to study dark matter distribution. First observed in 1979 ("Twin Quasar" Q0957+561). Hubble Space Telescope has captured stunning Einstein rings. (5) Black Holes: Predicted by Karl Schwarzschild (1916) as a solution to Einstein's field equations for a point mass. Schwarzschild radius Rₛ = 2GM/c² — for Sun, Rₛ ≈ 3 km; for Earth, ~9 mm. Event horizon: Boundary beyond which nothing can escape. Singularity: Point of infinite density at centre. Supermassive black holes at centres of galaxies: Sagittarius A* (Milky Way, 4.3 million M☉), M87* (6.5 billion M☉). First direct image of M87* event horizon by Event Horizon Telescope (2019, Nobel Prize 2020 — Genzel, Ghez, Penrose). Hawking radiation: Stephen Hawking (1974) predicted that black holes emit quantum radiation and eventually evaporate. (6) Gravitational Waves: Ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein (1916), first directly detected by LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) on 14 September 2015 — GW150914 from binary black hole merger (29 + 36 M☉ → 62 M☉, 3 M☉ converted to energy). Nobel Prize 2017 (Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne, Barry Barish). In 2017, GW170817 detected from binary neutron star merger — accompanied by gamma-ray burst and electromagnetic counterparts across spectrum, inaugurating multi-messenger astronomy. LIGO-India (Hingoli, Maharashtra) is under construction (~₹2,600 crore) as part of the global detector network. (7) Expanding Universe: Einstein initially added cosmological constant (Λ) to force a static universe. Hubble's 1929 discovery of expansion made Einstein call it his "biggest blunder." But the 2011 Nobel Prize (Perlmutter, Riess, Schmidt) showed expansion is accelerating — attributed to dark energy (Λ is the leading candidate). Dark energy constitutes ~68% of universe's energy, dark matter ~27%, ordinary matter only ~5%. Cosmic microwave background (CMB): Remnant radiation from the Big Bang (Penzias and Wilson, 1965, Nobel 1978) — blackbody spectrum at 2.725 K, provides snapshot of the universe at age 380,000 years.

2. Chemistry — Elements to Materials

Chemistry is the study of matter — its composition, structure, properties, and transformations. The discipline emerged from alchemy (practised in ancient Egypt, China, India, and the Islamic world) through the work of Robert Boyle (The Sceptical Chymist, 1661 — defined element), Antoine Lavoisier (father of modern chemistry, discovered role of oxygen in combustion, 1770s), John Dalton (atomic theory, 1808), Dmitri Mendeleev (periodic table, 1869), and Linus Pauling (chemical bonding). For UPSC, chemistry overlaps with environmental science, materials technology, and industrial processes.

Atomic Structure

Atom: Smallest unit of matter retaining chemical properties. Proton (+ve, mass ~1.0073 amu, discovered by Rutherford, 1919), Neutron (neutral, ~1.0087 amu, discovered by Chadwick, 1932 — Nobel 1935), Electron (−ve, mass 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg ~ 1/1836 amu, discovered by J.J. Thomson, 1897 — Nobel 1906). Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons — defines the element. Mass Number (A): Protons + neutrons. Isotopes: Same Z, different A (e.g., ¹H, ²H — deuterium, ³H — tritium). Isobars: Same A, different Z (e.g., ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰Ca). Isotones: Same number of neutrons (e.g., ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N).

Atomic Models (Historical Development)

  1. Dalton's Model (1808): Solid sphere — atoms are indivisible, identical for each element. Later disproven (subatomic particles discovered).
  2. Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1904): Positive sphere with embedded electrons. Disproven by Rutherford's gold foil experiment.
  3. Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911): Small dense positive nucleus surrounded by orbiting electrons. Gold foil (Geiger–Marsden) experiment: most α-particles passed through, some deflected → atoms are mostly empty space with tiny nucleus. Problem: orbiting electrons should radiate energy and spiral into nucleus — atom would be unstable.
  4. Bohr's Model (1913): Electrons orbit in fixed quantised energy levels (shells: K, L, M, N...). Angular momentum quantised: mvr = nh/2π. Electrons jump between levels by absorbing/emitting photons of energy ΔE = hν. Explained hydrogen spectrum perfectly but failed for multi-electron atoms. Niels Bohr — Nobel 1922.
  5. Quantum Mechanical Model (Schrödinger, 1926): Electrons exist as probability clouds (orbitals) described by wave function ψ. Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π. Orbitals: s (spherical, 1 orientation), p (dumbbell, 3 orientations: px, py, pz), d (cloverleaf, 5 orientations), f (7 orientations). Quantum numbers: n (principal, shell), l (azimuthal, subshell shape, 0 to n−1), m_l (magnetic, orientation, −l to +l), m_s (spin, ±½).

Periodic Table

7 periods, 18 groups. The modern periodic law: Physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) arranged elements by atomic weight, left gaps for undiscovered elements (predicted Ga, Ge, Sc), and reversed pairs (e.g., Te/I) to keep similar elements together — later confirmed by atomic numbers (Henry Moseley, 1913, using X-ray spectra).

Block Classification

  • s-block (Groups 1-2): Alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr — highly reactive, 1 valence e⁻, form +1 ions) and Alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra — 2 valence e⁻, form +2 ions).
  • p-block (Groups 13-18): Metals, metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te — properties between metals and non-metals), non-metals (C, N, O, P, S, Se, halogens), and noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn — full outer shell, inert).
  • d-block (Groups 3-12): Transition metals — variable oxidation states, coloured compounds, catalytic activity, magnetic properties. Examples: Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Au, Pt.
  • f-block (Lanthanides — Ce to Lu, and Actinides — Th to Lr): Inner transition elements. Lanthanides are chemically similar (lanthanide contraction — steady decrease in ionic radius across series due to poor shielding of 4f electrons). Actinides are radioactive — only Th, Pa, U occur naturally; others synthesised.

Periodic Trends

  • Atomic Radius: Decreases across a period (↑ effective nuclear charge, Z_eff, pulls electrons closer), increases down a group (↑ number of shells). Measured in pm (picometres).
  • Ionisation Energy (IE): Energy required to remove outermost electron. Increases across period, decreases down group. First IE generally increases left to right (exceptions: Be > B due to full s-orbital; N > O due to half-filled p-orbital). He has highest IE (2372 kJ/mol).
  • Electronegativity (EN): Tendency to attract shared electrons in a bond. Increases across period, decreases down group. F is highest (4.0) on the Pauling scale. Cs/Fr lowest (~0.7).
  • Electron Affinity (EA): Energy change when electron is added. Generally more negative (exothermic) across period. Cl has highest EA (−349 kJ/mol). Noble gases have positive EA (endothermic).
  • Metallic Character: Decreases across period, increases down group. Metals (left/bottom) — malleable, ductile, conduct heat/electricity. Non-metals (right/top) — brittle, insulators. Metalloids (staircase from B to At) — semiconducting.

Chemical Bonding

Atoms bond to achieve lower energy (greater stability). Octet Rule: Atoms tend to achieve 8 electrons in their valence shell (exceptions: H, He, Li need 2; expanded octet in PCl₅, SF₆).

  • Ionic Bond (Electrovalent): Complete electron transfer — metal loses electron(s) (cation), non-metal gains (anion). Electrostatic attraction holds them. E.g., NaCl (Na⁺Cl⁻), MgO, CaF₂. Properties: high melting/boiling points, soluble in water, conduct electricity when molten/dissolved (electrolytes), brittle. Lattice energy: energy released when ions come together to form crystal.
  • Covalent Bond: Electron sharing between non-metals. Single (σ bond — head-on overlap), Double (1σ + 1π — side-on overlap of p orbitals), Triple (1σ + 2π). E.g., H₂, O₂, N₂ (triple bond, very strong), CH₄, CO₂. Properties: low melting/boiling points, poor conductors, may be polar or non-polar. Polarity: due to EN difference between bonded atoms (e.g., H₂O is polar, CO₂ is non-polar due to linear symmetry). Polar covalent bond: Unequal sharing — partial charges δ⁺/δ⁻ (e.g., H—Cl).
  • Coordinate (Dative) Bond: Shared electrons both come from one atom. E.g., NH₃ → BF₃ adduct (N donates lone pair to B). NH₄⁺ (ammonium ion): one coordinate bond between NH₃ and H⁺.
  • Metallic Bond: Delocalised "sea of electrons" around positive metal ions. Explains high conductivity (electrons move freely), malleability, ductility, lustre.
  • Hydrogen Bond: Strong dipole-dipole attraction between H (covalently bonded to N/O/F) and lone pair of another electronegative atom. Intramolecular (within same molecule — o-nitrophenol) and Intermolecular (between molecules — H₂O, NH₃, HF). Responsible for: high boiling point of water (100°C vs H₂S −60°C), ice floating (less dense due to open hexagonal structure), DNA double helix (H-bonds between base pairs: A=T (2 bonds), G≡C (3 bonds)), protein secondary structure (α-helix, β-sheet).
  • van der Waals Forces: Weak intermolecular forces — London dispersion (instantaneous dipole-induced dipole, present in all molecules, strength ∝ molecular size/mass), dipole-dipole (polar molecules), dipole-induced dipole.

VSEPR Theory & Molecular Geometry

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) Theory (Gillespie & Nyholm, 1957): Electron pairs (bonded and lone) repel each other and arrange for maximum separation. Shapes: Linear (2 pairs — BeCl₂, CO₂), Trigonal planar (3 — BF₃, SO₃), Bent/V-shaped (2 bonded + 1 lone — SO₂), Tetrahedral (4 — CH₄, NH₃, H₂O), Trigonal bipyramidal (5 — PCl₅), Octahedral (6 — SF₆). Lone pair repulsion > bond pair repulsion → bond angles reduced (NH₃: 107°, H₂O: 104.5° vs CH₄: 109.5°).

Hybridisation

Mixing of atomic orbitals to form equivalent hybrid orbitals. sp (linear, 180° — BeCl₂, C₂H₂), sp² (trigonal planar, 120° — BF₃, C₂H₄, graphite), sp³ (tetrahedral, 109.5° — CH₄, diamond), sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal — PCl₅), sp³d² (octahedral — SF₆).

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals (bonding — lower energy, antibonding — higher energy). Bond order = (bonding e⁻ − antibonding e⁻)/2. Higher bond order → shorter, stronger bond. Paramagnetism/Diamagnetism: Unpaired electrons → paramagnetic (attracted to magnetic field — O₂). All paired → diamagnetic (repelled — N₂).

Catalysis

Catalyst: A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed in the process. Catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy (Eₐ). They do not affect the equilibrium constant or thermodynamics of the reaction — only the kinetics. The term was coined by Jöns Jacob Berzelius (1835). Enzymes are biological catalysts (proteins or RNA) with remarkable specificity and efficiency — rate enhancements up to 10¹⁷-fold (orotidine decarboxylase). Michaelis-Menten kinetics (1913) describes enzyme-catalysed reactions: v = V_max[S]/(K_m + [S]), where K_m is the substrate concentration at half-maximal velocity.

  • Homogeneous Catalysis: Catalyst and reactants in the same phase (usually liquid). Examples: Acid-catalysed esterification (H₂SO₄), Wilkinson's catalyst for hydrogenation (RhCl(PPh₃)₃), Ziegler-Natta catalysis for alkene polymerisation (TiCl₄/AlR₃).
  • Heterogeneous Catalysis: Catalyst in a different phase (usually solid, reactants gas/liquid). Surface reactions: adsorption → reaction → desorption. Examples: Haber-Bosch (Fe catalyst for NH₃), Contact process (V₂O₅ for SO₂ → SO₃), Ostwald process (Pt/Rh for NH₃ → NO), catalytic converters (Pt/Pd/Rh), Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (Fe/Co for CO + H₂ → hydrocarbons).
  • Phase Transfer Catalysis: Quaternary ammonium salts transfer ionic reactants into organic phase (e.g., benzyltriethylammonium chloride).
  • Nanocatalysis: Nanoparticles have very high surface-to-volume ratio — enhanced catalytic activity. Examples: Au nanoparticles for CO oxidation, Pt nanoparticles for fuel cells, Pd nanoparticles for coupling reactions.
  • Catalytic Poisons and Promoters: Poisons (e.g., S, Pb, As) bind irreversibly to active sites and deactivate catalysts (hence unleaded petrol needed for catalytic converters). Promoters (e.g., K₂O, Al₂O₃ in Haber-Bosch Fe catalyst) enhance activity without being catalytic themselves.
  • Automotive Catalysis: Three-way catalytic converter simultaneously oxidises CO and hydrocarbons (to CO₂ + H₂O) and reduces NOₓ (to N₂). Operates at ~400°C, requires stoichiometric air-fuel ratio (λ = 1). Oxygen sensor (ZrO₂-based) provides feedback for engine management.

States of Matter & Intermolecular Forces

Solid: Fixed shape/volume. Crystalline (regular repeating structure — NaCl, diamond, ice) vs Amorphous (no long-range order — glass, plastic). Liquid: Fixed volume, variable shape — flows. Surface tension, viscosity, capillary action. Gas: Variable shape/volume — fills container. Ideal Gas Law: PV = nRT. Real gases deviate at high pressure/low temperature (van der Waals equation corrects for molecular volume and intermolecular attraction). Plasma: Ionised gas (electrons stripped from atoms) — stars, lightning, neon signs, fusion reactors. Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC): Ultra-cold bosons (near 0 K) occupy same quantum state — behaves as single macroscopic quantum entity. Predicted by Satyendra Nath Bose and Albert Einstein (1924–25); first realised in lab (1995, Cornell/Wieman — Rb-87, Nobel 2001). Fermionic Condensate (Deborah Jin, 2003) — analogous for fermions.

Solutions, Acids, Bases & Redox

Concentration: Molarity (M = moles of solute/L of solution), Molality (m = moles/kg solvent), Normality (N = equivalents/L), Mole fraction (X), Mass percent. Colligative Properties: Depend only on number of solute particles, not identity. (1) Relative lowering of vapour pressure (Raoult's law), (2) Boiling point elevation (ΔT_b = K_b·m), (3) Freezing point depression (ΔT_f = K_f·m — used for anti-freeze, de-icing roads), (4) Osmotic pressure (π = iCRT — van't Hoff equation). Osmosis: Movement of solvent through semi-permeable membrane from low to high solute concentration. Reverse osmosis (RO) — applied pressure forces solvent opposite direction, used for water purification. van't Hoff factor (i): Accounts for dissociation/association of solute (NaCl → i ≈ 2, CaCl₂ → i ≈ 3).

pH Scale: 0–14; pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. 7 = neutral (pure water at 25°C), < 7 acidic, > 7 basic (alkaline). Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) fully dissociate. Weak acids (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃) partially dissociate. Indicators: Litmus (red in acid, blue in base), phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in base), methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base), universal indicator. Buffers: Resist pH change — weak acid + its salt (e.g., CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa) or weak base + its salt (e.g., NH₃/NH₄Cl). Blood bicarbonate buffer: H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ maintains pH ~7.35–7.45. Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKₐ + log([A⁻]/[HA]).

Oxidation-Reduction (Redox): Loss-gain of electrons (OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain). Oxidising agent gains electrons (gets reduced). Reducing agent loses electrons (gets oxidised). Oxidation Number: Artificial charge assigned to atoms. Rules: Free element = 0; Monatomic ion = charge; O usually −2 (except peroxides −1); H usually +1 (except metal hydrides −1); Sum = charge of species. Disproportionation: Same element simultaneously oxidised and reduced (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O). Electrochemical Cell: Chemical → electrical energy (voltaic/galvanic cell — spontaneous reaction). Daniel cell: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s), E° = 1.10 V. Standard electrode potential (E°): Measured relative to Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE = 0 V). Electrolytic Cell: Electrical → chemical energy (non-spontaneous, electrolysis). Faraday's laws: mass deposited ∝ charge passed (m = ZQ = (M/Fn)Q). Nernst Equation: E = E° − (RT/nF)lnQ — relates cell potential to concentration. Fuel Cell: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O + electricity (alkaline fuel cell used in Apollo/Shuttle missions, PEM fuel cells for vehicles). Batteries: Primary (non-rechargeable — dry cell/Leclanché cell, alkaline battery). Secondary (rechargeable — Li-ion: highest energy density, used in phones/laptops/EVs; lead-acid: car batteries). Li-ion battery: Nobel Prize 2019 (Goodenough, Whittingham, Yoshino).

Organic Chemistry

Study of carbon compounds. Carbon is unique due to catenation (ability to form long chains/bonds with itself) and formation of multiple bonds. Over 10 million known organic compounds. Key pioneers: Friedrich Wöhler (1828, synthesised urea from inorganic — disproved vitalism), August Kekulé (structure of benzene, 1865).

Hydrocarbons

  • Alkanes (Saturated): C—C single bonds, C_nH_{2n+2}. sp³ hybridised. Tetrahedral geometry. Nomenclature: methane (C₁), ethane (C₂), propane (C₃), butane (C₄), pentane (C₅)... Combustion: C_nH_{2n+2} + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → nCO₂ + (n+1)H₂O + heat. Substitution reactions (halogenation — RH + X₂ → RX + HX).
  • Alkenes (Unsaturated): C=C double bond, C_nH_{2n}. sp² hybridised, trigonal planar. π bond (weaker, more reactive). Geometrical isomerism (cis-trans). Addition reactions: hydrogenation (H₂/Pt → alkane), halogenation (Br₂ → dibromo), hydrohalogenation (HX → alkyl halide — Markovnikov's rule: H adds to C with more H's), hydration (H₂O/H⁺ → alcohol). Polymerisation: ethene → polyethylene.
  • Alkynes (Unsaturated): C≡C triple bond, C_nH_{2n−2}. sp hybridised, linear. Even more reactive. Addition reactions can go to alkene then alkane. Acetylene (C₂H₂) used in welding (oxyacetylene flame ~3500°C).
  • Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Benzene (C₆H₆) — planar hexagonal, sp² hybridised, delocalised π electrons (resonance — Kekulé structure with alternating double/single bonds). Hückel's rule: (4n+2)π electrons for aromaticity (n = 1 for benzene → 6π). Aromatic substitution reactions (electrophilic — nitration, sulphonation, halogenation, Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation).

Polymers

Large molecules (macromolecules) composed of repeating monomer units. Addition Polymerisation: Monomers with C=C double bonds add together (free radical mechanism). Examples: PE (polyethylene — plastic bags, bottles), PP (polypropylene — containers, ropes), PVC (polyvinyl chloride — pipes, flooring), PS (polystyrene — foam packaging), PTFE/Teflon (non-stick coating, chemically inert), PMMA/Acrylic (plexiglass, shatterproof alternative to glass). Condensation Polymerisation: Monomers join with elimination of small molecule (H₂O, HCl). Examples: Nylon-66 (polyamide — textiles, cordage — hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid), Polyester (PET — textile fibre, plastic bottles — ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid), Bakelite (phenol-formaldehyde, thermosetting — electrical switches, handles), Kevlar (aramid — high strength, bulletproof vests). Natural Polymers: Cellulose (plant cell walls), Starch (energy storage in plants), Proteins (amino acid polymers), DNA/RNA (nucleic acid polymers), Rubber (polyisoprene from Hevea brasiliensis). Biodegradable Polymers: PLA (polylactic acid from corn starch, used in 3D printing, compostable packaging), PHBV, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA).

Important Organic Reactions

  • Substitution: One atom/group replaces another (alkane halogenation, aromatic substitution). SN1 (unimolecular, carbocation intermediate, tertiary > secondary > primary) and SN2 (bimolecular, concerted, primary > secondary > tertiary, inversion of configuration).
  • Addition: Atoms add across double/triple bond (hydrogenation, halogenation, hydrohalogenation, hydration).
  • Elimination: Removal of atoms to form double bond — E1 and E2 (dehydration of alcohol, dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halide — Saytzeff rule).
  • Oxidation: Alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺, KMnO₄).
  • Reduction: Aldehyde/ketone → alcohol (NaBH₄, LiAlH₄). Carboxylic acid → alcohol (LiAlH₄). Nitro → amine (Sn/HCl).
  • Esterification: Carboxylic acid + Alcohol → Ester + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst).
  • Saponification: Ester + NaOH → Alcohol + Sodium carboxylate (soap — salts of long-chain fatty acids).

Environmental Chemistry

Air Pollution: Primary pollutants (directly emitted — CO, NOₓ, SO₂, PM, VOCs) and Secondary pollutants (formed in atmosphere — O₃ (tropospheric), PAN, smog). Photochemical Smog: NOₓ + VOCs + sunlight → O₃ + PAN (brown haze, eye irritation, respiratory issues — Los Angeles type). London Smog: SO₂ + soot + fog (reducing smog — 1952 London killed ~12,000). Greenhouse Gases: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, CFCs, SF₆, HFCs, PFCs. Global Warming Potential (GWP): CO₂ = 1 (reference), CH₄ = 28, N₂O = 265, SF₆ = 23,500, CFC-12 = 10,200. Catalytic Converter: Pt/Pd/Rh catalyst — converts CO → CO₂, HC → CO₂ + H₂O, NOₓ → N₂. Ozone Depletion: CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) release Cl radicals in stratosphere via UV photolysis: Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂; ClO + O → Cl + O₂ — catalytic cycle destroys thousands of O₃ molecules per Cl atom. Montreal Protocol (1987): Phased out CFCs — ozone layer expected to recover by ~2060. Kigali Amendment (2016): Phase down HFCs.

Water Pollution: BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand — amount of O₂ consumed by microbes to decompose organic matter; higher BOD = more pollution). Clean water: BOD < 3 mg/L; Polluted: > 8 mg/L. COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand — total organic matter oxidisable chemically). Eutrophication: Excess nutrients (N, P from fertilisers/detergents/sewage) → algal bloom → algal death → microbial decomposition depletes O₂ → fish kills, dead zones (e.g., Gulf of Mexico, Lake Erie). Heavy Metal Pollution: Hg (Minamata disease — Japan, methylmercury in seafood), Cd (Itai-itai — Japan, cadmium poisoning), Pb (neurological damage, especially in children), As (arsenicosis — West Bengal, Bangladesh groundwater). Biomagnification: Increase in concentration of persistent pollutants (DDT, Hg, PCBs) as they move up food chain. Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs): Regulated under Stockholm Convention (2001).

Soil Pollution: Pesticides (DDT banned in agriculture, still used for malaria vector control), herbicides, industrial waste, heavy metals, microplastics, excessive fertiliser use → soil degradation, salinisation, desertification.

Metallurgy & Important Compounds

Metallurgy: Science of extracting metals from ores. Ores are minerals from which metals can be economically extracted. Gangue: Unwanted rocky material in ore. Extraction: Pyrometallurgy (heat — calcination (carbonates → oxide + CO₂), roasting (sulphides → oxide + SO₂), smelting (reduction of oxide using C/CO — blast furnace for Fe)). Hydrometallurgy (aqueous solution — leaching of Au using NaCN, Cu using dilute H₂SO₄). Electrometallurgy (electrolysis — extraction of highly reactive metals: Na, Mg, Al — Hall-Héroult process for Al). Refining: Electrolytic refining (Cu — anode: impure Cu, cathode: pure Cu, electrolyte: CuSO₄), Zone refining (Si, Ge — ultra-pure semiconductors), Mond's process (Ni — carbonyl complex formation/decomposition).

Important Industrial Compounds

  • Ammonia (NH₃) — Haber-Bosch Process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (Fe catalyst, 450°C, 200 atm). Feedstock for fertilisers (urea, ammonium nitrate), explosives (HNO₃), plastics. Fritz Haber (Nobel 1918), Carl Bosch (Nobel 1931).
  • Sulfuric Acid (H₂SO₄) — Contact Process: S → SO₂ → SO₃ (V₂O₅ catalyst) → H₂SO₄. "King of chemicals" — used in fertilisers, petroleum refining, batteries, chemical manufacturing.
  • Nitric Acid (HNO₃) — Ostwald Process: NH₃ → NO → NO₂ → HNO₃. Used for fertilisers (NH₄NO₃), explosives (TNT, RDX, nitroglycerine).
  • Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) — Caustic Soda: Electrolysis of brine (Chlor-alkali process: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂). Used in soap, paper, textiles.
  • Sodium Carbonate (Na₂CO₃) — Solvay Process: NaCl + CaCO₃ + NH₃ → Na₂CO₃ (soda ash — glass manufacturing, detergents).
  • Baking Soda (NaHCO₃) — Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2NaHCO₃. Used in baking (CO₂ released), antacid, fire extinguisher.
  • Bleaching Powder (CaOCl₂): Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O. Used for bleaching textiles, water disinfection.
  • Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O): Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) heated to 150°C. Sets when mixed with water. Used in casts, moulds, construction.

Analytical Chemistry Techniques

Spectroscopy: Study of interaction between matter and electromagnetic radiation. Used for qualitative and quantitative analysis. UV-Visible Spectroscopy: Measures absorption of UV (200–400 nm) and visible (400–800 nm) light. Electronic transitions (σ→σ*, n→σ*, π→π*, n→π*). Beer-Lambert Law: A = εlc (absorbance = molar absorptivity × path length × concentration). Used for quantifying transition metal ions, organic chromophores, DNA/protein concentration (260/280 nm ratio). Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy: Measures molecular vibrations — stretching, bending, rocking. Characteristic absorptions: O–H (~3300 cm⁻¹ broad), N–H (~3300 cm⁻¹), C=O (~1700 cm⁻¹), C–O (~1100 cm⁻¹). Used for functional group identification. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) Spectroscopy: Uses magnetic fields and radio waves to probe atomic nuclei (¹H, ¹³C). Chemical shifts (δ, ppm) reveal electronic environment. Spin-spin coupling (splitting patterns: singlet, doublet, triplet, quartet) reveals neighbouring protons. 2D NMR (COSY, HSQC, HMBC) for structure determination. Discovered independently by Felix Bloch and Edward Mills Purcell (1946, Nobel 1952). MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) is an application of NMR for medical imaging. Mass Spectrometry (MS): Ionises molecules and separates them by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Ionisation methods: EI (electron impact), ESI (electrospray), MALDI (matrix-assisted laser desorption). Mass analysers: quadrupole, TOF (time-of-flight), ion trap, Orbitrap. Applications: molecular weight determination, fragmentation pattern analysis, proteomics, metabolomics, drug testing, environmental analysis. X-ray Crystallography: Bombards crystal with X-rays → diffraction pattern → electron density map → atomic structure. Solved by Max von Laue (1912, Nobel 1914); applied by W.H. and W.L. Bragg (Bragg's law: nλ = 2d sinθ). Used to determine structure of DNA (Franklin, Watson, Crick, 1953), proteins (Kendrew, Perutz), and complex molecules.

Chromatography: Separates mixtures based on differential partitioning between stationary and mobile phases. Paper Chromatography: Stationary phase = paper, mobile phase = solvent. Used for amino acid, pigment separation. Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC): Faster, more sensitive — silica gel on glass/plastic plate. Column Chromatography: Stationary phase packed in column, elution by solvent gradient. Gas Chromatography (GC): Mobile phase = inert gas (He, N₂), stationary phase = liquid coated on column. Used for volatile compounds — petrochemicals, pesticides, essential oils. GC-MS combines separation with mass identification. High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC): High pressure forces mobile phase through packed column. Reversed-phase (C₁₈ columns) most common. Applications: pharmaceutical analysis, drug purity testing, environmental monitoring. Ion Exchange Chromatography: Separates ions and polar molecules based on charge. Used in water purification, protein purification. Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC): Separates by molecular size — larger molecules elute first. Affinity Chromatography: Exploits specific binding (e.g., antibody-antigen, enzyme-substrate) — highly selective purification.

Electroanalytical Methods: Potentiometry: Measures potential (voltage) between electrodes — pH meter uses glass electrode, ion-selective electrodes (ISE) for F⁻, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺. Coulometry: Measures charge passed. Voltammetry: Measures current as function of applied potential — cyclic voltammetry for studying redox behaviour. Conductometry: Measures solution conductivity — used for determining purity of water, endpoint detection in titrations. Thermal Analysis: TGA (thermogravimetric analysis — mass change vs temperature), DSC (differential scanning calorimetry — heat flow vs temperature), DTA (differential thermal analysis). Used for studying decomposition, phase transitions, purity, and thermal stability of materials.

3. Biology — Human Body & Health

Biology (from Greek bios = life, logos = study) is the science of living organisms and life processes. Major milestones: Aristotle (384–322 BCE) — first classification. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723) — discovered microorganisms. Robert Hooke (1665) — coined "cell." Charles Darwin (1859) — theory of evolution by natural selection. Gregor Mendel (1865) — laws of inheritance, father of genetics. James Watson & Francis Crick (1953) — double helix structure of DNA. Human Genome Project (1990–2003) — sequenced the entire human genome.

Cell: The Unit of Life

The Cell Theory (Schleiden & Schwann, 1838–39; refined by Virchow, 1855): (1) All living organisms are composed of cells. (2) The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. (3) All cells arise from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula e cellula).

Cell Organelles — Structure & Function

  • Plasma Membrane: Phospholipid bilayer (~8 nm thick) with embedded proteins. Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicholson, 1972). Functions: selective permeability, cell signalling, cell-cell recognition. Transport: Passive (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion — carrier/channel proteins, osmosis) and Active (requires ATP — Na⁺/K⁺ pump, endocytosis, exocytosis).
  • Nucleus: Largest organelle (~5–10 μm). Surrounded by double membrane (nuclear envelope) with nuclear pores. Contains: Chromatin (DNA + histone proteins — nucleosomes; condenses into chromosomes during cell division). Nucleolus: Site of rRNA synthesis and ribosome assembly.
  • Mitochondria: "Powerhouse of the cell." Double membrane: outer membrane (smooth), inner membrane (folded into cristae — increases surface area for ETC). Matrix contains circular DNA (mtDNA — maternal inheritance, ~16.6 kb in humans), 70S ribosomes, and enzymes for Krebs cycle. Cellular Respiration: Glycolysis (cytoplasm, 2 ATP) → Krebs Cycle (mitochondrial matrix, 2 ATP) → ETC (inner membrane, ~34 ATP via oxidative phosphorylation). Total: ~36–38 ATP per glucose. Endosymbiotic Theory (Lynn Margulis, 1967): Mitochondria and chloroplasts originated from free-living prokaryotes engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cell.
  • Chloroplast (Plants only): Double membrane + internal thylakoid membrane system (grana — stacks of thylakoids). Thylakoid membrane contains chlorophyll and ETC for light reactions. Stroma (fluid matrix) contains Calvin cycle enzymes. Circular DNA, 70S ribosomes.
  • Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): Network of membranous tubules and sacs (cisternae). Rough ER (studded with ribosomes) — protein synthesis, folding, glycosylation. Smooth ER (no ribosomes) — lipid synthesis, detoxification (liver — cytochrome P450), Ca²⁺ storage (muscle — sarcoplasmic reticulum).
  • Golgi Apparatus: Stack of flattened cisternae (4–8). Cis face (receiving, near ER) → medial cisternae → trans face (shipping). Functions: modification (glycosylation, sulfation), sorting, packaging of proteins into vesicles.
  • Lysosomes: Membrane-bound vesicles containing hydrolytic enzymes (acid hydrolases). pH ~5 (maintained by H⁺ pump). Functions: intracellular digestion (phagocytosis — engulfed bacteria digested, autophagy — recycling old organelles). Suicidal bags of cell: Rupture leads to autolysis. Tay-Sachs disease: Lysosomal storage disorder.
  • Ribosomes: Non-membranous, composed of rRNA (~60%) and proteins (~40%). Two subunits: 70S in prokaryotes, 80S in eukaryotes. S = Svedberg unit. Free ribosomes — cytoplasmic proteins. Bound ribosomes (on RER) — secretory/membrane proteins. Polyribosomes (Polysomes): Multiple ribosomes translating same mRNA simultaneously.
  • Peroxisomes: Single membrane, contain oxidases (produce H₂O₂) and catalase (breaks down H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂). Functions: β-oxidation of fatty acids, detoxification of alcohol (liver).
  • Cytoskeleton: Protein filaments providing structural support, cell shape, movement, and intracellular transport. Microfilaments (actin — 7 nm, cell movement, muscle contraction, cytokinesis). Intermediate filaments (10 nm — keratin in skin, desmin in muscle, neurofilaments in neurons). Microtubules (25 nm — hollow tubes of α/β-tubulin, components of cilia, flagella, spindle fibres, intracellular transport via motor proteins: kinesin and dynein).
  • Centrosome/Centrioles: Microtubule organising centre (MTOC). Centrioles (9+0 triplet arrangement) — involved in spindle formation during cell division. Absent in higher plants.
  • Cilia & Flagella: Hair-like projections with 9+2 microtubule arrangement (9 outer doublets + 2 central singlets). Dynein motor proteins cause sliding → bending. Cilia: Short, numerous — move fluid/mucus (respiratory tract). Flagella: Long, few — cell propulsion (sperm tail).
  • Cell Wall (Plants, Fungi, Bacteria): Primary cell wall (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin) — flexible, allows growth. Secondary cell wall (lignin — rigid, waterproof — wood). Plasmodesmata: Cytoplasmic channels connecting adjacent plant cells.

Cell Division

Cell Cycle: Interphase (G₁ — cell growth; S — DNA synthesis; G₂ — preparation for mitosis) + M Phase (Mitosis + Cytokinesis). Checkpoints: G₁/S (restriction point — p53 tumour suppressor crucial here), G₂/M (checks DNA replication completeness), M (spindle checkpoint). Mitosis: Produces 2 genetically identical diploid daughter cells. Stages: Prophase (chromosomes condense, nuclear envelope breaks, spindle forms), Metaphase (chromosomes align at metaphase plate), Anaphase (sister chromatids separate), Telophase (chromosomes decondense, nuclear envelope reforms). Cytokinesis: animal cells (cleavage furrow), plant cells (cell plate). Functions: growth, tissue repair, asexual reproduction. Meiosis: Produces 4 genetically non-identical haploid gametes. Meiosis I (Reductional): homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) → crossing over (chiasmata) → homologous chromosomes separate. Meiosis II (Equational): sister chromatids separate. Significance: genetic variation (crossing over + independent assortment). Nondisjunction → aneuploidy (Down syndrome — trisomy 21, Turner — XO, Klinefelter — XXY).

Genetics and Heredity

Gregor Mendel (1822–1884): Father of genetics. Experiments on pea plants (Pisum sativum) at Brno monastery (1856–1863). Studied seven pairs of contrasting traits. Mendel's Laws: (1) Law of Segregation: Alleles segregate during gamete formation — each gamete carries only one allele for each gene. (2) Law of Independent Assortment: Genes for different traits assort independently during gamete formation (applies only to genes on different chromosomes or far apart on same chromosome). (3) Law of Dominance: In a heterozygote, the dominant allele masks the recessive allele. Monohybrid cross: 3:1 phenotypic ratio. Dihybrid cross: 9:3:3:1 ratio. Mendel's work was published (1866) but ignored until rediscovered independently by Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak in 1900.

Extensions of Mendelian Genetics: Incomplete Dominance: Heterozygote shows intermediate phenotype (snapdragon flower colour — RR = red, Rr = pink, rr = white). Codominance: Both alleles expressed simultaneously (ABO blood group — IA and IB alleles both expressed in AB type). Multiple Alleles: More than two alleles exist for a gene (ABO — three alleles: IA, IB, i). Pleiotropy: One gene affects multiple traits (Marfan syndrome, PKU). Epistasis: One gene masks/modifies expression of another gene (coat colour in mice — B gene for colour, C gene for pigment deposition; cc is albino regardless of B). Polygenic Inheritance: Multiple genes contribute to a continuous trait (height, skin colour, IQ — shows normal distribution). Linkage and Recombination: Genes on same chromosome are linked (tend to be inherited together). Crossing over during meiosis I creates recombination. T.H. Morgan (Nobel 1933) discovered linkage using fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster). Recombination frequency → genetic map distance (centimorgans, cM). 1% recombination = 1 cM.

Sex Determination: Humans: XX (female), XY (male). The SRY gene on Y chromosome triggers male development. Sex-linked Inheritance: X-linked recessive — colour blindness, haemophilia (more common in males — need only one recessive X). Carrier females transmit to sons. X-linked dominant: Rett syndrome, fragile X syndrome. Y-linked: SRY, hairy ears (very few traits). Sex determination in other organisms: Birds (ZZ male, ZW female), Grasshoppers (XO male, XX female), Honeybees (haplodiploidy — drones are haploid). Barr Body: Inactivated X chromosome in female mammalian cells — discovered by Murray Barr (1949). Random X-inactivation occurs early in embryonic development (calico cats — mosaic of orange/black coat colour).

Chromosomal Disorders: Aneuploidy: Nondisjunction during meiosis. Autosomal: Down syndrome (trisomy 21 — 47, +21; characterised by intellectual disability, flat facial profile, single palmar crease; risk increases with maternal age >35). Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18), Patau syndrome (trisomy 13). Sex Chromosome: Turner syndrome (45, X — monosomy X, short stature, webbed neck, sterile). Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY — tall, small testes, infertility). XYY syndrome (47, XYY — normal, slightly taller). Structural Abnormalities: Deletions (Cri-du-chat — deletion of chromosome 5p), Duplications, Inversions, Translocations (Philadelphia chromosome — t(9;22) in chronic myeloid leukaemia).

Pedigree Analysis: Family tree showing inheritance patterns — used to determine mode of inheritance (autosomal dominant/recessive, X-linked, Y-linked). Symbols: squares (male), circles (female), shaded (affected), half-shaded (carrier).

Tissues

  • Epithelial Tissue: Covering/lining surfaces (skin epidermis, gut lining, blood vessels endothelium). Functions: protection, absorption (intestinal epithelium — microvilli), secretion (glandular epithelium), sensation. Types: simple (single layer — squamous, cuboidal, columnar, pseudostratified) and stratified (multiple layers — protection). Connected by tight junctions, adherens junctions, desmosomes, gap junctions.
  • Connective Tissue: Most abundant and diverse. Cells scattered in extracellular matrix (ECM: protein fibres + ground substance). Types: Loose (areolar — under skin, around organs; adipose — fat storage, insulation), Dense (regular — tendons/ligaments; irregular — dermis), Cartilage (hyaline — nose, trachea, joints; elastic — ear; fibrocartilage — intervertebral discs), Bone (compact and spongy — osteocytes in lacunae), Blood (plasma + cells).
  • Muscle Tissue: Skeletal (striated, voluntary — attached to bones, multinucleated fibres, fast contraction). Smooth (non-striated, involuntary — walls of hollow organs, spindle-shaped, single nucleus, slow sustained contraction). Cardiac (striated, involuntary — heart only, branched cells with intercalated discs, autorhythmic, does not fatigue). Sliding filament theory: actin filaments slide over myosin filaments, powered by ATP. Sarcomere (Z-line to Z-line) — basic contractile unit.
  • Nervous Tissue: Neurons: Conduct electrical impulses. Structure: dendrites, cell body/soma, axon (myelinated by Schwann cells in PNS, oligodendrocytes in CNS — Nodes of Ranvier speed conduction via saltatory conduction). Types: sensory (afferent), motor (efferent), interneurons. Glial Cells: Astrocytes (blood-brain barrier), Microglia (immune defence), Oligodendrocytes (myelination in CNS), Schwann cells (myelination in PNS), Ependymal cells (produce CSF).

Plant Physiology (Basic Concepts)

Photosynthesis: The process by which plants convert light energy into chemical energy. 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Discovered by Jan Ingenhousz (1779) who showed that plants release O₂ only in sunlight; earlier, Joseph Priestley (1771) had shown plants restore air consumed by candles. Light Reactions: Occur in thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts. Photosystems PSII (P680) and PSI (P700) absorb photons → excite electrons → electron transport chain generates ATP (photophosphorylation) and NADPH. Water is split (photolysis) releasing O₂. Calvin Cycle (Dark Reactions): Occurs in stroma. CO₂ is fixed by RuBisCO (most abundant protein on Earth) into 3-phosphoglycerate → reduced to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) using ATP and NADPH. C₃, C₄, and CAM Pathways: C₃ (most plants — rice, wheat, soybean — photorespiration reduces efficiency). C₄ (maize, sugarcane — spatial separation of carbon fixation and Calvin cycle — Kranz anatomy, bundle sheath cells — more efficient in hot/dry conditions). CAM (cacti, succulents — temporal separation, open stomata at night). Discoverers: C₄ pathway by Hatch and Slack (1966).

Plant Hormones (Phytohormones): Auxins (IAA) — cell elongation, apical dominance, phototropism, synthetic auxins (2,4-D) as herbicides. Discovered by F.W. Went (1926). Gibberellins — stem elongation, seed germination, bolting in rosette plants. Discovered from Gibberella fujikuroi fungus (Japan, 1920s). Cytokinins — cell division, shoot formation, delay senescence. Abscisic Acid (ABA) — stress hormone, stomatal closure, seed dormancy. Ethylene — fruit ripening (C₂H₄, gaseous), senescence, abscission. Used to ripen fruits artificially (bananas, mangoes). Ethephon releases ethylene and is used in agriculture. Brassinosteroids — cell division, stress tolerance. Strigolactones — branching inhibition, root symbiosis. Jasmonates — plant defence against herbivores and pathogens.

Transport in Plants: Xylem: Conducts water and minerals from roots to shoots — vessels and tracheids. Transpiration pull (cohesion-tension theory — Dixon and Joly, 1894): water evaporates from leaves → tension pulls water column upward → cohesion between water molecules maintains column. Transpiration also cools leaves and facilitates mineral transport. Phloem: Conducts sucrose and other organic compounds (translocation) from source to sink. Sieve tube elements + companion cells. Mass flow hypothesis (Ernst Münch, 1930): pressure gradient drives flow — loading at source (active transport) creates high osmotic pressure, unloading at sink lowers it. Mineral Nutrition: Essential elements: macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S — required in large amounts) and micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo, B, Cl, Ni — trace amounts). Nitrogen fixation: symbiotic (Rhizobium in root nodules of legumes) and free-living (Azotobacter, cyanobacteria). Mycorrhizae: fungal symbionts enhance water and mineral absorption.

Photoperiodism and Vernalisation: Flowering in response to day length — short-day plants (chrysanthemum, soybean), long-day plants (spinach, wheat), day-neutral plants (tomato, sunflower). Phytochrome: Photoreceptor pigment (P₆₆₀ and P₇₃₀ forms) that mediates photoperiodic responses. Vernalisation: cold treatment required for flowering in some plants (winter wheat, cabbage) — discovered by Trofim Lysenko.

Human Body Systems

Digestive System

Alimentary Canal: Mouth → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine (Duodenum → Jejunum → Ileum) → Large Intestine (Caecum → Colon → Rectum) → Anus. Accessory Organs: Salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual — secrete amylase), Liver (largest internal organ ~1.5 kg, produces bile — emulsifies fats, stores glycogen, detoxifies, synthesises plasma proteins, produces urea), Gallbladder (stores bile), Pancreas (exocrine — digestive enzymes: trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, pancreatic amylase, lipase; endocrine — insulin/glucagon from islets of Langerhans).

Digestion Process: Mouth: mechanical (teeth) + chemical (salivary amylase → starch → maltose). Oesophagus: peristalsis. Stomach: HCL (activates pepsinogen → pepsin, kills bacteria, denatures proteins), pepsin (proteins → peptides), intrinsic factor (B₁₂ absorption). Chyme passes to duodenum via pyloric sphincter. Small Intestine: complete digestion (pancreatic enzymes + bile + intestinal enzymes — maltase, sucrase, lactase, peptidases). Absorption: Villi and microvilli greatly increase surface area (~200 m²). Monosaccharides, amino acids → blood capillaries (hepatic portal vein → liver). Fatty acids + glycerol → lacteals (lymphatic) → blood. Large Intestine: Water and electrolyte absorption, bacterial fermentation (vitamin K, B₁₂, biotin production), faeces formation.

Respiratory System

Nose → Pharynx → Larynx (voice box — vocal cords) → Trachea (windpipe, C-shaped cartilage rings) → Bronchi (right — wider, shorter, more vertical; left — narrower, more horizontal) → Bronchioles (no cartilage, smooth muscle — asthma causes bronchoconstriction) → Alveolar ducts → Alveoli (~300 million, total surface area ~70–100 m²). Gas exchange across respiratory membrane (alveolar epithelium + capillary endothelium — each 0.2 μm thick).

Mechanism: Inhalation: Diaphragm contracts + external intercostals contract → thoracic volume increases → pressure decreases (Boyle's law) → air rushes in. Exhalation: passive — diaphragm relaxes, elastic recoil of lungs. Lung volumes: Tidal volume (~500 mL), Inspiratory Reserve Volume (~3000 mL), Expiratory Reserve Volume (~1200 mL), Residual Volume (~1200 mL). Vital Capacity (~4.8 L). Total Lung Capacity (~6 L). Gas Transport: O₂: 97% bound to haemoglobin (Hb), 3% dissolved. O₂ dissociation curve: sigmoid shape (cooperative binding). Factors shifting curve right (decreased affinity → O₂ unloading): ↑ temperature (exercise), ↓ pH (Bohr effect), ↑ 2,3-BPG. CO₂ transport: 70% as bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻ — carbonic anhydrase in RBC), 23% bound to Hb (carbaminohemoglobin), 7% dissolved. Haldane effect: Deoxygenated Hb has greater affinity for CO₂ — enhances CO₂ uptake in tissues.

Circulatory System

Heart: Four-chambered muscular pump (~300 g, beats ~100,000 times/day). Chambers: Right Atrium (receives deoxygenated blood from body via vena cava), Right Ventricle (pumps blood to lungs via pulmonary artery), Left Atrium (receives oxygenated blood from lungs via pulmonary veins), Left Ventricle (thickest wall — pumps blood to body via aorta). Valves: Atrioventricular (Tricuspid — right, Mitral/Bicuspid — left), Semilunar (Pulmonary, Aortic).

Cardiac Cycle: Systole (contraction) and Diastole (relaxation). Heart sounds: "lub" (S₁ — AV valves closing) and "dub" (S₂ — semilunar valves closing). ECG: P wave (atrial depolarisation), QRS complex (ventricular depolarisation), T wave (ventricular repolarisation). Cardiac Conduction: SA Node (pacemaker, 72 bpm, right atrium) → AV Node (delays signal ~0.1s) → Bundle of His → Left/Right Bundle Branches → Purkinje fibres.

Blood Vessels: Arteries (thick muscular/elastic walls, carry blood away under high pressure), Arterioles (resistance vessels), Capillaries (single endothelial cell layer — exchange site), Venules, Veins (thin walls, larger lumen, valves — capacitance vessels, hold ~60% blood volume). Blood Pressure: 120/80 mmHg typical. Hypertension (>140/90) — risk factor for heart disease, stroke.

Blood Components: Plasma (55% — water ~92%, proteins ~7%: albumin (osmotic pressure), globulins (antibodies, transport), fibrinogen (clotting), RBCs (45% — erythrocytes: 5–6 million/μL, 120-day lifespan, biconcave disc, no nucleus/mitochondria, Hb ~15 g/dL). WBCs (leukocytes): 4,000–11,000/μL. Granulocytes: Neutrophils (50–70%, phagocytosis), Eosinophils (2–4%, allergy/parasite defence), Basophils (<1%, histamine). Agranulocytes: Lymphocytes (20–40% — B cells, T cells, NK cells), Monocytes (2–8% → macrophages). Platelets (thrombocytes): 150,000–450,000/μL, clotting. Blood Groups (ABO & Rh): Discovered by Karl Landsteiner (1900, Nobel 1930). Type A (A antigen, anti-B), Type B (B antigen, anti-A), Type AB (both antigens, no antibodies — universal recipient), Type O (no antigens, both antibodies — universal donor). Rh factor: Rh+ (85% have D antigen), Rh− (15%, no D antigen → HDN risk in pregnancy → prevented by RhoGAM).

Excretory System

Kidneys: Pair of bean-shaped organs (~150 g each), retroperitoneal. Structure: Outer cortex (glomeruli, PCT, DCT) → Inner medulla (Loop of Henle, collecting ducts) → Renal pelvis → Ureter → Urinary bladder → Urethra. Nephron: Functional unit (~1 million per kidney). Components: Renal corpuscle (Glomerulus + Bowman's capsule). PCT — reabsorption of ~65% of filtrate (glucose, amino acids, Na⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻, water). Loop of Henle — establishes medullary osmotic gradient (countercurrent multiplier). Descending limb: water permeable, not salt. Ascending limb: salt actively pumped out. DCT — regulated reabsorption (Ca²⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻) and secretion (H⁺, K⁺). Collecting Duct — water reabsorption (regulated by ADH) and urea recycling.

Urine Formation: (1) Glomerular filtration (~180 L/day filtrate). (2) Tubular reabsorption (~178.5 L reabsorbed — 99% of filtrate). (3) Tubular secretion (H⁺, K⁺, NH₃, drug metabolites). Final urine: ~1.5 L/day. GFR: ~125 mL/min. Regulation: autoregulation, sympathetic nervous system, hormones (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system — RAAS, ANP). Dialysis: Artificial filtration for kidney failure — hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis.

Nervous System

CNS: Brain + Spinal Cord. PNS: Cranial nerves (12 pairs) + Spinal nerves (31 pairs). PNS divisions: Sensory (afferent) and Motor (efferent). Motor further: Somatic (voluntary — skeletal muscles) and Autonomic (involuntary). ANS: Sympathetic (fight or flight — ↑ HR, bronchodilation, ↑ blood glucose, ↓ digestion — noradrenaline). Parasympathetic (rest and digest — ↓ HR, ↑ digestion — acetylcholine). Enteric Nervous System: Controls GI tract independently.

Neuron Function: Resting potential: −70 mV. Action potential: Threshold → Na⁺ channels open → depolarisation (+30 mV) → K⁺ channels open → repolarisation → hyperpolarisation. All-or-none principle. Myelination → saltatory conduction. Synapse: Neurotransmitters: Acetylcholine, Glutamate (excitatory), GABA (inhibitory), Dopamine (reward, movement — Parkinson's: deficiency), Serotonin (mood — SSRIs for depression), Noradrenaline, Endorphins.

Brain: ~1.4 kg, consumes ~20% of body's O₂. Cerebrum: Largest part (85%), two hemispheres (corpus callosum). Cerebral cortex (grey matter). Lobes: Frontal (voluntary movement, decision-making, Broca's area — speech production), Parietal (somatosensory), Temporal (auditory, memory — hippocampus, Wernicke's area — speech comprehension), Occipital (vision). Basal ganglia: Movement control (Parkinson's). Limbic System: Emotion, memory — amygdala (fear), hippocampus (memory formation), hypothalamus (homeostasis: hunger, thirst, body temperature, pituitary control). Cerebellum: Coordination, balance. Brainstem: Midbrain (visual/auditory reflexes), Pons (sleep regulation), Medulla Oblongata (vital centres — cardiac, respiratory, vasomotor).

Spinal Cord: ~45 cm, extends to L₁-L₂. Reflex Arc: Sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector. Examples: knee-jerk reflex (monosynaptic), withdrawal reflex (polysynaptic). Meninges: Dura mater, Arachnoid mater, Pia mater. CSF: Produced in choroid plexuses, cushions, nourishes, removes waste.

Sense Organs: Eye: Cornea → pupil (iris) → lens (accommodation) → retina (rods — dim light, rhodopsin; cones — colour: S/M/L for blue/green/red). Visual pathway: photoreceptor → bipolar → ganglion → optic nerve → optic chiasm → LGN (thalamus) → visual cortex. Ear: Outer (pinna, ear canal, tympanic membrane). Evolution and Natural Selection

Charles Darwin (1809–1882) and Alfred Russel Wallace independently proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection. Darwin's five-year voyage on HMS Beagle (1831–1836) — particularly observations in the Galápagos Islands (finch beak variations, tortoise shell shapes, mockingbird differences) — led him to the idea that species change over time. His seminal work On the Origin of Species (1859) presented evidence and the mechanism of natural selection. Key points of natural selection: (1) Overproduction — more offspring produced than can survive. (2) Variation — individuals within a population vary in traits. (3) Inheritance — some variations are heritable. (4) Differential survival and reproduction — individuals with advantageous traits are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing those traits to the next generation. Over generations, this leads to adaptation and eventually speciation. Evidence for evolution: (1) Fossil record: Transitional forms (Archaeopteryx — dinosaur to bird; Tiktaalik — fish to tetrapod; whale ancestors with legs). (2) Homologous structures: Same skeletal plan in forelimbs of mammals (human arm, whale flipper, bat wing, cat leg) — common descent. (3) Analogous structures: Wings of insects, birds, bats — convergent evolution (similar function, different origin). (4) Vestigial organs: Appendix, coccyx (tailbone), wisdom teeth, nictitating membrane in humans; pelvic bones in whales. (5) Molecular evidence: Universal genetic code (DNA, RNA, same 20 amino acids); Cytochrome c sequence similarity reflects evolutionary distance; rRNA analysis (Woese, 1977) led to three-domain classification (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya). (6) Biogeography: Distribution of species matches geological history (marsupials in Australia, lemurs in Madagascar).

Pre-Darwinian Ideas: Lamarckism (Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, 1809) — inheritance of acquired characteristics (giraffes stretched necks → offspring inherited longer necks). Largely discredited, though epigenetics has revived interest in some aspects of transgenerational inheritance. Georges Cuvier — catastrophism (extinctions caused by catastrophic events). Charles Lyell — uniformitarianism (geological processes operate gradually over long time — influenced Darwin's thinking about gradual biological change). Modern Synthesis (Neo-Darwinism): Integration of Darwinian natural selection with Mendelian genetics and population genetics (R.A. Fisher, J.B.S. Haldane, Sewall Wright, 1930s–40s). Julian Huxley's Evolution: The Modern Synthesis (1942) coined the term. Hardy-Weinberg Principle: In a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation, migration, or genetic drift, allele frequencies remain constant across generations. p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Deviations indicate evolution is occurring.

Mechanisms of Evolution: Natural selection (directional — shifts mean; stabilising — maintains status quo; disruptive — favours extremes). Genetic drift (random allele frequency changes — bottleneck effect: population size drastically reduced; founder effect: new population started by few individuals). Gene flow (migration) — movement of alleles between populations. Mutation — ultimate source of new genetic variation. Types of selection: Stabilising (human birth weight), Directional (peppered moth — industrial melanism; Biston betularia in 19th-century England), Disruptive (African finches with large/small beaks in dry vs wet seasons). Speciation: Formation of new species. Allopatric (geographical separation — Darwin's finches on different Galápagos islands) and Sympatric (without physical barrier — polyploidy in plants). Rate of evolution: Gradualism (slow, steady change) vs Punctuated Equilibrium (Stephen Jay Gould, Niles Eldredge, 1972 — long periods of stasis interrupted by rapid change). Human Evolution: Hominin lineage diverged from chimpanzee lineage ~6–7 million years ago. Key fossils: Sahelanthropus tchadensis (~7 mya, Chad), Australopithecus afarensis (Lucy, ~3.2 mya, Ethiopia), Homo habilis (~2.4 mya, first toolmaker), Homo erectus (~1.8 mya, first to use fire, migrated out of Africa), Homo neanderthalensis (~400 kya, Europe/W Asia), Homo sapiens (~300 kya, Africa — Omo remains, Ethiopia). Out-of-Africa theory (replacement model) vs Multiregional hypothesis — genetic evidence strongly supports recent African origin (~70,000 years ago migration out of Africa). Neanderthal and Denisovan admixture: Modern non-African humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA; Melanesians carry ~3–5% Denisovan DNA.

4. Biotechnology & Modern Biology

Genetic Engineering (Recombinant DNA Technology)

Steps: 1) Isolation of desired gene (using restriction enzymes — molecular scissors that cut at specific palindromic sequences). 2) Insertion into vector (plasmid, bacteriophage, cosmid) — DNA ligase seals. 3) Introduction into host cell (bacteria, yeast, plant/animal cell — transformation/transfection). 4) Selection & expression. Key tools: restriction endonucleases (EcoRI, HindIII), plasmids (pBR322, pUC18), competent cells (E. coli).

PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)

Amplifies specific DNA sequences. Steps (repeated ~30 cycles): Denaturation (94–96°C — DNA strands separate), Annealing (50–65°C — primers bind), Extension (72°C — Taq polymerase synthesises new strand). Taq polymerase is heat-stable (from Thermus aquaticus). Applications: diagnostics (COVID-19, HIV), forensics, research.

DNA Fingerprinting

Uses VNTR (Variable Number Tandem Repeats) and STR (Short Tandem Repeats) — non-coding repetitive sequences unique to each individual (except identical twins). Steps: DNA extraction → restriction digestion → electrophoresis → Southern blotting → probe hybridisation → autoradiogram. Applications: forensics (crime solving), paternity testing, identification of remains.

Gene Therapy

Correction of defective genes by introducing functional copies. SCID (Severe Combined Immunodeficiency — ADA deficiency): First successful human gene therapy (1990), uses retroviral vector to deliver ADA gene into T cells. Cystic Fibrosis: CFTR gene delivery via adenovirus vector. Types: Somatic (non-heritable, current practice) vs Germline (heritable, ethically controversial).

CRISPR-Cas9 Gene Editing

CRISPR: Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats. Cas9: Endonuclease that cuts DNA at target site guided by sgRNA (single guide RNA). Mechanism: sgRNA binds complementary DNA → Cas9 cuts both strands → cell repairs via NHEJ (non-homologous end joining — gene knockout) or HDR (homology-directed repair — gene insertion/correction). Applications: genetic disease correction, crop improvement, cancer research. Ethical concerns: off-target effects, germline editing (2018 He Jiankui controversy — CRISPR babies), mosaic embryos.

GM Crops (Genetically Modified)

Bt Cotton: cry genes from Bacillus thuringiensis produce insecticidal protein against bollworm. Approved in India (2002). Bt Brinjal (approved in Bangladesh, not in India). GM Mustard (DMH-11): Bar/barnase/barstar system for hybrid seed production. Golden Rice: Engineered to produce β-carotene (provitamin A) to combat vitamin A deficiency. Not yet commercially released due to regulatory hurdles.

Monoclonal Antibodies (Hybridoma Technology)

Fusion of B-lymphocyte (produces desired antibody) with myeloma (cancer) cell → hybridoma → produces identical (monoclonal) antibodies indefinitely. Applications: diagnostics (ELISA, pregnancy test), therapeutics (rituximab, trastuzumab — Herceptin), targeted drug delivery.

ELISA

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay. Detects antigens or antibodies using enzyme-conjugated antibodies + colour change reaction. Types: Sandwich ELISA (detect antigen), Indirect ELISA (detect antibody). Used for HIV testing, pregnancy test (hCG), COVID-19 serosurveys.

Stem Cells & Tissue Culture

Embryonic Stem Cells (ESCs): Pluripotent — can form any cell type. Adult Stem Cells: Multipotent — limited differentiation (bone marrow — haematopoietic stem cells). iPSCs (induced Pluripotent Stem Cells): Adult cells reprogrammed to pluripotent state (Yamanaka factors — Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc; Nobel 2012). Applications: regenerative medicine (bone marrow transplant for leukaemia), drug testing, disease modelling. Tissue Culture: Micropropagation (rapid cloning of plants), somatic hybridisation, protoplast fusion.

Cloning & Insulin Production

Dolly the Sheep (1996): First mammal cloned from adult somatic cell using Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer (SCNT) — nucleus from mammary cell + enucleated egg → implanted into surrogate. Humulin (1982): First recombinant DNA drug — human insulin gene inserted into E. coli plasmid → bacteria produce human insulin. Replaced animal-derived insulin.

Vaccines

Types: Live Attenuated (MMR, BCG, oral polio — weakened pathogen), Killed/Inactivated (IPV, rabies, hepatitis A), Toxoid (tetanus, diphtheria), Subunit/Recombinant (HPV, hepatitis B), mRNA (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna COVID-19 — lipid nanoparticle-delivered mRNA encoding spike protein), Viral Vector (Covishield/AstraZeneca — ChAdOx1 chimpanzee adenovirus, Sputnik V — Ad26/Ad5). Indian COVID vaccines: Covaxin (inactivated — Bharat Biotech), Covishield (viral vector — Serum Institute), ZyCoV-D (DNA plasmid — Zydus Cadila, needle-free), Corbevax (protein subunit — Biological E).

Bioremediation & Biofuels

Bioremediation: Use of microbes to clean pollutants — oil spills (Pseudomonas), sewage treatment (activated sludge process), heavy metal removal. Biofuels: Ethanol (from sugarcane, corn — fermentation), Biodiesel (from vegetable oils — transesterification), Biogas (methane from organic waste — anaerobic digestion), Algal fuel (third-generation — high yield).

Enzyme Technology & Bioinformatics

Immobilised Enzymes: Enzymes attached to inert supports — reusable, stable. Industrial applications: glucose isomerase (HFCS), penicillin acylase (semi-synthetic antibiotics). Bioinformatics: Application of IT to biological data. Human Genome Project (HGP): 1990–2003, sequenced ~3.2 billion bp, identified ~20,000–25,000 genes. Indian Genome Project: Mapping genetic diversity of Indian populations for disease susceptibility research.

Bioethics & Nanobiotechnology

GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee): Indian regulatory body for GMO approval. Biosafety: Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety. IPR: Patents on life forms (e.g., basmati rice, neem, turmeric controversies). Nanobiotechnology: Nanoparticles for drug delivery, quantum dots for imaging, nanosensors for diagnostics.

5. Space Technology

5.1 Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)

ISRO, founded in 1969 and headquartered in Bengaluru, is India's national space agency under the Department of Space (DoS). The agency traces its origins to INCOSPAR (1962), established by Vikram Sarabhai. ISRO's motto is "Space technology in the service of humankind." As of 2026, Chairman is V. Narayanan, succeeding S. Somanath. The annual budget for 2026-27 is ₹13,705.63 crore (~US$1.4 billion). ISRO employs over 14,600 personnel.

  • SSLV: Small Satellite Launch Vehicle — new kid on the block for quick, low-cost small satellite launches.

5.2 Rocket Propulsion Technology

Chemical Rockets: Most launch vehicles use chemical propulsion — the controlled exothermic reaction of fuel and oxidiser produces high-temperature, high-velocity exhaust gases expelled through a de Laval nozzle (convergent-divergent). The nozzle accelerates exhaust to supersonic speeds. Specific Impulse (Isp): Measure of efficiency — thrust per unit propellant weight flow rate (seconds). Higher Isp = more efficient. Vacuum Isp: Solid motors ~250–300 s, Liquid engines ~300–350 s, Cryogenic engines ~440–460 s. Thrust Equation: F = ṁvₑ + (pₑ − pₐ)Aₑ. At high altitude, ambient pressure pₐ is low, so thrust increases; nozzles are optimised for specific altitudes.

  • Solid Propellant: Fuel + oxidiser mixed in a rubbery binder (e.g., AP/HTPB — ammonium perchlorate + hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene). Simple, reliable, high thrust, but cannot be throttled or stopped once ignited. Used in boosters (PSLV strap-ons, S-200 of LVM3, Space Shuttle SRBs). India's S-200 is the third-largest solid booster in the world.
  • Liquid Propellant: Fuel and oxidiser stored separately, pumped into combustion chamber. Throttleable, restartable. Earth-storable: UDMH (unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine) + N₂O₄ (nitrogen tetroxide) — hypergolic (ignite on contact), used in PS4, Vikas engine. Cryogenic: LH₂ (liquid hydrogen, −253°C) + LOX (liquid oxygen, −183°C). Highest Isp. Used in upper stages (GSLV CE-7.5/CE-20, Ariane 5 HM7B, RL-10). India developed indigenous cryogenic engine (CE-7.5) after US denied technology transfer. CE-20 (thrust ~200 kN) powers LVM3 upper stage.
  • Semi-Cryogenic: Refined kerosene (RP-1) + LOX — used in Falcon 9 (Merlin engine), Soyuz (RD-107/108). ISRO developing SCE-200 (2 MN thrust) for next-generation launch vehicles.
  • Electric/Ion Propulsion: Uses electric fields to accelerate ions (Xe, Kr). Very high Isp (3,000–10,000 s) but low thrust. Used for satellite station-keeping (ISRO's GSAT-9, GSAT-20 use electric propulsion for orbit raising). Hall-effect thrusters and gridded ion thrusters are common types. NASA's Dawn mission used ion propulsion to reach Vesta and Ceres.
  • Nuclear Propulsion: NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) tested in 1960s USA. Uses nuclear reactor to heat H₂ propellant. High Isp (~900 s). Not yet used operationally. Nuclear thermal propulsion under study for NASA's crewed Mars missions. Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs) use decay heat of Pu-238 for deep space probes (Voyager, Cassini, Perseverance rover — MMRTG).
  • Rocket Stages: Multistage rockets shed mass as propellant is consumed, improving efficiency. Tandem staging (stages stacked — LVM3: S200 boosters + L110 core + C25 upper). Parallel staging (strap-on boosters fire alongside core). Staging velocity: Δv = vₑ ln(m₀/m_f) — Tsiolkovsky rocket equation (1903), derived by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, the Russian "father of rocketry." The equation shows that achieving high Δv requires high exhaust velocity or high mass ratio (m₀/m_f).

5.2 Satellite Systems

5.2.1 INSAT Series

The Indian National Satellite System (INSAT) is a multipurpose geostationary satellite constellation for telecommunications, broadcasting, meteorology, and search-and-rescue. The first INSAT-1B was launched in 1983. Later renamed GSAT series. GSAT-9 (South Asia Satellite / SAARC Satellite) provides services to India's neighbours. The current nomenclature changed to CMS from 2020.

5.2.2 IRS Series

The Indian Remote Sensing (IRS) satellites form the largest civilian Earth observation constellation in the world. Sub-classes include Cartosat (cartography), Resourcesat (resource monitoring), Oceansat (oceanography), RISAT (radar imaging), and HysIS (hyperspectral). Since 2020, unified under the EOS (Earth Observation Satellite) prefix.

5.2.3 NavIC (IRNSS)

The Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (IRNSS), operational name NavIC, is India's independent regional satellite navigation system. It consists of 7 satellites + 2 standby, providing Standard Positioning Service (SPS) accuracy better than 20 m over India and the region extending 1,500 km beyond borders. Operational since 2018.

5.2.4 GAGAN

GPS-Aided GEO Augmented Navigation (GAGAN) is a satellite-based augmentation system (SBAS) jointly developed by ISRO and the Airports Authority of India for civil aviation navigation.

5.3 Major Missions

5.3.1 Chandrayaan-1 (2008)

India's first lunar mission. The Moon Impact Probe (MIP) discovered water molecules on the lunar surface — a landmark finding. The spacecraft conducted chemical and mineralogical mapping of the Moon over 312 days. Awarded AIAA SPACE 2009 award.

5.3.2 Chandrayaan-2 (2019)

Consisted of an orbiter, Vikram lander, and Pragyan rover. Launched on LVM3. The orbiter continues to function well (extended from 1 to 7 years). The Vikram lander crashed due to a software glitch during the final descent phase on 7 September 2019. Lessons learned informed Chandrayaan-3 design.

5.3.3 Chandrayaan-3 (2023)

India's second lunar landing attempt and the first-ever spacecraft to soft-land near the lunar south pole. Launched 14 July 2023 on LVM3-M4. Vikram lander + Pragyan rover touched down on 23 August 2023 at 18:04 IST at Statio Shiv Shakti (69°S). The rover confirmed presence of sulphur, aluminium, calcium, iron, and other elements on the lunar surface. PM Modi declared 23 August as National Space Day.

5.3.4 Mangalyaan / Mars Orbiter Mission (2013-2022)

India's first interplanetary mission. Launched 5 November 2013 on PSLV-C25. Lowest-cost Mars mission ever (₹450 crore / ~US$73 million). India became the first Asian nation and the first country to succeed on its maiden attempt. The orbiter functioned for over 8 years before losing contact in April 2022 due to a prolonged eclipse.

5.3.5 Aditya-L1 (2023)

India's first solar observatory, launched 2 September 2023 on PSLV-C57. Positioned at the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point. Studies solar corona, solar winds, and space weather using seven payloads.

5.3.6 XPoSat (2024)

X-ray Polarimeter Satellite, launched 1 January 2024 on PSLV-DL. Studies black holes and X-ray polarisation in the 5-30 keV energy range.

5.3.7 Gaganyaan (Human Spaceflight)

India's crewed orbital spacecraft programme. The 5.3-tonne capsule will carry 2-3 astronauts to 400 km low Earth orbit for up to 7 days. Vyommitra, a female-looking humanoid robot, will fly on uncrewed test missions. The HLVM3 (human-rated LVM3) will be the launch vehicle. First crewed mission target ~2027. Astronaut training facility established in Bengaluru. In 2025, Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla flew to ISS on Axiom Mission 4.

5.3.8 Other Planned Missions

  • Shukrayaan: Venus Orbiter Mission (planned)
  • Chandrayaan-4: Lunar sample return mission (upcoming)
  • Mars Lander Mission (MLM): Follow-up to MOM (target 2027)
  • Bharatiya Antariksh Station (BAS): India's modular space station (~20 tonnes), planned after Gaganyaan completion

5.4 Space Policy & Industry

  • IN-SPACe (2020): Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre — single-window nodal agency for private sector participation in space activities.
  • NSIL: NewSpace India Ltd — ISRO's commercial arm for technology transfer and launch services.
  • FDI in Space (2024): 74% FDI allowed in satellite manufacturing, 49% in launch vehicles.
  • Artemis Accords: India signed in 2023, joining the US-led lunar exploration framework.
  • Major private players: Skyroot Aerospace, Agnikul Cosmos, Bellatrix Aerospace, Pixxel, Dhruva Space.
  • SpaDeX (2025): ISRO successfully demonstrated in-space docking using two small satellites — a key technology for future space station and sample-return missions.

6. Defence Technology

6.1 Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (IGMDP)

The IGMDP was launched in 1983 under the leadership of Dr. APJ Abdul Kalam, then Director of DRDL. The programme aimed at self-reliance in missile technology and simultaneously developed five missile systems: Prithvi (SRBM), Agni (IRBM/ICBM), Akash (MRSAM), Trishul (SRSAM), and Nag (ATGM). The programme was formally completed in 2008.

6.2 Ballistic Missiles

6.2.1 Agni Series (ICBM/IRBM)

  • Agni-I: 700-900 km range, single-stage solid fuel.
  • Agni-II: 2,000-3,500 km range, two-stage solid.
  • Agni-III: 3,500-5,000 km range.
  • Agni-IV: 4,000 km range, can be launched from road mobile launcher.
  • Agni-V: 5,000+ km range, ICBM capability. MIRV (Multiple Independently Targetable Re-entry Vehicle) tested.
  • Agni-P: New-generation missile with canister launch, improved accuracy.

6.2.2 Prithvi Series (SRBM)

India's first indigenously developed ballistic missile. Range: 150-600 km. Variants: Prithvi-I (Army), Prithvi-II (Air Force), Prithvi-III / Dhanush (Navy). Being phased out in favour of Prahar and Pralay missiles.

6.2.3 Submarine-Launched Ballistic Missiles (SLBM)

  • K-15 Sagarika: 750 km range, for Arihant-class SSBNs.
  • K-4: 3,500 km range, tested successfully.
  • K-5, K-6: Under development (5,000+ km range).

6.3 Cruise Missiles

  • BrahMos: Supersonic cruise missile, Indo-Russian joint venture. Range 290-800 km. Mach 2.8-3.0 speed. Land, sea, air, and submarine variants. Currently being integrated on Su-30MKI, INS Vikrant, and mobile land launchers.
  • BrahMos-II / BrahMos-NG: Hypersonic version under development. Smaller, lighter BrahMos-NG for fighter aircraft.
  • Nirbhay: Subsonic cruise missile, range 1,000-1,500 km. Indigenous, terrain-hugging capability.

6.4 Surface-to-Air Missiles (SAM)

  • Akash: Medium-range SAM (30 km), Mach 2.5. Uses ramjet propulsion. Phased array Rajendra radar. Inducted by IAF and Army.
  • Akash-NG: Next-generation version with improved range and accuracy.
  • QRSAM: Quick Reaction Surface-to-Air Missile — designed for mobile army units.
  • Trishul: Short-range SAM (12 km), limited success, project delinked in 2008 and used as technology demonstrator.
  • Barak 8: Indo-Israeli long-range SAM (70-100 km), deployed on naval ships.

6.5 Air-to-Air & Air-to-Surface Missiles

  • Astra: Beyond-visual-range (BVR) air-to-air missile. Astra Mk I: 110 km range. Astra Mk II: 160 km range, under development.
  • Rudram-1: Anti-radiation missile for destroying enemy radar installations. Rudram-2, Rudram-3 under development.
  • Helina / Dhruvastra: Helicopter-launched anti-tank guided missile (ATGM).

6.6 Anti-Tank Missiles

  • Nag: Third-generation fire-and-forget ATGM. Imaging Infrared (IIR) seeker. Range: 0.5-4 km. NAMICA carrier vehicle. Helina is the helicopter-launched variant.
  • MPATGM: Man-Portable Anti-Tank Guided Missile, under development.

6.7 Ballistic Missile Defence (BMD)

India's two-layer BMD system:

  • Prithvi Defence Vehicle (PDV) Mk II: Exo-atmospheric interceptor (altitude >50 km).
  • Advanced Air Defence (AAD): Endo-atmospheric interceptor (altitude 15-40 km).
  • Swordfish Radar: Long-range tracking radar, 800 km coverage.

6.8 Defence Research & Organisation

DRDO (Defence Research and Development Organisation), under Dr. Samir V Kamath, operates a network of over 50 laboratories across India developing defence technologies, from missiles to armoured vehicles, radars, and electronic warfare systems.

6.9 Nuclear Triad

India has a credible nuclear triad — the ability to deliver nuclear weapons from land, air, and sea:

  • Land: Agni series (I to V) — IRBM/ICBM deployed by Strategic Forces Command (SFC).
  • Air: Mirage 2000, Rafale, Su-30MKI capable of nuclear delivery.
  • Sea: INS Arihant (SSBN) — operational with K-15 Sagarika. INS Arighaat — second SSBN commissioned. Four Arihant-class submarines planned. K-4 missile tested (3,500 km).

6.9.1 Nuclear Command Authority

  • Political Council: Chaired by PM — authorises nuclear use.
  • Executive Council: Chaired by NSA — executes command.
  • SFC: Strategic Forces Command — manages nuclear arsenal.

6.9.2 No First Use (NFU) Policy

India maintains a No First Use (NFU) doctrine — nuclear weapons will only be used in retaliation. However, there has been debate in recent years about reviewing this policy in light of regional security dynamics.

6.10 Defence Industrial Base & Make in India

  • OFB corporatised (2021): Ordnance Factory Board restructured into 7 Defence Public Sector Undertakings (DPSUs).
  • Major DPSUs: HAL, BEL, BDL, MDL, GRSE, BEML, Mazagon Dock.
  • FDI in Defence: 74% through automatic route, 100% in niche technologies.
  • Positive Indigenisation Lists (4,666 items phased out by 2029).
  • Tejas (LCA): Light Combat Aircraft. Mk1A (83 ordered), Mk2 under development. Export interest from Malaysia, Egypt, Nigeria.
  • Rafale: 36 ordered, delivered 2020-2022.
  • Helicopters: Prachand (LCH), Rudra (ALH-WSI), Dhruv (ALH).
  • AWACS: Netra (DRDO/Airbus), Phalcon (Israeli).

6.11 Naval Platforms

  • INS Vikramaditya: Modified Kiev-class aircraft carrier (ex-Russian Admiral Gorshkov).
  • INS Vikrant (IAC-1): First indigenously built aircraft carrier, commissioned 2022.
  • Scorpène-class submarines: Kalvari class (6 boats), built under technology transfer from France.
  • Arihant-class: Nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines (SSBN).
  • P-75I: Project for 6 new submarines with Air Independent Propulsion (AIP).
  • INS Chakra: Akula-class nuclear submarine, leased from Russia.

6.12 Special Forces & Security

  • NSG: National Security Guard (Black Cats) — counter-terrorism.
  • MARCOS: Marine Commandos (Indian Navy).
  • Garud: Indian Air Force special forces.
  • Border forces: BSF, ITBP, Assam Rifles, SSB, CRPF.

Self-reliance in defence (‘Atmanirbhar Bharat’) is a major policy thrust. Pay attention to the positive indigenisation lists, FDI changes, and corporatisation of OFB for UPSC.

7.2 Artificial Intelligence

7.2.1 National Strategy for AI

NITI Aayog released the National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (#AIForAll) in 2018, focusing on healthcare, agriculture, education, smart cities, and smart mobility. India's AI market projected to reach $8 billion by 2025 (40% CAGR). India ranks 5th globally in AI investments (Stanford AI Index 2022).

7.2.2 IndiaAI Mission (2024)

Approved with an outlay of ₹10,371.92 crore. Key pillars:

  • IndiaAI Compute: 18,693 GPUs (Nvidia H100/H200, AMD MI300 series), subsidised compute at ~₹100/hr.
  • AIKosha: IndiaAI Datasets Platform — 300+ datasets, 80+ models.
  • AIRAWAT: AI supercomputer at C-DAC Pune, peak 13,170 teraflops.
  • BharatGen: Sovereign multimodal, multilingual foundational AI model (₹235 crore, DST-funded).
  • Sarvam AI: Selected to develop India's first sovereign LLM under IndiaAI Mission (2025).

7.2.3 Generative AI & Applications

Key Indian AI developments: Hanooman series LLMs (22 Indian languages), Krutrim AI (Ola), Dhenu (agriculture LLM for farmers), SAKHI (space assistant for Gaganyaan). AI applications in agriculture (precision farming, crop prediction), healthcare (diagnostic imaging), defence (autonomous systems), and governance (Bhashini language translation).

7.3 Cybersecurity

India faces growing cyber threats with the rapid expansion of digital infrastructure. Key institutions:

  • CERT-In (Indian Computer Emergency Response Team) — nodal agency for cybersecurity incident response.
  • National Cyber Security Strategy 2020 — framework for securing cyberspace.
  • Data Protection Bill 2023 (Digital Personal Data Protection Act) — governs personal data processing and privacy.
  • National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC) — protects critical sectors (power, banking, telecom, defence).

Major cyber threats: phishing, ransomware, zero-day attacks, cyber warfare, VPN regulation. The Indian cybercrime rate has risen, with state-sponsored attacks targeting infrastructure. Defence Cyber Agency (DCA) and Defence Intelligence Agency (DIA) strengthen military cybersecurity.

7.4 Quantum Computing

National Quantum Mission (2023) — ₹6,003 crore outlay over 5-8 years. Four thematic hubs (TIFR Mumbai, IISc Bengaluru, IITs). Focus areas: qubit development, quantum communication, quantum cryptography, quantum sensing, and quantum materials.

  • Qubit: Basic unit of quantum information, leveraging superposition and entanglement.
  • Quantum Supremacy: Demonstrated by Google (2019) with Sycamore processor.
  • Shor's Algorithm: Can factor large numbers exponentially faster, threatening RSA encryption.
  • Post-Quantum Cryptography: Algorithms resistant to quantum attacks are being standardised.
  • I-Hub Quantum Technology Foundation at IISER Pune under NM-ICPS.

7.5 Blockchain

Blockchain uses cryptographic hashing, distributed ledger technology (DLT), and smart contracts. Applications in India:

  • Land records (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra pilots).
  • Certificate issuance (academic degrees, caste certificates).
  • Supply chain transparency (agriculture, pharmaceuticals).
  • Digital rupee (CBDC) launched by RBI using blockchain.
  • National Blockchain Framework (NIC) — under development.

7.6 5G / 6G

  • 5G Rollout: Airtel & Jio launched 5G services in 2022. mmWave and mid-band spectrum used.
  • PM-WANI: Prime Minister’s Wi-Fi Access Network Interface — public Wi-Fi hotspots.
  • Bharat 6G Vision: 6G R&D roadmap. Target for 6G deployment ~2030.

7.7 Internet of Things (IoT)

IoT connects physical objects with sensors, processing ability, and software to exchange data over networks. Global connected IoT devices ~16.6 billion (2023). India applications:

  • Smart Cities Mission: 100 smart cities with IoT sensors for traffic, waste, water, and energy management.
  • Smart Agriculture: Soil sensors, weather stations, drone monitoring, precision irrigation.
  • Industrial IoT (IIoT): Predictive maintenance, asset tracking, smart manufacturing (Industry 4.0).
  • Smart Homes: Connected appliances, lighting, security, energy management.
  • NB-IoT, LTE-M, LoRaWAN networks being deployed for nationwide IoT coverage.

7.8 Cloud Computing

  • IaaS / PaaS / SaaS models. India cloud market estimated at ~$13 billion (2025).
  • MeghRaj: Government of India's cloud initiative (GI Cloud) — centralised cloud for government services.
  • Major players: AWS, Azure, GCP, and Indian providers (Jio Cloud, Tata Communications, E2E Networks, Yotta).

7.9 Robotics

Robotics combines mechanical engineering, electronics, and computer science. India's focus areas:

  • Industrial Robots: Automotive, electronics, and FMCG manufacturing. India ranks 10th globally in robot density.
  • Collaborative Robots (Cobots): Used in small/medium enterprises for assembly, packaging.
  • Humanoid Robots: Vyommitra (ISRO), research at IITs.
  • Military Robots: Daksh (DRDO bomb disposal), surveillance drones, MARS unmanned vehicles.
  • Drone Technology: Drone Rules 2021 liberalised regulations. Namo Drone Didi scheme for women self-help groups. Kisan Drones for agriculture. Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for drones.
  • National Mission on Interdisciplinary Cyber-Physical Systems (NM-ICPS): ₹3,660 crore, 25 Technology Innovation Hubs including robotics and autonomous systems (IISc Bengaluru, IIT Hyderabad TiHAN).

7.10 Other Emerging Technologies

  • AR/VR: Augmented & virtual reality in gaming, training, tourism, architecture. Drishti (IIT Jodhpur) hub under NM-ICPS.
  • 3D Printing (Additive Manufacturing): DMLS, FDM, SLA. Medical implants (IIT Madras), aerospace components (ISRO 3D-printed PS4 engine), construction 3D printing. Wipro 3D, Imaginarium, and other Indian firms in this space.
  • Edge Computing: Processing data closer to source (fog nodes). Critical for low-latency IoT and AI applications.
  • Big Data: Large-scale data analytics driving decision-making across sectors. India Data Bank & Data Services hub at IIIT Hyderabad.
  • Digital Twin: Virtual replica of physical systems for simulation, monitoring, and optimisation. Used in smart cities, manufacturing, healthcare.

8. Nanotechnology and Miscellaneous

8.1 Nanotechnology

Nanotechnology involves manipulation of matter with at least one dimension between 1-100 nanometres. At this scale, surface area and quantum mechanical effects dominate material properties. The concept was seeded by Richard Feynman's 1959 talk "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom." The term was coined by Norio Taniguchi (1974) and popularised by K. Eric Drexler (1986).

8.1.1 Nanomaterials

  • Carbon Nanotubes (CNTs): Single-wall (SWCNT) and multi-wall (MWCNT). High tensile strength, electrical conductivity. Applications: batteries, composites, sensors, electronics.
  • Graphene: 2D carbon allotrope (one atom thick). Nobel Prize 2010 (Geim & Novoselov). Applications: flexible electronics, supercapacitors, water purification membranes, conductive inks.
  • Quantum Dots: Semiconductor nanoparticles with size-tuneable optical properties. Used in displays (QLED), solar cells, biological imaging.
  • Fullerenes: C60 buckyballs and related carbon structures.
  • Nanoparticles: Gold, silver, iron oxide, titanium dioxide, zinc oxide — used in medicine, cosmetics, catalysis, antimicrobial coatings.
  • Nanocomposites, Nanorods, Nanowires, Nanoshells — engineered nanostructures for specific functions.

8.1.2 Synthesis Methods

  • Top-down: Lithography (optical, electron beam, nanoimprint), etching, milling — making small from large.
  • Bottom-up: Chemical vapour deposition (CVD), sol-gel, molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), self-assembly — building from atomic/molecular precursors.

8.1.3 Applications

8.1.3.1 Medicine (Nanomedicine)

  • Drug Delivery: Targeted nanocarriers (liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers) for cancer therapy. Reduced side effects, controlled release.
  • Diagnostic Imaging: Nano contrast agents for MRI, CT, ultrasound. Quantum dots for fluorescence imaging.
  • Nanorobots: DNA origami-based nanobots for targeted drug delivery (demonstrated in cockroaches).
  • Tissue engineering: Nanoscaffolds mimicking extracellular matrix for bone, cartilage, and skin regeneration.

8.1.3.2 Electronics

  • Nanotransistors: Sub-10 nm gate length transistors in modern CPUs.
  • Nanosensors: Highly sensitive chemical, biological, and physical sensors.
  • Flexible Electronics: Graphene and CNT-based printed electronics for wearables and displays.
  • Spintronics, molecular electronics, memristors.

8.1.3.3 Energy

  • Nanobatteries: CNT and silicon nanowire anodes for higher capacity Li-ion batteries.
  • Supercapacitors: Graphene-based supercapacitors for rapid charging.
  • Perovskite Solar Cells: Nanostructured perovskite materials for high-efficiency, low-cost photovoltaics.
  • SWCNTs address key Li-ion battery challenges: energy density, charge rate, cycle life.

8.1.3.4 Environment

  • Water Purification: Nanofiltration membranes (CNT, graphene oxide), nano-adsorbents for heavy metals and pathogens.
  • Nano-catalysts: For pollution control (vehicle exhaust catalytic converters, industrial emission treatment).
  • Air Pollution Control: Nanofilters for particulate matter (PM2.5) capture.

8.1.4 Risks & Regulation

  • Nanotoxicity: Nanoparticles can enter the body via inhalation, ingestion, or skin contact. Potential for inflammation, oxidative stress, DNA damage, and fibrosis (e.g., carbon nanotubes may behave like asbestos).
  • Environmental Fate: Nanoparticles released into water/soil can affect ecosystems. Silver nanoparticles in consumer products destroy beneficial bacteria in wastewater.
  • Regulation: Global efforts towards standardisation (ISO TC 229). India has no specific nano-regulation; Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) developing guidelines.

8.2 SMART Materials

  • Piezoelectric Materials: Generate electric charge under mechanical stress (PZT, PVDF). Used in sensors, actuators, energy harvesting.
  • Shape Memory Alloys (SMA): Nitinol (Ni-Ti alloy) — "remember" original shape and return to it upon heating. Used in stents, actuators, robotics.
  • Magnetostrictive Materials: Change shape in magnetic field (Terfenol-D).
  • Electro/Magnetorheological Fluids: Smart fluids that change viscosity under electric/magnetic fields. Used in dampers, clutches, haptic devices.

8.3 Photonics & MEMS/NEMS

  • Photonics: Science of light generation, detection, and manipulation. Applications: fibre optics, lasers, LEDs, optical computing, LiDAR.
  • MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems): Micro-scale mechanical & electrical components. Used in accelerometers, gyroscopes, inkjet printers, DLP projectors.
  • NEMS (Nano-Electro-Mechanical Systems): Nanoscale equivalents, under development.

8.4 Nuclear Technology

8.4.1 India's Three-Stage Nuclear Programme

Conceived by Dr. Homi J. Bhabha to leverage India's vast thorium reserves (25% of world's thorium).

  • Stage 1: Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWR) using natural uranium fuel. Plutonium produced as by-product.
  • Stage 2: Fast Breeder Reactors (FBR) using plutonium as fuel. Irradiate thorium to produce U-233. PFBR (Kalpakkam, 500 MW) achieved first criticality on 6 April 2026.
  • Stage 3: Advanced Heavy Water Reactors (AHWR) and Molten Salt Breeder Reactors (MSBR) using U-233 and thorium. Self-sustaining thorium cycle.

8.4.3 Nuclear Fuel Cycle & Uranium Supply

India has limited domestic uranium reserves (~92,000 tonnes). Major suppliers: Kazakhstan (5,000 tonnes), Canada, Australia, Russia, Mongolia, Namibia. Thorium reserves estimated at 518,000 tonnes (monazite in Kerala, Odisha, Tamil Nadu beach sands).

8.4.4 Nuclear Liability

Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act (2010) — establishes supplier liability for nuclear accidents. This clause has caused delays in reactor imports (e.g., US-based Westinghouse and GE). The SHANTI Bill (2025) aims to amend liability provisions and enable limited private participation.

8.4.5 International Collaborations

  • ITER: India is a partner in the international fusion energy experiment (France).
  • LIGO-India: Gravitational wave observatory approved (2023, ₹2,600 crore), to be built in Hingoli, Maharashtra.
  • CERN: India is an Associate Member.
  • SESAME: India is a member of the synchrotron light source in Jordan.

8.5 Science, Technology & Innovation Policy

  • STIP 2020: Science, Technology and Innovation Policy — 5-year cycles, aims to boost R&D investment and autonomy to scientific departments.
  • ANRF 2023: Anusandhan National Research Foundation — ₹50,000 crore over 5 years, to fund research across all disciplines.
  • Key scientific agencies: DST, DBT, CSIR, ICMR, ICAR.
  • Global Innovation Index: India ranks 40th (2023) — top innovator among lower-middle-income countries.
  • GERD: Gross Expenditure on R&D ~0.7% of GDP (target 2% by STIP 2020).
  • S&T Budget: Allocation in Union Budget for scientific departments.

8.6 Other Important Technologies

8.6.1 Biotechnology

India's biotechnology sector valued at ~$80 billion (2025). Key institutions: DBT (Department of Biotechnology), BIRAC (Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council). Applications in healthcare (vaccines, diagnostics, biosimilars), agriculture (GM crops, biofertilisers), and industrial biotechnology (biofuels, enzymes). India is a major vaccine producer globally.

8.6.2 Supercomputing

National Supercomputing Mission (NSM) — building a network of supercomputers across India. PARAM Siddhi-AI (210 AI Petaflops), AIRAWAT (13,170 teraflops for AI). Rudra servers (indigenously built). Target: 70+ supercomputers by 2025-26.

8.6.3 Space Technology Spin-offs

ISRO technologies have been commercialised through Antrix Corporation and NSIL. Applications in telemedicine, disaster management, biodiversity information systems, cartography, and satellite communication for education (EDUSAT, GRAMSAT).
