1. Number System

1.1 Divisibility Rules and Their Applications

The foundation of rapid calculation in the number system rests on divisibility rules, which allow a candidate to decide whether a large integer can be divided by a small integer without performing long division. For instance, a number is divisible by 2 when its unit digit is even, by 3 when the sum of its digits is a multiple of 3, by 4 when the last two digits form a number divisible by 4, by 5 when the unit digit is 0 or 5, by 6 when it satisfies both the rules for 2 and 3, by 8 when the last three digits form a number divisible by 8, by 9 when the digit sum is a multiple of 9, by 10 when the unit digit is 0, and by 11 when the difference between the sum of digits in odd positions and the sum of digits in even positions is either 0 or a multiple of 11. Mastery of these rules enables quick elimination of answer choices in multiple‑choice questions and supports the simplification of fractions before further arithmetic.

1.2 Least Common Multiple and Highest Common Factor

The concepts of least common multiple (LCM) and highest common factor (HCF) are intertwined through the relationship LCM × HCF = product of the two numbers, a property that holds for any pair of positive integers. LCM is the smallest positive integer that is a multiple of each given number, while HCF is the largest positive integer that divides each of them without remainder. In problems involving periodic events — such as bells ringing at different intervals or runners meeting at a starting point — LCM determines the first simultaneous occurrence, whereas HCF is used when dividing quantities into the largest possible equal groups. The prime factorisation method, where each number is expressed as a product of primes raised to appropriate powers, provides a systematic way to compute both LCM (by taking the highest power of each prime) and HCF (by taking the lowest power of each common prime).

1.3 Remainder Theorem and Modular Arithmetic

When a polynomial f(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder is f(a); this remainder theorem extends naturally to integers under modular arithmetic, where the remainder of a division by n is the residue class modulo n. In competitive examinations, questions often ask for the remainder of large powers such as 7100 divided by 5, which can be solved by observing the cyclicity of remainders: 7 ≡ 2 (mod 5), 21 ≡ 2, 22 ≡ 4, 23 ≡ 3, 24 ≡ 1, and the cycle repeats every four powers, so 7100 ≡ 2100 ≡ (24)25 ≡ 125 ≡ 1 (mod 5). Understanding cyclicity of unit digits and remainders reduces massive exponentiation to a few simple steps, a technique that is indispensable for time‑bound tests.

1.4 Surds, Indices and Unit Digit Cyclicity

Surds are irrational roots such as √2 or ∛5 that cannot be simplified to a rational number; they obey the laws of indices, for example √a × √b = √(ab) and (√a)2 = a. Indices (or exponents) follow rules like a^m × a^n = a^(m+n), a^m ÷ a^n = a^(m−n), and (a^m)^n = a^(mn). The unit digit of powers of any integer repeats in a cycle of at most four: for 2 the cycle is 2, 4, 8, 6; for 3 it is 3, 9, 7, 1; for 7 it is 7, 9, 3, 1; for 8 it is 8, 4, 2, 6. Recognising these cycles allows a candidate to answer questions such as “find the unit digit of 753” by dividing the exponent by 4, taking the remainder (1), and selecting the first element of the cycle (7). This skill, combined with the ability to rationalise denominators containing surds, completes the toolkit for number‑system questions.

2. Percentages

2.1 Fraction Percentage Equivalents and Mental Conversion

Percentages are fractions with denominator 100, and the ability to convert between common fractions and their percentage equivalents is a hallmark of speed in quantitative aptitude. The most frequently used equivalents are 1/2 = 50%, 1/3 ≈ 33.33%, 1/4 = 25%, 1/5 = 20%, 1/6 ≈ 16.67%, 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/9 ≈ 11.11%, 1/10 = 10%, 1/12 ≈ 8.33%, and 1/16 = 6.25%. When a problem states “a number is increased by 12.5%”, recognising 12.5% as 1/8 allows the candidate to multiply the original number by 9/8 instead of performing decimal multiplication. Similarly, a decrease of 16.67% corresponds to multiplying by 5/6. This mental conversion reduces calculation time and minimises errors caused by decimal approximations.

2.2 Successive Percentage Change and Net Effect

When a quantity undergoes two successive percentage changes, the net percentage change is not the arithmetic sum of the two percentages but is given by the formula x + y + (xy/100), where x and y are the individual percentage changes (positive for increase, negative for decrease). This formula arises because the second change is applied to the already altered base. For example, a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease yields a net change of 20 + (−20) + (20×−20)/100 = −4%, i.e., a 4% decrease overall. The same principle extends to three or more successive changes by applying the formula iteratively. Understanding this prevents the common mistake of assuming that equal percentage increase and decrease cancel each other.

2.3 Percentage Increase Decrease and Base Shifting

The percentage change between an initial value and a final value is calculated as (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. A frequent source of error is using the wrong base: when a price is reduced by 20% and then increased by 20%, the base for the increase is the reduced price, not the original price, leading to a net loss. In problems involving “A is 25% more than B”, the relationship can be expressed as A = B × (1 + 25/100) = 1.25B, which implies B = A ÷ 1.25 = 0.8A, so B is 20% less than A. Recognising that the base shifts with each operation is essential for accurate interpretation of statements like “20% less than” versus “20% of”.

2.4 Applications in Profit Loss and Interest

Percentage concepts form the backbone of profit‑loss and simple‑compound interest calculations. Profit percentage is (profit ÷ cost price) × 100, while loss percentage is (loss ÷ cost price) × 100. Marked price, discount, and selling price are linked by selling price = marked price × (1 − discount%/100). In simple interest, the interest amount = principal × rate × time ÷ 100, and the amount = principal + interest. Compound interest uses the formula amount = principal × (1 + rate/100)^time, where the interest earned each period is added to the principal for the next period. The effective annual rate when compounding occurs more frequently than annually is (1 + r/n)^n − 1, with r as the nominal rate and n as the number of compounding periods per year. These formulas are direct applications of percentage growth and decay.

Example: Successive Percentage Change
A shopkeeper marks up the price of an article by 30% and then offers a discount of 20% on the marked price. What is the net profit or loss percentage?
Solution: Let the cost price be 100. After a 30% markup, marked price = 130. A 20% discount on 130 gives selling price = 130 × (1 − 20/100) = 130 × 0.8 = 104. Profit = 104 − 100 = 4. Profit percentage = (4 ÷ 100) × 100 = 4%. Using the successive change formula: net % = 30 + (−20) + (30×−20)/100 = 10 − 6 = 4% profit.
Exam‑Day Insight
In percentage questions, always identify the base explicitly before applying any formula. A quick check: if the problem says “A is 20% more than B”, write A = 1.2B; if it says “A is 20% less than B”, write A = 0.8B. This habit eliminates base‑confusion errors that are the most common cause of lost marks in the quant section.

3. Profit and Loss

3.1 Cost Price Selling Price and Marked Price Relationships

The three key prices in any transaction are cost price (CP), the amount paid to acquire the article; selling price (SP), the amount received from the buyer; and marked price (MP), the price displayed before any discount. The fundamental relationships are SP = CP + profit = CP − loss, and SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100). When a trader marks up the cost price by a certain percentage, the marked price becomes CP × (1 + markup%/100). These equations form a closed system: knowing any two of CP, SP, MP, profit%, loss%, discount%, or markup% allows the determination of the remaining variables. In examination questions, the data is often given in a mixed format — for example, “a 20% discount on the marked price yields a 10% profit” — requiring the candidate to set up simultaneous equations and solve for the unknown ratio of CP to MP.

3.2 Profit Percentage Loss Percentage and Discount Calculations

Profit percentage is always calculated on cost price unless explicitly stated otherwise, while discount percentage is calculated on marked price. A common trap is to compute profit on selling price, which yields a different figure. For instance, if CP = 80 and SP = 100, profit = 20, profit% on CP = 25%, but profit% on SP = 20%. The phrase “gain of 20% on selling price” translates to profit = 0.2 × SP, so CP = SP − 0.2SP = 0.8SP, implying a profit of 25% on CP. Discount calculations follow a similar pattern: a discount of 15% on MP means SP = 0.85MP. When successive discounts are offered, the equivalent single discount is not the sum but is given by d1 + d2 − (d1×d2)/100, derived from the same successive‑change principle used in percentages.

3.3 Successive Discounts and Equivalent Single Discount

When two discounts d1% and d2% are applied successively, the final selling price is MP × (1 − d1/100) × (1 − d2/100). The equivalent single discount D satisfies (1 − D/100) = (1 − d1/100)(1 − d2/100), which simplifies to D = d1 + d2 − (d1×d2)/100. For example, successive discounts of 20% and 10% give an equivalent discount of 20 + 10 − (200/100) = 28%. This formula is symmetric and extends to three or more discounts by repeated application. In problems where a trader wants to offer a single discount equivalent to a series of discounts, this calculation directly provides the answer without needing to assume a marked price.

3.4 Break Even Analysis and Margin Concepts

Break‑even occurs when total revenue equals total cost, i.e., SP × quantity = (fixed cost + variable cost per unit × quantity). The contribution margin per unit is SP − variable cost per unit, and the break‑even quantity = fixed cost ÷ contribution margin. Margin of safety measures how much sales can drop before losses occur, expressed as (actual sales − break‑even sales) ÷ actual sales. Although these concepts are more common in business studies, they appear in CSAT as word problems where a manufacturer’s fixed and variable costs are given, and the candidate must find the minimum number of units to sell to avoid loss. The underlying arithmetic is simple linear equations, but the phrasing tests the ability to translate a real‑world scenario into algebraic form.

4. Time and Work

4.1 Work Rate Concept and Unitary Method

The central idea in time‑work problems is that work done is the product of rate and time. If a person can complete a job in n days, the person’s one‑day work (rate) is 1/n of the total job. This unitary approach converts the problem into addition of fractions: when multiple agents work together, their rates add up. For example, if A finishes a task in 12 days and B in 18 days, A’s rate = 1/12, B’s rate = 1/18, combined rate = 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 per day, so together they finish in 36/5 = 7.2 days. The LCM method — taking the LCM of individual times as the total work unit — avoids fractions: LCM(12,18)=36 units of work; A does 3 units/day, B does 2 units/day, together 5 units/day, time = 36/5 days. Both methods are equivalent; the LCM method is often faster for mental calculation.

4.2 Combined Work and Efficiency Ratios

Efficiency is inversely proportional to the time taken for the same work. If A is twice as efficient as B, A takes half the time B takes. In problems where efficiencies are given in ratios, such as A:B:C = 3:2:1, the work done in a day by each is in the same ratio. The total work can be assumed as the sum of the ratio terms multiplied by a convenient factor, or the LCM of the times derived from the ratios. When workers join or leave midway, the work done in each phase is calculated separately using the rates applicable during that phase, and the sum of work done across phases equals the whole job. This segmentation technique is essential for problems where “A works for 3 days, then B joins, and they finish together”.

4.3 Pipes and Cisterns as Work Problems

Pipes filling or emptying a cistern are direct analogues of workers doing a job. An inlet pipe has a positive rate (fills the tank), an outlet pipe has a negative rate (empties the tank). If a pipe fills a tank in 6 hours, its rate = 1/6 tank per hour; if another empties it in 9 hours, its rate = −1/9 tank per hour. When both operate simultaneously, the net rate = 1/6 − 1/9 = 1/18 tank per hour, so the tank fills in 18 hours. Problems may involve multiple pipes opening at different times, or a leak that starts after the tank is partially filled. The same LCM technique applies: take LCM of all individual times as the tank capacity in units, compute each pipe’s units per hour, and sum them with appropriate signs.

4.4 Work and Wages Distribution

When a group of workers completes a job and the total wages are to be divided in proportion to the work done by each, the share of a worker = (worker’s rate ÷ sum of all rates) × total wages. If A, B, C work together and finish a job for ₹1800, with individual times 6, 8, 12 days respectively, their rates are 1/6, 1/8, 1/12. The ratio of rates = 4:3:2 (multiplying by LCM 24). Total parts = 9, so A gets 4/9 × 1800 = ₹800, B gets ₹600, C gets ₹400. If workers work for different durations, the work done by each = rate × days worked, and wages are divided in the ratio of these work amounts. This principle extends to situations where a worker is paid a fixed daily wage plus a bonus for early completion.

5. Speed Distance and Time

5.1 Basic Relationship and Unit Conversion

The fundamental relationship is speed = distance ÷ time, distance = speed × time, and time = distance ÷ speed. Consistency of units is critical: speed may be given in km/h while time is in minutes, or distance in metres while speed is in km/h. The conversion factors are 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s and 1 m/s = 18/5 km/h. For example, a speed of 90 km/h equals 90 × 5/18 = 25 m/s. In problems involving a journey with multiple segments, each segment’s distance, speed, and time must be expressed in compatible units before any addition or averaging. A common error is to average speeds by arithmetic mean when the distances are equal; the correct average speed for equal distances is the harmonic mean 2ab/(a+b), while for equal times it is the arithmetic mean (a+b)/2.

5.2 Relative Speed in Same and Opposite Directions

When two objects move in opposite directions, their relative speed is the sum of their individual speeds because the distance between them closes at the combined rate. When they move in the same direction, the relative speed is the absolute difference of their speeds, as the faster object gains on the slower one at that rate. This concept is used in problems of trains crossing each other, a train overtaking a man, or two persons running on a circular track. For instance, a train 200 m long running at 72 km/h crosses a man running at 6 km/h in the same direction. Convert speeds to m/s: train = 20 m/s, man = 5/3 m/s. Relative speed = 20 − 5/3 = 55/3 m/s. Time to cross = length of train ÷ relative speed = 200 ÷ (55/3) = 600/55 ≈ 10.9 seconds.

5.3 Average Speed for Equal Distances and Equal Times

Average speed for a whole journey is total distance ÷ total time. If a person travels a distance d at speed a and returns the same distance at speed b, total distance = 2d, total time = d/a + d/b = d(a+b)/(ab). Hence average speed = 2d ÷ [d(a+b)/(ab)] = 2ab/(a+b). This is the harmonic mean of the two speeds. If instead the person travels for equal times t at speeds a and b, total distance = at + bt = t(a+b), total time = 2t, average speed = (a+b)/2, the arithmetic mean. Recognising which condition applies — equal distances or equal times — is the key to selecting the correct formula. Many examination questions disguise the condition by stating “a man goes to his office at 30 km/h and returns at 50 km/h”, which implies equal distances.

5.4 Trains Boats and Streams Applications

Train problems often involve the length of the train and the length of a platform or bridge. The distance covered while crossing a stationary object equals the length of the train; while crossing a platform, it equals train length + platform length. For a train crossing another train, the distance is the sum of both lengths, and relative speed depends on direction. Boats and streams introduce the concept of still‑water speed (boat speed in still water) and stream speed. Downstream speed = boat speed + stream speed; upstream speed = boat speed − stream speed. If a boat takes 4 hours to go 40 km downstream and 5 hours to return, downstream speed = 10 km/h, upstream speed = 8 km/h. Solving the two equations gives boat speed = 9 km/h, stream speed = 1 km/h. These problems are solved by setting up two linear equations in the two unknowns.

Chinese Remainder Theorem and Simultaneous Congruences

The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) provides a systematic way to solve a system of linear congruences with pairwise coprime moduli. Given congruences x ≡ a₁ (mod m₁), x ≡ a₂ (mod m₂), …, x ≡ aₖ (mod mₖ) where each mᵢ is coprime to the others, there exists a unique solution modulo M = m₁·m₂·…·mₖ. The constructive proof builds the solution as x = Σ aᵢ·Mᵢ·yᵢ (mod M), where Mᵢ = M/mᵢ and yᵢ is the modular inverse of Mᵢ modulo mᵢ (i.e., Mᵢ·yᵢ ≡ 1 (mod mᵢ)). In examinations, the moduli are often small (≤ 20) so the inverses can be found by inspection or the extended Euclidean algorithm. An example: “Find the smallest positive integer that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 3, remainder 3 when divided by 5, and remainder 2 when divided by 7.” The CRT yields the answer 23. When moduli are not coprime, the system may have no solution or a solution modulo the least common multiple; the condition for consistency is that for every pair i, j, aᵢ ≡ aⱼ (mod gcd(mᵢ, mⱼ)). Mastery of CRT allows rapid solving of “find the number” puzzles that otherwise require tedious trial and error.

Step‑by‑step algorithm

  1. Verify pairwise coprimality; if not, reduce each congruence by dividing by common factors and check consistency.
  2. Compute M as the product of all moduli.
  3. For each i, compute Mᵢ = M/mᵢ.
  4. Find yᵢ such that Mᵢ·yᵢ ≡ 1 (mod mᵢ) using the extended Euclidean algorithm.
  5. Calculate x = (Σ aᵢ·Mᵢ·yᵢ) mod M.
  6. If the problem asks for the smallest positive integer, take x if x > 0, otherwise add M.

Practice with three‑moduli sets builds speed; some aptitude questions limit to three congruences because the arithmetic stays manageable within the time limit.

Factorial Trailing Zeros and Prime Exponent Extraction

The number of trailing zeros in n! (base 10) equals the exponent of 10 in its prime factorisation, which is the minimum of the exponents of 2 and 5. Since factors of 2 far outnumber factors of 5, the count reduces to the exponent of 5 in n!. The standard formula is ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + … until the division yields zero. For example, 100! has ⌊100/5⌋ + ⌊100/25⌋ = 20 + 4 = 24 trailing zeros. A common extension asks for the highest power of a composite number k dividing n!. Factorise k = p₁e₁ p₂e₂ …. For each prime pᵢ, compute its exponent in n! using the same floor‑sum method, then divide by eᵢ and take the floor. The answer is the minimum of these quotients. For instance, the highest power of 12 (= 2²·3) dividing 50! is min(⌊exp₂(50!)/2⌋, ⌊exp₃(50!)⌋). Here exp₂(50!) = 25+12+6+3+1 = 47, exp₃(50!) = 16+5+1 = 22. Thus ⌊47/2⌋ = 23 and 22; the limiting factor is 3, so the highest power is 22. Another twist: find the last non‑zero digit of n! in base 10. This requires removing pairs of (2,5) and computing the product of remaining residues modulo 10, often using cyclic patterns of 2, 3, 7, 9. While the full algorithm is lengthy, some questions restrict n to ≤ 30, allowing direct multiplication after stripping zeros.

When a question mixes CRT with a factorial property, solve each part independently first. Write the general CRT solution as a linear expression (a + Mk). Then impose the extra divisibility condition as a congruence on k. If the resulting linear congruence has no solution because the coefficient and modulus share a factor that does not divide the constant, state “no solution” confidently — this is a known pattern in some examiner traps.

Compound Interest with Varying Rates and Installments

Standard compound interest assumes a constant annual rate. In advanced problems, the rate may change each year, or the principal may receive periodic contributions (installments). For varying rates, the amount after n years is A = P × (1 + r₁/100) × (1 + r₂/100) × … × (1 + rₙ/100). If rates are given as percentages, convert each to a multiplier. An example: a principal of ₹10,000 at 5% for year 1, 6% for year 2, and 7% for year 3 yields A = 10000 × 1.05 × 1.06 × 1.07 = ₹11,938.10. When installments are added at the end of each year, each installment compounds for the remaining years. If an amount X is deposited annually for n years at a constant rate r, the future value is X × [(1+r)n - 1] / r (with r as a decimal). With varying rates, the formula becomes a sum: FV = Σk=1n X × Πj=k+1n (1 + rⱼ). An example: “A person invests ₹5,000 at the end of each year for 3 years. The interest rates are 8%, 9%, and 10% respectively. Find the total amount at the end of year 3.” Compute: first installment compounds for 2 years → 5000 × 1.09 × 1.10 = ₹5,995; second installment compounds for 1 year → 5000 × 1.10 = ₹5,500; third installment earns no interest → ₹5,000. Total = ₹16,495. When installments are made at the beginning of each period (annuity due), each installment gains one extra compounding period; multiply the ordinary annuity result by (1 + r) for constant rate, or adjust the product accordingly for varying rates. Another twist: find the present value of a series of future payments discounted at varying rates. The present value is PV = Σk=1n Cₖ / Πj=1k (1 + rⱼ). Practising both future‑value and present‑value variants ensures readiness for any phrasing.

Installment loan amortisation (constant rate)

For a loan of principal P repaid in n equal annual installments E at rate r, the installment is E = P·r·(1+r)n / [(1+r)n - 1]. The interest portion of the k‑th installment equals the outstanding principal before that payment times r. The principal repaid in that installment is E - interest. Building an amortisation table is rarely required in full; instead, questions ask for the interest paid in a specific year or the outstanding balance after a certain number of payments. Use the formula for outstanding balance after m payments: Bₘ = P·(1+r)m - E·[(1+r)m - 1]/r.

Circular Track Meeting Problems and Relative Motion

Circular track questions extend relative speed concepts to closed loops. Two or more runners start from the same point (or different points) and run in the same or opposite directions. The key is to work with the track length L as the “distance” for meeting calculations. When runners move in opposite directions, their relative speed is the sum of speeds; they meet every L / (v₁ + v₂) time units. When moving in the same direction, the faster gains on the slower at relative speed |v₁ - v₂|; they meet every L / |v₁ - v₂| time units. If three runners are involved, pairwise meeting times can be computed, but the time when all three meet simultaneously is the least common multiple of the pairwise meeting times (provided they start together). An example: “A and B run on a 400 m track. A runs at 5 m/s, B at 3 m/s, same direction. After how many seconds will A overtake B for the third time?” Relative speed = 2 m/s. Time for one overtaking = 400/2 = 200 s. Third overtaking = 3 × 200 = 600 s. If they start at different points, the initial separation must be added to the track length for the first meeting. For opposite directions, the first meeting occurs when the sum of distances covered equals the initial separation (or L if they start together). Another example: “A runs at 6 m/s, B at 4 m/s on a 600 m track. They start together but run in opposite directions. How many distinct meeting points will there be on the track before they both return to the start simultaneously?” The number of distinct meeting points equals the number of times they meet in one LCM of their lap times. Lap times: A = 100 s, B = 150 s. LCM = 300 s. In 300 s, A completes 3 laps, B completes 2 laps. They meet every L/(v₁+v₂) = 600/10 = 60 s. Number of meetings = 300/60 = 5. Since they start together, the meeting points are equally spaced; there are 5 distinct points. If the ratio of speeds is a rational number p/q in lowest terms, the number of distinct meeting points for same‑direction motion is |p - q|; for opposite directions it is p + q. This shortcut saves time.

Escalator problems as circular tracks

An escalator of length L moving at speed vₑ with a person walking at speed vₚ relative to ground is analogous to a circular track where the “track” moves. When the person walks in the direction of the escalator, effective speed = vₚ + vₑ; time to cover length = L/(vₚ+vₑ). Walking opposite gives L/|vₚ - vₑ|. If the person stands still, time = L/vₑ. An example: “A person takes 30 s to walk up a moving escalator and 90 s to walk down the same escalator. How long would he take if the escalator were stationary?” Let L be length, vₚ walking speed, vₑ escalator speed. Up: L = (vₚ+vₑ)·30. Down: L = (vₚ - vₑ)·90. Equate: 30(vₚ+vₑ) = 90(vₚ - vₑ) ⇒ vₚ+vₑ = 3vₚ - 3vₑ ⇒ 4vₑ = 2vₚ ⇒ vₚ = 2vₑ. Substitute: L = (2vₑ+vₑ)·30 = 90vₑ. Stationary time = L/vₚ = 90vₑ / 2vₑ = 45 s.

Advanced Data Interpretation: Caselets, Mixed Charts, and Approximation Strategies

Data Interpretation (DI) sets in competitive exams increasingly combine multiple chart types — bar, line, pie, table — within a single caselet. The first step is to read the narrative carefully and map each data representation to the relevant variable. For example, a caselet may describe a company’s quarterly revenue (bar chart), profit margin trend (line chart), and segment‑wise revenue share (pie chart). Questions often require cross‑referencing: “If the total revenue in Q3 was ₹200 cr and the profit margin was 12%, what was the absolute profit from the ‘Services’ segment which accounted for 30% of revenue?” Here profit = 200 × 0.12 = ₹24 cr; Services revenue = 200 × 0.30 = ₹60 cr; Services profit = 60 × 0.12 = ₹7.2 cr (assuming uniform margin). However, margins may differ by segment; the line chart could show segment‑wise margins. Always verify whether a given percentage applies globally or locally. Approximation is vital: when numbers are large (e.g., 1,23,45,678), round to the nearest thousand or lakh if answer options are spaced widely. Use the “percentage of percentage” shortcut: a% of b% of X = (a×b/100)% of X. For growth rates, the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) over n years from start value S to end value E is (E/S)1/n - 1. In some exams, CAGR is occasionally approximated by the arithmetic mean of yearly growth rates when the period is short and rates are small; use this cautiously. Another technique: when a table shows year‑on‑year percentage changes, the overall change after two years is not the sum but (1+r₁)(1+r₂) - 1. For quick mental calculation, if r₁=10% and r₂=20%, overall ≈ 32% (exact 1.1×1.2-1=0.32). Practice converting between absolute and relative figures: “Revenue increased by ₹50 cr, which is a 25% increase.” Then base revenue = 50/0.25 = ₹200 cr. Mixed charts may use different scales; always check axis labels and units (crores vs lakhs, thousands vs units). A systematic approach: (1) list all given figures with units, (2) identify the question’s required output unit, (3) perform conversions before arithmetic, (4) use approximation only when options differ by >5%.

Common DI question patterns

  • “What is the ratio of X in year A to Y in year B?" — extract both values, ensure same unit, simplify ratio.
  • “By what percentage did Z change from year 1 to year 3?" — compute absolute values for both years, then percentage change.
  • “If the total market size grew at 8% CAGR, what was the market size in year 5?" — apply compound growth formula.
  • “Which segment showed the highest absolute growth in rupees?" — calculate absolute growth for each segment using segment share and total revenue.

Euler's Totient Function and Fermat's Little Theorem in Remainder Problems

The Euler totient function φ(n) counts the positive integers up to n that are coprime to n. For a prime p, φ(p) = p‑1; for a prime power p^k, φ(p^k) = p^k – pk‑1. If n = ∏ p_ik_i, then φ(n) = n ∏ (1 – 1/p_i). This multiplicative property allows rapid computation of φ for large numbers once the prime factorisation is known. Fermat's Little Theorem states that if p is prime and a is not divisible by p, then ap‑1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Euler's theorem generalises this: for any a coprime to n, aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). These results turn seemingly intractable exponentiation remainders into simple modular reductions. For example, to find the remainder of 7100 divided by 12, note φ(12) = 4. Since gcd(7,12)=1, 74 ≡ 1 (mod 12). Then 7100 = (74)25 ≡ 125 ≡ 1 (mod 12). When the base and modulus are not coprime, factor out the common divisor and apply the theorem to the reduced modulus. A common examiner twist is to ask for the last two digits of a large power, which is equivalent to computing modulo 100. Because φ(100)=40, any base coprime to 100 can have its exponent reduced modulo 40. For bases sharing factors with 100 (e.g., 2, 5), separate the power of 2 and 5 using the Chinese Remainder Theorem or direct pattern observation. Mastery of φ(n) and its interplay with Carmichael function λ(n) (the least universal exponent) gives an edge in problems where the exponent itself is a large expression, such as a^b^c mod m.

Key Properties and Shortcuts

  • φ(n) is even for all n > 2.
  • If n = 2^k with k ≥ 3, φ(n) = 2k‑1 but λ(n) = 2k‑2.
  • For n = pq (product of two distinct primes), φ(n) = (p‑1)(q‑1).
  • When solving a^b mod m, reduce b modulo φ(m) only if gcd(a,m)=1; otherwise use the method of successive squaring combined with factorisation of m.

Partnership and Profit Sharing with Time‑Weighted Capital

In a partnership, profit is distributed in proportion to the product of capital invested and the time for which it remains invested. If partner A invests C_A for T_A months and partner B invests C_B for T_B months, the profit sharing ratio is C_A·T_A : C_B·T_B. This extends to multiple partners and to situations where capital is added or withdrawn mid‑period. For instance, A starts with ₹50,000; after 4 months adds ₹20,000; after 8 months withdraws ₹10,000. The effective capital‑month contribution is 50,000×4 + 70,000×4 + 60,000×4 = 7,20,000 capital‑months. When partners receive salaries or interest on capital before profit division, those amounts are deducted from the total profit first. The remaining profit is then split according to the capital‑time ratio. A frequent twist: one partner is a sleeping partner who contributes only capital, while the working partner receives a management fee. The fee is treated as an expense, not part of profit sharing. Another variation involves changing profit‑sharing ratios after a certain period (e.g., due to admission of a new partner). In such cases, compute the profit for each sub‑period separately using the respective ratios, then aggregate each partner's share. The concept of "sacrificing ratio" and "gaining ratio" appears when an existing partner surrenders a fraction of his share to a new entrant; the new partner's share is taken from the old partners in their sacrificing ratio. Examiners often embed these ideas in word problems that also test percentage and average concepts, such as "A and B start a business with capitals in the ratio 3:2. After 6 months, A withdraws half his capital. If the annual profit is ₹1,20,000, find B's share." Here A's capital‑months = 3×6 + 1.5×6 = 27 units; B's = 2×12 = 24 units. Ratio 27:24 = 9:8. B's share = 8/17 × 1,20,000 = ₹56,470.59 (rounded as per options).

Technique: Capital‑Month Table
Three partners X, Y, Z invest ₹40,000, ₹60,000, ₹80,000 respectively. After 4 months, X adds ₹10,000; after 8 months, Y withdraws ₹20,000; Z keeps his investment unchanged throughout the year. Total profit at year‑end is ₹2,10,000. Find Z's share.
Step 1: Compute capital‑months for each partner.
X: 40,000 × 4 = 1,60,000; then 50,000 × 8 = 4,00,000. Total = 5,60,000.
Y: 60,000 × 8 = 4,80,000; then 40,000 × 4 = 1,60,000. Total = 6,40,000.
Z: 80,000 × 12 = 9,60,000.
Step 2: Ratio X:Y:Z = 5,60,000 : 6,40,000 : 9,60,000 = 56 : 64 : 96 = 7 : 8 : 12.
Step 3: Total parts = 7+8+12 = 27. Z's share = (12/27) × 2,10,000 = ₹93,333.33 (approx). If options are integers, the exact fraction 12/27 × 2,10,000 = 93,333⅓; the nearest option is chosen.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Draw a timeline for each partner; mark every change of capital with the month number. Convert each segment into capital‑months immediately. This avoids missing a withdrawal or addition. When profit includes salary or interest, subtract those first before applying the ratio. Always simplify the ratio to lowest terms before multiplying by total profit to reduce arithmetic errors.

Work Equivalence and Negative Work in Time‑Work Problems

The work equivalence principle states that if a group of workers can complete a task in a certain time, any other group with the same total efficiency will take the same time. Efficiency is defined as work done per unit time. When workers have different efficiencies, we can express each worker's efficiency relative to a base worker. For example, if A is twice as efficient as B, then A's work rate = 2 units/day, B's = 1 unit/day. The combined rate is 3 units/day. Negative work arises when a worker or agent undoes part of the work, such as a leak in a cistern or a worker who damages the product. In pipe‑cistern problems, an inlet pipe does positive work (filling), while an outlet pipe does negative work (emptying). The net rate is the algebraic sum of individual rates. If an inlet fills at 1/6 tank per hour and an outlet empties at 1/8 tank per hour, net rate = 1/6 – 1/8 = 1/24 tank per hour; time to fill = 24 hours. A more subtle case: multiple outlets with different opening times. Suppose a tank has two outlets; outlet A opens at start, outlet B opens after 2 hours. The work done by outlet A in the first 2 hours is negative and must be accounted separately. The general approach: break the timeline into intervals where the set of active workers/pipes is constant. Compute net work done in each interval, sum to the total work (usually 1 unit). Solve for unknown time or rate. Examiners also test "work and wages" where wages are proportional to work done. If A, B, C together earn ₹6,000 for a job and their efficiencies are in ratio 3:2:1, then A's wage = 3/6 × 6,000 = ₹3,000. When a worker joins late or leaves early, his wage share is based on his actual work contribution (efficiency × time). A classic trap: "A and B can do a work in 12 days. B and C in 15 days. C and A in 20 days. How long will A alone take?" Using work equivalence: let total work = LCM(12,15,20)=60 units. A+B = 5 units/day, B+C = 4, C+A = 3. Adding: 2(A+B+C)=12 → A+B+C=6. Then A = 6 – (B+C) = 6 – 4 = 2 units/day. Time for A alone = 60/2 = 30 days.

Negative Work with Variable Rates

When the rate of negative work changes (e.g., a leak that widens over time), the problem becomes a differential equation, but exam questions approximate by piecewise constant rates. For instance, a cistern has a leak that empties it in 10 hours when full. After half the water is gone, the leak rate halves because pressure drops. The time to empty the full cistern is not simply 10 hours; one must integrate or use average rate. In competitive exams, such problems are simplified: "A tank has a leak that can empty it in 8 hours. A tap fills it at 6 litres/min. The tank empties in 12 hours when both are open. Find capacity." Let capacity = C litres. Leak rate = C/8 litres per hour = C/480 litres per minute. Tap rate = 6 litres/min. Net emptying rate = C/480 – 6. Time to empty = C / (C/480 – 6) = 12 hours = 720 minutes. Solve: C = 720(C/480 – 6) → C = 1.5C – 4320 → 0.5C = 4320 → C = 8640 litres.

Races, Head Starts, and Meeting Points on Linear Tracks

Race problems involve two or more participants moving on a straight track with given speeds, often with head starts or different start times. The core concept is relative speed. If two runners start simultaneously from the same point in the same direction, the faster gains on the slower at a rate equal to the difference of their speeds. The time for the faster to lap the slower (on a circular track) or to be ahead by a certain distance (on a linear track) is distance / relative speed. On a linear track of length L, if A gives B a head start of h metres, then A must cover L metres while B covers L – h metres. Since time is same, L / v_A = (L – h) / v_B → v_A / v_B = L / (L – h). This ratio is often used to find the length of the track or the head start. When three runners are involved, pairwise meeting times can be computed. For example, A, B, C run on a 400 m circular track at speeds 5, 7, 9 m/s. They start together. The time when all three meet again at the starting point is LCM of their individual lap times: 400/5=80s, 400/7≈57.14s, 400/9≈44.44s. LCM of fractions is LCM(numerators)/HCF(denominators) after expressing in simplest fractional form. A more common question: "A and B run a 1 km race. A gives B a start of 100 m and still beats him by 20 seconds. If A gives B a start of 25 seconds, the race ends in a dead heat. Find A's speed." Let v_A, v_B be speeds in m/s. First condition: A runs 1000 m, B runs 900 m. Time_A = 1000/v_A, Time_B = 900/v_B. Time_B – Time_A = 20. Second condition: A runs 1000 m, B runs 1000 m but starts 25 s earlier. So Time_B = Time_A + 25. Solve the two equations to get v_A = 10 m/s, v_B = 8 m/s. Examiners also test "meeting point" problems: two persons start from opposite ends of a straight road, move towards each other, meet, continue to ends, turn back, meet again. The distance covered by each between meetings is in the ratio of their speeds. The total distance covered by both together between first and second meeting is twice the length of the road. This pattern extends to multiple meetings.

Head Start Expressed in Time vs Distance

A head start can be given as a distance (e.g., 50 m) or as a time (e.g., 10 s). If given as time, the distance covered by the starter in that time is v_starter × head_start_time. This distance then acts as the effective distance head start for the other runner. Always convert to a common unit before setting up equations. A typical twist: "In a 200 m race, A beats B by 20 m and C by 38 m. In a race of 300 m, by how much will B beat C?" When A runs 200, B runs 180, C runs 162. Ratio B:C = 180:162 = 10:9. In 300 m, when B runs 300, C runs 270. So B beats C by 30 m.

Data Interpretation with Missing Data and Logical Constraints

Advanced DI sets often present tables or charts with missing entries that must be deduced from logical constraints such as totals, percentages, or relationships between rows and columns. The first step is to identify all given figures and the constraints linking them. For example, a table shows quarterly sales of four products across three regions, with some cells blank. The row totals (product‑wise annual sales) and column totals (region‑wise quarterly sales) are provided. This creates a system of linear equations. Since the numbers are integers (sales units), the solution is often unique. A systematic approach: (1) list all known values, (2) write equations for each total, (3) solve sequentially starting with rows or columns having the fewest unknowns. When percentages are involved, convert to absolute numbers using a known base. If a pie chart shows percentage shares but the total is missing, look for an absolute figure elsewhere in the caselet (e.g., "Product A sold 12,000 units in Q1"). That absolute figure anchors the percentages. Another common constraint: "The ratio of sales in Region X to Region Y is 3:2 in Q2." This gives a linear relation between two missing cells. Examiners also embed logical conditions like "No region's sales decreased from Q1 to Q2" or "Product B's sales in Q3 were exactly the average of its Q1 and Q2 sales." These inequalities or equalities restrict the feasible values. In approximation‑heavy sets, the missing data may not need exact computation; instead, the question asks "Which of the following could be the value of …?" Use the constraints to bound the variable. For instance, if total sales = 1,00,000 and three products account for 30%, 25%, 20% respectively, the fourth product's share is 25% exactly. If the percentages are approximate (rounded to nearest integer), the fourth product's share lies between 24.5% and 25.5%. Always check whether percentages are exact or rounded. A frequent trap: a stacked bar chart where the segments represent percentages of different totals (e.g., each bar is 100% but the absolute totals differ across bars). Comparing segment heights across bars without adjusting for the different totals leads to errors. The correct method: compute absolute values for each segment using the bar's total (given or derived), then compare.

Base Conversion and Divisibility in Non‑Decimal Systems

Binary, octal, and hexadecimal representations appear regularly in quantitative aptitude papers. Any integer can be expressed in base b as a sum of digits multiplied by powers of b. Conversion from base b to decimal uses the expansion dkbk + dk-1bk-1 + … + d1b + d0. The reverse conversion repeatedly divides the decimal number by b, recording remainders. For example, decimal 45 to binary: 45 ÷ 2 = 22 rem 1, 22 ÷ 2 = 11 rem 0, 11 ÷ 2 = 5 rem 1, 5 ÷ 2 = 2 rem 1, 2 ÷ 2 = 1 rem 0, 1 ÷ 2 = 0 rem 1 → reading remainders upward gives 1011012.

Divisibility rules in other bases follow the same modular logic as base 10. A number in base b is divisible by b−1 if the sum of its digits is divisible by b−1 (analogue of the 9‑rule). It is divisible by b+1 if the alternating sum of digits is divisible by b+1 (analogue of the 11‑rule). For binary (b=2), divisibility by 3 corresponds to the alternating sum of bits. In hexadecimal (b=16), divisibility by 15 uses digit‑sum, and divisibility by 17 uses alternating sum. These shortcuts avoid full conversion when only a divisibility check is required.

Typical examiner twists: (1) a base-7 number divided by 6 — apply the digit‑sum rule in base 7; (2) “smallest 8‑bit number divisible by 5” — find the smallest multiple of 5 in 0–255; (3) mixed‑base arithmetic: add (123)4 and (321)5, express in base 6 — convert to decimal, add, convert to base 6. Mastery of these algorithms makes such problems solvable in under a minute.

Key Conversion Algorithms

  • Decimal → base b: repeated division, collect remainders in reverse order.
  • Base b → decimal: evaluate polynomial using Horner’s method: (((dk×b + dk-1)×b + … )×b + d0).
  • Base b → base c (both non‑decimal): convert to decimal first, then to target base; or use grouping when b and c are powers of the same integer (e.g., binary ↔ octal via groups of three bits, binary ↔ hexadecimal via groups of four bits).

Divisibility Shortcuts

  • Base b, divisor b−1: sum of digits ≡ 0 (mod b−1).
  • Base b, divisor b+1: alternating sum of digits ≡ 0 (mod b+1).
  • Base b, divisor d where d | (bk ± 1): group digits in blocks of k and apply sum or alternating sum.

Advanced Permutation and Combination with Constraints

Beyond the basic formulas nPr = n!/(n−r)! and nCr = n!/(r!(n−r!)), competitive exams test restricted arrangements. The most frequent constraints: (i) objects always together — treat the block as a single unit, then permute internally; (ii) objects never together — use the gap method: arrange unrestricted objects, then choose gaps for restricted ones; (iii) fixed positions — reduce the problem size by placing those objects first; (iv) circular permutations — for n distinct objects around a circle the count is (n−1)!; if reflections are identical (necklace problems), divide by 2.

Derangements (permutations with no fixed points) appear in “letters into wrong envelopes” questions. The number of derangements of n items, denoted !n, satisfies !n = n! Σk=0n (−1)k/k! and the recurrence !n = (n−1)(!(n−1) + !(n−2)). For small n memorise: !1=0, !2=1, !3=2, !4=9, !5=44.

Inclusion–exclusion handles multiple overlapping restrictions. Example: “Arrange the letters of ‘ENGINEERING’ so that no two E’s are adjacent and the three N’s are not all together.” Total arrangements = 11!/(3!3!2!2!). Subtract arrangements with E’s adjacent (treat EE as a block), subtract those with N’s together (treat NNN as a block), add back where both hold. The principle extends to any number of forbidden patterns.

Combinations with repetition (stars and bars) solve “number of non‑negative integer solutions to x₁+x₂+…+xr = n” = C(n+r−1, r−1). With lower bounds ai, substitute yi = xi−ai and apply the formula. Upper bounds require inclusion–exclusion or generating functions; in exams, upper bounds are usually small enough to enumerate complementary cases.

Common Constraint Patterns

  • “Vowels never together” → arrange consonants, then choose gaps for vowels.
  • “Particular persons always sit together at a round table” → block method, then (n−k)! × k! for internal order.
  • “Select a committee with at least one member from each department” → total selections minus selections missing at least one department (inclusion–exclusion).
Inclusion–Exclusion for Arrangements with Forbidden Positions
In how many ways can the letters of the word “COMPUTER” be arranged so that the vowels O, U, E never occupy the first, third, and fifth positions respectively? (Each vowel has its own forbidden position.)

Total arrangements of 8 distinct letters = 8! = 40320.

Let A = arrangements with O in position 1, B = arrangements with U in position 3, C = arrangements with E in position 5.

|A| = 7! = 5040 (fix O, permute remaining 7). Similarly |B| = |C| = 5040.

|A∩B| = 6! = 720 (fix O and U). |A∩C| = |B∩C| = 720.

|A∩B∩C| = 5! = 120 (fix all three vowels).

By inclusion–exclusion, arrangements with at least one forbidden placement = |A|+|B|+|C| − (|A∩B|+|A∩C|+|B∩C|) + |A∩B∩C| = 3×5040 − 3×720 + 120 = 15120 − 2160 + 120 = 13080.

Valid arrangements = 40320 − 13080 = 27240.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Permutation‑Combination
Identify the constraint type within 10 seconds: together → block; never together → gap method; fixed positions → reduce n; circular → (n−1)!; derangement → use !n table. Write the unrestricted total first, then apply subtraction or inclusion–exclusion. For combination with repetition, instantly recall C(n+r−1, r−1). Keep a mental list of !n up to 6 to avoid factorial expansions under time pressure.

Conditional Probability and Bayes' Theorem in Quantitative Reasoning

Conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) (provided P(B)>0) is the foundation of Bayes' theorem: P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B). In exam questions, the sample space is often described in words — “a bag contains 4 red and 6 blue balls; two balls are drawn without replacement”. Tree diagrams clarify sequential draws: first branch for colour of first ball, second branch for colour of second ball conditional on the first. The probability of a path is the product of branch probabilities.

Typical Bayes' scenario: “A diagnostic test for a disease has 95% sensitivity (true positive rate) and 90% specificity (true negative rate). The disease prevalence is 2%. A person tests positive. What is the probability they actually have the disease?” Let D = disease, T = positive test. P(D)=0.02, P(T|D)=0.95, P(T|¬D)=0.10. P(T) = P(T|D)P(D) + P(T|¬D)P(¬D) = 0.95×0.02 + 0.10×0.98 = 0.019 + 0.098 = 0.117. Then P(D|T) = 0.95×0.02 / 0.117 ≈ 0.1624 (16.24%). The low posterior despite high sensitivity illustrates base‑rate neglect — a common examiner trap.

Questions may combine conditional probability with combinatorics: “From a deck of 52 cards, two cards are drawn without replacement. Given that at least one is an ace, find the probability that both are aces.” Let A = both aces, B = at least one ace. P(A) = C(4,2)/C(52,2) = 6/1326. P(B) = 1 − C(48,2)/C(52,2) = 1 − 1128/1326 = 198/1326. P(A|B) = (6/1326) / (198/1326) = 6/198 = 1/33.

When events are independent, P(A|B)=P(A). Recognising independence (e.g., draws with replacement, separate experiments) saves computation. Conversely, “without replacement” always creates dependence.

Quick Reference for Common Set‑ups

  • Two‑stage experiments: draw tree, multiply along branches, sum for total probability of final outcome.
  • Medical / quality‑control testing: define disease/defect as event D, test result as T; apply Bayes' formula directly.
  • “At least one” conditions: use complement (none) to find denominator.
  • Odds form of Bayes: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.

Mixture and Alligation with Multiple Components and Replacement

The classic alligation rule solves “in what ratio must two ingredients at prices p₁ and p₂ be mixed to obtain a mixture at price pₘ?” The ratio is (p₂−pₘ) : (pₘ−p₁). This extends to three components by pairwise alligation or by solving the linear system: let quantities be x, y, z; then (p₁x + p₂y + p₃z)/(x+y+z) = pₘ. With one degree of freedom, infinite solutions exist; additional constraints (e.g., total quantity fixed, or one component quantity given) yield a unique answer.

Repeated replacement problems (e.g., “a container holds 40 L of milk; 4 L is removed and replaced with water, repeated 3 times”) follow the formula: final amount of original substance = initial × (1 − r/V)n, where r = volume removed each time, V = total volume, n = number of replacements. For milk‑water, the fraction of milk after n replacements = (1 − r/V)n. If the removed volume differs each step, multiply the successive factors.

When two liquids are mixed and then a portion is replaced by a third liquid, track each component separately. Example: “10 L of a 30% alcohol solution is mixed with 5 L of a 50% alcohol solution. Then 3 L of the mixture is removed and replaced with pure water. Find the final alcohol percentage.” Step 1: total alcohol = 10×0.3 + 5×0.5 = 5.5 L; total volume = 15 L → concentration = 5.5/15 = 11/30. Step 2: remove 3 L → alcohol removed = 3×(11/30) = 1.1 L; remaining alcohol = 4.4 L; volume returns to 15 L after adding water → final concentration = 4.4/15 ≈ 29.33%.

Alligation can also handle “mixing three alloys with different copper percentages to achieve a target”. Write the copper balance equation and use the method of “alligation alternate” — pair each component with the target, compute ratios, then combine. If the target lies outside the range of given percentages, the problem has no solution (examiner may ask “which of the following cannot be the resulting percentage?”).

Replacement with Changing Volumes

  • If after each replacement the total volume changes (e.g., some mixture is discarded and not fully replenished), adjust V for each step.
  • For “remove x L, add y L of another substance”, the factor for the original substance becomes (V−x)/V; the added substance accumulates additively.

Advanced Average and Weighted Average Techniques for Data Interpretation

The arithmetic mean is a special case of the weighted average where all weights are equal. In DI, weighted averages appear when combining groups of different sizes: overall average = (Σ ni·ai) / Σ ni. The deviation method speeds up calculation: choose a convenient assumed mean A, compute deviations di = ai − A, then overall average = A + (Σ nidi)/Σ ni. This avoids large multiplications.

Harmonic mean is the correct average for rates when distances are equal (e.g., average speed for equal distances). For two speeds v₁ and v₂ over equal distances, harmonic mean = 2v₁v₂/(v₁+v₂). For more than two equal‑distance segments, harmonic mean = n / Σ(1/vi). Weighted harmonic mean applies when distances differ: total distance / total time.

Average of averages is a common pitfall.

Digital Root and Casting Out Nines in Number System

The digital root of a positive integer is obtained by repeatedly summing its digits until a single digit remains. For any integer n, the digital root equals n mod 9, with the convention that a remainder of 0 corresponds to a digital root of 9 (except for n = 0). This property follows directly from the fact that 10 ≡ 1 (mod 9), so each decimal place contributes its digit value modulo 9. Consequently, the digital root provides an instant check for divisibility by 3 and 9: a number is divisible by 3 iff its digital root is 3, 6, or 9; it is divisible by 9 iff its digital root is 9.

Casting out nines extends this idea to verify arithmetic operations. To test an addition, compute the digital root of each addend, sum those digital roots, reduce to a single digit, and compare with the digital root of the claimed sum. If they differ, an error is certain; if they match, the calculation is likely correct (though not guaranteed because different numbers can share the same digital root). The same procedure works for subtraction, multiplication, and division (by checking the product of divisor and quotient plus remainder). In competitive examinations, casting out nines is a rapid sanity‑check for large multiplications or for confirming the correctness of a derived answer without re‑doing the entire computation.

Beyond verification, digital roots simplify problems involving repeated exponentiation. For example, to find the digital root of 7100, note that the digital root of powers of 7 cycles every 6: 7, 4, 1, 7, 4, 1 … Since 100 mod 6 = 4, the digital root equals the fourth element of the cycle, which is 7. This cyclicity is a direct consequence of Euler’s theorem for modulus 9 (φ(9)=6) and avoids heavy modular arithmetic. Examiners often frame questions such as “Find the remainder when 123456789 is divided by 9” — the answer is simply the digital root of the number, here 9, so the remainder is 0.

When dealing with very large numbers expressed in factorial or combinatorial form, digital roots can be combined with Legendre’s formula for the exponent of 3 in n! to determine the digital root of the factorial after removing all factors of 3. This technique appears in advanced number‑system questions where the examiner expects the candidate to recognise that the digital root of n! for n ≥ 6 is always 9 because the factorial contains at least two factors of 3, making it a multiple of 9. Mastery of digital root and casting out nines therefore yields both a verification tool and a shortcut for remainder‑type problems.

LCM and HCF of Fractions and Decimals

The concepts of least common multiple (LCM) and highest common factor (HCF) extend naturally from integers to rational numbers. For two fractions a/b and c/d in lowest terms, the LCM is defined as the smallest positive rational number that is an integer multiple of both fractions. The standard formula is LCM(a/b, c/d) = LCM(a, c) / HCF(b, d). Similarly, the HCF of the fractions is the largest rational number that divides both exactly, given by HCF(a/b, c/d) = HCF(a, c) / LCM(b, d). These formulas hold for any number of fractions by iteratively applying the pairwise rule.

When decimals appear, convert each to a fraction with a power‑of‑10 denominator, reduce to lowest terms, and then apply the fraction formulas. For instance, to find the LCM of 0.6, 0.15, and 0.2, write them as 3/5, 3/20, and 1/5. The numerators are 3, 3, 1 → LCM = 3. The denominators are 5, 20, 5 → HCF = 5. Hence LCM = 3/5 = 0.6. The HCF would be HCF(3,3,1) / LCM(5,20,5) = 1 / 20 = 0.05. This method avoids the common pitfall of treating decimals as integers after shifting the decimal point, which can produce an incorrect LCM because the scaling factor must be common to all numbers.

A frequent examiner twist asks for the LCM of a set that includes both fractions and integers. Treat integers as fractions with denominator 1. For example, LCM of 4, 3/2, and 5/3: numerators 4, 3, 5 → LCM = 60; denominators 1, 2, 3 → HCF = 1; result = 60/1 = 60. The HCF of the same set: HCF(4,3,5)=1; LCM(1,2,3)=6; HCF = 1/6. Understanding that the LCM of fractions can be an integer (when the denominator HCF is 1) and that the HCF of fractions is often a proper fraction is crucial for selecting the correct option in multiple‑choice questions.

Applications extend to problems involving periodic events with fractional periods. If three bells ring every 2/3 minute, 3/4 minute, and 5/6 minute, they will ring together again after LCM(2/3, 3/4, 5/6) minutes. Compute LCM of numerators 2,3,5 = 30; HCF of denominators 3,4,6 = 1; LCM = 30 minutes. This is far quicker than converting each period to seconds and finding the LCM of large integers. Mastery of fraction LCM/HCF therefore saves time and reduces arithmetic errors in time‑work and speed‑distance problems that involve rational rates.

LCM of Fractions Shortcut
Find the LCM of 3/4, 5/6, and 7/8.
Step 1: Identify numerators 3, 5, 7. LCM(3,5,7) = 105. Step 2: Identify denominators 4, 6, 8. HCF(4,6,8) = 2. Step 3: Apply formula LCM = LCM(numerators) / HCF(denominators) = 105 / 2 = 52.5 or 105/2. The smallest positive rational that is an integer multiple of each given fraction is 105/2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the two fraction formulas: LCM = LCM(num)/HCF(den) and HCF = HCF(num)/LCM(den). Convert decimals to fractions by counting decimal places, then reduce before applying the formulas. Practice a set of 10 mixed fraction‑decimal LCM/HCF problems daily to internalise the steps and avoid the common mistake of scaling decimals unevenly.

Time and Work with Alternating Workers and Rest Periods

Many time‑work questions move beyond constant combined efficiency and introduce workers who operate on alternating days or who take mandatory rest days. The core technique is to determine a repeating cycle that captures the pattern of work and rest, compute the work done in one cycle, and then scale to the total work. For example, if A works on day 1, rests on day 2, B works on day 2, rests on day 1, and both work together on day 3, the 3‑day cycle contains A’s work for 2 days, B’s work for 2 days, and joint work for 1 day. Express each worker’s daily work as a fraction of the total job (1/individual time). Sum the contributions over the cycle to get the fraction of the job completed per cycle. The number of full cycles needed is the integer part of (1 ÷ work per cycle). The remaining fraction is finished by simulating the next days in order until the job is done.

When rest periods are not symmetric, the cycle length becomes the least common multiple of the individual work‑rest periods. Suppose A works 2 days, rests 1 day (period 3), and B works 3 days, rests 2 days (period 5). The combined pattern repeats every LCM(3,5)=15 days. Build a 15‑day table marking each day as work or rest for each worker, compute total work done in 15 days, and proceed as before. This systematic approach eliminates guesswork and handles any number of workers with distinct schedules.

Efficiency changes mid‑project (e.g., a worker’s speed doubles after a certain date) are handled by splitting the timeline into phases. Compute the work done in each phase using the appropriate rates, then add the phases. If the change occurs at a fractional day, treat the partial day with the old rate for the fraction before the change and the new rate for the remainder. Examiners often combine alternating schedules with efficiency changes, requiring the candidate to first find the cycle for the initial phase, then recompute for the second phase.

A classic trap is to assume that the average daily work equals the arithmetic mean of individual daily works. This is false when workers do not work simultaneously every day. The correct average is total work in a cycle divided by cycle length. Another trap is ignoring the possibility that the job finishes mid‑cycle; the answer must be expressed in days (possibly fractional) rather than in whole cycles. Practising with a variety of alternating‑worker scenarios builds the intuition to set up the cycle table quickly and to recognise when the LCM of periods becomes unwieldy — in such cases, simulating day‑by‑day for a few cycles until the remaining work is small can be faster than computing a huge LCM.

Relative Motion on Circular Tracks with Multiple Runners and Staggered Starts

Circular track problems with more than two runners and staggered start times require a clear separation of two concepts: meeting points and meeting times. For any pair of runners moving in the same direction, the relative speed is the difference of their speeds; in opposite directions, it is the sum. The time for the faster runner to gain one full lap on the slower is track length ÷ relative speed. When three or more runners start together, the time when all meet at the starting point is the LCM of their individual lap times. However, if they start at different times, the first simultaneous meeting at the start occurs at the smallest time t such that for each runner i, (t − start_i) is a multiple of lap_i. This is a system of congruences solvable by the Chinese Remainder Theorem when the lap times are pairwise coprime, or by iterative search otherwise.

Meeting anywhere on the track (not necessarily at the start) is more frequent. For two runners, the number of distinct meeting points on the track equals the relative speed divided by the GCD of their speeds (when expressed in integer units per lap). For multiple runners, the set of meeting points is the intersection of the pairwise meeting‑point sets. A practical shortcut: convert all speeds to a common unit (e.g., laps per minute), then the positions of runners at time t are fractional parts of (speed_i × t + offset_i). Runners meet when these fractional parts coincide. Plotting the positions on a unit circle for a few LCM‑based time intervals often reveals the pattern without heavy algebra.

Staggered starts are equivalent to giving each runner an initial angular offset. If runner A starts 2 minutes before runner B on a 400 m track, and their speeds are 5 m/s and 7 m/s, then at the moment B starts, A has already covered 600 m, i.e., 1.5 laps. The effective offset for A relative to B is 0.5 lap. The first meeting after B’s start occurs when the relative distance (7−5)×t equals 0.5 lap (200 m), giving t = 100 s. Subsequent meetings occur every 200 s (track length ÷ relative speed). When three runners have different offsets, compute pairwise meeting times and then find the common time satisfying all pairwise conditions.

Examiners frequently ask for the number of meetings in a given duration, or the time of the k‑th meeting. The formula for the k‑th meeting of two runners is t_k = (initial offset + k × track length) ÷ relative speed. For multiple runners, the k‑th simultaneous meeting of all is the k‑th common element of the pairwise meeting sequences, which can be found by generating the sequences up to the required k (usually small) or by solving the congruence system. Mastery of these techniques turns seemingly complex circular‑track questions into systematic arithmetic.

Data Interpretation Using Venn Diagrams and Overlapping Sets

Data interpretation sets often present information about populations classified by two or three attributes (e.g., students taking Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry). The principle of inclusion‑exclusion provides the algebraic backbone: for two sets A and B, |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. For three sets, |A ∪ B ∪ C| = Σ|singles| − Σ|pairwise intersections| + |triple intersection|. In DI questions, the total surveyed population may be given, or the number of elements outside all sets (the complement) may be provided, allowing the union size to be deduced.

A typical DI table might list the number of candidates who passed each subject and the number who passed various combinations. The candidate must fill the Venn diagram regions systematically: start with the triple intersection (if given), then compute each pairwise‑only region by subtracting the triple from the given pairwise totals, then compute each single‑only region by subtracting the relevant pairwise‑only and triple from the individual totals. Finally, the complement (none) is total minus sum of all seven regions. Errors often arise from double‑counting the triple intersection when subtracting pairwise totals; the rule is to subtract the triple exactly once from each pairwise total.

When the data is presented as percentages rather than absolute numbers, convert to absolute numbers using the total sample size before drawing the diagram. If the total is not given, treat the percentages as parts of 100 and work with the 100‑base; the final answer may be required as a percentage, which can be read directly from the diagram. Questions may ask for the maximum or minimum possible value of a region given incomplete data. For instance, “At least 70% like tea, at least 80% like coffee, what is the minimum percentage that like both?” The minimum overlap occurs when the union is as large as possible (capped at 100%), giving min(both) = 70% + 80% − 100% = 50%. The maximum overlap is the smaller of the two percentages (70%). These extremal problems test the candidate’s ability to manipulate the inclusion‑exclusion bounds.

Advanced DI sets combine Venn logic with other quantitative concepts: the regions may represent amounts of money, quantities of goods, or time durations, and subsequent questions may ask for weighted averages, profit‑loss calculations, or ratio comparisons based on the region values. The workflow remains: extract the region values accurately, then apply the required arithmetic. Practising the translation from textual or tabular data to a fully labelled Venn diagram is the single most effective skill for this question type, because it makes all subsequent calculations transparent and reduces the chance of misreading overlapping categories.

  • Applying integer LCM/HCF formulas directly to decimals without converting to reduced fractions.
  • Treating alternating‑worker schedules as simultaneous work and averaging daily outputs.
  • Confusing meeting at the starting point with meeting anywhere on a circular track.
  • Forgetting to subtract the triple intersection once from each pairwise total when filling a three‑set Venn diagram.
  • Using the arithmetic mean of percentages instead of the inclusion‑exclusion bounds for minimum/maximum overlap.
  • Neglecting to convert staggered start times into angular offsets before computing relative meetings.

These advanced techniques complete the quantitative toolkit needed for high‑difficulty aptitude questions.

Variable Speed Motion and Piecewise Distance Problems

Many aptitude questions move beyond the single‑speed model and present a journey split into two or more legs, each with its own constant speed. The core principle remains distance = speed × time, but the solver must write separate equations for each leg and then link them through the total distance or total time. A typical pattern gives the speed for the first part, the speed for the second part, and either the total time or the total distance, asking for the unknown distance of one leg. The algebraic route is to let the unknown distance be x, express the time for each leg as x / v₁ and (D – x) / v₂, and equate the sum to the given total time. When the total distance is unknown but the total time and the two speeds are known, the same method yields a linear equation in the total distance.

A frequent twist is the “speed change after a fixed time” variant. Here the traveller moves at speed v₁ for t₁ hours, then at v₂ for the remaining time. The distance covered in the first phase is v₁ t₁; the remaining distance is v₂ (T – t₁). The total distance is the sum, and the average speed for the whole journey is total distance divided by total time T. Candidates often mistakenly average the two speeds arithmetically; the correct average is the arithmetic mean weighted by the times spent at each speed.

Piecewise problems also appear in “round‑trip with different speeds” format. If a vehicle goes from A to B at v₁ and returns at v₂, the average speed for the round trip is 2 v₁ v₂ / (v₁ + v₂). This formula is derived by letting the one‑way distance be d, total distance 2d, total time d/v₁ + d/v₂, and simplifying. Examiners may embed this in a larger puzzle where the round‑trip time is given and the one‑way distance is asked.

Another layer is the “variable speed with acceleration ignored” scenario: a train passes a pole in t₁ seconds and a platform of length L in t₂ seconds. The train’s length ℓ and speed v satisfy ℓ = v t₁ and ℓ + L = v t₂. Subtracting gives L = v (t₂ – t₁), so v = L / (t₂ – t₁) and ℓ = v t₁. This two‑equation method is a staple of high‑difficulty sets.

When three or more legs are involved, the system stays linear. Write one equation per leg, sum the times (or distances), and solve the resulting single‑variable equation. The key discipline is to keep units consistent — convert km/h to m/s by multiplying by 5/18, and hours to seconds by multiplying by 3600 — before forming equations.

Multi‑Stage Profit Loss with Tax, Commission, and Successive Transactions

Real‑world profit‑loss questions often stack several financial layers: a manufacturer marks up cost by a percentage, a wholesaler adds a margin, a retailer offers a discount, and a government levies a tax (e.g., GST) on the final selling price. The examiner expects the candidate to track the cost price (CP) through each stage, applying the appropriate percentage on the correct base. The golden rule: every percentage is applied on the immediate preceding amount, not on the original CP unless explicitly stated.

Consider a product with CP = ₹100. Manufacturer marks up 20 % → marked price (MP₁) = ₹120. Wholesaler buys at MP₁, adds 15 % margin → MP₂ = ₹120 × 1.15 = ₹138. Retailer gives 10 % discount on MP₂ → selling price before tax = ₹138 × 0.90 = ₹124.20. GST at 18 % is levied on this discounted price → final price paid by customer = ₹124.20 × 1.18 = ₹146.556. The overall profit percent for the manufacturer‑wholesaler‑retailer chain is calculated on the original CP: ((146.556 – 100) / 100) × 100 = 46.556 %. Note that the GST is not a profit for any trader; it is a liability collected on behalf of the government.

Commission problems follow the same logic. An agent sells goods worth ₹50,000 at a 5 % commission. The agent receives ₹2,500; the principal receives ₹47,500. If the principal’s cost was ₹40,000, the principal’s profit percent = ((47,500 – 40,000) / 40,000) × 100 = 18.75 %. When multiple agents share commission in a given ratio, split the total commission accordingly before computing individual profits.

Successive transactions (buy‑sell‑buy‑sell) are another favourite. A person buys an article at ₹800, sells at 10 % profit, buys it back at 5 % loss on the selling price, and finally sells again at 12 % profit on the second buying price. Track each CP and SP: first SP = 800 × 1.10 = ₹880; second CP = 880 × 0.95 = ₹836; final SP = 836 × 1.12 = ₹936.32. Overall profit = ₹936.32 – ₹800 = ₹136.32 → profit percent = (136.32 / 800) × 100 = 17.04 %. The shortcut is to multiply the successive factors: 1.10 × 0.95 × 1.12 = 1.1704, confirming the 17.04 % net gain.

Examiners also embed “discount on marked price vs discount on cost price” traps. A 20 % discount on MP is not equivalent to a 20 % discount on CP unless MP = CP. Always identify the base explicitly.

Technique: Chain Factor Multiplication
A shopkeeper buys an item for ₹1,200. He marks it up by 25 %, offers a 10 % discount on the marked price, and then pays 12 % GST on the discounted price. What is the final price paid by the customer and the shopkeeper’s effective profit percent on the original cost?
Step 1: Marked price = 1,200 × 1.25 = ₹1,500.
Step 2: Discounted price = 1,500 × 0.90 = ₹1,350.
Step 3: Final price with GST = 1,350 × 1.12 = ₹1,512.
Step 4: Profit = 1,512 – 1,200 = ₹312.
Step 5: Profit percent = (312 / 1,200) × 100 = 26 %.
Chain factor = 1.25 × 0.90 × 1.12 = 1.26 → 26 % net increase.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a profit‑loss chain appears, write the multiplicative factor for each stage (1 + p/100 for profit/markup, 1 – d/100 for discount/loss, 1 + t/100 for tax). Multiply all factors; the product minus 1 gives the overall percentage change. This avoids repeated intermediate rounding and saves time.

Advanced Mixture Problems: Multi‑Component Replacement and Concentration Dynamics

Classic alligation handles two ingredients. Advanced sets introduce three or more components, or a process where a portion of the mixture is repeatedly removed and replaced by a single component (often pure solvent or pure solute). The concentration after each replacement follows a geometric progression. If a vessel contains V litres of a solution with initial concentration C₀ (fraction of solute), and each operation removes r litres of the mixture and replaces it with pure solvent (concentration 0), the concentration after n operations is Cₙ = C₀ × (1 – r/V)ⁿ. The factor (1 – r/V) is the fraction of original solution that remains after one replacement.

When the replacement liquid is not pure solvent but another solution of concentration Cᵣ, the recurrence becomes Cₙ₊₁ = Cₙ (1 – r/V) + Cᵣ (r/V). This linear recurrence solves to Cₙ = Cᵣ + (C₀ – Cᵣ) (1 – r/V)ⁿ. The steady‑state concentration (as n → ∞) is Cᵣ, which is intuitive: infinite replacements make the mixture identical to the replacement liquid.

Multi‑component alligation uses the concept of weighted average. For three components with concentrations c₁, c₂, c₃ mixed in ratios w₁ : w₂ : w₃, the resultant concentration is (w₁c₁ + w₂c₂ + w₃c₃) / (w₁ + w₂ + w₃). To find the ratio that yields a target concentration T, set up two independent alligation steps: first combine two components to get an intermediate concentration, then combine that with the third. The ratios from each step multiply to give the final three‑way ratio.

A common examiner twist is “changing volume” replacement: after each removal the total volume changes because the replacement quantity differs from the removed quantity. If r litres are removed and s litres of a different liquid are added, the volume after one operation becomes V – r + s. The concentration update must use the new volume for the next step, leading to a non‑constant factor. In such cases, simulate the first two or three steps to detect a pattern, or set up the recurrence with the varying volume explicitly.

Questions may also ask for the amount of pure solute to be added to raise concentration from C₁ to C₂ in a fixed volume V. The equation is V C₁ + x = (V + x) C₂ → x = V (C₂ – C₁) / (1 – C₂). This formula is derived by equating solute before and after addition.

Data Interpretation: Multi‑Source Fusion, Ratio Consistency, and Approximation Bounds

High‑difficulty DI sets rarely present a single table. Instead, they give a bar chart for yearly sales, a pie chart for product‑wise share, and a caselet describing regional distribution. The candidate must fuse these sources: convert percentages to absolute numbers using a common base (usually total revenue or total units), then cross‑check for consistency. If the bar chart says total revenue in 2023 was ₹500 cr and the pie chart assigns 30 % to Product A, then Product A’s revenue is ₹150 cr. If the caselet states that the North region contributed 40 % of Product A’s revenue, the North‑Product‑A figure is ₹60 cr. Any subsequent question asking for the South region’s share of Product A can be answered by subtracting the known regional parts from the product total.

Ratio consistency checks are a powerful error‑detection tool. Suppose a table gives the ratio of male to female employees in three departments as 3:2, 4:5, and 7:3, and the overall company ratio is 5:4. The weighted average of the departmental ratios must equal the overall ratio when weighted by department sizes. If the department sizes are unknown, the overall ratio imposes a linear constraint that can be used to solve for missing department totals.

Approximation bounds become essential when exact arithmetic is cumbersome. For a fraction a/b where a and b are large, round both to the nearest hundred or thousand, compute the approximate quotient, and then adjust using the direction of rounding. If both numerator and denominator are rounded up, the true value lies between the two extreme approximations. Examiners often ask “which of the following is closest to …?” — a quick bounding calculation eliminates three options instantly.

Another pattern is “missing data with logical constraints”. A 3 × 3 table may have four empty cells but the row totals, column totals, and grand total are given. The system of linear equations is usually determined; solve by substitution starting from the row or column with the fewest unknowns. If the system is under‑determined, the question will ask for a range (minimum/maximum) of a cell, which is solved by pushing other cells to their extreme permissible values (zero or the relevant total).

Caselets with interlinked tables require building a master spreadsheet mentally. Identify the primary key (e.g., year, product, region) that appears in every sub‑table. Populate a master grid with that key as rows and all attributes as columns. Fill known cells, then propagate derived values across the grid. This systematic approach prevents double‑counting and ensures that every piece of information is used exactly once.

Number System: Continued Fractions, Best Rational Approximations, and Pell’s Equation Basics

Continued fractions provide the most efficient way to obtain the best rational approximations to irrational numbers. For a real number x, its simple continued fraction expansion is x = a₀ + 1/(a₁ + 1/(a₂ + …)) where the aᵢ are positive integers (except possibly a₀). The convergents pₖ/qₖ obtained by truncating after aₖ satisfy the recurrence pₖ = aₖ pₖ₋₁ + pₖ₋₂, qₖ = aₖ qₖ₋₁ + qₖ₋₂ with initial values p₋₂=0, p₋₁=1, q₋₂=1, q₋₁=0. Each convergent is a best approximation in the sense that any other fraction with denominator ≤ qₖ is farther from x.

For quadratic irrationals such as √D (D non‑square), the continued fraction is eventually periodic. The period length determines the fundamental solution of Pell’s equation x² – D y² = 1. If the period length is even, the fundamental solution is given by the convergent at the end of the first period; if odd, it is the convergent at the end of the second period. For example, √2 = [1; 2, 2, 2, …] with period 1 (odd). The convergents are 1/1, 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, … The fundamental solution of x² – 2y² = 1 is (3, 2) from the second convergent. All solutions are generated by (3 + 2√2)ⁿ.

In aptitude exams, Pell’s equation appears indirectly: “Find the smallest integer n such that n² – 5 m² = 1 for some integer m.” Recognising D = 5, the continued fraction of √5 = [2; 4, 4, 4, …] (period 1, odd). The fundamental solution is (9, 4) from the second convergent, so the smallest n is 9. Candidates who know the periodic pattern for √2, √3, √5, √6, √7, √10 can answer such questions in seconds.

Best rational approximation also solves “find the fraction with denominator ≤ 100 closest to π”. The convergents of π are 3/1, 22/7, 333/106, 355/113, … The denominator 113 exceeds 100, so the best approximation with denominator ≤ 100 is 22/7. This technique replaces trial‑and‑error with a deterministic algorithm.

Examiners may ask for the sum of the first few partial quotients or the value of a convergent modulo some number. Because the recurrence is linear, modular arithmetic can be applied directly to the recurrence to compute pₖ mod M and qₖ mod M without handling huge integers.

Modular Exponentiation with Binary Exponentiation and Carmichael Reduction

When a question asks for the remainder of a large power such as 712345 ÷ 100, direct computation is impossible. The standard tool is binary (fast) exponentiation combined with a reduction of the exponent using the Carmichael function λ(n). For any integer a coprime to n, aλ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). λ(n) is the least common multiple of the Carmichael values of the prime‑power factors of n. For n = 100 = 22·52, λ(100) = lcm(λ(4), λ(25)) = lcm(2, 20) = 20. Hence 720 ≡ 1 (mod 100) and the exponent 12345 can be reduced modulo 20: 12345 mod 20 = 5. The problem collapses to 75 mod 100 = 16807 mod 100 = 7.

Binary exponentiation computes ae mod m in O(log e) multiplications by repeatedly squaring the base and multiplying into the result when the current bit of e is 1. The algorithm:

  1. Set result = 1, base = a mod m.
  2. While e > 0: if e is odd, result = (result × base) mod m; base = (base × base) mod m; e = e // 2.
  3. Return result.

When a and m are not coprime, Euler’s theorem does not apply directly. In that case factor m, compute the remainder modulo each prime power using the lifting‑the‑exponent lemma or by separating the common factor, then recombine with the Chinese Remainder Theorem. For example, to find 12100 mod 18, note gcd(12,18)=6. Write 12 = 6·2, 18 = 6·3. Then 12100 = 6100·2100. Modulo 18, 62 = 36 ≡ 0, so for any exponent ≥2 the term is 0. Hence the remainder is 0.

Examiners often combine this with digit‑cyclicity: the last two digits of powers of 7 repeat every 20 because λ(100)=20. Recognising the cycle length avoids the full binary exponentiation for many standard bases (2,3,7,8,9). A quick reference table of λ(n) for n ≤ 1000 is a valuable memorisation target.

Successive Percentage Change with Shifting Base and Compound Discount Layers

Successive percentage changes are multiplicative, not additive. If a price is increased by 20 % and then decreased by 20 %, the net factor is 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96, a 4 % loss. The trap appears when the base for the second change is not the original amount but the already‑changed amount — this is the default, yet many candidates mistakenly apply the second percentage to the original base. A more subtle variation introduces a shifting base: “A shopkeeper marks up an article by 30 %, then offers a 10 % discount on the marked price, and finally adds a 5 % sales tax on the discounted price.” The overall factor is 1.30 × 0.90 × 1.05 = 1.2285, i.e. a 22.85 % increase over cost.

Compound discount layers occur in trade scenarios: “A wholesaler gives a 15 % trade discount, then a 5 % cash discount on the reduced price, and the retailer further offers a 10 % seasonal discount.” The net selling price factor relative to the list price is 0.85 × 0.95 × 0.90 = 0.72675. If the retailer’s cost price is known, the profit percentage is (1 ÷ 0.72675 − 1) × 100 ≈ 37.6 %.

When percentages are applied to different bases — e.g., a 10 % increase on cost, then a 10 % decrease on selling price — the net effect is not zero. Let cost = C. Marked price = 1.10C. Selling price after 10 % discount = 0.90 × 1.10C = 0.99C. The trader incurs a 1 % loss. The general formula for two successive changes p % and q % (with q applied on the new base) is net factor = (1 + p/100)(1 + q/100). If the second change is on the original base, the factor becomes 1 + (p+q)/100, which is a common distractor.

In data‑interpretation sets, a table may show year‑on‑year growth rates for revenue and expense with different bases. To compare profitability, convert each series to a common base year using cumulative multiplication of growth factors. This avoids the error of averaging percentages across years.

Worked Example: Layered Discount and Tax
A product’s list price is ₹10,000. The seller offers a 20 % trade discount, then a 5 % cash discount on the reduced price, and finally charges 12 % GST on the final discounted price. Find the final amount payable by the customer.
Step 1: Apply trade discount: 10,000 × (1 − 0.20) = 8,000.
Step 2: Apply cash discount on ₹8,000: 8,000 × (1 − 0.05) = 7,600.
Step 3: Apply GST on ₹7,600: 7,600 × (1 + 0.12) = 7,600 × 1.12 = 8,512.
Final amount = ₹8,512. The net multiplication factor is 0.80 × 0.95 × 1.12 = 0.8512, confirming a 14.88 % reduction from list price.
Exam‑Day Strategy
For any chain of percentage changes, write the multiplication factor for each step (1 ± p/100) and multiply them in order. Keep the product as a decimal; convert to percentage only at the end. This eliminates base‑shift errors and works even when the number of steps exceeds three.

Time and Work: Fractional Efficiencies, Partial Completion, and Man‑Day Equivalence

Standard time‑work problems assume constant efficiency. Advanced variants introduce fractional efficiencies (e.g., a worker’s output drops to 3/4 after half the work) or partial completion where a group leaves mid‑project. The man‑day (or person‑day) concept remains the anchor: total work = Σ (efficiency × days). If efficiencies differ, convert each worker to an equivalent number of standard workers. For instance, A works at 1.2 standard units, B at 0.8. Together they equal 2.0 standard workers. If the job requires 100 standard man‑days, they finish in 100 ÷ 2.0 = 50 days.

When efficiency changes at a known fraction of work, split the project into phases. Suppose A and B together complete a task in 12 days at constant rates. After 1/3 of the work, A’s efficiency falls to half. Let total work = 1 unit. Combined rate initially = 1/12 per day. In phase 1 (1/3 work), time = (1/3) ÷ (1/12) = 4 days. Remaining work = 2/3. New combined rate = (A/2 + B). Since original A+B = 1/12, and we need individual rates, assume A = a, B = b with a+b = 1/12. Without extra data the problem is under‑determined; examiners usually provide either individual times or the ratio of efficiencies. If A alone takes 20 days, a = 1/20, b = 1/12 − 1/20 = 1/30. New rate = (1/40 + 1/30) = 7/120. Phase 2 time = (2/3) ÷ (7/120) = 80/7 ≈ 11.43 days. Total ≈ 15.43 days.

Partial completion with workers joining/leaving: “A starts a job, after 5 days B joins, after another 3 days C joins, and the work finishes in 4 more days. If A alone takes 30 days, B 20 days, C 15 days, find the fraction of work done by each.” Convert to rates: a=1/30, b=1/20, c=1/15. Work done = a·5 + (a+b)·3 + (a+b+c)·4. Compute each contribution and divide by total work (which equals 1). This yields exact fractions.

Man‑day equivalence extends to machines with different power ratings. If machine X does a job in 8 hours and machine Y in 12 hours, their power ratio is 3:2. When both run together, effective power = 5 units, time = (8×3) ÷ 5 = 4.8 hours. This avoids the harmonic‑mean formula and scales to any number of machines.

Relative Motion in Two Dimensions: River Crossing, Wind Drift, and Minimum Time Paths

One‑dimensional relative speed (trains, boats on a straight river) is familiar. Two‑dimensional motion appears when a boat must cross a flowing river and reach a point directly opposite, or when an aircraft flies in a crosswind. The velocity vectors add: resultant = boat velocity relative to water + water velocity relative to ground. To reach the point opposite, the boat must aim upstream at an angle θ such that the upstream component cancels the current: vboat sin θ = vcurrent. The crossing time = width ÷ (vboat cos θ). The minimum crossing time occurs when the boat points straight across (θ=0), but then it drifts downstream. The minimum drift for a given crossing time is a constrained optimisation problem often solved by calculus, but in aptitude tests the numbers are chosen so that Pythagorean triples appear.

Example: River width = 400 m, current = 3 m/s, boat speed in still water = 5 m/s. To go straight across, sin θ = 3/5 ⇒ θ ≈ 36.87°, cos θ = 4/5. Crossing time = 400 ÷ (5 × 4/5) = 400 ÷ 4 = 100 s. Drift = 0. If the boat heads straight across, crossing time = 400 ÷ 5 = 80 s, drift = 3 × 80 = 240 m.

Wind drift for aircraft follows the same vector addition. If an aircraft’s airspeed is 600 km/h and wind blows at 80 km/h from the west, to maintain a northward ground track the pilot must head slightly west of north. The required heading angle satisfies 600 sin θ = 80 ⇒ sin θ = 2/15. Ground speed northward = 600 cos θ = 600 √(1 − 4/225) ≈ 594.6 km/h.

Minimum‑time path problems: “A man can row at 4 km/h in still water and walk at 5 km/h. He is on one bank of a 1 km wide river flowing at 2 km/h. He wants to reach a point 3 km downstream on the opposite bank in minimum time.” He can row to some landing point x km downstream, then walk the remaining (3−x) km. Rowing time = √(1² + x²) ÷ 4 (since resultant speed across is √(4²−2²)? Actually rowing velocity relative to ground has components: across = √(4²−2²) if he aims upstream to cancel drift? The standard approach: let him aim at angle α upstream. Across component = 4 cos α, downstream component = 4 sin α − 2. To land at x, downstream displacement = (4 sin α − 2) × (1 ÷ (4 cos α)) = x. Solve for α, then total time = crossing time + walking time. In exams, the numbers are chosen so that the optimal x is an integer or simple fraction, often found by testing options.

Data Interpretation: Linear Programming Formulation and Corner‑Point Optimization

Advanced DI sets sometimes present a resource‑allocation scenario disguised as a table: “A factory produces two products P and Q. Each unit of P requires 2 h on machine A and 1 h on machine B. Each unit of Q requires 1 h on A and 3 h on B. Machine A is available 100 h, machine B 90 h. Profit per unit: P ₹30, Q ₹40. Maximise profit.” This is a linear programming problem. Define variables x = units of P, y = units of Q. Constraints: 2x + y ≤ 100; x + 3y ≤ 90; x, y ≥ 0. Objective: maximise Z = 30x + 40y.

The feasible region is a convex polygon. The optimum occurs at a corner point (vertex) of this polygon. Find intersections: (0,0), (50,0) from 2x+y=100, (0,30) from x+3y=90, and intersection of 2x+y=100 and x+3y=90. Solve: multiply second by 2 → 2x+6y=180; subtract first → 5y=80 ⇒ y=16, x=42. Evaluate Z at each vertex: (0,0)=0; (50,0)=1500; (0,30)=1200; (42,16)=30·42+40·16=1260+640=1900. Maximum profit = ₹1900 at (42,16).

When the DI table includes additional constraints such as minimum production quotas or integer requirements, the same corner‑point method applies; if integer solutions are required, test the integer points near the optimal vertex. In multiple‑choice questions, the options are often the objective values at the vertices, so computing all vertex values is sufficient.

Another pattern: “A diet must contain at least 50 g protein and 30 g fat. Food X gives 10 g protein, 5 g fat per unit, cost ₹20. Food Y gives 5 g protein, 10 g fat per unit, cost ₹15. Minimise cost.” Variables x,y ≥ 0. Constraints: 10x+5y ≥ 50; 5x+10y ≥ 30. Objective: minimise C = 20x+15y. Convert to standard form by multiplying inequalities by −1 if needed, then find corner points of the feasible region (which is unbounded but the objective gradient points toward a bounded minimum). The minimum will be at the intersection of the two lines: 10x+5y=50 and 5x+10y=30 → solving gives x=4, y=2. Cost = 20·4+15·2 = 110.

Examiners may hide the linear‑programming structure inside a caselet with multiple products and shared resources. The key is to identify decision variables, write constraints from the resource limits, and recognise the linear objective. Once formulated, the graphical method (for two variables) or the simplex insight (optimum at a vertex) yields the answer quickly.

  • Assuming the exponent reduction modulus is φ(n) instead of λ(n) when the base is not coprime to the modulus.
  • Applying the second percentage to the original base instead of the already‑changed base in successive change problems.
  • Treating fractional efficiency changes as additive rather than multiplicative on the rate.
  • Ignoring the vector nature of river‑crossing problems and using scalar relative speed formulas.
  • Forgetting to check all corner points of the feasible region in linear‑programming DI sets, especially when the feasible region is unbounded.
  • Using the harmonic mean for average speed when the journey segments have different distances but the same time, or vice‑versa.
  • Confusing the direction of inequality when converting a “minimum requirement” constraint into standard ≤ form for graphical solution.

Quadratic Residues and Legendre Symbol in Modular Arithmetic

A quadratic residue modulo an odd prime p is an integer a such that the congruence x² ≡ a (mod p) has a solution. The Legendre symbol ( a | p ) is defined as 1 if a is a quadratic residue and a ≢ 0 (mod p), −1 if a is a non‑residue, and 0 if p divides a. Its fundamental properties are multiplicativity ( ab | p ) = ( a | p )( b | p ), Euler’s criterion ( a | p ) ≡ a^((p−1)/2) (mod p), and the law of quadratic reciprocity: for distinct odd primes p, q, ( p | q )( q | p ) = (−1)^((p−1)(q−1)/4). Supplementary laws give ( −1 | p ) = (−1)^((p−1)/2) and ( 2 | p ) = (−1)^((p²−1)/8). These tools let us decide solvability of x² ≡ a (mod p) without trial, and they underpin primality tests such as Solovay–Strassen, where the Jacobi symbol (a generalisation to composite moduli) replaces the Legendre symbol. In examinations, a typical twist is to ask for the value of ( a | p ) for a large a by reducing a modulo p first, then applying reciprocity repeatedly. Another common trap is to treat the Jacobi symbol as a true indicator of quadratic residuosity when the modulus is composite; the Jacobi symbol can be 1 even if a is a non‑residue modulo a composite number.

Arithmetico‑Geometric Series and Telescoping Sums

An arithmetico‑geometric series has the form ∑k=0n-1 (a + kd) r^k, where the coefficients follow an arithmetic progression while the powers follow a geometric progression. The closed form for r ≠ 1 is obtained by multiplying the series by r, subtracting, and solving for the sum: S_n = (a − (a + nd) r^n) / (1 − r) + d r (1 − r^n) / (1 − r)². When r = 1 the series collapses to an arithmetic sum n(2a + (n−1)d)/2. For an infinite series the condition |r| < 1 is required; then S_∞ = a/(1−r) + d r/(1−r)². Telescoping sums exploit the identity u_k = vk+1 − v_k so that ∑k=mn u_k = vn+1 − v_m. Classic examples include ∑ 1/(k(k+1)) = 1 − 1/(n+1) and ∑ k/(k+1)! = 1 − 1/(n+1)!. In data‑interpretation sets these series appear as present‑value calculations for a growing annuity or as cumulative growth models where the growth rate and the increment both change each period. Examiners often hide the telescoping structure by writing terms as fractions with factorials or binomial coefficients; recognising the difference of successive terms is the key to a quick solution.

Technique: Summing an Arithmetico‑Geometric Series
Find the value of S = 1·2⁰ + 2·2¹ + 3·2² + … + 10·2⁹.
Here a = 1, d = 1, r = 2, n = 10. Since r ≠ 1, use the finite formula: S = (a − (a + nd) rⁿ)/(1 − r) + d r (1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r)². Substituting: (1 − (1 + 10·1)·2¹⁰)/(1 − 2) + 1·2·(1 − 2¹⁰)/(1 − 2)² = (1 − 11·1024)/(−1) + 2·(1 − 1024)/1 = (1 − 11264)/(−1) + 2·(−1023) = 11263 − 2046 = 9217. The sum is 9217.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a series mixes arithmetic and geometric progressions, first identify the common ratio r. If |r|≠1, use the standard formula S_n = (a − (a+nd)r^n)/(1−r) + dr(1−r^n)/(1−r)². For r=1 the series reduces to an arithmetic sum. Always check whether the question asks for a finite sum or an infinite sum; the infinite sum exists only when |r|<1. Write the formula once, substitute numbers, and simplify using powers of two or three that are common in the paper.

Vieta’s Formulas and Symmetric Polynomial Identities

For a monic polynomial P(x) = xⁿ + c₁ xⁿ⁻¹ + c₂ xⁿ⁻² + … + cₙ with roots α₁, α₂, …, αₙ, Vieta’s formulas state that the elementary symmetric sums of the roots equal (−1)ᵏ cₖ: ∑ αᵢ = −c₁, ∑i<j</sub> αᵢαⱼ = c₂, …, α₁α₂…αₙ = (−1)ⁿ cₙ. These relations hold for any polynomial after dividing by the leading coefficient. Newton’s identities connect power sums pₖ = ∑ αᵢᵏ to the elementary symmetric sums eₖ: p₁ = e₁, p₂ = e₁p₁ − 2e₂, p₃ = e₁p₂ − e₂p₁ + 3e₃, and in general pₖ = ∑i=1k (−1)i-1 eᵢ pk-i + (−1)k-1 k eₖ. This allows evaluation of expressions such as α₁²+α₂²+α₃² = (α₁+α₂+α₃)² − 2(α₁α₂+α₂α₃+α₃α₁) without solving the cubic. In competitive questions, a system of equations symmetric in the variables can be transformed into a polynomial whose roots are the unknowns; Vieta then gives the required symmetric functions directly. A frequent examiner twist is to present a non‑monic polynomial and ask for a symmetric expression; forgetting to normalise by the leading coefficient leads to sign errors. Another trap is to assume Vieta applies to non‑polynomial equations (e.g., rational equations) without first clearing denominators and checking for extraneous roots.

Expected Value and Decision Trees in Quantitative Reasoning

The expected value of a discrete random variable X is E[X] = ∑ xᵢ P(X = xᵢ). Linearity of expectation holds regardless of dependence: E[X+Y] = E[X] + E[Y]. A decision tree models sequential choices under uncertainty: square nodes represent decisions, circular nodes represent chance events with associated probabilities, and leaf nodes carry payoffs. To evaluate a tree, work backwards from the leaves: at each chance node compute the expected payoff, at each decision node choose the branch with the highest expected payoff (or lowest expected cost). This framework appears in profit‑loss scenarios with probabilistic demand, insurance premium calculations, and simple game‑theory payoffs. Examiners may embed a decision tree inside a caselet where probabilities are derived from a table of historical frequencies. A common twist is to ask for the expected value of a product of dependent variables; linearity does not apply to products, so one must either compute the joint distribution or use conditional expectation E[XY] = E[X·E[Y|X]]. Another pitfall is to ignore the requirement that chance‑node probabilities sum to one; missing a branch invalidates the expected‑value computation. When the tree includes a “do nothing” option with zero cost, that branch must be included in the maximisation step.

Optimization with Convex Functions and Jensen’s Inequality

A function f is convex on an interval if for any λ ∈ [0,1] and any x, y in the interval, f(λx + (1−λ)y) ≤ λf(x) + (1−λ)f(y). Jensen’s inequality generalises this to finite sums: f(∑ λᵢ xᵢ) ≤ ∑ λᵢ f(xᵢ) for non‑negative weights λᵢ summing to 1. The inequality reverses for concave functions. Classic consequences include the arithmetic‑mean–geometric‑mean inequality (AM ≥ GM) by applying Jensen to the concave logarithm, and the Cauchy‑Schwarz inequality via the convexity of x². In resource‑allocation problems the cost function is often convex (e.g., quadratic cost), so the minimum of ∑ f(xᵢ) subject to a linear constraint occurs when the marginal costs are equal, a condition that can be derived from Jensen or from Lagrange multipliers. Examiners test the direction of the inequality: applying Jensen to a convex function when the problem asks for a maximum, or vice‑versa, yields the wrong bound. Another trap is to assume the equality case (all xᵢ equal) is attainable when the variables are required to be integers; the true integer optimum may lie at a neighbouring lattice point. Finally, Karamata’s inequality extends Jensen to majorised vectors, but it is rarely needed unless the problem explicitly involves a sequence of ordered variables.

  • Confusing the Legendre symbol with the Jacobi symbol when the modulus is composite.
  • Forgetting the convergence condition |r| < 1 for infinite arithmetico‑geometric series.
  • Applying Vieta’s formulas to a non‑monic polynomial without dividing by the leading coefficient.
  • Assuming linearity of expectation holds for products of dependent random variables.
  • Using Jensen’s inequality in the wrong direction for concave versus convex functions.
  • Overlooking that decision‑tree chance nodes must have probabilities summing to one.
  • Ignoring integer constraints when a continuous convex optimisation yields a non‑integer solution.

These advanced tools expand the problem‑solving toolkit for high‑difficulty quantitative questions.

Inclusion–Exclusion Principle and Derangements in Counting Problems

The inclusion–exclusion principle provides a systematic way to count the number of elements in the union of several finite sets by alternately adding and subtracting the sizes of their intersections. For three sets A, B, C the formula is |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|. The pattern extends to any number of sets: add the sizes of all single sets, subtract the sizes of all pairwise intersections, add the sizes of all triple intersections, and so on, alternating signs until the intersection of all sets is reached. This principle is indispensable when direct counting is obstructed by overlapping conditions, such as counting integers up to N that are divisible by at least one of several given divisors.

A classic application is the derangement problem: the number of permutations of n distinct objects in which no object appears in its original position. Let Dₙ denote this number. Using inclusion–exclusion on the sets Aᵢ = {permutations where object i is fixed}, we obtain Dₙ = n! ∑k=0n (−1)ᵏ / k!. The recurrence Dₙ = (n−1)(Dₙ₋₁ + Dₙ₋₂) with D₀ = 1, D₁ = 0 follows from a simple combinatorial argument and is often faster for computation. In examinations, derangements appear disguised as “letters placed in wrong envelopes”, “secret Santa assignments with no self‑gifts”, or “seating arrangements where no person sits in their designated chair”. Recognising the underlying structure allows the candidate to apply the closed form or the recurrence without enumerating cases.

When the sets involved are not symmetric, the principle still works but the bookkeeping becomes heavier. A useful shortcut is to compute the complement: count the total number of objects and subtract those that satisfy none of the conditions. For instance, to find how many integers between 1 and 1000 are divisible by 2, 3, or 5, compute 1000 − (numbers divisible by none of 2,3,5). The latter is obtained by inclusion–exclusion on the complements, which often involves fewer terms because the least common multiples grow quickly. Candidates should practice converting word problems into set notation, identifying the universal set, and writing the alternating sum explicitly before simplifying.

Another frequent twist is the “at least k conditions” variant. If a problem asks for the number of elements that belong to at least two of the given sets, one can sum the sizes of all pairwise intersections and then subtract three times the triple intersections, etc., or use the identity: |elements in ≥2 sets| = ∑|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ| − 2∑|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ| + 3|A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ A₃ ∩ A₄| − … . Mastery of these variations separates high scorers from those who rely solely on the basic union formula.

Logarithmic Equations and Exponential Growth Models

Logarithms transform multiplicative relationships into additive ones, making them the natural tool for solving equations where the unknown appears in an exponent. The fundamental identities logₐ(xy) = logₐ x + logₐ y, logₐ(x/y) = logₐ x − logₐ y, and logₐ(xᵏ) = k logₐ x hold for any base a > 0, a ≠ 1. In competitive papers the base is often 10 (common logarithm) or e (natural logarithm, denoted ln). Changing base is achieved by logₐ x = log_b x / log_b a, a formula that must be memorised because calculators are not permitted.

Exponential growth and decay models appear in population dynamics, compound interest, radioactive decay, and bacterial growth. The standard form is N(t) = N₀ ekt for continuous growth, where k > 0 indicates growth and k < 0 decay. The doubling time T₂ = ln 2 / k and half‑life T₁/₂ = ln 2 / |k| are direct consequences. When the compounding is discrete, the model becomes N(t) = N₀ (1 + r)ᵗ with r the per‑period rate. Candidates must be able to switch between continuous and discrete forms, recognising that ek = 1 + r.

Solving logarithmic equations often requires exponentiating both sides to eliminate the logarithm, but one must check for extraneous roots because the argument of a logarithm must be positive. For example, log₂(x−1) + log₂(x+3) = 3 leads to (x−1)(x+3) = 8, giving x = 3 or x = −5; the latter is rejected because x−1 would be negative. A common examiner trap is to present an equation like logₓ 8 = 3, which rewrites to x³ = 8, yielding x = 2, but the base condition x > 0, x ≠ 1 must be verified.

In data interpretation, exponential trends are linearised by taking logarithms: plotting ln y against t yields a straight line whose slope is k and intercept ln N₀. This technique is used to estimate growth rates from tabular data. Approximation skills are crucial: knowing that ln 2 ≈ 0.693, ln 3 ≈ 1.099, ln 5 ≈ 1.609, and ln 10 ≈ 2.303 allows quick mental estimates. Similarly, log₁₀ 2 ≈ 0.301, log₁₀ 3 ≈ 0.477, log₁₀ 5 ≈ 0.699, log₁₀ 7 ≈ 0.845. These constants enable rapid comparison of magnitudes without a calculator.

Worked Example: Exponential Growth with Discrete Compounding
A bacterial culture doubles every 3 hours. If the initial count is 500, after how many hours will the count first exceed 1,00,000?
The discrete model is N = 500 × 2t/3. We need 500 × 2t/3 > 100000 ⇒ 2t/3 > 200. Taking log₁₀: (t/3) log₁₀ 2 > log₁₀ 200 = log₁₀ 2 + 2 = 0.301 + 2 = 2.301. So t/3 > 2.301 / 0.301 ≈ 7.645. Hence t > 22.935 hours. Since t must be a multiple of 3 hours for exact doubling, the first integer multiple exceeding 22.935 is 24 hours. At t = 24, N = 500 × 2⁸ = 500 × 256 = 128000 > 100000. Answer: 24 hours.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the key logarithm values (log₁₀ 2,3,5,7 and ln 2,3,5,10). When an exponential equation appears, first decide whether the compounding is continuous or discrete; write the appropriate model, then take logarithms. Always check the domain of the logarithmic arguments before finalising the answer. For “first exceed” questions, solve the inequality, then round up to the nearest admissible time unit (often the compounding period).

Coordinate Geometry: Distance, Section Formula, and Locus of Points

Coordinate geometry translates geometric problems into algebraic equations, allowing the use of algebraic manipulation to derive lengths, ratios, and equations of curves. The distance between two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) is √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]. The section formula gives the coordinates of a point dividing the segment PQ internally in the ratio m:n as ((mx₂ + nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂ + ny₁)/(m+n)). For external division the signs of m and n are opposite. These formulas are the backbone of problems involving centroids, incentres, and points of trisection.

The locus of a point satisfying a given geometric condition is the set of all its possible positions. For example, the locus of points equidistant from two fixed points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB, whose equation can be derived by setting the squared distances equal: (x−x₁)² + (y−y₁)² = (x−x₂)² + (y−y₂)², which simplifies to a linear equation. The locus of points at a constant distance r from a fixed point (h,k) is the circle (x−h)² + (y−k)² = r². More complex loci, such as the set of points where the sum of distances to two foci is constant, yield ellipses; the difference of distances yields hyperbolas. In the examination, candidates are rarely asked to derive the full conic equation but may need to identify the type of curve from a given condition or to find the equation of a tangent/normal using the derivative concept (though calculus is not required, the geometric property that the radius is perpendicular to the tangent suffices).

A frequent question type provides the coordinates of three vertices of a triangle and asks for the area, the equation of a median, or the coordinates of the orthocentre. The area can be computed via the determinant formula ½ |x₁(y₂−y₃) + x₂(y₃−y₁) + x₃(y₁−y₂)|. The centroid is simply the average of the vertices. The orthocentre requires finding the intersection of two altitudes; each altitude is a line through a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side. The slope of a line through (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) is (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁); the perpendicular slope is its negative reciprocal. Setting up the two altitude equations and solving the linear system yields the orthocentre. Practice with these steps reduces the chance of algebraic slips.

Another examiner favourite is the “variable point” problem: a point P moves such that its distance from a fixed point is k times its distance from a fixed line. The resulting locus is a conic (ellipse if k<1, parabola if k=1, hyperbola if k>1). Recognising the eccentricity definition allows immediate classification without deriving the full equation. Candidates should also be comfortable with the parametric form of a line (x = x₁ + t cos θ, y = y₁ + t sin θ) for problems involving distance along a line from a given point.

Inequalities: AM–GM, Cauchy–Schwarz, and Rearrangement in Optimization

Inequalities are powerful tools for finding extrema without calculus. The Arithmetic Mean–Geometric Mean inequality states that for non‑negative real numbers a₁, a₂, …, aₙ, (a₁ + a₂ + … + aₙ)/n ≥ (a₁ a₂ … aₙ)1/n, with equality iff all numbers are equal. This is often used to bound products given a fixed sum, or sums given a fixed product. A typical application: if x + y = 10, the maximum of xy occurs when x = y = 5, giving xy ≤ 25. For three variables with a fixed sum, the product is maximised when they are equal.

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in its simplest form says (∑ aᵢ²)(∑ bᵢ²) ≥ (∑ aᵢ bᵢ)². In the Engel form, (∑ aᵢ² / bᵢ) ≥ (∑ aᵢ)² / (∑ bᵢ) for positive bᵢ. This form is extremely useful for minimising expressions like x²/a + y²/b + z²/c subject to x + y + z = constant. By setting aᵢ = x, y, z and bᵢ = a, b, c, the minimum is (x+y+z)²/(a+b+c), attained when x/a = y/b = z/c. Many “minimum value of” questions in the paper are direct applications of this form.

The Rearrangement inequality states that for two sequences sorted in the same order, the sum of pairwise products is maximal; for opposite order it is minimal. If a₁ ≤ a₂ ≤ … ≤ aₙ and b₁ ≤ b₂ ≤ … ≤ bₙ, then ∑ aᵢ bσ(i) is maximised when σ is the identity permutation and minimised when σ is the reverse permutation. This principle solves problems like “given two sets of numbers, pair them to maximise the sum of products” without trial and error. It also underlies Chebyshev’s inequality: if both sequences are similarly sorted, (1/n)∑ aᵢ bᵢ ≥ ((1/n)∑ aᵢ)((1/n)∑ bᵢ).

In optimisation, these inequalities often combine. For instance, to minimise (x + y)(1/x + 1/y) for positive x, y, expand to 2 + x/y + y/x. By AM–GM, x/y + y/x ≥ 2, so the expression ≥ 4, equality at x = y. Another classic: minimise a/x + b/y subject to x + y = c. By Cauchy–Schwarz, (a/x + b/y)(x + y) ≥ (√a + √b)², so the minimum is (√a + √b)² / c, attained when x/√a = y/√b. Recognising the pattern “sum of fractions with linear constraint” triggers the Cauchy–Schwarz approach instantly.

Recurrence Relations and Generating Functions for Sequence Summation

Many quantitative problems define a sequence recursively, e.g., aₙ = 3aₙ₋₁ − 2aₙ₋₂ with given initial terms. Solving such linear recurrences with constant coefficients involves finding the characteristic equation r² − 3r + 2 = 0, whose roots r = 1, 2 give the general solution aₙ = A·1ⁿ + B·2ⁿ = A + B·2ⁿ. The constants A, B are determined from the initial conditions. When the characteristic equation has a repeated root r, the general term includes a polynomial factor: aₙ = (A + Bn) rⁿ. Mastery of this method allows closed‑form evaluation of the nth term in O(1) time, which is essential when n is large (e.g., 10⁹).

Generating functions provide an alternative algebraic framework. The ordinary generating function of a sequence {aₙ} is G(x) = ∑n≥0 aₙ xⁿ. For a linear recurrence, multiplying the recurrence by xⁿ and summing over n yields an equation for G(x) that can be solved as a rational function. Partial fraction decomposition then extracts the coefficient of xⁿ, giving the closed form. While the full generating function machinery is rarely required in the examination, the concept of “sum of series” often reduces to evaluating ∑ aₙ xⁿ at a specific x (e.g., x = 1/2). Knowing that ∑n≥0 rⁿ = 1/(1−r) for |r|<1, and ∑n≥0 n rⁿ = r/(1−r)², enables quick summation of arithmetico‑geometric series.

Non‑homogeneous recurrences such as aₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + n are solved by finding a particular solution (often a polynomial of the same degree as the non‑homogeneous term) and adding the homogeneous solution. For aₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + n, try a particular solution of the form An + B; substitution gives A = −1, B = −2, so aₙ = C·2ⁿ − n − 2. The constant C comes from the initial term. This technique appears in problems modelling “population with constant immigration” or “savings with fixed monthly deposit”.

Summation of series defined by recurrences can be tackled by telescoping. If aₙ = bₙ − bₙ₋₁, then ∑k=1n aₖ = bₙ − b₀. Identifying such a representation often involves rewriting the term using partial fractions or known identities. For example, 1/(k(k+1)) = 1/k − 1/(k+1) telescopes to 1 − 1/(n+1). More complex telescoping uses identities like 1/(k(k+1)(k+2)) = ½[1/(k(k+1)) − 1/((k+1)(k+2))]. Practising these patterns builds the intuition needed to spot telescoping opportunities under time pressure.

  • Applying inclusion–exclusion without clearly defining the universal set, leading to off‑by‑one errors in complement counts.
  • Forgetting to reject extraneous roots when solving logarithmic equations, especially when the argument becomes non‑positive.
  • Misusing the section formula for external division by not assigning opposite signs to the ratio components.
  • Assuming equality case of AM–GM is always attainable when variables are constrained to be integers; the true optimum may be at a neighbouring integer point.
  • Applying Cauchy–Schwarz in the wrong direction (e.g., using it to find a maximum when it gives a minimum) because the inequality sign is reversed for the Engel form if the denominator sum is not constant.
  • Overlooking the characteristic equation’s repeated root case, resulting in a missing polynomial factor in the closed form of a recurrence.
  • Failing to check the convergence condition |r| < 1 before using the infinite geometric series formula in generating‑function summations.

These advanced topics deepen the quantitative toolkit and, when mastered, allow a candidate to solve high‑difficulty problems with speed and confidence.

Mensuration of Three‑Dimensional Solids: Volume, Surface Area, and Frustum Formulas

Three‑dimensional mensuration appears regularly in the quantitative section, often combined with cost‑price or material‑usage scenarios. The core solids are the cube, cuboid, right circular cylinder, right circular cone, sphere, hemisphere, and the frustum of a cone. For each solid two surface‑area measures are relevant: the curved (or lateral) surface area and the total surface area which includes the base(s). Volume formulas are derived from the base area multiplied by height (for prisms and cylinders) or one‑third of that product (for pyramids and cones).

Cube (side a): volume = a³; total surface area = 6a²; lateral surface area = 4a². Cuboid (length l, breadth b, height h): volume = l b h; total surface area = 2(lb + bh + hl); lateral surface area = 2h(l + b). Right circular cylinder (radius r, height h): volume = πr²h; curved surface area = 2πr h; total surface area = 2πr(r + h). Right circular cone (radius r, height h, slant height l = √(r²+h²)): volume = ⅓πr²h; curved surface area = πr l; total surface area = πr(r + l). Sphere (radius r): volume = ⁴⁄₃πr³; surface area = 4πr². Hemisphere: volume = ²⁄₃πr³; curved surface area = 2πr²; total surface area = 3πr² (includes the circular base).

The frustum of a cone (radii R > r, height h, slant height L = √((R−r)²+h²)) is a frequent source of tricky questions. Its volume = ⅓πh(R²+Rr+r²). Curved surface area = πL(R+r). Total surface area = π[L(R+r) + R² + r²]. A useful shortcut: if a cone is cut by a plane parallel to its base, the removed top is a similar cone; the ratio of corresponding linear dimensions equals the ratio of the radii. This similarity lets you find the height of the original cone when only the frustum dimensions are given.

Combined solids (e.g., a cylinder surmounted by a hemisphere, or a cone placed on a cylinder) require adding volumes and the relevant exposed surface areas. When a solid is melted and recast, volume remains constant — equate the volume of the original solid to the volume of the new shape. For hollow solids (pipes, bowls) subtract the inner volume from the outer volume. In cost‑price problems, compute the volume of material used, multiply by the rate per cubic unit, and add any fixed overhead.

Common examiner twists: (1) giving the total surface area of a closed cylinder and asking for the volume, which requires solving for r and h from 2πr(r+h) = given value; (2) asking for the ratio of volumes of two similar solids when the linear scale factor is given — volume ratio is the cube of the linear ratio; (3) embedding a frustum inside a larger cone and asking for the volume of the remaining part, which tests the similarity property.

Trigonometric Heights and Distances: Angle of Elevation, Depression, and Practical Shortcuts

Heights‑and‑distances problems reduce to right‑triangle trigonometry. The angle of elevation is the angle between the horizontal line from the observer and the line of sight to an object above the horizontal. The angle of depression is the angle between the horizontal and the line of sight to an object below the horizontal; it equals the angle of elevation from the object to the observer because of alternate interior angles. The primary ratio used is the tangent: tan θ = opposite/adjacent = height / horizontal distance.

Standard angles and their tangent values must be memorised: tan 0° = 0; tan 30° = 1/√3; tan 45° = 1; tan 60° = √3; tan 90° is undefined. For complementary angles (θ and 90°−θ), tan θ = cot(90°−θ). This yields a handy shortcut: if the angles of elevation from two points on the same line to the top of a tower are complementary, the height of the tower equals the distance between the two points.

Typical problem patterns: (1) Single observation — given angle and distance, find height (or vice versa). (2) Two observations from the same point — e.g., angles to the top and bottom of a flagstaff on a building; the difference of tangents gives the flagstaff height. (3) Two observations from different points on the same line — moving towards or away from the object. Set up two equations: tan θ₁ = h/d₁ and tan θ₂ = h/d₂, with d₁−d₂ = known movement. Eliminate the distance to solve for h. (4) Shadow problems — the length of a shadow changes with the sun’s altitude; the same tangent relation applies.

When the observer’s eye level is not at ground level, adjust the height: the vertical side of the right triangle becomes (object height − eye level) for elevation, or (eye level − object height) for depression. In problems involving a tower and a building, draw separate right triangles sharing the horizontal distance; the vertical sides are the tower height and the building height.

Examiner traps: using sine or cosine instead of tangent when the horizontal distance is the adjacent side; forgetting to convert angles given in degrees‑minutes to decimal degrees; mixing up angle of elevation and depression when the observer is above the object; assuming the line of sight is horizontal when the problem states “from the top of a cliff”. A clean diagram with labelled angles and sides eliminates most errors.

Technique: Tangent Method for Height‑Distance
From a point on the ground, the angle of elevation of the top of a tower is 30°. On moving 50 m towards the tower, the angle becomes 60°. Find the height of the tower.
Let the height be h and the initial distance be d. From the first position, tan 30° = h/d → d = h/ tan 30° = h√3. From the second position, tan 60° = h/(d−50) → d−50 = h/ tan 60° = h/√3. Substitute d: h√3 − 50 = h/√3 → multiply by √3: 3h − 50√3 = h → 2h = 50√3 → h = 25√3 m ≈ 43.3 m.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the standard values of tan for 30°, 45°, 60° (1/√3, 1, √3). Draw a quick right‑triangle diagram; label known angles and sides. Use the tangent ratio directly to avoid solving simultaneous equations when only one movement is given. If two movements are given, set up two equations and eliminate the distance variable as shown.

Clock Problems: Relative Speed of Hands, Coincidence, and Mirror Image Time

Clock problems are essentially relative‑speed problems on a circular dial of 360°. The minute hand moves 360° in 60 minutes → 6° per minute. The hour hand moves 360° in 12 hours (720 minutes) → 0.5° per minute. The relative speed of the minute hand with respect to the hour hand is 6° − 0.5° = 5.5° per minute. This single fact solves most clock questions.

Angle between hands at H hours M minutes: the hour hand is at 30H + 0.5M degrees from 12 o’clock; the minute hand is at 6M degrees. The angle = |30H − 5.5M|. If the result exceeds 180°, subtract from 360° to get the smaller angle. Coincidence (overlap) occurs when the angle is 0° → 30H = 5.5M → M = (60H)/11 minutes past H. Thus the hands overlap every 65 5⁄11 minutes (≈ 1 hour 5 5⁄11 minutes). In 12 hours they overlap 11 times; in 24 hours, 22 times.

Right angle (90°) occurs when |30H − 5.5M| = 90 or 270. This yields two times per hour (except between 2‑3 and 8‑9 where only one occurs). Straight line (180°) occurs when |30H − 5.5M| = 180 → M = (30H ± 180)/5.5.

Mirror image time: the mirror image of a clock showing H:M (12‑hour format) is (11:60) − H:M, i.e., subtract the given time from 12:00. For example, mirror of 3:40 is 8:20. If the given time is exactly on the hour (e.g., 6:00), the mirror is 6:00 as well. For a 24‑hour clock, subtract from 24:00.

Gain/loss of a clock: if a clock gains x minutes per day, its speed is (1440+x)/1440 times the correct speed. The time shown after t correct minutes = t × (1440+x)/1440. Conversely, to find correct time when the faulty clock shows a certain time, divide by that factor.

Shortcut table for quick reference: (1) Overlap after H o’clock = 60H/11 minutes past H. (2) Right angle after H o’clock = (60H ± 180)/11 minutes past H. (3) Straight line after H o’clock = (60H ± 360)/11 minutes past H. (4) Angle θ at H:M = |30H − 5.5M|; if >180, use 360−angle.

Calendar Calculations: Leap Year Rules, Odd Days, and Day‑of‑Week Determination

Calendar problems rely on the concept of odd days — the number of days more than complete weeks in a given period. A normal year has 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 odd day. A leap year has 366 days = 52 weeks + 2 odd days. The leap year rule: a year divisible by 4 is a leap year, except century years (divisible by 100) which are not leap years unless they are also divisible by 400. Thus 1900 is not a leap year, 2000 is.

Odd days in centuries: 100 years = 76 normal + 24 leap = 76×1 + 24×2 = 124 odd days = 124 mod 7 = 5 odd days. 200 years = 2×5 = 10 ≡ 3 odd days. 300 years = 3×5 = 15 ≡ 1 odd day. 400 years = 4×5 + 1 (because the 400th year is a leap) = 21 ≡ 0 odd days. This 400‑year cycle repeats the calendar exactly.

To find the day of the week for a given date (day, month, year): (1) Split the year into complete centuries, remaining years, months, and days. (2) Compute odd days for each component. (3) Sum all odd days modulo 7. (4) Map 0→Sunday, 1→Monday, …, 6→Saturday (or use a known reference date, e.g., 1 Jan 1900 was a Monday). For months, use the cumulative odd days up to the previous month (January 0, February 3, March 3, April 6, May 1, June 4, July 6, August 2, September 5, October 0, November 3, December 5) — adjust for leap year after February.

Zeller’s Congruence provides a direct formula for the Gregorian calendar: h = (q + ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + ⌊J/4⌋ + 5J) mod 7, where q = day, m = month (3=March,…,14=February), K = year of century (year mod 100), J = zero‑based century (year/100). h = 0→Saturday, 1→Sunday, …, 6→Friday. This formula works for any date after 1582.

Common examiner twists: (1) Asking for the day of the week on a date in a year before 1900, requiring the 400‑year cycle adjustment. (2) Giving two dates and asking for the number of days between them — compute odd days for each and subtract, or use the fact that the difference in days modulo 7 equals the difference in weekdays. (3) Problems involving “the first Monday of May” — find the weekday of 1 May, then add the required offset.

Simple Interest and Compound Interest: Comparative Mechanics, Effective Rate, and Shortcut Techniques

Interest calculations form the backbone of many financial‑mathematics questions. Simple Interest (SI) is calculated only on the original principal: SI = P × R × T / 100, where P = principal, R = annual rate (percent), T = time in years. The amount A = P + SI = P(1 + RT/100). Compound Interest (CI) adds interest to the principal at regular intervals: A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ for annual compounding, where n = number of years. For compounding k times a year at nominal rate R, the periodic rate is R/k and the number of periods is nk: A = P(1 + R/(100k))ⁿᵏ.

The effective annual rate (EAR) for a nominal rate R compounded k times a year is (1 + R/(100k))ᵏ − 1, expressed as a percentage. This allows comparison of different compounding frequencies. For continuous compounding, A = Pe^(RT/100) where e ≈ 2.71828.

Key shortcuts: (1) Difference between CI and SI for 2 years = P(R/100)². For 3 years, difference = P(R/100)²(3 + R/100). (2) If a sum becomes x times in n years at SI, the rate = 100(x−1)/n. At CI, rate = 100(x^(1/n) − 1). (3) For fractional years at CI, use A = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ × (1 + (fraction)×R/100) for the fractional part (simple interest on the amount after full years). (4) Installment problems: the present value of an annuity of n equal payments of X at rate R is X × [1 − (1+R/100)⁻ⁿ] / (R/100). Equate this to the loan amount to find X.

When rates vary year by year, compute the amount iteratively: A₁ = P(1+R₁/100), A₂ = A₁(1+R₂/100), etc. For a loan repaid in equal annual instalments with constant rate, the instalment X satisfies P = X/(1+r) + X/(1+r)² + … + X/(1+r)ⁿ, where r = R/100. This is a geometric series; solving gives X = P r (1+r)ⁿ / [(1+r)ⁿ − 1].

Examiner traps: (1) Using the simple‑interest formula for the fractional part of a compound‑interest period when the problem states “compounded annually” — the correct method is to compound for full years and then apply simple interest for the fraction. (2) Confusing nominal rate with effective rate when compounding is semi‑annual or quarterly. (3) Forgetting that the first instalment in an annuity due is paid at the beginning of the period, changing the present‑value factor by (1+r). (4) Assuming the difference between CI and SI for 2 years is P(R/100)² even when the rate is different for the two years — the formula holds only for constant rate.

  • Confusing total surface area with curved surface area in solids, leading to extra base areas in the answer.
  • Using sine or cosine instead of tangent when the horizontal distance is the adjacent side in height‑distance problems.
  • Forgetting that the clock angle formula gives the larger angle; always take the minimum of the angle and 360°−angle.
  • Misapplying the leap‑year rule for century years (e.g., treating 1900 as a leap year).
  • Assuming simple interest and compound interest are equal for the first year when the compounding frequency is more than annual.
  • Ignoring that the mirror‑image time subtracts from 12:00, not 11:60, causing off‑by‑one‑minute errors.
  • Overlooking that the frustum volume formula requires both radii; using only the larger radius gives the volume of the full cone.
