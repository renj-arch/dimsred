1. Number System

1.1 Divisibility Rules and Their Applications

The foundation of rapid calculation in the number system rests on divisibility rules, which allow a candidate to decide whether a large integer can be divided by a small integer without performing long division. For instance, a number is divisible by 2 when its unit digit is even, by 3 when the sum of its digits is a multiple of 3, by 4 when the last two digits form a number divisible by 4, by 5 when the unit digit is 0 or 5, by 6 when it satisfies both the rules for 2 and 3, by 8 when the last three digits form a number divisible by 8, by 9 when the digit sum is a multiple of 9, by 10 when the unit digit is 0, and by 11 when the difference between the sum of digits in odd positions and the sum of digits in even positions is either 0 or a multiple of 11. Mastery of these rules enables quick elimination of answer choices in multiple‑choice questions and supports the simplification of fractions before further arithmetic.

1.2 Least Common Multiple and Highest Common Factor

The concepts of least common multiple (LCM) and highest common factor (HCF) are intertwined through the relationship LCM × HCF = product of the two numbers, a property that holds for any pair of positive integers. LCM is the smallest positive integer that is a multiple of each given number, while HCF is the largest positive integer that divides each of them without remainder. In problems involving periodic events — such as bells ringing at different intervals or runners meeting at a starting point — LCM determines the first simultaneous occurrence, whereas HCF is used when dividing quantities into the largest possible equal groups. The prime factorisation method, where each number is expressed as a product of primes raised to appropriate powers, provides a systematic way to compute both LCM (by taking the highest power of each prime) and HCF (by taking the lowest power of each common prime).

1.3 Remainder Theorem and Modular Arithmetic

When a polynomial f(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder is f(a); this remainder theorem extends naturally to integers under modular arithmetic, where the remainder of a division by n is the residue class modulo n. In competitive examinations, questions often ask for the remainder of large powers such as 7100 divided by 5, which can be solved by observing the cyclicity of remainders: 7 ≡ 2 (mod 5), 21 ≡ 2, 22 ≡ 4, 23 ≡ 3, 24 ≡ 1, and the cycle repeats every four powers, so 7100 ≡ 2100 ≡ (24)25 ≡ 125 ≡ 1 (mod 5). Understanding cyclicity of unit digits and remainders reduces massive exponentiation to a few simple steps, a technique that is indispensable for time‑bound tests.

1.4 Surds, Indices and Unit Digit Cyclicity

Surds are irrational roots such as √2 or ∛5 that cannot be simplified to a rational number; they obey the laws of indices, for example √a × √b = √(ab) and (√a)2 = a. Indices (or exponents) follow rules like a^m × a^n = a^(m+n), a^m ÷ a^n = a^(m−n), and (a^m)^n = a^(mn). The unit digit of powers of any integer repeats in a cycle of at most four: for 2 the cycle is 2, 4, 8, 6; for 3 it is 3, 9, 7, 1; for 7 it is 7, 9, 3, 1; for 8 it is 8, 4, 2, 6. Recognising these cycles allows a candidate to answer questions such as “find the unit digit of 753” by dividing the exponent by 4, taking the remainder (1), and selecting the first element of the cycle (7). This skill, combined with the ability to rationalise denominators containing surds, completes the toolkit for number‑system questions.

2. Percentages

2.1 Fraction Percentage Equivalents and Mental Conversion

Percentages are fractions with denominator 100, and the ability to convert between common fractions and their percentage equivalents is a hallmark of speed in quantitative aptitude. The most frequently used equivalents are 1/2 = 50%, 1/3 ≈ 33.33%, 1/4 = 25%, 1/5 = 20%, 1/6 ≈ 16.67%, 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/9 ≈ 11.11%, 1/10 = 10%, 1/12 ≈ 8.33%, and 1/16 = 6.25%. When a problem states “a number is increased by 12.5%”, recognising 12.5% as 1/8 allows the candidate to multiply the original number by 9/8 instead of performing decimal multiplication. Similarly, a decrease of 16.67% corresponds to multiplying by 5/6. This mental conversion reduces calculation time and minimises errors caused by decimal approximations.

2.2 Successive Percentage Change and Net Effect

When a quantity undergoes two successive percentage changes, the net percentage change is not the arithmetic sum of the two percentages but is given by the formula x + y + (xy/100), where x and y are the individual percentage changes (positive for increase, negative for decrease). This formula arises because the second change is applied to the already altered base. For example, a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease yields a net change of 20 + (−20) + (20×−20)/100 = −4%, i.e., a 4% decrease overall. The same principle extends to three or more successive changes by applying the formula iteratively. Understanding this prevents the common mistake of assuming that equal percentage increase and decrease cancel each other.

2.3 Percentage Increase Decrease and Base Shifting

The percentage change between an initial value and a final value is calculated as (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. A frequent source of error is using the wrong base: when a price is reduced by 20% and then increased by 20%, the base for the increase is the reduced price, not the original price, leading to a net loss. In problems involving “A is 25% more than B”, the relationship can be expressed as A = B × (1 + 25/100) = 1.25B, which implies B = A ÷ 1.25 = 0.8A, so B is 20% less than A. Recognising that the base shifts with each operation is essential for accurate interpretation of statements like “20% less than” versus “20% of”.

2.4 Applications in Profit Loss and Interest

Percentage concepts form the backbone of profit‑loss and simple‑compound interest calculations. Profit percentage is (profit ÷ cost price) × 100, while loss percentage is (loss ÷ cost price) × 100. Marked price, discount, and selling price are linked by selling price = marked price × (1 − discount%/100). In simple interest, the interest amount = principal × rate × time ÷ 100, and the amount = principal + interest. Compound interest uses the formula amount = principal × (1 + rate/100)^time, where the interest earned each period is added to the principal for the next period. The effective annual rate when compounding occurs more frequently than annually is (1 + r/n)^n − 1, with r as the nominal rate and n as the number of compounding periods per year. These formulas are direct applications of percentage growth and decay.

Example: Successive Percentage Change
A shopkeeper marks up the price of an article by 30% and then offers a discount of 20% on the marked price. What is the net profit or loss percentage?
Solution: Let the cost price be 100. After a 30% markup, marked price = 130. A 20% discount on 130 gives selling price = 130 × (1 − 20/100) = 130 × 0.8 = 104. Profit = 104 − 100 = 4. Profit percentage = (4 ÷ 100) × 100 = 4%. Using the successive change formula: net % = 30 + (−20) + (30×−20)/100 = 10 − 6 = 4% profit.
Exam‑Day Insight
In percentage questions, always identify the base explicitly before applying any formula. A quick check: if the problem says “A is 20% more than B”, write A = 1.2B; if it says “A is 20% less than B”, write A = 0.8B. This habit eliminates base‑confusion errors that are the most common cause of lost marks in the quant section.

3. Profit and Loss

3.1 Cost Price Selling Price and Marked Price Relationships

The three key prices in any transaction are cost price (CP), the amount paid to acquire the article; selling price (SP), the amount received from the buyer; and marked price (MP), the price displayed before any discount. The fundamental relationships are SP = CP + profit = CP − loss, and SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100). When a trader marks up the cost price by a certain percentage, the marked price becomes CP × (1 + markup%/100). These equations form a closed system: knowing any two of CP, SP, MP, profit%, loss%, discount%, or markup% allows the determination of the remaining variables. In examination questions, the data is often given in a mixed format — for example, “a 20% discount on the marked price yields a 10% profit” — requiring the candidate to set up simultaneous equations and solve for the unknown ratio of CP to MP.

3.2 Profit Percentage Loss Percentage and Discount Calculations

Profit percentage is always calculated on cost price unless explicitly stated otherwise, while discount percentage is calculated on marked price. A common trap is to compute profit on selling price, which yields a different figure. For instance, if CP = 80 and SP = 100, profit = 20, profit% on CP = 25%, but profit% on SP = 20%. The phrase “gain of 20% on selling price” translates to profit = 0.2 × SP, so CP = SP − 0.2SP = 0.8SP, implying a profit of 25% on CP. Discount calculations follow a similar pattern: a discount of 15% on MP means SP = 0.85MP. When successive discounts are offered, the equivalent single discount is not the sum but is given by d1 + d2 − (d1×d2)/100, derived from the same successive‑change principle used in percentages.

3.3 Successive Discounts and Equivalent Single Discount

When two discounts d1% and d2% are applied successively, the final selling price is MP × (1 − d1/100) × (1 − d2/100). The equivalent single discount D satisfies (1 − D/100) = (1 − d1/100)(1 − d2/100), which simplifies to D = d1 + d2 − (d1×d2)/100. For example, successive discounts of 20% and 10% give an equivalent discount of 20 + 10 − (200/100) = 28%. This formula is symmetric and extends to three or more discounts by repeated application. In problems where a trader wants to offer a single discount equivalent to a series of discounts, this calculation directly provides the answer without needing to assume a marked price.

3.4 Break Even Analysis and Margin Concepts

Break‑even occurs when total revenue equals total cost, i.e., SP × quantity = (fixed cost + variable cost per unit × quantity). The contribution margin per unit is SP − variable cost per unit, and the break‑even quantity = fixed cost ÷ contribution margin. Margin of safety measures how much sales can drop before losses occur, expressed as (actual sales − break‑even sales) ÷ actual sales. Although these concepts are more common in business studies, they appear in CSAT as word problems where a manufacturer’s fixed and variable costs are given, and the candidate must find the minimum number of units to sell to avoid loss. The underlying arithmetic is simple linear equations, but the phrasing tests the ability to translate a real‑world scenario into algebraic form.

4. Time and Work

4.1 Work Rate Concept and Unitary Method

The central idea in time‑work problems is that work done is the product of rate and time. If a person can complete a job in n days, the person’s one‑day work (rate) is 1/n of the total job. This unitary approach converts the problem into addition of fractions: when multiple agents work together, their rates add up. For example, if A finishes a task in 12 days and B in 18 days, A’s rate = 1/12, B’s rate = 1/18, combined rate = 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 per day, so together they finish in 36/5 = 7.2 days. The LCM method — taking the LCM of individual times as the total work unit — avoids fractions: LCM(12,18)=36 units of work; A does 3 units/day, B does 2 units/day, together 5 units/day, time = 36/5 days. Both methods are equivalent; the LCM method is often faster for mental calculation.

4.2 Combined Work and Efficiency Ratios

Efficiency is inversely proportional to the time taken for the same work. If A is twice as efficient as B, A takes half the time B takes. In problems where efficiencies are given in ratios, such as A:B:C = 3:2:1, the work done in a day by each is in the same ratio. The total work can be assumed as the sum of the ratio terms multiplied by a convenient factor, or the LCM of the times derived from the ratios. When workers join or leave midway, the work done in each phase is calculated separately using the rates applicable during that phase, and the sum of work done across phases equals the whole job. This segmentation technique is essential for problems where “A works for 3 days, then B joins, and they finish together”.

4.3 Pipes and Cisterns as Work Problems

Pipes filling or emptying a cistern are direct analogues of workers doing a job. An inlet pipe has a positive rate (fills the tank), an outlet pipe has a negative rate (empties the tank). If a pipe fills a tank in 6 hours, its rate = 1/6 tank per hour; if another empties it in 9 hours, its rate = −1/9 tank per hour. When both operate simultaneously, the net rate = 1/6 − 1/9 = 1/18 tank per hour, so the tank fills in 18 hours. Problems may involve multiple pipes opening at different times, or a leak that starts after the tank is partially filled. The same LCM technique applies: take LCM of all individual times as the tank capacity in units, compute each pipe’s units per hour, and sum them with appropriate signs.

4.4 Work and Wages Distribution

When a group of workers completes a job and the total wages are to be divided in proportion to the work done by each, the share of a worker = (worker’s rate ÷ sum of all rates) × total wages. If A, B, C work together and finish a job for ₹1800, with individual times 6, 8, 12 days respectively, their rates are 1/6, 1/8, 1/12. The ratio of rates = 4:3:2 (multiplying by LCM 24). Total parts = 9, so A gets 4/9 × 1800 = ₹800, B gets ₹600, C gets ₹400. If workers work for different durations, the work done by each = rate × days worked, and wages are divided in the ratio of these work amounts. This principle extends to situations where a worker is paid a fixed daily wage plus a bonus for early completion.

5. Speed Distance and Time

5.1 Basic Relationship and Unit Conversion

The fundamental relationship is speed = distance ÷ time, distance = speed × time, and time = distance ÷ speed. Consistency of units is critical: speed may be given in km/h while time is in minutes, or distance in metres while speed is in km/h. The conversion factors are 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s and 1 m/s = 18/5 km/h. For example, a speed of 90 km/h equals 90 × 5/18 = 25 m/s. In problems involving a journey with multiple segments, each segment’s distance, speed, and time must be expressed in compatible units before any addition or averaging. A common error is to average speeds by arithmetic mean when the distances are equal; the correct average speed for equal distances is the harmonic mean 2ab/(a+b), while for equal times it is the arithmetic mean (a+b)/2.

5.2 Relative Speed in Same and Opposite Directions

When two objects move in opposite directions, their relative speed is the sum of their individual speeds because the distance between them closes at the combined rate. When they move in the same direction, the relative speed is the absolute difference of their speeds, as the faster object gains on the slower one at that rate. This concept is used in problems of trains crossing each other, a train overtaking a man, or two persons running on a circular track. For instance, a train 200 m long running at 72 km/h crosses a man running at 6 km/h in the same direction. Convert speeds to m/s: train = 20 m/s, man = 5/3 m/s. Relative speed = 20 − 5/3 = 55/3 m/s. Time to cross = length of train ÷ relative speed = 200 ÷ (55/3) = 600/55 ≈ 10.9 seconds.

5.3 Average Speed for Equal Distances and Equal Times

Average speed for a whole journey is total distance ÷ total time. If a person travels a distance d at speed a and returns the same distance at speed b, total distance = 2d, total time = d/a + d/b = d(a+b)/(ab). Hence average speed = 2d ÷ [d(a+b)/(ab)] = 2ab/(a+b). This is the harmonic mean of the two speeds. If instead the person travels for equal times t at speeds a and b, total distance = at + bt = t(a+b), total time = 2t, average speed = (a+b)/2, the arithmetic mean. Recognising which condition applies — equal distances or equal times — is the key to selecting the correct formula. Many examination questions disguise the condition by stating “a man goes to his office at 30 km/h and returns at 50 km/h”, which implies equal distances.

5.4 Trains Boats and Streams Applications

Train problems often involve the length of the train and the length of a platform or bridge. The distance covered while crossing a stationary object equals the length of the train; while crossing a platform, it equals train length + platform length. For a train crossing another train, the distance is the sum of both lengths, and relative speed depends on direction. Boats and streams introduce the concept of still‑water speed (boat speed in still water) and stream speed. Downstream speed = boat speed + stream speed; upstream speed = boat speed − stream speed. If a boat takes 4 hours to go 40 km downstream and 5 hours to return, downstream speed = 10 km/h, upstream speed = 8 km/h. Solving the two equations gives boat speed = 9 km/h, stream speed = 1 km/h. These problems are solved by setting up two linear equations in the two unknowns.
