1. What is Decision Making?

Decision Making in UPSC CSAT tests your ability to analyse situations, evaluate options, and make reasoned judgments — especially in administrative and ethical contexts. This section presents situational problems where you must choose the most appropriate course of action, considering ethical principles, practicality, legal implications, and administrative procedures.

1. Ethical Decision Making Framework: Identify the problem clearly → Identify stakeholders affected → List possible actions → Evaluate each option against ethical principles (honesty, integrity, fairness, empathy, justice) → Consider legal and administrative constraints → Choose the option that maximises public good while minimising harm.

2. Types of Situations: Ethical dilemmas — conflict between two moral values (e.g., honesty vs. loyalty). Administrative problems — resource allocation, staff management, public service delivery. Policy conflicts — balancing individual rights vs. collective good. Crisis situations — natural disasters, accidents, law and order problems.

3. Principles to Apply: Public interest above personal interest. Rule of law — decisions must be legal and constitutional. Transparency and accountability. Proportionality — response should match the gravity of the situation. Non-discrimination and fairness. Administrative efficiency and practicality.

4. Common Traps: Overly idealistic options that ignore practical constraints. Self-serving options that prioritise personal benefit. Options that violate rules or procedures. Options that kick the can down the road without solving the problem. Extreme options that are disproportionate to the situation.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Ethical Dilemma)
You are a government officer. Your close friend's son has applied for a government job and is equally qualified as other candidates. Your friend asks you to use your influence to ensure his son gets selected. What will you do?

a) Use your influence to help your friend's son since he is equally qualified b) Refuse and explain that the selection must be fair and transparent c) Ask your friend to approach someone else in the department d) Give the job to your friend's son but make it look like a fair selection
Solution: b) Refuse and explain that the selection must be fair and transparent. Option a violates the principle of merit and fairness. Option c avoids responsibility and is unethical. Option d is dishonest and illegal. Option b upholds integrity, transparency, and follows proper procedures — it is the most ethical and legally correct choice.
Example 2 (Administrative Problem)
You are a district magistrate. A village has been flooded and people are stranded. The nearest rescue team will take 6 hours to arrive. You have a small team of local volunteers available immediately. What should you do?

a) Wait for the rescue team to arrive as they are more trained b) Send the volunteers immediately while coordinating with the rescue team c) Ask the villagers to manage on their own until help arrives d) Call multiple nearby districts for help and wait
Solution: b) Send the volunteers immediately while coordinating with the rescue team. Option a wastes critical time — lives may be lost in 6 hours. Option c is negligent and irresponsible. Option d causes unnecessary delay. Option b is the best — use available resources immediately while also arranging professional help. This balances urgency with effectiveness.
Example 3 (Policy Decision)
A government policy aims to build a dam that will provide irrigation and electricity to thousands but will displace a small tribal community. As a policy advisor, what is your recommendation?

a) Oppose the dam entirely since it displaces people b) Support the dam since it benefits a larger number of people c) Recommend the dam with comprehensive rehabilitation and compensation for the displaced community d) Suggest an alternative location that affects fewer people even if it costs more
Solution: c) Recommend the dam with comprehensive rehabilitation and compensation. Option a ignores the benefits to thousands. Option b ignores the rights of the displaced. Option d may be impractical if geography dictates the location. Option c balances development needs with social justice — the dam proceeds but the displaced community receives fair rehabilitation, compensation, and resettlement.

2. Shortcut Techniques

  • Always eliminate options that are clearly unethical or illegal first — this narrows down choices quickly.
  • Prefer options that balance multiple considerations rather than extreme positions. The 'middle path' is often correct in CSAT decision-making.
  • Look for options that demonstrate proactive problem-solving — doing something productive rather than waiting or passing responsibility.
  • Consider consequences for all stakeholders — the best decision usually maximises benefit for the most people while minimising harm.
  • Options that involve 'asking someone else' or 'waiting' are usually wrong — CSAT values initiative and decisiveness in administrators.

5. Administrative Discretion: The Doctrine of Wednesbury and its Evolution

In administrative law, executive action involves a degree of discretion. However, this discretion is not absolute; it must be exercised reasonably, fairly, and within statutory bounds. The legal framework evolved through landmark judgments defining "unreasonable" or "arbitrary" conduct. The foundational case is Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v Wednesbury Corporation (1948). Here, the court established a test for judicial review: an administrative body acts unlawfully if its decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could ever have come to it. This became known as the Wednesbury Principle.

The doctrine rests on three grounds for illegality: (1) acting outside conferred powers (ultra vires); (2) taking into account irrelevant considerations; and (3) acting in bad faith or perversely. The "Wednesbury unreasonableness" standard is high. Courts generally do not interfere unless a decision is absurd, irrational, or manifestly unjust. For instance, closing a public park based on an irrelevant consideration (e.g., aesthetic preference) would be illegal. Conversely, closing it due to budget constraints (a relevant consideration) is lawful even if the financial analysis is disputed, unless the decision was so irrational that no sensible person could have made it.

This doctrine protects administrative efficiency by preventing courts from substituting their judgment for that of experts or elected bodies. Yet, it does not grant immunity from scrutiny. The evolution of this principle is evident in cases like R v North and East Devon Health Authority, ex parte Coughlan (2001), where the court examined whether an administrative body acted compatibly with legitimate expectations created by its own representations. This shift acknowledges that discretion must be exercised with fairness and adherence to promises made to citizens.

Examiner's trap
A common error is assuming any decision under statutory powers is automatically legal. Incorrect answer: "The authority had the power, so the decision stands." Correct analysis: Even within granted powers, a decision must be reasonable (Wednesbury), relevant, and in good faith. If the authority ignored a mandatory consideration or acted perversely, the decision is voidable.
Exam-Day Strategy
When facing a scenario involving administrative discretion, first identify if the action was "ultra vires" (beyond power). If within power, apply the Wednesbury test: Is the decision so absurd that no reasonable person would make it? If yes, mark the option as illegal. Do not confuse "disagreement with policy" with "unreasonable exercise of power."

6. The Basic Structure Doctrine and Constitutional Limitations

While administrative discretion operates under statutes, the Constitution imposes supreme limits on both Parliament and the Executive. The most profound limitation is the Basic Structure Doctrine. This principle asserts that while Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter its "basic structure." This doctrine was crystallized in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973). The Supreme Court held that amendments violating the basic structure are void. Though the judgment lists various features—such as secularism, democracy, federalism, and judicial review—as potentially part of the basic structure, it left the exact list open-ended.

The significance of this doctrine lies in its role as a check on majoritarian excess. It ensures that the Constitution remains a living document but not one that can be rewritten to destroy its own identity. For example, if Parliament attempted to abolish the judiciary or convert India into a theocracy, such amendments would be struck down. The concept was further reinforced in Indra Sawhney v Union of India (1992), where the Court ruled that the 50% cap on total reservations is a judicial guideline, not a basic structure feature because it ensured social balance and prevented dominance by a single group.

In decision-making questions, this doctrine implies that no administrative or legislative option can override fundamental constitutional values. If an option suggests suspending free speech in a way that destroys the essence of democracy, it violates the basic structure. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the power to amend is not unlimited; it is constrained by the very nature of the Constitution.

7. Legitimate Expectation and Procedural Fairness

A critical component of administrative justice is legitimate expectation. This arises when a public authority makes a promise, follows a consistent practice, or issues a representation that leads a citizen to expect a certain course of action. If the authority reverses this without valid reasons or due process, it violates fairness. In Coughlan (2001), the Court held that a legitimate expectation could be protected even if not strictly a legal right, provided the promise was clear and unambiguous.

This concept is vital in scenarios involving policy shifts. For instance, cancelling an announced subsidy scheme without notice or hearing violates legitimate expectation. Similarly, in Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978), the Court expanded procedural fairness beyond mere "natural justice" to include the right to be heard before deprivation of life or liberty. This means administrative decisions affecting rights must follow a transparent process: notice, opportunity to represent, and reasoned orders.

8. Proportionality Analysis in Modern Administrative Law

The doctrine of proportionality has emerged as a robust tool for evaluating administrative actions, particularly after the incorporation of European Union law principles into Indian jurisprudence. Proportionality requires that any restriction on a fundamental right must be (1) suitable to achieve a legitimate aim, (2) necessary (no less restrictive alternative exists), and (3) balanced in its effects (the harm to the individual is not excessive compared to the public benefit). This three-step test was adopted by the Supreme Court in cases like Manohar Lal v Union of India (2017) regarding restrictions on liquor sales.

In decision-making questions, proportionality helps distinguish between a reasonable restriction and an arbitrary one. For example, banning all vehicles to reduce pollution might be suitable, but if it disproportionately affects public transport workers without providing alternatives, it fails the necessity and balance tests. This framework ensures that administrative power is not used as a blunt instrument but with precision and care for individual rights.

9. Non-Justiciability and Political Questions

Not all disputes are suitable for judicial resolution. The doctrine of non-justiciability or "political questions" limits the scope of courts to certain types of issues. Matters involving foreign policy, war and peace, or the conduct of elections are often considered non-justiciable because they require political judgment rather than legal interpretation. In Rajendra Prasad v Union of India, the Court declined to interfere with the President's decision on the appointment of a Governor, citing its discretionary nature.

However, this does not mean courts cannot review these areas entirely. If an executive action in foreign policy violates a statute or constitutional provision, it remains subject to review. The distinction lies between "policy" (which courts generally avoid) and "law" (which they enforce). In decision-making scenarios, options suggesting judicial intervention in purely political matters without legal basis are incorrect.

  • Misinterpreting Wednesbury: Assuming any unreasonable decision is illegal. In reality, only decisions so absurd no reasonable authority could make them are voidable.
  • Overlooking Legitimate Expectation: Ignoring that administrative promises or consistent practices create enforceable expectations even without explicit statutory backing.
  • Confusing Basic Structure with Ordinary Amendments: Believing Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution. The basic structure (democracy, secularism, etc.) is inviolable.

Ethical Decision‑Making Frameworks: Overview and Classification

Major Schools Relevant to CSAT Decision Questions

CSAT decision‑making items test the ability to choose the most appropriate administrative action when competing values clash. The three classical schools—utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics—form the analytical backbone. In Indian administrative law they are over‑laid with constitutional mandates (Articles 14, 19, 21) and the judicial doctrines of proportionality, legitimate expectation, and Wednesbury unreasonableness.

FrameworkCore QuestionTypical CSAT TriggerKey Constitutional Anchor
Utilitarian / ConsequentialistWhich option maximises overall welfare?“Greatest good for greatest number” phrasing; cost‑benefit language.Article 21 (right to life & personal liberty interpreted as quality of life).
Deontological / Rule‑BasedDoes the action respect duties, rights, and procedural rules?References to “legal duty”, “mandatory procedure”, “non‑derogable rights”.Article 14 (equality before law), Article 19 (freedoms), statutory mandates.
Virtue Ethics / Integrity‑BasedWhat would a prudent, honest administrator do?“Public trust”, “integrity”, “accountability” cues.Directive Principles (Art. 38, 39), Second ARC recommendations on ethics.

Exam Tip: Identify the “trigger word” in the stem. If the question asks “most appropriate” without a quantitative cue, lean toward deontological or proportionality analysis. If it asks “maximise benefit” or “least cost”, a utilitarian lens is expected.

Mapping Frameworks to Administrative Law Doctrines

  • Wednesbury Unreasonableness – a deontological check: decision must not be so irrational that no reasonable authority could have taken it.
  • Legitimate Expectation – procedural fairness rooted in deontology; protects reliance interests.
  • Proportionality (Four‑Step Test) – blends utilitarian balancing with deontological rights protection.
  • Basic Structure Doctrine – constitutional ceiling; any framework that violates basic features (rule of law, separation of powers) is impermissible.
Worked Example: A district collector must decide whether to acquire tribal land for a highway. The project promises economic uplift for 2 lakh people but displaces 500 families.
  • Utilitarian view: Net welfare gain → acquire.
  • Deontological view: Forest Rights Act, 2006 mandates free, prior, informed consent → cannot acquire without consent.
  • Proportionality: Is there a less restrictive alignment? If yes, choose alternative.
  • Virtue ethics: Collector’s duty to protect vulnerable communities → seek consensus.

Correct CSAT answer usually reflects the constitutional‑statutory hierarchy: statutory consent requirement (deontological) outweighs pure welfare calculus.

Utilitarian Calculus in CSAT Scenarios

Quantitative and Qualitative Benefit‑Cost Assessment

Utilitarian reasoning in CSAT is rarely a full‑blown cost‑benefit spreadsheet; it appears as comparative language—“greater public interest”, “maximum coverage”, “least expenditure”. The examiner expects the candidate to recognise when a utilitarian argument is legally permissible and when it is overridden by rights‑based provisions.

Step‑by‑Step Utilitarian Checklist

  1. Identify all affected stakeholder groups.
  2. Estimate magnitude of benefit/harm for each group (qualitative ranking: high/medium/low).
  3. Aggregate: does the option produce the highest net positive rank?
  4. Cross‑check against non‑derogable rights (Art. 21, Art. 14, statutory safeguards).
  5. If a rights violation is present, the utilitarian option is disqualified unless the law expressly permits a proportional limitation.
Scenario TypeTypical Utilitarian CueCommon Override
Resource allocation (health, education)“Maximum coverage”, “cost‑effective”Right to health (Art. 21), SC/ST protective statutes
Infrastructure vs. environment“Economic growth”, “employment generation”Forest Conservation Act, 1980; NGT orders
Disaster relief prioritisation“Save maximum lives”Non‑discrimination (Art. 15), vulnerable‑group guidelines
Worked Example: During a flood, a relief commissioner has 1000 food packets for three villages: A (population 2000, 30 % affected), B (1500, 50 % affected), C (1000, 80 % affected).
  • Utilitarian allocation: prioritize C (highest affected proportion) → 800 packets, remaining 200 to B.
  • Deontological check: National Disaster Management Guidelines mandate “equitable distribution based on vulnerability”. The utilitarian outcome aligns, so it is acceptable.
Examiner Trap: A question may present a utilitarian “greater good” option that violates a statutory quota (e.g., 30 % reservation for women in panchayat funds). The correct answer respects the quota even if the utilitarian calculus suggests otherwise.

Deontological Rules and Constitutional Mandates

Duty‑Based Reasoning in Administrative Choices

Deontology in CSAT centres on compliance with constitutional provisions, statutory duties, and procedural fairness. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that administrative discretion cannot be exercised arbitrarily (Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, 1978) and that procedural requirements are not mere formalities but substantive rights.

Key Constitutional Articles Frequently Tested

  • Article 14 – Equality before law; classification must be reasonable and non‑arbitrary.
  • Article 19(1)(a)–(g) – Freedoms; any restriction must satisfy the “reasonable restriction” test (Art. 19(2)–(6)).
  • Article 21 – Right to life and personal liberty; expanded to include dignity, livelihood, environment.
  • Article 300A – Property right; deprivation only by authority of law.
  • Article 311 – Safeguards for civil servants; procedural due process in disciplinary action.

Statutory Duty Checklist for CSAT

Forest Rights Act, 2006Gram Sabha consent for diversion (Sec. 4(5))Collector approves mining without Gram Sabha resolution.

Worked Example: A municipal commissioner issues an order to demolish unauthorised constructions without giving a show‑cause notice. The order cites “public safety”.
  • Deontological analysis: Article 21 + Municipal Corporation Act require notice and hearing before demolition (principle of natural justice).
  • Even if safety is urgent, the law provides for emergency powers with post‑facto justification; the commissioner must follow that specific provision.
  • Correct answer: The order is invalid for breach of procedural duty.
Examiner Trap: Questions may present a “good outcome” achieved through an illegal shortcut (e.g., bypassing tender process to expedite a hospital). The deontological answer always favours the lawful procedure unless a statutory emergency clause expressly permits deviation.

Virtue Ethics and Administrative Integrity

Character‑Based Decision Making in Public Service

Virtue ethics asks what a “prudent, honest, and compassionate” administrator would do. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC, 2005‑2009) and the Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964 codify virtues such as integrity, impartiality, and accountability. CSAT questions on “ethical dilemmas” often test whether the candidate recognises the virtue‑ethics dimension beyond rule‑following.

Core Virtues and Their Operational Indicators

VirtueOperational IndicatorCSAT Cue
IntegrityRefusal of undue favours; transparent asset declaration“Officer offered a gift by contractor”
ImpartialityEqual treatment irrespective of political pressure“Minister asks to fast‑track a file”
CompassionProactive outreach to marginalized beneficiaries“Elderly widow denied pension due to clerical error”
CourageWhistleblowing on corruption despite risk“Officer discovers fund diversion”
AccountabilityWillingness to explain decisions publicly“RTI query on selection criteria”

Virtue‑Ethics Decision Algorithm

  1. Identify the virtues at stake.
  2. Ask: “Would a virtuous officer act this way?”
  3. Check if any rule or law mandates a contrary action; if so, virtue yields to law (lex superior).
  4. If multiple virtuous actions possible, choose the one that best upholds public trust.
Worked Example: A sub‑divisional magistrate learns that a local MLA’s relative has been allotted a shop in a market complex without auction. The magistrate can (a) cancel the allotment, (b) report to higher authority, (c) ignore due to political pressure.
  • Virtue analysis: Integrity and impartiality demand cancellation and reporting.
  • Legal check: Public Premises (Eviction) Act, 1971 requires due process; cancellation must follow procedure.
  • Best answer: Initiate lawful cancellation and simultaneously report to the vigilance department.
Examiner Trap: A “compassionate” option that violates a clear rule (e.g., granting a licence without qualification) is a distractor. Virtue ethics never overrides statutory mandate; it guides discretion *within* the legal framework.

Proportionality and the Doctrine of Double Effect

Four‑Step Proportionality Test (Supreme Court Template)

Since K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) and Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016), the Court applies a structured proportionality analysis: (1) legitimate aim, (2) rational connection, (3) necessity (least restrictive means), (4) balancing (proportionality stricto sensu). CSAT questions on “restriction of rights” expect the candidate to run this test mentally.

Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE) in Administrative Action

DDE permits an action with a foreseen harmful side‑effect if: (i) the act itself is good or neutral, (ii) the good effect is intended, (iii) the bad effect is not a means to the good, (iv) there is a proportionate reason. The Supreme Court referenced DDE in State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2020) concerning demolition of illegal structures causing incidental loss of livelihood.

ElementProportionality TestDouble Effect Test
Legitimate AimPublic health, national security, etc.Good effect intended (e.g., clearing fire hazard).
Rational ConnectionMeasure must logically advance aim.Act must directly produce good effect.
Necessity / Least RestrictiveNo less intrusive alternative.Bad effect not used as means.
Balancing / Proportionality Stricto SensuBenefits outweigh harms.Proportionate reason for tolerating bad effect.
Worked Example: During a pandemic, a district magistrate orders compulsory vaccination for all residents of a containment zone. Some residents object on religious grounds.
  • Legitimate aim: control of infectious disease (Art. 21, Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897).
  • Rational connection: vaccination reduces transmission.
  • Necessity: no less restrictive measure achieves herd immunity in time.
  • Balancing: public health benefit outweighs individual objection; exemption only for medical contraindication.
  • DDE check: The order’s primary effect is health protection; the incidental burden on religious practice is not the means to the end.
  • Correct CSAT answer: Order is proportionate and permissible.
Examiner Trap: A question may present a “blanket ban” (e.g., total internet shutdown) where a targeted suspension would suffice. The necessity step fails → answer: disproportionate.

Examiner Traps and Quick‑Reference Decision Matrix

Common Distractor Patterns

  • Outcome‑Only Appeal: “This will benefit 90 % of people” – ignores procedural illegality.
  • Authority‑Only Appeal: “The Minister directed it” – ignores constitutional limits on political interference.
  • Urgency‑Only Appeal: “Immediate action needed” – bypasses statutory emergency provisions.
  • Precedent‑Misquote: Citing a case out of context (e.g., using Maneka Gandhi for a pure policy choice).
  • Virtue‑Without‑Law: “Compassionate officer would waive the rule” – violates statutory mandate.

Rapid‑Fire Practice:

Stem: “A collector orders demolition of 200 shanties to widen a road, citing traffic congestion. No notice given. Residents claim violation of Article 21.”

  1. Identify keyword: “no notice” → procedural duty (deontological).
  2. Eliminate utilitarian “traffic relief” option.
  3. Check proportionality: less restrictive alternative (phased widening) exists → fails necessity.
  4. Correct answer: Order illegal; must follow due process under Municipal Act and Art. 21.
Final Exam‑Day Checklist:
  • Read the stem twice; underline the trigger word.
  • Map trigger to framework using the matrix.
  • Run the relevant constitutional/statutory check.
  • Apply proportionality/DDE only if the framework calls for balancing.
  • Select the option that survives all filters.
