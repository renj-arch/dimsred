Geology Paper II — Palaeontology, Economic Geology, Hydrogeology and Remote Sensing

Palaeontology

  • Fossilisation: petrification, carbonisation, moulds and casts, trace fossils, amber and ice preservation.
  • Microfossils: foraminifera, ostracods, conodonts, spores and pollen; biostratigraphy and palaeoclimate.
  • Index fossils: short stratigraphic range and wide geographic distribution — ammonites, graptolites, trilobites.
  • Indian fossil records: Gondwana flora (Glossopteris), Siwalik mammals, dinosaurs of Lameta beds, marine Cretaceous faunas.

Economic Geology

  • Ore genesis: magmatic segregation, hydrothermal, sedimentary, residual, metamorphic and supergene enrichment.
  • Indian deposits: iron ore (Bailadila, Kudremukh, Noamundi), manganese (Balaghat, Keonjhar), bauxite (Odisha), copper (Khetri, Malanjkhand), gold (Kolar, Hutti), coal and petroleum.
  • Exploration: geological mapping, geophysical methods (gravity, magnetic, seismic, resistivity), geochemical sampling, drilling.

Hydrogeology

  • Aquifers: confined and unconfined; porosity, permeability, specific yield, transmissivity.
  • Darcy’s law: Q = K·A·(dh/dL), where K is hydraulic conductivity and dh/dL is the hydraulic gradient.
  • Wells: water table, drawdown, cone of depression, pumping tests, specific capacity.
  • Groundwater provinces of India: Indo-Gangetic alluvial plains, peninsular crystalline, Deccan trap, coastal and Himalayan provinces.

Remote Sensing and Applied Geology

  • Remote sensing: electromagnetic spectrum, passive and active sensors, platforms (satellite, airborne), resolutions — spatial, spectral, radiometric, temporal.
  • GIS and GPS: layers, raster and vector data, overlay analysis, digital elevation models.
  • Applications: mineral exploration, groundwater targeting, landslide hazard mapping, land-use planning, environmental monitoring.
  • Engineering geology: rock mass classification, slope stability, tunnel and dam site investigation.
Solved Example: Darcy’s Law
Question: An aquifer has hydraulic conductivity 10 m/day, cross-sectional area 100 m² and hydraulic gradient 0.1. Find the discharge.
Answer: Q = K·A·i = 10 × 100 × 0.1 = 100 m³/day.

Common Traps

  • Confusing porosity with permeability.
  • Naming an ore deposit without its genetic class.
  • Mixing spectral with spatial resolution.
  • Using distant foreign examples instead of Indian mineral belts.
  • Forgetting units in Darcy’s law calculations.
Mains strategy
Pair every concept with an Indian example and a sketch: a stratigraphic range chart for fossils, a mineral belt map, a groundwater cross-section, a remote-sensing workflow. For applied questions, structure answers as site investigation → method → interpretation → recommendation.

The Pashupati Seal, The Great Unconformity, And The Kallur Formation: Reading India's Deep Time

In the grand narrative of Indian Geology Paper II, the transition from recognizing fossils to understanding the tectonic and sedimentary history of the subcontinent requires a mastery of specific stratigraphic markers and geological events that define the country's evolution. The syllabus often lists "Stratigraphy" broadly, but for a high-scoring answer, one must delve into the specific unconformities and boundary definitions that separate major eras.

The Great Unconformity at Kallur: One of the most significant geological discoveries in recent Indian history is the identification of the "Great Unconformity" at Kallur, Karnataka. This site represents a critical break in the stratigraphic record between the Peninsular Gneisses (Archaean to Proterozoic basement) and the overlying Vindhyan Supergroup sediments. The discovery here is not merely about dating rocks; it is about understanding the tectonic quiescence that allowed for the deposition of thick sedimentary sequences after a long period of mountain building and erosion. The Kallur Formation, part of the lower Vindhyan sequence, contains unique lithologies including quartzites and sandstones that record paleocurrent directions essential for reconstructing the palaeogeography of Gondwana India during the Cambrian to Ordovician periods.

The Pashupati Seal: A Stratigraphic Milestone: Moving from deep time to the Quaternary, the discovery of the "Pashupati Seal" in the Siwalik Group (specifically within the upper beds) offers a fascinating case study in biostratigraphy and human evolution. Found in the Narmada Valley region and associated Siwalik formations, this seal is not just an artifact but a stratigraphic marker for the late Miocene to Pliocene epochs. Its presence helps correlate sedimentary layers across the Himalayan foothills, linking the geological history of the uplifting mountains with the biological evolution of hominids in South Asia. The stratigraphic context of such finds is crucial; they are often encased in conglomerates and sandstones that record the erosional products of the rising Himalayas being transported southwards. Understanding the provenance of these sediments—linking them to specific source areas in the Greater Himalaya or Lesser Himalaya—is a key skill tested in advanced geology questions.

The Deccan Traps: A Chronological Puzzle: The Deccan Traps represent one of the largest volcanic provinces on Earth, but their exact chronology and relationship to the Cretaceous-Paleogene (K-Pg) extinction event remain a subject of intense debate. While traditionally linked to the end-Cretaceous mass extinction, recent high-precision dating suggests that the main phase of Deccan volcanism continued well into the early Paleocene, overlapping with the recovery of life after the asteroid impact. This temporal overlap is critical for understanding the "cause and effect" in geological history: did the volcanism contribute to the climate change that facilitated the dinosaur extinction, or was it a separate event? The answer lies in the geochemical signatures found within the basalt flows, specifically the iridium anomalies and sulfur spikes. A senior aspirant must be able to distinguish between the "main pulse" of volcanism (late Maastrichtian) and the "post-extinction pulses" (early Danian).

Lithostratigraphic Boundaries in India: Another advanced concept is the definition of lithostratigraphic boundaries. For instance, the boundary between the Vindhyan Supergroup and the overlying Gondwana System is often debated based on paleontological evidence (appearance of specific fish and amphibians) versus lithological changes (transition from quartzitic sandstones to coal-bearing shales). Similarly, the distinction between the "Lower" and "Upper" Gondwana is not just a vertical division but a functional one marking the shift from terrestrial red beds to extensive peat swamps. The "Gondwana Flora" mentioned in basic texts includes Glossopteris, but advanced study requires knowing the specific sub-families like Phyllotheca and their correlation with the Permian-Triassic boundary.

Tectonic Synthesis: Finally, integrating these elements requires understanding the "Suture Zones." The identification of suture zones in the Himalayas (e.g., the Main Central Thrust, Main Boundary Thrust) is essential for mapping the accretionary prism. The geological history of India cannot be written without referencing the closure of the Tethys Ocean and the collision of the Indian Plate with Eurasia. This process created the "Himalayan Orogeny," a complex interplay of subduction, slab break-off, and continental collision that continues to shape the region's seismotectonics today.

Question: Interpret the stratigraphic significance of the Kallur Formation and its relationship with the Peninsular Gneisses. How does this relate to the concept of a Great Unconformity?
The Kallur Formation in Karnataka is renowned for containing a distinct gap in the geological record between the Archaean-Proterozoic basement rocks and the overlying sedimentary sequences of the Vindhyan Supergroup. Discuss the implications of this "Great Unconformity" for understanding the palaeogeography of Gondwana India during the Cambrian period.
Answer:

The Kallur Formation represents a critical stratigraphic horizon in the lower Vindhyan sequence, specifically located in the Dharwar Craton region. The term "Great Unconformity" refers to a significant gap in the geological record where a vast span of time (often hundreds of millions of years) is missing due to erosion and non-deposition.

Stratigraphic Relationship:
The Kallur Formation sits directly atop the Peninsular Gneisses, which are primarily Archaean to early Proterozoic in age. The contact between these two units is an angular unconformity or a disconformity, depending on the local structural regime. This indicates that after the formation of the stable craton (the Peninsular Gneisses), there was a long period of tectonic stability and sub-aerial erosion, followed by a transgressive marine event that deposited the Vindhyan sediments.

Implications for Palaeogeography:
1. Sedimentary Gap: The unconformity implies that the region was likely an emergent landmass or a shallow shelf undergoing erosion before the Cambrian transgression. This aligns with the global trend of the "Cambrian Explosion" where marine conditions returned to continental shelves.

2. Lithological Evidence: The Kallur Formation itself consists of quartzites, sandstones, and shales. These lithologies suggest a high-energy depositional environment typical of shallow marine settings or deltaic plains during the early Palaeozoic.

3. Biostratigraphic Correlation: While the lower Vindhyan is often considered Cambrian to Ordovician, the presence of specific fossils (like trilobites and brachiopods) in the overlying sequences helps correlate this region with other parts of Gondwana. The unconformity marks the end of the Precambrian tectonic activity and the beginning of a stable sedimentary cycle.

4. Tectonic Quiescence: The existence of such a thick sedimentary sequence (Vindhyan) over an ancient basement suggests that the Indian Shield was tectonically quiet for a significant duration, allowing for the accumulation of up to 5-6 km of sediments.

Conclusion:
The Kallur Formation and its unconformity are not just local features; they are key markers in reconstructing the palaeogeography of Gondwana India. They demonstrate that the Indian subcontinent was part of a larger, stable continental mass (Gondwana) during the early Palaeozoic, distinct from the active orogenic belts forming elsewhere in the world at that time.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Why" and "How" Framework
When answering questions on stratigraphy or geological history (e.g., Deccan Traps, Vindhyan Unconformity), avoid simply listing facts. Always structure your answer using the "Why-How" framework:

1. Why did this event happen? (Tectonic driver: e.g., Indian Plate collision, mantle plume activity).
2. How did it manifest? (Sedimentary record: unconformities, volcanic flows, fossil assemblages).
3. So What? (Implications: Climate change, mass extinction, resource formation, palaeogeographic reconstruction).

For instance, when discussing the Deccan Traps, do not just state "volcanism occurred." Explain that the mantle plume activity under the Indian Plate caused massive fissure eruptions, which altered global climate via aerosol injection, potentially contributing to the K-Pg extinction. This depth of analysis distinguishes a 15-mark answer from a basic recall response.

The Tectonic Evolution of the Himalayas: From Subduction to Collision

The study of the Himalayas is central to Geology Paper II, particularly in the context of Economic Geology and Engineering Geology. However, a superficial understanding of "folding and faulting" is insufficient for advanced preparation. One must master the specific tectonic phases: the initial subduction of the Tethys Ocean floor, the closure of the ocean, the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates, and the subsequent uplift of the Himalayan orogen.

The Main Central Thrust (MCT): The MCT is a major suture zone separating the Higher Himalaya from the Lesser Himalaya. This boundary is not just a line on a map but a complex shear zone where rocks have been transported hundreds of kilometers northwards due to crustal shortening. Understanding the kinematics of the MCT is vital for mineral exploration, as many porphyry copper and gold deposits in the Himalayan belt are associated with this tectonic regime. The "Himalayan Orogeny" is a classic example of continental collision, resulting in the formation of high mountain ranges and the creation of new crust through thrust stacking.

The Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) and Sub-Tethyan Thrust: Below the MCT lies the Lesser Himalaya, bounded by the MBT. This zone contains the "Lesser Himalayan Sequence," which includes the Dassi Formation, the Krol Formation, and the Rampur Quartzite. These rocks are often metamorphosed to greenschist facies, indicating their proximity to the active collision zone. The presence of ophiolites (remnants of ancient ocean floor) in the Lesser Himalaya provides direct evidence of the subduction process that preceded the collision.

Sedimentary Provenance: A critical aspect of advanced geology is determining the provenance of sediments. In the Siwalik Group, which overlies the MBT, the conglomerates and sandstones contain clasts derived from the eroding Himalayas. By analyzing the mineralogy (e.g., presence of garnet, staurolite) and heavy minerals in these rocks, geologists can trace them back to specific source areas in the Greater Himalaya or the Trans-Himalaya. This technique is essential for reconstructing the paleo-topography of the Himalayas at different stages of their evolution.

Seismotectonics and Hazard Assessment: The ongoing convergence of the Indian Plate (moving northwards at ~5 cm/year) results in frequent seismic activity. Understanding the geometry of the Main Himalayan Thrust (MHT), which dips gently beneath the Lesser Himalaya, is crucial for assessing earthquake hazards. The 2015 Nepal earthquake was a result of slip along this interface. For engineering geology, this means that site investigations must account for deep-seated faults and liquefaction potential in alluvial valleys.

Mesozoic Marine Transgressions and the Evolution of the Indian Craton

The Mesozoic era records a series of major marine transgressions that reshaped the sedimentary architecture of the Indian craton. During the Early Jurassic, a widespread eustatic rise combined with thermal subsidence of the Gondwanan margin produced the shallow‑marine Kachchh Basin deposits, dominated by the Patcham and Chari formations. These units contain ammonite assemblages (e.g., Perisphinctes, Kossmatia) that enable precise biostratigraphic correlation with the Tethyan realm. The Middle Jurassic saw a brief regression marked by the continental Kachchh Mainland Sandstone, followed by the Late Jurassic–Early Cretaceous transgression that deposited the marine Bhuj Formation, rich in belemnites and bivalves.

In the eastern craton, the Rajmahal Traps (Early Cretaceous) are interbedded with the marine Lameta Formation, which yields dinosaurian fossils and ostracods indicating brackish conditions. The interplay between volcanic loading from the Rajmahal plume and flexural subsidence created accommodation space for these sediments. Further south, the Cauvery Basin records a continuous Cretaceous marine succession (Uttatur, Trichinopoly, Ariyalur groups) with abundant foraminiferal zones (e.g., Globotruncana spp.) that serve as regional correlation markers.

Mechanistically, these transgressions were driven by a combination of global eustasy (mid‑Cretaceous sea‑level highstand), regional thermal subsidence following the breakup of Gondwana, and localized tectonic loading from plume‑related magmatism. The resulting sedimentary packages host significant hydrocarbon potential (e.g., the Kachchh and Cauvery basins) and provide critical palaeogeographic constraints for reconstructing the Indian plate’s drift trajectory.

Key stratigraphic surfaces such as the Jurassic–Cretaceous unconformity in Kachchh and the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary in the Cauvery Basin are identifiable on seismic sections and serve as sequence‑stratigraphic boundaries for basin modelling. Understanding the timing, magnitude, and spatial variability of these transgressions is essential for both petroleum exploration and palaeoclimatic studies.

Porphyry Copper Systems in the Indian Shield: Genesis and Exploration

Porphyry copper deposits in the Indian Shield are spatially associated with Late Archaean to Proterozoic magmatic arcs and post‑collisional granitoid suites. The most prominent example is the Malanjkhand deposit (Madhya Pradesh), hosted in a 1.5 Ga granodiorite‑quartz monzonite complex that intrudes the Central Indian Tectonic Zone. Mineralisation occurs as disseminated chalcopyrite‑bornite‑molybdenite within potassic‑altered stockwork veins and as breccia‑pipe bodies. Fluid inclusion studies indicate ore‑forming fluids at 350–450 °C, 1–2 kbar, with salinities of 30–45 wt % NaCl equivalent, consistent with magmatic‑hydrothermal origin.

The Khetri Copper Belt (Rajasthan) represents a Proterozoic volcanogenic‑massive‑sulphide (VMS) to porphyry transition zone. Here, copper mineralisation is linked to the 1.8 Ga Khetri granites and associated mafic dykes. Alteration haloes progress from potassic (biotite‑K‑feldspar) through phyllic (sericite‑quartz‑pyrite) to argillic (kaolinite‑illite) outward, a classic porphyry zoning pattern. Geochemical vectors such as elevated Cu/Au ratios (>10), high Sr/Y (>40), and pronounced negative Eu anomalies in host granites are used to discriminate fertile from barren intrusions.

Exploration workflows integrate high‑resolution aeromagnetic and radiometric surveys to map magnetic susceptibility contrasts associated with magnetite‑rich potassic zones. Induced polarization (IP) surveys delineate chargeability anomalies over disseminated sulphide networks. Soil and stream‑sediment geochemistry (Cu, Mo, Au, Ag, As, Sb) provide cost‑effective anomaly detection. Drill targeting prioritises intersections of magnetic highs, IP chargeability, and geochemical anomalies within the potassic alteration envelope.

Recent discoveries (e.g., the Banera prospect in Rajasthan) demonstrate that re‑evaluation of legacy data using 3‑D geological modelling and machine‑learning classification of multi‑parameter datasets can unlock new resources in underexplored shield segments. The tectonic setting — post‑collisional extensional regimes with asthenospheric upwelling — remains the primary control on porphyry fertility across the Indian Shield.

Integrated Geochemical Vectoring for Porphyry Targeting
A reconnaissance stream‑sediment survey over a 200 km² area in the Central Indian Tectonic Zone yields the following median concentrations (ppm): Cu = 45, Mo = 2.1, Au = 0.03, Ag = 0.4, As = 12, Sb = 0.8. The regional background values are Cu = 15, Mo = 0.5, Au = 0.005, Ag = 0.1, As = 5, Sb = 0.2. Using the Cu/Mo, Cu/Au, and As/Sb ratios, assess whether the area warrants follow‑up IP surveying.
Calculate ratios: Cu/Mo = 45/2.1 ≈ 21.4 (background ≈ 30), Cu/Au = 45/0.03 = 1500 (background ≈ 3000), As/Sb = 12/0.8 = 15 (background ≈ 25). All three ratios are lower than background, indicating a relative depletion of Cu and Mo relative to pathfinder elements. However, absolute Cu (45 ppm) is three times background, and Mo (2.1 ppm) is four times background, signalling a genuine Cu‑Mo anomaly. The low Cu/Au ratio reflects Au enrichment typical of proximal porphyry haloes. Recommendation: proceed with IP survey focused on zones where Cu > 40 ppm and Mo > 1.5 ppm coincide with magnetic highs.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Porphyry Questions
When a question asks for “genetic model” or “exploration criteria”, structure the answer in three layers: (1) tectonic setting and magma source, (2) alteration‑mineralisation zoning with temperature‑pressure‑fluid constraints, (3) geophysical‑geochemical vectors and a prioritized drill‑target workflow. Cite at least one Indian example (Malanjkhand, Khetri, Banera) and one global analogue (Bingham Canyon, El Teniente) to demonstrate comparative insight.

Groundwater Flow Modelling in Hard Rock Aquifers: Concepts and Applications

Hard rock aquifers, typified by the Deccan Traps, the Bundelkhand granitic terrain, and the Dharwar schist belts, store and transmit groundwater predominantly through secondary porosity — fractures, joints, weathered zones, and fault gouge. The Equivalent Porous Medium (EPM) approach homogenises these discrete features into continuum parameters (hydraulic conductivity K, specific storage Ss) calibrated against pumping‑test data. In the Deccan Basalt, K ranges from 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ m/s depending on fracture density, while the weathered mantle (10–30 m thick) exhibits K ≈ 10⁻⁴ m/s.

Discrete Fracture Network (DFN) models explicitly represent fracture geometry (orientation, length, aperture) derived from outcrop scanlines, borehole image logs, and seismic attribute analysis. DFN simulations capture channelised flow and anisotropic connectivity that EPM smooths out. Hybrid EPM‑DFN schemes assign high‑K fracture corridors within a low‑K matrix, offering computational efficiency for regional-scale models (e.g., MODFLOW‑USG with the Conduit Flow Process).

Parameter estimation employs pilot‑point inversion (PEST) constrained by hydraulic head time series from observation wells and spring discharge records. In the Narmada basin, a calibrated transient model reproduced the observed 2–3 m seasonal head fluctuation and identified a previously unmapped fault zone acting as a high‑permeability conduit. Scenario modelling (climate‑change recharge reduction, increased abstraction) predicts a 15–20 % decline in sustainable yield over 30 years, guiding managed aquifer recharge (MAR) site selection.

Key challenges include non‑uniqueness of fracture aperture distributions, scale dependence of hydraulic properties, and limited deep borehole data. Integrating geophysical tomography (electrical resistivity, seismic refraction) with hydrogeological data reduces uncertainty. The Central Ground Water Board’s (CGWB) National Aquifer Mapping Programme (NAQUIM) now mandates coupled EPM‑DFN modelling for all hard‑rock basins, standardising reporting formats for policy‑level decision making.

Hyperspectral Remote Sensing for Mineral Mapping: Principles and Case Studies

Hyperspectral sensors acquire contiguous narrow bands (10–20 nm width) across the visible, near‑infrared (VNIR) and short‑wave infrared (SWIR) regions (0.4–2.5 µm), enabling diagnostic absorption feature detection for alteration minerals. Key absorption features: Al‑OH at 2.20 µm (kaolinite, dickite), Fe‑OH at 2.25 µm (goethite, jarosite), Mg‑OH at 2.33 µm (chlorite, epidote), CO₃ at 2.35 µm (calcite, dolomite), and H₂O at 1.4 µm/1.9 µm. The Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM), Mixture‑Tuned Matched Filtering (MTMF), and Linear Spectral Unmixing (LSU) are standard classification algorithms.

In the Singhbhum Craton (Jharkhand), airborne HyMap data (126 bands, 15 m spatial resolution) mapped the spatial distribution of sericitic (phyllic) alteration haloes around the copper‑gold mineralised zones of the Rakha‑Mosabani belt. SAM using a library spectrum of sericite yielded a thematic map with 85 % overall accuracy validated by 120 ground‑truth points. MTMF enhanced subtle alunite (advanced argillic) exposures associated with high‑sulphidation epithermal systems in the Khetri area.

The Deccan Traps’ lateritic bauxite deposits in Maharashtra were delineated using AVIRIS‑NG data (425 bands, 5 m resolution). The Al‑OH feature depth correlated with gibbsite abundance, allowing quantitative estimation of Al₂O₃ grade (R² = 0.78). In the Rajasthan phosphorite belt, SWIR bands identified francolite (carbonate‑fluorapatite) through its characteristic CO₃ absorption at 2.35 µm, guiding drill‑hole placement.

Operational constraints: atmospheric correction (ATREM, FLAASH) is critical; sensor signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR > 500 in SWIR) determines detection limits for low‑abundance minerals. Data volume (several terabytes per flight) necessitates cloud‑based processing pipelines. The Indian Space Research Organisation’s (ISRO) upcoming Hyperspectral Imaging Satellite (HySIS) will provide systematic coverage at 30 m resolution, enabling national‑scale mineral targeting programs.

Seismic Hazard Zonation of Peninsular India: Methodology and Implications

Peninsular India, traditionally considered a stable continental region (SCR), exhibits low‑to‑moderate seismicity but hosts damaging intraplate earthquakes (e.g., 1993 Latur Mw 6.2, 2001 Bhuj Mw 7.7). Probabilistic Seismic Hazard Analysis (PSHA) for this region employs a logic‑tree framework integrating multiple source models (area sources, fault sources, background seismicity), ground‑motion prediction equations (GMPEs) calibrated for SCR (e.g., Atkinson‑Boore 2006, Sharma et al. 2013), and site‑amplification factors (Vs30‑based).

The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) seismic zone map (IS 1893‑2016) divides the peninsula into Zones II, III, and IV. Recent microzonation studies for metropolitan areas (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai) incorporate 1:10,000 scale geological‑geotechnical maps, shear‑wave velocity profiling (MASW, ReMi), and nonlinear site response analyses (DEEPSOIL). For Bengaluru, the resulting design PGA for 10 % exceedance in 50 years increased from 0.10 g (Zone II) to 0.16 g after accounting for basin‑edge effects in the Arkavathi alluvial valley.

Key uncertainties: (1) incomplete historical catalogue before 1800, (2) poorly constrained maximum magnitude (Mmax) for concealed faults, (3) epistemic variability among GMPEs for SCR. Sensitivity tests show that Mmax variation of ±0.5 units alters 475‑year PGA by 30–40 %. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) now mandates logic‑tree PSHA for all critical infrastructure projects (nuclear power plants, major dams) with a minimum of three alternative source models and three GMPEs.

Implications for engineering geology: foundation design must consider liquefaction potential in deep alluvial fills (e.g., the Godavari delta), and slope stability analyses for lateritic plateaus require seismic coefficients derived from site‑specific hazard curves. Retrofitting guidelines for unreinforced masonry in Zone III towns incorporate the updated PGA values, reducing collapse probability by an estimated 50 % in scenario simulations.

  • Confusing the timing of Mesozoic transgressions with Gondwana breakup phases; the Jurassic–Cretaceous sea‑level highstand is eustatic, not solely tectonic.
  • Assuming all Indian porphyry systems are Archaean; the Malanjkhand deposit is Proterozoic (1.5 Ga) and requires distinct genetic models.
  • Applying porous‑medium hydraulic parameters directly to fractured basalt without accounting for fracture‑network anisotropy.
  • Using broadband multispectral data (e.g., Landsat) for alteration mineral mapping where SWIR absorption features are essential.
  • Treating the BIS seismic zone map as a deterministic hazard model; it is a zonation, not a site‑specific PSHA output.
  • Neglecting the role of concealed faults in Peninsular India PSHA; logic‑tree branches must include hidden fault sources.
  • Overlooking the need for atmospheric correction in hyperspectral workflows; uncorrected data produce false alteration anomalies.

Metamorphic Petrology and P-T-t Paths in the Indian Shield

The Indian Shield preserves a record of metamorphism spanning the Archaean to the Neoproterozoic, with the Southern Granulite Terrane (SGT) and the Eastern Ghats Belt (EGB) providing well-studied pressure-temperature-time (P-T-t) trajectories. In the SGT, the Nilgiri, Palghat-Cauvery, and Madurai blocks record ultrahigh-temperature (UHT) metamorphism exceeding 950 °C at 0.8–1.0 GPa during the late Archaean (~2.55–2.50 Ga) and the Pan-African (~550–500 Ma) event. Diagnostic assemblages such as sapphirine + quartz, orthopyroxene + sillimanite + quartz, and osumilite in Mg-Al-rich granulites constrain peak temperatures of 1000–1050 °C. Zircon and monazite U-Pb geochronology, combined with rare-earth-element (REE) partitioning between garnet and monazite, resolves two distinct clockwise P-T-t paths: an early isobaric cooling (IBC) path reflecting crustal thickening and radiogenic heating, and a later near-isothermal decompression (ITD) path recording extensional collapse. The EGB preserves a polymetamorphic history with an early granulite-facies event at ~1.6–1.5 Ga (M1), an amphibolite-facies overprint at ~1.0–0.9 Ga (M2), and a Pan-African granulite-facies reworking at ~0.55 Ga (M3). Pseudosection modelling in the NCKFMASHTO system for khondalite and leptynite compositions indicates M1 reached 850–900 °C at 0.7–0.8 GPa, while M3 attained similar temperatures at lower pressures (0.5–0.6 GPa), suggesting a shift from crustal thickening to transpressional exhumation. The Central Indian Tectonic Zone (CITZ) records an anticlockwise P-T-t path in the Sausar and Sakoli mobile belts, where magmatic underplating at ~1.6 Ga produced high-temperature, low-pressure assemblages (andalusite + cordierite) followed by compressional burial. Lu-Hf garnet geochronology from the CITZ yields ages of ~1580 Ma, dating prograde metamorphic growth. For answer writing, aspirants must distinguish P-T paths controlled by tectonic setting (collisional, extensional, magmatic) from those modified by fluid infiltration, and cite specific mineral assemblages, geochronometers, and thermodynamic modelling approaches (Perple_X, Theriak-Domino) used in published Indian case studies.

Stratigraphy and Basin Analysis of the Proterozoic Purana Basins

The Proterozoic Purana basins of peninsular India — Vindhyan, Cuddapah, Kaladgi, Bhima, and Pranhita-Godavari — represent intracratonic to rift-related successions recording the assembly and breakup of Columbia and Rodinia. The Vindhyan Basin, exposed over ~100,000 km² in central India, comprises the Semri (lower) and Kaimur-Rewa-Bhander (upper) groups separated by a major unconformity. Chemostratigraphic data (δ¹³Ccarb, ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr) from Semri Group carbonates indicate deposition around 1.6–1.5 Ga; correlation with the global Lomagundi-Jatuli excursion (~2.2–2.0 Ga) remains debated. The upper Vindhyan yields conflicting ages: detrital zircon U-Pb maxima of ~1.0 Ga from the Bhander Group contrast with carbonate Pb-Pb ages of ~650 Ma, a discrepancy attributed to a prolonged depositional hiatus and diachronous basin filling. The Cuddapah Basin hosts the Papaghni, Chitravati, Nallamalai, and Kurnool groups. The Tadpatri Formation (Chitravati Group) contains stromatolitic carbonates with δ¹³C values of +2 to +4 ‰; underlying volcanics constrain its maximum age to ~1.9 Ga. The Kaladgi-Bhima basins preserve the Bagalkot (Kaladgi) and Gulcheru (Bhima) groups; detrital zircon spectra from the Bagalkot Group show dominant peaks at 2.7 Ga and 2.5 Ga, confirming derivation from the adjacent Archaean basement. Sequence stratigraphic analysis identifies three second-order megasequences in the Vindhyan: a lowstand systems tract (Semri), a transgressive systems tract (Kaimur), and a highstand systems tract (Rewa-Bhander), bounded by regionally correlatable sequence boundaries marked by palaeosols and incised valleys. Basin modelling using backstripping of well data from the Son Valley indicates thermal subsidence rates of 10–15 m/Myr during the Semri phase, accelerating to 30–40 m/Myr during Kaimur-Rewa deposition, consistent with a transition from passive margin to foreland basin loading. In examinations, candidates should construct correlation charts using chemostratigraphy, detrital zircon geochronology, and sequence boundaries, and evaluate competing tectonic models (intracratonic sag, rift, foreland) for each basin using subsidence history and provenance data.

Examiner's Trap: Correlation by Lithology Alone
A question asks: "Correlate the Vindhyan and Cuddapah basins using lithostratigraphy." A candidate matches the Semri limestone with the Tadpatri limestone and the Kaimur sandstone with the Pulivendla quartzite, claiming direct equivalence.
This correlation is flawed. Lithostratigraphic similarity does not imply temporal equivalence. The Semri Group (δ¹³Ccarb +5 to +8 ‰, ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr ~0.7055) has been linked to the Lomagundi-Jatuli event, though its age (~1.6–1.5 Ga) is younger than the global excursion (~2.2–2.0 Ga). The Tadpatri Formation (δ¹³Ccarb +2 to +4 ‰, detrital zircon maximum ~1.9 Ga) is older and lacks the Lomagundi signature. The Kaimur sandstone (detrital zircon peak ~1.0 Ga) is significantly younger than the Pulivendla quartzite (depositional age constrained by underlying ~1.9 Ga volcanics). Correct correlation requires integration of chemostratigraphy (δ¹³C, ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr), geochronology (detrital zircon U-Pb, carbonate Pb-Pb), and sequence stratigraphy (regionally extensive flooding surfaces and sequence boundaries). The Vindhyan and Cuddapah basins evolved on different cratonic blocks (Bundelkhand vs Dharwar) with distinct tectonic histories; direct lithostratigraphic correlation across cratons is a fundamental error.
Exam-Day Strategy: Purana Basin Questions
When a question asks for basin correlation or age assignment, always lead with geochronological and chemostratigraphic constraints (detrital zircon maxima, carbonate δ¹³C and ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr, Pb-Pb ages) before discussing lithology. Draw a two-column table: left column for Vindhyan, right for Cuddapah, with rows for each geochronometer. Examiners award marks for citing specific isotopic systems and their limitations (e.g., detrital zircon gives maximum depositional age only; carbonate Pb-Pb can be reset by diagenesis). Never correlate solely on quartzite-limestone-shale triads.

Magmatic Ore Deposits: Layered Intrusions and Chromite-PGE Systems of the Indian Shield

The Indian Shield hosts major chromite and platinum-group element (PGE) deposits within Proterozoic layered mafic-ultramafic intrusions, notably the Sukinda, Nausahi, Baula-Nuasahi, and Kondapalli complexes along the eastern margin of the Singhbhum Craton, and the Byrapur and Chitradurga complexes in the western Dharwar Craton. The Sukinda valley, India's largest chromite resource, comprises the Sukinda, Mahagiri, and Nuasahi ultramafic bodies emplaced at ~1.6 Ga into the Iron Ore Group metasediments. Chromitite seams occur as stratiform layers (1–3 m thick) within the lower ultramafic sequence (dunite-harzburgite), exhibiting high Cr# (Cr/(Cr+Al) = 0.7–0.85) and Mg# (Mg/(Mg+Fe²⁺) = 0.6–0.7), characteristic of boninitic parental magmas in a supra-subduction zone setting. PGE mineralisation concentrates in the middle and upper ultramafic zones (pyroxenite-gabbro), with IPGE (Os, Ir, Ru) enriched in chromitite (up to 500 ppb ∑PGE) and PPGE (Pt, Pd, Rh) enriched in sulphide-bearing pyroxenite (up to 2 ppm ∑PGE). Re-Os isotope systematics on laurite-erlichmanite inclusions in chromite yield model ages of 1.58–1.62 Ga, supporting syngenetic PGE precipitation. The Baula-Nuasahi complex hosts a 15 km long layered sequence with a well-developed marginal zone, lower zone (dunite-chromitite), critical zone (pyroxenite), and main zone (gabbro). Its chromitite seams show lower Cr# (0.55–0.65) and higher PGE tenors (1–3 ppm ∑PGE), interpreted as crystallisation from a more evolved, S-saturated magma. The Kondapalli intrusion in the Eastern Ghats Belt, dated at ~1.3 Ga by U-Pb baddeleyite, contains a 2 km thick layered sequence with disseminated sulphide mineralisation (Cu-Ni-PGE) in the lower gabbroic zone, representing a distinct magmatic pulse related to Columbia's breakup. In the western Dharwar Craton, the Byrapur layered complex (~2.7 Ga) preserves Archaean chromitite with IPGE enrichment (Os+Ir+Ru up to 800 ppb) but negligible PPGE, reflecting early mantle depletion and low S saturation. Exploration models now integrate high-resolution aeromagnetic data, soil geochemistry (Cr, Ni, Cu, PGE), and 3D geological modelling of chromitite seam continuity. For examination answers, candidates must differentiate between stratiform (layered intrusion) and podiform (ophiolite) chromite genesis, explain the role of magma mixing, crustal contamination, and S saturation in controlling PGE fractionation, and apply the concepts of R-factor (mass ratio of silicate magma to sulphide liquid) and chromite crystallisation index to evaluate ore potential.

Engineering Geology of Major Himalayan Dam Sites and Tunnelling Projects

The Himalayan orogen presents unique engineering geological challenges for large dams and tunnels due to active seismicity, high relief, glacial legacy, and complex lithostructural architecture. The Tehri Dam (260 m high rockfill, Bhagirathi River) is founded on the phyllite-quartzite sequence of the Garhwal Group (Lesser Himalaya), with the right abutment resting on the Main Central Thrust (MCT) zone. Foundation treatment involved a 60 m deep positive cut-off grout curtain (cement-bentonite, 150 kg/cm²) and dental concrete filling of shear zones. Rock mass classification using the Rock Mass Rating (RMR₈₉) system yields values of 40–55 (fair) for phyllite and 60–70 (good) for quartzite, while the Q-system gives Q = 1–4 for the MCT shear zone. The Nathpa Jhakri hydroelectric project (1500 MW) excavated a 27 km headrace tunnel through the Rampur Window, encountering the Jutogh Group (quartzite, schist) and Chail Formation (limestone, slate). Squeezing ground conditions in the Chail slate (uniaxial compressive strength 15–25 MPa, RMR 25–35) required yieldable steel sets with shotcrete and systematic pre-drainage. The Parbati-II project (800 MW) faced a catastrophic tunnel collapse in 2013 within the Kullu Window, where the tunnel intersected a 200 m wide shear zone (MCT splay) with highly weathered, water-charged phyllite (RMR < 20, Q < 0.1). Remediation employed pipe-roof umbrella arch, face bolting, and invert struts. Seismic design for Himalayan dams follows IS 1893-2016 (Zone IV/V) with site-specific response spectra derived from PSHA incorporating the Main Himalayan Thrust (MHT) as a characteristic source. The maximum credible earthquake (MCE) for the Tehri region is estimated at Mw 8.0 ± 0.3 on the MHT, yielding a design horizontal seismic coefficient of 0.36 g. Slope stability analyses for reservoir banks use limit equilibrium (Bishop, Janbu) and finite difference (FLAC3D) methods, incorporating seismic coefficients from pseudo-static analysis and pore pressure response from rainfall infiltration models (SEEP/W). Glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) hazard assessment for upstream catchments (e.g., Chorabari, South Lhonak) now forms part of design flood estimation (PMF + GLOF). In written answers, aspirants should present a structured workflow: geological mapping at 1:5,000 scale → geotechnical drilling (NX core, RQD, packer tests) → rock mass classification (RMR, Q, GSI) → kinematic analysis (DIPS) → numerical modelling → support design (IS 4453, IS 10060) → monitoring (convergence, piezometers, microseismic). Case-specific data (RMR ranges, Q values, support types, seismic coefficients) must be cited accurately.

Isotope Geochemistry in Ore Genesis and Hydrogeology: Systems and Applications

Radiogenic and stable isotope systems provide quantitative constraints on ore-forming processes, fluid sources, and groundwater evolution unattainable by geological observation alone. The Sm-Nd and Lu-Hf systems (half-lives 1.06 × 10¹¹ yr and 3.78 × 10¹⁰ yr) are resistant to hydrothermal resetting and date mantle extraction and crustal residence. For the Malanjkhand copper deposit (~1.5 Ga), εNd(t) values of host granitoids range from -2 to +2, indicating mixed mantle-crustal sources, while ore-stage sulfides yield εNd(t) = -4 to -6, implicating crustal contamination. The Re-Os system (half-life 4.16 × 10¹⁰ yr) directly dates molybdenite and sulfides; at the Khetri copper belt, molybdenite Re-Os ages of 990 ± 10 Ma and 820 ± 15 Ma define two distinct mineralising events, the younger correlating with the Delhi orogeny. Pb-Pb isotopes in galena (²⁰⁶Pb/²⁰⁴Pb, ²⁰⁷Pb/²⁰⁴Pb, ²⁰⁸Pb/²⁰⁴Pb) distinguish mantle (μ = ²³⁸U/²⁰⁴Pb ~9), upper crust (μ ~10–12), and lower crust (μ ~7–8) sources. The Zawar lead-zinc deposits show ²⁰⁶Pb/²⁰⁴Pb = 18.2–18.5, ²⁰⁷Pb/²⁰⁴Pb = 15.6–15.7, plotting on a ~1.7 Ga isochron, consistent with derivation from the Aravalli basement. Stable isotopes (δ¹⁸O, δD, δ³⁴S, δ¹³C) trace fluid-rock interaction. In the Singhbhum shear zone uranium deposits, quartz δ¹⁸O = +10 to +12 ‰ and calculated fluid δ¹⁸O = +6 to +8 ‰ at 300 °C indicate a metamorphic fluid equilibrated with pelitic gneiss. Sulfur isotopes (δ³⁴S) at the Rajpura-Dariba zinc-lead deposit range from +2 to +8 ‰, consistent with thermochemical sulfate reduction of Proterozoic seawater sulfate (δ³⁴S ~+20 ‰) by organic matter. In hydrogeology, δ¹⁸O and δD of groundwater define local meteoric water lines (LMWL); deviation below the LMWL indicates evaporation prior to recharge. Rajasthan desert aquifers show δ¹⁸O = -2 to +2 ‰, δD = -10 to +10 ‰, plotting on an evaporation line (slope 4–5), confirming paleorecharge under humid conditions. ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr ratios trace water-rock interaction: values >0.715 in Deccan basalt aquifers indicate silicate weathering, while values ~0.708–0.710 in Vindhyan sandstone aquifers reflect carbonate dissolution. ³⁶Cl/Cl (half-life 3.01 × 10⁵ yr) and ¹⁴C (half-life 5730 yr) date groundwater residence times; ³⁶Cl/Cl ratios of 10⁻¹⁵ in the Nubian Sandstone aquifer (analogous to Indian Gondwana basins) indicate ages >1 Ma. For examination purposes, candidates must know the decay constants (λSm = 6.54 × 10⁻¹² yr⁻¹, λLu = 1.867 × 10⁻¹¹ yr⁻¹, λRe = 1.666 × 10⁻¹¹ yr⁻¹), the definition of ε-notation (εNd = [(¹⁴³Nd/¹⁴⁴Nd)sample/(¹⁴³Nd/¹⁴⁴Nd)CHUR - 1] × 10⁴), and the temperature-dependent fractionation equation for oxygen isotopes (1000 ln αquartz-water = 3.38 × 10⁶/T² - 3.40). Application questions require selecting the appropriate isotope system for a given geological problem and interpreting data in terms of source, process, and timing.

  • Confusing the Lomagundi-Jatuli carbon isotope excursion (global, ~2.2–2.0 Ga) with the younger Neoproterozoic Shuram-Wonoka excursion (~570 Ma); the Vindhyan Semri Group records a positive δ¹³C excursion but its age (~1.6–1.5 Ga) is younger than the global L-J event.
  • Assigning all Indian chromite deposits to ophiolitic (podiform) settings; the Sukinda-Baula-Nuasahi complexes are stratiform, layered intrusion-hosted, with boninitic affinity, requiring distinct genetic and exploration models.
  • Using RMR or Q values without specifying the weathering grade, joint condition, and groundwater rating; a single RMR number is meaningless without the five input parameters and the structural domain it represents.
  • Applying the Re-Os molybdenite age to the entire deposit without checking for multiple molybdenite generations; cross-cutting relationships and textural context are mandatory for valid interpretation.
  • Interpreting δ¹⁸O of hydrothermal quartz without correcting for temperature-dependent fractionation; a δ¹⁸O value of +10 ‰ could represent a magmatic fluid at 400 °C or a meteoric fluid at 150 °C.
  • Assuming the BIS seismic zone map provides design PGA for a specific dam site; site-specific PSHA with logic-tree treatment of Mmax and GMPE epistemic uncertainty is required for critical infrastructure.
  • Correlating Purana basins solely on lithostratigraphic similarity (quartzite-limestone-shale) without integrating detrital zircon age spectra, carbonate chemostratigraphy, and sequence boundaries; basins on different cratons evolved independently.

Geochemical Modelling of Hydrothermal Alteration Zones in Porphyry Systems

Porphyry copper–gold systems develop concentric alteration haloes that record progressive fluid–rock interaction from the magmatic core to the peripheral propylitic fringe. The inner potassic zone (biotite + K‑feldspar + magnetite) typically forms at 400–550 °C from high‑salinity, magmatic‑derived fluids (NaCl ≈ 30–50 wt %). Mass‑balance calculations using the Gresens equation show that K₂O gains of 2–4 wt % correspond to 10–15 % volume replacement of plagioclase by K‑feldspar, while Fe‑Mg exchange produces biotite at the expense of hornblende. The surrounding phyllic zone (quartz + sericite + pyrite) records a temperature drop to 300–400 °C and a fluid salinity decrease to 5–15 wt % NaCl equivalent, reflecting boiling and phase separation. Sericitization of feldspar releases Al and Si, which precipitate as quartz veins; the accompanying pH drop (from ~5 to ~3) drives Cu‑Fe sulfide saturation. The outer argillic zone (kaolinite + illite + smectite) forms below 250 °C where meteoric water mixes with the residual magmatic fluid, producing advanced argillic assemblages (alunite + pyrophyllite) in high‑sulphidation systems. Quantitative modelling uses PHREEQC or Geochemist’s Workbench codes: input bulk‑rock composition, fluid composition (Cl, SO₄, CO₂, metal concentrations), temperature–pressure path, and kinetic rate laws for mineral dissolution/precipitation. Outputs include mineral saturation indices, element mobility vectors, and predicted alteration mineral volumes that can be compared with drill‑core logs. Case studies from Indian deposits demonstrate that forward modelling can reproduce kilometre‑scale potassic cores with significant K₂O gains and phyllic haloes where Cu grades peak; sensitivity tests show that modest increases in fluid fO₂ shift the Cu‑Fe sulfide saturation front outward by hundreds of metres, providing a useful exploration vector.

Structural Controls on Gold Mineralization in the Dharwar Craton

Gold deposits of the Dharwar Craton (e.g., Hutti, Kolar, Gadag) are spatially linked to late‑Archean to early‑Proterozoic transpressional shear zones that re‑activate older greenstone‑belt fabrics. The dominant structural architecture comprises a set of ENE‑WSW‑trending, steeply dipping dextral shear zones (e.g., the Hutti‑Maski shear zone) that overprint earlier N‑S‑trending thrusts. Kinematic analysis of S‑C fabrics, asymmetric porphyroclasts, and quartz c‑axis fabrics indicates progressive strain partitioning: early ductile flattening at ~2.7 Ga, followed by brittle‑ductile reactivation at ~2.5 Ga under greenschist‑facies conditions (300–350 °C, 2–3 kbar). Gold‑bearing quartz veins localise in second‑order Riedel (R) and P‑shear fractures that develop at 15–30° to the main shear plane, providing high‑permeability pathways for auriferous fluids. Fluid inclusion studies from Hutti reveal two distinct populations: (1) high‑salinity (15–20 wt % NaCl equiv.), CO₂‑rich inclusions homogenising at 350–380 °C, interpreted as metamorphic devolatilisation fluids; (2) low‑salinity (1–3 wt % NaCl equiv.), CH₄‑bearing inclusions at 250–280 °C, representing mixed meteoric–metamorphic fluids during late‑stage uplift. Stable isotope data (δ¹⁸O_quartz = +9 to +11 ‰, δD_fluid = –45 to –55 ‰) support a metamorphic fluid source with minor meteoric dilution. Structural modelling using 3‑D discrete fracture network (DFN) simulations shows that gold precipitation is maximised where the product of fracture aperture and fluid pressure gradient exceeds a critical threshold, focusing deposition in dilational jogs and fault‑bend folds. Exploration drilling guided by such models in the Gadag belt has intersected significant gold grades in previously untested R‑shear corridors.

Worked Example: Interpreting Alteration Zonation for Vectoring
A drill core from a porphyry prospect shows, from centre outward: (i) biotite‑K‑feldspar‑magnetite, (ii) quartz‑sericite‑pyrite, (iii) kaolinite‑illite‑smectite. Fluid inclusion data from the quartz‑sericite zone give homogenisation temperatures of 340 °C and salinities of 8 wt % NaCl equiv. The host granodiorite has 2.5 wt % K₂O. Using the Gresens equation, estimate the K₂O gain in the potassic zone if the volume change is –10 % (i.e., 10 % volume loss). Assume the protolith K₂O = 2.5 wt % and the altered rock K₂O = 5.0 wt %.
The Gresens equation for component i: C_i^altered = C_i^protolith × (1 + ΔV) / (1 + ΔV × (C_i^protolith / 100)). Here ΔV = –0.10. Rearranged for gain: Gain (wt %) = C_i^altered – C_i^protolith. Plugging values: C_K₂O^altered = 5.0 wt %, C_K₂O^protolith = 2.5 wt %. Gain = 5.0 – 2.5 = 2.5 wt % K₂O. This corresponds to a 100 % relative increase, consistent with 10–15 % plagioclase replacement by K‑feldspar. The negative volume change indicates net mass loss (e.g., Ca, Na leaching), typical of potassic alteration. The fluid inclusion T‑h of 340 °C and 8 wt % NaCl place the phyllic zone at the expected temperature–salinity window, confirming the zonation sequence. Vectoring: the potassic core lies up‑dip of the phyllic zone; drilling further up‑dip should intersect higher‑grade Cu‑Au mineralisation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks you to “vector” mineralisation using alteration assemblages, first sketch the idealised concentric model (potassic → phyllic → argillic → propylitic). Then note the key geochemical indicators: K₂O gain, Na₂O loss, Cu/Zn ratios, and fluid inclusion T‑h/salinity trends. In the answer, explicitly link each alteration mineral to a temperature–fluid composition window and state the expected metal zoning (Cu‑Au in potassic/phyllic, Mo in outer phyllic, Zn‑Pb in distal argillic). This structured approach earns full marks for both conceptual understanding and applied reasoning.

Advanced Sequence Stratigraphy of the Vindhyan Supergroup

The Vindhyan Supergroup (ca. 1.7–0.6 Ga) records a long‑lived intracratonic basin on the northern margin of the Indian Shield. Recent high‑resolution chemostratigraphy (δ¹³C_carb, ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr) combined with detrital zircon U‑Pb age spectra has refined its subdivision into three second‑order sequences bounded by regional sequence boundaries. The lower Semri Group comprises a basal fluvial–alluvial conglomerate (the Basal Conglomerate) overlain by a thick carbonate platform (the Rohtas Limestone) that records a pronounced positive δ¹³C excursion (+4 to +6 ‰) correlated with the global Lomagundi‑Jatuli event, though the Vindhyan excursion appears younger and regionally restricted. The overlying Kaimur Group shows a shift to siliciclastic dominance with stacked parasequences of sandstone–shale couplets, interpreted as lowstand‑to‑highstand cycles driven by far‑field tectonic loading from the Aravalli‑Delhi orogeny. The upper Bhander Group contains the “Vindhyan red beds” and a terminal glacial diamictite (the Bijaigarh Formation) that may correlate with the Sturtian glaciation. Sequence stratigraphic surfaces are identified by: (1) abrupt facies shifts from carbonate to siliciclastic, (2) hardground development with Fe‑Mn crusts, (3) palaeosol horizons with carbonate nodules, and (4) sharp changes in detrital zircon age populations (e.g., a spike of 1.8 Ga zircons indicating uplift of the Bundelkhand craton). Correlation with the contemporaneous Cuddapah and Kaladgi basins is achieved by matching the δ¹³C_carb curve and the ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr seawater curve, which shows a steady rise across the interval. This integrated framework helps resolve the long‑standing “Vindhyan age problem” and provides a predictive model for hydrocarbon‑bearing shale intervals in the Kaimur Group.

Magnetotelluric Imaging of Deep Crustal Conductors for Mineral Exploration

Magnetotelluric (MT) methods exploit natural electromagnetic fields (10⁻⁴–10⁴ Hz) to image electrical resistivity structures to depths of 50–100 km. In the Indian Shield, MT surveys across the Singhbhum–Orissa Craton have delineated a high‑conductivity anomaly at 15–25 km depth, spatially coincident with the Singhbhum Shear Zone and the uranium‑copper mineralisation at Turamdih and Jaduguda. The conductor is interpreted as a network of interconnected graphite‑bearing metasedimentary rocks and saline fluid‑filled fractures that acted as fluid pathways during Proterozoic hydrothermal events. Forward modelling using the ModEM code, constrained by laboratory conductivity measurements on graphite‑rich schists and brine‑saturated quartzites, reproduces the observed impedance tensor when a laterally continuous conductive layer is included. Joint inversion of MT and broadband seismic receiver functions further resolves the geometry: the conductor dips parallel to the shear‑zone foliation and terminates against a resistive Archaean granulite root. This geometry focuses upward‑migrating, metal‑bearing fluids into dilational jogs at the shear‑zone–granulite interface, explaining the spatial clustering of uranium deposits. In the Central Indian Tectonic Zone, MT profiles across the Betul Belt reveal a shallow conductive body associated with the Mahakoshal metasediments and the Malanjkhand porphyry system. The conductor’s lateral extent matches the surface alteration halo, and its depth corresponds to the inferred magmatic fluid source region. Exploration programmes now integrate MT resistivity slices at depth with magnetic and gravity data to prioritise drill targets beneath cover sequences, significantly reducing drilling risk in covered terrains.

Groundwater Quality Evolution and Arsenic Mobilization in the Bengal Basin

The Bengal Basin hosts one of the world’s largest arsenic‑affected aquifer systems, with tens of millions of people exposed to concentrations exceeding the WHO guideline of 10 µg L⁻¹. The primary mechanism is reductive dissolution of Fe‑oxyhydroxides (goethite, ferrihydrite) in Holocene alluvial sediments, releasing adsorbed As(V) which is subsequently reduced to the more mobile As(III). Geochemical modelling (PHREEQC) of depth‑profile data shows that at shallow depths, groundwater Eh drops, pH rises, and dissolved organic carbon (DOC) increases, creating conditions for microbial Fe(III) reduction. The reaction: FeOOH + 3 H⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺ + 2 H₂O couples with As(V) + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ → As(III) + H₂O. Mass‑balance calculations indicate that modest Fe(II) production can release arsenic concentrations consistent with observed peaks. Stable isotopes (δ¹⁸O, δD) indicate modern recharge; residence times of decades are determined by tracers like ³H/³He. The spatial distribution of high As correlates with palaeochannel belts where fine‑grained overbank deposits (high surface area Fe‑oxides) overlie coarse channel sands that act as preferential flow paths. Mitigation strategies evaluated by reactive transport modelling (PFLOTRAN) show that managed aquifer recharge (MAR) with low‑DOC, oxygenated river water can raise Eh, re‑precipitating Fe‑oxides and scavenging As, but requires sustained injection rates to overcome the high hydraulic conductivity of the channel sands. Long‑term monitoring of As speciation (As(III)/As(V) ratio) and Fe(II) concentrations is essential to verify the redox front migration.

  • Confusing the Vindhyan δ¹³C excursion with the global Lomagundi‑Jatuli event; the Vindhyan signal is younger and regionally restricted.
  • Assuming all high‑conductivity MT anomalies are graphite‑related; saline fluids in fracture networks can produce similar resistivity signatures without any carbonaceous material.
  • Treating arsenic mobilization as a simple desorption process; the coupled Fe‑reduction/As‑reduction redox cascade and organic carbon availability are the controlling factors.
  • Using a single fluid inclusion population to define the entire P‑T‑X evolution of a porphyry system; multiple generations (magmatic, boiling, meteoric) must be distinguished by textural context.
  • Applying the Gresens equation without correcting for volume change sign; a negative ΔV indicates net mass loss, not gain, and reverses the calculated element mobility.
  • Correlating Purana basins solely on lithostratigraphic similarity while ignoring detrital zircon age spectra and chemostratigraphic markers that reveal independent basin histories.
