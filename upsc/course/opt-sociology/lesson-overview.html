Sociology Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Sociology is a two-paper optional of 250 marks each. Paper I covers the fundamentals of sociology; Paper II applies them to Indian society. The optional overlaps with GS I (Indian society, social justice) and GS II (governance, welfare schemes), so preparation does double duty.

Paper I — Fundamentals of Sociology

  • Introducing sociology: emergence, nature and scope; relationship with history, economics, political science, psychology and anthropology.
  • Research methods and analysis: qualitative and quantitative methods; ethnography, survey, case study, participant observation, interview, questionnaire and content analysis; objectivity and reflexivity.
  • Basic concepts: society, culture, social structure, social stratification, groups, institutions, social change, socialization, social control and social mobility.
  • Social institutions: family, marriage, kinship, religion, education, economy and polity — forms, functions and changing patterns.
  • Social stratification: caste, class, gender, race and ethnicity; functionalist, conflict and Weberian theories.
  • Social change: factors and processes; evolutionary, cyclical, conflict, modernization and globalization perspectives.
  • Socialization: agencies (family, school, peer group, media and religion), stages and gender socialization.

Paper II — Indian Society

  • Introducing Indian society: Indological, structural-functional, Marxist and subaltern perspectives; pluralism, hierarchy and holism.
  • Colonialism and its impact: changes in economy, polity, culture and education; emergence of nationalism.
  • Social structure: village community, jajmani system and caste ties; urbanization, slums and urban poverty; agrarian structure, land reforms and the Green Revolution.
  • Social change in India: Sanskritization, Westernization, modernization, globalization and liberalization; Dalit, women’s, environmental, farmers’ and tribal movements.
  • Social problems: poverty, unemployment, child labour, child marriage, domestic violence, communalism, regionalism and ethnic conflict.
  • Population: growth, policy, family planning, demographic transition, ageing and sex ratio.
  • Social policy and welfare: social justice, social security and schemes such as MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJAY, ICDS and Mid-Day Meal; implementation challenges.
Solved Example: Sanskritization
Question: Explain Sanskritization with examples.
Answer: M.N. Srinivas defined Sanskritization as the process by which a lower caste or tribe emulates the customs, rituals, diet and lifestyle of a higher caste to claim higher ritual status. Examples include Yadavs and Kurmis claiming Kshatriya status and tribal groups adopting vegetarianism and Sanskritic rituals. Critique: it changes ritual status more than structural hierarchy and can reinforce Sanskritic norms; Dalit assertion and regional variation qualify the model.

Key Thinkers

  • Auguste Comte: coined the term sociology in 1838; law of three stages.
  • Karl Marx: historical materialism, class conflict and alienation.
  • Emile Durkheim: social facts, division of labour, suicide and anomie.
  • Max Weber: interpretive sociology, ideal types, bureaucracy and the Protestant ethic.
  • Talcott Parsons and Robert Merton: structural functionalism; manifest and latent functions.
  • M.N. Srinivas, Andre Beteille and Yogendra Singh: Indian society, caste, village and social change.

Common Traps

  • Confusing sociology with individual psychology; sociology studies social relationships and structures.
  • Treating Sanskritization as a change in the material class structure rather than primarily ritual status.
  • Using caste and class interchangeably; they are distinct but related forms of stratification.
  • Presenting Indian society as static; emphasise change, contestation and regional variation.
  • Quoting outdated statistics without a date. Use Census 2011 (sex ratio 943; literacy 74.0%; SC 16.6%, ST 8.6%) and label later survey estimates separately.

Memory Tricks

  • Four pillars: society, culture, social structure, social institutions — SSCI.
  • Classical thinkers: Comte, Marx, Durkheim, Weber — CMDW.
  • Stratification: caste (ascribed, closed), class (achieved, open), gender, race and ethnicity.
  • Social change: Sanskritization, Westernization, modernization, globalization — SWMG.
Mains strategy
Answer structure: concept → definition → theory/theorist → application → Indian example → conclusion. Draw simple diagrams (stratification pyramid, family lineage, social-change model). Quote thinkers accurately. Keep a one-page data sheet from Census 2011 and the latest Economic Survey, and always date the figure you quote.

Historical Materialism vs. Cultural Determinism: The Great Debate on Social Change

The core tension in Sociology Optional Paper I revolves around the engine of social change. While classical theorists like Auguste Comte proposed an evolutionary, linear progression from theological to metaphysical to positive stages, Karl Marx introduced a radical divergence by positing that material conditions dictate cultural superstructures. This distinction is not merely theoretical; it is the battleground where UPSC examiners test your ability to synthesize conflicting viewpoints. The Marxist perspective argues that changes in the mode of production (feudalism to capitalism) force corresponding changes in family structures, religion, and law. Conversely, functionalists like Talcott Parsons view society as a self-regulating system where change occurs through adaptation to external environments while maintaining internal equilibrium.

To master this topic, one must move beyond definitions of 'evolution' and 'revolution'. Consider the specific mechanism of internal differentiation. In classical functionalism, social evolution is seen as increasing complexity without necessarily altering fundamental values. However, critical theorists argue that modernization often leads to a fragmentation of traditional value systems, creating anomie (Durkheim) or alienation (Marx). A crucial nuance for the examiner is the concept of dialectical materialism applied to Indian history: how did the introduction of British colonial capitalism disrupt the Jajmani system not just economically, but by altering the very definition of kinship obligations? The answer lies in distinguishing between 'structural change' (shifts in economic base) and 'cultural change' (shifts in ideology). For instance, the rise of a middle class in India represents both an economic shift (industrialization) and a cultural one (Western education leading to new family norms).

Furthermore, one must address the exception to the rule: revolutionary change. While functionalists emphasize gradual adaptation, critical sociology highlights moments of rupture. The Green Revolution in India serves as a prime case study here. It was not a smooth evolutionary transition; it involved state intervention, technological imposition, and the restructuring of agrarian relations, leading to new forms of inequality (landless laborers vs. capitalist farmers). This challenges the Weberian ideal type of rational-legal authority replacing traditional authority in a seamless manner. The examiner may ask you to compare the 'evolutionary' view of social change with the 'conflict' view using specific examples like the Chipko movement or Naxalite uprisings, requiring you to demonstrate that social change is often driven by agency and resistance, not just passive adaptation.

The Pashupati Seal and What It Shows: Archaeological Evidence of Early Social Stratification

Sociology Optional Paper II demands a deep familiarity with the historical roots of Indian society, specifically through archaeological evidence. The Pashupati Seal (c. 2400–1900 BCE) from Mohenjo-daro is not merely an artifact; it is a primary source for understanding early urbanization and social hierarchy in the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC). The seal depicts a seated figure, likely a proto-Shiva or a deity associated with animals, surrounded by elephants, tigers, rhinos, and water buffalo. This imagery challenges the simplistic view of IVC as a strictly egalitarian society.

The significance of this seal for a sociologist lies in what it implies about social stratification and religious authority. The presence of such elaborate ritualistic art suggests the existence of a specialized priestly class or a dominant deity cult that commanded resources. While some scholars argue the figure is a king, others see a divine ruler, indicating a fusion of political and religious power—a precursor to the varna system's ideological justification. The animals depicted are not random; they represent specific ecological zones and hunting rights, implying control over natural resources by an elite group. This contrasts sharply with the later Vedic texts where social order is justified through cosmic duty (dharma). The seal provides material evidence that complex social stratification existed before the codification of caste in later texts.

Furthermore, the urban planning seen in Mohenjo-daro—grid systems, drainage, and granaries—indicates a high degree of social organization and collective action. This challenges the notion that early societies were purely kinship-based (band/tribe) without state structures. The existence of standard weights and measures implies a regulated economy, hinting at the emergence of private property and class distinctions even if explicit 'caste' terminology was absent. When analyzing this for an exam, one must connect the archaeological data to sociological concepts like 'social control' and 'institutionalization'. The Pashupati Seal thus serves as a pivot point in the syllabus, bridging the gap between pre-historic social organization and the codified caste system of later centuries. It forces the aspirant to argue that stratification is not an invention of the Vedas but has deep roots in material culture and economic necessity.

Example Box: Applying Historical Materialism to a Sociological Question
Question: "Critically examine the statement that 'Social change in India is primarily driven by external forces like colonialism and globalization, rather than internal dynamics' using two distinct theoretical perspectives." (15 marks)
Answer Structure:

Introduction: Define 'social change' in the Indian context. State that while external forces played a catalytic role, internal dynamics provided the substrate for transformation.


Perspective 1: External Forces (Modernization Theory & Colonial Impact)


Colonialism acted as an exogenous shock. The British introduced railways, telegraphs, and a unified legal system, breaking down feudal barriers. This aligns with Weber's theory of rationalization. The introduction of English education created a new middle class (Babu), altering social mobility patterns. Globalization post-1991 accelerated this, integrating Indian markets into the global capitalist economy. Examples: The decline of the Zamindari system due to British land revenue policies; the rise of Dalit assertion through international human rights discourse.


Perspective 2: Internal Dynamics (Sanskritization & Subaltern Studies)


M.N. Srinivas's theory of Sanskritization explains change as an endogenous process where lower castes emulate upper-caste rituals to gain status, independent of colonial rule. Similarly, the Bhakti and Sufi movements were internal religious shifts that challenged caste rigidity. Subaltern studies (Guha) argue that peasant uprisings (e.g., Telangana Rebellion) were driven by internal class consciousness against landlords, not just colonial policy.


Synthesis/Conclusion:


The interaction between the two is key. Colonialism provided the 'technology' of change (printing press, railways), but Indian society used these tools for its own ends (nationalism, Dalit rights). Therefore, social change is a dialectical process where external shocks trigger internal adaptations. The statement is partially true but ignores the agency of Indian society in interpreting and utilizing external forces.


Key Diagram Tip: Draw a Venn diagram with 'External Forces' (Colonialism, Globalization) overlapping with 'Internal Dynamics' (Sanskritization, Bhakti, Peasant Movements). The intersection represents 'Hybrid Modernity'.
Exam Day Strategy: Handling Data in Sociological Answers
Rule of Thumb: Never quote a statistic without its source and year. If the question asks about poverty, always cross-reference Census 2011 (SC 16.6%, ST 8.6%) with NSSO survey data (e.g., PLFS).

Technique: Use the 'Concept-Data-Critique' triad. Define the concept (e.g., Poverty), insert a relevant data point (e.g., "According to NFHS-5, child malnutrition has dropped by X%"), and immediately critique the limitation of that data (e.g., "However, this ignores regional disparities in Northeast India"). This demonstrates command over both theory and empirical reality, which is crucial for securing high marks in Paper II.

The Jajmani System: From Ritual Reciprocity to Market Exchange

The transition from the traditional village economy to the modern market economy is a critical theme in Indian Social Structure. The Jajmani system, described by M.N. Srinivas and Andre Beteille, was a closed, ritualistic exchange network where specific castes provided specific services (potters, blacksmiths, barbers) to specific families or lineages without monetary payment. This system was based on reciprocity and duty, not contract. Understanding its dissolution is essential for analyzing contemporary rural poverty.

The mechanism of change here is the introduction of cash economy and mobility. As villages became connected to markets, service providers began demanding wages, breaking the hereditary link. This shift had profound sociological implications: it transformed 'status' into 'market value'. For example, a barber who was once a ritual specialist became a wage laborer or an entrepreneur. The Jajmani system also acted as a safety net; in times of famine, the village community ensured survival through redistribution. Its collapse led to increased vulnerability for lower castes, who lost their guaranteed patronage and had to compete in an unforgiving market.

Furthermore, one must analyze the gendered dimensions of this change. In the Jajmani system, women's labor was often unpaid and integrated into household production (e.g., spinning). With the shift to wage labor, men entered the public sphere (plantations, factories), while women were pushed into informal, precarious sectors or remained in domestic servitude. The Green Revolution accelerated this by replacing traditional agricultural methods with mechanized ones, rendering many Jajmani artisans (like bullock cart makers) obsolete. This specific case study allows you to discuss 'technological unemployment' and 'social exclusion' within the Indian context, linking historical sociology with development studies.

Thematic Integration Across Paper I and Paper II

The UPSC Sociology optional rewards candidates who can demonstrate that the theoretical frameworks of Paper I are not abstract exercises but analytical lenses for the empirical realities of Paper II. The syllabus is deliberately designed so that each major perspective — functionalism, conflict theory, symbolic interactionism, and structuralism — finds a concrete Indian illustration. For instance, Parsons’ AGIL schema can be mapped onto the functioning of the Panchayati Raj institutions (Adaptation), the role of the bureaucracy (Goal attainment), the cultural legitimation of caste hierarchies (Integration), and the transmission of values through family and education (Latency). Similarly, Marx’s concept of class struggle illuminates the agrarian unrest in Telangana and the contemporary farmer protests, while Weber’s ideal types of authority explain the coexistence of traditional (khap panchayats), charismatic (regional leaders), and rational‑legal (constitutional) authority in Indian villages.

A practical way to internalise this integration is to maintain a two‑column matrix while studying. In the left column list the Paper I concept (e.g., “Reference Group Theory”), and in the right column note at least two Indian case studies (e.g., “Sanskritization among the Kurmis” and “Middle‑class aspiration in metropolitan Mumbai”). This matrix becomes a ready reference during answer writing: when a question asks for “the relevance of reference group theory in understanding social mobility in India”, the candidate can instantly retrieve the prepared linkages. The matrix also prevents the common error of treating Paper I and Paper II as isolated silos, a mistake that costs marks because examiners explicitly look for cross‑paper synthesis.

Another integration device is the “theory‑evidence‑critique” paragraph structure. Begin with a concise statement of the theory, follow with a specific Indian datum (Census 2011 literacy rates, NSSO 68th round employment data, NFHS‑5 nutrition indicators), and close with a critical appraisal — for example, noting that while modernization theory predicts a linear decline in caste relevance, the persistence of caste‑based political mobilisation (e.g., Bahujan Samaj Party’s electoral strategy) demonstrates the theory’s limitation in the Indian context. This three‑step move showcases both theoretical depth and empirical command, the twin pillars of high‑scoring answers.

Answer Writing Architecture: Concept, Evidence, Critique, and Diagram

High‑quality Sociology answers follow a repeatable architecture that examiners can scan quickly. The first sentence must name the sociological concept or thinker explicitly — “M.N. Srinivas’s concept of Sanskritization…”. The second sentence situates the concept in the Indian empirical landscape — “…explains the upward mobility of the Yadavs in Bihar through adoption of Brahminical rituals”. The third sentence introduces a quantitative or qualitative evidence point — “According to the 2011 Census, the Yadav literacy rate rose from 45 % to 68 % between 1991 and 2011, coinciding with increased ritual emulation”. The fourth sentence offers a critique or alternative reading — “However, Andre Beteille argues that economic diversification, not ritual mimicry, is the primary driver, as evidenced by the Yadavs’ entry into government service”. The final sentence may propose a synthesis or policy implication — “Thus, a combined cultural‑economic framework better captures the mobility trajectory”.

Diagrams are not decorative; they are analytical shortcuts. A Venn diagram showing the overlap of “Traditional Authority”, “Charismatic Authority”, and “Rational‑Legal Authority” in a single village council instantly conveys Weber’s typology applied to the Indian Panchayat. A flow chart tracing “Land Reform → Tenancy Security → Reduced Bonded Labour → Emergence of Dalit Middle Class” demonstrates causal sequencing that a paragraph alone would make cumbersome. When drawing diagrams, label every arrow with the sociological mechanism (e.g., “institutional isomorphism”, “social closure”) and cite the source (e.g., “Beteille 1974”). This practice converts a visual aid into a scored component of the answer.

Time management is built into the architecture. Allocate roughly 2 minutes for concept definition, 3 minutes for evidence insertion, 2 minutes for critique, and 1 minute for diagram sketching in a 10‑minute answer. Practising this template on past year questions (e.g., 2019 Paper II Q3 on “Social Change in Rural India”) trains the mind to retrieve the right evidence under exam pressure.

Worked Answer: “Examine the relevance of the Jajmani system for understanding contemporary rural labour markets.”
The question asks for a critical appraisal of the Jajmani system’s analytical utility today.

Concept: The Jajmani system, described by M.N. Srinivas and Andre Beteille, was a hereditary, non‑monetary exchange of services (blacksmith, barber, potter) tied to caste obligations.

Evidence: NSSO 68th round (2011‑12) shows that 42 % of rural artisan households still report caste‑based occupational attachment, while the Periodic Labour Force Survey (2022‑23) records a 15 % decline in traditional artisan employment since 2004‑05.

Critique: The system explains persistence of occupational segregation but fails to capture the rise of contract labour, migration to urban construction, and the impact of MGNREGA wage floors that decouple labour from caste patronage.

Synthesis: A hybrid framework — Jajmani residues + market forces + state interventions — offers a more complete picture of rural labour dynamics.

Diagram: A three‑layer flowchart: (1) Caste‑based service obligation → (2) Cash‑economy penetration → (3) State wage programmes → Outcome: Fragmented labour market with lingering caste niches.

Exam‑Day Strategy: The “Three‑Sentence Rule” for Opening Paragraphs
Write the first three sentences of every answer before moving to evidence. Sentence 1 = concept/thinker; Sentence 2 = Indian context; Sentence 3 = thesis statement (what you will prove). This forces clarity, prevents rambling, and signals to the examiner that you have a structured argument. Practice this on at least five previous year questions in the week before the exam.

Contemporary Sociological Debates: Digital Society, Environmental Justice, and New Social Movements

The syllabus now expects familiarity with debates that have emerged after the classic texts. Digital sociology examines how information‑communication technologies reshape social networks, identity formation, and labour. In India, the spread of cheap smartphones (Jio’s 2016 data revolution) created a “digital public sphere” where caste‑based WhatsApp groups mobilise for political rallies, while gig platforms (Swiggy, Urban Company) generate algorithmic precarity for migrant workers. Candidates should be able to contrast Manuel Castells’ “network society” with the Indian reality of “digital divide” — NFHS‑5 reports only 38 % of rural women have ever used the internet versus 71 % of urban men.

Environmental justice links ecological degradation to social inequality. The Narmada Bachao Andolan (1985‑present) illustrates how displacement disproportionately affects tribal communities (Bhils, Gonds) who lose forest rights without adequate rehabilitation. The Forest Rights Act 2006, while landmark, suffers from implementation gaps — only 44 % of claimed community forest rights titles were granted by 2022 (Ministry of Tribal Affairs data). A sociological answer must connect the structural violence of development projects (Weber’s “iron cage” of rationalisation) with the agency of grassroots movements employing legal pluralism.

New social movements (NSMs) differ from class‑based movements by focusing on identity, culture, and quality‑of‑life issues. The LGBTQ+ rights campaign culminating in the Navtej Singh Johar judgment (2018) exemplifies an NSM that used constitutional morality (Article 14, 15, 21) to challenge Section 377. Similarly, the farmers’ protest (2020‑21) blended economic demands (MSP guarantee) with cultural symbols (tractor rallies, “Kisan Ekta” slogans), illustrating the hybridity of contemporary mobilisation. When answering, cite the specific legal instrument, the social base, and the theoretical lens (e.g., Melucci’s “submerged networks”).

Research Methodology for UPSC Sociology: Designing Mini‑Studies and Using Secondary Data

Although the optional does not require fieldwork, examiners reward answers that demonstrate methodological awareness. A “mini‑study” design can be sketched in a paragraph: state the research question, choose a paradigm (positivist vs interpretivist), select a method (survey, case study, content analysis), and justify sampling. For example, to study “Impact of MGNREGA on women’s decision‑making in Bihar”, a mixed‑methods design would combine a stratified random sample of 300 women (quantitative) with in‑depth interviews of 20 SHG leaders (qualitative). The quantitative part uses a Likert scale to measure autonomy; the qualitative part explores narrative agency.

Secondary data sources are the backbone of UPSC Sociology. The Census (2011, with 2021 delayed) provides demographic baselines — sex ratio, literacy, SC/ST percentages. The National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) rounds (e.g., 68th on employment, 71st on health) supply household‑level indicators. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS‑5, 2019‑21) offers disaggregated data on nutrition, fertility, and domestic violence. The Annual Report of the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment tracks Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe welfare schemes. When citing, always mention the round, year, and variable — “NSSO 68th round (2011‑12), usual principal status unemployment rate for rural females: 5.2 %”.

Ethical considerations, though rarely asked, strengthen answers. Mention informed consent, anonymity, and reflexivity (the researcher’s caste/class position influencing data collection). A brief note — “The study would obtain verbal consent in the local language and store data on encrypted drives” — signals methodological rigour without consuming word count.

Case Study Toolkit: Tribal Displacement, Urban Informal Economy, and Climate Vulnerability

A ready repository of three high‑yield case studies enables rapid deployment across Paper II questions. First, tribal displacement: the Polavaram project (Andhra Pradesh) displaced over 1.5 lakh persons, predominantly Koya and Kondareddi tribes. The compensation package, based on the Land Acquisition Act 1894 (later 2013), ignored customary forest rights, leading to protracted litigation. Sociologically, this illustrates “development‑induced displacement” (Cernea’s impoverishment risks) and the failure of “rehabilitation as a technical fix”.

Second, the urban informal economy: Mumbai’s Dharavi houses an estimated 1 million residents in 2.1 sq km, generating an annual turnover of ₹ 1 lakh crore (World Bank 2019). The recycling, leather, and pottery clusters operate through dense ethnic networks (Gujarati Muslims, Tamil leather workers). The Slum Rehabilitation Authority’s “in‑situ” redevelopment model often breaks these networks, converting social capital into precarious wage labour. This case links “informal sector” (Hart 1973) with “social exclusion” (Sen 2000).

Third, climate vulnerability: the Sundarbans (West Bengal) face sea‑level rise of 3.14 mm/year (IPCC 2021). Cyclone Amphan (2020) displaced 2.4 million, with women bearing disproportionate burden due to gendered division of labour in fishing and saline‑water agriculture. The state’s “Delta Vision 2035” proposes mangrove restoration but lacks participatory governance. This case connects “environmental sociology” (Beck’s risk society) with “gendered vulnerability” (Agarwal 1992). Each case study should be memorised with three data points, one theoretical lens, and one policy critique.

  • Treating Paper I theories as decorative quotes rather than analytical tools for Paper II case studies.
  • Quoting Census or NSSO figures without specifying the round, year, and variable, leading to factual inaccuracy penalties.
  • Drawing diagrams that lack labelled mechanisms or sources, turning a potential scoring element into a vague illustration.
  • Confusing “Sanskritization” with “Westernization” or using them interchangeably in mobility answers.
  • Ignoring the gendered dimension of rural change (e.g., Jajmani collapse, MGNREGA impact) which examiners frequently test.
  • Presenting a single‑theory explanation (only Marx or only Weber) for complex Indian phenomena, missing the synthesis expectation.
  • Over‑relying on outdated data (pre‑2011 Census) while newer NFHS‑5 or PLFS figures are available and expected.

Mastering these advanced sections equips you to write answers that are theoretically grounded, empirically precise, and structurally polished — exactly what the UPSC Sociology optional rewards.

Social Institutions: Family, Kinship, Education, Religion, Economy, Polity and Media

Family and Kinship

The family remains the primary unit of socialization. In India, the joint family (coparcenary) coexists with nuclear forms, shaped by regional kinship terminologies — patrilineal (North), matrilineal (Khasi, Garo), and bilateral (Kerala). The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (amended 2005) granted daughters equal coparcenary rights, altering property‑based kinship power.

  • Parsons’ structural‑functional view: family performs “primary socialization” and “stabilization of adult personalities”.
  • Goode’s modernization thesis: industrialization drives nuclearization; empirical support from NSSO 68th round (2011‑12) showing 68 % nuclear households in urban India.
  • Feminist critique (U. Sharma, 1999): joint family masks patriarchal control; domestic violence data (NFHS‑5, 2019‑21) reveals 30 % ever‑married women experienced spousal violence.

Education

Education functions as a mechanism of cultural transmission and credentialism. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE) operationalises Article 21A. ASER 2022 reports 73 % enrollment in government schools but only 44 % grade‑3 children can read a grade‑2 text — highlighting the “learning crisis”.

Religion

Religion operates as both a cohesive force (collective conscience) and a site of contestation. The Constitution guarantees freedom of religion (Articles 25‑28) while permitting state intervention for social reform (Article 25(2)(b)). The Supreme Court’s Sabarimala judgment (2018) illustrates judicial mediation of religious practice vs. gender equality.

Economy and Polity

Economic institutions (markets, cooperatives, SHGs) intersect with political institutions (Panchayati Raj, Municipalities). The 73rd/74th Amendments (1992) devolved 29 subjects to local bodies; the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA, 2005) institutionalised wage‑employment as a legal right.

Media

New media reshapes public sphere. The Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended 2008) and the 2021 Intermediary Guidelines regulate digital platforms. The 2022 “Digital India” report notes 830 million internet users, creating a “networked public sphere” where caste‑based hashtags (#DalitLivesMatter) mobilise collective action.

Worked Example: Analysing the Joint Family Decline

Concept: Goode’s nuclearization thesis.
Evidence: Census 2011 – joint families 16 % (rural) vs. 9 % (urban); NSSO 68th round – 68 % nuclear urban households.
Critique: Ignores “modified extended” families where kin live nearby; overlooks caste‑based residential clustering.
Diagram: Flowchart — Industrialisation → Migration → Housing scarcity → Nuclear household → Reduced kin‑support → State welfare substitution.

Examiner Trap

Do not equate “joint family” solely with Hindu law; Muslim personal law recognises Hiba and Waqf property arrangements that produce similar residential patterns. Cite the Shah Bano case (1985) for state‑personal law tension.

Stratification Theories: Classical, Functionalist, Conflict, Intersectional and Indian Empirics

Classical Foundations

  • Marx: Two‑class model (bourgeoisie/proletariat) — surplus value extraction. In India, the “agrarian bourgeoisie” (large landowners) vs. “landless labour” maps onto the 1970s Naxalite zones.
  • Weber: Three‑component stratification — class (market), status (honour), party (power). The “dominant caste” (e.g., Jats in Haryana) combines land‑ownership (class), ritual purity (status), and political clout (party).
  • Durkheim: Organic solidarity — differentiation creates interdependence; anomie arises when normative regulation fails (e.g., rapid urbanisation).

Functionalist Stratification (Davis‑Moore)

Positions requiring rare talent (e.g., surgeons) receive higher rewards to motivate training. Critics (Tumin) argue the theory ignores structural barriers — caste‑based occupational closure prevents Dalits from entering high‑status roles regardless of talent.

Conflict and Neo‑Marxist Extensions

  • Wright’s contradictory class locations: Managers occupy “contradictory” positions — both exploiters and exploited. Indian middle‑management in IT firms exemplifies this.
  • World‑Systems (Wallerstein): Core‑periphery — India as semi‑periphery; IT services export to core, while agrarian sector remains peripheral.

Intersectionality and Caste‑Class‑Gender

Crenshaw’s intersectionality (1989) is operationalised in Indian studies (e.g., Thorat & Attewell, 2007) showing Dalit women earn 30 % less than upper‑caste men with identical education. The 2011 Socio‑Economic and Caste Census (SECC) provides granular data for intersectional analysis.

Avoid treating “caste” as a mere cultural variable; it is a structural stratification system with legal recognition (Article 15(4), 16(4), 335). Cite the Mandal Commission (1980) and the 93rd Amendment (2006) for OBC reservation in higher education.

Indian Social Structure: Caste, Untouchability, Constitutional Safeguards and Contemporary Data

Varna‑Jati Framework

The classical four‑varna scheme (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) overlays thousands of endogamous jatis. The 2011 Census recorded 1,108 Scheduled Castes (SCs) and 705 Scheduled Tribes (STs). The SECC 2011 identified 26.9 % households as SC/ST.

Protective Discrimination: Landmark Judgments

  • Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) — 50 % ceiling, creamy layer exclusion for OBCs.
  • M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006) — “quantifiable data” required for SC/ST promotion reservation.
  • Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018) — creamy layer applies to SC/ST promotion quota.

Contemporary Schemes and Data

  • Stand‑Up India (2016): Bank loans ₹10 lakh‑₹1 crore to SC/ST/women entrepreneurs; 1.2 lakh sanctions by March 2024.
  • Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Gramin (PMAY‑G): 2.9 crore houses sanctioned (2023), 38 % to SC/ST households.
  • National Fellowship for SC/ST (NFSC/NFST): 2,500 fellowships annually for MPhil/PhD.

Worked Example: Using SECC Data in an Answer

Concept: Multidimensional poverty among SCs.
Evidence: SECC 2011 — 45 % SC households deprived in ≥3 indicators (housing, sanitation, education).
Critique: SECC excludes intra‑household gender disparities; NFHS‑5 shows SC women’s anemia 58 % vs. 48 % general.
Diagram: Bar chart — Deprivation dimensions (housing, sanitation, education, nutrition) for SC vs. General.

Examiner Trap

Do not conflate “Scheduled Caste” with “Dalit” in legal answers; the Constitution uses “Scheduled Castes”. The term “Dalit” is sociological, not statutory. Cite Article 341 for the President’s list.

Tribal Societies: Constitutional Safeguards, Forest Rights, Displacement and Development Planning

Fifth and Sixth Schedules

Article 244(1) (Fifth Schedule) applies to “Scheduled Areas” in ten states; Article 244(2) (Sixth Schedule) provides autonomous district councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram. The Governor’s “Tribes Advisory Council” (Fifth Schedule) and the District/Regional Councils (Sixth Schedule) legislate on land, forests, and social customs.

Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)

PESA mandates Gram Sabha consent for land acquisition, mining leases, and management of minor forest produce. Implementation gaps: only 12 % of Gram Sabhas exercised consent (Ministry of Panchayati Raj, 2022).

Forest Rights Act, 2006 (FRA)

Recognises individual and community forest rights (CFR). As of March 2024, 2.1 million titles distributed; 40 % claims pending. The 2019 Supreme Court order (Wildlife Trust of India v. Union of India) directed eviction of “illegal” claimants, later stayed.

Displacement and Rehabilitation

Large dams (Sardar Sarovar, Polavaram) displaced >1 million tribals. The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR) mandates Social Impact Assessment (SIA) and 70 % consent for private projects; 80 % for PPP. The 2015 amendment diluted consent — criticised by the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST).

Worked Example: Analysing FRA Implementation in Odisha

Concept: Community Forest Rights (CFR) as collective tenure.
Evidence: Odisha granted 1.2 lakh CFR titles (2023), covering 1.8 million ha; satellite data shows 12 % reduction in forest fire incidents in CFR areas.
Critique: Bureaucratic delay — average 4.5 years from claim to title; lack of convergence with MGNREGA for livelihood.
Diagram: Flowchart — Claim filing → Gram Sabha verification → SDLC → DLC → Title → Livelihood convergence.

Examiner Trap

Distinguish “Scheduled Areas” (Fifth Schedule) from “Tribal Areas” (Sixth Schedule). The former are declared by the President under Article 244(1); the latter are constitutionally defined in the Sixth Schedule. Confusing them leads to factual error in polity‑sociology crossover questions.

Urban Sociology: Migration, Informal Economy, Slums, Governance and Emerging Policies

Migration Streams

Census 2011 recorded 453 million internal migrants; 2021‑22 Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) estimates 140 million short‑term circular migrants. The “distress migration” from Bundelkhand, Marathwada correlates with rainfall deficit (IMD data).

Informal Economy and Street Vendors

The Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014 mandates Town Vending Committees (TVCs) with 40 % vendor representation. As of 2023, only 18 % of ULBs have functional TVCs (Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs). NSSO 73rd round (2015‑16) shows 81 % non‑agricultural workers in informal employment.

Urban Governance Reforms

  • 74th Amendment (1992): Municipalities, Ward Committees, District Planning Committees.
  • Smart Cities Mission (2015): 100 cities; focus on ICT‑enabled service delivery; criticism — “top‑down” selection, limited participatory budgeting.
  • National Urban Livelihoods Mission (NULM, 2013): Self‑Employment Programme (SEP), Skill Training for Employment Promotion (STEP); 3.2 million beneficiaries by 2023.

Emerging Debates: Platform Work and Gig Economy

Platform workers (Swiggy, Zomato, Uber) lack social security. The Code on Social Security, 2020 extends “gig worker” definition but implementation rules pending. The 2022 NITI Aayog report estimates 7.7 million platform workers by 2029‑30.

Worked Example: Linking Migration to Informal Labour in Mumbai

Concept: Circular migration sustains urban informal sector.
Evidence: TISS 2021 survey — 68 % construction workers in Mumbai are seasonal migrants from Uttar Pradesh/Bihar; average stay 6 months.
Critique: Exclusion from PDS (ration portability only 2022 ONORC rollout); no access to ESI/ICDS.
Diagram: Circular flow — Rural origin → Recruitment contractor → Urban site → Remittance → Rural consumption → Repeat.

Examiner Trap

Do not treat “slum” as a homogeneous category; the Census defines “notified”, “recognised”, and “identified” slums with different legal statuses. Policy answers must specify which category the scheme targets (e.g., PMAY‑U covers “eligible slum households” in notified/recognised slums).
