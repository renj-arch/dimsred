1. Management Paper II — HRM, Marketing, Financial Management & Operations

2. Human Resource Management

  • HRM vs HRD: HRM (maintenance, administration) vs HRD (development, growth of people).
  • Job Analysis: Job analysis (process of collecting info about jobs), Job description (duties, responsibilities), Job specification (qualifications, skills, experience required).
  • Recruitment: Internal sources (promotions, transfers, employee referrals) & External sources (advertisements, employment exchanges, campus recruitment, labour contractors).
  • Selection: Application form, preliminary interview, selection tests (intelligence, aptitude, proficiency, personality), interview, reference check, medical examination, final selection.
  • Training & Development: On-the-job (apprenticeship, coaching, job rotation, internship) & Off-the-job (lectures, case studies, role playing, simulation, vestibule training).
  • Performance Appraisal: Methods — Traditional (ranking, paired comparison, graphic rating scale, checklist), Modern (MBO, 360-degree feedback, BARS, Assessment centre).
  • Compensation: Components — Basic pay, Dearness allowance, House rent allowance, Incentives, Fringe benefits (provident fund, gratuity, insurance, medical).
  • Industrial Relations: Trade unions, Collective bargaining, Grievance procedure, Industrial disputes (strike, lockout), Labour laws (Factories Act 1948, Industrial Disputes Act 1947, Minimum Wages Act 1948, Payment of Wages Act 1936, Employees' Provident Fund Act 1952, Employees' State Insurance Act 1948).

3. Marketing Management

  • Marketing Concept: Production concept, Product concept, Selling concept, Marketing concept, Societal marketing concept.
  • Marketing Mix (4Ps): Product (product mix, product life cycle — introduction, growth, maturity, decline), Price (pricing methods — cost-plus, skimming, penetration, psychological, differential), Place (channels of distribution — direct, indirect, intensive/selective/exclusive distribution), Promotion (promotion mix — advertising, personal selling, sales promotion, public relations, direct marketing).
  • STP Framework: Segmentation (geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioural), Targeting (undifferentiated, differentiated, concentrated), Positioning (positioning statement, perceptual mapping).
  • Consumer Behaviour: Buying decision process (need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, post-purchase behaviour), Factors affecting consumer behaviour (cultural, social, personal, psychological).
  • Marketing Research: Marketing research process (define problem, develop research plan, collect information, analyse information, present findings), Marketing Information System (MIS).

4. Financial Management

  • Financial Management: Meaning, scope, objectives (profit maximisation vs wealth maximisation), finance functions (investment, financing, dividend, liquidity decisions).
  • Capital Budgeting: Techniques — Payback period, Accounting rate of return, Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Profitability Index.
  • Capital Structure: Capital structure (mix of debt and equity), Cost of capital (cost of debt, cost of equity, WACC), Leverage (operating leverage, financial leverage, combined leverage), Capital structure theories (Net income approach, Net operating income approach, Traditional approach, MM approach).
  • Working Capital Management: Working capital (gross & net), Factors affecting working capital, Cash management (motives for holding cash, cash management), Receivables management (credit policy, credit terms, collection policy), Inventory management (EOQ, ABC analysis, JIT).
  • Dividend Policy: Dividend policy factors (earnings stability, cash flow position, growth opportunities, legal constraints, shareholder preferences), Dividend models (Walter's model, Gordon's growth model, MM hypothesis), Bonus shares & Stock split.

5. Production & Operations Management

  • Production Planning & Control: Production planning (routing, scheduling, dispatching, follow-up), Production control (dispatching, expediting, inspection, corrective action).
  • Inventory Management: Inventory costs (ordering cost, carrying cost, shortage cost), EOQ (Economic Order Quantity = √(2DS/H)), ABC analysis, JIT (Just-in-Time), MRP (Material Requirements Planning).
  • Quality Management: Quality control (inspection, statistical quality control), TQM (Total Quality Management), Six Sigma (DMAIC — Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control), ISO 9000 series, Kaizen.
  • Facility Location & Layout: Factors affecting location (proximity to market, raw materials, labour, transportation, utilities), Layout types (process layout, product layout, fixed position layout, combination layout).
  • Project Management: PERT (Program Evaluation & Review Technique), CPM (Critical Path Method), Critical path, Float/Slack, Crashing.
Technique: EOQ Calculation
Question: Annual demand = 10,000 units, Ordering cost = ₹100 per order, Holding cost = ₹5 per unit per year. Calculate EOQ.
Answer: EOQ = √(2DS/H) = √(2 × 10,000 × 100 / 5) = √(200,000) ≈ 447 units. Annual ordering cost = (D/Q) × S = (10,000/447) × 100 ≈ ₹2,237. Annual holding cost = (Q/2) × H = (447/2) × 5 ≈ ₹1,117.50.

Memory Tricks

  • POSDCORB: Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing, Coordinating, Reporting, Budgeting.
  • 4Ps of Marketing: Product, Price, Place, Promotion.
  • STP: Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning.

6. Strategic Management: The Resource-Based View (RBV) vs. Positioning Schools

While traditional strategic management often relies on the Industrial Organisation (IO) Paradigm—which assumes that industry structure (Porter's Five Forces) determines profitability and suggests firms should position themselves within that structure—the Resource-Based View (RBV) offers a contrasting, firm-centric perspective. RBV posits that sustainable competitive advantage stems not from the external environment but from internal resources and capabilities that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (VRIN framework). This shift is critical for UPSC answers because it allows candidates to move beyond generic advice like "innovate more" to specific, theoretically grounded arguments about why certain firms succeed while others fail despite similar market conditions.

The distinction is vital when analyzing Indian corporate history. For instance, the decline of many public sector undertakings (PSUs) in sectors like textiles or steel was not merely due to external competition but often a failure to develop unique internal capabilities that could withstand price wars. Conversely, private players like Bajaj Auto or Mahindra & Mahindra succeeded by leveraging specific resources (e.g., R&D in two-wheelers, rural distribution networks) that were difficult for competitors to copy quickly. The VRIN framework provides the analytical lens to dissect these cases: Was a resource valuable? Did it allow the firm to exploit opportunities or neutralize threats? Was it rare among current and potential competitors? Could it be imitated only at high cost? And was there no strategic equivalent substitute?

A critical nuance often missed in standard textbooks is the evolution of RBV into the Dynamic Capabilities framework by David Teece. In fast-changing environments (like India's digital economy), static resources are insufficient. Firms must possess dynamic capabilities—the ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competencies to address rapidly changing environments. For example, a legacy bank with high capital reserves (a valuable resource) may still fail if it lacks the dynamic capability to pivot its IT infrastructure for fintech partnerships. This distinction helps in answering questions about "Make in India" or "Startup India": mere asset creation is not enough; the ecosystem must foster dynamic capabilities for adaptation.

Examiner's trap
Students often confuse Positioning Strategy with Resource-Based Strategy. A common error is stating: "Tata Motors' success in the JLR venture was due to its brand positioning." Correct analysis: The primary driver was the acquisition of a unique resource (Jaguar Land Rover's technology and brand equity) which was rare and hard to imitate, combined with Tata's specific capability to integrate it. Positioning is about where you stand; resources are what enable you to stand there.
Exam-Day Strategy: Diagramming RBV
When asked to compare IO and RBV schools, draw a simple Venn diagram. Left circle: "External Factors" (Porter's 5 Forces, Threat of Entry). Right circle: "Internal Factors" (VRIN Resources, Core Competencies). Overlap: "Strategy Formulation". Label the overlap with "Sustainable Competitive Advantage". This visual instantly clarifies that while IO looks outward, RBV looks inward, and the most robust strategy integrates both.

7. Business Environment: The Legal Framework for Corporate Governance in India

The business environment in India is governed by a complex interplay of statutes designed to balance corporate efficiency with social responsibility. A robust answer here requires moving beyond listing acts to explaining their *impact* on strategy. The Companies Act, 2013 represents a paradigm shift from shareholder primacy to stakeholder capitalism. Key provisions include the mandatory formation of a Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) committee for companies meeting specific thresholds (net worth of ₹500 crore or more, turnover of ₹1,000 crore, or net profit of ₹5 crore in the preceding three financial years). This is not merely compliance; it is strategic resource allocation. Companies must now integrate CSR into their business models, often leading to innovations like "green" supply chains or affordable housing projects that serve as both social good and brand differentiation.

Furthermore, the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) regulations, particularly the Listing Agreement and the Investor Education and Protection Fund Authority (IEPFA) Act, shape the disclosure environment. The requirement for related-party transaction disclosures prevents tunneling of assets to promoters, thereby protecting minority shareholders. In a UPSC answer, linking these legal frameworks to the concept of "Trust Deficit" is crucial. For instance, the Satyam scam highlighted why SEBI's audit oversight and insider trading norms are non-negotiable. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 is another critical element; it introduced a time-bound resolution process for distressed companies, shifting power from creditors to operational turnaround experts (Resolution Professional). This changed the risk calculus for lenders and investors, encouraging stricter credit assessment before lending.

The Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999, replacing the restrictive FERA, liberalised foreign investment flows. The automatic route for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) in most sectors (with sectoral caps) facilitated the entry of global giants like Samsung and Apple into India's manufacturing ecosystem. However, specific restrictions remain (e.g., 100% FDI not allowed in news media or gambling). Understanding these nuances allows a candidate to argue for "Atmanirbhar Bharat" (Self-reliant India) not just as a slogan but as a policy choice involving selective protectionism versus open markets.

Examiner's trap
A frequent mistake is conflating Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) with Corporate Philanthropy. CSR under the Companies Act, 2013 is a legal mandate for eligible companies to spend at least 2% of average net profits. Philanthropy is voluntary. In an answer, specify that CSR funds must be spent on specific activities (education, environment, rural development) and reported separately in financial statements, whereas philanthropy has no statutory reporting requirement. This distinction proves deep conceptual clarity.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "So What?" Test
When describing a business law (e.g., FEMA or IBC), always add a sentence starting with "This implies..." or "Consequently...". For example, after stating the IBC provisions, write: "Consequently, banks now conduct stricter due diligence during loan disbursement to avoid future insolvency proceedings." This connects dry legal facts to real-world business strategy, which examiners reward.

8. Financial Management: Capital Structure Theories and Their Practical Limitations

The Modigliani-Miller (MM) theorem is the cornerstone of capital structure theory. MM Proposition I (1958), assuming no taxes, bankruptcy costs, or asymmetric information, states that a firm's value is independent of its capital structure. In other words, whether a firm is financed by debt or equity does not change its total market value. This counter-intuitive result was revolutionary but relied on unrealistic assumptions. MM Proposition II introduced the concept of leverage, stating that the cost of equity increases with financial leverage due to higher risk for shareholders.

The introduction of corporate taxes changed the narrative. With taxes, debt becomes advantageous because interest payments are tax-deductible (creating a "tax shield"), while dividends on equity are not. This led to MM Proposition II with taxes: the value of a levered firm equals the value of an unlevered firm plus the present value of the tax shield. However, this logic ignores bankruptcy costs. As debt increases, the probability of financial distress rises, leading to direct costs (legal fees) and indirect costs (lost sales, customer defection).
