1. Management Paper I — Management Concepts & Organisational Behaviour

2. Management: Nature, Scope & Functions

  • Definition: Management is the process of planning, organising, leading, and controlling resources to achieve organisational goals efficiently and effectively.
  • Functions of Management: Planning (setting objectives, deciding course of action), Organising (grouping activities, assigning resources), Staffing (recruitment, selection, training), Directing (leading, motivating, communicating), Controlling (monitoring performance, taking corrective action).
  • Levels of Management: Top-level (strategic, board/CEO), Middle-level (department heads, implementation), First-line/supervisory (supervisors, day-to-day operations).
  • Managerial Skills: Technical skills (specialised knowledge), Human skills (working with people), Conceptual skills (abstract thinking, seeing the organisation as a whole).

3. Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

  • 1. Division of Work — specialisation increases efficiency
  • 2. Authority & Responsibility — authority brings responsibility
  • 3. Discipline: obedience, respect for authority
  • 4. Unity of Command: one employee, one boss
  • 5. Unity of Direction: one head, one plan for a group
  • 6. Subordination of Individual Interest: general interest over individual interest
  • 7. Remuneration: fair pay for both employee and employer
  • 8. Centralisation: degree to which authority is concentrated
  • 9. Scalar Chain: chain of superiors from top to bottom
  • 10. Order: right place for everything and everyone
  • 11. Equity: kindliness and justice
  • 12. Stability of Tenure: stable workforce reduces turnover
  • 13. Initiative: freedom to think out and execute plans
  • 14. Esprit de Corps: team spirit, union is strength

4. Planning

  • Nature: primary function, intellectual process, goal-oriented, pervasive
  • Types: Strategic (long-term, top-level), Tactical (medium-term, middle-level), Operational (short-term, lower-level)
  • Planning Process: Establish objectives → Identify alternatives → Evaluate alternatives → Select alternative → Implement plan → Follow up
  • MBO (Management by Objectives): Peter Drucker — joint goal setting between manager and subordinate, periodic review, focus on results
  • Limitations: Rigidity, time-consuming, costly, inability to predict future accurately

5. Organising

  • Organisational Structures: Line (direct authority), Functional (specialisation), Line & Staff (combination), Matrix (dual reporting), Divisional (product/geography based), Matrix (project + function)
  • Departmentation: Grouping activities into departments (by function, product, territory, customer, process, time)
  • Delegation: Assigning authority and responsibility to subordinates — elements: authority, responsibility, accountability
  • Decentralisation: Dispersal of decision-making authority to lower levels — advantages: quicker decisions, executive development, motivation; disadvantages: lack of uniformity, coordination problems
  • Span of Control: Number of subordinates a supervisor can effectively manage — narrow span (tall structure) vs wide span (flat structure)

6. Staffing

  • Recruitment: Internal (promotions, transfers) & External (advertisements, campus recruitment, employment agencies)
  • Selection: Application, screening, tests, interviews, reference checks, medical examination, selection decision
  • Training & Development: On-the-job (apprenticeship, coaching, job rotation) & Off-the-job (lectures, case studies, simulations)
  • Performance Appraisal: Methods — Graphic rating scale, 360-degree feedback, MBO, BARS (Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales)

7. Directing

  • Leadership Theories: Trait theory (born leaders), Behavioural theories (Ohio State, Michigan studies), Contingency theory (Fiedler's contingency model, Hersey-Blanchard situational leadership)
  • Motivation Theories:
    • Maslow's Hierarchy: Physiological → Safety → Social → Esteem → Self-actualisation
    • Herzberg's Two-Factor: Motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth) vs Hygiene factors (company policy, supervision, salary, interpersonal relations, working conditions)
    • McGregor's Theory X (negative view of employees) vs Theory Y (positive view)
    • Expectancy Theory (Vroom): Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence
    • Equity Theory (Adams): Inputs/outcomes comparison with others
  • Communication: Communication process (sender, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback), Barriers (semantic, psychological, physical, organisational), Grapevine (informal communication)

8. Controlling

  • Control Process: Establish standards → Measure performance → Compare actual with standards → Take corrective action
  • Types: Feedforward (preliminary/feedforward), Concurrent (during), Feedback (after)
  • Techniques: Budgetary control, PERT/CPM, Break-even analysis, ROI (Return on Investment), Management audit
  • Limitations: External factors beyond control, employee resistance, costly, time-consuming
Technique: Maslow's Hierarchy Application
Question: 'How does Maslow's hierarchy apply to employee motivation?'
Answer: Employees progress through levels: physiological (salary), safety (job security), love/belonging (team belonging), esteem (recognition, promotion), self-actualisation (growth, creativity). Once lower needs are met, higher needs become motivators.

9. Strategic Management: From Concept to Corporate Governance

While the functional areas of management—marketing, finance, HR, and operations—are essential for daily execution, strategic management provides the overarching framework that determines an organisation's long-term viability. It is distinct from tactical planning; whereas tactics deal with short-term resource allocation to achieve specific goals, strategy involves defining the fundamental nature of the business, selecting which markets to compete in, and determining how to sustain a competitive advantage over time.

The core mechanism of strategic management is the Strategic Management Process, a cyclical flow that begins with environmental scanning and ends with implementation and evaluation. The process typically follows these logical steps: Environmental Scanning (identifying opportunities/threats), Strategy Formulation (developing mission, vision, objectives, and strategies), Strategy Implementation (organising resources, culture, structure), and Evaluation/Control (measuring performance against goals). This cycle ensures that an organisation remains dynamic and responsive to changes in the external environment.

A critical theoretical underpinning is Porter's Five Forces Model, which analyses the attractiveness of an industry. The five forces are: 1) Threat of new entrants (barriers to entry like capital requirements, economies of scale, switching costs), 2) Bargaining power of suppliers, 3) Bargaining power of buyers, 4) Threat of substitute products or services, and 5) Rivalry among existing competitors. A sixth force, often cited in modern contexts, is the Complementarity (forces that enhance the value of a product). Understanding these forces allows managers to position their firm effectively. For instance, high barriers to entry protect profits; strong buyer power erodes margins.

Another vital framework is SWOT Analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats), which bridges internal capabilities with external realities. Strengths and weaknesses are internal factors (e.g., brand equity, proprietary technology, high employee turnover); opportunities and threats are external (e.g., emerging markets, regulatory changes). The strategic challenge is to leverage strengths to seize opportunities while mitigating weaknesses and defending against threats. This leads to the formulation of generic strategies proposed by Michael Porter: Cost Leadership (being the lowest-cost producer), Differentiation (offering unique products valued by customers), and Focus (targeting a narrow market segment). A firm attempting to pursue all three simultaneously often fails, falling into the "stuck in the middle" trap.

The evolution of strategy has also seen the rise of Resource-Based View (RBV). Unlike Porter's industry-structure approach (which assumes competition is driven by external market forces), RBV argues that sustainable competitive advantage stems from internal resources that are Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Non-substitutable (VRIN). These resources can be tangible (cash, plants) or intangible (brand reputation, organisational culture, patents). For example, Coca-Cola's secret formula is a resource that is rare and difficult to imitate, providing a long-term advantage regardless of industry structure shifts.

In the realm of Corporate Governance, strategy must align with ethical standards and stakeholder expectations. The Stakeholder Theory (R. Edward Freeman) posits that firms should create value for all stakeholders (shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, community), not just shareholders. This contrasts with the traditional Shareholder Primacy model (Friedman Doctrine). Modern governance codes in India and globally now mandate Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) reporting, reflecting a shift from profit-maximisation to sustainable value creation.

Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A) represent a major strategic tool. The primary motives include horizontal integration (reducing competition), vertical integration (controlling supply chain), and conglomerate diversification (spreading risk). However, M&A success rates are notoriously low due to "integration failure"—cultural clashes, loss of key talent, and overpayment. Due diligence is the critical pre-acquisition step to verify financial health and strategic fit.

Examiner's trap
Students often confuse "tactics" with "strategy". Tactics are short-term actions (e.g., a discount sale); strategy is the long-term plan to win in a market. Another common error is applying Porter's Five Forces to a firm's internal problems rather than its external industry environment. Also, do not assume that Cost Leadership always implies low quality; it implies low cost structure achieved through efficiency or scale.
Exam Day Strategy
When answering questions on Strategic Management, start with a clear definition of the concept (e.g., "Strategic management is the process..."). Immediately follow with a diagram (SWOT or Five Forces) to visually anchor your argument. Then, apply the theory to a real-world example (e.g., "Tata Motors' acquisition of Jaguar Land Rover was a vertical/horizontal integration move..."). Conclude by mentioning limitations or recent trends like digital disruption. Always mention VRIN criteria when discussing resources.

10. Human Resource Management: From Selection to Retention

Human Resource Management (HRM) has evolved from an administrative function focused on hiring and firing to a strategic partner in organisational success. The core distinction lies between Traditional Personnel Management (transactional, compliance-oriented, viewing employees as costs) and Modern Strategic HRM (relational, business-partner oriented, viewing human capital as an asset). Strategic HRM aligns HR practices with business goals to drive performance.

The recruitment and selection process is the gateway to this strategic alignment. It involves Talent Acquisition, which includes employer branding and sourcing. The selection process must be valid (predicts job performance) and reliable (consistent results). Key tools include: 1) Application forms, 2) Aptitude tests (numerical, verbal, spatial), 3) Personality tests (Big Five traits: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism), 4) Integrity tests, and 5) Structured interviews (reducing bias). A critical concept here is Person-Job Fit (skills match the role) vs. Person-Organisation Fit (values match the culture). Poor fit leads to early turnover.

Training and Development (T&D) are distinct but related. Training focuses on current job skills (hard skills), while development prepares for future roles (soft skills, leadership). Methods include: On-the-job (OJT) like coaching, mentoring, job rotation, and apprenticeship; Off-the-job like classroom lectures, case studies, role-playing, simulations, and management games. The 70-20-10 Model suggests that learning occurs 70% from experience, 20% from exposure/mentoring, and 10% from formal training. This highlights the importance of creating a learning culture.

Performance Management is no longer just an annual appraisal event but a continuous process. Traditional appraisals often suffered from recency bias (rating based on last month's performance) and halo/horn effects. Modern approaches include: 1) 360-Degree Feedback (feedback from peers, subordinates, supervisors), 2) MBO (Management by Objectives), where goals are set jointly, and 3) BARS (Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales), which use specific behavioural examples to rate performance. The output feeds into reward systems.

Compensation and Rewards must be competitive yet fair. Components include: Direct pay (base salary, bonuses, commissions) and Indirect pay (benefits like health insurance, retirement plans). Non-monetary rewards include recognition, career growth opportunities, and flexible work arrangements. Equity Theory (Adams) states that employees compare their input/output ratio with others; perceived inequity leads to reduced motivation or turnover.

Employee Relations and Retention are critical in the gig economy.
