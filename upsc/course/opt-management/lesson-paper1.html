Management Paper I — Management Concepts & Organisational Behaviour

Management: Nature, Scope & Functions

  • Definition: Management is the process of planning, organising, leading, and controlling resources to achieve organisational goals efficiently and effectively.
  • Functions of Management: Planning (setting objectives, deciding course of action), Organising (grouping activities, assigning resources), Staffing (recruitment, selection, training), Directing (leading, motivating, communicating), Controlling (monitoring performance, taking corrective action).
  • Levels of Management: Top-level (strategic, board/CEO), Middle-level (department heads, implementation), First-line/supervisory (supervisors, day-to-day operations).
  • Managerial Skills: Technical skills (specialised knowledge), Human skills (working with people), Conceptual skills (abstract thinking, seeing the organisation as a whole).

Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

  • 1. Division of Work — specialisation increases efficiency
  • 2. Authority & Responsibility — authority brings responsibility
  • 3. Discipline: obedience, respect for authority
  • 4. Unity of Command: one employee, one boss
  • 5. Unity of Direction: one head, one plan for a group
  • 6. Subordination of Individual Interest: general interest over individual interest
  • 7. Remuneration: fair pay for both employee and employer
  • 8. Centralisation: degree to which authority is concentrated
  • 9. Scalar Chain: chain of superiors from top to bottom
  • 10. Order: right place for everything and everyone
  • 11. Equity: kindliness and justice
  • 12. Stability of Tenure: stable workforce reduces turnover
  • 13. Initiative: freedom to think out and execute plans
  • 14. Esprit de Corps: team spirit, union is strength

Planning

  • Nature: primary function, intellectual process, goal-oriented, pervasive
  • Types: Strategic (long-term, top-level), Tactical (medium-term, middle-level), Operational (short-term, lower-level)
  • Planning Process: Establish objectives → Identify alternatives → Evaluate alternatives → Select alternative → Implement plan → Follow up
  • MBO (Management by Objectives): Peter Drucker — joint goal setting between manager and subordinate, periodic review, focus on results
  • Limitations: Rigidity, time-consuming, costly, inability to predict future accurately

Organising

  • Organisational Structures: Line (direct authority), Functional (specialisation), Line & Staff (combination), Matrix (dual reporting), Divisional (product/geography based), Matrix (project + function)
  • Departmentation: Grouping activities into departments (by function, product, territory, customer, process, time)
  • Delegation: Assigning authority and responsibility to subordinates — elements: authority, responsibility, accountability
  • Decentralisation: Dispersal of decision-making authority to lower levels — advantages: quicker decisions, executive development, motivation; disadvantages: lack of uniformity, coordination problems
  • Span of Control: Number of subordinates a supervisor can effectively manage — narrow span (tall structure) vs wide span (flat structure)

Staffing

  • Recruitment: Internal (promotions, transfers) & External (advertisements, campus recruitment, employment agencies)
  • Selection: Application, screening, tests, interviews, reference checks, medical examination, selection decision
  • Training & Development: On-the-job (apprenticeship, coaching, job rotation) & Off-the-job (lectures, case studies, simulations)
  • Performance Appraisal: Methods — Graphic rating scale, 360-degree feedback, MBO, BARS (Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales)

Directing

  • Leadership Theories: Trait theory (born leaders), Behavioural theories (Ohio State, Michigan studies), Contingency theory (Fiedler's contingency model, Hersey-Blanchard situational leadership)
  • Motivation Theories:
    • Maslow's Hierarchy: Physiological → Safety → Social → Esteem → Self-actualisation
    • Herzberg's Two-Factor: Motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth) vs Hygiene factors (company policy, supervision, salary, interpersonal relations, working conditions)
    • McGregor's Theory X (negative view of employees) vs Theory Y (positive view)
    • Expectancy Theory (Vroom): Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence
    • Equity Theory (Adams): Inputs/outcomes comparison with others
  • Communication: Communication process (sender, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback), Barriers (semantic, psychological, physical, organisational), Grapevine (informal communication)

Controlling

  • Control Process: Establish standards → Measure performance → Compare actual with standards → Take corrective action
  • Types: Feedforward (preliminary/feedforward), Concurrent (during), Feedback (after)
  • Techniques: Budgetary control, PERT/CPM, Break-even analysis, ROI (Return on Investment), Management audit
  • Limitations: External factors beyond control, employee resistance, costly, time-consuming
Technique: Maslow's Hierarchy Application
Question: 'How does Maslow's hierarchy apply to employee motivation?'
Answer: Employees progress through levels: physiological (salary), safety (job security), love/belonging (team belonging), esteem (recognition, promotion), self-actualisation (growth, creativity). Once lower needs are met, higher needs become motivators.

Strategic Management: From Concept to Corporate Governance

While the functional areas of management—marketing, finance, HR, and operations—are essential for daily execution, strategic management provides the overarching framework that determines an organisation's long-term viability. It is distinct from tactical planning; whereas tactics deal with short-term resource allocation to achieve specific goals, strategy involves defining the fundamental nature of the business, selecting which markets to compete in, and determining how to sustain a competitive advantage over time.

The core mechanism of strategic management is the Strategic Management Process, a cyclical flow that begins with environmental scanning and ends with implementation and evaluation. The process typically follows these logical steps: Environmental Scanning (identifying opportunities/threats), Strategy Formulation (developing mission, vision, objectives, and strategies), Strategy Implementation (organising resources, culture, structure), and Evaluation/Control (measuring performance against goals). This cycle ensures that an organisation remains dynamic and responsive to changes in the external environment.

A critical theoretical underpinning is Porter's Five Forces Model, which analyses the attractiveness of an industry. The five forces are: 1) Threat of new entrants (barriers to entry like capital requirements, economies of scale, switching costs), 2) Bargaining power of suppliers, 3) Bargaining power of buyers, 4) Threat of substitute products or services, and 5) Rivalry among existing competitors. A sixth force, often cited in modern contexts, is the Complementarity (forces that enhance the value of a product). Understanding these forces allows managers to position their firm effectively. For instance, high barriers to entry protect profits; strong buyer power erodes margins.

Another vital framework is SWOT Analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats), which bridges internal capabilities with external realities. Strengths and weaknesses are internal factors (e.g., brand equity, proprietary technology, high employee turnover); opportunities and threats are external (e.g., emerging markets, regulatory changes). The strategic challenge is to leverage strengths to seize opportunities while mitigating weaknesses and defending against threats. This leads to the formulation of generic strategies proposed by Michael Porter: Cost Leadership (being the lowest-cost producer), Differentiation (offering unique products valued by customers), and Focus (targeting a narrow market segment). A firm attempting to pursue all three simultaneously often fails, falling into the "stuck in the middle" trap.

The evolution of strategy has also seen the rise of Resource-Based View (RBV). Unlike Porter's industry-structure approach (which assumes competition is driven by external market forces), RBV argues that sustainable competitive advantage stems from internal resources that are Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Non-substitutable (VRIN). These resources can be tangible (cash, plants) or intangible (brand reputation, organisational culture, patents). For example, Coca-Cola's secret formula is a resource that is rare and difficult to imitate, providing a long-term advantage regardless of industry structure shifts.

In the realm of Corporate Governance, strategy must align with ethical standards and stakeholder expectations. The Stakeholder Theory (R. Edward Freeman) posits that firms should create value for all stakeholders (shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, community), not just shareholders. This contrasts with the traditional Shareholder Primacy model (Friedman Doctrine). Modern governance codes in India and globally now mandate Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) reporting, reflecting a shift from profit-maximisation to sustainable value creation.

Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A) represent a major strategic tool. The primary motives include horizontal integration (reducing competition), vertical integration (controlling supply chain), and conglomerate diversification (spreading risk). However, M&A success rates are notoriously low due to "integration failure"—cultural clashes, loss of key talent, and overpayment. Due diligence is the critical pre-acquisition step to verify financial health and strategic fit.

Examiner's trap
Students often confuse "tactics" with "strategy". Tactics are short-term actions (e.g., a discount sale); strategy is the long-term plan to win in a market. Another common error is applying Porter's Five Forces to a firm's internal problems rather than its external industry environment. Also, do not assume that Cost Leadership always implies low quality; it implies low cost structure achieved through efficiency or scale.
Exam Day Strategy
When answering questions on Strategic Management, start with a clear definition of the concept (e.g., "Strategic management is the process..."). Immediately follow with a diagram (SWOT or Five Forces) to visually anchor your argument. Then, apply the theory to a real-world example (e.g., "Tata Motors' acquisition of Jaguar Land Rover was a vertical/horizontal integration move..."). Conclude by mentioning limitations or recent trends like digital disruption. Always mention VRIN criteria when discussing resources.

Human Resource Management: From Selection to Retention

Human Resource Management (HRM) has evolved from an administrative function focused on hiring and firing to a strategic partner in organisational success. The core distinction lies between Traditional Personnel Management (transactional, compliance-oriented, viewing employees as costs) and Modern Strategic HRM (relational, business-partner oriented, viewing human capital as an asset). Strategic HRM aligns HR practices with business goals to drive performance.

The recruitment and selection process is the gateway to this strategic alignment. It involves Talent Acquisition, which includes employer branding and sourcing. The selection process must be valid (predicts job performance) and reliable (consistent results). Key tools include: 1) Application forms, 2) Aptitude tests (numerical, verbal, spatial), 3) Personality tests (Big Five traits: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism), 4) Integrity tests, and 5) Structured interviews (reducing bias). A critical concept here is Person-Job Fit (skills match the role) vs. Person-Organisation Fit (values match the culture). Poor fit leads to early turnover.

Training and Development (T&D) are distinct but related. Training focuses on current job skills (hard skills), while development prepares for future roles (soft skills, leadership). Methods include: On-the-job (OJT) like coaching, mentoring, job rotation, and apprenticeship; Off-the-job like classroom lectures, case studies, role-playing, simulations, and management games. The 70-20-10 Model suggests that learning occurs 70% from experience, 20% from exposure/mentoring, and 10% from formal training. This highlights the importance of creating a learning culture.

Performance Management is no longer just an annual appraisal event but a continuous process. Traditional appraisals often suffered from recency bias (rating based on last month's performance) and halo/horn effects. Modern approaches include: 1) 360-Degree Feedback (feedback from peers, subordinates, supervisors), 2) MBO (Management by Objectives), where goals are set jointly, and 3) BARS (Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales), which use specific behavioural examples to rate performance. The output feeds into reward systems.

Compensation and Rewards must be competitive yet fair. Components include: Direct pay (base salary, bonuses, commissions) and Indirect pay (benefits like health insurance, retirement plans). Non-monetary rewards include recognition, career growth opportunities, and flexible work arrangements. Equity Theory (Adams) states that employees compare their input/output ratio with others; perceived inequity leads to reduced motivation or turnover.

Employee Relations and Retention are critical in the gig economy.

Motivation Theories: Content and Process Perspectives

Motivation theory divides into content theories (what motivates) and process theories (how motivation occurs). Content theories identify specific needs driving behaviour. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs arranges five levels — physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation — proposing that lower-level needs must be substantially satisfied before higher needs become dominant. The hierarchy is not rigid; need satisfaction overlaps and cultural variations exist (collectivist cultures may prioritise social needs over self-actualisation). Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory distinguishes hygiene factors (salary, working conditions, company policy, supervision) whose absence causes dissatisfaction but whose presence does not motivate, from motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, advancement) that create positive satisfaction. A critical examiner trap: hygiene factors are not "unimportant" — they prevent dissatisfaction but cannot generate motivation. McClelland's Acquired Needs Theory identifies three learned needs: Need for Achievement (nAch) — desire for personal accomplishment; Need for Power (nPow) — desire to influence others; Need for Affiliation (nAff) — desire for friendly relationships. High nAch individuals prefer moderate risk, immediate feedback, and personal responsibility — making them effective entrepreneurs but not necessarily top managers who require high nPow (specifically socialised power, not personalised power).

Process theories explain the cognitive mechanisms. Vroom's Expectancy Theory posits that Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence. Expectancy is the belief that effort leads to performance (E→P); Instrumentality is the belief that performance leads to outcomes (P→O); Valence is the value the individual places on the outcome. If any component is zero, motivation collapses. This explains why high rewards fail if employees doubt their ability (low expectancy) or trust the reward system (low instrumentality). Adams' Equity Theory states that individuals compare their input-outcome ratio (effort, skill, time vs. pay, recognition, promotion) with a referent other. Perceived inequity (under-reward or over-reward) creates tension leading to behavioural changes: altering inputs, altering outcomes, distorting perceptions, changing referents, or leaving the field. Locke's Goal-Setting Theory establishes that specific, challenging goals with feedback lead to higher performance than vague "do your best" goals. Goal commitment, task complexity, and self-efficacy moderate this relationship. Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan) differentiates intrinsic motivation (autonomy, competence, relatedness) from extrinsic motivation, warning that extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks (overjustification effect).

Leadership: Trait, Behavioural, Contingency and Contemporary Approaches

Leadership research evolved through four paradigms. Trait theory (1930s-1940s) sought universal leader characteristics — intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, sociability — but failed to identify a definitive trait set; traits predict emergence more than effectiveness. Behavioural theories shifted focus to what leaders do. The Ohio State Studies identified two independent dimensions: Initiating Structure (task-oriented, defining roles) and Consideration (relationship-oriented, mutual trust). The University of Michigan Studies contrasted Employee-Centred vs Job-Centred supervisors. Blake and Mouton's Managerial Grid plotted Concern for Production (1-9) against Concern for People (1-9), identifying five styles; the 9,9 "Team Management" ideal is context-dependent. Contingency theories argue effectiveness depends on matching style to situation. Fiedler's Contingency Model measures leadership style via Least Preferred Co-worker (LPC) score: high LPC = relationship-oriented, low LPC = task-oriented. Situational favourability depends on Leader-Member Relations, Task Structure, and Position Power. The model predicts low-LPC leaders perform best in very favourable or very unfavourable situations; high-LPC leaders excel in moderately favourable situations. Critically, Fiedler assumes style is fixed — leaders must be placed in matching situations or the situation engineered. Hersey-Blanchard Situational Leadership matches four styles (Telling, Selling, Participating, Delegating) to follower readiness (ability + willingness). Path-Goal Theory (House) derives from Expectancy Theory: leaders clarify paths to goals, removing obstacles. Four behaviours — Directive, Supportive, Participative, Achievement-Oriented — are contingent on subordinate characteristics (locus of control, experience) and environmental factors (task structure, formal authority).

Contemporary approaches address transformation and ethics. Transformational Leadership (Burns, Bass) comprises Four I's: Idealised Influence (charisma), Inspirational Motivation, Intellectual Stimulation, Individualised Consideration. It raises followers' maturity and converts self-interest into collective interest. Transactional Leadership relies on Contingent Reward and Management-by-Exception (active/passive). Research shows transformational leadership correlates with higher performance, satisfaction, and organisational citizenship behaviour across cultures. Servant Leadership (Greenleaf) prioritises follower growth, community building, and stewardship — the leader serves first. Authentic Leadership emphasises self-awareness, relational transparency, balanced processing, and internalised moral perspective. Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) Theory focuses on dyadic relationships: leaders form in-groups (high trust, latitude, influence) and out-groups (formal contract compliance). High-quality LMX predicts lower turnover, higher performance. Implicit Leadership Theory recognises that leadership is partly a perception — followers' prototypes shape who they accept as leaders. Cross-cultural research (GLOBE project) identifies culturally endorsed leadership dimensions: Charismatic/Value-Based, Team-Oriented, Participative, Humane-Oriented, Autonomous, Self-Protective — with significant country-cluster variations.

Worked Answer: Applying Contingency Theory
A newly appointed plant manager inherits a workforce with low skill levels, high turnover, and a highly structured assembly-line process. The previous manager used a participative style, resulting in missed targets. Using Fiedler's Contingency Model and Hersey-Blanchard Situational Leadership, diagnose the situation and prescribe the appropriate leadership style.
Fiedler Analysis: Leader-Member Relations = Poor (low trust, high turnover). Task Structure = High (assembly line, standardised). Position Power = Strong (formal authority to hire/fire/reward). This yields Situation Category IV (Poor relations, High structure, Strong power) — moderately favourable. Fiedler predicts a high-LPC (relationship-oriented) leader performs best here. However, if the new manager's LPC is low (task-oriented), the situation must be engineered: improve relations (team building) or reduce power (delegate less) to shift to Category III or V where low-LPC excels.

Hersey-Blanchard Analysis: Follower Readiness = R1 (Unable and Unwilling/Insecure — low skill, low confidence due to turnover). Prescribed Style = S1: Telling (High Task, Low Relationship) — specific instructions, close supervision. As skills develop through training, move to S2 (Selling), then S3 (Participating), finally S4 (Delegating).

Synthesis: Immediate need: Directive/Structuring behaviour (S1, low-LPC actions) to stabilise operations. Parallel investment: Relationship-building (high-LPC orientation) to improve LMX and shift situational favourability. The participative style failed because it matched R3/R4 followers, not R1 reality.
Exam-Day Strategy for Leadership Questions
When a question asks "Which leadership style is best?", never name a single style. Structure the answer: (1) State the contingency premise — effectiveness depends on situational variables. (2) Identify the relevant model (Fiedler, Hersey-Blanchard, Path-Goal). (3) Diagnose the case facts against the model's situational variables. (4) Prescribe the matched style with justification. (5) Mention the limitation (e.g., Fiedler's style rigidity, Hersey-Blanchard's readability assessment difficulty). Always define LPC, Readiness, or Path-Goal behaviours before applying them. Draw the Managerial Grid or Situational Leadership quadrant diagram if space permits.

Organisational Culture and Change Management

Organisational culture, defined by Schein as "a pattern of shared basic assumptions learned by a group as it solved its problems of external adaptation and internal integration," operates at three levels. Artefacts (visible: dress code, office layout, rituals, jargon, stories) are easy to observe but hard to decipher. Espoused Values (stated strategies, goals, philosophies) may not reflect actual behaviour. Basic Underlying Assumptions (unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs about human nature, truth, relationships) are the essence of culture and resist change. The Competing Values Framework (Quinn & Cameron) maps culture on two axes: Flexibility vs Stability, Internal vs External focus, yielding four types: Clan (collaborate, internal, flexible — family-like, mentorship), Adhocracy (create, external, flexible — innovative, entrepreneurial), Market (compete, external, stable — results-driven, competitive), Hierarchy (control, internal, stable — efficient, bureaucratic). No type is universally superior; alignment with strategy and environment determines effectiveness. Strong cultures (widely shared, intensely held values) enhance behavioural consistency and reduce need for formal controls but risk groupthink and resistance to change. Subcultures (departmental, geographic, hierarchical) coexist; countercultures actively oppose core values.

Change management addresses the transition from current to desired state. Lewin's Three-Step Model remains foundational: Unfreeze (create motivation to change by disconfirming present state, psychological safety), Change (cognitive restructuring — new concepts, meanings, standards via identification with role models or scanning environment), Refreeze (stabilise change by integrating into culture, policies, rewards). Kotter's Eight-Step Process operationalises this for large-scale transformation: (1) Establish urgency, (2) Form guiding coalition, (3) Create vision, (4) Communicate vision, (5) Empower broad-based action, (6) Generate short-term wins, (7) Consolidate gains, (8) Anchor in culture. Common failure modes: skipping urgency, under-communicating vision (10x rule), declaring victory too early. The ADKAR Model (Prosci) focuses on individual change: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement — useful for tracking adoption. Resistance to change stems from: individual factors (habit, security, fear of unknown, selective perception), organisational factors (structural inertia, limited focus of change, group inertia, threat to expertise/power/resource allocation). Overcoming resistance: Education & Communication (for misinformation), Participation & Involvement (for commitment), Facilitation & Support (for adjustment problems), Negotiation & Agreement (for powerful losers), Manipulation & Co-optation (last resort), Explicit & Implicit Coercion (speed essential, high stakes). Organisational Development (OD) interventions — Survey Feedback, Process Consultation, Team Building, Intergroup Development, Appreciative Inquiry — apply behavioural science to improve effectiveness. Appreciative Inquiry (Cooperrider) shifts from problem-solving (deficit model) to discovering "what gives life" (4-D cycle: Discovery, Dream, Design, Destiny).

Marketing Management: Strategic Frameworks and Consumer Behaviour

Marketing management follows the STP-DP framework: Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning — Differentiation, Positioning (execution). Segmentation divides heterogeneous markets into homogeneous segments. Bases: Geographic, Demographic, Psychographic (VALS framework: Innovators, Thinkers, Achievers, Experiencers, Believers, Strivers, Makers, Survivors), Behavioural (occasions, benefits, user status, usage rate, loyalty status). Effective segments are Measurable, Accessible, Substantial, Differentiable, Actionable (MASDA). Targeting evaluates segment attractiveness (size, growth, structural attractiveness — Porter's Five Forces applied at segment level) and company objectives/resources. Strategies: Undifferentiated (mass), Differentiated (multiple segments, separate offers), Concentrated (niche), Micromarketing (local, individual). Positioning defines the brand's place in target minds relative to competitors. Perceptual mapping visualises positioning on key attributes. Value Proposition answers "Why choose us?" — Points of Parity (POPs, category essentials) and Points of Difference (PODs, unique, desirable, deliverable). Repositioning becomes necessary when environment shifts (e.g., Old Spice from "grandfather's brand" to "youthful masculinity").

The Marketing Mix (4Ps) operationalises positioning. Product decisions span levels: Core Benefit, Basic Product, Expected Product, Augmented Product, Potential Product. Product Mix dimensions: Width (lines), Length (items), Depth (variants), Consistency (relatedness). Product Life Cycle (Introduction, Growth, Maturity, Decline) dictates strategy shifts — but not all products follow the bell curve (style, fashion, fad cycles differ). Price strategies: Cost-plus, Value-based, Competition-based, Penetration (low initial), Skimming (high initial), Psychological (odd-even, prestige), Product-line (captive, optional, by-product). Price elasticity, cross-elasticity, and income elasticity inform decisions. Place (Distribution) designs channel structure: Direct vs Indirect, Intensive/Selective/Exclusive intensity. Channel conflict (vertical, horizontal, multi-channel) requires management via leadership, contracts, or vertical marketing systems (Corporate, Contractual, Administered). Promotion mixes Advertising, Sales Promotion, Personal Selling, Public Relations, Direct/Digital Marketing. Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC) ensures consistency across touchpoints. The Extended 7Ps for services add People, Process, Physical Evidence — critical for service quality (SERVQUAL dimensions: Reliability, Responsiveness, Assurance, Empathy, Tangibles).

Consumer Behaviour models the decision process: Problem Recognition → Information Search (internal, external) → Evaluation of Alternatives (evoked set, inert set, inept set; compensatory vs non-compensatory rules) → Purchase Decision → Post-Purchase Behaviour (satisfaction, dissonance, disposal). Influences: Cultural (culture, subculture, social class), Social (reference groups, family, roles/status), Personal (age, occupation, lifestyle, personality), Psychological (motivation — Maslow/Herzberg applied, perception — selective exposure/distortion/retention, learning — classical/operant/cognitive, beliefs/attitudes). The Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo) explains persuasion: Central Route (high involvement, strong arguments) vs Peripheral Route (low involvement, cues like attractiveness, celebrity). Brand Equity (Aaker, Keller) comprises Brand Awareness, Perceived Quality, Brand Associations, Brand Loyalty, Proprietary Assets. Keller's Customer-Based Brand Equity Model builds resonance through Salience, Performance/Imagery, Judgments/Feelings, Resonance (loyalty, attachment, community, engagement).

Industrial Relations and Labour Legislation Framework

Industrial Relations (IR) studies the employment relationship among three actors: Employers (management), Employees (trade unions), and Government (state). The Systems Approach (Dunlop) views IR as a sub-system converting inputs (actors, contexts, ideology) into outputs (rules, wages, conditions) via processes (collective bargaining, grievance handling). Trade Unions pursue economic (wages, benefits), non-economic (job security, working conditions), and political objectives. Union structure: Craft (skill-based), Industrial (industry-based), General (all workers), Federations (apex bodies — INTUC, AITUC, HMS, CITU, BMS in India). Union recognition criteria vary: check-off system, verification of membership, Code of Discipline. Collective Bargaining is the core IR process — negotiation between employer and union to determine terms. Forms: Distributive (win-lose, fixed pie), Integrative (win-win, expanding pie), Attitudinal Structuring (relationship building), Intra-organisational (internal consensus). The bargaining zone exists between union's resistance point and management's resistance point. Third-party interventions: Conciliation (facilitation), Mediation (suggestion), Arbitration (binding decision — voluntary or compulsory). Grievance Handling follows a stepped procedure: Shop steward → Supervisor → Department Head → Grievance Committee → Arbitration. Model Grievance Procedure (India) specifies time limits at each stage. Discipline follows the "Hot Stove Rule" (immediate, consistent, impersonal, warning) and principles of natural justice (notice, hearing, evidence, proportionate penalty). Domestic Enquiry must be fair, unbiased, recorded.

India's labour legislation consolidated 29 central laws into Four Labour Codes (2019-2020), not yet fully implemented: (1) Code on Wages, 2019 — universalises minimum wage, timely payment, equal remuneration, bonus; defines "wages" uniformly (50% threshold for allowances). (2) Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — raises threshold for standing orders to 300 workers (from 100), mandates negotiation union (51% membership) or negotiating council, defines strike/lockout notice periods (14 days), establishes Industrial Tribunals. (3) Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 — covers factories, mines, plantations, construction; mandates registration, licensing, welfare facilities, inter-state migrant worker protections (portability of benefits). (4) Code on Social Security, 2020 — universalises social security (EPF, ESI, gratuity, maternity) to gig/platform/unorganised workers; creates Social Security Fund; National/State Boards for unorganised workers. Key ILO Conventions ratified by India: C87 (Freedom of Association), C98 (Right to Organise), C100 (Equal Remuneration), C111 (Discrimination), C138 (Minimum Age), C182 (Worst Forms of Child Labour). Unratified core conventions: C87 (ratified 2024), C98 (ratified 2024 — historic shift). The Contract Labour (Regulation & Abolition) Act, 1970 regulates, not abolishes, contract labour; principal employer liable for welfare if contractor fails. Factories Act, 1948 (subsumed in OSH Code) defined factory (10+ workers with power, 20+ without), regulated health, safety, welfare, working hours (48/week, 9/day), overtime (double wages), annual leave (1 day/20 days).

  • Trap 1: Confusing Herzberg's hygiene factors with "low-level needs" — hygiene factors include salary and supervision, which span Maslow levels; the theory is about dissatisfaction vs satisfaction, not hierarchy.
  • Trap 2: Assuming Fiedler's LPC score can be changed by training — Fiedler explicitly states leadership style is fixed; the remedy is changing the situation or the leader.
  • Trap 3: Equating "strong culture" with "good culture" — strong cultures can be toxic (Enron, Volkswagen); strength refers to consensus and intensity, not content.
  • Trap 4: Applying the Product Life Cycle as a predictive law — PLC describes typical patterns but cannot forecast duration or shape for a specific product; strategic decisions require market data, not curve position alone.
  • Trap 5: Treating the Four Labour Codes as currently fully enforceable — they are passed but implementation depends on state rules, notification dates, and judicial challenges; cite "as enacted" not "as in force" unless specified.
  • Trap 6: Using "collective bargaining" and "negotiation" interchangeably — collective bargaining has legal status, defined parties, statutory outcomes (settlement/award); negotiation is a generic process.
  • Trap 7: Assuming high LMX (Leader-Member Exchange) is always desirable — in-group favouritism can create out-group resentment, reduce organisational citizenship behaviour among out-group, and expose leaders to bias accusations.

Decision Making: Bounded Rationality, Heuristics, and Organisational Choice

Herbert Simon’s critique of the rational-economic model remains the cornerstone of administrative behaviour. The rational model assumes complete information, well-defined preferences, and optimising choice; Simon demonstrated that human cognitive limits and organisational constraints produce bounded rationality. Decision makers satisfice — they search until an alternative meets an aspiration level, then stop. The aspiration level itself adjusts endogenously: repeated failure lowers it; success raises it. This dynamic explains why organisations often persist with suboptimal routines.

Simon identified three decision categories: programmed (repetitive, rule-driven, e.g., reorder points), non-programmed (novel, unstructured, e.g., market entry), and mixed. The shift from programmed to non-programmed decisions as one moves up the hierarchy explains the increasing reliance on judgement and intuition at senior levels. March and Simon extended this with the Garbage Can Model (1972) for organised anarchies — organisations with problematic preferences, unclear technology, and fluid participation. In such settings, decisions emerge from the random intersection of four independent streams: problems, solutions, participants, and choice opportunities. A solution may attach to a problem not because it fits but because both arrive at the same choice opportunity. This model predicts resolution by oversight (problem solved without attention), resolution by flight (participants leave), and decision by garbage can (choice made without solving the problem).

Kahneman and Tversky’s heuristics and biases programme operationalised bounded rationality at the individual level. Availability (judging frequency by ease of recall), representativeness (judging probability by similarity to a prototype), and anchoring-and-adjustment (insufficient adjustment from an initial value) systematically distort probability estimates. In organisations, escalation of commitment (Staw, 1976) — continuing investment in a failing course — arises from self-justification, prospect theory’s loss aversion, and institutional pressures. The Abilene Paradox (Harvey, 1974) describes collective decisions that contradict individual preferences because members misread consensus. Groupthink (Janis, 1972) — concurrence-seeking overriding realistic appraisal — is fuelled by high cohesion, insulation, directive leadership, and lack of impartial procedures. Antidotes include devil’s advocacy, dialectical inquiry, and the Nominal Group Technique (structured silent idea generation followed by round-robin sharing and ranking) which decouples idea generation from social pressure.

At the strategic level, Mintzberg’s modes of strategic decision making — entrepreneurial (vision-driven, centralised), adaptive (reactive, incremental), and planning (systematic, analytical) — map onto environmental hostility, organisational complexity, and top-management cognition. The logical incrementalism (Quinn, 1980) perspective argues that effective strategic decisions evolve through a sequence of small, reversible commitments that build consensus and reduce uncertainty, rather than through a single grand design. This aligns with Lindblom’s muddling through (successive limited comparisons) but adds intentional learning loops.

Power, Politics, and Conflict Management in Organisations

Power is the capacity to influence others’ behaviour against resistance. French and Raven’s five bases of power (1959) remain the standard taxonomy: legitimate (formal authority), reward (control over valued outcomes), coercive (capacity to punish), expert (specialised knowledge), and referent (identification/admiration). Later work added information power (control over access to data) and connection power (access to influential others). Structural power derives from position in the network: centrality (degree, betweenness, closeness), criticality (non-substitutability of function), and coping with uncertainty (Hickson et al., 1971). Subunits gain power by controlling contingencies others cannot manage — e.g., R&D in high-tech firms, finance in capital-intensive ones.

Organisational politics is the use of power to obtain preferred outcomes where choices are ambiguous or contested. Pfeffer’s political framework identifies conditions that intensify politics: resource scarcity, non-routine technology, goal ambiguity, and external turbulence. Political tactics include controlling the agenda, building coalitions, co-opting opposition, using outside experts to legitimise preferred options, and managing impressions. The Iron Law of Oligarchy (Michels) suggests that even democratic organisations develop elite control over information and decision premises.

Conflict is perceived divergence of interests. The traditional view treated conflict as dysfunctional; the interactionist view (Robbins) distinguishes functional conflict (supports goals, stimulates innovation) from dysfunctional conflict (hinders performance). Thomas-Kilmann Instrument maps five conflict-handling modes on two dimensions — assertiveness and cooperativeness: competing (high assertiveness, low cooperativeness), collaborating (high both), compromising (moderate both), avoiding (low both), accommodating (low assertiveness, high cooperativeness). No single mode is universally optimal; effectiveness depends on issue importance, relationship importance, time pressure, and power balance. Pondy’s conflict process model (1967) traces five stages: latent (conditions for conflict exist), perceived (parties aware), felt (emotional involvement), manifest (overt behaviour), aftermath (resolution or suppression shaping future latent conditions).

Third-party intervention ranges from consultation (process help) to mediation (suggesting solutions) to arbitration (imposing settlement). Integrative bargaining (expanding the pie) requires separating people from the problem, focusing on interests not positions, inventing options for mutual gain, and using objective criteria (Fisher & Ury). Distributive bargaining (fixed pie) relies on anchoring, concession patterns, and resistance points. The Negotiator’s Dilemma — tension between value creation and value claiming — is resolved by contingent contracts (bets on future events) and post-settlement settlements (improving the deal after agreement).

Case Analysis Technique
A mid-sized IT services firm faces a conflict between the Sales unit (pushing for customised solutions to win large deals) and the Delivery unit (insisting on standardised offerings to protect margins and utilisation). Sales argues customisation is essential for competitive differentiation; Delivery argues it creates unmanageable scope creep. The CEO has asked you, as a consultant, to diagnose the conflict and recommend a resolution process. Identify the conflict type, structural sources, and a step-by-step intervention plan using the frameworks above.
Diagnosis: This is a subunit conflict rooted in differentiation (Lawrence & Lorsch) — Sales and Delivery have different time horizons, goal orientations, and task structures. Structural sources: (1) Resource interdependence — Sales needs Delivery’s capacity; Delivery needs Sales’ revenue. (2) Goal incompatibility — Sales incentivised on revenue/bookings; Delivery on margin/utilisation. (3) Ambiguous jurisdiction — who defines scope boundaries? (4) Uncertainty — custom deals have unpredictable effort. Conflict type: Currently perceived/felt (Pondy); risk of escalation to manifest (sabotage, blame). Intervention Plan: 1. Data-driven joint diagnosis: Map the last 20 deals — customisation level vs. margin variance vs. client satisfaction. 2. Reframe as integrative bargaining: Shared interest = profitable growth. Invent options: tiered customisation menu (standard core + configurable modules), pricing premium for custom work, dedicated “custom delivery” pod with separate P&L. 3. Structural realignment: Create a Solution Architect role (boundary spanner) reporting to COO, accountable for scoping feasibility pre-sale. 4. Incentive alignment: Add margin component to Sales variable pay; add revenue-growth component to Delivery bonus. 5. Process institutionalisation: Mandatory pre-sale technical review gate (Thomas-Kilmann collaborating mode). 6. Review at 90 days: Track custom-deal margin, cycle time, win rate. Escalation path: CEO as arbitrator only if joint team deadlocks.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a case asks for “diagnosis and intervention”, structure your answer in three labelled paragraphs: (1) Conflict Diagnosis (type, stage, structural sources), (2) Theoretical Lens (cite Pondy, Thomas-Kilmann, Lawrence & Lorsch, Hickson), (3) Intervention Plan (sequenced steps with rationale). Examiners reward explicit framework naming over generic advice. Always link the intervention to the diagnosed source — e.g., if the source is goal incompatibility, the fix must alter incentives or create superordinate goals.

Organisational Structure and Design: Contingency Approaches and Emerging Forms

Organisational structure defines how tasks are divided, grouped, and coordinated. The contingency thesis asserts that no single structure is universally optimal; effectiveness depends on fit with contextual variables. Burns and Stalker (1961) contrasted mechanistic (rigid hierarchy, vertical communication, centralised decisions) with organic (fluid roles, lateral communication, decentralised) structures, linking mechanistic to stable environments and organic to turbulent ones. Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) introduced differentiation (differences in subunit orientation and structure) and integration (quality of collaboration across subunits). High-performing firms in uncertain environments achieve high differentiation and high integration via integrating devices: liaison roles, task forces, cross-functional teams, and integrating departments.

Mintzberg’s configurational approach (1979) identifies five structural archetypes, each a coherent cluster of design parameters: (1) Simple Structure — direct supervision, strategic apex dominant, minimal technostructure; typical of young, small firms. (2) Machine Bureaucracy — standardisation of work processes, technostructure dominant, highly formalised; fits mass production, stable environments. (3) Professional Bureaucracy — standardisation of skills, operating core dominant (professionals), decentralised; hospitals, universities, law firms. (4) Divisionalised Form — standardisation of outputs, middle line dominant, market-based divisions; large, diversified corporations. (5) Adhocracy — mutual adjustment, selective decentralisation, low formalisation; innovative organisations (R&D labs, film crews, consulting firms). Two subtypes: operating adhocracy (project-based) and administrative adhocracy (expert-driven).

Galbraith’s Star Model (1995) treats structure as one of five design policies — strategy, structure, processes, rewards, people — that must be internally consistent. Matrix structure overlays a second dimension (product, geography, customer) on the functional hierarchy, creating dual reporting. It achieves resource efficiency (shared functional pools) and focus (project/product accountability) but introduces power struggles, ambiguity, and decision slowness. Success conditions: balanced power, collaborative culture, mature conflict resolution, and clear role definitions. Network structure (Miles & Snow, 1986) disaggregates the value chain; the core firm coordinates a web of alliances, outsourcing non-core activities. It maximises flexibility and focus but creates coordination costs, partner dependency, and knowledge leakage risks. Virtual organisation takes this to the extreme — geographically dispersed, ICT-mediated, often project-based with no permanent physical headquarters.

Holacracy (Robertson, 2007) and Teal organisations (Laloux, 2014) represent radical decentralisation: authority distributed to self-organising circles/roles, dynamic governance processes, and evolutionary purpose. Empirical evidence is mixed; they thrive in knowledge-intensive, low-asset-specificity settings but struggle with scale, legal accountability, and external stakeholder expectations. Ambidextrous organisation (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2004) structurally separates exploitation units (efficiency, incremental innovation) from exploration units (experimentation, radical innovation) while linking them through a shared senior team and common culture. This resolves the innovation dilemma without requiring the whole firm to be organic.

Communication: Process, Networks, Barriers, and Organisational Effectiveness

Communication is the transfer and understanding of meaning. The Shannon-Weaver model (1949) — sender, encoding, channel, decoding, receiver, noise, feedback — remains the engineering baseline. Schramm’s interactional model adds the field of experience overlap between sender and receiver; meaning resides in the intersection. Berlo’s SMCR model (Source-Message-Channel-Receiver) details factors at each component: communication skills, attitudes, knowledge, social system, culture for source/receiver; code, content, treatment, structure for message; seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, tasting for channel.

Formal communication follows the authority chain: downward (instructions, policies), upward (reports, grievances, suggestions), horizontal (coordination), diagonal (cross-functional). Informal communication — the grapevine — operates through social networks. Davis identified four grapevine chains: single strand (A→B→C), gossip (one-to-many), probability (random), cluster (trusted circle). Research shows 70-90% of grapevine information is accurate; it fills gaps left by formal channels, serves as a sentiment barometer, and can be managed by feeding accurate information to central nodes.

Communication networks in small groups (Bavelas, Leavitt): Wheel (centralised, fast for simple tasks, low morale), Chain (hierarchical, moderate speed/accuracy), Circle (decentralised, slower, higher satisfaction), All-Channel (fully connected, best for complex tasks, highest satisfaction). Centrality predicts influence and information control. Media Richness Theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986) ranks channels by capacity to process ambiguous information: face-to-face > video conference > telephone > email > formal documents. Rich media (multiple cues, immediate feedback, personal focus, language variety) suit equivocal situations (strategy, conflict); lean media suit uncertain but analyzable situations (data transmission). Social Presence Theory and Media Synchronicity Theory refine this for digital contexts.

Barriers are categorised as: semantic (jargon, ambiguity), psychological (filtering, selective perception, emotional blocks, defensiveness), organisational (hierarchy levels, structural complexity, information overload, climate), physical (noise, distance, medium limitations), cross-cultural (high-context vs low-context cultures, power distance, non-verbal norms). Johari Window (Luft & Ingham) maps self-disclosure and feedback to expand the open area (known to self and others), reducing blind spots and hidden agendas. Active listening (paraphrasing, reflecting feelings, summarising) and non-violent communication (observation, feeling, need, request) are teachable skills that reduce distortion.

Organisational Development: Diagnostic Models and Intervention Strategies

Organisational Development (OD) is a planned, systematic, long-range effort to improve organisation effectiveness and health through planned interventions in the organisation’s processes, using behavioural-science knowledge. Lewin’s Three-Step Model — Unfreeze (create motivation to change), Change (cognitive restructuring, new behaviours), Refreeze (stabilise new patterns) — underpins the Action Research Model (diagnosis, feedback, planning, intervention, evaluation). Burke-Litwin Causal Model (1992) distinguishes transformational factors (mission, strategy, leadership, culture) from transactional factors (structure, systems, management practices, climate) and traces their causal links to individual and organisational performance. Weisbord’s Six-Box Model (purposes, structure, relationships, rewards, leadership, helpful mechanisms) and Nadler-Tushman Congruence Model (fit among work, people, structure, culture) are diagnostic frameworks for identifying misalignments.

OD Interventions are classified by target level: Individual (coaching, counselling, training, career planning), Group/Team (team building, process consultation, intergroup dialogue, conflict resolution), Organisation-wide (survey feedback, structural redesign, culture change, strategic planning, large-group interventions like Future Search, Open Space Technology, Appreciative Inquiry). Appreciative Inquiry (Cooperrider & Srivastva, 1987) follows the 4-D cycle: Discovery (what gives life), Dream (what might be), Design (what should be), Destiny (what will be). It shifts focus from problem-solving to possibility-generating.

Process Consultation (Schein) differs from expert consultation: the consultant helps the client perceive, understand, and act on process events (communication, roles, norms, leadership) rather than prescribing content solutions. Survey Feedback (Mann, Likert) collects perceptual data, feeds it back to the generating units, and facilitates action planning. Critical success factors: anonymity, relevance, ownership, follow-through. Large-Scale Interventions engage the whole system in real time. Future Search brings 60-80 stakeholders for 2.5 days to explore past, present, future, and common ground. Open Space lets participants self-organise agenda around a theme. Real-Time Strategic Change combines large-group dialogue with structured design.

Evaluation uses Kirkpatrick’s four levels (reaction, learning, behaviour, results) plus ROI (Phillips). Ethical dilemmas in OD include: informed consent vs. organisational mandate, data confidentiality vs. management access, depth of intervention vs. client readiness, and consultant’s own values. The OD Practitioner’s Code (OD Network) emphasises client autonomy, confidentiality, competence, and systemic responsibility.

  • Trap 1: Confusing bounded rationality with irrationality — satisficing is adaptive, not defective; the rational model is the benchmark, not the description.
  • Trap 2: Treating French & Raven’s five bases as mutually exclusive — power holders typically combine bases; referent and expert power are most durable because they do not depend on position.
  • Trap 3: Assuming matrix structure is “better” than functional — matrix solves specific coordination problems but creates new ones; the contingency question is fit, not fashion.
  • Trap 4: Equating grapevine with rumour — grapevine is a structural network; rumour is unverified content that can travel on any channel.
  • Trap 5: Using Appreciative Inquiry as a synonym for “positive thinking” — AI is a rigorous, data-based methodology with defined phases, not merely optimism.
  • Trap 6: Citing Lewin’s three steps as a complete change model — it is a high-level metaphor; operational models (Kotter’s 8 steps, ADKAR, Burke-Litwin) provide actionable detail.
  • Trap 7: Assuming media richness implies “always use face-to-face” — lean media are superior for routine, unambiguous communication because they reduce overload and create audit trails.
