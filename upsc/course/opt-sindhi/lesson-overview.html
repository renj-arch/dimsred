Sindhi Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Sindhi is Indo-Aryan and is written in Perso-Arabic script (also Devanagari in India). It is spoken mainly in Sindh (now Pakistan) and the Sindhi diaspora in India. It was added to the Eighth Schedule by the 21st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1967. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Sufi poetry, Sindhi renaissance, Progressive movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: Sufi interpretation, traditional rasa criticism, progressive and feminist approaches
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Early period (16th–18th c.): Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Sufi poetic tradition.
  • Nineteenth–early twentieth century: Print, prose and the Sindhi renaissance.
  • Modern period (20th c.–present): Partition literature, fiction, poetry and diaspora writing.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Sindhi literature.
Answer: Early period (16th–18th c.) — Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Sufi poetic tradition.; Nineteenth–early twentieth century — Print, prose and the Sindhi renaissance.; Modern period (20th c.–present) — Partition literature, fiction, poetry and diaspora writing..

Standard Sources

  • Sahitya Akademi histories of Sindhi literature
  • Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature
  • University syllabi and prescribed texts
  • Standard Sindhi literary histories

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Sindhi authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Sindhi literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Comparative Analysis: The Sufi vs. Progressive Dialectic

While standard texts outline the transition from Sufi to Progressive literature, a high-scoring answer must demonstrate the ability to analyze the tension between these movements rather than just listing them. The examiner often asks questions that require you to identify where a text defies its expected genre classification.

Mechanism of Analysis: Do not treat Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Progressive writers as isolated entities. Instead, analyze the continuity of social critique. For instance, while Bhittai used Sufi metaphors to critique feudalism (e.g., in Suhag-e-Mah), progressive writers like Mirza Yusuf Hashmi or Ghulam Farid used direct political satire. The advanced strategy is to trace the evolution of the "voice" from mystical allegory to secular realism.

Exception Case: A crucial exception to note is the work of Mir Ali Shah Wali. His poetry often straddles the line between traditional Sufi mysticism and early social reform, serving as a bridge before the full-blown renaissance. Mentioning such transitional figures shows depth beyond the standard syllabus.

Technique: The Comparative Bridge
Question: "Critically examine the shift in thematic focus from Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai to Ghulam Farid. Is it a complete break or an evolution?"
Solution:
The shift is not a rupture but an evolution of the social conscience. Bhittai's critique was embedded in the metaphysical realm (e.g., the 'Bhagat' vs. 'Sufi' dichotomy), whereas Ghulam Farid's Khayal collection explicitly targets the caste system and feudal exploitation with secular language.

Key Argument: The underlying mechanism of empathy for the oppressed remains constant, but the medium changes from divine allegory to humanist realism. This evolution mirrors the broader Indian literary shift from medieval to modern sensibilities.

The Diaspora Discourse: Identity in Exile

Standard sources often focus on pre-1947 and post-Partition literature within Sindh or Pakistan. However, a significant portion of contemporary Sindhi literature exists in the Indian diaspora (Gujarat, Mumbai, Delhi). The examiner may ask about the "Diaspora Syndrome"—how displacement alters literary form.

Applied Scenario: When analyzing texts by authors like Muhammad Iqbal (in his Sindhi context) or contemporary poets in Ahmedabad, look for themes of linguistic preservation. The use of the Perso-Arabic script in India became a political statement against Hindi imposition. Your answer should highlight how language policy influenced literary production.

Data Point: Note the surge in Sindhi fiction publishing in Ahmedabad and Mumbai post-1971 (Operation Black Watch). This data point is essential for answering questions on "Contemporary Trends." It shifts the center of gravity from Karachi to Indian cities, a fact often missed in general histories.

Technique: Contextualizing Displacement
Question: "Discuss the impact of the 1971 Partition on Sindhi fiction. How did the 'refugee' experience change the narrative voice?"
Solution:
The 1971 partition forced a re-evaluation of identity. Pre-1947 literature was often rooted in agrarian Sindh; post-1971 fiction shifted to urban displacement, loss of homeland, and the struggle for linguistic rights.

Narrative Voice: The tone changed from celebratory or mystical to melancholic and assertive. Authors began using the term 'Muhajir' not just as a demographic label but as a literary identity marker. This requires citing works that explicitly deal with the trauma of uprooting, moving beyond mere plot summaries.

Critical Evaluation: Beyond Plot Summary

In Paper II, students frequently fail by providing detailed plot summaries of prescribed texts (e.g., Jawab-e-Khat or Khwahish). The examiner explicitly penalizes this. You must apply literary criticism frameworks.

Mechanism: Use the structure: Context → Theme → Form/Language → Critical Evaluation.
1. Context: Historical backdrop (e.g., British Raj, Partition).
2. Theme: Central idea (e.g., love, rebellion, exile).
3. Form: Poetic meter (Bait-e-Mukhannas vs. Nazm), prose style.
4. Critical Evaluation: Does the work challenge norms? How does it reflect the Zeitgeist?

Landmark Case: When analyzing Suhag-e-Mah, do not just say "it is about love." Analyze how Bhittai uses the metaphor of the 'Mah' (queen) to represent the ideal society and the 'Bhagat' as the corrupt elite. This moves from summary to interpretation.

Technique: The Critical Lens
Question: "Analyze the use of symbolism in Bhittai's 'Suhag-e-Mah'. Is it purely romantic?"
Solution:
The symbolism is socio-political. The 'Mah' represents a utopian society where justice prevails, contrasting with the 'Bhagat' who represents feudal oppression.

Critical Evaluation: The romance is secondary to the social critique. Bhittai uses the personal relationship to allegorize the state of the nation. A high-scoring answer would cite specific verses where the beauty of nature reflects the purity of the ideal society, contrasting it with the decay of human institutions.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Script" Advantage
In Paper I, if asked about the language history, always mention the script debate. Mentioning that Sindhi is written in Perso-Arabic script (with Devanagari as a secondary standard in India) and citing the 21st Constitutional Amendment Act adds immediate technical precision. If you quote a verse, write it in the original script (if you know it) or provide the transliteration with an English gloss. Never translate a poem line-by-line without analyzing its meter; instead, paraphrase the sentiment while retaining the core metaphor.
  • Trap: Treating "Sindhi Literature" as synonymous only with Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai. This ignores the vast corpus of prose, modern poetry, and fiction.
  • Trap: Confusing the dates of the Sufi movement (16th-18th c.) with the Renaissance (late 19th-early 20th c.). Always specify centuries to avoid ambiguity.
  • Trap: Ignoring the script question. In Paper I, failing to mention the Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari debate is a significant oversight given the constitutional context.
  • Trap: Providing plot summaries for Paper II texts instead of critical analysis. The examiner wants your interpretation, not a story recap.
  • Trap: Quoting translations as originals without attribution. Always state "As translated by [Name]" or "Paraphrased from [Text Name]" to maintain academic integrity.

Script Politics and Standardization: The 1948–1968 Constitutional Debate

The script question in Sindhi is not merely orthographic; it is a site where language planning, minority rights, and post-Partition identity politics converge. In 1948, the Government of Pakistan adopted the Perso-Arabic script (with diacritical modifications by the Sindhi Adabi Board) as the official standard for Sindhi in West Pakistan. Simultaneously, in India, the 1951 Census recognized Sindhi as a mother tongue but left the script unresolved. The 1967–1968 agitation led by the Sindhi Language Committee under the chairmanship of L. H. Ajwani culminated in the 21st Constitutional Amendment Act (1967), which inserted Sindhi into the Eighth Schedule. The amendment text did not prescribe a script. The 1968 Government of India notification (Resolution No. 1/13/67-O.L.) subsequently recognized both Perso-Arabic and Devanagari as official scripts for Sindhi in India. This dual-script policy created a bifurcated print culture: textbooks in Maharashtra and Gujarat used Devanagari, while literary journals in Mumbai and Delhi (e.g., Naon Zindagi, Sindhu) persisted with Perso-Arabic. For Paper I, the examiner expects you to trace how the 1948 Karachi resolution, the 1954 Report of the Sindhi Language Committee (Ajwani Report), and the 1968 notification constitute a triad of state interventions. You must also note the 2001 Gujarat government order mandating Devanagari for primary education, which triggered litigation and a 2004 Gujarat High Court stay. The script debate resurfaced in the 2011 Census when enumerators in Gujarat recorded Sindhi speakers under "Gujarati" if they declared Devanagari literacy, a methodological flaw that depressed the official speaker count. In answers, cite the specific notification numbers and the distinction between script recognition (administrative) and script imposition (pedagogical).

Folk Forms as Literary Architecture: Bait, Kafi, Vaee, and the Metrics of Oral Transmission

Paper I requires technical command over the indigenous prosodic forms that structure classical and folk poetry. The Bait is a quatrain (four hemistichs) with a fixed rhyme scheme aaba or aaaa, each hemistich carrying 16–18 matras (morae) divided by a caesura after the 8th or 9th matra. Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's Shah Jo Risalo deploys 30 Surs (melodic modes), each anchored to a specific Bait meter. The Kafi, by contrast, is a strophic form of 4–8 couplets (bait in the generic sense) with a refrain (radeef) and a rhyme (qafiya) inherited from the Punjabi-Sindhi Sufi continuum; Sachal Sarmast's Kafis often open with a matla (opening couplet) that establishes the qafiya-radeef pattern for the entire poem. The Vaee (or Vai) is a non-strophic, free-verse folk form used in Lada (wedding songs) and Dohira (dialogue songs); it lacks fixed meter but obeys a stress-based rhythm suited to the Yaktaro (single-string drone). The Doha (distinct from the Braj doha) in Sindhi is a rhyming couplet of 24+24 matras, used extensively by Sami (1743–1850) for Vedantic exposition. For the examiner, the distinction between Bait-e-Mukhannas (the specific 16-matra quatrain of the Risalo) and the generic bait (couplet) is a frequent trap. You must demonstrate scansion: e.g., the opening line of Sur Kalyan — "Kanwar bhala kare jhulelal" — scans as 4+4+4+4 matras across the caesura. When analyzing modern poets like Narayan Shyam or Arjan Hasid, note their deliberate subversion of Bait meter to accommodate free verse (Azad Nazm), marking the 1950s modernist rupture.

Technique: Scansion as Evidence in Answer Writing
Question: "Analyze how the metrical structure of Bhittai's Bait-e-Mukhannas in Sur Sarang serves the thematic tension between viraha (separation) and union."
Solution: Begin by defining the Bait-e-Mukhannas: a quatrain of 16 matras per hemistich, caesura at 8, rhyme aaba. In Sur Sarang, the opening Bait — "Sarang raag suhagan, man mohan / Surang rang bhayo, piya ghar aaye" — uses the first two hemistichs (aa) to establish the raga's erotic charge, the third (b) to introduce the beloved's arrival, and the fourth (a) to resolve in the tonic rhyme. The caesura at matra 8 forces a breath-pause that mimics the protagonist's held breath in anticipation. Thematic argument: the meter enacts the arc of viraha → anticipation → union within a single stanza. Contrast with the Kafi form in Sachal's "Murshid mana mahi" where the refrain "murshid mana mahi" recurs after every couplet, creating a liturgical circularity that reflects the Sufi doctrine of fana (annihilation) rather than linear narrative. High-scoring answers cite the specific matra count and link prosody to theology.
Exam-Day Strategy: The Prosody Cheat Sheet
Memorize a one-page table: Form | Hemistichs | Matras/Hemistich | Caesura | Rhyme | Key Exponent. For Bait-e-Mukhannas: 4 | 16 | 8 | aaba | Bhittai. For Kafi: 4–8 couplets | variable | none | qafiya-radeef | Sachal. For Vaee: free | stress-based | none | none | Folk. For Doha: 2 | 24 | 12 | aa | Sami. For Nazm: free | syllabic/tonic | none | free | Shyam/Hasid. Reproduce this table in the rough margin; it signals technical mastery before the examiner reads a word of your essay.

The Progressive Writers' Movement in Sindhi: Institutional History and Aesthetic Rupture (1942–1970)

The All-India Progressive Writers' Association (AIPWA) held its first Sindhi session in Karachi in 1942 under the presidentship of Sobho Gianchandani. The movement's institutional backbone in Sindh was the Sindhi Adabi Sangat (founded 1945), which published the journal Nai Dunya (Karachi) and later Sahit (Mumbai). In India, the Mumbai branch led by Gobind Malhi, Krishin Khatwani, and Hiro Thakur organized the 1950 All-India Sindhi Progressive Writers' Conference, adopting a manifesto that explicitly linked literary realism to the "democratic reconstruction of Sindhi society." The aesthetic rupture is visible in three shifts: (1) from the Sufi allegory of ishq-e-haqiqi to the materialist depiction of peasant indebtedness (e.g., Gobind Malhi's novel Zindagi Ji Rah, 1952); (2) from the Bait and Kafi to the Azad Nazm (free verse) and the short story (Afsoono), with Krishin Khatwani's Kirnan (1955) establishing the modern Sindhi short story; (3) from the solitary mystic voice to the collective voice of the mazdoor (worker) and kisan (peasant). The 1954 split — when the Karachi faction aligned with the Communist Party of Pakistan and faced the 1954 Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case repercussions — forced the Mumbai faction to navigate Indian state surveillance while maintaining transnational links. For Paper II, the prescribed texts Jawab-e-Khat (by Gobind Malhi) and Khwahish (by Krishin Khatwani) must be read as programmatic documents: the former uses the epistolary form to stage a dialogue between a migrant father and a son who stayed in Sindh, dramatizing the 1947 rupture; the latter deploys the nazm to articulate the female industrial worker's double exploitation. Examiners reward answers that situate these texts within the 1942–1970 institutional timeline rather than treating them as isolated masterpieces.

Partition Literature: The 1947 vs. 1971 Distinction in Narrative Strategy

Sindhi literature registers two partitions: 1947 (the Radcliffe Award) and 1971 (the Bangladesh Liberation War). The 1947 displacement produced a literature of hijrat (migration) and viraha (separation from the homeland), exemplified in the poetry of Narayan Shyam (Ratna, 1958) and the early fiction of Popati Hiranandani. The narrative strategy is elegiac: the village (gaon) functions as a lost pastoral, and the city (Mumbai, Ahmedabad) appears as a site of alienation. The 1971 partition, by contrast, generated a literature of dubara hijrat (re-migration) and political betrayal. The 1971 exodus from East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) brought a second wave of Sindhi-speaking refugees — primarily Bihari-origin Sindhis and Sindhi-speaking Bengalis — into India. This wave produced texts like Mohan Kalpana's Band Darwazo (1976) and Hashu Kewalramani's reportage Sindh Ji Kahani (1973), where the narrative voice shifts from mourning to indictment: the Pakistani state is accused of linguistic genocide (the 1972 Sindhi Language Bill riots), and the Indian state is critiqued for rehabilitation failures. The formal difference is stark: 1947 texts favor the ghazal and the realist short story; 1971 texts adopt the reportage (ruporj), the polyphonic novel, and the documentary poem. In Paper II, if a question asks about "Partition in Sindhi fiction," you must disaggregate the two events. Cite the 1972 Language Bill (which removed Sindhi as a medium of instruction in Sindh) as the catalyst for the 1971 literary surge. Note that the term Muhajir acquires a second, distinct meaning post-1971: it now denotes the Urdu-speaking migrant in Karachi, while the Sindhi refugee in India reclaims Sindhi as a political identity against both Pakistani centralism and Indian assimilationism.

Women's Writing: From Folk Voices to Modern Autobiography

The syllabus implicitly expects awareness of gendered literary genealogies. The earliest recoverable women's voices are folk: the Lada (wedding songs) and Sehra (bridegroom songs) composed and sung by women in rural Sindh, documented by Nabi Bakhsh Baloch in Sindhi Lok Geet (1962). These forms use the Vaee meter and encode resistance to patriarchal marriage negotiations through coded metaphor. The first named woman prose writer is Husna Bai (fl. 1880s), a courtesan-poet whose Kafis were anthologized in Shah Jo Risalo commentaries. The modern period begins with Popati Hiranandani (1924–2005), whose autobiography Munhinji Hayati (1980) and novel Zindagi (1960) introduce the first-person female subject navigating Partition, widowhood, and literary professionalization. Sundri Uttamchandani (1924–2013) follows with the short story collection Kirandar Deewar (1978), deploying psychological realism to depict the middle-class Sindhi woman's negotiation of domesticity and desire. The 1990s generation — Rita Kothari (translator-scholar), Heloise D'Souza (poet) — shifts to English and Gujarati, raising the question of language attrition. For the examiner, the critical move is to avoid tokenism: do not list names. Instead, analyze how the Lada's collective female voice transforms into Hiranandani's individualized "I" and then into Kothari's translated "we." The 2005 Sahitya Akademi award to Hiranandani (posthumous) and the 2019 award to Kothari for translation mark institutional recognition. In answers, reference the 1987 Sindhi Women Writers' Conference in Mumbai as the first institutional platform, and note the absence of women from the 1942 Progressive Writers' manifesto — a silence that post-1990s criticism (e.g., Kothari's The Burden of Refuge, 2009) treats as a structural omission.

  • Treating the 1968 dual-script notification as a settled consensus; the 2001 Gujarat order and 2004 High Court stay prove the dispute remains live.
  • Confusing Bait-e-Mukhannas (16 matras, caesura at 8, aaba) with the generic bait (couplet) or the Braj doha (24+24 matras).
  • Reading Progressive Writers' texts as purely aesthetic objects without referencing the 1942 Karachi session, the 1954 split, or the Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case.
  • Collapsing the 1947 and 1971 partitions into a single "Partition literature" category; the narrative forms, political targets, and refugee demographics differ fundamentally.
  • Listing women writers chronologically without analyzing the formal shift from collective folk forms (Lada, Vaee) to individualized autobiography (Hiranandani) to translated scholarship (Kothari).
  • Quoting translated verses as if they were the original meter; always specify the translator and note metrical loss or gain in translation.
  • Ignoring the 1972 Sindhi Language Bill riots in Pakistan as a catalyst for the 1970s literary surge in India; this causal link is a standard examiner expectation.
