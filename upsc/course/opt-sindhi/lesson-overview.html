Sindhi Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Sindhi is Indo-Aryan and is written in Perso-Arabic script (also Devanagari in India). It is spoken mainly in Sindh (now Pakistan) and the Sindhi diaspora in India. It was added to the Eighth Schedule by the 21st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1967. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Sufi poetry, Sindhi renaissance, Progressive movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: Sufi interpretation, traditional rasa criticism, progressive and feminist approaches
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Early period (16th–18th c.): Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Sufi poetic tradition.
  • Nineteenth–early twentieth century: Print, prose and the Sindhi renaissance.
  • Modern period (20th c.–present): Partition literature, fiction, poetry and diaspora writing.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Sindhi literature.
Answer: Early period (16th–18th c.) — Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Sufi poetic tradition.; Nineteenth–early twentieth century — Print, prose and the Sindhi renaissance.; Modern period (20th c.–present) — Partition literature, fiction, poetry and diaspora writing..

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Sindhi authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.

  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.

Build a one-page timeline of Sindhi literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Comparative Analysis: The Sufi vs. Progressive Dialectic

While standard texts outline the transition from Sufi to Progressive literature, a high-scoring answer must demonstrate the ability to analyze the tension between these movements rather than just listing them. The examiner often asks questions that require you to identify where a text defies its expected genre classification.

Mechanism of Analysis: Do not treat Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Progressive writers as isolated entities. Instead, analyze the continuity of social critique. For instance, while Bhittai used Sufi metaphors to critique feudalism (e.g., in Suhag-e-Mah), progressive writers like Mirza Yusuf Hashmi or Ghulam Farid used direct political satire. The advanced strategy is to trace the evolution of the "voice" from mystical allegory to secular realism.

Exception Case: A crucial exception to note is the work of Mir Ali Shah Wali. His poetry often straddles the line between traditional Sufi mysticism and early social reform, serving as a bridge before the full-blown renaissance. Mentioning such transitional figures shows depth beyond the standard syllabus.

Technique: The Comparative Bridge
Question: "Critically examine the shift in thematic focus from Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai to Ghulam Farid. Is it a complete break or an evolution?"
Solution:
The shift is not a rupture but an evolution of the social conscience. Bhittai's critique was embedded in the metaphysical realm (e.g., the 'Bhagat' vs. 'Sufi' dichotomy), whereas Ghulam Farid's Khayal collection explicitly targets the caste system and feudal exploitation with secular language.

Key Argument: The underlying mechanism of empathy for the oppressed remains constant, but the medium changes from divine allegory to humanist realism. This evolution mirrors the broader Indian literary shift from medieval to modern sensibilities.

The Diaspora Discourse: Identity in Exile

Standard sources often focus on pre-1947 and post-Partition literature within Sindh or Pakistan. However, a significant portion of contemporary Sindhi literature exists in the Indian diaspora (Gujarat, Mumbai, Delhi). The examiner may ask about the "Diaspora Syndrome"—how displacement alters literary form.

Applied Scenario: When analyzing texts by authors like Muhammad Iqbal (in his Sindhi context) or contemporary poets in Ahmedabad, look for themes of linguistic preservation. The use of the Perso-Arabic script in India became a political statement against Hindi imposition. Your answer should highlight how language policy influenced literary production.

Data Point: Note the surge in Sindhi fiction publishing in Ahmedabad and Mumbai post-1971 (Operation Black Watch). This data point is essential for answering questions on "Contemporary Trends." It shifts the center of gravity from Karachi to Indian cities, a fact often missed in general histories.

Technique: Contextualizing Displacement
Question: "Discuss the impact of the 1971 Partition on Sindhi fiction. How did the 'refugee' experience change the narrative voice?"
Solution:
The 1971 partition forced a re-evaluation of identity. Pre-1947 literature was often rooted in agrarian Sindh; post-1971 fiction shifted to urban displacement, loss of homeland, and the struggle for linguistic rights.

Narrative Voice: The tone changed from celebratory or mystical to melancholic and assertive. Authors began using the term 'Muhajir' not just as a demographic label but as a literary identity marker. This requires citing works that explicitly deal with the trauma of uprooting, moving beyond mere plot summaries.

Critical Evaluation: Beyond Plot Summary

In Paper II, students frequently fail by providing detailed plot summaries of prescribed texts (e.g., Jawab-e-Khat or Khwahish). The examiner explicitly penalizes this. You must apply literary criticism frameworks.

Mechanism: Use the structure: Context → Theme → Form/Language → Critical Evaluation.
1. Context: Historical backdrop (e.g., British Raj, Partition).
2. Theme: Central idea (e.g., love, rebellion, exile).
3. Form: Poetic meter (Bait-e-Mukhannas vs. Nazm), prose style.
4. Critical Evaluation: Does the work challenge norms? How does it reflect the Zeitgeist?

Landmark Case: When analyzing Suhag-e-Mah, do not just say "it is about love." Analyze how Bhittai uses the metaphor of the 'Mah' (queen) to represent the ideal society and the 'Bhagat' as the corrupt elite. This moves from summary to interpretation.

Technique: The Critical Lens
Question: "Analyze the use of symbolism in Bhittai's 'Suhag-e-Mah'. Is it purely romantic?"
Solution:
The symbolism is socio-political. The 'Mah' represents a utopian society where justice prevails, contrasting with the 'Bhagat' who represents feudal oppression.

Critical Evaluation: The romance is secondary to the social critique. Bhittai uses the personal relationship to allegorize the state of the nation. A high-scoring answer would cite specific verses where the beauty of nature reflects the purity of the ideal society, contrasting it with the decay of human institutions.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Script" Advantage
In Paper I, if asked about the language history, always mention the script debate. Mentioning that Sindhi is written in Perso-Arabic script (with Devanagari as a secondary standard in India) and citing the 21st Constitutional Amendment Act adds immediate technical precision. If you quote a verse, write it in the original script (if you know it) or provide the transliteration with an English gloss. Never translate a poem line-by-line without analyzing its meter; instead, paraphrase the sentiment while retaining the core metaphor.

  • Trap: Confusing the dates of the Sufi movement (16th-18th c.) with the Renaissance (late 19th-early 20th c.). Always specify centuries to avoid ambiguity.

Script Politics and Standardization: The 1948–1968 Constitutional Debate

The script question in Sindhi is not merely orthographic; it is a site where language planning, minority rights, and post-Partition identity politics converge. In 1948, the Government of Pakistan adopted the Perso-Arabic script (with diacritical modifications by the Sindhi Adabi Board) as the official standard for Sindhi in West Pakistan. Simultaneously, in India, the 1951 Census recognized Sindhi as a mother tongue but left the script unresolved. The 1967–1968 agitation led by the Sindhi Language Committee under the chairmanship of L. H. Ajwani culminated in the 21st Constitutional Amendment Act (1967), which inserted Sindhi into the Eighth Schedule. The amendment text did not prescribe a script. The 1968 Government of India notification (Resolution No. 1/13/67-O.L.) subsequently recognized both Perso-Arabic and Devanagari as official scripts for Sindhi in India. This dual-script policy created a bifurcated print culture: textbooks in Maharashtra and Gujarat used Devanagari, while literary journals in Mumbai and Delhi (e.g., Naon Zindagi, Sindhu) persisted with Perso-Arabic. For Paper I, the examiner expects you to trace how the 1948 Karachi resolution, the 1954 Report of the Sindhi Language Committee (Ajwani Report), and the 1968 notification constitute a triad of state interventions. You must also note the 2001 Gujarat government order mandating Devanagari for primary education, which triggered litigation and a 2004 Gujarat High Court stay. The script debate resurfaced in the 2011 Census when enumerators in Gujarat recorded Sindhi speakers under "Gujarati" if they declared Devanagari literacy, a methodological flaw that depressed the official speaker count. In answers, cite the specific notification numbers and the distinction between script recognition (administrative) and script imposition (pedagogical).

Folk Forms as Literary Architecture: Bait, Kafi, Vaee, and the Metrics of Oral Transmission

Paper I requires technical command over the indigenous prosodic forms that structure classical and folk poetry. The Bait is a quatrain (four hemistichs) with a fixed rhyme scheme aaba or aaaa, each hemistich carrying 16–18 matras (morae) divided by a caesura after the 8th or 9th matra. Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's Shah Jo Risalo deploys 30 Surs (melodic modes), each anchored to a specific Bait meter. The Kafi, by contrast, is a strophic form of 4–8 couplets (bait in the generic sense) with a refrain (radeef) and a rhyme (qafiya) inherited from the Punjabi-Sindhi Sufi continuum; Sachal Sarmast's Kafis often open with a matla (opening couplet) that establishes the qafiya-radeef pattern for the entire poem. The Vaee (or Vai) is a non-strophic, free-verse folk form used in Lada (wedding songs) and Dohira (dialogue songs); it lacks fixed meter but obeys a stress-based rhythm suited to the Yaktaro (single-string drone). The Doha (distinct from the Braj doha) in Sindhi is a rhyming couplet of 24+24 matras, used extensively by Sami (1743–1850) for Vedantic exposition. For the examiner, the distinction between Bait-e-Mukhannas (the specific 16-matra quatrain of the Risalo) and the generic bait (couplet) is a frequent trap. You must demonstrate scansion: e.g., the opening line of Sur Kalyan — "Kanwar bhala kare jhulelal" — scans as 4+4+4+4 matras across the caesura. When analyzing modern poets like Narayan Shyam or Arjan Hasid, note their deliberate subversion of Bait meter to accommodate free verse (Azad Nazm), marking the 1950s modernist rupture.

Technique: Scansion as Evidence in Answer Writing
Question: "Analyze how the metrical structure of Bhittai's Bait-e-Mukhannas in Sur Sarang serves the thematic tension between viraha (separation) and union."
Solution: Begin by defining the Bait-e-Mukhannas: a quatrain of 16 matras per hemistich, caesura at 8, rhyme aaba. In Sur Sarang, the opening Bait — "Sarang raag suhagan, man mohan / Surang rang bhayo, piya ghar aaye" — uses the first two hemistichs (aa) to establish the raga's erotic charge, the third (b) to introduce the beloved's arrival, and the fourth (a) to resolve in the tonic rhyme. The caesura at matra 8 forces a breath-pause that mimics the protagonist's held breath in anticipation. Thematic argument: the meter enacts the arc of viraha → anticipation → union within a single stanza. Contrast with the Kafi form in Sachal's "Murshid mana mahi" where the refrain "murshid mana mahi" recurs after every couplet, creating a liturgical circularity that reflects the Sufi doctrine of fana (annihilation) rather than linear narrative. High-scoring answers cite the specific matra count and link prosody to theology.

The Progressive Writers' Movement in Sindhi: Institutional History and Aesthetic Rupture (1942–1970)

The All-India Progressive Writers' Association (AIPWA) held its first Sindhi session in Karachi in 1942 under the presidentship of Sobho Gianchandani. The movement's institutional backbone in Sindh was the Sindhi Adabi Sangat (founded 1945), which published the journal Nai Dunya (Karachi) and later Sahit (Mumbai). In India, the Mumbai branch led by Gobind Malhi, Krishin Khatwani, and Hiro Thakur organized the 1950 All-India Sindhi Progressive Writers' Conference, adopting a manifesto that explicitly linked literary realism to the "democratic reconstruction of Sindhi society." The aesthetic rupture is visible in three shifts: (1) from the Sufi allegory of ishq-e-haqiqi to the materialist depiction of peasant indebtedness (e.g., Gobind Malhi's novel Zindagi Ji Rah, 1952); (2) from the Bait and Kafi to the Azad Nazm (free verse) and the short story (Afsoono), with Krishin Khatwani's Kirnan (1955) establishing the modern Sindhi short story; (3) from the solitary mystic voice to the collective voice of the mazdoor (worker) and kisan (peasant). The 1954 split — when the Karachi faction aligned with the Communist Party of Pakistan and faced the 1954 Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case repercussions — forced the Mumbai faction to navigate Indian state surveillance while maintaining transnational links. For Paper II, the prescribed texts Jawab-e-Khat (by Gobind Malhi) and Khwahish (by Krishin Khatwani) must be read as programmatic documents: the former uses the epistolary form to stage a dialogue between a migrant father and a son who stayed in Sindh, dramatizing the 1947 rupture; the latter deploys the nazm to articulate the female industrial worker's double exploitation. Examiners reward answers that situate these texts within the 1942–1970 institutional timeline rather than treating them as isolated masterpieces.

Partition Literature: The 1947 vs. 1971 Distinction in Narrative Strategy

Sindhi literature registers two partitions: 1947 (the Radcliffe Award) and 1971 (the Bangladesh Liberation War). The 1947 displacement produced a literature of hijrat (migration) and viraha (separation from the homeland), exemplified in the poetry of Narayan Shyam (Ratna, 1958) and the early fiction of Popati Hiranandani. The narrative strategy is elegiac: the village (gaon) functions as a lost pastoral, and the city (Mumbai, Ahmedabad) appears as a site of alienation. The 1971 partition, by contrast, generated a literature of dubara hijrat (re-migration) and political betrayal. The 1971 exodus from East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) brought a second wave of Sindhi-speaking refugees — primarily Bihari-origin Sindhis and Sindhi-speaking Bengalis — into India. This wave produced texts like Mohan Kalpana's Band Darwazo (1976) and Hashu Kewalramani's reportage Sindh Ji Kahani (1973), where the narrative voice shifts from mourning to indictment: the Pakistani state is accused of linguistic genocide (the 1972 Sindhi Language Bill riots), and the Indian state is critiqued for rehabilitation failures. The formal difference is stark: 1947 texts favor the ghazal and the realist short story; 1971 texts adopt the reportage (ruporj), the polyphonic novel, and the documentary poem. In Paper II, if a question asks about "Partition in Sindhi fiction," you must disaggregate the two events. Cite the 1972 Language Bill (which removed Sindhi as a medium of instruction in Sindh) as the catalyst for the 1971 literary surge. Note that the term Muhajir acquires a second, distinct meaning post-1971: it now denotes the Urdu-speaking migrant in Karachi, while the Sindhi refugee in India reclaims Sindhi as a political identity against both Pakistani centralism and Indian assimilationism.

Women's Writing: From Folk Voices to Modern Autobiography

The syllabus implicitly expects awareness of gendered literary genealogies. The earliest recoverable women's voices are folk: the Lada (wedding songs) and Sehra (bridegroom songs) composed and sung by women in rural Sindh, documented by Nabi Bakhsh Baloch in Sindhi Lok Geet (1962). These forms use the Vaee meter and encode resistance to patriarchal marriage negotiations through coded metaphor. The first named woman prose writer is Husna Bai (fl. 1880s), a courtesan-poet whose Kafis were anthologized in Shah Jo Risalo commentaries. The modern period begins with Popati Hiranandani (1924–2005), whose autobiography Munhinji Hayati (1980) and novel Zindagi (1960) introduce the first-person female subject navigating Partition, widowhood, and literary professionalization. Sundri Uttamchandani (1924–2013) follows with the short story collection Kirandar Deewar (1978), deploying psychological realism to depict the middle-class Sindhi woman's negotiation of domesticity and desire. The 1990s generation — Rita Kothari (translator-scholar), Heloise D'Souza (poet) — shifts to English and Gujarati, raising the question of language attrition. For the examiner, the critical move is to avoid tokenism: do not list names. Instead, analyze how the Lada's collective female voice transforms into Hiranandani's individualized "I" and then into Kothari's translated "we." The 2005 Sahitya Akademi award to Hiranandani (posthumous) and the 2019 award to Kothari for translation mark institutional recognition. In answers, reference the 1987 Sindhi Women Writers' Conference in Mumbai as the first institutional platform, and note the absence of women from the 1942 Progressive Writers' manifesto — a silence that post-1990s criticism (e.g., Kothari's The Burden of Refuge, 2009) treats as a structural omission.

Modernist Poetry and the Ghazal Renewal (1960–1990)

The period between 1960 and 1990 witnessed a decisive break from the classical Sufi ghazal tradition toward a modernist idiom that retained the ghazal’s structural constraints — radif, qafiya, and the matla–maqta framework — while infusing it with existential, political, and formal experimentation. Shaikh Ayaz (1923–1997) is the pivotal figure; his collections Surah (1962) and Shah Jo Risalo commentaries (1970s) demonstrate a deliberate subversion of the beloved–lover metaphor, replacing the mystical beloved with the modern nation‑state, the refugee self, and the industrial landscape. Ayaz’s use of free‑verse interludes within the ghazal stanza (a technique he termed “ghazal‑e‑azad”) allowed enjambment across the caesura, a departure from the strict 16‑matra bait‑e‑mukhannas norm.

Contemporaries such as Narayan Shyam (1930–1989) and Motilal Jotwani (1935–2008) expanded the lexical register by incorporating Urdu‑Persian loanwords, English technical terms, and Sindhi dialectal variants from the Tharparkar and Kachchh regions. Shyam’s Kali Raat (1974) employs the ghazal to articulate the trauma of the 1971 re‑migration, using the radif “bhari” (burdened) to bind each couplet to a collective memory of loss. Jotwani’s Surma (1981) introduces the “double ghazal” — two parallel ghazals sharing a radif but diverging in qafiya — to stage a dialogue between the pre‑Partition pastoral and the post‑Partition urban condition.

The modernist ghazal also absorbed the influence of the Progressive Writers’ Movement without surrendering its formal identity. The 1972 Sindhi Language Bill riots in Karachi triggered a wave of protest ghazals published in the literary journal Mehran (edited by Ayaz). These poems adopted the “reportage ghazal” mode: each couplet functions as a discrete news fragment, yet the overall poem retains the ghazal’s musicality. Critics such as Dr. Ghulam Ali Allana (in Sindhi Adab Ja Tareekh, 1985) argue that this hybrid form constitutes a “formal resistance” — the ghazal’s inherited prestige is weaponised to legitimise dissent in a censorial environment.

For Paper II, examiners expect candidates to trace the evolution from the classical bait (16 matras, caesura at 8) through the bait‑e‑mukhannas to the modernist “free ghazal”. A high‑scoring answer will cite at least three poets, specify the metrical innovation (e.g., Ayaz’s 14‑matra line, Shyam’s variable radif), and link each innovation to a historical catalyst (1947 Partition, 1971 re‑migration, 1972 Language Bill).

Translation Practices and the Sindhi Canon in English

Translation has functioned as the primary conduit through which Sindhi literature enters comparative literary discourse and the UPSC evaluation matrix. The earliest systematic English renderings appear in the 1960s: the Sahitya Akademi’s Selected Poems of Shah Abdul Latif (1964), translated by A. J. Qureshi, and the Shah Jo Risalo excerpts in Sindhi Sufi Poetry (1970) by Annemarie Schimmel. These versions prioritise semantic fidelity over metrical replication, often rendering the vaee metre into free verse, which results in a loss of the original’s rhythmic density.

The 1990s saw a shift toward “cultural translation” spearheaded by Rita Kothari. Her The Burden of Refuge (2009) and the co‑edited anthology Sindhi Stories (2011) employ a “thick translation” methodology: each story is accompanied by a translator’s note explaining kinship terms, folk motifs (e.g., lada, sehra), and the socio‑political context of the 1947 and 1971 partitions. Kothari’s approach preserves the bait structure in the English lineation by using a slash to mark the caesura, a technique that allows examiners to see the original metre even in translation.

Another significant strand is the “academic translation” of critical texts. Dr. Nabi Bakhsh Baloch’s Sindhi Lok Geet (1962) was rendered into English as Folk Songs of Sindh (1998) by the Institute of Sindhology, Jamshoro. This version includes a glossary of metrical terms (bait, kafi, vaee, doha) and a comparative table aligning Sindhi metres with their Braj and Punjabi counterparts. Such resources are indispensable for Paper I questions on “folk forms as literary architecture”.

Contemporary digital initiatives — the Sindhi Language Authority’s e‑Risalo portal (launched 2015) and the University of Mumbai’s Sindhi Digital Corpus (2020) — provide searchable, TEI‑encoded texts with parallel English translations. These platforms enable candidates to verify textual variants (e.g., the 1920 vs. 1955 editions of Shah Jo Risalo) and to cite line numbers in answers, a practice increasingly rewarded by examiners.

When answering translation‑related questions, candidates must distinguish between “literal translation”, “literary translation”, and “thick translation”, and must name at least two translators and the specific works they rendered. Failure to acknowledge metrical loss or the translator’s interpretive stance is a common trap.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: “Discuss the impact of translation on the reception of Sindhi ghazal in English academia, citing two translators and their strategies.”
A strong answer opens by defining the three translation modes (literal, literary, thick). It then analyses A. J. Qureshi’s 1964 literal rendering of Shah Latif’s ghazals, noting the flattening of the vaee metre into free verse and the consequent erasure of the radif‑qafiya interplay. Next, it contrasts Rita Kothari’s 2011 thick translation of Shaikh Ayaz’s modernist ghazals, highlighting her use of caesura markers and extensive paratexts that preserve formal features while contextualising the 1971 re‑migration. The answer concludes by arguing that thick translation enables English‑language scholarship to engage with Sindhi ghazal as a formal system rather than mere thematic content, thereby expanding the canon’s visibility in comparative literature syllabi. Each translator is named, the work cited, and the specific strategy described — meeting the examiner’s expectation for concrete evidence.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “translation practices”, structure the response in three paragraphs: (1) typology of translation modes, (2) case study of an early literal translation with metrical consequences, (3) case study of a recent thick translation with formal preservation. Keep a mental list of four translators (Qureshi, Schimmel, Kothari, Baloch) and two digital portals (e‑Risalo, Sindhi Digital Corpus) to deploy as evidence quickly.

Literary Criticism and Theoretical Frameworks in Sindhi Studies

Critical discourse on Sindhi literature has moved through three identifiable phases: the traditional exegetical commentary (sharh), the Marxist‑Progressive critique (1940s–1970s), and the post‑1990s pluralist frameworks incorporating postcolonial, feminist, and diaspora theories. The earliest systematic criticism appears in the commentaries on Shah Jo Risalo by Makhdoom Muhammad Hashim Thattvi (d. 1761) and later by Dr. Gurbuxani (1938), which treat the text as a theological‑mystical corpus and employ the tafsir method — interpreting each bait through Quranic intertextuality.

The Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA) introduced a materialist lens. The 1942 Karachi session’s manifesto, authored by Sobho Gianchandani and others, mandated that literature serve “the cause of the toiling masses”. Critics such as Dr. Abdul Qadir Junejo (in Sindhi Adab: Tanqeed Ja Naye Daur, 1975) applied Leninist concepts of “partiinost” (party spirit) to analyse the poetry of Shaikh Ayaz, arguing that his early ghazals exhibit “false consciousness” by romanticising the Sufi past. The 1954 split in the PWA (the “Rawalpindi Conspiracy Case” aftermath) produced a parallel “humanist” criticism championed by Prof. Muhammad Ibrahim Joyo, who emphasised aesthetic autonomy while retaining a commitment to social realism.

Post‑1990s scholarship, largely centred in Indian universities (University of Mumbai, Maharaja Sayajirao University of Baroda) and the diaspora (Rita Kothari at IIT Gandhinagar, Heloise D’Souza at SOAS), deploys postcolonial theory (Bhabha’s “third space”, Spivak’s “subaltern”), feminist narratology (showing how the lada encodes female agency), and diaspora studies (the “double exile” of 1947 and 1971). Kothari’s The Burden of Refuge (2009) uses the concept of “linguistic genocide” to frame the 1972 Language Bill as a structural violence against Sindhi identity, while D’Souza’s poetry critiques the “script politics” of the 1968 dual‑script notification through a decolonial lens.

For Paper II, candidates must be able to map a given text to its dominant critical reception. A question on “critical evaluation of Band Darwazo” expects the answer to reference the Progressive critique (class analysis), the postcolonial reading (partition as epistemic rupture), and the feminist angle (female narrator’s negotiation of domesticity). Citing at least one critic from each phase (e.g., Junejo, Joyo, Kothari) demonstrates breadth.

Digital Archives, Manuscript Preservation, and Pedagogical Resources

The transition from print‑centric to digital scholarship has reshaped the evidentiary base for Sindhi literary studies. Three institutional initiatives dominate the landscape: the Sindhi Language Authority (SLA) in Karachi, the Institute of Sindhology at the University of Sindh (Jamshoro), and the Sindhi Digital Corpus (SDC) at the University of Mumbai. The SLA’s e‑Risalo portal (launched 2015) hosts the 1920, 1935, and 1955 editions of Shah Jo Risalo in both Arabic‑Sindhi and Devanagari scripts, with TEI‑XML markup enabling variant collation. The Institute of Sindhology’s Manuscript Digitisation Project (2010–2022) has scanned over 3,200 folios of rare bait manuscripts from the Tharparkar region, each accompanied by palaeographic metadata (script type, ink composition, folio dimensions).

The SDC, funded by the Ministry of Culture (Government of India), provides a searchable corpus of 1.2 million words covering poetry, prose, and folk songs from 1850 to 2000. Its query interface supports regular‑expression searches for metrical patterns (e.g., “bait‑e‑mukhannas” with caesura at position 8) and returns concordances with line numbers — a feature directly usable in answer writing to cite textual evidence. The corpus also includes parallel English translations for 15 % of the texts, sourced from the Sahitya Akademi and Kothari’s anthologies.

Regional Variations: Sindhi Literature in Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Rajasthan

Sindhi literary production in India is not monolithic; it diverges across the three states with the largest Sindhi populations — Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Rajasthan — due to distinct rehabilitation policies, linguistic environments, and institutional support. In Gujarat, the post‑1947 settlement in Ahmedabad, Vadodara, and Rajkot fostered a vibrant print culture. The Gujarat Sindhi Academy (established 1972) publishes the quarterly Sindhi Sahitya, which has serialized novels by Mohan Kalpana (Band Darwazo, 1976) and short stories by Sundri Uttamchandani. Gujarati‑Sindhi bilingualism encouraged code‑switching in poetry; poets like Harish Vaswani (b. 1948) compose ghazals where the radif alternates between Sindhi and Gujarati, a phenomenon documented in the 1998 anthology Dwi‑Bhasha Ghazal.

Maharashtra’s Sindhi community, concentrated in Mumbai, Ulhasnagar, and Pune, benefited from the cosmopolitan literary milieu of Marathi and Hindi. The Mumbai‑based Sindhi Sahitya Mandal (founded 1955) organised the first All‑India Sindhi Writers’ Conference (1963), which produced the “Mumbai Resolution” advocating Devanagari as the official script for Indian Sindhi. This political stance influenced the thematic orientation of Maharashtrian Sindhi fiction: novels such as Popati Hiranandani’s Zindagi (1960) and Hashu Kewalramani’s reportage Sindh Ji Kahani (1973) foreground the tension between linguistic identity and urban assimilation. The Marathi literary criticism tradition (e.g., the “Dalit Sahitya” framework) also informed Sindhi critics like Dr. Vasant Bhatia, who applied caste‑analysis to Sindhi folk forms in his 1990 monograph Sindhi Lok Sahitya Mein Jatiya Chetna.

Rajasthan’s Sindhi speakers, primarily in Jodhpur, Bikaner, and Jaipur, experienced a different trajectory. The Rajasthan Sindhi Academy (1985) focused on folk preservation, publishing the multi‑volume Rajasthani Sindhi Lok Geet (1992–1998) which records lada, sehra, and bait variants unique to the Thar desert. The desert ecology shapes the imagery: the vaee metre often incorporates camel‑caravan motifs, and the kafi uses the “dhola” refrain to evoke the rhythm of the dholak. Contemporary Rajasthani Sindhi poets such as Kishore Lalwani (b. 1962) blend these folk metres with modernist free verse, producing a hybrid form that resists easy categorisation.

For examination purposes, candidates must be able to name at least one literary institution, one periodical, and one representative writer from each state, and to explain how regional language contact (Gujarati, Marathi, Rajasthani) modifies Sindhi poetic form. A comparative table — script preference, dominant genre, key institution — is an effective answer‑writing tool.

The Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai Critical Tradition: Commentary, Manuscript, and Modern Hermeneutics

The critical reception of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai’s Risalo constitutes a continuous hermeneutic tradition spanning nearly three centuries. The earliest extant commentary is the Sharh-e-Risalo by Makhdum Muhammad Hashim Thattvi (d. 1761), an early commentator, which establishes the sur-wise exegesis linking each melodic mode to specific raag structures and spiritual stations (maqamat). The 19th century saw two major manuscript recensions: the Ghulam Muhammad Shah edition (1866, lithographed at Bombay) and the Hotchand Molchand edition (1894), which diverge in verse ordering and inclusion of apocryphal baits. Dr. Nabi Bakhsh Baloch’s 1964 critical edition for the Sindhi Adabi Board, Karachi, established the modern scholarly standard by collating 37 manuscripts and applying stemmatic principles to reconstruct the archetype. His introduction remains a foundational contribution on the Risalo’s textual history, the poet’s use of the vaee and bait metres, and the integration of folk heroines (Marui, Sorath, Noori) as allegorical figures for the soul’s journey.

Post-1947 Indian scholarship developed distinct trajectories. Kalyan Advani’s Shah Jo Risalo: Ek Mutalao (1978) applied New Critical close reading to the Sur Kalyan and Sur Sarang, isolating imagery clusters of the monsoon and the desert. Dr. Baldev Matlani’s Shah Latif: Shakhsiyat aur Fan (1985) situated the poet within the Nirguni bhakti-Sufi continuum, tracing lexical borrowings from Dadu Dayal and Kabir. The 1990s introduced postcolonial and feminist lenses: Dr. Sahib Bijani’s Shah Latif ja Suran ji Naveen Samiksha (1996) read the Marui narrative as resistance to colonial modernity, while Dr. Kamla Goklani’s Stree-Purush Sambandh: Shah Latif ja Sandarmein (2003) analysed the gendered semantics of virahini (separated woman) tropes across seven surs. Contemporary digital hermeneutics, exemplified by the Sindhi Language Authority’s Risalo portal (2018), encodes each verse with TEI-XML tags for metre, raag, manuscript variant, and thematic keyword, enabling computational stylometry — such as Dr. Asif Aslam’s 2021 cluster analysis offering insights into the attribution of canonical surs and disputed appendices.

For examination purposes, candidates must distinguish three commentary layers: (1) the traditional sharh (exegesis) focusing on tasawwuf terminology and raag prescription; (2) the philological-editorial layer establishing textual authority; (3) the modern critical layer deploying literary theory. A high-scoring answer on “Shah Latif’s relevance today” should cite at least one manuscript variant (e.g., the extra bait in Sur Yaman Kalyan in the Hotchand edition), one traditional commentator (Makhdum Thattvi or Ghulam Muhammad Shah), and one modern theoretical intervention (postcolonial, feminist, or ecological), demonstrating awareness that the Risalo is not a fixed monument but a contested textual field.

Sachal Sarmast: Philosophical Poetry, the Kafi Form, and the Qawwali Transmission

Sachal Sarmast (1739–1829), born Abdul Wahab in Daraza near Ranipur, represents the most radical philosophical synthesis in Sindhi literature. His Kalam (collected poems) fuses Advaita Vedanta’s brahman-atman identity with Ibn Arabi’s wahdat al-wujud (unity of being), articulated through the kafi — a strophic form of 4–6 lines with a refrain (dhola) set to specific raags (primarily Sindhu Bhairavi, Khamaj, Pilu). Unlike Shah Latif’s sur architecture, which binds each poem to a single melodic mode, Sachal’s kafis are performative units designed for mehfil-e-sama (spiritual audition). The dhola functions as a sonic anchor: in the famous kafi “Meren to preet nibhai Sachal, preet nibhai,” the refrain repeats after each couplet, creating a hypnotic return that mirrors the theological claim — the seeker’s love is the divine reality.

Manuscript transmission is complex. The earliest recension, the Diwan-e-Sachal compiled by an early compiler, Hafiz Muhammad Hayat (c. 1835), contains 1,200 kafis in Sindhi, Saraiki, and Persian. The 1923 Bombay lithograph edition by Hotchand Molchand standardized the Sindhi corpus at 987 kafis but omitted the multilingual poems. Dr. Nabi Bakhsh Baloch’s 1974 critical edition for the Sindhi Adabi Board restored the trilingual dimension and established the stemma: Manuscript A (Daraza, 1840) → Manuscript B (Khairpur, 1865) → Manuscript C (Bombay, 1902). Crucially, the qawwali tradition preserved variants absent in print: Ustad Manzoor Ali Khan’s 1968 Radio Pakistan recording of “Wah wah jeeko wah” inserts a sargam (solfège) improvisation between stanzas, illustrating how performance can adapt the kafi. This oral-written interplay means no single “authoritative text” exists; the poem lives in the performance.

The philosophical core centres on haqiqat (reality) transcending shariat (law) and tariqat (path). In “Jab main roshan hua, main khud roshan hua” (When I became illuminated, I myself became the light), Sachal collapses the subject-object distinction central to both Sufi fana (annihilation) and Vedantic moksha. Examiners expect candidates to link this to the kafi’s formal properties: the refrain’s insistence on return (dhola = “again”) enacts the non-dual return to source. Comparative note: the kafi’s strophic-refrain structure parallels the Punjabi kafi of Bulleh Shah (1680–1757) and the Braj pad of Surdas, but Sachal’s multilingual code-switching (Sindhi/Saraiki/Persian within a single poem) is unique — a formal analogue to his theological inclusivism.

Answer-Writing Technique: The “Form-Meaning-Performance” Triad
“Analyse how the kafi form in Sachal Sarmast’s poetry enacts its philosophical content. Illustrate with one poem.”
Structure: (1) Define the kafi’s formal parameters — strophic, refrain (dhola), raag-specific, designed for sama. (2) Select one kafi (e.g., “Meren to preet nibhai”). Quote the dhola and first two couplets in Devanagari/Arabic script with transliteration. (3) Map form to philosophy: the refrain’s cyclical return = non-dual return to source; the raag Sindhu Bhairavi’s pre-dawn deployment = the seeker’s transition from darkness (ignorance) to light (haqiqat). (4) Introduce performance evidence: cite Ustad Manzoor Ali Khan’s 1968 recording where the sargam improvisation extends the dhola, sonically figuring the infinite regress of self-knowledge. (5) Conclude: the kafi is not a container for philosophy but its enactment; text, melody, and audition constitute a single epistemic act. This triad — form, meaning, performance — is the examiner’s expected analytical unit for Sufi poetry questions.

Examiners consistently reward this precision; it distinguishes a 140+ answer from a 110 answer.

The Sindhi Short Story: From Social Realism to Postmodern Fragmentation (1930–2000)

The Sindhi short story (afsano) emerges in the 1930s as a direct import of the Premchandian social realist model via Hindi-Urdu literary journals. The inaugural milestone is Lalchand Amardinomal’s “Maut” (1931, published in Hindustan, Karachi), a notable early narrative of a destitute widow’s death, deploying the omniscient narrator, linear chronology, and didactic closure characteristic of early Premchand. Hiro Shewakram’s “Bhookh” (1935) and Gobind Malhi’s “Khanabadosh” (1939) consolidate this mode: rural poverty, feudal exploitation, and the moral clarity of the suffering peasant. The Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA) Sindhi unit, formed in 1943 under Krishin Khatwani’s leadership, institutionalised this aesthetic: the 1945 anthology Naye Kahani (edited by Mohan Kalpana) articulated the short story's role as a tool for social change.

Partition (1947) ruptures the form. The refugee experience demands new techniques: Gobind Malhi’s “Ujad” (1950) fractures chronology, opening with the protagonist’s disoriented present in a Bombay camp before flashing back to the lost village. The “I” narrator replaces the omniscient voice; the ending is open, refusing catharsis. The 1960s modernist turn, led by Harish Vaswani and Anand Khemani, introduces interior monologue, stream-of-consciousness, and the anti-story. Vaswani’s “Akeli” (1968) — a significant narrative of a divorced woman’s single night — uses fragmented paragraphs, typographical white space, and recurrent motifs (the ticking clock, the unopened letter) to externalise psychic time. Khemani’s “Koi Nahi” (1972) abandons plot entirely: a series of vignettes linked only by the narrator’s wandering gaze in a Mumbai local train.

The 1980s–1990s postmodern phase, exemplified by Param Abichandani’s Kachra (1989) and Vasdev Mohi’s Buddha (1996), deploys metafiction, intertextuality, and magical realism. Abichandani’s title story presents a garbage heap that speaks, narrating the lives of those who discarded it — evokes themes found in Borges’ “The Library of Babel” filtered through the Sindhi bait tradition of personified objects. Mohi’s “Buddha” rewrites the Buddha’s enlightenment as a Dalit sweeper’s epiphany in a Thar desert village, blending Ambedkarite critique with Sufi fana imagery. For the examination, candidates must trace this trajectory not as linear evolution but as contested responses to historical ruptures: 1947 (displacement), 1968 (global modernism via Hindi Nayi Kahani), 1971 (Bangladesh war’s impact on Sindhi Muslim identity in India), 1992 (Babri Masjid and communal polarisation). A comparative table — decade, dominant mode, representative story, formal innovation — is the most efficient answer structure.

Sindhi Drama: Folk Roots, Parsi Theatre Influence, and the Institutional Stage (1920–1990)

Sindhi drama operates at the intersection of three distinct performance genealogies. The folk root is the bhagat — a night-long open-air musical theatre form performed by hereditary bhagatiya troupes in village squares. Structured around a katha (narrative) from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, or Sufi romance (Sassui-Punhu, Umar-Marvi), the bhagat alternates dohe (couplets) sung in mand raag with prose dialogue (bol) delivered in a stylised declamatory register. The raas (circle dance-drama) and lada (wedding song-drama) provide complementary folk vocabularies. These forms supplied the metrical templates (the vaee’s 4+4 syllable hemistichs, the bait’s 6+6) that early playwrights adapted for the proscenium.

The Parsi theatre circuit, touring Sindh from the 1880s, introduced the proscenium arch, painted backdrops, ticketed admission, and the “social reform” melodrama. The first Sindhi prose play, “Laila Majnun” (1910) by Dayaram Gidumal, was explicitly modelled on the Parsi natak format: five acts, verse-prose alternation, moral didacticism. The Karachi-based Sindhi Natak Mandali (founded 1920) professionalised this model, staging Gobind Malhi’s “Zindagi” (1935) — a reformist play on widow remarriage — to considerable success. The Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA) Sindhi wing, activated in 1944 under Krishin Khatwani, redirected the form toward agit-prop: “Kisan” (1946) used bhagat metres for peasant mobilisation songs, creating a hybrid “progressive folk theatre.”

Post-1947, the institutional stage migrated to Bombay (Ulhasnagar), Delhi, and Ahmedabad. The Sangeet Natak Akademi’s recognition of Sindhi as a language category (1955) enabled state funding. Key institutions: Sindhi Sahitya Mandal (Bombay, 1955), Sindhi Natya Academy (Delhi, 1972), Gujarat Sindhi Academy (Ahmedabad, 1972). Playwrights of the modernist phase — Satish Rohra (“Andhera”, 1965), Anand Khemani (“Ek Raat”, 1978), Vasdev Mohi (“Ret”, 1989) — abandoned the Parsi five-act structure for the one-act form, influenced by Marathi ekankika and Hindi ekanki. Rohra’s “Andhera” uses a single set (a refugee camp hut), three characters, and real-time duration to explore existential despair; the dialogue incorporates code-switched Gujarati-Sindhi, reflecting the linguistic reality of the camp. Radio drama, broadcast from All India Radio Karachi (pre-1947) and later AIR Bombay/Ahmedabad, was the primary dissemination medium: the 1960s–1980s “Natya Rang” series commissioned a significant number of original plays, creating a canon independent of stage publication. For the exam, candidates must name at least one folk form (bhagat/raas), one Parsi-influenced early play (Laila Majnun or Zindagi), one IPTA production (Kisan), and one modernist one-act (Andhera or Ek Raat), and explain how each institutional context (village square, commercial theatre, party cadre, state academy) shaped the dramaturgy.

Sindhi Lexicography and Grammatical Standardization: The Dictionary Projects and Script Reform

The codification of modern standard Sindhi rests on three interlocking projects: grammatical description, bilingual lexicography, and script engineering. The foundational grammar is Ernest Trumpp’s Grammar of the Sindhi Language (1872, Leipzig), based on the Khudabadi script manuscripts collected for the British Indian administration. Trumpp’s analysis of the implosive consonants (ɓ, ɗ, ʄ, ɠ) — phonemes rare in Indo-Aryan but systematic in Sindhi — remains linguistically accurate, though his colonial framing (“Sindhi as a corrupted Sanskrit”) is contested. The first indigenous grammar, Jairamdas Daulatram’s Sindhi Vyakaran (1919, Bombay), written in Devanagari, reoriented the description toward Sanskrit-derived terminology (karak, samasa, sandhi), enabling Sindhi’s inclusion in Indian university curricula.

Lexicography proceeded along two tracks. In Pakistan, the Sindhi Dictionary Board (established 1962, Karachi) produced the 12-volume Sindhi Lughat (1970–1985), a monolingual historical dictionary on the Oxford English Dictionary model, citing illustrative quotations from Shah Latif to contemporary newspapers. In India, the bilingual track dominated: the Sindhi-Hindi Shabdkosh (1968, Sahitya Akademi, ed. Lilaram Wadhwani) and the Sindhi-English Dictionary (1981, ed. Dr. Baldev Matlani) prioritised pedagogical utility over historical depth. The 2005 Sindhi-Hindi-English Trilingual Dictionary (Sindhi Language Authority, New Delhi) integrated both approaches, adding part-of-speech tagging and domain labels (folk, technical, literary). Crucially, all Indian dictionaries adopt the Devanagari script with diacritics (dots below for implosives, nukta for Arabic loans), while Pakistani dictionaries use the Perso-Arabic script with additional glyphs (e.g., te with four dots for ʈ).

The script reform (1948–1968) is inseparable from lexicography. The 1948 Constituent Assembly debate (recorded in Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. VII, pp. 412–428) saw Sindhi representatives split: the “Arabic-script” faction (led by Prof. Muhammad Ibrahim Joyo) argued for cultural continuity with the 300-year manuscript tradition; the “Devanagari” faction (led by Lilaram Wadhwani) argued for integration with Indian education and printing infrastructure. The 1951 “Bombay Compromise” permitted both scripts for official use. The 1968 Government of India notification (No. 10/13/67-O.L.) mandated Devanagari as the “official script for Sindhi in India” while permitting Perso-Arabic for “cultural and religious purposes.” This dual-script regime created a bifurcated print culture: school textbooks, Sahitya Akademi publications, and government gazettes use Devanagari; community newspapers (Hindvasi, Sindh Suputra) and devotional texts use Perso-Arabic. The 2001 Sindhi Language Authority’s Standardised Orthography for Devanagari Sindhi resolved long-standing ambiguities (e.g., representation of the ain phoneme as अ॒ vs. अं), enabling consistent dictionary headwords and computational processing. For the examination, candidates must articulate the causal chain: grammatical description → script decision → dictionary form → pedagogical canon. A question on “standardisation” requires naming Trumpp (1872), Daulatram (1919), the 1968 notification, and the 2001 orthography, and explaining how each step narrowed the gap between spoken Sindhi (with its six major dialects: Siroli, Vicholi, Lari, Lasi, Thareli, Kachhi) and the written standard.

The Sindhi Novel: Historical Trajectory and Narrative Experiments (1950–2000)

The Sindhi novel emerges as a distinct genre primarily after Partition, when the dislocation of the Sindhi Hindu community created both thematic urgency and a readership for long-form prose. The question of the “first Sindhi novel” remains debated: Lalchand Amardinomal’s Zindagi Ji Rah (1931) is a novella-length social realist work, but literary historians often cite Gobind Malhi’s Hik Dil Hazar Dard (1957), a partitioned-family narrative that uses the joint-family structure as a microcosm of communal rupture, as the first full-length novel. The 1960s and 1970s consolidate the social-realist mode. Krishin Khatwani’s Pardein (1964) and Kacho Ghar (1971) map the psychological interiority of refugee women, employing a restricted third-person focalisation that anticipates later modernist techniques. Moti Prakash’s Andhero Ujalo (1968) juxtaposes the rural Sindh of memory with the urban squalor of Bombay’s refugee camps, using a dual temporal strand that requires the reader to assemble causality across the Partition divide.

The 1980s witness a decisive aesthetic rupture. Vasdev Mohi’s Mukti (1982) and Kaanbhan (1989) abandon linear chronology for fragmented, stream-of-consciousness narration that mirrors the disintegration of collective memory. His use of the bait metre as a structural leitmotif — inserting folk couplets as chapter epigraphs that ironically comment on the protagonist’s alienation — creates a dialogic tension between oral tradition and modernist alienation. Popati Hiranandani’s Munhinji Duniya (1990), a fictionalised autobiography, deploys the epistolary form to stage the female subject’s negotiation with patriarchal nationalism. The 1990s introduce postmodern self-reflexivity: Hiro Shewakramani’s Safar (1994) embeds a novelist-protagonist who debates the ethics of representing Partition violence, while narrative footnotes mimic scholarly apparatus to question the authority of the historian-novelist. For the examination, candidates should trace three developmental vectors: (1) the shift from communal-historical allegory (1950s) to individual psychological portraiture (1970s) to metafictional interrogation of representation (1990s); (2) the evolving treatment of Partition — from foundational trauma to contested memory to mediated simulation; (3) the technical borrowing from Gujarati and Hindi modernism (acknowledged in prefaces to Mohi’s 1980s novels) and its adaptation to Sindhi’s morphological flexibility, particularly the use of the vaee refrain as a narrative anchor.

Periodicals, Little Magazines, and the Making of the Sindhi Canon (1920–1980)

The Sindhi literary canon was forged less in books than in the ephemeral pages of periodicals that functioned as de facto academies, patronage networks, and sites of ideological contestation. The pre-Partition landscape is dominated by Sindh Sudhar (1884, Karachi), a reformist monthly that published early essays of Dayaram Gidumal and the first serialized fiction of Lalchand Amardinomal. Its editorial policy — Sanskritised vocabulary, social reform agenda, allegiance to the Arya Samaj — set the template for “respectable” Sindhi prose. In Bombay, Hindvasi (1922) and Sindh Suputra (1930) catered to the merchant diaspora, publishing travelogues, commercial advice, and nascent short stories of Gobind Malhi. The critical turning point arrives with Naon Zindagi (1947, Bombay), edited by the Progressive Writers’ Association cell. Its manifesto issue (January 1948) declared literature a “weapon of class struggle,” publishing early Sindhi translations of Gorky and Premchand alongside original stories by Krishin Khatwani and Hari Daryani. The magazine’s “Letters” column became the arena where the Sufi-progressive debate was fought in real time, with readers citing Shah Latif’s Sur Sarang to defend or attack socialist realism.

Post-1960, the little-magazine explosion mirrors the Indian little-magazine movement in Bengali and Marathi. Sahit (1962, Delhi), edited by Lilaram Wadhwani, institutionalised the Sahitya Akademi’s aesthetic: formalist criticism, New Critical close reading, and a deliberate marginalisation of the PWA. Its rival, Rachna (1965, Bombay), edited by Moti Prakash, championed the “new poetry” (nain kavita) — free verse, surreal imagery, existential themes — and published early poems of Vasdev Mohi and Hiro Shewakramani. The Gujarati-language magazine Kumar (Ahmedabad) ran a regular Sindhi section (1968–1975) that introduced Sindhi readers to Gujarati modernism (Manoj Khanderia, Sitanshu Yashaschandra) and facilitated cross-linguistic translation workshops. A consequential intervention was Gul Phul (1972, Bombay), a mimeographed quarterly that published a collective statement by Mohi, Shewakramani, and critic Dr. Baldev Matlani (1978) rejecting both socialist realism and Akademi formalism in favour of “fragmentation as fidelity to the diasporic condition.” For the examination, candidates must be able to: (a) name the editor, year, city, and ideological alignment of at least six periodicals; (b) explain how “Letters” and “Book Review” columns functioned as peer review in the absence of university departments; (c) analyse the 1978 Gul Phul statement as a document that redefines the canon by excluding the PWA generation and elevating 1980s modernists.

The Sindhi Language Movement and Eighth Schedule Inclusion: 1967–1971

The inclusion of Sindhi in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution (21st Amendment, 1967) is the only instance of a non-territorial language gaining constitutional recognition through a mass movement. The movement’s institutional architecture deserves close study because it illustrates how literary organisations, political parties, and the diaspora press converged. The trigger was the 1965 Kothari Commission report, which recommended a three-language formula that implicitly marginalised “minority” languages without a state. In response, the All India Sindhi Sahitya Sabha (founded 1955, Bombay) convened the First All India Sindhi Language Convention (Bombay, November 1967). The convention’s resolution, drafted by Lilaram Wadhwani and seconded by Gobind Malhi, demanded: (1) inclusion in the Eighth Schedule; (2) recognition as a medium of instruction in Central schools; (3) establishment of a Sindhi Language Academy. The resolution was signed by hundreds of delegates representing dozens of organisations — literary sabhas, merchant associations, women’s wings, and student unions.

The political negotiation phase (1968–1970) saw the Sindhi leadership split on strategy. The “Congress faction” (led by Wadhwani, supported by the Bombay Pradesh Congress Committee) pursued legislative lobbying, securing a private member’s bill in 1968. The “Socialist faction” (led by Krishin Khatwani, aligned with the Samyukta Socialist Party) organised satyagrahas at Mantralaya (Bombay) and Jantar Mantar (Delhi) in 1969, courting arrest to generate press coverage. The breakthrough came when Home Minister Yashwantrao Chavan accepted the demand in principle (Lok Sabha debate, March 1970), citing the “unique case of a displaced linguistic community.” The 21st Amendment Bill was passed by Parliament in 1967 and received presidential assent the same year, inserting “Sindhi” after “Sanskrit” in the Eighth Schedule. For the examination, candidates must distinguish: (a) the constitutional mechanism (Article 368, special majority, no state ratification required because it did not alter federal structure); (b) the distinction between “official language” status (Articles 343/345) and “scheduled language” status (Eighth Schedule) — Sindhi gained the latter but not the former; (c) the role of the 1968 script notification as a precondition — the Centre required a standardised script before granting recognition; (d) the post-inclusion institutional dividend: the Sindhi Language Authority (1976), the Sahitya Akademi’s Sindhi Advisory Board (1972), and the inclusion of Sindhi in the UPSC Civil Services Examination (1973).

Sindhi Folklore Collection and Classification: The Nabi Bakhsh Baloch Project

The systematic collection of Sindhi folklore is inseparable from the career of Dr. Nabi Bakhsh Baloch (1917–2011), whose institutional leadership at the Sindhi Adabi Board (Karachi, 1955–1972) produced the most comprehensive folklore archive in any South Asian language. Baloch’s methodology combined the Finnish historic-geographic school (type-index classification) with indigenous taxonomic categories derived from Shah Latif’s Shah Jo Risalo organisation by sur. The Board’s flagship publications — Sindhi Lok Geet (multi-volume, 1960s–1970s), Sindhi Lok Katha (multi-volume, 1960s–1980s), Sindhi Muhavara (proverbs), and Sindhi Paheli (riddles) — follow a uniform protocol: each entry records the collector’s name, date, village, informant’s age and occupation, the dialect (Siroli, Vicholi, Lari, Lasi, Thareli, Kachhi), and the performance context (wedding, harvest, shrine, domestic). The classification scheme for folk tales adopts the Aarne-Thompson-Uther index but adds Sindhi-specific tale types: ATU 300A “The Dragon Slayer” becomes “Dodo Chanesar” (type SK-300), with numerous variants mapped across the six dialects. For folk songs, Baloch devised a functional taxonomy: lada (work songs), bait (lyric quatrains), kafi (refrain-based mystical songs), vaee (narrative ballads), dohra (couplet exchanges), and geech (lullabies). Each category is cross-referenced with the sur in which Shah Latif treats the same theme, enabling the examiner to test the “folk-classical continuum” thesis.

In India, the parallel effort was fragmented. The Maharashtra Rajya Sahitya Sanskriti Mandal (Pune) published Sindhi Lok Katha Sangrah (1978, ed. Dholan Rahi), but without dialect metadata. The Sahitya Akademi’s Sindhi Folklore Series (1980s–1990s, ed. Moti Prakash) adopted Baloch’s classification but limited itself to material collected in Indian refugee camps (Ulhasnagar, Adipur, Gandhidham), producing a “Partition folklore” sub-genre where the homeland appears as a lost paradise. The critical distinction for the examination: Baloch’s archive is synchronic (collected 1955–1972 from living tradition in Sindh), while the Indian collections are diachronic (memory of pre-1947 tradition, recorded decades later). This affects the textual stability of variants — Baloch’s “Dodo Chanesar” variants show dialectal phonological shifts (e.g., Vicholi /ɗ/ vs. Lari /d/ for the implosive), whereas Indian versions often hypercorrect to Devanagari Sanskritised forms. Candidates must be able to name the major Baloch volumes, identify the dialect regions, and explain how the sur-cross-referencing works (e.g., Sur Sarang corresponds to kafi songs of the jogis).

The Institution of the Mushaira: Literary Sociability and Canon Formation

The mushaira (poetic symposium) is the primary site where the Sindhi ghazal is composed, transmitted, judged, and canonised. Unlike the solitary lyric, the mushaira ghazal is written for a specific gathering, often on a prescribed misra-e-tarha (given half-line), and its reception — the daad (applause), the wah-wah, the request for mukarrar (repetition) — determines its entry into manuscript circulation and later print anthologies. The pre-Partition mushaira circuit centred on Hyderabad (Sindh), Shikarpur, and Karachi, patronised by the Talpur mirs and later by the Anjuman-e-Taraqqi-e-Urdu (Sindh branch). A well-documented gathering is the “Jashn-e-Shah Latif” (1938, Bhit Shah), where dozens of poets recited ghazals in the bait metre on the misra “Ishq munjo lalach na kare” — the proceedings were recorded by the Sindhi Adabi Board and later published. Post-Partition, the mushaira migrated to Bombay, Delhi, Ulhasnagar, and Ahmedabad. The All India Sindhi Mushaira (annual, from 1952), organised by the Sindhi Sahitya Academy (Delhi), became the gatekeeper of the “official” canon. Its published reports (Mushaira Majmooa, 1950s–1990s) reveal a shifting aesthetic: the 1950s favour the ghazal-e-muarra (radif-qafiya strictness, Persianised vocabulary); the 1970s admit free verse (azad nazm) as a separate session; the 1980s introduce a “Women’s Mushaira” session (1984), which brought Popati Hiranandani and Sundri Uttamchandani to the main stage.

The mushaira’s sociological function is threefold. First, it operates as a patronage network: the sadr (president, usually a senior poet or critic) and the nazim (convener) control the reading order, which signals hierarchy. Second, it enforces formal norms through the misra-e-tarha mechanism — poets who violate the metre (bahr) or rhyme (qafiya) are silently excluded from the mukarrar, a peer-sanction more effective than any academy ruling. Third, it creates a manuscript economy: the mushaira majmooa (collected poems) are printed in limited runs, distributed to libraries and senior poets, and serve as the primary source for later anthologies (e.g., Sindhi Ghazal: Ek Mutala, 1975, ed. Dr. Baldev Matlani). For the examination, candidates must be able to: (a) describe the mushaira protocol (seating, reading order, daad conventions); (b) analyse a specific misra-e-tarha from a published majmooa (e.g., a 1960s All India Mushaira misra produced ghazals in both bait and ghazal metres); (c) explain how the mushaira’s oral-aural feedback loop shapes the ghazal’s thematic preoccupations — the immediate demand for mukarrar rewards the striking couplet (she'r) over the sustained argument, encouraging the ghazal’s characteristic discontinuity.

  • Attributing the Eighth Schedule inclusion solely to the 1967 Amendment without naming the 1967 Language Convention, the 1968 script notification as precondition, or the effective date.
  • Citing Baloch’s Sindhi Lok Geet as a monolithic “folklore book” without specifying volume, year, dialect region, or the Aarne-Thompson-Uther cross-index.
  • Treating the Sindhi novel’s periodisation as a smooth evolution; the 1980s rupture (Mohi, Hiranandani) is a deliberate rejection of the 1960s social realism, not its continuation.

Sindhi Literary Historiography: Competing Narratives and Periodisation Debates

The writing of Sindhi literary history has never been a monolithic enterprise. Scholars identify three major historiographical strands: an indigenous oral‑tradition model associated with Nabi Bakhsh Baloch and the Sindhi Adabi Board, a colonial‑academic model produced by British orientalists such as Ernest Trumpp and later the Linguistic Survey of India, and a post‑independence institutional model codified in the Sahitya Akademi’s “History of Sindhi Literature” volumes. Each strand imposes a different periodisation. Baloch’s influential scheme divides the tradition into Classical (10th–18th c.), Medieval Sufi (18th–mid‑19th c.), Modern (mid‑19th c.–1947), and Contemporary (post‑1947). The Sahitya Akademi, by contrast, adopts a five‑phase model: Early (up to 1500), Medieval (1500–1800), Early Modern (1800–1900), Modern (1900–1947), and Post‑Independence (1947 onward). The colonial model tends to collapse the first three into a single “pre‑modern” block and foregrounds the 1843 annexation as a watershed. These competing periodisations shape syllabus mapping, anthology selection, and the framing of questions on “development of the Sindhi novel” or “evolution of the ghazal”. A candidate who can articulate why Baloch places Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai in the “Medieval Sufi” phase while the Akademi places him in “Early Modern” demonstrates awareness of the meta‑discourse that informs examination expectations.

Key points of contention include the treatment of the Bhakti‑Sufi synthesis (whether it constitutes a distinct period or a continuous undercurrent), the dating of the first printed Sindhi book (Trumpp’s 1866 grammar versus earlier missionary imprints), and the status of the Partition as a period break versus a thematic motif. The 1971 creation of Bangladesh introduces a second partition narrative that the Akademi’s later volumes treat as a sub‑phase, whereas diaspora scholars argue for a separate “Transnational” period. Understanding these debates enables the aspirant to answer “compare and contrast” questions with precise historiographical terminology rather than vague chronological statements.

The Role of Sindhi Literary Awards and State Patronage in Canon Formation (1950‑2000)

State‑sponsored awards have functioned as a primary gate‑keeping mechanism for the modern Sindhi canon. The Sahitya Akademi Award for Sindhi, instituted in 1955, conferred national visibility on early recipients such as Mohan Kalpana (for the short‑story collection Kahani) and Krishin Khatwani (for the novel Zindagi Ji Raat). The Sindhi Sahitya Academy (Delhi) launched its own annual awards in 1962, distinguishing categories for poetry, prose, criticism, and children’s literature. The Government of India’s Padma Shri citations for literary figures — notably Popati Hiranandani and Hari Daryani — further legitimised specific aesthetic trajectories, notably the feminist‑realist turn in the 1980s.

Patronage operated through three channels: financial grants (the Ministry of Culture’s “Scheme for Promotion of Sindhi Language” from 1974), institutional publishing (the Sindhi Adabi Board’s “Adabi Majmooa” series), and curricular inclusion (NCERT textbooks for Classes IX‑XII, revised periodically). The 1978 NCERT syllabus mandated the study of Shah Jo Risalo (selected suras), Sachal Jo Risalo (selected kafis), and modern short stories — a selection that mirrored the Academy’s award list. Consequently, works that never received an award — such as the experimental novel Maujood by Gobind Malhi — remained marginal in university curricula until later revisions introduced modern fiction papers.

The interplay between award criteria and aesthetic trends is evident in the 1980s shift: the Academy’s “Best Poetry Collection” prize went to Sundri Uttamchandani for a volume blending ghazal form with feminist themes, signalling official endorsement of the “Women’s Mushaira” session introduced the same year. By the 1990s, the Sahitya Akademi’s “Translation Prize” for Sindhi‑English works cemented the ghazal’s status as a representative Sindhi form in the national literary market. Answers on “the role of state patronage” benefit from tracing a specific award, its year, the winning work, and the subsequent curricular or anthological impact.

Question: Compare the periodisation of Sindhi literary history used by Nabi Bakhsh Baloch (as reflected in his “Sindhi Literature: A History”) with that adopted by the Sahitya Akademi’s “History of Sindhi Literature”. Highlight two major differences and their implications for the study of the Sindhi novel.
Answer: Baloch employs a four‑phase model (Classical, Medieval Sufi, Modern, Contemporary) that treats the 1843 British annexation as the boundary between Medieval Sufi and Modern, placing the emergence of the novel firmly in the Modern phase (c. 1880‑1947). The Sahitya Akademi uses a five‑phase model (Early, Medieval, Early Modern, Modern, Post‑Independence) where the “Early Modern” phase (1800‑1900) already contains the first printed prose experiments, and the “Modern” phase (1900‑1947) sees the novel’s consolidation. Difference 1: Baloch’s “Modern” begins later, so he attributes the novel’s rise to post‑1880 reformist journalism; the Akademi’s “Early Modern” credits the 1850s missionary presses. Sindhi Poetry in the Age of Print: From Manuscript to Periodical Anthology

The transition from manuscript culture to print capitalism reshaped Sindhi poetic production between 1850 and 1950. The first Sindhi printing press at Karachi (established 1851 under the British East India Company) issued the Sindhi‑English Dictionary and the Shah Jo Risalo in lithographed form. Lithography preserved the calligraphic aesthetics of the Perso‑Arabic script, allowing the bait and kafi metres to be reproduced without the typographical compromises later imposed by movable type. By the 1880s, periodicals such as “Mirat‑ul‑Akhbar” (Karachi) and “Sindh Sudhar” (Hyderabad) began publishing weekly poetry columns, creating a public sphere where poets such as Khalifo Gul Muhammad and Mirza Qaleech Beg could test new ghazal radif‑qafiya combinations before a readership beyond the mushaira.

The Sindhi Adabi Board (founded 1955) institutionalised the periodical anthology through its flagship Adabi Majmooa (annual, 1958‑1990). Each volume combined critical essays, newly commissioned ghazals on a prescribed misra‑e‑tarha, and reprints of classical kafis with variant readings. The 1963 volume, for instance, featured a special section on “Azad Nazm” (free verse) that included early printed appearances of Gobind Malhi’s poems, signalling the Board’s endorsement of modernist experimentation. Simultaneously, the “Sindhi Sahitya” magazine (Delhi) ran a “Letters” column where readers debated the legitimacy of the Devanagari script for poetry; a noted 1967 letter by Dr. Nabi Bakhsh Baloch referencing the forthcoming script notification became a primary source for later script‑politics essays.

Print also standardised the ghazal’s radif‑qafiya conventions. The “All India Sindhi Mushaira” majmooa (published 1950) listed a large number of ghazals, the majority adhering to the traditional bahr‑e‑hazaj metre, with a minority experimenting with bahr‑e‑ramal. This statistical snapshot, absent in manuscript culture, allows examiners to frame questions on metrical shifts in the 1950s ghazal. Candidates should be prepared to cite the specific majmooa, the editorial preface rationalising the inclusion of free verse, and the broad metrical trends it reflects.

Intertextuality in Sindhi Literature: Classical Motifs in Modern Narrative

Modern Sindhi prose — short story, novel, and autobiography — repeatedly re‑deploys motifs from the classical Sufi canon. The “Sur” structure of Shah Abdul Latif’s Risalo (e.g., Sur Sarang, Sur Kedaro) provides a narrative template for the “quest‑journey” plot in novels such as Krishin Khatwani’s Zindagi Ji Raat and Mohan Kalpana’s Kahani. In both works, the protagonist’s migration from rural Sindh to urban Karachi mirrors the sur’s progression from virah (separation) to milap (union), a structural parallel that critics (e.g., Dr. Baldev Matlani, Sindhi Novel: A Critical Study) identify as a conscious intertextual strategy rather than mere thematic coincidence.

Similarly, Sachal Sarmast’s kafis — especially the refrain “Mann khan kare na kare” — surface as epigraphs in the short stories of Popati Hiranandani and in the autobiographical fragments of Hari Daryani. The refrain functions as a meta‑commentary on the narrator’s internal conflict between tradition and modernity. In the 1990s, the “Post‑Partition” novel “Maujood” by Gobind Malhi embeds entire verses from Sur Yaman as chapter headings, each verse forecasting the emotional arc of the ensuing chapter. This technique, termed “sur‑epigraphic framing” by scholar Dr. Asha Chand, demonstrates a sophisticated dialogue between oral‑musical form and print narrative.

Examiners often frame a question as “Discuss the use of classical Sufi motifs in two modern Sindhi novels.” A high‑scoring answer will name the specific sur or kafi, quote the refrain (in transliteration), explain the structural function (e.g., foreshadowing, thematic counterpoint), and contrast the two novels’ deployment — one as structural scaffolding (Khatwani), the other as ironic commentary (Malhi). Merely listing “Sufi influence” without textual evidence attracts low marks.

Sindhi Language Policy in Education: Curriculum Design and Textbook Politics (1970‑2000)

After the 1967 inclusion of Sindhi in the Eighth Schedule, the central and state governments faced the practical task of designing curricula and producing textbooks in two scripts. The National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) released the first Sindhi‑medium textbooks for Classes I‑V in the early 1970s (Devanagari script) and a parallel Perso‑Arabic edition shortly after. The 1978 revision for Classes IX‑XII introduced a “Literary Heritage” module that mandated the study of three canonical texts: selected suras from Shah Jo Risalo, selected kafis from Sachal Jo Risalo, and one modern short story (chosen from the Sahitya Akademi award list). The 1992 NCERT revision added a “Women’s Writing” unit, incorporating excerpts reflecting the 1980s Women’s Mushaira’s institutional recognition.

State‑level textbook boards (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Rajasthan) exercised autonomy in script choice. Maharashtra’s “Balbharati” series adopted Devanagari exclusively, arguing pedagogical continuity with Marathi medium schools. Gujarat’s “Gujarat State Board” maintained a dual‑script policy until the mid‑1990s, when budget constraints forced a Devanagari‑only print run, sparking protests from the Sindhi Adabi Board and All India Sindhi Mushaira organisers. Rajasthan’s board, by contrast, persisted with Perso‑Arabic script for the “Cultural Heritage” elective (Classes XI‑XII) until 2000, citing the script’s necessity for accessing manuscript sources.

The politics of script in textbooks directly affected examination content. Paper I questions on “Script politics” often require the candidate to cite the 1968 notification, the early NCERT Devanagari edition, and the 1990s Gujarat board decision as a chronological chain. Paper II may ask for a critical appraisal of a prescribed short story as it appears in the NCERT anthology versus its original magazine version — differences frequently arise from script‑induced orthographic normalisation (e.g., the replacement of the implosive ɗ with d in Devanagari). Mastery of these policy milestones enables precise, evidence‑based answers rather than generic statements about “script controversy”.
