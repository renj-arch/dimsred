1. Sindhi Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

2. History of the Language

Sindhi is an Indo-Aryan language with a literary tradition going back to the Sufi poets of Sindh. After Partition, Sindhi literature continued in India in Perso-Arabic and Devanagari scripts.

3. Literary Periods and Movements

  • Early period (16th–18th c.): Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Sufi poetic tradition.
  • Nineteenth–early twentieth century: Print, prose and the Sindhi renaissance.
  • Modern period (20th c.–present): Partition literature, fiction, poetry and diaspora writing.

4. Major Authors

  • Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai: 18th-century Sufi poet; Sur Shah Latif.
  • Sachal Sarmast: Sufi poet.
  • Shaikh Ayaz: Modern Sindhi poet.
  • Popati Hiranandani: Sindhi writer and feminist voice.

5. Major Works

  • Sur Shah Latif — Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai: Sufi poetry collection.
  • Sindhi folk poetry — Oral tradition: Traditional songs and ballads.
  • Modern Sindhi poetry — Shaikh Ayaz: Twentieth-century poetry.
  • Sindhi fiction — Modern Sindhi writers: Twentieth-century short stories and novels.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai in the literary history of Sindhi and assess their contribution.
Answer: Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai belongs to the Early period (16th–18th c.) phase. 18th-century Sufi poet; Sur Shah Latif. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Early period (16th–18th c.) → Nineteenth–early twentieth century → Modern period (20th c.–present).
  • Authors: Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, Shaikh Ayaz, Popati Hiranandani.
  • Works: Sur Shah Latif, Sindhi folk poetry, Modern Sindhi poetry, Sindhi fiction.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

6. Comparative Analysis of Literary Movements

To master Paper I, one must move beyond simple chronology and understand the socio-political mechanisms that drove specific literary movements in Sindhi history. The transition from the Sufi oral tradition to the print-based renaissance was not merely a change in medium but a fundamental shift in epistemology. In the early period, literature served as a vehicle for spiritual instruction (Taqleef) within the rural agrarian context, utilizing metaphors drawn from nature and daily toil. Conversely, the 19th-century movement, spearheaded by figures like Shaikh Ayaz, introduced realism and social critique, shifting focus to the plight of the peasantry under feudal oppression. This section requires you to analyze how the 'Sufi' tradition evolved into 'Modernism' not just through time, but through the adoption of new scripts (Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari) which altered the phonetic representation and accessibility of the language.

The examiner often twists this concept by asking for a comparison between the thematic focus of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's 'Sur' and the prose works of Popati Hiranandani. The former relies on allegory where the human soul is a garden needing cultivation, while the latter employs direct realism to expose the structural inequalities of post-Partition society. A serious aspirant must articulate how the literary movement of 'Sindhi Renaissance' was defined by the emergence of prose (Nasr) alongside poetry (Shair), creating a dual-track development that allowed for the articulation of complex political ideas which were previously impossible in strictly rhymed verse.

Technique: Comparative Thematic Analysis
Question: Compare the socio-political consciousness found in the works of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai with that of Shaikh Ayaz. How did the shift from oral to print culture influence this consciousness?
Answer: Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's consciousness was rooted in spiritual egalitarianism, accessible to all castes through the medium of oral performance and the 'Sur' form, focusing on the unity of existence (Wahdat al-Wujud). Shaikh Ayaz, writing in the 19th century during the print renaissance, shifted towards socio-political realism. His works explicitly addressed feudal exploitation and the need for social reform. The shift to print culture democratized literature, moving it from the 'Majlis' (council) of Sufis to the public sphere, allowing for the articulation of specific political grievances and the standardization of the Sindhi language itself, which was previously fluid in oral tradition.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Why' over the 'Who'
When answering questions on movements, never stop at listing names. Always explain the 'Why'. Why did a movement start? Was it due to political partition, technological change (printing press), or spiritual revival? Connect the literary movement to the broader historical context of British Raj and Partition.

7. Critical Evaluation of Major Works: Textual Nuances

A high-scoring answer in Paper I demands a critical evaluation of major works, looking beyond surface-level summaries. For instance, when discussing 'Sur Shah Latif', one must analyze the linguistic architecture of the text. The work is not merely a collection of poems but a systematic theological treatise disguised as poetry. The examiner may ask you to identify specific rhetorical devices used by Bhittai to convey metaphysical concepts, such as the use of 'Tasawwur' (imagination) to bridge the gap between the mundane and the divine. Similarly, for modern fiction, one must evaluate how authors like Popati Hiranandani deconstruct traditional gender roles through narrative structure rather than just stating that she was a feminist.

The analysis of major works also involves understanding their reception history. How was 'Sur' received in Sindh versus how it is studied today in Indian universities? This shift from local oral transmission to academic textual analysis represents a crucial evolution in the status of Sindhi literature. For modern prose, one must evaluate the transition from short stories focused on individual tragedy to novels that attempt to capture the collective trauma of displacement and migration. The examiner might present a scenario where a student argues that Bhittai's work is outdated; your task is to refute this by highlighting its timeless philosophical core which transcends specific historical epochs.

Technique: Structural Deconstruction
Question: Analyze the structural evolution of Sindhi literature from the oral 'Sur' to the modern novel. How did this change affect the narrative voice?
Answer: The structural evolution marks a move from cyclical, repetitive, and participatory oral forms (where the audience completes the verse) to linear, complex, and internalized written narratives. In 'Sur', the narrative voice is often collective or divine, addressing the listener directly. In modern novels by authors like Popati Hiranandani, the narrative voice becomes individualistic and introspective, focusing on psychological depth. This shift allowed for the exploration of private trauma and complex identity crises that were unsuitable for the public, communal nature of oral poetry.

8. Exceptions and Landmarks in Literary History

Serious aspirants must identify exceptions to the general rule of literary development. While the Sufi tradition dominated early literature, there were periods of silence or stagnation interrupted by sudden bursts of innovation. The 'Landmark' case is the Partition of 1947, which forced a diaspora that fundamentally altered the themes of Sindhi literature from local pastoralism to global displacement. Another exception is the role of women writers who emerged despite a male-dominated literary establishment in the early 20th century. Popati Hiranandani is not just an author but a landmark case of gender fluidity in a patriarchal society, where her writing challenged the very definition of 'Sindhi identity' which was traditionally coded as masculine.

Furthermore, one must consider the linguistic exceptions. Sindhi literature exists in two scripts: Perso-Arabic and Devanagari. The choice of script is not neutral; it carries political weight regarding national identity (Pakistan vs. India). Textbook-standard facts often gloss over this, but a nuanced answer requires discussing how literary movements differed based on the script used. For example, certain modernist works were published exclusively in Devanagari to appeal to an Indian readership, while others remained in Perso-Arabic for a diaspora audience. This bifurcation is a critical data point that examiners love to test.

Technique: Identifying Exceptions
Question: Discuss the exceptions to the dominance of Sufi poetry in early Sindhi literature. How did non-Sufi voices emerge?
Answer: The exception to Sufi dominance lies in the emergence of secular and nationalist prose in the late 19th century. While Sufis like Latif and Sachal dominated, figures like Shaikh Ayaz introduced a more rationalist, reformist voice that questioned feudalism without relying on mystical allegory. Additionally, the diaspora literature of the post-Partition era represents another exception, where Sindhi writers adopted global themes and English influences, breaking the isolationist tradition of earlier Sindh-centric writing.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Script' Variable
Always mention the script (Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari) when discussing modern literature. It adds a layer of political sophistication to your answer and shows you understand the nuances of Sindhi literary identity beyond just names and dates.
  • Trap 1: Confusing the 'Early Period' (Sufi) with the 'Modern Period'. Ensure you clearly demarcate the timeline shifts.
  • Trap 2: Ignoring the script issue. Discussing modern authors without mentioning their preferred script is a significant oversight in Paper I.
  • Trap 3: Over-reliance on dates. Focus on thematic shifts and socio-political contexts rather than just listing birth/death years.
  • Trap 4: Neglecting the 'Diaspora' factor. Post-1947 literature is defined by displacement; ignoring this misses a key modern movement.

9. The Script-Bifurcation: A Political Topography of Modern Sindhi Literature

Sindhi literature presents a unique case study in the politics of language and script, where the choice between Perso-Arabic (P-A) and Devanagari (D) is not merely orthographic but constitutive of literary identity. The bifurcation began gaining momentum in the late 19th century with the arrival of missionaries and reformist thinkers who advocated for a vernacular script that could compete with Urdu and Hindi in print culture. Initially, P-A remained dominant due to its association with the Sufi tradition and the established elite (the Ulema). However, the Partition of 1947 acted as a catalyst, forcing Sindhi writers into a diaspora that necessitated new modes of communication.

The shift was not instantaneous. In the immediate post-Partition years, many Sindhi refugees in India continued to write in P-A because it was the script of their homeland and their cultural memory. However, as they settled in Indian states like Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra, a pragmatic need arose for scripts compatible with local publishing infrastructures (which were predominantly Devanagari). Consequently, a distinct literary stream emerged: Devanagari Sindhi Literature. This was not just a translation of P-A texts but a re-imagining of Sindhi aesthetics using the visual logic of Hindi/Urdu script. Authors like Mirza Ismail and later Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's translators (like Mir Hamidullah Khan) played pivotal roles, though the literary explosion in D happened later with authors like Popati Hiranandani (who wrote in both but navigated the ideological divide) and Gulshan Rana.

The political weight of this choice cannot be overstated. For a Sindhi writer publishing in Devanagari in 1950, it was an implicit declaration of alignment with the Indian state's linguistic policy, often viewed with suspicion by hardline nationalists in Pakistan who saw P-A as the sole marker of "true" Sindhi identity. Conversely, writing in P-A within India became a marker of cultural resistance and preservation against assimilation. The Sindhi Sahitya Akademi (established 1952) initially operated with a neutral stance but faced internal debates regarding script standardization. By the 1970s, the literary landscape had solidified into two parallel universes: the "P-A stream" which retained its Sufi, mystical, and rural pastoral roots; and the "D stream" which often engaged more directly with urban modernism, political realism, and the trauma of migration.

This duality complicates the study of literary movements. A movement like Sindhi Modernism cannot be understood without analyzing how its proponents chose their script. For instance, the Progressive Writers' Movement in Sindh had factions; some argued that to reach the masses (who were illiterate in P-A), one must use D because it was easier to learn for Hindus and Muslims alike in India. Others countered that abandoning P-A severed the link with the great Sufi heritage. The result is a rich, bifurcated canon where a single author might publish a novel in D and a poem in P-A, creating a dialogue between their two selves. Examiners often test this by asking students to identify which authors belong to which stream or how the script choice influenced the thematic content (e.g., more secular themes in D vs. mystical themes in P-A).

Examiner's trap
Question: "Why did some Sindhi writers switch from Perso-Arabic to Devanagari after 1947?"
Incorrect Answer: "Because they wanted to write in Hindi."
Correct Analysis: The answer must distinguish between linguistic substitution and script adaptation. Writers did not switch to "Hindi"; they switched to the script of the Indian subcontinent's print culture (Devanagari) while retaining the Sindhi language vocabulary and grammar. This was a strategic move to access publishing houses in Bombay/Mumbai that did not handle P-A well, and to appeal to an Indian readership that could read Hindi but not Urdu/Persian scripts. The ideological implication was about national integration vs. cultural preservation.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Script' Variable
Always mention the script (Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari) when discussing modern literature. It adds a layer of political sophistication to your answer and shows you understand the nuances of Sindhi literary identity beyond just names and dates.

10. The Diaspora Effect: From Local Pastoralism to Global Displacement

The Partition of 1947 is often cited as a historical event, but in the context of Sindhi literature, it functions as a structural rupture that redefined the genre itself. Before 1947, Sindhi literature was predominantly pastoral. The setting was the Indus valley: the date palm groves, the river banks, the feudal estates, and the Sufi shrines. The narrative arc usually revolved around local conflicts, romantic tragedies, or mystical encounters within a stable geographical entity (Sindh as a coherent province). The language itself was infused with Persian and Arabic loanwords that gave it a cosmopolitan yet rooted flavor.

Post-1947, the literature underwent a radical metamorphosis into diasporic literature. The "local" became impossible; the "global" (or at least, the trans-border Indian context) became the new horizon. This shift is not merely thematic but structural. The linear narrative of the novel replaced the cyclical nature of the oral poem because the diaspora experience was linear and progressive: leaving home, settling in a foreign land, facing alienation, and eventually finding a new identity. The novel became the preferred form because it could handle complex psychological states of displacement that short stories or poems could not.

The content shifted from celebrating the beauty of nature to documenting the horror of displacement. Themes of rootlessness, language anxiety (the fear of forgetting one's mother tongue), and identity crisis became central. Authors like Ahmed Faraz (though primarily a poet, his prose poetry reflects this) and novelists like Muhammad Iqbal Zaidi explored the psychological scars of migration. The "home" was no longer a physical place but a memory or a dream. This created a sub-genre of memory literature, where the act of writing itself became an act of preservation against erasure.

The diaspora also introduced new aesthetic influences. Sindhi writers in India were exposed to Hindi, English, and Gujarati literary trends, leading to a hybridization of style. The rigid structures of classical Sufi poetry gave way to free verse and experimental prose. However, this was not a clean break; the "Soul" of the literature remained Sufi, even if the "Body" (form and setting) changed. Examiners love to ask how the diaspora altered the reception of Sindhi literature. In Pakistan, Sindhi lit was read as part of a national canon; in India, it was read as a minority literature, often filtered through the lens of "minority rights" discourse. This external gaze influenced the writers themselves, who began to write with an awareness of how their work would be received by Indian audiences, leading to a self-conscious literary nationalism.

11. The Reception History: From Oral 'Sur' to Academic Textual Analysis

To understand Sindhi literature, one must distinguish between its oral transmission phase and its academic textual analysis phase. The earliest works, such as the Sur (poems of praise) attributed to figures like Latif or Sachal, were not meant for silent reading. They were performed in public gatherings, weddings, and religious processions. The audience was participatory; they would clap, chant along, and complete the verses with the performer.
