Sindhi Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Sindhi is an Indo-Aryan language with a literary tradition going back to the Sufi poets of Sindh. After Partition, Sindhi literature continued in India in Perso-Arabic and Devanagari scripts.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Early period (16th–18th c.): Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and the Sufi poetic tradition.
  • Nineteenth–early twentieth century: Print, prose and the Sindhi renaissance.
  • Modern period (20th c.–present): Partition literature, fiction, poetry and diaspora writing.

Major Authors

  • Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai: 18th-century Sufi poet; Sur Shah Latif.
  • Sachal Sarmast: Sufi poet.
  • Shaikh Ayaz: Modern Sindhi poet.
  • Popati Hiranandani: Sindhi writer and feminist voice.

Major Works

  • Sur Shah Latif — Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai: Sufi poetry collection.
  • Sindhi folk poetry — Oral tradition: Traditional songs and ballads.
  • Modern Sindhi poetry — Shaikh Ayaz: Twentieth-century poetry.
  • Sindhi fiction — Modern Sindhi writers: Twentieth-century short stories and novels.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai in the literary history of Sindhi and assess their contribution.
Answer: Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai belongs to the Early period (16th–18th c.) phase. 18th-century Sufi poet; Sur Shah Latif. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Early period (16th–18th c.) → Nineteenth–early twentieth century → Modern period (20th c.–present).
  • Authors: Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, Shaikh Ayaz, Popati Hiranandani.
  • Works: Sur Shah Latif, Sindhi folk poetry, Modern Sindhi poetry, Sindhi fiction.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

Comparative Analysis of Literary Movements

To master Paper I, one must move beyond simple chronology and understand the socio-political mechanisms that drove specific literary movements in Sindhi history. The transition from the Sufi oral tradition to the print-based renaissance was not merely a change in medium but a fundamental shift in epistemology. In the early period, literature served as a vehicle for spiritual instruction (Taqleef) within the rural agrarian context, utilizing metaphors drawn from nature and daily toil. Conversely, the 19th-century movement, spearheaded by figures like Shaikh Ayaz, introduced realism and social critique, shifting focus to the plight of the peasantry under feudal oppression. This section requires you to analyze how the 'Sufi' tradition evolved into 'Modernism' not just through time, but through the adoption of new scripts (Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari) which altered the phonetic representation and accessibility of the language.

The examiner often twists this concept by asking for a comparison between the thematic focus of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's 'Sur' and the prose works of Popati Hiranandani. The former relies on allegory where the human soul is a garden needing cultivation, while the latter employs direct realism to expose the structural inequalities of post-Partition society. A serious aspirant must articulate how the literary movement of 'Sindhi Renaissance' was defined by the emergence of prose (Nasr) alongside poetry (Shair), creating a dual-track development that allowed for the articulation of complex political ideas which were previously impossible in strictly rhymed verse.

Technique: Comparative Thematic Analysis
Question: Compare the socio-political consciousness found in the works of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai with that of Shaikh Ayaz. How did the shift from oral to print culture influence this consciousness?
Answer: Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's consciousness was rooted in spiritual egalitarianism, accessible to all castes through the medium of oral performance and the 'Sur' form, focusing on the unity of existence (Wahdat al-Wujud). Shaikh Ayaz, writing in the 19th century during the print renaissance, shifted towards socio-political realism. His works explicitly addressed feudal exploitation and the need for social reform. The shift to print culture democratized literature, moving it from the 'Majlis' (council) of Sufis to the public sphere, allowing for the articulation of specific political grievances and the standardization of the Sindhi language itself, which was previously fluid in oral tradition.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Why' over the 'Who'
When answering questions on movements, never stop at listing names. Always explain the 'Why'. Why did a movement start? Was it due to political partition, technological change (printing press), or spiritual revival? Connect the literary movement to the broader historical context of British Raj and Partition.

Critical Evaluation of Major Works: Textual Nuances

A high-scoring answer in Paper I demands a critical evaluation of major works, looking beyond surface-level summaries. For instance, when discussing 'Sur Shah Latif', one must analyze the linguistic architecture of the text. The work is not merely a collection of poems but a systematic theological treatise disguised as poetry. The examiner may ask you to identify specific rhetorical devices used by Bhittai to convey metaphysical concepts, such as the use of 'Tasawwur' (imagination) to bridge the gap between the mundane and the divine. Similarly, for modern fiction, one must evaluate how authors like Popati Hiranandani deconstruct traditional gender roles through narrative structure rather than just stating that she was a feminist.

The analysis of major works also involves understanding their reception history. How was 'Sur' received in Sindh versus how it is studied today in Indian universities? This shift from local oral transmission to academic textual analysis represents a crucial evolution in the status of Sindhi literature. For modern prose, one must evaluate the transition from short stories focused on individual tragedy to novels that attempt to capture the collective trauma of displacement and migration. The examiner might present a scenario where a student argues that Bhittai's work is outdated; your task is to refute this by highlighting its timeless philosophical core which transcends specific historical epochs.

Technique: Structural Deconstruction
Question: Analyze the structural evolution of Sindhi literature from the oral 'Sur' to the modern novel. How did this change affect the narrative voice?
Answer: The structural evolution marks a move from cyclical, repetitive, and participatory oral forms (where the audience completes the verse) to linear, complex, and internalized written narratives. In 'Sur', the narrative voice is often collective or divine, addressing the listener directly. In modern novels by authors like Popati Hiranandani, the narrative voice becomes individualistic and introspective, focusing on psychological depth. This shift allowed for the exploration of private trauma and complex identity crises that were unsuitable for the public, communal nature of oral poetry.

Exceptions and Landmarks in Literary History

Serious aspirants must identify exceptions to the general rule of literary development. While the Sufi tradition dominated early literature, there were periods of silence or stagnation interrupted by sudden bursts of innovation. The 'Landmark' case is the Partition of 1947, which forced a diaspora that fundamentally altered the themes of Sindhi literature from local pastoralism to global displacement. Another exception is the role of women writers who emerged despite a male-dominated literary establishment in the early 20th century. Popati Hiranandani is not just an author but a landmark case of gender fluidity in a patriarchal society, where her writing challenged the very definition of 'Sindhi identity' which was traditionally coded as masculine.

Furthermore, one must consider the linguistic exceptions. Sindhi literature exists in two scripts: Perso-Arabic and Devanagari. The choice of script is not neutral; it carries political weight regarding national identity (Pakistan vs. India). Textbook-standard facts often gloss over this, but a nuanced answer requires discussing how literary movements differed based on the script used. For example, certain modernist works were published exclusively in Devanagari to appeal to an Indian readership, while others remained in Perso-Arabic for a diaspora audience. This bifurcation is a critical data point that examiners love to test.

Technique: Identifying Exceptions
Question: Discuss the exceptions to the dominance of Sufi poetry in early Sindhi literature. How did non-Sufi voices emerge?
Answer: The exception to Sufi dominance lies in the emergence of secular and nationalist prose in the late 19th century. While Sufis like Latif and Sachal dominated, figures like Shaikh Ayaz introduced a more rationalist, reformist voice that questioned feudalism without relying on mystical allegory. Additionally, the diaspora literature of the post-Partition era represents another exception, where Sindhi writers adopted global themes and English influences, breaking the isolationist tradition of earlier Sindh-centric writing.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Script' Variable
Always mention the script (Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari) when discussing modern literature. It adds a layer of political sophistication to your answer and shows you understand the nuances of Sindhi literary identity beyond just names and dates.
  • Trap 1: Confusing the 'Early Period' (Sufi) with the 'Modern Period'. Ensure you clearly demarcate the timeline shifts.
  • Trap 2: Ignoring the script issue. Discussing modern authors without mentioning their preferred script is a significant oversight in Paper I.
  • Trap 3: Over-reliance on dates. Focus on thematic shifts and socio-political contexts rather than just listing birth/death years.
  • Trap 4: Neglecting the 'Diaspora' factor. Post-1947 literature is defined by displacement; ignoring this misses a key modern movement.

The Script-Bifurcation: A Political Topography of Modern Sindhi Literature

Sindhi literature presents a unique case study in the politics of language and script, where the choice between Perso-Arabic (P-A) and Devanagari (D) is not merely orthographic but constitutive of literary identity. The bifurcation began gaining momentum in the late 19th century with the arrival of missionaries and reformist thinkers who advocated for a vernacular script that could compete with Urdu and Hindi in print culture. Initially, P-A remained dominant due to its association with the Sufi tradition and the established elite (the Ulema). However, the Partition of 1947 acted as a catalyst, forcing Sindhi writers into a diaspora that necessitated new modes of communication.

The shift was not instantaneous. In the immediate post-Partition years, many Sindhi refugees in India continued to write in P-A because it was the script of their homeland and their cultural memory. However, as they settled in Indian states like Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra, a pragmatic need arose for scripts compatible with local publishing infrastructures (which were predominantly Devanagari). Consequently, a distinct literary stream emerged: Devanagari Sindhi Literature. This was not just a translation of P-A texts but a re-imagining of Sindhi aesthetics using the visual logic of Hindi/Urdu script. Authors like Mirza Ismail and later Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai's translators (like Mir Hamidullah Khan) played pivotal roles, though the literary explosion in D happened later with authors like Popati Hiranandani (who wrote in both but navigated the ideological divide) and Gulshan Rana.

The political weight of this choice cannot be overstated. For a Sindhi writer publishing in Devanagari in 1950, it was an implicit declaration of alignment with the Indian state's linguistic policy, often viewed with suspicion by hardline nationalists in Pakistan who saw P-A as the sole marker of "true" Sindhi identity. Conversely, writing in P-A within India became a marker of cultural resistance and preservation against assimilation. The Sindhi Sahitya Akademi (established 1952) initially operated with a neutral stance but faced internal debates regarding script standardization. By the 1970s, the literary landscape had solidified into two parallel universes: the "P-A stream" which retained its Sufi, mystical, and rural pastoral roots; and the "D stream" which often engaged more directly with urban modernism, political realism, and the trauma of migration.

This duality complicates the study of literary movements. A movement like Sindhi Modernism cannot be understood without analyzing how its proponents chose their script. For instance, the Progressive Writers' Movement in Sindh had factions; some argued that to reach the masses (who were illiterate in P-A), one must use D because it was easier to learn for Hindus and Muslims alike in India. Others countered that abandoning P-A severed the link with the great Sufi heritage. The result is a rich, bifurcated canon where a single author might publish a novel in D and a poem in P-A, creating a dialogue between their two selves. Examiners often test this by asking students to identify which authors belong to which stream or how the script choice influenced the thematic content (e.g., more secular themes in D vs. mystical themes in P-A).

Examiner's trap
Question: "Why did some Sindhi writers switch from Perso-Arabic to Devanagari after 1947?"
Incorrect Answer: "Because they wanted to write in Hindi."
Correct Analysis: The answer must distinguish between linguistic substitution and script adaptation. Writers did not switch to "Hindi"; they switched to the script of the Indian subcontinent's print culture (Devanagari) while retaining the Sindhi language vocabulary and grammar. This was a strategic move to access publishing houses in Bombay/Mumbai that did not handle P-A well, and to appeal to an Indian readership that could read Hindi but not Urdu/Persian scripts. The ideological implication was about national integration vs. cultural preservation.

Always mention the script (Perso-Arabic vs. Devanagari) when discussing modern literature. It adds a layer of political sophistication to your answer and shows you understand the nuances of Sindhi literary identity beyond just names and dates.

The Diaspora Effect: From Local Pastoralism to Global Displacement

The Partition of 1947 is often cited as a historical event, but in the context of Sindhi literature, it functions as a structural rupture that redefined the genre itself. Before 1947, Sindhi literature was predominantly pastoral. The setting was the Indus valley: the date palm groves, the river banks, the feudal estates, and the Sufi shrines. The narrative arc usually revolved around local conflicts, romantic tragedies, or mystical encounters within a stable geographical entity (Sindh as a coherent province). The language itself was infused with Persian and Arabic loanwords that gave it a cosmopolitan yet rooted flavor.

Post-1947, the literature underwent a radical metamorphosis into diasporic literature. The "local" became impossible; the "global" (or at least, the trans-border Indian context) became the new horizon. This shift is not merely thematic but structural. The linear narrative of the novel replaced the cyclical nature of the oral poem because the diaspora experience was linear and progressive: leaving home, settling in a foreign land, facing alienation, and eventually finding a new identity. The novel became the preferred form because it could handle complex psychological states of displacement that short stories or poems could not.

The content shifted from celebrating the beauty of nature to documenting the horror of displacement. Themes of rootlessness, language anxiety (the fear of forgetting one's mother tongue), and identity crisis became central. Authors like Ahmed Faraz (though primarily a poet, his prose poetry reflects this) and novelists like Muhammad Iqbal Zaidi explored the psychological scars of migration. The "home" was no longer a physical place but a memory or a dream. This created a sub-genre of memory literature, where the act of writing itself became an act of preservation against erasure.

The diaspora also introduced new aesthetic influences. Sindhi writers in India were exposed to Hindi, English, and Gujarati literary trends, leading to a hybridization of style. The rigid structures of classical Sufi poetry gave way to free verse and experimental prose. However, this was not a clean break; the "Soul" of the literature remained Sufi, even if the "Body" (form and setting) changed. Examiners love to ask how the diaspora altered the reception of Sindhi literature. In Pakistan, Sindhi lit was read as part of a national canon; in India, it was read as a minority literature, often filtered through the lens of "minority rights" discourse. This external gaze influenced the writers themselves, who began to write with an awareness of how their work would be received by Indian audiences, leading to a self-conscious literary nationalism.

The Reception History: From Oral 'Sur' to Academic Textual Analysis

To understand Sindhi literature, one must distinguish between its oral transmission phase and its academic textual analysis phase. The earliest works, such as the Sur (poems of praise) attributed to figures like Latif or Sachal, were not meant for silent reading. They were performed in public gatherings, weddings, and religious processions. The audience was participatory; they would clap, chant along, and complete the verses with the performer.

Sufi Poetics and the Evolution of the Kafi Form

The kafi stands as the most distinctive poetic vehicle of Sindhi Sufi expression. Rooted in the oral traditions of the Indus delta, the form crystallised during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries when wandering mystics adapted the Persian ghazal’s rhyme scheme to the rhythmic cadences of local folk metres. Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai (1689–1752) systematised the kafi by organising his Shah Jo Risalo into thirty‑seven sur (musical modes), each anchored to a specific raga and a thematic cluster — love, separation, divine union. His kafis employ a refrain (dhruv) that recurs after every couplet, creating a cyclical structure that mirrors the Sufi practice of dhikr (remembrance).

Sachal Sarmast (1739–1829) expanded the kafi’s semantic range by infusing it with philosophical discourse drawn from Ibn Arabi’s wahdat al‑wujud and the Nath yogic vocabulary. His kafis often juxtapose the language of the Quran with the terminology of Hindu tantra, a syncretic strategy that made the form accessible across communal lines. The refrain in Sachal’s work becomes a doctrinal anchor rather than a purely musical device, signalling a shift from performative devotion to textual theology.

During the nineteenth century, the advent of print culture (the first Sindhi press at Karachi, 1848) forced the kafi from fluid oral transmission into fixed textual editions. Editors such as Mirza Qaleech Beg standardised orthography in the Perso‑Arabic script, which inadvertently fossilised certain dialectal variants. The printed Risalo became a canonical reference, and later poets — notably Bedil (1815–1873) and Bekas (1855–1928) — composed kafis that consciously echoed Latif’s metrical patterns while addressing colonial modernity, British legal codes, and the rise of vernacular journalism.

In the twentieth century, the kafi survived both the Partition rupture and the script bifurcation. In Pakistan, the form remained a staple of radio broadcasts (Radio Pakistan’s Sur Sangam programme, 1950s) and was taught in university curricula as a classical genre. In India, writers like Sheikh Ayaz (1923–1997) re‑imagined the kafi in free verse, stripping the refrain but retaining the thematic core of ishq (love) as a metaphor for political resistance. This dual trajectory illustrates how a single poetic architecture can accommodate both preservationist and avant‑garde impulses without losing its identificatory force.

Modernist Experiments: Free Verse and the Progressive Writers' Association in Sindhi

The All‑India Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA), founded in 1936, established a Sindhi chapter in Bombay by 1938 under the leadership of Narayan Shyam and Gobind Malhi. The PWA’s manifesto demanded literature that exposed feudal exploitation, colonial oppression, and communal prejudice. Sindhi writers responded by abandoning the rigid metres of the kafi and the bait in favour of free verse (azad nazm) and socially realistic short stories. The first major anthology of Sindhi free verse, Naye Sur (1944), edited by Ayaz, showcased poems that employed enjambment, colloquial diction, and direct address — techniques borrowed from Hindi chhayavad and Urdu jadidiyat.

Sheikh Ayaz’s poem “Zindagi” (1946) exemplifies the modernist break: it discards the refrain, uses irregular line lengths, and juxtaposes industrial imagery (factory chimneys, railway tracks) with the traditional motif of the Indus river. The poem’s speaker is a displaced labourer, a figure absent from classical Sufi poetry. Similarly, the short‑story writer Muhammad Iqbal Zaidi (1915–1975) adopted a Chekhovian slice‑of‑life technique in “Gharib” (1950), focusing on the psychological interiority of a migrant family in Karachi’s refugee camps.

The PWA’s influence waned after the 1950s due to state censorship in Pakistan and the rise of cultural nationalism in India, but its formal legacy persisted. In the 1970s, the “Naya Kavita” movement, spearheaded by poets such as Attiya Dawood (b. 1958) and Harris Khalique (b. 1966), reintroduced experimental typography, code‑switching between Sindhi, Urdu, and English, and intertextual references to global modernists like T.S. Eliot and Pablo Neruda. These poets treated the Sindhi language as a pliable medium capable of bearing postmodern fragmentation while still invoking the Sufi lexicon of faqr (poverty) and sabr (patience) as subversive counters to state narratives.

Critically, the modernist shift was not a clean rupture. Many PWA members continued to compose kafis for ceremonial occasions, and the Risalo remained a required text in university syllabi. The coexistence of classical and modernist registers created a bilingual literary field where a single author could publish a traditional kafi in a Sufi shrine’s newsletter and a free‑verse polemic in a literary journal within the same year. This duality is a hallmark of Sindhi literary modernity and a frequent focus of examination questions on form‑history interaction.

Technique
Explain how the Progressive Writers' Association reshaped Sindhi poetic form in the 1940s, citing at least two specific textual innovations.
The PWA introduced free verse (azad nazm) by abandoning the fixed refrain and metrical cycles of the kafi, allowing enjambment and irregular line lengths. It also adopted colloquial, urban diction — factory imagery, railway motifs — replacing the pastoral vocabulary of the Indus delta. These innovations appear in Sheikh Ayaz’s “Zindagi” (1946) and the anthology Naye Sur (1944), marking a shift from devotional cyclicality to linear, socially engaged narration.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for “formal innovation”, name the specific device (e.g., enjambment, code‑switching, typographic experimentation) and link it to a named work and year. Avoid vague statements like “they modernised poetry”.

Women's Voices: From Folk Ballads to Contemporary Feminist Narratives

Sindhi women’s literary production begins in the oral sphere with the lada (wedding songs) and doha (couplets) performed at lifecycle rituals. These genres, transmitted matrilineally, encode gendered knowledge — childbirth, marital negotiation, mourning — within metaphorical frameworks that often subvert patriarchal norms. For instance, the lada “Mohan jo Sohag” uses the bride’s adornment as a coded critique of dowry demands, a reading confirmed by ethnographic work of the 1970s (e.g., N. A. Baloch, Sindhi Folk Literature, 1979).

The first published woman poet in Sindhi is widely recognised as Husna Bai (fl. 1920s), whose ghazals appeared in the Karachi journal Mehran. Her work retains the Persianised lexicon of the classical ghazal but introduces a first‑person female speaker who articulates desire without the mediation of a male mystic persona. In the post‑Partition era, the refugee experience amplified women’s writing. The short‑story writer Popati Hiranandani (1924–2005) published Kahani (1955), a collection that foregrounds the loss of domestic space and the renegotiation of identity in Bombay’s Sindhi colonies.

From the 1980s onward, a self‑consciously feminist current emerges. Attiya Dawood’s poetry volumes Sindhri (1990) and Raat (1998) deploy free verse, fragmented syntax, and direct address to the state, confronting honour killings, forced marriages, and the policing of women’s mobility. Her poem “Chadar” (1995) re‑appropriates the veil as a symbol of resistance rather than submission. Simultaneously, the novelist Naseem Kharal (b. 1962) published Zindagi ke Rang (2001), a realist narrative that traces three generations of Sindhi women navigating education, employment, and diaspora.

Academic reception has shifted from treating these texts as “women’s writing” to analysing them through intersectional frameworks that consider class, script choice (Perso‑Arabic vs. Devanagari), and the politics of translation. The Sahitya Akademi’s 2015 award to Attiya Dawood marked institutional recognition of feminist Sindhi literature as a canonical strand rather than a peripheral category.

Translation Politics: Sindhi Literature in English and Hindi Mediations

Translation has functioned as both a bridge and a gatekeeper for Sindhi literature. The earliest English renderings appeared in the late nineteenth century when British civil servants such as Richard Francis Burton produced selections from the Risalo for the Royal Asiatic Society (1880). These translations privileged the mystical dimension, often excising the socio‑political commentary embedded in Latif’s sur. The colonial framing positioned Sindhi Sufism as a “peaceful” counterpart to “militant” Islam, a trope that persists in some Western anthologies.

In the post‑Independence period, the Sahitya Akademi launched a systematic translation programme. The first volume of Sindhi Short Stories in English Translation (1972), edited by K. R. Srinivasa Iyengar, introduced writers like Sheikh Ayaz and Muhammad Iqbal Zaidi to a national readership. However, the editorial policy favoured stories that conformed to the “national integration” narrative, marginalising Partition‑trauma narratives that were deemed politically sensitive.

Hindi translations followed a different trajectory. The Delhi‑based Sindhi Academy (established 1975) commissioned Hindi versions of classical texts, notably the Shah Jo Risalo (Hindi, 1983) translated by Dr. L. H. Ajwani. These editions used Devanagari script and Sanskritised vocabulary, a choice that facilitated access for Hindi‑speaking scholars but altered the phonological texture of the original. Critics argue that such translations perform a subtle cultural assimilation, replacing Perso‑Arabic lexical layers with Sanskritic equivalents.

Contemporary translation practice is more plural. The bilingual journal Sindhi World (launched 2005) publishes parallel texts in Sindhi (both scripts), English, and Hindi, allowing comparative reading. Digital platforms like the “Sindhi Digital Library” (2018) host community‑sourced translations, enabling diaspora readers to contribute annotations. This decentralised model challenges the earlier top‑down canonisation and raises new questions about authority, fidelity, and the politics of script in translation.

Institutional Frameworks: Sahitya Akademi, Sindhi Academy, and Canon Formation

The institutional architecture that shapes Sindhi literary canonisation operates at three levels: national (Sahitya Akademi), state (Sindhi Academy, established in Delhi 1975 and in Karachi 1970), and civil society (literary associations such as the Sindhi Adabi Sangat, founded 1952). The Sahitya Akademi’s recognition of Sindhi as one of its twenty‑four languages in 1955 enabled annual awards, translation grants, and the publication of the Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature entries on Sindhi authors. The first Sahitya Akademi Award for Sindhi went to Sheikh Ayaz in 1968 for his poetry collection Kali Patam, signalling the state’s endorsement of modernist poetry over classical Sufi forms.

The Sindhi Academy in Delhi has focused on language preservation through textbook production, script standardisation (promoting Devanagari alongside Perso‑Arabic), and the organisation of the annual Sindhi Sahitya Sammelan. Its 1990 decision to publish a Devanagari‑script edition of the Risalo sparked debate: proponents argued it democratised access for Hindi‑educated youth; opponents viewed it as a dilution of the script’s historical continuity. The Academy’s funding priorities — favouring prose fiction and critical essays over oral genres — have steered scholarly attention toward the novel and short story, marginalising the sur and bait traditions.

In Pakistan, the Sindhi Language Authority (established 1990) and the University of Sindh’s Department of Sindhi perform analogous functions. The Authority’s 1995 orthographic reform standardised spelling in the Perso‑Arabic script, a move that facilitated digital encoding but erased regional orthographic variations. The University’s journal Mehran (revived 1972) remains the primary venue for peer‑reviewed criticism, yet its editorial board has historically privileged Marxist‑oriented literary criticism, shaping the interpretive lens through which modern Sindhi literature is taught.

Civil‑society organisations such as the Sindhi Adabi Sangat (Karachi) and the All‑India Sindhi Writers’ Forum (Mumbai) operate independently of state funding, hosting readings, publishing little magazines (Kahani, Sur), and maintaining archives of manuscript material. Their role in canon formation is evident in the posthumous revival of neglected writers like the poet‑journalist Gobind Malhi (1910–1979), whose collected works were published by the Sangat in 2003 after decades of obscurity. The interplay between state institutions and autonomous literary networks creates a dynamic, contested field where canonisation is never final but continuously negotiated.

The Role of the Printing Press and Periodicals in Shaping Modern Sindhi Prose

The introduction of movable‑type printing to Sindh in the 1850s, first through the Karachi‑based Sindh Gazette (1855) and later the missionary press at Hyderabad (1862), created a material infrastructure that transformed oral‑dominant literary culture into a print‑centric one. Early periodicals such as Mahanama (1880), Sindh Sudhar (1889) and Al‑Watan (1908) served three interlocking functions: they standardised orthography in the Perso‑Arabic script, they provided a regular venue for serialized fiction, and they forged a reading public that cut across the traditional guru‑shishya transmission of Sufi poetry. The editorial policies of these journals were explicitly reformist; Sindh Sudhar championed women’s education and published the first short stories by women writers such as Begum Khairunnisa (c. 1910). By the 1920s the literary magazine Mehran (revived 1925) became the primary arena for the Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA) in Sindh, publishing manifestoes, critical essays, and the early poems of Sheikh Ayaz. The periodical format also enabled the short story to overtake the long narrative poem as the dominant prose form, because the economics of monthly issues favoured concise, self‑contained narratives. Consequently, the canon of modern Sindhi prose — represented by the collections Kahaniyun (1935) by Gobind Malhi and Zindagi Ji Kahani (1942) by Jamal Abro — was largely assembled in the pages of these journals before any book‑length publication existed.

The Partition of 1947 and its Impact on Sindhi Literary Geography

The 1947 Partition displaced the entire Sindhi Hindu intelligentsia from the newly created Pakistan to India, producing a bifurcated literary field that still defines Sindhi letters. In Pakistan, the Sindhi language retained its official status in the province of Sindh, and the University of Sindh (established 1947) became the institutional hub for research, criticism, and the preservation of the Perso‑Arabic script. The literary journal Mehran continued uninterrupted, and the Sindhi Language Authority (1990) later standardised orthography for digital encoding. In India, the refugee community established the Sindhi Academy in Delhi (1975) and the All‑India Sindhi Writers’ Forum (Mumbai, 1952) to compensate for the loss of a territorial base. The Indian side adopted Devanagari as a parallel script from the 1960s onward, a decision driven by the need to integrate Sindhi into the Hindi‑medium school system. This script bifurcation produced two distinct canons: Pakistani anthologies foreground the classical Sufi corpus (Shah Abdul Latif, Sachal Sarmast) and the Marxist‑oriented modernism of the PWA, while Indian anthologies emphasise the short story, the novel, and the diaspora experience, often rendered in Devanagari. The Partition also generated a body of “migration literature” — memoirs such as Muhajir (1955) by Krishin Khatwani and the poetry collection Vichhoda (1960) by Motilal Jotwani — that articulates loss, linguistic alienation, and the negotiation of identity in a new national context. Comparative study of these parallel trajectories reveals how political borders reshape literary taste, institutional support, and even the material form of the text.

The Development of Sindhi Drama: From Folk Performance to Modern Stage

Sindhi dramatic tradition originates in the folk performance forms of Bhagat (musical storytelling), Ras (dance‑drama), and the Sufi Dhamal gatherings at shrines. These oral‑performative genres relied on improvisation, communal participation, and the sur melodic framework. The first scripted play in Sindhi, Shah Jo Risalo‑inspired Momal Rano (1912) by Mirza Qaleech Beg, marked the transition to a written dramatic text intended for proscenium staging. The 1930s saw the emergence of the “social reform” theatre, exemplified by Sindh Ji Kahani (1935) by Gobind Malhi, which tackled child marriage and widow remarriage. After Partition, the Indian diaspora established the Sindhi Natya Mandal (Mumbai, 1955) and the Delhi‑based Sindhi Rangmanch (1962), producing plays such as Udaas Naslain (1968) by Krishin Khatwani that dramatised refugee trauma. In Pakistan, the Pakistan Television Corporation (PTV) began broadcasting Sindhi teleplays in the 1970s, giving playwrights like Amar Jaleel a mass audience. The 1990s introduced experimental theatre influenced by Bertolt Brecht and Augusto Boal, with groups such as the Karachi‑based “Tehreek‑e‑Adab” staging Kafir (1994) by Aslam Ansari, which interrogated religious orthodoxy. Contemporary Sindhi drama thus oscillates between community‑centred folk revival (e.g., the annual Bhagat festivals in Gujarat) and avant‑garde political theatre, reflecting the dual pressures of cultural preservation and modern critical discourse.

The Evolution of Sindhi Criticism and Literary Theory

Literary criticism in Sindhi moved from the traditional tafsir (exegesis) of Sufi poetry — practiced by scholars such as Makhdoom Muhammad Hashim Thattvi (d. 1761) — to formal academic criticism after the establishment of the University of Sindh’s Department of Sindhi (1951). The first generation of modern critics, led by Dr. Nabi Bux Baloch, applied historical‑philological methods to establish textual chronologies for the Risalo and the Kafi corpus. The 1970s witnessed the import of Marxist aesthetics through the Progressive Writers’ Association; critics like Shaikh Ayaz (in his essays collected as Adabi Tanqeed, 1975) argued for literature as a vehicle of class consciousness. In the 1990s, post‑structuralist and feminist frameworks entered Sindhi academia via translations of Terry Eagleton and Gayatri Spivak, prompting scholars such as Dr. Attiya Dawood to reinterpret folk songs as sites of gender resistance. The journal Mehran (revived 1972) remains the primary peer‑reviewed venue, but its editorial board has historically privileged Marxist‑oriented readings, marginalising postcolonial and diaspora perspectives. Recent digital initiatives — the Sindhi Digital Library (2018) and the open‑access repository “Sindhi Shodh” — have democratized access to critical essays, enabling younger scholars to contest the established canon and to apply ecocriticism, trauma theory, and translation studies to Sindhi texts.

The Interplay of Language Policy and Education in Post‑Independence India

India’s constitutional recognition of Sindhi as a scheduled language (1967) triggered a series of policy decisions that shaped literary production. The Three‑Language Formula (1968) mandated Sindhi as a third language in schools of states with significant Sindhi populations — primarily Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Rajasthan. Textbook committees, dominated by the Sindhi Academy (Delhi), opted for Devanagari script editions from the 1970s, arguing that the script facilitated learning for children already literate in Hindi. This choice sparked a script‑politics debate: proponents claimed increased enrolment; opponents warned of lexical Sanskritisation eroding the Perso‑Arabic lexical layer that carries Sufi and Persianate nuance. The 1992 National Curriculum Framework reinforced mother‑tongue education, leading to the publication of the Sindhi Bhasha Pathyakram (1995) — a standardized grammar and reader used across state boards. Simultaneously, the University Grants Commission funded Sindhi departments at Mumbai University (1970) and Gujarat University (1975), creating academic posts that incentivised research in modern criticism and comparative literature. The policy environment also affected publishing: the National Book Trust’s Sindhi series (launched 1980) prioritized novels and short‑story anthologies over poetry, aligning with the prose‑centric curriculum. Consequently, the post‑independence generation of writers — such as Vasdev Mohi, Hiro Shewakramani, and Popati Hiranandani — produced predominantly prose fiction, while the classical poetic forms survived mainly in community‑run mushairas and shrine festivals.

Sindhi Lexical Borrowings and Semantic Shifts

The vocabulary of modern Sindhi is a palimpsest of Persian‑Arabic, Sanskrit‑Prakrit, and English layers, each entering through distinct historical channels. Persian‑Arabic loans arrived with Sufi orders and Mughal administration (13th–18th centuries), contributing terms such as ilm (knowledge), adab (literature), and mazhab (religion). Sanskrit‑derived words entered via the Bhakti movement and later through Arya Samaj reformists, giving gyan, dharm, and punya. The colonial period introduced English technical and administrative vocabulary — court, police, station — which were nativised phonologically (e.g., kort, pulīs).

Semantic shift is evident in words that retain the original form but acquire new cultural valences. Sufi originally denoted a mystic; in contemporary Sindhi criticism it also signals a literary aesthetic of interiority. Kafi shifted from a musical genre to a poetic form signifying philosophical brevity. The term adabi moved from “pertaining to literature” to a qualifier for “intellectual” in academic discourse. Borrowings from English often undergo morphological integration: print → prinṭ, publish → pablis, with the Sindhi suffix -ando forming verbs (prinṭando). Mid‑20th‑century Devanagari textbooks accelerated Sanskritisation of technical terminology (e.g., vigyan for science), prompting a counter‑movement among Persian‑script writers to preserve the older lexical stratum.

Standard dictionaries such as the Sindhi‑English Dictionary and the later Sindhi Lughat document these layers, but the living language continues to negotiate them in journalism, digital media, and diaspora poetry. Understanding the chronology of borrowing aids textual criticism: a poem using ilm in a pre‑colonial manuscript signals Sufi provenance, whereas the same word in a late‑20th‑century essay may simply denote “education”.

Sindhi Short Story: From Realism to Postmodern Fragmentation

The Sindhi short story (afsano) emerged in the 1920s through periodicals like Sindh Sudhar and Mehran. Early practitioners — Gobind Malhi, Lalchand Amardinomal — employed social realism, depicting rural poverty, zamindari oppression, and the trauma of Partition. Their narratives follow a linear cause‑effect structure, omniscient narration, and a clear moral resolution, mirroring the Progressive Writers’ Association manifesto (1936).

The 1960s–70s saw a modernist turn. Writers such as Shaikh Ayaz and Vasdev Mohi experimented with interior monologue, non‑chronological sequencing, and symbolic imagery. Ayaz’s Kali‑Kali Raat fragments the protagonist’s memory of migration into disjointed vignettes, reflecting the influence of European existentialism transmitted via Hindi and Urdu translations. The Progressive critique of feudalism persisted but was refracted through psychological depth rather than external exposition.

From the 1990s onward, postmodern techniques — metafiction, intertextuality, unreliable narration — appear in the work of younger authors like Hiro Shewakramani and Popati Hiranandani. Shewakramani’s Chhundo embeds a story within a story, commenting on the act of storytelling itself, while Hiranandani’s Manjhi juxtaposes folk motifs with urban alienation, using code‑switching between Devanagari and Perso‑Arabic scripts to visualise linguistic dislocation. The diaspora context (Gujarat, Maharashtra, UAE) adds a transnational dimension: stories circulate in digital magazines where hypertextual links replace footnotes.

Critical reception tracks this trajectory. Early Marxist critics praised the realist phase for its class consciousness; later scholars apply narratology and trauma theory to read fragmentation as a symptom of Partition‑induced memory rupture. The short story thus serves as a laboratory for Sindhi literary modernity, compressing the language’s historical pressures into a form that remains both accessible and experimentally fertile.

Sindhi Literary Journals and Canon Formation

Periodicals have functioned as the primary gatekeepers of the Sindhi canon since the late 19th century. Sindh Sudhar, edited by Dayaram Gidumal, published early printed versions of Shah Abdul Latif’s Risalo alongside reformist essays, establishing a textual baseline. The launch of Mehran created a sustained platform for progressive poetry, criticism, and short fiction; its editorial board, dominated by Marxist critics, privileged works that aligned with class‑struggle narratives, thereby shaping the “Progressive canon” of the mid‑20th century.

Simultaneously, Kahani and Sindhi Adab specialised in short stories and literary criticism respectively, providing space for modernist experimentation that Mehran occasionally marginalised. Later decades saw the emergence of peer‑reviewed journals backed by university departments, which introduced post‑structuralist and feminist frameworks. Digital repositories now host scanned runs of these journals, enabling citation‑network analysis that reveals how certain authors accumulate canonical capital through repeated anthologisation.

Canon formation is thus not a static list but a dynamic process mediated by editorial policy, institutional funding, and script choice. Journals publishing in Devanagari after the textbook shift expanded readership among Hindi‑literate Sindhi youth but simultaneously excluded Perso‑Arabic‑script contributors, creating a bifurcated canon that scholars now attempt to reconcile through bilingual critical editions.

Sindhi Orthography Debates and Script Reform

The script question has been a site of linguistic politics since the mid‑19th‑century British administration standardised the Perso‑Arabic script for official use. The 1947 Partition displaced the script’s institutional base to Pakistan, while India’s Sindhi refugees adopted Devanagari for pragmatic reasons — compatibility with Hindi‑medium schools and the Three‑Language Formula (1968). The Sindhi Academy (Delhi) commissioned a Devanagari‑based grammar and reader, arguing that the script reduces the learning curve for children already literate in Hindi.

Opponents, notably the All‑India Sindhi Writers’ Forum, contend that Devanagari imposes Sanskrit phonology (e.g., retroflex ṭ, ḍ vs. dental t, d) and erodes the Perso‑Arabic lexical layer that carries Sufi nuance. They point to the loss of the ʿayn and ḥāʾ phonemes, which have no Devanagari equivalents, leading to homophony in words like ‘ilm (knowledge) and ilm (a variant spelling). The Unicode inclusion of the Khudabadi script revived a historic indigenous script, but adoption remains limited to cultural societies.

Scholarly studies suggest that Devanagari textbooks increased enrolment in some regions but correlated with a measurable decline in students’ ability to read classical Perso‑Arabic manuscripts. The debate therefore balances pedagogical access against philological fidelity — a tension that exam questions often frame as “script policy versus literary heritage”.

Sindhi Folk Narrative Structures and Their Literary Adaptations

Oral folk narratives — dastan (epic romance), lada (dialogue song), bait (couplet) — provide structural templates that modern writers consciously rework. The dastan of Umar‑Marvi and Sassui‑Punhu follows a tripartite pattern: separation, trial, and reunion, mirrored in the narrative arc of Vasdev Mohi’s novel Jeevan‑Jyot, where the protagonist’s migration, existential crisis, and return to cultural roots echo the folk schema.

The lada form, a call‑and‑response between male and female voices, informs the dialogic structure of Shaikh Ayaz’s poem Samvad, where the poet alternates between the voice of the displaced self and the collective memory of the homeland. Bait couplets, with their strict metrical symmetry, underlie the concise aphoristic style of contemporary micro‑fiction published in journals like Kahani.

Scholars such as Dr. Nabi Bux Baloch have mapped these correspondences through comparative textual analysis, demonstrating that adaptation is not mere quotation but a re‑functionalisation: the folk motif of the “river crossing” becomes a metaphor for linguistic transition in diaspora poetry. Recognising these deep structures enables candidates to trace continuity beneath apparent modernist rupture, a frequent requirement in comparative literature questions.

Persianate Literary Models and the Formation of Classical Sindhi Poetics

The classical phase of Sindhi poetry (c. 1600‑1850) cannot be understood without reference to the Persianate literary ecology that dominated the courts of the Kalhora and Talpur dynasties. Persian was the language of administration, high culture, and Sufi discourse; Sindhi poets therefore absorbed Persian metres (ʿarūḍ), forms (ghazal, masnavi, qaṣīda, rubāʿī), and a shared symbolic lexicon — wine, tavern, nightingale, rose — that mediated mystical experience. Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai (1689‑1752) composed his Shah Jo Risalo in the kafi and bait forms, yet the structural scaffolding of his sur cycles mirrors the Persian masnavi’s narrative progression. Sachal Sarmast (1739‑1829) explicitly invoked Persian masters such as Rumi and Hafiz, embedding Persian couplets within his Sindhi verses to signal doctrinal continuity. The lexical borrowing was systematic: words for abstract concepts (e.g., ishq, maʿrifat, taṣawwuf) entered Sindhi without phonological adaptation, preserving the semantic nuance of the Persian original. This borrowing was not superficial; it reshaped Sindhi prosody, introducing quantitative metres that coexisted with the indigenous stress‑based bait. Manuscript colophons from the 18th‑century libraries of Thatta and Sehwan record the copying of Persian anthologies alongside Sindhi collections, confirming a shared scribal culture. The result is a hybrid poetics where the kafi retains its folk‑rooted refrain while its internal architecture follows Persian rhyme‑scheme logic. Candidates should be able to trace a specific motif — such as the “cup‑bearer” (sāqī) — from Persian ghazal into Latif’s Sur Sarang and explain how the motif’s theological valence shifts from erotic longing to divine grace.

Equally important is the role of the madrasa and khanqah networks in transmitting Persian literary theory. The Dars‑e‑Nizami curriculum, adapted in Sindhi seminaries, taught Persian rhetoric (balāgha) and prosody (ʿilm al‑ʿarūḍ) as core subjects. Graduates such as Sami (1743‑1850) produced commentaries on Persian classics while composing Sindhi poetry that demonstrated mastery of both systems. This bilingual scholarly class ensured that Persian aesthetic criteria — husn‑e‑taʿlīl (beauty of justification), iqtibās (allusion) — became evaluative standards for Sindhi verse. When the British annexed Sindh in 1843, the Persianate patronage structure collapsed, but the internalised criteria persisted, informing the critical vocabulary of later reformers like Mirza Qaleech Beg. Understanding this continuity explains why early modern Sindhi criticism (late 19th century) still judges poetry by “Persianate” standards of metaphorical density and metrical perfection, even as new forms such as free verse emerge.

Colonial Education and the Rise of Modern Sindhi Prose

The British conquest of Sindh (1843) introduced a colonial education apparatus that fundamentally altered the production and circulation of Sindhi prose. The 1853 Sindhi alphabet standardisation, undertaken by the Bombay Presidency’s Education Department under Sir Bartle Frere, fixed a Perso‑Arabic script with diacritics for Sindhi phonemes (e.g., ṭ, ḍ, ṇ). This official script became the medium for government schools, the 1855‑established Sindhi‑language printing press at Karachi, and the first vernacular newspaper Sindh Sudhar (1884). Missionary societies, notably the Church Missionary Society, supplemented state efforts by publishing primers, grammars (e.g., Henry Carter’s Grammar of the Sindhi Language, 1868), and translated Christian texts, which modelled expository prose structures — logical paragraphing, argumentative essay, dialogue form — previously absent from the predominantly poetic tradition.

The curriculum of the new vernacular schools emphasised “useful knowledge”: history, geography, arithmetic, and moral science, all taught through Sindhi textbooks. This created a reading public that demanded prose for information rather than aesthetic pleasure. The first generation of modern prose writers — Mirza Qaleech Beg (1853‑1929), Dayaram Gidumal (1857‑1927), and Kauromal Khilnani (1862‑1931) — emerged from this schooled milieu. Their output includes essays on social reform, biographical sketches, and the earliest Sindhi novels (e.g., Qaleech Beg’s Zeenat, 1905). These works exhibit a clear break from the dastan oral narrative: they employ a third‑person omniscient narrator, linear chronology, and a didactic tone aimed at moral instruction. The printing press also enabled the periodical Hilal‑e‑Sindh (1908) and later Mehran (1925), which serialized fiction, published literary criticism, and forged a public sphere where prose could be debated. By the 1930s, the prose tradition had diversified into realism, social satire, and the beginnings of psychological fiction, laying the groundwork for the post‑Partition novel.

Crucially, colonial language policy was not monolithic. The 1904 Indian Universities Act and the 1917 Calcutta University Commission recommended mother‑tongue instruction at the primary level, reinforcing Sindhi’s status in the Bombay Presidency. However, the 1935 Government of India Act transferred education to provincial ministries, and the Sindh Legislative Assembly’s 1938 resolution made Sindhi the medium of instruction up to matriculation. This legislative backing expanded the prose readership but also entrenched a bureaucratic register — “official Sindhi” — that privileged Sanskritised vocabulary over the Perso‑Arabic lexical layer. The tension between this register and the Sufi‑inflected literary language resurfaced in the script debates of the 1950s and continues to shape contemporary orthography discussions.

The Sindhi Novel: Narrative Strategies and Historical Consciousness

The Sindhi novel, though a late arrival compared to poetry, quickly became the primary vehicle for negotiating historical trauma, especially the 1947 Partition and the subsequent diaspora. The first full‑length novels appeared in the 1930s, employing social realism to critique feudal patriarchy, religious orthodoxy, and colonial modernity. By the 1960s, writers such as Vasdev Mohi (Jeevan‑Jyot, 1972) and Krishin Khatwani introduced a fragmented narrative voice that mirrors the dislocation of Sindhi refugees in India. Mohi’s protagonist moves from a rural Sindh village to a Bombay refugee camp, and the novel’s structure — alternating flashbacks, interior monologue, and documentary inserts (government notices, camp registers) — enacts the rupture of memory. This technique aligns with the “historiographic metafiction” identified in postcolonial theory, though the Sindhi practitioners pre‑date the term.

In Pakistan, the novel took a different trajectory. The 1950s‑70s saw the rise of the “progressive novel” under the influence of the Progressive Writers’ Association, with authors like Shaikh Ayaz (though primarily a poet) and Amar Jaleel deploying allegory to evade censorship. Jaleel’s Na Kahān Na Kahān (1990) uses a mythic frame — the legend of the river Indus changing course — to comment on the political manipulation of language policy. The narrative strategy here is intertextual layering: folk motif, Quranic allusion, and contemporary reportage coexist, demanding a reader who can decode multiple registers. This polyphonic texture distinguishes the Sindhi novel from its Urdu and Hindi counterparts, which often maintain a single dominant discourse.

Post‑1990s diaspora writing adds a transnational dimension. Writers based in the Gulf, Europe, and North America (e.g., Hassan Dars, Ghulam Rabbani Agro) compose in Sindhi but publish through digital platforms, producing hypertextual novels where hyperlinks replace footnotes. Their narrative consciousness is shaped by “double displacement” — from Sindh to India/Pakistan, then to a global labour market. The resulting prose mixes English syntactic calques, code‑switching, and a deliberate archaism that invokes classical bait metre as a cultural anchor. Critics note that this self‑reflexive archaism functions as a resistance strategy against linguistic assimilation, turning the novel into a site of language preservation as much as storytelling.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to compare at least two novels across the Partition divide, identifying (a) the dominant narrative mode (realist, allegorical, metafictional), (b) the treatment of historical memory (direct testimony vs. mythic substitution), and (c) the linguistic register (Sanskritised, Perso‑Arabic, code‑mixed). A typical comparative question might ask: “How do Vasdev Mohi and Amar Jaleel differently negotiate the loss of homeland through narrative form?” The answer requires concrete textual evidence — Mohi’s use of camp registers versus Jaleel’s river allegory — rather than thematic generalisation.

Sufi Institutional Patronage and Literary Production

Sufi orders (ṭarīqas) operated as the most durable literary institutions in pre‑colonial Sindh, providing material support, manuscript preservation, and performance venues for poets. The Qādirī order, centred at the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif in Bhit Shah, maintained a langar (community kitchen) and a scriptorium where copyists produced multiple recensions of the Risalo. The Naqshbandī order, with its hub at the dargah of Sachal Sarmast in Daraza, similarly sponsored the compilation of diwans and the training of qawwals who rendered poetry in musical modes (rāgs) prescribed by the order’s pir. This patronage was not merely charitable; it served the order’s doctrinal propagation, as the poetry encoded the sharīʿa‑ṭarīqa‑ḥaqīqa hierarchy in vernacular form accessible to non‑Arabic speakers.

The institutional logic shaped poetic form. The kafi refrain, repeated in congregational samaʿ sessions, functioned as a mnemonic device for initiates to internalise the dhikr formula. The bait couplet, with its strict metrical symmetry, facilitated antiphonal singing between male and female voices during urs festivals. Manuscript colophons frequently record the name of the sponsoring pir, the date of the urs, and the number of copies commissioned, allowing scholars to map the geographic spread of a poet’s influence. For instance, the 1820 colophon of a Risalo copy notes “commissioned by Pir Muhammad Qasim, Qādirī, for the urs of 1235 AH”, confirming the order’s role in textual stabilisation.

British annexation disrupted this economy. The 1850s “Waqf” legislation placed shrine lands under colonial revenue administration, reducing the financial autonomy of pirs. Simultaneously, the printing press enabled mass production of poetry outside the scriptorium, diluting the order’s control over textual authority. Yet the orders adapted: the Qādirī pirs began publishing lithographed editions of the Risalo with their own commentaries, asserting interpretive dominance in the new print public sphere. By the early 20th century, the pir‑author nexus had shifted from manuscript patronage to editorial prefaces, a transition visible in the 1923 edition of Sachal’s Diwan prefaced by the Naqshbandī pir of Daraza. Understanding this institutional continuity explains why contemporary Sindhi critics still invoke “the Qādirī reading” or “the Naqshbandī reading” as hermeneutic categories, even in secular academic contexts.

Global Literary Currents in Sindhi Modernism: Symbolism, Existentialism, and the Avant‑Garde

The mid‑20th‑century Sindhi modernist movement did not develop in isolation; it actively absorbed European avant‑garde currents through translation, little magazines, and the diaspora’s direct exposure. The 1940s‑50s saw the circulation of French Symbolist poetry (Baudelaire, Mallarmé) in Sindhi translation via the journal Kahani, edited by Shaikh Ayaz. Ayaz’s own early collections (Bhool, 1950) exhibit Symbolist hallmarks: synaesthetic imagery (“the scent of silence”), deliberate obscurity, and the poem as autonomous aesthetic object rather than moral instruction. This marked a decisive break from the Progressive Writers’ Association’s socialist realism, which demanded clarity and didacticism.

By the 1960s, existentialist themes — absurdity, alienation, the burden of freedom — entered Sindhi verse through the mediation of Hindi and Urdu translations of Sartre and Camus. Adal Soomro (1940‑2020), a poet and philosopher, articulated an explicitly existentialist poetics in essays such as “Man and the Void” (1972), arguing that the Sindhi kafi could be refashioned to express “the nausea of existence” without recourse to Sufi metaphysics. His poem “Raat” (Night) juxtaposes the traditional night‑bird motif with a stark, unmetaphorical description of insomnia, signalling a shift from symbolic to phenomenological language. The little magazine Sindhi Adab (1968‑1990) published such experiments alongside critical essays debating the legitimacy of “Western” forms in a language rooted in oral tradition.

The 1970s‑80s witnessed an avant‑garde push that combined visual poetry, concrete poetry, and performance. Poets like Hassan Dars and Ghulam Rabbani Agro experimented with typographic layout, using the Perso‑Arabic script’s diacritical marks as visual elements. Dars’s 1985 chapbook “Akhar” arranges letters in spirals that must be rotated to read, invoking the Sufi practice of tawaf (circumambulation) while simultaneously referencing concrete poetry’s spatial semantics. This dual coding — religious ritual and modernist form — exemplifies the hybridity that characterises Sindhi modernism. The movement’s theoretical self‑awareness is documented in the 1992 symposium “Sindhi Poetry After Modernism”, where participants explicitly positioned their work against both the Progressive canon and the revivalist “classical” faction.

For exam answers, candidates should be able to trace a single European movement (Symbolism, Existentialism, or Concrete Poetry) through at least two Sindhi texts, identifying the translational mediator (journal, translator, bilingual poet) and the formal adaptation (e.g., replacement of radif with free‑verse lineation). A common examiner trap is to treat “modernism” as a monolith; the syllabus rewards distinction between the Symbolist phase (1940s‑50s), the Existentialist phase (1960s‑70s), and the Avant‑Garde phase (1980s‑90s), each with its own institutional base and critical discourse.

  • Treating the Sindhi novel as a uniform realist genre; the Partition divide, diaspora hypertextuality, and allegorical modes require separate analytical frameworks.
  • Assuming Sufi orders ceased to influence literature after British annexation; their editorial strategies migrated into print culture and continue to inform critical hermeneutics.
  • Collapsing Symbolism, Existentialism, and Concrete Poetry into a single “modernist” label without identifying the distinct translational mediators and formal innovations of each phase.
  • Overlooking the impact of the 1853 script standardisation on the later Devanagari‑Perso‑Arabic bifurcation; the colonial orthography decision set the parameters for post‑Independence script politics.
  • Neglecting to cite concrete textual evidence — such as a specific colophon, a journal issue, or a poem’s lineation — when arguing for institutional or transnational influence.
