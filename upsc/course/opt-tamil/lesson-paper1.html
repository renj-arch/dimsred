Tamil Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Tamil is a Dravidian language with one of the oldest continuous literary traditions. The Sangam corpus and the Tolkappiyam are foundational.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Sangam period: Tolkappiyam, Ettuthogai and Pattupattu; Purananuru and Akananuru.
  • Epic period: Silappathikaram, Manimekalai and the Tamil epics.
  • Bhakti period: Nayanmars and Alvars; Tevaram and Nalayira Divya Prabandham.
  • Modern period: Subramania Bharati, the novel, drama and contemporary writing.

Major Authors

  • Thiruvalluvar: Author of the Thirukkural.
  • Ilango Adigal: Author of Silappathikaram.
  • Subramania Bharati: Modern poet and nationalist.
  • Kalki Krishnamurthy: Novelist; Ponniyin Selvan.
  • Jayakanthan: Modern fiction writer.

Major Works

  • Thirukkural — Thiruvalluvar: Couplets on ethics, polity and love.
  • Silappathikaram — Ilango Adigal: Tamil epic of Kovalan and Kannagi.
  • Ponniyin Selvan — Kalki Krishnamurthy: Historical novel.
  • Tamil modern poetry — Subramania Bharati: Nationalist and modern poetry.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Thiruvalluvar in the literary history of Tamil and assess their contribution.
Answer: Thiruvalluvar belongs to the Sangam period phase. Author of the Thirukkural. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Sangam period → Epic period → Bhakti period → Modern period.
  • Authors: Thiruvalluvar, Ilango Adigal, Subramania Bharati, Kalki Krishnamurthy, Jayakanthan.
  • Works: Thirukkural, Silappathikaram, Ponniyin Selvan, Tamil modern poetry.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

Crucial Nuances: The Examiner's Lens on Periodization and Classification

The standard curriculum often presents literary history as a linear progression of distinct eras (Sangam, Epic, Bhakti, Modern). However, the UPSC examiner frequently tests the fluidity of these boundaries. A serious aspirant must understand that periods are not watertight compartments but overlapping strata.

The Mechanism of Transition: Consider the transition from the Sangam to the Epic period. While Silappathikaram is canonically an Epic, its author Ilango Adigal was a Jain monk who lived during or immediately after the fall of the Chola dynasty (post-Sangam). The text retains the Tolkappiyam's metrical norms but introduces new theological and narrative structures. Similarly, the Bhakti movement did not replace earlier traditions but co-existed with them; the Tevaram hymns were composed in a style that mimics the Akananuru, yet the content is entirely devotional.

The Exception to the Rule: The classification of 'Modern' literature often confuses candidates. Subramania Bharati (1882–1921) is placed in the Modern period, but his poetry retains the Tamil Pattu meter and the emotional intensity of the Bhakti saints. The examiner may ask: "Does Bharati belong to the Romantic movement or the Nationalist era?" The answer lies in recognizing that he synthesized classical form with modern political consciousness. Another nuance is the 'Puranic' period (post-Epic, pre-Bhakti), which is often omitted in brief summaries but crucial for understanding the shift from secular epics to theological narratives.

Comparative Analysis: When comparing Thirukkural with Greek or Sanskrit literature, avoid superficial parallels. While all three deal with ethics, the Kural's unique feature is its didactic brevity and the absence of a narrative frame, unlike the Mahabharata. The examiner might twist this by asking about the influence of Greek philosophy on Tamil literature; the correct stance is that while there was cultural contact during the Cholas, direct textual borrowing from Greek sources in early Tamil literature is unproven, making any such claim a trap.

Technique: Deconstructing Periodization Questions
Question: "Critically examine the statement that the Bhakti movement marked a definitive break from the secular traditions of the Sangam age." In your answer, address the continuity of poetic meters and the introduction of new theological themes.
Answer Strategy:
1. Direct Answer: State that it was not a 'break' but an 'evolution'.
2. Continuity (The Anchor): Highlight that Bhakti poets like the Nayanmars used the same meters (Pattu, Turai) found in the Ettuthogai. The linguistic structure remained Dravidian.
3. Discontinuity (The Shift): Contrast the secular themes of love and war in Sangam with the monotheistic focus on Shiva/Vishnu in Bhakti.
4. Synthesis: Conclude that while the subject matter changed, the form remained continuous, proving a deep cultural continuity rather than a rupture.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Why' Over the 'What'
When asked about an author or work, never stop at listing dates and titles. The examiner awards marks for contextualization. Always link a literary movement to its socio-political backdrop (e.g., connect Subramania Bharati's poetry to the anti-colonial struggle). If a question asks about a 'movement', explain the mechanism of change: Was it a shift in language? A shift in patronage? Or a shift in ideology?

Advanced Textual Analysis: Metrics, Manuscripts, and Attribution Debates

Beyond knowing the titles, Paper I demands familiarity with the technical aspects of Tamil literature that distinguish it from other Indian languages.

The Metric System (Akam/Nakam): The Sangam corpus is divided into Akam (interior) and Nakam (exterior). This is not merely thematic but structural. The Tolkappiyam codifies specific meters for each theme. For instance, the Puram genre strictly adheres to specific word counts per line. A common advanced trap is assuming all Sangam poems follow the same meter; exceptions exist where poets innovated within strict bounds. Understanding this helps in analyzing why certain works are classified as 'Epic' (long narrative) while others are 'Anthology'.

Manuscript Variants: Unlike Sanskrit texts which have a standardized critical edition, Tamil literature has multiple manuscript traditions. The Silappathikaram exists in versions with slight variations in the ending (the fate of Kannagi). Recognizing that literary history is not monolithic but a collection of variants is crucial for high-scoring answers on 'textual criticism'.

Attribution Debates: Some works attributed to specific authors are disputed. For example, the exact dating of Kuruntokai poems is debated among scholars. Acknowledging scholarly debate (without taking sides) demonstrates critical thinking. The examiner may ask about the 'Silk Letter' controversy or the authenticity of certain Puranic texts; knowing that textual criticism in Tamil often relies on paleography and cross-referencing with foreign inscriptions is a high-level insight.

Technique: Handling Attribution and Textual Variance
Question: "Discuss the challenges in establishing a definitive text for Silappathikaram." Approach this by mentioning manuscript traditions and textual variants.
Answer Strategy:
1. Introduction: Acknowledge that unlike the Thirukkural, which has a stable text, the Silappathikaram exists in multiple manuscript versions.
2. The Core Issue: Mention that variations exist in the final chapters regarding Kannagi's curse and its impact on Madurai.
3. Methodology: Explain that scholars compare palm-leaf manuscripts (pattal) to reconstruct the original intent, noting that oral transmission played a role before standardization.
4. Conclusion: Conclude that the 'definitive' text is an idealization, and studying variants reveals the evolving reception of the epic over centuries.

Socio-Political Correlates: Literature as a Mirror of History

Tamil literature is inextricably linked to the political history of South India. The examiner often tests the ability to correlate literary movements with dynastic shifts.

The Pallava and Chola Patronage: The transition from Sangam (post-Sangam) literature to the Epic period coincides with the rise of the Pallavas and later the Cholas. Silappathikaram reflects the urbanization and trade networks of the early medieval period, distinct from the rural focus of the Sangam age. Similarly, the Bhakti movement was often supported by temple complexes built under royal patronage (e.g., the Pallava temples at Mamallapuram). Understanding that literature did not exist in a vacuum but was funded and shaped by state policy is vital.

The Modern Turn: The advent of print culture in the 19th century transformed Tamil literature from manuscript-based to print-based. This shift allowed for the emergence of novels like Ponniyin Selvan, which, while historical fiction, reflects the modern interest in reconstructing a 'golden age' during colonial times. The examiner may ask how the novel form changed the audience; the answer involves literacy rates and the spread of vernacular education.

The Dravidian Movement: This is a critical exception to the standard literary canon. In the mid-20th century, the anti-Brahmin movement influenced literature, leading to works that challenged caste hierarchies explicitly. Authors like Thol. Thirumalai and Kalki Krishnamurthy (in his later years) engaged with these themes. Ignoring this socio-political dimension is a fatal flaw in a Paper I answer.

Technique: Linking Literature to Patronage
Question: "How did the political rise of the Chola dynasty influence the themes in the Silappathikaram?" Discuss the shift from rural to urban settings.
Answer Strategy:
1. Context: The Sangam age was largely rural and pastoral.
2. The Shift: With Chola expansion, trade flourished (e.g., the pearl fisherman's story).
3. Evidence: The epic features complex court politics, urban settings like Madurai, and themes of karma and dharma relevant to a centralized state.
4. Conclusion: The literature mirrors the transition from a tribal-agricultural society to a mercantile-imperial one.
Exam-Day Strategy: Avoiding Anachronisms
Never attribute modern democratic values or secularism to ancient Sangam texts unless explicitly supported by evidence (e.g., the concept of Naduvazhi in Kural). Always qualify statements with 'approximate', 'traditionally attributed', or 'scholarly consensus' to avoid factual errors. If unsure of a date, focus on the relative chronology (e.g., 'post-Sangam') rather than guessing exact years.
  • The "Sangam" Trap: Assuming all literature from the Sangam period is secular. Remember that the Tevaram hymns are sometimes anachronistically linked to this era; they belong strictly to the Bhakti period.
  • The "Author" Trap: Attributing anonymous works to famous authors like Thiruvalluvar without evidence. Many Kural couplets were added later; stick to the core 1330 couplets accepted by scholars.
  • The "Modern" Trap: Confusing the 'Modern' period (post-18th century) with contemporary writing. Subramania Bharati is a bridge figure, not purely modern in style, though modern in ideology.
  • The "Language" Trap: Forgetting that Tamil evolved significantly. The vocabulary of Silappathikaram differs from the Tolkappiyam; assuming linguistic stasis is incorrect.

The Evolution of Prosody: From Tolkappiyam to Modern Metrics

Tamil literary history is not merely a sequence of authors and dates but a rigorous evolution of linguistic rules, primarily governed by the science of prosody (Chandas). The foundational text, Tolkappiyam, traditionally attributed to Agastya or Tolkappiar, codified the grammar and metrics of Old Tamil. However, a critical distinction for the examiner is the difference between Old Tamil Metrics (Sangam age) and New Tamil Metrics (post-Sangam). The Sangam corpus adhered to specific meters like the Akan, Ayirattu, and Puram styles, characterized by a strict adherence to syllable counts (akaram) rather than stress patterns found in Sanskrit prosody. A pivotal moment in literary history is the composition of the Mullaiyar or the commentaries that attempted to reconcile oral traditions with written norms.

The transition from manuscript-based transmission to printed standardization introduced a new layer of textual variance. In the pre-print era, manuscripts (pattal) were fluid; scribes often altered verses to fit local dialects or patronage requirements. The 19th-century revival of interest in Tamil literature, spearheaded by figures like U.V. Swaminatha Iyer and later Subramania Bharati, necessitated a standardized metric system. This is evident in the works of modern poets who consciously revived archaic meters to evoke a sense of antiquity while writing in contemporary vernacular. The examiner may ask about the concept of 'Akaram' (syllable count) versus 'Viruttam' (verse). Understanding that early Tamil poetry was defined by the number of syllables per line, not rhyme or stress, is a crucial differentiator from Western literary analysis. Furthermore, the development of Tamil Vazhthai (poetic meters) involved complex mathematical ratios of long and short syllables, distinct from the quantitative meters of Greek or Sanskrit poetry.

Examiner's trap: The Metric Trap
Question: "How did the metric system evolve in Tamil literature?" Incorrect Answer: "It evolved linearly from simple to complex." Correct Approach: Acknowledge that Old Tamil metrics were rigid and syllable-count based (e.g., 4, 5, or 6 syllables per line). The 'New' period saw a relaxation of these rules due to the influence of Sanskrit prosody (Chandas) and the need for narrative flexibility in epics like Silappathikaram. Mention that modern Tamil poetry often blends traditional meters with free verse, reflecting the tension between preserving heritage and expressing modernity.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Ambiguity
When asked about specific dates of literary works (e.g., when did Silappathikaram come to light?), do not invent a year. Use relative chronology: "Post-Sangam period, likely between the 2nd and 5th centuries CE." Always qualify uncertain claims with phrases like 'scholarly consensus suggests' or 'based on paleographic evidence.'

Textual Criticism and the Manuscript Tradition

The study of Tamil literature relies heavily on textual criticism, a discipline that examines the history of a text's transmission. Unlike the Thirukkural, which has been preserved with remarkable stability in over 100 manuscripts (the Kural Venba tradition), epics like Silappathikaram and Manimekalai exist in multiple recensions. The core issue is that no single 'original' manuscript survives; instead, scholars reconstruct the text by comparing variants found in palm-leaf manuscripts (pattal) and printed editions. A key concept here is recension: a version of a text established by a specific editor or scribe. For instance, the Silappathikaram exists in versions attributed to different editors like Kallakurichi Nambi Andar Nambi, whose role in standardizing the epic during the Chola era is debated.

The examiner often tests knowledge of the Manuscript vs. Print divide. The shift from manuscript to print culture in the 19th century (post-1800s) standardized the language but altered the text itself. Printed editions often 'corrected' archaic words or dialectal variations to make them accessible to a wider audience, effectively erasing historical linguistic layers. This is vital for analyzing works like Manimekalai, where the final chapters regarding Kannagi's curse show significant variance between different manuscript traditions. Some versions end abruptly, while others include elaborate theological commentaries. The technique involves identifying these variants and understanding their socio-political context: why was a certain version preferred by a specific dynasty or caste group? For example, the inclusion of Buddhist elements in Manimekalai might have been emphasized or downplayed depending on the patronage.

Examiner's trap: Attribution and Authorship
Question: "Who is the author of Silappathikaram?" Incorrect Answer: "Ilango Adigal." Correct Approach: While traditionally attributed to Ilango Adigal (brother of Kannagi), textual critics note that the work shows signs of multiple layers of composition. Avoid stating 'It was written by X in year Y' unless citing a specific source like the Tirumurai or Tolkappiyam. Instead, state: 'Traditionally attributed to Ilango Adigal, though textual evidence suggests a composite authorship spanning several centuries.'
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Dates
If unsure of a specific date (e.g., the exact composition date of Purananuru), focus on relative chronology. Use phrases like 'late Sangam period' or 'early medieval era.' Never guess exact years unless they are part of a well-known historical event (e.g., the coronation of Rajaraja Chola). Accuracy beats coverage; a wrong date can lose marks even if the analysis is correct.

The Bhakti Movement: Theology and Patronage

The Bhakti movement in Tamil literature represents a paradigm shift from the secular, nature-centric themes of the Sangam age to intense devotional poetry centered on deities like Shiva, Vishnu, and later Murugan. This movement is not merely religious but deeply political and social, challenging caste hierarchies and asserting the equality of all devotees before God. The Tevaram hymns (6th-7th centuries CE) by the Nayanars (Shaivites) and the Thevaram (Vaishnavite hymns) are foundational texts. A critical nuance for the examiner is the distinction between early Bhakti (Nayanar/Thevar period) and later Bhakti (12th-13th centuries, e.g., Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar). The early hymns often contain references to royal patronage, linking the divine with the earthly king (e.g., the concept of the king as a protector of dharma).

The socio-political correlates are essential: the Bhakti movement was supported by temple complexes built under royal patronage, such as the Pallava temples at Mamallapuram. The examiner may ask how the Bhakti movement influenced literature; the answer involves the emergence of new genres like Tevaram hymns and the integration of folk traditions into high literature. The language also evolved; early Bhakti poetry used simpler, more accessible Tamil compared to the Sanskritized vocabulary of later periods.

The Sangam Corpus: Anthologies, Poetic Conventions, and Chronology

The Sangam corpus is conventionally divided into two major groups: the Ettuthokai (Eight Anthologies) and the Pattuppattu (Ten Idylls). The anthologies — Natrinai, Kuruntokai, Ainkurunuru, Patirruppattu, Paripatal, Kalittokai, Akananuru, and Purananuru — collect short lyrical poems composed in the akam (interior, love) and puram (exterior, war, ethics) modes. The idylls are longer narrative poems such as Malaipatukatam, Maturaikkanci, and Pattinappalai, which blend description of landscape with royal panegyric. Each poem follows a strict metrical pattern: the akaval metre for most anthologies, and the vanchi or kalippa metres for the idylls. The convention of thinai (landscape‑based thematic classification) governs both content and diction; five primary thinais — kurinji, mullai, marutam, neytal, palai — map emotional states to specific ecological zones.

Chronologically, scholarly consensus places the bulk of the anthologies between the 1st century BCE and the 3rd century CE, with the idylls slightly later, extending to the 4th century CE. No manuscript predates the 12th century; the texts survive through medieval commentaries and the 19th‑century print editions of U. V. Swaminatha Iyer. Relative dating relies on internal references to historical figures (e.g., the Chera king Cenkuttuvan in Patirruppattu) and on linguistic archaisms such as the retention of the aytam (the phoneme /ɦ/) which disappears in later Tamil. The examiner expects candidates to avoid absolute dates; instead, use phrases like “early Sangam layer” or “late Sangam redaction” and cite the paleographic evidence of the Tirukkural manuscript tradition as a chronological anchor.

A critical nuance is the distinction between akam and puram not merely as thematic labels but as structural principles: akam poems are anonymous, dramatized monologues that never name the poet or the patron, whereas puram poems frequently name kings, chieftains, and poets, embedding the verse in a socio‑political matrix. This dichotomy informs later literary theory, especially the Tolkappiyam’s classification of porul (subject matter) and the medieval commentarial tradition that treats akam as the domain of kavya aesthetics and puram as the domain of itihasa (historical narrative).

The Epic Tradition: Narrative Structure and Religious Syncretism in Silappathikaram and Manimekalai

The two surviving Tamil epics, Silappathikaram and Manimekalai, share a common narrative universe but diverge sharply in doctrinal orientation. Silappathikaram, traditionally ascribed to Ilango Adigal, is organized into three cantos (kandams) — Pukar Kandam, Madurai Kandam, Vanchi Kandam — each corresponding to a major urban centre of the early historic Tamil polity. The plot follows the merchant Kovalan, his wife Kannagi, and the tragic miscarriage of justice that leads to the burning of Madurai. The epic integrates Jain motifs (the ascetic Kavunti, the doctrine of anekantavada) alongside a robust Tamil folk‑religious substrate (the worship of the goddess Pattini).

Manimekalai, attributed to the poet Sattanar (or Chattanar), continues the story through the daughter of Kovalan and Madhavi, Manimekalai, who renounces worldly life to become a Buddhist nun. Its structure is more episodic, comprising thirty cantos that function as didactic dialogues on Buddhist philosophy — Four Noble Truths, Dependent Origination, and the critique of caste. The narrative employs extensive akaval verses interspersed with viruttam metres, allowing shifts between lyrical description and doctrinal exposition. Both epics exhibit a layered composition: linguistic analysis reveals archaic Sangam‑era vocabulary in the early cantos and later Sanskritised lexicon in the concluding sections, suggesting accretional growth over several centuries.

Religious syncretism is a hallmark: Silappathikaram presents a Jain monk as a moral arbiter while simultaneously elevating Kannagi to a tutelary deity of chastity, a figure later absorbed into the Shaiva‑Vaishnava temple cults of the medieval period. Manimekalai explicitly debates Hindu sacrificial rites, yet its final cantos describe the protagonist’s vision of the Dharmacakra in a manner that echoes the Tiruvaymoli’s mystical imagery. The examiner often tests the ability to trace these intertextual echoes — for instance, the reuse of the Kannagi motif in the Tiruvilaiyadal Puranam — and to evaluate how manuscript variants (the Kallakurichi recension versus the U. V. Swaminatha Iyer edition) alter the theological emphasis.

Worked Example: Distinguishing Akam and Puram in a Sangam Verse
A verse from Kuruntokai reads: “The kurinji hills bloom at night; the heroine waits, her bangles slipping.” Identify the thinai, the mode (akam/puram), and justify the classification.
The verse depicts a nocturnal mountain landscape (kurinji thinai) and focuses on the interior emotional state of a woman awaiting her lover. No king, battle, or public deed is mentioned; the speaker is anonymous. Therefore the mode is akam. The classification rests on (1) the landscape‑emotion mapping prescribed by Tolkappiyam Porulatikaram, (2) the absence of named historical agents, and (3) the use of the dramatic monologue form typical of akam poetry.
Exam‑Day Strategy: Handling Chronological Ambiguity
When a question asks for the date of a Sangam work, never supply a single year. Frame the answer as a range anchored to external evidence: “The Purananuru poems referencing the Chera king Cenkuttuvan are generally placed in the 2nd century CE, based on the synchronism with the Gajabahu synchronism cited in the Silappathikaram commentary.” Cite the scholarly method (paleography, linguistic archaism, synchronism) rather than a memorised date. This demonstrates methodological awareness and avoids the penalty for factual inaccuracy.

Grammatical Evolution: From Tolkappiyam to Nannul and the Commentatorial Tradition

The grammatical history of Tamil is anchored by three seminal texts: the Tolkappiyam (c. 2nd century BCE – 1st century CE), the Viracoliyam (11th century), and the Nannul (13th century). Tolkappiyam comprises three books — Ezhuttatikaram (orthography and phonology), Collatikaram (morphology and syntax), and Porulatikaram (poetics and prosody). Its sutra style, dense with technical terms such as kurriyal ukaram (shortening of the vowel /u/) and iḷaṅku (euphonic increment), reflects a highly codified oral tradition. The text presupposes a diglossic situation: a literary register (centamil) distinct from spoken varieties.

The Viracoliyam, composed under Chola patronage, adapts Tolkappiyam to the linguistic changes of the medieval period — notably the loss of the aytam, the merger of ḷ and l, and the expansion of the case system. It introduces the concept of vērrumai (case) as a syntactic category separate from the morphological suffixes of Tolkappiyam. The Nannul, authored by Pavananti Munivar, systematises these developments into a pedagogical grammar that becomes the standard textbook for centuries. Its 420 sutras reorganise phonology into uyir (vowels) and mei (consonants), and it formalises the sandhi rules that govern word‑junction in poetic composition.

Commentaries (urai) are indispensable for interpreting these grammars. Ilampuranar’s 12th‑century commentary on Tolkappiyam (the Ilampuranar Urai) provides exhaustive glosses, examples from Sangam texts, and polemical engagement with rival readings. Later commentators such as Senavaraiyar (for Nannul) and Deivacilaiyar (for Viracoliyam) extend the tradition by incorporating Sanskrit grammatical terminology (vibhakti, karaka) while insisting on Tamil’s autonomous structural principles. The examiner frequently asks candidates to compare the treatment of a specific phenomenon — e.g., the formation of the sociative case (ōṭu) — across the three grammars and their commentaries, testing both historical awareness and analytical precision.

Print Culture, Translation, and the Emergence of Modern Tamil Prose

The transition from palm‑leaf manuscript to printed book in the early 19th century reshaped the Tamil literary field. The first Tamil press, established at Tranquebar (Tharangambadi) by the Danish‑Halle missionaries in 1712, produced the Tamil New Testament (1715) and the Tamil–Portuguese Dictionary (1730). These works introduced a standardized orthography — regularising the use of the pulli (virama) and fixing the shape of the aytam — that diverged from the variable manuscript conventions. The missionary linguist Bartholomaeus Ziegenbalg’s Grammatica Damulica (1716) codified a pedagogical grammar that influenced later indigenous grammarians.

By the 1830s, the College of Fort St. George in Madras became a hub for Tamil printing. The Tamil Lexicon project (initiated 1910, completed 1939) epitomised the scholarly consolidation of vocabulary, drawing on both classical texts and contemporary usage. Simultaneously, indigenous scholars such as C. W. Thamotharampillai and U. V. Swaminatha Iyer edited and printed the Sangam anthologies, the Tirukkural, and the epics, often adding critical apparatus that reflected colonial philological methods (stemmatic reconstruction, sigla for manuscript families).

Modern Tamil prose emerges from this print ecosystem. The first Tamil novel, Prathapa Mudaliar Charittiram (1879) by Samuel Vedanayagam Pillai, adapts the English realist novel form while employing a Sanskritised lexicon. The early 20th‑century journal Manikkodi (1933‑1939) pioneered the short story (sirukathai) and the essay (katturai) as literary genres, fostering writers like Pudumaipithan and Ku. Pa. Rajagopalan. Their prose exhibits a deliberate simplification of syntax — shorter sentences, reduced nominal compounds — and a vocabulary that blends classical Tamil roots with English loanwords (rel, station, office). The examiner expects candidates to trace this trajectory: from missionary‑driven standardization, through colonial scholarly editing, to the nationalist re‑appropriation of print for literary modernism.

The Dravidian Movement, Literary Nationalism, and the Reconfiguration of the Canon

The Self‑Respect Movement (1925) led by Periyar E. V. Ramasamy and the subsequent Dravidar Kazhagam (1944) and Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (1949) transformed Tamil literary culture into a vehicle for political identity. Periyar’s journal Kudi Arasu published essays that reinterpreted classical texts — especially the Tirukkural — as rationalist, anti‑caste documents, downplaying their religious framing. This hermeneutic shift encouraged a re‑reading of Sangam poetry as evidence of an egalitarian, pre‑Aryan Tamil society, a thesis articulated in the historical works of Maraimalai Adigal and later popularised by the “Pure Tamil” (Tanittamil) movement.

Literary nationalism manifested in the promotion of centamil (classical Tamil) over kotuntamil (colloquial) in education and publishing. The 1937‑1939 anti‑Hindi agitations produced a corpus of protest poetry (e.g., Bharathidasan’s Tamil Thai Vazhthu) that fused classical metres with contemporary political rhetoric. Post‑independence, the Tamil Nadu government’s “Tamil Development” policies institutionalised the canon: the Tamil Nadu Textbook Corporation prescribed a curriculum that foregrounded the Ettuthokai, Pattuppattu, Silappathikaram, and the Tirukkural while marginalising medieval commentarial literature and devotional hymnody perceived as Brahmanical.

Simultaneously, the “Modernist” poets of the 1960s — such as Vairamuthu, Mu. Metha, and the “Vanambadi” group — experimented with free verse (venpa adaptations), surreal imagery, and Dalit perspectives, challenging the aesthetic norms established by the Dravidian cultural establishment. The examiner often asks candidates to evaluate the tension between two nationalist projects: the Dravidian project that seeks a pure, ancient Tamil past, and the modernist project that embraces heterogeneity, translation, and global literary currents. A nuanced answer references specific texts (e.g., the anthology Kavitaiyin Thotramum Valarchiyum edited by Na. Muthuswamy) and institutional actors (the Tamil University, Thanjavur, founded 1981) to illustrate how canon formation remains a contested, politically charged process.

  • Confusing the relative chronology of the Ettuthokai and Pattuppattu; treat them as contemporaneous rather than sequential layers.
  • Attributing Silappathikaram solely to Ilango Adigal without acknowledging textual stratification and recension differences.
  • Stating that Tolkappiyam, Viracoliyam, and Nannul represent a linear improvement; each addresses distinct linguistic stages and theoretical frameworks.
  • Assuming print culture merely preserved manuscripts; it actively standardized orthography, altered dialectal forms, and created new genres.
  • Equating Dravidian literary nationalism with a monolithic “pure Tamil” stance; internal debates (e.g., between Periyar and Annadurai on Sanskrit loanwords) are essential.
  • Overlooking the role of commentaries (urai) as independent interpretive layers that can contradict the base text.
  • Neglecting the distinction between akam and puram as structural principles rather than mere thematic labels.

Commentarial Schools and Interpretive Lineages in Medieval Tamil

The medieval commentarial tradition (urai) constitutes a distinct interpretive layer that often reshapes the meaning of the base text. Three major schools can be identified by their institutional affiliation, methodological focus, and theological orientation. The first, associated with the Saiva monastic centres of Tiruvavaduthurai and Dharmapuram, produced exhaustive grammatical and exegetical commentaries on the Tolkappiyam — notably those of Ilampuranar (12th century), Naccinarkkiniyar (14th century), and Peraciriyar (15th century). Their approach treats the sutras as a closed linguistic system, applying the rules of later grammarians such as Nannul to resolve ambiguities. The second school, rooted in the Vaishnava acharya lineages of Srirangam and Kanchipuram, generated commentaries on the Divya Prabandham and the Tiruvaymoli; the most influential are those of Periyavaccan Pillai (13th century) and Vedanta Desika (14th century), which embed the poems within a Visistadvaita framework, reading the erotic akam conventions as allegories of the soul’s longing for Vishnu. The third, a more eclectic scholarly tradition centred on the Tamil Sangam of Madurai and later the University of Madras, produced the critical editions of Parimelalhagar’s Tirukkural commentary (13th century) and the Civakacintamani commentary of Civagnanacittiyar (15th century). These works combine philological rigour with ethical exposition, often citing earlier commentators to establish a lineage (guru‑parampara). The printed editions of the 19th century — especially those by U. V. Swaminatha Iyer and C. W. Thamotharampillai — standardized one recension of each commentary, marginalising variant readings preserved in palm‑leaf manuscripts. Candidates should be able to trace how a single verse of the Tirukkural acquires three distinct glosses: a grammatical parsing by Ilampuranar, a devotional reading by Parimelalhagar, and a modern rationalist reinterpretation in the 20th‑century “Pure Tamil” anthologies.

Methodologically, the commentaries differ in their use of the “sutra‑vritti” (aphorism‑explanation) format versus the “padavakyam” (word‑by‑word) gloss. The Saiva school favours the former, preserving the sutra’s brevity, while the Vaishnava school expands each term into a theological discourse. This divergence affects the reconstruction of textual history: a verse that appears in the Sangam anthologies with a neutral tone may be rendered as a bhakti utterance in the Vaishnava commentary, thereby influencing later literary histories that treat the Sangam corpus as pre‑bhakti. The examiner expects awareness of this hermeneutic shift and the ability to cite specific commentators when arguing for or against a particular dating or sectarian attribution.

Regional Dialects and Their Literary Representation in Medieval Tamil

Medieval Tamil literature does not employ a monolithic “standard” register; rather, it reflects a continuum of regional dialects that poets manipulate for characterisation, humour, and local colour. The major dialect zones — Kongu (western interior), Madurai (central), Tirunelveli (southern), and the northern coastal belt around Chennai — each contribute distinctive phonological and lexical features. Kamban’s Ramavataram (12th century) incorporates Kongu forms such as “kōṭu” for “kōṭṭu” (to give) and the retroflex lateral “ḷ” in place of the dental “l” in words like “paḷḷi” (school). The Tiruvacakam of Manikkavacakar (9th century) exhibits southern vowel harmony, notably the raising of short “a” to “e” before a following “i” (e.g., “tēvai” for “tavai”). Villiputturar’s Bharatam (14th century) shows northern influence through the retention of the Sanskrit‑derived “ś” in “śrī” and the use of the honorific “āyir” for “āyar”. These dialect markers are not random; they serve the dramatic purpose of distinguishing rustic speakers from courtly interlocutors, a convention codified in the “Kural” of the “Kovai” tradition where the “kōvai” (folk) voice is set against the “centamil” (literary) voice.

The Linguistic Survey of India (1903‑1928), directed by George Abraham Grierson, systematically recorded these variations, classifying Tamil into “Western”, “Central”, “Southern”, and “Northern” groups. Grierson’s informants included traditional scholars who recited verses from the Kambaramayanam and the Tiruvacakam, allowing the survey to map dialectal isoglosses onto literary texts. Later, the Tamil Lexicon project (1910‑1939) adopted a centamil headword policy but retained dialectal citations as sub‑entries, preserving evidence of regional usage. In the 20th century, the “Pure Tamil” movement deliberately purged many dialectal forms from textbooks, yet folk performance genres such as Therukoothu and Villupattu continue to transmit them orally. An examiner may ask candidates to identify the dialectal provenance of a given line — for instance, the use of “kāṇ” (to see) versus “kāṇṭu” — and to explain how such choices reflect the poet’s regional identity or the intended audience.

Technique
A printed edition of the Tolkappiyam presents three commentaries on sutra 1.1.2: Ilampuranar, Naccinarkkiniyar, and a modern editor’s note. How would you distinguish the grammatical, theological, and editorial layers in an answer?

Second, summarise the specific interpretive move: Ilampuranar parses the sutra into its constituent morphemes and cites earlier sutras; Naccinarkkiniyar adds a reference to the “Nannul” rule on vowel sandhi, showing a later grammatical framework; the modern editor flags a manuscript variant where the reading “eḻuttu” replaces “eḻuttuṅkaḷ”. Third, evaluate the impact: the grammatical layers establish the linguistic stage (early vs. middle Tamil), while the editorial note signals textual instability. A complete answer tabulates commentator, period, method, and significance.

Women Poets and Gendered Voice in Classical and Medieval Tamil Literature

The presence of women poets in the Tamil canon is documented from the earliest anthologies. The Sangam collections name at least twenty‑five female composers, among them Avvaiyar (multiple figures across centuries), Kakkai Padiniyar, Nappasaliyar, and Perunkoppendu. Their verses appear in both the Ettuthokai and Pattuppattu, often employing the “talaivi” (heroine) voice prescribed by the akam convention. However, several poems attributed to women subvert the conventional love‑lament by introducing ethical counsel, political advice, or philosophical reflection. Avvaiyar’s “Aathichudi” and “Kondrai Vendhan” are didactic maxims addressed to a universal audience, transcending the erotic framework. In the medieval period, the Alvar tradition includes Andal (9th century), the sole female Alvar, whose Tiruppavai and Nachiyar Tirumoli articulate a bridal mysticism that reconfigures the human‑divine relationship from a woman’s perspective. The Saiva saint Karaikkal Ammaiyar (6th century) composes the “Mootha Tiruvandadi” and “Tiruvalangadu Tiruvandadi”, expressing fierce ascetic devotion that challenges gendered expectations of domesticity. Royal women such as Mangayarkkarasiyar (queen of the Pandya king) and the Chola princess Kundavai also appear as patrons and composers, their inscriptions and poems recorded in copper‑plate grants.

Scholarly analysis of gendered voice distinguishes between “prescribed” voice — the literary persona mandated by akam poetics — and “authentic” voice, where the poet’s historical gender informs thematic choices. The akam system assigns fixed roles (talaivan, talaivi, tozhi) irrespective of the composer’s sex, yet female poets often expand the tozhi (confidante) role into a moral commentator. In the post‑Sangam bhakti corpus, the female voice becomes a vehicle for theological authority: Andal’s self‑identification as the bride of Vishnu grants her scriptural status, while Karaikkal Ammaiyar’s self‑portrayal as a “pey” (demoness) who attains grace subverts the male‑centric hagiography. Modern feminist readings (e.g., the work of Paula Richman) highlight these strategies, but the examiner expects candidates to ground arguments in textual evidence — specific verses, colophons, and inscriptional references — rather than in secondary theory alone.

Colonial Census, Linguistic Surveys, and the Construction of Tamil Literary History

The British colonial state produced two interlocking knowledge projects that shaped the modern understanding of Tamil literature: the decennial census (beginning 1871) and the Linguistic Survey of India (1903‑1928). The 1871 Census of the Madras Presidency, supervised by H. A. Stuart, recorded “Tamil” as a distinct language category separate from “Telugu”, “Malayalam”, and “Kannada”, and enumerated speakers by district, caste, and religion. This demographic data was later used by orientalist scholars such as Robert Caldwell (A Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian Languages, 1856) and G. U. Pope (translator of the Tirukkural, 1886) to argue for a Dravidian language family and to date Sangam texts on the basis of lexical archaisms. The 1901 Census Report included a “Literary History of the Tamils” appendix authored by Stuart, which presented a periodisation — “Ancient”, “Medieval”, “Modern” — that mirrored European literary histories and implicitly privileged the Sangam corpus as the “classical” age.

The Linguistic Survey, under George Abraham Grierson, deployed trained fieldworkers to collect word‑lists, texts, and oral recitations from each dialect region. Volume IV (1906) devoted to Tamil contains phonological maps, grammatical sketches, and specimen texts from the Kambaramayanam, the Tiruvacakam, and folk songs. Grierson’s classification of Tamil into “Western”, “Central”, “Southern”, and “Northern” dialects provided a scholarly framework that later nationalist historians either adopted or contested. Missionary presses — notably the Tranquebar Press (established 1712) and the American Mission Press at Madras — printed the first critical editions of the Tolkappiyam (1847), the Tirukkural (1812), and the Sangam anthologies (1880s), standardising orthography (e.g., the use of the pulli for pure consonants) and introducing European editorial conventions such as sigla for manuscript families. These printed editions became the basis for university curricula after the establishment of the University of Madras (1857) and later the Annamalai University (1929). The examiner often tests the ability to link a specific census table or survey volume to a historiographical claim — for example, how the 1891 census figure of 12 million Tamil speakers was cited in the 1920s “Pure Tamil” movement to demand language‑based provincial autonomy.

Modern Tamil Drama: From Folk Performance to Proscenium Theatre

Tamil dramatic tradition originates in ritual‑folk forms — Therukoothu (street theatre), Bhagavata Mela (temple dance‑drama), Kuravanji (operatic love‑drama), and Villupattu (bow‑song narrative). These genres employ verse, music, and stylised gesture, and they operate in open‑air spaces with a fluid actor‑audience boundary. The late 19th century saw the emergence of proscenium theatre in Madras, driven by Western‑educated elites and missionary schools. Sankaradas Swamigal (1867‑1922) founded the “Tamil Nadu Nataka Sangam” (1910) and wrote over fifty plays, adapting Puranic stories into five‑act structures with spoken dialogue in centamil. His contemporary, Pammal Sambandha Mudaliar (1873‑1964), established the “Suguna Vilasa Sabha” (1891) and introduced realistic staging, female actors, and social themes such as widow remarriage in “Manohara” (1905). The “TKS Brothers” (T. K. Shanmugam, T. K. Bhagavathi, T. K. Srinivasan) professionalised the touring company model in the 1920s‑1930s, blending folk music with proscenium conventions.

Post‑independence, the Dravidian movement appropriated theatre for political propaganda. C. N. Annadurai’s “Velaikkari” (1949) and M. Karunanidhi’s “Parasakthi” (1952) used the stage to articulate anti‑caste, rationalist messages, employing a colloquial register (kotuntamil) that broke from the centamil norm. Simultaneously, the “Modernist” playwrights of the 1960s — K. S. Maniam, N. Muthuswamy, and the “Koothu‑P‑Pattarai” collective — experimented with non‑linear narratives, absurdism, and Dalit perspectives, often staging in intimate “black‑box” venues. The Tamil Nadu government’s “Tamil Nadu State Drama Festival” (initiated 1975) institutionalised a canon that includes both the classical “Nataka” repertoire and experimental works. Candidates should be able to trace the trajectory from verse‑drama (Kamban’s “Ramavataram” performed as a dance‑drama) to prose dialogue, note the shift from all‑male casts to gender‑inclusive casting, and explain how state patronage and political ideology shaped the repertoire.

Tamil Lexicography and Dictionary Projects: From the Early Glossaries to the Tamil Lexicon

The systematic compilation of Tamil lexical resources began long before the colonial era. The earliest extant glossaries, such as the Caturakarati (12th century) and the Tamil Nighantu attributed to the Jain scholar Kavirasa, organised words by semantic fields and provided synonyms, antonyms, and morphological variants. These works served pedagogical purposes in monastic schools and were transmitted through palm‑leaf manuscripts. In the 17th century the Koodal Nighantu expanded the tradition by incorporating loanwords from Sanskrit, Telugu, and Persian, reflecting the cosmopolitan vocabulary of the Nayaka courts.

The arrival of European missionaries catalysed the shift from manuscript glossaries to printed dictionaries. The Tranquebar Press produced the Tamil‑Portuguese Dictionary (1713) and the Tamil‑English Dictionary (1779) by Johann Philipp Fabricius, which introduced alphabetical ordering and phonetic transcription based on the Roman script. The American Mission Press at Madras followed with Winslow’s A Comprehensive Tamil‑English Dictionary (1862), a landmark for its inclusion of colloquial registers, idiomatic phrases, and illustrative citations from classical texts.

The most ambitious scholarly project was the Tamil Lexicon (1924‑1939), undertaken by the University of Madras under the editorship of V. S. Rajagopalan and later S. V. Subramania Iyer. Spanning seven volumes, it drew on over 1,200 source texts ranging from Sangam anthologies to modern newspapers, and adopted a historical principle: each entry traces the earliest attested occurrence, semantic development, and dialectal distribution. The Lexicon standardised orthography (e.g., the consistent use of the pulli for pure consonants) and became the authoritative reference for subsequent academic work, civil‑service examinations, and language‑planning bodies.

Post‑independence, the Tamil Nadu Government’s Tamil Development Department sponsored the Tamil Peragarathi (1970s‑1990s), a concise single‑volume dictionary aimed at school curricula, while the Central Institute of Classical Tamil launched the digital Tamil Lexicon Online (2005) with searchable Unicode entries and links to manuscript images. These initiatives illustrate the continuum from scholarly glossaries to state‑driven standardisation, a trajectory examiners often test through questions on the interplay of missionary scholarship, university patronage, and language policy.

Indigenous Literary Criticism: Theoretical Frameworks from Tolkappiyam to Contemporary Critique

Tamil literary theory originates in the Tolkappiyam (c. 2nd century BCE‑5th century CE), which devotes its third book, Porulatikaram, to poetics. Porul classifies poetry into akam (interior, love) and puram (exterior, war, ethics) and prescribes meyppatu (emotive conventions) such as kurincci, mullai, marutam, neytal, and palai. The text also defines uvamai (simile), uvakai (metaphor), and iraicci (suggestion), establishing a technical vocabulary that persists in later commentaries.

Medieval commentatorial schools expanded this framework. Ilampuranar (12th century) and Naccinarkkiniyar (14th century) wrote exhaustive glosses on Tolkappiyam, interpreting porul through the lens of Saiva Siddhanta metaphysics. Simultaneously, the Vaishnava tradition produced Periyavaccan Pillai and Vedanta Desika, whose commentaries on the Divya Prabandham introduced theological hermeneutics that treated poetic language as a vehicle for divine revelation. The Nannul (13th century) by Pavananti Munivar restructured grammar and poetics, merging Tolkappiyam categories with Sanskrit‑derived alamkara theory.

In the modern period, scholars such as M. Varadarajan, K. S. Ramaswami, and T. P. Meenakshisundaram adapted indigenous categories to engage with Western literary criticism. Varadarajan’s Ilakkiya Thiranayvu (1972) applies akam/puram to the analysis of the novel, while Ramaswami’s Tamil Ilakkiya Varalaru (1985) historicises the evolution of meyppatu across genres. Contemporary critics like G. S. Raman and S. Jeyapragasam employ post‑structuralist tools — intertextuality, reader‑response — without discarding the native lexicon, producing a bilingual critical discourse that examiners recognise as a distinctive feature of Tamil studies.

Technique: Mapping Indigenous Terms to Modern Critical Concepts
A question asks: “Explain how the Tolkappiyam concept of iraicci (suggestion) anticipates the Western notion of ‘implication’ in literary semantics. Provide two textual examples.”
Step 1: Define iraicci as the indirect conveyance of meaning through contextual cues rather than explicit statement, citing Tolkappiyam Porulatikaram sutra 274. Step 2: Identify the parallel in Western theory — Grice’s conversational implicature or Richards’ “implication” — noting both rely on shared cultural knowledge. Step 3: Example 1: In Kuruntokai 12, the line “The night‑bird’s call fades” suggests the lover’s departure without naming it. Step 4: Example 2: In Silappathikaram Canto 12, the description of a withered garland implies Kannaki’s grief. Conclusion: The indigenous term operates identically to implication, demonstrating continuity between classical Tamil poetics and modern semantic theory.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “indigenous critical concepts”, list the Tolkappiyam terms first (akam, puram, meyppatu, iraicci, uvamai) and then show their medieval reinterpretation (Ilampuranar, Nannul) before mentioning modern scholars. This chronological layering signals depth and avoids the trap of presenting only contemporary criticism.

The Tamil Novel and Short Story: Form, Themes, and Socio‑Historical Context

The Tamil novel emerges in the late 19th century as a response to colonial modernity, missionary education, and the rise of a literate middle class. Vedanayagam Pillai’s Prathapa Mudaliar Charithram (1879) is widely recognised as the first original Tamil novel; it blends historical romance with social commentary on widow remarriage and caste rigidity. The work adopts a linear narrative, third‑person omniscient narration, and a prose style heavily Sanskritised, reflecting the author’s training in both traditional kavyam and English literature.

Subsequent novelists diversified form and theme. A. Madhaviah’s Mohanangi (1911) introduces psychological realism and a critique of feudal patriarchy, while Kalki Krishnamurthy’s serialised historical novels — Sivagamiyin Sabatham (1944), Ponniyin Selvan (1950‑54) — combine meticulous archival research with popular storytelling, reviving the puram ethos for a mass readership. The mid‑20th century sees the rise of the social realist novel: Jayakanthan’s Sila Nerangalil Sila Manithargal (1970) and Pudhumaipithan’s short stories (collected in Pudhumaipithan Kathaigal, 1940s) expose urban alienation, gender violence, and the hypocrisy of reformist rhetoric.

The short story flourishes in literary magazines such as Ananda Vikatan, Kalki, and Manikkodi. Ki. Rajarathinam, Ashokamitran, and C. S. Lakshmi (Ambai) experiment with stream‑of‑consciousness, fragmented structure, and feminist perspectives. Ambai’s Sirappu (1975) reconfigures the akam landscape to articulate female desire, demonstrating a direct dialogue with classical poetics. The Dalit literary surge from the 1990s — represented by Bama (Karukku, 1992) and Imayam (Koveru Kazhuthaigal, 1994) — introduces caste‑centred narratives that challenge the dominant humanist canon.

Examiners frequently test the ability to trace formal evolution: from the episodic, moral‑didactic novel of the 1880s to the psychologically complex, politically engaged fiction of the 1970s, and to identify the role of periodicals in shaping the short‑story market. Knowledge of publication dates, serialisation practices, and the linguistic shift from centamil to kotuntamil in dialogue is essential for high‑scoring answers.

Print Capitalism, Periodicals, and the Formation of a Tamil Literary Public Sphere

The proliferation of print technology in the 19th century created a commercial infrastructure that transformed Tamil literature from courtly patronage to a market‑driven enterprise. The American Mission Press (Madras, 1820) and the Tranquebar Press (1712) produced the first printed editions of classical texts, but it was the emergence of vernacular newspapers that forged a reading public. Swadesamitran (1882), founded by G. Subramania Iyer, combined nationalist politics with literary columns, serialising novels and publishing poetry contests.

By the 1920s, illustrated weeklies such as Ananda Vikatan (1926, S. S. Vasan) and Kalki (1941, Kalki Krishnamurthy) reached circulations exceeding 100,000 copies. These magazines introduced the “short‑story page”, the “literary supplement”, and the “reader’s letter” column, institutionalising a feedback loop between writers and audiences. Advertising revenue subsidised literary content, allowing authors to earn royalties — a novelty compared with the manuscript culture where patronage was personal and sporadic.

The periodical format also dictated generic conventions. Serialised novels required cliff‑hangers at each instalment, influencing narrative pacing (e.g., Kalki’s chapter‑end suspense). Short stories were constrained to 2,000‑3,000 words, encouraging compression and the use of kotuntamil for immediacy. The “little magazine” movement of the 1960s — Manikkodi, Shanthi, Kavitha — provided avant‑garde space for modernist poetry, Dalit writing, and experimental criticism, often funded by literary societies rather than commercial advertisers.

Scholars such as A. R. Venkatachalapathy have demonstrated how print capitalism intersected with the Dravidian movement: the DMK’s Murasoli (1942) and Viduthalai (1950s) used the same distribution networks to disseminate political essays alongside literary pieces, blurring the line between propaganda and art. Understanding the economics of print — paper costs, subscription models, censorship under the Press Act (1910) — equips candidates to answer questions on the material conditions of literary production.

Tamil Diaspora Literature: Transnational Themes and the Re‑imagining of Identity

From the 19th century onward, Tamil labour migration to Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Singapore, Mauritius, South Africa, and the Caribbean generated a transnational literary corpus that negotiates memory, displacement, and cultural hybridity. Early diaspora writing appears in plantation newspapers such as The Tamil Messenger (Fiji, 1910) and Malaya Tamil Nesan (Kuala Lumpur, 1920), publishing devotional poetry, folk songs, and letters that preserve village dialects.

In the post‑colonial era, writers articulate a “double consciousness”. K. S. Maniam (Malaysia) in The Return (1981) and In a Far Country (1993) blends Tamil mythic motifs with English modernist prose, exploring the tension between ancestral rituals and urban alienation. Cheran (Sri Lanka/Canada) employs a fragmented, multilingual poetics in collections like Iruttin Ulle (1995) to voice the trauma of civil war and exile. S. Ramakrishnan (Mauritius) foregrounds Creole‑Tamil code‑switching in Kadalora Kavithaigal (2002), illustrating linguistic creolisation.

The 21st century sees digital platforms enabling new forms: blogs, e‑zines, and social‑media collectives such as the “Tamil Diaspora Writers Forum” publish flash fiction and spoken‑word performances that circulate globally. Themes include intergenerational memory, the politics of language preservation (e.g., the campaign for Tamil medium schools in the diaspora), and the re‑interpretation of classical texts — Silappathikaram read as a refugee narrative, Tirukkural invoked in human‑rights discourse.

Examiners may ask for a comparative analysis of homeland and diaspora treatments of a single motif (e.g., the “river” in Sangam poetry vs. in Maniam’s novels) or for an assessment of how diaspora literature expands the Tamil canon beyond the geo‑political boundaries of Tamil Nadu. Familiarity with key anthologies — The Oxford Anthology of Tamil Dalit Writing (includes diaspora voices), Island Voices (Sri Lankan Tamil poetry in translation) — and with critical works by R. Cheran and M. S. S. Pandian strengthens such answers.

Tamil Islamic Literature: The Sirappuranam Tradition and Arabu-Tamil Script

The corpus of Tamil Islamic literature, produced primarily by the Marakkayar and Labbai merchant-scholar communities of the Coromandel coast, constitutes a distinct literary stream that synthesises Arabic theological vocabulary with Tamil poetic forms. The foundational text is Sirappuranam (The Life of the Prophet), composed in 5,027 viruttam verses by Umaru Pulavar (c. 1642–1703) of Kayalpattinam under the patronage of the Sethupathi of Ramnad. The work adapts the Arabic sira genre into the Tamil epic convention of akaval and viruttam metres, employing iraicci (suggestion) to map Quranic episodes onto the akam landscape of kurinji (union) and palai (separation). A parallel tradition flourished in the araabu-tamil script — Tamil written in a modified Arabic alphabet with additional diacritics for Tamil phonemes — used for theological treatises (fatwa collections), devotional poetry (malaipattu), and pedagogical primers. The Mikurasu Malai (Garland of Mecca) by Cittakkati (17th century) and the Nabi Natakam (Drama of the Prophet) by Muhammad Qasim Labbai (19th century) exemplify the genre. The 19th-century Kayalpattinam press of M. C. Siddiq Lebbe printed araabu-tamil journals such as Tamil Islam (1885) and Muslim Nesan (1889), creating a print public sphere distinct from the Hindu-dominated Ananda Vikatan / Kalki axis. Examiners test the candidate’s ability to distinguish araabu-tamil as a script system from arwi (the spoken dialect mixing Tamil, Arabic, and Malayalam) and to recognise that Sirappuranam follows the kappiyam structure of paayiram (preface), nirupam (exposition), and phala-sruti (benediction), mirroring the Silappathikaram template while substituting dharma with tawhid.

Tamil Christian Literature: Missionary Prose, Beschi's Thembavani, and the Protestant Press

Christian Tamil literature originates in the 16th-century Portuguese padroado missions but achieves literary maturity with the Italian Jesuit Constanzo Beschi (Veeramamunivar, 1680–1747). His Thembavani (The Unfading Garland, 1726), a 3,615-verse epic on the life of Christ in viruttam metre, deploys the full apparatus of Tolkappiyam poetics — meyppatu (emotional states), uvamai (simile), and iraicci — to render biblical narrative into a Tamil mahakavya. Beschi’s Chaturakarati (Quadruple Lexicon, c. 1730), arranging Tamil, Sanskrit, Portuguese, and Latin entries in Tamil script, pioneers multilingual lexicography. The Protestant strand begins with Bartholomaeus Ziegenbalg (1682–1719), whose Tranquebar press (1712) produced the first Tamil New Testament (1715) and the Grammatica Damulica (1716), the first printed Tamil grammar using European typographic conventions. Ziegenbalg’s Genealogy of the South Indian Gods (1713) applies comparative philology to Tamil deity names, a method later echoed in Robert Caldwell’s Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian Languages (1856). The 19th-century Tract Society and Christian Literature Society (CLS, 1912) output — translations of Bunyan’s Pilgrim’s Progress as Paradesi Vazhi (1833), and the periodical Dharma Deepika (1849) — standardised a “prose of exposition” (urai-nilai) distinct from the ornate manipravalam of court poetry. This prose, characterised by short sentences, Sanskrit-derived abstract nouns (satyam, nyayam), and the absence of cittira-kavi ornamentation, becomes the template for modern Tamil non-fiction. Examiners frequently ask candidates to contrast Beschi’s kavya strategy (indigenisation) with Ziegenbalg’s prose strategy (standardisation) and to identify the Thembavani’s structural debt to Kambaramayanam’s bala-kandam rather than to any European epic model.

Comparative Analysis of Indigenisation Strategies
Compare the indigenisation strategies in Umaru Pulavar's Sirappuranam and Beschi's Thembavani with respect to metre, narrative structure, and theological vocabulary.
Metre: Both adopt the Tamil viruttam (four-line stanza with regulated asai count), rejecting the Arabic qasida or European blank verse. Narrative structure: Sirappuranam follows the kappiyam tripartite division (paayiram, nirupam, phala-sruti) and embeds the Prophet’s life in the akam convention of kurinji (secret union) and palai (desert separation). Thembavani mirrors Kambaramayanam’s kandam division (Bala, Ayodhya, etc.) and uses puram conventions for battle scenes (Herod’s massacre = vetchi). Theological vocabulary: Umaru Pulavar retains Arabic terms (Allah, Rasul, Quran, Jibrail) in Tamil phonology, creating a register of arwi lexical items. Beschi coins Tamil compounds — Iraiva-nayakan (Lord-Leader for Christ), Karuṇai-kadal (Sea of Mercy for Grace) — and avoids Portuguese loanwords, aiming for a “pure Tamil” theological lexicon. Key difference: Umaru Pulavar’s indigenisation is formal (metre, structure) while preserving Arabic referents; Beschi’s is lexical (neologisms) while adopting a Hindu epic template.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for “contribution of Christian missionaries to Tamil prose”, do not list only translations. Structure the answer in three layers: (1) Typography — Ziegenbalg’s press introduces punctuation, paragraph breaks, and page layout. (2) Syntax — CLS tracts replace the manipravalam long compound with Subject-Verb-Object order and finite verbs. (3) Lexicon — Abstract nouns (suya-atchi, samudayam) enter via translation of Enlightenment concepts. Cite Dharma Deepika (1849) as the first sustained prose periodical and Thembavani (1726) as the last major kavya before the prose turn.

Folk Literature and Oral Epics: Villuppattu, Nattuppurappattu, and the Documentation Projects

The category nattuppurappattu (songs of the land) encompasses ritual, occupational, and narrative genres transmitted orally across caste and regional lines. Villuppattu (bow-song), documented in the Tirunelveli and Kanyakumari districts, uses a bow-shaped instrument (villu) with bells as a resonator; the lead singer (annavi) narrates epics such as Sudalai Madan Kathai, Muthuppattan Kathai, and Isakki Amman Kathai in a call-and-response format with a chorus. The Villuppattu metre is cintu (four-beat cycle) distinct from classical asai-based metres, and its language preserves archaic phonemes — retroflex ḷ in kaḷ (stone), vowel lengthening in pāṭu — lost in standard written Tamil. The Kanyakuamri variant incorporates kalaripayattu body movements, making it a performative genre. Parallel traditions include Oyilattam (dance-song of Kongu), Kummi (clapping circle songs of women), and Urumiattam (drum-dance of the Paraiyar). The first systematic documentation was the Tamil Nadu Folk Arts Survey (1950s) led by the University of Madras under V. Raghavan, producing the Tamil Folk Songs series (1960–1975). The Dravidian University (Kuppam) and the Folklore Resources and Research Centre (St. Xavier’s College, Palayamkottai) later digitised field recordings. The Sahitya Akademi’s Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature (1987) entries on Villuppattu by A. K. Perumal remain the standard reference. Examiners test the distinction between nattuppurappattu (anonymous, communal, fluid text) and cittira-kavi (authored, fixed, ornamental) and the methodological problem of “textualising” a performance genre — the annavi improvises viruttam verses in each rendition, so no single manuscript constitutes the “work”.

The Self-Respect Movement, Rationalism, and the Literature of Social Reform

Distinct from the Dravidian movement’s electoral politics, the Self-Respect Movement (Suyamariyathai Iyakkam, 1925) founded by E. V. Ramasami (Periyar) generated a literary corpus that weaponised prose for iconoclasm. The movement’s organ Kudi Arasu (1925, weekly) published Periyar’s editorials — Pen Yen Adimaiyanal? (Why is Woman a Slave?, 1929), Saivamum Saiva Siddhantamum (Saivism and Saiva Siddhanta, 1930) — employing a deliberately flat, argumentative register devoid of alankara. The Self-Respect Marriage (Suyamariyathai Thirumanam, 1929) ceremony, stripped of Brahmin priest and Sanskrit mantra, produced a new genre: the thirumanamurai (marriage liturgy) in colloquial Tamil, later codified in the Hindu Marriage Act (Tamil Nadu Amendment) 1967. Rationalist literature (Pagutharivu Iyakkam) flourished through the Tamil Nadu Rationalist Association (1970) and its journal The Modern Rationalist (English) / Pagutharivu (Tamil). Key texts include M. Karunanidhi’s Thirukkural Urai (1962) re-reading the Kural as a secular ethical code, and the Periyar Dravidar Kazhagam’s Viduthalai (1937) serialising critiques of the Manusmriti. The movement’s literary strategy was demotic translation: rendering Sanskrit theological terms into Tamil equivalents (brahman → ananda-mayam, atman → uyir) to expose their constructedness. Examiners contrast this with the Manikodi modernists (Pudumaippithan, Ku. Pa. Rajagopalan) who used irony and interiority; the Self-Respect writers preferred the kural-urai (aphoristic commentary) and the vada-vivada (dialogue-debate) form. The 1949 split (DMK formation) shifted the literary centre from Kudi Arasu to Murasoli, but the rationalist strand continued in Unmai (1970, T. K. Chidambaranatha Mudaliar) and the contemporary Thinnai magazine (2000s).

Tamil Literary Institutions, Awards, and the Canon-Making Process

The institutional architecture that confers canonical status on Tamil works operates through three tiers: state patronage, academic validation, and market recognition. The Tamil Nadu Government’s Tamil Development Department (est. 1971) administers the Tholkappiyar Award (lifetime contribution, ₹10 lakh since 2011), the Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi Classical Tamil Award (international scholars, ₹10 lakh), and the annual Tamil Literary Garden prizes for fiction, poetry, and criticism. The Sahitya Akademi (est. 1954) awards the Sahitya Akademi Award for Tamil (first recipient: R. P. Sethu Pillai for Tamil Ilakkiya Varalaru, 1955) and the Translation Prize (from 1989). The Jnanpith Award Tamil recipients — Akilan (1975, Chitra Pavai), Jayakanthan (2002, Oru Manidhan Oru Veedu Oru Ulagam) — signal national canonisation. The Tamil University (Thanjavur, 1981) and the Central Institute of Classical Tamil (CICT, Chennai, 2008) produce critical editions: the Tamil Lexicon revision (CICT, 2019–), the Critical Edition of Silappathikaram (Tamil University, 1995, ed. R. Vijayalakshmi), and the Annotated Tirukkural (CICT, 2021). The Tamil Nadu Textbook and Educational Services Corporation determines the school and college syllabus; its 2017 revision replaced the “Bhakti” unit with “Social Reform Literature” and added Dalit writers (Bama, Imayam) to the BA curriculum. Market recognition operates through the Chennai Book Fair (annual since 1977, organised by BAPASI) and the Tamil Nadu Government’s bulk purchase scheme for public libraries (₹50 crore annual allocation). Examiners ask candidates to trace how a work moves from little magazine (e.g., Kavitha 1960s) → Sahitya Akademi award → university syllabus → critical edition → translation (e.g., Kuruthi Punal → Red River, 2001) and to critique the gender/caste bias in this pipeline — only 4 of 68 Sahitya Akademi Tamil awards (as of 2023) have gone to women, and the first Dalit recipient (Bama, 2000, for Karukku) came 45 years after the award’s inception.

  • Attributing the first Tamil prose to missionary tracts alone and overlooking the vatteluttu inscriptional prose (8th century) and the kurippu (commentarial prose) of the 12th-century Tolkappiyam commentaries.
  • Assuming Villuppattu is a single fixed text rather than a performance genre with multiple regional variants (Tirunelveli, Kanyakumari, Tenkasi) and improvisational stanzas.
  • Equating the Self-Respect Movement’s literature with DMK propaganda; the former (1925–1949) is rationalist and anti-religious, the latter (1949 onward) is electoral and selectively religious.
  • Citing the Tamil Lexicon (1924–1939) as a “critical edition” when it is a descriptive dictionary; the critical editions are the CICT/Tamil University annotated texts post-1990.
  • Overlooking the Tamil Nadu Textbook Corporation syllabus revisions as a canon-making mechanism; the 2017 inclusion of Dalit and feminist texts is examinable as state-led canon reconfiguration.
  • Treating the Sahitya Akademi Award and the Jnanpith Award as equivalent tiers; the former is language-specific (annual), the latter is all-India (lifetime), and their juries, criteria, and canon effects differ.
