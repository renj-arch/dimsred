Tamil Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

History of the Language

Tamil is a Dravidian language with one of the oldest continuous literary traditions. The Sangam corpus and the Tolkappiyam are foundational.

Literary Periods and Movements

  • Sangam period: Tolkappiyam, Ettuthogai and Pattupattu; Purananuru and Akananuru.
  • Epic period: Silappathikaram, Manimekalai and the Tamil epics.
  • Bhakti period: Nayanmars and Alvars; Tevaram and Nalayira Divya Prabandham.
  • Modern period: Subramania Bharati, the novel, drama and contemporary writing.

Major Authors

  • Thiruvalluvar: Author of the Thirukkural.
  • Ilango Adigal: Author of Silappathikaram.
  • Subramania Bharati: Modern poet and nationalist.
  • Kalki Krishnamurthy: Novelist; Ponniyin Selvan.
  • Jayakanthan: Modern fiction writer.

Major Works

  • Thirukkural — Thiruvalluvar: Couplets on ethics, polity and love.
  • Silappathikaram — Ilango Adigal: Tamil epic of Kovalan and Kannagi.
  • Ponniyin Selvan — Kalki Krishnamurthy: Historical novel.
  • Tamil modern poetry — Subramania Bharati: Nationalist and modern poetry.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Thiruvalluvar in the literary history of Tamil and assess their contribution.
Answer: Thiruvalluvar belongs to the Sangam period phase. Author of the Thirukkural. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Sangam period → Epic period → Bhakti period → Modern period.
  • Authors: Thiruvalluvar, Ilango Adigal, Subramania Bharati, Kalki Krishnamurthy, Jayakanthan.
  • Works: Thirukkural, Silappathikaram, Ponniyin Selvan, Tamil modern poetry.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

Crucial Nuances: The Examiner's Lens on Periodization and Classification

The standard curriculum often presents literary history as a linear progression of distinct eras (Sangam, Epic, Bhakti, Modern). However, the UPSC examiner frequently tests the fluidity of these boundaries. A serious aspirant must understand that periods are not watertight compartments but overlapping strata.

The Mechanism of Transition: Consider the transition from the Sangam to the Epic period. While Silappathikaram is canonically an Epic, its author Ilango Adigal was a Jain monk who lived during or immediately after the fall of the Chola dynasty (post-Sangam). The text retains the Tolkappiyam's metrical norms but introduces new theological and narrative structures. Similarly, the Bhakti movement did not replace earlier traditions but co-existed with them; the Tevaram hymns were composed in a style that mimics the Akananuru, yet the content is entirely devotional.

The Exception to the Rule: The classification of 'Modern' literature often confuses candidates. Subramania Bharati (1882–1921) is placed in the Modern period, but his poetry retains the Tamil Pattu meter and the emotional intensity of the Bhakti saints. The examiner may ask: "Does Bharati belong to the Romantic movement or the Nationalist era?" The answer lies in recognizing that he synthesized classical form with modern political consciousness. Another nuance is the 'Puranic' period (post-Epic, pre-Bhakti), which is often omitted in brief summaries but crucial for understanding the shift from secular epics to theological narratives.

Comparative Analysis: When comparing Thirukkural with Greek or Sanskrit literature, avoid superficial parallels. While all three deal with ethics, the Kural's unique feature is its didactic brevity and the absence of a narrative frame, unlike the Mahabharata. The examiner might twist this by asking about the influence of Greek philosophy on Tamil literature; the correct stance is that while there was cultural contact during the Cholas, direct textual borrowing from Greek sources in early Tamil literature is unproven, making any such claim a trap.

Technique: Deconstructing Periodization Questions
Question: "Critically examine the statement that the Bhakti movement marked a definitive break from the secular traditions of the Sangam age." In your answer, address the continuity of poetic meters and the introduction of new theological themes.
Answer Strategy:
1. Direct Answer: State that it was not a 'break' but an 'evolution'.
2. Continuity (The Anchor): Highlight that Bhakti poets like the Nayanmars used the same meters (Pattu, Turai) found in the Ettuthogai. The linguistic structure remained Dravidian.
3. Discontinuity (The Shift): Contrast the secular themes of love and war in Sangam with the monotheistic focus on Shiva/Vishnu in Bhakti.
4. Synthesis: Conclude that while the subject matter changed, the form remained continuous, proving a deep cultural continuity rather than a rupture.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Why' Over the 'What'
When asked about an author or work, never stop at listing dates and titles. The examiner awards marks for contextualization. Always link a literary movement to its socio-political backdrop (e.g., connect Subramania Bharati's poetry to the anti-colonial struggle). If a question asks about a 'movement', explain the mechanism of change: Was it a shift in language? A shift in patronage? Or a shift in ideology?

Advanced Textual Analysis: Metrics, Manuscripts, and Attribution Debates

Beyond knowing the titles, Paper I demands familiarity with the technical aspects of Tamil literature that distinguish it from other Indian languages.

The Metric System (Akam/Nakam): The Sangam corpus is divided into Akam (interior) and Nakam (exterior). This is not merely thematic but structural. The Tolkappiyam codifies specific meters for each theme. For instance, the Puram genre strictly adheres to specific word counts per line. A common advanced trap is assuming all Sangam poems follow the same meter; exceptions exist where poets innovated within strict bounds. Understanding this helps in analyzing why certain works are classified as 'Epic' (long narrative) while others are 'Anthology'.

Manuscript Variants: Unlike Sanskrit texts which have a standardized critical edition, Tamil literature has multiple manuscript traditions. The Silappathikaram exists in versions with slight variations in the ending (the fate of Kannagi). Recognizing that literary history is not monolithic but a collection of variants is crucial for high-scoring answers on 'textual criticism'.

Attribution Debates: Some works attributed to specific authors are disputed. For example, the exact dating of Kuruntokai poems is debated among scholars. Acknowledging scholarly debate (without taking sides) demonstrates critical thinking. The examiner may ask about the 'Silk Letter' controversy or the authenticity of certain Puranic texts; knowing that textual criticism in Tamil often relies on paleography and cross-referencing with foreign inscriptions is a high-level insight.

Technique: Handling Attribution and Textual Variance
Question: "Discuss the challenges in establishing a definitive text for Silappathikaram." Approach this by mentioning manuscript traditions and textual variants.
Answer Strategy:
1. Introduction: Acknowledge that unlike the Thirukkural, which has a stable text, the Silappathikaram exists in multiple manuscript versions.
2. The Core Issue: Mention that variations exist in the final chapters regarding Kannagi's curse and its impact on Madurai.
3. Methodology: Explain that scholars compare palm-leaf manuscripts (pattal) to reconstruct the original intent, noting that oral transmission played a role before standardization.
4. Conclusion: Conclude that the 'definitive' text is an idealization, and studying variants reveals the evolving reception of the epic over centuries.

Socio-Political Correlates: Literature as a Mirror of History

Tamil literature is inextricably linked to the political history of South India. The examiner often tests the ability to correlate literary movements with dynastic shifts.

The Pallava and Chola Patronage: The transition from Sangam (post-Sangam) literature to the Epic period coincides with the rise of the Pallavas and later the Cholas. Silappathikaram reflects the urbanization and trade networks of the early medieval period, distinct from the rural focus of the Sangam age. Similarly, the Bhakti movement was often supported by temple complexes built under royal patronage (e.g., the Pallava temples at Mamallapuram). Understanding that literature did not exist in a vacuum but was funded and shaped by state policy is vital.

The Modern Turn: The advent of print culture in the 19th century transformed Tamil literature from manuscript-based to print-based. This shift allowed for the emergence of novels like Ponniyin Selvan, which, while historical fiction, reflects the modern interest in reconstructing a 'golden age' during colonial times. The examiner may ask how the novel form changed the audience; the answer involves literacy rates and the spread of vernacular education.

The Dravidian Movement: This is a critical exception to the standard literary canon. In the mid-20th century, the anti-Brahmin movement influenced literature, leading to works that challenged caste hierarchies explicitly. Authors like Thol. Thirumalai and Kalki Krishnamurthy (in his later years) engaged with these themes. Ignoring this socio-political dimension is a fatal flaw in a Paper I answer.

Technique: Linking Literature to Patronage
Question: "How did the political rise of the Chola dynasty influence the themes in the Silappathikaram?" Discuss the shift from rural to urban settings.
Answer Strategy:
1. Context: The Sangam age was largely rural and pastoral.
2. The Shift: With Chola expansion, trade flourished (e.g., the pearl fisherman's story).
3. Evidence: The epic features complex court politics, urban settings like Madurai, and themes of karma and dharma relevant to a centralized state.
4. Conclusion: The literature mirrors the transition from a tribal-agricultural society to a mercantile-imperial one.
Exam-Day Strategy: Avoiding Anachronisms
Never attribute modern democratic values or secularism to ancient Sangam texts unless explicitly supported by evidence (e.g., the concept of Naduvazhi in Kural). Always qualify statements with 'approximate', 'traditionally attributed', or 'scholarly consensus' to avoid factual errors. If unsure of a date, focus on the relative chronology (e.g., 'post-Sangam') rather than guessing exact years.
  • The "Sangam" Trap: Assuming all literature from the Sangam period is secular. Remember that the Tevaram hymns are sometimes anachronistically linked to this era; they belong strictly to the Bhakti period.
  • The "Author" Trap: Attributing anonymous works to famous authors like Thiruvalluvar without evidence. Many Kural couplets were added later; stick to the core 1330 couplets accepted by scholars.
  • The "Modern" Trap: Confusing the 'Modern' period (post-18th century) with contemporary writing. Subramania Bharati is a bridge figure, not purely modern in style, though modern in ideology.
  • The "Language" Trap: Forgetting that Tamil evolved significantly. The vocabulary of Silappathikaram differs from the Tolkappiyam; assuming linguistic stasis is incorrect.

The Evolution of Prosody: From Tolkappiyam to Modern Metrics

Tamil literary history is not merely a sequence of authors and dates but a rigorous evolution of linguistic rules, primarily governed by the science of prosody (Chandas). The foundational text, Tolkappiyam, traditionally attributed to Agastya or Tolkappiar, codified the grammar and metrics of Old Tamil. However, a critical distinction for the examiner is the difference between Old Tamil Metrics (Sangam age) and New Tamil Metrics (post-Sangam). The Sangam corpus adhered to specific meters like the Akan, Ayirattu, and Puram styles, characterized by a strict adherence to syllable counts (akaram) rather than stress patterns found in Sanskrit prosody. A pivotal moment in literary history is the composition of the Mullaiyar or the commentaries that attempted to reconcile oral traditions with written norms.

The transition from manuscript-based transmission to printed standardization introduced a new layer of textual variance. In the pre-print era, manuscripts (pattal) were fluid; scribes often altered verses to fit local dialects or patronage requirements. The 19th-century revival of interest in Tamil literature, spearheaded by figures like U.V. Swaminatha Iyer and later Subramania Bharati, necessitated a standardized metric system. This is evident in the works of modern poets who consciously revived archaic meters to evoke a sense of antiquity while writing in contemporary vernacular. The examiner may ask about the concept of 'Akaram' (syllable count) versus 'Viruttam' (verse). Understanding that early Tamil poetry was defined by the number of syllables per line, not rhyme or stress, is a crucial differentiator from Western literary analysis. Furthermore, the development of Tamil Vazhthai (poetic meters) involved complex mathematical ratios of long and short syllables, distinct from the quantitative meters of Greek or Sanskrit poetry.

Examiner's trap: The Metric Trap
Question: "How did the metric system evolve in Tamil literature?" Incorrect Answer: "It evolved linearly from simple to complex." Correct Approach: Acknowledge that Old Tamil metrics were rigid and syllable-count based (e.g., 4, 5, or 6 syllables per line). The 'New' period saw a relaxation of these rules due to the influence of Sanskrit prosody (Chandas) and the need for narrative flexibility in epics like Silappathikaram. Mention that modern Tamil poetry often blends traditional meters with free verse, reflecting the tension between preserving heritage and expressing modernity.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Ambiguity
When asked about specific dates of literary works (e.g., when did Silappathikaram come to light?), do not invent a year. Use relative chronology: "Post-Sangam period, likely between the 2nd and 5th centuries CE." Always qualify uncertain claims with phrases like 'scholarly consensus suggests' or 'based on paleographic evidence.'

Textual Criticism and the Manuscript Tradition

The study of Tamil literature relies heavily on textual criticism, a discipline that examines the history of a text's transmission. Unlike the Thirukkural, which has been preserved with remarkable stability in over 100 manuscripts (the Kural Venba tradition), epics like Silappathikaram and Manimekalai exist in multiple recensions. The core issue is that no single 'original' manuscript survives; instead, scholars reconstruct the text by comparing variants found in palm-leaf manuscripts (pattal) and printed editions. A key concept here is recension: a version of a text established by a specific editor or scribe. For instance, the Silappathikaram exists in versions attributed to different editors like Kallakurichi Nambi Andar Nambi, whose role in standardizing the epic during the Chola era is debated.

The examiner often tests knowledge of the Manuscript vs. Print divide. The shift from manuscript to print culture in the 19th century (post-1800s) standardized the language but altered the text itself. Printed editions often 'corrected' archaic words or dialectal variations to make them accessible to a wider audience, effectively erasing historical linguistic layers. This is vital for analyzing works like Manimekalai, where the final chapters regarding Kannagi's curse show significant variance between different manuscript traditions. Some versions end abruptly, while others include elaborate theological commentaries. The technique involves identifying these variants and understanding their socio-political context: why was a certain version preferred by a specific dynasty or caste group? For example, the inclusion of Buddhist elements in Manimekalai might have been emphasized or downplayed depending on the patronage.

Examiner's trap: Attribution and Authorship
Question: "Who is the author of Silappathikaram?" Incorrect Answer: "Ilango Adigal." Correct Approach: While traditionally attributed to Ilango Adigal (brother of Kannagi), textual critics note that the work shows signs of multiple layers of composition. Avoid stating 'It was written by X in year Y' unless citing a specific source like the Tirumurai or Tolkappiyam. Instead, state: 'Traditionally attributed to Ilango Adigal, though textual evidence suggests a composite authorship spanning several centuries.'
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Dates
If unsure of a specific date (e.g., the exact composition date of Purananuru), focus on relative chronology. Use phrases like 'late Sangam period' or 'early medieval era.' Never guess exact years unless they are part of a well-known historical event (e.g., the coronation of Rajaraja Chola). Accuracy beats coverage; a wrong date can lose marks even if the analysis is correct.

The Bhakti Movement: Theology and Patronage

The Bhakti movement in Tamil literature represents a paradigm shift from the secular, nature-centric themes of the Sangam age to intense devotional poetry centered on deities like Shiva, Vishnu, and later Murugan. This movement is not merely religious but deeply political and social, challenging caste hierarchies and asserting the equality of all devotees before God. The Tevaram hymns (6th-7th centuries CE) by the Nayanars (Shaivites) and the Thevaram (Vaishnavite hymns) are foundational texts. A critical nuance for the examiner is the distinction between early Bhakti (Nayanar/Thevar period) and later Bhakti (12th-13th centuries, e.g., Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar). The early hymns often contain references to royal patronage, linking the divine with the earthly king (e.g., the concept of the king as a protector of dharma).

The socio-political correlates are essential: the Bhakti movement was supported by temple complexes built under royal patronage, such as the Pallava temples at Mamallapuram. The examiner may ask how the Bhakti movement influenced literature; the answer involves the emergence of new genres like Tevaram hymns and the integration of folk traditions into high literature. The language also evolved; early Bhakti poetry used simpler, more accessible Tamil compared to the Sanskritized vocabulary of later periods.
