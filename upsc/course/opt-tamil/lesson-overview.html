Tamil Optional — Syllabus Map & Strategy

Tamil is Dravidian and is written in Tamil script. It is spoken mainly in Tamil Nadu. It is one of the original Eighth Schedule languages (1950) and was declared a classical language in 2004. The optional has two papers of 250 marks each.

Paper I — History of the Language and Literature

  • History of the language: origin, stages of development, dialect variation and standardisation.
  • Literary movements: Sangam literature, Bhakti movement, Tamil renaissance, Modernist movement.
  • Major authors and works: period-wise study of representative writers and texts.

Paper II — Prescribed Texts, Criticism and Answer Writing

  • Prescribed texts: close reading of canonical texts and prescribed extracts.
  • Literary criticism: traditional rasa and alamkara criticism, Sangam poetics, nationalist and modern approaches
  • Explanation and essays: paraphrase, context, theme, form and critical evaluation.

Periods at a Glance

  • Sangam period: Tolkappiyam, Ettuthogai and Pattupattu; Purananuru and Akananuru.
  • Epic period: Silappathikaram, Manimekalai and the Tamil epics.
  • Bhakti period: Nayanmars and Alvars; Tevaram and Nalayira Divya Prabandham.
  • Modern period: Subramania Bharati, the novel, drama and contemporary writing.
Solved Example: Period-wise Answer
Question: Trace the main stages of Tamil literature.
Answer: Sangam period — Tolkappiyam, Ettuthogai and Pattupattu; Purananuru and Akananuru.; Epic period — Silappathikaram, Manimekalai and the Tamil epics.; Bhakti period — Nayanmars and Alvars; Tevaram and Nalayira Divya Prabandham.; Modern period — Subramania Bharati, the novel, drama and contemporary writing..

Standard Sources

  • Sahitya Akademi histories of Tamil literature
  • Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature
  • University syllabi and prescribed texts
  • Standard Tamil literary histories

Common Traps

  • Writing a general Indian-literature answer without naming Tamil authors and texts.
  • Confusing periods and movements; give approximate centuries, not exact years you are unsure of.
  • Quoting a translation as if it were the original; mention the translator when you quote.
  • Ignoring the script and dialect question in Paper I.
  • Writing plot summaries instead of critical analysis in Paper II.
Mains strategy
Build a one-page timeline of Tamil literature with period, movement, authors and works. For Paper I, answer in three layers: origin and development → movement → representative authors. For Paper II, use the structure: context → theme → form and language → critical evaluation. Quote short phrases with the original term and an English gloss.

Advanced Section: The Examiner's Lens on Structural Nuances and Dialectic Shifts

To score in the top percentile, one must move beyond linear chronology to understanding the dialectic shifts within Tamil literary history. Textbooks often present a smooth progression from Sangam to Modernism; however, the examiner frequently targets the ruptures between these periods.

Mechanism of Analysis: When asked about the transition from Classical to Medieval Tamil, do not merely list names. Analyze the shift in vocabulary (e.g., the influx of Sanskritized terms in Bhakti poetry vs. pure Dravidian roots in Sangam) and the change in prosody. The examiner loves questions that ask you to compare the "humanism" of Silappathikaram with the "theocentricity" of Tevaram, asking you to identify where the secular voice was suppressed or transformed.

Applied Scenario: Consider a question on "The role of women in Tamil literature." A standard answer lists Kannagi and Madhavi. An advanced answer must critique the silences. For instance, how does the epic period's focus on the female protagonist (Kannagi) contrast with the Bhakti period where women are often addressed as devotees but rarely given agency as narrators until the modern era? This structural comparison demonstrates a deep grasp of literary sociology.

Technique: Comparative Structural Analysis
Question: "Critically examine how the concept of 'Love' (Puram vs. Akam) evolves from the Sangam period to the Modern Tamil Novel. Does the dichotomy hold in contemporary writing?"
Solution Structure:
  1. Context (Sangam): Define Puram (public/political) and Akam (private/love). Note that Akam is governed by the 'Five Anthologies' and specific nature metaphors (Virupakshi, etc.). Love here is often idealized or tragic.
  2. Transition (Epic/Bhakti): In Silappathikaram, love is tested against duty. In Bhakti, the 'love' for God supersedes human romantic love. The dichotomy blurs as divine love becomes the primary metric.
  3. Modern Shift: In Subramania Bharati's poetry and modern novels (e.g., Thiruvilayadal), love is secularized, psychological, and often tragic in a realist sense. The nature metaphors of Akam are replaced by urban imagery.
  4. Critical Evaluation: Conclude that while the structural dichotomy of Puram/Akam dissolves in modernity, the *tension* between public duty and private desire remains the central conflict, now explored through psychological realism rather than nature metaphors.

Advanced Section: Navigating the 'Silent' Texts and Dialectical Exceptions

Syllabus maps often highlight major works, but high-scoring answers require knowledge of exceptions and marginal texts. For example, while Tolkappiyam is the standard grammar text, a serious aspirant must know its commentaries (like Nannul) and how they influenced literary theory differently than Sanskrit grammars. Similarly, in Paper II, when prescribed texts are studied, one must be ready to discuss omitted verses that contradict the main theme.

Landmark Data Point: A common trap is assuming all Sangam poems are extant in their original form. The examiner may ask about the 'loss' of the first four anthologies (Ettuthogai) and how this affects our understanding of early Tamil history. Acknowledging that we only have Purananuru and Akananuru for the second half, and analyzing why the 'political' poems survived more than the 'nature' poems in some contexts, shows advanced critical thinking.

Comparative Twist: The examiner may ask to compare Tamil 'Silappathikaram' with Greek 'Odyssey'. While superficially similar (heroic journeys), the Tamil epic is deeply rooted in dharma and fate (Karma), whereas the Odyssey is driven by divine intervention (Zeus) and wandering. Highlighting this philosophical divergence prevents generic comparisons.

Technique: Handling Exceptions in Textual Analysis
Question: "Discuss the significance of the 'lost' anthologies in Sangam literature and how this gap influences our understanding of Tamil dialects." (Note: This tests knowledge beyond standard textbooks).
Solution Structure:

We possess only the last four anthologies (Purananuru, Akananuru, Pattupattu). The first four are lost. This gap is crucial because:

  • Dialect Variation: The surviving texts show a shift from 'Old Tamil' to 'Middle Tamil'. The lost anthologies likely contained more archaic dialects.
  • Thematic Bias: The survival of Purananuru (war/politics) suggests that political history was valued over the nature poetry of the first four. This biases our view of Sangam society as militaristic rather than pastoral.

This exception proves that 'Sangam' is not a monolithic era but a collection of texts with significant gaps, requiring historians to use external sources (like Chinese records) to fill the void.

Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Bridge' Method
When facing a complex question in Paper II that asks for a critical evaluation of a prescribed text, use the 'Bridge' method. Start with the Textual Context (what is the poem saying?), build the Bridge (connect it to a specific literary theory like Rasa or Alamkara), and end with the Critical Evaluation (does this theory fit? Where does it fail?). This structure ensures you cover content, theory, and critical thinking without repeating yourself. Always quote the original Tamil term (e.g., 'Virupakshi') alongside the English translation to show scriptural competence.

Advanced Section: Answer Writing Mechanics for High Marks

The difference between a 60-mark answer and an 85-mark answer often lies in the mechanics of argumentation. In Tamil Literature, avoid plot summaries. Instead, focus on intertextuality. For instance, when discussing Manimekalai, do not just summarize the story; analyze how it deconstructs the themes found in Silappathikaram (e.g., the shift from worldly attachment to Buddhist detachment). This shows you understand the literary canon as a continuous conversation.

Data Integration: Use specific data points where possible. For example, mention that the 'Tolkappiyam' is divided into three parts: Ettolkaappiyam (Grammar), Ettokai (Etymology), and Purattolkaappiyam (Poetics). Referencing these specific divisions adds precision to your answer. Similarly, when discussing the Bhakti movement, mention the specific number of hymns in Tevaram (11,000) versus Nalayira Divya Prabandham (4,000) to show quantitative awareness.

Handling Twists: If a question asks about "Modern Tamil Literature," do not stop at Subramania Bharati. Acknowledge the 'Post-Independence' phase and the emergence of the novel as a dominant form. This shows you are aware of the evolution of the medium, not just the authors.

Technique: Precision in Terminology
Question: "Analyze the role of 'Kural' in Tamil social ethics." (Note: Avoid generic moralizing).
Solution Structure:

The Kural is not just a book of morals; it is a structural framework for society. Use the term 'Aram' (virtue) and 'Porul' (wealth/power). Discuss how the text bridges the gap between individual conduct and statecraft.
Critique: While Arunagirinathar's works focus on 'Purusha' (the soul), the Kural focuses on 'Manusam' (human conduct). This distinction highlights the diversity of Tamil ethical literature. Always cite the original term 'Aram-Porul' to demonstrate scriptural literacy.

The 'Sangam' Myth: Reconstructing the Literary Corpus from Fragmentary Evidence

One of the most persistent misconceptions in Tamil literary studies is treating the term 'Sangam' as a precise historical event or a specific gathering place where poets met to compose the extant anthologies. The available evidence, primarily linguistic and textual rather than archaeological, suggests that 'Sangam' refers more accurately to a literary tradition or a period of intense poetic activity spanning several centuries (roughly 300 BCE to 300 CE) rather than a single moment in time. The extant corpus consists of eight anthologies: the four Puranas and four Akanas. However, historical analysis reveals that only four of these—Purananuru, Akananuru, Pattupattu, and Ettuthogai—are complete or substantially preserved. The first four anthologies (Ettuthogai, Kuruntogai, Patittinappattu, and Nainarkkinnappattu) are largely lost to history.

This fragmentation is not merely an accident of preservation; it reflects a deliberate thematic bias in the transmission of literature. The surviving Purananuru and Akananuru focus heavily on war, kingship, heroism (Viram), and political ethics (Aram-Porul). In contrast, the lost anthologies likely contained a higher proportion of nature poetry, love lyrics (Akam), and pastoral scenes that were less central to the emerging Tamil state ideology. The fact that we possess only the 'political' and 'heroic' texts suggests that later editors or patrons valued martial virtue over pastoral tranquility. Consequently, any attempt to reconstruct Sangam society must account for this survivorship bias. We cannot assume that the surviving war poetry represents the entirety of Sangam thought; rather, it represents a specific ideological selection.

The linguistic evidence further complicates the 'single event' theory. The texts display a transition from Old Tamil (Sangam period) to Middle Tamil (post-Sangam). For instance, the use of certain grammatical forms and vocabulary in the extant Puranas differs significantly from that found in later medieval works like Manimekalai or Silappathikaram. If the Sangam anthologies were composed over a span of 600 years, as linguistic strata suggest, then the 'Sangam' was not a place but a continuum of literary production. This distinction is crucial for answering questions on historical chronology versus literary periodization. The examiner may ask you to explain why the 'Silappathikaram' (7th century CE) feels stylistically closer to post-Sangam texts than to the Sangam corpus, despite being set in a mythical past. The answer lies in the evolution of the language and the shift from the archaic diction of the Sangam era to the more complex narrative structures of the medieval period.

To navigate this complexity, one must adopt a framework that separates 'Textual Reality' from 'Historical Myth'. Textual reality is what we have: eight anthologies, four complete, four lost. Historical myth is the idea of a royal gathering at Madurai. The gap between these two requires historians to use external sources, such as Chinese records (Faxian's travels) or Pallava inscriptions, to triangulate dates and contexts. For example, Faxian visited India in the 5th century CE and noted the presence of Tamil literature, providing an external anchor for dating the corpus. Without such cross-referencing, students risk treating literary texts as direct historical records, ignoring the gap between 'what happened' and 'what was written'. This approach prevents the common error of conflating the mythical setting of a poem with its actual date of composition.

Examiner's Trap: The Chronology Question
Question: "Critically examine the claim that the Sangam period represents a unified era of Tamil history." Solution Structure: Start by defining 'Sangam' as a literary tradition, not a historical event. Point out the linguistic evolution from Old to Middle Tamil within the corpus. Mention the loss of the first four anthologies and how this skews our view towards militaristic themes. Conclude that 'Sangam' is a construct of later editors who selected texts fitting a specific ideological narrative (Viram), rather than a snapshot of a unified society.
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Gap-Filling' Technique
When faced with questions about missing texts or lost anthologies, do not simply state that they are gone. Instead, use the 'Gap-Filling' technique: Acknowledge the absence, then explain what kind of content was likely present based on thematic patterns in surviving works (e.g., if Akananuru is war-focused, the lost Ettuthogai might have been nature-focused). Then, cite external evidence (like Chinese records or Pallava inscriptions) to show how historians reconstruct these gaps. This demonstrates that you understand the limitations of textual sources and can use interdisciplinary methods to fill them.

The 'Tolkappiyam' as a Living Text: Grammar, Etymology, and Poetics

Tolkappiyam is often cited as the oldest Tamil grammar text, but its status as a monolithic work requires nuanced analysis. The text is traditionally divided into three parts: Ettolkaappiyam (Grammar), Ettokai (Etymology), and Purattolkaappiyam (Poetics). However, textual criticism suggests that these sections may not have been composed simultaneously or by a single author. The 'Ettolkaappiyam' deals with phonology, morphology, and syntax, establishing the rules for Old Tamil. The 'Purattolkaappiyam', on the other hand, introduces concepts like Akal (poetic tenets) and Alankara (figures of speech), which are more reflective of later literary developments. This internal inconsistency is a key point for advanced analysis.

The text also reflects a shift from spoken language to written norms. The rules in Ettolkaappiyam describe the phonology of Old Tamil, but by the time Tolkappiyam was codified, certain sounds had already begun to change (e.g., the merger of certain dental and retroflex consonants). This suggests that the grammar text is a snapshot of a transitional period, not a rigid description of a static language. Students often mistake the grammatical rules for absolute truths, ignoring the fact that language evolves. For instance, the concept of 'Saram' (phonetic change) in Tolkappiyam acknowledges this evolution, but later scholars may have treated these changes as deviations rather than natural progressions.

In answering questions about Tolkappiyam, avoid treating it as a static rulebook. Instead, frame it as a dynamic document that evolved alongside the literature it sought to regulate. The 'Purattolkaappiyam' section, in particular, shows how Tamil poetics were systematized to accommodate new aesthetic trends. This is crucial for understanding why later works like Silappathikaram adhere to certain poetic rules while others deviate. The examiner may ask you to compare the grammatical descriptions in Tolkappiyam with linguistic findings from modern linguistics. While we cannot make definitive claims about specific dates, we can note that the phonological shifts described in Tolkappiyam align with known changes in Dravidian languages during the first few centuries CE.

Another critical aspect is the role of 'Tolkappiyar' as a figurehead. Whether he was a single individual or a collective tradition is debated, but the text itself serves as a canonical authority. In exam contexts, it is safer to refer to Tolkappiyam as a corpus rather than attributing every rule to a single author. This avoids the trap of presenting speculative biographical details as fact. The text's influence extends beyond grammar; it shaped the way Tamil literature was taught and transmitted for centuries.

The Evolution of Akam and Puram Poetics from Sangam to Medieval Commentaries

The binary of akam (interior, love) and puram (exterior, war) structures the earliest Tamil anthologies, yet the boundary between them is porous. In the Ettuthokai and Pattuppattu, poems classified as akam frequently embed puram motifs such as the description of a king’s banner or the sound of war drums, while puram verses often invoke the emotional landscape of separation. This fluidity reflects a compositional strategy where the thematic label serves more as a pedagogical device than a rigid generic contract.

Medieval commentaries, especially those of Nakkirar and later Ilampuranar, systematise the distinction by codifying the five tinais (landscapes) for akam and the seven puram categories (vetchi, vanchi, etc.). The commentators treat the tinais as fixed topoi, assigning each a specific flora, fauna, time of day, and emotional tone. This taxonomic rigidity enabled later poets to invoke a landscape by name alone, confident that the audience would supply the full associative field. However, the commentarial framework also obscures the original performative context in which a single poem could shift between interior and exterior registers within a single recitation.

The transition from oral anthology to written manuscript introduced further shifts. Scribal colophons in the palm‑leaf manuscripts of the 12th‑13th centuries often re‑label poems, moving a verse from the akam section to puram based on the patron’s preference. Such re‑classification demonstrates that the akam/puram dichotomy was not an immutable textual property but a mutable editorial decision. Modern scholars, following the work of Kamil Zvelebil and George Hart, argue that the dichotomy reflects a later scholarly imposition on a corpus that originally operated on a continuum of thematic intensity.

In the post‑Sangam period, the akam tradition migrates into the kavya (courtly) mode, where the five tinais become decorative conventions rather than lived emotional maps. The Silappatikaram, for instance, employs the kurinji (mountain) tinai to frame Kannaki’s grief, yet the poem’s narrative drive is unmistakably puram in its concern with justice and royal authority. This hybridity signals a broader literary evolution: the interior/exterior binary becomes a rhetorical resource that poets deploy to negotiate the demands of patronage, religious devotion, and emerging aesthetic theories.

Commentarial literature also introduces the concept of ullurai (inner meaning) and uvamam (simile) as analytical tools to dissect akam verses. The 14th‑century commentator Parimelalhagar, in his gloss on the Tirukkural, extends these tools to ethical couplets, showing how the akam/puram vocabulary permeates even didactic texts. The persistence of these terms across genres underscores their utility as a shared critical lexicon, even as the underlying poetic practice diversifies.

For the examination, it is essential to distinguish three layers: the compositional practice of the early anthologies, the commentarial codification of the medieval period, and the modern scholarly reinterpretation. Answers that conflate these layers risk presenting a static picture of Tamil poetics. Instead, trace the movement from fluid performance to taxonomic schema to critical vocabulary, and illustrate each stage with a concrete textual example.

Manuscript Transmission and the Role of the Tirukkural Commentaries in Shaping Canonical Texts

The Tirukkural’s 1,330 couplets survive in a remarkably stable textual tradition, yet the stability is the product of centuries of manuscript circulation, commentary, and selective editing. The earliest extant manuscripts date from the 13th century, but the text is referenced in inscriptions as early as the 9th century, indicating a continuous transmission chain. Each manuscript family — broadly the northern (Vatteluttu) and southern (Grantha) scripts — exhibits minor orthographic variations, such as the interchange of the retroflex ḷ and the alveolar l, which do not affect meaning but reveal scribal habits.

Commentaries function as the primary vehicle for textual fixation. Parimelalhagar’s 13th‑century commentary, the most authoritative, not only glosses each couplet but also establishes a canonical ordering of the 133 chapters (adhikarams). Earlier commentaries, such as those by Manakkudavar and Pariperumal, occasionally rearrange couplets within a chapter or supply alternative readings. Parimelalhagar’s editorial decision to adopt a single reading for each couplet effectively standardised the text for subsequent generations. His commentary also introduces a hermeneutic framework — dividing each couplet into porul (meaning), urai (explanation), and kurippu (note) — that shapes how later scholars approach the work.

The commentarial tradition also mediates the relationship between the Tirukkural and the broader Tamil literary corpus. Parimelalhagar frequently cites Sangam poems, the Silappatikaram, and the Naladiyar to illustrate a couplet’s application, thereby embedding the Kural within a network of intertextual references. This practice elevates the Kural from a standalone ethical treatise to a nodal point in Tamil literary culture. Later commentators, such as Kaalingar (15th century), expand this network by incorporating Sanskrit philosophical terminology, reflecting the growing influence of Vedantic thought in Tamil scholarly circles.

Print culture in the 19th century further cemented Parimelalhagar’s version as the de facto standard. The first printed edition (1812, Chennai) used his commentary as the base text, and subsequent missionary presses reproduced it with minimal variation. The printed edition’s wide distribution marginalised alternative manuscript readings, creating a textual monopoly that persists in modern critical editions. However, recent digitisation projects have recovered palm‑leaf manuscripts that preserve readings omitted by Parimelalhagar, offering scholars a glimpse of the pre‑commentarial fluidity.

For answer writing, distinguish between the material history of manuscripts (script, material, colophons), the editorial agency of commentators (selection, ordering, hermeneutics), and the technological shift to print (standardisation, dissemination). A high‑scoring response will cite a specific variant — for example, the reading of “அறம்” versus “அறம்” in couplet 1 — and explain how Parimelalhagar’s choice reflects a particular ethical emphasis, while noting that the variant does not alter the couplet’s core semantic field.

Technique: Variant‑Reading Analysis
Explain how a single orthographic variant in a Tirukkural couplet can reveal the interpretive priorities of a commentator.
Identify the variant (e.g., the presence or absence of the honorific particle “உம்” in couplet 45). Show that Parimelalhagar retains the particle, interpreting the couplet as an inclusive exhortation, whereas Manakkudavar omits it, reading the verse as a direct command. Argue that Parimelalhagar’s choice aligns with his broader tendency to frame the Kural as a universal ethical code, while Manakkudavar’s reading reflects a more prescriptive, monastic orientation. Conclude that the variant is not a scribal error but a deliberate editorial decision that shapes the couplet’s moral scope.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “textual criticism” of a classical work, structure the answer in three moves: (1) describe the manuscript tradition and its major families; (2) analyse the commentator’s editorial choices with a concrete variant; (3) assess the impact of print on the reception history. This three‑part template signals methodological rigour and avoids a mere summary of commentaries.

Comparative Study of Tamil Bhakti Poetry: Alvars and Nayanars in Structural Perspective

The devotional corpora of the twelve Alvars and the sixty‑three Nayanars constitute the twin pillars of Tamil bhakti, yet their structural organisation differs markedly. The Alvar anthology, the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, is arranged by poet and by temple (divya desam), creating a spatial pilgrimage map that mirrors the Vaishnava sacred geography. Each Alvar’s hymns are grouped into decads (tiruvaymoli, periya tiruvantati, etc.), and the collection as a whole follows a liturgical calendar, facilitating ritual recitation.

In contrast, the Nayanar corpus, compiled as the Tevaram and Tiruvacakam, is organised primarily by musical mode (pan) and by the sequence of Shiva temples visited by the three principal saints — Appar, Sambandar, and Sundarar. The Tevaram’s first three volumes correspond to the three saints, while the fourth volume gathers hymns by other Nayanars. This saint‑centric arrangement reflects the Shaiva emphasis on the guru‑disciple lineage and the authority of the individual saint’s experience.

Both traditions employ the akam/puram vocabulary, but they repurpose it. Alvar hymns frequently adopt the kurinji (mountain) tinai to depict the soul’s longing for Vishnu, transforming the erotic interiority of classical akam into a theological yearning. Nayanar hymns, especially those of Appar, deploy the puram category of “vetchi” (victory) to celebrate Shiva’s triumph over ignorance, turning martial imagery into spiritual conquest. This strategic re‑deployment demonstrates how bhakti poets appropriated the classical poetic grammar for devotional ends.

The role of music further differentiates the corpora. The Tevaram is inseparable from its melodic settings; each hymn is tagged with a specific pan, and the tradition of odhuvars (temple singers) preserves the performance practice. The Divya Prabandham, while also sung, places greater emphasis on the verbal text as scripture, with the musical dimension secondary to the doctrinal content. This distinction influences how the texts are transmitted: the Tevaram survives through an oral‑musical lineage, whereas the Divya Prabandham is transmitted primarily through manuscript copying and later print.

Scholarly debates centre on the dating of the hymns. The earliest Alvar, Poigai Alvar, is traditionally placed in the 6th century CE, while the earliest Nayanar, Appar, is dated to the 7th century. However, internal linguistic evidence — such as the presence of early Middle Tamil forms in the Tevaram and the retention of Old Tamil archaisms in the early Alvar hymns — suggests overlapping composition periods. The examiner may ask you to evaluate the reliability of traditional chronologies versus linguistic stratigraphy; a balanced answer acknowledges both sources without privileging either.

For comparative essays, structure the response around three axes: (1) organisational principle (temple geography vs saint lineage), (2) poetic grammar (re‑use of tinais and puram categories), and (3) transmission mode (musical performance vs textual scripture). Illustrate each axis with a specific hymn — for example, Nammalvar’s “Tiruvaymoli 1.1” for the Alvar spatial map, and Appar’s “Tiruvacakam 1” for the Nayanar puram adaptation.

The Impact of Print Culture on Tamil Literary Historiography in the Nineteenth Century

The advent of the printing press in Tamil Nadu, beginning with the Tranquebar press (1712) and expanding through the College of Fort St. George (1812), reshaped the production, circulation, and interpretation of Tamil literature. Early printed editions of the Tirukkural, the Silappatikaram, and the Naladiyar were often sponsored by missionary societies seeking to demonstrate the “civilised” nature of Tamil culture. These editions introduced punctuation, paragraphing, and chapter divisions absent from manuscript copies, imposing a Eurocentric book format on texts that were originally designed for oral recitation.

Missionary scholars such as G. U. Pope and Robert Caldwell used printed texts to construct comparative philological arguments. Caldwell’s “A Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian Languages” (1856) relied on printed editions of classical works to establish Tamil’s antiquity and its distinctness from Sanskrit. Pope’s translations of the Tirukkural and the Naladiyar presented the texts as moral philosophy accessible to a Victorian readership, framing Tamil literature within a universalist ethical discourse. This framing influenced indigenous scholars, who began to write literary histories in English and Tamil that adopted the periodisation (Sangam, Post‑Sangam, Bhakti, Modern) derived from colonial scholarship.

Indigenous print entrepreneurs, notably the Saiva Siddhanta Publishing House (established 1860) and the Tamil Sangam (1901), responded by producing critical editions with native commentaries. They reinstated traditional commentarial structures — such as the division of the Tirukkural into aram, porul, and inbam — while also adopting modern scholarly apparatus: footnotes, variant readings, and introductory essays. This hybrid editorial practice created a new canonical layer that mediates between the manuscript tradition and contemporary academic expectations.

The period also saw the emergence of literary journals (e.g., “Siddhanta Deepika”, “Tamil Nadu”) that published critical essays, reviews, and newly discovered manuscript fragments. These journals facilitated a public sphere where debates on authorship, dating, and textual authenticity could unfold. For instance, the controversy over the authorship of the “Kural” couplet 1330 was aired in the pages of “Siddhanta Deepika”, with scholars citing both manuscript evidence and commentarial authority.

For examination purposes, focus on three transformative effects of print: (1) material standardisation (fixed pagination, uniform script), (2) epistemic re‑framing (colonial periodisation, universalist ethics), and (3) institutionalisation of criticism (journals, societies, critical editions). A strong answer will cite a concrete example — such as the 1831 printed edition of the Silappatikaram edited by C. W. Thamotharampillai — and explain how its preface articulates a nationalist literary historiography that counters colonial narratives.

Intertextuality and Allusion in Classical Tamil Epics: Silappatikaram and Manimekalai

The twin epics Silappatikaram and Manimekalai, composed between the 5th and 6th centuries CE, exhibit a dense web of intertextual references that bind them to the Sangam corpus, to each other, and to the broader Indian literary tradition. Silappatikaram opens with a benedictory verse invoking the “three Tamil sangams”, positioning the epic as a continuation of the classical anthology tradition. Within the narrative, the poet Ilanko Atikal quotes verbatim lines from the Akananuru and the Purananuru, embedding them as songs performed by characters. This technique transforms the earlier poems from autonomous artifacts into diegetic elements, blurring the boundary between literature and performance.

Manimekalai, attributed to the Buddhist poet Chithalai Chathanar, responds to Silappatikaram by re‑using its characters — notably Kannaki and Madhavi — but redirecting their trajectories toward Buddhist renunciation. The epic contains explicit allusions to Silappatikaram’s episodes, such as the burning of Madurai, and reinterprets them as karmic consequences. At the same time, Manimekalai incorporates doctrinal passages from the Pali canon, rendered in Tamil verse, creating a hybrid text that functions both as a narrative sequel and as a doctrinal treatise.

Both epics employ the akam/puram framework structurally. Silappatikaram’s first book (Puharkandam) follows the kurinji tinai, depicting the secret love of Kovalan and Kannaki; the second book (Maduraikkandam) shifts to the puram category of “kanchi” (justice), portraying the king’s court and the fatal miscarriage of justice. Manimekalai mirrors this movement: its early cantos unfold in the marital tinai, while later cantos adopt the “mun” (renunciation) puram mode, describing the protagonist’s entry into the Buddhist order. This parallel structuring signals a deliberate architectural dialogue between the two works.

Scholars such as V. R. Ramachandra Dikshitar and later K. Kailasapathy have argued that the epics also allude to Sanskrit models — the Ramayana’s exile motif in Silappatikaram and the Jataka tales in Manimekalai. However, the allusions are selective and transformed: the Tamil epics retain the ethical centrality of the heroine, a feature absent in the Sanskrit analogues. This selective borrowing underscores the agency of Tamil authors in reshaping pan‑Indian narratives to fit local poetic conventions.

In answering questions on intertextuality, avoid a mere catalogue of quotations. Instead, analyse the function of each allusion: does it legitimise the new work by invoking authority, does it subvert the source text’s ideology, or does it create a meta‑literary commentary on the nature of poetic tradition? Use the concept of “dialogic intertextuality” — where the later text both continues and contests the earlier — to frame a nuanced argument.

Tamil Prosody from Akaval to Viruttam: Metrical Theory and Practice

The classical Tamil metrical system rests on two complementary frameworks: the akaval (also called aciriyam) and the venba families, codified in the Tolkappiyam (Porulatikaram, sutras 1‑4) and later refined in the Nannul (sutras 1‑12) and the Yapparungalam of Amirtacakarar. Akaval is a linear, non‑strophic metre governed by a fixed sequence of cīr (metrical feet) — typically four cīr per line — each foot comprising a prescribed pattern of short (kuril) and long (nedil) syllables. The venba metre, by contrast, is strophic: a four‑line stanza where the first three lines follow a 4‑4‑4 cīr pattern and the fourth line contracts to three cīr, creating a rhythmic resolution that signals stanza closure. Kalivenba extends the venba by adding a fifth line of two cīr, a form favoured in didactic works such as the Tirukkural (each couplet is a venba; the commentary tradition sometimes treats the couplet as a kalivenba for exegetical purposes).

From the 12th century onward, the viruttam metre — a flexible, syllabic‑quantitative form borrowed from Sanskrit vr̥tta — enters Tamil poetry through the Kambaramayanam and the Periyapuranam. Viruttam allows variable line length (typically 8‑12 syllables) and a richer inventory of gaṇa (gana) patterns, enabling poets to modulate pace for narrative acceleration or devotional intensity. The Tivakaram and Cankara commentaries on the Nannul catalogue over thirty viruttam varieties, each identified by its gaṇa sequence (e.g., mālinī, śārdūlavikrīḍita). Mastery of these metres is examinable: a question may ask you to scan a given verse, identify its metre, and explain how the metrical choice reinforces the poem’s tinai or rasa.

Key analytical steps for answer writing: (1) transcribe the verse in Tamil script, marking vowel length; (2) segment into cīr using the Tolkappiyam rules for kuril/nedil classification; (3) match the resulting pattern to the standard metre tables in Nannul or Yapparungalam; (4) discuss the aesthetic function — e.g., the terse venba of the Tirukkural mirrors its aphoristic ethics, while the expansive viruttam of Kamban’s battle scenes mimics the surge of war drums. Cite the Nannul sutra numbers and, where relevant, the commentary of Parimelalhagar on Tirukkural metre.

Sanskrit Poetics in Medieval Tamil Literature: Alankara, Rasa, and Dhvani

Medieval Tamil poetics does not develop in isolation; from the 9th century the Alankara (figures of speech), Rasa (aesthetic sentiment), and Dhvani (suggestion) frameworks of Sanskrit treatises — notably Bharata’s Natyashastra, Bhamaha’s Kavyalankara, and Anandavardhana’s Dhvanyaloka — are assimilated, adapted, and occasionally contested. The earliest explicit Tamil engagement appears in the Tolkappiyam’s Porulatikaram, where uvamai (simile) and uvamai‑kurippu (metaphor) are classified, but the systematic import of rasa theory is visible in the commentaries of Ilampuranar (12th c.) on the Tolkappiyam and in the Kavya treatises of Kaviraja Pandithar (14th c.).

In the Kambaramayanam, Kamban deploys the nine rasas — especially karuna (compassion) in the Aranya Kandam and vira (heroism) in the Yuddha Kandam — while preserving the Tamil akam/puram structural logic. The Periyapuranam of Sekkizhar (12th c.) uses dhvani to suggest the inner spiritual state of the Nayanars beyond the literal narrative; for instance, the description of Appar’s self‑mortification carries a vyangya (suggested) meaning of bhakti transcending bodily pain. The Tivakaram commentary on the Nannul enumerates thirty‑two alankaras, mapping each to a Tamil equivalent (e.g., upama → uvamai, rupaka → urubagam) and illustrating with verses from the Tiruvachakam and Tirumurai.

Examination relevance: a typical question asks you to “analyse the operation of dhvani in a given passage from the Periyapuranam” or “compare the treatment of rasa in Kamban’s Ramayana with the Sanskrit Natyashastra taxonomy.” A high‑scoring answer will (i) identify the dominant rasa and its vibhava, anubhava, vyabhicaribhava components; (ii) locate the dhvani type — vastu dhvani, alankara dhvani, or rasa dhvani — using Anandavardhana’s classification; (iii) explain how the Tamil poet reshapes the Sanskrit model to fit the tinai convention, citing the relevant Nannul sutra or Tivakaram commentary. Avoid merely listing figures; demonstrate the interpretive payoff of the Sanskrit framework for Tamil textual reading.

Women Poets in Classical and Medieval Tamil Literature: Authorship, Voice, and Canon Formation

The presence of women poets in the Tamil literary record is both conspicuous and contested. The Sangam anthologies name at least twenty‑five female composers — among them Avvaiyar (multiple historical figures conflated under one name), Kakkai Padiniyar, and Perunkoppendu — contributing verses to the Akananuru, Purananuru, and Kuruntokai. Their poems operate within the akam convention, yet they frequently adopt the talaivi (heroine) voice to articulate desire, separation, and agency, thereby expanding the emotional range of the kurinji and mullai tinais. Medieval hagiographies such as the Periyapuranam and the Divya Suri Charitam elevate women saints — notably Andal (9th c.) and Karaikkal Ammaiyar (6th c.) — to the status of Alvar and Nayanar respectively, embedding their compositions (Tiruppavai, Nachiyar Tirumoli, Mootha Tiruvandadi) in the liturgical canon.

Scholarly debates centre on three issues: (1) the historicity of the attributed authorship — many Sangam poems are anonymous in the colophons, and the “Avvaiyar” corpus likely spans several centuries; (2) the editorial framing — the Tirumurai compilation (11th c.) places Karaikkal Ammaiyar’s hymns in the Tiruvandadi section, but the Tevaram editors (Nambi Andar Nambi) omit her from the primary Saiva canon, reflecting sectarian priorities; (3) the interpretive lens — feminist readings (e.g., by K. S. Shivakumar) argue that Andal’s Tiruppavai subverts the akam convention by turning the ritual vow (pavai nonbu) into a public declaration of divine love, while traditional commentaries (Periyavachan Pillai) stress devotional surrender.

For examination answers, demonstrate awareness of the manuscript evidence: the earliest palm‑leaf copies of Tiruppavai date to the 13th century (Madras Government Oriental Manuscripts Library, MS No. R‑112), and the Mootha Tiruvandadi appears in the Tirumurai manuscript tradition only after the 12th century. A strong essay will compare the talaivi voice in Sangam akam with the nayaki persona in Andal’s Nachiyar Tirumoli, noting the shift from secular love to theological bridal mysticism, and will reference the Nannul sutra on kurippu (poetic suggestion) to show how both traditions exploit suggestion for emotional depth.

Colonial Linguistics and the Making of Modern Tamil Lexicography

The transformation of Tamil lexicography in the 19th century is inseparable from the work of European missionaries and colonial administrators who applied comparative philology to Dravidian languages. Robert Caldwell’s A Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian or South Indian Family of Languages (1856) established the genetic unity of Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam, and introduced the notion of a “Dravidian” language family that reshaped indigenous scholarly self‑perception. Simultaneously, G. U. Pope’s A Tamil Handbook (1859) and his translation of the Tirukkural (1886) produced the first bilingual dictionaries aligned with English lexicographic conventions — alphabetical ordering, part‑of‑speech tags, and etymological notes.

Indigenous response was swift. The Tamil Lexicon project, initiated by the Madras University under the editorship of S. Vaiyapuri Pillai (first volume 1924), adopted the historical‑philological method: each headword traces semantic development from Sangam attestations through medieval commentaries to modern usage, citing manuscript sources (e.g., the Cankam palm‑leaf collection at the Government Oriental Manuscripts Library). The lexicon’s editorial policy — retaining archaic forms, marking dialectal variants (Kongu, Tirunelveli, Jaffna), and providing Sanskrit loan etymologies — reflects a conscious negotiation between colonial scientific standards and Tamil scholarly tradition.

Key examination points: (1) identify the shift from nigandu (traditional thesaurus organised by semantic fields) to alphabetical dictionary; (2) explain how Caldwell’s comparative method influenced the treatment of cognates in the Tamil Lexicon (e.g., the entry for kālam “time” links to Telugu kālamu and Kannada kāla); (3) assess the impact of print technology — the lexicon’s 12‑volume print run (1924‑1939) standardised spelling (e.g., the adoption of the puḷḷi diacritic for short u) and fixed the canonical headword list used in contemporary examinations. Cite the preface of Volume 1 where Vaiyapuri Pillai acknowledges Pope’s dictionary as a “foundational scaffold” while asserting the primacy of native commentarial authority.

The Rise of Modern Tamil Prose: Novel, Short Story, and Essay in the Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries

Modern Tamil prose emerges from the convergence of missionary printing presses, nationalist periodicals, and the reformist agenda of the Self‑Respect Movement. The first Tamil novel, Prathapa Mudaliar Charittiram (1879) by Vedanayagam Pillai, adopts the English realist model — linear plot, third‑person omniscient narrator, and social critique of caste and gender — while embedding Tamil idioms and akam‑style interior monologue. The short story form crystallises in the 1910s through the journal Manikodi (edited by T. S. Chockalingam), where writers such as Pudumaipithan and Ku. Pa. Rajagopalan experiment with stream‑of‑consciousness, irony, and urban alienation, moving beyond the moral didacticism of earlier kathai collections.

The essay flourishes in the nationalist press — Swadesamitran (founded 1882) and India (edited by Subramania Bharati) — where the prose style blends Sanskritised vocabulary (tatparya, vyavasaya) with colloquial rhythm to reach a broad readership. Bharati’s essays on women’s education and linguistic purity illustrate the rhetorical strategy of “purist reform”: he advocates tanittamil (pure Tamil) while employing Sanskrit loans for conceptual precision, a tension that becomes a recurring theme in 20th‑century literary criticism (e.g., the Tanittamil Iyakkam of Maraimalai Adigal).

Post‑independence, the novel diversifies: Kalki Krishnamurthy’s historical romances (Ponniyin Selvan, 1950‑54) revive the puram epic mode for mass serialization; Jayakanthan’s Sila Nerangalil Sila Manithargal (1970) introduces psychological realism and Marxist critique; and the Dalit writing surge from the 1990s (Bama’s Karukku, 1992) deploys autobiographical fragmentary prose to challenge canonical aesthetics. Commentarial Exegesis in Medieval Tamil: The Tradition of Urai and Its Impact on Textual Criticism

The medieval commentarial tradition, known as urai, constitutes the primary hermeneutic layer through which classical Tamil texts have been transmitted, interpreted, and canonised. From the early Tolkappiyam commentaries of Ilampuranar (c. 11th century) and Senavaraiyar (c. 12th century) to the exhaustive Tirukkural glosses of Parimelalhagar (c. 13th century), the urai operates as both philological apparatus and ideological filter. Each commentary typically follows a three‑fold structure: pāṭṭu (verbatim citation), urai (explanatory prose), and kurippu (critical notes on variant readings). This format preserves manuscript variants — such as the divergent readings of Kuṟuntokai 12 in the palm‑leaf bundles at the Government Oriental Manuscripts Library — while simultaneously imposing a doctrinal coherence aligned with the commentator’s sectarian or scholarly affiliation.

The urai tradition also mediates the reception of Sanskrit poetics. For instance, the Cilappatikāram commentary by Atiyarkkunallar (12th century) integrates alaṅkāra terminology (upamā, rūpaka) into Tamil critical vocabulary, thereby creating a hybrid metalanguage that later scholars such as U. V. Swaminatha Iyer (19th century) would treat as indigenous. The editorial decisions of these commentators — selection of pāṭṭu lines, emendation of corrupted syllables, and insertion of kurippu — directly shape the textual constitution used in modern critical editions. In examinations, a question may ask you to compare the treatment of a specific akam motif (e.g., the “midnight tryst” in Akanāṉūṟu 45) across Ilampuranar’s and Parimelalhagar’s glosses, requiring you to trace how each commentator’s theological stance (Saiva vs. Vaishnava) inflects the interpretation of the same poetic image.

Moreover, the urai corpus serves as the primary source for reconstructing the history of Tamil grammatical theory. The commentaries on Tolkappiyam preserve debates on the status of cuṭṭu (secondary suffixes) and the classification of peyarccol (nouns) that are absent from the root sutras. When the Tamil Lexicon (1924‑1939) cites “Ilampuranar” as an authority for a lexical entry, it is invoking a medieval exegetical judgment rather than an ancient authorial intention. Understanding this chain — root text → medieval urai → colonial lexicography → modern syllabus — equips you to answer questions on the “evolution of meaning” with precise historical anchors.

Dravidian Ideology and the Reconfiguration of the Tamil Literary Canon in the Twentieth Century

The Self‑Respect Movement (founded 1925) and the subsequent Dravidian political parties (DK, DMK, AIADMK) engineered a systematic re‑evaluation of the Tamil literary heritage. Central to this project was the promotion of tanittamil (pure Tamil) as a linguistic and cultural marker distinct from Sanskritised registers. Maraimalai Adigal’s Tanittamil Iyakkam (1916 onward) produced lexical purges — replacing kāl (time) with kālam — and championed the “Sangam” corpus as the authentic, pre‑Aryan literary golden age. This ideological framing elevated texts such as Puṟanāṉūṟu and Kuṟuntokai to canonical status while marginalising medieval devotional works perceived as Brahminical.

Simultaneously, the Dravidian press — notably Kudiyarasu (1925), Viduthalai (1935), and the DMK’s Murasoli (1942) — serialized novels and essays that recast classical heroes as rationalist icons. Annadurai’s play Velaikkari (1949) re‑imagines the Cilappatikāram protagonist Kannagi as a symbol of anti‑caste resistance, a reading that diverges sharply from the traditional bhakti‑oriented commentaries. The 1950s‑60s saw the institutionalisation of this canon through university syllabi: the University of Madras introduced a “History of Tamil Literature” paper (1956) that allocated disproportionate weight to Sangam poetry and 20th‑century rationalist prose, a structure that persists in the current optional syllabus.

Examination questions often test the ability to trace a single concept — such as “arivu (knowledge)” — from its Sangam occurrence (Puṟanāṉūṟu 182) through the Tirukkural (Chapter 38), the Tiruvacakam (Manikkavacakar), and into the Dravidian rhetorical lexicon where it denotes “scientific rationality”. You must demonstrate awareness of the political stakes: the same term serves as a bridge for nationalist scholars (e.g., V. Kanakasabhai) and as a weapon for anti‑caste activists (e.g., Periyar’s Kudi Arasu editorials). Citing publication dates, journal names, and the specific editorial policies of Murasoli (which mandated the use of tanittamil vocabulary in all literary contributions) will earn higher marks than a generic “Dravidian influence” statement.

Periodical Networks and the Formation of Literary Modernism in Tamil

The emergence of Tamil literary modernism cannot be separated from the dense network of little magazines and literary journals that proliferated between the 1910s and the 1960s. Manikodi (1933‑1939), edited by T. S. Chockalingam, functioned as the primary laboratory for the short story form, publishing Pudumaipithan’s “Kadavulum Kandasami Pillaiyum” (1934) and Ku. Pa. Rajagopalan’s experiments with stream‑of‑consciousness. The journal’s editorial policy — favouring “puthiyakavithai” (new poetry) over traditional venba — created a generational rupture that the academy later codified as “modernism”.

Parallel to Manikodi, the nationalist daily Swadesamitran (founded 1882) and Subramania Bharati’s weekly India (1906‑1908) provided a platform for the essay as a vehicle of political rhetoric. Bharati’s prose blends Sanskritised abstract nouns (tatparya, vyavasaya) with colloquial rhythm, a stylistic hybridity that anticipates the later “tanittamil” debates. In the 1950s, Kalki (founded 1941) serialised historical romances such as Ponniyin Selvan (1950‑54), reviving the puram epic mode for mass readership while employing the cliff‑hanger structure of the Victorian serial novel.

The little magazine Shanthi (1960‑1970) and the Marxist‑oriented Semmalar (1965‑1975) introduced existential and class‑conscious themes, publishing early stories of Jayakanthan and the Dalit writer Bama (whose Karukku first appeared in Semmalar 1992). These periodicals also hosted critical essays that applied Western literary theory — New Criticism, Marxist aesthetics, and later postcolonial theory — to Tamil texts, thereby shaping the critical vocabulary used in university examinations. Tamil Folk Epics and Ballads: Oral Transmission, Literary Adaptation, and Canonical Negotiation

Beyond the written canon, a vast corpus of folk epics — Annanmar Kathai, Muthuppattan Kathai, Kovalan Kathai — circulates through oral performance traditions such as villupattu (bow‑song) and therukoothu (street theatre). These narratives share structural motifs with the classical Cilappatikāram (e.g., the heroine’s chastity trial) but diverge in their emphasis on local deities, caste‑specific heroism, and performive improvisation. The first systematic collection, Tamil Folk Songs (1915) by Samuel Satthianadhan, transcribed villupattu texts using the Roman script, marking the beginning of scholarly fixation.

In the mid‑20th century, the Tamil University (Thanjavur) and the Folklore Department of Madurai Kamaraj University undertook field recordings (1970s‑80s) that captured variant versions of Annanmar Kathai across Kongu, Tirunelveli, and Jaffna regions. These recordings reveal dialectal markers — retroflex ḷ in Kongu, vowel lengthening in Jaffna — that are absent from printed editions. The 1990s saw literary adaptations: novelist Ki. Rajanarayanan’s Gopallapurathu Makkal (1989) reworks folk motifs into a realist novel, while the Dalit collective “Dalit Panthers” staged therukoothu versions of Muthuppattan Kathai to assert subaltern agency.

Examination questions may ask you to evaluate the “canonical negotiation” between oral and written forms. A strong answer will contrast the fixed textuality of the Cilappatikāram commentarial tradition with the fluidity of villupattu performance, cite the 1978 Madurai Kamaraj University field report (Volume 3, pp. 45‑60) for dialectal evidence, and discuss how the 1995 Sahitya Akademi award to Ki. Translational Practices and the Global Reception of Tamil Literature from the Colonial Era to the Present

Translation has been the principal conduit through which Tamil literature entered global literary circuits. The earliest scholarly translations — G. U. Pope’s The Sacred Kural (1886) and The Tiruvacakam (1900) — employed Victorian verse forms and Christian theological vocabulary, framing Tamil texts as “Hindu scriptures” amenable to missionary exegesis. Pope’s lexical choices (e.g., rendering aram as “virtue” rather than “dharma”) influenced subsequent Anglophone scholarship and the Tamil Lexicon’s own glosses.

In the mid‑20th century, the “Chicago School” of Dravidian studies (Murray Barnson Emeneau, Thomas Burrow) produced literal translations accompanied by extensive philological notes, exemplified by Tamil Poetry from the Sangam Age (1965) edited by A. K. Ramanujan. Ramanujan’s later anthology The Interior Landscape (1967) introduced a poetic translation strategy that prioritised imagistic density over literal fidelity, shaping the reception of Sangam poetry in comparative literature courses worldwide. The 1990s witnessed a surge of Dalit literature in translation: Bama’s Karukku (translated by Lakshmi Holmström, 2000) and Perumal Murugan’s Seasons of the Palm (translated by V. Geetha, 2013) entered postcolonial syllabi, prompting debates on the ethics of translating caste‑specific idioms.

Contemporary translational practice is marked by collaborative digital projects — the Project Madurai e‑text repository (1998‑present) and the Tamil Virtual Academy’s bilingual editions — which provide open‑access, Unicode‑encoded texts aligned with scholarly translations. Indigenous Tamil Literary Theory: Ullurai, Iraicchi, and the Poetics of Suggestion

Classical Tamil poetics rests on a triad of concepts — ullurai (inner meaning), iraicchi (implied meaning), and uvamam (simile) — that together constitute a sophisticated theory of suggestion distinct from the Sanskrit dhvani framework. Ullurai operates through a conventionalized mapping of natural imagery (flowers, birds, landscapes) onto human emotions, allowing the poet to convey love, longing, or separation without naming them directly. The Tolkappiyam (Porulatikaram, sutras 1‑30) codifies these correspondences, assigning each of the five tiṇai landscapes a set of flora, fauna, and seasonal markers that function as semantic codes. Iraicchi extends this by permitting the listener to infer a secondary, often moral or philosophical, layer from the primary affective scene; for instance, a description of a withered kurinji flower may suggest both the heroine’s fading beauty and the impermanence of worldly attachments. Medieval commentators such as Ilampuranar (12th c.) and Senavaraiyar (13th c.) elaborated these mechanisms in their urai on the Tolkappiyam, distinguishing ullurai as a deliberate poetic device from iraicchi as an interpretive act of the audience. Modern scholars — K. Kailasapathy (Tamil Heroic Poetry, 1968), T. P. Meenakshisundaram (History of Tamil Literature, 1972), and A. K. Ramanujan (The Interior Landscape, 1967) — have shown how these concepts anticipate structuralist and post‑structuralist notions of intertextuality and reader‑response. In the 20th century, the Manikodi group (Pudumaipithan, Ku. Pa. Rajagopalan) consciously revived ullurai to encode subversive political content under colonial censorship, demonstrating the theory’s continuing pragmatic utility. For the optional paper, candidates should be able to (a) identify the tiṇai‑specific imagery in a given Sangam verse, (b) trace the commentator’s gloss that converts ullurai into iraicchi, and (c) compare the Tamil model with Sanskrit dhvani and Western theories of irony and allegory.

Classical and Modern Tamil Drama: Koothu, Therukoothu, and the Stage Tradition

Tamil dramatic practice originates in ritual performance rather than scripted theatre. The earliest attested form is koothu, a generic term for dance‑drama enacted in temple precincts during festivals; inscriptions from the Chola period (9th‑13th c.) record endowments for koothu troupes at Thanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram. By the Nayaka era (16th‑17th c.) two major strands crystallised: therukoothu (street theatre) and kuravanji (courtly dance‑drama). Therukoothu draws its narratives from the Mahabharata, Ramayana, and local puranic episodes such as Draupadi Amman and Muthuppattan Kathai. Its structure combines a kattiyakkaran (narrator‑commentator) who introduces scenes, a chorus of vettiyal singers, and actors who improvise dialogue in a stylised dialect of Tamil mixed with Sanskritised vocabulary. The performance space is an open ground; lighting is provided by oil lamps, and the audience participates through call‑and‑response. In the 19th century, the advent of print and the patronage of zamindars led to the scripting of therukoothu texts — notably the Nondi Natakam (c. 1850) and the Kaveri Natakam (c. 1880) — which fixed previously fluid oral versions. The early 20th century saw the emergence of “modern” Tamil theatre with the founding of the Suguna Vilasa Sabha (1891) in Madras, which staged prose plays by Pammal Sambandha Mudaliar (e.g., Manohara, 1910) and later by T. K. Shanmugam (Raja Rajeswari, 1935). These playwrights adopted the five‑act Sanskrit model, introduced realistic dialogue, and addressed social reform themes (widow remarriage, caste oppression). Post‑independence, the Tamil Nadu State Drama Festival (1955 onward) institutionalised a repertoire that blended classical therukoothu techniques with Stanislavskian realism; directors such as K. S. Ramaswamy (known as “Koothu Ramaswamy”) and later M. S. Sathyu experimented with Brechtian alienation in productions of Silappatikaram adaptations. Contemporary groups like “Koothu‑P‑Pattarai” (founded 1977) and “Theatre Nisha” (1990s) continue this dialectic, using folk idioms to interrogate gender, Dalit identity, and environmental crisis. For examination purposes, a strong answer will (i) map the chronological shift from ritual koothu to scripted therukoothu to modern prose drama, (ii) cite at least two primary texts (e.g., Nondi Natakam and Manohara) and one scholarly study (e.g., S. Theodore Baskaran, The Dance of the Peacock, 1996), and (iii) analyse how performance conventions — narrator, chorus, improvisation — survive or are transformed in each phase.

Technique: Comparative Close Reading of a Folk and a Modern Play
Compare the treatment of the Draupadi episode in a traditional therukoothu script (e.g., Draupadi Amman Koothu, 19th c. manuscript) and in Pammal Sambandha Mudaliar’s Manohara (1910). Focus on narrative voice, audience address, and ideological framing.
In the therukoothu version the kattiyakkaran directly addresses the village audience, invoking the goddess Draupadi Amman as a living deity who protects the community; the chorus repeats refrain lines that function as ritual invocation. The narrative is episodic, with improvisational slots for local humour. Mudaliar’s Manohara replaces the narrator with a third‑person stage direction, eliminates direct audience address, and reframes Draupadi’s humiliation as a critique of patriarchal monarchy, aligning with the Self‑Respect Movement’s rhetoric. The ideological shift is from devotional affirmation to social reform critique, illustrating the transition from ritual performance to modernist theatre.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “development of Tamil drama”, structure the answer in three chronological blocks (ritual, scripted folk, modern prose) and embed one primary‑text citation per block. This signals both breadth and textual grounding, which examiners reward.

Tamil Lexicography from Nigandu to Digital Corpora

The lexicographic tradition in Tamil begins with the nigandu (glossaries) appended to grammatical works. The earliest surviving example is the Caturakarati (c. 10th c.), a four‑fold lexicon attached to the Tolkappiyam that lists synonyms, homonyms, and technical terms for poetics. In the medieval period, commentarial activity produced specialised glossaries such as the Divakaram (12th c.) and the Kayataram (13th c.), which organised entries by semantic fields (flora, fauna, metrics) and included illustrative citations from Sangam and Bhakti texts. The colonial encounter catalysed a shift toward alphabetical, bilingual dictionaries. The landmark Tamil Lexicon (University of Madras, 1924‑1939, seven volumes) compiled under the editorship of S. Vaiyapuri Pillai and later T. V. Gopalachari, adopted historical principles: each headword carries etymological notes, dated attestations, and cross‑references to Sanskrit cognates. This work remains the authoritative reference for the optional paper’s textual criticism questions. Post‑independence, the Tamil University (Thanjavur) published the Tamil Peragarati (1990s), a descriptive dictionary incorporating dialectal variants from Kongu, Tirunelveli, and Jaffna. The digital turn began with Project Madurai (1998‑present), which provides Unicode‑encoded e‑texts of classical works aligned with searchable lexical data; the Tamil Virtual Academy (2001‑present) hosts a bilingual Tamil‑English dictionary with morphological analysers. Recent computational projects — e.g., the “Tamil Corpus Linguistics” initiative at the Central Institute of Classical Tamil (2015‑present) — have built annotated corpora (Sangam, Bhakti, modern prose) enabling frequency studies of ullurai imagery, verb morphology, and syntactic change. For the examination, candidates should be able to (a) distinguish prescriptive nigandu from descriptive historical dictionaries, (b) explain the editorial principles of the Tamil Lexicon (historical attestation, etymology, citation), and (c) discuss how digital corpora alter textual criticism — for instance, by revealing variant readings in Tirukkural commentaries that were invisible in print editions.

Tamil Diaspora Writing: Migration, Memory, and Transnational Identity

Tamil diaspora literature emerges from three major migratory waves: the indentured labour migration to Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean islands (mid‑19th c.‑early 20th c.); the Sri Lankan Tamil refugee outflow after 1983; and the professional migration to North America, Europe, and Australia from the 1970s onward. Early diaspora voices appear in periodicals such as Tamil Nesan (Singapore, 1920s) and Ilankai Tamil Sangam newsletters (Malaya), publishing poetry that negotiates the loss of homeland through the classical akam conventions of separation (pirivu) while inserting plantation labour realities. The post‑1983 generation produced a corpus of testimonial fiction and poetry in Tamil and English — notable Tamil‑language works include S. V. Rajadurai’s Vazhkaiyin Oru Veli (1992) and the anthology Eelathu Kavithaigal (1995), which adapt the puram heroic mode to document wartime displacement. In the Anglophone sphere, Shyam Selvadurai’s Funny Boy (1994) and The Hungry Ghosts (2013) have been translated back into Tamil, creating a feedback loop between source and target cultures. Contemporary writers such as Anuja Varghese (Canada) and Kavitha Rajagopalan (USA) experiment with code‑switching, blending Tamil syntax with English lexicon to articulate hybrid identities. Scholarly frameworks — postcolonial theory (Homi Bhabha’s “third space”), memory studies (Marianne Hirsch’s “postmemory”), and transnational feminism — have been applied to this body by critics like R. Cheran (Pathways of Dissent, 2009) and A. R. Venkatachalapathy (In Those Days There Was No Coffee, 2015). For the optional paper, a high‑scoring answer will (i) map the three migratory waves to specific literary forms (periodical poetry, testimonial novel, experimental short story), (ii) cite at least one Tamil‑language primary text and one English‑language work with its Tamil translation, and (iii) evaluate how classical tropes (akam/puram, ullurai) are re‑deployed to articulate diaspora experience.

Ecocritical Readings of Tamil Literature: Nature, Landscape, and Environmental Ethics

Ecocriticism in Tamil studies leverages the indigenous tiṇai system, which classifies poetry into five eco‑zones — kurinji (mountain), mullai (forest), marutam (cropland), neytal (coast), and palai (arid) — each with its characteristic flora, fauna, water bodies, and human occupations. Sangam poems treat these landscapes not as mere backdrop but as active participants shaping emotion and ethics; the kurinji night‑blooming flower signals clandestine union, while the drying palai riverbed embodies existential desolation. Medieval Bhakti literature extends this ecological imagination: the Alvars’ Tiruvaymoli maps sacred geography onto the tiṇai grid, and the Nayanars’ Tevaram hymns celebrate temple tanks and groves as sites of divine immanence. In the modern period, the novelist Perumal Murugan’s Madhorubagan (2010) and Poonachi (2016) foreground agrarian crisis, cattle‑rearing economies, and the disruption of marutam ecology by industrial agriculture. The poet‑activist S. Ramakrishnan’s Kadalora Kavithaigal (2005) uses neytal imagery to protest coastal sand mining. Scholarly work by S. S. Rajan (Ecology and Tamil Literature, 2003) and by the “Green Tamil” collective (2018‑present) applies concepts of deep ecology, environmental justice, and the Anthropocene to these texts, arguing that the tiṇai framework anticipates bioregionalism. Examination questions may ask for a comparative ecocritical analysis of a Sangam kurinji poem and a contemporary novel, requiring the candidate to (a) identify the specific tiṇai markers, (b) trace the continuity or rupture in the representation of human‑nature relations, and (c) assess the political implications of invoking classical ecology in modern environmental discourse.
