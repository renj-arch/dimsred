1. Maithili Paper I — History of the Language, Movements, Authors and Works

2. History of the Language

Maithili is an Indo-Aryan language of the Bihari group, with a literary tradition going back to Vidyapati in the 14th–15th century. It is written in Devanagari.

3. Literary Periods and Movements

  • Early period (14th–16th c.): Vidyapati’s padavali and devotional poetry.
  • Medieval period (17th–19th c.): Courtly and devotional writing; Maithili drama and song.
  • Modern period (20th c.–present): Prose, fiction, drama and literary criticism.

4. Major Authors

  • Vidyapati: 14th–15th century poet; his padavali shaped Maithili and neighbouring literatures.
  • Jyotirishwar Thakur: Fourteenth-century author of Varnaratnakara, an early Maithili prose work.
  • Harimohan Jha: Modern Maithili writer and essayist.

5. Major Works

  • Padavali — Vidyapati: Devotional songs of Radha and Krishna.
  • Varnaratnakara — Jyotirishwar Thakur: Early Maithili prose work on poetics and social life.
  • Maithili folk songs — Oral tradition: Traditional songs of Mithila.
  • Modern Maithili fiction — Modern Maithili writers: Twentieth-century prose fiction.
Solved Example: Author and Period
Question: Place Vidyapati in the literary history of Maithili and assess their contribution.
Answer: Vidyapati belongs to the Early period (14th–16th c.) phase. 14th–15th century poet; his padavali shaped Maithili and neighbouring literatures. The contribution should be assessed through language, theme and form, and through influence on later writers.

Memory Tricks

  • Timeline: Early period (14th–16th c.) → Medieval period (17th–19th c.) → Modern period (20th c.–present).
  • Authors: Vidyapati, Jyotirishwar Thakur, Harimohan Jha.
  • Works: Padavali, Varnaratnakara, Maithili folk songs, Modern Maithili fiction.
Mains strategy
For Paper I, use the sequence: language origin → period → movement → author → work. Add one short quotation or native term per answer. Draw a timeline in the margin if the question asks for development. Avoid unverified dates; approximate centuries are safer.

6. Advanced Section: The Structural Pivot from Oral to Written Canon

The standard curriculum often treats the transition from oral tradition to written literature as a linear progression. However, for Paper I, you must understand the structural pivot where Maithili diverged from its Bihari siblings (Bhojpuri and Awadhi). The critical mechanism here is not just the adoption of Devanagari script, but the specific syntactic shift from the vernacular's fluid sandhi rules to a standardized literary grammar codified by Vidyapati. Unlike Hindi or Bengali which evolved through distinct colonial administrative interventions, Maithili's literary consolidation was an endogenous process driven by the Bhakti movement's need for precise theological expression.

The Mechanism of Exception: The exception lies in the treatment of the 'Mithila Bhasha' versus 'Maithili'. In the 18th century, the term was often used interchangeably with 'Bhojpuri' by outsiders. The examiner may twist this by asking about the Varnaratnakara not as a prose work, but as a prescriptive grammar that froze the language at a specific phonological stage. You must analyze how Jyotirishwar Thakur's work acted as a firewall against linguistic drift, creating a static literary standard while the spoken language continued to evolve. This is a classic case of diglossia where the high variety (literary) and low variety (colloquial) diverged significantly earlier than in other Indian languages.

Technique: Comparative Structural Analysis
Question: Compare the role of Vidyapati's 'Padavali' with the 'Varnaratnakara' in establishing Maithili as a distinct literary entity. How did their respective genres influence the standardization of the language?
Solution:
Vidyapati's Padavali established the metric and phonetic norms through the rigid structure of 'Doha' (couplet) and 'Chaurasa' (quartet), which required precise rhyme schemes that only Maithili could satisfy. It served as a high-water mark for poetic diction.

Conversely, Jyotirishwar Thakur's Varnaratnakara provided the syntactic and lexical framework. Unlike Vidyapati who worked in verse, Thakur codified prose structures. The key distinction is that Vidyapati defined the soul (rasa) of Maithili, while Thakur defined its body (grammar). Together, they created a complete linguistic ecosystem. The examiner expects you to note that without Thakur's prose codification, Maithili might have remained a purely oral devotional dialect like early Bengali or Marathi did before the 19th century.

7. Advanced Section: The Socio-Political Context of Literary Movements

Textbooks list "Modern Period" as starting in the 20th century. This is a superficial view. The real literary movement began with the Mithila Prakashini and the influence of the British educational system on the local gentry, but it was deeply intertwined with the socio-political upheavals of the 19th century. You must analyze how the 'Maithili Bhasha Samaj' (though often conflated with Hindi movements) pushed for a distinct identity separate from Awadhi.

Applied Scenario: Consider the question: "Why did Maithili literature flourish in prose during the British era while poetry remained dominant in earlier centuries?" The answer lies in the shift from devotional to civic expression. The early period was dominated by Vidyapati's Krishna-Bhakti, which was personal and mystical. The medieval period saw courtly patronage (e.g., Raja Ram Singh of Jaipur influencing local styles). The modern shift occurred because the colonial administration needed a vernacular for law and education, forcing writers like Harimohan Jha to move from metaphysical poetry to social realism and journalism.

The Examiner's Twist: The examiner may present data on print runs or book prices in the early 20th century to test if you understand that Maithili literature was never a mass-market phenomenon like Hindi. It remained an elite, intellectual pursuit. A "landmark case" is the publication of Maithili Dharani (anthologies) which marked the shift from manuscript culture to print culture. You must argue that this shift democratized access but also standardized the language, killing off dialectal variations that existed in oral folk songs.

Technique: Causal Linkage Analysis
Question: Analyze the causal link between the British educational reforms and the rise of Maithili prose fiction in the early 20th century. Provide two specific examples of authors who bridged this gap.
Solution:
The causal link is the introduction of English-medium education which created a class of bilingual intellectuals (Maithili-English) who could write fiction for both local and colonial audiences. Unlike Hindi, which had a strong nationalist movement backing it, Maithili's rise was organic but slower.

Example 1: Harimohan Jha, who wrote essays blending traditional wisdom with modern rationalism, bridged the gap by using Maithili to discuss social issues like caste and gender, moving away from purely religious themes.
Example 2: The transition from 'Bhakti' to 'Social Realism' is seen in works that began addressing the plight of the Mithila peasant. This shift was not sudden but a gradual evolution over three decades (1890–1920), contrasting with the rapid Hindi movement led by Mahatma Gandhi and others.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Maithila" vs. "Mithila" Nuance
When answering questions on history, always distinguish between the region (Mithila) and the language (Maithili). Historically, the language was often called 'Mithila Bhasha' in manuscripts. In your answer, explicitly state: "While the region is Mithila, the literary language is Maithili." This shows depth. If a question asks about 'movements', do not just list names; explain the ideological shift. For instance, the movement from 'Padavali' (devotional) to 'Natak' (drama) represents a shift from individual spirituality to social interaction.

8. Advanced Section: Critical Evaluation of Major Works through Comparative Literature

The standard list includes 'Padavali', 'Varnaratnakara', and 'Modern Fiction'. To reach the next level, you must evaluate these not as isolated works but as part of a comparative literature framework. For instance, compare Vidyapati's 'Padavali' with Mirabai's poetry in Hindi or Chandidas in Bengali. The key differentiator is the linguistic purity and the use of specific Maithili vocabulary (e.g., words derived from Sanskrit but pronounced differently).

Landmark Data/Concept: A crucial concept is the metric system. Vidyapati used a unique meter called 'Doha' which was distinct from the Hindi 'Doha'. The examiner may ask you to analyze the rhyme scheme. In Maithili, the rhyme often falls on the last syllable of the line, whereas in Hindi it might fall earlier. This technical detail proves your command over the subject.

The Examiner's Twist: You might be asked: "Why is 'Varnaratnakara' considered a prose work despite its poetic nature?" The answer requires analyzing the prose-poetry continuum in medieval India. Jyotirishwar Thakur wrote in a style that blended prose narration with poetic couplets, a hybrid form unique to Maithili literature of that era. This is an exception to the rule where poetry and prose were strictly separated.

Technique: Textual Criticism Approach
Question: Evaluate 'Varnaratnakara' as a bridge between oral tradition and written literature. How does it differ from Vidyapati's works in terms of audience and purpose?
Solution:
Varnaratnakara serves as the bridge because it codified rules for composition, effectively turning oral improvisation into a written discipline. Its audience was the courtly elite and aspiring poets, unlike Vidyapati's 'Padavali' which was devotional and aimed at the common devotee.

Difference in Purpose: Vidyapati's work was theocentric (God-centered), focusing on the love of Radha-Krishna. Thakur's work was anthropocentric (human-centered), focusing on social conduct, ethics, and poetics. This shift from divine love to human ethics marks the transition from medieval to early modern sensibilities in Maithili literature.
  • Trap 1: Conflating Regions with Languages. Do not confuse 'Mithila' (the geographical region) with 'Maithili' (the language). While they overlap, the language exists in pockets outside Mithila, and Mithila has dialects that are not Maithili. Always specify "Maithili Bhasha" when discussing literature.
  • Trap 2: Ignoring the 'Oral' Component. Textbooks often skip the oral tradition. In your answer, always mention that early Maithili literature was transmitted orally before being written down by Vidyapati and Thakur. This adds depth to your historical analysis.
  • Trap 3: Overlooking the 'British' Influence. Do not attribute all modern developments solely to indigenous movements. Acknowledge that British education and print culture played a role in standardizing Maithili, similar to how it affected Hindi and Bengali, but note that Maithili's path was more gradual.
  • Trap 4: Misidentifying the 'Modern' Period. Do not assume the modern period starts only after independence. The modern phase of Maithili literature began in the late 19th century with the emergence of prose fiction and journalism, well before 1947.

9. The Pashupati Seal and the Archaeological Roots of Maithili Identity

To understand the genesis of Maithili literature, one must first look beyond the written manuscripts to the material culture that predates them by centuries. The most significant archaeological anchor for Maithili identity is the Pashupati Seal, discovered in 1926 at Vaishali (Muzaffarpur district). While often associated with the Indus Valley Civilization, its specific location and iconography provide a critical link between ancient Mithila and the later literary tradition. The seal depicts a seated figure identified as Pashupati (Lord of Animals), surrounded by animals such as elephants, tigers, rhinos, and bulls. This iconography is not merely religious; it establishes a cultural continuity where the deity worshipped in the oral traditions of the peasantry—often associated with nature and fertility—finds expression in high art.

The linguistic implication of this seal is profound. The script on the seal belongs to the Brahmi family, specifically a variant that shows affinities with later Maithili epigraphy. While the exact phonetic value of every character remains a subject of scholarly debate, the structural evolution from these early Brahmi inscriptions to the Brihat Charit and Vidyapati's Padavali represents a linear progression of scriptural development. The seal proves that the region was not culturally isolated but part of a wider north-eastern Indian cultural sphere where Sanskritized vernaculars were emerging. For an examiner, this artifact serves as proof that Maithili literature did not emerge in a vacuum; it grew out of a soil where religious iconography and local dialects coexisted.

The transition from the seal's era to the medieval period is marked by the shift from iconographic worship (worshipping images) to linguistic devotion. In early Mithila, the "seal" was a physical object of power. In Vidyapati's time, the "seal" became the written word itself. The seal's animals represented the natural world; Vidyapati's poetry represented the emotional world of Radha-Krishna. This shift from stone to paper is the first major pivot in Maithili history. It signifies the moment when the oral tradition, previously preserved by bards and priests using memory aids (like the seal's imagery), was codified into a written canon. The Pashupati Seal thus acts as the archaeological "zero point" for understanding how Maithili moved from being a spoken dialect of ritual to a literary language capable of expressing complex philosophical ideas.

Furthermore, the location of the seal at Vaishali is crucial. Vaishali was not just a capital; it was a center of learning where Buddhist and Jain traditions flourished alongside Hindu sects. This pluralistic environment fostered a literature that could absorb influences from different faiths while maintaining a distinct local flavor. The Maithili language, unlike Hindi or Bengali which were heavily influenced by Persian later on, retained a strong Sanskrit base but with unique phonetic features derived from this ancient, pre-Islamic substrate. The seal's discovery validates the claim that Mithila was a cradle of civilization where language and culture evolved in tandem.

Examiner's Trap: The Seal vs. The Manuscript
Question: "Does the Pashupati Seal prove that Maithili literature existed in the Indus Valley period?"
Solution: No. This is a classic trap. The seal proves the existence of a cultural and linguistic continuity (Brahmi script, local deities) but does not prove that a full-fledged literature (poetry, prose fiction) existed then. Literature requires a canon and an audience. The seal is an artifact of state/religious power; literature is the product of courtly or devotional patronage. Correct answer: The seal establishes the archaeological root of Maithili identity, while Vidyapati's works establish the literary canon. Do not conflate material culture with literary output.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Oral-to-Text" Bridge
When asked about the origins of Maithili literature, never start with Vidyapati (14th century). Always create a bridge. Mention the oral tradition, the Pashupati Seal (archaeological root), and then transition to the medieval codification. Use the phrase: "The transition from the iconographic seal to the poetic padavali represents the shift from visual memory aids to textual canon." This shows you understand that literature is not just writing; it is a cultural evolution.

10. The Metric Revolution: Doha and the Rhythmic Distinctiveness of Maithili

In the realm of prosody (Chandas), Maithili literature possesses a unique metric system that distinguishes it from its Hindi and Bengali counterparts. The most celebrated form is the Doha (couplet). While Hindi Doha often follows the Shloka meter (two lines of 32 syllables each, with specific caesura), Maithili Doha is distinct in its rhythmic structure and rhyme scheme. The standard Maithili Doha typically consists of two lines where the rhyme falls on the last syllable of the line (shuddha rhyme). This technical detail is crucial for textual criticism.

The metric system of Maithili literature also includes the Doha-Vrittanta and the Chandavali. Vidyapati's mastery lay in his ability to use these meters to express deep emotion without breaking the rhythmic flow. The "metric revolution" refers to the standardization of these forms during the medieval period, moving away from the irregular oral improvisations of earlier times. This standardization allowed for the creation of a "literary language" that could be read aloud with a specific cadence, distinguishing Maithili pronunciation from Hindi.

Another key metric is the Gita-Vritt, which was influenced by Sanskrit but adapted to local phonology. The rhyme scheme in Maithili often involves a falling tone at the end of the line, creating a musical quality that differs from the rising tones of Hindi poetry. This acoustic difference is why Maithili literature sounds distinct even when read in Devanagari script. The examiner may ask you to analyze a sample couplet and identify the meter. You must be able to count the syllables and identify the caesura point.

The metric system also evolved to include Avasthapaniya (narrative verses) and Varnan (descriptive verses). These forms were used in courtly poetry to praise kings or describe nature. The shift from purely devotional meters to narrative meters marks the transition from medieval spirituality to early modern secularism. Vidyapati's later works show this evolution, where he uses complex meters to describe social scenes, not just divine love.

Examiner's Trap: The "Doha" Confusion
Question: "Compare the rhyme scheme of Hindi Doha and Maithili Doha."
Solution: Do not say they are the same. In Hindi Doha, the rhyme often falls on the penultimate syllable or follows a specific Shloka pattern (32+32).
