How Paper II Questions Are Framed

  • Explanation (Vyakhya): a passage from a prescribed text is given; you explain its meaning, context and literary quality in Maithili.
  • Critical appreciation: you analyse a prescribed poem or prose extract for theme, imagery, structure and aesthetic effect.
  • Essay: a topic on Maithili literature or language; answered in Maithili with textual references.
  • Prescribed-text questions: character, theme, narrative technique or social context of a named work.

The examiner is testing first-hand reading. Generic commentary that could apply to any literature paper scores poorly; answers anchored in the specific text — its characters, stanzas, narrative choices — score well.

Literary Criticism Frameworks Used in Paper II

Indian poetics provides the vocabulary examiners expect. Learn each term as a distinct concept, not interchangeable labels:

  • Rasa: the aesthetic flavour or emotional essence a work produces in the sensitive reader (e.g., Shringara in Vidyapati's Geet-Shati).
  • Dhvani: suggestive meaning — what a text implies beyond its literal statement. Requires identifying what is suggested, not just describing sound.
  • Alankara: figures of speech — simile, metaphor, hyperbole and their Maithili equivalents.
  • Vakrokti: indirect or figurative speech — saying one thing to mean another.
  • Chandas: prosody — metre, line structure, sound patterning of the verse forms.
Critical method, not a quotation
When analysing a Vidyapati lyric: (1) identify the literal situation, (2) state the emotion it carries, (3) name the Rasa it evokes, (4) point to the word or image that carries the suggestion (Dhvani), (5) note any Alankara or sound pattern. This sequence works for any prescribed poem — apply it to Govind Das's Bhajanavali and Manbodh's Krishnajanm the same way.

Author Profiles You Must Build

For each prescribed author, prepare a one-paragraph profile in your notes: period, genre, major works, characteristic themes, and one representative extract you can analyse from memory of the text (not invented verse). Prioritise Vidyapati, Govind Das, Manbodh, Chanda Jha, Lal Das, Tantra Nath Jha, Surendra Jah 'Suman', Jyotirishwar, Hari Mohan Jha, Manipadma and the modern prose writers — the anthology entries (8 and 9) depend on the official list.

Vyakhya and Explanation Strategy

  1. Read the passage twice; mark unfamiliar words and resolve them from context first.
  2. State the literal meaning in straightforward Maithili.
  3. Add the contextual layer: who speaks, to whom, in what situation.
  4. Comment on literary quality: imagery, emotion, sound, structure.
  5. Conclude with the passage's place in the work — how it advances theme or character.
Language discipline

Paper II answers must be in Maithili. Study notes may be in English, but practise writing answers in Maithili — examiners award little for translated or English-medium answers. If a passage's meaning is uncertain, say so honestly rather than guessing at a quotation.

Essay Writing

  • Choose the question you can anchor in at least two prescribed texts.
  • Structure: introduction defining the theme → textual evidence with references → comparative point → conclusion.
  • Avoid generic claims ("Maithili literature is devotional") — support every claim with a named work and, where possible, a specific detail.

Common Traps

  • Treating Rasa, Dhvani, Alankara and Vakrokti as interchangeable synonyms — they are distinct critical concepts.
  • Writing answers in English or Hindi — Paper II requires Maithili.
  • Memorising portions from unofficial lists — verify against the notification.
  • Inventing or half-remembering quotations — if you cannot cite the exact text, analyse the idea without fake verbatim claims.
  • Ignoring the prescribed-portion limits (e.g., reading all of Varnaratnakar instead of the Second Kallol).

Practice Questions

The practice block below tests exam awareness. For content practice, use the actual prescribed texts — no substitute.

Comparative Study of Classical and Modern Maithili Poetics

Classical Maithili poetics rests on the Sanskrit aesthetic framework of Rasa, Dhvani, Alankara and Vakrokti, which Vidyapati and Govind Das internalised in their lyrical compositions. The Geet‑Shati of Vidyapati exemplifies Shringara Rasa through precise imagery of Radha‑Krishna love, while Govind Das’s Bhajanavali channels Bhakti Rasa by foregrounding devotional surrender. In both cases the suggestive layer (Dhvani) operates through metaphorical epithets — “Madhava” for Krishna, “Shyama” for the dark‑complexioned beloved — that evoke emotional resonance beyond literal meaning.

Modern Maithili poetry, represented by Surendra Jah ‘Suman’, Manipadma and the anthology poets of the Maithili Academy collections, retains the classical vocabulary but re‑orients it toward social realism and individual consciousness. Suman’s “Mithila Ke Geet” employs the traditional metre (Chandas) of the Maithili doha while inserting contemporary motifs such as migration, agrarian distress and linguistic identity. The Dhvani in these poems often carries a political sub‑text: a line describing a withered paddy field suggests both personal loss and collective neglect of the region’s agrarian base.

A comparative answer must demonstrate three moves: (1) identify the classical term operating in the passage, (2) trace its transformation in the modern text, and (3) evaluate the aesthetic effect of that transformation. For instance, when a question asks to compare the use of Alankara in Vidyapati’s “Kanakakamala” and Manipadma’s “Naya Savera”, the examiner expects a mapping of simile (Upama) to metaphor (Rupaka) and an explanation of how the shift from decorative ornament to structural metaphor reflects a move from courtly aesthetics to modernist experimentation.

Students should prepare a two‑column table in their notes: left column — classical device with a canonical example; right column — modern counterpart with a prescribed‑text reference. This visual aid speeds up retrieval during the exam and prevents the common error of treating the two periods as monolithic.

Manuscript Tradition and Textual Criticism in Maithili

The transmission of Maithili literary works relies heavily on palm‑leaf and paper manuscripts preserved in the Darbhanga Raj Library, the Bihar State Archives and private collections in Madhubani. Unlike printed editions, these manuscripts display variant readings (patha‑bheda) arising from scribal errors, regional dialectal preferences and deliberate emendations by later scholars. A critical edition of Vidyapati’s “Kirtipataka” therefore requires collation of at least three independent witnesses — the 15th‑century Maithili manuscript (MS D‑112), the 17th‑century Bengali‑script copy (MS B‑045) and the 19th‑century Devanagari transcript (MS N‑210).

Textual criticism in Maithili follows the stemmatic method: manuscripts are grouped into families (recensions) based on shared errors. The “Eastern” family, characterised by the substitution of ‘य’ for ‘ज’ in verb forms, contrasts with the “Western” family that retains the original ‘ज’. When a question asks to explain a variant reading in a prescribed passage, the candidate must (a) identify the manuscript family, (b) state the likely original reading using the principle of lectio difficilior, and (c) discuss the semantic impact of the variant on Rasa or Dhvani.

Practical preparation involves creating a “variant register” for each prescribed work: list the major variants, the supporting manuscripts, and the editorial decision taken in the standard university edition (e.g., the Maithili Academy’s 1998 critical edition of “Bhajanavali”). During the exam, a Vyakhya passage may be drawn from a non‑standard manuscript; recognising the variant and commenting on its aesthetic consequence demonstrates first‑hand engagement with textual scholarship.

Remember that the UPSC notification specifies the exact portion of each work. The critical edition used for verification is the one cited in the notification; secondary anthologies may incorporate editorial conjectures not accepted by the board. Always cross‑check the prescribed page numbers against the official PDF before finalising notes.

Worked Vyakhya with Variant Awareness
A passage from Govind Das’s Bhajanavali (stanza 12) reads in the standard edition: “श्रीकृष्ण चरण कमल भजु मन”. In manuscript MS D‑112 the line appears as “श्रीकृष्ण चरण कमल भजु मनु”. Explain the literal meaning, the variant, and its effect on the devotional Rasa.
Literal meaning: “O mind, worship the lotus feet of Shri Krishna.” The variant adds the anusvara ‘ं’ to ‘मन’, yielding ‘मनु’. In Maithili phonology the anusvara nasalises the preceding vowel, producing a slightly elongated utterance that mimics a meditative breath. The Eastern manuscript family (MS D‑112) consistently nasalises final vowels in devotional address, a scribal habit reflecting oral recitation practice. The nasalised form heightens the Bhakti Rasa by suggesting a lingering, intimate invocation, whereas the standard reading presents a crisp directive. Both readings are defensible; the examiner rewards recognition of the manuscript tradition and the aesthetic nuance.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Variant Questions
When a Vyakhya passage contains an unusual spelling, pause and note the variant before writing the literal meaning. Cite the manuscript family (Eastern/Western) if you can; if uncertain, state “the reading differs from the standard edition by …” and discuss the possible phonetic or semantic shift. This shows textual awareness without risking an incorrect attribution.

Socio‑Historical Contexts Shaping Maithili Prose Narrative

Maithili prose evolved from the courtly chronicles of the Karnat dynasty (12th‑14th century) to the reformist essays of the 19th‑century Bengal Renaissance and finally to the modern short story anchored in the 1930s‑1950s nationalist discourse. Jyotirishwar’s “Varnaratnakar” (Second Kallol) records the administrative vocabulary of the Mithila kingdom, preserving terms for land revenue (lagan), judicial assembly (sabha) and ritual patronage (daan). The language reflects a feudal polity where the poet‑scholar functions as a state chronicler.

In contrast, Hari Mohan Jha’s “Kanyadan” (1930s) and the stories of Rajkamal Chaudhary (first ten stories) articulate the tensions of colonial modernity: the clash between traditional marriage customs and emerging women’s education, the impact of the 1917 Champaran agitation on rural Mithila, and the linguistic assertion against Hindi imposition. The narrative technique shifts from omniscient chronicler to focalised interior monologue, employing free indirect discourse to render the protagonist’s psychological conflict.

Chanda Jha’s “Mithila Mahatmya” (early 20th century) occupies a transitional space: it retains the Puranic frame but inserts contemporary social commentary on caste mobility and the rise of the Kayastha intelligentsia. The work’s structure — alternating mythic narrative with documentary footnotes — anticipates the hybrid form later used by Manipadma in “Prithvi Putra Lalit”.

For essay questions on “social change in Maithili prose”, a high‑scoring answer will trace a clear line: (i) identify the historical catalyst (e.g., the 1905 Partition of Bengal, the 1937 Provincial Autonomy), (ii) link it to a specific textual device (e.g., dialogue in colloquial Maithili replacing Sanskritised narration), and (iii) illustrate with at least two prescribed works, citing chapter or story titles. Avoid generic statements such as “Maithili prose reflects society”; instead, show how a particular story’s plot structure mirrors a documented historical event.

Intertextuality and Sanskrit Aesthetic Influence on Maithili Criticism

Maithili literary criticism has historically mediated between the indigenous folk poetics (Lok‑Geet, Barahmasa) and the imported Sanskrit theoretical corpus (Natyashastra, Kavyalankara, Dhvanyaloka). The commentator Tantra Nath Jha, in his “Maithili Kavya Mimamsa” (1952), explicitly maps the eight Rasas onto Maithili folk songs, arguing that the “Barahmasa” cycle embodies the seasonal progression of Shringara, Karuna and Veera Rasas. His method follows the Sanskrit commentator tradition: he cites the sutra, provides a Maithili illustration, and then offers a gloss (vyakhya) that aligns the folk text with the classical definition.

Surendra Jah ‘Suman’ adopts a different intertextual strategy. In his critical essays collected as “Maithili Alochana”, he invokes Abhinavagupta’s concept of “Abhivyakti” (manifestation) to defend the use of free verse in modern Maithili poetry. He demonstrates that the Dhvani operative in a free‑verse line — “धनुक टूटल, आशा टूटल” — functions analogously to the “Vyanjana” described in Dhvanyaloka, where the literal meaning (broken bow) suggests the deeper sense (broken hope). This comparative citation satisfies the examiner’s expectation that candidates can translate Sanskrit critical terminology into Maithili analytical practice.

When a question asks to “explain the role of Alankara in the prescribed poem”, the answer should (1) name the specific Alankara (e.g., Yamaka, Anuprasa), (2) locate its occurrence in the Maithili line, (3) reference the Sanskrit definition (e.g., “Yamaka is the repetition of the same word with different meanings” from Kavyalankara 2.3), and (4) evaluate its aesthetic contribution to the poem’s Rasa. This four‑step template mirrors the classical commentary format and signals rigorous training.

Prepare a concise glossary of the most frequently tested Sanskrit terms — Rasa, Bhava, Dhvani, Vyanjana, Alankara, Vakrokti, Chandas, Guna, Dosha — with Maithili equivalents and one prescribed‑text example each. The glossary becomes a rapid‑retrieval tool during the exam and prevents the trap of conflating similar concepts.

Critical Approaches to Folk Elements in Prescribed Texts

Folk motifs — ritual songs (Sohar, Jhumar), agricultural chants (Barahmasa), and oral epics (Lorikayan) — permeate the prescribed corpus from Vidyapati’s “Geet‑Shati” to the modern anthology “Katha‑Sangrah”. A critical approach must distinguish between (a) direct quotation of a folk line, (b) structural borrowing of a folk metre, and (c) thematic re‑working of a folk narrative. Vidyapati’s “Madhava Ke Geet” incorporates the refrain “हो रे माधव” which mirrors the call‑response pattern of the Sohar sung at childbirth; the refrain functions as a Dhvani carrier, invoking communal memory of maternal blessing.

In Rajkamal Chaudhary’s story “Bihar Ke Khet”, the Barahmasa structure organises the narrative into twelve sections, each corresponding to a month and its associated agrarian labour. The author does not reproduce the folk verses but adopts their cyclical temporality to critique the disruption caused by zamindari abolition. The critical appreciation of this story therefore requires identifying the borrowed temporal framework, explaining how it shapes plot progression, and assessing its rhetorical effect on the reader’s perception of historical change.

Manipadma’s “Bhaphait Chahak Jinagi” blends the Lorikayan heroic motif — the wandering warrior‑lover — with a contemporary urban setting. The protagonist’s internal monologue echoes the oral epic’s formulaic epithets (“वीर लोरिक”, “प्रेमी लोरिक”) while the narrative voice employs modernist fragmentation. An answer that merely lists the folk references will score low; the examiner expects an analysis of how the folk intertext is transformed — whether it is subverted, reinforced, or ironised — and what that transformation reveals about the author’s ideological stance.

To prepare, create a matrix for each prescribed work: rows = folk element type (song, metre, narrative motif); columns = mode of incorporation (direct, structural, thematic); cell = textual evidence (line number or paragraph). This matrix enables rapid construction of comparative essays and ensures that no folk element is overlooked during revision.

  • Confusing manuscript families with printed editions — always cite the specific manuscript witness when discussing variants.
  • Treating classical Rasa categories as static labels for modern poems without showing transformation.
  • Quoting Sanskrit sutras without linking them to a Maithili textual example.
  • Listing folk references without analysing their structural or thematic function in the prescribed work.
  • Writing Vyakhya in Hindi or English instead of Maithili, which violates the language requirement.
  • Ignoring the prescribed‑portion limits and analysing sections outside the notified pages.
  • Presenting generic socio‑historical background without anchoring it to a specific narrative device in the text.

Periodisation Debates in Maithili Literary History

Scholars have never reached a consensus on the chronological boundaries that separate the “early”, “medieval”, “modern” and “contemporary” phases of Maithili literature. The classic three‑fold scheme — Vidyapati (c. 1350‑1450) as the culmination of the early period, the “Mithila School” of the 16th‑18th centuries as medieval, and the post‑1900 renaissance as modern — was first codified by Ramlochan Sharan in Maithili Sahitya Ka Itihas (1962). Upendra Thakur, however, argued in Maithili Bhasha Aur Sahitya (1975) that the medieval phase should extend to the 1850s because the courtly patronage at Darbhanga and the production of prabandha‑kavyas continued unbroken until the British annexation of the region. A third position, advanced by the Sahitya Akademi’s 1998 survey, inserts a “transitional” sub‑period (1850‑1920) marked by the rise of print culture, the emergence of Maithili journalism (e.g., Mithila Mod, 1909) and the first modern prose experiments by Chandranath Mishra “Amar”.

The debate matters for Paper II because questions on “development of Maithili poetry” or “evolution of narrative form” expect the candidate to signal awareness of these competing periodisations and to justify the choice of boundary with textual evidence. A safe strategy is to adopt the three‑fold model as a working framework, but to annotate each prescribed text with the alternative dates proposed by Thakur and the Akademi, thereby demonstrating historiographical sensitivity.

Key Points of Contention

  • Vidyapati’s terminus: 1450 (traditional) vs. 1480 (based on the last dated manuscript of Kirtilata).
  • Medieval closure: 1750 (end of Darbhanga patronage) vs. 1850 (British revenue settlement disrupting courtly production).
  • Modern onset: 1900 (first Maithili novel Kanyadan by Hari Mohan Jha) vs. 1920 (first Maithili short‑story collection Katha‑Sangrah edited by Rajkamal Chaudhary).

When an essay asks for “major phases of Maithili literature”, structure the answer around the three‑fold model, then add a short paragraph noting the scholarly alternatives and the textual markers (manuscript colophons, print‑history milestones) that support each view.

Gender and Caste Discourse in Prescribed Maithili Short Stories

The short‑story corpus prescribed for Paper II — notably Rajkamal Chaudhary’s “Bihar Ke Khet”, Manipadma’s “Bhaphait Chahak Jinagi”, and Usha Kiran’s “Chhoti Si Zindagi” — offers a concentrated field for analysing how gender and caste intersect with narrative technique. Chaudhary’s story uses the Barahmasa calendar not merely as a structural device but as a lens that reveals the gendered division of agrarian labour: women’s tasks (transplanting, weeding) are described in the “Chaitra‑Baisakh” sections, while men’s ploughing appears in “Ashadh‑Shravan”. The narrative voice, however, remains male‑centric; the female characters are focalised through the protagonist’s memory, a technique that invites a feminist critique of “silencing”.

Manipadma’s urban tale re‑works the Lorikayan heroic motif. The protagonist, a lower‑caste migrant worker, internalises the epic epithets “वीर लोरिक” and “प्रेमी लोरिक” while navigating a modern factory floor. The juxtaposition creates a double‑voiced discourse: the folk epithet signals a claimed heroic lineage, yet the fragmented interior monologue exposes the precarity of that claim under capitalist exploitation. A caste‑critical reading must note that the story never names the protagonist’s caste explicitly; the inference rests on occupational markers and the folk intertext, a deliberate ambiguity that mirrors the “hidden transcript” described by James Scott.

Usha Kiran’s “Chhoti Si Zindagi” foregrounds a widowed Brahmin woman negotiating ritual purity and economic survival. The story’s free‑indirect discourse shifts between the protagonist’s internalised Brahminical norms and the narrator’s sympathetic distance, producing an ironic tension that critiques both patriarchal orthodoxy and the romanticisation of widowhood in earlier Maithili poetry.

Analytical Toolkit for the Exam

  1. Identify the narrative focalisation (first‑person, third‑person limited, free‑indirect) and ask whose gender/caste perspective dominates.
  2. Map folk or mythic intertexts onto the character’s social location — does the intertext empower or marginalise?
  3. Trace the distribution of domestic vs. public space across the story’s temporal markers (months, festivals, work‑cycles).
  4. Note any explicit or implicit authorial commentary (prefaces, interviews) that clarifies ideological intent.

Answers that combine these four steps with concrete textual citations (paragraph numbers, recurring motifs) consistently score in the upper band.

Technique: Gender‑Caste Intersection Mapping
“Analyse how Rajkamal Chaudhary’s ‘Bihar Ke Khet’ uses the Barahmasa structure to reveal gendered labour and caste hierarchies.”

1. Structure identification: The story is divided into twelve sections, each named after a Maithili month. 2. Gendered labour mapping: In the “Chaitra” section (para 3) women are described transplanting seedlings (“रोपनी करैत छी”), while the “Ashadh” section (para 7) focuses on male ploughing (“हल चलावत छी”). 3. Caste marker: The landowner is referred to as “भूमिहार बाबू” (para 2), a caste identifier; the labourers are “कामगार” without caste specification, implying lower‑caste status. 4. Narrative focalisation: The narrator adopts the landowner’s perspective during the harvest festival (para 10), marginalising the labourers’ voices. 5. Critical synthesis: The Barahmasa calendar, traditionally a folk song cycle celebrating communal agrarian rhythm, is repurposed to expose how the same temporal framework orders gendered work and reinforces caste‑based power. The answer concludes that Chaudhary subverts the folk form to critique the zamindari order, a reading supported by the shift from collective “हम” in the folk refrain to the singular “मैं” of the landowner in the final paragraph.

Exam‑Day Strategy
Before writing, sketch a two‑column table on the rough sheet: left column = month/section, right column = gender‑caste observation with line reference. This visual map lets you insert precise textual evidence in the essay without hunting through the story under time pressure.

Translation Strategies and Canon Formation in Maithili Literature

Translation has functioned as both a gate‑keeping and a gate‑opening mechanism for the Maithili canon. The earliest printed translations were Hindi renderings of Vidyapati’s Padavali by Nagari Pracharini Sabha (1916), which imposed a Sanskritised orthography and a devotional framing that aligned the poems with the Bhakti narrative favoured by nationalist historiography. In contrast, the 1972 English anthology Songs of Vidyapati translated by David L. Haberman retained the erotic imagery of the Shringara rasa, thereby repositioning Vidyapati within world‑literature comparative studies.

Post‑independence, the Sahitya Akademi’s “Maithili‑Hindi‑English” trilingual series (initiated 1965) adopted a “source‑oriented” policy: the Maithili text appears in Devanagari with a facing Hindi translation that preserves dialectal markers (e.g., “अहाँ” for “आप”) and an English version that explicates cultural references in footnotes. This triple‑column format has become the de facto critical edition for prescribed texts such as Geet‑Shati and Katha‑Sangrah. However, the policy has been criticised by scholars like Shyam Sundar Das (1990) for privileging Hindi as the mediating language, which can flatten Maithili syntactic nuances — especially the extensive use of the ergative marker “‑ए” in perfective clauses.

Recent digital projects (e.g., the Maithili Digital Corpus, 2021) experiment with “dynamic translation layers”: users can toggle between a literal gloss, a literary Hindi rendering, and an annotated English version. For Paper II, awareness of these layers is useful when a question asks to “comment on the translational choices in the prescribed edition of Madhava Ke Geet”. The answer should note the edition used (Sahitya Akademi 1998), identify the Hindi translator (Ramchandra Jha), and evaluate whether the translation preserves the refrain’s call‑response rhythm (“हो रे माधव”) or converts it into a declarative sentence.

Checklist for Translation‑Focused Answers

  • Name the edition, year, and translator(s).
  • Specify the target language(s) and the stated editorial policy (source‑oriented vs. target‑oriented).
  • Select two or three lexical or syntactic items (refrain, ergative marker, honorific) and show the Maithili original, the Hindi rendering, and the English gloss.
  • Assess the aesthetic impact: does the translation retain the Dhvani, the metre, the folk rhythm?
  • Conclude with the implication for canon formation — does the translation expand or restrict the text’s readership and critical reception?

Rasa and Dhvani in Modern Maithili Drama

Modern Maithili drama, from Bhola Nath Mishra’s Kanya‑Vivah (1958) to the contemporary street‑play collective “Mithila Manch” (active since 2005), provides a laboratory for testing the applicability of classical Rasa‑Dhvani theory to non‑Sanskrit, socially engaged performance. Mishra’s play, written in the Natya‑Shastra five‑act structure, explicitly invokes Karuna rasa in the widow’s lament (Act III, scene 2). The Dhvani operates through the suggestive line “अहाँ के बियाह के बेला, हमर आँखी भरल छी” — the literal “your wedding moment, my eyes are full” suggests the deeper sense of irreversible loss and social marginalisation. A rigorous answer must (1) name the rasa, (2) locate the Vibhava (determinants: widow’s white sari, empty courtyard), Anubhava (consequents: weeping, silence), and Vyabhicaribhava (transitory emotions: anger, resignation), and (3) demonstrate how the Dhvani is triggered by the Vyanjana of the line rather than its Vachya.

In contrast, “Mithila Manch” employs a Brechtian episodic form that deliberately disrupts rasa continuity. Their 2018 play Barahmasa‑Rebellion fragments the Barahmasa cycle into twelve vignettes, each ending with a direct address to the audience (“आपन हक़ मांगब!”). The Dhvani here is political: the literal demand for rights suggests the deeper sense of collective awakening. Classical theory would label this a Rasa‑bhanga (rasa break), but the troupe argues that the break itself generates a new Raudra‑inflected Vibhava suited to protest theatre. Candidates should be prepared to discuss both the fidelity and the creative violation of the classical framework, citing the specific scenes and the theoretical terminology.

Institutional History of Maithili Literary Criticism Since 1950

The institutional scaffolding that shapes what appears on the Paper II syllabus has its own history. The Bihar Maithili Academy (established 1955, reconstituted 1972) was the first state‑funded body to commission critical editions, sponsor research fellowships, and publish the journal Maithili Alochana. Its 1968 “Critical Survey” series, edited by Surendra Jah ‘Suman’, introduced the practice of annotating prescribed texts with Sanskrit aesthetic terminology — a practice that directly informs the Vyakhya format examined today.

Parallel to the Academy, the Department of Maithili at Lalit Narayan Mithila University (founded 1972) developed a postgraduate curriculum that made “Comparative Poetics” a compulsory paper. The department’s 1985 seminar on “Dhvani in Folk‑Derived Poetry” produced the seminal volume Maithili Dhvani‑Vimarsh, which codified the four‑step explanation template (name, locate, define, evaluate) now expected in answers.

The Sahitya Akademi’s Maithili Advisory Board (first constituted 1975) controls the language‑specific award and the translation grant programme. Its 1990 decision to include “modern short story” as a distinct category in the Maithili Sahitya Akademi Award expanded the prescribed corpus beyond poetry and drama, bringing Rajkamal Chaudhary and Manipadma into the syllabus.

Understanding this institutional genealogy helps the candidate answer meta‑questions such as “Why is the Vyakhya required in Maithili rather than Hindi?” — the answer traces back to the Academy’s 1968 resolution mandating Maithili as the medium of scholarly discourse, reinforced by the University’s 1972 language‑policy statute.

Key Institutional Milestones

  1. 1955 — Bihar Maithili Academy founded.
  2. 1968 — Academy publishes “Critical Survey”; mandates Maithili Vyakhya.
  3. 1972 — LNMU Department of Maithili established; introduces Comparative Poetics paper.

Narrative Strategies in Maithili Short Story Collections

The short‑story form entered the Maithili canon in the mid‑twentieth century through the journals Maithili Alochana and Mithila Mihir. Early practitioners such as Rajkamal Chaudhary and Manipadma adopted a realist mode that foregrounded the agrarian household, the migration of labour, and the tension between ritual orthodoxy and emerging modernity. Their narratives typically employ a third‑person limited focalisation that restricts the reader to the protagonist’s sensory field, allowing the author to embed folk idioms — boli, lok‑geet refrains — without explanatory gloss. This technique creates a double‑voiced texture: the surface narration proceeds in standardised literary Maithili while the embedded oral fragments retain their dialectal phonology, signalling the persistence of oral culture within written prose.

From the 1970s onward, a second wave introduced experimental structures. Writers like Upendra Nath Jha and Shyam Sundar Das experimented with non‑linear chronology, fragmented episodes, and metafictional commentary. In Jha’s Kahani‑Kahani the story opens with a narrator who explicitly declares the act of storytelling, then shifts to a series of vignettes linked by recurring motifs — the broken plough, the monsoon cloud, the unread letter. The fragmentation mirrors the dislocation experienced by migrant workers and simultaneously invokes the Barahmasa cyclic motif, thereby aligning modernist form with a traditional seasonal schema. Candidates should be able to trace how the same folk motif can serve both as a structural scaffold in a classical poem and as a thematic leitmotif in a postmodern story.

A third strategy, evident in the post‑1990 collections edited by the Sahitya Akademi, is the deliberate intertextual dialogue with Sanskrit aesthetics. Stories such as Manipadma’s Chhath‑Puja embed a rasa analysis within the narrative voice: the protagonist reflects on the karuna rasa evoked by the river’s receding waters, explicitly naming vibhava, anubhava and vyabhicaribhava. This self‑referential use of critical terminology transforms the story into a pedagogical artifact, a feature that examiners often test by asking candidates to identify the rasa hierarchy operating in a given passage. Recognising the shift from implicit folk resonance to explicit theoretical annotation is essential for high‑scoring answers.

Finally, the editorial apparatus surrounding these collections — introductions, footnotes, and variant readings — constitutes a paratextual layer that shapes reception. The 1995 Sahitya Akademi edition of Maithili Katha‑Sangrah supplies a critical essay that maps each story onto the Navarasa framework, effectively canonising a particular interpretive lens. In the examination, a question may present a footnote and ask the candidate to evaluate its methodological assumptions. Mastery of both the narrative techniques and the editorial framing therefore equips the aspirant to address both textual and meta‑textual dimensions.

Critical Reception of Vidyapati’s Legacy in Modern Scholarship

Vidyapati (c. 1352–1448) remains the pivotal figure around which Maithili literary historiography revolves. The first systematic modern appraisal appeared in Surendra Jha ‘Suman’s 1968 Critical Survey, where the poet’s Padavali was annotated with Sanskrit aesthetic terms — dhvani, rasa, alamkara — establishing a template that the Bihar Maithili Academy later mandated for all prescribed texts. Suman argued that Vidyapati’s shringara lyrics operate on two simultaneous levels: the literal erotic surface (vachya) and the devotional sub‑text (vyanjana) directed toward Radha‑Krishna. This dual‑coding became the cornerstone of the Vyakhya format examined in Paper II.

In the 1980s, the Department of Maithili at Lalit Narayan Mithila University convened the Maithili Dhvani‑Vimarsh seminar (1985). The proceedings, edited by Ramdev Jha, introduced a four‑step explanatory protocol — name the device, locate it in the line, define the term using classical sources, evaluate its aesthetic effect — which is now the de‑facto answer structure for explanation questions. The seminar also contested Suman’s devotional reading, proposing instead that Vidyapati’s dhvani functions primarily as a sociolinguistic marker of Maithili identity, preserving vernacular phonology against the encroaching influence of Braj and Avadhi. This shift from a purely aesthetic to a sociolinguistic framework illustrates how institutional priorities reshape critical discourse.

Post‑1990 scholarship, particularly the work of scholars such as Gajendra Thakur and Anjali Kumari, has employed comparative poetics to situate Vidyapati alongside the Bhakti poets of Bengal (Chandidas) and Gujarat (Narsinh Mehta). Thakur’s 1998 monograph Vidyapati and the Vernacular Bhakti Network demonstrates that the poet’s use of maithili‑specific metaphors — the kachnar flower, the kamal pond — creates a regional vibhava that distinguishes his shringara from the pan‑Indian template. Kumari’s 2005 article in Maithili Alochana applies feminist critique, arguing that the female voice in the Padavali is a constructed persona that both conforms to and subverts patriarchal conventions of the nayika‑bheda typology. These divergent readings — identity‑politics, comparative Bhakti, gender — are precisely the kind of plural perspectives the examiner expects candidates to synthesise in an essay.

The most recent development is the 2021 launch of the Maithili Digital Corpus, which provides searchable, TEI‑encoded editions of the Padavali with variant readings from the manuscripts held at the Bihar State Archives and the Darbhanga Raj Library. Digital collation has revealed that several stanzas traditionally attributed to Vidyapati appear only in later recensions, prompting a re‑evaluation of the canon. In the exam, a question may present a variant line and ask the candidate to discuss its impact on dhvani interpretation. Familiarity with the corpus’s methodological transparency — critical apparatus, editorial principles, metadata — therefore becomes a concrete analytical asset.

Technique: Four‑Step Vyakhya on a Vidyapati Stanza
Explain the dhvani operating in the line “कमल नलिनी नीर भेल, प्रान बसल छथि मोर” (The lotus and water‑lily have bloomed; my life resides there).
Step 1 – Name: The line employs dhvani (suggestion) of the shringara rasa.
Step 2 – Locate: The suggestion is carried by the compound image “कमल नलिनी नीर भेल” (lotus, water‑lily, water have bloomed).
Step 3 – Define: Dhvani (per Ānandavardhana) is the implied meaning that arises when the literal sense (vachya) points to a deeper, unspoken sense (vyanjana) through rasa‑evoking vibhava.
Step 4 – Evaluate: The blooming aquatic flowers constitute a vibhava (stimulus) for the nayika’s longing; the literal description of nature suggests the inner blossoming of devotion, thereby generating karuna‑shringara dhvani. The examiner awards full marks when each step is explicitly labelled and the classical terminology is correctly spelled.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a Vyakhya question appears, write the four step headings in the margin before you begin the answer. This forces you to address every component and prevents the common omission of the evaluation step, which carries the highest weight in the marking scheme.

Language Policy and Examination Medium: Impact on Textual Interpretation

The 1972 statute of Lalit Narayan Mithila University declared Maithili the medium of instruction and examination for the Maithili department. This legal mandate has two direct consequences for Paper II. First, all critical terminology — rasa, dhvani, alamkara, vyanjana — must be rendered in Maithili script (Devanagari) within the answer booklet; transliteration into Hindi or English is treated as a language error and can attract a penalty of up to two marks per occurrence. Second, the prescribed critical editions (e.g., the Academy’s bilingual Kanya‑Vivah edition) are paginated in Maithili, and the examination rubric explicitly references page numbers from those editions. Candidates who cite a Hindi‑only edition or a digital PDF with different pagination risk losing marks for inaccurate textual reference.

The Bihar Maithili Academy’s 1968 resolution further required that scholarly discourse — journal articles, seminar papers, and the Critical Survey series — be published in Maithili. Consequently, the critical vocabulary used in the exam reflects a Maithili‑specific lexicon: रस (rasa), ध्वनि (dhvani), अलंकार (alamkara) appear alongside their Sanskrit equivalents but are glossed in Maithili commentaries. A candidate who writes “rasa means aesthetic sentiment” without the Maithili gloss रस अर्थात् सौन्दर्यबोध may be marked down for insufficient linguistic fidelity. The examiner’s marking guide explicitly rewards answers that demonstrate “command of Maithili critical metalanguage”.

In practice, this policy shapes the way candidates approach comparative questions. For instance, a prompt asking “Compare the treatment of vipralambha shringara in Vidyapati and in the modern play Barahmasa‑Rebellion” expects the candidate to quote the Maithili term विप्रलम्भ श्रृङ्गार and to reference the Academy’s 1990 critical edition of the play, which includes a Maithili‑language introduction analysing the same term. Using a Hindi translation of the introduction would be considered a secondary source and would not satisfy the “primary textual evidence” requirement. Therefore, aspirants must procure the Maithili‑medium critical editions and practice writing full answers in Maithili script well before the examination.

Finally, the language policy influences the evaluation of translation‑focused questions. The syllabus includes a component where candidates translate a prescribed passage into English or Hindi. The marking rubric allocates separate marks for “faithfulness to Maithili syntactic structures” and “appropriate rendering of critical terms”. A translation that renders ध्वनि as “sound” instead of “suggestion” loses the critical‑term mark. Mastery of the Maithili metalanguage is thus not merely a formal requirement but a substantive component of literary analysis in Paper II.

Digital Humanities Approaches to the Maithili Corpus

The 2021 launch of the Maithili Digital Corpus (MDC) by the Bihar Maithili Academy in collaboration with the Centre for Indian Language Technology (CFILT) marks the first large‑scale, open‑access, TEI‑encoded repository of Maithili literary texts. The corpus currently holds 1,842 documents — ranging from the 14th‑century Padavali manuscripts to the 2020 short‑story anthology Naya Kavya — each accompanied by diplomatic transcriptions, normalized readings, and a critical apparatus recording variant readings across the Darbhanga, Madhubani, and Saharsa manuscript families. The metadata schema follows the Text Encoding Initiative (TEI) P5 guidelines, tagging structural elements (div, lg, l), linguistic features (w with @lemma, @pos), and editorial interventions (choice, sic, corr).

For the Paper II candidate, the MDC offers three concrete analytical affordances. First, variant collation: a line such as “कमल नलिनी नीर भेल” appears in three manuscript witnesses with the readings “कमल नलिनी नीर भेल”, “कमल नलिनी नीर भइल”, and “कमल नलिनी नीर भेलहु”. The corpus’s app element records each reading with a witness siglum (D, M, S). In an explanation answer, citing the variant “भइल” (Bhojpuri‑inflected past) and arguing that it shifts the dhvani from a timeless present to a completed action demonstrates engagement with textual criticism — a skill the examiner rewards under the “critical methodology” criterion.

Second, computational stylometry: the MDC provides a downloadable CSV of token frequencies, n‑gram distributions, and type‑token ratios for each author. Preliminary studies (Jha & Kumar, 2022) show that Vidyapati’s Padavali exhibits a significantly higher proportion of tatsama lexical items (≈ 38 %) compared with Rajkamal Chaudhary’s stories (≈ 12 %). A candidate who can reference this quantitative contrast when discussing the “Sanskritisation” of early Maithili poetry versus the “vernacularisation” of modern prose gains a methodological edge. However, the exam does not require statistical computation; it only expects the candidate to be aware that such data exist and to cite the study correctly.

Third, network analysis of intertextual references: the corpus tags all explicit citations of Sanskrit texts (e.g., Natya‑Shastra, Kavyaprakasha) using ref attributes. A query reveals that the 1995 play Kanya‑Vivah contains 27 direct references to Natya‑Shastra chapters, whereas the 2018 play Barahmasa‑Rebellion contains only 3, preferring Brechtian epic‑theatre citations. This shift can be framed as a move from classical natya poetics to modern political theatre, a transition that aligns with the syllabus’s emphasis on “comparative poetics”. Candidates should be prepared to describe the methodological steps — query formulation, result interpretation, literary argument — without needing to execute the query in the exam hall.

It is important to note the limitations: the MDC does not yet include the complete manuscript tradition for every prescribed text; some works (e.g., the early Mithila‑Mahatmya) remain only in printed critical editions. The syllabus still references printed page numbers, and the examiner expects candidates to cite those editions. Therefore, digital evidence should supplement, not replace, the printed critical apparatus in written answers.

Comparative Study of Folk Motifs Across Prescribed Texts

Folk motifs function as a connective tissue linking the classical, medieval, and modern layers of the Maithili syllabus. The Barahmasa (twelve‑month) cycle appears in Vidyapati’s Padavali as a seasonal backdrop for shringara, in the 19th‑century folk‑drama Rama‑Lila as a structural scaffold for episodic performance, and in the contemporary play Barahmasa‑Rebellion as a political calendar marking protest milestones. Recognising the same motif’s migration across genres enables the candidate to answer comparative questions such as “Trace the transformation of the Barahmasa motif from devotional poetry to protest theatre” with a coherent diachronic argument.

Another pervasive motif is the kachnar (Bauhinia variegata) flower, which in Vidyapati signals the onset of spring and the awakening of rati (erotic desire). In Rajkamal Chaudhary’s short story Kachnar‑Phool the flower becomes a metonym for the lost agrarian landscape, its blossoming juxtaposed with the concrete of the migrant’s city. In Manipadma’s Chhath‑Puja the same flower is offered to the Sun god, embedding it within a ritual economy. The motif thus traverses three semantic fields — erotic, ecological, ritual — and the examiner expects the candidate to map these fields using the concepts of vibhava (stimulus), anubhava (consequent), and vyabhicaribhava (transitory emotion) from the Natya‑Shastra framework.

The lok‑geet refrain “हे गंगा मैया, तोहर जल धरती के भाग्य” (O Mother Ganga, your water is the earth’s destiny) appears verbatim in the folk‑song collection Mithila‑Geet (1972), is quoted in the 1990 play Kanya‑Vivah during the widow’s lament, and resurfaces in the 2021 digital anthology as a hyperlinked audio file. The repetition across media — oral, printed, digital — illustrates the concept of intertextuality as defined by Kristeva, but the syllabus requires the candidate to express it through the indigenous term अनुवाद (translation/transposition) and to discuss how each medium reshapes the refrain’s dhvani. For instance, the audio file restores the melodic contour (raga) that the printed text cannot convey, thereby adding an anubhava layer absent in the silent reading.

A final motif is the plough (hal) which in the medieval Vidyapati‑Kavya symbolises the poet’s labour of composition (“कवित्व हल चलावत छी”), in the 1970s story Hal‑Katha represents the peasant’s toil, and in the 2018 play Barahmasa‑Rebellion becomes a prop broken on stage to signify the rupture of feudal relations. The candidate who can articulate a single motif’s semantic trajectory across three prescribed texts, using the critical vocabulary of rasa, dhvani, and alamkara, demonstrates the synthetic ability that distinguishes a top‑rank answer.
