Mains Precision: Which Poverty Line to Cite?
For Mains answers: (1) Tendulkar (2009) is the *official* line used for scheme targeting (BPL lists) until SECC 2011. (2) Rangarajan (2014) was *not accepted* by the Government — cite it only to show methodological debate. (3) Current policy uses **SECC 2011 deprivation criteria** (not a poverty line) for targeting (PMJAY, PMUY, PMAY, NSAP). (4) For analytical depth, cite **NITI Aayog MPI** — it captures non-income deprivations and is the Government's own progress metric. Never cite the old Planning Commission 2011-12 estimates (21.9% poverty) as current data.

1.4 The Sub-Plan Approach: SCSP and TSP

The Scheduled Caste Sub-Plan (SCSP) and Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP) mandate earmarking of plan funds proportional to SC/ST population share. Originated in 1979 (SCSP) and 1974 (TSP) (Source: Planning Commission Guidelines, 1979/1974; Ministry of Social Justice & Empowerment / Ministry of Tribal Affairs Guidelines).

  • Legal Status: Not statutory — based on Planning Commission (now NITI Aayog) guidelines. Some States (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka) enacted SCSP/TSP Acts.
  • Allocation Principle: Funds earmarked = (% SC/ST population in State) × Total State Plan Outlay. Non-divertible, non-lapsable.
  • Critique: CAG (2018) found diversion, under-utilisation, and "notional" earmarking (schemes not SC/ST-specific). The 2018 amendment to SC/ST (PoA) Act mandated Special Courts — but SCSP/TSP remains a guideline.
  • Current Framework: NITI Aayog's "Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework" tracks SCSP/TSP expenditure. Union Budget 2023-24: SCSP ₹1.45 lakh crore, TSP ₹1.19 lakh crore (Budget Estimates) (Source: Union Budget 2024-25, Statement 10A/10B; NITI Aayog OOMF Dashboard).
PYQ 2020 GS2 Q5
"The Constitution of India provides for a comprehensive framework for the protection of vulnerable sections. However, the implementation gap remains a major challenge." Critically examine with reference to SCs, STs, and OBCs. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2020 Q5
  1. Introduction (40 words): Constitution: Fundamental Rights (Art. 15, 16, 17), Directive Principles (Art. 46), Special Provisions (Art. 330, 332, 335, 338, 338A, 338B, Fifth/Sixth Schedules), Statutory bodies (NCSC, NCST, NCBC).
  2. SCs — Framework vs Reality (120 words): PoA Act 1989 (amended 2015, 2018 — stringent, special courts); SCSP (guideline, not law); Manual Scavenging Act 2013. Gaps: Conviction rate under PoA Act ~30% (NCRB 2022); SCSP diversion (CAG 2018); manual scavenging deaths persist (530+ deaths 2017-2022, NSKFDC).
  3. STs — Framework vs Reality (120 words): Fifth/Sixth Schedules (self-governance); PESA 1996 (Gram Sabha powers); FRA 2006 (CFR/IFR); TSP. Gaps: PESA not fully implemented in 10 States; FRA claims rejected (30%+ rejection rate, MoTA 2023); TSP under-utilisation; displacement without consent (mining, dams).
  4. OBCs — Framework vs Reality (100 words): 27% reservation (Indra Sawhney, 1992 — creamy layer); NCBC constitutional status (102nd Amend, 2018); Rohini Commission (sub-categorisation). Gaps: Creamy layer ceiling (₹8 lakh) outdated; sub-categorisation pending; OBC data in Census absent (SECC 2011 caste data not released); private sector reservation absent.
  5. Systemic Implementation Gaps (80 words): Institutional: Vacancies in NCSC/NCST/NCBC; Special Courts under PoA not functional in all districts. Fiscal: SCSP/TSP not statutory. Data: No caste Census since 1931 — targeting relies on SECC 2011 (deprivation, not caste). Political: Reservation in legislatures extended but local body reservations (Art. 243D/T) not implemented in some States.
  6. Conclusion (40 words): Constitutional architecture is robust — the deficit is in *institutional capacity*, *fiscal federalism* (SCSP/TSP as guidelines), and *data blindness* (no caste Census). Solution: Statutory SCSP/TSP, fill constitutional body vacancies, implement Rohini Commission, conduct caste Census.

1.5 Intersectionality and Multiple Disadvantage

Vulnerability is rarely single-axis. An SC woman with disability in a tribal area faces compounded exclusion. The **National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5)** and **NITI Aayog MPI** reveal intersectional deprivations:

  • ST women: Highest anaemia (65.5%), lowest literacy (49.4%), highest child marriage (26.8%) (Source: NFHS-5, 2019-21, Ministry of Health & Family Welfare).
  • SC households: Highest multidimensional poverty headcount (27.2% vs 8.1% for General category) (Source: NITI Aayog MPI Progress Review 2023).
  • PwDs: Labour force participation rate 36.8% (vs 50.3% for non-disabled); 64% not in workforce (PLFS 2022-23) (Source: Periodic Labour Force Survey 2022-23, MoSPI).
Solved Mini-Example: Intersectional Targeting
Question: "Welfare schemes fail because they treat vulnerability as single-axis." Examine with reference to any two schemes. (10 marks)
PMJAY: Targets SECC deprivation (D1-D5, D7) — misses intersectionality (e.g., ST woman with disability may not hit D1-D5 but faces triple exclusion). Fix: Add "vulnerability weightage" in targeting. PMMVY: Maternity benefit (₹5,000) for first living child — excludes women with stillbirths/miscarriages, informal workers without documentation. Fix: Universalise (as NFSA Schedule II mandates ₹6,000), delink from birth order, use Aadhaar+e-Shram for identification. Conclusion: Single-axis targeting (caste OR gender OR disability) leaves gaps at intersections. Data integration (SECC + e-Shram + UDID + NFHS) needed.

Mains Trap: Health is a State Subject
Always mention: Health is **State List (Entry 6)** — States spend ~65% of total govt health expenditure. Centre supplements via Centrally Sponsored Schemes (NHM, PMJAY, PMABHIM). This federal asymmetry explains why outcomes vary wildly (Kerala IMR 6 vs MP 43; MMR 19 vs 173). In answers, cite **NITI Aayog Health Index** (weights outcomes, governance, inputs) — shows States' performance gap. The 15th FC recommended health grants to States (₹70,051 crore for 2021-26) — a fiscal federalism correction.

2.6 Systemic Challenges in Indian Health System

  1. Human Resource Crisis: Doctor-population ratio 1:834 (2022, including AYUSH) vs WHO norm 1:1000; Specialist shortfall at CHCs: Surgeons 83%, Obstetricians 74%, Physicians 79%, Paediatricians 81% (Rural Health Statistics 2022) (Source: Rural Health Statistics 2022, MoHFW). Nurses: 1.96 per 1,000 (WHO: 3.5). Urban-rural maldistribution: 65% doctors serve 35% urban population.
  2. High OOPE and Medical Impoverishment: 5.5 crore pushed into poverty annually due to health spending (PHFI 2018). Drugs constitute ~60% of OOPE. Free Drugs/Diagnostics initiatives (NHM) cover only public facilities.
  3. NCD Epidemic: NCDs account for 63% of all deaths (WHO 2022). Diabetes: 101 million (ICMR-INDIAB 2023); Hypertension: 315 million. NPCDCS covers 640 districts but screening-treatment cascade is broken.
  4. Mental Health Neglect: Treatment gap 70-92% (NMHS 2016). Mental Healthcare Act 2017 mandates services at district level — <10% districts have functional Mental Health Review Boards. Budget allocation: <0.5% of health budget.
  5. Regulatory Capture and Quality: Clinical Establishments Act, 2010 adopted by only 11 States/UTs. NABH accreditation: <5% hospitals. Medical Council replaced by NMC (2019) — NEXT exam for licensure not yet operational.
PYQ 2021 GS2 Q5
"The public health system has limitations in providing universal health coverage. Do you think the private sector can help in bridging the gap? What other viable alternatives do you suggest?" (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2021 Q5
  1. Introduction (40 words): UHC = access to quality health services without financial hardship (WHO). India: 47% OOPE, specialist shortfall 74-83% at CHCs, 1.3% GDP on health. Public system necessary but insufficient alone.
  2. Private Sector Role (120 words): Already provides 55% hospital beds, 80% doctors. PMJAY empanels 29,000+ hospitals (55% public). Pros: capacity, technology, reach. Cons: profit motive → over-treatment, upcoding (PMJAY fraud), cherry-picking profitable packages, weak regulation (Clinical Establishments Act not universal). Not a substitute — a *regulated supplement*.
  3. Alternative 1: Strengthen Public Primary Care (100 words): HWCs (1.5 lakh) → CPHC with 12 services. Invest in CHOs (mid-level providers), drug/diagnostic supply, e-Sanjeevani telemedicine. Evidence: Thailand's UHC built on primary care; Brazil's Family Health Strategy. Cost-effective — prevents secondary/tertiary burden.
  4. Alternative 2: Strategic Purchasing & Regulation (100 words): Move from input-based to output-based financing. DRG-based payment (PMJAY moving this way). Regulate prices (NPPA for drugs, stents, knee implants). Clinical Establishments Act mandatory for all States. Standard treatment guidelines (STGs) — NHA developing for PMJAY packages.
  5. Alternative 3: Health Financing Reforms (80 words): 15th FC health grants (₹70,051 crore) to States — conditional on outcomes. Consolidate CSSs into flexible pools. Explore mandatory health insurance (like Thailand's UC Scheme) or employer-based pooling for formal sector. Increase public spending to 2.5% GDP (NHP 2017).
  6. Conclusion (40 words): Private sector is inevitable but must be *strategically purchased* and *tightly regulated*. The backbone of UHC is a strengthened public primary care system — HWCs, CHOs, free drugs/diagnostics, digital integration (ABDM). Federal fiscal transfers (15th FC) must incentivise outcomes, not inputs.

Common Traps

  • Confusing NFSA coverage (75% rural / 50% urban) with universal coverage — NFSA is targeted, not universal.
  • Thinking RTE covers children up to age 18 — it covers ages 6–14 only (Art. 21A); Art. 45 covers ECCE <6 years.
  • Assuming MGNREGA work is guaranteed in urban areas — it is rural only (Art. 243G, 11th Schedule).
  • Missing that Ayushman Bharat targets SECC 2011-deprived families, not all citizens — ~40% population covered.
  • Overlooking that health is primarily a State subject — the Centre supplements through Centrally Sponsored Schemes (NHM, PMJAY).
  • Confusing PMMVY (₹5,000, first child only, conditional) with NFSA maternity benefit (₹6,000, universal, Schedule II) — they are different entitlements.
  • Assuming SCSP/TSP are statutory — they are Planning Commission/NITI Aayog guidelines; only some States enacted laws.
  • Missing that 102nd Amendment (2018) gave NCBC constitutional status but 105th Amendment (2021) restored State power to identify OBCs.
  • Thinking NEP 2020's 5+3+3+4 structure is implemented nationwide — it is a policy framework; States implement at different paces.

6.1 Health Equity: The Architecture of Universal Health Coverage in India

Health equity in India is operationalised through a layered architecture: primary care (Health & Wellness Centres under Ayushman Bharat), financial protection (PMJAY for secondary/tertiary care), public health functions (NHM, disease control programmes), and social determinants (Poshan 2.0, Jal Jeevan Mission, Swachh Bharat). The constitutional anchor remains Article 21 (read with Art. 47) — the Supreme Court in Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. 6.5 NEP 2020: Equity Provisions and Implementation Status

NEP 2020's equity architecture includes:

  • Foundational Literacy & Numeracy (FLN) Mission: NIPUN Bharat (2021) — universal FLN by Grade 3 by 2026-27. Budget: ₹2,688 crore (2021-26). Early grade assessments show incremental improvement but 2026 target ambitious (Source: MoE, NIPUN Bharat Guidelines, 2021; Annual Reports).
  • Gender Inclusion Fund: For ST/SC/minority girls — States to design interventions (transport, hostels, stipends). Not yet a distinct Central budget line; subsumed in Samagra Shiksha.
  • Special Education Zones (SEZs): Regions with high concentration of disadvantaged groups — targeted resources. Not yet operationalised; pilot in Aspirational Districts.
  • Mother Tongue / Local Language Instruction: Up to Grade 5 (preferably Grade 8). Implementation varies — States control medium of instruction; English-medium demand from parents creates tension.
  • Vocational Education from Grade 6: Integration with academic curriculum. Samagra Shiksha covers 14,000+ schools; but industry linkage and certification remain weak.
  • Equitable Access — EWS/DG Quota (Sec. 12(1)(c) RTE Act): 25% seats in private unaided schools. Fill rate ~30-40% nationally; reimbursement delays (States owe schools ₹5,000+ crore), document barriers, social exclusion in schools.

6.7 Intersectional Gaps: Health-Education-Skill Nexus

Equity is not sectoral. A tribal girl in a forested block faces: (1) Health: No HWC within 5 km; anaemia (65.5%, NFHS-5); teenage pregnancy risk. (2) Education: Single-teacher school; language barrier (instruction in State language, not mother tongue); drops out at menarche (no toilets). (3) Skills: No ITI nearby; apprenticeship in distant town — unsafe migration. (4) Social Protection: No Aadhaar (birth not registered); excluded from PMJAY, PMMVY, scholarship.

  2. Map sectoral schemes to the intersection: Health (HWC, PMJAY, Poshan 2.0), Education (RTE, Samagra Shiksha, NIPUN), Skills (PMKVY, NAPS), Social Protection (NSAP, PMMVY, ONORC).
  3. Diagnose the delivery failure at each layer: Infrastructure (distance, connectivity), Human resource (vacancy, language, caste bias), Information (awareness, digital access), Administrative (Aadhaar, documentation, portability).
  4. Cite the judicial/constitutional anchor: Art. 21 (health), 21A (education), 15/16/46 (non-discrimination), Fifth Schedule / PESA / FRA (tribal autonomy).
  5. Propose integrated solutions: Convergent action plans (Aspirational Blocks), single-window registration (e-Shram + ABHA + Aadhaar), mobile service delivery, community cadres (ASHA + Anganwadi + SHG convergence).

7.2 Scheduled Tribes: FRA, PESA, Fifth/Sixth Schedules, and Forest Rights

The Forest Rights Act, 2006 (FRA) recognises Individual Forest Rights (IFR) and Community Forest Rights (CFR). Solved Mini-Example: FRA Implementation — The Consent Gap

7.3 Other Backward Classes: Sub-Categorisation, Creamy Layer, and the Data Vacuum

The 102nd Amendment (2018) gave NCBC constitutional status (Art. 338B). The 105th Amendment (2021) restored State power to identify OBCs (Art. 342A) after the Supreme Court in Maratha Reservation Case (2021) interpreted the 102nd Amendment as removing State power (Source: Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, Maharashtra, decided 5 May 2021; 105th Amendment Act, 2021).

  • Creamy Layer: Income ceiling ₹8 lakh/year (revised 2017 from ₹6 lakh). Excludes: Constitutional posts, Group A/Class I officers, income from salary/agriculture >₹8 lakh. Critique: Ceiling not indexed to inflation; agricultural income exemption benefits land-owning OBCs; no sub-categorisation within creamy layer.
  • Rohini Commission (Sub-categorisation): Constituted 2017 (Justice G. Rohini), report submitted 2023. Recommended splitting 27% quota into 4 sub-categories based on "backwardness gradient" — benefits to flow to most backward OBCs. Not yet implemented; political sensitivity high.
  • Caste Census Vacuum: No caste enumeration since 1931. SECC 2011 collected caste data but not released. Mandal Commission (1980) used 1931 data. NSSO 2011-12: OBC ~41% population. Without current data, targeting (Scholarships, OBC Sub-Plan) and sub-categorisation are evidence-free.
Mains Precision: 102nd vs 105th Amendment
102nd Amendment (2018): Inserted Art. 338B (NCBC constitutional status), Art. 342A (President notifies Central OBC list), Art. 366(26C) (definition of "Socially and Educationally Backward Classes"). Supreme Court (Maratha case, 2021) read Art. 342A as *exhaustive* — only President can notify, States cannot. 105th Amendment (2021): Amended Art. 342A(3) — "State may by law identify SEBCs"; Art. 338B(9) — NCBC consults States; Art. 366(26C) — definition preserved. Result: Dual lists (Central + State) restored. In answers, cite both Amendments and the Maratha judgment.

NSAP (IGNOAPS, IGNWPS, IGNDPS, NFBS) — Central pension ₹200-500/month (since 2006, not indexed); States top up. Atal Vayo Abhyuday Yojana (AVYAY, 2021) — senior citizen homes, helplines, physio clinics. Gap: 13.8 crore elderly (60+, Census 2011 projected 2021); <10% covered by any pension; long-term care (dementia, palliative) absent from public system; Maintenance Tribunals dysfunctional (vacancies, no enforcement).

7.8 Migrant Workers: e-Shram, ONORC, and the Portability Architecture

e-Shram Portal (2021): National database of unorganised workers — 28+ crore registered (2024). Captures occupation, skills, migration intent. Linked to PM-SYM (pension), PMJAY, ONORC, BOCW welfare boards. One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC): Portability of NFSA entitlements — 35 States/UTs onboarded; ~10 crore portability transactions (2023). Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act, 1979 subsumed in Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code, 2020 — not yet notified. Gap: e-Shram registration voluntary; inter-State coordination weak; portability of social security (EPFO, ESI) across States incomplete; urban housing for migrants (PMAY-U) slow.

7.10 PYQ Integration: Vulnerable Groups

PYQ 2019 GS2 Q5
"The Forest Rights Act, 2006 was a landmark legislation for tribal empowerment but its implementation remains tardy." Critically examine. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2019 Q5
  1. Introduction (40 words): FRA 2006 — recognises IFR, CFR, habitat rights, minor forest produce. Historical injustice correction. Potential: 40 million ha forest land, 150 million tribals/forest-dwellers.
  2. Achievements (100 words): ~23 lakh IFR titles (~40 lakh ha); ~1.1 lakh CFR titles (~15 lakh ha). MFP ownership — tendu, bamboo, mahua income to Gram Sabhas. Niyamgiri (2013) — Gram Sabha consent upheld. CFR management plans in Maharashtra, Odisha, Gujarat show conservation + livelihood.
  3. Implementation Failures (120 words): Rejection rate >30% (MoTA 2023) — "conservation" grounds, procedural lapses. Claims pending >5 lakh. CFR recognition <5% of potential. State forest depts resist — JFM committees parallel to Gram Sabhas. Compensatory Afforestation (CAF Act 2016) on FRA land. PESA Rules not notified in 3 of 10 States — Gram Sabha powers over MFP, minor minerals, land acquisition unrealised.
  4. Structural Barriers (100 words): Evidence requirements (75 years / 3 generations) — oral history not accepted. Forest dept controls maps, GPS, survey — Gram Sabha dependent. No title for "habitat rights" (PVTGs). Mining clearances via "deemed consent". Political economy: forest bureaucracy + mining interests vs tribal autonomy. Budget: MoTA allocation ~₹6,000 crore — FRA implementation not a distinct line.
  5. Way Forward (80 words): Amend FRA Rules — oral evidence accepted; Gram Sabha as mapping authority; CFR titles time-bound; CAF Act amended for Gram Sabha consent; PESA Rules in all 10 States; Special Courts for FRA disputes; MoTA capacity building for Gram Sabhas; convergent action (FRA + PESA + MGNREGA + MFP-MSP).
  6. Conclusion (40 words): FRA is unimplemented radicalism. The forest bureaucracy remains colonial; Gram Sabhas lack technical/legal power. Empowerment requires shifting forest governance from "control" to "co-management" — legislative amendment, budgetary autonomy, judicial enforcement of Niyamgiri principle.
PYQ 2021 GS2 Q4
"The 102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018 and the subsequent 105th Amendment Act, 2021 reflect the Centre-State tension in OBC identification." Discuss. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2021 Q4
  1. Introduction (40 words): 102nd Amendment (2018): Art. 338B (NCBC constitutional status), Art. 342A (President notifies Central OBC list), Art. 366(26C) (SEBC definition). 105th Amendment (2021): Restored State power to identify OBCs after Maratha judgment (2021).
  2. 102nd Amendment — Centralisation (100 words): Art. 342A(1): "President may specify SEBCs" — read as exhaustive list. Art. 342A(2): Parliament can include/exclude. Art. 338B: NCBC constitutional — consults Centre, not States. Maratha judgment (2021): States lost power to identify OBCs — only President's list valid.
  3. Maratha Judgment — The Trigger (100 words): Maharashtra gave 16% Maratha quota (total 68% — breached 50% ceiling). Supreme Court: (a) 50% ceiling sacrosanct (Indra Sawhney); (b) Marathas not "socially/educationally backward" (no quantifiable data); (c) 102nd Amendment removes State power — Art. 342A exhaustive. Dissent (Justice Ravindra Bhat): Federalism violated.
  4. 105th Amendment — Federal Correction (100 words): Art. 342A(3): "State may by law identify SEBCs" — restored State list. Art. 338B(9): NCBC shall consult State Govts. Art. 366(26C): SEBC definition preserved. Dual lists (Central + State) now explicit. President's list for Central institutions; State list for State institutions.
  5. Unresolved Tensions (80 words): (a) Creamy layer: Central ceiling ₹8 lakh; States can vary? Unclear. (b) Sub-categorisation: Rohini Commission report (2023) — Central list split; States may differ. (c) Caste Census: None since 1931 — both lists evidence-free. (d) NCBC role: Advisory only; no binding power on inclusion/exclusion.
  6. Conclusion (40 words): 102nd/105th Amendments reveal OBC politics as federal contest. Resolution: Caste Census (evidence), Rohini implementation (equity within OBCs), NCBC empowerment (institutional), creamy layer indexing (fairness). Cooperative federalism in backwardness identification — not Centre vs State.
PYQ 2020 GS2 Q4
"The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 is a paradigm shift but the accessibility gap remains." Discuss. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2020 Q4
  1. Introduction (40 words): RPwD Act 2016 (replaced 1995 Act) — UNCRPD compliant. Disabilities: 7 → 21. Rights-based (not welfare): 4% reservation (jobs/education), accessibility standards, anti-discrimination, guardianship reform.
  2. Paradigm Shift (100 words): (a) Social model — disability = interaction of impairment with barriers. (b) 21 conditions (acid attack, Parkinson's, autism, SLD, mental illness, etc.). (c) 4% reservation (Sec. 34, 35) — horizontal across categories. (d) Accessibility: built environment, transport, ICT (Sec. 40-46) — time-bound. (e) Limited guardianship (Sec. 14) — supported decision-making. (f) Special Courts (Sec. 84) — speedy trial.
  3. Accessibility Gap — Built Environment (100 words): Accessible India Campaign (2015): 1,600+ buildings audited; <20% fully compliant. 2021 deadline missed. Retrofitting cost not budgeted. Railways: 50% stations with lifts/ramps; coaches not accessible. Bus transport: <10% low-floor.
  4. Accessibility Gap — ICT & Education (100 words): GIGW mandated; >60% govt portals non-WCAG 2.1 compliant. Education: 5% reservation (Sec. 35) but <1% CwDs in higher education (AISHE 2022). No scribes, accessible textbooks, assistive tech. Special educators: <50,000 for 78 lakh CwDs (UDISE+).
  5. Employment & Certification Gaps (80 words): 4% reservation — backlog >1 lakh (DoPT 2023). Promotion reservation litigated (Sec. 34 vs DoPT OMs). UDID: 1.1 crore issued vs 2.68 crore Census 2011. Medical boards scarce; certificate validity inter-State issues. Private sector: no reservation mandate (only incentivisation).
  6. Conclusion (40 words): RPwD Act 2016 is legislative excellence — implementation is the disability. Fix: Accessibility as non-negotiable procurement condition; UDID universalisation; special educator cadre; private sector quota (start with govt contractors); disability budgeting (Statement 12 in Union Budget).
