Key Concepts

Understanding Social Justice and Welfare Schemes is fundamental for the UPSC Mains GS II paper, as it bridges the gap between constitutional ideals and ground-level governance. This lesson will dissect the mechanisms designed to uplift vulnerable sections, manage poverty through data-driven approaches, and ensure equitable access to health and education. By mastering the specific rules, historical context, and implementation strategies of these schemes, you will be able to answer analytical questions with precision.

Poverty: Measurement and Alleviation

The concept of poverty in India has evolved from a simple income-based definition to a multidimensional approach. The Planning Commission's original method relied on the Tendulkar Committee (2011), which defined poverty based on calorie requirements (2,400 kcal for rural and 2,100 kcal for urban areas) adjusted for inflation using CPI.

Why this rule works: It assumes a minimum nutritional intake is the baseline for survival. However, the NITI Aayog's Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) now complements this by looking at deprivations in health, education, and standard of living simultaneously.

  • Poverty Line: The monetary threshold below which a household is considered poor. It changes annually based on CPI.
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI): A composite measure that identifies people who are simultaneously deprived in multiple dimensions like nutrition, schooling, and electricity.

The examiner often asks about the shift from monetary poverty to multidimensional poverty or the specific calorie norms adopted by different committees. Remember that the Tendulkar Committee is the last major committee to define poverty purely on income, while the MPI focuses on capabilities.

Vulnerable Sections: Rights and Schemes

The Constitution of India explicitly identifies Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST), and Other Backward Classes (OBC) as vulnerable sections requiring affirmative action. The state provides specific welfare schemes to ensure their social and economic integration.

Key Rules and Patterns:

  • SC/ST: Governed by the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989. Key schemes include the National Rural Livelihood Mission (MGNREGA has specific quotas for SC/ST).
  • OBC: Defined under the Constitution (Other Backward Classes) Order, 1993. The Pradhan Mantri Rozgar Protsahan Yojana often includes OBC beneficiaries.
  • Poor and Deprived: Covered by schemes like the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) which provides pensions to widows, disabled persons, and large families regardless of caste.

The logic here is that welfare schemes are not just charity but a fulfillment of Fundamental Duties and Directive Principles of State Policy. When answering questions, always link the scheme to the specific article or constitutional provision it upholds.

Health: Access and Infrastructure

Health is a state subject under the Concurrent List, meaning both Centre and States have jurisdiction. The primary goal of health schemes is to ensure Universal Health Coverage (UHC) by reducing out-of-pocket expenditures.

The Exact Formula for Success:

  1. Infrastructure: Establishment of Primary Health Centers (PHCs) and Sub-Centres in every village/block.
  2. Service Delivery: Implementation of the National Health Mission (NHM), which replaced the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM).
  3. Financial Protection: The Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY) provides health insurance coverage of Rs. 5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care hospitalization.

Why this works: By combining infrastructure (hardware) with financial protection (software), the state ensures that even the poorest can access life-saving treatments without falling into poverty traps.

Education: Universalization and Quality

Education is a fundamental right under Article 21A, making it a central concern for welfare schemes. The focus has shifted from mere enrollment to retention and quality of learning.

Key Rules and Patterns:

  • School Education: Right to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009 ensures 25% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) in private schools.
  • Higher Education: The National Scholarship Portal (NSP) consolidates various scholarships. The Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan integrates schemes for school education, special education, and educational technology.
  • Mid-Day Meal Scheme: A nutrition intervention that ensures children attend school by providing free meals, directly linking hunger alleviation to education.

The examiner loves asking about the RTE Act's reservation norms or the specific goals of Samagra Shiksha. Always remember that education schemes are designed to break the intergenerational cycle of poverty.

Welfare Schemes: A Unified View

Welfare schemes in India are broadly categorized into social security, employment guarantee, and skill development. The overarching philosophy is "Inclusive Growth."

  • Social Security: Includes the National Pension System (NPS) for voluntary retirement income.
  • Employment Guarantee: MGNREGA guarantees 100 days of wage employment to every rural household that demands it, making it a legal right under the Act.
  • Skill Development: The Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) focuses on employability skills for youth.
Technique: Linking Scheme to Constitutional Provision
Question: "Analyze how the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) aligns with the Directive Principles of State Policy."
Solution:
To answer this, you must first identify the core objective of PMJDY: Financial Inclusion. Next, recall that Article 38(2) of the DPSP directs the state to secure a social order for the promotion of welfare of the people. Furthermore, Article 41 guarantees the right to work, public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement. PMJDY operationalizes these by ensuring every citizen has access to banking services, thereby securing economic dignity. This connects a specific scheme to the constitutional mandate for social justice.
Technique: Calculating MPI Deprivation Score
Question: "If a household is deprived in 3 out of 10 indicators (Health, Education, Standard of Living) and the weights are equal, what is their deprivation score?"
Solution:
The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) calculates a household's deprivation score based on weighted indicators. If we assume equal weight for simplicity in this theoretical question: Total Indicators = 10. Deprived Indicators = 3. The formula is (Number of Deprivations / Total Indicators). Therefore, the score is 3/10 or 0.3. Since the MPI threshold for being classified as multidimensionally poor is usually a deprivation index of 0.33 or higher, this household would NOT be classified as 'poor' under strict MPI definitions unless the weights are adjusted. This demonstrates how specific rules determine classification.

Common Traps

  • Mistake 1: Confusing the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog. The Tendulkar Committee was under the Planning Commission; the MPI is developed by NITI Aayog.
  • Mistake 2: Assuming RTE applies to all private schools equally without mentioning the 25% reservation rule for EWS.
  • Mistake 3: Thinking MGNREGA is a discretionary scheme. It is a legal right under Section 3(a) of the Act.
  • Mistake 4: Overlooking that Health and Education are State subjects, though the Centre funds them through specific schemes like NHM and Samagra Shiksha.

Memory Tricks

  • Mnemonic for Welfare Schemes: Use the acronym "HEART": Health (Ayushman Bharat), Education (Samagra Shiksha), Agriculture (PM-KISAN - often linked to rural welfare), Rural Employment (MGNREGA), Tribal Welfare (Tribal Sub-Plan).
  • Comparison Trick: Compare RTE (2009) with Mid-Day Meal. RTE is about access to school; MDM is about nutrition to keep the child in school. Both target the same demographic but solve different problems.
  • Rule of Thumb: If a scheme name starts with "Pradhan Mantri", it is usually a flagship central sector scheme with full funding from the Centre. If it ends with "Abhiyan", it involves shared responsibility (Centre and State).
Exam-Day Strategy
When writing your answer on Social Justice, always start with the Constitutional basis (Articles 14, 15, 38, 47). Then, mention one flagship scheme relevant to the specific topic. Finally, conclude by highlighting a current challenge or a recent government initiative. This structure ensures you cover the legal, practical, and analytical dimensions required for high marks.
