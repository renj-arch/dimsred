Mains Precision: Which Poverty Line to Cite?
For Mains answers: (1) Tendulkar (2009) is the *official* line used for scheme targeting (BPL lists) until SECC 2011. (2) Rangarajan (2014) was *not accepted* by the Government — cite it only to show methodological debate. (3) Current policy uses **SECC 2011 deprivation criteria** (not a poverty line) for targeting (PMJAY, PMUY, PMAY, NSAP). (4) For analytical depth, cite **NITI Aayog MPI** — it captures non-income deprivations and is the Government's own progress metric. Never cite the old Planning Commission 2011-12 estimates (21.9% poverty) as current data.

1.2 Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC) 2011: The Targeting Engine

SECC 2011 replaced BPL Census as the targeting instrument for major welfare schemes. It enumerated 24.49 crore households (17.97 crore rural, 6.52 crore urban) using **automatic exclusion**, **automatic inclusion**, and **graded deprivation** criteria (Source: SECC 2011, Ministry of Rural Development / Ministry of Housing & Urban Poverty Alleviation).

1.4 The Sub-Plan Approach: SCSP and TSP

The Scheduled Caste Sub-Plan (SCSP) and Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP) mandate earmarking of plan funds proportional to SC/ST population share. Originated in 1979 (SCSP) and 1974 (TSP) (Source: Planning Commission Guidelines, 1979/1974; Ministry of Social Justice & Empowerment / Ministry of Tribal Affairs Guidelines).

  • Legal Status: Not statutory — based on Planning Commission (now NITI Aayog) guidelines. Some States (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka) enacted SCSP/TSP Acts.
  • Allocation Principle: Funds earmarked = (% SC/ST population in State) × Total State Plan Outlay. Non-divertible, non-lapsable.
  • Critique: CAG (2018) found diversion, under-utilisation, and "notional" earmarking (schemes not SC/ST-specific). The 2018 amendment to SC/ST (PoA) Act mandated Special Courts — but SCSP/TSP remains a guideline.
  • Current Framework: NITI Aayog's "Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework" tracks SCSP/TSP expenditure. Union Budget 2023-24: SCSP ₹1.45 lakh crore, TSP ₹1.19 lakh crore (Budget Estimates) (Source: Union Budget 2024-25, Statement 10A/10B; NITI Aayog OOMF Dashboard).
PYQ 2020 GS2 Q5
"The Constitution of India provides for a comprehensive framework for the protection of vulnerable sections. However, the implementation gap remains a major challenge." Critically examine with reference to SCs, STs, and OBCs. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2020 Q5
  1. Introduction (40 words): Constitution: Fundamental Rights (Art. 15, 16, 17), Directive Principles (Art. 46), Special Provisions (Art. 330, 332, 335, 338, 338A, 338B, Fifth/Sixth Schedules), Statutory bodies (NCSC, NCST, NCBC).
  2. SCs — Framework vs Reality (120 words): PoA Act 1989 (amended 2015, 2018 — stringent, special courts); SCSP (guideline, not law); Manual Scavenging Act 2013. Gaps: Conviction rate under PoA Act ~30% (NCRB 2022); SCSP diversion (CAG 2018); manual scavenging deaths persist (530+ deaths 2017-2022, NSKFDC).
  3. STs — Framework vs Reality (120 words): Fifth/Sixth Schedules (self-governance); PESA 1996 (Gram Sabha powers); FRA 2006 (CFR/IFR); TSP. Gaps: PESA not fully implemented in 10 States; FRA claims rejected (30%+ rejection rate, MoTA 2023); TSP under-utilisation; displacement without consent (mining, dams).
  4. OBCs — Framework vs Reality (100 words): 27% reservation (Indra Sawhney, 1992 — creamy layer); NCBC constitutional status (102nd Amend, 2018); Rohini Commission (sub-categorisation). Gaps: Creamy layer ceiling (₹8 lakh) outdated; sub-categorisation pending; OBC data in Census absent (SECC 2011 caste data not released); private sector reservation absent.
  5. Systemic Implementation Gaps (80 words): Institutional: Vacancies in NCSC/NCST/NCBC; Special Courts under PoA not functional in all districts. Fiscal: SCSP/TSP not statutory. Data: No caste Census since 1931 — targeting relies on SECC 2011 (deprivation, not caste). Political: Reservation in legislatures extended but local body reservations (Art. 243D/T) not implemented in some States.
  6. Conclusion (40 words): Constitutional architecture is robust — the deficit is in *institutional capacity*, *fiscal federalism* (SCSP/TSP as guidelines), and *data blindness* (no caste Census). Solution: Statutory SCSP/TSP, fill constitutional body vacancies, implement Rohini Commission, conduct caste Census.

1.5 Intersectionality and Multiple Disadvantage

Vulnerability is rarely single-axis. An SC woman with disability in a tribal area faces compounded exclusion. The **National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5)** and **NITI Aayog MPI** reveal intersectional deprivations:

  • ST women: Highest anaemia (65.5%), lowest literacy (49.4%), highest child marriage (26.8%) (Source: NFHS-5, 2019-21, Ministry of Health & Family Welfare).
  • SC households: Highest multidimensional poverty headcount (27.2% vs 8.1% for General category) (Source: NITI Aayog MPI Progress Review 2023).
  • PwDs: Labour force participation rate 36.8% (vs 50.3% for non-disabled); 64% not in workforce (PLFS 2022-23) (Source: Periodic Labour Force Survey 2022-23, MoSPI).
Solved Mini-Example: Intersectional Targeting
Question: "Welfare schemes fail because they treat vulnerability as single-axis." Examine with reference to any two schemes. (10 marks)
PMJAY: Targets SECC deprivation (D1-D5, D7) — misses intersectionality (e.g., ST woman with disability may not hit D1-D5 but faces triple exclusion). Fix: Add "vulnerability weightage" in targeting. PMMVY: Maternity benefit (₹5,000) for first living child — excludes women with stillbirths/miscarriages, informal workers without documentation. Fix: Universalise (as NFSA Schedule II mandates ₹6,000), delink from birth order, use Aadhaar+e-Shram for identification. Conclusion: Single-axis targeting (caste OR gender OR disability) leaves gaps at intersections. Data integration (SECC + e-Shram + UDID + NFHS) needed.

Mains Trap: Health is a State Subject
Always mention: Health is **State List (Entry 6)** — States spend ~65% of total govt health expenditure. Centre supplements via Centrally Sponsored Schemes (NHM, PMJAY, PMABHIM). This federal asymmetry explains why outcomes vary wildly (Kerala IMR 6 vs MP 43; MMR 19 vs 173). In answers, cite **NITI Aayog Health Index** (weights outcomes, governance, inputs) — shows States' performance gap. The 15th FC recommended health grants to States (₹70,051 crore for 2021-26) — a fiscal federalism correction.

2.6 Systemic Challenges in Indian Health System

  1. Human Resource Crisis: Doctor-population ratio 1:834 (2022, including AYUSH) vs WHO norm 1:1000; Specialist shortfall at CHCs: Surgeons 83%, Obstetricians 74%, Physicians 79%, Paediatricians 81% (Rural Health Statistics 2022) (Source: Rural Health Statistics 2022, MoHFW). Nurses: 1.96 per 1,000 (WHO: 3.5). Urban-rural maldistribution: 65% doctors serve 35% urban population.
  2. High OOPE and Medical Impoverishment: 5.5 crore pushed into poverty annually due to health spending (PHFI 2018). Drugs constitute ~60% of OOPE. Free Drugs/Diagnostics initiatives (NHM) cover only public facilities.
  3. NCD Epidemic: NCDs account for 63% of all deaths (WHO 2022). Diabetes: 101 million (ICMR-INDIAB 2023); Hypertension: 315 million. NPCDCS covers 640 districts but screening-treatment cascade is broken.
  4. Mental Health Neglect: Treatment gap 70-92% (NMHS 2016). Mental Healthcare Act 2017 mandates services at district level — <10% districts have functional Mental Health Review Boards. Budget allocation: <0.5% of health budget.
  5. Regulatory Capture and Quality: Clinical Establishments Act, 2010 adopted by only 11 States/UTs. NABH accreditation: <5% hospitals. Medical Council replaced by NMC (2019) — NEXT exam for licensure not yet operational.
PYQ 2021 GS2 Q5
"The public health system has limitations in providing universal health coverage. Do you think the private sector can help in bridging the gap? What other viable alternatives do you suggest?" (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2021 Q5
  1. Introduction (40 words): UHC = access to quality health services without financial hardship (WHO). India: 47% OOPE, specialist shortfall 74-83% at CHCs, 1.3% GDP on health. Public system necessary but insufficient alone.
  2. Private Sector Role (120 words): Already provides 55% hospital beds, 80% doctors. PMJAY empanels 29,000+ hospitals (55% public). Pros: capacity, technology, reach. Cons: profit motive → over-treatment, upcoding (PMJAY fraud), cherry-picking profitable packages, weak regulation (Clinical Establishments Act not universal). Not a substitute — a *regulated supplement*.
  3. Alternative 1: Strengthen Public Primary Care (100 words): HWCs (1.5 lakh) → CPHC with 12 services. Invest in CHOs (mid-level providers), drug/diagnostic supply, e-Sanjeevani telemedicine. Evidence: Thailand's UHC built on primary care; Brazil's Family Health Strategy. Cost-effective — prevents secondary/tertiary burden.
  4. Alternative 2: Strategic Purchasing & Regulation (100 words): Move from input-based to output-based financing. DRG-based payment (PMJAY moving this way). Regulate prices (NPPA for drugs, stents, knee implants). Clinical Establishments Act mandatory for all States. Standard treatment guidelines (STGs) — NHA developing for PMJAY packages.
  5. Alternative 3: Health Financing Reforms (80 words): 15th FC health grants (₹70,051 crore) to States — conditional on outcomes. Consolidate CSSs into flexible pools. Explore mandatory health insurance (like Thailand's UC Scheme) or employer-based pooling for formal sector. Increase public spending to 2.5% GDP (NHP 2017).
  6. Conclusion (40 words): Private sector is inevitable but must be *strategically purchased* and *tightly regulated*. The backbone of UHC is a strengthened public primary care system — HWCs, CHOs, free drugs/diagnostics, digital integration (ABDM). Federal fiscal transfers (15th FC) must incentivise outcomes, not inputs.

Common Traps

  • Confusing NFSA coverage (75% rural / 50% urban) with universal coverage — NFSA is targeted, not universal.
  • Thinking RTE covers children up to age 18 — it covers ages 6–14 only (Art. 21A); Art. 45 covers ECCE <6 years.
  • Assuming MGNREGA work is guaranteed in urban areas — it is rural only (Art. 243G, 11th Schedule).
  • Missing that Ayushman Bharat targets SECC 2011-deprived families, not all citizens — ~40% population covered.
  • Overlooking that health is primarily a State subject — the Centre supplements through Centrally Sponsored Schemes (NHM, PMJAY).
  • Confusing PMMVY (₹5,000, first child only, conditional) with NFSA maternity benefit (₹6,000, universal, Schedule II) — they are different entitlements.
  • Assuming SCSP/TSP are statutory — they are Planning Commission/NITI Aayog guidelines; only some States enacted laws.
  • Missing that 102nd Amendment (2018) gave NCBC constitutional status but 105th Amendment (2021) restored State power to identify OBCs.
  • Thinking NEP 2020's 5+3+3+4 structure is implemented nationwide — it is a policy framework; States implement at different paces.

Mains Exam-Day Strategy for Social Justice
For social justice questions: (1) Anchor in constitutional provision (Art. 15, 16, 17, 21A, 24, 38, 39, 46, 47); (2) Cite the *Act* (MGNREGA, RTE, NFSA, FRA, PoA Act, RPwD Act, Mental Healthcare Act) — distinguish statutory rights from executive schemes; (3) Use *recent data sparingly and precisely* (NFHS-5, PLFS 2022-23, NITI MPI 2023, SRS 2020, NHA 2019-20); (4) Cite Supreme Court judgments (NALSA 2014, Paschim Banga 1996, Indra Sawhney 1992, T.S.R. Subramanian 2013, Justice K.S. Puttaswamy 2017 for Aadhaar-welfare link); (5) End with *implementation gaps* — exclusion errors, Aadhaar authentication failures, fund underutilisation, vacancy in constitutional bodies, federal asymmetry — to show critical thinking.
