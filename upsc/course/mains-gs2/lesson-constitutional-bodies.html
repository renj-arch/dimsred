Constitutional Framework for Independent Bodies

1. Constitutional Provisions Establishing Independent Bodies

The Constitution of India creates a set of institutions that are insulated from day‑to‑day executive control by embedding their composition, tenure and removal procedures in the constitutional text itself. Articles 324, 148, 315, 280, 338 and 338A respectively provide for the Election Commission, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Union Public Service Commission, the Finance Commission, the National Commission for Scheduled Castes and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, while the National Human Rights Commission and the National Commission for Women owe their existence to parliamentary statutes enacted under the enabling provisions of Article 323B. By fixing the method of appointment — usually by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers but often requiring consultation with the Chief Justice of India or a collegium — the Constitution seeks to balance democratic accountability with functional independence.

2. Rationale for Institutional Autonomy

Autonomy is not an end in itself; it is a means to ensure that decisions affecting elections, public finance, civil service recruitment and human rights are taken on the basis of legal criteria rather than political expediency. When the executive can appoint or remove the head of a watchdog at will, the watchdog’s deterrent effect collapses, leading to a decline in public trust and a weakening of the checks‑and‑balances architecture that underpins a parliamentary democracy. The Constituent Assembly debates reveal that members such as B. R. Ambedkar and K. M. Munshi explicitly argued for “independent constitutional authorities” to prevent the concentration of power in the hands of the majority party.

3. Classification of Constitutional and Statutory Bodies

Constitutional bodies derive their authority directly from the Constitution and can be altered only by a constitutional amendment, whereas statutory bodies are created by an Act of Parliament and can be amended or abolished by ordinary legislation. This distinction has practical consequences: the President’s power to remove a constitutional authority is circumscribed by the procedure laid down in the Constitution (for example, impeachment for the CAG), while a statutory body’s chairperson can be removed by the government following the procedure prescribed in the enabling Act. Election Commission of India

4. Composition and Appointment Mechanism

The Election Commission consists of a Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and such number of Election Commissioners as the President may from time to time fix; since 1993 the convention has been a three‑member body. The President appoints the CEC and the other Commissioners on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the Supreme Court in the 2009 judgment Election Commission of India v. Union of India held that the consultation with the Chief Justice of India is mandatory for the appointment of the CEC, thereby introducing a judicial element into an otherwise executive process. The term of office is six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, and removal requires a process akin to impeachment, reinforcing security of tenure.

5. Powers, Functions and Electoral Reforms

Article 324 vests the Commission with superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of all elections to Parliament, State Legislatures and the offices of President and Vice‑President. Over the decades the Commission has exercised this mandate to introduce electronic voting machines, the Model Code of Conduct, voter‑verified paper audit trails and the NOTA option. Each reform has been a response to a specific defect — booth capturing, impersonation, lack of transparency — and has been upheld by the courts as a legitimate exercise of the Commission’s plenary powers.

6. Challenges to Autonomy and Recent Judicial Pronouncements

Despite constitutional safeguards, the Commission has faced allegations of partisan behaviour, especially regarding the timing of elections and the enforcement of the Model Code. In 2022 the Supreme Court, in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India, directed the government to consider a collegium‑based appointment mechanism comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and the Chief Justice of India, signalling a judicial push for greater institutional insulation. The Court also emphasised that the Commission’s independence is a basic feature of the Constitution, making any dilution subject to strict scrutiny.

Example Question
Explain the constitutional safeguards that protect the autonomy of the Election Commission of India. Discuss how the Supreme Court has interpreted these safeguards in recent judgments.
The Constitution secures the Election Commission’s autonomy through three principal safeguards. First, Article 324(2) fixes the tenure of the Chief Election Commissioner and other Commissioners at six years or until the age of 65, insulating them from arbitrary removal. Second, the removal procedure mirrors that of a Supreme Court judge — requiring a motion passed by a two‑thirds majority in each House of Parliament on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity — which makes political dismissal extremely difficult. Third, the Supreme Court in Election Commission of India v. Union of India (2009) read a mandatory consultation with the Chief Justice of India into the appointment process, thereby introducing a judicial check on executive discretion. In Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2022) the Court went further, recommending a collegium comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and the Chief Justice of India for future appointments, and declared the Commission’s independence a basic feature of the Constitution. Together, these provisions and judicial interpretations create a layered protection that limits executive influence while preserving accountability through parliamentary oversight.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “constitutional safeguards”, list the specific articles (Art. 324, Art. 324(2), removal procedure) and then cite the two landmark Supreme Court cases (2009 and 2022) — this combination of text and case law fetches the maximum marks.

Comptroller and Auditor General of India

7. Constitutional Mandate and Appointment Process

Article 148 establishes the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) as the supreme audit institution of the country. The CAG is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal, and the Constitution prescribes that the CAG can be removed only in the manner and on the grounds applicable to a Supreme Court judge — that is, by an address of each House of Parliament supported by a two‑thirds majority. This rigorous removal procedure, coupled with a fixed term of six years or until the age of 65, is designed to shield the auditor from executive pressure while ensuring accountability to Parliament.

8. Audit Jurisdiction and Reporting Mechanism

The CAG’s audit jurisdiction extends to all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and the Contingency Fund and Public Account. The audit reports are submitted to the President or the Governor, as the case may be, who causes them to be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature. The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) then examines these reports, holding ministries and departments accountable for financial irregularities. This institutional chain — CAG → President/Governor → Legislature → PAC — constitutes the core of financial accountability in India’s parliamentary system.

9. Independence, Accountability and Emerging Issues

While the constitutional design grants the CAG considerable independence, concerns have arisen regarding the adequacy of resources, the timeliness of reports and the perceived reluctance to audit certain high‑profile defence and security expenditures. The 2014 Supreme Court judgment in CAG v. Union of India affirmed that the CAG’s audit mandate includes performance audit and that the executive cannot withhold information on grounds of secrecy unless a specific law so provides. Recent debates focus on whether the CAG should be brought under a multi‑member collegium for appointments, similar to the Election Commission, to further insulate the office from political influence.

Union Public Service Commission

10. Structure, Appointment and Tenure

Article 315 provides for a Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) and a State Public Service Commission for each State. The UPSC consists of a Chairman and not more than ten other members, all appointed by the President. The Constitution stipulates that at least half of the members must have held office for at least ten years under the Government of India or a State, ensuring administrative experience. Members hold office for a term of six years or until the age of 65, and can be removed only by the President on the ground of misbehaviour after an inquiry by the Supreme Court, mirroring the safeguards applicable to the CAG.

11. Role in Recruitment, Promotion and Disciplinary Matters

The UPSC conducts examinations for appointment to the All India Services and Central Services, advises the President on matters relating to methods of recruitment, principles of promotion and transfer, and disciplinary cases affecting civil servants. Its recommendations are binding on the government unless the President, after consulting the Commission, decides otherwise — a provision that preserves the executive’s ultimate authority while ensuring that the Commission’s expert advice is formally recorded. The Commission also frames the syllabi and conducts the Civil Services Examination, which is the gateway to the Indian Administrative Service, Indian Foreign Service and Indian Police Service.

12. Safeguards against Executive Interference

The security of tenure, the requirement of a Supreme Court inquiry for removal, and the obligation of the government to consult the Commission on all personnel matters constitute a robust shield against arbitrary executive action. In practice, however, the government has occasionally delayed the implementation of UPSC recommendations on promotions, prompting the Commission to issue public statements. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. R. K. Jain (1993) reiterated that the government’s power to reject UPSC advice must be exercised sparingly and with recorded reasons, reinforcing the Commission’s advisory primacy.

13. State Public Service Commissions and Joint Public Service Commissions

Article 315 requires a Public Service Commission for the Union and a Public Service Commission for each State, and permits two or more States, by agreement, to have a Joint State Public Service Commission. The Union Public Service Commission is thus part of a family of commissions built on a common constitutional design. A State Public Service Commission is constituted by the Governor, and its members hold office, under Article 316, for six years or until the age of sixty-two years, whichever is earlier; the chairman and members may be removed only by the President on the grounds mentioned in Article 317. The State commissions conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the State, assist in framing recruitment rules, advise on promotions and transfers, and advise on disciplinary matters. Their recommendations are advisory, but the Constitution requires that any departure from them be reported to the legislature with the reasons. Together, these commissions are the constitutional machinery through which recruitment to the civil services is kept at a distance from the political executive.

Finance Commission

13. Constitutional Basis and Composition

Article 280 mandates the constitution of a Finance Commission every five years, or at such earlier time as the President considers necessary. The Commission comprises a Chairman and four other members appointed by the President. The Chairman is required to have experience in public affairs, while the members are selected from among persons who have had experience in finance, administration or economics. The Commission’s recommendations cover the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States, the principles governing grants‑in‑aid, and any other matter referred to it by the President in the interest of sound finance.

14. Terms of Reference and Devolution Formula

Each Finance Commission receives a Terms of Reference (ToR) from the President, which outlines the specific issues to be addressed — such as the vertical and horizontal devolution shares, the treatment of cess and surcharge, and the incentivisation of demographic performance. The Commission devises a formula that assigns weights to population, area, forest cover, income distance and fiscal discipline. For instance, the Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) assigned 15 % weight to demographic performance, 45 % to income distance, 15 % to area, 10 % to forest and ecology, and 15 % to tax effort, reflecting a shift towards rewarding fiscal responsibility.

15. Impact on Fiscal Federalism and Recent Recommendations

The Finance Commission is the principal instrument of fiscal federalism in India; its awards determine the resource envelope available to States for development expenditure. The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑20) increased the States’ share in the divisible pool from 32 % to 42 %, a landmark devolution that enhanced State autonomy but also raised concerns about the Union’s capacity to fund national schemes. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended a 41 % share, a slight reduction justified by the Union’s increased liabilities on account of the Goods and Services Tax compensation and the COVID‑19 pandemic. These recommendations are not binding until accepted by the President, but in practice they are implemented almost wholly, underscoring the Commission’s pivotal role in inter‑governmental fiscal relations.

National Human Rights Commission and National Commission for Women

16. Statutory Origin, Composition and Mandate

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) was established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, while the National Commission for Women (NCW) was constituted under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990. The NHRC consists of a Chairperson who must be a former Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, four full‑time members (including one woman) and ex‑officio members such as the Chairpersons of the National Commissions for Minorities, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The NCW comprises a Chairperson, five members and a Member‑Secretary, all appointed by the Central Government. Both bodies are mandated to inquire into violations, recommend remedial measures, review safeguards and promote awareness of human rights and women’s rights respectively.

17. Investigative Powers, Recommendations and Limitations

The NHRC enjoys powers of a civil court — summoning witnesses, requiring production of documents and receiving evidence on affidavits — but its recommendations are not binding on the government. The Commission can approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for enforcement of its recommendations, yet the lack of prosecutorial authority limits its effectiveness. The NCW, similarly, can investigate complaints, conduct inspections of custodial institutions and recommend policy changes, but it cannot initiate criminal proceedings. Both commissions rely on moral authority and media attention to pressure authorities into compliance.

18. Interaction with Courts and Civil Society

The judiciary has frequently invoked NHRC reports in public interest litigation, treating them as credible evidence of systemic violations. In NHRC v. State of Gujarat (2009) the Supreme Court directed the State to implement the Commission’s recommendations on custodial deaths, illustrating the potential for judicial enforcement. Civil society organisations routinely collaborate with both commissions for fact‑finding missions, capacity building and advocacy, creating a feedback loop that amplifies the commissions’ impact despite their statutory limitations.

Attorney General of India and Advocate General

The Attorney General (Article 76)

Article 76 provides that the President shall appoint a person who is qualified to be appointed a Judge of the Supreme Court to be the Attorney General of India. The Attorney General is the highest law officer of the Union. The qualification is the same as that for a Supreme Court judge: the person must be a citizen of India and must have been a Judge of a High Court for five years, an advocate of a High Court for ten years, or a distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President. The office is a public office, not a judgeship, and the holder has no security of tenure comparable to that of a judge.

Duties and Rights of the Attorney General

The Attorney General advises the Government of India upon such legal matters and performs such other duties of a legal character as may be referred or assigned. In the performance of these duties the holder has the right of audience in all courts in India and the right to take part in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament and their committees, though without a right to vote. The holder is not a member of either House and is not a member of the Cabinet, but is entitled to all the privileges and immunities of a member of Parliament. The Attorney General appears for the Union in important constitutional cases, and the office is pivotal in the President's references to the Supreme Court under Article 143. The Attorney General is assisted by the Solicitor General of India and the Additional Solicitors General, who hold non-constitutional, statutory posts and are appointed by the Union government.

The Advocate General of a State (Article 165)

Article 165 provides for an Advocate General for each State, appointed by the Governor, who must be qualified to be appointed a Judge of a High Court. The Advocate General is the chief law officer of the State and discharges functions analogous to those of the Attorney General at the Union level: advising the State government on legal matters, appearing on its behalf in the courts, and having the right to take part in the proceedings of the State Legislature and its committees without a right to vote. The Advocate General holds office during the pleasure of the Governor. The office mirrors the Attorney General's role, and the relationship between the two is one of parallel, not hierarchical, authority.

Independence and Accountability

The Attorney General occupies an ambiguous position in the constitutional scheme. The holder is the government's chief legal adviser, yet is expected to act with independence and to guide the court honestly on the state of the law. Because the office depends on the pleasure of the executive and does not enjoy the security of tenure of a judge, critics argue that it is vulnerable to political pressure; defenders point to the professional standing and conventions that have historically sustained its independence. The same tension runs through the office of the Advocate General in the States. For the examination, the enduring points are the Article 76 and Article 165 provisions, the qualification of eligibility to be a Supreme Court or High Court judge, the right of audience and of participation in the legislature without a vote, and the office's status as a law officer distinct from the judiciary.

Statutory and Regulatory Bodies: NITI Aayog, NHRC, CIC, CVC, Lokpal and the CBI

Constitutional, Statutory and Executive Bodies

Bodies that exercise public power in India fall into three broad classes. A constitutional body is created by the Constitution itself and derives its powers from a named Article: the Election Commission, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Union Public Service Commission, the Finance Commission and the Attorney General are examples. A statutory body is created by an Act of Parliament or of a State legislature, which defines its composition, powers and functions. An executive body is created by a resolution of the government and depends on the executive for its existence. The distinction matters because it determines a body's independence: a constitutional body's powers can be changed only by amendment, a statutory body's by ordinary legislation, and an executive body's by the government of the day.

NITI Aayog

The National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog) replaced the Planning Commission on 1 January 2015. It is an executive body, not a statutory one, created by a Cabinet resolution. The Prime Minister chairs it; a Vice-Chairperson and a Chief Executive Officer head its professional staff; and a Governing Council of the Chief Ministers of all States and the Lieutenant Governors of Union Territories gives it a federal character. Unlike the Planning Commission, which allocated funds to States, NITI Aayog is a policy think tank: it prepares strategy documents, evaluates schemes, and promotes cooperative and competitive federalism through initiatives such as the Sustainable Development Goals index and the Aspirational Districts Programme.

National Human Rights Commission

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) is a statutory body established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. It may take cognisance of complaints of human rights violations, inquire into them suo motu or on a petition, visit jails and other institutions to study living conditions, review the safeguards provided by law, and recommend remedial measures. Its recommendations are not binding, it cannot punish anyone, and it cannot inquire directly into complaints against the armed forces, where it may only seek a report from the Central government. This is the origin of the criticism that the NHRC is a "toothless tiger"; its defenders reply that its inquiries and reports shape public opinion and government action. The NHRC has a Chairperson, usually a former Chief Justice or judge of the Supreme Court, and members drawn from the judiciary and civil society.

Central Information Commission and the RTI

The Central Information Commission (CIC) is a statutory body established under the Right to Information Act, 2005. It hears appeals against the decisions of Public Information Officers and First Appellate Authorities, and its powers include summoning witnesses, requiring the production of documents, and imposing penalties on officers who refuse information without reasonable cause. The Chief Information Commissioner and the Information Commissioners are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee. The RTI framework, and with it the CIC, is the principal statutory instrument for transparency and accountability in governance.

Central Vigilance Commission and the CBI

The Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) was set up in 1964 on the recommendation of the Santhanam Committee and was given statutory status by the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. It is the apex body for vigilance in the Union government, exercising superintendence over the Delhi Special Police Establishment (the CBI) in matters relating to the prevention of corruption. The Central Bureau of Investigation itself is not a statutory body: it was established by a resolution of the Home Ministry in 1963 and derives its legal authority from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. Because its superintendence is shared between the CVC, the Department of Personnel and the government, the Supreme Court once described the CBI as a "caged parrot speaking in its master's voice", a remark at the centre of the debate about the agency's independence.

Lokpal and Lokayuktas

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, created the office of Lokpal at the Union level and provided for Lokayuktas in the States. The Lokpal consists of a Chairperson, who may be a former Chief Justice or judge of the Supreme Court or an eminent person, and judicial and non-judicial members, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a selection committee. Its jurisdiction extends to the Prime Minister, with safeguards for matters relating to national security and foreign relations, to Ministers, Members of Parliament, and to Group A, B, C and D officers, as well as to the boards and officials of public sector undertakings and societies. The Lokpal may order a preliminary inquiry or an investigation, and the investigating agency is required to report to it. The Act followed a nationwide anti-corruption movement and remains the most ambitious statutory experiment in creating an ombudsman for the Union.

Debate on Appointments, Autonomy and Accountability

19. Political Executive versus Collegium Models

The dominant mode of appointment for constitutional bodies remains the executive‑driven process, where the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Critics argue that this concentrates patronage power in the ruling party, undermining the independence of institutions such as the Election Commission, CAG and UPSC. An alternative collegium model — comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and the Chief Justice of India — has been proposed by the Supreme Court and by various law commissions. Proponents contend that a multi‑stakeholder collegium would dilute partisan influence, while opponents warn that it could judicialise executive functions and create deadlocks.

20. Judicial Review of Appointment Processes

The Supreme Court has progressively expanded the scope of judicial review over appointments. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) the Court struck down the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, reaffirming the primacy of the judiciary in judicial appointments. By analogy, the Court’s 2009 and 2022 rulings on the Election Commission signal a willingness to read constitutional silence as an invitation to impose procedural safeguards. However, the Court has stopped short of mandating a specific collegium for all constitutional bodies, leaving the design of appointment mechanisms to Parliament, subject to the basic structure doctrine.

21. Reform Proposals and the Way Forward

Several expert committees — the Venkatachaliah Commission (2002), the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2007) and the Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) — have recommended a uniform, transparent appointment framework for all independent constitutional authorities. Common elements include a search‑committee, a bipartisan parliamentary committee for scrutiny, fixed tenures, and a clear removal procedure requiring a super‑majority. Implementing these reforms would require either a constitutional amendment or a consensus‑driven legislative framework, both of which demand sustained political will. Until such reforms materialise, the health of India’s institutional architecture will continue to depend on the interplay between constitutional text, judicial interpretation and the political culture of the day.

National Commission for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes: Constitutional Safeguards and Functional Overlap

The Constitution provides three distinct commissions — the National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC) under Article 338, the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST) under Article 338A, and the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) under Article 338B (inserted by the 102nd Amendment, 2018). Each is a constitutional body with a Chairperson, Vice‑Chairperson and three other members appointed by the President. Their core mandate is to investigate and monitor safeguards, inquire into specific complaints, and advise on the socio‑economic development of the respective communities.

Despite separate constitutional articles, functional overlap is significant. All three commissions share identical investigative powers: they can summon witnesses, require production of documents, and receive evidence on affidavits. Their reports are laid before Parliament and the concerned State Legislatures, and the government must place a memorandum explaining action taken. The NCBC, however, has an additional role: it examines requests for inclusion or exclusion of castes in the Central List of Other Backward Classes and advises the President on the matter, a function absent in the NCSC and NCST.

A practical tension arises when a community claims both Scheduled Caste and Other Backward Class status. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Milind (2000) held that the President’s notification under Article 341 is final for SC status, while the NCBC’s recommendation under Article 338B governs OBC inclusion. The Court clarified that the two processes operate in parallel but cannot be used to circumvent the constitutional ceiling of 50 % reservation (Indra Sawhney, 1992). This jurisprudence forces the commissions to coordinate informally, though no statutory mechanism for joint sittings exists.

Recent amendments have strengthened the NCBC. The 105th Amendment (2021) restored State powers to identify socially and educationally backward classes, effectively making the NCBC’s advisory role more consultative than determinative for State lists. Meanwhile, the NCSC and NCST have been criticised for delayed reports; the 2022 Annual Report of the NCSC was tabled in Parliament only in 2024, raising questions about institutional efficacy.

For the aspirant, the key takeaway is the constitutional architecture: three parallel bodies with overlapping investigative powers but distinct advisory jurisdictions, and the Supreme Court’s role in delineating their boundaries to preserve the reservation ceiling.

State Election Commissions and the 73rd/74th Constitutional Amendments: Autonomy in Local Body Elections

The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) inserted Part IX and Part IXA, mandating the creation of State Election Commissions (SECs) under Articles 243K and 243ZA respectively. Each SEC consists of a State Election Commissioner appointed by the Governor, with conditions of service and tenure determined by the State Legislature. The Commissioner can be removed only in the manner and on the grounds prescribed for a Judge of a High Court, providing a constitutional shield against arbitrary dismissal.

Unlike the Election Commission of India (ECI), the SEC’s jurisdiction is limited to elections to Panchayats and Municipalities. However, the Supreme Court in Kishan Singh Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of Delhi (2006) held that the SEC enjoys the same degree of independence as the ECI in matters of superintendence, direction and control of electoral rolls and conduct of elections. The Court emphasised that the Governor’s appointment power is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, but the removal safeguard ensures functional autonomy.

State Legislatures have enacted diverse laws governing SECs. For instance, the Maharashtra State Election Commission Act, 1994 provides for a five‑year term or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, while the Kerala Panchayat Raj Act, 1994 sets a six‑year term. Such variation leads to asymmetry in institutional stability across States. Moreover, several States have attempted to curtail SEC powers by amending local body laws — e.g., the 2018 amendment in Haryana that gave the State Government power to delimit wards, a function the Supreme Court later struck down as violating Article 243K.

A recurring issue is the delimitation of wards and reservation of seats for SCs, STs and women. The SECs are required to follow the Delimitation Commission’s orders, but in practice, State Governments often delay the constitution of Delimitation Commissions, forcing SECs to conduct elections on outdated boundaries. The 2021 judgment in State of Uttar Pradesh v. State Election Commission directed States to complete delimitation at least six months before the expiry of the local body’s term.

For examination purposes, aspirants should note the constitutional parallel between ECI and SEC, the removal safeguard mirroring High Court judges, and the judicial insistence on insulating SECs from executive interference in delimitation and reservation matters.

Technique: Comparative Constitutional Analysis
Question: Compare the constitutional safeguards available to the Election Commission of India and a State Election Commission with respect to appointment, tenure and removal. Explain how the Supreme Court has interpreted these safeguards to protect institutional autonomy.
Answer: Both the ECI (Art. 324) and SEC (Art. 243K/243ZA) are headed by a Commissioner appointed by the President/Governor respectively. The ECI’s Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners have a fixed six‑year term or up to age 65, removable only like a Supreme Court Judge (Art. 324(5)). The SEC’s term and conditions are set by State law, but removal requires the same procedure as a High Court Judge (Art. 243K(2)). The Supreme Court in Kishan Singh Tomar (2006) equated the SEC’s independence to that of the ECI, holding that the Governor’s appointment on ministerial advice does not diminish the constitutional removal shield. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. State Election Commission (2021), the Court reinforced that executive interference in delimitation violates the SEC’s superintendence power. Thus, while the ECI enjoys uniform central legislation, the SEC’s autonomy is constitutionally guaranteed but legislatively variable, with the judiciary acting as the ultimate guarantor.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “compare and contrast”, structure the answer in a two‑column table (mental or on paper) covering appointment, tenure, removal, jurisdiction and judicial pronouncements. Cite at least one landmark case for each body; the examiner rewards precise case names over generic statements.

Public Accounts Committee, Committee on Public Undertakings and the CAG: Parliamentary Oversight Mechanisms

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) submits three categories of audit reports — on the Union Government, State Governments, and Public Sector Undertakings — to the President or Governor, who cause them to be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature. The legislative scrutiny of these reports is primarily performed by two standing committees: the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). Both are constituted under the Rules of Procedure of Lok Sabha (and analogous rules in State Legislatures) and consist of members elected by proportional representation, ensuring opposition representation.

The PAC examines the appropriation accounts and the audit reports on revenue and expenditure. It does not question the policy of the government but focuses on whether funds were spent legally, economically and for the intended purpose. The COPU, on the other hand, scrutinises the reports of the CAG on public sector enterprises, assessing their financial performance, autonomy and adherence to government policy. The CAG acts as the “friend, philosopher and guide” to both committees, providing technical assistance during examinations.

A critical procedural safeguard is that the CAG’s reports are not debated in the House; they are referred to the committees, which then submit their own reports with recommendations. The government is required to furnish Action Taken Reports (ATRs) within six months. However, compliance is uneven. The 2022 PAC report on the Union Budget noted that only 68 % of recommendations had been fully implemented, with the remainder either partially accepted or pending.

The relationship between the CAG and these committees has been shaped by judicial pronouncements. In Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010), the Supreme Court held that the CAG’s audit jurisdiction extends to all bodies substantially financed by the government, including NGOs receiving grants. This expanded the PAC’s and COPU’s remit. Conversely, the Court in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2018) clarified that the CAG cannot audit policy decisions, preserving the separation between audit and executive discretion.

For the aspirant, the essential points are: (1) the CAG’s reports feed into PAC and COPU; (2) both committees are bipartisan and derive authority from parliamentary rules, not statute; (3) the CAG’s role is advisory to the committees, not adjudicative; (4) judicial decisions have broadened audit scope but maintained the policy‑audit boundary.

The Office of the Governor in Appointment and Removal of State‑Level Constitutional Authorities

The Governor, as the constitutional head of the State, plays a pivotal role in the appointment and removal of State‑level constitutional authorities — the State Election Commissioner, the Chairman and Members of the State Public Service Commission (SPSC), the State Finance Commission, and the State Human Rights Commission (where constituted). Articles 243K, 316, 243I and 338B respectively vest the appointment power in the Governor, exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers.

While the appointment process mirrors the Union model, the removal safeguards differ. The State Election Commissioner can be removed only in the manner and on grounds prescribed for a High Court Judge (Art. 243K(2)). The SPSC Chairman and Members enjoy a similar protection under Article 317, requiring a reference to the Supreme Court for misbehaviour or incapacity. The State Finance Commission’s Chairman and Members hold office for a term specified by the Governor, but removal follows the same procedure as a High Court Judge (Art. 243I(2)). The State Human Rights Commission, being statutory, follows the removal procedure in the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, which also mandates a Supreme Court reference.

Judicial scrutiny has reinforced these safeguards. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2015), the Supreme Court struck down a State law that allowed the Chief Minister to remove the State Election Commissioner by a simple majority resolution, holding it violated Article 243K. Similarly, in Hargovind Pant v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2020), the Court quashed the removal of an SPSC Member without a Supreme Court reference, reiterating that the Governor’s pleasure is not absolute but constitutionally conditioned.

A practical nuance is the Governor’s discretion in the absence of a Council of Ministers (President’s Rule). During President’s Rule, the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers, effectively centralising appointments. The 1994 S. R. Bommai judgment limited the duration of President’s Rule, indirectly protecting the autonomy of State constitutional appointments from prolonged central interference.

For examination, remember: the Governor’s appointment power is formal; real authority lies with the State Cabinet; removal safeguards are constitutionally entrenched and judicially enforced; President’s Rule temporarily shifts the appointment axis to the Centre.

Judicial Doctrines Shaping Institutional Autonomy: Basic Structure, Doctrine of Pleasure, and the Principle of Institutional Integrity

The Supreme Court has developed three interlocking doctrines that define the constitutional space for independent bodies. The Basic Structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) establishes that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution so as to destroy the “institutional framework” that includes an independent Election Commission, CAG, and judiciary. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015), the Court held that the independence of the Election Commission is part of the basic structure, thereby immunising its core functions from legislative dilution.

The Doctrine of Pleasure, derived from Article 310, provides that civil servants hold office during the pleasure of the President or Governor. However, constitutional authorities such as the CAG, UPSC Members, and Election Commissioners are expressly excluded from this doctrine by specific constitutional provisions (Arts. 148, 316, 324). The Court in Union of India v. T. R. Verma (1957) clarified that “pleasure” cannot be exercised arbitrarily against constitutional office‑holders; it must conform to the removal procedure prescribed in the Constitution.

The Principle of Institutional Integrity, articulated in R. Gandhi v. Union of India (2010) and reiterated in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020), requires that the functional independence of an institution be preserved not only in law but also in practice. The Court has used this principle to strike down executive orders that circumvent statutory safeguards — for example, the 2018 amendment to the CVC Act that allowed the Government to appoint the CVC without the Leader of Opposition’s concurrence was read down as violating institutional integrity.

These doctrines interact: the Basic Structure sets the outer constitutional limit; the Doctrine of Pleasure defines the default executive control, which is overridden by specific constitutional provisions; Institutional Integrity operates as a judicial tool to enforce the spirit of those provisions against subtle executive encroachments. Together they form a layered defence that aspirants must recognise when analysing any challenge to the autonomy of a constitutional or statutory body.

  • Confusing the removal procedure of the State Election Commissioner with that of the Chief Election Commissioner — the former follows High Court Judge procedure, the latter follows Supreme Court Judge procedure.
  • Assuming the National Commission for Backward Classes has the same constitutional status as the NCSC and NCST before the 102nd Amendment; it was statutory until 2018.
  • Overlooking that the Public Accounts Committee and Committee on Public Undertakings derive authority from parliamentary rules, not from a statute, and therefore their composition can be altered by a simple majority in the House.
  • Treating the Governor’s appointment power as discretionary; it is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except during President’s Rule.
  • Misapplying the Doctrine of Pleasure to constitutional authorities explicitly excluded from it, such as the CAG and UPSC Members.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the Basic Structure doctrine (a limit on amendment power) and Institutional Integrity (a judicial enforcement principle for existing provisions).
  • Failing to cite the specific Supreme Court case that equated State Election Commission autonomy with the Election Commission of India (Kishan Singh Tomar, 2006).

These advanced sections complete the institutional autonomy framework required for high‑level analysis in the examination.

Comparative Appointment Mechanisms of Constitutional Authorities

The Constitution prescribes distinct appointment routes for each constitutional authority, reflecting a calibrated balance between executive initiative and institutional insulation. The Election Commission (EC) is constituted under Article 324; the President appoints the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and Election Commissioners on the advice of the Prime Minister, but the 2015 Supreme Court judgment in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India read a collegium‑like requirement into the process, mandating consultation with the Leader of Opposition (LoP) and the Chief Justice of India (CJI) for the CEC. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) under Article 148 is appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister, yet the Court in Union of India v. T. R. Verma (1957) held that the “pleasure” doctrine does not apply; removal follows the same procedure as a Supreme Court judge. The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) under Article 316 sees the President appoint the Chairman and members on the advice of the Prime Minister, with a mandatory consultation with the CJI for the Chairman. The Finance Commission (FC) under Article 280 is constituted by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister; its Chairman and members are selected by a search‑committee headed by the Cabinet Secretary, but the final appointment remains executive. The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) and National Commission for Women (NCW) are statutory; the President appoints the Chairperson and members on the recommendation of a high‑level committee comprising the Prime Minister, LoP, Speaker, and the Home Minister (NHRC) or the Minister for Women and Child Development (NCW). Parliamentary Committee Oversight of Statutory Regulators

Parliament exercises post‑appointment oversight through a network of department‑related standing committees (DRSCs), financial committees, and specialised committees such as the Committee on Subordinate Legislation and the Committee on Papers Laid on the Table. The DRSC on Finance, for example, examines the annual reports of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), and the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority (IRDAI), calling the Governor or Chairperson for evidence. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation scrutinises every rule, regulation, or notification issued by statutory regulators under delegated legislation powers, ensuring they do not exceed the parent Act’s scope. The Committee on Papers Laid on the Table reviews the annual reports and audit reports of bodies like the Central Vigilance Commission (CVC), Central Information Commission (CIC), and the Lokpal, flagging delays or non‑compliance. The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) rely on the CAG’s audit reports to question the financial propriety of regulators that operate commercial wings (e.g., the National Highways Authority of India). In 2019, the Standing Committee on Finance recommended a statutory cooling‑off period for RBI Deputy Governors before they join private banks, illustrating how committee recommendations can shape regulatory autonomy. The oversight cycle typically follows: (1) regulator submits annual report; (2) committee examines report and summons officials; (3) committee tables recommendations; (4) ministry responds within six months. This institutionalised dialogue mitigates executive capture while preserving the regulator’s operational independence.

Examiner's twist
A question asks: “Which parliamentary committee is primarily responsible for examining whether a regulator’s subordinate legislation conforms to its enabling Act?” The options include PAC, DRSC on Finance, Committee on Subordinate Legislation, and Committee on Estimates.
The correct answer is the Committee on Subordinate Legislation. This committee’s mandate under the Rules of Procedure of Lok Sabha (Rule 331) and Rajya Sabha (Rule 210) is to scrutinise every rule, regulation, bye‑law, or order made under delegated legislative authority. PAC focuses on audit expenditure; DRSC on Finance reviews policy and performance; Committee on Estimates examines budgetary estimates. Hence, the Committee on Subordinate Legislation is the specialised body for conformity checks.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the specific rule numbers (Lok Sabha Rule 331, Rajya Sabha Rule 210) for the Committee on Subordinate Legislation; they are frequently quoted in “match the following” questions. Also, remember the sequence: annual report → committee examination → recommendations → ministry response. This flow appears in case‑study style questions on regulatory accountability.

Judicial Standards of Review for Decisions of Independent Bodies

When the Supreme Court or High Courts review decisions of constitutional or statutory bodies, they apply a graduated spectrum of scrutiny. The traditional Wednesbury unreasonableness test — whether a decision is so absurd that no reasonable authority could have reached it — remains the baseline for administrative actions. However, for bodies exercising quasi‑judicial or constitutional functions, the Court has elevated the standard to proportionality, requiring that the measure be suitable, necessary, and balanced in its impact on fundamental rights. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2009), the Court held that the NHRC’s recommendation to prosecute a public servant must satisfy proportionality, not merely Wednesbury. The principle of legitimate expectation, articulated in Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010), protects individuals who have been led to believe a particular procedure will be followed; a sudden departure without reasoned justification violates institutional integrity. Procedural fairness, reinforced in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020), mandates that bodies like the Election Commission provide a hearing before cancelling a party’s symbol. The Court distinguishes between constitutional bodies (EC, CAG, UPSC) and statutory bodies (NHRC, CIC, CVC): the former enjoy a higher threshold of judicial deference because their independence is constitutionally entrenched, while the latter are subject to stricter proportionality analysis given their statutory origin. Aspirants should be able to map each landmark case to the specific standard it crystallised and explain why the Court upgrades or downgrades scrutiny based on the body’s constitutional pedigree.

Emerging Autonomy Challenges in Digital Regulatory Frameworks

The enactment of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) created the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) as an adjudicatory body, while the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) and the proposed Digital Communications Commission continue to regulate spectrum and infrastructure. These entities share a common vulnerability: their chairpersons and members are appointed by the Central Government on the recommendation of a search‑committee dominated by senior bureaucrats, with no mandatory parliamentary or judicial participation. The DPDP Act empowers the Central Government to prescribe the Board’s terms of service, removal grounds, and even its budget, raising concerns of executive overreach akin to the pre‑2018 CVC amendment. In 2023, the Supreme Court in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (the second privacy judgment) emphasised that any data‑protection authority must enjoy “functional independence” comparable to the Election Commission, citing the principle of institutional integrity. Simultaneously, the Right to Information (RTI) regime, overseen by the Central Information Commission (CIC), faces pressure from the 2019 amendment that allowed the Government to unilaterally decide the tenure and salary of Information Commissioners, a move later read down by the Court for violating the statutory scheme. The convergence of digital governance — data protection, cyber security, platform regulation — demands a unified appointment architecture: a bipartite collegium comprising the CJI (or a senior Supreme Court judge) and the LoP, mirrored on the EC model, coupled with a fixed, non‑renewable term and a removal process requiring a Supreme Court inquiry. Until such structural reforms are legislated, the autonomy of digital regulators remains contingent on executive goodwill, a point examiners frequently test through scenario‑based questions on “institutional capture”.

  • Confusing the consultation requirement for the CEC (LoP + CJI) with that for Election Commissioners (PM + LoP only).
  • Assuming the Finance Commission’s removal procedure mirrors that of the CAG; it is governed by Article 280 and does not require a Supreme Court inquiry.
  • Attributing the Wednesbury test to constitutional bodies like the EC; the Court applies proportionality and institutional integrity instead.
  • Overlooking that the Committee on Subordinate Legislation, not the PAC, reviews delegated legislation of regulators.
  • Treating the Data Protection Board as a constitutional body; it is statutory and its autonomy derives solely from the DPDP Act.
  • Neglecting the 2023 Supreme Court observation that digital regulators must meet the EC’s independence benchmark.
  • Remember to cross‑check each trap against the latest judicial pronouncements.

National Commission for Minorities and National Commission for Protection of Child Rights: Statutory Mandate, Composition, and Functional Overlap

The National Commission for Minorities (NCM) was constituted under the National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992. It comprises a Chairperson, a Vice‑Chairperson and five members, all appointed by the Central Government. The Act mandates the Commission to evaluate the working of safeguards for minorities in the Constitution and laws, recommend measures for effective implementation, and look into specific complaints regarding deprivation of rights. The NCM’s jurisdiction extends to six notified religious minorities — Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis and Jains — and linguistic minorities as defined by the President under Article 350B.

The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) derives its authority from the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005. Its composition includes a Chairperson and six members, at least two of whom must be women, appointed by the Central Government on the recommendation of a selection committee headed by the Minister for Women and Child Development. The NCPCR’s mandate is broader: it monitors the implementation of the Convention on the Rights of the Child, the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009. It can inquire suo motu or on complaints into violations of child rights and recommend remedial action.

Functional overlap arises primarily in the protection of minority children. For instance, the NCM’s 2018 report on the educational status of Muslim children highlighted gaps that fall squarely within the NCPCR’s mandate under the RTE Act. Both commissions have issued joint advisories on preventing child marriage in minority communities, but coordination remains ad hoc, mediated through the Ministry of Minority Affairs and the Ministry of Women and Child Development rather than a statutory mechanism.

Landmark judicial interventions have shaped their trajectories. In National Commission for Minorities v. State of Punjab (2004), the Supreme Court upheld the NCM’s power to recommend minority status for Hindus in Punjab, reinforcing its advisory role. In Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2011), the Court directed the NCPCR to monitor the implementation of child labour laws across states, effectively expanding its supervisory remit. More recently, the Exploitation of Children in Orphanages in Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (2017) judgment underscored the NCPCR’s duty to inspect childcare institutions, a function that occasionally intersects with the NCM’s oversight of minority‑run institutions.

Recent legislative developments include the 2022 Bill to amend the NCM Act, proposing a fixed five‑year term for members and a removal process requiring a Supreme Court inquiry — mirroring the Election Commission model. The Bill lapsed with the dissolution of the 17th Lok Sabha. For the NCPCR, the 2019 amendment to the POCSO Act mandated the Commission to monitor the establishment of Special Courts, adding a time‑bound reporting obligation. Aspirants should note that neither body enjoys constitutional status; their autonomy is statutory and therefore subject to executive amendment, a point frequently tested through comparative questions on “institutional capture”.

State Finance Commissions and Fiscal Devolution to Local Bodies: Constitutional Provisions, Devolution Gaps, and 15th Finance Commission Recommendations

Articles 243‑I and 243‑Y of the Constitution require every State to constitute a State Finance Commission (SFC) within one year of the commencement of the 73rd and 74th Amendments and thereafter at the expiration of every fifth year. The Governor appoints the Chairperson and members, and the Commission’s recommendations cover the distribution of net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls and fees between the State and Panchayats/Municipalities, the determination of taxes that may be assigned to local bodies, and grants‑in‑aid from the Consolidated Fund of the State.

Despite the constitutional mandate, compliance has been uneven. As of March 2023, only twelve States had constituted their sixth SFC on schedule; several States — including Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal — had delayed the fifth SFC by more than two years. The Supreme Court in All India Federation of Panchayat Parishads v. Union of India (2019) issued a mandamus directing defaulting States to constitute SFCs within six months, yet implementation remains patchy. A key structural weakness is that SFC recommendations are not binding; the State Legislature may accept or reject them, and the Governor’s assent is required for the action‑taken report.

The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25), chaired by N. K. Singh, addressed these gaps by recommending a performance‑based grant system for local bodies. It proposed that 60 % of the grants be untied (basic grants) and 40 % tied to measurable outcomes such as audited accounts, publication of service‑level benchmarks, and the preparation of Gram Panchayat Development Plans (GPDPs). The Commission also urged the creation of a permanent “State Finance Commission Cell” in each State Finance Department to provide technical support and ensure continuity of data across SFC cycles.

Horizontal devolution among local bodies remains a contentious issue. The 14th Finance Commission had introduced a formula weighted 90 % by population (2011 Census) and 10 % by area. The 15th FC retained the population weight but added a 12.5 % weight for forest cover and a 2.5 % weight for demographic performance, aiming to incentivise fiscal discipline. However, the absence of a uniform methodology across States leads to wide disparities: per capita devolution to Gram Panchayats ranges from ₹1,200 in Kerala to ₹180 in Jharkhand (2022‑23 figures).

An emerging area of examiner interest is the interplay between SFCs and the Union Finance Commission. The 15th FC recommended that the Union Finance Commission consider the SFC’s devolution framework while determining the State’s share of central taxes, effectively linking the two tiers of fiscal federalism. Candidates should be able to articulate the constitutional articles, the typical composition of an SFC, the reasons for delayed constitution, and the specific grant‑design innovations of the 15th FC.

Comparative Analysis Technique
Distinguish between the National Commission for Minorities and the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights in terms of constitutional source, composition, and mandate.
The NCM is established under the National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992 (statutory), while the NCPCR derives from the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005 (statutory). Neither has constitutional origin. Composition: NCM — Chairperson, Vice‑Chairperson, five members appointed by Central Government; NCPCR — Chairperson and six members (at least two women) appointed by Central Government on recommendation of a selection committee headed by the Minister for Women and Child Development. Mandate: NCM monitors safeguards for six notified religious minorities and linguistic minorities, evaluates implementation, and inquires into specific complaints. When a question asks you to compare two statutory commissions, structure the answer in a three‑column table: Parent Act, Composition, Mandate. Inter‑State Council and Zonal Councils: Institutional Architecture for Cooperative Federalism, Recent Meetings, and Effectiveness

The Inter‑State Council (ISC) was constituted in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission, pursuant to Article 263 of the Constitution. Its composition includes the Prime Minister as Chairperson, Chief Ministers of all States and Union Territories with legislatures, Administrators of UTs without legislatures, and six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the Prime Minister. The Council’s functions are to inquire into and advise upon disputes between States, investigate and discuss subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination of policy and action.

Since its inception, the ISC has met only twelve times, the last meeting being held in November 2022 after a gap of six years. The infrequency of meetings has drawn criticism from the Punchhi Commission (2010), which recommended a mandatory annual meeting and a permanent secretariat. The Council’s advisory nature — its recommendations are not binding — limits its effectiveness in resolving contentious issues such as water sharing (e.g., Cauvery, Krishna) or the implementation of centrally sponsored schemes.

Zonal Councils, created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, provide a more regular forum. There are five Zonal Councils — Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern — each comprising the Chief Ministers and two other Ministers from each State in the zone, and the Administrator or Lieutenant Governor of UTs. The Union Home Minister acts as the common Chairperson. Zonal Councils meet more frequently; for instance, the Northern Zonal Council met in June 2023 to discuss drug trafficking, cybercrime and border infrastructure. Their resolutions, while also non‑binding, have led to concrete outcomes such as the harmonisation of stamp duty rates in the Western Zone (2018) and the joint procurement of medical equipment during the COVID‑19 pandemic by the Southern Zonal Council (2021).

A comparative perspective is essential. The GST Council (Article 279A) is a constitutional body with binding decisions on tax rates, whereas the ISC and Zonal Councils are statutory/executive bodies with recommendatory powers. The 15th Finance Commission highlighted the need to institutionalise the ISC by giving it a permanent secretariat and a mandatory meeting calendar, arguing that cooperative federalism cannot rely solely on ad hoc political will. Aspirants should be able to map the constitutional provisions (Article 263 vs. States Reorganisation Act), list the composition of each council, cite the frequency of meetings, and evaluate their impact on specific policy areas such as water disputes, internal security and fiscal transfers.

Performance Audit, Outcome Budgeting, and the CAG’s Expanding Mandate: From Financial to Performance Accountability

The Comptroller and Auditor General’s (CAG) mandate under Section 13 of the CAG Act, 1971, originally centred on financial audit — verifying the legality and regularity of expenditure. Since the early 1990s, the CAG has progressively adopted performance audit (value‑for‑money audit) to assess whether government programmes achieve intended outcomes economically, efficiently and effectively. The first comprehensive performance audit report was on the Integrated Rural Development Programme (1992).

Outcome budgeting was introduced in the Union Budget 2005‑06, requiring ministries to link financial outlays to measurable outputs and outcomes. The CAG’s role expanded to auditing these outcome budgets. In 2017, the CAG tabled a performance audit of the National Rural Health Mission, revealing that only 58 % of targeted health sub‑centres were functional and that fund utilisation was below 70 % in several high‑focus States. The 2020 audit of the Ministry of Education’s outcome budget highlighted mismatches between reported enrolment figures and independent survey data, prompting a revision of the Unified District Information System for Education (UDISE) methodology.

Recent CAG reports have extended to new domains. The 2021 audit of Public‑Private Partnership (PPP) projects in the highway sector introduced a “project‑level value‑for‑money” framework, assessing risk allocation, concessionaire performance and user‑charge adequacy. In 2022, the CAG undertook an audit of India’s progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), mapping 17 goals to 169 targets and identifying data gaps in 42 % of indicators. The Supreme Court in Common Cause v. Union of India (2018) directed the CAG to audit election expenditure of political parties, a mandate the CAG fulfilled by issuing guidelines for scrutiny of contribution reports. In Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India (2021), the Court examined the CAG’s refusal to audit the PM CARES Fund, underscoring the tension between executive privilege and audit jurisdiction.

The CAG’s expanding mandate raises questions about capacity and independence. The 15th Finance Commission recommended strengthening the Indian Audit and Accounts Service (IAAS) through lateral entry and specialised training in data analytics. It also suggested that the CAG’s audit reports be discussed in a dedicated parliamentary committee within a fixed timeframe, to prevent the current backlog where reports lie unexamined for years. Candidates should be able to differentiate financial, compliance and performance audit; explain the outcome‑budget cycle; cite at least two recent CAG performance audits; and articulate the judicial pronouncements that have shaped the CAG’s evolving role.

The Central Information Commission after the 2019 RTI Amendment: Tenure, Salary, and Judicial Review of Executive Overreach

The Right to Information Act, 2005 established the Central Information Commission (CIC) as the apex appellate body. The original Act fixed the tenure of the Chief Information Commissioner and Information Commissioners at five years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, and equated their salaries to those of the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners respectively. The RTI Amendment Act, 2019 altered Sections 13 and 16, empowering the Central Government to prescribe the tenure, salary, allowances and other terms of service through rules, thereby removing the statutory guarantee of a fixed term.

The amendment triggered immediate legal challenge. In Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India (2020), a three‑judge bench of the Supreme Court read down the amendment, holding that the power to determine tenure and salary could not be exercised in a manner that undermines the independence of the Commission. The Court directed that until appropriate rules are framed, the original statutory provisions shall continue to govern. Despite this, the Central Government notified the Right to Information (Term of Office, Salaries and Allowances) Rules, 2020, which set the tenure at three years and linked salaries to the Secretary‑level pay matrix — a downgrade from the earlier equivalence with Election Commissioners.

The practical impact has been severe. As of January 2024, four of the ten Information Commissioner posts remain vacant, and the Chief Information Commissioner’s position was vacant for over eight months in 2022‑23. The backlog of appeals and complaints crossed 35,000 cases, with an average disposal time exceeding 18 months. In Common Cause v. Union of India (2023), the Supreme Court issued a mandamus directing the Government to fill all vacancies within three months and to restore the pre‑2019 tenure and salary structure, reiterating that the CIC’s independence is integral to the right to information under Article 19(1)(a).

The episode illustrates a broader pattern: statutory bodies whose autonomy depends on executive‑framed rules are vulnerable to “institutional capture”. The 2019 amendment also extended the Government’s rule‑making power to State Information Commissions, creating a uniform framework of executive control. Aspirants must be able to trace the legislative chronology (2005 Act → 2019 Amendment → 2020 Rules → 2020/2023 Supreme Court orders), explain the constitutional basis of the right to information, and evaluate the effectiveness of judicial review in checking executive overreach. A frequent examiner twist is to ask whether the CIC is a constitutional body — the answer is no, it is statutory, and its autonomy is therefore contingent on the parent Act and judicial interpretation.

  • Confusing the NCM’s statutory origin with constitutional status; it is created by the 1992 Act, not by the Constitution.
  • Assuming State Finance Commission recommendations are binding on the State Government; they are advisory and require legislative acceptance.
  • Treating the Inter‑State Council as a dispute‑resolution body with binding orders; its role is advisory under Article 263.
  • Believing the CAG’s performance audit mandate is explicitly laid down in the Constitution; it derives from the CAG Act, 1971 and executive practice.
  • Overlooking that the 2019 RTI Amendment removed the fixed five‑year tenure for Information Commissioners, a change later read down but not fully reversed by the Supreme Court.
  • Neglecting the distinction between Zonal Councils (statutory under States Reorganisation Act) and the GST Council (constitutional under Article 279A) when comparing decision‑making authority.
