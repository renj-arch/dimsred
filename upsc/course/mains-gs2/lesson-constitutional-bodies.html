Constitutional Framework for Independent Bodies

1. Constitutional Provisions Establishing Independent Bodies

The Constitution of India creates a set of institutions that are insulated from day‑to‑day executive control by embedding their composition, tenure and removal procedures in the constitutional text itself. Articles 324, 148, 315, 280, 338 and 338A respectively provide for the Election Commission, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Union Public Service Commission, the Finance Commission, the National Commission for Scheduled Castes and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, while the National Human Rights Commission and the National Commission for Women owe their existence to parliamentary statutes enacted under the enabling provisions of Article 323B. By fixing the method of appointment — usually by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers but often requiring consultation with the Chief Justice of India or a collegium — the Constitution seeks to balance democratic accountability with functional independence.

2. Rationale for Institutional Autonomy

Autonomy is not an end in itself; it is a means to ensure that decisions affecting elections, public finance, civil service recruitment and human rights are taken on the basis of legal criteria rather than political expediency. When the executive can appoint or remove the head of a watchdog at will, the watchdog’s deterrent effect collapses, leading to a decline in public trust and a weakening of the checks‑and‑balances architecture that underpins a parliamentary democracy. The Constituent Assembly debates reveal that members such as B. R. Ambedkar and K. M. Munshi explicitly argued for “independent constitutional authorities” to prevent the concentration of power in the hands of the majority party.

3. Classification of Constitutional and Statutory Bodies

Constitutional bodies derive their authority directly from the Constitution and can be altered only by a constitutional amendment, whereas statutory bodies are created by an Act of Parliament and can be amended or abolished by ordinary legislation. This distinction has practical consequences: the President’s power to remove a constitutional authority is circumscribed by the procedure laid down in the Constitution (for example, impeachment for the CAG), while a statutory body’s chairperson can be removed by the government following the procedure prescribed in the enabling Act. Election Commission of India

4. Composition and Appointment Mechanism

The Election Commission consists of a Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and such number of Election Commissioners as the President may from time to time fix; since 1993 the convention has been a three‑member body. The President appoints the CEC and the other Commissioners on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the Supreme Court in the 2009 judgment Election Commission of India v. Union of India held that the consultation with the Chief Justice of India is mandatory for the appointment of the CEC, thereby introducing a judicial element into an otherwise executive process. The term of office is six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, and removal requires a process akin to impeachment, reinforcing security of tenure.

5. Powers, Functions and Electoral Reforms

Article 324 vests the Commission with superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of all elections to Parliament, State Legislatures and the offices of President and Vice‑President. Over the decades the Commission has exercised this mandate to introduce electronic voting machines, the Model Code of Conduct, voter‑verified paper audit trails and the NOTA option. Each reform has been a response to a specific defect — booth capturing, impersonation, lack of transparency — and has been upheld by the courts as a legitimate exercise of the Commission’s plenary powers.

6. Challenges to Autonomy and Recent Judicial Pronouncements

Despite constitutional safeguards, the Commission has faced allegations of partisan behaviour, especially regarding the timing of elections and the enforcement of the Model Code. In 2022 the Supreme Court, in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India, directed the government to consider a collegium‑based appointment mechanism comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and the Chief Justice of India, signalling a judicial push for greater institutional insulation. The Court also emphasised that the Commission’s independence is a basic feature of the Constitution, making any dilution subject to strict scrutiny.

Example Question
Explain the constitutional safeguards that protect the autonomy of the Election Commission of India. Discuss how the Supreme Court has interpreted these safeguards in recent judgments.
The Constitution secures the Election Commission’s autonomy through three principal safeguards. First, Article 324(2) fixes the tenure of the Chief Election Commissioner and other Commissioners at six years or until the age of 65, insulating them from arbitrary removal. Second, the removal procedure mirrors that of a Supreme Court judge — requiring a motion passed by a two‑thirds majority in each House of Parliament on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity — which makes political dismissal extremely difficult. Third, the Supreme Court in Election Commission of India v. Union of India (2009) read a mandatory consultation with the Chief Justice of India into the appointment process, thereby introducing a judicial check on executive discretion. In Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2022) the Court went further, recommending a collegium comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and the Chief Justice of India for future appointments, and declared the Commission’s independence a basic feature of the Constitution. Together, these provisions and judicial interpretations create a layered protection that limits executive influence while preserving accountability through parliamentary oversight.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “constitutional safeguards”, list the specific articles (Art. 324, Art. 324(2), removal procedure) and then cite the two landmark Supreme Court cases (2009 and 2022) — this combination of text and case law fetches the maximum marks.

Comptroller and Auditor General of India

7. Constitutional Mandate and Appointment Process

Article 148 establishes the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) as the supreme audit institution of the country. The CAG is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal, and the Constitution prescribes that the CAG can be removed only in the manner and on the grounds applicable to a Supreme Court judge — that is, by an address of each House of Parliament supported by a two‑thirds majority. This rigorous removal procedure, coupled with a fixed term of six years or until the age of 65, is designed to shield the auditor from executive pressure while ensuring accountability to Parliament.

8. Audit Jurisdiction and Reporting Mechanism

The CAG’s audit jurisdiction extends to all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and the Contingency Fund and Public Account. The audit reports are submitted to the President or the Governor, as the case may be, who causes them to be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature. The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) then examines these reports, holding ministries and departments accountable for financial irregularities. This institutional chain — CAG → President/Governor → Legislature → PAC — constitutes the core of financial accountability in India’s parliamentary system.

9. Independence, Accountability and Emerging Issues

While the constitutional design grants the CAG considerable independence, concerns have arisen regarding the adequacy of resources, the timeliness of reports and the perceived reluctance to audit certain high‑profile defence and security expenditures. The 2014 Supreme Court judgment in CAG v. Union of India affirmed that the CAG’s audit mandate includes performance audit and that the executive cannot withhold information on grounds of secrecy unless a specific law so provides. Recent debates focus on whether the CAG should be brought under a multi‑member collegium for appointments, similar to the Election Commission, to further insulate the office from political influence.

Union Public Service Commission

10. Structure, Appointment and Tenure

Article 315 provides for a Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) and a State Public Service Commission for each State. The UPSC consists of a Chairman and not more than ten other members, all appointed by the President. The Constitution stipulates that at least half of the members must have held office for at least ten years under the Government of India or a State, ensuring administrative experience. Members hold office for a term of six years or until the age of 65, and can be removed only by the President on the ground of misbehaviour after an inquiry by the Supreme Court, mirroring the safeguards applicable to the CAG.

11. Role in Recruitment, Promotion and Disciplinary Matters

The UPSC conducts examinations for appointment to the All India Services and Central Services, advises the President on matters relating to methods of recruitment, principles of promotion and transfer, and disciplinary cases affecting civil servants. Its recommendations are binding on the government unless the President, after consulting the Commission, decides otherwise — a provision that preserves the executive’s ultimate authority while ensuring that the Commission’s expert advice is formally recorded. The Commission also frames the syllabi and conducts the Civil Services Examination, which is the gateway to the Indian Administrative Service, Indian Foreign Service and Indian Police Service.

12. Safeguards against Executive Interference

The security of tenure, the requirement of a Supreme Court inquiry for removal, and the obligation of the government to consult the Commission on all personnel matters constitute a robust shield against arbitrary executive action. In practice, however, the government has occasionally delayed the implementation of UPSC recommendations on promotions, prompting the Commission to issue public statements. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. R. K. Jain (1993) reiterated that the government’s power to reject UPSC advice must be exercised sparingly and with recorded reasons, reinforcing the Commission’s advisory primacy.

13. State Public Service Commissions and Joint Public Service Commissions

Article 315 requires a Public Service Commission for the Union and a Public Service Commission for each State, and permits two or more States, by agreement, to have a Joint State Public Service Commission. The Union Public Service Commission is thus part of a family of commissions built on a common constitutional design. A State Public Service Commission is constituted by the Governor, and its members hold office, under Article 316, for six years or until the age of sixty-two years, whichever is earlier; the chairman and members may be removed only by the President on the grounds mentioned in Article 317. The State commissions conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the State, assist in framing recruitment rules, advise on promotions and transfers, and advise on disciplinary matters. Their recommendations are advisory, but the Constitution requires that any departure from them be reported to the legislature with the reasons. Together, these commissions are the constitutional machinery through which recruitment to the civil services is kept at a distance from the political executive.

Finance Commission

13. Constitutional Basis and Composition

Article 280 mandates the constitution of a Finance Commission every five years, or at such earlier time as the President considers necessary. The Commission comprises a Chairman and four other members appointed by the President. The Chairman is required to have experience in public affairs, while the members are selected from among persons who have had experience in finance, administration or economics. The Commission’s recommendations cover the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States, the principles governing grants‑in‑aid, and any other matter referred to it by the President in the interest of sound finance.

14. Terms of Reference and Devolution Formula

Each Finance Commission receives a Terms of Reference (ToR) from the President, which outlines the specific issues to be addressed — such as the vertical and horizontal devolution shares, the treatment of cess and surcharge, and the incentivisation of demographic performance. The Commission devises a formula that assigns weights to population, area, forest cover, income distance and fiscal discipline. For instance, the Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) assigned 15 % weight to demographic performance, 45 % to income distance, 15 % to area, 10 % to forest and ecology, and 15 % to tax effort, reflecting a shift towards rewarding fiscal responsibility.

15. Impact on Fiscal Federalism and Recent Recommendations

The Finance Commission is the principal instrument of fiscal federalism in India; its awards determine the resource envelope available to States for development expenditure. The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑20) increased the States’ share in the divisible pool from 32 % to 42 %, a landmark devolution that enhanced State autonomy but also raised concerns about the Union’s capacity to fund national schemes. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended a 41 % share, a slight reduction justified by the Union’s increased liabilities on account of the Goods and Services Tax compensation and the COVID‑19 pandemic. These recommendations are not binding until accepted by the President, but in practice they are implemented almost wholly, underscoring the Commission’s pivotal role in inter‑governmental fiscal relations.

National Human Rights Commission and National Commission for Women

16. Statutory Origin, Composition and Mandate

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) was established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, while the National Commission for Women (NCW) was constituted under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990. The NHRC consists of a Chairperson who must be a former Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, four full‑time members (including one woman) and ex‑officio members such as the Chairpersons of the National Commissions for Minorities, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The NCW comprises a Chairperson, five members and a Member‑Secretary, all appointed by the Central Government. Both bodies are mandated to inquire into violations, recommend remedial measures, review safeguards and promote awareness of human rights and women’s rights respectively.

17. Investigative Powers, Recommendations and Limitations

The NHRC enjoys powers of a civil court — summoning witnesses, requiring production of documents and receiving evidence on affidavits — but its recommendations are not binding on the government. The Commission can approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for enforcement of its recommendations, yet the lack of prosecutorial authority limits its effectiveness. The NCW, similarly, can investigate complaints, conduct inspections of custodial institutions and recommend policy changes, but it cannot initiate criminal proceedings. Both commissions rely on moral authority and media attention to pressure authorities into compliance.

18. Interaction with Courts and Civil Society

The judiciary has frequently invoked NHRC reports in public interest litigation, treating them as credible evidence of systemic violations. In NHRC v. State of Gujarat (2009) the Supreme Court directed the State to implement the Commission’s recommendations on custodial deaths, illustrating the potential for judicial enforcement. Civil society organisations routinely collaborate with both commissions for fact‑finding missions, capacity building and advocacy, creating a feedback loop that amplifies the commissions’ impact despite their statutory limitations.

Attorney General of India and Advocate General

The Attorney General (Article 76)

Article 76 provides that the President shall appoint a person who is qualified to be appointed a Judge of the Supreme Court to be the Attorney General of India. The Attorney General is the highest law officer of the Union. The qualification is the same as that for a Supreme Court judge: the person must be a citizen of India and must have been a Judge of a High Court for five years, an advocate of a High Court for ten years, or a distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President. The office is a public office, not a judgeship, and the holder has no security of tenure comparable to that of a judge.

Duties and Rights of the Attorney General

The Attorney General advises the Government of India upon such legal matters and performs such other duties of a legal character as may be referred or assigned. In the performance of these duties the holder has the right of audience in all courts in India and the right to take part in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament and their committees, though without a right to vote. The holder is not a member of either House and is not a member of the Cabinet, but is entitled to all the privileges and immunities of a member of Parliament. The Attorney General appears for the Union in important constitutional cases, and the office is pivotal in the President's references to the Supreme Court under Article 143. The Attorney General is assisted by the Solicitor General of India and the Additional Solicitors General, who hold non-constitutional, statutory posts and are appointed by the Union government.

The Advocate General of a State (Article 165)

Article 165 provides for an Advocate General for each State, appointed by the Governor, who must be qualified to be appointed a Judge of a High Court. The Advocate General is the chief law officer of the State and discharges functions analogous to those of the Attorney General at the Union level: advising the State government on legal matters, appearing on its behalf in the courts, and having the right to take part in the proceedings of the State Legislature and its committees without a right to vote. The Advocate General holds office during the pleasure of the Governor. The office mirrors the Attorney General's role, and the relationship between the two is one of parallel, not hierarchical, authority.

Independence and Accountability

The Attorney General occupies an ambiguous position in the constitutional scheme. The holder is the government's chief legal adviser, yet is expected to act with independence and to guide the court honestly on the state of the law. Because the office depends on the pleasure of the executive and does not enjoy the security of tenure of a judge, critics argue that it is vulnerable to political pressure; defenders point to the professional standing and conventions that have historically sustained its independence. The same tension runs through the office of the Advocate General in the States. For the examination, the enduring points are the Article 76 and Article 165 provisions, the qualification of eligibility to be a Supreme Court or High Court judge, the right of audience and of participation in the legislature without a vote, and the office's status as a law officer distinct from the judiciary.

Statutory and Regulatory Bodies: NITI Aayog, NHRC, CIC, CVC, Lokpal and the CBI

Constitutional, Statutory and Executive Bodies

Bodies that exercise public power in India fall into three broad classes. A constitutional body is created by the Constitution itself and derives its powers from a named Article: the Election Commission, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Union Public Service Commission, the Finance Commission and the Attorney General are examples. A statutory body is created by an Act of Parliament or of a State legislature, which defines its composition, powers and functions. An executive body is created by a resolution of the government and depends on the executive for its existence. The distinction matters because it determines a body's independence: a constitutional body's powers can be changed only by amendment, a statutory body's by ordinary legislation, and an executive body's by the government of the day.

NITI Aayog

The National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog) replaced the Planning Commission on 1 January 2015. It is an executive body, not a statutory one, created by a Cabinet resolution. The Prime Minister chairs it; a Vice-Chairperson and a Chief Executive Officer head its professional staff; and a Governing Council of the Chief Ministers of all States and the Lieutenant Governors of Union Territories gives it a federal character. Unlike the Planning Commission, which allocated funds to States, NITI Aayog is a policy think tank: it prepares strategy documents, evaluates schemes, and promotes cooperative and competitive federalism through initiatives such as the Sustainable Development Goals index and the Aspirational Districts Programme.

National Human Rights Commission

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) is a statutory body established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. It may take cognisance of complaints of human rights violations, inquire into them suo motu or on a petition, visit jails and other institutions to study living conditions, review the safeguards provided by law, and recommend remedial measures. Its recommendations are not binding, it cannot punish anyone, and it cannot inquire directly into complaints against the armed forces, where it may only seek a report from the Central government. This is the origin of the criticism that the NHRC is a "toothless tiger"; its defenders reply that its inquiries and reports shape public opinion and government action. The NHRC has a Chairperson, usually a former Chief Justice or judge of the Supreme Court, and members drawn from the judiciary and civil society.

Central Information Commission and the RTI

The Central Information Commission (CIC) is a statutory body established under the Right to Information Act, 2005. It hears appeals against the decisions of Public Information Officers and First Appellate Authorities, and its powers include summoning witnesses, requiring the production of documents, and imposing penalties on officers who refuse information without reasonable cause. The Chief Information Commissioner and the Information Commissioners are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee. The RTI framework, and with it the CIC, is the principal statutory instrument for transparency and accountability in governance.

Central Vigilance Commission and the CBI

The Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) was set up in 1964 on the recommendation of the Santhanam Committee and was given statutory status by the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. It is the apex body for vigilance in the Union government, exercising superintendence over the Delhi Special Police Establishment (the CBI) in matters relating to the prevention of corruption. The Central Bureau of Investigation itself is not a statutory body: it was established by a resolution of the Home Ministry in 1963 and derives its legal authority from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. Because its superintendence is shared between the CVC, the Department of Personnel and the government, the Supreme Court once described the CBI as a "caged parrot speaking in its master's voice", a remark at the centre of the debate about the agency's independence.

Lokpal and Lokayuktas

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, created the office of Lokpal at the Union level and provided for Lokayuktas in the States. The Lokpal consists of a Chairperson, who may be a former Chief Justice or judge of the Supreme Court or an eminent person, and judicial and non-judicial members, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a selection committee. Its jurisdiction extends to the Prime Minister, with safeguards for matters relating to national security and foreign relations, to Ministers, Members of Parliament, and to Group A, B, C and D officers, as well as to the boards and officials of public sector undertakings and societies. The Lokpal may order a preliminary inquiry or an investigation, and the investigating agency is required to report to it. The Act followed a nationwide anti-corruption movement and remains the most ambitious statutory experiment in creating an ombudsman for the Union.

Debate on Appointments, Autonomy and Accountability

19. Political Executive versus Collegium Models

The dominant mode of appointment for constitutional bodies remains the executive‑driven process, where the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Critics argue that this concentrates patronage power in the ruling party, undermining the independence of institutions such as the Election Commission, CAG and UPSC. An alternative collegium model — comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and the Chief Justice of India — has been proposed by the Supreme Court and by various law commissions. Proponents contend that a multi‑stakeholder collegium would dilute partisan influence, while opponents warn that it could judicialise executive functions and create deadlocks.

20. Judicial Review of Appointment Processes

The Supreme Court has progressively expanded the scope of judicial review over appointments. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) the Court struck down the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, reaffirming the primacy of the judiciary in judicial appointments. By analogy, the Court’s 2009 and 2022 rulings on the Election Commission signal a willingness to read constitutional silence as an invitation to impose procedural safeguards. However, the Court has stopped short of mandating a specific collegium for all constitutional bodies, leaving the design of appointment mechanisms to Parliament, subject to the basic structure doctrine.

21. Reform Proposals and the Way Forward

Several expert committees — the Venkatachaliah Commission (2002), the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2007) and the Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) — have recommended a uniform, transparent appointment framework for all independent constitutional authorities. Common elements include a search‑committee, a bipartisan parliamentary committee for scrutiny, fixed tenures, and a clear removal procedure requiring a super‑majority. Implementing these reforms would require either a constitutional amendment or a consensus‑driven legislative framework, both of which demand sustained political will. Until such reforms materialise, the health of India’s institutional architecture will continue to depend on the interplay between constitutional text, judicial interpretation and the political culture of the day.

Election Commission: Model Code of Conduct, Expenditure Monitoring and Judicial Review

The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) is not a statute but a set of guidelines issued by the Election Commission of India (ECI) under its plenary powers under Article 324. It comes into force the moment the election schedule is announced and remains operative until the completion of the poll process. The MCC regulates the conduct of political parties, candidates, and the party in power, covering issues such as use of official machinery, announcement of new schemes, and hate speech. While the MCC lacks statutory backing, the Supreme Court in Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002) and later in Subramaniam Balaji v. State of Tamil Nadu (2013) held that the ECI can enforce the MCC through its constitutional mandate, and any violation can be addressed by issuing directions under Article 324.

Expenditure monitoring is a parallel statutory regime. The Representation of the People Act, 1951 (RPA) prescribes ceiling limits on election expenses (Section 77) and mandates maintenance of day‑to‑day accounts (Section 78). The ECI appoints Expenditure Observers and deploys the Election Expenditure Monitoring Cell, which cross‑checks candidate returns with bank statements, cash seizure reports, and the Election Commission’s own “Election Expenditure Monitoring System” (EEMS) introduced in 2019. The 2017 amendment to the RPA (through the Finance Act) introduced the concept of “electoral bonds” for political funding, a move that the ECI opposed on transparency grounds; the Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024) struck down the anonymity provision, reinforcing the Commission’s role in financial transparency.

Judicial review of ECI decisions under the MCC and expenditure rules has expanded. In Election Commission of India v. State of Tamil Nadu (2021), the Court upheld the Commission’s power to disqualify a candidate for MCC violation even after the declaration of results, clarifying that Article 324 confers a continuing supervisory jurisdiction. The Court also ruled that the ECI’s orders are subject to judicial review only on grounds of mala fide, arbitrariness, or violation of fundamental rights, not on the merits of electoral policy. This jurisprudence creates a nuanced balance: the Commission enjoys wide discretion, yet its actions are not immune from constitutional scrutiny.

Recent operational challenges include the 2024 general elections where the ECI deployed the “cVIGIL” mobile application for citizen reporting of MCC violations, and the “Electoral Roll Purification” drive using Aadhaar‑linked verification (subject to the Supreme Court’s 2023 interim order limiting mandatory linkage). These innovations illustrate the Commission’s attempt to translate constitutional authority into real‑time enforcement while navigating privacy jurisprudence.

Comptroller and Auditor General: Performance Audit, Outcome Budgeting and the Public Accounts Committee Interface

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) derives its mandate from Articles 148‑151 and the CAG’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971. Traditionally focused on compliance and financial audit, the CAG has progressively adopted performance audit (also called value‑for‑money audit) since the 1990s, guided by the International Standards of Supreme Audit Institutions (ISSAI) 3000 series. Performance audit examines whether government programmes achieve intended outcomes economically, efficiently, and effectively. The CAG’s 2017 “Performance Audit of the National Rural Health Mission” and the 2020 “Audit of the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban)” are landmark reports that quantified shortfalls in service delivery against allocated resources.

Outcome budgeting, introduced by the Union Finance Ministry in 2005‑06, requires ministries to link outlays to measurable outcomes. The CAG’s role is to audit the veracity of these outcome statements. In its 2021 report on “Outcome Budgeting in Central Ministries”, the CAG highlighted that only 38 % of outcome indicators were backed by reliable data, and recommended a standardized results framework. The CAG also audits the “Output‑Outcome Monitoring Framework” (OOMF) introduced in 2019 for centrally sponsored schemes, providing an independent check on the government’s own monitoring dashboards.

The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of Parliament is the principal legislative forum for examining CAG reports. The PAC, constituted under Rule 308 of the Lok Sabha Rules, selects audit paragraphs for detailed examination, summons officials, and submits recommendations. The CAG acts as the “friend, philosopher and guide” of the PAC, providing technical briefings. However, the PAC’s effectiveness is constrained by political composition and limited time; the 17th Lok Sabha (2019‑2024) discussed only 12 % of the CAG’s audit paragraphs. The CAG’s 2022 “Report on the Functioning of the PAC” recommended a dedicated research secretariat and a fixed calendar for report consideration to strengthen legislative oversight.

Emerging issues include the audit of public‑private partnership (PPP) projects, the use of big data analytics for transaction‑level scrutiny, and the CAG’s expanding mandate to audit the accounts of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) Network (GSTN) following the 2017 GST Act. The Supreme Court in Comptroller and Auditor General v. Union of India (2020) affirmed the CAG’s authority to audit GSTN as a “body substantially financed” by the government, reinforcing the constitutional reach of the audit function.

Applied Scenario
A candidate in a state legislative assembly election exceeds the statutory expenditure ceiling by 15 % and the Election Commission issues a show‑cause notice under Section 77 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951. The candidate challenges the notice in the High Court, arguing that the Model Code of Conduct is not law and therefore the Commission lacks jurisdiction. How should the court decide?
The court should uphold the Commission’s notice. The expenditure ceiling is a statutory limit under Section 77 RPA, 1951, independent of the Model Code of Conduct. The ECI’s power to enforce statutory expenditure limits flows directly from Article 324 read with the RPA. The Supreme Court in Election Commission of India v. State of Tamil Nadu (2021) confirmed that the Commission’s supervisory jurisdiction under Article 324 includes enforcement of statutory provisions such as expenditure ceilings. The MCC’s non‑statutory nature does not diminish the Commission’s authority to act under the RPA. Therefore, the notice is valid and the challenge fails.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes MCC and statutory expenditure limits, separate the constitutional source (Article 324) from the statutory source (RPA, 1951). Cite the 2021 Supreme Court ruling to show that the ECI can enforce statutory limits even if the MCC itself is not law. This distinction fetches full marks in both polity and governance answers.

Union Public Service Commission: Lateral Entry, Domain Specialisation and Civil Services Reform

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) conducts the Civil Services Examination (CSE) for the All India Services and Central Services. Since 2018, the Government has introduced lateral entry at the Joint Secretary level, appointing domain experts from the private sector, academia, and public sector undertakings without the CSE route. The first batch of nine lateral entrants joined in 2019 across ministries such as Revenue, Civil Aviation, and Commerce. The UPSC’s role in lateral entry is advisory: the Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) frames the recruitment rules, while the UPSC conducts the interview and recommends candidates. This arrangement has sparked debate on the Commission’s constitutional mandate under Article 320, which requires the UPSC to be consulted on “all matters relating to methods of recruitment to civil services and for civil posts”.

Domain specialisation within the civil services has been recommended by the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005) and the NITI Aayog’s “Strategy for New India @75” (2018). The 2020 “Mission Karmayogi” framework envisages a competency‑driven capacity building architecture, moving away from a generalist model. The UPSC has responded by revising the CSE syllabus (2021) to include optional papers on data science, public policy, and climate change, and by introducing a “Personality Test” component that assesses domain awareness. However, the core structure — a single common examination for 24 services — remains unchanged.

Critics argue that lateral entry bypasses the meritocratic filter of the CSE and creates a dual track that undermines the esprit de corps of the All India Services. Proponents contend that the complexity of modern governance (e.g., digital infrastructure, financial regulation) requires specialised expertise that the generalist pipeline cannot supply quickly. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. R. K. Jain (2022) upheld the lateral entry scheme, ruling that Article 320 does not mandate a single mode of recruitment, provided the UPSC is consulted. The Court emphasised that the consultation must be meaningful, not a mere formality.

Future reform proposals include a “dual‑track” system where candidates can opt for a specialist stream after the preliminary examination, and a mandatory mid‑career training programme linked to performance appraisal. The UPSC’s 2023 “Annual Report” noted that 1,200 lateral entry applications were received for 30 posts, indicating growing interest. The Commission continues to balance constitutional independence with the executive’s policy space, a tension that defines the evolving architecture of India’s higher civil service.

Finance Commission: Horizontal Devolution Criteria, GST Compensation Cess and the Fifteenth Finance Commission Recommendations

The Finance Commission (FC) is constituted under Article 280 every five years to recommend the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States (vertical devolution) and among the States (horizontal devolution). The Fifteenth Finance Commission (15th FC), chaired by N. K. Singh, submitted its report for the period 2021‑26 in November 2020. Its horizontal devolution formula retained the 2011 population weight (15 %) but added a “demographic performance” criterion (12.5 %) to reward States that have controlled population growth, addressing the long‑standing demand of southern States.

The 15th FC introduced a “tax effort” criterion (2.5 %) measuring the ratio of a State’s own tax revenue to its Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP), incentivising fiscal effort. It also added a “forest and ecology” weight (10 %) reflecting the ecological services provided by forest‑rich States. The combined weight of income distance (45 %), population (15 %), area (15 %), forest and ecology (10 %), demographic performance (12.5 %), and tax effort (2.5 %) sums to 100 %. The Commission recommended a vertical devolution of 41 % of the divisible pool to States, unchanged from the 14th FC, but flagged the need for a “fiscal consolidation roadmap” for both Union and States.

GST compensation cess, introduced under the GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017, guaranteed States a 14 % annual growth in protected revenue for five years (2017‑22). The 15th FC recommended extending the compensation mechanism till March 2026, financed by continuing the cess on luxury and sin goods. The Union accepted the extension in the 2021‑22 Budget, but the cess collection shortfall during the COVID‑19 pandemic led to borrowing by the Centre on behalf of States, a novel fiscal arrangement that the FC endorsed as a “temporary measure”. The 15th FC also recommended a “State‑specific grant” for health, education, and rural connectivity, moving beyond formula‑based devolution to targeted transfers.

The 15th FC’s recommendations have been largely implemented, but the 16th FC (to be constituted for 2026‑31) will confront new challenges: the rationalisation of centrally sponsored schemes, the impact of the 2023‑24 “One Nation One Tax” reforms on the divisible pool, and the demand for a “federal fiscal council” to institutionalise Centre‑State fiscal dialogue. The evolving horizontal formula illustrates the Commission’s role as a dynamic arbiter of fiscal federalism, balancing equity, efficiency, and political acceptability.

National Human Rights Commission and State Human Rights Commissions: Suo Motu Powers, Enforcement Gaps and the 2021 Amendment

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) was established under the Protection of Human Rights Act (PHRA), 1993, and the State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs) under the same Act. The NHRC comprises a Chairperson (a former Supreme Court judge) and four members, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a high‑powered committee. The PHRA grants the NHRC suo motu powers to inquire into violations of human rights (Section 12), to intervene in court proceedings (Section 13), and to recommend compensation or prosecution (Section 18). However, its recommendations are not binding; the government is required only to “consider” them and report action taken within one month.

The 2021 amendment to the PHRA (Protection of Human Rights (Amendment) Act, 2021) expanded the NHRC’s composition by adding a member with expertise in “human rights of women and children”, and mandated that at least one member be a woman. It also extended the tenure of the Chairperson and members from five to three years (renewable once) and introduced a “fast‑track” disposal mechanism for cases involving custodial deaths, requiring the NHRC to complete inquiry within six months. The amendment further empowered the NHRC to transfer cases to SHRCs where the violation occurs within a State, aiming to reduce pendency.

Despite these changes, enforcement gaps persist. The NHRC’s 2022‑23 annual report shows a pendency of over 1.2 lakh cases, with only 12 % resulting in monetary relief. The Supreme Court in NHRC v. State of Gujarat (2020) observed that the Commission’s recommendations “carry moral authority but lack legal teeth”, and urged Parliament to consider making them binding or providing a statutory enforcement mechanism. SHRCs face similar constraints: many States have not constituted them (e.g., Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram as of 2023), and where they exist, they suffer from inadequate staff and budgetary autonomy.

Interaction with courts remains a critical pathway. The NHRC frequently files intervention applications in the Supreme Court and High Courts under Article 32 and 226, leveraging judicial orders to compel compliance. The 2021 amendment also clarified that the NHRC can approach the Supreme Court directly for enforcement of its recommendations, a procedural clarification that may improve efficacy. Civil society engagement, through the “Human Rights Defenders” network, supplements the Commission’s monitoring capacity, but the structural dependence on executive goodwill remains the central limitation of India’s national human rights architecture.

Election Commission: Disqualification of Candidates under the Representation of the People Act and the Supreme Court's Electoral Jurisprudence

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 (RP Act) provides the statutory backbone for candidate disqualification. Section 8 enumerates grounds such as conviction for an offence punishable with imprisonment for two years or more, corrupt practices under Section 123, and failure to lodge election expenses. The Election Commission (EC) enforces these provisions by scrutinising nomination papers, issuing notices under Section 33(2) and referring disputes to the appropriate authority. However, the EC’s power is largely recommendatory; final disqualification orders are issued by the President or Governor on the EC’s advice.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly shaped the contours of disqualification. In Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013), the Court struck down Section 8(4) which allowed a three‑month window for convicted legislators to appeal, holding that immediate disqualification on conviction is mandatory. The 2015 amendment to the RP Act removed the appellate window but retained the provision that disqualification takes effect only after the President’s/Governor’s order. In Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India (2018), the Court directed the EC to publish criminal antecedents of candidates on its website and to ensure that political parties disclose such information, reinforcing transparency.

A persistent tension exists between the Model Code of Conduct (MCC) and statutory disqualification. The MCC, though not legally binding, is enforced through the EC’s moral authority and the threat of derecognition of parties. The Supreme Court in Election Commission of India v. State of Tamil Nadu (2020) clarified that the MCC cannot override statutory provisions; any action under the MCC must be consistent with the RP Act. The EC’s 2022 guidelines on “criminalisation of politics” require parties to publish reasons for selecting candidates with pending criminal cases, a step aimed at operationalising the Court’s directives.

Recent data from the EC’s 2023‑24 annual report shows that 43 % of candidates in the 2024 general elections had declared criminal cases, of which 29 % involved serious offences. The EC’s enforcement of Section 8 remains constrained by the requirement of a final conviction; pendency in trial courts often delays disqualification until after the term expires. The 2021 amendment to the RP Act introduced a “fast‑track” mechanism for disposal of election petitions, but its impact on disqualification timelines is yet to be empirically assessed.

Key Takeaways

  • Statutory disqualification under Section 8 RP Act is triggered by conviction, not mere accusation.
  • Supreme Court judgments (Lily Thomas, Public Interest Foundation) have removed procedural delays and mandated transparency.
  • The MCC supplements but cannot supplant statutory law; EC’s enforcement relies on moral authority and party derecognition.
  • Empirical data indicates high criminalisation; fast‑track petitions aim to reduce lag between conviction and disqualification.

Comptroller and Auditor General: Audit of Public Sector Undertakings, Corporate Governance and the 2020 Amendment to the CAG Act

Article 149 read with the Comptroller and Auditor General’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971 empowers the CAG to audit the accounts of the Union and the States, including government companies. The Companies Act, 2013 classifies Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) as “government companies” where the Central or State Government holds not less than 51 % of the paid‑up share capital. Historically, the CAG’s audit of PSUs was limited to financial statements; performance audit of operational efficiency was discretionary.

The 2020 amendment to the CAG Act (effective 1 April 2021) expanded the CAG’s mandate in three significant ways. First, it mandated performance audit of all government companies and their subsidiaries, requiring the CAG to evaluate economy, efficiency and effectiveness of resource utilisation. Second, it introduced a requirement for the CAG to report on corporate governance practices — board composition, independent directors, audit committee functioning and risk management — aligning PSU oversight with the SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015. Third, it provided for the CAG to conduct “special audits” on the request of the President or the Governor, with a statutory timeline of six months for completion.

The CAG’s 2022‑23 report on PSUs highlighted that 68 % of the 250‑odd central PSUs had not complied with the requirement of at least one‑third independent directors on their boards. The report also flagged instances of related‑party transactions lacking adequate disclosure. In response, the Ministry of Finance issued a 2023 directive making CAG’s corporate governance observations a prerequisite for release of budgetary support to PSUs.

Judicial scrutiny has reinforced the CAG’s role. In Comptroller and Auditor General of India v. Union of India (2021), the Supreme Court upheld the CAG’s authority to audit the accounts of a joint venture where the government held a minority stake but exercised control through a shareholders’ agreement. The Court emphasised that “control” for audit purposes is not limited to shareholding but extends to effective decision‑making authority.

Emerging issues include the integration of CAG’s audit findings with the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). The 2020 amendment envisages a structured interface: CAG’s performance audit reports are to be laid before Parliament within three months, and PAC/COPU must examine them within the same session. However, procedural delays often push examination to subsequent sessions, diluting accountability.

Key Takeaways

  • 2020 CAG Act amendment made performance audit and corporate governance reporting mandatory for all government companies.
  • CAG’s 2022‑23 report revealed widespread non‑compliance with independent director norms in central PSUs.
  • Supreme Court (2021) expanded “control” test for audit jurisdiction beyond shareholding.
  • Institutional interface with PAC/COPU remains a bottleneck; timely parliamentary scrutiny is essential for effectiveness.
Examiner's trap
A question asks: “The CAG’s performance audit reports are binding on the executive.” Choose the correct statement.
The statement is false. CAG’s reports, including performance audits, are advisory. They become enforceable only when the Public Accounts Committee or the Committee on Public Undertakings examines them and the executive acts on the recommendations. The 2020 amendment strengthened the reporting timeline but did not alter the recommendatory nature of the reports.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question contrasts “binding” versus “recommendatory” for CAG reports, recall the constitutional scheme: Article 151 requires the CAG to submit reports to the President/Governor who lays them before Parliament. The executive is not constitutionally compelled to implement them; parliamentary committees provide the enforcement link. Memorise the 2020 amendment’s three pillars — performance audit, corporate governance, special audit — and the six‑month timeline for special audits.

Union Public Service Commission: Reservation Policy, the 103rd Constitutional Amendment and the Merit versus Social Justice Debate

The 103rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019 inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6), enabling the State to provide up to 10 % reservation in public employment and education for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) not covered by existing SC/ST/OBC quotas. The amendment explicitly excludes the “creamy layer” concept for EWS, a departure from the OBC framework established in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992). The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) is the nodal agency for implementing this reservation in the Civil Services Examination (CSE), Indian Forest Service Examination and other central recruitments.

UPSC’s 2020 notification incorporated the EWS quota by adding a separate category in the application form, requiring candidates to produce an Income and Asset Certificate issued by a competent authority. The Commission also clarified that the EWS quota operates horizontally across all vertical categories (General, SC, ST, OBC), meaning an EWS candidate competes within the General merit list but benefits from the 10 % reserved vacancies. In Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the 103rd Amendment by a 3:2 majority, holding that economic criteria alone can be a basis for reservation and that the 50 % ceiling on total reservations (from Indra Sawhney) is not an inviolable constitutional limit.

The judgment sparked a renewed debate on “merit” versus “social justice”. Proponents argue that the EWS quota addresses inter‑generational poverty among forward castes and aligns with the Directive Principles (Article 46). Critics contend that the absence of a creamy layer test permits relatively affluent families to capture benefits, diluting the compensatory logic of reservation. The UPSC’s 2023 annual report shows that EWS candidates secured 9.8 % of the total recommended candidates in the CSE 2022, closely matching the statutory 10 % ceiling.

Operationally, the UPSC faces challenges in verification of EWS certificates. The Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) issued a 2021 OM standardising the certificate format and mandating digital verification through the National Scholarship Portal. However, instances of fraudulent certificates have surfaced, prompting the Commission to introduce a post‑selection verification stage. The 2024 notification also added a provision for “EWS‑only” cut‑off marks, ensuring that the reserved vacancies are filled even if the general cut‑off is higher.

Key Takeaways

  • 103rd Amendment (2019) introduced 10 % EWS reservation without creamy layer exclusion.
  • UPSC implements EWS quota horizontally across all vertical categories in central recruitments.
  • Supreme Court (Janhit Abhiyan, 2022) upheld the amendment, ruling the 50 % ceiling is not absolute.
  • Verification of EWS certificates remains a practical challenge; digital verification and post‑selection checks are evolving.

Finance Commission: GST Impact on the Divisible Pool, the 15th FC's Approach and the Emerging Federal Fiscal Council Proposal

The Goods and Services Tax (GST), launched on 1 July 2017, subsumed multiple indirect taxes and altered the composition of the divisible pool shared between the Centre and the States. Under Article 270, the net proceeds of Union taxes (excluding surcharges and cesses) form the divisible pool. GST revenues are split into Central GST (CGST) and State GST (SGST); the Integrated GST (IGST) on inter‑state supplies is apportioned between the Centre and the destination State. The 15th Finance Commission (FC‑XV), constituted in 2017 for the 2020‑25 period, was the first to operate fully under the GST regime.

FC‑XV’s Terms of Reference (ToR) required it to assess the impact of GST on the fiscal capacity of States and to recommend a revised vertical devolution formula. The Commission retained the 41 % vertical share for States (same as FC‑XIV) but introduced new horizontal criteria: “demographic performance” (12.5 %), “tax effort” (2.5 %), “forest and ecology” (10 %) and “income distance” (45 %). The inclusion of forest and ecology recognised the fiscal disability of States with large forest cover that cannot exploit mineral resources. The Commission also recommended a “GST compensation cess” to be levied on sin goods and luxury items until 2026, with proceeds earmarked for compensating States for revenue loss during the transition.

A notable recommendation of FC‑XV was the establishment of a “Federal Fiscal Council” (FFC) — a permanent, institutionalised platform for Centre‑State fiscal dialogue, modelled on the Australian Commonwealth Grants Commission. The FFC would advise on GST rate changes, compensation cess design, and the rationalisation of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS). The 2023‑24 Union Budget announced the formation of a high‑level committee to examine the FFC proposal, but legislative backing is pending.

The 16th Finance Commission (to be constituted for 2026‑31) will confront the expiry of the GST compensation cess, the integration of the “One Nation One Tax” reforms (e‑invoicing, e‑way bill) into revenue forecasting, and the demand for a more dynamic horizontal formula that captures real‑time economic activity. The evolving architecture underscores the Finance Commission’s role as a dynamic arbiter of fiscal federalism, balancing equity, efficiency and political acceptability.

Key Takeaways

  • GST reshaped the divisible pool; FC‑XV was the first Commission to fully incorporate GST revenues.
  • FC‑XV retained 41 % vertical devolution but introduced new horizontal weights (demographic performance, tax effort, forest ecology).
  • GST compensation cess recommended until 2026; its expiry will be a key issue for FC‑XVI.
  • Federal Fiscal Council proposal aims to institutionalise Centre‑State fiscal dialogue; still at committee stage.

Common Traps

  • Assuming the Model Code of Conduct has statutory force; it is non‑statutory and enforceable only through the Election Commission’s moral authority.
  • Treating CAG’s performance audit reports as automatically binding on the executive; they are advisory and require Parliamentary committee action.
  • Believing the 103rd Amendment’s EWS quota is subject to a creamy layer test; the amendment explicitly excludes creamy layer for EWS.
  • Confusing the vertical devolution share (41 % under FC‑XV) with horizontal distribution weights; they operate at different levels of the fiscal transfer architecture.
  • Thinking the GST compensation cess is a permanent feature; it is slated to expire in 2026 unless extended by legislation.
  • Overlooking that the Supreme Court’s Janhit Abhiyan judgment upheld the 103rd Amendment by a narrow 3:2 majority, leaving room for future constitutional challenges.
  • Ignoring the 2020 CAG Act amendment’s six‑month timeline for special audits; this procedural mandate is often missed in answer writing.
