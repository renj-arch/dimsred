Section 1: Governance — Meaning, Dimensions & Theoretical Frameworks

1.1 Defining Governance: From Government to Governance

Governance is not synonymous with government. Government refers to the formal institutions of the state — legislature, executive, judiciary — that possess a monopoly on legitimate coercion. Governance is broader: it encompasses the processes, mechanisms, and institutions through which citizens and groups articulate their interests, exercise legal rights, meet obligations, and mediate differences (Source: UNDP, "Governance for Sustainable Human Development", 1997). The World Bank defines governance as "the manner in which power is exercised in the management of a country's economic and social resources for development" (Source: World Bank, "Governance and Development", 1992).

In the Indian constitutional scheme, governance operates at three tiers — Union, State, and Local (Panchayats and Municipalities) — each with distinct legislative, executive, and financial powers under Schedules VII, XI, and XII. The shift from "government" to "governance" in policy discourse post-1990s reflects the recognition that state capacity alone cannot deliver development; markets, civil society, and citizen participation are co-producers of public outcomes (Source: 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission, 1st Report "Right to Information", 2005).

ActorsState institutions onlyState, market, civil society, citizens
ModeHierarchical commandNetworks, partnerships, co-production

1.3 Theoretical Perspectives on Governance

For Mains answer-writing, citing the theoretical lens adds analytical depth. Three frameworks are most relevant:

  • New Public Management (NPM): Market-inspired reforms — competition, performance measurement, customer orientation, disaggregation of monolithic departments. In India: agencification (CBEC → GSTN, CBDT → Income Tax Department restructuring), PPP models, lateral entry, Mission Karmayogi (Source: 2nd ARC, 10th Report "Refurbishing of Personnel Administration", 2008).
  • New Public Governance (NPG): Emphasises networks, trust, co-production, and collaborative governance over contracts and competition. In India: Gram Sabhas, Ward Committees, School Management Committees (RTE Act), Rogi Kalyan Samitis (NHM), JFM Committees (Source: PRIA & UN, "State of Participatory Governance in India", 2014).
  • Digital Governance / Algorithmic Governance: Data-driven decision-making, platform government (India Stack: Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, e-KYC), and the emerging challenge of algorithmic accountability. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the proposed Digital India Act address this (Source: Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023; MeitY White Paper on Digital India Act, 2023).
Mains Answer-Writing: Theoretical Frameworks
When a question asks "examine governance challenges in X", open with a one-sentence definition citing UNDP/World Bank, then structure the body using the good governance characteristics as sub-headings. In the conclusion, name the theoretical lens (NPM/NPG/Digital Governance) that best explains the reform trajectory. This signals analytical rigour to the examiner.

1.4 Governance Deficits in India: A Diagnosed Critique

Despite constitutional architecture and reform waves, persistent deficits are documented across official reports:

  • Implementation Gap: The "knowing-doing gap" — policies are well-designed but poorly executed. The 2nd ARC (14th Report, "Strengthening Financial Management Systems", 2009) noted that only 15-20% of plan outlays translate into outcomes at the cutting edge.
  • Accountability Vacuum: Horizontal accountability (CAG, CVC, Lokpal, CIC) is strong on paper but weakened by vacancies, delayed appointments, and resource constraints. The Lokpal chairperson was appointed in March 2019 — five years after the Act (Source: Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013; Gazette Notification, 23 March 2019).
  • Citizen Disconnect: Social audits (mandatory under MGNREGA) are ritualistic in many states. The 2nd ARC (1st Report) observed that RTI applications are often met with delays, denials on flimsy grounds, and hostile PIOs.
  • Federal Asymmetry: States vary widely in administrative capacity. The 15th Finance Commission highlighted that per capita administrative expenditure ranges from ~₹3,000 in Bihar to ~₹15,000 in Goa, affecting service delivery (Source: 15th Finance Commission Report, 2020-25).
  • Criminalisation of Politics: ADR data shows 43% of MPs in the 17th Lok Sabha (2019) had declared criminal cases; 29% had serious charges (murder, rape, kidnapping) (Source: Association for Democratic Reforms, "Analysis of Criminal Background of MPs", 2019). The Supreme Court in Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India (2018) directed parties to publish candidates' criminal antecedents but enforcement remains weak.
PYQ 2021 GS2 Q3

Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2021 Q3

  2. Body Paragraph 1 — Necessity for Development (120 words): Evidence: DBT reduced leakages by ₹2.7 lakh crore (2014-2023) (Source: DBT Bharat Portal, 2023); Ayushman Bharat covers 10.74 crore families; Gram Sabha participation improved MGNREGA asset quality (CAG Audit 2018).

1.6 Critique: The "Governance" Label as Evasion

A critical perspective: the shift from "government" to "governance" can mask state retreat. Scholars like Jesse Robison (2015) argue that "governance" language legitimises privatisation of public functions without democratic accountability. In India, PPPs in health (PMJAY empanelment), education (adarsh schools), and infrastructure (toll-operate-transfer) transfer risk to the private sector while the state bears fiscal liability. The CAG's 2020 report on PPPs in NHAI highlighted revenue leakage and concessionaire-friendly renegotiations (Source: CAG Report No. 15 of 2020, "Public Private Partnerships in National Highways"). For Mains, this critique should appear in "critical analysis" questions — balance it with the counter-argument that state capacity is finite and partnerships expand the resource envelope.

Solved Mini-Example: Governance vs Government in a Mains Question
Question: "The shift from government to governance has strengthened democracy in India." Critically examine. (10 marks)
Structure: (1) Define both terms in 30 words. (2) Three arguments FOR: decentralisation (73rd/74th Amendments), RTI empowering citizens, participatory mechanisms (Gram Sabha, social audit). (3) Three arguments AGAINST: accountability deficit in PPPs/NGOs, digital exclusion, criminalisation of politics unaddressed. (4) Synthesis: Governance deepens democracy only when state capacity and citizen power grow together — not when state withdraws. Cite 2nd ARC, CAG reports, ADR data.

2.2 The "Pleasure Doctrine" and Article 311: Judicial Interpretation

Article 310 states civil servants hold office "during the pleasure of the President/Governor". In English law, this means termination at will. In India, the Supreme Court has read Article 311 as a constitutional limitation on the pleasure doctrine:

  • State of Bihar v. Abdul Majid (1954): "Pleasure" under Art. 310 is not absolute; it is subject to Art. 311 safeguards. Dismissal without inquiry violates Art. 311(2) (Source: AIR 1954 SC 245).
  • Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985): The "impracticability" exception (Art. 311(2)(c)) is subjective satisfaction of the President/Governor but must be bona fide and not arbitrary. The Court upheld the 1976 amendment inserting Art. 311(2)(c) (Source: AIR 1985 SC 1416).
  • State of UP v. Singhara Singh (1964): "Reduction in rank" includes reversion from officiating to substantive post; triggers Art. 311(2) (Source: AIR 1964 SC 583).
  • Khem Chand v. Union of India (1958): Probationers are not entitled to Art. 311(2) protection during probation; but once confirmed, protection applies (Source: AIR 1958 SC 300).
  • Shyam Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1973): Article 311 protection extends to "persons appointed to civil services/posts under the Union/State" — not to defence services (Art. 310 exception) or holders of political offices (Source: AIR 1973 SC 1073).
Exam Trap: Article 311 Does NOT Protect Against Transfer
Article 311 safeguards apply only to dismissal, removal, or reduction in rank. Routine transfers, even if punitive in effect, are not covered unless they amount to "reduction in rank" (e.g., reversion from a higher post). The Supreme Court in State of Punjab v. Joginder Singh (1990) held that transfer is an incident of service, not a penalty. However, 2nd ARC (10th Report) recommended that transfers of honest officers as punishment should be treated as a "penalty" requiring Art. 311 inquiry — this remains unimplemented legislatively.

2.3 All India Services: Constitutional Design and Federal Tension

Article 312 is a unique federal innovation. The Constitution-makers (Dr. Ambedkar, Sardar Patel) designed AIS to:

  • Ensure a "steel frame" of administration with national outlook
  • Provide trained personnel to States, especially weaker ones
  • Maintain Centre-State coordination in critical domains (law & order, revenue, forestry)

Legal Position: AIS officers are recruited by UPSC (Centre), trained at LBSNAA (Centre), but serve under State governments. Their cadre allocation is by Centre (DoPT), but disciplinary control is shared — the State government initiates proceedings, but the Central Government (DoPT) has appellate/revisional authority under the AIS (D&A) Rules, 1969 (Source: All India Services (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969; Rule 7 — Central Government as appellate authority).

PYQ 2018 GS2 Q4
"The All India Services are a unique feature of Indian federalism." Discuss the constitutional provisions and the tensions in their operation. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2018 Q4
  1. Introduction (40 words): Art. 312 origin (Sardar Patel's vision); Rajya Sabha resolution requirement (federal consensus); three AIS — IAS, IPS, IFoS.
  2. Constitutional Design (100 words): Dual control — recruitment/training by Centre, field service under States; Art. 311 protection; AIS (D&A) Rules give Centre revisional powers; Art. 312A allows Parliament to vary service conditions.
  3. Tensions — Federal (120 words): States allege "centralisation" — cadre allocation (Centre decides), deputation (Centre "poaches" best officers), disciplinary control (Centre overrides State). Examples: West Bengal (1990s), Tamil Nadu (2019) disputes over deputation of IPS officers. Punjab (1980s) sought State-specific services.
  4. Tensions — Operational (100 words): "Cadre depletion" — States reluctant to relieve officers for Central deputation; "insider-outsider" friction; politicised postings (transfer as punishment); 2nd ARC noted AIS officers perceive Centre as "real employer".
  5. Reforms / Way Forward (80 words): 2nd ARC: State-specific cadres within AIS; fixed tenure (3 years) for field postings; Civil Services Board for transfers; lateral entry at JS level to reduce Centre's monopoly on talent. NITI Aayog's "Strategy for New India @75" echoed fixed tenure.
  6. Conclusion (40 words): AIS remains the "steel frame" but federal friction is structural. Resolution lies in institutionalising Civil Services Boards (statutory backing) and giving States greater say in cadre management — cooperative federalism in personnel administration.

2.6 Mission Karmayogi: The Competency-Based Shift

Launched in September 2020, the National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building (NPCSCB) — "Mission Karmayogi" — marks a paradigm shift from rule-based to role-based, and from training to continuous learning.

  • Institutional Architecture: Prime Minister's HR Council (apex) → Cabinet Secretary Coordination Unit → Capacity Building Commission (CBC) → Karmayogi Bharat (SPV, operates iGOT platform) (Source: Cabinet Approval, 2 Sept 2020; NPCSCB Guidelines).
  • iGOT Karmayogi Platform: Integrated Government Online Training — 800+ courses, 30 lakh+ registered learners (2024). Courses on domain knowledge, functional skills, behavioural competencies, and constitutional values.
  • FRACs (Framework for Roles, Activities, Competencies): Each post mapped to roles → activities → competencies (domain, functional, behavioural). Replaces generic "seniority-based" promotion with competency assessment.
  • Annual Capacity Building Plan (ACBP): Every Ministry/Department/State prepares ACBP aligned to national priorities (Aspirational Districts, Gati Shakti, Green Growth, etc.).
  • Shift in Appraisal: SPARROW (Smart Performance Appraisal Report Recording Online Window) now incorporates 360° feedback and competency evaluation, not just ACR/APAR grading.
Solved Mini-Example: Mission Karmayogi vs. Traditional Training
Question: How does Mission Karmayogi differ from the traditional training model for civil servants? (10 marks)
Traditional: Rule-based, one-time (induction + mid-career), supply-driven (LBSNAA/IIPA decide curriculum), classroom-heavy, seniority-linked promotion. Mission Karmayogi: Role-based, continuous (lifelong learning), demand-driven (FRACs map competencies to posts), digital-first (iGOT), competency-linked career progression. Key shift: from "training as event" to "learning as process"; from "generalist" to "specialist-generalist" (T-shaped officers).

2.7 Lateral Entry: Breaking the Monopoly

Since 2018, the Government has advertised lateral entry positions at Joint Secretary (JS) and Director/Deputy Secretary levels in Central Ministries. As of 2024, nine batches have been recruited — domain experts from private sector, academia, PSUs, and State services (Source: DoPT Advertisements 2018-2024; PIB Releases).

  • Rationale: 2nd ARC (10th Report) recommended lateral entry to infuse domain expertise (e.g., finance, technology, climate, infrastructure) at policy-making levels. NITI Aayog's "Strategy for New India @75" (2018) endorsed it.
  • Critique: Bypasses UPSC (constitutional recruiter under Art. 320); no reservation (SC/ST/OBC/EWS) in initial batches — criticised by parliamentary committees; short tenure (3-5 years) may not build institutional memory; "insider-outsider" friction with regular AIS officers.
  • Counter-measures: DoPT issued guidelines (2021) for reservation in lateral entry; 2023 advertisements included SC/ST/OBC/EWS quotas. Karmayogi Bharat aims to upskill regular officers in parallel.
PYQ 2022 GS2 Q5
"Lateral entry into civil services is a necessary reform but raises constitutional and equity concerns." Discuss. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2022 Q5
  1. Introduction (40 words): Lateral entry = domain experts recruited at JS/DS level (since 2018). Rationale: 2nd ARC, NITI Aayog — generalist IAS cannot cover all specialisations (AI, climate, fintech).
  2. Constitutional Concerns (100 words): Art. 320 — UPSC shall be consulted on recruitment to civil services. Lateral entry via DoPT advertisement bypasses UPSC. Art. 14/16 — equality of opportunity; no reservation initially violated Art. 16(4). Art. 311 — lateral entrants not covered by Art. 311 safeguards (contractual).
  3. Equity Concerns (100 words): No SC/ST/OBC/EWS quota in first 5 batches (2018-2022) — Parliamentary Committee on Personnel (2021) flagged this. "Privatisation by stealth" — regular officers face stagnation. Short tenure (3-5 yrs) vs. lifetime career — accountability gap.
  4. Merits / Necessity (100 words): Domain gaps: e.g., NITI Aayog's AI cell, Climate Finance unit, Gati Shakti. Private sector talent (ex-McKinsey, World Bank, IIT professors) brings global best practices. 2nd ARC: "Civil services must reflect the complexity of modern governance."
  5. Reforms / Way Forward (80 words): DoPT 2023 guidelines: reservation applied. UPSC involvement in selection (screening/interview) — restores Art. 320. Extend Art. 311-type safeguards via contract terms. Hybrid model: "Specialist Cadre" within AIS (2nd ARC recommendation) — lateral entry as feeder, not parallel track.
  6. Conclusion (40 words): Lateral entry is inevitable for a $5T economy but must be constitutionally anchored — UPSC-led, reservation-compliant, with tenure security. The end-state: a unified civil service with generalist and specialist streams.

2.8 Civil Service Neutrality vs. Commitment: The Enduring Debate

The tension between neutrality (serving any elected government impartially) and commitment (aligning with the government's development agenda) is central to Indian civil service ethics.

  • Neutrality Model (Weberian): Civil servants are "anonymous" — Minister answers to Parliament; civil servant implements lawfully. Art. 311 safeguards protect against political victimisation. 2nd ARC (10th Report) reaffirmed neutrality as non-negotiable.
  • Committed Bureaucracy (1970s): During Emergency (1975-77), "committed bureaucracy" was advocated — civil servants should be ideologically aligned with the ruling party. The Shah Commission (1978) condemned this as subversion of constitutional neutrality.
  • Contemporary Synthesis: "Neutrality in means, commitment to ends." Civil servants must implement the government's lawful policies (commitment to constitutional goals — Art. 38, 39) but resist unlawful/partisan directives. The Conduct Rules (1964, updated 2014) prohibit political activity (Rule 5) but require "courtesy and consideration to MPs/MLAs" (Rule 3A) — a practical balance.
  • Judicial View: In T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013), the Supreme Court directed fixed tenure (min. 2 years) for civil servants, Civil Services Boards for transfers, and protection from oral directives — reinforcing neutrality (Source: Writ Petition (Civil) No. 82 of 2011, decided 31 Oct 2013).
Mains Keyword: "Neutrality is not Passivity"
In answers, distinguish: Neutrality ≠ Inaction. A neutral civil servant actively implements the government's lawful programme, offers frank advice (including dissent on file), and resists illegal orders. The T.S.R. Subramanian judgment (2013) and 2nd ARC (10th Report) are the authoritative sources. Quote the Conduct Rules: "shall not canvass for political party" but "shall implement policies of the government of the day."

2.10 Critique: The "Steel Frame" in the 21st Century

Three structural critiques dominate contemporary discourse:

  1. Generalist Dominance in a Specialist Age: IAS officers head Ministries of Health, Environment, Science & Technology, Electronics & IT — domains requiring deep technical expertise. The 2nd ARC (10th Report) recommended "specialist streams" within AIS; NITI Aayog (2017) proposed "domain-based recruitment" at entry. Lateral entry is a partial fix but does not address the pipeline for 5,000+ IAS officers.
  2. Accountability Without Authority — Authority Without Accountability: Field officers (District Collectors, SPs) have immense authority but weak outcome accountability (transfers are the only lever). Senior officers (Secretaries) have outcome responsibility but limited authority over field implementation. The "District Collector as CEO" model (2nd ARC, 6th Report) remains unrealised in most States.
  3. Risk Aversion and "File Notings" Culture: Fear of investigative agencies (CBI, CVC, ED) induces defensive decision-making. The 2nd ARC (4th Report "Ethics in Governance") recommended distinguishing bona fide errors from malafide corruption — only the latter should attract criminal proceedings. The Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018 introduced "prior sanction" for investigating public servants (Sec. 17A) but its application remains contested (Source: Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018; Sec. 17A).
Solved Mini-Example: Risk Aversion in Civil Services
Question: "Risk aversion among civil servants is the biggest impediment to decision-making." Examine with reference to the Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018. (10 marks)
Structure: (1) Acknowledge the problem — "3C" syndrome (CBI, CVC, Courts) creates fear of retrospective scrutiny. (2) PCA Amendment 2018: Sec. 17A — prior sanction for investigation; Sec. 13(1)(d) narrowed — "disproportionate assets" requires proof of intentional enrichment; Sec. 19 — prior sanction for prosecution retained. (3) Critique: Sec. 17A applies even to "bona fide" decisions; sanctioning authority is often the same Ministry — conflict of interest. (4) Way forward: 2nd ARC recommendation — "error of judgment" vs "corruption" distinction in law; independent prosecution sanction authority; sunset clauses for old decisions; protect whistleblowers (Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 — not fully operationalised).

Common Traps

  • Confusing Central Services with All India Services — AIS officers serve under States but are recruited and controlled by the Centre (DoPT).
  • Thinking Article 311 protects against transfer — it covers only dismissal, removal, reduction in rank. Transfer is an executive prerogative (unless it amounts to reduction in rank).
  • Believing Citizen's Charter is legally enforceable — unlike RTI, it has no statutory backing; Sevottam is a quality model, not a law.
  • Assuming lateral entry positions are permanent — they are contractual (3-5 years), not cadre posts.
  • Missing that UPSC is consulted on Art. 320 matters — but lateral entry initially bypassed this; 2023 guidelines restore UPSC role.
  • Overlooking that 73rd/74th Amendments created District Planning Committees (Art. 243ZD) — a constitutional bridge between Panchayats/Municipalities and district administration.
  • Confusing CVC (vigilance oversight) with Lokpal (anti-corruption ombudsman) — CVC advises; Lokpal inquires and prosecutes.

Memory Tricks

  • SMART: Simple, Moral, Accountable, Responsive, Transparent — e-governance goals (NeGP/Digital India).
  • RTI Flow: PIO → First Appellate Authority → State/Central Information Commission.
  • Services Pyramid: All India Services (Centre+State) → Central Services (Centre) → State Services (State) → Local Services (Local Body).
  • Accountability Trio: CAG (Audit, Art. 148), CVC (Vigilance, 2003 Act), Lokpal (Corruption, 2013 Act).
  • 2nd ARC Reports Map: 1-RTI, 4-Ethics, 5-Public Order, 6-Local Governance, 7-Capacity Building, 10-Personnel, 12-Citizen Centric, 14-Financial Management, 15-State Administration.
  • Pleasure Doctrine Exceptions: Art. 311(2) — Inquiry mandatory; 311(2)(a) criminal conviction; 311(2)(b) security of State; 311(2)(c) impracticability.
Mains Exam-Day Strategy for Governance & Civil Services
For governance questions: (1) Define using UNDP/World Bank; (2) Map each dimension to Indian constitutional/statutory provision (Art. 243G, RTI Act, CAG, etc.); (3) Cite 2nd ARC report numbers; (4) Use data (DBT savings, MPI, NFHS, PLFS) sparingly but precisely; (5) End with a reform agenda (Mission Karmayogi, lateral entry, Civil Services Boards). For civil services questions: always cite Art. 309-323, Art. 311, T.S.R. Subramanian (2013), 2nd ARC 10th Report. Distinguish neutrality vs commitment, generalist vs specialist, Centre vs State control. The phrase "cooperative federalism in personnel administration" is a high-value conclusion.
