1. Constitutional Framework and Appointment Mechanism

1.1 The Constitutional Basis of the Judiciary

The Constitution of India establishes an integrated judicial system with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts at the state level, and a subordinate judiciary beneath them. Article 124 provides for the establishment of the Supreme Court, while Article 214 creates a High Court for each state. The judiciary is designed to be independent of the executive and legislature, a principle reinforced by the security of tenure, fixed service conditions, and the requirement that salaries and allowances be charged on the Consolidated Fund of India. This structural independence is intended to enable courts to interpret and apply the law without external pressure, thereby upholding the rule of law and protecting fundamental rights.

1.2 Appointment of Judges: From Executive Discretion to the Collegium

Originally, the President appointed Supreme Court and High Court judges on the advice of the Council of Ministers, which meant the executive exercised effective control over judicial appointments. The Supreme Court, in the First Judges Case (1981), held that the opinion of the Chief Justice of India was not binding on the President. This position was reversed in the Second Judges Case (1993), where a nine‑judge bench declared that the Chief Justice’s recommendation, formed after consultation with the two senior‑most judges, would be binding. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the Chief Justice and the four senior‑most judges of the Supreme Court for Supreme Court appointments, and to the Chief Justice and two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments. The collegium system thus emerged as a judicial self‑selection mechanism intended to safeguard independence, though it has been criticised for opacity and lack of accountability.

1.3 The National Judicial Appointments Commission and Its Aftermath

In 2014 Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act and the 99th Constitutional Amendment, seeking to replace the collegium with a body comprising the Chief Justice, two senior judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court struck down both the Amendment and the Act in 2015, holding that they violated the basic structure doctrine by compromising judicial independence. The Court reiterated that the primacy of the judiciary in appointments is an essential feature of the Constitution. Since then, the collegium continues to operate, while the government and the judiciary have engaged in a memorandum of procedure to introduce greater transparency, such as publishing reasons for recommendations and establishing a secretariat. The debate remains live, with arguments focusing on the balance between independence, accountability, and diversity in the higher judiciary.

2. Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts

2.1 Original, Appellate and Advisory Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

Article 131 confers original jurisdiction on the Supreme Court in disputes between the Government of India and one or more states, or between states themselves. This jurisdiction is exclusive, meaning no other court can entertain such disputes. The appellate jurisdiction, under Articles 132, 133 and 134, allows the Supreme Court to hear appeals from High Courts in constitutional, civil and criminal matters, provided the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law. Additionally, Article 136 grants the Supreme Court discretionary special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree or order of any court or tribunal in the territory of India, making it the ultimate appellate forum. The advisory jurisdiction under Article 143 enables the President to refer questions of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its opinion, though the opinion is not binding.

2.2 Jurisdiction and Powers of High Courts

Each High Court enjoys original jurisdiction in matters such as writ petitions under Article 226, which is broader than the Supreme Court’s writ jurisdiction under Article 32 because it extends to any legal right, not only fundamental rights. High Courts also exercise appellate jurisdiction over subordinate courts within their territorial limits, and they have supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227, allowing them to ensure that lower courts and tribunals act within their authority. The power to issue directions, orders or writs for the enforcement of fundamental rights and for any other purpose makes High Courts the primary guardians of constitutional rights at the state level. Their decisions are binding on all subordinate courts within the state, and they play a crucial role in shaping the jurisprudence that eventually reaches the Supreme Court.

2.3 Inter‑Court Relations and the Doctrine of Stare Decisis

The Supreme Court’s decisions are binding on all courts in India under Article 141, establishing a vertical stare decisis that ensures uniformity of law. High Courts are bound by Supreme Court precedents, but they are not bound by decisions of other High Courts, though such decisions carry persuasive value. When a High Court disagrees with a Supreme Court ruling, it must follow the Supreme Court; however, it may refer the matter to a larger bench of the Supreme Court for reconsideration. This hierarchical structure, combined with the power of the Supreme Court to transfer cases from one High Court to another under Article 139A, maintains coherence in the judicial system while allowing regional courts to develop context‑specific jurisprudence within the bounds of the supreme law.

Example Question
Explain the distinction between the original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 131 and the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226. Illustrate with one scenario where each would be invoked.
The original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 131 is limited to disputes between the Union and one or more states, or between states inter se. It does not extend to disputes involving private parties or violations of fundamental rights. For instance, a water‑sharing dispute between State A and State B would be filed directly in the Supreme Court under Article 131. In contrast, the writ jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226 is wide: it can issue writs for the enforcement of fundamental rights and for any other legal right. A citizen whose property has been illegally acquired by a municipal corporation can approach the relevant High Court under Article 226 seeking a writ of mandamus or certiorari. Thus, Article 131 addresses inter‑governmental disputes, while Article 226 provides a remedy against any authority for breach of legal rights.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks you to compare jurisdictions, structure the answer around the constitutional articles, the nature of parties involved, and the type of relief available. Explicitly mention Article 131 for Supreme Court original jurisdiction and Article 226 for High Court writ jurisdiction; this signals precise knowledge to the examiner.

2.4 Special Leave and the Reach of the Supreme Court

Beyond the jurisdictions named in the Constitution, Article 136 confers on the Supreme Court a residuary power to grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order of any court or tribunal in the territory of India, except a court-martial. This discretionary power is wider than any ordinary appellate jurisdiction: it is not confined to civil or criminal matters and is not limited by the value or the nature of the dispute. The Court has repeatedly held that although the power is discretionary it must be exercised on settled principles rather than arbitrarily. Read with Article 124, which establishes the Court and the process for the appointment and removal of its judges, Article 141, which makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts, and Article 142, which empowers it to pass any order necessary for complete justice, Article 136 explains why the Court sits at the apex of a single, integrated judicial system rather than as one final court among several in a federal structure.

3. Judicial Review and its Evolution

3.1 The Constitutional Foundation of Judicial Review

Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive actions. Although the Constitution does not use the term “judicial review”, Articles 13, 32, 136, 141, 142 and 226 collectively confer this authority. Article 13 declares that any law inconsistent with fundamental rights shall be void to the extent of the inconsistency, and it empowers the courts to strike down such laws. The Supreme Court, in the landmark case of Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), held that judicial review is a basic feature of the Constitution, meaning it cannot be abrogated even by a constitutional amendment. This doctrine ensures that the supremacy of the Constitution is maintained and that the legislature and executive do not exceed their prescribed limits.

3.2 Expansion of Judicial Review through Landmark Cases

After Kesavananda, the scope of judicial review widened considerably. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Court struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that sought to oust judicial review of constitutional amendments, reinforcing the basic structure doctrine. The decision in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) extended judicial review to laws placed in the Ninth Schedule, holding that any law violating fundamental rights could be examined despite its inclusion in the Schedule. The Court has also reviewed executive actions for arbitrariness, as in the case of Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the procedure established by law was read to require fairness, reasonableness and non‑arbitrariness. These rulings collectively transformed judicial review from a narrow check on legislative competence into a robust instrument for protecting constitutional values against majoritarian excesses.

3.3 Limits and Safeguards on Judicial Review

While judicial review is extensive, it is not unlimited. The doctrine of political questions, though not formally adopted in India, operates informally; courts generally refrain from adjudicating matters that are constitutionally committed to the political branches, such as the conduct of foreign policy or the declaration of war. The principle of separation of powers requires courts to respect the domain of the legislature in policy formulation, intervening only when constitutional boundaries are crossed. Moreover, the Supreme Court has self‑imposed restraints, such as the requirement of a substantial question of law for appellate review and the reluctance to interfere with contractual obligations of the state unless fundamental rights are implicated. These limits preserve the democratic legitimacy of the judiciary while allowing it to act as the final arbiter of constitutional disputes.

4. Judicial Activism and Public Interest Litigation

4.1 Emergence of Public Interest Litigation

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) emerged in the late 1970s as a judicial innovation to provide access to justice for marginalised groups who could not approach courts individually. The relaxation of the rule of locus standi allowed any public‑spirited citizen or organisation to file a petition on behalf of those whose rights were being violated. The Supreme Court, in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981), recognised that the procedural rigours of traditional litigation should not become a barrier to the enforcement of fundamental rights. This shift transformed the judiciary from a passive adjudicator into an active participant in governance, enabling courts to issue directions on issues ranging from environmental protection to prison reforms.

4.2 Landmark PIL Decisions and Their Impact

Several PIL judgments have reshaped public policy. In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987), the Court ordered the closure of polluting industries in the Ganga basin, establishing the principle of absolute liability for hazardous activities. The Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) case laid down guidelines for preventing sexual harassment at the workplace, which later formed the basis of the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013. In the Right to Food case (People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India, 2001), the Court converted the directive principles into enforceable obligations, directing the government to implement mid‑day meal schemes and food security programmes. These decisions illustrate how PIL has compelled the executive to act in areas where legislative or administrative inertia persisted.

4.3 Critiques and the Doctrine of Judicial Restraint

Despite its achievements, judicial activism through PIL has attracted criticism. Critics argue that courts sometimes assume executive functions, such as monitoring the implementation of welfare schemes, which may exceed their institutional competence and undermine accountability mechanisms. The Supreme Court itself has acknowledged the need for restraint; in the case of Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass (2008), it cautioned that courts should not become “super‑administrators”. The doctrine of judicial restraint advocates that courts should defer to the elected branches unless a clear constitutional violation is demonstrated. Balancing activism with restraint remains a central challenge, requiring courts to protect rights without displacing democratic decision‑making.

5. The Collegium System and Contemporary Debates

5.1 Functioning of the Collegium in Practice

In practice, the collegium for Supreme Court appointments consists of the Chief Justice of India and the four senior‑most judges. For High Court appointments, the collegium comprises the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court and its two senior‑most judges, whose recommendations are forwarded to the Supreme Court collegium for approval. The process involves evaluation of candidates’ integrity, competence, and seniority, though the criteria are not codified. The collegium’s decisions are communicated to the government, which may seek reconsideration but ultimately must appoint the recommended persons if the collegium reiterates its recommendation. This opaque, consensus‑based method has been praised for shielding appointments from political interference but criticised for lacking transparency, diversity, and a formal mechanism for public scrutiny.

5.2 Transparency Reforms and the Memorandum of Procedure

Following the NJAC judgment, the Supreme Court and the government negotiated a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) to introduce procedural safeguards. The MoP envisages the publication of the collegium’s resolutions, the establishment of a secretariat to assist in candidate evaluation, and the consideration of diversity in terms of region, gender, and marginalised communities. It also provides for a complaint mechanism against candidates and a time‑frame for the government to act on recommendations. While the MoP has improved procedural regularity, its implementation has been uneven, and the core deliberative secrecy of the collegium remains intact. The ongoing dialogue reflects the tension between preserving judicial independence and meeting democratic expectations of openness.

5.3 Future Trajectories: Institutionalising Appointments

The future of judicial appointments in India hinges on finding a model that reconciles independence with accountability. Proposals include a statutory appointments commission with a judicial majority, a transparent rating system for candidates, and a parliamentary oversight committee that reviews the collegium’s outcomes without veto power. Comparative experience from jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom’s Judicial Appointments Commission and South Africa’s Judicial Service Commission suggests that a mixed composition with clear criteria and public reporting can enhance legitimacy. Any reform must, however, respect the basic structure doctrine affirmed by the Supreme Court, ensuring that the primacy of the judiciary in appointments is not diluted. The evolution of this debate will shape the credibility of the higher judiciary for decades to come.

The judiciary’s constitutional design, its expanding review powers, and the ongoing discourse on appointments together define the dynamic equilibrium between law and governance in India.

Tribunals in India: Articles 323A and 323B

Constitutional Basis

The 42nd Amendment, 1976, introduced two enabling provisions. Article 323A allows Parliament to establish administrative tribunals for the adjudication of disputes relating to the recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or of a State. Article 323B allows the appropriate legislature, Parliament or a State legislature, to establish tribunals for the adjudication of other specified matters, such as taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, ceilings on urban property, elections to Parliament and the State legislatures, rent and tenancy, and the supply of essential goods and services. Article 323B therefore contemplates a much wider field of tribunalisation than Article 323A.

Administrative Tribunals and the CAT

Using Article 323A, Parliament enacted the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, creating the Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) and State Administrative Tribunals. These tribunals displaced the jurisdiction of the ordinary courts in service matters, and their orders are challenged before the High Courts. The CAT sits in benches across the country and is the principal forum for the service disputes of central government employees. Other specialised tribunals created under ordinary statutes include the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal, the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, the National Company Law Tribunal and its appellate tribunal, the Debt Recovery Tribunals, the Armed Forces Tribunal, and the National Green Tribunal established by the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.

Judicial Review and the Basic Structure

The constitutionality of excluding the High Courts' jurisdiction was settled in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997). The Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review vested in the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the basic structure of the Constitution and cannot be ousted. Tribunals may function as courts of first instance, but their decisions must remain subject to the supervisory jurisdiction of the High Courts. In later cases, including Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019), the Supreme Court struck down provisions of the Finance Act relating to tribunal appointments and called for the creation of a National Tribunals Commission to make appointments independent and transparent.

The Tribunal System and Its Criticisms

Tribunals were intended to provide speedy, specialised and less expensive adjudication than the ordinary courts. In practice, critics point to inconsistent service conditions and tenure, executive influence over appointments, vacancies and backlog, and the practice of staffing tribunals with retired judges and officers. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasised that the independence of tribunals requires security of tenure, fixed qualifications and transparent appointments, and the proposal for a National Tribunals Commission remains the principal reform on the table. Article 227 gives the High Courts superintendence over tribunals within their territorial jurisdiction, which is the principal institutional safeguard of accountability.

Basic Structure Doctrine: Evolution, Scope, and Limits

The basic structure doctrine, first articulated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), holds that certain constitutional features cannot be amended by Parliament under Article 368. The 13‑judge bench identified a non‑exhaustive list — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law — as forming the “basic structure”. Subsequent judgments refined the doctrine: Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that curtailed judicial review, affirming that the power of judicial review itself is part of the basic structure. In Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) the Court applied the doctrine prospectively, protecting amendments made before 24 April 1973 from challenge. The I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) decision extended basic‑structure protection to laws placed in the Ninth Schedule, ruling that any law violating fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure is open to judicial review despite its Schedule placement. The doctrine thus operates as a substantive limitation on constituent power, ensuring that the Constitution’s identity remains intact while allowing evolutionary amendments that do not alter its core.

Key Judicial Tests

  • Essential Features Test – a feature is “basic” if its removal would change the identity of the Constitution (Kesavananda).
  • Width Test – the amendment must not destroy the “essential features” of the Constitution (Minerva Mills).
  • Prospective Application – amendments before the Kesavananda date are immune (Waman Rao).

Practical Implications for Governance

Parliament may amend any provision, including fundamental rights, provided the amendment does not abrogate the basic structure. This has enabled significant reforms — such as the 73rd and 74th Amendments on local self‑government — while preventing attempts to concentrate power, as seen in the failed 42nd Amendment’s attempt to curtail judicial review. The doctrine also guides the Court when examining constitutional amendments that affect federal balance, secular character, or the independence of the judiciary.

Contempt of Court: Constitutional Provisions, Landmark Judgments, and Free Speech Tensions

Articles 129 and 215 empower the Supreme Court and High Courts respectively to punish contempt of themselves. The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 codifies civil contempt (wilful disobedience of judgments) and criminal contempt (scandalising the court, prejudicing judicial proceedings, or interfering with administration of justice). The Supreme Court in Baradakanta Mishra v. Registrar, Orissa High Court (1974) held that fair criticism of judicial decisions, made in good faith and without malice, does not constitute contempt. In Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998) the Court clarified that the power to punish contempt is inherent and not subject to legislative abridgement, but must be exercised sparingly to avoid chilling free speech under Article 19(1)(a). The Prashant Bhushan case (2020) illustrated the tension: the Court convicted the activist‑lawyer for tweets criticising the Chief Justice, sparking debate on proportionality and the scope of “scandalising”. The Court later imposed a nominal fine of ₹1, signalling a shift towards restraint. The Law Commission’s 274th Report (2018) recommended narrowing criminal contempt to “wilful disobedience” and removing “scandalising” as an offence, a proposal still under legislative consideration.

Balancing Test Applied by Courts

  1. Identify whether the statement is a fair, bona‑fide critique of a judicial act.
  2. Assess whether the publication creates a real and substantial risk of obstructing justice.
  3. Apply the principle of proportionality — punishment must be the least restrictive measure.

Exam‑Relevant Nuances

Questions often test the distinction between civil and criminal contempt, the constitutional source of contempt power, and the interplay with Article 19(2) (reasonable restrictions). A typical twist: “Can a citizen be punished for publishing a cartoon that ridicules a judge’s reasoning?” Answer: Only if the cartoon crosses from fair comment to scandalising the court with intent to undermine public confidence.

Examiner's trap
A question states: “The power of the Supreme Court to punish for contempt is derived solely from the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971.” Identify the error and give the correct constitutional basis.
The statement is false. The Supreme Court’s contempt power originates from Article 129 of the Constitution, which declares the Supreme Court a court of record and empowers it to punish for its contempt. The 1971 Act merely regulates the procedure; it does not confer the power. Similarly, High Courts derive the power from Article 215.
Exam‑day strategy
When a contempt question appears, first cite the constitutional article (129 or 215), then mention the 1971 Act for procedural details. Remember the “fair criticism” exception from Baradakanta Mishra and the proportionality principle from the Prashant Bhushan judgment. This two‑step citation fetches full marks.

Judicial Accountability: In‑House Procedure, Impeachment, and the Role of the Judges Inquiry Act

Accountability of higher‑court judges operates through three layers. The in‑house procedure, adopted by the Supreme Court in 1999 and later by High Courts, provides a confidential mechanism for the Chief Justice to receive complaints, constitute a three‑judge committee, and recommend corrective action — ranging from advice to voluntary retirement. It is not statutory and its outcomes are not publicly disclosed, which has drawn criticism for opacity. The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 governs the statutory impeachment process under Article 124(4) and Article 217(1)(b). A motion signed by at least 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members triggers an inquiry committee comprising a Supreme Court judge, a High Court Chief Justice, and a distinguished jurist. If the committee finds “proved misbehaviour or incapacity”, Parliament may remove the judge by a special majority in each House. Only two judges have faced impeachment proceedings: Justice V. Ramaswami (1993) — the inquiry committee found him guilty, but the Lok Sabha motion failed for lack of majority; and Justice Soumitra Sen (2011) — the Rajya Sabha passed the motion, but he resigned before Lok Sabha could act. The Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill (lapsed in 2014) sought to create a National Judicial Oversight Committee with lay members, but concerns over judicial independence stalled it. Current discourse focuses on strengthening the in‑house mechanism with a transparent complaint‑tracking portal while preserving the constitutional impeachment route as the ultimate safeguard.

Procedural Timeline for Impeachment

  1. Notice of motion submitted to Speaker/Chairman.
  2. Admission and constitution of three‑member inquiry committee.
  3. Committee submits report within three months (extendable).
  4. If report finds guilt, motion debated and voted in both Houses.
  5. Removal requires two‑thirds majority of members present and voting, and a majority of total membership.

Key Exam Points

  • Distinguish between “misbehaviour” (ethical breach) and “incapacity” (mental/physical).
  • In‑house procedure is administrative; impeachment is constitutional.
  • No judge has been removed via impeachment; the process serves as a deterrent.

Comparative Judicial Appointments: Lessons from the United Kingdom, United States, and South Africa

India’s collegium system is often benchmarked against three contrasting models. The United Kingdom established the Judicial Appointments Commission (JAC) in 2006 under the Constitutional Reform Act 2005. The JAC comprises 15 members — judicial, legal, and lay — and selects candidates on merit through open competition, publishing selection criteria and diversity statistics annually. The Lord Chancellor retains a limited veto on national security grounds. The United States follows a political appointment model: the President nominates, the Senate Judiciary Committee holds hearings, and the full Senate confirms by simple majority. This process subjects nominees to intense partisan scrutiny, resulting in ideological alignment but also prolonged vacancies. The South Africa model, created by the 1996 Constitution, uses the Judicial Service Commission (JSC) — a 23‑member body including the Chief Justice, Ministers, MPs, legal practitioners, and civil‑society representatives. The JSC interviews candidates publicly, recommends to the President, who must appoint. The South African system emphasises representivity and transformation, yet faces criticism for political interference in interviews. Comparative lessons for India include: (1) a mixed‑composition commission with a judicial majority can preserve primacy while adding transparency; (2) codified, merit‑based criteria and public reporting enhance legitimacy; (3) a fixed timeline for executive action prevents indefinite delays. The pending Memorandum of Procedure reforms in India echo the UK’s emphasis on published resolutions and diversity targets, while the NJAC judgment’s insistence on judicial primacy mirrors the South African constitutional guarantee of judicial independence.

Any Indian reform must respect the basic structure principle that judicial primacy in appointments is inviolable (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 2015). A statutory commission with a judicial majority, clear merit criteria, mandatory publication of shortlists, and a parliamentary oversight committee without veto power would align with comparative best practices while satisfying the Court’s constitutional mandate.

  • Confusing the in‑house procedure with the statutory impeachment process — they are distinct in source, scope, and outcome.
  • Assuming the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 is the sole source of contempt power; the Constitution (Articles 129, 215) is the fountainhead.
  • Treating the basic structure doctrine as a static list; the Court has repeatedly held it is an evolving concept.
  • Overlooking the prospective application rule from Waman Rao when analysing amendments before 1973.
  • Equating the UK JAC’s lay membership with the Indian collegium’s all‑judge composition — the former includes non‑lawyers, the latter does not.
  • Believing that the US Senate confirmation is a mere formality; historical data shows frequent rejections and strategic delays.
  • Ignoring the South African JSC’s transformation mandate, which explicitly considers race and gender — a factor absent from the current Indian MoP.

Article 142 and the Doctrine of Complete Justice

Article 142 of the Constitution empowers the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order necessary for doing “complete justice” in any cause or matter pending before it. The provision is unique because it confers a plenary, equitable jurisdiction that is not limited by statutory procedural codes. The Court has repeatedly held that Article 142 is a reservoir of power to be invoked only when the existing law provides no adequate remedy, and it cannot be used to override substantive statutory provisions or to create new rights.

The seminal exposition came in Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998), where a Constitution Bench ruled that the power under Article 142 is curative, not legislative. The Court may fill procedural gaps, modify its own orders, or direct executive action to give effect to its judgments, but it may not amend a statute or ignore a clear legislative mandate. In Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998), the Court used Article 142 to lay down guidelines for the functioning of the Central Bureau of Investigation, effectively creating a temporary regulatory framework until Parliament enacted the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003.

Limitations were sharpened in Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India (1991), where the Court refused to increase the compensation awarded under the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985, stating that Article 142 cannot be used to enhance statutory compensation. More recently, in Re: Distribution of Essential Supplies during COVID‑19 (2021), the Court directed the Centre to allocate oxygen to states, invoking Article 142 to overcome administrative inertia. The judgment reiterated that the power is extraordinary, to be exercised sparingly, and must be accompanied by reasons demonstrating that no alternative remedy exists.

Key take‑aways for analysis: (1) Article 142 is a supplementary jurisdiction, not a parallel legislative authority. (2) The Court must record a finding that the existing law is insufficient to achieve complete justice. (3) Orders under Article 142 are binding on all authorities, including the executive and legislature, but they remain subject to constitutional review.

Curative Petitions: The Final Tier of Judicial Review

The curative petition is a judicial innovation that provides a last‑resort remedy after a review petition has been dismissed. It originated in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002), where a Constitution Bench recognised that the review jurisdiction under Article 137 may not cure a gross miscarriage of justice caused by a violation of natural justice or a palpable error apparent on the face of the record. The Court laid down four cumulative conditions for entertaining a curative petition:

  1. The petitioner must establish a genuine violation of the principles of natural justice, such as bias or denial of a fair hearing.
  2. There must be a manifest error of law or fact that is apparent on the face of the record, not merely a debatable interpretation.
  3. The petition must be certified by a senior advocate of the Supreme Court, attesting that it meets the above criteria.
  4. The petition is circulated to the same Bench that decided the review petition, and if that Bench is unavailable, to the three senior‑most judges.

Since 2002, the Supreme Court has admitted curative petitions in only a handful of cases. In Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010), a curative petition was allowed where the original judgment had been delivered without hearing one of the parties. In State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh (2015), the Court rejected a curative petition because the alleged error was a matter of judicial discretion, not a patent illegality. The rarity of success underscores the doctrine’s role as a safety valve rather than a regular appellate tier.

Procedurally, a curative petition is filed under Order XLVIII of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013. It must be accompanied by an affidavit, the certified copy of the review order, and the senior advocate’s certificate. The Court may impose exemplary costs if the petition is found to be frivolous. The curative mechanism thus balances finality of litigation with the constitutional imperative of justice.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: “Explain the criteria for entertaining a curative petition under Indian law and illustrate with a landmark case.”
A model answer should first cite Article 137 and the 2002 Rupa Ashok Hurra judgment as the constitutional and judicial basis. Then list the four conditions: (i) violation of natural justice, (ii) manifest error apparent on record, (iii) senior advocate certification, (iv) circulation to the original Bench. Next, discuss the 2010 Union of India v. R. Gandhi case where a curative petition succeeded because a party was not heard. Conclude by noting the Court’s restrictive approach, citing the 2015 Jalour Singh rejection, and emphasise that curative petitions are exceptional, not a substitute for appeal.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three‑step hierarchy: review petition (Article 137) → curative petition (Rupa Ashok Hurra) → presidential pardon (Article 72). In answers, quote the four Hurra conditions verbatim and name at least one case where a curative petition was allowed and one where it was rejected. This demonstrates both doctrinal precision and awareness of judicial practice.

Judicial Review of Administrative Action: Wednesbury, Proportionality, and Legitimate Expectation

Indian administrative law has imported three principal standards of review from English jurisprudence and refined them under Articles 14 and 21. The Wednesbury unreasonableness test, originating in Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v. Wednesbury Corporation (1948), asks whether a decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have reached it. The Supreme Court adopted this in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977), holding that executive action must not be arbitrary, capricious, or devoid of rational nexus.

The proportionality test, stricter than Wednesbury, requires that the measure adopted be suitable, necessary, and balanced in its impact on fundamental rights. The Court formally embraced proportionality in Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000), stating that when a fundamental right is infringed, the State must demonstrate that the restriction is the least intrusive means to achieve a legitimate aim. This standard has since been applied in cases concerning preventive detention (Kharak Singh v. State of UP, 1963 reinterpreted), internet shutdowns (Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, 2020), and environmental clearances.

The doctrine of legitimate expectation protects a person’s reasonable anticipation of a benefit or procedure based on a consistent past practice or an explicit promise by the authority. In Navjot Singh v. State of Punjab (2005), the Court held that a candidate who had been assured of a particular recruitment process could claim a legitimate expectation, and any deviation required a fair hearing. The expectation must be reasonable, arise from a representation or consistent practice, and not conflict with statutory provisions.

These three standards operate hierarchically: Wednesbury is the baseline for non‑fundamental‑right cases; proportionality applies when fundamental rights are engaged; legitimate expectation adds a procedural fairness layer. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. G. Ganayutham (1997) clarified that legitimate expectation does not create a substantive right but entitles the claimant to a hearing before the expectation is frustrated.

The Supreme Court's Original Jurisdiction in Federal Disputes: Inter‑State Water and Boundary Cases

Article 131 confers exclusive original jurisdiction on the Supreme Court in disputes between the Government of India and one or more States, or between two or more States. This jurisdiction is both a constitutional safeguard of federalism and a practical forum for resolving inter‑state resource conflicts. The Court has interpreted “dispute” to require a legal right or obligation, not merely a political disagreement.

The most prominent water disputes have arisen under the Inter‑State Water Disputes Act, 1956, which provides for tribunal adjudication, but the Supreme Court retains the power to hear appeals and to issue directions for compliance. In State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu (Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal award, 2007; Supreme Court judgment 2018), the Court modified the tribunal’s final award, directing Karnataka to release 177.25 TMC of water annually, and established a supervisory mechanism for implementation. The judgment underscored that the Court’s original jurisdiction includes the authority to enforce tribunal awards and to issue interim directions.

Boundary disputes have also invoked Article 131. In State of Maharashtra v. State of Gujarat (1991), the Court demarcated the border based on the 1960 Bombay Reorganisation Act and historical revenue records. More recently, State of Assam v. State of Nagaland (2022) saw the Court appoint a boundary commission and direct maintenance of status quo pending final demarcation.

Procedurally, a State files a suit under Order I, Rule 1 of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013. The Court may appoint a commissioner, seek expert reports, or refer technical aspects to a tribunal. The judgments are binding and enforceable under Article 142, reinforcing the Court’s role as the ultimate arbiter of federal disputes.

Post-Retirement Appointments and the 'Cooling-Off' Debate

The independence of the judiciary is a cornerstone of constitutional democracy, safeguarded by provisions such as security of tenure, fixed salaries, and protection from arbitrary removal. However, a persistent debate revolves around the practice of judges accepting post-retirement appointments, particularly in governmental bodies or tribunals. While there is no explicit constitutional bar on such appointments for Supreme Court and High Court judges, except for the prohibition on practicing law in any court or before any authority in India (Article 124(7) for SC judges) or in the same High Court (Article 220 for HC judges), the ethical implications are significant.

Critics argue that the prospect of post-retirement sinecures can subtly influence judicial decision-making during active service, potentially compromising impartiality and public trust. The concern is that judges might consciously or unconsciously deliver judgments favourable to the executive or specific interests, anticipating a lucrative or prestigious appointment after demitting office. This perceived lack of a 'cooling-off' period is seen as undermining the very independence the Constitution seeks to protect.

Conversely, proponents argue that such appointments utilise valuable judicial experience and expertise for the benefit of the nation. Many retired judges head crucial commissions, tribunals, or serve as arbitrators, where their legal acumen and integrity are invaluable. Furthermore, given the relatively modest post-retirement benefits for judges compared to their private sector counterparts, these appointments can offer financial security and continued engagement in public service. The argument is also made that a blanket ban would be an overreaction, penalising competent individuals and depriving the state of their services.

Several bodies and committees have addressed this issue. The 14th Law Commission Report (1958) recommended a ban on post-retirement government appointments for Supreme Court judges. More recently, the 230th Law Commission Report (2009) reiterated concerns, suggesting a two-year 'cooling-off' period for judges before taking up any government position. Despite these recommendations, no formal legislation or constitutional amendment has been enacted to impose a mandatory cooling-off period or a complete ban. The practice continues, with retired judges often appointed as Governors, chairpersons of various commissions (e.g., National Human Rights Commission, Central Administrative Tribunal), or members of the Rajya Sabha. The absence of clear guidelines leaves the matter open to executive discretion and public scrutiny, perpetuating the debate on judicial independence versus the utilisation of expertise.

Judicial Delays, Case Management, and Alternative Dispute Resolution

One of the most pressing challenges facing the Indian judiciary is the colossal backlog of cases, leading to inordinate delays in justice delivery. Millions of cases are pending across all tiers of the judicial system, from subordinate courts to the Supreme Court. This pendency undermines the constitutional promise of speedy justice, erodes public faith in the system, and has significant socio-economic costs.

The causes of judicial delays are multi-faceted: a severe shortage of judges and judicial infrastructure, frequent adjournments, complex procedural laws, inadequate use of technology, and the sheer volume of litigation, including frivolous cases. The impact is profound: denial of timely justice often amounts to denial of justice itself, particularly for vulnerable sections of society. It also contributes to economic inefficiency by delaying dispute resolution for businesses and investors.

To address this, several reform initiatives have been undertaken. Case Management strategies aim to streamline judicial processes. The e-Courts Project, for instance, seeks to digitise court records, enable online filing, and facilitate virtual hearings, enhancing efficiency and transparency. The National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG) provides real-time data on case pendency, helping in better resource allocation and monitoring. Fast Track Courts were established for specific categories of cases, and the Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 aimed to provide access to justice at the grassroots level, though its implementation has been uneven.

Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) mechanisms have gained prominence as a means to decongest courts and provide quicker, more amicable solutions. Article 39A of the Constitution, which mandates the state to secure a legal system that promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity, implicitly supports ADR. Key ADR methods include:

  • Lok Adalats: Statutory bodies under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, facilitating amicable settlement of disputes through conciliation. Their awards are deemed civil court decrees.
  • Arbitration: Governed by the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, it involves a neutral third party (arbitrator) making a binding decision. It is widely used in commercial disputes.
  • Mediation: A voluntary process where a neutral mediator facilitates communication and negotiation between parties to reach a mutually acceptable settlement. It is often employed in family, labour, and commercial disputes.
  • Conciliation: Similar to mediation, but the conciliator may also suggest possible solutions.

While ADR mechanisms offer flexibility, cost-effectiveness, and speed, challenges remain in their widespread adoption and ensuring the enforceability of awards. The success of these reforms hinges on continuous investment in infrastructure, judicial appointments, and a cultural shift towards proactive case management and dispute resolution.

Examiner's technique: Distinguishing Judicial Independence from Accountability

Consider the following statements regarding the Indian Judiciary:

  1. Judicial independence is primarily ensured by the process of impeachment and the 'cooling-off' period for retired judges.
  2. Judicial accountability is upheld through the power of judicial review and the doctrine of judicial restraint.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(A) 1 only
(B) 2 only
(C) Both 1 and 2
(D) Neither 1 nor 2

Solution: (D) Neither 1 nor 2

Statement 1 is incorrect: While impeachment is a mechanism for removal (linked to independence by ensuring security of tenure), the 'cooling-off' period is a *proposed measure* to strengthen independence, not a currently enforced constitutional guarantee. More importantly, judicial independence is ensured by a host of constitutional provisions like security of tenure, fixed salaries, non-discussion of conduct in legislature (except impeachment), and separation of powers, not *primarily* by impeachment and a non-existent cooling-off period.

Statement 2 is incorrect: Judicial accountability refers to judges being answerable for their conduct and decisions. Mechanisms for accountability include the in-house procedure, impeachment, contempt of court, and the requirement to give reasoned judgments. Judicial review is a power of the judiciary to check the executive and legislature, not a mechanism for its own accountability. Judicial restraint is a self-imposed discipline, a principle of judicial behaviour, not an external mechanism for accountability.

The statements confuse the concepts and misattribute the mechanisms. Independence refers to the judiciary's ability to function without external pressure, while accountability refers to its answerability for its actions.

Mastering Conceptual Distinctions
For GS II, clarity on core concepts like 'judicial independence', 'judicial accountability', 'judicial review', and 'judicial activism' is paramount. Examiners often test your ability to distinguish between these closely related but distinct ideas. Create a mental or written matrix comparing their definitions, constitutional bases, key mechanisms, and implications. This helps avoid common traps where terms are used interchangeably or mechanisms are wrongly associated.

Doctrines of Severability and Colourable Legislation

The power of judicial review, a cornerstone of India's constitutional system, is often exercised through specific legal doctrines that guide the courts in determining the constitutionality of laws. Two such fundamental doctrines are the Doctrine of Severability and the Doctrine of Colourable Legislation, both rooted in the principle of constitutional supremacy.

The Doctrine of Severability (Article 13)

This doctrine, also known as the Doctrine of Separability, is enshrined in Article 13 of the Constitution. It states that if a part of a statute is unconstitutional, but the remaining part can stand independently and is workable without the offending part, then the unconstitutional part can be severed, and the rest of the statute remains valid. The core idea is to save as much of the legislation as possible, rather than striking down the entire law for a partial defect.

The test for severability involves examining whether the valid and invalid parts of the law are so inextricably mixed that they cannot be separated. The intention of the legislature is crucial: would the legislature have enacted the valid part without the invalid one? If the valid part is complete in itself and capable of independent existence, it can be severed. If, however, the invalid part is so integral that its removal would render the remaining part meaningless or fundamentally alter the legislative intent, then the entire statute may be struck down.

Landmark cases illustrating this doctrine include A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), where the Supreme Court held that certain provisions of the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, could be severed without invalidating the entire Act. In R.M.D.C. v. Union of India (1957), the Court further clarified that the doctrine applies only when the valid and invalid parts are distinct and separable, and the remaining part can achieve the legislative purpose. This doctrine acts as a safeguard, preventing the wholesale invalidation of laws due to minor constitutional infirmities.

The Doctrine of Colourable Legislation

This doctrine is based on the principle that what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly. It applies when a legislature, while purporting to act within its legislative competence (as defined by the Seventh Schedule), indirectly encroaches upon a field outside its competence or violates constitutional limitations. The term 'colourable' implies that the legislature's action has the 'colour' or appearance of legality, but in 'substance' or reality, it is unconstitutional.

The doctrine is not concerned with the motives of the legislature, but with its legislative competence and the true nature of the enactment. The court looks at the 'pith and substance' of the legislation to determine if it is genuinely within the legislature's power or if it is merely a disguised attempt to legislate on a prohibited subject. If the legislature attempts to achieve indirectly what it is constitutionally forbidden to do directly, the law is deemed 'colourable' and unconstitutional.

Key cases include K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Odisha (1953), where the Supreme Court articulated the doctrine, stating that the court must look to the 'substance' of the Act rather than its 'form'. In State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh (1952), the Court applied this doctrine to strike down certain provisions of the Bihar Land Reforms Act, holding that the state legislature had indirectly attempted to confiscate property without adequate compensation, a matter outside its competence at the time. This doctrine reinforces the federal structure and the principle of limited government, ensuring that legislative bodies operate strictly within their constitutional boundaries.

Judicial Impact Assessment and the Enforcement of Decrees

The effectiveness of the judiciary is not solely measured by the pronouncement of judgments but also by their practical implementation and the overall impact of legislation on the judicial system. This brings into focus two critical, albeit often overlooked, aspects: Judicial Impact Assessment (JIA) and the challenges in enforcing judicial decrees.

Judicial Impact Assessment (JIA)

Judicial Impact Assessment is a systematic process of evaluating the potential effects of proposed legislation on the judiciary. This includes assessing the likely increase in workload, the need for additional judicial personnel, infrastructure, and financial resources that a new law might entail. The objective of JIA is to ensure that new laws are enacted with a clear understanding of their implications for the justice system, thereby preventing further overburdening of courts and ensuring that the judiciary has the capacity to effectively implement them.

In India, a formal, institutionalised JIA mechanism is largely absent. While parliamentary committees and the Law Commission have occasionally highlighted the need for such an assessment (e.g., the 245th Law Commission Report on Arrears and Backlog), it has not been integrated into the legislative process. The absence of JIA often leads to situations where new laws, even well-intentioned ones, add significantly to judicial pendency without corresponding increases in judicial capacity. For example, new criminal laws or specific economic legislations can generate a surge in litigation, for which the existing judicial infrastructure is unprepared. Implementing JIA would enable proactive planning, better resource allocation, and ultimately contribute to a more efficient and responsive justice delivery system.

Enforcement of Judicial Decrees and Orders

The authority of judicial pronouncements is enshrined in the Constitution, with Article 142 empowering the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order necessary for doing 'complete justice' and Article 144 mandating all civil and judicial authorities to act in aid of the Supreme Court. Despite these provisions, the enforcement of judicial decrees, particularly those involving complex policy matters or social reforms, often faces significant challenges.

Challenges to enforcement can arise from various quarters: non-compliance by the executive, legislative inaction or resistance, bureaucratic inertia, lack of public awareness or cooperation, and the inherent complexity of translating broad judicial directives into actionable policy. For instance, judgments related to environmental protection (e.g., the Ganga pollution cases), prison reforms, or social justice issues often require sustained executive action, inter-departmental coordination, and significant resource allocation. The judiciary can issue directions, appoint monitoring committees (e.g., in the case of the National Green Tribunal or specific Supreme Court directives), and even initiate contempt proceedings (which is an existing topic) to ensure compliance. However, the ultimate responsibility for implementation largely rests with the executive and legislative branches.

The effectiveness of the judiciary, therefore, is not just about its power to review and interpret laws, but also about the robustness of the mechanisms that ensure its decisions are respected and implemented. The tension between judicial pronouncement and executive implementation is a recurring theme in governance, underscoring the delicate balance of powers and the need for inter-institutional cooperation for effective justice delivery.

Judicial Ethics, Conflict of Interest, and Declarations

Beyond the formal mechanisms of accountability like impeachment, the integrity and impartiality of the judiciary are profoundly shaped by judicial ethics and the management of potential conflicts of interest. While India does not have a codified judicial code of conduct enforced by statute, a framework of ethical principles has evolved through conventions, resolutions, and pronouncements by the Supreme Court.

The 'Restatement of Values of Judicial Life' (1997), adopted by the Supreme Court, serves as a guiding document for judges. It outlines principles such as avoiding close association with lawyers, refraining from financial dealings that could give rise to a conflict of interest, not accepting gifts, and maintaining dignity both in and out of court. These values aim to preserve the independence and impartiality of the judiciary, ensuring that judges are perceived as, and indeed are, free from external influence or personal bias.

A significant aspect of judicial ethics is the management of conflicts of interest. A judge is expected to recuse themselves from a case where there is a real likelihood of bias, either personal or pecuniary. This principle of 'recusal' is based on the maxim "nemo judex in causa sua" (no one should be a judge in their own cause). While the decision to recuse is primarily left to the individual judge's conscience, the lack of clear, uniform guidelines and a transparent process for recusal has led to debates. Concerns arise when judges recuse without stating reasons, or when allegations of conflict of interest are not adequately addressed, potentially impacting public confidence.

Another area of discussion involves the declaration of assets by judges. While the Supreme Court passed a resolution in 1997 making it mandatory for judges to declare their assets to the Chief Justice, this information is not made public. Arguments for public disclosure centre on enhancing transparency and accountability, aligning the judiciary with other public functionaries who are required to declare assets publicly. Opponents argue that public disclosure could expose judges and their families to security risks and undue scrutiny, potentially undermining their independence. The debate highlights the ongoing tension between the need for judicial independence and the demand for greater transparency and accountability in a democratic system.

The evolution of judicial ethics in India reflects a continuous effort to balance these competing demands, aiming to foster a judiciary that is not only independent but also seen to be beyond reproach, thereby upholding the rule of law and public trust.

Common Traps:

  • Confusing the 'cooling-off' period as a constitutionally mandated provision; it is a debated reform, not a current law.
  • Assuming that judicial review is a mechanism for judicial accountability; it is a power of the judiciary to check other branches.
  • Believing the Doctrine of Severability allows courts to rewrite legislation; it only allows separating valid from invalid parts if the remaining part is workable as originally intended.
  • Misinterpreting the Doctrine of Colourable Legislation as being about the legislature's mala fide intent; it is about legislative competence and the substance of the law.
  • Assuming Judicial Impact Assessment is a formal, institutionalised part of India's legislative process; it is a recommended reform, largely absent in practice.
  • Overlooking the distinction between the Supreme Court's 'Restatement of Values of Judicial Life' and a statutory code of conduct; the former is a guiding resolution, not a legally enforceable statute.
  • Believing that public disclosure of judges' assets is mandatory; while internal declaration exists, public disclosure is still a matter of debate and not mandated.

Doctrine of Prospective Overruling: Evolution, Scope, and Landmark Applications

The doctrine of prospective overruling permits a court to declare that its new interpretation of the law will apply only to future cases, leaving past transactions and decisions unaffected. In Indian constitutional jurisprudence the doctrine emerged as a pragmatic tool to avoid the chaos that a purely retrospective declaration of invalidity could create, especially when a long‑standing precedent is overturned. The Supreme Court first articulated the principle in Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967), where it held that the judgment striking down the power of Parliament to amend fundamental rights would operate prospectively, thereby protecting amendments already made. The Court reasoned that a retrospective nullification would unsettle the constitutional order and undermine the legitimacy of legislative acts performed in good faith under the earlier precedent.

Subsequent cases refined the scope. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) the majority, while establishing the basic structure doctrine, applied prospective overruling to the extent that the decision would not invalidate constitutional amendments enacted before the date of judgment. The Court emphasized that the doctrine is not a matter of judicial discretion alone; it must be justified by considerations of justice, public interest, and the need to maintain stability in the legal system. Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) reiterated that prospective overruling is an exception to the general rule that a declaration of law is retrospective, and it can be invoked only when the Court expressly states that its ruling shall have prospective effect.

The doctrine has also been employed in statutory interpretation. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) the Court held that a new construction of a tax statute would apply prospectively to avoid hardship to taxpayers who had relied on the earlier interpretation. More recently, in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018) (the Sabarimala case), the majority declined to apply prospective overruling, holding that the declaration of unconstitutionality of the practice must take immediate effect because the right to equality cannot be deferred. This illustrates that the Court balances the need for legal certainty against the imperative of enforcing fundamental rights.

Key limitations: the doctrine cannot be used to validate an unconstitutional law retroactively; it only postpones the operative effect of a judicial declaration. Moreover, the Court must explicitly state the prospective nature of its ruling; silence is presumed to mean retrospective application. The doctrine thus serves as a calibrated instrument, allowing the judiciary to correct jurisprudential errors without destabilising the legal order.

Constitutional Morality: Judicial Interpretation and Its Impact on Governance

Constitutional morality refers to the adherence to the core values and principles enshrined in the Constitution — such as justice, liberty, equality, fraternity, and the rule of law — as a guiding standard for both state action and judicial review. Unlike popular morality, which may shift with social majorities, constitutional morality is anchored in the text and the transformative vision of the Constitution. The Supreme Court has increasingly invoked this concept to adjudicate contentious social issues, often placing it in tension with majoritarian impulses.

The term gained doctrinal prominence in M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006), where the Court held that the State’s power to provide reservations in promotions must be exercised in a manner consistent with constitutional morality, requiring quantifiable data on backwardness and adequacy of representation. In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), the Court decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, declaring that constitutional morality demands protection of the dignity and autonomy of LGBTQ+ individuals, even if societal morality disagrees. The judgment stressed that the Constitution’s transformative potential cannot be hostage to prevailing social norms.

In Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018), which struck down the adultery provision (Section 497 IPC), the Court again relied on constitutional morality, holding that a law that treats women as property of their husbands violates the constitutional guarantee of equality and dignity. The Court observed that constitutional morality requires the law to reflect the egalitarian ethos of the Constitution, not patriarchal traditions.

The concept also shapes the Court’s approach to federalism and minority rights. In K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), the right to privacy was grounded in constitutional morality, emphasizing that the State’s intrusion into personal autonomy must meet the stringent test of proportionality. The Court has warned that constitutional morality is not a blank cheque for judicial activism; it must be derived from the Constitution’s text, structure, and history, and applied with restraint.

Critics argue that the open‑ended nature of constitutional morality grants judges excessive discretion, potentially undermining democratic accountability. Proponents counter that it is precisely the judiciary’s role to safeguard the Constitution’s core commitments against transient majorities. The ongoing debate underscores the dynamic interplay between judicial interpretation and governance in a constitutional democracy.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: "Explain how the Supreme Court has used the doctrine of prospective overruling to balance legal certainty with the enforcement of fundamental rights. Illustrate with two landmark cases."
Answer: The doctrine of prospective overruling allows the Court to limit the retroactive effect of a new constitutional interpretation. In Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967), the Court held that its ruling that Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights would apply prospectively, preserving the validity of amendments already enacted. This prevented legal chaos and respected legislative actions taken under the previous precedent. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), while establishing the basic structure doctrine, the Court declared that the judgment would not invalidate constitutional amendments made before the date of the decision, thereby maintaining stability in the constitutional framework. Both cases show the Court explicitly stating prospective application to protect reliance interests while still asserting the supremacy of fundamental rights. The key is the Court’s express declaration; absent such a statement, the default is retrospective effect.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “prospective overruling” or “constitutional morality”, always cite the case where the Court *expressly* limited its ruling to future effect (Golaknath, Kesavananda) and contrast it with a case where the Court refused prospective relief (Sabarimala). For constitutional morality, link the term to the specific constitutional value invoked (dignity, equality, privacy) and the judgment that articulated it (Navtej, Joseph Shine, Puttaswamy). Use the phrase “transformative constitutionalism” to show depth.

Judicial Review of Ordinances: Constitutional Limits and Judicial Scrutiny

Articles 123 and 213 empower the President and Governors respectively to promulgate ordinances when Parliament or the State Legislature is not in session, provided the executive is satisfied that circumstances exist which render immediate action necessary. This ordinance‑making power is a legislative function delegated to the executive, and the Constitution subjects it to judicial review to prevent its misuse as a parallel legislative route.

The Supreme Court in R. C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) (the Bank Nationalisation case) held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 123 is not immune from judicial scrutiny; the Court can examine whether the requisite “immediate action” existed. However, the Court also recognised that the satisfaction is subjective and the judiciary should not substitute its own judgment for that of the executive unless the satisfaction is mala fide or based on irrelevant considerations.

In D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), the Court struck down the practice of repromulgating ordinances repeatedly without placing them before the legislature, terming it a “fraud on the Constitution”. The judgment established that ordinances must be laid before the legislature at its next session and cease to operate if not approved within six weeks. The Court emphasized that the ordinance power is an emergency provision, not a substitute for legislative deliberation.

The most recent authoritative pronouncement is Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017), where a seven‑judge bench held that the Governor’s ordinance‑making power under Article 213 is subject to the same constitutional limitations as the President’s power under Article 123. The Court ruled that an ordinance cannot be used to circumvent the legislative process, and that the Governor’s satisfaction is justiciable. It also clarified that an ordinance that substantially alters the legal framework without legislative approval is vulnerable to challenge.

Judicial review of ordinances thus operates on two planes: (i) procedural compliance — whether the ordinance was laid before the legislature and ceased to operate within the prescribed period; and (ii) substantive compliance — whether the executive’s satisfaction was genuine and the ordinance does not encroach upon the legislative domain. The Court has consistently refused to allow the ordinance route to become a tool for executive law‑making, reinforcing the principle of separation of powers.

The Supreme Court's Role in Election Disputes: Representation of the People Act and Judicial Oversight

Election disputes in India are governed primarily by the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (RPA), and the Constitution vests the Supreme Court with appellate jurisdiction over election petitions under Article 136 (special leave) and, in certain matters, original jurisdiction under Article 71 (presidential and vice‑presidential elections). The Court’s intervention has shaped the integrity of the electoral process, balancing the autonomy of the Election Commission with the need for judicial remedies.

The landmark case Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) arose from an election petition challenging the Prime Minister’s election to the Lok Sabha. The Allahabad High Court found her guilty of corrupt practices, and the Supreme Court, on appeal, upheld the disqualification but stayed the operation of the judgment, leading to the imposition of Emergency. This case underscored the Court’s power to adjudicate electoral malpractices and its willingness to enforce the RPA’s provisions against the highest office‑holders.

In Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013), the Court struck down Section 8(4) of the RPA, which allowed a convicted legislator to continue in office until an appeal was decided. The Court held that the provision violated Article 102(1)(e) and Article 191(1)(e) of the Constitution, which mandate immediate disqualification upon conviction for certain offences. The judgment reinforced the principle that law‑makers must be subject to the same legal consequences as ordinary citizens.

K. Prabhakaran v. P. Jayarajan (2005) dealt with the interpretation of “disqualification on conviction” and clarified that the disqualification operates from the date of conviction, not from the date of sentencing. The Court also emphasized that the Election Commission’s power to remove a candidate’s name from the electoral roll is statutory and must be exercised in accordance with the RPA.

More recently, in Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India (2019), the Court directed political parties to publish criminal antecedents of candidates on their websites and in newspapers, invoking its powers under Article 142 to ensure transparency. The Court has also entertained petitions under Article 32 seeking directions to the Election Commission for stricter enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct, illustrating the expanding scope of judicial oversight in electoral governance.

While the Election Commission enjoys constitutional autonomy under Article 324, the Supreme Court’s appellate and supervisory jurisdiction ensures that statutory provisions are not rendered nugatory. The Court’s jurisprudence reflects a commitment to preserving the purity of elections, even when it requires confronting powerful political actors.

Principle of Natural Justice in Judicial and Quasi‑Judicial Proceedings

The principles of natural justice — audi alteram partem (hear the other side) and nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be a judge in his own cause) — constitute the bedrock of fair procedure in both judicial and quasi‑judicial forums. Although not expressly enumerated in the Constitution, the Supreme Court has read them into Articles 14 and 21, making their observance a constitutional imperative.

In A. K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1969), the Court held that the selection committee for the Indian Forest Service, though an administrative body, was bound by natural justice because its decision affected the petitioner’s right to livelihood. The Court articulated that the duty to act fairly arises whenever a decision affects a person’s rights, interests, or legitimate expectations, irrespective of the label attached to the proceeding.

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded the reach of natural justice by linking it to the right to personal liberty under Article 21. The Court ruled that the procedure established by law must be “fair, just and reasonable”, and that the passport authority’s refusal to give reasons for impounding a passport violated audi alteram partem. This judgment transformed natural justice from a common‑law doctrine into a constitutional guarantee.

The principle against bias was rigorously applied in State of Orissa v. Dr. (Miss) Binapani Dei (1967), where the Court quashed a disciplinary order because the inquiring officer had previously expressed an opinion on the matter. The Court emphasized that the appearance of bias is sufficient to vitiate a decision; actual bias need not be proved.

In quasi‑judicial tribunals, the Court has insisted on reasoned orders. In Union of India v. T. R. Verma (1971), the Court held that a tribunal’s order without reasons violates natural justice because it denies the aggrieved party the opportunity to challenge the decision effectively. The requirement of a speaking order is now a settled facet of procedural fairness.

Exceptions exist: in urgent matters where immediate action is necessary (e.g., preventive detention under Article 22), the Court has allowed post‑decisional hearings. However, even in such cases, the State must provide the earliest possible opportunity to be heard. The jurisprudence thus balances administrative efficiency with the constitutional mandate of fairness, ensuring that natural justice remains a living safeguard against arbitrary power.

  • Believing the Election Commission’s decisions are immune from judicial scrutiny; the Supreme Court exercises appellate and supervisory jurisdiction under Articles 136 and 32.
  • Thinking natural justice applies only to courts; it extends to any authority whose decision affects rights, including administrative and quasi‑judicial bodies.
  • Overlooking that a speaking order (reasoned decision) is a component of natural justice; absence of reasons can render a quasi‑judicial order void.
  • Misapplying the doctrine of prospective overruling to validate an unconstitutional law retroactively; it only postpones the effect of a declaration of invalidity.
The advanced sections above deepen the aspirant’s grasp of nuanced judicial doctrines and their practical interplay with governance.
