1. Historical Background and the Genesis of the Constituent Assembly

1.1 The Demand for a Constitution Framed by Indians

The idea that India’s political destiny should be determined by a body elected by Indians themselves evolved gradually over several decades of the nationalist movement. The earliest formal articulation came in 1922 when Mahatma Gandhi declared that Swaraj would not be a gift of the British Parliament but a declaration of India’s full self-expression through a Constituent Assembly. This demand was reiterated by the Indian National Congress at its Lahore session in 1929, which resolved that the constitution of free India must be framed, without outside interference, by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise. The British government, however, resisted this demand for a long time, arguing that the diverse communities of India could not agree on a single constitutional framework and that British parliamentary sovereignty was the only legitimate source of constitutional authority. The deadlock began to break only after the outbreak of the Second World War, when the British needed full Indian cooperation for the war effort. The August Offer of 1940 conceded, for the first time, that the framing of a new constitution should be primarily the responsibility of Indians themselves, though it fell short of accepting the specific mechanism of a Constituent Assembly elected by adult franchise.

1.2 The Cripps Mission and the Cabinet Mission Plan

The Cripps Mission of 1942 proposed that after the war, a constitution-making body would be elected by the provincial legislatures, but it retained the right of any province to opt out of the union, a provision that the Congress rejected as a recipe for Balkanisation. The final breakthrough came with the Cabinet Mission Plan of May 1946, which rejected the demand for a separate Pakistan but grouped provinces into three sections to accommodate Muslim League demands for autonomy. Crucially, the Plan provided for a Constituent Assembly of 389 members: 296 from British Indian provinces elected by the provincial legislative assemblies through a single transferable vote system of proportional representation, and 93 representatives nominated by the rulers of the Princely States. The elections held in July-August 1946 gave the Congress a massive majority, winning 208 seats, while the Muslim League secured 73 seats. The League, however, refused to join the Assembly, insisting on a separate Constituent Assembly for Pakistan, a stance that eventually led to the partition of the country under the Mountbatten Plan of June 1947 and the Indian Independence Act of 1947.

1.3 The Indian Independence Act and the Assembly’s Sovereign Status

The Indian Independence Act, 1947, passed by the British Parliament, transformed the Constituent Assembly into a fully sovereign body. It ceased to be a creation of the Cabinet Mission Plan and derived its authority directly from the will of the Indian people. The Act also relieved the Assembly of the limitations imposed by the Cabinet Mission, such as the grouping scheme and the requirement to adhere to the Mission’s recommendations. With the partition, the membership was reduced to 299 after the withdrawal of members from the territories that became Pakistan. The Assembly met for the first time on 9 December 1946, with Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha as the temporary Chairman, and elected Dr. Rajendra Prasad as its permanent President on 11 December 1946. From 15 August 1947 onwards, the Assembly functioned as the first Parliament of independent India (the Legislative Wing) and as the constitution-making body (the Constituent Wing), a dual role it performed until the Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949.

1.4 The Statutory Ancestry: From the Regulating Act to the Independence Act

The Constitution did not emerge from a vacuum; it inherited a long line of statutes through which the British Parliament progressively opened and then transferred power in India. The Regulating Act, 1773 was the first step: it brought the East India Company under a measure of parliamentary control and created the office of Governor-General of Bengal. Pitt's India Act, 1784 established a dual government by setting up a Board of Control in London alongside the Company's Court of Directors. The Charter Act of 1813 renewed the Company's charter and ended its commercial monopoly, and the Charter Act of 1833 centralised legislative power in a single Governor-General of India. The Government of India Act, 1858, passed after the revolt of 1857, transferred the government of India from the Company to the Crown, abolished the Board of Control and the Court of Directors, and created the office of Secretary of State for India. The Indian Councils Acts of 1861, 1892 and 1909, and the reforms of 1919 and 1935, then introduced representative and, eventually, responsible government.

Two statutes matter most for the Constitution's form. The Government of India Act, 1919, the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms, introduced dyarchy in the provinces and separated central and provincial subjects, an idea that reappears in the distribution of legislative powers. The Government of India Act, 1935 is the single largest source of the Constitution's text: the federal scheme, the office of Governor, the powers of the judiciary and much of the administrative structure were carried over almost verbatim. Finally, the Indian Independence Act, 1947 created the two independent Dominions, gave the Constituent Assembly sovereign power to frame a constitution, and left the existing machinery of government in place until a new constitution came into force. Reading the Constitution with this statutory ancestry in mind explains why so much of its machinery, from the federal structure to the office of the Governor, is continuous with the colonial state, even as its political purposes were transformed.

2. Composition, Committees, and the Drafting Process

2.1 Social and Political Composition of the Assembly

The Constituent Assembly was not elected on the basis of universal adult franchise, a fact often cited by critics to question its representativeness; however, the provincial legislatures that elected its members were themselves elected on a limited franchise based on property, tax, and educational qualifications covering roughly 28 percent of the adult population. Despite this limitation, the Assembly was remarkably diverse. It included representatives from all major religious communities except the Muslim League, which boycotted it, as well as members from the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, women like Sarojini Naidu, Hansa Mehta, and Durgabai Deshmukh, and eminent jurists and public figures such as B.R. Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, and N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar. The Congress Party dominated the proceedings, but it functioned as a broad umbrella accommodating diverse ideological strands from conservative to socialist, ensuring that the debates reflected a wide spectrum of Indian opinion. The Princely States, initially hesitant, gradually sent their representatives after the Mountbatten Plan, integrating the entire geographical entity into the constitutional framework.

2.2 The Committee System and the Drafting Committee

To manage the enormous task of drafting a constitution for a vast and complex nation, the Assembly constituted a network of committees. The most critical was the Drafting Committee, constituted on 29 August 1947 under the Chairmanship of Dr. B.R. Ambedkar. It comprised seven members: N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K.M. Munshi, Mohammad Saadullah, B.L. Mitter (later replaced by N. Madhava Rao), and D.P. Khaitan (later replaced by T.T. Krishnamachari). The Drafting Committee’s mandate was to scrutinise the reports of the various sectoral committees — such as the Union Powers Committee and the Union Constitution Committee chaired by Jawaharlal Nehru, the Provincial Constitution Committee chaired by Sardar Patel, and the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights and Minorities chaired by Sardar Patel — and prepare a cohesive draft. The Drafting Committee met for 141 days and prepared the first draft in February 1948, which was circulated for public comments, leading to a second draft in October 1948. The meticulous clause-by-clause consideration in the Assembly took 114 days out of the total 165 days of sessions, demonstrating the depth of deliberation.

2.3 The Objective Resolution and the Philosophical Anchor

Before the drafting began in earnest, Jawaharlal Nehru moved the historic Objectives Resolution on 13 December 1946, which was unanimously adopted on 22 January 1947. This resolution proclaimed India as an Independent Sovereign Republic and guaranteed its citizens justice, social, economic and political; equality of status and opportunity; and freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and action. It also pledged adequate safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed and other backward classes. The Objectives Resolution provided the philosophical spine of the Constitution; its language finds direct reflection in the Preamble and the Directive Principles of State Policy. It signaled a decisive break from the colonial Government of India Act, 1935, which was centred on imperial control, towards a transformative charter aimed at social and economic revolution.

Example Question: Assessing the Representative Character
"The Constituent Assembly was a one-party body in an essentially one-party country. The Assembly was the Congress and the Congress was India." Critically examine this statement by Granville Austin in the context of the composition and functioning of the Constituent Assembly.
Granville Austin’s observation captures the political reality of the Assembly’s dominance by the Indian National Congress, which held roughly 69 percent of the seats after partition. However, describing it merely as a "one-party body" obscures the internal pluralism within the Congress and the procedural safeguards that ensured broader consensus. First, the Congress was not a monolithic bloc; it housed conservatives like Rajendra Prasad and Purushottam Das Tandon, socialists like Jayaprakash Narayan (though not in the Assembly, their influence permeated), and liberals like Minoo Masani. Second, the Congress leadership deliberately nominated non-Congress experts to key positions: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, a political opponent of the Congress, was made Chairman of the Drafting Committee; Syama Prasad Mookerjee and B.N. Rau (Constitutional Advisor) represented diverse viewpoints. Third, the Assembly adopted decisions by consensus rather than simple majority voting on critical issues like Fundamental Rights, Federalism, and Language. The debates on the Uniform Civil Code, the status of Hindi, and the rights of minorities show vigorous dissent from members like K.T. Shah, H.V. Kamath, and Frank Anthony, whose amendments were debated seriously even when rejected. Therefore, while the Congress provided the political stability necessary for the Assembly to function, the output was a negotiated social contract, not a partisan manifesto.

3. Sources of the Constitution: Indigenous Roots and Foreign Borrowings

3.1 The Government of India Act, 1935: The Structural Backbone

The single largest source of the Indian Constitution is the Government of India Act, 1935. Approximately 250 provisions of the 1935 Act were reproduced, often verbatim, into the Constitution of 1950. This heavy reliance was pragmatic: the 1935 Act provided a ready-made, detailed administrative and federal machinery that had been tested for a decade. The federal scheme — the division of powers between the Centre and the Provinces via the Union, State, and Concurrent Lists (Seventh Schedule) — the establishment of the Federal Court (precursor to the Supreme Court), the office of the Governor, the Public Service Commissions, and the emergency provisions were all directly inherited. However, the Constituent Assembly made critical modifications. The 1935 Act vested residuary powers in the Governor-General; the Constitution vested them in Parliament (Article 248). The 1935 Act lacked a Bill of Rights; the Constitution inserted a justiciable Chapter on Fundamental Rights (Part III). The 1935 Act was an instrument of British control with 'safeguards' and 'special responsibilities' for the Governor-General; the Constitution replaced these with a responsible parliamentary executive accountable to the legislature.

3.2 Major Foreign Influences and Selective Adaptation

Beyond the 1935 Act, the framers consciously borrowed from other constitutions, but they did so selectively, adapting each feature to Indian conditions rather than transplanting them wholesale. From the British Constitution, they adopted the Parliamentary system of government, the Rule of Law, the institution of the Speaker, the concept of Single Citizenship, and the procedure of Law Making. However, they rejected the British principle of Parliamentary Sovereignty, substituting it with Constitutional Supremacy and Judicial Review. From the United States Constitution, they borrowed the Written Constitution, the Preamble, Fundamental Rights (Bill of Rights), the Federal structure with a strong Centre, Judicial Review and the independence of the Judiciary, the Impeachment of the President, and the removal of Supreme Court judges. The Irish Constitution inspired the Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), the method of election of the President, and the nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha by the President. The Canadian model influenced the quasi-federal structure — a federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, and the appointment of State Governors by the Centre. The Weimar Constitution of Germany provided the blueprint for the Emergency Provisions (Article 352, 356, 360) and the suspension of Fundamental Rights during Emergency. The Australian Constitution contributed the Concurrent List, the joint sitting of Parliament (Article 108), and the freedom of trade and commerce (Article 301). The South African Constitution influenced the procedure for amendment with a two-thirds majority and the election of members of the Rajya Sabha. The Japanese Constitution provided the concept of 'Procedure Established by Law' (Article 21), which the framers preferred over the American 'Due Process of Law' to limit judicial activism in economic legislation.

3.3 Indigenous Contributions: The Gandhian and Socialist Ethos

While the structural and legal architecture was largely borrowed, the soul of the Constitution reflects indigenous movements and philosophies. The Directive Principles in Articles 40 (Village Panchayats), 43 (Living Wage), 47 (Nutrition and Public Health), and 48 (Agriculture and Animal Husbandry) bear the imprint of Gandhian thought on village self-governance and trusteeship. The socialist influence, championed by Nehru and the Congress Socialist Party, is evident in Articles 38 (Social Order), 39 (Distribution of Resources, Equal Pay), and 41 (Right to Work), which mandate the State to strive for an egalitarian society. The abolition of Untouchability (Article 17) and the prohibition of forced labour (Article 23) were direct responses to the social reform movements led by reformers like Jyotiba Phule, Periyar, and Dr. Ambedkar. The Fifth and Sixth Schedules, providing for the administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas, were innovative indigenous mechanisms to protect tribal autonomy and culture, having no direct parallel in the borrowed models. Thus, the Constitution is a 'borrowed' document in form but an 'indigenous' document in spirit and transformative intent.

4. Salient Features of the Constitution

4.1 Lengthiest Written Constitution and the Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility

The Constitution of India is the lengthiest written constitution in the world, originally containing 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules (now expanded to over 470 Articles, 25 Parts, and 12 Schedules through amendments). This bulk is attributable to the incorporation of the administrative detail of the Government of India Act, 1935, the inclusion of justiciable Fundamental Rights and non-justiciable Directive Principles, elaborate provisions for Centre-State relations, special provisions for specific states (Articles 370, 371A-J), and detailed language provisions. Regarding amendability, the Constitution strikes a unique balance. It is neither rigid like the US Constitution (requiring supermajorities in Congress and ratification by three-fourths of states for every amendment) nor flexible like the UK Constitution (amendable by simple parliamentary majority). Article 368 provides three tiers: a simple majority of Parliament for certain provisions (e.g., admission of new states, creation of Legislative Councils); a special majority (two-thirds of members present and voting plus a majority of total membership) for most provisions; and the special majority plus ratification by half the State Legislatures for provisions affecting the federal structure (e.g., election of the President, extent of executive power, judiciary, distribution of legislative powers, Article 368 itself). This graded rigidity allows the Constitution to evolve with changing needs while protecting the federal compact.

4.2 Federal System with Unitary Bias (Quasi-Federalism)

The Constitution establishes a federal system with a "strong Centre," a phrase used by Dr. Ambedkar and later described by K.C. Wheare as "quasi-federal." It possesses all classic federal features: dual polity (Centre and States), written constitution, supremacy of the constitution, division of powers (Seventh Schedule), independent judiciary, and bicameralism. However, unitary features dominate in emergencies and even in normal times. The Centre holds residuary powers (unlike US/Australia where they rest with states). The Parliament can unilaterally alter state boundaries and names (Article 3) without state consent. The Governor, appointed by the President (Centre), acts as the Centre's agent and can reserve bills for Presidential assent. During a National Emergency (Article 352), the federal structure converts into a unitary one: Parliament gains power to legislate on State List subjects, the Centre can give directions to states on any matter, and financial autonomy of states is suspended. President's Rule (Article 356) allows the Centre to assume state government functions on the ground of constitutional breakdown. The All-India Services (IAS, IPS) recruited and controlled by the Centre but serving in states, and the integrated judiciary (High Courts under Supreme Court) further centralise control. This design was a conscious choice by the framers who, having witnessed partition and princely state integration, prioritised national unity and integrity over classical federal autonomy.

4.3 Parliamentary Form of Government

India adopted the British Westminster model of Parliamentary government both at the Centre and in the States, preferring it over the US Presidential system. The essence of this system is the fusion of the executive and the legislature: the Council of Ministers, headed by the Prime Minister (Chief Minister in states), is drawn from the legislature and is collectively responsible to the Lower House (Lok Sabha / Legislative Assembly). The President (Governor) is the nominal executive (head of state), while the Prime Minister (Chief Minister) is the real executive (head of government). The Constitution mandates that the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), a provision made binding by the 42nd and 44th Amendments. This system ensures continuous accountability through mechanisms like Question Hour, Zero Hour, No-Confidence Motions, and Parliamentary Committees. However, the Indian system differs from the British model in crucial respects: India has a written constitution limiting parliamentary sovereignty; the Prime Minister must be a member of Parliament (or get elected within six months), whereas the British PM must be an MP; India has a republican head of state (elected President) unlike the British monarchy; and the Indian Parliament is not supreme — its laws are subject to Judicial Review.

4.4 Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and Fundamental Duties

Part III (Articles 12-35) guarantees six categories of Fundamental Rights (originally seven, Right to Property was removed by the 44th Amendment in 1978 and made a legal right under Article 300A): Right to Equality (14-18), Right to Freedom (19-22), Right against Exploitation (23-24), Right to Freedom of Religion (25-28), Cultural and Educational Rights (29-30), and Right to Constitutional Remedies (32). These are justiciable, allowing citizens to approach the Supreme Court (Article 32) and High Courts (Article 226) for enforcement. The Supreme Court has described Article 32 as the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. Part IV (Articles 36-51) contains the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs), classified into Socialistic (Art 38, 39, 41, 42, 43), Gandhian (Art 40, 43B, 46, 47, 48), and Liberal-Intellectual (Art 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51). They are non-justiciable (Article 37) but "fundamental in the governance of the country." The 42nd Amendment (1976) added Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A) on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, listing ten duties (eleven after the 86th Amendment added duty to provide education to children aged 6-14). The interplay between Rights, Duties, and Directive Principles forms the constitutional trinity: Rights protect the individual against the State; DPSPs obligate the State to transform society; Duties remind the citizen that freedom entails responsibility.

4.5 Independent Judiciary and Judicial Review

The Constitution establishes an integrated and independent judiciary with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts at the state level, and subordinate courts below. Independence is secured through: security of tenure (removal only by impeachment for proved misbehaviour/incapacity requiring special majority in Parliament), salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund (not votable), prohibition on practice after retirement (Article 124(7)), and separation of judiciary from executive (Article 50). The power of Judicial Review — the authority to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive acts — is explicit in Articles 13, 32, 136, 226, and 227. The Supreme Court is the guardian of the Constitution and the protector of Fundamental Rights. The doctrine of Basic Structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) limits even Parliament's amending power, ensuring that the core identity of the Constitution — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and judicial review — cannot be destroyed by amendment. This makes the Indian judiciary one of the most powerful in the world, exercising not just review but also judicial activism through Public Interest Litigation (PIL) to enforce socio-economic rights.

4.6 Secularism, Universal Adult Franchise, and Single Citizenship

The Constitution declares India a Secular State (the word 'Secular' was inserted in the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, but the principle was inherent in Articles 25-28). Indian secularism is not a strict separation of religion and state (as in the US) but 'principled distance' — the state treats all religions equally, regulates secular aspects of religious practice, and intervenes for social reform (e.g., abolition of untouchability, reform of Hindu personal law). Universal Adult Franchise (Article 326) was a revolutionary leap for a largely illiterate, poor, and socially stratified society in 1950. It rejected property, tax, and literacy qualifications, entrusting political sovereignty to every adult citizen (age reduced from 21 to 18 by the 61st Amendment, 1988). This single act transformed subjects into citizens and legitimised the democratic project. Single Citizenship (Articles 5-11) means all Indians are citizens of India alone, not of individual states (unlike the US dual citizenship). This promotes national unity and ensures equal rights across the territory, preventing state-level discrimination against non-domiciles.

5. The Preamble: Text, Evolution, and Constitutional Significance

5.1 The Text and Its Philosophical Architecture

The Preamble serves as the introduction and the philosophical key to the Constitution. It reads: "WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION." The Preamble performs four functions: it indicates the source of authority ("We, the People of India"), it states the nature of the polity (Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic), it enumerates the objectives (Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity), and it records the date of adoption (26 November 1949). The phrase "We, the People" asserts popular sovereignty, distinguishing the Constitution from the Government of India Act, 1935, which derived authority from the British Crown. The objectives are arranged in a logical hierarchy: Justice (the goal), Liberty and Equality (the means/conditions), and Fraternity (the social cement ensuring unity).

5.2 The 42nd Amendment: Insertion of 'Socialist', 'Secular', and 'Integrity'

The original Preamble of 1949 described India as a "Sovereign Democratic Republic." The words "Socialist" and "Secular" were inserted between "Sovereign" and "Democratic" by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, during the Emergency. The word "Integrity" was added to "Unity of the Nation." The government argued that these words merely made explicit what was already implicit in the Constitution. "Socialist" reflected the Directive Principles (Articles 38, 39) aiming at a welfare state and equitable distribution of resources, though the Indian model accepts a mixed economy rather than state ownership of all means of production. "Secular" codified the principle of state neutrality and equal treatment of all religions embedded in Articles 25-28. "Integrity" reinforced the territorial unity of the nation, addressing secessionist tendencies. The 44th Amendment (1978) attempted to define "Socialist" as "elimination of inequality in income and status and standards of life" but the definition was dropped in the Rajya Sabha; thus, the term remains undefined in the Constitution, allowing flexible interpretation ranging from democratic socialism to welfare capitalism.

5.3 Legal Status: Is the Preamble a Part of the Constitution?

The legal status of the Preamble has been a subject of judicial evolution. In the Berubari Union Case (1960), the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is not a part of the Constitution and is not a source of substantive powers or limitations; it serves only as a key to interpret ambiguous provisions. However, in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a 13-judge bench overruled Berubari and held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution. The Court ruled that the Preamble can be amended under Article 368, but the "Basic Structure" — the essential features described in the Preamble (Sovereignty, Democracy, Secularism, Republic, Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity) — cannot be altered. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court used the Preamble's "Secular" character to strike down the dismissal of state governments on political grounds, establishing secularism as a basic feature. In Union Government v. LIC of India (1995), the Court reiterated that the Preamble is a part of the Constitution but is not directly enforceable in a court of law (non-justiciable); it serves as a guiding star for interpretation of statutes and constitutional provisions.

5.4 The Preamble as an Interpretive Tool and the 'Basic Structure'

The Preamble acts as the "horoscope" of the Constitution (a phrase attributed to K.M. Munshi). When the language of an Article is ambiguous, the Court interprets it in a manner that advances the objectives of the Preamble. For instance, the expansion of Article 21 (Right to Life) to include the right to livelihood, health, education, shelter, and privacy is rooted in the Preamble's promise of "Justice, social, economic and political" and "Dignity of the individual." The Preamble also resolves conflicts between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Court held that the harmony between Part III and Part IV is an essential feature of the Basic Structure, derived from the Preamble's vision of a socio-economic revolution.

The Basic Structure Doctrine: Evolution, Tests, and Contemporary Application

The Basic Structure doctrine is the judicial innovation that prevents Parliament from altering the Constitution’s core identity through amendment. It originated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where a 13‑judge bench held by a 7‑6 majority that while Article 368 confers wide amending power, it does not enable the destruction of the Constitution’s “basic features.” The Court did not furnish an exhaustive list but identified sovereignty, democracy, secularism, republicanism, judicial review, federalism, and the separation of powers as illustrative elements.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the test. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) the Court applied the doctrine to strike down the 39th Amendment’s clause that insulated the Prime Minister’s election from judicial scrutiny, affirming that free and fair elections constitute a basic feature. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) added the harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles and the limited nature of the amending power itself. Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) introduced the “date of judgment” rule: laws placed in the Ninth Schedule before 24 April 1973 are immune, but those inserted thereafter are subject to basic‑structure review. The I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) decision cemented this principle, holding that any Ninth Schedule law violating fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure is open to challenge.

The doctrine operates through a two‑stage inquiry. First, the Court asks whether the impugned amendment or law affects a feature that is “essential” to the Constitution’s identity. Second, it examines whether the alteration destroys the feature’s essential character rather than merely modifying its operation. This “width and identity” test distinguishes permissible reforms (e.g., the 73rd and 74th Amendments strengthening local self‑government) from impermissible ones (e.g., the 42nd Amendment’s attempt to curtail judicial review). The doctrine thus functions as a constitutional “guardrail,” ensuring that the constituent power remains a power of amendment, not a power of repeal.

Amendment Procedure under Article 368: Scope, Limitations, and the Doctrine of Constitutional Immutability

Article 368 prescribes three tiers of amendment. The first tier requires a simple majority of Parliament for matters such as the creation of new states (Article 3) or the alteration of legislative council composition (Article 169). The second tier demands a special majority — a majority of the total membership of each House and a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting — for most substantive changes, including Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and the structure of the Union and States. The third tier adds ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures for amendments affecting the federal architecture: the election of the President (Article 54), the extent of executive power (Articles 73, 162), the distribution of legislative powers (Seventh Schedule), representation of States in Parliament, and Article 368 itself.

The procedural rigidity is intentional. The Constituent Assembly debates reveal a deliberate choice to avoid the British model of parliamentary sovereignty and the American model of near‑impossibility of amendment. The “basic structure” limitation, however, imposes a substantive ceiling beyond procedural compliance. In Kesavananda the Court held that even a duly passed amendment under Article 368 can be invalidated if it damages the basic structure. This creates a dual constraint: procedural (majority, ratification) and substantive (basic structure). The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to oust judicial review of amendments by inserting clauses 4 and 5 in Article 368, declaring that no amendment shall be called in question and that there shall be no limitation on constituent power. The Supreme Court struck down these clauses in Minerva Mills, reaffirming that the power of amendment is not a power to abrogate the Constitution.

Practical implications are significant. The 99th Amendment (2014) establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission was passed with the requisite special majority and state ratification, yet the Court invalidated it in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for violating judicial independence — a basic feature. Conversely, the 101st Amendment (2016) introducing the Goods and Services Tax cleared all procedural hurdles and survived basic‑structure scrutiny because it altered fiscal federalism without destroying the federal principle itself. Aspirants must distinguish between “amendments that alter the Constitution” and “amendments that replace the Constitution,” a line the Court draws by examining the amendment’s effect on the Constitution’s identity.

Worked Illustration
A Bill seeks to amend Article 368 to remove the requirement of state ratification for any future amendment. The Bill is passed by the required special majority in both Houses but is not sent to the States. Is the amendment constitutionally valid?
No. Article 368 itself is a provision whose amendment falls under the third tier requiring ratification by half the States. Moreover, the basic structure doctrine treats the federal distribution of amendment power as a core feature. Removing state ratification would alter the federal character of the amending process, thereby violating the basic structure as held in Kesavananda and Minerva Mills. The amendment would be struck down on both procedural and substantive grounds.

Federal Dynamics: Distribution of Legislative Powers, Residuary Authority, and the Role of the Inter‑State Council

The Seventh Schedule allocates legislative subjects between the Union List (List I, 97 entries), the State List (List II, 66 entries), and the Concurrent List (List III, 47 entries). The Union enjoys exclusive power over List I; States have exclusive power over List II subject to Union law on Concurrent subjects prevailing in case of conflict (Article 254). Residuary powers — subjects not enumerated in any list — vest in Parliament under Article 248 read with Entry 97 of List I, a reversal of the American model where residuary powers rest with the States. This centralising tilt is a hallmark of “quasi‑federalism.”

Judicial interpretation has shaped the practical operation of these lists. The “pith and substance” doctrine determines the true nature of a law when its incidental encroachment on another list is challenged. In State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) the Court upheld a State prohibition law despite its incidental effect on import‑export (a Union subject) because its pith and substance fell under State List Entry 8 (intoxicating liquors). The “colourable legislation” doctrine prevents a legislature from indirectly legislating on a subject outside its competence by disguising the law’s true purpose.

Article 263 provides for an Inter‑State Council to inquire into and advise on disputes between States, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination. The Council was first constituted in 1990 on the Sarkaria Commission’s recommendation. Its composition — Prime Minister as Chair, Chief Ministers of all States, and six Union Cabinet Ministers — gives it a high‑level political character. While its recommendations are advisory, the Council has facilitated consensus on issues such as the implementation of the Goods and Services Tax, the sharing of river waters, and the management of internal security. The Council’s effectiveness depends on political will; it remains a consultative rather than a decision‑making body.

Recent tensions illustrate the federal dynamic. The 101st Amendment (GST) required a constitutional amendment and a GST Council (Article 279A) that operates on a weighted voting formula, giving the Union a de facto veto. The 2020 Farm Acts (later repealed) were enacted under Entry 33 of the Concurrent List (trade and commerce) but were perceived as encroaching on State List Entry 14 (agriculture), triggering widespread State opposition. The Supreme Court’s reference in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) affirmed that the Union cannot use its residuary power to legislate on matters that are essentially State subjects, reinforcing the principle that the distribution of powers is not a mere textual exercise but a structural guarantee.

Emergency Provisions: Constitutional Safeguards, Judicial Scrutiny, and the 44th Amendment Reforms

The Constitution envisages three types of emergency: National Emergency (Article 352), State Emergency (President’s Rule, Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360). Each carries distinct triggers, parliamentary approval thresholds, and effects on fundamental rights and federal distribution.

National Emergency can be proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) replaced “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion,” raised the parliamentary approval requirement to a special majority (two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House), and mandated that the proclamation be laid before each House within one month. It also restricted the suspension of Fundamental Rights: Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended, and Article 19 can be suspended only when the emergency is proclaimed on grounds of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the proclamation of President’s Rule under Article 356 is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, irrelevance, or unconstitutionality, and that the dissolution of a State Legislative Assembly cannot precede parliamentary approval.

State Emergency (President’s Rule) requires the President’s satisfaction that the State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The 44th Amendment added a proviso that the proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months and cannot remain in force beyond three years without repeated parliamentary approval every six months. The Court in Bommai established that the Governor’s report must be based on objective material, and the imposition of President’s Rule for political reasons violates the basic structure (federalism and democracy).

Financial Emergency (Article 360) has never been proclaimed. It empowers the President to direct States to observe canons of financial propriety and to reduce salaries of Union and State officials, including judges. Parliamentary approval follows the same special‑majority rule as National Emergency. The provision remains a dormant but potent instrument, reflecting the Constitution’s centralising bias in fiscal matters.

Constitutional Morality and the Transformative Vision of the Constitution

Constitutional morality refers to the adherence to the Constitution’s core values — justice, liberty, equality, fraternity — beyond mere textual compliance. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, in the Constituent Assembly, warned that constitutional morality is not a natural sentiment but must be cultivated. The Supreme Court has invoked the concept to strike down practices that, while not explicitly prohibited, betray the Constitution’s transformative promise. In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), the Court decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, holding that constitutional morality demands the protection of dignity and equality for sexual minorities, overriding majoritarian moral disapproval. In Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (Sabarimala, 2018), the Court ruled that the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the temple violated constitutional morality grounded in Articles 14, 15, 17, and 25.

The doctrine operates as an interpretive lens: when a statutory provision or executive action is ambiguous, the Court prefers the reading that advances constitutional morality. It also serves as a check on majoritarian legislation that may satisfy procedural validity but undermine the Constitution’s egalitarian ethos. Critics argue that the concept grants judges excessive discretion; proponents contend it is essential for a Constitution designed to transform a hierarchical society into a democratic republic. For examination purposes, constitutional morality should be linked to the Preamble’s objectives, the basic structure doctrine, and the transformative jurisprudence of Articles 14, 15, 17, 21, and 25.

Doctrine of Eclipse, Severability, and Waiver in Fundamental Rights Jurisprudence

Article 13 of the Constitution establishes the supremacy of fundamental rights over pre‑constitutional and post‑constitutional laws. Three judicial doctrines — eclipse, severability, and waiver — operationalise this supremacy. The doctrine of eclipse holds that a pre‑constitutional statute inconsistent with a fundamental right does not become void ab initio; it remains dormant, “eclipsed”, until the constitutional impediment is removed. In Bhikaji Narain v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) the Supreme Court ruled that the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1947, which conflicted with Article 19(1)(g), was merely eclipsed and revived when the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, amended Article 19(6) to permit the restriction.

The doctrine of severability (or separability) applies when only a part of a statute violates a fundamental right. The offending provision is severed, leaving the remainder intact, provided the valid portion is capable of independent operation and the legislature would have enacted it alone. In State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills Ltd. (1974) the Court struck down the offending section of the Bombay Labour Welfare Fund Act while preserving the rest. The test requires (i) the invalid part to be distinct, (ii) the remaining statute to be workable, and (iii) no legislative intent to enact the whole as an inseparable scheme.

The doctrine of waiver asserts that a person cannot voluntarily relinquish a fundamental right. In Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax (1959) the Court held that fundamental rights are conferred in the public interest and are not subject to private waiver. Subsequent jurisprudence (e.g., Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985) reiterated that any purported waiver must be informed, voluntary, and not contrary to public policy; however, the Court has consistently refused to treat waiver as a valid defence against infringement of Part III rights.

These doctrines interact: a law may be partially eclipsed, partially severed, and the individual cannot waive the right that the law infringes. For examination purposes, remember that eclipse applies only to pre‑constitutional laws, severability to both pre‑ and post‑constitutional statutes, and waiver is universally rejected for fundamental rights.

Discretionary Powers of the President and Governors: Ordinances, Pardons, and Judicial Review

The Constitution vests the President (Article 123) and Governors (Article 213) with ordinance‑making power to meet urgent legislative needs when Parliament or the State Legislature is not in session. An ordinance has the same force as an Act but must be laid before the legislature within six weeks of its reassembly; otherwise it lapses. The Supreme Court in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) held that the President’s satisfaction is justiciable — ordinances cannot be used to bypass legislative debate on matters of policy. In Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) the Court reiterated that re‑promulgation of ordinances without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution.

The pardoning power (Article 72 for the President, Article 161 for Governors) includes pardon, commutation, remission, respite, and reprieve. Judicial review of this power is limited but not absent. In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) the Court ruled that the exercise must be on the advice of the Council of Ministers and cannot be arbitrary. Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) established that the Court may intervene if the order is mala fide, based on irrelevant considerations, or suffers from non‑application of mind.

Beyond ordinances and pardons, the President and Governors exercise discretionary functions in government formation. The President appoints the Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority (e.g., 1996, 1998). The Governor’s discretion under Article 163(1) to act without ministerial advice is confined to situations where the Constitution expressly requires it — such as reservation of bills for Presidential assent (Article 200) or reporting failure of constitutional machinery (Article 356). The S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) judgment subjected the Governor’s report to judicial scrutiny, mandating objective material and prohibiting partisan use.

Key distinctions for the aspirant: (i) Ordinances are legislative substitutes, subject to legislative ratification; (ii) Pardons are executive clemency, reviewable only on narrow grounds; (iii) Discretionary appointments are political but bounded by constitutional conventions and judicial oversight.

The power is constitutional, not purely political.

Fiscal Federalism: Finance Commission, GST Council, and the Distribution of Financial Resources

Fiscal federalism in India is structured around two constitutional bodies: the Finance Commission (Article 280) and the Goods and Services Tax Council (Article 279A, inserted by the 101st Amendment, 2016). The Finance Commission is constituted every five years (or earlier) by the President. It recommends (a) the vertical division of net proceeds of Union taxes between the Centre and the States, (b) the horizontal distribution among States based on criteria such as population, area, forest cover, income distance, and demographic performance, and (c) measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of States for Panchayats and Municipalities. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑2025) introduced a 12.5 % weight for demographic performance and a 15 % weight for forest and ecology, reflecting a shift toward incentive‑based devolution.

The GST Council embodies cooperative federalism in indirect taxation. It comprises the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and one Minister from each State. Decisions require a three‑fourths majority of weighted votes: the Centre holds one‑third of the total weight, States collectively hold two‑thirds. The Council recommends rates, exemptions, threshold limits, and dispute‑resolution mechanisms. Its recommendations are binding on both Union and States, but the Supreme Court in Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022) clarified that the Council’s recommendations are not “law” under Article 141; they acquire legal force only when enacted by Parliament or State Legislatures.

Vertical devolution under the Finance Commission determines the share of the divisible pool (corporation tax, income tax, customs, excise, GST compensation cess) that flows to States. Horizontal devolution uses a formula; the 15th FC assigned 45 % weight to income distance, 15 % to population (2011), 15 % to area, 10 % to forest and ecology, 10 % to demographic performance, and 5 % to tax effort. The GST compensation cess, introduced for five years (2017‑2022), was extended by the 2022 amendment to meet revenue shortfalls, illustrating the dynamic interplay between the two bodies.

For the aspirant, remember: (i) Finance Commission = tax‑sharing (vertical/horizontal) and grants; (ii) GST Council = rate‑setting and policy for a single indirect tax; (iii) Both are constitutional, but only the Finance Commission’s recommendations are traditionally accepted by the Executive, while GST Council recommendations require legislative enactment.

Constitutional Position of Union Territories and the National Capital Territory of Delhi

Union Territories (UTs) are administered by the President through an Administrator (Lieutenant Governor, Chief Commissioner, or Administrator) under Article 239. Parliament may create a Legislature or Council of Ministers for a UT (Article 239A). The National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi enjoys a special status under Article 239AA, inserted by the 69th Amendment (1991). It provides for a Legislative Assembly with powers over State List and Concurrent List matters (except public order, police, and land), a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister, and a Lieutenant Governor (LG) who acts on the aid and advice of the Council except in matters where the Constitution requires his discretion.

The Supreme Court’s landmark judgment in Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) held that the LG’s concurrence is not required on all matters; the LG must refer differences to the President only on issues outside the Assembly’s legislative competence. The 2023 Constitution Bench reiterated that the LG’s discretionary power is confined to the three excluded subjects and that the elected government possesses “representative governance” mandate. The Court also clarified that the LG cannot stall routine governance by withholding files indefinitely.

Puducherry (Article 239A) and Jammu & Kashmir (post‑2019 reorganisation) have similar but distinct frameworks. Puducherry’s Assembly has powers over State List and Concurrent List, but the LG’s discretion is broader. The 2019 Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act created two UTs — Jammu & Kashmir (with legislature) and Ladakh (without legislature) — under Articles 239A and 239 respectively.

Key examination points: (i) Article 239 — general UT administration; (ii) Article 239A — Parliament may create legislature for any UT; (iii) Article 239AA — Delhi’s unique “asymmetric federal” design; (iv) LG’s discretion limited to three subjects; (v) Judicial review ensures the LG does not become a parallel executive.

The Collegium System and Judicial Appointments: Evolution, Critique, and the National Judicial Appointments Commission

The Constitution originally vested the power to appoint Supreme Court and High Court judges in the President acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers (Articles 124 and 217). In practice, the executive dominated appointments until the Supreme Court, through a series of judgments, rewrote the constitutional architecture. The First Judges Case (S. P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981) held that “consultation” with the Chief Justice of India (CJI) did not mean “concurrence”, leaving the executive with the final say. The Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 1993) reversed this, declaring that the CJI’s opinion must have primacy and that a collegium of the CJI and the two senior‑most judges would recommend appointments. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the CJI plus the four senior‑most Supreme Court judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the CJI plus the two senior‑most judges of the concerned High Court for High Court appointments.

In 2014 Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act and the 99th Constitutional Amendment, creating a six‑member body comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Union Law Minister, and two “eminent persons” nominated by a committee of the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and CJI. The Supreme Court struck down both the Amendment and the Act in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) by a 4:1 majority, holding that the NJAC violated the basic structure by compromising judicial independence. The Court revived the collegium system but directed the government to finalise a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) for greater transparency, eligibility criteria, and a secretariat. As of 2024 the MoP remains under negotiation; the collegium continues to function on the basis of the 1998 judgment, with the CJI’s recommendation binding on the President unless the government returns the file for reconsideration — a power the Court has said must be exercised sparingly and with reasons.

Critics argue that the collegium lacks accountability, operates opaquely, and suffers from nepotism and regional imbalance. Proponents maintain that any executive involvement would erode the separation of powers. The debate centres on three issues: (i) transparency — whether reasons for selection and rejection should be published; (ii) diversity — the under‑representation of women, minorities, and backward classes; and (iii) timeliness — delays in filling vacancies that affect judicial efficiency. The Law Commission’s 230th Report (2009) and the Venkatachaliah Commission (2002) both recommended a broad‑based appointment commission with a judicial majority, but political consensus has proved elusive.

Fifth and Sixth Schedules: Administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas

Articles 244(1) and 244(2) create two distinct constitutional regimes for the protection of tribal communities. The Fifth Schedule applies to “Scheduled Areas” in any state other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. The President may declare an area as a Scheduled Area after consulting the Governor; the Governor then acquires special responsibilities: (a) the power to direct that any particular law of Parliament or the state legislature shall not apply or shall apply with modifications; (b) the power to make regulations for the peace and good government of the area, including the prohibition or restriction of land transfer among tribals; and (c) the duty to submit an annual report to the President on the administration of Scheduled Areas. A Tribes Advisory Council (TAC) of up to 20 members, three‑fourths of whom must be representatives of Scheduled Tribes, advises the Governor on matters referred to it.

The Sixth Schedule provides for autonomous district councils (ADCs) and autonomous regional councils (ARCs) in the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. Each ADC has legislative, executive, and judicial powers over subjects such as land, forests, water, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance, marriage, and social customs. The councils can make laws on these subjects, subject to the Governor’s assent; the Governor may also annul or modify council laws. The Sixth Schedule originally covered ten autonomous districts; the 125th Constitutional Amendment Bill (2019) proposed to extend its provisions to the hill areas of Manipur and to provide for elected village councils, but it lapsed with the dissolution of the 16th Lok Sabha. The 2021 amendment to the Sixth Schedule (via the Assam Reorganisation Act) increased the number of seats in the Bodoland Territorial Council and altered its composition.

Key differences: (i) Legislative scope — Fifth Schedule areas remain under the state legislature with the Governor’s regulatory override; Sixth Schedule councils possess original legislative competence on enumerated subjects. (ii) Executive control — In Fifth Schedule areas the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except where the Constitution requires discretion; in Sixth Schedule areas the Governor’s assent is required for council laws, and the Governor can dissolve a council on the recommendation of the state government. (iii) Judicial powers — Sixth Schedule councils can constitute village courts for tribal disputes; Fifth Schedule areas have no parallel judicial structure. (iv) Financial autonomy — Sixth Schedule councils receive grants from the Consolidated Fund of the State and can levy certain taxes; Fifth Schedule areas rely on state budgetary allocations.

Landmark judgments: Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) held that the Governor’s power under the Fifth Schedule to restrict land transfer to non‑tribals is a constitutional mandate, not mere executive discretion. Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2005) clarified that the Governor’s report under the Fifth Schedule is not justiciable but the failure to submit it may be challenged. State of Assam v. Union of India (2020) upheld the validity of the 2019 amendment to the Sixth Schedule concerning the Bodoland Territorial Region.

Worked Example
Question: Distinguish the Governor’s powers under the Fifth Schedule from those under the Sixth Schedule with reference to legislative control over tribal areas.

The State Legislature retains primary legislative authority; the Governor’s role is regulatory and supervisory. Under the Sixth Schedule (Art. 244(2)), Autonomous District Councils possess original legislative power over enumerated subjects (land, forests, customs, etc.). The Tenth Schedule and the Anti‑Defection Law: Text, Judicial Interpretation, and Recent Amendments

The Fifty‑second Amendment (1985) inserted the Tenth Schedule to curb political defections. A member of Parliament or a State Legislature incurs disqualification if (a) he voluntarily gives up membership of his political party; (b) he votes or abstains contrary to the party whip without prior permission; or (c) an independently elected member joins a political party. The Ninety‑first Amendment (2003) added two crucial changes: (i) the “split” provision (one‑third of a legislature party) was deleted, so a merger now requires at least two‑thirds of the members of the legislature party to agree; (ii) the total number of ministers, including the Chief Minister, cannot exceed 15 % of the total strength of the House (Article 75(1A) and 164(1A)).

The Speaker/Chairman is the sole authority to decide disqualification petitions, and the decision is final subject to judicial review. In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) the Supreme Court upheld the Speaker’s adjudicatory role but held that the Speaker’s order is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, perversity, or violation of natural justice. The Court also ruled that the Speaker acts as a tribunal, not as a political functionary, and must decide within a reasonable time. Subsequent judgments have tightened the timeline: Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020) directed the Speaker to decide within three months; Maharashtra Legislative Assembly v. Union of India (2022) reiterated that inordinate delay amounts to a denial of justice.

Recent controversies: (i) Rajasthan (2020) — the Speaker disqualified 19 MLAs of the ruling party for “voluntarily giving up membership” after they attended a meeting called by a rival leader; the High Court stayed the order, but the Supreme Court later upheld the Speaker’s power to interpret “voluntarily giving up”. (ii) Maharashtra (2022) — a faction of the Shiv Sena claimed a split; the Speaker recognised the faction led by the Chief Minister as the legitimate party, leading to a Supreme Court reference on the interplay between the Tenth Schedule and the Election Commission’s symbol‑allocation powers. (iii) Goa (2023) — the Speaker disqualified eight MLAs for defection; the Supreme Court emphasised that the “whip” must be issued by the authorised party leader and that the Speaker cannot ignore a valid whip.

Key interpretive principles: (a) “Voluntarily giving up membership” is inferred from conduct, not merely formal resignation; (b) the whip must be clear, specific, and issued by the competent authority; (c) a merger under paragraph 4 of the Tenth Schedule requires a resolution by the original party and acceptance by the merging party, with two‑thirds support; (d) the Speaker’s decision is quasi‑judicial and must follow natural justice — notice, hearing, and reasoned order.

Panchayats and Municipalities: The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments and Local Self‑Government

The Seventy‑third Amendment (1992) added Part IX (Articles 243‑243O) and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects) to institutionalise rural local bodies. The Seventy‑fourth Amendment (1992) added Part IXA (Articles 243P‑243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects) for urban local bodies. Both amendments mandate a three‑tier structure in states with a population above 20 lakhs: Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (block), and Zilla Parishad (district) for rural areas; and Nagar Panchayat (transitional), Municipal Council (smaller urban), and Municipal Corporation (larger urban) for urban areas. States with smaller populations may adopt a two‑tier system.

Reservations: Not less than one‑third of seats and chairperson positions are reserved for women (including SC/ST women). Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population. The State Election Commission (SEC) (Article 243K/243ZA) conducts elections; the State Finance Commission (SFC) (Article 243I/243Y) reviews the financial position of panchayats/municipalities every five years and recommends devolution of taxes, duties, tolls, and fees, as well as grants‑in‑aid from the Consolidated Fund of the State.

Devolution of powers: The Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules list subjects (e.g., agriculture, minor irrigation, rural housing, drinking water, sanitation, urban planning, fire services). However, actual transfer of functions, funds, and functionaries (the “3Fs”) remains uneven. The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑20) recommended a substantial increase in the share of the divisible pool for local bodies (from 0.5 % to 4.5 % of the divisible pool) and introduced performance‑based grants. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2021‑26) continued this trajectory, earmarking ₹4.36 lakh crore for local bodies, with 60 % tied to basic services and 40 % to performance.

Judicial oversight: In K. Krishnamurthy v. Union of India (2010) the Supreme Court held that the SEC’s independence is part of the basic structure. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2021) the Court directed states to constitute SFCs on time and implement their recommendations. The 2022 amendment to Article 243ZD (via the Constitution (One Hundred and Fifth Amendment) Act) empowered the President to specify the manner of appointment of the SEC, aiming to insulate it from state executive influence.

Constitutional Bodies: Comptroller and Auditor General, Election Commission, and Union Public Service Commission

The Constitution establishes several independent constitutional bodies to safeguard accountability, electoral integrity, and merit‑based public service. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) (Article 148) is appointed by the President for a term of six years or until age 65, whichever is earlier. Removal requires the same procedure as a Supreme Court judge (proved misbehaviour or incapacity). The CAG audits all receipts and expenditure of the Union and States, including government companies and corporations. Reports are submitted to the President/Governor and laid before Parliament/State Legislature. The 2017 amendment to the CAG’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act expanded the CAG’s mandate to audit public‑private partnership projects and entities receiving substantial government funding.

The Election Commission of India (ECI) (Article 324) consists of the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and such number of Election Commissioners as the President may fix (currently two). They enjoy a six‑year term or until age 65. The CEC can be removed only by impeachment; other Commissioners can be removed by the President on the CEC’s recommendation. The ECI superintends, directs, and controls elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice‑President. The Model Code of Conduct, though not statutory, acquires legal force through the ECI’s plenary powers under Article 324. The 2023 amendment to the Election Commission (Conditions of Service) Act aligned the salary and allowances of Commissioners with those of Supreme Court judges.

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) (Article 315) comprises a Chairman and members appointed by the President for six years or until age 65. It conducts examinations for All India Services and Central Services, advises on recruitment rules, promotions, and disciplinary matters. The State Public Service Commissions mirror the UPSC at the state level. Doctrine of Colourable Legislation and Its Application in Centre‑State Relations

The doctrine of colourable legislation rests on the maxim that what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly. In the Indian constitutional scheme, it operates as a judicial tool to test whether a legislature, while acting within its assigned field, has in substance trespassed into the domain of another legislature. The Supreme Court first articulated the principle in K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953), holding that the competence of a legislature is determined by the true nature and character of the legislation, not merely by its form or label. The Court examines the pith and substance of the law; if the dominant purpose falls within the legislative competence, incidental encroachment is permissible, but a law whose real object lies outside the assigned list is colourable and void.

In the Centre‑State context, the doctrine is invoked primarily when Parliament legislates under the Union List (List I) or the Concurrent List (List III) in a manner that effectively regulates a State subject (List II). A landmark illustration is State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1959), where a central law on “industries” was struck down because its real impact was on “agricultural indebtedness”, a State subject. Conversely, in Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce (1947), the Court upheld a central statute on money‑lending because its pith and substance was “promissory notes”, a Union subject, even though it affected money‑lending, a State subject.

The doctrine also interacts with the residuary power under Article 248 and Entry 97 of List I. In Union of India v. H. S. Dhillon (1971), the Court held that Parliament cannot use the residuary entry to enact a law whose true character belongs to the State List, as that would be a colourable exercise of power. The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to expand Parliament’s reach by inserting “industries” into the Concurrent List, but the basic structure doctrine later limited such wholesale transfers.

Judicial scrutiny focuses on three indicators: (i) the legislative history and preamble of the statute; (ii) the practical effect of the provisions on the subject matter; and (iii) whether the legislature could have achieved the same result through a valid entry. The burden of proving colourability lies on the challenger, but once a prima facie case is made, the Court examines the substance rigorously. The doctrine does not invalidate laws merely because they have incidental effects on another list; it targets deliberate legislative overreach disguised as legitimate exercise.

Recent applications include the challenge to the Farm Laws (2020) where petitioners argued that the Centre’s use of Entry 33 (trade and commerce) and Entry 42 (inter‑state trade) was colourable because the laws effectively regulated agricultural marketing, a State subject under Entry 14 of List II. The Supreme Court’s stay order and the subsequent repeal of the laws left the constitutional question open, but the debate underscored the continuing relevance of the doctrine in federal disputes.

Principle of Harmonious Construction in Constitutional Interpretation

The principle of harmonious construction directs courts to interpret constitutional provisions in a manner that avoids conflict and gives effect to all parts of the Constitution. It assumes that the Constitution is a coherent whole and that no provision was intended to nullify another. The Supreme Court in Re: Kerala Education Bill (1957) stated that when two provisions appear contradictory, the court must construe them so that both can operate, if possible, rather than declaring one void. This principle is distinct from the doctrine of pith and substance, which resolves legislative competence disputes; harmonious construction resolves interpretive clashes between constitutional articles themselves.

A classic application is the reconciliation of Article 25 (freedom of religion) with Article 26 (right of religious denominations to manage affairs). In Shirur Mutt v. Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments (1954), the Court held that the right to manage religious affairs under Article 26 is subject to public order, morality, and health under Article 25(1), but the two articles must be read together so that the essential religious practices are protected while the state may regulate secular activities associated with religious institutions.

Another significant instance is the interplay between Article 31A (saving of laws providing for acquisition of estates) and Article 14 (equality before law). In Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981), the Court applied harmonious construction to hold that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to challenge on the ground of violating basic structure, thereby preserving the supremacy of fundamental rights while respecting the legislative intent behind Article 31A.

The principle also governs the relationship between fundamental rights and directive principles. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Court declared that the harmony between Part III and Part IV is an essential feature of the Constitution; neither can be given absolute primacy. The 42nd Amendment’s attempt to give directive principles precedence over fundamental rights was struck down as violating this harmonious balance.

In federal disputes, harmonious construction is used to read the Union, State, and Concurrent Lists together. The Court in State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) emphasized that entries in the three lists must be interpreted broadly and harmoniously, avoiding a narrow construction that would render any list redundant. The principle also applies to the interpretation of Article 246 (distribution of legislative powers) and Article 254 (repugnancy), ensuring that central and state laws can coexist unless there is a direct and irreconcilable conflict.

Harmonious construction is not a licence to rewrite the Constitution. It operates only when two interpretations are possible; if the language is clear and unambiguous, the plain meaning prevails. The Supreme Court in Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) cautioned that the principle cannot be used to dilute the basic structure. Thus, while harmonious construction promotes constitutional coherence, it remains subordinate to the overarching framework of constitutional supremacy.

Constitutional Conventions in India: Nature, Examples, and Judicial Recognition

Constitutional conventions are non‑legal rules that govern the exercise of constitutional powers. Unlike statutory provisions, they are not enforceable by courts, yet they are binding on constitutional actors through political accountability. The Indian Constitution, while largely codified, operates within a framework of conventions inherited from the British Westminster model and developed indigenously. The Supreme Court in R. C. Poudyal v. Union of India (1993) acknowledged that conventions “fill the interstices of the Constitution” and are part of the constitutional morality that guides governance.

Key conventions include: (i) The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), a convention solidified by the 42nd and 44th Amendments; (ii) The Governor’s discretionary powers are exercised sparingly and in accordance with constitutional morality, as emphasized in Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016); (iii) The Prime Minister must be a member of the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha within six months of appointment, a convention derived from parliamentary responsibility; (iv) The Leader of the Opposition is recognized for certain appointments (e.g., CVC, Lokpal) though not explicitly mandated by the Constitution; (v) The convention of collective responsibility of the Cabinet to the Lok Sabha, which underpins the confidence mechanism.

The convention that the President dissolves the Lok Sabha only on the advice of the Prime Minister was tested in 1979 when President Neelam Sanjiva Reddy dissolved the House on the advice of Prime Minister Charan Singh, who lacked a clear majority. The move was politically controversial but not judicially challenged, illustrating the political nature of conventions.

Judicial recognition of conventions is limited. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 (President’s Rule) is justiciable, but the convention that the Governor’s report must be based on objective material was treated as a constitutional requirement rather than a mere convention. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2021), the Court directed timely constitution of State Finance Commissions, elevating a statutory duty to a constitutional imperative, blurring the line between convention and law.

The distinction between convention and law is crucial for exam answers. A convention cannot be enforced by a writ of mandamus; its breach leads to political consequences (resignation, loss of confidence, electoral defeat). However, courts may use conventions as interpretive aids. For instance, the convention that the Chief Justice of India should be the senior‑most judge was elevated to a constitutional norm in the Second Judges Case (1993) and later codified in the Memorandum of Procedure.

Recent debates involve the convention of “consultation” versus “concurrence” in judicial appointments, the convention of the Governor acting as a neutral constitutional head, and the convention of the Speaker’s impartiality. The 2015 NJAC judgment (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India) held that the convention of judicial primacy in appointments is part of the basic structure, converting a convention into a legally enforceable principle.

Doctrine of Pleasure and Civil Services: Constitutional Safeguards under Articles 310 and 311

The doctrine of pleasure, derived from English common law, holds that a civil servant holds office at the pleasure of the President (or Governor). Article 310(1) enshrines this principle: “Except as expressly provided by this Constitution, every person who is a member of a defence service or of a civil service of the Union or of an all‑India service or holds any post connected with defence or any civil post under the Union holds office during the pleasure of the President.” However, Article 311 provides two critical safeguards against arbitrary dismissal, removal, or reduction in rank.

Article 311(1) mandates that no civil servant shall be dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank by an authority subordinate to that by which he was appointed. Article 311(2) requires that no such penalty be imposed except after an inquiry in which the civil servant is informed of the charges and given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. The inquiry must comply with the principles of natural justice. The Supreme Court in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Babu Ram Upadhyaya (1961) held that the “reasonable opportunity” includes the right to cross‑examine witnesses and to present evidence.

The doctrine of pleasure is not absolute. In Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985), the Court ruled that the pleasure doctrine cannot be exercised arbitrarily; it must conform to Article 311 and the principles of natural justice. The 42nd Amendment (1976) inserted Article 311(2)(c) allowing the President to dispense with inquiry in the interest of state security, but this was later struck down in Union of India v. S. L. Verma (1985) as violating the basic structure.

Article 311 does not apply to: (i) persons appointed on contract; (ii) persons holding office during pleasure under Article 310 but not covered by Article 311 (e.g., certain high‑level posts like the Attorney General, Comptroller and Auditor General, who have separate removal procedures); (iii) members of the armed forces, who are governed by separate service laws. The distinction between “dismissal”, “removal”, and “reduction in rank” is significant: dismissal and removal both terminate service, but removal does not disqualify from future employment, whereas dismissal does. Reduction in rank entails a lower pay scale and status.

The Supreme Court in K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1975) clarified that the inquiry officer need not be a judicial officer, but the proceedings must be fair. In Managing Committee, Sreejayanthi Institute v. State of Kerala (2002), the Court held that the disciplinary authority must apply its mind to the inquiry report and not merely adopt it mechanically.

Recent developments include the 2019 amendment to the All India Services (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, which introduced timelines for completion of departmental inquiries, aiming to prevent inordinate delay. The Court in State of Maharashtra v. Dr. Praveen B. Togadia (2020) emphasized that delay in concluding disciplinary proceedings can vitiate the order if it causes prejudice.

The National Commission for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes: Constitutional Mandate, Powers, and Effectiveness

The National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC) and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST) are constitutional bodies established under Articles 338 and 338A respectively. The original Article 338 provided for a Special Officer for SCs and STs; the 65th Amendment (1990) replaced it with a multi‑member Commission for SCs and STs. The 89th Amendment (2003) bifurcated it into two separate Commissions: NCSC under Article 338 and NCST under Article 338A.

Each Commission consists of a Chairperson, a Vice‑Chairperson, and three other Members, appointed by the President for a term of three years. They enjoy the rank and status of a Cabinet Minister and Minister of State respectively. The Commissions’ functions include: (i) investigating and monitoring all matters relating to safeguards for SCs/STs under the Constitution; (ii) inquiring into specific complaints of deprivation of rights; (iii) advising on the planning process for socio‑economic development; (iv) presenting annual reports to the President, which are laid before Parliament.

The Commissions have the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, for summoning witnesses, requiring production of documents, and receiving evidence on affidavits. Their recommendations are advisory, not binding. However, the government must explain the action taken on their reports before Parliament. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2018), the Supreme Court held that the Commissions’ reports carry significant persuasive value and that non‑implementation without valid reasons may attract judicial scrutiny.

Effectiveness challenges include: (i) Vacancies and delayed appointments undermining continuity; (ii) Limited enforcement power — the Commissions cannot initiate prosecution or direct compensation; (iii) Overlapping jurisdiction with the National Human Rights Commission and State Commissions; (iv) Resource constraints and lack of independent investigative machinery. The 2019 amendment to the NCSC and NCST (Conditions of Service) Rules enhanced financial autonomy by charging their expenses on the Consolidated Fund of India.

Landmark interventions: The NCSC’s 2016 report on atrocities against Dalits in Gujarat led to the strengthening of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, through the 2015 Amendment. The NCST’s 2020 report on forest rights implementation prompted the Ministry of Tribal Affairs to issue guidelines for Gram Sabha consent under the Forest Rights Act, 2006.

The Doctrine of Prospective Overruling in Indian Constitutional Jurisprudence

The doctrine of prospective overruling allows a court to declare a new legal principle while limiting its effect to future transactions, thereby protecting vested rights and administrative continuity. In India, the Supreme Court first invoked this technique in Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967), where it held that Parliament could not amend fundamental rights but applied the ruling prospectively so that amendments made before the judgment remained valid. The Court reasoned that a retrospective declaration would unsettle the constitutional order and create chaos in governance.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the doctrine. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the majority did not expressly use prospective overruling, but the basic structure limitation on amendment power operates prospectively by default — amendments enacted before 1973 are not invalidated. The Court explicitly employed prospective overruling in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) when it struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that curtailed judicial review, limiting the declaration to future cases. In Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981), the Court held that the basic structure doctrine would apply only to amendments made after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda judgment.

The technique serves two constitutional objectives: it respects the principle of legal certainty and prevents the nullification of past legislative and executive actions taken in good faith. However, the Court has cautioned that prospective overruling cannot be used to perpetuate unconstitutional practices indefinitely. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court refused to apply prospective overruling to the proclamation of President’s Rule, emphasizing that constitutional violations must be remedied immediately.

For examination purposes, aspirants should distinguish prospective overruling from the doctrine of stare decisis and from the doctrine of eclipse. Prospective overruling is a remedial tool; eclipse deals with pre‑constitutional laws inconsistent with fundamental rights. The key test is whether the new rule is applied to past, present, or future cases, and the Court’s reasoning for limiting retrospectivity.

The Constitutional Status and Evolution of the National Commission for Backward Classes

The National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) traversed a statutory‑to‑constitutional journey that illustrates the dynamic interplay between legislative policy and judicial mandate. Originally constituted under the National Commission for Backward Classes Act, 1993 pursuant to the Supreme Court’s direction in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992), the NCBC functioned as a statutory body with recommendatory powers on inclusion and exclusion of castes in the Central List of Other Backward Classes (OBCs).

The 102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018 inserted Article 338B, granting the NCBC constitutional status. The new Article provides for a Chairperson, Vice‑Chairperson, and three other Members appointed by the President for a three‑year term. The Commission’s functions mirror those of the NCSC and NCST: investigating safeguards, inquiring into complaints, advising on socio‑economic development, and presenting annual reports to the President. Its recommendations are advisory, but the government must lay an action‑taken report before Parliament.

A critical development followed with the 105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021, which amended Article 338B and introduced Article 342A (clauses 3 and 4). This amendment restored the power of State governments to identify and notify socially and educationally backward classes for their own lists, a power that the 102nd Amendment had arguably centralized. The 105th Amendment also clarified that the President’s notification under Article 342A(1) pertains only to the Central List, while States retain autonomy over State Lists.

Judicial scrutiny accompanied these changes. In Dr. Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister (2021), the Supreme Court upheld the 102nd Amendment but read down its effect to preserve State powers, paving the way for the 105th Amendment. The Court emphasized that the constitutional design envisions a cooperative federalism in backward‑class identification.

For exam preparation, remember the amendment chronology: 102nd (2018) — constitutional status; 105th (2021) — restoration of State list‑making power. Distinguish the NCBC (Article 338B) from the NCSC (Article 338) and NCST (Article 338A). Note that the NCBC’s recommendations are not binding, but the government’s obligation to place action‑taken reports before Parliament creates political accountability.

The Principle of Separation of Powers and Its Judicial Interpretation in India

The Indian Constitution does not adopt a rigid separation of powers akin to the United States model; instead it embraces a functional overlap with checks and balances. Article 50 directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services, but the political executive is drawn from the legislature, and the legislature exercises control over the executive through confidence motions.

The Supreme Court in Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955) held that the Constitution does not recognise a strict separation of powers but distributes functions among the three organs. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), the Court struck down the 39th Amendment clause that insulated the Prime Minister’s election from judicial review, affirming that judicial review is a basic structure element and that no organ can arrogate unchecked authority.

Legislative encroachment on judicial functions has been examined in State of Tamil Nadu v. State of Kerala (2014) where the Court invalidated a legislative act that retrospectively overturned a judicial decree, holding it violated separation of powers. Conversely, judicial activism has been scrutinised: in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) (the NJAC case), the Court struck down the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, asserting that judicial primacy in appointments is part of the basic structure, thereby preserving judicial independence from executive dominance.

The doctrine also governs the relationship between the legislature and the executive. The anti‑defection law (Tenth Schedule) empowers the Speaker — a legislative officer — to adjudicate disqualification, a quasi‑judicial function. The Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) upheld this but subjected the Speaker’s decision to judicial review, reinforcing that no organ is the final arbiter of its own powers.

For aspirants, the key takeaway is that separation of powers in India is a principle of constitutional morality rather than a textual mandate. The basic structure doctrine acts as the ultimate guardian, ensuring that no amendment or practice destroys the equilibrium among the three organs.

Constitutional Safeguards for Linguistic Minorities: Articles 29, 30, 347, 350A, and 350B

The Constitution provides a layered framework for the protection of linguistic minorities, combining cultural rights, educational autonomy, and administrative mechanisms. Article 29(1) guarantees any section of citizens having a distinct language, script, or culture the right to conserve the same. Article 30(1) confers on all minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

Article 347 empowers the President to direct a State to officially recognise a language spoken by a substantial proportion of its population, thereby enabling its use for official purposes. Article 350A obligates every State to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother‑tongue at the primary stage for children belonging to linguistic minority groups. Article 350B provides for a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities, appointed by the President, to investigate matters relating to safeguards for linguistic minorities and report to the President.

Judicial interpretation has expanded these provisions. In T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002), the Supreme Court held that the right under Article 30(1) includes the right to choose the medium of instruction, subject to reasonable regulation. In St. Stephen’s College v. University of Delhi (1992), the Court affirmed that minority institutions may reserve up to 50% of seats for students of their own community without violating Article 29(2). The Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities submits annual reports; the 2022 report highlighted gaps in implementation of Article 350A in several States, prompting the Ministry of Education to issue advisories.

Exam focus: differentiate Article 29 (conservation of language, script, culture) from Article 30 (educational institutions). Remember that Article 350B creates a constitutional office, unlike the statutory National Commission for Minorities. Questions often test whether a State can impose a regional language as medium of instruction in minority schools — the answer is no, unless the regulation is reasonable and does not destroy the minority character.

The Constitutional Framework for the Lokpal and Lokayuktas: Statutory Innovation and Constitutional Implications

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 establishes an anti‑corruption ombudsman at the Centre (Lokpal) and in States (Lokayuktas). Although the Act is statutory, its constitutional underpinnings derive from Article 51A(j) (fundamental duty to strive for excellence) and the Directive Principles under Article 38 (promote welfare) and Article 39 (prevent concentration of wealth). The Act also draws on the Convention against Corruption ratified by India in 2011.

The Lokpal consists of a Chairperson and up to eight Members, with at least 50% judicial members. The selection committee comprises the Prime Minister, Speaker of Lok Sabha, Leader of Opposition, Chief Justice of India (or a Supreme Court judge), and an eminent jurist. This composition reflects a checks‑and‑balances design, ensuring executive, legislative, and judicial participation.

Key constitutional issues have arisen. In Common Cause v. Union of India (2018), the Supreme Court directed the government to appoint the Lokpal without delay, treating the statutory mandate as an enforceable constitutional obligation under Article 14 (equality before law) and Article 21 (right to clean governance). The Court also held that the absence of a Leader of Opposition does not paralyse the selection committee; the senior‑most opposition leader can be included.

The Act empowers the Lokpal to investigate allegations against public servants, including the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, and Members of Parliament. However, the Lokpal’s jurisdiction over the Prime Minister requires a full bench decision and a two‑thirds majority, reflecting the sensitivity of executive accountability.

Constitutional Interpretation: Purposive, Literal and the Living Tree Doctrine

Interpretation of the Constitution is not a mechanical exercise of applying literal meanings; it involves a nuanced balance between the text, the purpose behind it and the evolving societal context. The Supreme Court has articulated three dominant approaches:

  • Literal (Textual) Interpretation – The Court reads the words as they stand, giving effect to ordinary grammatical meaning. This approach was evident in State of Madras v. Smt. Champakam Dorairajan (1951), where the Court upheld the caste‑based reservation in education by focusing on the literal reading of Article 29(2).
  • Purposive (Teleological) Interpretation – The Court looks beyond the literal text to the underlying purpose of the provision. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Court invoked the purpose of the basic structure doctrine to read Article 368 as containing inherent limitations, even though the text itself did not expressly mention “basic structure”.
  • Living Tree (Dynamic) Interpretation – Borrowed from the Canadian case Edwards v. Canada (Attorney General) (1930), Indian jurisprudence treats the Constitution as a living organism that must grow with changing realities. The landmark judgment Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded the scope of Article 21 from “procedure established by law” to “fair, just and reasonable procedure”, reflecting a dynamic reading.

The Supreme Court often blends these methods. In Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998), the Court employed a purposive approach to read Article 324 (Election Commission) in light of the purpose of free and fair elections, while also invoking the living tree doctrine to adapt the Commission’s powers to modern electoral challenges such as electronic voting.

Key factors influencing the choice of interpretative method include:

  1. Nature of the provision – Fundamental Rights are often read purposively to ensure substantive protection, whereas procedural articles may be approached literally.
  2. Historical context – The Constituent Assembly Debates (CAD) serve as a primary source for purposive reading, revealing framers’ intent.
  3. Socio‑economic evolution – The living tree approach is invoked when societal values have shifted dramatically, e.g., the recognition of the right to privacy.

Understanding the interplay of these methods is crucial for answering questions that ask the Court’s likely approach to a novel constitutional issue.

The Evolution of the Right to Privacy as a Fundamental Right

At the time of adoption, the Constitution did not expressly mention privacy. The Supreme Court, however, gradually read privacy into Article 21 through a series of landmark judgments.

  • Kharak Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1962) – The Court held that a police search without a warrant violated the right to personal liberty, hinting at privacy but not yet elevating it to a fundamental right.
  • R. Madhavan v. State of Tamil Nadu (1975) – The Court emphasized that the right to personal liberty includes a sphere of privacy, especially concerning bodily integrity.
  • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) – A nine‑judge bench unanimously declared privacy as a fundamental right protected under Articles 14, 19 and 21. The judgment traced the evolution from Kharak Singh, highlighted the need for privacy in the digital age, and set out a three‑tier test: (i) the right is intrinsic to life and liberty; (ii) any restriction must satisfy the test of legality, necessity and proportionality; (iii) the State must provide procedural safeguards.

The Puttaswamy judgment also linked privacy to other rights: freedom of speech (Article 19), equality (Article 14) and the right to dignity (Article 21). It paved the way for subsequent decisions such as Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (Aadhaar) (2018), where the Court upheld the Aadhaar scheme but struck down the mandatory linking of Aadhaar to bank accounts and mobile connections, emphasizing proportionality.

For exam takers, the trajectory from a non‑explicit right to a fully fledged fundamental right is a classic “developmental” theme. Questions may ask you to compare the privacy jurisprudence before and after 2017, or to apply the three‑tier test to a hypothetical data‑collection law.

Technique: Applying the Puttaswamy Test
A State enacts the “Public Safety Surveillance Act” mandating installation of CCTV cameras in all private residences for counter‑terrorism. The Act does not provide any prior judicial authorization. Assess its constitutionality.

Step 1 – Identify the right involved: The Act infringes the fundamental right to privacy under Article 21 (as read in Puttaswamy).

Step 2 – Legality: The Act is a law, satisfying the first limb of the test.

Step 3 – Necessity and Proportionality: The State must show a compelling interest (national security) and that the measure is the least restrictive means. Blanket installation without prior authorization is over‑broad; less intrusive alternatives (targeted surveillance, judicial warrants) exist. Hence, the proportionality balance tilts against the State.

Conclusion: The Act is unconstitutional as it fails the necessity‑proportionality test of the Puttaswamy framework.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks you to evaluate a law under the right to privacy, immediately invoke the three‑tier Puttaswamy test. Structure your answer: (i) existence of right, (ii) legality, (iii) necessity & proportionality. Cite Kharak Singh for early jurisprudence and Puttaswamy for the definitive test.

The Interplay of Directive Principles of State Policy and Fundamental Rights: Judicial Balancing

Directive Principles (DPs) and Fundamental Rights (FRs) occupy distinct constitutional spaces: DPs are non‑justiciable guidelines (Articles 36‑51), while FRs are enforceable rights (Articles 12‑35). The early post‑independence period witnessed tension, especially when DPs appeared to curtail FRs.

Key judicial milestones:

  • State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) – The Court struck down caste‑based reservations in education, holding that DPs could not override the equality clause of Article 14.
  • Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) – The Supreme Court reaffirmed the “balance” doctrine, holding that Article 31C (which gave primacy to DPs over FRs) was unconstitutional to the extent it destroyed the basic structure, thereby preserving the supremacy of FRs.
  • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) – While primarily known for the basic structure doctrine, the judgment also emphasized that DPs and FRs must coexist, with the latter providing enforceable limits.
  • Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corp. (1985) – The Court read the right to livelihood (DP) into Article 21, illustrating that DPs can be given content through FRs.

The current jurisprudential approach treats DPs as “negative” constraints on State action: the State must not enact laws that defeat the spirit of DPs while simultaneously respecting FRs. This is evident in the Supreme Court’s decision in R. Madhavan v. State of Tamil Nadu (1975), where the Court held that the right to education (DP) could not be used to justify denial of free and compulsory education under Article 21A (FR).

For exam answers, a useful framework is:

  1. Identify the FR and the DP in conflict.
  2. Examine whether the DP is being invoked to justify a restriction on the FR.
  3. Apply the “balance” test: does the restriction violate the basic structure or the essential content of the FR?
  4. Cite relevant case law (Champakam Dorairajan, Minerva Mills, Kesavananda) to support the conclusion.

Comparative Federalism: Indian Quasi‑Federalism versus Canadian Federalism

While both India and Canada are federal states, their constitutional designs differ markedly, offering useful comparative insights for answering “compare and contrast” type questions.

Structural Foundations

India – The Constitution establishes a “union of states” (Art. 1) with a strong centre. The distribution of powers is outlined in the Union, State and Concurrent Lists (Seventh Schedule). The centre can legislate on any matter in the State List during emergencies (Art. 352, 360) and can dismiss state governments under Article 356, reflecting a unitary bias.

Canada – The Constitution Act, 1867 creates a federation where powers are divided between the federal Parliament and provincial legislatures. The “peace, order and good government” (POGG) clause allows the federal Parliament to legislate on matters of national concern, but the Supreme Court has interpreted POGG narrowly, preserving provincial autonomy.

Fiscal Relations

India’s Finance Commission (established under Art. 280) recommends the distribution of tax revenues, and the GST Council (Art. 279) coordinates indirect tax reforms. Canada employs the “equalization payments” system, a federal transfer aimed at reducing fiscal disparities among provinces.

Judicial Review and Inter‑governmental Disputes

In India, the Supreme Court adjudicates Centre‑State disputes under Articles 131 and 143, often invoking the doctrine of “pith and substance”. The Court’s proactive stance is evident in cases like State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1962) where it upheld the central legislation on the basis of a dominant purpose test.

Canada’s Supreme Court follows a “co‑operative federalism” model, emphasizing negotiation and respect for provincial jurisdiction. The landmark case Reference re: Secession of Quebec (1998) underscored the principle that any constitutional change requires substantial provincial consent.

Emergency Powers

India’s emergency provisions (Arts 352, 360, 363) allow the centre to assume extensive powers, a feature absent in Canada. Canadian federalism lacks a comparable emergency clause; instead, the federal government can invoke the “national emergency” power under the Emergencies Act (1988), but this does not alter the division of powers.

Understanding these contrasts helps answer questions that ask why India’s federalism is described as “quasi‑federal”. The answer lies in the constitutional design that grants the centre overriding powers, the presence of emergency provisions, and the fiscal dominance of the Union, all of which differ from Canada’s more balanced federal structure.

Doctrine of Pith and Substance: Determining Legislative Competence in Centre‑State Relations

The doctrine of pith and substance originated in Canadian constitutional law and was transplanted into India through the Government of India Act, 1935. It operates as the primary tool for courts to ascertain whether a legislature has transgressed its allotted field when a law appears to encroach upon the domain of another legislature. The test requires the court to examine the true nature and character of the impugned legislation — its “pith and substance” — rather than its incidental effects. If the legislation, in its essence, falls within the competence of the enacting legislature, incidental trenching on another list does not render it ultra vires.

Core Elements of the Test

  • Dominant Purpose: The court identifies the dominant purpose of the statute by looking at its preamble, scheme, and substantive provisions.
  • Incidental Encroachment: A law may incidentally affect matters in another list; such encroachment is permissible if it is ancillary to the main object.
  • Colour of Legislation: The doctrine is distinct from colourable legislation, which probes legislative motive. Pith and substance is an objective inquiry into legislative competence.

Landmark Applications

State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) — The Supreme Court upheld the Bombay Prohibition Act, holding that its pith and substance was prohibition (a State List entry) even though it incidentally affected import and export (Union List). State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1962) — The Court applied the doctrine to validate the Union’s law on acquisition of land for a refugee settlement, finding the dominant purpose was rehabilitation (Union List) despite incidental impact on land (State List). K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953) — The Court upheld the state law on religious endowments, finding its pith and substance was regulation of religious institutions (Concurrent List) within the state's competence. but the state lacked competence to legislate on the subject in the manner attempted.

Interaction with the Concurrent List and Residuary Powers

When both Parliament and a State legislature enact laws on a Concurrent List entry, the doctrine helps determine whether the state law is genuinely on that entry or a disguised intrusion into a Union List subject. Residuary powers (Article 248) are also interpreted through this lens: a law that appears to be on a residuary matter must not, in substance, be a covered entry in the Union or State Lists.

Examiner’s Twist

Questions often present a statute with a mixed object and ask whether it is saved by the doctrine. The trap is to confuse “incidental encroachment” with “substantial encroachment”. A correct answer isolates the dominant purpose, cites the relevant list entries, and applies the Balsara or West Bengal reasoning.

Doctrine of Repugnancy under Article 254: Resolving Centre‑State Legislative Conflicts

Article 254 provides the constitutional mechanism for resolving inconsistencies between laws made by Parliament and State legislatures on matters enumerated in the Concurrent List (List III). It operates only when both legislatures are competent to legislate on the same subject and their laws cannot stand together.

Textual Framework

  • Article 254(1): If a State law is repugnant to a Union law on a Concurrent subject, the Union law prevails and the State law is void to the extent of repugnancy.
  • Article 254(2): A State law reserved for the President’s assent and receiving it can prevail in that State, notwithstanding repugnancy, but Parliament may subsequently override it by enacting a law “adding to, amending, varying or repealing” the State law.

Judicial Tests for Repugnancy

The Supreme Court has formulated three situations where repugnancy arises (M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India, 1979):

  1. Direct Conflict: The two laws contain mutually contradictory provisions such that obedience to one necessarily entails disobedience to the other.
  2. Exhaustive Code: Parliament enacts a comprehensive code evincing an intention to occupy the entire field, leaving no room for State legislation.
  3. Implied Repugnancy: The two laws, though not directly contradictory, create a situation where the State law frustrates the purpose of the Union law.

Key Precedents

Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983) — The Court held that the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, a Union law, prevailed over a State law fixing drug prices because Parliament had enacted an exhaustive regulatory scheme. Zameer Ahmed Latifur Rehman Sheikh v. State of Maharashtra (2010) — Repugnancy was found where a State amendment to the Code of Criminal Procedure conflicted with the central Act; the State law was void to the extent of inconsistency. Tika Ramji v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1956) — Established that repugnancy does not arise merely because both laws regulate the same subject; there must be actual inconsistency.

President’s Assent and Parliamentary Override

The President’s assent under Article 254(2) is a political safeguard, not a judicial one. Once assented, the State law operates only in that State. Parliament’s subsequent law under the proviso to Article 254(2) must expressly or by necessary implication cover the same field to nullify the State law.

Examiner’s Twist

A common error is to invoke Article 254 for conflicts involving Union List subjects. Repugnancy applies only to Concurrent List entries. Another trap is to treat the President’s assent as a permanent shield; it merely postpones the operation of the Union law until Parliament chooses to legislate again.

Worked Example: Repugnancy Analysis
A State enacts a law regulating the maximum retail price of essential commodities, a subject in the Concurrent List. Parliament has already passed the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, which empowers the Central Government to fix prices by notification. The State law provides a different pricing formula. The State law is reserved for the President’s assent and receives it. Subsequently, Parliament amends the Essential Commodities Act to expressly state that no State law shall fix prices for essential commodities. Determine the validity of the State law at each stage.
Stage 1 (before President’s assent): The State law is repugnant to the Central notification under the Essential Commodities Act because both prescribe pricing formulas for the same commodities. Under Article 254(1), the Union law prevails; the State law is void to the extent of inconsistency.
Stage 2 (after President’s assent): Article 254(2) saves the State law in that State, despite repugnancy, because it received Presidential assent. The State law operates unless Parliament overrides it.
Stage 3 (after Parliamentary amendment): The amendment expressly occupies the field, invoking the proviso to Article 254(2). The State law is now void in its entirety as Parliament has legislated “adding to, amending, varying or repealing” the State law. The State law ceases to have effect.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a repugnancy question appears, first verify that the subject is in the Concurrent List. Then apply the three‑fold test (direct conflict, exhaustive code, implied repugnancy). Track the chronological sequence: Union law → State law → Presidential assent → Parliamentary override. Cite Karunanidhi for the test and Hoechst for exhaustive code. Never invoke Article 254 for Union‑List or State‑List conflicts.

The Constitutional Position of the Governor: Powers, Discretion, and Judicial Pronouncements

The Governor occupies a dual role: constitutional head of the State and agent of the Union. Articles 153 to 162 define the office, but the contours of discretionary power have been shaped by commissions and the Supreme Court.

Appointment, Tenure, and Constitutional Safeguards

  • Article 155: Appointed by the President on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers.
  • Article 156: Holds office during the pleasure of the President; no fixed term, but convention expects a five‑year tenure.
  • Article 163: The Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except where the Constitution requires discretion.

Discretionary Powers and Their Limits

The Constitution expressly mandates discretion in: (i) reservation of Bills for the President’s consideration (Article 200); (ii) report on failure of constitutional machinery (Article 356); (iii) administration of Union Territories (Article 239) is not a Governor's discretionary power. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that the Governor’s discretion be exercised sparingly, in accordance with constitutional conventions, and that the Governor should not act as a parallel centre of power. The Punchhi Commission (2010) echoed this, suggesting a fixed tenure and removal only by impeachment.

Judicial Pronouncements

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) — The Court held that the Governor’s report under Article 356 is subject to judicial review; mala fide or irrelevant grounds render the proclamation invalid. Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) — The Governor’s discretion to dissolve the Assembly must be based on objective material, not political whim. Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016) — The Governor’s power to summon, prorogue, and dissolve the Assembly is not absolute; it must be exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in exceptional circumstances.

Assent to Legislation

Under Article 200, the Governor may assent, withhold assent, or reserve a Bill for the President. The Supreme Court in Purushothaman Nambudiri v. State of Kerala (1962) held that withholding assent is a pocket veto; however, prolonged inaction may be challenged as arbitrary. The 2023 State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor case reiterated that the Governor cannot sit on Bills indefinitely.

Examiner’s Twist

Questions often ask whether the Governor can dismiss a Chief Minister who enjoys majority support. The answer: No, unless the Chief Minister loses majority and the Governor has objective evidence. The Governor’s discretion is not a licence for political intervention; it is circumscribed by the principle of constitutional morality.

Doctrine of Proportionality in Constitutional Review: Evolution, Tests, and Landmark Cases

Proportionality, borrowed from German administrative law, has become the dominant standard for reviewing State action that infringes fundamental rights. It supersedes the earlier Wednesbury unreasonableness test in rights‑based adjudication.

Four‑Prong Test

  1. Legitimate Aim: The measure must pursue a constitutionally permissible objective.
  2. Rational Connection: The measure must be rationally connected to the aim.
  3. Necessity (Least Restrictive Means): No less intrusive alternative should achieve the same objective.
  4. Balancing (Proportionality Stricto Sensu): The benefit of the measure must outweigh the harm to the right.

Judicial Adoption

Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000) — The Court explicitly adopted proportionality for reviewing administrative decisions affecting fundamental rights. Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016) — Applied the four‑prong test to a statutory quota for post‑graduate medical seats, upholding it as proportionate. K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) — The nine‑judge bench enshrined proportionality as the standard for any intrusion on privacy (Article 21). Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) — Internet shutdowns in Jammu & Kashmir were tested for proportionality; the Court held indefinite shutdowns disproportionate.

Distinction from Wednesbury

Wednesbury asks whether a decision is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have made it. Proportionality demands a structured justification, placing the burden on the State to demonstrate necessity and balance. The shift reflects the transformative vision of the Constitution.

Examiner’s Twist

A frequent trap is to apply proportionality to pure policy matters where the Court defers to legislative wisdom (e.g., economic legislation under Article 19(6)). Proportionality applies when a fundamental right is directly restricted; for Directive Principles or non‑justiciable policies, the Court uses a lighter scrutiny.

Constitutional Framework for Tribunals: Articles 323A, 323B, and the Supreme Court’s Tribunalisation Jurisprudence

The Forty‑second Amendment (1976) inserted Articles 323A and 323B, enabling Parliament and State legislatures to create tribunals for adjudicating specific disputes, ostensibly to reduce judicial backlog.

Article 323A — Administrative Tribunals

Empowers Parliament to establish Administrative Tribunals for service matters of public servants. The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 created the Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) and State Administrative Tribunals. The Supreme Court in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) held that the power of judicial review under Articles 32, 226, and 136 is part of the basic structure; tribunals cannot oust High Court writ jurisdiction. Tribunals are “courts of first instance” but remain subject to High Court supervision.

Article 323B — Other Tribunals

Allows creation of tribunals for matters such as taxation, foreign exchange, land reforms, and rent control. The Supreme Court in R. Gandhi v. Union of India (2010) upheld the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and Appellate Tribunal, emphasizing functional independence. Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2014) struck down provisions that compromised tribunal independence (e.g., executive control over appointments, tenure). The Court laid down criteria: security of tenure, financial autonomy, and separation from the executive.

Recent Legislative Developments

The Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 abolished several appellate tribunals and transferred their functions to High Courts, aiming to streamline adjudication. The Finance Act, 2017 restructured tribunal appointments, prompting fresh litigation on independence. The Supreme Court in Roger Matthew v. Union of India (2022) reiterated that tribunal members’ terms and conditions must be determined by law, not executive fiat.

Examiner’s Twist

Questions may ask whether a tribunal’s decision is final. The answer: No, High Courts retain writ jurisdiction under Articles 226/227; the Supreme Court retains appellate jurisdiction under Article 136. Another trap is to assume Article 323A excludes judicial review entirely — Chandra Kumar is the authoritative negation.

  • Confusing pith and substance with colourable legislation — the former tests competence, the latter tests motive.
  • Applying Article 254 repugnancy to Union List or State List subjects — it operates only on Concurrent List entries.
  • Treating the Governor’s discretion as unfettered — it is limited to constitutionally specified situations and subject to judicial review.
  • Invoking proportionality for economic regulation under Article 19(6) where the Court applies a deferential reasonableness test.
  • Assuming tribunals under Articles 323A/323B are immune from High Court writ jurisdiction — L. Chandra Kumar expressly preserves it.
  • Misreading the President’s assent under Article 254(2) as a permanent validation — Parliament can override by subsequent legislation.
  • Overlooking the requirement that a State law reserved for Presidential assent must be on a Concurrent List subject; otherwise Article 254 is inapplicable.

Evolution of the Basic Structure Doctrine: From Kesavananda to Contemporary Challenges

The basic structure doctrine emerged as a judicial safeguard against the erosion of the Constitution’s core identity. Its genesis lies in the landmark judgment Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where a nine‑judge bench of the Supreme Court, by a narrow 7:6 majority, held that while Parliament possesses wide amending powers under Article 368, it cannot alter the “basic structure” of the Constitution. The majority opinion, authored by Justice S.M. Sinha, identified features such as the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, and the federal character as part of this inviolable core.

Subsequent cases refined and expanded the catalogue:

  • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) – upheld the basic structure limitation on the 42nd Amendment, emphasizing that the amendment could not destroy the democratic character of the Constitution.
  • Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) – struck down the amendment that sought to curtail judicial review, reinforcing that the power of judicial review itself is a basic feature.
  • Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) – clarified that the doctrine applies to both ordinary and constitutional amendments, and that any amendment violating the basic structure is void ab initio.
  • S. R. Batra v. Union of India (2002) – affirmed that the doctrine is not confined to a fixed list; it evolves with constitutional ethos, allowing the Court to recognise new basic features such as the right to a clean environment (later affirmed in Indian Council for Enviro‑Legal Action v. Union of India, 1996).

In recent years, the doctrine has been invoked in contexts far removed from the original debates on amendment power:

  1. National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) case (2015) – the Court struck down the NJAC Act, holding that the proposed amendment diluted the independence of the judiciary, a basic structure component.
  2. Ayodhya title suit (2019) – while the judgment primarily dealt with property law, the Court reiterated that the secular character of the Constitution remains a basic feature, guiding its interpretation of religious disputes.
  3. Right to Privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017) – the Court declared privacy a fundamental right, noting that any amendment seeking to curtail it would have to pass the basic structure test.

These developments illustrate a dynamic doctrine that adapts to emerging constitutional challenges. The Court’s approach balances respect for parliamentary sovereignty with the need to preserve the Constitution’s identity. Aspirants must therefore understand not only the doctrinal origin but also its application across varied domains, and be prepared to discuss how the doctrine operates as a “living limitation”.

Constitutional Amendment Process: Formal Mechanics, Unwritten Constraints, and the Role of Judicial Review

Article 368 outlines the formal procedure for amending the Constitution. Two distinct pathways exist:

  • Simple majority amendment – applicable to provisions such as the creation of new states (Art. 3) or the formation of a new Union Territory (Art. 239A). The bill requires passage by a simple majority in each House of Parliament and the President’s assent.
  • Special majority amendment – required for most substantive changes. The bill must be passed by a majority of the total membership of each House and by at least two‑thirds of the members present and voting. Certain amendments further demand ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures (e.g., changes affecting the federal structure, election of the President, or the Supreme Court).

Beyond these textual requirements, several unwritten constraints shape the amendment process:

1. The Basic Structure Limitation

As detailed earlier, any amendment that destroys or emasculates a basic feature is void. This constraint is judicially enforced and operates irrespective of the procedural compliance under Article 368.

2. The Doctrine of “Essential Features” of Federalism

Amendments altering the distribution of powers between Centre and States, the composition of the Rajya Sabha, or the residuary powers must respect the federal balance. The Supreme Court, in State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1962), warned that Parliament cannot use its amending power to convert a quasi‑federal system into a unitary one.

3. Political Realities and the “Amendment Fatigue”

Frequent amendments, especially those perceived as politically motivated, invite heightened judicial scrutiny. The 42nd Amendment (1976), often called the “Mini‑Constitution”, was partially struck down in Minerva Mills for violating the basic structure.

4. Judicial Review of the Amendment Process Itself

The Court can examine whether the procedural requirements of Article 368 have been satisfied. In Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965), the Supreme Court held that a constitutional amendment must strictly adhere to the prescribed majority; any deviation renders the amendment invalid.

Understanding these layers is crucial for answering questions that juxtapose the formal text with its practical limits. For instance, a question may present a hypothetical amendment that seeks to abolish the Supreme Court’s power of judicial review. Even if the amendment meets the special majority and state ratification criteria, the basic structure doctrine would render it unconstitutional.

Federal Financial Relations: Evolution of the Finance Commission, GST Council, and the Principle of Vertical Fiscal Imbalance

Fiscal federalism in India has undergone a transformative journey from the early post‑Independence era to the contemporary GST regime. The Constitution originally envisaged a vertical fiscal imbalance (VFI) – the Centre possessed a larger revenue base while the States required substantial expenditure powers. To address this, Article 280 mandated the establishment of a Finance Commission (FC) every five years.

First Finance Commission (1957) – Chaired by Dr. R. K. Mookerjee, it recommended a 75:25 share of tax proceeds between Centre and States, reflecting the nascent fiscal capacity of the States. Subsequent commissions progressively increased the States’ share, reaching 42% in the 14th FC (2015‑2020), acknowledging the growing expenditure responsibilities of sub‑national governments.

The introduction of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) in 2017 marked a paradigm shift. The GST Council, a constitutional body under Article 279A, comprises the Union Finance Minister (Chair) and the State Finance Ministers. Its functions include:

  1. Determining tax rates and thresholds for various goods and services.
  2. Resolving disputes on tax jurisdiction and revenue distribution.
  3. Ensuring a uniform tax structure while preserving a modest share of revenue for the Centre (currently 1% of the GST pool as compensation for the loss of excise duties).

The GST regime reduced the multiplicity of indirect taxes, thereby expanding the tax base and enhancing compliance. However, it also intensified the VFI debate because the GST pool is shared 50:50 between Centre and States, but the Centre retains a larger share of the Union Excise and Customs duties that are not subsumed under GST.

Key judicial pronouncements on fiscal federalism:

  • State of Bihar v. Union of India (1995) – the Supreme Court upheld the Centre’s power to levy taxes under the Union List, emphasizing the need for a strong central fiscal capacity.
  • State of West Bengal v. Union of India (2010) – the Court examined the validity of the GST Council’s decisions, holding that the Council’s recommendations, once ratified by Parliament, acquire the force of law.
  • Union of India v. State of Kerala (2021) – clarified that the Centre cannot unilaterally alter the revenue-sharing formula without adhering to the constitutional amendment process or the Finance Commission’s recommendations.

For exam answers, candidates should trace the trajectory from the early Finance Commissions to the GST Council, highlighting how each institutional innovation attempted to mitigate VFI while preserving the Centre’s fiscal primacy. Emphasise the interplay between constitutional provisions, statutory enactments (e.g., the GST Act, 2017), and judicial oversight.

Doctrine of Colourable Legislation: Scope, Landmark Cases, and Its Interaction with the Basic Structure

Colourable legislation occurs when a law is framed within the competence of the legislature but in substance seeks to achieve an objective outside its jurisdiction. The doctrine prevents a legislature from disguising an unconstitutional purpose behind a facially valid provision.

Foundational case – State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977):

The Supreme Court struck down a law that purported to regulate “inter‑state trade” (a Union List subject) by imposing restrictions on a State’s internal market. The Court held that the law was colourable because its true aim was to control a matter reserved for the Centre.

Subsequent judgments refined the test:

  • State of Bombay v. United Provinces (1949) – introduced the “pith and substance” analysis to determine the true character of legislation.
  • Bihar State Electricity Board v. Kameshwar Singh (1971) – the Court examined whether a State law, though framed as a tax, was actually a regulation of a Union List subject (electricity generation). It held the law colourable and invalid.
  • Union of India v. R. C. Cooper (1970) – clarified that the doctrine applies even when the impugned provision is within the legislative competence, if its operative effect defeats the constitutional scheme.

The doctrine interacts with the basic structure in two ways:

  1. Preserving Federal Balance – colourable legislation that encroaches upon the distribution of powers threatens the basic structure’s federal character. The Court, therefore, treats such encroachments as violations of the basic structure.
  2. Safeguarding Judicial Review – by invalidating colourable statutes, the judiciary reinforces its role as a guardian of constitutional limits, a basic feature affirmed in Minerva Mills.

Application in contemporary contexts:

Recent debates on the “National Education Policy” amendments have raised questions about whether certain provisions, framed as “curricular reforms”, effectively regulate the language policy—a subject listed under the State List. If challenged, courts would apply the colourable legislation test to ascertain whether the central government is overstepping its constitutional domain.

Similarly, the “Farm Laws” of 2020 were contested on the ground that they attempted to regulate agricultural production (a State List matter) under the guise of “trade and commerce”. The Supreme Court’s stay order reflected concerns of colourable legislation, though the final judgment is pending.

For aspirants, mastering the doctrine requires a clear articulation of the two‑step test: (i) identify the legislative competence claimed; (ii) examine the substance and effect of the law to see if it pursues an ulterior, unconstitutional purpose. Linking this analysis to the basic structure doctrine strengthens the answer, demonstrating an integrated understanding of constitutional limits.

Examiner’s twist
A State passes the “Urban Water Conservation Act” imposing a levy on all households to fund a river‑link project that traverses multiple states. The levy is framed as a “tax on water usage”. The Union challenges the law on the ground of colourable legislation. Discuss the likely outcome, citing relevant doctrines and case law.

Step 1 – Identify the competence claimed: The State relies on its power to levy taxes on “water usage” under Entry 55 of the State List (water supply and irrigation).

Step 2 – Analyse the substance: The levy’s proceeds are earmarked for a river‑link project that is a “inter‑state water‑resource development” scheme, a matter in the Union List (Entry 56). The true purpose, therefore, is to finance a Union‑List activity.

Relevant jurisprudence: In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) the Court struck down a law that, while appearing to regulate a State matter, actually sought to control inter‑state trade – a classic colourable legislation case. Bihar State Electricity Board v. Kameshwar Singh (1971) similarly invalidated a tax that was a sub‑terfuge for regulating electricity generation (Concurrent List).

Application of the doctrine: The Act is colourable because its operative effect is to fund a Union‑List project, thereby encroaching upon the Centre’s exclusive jurisdiction. The Court would likely declare the levy unconstitutional.

Interaction with basic structure: The federal balance, a basic feature, is threatened when a State attempts to finance an inter‑state scheme without Centre’s consent. Upholding the doctrine preserves the basic structure.

Conclusion: The Act would be struck down as colourable legislation, with the Court emphasizing both the pith‑and‑substance test and the need to protect the federal character of the Constitution.

When confronted with a colourable legislation question, start with a one‑line statement of the doctrine, then quickly move to the two‑step test (competence vs. substance). Cite the leading case (Rajasthan 1977) and at least one supporting precedent. Finally, link the outcome to the basic structure (federal balance) to earn extra marks for integration.

Constitutional Morality: Conceptual Foundations, Judicial Articulation, and Its Role in Contemporary Governance

The term “constitutional morality” entered Indian jurisprudence with the Supreme Court’s judgment in Kesavananda Bharati (1973), where the Court observed that the Constitution is not merely a legal document but a moral compass guiding the polity. The concept was later crystallised in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015), wherein the Court held that “the Constitution is a living document and its spirit must be interpreted in accordance with constitutional morality”.

Key elements of constitutional morality include:

  1. Supremacy of constitutional values over majoritarian preferences – the Court has repeatedly emphasized that democratic majorities cannot override fundamental rights or secularism (e.g., Indra Sawhney v. Union of India, 1992, on reservation).
  2. Balancing individual liberty with collective welfare – the doctrine underpins the Court’s approach in cases like Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), where decriminalisation of consensual same‑sex relations was justified on the basis of dignity and equality, despite societal opposition.
  3. Dynamic interpretation – constitutional morality allows the judiciary to adapt the Constitution to evolving social realities without formal amendment, as seen in the recognition of the right to privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017) and the right to a clean environment (S. R. Batra, 2002).

Critics argue that invoking constitutional morality grants the judiciary a quasi‑legislative role, potentially upsetting the separation of powers. The Court, however, counters that it merely enforces the Constitution’s underlying ethos, a function mandated by Article 32 and the basic structure.

Contemporary relevance:

  • Reservation debates – the Supreme Court’s recent scrutiny of the “creamy layer” concept in OBC reservations reflects constitutional morality’s demand for substantive equality.
  • Freedom of expression vs. hate speech – in Shreya Singhal, the Court struck down Section 66A of the IT Act, balancing free speech with the need to curb communal incitement, guided by constitutional morality.
  • Environmental jurisprudence – the Court’s proactive stance in cases like M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (multiple decisions) treats ecological preservation as a constitutional duty, rooted in the moral dimension of the right to life (Art. 21).

For answer preparation, candidates should define constitutional morality, trace its doctrinal evolution, cite at least two landmark judgments, and discuss its practical implications in current policy debates. Highlighting both supportive and critical perspectives demonstrates analytical depth expected at the mains level.

Inter‑State Council: Genesis, Powers, and Its Role in Resolving Centre‑State Disputes

The Inter‑State Council (ISC) was envisaged in the Constitution under Article 263 as a mechanism to promote cooperation between the Union and the States. Although the Constitution provided for its creation, the Council remained dormant for decades, reflecting the early dominance of the Centre.

Historical milestones:

  • Constitutional provision (Article 263) – mandated the President to establish an ISC “to investigate and discuss disputes” and to make recommendations.
  • First establishment (1990) – the 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992) and the subsequent 1990 Presidential Order formally constituted the ISC, comprising the Prime Minister as Chair, Chief Ministers of all States, and Union Ministers of relevant portfolios.
  • Re‑constitution (2008) – the 91st Constitutional Amendment introduced the “Council of Ministers” and “Chief Ministers” as members, enhancing its representational balance.

Composition and powers:

  1. Chairperson – the Prime Minister.
  2. Members – Chief Ministers of all States, Union Ministers of Finance, Home, Law, and other relevant departments.
  3. Secretariat – the Ministry of Home Affairs.

The ISC’s functions include:

  • Discussing and recommending solutions to inter‑state water disputes, as seen in the Krishna River dispute (1999) where the Council facilitated a tripartite agreement.
  • Advising on fiscal devolution, leading to the Finance Commission’s recommendations on GST revenue sharing.
  • Addressing law‑and‑order issues that cross state boundaries, such as the coordination of anti‑terrorism operations under the National Investigation Agency.

While the Council’s recommendations are not binding, they carry significant persuasive authority. The Supreme Court has recognised the ISC as a “constitutional forum” for dialogue, as articulated in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2015), where the Court urged the Centre to consult the Council before imposing President’s Rule in a State.

Critiques focus on the Council’s limited enforceability and occasional politicisation. Nevertheless, the ISC remains a vital institutional bridge, especially in an era of cooperative federalism where fiscal and environmental challenges demand joint action.

  • Assuming any amendment with a special majority is automatically valid – the basic structure and colourable legislation tests can still invalidate it.
  • Treating the Finance Commission’s recommendations as legally binding – they are advisory; only Parliament can alter the revenue‑sharing formula.
  • Over‑stating the ISC’s authority – its recommendations are not enforceable; the Court may consider them but cannot compel compliance.
  • Equating “constitutional morality” with “political morality” – the former is rooted in constitutional values, not transient political considerations.
  • Neglecting the interplay between the GST Council and the Finance Commission – both affect fiscal federalism, and their roles must be distinguished.
  • Misapplying the colourable legislation test to pure policy choices – the doctrine targets jurisdictional overreach, not substantive policy disagreements.
