1. Historical Background and the Genesis of the Constituent Assembly

1.1 The Demand for a Constitution Framed by Indians

The idea that India’s political destiny should be determined by a body elected by Indians themselves evolved gradually over several decades of the nationalist movement. The earliest formal articulation came in 1922 when Mahatma Gandhi declared that Swaraj would not be a gift of the British Parliament but a declaration of India’s full self-expression through a Constituent Assembly. This demand was reiterated by the Indian National Congress at its Lahore session in 1929, which resolved that the constitution of free India must be framed, without outside interference, by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise. The British government, however, resisted this demand for a long time, arguing that the diverse communities of India could not agree on a single constitutional framework and that British parliamentary sovereignty was the only legitimate source of constitutional authority. The deadlock began to break only after the outbreak of the Second World War, when the British needed full Indian cooperation for the war effort. The August Offer of 1940 conceded, for the first time, that the framing of a new constitution should be primarily the responsibility of Indians themselves, though it fell short of accepting the specific mechanism of a Constituent Assembly elected by adult franchise.

1.2 The Cripps Mission and the Cabinet Mission Plan

The Cripps Mission of 1942 proposed that after the war, a constitution-making body would be elected by the provincial legislatures, but it retained the right of any province to opt out of the union, a provision that the Congress rejected as a recipe for Balkanisation. The final breakthrough came with the Cabinet Mission Plan of May 1946, which rejected the demand for a separate Pakistan but grouped provinces into three sections to accommodate Muslim League demands for autonomy. Crucially, the Plan provided for a Constituent Assembly of 389 members: 296 from British Indian provinces elected by the provincial legislative assemblies through a single transferable vote system of proportional representation, and 93 representatives nominated by the rulers of the Princely States. The elections held in July-August 1946 gave the Congress a massive majority, winning 208 seats, while the Muslim League secured 73 seats. The League, however, refused to join the Assembly, insisting on a separate Constituent Assembly for Pakistan, a stance that eventually led to the partition of the country under the Mountbatten Plan of June 1947 and the Indian Independence Act of 1947.

1.3 The Indian Independence Act and the Assembly’s Sovereign Status

The Indian Independence Act, 1947, passed by the British Parliament, transformed the Constituent Assembly into a fully sovereign body. It ceased to be a creation of the Cabinet Mission Plan and derived its authority directly from the will of the Indian people. The Act also relieved the Assembly of the limitations imposed by the Cabinet Mission, such as the grouping scheme and the requirement to adhere to the Mission’s recommendations. With the partition, the membership was reduced to 299 after the withdrawal of members from the territories that became Pakistan. The Assembly met for the first time on 9 December 1946, with Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha as the temporary Chairman, and elected Dr. Rajendra Prasad as its permanent President on 11 December 1946. From 15 August 1947 onwards, the Assembly functioned as the first Parliament of independent India (the Legislative Wing) and as the constitution-making body (the Constituent Wing), a dual role it performed until the Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949.

1.4 The Statutory Ancestry: From the Regulating Act to the Independence Act

The Constitution did not emerge from a vacuum; it inherited a long line of statutes through which the British Parliament progressively opened and then transferred power in India. The Regulating Act, 1773 was the first step: it brought the East India Company under a measure of parliamentary control and created the office of Governor-General of Bengal. Pitt's India Act, 1784 established a dual government by setting up a Board of Control in London alongside the Company's Court of Directors. The Charter Act of 1813 renewed the Company's charter and ended its commercial monopoly, and the Charter Act of 1833 centralised legislative power in a single Governor-General of India. The Government of India Act, 1858, passed after the revolt of 1857, transferred the government of India from the Company to the Crown, abolished the Board of Control and the Court of Directors, and created the office of Secretary of State for India. The Indian Councils Acts of 1861, 1892 and 1909, and the reforms of 1919 and 1935, then introduced representative and, eventually, responsible government.

Two statutes matter most for the Constitution's form. The Government of India Act, 1919, the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms, introduced dyarchy in the provinces and separated central and provincial subjects, an idea that reappears in the distribution of legislative powers. The Government of India Act, 1935 is the single largest source of the Constitution's text: the federal scheme, the office of Governor, the powers of the judiciary and much of the administrative structure were carried over almost verbatim. Finally, the Indian Independence Act, 1947 created the two independent Dominions, gave the Constituent Assembly sovereign power to frame a constitution, and left the existing machinery of government in place until a new constitution came into force. Reading the Constitution with this statutory ancestry in mind explains why so much of its machinery, from the federal structure to the office of the Governor, is continuous with the colonial state, even as its political purposes were transformed.

2. Composition, Committees, and the Drafting Process

2.1 Social and Political Composition of the Assembly

The Constituent Assembly was not elected on the basis of universal adult franchise, a fact often cited by critics to question its representativeness; however, the provincial legislatures that elected its members were themselves elected on a limited franchise based on property, tax, and educational qualifications covering roughly 28 percent of the adult population. Despite this limitation, the Assembly was remarkably diverse. It included representatives from all major religious communities except the Muslim League, which boycotted it, as well as members from the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, women like Sarojini Naidu, Hansa Mehta, and Durgabai Deshmukh, and eminent jurists and public figures such as B.R. Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, and N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar. The Congress Party dominated the proceedings, but it functioned as a broad umbrella accommodating diverse ideological strands from conservative to socialist, ensuring that the debates reflected a wide spectrum of Indian opinion. The Princely States, initially hesitant, gradually sent their representatives after the Mountbatten Plan, integrating the entire geographical entity into the constitutional framework.

2.2 The Committee System and the Drafting Committee

To manage the enormous task of drafting a constitution for a vast and complex nation, the Assembly constituted a network of committees. The most critical was the Drafting Committee, constituted on 29 August 1947 under the Chairmanship of Dr. B.R. Ambedkar. It comprised seven members: N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K.M. Munshi, Mohammad Saadullah, B.L. Mitter (later replaced by N. Madhava Rao), and D.P. Khaitan (later replaced by T.T. Krishnamachari). The Drafting Committee’s mandate was to scrutinise the reports of the various sectoral committees — such as the Union Powers Committee and the Union Constitution Committee chaired by Jawaharlal Nehru, the Provincial Constitution Committee chaired by Sardar Patel, and the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights and Minorities chaired by Sardar Patel — and prepare a cohesive draft. The Drafting Committee met for 141 days and prepared the first draft in February 1948, which was circulated for public comments, leading to a second draft in October 1948. The meticulous clause-by-clause consideration in the Assembly took 114 days out of the total 165 days of sessions, demonstrating the depth of deliberation.

2.3 The Objective Resolution and the Philosophical Anchor

Before the drafting began in earnest, Jawaharlal Nehru moved the historic Objectives Resolution on 13 December 1946, which was unanimously adopted on 22 January 1947. This resolution proclaimed India as an Independent Sovereign Republic and guaranteed its citizens justice, social, economic and political; equality of status and opportunity; and freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and action. It also pledged adequate safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed and other backward classes. The Objectives Resolution provided the philosophical spine of the Constitution; its language finds direct reflection in the Preamble and the Directive Principles of State Policy. It signaled a decisive break from the colonial Government of India Act, 1935, which was centred on imperial control, towards a transformative charter aimed at social and economic revolution.

Example Question: Assessing the Representative Character
"The Constituent Assembly was a one-party body in an essentially one-party country. The Assembly was the Congress and the Congress was India." Critically examine this statement by Granville Austin in the context of the composition and functioning of the Constituent Assembly.
Granville Austin’s observation captures the political reality of the Assembly’s dominance by the Indian National Congress, which held roughly 69 percent of the seats after partition. However, describing it merely as a "one-party body" obscures the internal pluralism within the Congress and the procedural safeguards that ensured broader consensus. First, the Congress was not a monolithic bloc; it housed conservatives like Rajendra Prasad and Purushottam Das Tandon, socialists like Jayaprakash Narayan (though not in the Assembly, their influence permeated), and liberals like Minoo Masani. Second, the Congress leadership deliberately nominated non-Congress experts to key positions: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, a political opponent of the Congress, was made Chairman of the Drafting Committee; Syama Prasad Mookerjee and B.N. Rau (Constitutional Advisor) represented diverse viewpoints. Third, the Assembly adopted decisions by consensus rather than simple majority voting on critical issues like Fundamental Rights, Federalism, and Language. The debates on the Uniform Civil Code, the status of Hindi, and the rights of minorities show vigorous dissent from members like K.T. Shah, H.V. Kamath, and Frank Anthony, whose amendments were debated seriously even when rejected. Therefore, while the Congress provided the political stability necessary for the Assembly to function, the output was a negotiated social contract, not a partisan manifesto.

3. Sources of the Constitution: Indigenous Roots and Foreign Borrowings

3.1 The Government of India Act, 1935: The Structural Backbone

The single largest source of the Indian Constitution is the Government of India Act, 1935. Approximately 250 provisions of the 1935 Act were reproduced, often verbatim, into the Constitution of 1950. This heavy reliance was pragmatic: the 1935 Act provided a ready-made, detailed administrative and federal machinery that had been tested for a decade. The federal scheme — the division of powers between the Centre and the Provinces via the Union, State, and Concurrent Lists (Seventh Schedule) — the establishment of the Federal Court (precursor to the Supreme Court), the office of the Governor, the Public Service Commissions, and the emergency provisions were all directly inherited. However, the Constituent Assembly made critical modifications. The 1935 Act vested residuary powers in the Governor-General; the Constitution vested them in Parliament (Article 248). The 1935 Act lacked a Bill of Rights; the Constitution inserted a justiciable Chapter on Fundamental Rights (Part III). The 1935 Act was an instrument of British control with 'safeguards' and 'special responsibilities' for the Governor-General; the Constitution replaced these with a responsible parliamentary executive accountable to the legislature.

3.2 Major Foreign Influences and Selective Adaptation

Beyond the 1935 Act, the framers consciously borrowed from other constitutions, but they did so selectively, adapting each feature to Indian conditions rather than transplanting them wholesale. From the British Constitution, they adopted the Parliamentary system of government, the Rule of Law, the institution of the Speaker, the concept of Single Citizenship, and the procedure of Law Making. However, they rejected the British principle of Parliamentary Sovereignty, substituting it with Constitutional Supremacy and Judicial Review. From the United States Constitution, they borrowed the Written Constitution, the Preamble, Fundamental Rights (Bill of Rights), the Federal structure with a strong Centre, Judicial Review and the independence of the Judiciary, the Impeachment of the President, and the removal of Supreme Court judges. The Irish Constitution inspired the Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), the method of election of the President, and the nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha by the President. The Canadian model influenced the quasi-federal structure — a federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, and the appointment of State Governors by the Centre. The Weimar Constitution of Germany provided the blueprint for the Emergency Provisions (Article 352, 356, 360) and the suspension of Fundamental Rights during Emergency. The Australian Constitution contributed the Concurrent List, the joint sitting of Parliament (Article 108), and the freedom of trade and commerce (Article 301). The South African Constitution influenced the procedure for amendment with a two-thirds majority and the election of members of the Rajya Sabha. The Japanese Constitution provided the concept of 'Procedure Established by Law' (Article 21), which the framers preferred over the American 'Due Process of Law' to limit judicial activism in economic legislation.

3.3 Indigenous Contributions: The Gandhian and Socialist Ethos

While the structural and legal architecture was largely borrowed, the soul of the Constitution reflects indigenous movements and philosophies. The Directive Principles in Articles 40 (Village Panchayats), 43 (Living Wage), 47 (Nutrition and Public Health), and 48 (Agriculture and Animal Husbandry) bear the imprint of Gandhian thought on village self-governance and trusteeship. The socialist influence, championed by Nehru and the Congress Socialist Party, is evident in Articles 38 (Social Order), 39 (Distribution of Resources, Equal Pay), and 41 (Right to Work), which mandate the State to strive for an egalitarian society. The abolition of Untouchability (Article 17) and the prohibition of forced labour (Article 23) were direct responses to the social reform movements led by reformers like Jyotiba Phule, Periyar, and Dr. Ambedkar. The Fifth and Sixth Schedules, providing for the administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas, were innovative indigenous mechanisms to protect tribal autonomy and culture, having no direct parallel in the borrowed models. Thus, the Constitution is a 'borrowed' document in form but an 'indigenous' document in spirit and transformative intent.

4. Salient Features of the Constitution

4.1 Lengthiest Written Constitution and the Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility

The Constitution of India is the lengthiest written constitution in the world, originally containing 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules (now expanded to over 470 Articles, 25 Parts, and 12 Schedules through amendments). This bulk is attributable to the incorporation of the administrative detail of the Government of India Act, 1935, the inclusion of justiciable Fundamental Rights and non-justiciable Directive Principles, elaborate provisions for Centre-State relations, special provisions for specific states (Articles 370, 371A-J), and detailed language provisions. Regarding amendability, the Constitution strikes a unique balance. It is neither rigid like the US Constitution (requiring supermajorities in Congress and ratification by three-fourths of states for every amendment) nor flexible like the UK Constitution (amendable by simple parliamentary majority). Article 368 provides three tiers: a simple majority of Parliament for certain provisions (e.g., admission of new states, creation of Legislative Councils); a special majority (two-thirds of members present and voting plus a majority of total membership) for most provisions; and the special majority plus ratification by half the State Legislatures for provisions affecting the federal structure (e.g., election of the President, extent of executive power, judiciary, distribution of legislative powers, Article 368 itself). This graded rigidity allows the Constitution to evolve with changing needs while protecting the federal compact.

4.2 Federal System with Unitary Bias (Quasi-Federalism)

The Constitution establishes a federal system with a "strong Centre," a phrase used by Dr. Ambedkar and later described by K.C. Wheare as "quasi-federal." It possesses all classic federal features: dual polity (Centre and States), written constitution, supremacy of the constitution, division of powers (Seventh Schedule), independent judiciary, and bicameralism. However, unitary features dominate in emergencies and even in normal times. The Centre holds residuary powers (unlike US/Australia where they rest with states). The Parliament can unilaterally alter state boundaries and names (Article 3) without state consent. The Governor, appointed by the President (Centre), acts as the Centre's agent and can reserve bills for Presidential assent. During a National Emergency (Article 352), the federal structure converts into a unitary one: Parliament gains power to legislate on State List subjects, the Centre can give directions to states on any matter, and financial autonomy of states is suspended. President's Rule (Article 356) allows the Centre to assume state government functions on the ground of constitutional breakdown. The All-India Services (IAS, IPS) recruited and controlled by the Centre but serving in states, and the integrated judiciary (High Courts under Supreme Court) further centralise control. This design was a conscious choice by the framers who, having witnessed partition and princely state integration, prioritised national unity and integrity over classical federal autonomy.

4.3 Parliamentary Form of Government

India adopted the British Westminster model of Parliamentary government both at the Centre and in the States, preferring it over the US Presidential system. The essence of this system is the fusion of the executive and the legislature: the Council of Ministers, headed by the Prime Minister (Chief Minister in states), is drawn from the legislature and is collectively responsible to the Lower House (Lok Sabha / Legislative Assembly). The President (Governor) is the nominal executive (head of state), while the Prime Minister (Chief Minister) is the real executive (head of government). The Constitution mandates that the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), a provision made binding by the 42nd and 44th Amendments. This system ensures continuous accountability through mechanisms like Question Hour, Zero Hour, No-Confidence Motions, and Parliamentary Committees. However, the Indian system differs from the British model in crucial respects: India has a written constitution limiting parliamentary sovereignty; the Prime Minister must be a member of Parliament (or get elected within six months), whereas the British PM must be an MP; India has a republican head of state (elected President) unlike the British monarchy; and the Indian Parliament is not supreme — its laws are subject to Judicial Review.

4.4 Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and Fundamental Duties

Part III (Articles 12-35) guarantees six categories of Fundamental Rights (originally seven, Right to Property was removed by the 44th Amendment in 1978 and made a legal right under Article 300A): Right to Equality (14-18), Right to Freedom (19-22), Right against Exploitation (23-24), Right to Freedom of Religion (25-28), Cultural and Educational Rights (29-30), and Right to Constitutional Remedies (32). These are justiciable, allowing citizens to approach the Supreme Court (Article 32) and High Courts (Article 226) for enforcement. The Supreme Court has described Article 32 as the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. Part IV (Articles 36-51) contains the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs), classified into Socialistic (Art 38, 39, 41, 42, 43), Gandhian (Art 40, 43B, 46, 47, 48), and Liberal-Intellectual (Art 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51). They are non-justiciable (Article 37) but "fundamental in the governance of the country." The 42nd Amendment (1976) added Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A) on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, listing ten duties (eleven after the 86th Amendment added duty to provide education to children aged 6-14). The interplay between Rights, Duties, and Directive Principles forms the constitutional trinity: Rights protect the individual against the State; DPSPs obligate the State to transform society; Duties remind the citizen that freedom entails responsibility.

4.5 Independent Judiciary and Judicial Review

The Constitution establishes an integrated and independent judiciary with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts at the state level, and subordinate courts below. Independence is secured through: security of tenure (removal only by impeachment for proved misbehaviour/incapacity requiring special majority in Parliament), salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund (not votable), prohibition on practice after retirement (Article 124(7)), and separation of judiciary from executive (Article 50). The power of Judicial Review — the authority to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive acts — is explicit in Articles 13, 32, 136, 226, and 227. The Supreme Court is the guardian of the Constitution and the protector of Fundamental Rights. The doctrine of Basic Structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) limits even Parliament's amending power, ensuring that the core identity of the Constitution — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and judicial review — cannot be destroyed by amendment. This makes the Indian judiciary one of the most powerful in the world, exercising not just review but also judicial activism through Public Interest Litigation (PIL) to enforce socio-economic rights.

4.6 Secularism, Universal Adult Franchise, and Single Citizenship

The Constitution declares India a Secular State (the word 'Secular' was inserted in the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, but the principle was inherent in Articles 25-28). Indian secularism is not a strict separation of religion and state (as in the US) but 'principled distance' — the state treats all religions equally, regulates secular aspects of religious practice, and intervenes for social reform (e.g., abolition of untouchability, reform of Hindu personal law). Universal Adult Franchise (Article 326) was a revolutionary leap for a largely illiterate, poor, and socially stratified society in 1950. It rejected property, tax, and literacy qualifications, entrusting political sovereignty to every adult citizen (age reduced from 21 to 18 by the 61st Amendment, 1988). This single act transformed subjects into citizens and legitimised the democratic project. Single Citizenship (Articles 5-11) means all Indians are citizens of India alone, not of individual states (unlike the US dual citizenship). This promotes national unity and ensures equal rights across the territory, preventing state-level discrimination against non-domiciles.

5. The Preamble: Text, Evolution, and Constitutional Significance

5.1 The Text and Its Philosophical Architecture

The Preamble serves as the introduction and the philosophical key to the Constitution. It reads: "WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION." The Preamble performs four functions: it indicates the source of authority ("We, the People of India"), it states the nature of the polity (Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic), it enumerates the objectives (Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity), and it records the date of adoption (26 November 1949). The phrase "We, the People" asserts popular sovereignty, distinguishing the Constitution from the Government of India Act, 1935, which derived authority from the British Crown. The objectives are arranged in a logical hierarchy: Justice (the goal), Liberty and Equality (the means/conditions), and Fraternity (the social cement ensuring unity).

5.2 The 42nd Amendment: Insertion of 'Socialist', 'Secular', and 'Integrity'

The original Preamble of 1949 described India as a "Sovereign Democratic Republic." The words "Socialist" and "Secular" were inserted between "Sovereign" and "Democratic" by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, during the Emergency. The word "Integrity" was added to "Unity of the Nation." The government argued that these words merely made explicit what was already implicit in the Constitution. "Socialist" reflected the Directive Principles (Articles 38, 39) aiming at a welfare state and equitable distribution of resources, though the Indian model accepts a mixed economy rather than state ownership of all means of production. "Secular" codified the principle of state neutrality and equal treatment of all religions embedded in Articles 25-28. "Integrity" reinforced the territorial unity of the nation, addressing secessionist tendencies. The 44th Amendment (1978) attempted to define "Socialist" as "elimination of inequality in income and status and standards of life" but the definition was dropped in the Rajya Sabha; thus, the term remains undefined in the Constitution, allowing flexible interpretation ranging from democratic socialism to welfare capitalism.

5.3 Legal Status: Is the Preamble a Part of the Constitution?

The legal status of the Preamble has been a subject of judicial evolution. In the Berubari Union Case (1960), the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is not a part of the Constitution and is not a source of substantive powers or limitations; it serves only as a key to interpret ambiguous provisions. However, in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), a 13-judge bench overruled Berubari and held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution. The Court ruled that the Preamble can be amended under Article 368, but the "Basic Structure" — the essential features described in the Preamble (Sovereignty, Democracy, Secularism, Republic, Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity) — cannot be altered. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court used the Preamble's "Secular" character to strike down the dismissal of state governments on political grounds, establishing secularism as a basic feature. In Union Government v. LIC of India (1995), the Court reiterated that the Preamble is a part of the Constitution but is not directly enforceable in a court of law (non-justiciable); it serves as a guiding star for interpretation of statutes and constitutional provisions.

5.4 The Preamble as an Interpretive Tool and the 'Basic Structure'

The Preamble acts as the "horoscope" of the Constitution (a phrase attributed to K.M. Munshi). When the language of an Article is ambiguous, the Court interprets it in a manner that advances the objectives of the Preamble. For instance, the expansion of Article 21 (Right to Life) to include the right to livelihood, health, education, shelter, and privacy is rooted in the Preamble's promise of "Justice, social, economic and political" and "Dignity of the individual." The Preamble also resolves conflicts between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Court held that the harmony between Part III and Part IV is an essential feature of the Basic Structure, derived from the Preamble's vision of a socio-economic revolution.

The Basic Structure Doctrine: Evolution, Tests, and Contemporary Application

The Basic Structure doctrine is the judicial innovation that prevents Parliament from altering the Constitution’s core identity through amendment. It originated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where a 13‑judge bench held by a 7‑6 majority that while Article 368 confers wide amending power, it does not enable the destruction of the Constitution’s “basic features.” The Court did not furnish an exhaustive list but identified sovereignty, democracy, secularism, republicanism, judicial review, federalism, and the separation of powers as illustrative elements.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the test. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) the Court applied the doctrine to strike down the 39th Amendment’s clause that insulated the Prime Minister’s election from judicial scrutiny, affirming that free and fair elections constitute a basic feature. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) added the harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles and the limited nature of the amending power itself. Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) introduced the “date of judgment” rule: laws placed in the Ninth Schedule before 24 April 1973 are immune, but those inserted thereafter are subject to basic‑structure review. The I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) decision cemented this principle, holding that any Ninth Schedule law violating fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure is open to challenge.

The doctrine operates through a two‑stage inquiry. First, the Court asks whether the impugned amendment or law affects a feature that is “essential” to the Constitution’s identity. Second, it examines whether the alteration destroys the feature’s essential character rather than merely modifying its operation. This “width and identity” test distinguishes permissible reforms (e.g., the 73rd and 74th Amendments strengthening local self‑government) from impermissible ones (e.g., the 42nd Amendment’s attempt to curtail judicial review). The doctrine thus functions as a constitutional “guardrail,” ensuring that the constituent power remains a power of amendment, not a power of repeal.

Amendment Procedure under Article 368: Scope, Limitations, and the Doctrine of Constitutional Immutability

Article 368 prescribes three tiers of amendment. The first tier requires a simple majority of Parliament for matters such as the creation of new states (Article 3) or the alteration of legislative council composition (Article 169). The second tier demands a special majority — a majority of the total membership of each House and a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting — for most substantive changes, including Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and the structure of the Union and States. The third tier adds ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures for amendments affecting the federal architecture: the election of the President (Article 54), the extent of executive power (Articles 73, 162), the distribution of legislative powers (Seventh Schedule), representation of States in Parliament, and Article 368 itself.

The procedural rigidity is intentional. The Constituent Assembly debates reveal a deliberate choice to avoid the British model of parliamentary sovereignty and the American model of near‑impossibility of amendment. The “basic structure” limitation, however, imposes a substantive ceiling beyond procedural compliance. In Kesavananda the Court held that even a duly passed amendment under Article 368 can be invalidated if it damages the basic structure. This creates a dual constraint: procedural (majority, ratification) and substantive (basic structure). The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to oust judicial review of amendments by inserting clauses 4 and 5 in Article 368, declaring that no amendment shall be called in question and that there shall be no limitation on constituent power. The Supreme Court struck down these clauses in Minerva Mills, reaffirming that the power of amendment is not a power to abrogate the Constitution.

Practical implications are significant. The 99th Amendment (2014) establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission was passed with the requisite special majority and state ratification, yet the Court invalidated it in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for violating judicial independence — a basic feature. Conversely, the 101st Amendment (2016) introducing the Goods and Services Tax cleared all procedural hurdles and survived basic‑structure scrutiny because it altered fiscal federalism without destroying the federal principle itself. Aspirants must distinguish between “amendments that alter the Constitution” and “amendments that replace the Constitution,” a line the Court draws by examining the amendment’s effect on the Constitution’s identity.

Worked Illustration
A Bill seeks to amend Article 368 to remove the requirement of state ratification for any future amendment. The Bill is passed by the required special majority in both Houses but is not sent to the States. Is the amendment constitutionally valid?
No. Article 368 itself is a provision whose amendment falls under the third tier requiring ratification by half the States. Moreover, the basic structure doctrine treats the federal distribution of amendment power as a core feature. Removing state ratification would alter the federal character of the amending process, thereby violating the basic structure as held in Kesavananda and Minerva Mills. The amendment would be struck down on both procedural and substantive grounds.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks whether a particular amendment is valid, first identify the tier of Article 368 it belongs to. Check if the procedural requirements (majority, ratification) are satisfied. Then ask whether the amendment touches a basic feature — sovereignty, democracy, secularism, judicial review, federalism, separation of powers, or the limited amending power itself. If it does, the amendment is vulnerable regardless of procedural compliance. Memorise the list of provisions requiring state ratification (Article 368 proviso) and the illustrative basic features from Kesavananda and Minerva Mills.

Federal Dynamics: Distribution of Legislative Powers, Residuary Authority, and the Role of the Inter‑State Council

The Seventh Schedule allocates legislative subjects between the Union List (List I, 97 entries), the State List (List II, 66 entries), and the Concurrent List (List III, 47 entries). The Union enjoys exclusive power over List I; States have exclusive power over List II subject to Union law on Concurrent subjects prevailing in case of conflict (Article 254). Residuary powers — subjects not enumerated in any list — vest in Parliament under Article 248 read with Entry 97 of List I, a reversal of the American model where residuary powers rest with the States. This centralising tilt is a hallmark of “quasi‑federalism.”

Judicial interpretation has shaped the practical operation of these lists. The “pith and substance” doctrine determines the true nature of a law when its incidental encroachment on another list is challenged. In State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) the Court upheld a State prohibition law despite its incidental effect on import‑export (a Union subject) because its pith and substance fell under State List Entry 8 (intoxicating liquors). The “colourable legislation” doctrine prevents a legislature from indirectly legislating on a subject outside its competence by disguising the law’s true purpose.

Article 263 provides for an Inter‑State Council to inquire into and advise on disputes between States, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination. The Council was first constituted in 1990 on the Sarkaria Commission’s recommendation. Its composition — Prime Minister as Chair, Chief Ministers of all States, and six Union Cabinet Ministers — gives it a high‑level political character. While its recommendations are advisory, the Council has facilitated consensus on issues such as the implementation of the Goods and Services Tax, the sharing of river waters, and the management of internal security. The Council’s effectiveness depends on political will; it remains a consultative rather than a decision‑making body.

Recent tensions illustrate the federal dynamic. The 101st Amendment (GST) required a constitutional amendment and a GST Council (Article 279A) that operates on a weighted voting formula, giving the Union a de facto veto. The 2020 Farm Acts (later repealed) were enacted under Entry 33 of the Concurrent List (trade and commerce) but were perceived as encroaching on State List Entry 14 (agriculture), triggering widespread State opposition. The Supreme Court’s reference in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) affirmed that the Union cannot use its residuary power to legislate on matters that are essentially State subjects, reinforcing the principle that the distribution of powers is not a mere textual exercise but a structural guarantee.

Emergency Provisions: Constitutional Safeguards, Judicial Scrutiny, and the 44th Amendment Reforms

The Constitution envisages three types of emergency: National Emergency (Article 352), State Emergency (President’s Rule, Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360). Each carries distinct triggers, parliamentary approval thresholds, and effects on fundamental rights and federal distribution.

National Emergency can be proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) replaced “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion,” raised the parliamentary approval requirement to a special majority (two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House), and mandated that the proclamation be laid before each House within one month. It also restricted the suspension of Fundamental Rights: Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended, and Article 19 can be suspended only when the emergency is proclaimed on grounds of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the proclamation of President’s Rule under Article 356 is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, irrelevance, or unconstitutionality, and that the dissolution of a State Legislative Assembly cannot precede parliamentary approval.

State Emergency (President’s Rule) requires the President’s satisfaction that the State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The 44th Amendment added a proviso that the proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months and cannot remain in force beyond three years without repeated parliamentary approval every six months. The Court in Bommai established that the Governor’s report must be based on objective material, and the imposition of President’s Rule for political reasons violates the basic structure (federalism and democracy).

Financial Emergency (Article 360) has never been proclaimed. It empowers the President to direct States to observe canons of financial propriety and to reduce salaries of Union and State officials, including judges. Parliamentary approval follows the same special‑majority rule as National Emergency. The provision remains a dormant but potent instrument, reflecting the Constitution’s centralising bias in fiscal matters.

Constitutional Morality and the Transformative Vision of the Constitution

Constitutional morality refers to the adherence to the Constitution’s core values — justice, liberty, equality, fraternity — beyond mere textual compliance. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, in the Constituent Assembly, warned that constitutional morality is not a natural sentiment but must be cultivated. The Supreme Court has invoked the concept to strike down practices that, while not explicitly prohibited, betray the Constitution’s transformative promise. In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), the Court decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, holding that constitutional morality demands the protection of dignity and equality for sexual minorities, overriding majoritarian moral disapproval. In Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (Sabarimala, 2018), the Court ruled that the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the temple violated constitutional morality grounded in Articles 14, 15, 17, and 25.

The doctrine operates as an interpretive lens: when a statutory provision or executive action is ambiguous, the Court prefers the reading that advances constitutional morality. It also serves as a check on majoritarian legislation that may satisfy procedural validity but undermine the Constitution’s egalitarian ethos. Critics argue that the concept grants judges excessive discretion; proponents contend it is essential for a Constitution designed to transform a hierarchical society into a democratic republic. For examination purposes, constitutional morality should be linked to the Preamble’s objectives, the basic structure doctrine, and the transformative jurisprudence of Articles 14, 15, 17, 21, and 25.

  • Confusing the three tiers of amendment under Article 368 — especially forgetting that alteration of Article 368 itself requires state ratification.
  • Assuming that a duly passed amendment is immune from judicial review; the basic structure doctrine applies irrespective of procedural perfection.
  • Misidentifying the residuary power as belonging to the States; it vests in Parliament under Article 248 and Entry 97 of List I.
  • Treating the Inter‑State Council as a binding decision‑making body; its role is advisory under Article 263.
  • Believing that Fundamental Rights can be suspended in any National Emergency; Articles 20 and 21 are non‑suspendable, and Article 19 only during war or external aggression.
  • Overlooking the 44th Amendment’s replacement of “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion” as the ground for National Emergency.
  • Equating constitutional morality with popular morality; the former is anchored in the Constitution’s transformative values, not majoritarian sentiment.

Doctrine of Eclipse, Severability, and Waiver in Fundamental Rights Jurisprudence

Article 13 of the Constitution establishes the supremacy of fundamental rights over pre‑constitutional and post‑constitutional laws. Three judicial doctrines — eclipse, severability, and waiver — operationalise this supremacy. The doctrine of eclipse holds that a pre‑constitutional statute inconsistent with a fundamental right does not become void ab initio; it remains dormant, “eclipsed”, until the constitutional impediment is removed. In Bhikaji Narain v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) the Supreme Court ruled that the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1947, which conflicted with Article 19(1)(g), was merely eclipsed and revived when the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, amended Article 19(6) to permit the restriction.

The doctrine of severability (or separability) applies when only a part of a statute violates a fundamental right. The offending provision is severed, leaving the remainder intact, provided the valid portion is capable of independent operation and the legislature would have enacted it alone. In State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills Ltd. (1974) the Court struck down the offending section of the Bombay Labour Welfare Fund Act while preserving the rest. The test requires (i) the invalid part to be distinct, (ii) the remaining statute to be workable, and (iii) no legislative intent to enact the whole as an inseparable scheme.

The doctrine of waiver asserts that a person cannot voluntarily relinquish a fundamental right. In Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax (1959) the Court held that fundamental rights are conferred in the public interest and are not subject to private waiver. Subsequent jurisprudence (e.g., Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985) reiterated that any purported waiver must be informed, voluntary, and not contrary to public policy; however, the Court has consistently refused to treat waiver as a valid defence against infringement of Part III rights.

These doctrines interact: a law may be partially eclipsed, partially severed, and the individual cannot waive the right that the law infringes. For examination purposes, remember that eclipse applies only to pre‑constitutional laws, severability to both pre‑ and post‑constitutional statutes, and waiver is universally rejected for fundamental rights.

Discretionary Powers of the President and Governors: Ordinances, Pardons, and Judicial Review

The Constitution vests the President (Article 123) and Governors (Article 213) with ordinance‑making power to meet urgent legislative needs when Parliament or the State Legislature is not in session. An ordinance has the same force as an Act but must be laid before the legislature within six weeks of its reassembly; otherwise it lapses. The Supreme Court in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) held that the President’s satisfaction is justiciable — ordinances cannot be used to bypass legislative debate on matters of policy. In Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) the Court reiterated that re‑promulgation of ordinances without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution.

The pardoning power (Article 72 for the President, Article 161 for Governors) includes pardon, commutation, remission, respite, and reprieve. Judicial review of this power is limited but not absent. In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) the Court ruled that the exercise must be on the advice of the Council of Ministers and cannot be arbitrary. Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) established that the Court may intervene if the order is mala fide, based on irrelevant considerations, or suffers from non‑application of mind.

Beyond ordinances and pardons, the President and Governors exercise discretionary functions in government formation. The President appoints the Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority (e.g., 1996, 1998). The Governor’s discretion under Article 163(1) to act without ministerial advice is confined to situations where the Constitution expressly requires it — such as reservation of bills for Presidential assent (Article 200) or reporting failure of constitutional machinery (Article 356). The S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) judgment subjected the Governor’s report to judicial scrutiny, mandating objective material and prohibiting partisan use.

Key distinctions for the aspirant: (i) Ordinances are legislative substitutes, subject to legislative ratification; (ii) Pardons are executive clemency, reviewable only on narrow grounds; (iii) Discretionary appointments are political but bounded by constitutional conventions and judicial oversight.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “The President’s ordinance‑making power under Article 123 is not subject to judicial review because it is a political discretion.” Choose the correct response.
The statement is false. In R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) and Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) the Supreme Court held that the President’s satisfaction is justiciable; an ordinance promulgated mala fide or to circumvent legislative scrutiny can be struck down. The power is constitutional, not purely political.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mixes ordinance, pardon, and discretionary appointment, isolate the constitutional article first (123, 72, 75/164). Then recall the leading case that defines the scope of judicial review for that article. Write the article number, the case name, and the principle in one sentence — this fetches full marks for “case law” components.

Fiscal Federalism: Finance Commission, GST Council, and the Distribution of Financial Resources

Fiscal federalism in India is structured around two constitutional bodies: the Finance Commission (Article 280) and the Goods and Services Tax Council (Article 279A, inserted by the 101st Amendment, 2016). The Finance Commission is constituted every five years (or earlier) by the President. It recommends (a) the vertical division of net proceeds of Union taxes between the Centre and the States, (b) the horizontal distribution among States based on criteria such as population, area, forest cover, income distance, and demographic performance, and (c) measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of States for Panchayats and Municipalities. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑2025) introduced a 12.5 % weight for demographic performance and a 15 % weight for forest and ecology, reflecting a shift toward incentive‑based devolution.

The GST Council embodies cooperative federalism in indirect taxation. It comprises the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and one Minister from each State. Decisions require a three‑fourths majority of weighted votes: the Centre holds one‑third of the total weight, States collectively hold two‑thirds. The Council recommends rates, exemptions, threshold limits, and dispute‑resolution mechanisms. Its recommendations are binding on both Union and States, but the Supreme Court in Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022) clarified that the Council’s recommendations are not “law” under Article 141; they acquire legal force only when enacted by Parliament or State Legislatures.

Vertical devolution under the Finance Commission determines the share of the divisible pool (corporation tax, income tax, customs, excise, GST compensation cess) that flows to States. Horizontal devolution uses a formula; the 15th FC assigned 45 % weight to income distance, 15 % to population (2011), 15 % to area, 10 % to forest and ecology, 10 % to demographic performance, and 5 % to tax effort. The GST compensation cess, introduced for five years (2017‑2022), was extended by the 2022 amendment to meet revenue shortfalls, illustrating the dynamic interplay between the two bodies.

For the aspirant, remember: (i) Finance Commission = tax‑sharing (vertical/horizontal) and grants; (ii) GST Council = rate‑setting and policy for a single indirect tax; (iii) Both are constitutional, but only the Finance Commission’s recommendations are traditionally accepted by the Executive, while GST Council recommendations require legislative enactment.

Constitutional Position of Union Territories and the National Capital Territory of Delhi

Union Territories (UTs) are administered by the President through an Administrator (Lieutenant Governor, Chief Commissioner, or Administrator) under Article 239. Parliament may create a Legislature or Council of Ministers for a UT (Article 239A). The National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi enjoys a special status under Article 239AA, inserted by the 69th Amendment (1991). It provides for a Legislative Assembly with powers over State List and Concurrent List matters (except public order, police, and land), a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister, and a Lieutenant Governor (LG) who acts on the aid and advice of the Council except in matters where the Constitution requires his discretion.

The Supreme Court’s landmark judgment in Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) held that the LG’s concurrence is not required on all matters; the LG must refer differences to the President only on issues outside the Assembly’s legislative competence. The 2023 Constitution Bench reiterated that the LG’s discretionary power is confined to the three excluded subjects and that the elected government possesses “representative governance” mandate. The Court also clarified that the LG cannot stall routine governance by withholding files indefinitely.

Puducherry (Article 239A) and Jammu & Kashmir (post‑2019 reorganisation) have similar but distinct frameworks. Puducherry’s Assembly has powers over State List and Concurrent List, but the LG’s discretion is broader. The 2019 Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act created two UTs — Jammu & Kashmir (with legislature) and Ladakh (without legislature) — under Articles 239A and 239 respectively.

Key examination points: (i) Article 239 — general UT administration; (ii) Article 239A — Parliament may create legislature for any UT; (iii) Article 239AA — Delhi’s unique “asymmetric federal” design; (iv) LG’s discretion limited to three subjects; (v) Judicial review ensures the LG does not become a parallel executive.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse (pre‑constitutional laws only) with severability (both pre‑ and post‑constitutional).
  • Assuming the President’s ordinance‑making power is immune from judicial review; it is justiciable on mala fide or constitutional fraud grounds.
  • Treating the Governor’s pardoning power as absolute; it is reviewable for non‑application of mind or irrelevant considerations.
  • Believing the Finance Commission’s recommendations are legally binding; they are advisory, though conventionally accepted.
  • Thinking the GST Council’s recommendations automatically become law; they require legislative enactment by Parliament or State Legislatures.
  • Equating the LG of Delhi with a Governor; the LG’s discretion is constitutionally circumscribed to three subjects only.
  • Overlooking that the 101st Amendment inserted Article 279A, making the GST Council a constitutional body, not a statutory one.
