1. Constitutional Basis and Composition of Parliament

1.1 Constitutional Provisions

The Constitution of India establishes Parliament as the supreme legislative authority under Article 79, which declares that there shall be a Parliament for the Union consisting of the President and two Houses, namely the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha). Article 80 defines the composition of the Rajya Sabha, providing for a maximum of two hundred and fifty members, of whom twelve are nominated by the President for their expertise in literature, science, art and social service, while the remaining members are elected by the legislative assemblies of the states and union territories through proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote. Article 81 fixes the maximum strength of the Lok Sabha at five hundred and fifty‑two members, of whom not more than five hundred and thirty represent the states, not more than twenty represent the union territories, and two may be nominated by the President from the Anglo‑Indian community if the President is of the opinion that the community is not adequately represented. These provisions together create a bicameral legislature that balances representation of the people with representation of the federal units.

1.2 Lok Sabha Composition

The Lok Sabha, or House of the People, is directly elected on the basis of adult suffrage, making it the primary embodiment of popular sovereignty. Each state is allocated seats in proportion to its population as ascertained by the latest census, subject to the overall ceiling of five hundred and thirty seats for the states. The delimitation of constituencies is carried out by the Delimitation Commission constituted under the Delimitation Act, 2002, which ensures that each constituency has roughly equal population, thereby upholding the principle of one person one vote. The term of the Lok Sabha is five years from the date appointed for its first meeting unless dissolved earlier by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The Speaker, elected from among its members, presides over its proceedings and enjoys a casting vote in case of a tie, a feature that underscores the House’s role in ensuring legislative continuity.

1.3 Rajya Sabha Composition

The Rajya Sabha, or Council of States, represents the federal character of the Union by giving each state a voice proportionate to its population but with a minimum of one seat, ensuring that smaller states are not marginalised. Members are elected by the elected members of the state legislative assemblies using the single transferable vote system, which tends to produce a more consensual and less majoritarian outcome than the first‑past‑the‑post method used for the Lok Sabha. One‑third of its members retire every two years, giving the House a permanent character and preventing a complete turnover at any single election. The Vice‑President of India serves as the ex‑officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, presiding over its sessions and exercising a casting vote only when the House is equally divided, a role that mirrors the Speaker’s function in the lower House.

1.4 President’s Role in Parliament

Although the President is not a member of either House, the Constitution makes the President an integral part of Parliament under Article 79. The President summons and prorogues the Houses, addresses both Houses at the commencement of the first session after each general election and at the first session of each year, and may send messages to either House concerning any pending bill or other matter. The President’s assent is required for a bill to become law; however, the President may return a non‑money bill for reconsideration, and if the bill is passed again by both Houses with or without amendments, the President must give assent. In the case of a money bill, the President cannot return it for reconsideration, reflecting the special constitutional status of financial legislation. The President also has the power to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session, subject to parliamentary approval within six weeks of reassembly, a mechanism that enables executive action in urgent circumstances while preserving legislative supremacy.

1.5 Duration, Sessions, Quorum and Presiding Officers

Article 83 fixes the life of the two Houses: the Council of States is a permanent body of which one-third of the members retire every second year, while the House of the People has a term of five years from the date appointed for its first sitting, which may be extended by one year at a time during a national emergency. Article 85 requires the President to summon each House at such intervals that six months do not intervene between its last sitting in one session and the date appointed for its first sitting in the next, and empowers the President to prorogue a House and to dissolve the House of the People. A session runs from summoning to prorogation and may contain many sittings and adjournments; prorogation ends a session, while dissolution ends the life of the House of the People and is followed by a general election. Quorum under Article 100 is one-tenth of the total number of members of the House, and all questions are decided by a majority of the members present and voting, with the presiding officer exercising a casting vote only in the case of an equality of votes.

Article 93 provides for the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker of the House of the People, and Article 89 for the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Council of States. The Speaker is the guardian of the House's privileges, controls its procedure and debates, decides points of order and, under the Tenth Schedule, decides questions of disqualification on grounds of defection. The Speaker does not vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote. The office is expected to be politically impartial even though the holder is elected by the House from among its members. Procedure inside the House is organised around tools of accountability: the Question Hour, the first hour of a sitting, is devoted to questions to Ministers, while Zero Hour is the period immediately after Question Hour in which members raise urgent matters without prior notice. Financial oversight runs through the Public Accounts Committee and the Departmentally Related Standing Committees, which examine the expenditure and administration of particular ministries. The Speaker's rulings on the admission of adjournment motions, and the Speaker's certification of a Bill as a Money Bill, have repeatedly been the subject of litigation.

2. Legislative Process and Money Bills

2.1 Ordinary Bill Passage

An ordinary bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament, except that a bill concerning the creation of new states or alteration of boundaries must be introduced in the Lok Sabha on the recommendation of the President. After introduction, the bill undergoes a first reading where its title and objectives are read out, followed by a second reading that comprises a general discussion on the principles of the bill, a clause‑by‑clause consideration, and voting on each clause. Amendments may be moved during the clause‑by‑clause stage, and the bill is then subjected to a third reading where only formal, verbal or consequential amendments are permitted before the final vote. If the bill is passed by the originating House, it is transmitted to the other House, which follows the same three‑reading procedure. Should the second House amend the bill, it returns to the first House for concurrence; if disagreement persists, the President may summon a joint sitting of both Houses under Article 108, where the bill is decided by a simple majority of the total number of members present and voting, thereby resolving deadlocks between the two chambers.

2.2 Money Bill Definition and Procedure

Article 110 defines a money bill as a bill that contains only provisions dealing with the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax; the regulation of the borrowing of money or the giving of any guarantee by the Government of India; the custody of the Consolidated Fund or the Contingency Fund of India; the appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund; the declaring of any expenditure to be expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund; the receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or the public account of India; or any matter incidental to any of the matters specified above. A money bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha and only on the recommendation of the President. After it is passed by the Lok Sabha, it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha, which may not amend it but may recommend amendments within fourteen days. The Lok Sabha may either accept or reject the recommendations; in either case the bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form finally passed by the Lok Sabha. The President’s assent is then mandatory, and the President cannot return a money bill for reconsideration, underscoring the primacy of the directly elected House in financial matters.

2.3 Financial Bills and Distinction

Financial bills are of two kinds: those that contain provisions of a money bill together with other matters (Article 117(1)) and those that involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund but do not fall within the strict definition of a money bill (Article 117(3)). The first category follows the same procedure as a money bill, except that the Rajya Sabha may amend it and the President may return it for reconsideration. The second category can be introduced in either House, but the Rajya Sabha has full power to amend or reject it, and the President may return it for reconsideration. This distinction ensures that while the Lok Sabha retains control over taxation and appropriation, the Rajya Sabha can still influence legislation that has financial implications but is not purely a money bill, preserving the bicameral balance.

2.4 Joint Sitting Provision

Article 108 provides for a joint sitting of the two Houses to resolve a deadlock over an ordinary bill when the bill has been passed by one House and rejected by the other, or when the Houses have finally disagreed on the amendments to be made, or when more than six months have elapsed since the bill was received by the other House without it being passed. The joint sitting is summoned by the President and presided over by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, or in the Speaker’s absence by the Deputy Speaker, or if both are absent, by the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The quorum for a joint sitting is one‑tenth of the total number of members of both Houses. Decisions are taken by a simple majority of members present and voting, which, given the larger numerical strength of the Lok Sabha, effectively gives the lower House a decisive advantage in joint sittings. This mechanism is a safety valve that prevents legislative paralysis while respecting the federal principle embodied in the Rajya Sabha.

Example Question
Explain the constitutional distinction between a Money Bill and a Financial Bill under Article 110 and Article 117 of the Constitution of India. Illustrate with one example each of a Money Bill and a Financial Bill that is not a Money Bill.
A Money Bill, as defined in Article 110, contains exclusively provisions relating to taxation, borrowing, custody of the Consolidated Fund, appropriation, declaration of charged expenditure, receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or public account, or matters incidental thereto. It can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha on the President’s recommendation, the Rajya Sabha may only recommend amendments within fourteen days, and the President cannot return it for reconsideration. An example is the Finance Bill, 2023, which solely deals with tax proposals and appropriation. A Financial Bill under Article 117(1) includes Money Bill provisions together with other legislative matters; it follows the Money Bill procedure but the Rajya Sabha may amend it and the President may return it. An example is a bill that proposes a new tax and also creates a regulatory authority for its administration. A Financial Bill under Article 117(3) involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund but does not fall within the Money Bill definition; it can be introduced in either House, the Rajya Sabha has full amendment powers, and the President may return it. An example is a bill authorising expenditure for a new infrastructure project without any tax provision.

3. Parliamentary Committees

3.1 Standing Committees

Standing committees are permanent bodies constituted each year or for the duration of the Lok Sabha, tasked with detailed examination of bills, budgets, and policy implementation. The most prominent among them are the Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), each shadowing a specific ministry or group of ministries, and the Financial Committees — the Public Accounts Committee (PAC), the Committee on Estimates, and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). These committees operate on a non‑partisan basis, with membership reflecting the proportional strength of parties in the House, and they derive their authority from the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha. Their reports, though not binding, carry significant moral and political weight, often prompting executive action or legislative amendment. The committee system thus extends parliamentary scrutiny beyond the limited time available on the floor of the House, enabling expert examination and stakeholder consultation.

3.2 Ad Hoc Committees

Ad hoc committees are constituted for a specific purpose and cease to exist once they submit their report. They include select committees and joint committees formed to examine a particular bill in detail, as well as inquiry committees appointed to investigate specific issues such as the 2G spectrum allocation or the coal block allocation. The Speaker or Chairman, as the case may be, appoints members in consultation with party leaders, ensuring representation of major political formations. Because they are time‑bound and focused, ad hoc committees can undertake intensive fact‑finding, summon witnesses, and call for documents, thereby supplementing the work of standing committees. Their reports often become the basis for legislative amendments or policy reforms, illustrating the flexibility of the committee mechanism to respond to emerging governance challenges.

3.3 Committee on Estimates and Public Accounts Committee

The Committee on Estimates examines the estimates of expenditure included in the budget and suggests economies, improvements in organisation, efficiency, or administrative reform consistent with the policy underlying the estimates. It does not, however, question the policy approved by Parliament. The Public Accounts Committee, on the other hand, scrutinises the appropriation accounts and the audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) to ensure that money granted by Parliament has been spent within the scope of the demand and for the intended purpose. The PAC is traditionally chaired by a member of the opposition, a convention that enhances its credibility as a watchdog. Both committees rely heavily on the CAG’s reports, and their deliberations often reveal systemic weaknesses in financial management, prompting corrective executive action.

3.4 Committee on Subordinate Legislation

The Committee on Subordinate Legislation examines whether the powers to make regulations, rules, sub‑rules, bye‑laws, and other statutory instruments conferred by the Constitution or delegated by Parliament are being properly exercised within the scope of the delegation. It reviews each statutory instrument laid before Parliament, assesses its conformity with the parent Act, and reports any excess, inconsistency, or lack of clarity. This committee acts as a safeguard against executive overreach through delegated legislation, ensuring that the principle of parliamentary supremacy is not eroded by the proliferation of subordinate rules. Its work is especially critical in areas such as environmental regulation, labour law, and financial sector oversight, where detailed technical rules are framed by ministries under broad legislative mandates.

4. Parliamentary Privileges and Immunities

4.1 Constitutional Basis of Privileges

Article 105 of the Constitution confers on each House of Parliament, its members and committees, certain powers, privileges and immunities that are necessary for the independent and effective functioning of the legislature. These include freedom of speech in Parliament, immunity from legal proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given in Parliament or its committees, and exemption from liability for the publication of reports, papers, votes or proceedings by or under the authority of either House. The article also empowers Parliament to define its privileges by law; until such law is enacted, the privileges are those enjoyed by the House of Commons of the United Kingdom at the commencement of the Constitution. This reference to the British model provides a historical benchmark while allowing Parliament to evolve its own privilege regime through legislation such as the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977.

4.2 Freedom of Speech

The freedom of speech guaranteed under Article 105(1) is absolute within the precincts of Parliament, meaning that no member can be sued or prosecuted for any statement made during proceedings. This immunity extends to words spoken in committees and to the publication of parliamentary debates by authorised agencies. However, the freedom is subject to the rules of procedure framed by each House, which prohibit unparliamentary language, personal allegations against a member without prior notice, and discussion of matters sub judice. The Speaker or Chairman enforces these rules, and a member who violates them may be named, suspended, or expelled from the House. The balance between absolute immunity and procedural discipline ensures robust debate while maintaining decorum.

4.3 Contempt of Parliament

Contempt of Parliament refers to any act or omission that obstructs or impedes the House in the performance of its functions, or that tends to bring the House or its members into disrepute. It includes disobedience to a lawful order of the House or a committee, refusal to give evidence or produce documents when summoned, and publication of false or misleading reports of parliamentary proceedings. The power to punish contempt is inherent in each House and is exercised through a motion of breach of privilege, which is examined by the Committee of Privileges. The committee may recommend admonition, reprimand, suspension, or even expulsion of a member, or in the case of a non‑member, imprisonment or fine. The judiciary has generally refrained from interfering with the internal disciplinary powers of Parliament, recognising the principle of separation of powers, although the Supreme Court in the case of *Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly* (1965) held that the power to punish contempt is subject to judicial review for violation of fundamental rights.

4.4 Judicial Review of Privileges

While Parliament enjoys wide discretion in defining and enforcing its privileges, the Supreme Court has asserted its authority to examine whether a claimed privilege is consistent with the Constitution, particularly the fundamental rights guaranteed under Part III. In *Raja Ram Pal v. Hon’ble Speaker, Lok Sabha* (2007), the Court held that the power to expel a member for breach of privilege is subject to judicial review and must comply with the principles of natural justice. Similarly, in *State of Kerala v. K. Ajith* (2021), the Court clarified that legislative privilege cannot be invoked to shield criminal acts committed within the House premises. These judgments illustrate the evolving jurisprudence that seeks to harmonise parliamentary autonomy with constitutional supremacy, ensuring that privileges serve the democratic purpose for which they were conferred.

5. Recent Reforms and Challenges

5.1 Anti‑Defection Law Impact

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the Fifty‑second Amendment Act, 1985, seeks to curb political defections by disqualifying members who voluntarily give up membership of their party or vote contrary to party directions. While the law has reduced overt floor‑crossing, it has also strengthened party whips, limiting individual legislators’ freedom to vote according to conscience or constituency interests. The Supreme Court in *Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992) upheld the constitutional validity of the Schedule but subjected the Speaker’s decision to judicial review, establishing that the Speaker acts as a tribunal and must follow principles of natural justice. Recent debates focus on whether the law should be amended to allow a split in a party if a substantial fraction of its legislators dissent, thereby balancing party discipline with democratic representation.

5.2 Role of Whips

Party whips are appointed by political parties to ensure attendance and voting discipline among their members. The Chief Whip of the ruling party coordinates legislative business with the government, while opposition whips mobilise their members for debates and divisions. The anti‑defection law has amplified the whip’s authority, as defiance can lead to disqualification. However, the whip’s directives are not legally binding on members in matters of conscience such as the election of the President or Vice‑President, where a secret ballot is used. The tension between party discipline and individual accountability remains a central theme in parliamentary reform discussions.

5.3 Digital Initiatives

In recent years Parliament has adopted digital platforms to enhance transparency and efficiency. The National e‑Vidhan Application (NeVA) aims to make legislative proceedings paperless, providing real‑time access to bills, questions, and committee reports for members and the public. Live telecast of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha proceedings on dedicated television channels and streaming services has increased public engagement. The introduction of a digital question‑hour portal allows members to submit starred and unstarred questions electronically, reducing administrative delays. These initiatives reflect a broader governance shift towards e‑governance, though challenges such as digital divide among members and cybersecurity concerns persist.

5.4 Accountability Mechanisms

Beyond the traditional tools of questions, motions, and committees, Parliament has experimented with new accountability mechanisms such as the Zero Hour, where members raise urgent matters without prior notice, and the Calling Attention Motion, which compels a minister to make a statement on a matter of urgent public importance. The establishment of the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) has been proposed to provide independent fiscal analysis, strengthening the legislature’s capacity to scrutinise the budget. Moreover, the practice of departmental standing committees inviting civil society experts and stakeholders for evidence‑based deliberations has enriched legislative oversight. These evolving mechanisms aim to bridge the gap between executive action and legislative scrutiny, ensuring that Parliament remains an effective forum for democratic accountability.

The lesson concludes with a comprehensive overview of Parliament’s structure, legislative procedures, committee system, privileges, and contemporary reforms, equipping the student with the analytical depth required for the mains examination.

State Legislature: Assembly, Council and Law-Making

Constitutional Basis (Article 168)

Article 168 requires that for every State there shall be a Legislature consisting of the Governor and one or two Houses. Where there are two Houses, they are the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) and the Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad). The Constitution does not prescribe bicameralism for every State; the choice has been a matter of legislative decision and political preference, and the general trend after 1956 was towards unicameralism. At present six States have Legislative Councils, namely Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh, while the rest have only an Assembly. Under Article 169, Parliament may create or abolish a Council in a State by ordinary law, but only if the Assembly of that State first passes a resolution to that effect by a majority of its total membership and by a two-thirds majority of the members present and voting.

Composition of the Legislative Assembly (Article 170)

The Assembly consists of not more than five hundred and not fewer than sixty members chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies. Seats are readjusted after each census by a Delimitation Commission, which redraws constituency boundaries so that the population-to-seat ratio is, as far as practicable, the same throughout the State. A few States are exceptions to the general floor because of their small size or special history. Members are elected on the basis of adult suffrage, and a person must have completed twenty-five years of age to be a member of the Assembly.

Composition of the Legislative Council (Article 171)

Where a Council exists, its strength cannot exceed one-third of the strength of the Assembly and cannot be less than forty members, again with a few exceptions for smaller States. The Council is a partly elected, partly nominated body. As nearly as may be, one-third of its members are elected by members of local bodies, one-third by the members of the Assembly from among persons who are not Assembly members, one-twelfth by graduates of three years' standing, and one-twelfth by teachers; the remainder, about one-sixth, are nominated by the Governor for their special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, the co-operative movement and social service. A member of the Council must have completed thirty years of age. This design makes the Council a chamber of diverse, largely local-government and professional electorates rather than a mirror of the general electorate.

Duration and Presiding Officers (Articles 172, 178 and 182)

The Assembly has a normal term of five years from the date appointed for its first sitting, though this may be extended by one year at a time during a national emergency. The Council is a permanent body and cannot be dissolved: one-third of its members retire every second year, and each member serves a six-year term. The Assembly is presided over by a Speaker and a Deputy Speaker, and the Council by a Chairman and a Deputy Chairman, chosen by the members from among themselves. The Speaker's role mirrors that of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, including the power to decide questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule.

Law-Making and the Position of the Council

The legislative procedure in a State is broadly the same as in Parliament, with one decisive difference. A Money Bill may be introduced only in the Assembly, and the Council may retain it for a maximum of fourteen days, during which it can only make recommendations that the Assembly may accept or reject. For an ordinary Bill, the Council can delay the Assembly at most three months at the first reading and one month at the second, after which the Assembly may pass the Bill again and it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses. The Council therefore possesses no absolute veto and no power to amend a Money Bill. It has none of the special federal powers that the Rajya Sabha enjoys, such as the power under Article 249 to enable Parliament to legislate on a State subject; those powers are reserved to the Council of States at the Union level. After a Bill is passed, it is presented to the Governor, who may assent, withhold assent, return it for reconsideration, or reserve it for the consideration of the President.

Why the Second Chamber Matters

Critics argue that Legislative Councils are costly and can obstruct a popularly elected Assembly. Defenders reply that a permanent, indirectly elected chamber provides continuity, brings experience and specialised knowledge into legislation, and acts as a revising and delaying chamber free from the immediate pressures of the general electorate. The debate recurs whenever a State proposes to create or abolish a Council, and Article 169 leaves the final word with Parliament on the initiative of the Assembly. For the examination, the practical points are the Article 169 procedure, the Article 171 composition formula, the four-month outer limit on the Council's delaying power, and the fact that a Council takes no part in the election of the President and has no special federal powers.

Elections and Electoral Reform

Constitutional Framework (Articles 324 to 329)

Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls, and of the conduct of elections to Parliament, the State legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President, in an Election Commission. Article 325 provides for a single general electoral roll for every constituency, so that no person is excluded from a roll on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex. Article 326 confers the vote on every citizen who has completed eighteen years of age, the voting age having been lowered from twenty-one by the 61st Amendment, 1988. Articles 327 and 328 empower Parliament and the State legislatures respectively to make laws on electoral matters, and Article 329 bars the courts from calling an election in question except through an election petition.

The Representation of the People Acts

The conduct of elections is governed by two statutes. The Representation of the People Act, 1950, deals with the allocation of seats, the delimitation of constituencies and the preparation of electoral rolls. The Representation of the People Act, 1951, deals with the actual conduct of elections, the qualifications and disqualifications of candidates, the decision of doubts and disputes, and corrupt practices and electoral offences. Disputes about the validity of an election are decided by the High Court on an election petition. The Election Commission is a multi-member body, consisting of the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners, and its independence is protected by the requirement that the Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in the manner provided for a judge of the Supreme Court.

Electoral Reform and the Model Code of Conduct

The Model Code of Conduct is not a statute; it is a consensus document evolved by the Election Commission and enforced during the election period through the Commission's powers under Article 324. It regulates the conduct of parties and candidates, the use of official machinery, and the content of campaigning. Statutory and judicial reforms have accumulated over time: the anti-defection law through the Tenth Schedule, the lowering of the voting age, the introduction of electronic voting machines and later of the Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail, and the introduction of the None of the Above option (NOTA) in 2013 to allow a voter to reject all candidates without disclosing a preference. Public interest litigation led to the requirement that candidates disclose their criminal antecedents and assets, and the Supreme Court has directed political parties to publish the criminal records of their candidates.

Electoral Bonds and the Right to Information

The electoral bond scheme, introduced in 2018, allowed individuals and companies to donate to political parties through bearer instruments purchased from a designated bank, without disclosing the identity of the donor. In Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024) the Supreme Court struck down the scheme as unconstitutional, holding that it violated the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a) by concealing the source of political funding, and the State Bank of India was directed to disclose the details of the bonds purchased and encashed. The judgment revived the debate on state funding of elections, transparency in political finance and the ceiling on electoral expenditure. The appointment of Election Commissioners was also the subject of litigation: in Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023) the Supreme Court prescribed a selection committee for appointments to the Commission, and Parliament then enacted a law providing for a different selection committee, which has itself been the subject of challenge. For the examination, the enduring themes are the constitutional powers of the Election Commission, the Model Code, the Representation of the People Acts, NOTA, and the tension between political finance and the right to information.

Parliamentary Motions and Their Strategic Use

Parliamentary motions are the formal devices through which members initiate debate, test the confidence of the government, or demand accountability on specific issues. The Rules of Procedure categorise motions into substantive motions (which propose a decision) and subsidiary motions (which regulate the conduct of debate). Key substantive motions include the No‑Confidence Motion (Lok Sabha only), the Censure Motion (moved in Lok Sabha against the council of ministers or individual ministers), the Adjournment Motion (requires the support of at least 50 members and the Speaker’s consent to discuss a matter of urgent public importance), and the Calling Attention Motion (a member calls the attention of a minister to a matter of urgent public importance, compelling a statement). The Short Duration Discussion (Rule 193 in Lok Sabha, Rule 176 in Rajya Sabha) allows a time‑limited debate without a formal vote, often used for policy scrutiny without the political risk of a division.

Strategically, the opposition uses the Adjournment Motion to force a debate on a burning issue, knowing that the government must respond on the floor. The No‑Confidence Motion, though rarely successful, serves as a barometer of coalition stability; its admission requires the support of 50 members and a discussion within 10 days. The Censure Motion, unlike a No‑Confidence Motion, does not require the government to resign if passed, but it carries significant political weight. The Calling Attention Motion is less disruptive but guarantees a ministerial statement, making it a preferred tool for targeted accountability. The Zero Hour, though not a formal motion, allows members to raise matters without prior notice, and its effectiveness depends on the Speaker’s discretion in allocating time.

Procedural nuances shape outcomes. For instance, an Adjournment Motion lapses if the House is adjourned before discussion, while a Calling Attention Motion lapses if the minister fails to respond within the session. The Speaker’s power to admit or reject motions under Rules 56 (Lok Sabha) and 180 (Rajya Sabha) is a critical gatekeeping function. In practice, the government often uses its majority to defer or dilute motions through procedural amendments, such as moving a motion to refer the matter to a committee. Understanding these tactical dimensions is essential for analysing parliamentary behaviour in mains answers.

Speaker and Chairman: Powers, Impartiality and Controversies

The Speaker of the Lok Sabha and the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (the Vice‑President ex officio) are the presiding officers vested with extensive powers to regulate proceedings, maintain order, and interpret the Rules of Procedure. Under Article 94 and Article 89 respectively, they are elected by the House (Speaker) or assume office by virtue of the Vice‑Presidency (Chairman). Their powers include deciding points of order, certifying Money Bills (Article 110), recognising members to speak, allocating time for debates, and exercising a casting vote in case of a tie. The Speaker also adjudicates disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule, a quasi‑judicial function subject to judicial review as established in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992).

Impartiality is a constitutional expectation, reinforced by conventions such as the Speaker’s distancing from party politics upon election and the practice of not participating in debates. However, controversies have arisen when presiding officers were perceived to act in a partisan manner — instances include contested rulings on whips during confidence votes and the certification of certain bills as Money Bills, which sparked debate over the scope of Article 110. The Chairman’s role is similarly scrutinised; the Vice‑President’s dual capacity as a political office‑holder and presiding officer has led to allegations of bias during crucial legislative debates.

Removal procedures differ: the Speaker can be removed by a resolution passed by a majority of the total membership of the Lok Sabha (Article 94(c)), while the Chairman can only be removed through the impeachment process applicable to the Vice‑President (Article 67(b)). Both offices have administrative control over the Secretariat, influencing the flow of legislative business. The Speaker’s certification of a Bill as a Money Bill is final in the sense that it cannot be questioned in Parliament, but the Supreme Court in Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019) held that such certification must not be a colourable exercise of power and is subject to judicial review on that ground. These institutional dynamics are central to understanding the balance between parliamentary autonomy and executive accountability.

Worked Example: Distinguishing Cut Motions from the Guillotine
A question asks: "Explain the difference between a Policy Cut Motion and the Guillotine in the context of the budgetary process. How does each affect the passage of Demands for Grants?"
Answer: A Policy Cut Motion is a substantive motion moved during the discussion on a specific Demand for Grants, proposing to reduce the amount to Re 1 to signal disapproval of the policy underlying that demand. It is debated and voted upon individually. An Economy Cut Motion seeks a specific reduction in the amount, while a Token Cut Motion reduces the demand by a nominal sum (Rs 100) to ventilate a grievance without rejecting the policy. The Guillotine, by contrast, is a procedural device invoked by the Speaker at the conclusion of the allotted time for the discussion of Demands for Grants. When the Guillotine is applied, all outstanding Demands for Grants — whether discussed or not — are put to vote en bloc without further debate. Thus, a Cut Motion allows targeted expression of dissent on a particular demand, whereas the Guillotine curtails debate and forces a collective vote on all remaining demands, often limiting the opposition’s ability to scrutinise individual allocations.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks you to “compare” two parliamentary instruments, structure the answer in a three‑column table: (1) Constitutional/Rule basis, (2) Procedural trigger and voting requirement, (3) Political effect. This format forces precision and saves time. For motions, always note whether the motion can be moved in both Houses or only Lok Sabha, and whether it leads to a government resignation. For budgetary tools, remember that Cut Motions are substantive and debated; the Guillotine is a time‑management device that ends debate. Cite the relevant Rule numbers (e.g., Rule 211 for Cut Motions, Rule 212 for Guillotine in Lok Sabha) to demonstrate depth.

Department‑Related Standing Committees: Structure, Functioning and Impact

The Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) were constituted in 1993 on the recommendation of the Rules Committee to enhance specialised legislative scrutiny. There are 24 such committees, each shadowing a set of ministries or departments. Each committee comprises 31 members — 21 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha — nominated by the Speaker and the Chairman respectively, broadly reflecting party strength. The term of a committee is one year, after which it is reconstituted. The Chairpersons are appointed by the Speaker (for committees under Lok Sabha’s purview) or the Chairman (for those under Rajya Sabha), with a convention of allocating chairpersonships to opposition parties for key committees such as Home Affairs, Finance, and External Affairs.

DRSCs perform three core functions: (1) examination of Demands for Grants of the concerned ministries and reporting to the House before the budget is voted; (2) scrutiny of Bills referred to them by the presiding officers, including clause‑by‑clause analysis and stakeholder consultations; (3) review of annual reports, long‑term policy documents, and matters referred by the House. Their reports are recommendatory; the government is not bound to accept them, but a formal response (Action Taken Report) is expected. The committees operate through sittings that are not open to the public, though they may invite experts, civil society representatives, and ministry officials for evidence. The confidentiality of proceedings encourages candid deliberation, but it also limits transparency.

Assessments indicate that DRSCs have improved the quality of legislative scrutiny — Bills referred to committees undergo more amendments and are less likely to be passed without debate. However, challenges persist: limited time (committees often meet only during sessions), party whips influencing members’ positions, and the absence of a dedicated research staff comparable to the Congressional Research Service in the United States. The 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission (2008) recommended strengthening committee secretariats and extending tenure to two years. These reforms remain largely unimplemented, making the DRSCs a recurring theme in questions on parliamentary effectiveness.

Budgetary Scrutiny: Demands for Grants, Cut Motions and the Guillotine

The Union Budget is presented in two parts: the Railway Budget (merged since 2017) and the General Budget. After the Finance Minister’s speech, the Lok Sabha takes up the Demands for Grants — ministry‑wise expenditure proposals — for discussion and voting. Each Demand is a separate motion; members may move Cut Motions to express disapproval. A Policy Cut Motion reduces the demand to Re 1, signalling rejection of the underlying policy. An Economy Cut Motion proposes a specific reduction in the amount, while a Token Cut Motion reduces the demand by a nominal sum (Rs 100) to ventilate a grievance without rejecting the policy. Only members of the Lok Sabha can move Cut Motions; the Rajya Sabha discusses the budget but does not vote on Demands for Grants.

The discussion on Demands for Grants is allotted a fixed number of days (usually 26 days for the General Budget). At the end of the allotted time, the Speaker applies the Guillotine (Rule 212 of Lok Sabha Rules), putting all outstanding Demands — whether discussed or not — to vote en bloc. This device ensures that the budget is passed within the session but curtails detailed scrutiny of individual demands. After the Demands are voted, the Appropriation Bill is introduced to authorise withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund, followed by the Finance Bill to give effect to taxation proposals. The Rajya Sabha can only recommend amendments to the Finance Bill; it cannot amend or reject the Appropriation Bill.

Strategically, the opposition uses Cut Motions to force debates on controversial allocations, but the government’s majority usually defeats them. The Guillotine is often criticised for reducing parliamentary control over the purse strings. Various expert bodies have recommended that the Guillotine be applied only after a minimum threshold of discussion on each Demand, and that a Parliamentary Budget Office be established to provide independent fiscal analysis. The absence of such an office means that members rely on ministry‑provided data, limiting effective oversight. Understanding the interplay between Cut Motions, the Guillotine, and the passage of the Appropriation and Finance Bills is essential for answering questions on financial accountability.

Rajya Sabha’s Special Powers and Federal Role

The Rajya Sabha, as the Council of States, possesses distinct constitutional powers that reinforce India’s federal character. Under Article 249, if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two‑thirds of the members present and voting, Parliament can legislate on a subject in the State List in the national interest for a period of one year, extendable by further resolutions. Article 312 empowers the Rajya Sabha to create new All India Services by a similar two‑thirds resolution, a power used to establish the Indian Forest Service. The Rajya Sabha also has a decisive role in the proclamation of Emergency: under Article 352, a proclamation of Emergency must be approved by both Houses within one month; if the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the Rajya Sabha’s approval suffices for a limited period.

In constitutional amendments, the Rajya Sabha enjoys equal status with the Lok Sabha — both must pass the amendment bill by the special majority prescribed in Article 368. However, for amendments affecting the federal structure (e.g., alteration of state boundaries, representation of states in Parliament), the Rajya Sabha’s concurrence is particularly significant because it represents the states. The Rajya Sabha also participates in the election of the President and Vice‑President, and in the impeachment of the President (Article 61). Its members are elected by the Legislative Assemblies of states through proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote, ensuring that the House reflects the federal composition.

Despite these powers, the Rajya Sabha cannot introduce or amend Money Bills (Article 110) and has only recommendatory powers over the Finance Bill. Its inability to vote on Demands for Grants limits its direct control over the purse. The tension between its federal mandate and its subordinate financial role has sparked debates on whether the Rajya Sabha should be given greater fiscal oversight, such as a veto on the Appropriation Bill or a dedicated budget committee. The Supreme Court has affirmed that the Rajya Sabha’s special powers under Articles 249 and 312 are not merely advisory but constitute a substantive federal check. This jurisprudence underscores the House’s role as a guardian of state interests within the parliamentary framework.

Parliamentary Questions: Types, Procedure, and Executive Accountability

Parliamentary questions constitute the primary daily instrument through which members hold the executive to account. The Lok Sabha Rules (Rules 32‑34) and the Rajya Sabha Rules (Rules 31‑33) recognise four categories: Starred Questions, Unstarred Questions, Short‑Notice Questions and Supplementary Questions. A Starred Question requires an oral answer on the floor; the member asking may ask up to two supplementaries after the minister’s reply. An Unstarred Question receives a written answer laid on the Table, and no supplementaries are permitted. Short‑Notice Questions are admitted only when the matter is of urgent public importance and the Minister concerned agrees to answer at short notice; they are treated as Starred Questions for the purpose of supplementaries. Supplementary Questions arise spontaneously during the answer to a Starred or Short‑Notice Question and are limited to two per original question.

The notice period for a Starred or Unstarred Question is fifteen clear days; for a Short‑Notice Question it is three days. The Speaker or Chairman may disallow a question if it is vague, relates to a matter sub‑judice, or seeks information that would compromise national security. The Question Hour, the first hour of each sitting, is reserved exclusively for these questions. If the House decides to suspend Question Hour (for example, during a special session), the questions are carried over to the next sitting. Ministers are constitutionally bound to answer; failure to do so without valid reason can be treated as a breach of privilege. The data elicited through questions frequently forms the basis for debates, committee inquiries, and media scrutiny, making the question mechanism a cornerstone of parliamentary oversight.

In practice, the government answers roughly 70‑80 % of Starred Questions orally, while the remainder are converted to Unstarred status due to time constraints. The Rajya Sabha follows an identical pattern, though its Question Hour is held on alternate days when the House sits for a shorter duration. The effectiveness of the system depends on the quality of the notice, the minister’s preparedness, and the willingness of the presiding officer to protect the member’s right to ask supplementaries. Recent reforms have introduced an online portal for submitting notices and tracking the status of answers, improving transparency and reducing the backlog of pending questions.

Private Members' Bills and Resolutions: Legislative Space for Non‑Government Members

Any Member of Parliament who is not a Minister may introduce a Private Member’s Bill. The procedure is governed by Rule 71 of the Lok Sabha Rules and Rule 70 of the Rajya Sabha Rules. The member must give a written notice of at least one month before the intended date of introduction. All notices received are placed in a ballot conducted by the Secretary‑General; only the bills that win the ballot are taken up for discussion. The House allocates a maximum of two and a half hours on each Friday (or the last sitting day of the week) for Private Members’ Bills, and each bill receives a maximum of thirty minutes for debate. If the bill is not disposed of within the allotted time, it lapses.

Since the first Lok Sabha in 1952, only fourteen Private Members’ Bills have been enacted into law, the most recent being the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 (originally introduced as a Private Member’s Bill in 2014). The low success rate reflects the government’s control over the legislative agenda and the limited time available. Private Members’ Resolutions follow a similar ballot and time‑allocation process; they are non‑binding but can influence policy discourse. The Speaker or Chairman may also allow a Private Member’s Bill to be taken up as a Government Bill if the Ministry concerned adopts it, thereby bypassing the ballot constraint. The procedural safeguards — notice period, ballot, limited debate time — are designed to balance the right of individual legislators to propose legislation with the need for orderly parliamentary business.

Worked Example: Private Member’s Bill Procedure
A member of the Lok Sabha wishes to introduce a Bill to amend the Representation of the People Act, 1951. Outline the procedural steps the member must follow from notice to possible enactment, highlighting the role of the ballot and the time allocated for debate.
1. The member submits a written notice of the Bill to the Secretary‑General at least one month before the desired introduction date (Rule 71). 2. All notices received are placed in a ballot conducted by the Secretary‑General; only the bills that win the ballot are listed for introduction. 3. On the allotted Friday, the member moves the motion for leave to introduce the Bill; the Speaker puts the motion to vote. 4. If leave is granted, the Bill is introduced and referred to the relevant Department‑Related Standing Committee for examination. 5. The Committee reports back; the Bill is then debated during the Private Members’ Bill slot (maximum thirty minutes). 6. If passed by the Lok Sabha, it proceeds to the Rajya Sabha, where it follows the same Private Members’ Bill procedure. 7. Only after both Houses pass the Bill and the President assents does it become law. The ballot and the limited Friday time‑slot are the critical bottlenecks that explain why only fourteen such Bills have become law since 1952.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the “legislative space” for non‑government members, focus on three procedural gatekeepers: the one‑month notice, the ballot conducted by the Secretary‑General, and the fixed Friday time‑limit. Mention the fourteen‑Bill statistic to demonstrate depth; avoid conflating Private Members’ Bills with Government Bills that merely originate from a private member’s idea.

Committee on Public Undertakings: Mandate, Working and Impact on PSU Governance

The Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) was constituted in 1964 on the recommendation of the Krishna Menon Committee. It consists of twenty‑two members — fifteen elected from the Lok Sabha and seven from the Rajya Sabha — on the principle of proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote. The Chairman is appointed by the Speaker from among the Lok Sabha members of the Committee. COPU examines the reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) on public sector undertakings (PSUs), reviews the autonomy and efficiency of these enterprises, and assesses whether their affairs are managed in accordance with sound commercial principles.

The Committee can summon officials, call for records, and undertake on‑site visits. Its reports are presented to both Houses and the government is required to furnish an Action Taken Report within six months. Although the recommendations are not binding, they carry significant moral and political weight; ministries often implement suggestions to avoid adverse parliamentary debate. Notable inquiries include the 2001 examination of Air India’s financial restructuring, the 2015 review of Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited’s (BSNL) revival plan, and the 2020 scrutiny of the strategic disinvestment process for several PSUs. COPU works in tandem with the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Estimates Committee, but its exclusive focus on commercial undertakings gives it a unique oversight niche.

In recent years, the Committee has recommended the establishment of a dedicated PSU performance dashboard, greater board independence, and the adoption of corporate governance norms aligned with the Companies Act, 2013. The government’s acceptance rate of COPU recommendations has hovered around 60 % over the last decade, reflecting both the Committee’s influence and the practical constraints of PSU reform. Understanding COPU’s composition, powers, and interaction with the CAG is essential for answering questions on parliamentary control over the public sector.

Parliamentary Scrutiny of International Treaties and Agreements

Article 253 empowers Parliament to make any law for the whole or any part of the territory of India for implementing any treaty, agreement or convention with any other country or countries. The Constitution does not require parliamentary ratification of treaties per se; the executive can enter into binding international obligations, but any treaty that requires a change in domestic law must be backed by legislation. The Ministry of External Affairs lays the text of treaties before Parliament, usually through a statement by the Minister of External Affairs. The Department‑Related Standing Committee on External Affairs examines the treaty’s implications and may call for evidence from the Ministry.

Parliament’s role is therefore legislative rather than ratificatory. If a treaty involves financial commitments, the Appropriation Bill must authorise the expenditure. The Supreme Court, in the case of State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1997), held that the executive cannot bypass Parliament when a treaty obliges the State to enact a law. The Rajya Sabha enjoys equal legislative power in this respect; both Houses must pass the implementing legislation. Executive agreements that do not require legislative change — such as memoranda of understanding on technical cooperation — are not placed before Parliament, though the government may voluntarily inform the Houses. This distinction between “treaties requiring legislation” and “executive agreements” is a frequent examiner trap.

Recent practice shows an increase in the number of treaties laid before Parliament, reflecting a trend toward greater transparency. The Committee on External Affairs has recommended that all treaties with significant domestic impact be referred to it before the government deposits the instrument of ratification. While not constitutionally mandated, this practice strengthens parliamentary oversight of foreign policy commitments.

No‑Confidence and Confidence Motions: Procedural Nuances and Political Consequences

Article 75(3) enshrines the principle of collective responsibility: the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. A Motion of No‑Confidence can be moved only in the Lok Sabha under Rule 198. It requires the written support of at least fifty members. The Speaker examines the notice for admissibility; if admitted, the motion is placed on the agenda for a day’s debate. The Prime Minister or any Minister may reply. A simple majority of members present and voting decides the outcome. If the motion is carried, the Council of Ministers must resign, and the President may invite the Leader of the Opposition to form a government or dissolve the House.

A Confidence Motion, by contrast, is moved by the Prime Minister to demonstrate that the government still enjoys the confidence of the House. It follows the same voting threshold but is politically initiated by the executive. The Rajya Sabha has no role in either motion. Historically, No‑Confidence Motions have been admitted in 1979 (Charan Singh), 1990 (V. P. Singh), 1999 (A. B. Vajpayee), 2008 (Manmohan Singh) and 2018 (Narendra Modi). In each case, the debate provided a platform for the opposition to articulate policy critiques, even when the government survived. The Speaker’s discretion in admitting the motion — especially the requirement that the notice be signed by fifty members — is a procedural safeguard against frivolous attempts.

The political consequences extend beyond the immediate vote: a defeated No‑Confidence Motion can strengthen the government’s mandate, while a successful one triggers a constitutional crisis requiring the President’s intervention. The interplay between the motion, the whip system, and party discipline makes this procedure a focal point for questions on parliamentary democracy and executive accountability.

Ethics Committee and Committee on Privileges: Guardians of Parliamentary Conduct

The Ethics Committee of the Lok Sabha was first constituted in 1997 to examine complaints relating to the ethical conduct of members. Its mandate covers allegations of misuse of position, conflict of interest, and breach of the code of conduct adopted by the House. The Committee on Privileges, a standing committee since 1952, deals with breaches of parliamentary privilege and contempt of the House. While the Ethics Committee focuses on moral standards, the Privileges Committee addresses legal‑constitutional violations such as obstruction of proceedings, intimidation of members, or publication of expunged proceedings.

Composition and Procedure

Both committees consist of 15 members nominated by the Speaker, reflecting party strength. The Ethics Committee elects its own chairperson; the Privileges Committee is chaired by the Deputy Speaker by convention. A complaint to the Ethics Committee may be filed by any member, a citizen, or suo motu by the Speaker. The committee conducts a preliminary inquiry, may summon witnesses, and submits a report with recommendations — admonition, suspension, or expulsion — to the House. The Privileges Committee follows a similar quasi‑judicial process: notice to the alleged contemnor, opportunity to be heard, and a report recommending censure, imprisonment (up to the remainder of the session), or referral to the courts for criminal contempt.

Landmark Cases

In Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly (1965), the Supreme Court upheld the power of the House to punish contempt, affirming that parliamentary privilege is part of the law of the land. The 2005 Cash‑for‑Questions scandal led to the expulsion of 11 members on the Ethics Committee’s recommendation, the first mass expulsion in independent India. The Ethics Committee has also acted in cases involving unauthorised disclosure of confidential committee proceedings, reinforcing that such confidentiality is a privilege.

Interplay with Judicial Review

Article 122 bars courts from inquiring into the validity of proceedings of Parliament, but the Supreme Court in Raja Ram Pal v. Hon’ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007) held that judicial review is available where the House acts without jurisdiction or in violation of fundamental rights. The Ethics Committee’s recommendations are not immune; a member expelled on procedural grounds may challenge the decision under Article 32. The Privileges Committee’s power to imprison is subject to the condition that the contemnor is produced before a magistrate within 24 hours, a safeguard rooted in the M. S. M. Sharma v. Shree Krishna Sinha (1959) judgment and criminal procedure.

Committee on Government Assurances: Monitoring Executive Commitments

The Committee on Government Assurances, constituted in 1953, scrutinises assurances, promises, and undertakings given by ministers on the floor of the House. Its objective is to ensure that the executive does not treat parliamentary statements as mere rhetoric. The committee examines the implementation status of each assurance, classifies them as “fulfilled”, “partially fulfilled”, or “not fulfilled”, and reports to the House. Ministers are required to submit Action Taken Reports (ATRs) within three months of the committee’s recommendation.

Working Methodology

Assurances are extracted from the verbatim records of debates, questions, and statements. The committee maintains a register of thousands of assurances since inception. It prioritises assurances involving financial outlays, legislative changes, or policy shifts. The committee may summon the concerned secretary, seek clarifications, and conduct on‑spot visits. Its reports are laid on the Table and debated during the “Assurances” segment of the Business Advisory Committee’s schedule.

Impact and Limitations

The committee has reported a substantial fulfilment rate for assurances tracked over recent years. High‑profile cases include persistent follow‑up on the GST compensation cess assurance, which compelled the Ministry of Finance to release pending dues to states. However, the committee lacks enforcement powers; it cannot compel compliance beyond moral pressure and the political cost of adverse reports. A recent report highlighted that a significant share of assurances older than five years remained unfulfilled, prompting a recommendation for statutory backing — a proposal still under consideration.

Interaction with Other Oversight Mechanisms

The committee’s work complements the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Estimates. While PAC audits expenditure after the fact, the Assurances Committee monitors the executive’s forward‑looking commitments. The Department‑Related Standing Committees often refer assurances relevant to their ministries to the Assurances Committee for specialised tracking. This layered oversight reduces the risk of assurances slipping through the cracks.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which committee monitors the implementation of assurances given by ministers on the floor of the House?” Many aspirants answer “Public Accounts Committee” because it deals with executive accountability. The correct answer is the Committee on Government Assurances, a distinct standing committee with a specific mandate.
The Committee on Government Assurances was constituted in 1953 under Rule 212 of the Lok Sabha Rules. Its sole function is to examine assurances, promises, and undertakings given by ministers during proceedings and to report on their implementation. The Public Accounts Committee, by contrast, examines the audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General and focuses on past expenditure. Remember the keyword “assurances” — it appears only in the name and mandate of this committee.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mentions “assurances”, “promises on the floor”, or “Action Taken Report on ministerial statements”, immediately map it to the Committee on Government Assurances. Create a one‑line flashcard: “Assurances → Committee on Government Assurances (1953) → monitors implementation → no enforcement power”. This eliminates confusion with PAC, Estimates Committee, or Department‑Related Standing Committees.

Parliamentary Scrutiny of Ordinances: Constitutional Checks and Judicial Review

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session, provided the President is satisfied that circumstances exist which render immediate action necessary. An ordinance has the same force as an Act of Parliament but must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble. It ceases to operate at the expiration of six weeks from reassembly unless approved by both Houses. The Rajya Sabha enjoys equal power to approve or reject an ordinance, reinforcing the federal character of legislative authority.

Procedural Safeguards

The Constitution requires that the ordinance be placed before Parliament “as soon as possible” after reassembly. In practice, the government introduces a Bill to replace the ordinance; the Bill follows the normal legislative process. If either House disapproves the ordinance, it lapses. The Speaker/Chairman may allow a discussion on the ordinance before the replacement Bill is taken up, providing a forum for scrutiny. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation does not examine ordinances, as they are primary legislation, but the Department‑Related Standing Committee concerned may be consulted on the replacement Bill.

Judicial Pronouncements

The Supreme Court in D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) held that repeated re‑promulgation of ordinances without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution. The Court laid down that ordinances must be a “temporary” measure, not a substitute for parliamentary legislation. In Krishna Kumar v. State of Bihar (2017), a seven‑judge bench reiterated that the satisfaction of the President is justiciable to the extent of examining whether the precondition — Parliament not being in session — existed, and whether the ordinance was a colourable exercise of power. The Court also clarified that an ordinance cannot amend the Constitution; constitutional amendments require the special procedure under Article 368.

Recent Trends

In recent years, the Union government has promulgated a significant number of ordinances, most of which were replaced by Acts. The 2020 Ordinance on the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) was challenged for bypassing parliamentary debate during the pandemic; the Supreme Court upheld it, noting the extraordinary circumstances satisfied the “immediate action” test. However, the Court warned against routine use of ordinances for policy changes that could await the next session. Instances of the Rajya Sabha rejecting a replacement Bill, causing an ordinance to lapse, remain rare but underscore the Upper House’s constitutional role.

Zero Hour, Calling Attention and Short Duration Discussions: Instruments of Immediate Accountability

Zero Hour is an informal parliamentary innovation that begins immediately after Question Hour (usually at 12 noon) and allows members to raise matters of urgent public importance without prior notice. Though not codified in the Rules of Procedure, the Speaker permits a set number of members to speak for a short duration each, subject to the discretion of the Chair. The government is not obliged to reply, but ministers often respond to avoid adverse publicity.

Calling Attention Motion

Under Rule 197 (Lok Sabha) and Rule 180 (Rajya Sabha), a member may, with the Speaker’s/Chairman’s consent, call the attention of a minister to a matter of urgent public importance. The minister makes a brief statement, followed by clarifications from members. Unlike Zero Hour, the motion requires prior notice (usually 15 minutes before the sitting) and the minister’s reply is mandatory. The motion does not entail a vote.

Short Duration Discussion

Rule 193 (Lok Sabha) and Rule 176 (Rajya Sabha) provide for a Short Duration Discussion on a matter of urgent public importance, limited to two and a half hours. The member gives notice, the Business Advisory Committee allocates time, and the minister replies at the end. No formal motion or voting occurs. This device is used for issues such as natural disasters, communal tensions, or sudden policy reversals where a full debate is impractical.

Comparative Effectiveness

Zero Hour offers maximum flexibility but minimal accountability; the government may ignore the issues raised. Calling Attention guarantees a ministerial response, making it a stronger accountability tool. Short Duration Discussion allows a more structured exchange but consumes significant House time, so it is reserved for high‑impact topics. Data from recent Lok Sabhas show a high volume of Zero Hour mentions, a moderate number of Calling Attention motions admitted, and fewer Short Duration Discussions. The acceptance rate for Calling Attention notices reflects the Speaker’s gatekeeping role.

Strategic Use by Opposition

Opposition parties coordinate Zero Hour mentions to create a narrative on a single theme — for example, a series of mentions on farm‑loan waivers can pressure the government into a policy announcement. Calling Attention motions are often used to force a minister on record before a scheduled debate. Short Duration Discussions are negotiated through the Business Advisory Committee, giving the opposition a platform to shape the agenda without needing a majority.

Leader of Opposition and Deputy Speaker: Institutional Roles and Constitutional Significance

The Leader of the Opposition (LoP) in the Lok Sabha is the leader of the largest party not in government, provided it holds at least one‑tenth of the total seats (55 seats in a 543‑member House). The post received statutory recognition through the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977, which equates the LoP’s status, salary, and facilities to those of a Cabinet Minister. The LoP is a member of key selection committees — for the Chief Election Commissioner, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Central Vigilance Commissioner, and the Chairperson of the National Human Rights Commission — ensuring bipartisan oversight of constitutional appointments.

Deputy Speaker

Article 93 mandates the election of a Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha “as soon as may be” after the House constitutes. The Deputy Speaker presides in the Speaker’s absence, chairs the Committee on Private Members’ Bills and Resolutions, and by convention chairs the Committee on Privileges. The post has remained vacant for extended periods (e.g., 2014‑2019, 2019‑2024), raising concerns about institutional continuity. The Rajya Sabha’s Deputy Chairman, elected under Article 89, performs analogous functions.

Functional Impact

The LoP’s role in the selection committees is a critical check on executive dominance. In recent years, the LoP’s dissenting notes on key appointments have been recorded, illustrating the institutional weight of the position. The Deputy Speaker’s absence deprives the House of a neutral presiding officer for the Privileges Committee and reduces the Speaker’s workload distribution. A recent Report of the Committee on Rules recommended a constitutional amendment to fix a timeline for the election of the Deputy Speaker, a proposal pending political consensus.

Comparative Perspective

In the United Kingdom, the Leader of the Opposition is a recognised constitutional figure with a statutory salary since 1937 and a formal role in the Privy Council. Judicial Review of Parliamentary Privileges: Landmark Judgments and Emerging Jurisprudence

The Constitution grants each House of Parliament certain privileges under Article 105, but does not enumerate them exhaustively. The Supreme Court has held that parliamentary privileges are subject to fundamental rights and judicial review, albeit with a high threshold. In Keshav Singh v. Speaker, Legislative Assembly (1965), the Court ruled that a House cannot punish a person for contempt if the act does not constitute a breach of privilege recognised at the commencement of the Constitution, establishing that the power to punish is not absolute and must conform to natural justice.

In Searchlight v. State of Bihar (1959), the Court held that the publication of a substantially true report of parliamentary proceedings, even with expunged portions omitted, does not amount to breach of privilege, reinforcing the principle that freedom of the press under Article 19(1)(a) prevails over an expansive claim of privilege. The Raja Ram Pal v. Hon’ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007) case clarified that the judiciary can examine whether a claimed privilege is “necessary for the discharge of its functions” and whether the procedure followed meets fairness standards, and that the expulsion of a member for “unparliamentary conduct” is justiciable when it affects fundamental rights.

In a recent decision, the Supreme Court re-examined the scope of Article 122, which bars courts from inquiring into the validity of parliamentary proceedings on the ground of alleged procedural irregularity. The Court reiterated that Article 122 does not oust judicial review when the challenge is based on a violation of constitutional provisions or fundamental rights, distinguishing procedural irregularities from substantive constitutional breaches. The emerging jurisprudence thus balances parliamentary autonomy with constitutional supremacy, ensuring that privileges remain a shield for legislative functioning rather than a sword against accountability.

Business Advisory Committee: Agenda Control, Time Allocation and Opposition Leverage

The Business Advisory Committee (BAC) is the principal instrument for managing the legislative calendar of each House. Constituted under Rule 331 of the Lok Sabha Rules and Rule 236 of the Rajya Sabha Rules, the BAC consists of the Speaker or Chairman as ex‑officio chairperson, the Leader of the House, the Leader of the Opposition, and a few other members nominated by the presiding officer to reflect party strength. Its primary function is to allocate time for government bills, private members’ bills, discussions, and other business, and to recommend the order in which items are taken up.

In practice, the BAC meets at the beginning of each session and thereafter as needed. The government typically secures the bulk of time for its legislative agenda, but the presence of the Leader of the Opposition (or the leader of the largest opposition party when the LoP post is vacant) ensures that opposition priorities — such as debates on price rise, agrarian distress, or alleged misuse of agencies — can be slotted. The committee’s recommendations are not binding on the House, but the presiding officer almost always follows them, making the BAC a de facto agenda‑setting body.

The BAC also decides on the allocation of “short duration discussions”, “calling attention motions”, and “zero hour” mentions, thereby influencing the intensity of executive scrutiny. When the opposition holds a significant number of seats, it can negotiate for dedicated debate slots on contentious issues, effectively using the BAC as a platform for political bargaining. Conversely, a dominant ruling party may marginalise opposition demands by packing the committee with its nominees, a concern highlighted in the 17th Lok Sabha (2019‑2024) where the Deputy Speaker’s post remained vacant and the BAC’s composition was perceived as skewed.

Transparency of BAC proceedings is limited; minutes are not routinely published, and the criteria for time allocation are not codified beyond the rules. This opacity has prompted calls from civil‑society groups and a recent report of the Committee on Rules for a more structured, rule‑based framework that mandates a minimum quota for opposition‑initiated business, akin to the “Opposition Day” in the UK House of Commons.

A question asks: “The Business Advisory Committee’s recommendations are binding on the House.” True or False?

Committee on Petitions: Grievance Redressal Mechanism and Its Effectiveness

The Committee on Petitions, established in 1952 under Rule 305 of the Lok Sabha Rules (and Rule 212 of the Rajya Sabha Rules), examines petitions signed by citizens on matters of public interest that fall within the legislative competence of Parliament. The committee comprises 15 members in the Lok Sabha and 10 in the Rajya Sabha, nominated by the Speaker and Chairman respectively, reflecting party proportions. Its mandate includes: (i) considering petitions referred to it by the House; (ii) reporting on the grievances raised; and (iii) suggesting remedial legislative or administrative action.

Petitions may be submitted by any citizen, either individually or collectively, and must be addressed to the Speaker or Chairman. The committee does not adjudicate; it forwards its findings to the concerned ministry, which is expected to respond within a stipulated period. Over the decades, the committee has dealt with issues ranging from land acquisition disputes, environmental clearances, pension anomalies, to allegations of misuse of central agencies. Its reports are tabled in the House, but there is no statutory obligation on the executive to implement recommendations.

Effectiveness is mixed. The committee’s strength lies in providing a formal parliamentary platform for citizen grievances, compelling ministries to furnish explanations on record. However, the absence of enforcement powers, limited staff, and the sheer volume of petitions — often running into thousands per session — constrain thorough scrutiny. A 2018 report of the Committee on Rules recommended digitising the petition process, setting time‑bound response norms for ministries, and granting the committee the power to summon officials for oral evidence; these proposals remain unimplemented.

Constitutional Amendment Procedure: Special Majorities, State Ratification and the President’s Assent

Article 368 governs the amendment of the Constitution and distinguishes categories of amendments based on the subject matter. First, amendments that affect the federal structure — such as the election of the President (Article 54), the extent of executive power of the Union and States (Articles 73, 162), the representation of States in Parliament (Articles 80, 81), and the provisions of Article 368 itself — require a special majority of two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House, and ratification by the legislatures of not less than one‑half of the States by a simple majority.

Second, amendments that do not touch the federal provisions but alter other parts of the Constitution — for example, the amendment of Fundamental Rights (Part III), or the Directive Principles (Part IV) — need only the special majority in each House (two‑thirds of members present and voting, and a majority of the total membership of each House). No state ratification is required.

Third, a small set of provisions — such as the creation of new States (Article 3), the abolition or creation of Legislative Councils in States (Article 169), the administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribes (Fifth and Sixth Schedules), and the salaries of the President, Vice‑President, and Governors — can be amended by a simple majority through ordinary legislation under the relevant articles; the Constitution explicitly excludes them from the Article 368 procedure.

After passage by both Houses (and state ratification where applicable), the bill is presented to the President. Under Article 368(2), the President must give assent; there is no pocket veto or discretionary power. The 24th Amendment (1971) clarified that the President’s assent is obligatory, removing any ambiguity created by the earlier practice. Notably, there is no provision for a joint sitting of the two Houses to resolve a deadlock on a constitutional amendment bill, unlike ordinary legislation under Article 108.

Parliamentary Diplomacy: India’s Engagement with the Inter‑Parliamentary Union and Commonwealth Parliamentary Association

Parliamentary diplomacy refers to the structured interaction between legislatures of sovereign states to promote mutual understanding, share legislative best practices, and coordinate positions on global issues. India has been a member of the Inter‑Parliamentary Union (IPU) since 1952, and has hosted the IPU Assembly twice — in New Delhi (1997) and in Hyderabad (2023). The Indian Parliamentary Group (IPG), constituted under the Speaker’s chairmanship, serves as the national group for both the IPU and the Commonwealth Parliamentary Association (CPA).

Through the IPU, Indian delegations participate in the adoption of resolutions on democracy, human rights, sustainable development, and gender equality. India has leveraged the IPU platform to advocate for reform of the United Nations Security Council, climate justice, and the protection of parliamentary autonomy. In the CPA, India engages with over 180 Commonwealth legislatures, contributing to capacity‑building programmes for parliamentary staff, election observation missions, and the promotion of the “Commonwealth Parliamentary Association Benchmarks for Democratic Legislatures”.

Domestically, the IPG organises annual “Parliamentary Delegation” visits, where Indian MPs travel abroad and host foreign counterparts. These exchanges have facilitated the adoption of procedural innovations such as “Question Hour” reforms inspired by the UK House of Commons, and proposals for a “Parliamentary Budget Office” model drawn from the Canadian experience. However, critics argue that parliamentary diplomacy often remains ceremonial, with limited follow‑up on policy commitments made in multilateral forums. The 2021 report of the Committee on External Affairs recommended a formal mechanism to track implementation of IPU and CPA resolutions within Indian legislative processes.

Evolution of Parliamentary Procedures and Conventions: Adapting to Political Realities

The operational framework of Parliament extends beyond its written rules (Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business) to a complex web of unwritten conventions, precedents, and practices evolved over decades. These conventions fill gaps in formal rules, provide flexibility, and reflect the changing political landscape. For instance, the 'Zero Hour', a unique Indian parliamentary innovation, emerged organically in the 1960s as an informal device for members to raise urgent matters of public importance immediately after Question Hour, without prior notice. It gained acceptance through practice before being formally acknowledged in procedural manuals, though it remains outside the formal Rules of Procedure.

Similarly, the evolution of the 'No-Confidence Motion' reflects a shift from a general censure motion to a specific instrument for testing the government's majority. While Article 75(3) mandates collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha, the specific procedure is laid down in Rule 198 of the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha. Its strategic use by the opposition has evolved into a potent political tool. The Speaker's discretion in admitting such motions, especially regarding timing and substance, has developed through numerous rulings and precedents.

The role of the Business Advisory Committee (BAC) in allocating time for legislative and other business is another example of an evolving convention. While its recommendations are advisory, they are usually adopted by the House, reflecting a consensus-building approach among party leaders. However, in periods of heightened political polarization, the BAC's ability to forge consensus can be strained, leading to more frequent resort to the 'guillotine' – a procedural device to put outstanding demands for grants to vote without discussion due to time constraints. This highlights how procedural mechanisms adapt, or sometimes fail to adapt, to the prevailing political climate.

The increasing frequency of disruptions and adjournments has also led to informal understandings and interventions by presiding officers to restore order, often drawing upon past precedents. The 'Code of Conduct' for members, while not strictly a procedural rule, is an evolving convention aimed at maintaining decorum. The shift from single-party dominance to coalition governments in the late 20th and early 21st centuries significantly impacted parliamentary conventions, necessitating greater consensus-building, more frequent inter-party consultations, and a nuanced approach to legislative management. The dynamics of parliamentary functioning are thus a continuous interplay between formal rules, unwritten conventions, and ever-changing political realities.

The Speaker's Office: Navigating Political Pressures and Ensuring Procedural Integrity

The Speaker of the Lok Sabha, and the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, are not merely presiding officers but pivotal figures in maintaining the dignity, decorum, and procedural integrity of their respective Houses. While the Constitution mandates their impartiality (Article 93 for Speaker, Article 89 for Chairman), political realities often challenge this ideal. The Speaker, typically elected from among the members of the Lok Sabha, often has a long-standing party affiliation, which can lead to perceptions of bias, especially in contentious rulings or decisions regarding the Anti-Defection Law.

The Speaker's discretionary powers are extensive. These include admitting or rejecting adjournment motions, no-confidence motions, and questions of privilege; certifying a bill as a Money Bill (Article 110(3)), a decision constitutionally declared final; and exercising the casting vote in case of a tie. The Speaker also has the power to discipline unruly members, including suspending them from the House. The exercise of these powers, particularly in politically charged situations, often invites scrutiny and criticism from the opposition, testing the Speaker's ability to rise above partisan considerations.

A critical aspect of the Speaker's role involves decisions under the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law). The Speaker acts as the tribunal for disqualification petitions, and while these decisions are subject to judicial review (Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu, 1992), the initial ruling carries significant weight and can have profound political consequences. Delays in deciding such petitions have often been a point of contention, raising questions about the Speaker's impartiality and the effectiveness of the anti-defection mechanism.

Furthermore, the Speaker is the administrative head of the Lok Sabha Secretariat and the principal spokesperson of the House. They represent the House in its relations with the President, the Rajya Sabha, and other authorities. The Speaker's role in guiding parliamentary delegations abroad (as head of the Indian Parliamentary Group) and in presiding over conferences of presiding officers further underscores the multifaceted nature of the office. The challenge for any Speaker lies in balancing the need to facilitate government business with ensuring adequate space for the opposition, maintaining order, and upholding the constitutional spirit of parliamentary democracy, often under intense political pressure.

Examiner's trap
Consider the following statement: "The Speaker's decision to certify a bill as a Money Bill is final and cannot be challenged in any court." Evaluate the accuracy of this statement in light of constitutional provisions and judicial pronouncements.
The statement is largely accurate, but with a nuanced constitutional backing. Article 110(3) of the Constitution explicitly states that "If any question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill or not, the decision of the Speaker of the House of the People thereon shall be final." This provision grants the Speaker absolute authority in this matter, making their certification conclusive. Historically, courts have generally refrained from interfering with the Speaker's decision on this specific issue, respecting the constitutional mandate of parliamentary autonomy. While the Supreme Court in Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007) affirmed that parliamentary proceedings are generally subject to judicial review on grounds of illegality or unconstitutionality, it did not specifically overturn the finality clause of Article 110(3) regarding Money Bills. The intent behind this finality is to prevent obstruction of financial legislation by the Rajya Sabha. Therefore, while judicial review can broadly scrutinize parliamentary actions, the Speaker's certification of a Money Bill remains a strong exception due to the explicit constitutional provision.
Strategic Analysis of Constitutional Provisions
When encountering constitutional provisions that declare a decision "final" (e.g., Speaker's decision on Money Bill, President's decision on disqualification of members under Article 103), always consider the broader context of judicial review. While such clauses aim to limit judicial intervention, landmark judgments (like Kihoto Hollohan or Raja Ram Pal) have established that even parliamentary actions are not entirely immune from judicial scrutiny on grounds of illegality, unconstitutionality, or procedural impropriety. The key is to identify *which specific aspects* remain beyond review and *why* (e.g., explicit constitutional bar, matter of internal parliamentary procedure) versus those that have been brought under judicial purview.

Parliamentary Oversight of Executive Policy Implementation: Depth and Limitations

Beyond immediate accountability mechanisms like Question Hour and various motions, Parliament exercises continuous oversight over executive policy implementation primarily through its committee system. Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) scrutinize the demands for grants of ministries, review bills referred to them, and examine annual reports. The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) play a crucial role in scrutinizing government expenditure and the functioning of Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) based on reports from the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG). The Committee on Government Assurances monitors the implementation of promises made by ministers on the floor of the House.

However, the depth and effectiveness of this oversight face several limitations. First, committees often face a severe crunch of time and resources. They lack independent research staff comparable to their counterparts in many developed democracies, relying heavily on information provided by the very ministries they are scrutinizing. This information asymmetry can hinder thorough investigation. Second, the recommendations of parliamentary committees are generally advisory and not binding on the executive. While the government is expected to provide 'Action Taken Reports' (ATRs), there is no strict mechanism to compel compliance, and delays in submitting ATRs are common.

Third, the sheer volume and complexity of government business mean that committees can only scratch the surface of policy implementation. Many reports, especially those of the CAG, are presented to Parliament long after the events they describe, making timely corrective action difficult. Fourth, executive dominance, particularly when a single party enjoys a comfortable majority, can dilute the effectiveness of oversight. Party lines often prevail in committee deliberations, reducing the scope for non-partisan scrutiny. Finally, the declining number of sittings of Parliament and the increasing frequency of disruptions reduce the time available for discussing committee reports and holding the executive accountable on the floor of the House. Despite these limitations, parliamentary oversight remains a vital democratic check, providing a platform for detailed scrutiny and bringing executive actions into the public domain.

Party Discipline and Coalition Dynamics: Shaping Legislative Outcomes

The functioning of Parliament is profoundly influenced by the dynamics of political parties, particularly party discipline and the complexities of coalition governance. Party discipline, enforced primarily through the party whip, ensures that members vote along party lines on most legislative matters. The Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law) further strengthens the whip's authority by penalizing defection, thereby reducing the autonomy of individual Members of Parliament (MPs) to vote according to their conscience or constituency interests on critical issues. This centralization of power within parties can streamline the legislative process for the ruling dispensation but can also stifle independent thought and robust debate.

In a single-party majority government, the legislative agenda is largely driven by the ruling party's manifesto and policy priorities. Bills are often introduced and passed with minimal amendments, as the government can rely on its numerical strength. However, the scenario changes significantly in a coalition government. Coalition dynamics introduce an additional layer of negotiation and compromise. Legislative proposals must often be vetted and agreed upon by all coalition partners, leading to potential delays, modifications, or even abandonment of bills that lack consensus. The Common Minimum Programme (CMP) often serves as the guiding document for coalition governments, outlining shared policy objectives.

The need for consensus in coalitions can sometimes lead to 'lowest common denominator' policies or, conversely, to more inclusive legislative outcomes that reflect a broader range of interests. The whip's role in a coalition becomes more complex, as it must ensure not only party discipline but also inter-party coordination. Instances of 'cross-voting' or dissent within a coalition are rare but can destabilize the government, as seen in various historical examples. The Speaker's role in managing the House in a coalition era also becomes more challenging, as they must navigate a more fragmented political landscape with multiple power centers. Ultimately, the interplay of party discipline and coalition dynamics shapes not only the passage of legislation but also the nature of parliamentary debate, the effectiveness of opposition, and the overall health of deliberative democracy.

Legislative Drafting and Quality of Law-Making: Challenges and Reforms

The effectiveness and enforceability of laws hinge significantly on the quality of legislative drafting. In India, the primary responsibility for drafting legislation rests with the Legislative Department of the Ministry of Law and Justice, which assists various ministries in formulating bills. However, the process often faces several challenges that impact the clarity, precision, and coherence of enacted laws. One major issue is the hurried passage of legislation, particularly towards the end of parliamentary sessions, with inadequate scrutiny by parliamentary committees or public consultation. This can lead to ambiguities, internal inconsistencies, and unintended consequences in the final statute.

Another challenge is the technical complexity of modern legislation, which often requires specialized knowledge across diverse fields. While the Legislative Department has expertise in legal language, a deeper engagement with domain experts during the drafting phase is often lacking. This can result in laws that are difficult to interpret, implement, or enforce, leading to increased litigation and administrative burden. The absence of a robust pre-legislative scrutiny mechanism, where draft bills are mandatorily placed in the public domain for comments and subjected to detailed expert review before introduction in Parliament, further exacerbates these issues.

Several reforms have been proposed to enhance the quality of law-making. One key suggestion is the establishment of an independent 'Parliamentary Budget Office' or a dedicated 'Legislative Drafting Service' with specialized staff, similar to models in other democracies. This would provide independent, expert assistance to MPs and committees, improving the technical quality of bills and amendments. Enhancing the capacity of Department-Related Standing Committees to undertake detailed clause-by-clause scrutiny of bills, including inviting expert testimony and public feedback, is another crucial reform. Furthermore, mandating pre-legislative consultation policies, where draft bills are compulsorily published for public comments for a specified period, would ensure greater transparency and public participation in the law-making process. Such reforms are essential to ensure that laws are not only constitutionally sound but also effective, clear, and reflective of societal needs.

Office of Profit Disqualification: Constitutional Framework, Judicial Tests and Legislative Responses

Articles 102(1)(a) and 191(1)(a) disqualify a person from Parliament or a State Legislature if they hold an office of profit under the Government of India or a State, unless exempted by law. The Constitution does not define "office of profit"; the Supreme Court has evolved a multi-factor test. In Pradyut Bordoloi v. Swapan Roy (2001), the Court identified five indicia: (i) government appointment; (ii) government power to remove; (iii) government remuneration; (iv) governmental functions; (v) patronage or influence. No single factor is decisive; the totality of circumstances determines the character of the office.

The Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959, as amended, lists exempted offices. The 2006 amendment, passed retrospectively, was challenged in Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (2009). The Court upheld it but ruled the exemption power cannot be exercised arbitrarily; classification must satisfy Article 14. The Court held that "office of profit" must be read in the context of separation of powers: holding an executive office creates a conflict of interest and undermines legislative independence.

State legislatures have similar exemption laws. In Jaya Bachchan v. Union of India (2006), the Court held that the Chairperson of the Uttar Pradesh Film Development Council was an office of profit, as it carried remuneration, government appointment, and governmental functions, rejecting the "merely advisory" argument. In a 2019 Madras High Court ruling, the Chairperson of a government-owned company was held not to automatically hold an office of profit if remuneration is nominal and functions non-executive. The Election Commission, under Articles 103/192, gives its opinion on disqualification, which the President/Governor must follow; this opinion is subject to judicial review for mala fides, perversity, or violation of natural justice.

Recent controversies involve Parliamentary Secretaries in Delhi, Punjab, and West Bengal, treated as offices of profit by the Election Commission and courts. The Delhi High Court (2018) set aside the appointment of 20 AAP MLAs as Parliamentary Secretaries. The Supreme Court has stressed that the exemption power must be used sparingly and transparently. The Law Commission's 250th Report (2014) recommended reviewing exemption lists, suggesting only purely ceremonial offices with no executive function or pecuniary benefit be exempted. The tension between executive accommodation and legislative independence persists.

Delegated Legislation: Parliamentary Control Mechanisms Beyond Committee Scrutiny

Delegated legislation — rules, regulations, bye-laws, orders, and notifications made by the executive under parliamentary authority — constitutes the bulk of daily governance. The Constitution permits delegation subject to the "essential legislative functions" doctrine: Parliament cannot delegate core policy-making but may delegate ancillary matters. The Supreme Court in Hamid Khan v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1974) and Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India (1985) held that excessive delegation without adequate guidelines violates the non-delegation principle inherent in the constitutional scheme.

Parliament exercises control through three mechanisms. First, the laying requirement: most parent Acts require delegated legislation to be laid before each House. The General Clauses Act, 1897 (Section 23) and House Rules provide it must be laid as soon as possible after making. Second, the affirmative resolution procedure: critical delegated legislation (e.g., certain tax notifications, Essential Commodities Act rules) takes effect only after both Houses approve. Third, the negative resolution procedure: the common mode, where legislation takes effect on lapsing but ceases if either House disapproves within a specified period (usually 30 sitting days). The parent Act determines the applicable procedure.

The Committee on Subordinate Legislation (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) examines every instrument for conformity with delegated power, retrospective taxation, judicial review exclusion, and unusual provisions. Its recommendations are advisory; binding power lies in the resolution procedures. Negative resolutions are rarely moved due to time constraints and government majority. The Lok Sabha has passed a disapproval motion only once (1964, Indian Post Office Act rule). The Rajya Sabha has done so a few times. The government often uses the "guillotine" on financial business to avoid debate.

Judicial review provides a parallel check. Courts can strike down delegated legislation for ultra vires, fundamental rights violations, arbitrariness (Article 14), or procedural defects (failure to consult, publish, or lay). In Kerala Samasthana Chetu Thozhilali Union v. State of Kerala (2006), non-laying as mandated rendered a rule unenforceable. In Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. v. Essar Power Ltd. (2008), the Court held laying is mandatory, not directory. The Law Commission (2002) recommended making the affirmative procedure the default for significant delegated legislation, creating a "Delegated Legislation Monitoring Cell" in the Parliament Secretariat, and mandating regulatory impact assessment. These reforms remain unimplemented.

Application Scenario
A Ministry issues a notification under Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, imposing stock limits on onions. The parent Act requires such notifications to be laid before Parliament and cease to operate if either House passes a resolution disapproving them within 30 sitting days. The notification is laid on Day 1 of the Monsoon Session. On Day 15, the Opposition moves a disapproval resolution in the Rajya Sabha. The government argues that since the notification relates to price control, it is a "financial matter" and the Rajya Sabha cannot vote on it. Is the government's contention correct?
No. The government's contention is incorrect. The Rajya Sabha has equal power with the Lok Sabha in the negative resolution procedure for delegated legislation. The classification of a notification as a "financial matter" is relevant only for Money Bills (Article 110) and Financial Bills (Article 117). Delegated legislation control is a distinct parliamentary function under the parent Act's procedural provisions. The Rajya Sabha can debate and vote on the disapproval resolution. If passed, the notification ceases to have effect. The government's argument confuses Money Bill procedure with delegated legislation oversight.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about parliamentary control over delegated legislation, distinguish between the Committee's scrutiny (advisory, continuous) and the House's resolution power (binding, time-bound). Remember: negative resolution = most common; affirmative resolution = higher threshold (prior approval); laying = mandatory precondition. Cite the General Clauses Act, 1897 (Section 23) for the default laying rule. For judicial review, recall the ultra vires doctrine and the mandatory nature of laying (Gujarat Urja Vikas case).

Parliamentary Scrutiny of Constitutional Bodies: CAG, Election Commission, Finance Commission and Regulators

Constitutional bodies — the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG), the Election Commission (EC), the Finance Commission (FC), the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC), and the National Commissions for SCs, STs, BCs, and Minorities — are independent of the executive but accountable to Parliament. Their reports are laid before Parliament under specific provisions (Article 151 for CAG, Article 324 for EC, Article 281 for FC). Scrutiny mechanisms vary and are often less rigorous than for ministries.

The CAG's audit reports (Appropriation Accounts, Finance Accounts, Performance Audits) are examined by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU). The PAC, chaired by an Opposition member by convention, selects audit paragraphs, summons officials, and presents reports with recommendations. The government responds through Action Taken Reports (ATRs). This is the most structured accountability loop. However, CAG reports on regulatory bodies, autonomous institutions, and PPP projects often receive less attention due to workload. The 2nd ARC (2007) recommended expanding the PAC's mandate to cover all CAG reports and forming a dedicated sub-committee for performance audits.

The Election Commission's annual report is laid under Article 324(5). No dedicated committee scrutinises it; discussion occurs during the General Discussion on the Budget or Short Duration Discussions. The EC's independence is protected by Article 324(5) (removal like a Supreme Court judge), but accountability is largely informal. The Law Commission (255th Report, 2015) recommended a dedicated Parliamentary Committee on Electoral Reforms to scrutinise the EC's functioning, expenditure, and recommendations. The EC's expenditure is charged on the Consolidated Fund, bypassing the Demand for Grants process, reducing financial oversight.

The Finance Commission's report, with the Explanatory Memorandum, is laid under Article 281. It is usually discussed during the Budget session. Recommendations on vertical/horizontal devolution, grants-in-aid, and fiscal consolidation are critical for federal finances, but debate often lacks technical depth. The 14th FC (2015) recommended a Standing Committee on Fiscal Federalism to scrutinise FC reports and monitor implementation; this has not been done.

Regulators (TRAI, SEBI, RBI, CCI, PFRDA, etc.) are statutory bodies exercising quasi-legislative, quasi-judicial, and executive powers. Their annual reports are laid under their parent Acts. Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) of the parent ministry examine these reports. However, regulators often resist detailed questioning citing operational autonomy. The Supreme Court in Securities and Exchange Board of India v. Rakesh Agrawal (2003) recognised regulators' autonomy but affirmed legislative supremacy. The Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission (FSLRC, 2013) recommended a unified Financial Sector Appellate Tribunal and a Financial Stability and Development Council with parliamentary accountability. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, created the IBBI with a mandatory annual report to Parliament. The trend is toward stronger statutory accountability, but enforcement depends on committee diligence.

Tenth Schedule Disqualification: Speaker's Adjudicatory Role, Judicial Review and Reform Proposals

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985) and modified by the 91st Amendment (2003), provides for disqualification on grounds of defection. Paragraph 6 vests the power to decide disqualification in the Chairman/Speaker, whose decision is "final". The 91st Amendment deleted Paragraph 7, which barred judicial review, restoring the Supreme Court's and High Courts' jurisdiction under Articles 32, 136, 226, and 227. The Speaker acts as a tribunal when adjudicating under the Tenth Schedule.

In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Court upheld the Tenth Schedule's validity but held the Speaker's decision subject to judicial review for mala fides, perversity, violation of constitutional mandate, and non-compliance with natural justice. The "finality" clause does not immunise the order. In Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994), the Court expanded "voluntarily giving up membership" to include conduct indicating abandonment of party allegiance. In G. Viswanathan v. Speaker, Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly (1996), the Court held an expelled member continues to belong to the original party; joining another party attracts disqualification.

The 91st Amendment (2003) made critical changes: (i) deleted the "split" provision (Paragraph 3) allowing one-third to split; (ii) retained the "merger" provision (Paragraph 4) requiring two-thirds agreement; (iii) barred disqualified members from ministerial or remunerative political posts until re-elected; (iv) limited the Council of Ministers to 15% of House strength. Despite these, the Speaker's partisan conduct remains a concern. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Court directed the Speaker to decide petitions within a reasonable time (four weeks) and suggested Parliament consider vesting adjudication in an independent tribunal (e.g., the Election Commission or a retired judge).

In Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2019), the Court held the Speaker cannot disqualify a member beyond the remainder of the House's term, as this bars re-election. The Speaker's power is quasi-judicial and must follow natural justice: notice, hearing, reasoned order. The Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra political crises (2019-2022) highlighted delays, selective disposal, and the Speaker's dual role. The Law Commission (170th Report, 1999), 2nd ARC (2007), Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990), and Venkatachaliah Commission (2002) recommended transferring adjudication to the Election Commission or an independent tribunal. No government has acted, as the power to control defections remains a valuable political tool.

Parliamentary Procedure During Emergency: Special Sessions, Approval Requirements and Judicial Oversight

The Constitution provides for three emergencies: National Emergency (Article 352), State Emergency/President's Rule (Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360). Each triggers specific parliamentary procedures modifying the legislative calendar and federal balance. Under Article 352, a Proclamation must be laid before each House and ceases unless approved within one month by a special majority (majority of total membership and two-thirds present and voting). If the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the Rajya Sabha's approval suffices for 30 days after the new Lok Sabha meets. The 44th Amendment (1978) added safeguards: the Proclamation must be based on a written Cabinet decision; approval requires special majority; the Lok Sabha can disapprove by simple majority (Article 352(7)); and the Proclamation must be reviewed every six months.

During a National Emergency, Parliament's legislative competence extends to State List subjects (Article 250). Such laws cease six months after the Emergency ends. The House can meet in special sessions; the President can summon Parliament under Article 85(1) with no six-month gap. The 42nd Amendment (1976) extended the Lok Sabha's term by one year during Emergency; the 44th Amendment reversed this, limiting extension to one year at a time, not beyond six months after the Emergency ends. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA) and the Defence of India Rules were enacted under Emergency powers in the 1970s.

Under Article 356, the Proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months. If approved, it lasts six months, extendable up to three years with parliamentary approval every six months. Beyond one year, extension requires a National Emergency or the Election Commission's certification that elections cannot be held (44th Amendment). In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court held the Proclamation subject to judicial review; the President's satisfaction must be based on relevant material; the State Assembly cannot be dissolved before parliamentary approval; secularism is a basic structure feature, so a Proclamation dismissing a government on religious grounds is invalid; and the "floor test" is the proper method to ascertain majority.

Article 360 (Financial Emergency) has never been invoked. If proclaimed, it must be approved by both Houses within two months. During its operation, the Union can direct states to observe financial propriety, reduce salaries of judges and civil servants, and reserve Money Bills for Presidential consideration. The 44th Amendment removed the bar on judicial review for Financial Emergency. Parliamentary approval for all three emergencies involves both Houses voting separately; there is no joint sitting provision. The Rajya Sabha's role is critical when the Lok Sabha is dissolved, as it becomes the sole parliamentary check. The 44th Amendment's special majority requirement for Article 352 and six-monthly review are designed to prevent the executive from normalising emergency rule. The 1975-77 experience remains the benchmark for evaluating these safeguards.

Parliamentary Secretariat and the Secretary‑General: Administrative Backbone of Legislative Business

The Secretariat of each House, headed by a Secretary‑General, is the permanent, non‑partisan machinery that enables Parliament to function continuously despite changes in political composition. The Secretary‑General of the Lok Sabha and the Secretary‑General of the Rajya Sabha are appointed by the President on the recommendation of the respective Presiding Officers, and they hold office until the age of 60 years or until they resign, whichever is earlier. Their rank is equivalent to that of a Secretary to the Government of India, with corresponding pay, allowances and pensionary benefits.

The Secretariat performs three broad categories of work. First, it provides procedural advice to the Speaker or Chairman on the interpretation of rules, precedents and constitutional provisions — drafting the List of Business, preparing the agenda for the Business Advisory Committee, and advising on the admissibility of motions, questions and amendments. Second, it manages the legislative pipeline: bills received from the government are examined for constitutional compliance, formatted, printed and circulated; amendments are processed; and the final authenticated text is prepared for Presidential assent. Third, it services the committee system — scheduling meetings, preparing background notes, recording minutes and ensuring that committee reports are presented to the House within prescribed time‑frames.

In addition, the Secretariat maintains the parliamentary library, the research and reference division, and the parliamentary reporting service (the Official Report). The research wing supplies members with non‑partisan briefs on policy issues, statistical data and comparative legislative practices, which is especially valuable during the consideration of complex legislation. The reporting service produces verbatim records of proceedings, which constitute the authoritative source for parliamentary privilege cases and for judicial review of legislative proceedings.

The Secretary‑General also acts as the custodian of parliamentary privileges. When a question of breach of privilege arises, the Secretariat conducts the preliminary inquiry, compiles the relevant records and assists the Committee of Privileges in its deliberations. The Secretariat’s impartiality is protected by the fact that its officers are not subject to the direction of the executive; they serve the House as an institution. This institutional independence has been reinforced by judicial pronouncements holding that the Secretary‑General’s functions are integral to the autonomy of Parliament and cannot be curtailed by executive order.

Recent reforms have focused on digitising secretariat workflows. The e‑Parliament initiative integrates bill tracking, question management and committee scheduling on a single platform, reducing paper usage and enabling real‑time access for members. The Secretariat also coordinates with the Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs for the smooth conduct of sessions, especially during sittings when the legislative agenda is dense. Despite these upgrades, challenges remain: the Secretariat’s staffing levels have not kept pace with the growth in legislative output, and the reliance on contractual researchers sometimes affects continuity in policy analysis.

Parliamentary Budget Office: International Models, Indian Proposals and Feasibility

A Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) is an independent, non‑partisan body that provides legislatures with objective analysis of fiscal policy, macro‑economic forecasts and cost estimates of legislative proposals. The concept originated in the United States with the Congressional Budget Office (established 1974) and has since been adopted in Canada (Parliamentary Budget Officer, 2006), the United Kingdom (Office for Budget Responsibility, 2010), Australia (Parliamentary Budget Office, 2012) and South Africa (Parliamentary Budget Office, 2013). These offices share common features: statutory independence, a mandate to publish baseline economic and fiscal projections, and the authority to cost private members’ bills and government proposals on request.

In India, the demand for a PBO has been articulated by the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2008), the 15th Finance Commission (2020) and several parliamentary committees, including the Standing Committee on Finance (2017). The primary rationale is that the Executive currently controls the fiscal data pipeline — the Union Budget, the Medium‑Term Fiscal Policy Statement and the FRBM disclosures — while Parliament relies on the same data without an independent verification mechanism. A PBO would enable members to scrutinise revenue assumptions, expenditure ceilings and debt sustainability with their own analytical capacity.

Design proposals for an Indian PBO differ on institutional placement. One model suggests locating it under the Speaker’s authority, reporting directly to the Lok Sabha, with a parallel unit for the Rajya Sabha. Another proposes a joint statutory body accountable to both Houses, headed by a Director‑General appointed by a bipartisan selection committee comprising the Speaker, the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, the Leader of the Opposition in both Houses and the Finance Minister. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a “light‑touch” PBO initially staffed by a modest team of analysts, drawing on the existing expertise of institutions such as the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy and the Reserve Bank of India’s research department.

Key functional requirements include: (a) an annual Economic and Fiscal Outlook published before the Budget session; (b) cost‑estimates for all government bills with a financial memorandum, delivered within a defined turnaround; (c) on‑demand costing of private members’ bills; and (d) post‑implementation evaluation of major schemes. The PBO would also be tasked with monitoring compliance with the FRBM Act’s fiscal targets and flagging deviations in real time.

Feasibility challenges are both political and operational. Politically, the Executive may resist an institution that could publicly question its fiscal assumptions. Operationally, recruiting economists with public‑finance expertise willing to work under parliamentary salary scales is difficult. Moreover, the PBO’s credibility hinges on guaranteed access to granular fiscal data — currently dispersed across ministries, the Controller General of Accounts and the Comptroller and Auditor General. Legislative amendments to the FRBM Act and the Rules of Procedure would be required to mandate data sharing and to protect the PBO’s outputs from executive interference.

Examiner's twist
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about the proposed Parliamentary Budget Office in India is correct? (a) It will be a part of the Ministry of Finance. (b) It will be established by a constitutional amendment. (c) It will provide independent cost‑estimates for legislative proposals. (d) It will replace the Comptroller and Auditor General.”
The correct answer is (c). The PBO is envisaged as an independent parliamentary body, not under the Ministry of Finance, and does not require a constitutional amendment — a statutory enactment or a resolution of both Houses suffices. It complements, not replaces, the CAG, whose mandate is audit of accounts after expenditure, whereas the PBO focuses on ex‑ante fiscal analysis and costing of proposals.

Committee on Papers Laid on the Table: Scrutiny of Reports from Statutory Regulators and Autonomous Bodies

The Committee on Papers Laid on the Table (CPLT) is a standing committee in each House tasked with examining all documents that are required by law to be laid before Parliament. These documents include annual reports and audited accounts of statutory regulators (such as SEBI, TRAI, CERC), autonomous bodies (like the UGC, ICMR), and public sector undertakings not covered by the Committee on Public Undertakings. The CPLT’s mandate derives from Rule 331 of the Lok Sabha Rules and Rule 219 of the Rajya Sabha Rules.

The Committee’s work proceeds in two stages. First, it verifies whether the prescribed documents have been laid within the statutory time‑frame — usually within nine months of the close of the financial year. Delays are reported to the House, and the concerned ministry is asked to explain. Second, the Committee examines the content of the reports for compliance with the enabling legislation, financial propriety, and policy outcomes. It may call for additional information, summon officials, and hold hearings. Its observations are compiled in a report presented to the House, often including recommendations for legislative amendments or executive action.

Unlike department‑related standing committees, the CPLT does not have a fixed subject‑matter jurisdiction; its scope is defined by the laying requirements scattered across dozens of statutes. This makes its workload highly variable. In recent Lok Sabhas, the CPLT has examined a large volume of papers, ranging from the annual report of the National Green Tribunal to the audited accounts of the National Highways Authority of India. The Committee’s reports have highlighted recurring issues: persistent vacancies in regulatory boards, delays in tariff determination, and non‑compliance with the Right to Information Act in the functioning of autonomous bodies.

The CPLT’s effectiveness is constrained by three factors. First, it lacks the power to enforce its recommendations; it can only urge the government to act. Second, the sheer volume of papers means that detailed scrutiny is often limited to a sample, leaving many reports unexamined. Third, the Committee’s composition — usually 15 members in the Lok Sabha and 10 in the Rajya Sabha — does not always include members with specialised domain knowledge, although the Secretariat provides research support. Recent proposals from the Committee on Rules suggest creating thematic sub‑committees within the CPLT to deepen sectoral expertise.

Judicial pronouncements have reinforced the CPLT’s role. In Union of India v. R. K. Jain (1993), the Supreme Court observed that the laying of reports before Parliament is a constitutional safeguard ensuring executive accountability, and that the CPLT’s examination constitutes a vital link in that chain. The Court also held that failure to lay a report does not automatically invalidate the regulator’s actions, but it does constitute a breach of statutory duty that the Committee is empowered to flag.

Negative and Affirmative Resolution Procedures for Delegated Legislation: Operational Gaps and Judicial Intervention

Delegated legislation — rules, regulations, orders and bye‑laws made by the executive under authority conferred by an Act of Parliament — is subject to parliamentary control through the “laying” mechanism. The parent statute typically prescribes one of two resolution procedures: negative resolution (the instrument lies on the table for a specified period, usually 30 sitting days, and ceases to have effect if either House passes a resolution disapproving it) or affirmative resolution (the instrument cannot come into force unless both Houses pass a resolution approving it). The General Clauses Act, 1897, and the respective Rules of Procedure of each House codify these procedures.

In practice, the negative resolution route dominates. Most rule‑making powers — for instance, under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, the Companies Act, 2013, and the Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 — are subject to negative resolution. The affirmative route is reserved for matters of exceptional significance, such as the notification of a new tax rate under the GST Act or the declaration of a notified area under the Special Economic Zones Act, 2005. The procedural steps are: (i) the minister lays the instrument; (ii) the Secretariat circulates it to members; (iii) a motion for disapproval (negative) or approval (affirmative) may be moved within the prescribed period; (iv) if no motion is moved, the instrument is deemed to have parliamentary acquiescence.

Operational gaps undermine this control. First, the sheer volume of instruments — thousands of statutory rules are laid annually — makes it impossible for members to scrutinise each one. The Business Advisory Committee allocates limited time for “discussion on statutory rules”, often clubbing dozens of instruments into a single debate. Second, the negative resolution period is counted in “sitting days”, which can be stretched by adjournments, effectively giving the executive a longer window. Third, there is no institutional mechanism to track whether a disapproval motion has been moved; the onus rests entirely on vigilant members.

Judicial intervention has clarified the consequences of non‑compliance. The Supreme Court in K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1969) held that failure to lay a rule before Parliament does not automatically render it void unless the enabling Act expressly makes laying a condition precedent to validity. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977), the Court ruled that a rule made under a statute requiring affirmative resolution cannot be enforced until the resolution is passed. More recently, the Court has emphasised that the laying requirement is a constitutional safeguard under Article 13(3)(a) and that systematic non‑compliance may attract judicial review for violation of the rule of law.

Reform proposals include: (a) a mandatory “regulatory impact statement” attached to each instrument, summarising its fiscal and administrative implications; (b) a digital tracker, accessible to all members, showing the status of every laid instrument and the expiry of the resolution period; (c) empowering the Committee on Subordinate Legislation to issue “advisory opinions” on the vires of instruments before the resolution period ends; and (d) converting high‑impact delegated legislation to the affirmative route by default, as recommended by the 2nd ARC.

Parliamentary Privilege versus Media Freedom: Contempt Proceedings, Judicial Balancing and Recent Cases

The tension between parliamentary privilege — especially the power to punish for contempt — and the fundamental right to freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) has produced a nuanced jurisprudence. Article 105(2) grants members immunity for anything said in the House, while Article 105(3) empowers each House to define and punish contempt. The media, as a watchdog, often publishes reports on parliamentary proceedings, leaks of committee deliberations, or commentary on members’ conduct, triggering privilege notices.

Landmark judgments established that the House’s contempt power is not absolute; it is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, excess of jurisdiction, or violation of natural justice. The courts have held that the privilege of freedom of speech inside the House does not extend to publications outside, and that the media’s right to report parliamentary proceedings fairly and accurately is protected, provided the report is not a “misrepresentation” or “malicious distortion”.

Subsequent judgments refined the balance. The Supreme Court recognised a “qualified privilege” for media reports of parliamentary proceedings, akin to the common‑law defence of fair and accurate reporting. It has cautioned that contempt proceedings initiated by the House must not be used to stifle legitimate public criticism. In a 2017 ruling (primarily on defamation), the Court reiterated that parliamentary privilege cannot override the constitutional guarantee of free speech unless the publication constitutes a clear and present danger to the dignity or authority of the House.

Recent controversies illustrate the practical friction. In one instance, a House Committee of Privileges issued a notice to a news portal for publishing an alleged “leaked” draft report of a standing committee before its formal presentation; the matter was later dropped after the Presiding Officer advised restraint. In another, a privilege motion was referred against a television anchor for “derogatory remarks” about a member’s conduct; the Committee of Privileges recommended a reprimand, but the House adopted a milder censure, signalling a preference for self‑regulation over punitive action.

The procedural framework for privilege motions requires a member to give notice, the Speaker/Chairman to refer it to the Committee of Privileges, and the Committee to inquire and report. The media is not a party to the proceedings but may be summoned as a witness. The Committee’s recommendations are advisory; the House decides the final punishment, which may range from admonition to imprisonment (rarely invoked). An amendment to the Rules of Procedure introduced a “cooling‑off” period before a privilege motion can be taken up, aimed at reducing politically motivated notices.

Scholarly consensus suggests that a codified “Parliamentary Privileges Act” — as recommended by the 2nd ARC and the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (2002) — would delineate the contours of contempt, define “fair and accurate reporting”, and provide an appellate mechanism to the Supreme Court, thereby reducing ad‑hoc adjudication and enhancing both parliamentary dignity and media freedom.
