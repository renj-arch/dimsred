1. Constitutional Foundations of Centre‑State Relations

1.1 Federal Structure in the Indian Constitution

The Indian Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal system, often described as “co‑operative federalism”. While it adopts a federal pattern of division of powers between the Union and the States, the supremacy of the Union is entrenched through several provisions. Article 1 declares India a Union of States, and Article 2 empowers Parliament to admit new territories, underscoring the central authority to re‑configure the federation. The doctrine of “pith‑and‑substance” has been used by the Supreme Court to preserve the balance of power, ensuring that legislation cannot be labelled a law on a State subject merely by changing its form.

1.2 Distribution of Legislative Powers

Legislative competence is allocated through three lists in the Seventh Schedule: the Union List, the State List, and the Concurrent List. The Union List contains 97 subjects, ranging from defence and foreign affairs to atomic energy, reflecting areas where uniform national policy is essential. The State List, with 66 subjects, includes matters such as police, public health, and agriculture, allowing states to tailor policies to local conditions. The Concurrent List, comprising 47 subjects, permits both levels to legislate, but in case of conflict, the Union law prevails under Article 254. This hierarchy ensures that national imperatives can override divergent state legislation, while still preserving a sphere for state autonomy.

1.3 The Role of the President and the Governor

The President, as the constitutional head of the Union, exercises powers that affect centre‑state relations, notably the power to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session, and the authority to summon, prorogue or dissolve the Lok Sabha. The Governor, appointed by the President, acts as the Union’s representative in the state. The Governor’s discretionary powers—such as recommending President’s Rule under Article 356, or reserving certain bills for the President’s consideration—serve as a bridge between the two tiers, but have also been a source of controversy when perceived as political interference.

1.4 Evolution of Centre‑State Relations

Since independence, the balance of power has shifted. The early years witnessed a strong central bias, exemplified by the use of Article 356 in the 1950s and 1960s. The 42nd Amendment (1976) further expanded Union powers, but the Supreme Court’s landmark decision in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) curtailed the misuse of President’s Rule, emphasizing that it must be based on objective material. Subsequent constitutional amendments, notably the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992), empowered local self‑government, indirectly strengthening state autonomy by devolving functions to Panchayats and Municipalities.

2. Administrative Relations and Mechanisms of Cooperation

2.1 Inter‑State Council: Composition and Functions

The Inter‑State Council, envisaged under Article 263, was constituted by the Constitution (Inter‑State Council) Act, 1990. It comprises the Union Home Minister (as chairperson), Chief Ministers of all states, and the Lieutenant Governors of Union territories. The Council serves as a forum for discussing and resolving inter‑governmental disputes, coordinating policies on matters of common interest, and recommending reforms to improve administrative efficiency. Its recommendations, though not binding, carry significant moral weight and have been instrumental in shaping policies on water sharing, transport, and disaster management.

2.2 Mechanisms of Administrative Coordination

Administrative coordination is achieved through a network of joint committees, memoranda of understanding (MoUs), and regular meetings of senior bureaucrats. The All‑India Services—Indian Administrative Service (IAS), Indian Police Service (IPS), and Indian Forest Service (IFS)—play a pivotal role, as officers are posted both in the Union and the states, ensuring a common administrative culture. Additionally, the Central Vigilance Commission and the Comptroller and Auditor General provide oversight that transcends jurisdictional boundaries, fostering accountability.

2.3 Cooperative Federalism: Principles and Practice

Cooperative federalism rests on the principle that the Union and the states work together to achieve national objectives while respecting each other’s constitutional domains. This is evident in joint programmes such as the National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (NREGA), where central funding is complemented by state implementation. The success of such schemes depends on clear delineation of responsibilities, transparent fund flow, and robust monitoring mechanisms. When cooperation falters, disputes often arise over jurisdiction, leading to litigation or political standoffs.

2.4 Competitive Federalism: Emerging Trends

Competitive federalism encourages states to innovate and compete in policy formulation, aiming to improve service delivery and attract investment. Examples include the implementation of Goods and Services Tax (GST) reforms, where states voluntarily aligned their tax structures to create a unified market, and the adoption of the “Make in India” initiative, prompting several states to offer fiscal incentives to manufacturers. While competition can drive efficiency, it may also exacerbate disparities if wealthier states outpace poorer ones, necessitating a balancing role for the Centre.

Example Question

Discuss the role of the Finance Commission in maintaining fiscal balance between the Union and the States. Illustrate your answer with two specific recommendations made by any Finance Commission.

The Finance Commission, constituted under Article 280, is a constitutional body appointed every five years to recommend the distribution of tax revenues between the Union and the states, and to suggest measures for improving fiscal discipline. Its primary function is to ensure a fair and equitable sharing of resources, thereby reducing fiscal imbalances that could threaten the stability of the federation.

First, the Commission recommends the percentage of central taxes to be de‑centralised to the states. For instance, the 14th Finance Commission (2007‑12) suggested that 32 % of the net proceeds of taxes should be allocated to the states, a figure that reflected the need to empower states with greater fiscal autonomy.

Second, it advises on grants-in‑aid to address vertical imbalances. The 15th Finance Commission (2017‑22) introduced the “State Finance Commission” concept, recommending a “Performance‑Based Grant” to reward states that achieve higher fiscal outcomes, such as improved tax collection efficiency.

These recommendations illustrate how the Finance Commission acts as a balancing mechanism, aligning fiscal capacity with expenditure responsibilities, and fostering cooperative federalism by encouraging states to adopt prudent fiscal practices.

Exam‑Day Insight

When answering a question on centre‑state financial relations, first outline the constitutional provisions (Article 280, Finance Commission) before moving to the functional aspects (tax de‑volution, grants). This structure signals a clear grasp of both the legal framework and its practical implications.

2.5 The Inter-State Council and the Zonal Councils in Practice

Article 263 allows the President to establish an Inter-State Council to inquire into and advise upon disputes between States, to investigate subjects of common interest and to recommend better coordination of policy and action. The Council was constituted in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission and is chaired by the Prime Minister, with all Chief Ministers, the Chief Ministers of Union Territories with legislatures and six Union Ministers as members. Its role is advisory: it deliberates on the recommendations of the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions and on centre-state issues referred to it, but it has no power to bind either level of government. The five Zonal Councils, statutory bodies created by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, serve a parallel purpose in the northern, central, eastern, western and southern zones, and the North Eastern Council performs a similar function for the north-east. Both institutions illustrate the difference between cooperative machinery, which softens the edges of a federal dispute, and adjudicatory machinery, which decides it. They also sit alongside the constitutional rules on legislative relations and administrative relations that distribute competence between the Union and the States.

3. Financial Relations: Revenue Sharing and Devolution

3.1 Taxation Powers of the Union and the States

The Union enjoys exclusive rights to levy customs duties, income tax, corporation tax, and excise duties on certain goods, as enumerated in the Union List. States, on the other hand, can levy taxes on land, agricultural income, sales of goods (subject to GST), and professional taxes. The introduction of GST in 2017 merged many indirect taxes into a single national tax, creating a dual‑layer structure: the Central GST (CGST) and the State GST (SGST). While GST has streamlined tax administration, it also required a new mechanism for revenue sharing, overseen by the GST Council, which includes the Union Finance Minister and the finance ministers of all states.

3.2 The Finance Commission: Composition and Mandate

The Finance Commission is chaired by a distinguished economist or public administrator and includes members with expertise in finance, economics, and public policy. Its mandate extends beyond revenue sharing; it also advises on measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of the Union and the states, and on the principles governing grants-in‑aid. The Commission’s recommendations are binding on the Union, but the states may accept or reject specific grants, influencing the dynamics of fiscal federalism.

3.3 Devolution of Funds: Grants, Loans, and Shared Taxes

Devolution occurs through three main channels: (i) de‑centralised taxes, where a fixed share of Union taxes is transferred to states; (ii) grants-in‑aid, which are conditional or unconditional transfers to address specific needs such as education, health, or disaster relief; and (iii) loans, typically provided by the Centre for large‑scale infrastructure projects. The 14th Finance Commission introduced the concept of “de‑centralised revenue deficit” to assess the fiscal health of states, encouraging them to improve their own tax bases before relying on central assistance.

3.4 Fiscal Discipline and the Role of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)

The CAG audits both Union and state expenditures, ensuring that funds are utilised as intended. Its reports often highlight irregularities in fund utilisation, prompting corrective actions. Moreover, the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act imposes targets for deficit reduction, influencing both Union and state budgets. While the FRBM primarily binds the Union, many states have adopted similar fiscal responsibility legislations, reflecting a diffusion of fiscal prudence across the federation.

4. Dispute Resolution and Institutional Safeguards

4.1 Article 356 and President’s Rule

Article 356 empowers the President to assume control of a state’s administration when the constitutional machinery fails. Historically, this provision was misused for political ends, leading to frequent dismissals of state governments. The Supreme Court, through the S.R. Bommai judgment, introduced a stringent test of “material breach” and mandated that any proclamation of President’s Rule be subject to judicial review within two months. This safeguard has reduced arbitrary dismissals and reinforced the principle of federal autonomy.

4.2 Judicial Review of Centre‑State Conflicts

The Supreme Court acts as the ultimate arbiter of centre‑state disputes. Cases such as State of West Bengal v. Union of India (regarding the GST Council) and State of Karnataka v. Union of India (water sharing) illustrate how the Court balances constitutional intent with pragmatic governance. The doctrine of “pith‑and‑substance” allows the Court to look beyond the form of legislation to its true nature, ensuring that neither level encroaches upon the other's domain.

4.3 Role of the Inter‑State Council in Dispute Settlement

Beyond advisory functions, the Inter‑State Council facilitates dialogue on contentious issues such as river water allocation, inter‑state transport, and shared infrastructure. Its recommendations have led to the formation of specialised tribunals, for example the Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal, which, although a judicial body, was constituted following inter‑state negotiations mediated by the Council.

4.4 Emerging Mechanisms: Digital Governance and Data Sharing

Advances in technology have introduced new platforms for cooperation. The Integrated Financial Management System (IFMS) enables real‑time tracking of fund transfers between the Centre and states, reducing delays and enhancing transparency. Similarly, the National Data Sharing and Accessibility Policy (NDSAP) encourages states to share health, education, and agricultural data with central agencies, fostering evidence‑based policy making while respecting data sovereignty.

5. Contemporary Challenges and the Way Forward

5.1 Fiscal Imbalance and the Need for Reform

Despite periodic adjustments by the Finance Commission, many states continue to face fiscal deficits, relying heavily on central transfers. This dependence can undermine state autonomy and create a cycle of indebtedness. Proposals such as a permanent Goods and Services Tax (GST) compensation fund, greater de‑volution of income tax, and the establishment of a “National Fiscal Council” aim to create a more predictable and equitable fiscal framework.

5.2 Asymmetric Federalism and Special Provisions

India’s federalism accommodates diversity through special provisions for certain regions, such as Article 371 (which grants special status to states like Jammu & Kashmir, Nagaland, and Assam). While these provisions recognize unique cultural and geographic contexts, they also raise questions about uniformity and the potential for inter‑state resentment. Balancing special autonomy with national integration remains a delicate task.

5.3 Strengthening Cooperative Mechanisms

To enhance cooperation, scholars recommend institutional reforms such as granting the Inter‑State Council a statutory status, expanding its membership to include civil society representatives, and mandating periodic joint reviews of major policies. Strengthening the role of the GST Council and creating a dedicated “Centre‑State Coordination Committee” for climate change mitigation could also improve policy coherence.

5.4 The Impact of Globalisation and International Obligations

International agreements, such as the World Trade Organization (WTO) commitments and climate accords, often require uniform implementation across the country. This places additional pressure on centre‑state relations, as the Union must ensure compliance while respecting state competencies. The recent push for a “National Energy Policy” illustrates how global imperatives can drive cooperative federalism, compelling states to align their renewable energy targets with national goals.

The Chief Minister and the State Council of Ministers

Constitutional Position (Articles 163 and 164)

Article 163 provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers, with the Chief Minister at its head, to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of his functions, except in so far as he is required to exercise any function in his discretion. Article 164 lays down that the Chief Minister is appointed by the Governor and the other Ministers are appointed by the Governor on the advice of the Chief Minister. The Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the Governor, but since the Council is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly under Article 164(2), the pleasure of the Governor is in practice governed by the confidence of the Assembly. The Chief Minister is thus the real executive head of the State, while the Governor is the constitutional head, precisely as the Prime Minister and the President stand at the Centre.

Appointment and the Governor's Discretion

Where a single party or a pre-poll alliance commands a clear majority in the Assembly, the Governor invites its leader to be Chief Minister. Where there is a hung Assembly, the Governor must choose a person who is most likely to command a majority, and the choice is one of the recognised areas of discretionary judgement. The Governor may also dismiss a Chief Minister who has lost the confidence of the House, but the Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the floor of the Assembly, not the Governor's subjective satisfaction, is the proper test of majority, and that the Governor's assessment is subject to judicial review. The number of Ministers that the Chief Minister may appoint is capped by Article 164(1A), inserted by the 91st Amendment, at fifteen per cent of the total strength of the Assembly, with a minimum of twelve.

Powers and Functions of the Chief Minister

The Chief Minister is the keystone of the State government. The office allocates portfolios among the Ministers, chairs Cabinet meetings, coordinates the work of departments, and is the principal channel of communication between the Governor and the Council of Ministers. Article 167 imposes duties analogous to those of Article 78: the Chief Minister must communicate to the Governor all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of the State and to proposals for legislation, furnish such information as the Governor may call for, and place before the Council any matter on which the Governor has decided that the Council's opinion should be obtained. The Chief Minister leads the Assembly's majority, steers the government's legislative programme, advises the Governor on the summoning, prorogation and dissolution of the Assembly, and recommends appointments to a range of State institutions.

Composition and Ranks of the Council

The State Council of Ministers has the same internal hierarchy as the Union Council: Cabinet Ministers who head major departments and form the inner Cabinet, Ministers of State who hold independent or attached charges, and Deputy Ministers who assist senior Ministers. Decisions are taken collectively, and the principle of collective responsibility means that every Minister must publicly support the decisions of the Council or resign. A Minister who is not a member of either House of the State Legislature must become one within six months, failing which the office lapses.

Relationship with the Governor

The relationship between the Chief Minister and the Governor is defined by Article 163 and by convention. In matters where the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council, the Chief Minister's advice is binding. In the few matters where the Governor exercises functions in his discretion, such as the choice of a Chief Minister in a hung Assembly or the reservation of a Bill for the President, the Chief Minister's advice is not binding, and the Governor's decision is subject to judicial review on grounds of malafide or irrelevance. Controversies over the Governor's discretion, the timing of floor tests and the assent to Bills are recurring themes in Indian federal politics and are frequently tested in the Mains examination.

Scheduled and Tribal Areas: The Fifth and Sixth Schedules

The Two Schedules (Article 244)

Article 244 provides for the administration of two distinct categories of territory. Clause (1) applies the Fifth Schedule to the administration of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Clause (2) applies the Sixth Schedule to the administration of tribal areas in those four States. The distinction matters because the two schedules give very different degrees of autonomy: the Fifth Schedule integrates tribal areas within the ordinary State administration with special safeguards, while the Sixth Schedule creates autonomous self-governing institutions with legislative, executive and judicial powers.

The Fifth Schedule

Under the Fifth Schedule, the President may declare any area inhabited by Scheduled Tribes to be a Scheduled Area, and the Governor of the State is given special responsibility for its administration. Every State having Scheduled Areas must have a Tribes Advisory Council, consisting of not more than twenty members, of whom about three-fourths are to be representatives of Scheduled Tribes in the State Legislative Assembly. The Governor may make regulations for the peace and good government of a Scheduled Area, and may repeal or amend any Act of Parliament or of the Legislature in its application to that area, subject to the assent of the President. This is a significant power because it allows the Governor, with the President's assent, to modify general law in its application to tribal areas.

The Sixth Schedule

The Sixth Schedule provides for the creation of Autonomous District Councils and, within them, Regional Councils, in the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. These councils consist of elected members and a minority of nominated members. They have the power to make laws on subjects such as land, forest, inheritance, marriage, social customs and money-lending; to establish and administer schools, markets, roads and dispensaries; and to constitute village councils and courts for the trial of certain offences between Scheduled Tribe members. They also levy and collect land revenue and certain taxes and receive grants from the State government. The Sixth Schedule thus institutes a system of limited self-government, and its councils are often described as mini-legislatures within the State.

PESA and Panchayati Raj in Scheduled Areas

When the 73rd Amendment extended Panchayati Raj to the whole country, it was thought necessary to protect the traditional community institutions of tribal areas, and Parliament enacted the Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996, known as PESA. PESA applies to the Fifth Schedule areas and makes the Gram Sabha the cornerstone of self-governance, giving it powers over minor minerals, land alienation, forest produce, minor water bodies and the management of village markets and intoxicants, as well as the power to be consulted before land acquisition. Together, the Fifth Schedule, the Sixth Schedule and PESA define the constitutional architecture of tribal self-rule, and they remain central to debates on land, forests, mining and the displacement of tribal communities.

The Governor: Appointment, Powers and Discretion

Appointment and Tenure (Articles 153 to 156)

Article 153 provides that there shall be a Governor for each State, and the Seventh Amendment allows the same person to be appointed Governor for two or more States. The Governor is appointed by the President under Article 155 and is neither elected nor drawn from the State legislature. Under Article 156 the Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President: the normal term is five years, but the holder may be removed earlier without any stated cause and may resign by addressing the President. This absence of security of tenure is the central criticism of the office, because the Governor is a nominee of the Union who can be transferred or removed at will. The Sarkaria Commission and later commissions recommended that a Governor be an eminent person from outside the State's active politics, appointed after consultation with the Chief Minister and given a fixed term; these remain conventions, not enforceable rules. A candidate must be a citizen of India who has completed thirty-five years of age, and a Governor may not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a State Legislature.

Executive and Legislative Powers (Articles 163 to 167)

Article 163 requires the Governor to act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister, except where the Constitution requires him to exercise a function in his discretion. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, the other Ministers under Article 164; summons, prorogues and may dissolve the Legislative Assembly; addresses the House; and causes the annual budget to be laid. In the legislative field, Article 200 deals with assent to Bills: the Governor may assent, withhold assent, return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration, or reserve a Bill for the consideration of the President, who may then assent or withhold assent under Article 201. Article 213 empowers the Governor to promulgate ordinances when the legislature is not in session.

The Pardoning Power (Article 161)

Article 161 gives the Governor the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the State extends. The power is narrower than the President's under Article 72: it does not extend to death sentences, to sentences imposed by a court-martial, or to offences against Union laws. Its exercise is subject to limited judicial review. In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) the Supreme Court held that the power must be exercised on relevant material and in accordance with the advice of the government, and in Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) it held that an order under Article 161 could be quashed if it was malafide, arbitrary or passed without application of mind.

Discretionary Powers

The Constitution recognises a limited sphere of discretion. The Governor may act independently in choosing a Chief Minister when no party commands a clear majority, in dismissing a Ministry that has lost the confidence of the Assembly, in dissolving the Assembly, in reserving a Bill for the President, and in recommending the imposition of President's rule under Article 356. These powers are not absolute. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court held that the floor of the Assembly is the test of majority, that the Governor's report to the President under Article 356 is subject to judicial review, and that a Ministry cannot be dismissed without giving it an opportunity to prove its majority. In Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) a dissolution of the Bihar Assembly based on the Governor's report was held unconstitutional. Discretion, in other words, must be exercised on relevant material and for the purpose for which it was granted.

Position and the Federal Debate

As the constitutional head of the State the Governor performs a largely ceremonial role, but the office is also the channel of communication between the State and the Union, and the Governor's report is the trigger for the use of Article 356. Critics argue that a Governor drawn from Union politics, and removable at the Union's pleasure, can be used to destabilise a State government that is not of the Union's political colour; defenders reply that the office provides continuity, ensures that the State government functions within the Constitution, and protects the interests of the Centre in matters of national concern. The recurring controversies over floor tests, the timing of Assembly sessions and the assent to Bills make the Governor one of the most litigated offices in Indian federalism.

Union Territories: Administration and the Special Case of Delhi

Constitutional Position (Articles 1, 239 and 240)

Article 1(3) describes the territory of India as comprising the territories of the States, the Union Territories specified in the First Schedule, and any other territory that may be acquired. Unlike a State, a Union Territory is not a self-contained constitutional unit: it is administered by the President acting through an Administrator or Lieutenant Governor appointed under Article 239. Article 240 allows the President to make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of certain Union Territories, and such regulations may override an Act of Parliament in its application to the territory. Union Territories differ from States in that the Union has direct legislative competence over them under Entries 1 to 3 of the Union List, and Parliament may legislate on any subject for a Union Territory under Article 246(4).

Union Territories With and Without Legislatures

Most Union Territories are administered directly; a few have a legislature. Puducherry has a Legislative Assembly under Article 239A, and the National Capital Territory of Delhi has one under Article 239AA. After the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, Jammu and Kashmir became a Union Territory with a legislature, while Ladakh became a Union Territory without one. The remaining Union Territories, such as Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, and Lakshadweep, are administered by an Administrator or Lieutenant Governor without a legislature. Union Territories with legislatures are represented in the Rajya Sabha; Delhi and Puducherry send members to the Council of States.

The Special Case of Delhi (Article 239AA)

Delhi occupies a hybrid position that is neither a full State nor an ordinary Union Territory. The 69th Amendment, 1991, inserted Article 239AA, which gives the National Capital Territory a Legislative Assembly with power to make laws on matters in the State List and the Concurrent List, except public order, police and land, which are reserved to the Union. The Lieutenant Governor acts as the President's representative, and Article 239AB allows the President to suspend the Assembly's functions if the administration cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The relationship between the elected government and the Lieutenant Governor has been the subject of repeated litigation, and in Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) the Supreme Court held that the Lieutenant Governor has no independent decision-making power but must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, with a narrowly defined exception for matters where he is required to act in his discretion.

Why Union Territories Exist and Why They Are Debated

Union Territories exist for reasons of strategic importance, small size, administrative convenience or a special history: island territories, a national capital and a newly reorganised region have been the usual cases. Because they are administered by the Union, critics describe them as undemocratic where they have no legislature, and argue that the residents of a Union Territory have a weaker voice than the residents of a State. The Supreme Court's judgments on Delhi, in particular, have defined the balance between the Union's special responsibility for the capital and the democratic mandate of its elected government, and these judgments remain central to any discussion of asymmetric federalism in India.

Vertical and Horizontal Devolution: The Finance Commission’s Formula in Practice

The Finance Commission (FC) determines how the divisible pool of central taxes is split between the Union and the states (vertical devolution) and how the states’ share is further distributed among them (horizontal devolution). Since the 10th FC (1995‑2000) the vertical share has risen from 29 % to 41 % under the 15th FC (2021‑2026), reflecting a deliberate policy to enlarge state fiscal space. The horizontal formula combines population (1971 census weight 15 %, 2011 census weight 10 %), area (15 %), forest cover (10 %), income distance (45 %), and demographic performance (12.5 %). The 15th FC introduced a “performance‑based” component rewarding states for GST compliance, ease‑of‑doing‑business rankings, and health‑education outcomes.

The GST Council as a Federal Bargaining Forum

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council, constituted under Article 279A, is the most consequential institutional innovation for cooperative federalism since the Inter‑State Council. It comprises the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), the Union Minister of State for Finance, and one minister from each state, usually the state finance minister. Decisions require a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes: the Centre holds one‑third of the total voting weight, while the states collectively hold two‑thirds. This voting structure forces consensus on rate changes, exemption lists, and compensation mechanisms.

Since its first meeting in September 2016, the Council has deliberated on over 50 rate rationalisation exercises, the transition from the origin‑based to destination‑based taxation principle, and the design of the GST compensation cess. The compensation guarantee, valid for five years (2017‑2022), was a political bargain to secure state buy‑in; its expiry triggered a new round of negotiations on revenue‑sharing formulas and the continuation of the cess. The Council’s secretariat, housed in the Department of Revenue, provides technical support but does not vote, preserving the political character of the forum.

Judicial scrutiny of Council decisions remains limited. In Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022) the Supreme Court held that the Council’s recommendations are not justiciable as legislative acts, but the Court can examine whether the Council acted within its constitutional mandate. The judgment underscored that the Council’s “recommendations” become binding only after the President’s assent to the corresponding GST Acts, preserving parliamentary supremacy while recognising the Council’s federal character.

The Council’s working groups on IT infrastructure (GSTN), anti‑profiteering, and sector‑specific issues (textiles, real estate) illustrate how technical coordination is embedded in a political bargaining space. States have used the Council to press for higher compensation, lower rates on essential goods, and greater autonomy in setting state‑specific cesses. The Centre, in turn, leverages its one‑third vote to block proposals that threaten fiscal consolidation. This dynamic makes the Council a live laboratory for observing how federal bargaining translates into tax policy.

Worked Answer Technique
Explain how the voting weight distribution in the GST Council reflects the principle of cooperative federalism and why the Centre’s one‑third vote is constitutionally significant.
The GST Council’s voting design gives the Centre one‑third of total voting weight and the states two‑thirds, mirroring the federal principle that taxation powers are shared but not dominated by either tier. The Centre’s one‑third vote ensures that any decision requires substantial state support, preventing unilateral central imposition of rates. At the same time, the Centre’s presence guarantees that national fiscal objectives — such as revenue stability and compliance — are represented. This weighted majority (three‑fourths) means that a coalition of states cannot override the Centre entirely, nor can the Centre act without a broad state consensus. The constitutional significance lies in Article 279A(9), which embeds this balance in the supreme law, making the Council a constitutional federal forum rather than a mere administrative committee.

State Finance Commissions and Local Government Fiscal Autonomy

Article 243I mandates that every state constitute a State Finance Commission (SFC) within one year of the commencement of the Constitution (Seventy‑Third Amendment) Act, 1992, and thereafter at intervals of five years. The SFC reviews the financial position of panchayats and municipalities, recommends the distribution of state‑net‑proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls, and fees between the state and local bodies, and suggests grants‑in‑aid from the state’s Consolidated Fund. Despite the constitutional mandate, many states have delayed SFC constitution, leading to ad‑hoc devolution and weakened local fiscal capacity.

The 14th and 15th Finance Commissions linked central local‑body grants to the timely constitution and acceptance of SFC recommendations. The 15th FC earmarked ₹90,000 crore for rural local bodies and ₹30,000 crore for urban local bodies for the 2021‑2026 period, conditional on states submitting action‑taken reports on SFC recommendations. This conditionalities framework has improved compliance: by 2023, 28 states had constituted their sixth SFC, though only 19 had implemented the full recommendation package.

SFCs typically adopt a formula combining population (70 %), area (10 %), and a “backwardness” index (20 %). Some states, such as Kerala and Karnataka, have introduced performance‑based components rewarding panchayats for own‑source‑revenue mobilisation and service‑delivery benchmarks. The SFC’s recommendations are advisory; the state legislature must pass an appropriation act to give them effect. This legislative gatekeeping sometimes results in dilution of the recommended shares, especially when state finances are stressed.

The interplay between the Union Finance Commission (vertical/horizontal devolution) and SFCs creates a two‑tier equalisation mechanism. The 15th FC’s “local‑body grants” are transferred directly to panchayats and municipalities, bypassing the state treasury, to reduce leakages. However, the grants are still routed through the state’s Public Financial Management System, and states retain discretion over the timing of release. Strengthening SFCs through statutory timelines, independent secretariats, and mandatory legislative debate on their reports remains a key reform agenda for deepening fiscal federalism at the grassroots.

Inter‑State River Water Disputes: Tribunal Mechanism and Supreme Court Oversight

The Inter‑State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 (ISRWDA) provides for the constitution of ad‑hoc tribunals when riparian states fail to reach agreement through negotiations. The Centre, on the request of a state, refers the dispute to a tribunal whose decision is final and binding, subject only to a limited review by the Supreme Court under Article 136 (special leave). Since 1956, eight tribunals have been constituted — Krishna (1969), Godavari (1969), Narmada (1969), Cauvery (1990), Ravi‑Beas (1986), Vansadhara (2010), Mahadayi (2010), and Mahanadi (2018). The Cauvery tribunal’s final order (2007) and the subsequent Supreme Court judgment (2018) illustrate the layered adjudication process.

The 2002 amendment to the ISRWDA introduced a “single‑tribunal” model with a permanent chairperson and a three‑member bench, aiming to reduce delays. The amendment also mandated that the tribunal’s report be laid before Parliament, enhancing legislative oversight. Despite these reforms, tribunals often take a decade or more to deliver final awards, during which interim orders govern water sharing. The Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment in State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu upheld the Cauvery tribunal’s award but modified the monthly release schedule, exercising its power to ensure “equitable apportionment” under Article 262.

Article 262 explicitly bars the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction over inter‑state water disputes, yet the Court has interpreted this bar as not precluding review of tribunal awards for constitutional violations, procedural fairness, or manifest error. This jurisprudential tension creates a de‑facto appellate layer that can both stabilise and prolong disputes. The 2020 amendment to the ISRWDA proposes a “Dispute Resolution Committee” at the basin level to attempt mediation before tribunal reference, reflecting a shift toward cooperative management.

Data‑sharing protocols under the National Water Mission and the River Basin Management Plans now feed real‑time flow data into tribunal proceedings, reducing factual disagreements. However, political resistance to data transparency persists, especially in basins with upstream‑downstream power asymmetries. The evolving framework — tribunal adjudication, Supreme Court oversight, and basin‑level mediation — embodies the constitutional design of “cooperative federalism” in a resource‑scarce context.

Judicial Doctrines Governing Legislative Competence: Pith and Substance, Colourable Legislation, and Repugnancy

When a law’s legislative competence is challenged, the Supreme Court employs three principal doctrines. The “pith and substance” test examines the true nature and character of the legislation, disregarding its incidental effects on entries outside the enacting legislature’s list. In State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) the Court upheld a state prohibition law that incidentally affected import‑export (a Union subject) because its pith and substance fell under the State List entry “intoxicating liquors”.

The doctrine of “colourable legislation” addresses enactments that, while formally within a legislature’s competence, in substance transgress constitutional limits. The Court looks for a “colour” or disguise masking an impermissible objective. In K. C. G. Narayanan v. State of Kerala (1965) a state law imposing a tax on “professions, trades, and callings” was struck down as colourable because its real purpose was to tax income, a Union subject.

“Repugnancy” under Article 254 resolves conflicts between Union and state laws on Concurrent List subjects. If a state law is repugnant to a Union law, the Union law prevails unless the state law has received Presidential assent under Article 254(2), in which case it operates notwithstanding the repugnancy. The Supreme Court in Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983) clarified that repugnancy arises only when two laws cannot stand together — either because they prescribe contradictory obligations or because the Union law intends to occupy the entire field.

These doctrines interact. A state law may survive a pith‑and‑substance challenge but fall to colourable legislation if its dominant purpose is ultra vires. Conversely, a law valid under pith and substance may still be invalidated by repugnancy if a subsequent Union law occupies the field. The Court’s 2021 decision in Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (regarding the GST Council’s recommendation on compensation cess) applied repugnancy principles to hold that the Union’s GST Act prevailed over a state’s parallel cess, reinforcing the hierarchical supremacy of Union legislation on Concurrent subjects.

For aspirants, the key is to identify the list entries involved, determine the dominant purpose of the impugned statute, and then apply the appropriate doctrine. Examination questions often present a factual matrix and ask which doctrine applies; a structured answer — list entry → pith and substance → colourable legislation check → repugnancy analysis — scores full marks.

Basic Structure Doctrine and Federalism: The S.R. Bommai and Kesavananda Jurisprudence

The basic structure doctrine, crystallised in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), holds that certain constitutional features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, and federalism — cannot be amended away by Parliament under Article 368. Federalism was explicitly recognised as a basic feature in the majority opinion of Justice Khanna and later affirmed in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994). In Bommai, the Supreme Court laid down stringent judicial review standards for the imposition of President’s Rule under Article 356, ruling that the proclamation is subject to judicial scrutiny on grounds of mala fide, irrelevance, or constitutional impropriety. The Court held that the federal balance is a core element of the basic structure; therefore, any arbitrary dismissal of a state government undermines the constitutional scheme.

The Bommai guidelines require the Centre to place before Parliament the material on which the President’s satisfaction is based, and they mandate a floor test in the state assembly before dissolution. Subsequent judgments — Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) and Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2021) — have reinforced that the Governor’s discretion must be exercised in harmony with federal principles. In NCT of Delhi, the Court clarified that the Lieutenant Governor’s concurrence is not required on matters within the legislative competence of the Delhi Assembly, except on land, police, and public order, thereby delineating a federal space for a Union Territory with a legislature.

These rulings collectively establish a jurisprudential framework where federalism is not merely a distribution of powers but a constitutional value protected from majoritarian erosion. For aspirants, the analytical chain is: identify the constitutional provision challenged → test whether the impugned action alters the federal balance → apply the basic structure test → conclude on validity. Examination questions often present a scenario of President’s Rule or Governor’s discretion and ask whether the basic structure doctrine is attracted; a structured answer referencing Kesavananda, Bommai, and the 2021 Delhi judgment scores full marks.

Centrally Sponsored Schemes and the Flexi‑Fund Mechanism: Redefining Cooperative Federalism

Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) have been the principal instrument of Union‑state fiscal cooperation since the First Five‑Year Plan. The 14th Finance Commission (2015‑20) recommended a shift from tied grants to untied fiscal transfers, but the Union retained CSS to pursue national priorities. In 2014, the Ministry of Finance introduced the flexi‑fund mechanism, allowing states to re‑allocate up to 10 % of the allocation under each CSS to state‑specific innovations without prior Union approval. The 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) raised this ceiling to 25 % and extended it to all CSS, subject to the condition that the re‑allocated funds remain within the broad sectoral objectives of the scheme.

The mechanism operates through an Office Memorandum (OM) issued by the Department of Expenditure. States must submit a flexi‑fund utilisation plan to the concerned line ministry, which monitors outcomes through the Public Financial Management System (PFMS). Flagship schemes such as the National Health Mission (NHM), Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana (PMGSY), and Samagra Shiksha have seen states deploy flexi‑funds for tele‑medicine hubs, rural road maintenance, and digital classrooms respectively. However, the Union retains the power to issue scheme‑specific guidelines that may restrict flexibility, and the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) audits flexi‑fund expenditure under the same standards as core CSS funds.

Critics argue that the 25 % ceiling is still modest compared with the 14th FC’s vision of full untied devolution, and that line ministries often delay approvals, negating the intended autonomy. Proponents contend that the mechanism preserves national standards while granting states operational discretion — a practical expression of cooperative federalism. In answer writing, cite the 15th FC’s Chapter 7, the 2021 OM (F.No. 1/2021‑PFMS), and the distinction between tied and untied components of CSS.

Digital Federalism: Inter‑State Data Sharing, the National Data Governance Framework and Emerging Legal Issues

The Digital India programme and the National Data Sharing and Accessibility Policy (NDSAP) (2012) laid the groundwork for open government data. In 2022, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology released the draft National Data Governance Framework Policy (NDGFP), proposing a unified architecture for data exchange across Union, state, and local bodies. The framework envisages a National Data Office to set standards, a Data Sharing Protocol for inter‑state transfers, and a Consent Management Layer aligned with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.

Practical implementations already exist: the Integrated Health Information Platform (IHIP) enables real‑time disease surveillance data flow from states to the National Centre for Disease Control; the Unified District Information System for Education (UDISE+) aggregates school‑level data; and the GST Network (GSTN) shares taxpayer data between the Centre and states for compliance analytics. However, legal clarity on data ownership remains contested. Article 299 (contracts by the Government) and Article 300 (suits by or against the Government) have been invoked in disputes over data‑access agreements, while the Supreme Court’s Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) privacy judgment imposes a proportionality test on any mandatory data sharing.

Key challenges include: (1) absence of a statutory mandate for the NDGFP, leaving it as an executive policy; (2) divergent state data‑localisation laws (e.g., Meghalaya’s 2021 Data Localisation Act); (3) the lack of a uniform consent framework for citizen‑level data across states. The 101st Constitutional Amendment and the Restructuring of State Taxation Powers

The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 introduced Goods and Services Tax (GST) by inserting Articles 246A, 269A, 279A and amending the Seventh Schedule. It subsumed a raft of state taxes — Value Added Tax (VAT), Central Sales Tax (CST), Entry Tax, Luxury Tax, Taxes on Advertisements, Taxes on Lotteries, Betting and Gambling, and State Cesses and Surcharges — into a single dual GST. The Amendment also created the GST Council (Article 279A) as a constitutional body with a three‑fourths weighted majority for decisions, giving the Union a de‑facto veto (one‑third vote weight).

To address revenue loss fears, the Amendment guaranteed compensation to states for five years (2017‑22) through a compensation cess on sin and luxury goods, backed by a statutory guarantee under the GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017. The 15th Finance Commission later recommended extending the compensation mechanism in a tapered manner, but the Union discontinued the cess after June 2022, leading to litigation in State of Kerala v. Union of India (2023) where the Supreme Court upheld the Union’s discretion to cease compensation after the constitutional period.

The restructuring has two major federal implications: (1) states lost independent rate‑setting power on subsumed taxes, reducing fiscal policy space; (2) the GST Council’s recommendatory decisions become binding only after Presidential assent under Article 279A(9), a nuance often missed. Aspirants should distinguish between the legislative power (Article 246A) and the administrative coordination (GST Council), and note that the compensation guarantee was a statutory not a constitutional entitlement beyond the five‑year window.

The Inter‑State Council in Practice: Case Studies of Cauvery, Krishna and Mahadayi Disputes

Established under Article 263 by a Presidential Order in 1990, the Inter‑State Council (ISC) is a recommendatory body for coordination on inter‑state disputes and common interests. While the Council has no adjudicatory authority, its deliberations have shaped the political climate around major water disputes.

Cauvery: The Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT) delivered its final award in 2007, modified by the Supreme Court in 2018 (Civil Appeal No. 2453/2018). The ISC met in 2018 and 2019 to discuss implementation of the Court’s direction for a Cauvery Management Board. The Council’s recommendation to constitute the Cauvery Water Management Authority and the Cauvery Water Regulation Committee was accepted by the Union, illustrating how ISC consensus can translate into executive action.

Krishna: The Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal (KWDT‑II) gave its report in 2010. The ISC’s 2011 meeting endorsed the Tribunal’s allocation but highlighted the need for a Krishna River Management Board. The Board was eventually notified in 2014 after prolonged negotiations, showing the Council’s role in breaking deadlocks.

Mahadayi (Mandovi): The Mahadayi Water Disputes Tribunal (MWDT) was constituted in 2010. The ISC’s 2016 session urged the parties (Goa, Karnataka, Maharashtra) to pursue mediated settlement. The Tribunal’s 2020 award allocated 13.4 tmcft to Karnataka, 24 tmcft to Goa, and 1.33 tmcft to Maharashtra. The Council’s 2021 meeting noted the award and recommended a Mahadayi River Basin Authority for implementation, which remains pending.

These cases demonstrate that the ISC functions as a political facilitator rather than a legal arbiter. Its effectiveness depends on the willingness of chief ministers to attend and compromise. Evolution of Horizontal Devolution Criteria: From the 10th to the 16th Finance Commission

The horizontal distribution formula has been reshaped at every Finance Commission (FC) since the 10th FC (1995‑2000). The 10th FC used three broad criteria: population (1971 Census) 25 %, area 10 %, and a composite index of fiscal capacity and fiscal discipline 65 %. The 11th FC (2000‑2005) added a tax‑effort variable (10 %) and reduced the weight of population to 20 %. The 12th FC (2005‑2010) introduced demographic change (population growth 1971‑2001) with a 10 % weight and gave 7.5 % to fiscal discipline measured by the ratio of own‑tax revenue to GSDP. The 13th FC (2010‑2015) brought forest cover (7.5 %) and a “forest‑adjusted” area factor, while retaining the 1971 population base. The 14th FC (2015‑2020) marked a structural shift: it raised the states’ share in the divisible pool to 42 % (from 32 %), adopted the 2011 Census for population (17.5 %), and assigned weights — income distance 50 %, population 17.5 %, area 15 %, forest cover 7.5 %, and demographic performance 10 %. The 15th FC (2020‑2025) kept the 42 % share, used the 2011 Census, and refined the formula to: income distance 45 %, population 15 %, area 15 %, forest and ecology 10 %, demographic performance 12.5 %, and tax effort 2.5 %. It also introduced a “performance‑based” grant window for health and education. The 16th FC (2025‑2030), whose terms of reference were notified in 2023, is mandated to consider climate‑change vulnerability, disaster‑risk exposure, and the fiscal impact of the GST compensation cessation, signalling a further evolution toward risk‑adjusted devolution.

The National Development Council, NITI Aayog and the Shift from Plan to Cooperative Federalism

The National Development Council (NDC) was constituted in August 1952 as the apex body for approving five‑year plans. Its membership comprised the Prime Minister, all Union Cabinet Ministers, Chief Ministers of all states, and administrators of Union Territories. The NDC met irregularly — only 57 times in six decades — and its resolutions were advisory, with the Planning Commission retaining the power to allocate plan outlays. In January 2015 the Government replaced the Planning Commission with the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog). The NITI Aayog’s Governing Council mirrors the NDC’s composition but adds the Lieutenant Governors of Union Territories with legislatures and the CEO of NITI Aayog as ex‑officio member‑secretary. Unlike the Planning Commission, NITI Aayog does not allocate financial resources; it formulates strategic and long‑term policy frameworks, monitors implementation through the “Aspirational Districts” programme, and facilitates “cooperative federalism” by requiring states to submit annual development plans. The first Governing Council meeting (February 2015) adopted the “Three‑Year Action Agenda” and the “Seven‑Year Strategy”. Subsequent meetings (2017, 2019, 2021, 2023) have focused on sustainable development goals, water‑security, and digital infrastructure. The shift from a top‑down plan‑allocation model to a bottom‑up, evidence‑based advisory model is the central federalism implication: states now negotiate policy priorities rather than receive earmarked plan funds.

Article 301‑307: Freedom of Inter‑State Trade, the GST Council and Emerging Jurisprudence

Articles 301 to 307 of the Constitution guarantee freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India, subject to parliamentary and state legislative powers. Article 301 declares that trade shall be free; Article 302 empowers Parliament to impose restrictions in the public interest; Article 303 prohibits discrimination between states by Parliament or state legislatures; Article 304 permits states to impose reasonable restrictions on goods imported from other states, provided they do not discriminate; Article 305 saves existing laws and laws providing for state monopolies; Article 306 (repealed) dealt with restrictions on inter‑state trade; Article 307 authorises Parliament to appoint an authority for carrying out the provisions of Articles 301‑304. The 101st Constitutional Amendment (2016) inserted Article 279A creating the GST Council, which recommends rates, exemptions, and threshold limits for the Goods and Services Tax. The Council’s decisions, though recommendatory, acquire binding force after Presidential assent under Article 279A(9). Judicial interpretation has clarified the interplay: in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2020) the Supreme Court held that the GST compensation cess is a statutory, not constitutional, entitlement beyond the five‑year period. In M/s. Jindal Stainless Ltd v. Performance‑Based Grants and the 15th Finance Commission’s Health and Education Conditionalities

The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑2025) introduced a performance‑based grant window amounting to ₹1.5 lakh crore for health and ₹1.2 lakh crore for education over the award period 2021‑26. For health, grants are linked to the National Health Mission (NHM) indicators: outpatient‑department attendance, institutional delivery rate, immunisation coverage, and the functional status of Health and Wellness Centres. The first tranche (2021‑22) saw 22 states qualify for the full health performance component, while 18 states met the education thresholds. The conditionalities aim to address the historic “fly‑paper effect” where untied grants did not translate into improved outcomes, but they have also raised concerns about administrative burden and the reliability of state‑reported data.

Disaster Management Federalism: The National Disaster Management Authority, State Disaster Response Funds and the Role of the Union

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 created a three‑tier institutional architecture: the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) chaired by the Prime Minister, State Disaster Management Authorities (SDMAs) headed by Chief Ministers, and District Disaster Management Authorities (DDMAs). Financing follows a shared pattern: the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) and State Disaster Response Funds (SDRFs) are funded through a 75:25 centre‑state contribution for general category states and 90:10 for special category states (including the North‑Eastern and hill states). The 15th Finance Commission recommended an enhanced allocation of ₹1.6 lakh crore to NDRF and ₹0.9 lakh crore to SDRFs for 2021‑26. In practice, the Union supplements SDRFs through “additional central assistance” when a disaster is declared “of severe nature” under Section 48 of the Act. Recent episodes illustrate the federal dynamics: the 2021 Uttarakhand flash floods triggered a central release of ₹1,200 crore from NDRF within 48 hours; the 2023 Odisha cyclone (Cyclone Mocha) saw the Union deploy NDRF battalions and release ₹2,500 crore as immediate relief; the COVID‑19 pandemic was notified as a “disaster” under the Act, enabling the Union to invoke the NDRF for health‑infrastructure augmentation and to coordinate vaccine procurement across states. The Act also empowers the NDMA to issue binding guidelines on preparedness, mitigation, and capacity‑building, which states must incorporate into their State Disaster Management Plans. The evolving jurisprudence, including the Supreme Court’s 2022 direction in State of Kerala v. Union of India on timely release of SDRF instalments, reinforces the principle that disaster response is a shared constitutional obligation, not a discretionary central largesse.

Article 249 and Article 250: Union Legislation on State Subjects in National Interest and Emergency

Article 249 empowers Parliament to legislate on any matter enumerated in the State List if the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) passes a resolution supported by not less than two‑thirds of the members present and voting, declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest. Such a resolution remains in force for a period of one year and may be renewed for further periods of one year each. The legislation enacted under this article ceases to have effect six months after the resolution lapses or is revoked, unless Parliament re‑enacts it before that period expires. Historically, the first resolution under Article 249 was adopted in 1952 concerning the regulation of essential commodities; subsequent resolutions have covered subjects such as the control of inter‑state trade in foodgrains (1976), the prevention of terrorism (1985), and the regulation of the telecommunications sector (1997). The Supreme Court in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) upheld the validity of a central law on the regulation of mines enacted under an Article 249 resolution, holding that the resolution satisfied the constitutional requirement of a two‑thirds majority and that the subject matter fell within the national interest.

Article 250 provides a parallel but distinct route: during the operation of a Proclamation of Emergency under Article 352, Parliament acquires the power to legislate on any State List subject for the whole or any part of the territory of India. Laws made under Article 250 remain in force for six months after the Emergency ceases to operate, unless Parliament extends them. The 1975‑77 Emergency saw a raft of central statutes on subjects such as public order, police, and local government enacted under Article 250. In Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980), the Court clarified that the emergency power under Article 250 is not unlimited; it must be exercised in consonance with the basic structure of the Constitution and cannot be used to amend the Constitution itself. Both Articles 249 and 250 illustrate the Constitution’s built‑in flexibility, allowing the Union to address pressing national concerns without a formal amendment of the Seventh Schedule, while the sunset clauses and judicial oversight act as safeguards against permanent encroachment on state autonomy.

Articles 256, 257 and 365: Union Directions to States and Consequences of Non‑Compliance

Article 256 obliges the executive power of every state to be exercised so as to ensure compliance with the laws made by Parliament and any existing laws which apply in that state. It also authorises the Union to give such directions to a state as may appear to the Government of India to be necessary for that purpose. Article 257 extends this principle to specific matters: the Union may direct a state to construct or maintain means of communication declared to be of national or military importance, to protect railways, and to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother‑tongue at the primary stage. Failure to comply with directions issued under Articles 256 or 257 triggers Article 365, which states that if a state fails to comply with, or to give effect to, any directions given by the Union, the President may hold that a situation has arisen in which the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. This provision is the constitutional gateway to the imposition of President’s Rule under Article 356.

The Supreme Court’s landmark judgment in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) examined the interplay of Articles 256, 257 and 365. The Court held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 must be based on objective material and that the mere existence of a political disagreement does not constitute a failure of constitutional machinery. It further ruled that directions under Article 257 must be specific, reasoned, and limited to the enumerated subjects; blanket directions to a state government to “maintain law and order” without reference to the listed matters exceed the Union’s authority. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977), the Court upheld a Union direction to the state to implement a central law on the regulation of inter‑state river waters, affirming that Article 256 empowers the Union to ensure compliance with parliamentary legislation even when the subject falls in the State List. The practical effect of these articles is a layered mechanism: the Union issues directions, the state is constitutionally bound to obey, and persistent non‑compliance can legitimise central intervention, subject to judicial review.

If the state refuses, Article 365 enables the President to conclude that the state government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, paving the way for President’s Rule under Article 356. Article 256 deals with general compliance with Union laws, not with specific infrastructure directions.

Article 355: Duty of the Union to Protect States against External Aggression and Internal Disturbance

Article 355 imposes a constitutional duty on the Union to protect every state against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure that the government of every state is carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. Unlike Articles 352, 356 or 360, Article 355 does not confer a discretionary power; it creates an obligation that the Union must fulfil. The provision has been invoked to justify the deployment of central armed police forces (CAPF) and the army in aid of civil power, the imposition of President’s Rule, and the enactment of special legislation such as the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 (AFSPA). In Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India (1998), the Supreme Court held that Article 355 is a justiciable duty and that the Union’s failure to act in the face of internal disturbance could be challenged. The Court also clarified that the duty under Article 355 is not a blank cheque for the Union to intervene in state affairs; any action must be proportionate and consistent with the federal balance.

Subsequent jurisprudence has refined the scope of Article 355. In State of Chhattisgarh v. Union of India (2011), the Court examined the deployment of central forces in Naxal‑affected areas and ruled that the Union’s duty under Article 355 includes providing adequate resources and coordination, but does not empower it to supersede the state’s executive authority without a Proclamation under Article 356. The 2015 amendment to the National Investigation Agency Act, which extended the NIA’s jurisdiction to scheduled offences across states, was also defended on the basis of Article 355. The provision thus operates as a constitutional bridge between the Union’s responsibility for national security and the states’ autonomy over law and order, requiring a calibrated response that respects both imperatives.

The Finance Commission’s Treatment of Cesses and Surcharges in the Divisible Pool

The Constitution distinguishes between taxes that form part of the divisible pool (Article 270) and cesses and surcharges that are levied for specific purposes and are not automatically shared with states. Historically, the Union has levied a multitude of cesses — such as the education cess, health cess, and the infrastructure cess — whose proceeds accrue entirely to the Centre. The 14th Finance Commission (2015‑20) recommended that the Union progressively subsume cesses and surcharges into the Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime, thereby expanding the divisible pool. However, several cesses remain outside GST, notably the cess on petroleum products, the cess on tobacco, and the health and education cess introduced in the 2018‑19 budget.

The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) addressed this asymmetry by recommending that a portion of the proceeds from specified cesses be transferred to states as a separate grant. It proposed that 1 % of the gross tax revenue from cesses and surcharges be earmarked for a “Cess and Surcharge Grant” to be distributed among states using the horizontal devolution formula. The Union accepted this recommendation in the 2021‑22 budget, allocating ₹1.2 lakh crore for the award period. The Commission also urged the Union to phase out cesses that duplicate GST subsumption, citing the petroleum cess as a prime candidate. The treatment of cesses and surcharges remains a contentious issue in fiscal federalism because it directly affects the vertical share: the 15th FC retained the 42 % vertical devolution of net proceeds of Union taxes (excluding cesses and surcharges), while the additional grant partially compensates states for the revenue foregone. The evolving stance of successive Finance Commissions reflects a gradual shift towards greater transparency and equity in the sharing of all Union revenue streams.

The GST Council’s Voting Structure: Weighted Voting, Consensus Requirement and Dispute Resolution Mechanism

The Goods and Services Tax Council, constituted under Article 279A, is the apex decision‑making body for GST policy. Its composition comprises the Union Finance Minister as Chairperson, the Union Minister of State for Finance, and the Finance Ministers of all states and Union territories with legislatures. The Council’s voting architecture is unique: the Union holds one‑third of the total voting weight, while the states collectively hold two‑thirds. Within the states’ share, each state has an equal vote, irrespective of population or revenue contribution. Decisions are ideally reached by consensus; if consensus fails, a resolution requires a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes. This weighted‑voting formula ensures that neither the Union nor a coalition of states can unilaterally impose decisions.

The Council’s Rules of Procedure provide a two‑tier dispute resolution mechanism. First, any disagreement on the interpretation or implementation of GST laws may be referred to the GST Council’s Standing Committee, which comprises senior officials from the Centre and states. If the Standing Committee cannot resolve the issue, the matter is placed before the full Council for a decision. Should a state remain aggrieved, Article 279A(11) empowers the Council to refer the dispute to the Supreme Court, whose decision is final. Since its inception in 2017, the Council has resolved major disputes — such as the classification of lottery services, the rate on electric vehicles, and the compensation cess shortfall — through consensus, rarely resorting to formal voting. The 2022 decision to extend the compensation cess beyond the original five‑year period was adopted by consensus after intense negotiations, illustrating the Council’s role as a federal bargaining forum where weighted voting underpins but does not dominate the collaborative ethos.

Article 131: The Supreme Court's Original Jurisdiction in Centre‑State Disputes

Article 131 of the Constitution confers exclusive original jurisdiction on the Supreme Court to adjudicate disputes between the Government of India and one or more States, or between two or more States. The provision is a cornerstone of federal adjudication because it bypasses the High Courts and provides a single forum for interpreting the constitutional distribution of powers. The Court has consistently held that the jurisdiction is not merely procedural but substantive: it can examine the constitutional validity of Union legislation, executive actions, and even the legality of Presidential proclamations under Article 356 when a State challenges them.

The leading precedent is State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977), where the Court entertained a suit by Karnataka challenging the Union’s directive to the State to implement a central scheme. The Court ruled that a dispute must involve a legal right, not a mere political grievance. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977), the Court clarified that the dispute must be justiciable and that the Union’s policy decisions, if within its legislative competence, are not subject to Article 131 unless they infringe a State’s constitutional entitlement. More recently, in State of Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (2017), the Court heard Tamil Nadu’s challenge to the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET) on the ground that it encroached on the State’s power over medical education under Entry 25 of List II. The Court upheld the Union law, finding it fell within Entry 66 of List I (coordination and standards in higher education).

Article 131 also covers inter‑State disputes, but only when they involve a legal right arising from the Constitution or a statute. The classic illustration is the State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) suit concerning the Union’s acquisition of land for a railway project; the Court held the dispute was justiciable because it involved the interpretation of the Union’s power under Entry 42 of List I. In contrast, the Court declined jurisdiction in State of Jharkhand v. State of Bihar (2015) over the sharing of mineral royalties, ruling that the matter was governed by a specific statutory tribunal under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957, and therefore fell outside Article 131.

Procedurally, a suit under Article 131 is instituted by a State filing a plaint directly in the Supreme Court. The Union is represented by the Attorney General, and the Court may appoint an amicus curiae. The Court’s orders are binding and enforceable under Article 142. Notably, the Court has refused to entertain suits that are essentially political, such as the 2020 challenge by Kerala to the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, where the Court held the dispute did not involve a legal right of the State under the federal scheme.

The jurisprudence underscores two limits: first, the dispute must be between governments, not between a State and a private party; second, the Court will not adjudicate matters that are constitutionally assigned to specialised tribunals (e.g., water disputes under Article 262). These limits preserve the balance between judicial federalism and the legislative design of dispute‑resolution mechanisms.

Article 200 and the Governor's Power to Reserve State Bills for Presidential Consideration

Article 200 empowers the Governor of a State to reserve any Bill passed by the State Legislature for the consideration of the President. Once reserved, the Bill does not become law until the President assents to it. The President may either assent, withhold assent, or return the Bill (if it is not a Money Bill) to the State Legislature with a message requesting reconsideration. This mechanism is a critical federal safeguard, allowing the Union to scrutinise State legislation that may conflict with Union laws, the Constitution, or national policy.

The Governor’s discretion to reserve a Bill is not unfettered. In Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016), the Supreme Court held that the Governor must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in situations where the Constitution expressly requires the Governor to act in his discretion (e.g., under Article 163(1) read with Article 200). The Court emphasised that the Governor cannot reserve a Bill merely because he disagrees with its policy; the reservation must be grounded in a constitutional concern such as repugnancy with a Union law (Article 254), violation of fundamental rights, or encroachment on the Union List.

When a Bill is reserved, the President’s decision is guided by the advice of the Union Council of Ministers under Article 74. The President’s assent transforms the Bill into an Act of the State Legislature, but the Act is deemed to have been passed by the State Legislature and assented to by the President. If the President withholds assent, the Bill lapses. The return of a Bill for reconsideration is rare; the only notable instance is the 1991 reservation of the Tamil Nadu Bill on the regulation of private educational institutions, which the President returned, leading to its eventual passage in a modified form.

The interplay between Article 200 and Article 254 (repugnancy) is significant. If a State law is repugnant to a Union law on a Concurrent List subject, the Union law prevails unless the State law has been reserved for the President’s consideration and has received his assent. In such a case, the State law prevails in that State, but Parliament can still override it by subsequent legislation. This dynamic was illustrated in the 2014 case State of Gujarat v. Union of India, where the Court upheld a State amendment to the Motor Vehicles Act that had received Presidential assent, despite a later Union amendment.

Critics argue that the reservation power can be misused to delay or block progressive State legislation, especially when the Union and the State are ruled by different political parties. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that the Governor should reserve a Bill only on the advice of the State Council of Ministers, except in cases of patent unconstitutionality. The Punchhi Commission (2010) echoed this, suggesting a time‑limit for Presidential decision to prevent indefinite pendency. As of 2023, several Bills — such as the Kerala Police (Amendment) Bill, 2021 — have remained pending with the President for over two years, highlighting the practical tension in this federal checkpoint.

Technique: Identifying the Constitutional Trigger for Reservation
A State Legislature passes a Bill regulating the use of groundwater, a subject under Entry 17 of the State List. The Union has enacted a comprehensive law on water conservation under Entry 56 of the Union List. The Governor reserves the State Bill for the President’s consideration. On what constitutional basis can the President legitimately withhold assent?
The President can withhold assent only if the State Bill is repugnant to the Union law on a Concurrent List subject (Article 254) or if it violates a constitutional provision (e.g., fundamental rights). Groundwater regulation is a State List subject; the Union law under Entry 56 deals with regulation of inter‑State rivers, not intra‑State groundwater. Since there is no Concurrent List overlap, repugnancy under Article 254 does not arise. Absent a constitutional violation, the President has no legitimate ground to withhold assent. The correct answer: the President cannot withhold assent because the State Bill falls squarely within the State’s exclusive legislative competence and does not conflict with any Union law on a Concurrent subject.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the Governor’s reservation power, first locate the legislative list (Union, State, Concurrent). If the Bill is on a State List subject and no Concurrent List Union law exists, the reservation is constitutionally suspect. Remember the Nabam Rebia principle: the Governor acts on ministerial advice unless the Constitution expressly requires discretion. Cite Article 200, Article 254, and the Nabam Rebia judgment for full marks.

Article 312: The All‑India Services and the Federal Balance

Article 312 authorises Parliament to create one or more All‑India Services (AIS) common to the Union and the States, provided the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) passes a resolution supported by not less than two‑thirds of the members present and voting. The Indian Administrative Service (IAS), Indian Police Service (IPS), and Indian Forest Service (IFoS) are the three extant AIS. Their creation reflects a deliberate constitutional design to maintain a cadre of officers who serve both the Centre and the States, thereby fostering administrative integration while preserving State autonomy in personnel matters.

Recruitment to the AIS is conducted by the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) under the All‑India Services Act, 1951. The Union controls the recruitment rules, training (at the Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration for IAS, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel National Police Academy for IPS, and Indira Gandhi National Forest Academy for IFoS), and the initial allocation of officers to State cadres. However, once allocated, the officers serve under the administrative control of the respective State governments for postings, promotions (up to the level of Secretary), and disciplinary proceedings. The Union retains a residual control through the Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) for matters such as deputation to the Centre, central deputation tenure, and the All‑India Services (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969.

The federal tension inherent in this dual control has been the subject of judicial scrutiny. In Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977), the Supreme Court upheld the Union’s power to transfer an IAS officer from a State cadre to a central deputation without the State’s consent, ruling that the All‑India Services Act empowers the Centre to manage the service as a whole. Conversely, in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (1992), the Court held that the State government’s authority over cadre postings is not absolute; the Union may issue guidelines to ensure equitable distribution of officers across States, especially for the IAS and IPS.

The 42nd Amendment (1976) added clause (2) to Article 312, enabling Parliament to create new All‑India Services by law, provided the Rajya Sabha resolution requirement is met. This provision was used to establish the Indian Forest Service in 1966 (by an earlier Act) and later the Indian Revenue Service (Customs and Central Excise) was proposed but not enacted. The creation of any new AIS requires a broad political consensus, reflecting the federal sensitivity of the matter.

In practice, the AIS acts as a binding agent of cooperative federalism. Officers on central deputation bring State‑level experience to Union ministries, while those returning to State cadres carry exposure to national policy formulation. The DoPT’s annual “Central Deputation Reserve” and the “State Deputation Reserve” mechanisms attempt to balance the needs of both levels. However, recurring disputes — such as the 2021 tussle between the West Bengal government and the Centre over the transfer of three IPS officers — illustrate the fragility of this balance. The Supreme Court, in State of West Bengal v. Union of India (2021), reiterated that the Centre’s power to call officers on deputation is statutory and must be exercised in consultation with the State, failing which it may amount to an infringement of the State’s executive authority under Article 162.

The 14th Finance Commission's 42% Vertical Devolution: Design, Implementation and Fiscal Impact

The Fourteenth Finance Commission (FC‑XIV), chaired by Dr. Y. V. Reddy and constituted in 2013, submitted its report in December 2014 for the award period 2015‑16 to 2019‑20. Its most consequential recommendation was to raise the vertical share of States in the net proceeds of Union taxes from 32 % (as recommended by the Thirteenth Finance Commission) to 42 %. This 10‑percentage‑point increase was the largest single jump in vertical devolution since the First Finance Commission and was intended to enhance the fiscal autonomy of States by giving them a larger untied resource envelope.

The Commission justified the increase on three grounds. First, the introduction of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) would subsume many Union and State indirect taxes, altering the revenue architecture; a higher vertical share would compensate States for the loss of autonomy in setting indirect tax rates. Second, the Commission observed that the Union’s own revenue buoyancy had improved, allowing a larger transfer without jeopardising Union fiscal commitments. Third, the Commission argued that States were better positioned to design and implement schemes tailored to local needs, and that tied grants (Centrally Sponsored Schemes) often distorted priorities.

Implementation began with the 2015‑16 Union Budget, which accepted the 42 % devolution. The actual devolution for 2015‑16 was ₹5.06 lakh crore, rising to ₹7.03 lakh crore in 2019‑20, reflecting both the higher percentage and growth in the divisible pool. However, the divisible pool excludes cesses and surcharges, which the Union continued to levy extensively (e.g., the petroleum cess, the health and education cess). Consequently, the effective share of States in total Union tax revenue remained around 35‑36 %, prompting criticism that the 42 % figure was notional.

On the horizontal front, FC‑XIV revised the devolution formula: it assigned 17.5 % weight to population (1971 Census), 10 % to demographic change (2011 Census), 50 % to income distance, 15 % to forest cover, and 7.5 % to area. The shift from the 1971 population base to a composite of 1971 and 2011 data was a compromise between the demands of high‑growth States and the concerns of populous States. The Commission also recommended a revenue deficit grant of ₹1.94 lakh crore for 11 States and a performance‑based grant for local bodies.

The fiscal impact was significant. States’ own tax revenues grew modestly, but the enlarged untied transfers allowed several States to increase capital expenditure. For instance, Bihar’s capital outlay rose from ₹12,000 crore in 2014‑15 to ₹22,000 crore in 2018‑19. However, the Union’s fiscal deficit targets were strained, and the 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) retained the 42 % vertical share but introduced a “Cess and Surcharge Grant” of 1 % of gross tax revenue to partially offset the exclusion of cesses. The 14th FC’s legacy thus lies in redefining the vertical balance, even as the treatment of cesses remains an unresolved federal issue.

The GST Compensation Cess: Structure, Revenue Shortfall and the 2022 Extension Decision

The Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017, enacted under Article 279A, established a compensation cess to guarantee States a 14 % annual growth in GST revenue over the base year 2015‑16 for a five‑year transition period (July 2017‑June 2022). The cess is levied on select “sin” and luxury goods — tobacco products, aerated waters, automobiles above a certain engine capacity, and coal — at rates specified in the GST (Compensation to States) Act and subsequent notifications. The proceeds are credited to the GST Compensation Fund, from which States are paid bi‑monthly.

During the first three years, cess collections broadly matched the projected compensation requirement. However, the COVID‑19 pandemic caused a sharp contraction in economic activity in 2020‑21, leading to a shortfall of approximately ₹1.1 lakh crore in the Compensation Fund. The Union, citing the Act’s provision that the Centre “shall” compensate States, initially proposed that States borrow from the market with the Centre providing a back‑stop guarantee. After protracted negotiations in the GST Council, a consensus emerged in October 2020 for the Centre to borrow ₹1.1 lakh crore on behalf of States and pass it on as back‑to‑back loans, which would not count towards States’ fiscal deficit limits.

The shortfall persisted into 2021‑22, and the original five‑year compensation period was due to end in June 2022. In its 47th meeting (June 2022), the GST Council, by consensus, decided to extend the levy of the compensation cess until March 2026 to repay the borrowings and meet any residual compensation arrears. The extension was formalised through the GST (Compensation to States) Amendment Act, 2022. The Council also agreed to rationalise the cess rate structure, merging certain rates and removing the cess on electric vehicles to promote green mobility.

The extension decision illustrates the GST Council’s role as a federal bargaining forum: the Union held one‑third voting weight, States two‑thirds, yet the outcome was reached by consensus without a formal vote. The 2022 amendment also clarified that the cess would continue to be levied at the same rates on the existing basket of goods, and that any future changes would require a three‑fourths weighted majority. As of March 2024, the Compensation Fund has repaid a substantial portion of the 2020‑21 borrowings, but the Centre continues to service the debt from cess collections, underscoring the fiscal interdependence created by the GST architecture.

Article 3 and the Reorganisation of States: Federal Consent, Parliamentary Procedure, and Recent Examples

Article 3 of the Constitution empowers Parliament to form a new State, increase or diminish the area of any State, alter the boundaries or the name of any State. The procedure is distinct from an ordinary constitutional amendment: a Bill under Article 3 requires the prior recommendation of the President and, before introduction, must be referred to the Legislature of the affected State for expressing its views within a period specified by the President. The State Legislature’s opinion is not binding; Parliament may proceed after the period expires or after the views are received. The Bill is passed by a simple majority in each House of Parliament and receives the President’s assent, after which the new State or altered boundaries come into existence on a date appointed by the President.

The first large‑scale exercise of this power was the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, which redrew the map on linguistic lines, creating 14 States and 6 Union Territories. Subsequent reorganisations followed a similar pattern. The States of Uttarakhand, Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand were carved out of Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Bihar respectively by the Uttar Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000, the Madhya Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000 and the Bihar Reorganisation Act, 2000. Each Bill was introduced after the President’s recommendation, referred to the concerned State Legislatures, and passed by Parliament without a special majority.

The most politically sensitive use of Article 3 in recent times was the creation of Telangana in 2014. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 was passed after the President’s recommendation, the Andhra Pradesh Legislative Assembly and Council were consulted, and the Bill received Presidential assent on 1 March 2014. Telangana formally came into existence on 2 June 2014. The exercise highlighted that the State Legislature’s view is advisory; the Andhra Pradesh Legislature had expressed strong opposition, yet Parliament proceeded.

In 2019, the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 bifurcated the erstwhile State of Jammu & Kashmir into two Union Territories — Jammu & Kashmir (with a legislature) and Ladakh (without a legislature). The Bill was introduced after the President’s recommendation under Article 3, but because the State was under President’s Rule (Article 356), the requirement of referring the Bill to the State Legislature was dispensed with by a Presidential order. The Act received Presidential assent on 9 August 2019 and came into force on 31 October 2019. This episode underscored that the procedural safeguard of State consultation can be overridden when the State is under Central rule.

Article 3 thus provides a flexible mechanism for territorial reorganisation while preserving a federal consultative step. The Supreme Court, in State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963), held that the power under Article 3 is plenary and not subject to judicial review on the ground of political expediency, provided the constitutional procedure is followed. The Court reiterated this in Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010), confirming that the President’s recommendation and the referral to the State Legislature are mandatory procedural requirements, but the substance of the reorganisation remains a political question for Parliament.

The Role of the Comptroller and Auditor General in Federal Audits: Audit of Union, State, and Local Bodies

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India, appointed under Article 148, is the supreme audit institution for both the Union and the States. The CAG’s mandate extends to the audit of all receipts and expenditure of the Union and State governments, including bodies substantially financed from the Consolidated Fund of India or of a State. The CAG submits audit reports to the President (for Union accounts) and to the Governor (for State accounts), which are then laid before Parliament and the respective State Legislatures. This dual reporting structure reinforces the federal character of financial accountability.

With the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992), the CAG’s audit jurisdiction was extended to Panchayats and Municipalities. Section 14 of the Comptroller and Auditor General’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971, as amended, empowers the CAG to audit the accounts of local bodies that receive grants from the State Consolidated Fund. In practice, the CAG conducts performance audits of centrally sponsored schemes implemented through Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs), such as the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (MGNREGS) and the Smart Cities Mission.

Recent CAG reports illustrate the breadth of this federal audit. The 2022 Report on the GST Compensation Cess (Report No. 12 of 2022) examined the adequacy of cess collections, the borrowing mechanism adopted in 2020‑21, and the repayment schedule extended to March 2026. The 2023 Report on Centrally Sponsored Schemes (Report No. 5 of 2023) highlighted delays in fund release, diversion of funds, and weak monitoring at the State level, recommending a stronger role for State Finance Commissions in ensuring fiscal discipline. The 2021 Audit of the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) and State Disaster Response Funds (SDRFs) pointed out discrepancies in the utilisation of funds across States and recommended a uniform accounting framework.

The CAG also audits the accounts of the Finance Commission’s recommendations, particularly the utilisation of revenue deficit grants and performance‑based grants for local bodies. In its 2020‑21 audit of the 15th Finance Commission’s health and education conditionalities, the CAG noted that several States failed to meet the prescribed output indicators, resulting in the withholding of a portion of the performance grant. This audit feeds back into the Finance Commission’s subsequent recommendations, creating a closed loop of accountability.

Judicially, the Supreme Court in Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010) affirmed that the CAG’s reports are not merely advisory; they constitute a constitutional check on executive financial management. The Court held that the legislature’s failure to discuss CAG reports undermines the constitutional scheme of financial accountability. Thus, the CAG operates as a pivotal institution that binds the Union, States, and local governments within a common audit framework, reinforcing cooperative federalism through transparency.

A question asks: “The CAG audits the accounts of the GST Council.” Is this statement correct?

The GST Council is a constitutional body under Article 279A, but its secretariat is funded by the Union; the Council’s own accounts are not separately audited by the CAG. The CAG audits the Union’s GST revenue collections and the compensation cess fund, not the Council’s internal decisions.

Constitutional Scheme for Inter‑State Trade and Commerce under Articles 301‑307 and Its Interaction with the GST Regime

Part XIII of the Constitution (Articles 301‑307) guarantees freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India. Article 301 declares that trade, commerce and intercourse shall be free, subject to the other provisions of this Part. Article 302 empowers Parliament to impose restrictions on such freedom in the public interest. Article 303 prohibits Parliament and State Legislatures from giving preference to one State over another or from making laws that discriminate between States in matters of trade and commerce. Article 304(a) allows a State to impose taxes on goods imported from other States if similar goods are taxed within the State, while Article 304(b) permits reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade in the public interest, subject to Presidential assent. Article 305 saves existing laws and laws providing for State monopolies, and Article 307 authorises Parliament to appoint an authority for carrying out the provisions of Articles 301‑304.

The 101st Constitutional Amendment (2016) introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST), subsuming a large number of State and Union indirect taxes. GST is a destination‑based consumption tax, and the GST Council, a constitutional body under Article 279A, decides on rates, exemptions and threshold limits. While GST has largely replaced the earlier regime of State VAT, Central Excise, Service Tax, and entry taxes, Articles 301‑307 continue to govern trade in goods that remain outside GST — notably petroleum products, alcoholic liquor for human consumption, and electricity. For these goods, State entry taxes, octroi, and other barriers are still tested against the freedom guaranteed by Article 301 and the non‑discrimination mandate of Article 303.

The Supreme Court has interpreted the interplay between the GST regime and Part XIII in several judgments. In State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu (1994), the Court held that a State law imposing a tax on entry of goods from other States violates Article 304(a) unless the same tax is levied on locally produced goods. In Union of India v. M/s. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022), the Court examined whether the GST Council’s recommendation to levy Integrated GST (IGST) on inter‑State supply of goods could be challenged under Article 301. The Court ruled that IGST, being a constitutional tax under Article 246A, is expressly authorised by the Constitution and therefore does not infringe the freedom of inter‑State trade; the GST Council’s decisions, when made in accordance with the constitutional framework, are immune from Article 301 challenge.

However, the Court in Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana (2017) struck down a State law imposing an entry tax on goods already subject to GST, holding that such a tax amounted to a restriction on inter‑State trade not saved by Article 304(a) because the goods were not similarly taxed within the State. This jurisprudence clarifies that while GST has unified the indirect tax base, the constitutional guarantee of free inter‑State trade remains a benchmark for any residual State levies.

Parliament’s power under Article 302 to impose restrictions in the public interest has been used to enact the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, and the Prevention of Black‑Marketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980. These laws continue to operate alongside GST, and their validity is tested against Article 303’s non‑discrimination clause. The GST Council’s voting structure — one‑third weight for the Union, two‑thirds for the States, with a three‑fourths weighted majority for decisions — reflects the federal bargain embedded in Part XIII, ensuring that neither the Centre nor a group of States can unilaterally impose trade barriers.

Doctrine of Repugnancy under Article 254: Judicial Interpretation, State Legislative Competence, and Presidential Assent

Article 254 resolves conflicts between Union and State laws on subjects enumerated in the Concurrent List (List III of the Seventh Schedule). Clause (1) provides that if a State law is repugnant to a Union law, the Union law prevails and the State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy. Clause (2) creates an exception: if the State law has been reserved for the President’s consideration and has received his assent, the State law prevails in that State, notwithstanding the repugnancy, unless Parliament subsequently enacts a law overriding it.

The Supreme Court has laid down a three‑step test for repugnancy in M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979): (i) the two laws must operate in the same field; (ii) they must be inconsistent or contradictory; (iii) the inconsistency must be such that both cannot stand together. In Hoechst Pharma Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983), the Court clarified that mere possibility of conflict is insufficient; there must be a direct collision making simultaneous obedience impossible. The doctrine does not apply where the Union law occupies the entire field (doctrine of occupation of field), a principle affirmed in Zameer Ahmed Latifur Rehman v. State of Maharashtra (2010).

The Presidential assent route under Article 254(2) has been invoked sparingly. A notable instance is the Tamil Nadu Land Reforms (Amendment) Act, 1978, which was reserved and received Presidential assent, allowing it to prevail over the Union’s Land Acquisition Act, 1894, in Tamil Nadu. In 2020, the State of Rajasthan reserved the Rajasthan Agricultural Produce Markets (Amendment) Bill, 2020, which sought to modify the operation of the Central Farm Acts; the President withheld assent, and the State law did not become operative, illustrating the political dimension of the assent power.

Recent jurisprudence has examined repugnancy in the GST context. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2021), the Supreme Court held that the Central GST Act, being a Union law on a Concurrent List entry (taxes on goods and services), prevails over any State law that attempts to impose an additional tax on the same supply. The Court reiterated that the GST Council’s recommendations, once enacted as law, acquire the status of Union law for the purposes of Article 254.

The interplay between Article 254 and the doctrine of pith and substance is critical. When a State law is challenged as encroaching on a Union subject, courts first apply the pith and substance test to determine the true character of the law. If the law falls within the Concurrent List, Article 254 becomes the governing provision for resolving any conflict with Union legislation. This layered analysis ensures that federal legislative competence is respected while providing a clear hierarchy for conflict resolution.

The Governor's Discretionary Powers and the Federal Balance

The Governor, as the constitutional head of a State, holds significant powers that can profoundly impact the federal balance, particularly through the exercise of discretion. Article 163 of the Constitution states that the Governor shall act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, except insofar as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions in his discretion. This 'discretion' is not absolute but is circumscribed by constitutional provisions, conventions, and judicial pronouncements.

Key areas where the Governor's discretion becomes critical in Centre-State relations include:

  1. Appointment of Chief Minister: When no single party secures a clear majority in the State Legislative Assembly, the Governor exercises discretion in inviting the leader of a party or coalition to form the government. This has often led to controversies, with allegations of partisan bias in favour of the ruling party at the Centre. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that the leader of the party or coalition that commands the widest support in the Assembly should be invited. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) and Nabam Rebia & Bamang Felix v. Deputy Speaker (2016) emphasised that the floor of the House is the only constitutionally ordained forum to test the majority of the government.
  2. Dismissal of a Ministry: While a Chief Minister holds office during the pleasure of the Governor, this pleasure is not arbitrary. It is contingent upon the Chief Minister retaining the confidence of the Legislative Assembly. The Governor cannot dismiss a Council of Ministers so long as it enjoys the confidence of the Assembly.
  3. Dissolution of the Legislative Assembly: The Governor's power to dissolve the Assembly is usually exercised on the advice of a Council of Ministers that has lost its majority. However, in situations where a Chief Minister who has lost confidence advises dissolution, the Governor's discretion to refuse such advice and explore alternative government formation becomes crucial.
  4. Reserving Bills for Presidential Consideration (Article 200): The Governor can reserve certain types of State Bills for the President's consideration. This power, though intended for constitutional propriety (e.g., bills repugnant to Union law, those endangering the High Court's position), has been criticised for being used to delay or obstruct State legislation, especially when the State government is politically opposed to the Centre.
  5. Recommending President's Rule (Article 356): The Governor's report is a primary basis for the imposition of President's Rule in a State. The S.R. Bommai judgment significantly curtailed the arbitrary use of Article 356 by making the Governor's report subject to judicial review and requiring objective material to justify the breakdown of constitutional machinery.

The Punchhi Commission (2010) further recommended that the Governor should have fixed five-year terms and that a procedure for impeachment similar to that of the President should be established to ensure greater independence and reduce political interference. The consistent judicial emphasis has been on the Governor acting as a constitutional functionary, not an agent of the Centre, and ensuring that discretionary powers are exercised judiciously and in good faith, upholding the spirit of federalism.

State Fiscal Autonomy: Debt, Deficits, and Conditionalities in Federal Transfers

While the Finance Commission provides a framework for vertical and horizontal devolution of funds, the actual fiscal health and autonomy of states are significantly influenced by their debt levels, deficit management, and the increasing conditionalities attached to central transfers. These factors often create friction in Centre-State financial relations, impacting the states' ability to formulate independent development policies.

State Debt and Deficits: States are empowered to borrow within the limits fixed by their respective legislatures, but they require the consent of the Union government if they are indebted to the Union or have given a guarantee in respect of a loan made by the Union (Article 293). This provision gives the Union significant control over state borrowings. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, at the Union level, inspired states to enact their own FRBM Acts, setting targets for fiscal deficit, revenue deficit, and debt-to-GSDP (Gross State Domestic Product) ratios. However, states often face challenges in adhering to these targets due to unforeseen expenditures (e.g., natural disasters, economic downturns), populist measures, or the need to fund critical infrastructure projects. Off-budget borrowings and guarantees by states, which do not directly reflect in their fiscal deficit calculations, have emerged as a concern, potentially masking the true extent of state indebtedness.

Conditionalities in Federal Transfers: Beyond the unconditional transfers recommended by the Finance Commission, a substantial portion of central assistance to states comes through Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) and other grants, which are often tied to specific conditions. These conditionalities, while sometimes aimed at achieving national priorities (e.g., health, education, rural development), can reduce the fiscal autonomy of states. States are often required to contribute a certain share to these schemes, diverting their own resources from state-specific priorities. The 15th Finance Commission, for instance, introduced performance-based grants with conditionalities related to health, education, and power sector reforms, linking transfers to states' performance on specific indicators. While proponents argue this promotes accountability and better outcomes, critics contend it infringes upon states' policy-making space and creates a 'one-size-fits-all' approach, ignoring local needs and capacities.

The increasing reliance on cesses and surcharges by the Union government also impacts state fiscal autonomy. These levies are not part of the divisible pool of taxes shared with states, thereby reducing the quantum of funds available for vertical devolution and increasing the Centre's discretionary fiscal space. The GST compensation cess, though designed to compensate states for revenue losses post-GST implementation, also highlighted the dependence of states on central mechanisms for fiscal stability. Managing state debt, ensuring fiscal discipline, and negotiating the terms of conditional grants remain critical aspects of maintaining a healthy and equitable fiscal federal structure in India.

Devolution to Local Governments: Challenges in Implementing the Third Tier of Federalism

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts of 1992 marked a significant step towards decentralisation, envisioning local self-governments (Panchayats and Municipalities) as the 'third tier' of federalism. These amendments mandated the establishment of these bodies, provided for their regular elections, reserved seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and women, and created State Election Commissions (SECs) and State Finance Commissions (SFCs). However, the effective implementation of this third tier faces substantial challenges, primarily revolving around the '3 Fs': Funds, Functions, and Functionaries.

  1. Funds (Financial Devolution):
    • Inadequate Own Revenue: Local bodies often have limited powers to levy and collect taxes, making them heavily reliant on grants from state governments. Their own revenue sources (e.g., property tax, professional tax, user charges) are often insufficient or poorly collected.
    • State Finance Commissions' Recommendations: While SFCs are mandated to recommend the distribution of state taxes and grants-in-aid to local bodies, their recommendations are often not fully implemented by state governments. This leads to persistent financial shortfalls.
    • Dependence on State Grants: The substantial dependence on state grants undermines the financial autonomy of local bodies, making them subservient to state governments rather than autonomous units of self-governance.
  2. Functions (Functional Devolution):
    • Incomplete Transfer of Subjects: The 73rd Amendment lists 29 subjects (Eleventh Schedule) and the 74th Amendment lists 18 subjects (Twelfth Schedule) that can be devolved to Panchayats and Municipalities, respectively. However, many state governments have not fully transferred these functions, or have done so only partially or conditionally.
    • Lack of Clarity: Even where functions are devolved, there is often a lack of clarity regarding the specific roles, responsibilities, and powers of local bodies vis-à-vis state departments, leading to overlapping jurisdictions and inefficiencies.
    • Centralised Planning: Despite the constitutional mandate for District Planning Committees (DPCs) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPCs), planning often remains centralised at the state level, limiting the ability of local bodies to address local needs effectively.
  3. Functionaries (Administrative Devolution):
    • Shortage of Staff: Local bodies often suffer from a severe shortage of qualified administrative and technical staff, hindering their capacity to plan, implement, and monitor development programs.
    • Control by State Cadres: Key administrative positions in local bodies are often filled by state government officials who may not be accountable to the elected local representatives, diluting local control.
    • Lack of Training and Capacity Building: Elected representatives and staff of local bodies often lack adequate training and capacity-building opportunities, impacting their effectiveness in governance.

The PESA Act, 1996, which extends the provisions of the 73rd Amendment to Scheduled Areas with modifications, also faces similar challenges in ensuring genuine devolution of power to Gram Sabhas, particularly concerning control over natural resources and local development. Overcoming these challenges requires greater political will from state governments, robust implementation of SFC recommendations, and a clear demarcation of roles and responsibilities to truly empower the third tier of federalism.

Resolving Inter-State Land Boundary Disputes and Resource Conflicts

Beyond river water disputes, India's federal landscape is marked by numerous inter-state land boundary disputes and conflicts over shared resources (other than water), which often stem from historical factors, administrative ambiguities, and linguistic reorganisation. These disputes pose significant challenges to cooperative federalism and occasionally escalate into law and order problems.

Historical Context and Origins: Many land boundary disputes trace their origins to the colonial era, where administrative boundaries were often drawn without precise surveys or clear demarcation. The States Reorganisation Act, 1956, and subsequent reorganisations, while largely based on linguistic principles, left certain areas contested due to mixed populations, economic interests, or differing interpretations of historical records. For instance, the Maharashtra-Karnataka boundary dispute over Belagavi (Belgaum) and surrounding Marathi-speaking areas dates back to the reorganisation of states, with both states claiming the region.

Nature of Disputes:

  1. Territorial Claims: Direct claims over specific villages, districts, or regions based on linguistic majority, historical ties, or administrative convenience. Examples include the Assam-Meghalaya dispute over 12 areas, the Odisha-Andhra Pradesh dispute over the Kotia group of villages, and the Haryana-Himachal Pradesh dispute over Parwanoo.
  2. Resource Sharing: Conflicts over shared forest resources, mineral wealth, or even administrative control over specific projects located near borders.
  3. Administrative Ambiguity: Lack of clear demarcation on the ground, leading to disputes over jurisdiction for law enforcement, revenue collection, and development projects.

Mechanisms for Resolution: Unlike inter-state river water disputes, which have a specific tribunal mechanism under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, there is no single, overarching constitutional or statutory framework for resolving land boundary disputes. The approaches typically include:

  1. Bilateral Negotiations: States are often encouraged to resolve disputes through direct talks and mutual agreement. However, these often fail due to entrenched positions and political sensitivities.
  2. Union Government Intervention: The Union government plays a crucial role as a mediator. It can facilitate discussions, constitute ad-hoc commissions (e.g., Mahajan Commission for Maharashtra-Karnataka in 1966, whose recommendations were not fully accepted by both states), or use its administrative authority to demarcate boundaries where ambiguity exists. The Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal ministry for these disputes.
  3. Judicial Recourse: States can approach the Supreme Court under its original jurisdiction (Article 131) for disputes between states. However, the Court often prefers to encourage political resolution, especially for highly sensitive territorial claims, as judicial solutions can be difficult to implement without political buy-in.
  4. Boundary Commissions: Occasionally, specific commissions are set up to survey and demarcate boundaries, but their recommendations are not always binding or accepted by all parties.

The resolution of these disputes requires a combination of political will, impartial mediation by the Union, and a spirit of compromise from the states. The absence of a robust, time-bound dispute resolution mechanism for land boundaries continues to be a lacuna in India's federal framework, often leading to protracted conflicts and hindering regional development. The ongoing efforts to digitise land records and use satellite imagery for precise demarcation offer a potential path towards more accurate and less contentious boundary management.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Governor's constitutional discretion with arbitrary power; discretion is always subject to constitutional limits and judicial review.
  • Assuming that all recommendations of State Finance Commissions are automatically implemented by state governments; they are advisory and often face implementation gaps.
  • Believing that the 73rd and 74th Amendments have fully achieved functional and financial autonomy for local bodies; significant challenges in devolution of '3 Fs' persist.
  • Overlooking the impact of off-budget borrowings and state guarantees on the true fiscal health of states, which may not be reflected in headline deficit figures.
  • Treating inter-state land boundary disputes as having the same resolution mechanism as river water disputes; land disputes lack a dedicated statutory tribunal framework.
  • Assuming that conditionalities in central transfers are always detrimental; while they impact autonomy, they can also drive national priorities and performance.
  • Neglecting the role of the Union government as a mediator in inter-state land boundary disputes, often preferring political solutions over judicial ones.

The 15th Finance Commission’s Revenue Deficit Grants and Their Effect on State Fiscal Discipline

The 15th Finance Commission (FC‑XV), chaired by N. K. Singh, introduced a distinct category of revenue deficit grants for the award period 2021‑26. Unlike the traditional tax‑devolution formula, these grants are targeted at states whose assessed revenue expenditure exceeds their revenue receipts after accounting for the devolved share of central taxes. The Commission identified 17 states as eligible and recommended a total envelope of approximately ₹2.9 lakh crore over the five‑year period. The grants are released in annual instalments contingent on the state meeting the fiscal deficit ceiling prescribed under the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act and on maintaining a prescribed revenue deficit trajectory.

The design of these grants reflects a shift from pure gap‑filling to incentive‑based fiscal discipline. By linking disbursement to compliance with FRBM targets, the Commission sought to prevent moral hazard — the risk that states might expand expenditure irresponsibly in anticipation of central bail‑outs. The conditionalities also require states to publish medium‑term fiscal plans and to adhere to the prescribed borrowing limits set by the Union Ministry of Finance. In practice, states such as Punjab, Kerala and West Bengal have received sizable tranches, while others have seen reductions when they breached deficit thresholds.

Empirical evidence from the first two years of the award period suggests a mixed impact. States that received the grants managed to contain revenue deficits within the glide path, but the overall fiscal deficit of several recipient states remained above the 3% of GSDP benchmark due to capital expenditure pressures. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has flagged instances where grants were used to finance recurring expenditure rather than to create productive assets, underscoring the need for tighter expenditure‑tracking mechanisms. The 15th FC’s own mid‑term review recommended strengthening the monitoring framework by integrating the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) with state treasury databases to ensure real‑time visibility of grant utilisation.

From a federalism perspective, the revenue deficit grants represent a calibrated central intervention that respects state autonomy while enforcing macro‑fiscal stability. They differ from the earlier plan‑grant regime in that they are formula‑driven, transparent, and subject to parliamentary scrutiny through the Finance Commission’s report. However, critics argue that the grants may inadvertently reinforce a dependency culture, especially in states with structural revenue weaknesses such as low tax buoyancy and high committed expenditure on salaries and pensions. The ongoing debate centres on whether the next Finance Commission should continue the revenue deficit grant window or replace it with a more comprehensive performance‑based incentive system that rewards outcomes in health, education and infrastructure.

The Federalism Implications of the National Education Policy 2020 on Curriculum, Language and Funding

The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020, approved by the Union Cabinet, proposes a sweeping restructuring of the Indian education system with direct implications for Centre‑State relations. Education resides in the Concurrent List (Entry 25, List III), giving both Parliament and state legislatures legislative competence. The NEP leverages this concurrency by setting a national curricular framework — the National Curriculum Framework for School Education (NCFSE) — while leaving the detailed syllabus, textbook preparation and medium of instruction to the states. This arrangement seeks to balance uniformity in learning outcomes with regional linguistic and cultural diversity.

On language, the policy endorses the three‑language formula but recommends that the medium of instruction, at least until Grade 5, be the home language or mother tongue. States retain the authority to decide the third language, a provision that has sparked political debate in non‑Hindi‑speaking states. The policy also proposes a National Assessment Centre (PARAKH) to set standards for student assessment, which will operate under the Union Ministry of Education but will require state participation for implementation. This creates a cooperative federalism dynamic where the Centre defines benchmarks and states adapt pedagogy.

Funding is the most contentious federal dimension. The NEP reiterates the long‑standing target of raising public investment in education to 6% of GDP, a goal that has remained elusive for decades. The policy envisages a larger share of central spending through schemes such as Samagra Shiksha, the Higher Education Financing Agency (HEFA) and the proposed National Research Foundation (NRF). However, the bulk of school education expenditure — teacher salaries, infrastructure, mid‑day meals — remains a state responsibility. The 15th Finance Commission’s education‑specific grants and the performance‑based incentives for learning outcomes are intended to nudge states toward higher spending efficiency. Yet, the conditionalities attached to these grants (e.g., adoption of the NCFSE, participation in PARAKH) have been criticised as encroaching on state legislative space.

Implementation experience since 2020 shows varied state responses. Some states have swiftly aligned their curricula with the NCFSE and introduced mother‑tongue instruction in early grades, while others have delayed citing resource constraints and linguistic politics. The Union Ministry’s role has largely been advisory, with the Central Advisory Board of Education (CABE) serving as the primary coordination forum. The policy’s success hinges on sustained fiscal transfers, capacity building at the state and district levels, and a genuine partnership that respects the constitutional division of powers while pursuing the shared goal of equitable quality education.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "The NEP 2020 makes the three‑language formula mandatory for all states." Identify the error and explain the correct constitutional position.
The statement is false. The NEP 2020 recommends the three‑language formula but does not make it mandatory; education is a Concurrent List subject, and the policy explicitly leaves the choice of the third language to the states. The Constitution does not prescribe a language formula; it only empowers both Centre and states to legislate on education. Therefore, any claim that the NEP imposes a binding three‑language requirement misreads both the policy document and the federal distribution of legislative powers.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mixes a policy recommendation with constitutional provisions, first locate the subject in the Seventh Schedule. If it falls under the Concurrent List, remember that both Centre and states can legislate, but central law prevails only in case of repugnancy (Art. 254). Policies like NEP are executive guidelines; they become binding on states only when enacted into law or tied to conditional fiscal transfers. Look for keywords such as "recommends", "envisages", "proposes" versus "mandates", "requires", "shall".

The Role of the Inter‑State Council in Coordinating National Health Missions and Pandemic Response

The Inter‑State Council (ISC), constituted under Article 263, is the premier institutional platform for Centre‑State consultation on matters of common interest. While its meetings have been infrequent — only 12 meetings since its inception in 1990 — the Council gained renewed relevance during the COVID‑19 pandemic. The Union government convened a special ISC meeting in August 2020 to discuss the National Health Mission (NHM), the rollout of Ayushman Bharat‑Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM‑JAY), and the coordination of vaccine procurement and distribution.

Health is a State subject (Entry 6, List II), but the Union plays a significant role through centrally sponsored schemes, the National Health Mission, and regulatory bodies such as the National Medical Commission. The ISC’s mandate allows it to investigate and discuss subjects in which some or all states have a common interest, making it a natural forum for aligning state health plans with national priorities. During the pandemic, the Council facilitated the sharing of best practices on containment zones, oxygen supply chains, and the use of the CoWIN digital platform for vaccination registration.

Despite its potential, the ISC’s effectiveness is constrained by its advisory nature; its recommendations are not binding on either the Centre or the states. The Council’s secretariat, housed in the Ministry of Home Affairs, lacks independent research capacity, and the agenda is often driven by the Union. States have occasionally expressed reluctance to attend, citing the dominance of central ministries in decision‑making. The 15th Finance Commission’s health‑sector performance grants, which require states to meet defined service‑delivery indicators, have introduced a fiscal incentive that complements the ISC’s coordinative role.

Going forward, strengthening the ISC’s health coordination function would require: (a) a standing sub‑committee on health with representation from state health secretaries, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, and the NITI Aayog; (b) a mandatory annual meeting calendar; and (c) a mechanism to track implementation of ISC resolutions through a shared dashboard. Such reforms would transform the Council from a sporadic talk‑shop into a durable pillar of cooperative federalism in health governance.

The Federal Dimensions of the National Infrastructure Pipeline: Centre‑State Project Financing and Risk Sharing

The National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP), launched in December 2019, outlines an investment target of ₹111 lakh crore for the period 2020‑25 across sectors such as energy, roads, railways, urban infrastructure and digital connectivity. The pipeline is a joint exercise between the Centre and states, with the Union government contributing roughly 39% of the projected outlay, states 40%, and the private sector the remainder. This financing pattern reflects the federal reality that most infrastructure assets — state highways, urban water supply, power distribution — are owned and operated by state governments or their agencies.

Project preparation and risk allocation are the key federal challenges. The NIP encourages the use of the Viability Gap Funding (VGF) scheme, where the Centre provides up to 20% of project cost as a grant to make projects commercially viable. States are expected to contribute land, right‑of‑way clearances, and a share of VGF. However, delays in land acquisition, environmental clearances, and state‑level regulatory approvals have been the primary bottlenecks. The Centre has responded by setting up the Project Monitoring Group (PMG) under the Prime Minister’s Office to fast‑track clearances, but states retain the authority to grant many permits.

Risk sharing is structured through model concession agreements (MCAs) that allocate construction risk to the private developer, demand risk to the authority (often a state entity), and policy risk to the government. The Centre’s role is largely to standardise MCAs, provide sovereign guarantees for certain payment obligations, and facilitate access to long‑term finance through institutions like the National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF). States, however, must honour payment commitments under power purchase agreements (PPAs) and toll‑road concessions, which has proven difficult for financially stressed state utilities and road corporations.

The 15th Finance Commission’s recommendation to link a portion of state borrowing limits to capital expenditure performance is intended to incentivise states to prioritise NIP projects. Early evidence suggests that states with stronger fiscal positions — Gujarat, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu — have been able to leverage the pipeline more effectively, while fiscally constrained states lag behind. A more equitable federal outcome would require a dedicated central grant window for project preparation in low‑capacity states, coupled with technical assistance from the NITI Aayog’s Infrastructure Development Cell.

The Union’s Role in Managing Inter‑State Migration and Labour Market Integration

Internal migration is a defining feature of India’s labour market, with the 2011 Census recording over 450 million internal migrants, a figure that has likely risen substantially. The Constitution guarantees freedom of movement and residence throughout the territory of India under Article 19(1)(d) and (e), but it also permits reasonable restrictions in the interest of the general public. States have occasionally imposed domicile‑based reservations in public employment and education, and during the COVID‑19 lockdown several states restricted entry of migrant workers, raising federal tensions.

The Union government’s legislative framework for migrant workers includes the Inter‑State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1979, which mandates registration of establishments employing inter‑state migrants and provides for basic welfare provisions. However, enforcement has been weak, and the Act covers only a fraction of the informal workforce. The more recent Code on Social Security, 2020, extends portable benefits such as provident fund and insurance to migrant workers, but its implementation depends on state‑level rule‑making and digital infrastructure.

During the 2020 lockdown, the Union Ministry of Home Affairs issued guidelines under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, directing states to facilitate the movement of stranded migrants and to provide food and shelter. The Supreme Court, exercising suo motu jurisdiction, ordered the Centre and states to ensure free transport and rations. This episode highlighted the Union’s coordinating capacity in a crisis, but also exposed the absence of a permanent institutional mechanism for inter‑state labour market integration.

A durable federal solution would involve: (a) a national migrant worker database, interoperable with state labour departments, to enable portability of entitlements; (b) a constitutional amendment or a central law under Article 249 (national interest) to create a uniform framework for registration, social security and grievance redressal; and (c) a fiscal incentive structure, perhaps through the Finance Commission, rewarding states that adopt portability standards and reduce entry barriers. Until such mechanisms are institutionalised, inter‑state migration will continue to be managed through ad‑hoc executive orders and judicial interventions, undermining the principle of cooperative federalism.
