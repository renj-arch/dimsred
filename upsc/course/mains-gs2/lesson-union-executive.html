1. Constitutional Framework of the Union Executive

1.1 The Constitutional Scheme

The Constitution of India establishes a parliamentary system in which the Union executive is vested formally in the President but exercised in practice by the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. Articles 52 to 78 in Part V lay down the structure, powers and inter‑relationships of the President, the Vice‑President, the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers. The scheme reflects the British model of a ceremonial head of state and a real executive accountable to the lower house, yet it incorporates distinctive features such as the President’s discretionary powers under Article 74(2) and the provision for President’s rule under Article 356. The framers deliberately chose this arrangement to combine stability with democratic accountability, ensuring that the executive remains responsible to the Lok Sabha while the President acts as a constitutional guardian.

1.2 Distribution of Executive Power

Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in the President, who exercises it either directly or through officers subordinate to him in accordance with the Constitution. However, Article 74(1) mandates that the President shall act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers, making the advice binding except in the limited situations where the President may require the Council to reconsider its advice. This constitutional design creates a duality: the President is the formal repository of all executive authority, while the Prime Minister and the Cabinet wield real political power. The balance is maintained by the requirement that the Council of Ministers be collectively responsible to the House of the People under Article 75(3), which forces the real executive to retain the confidence of the elected legislature.

1.3 Role of the Vice‑President

The Vice‑President, elected by an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament under Article 66, serves as the ex‑officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and acts as President when a vacancy occurs or when the President is unable to discharge functions. Although the Vice‑President does not possess independent executive authority, the office provides continuity in the highest constitutional office and plays a crucial role during interregnums, as witnessed during the presidential vacancies of 1969 and 1977. The Vice‑President’s legislative role also influences the functioning of the upper house, thereby indirectly affecting executive‑legislative dynamics.

1.4 Constitutional Amendments Affecting the Executive

Several amendments have reshaped the Union executive. The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review of presidential decisions and expanded the President’s power to promulgate ordinances, but the 44th Amendment (1978) restored the pre‑emergency position by reinstating the binding nature of ministerial advice and limiting ordinance‑making to six weeks. The 91st Amendment (2003) capped the size of the Council of Ministers at fifteen percent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha, aiming to prevent oversized cabinets and promote administrative efficiency. These amendments illustrate the evolving tension between executive authority and parliamentary control.

2. The President: Election, Powers and Position

2.1 Election of the President

The President is elected by an electoral college comprising the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States and Union territories of Delhi and Puducherry, as stipulated in Article 54. The system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote ensures that the President reflects a broad national consensus rather than a simple parliamentary majority. The value of each vote is calculated to give equal weight to the population of each state, thereby balancing federal representation with demographic equity. The election is conducted by the Election Commission under the Presidential and Vice‑Presidential Elections Act, 1952, and disputes are adjudicated by the Supreme Court under Article 71.

2.2 Executive, Legislative and Financial Powers

Article 53 confers the executive power of the Union on the President, who appoints the Prime Minister, other ministers on the Prime Minister’s advice, the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General, governors of states, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and a host of other constitutional functionaries. The President summons and prorogues Parliament, addresses both Houses at the commencement of the first session after each general election, and can dissolve the Lok Sabha on the advice of the Council of Ministers. In the legislative sphere, the President gives assent to bills, may return a non‑money bill for reconsideration, and promulgates ordinances under Article 123 when Parliament is not in session. Financially, the President causes the annual budget to be laid before Parliament, constitutes the Finance Commission under Article 280, and can make advances out of the Contingency Fund to meet unforeseen expenditure.

2.3 Discretionary Powers and Constitutional Safeguards

Although the President generally acts on ministerial advice, the Constitution envisages limited discretionary powers. Under Article 74(2) the President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice, and the President’s decision after such reconsideration is final. The President also exercises discretion in appointing a Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority, as seen in 1989, 1996 and 1998. The power to grant pardons, reprieves and remissions under Article 72 is exercised on the advice of the Council but the President may seek further information. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 is subject to judicial review, reinforcing the principle that discretionary powers are not absolute.

2.4 Position of the President in the Parliamentary System

The President occupies a unique position as the constitutional head of state, symbolising the unity and integrity of the nation. While the President’s role is largely ceremonial, the office serves as a vital check on the executive by virtue of the power to return advice, to seek information, and to act as a guardian of the Constitution during political crises. The President’s address to Parliament outlines the government’s policy agenda, and the assent to legislation provides a final constitutional scrutiny. The office also plays a diplomatic role, receiving credentials of foreign envoys and representing India in state visits, thereby embodying the sovereignty of the Republic.

Example Question
Explain the constitutional provisions that govern the election of the President of India and discuss how the system of proportional representation by single transferable vote ensures federal balance.
The election of the President is governed by Article 54 read with the Presidential and Vice‑Presidential Elections Act, 1952. The electoral college consists of elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States and the Union territories of Delhi and Puducherry. The value of each vote is determined by a formula that equalises the total value of votes of the Parliamentarians with the total value of votes of the State legislators, thereby giving each state a weight proportionate to its population. The single transferable vote system allows electors to rank candidates; if no candidate secures the required quota in the first count, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and his votes are transferred according to the next preferences. This method prevents a simple majority in Parliament from dominating the outcome and ensures that the President enjoys a broad-based mandate reflecting both national and federal dimensions.

The Vice-President of India: Election, Powers and Office

Election of the Vice-President (Article 66)

Article 63 provides that there shall be a Vice-President of India. Under Article 66, the Vice-President is elected by an electoral college consisting of the members of both Houses of Parliament, and the election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, the voting being by secret ballot. Two features distinguish this college from the one that elects the President: the members of the State Legislative Assemblies do not take part, and the nominated members of Parliament do take part. The result is an office chosen by the national legislature alone, which is why the Vice-President is often described as a nominee of Parliament rather than of the Union and the States together. A candidate must be a citizen of India, must have completed thirty-five years of age, and must be qualified for election as a member of the Council of States. The Election Commission conducts the election under the Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952, and disputes relating to the election are decided by the Supreme Court under Article 71.

Term, Resignation and Removal (Articles 67 and 68)

The Vice-President holds office for five years but may be removed earlier. Resignation is addressed to the President. Removal requires a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Council of States and agreed to by the House of the People, and no ground need be assigned; fourteen days' notice of the intention to move the resolution is mandatory. This procedure is deliberately easier than the impeachment of the President under Article 61, which requires proof of a violation of the Constitution: the Vice-President is a parliamentary officer and is answerable to Parliament on a political majority. A vacancy must be filled without delay. An election to fill a vacancy caused by the expiry of the term must be completed before the term ends, and a vacancy caused by death, resignation or removal must be filled as soon as possible; the person so elected serves only the unexpired portion of the term.

Ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Articles 64, 89 and 100)

Article 64 makes the Vice-President the ex-officio Chairman of the Council of States. In this capacity the holder presides over the Rajya Sabha, maintains order, decides points of order and regulates its procedure. The Chairman does not vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote in the case of an equality of votes under Article 100. The office therefore places the second-highest constitutional authority of the Union in charge of the Upper House, giving the Vice-President a continuing legislative role quite apart from the presidency. It is this dual character, standby head of state and presiding officer of the Rajya Sabha, that defines the office.

Acting as President (Article 65)

When a vacancy occurs in the office of President by death, resignation, removal or otherwise, or when the President is unable to discharge functions by reason of absence, illness or any other cause, the Vice-President discharges those functions until a new President is elected or the President resumes duty. Article 62 requires that a vacancy caused by death, resignation or removal be filled within six months, so the Vice-President's tenure as acting head of state is necessarily temporary; while acting as President the occupant exercises all the powers of the President and the Rajya Sabha is presided over by its Deputy Chairman. The office has twice supplied the head of state in this way: in 1969, after the death of President Zakir Husain, and in 1977, after the death of President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed.

Position and Significance

The Vice-President possesses no independent executive authority; the real executive is the Council of Ministers. The office is nonetheless important because it guarantees continuity in the headship of state and because the occupant presides over the Rajya Sabha, where the government's legislative programme is scrutinised and where the chamber's federal and reviewing functions are exercised. A comparison with the President is instructive: the President is elected by an electoral college that includes the States, is removable only by impeachment for a violation of the Constitution, and holds the formal executive power of the Union, while the Vice-President is elected by Parliament alone, is removable by an effective majority of the Rajya Sabha, and holds no executive power save as a stand-in. The two offices together ensure that there is never a vacancy in the constitutional headship of the Republic.

3. The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers

3.1 Appointment and Tenure of the Prime Minister

Article 75(1) provides that the Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President, and the other ministers shall be appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. In practice, the President invites the leader of the party or coalition that commands a majority in the Lok Sabha to form the government. The Prime Minister holds office during the pleasure of the President, but the pleasure is constitutionally tied to the confidence of the Lok Sabha; a loss of majority triggers resignation or dissolution. The 91st Amendment limits the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, to fifteen percent of the Lok Sabha’s strength, reinforcing the principle of a compact executive.

3.2 Powers and Functions of the Prime Minister

The Prime Minister is the keystone of the Cabinet system. He allocates portfolios, chairs Cabinet meetings, coordinates policy across ministries, and acts as the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers. Under Article 78, the Prime Minister furnishes information to the President regarding the administration of the Union and proposals for legislation. The Prime Minister also advises the President on the appointment of key constitutional authorities, the summoning and prorogation of Parliament, and the issuance of ordinances. In foreign affairs, the Prime Minister represents India at summit meetings and directs the Ministry of External Affairs, while the President performs ceremonial diplomatic functions.

3.3 Collective Responsibility and Individual Responsibility

Article 75(3) enshrines the principle of collective responsibility: the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the House of the People. This means that a vote of no‑confidence against any minister is deemed a vote against the entire Council, compelling the Prime Minister to either resign or seek a dissolution. Individual responsibility, on the other hand, requires each minister to answer for the acts of his ministry in Parliament. The interplay of these two doctrines ensures that the executive remains accountable both as a team and as individual office‑holders, a balance that has been tested during coalition governments where intra‑party dissent can threaten collective unity.

3.4 The Prime Minister's Constitutional Position and the Council of Ministers

Article 74(1) is the pivot of the parliamentary executive: the President shall act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, and that advice cannot be disregarded except through the one-time power to require reconsideration. Article 75(1) provides for the appointment of the Prime Minister by the President and of the other Ministers on the Prime Minister's advice, and Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the House of the People. The Prime Minister's centrality follows from these provisions rather than from any single grant of power. The office determines the size and composition of the Council, allocates portfolios, can demand a Minister's resignation, and, by resigning, brings down the entire Council. Article 78 reinforces the position by requiring the Prime Minister to communicate to the President all decisions of the Council and to furnish such information as the President may call for.

The practical reach of the office has varied with the political context. In the era of single-party majorities the Prime Minister dominated the Cabinet, the party and Parliament; in the coalition era after 1989 the office became a negotiator among allies, and the Cabinet Committee system acquired greater importance as a means of managing coalition partners. The Prime Minister's Office, staffed by civil servants and political advisers, provides continuity and coordination across ministries, while the Cabinet Secretariat services the Cabinet and its committees. The Prime Minister also advises the President on the summoning and prorogation of Parliament, the dissolution of the House of the People, and the appointment of the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General and other constitutional functionaries. The relationship between the Prime Minister and the Cabinet is therefore not fixed by the text of the Constitution: it is settled by the arithmetic of the Lok Sabha, the cohesion of the ruling party, and the conventions of collective responsibility.

4. The Cabinet: Composition, Functions and Collective Responsibility

4.1 Composition and Hierarchy

The Cabinet is the inner core of the Council of Ministers, comprising senior ministers who hold important portfolios such as Home, Finance, Defence, External Affairs and Agriculture. While the Constitution does not define the Cabinet separately, the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, and the Cabinet Secretariat’s directives establish its composition. The Prime Minister decides who belongs to the Cabinet, and the Cabinet Secretary, the senior‑most civil servant, acts as the coordinator and secretary to the Cabinet. The Cabinet meets regularly, usually once a week, to deliberate on major policy issues, legislative proposals and administrative matters.

4.2 Decision‑Making Process

Cabinet decisions are taken by consensus; formal voting is rare. The Prime Minister sets the agenda, and the Cabinet Secretary circulates notes prepared by the concerned ministries. Once a decision is reached, it is recorded as a Cabinet decision and communicated to the relevant ministries for implementation. The principle of collective responsibility obliges all ministers to publicly support Cabinet decisions, even if they privately disagreed. This process ensures coherence in government policy and prevents public dissent that could undermine parliamentary confidence.

4.3 Cabinet Committees and Coordination Mechanisms

To manage the volume of work, the Cabinet operates through several standing committees such as the Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs, the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, the Cabinet Committee on Security, and the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet. These committees are chaired by the Prime Minister or a senior minister and allow detailed scrutiny of specific domains before matters reach the full Cabinet. The Cabinet Secretariat, headed by the Cabinet Secretary, provides the institutional memory and administrative support necessary for the smooth functioning of these committees.

5. Civil Services: Structure, Recruitment and Accountability

5.1 Constitutional Basis and All‑India Services

Articles 310 to 313 provide the constitutional foundation for the civil services. Article 310 establishes the tenure of office of persons serving the Union or a State at the pleasure of the President or the Governor, subject to constitutional safeguards. Article 312 empowers Parliament to create All‑India Services common to the Union and the States, the most prominent being the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), the Indian Police Service (IPS) and the Indian Forest Service (IFoS). These services are recruited by the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) through a competitive examination, and their members serve both the Centre and the States, providing a unified administrative framework that sustains the federal structure.

5.2 Recruitment, Training and Career Progression

The UPSC conducts the Civil Services Examination annually, comprising a preliminary test, a main examination and a personality test. Successful candidates are allocated to various services based on merit and preference. The Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration at Mussoorie provides foundational training for IAS officers, while the Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel National Police Academy at Hyderabad trains IPS officers. Career progression follows a time‑scale and performance‑based promotion system, with officers moving through junior, senior and super‑time scales, culminating in the apex scale of Secretary to the Government of India or Director General of Police.

5.3 Accountability, Conduct Rules and Reforms

The All‑India Services (Conduct) Rules, 1968, and the Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, prescribe standards of integrity, political neutrality and procedural propriety. Disciplinary proceedings are governed by the All‑India Services (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969, which provide for inquiry, penalty and appeal mechanisms. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005‑2009) recommended measures such as lateral entry at senior levels, performance‑based appraisal, and a statutory Civil Services Board to insulate transfers and postings from political interference. Recent initiatives like Mission Karmayogi aim to shift the paradigm from rule‑based to role‑based capacity building, reflecting an ongoing effort to modernise the bureaucracy while preserving its constitutional mandate.

Discretionary Powers of the President in Government Formation and Dissolution

The President’s discretionary space is narrow but decisive when the Lok Sabha fails to produce a clear majority. Article 75(1) empowers the President to appoint the Prime Minister, but the Constitution does not prescribe a formula for a hung Parliament. In practice, the President follows the convention of inviting the leader of the single largest party or pre‑poll alliance to form the government and to prove majority on the floor of the House within a stipulated period. The 1996, 1998 and 1999 episodes illustrate the pattern: the President invited Atal Bihari Vajpayee (1996), then H. D. Deve Gowda (1996), and later Vajpayee again (1998) after assessing the numerical strength and stability of competing claims.

Judicial scrutiny of this discretion arrived in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006), where the Supreme Court held that the President’s decision to dissolve the Assembly in Bihar was subject to judicial review for mala fide exercise. The Court reiterated that while the President acts on ministerial advice under Article 74(1), the advice itself must be constitutionally valid. In a hung Parliament, the President may also exercise the power to dissolve the Lok Sabha under Article 85(2) on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but if the advice is tendered by a caretaker government that has lost the confidence of the House, the President may refuse dissolution and instead explore alternative formations. The 1999 Vajpayee government’s loss of a confidence motion led to the President inviting the opposition to form a government, a step that underscored the President’s role as a constitutional arbiter rather than a passive rubber stamp.

The President’s pardoning power under Article 72, though not directly linked to government formation, has been invoked in politically sensitive cases (e.g., the commutation of death sentences in the Rajiv Gandhi assassination case). The Supreme Court in Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) laid down that the exercise of clemency is subject to limited judicial review on grounds of arbitrariness, mala fide, or non‑application of mind. This principle extends to any discretionary act of the President, reinforcing that discretion is not absolute.

Key Takeaways

  • President invites the leader most likely to command a majority; no constitutional formula exists.
  • Judicial review available for mala fide or arbitrary exercise (Rameshwar Prasad).
  • Caretaker conventions restrict major policy decisions; President may refuse dissolution if advice is constitutionally suspect.
  • Pardoning power under Article 72 is reviewable for arbitrariness (Epuru Sudhakar).

Ordinance Making Power of the President: Scope, Limits and Judicial Review

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session and immediate action is deemed necessary. The ordinance has the same force as an Act of Parliament but must be laid before both Houses within six weeks of reassembly; otherwise it lapses. The power is conditional on the satisfaction of the President that circumstances exist rendering immediate legislation necessary. This satisfaction is not immune from judicial scrutiny. In D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), the Supreme Court struck down a series of repromulgated ordinances, holding that repeated re‑issuance without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution and an abuse of the ordinance‑making power.

The Court in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) refined the test: the President’s satisfaction is justiciable to the extent of examining whether the executive has placed relevant material before the President and whether the ordinance is a colourable exercise of power. The judgment also clarified that an ordinance cannot amend the Constitution, nor can it override a Supreme Court judgment that has declared a law unconstitutional. The 2015 ordinance on the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) was challenged on these grounds, though the matter was later rendered academic by the Court’s striking down of the NJAC Act itself.

Repromulgation remains a contentious practice. The 2013 ordinance on the Securities Laws (Amendment) was repromulgated thrice, prompting criticism that the executive was bypassing parliamentary debate. The Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) recommended a statutory limit on repromulgation and a requirement for the President to record reasons for each ordinance. While not yet enacted, the recommendation reflects the constitutional consensus that ordinance‑making is an emergency power, not a parallel legislative route.

Key Takeaways

  • Ordinance requires immediate necessity; satisfaction is justiciable (D.C. Wadhwa, Krishna Kumar Singh).
  • Cannot amend Constitution or override judicial declarations of unconstitutionality.
  • Repromulgation without legislative approval is constitutionally suspect.
  • Law Commission recommends statutory safeguards and reasoned orders.

When a question on ordinances appears, first identify whether the issue is about the initial promulgation (Article 123 conditions) or repromulgation (D.C. Wadhwa). Cite the exact Article and the case name; the examiner awards marks for precise linkage.

Cabinet Committees: Statutory Basis, Functional Dynamics and Recent Reforms

Cabinet Committees are not created by statute; they derive their existence from the executive authority of the Prime Minister under the Transaction of Business Rules framed under Article 77(3). The Rules allocate specific policy domains to standing committees — Political Affairs, Economic Affairs, Security, Parliamentary Affairs, Accommodation, and Appointments — each chaired by the Prime Minister or a senior minister. The Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS), for instance, comprises the Prime Minister, Ministers of Defence, Home, Finance, and External Affairs, and the National Security Advisor as a permanent invitee. Its decisions on defence procurement, intelligence appointments, and nuclear policy are binding on the full Cabinet unless overturned.

The decision‑making flow follows a structured hierarchy: proposals originate in ministries, are vetted by the Cabinet Secretariat, placed before the relevant committee, and, if approved, forwarded to the full Cabinet for ratification. The Cabinet Secretariat, headed by the Cabinet Secretary (the senior‑most civil servant), maintains institutional memory, circulates minutes, and ensures follow‑up action. In 2021, the government reconstituted the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs to include the Minister of Commerce and Industry and the Minister of Railways, reflecting a shift toward integrated infrastructure planning.

Coordination mechanisms extend beyond standing committees. The Group of Ministers (GoM) and Empowered Group of Ministers (EGoM) are ad‑hoc bodies formed for inter‑ministerial issues such as disinvestment, climate change, or pandemic response. Their recommendations are submitted to the appropriate Cabinet Committee. The 2020 pandemic saw the creation of a high‑level Group of Ministers on COVID‑19, which met daily and reported to the CCS, illustrating the flexibility of the committee system in crisis management.

Key Takeaways

  • Cabinet Committees are executive bodies under Transaction of Business Rules, not statutory.
  • CCS is the apex security decision‑making forum; includes NSA as permanent invitee.
  • Cabinet Secretariat provides secretarial support, minutes, and follow‑up.
  • Ad‑hoc GoMs/EGoMs address cross‑cutting issues; recommendations feed into standing committees.

Civil Services Reforms: Lateral Entry, Performance Appraisal and Judicial Oversight

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005‑2009) recommended lateral entry at joint secretary and director levels to infuse domain expertise into the higher bureaucracy. The government operationalised this in 2018 by advertising ten joint secretary‑level posts open to private‑sector professionals, academics, and state‑government officers. Subsequent rounds in 2020 and 2022 expanded the pool to include specialists in finance, agriculture, and technology. While lateral entrants bring fresh perspectives, concerns about cadre morale, seniority, and accountability persist.

Performance appraisal has shifted from the Annual Confidential Report (ACR) to the Performance Appraisal Report (PAR) and, more recently, to the 360‑degree feedback mechanism under Mission Karmayogi (launched 2020). Mission Karmayogi aims to transition from rule‑based to role‑based capacity building, linking training modules to competency frameworks defined for each post. The Integrated Government Online Training (iGOT) platform delivers these modules, and completion records feed into promotion considerations.

Judicial oversight of transfers and postings was strengthened in T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013), where the Supreme Court directed the establishment of a Civil Services Board at the Centre and in each state to recommend transfers, postings, and disciplinary actions, insulating them from political interference. The Court also mandated fixed tenures for key posts (e.g., Director General of Police, Chief Secretary) and required that any premature transfer be recorded with reasons. Despite the judgment, compliance remains uneven; several states have not constituted the Boards, and the Centre’s Civil Services Board functions largely in an advisory capacity.

Key Takeaways

  • Lateral entry introduced in 2018 for joint secretary posts; expands domain expertise.
  • Mission Karmayogi (2020) shifts to competency‑based, role‑linked training via iGOT.
  • Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian mandated Civil Services Boards and fixed tenures.
  • Implementation gaps persist; political interference in transfers continues in practice.
  • Confusing the President’s discretionary power with the Governor’s power under Article 163.
  • Assuming ordinance‑making power is absolute; forgetting the six‑week parliamentary approval requirement.
  • Treating Cabinet Committees as statutory bodies rather than executive creations under Transaction of Business Rules.
  • Overlooking the distinction between lateral entry and regular UPSC recruitment in exam answers.
  • Neglecting the Supreme Court’s direction for Civil Services Boards in T.S.R. Subramanian.

President's Veto Powers: Absolute, Suspensive and Pocket Veto

Article 111 of the Constitution governs the President's assent to Bills passed by Parliament. The President may give assent, withhold assent (absolute veto), or return a Bill (other than a Money Bill) for reconsideration (suspensive veto). If the Bill is passed again by both Houses with or without amendments, the President must give assent. The Constitution does not prescribe a time limit for the President to act, creating the possibility of a pocket veto — indefinite withholding of assent without formal rejection. In practice, the pocket veto has been exercised rarely; the most cited instance is the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986, which remained pending until the dissolution of the Lok Sabha. The Supreme Court in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) held that the President's power to withhold assent is subject to judicial review only on the ground of mala fides or constitutional violation, not on the merits of the legislation. The 42nd Amendment (1976) briefly made the President's assent obligatory on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the 44th Amendment (1978) restored the pre‑1976 position, reaffirming that the President acts on ministerial advice except in the narrow discretionary windows recognised by the Court.

Absolute Veto

An absolute veto occurs when the President withholds assent and the Bill lapses. This power is theoretically available for any Bill except Money Bills, but constitutional convention and the binding nature of ministerial advice (Article 74) make its unilateral exercise politically untenable. The only post‑independence instance of an absolute veto was President Zail Singh's refusal to assent to the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986, which was effectively a pocket veto because the Bill was not returned for reconsideration.

Suspensive Veto

The suspensive veto is the most frequently used formal power. When the President returns a Bill with a message, Parliament may reconsider it. If both Houses pass it again, the President is constitutionally bound to assent. This mechanism was invoked in 2006 when President A.P.J. Abdul Kalam returned the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Amendment Bill, 2006; Parliament re‑passed it and the President assented.

Pocket Veto and Judicial Scrutiny

Because the Constitution fixes no deadline, a President can keep a Bill pending indefinitely. The Supreme Court has refrained from prescribing a time limit, but in Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020) the Court observed that unreasonable delay in assent may be challenged as a violation of the constitutional scheme. The pocket veto thus remains a dormant but potent instrument, its use constrained more by political accountability than by legal sanction.

Article 143 Advisory Jurisdiction and Executive Reference Practice

Article 143 empowers the President to refer a question of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its opinion. The Court's advisory opinion is not binding on the President or the government, but it carries significant persuasive authority and has shaped executive action on several occasions. The first reference was made in 1951 on the Delhi Laws Act, where the Court upheld the validity of delegated legislation. Subsequent references have addressed politically sensitive issues: the 1998 Presidential Reference on the Ayodhya dispute (the Court declined to answer whether a temple existed prior to the mosque, citing the principle of secularism), the 2002 reference on the Gujarat communal violence (the Court opined on the constitutional duty of the state to protect minorities), and the 2019 reference on the constitutional validity of the abrogation of Article 370 (the Court held that the President's order under Article 370(3) was valid). The advisory jurisdiction is distinct from the Court's appellate and original jurisdictions; it does not create a lis inter partes and cannot be invoked by private parties. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in deciding whether to make a reference, but the Court has insisted that the reference must be genuine and not a device to circumvent judicial review of executive action.

Procedural Safeguards

The Supreme Court has laid down procedural norms: the reference must be specific, the question must be of public importance, and the Court may decline to answer if the matter is sub judice or involves political questions unsuitable for judicial determination. In Special Reference No. 1 of 1998, the Court refused to answer the factual question about the existence of a temple, stating that it fell outside the scope of Article 143. The Court also requires that the reference be accompanied by all relevant facts and documents to enable a meaningful opinion.

Impact on Executive Decision‑Making

Advisory opinions have influenced legislative drafting and policy. After the 2002 Gujarat reference, the government enacted the Communal Violence (Prevention, Control and Rehabilitation of Victims) Bill, 2005 (though it lapsed). The 2019 reference provided constitutional cover for the Presidential Order abrogating Article 370, reinforcing the executive's position in subsequent litigation. While the opinions are not binding, they serve as authoritative interpretations that guide the executive in avoiding future constitutional challenges.

Prime Minister's Office versus Cabinet Secretariat: Institutional Evolution and Power Dynamics

The Prime Minister's Office (PMO) and the Cabinet Secretariat are the two principal nerve centres of executive governance, yet their constitutional status, functional remit, and relative influence have evolved differently. The PMO originated as the Prime Minister's Secretariat in 1947, headed by a Secretary to the Prime Minister. Under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, it expanded dramatically, acquiring the Principal Secretary (often a senior IAS officer), the National Security Advisor, and specialised cells for economic, foreign, and domestic policy. The PMO now functions as the Prime Minister's personal staff, coordinating inter‑ministerial matters, monitoring flagship schemes, and managing political communication. In contrast, the Cabinet Secretariat, established under the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, is the institutional memory of the Cabinet system. Headed by the Cabinet Secretary — the senior‑most civil servant — it prepares the agenda, circulates minutes, ensures follow‑up on Cabinet decisions, and services Cabinet Committees. The Cabinet Secretary also chairs the Civil Services Board and the Committee of Secretaries.

Functional Overlap and Coordination

Both bodies handle inter‑ministerial coordination, but the PMO does so through direct political authority while the Cabinet Secretariat operates through bureaucratic procedure. The PMO's "subject‑matter" cells (e.g., Infrastructure, Social Sector) often bypass the formal Cabinet Committee route, issuing directives to ministries. The Cabinet Secretariat, however, enforces the Transaction of Business Rules, ensuring that decisions are recorded, minuted, and implemented through the established committee hierarchy. In practice, the Principal Secretary and the Cabinet Secretary meet regularly to align the political and administrative tracks. The National Security Council Secretariat, housed in the PMO, exemplifies the PMO's lead on strategic matters, whereas the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) remains the formal decision‑making forum serviced by the Cabinet Secretariat.

Accountability and Transparency

The PMO is not subject to the Right to Information Act in the same manner as the Cabinet Secretariat, leading to debates about opacity. The Cabinet Secretariat's minutes are accessible after a prescribed period, and its role in the Civil Services Board provides a check on arbitrary transfers. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2008) recommended strengthening the Cabinet Secretariat's coordination role and limiting the PMO's direct intervention in routine administrative matters to preserve the collective responsibility of the Cabinet. Nevertheless, the political reality of a strong Prime Minister has kept the PMO as the de facto apex executive office.

Doctrine of Pleasure under Article 310: Scope, Exceptions and Judicial Safeguards

Article 310 establishes the doctrine of pleasure: civil servants of the Union and the States hold office during the pleasure of the President or the Governor, as the case may be. This common‑law principle allows the executive to dismiss a servant without cause, subject to constitutional exceptions. Article 311 provides two substantive safeguards: (1) no dismissal, removal, or reduction in rank without an inquiry in which the servant has a reasonable opportunity to be heard; (2) no penalty on the basis of evidence not disclosed to the servant. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) held that the pleasure doctrine is not absolute and must be exercised in accordance with the principles of natural justice. In State of Bihar v. Abdul Majid (1954), the Court ruled that the pleasure of the President/Governor is not a personal discretion but a constitutional power exercised on ministerial advice.

Judicial Expansion of Protections

Subsequent judgments have read Article 311 expansively. In Khem Chand v. Union of India (1958), the Court held that the inquiry requirement applies even when the penalty is compulsory retirement. The landmark T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013) extended procedural safeguards to transfers and postings, mandating Civil Services Boards and fixed tenures for key posts such as Director General of Police and Chief Secretary. The Court also declared that the doctrine of pleasure cannot be used to circumvent the requirement of a fair inquiry, and that any premature transfer must be recorded with reasons. Despite these rulings, compliance remains uneven; many states have not constituted functional Civil Services Boards, and the Centre's Board operates largely in an advisory capacity.

Exceptions and Special Categories

Certain constitutional offices — the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner, and the Chairman and members of the Union Public Service Commission — are expressly excluded from the pleasure doctrine by Articles 148, 324, and 316 respectively. Their removal requires a process akin to impeachment. Similarly, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are protected under Articles 124 and 217. The doctrine thus applies primarily to the subordinate civil services, where the balance between executive control and servant security continues to be negotiated through litigation and administrative reform.

Civil Services Cadre Management: Allocation, Deputation and the Role of DoPT

The All India Services (AIS) — the Indian Administrative Service, Indian Police Service, and Indian Forest Service — are governed by the All India Services Act, 1951, and the Cadre Allocation Policy, 2017. Each state constitutes a cadre, and officers are allocated to a cadre at the time of recruitment based on merit, preference, and vacancy. The 2017 policy introduced a "zone‑based" allocation to promote national integration, dividing states into five zones and requiring officers to serve in at least one cadre outside their home zone. Deputation to the Centre is regulated by the Central Deputation Rules; officers may be deputed for a maximum of seven years (extendable to ten in exceptional cases) and must return to their parent cadre thereafter. The Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) is the nodal agency for cadre management, deputation, empanelment for senior posts, and disciplinary matters. DoPT maintains the Executive Record (ER) sheets, processes vigilance clearance, and convenes the Civil Services Board for the Centre.

Empanelment and Senior Appointments

For appointments at the level of Joint Secretary and above, DoPT prepares panels of eligible officers based on Annual Confidential Reports (now Performance Appraisal Reports), vigilance clearance, and cadre clearance. The Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC), chaired by the Prime Minister, makes the final selection. Lateral entry, introduced in 2018, allows domain experts from outside the service to be appointed at Joint Secretary and Director levels on contract, bypassing the regular empanelment route. This has created a parallel track that coexists with the traditional cadre‑based progression.

Challenges in Cadre Management

Cadre imbalances persist: some states face chronic shortages while others have surplus officers. The "cooling‑off" period after central deputation is often waived, leading to prolonged absence from the parent cadre. The Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian directed that deputation should not exceed the prescribed limit without compelling reasons, yet waivers remain common. DoPT's dual role as cadre controller and policy maker sometimes creates conflicts of interest, prompting calls for an independent Civil Services Authority. The Mission Karmayogi framework (2020) seeks to align cadre management with competency‑based role mapping, but integration with the existing allocation and deputation architecture is still evolving.

President's Emergency Powers: National Emergency, President's Rule and Financial Emergency

The Constitution vests the President with three distinct emergency powers under Articles 352, 356 and 360. Each operates under a different constitutional logic, procedural safeguard and judicial review standard. Article 352 empowers the President to proclaim a National Emergency when the security of India or any part thereof is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) replaced the term “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion” and required the proclamation to be laid before each House of Parliament within one month, failing which it ceases to operate. The proclamation must be approved by a special majority of both Houses and can continue for six months at a time, subject to periodic parliamentary ratification. During a National Emergency, the federal distribution of legislative powers is suspended: Parliament may legislate on State List subjects (Article 250) and the President may issue directions to States (Article 353). Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended (Article 358) while other rights may be suspended by a separate Presidential order under Article 359, subject to the proviso that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended. The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) held that the power to proclaim emergency is not immune from judicial review; the Court may examine whether the satisfaction of the President is based on relevant material and is not mala fide.

Article 356, commonly called President’s Rule, allows the President to assume the functions of a State government when he is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months and can remain in force for a maximum of three years, with parliamentary approval every six months. The 44th Amendment introduced the requirement that the proclamation be based on a report from the Governor or otherwise, and that the President’s satisfaction is subject to judicial scrutiny. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) a nine‑judge bench laid down that the President’s satisfaction is not absolute; the Court can review the material on which the satisfaction is based, and the proclamation is liable to be struck down if it is based on extraneous or irrelevant grounds. The judgment also mandated a floor test in the State Assembly within a reasonable time, preferably 48 hours, before the imposition of President’s Rule. Subsequent cases such as Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) reinforced that the dissolution of the Assembly cannot be a routine sequel to the proclamation.

Article 360 provides for a Financial Emergency when the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India or any part thereof is threatened. The proclamation must be laid before Parliament and ceases to operate after two months unless approved by both Houses. During a Financial Emergency, the President may direct States to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the State, and reserve all Money Bills for Presidential consideration. To date, a Financial Emergency has never been proclaimed. The provision remains a constitutional safeguard rather than an operational tool, and its justiciability has not been tested in the Supreme Court.

The President's Pardoning Power under Article 72: Constitutional Scheme, Judicial Standards and Comparative Perspective

Article 72 confers on the President the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the Union extends. The power extends to court‑martial sentences and death sentences. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), making the exercise of this power effectively a executive decision. However, the Supreme Court has held that the power is not a mere act of grace but a constitutional duty subject to judicial review on limited grounds. In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980) the Court ruled that the President’s order under Article 72 is amenable to judicial review for arbitrariness, mala fides, or failure to consider relevant material. The Court in Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1988) clarified that while the President is bound by ministerial advice, the advice itself must be based on a proper consideration of the case; a blanket refusal without reasons may be set aside. In Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) the Court enumerated the grounds for judicial interference: (i) the order is passed without application of mind; (ii) the order is mala fide; (iii) the order is based on wholly irrelevant considerations; (iv) the order suffers from arbitrariness. The Court also held that the President must consider the gravity of the offence, the conduct of the convict, and the impact on society.

Comparatively, the United States President enjoys an almost plenary pardon power under Article II, Section 2 of the US Constitution, which is not subject to congressional or judicial review, except for impeachment. The Indian model, by contrast, embeds the power within a parliamentary executive, making it accountable to the legislature through the Council of Ministers and reviewable by the judiciary for constitutional propriety. The 44th Amendment did not alter Article 72, but the growing jurisprudence reflects a trend towards ensuring that the pardoning power is exercised transparently, with reasoned orders, especially in death‑penalty cases where the Supreme Court has repeatedly urged the executive to apply its mind afresh after judicial confirmation of the sentence.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: “Explain the judicial standards evolved by the Supreme Court for reviewing the exercise of the President’s pardoning power under Article 72.”
A high‑scoring answer should (1) state that Article 72 power is exercised on ministerial advice under Article 74; (2) cite Maru Ram (1980) establishing that the order is subject to judicial review for arbitrariness and mala fides; (3) cite Kehar Singh (1988) holding that the advice must reflect due consideration; (4) cite Epuru Sudhakar (2006) listing the four specific grounds — non‑application of mind, mala fides, irrelevant considerations, arbitrariness; (5) note that the Court does not sit in appeal over the merits but ensures constitutional propriety; (6) conclude that the trend is towards reasoned orders, especially in capital cases, and that the power is not an unchecked prerogative.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “judicial standards” or “grounds of review”, structure the answer chronologically by landmark judgments. Use the case name, year, and the specific principle laid down. This demonstrates both knowledge of precedent and the ability to synthesise evolving doctrine — a hallmark of top‑tier answers.

Prime Minister in Coalition and Minority Governments: Confidence Motions, Floor Tests and the President’s Role

The parliamentary system envisages that the Prime Minister commands the confidence of the Lok Sabha. In a single‑party majority this is straightforward; in coalition or minority scenarios the dynamics become constitutionally intricate. The President’s role under Article 75(1) — appointing the Prime Minister — is guided by the convention that the leader of the largest party or pre‑poll alliance is invited to form the government. When no party secures a clear majority, the President may exercise discretion to explore alternatives, but this discretion is bounded by the principle that the appointee must be able to prove majority on the floor of the House. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai (1994) held that the Governor (and by analogy the President) cannot dismiss a ministry without a floor test, and that a floor test must be ordered within a reasonable period, preferably 48 hours, after a claim of loss of majority.

Historical instances illustrate the interplay. In 1996, Atal Bihari Vajpayee was invited as the single largest party leader but resigned after 13 days when he could not muster a majority. In 1998, Vajpayee again formed a coalition (National Democratic Alliance) and survived a confidence vote. In 2004, the Congress‑led United Progressive Alliance secured a majority with external support, and Manmohan Singh was appointed after the President verified the support letters. In 2019, the BJP secured a clear majority, but the President’s invitation followed the same constitutional script. The 10th Schedule (Anti‑Defection Law) further constrains coalition stability: a member disqualified under the Schedule loses membership, affecting the arithmetic for confidence motions. The Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) upheld the Speaker’s power to decide disqualification, subject to judicial review, which indirectly influences the viability of minority governments.

When a government loses a confidence motion, the President may either invite the opposition to form an alternative government or dissolve the Lok Sabha and call fresh elections. The discretion to dissolve is not unfettered; the President must be satisfied that no alternative government can be formed. In practice, the President relies on the advice of the outgoing Prime Minister, but the Supreme Court in Rameshwar Prasad (2006) cautioned against premature dissolution when a viable alternative exists. The President’s address to Parliament (Article 87) and the subsequent Motion of Thanks also serve as a barometer of the government’s legislative support. In minority situations, the Prime Minister often negotiates issue‑based support, and the Cabinet’s collective responsibility (Article 75(3)) obliges all ministers to defend government policy, even if individual parties in the coalition dissent publicly.

Civil Services Performance Management: Mission Karmayogi, 360‑Degree Appraisal and the Shift from Seniority to Competency

The traditional civil‑service appraisal in India relied on the Annual Confidential Report (ACR), a top‑down, seniority‑linked evaluation that emphasized length of service over demonstrated competence. Recognising the need for a dynamic, competency‑driven bureaucracy, the Government launched Mission Karmayogi in September 2020 as a National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building. The mission is anchored by the Capacity Building Commission (CBC), constituted in April 2021, and operates through the Integrated Government Online Training (iGOT) Karmayogi platform, which hosts over 800 courses across functional, behavioural and domain competencies.

A pivotal reform under Mission Karmayogi is the introduction of a 360‑degree Performance Appraisal Report (PAR) for officers at the level of Under Secretary and above. The new PAR replaces the ACR and incorporates feedback from superiors, peers, subordinates and, where relevant, external stakeholders. The assessment matrix covers six competency clusters: (i) strategic thinking, (ii) leadership and team building, (iii) result orientation, (iv) citizen‑centricity, (v) integrity and ethics, and (vi) digital literacy. Each cluster is measured on a five‑point scale with defined behavioural indicators. The CBC has mandated that the PAR be completed electronically on iGOT, ensuring time‑stamped submissions and audit trails.

The shift from seniority to competency also reshapes empanelment for senior posts (Joint Secretary and above). The Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC) now considers the 360‑degree PAR scores alongside vigilance clearance and cadre clearance. Lateral entry, introduced in 2018, runs parallel: domain experts are appointed on contract at Joint Secretary and Director levels without entering the regular cadre. Their performance is evaluated through the same competency framework, but they do not acquire cadre seniority. The Mission also envisages a “role‑based” deployment model where officers are matched to positions based on competency profiles rather than merely on batch seniority.

Challenges remain. The cultural transition from a confidential, hierarchical appraisal to an open, multi‑source feedback system meets resistance in many cadres. State governments have been uneven in adopting the 360‑degree PAR; several have not yet constituted functional State Civil Services Boards as directed by the Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013). Data integrity on iGOT, interoperability with existing Human Resource Management Systems, and the capacity of evaluators to apply behavioural indicators consistently are operational hurdles. The CBC’s annual “Capacity Building Plan” aims to address these through calibrated training of evaluators, periodic audits of PAR quality, and integration with the “Karmayogi Bharat” digital stack for real‑time competency mapping.

The President's Power to Appoint Governors: Constitutional Provisions, Judicial Interpretation and Political Practice

Article 155 vests the appointment of a State Governor in the President, who acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. The Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President (Article 156), a phrase that has generated extensive judicial scrutiny. In B. P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010), the Supreme Court held that the pleasure doctrine is not absolute; removal must not be arbitrary, mala fide or based on extraneous considerations. The Court mandated that a Governor removed before the completion of the five‑year term is entitled to a statement of reasons, and the decision is subject to judicial review for violation of constitutional morality.

The appointment process, though formally executive, has acquired political dimensions. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that the Governor be an eminent person from outside the state, preferably with a non‑partisan background, and that the Chief Minister of the concerned state be consulted. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (2002) echoed this, suggesting a collegium comprising the Prime Minister, Home Minister, Speaker of the Lok Sabha and the concerned Chief Minister. Neither recommendation has been codified, leaving the Prime Minister’s discretion largely unchecked.

In practice, Governors are often appointed as a reward for party loyalty or to manage Centre‑State relations. Instances such as the mass removal of Governors after a change of government at the Centre (1999, 2004, 2014) illustrate the political use of the pleasure power. The Supreme Court in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) cautioned that the Governor’s office must not become a tool for partisan interference, especially in the context of Article 356 (President’s Rule). The Court emphasised that the Governor’s report recommending President’s Rule must be based on objective material, not on the Central government’s political preferences.

Recent developments include the 2021 amendment to the Governors (Appointment and Conditions of Service) Act (a statutory framework that does not exist constitutionally but has been proposed in various private member bills) and the 2023 Supreme Court observation in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India that the Centre should evolve a transparent, consultative mechanism for Governor appointments to preserve federal balance. Until such a mechanism is institutionalised, the appointment and removal of Governors remain a focal point for Centre‑State tensions and a frequent subject of UPSC analytical questions.

The Cabinet Committee on Security: Composition, Decision‑Making Process and Strategic Significance

The Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) is the apex executive body for matters relating to national defence, internal security, intelligence, and foreign policy with security implications. It is chaired by the Prime Minister and comprises the Ministers of Defence, Home Affairs, Finance, and External Affairs as permanent members. The National Security Adviser (NSA) serves as the secretary to the CCS, while the Chiefs of the three armed services and the Director of the Intelligence Bureau attend as invitees. The Cabinet Secretary coordinates the secretariat support.

Unlike other Cabinet committees, the CCS does not have a statutory basis; it derives its authority from the executive’s prerogative under Article 77 (conduct of business of the Government of India). Its decisions are recorded as “CCS decisions” and are binding on the concerned ministries. The decision‑making process typically follows a structured agenda: the NSA circulates a background note, the service chiefs present operational assessments, and the Ministers deliberate. Consensus is sought, but the Prime Minister’s view prevails in case of divergence.

The CCS has been instrumental in major strategic choices: the 1998 nuclear tests (Pokhran‑II), the 2016 surgical strikes across the Line of Control, the 2019 Balakot air strike, and the 2020‑21 Ladakh border standoff management. In each case, the CCS met multiple times, evaluated intelligence, diplomatic options, and military readiness before authorising action. The committee also approves major defence procurement proposals exceeding ₹1,000 crore, oversees the Strategic Forces Command, and reviews the National Security Strategy document.

Judicial oversight of CCS decisions is limited. The Supreme Court in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2004) held that while the executive enjoys wide discretion in national security matters, it must not violate fundamental rights without justification. The Court has occasionally examined the procedural fairness of security‑related orders (e.g., detention under the National Security Act) but refrains from reviewing the merits of strategic decisions.

Recent reforms include the creation of the Chief of Defence Staff (CDS) in 2020, who acts as the principal military adviser to the CCS, and the institutionalisation of the Defence Planning Committee (DPC) to align long‑term capability development with CCS directives. The CCS’s effectiveness hinges on the quality of intelligence input, inter‑ministerial coordination, and the political will to implement tough decisions — factors that frequently appear in UPSC mains questions on national security architecture.

The CCS is not created by the Transaction of Business Rules or any statute; it is an executive committee constituted by the Prime Minister under the executive’s inherent power to organise the conduct of business (Article 77). Its composition and functions are governed by convention and executive orders, not by a statutory provision. The correct answer should highlight the non‑statutory nature, the role of the NSA as secretary, and the fact that its decisions are recorded as executive orders, not as statutory instruments.

The Role of the Union Public Service Commission in Civil Services Recruitment, Disciplinary Control and Independence

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) is a constitutional body under Articles 315‑323, mandated to conduct examinations for appointment to the All‑India Services and Central Civil Services (Group A and B). Its independence is safeguarded by security of tenure for the Chairman and members (six‑year term or until age 65), removal only on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity after a Supreme Court inquiry (Article 317), and financial autonomy through the Consolidated Fund of India.

Recruitment is primarily through the Civil Services Examination (CSE), a three‑stage process (Preliminary, Main, Personality Test) that tests general studies, optional subjects, and aptitude. The UPSC also conducts the Engineering Services Examination, Combined Medical Services Examination, and the Indian Forest Service Examination. In 2023, the Commission introduced a revised pattern for the CSE Main, increasing the weight of the essay and ethics papers to assess analytical and ethical competencies.

Beyond recruitment, the UPSC plays a pivotal role in disciplinary matters. Under Article 320(3), the Commission must be consulted on all disciplinary cases affecting a civil servant’s tenure, including major penalties (dismissal, removal, reduction in rank). The Supreme Court in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2005) held that non‑consultation with the UPSC in such cases renders the disciplinary order void. The Commission’s advice is not binding, but the government must record reasons for deviation.

The UPSC also advises on promotion, transfer, and deputation policies for Group A officers, and frames the Recruitment Rules (service conditions) for each cadre. The 2021 amendment to the UPSC (Exemption from Consultation) Regulations expanded the list of posts exempt from consultation, a move criticised for diluting the Commission’s oversight. The Commission’s annual report, laid before Parliament, provides data on vacancies, representation of SC/ST/OBC/EWS candidates, and pendency of disciplinary cases — information frequently used in UPSC mains answers on civil service reform.

Recent challenges include the backlog of disciplinary cases (over 1,200 pending as of 2023), the need to modernise the examination system (computer‑based testing, AI‑enabled evaluation), and demands for greater transparency in the personality test. The 2022 Committee on Reform of Civil Services Examination (chaired by former Cabinet Secretary B. K. Chaturvedi) recommended a shift towards competency‑based assessment, aligning with Mission Karmayogi’s framework.

The Prime Minister's Office and the Principal Secretary: Institutional Evolution, Functional Overlap and Accountability

The Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) has evolved from a modest secretariat in the 1950s to a powerful nerve centre of governance. The Principal Secretary to the Prime Minister, a senior Indian Administrative Service officer, heads the PMO and acts as the primary conduit between the Prime Minister and the bureaucracy. The post was formalised in 1990; prior to that, the Secretary to the Prime Minister performed similar functions.

The PMO’s functional overlap with the Cabinet Secretariat is a recurring theme. The Cabinet Secretary, as the senior‑most civil servant, coordinates inter‑ministerial matters and prepares the agenda for Cabinet meetings. The Principal Secretary, however, often drives the Prime Minister’s priority programmes (e.g., Swachh Bharat, Digital India, Atmanirbhar Bharat) and monitors implementation through direct communication with secretaries. This dual‑track coordination can lead to parallel decision‑making channels, a point highlighted by the Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2008) which recommended clearer delineation of roles.

Accountability mechanisms for the PMO are largely political. The PMO is not subject to the Right to Information Act (Section 8(1)(j) exemption for personal information and Section 24 for security/intelligence organisations), though certain administrative decisions have been brought under RTI scrutiny by the Central Information Commission. The Prime Minister answers to Parliament during Question Hour and debates, but the PMO’s internal deliberations remain opaque. The 2019 Prime Minister’s Office (Transparency and Accountability) Bill, a private member’s proposal, sought to mandate proactive disclosure of PMO communications on policy matters; it lapsed.

In coalition governments, the Principal Secretary often negotiates with alliance partners on portfolio allocation and policy compromises, extending the PMO’s political role. The 2014‑2024 period saw the PMO’s influence expand through the creation of dedicated mission directors (e.g., for the Aspirational Districts Programme) reporting directly to the Principal Secretary, bypassing line ministries. Critics argue this centralises power and weakens ministerial accountability; proponents contend it ensures speed and coherence in a fragmented administrative landscape.

UPSC examiners frequently test the distinction between the PMO and the Cabinet Secretariat, the constitutional basis (or lack thereof) of the Principal Secretary’s post, and the implications for collective responsibility. A nuanced answer should reference the 2008 ARC recommendation, the RTI exemption, and the practical impact on policy implementation.

Parliamentary Oversight of the Executive: The Role of Department‑Related Standing Committees and the Committee on Estimates

Parliamentary oversight of the executive is institutionalised through a system of Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) and financial committees. There are 24 DRSCs, each shadowing a set of ministries (e.g., Committee on Home Affairs, Committee on External Affairs). They examine bills referred by the Speaker/Chairman, scrutinise demand for grants, review annual reports, and study policy issues. Their reports are recommendatory but carry significant moral authority; ministries are expected to furnish Action Taken Reports (ATRs) within six months.

The Committee on Estimates, a financial committee of 30 members (all from Lok Sabha), examines whether public money is spent within the policy framework and suggests economies. Unlike the Public Accounts Committee (which audits past expenditure with the CAG), the Estimates Committee looks forward, evaluating the efficiency of proposed outlays. Its 2022 report on the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana highlighted under‑utilisation of funds and recommended outcome‑based budgeting.

The DRSCs’ effectiveness depends on bipartisan participation, expert testimony, and the quality of secretariat support. The 2019‑2024 Lok Sabha saw a decline in the number of bills referred to committees (only 27% of bills in the 17th Lok Sabha versus 71% in the 15th), raising concerns about legislative scrutiny. The Speaker’s discretion in referring bills, the government’s willingness to accept recommendations, and the committees’ capacity to engage domain experts are critical variables.

Judicial pronouncements have reinforced parliamentary oversight. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1975), the Supreme Court held that the executive cannot bypass parliamentary committees by issuing ordinances on matters requiring legislative scrutiny. More recently, the Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2023) underscored that the Committee on Estimates’ recommendations on electoral funding transparency deserve serious consideration.

For UPSC aspirants, key points include: the composition and mandate of DRSCs, the distinction between Estimates Committee and Public Accounts Committee, the procedural requirement for ATRs, the trend of declining bill referrals, and the constitutional principle that financial control resides with the Lok Sabha (Article 110). Comparative reference to the UK’s Select Committees and the US Congressional committees enriches analytical answers.

President’s Role in Treaty Making and International Obligations

The Constitution distributes treaty‑making power between the executive and Parliament. Article 73 extends the executive power of the Union to matters on which Parliament can legislate, while Article 253 empowers Parliament to make laws for implementing any treaty, agreement or convention. In practice, the President, acting on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, negotiates and signs treaties. Ratification, however, requires parliamentary approval when the treaty affects domestic law, alters territory, or imposes financial obligations. The landmark Keshav Singh case (1965) clarified that the President cannot ratify a treaty that amends the Constitution without a constitutional amendment under Article 368.

Two procedural routes exist. First, the executive may conclude executive agreements that do not require legislation; these are binding under international law but have no direct domestic effect unless implemented by statute. Second, for treaties requiring domestic legislation, the government introduces a Bill under Article 253. The 1971 Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain judgment affirmed that Parliament’s legislative competence under Article 253 is plenary, subject only to the basic structure doctrine. The 2017 Justice K. S. Puttaswamy ruling further held that any treaty obligation impacting fundamental rights must be scrutinised for proportionality.

Recent practice illustrates the interplay. The 2015 Paris Agreement was ratified after the Parliament passed the Climate Change Act, 2017, giving statutory force to the Nationally Determined Contributions. Conversely, the 2020 India‑Australia Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement entered into force through an executive notification because it fell within existing tariff‑setting powers under the Customs Act. The President’s formal instrument of ratification is published in the Gazette, but the political responsibility rests with the Prime Minister and the Ministry of External Affairs.

Judicial oversight remains limited to constitutional compliance. In State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court refused to interfere with the executive’s decision to sign the SAARC Charter, holding that treaty‑making is a political act. However, the Court has struck down executive actions that bypass Parliament when a treaty creates new legal obligations, as seen in the 2021 Madhav Rao Scindia case concerning the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea.

Prime Minister’s Authority over Key Constitutional Appointments

The Prime Minister exercises decisive influence over appointments to constitutional bodies, although the formal appointing authority is the President. Article 76 (Attorney General), Article 77 (Comptroller and Auditor General), Article 315 (UPSC Chairman and members), Article 324 (Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners), and Article 148 (CAG) all vest appointment power in the President “on the advice of the Council of Ministers”. In practice, the Prime Minister, as head of the Council, determines the shortlist and final choice.

The 1993 Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (the “Second Judges case”) established the collegium system for judicial appointments, limiting executive discretion. However, for non‑judicial constitutional posts, the executive retains primacy. The 2014 National Judicial Appointments Commission Act attempted to restructure judicial appointments but was struck down in 2015, reaffirming the collegium. For the CAG, the 1971 K. K. Verma case held that the President must act on the Prime Minister’s advice, and the appointee must be a person of “eminence in public life”.

The Election Commission’s composition has been a flashpoint. The 2019 Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India judgment mandated a transparent, consultative process, leading to the 2023 amendment of the Election Commission (Conditions of Service) Rules, which introduced a selection committee comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and a Union Cabinet Minister. The UPSC Chairman’s appointment follows a similar pattern: the Prime Minister recommends a name after consultation with the Union Minister of Personnel, and the President issues the warrant.

Political practice shows that the Prime Minister’s choice often reflects coalition compulsions. In minority governments, the Prime Minister may negotiate appointments with allies, as occurred in the 1996‑98 United Front government where the CAG and CEC posts were filled after consensus with supporting parties. The 2022 appointment of the CAG, a former finance secretary, illustrated the trend of selecting career bureaucrats with domain expertise, a shift from earlier political appointees.

Hence the Prime Minister initiates the selection, often after informal consultation with the Finance Minister and the outgoing CAG. The President’s role is ceremonial; the warrant is signed on the Prime Minister’s recommendation. The Supreme Court in K. K. Verma (1971) confirmed that the President cannot exercise discretion. In practice, a search committee of senior bureaucrats prepares a panel, the Prime Minister picks a name, and the President issues the appointment letter. Cabinet Secretariat: Crisis Management and Inter‑Ministerial Coordination

The Cabinet Secretariat, headed by the Cabinet Secretary (the senior‑most civil servant), is the nerve centre for inter‑ministerial coordination and crisis management. Unlike the Prime Minister’s Office, which is a political advisory body, the Cabinet Secretariat derives its authority from the Transaction of Business Rules, 1961, framed under Article 77(3). It services the Cabinet, Cabinet Committees, and the National Security Council (NSC).

During national emergencies — natural disasters, pandemics, or security threats — the Cabinet Secretariat activates the Crisis Management Group (CMG). The CMG, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, includes secretaries of Home, Defence, Health, and the Director General of the National Disaster Response Force. The 2020 COVID‑19 response saw the CMG meet daily, issuing directives under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, and coordinating with state chief secretaries through video conferences. The Cabinet Secretariat also monitors the implementation of Cabinet decisions through the “Action Taken Report” mechanism, ensuring ministries report compliance within stipulated timelines.

The Secretariat’s coordination role extends to legislative business. It prepares the Cabinet’s legislative agenda, circulates Bills for inter‑ministerial comments, and resolves conflicts before they reach the Cabinet. The 2019 amendment to the Transaction of Business Rules introduced a “fast‑track” route for Bills of urgent national importance, reducing the comment period from 21 to 7 days. This procedural tweak was used for the Farm Laws (later repealed) and the Citizenship Amendment Act.

In the realm of national security, the Cabinet Secretariat services the NSC and the Strategic Policy Group. The Cabinet Secretary is the ex‑officio secretary of the NSC, ensuring that intelligence inputs from the Intelligence Bureau, RAW, and the Defence Intelligence Agency are synthesized for the Prime Minister. The 2016 surgical strikes and the 2019 Balakot operation were coordinated through this channel, with the Cabinet Secretariat managing logistics, diplomatic clearances, and media strategy.

Judicial Review of Ordinances: The Supreme Court’s Evolving Standards

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session, provided the executive is satisfied that circumstances require immediate action. The Supreme Court has repeatedly delineated the boundaries of this power. In R. C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970), the Court held that an ordinance cannot be used to bypass legislative debate on matters of policy that are not urgent. The “satisfaction” of the President is justiciable, though the Court presumes bona fides unless mala fides is proven.

The 1998 Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar judgment introduced the “re‑promulgation” test: repeated re‑issuance of an ordinance without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution. The Court struck down a series of ordinances on land acquisition that were re‑promulgated seven times. In 2021, the Madhav Rao Scindia case extended this principle to central ordinances, ruling that the government must place the ordinance before Parliament at the earliest session; failure to do so renders the ordinance void ab initio.

Judicial scrutiny also covers the content of ordinances. The 2017 Justice K. S. Puttaswamy decision applied the proportionality test to an ordinance amending the Aadhaar Act, holding that the ordinance route could not be used to dilute privacy safeguards without parliamentary debate. The Court emphasized that ordinances are “emergency legislation” and must be limited in scope and duration.

Recent practice shows the executive’s reliance on ordinances for economic reforms. The 2020 Taxation Laws (Amendment) Ordinance and the 2022 Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Ordinance were both challenged. The Supreme Court, in a 2023 bench, upheld them, noting that the economic urgency justified immediate action and that Parliament ratified them within the stipulated six‑week window. The evolving jurisprudence thus balances executive flexibility with legislative supremacy, insisting on temporal limits and substantive justification.

Attorney General and Solicitor General: Legal Advice to the Executive

The Attorney General (AG) under Article 76 and the Solicitor General (SG) under the Law Officers (Conditions of Service) Rules, 1972, constitute the highest legal advisors to the Government of India. The AG is appointed by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers and holds office during the pleasure of the President. The SG is appointed by the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet, again on the Prime Minister’s recommendation.

The AG’s duties include giving advice on legal matters referred by the President, performing functions assigned by the Constitution (e.g., appearing in the Supreme Court under Article 143), and representing the Union in all courts. The SG assists the AG and appears in cases where the AG is unavailable. Both are not members of the Cabinet and do not enjoy parliamentary privilege, but they attend Cabinet meetings when legal issues arise.

The 1998 State of Punjab v. Union of India case clarified that the AG’s advice is not binding on the executive; the government may reject it, but must record reasons. The 2015 Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (Third Judges case) held that the AG’s opinion on judicial appointments carries weight but does not override the collegium. In 2020, the AG’s opinion on the constitutionality of the Farm Laws was sought; the AG advised that the laws were within legislative competence, a view later contested in the Supreme Court.

Accountability mechanisms are limited. The AG can be removed by the President at any time; the SG’s tenure is co‑terminus with the government. The 2018 Law Commission Report No. 266 recommended a fixed tenure and a transparent selection process to enhance independence. The government has not implemented this recommendation. The AG’s right of audience in all courts (Article 88) and the SG’s similar right under the Rules make them pivotal in shaping the Union’s litigation strategy, especially in high‑stakes cases involving federalism, fundamental rights, and economic policy.

President's Power to Summon, Prorogue and Dissolve the Lok Sabha: Constitutional Provisions, Conventions and Judicial Checks

Article 85(1) obliges the President to summon each House of Parliament at such intervals that six months shall not intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. The power to prorogue a session rests with the President under Article 85(2), while dissolution of the Lok Sabha is governed by Article 83(2) read with Article 85(2)(b). In all three actions the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, as mandated by Article 74(1).

Constitutional convention, however, creates a narrow zone of presidential discretion. If a Prime Minister who has lost the confidence of the House advises dissolution to avoid a no‑confidence motion, the President may refuse and instead invite the leader of the largest opposition party to form a government. The Supreme Court in K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1997) held that the President’s satisfaction in dissolving the Lok Sabha is not immune from judicial review; it must be based on genuine ministerial advice and cannot be exercised mala fide. The Court reiterated this principle in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) concerning the dissolution of a state assembly, extending the logic to the Union legislature.

The President’s power to summon is also subject to the six‑month rule. In practice, the government decides the dates of sessions, but the President may return the recommendation for reconsideration if the gap threatens to exceed the constitutional limit. The 1999 S. R. Bommai v. Union of India judgment, while primarily about President’s Rule, affirmed that the President’s constitutional functions are reviewable for constitutional propriety. Consequently, any attempt to prorogue Parliament indefinitely to evade legislative scrutiny would be struck down as a fraud on the Constitution.

Recent practice illustrates the tension. In 2020 the government advised a short winter session amid the pandemic; the President summoned Parliament after a 176‑day gap, the longest in recent decades, prompting criticism but no judicial challenge because the six‑month ceiling was not breached. In 2023 the President prorogued the Budget Session on the government’s advice, and the opposition alleged the move was timed to curtail debate on a controversial bill. No court intervened, underscoring that judicial intervention remains exceptional and confined to clear constitutional violations.

  • Summoning: Article 85(1) – six‑month maximum interval.
  • Prorogation: Article 85(2)(a) – ends a session without dissolving the House.
  • Dissolution: Article 83(2) read with Article 85(2)(b) – ends the Lok Sabha; fresh elections must follow.
  • All actions require ministerial advice (Article 74); presidential discretion limited to constitutional impropriety.
  • Judicial review available for mala fide or unconstitutional advice (K. K. Verma, Rameshwar Prasad).

Prime Minister's Leadership of the National Security Council: Structure, Decision‑Making Process and Accountability

The National Security Council (NSC) was constituted in 1998 by a executive resolution under Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee. The NSC is chaired by the Prime Minister and comprises the Ministers of Defence, Home Affairs, Finance and External Affairs, the National Security Advisor (NSA) who serves as its secretary, and the Chief of Defence Staff (CDS) since the post’s creation in 2020. The Council’s mandate covers external and internal security, nuclear policy, intelligence coordination and strategic defence planning.

Below the NSC sits the Strategic Policy Group (SPG), headed by the Cabinet Secretary. The SPG includes the three service chiefs, the Directors of the Intelligence Bureau and the Research and Analysis Wing, the Foreign Secretary, the Defence Secretary and the Home Secretary. The SPG prepares policy options, conducts threat assessments and forwards recommendations to the NSC. A further layer, the National Security Advisory Board (NSAB), consists of eminent experts from academia, diplomacy and the armed forces; it provides long‑term strategic perspectives and meets periodically to advise the NSA.

Decision‑making follows a structured flow: the NSA aggregates intelligence inputs, the SPG evaluates operational feasibility, the NSAB offers strategic alternatives, and the NSC, chaired by the Prime Minister, takes the final political decision. The 2019 Balakot air strike exemplifies this chain – the NSA presented actionable intelligence, the SPG assessed military options, the NSAB weighed diplomatic fallout, and the Prime Minister authorised the strike at an NSC meeting.

Accountability mechanisms are largely political. The Prime Minister answers to Parliament on national security matters, and the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Defence reviews broad policy directions. However, operational details remain classified; the Official Secrets Act, 1923 and the Intelligence Organisations (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1985 limit disclosure. The 2018 reconstitution of the NSC expanded its membership to include the CDS and formalised the SPG’s role, aiming to reduce ad‑hoc decision‑making. The 2021 National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS) restructuring introduced a dedicated cyber‑security coordinator, reflecting emerging threats.

  • SPG headed by Cabinet Secretary; operational arm with service and intelligence chiefs.
  • NSAB – expert advisory body for long‑term strategy.
  • Decision flow: intelligence → SPG options → NSAB perspective → NSC political authorisation.
  • Accountability: PM’s parliamentary answerability; limited judicial or legislative oversight of operational decisions.

The Cabinet Secretary's Role in Inter‑Ministerial Coordination, Policy Monitoring and the e‑Office Initiative

The Cabinet Secretary, as the senior‑most civil servant, heads the Cabinet Secretariat and functions as the principal coordinator among ministries. The post derives its authority from the Transaction of Business Rules, 1961, which empower the Cabinet Secretary to convene the Committee of Secretaries (CoS) and the Senior Officers’ Meeting (SOM) for resolving inter‑ministerial disputes before they reach the Cabinet. The CoS, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, meets weekly to review policy proposals, ensure coherence with the government’s agenda and flag implementation bottlenecks.

Policy monitoring is institutionalised through the Cabinet Secretariat’s Monitoring Mechanism, which tracks the progress of Cabinet decisions via a structured matrix of milestones, responsible ministries and timelines. Since 2019 the e‑Office platform, developed under the Digital India programme, has been mandated for all central ministries. The platform digitises file movement, enables real‑time tracking of decision‑making and provides the Cabinet Secretary with a dashboard to monitor pendency, adherence to timelines and compliance with the 100‑day agenda announced after each general election.

The Cabinet Secretary also serves as the ex‑officio chair of the Civil Services Board and the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC) secretariat, thereby influencing senior appointments and cadre management. During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the Cabinet Secretary chaired the National Crisis Management Committee (NCMC), coordinating the efforts of the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Home Affairs, the Indian Council of Medical Research and state governments. The NCMC’s daily situation reports were fed into the e‑Office system, illustrating the integration of crisis management with routine monitoring.

Recent reforms under Mission Karmayogi (2020) have expanded the Cabinet Secretary’s role in capacity building. The Capacity Building Commission, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, designs competency frameworks for civil servants, aligning training with the government’s strategic priorities. The 2022 Annual Performance Assessment Report (APAR) revamp introduced 360‑degree feedback, with the Cabinet Secretariat overseeing the calibration across services.

Civil Services Integrity Framework: Central Vigilance Commission, Lokpal and the Whistleblower Protection Act

The integrity architecture for the civil services rests on three pillars: the Central Vigilance Commission (CVC), the Lokpal and the Whistleblower Protection Act. The CVC was created by an executive resolution in 1964 and conferred statutory status by the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. It exercises superintendence over vigilance administration in all central government organisations, advises on disciplinary proceedings and reviews the progress of investigations by the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) in cases involving public servants. The CVC’s recommendations are advisory; prosecution requires sanction from the competent authority.

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 was notified in 2014 and the first Lokpal, Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, assumed office in March 2019. The Lokpal has jurisdiction over the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, Members of Parliament and Group A‑D officers. It can direct preliminary inquiries, order investigations by the CBI and recommend prosecution. The Act mandates a selection committee comprising the Prime Minister, the Speaker of Lok Sabha, the Leader of Opposition, the Chief Justice of India (or a Supreme Court judge) and an eminent jurist. The 2016 amendment exempted the Prime Minister from the Lokpal’s purview in matters of international relations, security and public order, subject to a two‑thirds majority of the Lokpal bench.

The Whistleblower Protection Act, 2014 (notified in 2014) provides a mechanism for public servants and citizens to disclose corruption or misuse of power. It establishes a Competent Authority (the CVC for central government) to receive complaints, protects the identity of the whistleblower and prescribes penalties for victimisation. The 2021 amendment strengthened protection by extending the limitation period for filing complaints, allowing anonymous disclosures in exceptional cases and expanding the definition of “victimisation” to include transfer, denial of promotion and hostile work environment.

Operational challenges persist. The CVC’s advisory nature limits its enforcement teeth; the Lokpal’s jurisdiction over the Prime Minister remains politically sensitive and has not been tested in a high‑profile case. The Whistleblower Act excludes intelligence and security agencies (IB, RAW, NTRO) from its ambit, creating a blind spot. Moreover, the overlap between CVC and Lokpal in handling Group A officers sometimes leads to jurisdictional ambiguity, which the 2018 Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) guidelines sought to clarify by designating the CVC as the primary vigilance body and the Lokpal as the appellate authority for corruption complaints.

  • Lokpal (2013/2019) – jurisdiction over PM (with safeguards), Ministers, MPs, Group A‑D officers; selection committee includes CJI.
  • Whistleblower Protection Act (2014/2021) – protected disclosures, identity protection, penalties for victimisation; excludes intelligence agencies.
  • Overlap mitigated by DoPT 2018 guidelines: CVC primary, Lokpal appellate for corruption.
  • Key gaps: CVC’s lack of prosecutorial power; Lokpal’s untested PM jurisdiction; whistleblower exclusion of security agencies.

Judicial Appointments: The President, the Collegium and the Executive's Consultative Role

Articles 124(2) and 217(1) vest the power to appoint Supreme Court and High Court judges in the President, who acts after consultation with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President may deem necessary. The evolution of the “consultation” requirement into a binding “concurrence” is traced through three landmark judgments. In the First Judges Case (S. P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981) the Court held that “consultation” does not mean “concurrence” and the executive could override judicial opinion. The Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 1993) reversed this, declaring that the Chief Justice of India’s opinion, formed in consultation with the two senior‑most Supreme Court judges, would have primacy. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the Chief Justice and the four senior‑most judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the Chief Justice plus two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments.

The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP), first issued in 1999 and revised in 2017 after extensive executive‑judicial negotiations, operationalises the collegium’s recommendations. It prescribes the criteria of merit, seniority, integrity and diversity, and mandates that the government may seek reconsideration only once; if the collegium reiterates its recommendation, the appointment must be made. The President’s role is formal – the warrant of appointment is issued under the President’s seal on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the advice is bound by the collegium’s binding recommendation.

The National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014 sought to replace the collegium with a six‑member commission comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Law Minister and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court struck down the NJAC in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015), holding that it violated the basic structure by compromising judicial independence. Since then, the collegium system continues, albeit with periodic friction. In 2022 the government returned the name of a High Court judge for reconsideration; the collegium reiterated, and the appointment was eventually notified in 2023.

Recent transparency measures include the publication of collegium resolutions on the Supreme Court website (since 2017) and the introduction of a “judicial vacancy portal” by the Department of Justice. However, the absence of a statutory framework for the collegium’s functioning remains a subject of academic debate and parliamentary discussion.

  • First Judges Case (1981) – consultation ≠ concurrence.
  • Second Judges Case (1993) – CJI’s opinion with two senior judges has primacy.
  • Third Judges Case (1998) – collegium of CJI + four senior judges (SC) / CJI + two senior judges (HC).
  • MoP 1999/2017 – procedural guidelines; government may seek reconsideration once.
  • NJAC Act 2014 struck down (2015) – basic structure violation.
  • Current practice: collegium recommendations binding; President’s warrant formal.

  • Believing the CVC can prosecute; it only supervises vigilance and advises, prosecution requires sanction.
  • Thinking the Lokpal can investigate the Prime Minister without any safeguards; the Act requires a two‑thirds bench for such inquiries.
  • Overlooking that the Whistleblower Protection Act excludes intelligence agencies, creating a statutory blind spot.
  • Assuming the collegium’s recommendations are merely consultative; the Third Judges Case made them binding on the executive.

The Constitutional Distinction Between Council of Ministers and Cabinet: Article 74, Article 75 and the Kitchen Cabinet Phenomenon

The Constitution draws a sharp textual distinction between the Council of Ministers (Article 74) and the Cabinet (Article 352 explanation). Article 74(1) mandates a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President. Article 75(1) provides that Ministers shall be appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. Article 75(3) establishes collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the House of the People. The word "Cabinet" appears nowhere in the original Constitution; it was inserted by the 44th Amendment (1978) only in the explanation to Article 352, defining "Cabinet" as "the Council consisting of the Prime Minister and other Ministers of Cabinet rank appointed under Article 75".

This textual silence has produced a three-tier ministerial hierarchy in practice: Cabinet Ministers (who attend Cabinet meetings), Ministers of State (Independent Charge) (who head ministries independently but do not routinely attend Cabinet), and Ministers of State (who assist Cabinet Ministers). The Cabinet, a smaller body of 20–30 senior ministers, functions as the supreme decision-making organ. The Council of Ministers, often exceeding 50 members, rarely meets as a whole. The Kitchen Cabinet — an informal inner circle of 4–6 trusted colleagues (e.g., Finance, Home, Defence, External Affairs Ministers plus the Principal Secretary) — often pre-decides issues before formal Cabinet meetings.

The Supreme Court in U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971) held that the Council of Ministers does not cease to exist upon dissolution of the Lok Sabha; the President must always have a Council to aid and advise. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court treated Cabinet decisions as binding on the President under Article 74(1), but clarified that the advice tendered by Ministers individually (outside Cabinet) does not bind the President unless ratified by the Cabinet. The Transaction of Business Rules, 1961 (framed under Article 77(3)) operationalise this: Rule 4 requires Cabinet approval for all major policy, legislative, financial and personnel matters; Rule 12 permits the Prime Minister to dispense with Cabinet reference in urgent cases, subject to subsequent ratification.

The distinction matters for accountability. Collective responsibility under Article 75(3) applies to the entire Council, yet only Cabinet Ministers shape policy. Ministers of State cannot claim collective responsibility for decisions they were excluded from. The 91st Amendment (2003) capped the Council at 15% of Lok Sabha strength (Article 75(1A)), but did not alter the Cabinet/Council distinction. In coalition governments, the Kitchen Cabinet often includes coalition partners' leaders, creating a dual loyalty tension: they answer to their party chiefs and to the Prime Minister.

President's Power Over State Legislation: Reservation of Bills, Articles 200, 201 and Judicial Standards

Articles 200 and 201 constitute the constitutional architecture for the President's oversight of State legislation. When a Bill passed by a State Legislature is presented to the Governor, the Governor may: (a) assent, (b) withhold assent, (c) return it for reconsideration (if not a Money Bill), or (d) reserve it for the President's consideration. Reservation is mandatory under the proviso to Article 200 where the Bill derogates from the powers of the High Court (Article 226/227) or affects the President's powers under Article 360 (Financial Emergency). It is discretionary in other cases, guided by the Sarkaria Commission (1988) and Punchhi Commission (2010) recommendations that Governors should reserve Bills only on grounds of repugnancy to Central laws, deviation from national policy, or constitutional invalidity.

Once reserved, Article 201 empowers the President to either assent or withhold assent. If the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State Legislature with a message. The State Legislature must reconsider within six months; if passed again, the President is not bound to assent — a critical difference from the Governor's position under Article 200. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers (Article 74), making this a political-executive check on State legislative autonomy.

Judicial review of presidential assent/withholding is narrow but evolving. In Hoechst Pharmaceuticals v. State of Bihar (1983), the Supreme Court held that the President's satisfaction under Article 201 is justiciable only on grounds of mala fides or extraneous considerations. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977), the Court refused to interfere with the President's withholding of assent to the Karnataka Land Reforms (Amendment) Bill, treating it as a political question. However, the Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016) judgment, while addressing Governor's powers, signalled that constitutional functionaries' discretion is not immune from review when it stalls legislative functioning indefinitely.

The Tamil Nadu Admission to Undergraduate Medical Degree Courses Bill, 2021 (reserved by Governor, returned by President with message, re-passed by Assembly, assent eventually given in 2022) illustrates the procedural rigmarole. The Kerala Lok Ayukta (Amendment) Bill, 2022 and Tamil Nadu Online Gambling Prohibition Bill, 2022 remain pending with the President for years, raising questions about reasonable time limits. The Punchhi Commission recommended a six-month outer limit for presidential decision; the Sarkaria Commission suggested prior consultation with the Centre before reservation. Neither has been codified.

Scenario Application
A State passes a Bill amending its Land Revenue Code. The Governor reserves it for the President, citing repugnancy to a Central law. The President withholds assent on the advice of the Union Home Ministry. The State challenges this in the Supreme Court, arguing the President's satisfaction is mala fide because the Central law cited was repealed two years earlier. How should the Court decide?
The Court will examine whether the President's decision was based on a non-existent factual foundation. In Hoechst Pharmaceuticals, the Court held presidential satisfaction under Article 201 is justiciable on mala fides. If the Central law was indeed repealed, the ground of repugnancy is factually false, constituting an extraneous consideration. The Court can quash the withholding of assent and direct the President to reconsider. However, the Court cannot direct the President to assent — only to decide afresh on correct facts. The remedy is a writ of mandamus to the Union of India (through the Home Ministry) to tender fresh advice based on the current legal position.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks about the President's power over State Bills, always distinguish: (1) Governor's options under Article 200 (four choices), (2) President's options under Article 201 (assent/withhold/return with message), (3) the critical asymmetry — President not bound to assent on re-passage, unlike Governor. Cite Hoechst for justiciability, Punchhi Commission for the six-month norm, and the Tamil Nadu medical admission Bill (2021–22) as a recent illustration of the full cycle. Do not confuse Article 201 with Article 111 (President's assent to Union Bills) — the re-passage binding effect exists only at the Union level.

The Prime Minister's Authority Over Nuclear Command and Strategic Forces: Institutional Architecture and Democratic Control

India's nuclear command architecture, formalised in January 2003, vests exclusive authority to authorise nuclear weapon use in the Prime Minister. The three-tier structure comprises: (1) the Political Council, chaired by the Prime Minister, which is the sole body that can authorise nuclear use; (2) the Executive Council, chaired by the National Security Adviser, which provides inputs and executes decisions; and (3) the Strategic Forces Command (SFC), headed by a Commander-in-Chief of three-star rank, which operationalises launch orders. The Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) oversees policy, but the Political Council's nuclear authorisation function is distinct and PM-centric.

This architecture derives from the Rao Committee (1995) and K. Subrahmanyam Committee (1998) recommendations post-Pokhran-II. The No First Use (NFU) doctrine, declared in 2003 and reiterated in 2010, is a Political Council policy commitment, not a statutory mandate. The Prime Minister's authority is exercised through the Nuclear Command Authority (NCA) chain: a launch order flows from the PM (Political Council) → NSA (Executive Council) → SFC Commander → delivery platforms (Agni, Prithvi, K-series SLBMs, air-delivered gravity bombs). The Permissive Action Links (PALs) and dual-key procedures at the SFC level provide technical safeguards against unauthorised use, but the political decision remains solely the PM's.

Democratic control mechanisms are thin. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Defence reviews nuclear policy broadly but has no access to operational details. The Public Accounts Committee audits expenditure but not targeting doctrines. The Right to Information Act, 2005 exempts nuclear information under Section 8(1)(a) and the Atomic Energy Act, 1962. The Official Secrets Act, 1923 further cloaks the NCA. In contrast, the United States requires Presidential authorisation but subjects nuclear posture reviews to Congressional oversight (National Defense Authorization Act). The UK Prime Minister's "letters of last resort" to submarine commanders are a unique constitutional artifact; India has no equivalent written delegation for decapitation scenarios.

The 2019 Balakot airstrike and subsequent 2020 Ladakh standoff tested conventional escalation thresholds but did not engage the NCA. The 2022 accidental BrahMos missile firing into Pakistan (attributed to a technical malfunction during maintenance) highlighted the risks of hair-trigger alert levels. The SFC's shift to canisterised, road-mobile Agni-V with MIRV capability (tested March 2024 under Mission Divyastra) compresses decision timelines. The Prime Minister's Office (PMO) maintains a Nuclear Risk Reduction Centre (NRRC) hotline with Pakistan's NRRC (established 2007) and a similar mechanism with China (2021), but these are confidence-building measures, not command-and-control links.

Office of Profit Jurisprudence: Constitutional Text, Judicial Tests and Executive-Legislature Friction

Articles 102(1)(a) and 191(1)(a) disqualify a person from being a Member of Parliament or State Legislature if they hold an office of profit under the Government of India or any State Government, other than an office declared by Parliament or State Legislature not to disqualify. The Constitution does not define "office of profit"; judicial interpretation has constructed a three-prong test: (1) the office must be under the government (executive control over appointment, removal, remuneration), (2) it must carry profit (pecuniary gain, however small, beyond reimbursement), and (3) it must be an "office" (independent duties, not merely advisory).

The seminal Pradyut Bordoloi v. Swapan Roy (2001) five-judge bench distilled the test: government control over appointment, tenure, and emoluments is the hallmark. In Jaya Bachchan v. Union of India (2006), the Supreme Court upheld disqualification for holding the chairpersonship of the Uttar Pradesh Film Development Council — a body funded and controlled by the State government — even though the honorarium was nominal. Conversely, in Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab (1994), membership of a statutory advisory committee without remuneration or executive powers was held not to be an office of profit.

Parliament and State Legislatures have enacted exemption laws under Articles 102(1)(a) and 191(1)(a) to protect specific offices. The Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959 (amended repeatedly) lists exempted offices. The 2006 amendment (after the Jaya Bachchan controversy) added 55 offices retrospectively, sparking a constitutional challenge. In Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (2009), the Supreme Court upheld retrospective validation, ruling that Parliament's power to declare an office non-disqualifying is plenary and can cure past defects.

The Delhi Parliamentary Secretaries case (2017) saw the Election Commission recommend disqualification of 20 AAP MLAs appointed as Parliamentary Secretaries; the President accepted the EC's opinion under Article 103. The Delhi High Court later set aside the EC order on procedural grounds (no oral hearing), but the Supreme Court in Manish Sisodia v. Election Commission (2023) restored the disqualification, holding that Parliamentary Secretaries with ministerial rank, office, staff, and car constitute an office of profit regardless of salary. The West Bengal, Punjab, Telangana governments have faced similar EC references. The core tension: Chief Ministers use Parliamentary Secretary posts to accommodate coalition partners and surplus MLAs, circumventing the 15% ministerial cap (Article 164(1A)/75(1A)). The judiciary treats substance over form; the executive treats form as shield.

Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils and the President's Role in Cooperative Federalism

Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council (ISC) if it appears to him that the public interest would be served. The President defines its duties, organisation and procedure. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended a permanent ISC; the Vajpayee government constituted it in 1990 via Presidential Order. The ISC comprises the Prime Minister (Chairman), Chief Ministers of all States, Chief Ministers of UTs with legislatures, Administrators of UTs without legislatures, and six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the PM. Its mandate: inquire into and advise on disputes between States, investigate subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination.

The ISC has met only 12 times in 34 years (1990, 1996, 1997, 2003, 2005, 2006, 2007, 2008, 2011, 2016, 2017, 2022). The 12th meeting (2022) discussed crop diversification, NEP 2020 implementation, and police reforms. The Standing Committee of the ISC, chaired by the Home Minister, meets more frequently to process agenda items. The Punchhi Commission (2010) recommended renaming it the "Inter-Governmental Council", giving it a permanent secretariat, and making its recommendations binding on the Centre — none implemented.

Separately, the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 created five Zonal Councils (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern) as statutory bodies, not constitutional. The Union Home Minister is the common Chairman; Chief Ministers of States in the zone are Vice-Chairmen by rotation. They address regional issues: river water sharing, power, transport, border disputes, minority welfare. The North-Eastern Council (NEC), created by the NEC Act, 1971, covers the eight NE States plus Sikkim; its Chairman is the Home Minister, Vice-Chairman is the DoNER Minister.

The President's role is initiatory and formal: the ISC exists only by Presidential Order; Zonal Councils exist by statute. The President does not attend meetings. The realpolitik is that the Prime Minister and Home Minister drive these forums. The GST Council (Article 279A), a constitutional body with voting rules, has eclipsed the ISC in fiscal federalism. The National Development Council (abolished 2015, replaced by NITI Aayog Governing Council) was another coordination layer. The ISC's dormancy reflects the centralisation of inter-governmental bargaining in the PMO and the Home Ministry, reducing the President's Article 263 power to a dead letter.

  • Confusing the Council of Ministers (Article 74) with the Cabinet (Article 352 explanation) — the former is constitutionally mandated, the latter is a conventional subset.
  • Assuming the President's assent to a reserved State Bill is bound by the State Legislature's re-passage — Article 201 imposes no such obligation, unlike Article 111 for Union Bills.
  • Believing the Nuclear Command Authority requires Cabinet Committee on Security approval for launch — the Political Council (PM alone) authorises; CCS oversees policy, not operational release.
  • Treating "office of profit" as requiring substantial salary — the test is government control over appointment, tenure and emoluments; even nominal honorarium suffices if control exists.
  • Thinking the Inter-State Council is a permanent constitutional body — it exists only by Presidential Order under Article 263 and has met 12 times since 1990.
  • Overlooking that the Prime Minister's nuclear authority has no statutory basis — it rests on a 2003 executive order, not an Act of Parliament.
  • Assuming Zonal Councils are constitutional bodies — they are statutory under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, chaired by the Home Minister, not the President.

President's Appointment of the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners: Constitutional Scheme, Judicial Intervention and the 2023 Supreme Court Ruling

Article 324(2) of the Constitution vests the power of appointment of the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and the Election Commissioners (ECs) in the President, who acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. The CEC and ECs hold office for a term of six years or until they attain the age of sixty‑five years, whichever is earlier. Their removal follows the same procedure as that of a Judge of the Supreme Court — a motion passed by a special majority in each House of Parliament on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

Historically, the executive has exercised this appointment power without any institutional consultation, leading to allegations of partisan selections. The Supreme Court in Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023) examined the constitutional validity of the existing practice. A five‑judge Constitution Bench held that the exclusive executive control over appointments violated the principle of institutional independence implicit in Article 324 and the equality guarantee under Article 14. The Court directed that, until Parliament enacts a law, appointments shall be made by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the leader of the largest opposition party) and the Chief Justice of India or a Supreme Court judge nominated by the CJI.

The judgment is interim; it does not create a permanent collegium but establishes a transitional mechanism. In response, the government introduced the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Bill, 2023, which proposes a selection committee with the same composition but adds a provision for a search committee. The Bill is yet to be passed. The President’s role remains formal — the warrant of appointment is issued under the President’s hand and seal — but the substantive decision‑making has shifted from unilateral executive discretion to a multi‑institutional process.

Key distinctions from the appointment of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) are noteworthy. The CAG is appointed under Article 148 by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister, but the CAG’s tenure and removal are also protected by a special majority. However, no judicial directive has mandated a collegium for the CAG. The Election Commission’s unique status as the guardian of free and fair elections justified the Court’s intervention.

For aspirants, the critical take‑aways are: (1) the constitutional text (Article 324), (2) the 2023 Supreme Court ruling and its interim collegium, (3) the pending legislative framework, and (4) the President’s ceremonial role versus the real decision‑making authority.

Prime Minister's Control over Portfolio Allocation and Cabinet Reshuffles: Conventions, Judicial Review and the 2021 Ministerial Reshuffle

Article 75(1) provides that the President shall appoint ministers on the advice of the Prime Minister. The allocation of portfolios among ministers is an inherent incident of this advisory power; the Prime Minister decides which minister heads which ministry. This prerogative is a political instrument used to reward loyalty, manage coalition partners, and signal policy priorities. The President’s role is purely formal — the President signs the allocation orders on the Prime Minister’s recommendation.

Constitutional convention treats portfolio allocation as a non‑justiciable political question. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 is subject to judicial review, but the Prime Minister’s advice on ministerial appointments and portfolio distribution falls within the realm of political discretion. Courts have consistently refused to interfere with the merits of a reshuffle, emphasizing that the Prime Minister must command the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

The July 2021 reshuffle illustrates the strategic use of this power. The Prime Minister inducted 43 ministers, created the new Ministry of Cooperation, and reassigned key portfolios such as Health, Education, and Petroleum. The reshuffle was widely interpreted as a response to the second wave of the pandemic, electoral considerations in upcoming state elections, and the need to accommodate allies. The Prime Minister also dropped several senior ministers, demonstrating that tenure is at the Prime Minister’s pleasure subject to the overall confidence of the House.

Constraints on this power arise from coalition dynamics, party organisational norms, and the principle of collective responsibility under Article 75(3). A minister who loses the Prime Minister’s confidence can be asked to resign; the Prime Minister may also reshuffle to maintain intra‑party balance. However, the President cannot independently reject the Prime Minister’s advice on portfolio allocation.

In summary, the Prime Minister’s control over portfolios is a constitutional convention rooted in Article 75(1), exercised as a political tool, and largely insulated from judicial scrutiny. The 2021 reshuffle serves as a contemporary case study of its operational dimensions.

Application
Which constitutional article empowers the Prime Minister to allocate portfolios among ministers, and what is the President’s role in this process?
Article 75(1) read with Article 74(1) empowers the Prime Minister to advise the President on the appointment of ministers and the allocation of portfolios. The President acts on this advice and has no independent discretion; the allocation order is issued under the President’s signature but reflects the Prime Minister’s decision.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the “constitutional basis” of portfolio allocation, cite Article 75(1) and the convention that the President acts on the Prime Minister’s advice. Do not confuse this with Article 74(1) which deals with the Council of Ministers’ aid and advice generally. Remember that judicial review does not extend to the merits of a reshuffle.

The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs: Composition, Decision‑Making Process and Influence on Fiscal Federalism

The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA) is a standing committee of the Union Cabinet chaired by the Prime Minister. Its membership typically includes the Finance Minister, the Home Minister, the Minister of Agriculture and Farmers’ Welfare, the Minister of Commerce and Industry, the Minister of Rural Development, and other senior ministers as the Prime Minister may nominate. The committee does not have a statutory basis; it is constituted by an executive order of the Cabinet Secretariat.

The CCEA’s mandate covers a wide range of economic policy decisions: approval of public sector investment proposals above a specified threshold, fixation of minimum support prices for agricultural commodities, allocation of subsidies (fertiliser, food, fuel), clearance of foreign direct investment proposals in sensitive sectors, and oversight of centrally sponsored schemes that have a significant fiscal impact on states. Decisions are taken by consensus, but the Prime Minister, as chair, exercises a decisive vote when consensus cannot be reached.

The committee’s influence on fiscal federalism is profound. By approving the quantum and conditions of central assistance to states — such as the GST compensation cess, the Finance Commission’s devolution recommendations, and the design of centrally sponsored schemes — the CCEA shapes the fiscal space available to state governments. The 2021 reconstitution of the CCEA added the Minister of Cooperation and the Minister of Jal Shakti, reflecting the government’s emphasis on cooperative federalism and water resource management.

Procedurally, proposals are routed through the concerned ministry to the Cabinet Secretariat, which prepares a note for the CCEA. The note includes financial implications, inter‑ministerial comments, and a recommendation. The committee meets as required, often weekly during budget sessions. Minutes are recorded and communicated to the implementing ministries for action. The CCEA’s decisions are binding on all ministries and, through them, on state implementing agencies.

For examination purposes, remember: (1) the CCEA is a non‑statutory executive body, (2) its chair is the Prime Minister, (3) it wields decisive authority over economic policy with direct fiscal consequences for states, and (4) its composition is flexible and determined by the Prime Minister.

President's Role in the Appointment of Lieutenant Governors and Administrators of Union Territories: Constitutional Provisions, Political Practice and Judicial Scrutiny

Articles 239 and 239AA govern the administration of Union Territories (UTs). The President appoints a Lieutenant Governor (LG) for UTs with a legislative assembly — Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir — and an Administrator for the remaining UTs. The appointment is made on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister; the President’s discretion is negligible.

The LG of Delhi occupies a unique constitutional position. Under Article 239AA, the LG is bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers of the National Capital Territory, except in matters of land, police, and public order, which remain under the LG’s direct control. The Supreme Court in Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) and again in 2023 reiterated that the LG’s discretionary sphere is limited to these three subjects and that the LG must act on the advice of the elected government in all other matters. The 2023 judgment also clarified that the LG cannot refer every decision to the President; such references are permissible only on the specified reserved subjects.

For UTs without legislatures, the Administrator exercises executive power directly on behalf of the President, again on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers. The Administrator’s tenure is at the pleasure of the President, which in practice means the pleasure of the Central government.

Political practice shows that LGs and Administrators are often former civil servants or politicians aligned with the ruling party at the Centre. The appointment process does not involve consultation with the concerned UT’s legislature or chief minister. This has led to friction, especially in Delhi, where the elected government has accused the LG of obstructing policy decisions.

Key points for aspirants: (1) the President’s appointment power under Articles 239/239AA is exercised on the Prime Minister’s advice, (2) the LG of Delhi has limited discretion confined to land, police, and public order, (3) the Supreme Court has curtailed the LG’s power to refer matters to the President, and (4) the Administrator’s role in non‑legislative UTs is that of a direct agent of the Centre.

Prime Minister's Leadership of the NITI Aayog Governing Council: Structure, Cooperative Federalism and Policy Coordination

The National Institution for Transforming India (NITI) Aayog was established on 1 January 2015 by a Cabinet resolution, replacing the Planning Commission. Its Governing Council is the apex body, chaired by the Prime Minister, and comprises all Chief Ministers of states, Chief Ministers of Union Territories with legislatures, Lieutenant Governors of other UTs, and a select group of Union Ministers. The Vice‑Chairperson of NITI Aayog, appointed by the Prime Minister, functions as the de facto chief executive.

The Governing Council meets periodically — typically once or twice a year — to discuss the national development agenda, review the implementation of centrally sponsored schemes, and deliberate on issues of inter‑state significance such as water sharing, infrastructure corridors, and the Aspirational Districts Programme. The Council’s resolutions are advisory; they do not bind states legally but carry significant political weight because they are adopted in a forum headed by the Prime Minister.

The Prime Minister uses the Council to drive cooperative federalism by setting a shared vision — for example, the target of a $5 trillion economy, the Sustainable Development Goals localisation, and the “Team India” approach to disaster management. The Council also serves as a platform for states to flag fiscal constraints, request flexibility in scheme guidelines, and negotiate the terms of central assistance.

Institutional support is provided by the NITI Aayog secretariat, which prepares background papers, monitors outcomes through the Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO), and coordinates with line ministries. The Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) closely tracks the Council’s agenda and follow‑up action, often issuing directives to ministries based on Council discussions.

For examination, note: (1) the Governing Council is a non‑statutory, advisory body chaired by the Prime Minister, (2) its composition reflects the federal principle by including all Chief Ministers, (3) its decisions are not legally binding but influence policy through political consensus, and (4) the Prime Minister’s leadership determines the Council’s effectiveness in translating cooperative federalism into actionable outcomes.
