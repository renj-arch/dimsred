1. Constitutional Framework of the Union Executive

1.1 The Constitutional Scheme

The Constitution of India establishes a parliamentary system in which the Union executive is vested formally in the President but exercised in practice by the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. Articles 52 to 78 in Part V lay down the structure, powers and inter‑relationships of the President, the Vice‑President, the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers. The scheme reflects the British model of a ceremonial head of state and a real executive accountable to the lower house, yet it incorporates distinctive features such as the President’s discretionary powers under Article 74(2) and the provision for President’s rule under Article 356. The framers deliberately chose this arrangement to combine stability with democratic accountability, ensuring that the executive remains responsible to the Lok Sabha while the President acts as a constitutional guardian.

1.2 Distribution of Executive Power

Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in the President, who exercises it either directly or through officers subordinate to him in accordance with the Constitution. However, Article 74(1) mandates that the President shall act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers, making the advice binding except in the limited situations where the President may require the Council to reconsider its advice. This constitutional design creates a duality: the President is the formal repository of all executive authority, while the Prime Minister and the Cabinet wield real political power. The balance is maintained by the requirement that the Council of Ministers be collectively responsible to the House of the People under Article 75(3), which forces the real executive to retain the confidence of the elected legislature.

1.3 Role of the Vice‑President

The Vice‑President, elected by an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament under Article 66, serves as the ex‑officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and acts as President when a vacancy occurs or when the President is unable to discharge functions. Although the Vice‑President does not possess independent executive authority, the office provides continuity in the highest constitutional office and plays a crucial role during interregnums, as witnessed during the presidential vacancies of 1969 and 1977. The Vice‑President’s legislative role also influences the functioning of the upper house, thereby indirectly affecting executive‑legislative dynamics.

1.4 Constitutional Amendments Affecting the Executive

Several amendments have reshaped the Union executive. The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review of presidential decisions and expanded the President’s power to promulgate ordinances, but the 44th Amendment (1978) restored the pre‑emergency position by reinstating the binding nature of ministerial advice and limiting ordinance‑making to six weeks. The 91st Amendment (2003) capped the size of the Council of Ministers at fifteen percent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha, aiming to prevent oversized cabinets and promote administrative efficiency. These amendments illustrate the evolving tension between executive authority and parliamentary control.

2. The President: Election, Powers and Position

2.1 Election of the President

The President is elected by an electoral college comprising the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States and Union territories of Delhi and Puducherry, as stipulated in Article 54. The system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote ensures that the President reflects a broad national consensus rather than a simple parliamentary majority. The value of each vote is calculated to give equal weight to the population of each state, thereby balancing federal representation with demographic equity. The election is conducted by the Election Commission under the Presidential and Vice‑Presidential Elections Act, 1952, and disputes are adjudicated by the Supreme Court under Article 71.

2.2 Executive, Legislative and Financial Powers

Article 53 confers the executive power of the Union on the President, who appoints the Prime Minister, other ministers on the Prime Minister’s advice, the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General, governors of states, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and a host of other constitutional functionaries. The President summons and prorogues Parliament, addresses both Houses at the commencement of the first session after each general election, and can dissolve the Lok Sabha on the advice of the Council of Ministers. In the legislative sphere, the President gives assent to bills, may return a non‑money bill for reconsideration, and promulgates ordinances under Article 123 when Parliament is not in session. Financially, the President causes the annual budget to be laid before Parliament, constitutes the Finance Commission under Article 280, and can make advances out of the Contingency Fund to meet unforeseen expenditure.

2.3 Discretionary Powers and Constitutional Safeguards

Although the President generally acts on ministerial advice, the Constitution envisages limited discretionary powers. Under Article 74(2) the President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice, and the President’s decision after such reconsideration is final. The President also exercises discretion in appointing a Prime Minister when no party commands a clear majority, as seen in 1989, 1996 and 1998. The power to grant pardons, reprieves and remissions under Article 72 is exercised on the advice of the Council but the President may seek further information. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 is subject to judicial review, reinforcing the principle that discretionary powers are not absolute.

2.4 Position of the President in the Parliamentary System

The President occupies a unique position as the constitutional head of state, symbolising the unity and integrity of the nation. While the President’s role is largely ceremonial, the office serves as a vital check on the executive by virtue of the power to return advice, to seek information, and to act as a guardian of the Constitution during political crises. The President’s address to Parliament outlines the government’s policy agenda, and the assent to legislation provides a final constitutional scrutiny. The office also plays a diplomatic role, receiving credentials of foreign envoys and representing India in state visits, thereby embodying the sovereignty of the Republic.

Example Question
Explain the constitutional provisions that govern the election of the President of India and discuss how the system of proportional representation by single transferable vote ensures federal balance.
The election of the President is governed by Article 54 read with the Presidential and Vice‑Presidential Elections Act, 1952. The electoral college consists of elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States and the Union territories of Delhi and Puducherry. The value of each vote is determined by a formula that equalises the total value of votes of the Parliamentarians with the total value of votes of the State legislators, thereby giving each state a weight proportionate to its population. The single transferable vote system allows electors to rank candidates; if no candidate secures the required quota in the first count, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and his votes are transferred according to the next preferences. This method prevents a simple majority in Parliament from dominating the outcome and ensures that the President enjoys a broad-based mandate reflecting both national and federal dimensions.
Exam Insight
When a question asks about the President’s discretionary powers, focus on the three recognized areas: appointment of the Prime Minister in a hung Parliament, the power to return advice for reconsideration under Article 74(2), and the pardoning power under Article 72. Cite the S.R. Bommai judgment to show judicial review of President’s rule.

The Vice-President of India: Election, Powers and Office

Election of the Vice-President (Article 66)

Article 63 provides that there shall be a Vice-President of India. Under Article 66, the Vice-President is elected by an electoral college consisting of the members of both Houses of Parliament, and the election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, the voting being by secret ballot. Two features distinguish this college from the one that elects the President: the members of the State Legislative Assemblies do not take part, and the nominated members of Parliament do take part. The result is an office chosen by the national legislature alone, which is why the Vice-President is often described as a nominee of Parliament rather than of the Union and the States together. A candidate must be a citizen of India, must have completed thirty-five years of age, and must be qualified for election as a member of the Council of States. The Election Commission conducts the election under the Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952, and disputes relating to the election are decided by the Supreme Court under Article 71.

Term, Resignation and Removal (Articles 67 and 68)

The Vice-President holds office for five years but may be removed earlier. Resignation is addressed to the President. Removal requires a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Council of States and agreed to by the House of the People, and no ground need be assigned; fourteen days' notice of the intention to move the resolution is mandatory. This procedure is deliberately easier than the impeachment of the President under Article 61, which requires proof of a violation of the Constitution: the Vice-President is a parliamentary officer and is answerable to Parliament on a political majority. A vacancy must be filled without delay. An election to fill a vacancy caused by the expiry of the term must be completed before the term ends, and a vacancy caused by death, resignation or removal must be filled as soon as possible; the person so elected serves only the unexpired portion of the term.

Ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Articles 64, 89 and 100)

Article 64 makes the Vice-President the ex-officio Chairman of the Council of States. In this capacity the holder presides over the Rajya Sabha, maintains order, decides points of order and regulates its procedure. The Chairman does not vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote in the case of an equality of votes under Article 100. The office therefore places the second-highest constitutional authority of the Union in charge of the Upper House, giving the Vice-President a continuing legislative role quite apart from the presidency. It is this dual character, standby head of state and presiding officer of the Rajya Sabha, that defines the office.

Acting as President (Article 65)

When a vacancy occurs in the office of President by death, resignation, removal or otherwise, or when the President is unable to discharge functions by reason of absence, illness or any other cause, the Vice-President discharges those functions until a new President is elected or the President resumes duty. Article 62 requires that a vacancy caused by death, resignation or removal be filled within six months, so the Vice-President's tenure as acting head of state is necessarily temporary; while acting as President the occupant exercises all the powers of the President and the Rajya Sabha is presided over by its Deputy Chairman. The office has twice supplied the head of state in this way: in 1969, after the death of President Zakir Husain, and in 1977, after the death of President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed.

Position and Significance

The Vice-President possesses no independent executive authority; the real executive is the Council of Ministers. The office is nonetheless important because it guarantees continuity in the headship of state and because the occupant presides over the Rajya Sabha, where the government's legislative programme is scrutinised and where the chamber's federal and reviewing functions are exercised. A comparison with the President is instructive: the President is elected by an electoral college that includes the States, is removable only by impeachment for a violation of the Constitution, and holds the formal executive power of the Union, while the Vice-President is elected by Parliament alone, is removable by an effective majority of the Rajya Sabha, and holds no executive power save as a stand-in. The two offices together ensure that there is never a vacancy in the constitutional headship of the Republic.

3. The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers

3.1 Appointment and Tenure of the Prime Minister

Article 75(1) provides that the Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President, and the other ministers shall be appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. In practice, the President invites the leader of the party or coalition that commands a majority in the Lok Sabha to form the government. The Prime Minister holds office during the pleasure of the President, but the pleasure is constitutionally tied to the confidence of the Lok Sabha; a loss of majority triggers resignation or dissolution. The 91st Amendment limits the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, to fifteen percent of the Lok Sabha’s strength, reinforcing the principle of a compact executive.

3.2 Powers and Functions of the Prime Minister

The Prime Minister is the keystone of the Cabinet system. He allocates portfolios, chairs Cabinet meetings, coordinates policy across ministries, and acts as the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers. Under Article 78, the Prime Minister furnishes information to the President regarding the administration of the Union and proposals for legislation. The Prime Minister also advises the President on the appointment of key constitutional authorities, the summoning and prorogation of Parliament, and the issuance of ordinances. In foreign affairs, the Prime Minister represents India at summit meetings and directs the Ministry of External Affairs, while the President performs ceremonial diplomatic functions.

3.3 Collective Responsibility and Individual Responsibility

Article 75(3) enshrines the principle of collective responsibility: the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the House of the People. This means that a vote of no‑confidence against any minister is deemed a vote against the entire Council, compelling the Prime Minister to either resign or seek a dissolution. Individual responsibility, on the other hand, requires each minister to answer for the acts of his ministry in Parliament. The interplay of these two doctrines ensures that the executive remains accountable both as a team and as individual office‑holders, a balance that has been tested during coalition governments where intra‑party dissent can threaten collective unity.

3.4 The Prime Minister's Constitutional Position and the Council of Ministers

Article 74(1) is the pivot of the parliamentary executive: the President shall act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, and that advice cannot be disregarded except through the one-time power to require reconsideration. Article 75(1) provides for the appointment of the Prime Minister by the President and of the other Ministers on the Prime Minister's advice, and Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the House of the People. The Prime Minister's centrality follows from these provisions rather than from any single grant of power. The office determines the size and composition of the Council, allocates portfolios, can demand a Minister's resignation, and, by resigning, brings down the entire Council. Article 78 reinforces the position by requiring the Prime Minister to communicate to the President all decisions of the Council and to furnish such information as the President may call for.

The practical reach of the office has varied with the political context. In the era of single-party majorities the Prime Minister dominated the Cabinet, the party and Parliament; in the coalition era after 1989 the office became a negotiator among allies, and the Cabinet Committee system acquired greater importance as a means of managing coalition partners. The Prime Minister's Office, staffed by civil servants and political advisers, provides continuity and coordination across ministries, while the Cabinet Secretariat services the Cabinet and its committees. The Prime Minister also advises the President on the summoning and prorogation of Parliament, the dissolution of the House of the People, and the appointment of the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General and other constitutional functionaries. The relationship between the Prime Minister and the Cabinet is therefore not fixed by the text of the Constitution: it is settled by the arithmetic of the Lok Sabha, the cohesion of the ruling party, and the conventions of collective responsibility.

4. The Cabinet: Composition, Functions and Collective Responsibility

4.1 Composition and Hierarchy

The Cabinet is the inner core of the Council of Ministers, comprising senior ministers who hold important portfolios such as Home, Finance, Defence, External Affairs and Agriculture. While the Constitution does not define the Cabinet separately, the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, and the Cabinet Secretariat’s directives establish its composition. The Prime Minister decides who belongs to the Cabinet, and the Cabinet Secretary, the senior‑most civil servant, acts as the coordinator and secretary to the Cabinet. The Cabinet meets regularly, usually once a week, to deliberate on major policy issues, legislative proposals and administrative matters.

4.2 Decision‑Making Process

Cabinet decisions are taken by consensus; formal voting is rare. The Prime Minister sets the agenda, and the Cabinet Secretary circulates notes prepared by the concerned ministries. Once a decision is reached, it is recorded as a Cabinet decision and communicated to the relevant ministries for implementation. The principle of collective responsibility obliges all ministers to publicly support Cabinet decisions, even if they privately disagreed. This process ensures coherence in government policy and prevents public dissent that could undermine parliamentary confidence.

4.3 Cabinet Committees and Coordination Mechanisms

To manage the volume of work, the Cabinet operates through several standing committees such as the Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs, the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, the Cabinet Committee on Security, and the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet. These committees are chaired by the Prime Minister or a senior minister and allow detailed scrutiny of specific domains before matters reach the full Cabinet. The Cabinet Secretariat, headed by the Cabinet Secretary, provides the institutional memory and administrative support necessary for the smooth functioning of these committees.

5. Civil Services: Structure, Recruitment and Accountability

5.1 Constitutional Basis and All‑India Services

Articles 310 to 313 provide the constitutional foundation for the civil services. Article 310 establishes the tenure of office of persons serving the Union or a State at the pleasure of the President or the Governor, subject to constitutional safeguards. Article 312 empowers Parliament to create All‑India Services common to the Union and the States, the most prominent being the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), the Indian Police Service (IPS) and the Indian Forest Service (IFoS). These services are recruited by the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) through a competitive examination, and their members serve both the Centre and the States, providing a unified administrative framework that sustains the federal structure.

5.2 Recruitment, Training and Career Progression

The UPSC conducts the Civil Services Examination annually, comprising a preliminary test, a main examination and a personality test. Successful candidates are allocated to various services based on merit and preference. The Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration at Mussoorie provides foundational training for IAS officers, while the Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel National Police Academy at Hyderabad trains IPS officers. Career progression follows a time‑scale and performance‑based promotion system, with officers moving through junior, senior and super‑time scales, culminating in the apex scale of Secretary to the Government of India or Director General of Police.

5.3 Accountability, Conduct Rules and Reforms

The All‑India Services (Conduct) Rules, 1968, and the Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964, prescribe standards of integrity, political neutrality and procedural propriety. Disciplinary proceedings are governed by the All‑India Services (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969, which provide for inquiry, penalty and appeal mechanisms. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005‑2009) recommended measures such as lateral entry at senior levels, performance‑based appraisal, and a statutory Civil Services Board to insulate transfers and postings from political interference. Recent initiatives like Mission Karmayogi aim to shift the paradigm from rule‑based to role‑based capacity building, reflecting an ongoing effort to modernise the bureaucracy while preserving its constitutional mandate.

Discretionary Powers of the President in Government Formation and Dissolution

The President’s discretionary space is narrow but decisive when the Lok Sabha fails to produce a clear majority. Article 75(1) empowers the President to appoint the Prime Minister, but the Constitution does not prescribe a formula for a hung Parliament. In practice, the President follows the convention of inviting the leader of the single largest party or pre‑poll alliance to form the government and to prove majority on the floor of the House within a stipulated period. The 1996, 1998 and 1999 episodes illustrate the pattern: the President invited Atal Bihari Vajpayee (1996), then H. D. Deve Gowda (1996), and later Vajpayee again (1998) after assessing the numerical strength and stability of competing claims.

Judicial scrutiny of this discretion arrived in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006), where the Supreme Court held that the President’s decision to dissolve the Assembly in Bihar was subject to judicial review for mala fide exercise. The Court reiterated that while the President acts on ministerial advice under Article 74(1), the advice itself must be constitutionally valid. In a hung Parliament, the President may also exercise the power to dissolve the Lok Sabha under Article 85(2) on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but if the advice is tendered by a caretaker government that has lost the confidence of the House, the President may refuse dissolution and instead explore alternative formations. The 1999 Vajpayee government’s loss of a confidence motion led to the President inviting the opposition to form a government, a step that underscored the President’s role as a constitutional arbiter rather than a passive rubber stamp.

The President’s pardoning power under Article 72, though not directly linked to government formation, has been invoked in politically sensitive cases (e.g., the commutation of death sentences in the Rajiv Gandhi assassination case). The Supreme Court in Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) laid down that the exercise of clemency is subject to limited judicial review on grounds of arbitrariness, mala fide, or non‑application of mind. This principle extends to any discretionary act of the President, reinforcing that discretion is not absolute.

Key Takeaways

  • President invites the leader most likely to command a majority; no constitutional formula exists.
  • Judicial review available for mala fide or arbitrary exercise (Rameshwar Prasad).
  • Caretaker conventions restrict major policy decisions; President may refuse dissolution if advice is constitutionally suspect.
  • Pardoning power under Article 72 is reviewable for arbitrariness (Epuru Sudhakar).

Ordinance Making Power of the President: Scope, Limits and Judicial Review

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session and immediate action is deemed necessary. The ordinance has the same force as an Act of Parliament but must be laid before both Houses within six weeks of reassembly; otherwise it lapses. The power is conditional on the satisfaction of the President that circumstances exist rendering immediate legislation necessary. This satisfaction is not immune from judicial scrutiny. In D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), the Supreme Court struck down a series of repromulgated ordinances, holding that repeated re‑issuance without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution and an abuse of the ordinance‑making power.

The Court in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) refined the test: the President’s satisfaction is justiciable to the extent of examining whether the executive has placed relevant material before the President and whether the ordinance is a colourable exercise of power. The judgment also clarified that an ordinance cannot amend the Constitution, nor can it override a Supreme Court judgment that has declared a law unconstitutional. The 2015 ordinance on the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) was challenged on these grounds, though the matter was later rendered academic by the Court’s striking down of the NJAC Act itself.

Repromulgation remains a contentious practice. The 2013 ordinance on the Securities Laws (Amendment) was repromulgated thrice, prompting criticism that the executive was bypassing parliamentary debate. The Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) recommended a statutory limit on repromulgation and a requirement for the President to record reasons for each ordinance. While not yet enacted, the recommendation reflects the constitutional consensus that ordinance‑making is an emergency power, not a parallel legislative route.

Key Takeaways

  • Ordinance requires immediate necessity; satisfaction is justiciable (D.C. Wadhwa, Krishna Kumar Singh).
  • Cannot amend Constitution or override judicial declarations of unconstitutionality.
  • Repromulgation without legislative approval is constitutionally suspect.
  • Law Commission recommends statutory safeguards and reasoned orders.
Examiner's Twist
A question asks: "The President promulgates an ordinance when Parliament is in recess. The ordinance is repromulgated twice without being placed before Parliament. Which constitutional principle is violated and which landmark case supports the answer?"
The principle violated is the prohibition against using ordinance‑making as a substitute for legislative power, amounting to a fraud on the Constitution. The landmark case is D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), where the Supreme Court held that repeated repromulgation without legislative approval is unconstitutional.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question on ordinances appears, first identify whether the issue is about the initial promulgation (Article 123 conditions) or repromulgation (D.C. Wadhwa). Cite the exact Article and the case name; the examiner awards marks for precise linkage.

Cabinet Committees: Statutory Basis, Functional Dynamics and Recent Reforms

Cabinet Committees are not created by statute; they derive their existence from the executive authority of the Prime Minister under the Transaction of Business Rules framed under Article 77(3). The Rules allocate specific policy domains to standing committees — Political Affairs, Economic Affairs, Security, Parliamentary Affairs, Accommodation, and Appointments — each chaired by the Prime Minister or a senior minister. The Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS), for instance, comprises the Prime Minister, Ministers of Defence, Home, Finance, and External Affairs, and the National Security Advisor as a permanent invitee. Its decisions on defence procurement, intelligence appointments, and nuclear policy are binding on the full Cabinet unless overturned.

The decision‑making flow follows a structured hierarchy: proposals originate in ministries, are vetted by the Cabinet Secretariat, placed before the relevant committee, and, if approved, forwarded to the full Cabinet for ratification. The Cabinet Secretariat, headed by the Cabinet Secretary (the senior‑most civil servant), maintains institutional memory, circulates minutes, and ensures follow‑up action. In 2021, the government reconstituted the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs to include the Minister of Commerce and Industry and the Minister of Railways, reflecting a shift toward integrated infrastructure planning.

Coordination mechanisms extend beyond standing committees. The Group of Ministers (GoM) and Empowered Group of Ministers (EGoM) are ad‑hoc bodies formed for inter‑ministerial issues such as disinvestment, climate change, or pandemic response. Their recommendations are submitted to the appropriate Cabinet Committee. The 2020 pandemic saw the creation of a high‑level Group of Ministers on COVID‑19, which met daily and reported to the CCS, illustrating the flexibility of the committee system in crisis management.

Key Takeaways

  • Cabinet Committees are executive bodies under Transaction of Business Rules, not statutory.
  • CCS is the apex security decision‑making forum; includes NSA as permanent invitee.
  • Cabinet Secretariat provides secretarial support, minutes, and follow‑up.
  • Ad‑hoc GoMs/EGoMs address cross‑cutting issues; recommendations feed into standing committees.

Civil Services Reforms: Lateral Entry, Performance Appraisal and Judicial Oversight

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2005‑2009) recommended lateral entry at joint secretary and director levels to infuse domain expertise into the higher bureaucracy. The government operationalised this in 2018 by advertising ten joint secretary‑level posts open to private‑sector professionals, academics, and state‑government officers. Subsequent rounds in 2020 and 2022 expanded the pool to include specialists in finance, agriculture, and technology. While lateral entrants bring fresh perspectives, concerns about cadre morale, seniority, and accountability persist.

Performance appraisal has shifted from the Annual Confidential Report (ACR) to the Performance Appraisal Report (PAR) and, more recently, to the 360‑degree feedback mechanism under Mission Karmayogi (launched 2020). Mission Karmayogi aims to transition from rule‑based to role‑based capacity building, linking training modules to competency frameworks defined for each post. The Integrated Government Online Training (iGOT) platform delivers these modules, and completion records feed into promotion considerations.

Judicial oversight of transfers and postings was strengthened in T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013), where the Supreme Court directed the establishment of a Civil Services Board at the Centre and in each state to recommend transfers, postings, and disciplinary actions, insulating them from political interference. The Court also mandated fixed tenures for key posts (e.g., Director General of Police, Chief Secretary) and required that any premature transfer be recorded with reasons. Despite the judgment, compliance remains uneven; several states have not constituted the Boards, and the Centre’s Civil Services Board functions largely in an advisory capacity.

Key Takeaways

  • Lateral entry introduced in 2018 for joint secretary posts; expands domain expertise.
  • Mission Karmayogi (2020) shifts to competency‑based, role‑linked training via iGOT.
  • Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian mandated Civil Services Boards and fixed tenures.
  • Implementation gaps persist; political interference in transfers continues in practice.
  • Confusing the President’s discretionary power with the Governor’s power under Article 163.
  • Assuming ordinance‑making power is absolute; forgetting the six‑week parliamentary approval requirement.
  • Treating Cabinet Committees as statutory bodies rather than executive creations under Transaction of Business Rules.
  • Overlooking the distinction between lateral entry and regular UPSC recruitment in exam answers.
  • Neglecting the Supreme Court’s direction for Civil Services Boards in T.S.R. Subramanian.
  • These traps highlight the nuanced areas where aspirants frequently lose marks.

President's Veto Powers: Absolute, Suspensive and Pocket Veto

Article 111 of the Constitution governs the President's assent to Bills passed by Parliament. The President may give assent, withhold assent (absolute veto), or return a Bill (other than a Money Bill) for reconsideration (suspensive veto). If the Bill is passed again by both Houses with or without amendments, the President must give assent. The Constitution does not prescribe a time limit for the President to act, creating the possibility of a pocket veto — indefinite withholding of assent without formal rejection. In practice, the pocket veto has been exercised rarely; the most cited instance is the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986, which remained pending until the dissolution of the Lok Sabha. The Supreme Court in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) held that the President's power to withhold assent is subject to judicial review only on the ground of mala fides or constitutional violation, not on the merits of the legislation. The 42nd Amendment (1976) briefly made the President's assent obligatory on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the 44th Amendment (1978) restored the pre‑1976 position, reaffirming that the President acts on ministerial advice except in the narrow discretionary windows recognised by the Court.

Absolute Veto

An absolute veto occurs when the President withholds assent and the Bill lapses. This power is theoretically available for any Bill except Money Bills, but constitutional convention and the binding nature of ministerial advice (Article 74) make its unilateral exercise politically untenable. The only post‑independence instance of an absolute veto was President Zail Singh's refusal to assent to the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986, which was effectively a pocket veto because the Bill was not returned for reconsideration.

Suspensive Veto

The suspensive veto is the most frequently used formal power. When the President returns a Bill with a message, Parliament may reconsider it. If both Houses pass it again, the President is constitutionally bound to assent. This mechanism was invoked in 2006 when President A.P.J. Abdul Kalam returned the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Amendment Bill, 2006; Parliament re‑passed it and the President assented.

Pocket Veto and Judicial Scrutiny

Because the Constitution fixes no deadline, a President can keep a Bill pending indefinitely. The Supreme Court has refrained from prescribing a time limit, but in Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020) the Court observed that unreasonable delay in assent may be challenged as a violation of the constitutional scheme. The pocket veto thus remains a dormant but potent instrument, its use constrained more by political accountability than by legal sanction.

Article 143 Advisory Jurisdiction and Executive Reference Practice

Article 143 empowers the President to refer a question of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its opinion. The Court's advisory opinion is not binding on the President or the government, but it carries significant persuasive authority and has shaped executive action on several occasions. The first reference was made in 1951 on the Delhi Laws Act, where the Court upheld the validity of delegated legislation. Subsequent references have addressed politically sensitive issues: the 1998 Presidential Reference on the Ayodhya dispute (the Court declined to answer whether a temple existed prior to the mosque, citing the principle of secularism), the 2002 reference on the Gujarat communal violence (the Court opined on the constitutional duty of the state to protect minorities), and the 2019 reference on the constitutional validity of the abrogation of Article 370 (the Court held that the President's order under Article 370(3) was valid). The advisory jurisdiction is distinct from the Court's appellate and original jurisdictions; it does not create a lis inter partes and cannot be invoked by private parties. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in deciding whether to make a reference, but the Court has insisted that the reference must be genuine and not a device to circumvent judicial review of executive action.

Procedural Safeguards

The Supreme Court has laid down procedural norms: the reference must be specific, the question must be of public importance, and the Court may decline to answer if the matter is sub judice or involves political questions unsuitable for judicial determination. In Special Reference No. 1 of 1998, the Court refused to answer the factual question about the existence of a temple, stating that it fell outside the scope of Article 143. The Court also requires that the reference be accompanied by all relevant facts and documents to enable a meaningful opinion.

Impact on Executive Decision‑Making

Advisory opinions have influenced legislative drafting and policy. After the 2002 Gujarat reference, the government enacted the Communal Violence (Prevention, Control and Rehabilitation of Victims) Bill, 2005 (though it lapsed). The 2019 reference provided constitutional cover for the Presidential Order abrogating Article 370, reinforcing the executive's position in subsequent litigation. While the opinions are not binding, they serve as authoritative interpretations that guide the executive in avoiding future constitutional challenges.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: "Explain the scope and limitations of the President's advisory jurisdiction under Article 143. Illustrate with two instances where the advisory opinion influenced executive action."
Answer: Article 143 allows the President to seek the Supreme Court's opinion on questions of law or fact of public importance. The opinion is advisory, not binding, and the Court may refuse to answer if the question is political, sub judice, or lacks specificity. Scope: (1) Clarifies constitutional doubts before legislative or executive action; (2) Provides authoritative interpretation guiding policy. Limitations: (1) No lis inter partes; (2) Cannot be invoked by private parties; (3) Court may decline on grounds of political question doctrine. Instances: (a) 2002 Gujarat reference — Court's opinion on state's duty to protect minorities influenced the drafting of the Communal Violence Bill, 2005. (b) 2019 Article 370 reference — Court upheld the Presidential Order, legitimising the executive's reorganisation of Jammu & Kashmir and shaping subsequent legislative measures.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for "scope and limitations", structure the answer in two columns: first list the powers (scope), then the constitutional and judicial constraints (limitations). Cite the specific Presidential References by year and subject; examiners reward precise recall of the 1998, 2002 and 2019 references. Avoid conflating Article 143 with Article 131 (original jurisdiction) or Article 32 (writ jurisdiction).

Prime Minister's Office versus Cabinet Secretariat: Institutional Evolution and Power Dynamics

The Prime Minister's Office (PMO) and the Cabinet Secretariat are the two principal nerve centres of executive governance, yet their constitutional status, functional remit, and relative influence have evolved differently. The PMO originated as the Prime Minister's Secretariat in 1947, headed by a Secretary to the Prime Minister. Under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, it expanded dramatically, acquiring the Principal Secretary (often a senior IAS officer), the National Security Advisor, and specialised cells for economic, foreign, and domestic policy. The PMO now functions as the Prime Minister's personal staff, coordinating inter‑ministerial matters, monitoring flagship schemes, and managing political communication. In contrast, the Cabinet Secretariat, established under the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, is the institutional memory of the Cabinet system. Headed by the Cabinet Secretary — the senior‑most civil servant — it prepares the agenda, circulates minutes, ensures follow‑up on Cabinet decisions, and services Cabinet Committees. The Cabinet Secretary also chairs the Civil Services Board and the Committee of Secretaries.

Functional Overlap and Coordination

Both bodies handle inter‑ministerial coordination, but the PMO does so through direct political authority while the Cabinet Secretariat operates through bureaucratic procedure. The PMO's "subject‑matter" cells (e.g., Infrastructure, Social Sector) often bypass the formal Cabinet Committee route, issuing directives to ministries. The Cabinet Secretariat, however, enforces the Transaction of Business Rules, ensuring that decisions are recorded, minuted, and implemented through the established committee hierarchy. In practice, the Principal Secretary and the Cabinet Secretary meet regularly to align the political and administrative tracks. The National Security Council Secretariat, housed in the PMO, exemplifies the PMO's lead on strategic matters, whereas the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) remains the formal decision‑making forum serviced by the Cabinet Secretariat.

Accountability and Transparency

The PMO is not subject to the Right to Information Act in the same manner as the Cabinet Secretariat, leading to debates about opacity. The Cabinet Secretariat's minutes are accessible after a prescribed period, and its role in the Civil Services Board provides a check on arbitrary transfers. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2008) recommended strengthening the Cabinet Secretariat's coordination role and limiting the PMO's direct intervention in routine administrative matters to preserve the collective responsibility of the Cabinet. Nevertheless, the political reality of a strong Prime Minister has kept the PMO as the de facto apex executive office.

Doctrine of Pleasure under Article 310: Scope, Exceptions and Judicial Safeguards

Article 310 establishes the doctrine of pleasure: civil servants of the Union and the States hold office during the pleasure of the President or the Governor, as the case may be. This common‑law principle allows the executive to dismiss a servant without cause, subject to constitutional exceptions. Article 311 provides two substantive safeguards: (1) no dismissal, removal, or reduction in rank without an inquiry in which the servant has a reasonable opportunity to be heard; (2) no penalty on the basis of evidence not disclosed to the servant. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) held that the pleasure doctrine is not absolute and must be exercised in accordance with the principles of natural justice. In State of Bihar v. Abdul Majid (1954), the Court ruled that the pleasure of the President/Governor is not a personal discretion but a constitutional power exercised on ministerial advice.

Judicial Expansion of Protections

Subsequent judgments have read Article 311 expansively. In Khem Chand v. Union of India (1958), the Court held that the inquiry requirement applies even when the penalty is compulsory retirement. The landmark T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013) extended procedural safeguards to transfers and postings, mandating Civil Services Boards and fixed tenures for key posts such as Director General of Police and Chief Secretary. The Court also declared that the doctrine of pleasure cannot be used to circumvent the requirement of a fair inquiry, and that any premature transfer must be recorded with reasons. Despite these rulings, compliance remains uneven; many states have not constituted functional Civil Services Boards, and the Centre's Board operates largely in an advisory capacity.

Exceptions and Special Categories

Certain constitutional offices — the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner, and the Chairman and members of the Union Public Service Commission — are expressly excluded from the pleasure doctrine by Articles 148, 324, and 316 respectively. Their removal requires a process akin to impeachment. Similarly, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are protected under Articles 124 and 217. The doctrine thus applies primarily to the subordinate civil services, where the balance between executive control and servant security continues to be negotiated through litigation and administrative reform.

Civil Services Cadre Management: Allocation, Deputation and the Role of DoPT

The All India Services (AIS) — the Indian Administrative Service, Indian Police Service, and Indian Forest Service — are governed by the All India Services Act, 1951, and the Cadre Allocation Policy, 2017. Each state constitutes a cadre, and officers are allocated to a cadre at the time of recruitment based on merit, preference, and vacancy. The 2017 policy introduced a "zone‑based" allocation to promote national integration, dividing states into five zones and requiring officers to serve in at least one cadre outside their home zone. Deputation to the Centre is regulated by the Central Deputation Rules; officers may be deputed for a maximum of seven years (extendable to ten in exceptional cases) and must return to their parent cadre thereafter. The Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT) is the nodal agency for cadre management, deputation, empanelment for senior posts, and disciplinary matters. DoPT maintains the Executive Record (ER) sheets, processes vigilance clearance, and convenes the Civil Services Board for the Centre.

Empanelment and Senior Appointments

For appointments at the level of Joint Secretary and above, DoPT prepares panels of eligible officers based on Annual Confidential Reports (now Performance Appraisal Reports), vigilance clearance, and cadre clearance. The Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC), chaired by the Prime Minister, makes the final selection. Lateral entry, introduced in 2018, allows domain experts from outside the service to be appointed at Joint Secretary and Director levels on contract, bypassing the regular empanelment route. This has created a parallel track that coexists with the traditional cadre‑based progression.

Challenges in Cadre Management

Cadre imbalances persist: some states face chronic shortages while others have surplus officers. The "cooling‑off" period after central deputation is often waived, leading to prolonged absence from the parent cadre. The Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian directed that deputation should not exceed the prescribed limit without compelling reasons, yet waivers remain common. DoPT's dual role as cadre controller and policy maker sometimes creates conflicts of interest, prompting calls for an independent Civil Services Authority. The Mission Karmayogi framework (2020) seeks to align cadre management with competency‑based role mapping, but integration with the existing allocation and deputation architecture is still evolving.

  • Assuming Article 143 advisory opinions are binding on the executive; they are persuasive only.
  • Treating the PMO and Cabinet Secretariat as interchangeable; the PMO is a political office, the Cabinet Secretariat a constitutional‑administrative body.
  • Overlooking that Article 311 protections apply only after a servant has been appointed to a substantive post, not during probation.
  • Believing lateral entrants become part of the regular AIS cadre; they serve on contract and do not acquire cadre seniority.

President's Emergency Powers: National Emergency, President's Rule and Financial Emergency

The Constitution vests the President with three distinct emergency powers under Articles 352, 356 and 360. Each operates under a different constitutional logic, procedural safeguard and judicial review standard. Article 352 empowers the President to proclaim a National Emergency when the security of India or any part thereof is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) replaced the term “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion” and required the proclamation to be laid before each House of Parliament within one month, failing which it ceases to operate. The proclamation must be approved by a special majority of both Houses and can continue for six months at a time, subject to periodic parliamentary ratification. During a National Emergency, the federal distribution of legislative powers is suspended: Parliament may legislate on State List subjects (Article 250) and the President may issue directions to States (Article 353). Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended (Article 358) while other rights may be suspended by a separate Presidential order under Article 359, subject to the proviso that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended. The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) held that the power to proclaim emergency is not immune from judicial review; the Court may examine whether the satisfaction of the President is based on relevant material and is not mala fide.

Article 356, commonly called President’s Rule, allows the President to assume the functions of a State government when he is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months and can remain in force for a maximum of three years, with parliamentary approval every six months. The 44th Amendment introduced the requirement that the proclamation be based on a report from the Governor or otherwise, and that the President’s satisfaction is subject to judicial scrutiny. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) a nine‑judge bench laid down that the President’s satisfaction is not absolute; the Court can review the material on which the satisfaction is based, and the proclamation is liable to be struck down if it is based on extraneous or irrelevant grounds. The judgment also mandated a floor test in the State Assembly within a reasonable time, preferably 48 hours, before the imposition of President’s Rule. Subsequent cases such as Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) reinforced that the dissolution of the Assembly cannot be a routine sequel to the proclamation.

Article 360 provides for a Financial Emergency when the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India or any part thereof is threatened. The proclamation must be laid before Parliament and ceases to operate after two months unless approved by both Houses. During a Financial Emergency, the President may direct States to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the State, and reserve all Money Bills for Presidential consideration. To date, a Financial Emergency has never been proclaimed. The provision remains a constitutional safeguard rather than an operational tool, and its justiciability has not been tested in the Supreme Court.

The President's Pardoning Power under Article 72: Constitutional Scheme, Judicial Standards and Comparative Perspective

Article 72 confers on the President the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the Union extends. The power extends to court‑martial sentences and death sentences. The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), making the exercise of this power effectively a executive decision. However, the Supreme Court has held that the power is not a mere act of grace but a constitutional duty subject to judicial review on limited grounds. In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980) the Court ruled that the President’s order under Article 72 is amenable to judicial review for arbitrariness, mala fides, or failure to consider relevant material. The Court in Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1988) clarified that while the President is bound by ministerial advice, the advice itself must be based on a proper consideration of the case; a blanket refusal without reasons may be set aside. In Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006) the Court enumerated the grounds for judicial interference: (i) the order is passed without application of mind; (ii) the order is mala fide; (iii) the order is based on wholly irrelevant considerations; (iv) the order suffers from arbitrariness. The Court also held that the President must consider the gravity of the offence, the conduct of the convict, and the impact on society.

Comparatively, the United States President enjoys an almost plenary pardon power under Article II, Section 2 of the US Constitution, which is not subject to congressional or judicial review, except for impeachment. The Indian model, by contrast, embeds the power within a parliamentary executive, making it accountable to the legislature through the Council of Ministers and reviewable by the judiciary for constitutional propriety. The 44th Amendment did not alter Article 72, but the growing jurisprudence reflects a trend towards ensuring that the pardoning power is exercised transparently, with reasoned orders, especially in death‑penalty cases where the Supreme Court has repeatedly urged the executive to apply its mind afresh after judicial confirmation of the sentence.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: “Explain the judicial standards evolved by the Supreme Court for reviewing the exercise of the President’s pardoning power under Article 72.”
A high‑scoring answer should (1) state that Article 72 power is exercised on ministerial advice under Article 74; (2) cite Maru Ram (1980) establishing that the order is subject to judicial review for arbitrariness and mala fides; (3) cite Kehar Singh (1988) holding that the advice must reflect due consideration; (4) cite Epuru Sudhakar (2006) listing the four specific grounds — non‑application of mind, mala fides, irrelevant considerations, arbitrariness; (5) note that the Court does not sit in appeal over the merits but ensures constitutional propriety; (6) conclude that the trend is towards reasoned orders, especially in capital cases, and that the power is not an unchecked prerogative.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “judicial standards” or “grounds of review”, structure the answer chronologically by landmark judgments. Use the case name, year, and the specific principle laid down. This demonstrates both knowledge of precedent and the ability to synthesise evolving doctrine — a hallmark of top‑tier answers.

Prime Minister in Coalition and Minority Governments: Confidence Motions, Floor Tests and the President’s Role

The parliamentary system envisages that the Prime Minister commands the confidence of the Lok Sabha. In a single‑party majority this is straightforward; in coalition or minority scenarios the dynamics become constitutionally intricate. The President’s role under Article 75(1) — appointing the Prime Minister — is guided by the convention that the leader of the largest party or pre‑poll alliance is invited to form the government. When no party secures a clear majority, the President may exercise discretion to explore alternatives, but this discretion is bounded by the principle that the appointee must be able to prove majority on the floor of the House. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai (1994) held that the Governor (and by analogy the President) cannot dismiss a ministry without a floor test, and that a floor test must be ordered within a reasonable period, preferably 48 hours, after a claim of loss of majority.

Historical instances illustrate the interplay. In 1996, Atal Bihari Vajpayee was invited as the single largest party leader but resigned after 13 days when he could not muster a majority. In 1998, Vajpayee again formed a coalition (National Democratic Alliance) and survived a confidence vote. In 2004, the Congress‑led United Progressive Alliance secured a majority with external support, and Manmohan Singh was appointed after the President verified the support letters. In 2019, the BJP secured a clear majority, but the President’s invitation followed the same constitutional script. The 10th Schedule (Anti‑Defection Law) further constrains coalition stability: a member disqualified under the Schedule loses membership, affecting the arithmetic for confidence motions. The Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) upheld the Speaker’s power to decide disqualification, subject to judicial review, which indirectly influences the viability of minority governments.

When a government loses a confidence motion, the President may either invite the opposition to form an alternative government or dissolve the Lok Sabha and call fresh elections. The discretion to dissolve is not unfettered; the President must be satisfied that no alternative government can be formed. In practice, the President relies on the advice of the outgoing Prime Minister, but the Supreme Court in Rameshwar Prasad (2006) cautioned against premature dissolution when a viable alternative exists. The President’s address to Parliament (Article 87) and the subsequent Motion of Thanks also serve as a barometer of the government’s legislative support. In minority situations, the Prime Minister often negotiates issue‑based support, and the Cabinet’s collective responsibility (Article 75(3)) obliges all ministers to defend government policy, even if individual parties in the coalition dissent publicly.

Civil Services Performance Management: Mission Karmayogi, 360‑Degree Appraisal and the Shift from Seniority to Competency

The traditional civil‑service appraisal in India relied on the Annual Confidential Report (ACR), a top‑down, seniority‑linked evaluation that emphasized length of service over demonstrated competence. Recognising the need for a dynamic, competency‑driven bureaucracy, the Government launched Mission Karmayogi in September 2020 as a National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building. The mission is anchored by the Capacity Building Commission (CBC), constituted in April 2021, and operates through the Integrated Government Online Training (iGOT) Karmayogi platform, which hosts over 800 courses across functional, behavioural and domain competencies.

A pivotal reform under Mission Karmayogi is the introduction of a 360‑degree Performance Appraisal Report (PAR) for officers at the level of Under Secretary and above. The new PAR replaces the ACR and incorporates feedback from superiors, peers, subordinates and, where relevant, external stakeholders. The assessment matrix covers six competency clusters: (i) strategic thinking, (ii) leadership and team building, (iii) result orientation, (iv) citizen‑centricity, (v) integrity and ethics, and (vi) digital literacy. Each cluster is measured on a five‑point scale with defined behavioural indicators. The CBC has mandated that the PAR be completed electronically on iGOT, ensuring time‑stamped submissions and audit trails.

The shift from seniority to competency also reshapes empanelment for senior posts (Joint Secretary and above). The Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC) now considers the 360‑degree PAR scores alongside vigilance clearance and cadre clearance. Lateral entry, introduced in 2018, runs parallel: domain experts are appointed on contract at Joint Secretary and Director levels without entering the regular cadre. Their performance is evaluated through the same competency framework, but they do not acquire cadre seniority. The Mission also envisages a “role‑based” deployment model where officers are matched to positions based on competency profiles rather than merely on batch seniority.

Challenges remain. The cultural transition from a confidential, hierarchical appraisal to an open, multi‑source feedback system meets resistance in many cadres. State governments have been uneven in adopting the 360‑degree PAR; several have not yet constituted functional State Civil Services Boards as directed by the Supreme Court in T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India (2013). Data integrity on iGOT, interoperability with existing Human Resource Management Systems, and the capacity of evaluators to apply behavioural indicators consistently are operational hurdles. The CBC’s annual “Capacity Building Plan” aims to address these through calibrated training of evaluators, periodic audits of PAR quality, and integration with the “Karmayogi Bharat” digital stack for real‑time competency mapping.

  • Confusing the President’s emergency powers under Article 352 with the Governor’s powers under Article 356; the President proclaims President’s Rule on the Governor’s report, but the constitutional provisions and judicial standards differ.
  • Assuming the President’s pardoning power under Article 72 is absolute; it is exercised on ministerial advice and subject to judicial review for arbitrariness, mala fides and non‑application of mind.
  • Treating a floor test as a mere political formality; the Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai mandated a time‑bound floor test and held that the President/Governor cannot dismiss a ministry without it.
  • Believing that lateral entrants under the 2018 scheme become part of the regular All India Services cadre; they serve on contract and do not acquire cadre seniority.
  • Overlooking that the 360‑degree PAR under Mission Karmayogi applies only from Under Secretary level upward; lower‑level officers continue under the older ACR system.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the President’s discretion to dissolve the Lok Sabha and the advice of the Council of Ministers; the President may refuse dissolution if a viable alternative government exists.
  • Ignoring the role of the Capacity Building Commission in setting competency standards; the CBC, not DoPT, defines the competency framework for the 360‑degree appraisal.
