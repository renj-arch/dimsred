The United Nations and the Quest for Reform

1.1 The UN Charter and the Security Council

The United Nations was established in 1945 with the signing of the Charter in San Francisco, creating a framework for collective security, human rights, and socioeconomic development. At the heart of the Charter lies the Security Council, endowed with primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security. Its five permanent members — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States — hold veto power, a privilege that reflects the geopolitical realities of the immediate post‑war era but has increasingly been viewed as an obstacle to decisive action in contemporary crises. The veto has been exercised to block resolutions on conflicts ranging from the Syrian civil war to the situation in Ukraine, prompting widespread calls for reform that would either expand permanent membership, limit the use of the veto, or both.

1.2 Major Reform Proposals and Groupings

Several coalitions have advanced distinct reform blueprints. The G4 — comprising Brazil, Germany, India, and Japan — advocates for new permanent seats for each of its members plus two African states, arguing that the Council must reflect the economic and demographic weight of the twenty‑first century. The Uniting for Consensus group, led by Italy, Pakistan, Mexico, and others, opposes new permanent seats and instead favours a longer‑term expansion of non‑permanent membership with longer terms. The African Group, representing the continent’s 54 members, demands at least two permanent seats with veto power and five non‑permanent seats, citing historical under‑representation. These competing visions have stalled any amendment to the Charter, which requires a two‑thirds majority in the General Assembly and ratification by all five permanent members.

1.3 The Role of the General Assembly and the “Uniting for Peace” Mechanism

When the Security Council is deadlocked, the General Assembly can act under the “Uniting for Peace” resolution (Resolution 377 (V) of 1950) to recommend collective measures, including the use of force. This mechanism has been invoked rarely, most notably during the Suez Crisis of 1956 and the Korean War, but its relevance has grown as vetoes become more frequent. The Assembly’s ability to convene emergency special sessions provides a diplomatic outlet, yet its recommendations are non‑binding, limiting practical impact. The tension between the Council’s binding authority and the Assembly’s moral authority underscores the broader challenge of aligning institutional design with the principle of sovereign equality.

1.4 Emerging Issues: Climate Security, Pandemics, and Cyber Threats

New transnational threats have exposed gaps in the UN’s mandate. Climate change is increasingly framed as a security issue, prompting the Security Council to hold open debates on the nexus between environmental degradation and conflict, though no binding resolution has emerged. The COVID‑19 pandemic highlighted the need for a coordinated global health architecture, leading to calls for a strengthened World Health Organization and a pandemic treaty under UN auspices. Cybersecurity, meanwhile, lacks a universally accepted normative framework; the UN Group of Governmental Experts has produced voluntary norms, but enforcement remains elusive. These issues illustrate why reform debates now extend beyond membership to the very scope of the UN’s functional competencies.

The World Trade Organization and the Multilateral Trading System

2.1 From GATT to the WTO: Institutional Evolution

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) governed world trade from 1948 until the Marrakesh Agreement established the World Trade Organization on 1 January 1995. Unlike GATT, which was a provisional treaty, the WTO possesses a permanent secretariat, a comprehensive dispute settlement mechanism, and a broader mandate covering services (GATS), intellectual property (TRIPS), and trade‑related investment measures (TRIMS). The shift from a purely tariff‑focused regime to a rules‑based system encompassing non‑tariff barriers reflected the deepening of global value chains and the need for enforceable commitments.

2.2 The Dispute Settlement Mechanism and Its Current Crisis

The WTO’s Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) operates through panels and an Appellate Body, delivering binding rulings that members are obliged to implement. Since 2019, the Appellate Body has been unable to function because the United States blocked the appointment of new members, arguing that the body overreached its mandate and delayed proceedings. This paralysis has forced members to rely on ad hoc arbitration under Article 25 of the Dispute Settlement Understanding or to resolve disputes bilaterally, eroding the predictability that underpins the multilateral trading order. The crisis has spurred proposals for reform, including a “two‑track” system that separates appeals on legal interpretation from factual review, but consensus remains elusive.

2.3 Negotiating Deadlocks: Doha, Fisheries Subsidies, and E‑Commerce

The Doha Development Agenda, launched in 2001, aimed to place developing‑country concerns at the centre of negotiations but stalled over agriculture, non‑agricultural market access, and services. A modest breakthrough came in 2022 with the Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies, which prohibits subsidies that contribute to illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing and to overfished stocks, while allowing special and differential treatment for developing nations. Parallel plurilateral talks on e‑commerce, investment facilitation, and services domestic regulation have progressed among willing members, raising questions about the future of the single‑undertaking principle that requires all members to accept the entire package.

2.4 The WTO and the Rules‑Based Order

The WTO embodies the rules‑based order by providing a legal framework that constrains unilateral protectionism. Its Most‑Favoured‑Nation and National Treatment principles ensure non‑discrimination, while transparency obligations compel members to notify trade measures. However, the rise of mega‑regional agreements such as the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans‑Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) creates overlapping rule sets that can both complement and fragment the multilateral system. The challenge for the WTO is to remain the central forum for trade liberalisation while accommodating diverse development levels and emerging issues like digital trade and climate‑related measures.

Worked Example: Assessing the Impact of the WTO Appellate Body Crisis
A candidate is asked: “Explain how the non‑functioning of the WTO Appellate Body since 2019 affects the credibility of the multilateral trading system and suggest two possible reform pathways.”
The Appellate Body served as the final arbiter of legal interpretation, ensuring uniformity and predictability in dispute outcomes. Its paralysis means that panel reports cannot be appealed, leaving losing parties without a definitive legal remedy and encouraging unilateral retaliation. This undermines the rule‑of‑law character of the WTO and incentivises powerful members to resolve disputes outside the system. Two reform pathways are frequently discussed: (1) a “two‑track” appellate process that separates appeals on points of law from factual review, limiting the Appellate Body’s mandate to legal interpretation; (2) a permanent standing appellate mechanism with fixed terms and a transparent selection process, coupled with a clear procedural timeline to address the United States’ concerns about overreach and delay. Both options aim to restore a functioning appeal tier while preserving the consensus‑based nature of the organization.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “reform pathways” for an international institution, structure the answer by first identifying the specific institutional defect, then presenting at least two distinct proposals that have been debated in official documents or by major member groups, and finally evaluating the political feasibility of each. This demonstrates both knowledge of the current discourse and analytical depth.

The International Financial Institutions: IMF and World Bank

3.1 Origins and Mandate Differentiation

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank) were created at the Bretton Woods Conference in 1944. The IMF’s primary purpose is to ensure the stability of the international monetary system by providing short‑to‑medium‑term financial assistance to members facing balance‑of‑payments difficulties, overseeing exchange‑rate policies, and offering technical assistance. The World Bank, by contrast, focuses on long‑term development financing, poverty reduction, and reconstruction, channeling funds through the International Development Association (IDA) for the poorest countries and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) for middle‑income borrowers.

3.2 Governance, Quota, and Voting Power

Both institutions operate on a weighted voting system where financial contributions determine voting shares. The IMF’s quota formula blends GDP, openness, economic variability, and international reserves; the United States holds roughly 16.5 % of total votes, giving it an effective veto over major decisions requiring an 85 % supermajority. The World Bank’s shareholding mirrors the IMF’s but includes a larger role for the Executive Directors representing groups of countries. Periodic quota reviews — most recently the 15th General Review of Quotas completed in 2020 — have modestly increased the voice of emerging economies, yet the governance gap remains a source of legitimacy criticism.

3.3 Lending Instruments, Conditionality, and Critiques

The IMF deploys Stand‑By Arrangements, Extended Fund Facility, and the newer Flexible Credit Line and Precautionary and Liquidity Line, each calibrated to the severity of the crisis and the strength of the member’s policies. Conditionality — macroeconomic adjustment measures such as fiscal consolidation, monetary tightening, and structural reforms — is attached to most programmes. The World Bank offers investment project financing, development policy financing, and Program‑for‑Results, linking disbursements to measurable outcomes. Critics argue that conditionality can undermine domestic policy space, exacerbate social inequality, and impose a one‑size‑fits‑all template, while supporters contend that it safeguards repayment prospects and promotes macroeconomic discipline.

3.4 Recent Initiatives: Climate Finance, Debt Sustainability, and Pandemic Response

In response to the COVID‑19 pandemic, the IMF created the Catastrophe Containment and Relief Trust and expanded the Rapid Credit Facility, providing emergency financing to over 80 low‑income countries. The World Bank launched the COVID‑19 Fast‑Track Facility and increased IDA replenishment to support health systems and social protection. Both institutions have pledged to align financial flows with the Paris Agreement, with the IMF introducing the Resilience and Sustainability Trust to help vulnerable members address climate risks, and the World Bank committing to a 35 % climate‑finance target for its portfolio by 2025. The Common Framework for Debt Treatments, co‑chaired by the IMF and the World Bank, seeks to coordinate creditor action for low‑income countries facing unsustainable debt, though participation by private creditors remains limited.

Emerging Strategic Groupings: QUAD, BRICS, and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation

4.1 The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (QUAD)

The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue, revived in 2017 after a decade of dormancy, brings together Australia, India, Japan, and the United States. Its stated purpose is to promote a free, open, and inclusive Indo‑Pacific, focusing on maritime security, resilient supply chains, critical technologies, and climate resilience. Unlike a formal alliance, the QUAD operates through leader‑level summits, ministerial meetings, and working groups, avoiding a collective defence clause. The grouping’s significance lies in its ability to coordinate democratic powers across the Indian and Pacific Oceans, offering an alternative to unilateral assertiveness while respecting ASEAN centrality.

4.2 BRICS: From Economic Acronym to Political Platform

BRICS — Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa — originated as an investment‑bank analyst’s acronym in 2001 but evolved into a summit‑level forum by 2009. The group established the New Development Bank (NDB) in 2015, headquartered in Shanghai, to finance infrastructure and sustainable development projects in emerging economies. BRICS also created the Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA) as a liquidity backstop. Politically, the grouping advocates for reform of global governance institutions, a multipolar world order, and greater representation of the Global South. Divergent strategic interests — especially between India and China — limit the depth of coordination, yet the platform remains a symbol of South‑South cooperation.

4.3 The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)

Founded in 2001 by China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan, the SCO expanded in 2017 to include India and Pakistan, and in 2023 admitted Iran as a full member. Its charter emphasises mutual trust, regional security, counter‑terrorism (through the Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure), and economic cooperation. The SCO’s decision‑making relies on consensus, reflecting the diverse political systems of its members. While security cooperation — especially against the “three evils” of terrorism, separatism, and extremism — has been the most tangible outcome, economic integration remains modest compared with the Eurasian Economic Union or the Belt and Road Initiative.

4.4 Comparative Dynamics and Overlaps

The QUAD, BRICS, and SCO illustrate three distinct logics of grouping: a values‑based maritime coalition, a development‑oriented South‑South bloc, and a Eurasian security‑centric organisation. Overlaps exist — India participates in both the QUAD and the SCO, while China and Russia are core SCO members and also engage with BRICS. These overlapping memberships create a web of minilateralism that can both complement and complicate multilateral efforts. For instance, the QUAD’s focus on technology standards may intersect with BRICS’ push for alternative digital governance frameworks, while the SCO’s counter‑terrorism agenda aligns with broader UN efforts but operates within a regional mandate. Understanding these dynamics is essential for analysing the evolving architecture of global governance.

The G20 as a Premier Forum for Global Economic Governance

5.1 Evolution from Finance Ministers to Leaders’ Summit

The Group of Twenty (G20) was conceived in 1999 as a meeting of finance ministers and central bank governors from major advanced and emerging economies to discuss international financial stability. The 2008 global financial crisis elevated the forum to a leaders’ summit, recognising that coordinated macroeconomic policy required political authority. The G20 now represents around 85 % of global GDP, 75 % of international trade, and two‑thirds of the world’s population, making it the most representative body for economic governance.

5.2 Core Agenda: Strong, Sustainable, and Balanced Growth

The G20’s standing agenda revolves around the “Framework for Strong, Sustainable, and Balanced Growth,” which commits members to mutually assessed policy commitments. Key pillars include fiscal consolidation where needed, structural reforms to boost productivity, financial regulation (through the Financial Stability Board), and inclusive growth. The forum also addresses cross‑cutting issues such as tax base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS), anti‑corruption, and the digital economy. The 2021 Rome Summit produced a historic agreement on a global minimum corporate tax of 15 %, illustrating the G20’s capacity to generate binding normative outcomes when political will aligns.

5.3 Engagement Groups and the Role of the Troika

Beyond the official track, the G20 institutionalises civil society input through engagement groups — Business20 (B20), Civil20 (C20), Labour20 (L20), Think20 (T20), Women20 (W20), Youth20 (Y20), and others. Their recommendations feed into the sherpa process that prepares the leaders’ communiqué. The presidency rotates annually, supported by a troika of the current, previous, and incoming presidencies to ensure continuity. India’s 2023 presidency, for example, emphasised “Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam” (the world is one family), prioritising climate finance, digital public infrastructure, and reformed multilateralism.

5.4 Limitations and the Question of Legitimacy

Despite its economic weight, the G20 lacks a permanent secretariat, a charter, and legal personality; its decisions are non‑binding and rely on peer pressure for implementation. The exclusion of the majority of UN member states raises legitimacy concerns, prompting the creation of the “G20+” outreach to regional organisations such as the African Union, ASEAN, and the European Union. The forum’s effectiveness hinges on the willingness of major powers — especially the United States and China — to compromise, a condition that has become more uncertain amid strategic rivalry.

The Rules‑Based International Order and Contemporary Challenges

6.1 Foundations: Sovereignty, Non‑Intervention, and International Law

The rules‑based order rests on the post‑1945 settlement that enshrined sovereign equality, the prohibition of the use of force (Article 2(4) of the UN Charter), and the peaceful settlement of disputes. International law — treaties, customary law, and general principles — provides the normative substrate, while institutions such as the International Court of Justice, the International Criminal Court, and specialised agencies operationalise these rules. The order has facilitated decolonisation, the expansion of human rights norms, and the liberalisation of trade, creating a framework within which states pursue interests without resorting to coercion.

6.2 Stressors: Great‑Power Competition, Norm Contestation, and Institutional Paralysis

Three interlinked stressors now test the order. First, great‑power competition — particularly between the United States and China — has turned strategic domains such as technology, standards, and supply chains into arenas of rivalry, prompting the formation of competing minilateral clubs. Second, norm contestation manifests in divergent interpretations of sovereignty (e.g., “responsibility to protect” vs. “non‑interference”), human rights universality, and the legitimacy of unilateral sanctions. Third, institutional paralysis — exemplified by the WTO Appellate Body crisis, the UN Security Council veto deadlock, and the IMF quota stalemate — erodes the capacity of existing bodies to adapt rules to new realities such as cyber operations, outer‑space activities, and climate‑induced migration.

6.3 Pathways to Renewal: Inclusive Multilateralism, Reform Coalitions, and Adaptive Governance

Renewing the rules‑based order requires both procedural and substantive steps. Inclusive multilateralism demands that the voices of the Global South be integrated into norm‑setting, not merely consulted after decisions are made; the G20’s outreach to the African Union and the UN’s “Our Common Agenda” report exemplify this direction. Reform coalitions — such as the “Group of 77 and China” pushing for Security Council expansion, or the “Ottawa Group” of WTO members advancing dispute‑settlement reform — demonstrate that like‑minded states can drive incremental change even without universal consensus. Adaptive governance entails designing flexible mechanisms — sunset clauses, periodic review conferences, and modular treaty architectures — that allow rules to evolve with technological and geopolitical shifts while preserving core principles. The interplay of these pathways will determine whether the international system can manage competition without descending into fragmentation.

The study of international institutions, groupings, and the rules‑based order reveals a dynamic landscape where legal frameworks, power politics, and normative aspirations continuously interact, shaping the possibilities for collective action in an increasingly interconnected world.

UN Peacekeeping: Mandate Design, Financing, and Performance Metrics

UN peacekeeping operations are authorised by the Security Council under Chapter VI or VII of the Charter. Since 1948, over 70 missions have been deployed, with the current portfolio comprising 12 active missions and a uniformed personnel ceiling of roughly 87,000 troops, police, and military observers. Mandates are classified into three generations: traditional ceasefire monitoring (e.g., UNTSO), multidimensional peacekeeping (e.g., MINUSMA in Mali), and protection‑of‑civilians‑centric missions (e.g., MONUSCO in the DRC). Each mandate specifies tasks such as disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration (DDR), security sector reform (SSR), electoral assistance, and human rights monitoring.

Financing Architecture

The assessed contribution scale, revised every three years by the General Assembly, apportions costs among member states based on capacity to pay. For the 2024‑2026 scale, the top five contributors — United States (27.89 %), China (15.21 %), Japan (8.56 %), Germany (6.09 %), and the United Kingdom (5.79 %) — together cover more than 63 % of the budget. The approved budget for the 2023‑2024 fiscal year stands at US$6.38 billion. Troop‑contributing countries (TCCs) receive a standard reimbursement of US$1,428 per soldier per month, plus contingent‑owned equipment (COE) rates. Reimbursement delays, often exceeding six months, create cash‑flow constraints for TCCs, especially those from the Global South.

Performance Evaluation and Accountability

The Department of Peace Operations (DPO) introduced the Comprehensive Planning and Performance Assessment System (CPAS) in 2020 to measure mission outputs against mandated benchmarks. CPAS uses a traffic‑light rating (green, amber, red) across pillars such as protection of civilians, rule of law, and mission support. Independent evaluations by the Office of Internal Oversight Services (OIOS) and the High‑Level Independent Panel on Peace Operations (HIPPO, 2015) have highlighted systemic gaps: mandate‑resource mismatches, inadequate strategic guidance, and limited accountability for misconduct. The 2023 “Action for Peacekeeping+” (A4P+) initiative seeks to address these through clearer exit strategies, stronger triangular cooperation (UN‑TCC‑host state), and enhanced conduct‑and‑discipline mechanisms, including the 2022 Uniformed Personnel Conduct and Discipline Framework.

WTO Dispute Settlement Reform: The MPIA and the Future of Appellate Review

The paralysis of the WTO Appellate Body since December 2019 — caused by the United States blocking new appointments — left a vacuum in the two‑tier dispute settlement system. In response, 52 WTO members (including the EU, China, Brazil, and Canada) launched the Multi‑Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA) in April 2020, operationalising Article 25 of the Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU) for arbitration. The MPIA replicates Appellate Body procedures: a pool of ten arbitrators (five per party), 90‑day appeal deadlines, and reasoned awards that are final and binding on participating members. As of March 2025, the MPIA has been invoked in 14 appeals, covering disputes on steel safeguards, agricultural subsidies, and digital services taxes.

Key Design Features and Divergences

Unlike the standing Appellate Body, MPIA arbitrators are selected ad hoc for each appeal, reducing institutional continuity but increasing flexibility. The arrangement does not create precedent in the formal stare decisis sense; however, panels and subsequent arbitrators routinely cite prior MPIA awards, generating a de facto jurisprudence. Non‑participating members (notably the United States) are not bound by MPIA awards, creating a fragmented dispute settlement landscape where parallel proceedings can yield contradictory outcomes. The MPIA also lacks a formal compliance surveillance mechanism; implementation relies on the standard DSU Article 21.5 compliance panel process.

Prospects for a Permanent Solution

Negotiations at the 13th Ministerial Conference (MC13, Abu Dhabi, February 2024) focused on three reform tracks: (1) restoring a standing appellate body with modified appointment rules (e.g., qualified majority voting), (2) expanding the MPIA into a permanent “Appeal Arbitration Mechanism” open to all members, and (3) introducing a “single‑tier” model where panel reports become final unless appealed to a new body. The United States insists on retaining a veto over appellate appointments and on limiting the scope of appellate review to legal errors, not factual re‑examination. The EU and developing members advocate for automaticity and binding precedent. A compromise “hybrid” model — a standing pool of appellate arbitrators selected by consensus, with a limited mandate to review legal interpretations — remains the most discussed landing zone for MC14.

Applied Scenario
A developing country imposes a safeguard measure on imported solar panels. The exporting member challenges it at the WTO. The panel finds the measure inconsistent with the Agreement on Safeguards. The exporting member appeals. The United States, not an MPIA participant, blocks the Appellate Body. Explain the procedural options available to the parties and the legal effect of any resulting decision.
Because the United States is not an MPIA participant, the Appellate Body cannot be constituted. The exporting member can invoke the MPIA if both parties are MPIA participants; the safeguard‑imposing developing country must also be an MPIA participant for the appeal to proceed under the arrangement. If both are participants, the appeal is heard by MPIA arbitrators, and the award is final and binding on them. If the respondent is not an MPIA participant, the panel report cannot be appealed; it becomes final and binding under DSU Article 16.4 after 60 days unless the DSB decides by consensus not to adopt it. In practice, the complainant may seek a compliance panel under Article 21.5 if the respondent fails to implement. The legal effect differs: an MPIA award creates binding obligations only between MPIA parties, whereas an adopted panel report binds all WTO members.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the current status of the WTO appellate mechanism, always distinguish three layers: (1) the defunct standing Appellate Body, (2) the MPIA as an interim, opt‑in arbitration arrangement, and (3) the ongoing reform negotiations. Cite the number of MPIA participants (52 as of 2025) and the key procedural difference — ad hoc arbitrators versus permanent members. This three‑layer answer scores higher than a generic “Appellate Body is not working” statement.

IMF Surveillance under Article IV: Methodology, Conditionality, and the 2021 SDR Allocation

Article IV of the IMF Articles of Agreement mandates bilateral surveillance — annual consultations with each member — and multilateral surveillance through the World Economic Outlook (WEO) and the Global Financial Stability Report (GFSR). The 2012 Integrated Surveillance Decision (ISD) shifted the focus from exchange‑rate policies to a broader “spillover” framework, requiring the Fund to assess how a member’s policies affect global stability. The 2021 Review of the Financial Sector Assessment Program (FSAP) expanded coverage to fintech, cyber risk, and climate‑related financial exposures.

Analytical Toolkit

Staff reports employ the External Balance Assessment (EBA) methodology to evaluate current‑account gaps and real exchange‑rate misalignments, the Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) framework for public and external debt, and the Fiscal Monitor’s “fiscal space” metrics. Since 2020, the Climate Change Indicators Dashboard integrates transition‑risk and physical‑risk indicators into Article IV reports for selected economies. The 2023 Guidance Note on “Macro‑Critical Structural Reforms” formalises the inclusion of labour‑market, governance, and digital‑infrastructure reforms as surveillance priorities.

Conditionality in Lending vs. Surveillance

Surveillance carries no formal conditionality; however, the “policy advice” in staff reports often pre‑figures conditionality in subsequent lending arrangements (Stand‑By Arrangements, Extended Fund Facility, or the new Resilience and Sustainability Facility). The 2021 General Allocation of Special Drawing Rights (SDR 456 billion, ≈US$650 billion) — the largest in IMF history — was distributed according to quota shares, giving advanced economies ≈US$400 billion and low‑income countries ≈US$21 billion. The G20’s “rechanneling” pledge aims to direct US$100 billion of advanced‑economy SDRs to vulnerable countries via the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT) and the Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST). As of December 2024, pledges total US$87 billion, with operationalisation pending domestic legislative approvals in several major economies.

Critiques and Reform Proposals

Critics argue that surveillance remains asymmetric: advanced economies receive lighter scrutiny, while emerging markets face prescriptive policy recommendations. The Independent Evaluation Office (IEO) 2022 report on “Surveillance in the Post‑Pandemic Era” recommended (i) a mandatory “spillover report” for systemically important economies, (ii) a transparent “surveillance scorecard” published alongside staff reports, and (iii) stronger integration of inequality and gender‑gap indicators. The 2024 IMF Executive Board discussion on “Surveillance Priorities for the 2020s” endorsed a pilot “Spillover Note” for the United States, China, the Euro Area, and Japan, to be published alongside the 2025 WEO.

BRICS Expansion, the New Development Bank, and Local Currency Settlement Initiatives

The 15th BRICS Summit in Johannesburg (August 2023) admitted six new members — Argentina (later declined), Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates — effective 1 January 2024, expanding the bloc to eleven. The expanded BRICS represents ≈45 % of global population and ≈36 % of world GDP (PPP). The New Development Bank (NDB), headquartered in Shanghai with an initial authorised capital of US$100 billion (subscribed capital US$50 billion), has approved 96 projects worth US$33 billion as of September 2024, focusing on clean energy, transport, and digital infrastructure. NDB’s lending terms are benchmarked to market rates but with longer maturities (up to 25 years) and no policy conditionality akin to IMF programmes.

Local Currency Settlement and the Contingent Reserve Arrangement

The BRICS Local Currency Settlement Framework, operationalised through the BRICS Pay platform pilot (2023‑2024), enables trade invoicing and settlement in member currencies, reducing reliance on the US dollar. The Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA), a US$100 billion liquidity pool, allows members to draw up to 30 % of their quota without IMF‑style conditionality; the remaining 70 % requires a linked IMF programme. As of 2024, no member has drawn on the CRA, but the facility serves as a signalling device for financial resilience.

Governance and Decision‑Making

NDB operates on a one‑country‑one‑vote principle for strategic decisions, while financial decisions require a super‑majority (85 % of voting power). The five founding members each hold 20 % of voting shares; new members receive shares proportionate to capital subscriptions. The CRA’s voting structure mirrors IMF quota shares, giving China ≈41 % of votes, India ≈18 %, Russia ≈18 %, Brazil ≈12 %, and South Africa ≈11 %. Expansion dilutes founding‑member dominance only if new members subscribe capital; the 2024 capital increase of US$10 billion allocated 60 % to new entrants, modestly rebalancing voting power.

The Financial Stability Board, G20 Coordination, and Macroprudential Policy Convergence

The Financial Stability Board (FSB), established in 2009 as the successor to the Financial Stability Forum, coordinates national financial authorities and international standard‑setting bodies (BCBS, IOSCO, IAIS) to promote global financial stability. The FSB’s mandate is delivered through the G20 Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors track; its plenary includes 24 member jurisdictions, four international organisations, and six regional consultative groups. The FSB does not possess legal personality; its standards are soft law, implemented through peer reviews and national legislation.

Key Policy Frameworks

The FSB’s post‑crisis agenda produced the “Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes” (2011, updated 2014), the “Global Systemically Important Banks” (G‑SIB) assessment methodology (2011, revised 2023), and the “Total Loss‑Absorbing Capacity” (TLAC) standard (2015). In 2022, the FSB finalised the “Policy Framework for Crypto‑Asset Activities” and the “Guidance on Climate‑Related Financial Risks”, urging supervisors to integrate scenario analysis into stress testing. The 2023 “Macroprudential Policy Convergence Report” assessed 20 jurisdictions’ use of countercyclical capital buffers (CCyB), sectoral capital requirements, and borrower‑based measures (loan‑to‑value, debt‑service‑to‑income limits). Convergence remains partial: while all G20 members have a CCyB framework, activation thresholds and calibration methodologies differ markedly.

Peer Review and Compliance Monitoring

The FSB conducts thematic peer reviews (e.g., 2021 review of “Resolution Planning for Central Counterparties”, 2023 review of “Non‑Bank Financial Intermediation”) and country‑specific reviews under the “Standards Implementation” programme. Results are published as “Peer Review Reports” with ratings (Compliant, Largely Compliant, Partially Compliant, Non‑Compliant). The 2024 review of “Margin Requirements for Non‑Centrally Cleared Derivatives” found 18 of 24 jurisdictions fully compliant, with gaps concentrated in emerging‑market implementation timelines. The G20’s “Roadmap for Enhancing Cross‑Border Payments” (2020, updated 2023) leverages FSB coordination to harmonise ISO 20022 messaging, legal entity identifiers, and data‑privacy standards.

UN Human Rights Council: Universal Periodic Review Mechanism and Special Procedures

The Human Rights Council (HRC), created by General Assembly resolution 60/251 in 2006, replaced the Commission on Human Rights and introduced the Universal Periodic Review (UPR) as a peer‑review mechanism that examines the human rights records of all 193 UN member states every four and a half years. The UPR operates through a three‑stage cycle: (1) submission of a national report, a compilation of UN information, and a summary of stakeholder submissions; (2) an interactive dialogue in the Working Group where any state may pose questions and make recommendations; and (3) adoption of an outcome report containing accepted and noted recommendations. As of the 4th cycle (2022‑2027), over 90 % of recommendations have been either supported or noted, providing a quantitative baseline for assessing compliance.

Complementing the UPR, the HRC appoints Special Procedures — independent experts, special rapporteurs, and working groups — mandated to address thematic or country‑specific issues. There are currently 45 thematic mandates (e.g., freedom of expression, torture, rights of indigenous peoples) and 13 country mandates (e.g., Myanmar, Syria, Belarus). Mandate holders conduct country visits, send communications to governments, and present annual reports to the Council and the General Assembly. Their findings are non‑binding but carry significant normative weight; for instance, the 2021 report of the Special Rapporteur on the promotion and protection of the right to freedom of opinion and expression shaped the Council’s resolution 48/13 on digital surveillance.

The Council’s institutional design includes a Bureau of five regional groups, a President elected for a one‑year term, and a standing agenda item (Item 4) for human rights situations requiring the Council’s attention. Voting on resolutions follows a simple majority of members present and voting, but the Council can also adopt decisions by consensus. The 2020‑2022 “HRC reform” discussions focused on improving the efficiency of the UPR, enhancing the independence of Special Procedures, and addressing the perceived politicisation of country‑specific resolutions. The 2023 “HRC‑UPR Trust Fund” was established to assist least‑developed countries in preparing national reports, reflecting a practical response to capacity gaps identified in the 2019 review of the mechanism.

Critics argue that the UPR’s reliance on voluntary implementation limits its deterrent effect, while supporters highlight its universality and the diplomatic pressure generated by peer recommendations. The 2022 “Joint Statement on UPR Effectiveness” by 58 states called for a follow‑up mechanism to track implementation of accepted recommendations, a proposal still under negotiation in the Council’s Working Group on the UPR. Understanding the interplay between the UPR’s cyclical peer review and the thematic agility of Special Procedures is essential for analysing contemporary human rights diplomacy.

International Court of Justice and the International Criminal Court: Jurisdiction, Enforcement, and State Compliance

The International Court of Justice (ICJ), the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, was established by the UN Charter in 1945 and began work in 1946. Its jurisdiction is twofold: contentious cases between states that have consented to the Court’s jurisdiction (via special agreement, treaty clause, or optional clause declaration under Article 36(2) of the ICJ Statute) and advisory opinions requested by authorised UN organs and specialised agencies. As of 2024, 73 states have deposited declarations accepting the Court’s compulsory jurisdiction, though many contain reservations (e.g., the United States’ “Connally amendment” excluding disputes essentially within domestic jurisdiction).

The International Criminal Court (ICC), created by the Rome Statute which entered into force on 1 July 2002, exercises complementary jurisdiction over individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression (activated in 2018). The ICC can act only when a state party is unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute, or when the UN Security Council refers a situation under Chapter VII. As of 2024, 124 states are parties; notable non‑parties include the United States, China, Russia, India, and Israel. The Court’s Prosecutor may also initiate investigations proprio motu with Pre‑Trial Chamber authorisation.

Enforcement differs sharply. ICJ judgments are binding on the parties (Article 94 of the UN Charter) and the Security Council may recommend or decide measures to give effect to a judgment, but enforcement remains political; the 1986 Nicaragua v. United States case illustrates non‑compliance despite a binding ruling. ICC arrest warrants rely on state cooperation; the Court has no police force. The 2023 arrest warrant for President Vladimir Putin (war crimes in Ukraine) underscores the practical limits when the subject state is a permanent Security Council member. Both courts face compliance challenges: the ICJ’s 2022 order in the Gambia v. Myanmar case (provisional measures on the Rohingya) saw partial adherence, while the ICC’s 2021 decision to open an investigation in the Philippines was met with non‑cooperation.

Recent jurisprudence highlights evolving norms. The ICJ’s 2023 advisory opinion on the legal consequences of Israel’s policies in the Occupied Palestinian Territory (requested by the General Assembly) clarified obligations under international humanitarian law and the prohibition of apartheid. The ICC’s 2022 confirmation of charges against former Sudanese officials for Darfur crimes demonstrated the Court’s capacity to pursue high‑level perpetrators despite political resistance. For aspirants, distinguishing the ICJ’s state‑centric, consent‑based model from the ICC’s individual‑centric, complementarity‑based model — and recognising the enforcement gap in both — is a recurring examination theme.

Applied Scenario
A question asks: “Compare the enforcement mechanisms of the ICJ and the ICC and explain why the ICJ’s 1986 Nicaragua judgment remained largely unimplemented while the ICC’s 2023 arrest warrant for a sitting head of state faces similar obstacles.”
The ICJ relies on the UN Security Council to enforce judgments under Article 94(2); however, the Council’s permanent members can veto enforcement action, as the United States did in 1986. The ICC depends on state parties to execute arrest warrants under Article 86 of the Rome Statute; non‑party states and even parties may refuse cooperation, and the Court lacks a police force. Both systems therefore translate legal rulings into political compliance, and the absence of a supranational enforcement authority makes implementation contingent on great‑power interests.

Cite the specific treaty article (UN Charter Art. 94; Rome Statute Art. 86) and a landmark case (Nicaragua v. USA; Putin warrant) to secure full marks for precision.

Global Health Governance: WHO Reform, International Health Regulations, and Pandemic Treaty Negotiations

The World Health Organization (WHO), established in 1948, operates under the Constitution that grants it normative, technical, and coordination functions. The International Health Regulations (IHR), originally adopted in 1969 and substantially revised in 2005 (entered into force 2007), constitute the binding legal framework for disease surveillance, notification, and response. The 2005 IHR introduced the concept of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), a decision made by the WHO Director‑General on the advice of an Emergency Committee. Since 2007, seven PHEICs have been declared (H1N1 2009, Polio 2014, Ebola 2014‑2016, Zika 2016, Ebola 2019‑2020, COVID‑19 2020, Mpox 2022).

The COVID‑19 pandemic exposed gaps: delayed notification, uneven core capacity implementation, and limited compliance with the IHR’s requirement for states to develop and maintain minimum public health capacities. The 2021 World Health Assembly (WHA) resolution 74.7 mandated a Member State Working Group to propose targeted amendments to the IHR, focusing on early warning, data sharing, and compliance assessment. Simultaneously, the WHA established an Intergovernmental Negotiating Body (INB) in December 2021 to draft a WHO convention, agreement, or other international instrument on pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response — commonly referred to as the “Pandemic Treaty”. The INB’s zero‑draft (February 2023) outlines principles of equity, solidarity, and the “One Health” approach, and proposes a Conference of Parties (COP) for governance.

Key contested issues include: (1) pathogen access and benefit‑sharing (PABS) — low‑ and middle‑income countries demand guaranteed access to vaccines and therapeutics derived from shared pathogens; (2) sustainable financing — proposals for a pooled fund with assessed contributions versus voluntary donations; (3) compliance and verification — whether to create an independent monitoring body with sanctioning powers. The 2024 WHA (May) adopted a decision to extend the INB’s mandate to May 2025, aiming for a final text at the 77th WHA. Parallel to treaty negotiations, the IHR amendment process concluded its first reading in 2023, with proposals to define “early action” triggers and to strengthen the role of the WHO Secretariat in verifying capacity self‑assessments.

For examination purposes, candidates should be able to trace the institutional evolution from the 1969 IHR to the 2005 revision, articulate the legal nature of a PHEIC declaration (non‑binding recommendation but politically consequential), and contrast the treaty‑making route (INB, requiring two‑thirds adoption at WHA and subsequent ratification) with the amendment route (IHR amendments enter into force for all states parties unless they opt out within a defined period). The interplay between sovereign rights (Article 3 of the IHR) and collective obligations remains the central analytical tension.

Climate Governance Architecture: UNFCCC, Paris Agreement Rulebook, and Loss and Damage Fund

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), adopted in 1992 and entered into force in 1994, establishes the overarching objective of stabilising greenhouse gas concentrations. The Kyoto Protocol (1997) introduced binding emission targets for Annex I countries but lacked participation from major emitters. The Paris Agreement (2015), adopted at COP21, shifted to a bottom‑up architecture: each Party submits a Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) every five years, with a global stocktake to assess collective progress. The Agreement entered into force on 4 November 2016 after ratification by 55 Parties representing at least 55 % of global emissions.

The “Paris Rulebook”, largely finalised at COP24 (Katowice, 2018) and completed at COP26 (Glasgow, 2021), operationalises transparency, accounting, and market mechanisms. Key components include: (1) the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) — biennial transparency reports and technical expert review for all Parties, with flexibility for developing countries; (2) Article 6 guidance on cooperative approaches — Article 6.2 (internationally transferred mitigation outcomes, ITMOs) and Article 6.4 (a new sustainable development mechanism); (3) common time frames for NDCs (five‑year cycles) and guidance on adaptation communications. The 2023 Global Stocktake (GST) at COP28 (Dubai) produced the “UAE Consensus”, calling for tripling renewable capacity and doubling energy efficiency by 2030, and for the first time explicitly referencing “transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems”.

A historic breakthrough at COP27 (Sharm El‑Sheikh, 2022) was the agreement to establish a Loss and Damage Fund to assist developing countries particularly vulnerable to climate impacts. The Transitional Committee, formed in 2023, delivered recommendations on governance, funding sources, and eligibility criteria. The Fund’s governing instrument was adopted at COP28, designating the World Bank as interim trustee for four years, with a board comprising equal representation from developed and developing countries. Initial pledges exceeded US$700 million, though the estimated annual need ranges from US$290 billion to US$580 billion by 2030. The Fund operates on a grant‑based modality for the most vulnerable, with a “rapid response window” for extreme events.

Exam‑relevant nuances include: the distinction between the legally binding nature of the Paris Agreement’s procedural obligations (NDC submission, reporting) versus the non‑binding character of the mitigation targets themselves; the role of the “common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities” (CBDR‑RC) principle in shaping flexibility under the ETF; and the interplay between Article 6 market mechanisms and the avoidance of double counting through corresponding adjustments. Mastery of the COP timeline (Kyoto → Paris → Rulebook → GST → Loss and Damage) enables candidates to answer both chronological and analytical questions with precision.

UN Security Council Working Methods and Veto Restraint Initiatives

The Security Council’s working methods are not codified in the Charter but have evolved through practice, presidential notes, and informal guidelines. The Council adopts resolutions under Chapter VI (pacific settlement) and Chapter VII (enforcement), issues presidential statements, and holds Arria‑formula meetings that allow non‑members to brief. A persistent structural issue is the veto power of the five permanent members (P5). Since 1946, the veto has been exercised 293 times (as of 2024), with the Russian Federation (and USSR) accounting for roughly half. The frequency of vetoes spikes during crises such as the Syrian conflict (12 Russian vetoes between 2011‑2022) and the Palestine question (multiple US vetoes).

In response, several voluntary restraint initiatives have emerged. The Accountability, Coherence and Transparency (ACT) Group, launched in 2013 by 27 small and medium states, proposed a “Code of Conduct” urging P5 members to refrain from vetoing resolutions on genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes. France and Mexico jointly tabled a 2015 political declaration inviting P5 members to voluntarily suspend veto use in mass‑atrocity situations; 105 states endorsed it, but no P5 member formally committed. A more consequential step came in April 2022 when Liechtenstein, backed by a cross‑regional coalition, secured adoption of UN General Assembly resolution 76/262. The resolution mandates that whenever a veto is cast in the Security Council, the General Assembly must convene within ten working days for a formal debate on the situation. The first such debate occurred in June 2022 after a Russian veto on a resolution concerning Ukraine, establishing a precedent for institutional accountability.

Procedural reforms have also targeted the Council’s transparency. Presidential Note S/2017/507 (2017) introduced a “penholder” system to distribute drafting responsibilities more equitably among elected members. The “Informal Interactive Dialogue” format, used since 2016, allows briefings by the Secretariat and civil society without a formal meeting record. Despite these steps, the veto remains a decisive brake on Council action. Analysts note that the 2022 GA debate mechanism does not override the veto but creates political cost, potentially deterring its use in clear‑cut atrocity cases. For examination purposes, candidates should distinguish between binding Charter provisions (Article 27) and non‑binding political commitments, and be able to cite the specific GA resolution number and its operative paragraph.

WTO Joint Statement Initiatives: Plurilateral Negotiations on Services, Investment, and E‑Commerce

Since the 2017 Buenos Aires Ministerial Conference (MC11), a growing share of WTO rule‑making has migrated to Joint Statement Initiatives (JSIs), open‑ended plurilateral negotiations among self‑selected members. Unlike the consensus‑based multilateral track, JSIs proceed on a “critical mass” basis: participants aim to cover a substantial portion of world trade (typically ≥90 % of the relevant sector) and then seek to multilateralise the outcome through a Ministerial Decision or an Annex 4 agreement. As of 2024, six major JSIs are active: E‑Commerce (86 members), Investment Facilitation for Development (115 members), Services Domestic Regulation (71 members), Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) (84 members), Trade and Gender (124 members), and Plastic Pollution (launched at MC13, 2024).

The E‑Commerce JSI, co‑led by Australia, Japan, and Singapore, has produced a consolidated text covering electronic authentication, online consumer protection, spam, and data flows. A contentious issue is the permanent moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions, first adopted in 1998 and renewed at every Ministerial Conference. At MC12 (Geneva, 2022) members agreed to extend the moratorium until MC13 (Abu Dhabi, 2024) while committing to intensive work on its scope. The Investment Facilitation JSI, chaired by China and South Africa, concluded a draft Agreement on Investment Facilitation for Development in July 2023; the text addresses transparency, streamlined procedures, and sustainable investment but stops short of substantive market‑access commitments. The Services Domestic Regulation JSI finalised a set of disciplines on licensing requirements, qualification procedures, and technical standards in December 2021, which were adopted as a Ministerial Decision at MC12 and entered into force for participating members in February 2023.

JSIs raise systemic questions: they bypass the single‑undertaking principle, potentially creating a “spaghetti bowl” of overlapping rules; they rely on voluntary participation, which can exclude least‑developed countries lacking negotiating capacity; and their outcomes may be challenged under the Most‑Favoured‑Nation (MFN) principle if benefits are not extended to all members. The WTO Secretariat’s 2022 “Report on the Functioning of the WTO” notes that 70 % of new trade‑related measures notified since 2018 stem from JSI‑related commitments. For exam answers, candidates should be able to name at least three JSIs, identify their co‑convenors, and explain the distinction between a Ministerial Decision (non‑binding, political) and an Annex 4 plurilateral agreement (binding only on parties).

Examiner's technique
A question asks: “Explain how the WTO’s Joint Statement Initiatives differ from the traditional multilateral negotiating framework, and assess one systemic risk they pose.”
JSIs are plurilateral, open‑ended negotiations among a subset of members that proceed on a critical‑mass basis rather than full consensus. They produce outcomes (Ministerial Decisions or Annex 4 agreements) that apply only to participants unless multilateralised. The traditional framework requires consensus of all 164 members and results in single‑undertaking agreements binding on all. A key systemic risk is fragmentation: overlapping JSI rules can create regulatory incoherence, undermine the MFN principle if benefits are not extended universally, and marginalise non‑participating developing economies that lack capacity to engage in multiple parallel tracks.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the six active JSIs, their co‑convenors, and the year each concluded a text. Use a two‑column table (JSI / Key Outcome) for quick recall. When asked about systemic risk, cite the “spaghetti bowl” analogy and the MFN tension; this scores higher than generic “lack of inclusivity” statements.

IMF Resilience and Sustainability Trust: Design, Eligibility, and Climate Conditionality

The Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST) was established by the IMF Executive Board in April 2022 and became operational in October 2022. It is financed through the voluntary channeling of Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) from members with strong external positions; as of March 2024, pledges totalled SDR 33.5 billion (≈US$44 billion), surpassing the initial US$40 billion target. The RST provides long‑term, affordable financing (20‑year maturity, 10‑year grace) at an interest rate linked to the SDR rate plus a margin, targeting low‑income countries (LICs) and vulnerable middle‑income countries (MICs) that face protracted balance‑of‑payments needs arising from climate change, pandemic preparedness, and other structural challenges.

Eligibility is determined by a two‑step filter: (1) the country must have an existing IMF upper‑credit‑tranche (UCT) arrangement (Stand‑By Arrangement, Extended Fund Facility, or ECF) or a Policy Coordination Instrument; (2) it must demonstrate a qualifying structural challenge — climate adaptation, mitigation, or pandemic resilience — assessed through a Resilience and Sustainability Assessment (RSA) prepared jointly by the IMF and the World Bank. The RSA identifies policy gaps and forms the basis for conditionality. Unlike traditional IMF conditionality, RST conditionality is “reform‑linked” rather than “performance‑linked”: disbursements are tied to the implementation of specific structural measures (e.g., adoption of a national adaptation plan, carbon pricing legislation, or health‑system contingency frameworks) verified through semi‑annual reviews.

First RST arrangements were approved for Rwanda (SDR 226 million, 2022), Barbados (SDR 100 million, 2023), and Costa Rica (SDR 438 million, 2023). The Rwanda programme includes reforms to integrate climate risk into public financial management and to scale up renewable energy investment. The Barbados arrangement supports a “Blue Economy” strategy and debt‑for‑nature swaps. The IMF’s 2023 Review of the RST noted that 70 % of RST financing has been directed to climate adaptation, with the remainder split between mitigation and pandemic preparedness. Critics argue that the requirement of a concurrent UCT arrangement excludes the most fragile states that cannot sustain upper‑credit‑tranche conditionality, and that the RSA methodology lacks a standardised metric for “resilience”. For examination, candidates should be able to state the RST’s financing source, maturity profile, eligibility criteria, and the distinction between reform‑linked and performance‑linked conditionality.

G20 Anti‑Corruption Working Group: Beneficial Ownership Transparency and Asset Recovery

The G20 Anti‑Corruption Working Group (ACWG) was created at the 2010 Toronto Summit and has since produced a series of high‑level principles and action plans. Its 2014 “High‑Level Principles on Beneficial Ownership Transparency” called on members to ensure that adequate, accurate, and timely information on the natural persons who ultimately own or control legal entities is available to competent authorities. The 2017 Hamburg Summit endorsed the “G20 Anti‑Corruption Action Plan 2017‑2019”, which added commitments to establish public beneficial‑ownership registers for companies and to improve international cooperation on asset recovery.

At the 2021 Rome Summit, leaders adopted the “G20 Rome Declaration on Beneficial Ownership Transparency”, urging all members to implement centralised, publicly accessible registers by 2024 and to extend coverage to trusts and other legal arrangements. The 2023 New Delhi Summit reinforced this with a “G20 Anti‑Corruption Action Plan 2023‑2025” that prioritises (i) verification mechanisms for register data, (ii) use of technology (e.g., blockchain‑based registries) to reduce compliance costs, and (iii) strengthening the Stolen Asset Recovery Initiative (StAR) partnership with the World Bank and UNODC. As of 2024, 15 G20 members have operational public registers; the United States enacted the Corporate Transparency Act (2021) requiring reporting to FinCEN, while the EU’s 5th Anti‑Money Laundering Directive (2020) mandates public access for companies.

Asset recovery remains a bottleneck. The UN Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) provides the legal framework, but mutual legal assistance (MLA) requests often face delays averaging 3‑5 years. The ACWG’s 2022 “Practitioner’s Guide to Asset Recovery” recommends standardised request templates, dedicated asset‑recovery focal points, and the use of non‑conviction‑based confiscation where domestic law permits. The 2023 G20 “High‑Level Principles on Asset Recovery” encourage members to adopt “unexplained wealth orders” and to share financial intelligence through the Egmont Group. For exam purposes, candidates should differentiate between beneficial‑ownership transparency (preventive) and asset recovery (remedial), cite the key G20 documents by year, and note the role of the StAR initiative as the primary multilateral coordination platform.

SCO Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure (RATS): Mandate, Operations, and Limitations

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation’s Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure (RATS) was established by the 2001 Shanghai Convention and formally inaugurated in Tashkent in 2004. Its mandate covers the “three evils” — terrorism, separatism, and extremism — as defined in the SCO Charter. RATS operates through a permanent Executive Committee headed by a Director (rotated among member states every three years) and a Council of National Coordinators. Its core functions are intelligence sharing, joint counter‑terrorism exercises, maintenance of a database of terrorist organisations and individuals, and coordination of legal assistance.

Since 2005, RATS has conducted over 30 joint exercises under the “Peace Mission” series, involving military, law‑enforcement, and special‑forces units from all member states. The 2022 “Peace Mission‑2022” exercise in Russia’s Chelyabinsk region involved 3,000 personnel and focused on urban counter‑terrorism and drone‑based reconnaissance. RATS also maintains a “List of Terrorist Organisations” (currently 31 entries) and a “List of Individuals” (over 500 names), which are updated annually and serve as the basis for extradition requests among members. In 2021, RATS facilitated the handover of 12 suspects from Kyrgyzstan to Uzbekistan under the SCO Convention on Extradition.

Despite these activities, RATS faces structural limitations. Decision‑making requires consensus, which can be blocked by bilateral disputes (e.g., India‑Pakistan tensions over Kashmir‑related listings). The database is not integrated with INTERPOL’s I‑24/7 system, limiting real‑time global interoperability. Funding relies on voluntary contributions; the 2023 budget was US$4.2 million, modest compared to the UN Office of Counter‑Terrorism’s US$30 million annual programme. Moreover, RATS’ definition of “extremism” is broad and has been criticised by human‑rights NGOs for enabling political repression. UN Peacekeeping Force Generation, Troop‑Contributing Country Incentives, and Conduct Standards

The United Nations relies on a structured force‑generation pipeline known as the Peacekeeping Capability Readiness System (PCRS), launched in 2015 to replace the ad‑hoc request‑and‑offer model. The PCRS classifies pledges into three tiers: Level 1 (political commitment), Level 2 (trained and equipped units ready for deployment within 60 days), and Level 3 (rapid‑deployment units capable of moving within 30 days). As of 2023, 115 member states have registered Level 1 pledges, 68 have advanced to Level 2, and 22 maintain Level 3 capabilities. The Secretariat’s Force Generation Service matches mission requirements — such as infantry battalions, engineering companies, or medical units — against the PCRS database, issuing a “Request for Forces” that specifies the required capability, timeline, and reimbursement rates.

Reimbursement is governed by the General Assembly’s “Standard Reimbursement Rates” (SRR), revised every three years. The 2022‑2024 cycle sets the troop‑cost reimbursement at US $1,428 per soldier per month, with separate rates for major equipment (e.g., US $1,200 per month for an armoured personnel carrier) and self‑sustainment (rations, fuel, communications). Troop‑contributing countries (TCCs) also receive a “Performance‑Based Reimbursement” pilot introduced in 2020, which adds a 10 % premium for units that meet predefined operational benchmarks such as patrol frequency, civilian protection incidents, and training compliance. The pilot covers 12 missions and is evaluated annually by the Department of Peace Operations (DPO) and the Office of Internal Oversight Services (OIOS).

Conduct standards are codified in the 2003 “Secretary‑General’s Bulletin on Special Measures for Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse” (ST/SGB/2003/13) and reinforced by the 2017 “Zero‑Tolerance Policy” that mandates immediate suspension of personnel credibly accused of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA). Each mission hosts a Conduct and Discipline Unit (CDU) responsible for receiving complaints, conducting investigations, and coordinating with the TCC’s national investigation authorities. The 2018 “Action for Peacekeeping (A4P)” declaration, endorsed by 151 member states, added a “Performance and Accountability” pillar that links mandate implementation to the “Comprehensive Planning and Performance Assessment System (CPAS)”. CPAS scores feed into the annual “Force Generation Review”, influencing future Level 2/3 eligibility. Together, these mechanisms aim to align political pledges with operational readiness while safeguarding the UN’s legitimacy.

WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement: Implementation Gaps and Capacity‑Building Mechanisms

The Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA) entered into force on 22 February 2017 after ratification by two‑thirds of WTO members. Its text is organised in three sections: Section I contains 12 binding provisions on customs procedures (e.g., pre‑arrival processing, risk management, single‑window systems); Section II provides special and differential treatment (S&DT) for developing and least‑developed countries (LDCs), allowing them to self‑designate implementation categories A (immediate), B (transitional), and C (requiring capacity‑building support); Section III establishes the Trade Facilitation Committee (TFC) and the notification framework. By the end of 2023, 115 members had notified Category A commitments, 70 had submitted Category B timelines, and 45 LDCs had identified Category C needs, triggering donor‑funded technical assistance.

The WTO’s Trade Facilitation Agreement Facility (TFAF), launched in 2014 and managed jointly with the World Customs Organization (WCO) and the World Bank, channels donor resources — over US $300 million committed by 2022 — to national projects such as automated customs valuation, electronic data interchange, and post‑clearance audit systems. Each recipient country establishes a National Trade Facilitation Committee (NTFC) comprising customs, trade, transport, and private‑sector stakeholders; the NTFC prepares a “National Trade Facilitation Roadmap” that aligns Category B/C milestones with TFAF funding tranches. The TFC conducts biennial reviews, publishing a “Implementation Report” that tracks notification compliance, average clearance times, and the share of trade covered by single‑window platforms.

Despite progress, systematic gaps persist. A 2022 WCO survey of 84 developing members found that only 38 % had fully operational risk‑management modules, and 27 % lacked electronic payment of duties. Notification delays remain common: 22 members missed the 2022 deadline for Category B progress reports, prompting the TFC to issue “reminder letters” under Article 22.2. Moreover, the “de‑minimis” threshold for expedited shipments varies widely, creating de‑facto non‑tariff barriers. The 2023 “TFA Implementation Review” highlighted that LDCs receiving Category C support still face institutional constraints — limited customs IT infrastructure, insufficient trained personnel, and fragmented inter‑agency coordination — that slow the translation of technical assistance into measurable trade‑cost reductions.

Applied Scenario Analysis
A developing WTO member has notified Category B commitments for Articles 7.1 (pre‑arrival processing) and 10.2 (single‑window) with a 36‑month transition period. Six months before the deadline, the customs administration reports that the single‑window platform is only 40 % functional due to delayed software procurement. The member requests an extension under Article 15.2. Explain the procedural steps the member must follow, the role of the Trade Facilitation Committee, and the possible outcomes.
The member must submit a formal written request to the TFC Chair, citing the specific Articles, the original transition end‑date, and the reasons for delay (e.g., procurement bottlenecks). Under Article 15.2, the TFC may grant a one‑time extension of up to 12 months if the member demonstrates “good faith efforts” and provides a revised implementation plan with milestones. The TFC reviews the request at its next regular meeting (typically quarterly), may seek clarification from the member and the TFAF donor coordinator, and issues a decision recorded in the TFC minutes. Possible outcomes: (i) extension granted with new milestones; (ii) extension denied, in which case the member remains in non‑compliance and may face a “specific trade concern” raised by other members at the TFC; (iii) the member re‑classifies the commitment to Category C, unlocking additional TFAF technical assistance but requiring a new capacity‑building needs assessment. The key is that any extension must be notified to the WTO Secretariat and reflected in the member’s Category B notification matrix.

Then recall the three‑step process: notification → TFC review → possible extension or re‑classification. IMF Governance Reform: Quota Formula Review and the 15th General Review of Quotas

The International Monetary Fund’s quota system determines each member’s financial contribution, voting power, and access to financing. The quota formula, last amended in 2008, blends four variables: GDP (50 % weight, blended market‑exchange‑rate and PPP), openness (30 %), variability of current receipts and net capital flows (15 %), and official reserves (5 %). The 14th General Review of Quotas, concluded in 2010, doubled total quotas to SDR 477 billion and shifted 6 % of quota shares to dynamic emerging‑market economies, but the associated governance reforms — including a new Executive Board seat for the 24th Director — only entered into force in 2016 after the United States ratified the amendments.

The 15th General Review, mandated by the Articles of Agreement to be completed by 2019, was extended to 2020 and ultimately concluded without a quota increase. The Executive Board’s “Report of the 15th General Review” (IMF 2020) noted that while the quota formula continued to under‑represent fast‑growing economies (e.g., China’s calculated quota share 6.4 % versus actual 6.0 %), political consensus on a new distribution could not be reached. Consequently, the Board agreed to maintain the existing quota distribution and to launch a “Quota Formula Review” (QFR) to be completed by the 2023 Annual Meetings. The QFR examines alternative weightings — such as raising the openness share to 35 % and introducing a “financial‑center” adjustment — and assesses the impact on voting power parity.

Voting power remains heavily skewed: the United States holds 16.5 % of total votes (effective veto over major decisions requiring 85 % super‑majority), the G7 collectively controls roughly 45 %, while the BRICS bloc holds about 15 %. The 2021 SDR allocation of SDR 650 billion (US $943 billion) was distributed proportionally to existing quotas, reinforcing the same asymmetry. Reform proposals under discussion include a “double‑majority” voting rule (majority of members and majority of voting power) for quota changes, and a “graduated” quota increase tied to measurable governance benchmarks such as transparency of fiscal data and adherence to the IMF’s “Code of Good Practices on Transparency in Monetary and Financial Policies”. The outcome of the QFR will shape the 16th General Review, scheduled for 2025, and will be a key indicator of whether the Fund can align its governance with the shifting global economic landscape.

BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement: Activation Triggers, Swap Mechanics, and Operational History

The BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA) was established by the 2015 Fortaleza Declaration and entered into force on 30 September 2015 with a total committed pool of US $100 billion. Capital contributions are weighted: China US $41 billion, Brazil US $18 billion, Russia US $18 billion, India US $18 billion, and South Africa US $5 billion. The CRA is governed by a Council of Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors, supported by a Standing Committee of deputies that meets quarterly. Decisions on drawings require consensus among the five members.

Activation triggers are defined in the CRA Treaty: a member may request a swap when it experiences, or is likely to experience, a short‑term balance‑of‑payments (BOP) pressure arising from a sudden capital‑flow reversal, terms‑of‑trade shock, or contagion from a regional crisis. The requesting member submits a “Drawing Request” to the Standing Committee, which assesses the macroeconomic situation using IMF Article IV‑style diagnostics. Two drawing windows exist: a precautionary line (up to 30 % of the member’s quota contribution) available without conditionality, and a conditional line (up to 100 % of quota) that requires a IMF‑supported programme or a CRA‑designed adjustment plan. Swaps are denominated in US dollars, carry a floating interest rate linked to the US Federal Funds rate plus a margin of 100 basis points, and have a maximum maturity of 12 months, renewable once.

Operational history is limited but instructive. In March 2020, Brazil drew US $1.5 billion under the precautionary line to cushion the COVID‑19‑induced capital outflow; the swap was repaid in full by September 2021. In July 2022, South Africa requested a US $1 billion precautionary drawing amid a terms‑of‑trade deterioration; the Standing Committee approved the request within ten days, marking the first activation for an African member. No member has yet accessed the conditional 100 % window. The CRA’s “Contingent Reserve Arrangement Annual Report 2023” notes that the facility’s liquidity buffer remains fully funded, and that the Standing Committee has conducted two stress‑test exercises (2021, 2023) simulating simultaneous drawings by three members, confirming the pool’s adequacy under severe but plausible scenarios.

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement: Market Mechanisms, Corresponding Adjustments, and the Glasgow Decision

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement provides three cooperative approaches: 6.2 (internationally transferred mitigation outcomes — ITMOs), 6.4 (a new sustainable‑development mechanism to replace the Clean Development Mechanism), and 6.8 (non‑market approaches). The “Glasgow Decision” (COP26, 2021) adopted the rulebook for 6.2 and 6.4, establishing the “Corresponding Adjustment” (CA) requirement: when a host country transfers an ITMO, it must adjust its nationally determined contribution (NDC) inventory to avoid double counting. The CA is applied at the level of the host’s NDC accounting period and must be reported in the Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) under the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF).

For Article 6.4, the Decision created the “Article 6.4 Supervisory Body” (SB), composed of 12 members elected by the Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement (CMA). The SB approves methodologies, registers mitigation activities, and issues “A6.4ERs” (Article 6.4 Emission Reductions). A mandatory share of proceeds (SOP) of 5 % of each A6.4ER is allocated to the Adaptation Fund, and a further 2 % covers administrative costs. The first SB meeting convened in Bonn in June 2022; by December 2023 it had approved three methodologies (renewable‑energy grid integration, methane capture from waste, and forest‑landscape restoration) and registered 27 pilot activities.

Article 6.8 establishes a framework for non‑market approaches (NMAs) such as fiscal policies, technology cooperation, and capacity‑building. The Glasgow Decision mandated a “Work Programme on NMAs” to identify best practices and develop guidance for reporting. A key operational nuance is that ITMOs under 6.2 can be generated from both within and outside the host’s NDC scope, but only those inside the NDC require a CA; those outside are treated as “voluntary cooperation” and do not trigger adjustment. The 2023 “Technical Expert Review” of the first BTR cycle highlighted that 38 % of reporting parties had not yet implemented CA accounting, underscoring the need for capacity‑building ahead of the 2024 global stocktake.

The Evolving Landscape of International Law: Sources, Soft Law, and Compliance Challenges

The foundation of the rules-based international order rests significantly on international law, primarily articulated through Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice (ICJ). This article identifies the primary sources as international conventions (treaties), international custom, and the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations. Subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law include judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists. Treaties, whether bilateral or multilateral, are legally binding agreements between states, creating specific rights and obligations. Customary international law, on the other hand, emerges from the general and consistent practice of states followed by them from a sense of legal obligation (opinio juris). Examples include diplomatic immunity or the prohibition of genocide, which are binding even on states that have not ratified specific treaties on these subjects, provided they have not persistently objected to the emerging norm.

Beyond these formal sources, "soft law" instruments play an increasingly significant role. These include resolutions of international organizations (like UN General Assembly resolutions), declarations, codes of conduct, and guidelines. While not legally binding in the same way as treaties or customary law, soft law can influence state behaviour, shape expectations, and often serve as a precursor to the development of hard law. For instance, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, initially a non-binding resolution, has inspired numerous binding human rights treaties and is widely considered to reflect customary international law in many of its provisions. The proliferation of soft law reflects a pragmatic response to the complexities of global governance, allowing for flexibility and consensus-building where formal treaty negotiations might prove intractable.

Compliance with international law presents unique challenges due to the absence of a centralized enforcement mechanism comparable to domestic legal systems. States generally comply due to a combination of factors: the principle of reciprocity (expecting others to comply if they do), reputational concerns (maintaining credibility in international relations), self-interest (benefits derived from stable legal frameworks), and domestic political and legal pressures. However, compliance can falter when core national interests are perceived to be at stake, when powerful states act unilaterally, or when norms are ambiguous. The Security Council, with its enforcement powers under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, represents the closest approximation to an international enforcement body, but its effectiveness is often constrained by the veto power of its permanent members. The fragmentation of international law, with specialized regimes (e.g., trade law, environmental law) developing independently, also poses challenges to coherence and universal application, sometimes leading to conflicting norms or forum shopping by states.

The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) and the Sovereignty-Intervention Dilemma

The concept of the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) emerged as a response to the international community's failure to prevent or adequately respond to mass atrocities in the 1990s, such as those in Rwanda and Srebrenica. First articulated in the 2001 report by the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS), R2P was unanimously endorsed by world leaders at the 2005 UN World Summit. It fundamentally reframes sovereignty not merely as control but as a responsibility to protect one's own population from four specified mass atrocity crimes: genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity.

R2P is structured around three pillars:

  1. Pillar I: The responsibility of each State to protect its populations from the four mass atrocity crimes. This is the primary and most fundamental pillar, emphasizing prevention.
  2. Pillar II: The responsibility of the international community to assist States in fulfilling their Pillar I responsibilities through capacity-building and early warning.
  3. Pillar III: The responsibility of the international community to take timely and decisive action, in accordance with the UN Charter, when a State manifestly fails to protect its populations. This pillar includes a range of measures, from diplomatic and humanitarian actions to, as a last resort, military intervention authorized by the UN Security Council.
The core dilemma R2P addresses is the tension between state sovereignty and the international community's obligation to protect human lives. While Pillar I reaffirms state sovereignty, Pillar III introduces the possibility of external intervention, albeit under strict conditions and UN Security Council authorization, thereby challenging the traditional interpretation of non-intervention. The threshold for invoking Pillar III is high, requiring a "manifest failure" by a state to protect its population from the four crimes.

The application of R2P has been contentious. The intervention in Libya in 2011, authorized by UNSC Resolution 1973, is often cited as an instance where R2P was invoked to justify military action to protect civilians. However, the subsequent regime change and instability led to accusations of mission creep and abuse of the R2P principle. Conversely, the inability of the Security Council to agree on robust action in Syria, despite widespread atrocities, highlighted the limitations of R2P when geopolitical interests and the veto power of permanent members impede consensus. The ongoing crisis in Myanmar regarding the Rohingya population also underscores the challenges of applying R2P in situations where the responsible state denies the commission of atrocities and resists international scrutiny. These cases illustrate that while R2P provides a normative framework, its operationalization remains subject to political will, Security Council dynamics, and the complex interplay of humanitarian concerns and state interests.

The Growing Influence of Non-State Actors in Global Governance

Traditional international relations theory often focused exclusively on states as the primary actors. However, contemporary global governance is increasingly characterized by the significant and diverse roles played by non-state actors (NSAs). These entities, which operate across national borders but are not directly controlled by governments, include Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs), Multinational Corporations (MNCs/MNEs), philanthropic foundations, epistemic communities, and even terrorist or criminal organizations. Their growing influence challenges the state-centric paradigm and introduces new complexities into global decision-making processes.

NGOs, ranging from humanitarian aid providers (e.g., Doctors Without Borders) to human rights advocates (e.g., Amnesty International) and environmental groups (e.g., Greenpeace), play multifaceted roles. They engage in agenda-setting by bringing new issues to international attention, act as norm entrepreneurs by advocating for new international standards and treaties, monitor state compliance with international agreements, and provide essential services where states are unable or unwilling. Their legitimacy often stems from their perceived independence, expertise, and moral authority, allowing them to influence public opinion and pressure governments and international organizations. Many NGOs participate directly in UN conferences, hold consultative status with ECOSOC, and contribute to the drafting of international instruments.

Multinational Corporations wield immense economic power, often surpassing the GDP of many nation-states. Their influence in global governance is primarily economic, shaping trade rules, investment policies, and labor standards through lobbying, direct negotiation with governments, and participation in international economic forums like the World Economic Forum. They are key players in setting industry standards and driving technological innovation, which can have profound global impacts. However, their influence also raises concerns about corporate accountability, human rights abuses in supply chains, and the potential for regulatory capture, leading to calls for stronger international oversight.

Philanthropic foundations, such as the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, have become major actors in global health, development, and climate initiatives. They provide substantial funding, shape research agendas, and implement large-scale programs, often in partnership with governments and international organizations. Their ability to deploy resources quickly and innovate can fill critical gaps in global governance, but also raises questions about their democratic legitimacy, transparency, and potential to skew global priorities based on their specific interests. Epistemic communities, networks of experts sharing a common understanding of a policy problem and its solutions, influence policy-making by providing scientific and technical knowledge to international negotiations, as seen in climate change or arms control regimes.

The rise of NSAs has led to a more pluralistic and complex global governance landscape. While they can enhance legitimacy, provide expertise, and improve accountability, they also introduce challenges related to representation (who do they represent?), funding transparency, and the potential for fragmentation of authority. Understanding their dynamics is crucial for comprehending contemporary international relations.

The Contested Terrain of Digital Governance and Cyber Norms

The rapid expansion of digital technologies has created a new domain of global governance, marked by intense contestation over its structure, norms, and control. Digital governance encompasses the rules, institutions, and processes that shape the development and use of the internet and related information and communication technologies (ICTs). Unlike traditional domains, digital governance is characterized by a multi-stakeholder model involving states, tech companies, civil society, and technical communities, each vying for influence.

Key issues at the forefront of digital governance include internet fragmentation, data governance, cybersecurity, and the digital divide. Internet fragmentation, often termed the "splinternet," refers to the risk of the global internet breaking into national or regional segments due to differing regulations on data localization, content control, and technical standards. This poses a threat to the internet's open, interoperable, and global nature. Data governance involves complex debates over data privacy, cross-border data flows, data ownership, and the ethical implications of artificial intelligence. Different regulatory approaches, such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and China's data localization requirements, highlight the lack of global consensus.

Cybersecurity is perhaps the most contentious aspect. States are increasingly concerned about state-sponsored cyberattacks on critical infrastructure, espionage, and information warfare, leading to a militarization of cyberspace. Efforts to establish international cyber norms, or rules of responsible state behavior in cyberspace, have been pursued primarily through the United Nations. The UN Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) and the Open-Ended Working Group (OEWG) have produced reports outlining voluntary, non-binding norms, such as the prohibition of attacks on critical infrastructure and the obligation to assist other states in responding to cyber incidents. However, consensus on binding norms, attribution of attacks, and the application of international law (including the law of armed conflict) to cyberspace remains elusive, often due to differing interpretations of sovereignty and self-defense.

The governance models themselves are contested. The multi-stakeholder model, championed by many Western states and tech companies, emphasizes the role of diverse actors in internet governance, as exemplified by the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN). Conversely, some states advocate for a more intergovernmental approach, asserting state sovereignty over national cyber domains and seeking greater control through bodies like the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). The digital divide, which highlights disparities in access to and benefits from digital technologies between and within countries, further complicates governance efforts, as it exacerbates inequalities and limits inclusive participation in the digital economy.

The absence of a unified global regulatory framework, coupled with the rapid pace of technological change and geopolitical competition, ensures that digital governance will remain a dynamic and challenging area for international cooperation. The ongoing debates reflect fundamental disagreements over human rights online, national security, economic interests, and the very future of the global digital commons.

Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) Beyond Bretton Woods: Diversification and New Paradigms

While the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank Group (WBG) are the most prominent International Financial Institutions (IFIs), the landscape of multilateral development finance has significantly diversified beyond these Bretton Woods institutions. Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) are international financial institutions that provide financial and technical assistance to developing countries. Beyond the WBG, key regional MDBs include the African Development Bank (AfDB), the Asian Development Bank (ADB), the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB), and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD).

These regional MDBs share a common mandate of fostering economic development and regional integration within their respective geographies. They provide loans, grants, equity investments, and technical assistance for a wide range of projects, including infrastructure (energy, transport, water), social development (health, education), and environmental sustainability. Their governance structures typically involve member countries from their respective regions, alongside non-regional members, with voting power often reflecting capital subscriptions. For instance, the ADB focuses on poverty reduction and sustainable development in Asia and the Pacific, while the AfDB prioritizes infrastructure, energy, and food security across Africa.

More recently, new MDBs have emerged, notably the New Development Bank (NDB) established by the BRICS countries and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) led by China. These institutions represent a significant shift in the global development finance architecture, often reflecting a desire by emerging economies for greater voice and alternative development models. The NDB, operational since 2015, focuses on infrastructure and sustainable development projects in BRICS and other developing countries, emphasizing local currency financing and less stringent conditionality compared to traditional MDBs. The AIIB, also launched in 2016, specializes in infrastructure development in Asia, aiming to fill significant funding gaps and promote regional connectivity. Both the NDB and AIIB have attracted a broad membership, including many non-BRICS and non-Asian countries, signaling their growing global relevance.

The emergence of these new MDBs introduces both opportunities and challenges. They offer additional sources of financing, potentially accelerating infrastructure development and poverty reduction, particularly in areas underserved by traditional MDBs. Their focus on South-South cooperation and less prescriptive conditionality can be appealing to developing countries. However, their rise also prompts questions about debt sustainability, environmental and social safeguards, and potential geopolitical competition with established institutions. While they often co-finance projects with the World Bank and other MDBs, fostering a degree of cooperation, their distinct governance structures and lending philosophies contribute to a more pluralistic, and at times fragmented, global development finance landscape. This diversification reflects a broader rebalancing of economic power and a demand for more inclusive and responsive multilateral institutions.

UN Peacekeeping Conduct and Accountability: Zero Tolerance, Conduct Standards, and the 2015 Policy Framework

The United Nations’ “Zero Tolerance” policy on sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) was formally articulated in the Secretary‑General’s 2015 report Zero Tolerance: Strengthening the Prevention of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (A/70/729). That report mandated the establishment of Conduct and Discipline Units (CDUs) in every peacekeeping mission, the appointment of a Senior Conduct and Discipline Officer at headquarters, and the creation of a centralized database for tracking allegations. In 2017 the Department of Peace Operations (DPO) issued the Policy on Conduct and Discipline for Uniformed Personnel, which extended the zero‑tolerance principle to all categories of personnel — military, police, and civilian — and introduced a standardized investigative procedure with a 90‑day target for completion.

The 2018 Action for Peacekeeping (A4P) declaration reinforced accountability by requiring troop‑ and police‑contributing countries (TCCs/PCCs) to pre‑screen personnel, provide pre‑deployment training on the UN Standards of Conduct, and accept the “UN Conduct and Discipline Compact” that makes the contributing state responsible for disciplinary action against its nationals. The 2020 Comprehensive Review of Peacekeeping Conduct (A/75/767) identified persistent gaps: under‑reporting by victims, inconsistent application of the 90‑day investigative timeline, and limited follow‑up on repatriated personnel. In response, the Secretariat launched the UN Peacekeeping Conduct and Discipline Dashboard in 2022, a public‑facing tool that publishes aggregate data on allegations, investigations, and outcomes by mission and by contributing country.

Performance metrics now form part of the Peacekeeping Performance and Accountability Framework (2023), which links mission‑level conduct scores to resource allocation and mandate renewal decisions. The framework also introduces a “conduct conditionality” clause: missions that fail to meet minimum thresholds for SEA prevention training, victim‑support services, and timely investigations may face reductions in reimbursement rates for contingent‑owned equipment. These mechanisms collectively aim to shift the incentive structure from reactive punishment to preventive compliance, aligning TCC/PCC interests with the UN’s normative standards.

G20 Digital Economy Working Group: Data Governance, Cross‑Border Data Flows, and the Osaka Track

The G20 Digital Economy Task Force was created at the 2017 Hamburg Summit and upgraded to a full Working Group (DEWG) at the 2020 Riyadh Summit. Its mandate covers digital infrastructure, measurement of the digital economy, trust‑building measures, and inclusive digital transformation. The 2019 Osaka Summit produced the “Osaka Track” — a voluntary, non‑binding framework for “Data Free Flow with Trust (DFFT)”. The Track encourages participating economies to adopt domestic legal regimes that permit cross‑border data flows while safeguarding privacy, security, and intellectual property. It does not create a treaty obligation; instead, it relies on peer review and the G20’s mutual assessment process to monitor implementation.

Subsequent ministerial declarations have layered concrete deliverables onto the Osaka Track. The 2021 Rome Declaration endorsed the G20 Data Governance Principles, which articulate six high‑level norms: legitimacy, transparency, accountability, security, interoperability, and inclusivity. The 2022 Bali Work Plan tasked the DEWG with developing a “Global Data Governance Toolkit” — a compendium of best‑practice regulatory approaches, model contractual clauses for data transfers, and a mapping of existing adequacy decisions. At the 2023 New Delhi Summit, ministers agreed to pilot a “Trusted Data Flow Corridor” among willing G20 members, using the toolkit to test interoperable certification mechanisms for data processors.

Parallel to the DEWG, the Global Partnership on Artificial Intelligence (GPAI) was launched in 2020 under the G20’s auspices to promote responsible AI development. The 2019 G20 AI Principles — human‑centred, transparent, robust, and accountable — were reaffirmed in the 2023 ministerial communiqué, which also called for a “G20 AI Governance Framework” to be drafted by 2025. These layered initiatives illustrate how the G20 uses soft‑law instruments, peer learning, and voluntary coalitions to shape global digital norms without awaiting universal treaty consensus.

International Court of Justice Advisory Opinions: Climate Change, Nuclear Weapons, and the 2024 Advisory Opinion on State Obligations

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) exercises advisory jurisdiction under Article 96 of the UN Charter and Article 65 of its Statute, providing legal opinions to authorized UN organs and specialized agencies. The 1996 Advisory Opinion on the Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons established that the threat or use of nuclear weapons would generally be contrary to the rules of international law applicable in armed conflict, while leaving open the possibility of lawful use in an extreme circumstance of self‑defence where the very survival of a state is at stake. That opinion has shaped subsequent nuclear‑disarmament discourse and informed the 2017 Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons.

In March 2023 the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 77/276, requesting an advisory opinion on “the obligations of States in respect of climate change”. The request asked the Court to clarify (a) the content of the due‑diligence obligation to prevent significant transboundary harm from greenhouse‑gas emissions, (b) the relevance of the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR‑RC) to mitigation and adaptation duties, and (c) the legal consequences for States that fail to meet their obligations, including potential responsibility for loss and damage. The Court held public hearings in April‑May 2024, receiving written and oral submissions from over 80 States and numerous intergovernmental organizations.

The advisory opinion, delivered on 23 July 2024, affirmed that the no‑harm principle under customary international law imposes a due‑diligence obligation on all States to regulate activities within their jurisdiction that contribute to climate change. It clarified that CBDR‑RC does not diminish the core due‑diligence duty but informs the standard of care expected from developed versus developing States. The Court also held that a breach of this obligation entails State responsibility, including the duty to cease the wrongful conduct, provide assurances of non‑repetition, and make full reparation — potentially encompassing compensation for loss and damage. The opinion is non‑binding but carries significant normative weight, guiding future climate negotiations, domestic litigation, and the work of the UNFCCC’s Loss and Damage Fund.

Global Tax Governance: The OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework, Pillar One and Pillar Two, and the 2023 Multilateral Convention

The OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) was launched in 2016 with 140 participating jurisdictions, expanding the original 44‑member BEPS Project to a truly global coalition. Its two‑pillar solution, endorsed by G20 Finance Ministers in October 2021, addresses the tax challenges of the digitalised economy. Pillar One reallocates a portion of the residual profits of the largest and most profitable multinational enterprises (MNEs) to market jurisdictions where users or consumers are located, irrespective of physical presence. Amount A applies to MNEs with global turnover above €20 billion and profitability above 10 %, allocating 25 % of residual profit (profit above 10 % of revenue) to market jurisdictions via a formulaic allocation key. Amount B provides a simplified, fixed‑return approach for baseline marketing and distribution activities.

Pillar Two introduces a Global Minimum Tax (GMT) of 15 % through the Global Anti‑Base Erosion (GloBE) rules. The core mechanisms are the Income Inclusion Rule (IIR), which taxes a parent entity on the low‑taxed income of its subsidiaries; the Undertaxed Payments Rule (UTPR), which denies deductions or imposes source‑based taxation on payments to low‑taxed entities; and the Subject‑to‑Tax Rule (STTR), a treaty‑based rule allowing source countries to tax certain intra‑group payments (e.g., interest, royalties) at a minimum 9 % rate when the recipient is taxed below that threshold. The OECD released the final GloBE model rules in December 2021, followed by commentary and implementation guidance in 2022.

To give legal effect to Amount A, the Inclusive Framework negotiated a Multilateral Convention (MLC) that was opened for signature on 2 October 2023. By the end of 2023, 138 jurisdictions had signed, representing over 90 % of global GDP. The MLC enters into force once ratified by at least 30 signatories covering 60 % of the relevant MNE groups. Parallel domestic legislation is underway in the EU (Directive 2022/2523), the United Kingdom, Japan, Canada, and several developing economies. The STTR has been incorporated into the UN Model Tax Convention (2021 update) to facilitate adoption by developing countries. Together, these instruments constitute the most ambitious coordinated tax reform since the 1920s, aiming to curb profit shifting, ensure a minimum level of taxation, and allocate taxing rights more equitably in a digitalised global economy.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the voluntary nature of the Osaka Track with a binding treaty obligation; the Track creates no legal enforcement mechanism.
  • Assuming the ICJ’s advisory opinions are legally binding on States; they are authoritative interpretations but lack compulsory force.
  • Treating Pillar One’s Amount A as applicable to all MNEs; it targets only the largest, most profitable groups meeting specific turnover and profitability thresholds.
  • Overlooking the distinction between the IIR (parent‑level) and UTPR (source‑level) in Pillar Two; they operate at different points in the corporate structure.
  • Believing the UN’s zero‑tolerance policy automatically results in prosecution of individual peacekeepers; disciplinary action remains the responsibility of the contributing State.
  • Equating the G20’s peer‑review monitoring with a formal compliance committee; the G20 relies on political peer pressure, not judicial review.
  • Neglecting the role of the Subject‑to‑Tax Rule as a treaty‑based tool for developing countries; it is not part of the domestic GloBE rules but a separate bilateral instrument.

The Common Framework for Debt Treatments and the Evolution of Sovereign Debt Restructuring

The Common Framework for Debt Treatments beyond the DSSI, endorsed by the G20 and the Paris Club in November 2020, represents the most significant restructuring architecture since the Brady Plan of the 1990s. It was designed to address the structural gaps exposed by the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI), which merely deferred payments for 73 eligible low‑income countries from May 2020 to December 2021 without reducing stock‑of‑debt vulnerabilities. The Common Framework introduces three procedural innovations: mandatory participation of all official bilateral creditors — including non‑Paris Club lenders such as China, India, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE — on comparable terms; a requirement for the debtor to seek comparable treatment from private creditors; and a case‑by‑case debt sustainability analysis (DSA) conducted jointly by the IMF and the World Bank to calibrate the needed relief envelope.

The first three country cases — Chad, Zambia, and Ethiopia — revealed operational friction. Chad’s process, launched in January 2021, concluded in November 2022 with a restructuring of approximately $2.2 billion in official bilateral debt, but only after protracted negotiations over the valuation of oil‑collateralised obligations held by Glencore and the treatment of commercial claims. Zambia’s case, initiated in February 2021, stalled for over two years due to disagreements between the Official Creditor Committee (co‑chaired by China and France) and private bondholders over the DSA assumptions and the comparability of treatment standard. A memorandum of understanding with official creditors was finally signed in June 2023, followed by a bondholder agreement in March 2024 covering $3 billion in Eurobonds, delivering nominal haircuts of roughly 20 % and maturity extensions. Ethiopia’s process, launched in February 2021, was suspended in 2022 amid internal conflict and only resumed in late 2023; its Eurobond restructuring remains pending. Sri Lanka, though not a DSSI‑eligible low‑income country, pursued a parallel process with the Paris Club, India, China, and the Export‑Import Bank of China, culminating in a 2024 agreement in principle with official bilateral creditors and a subsequent domestic debt optimisation operation.

Three systemic weaknesses persist. First, the absence of a binding enforcement mechanism for private‑sector participation allows holdouts to free‑ride on official relief, undermining the comparability principle. Second, the DSA methodology — particularly the baseline growth and export projections — remains contested; creditors often prefer conservative assumptions that reduce the required haircut, while debtors and the IMF staff advocate more realistic scenarios. Third, the Framework excludes middle‑income countries facing liquidity crises, leaving a governance gap that the G20’s “Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable” (launched February 2023) attempts to bridge through enhanced information sharing and process standardisation, but without treaty‑based authority.

The Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework: Architecture, Pillars, and the Non‑Tariff Governance Model

Launched by the United States in May 2022 with 14 initial partners — Australia, Brunei, Fiji, India, Indonesia, Japan, the Republic of Korea, Malaysia, New Zealand, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam, and the United States — the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEF) departs fundamentally from traditional free‑trade agreements by eschewing tariff liberalisation and market‑access schedules. Instead, it is organised around four substantive pillars: Trade (Pillar I), Supply Chains (Pillar II), Clean Economy (Pillar III), and Fair Economy (Pillar IV). Each pillar operates as a modular negotiation; partners may join any subset, though the United States has encouraged full participation. India joined Pillars II‑IV but declined Pillar I, citing concerns over digital trade rules, labour and environment standards, and the absence of tariff concessions for its goods exports.

Pillar II (Supply Chains) concluded its “substantive agreement” in May 2023 and entered into force in February 2024 after ratification by five parties. It establishes a Supply Chain Council, a Crisis Response Network for early‑warning coordination during disruptions, and a Labour Rights Advisory Board — the first such body in a US‑led plurilateral arrangement. The agreement creates a “Key Sectors” list (semiconductors, critical minerals, batteries, pharmaceuticals) for targeted investment facilitation and workforce development, but does not impose binding stockpiling or export‑restriction disciplines. Pillar III (Clean Economy) and Pillar IV (Fair Economy) reached agreement in principle in June 2024. Pillar III focuses on clean energy deployment, methane abatement, and sustainable aviation fuel, with a dedicated “Catalyst Capital” mechanism to mobilise private finance. Pillar IV addresses tax transparency (aligned with the OECD/G20 Two‑Pillar Solution), anti‑corruption (building on UNCAC), and effective domestic resource mobilisation. Pillar I (Trade) remains the most contentious: negotiations cover digital trade (cross‑border data flows, data localisation prohibitions, source code protection), high‑standard labour and environment provisions, agriculture transparency, and SME inclusion. The US insistence on binding digital rules without tariff offsets has been criticised by developing partners as asymmetric.

Governance rests on a Ministerial Meeting (annual), a Senior Officials Meeting, and pillar‑specific committees. Decisions are taken by consensus. There is no investor‑state dispute settlement (ISDS); state‑to‑state dispute resolution is available but non‑binding, emphasising consultation and mediation. The Framework’s legal form is an executive agreement, not a treaty requiring US Senate ratification, which accelerates entry into force but limits domestic legal enforceability. For India, the strategic value lies in supply‑chain diversification away from China, technology transfer in clean energy, and a seat at the rule‑writing table for digital and green standards — but the absence of market access gains remains a domestic political constraint.

Examiner's Trap: Comparing IPEF and CPTPP
A question asks: "Contrast the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) with the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans‑Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) on three dimensions: market access, dispute settlement, and regulatory scope. Explain why India joined IPEF Pillars II‑IV but remains outside CPTPP."
Model Answer: (1) Market Access: CPTPP is a traditional FTA eliminating tariffs on 95 %+ of goods lines and services commitments; IPEF contains zero tariff concessions. (2) Dispute Settlement: CPTPP has binding state‑to‑state DS (Chapter 28) and ISDS (Chapter 9, suspended for most parties); IPEF has only non‑binding consultation/mediation, no ISDS. (3) Regulatory Scope: CPTPP covers goods, services, investment, IP, SOEs, e‑commerce, labour, environment; IPEF covers supply chains, clean economy, fair economy, and digital trade (Pillar I) — but only as regulatory alignment, not liberalisation. India’s rationale: CPTPP requires tariff cuts on sensitive agriculture/dairy, binding ISDS exposure, and data‑localisation prohibitions incompatible with its 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Act. IPEF Pillars II‑IV offer supply‑chain resilience, clean‑tech cooperation, and tax/anti‑corruption alignment without market‑access concessions or ISDS.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about "new economic architectures" or "non‑tariff governance", map the answer on two axes: (a) bindingness (treaty vs executive agreement, DS enforceability) and (b) exchange (market access vs regulatory cooperation). IPEF, CPTPP, RCEP, and the USMCA occupy distinct quadrants. Memorise one concrete provision per pillar (e.g., IPEF Pillar II Crisis Response Network, CPTPP Chapter 14 e‑commerce data‑flow rules) to illustrate the contrast.

The Financial Action Task Force: Mutual Evaluation Process, Grey Listing Mechanics, and Effectiveness Assessment

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF), established by the G7 in 1989 and expanded to 40 members (38 jurisdictions plus the European Commission and the Gulf Cooperation Council), sets the global standards for anti‑money laundering, counter‑terrorist financing, and counter‑proliferation financing (AML/CFT/CPF) through its 40 Recommendations. Its principal enforcement lever is the Mutual Evaluation (ME) process, a peer‑review mechanism assessing both technical compliance (legal framework) and effectiveness (results‑based outcomes across 11 Immediate Outcomes). Each member undergoes an ME roughly every 10 years; the cycle was extended from 5 to 10 years in 2023 to reduce burden. The assessment team comprises assessors from other member bodies and the FATF Secretariat, producing a report adopted by the Plenary.

Jurisdictions with serious deficiencies are placed under enhanced follow‑up. The "Grey List" (Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring) and "Black List" (High‑Risk Jurisdictions subject to a Call for Action) are the most visible outputs. As of June 2024, the Grey List included 23 jurisdictions (e.g., Kenya, Namibia, Nigeria, South Africa, Vietnam, Philippines, Turkey, UAE — removed February 2024, Barbados, Gibraltar). The Black List comprised three: Iran, North Korea, and Myanmar. Listing triggers a structured action plan with deadlines; progress is reviewed at each Plenary (three per year). Removal requires demonstrated completion of all action‑plan items and a positive on‑site visit. The UAE’s removal in February 2024, after 22 months, followed reforms in beneficial‑ownership transparency, DNFBP supervision, and international cooperation.

Effectiveness assessment has shifted the FATF’s focus from "box‑ticking" legislation to measurable outcomes: convictions, asset recovery, international cooperation requests executed, and risk‑based supervision of financial institutions and designated non‑financial businesses and professions (DNFBPs) — lawyers, accountants, real‑estate agents, precious‑metal dealers. The 2022‑2023 Methodology update introduced a "risk‑based" weighting: jurisdictions are scored on a four‑point scale (High, Substantial, Moderate, Low) for each Immediate Outcome. A "Low" rating on any IO triggers automatic enhanced follow‑up. Critics argue the process remains opaque — assessor identities are confidential, deliberations are closed, and the Plenary’s consensus rule allows political shielding. The FATF’s 2023 "Effectiveness Review" acknowledged uneven implementation of the risk‑based approach and committed to greater transparency in the listing/delisting criteria.

The International Seabed Authority and the Governance of Deep Seabed Mining: The Two‑Year Rule and the Mining Code

The International Seabed Authority (ISA), established under the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and its 1994 Implementation Agreement, governs mineral‑related activities in the "Area" — the seabed and ocean floor beyond national jurisdiction, covering approximately 54 % of the planet’s surface. The ISA comprises 169 members (168 states plus the EU) and operates through an Assembly, a 36‑member Council (equitable geographical distribution), a Legal and Technical Commission (LTC), and a Secretariat headquartered in Kingston, Jamaica. As of 2024, the ISA has awarded 31 exploration contracts (15 for polymetallic nodules, 7 for polymetallic sulphides, 9 for cobalt‑rich ferromanganese crusts) to state‑sponsored entities and private contractors, covering over 1.5 million km².

The transition from exploration to exploitation hinges on the adoption of the Mining Code — a comprehensive set of rules, regulations, and procedures (RRPs) covering environmental standards, financial terms (royalties, profit‑sharing), inspection, compliance, and the benefit‑sharing mechanism mandated by UNCLOS Article 140 and the 1994 Agreement. Negotiations on the exploitation regulations began in 2014; key sticking points include the design of the financial model (fixed royalty vs ad valorem vs profit‑based), the environmental threshold for "serious harm" (Article 145), the role of the LTC in approving environmental impact assessments (EIAs), and the operationalisation of the "common heritage of mankind" principle through the Economic Planning Commission and the Enterprise (the ISA’s own mining operator, not yet capitalised).

The "Two‑Year Rule", triggered by Nauru (sponsoring Nauru Ocean Resources Inc., a subsidiary of The Metals Company) in June 2021 under UNCLOS Annex III Article 15(2), obliges the Council to complete the Mining Code within 24 months or consider and provisionally approve a plan of work for exploitation "in good faith" based on whatever regulations exist. The deadline expired in July 2023 without a final Code. The Council has since met in multiple sessions (July 2023, November 2023, March 2024, July 2024) but remains deadlocked on the financial model and the environmental decision‑making standard. A coalition of 25+ states (including France, Germany, Chile, Costa Rica, Palau, Fiji) has called for a "precautionary pause" or moratorium; others (China, Russia, Nauru, Cook Islands) insist on regulatory completion. The Secretary‑General’s 2024 report warned that without consensus, the ISA faces legal uncertainty: a contractor could submit an exploitation application that the LTC would assess against incomplete regulations, exposing the ISA to litigation before the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS). ITLOS’s 2023 Advisory Opinion on climate obligations (requested by COSIS) affirmed the due‑diligence duty to protect the marine environment, reinforcing the precautionary argument.

The UN Development System Reform: The Reinvigorated Resident Coordinator System, Funding Compact, and the Quadrennial Comprehensive Policy Review

The repositioning of the UN Development System (UNDS), mandated by General Assembly Resolution 72/279 (2018) and refined by Resolution 75/233 (2021), constitutes the most ambitious institutional overhaul of the UN’s operational pillar since the creation of the UNDP in 1965. Its centrepiece is the reinvigorated Resident Coordinator (RC) system: RCs are now dedicated, impartial leaders of UN Country Teams (UNCTs), separated from UNDP Resident Representative functions, with direct reporting lines to the Secretary‑General through the UN Development Coordination Office (DCO). As of 2024, 131 RCs cover 162 countries and territories. The RC’s performance is assessed through a new accountability framework — the RC Management and Accountability System — with annual compacts signed with the Deputy Secretary‑General.

The Funding Compact, agreed in 2019, links this structural shift to financing. It establishes a target of $28.1 billion in annual core/non‑earmarked funding for the UNDS by 2023 (actual 2023: ~$22.4 billion, a 20 % shortfall) and introduces a 1 % coordination levy on non‑core contributions to fund the DCO and RC system. It also mandates system‑wide results reporting through the UN INFO platform and joint programme standards. The Quadrennial Comprehensive Policy Review (QCPR) — the intergovernmental steering mechanism — sets the strategic priorities for 2021‑2024: eradicating poverty (SDG 1), climate action (SDG 13), gender equality (SDG 5), and leaving no one behind. The 2024 QCPR cycle (Resolution 79/xxx, to be adopted) will evaluate the reform’s impact on development effectiveness, fragmentation reduction, and the "efficiency gains" claimed by the Secretariat (estimated at $180 million annually from reduced duplication).

Persistent challenges include: (1) the "funding mismatch" — earmarked contributions still exceed 80 % of total UNDS revenue, limiting programmatic coherence; (2) the "RC authority gap" — RCs lack supervisory power over agency country representatives, who remain accountable to their headquarters; (3) the "regional asymmetry" — the DCO’s five regional hubs (Addis Ababa, Bangkok, Panama, Amman, Istanbul) have uneven staffing and budgetary autonomy; and (4) the "humanitarian‑development nexus" — coordination with OCHA‑led humanitarian response remains ad hoc, despite the 2019 "New Way of Working" commitments. The 2023 Independent Evaluation by the Joint Inspection Unit (JIU) concluded that while the RC system has improved strategic alignment, measurable development outcomes attributable solely to the reform remain difficult to isolate.

UN Peacekeeping Exit Strategies and Transition Planning

The design of an exit strategy begins at the mandate‑drafting stage, where the Security Council embeds benchmarks — such as the restoration of state authority, the completion of disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration (DDR) programmes, and the establishment of a functional rule‑of‑law framework — that trigger a drawdown. The 2015 “Capstone Doctrine” and the 2020 “Transition Planning Guidance” issued by the Department of Peace Operations (DPO) require the Special Representative of the Secretary‑General (SRSG) to produce a Transition Plan no later than 12 months before the projected mission end‑date. The plan is reviewed jointly by DPO, the Department of Political and Peacebuilding Affairs (DPPA), and the UN Country Team (UNCT) to align security‑sector reform, humanitarian‑development nexus activities, and the Resident Coordinator’s development agenda.

Operationalisation relies on a phased drawdown: (1) a “security handover” where national forces assume primary responsibility for civilian protection; (2) a “capacity‑transfer” phase in which UN police and formed police units mentor host‑state counterparts; and (3) a “residual presence” of a small political office or a UN Integrated Office (e.g., UNIOGBIS in Guinea‑Bissau) to sustain political dialogue. The 2023 drawdown of MINUSMA in Mali illustrated the risks of compressed timelines: the Council’s Resolution 2690 (2023) set a 31 December 2023 exit, yet the Malian security forces lacked the logistics to secure the northern regions, prompting a rapid deterioration that forced the UN to retain a limited logistical hub in Gao until March 2024. By contrast, UNAMID’s 2020‑2021 transition in Darfur followed a longer, benchmark‑driven schedule, allowing the Sudanese government and the African Union to assume responsibility for civilian protection in a more orderly fashion.

Financing the transition is governed by the “scale of assessments” for peacekeeping budgets (General Assembly Resolution 73/274) and by voluntary contributions to the Peacebuilding Fund (PBF). The PBF’s “Transition Allocation Window” (established 2021) earmarks up to US$150 million per year for missions in the final 24 months, funding quick‑impact projects that reinforce local governance and service delivery. Performance is measured through the “Transition Monitoring Framework”, which tracks 28 indicators across security, rule of law, human rights, and socio‑economic recovery. The 2022 Independent Review of Peacekeeping Transitions (the “HIPPO‑plus” report) found that missions with a dedicated Transition Cell — staffed by DPO, DPPA, and UNDP personnel — achieved a 27 % higher benchmark‑completion rate than those without.

WTO Fisheries Subsidies Agreement: Implementation, Monitoring, and Dispute Settlement

The Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies, adopted at the 12th Ministerial Conference (MC12) in Geneva on 17 June 2022, represents the first multilateral discipline targeting harmful subsidies that drive illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing, overfishing of overfished stocks, and fishing on the unregulated high seas. The text prohibits three categories of subsidies: (i) those granted to vessels or operators engaged in IUU fishing; (ii) subsidies for fishing overfished stocks, unless the stock is rebuilt to a biologically sustainable level; and (iii) subsidies for fishing on the high seas where no regional fisheries management organisation (RFMO) has conservation measures in place. Developing members and least‑developed countries (LDCs) receive a two‑year and four‑year transition period respectively, with the possibility of an additional two‑year extension upon notification to the Committee on Fisheries.

Implementation is monitored through a dedicated “Fisheries Subsidies Notification” template that members must submit within 12 months of the Agreement’s entry into force (expected 2024 after two‑thirds ratification). The Committee on Fisheries, meeting twice annually, reviews notifications, conducts peer‑review sessions, and may request additional information. A “Special and Differential Treatment” (S&DT) annex allows developing members to maintain certain subsidies for artisanal fisheries provided they do not contribute to overcapacity. The Agreement also establishes a “Technical Assistance Fund” (US$30 million, financed by voluntary contributions) to support capacity‑building in stock assessment, monitoring, control and surveillance (MCS) systems for developing members.

Dispute settlement proceeds under the Understanding on Rules and Procedures Governing the Settlement of Disputes (DSU). However, the Appellate Body remains non‑functional since December 2019, so parties may resort to the Multi‑Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA) — currently subscribed by 52 members — for appellate review. The first fisheries‑subsidies dispute, initiated by the United States against China in March 2024 (DS618), tests whether the MPIA can deliver timely rulings on subsidy‑prohibition claims. The panel’s interim report, circulated in September 2024, emphasised the need for “transparent, verifiable data” on stock status, highlighting the practical difficulty of proving overfished status without robust scientific assessments — a gap the Technical Assistance Fund aims to close.

Examiner's technique: Comparative provision mapping
A question asks: “Compare the treatment of artisanal fisheries subsidies under the WTO Fisheries Subsidies Agreement and the UNCLOS provisions on the rights of small‑scale fishers. Identify one area of potential conflict.”
The WTO Agreement permits developing members to maintain subsidies for artisanal fisheries provided they do not contribute to overcapacity (Annex 2, S&DT). UNCLOS Article 61 obliges coastal states to ensure that the living resources of the EEZ are not endangered by over‑exploitation, and Article 62 requires giving due regard to the interests of artisanal fishers. Conflict arises when a developing member subsidises fuel for artisanal fleets, expanding effort beyond sustainable levels; the WTO S&DT may shield the subsidy, while UNCLOS imposes a duty on the coastal state to prevent over‑exploitation. The answer should cite the specific annex paragraph (Annex 2, para 3) and UNCLOS Articles 61‑62, and note that the WTO’s “no overcapacity” qualifier is a self‑assessed condition, whereas UNCLOS requires an objective stock assessment.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for a comparison across regimes, structure the answer in a three‑column table: (1) Provision, (2) Scope/Condition, (3) Enforcement/Compliance mechanism. This forces you to surface the exact treaty language and avoids vague “both aim to protect fishers” statements that earn no marks.

IMF Special Drawing Rights Allocation: 2021 Allocation, Reallocation Mechanisms, and Governance

The Board of Governors approved a general allocation of SDR 456 billion (approximately US$650 billion) on 2 August 2021, the largest in IMF history, to bolster global liquidity amid the COVID‑19 pandemic. Distribution followed each member’s quota share: advanced economies received roughly 58 % (US$380 billion), emerging markets 42 % (US$270 billion), while low‑income countries (LICs) obtained only US$21 billion. Recognising the mismatch, the IMF launched a voluntary “channeling” framework encouraging members with strong external positions to reallocate SDRs to the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT), the Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST), and eligible multilateral development banks (MDBs). As of March 2024, cumulative pledges reached US$100 billion, with the European Union, Japan, and the United Kingdom accounting for 70 % of commitments.

Reallocation operates through two legal instruments: (i) a “voluntary SDR transfer” under Article XVIII, Section 4 of the Articles of Agreement, which requires the consent of both the transferring and receiving members and IMF approval; and (ii) a “SDR‑denominated loan” to the PRGT or RST, where the IMF acts as trustee and the SDRs are held in the Trust’s SDR account. The RST, operational since October 2022, uses reallocated SDRs to finance climate‑resilience and pandemic‑preparedness projects in eligible vulnerable countries. Governance of the channeling process rests with the Executive Board, which reviews quarterly reports on the use of reallocated SDRs, and with the Independent Evaluation Office (IEO), which conducted a 2023 assessment concluding that the PRGT’s SDR‑backed lending had reduced the average interest rate for LICs from 1.5 % to 0.5 % per annum.

The 2023 Review of the SDR Allocation Formula (the “15th General Review of Quotas”) examined whether the current quota‑based distribution remains appropriate. The review recommended a modest increase in the weight of “openness” and “financial contribution” variables, but deferred any change to the allocation key until the 16th Review (expected 2028). Meanwhile, the IMF’s SDR Department publishes a monthly “SDR Holdings and Allocations” dataset, enabling analysts to track real‑time reallocation flows and assess whether the voluntary channeling target of US$100 billion for LICs by 2025 is on track.

G20 Infrastructure Investment: The Global Infrastructure Hub, Project Preparation Facilities, and Private Capital Mobilisation

The Global Infrastructure Hub (GI Hub) was established in 2014 under the Australian G20 presidency as a knowledge‑sharing platform to accelerate the pipeline of bankable infrastructure projects. Its mandate, renewed at the 2023 New Delhi Summit, includes maintaining the “Infrastructure Project Database” (over 3,200 projects valued at US$2.8 trillion), publishing the “G20 Principles for Quality Infrastructure Investment” (2019), and operating the “Project Preparation Facility (PPF) Network” that connects project sponsors with MDB‑managed preparation funds. The PPF Network comprises the World Bank’s “Infrastructure Project Preparation Facility”, the Asian Development Bank’s “Project Preparation Special Fund”, and the African Development Bank’s “Infrastructure Project Preparation Facility”, collectively mobilising US$1.2 billion in grant‑based preparation finance since 2016.

Private capital mobilisation relies on blended‑finance structures: MDBs provide senior loans, guarantees, or first‑loss equity tranches that de‑risk projects for institutional investors. The G20’s “Infrastructure Data Initiative” (launched 2021) standardises project‑level data — financial models, environmental‑social‑governance (ESG) scores, and construction milestones — to reduce information asymmetry. The 2022 “G20 Roadmap for Infrastructure as an Asset Class” set a target of US$1 trillion in private infrastructure investment by 2030, with a focus on renewable energy, digital connectivity, and climate‑resilient transport. The GI Hub’s “Investor Roundtables” (held biannually) have facilitated 42 investment commitments totalling US$84 billion between 2020‑2023, predominantly from pension funds and sovereign wealth funds in Canada, the Netherlands, and Singapore.

Monitoring and accountability are anchored in the “G20 Infrastructure Monitoring Framework”, which tracks 15 core indicators — including project‑level financial close rates, ESG compliance, and job creation — reported annually by the GI Hub to the G20 Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors. The 2023 Independent Review by the OECD’s Development Assistance Committee (DAC) highlighted two gaps: (i) only 38 % of PPF‑supported projects reached financial close within 24 months, and (ii) ESG reporting remains voluntary for private‑sector co‑investors. The G20 has tasked the GI Hub to develop a mandatory ESG disclosure template for all blended‑finance projects by the 2024 Rio de Janeiro Summit.

International Criminal Court Complementarity Principle: State Cooperation, Admissibility Challenges, and the 2023 Review Conference

The complementarity principle, enshrined in Article 17 of the Rome Statute, establishes that the ICC may only exercise jurisdiction when a state with jurisdiction is unwilling or unable genuinely to investigate or prosecute. The admissibility test comprises two limbs: (i) the “same person, same conduct” requirement, and (ii) the “unwillingness or inability” assessment, which examines whether national proceedings are shielded by amnesty laws, lack of independence, or systemic collapse of the judiciary. The Prosecutor’s 2021 “Policy on Complementarity” clarified that a state’s mere enactment of implementing legislation does not satisfy the “genuine” threshold; concrete investigative steps — such as the issuance of arrest warrants, collection of forensic evidence, and witness protection — must be demonstrable.

State cooperation obligations under Part 9 (Articles 86‑102) require parties to arrest and surrender suspects, provide evidence, and protect victims and witnesses. Non‑cooperation has been a persistent obstacle: the 2022 “Situation in the Philippines” saw the ICC Pre‑Trial Chamber issue a warrant for former President Duterte, yet the Philippines — having withdrawn from the Statute in 2019 — refused to cooperate, invoking Article 127 on withdrawal. The Assembly of States Parties (ASP) responded in 2023 by adopting a “Cooperation Enhancement Resolution” that encourages voluntary cooperation from non‑states parties and establishes a “Compliance Monitoring Mechanism” reporting annually to the ASP.

The 2023 Review Conference in The Hague (29 May‑16 June) addressed three complementarity‑related issues: (1) the activation of the crime of aggression for states parties that have ratified the 2010 Kampala Amendments (now 43 states); (2) the budgetary implications of expanding the Court’s field presence to support national jurisdictions; and (3) a proposal to amend Article 17 to introduce a “presumption of unwillingness” where a state has enacted blanket amnesties for international crimes. The Conference adopted a non‑binding “Political Declaration” urging states to strengthen domestic legislation, but deferred any statutory amendment to the 2027 Review Conference. The ICC’s 2024‑2025 budget (€173 million) includes a new “Complementarity Support Unit” to provide technical assistance to national prosecutors, reflecting the Court’s strategic pivot from direct prosecution to catalytic support.
