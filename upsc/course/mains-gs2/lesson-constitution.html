1. Historical Underpinnings

The Indian Constitution did not emerge in 1950 from nothing. It evolved through a century of colonial governance experiments, and several institutional features trace directly to specific British Acts. Knowing this history lets you answer why certain choices were made — not just what they are.

Constitutional Evolution: British Acts (1773–1947)
Act / Year Key Provision Constitutional Legacy Source
Regulating Act, 1773 First parliamentary step to regulate the East India Company; created Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings) and a Supreme Court at Calcutta. Centralised authority under a Governor-General; judicial supremacy introduced. Historical underpinning; colonial precursor to executive centralisation
Pitt’s India Act, 1784 Dual control — Company managed commerce; British government managed politics via a Board of Control. Dual executive idea (nominal vs real) echoes in President vs Prime Minister. Board of Control precedent for political oversight
Charter Acts, 1813 • 1833 • 1853 Ended trade monopoly (except tea/opium); centralised law-making; introduced open competition for civil services (1853). Legislative centralisation; merit-based civil services (foundation of Article 315/320 UPSC/SPSC). Charter Act 1833 — first central legislature
Government of India Act, 1858 After the 1857 revolt, Crown took over; Secretary of State for India ruled through the Governor-General (now Viceroy). Unitary centre under Crown — precedent for strong Union in Article 1. Crown rule; end of Company rule
Indian Councils Act, 1861 Indians entered the Viceroy’s legislative council; legislative powers restored to Bombay and Madras presidencies. Decentralisation seed — evolved into federal distribution (Seventh Schedule). First legislative devolution
Indian Councils Act, 1892 Indirect election; budget discussion rights (no voting on demands). Representative principle — precursor to elected legislature. Budget discussion precedent
Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto) Separate electorates for Muslims; increased Indian membership in councils. Communal representation — later rejected by the Constitution (Article 15/16), but shaped minority safeguards. Separate electorates; communal award context
Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford) Dyarchy in provinces — transferred subjects (Indian ministers) and reserved subjects (British officials). Bicameral central legislature. Provincial autonomy precursor; dyarchy failed but taught power-sharing. Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms
Government of India Act, 1935 Proposed All-India Federation; provincial autonomy; dyarchy at Centre (not implemented); Federal Court; three lists (federal, provincial, concurrent). Administrative bulk of the Constitution (especially Seventh Schedule lists) is drawn from this Act. Primary structural source of the Constitution
Indian Independence Act, 1947 Ended British rule; created dominions of India and Pakistan; made the Constituent Assembly sovereign; ended treaty relations with princely states. Constituent Assembly became the sovereign constitution-making body. Transfer of power; sovereignty

Sources: Government of India Acts (1773–1935); Indian Independence Act, 1947; Constituent Assembly Debates. The Government of India Act, 1935 is the single largest structural source for the Indian Constitution.

Why history wins marks
A GS II answer on federalism that begins: “The strong Centre in Article 1 is not accidental — it reflects the unitary bias inherited from the Government of India Act, 1935, and the trauma of Partition” — scores higher than one that merely defines federalism. History explains design intent.

1.1 The 1857 Revolt as a Constitutional Turning Point

The revolt of 1857 ended Company rule and began direct Crown rule (Government of India Act, 1858). Constitutionally, it shifted sovereignty from a commercial corporation to the British Crown, and created the Secretary of State for India — a political executive in London answerable to Parliament. This chain of accountability (Crown → Secretary of State → Viceroy → provincial governments) is the colonial ancestor of India’s parliamentary executive: real power with the Council of Ministers, nominal authority with the head of state.

1.2 Why the Government of India Act, 1935 Dominates

The 1935 Act ran to 321 sections and 10 schedules. It introduced: (a) provincial autonomy with elected ministries; (b) a Federal Court (ancestor of the Supreme Court); (c) the three-fold distribution of legislative powers (Federal, Provincial, Concurrent Lists) that became the Seventh Schedule; (d) emergency and safeguard provisions; and (e) a public service commission framework. When the Constituent Assembly drafted the Constitution, it adopted most of the administrative machinery wholesale — which is why students of the Constitution must read the 1935 Act alongside it.

The Constituent Assembly was the sovereign body that drafted, debated, and adopted the Constitution. Its composition, procedures, and committee structure determined the final text.

2.2 The Objective Resolution (13 December 1946)

Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru, the Objective Resolution declared India as an independent sovereign republic, guaranteed justice (social, economic, political), liberty (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), equality of status and opportunity, and fraternity assuring dignity of the individual and unity of the nation. It became the philosophical foundation of the Preamble. CAD, 13 Dec 1946

Objective Resolution vs Preamble
The Objective Resolution (1946) used “independent sovereign republic”; the Preamble (1949) adopted “sovereign democratic republic” (socialist, secular added 1976). The Resolution’s “fraternity” survives verbatim. The Preamble is the Resolution’s constitutional enactment.

2.3 Critical Debates Shaping the Text

  • Federalism vs Unitary Bias: The Union Powers Committee (Nehru) recommended residuary powers to the Centre (Article 248), overriding the 1935 Act’s federal distribution. K.T. Shah and H.V. Kamath argued for stronger states; Patel’s pragmatism prevailed. CAD, Vol. II
  • Language: Hindi vs English; Munshi-Ayyangar formula (English for 15 years, Hindi as official language) became Articles 343–351. CAD, Vol. VII
  • Minority Safeguards: Separate electorates rejected (unlike 1909/1919/1935 Acts); replaced with reservation in legislatures (Articles 330, 332) and cultural/educational rights (Articles 29, 30). CAD, Vol. III
  • Judicial Review: Explicitly included (Article 13) despite British parliamentary sovereignty tradition; Ambedkar called it the “heart of the Constitution.” CAD, 25 Nov 1949 (Ambedkar’s closing speech)

3. Preamble

The Preamble is the Constitution’s identity card. It declares the source of authority, the nature of the state, the objectives, and the date of adoption.

3.1 Text and Component Analysis

Preamble Components
Component Phrasing Constitutional Significance Source / Amendment
Source of Authority “We, the people of India” Popular sovereignty; Constitution derives legitimacy from the people, not Crown or Parliament Original (1949)
Nature of State Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic Defines the republic’s character; each adjective is justiciable in context Original + 42nd Amdt (1976) added Socialist, Secular
Objectives Justice (social, economic, political); Liberty (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship); Equality (status, opportunity); Fraternity (dignity, unity, integrity) Directive Principles (Part IV) operationalise justice; Fundamental Rights (Part III) secure liberty/equality; Article 51A(e) codifies fraternity Original (from Objective Resolution)
Date of Adoption 26 November 1949 Constitution adopted; commenced 26 January 1950 (Article 394) Article 394

3.2 Is the Preamble Part of the Constitution? — Judicial Evolution

Preamble Justiciability: Key Judgments
Case Year Holding Significance
Re Berubari Union 1960 Preamble is not part of the Constitution; not a source of power or limitation Advisory opinion on Art. 143; strict textualism
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala 1973 Preamble is part of the Constitution; basic structure doctrine; Preamble aids interpretation 7:6 majority; overruled Berubari
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India 1994 Secularism is part of basic structure; Preamble’s adjectives are enforceable as constitutional values President’s Rule in states; secularism test
Union Government v. LIC of India 1995 Preamble is a key to the makers’ mind; can be used to resolve ambiguity Interpretive tool

3.3 The 42nd Amendment (1976) — “Socialist” and “Secular”

Inserted during the Emergency (1975–77). Kesavananda Bharati (1973) had already held the Preamble amendable subject to basic structure. The 42nd Amendment added “Socialist” and “Secular” and changed “unity of the nation” to “unity and integrity of the nation.” S.R. Bommai (1994) later confirmed secularism as basic structure. 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

Exam trap: “Preamble is not enforceable”
Wrong. Post-Kesavananda, the Preamble is part of the Constitution and its values (secularism, socialism, democracy, republic) are basic structure. However, the Preamble itself does not confer independent rights — it informs interpretation of Parts III and IV. Distinguish: source of power vs interpretive aid.

Preamble answer template
Intro: Quote Preamble; state it is part of Constitution (Kesavananda). Body: (1) Source – “We the people”; (2) Adjectives – sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic with case law; (3) Objectives – justice, liberty, equality, fraternity linked to Parts III/IV/IVA; (4) 42nd Amendment changes; (5) Basic structure. Conclusion: Preamble as interpretive key and constitutional conscience.

4. Salient Features

Salient features questions test whether you can distinguish India’s constitutional design from other models and explain why each feature exists.

4.1 Written, Lengthy, and Detailed

395 Articles (original), 12 Schedules, ~145,000 words — the world’s longest written constitution. Reason: (a) borrowed the Government of India Act, 1935’s administrative detail; (b) incorporated diversity management (linguistic, religious, tribal); (c) included justiciable Directive Principles (unlike Ireland) and Fundamental Duties (unlike USSR/Japan). CAD, Vol. VII (Ambedkar: “We have not merely copied”)

4.2 Borrowed Features — Source Constitutions

4.3 Federalism with Unitary Bias

India is a “Union of States” (Article 1) — not a federation of states. Key unitary features: (1) Single constitution for Centre and States; (2) Single citizenship; (3) Integrated judiciary (Supreme Court at apex); (4) All-India Services (Article 312); (5) Residuary powers to Centre (Article 248); (6) Parliament can alter state boundaries (Article 3); (7) Emergency provisions convert federation into unitary system (Articles 352, 356, 360); (8) Governor appointed by Centre (Article 155); (9) Integrated audit (CAG) and election (ECI).

Quasi-federal: The “amphibian” description
K.C. Wheare called India “quasi-federal”; Granville Austin “cooperative federalism.” S.R. Bommai (1994): “Federalism is basic structure, but the Constitution creates a strong Centre.” In exams, cite both: federal in form (dual polity, written constitution, division of powers, independent judiciary) but unitary in spirit (emergencies, residuary powers, single citizenship, All-India Services).

4.4 Parliamentary System

Executive responsible to legislature; President nominal head (Article 53), real power with Council of Ministers headed by PM (Article 74/75). Collective responsibility (Article 75(3)). Differences from UK: (a) Written constitution; (b) Judicial review; (c) Fixed term (5 years) unless dissolved; (d) Elected President (indirect); (e) Rajya Sabha not subject to dissolution.

4.5 Integrated and Independent Judiciary

Single hierarchy: Supreme Court (Article 124) → High Courts (Article 214) → Subordinate courts. No separate federal/state courts (unlike US). Independence: security of tenure (Art. 124(2)/217(1)), salaries charged on Consolidated Fund (Art. 125/221), appointment by President in consultation with CJI (collegium system post-Third Judges Case, 1998).

5. Union and Its Territory

Articles 1–4 define India’s territorial composition and Parliament’s power to reorganise it. This section is frequently tested for its procedural nuances.

5.1 Article 1 — Name and Territory

“India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.” The territory comprises: (a) territories of the States; (b) Union Territories; (c) territories that may be acquired. Key: “Union of States” (not “Federation of States”) — States have no right to secede. The First Schedule lists States and UTs. Article 1

5.2 Article 2 — Admission/Establishment of New States

Parliament may by law admit new States into the Union or establish new States on such terms as it thinks fit. Examples: Sikkim (36th Amendment, 1975 — admitted as State); Goa (1987); Telangana (2014). Requires simple majority (Article 4 declares such laws not amendments under Article 368). Article 2

5.3 Article 3 — Formation of New States and Alteration of Areas

Parliament may by law: (a) form a new State by separation/union; (b) increase/diminish area; (c) alter boundaries/names. Procedure: (1) Bill introduced in Parliament only on President’s recommendation; (2) President refers to affected State Legislature for views (not consent); (3) Parliament not bound by State’s views; (4) Simple majority. Not an Article 368 amendment (Article 4). Article 3

Article 3 vs Article 368 trap
Article 3 laws (state reorganisation) require simple majority and are not constitutional amendments under Article 368 (per Article 4). But if a law under Article 3 affects federal provisions (e.g., Seventh Schedule), it may need ratification. Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1960): President’s reference is mandatory but State’s view is not binding.

5.4 Article 4 — Supplemental Provisions

Laws under Articles 2/3 may contain supplemental, incidental, and consequential provisions (e.g., amendment of First Schedule, Fourth Schedule, representation in Parliament). Such laws are not deemed amendments under Article 368. Article 4

5.5 Union Territories — Articles 239–241

UTs administered by President through an Administrator (Lieutenant Governor/Chief Commissioner). Delhi and Puducherry have legislatures (Articles 239AA/239A). NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018, 2023): LG bound by aid and advice of CoM except land, police, public order; Parliament retains supreme legislative power over UT. Article 239AA; NCT of Delhi v. UOI (2018) 5 SCC 1; (2023) 5 SCC 1

5.6 Acquired Territories

Territories acquired by India (e.g., Goa 1961, Sikkim 1975, enclaves exchange with Bangladesh 2015 per 100th Amendment) become part of India’s territory under Article 1(c). No separate constitutional provision needed; executive action + Parliamentary law suffices. 100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015 (Land Boundary Agreement)

  • PYQ 2014: “Distinguish between Article 2 and Article 3 of the Constitution.” (10 marks) — PYQ 2014

Article 3 answer template
Intro: Quote Art. 3; state it’s not an Art. 368 amendment (Art. 4). Body: (1) Powers — form, increase, diminish, alter boundaries/names; (2) Procedure — President’s recommendation, State Legislature reference (mandatory but not binding); (3) Simple majority; (4) Babulal Parate (1960) on reference; (5) Examples: Telangana (2014), J&K Reorganisation (2019). Critique: Federal tension — Centre can redraw States unilaterally; contrast with US (State consent required). Conclusion: Strong Centre design intent.

6. Citizenship

Articles 5–11 (Part II) define citizenship at commencement; Parliament regulates subsequent acquisition/termination (Citizenship Act, 1955). Single citizenship for the whole of India (Article 9).

6.3 Key Amendments to the Citizenship Act

  • 1986 Amendment: Restricted birthright citizenship — at least one parent must be citizen (response to Assam agitation). Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1986
  • 2003 Amendment: Introduced “illegal migrant” definition; barred illegal migrants from citizenship by birth/registration/naturalisation; created National Register of Citizens (NRC) framework. Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003
  • 2015 Amendment: Merged PIO and OCI cards into OCI; relaxed residence for registration. Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2015
  • 2019 Amendment (CAA): Exempts Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, Christian migrants from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Pakistan (entered before 31 Dec 2014) from “illegal migrant” definition; reduces naturalisation residence from 11 to 5 years. Constitutional challenge pending (Indian Union Muslim League v. Union of India, SC Constitution Bench). Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019; Writ Petition (Civil) No. 1470/2019

6.4 Judicial Interpretation

  • State of Kerala v. V.V. Giri (1970): Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship terminates Indian citizenship automatically (Article 9). AIR 1970 SC 1505
  • Louis De Raedt v. Union of India (1991): Foreigners have no fundamental right to reside in India; government’s expulsion power is plenary. AIR 1991 SC 1886
  • Assam Sanmilita Mahasangha v. Union of India (2014): Referred Section 6A (Assam Accord citizenship cut-off 24 March 1971) to Constitution Bench; pending. Writ Petition (Civil) No. 562/2012
  • NRC/CAA Context: Supreme Court monitored NRC in Assam (2019); CAA constitutional validity under challenge (Article 14: reasonable classification vs arbitrariness; Article 21: due process). Supreme Court Constitution Bench hearings, 2023–24

6.5 Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)

Not dual citizenship. OCI cardholders: (a) lifelong visa-free travel; (b) parity with NRIs in economic/financial/educational fields; (c) no voting rights, no public office, no agricultural land purchase. Governed by Sections 7A–7D (inserted 2003, amended 2005/2015). Citizenship Act, 1955, Sections 7A–7D

Citizenship answer template
Intro: Single citizenship (Art. 9); Part II (Arts. 5–11) at commencement; Citizenship Act, 1955 thereafter. Body: (1) Arts. 5–8 categories; (2) 1955 Act modes (birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, territory); (3) Key amendments timeline (1986, 2003, 2015, 2019); (4) Termination (Art. 9, Sec. 9); (5) OCI vs dual citizenship; (6) Judicial cases (V.V. Giri, Louis De Raedt, Assam NRC/CAA). Conclusion: Evolution from jus soli to jus sanguinis; current contestation over CAA/NRC.

  • 10-Mark (~150 words): Intro (1 line) → 3–4 bullet points with provision + case law + example → 1-line conclusion. No critique paragraph.

7.5 Model 15-Mark Answer Structure: “Federalism in India”

Question: “The Indian Constitution is federal in form but unitary in spirit. Critically examine.” (15 marks, 2013/2023 pattern)
Intro: Article 1 declares India a “Union of States”; K.C. Wheare: “quasi-federal”; Granville Austin: “cooperative federalism.”

8. Federalism in Practice — Centre-State Relations

The constitutional text (Articles 245–263) provides the architecture; political practice, judicial interpretation, and fiscal realities determine how federalism actually works. This section moves from provisions to lived institutional dynamics.

8.1 Legislative Relations (Articles 245–255)

Distribution of Legislative Powers
List Subjects (Illustrative) Key Articles Judicial Principle
Union List (List I) — 97 subjects Defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, railways, banking, insurance, inter-state trade, citizenship, elections to Parliament Art. 246(1), Seventh Schedule Parliament has exclusive power; state law on Union List subject is void (State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla, 1959)
State List (List II) — 66 subjects Public order, police, public health, agriculture, land, water, local government, prisons, fisheries Art. 246(3), Seventh Schedule State has exclusive power; but Parliament can legislate under Arts. 249, 250, 252, 253
Concurrent List (List III) — 47 subjects Criminal law, marriage, contracts, bankruptcy, forests, education, population control, labour welfare, electricity Art. 246(2), Seventh Schedule Both can legislate; Central law prevails in conflict (Art. 254) Art. 254

Doctrine of Pith and Substance
When a law’s true nature (pith and substance) falls within a legislature’s competence, incidental encroachment on another list is permissible. State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951): Bombay prohibition law valid though it incidentally affected import/export (Union subject). AIR 1951 SC 318
Use this doctrine when a state law is challenged for encroaching on Union List — examine the dominant purpose.
Doctrine of Colourable Legislation
What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly. If a legislature lacks competence, it cannot achieve the same result by labelling the law differently. K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953): Orissa estate abolition law was colourable — real purpose was to evade compensation (Union subject). AIR 1954 SC 568
Substance over form. Look at the true intent and effect, not the legislative label.

8.4 Inter-State Council (Article 263) — Dormant Potential

  • Established: 1990 via Presidential Order on Sarkaria Commission recommendation (Chairman: PM; Members: 6 Union Ministers, all CMs, 5 UTs with legislatures, 5 UTs without).
  • Mandate: Inquire into disputes; discuss subjects of common interest; make recommendations for better coordination.
  • Performance: Only 12 meetings in 34 years (last 2016). Standing Committee (Home Minister + 5 CMs) more active but advisory.
  • Critique: No binding decisions; no independent secretariat; meets only when Centre convenes. Contrast with Australia’s COAG (Council of Australian Governments) — binding intergovernmental agreements.
  • Reform: Punchhi Commission: make ISC a permanent constitutional body with independent secretariat, mandatory annual meetings, and recommendatory opinions binding on Centre in fiscal matters.
Sources: Sarkaria Commission Report (1988), Ch. 12; Punchhi Commission Report (2010), Vol. II; Inter-State Council Secretariat annual reports; S.R. Bommai (1994) on cooperative federalism.

8.5 GST Council (Article 279A) — Fiscal Federalism in Action

GST Council: Structure and Dynamics
Aspect Constitutional Provision Operational Reality
Composition Chair: FM; Members: MoS (Finance), one minister per State (Finance/Tax) States have 2/3 voting weight; Centre has 1/3. Decisions by 3/4 weighted majority.
Voting Art. 279A(9): 3/4 weighted majority; Centre = 1/3, States = 2/3 Centre effectively has veto (1/3 > 1/4). No decision can pass without Centre’s support.
Dispute Resolution Art. 279A(11): Council decides mechanism GST Council Secretariat adjudicates; no independent tribunal yet. States complain of Centre dominating agenda.

GST Council as a Model?
S.R. Bommai (1994) called federalism basic structure. GST Council is the first institutionalised, constitutional, recurring Centre-State bargaining forum. It works by consensus (most decisions unanimous), but the Centre’s veto-weighted voting and control over compensation cess reveal asymmetrical fiscal federalism. For exams: cite GST Council as “cooperative federalism institutionalised” but flag the Centre’s structural veto and compensation cessation as federal tension points. 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016

9. Emergency Provisions & Basic Structure Doctrine

Articles 352–360 convert the federal Constitution into a unitary one. The Basic Structure doctrine (from Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) is the Supreme Court’s self-imposed limit on parliamentary amendment power. Together, they define the Constitution’s elastic limits.

9.1 Emergency Provisions — Articles 352, 356, 360

Three Emergencies: Constitutional Architecture

Financial Emergency Art. 360 Financial stability/credit of India threatened Parliament simple majority within 2 months Indefinite until revoked Centre can direct States on financial propriety; reduce salaries of State/Union judges, All-India Services No FR suspension

Post-Emergency (1975–77) reforms: (1) “Internal disturbance” → “armed rebellion” (Art. 352); (2) Cabinet written advice mandatory (Art. 352); (3) Parliamentary approval in 1 month (not 2); (4) Art. 19 suspended only for war/external aggression, not armed rebellion (Art. 358); (5) Art. 20, 21 never suspendable (Art. 359); (6) President’s Rule max 3 years with conditions (Art. 356); (7) Judicial review of Emergency proclamation restored (Art. 352/356/360). 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978

9.4 Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — The 7:6 Majority That Saved the Constitution

  • Petitioner: Swami Kesavananda Bharati (Edneer Mutt, Kerala) challenged Kerala land reform laws under Art. 26.
  • Core Question: Is Parliament’s amending power under Art. 368 unlimited?
  • Majority (7): Sikri CJ, Shelat, Grover, Hegde, Mukherjea, Jaganmohan Reddy, Khanna JJ. Khanna J’s pivotal view: “Amendment power is wide but not wide enough to destroy the Constitution’s identity.”
  • Minority (6): Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi, Chandrachud JJ: Amending power is plenary.
  • Outcome: 24th, 25th, 29th Amendments upheld subject to basic structure. Golaknath overruled prospectively.
  • Significance: India avoided the “parliamentary sovereignty” trap (UK) and the “rigid constitution” trap (US). The Court became the Constitution’s guardian of identity.
Sources: Kesavananda Bharati (1973) 4 SCC 225; Granville Austin, The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (1999), Ch. 10; M.P. Jain, Indian Constitutional Law (8th ed.).

9.5 Minerva Mills (1980) — Balance as Basic Structure

The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to make Parliament’s amending power absolute (Art. 368(4)–(5)) and give DPSPs primacy over FRs (Art. 31C expanded). Minerva Mills struck down both:

  1. Limited amending power is basic structure: Parliament cannot convert its limited power into unlimited power (Art. 368(4)–(5) destroyed the Kesavananda check).
  2. Harmony between FRs and DPSPs is basic structure: “The Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV. To give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution.” (Chandrachud CJ) (1980) 3 SCC 625
Why Minerva Mills matters for answers
It is the most cited basic structure case after Kesavananda. Use it when asked: (a) Can Parliament amend Art. 368 itself? No — limited amending power is basic structure. (b) FRs vs DPSP conflict? Harmony/balance is basic structure; neither has absolute primacy. (c) Judicial review of amendments? Affirmed as basic structure (Art. 368(4)–(5) struck down).
