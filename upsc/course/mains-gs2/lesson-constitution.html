Key Concepts

Historical Underpinnings

The Indian Constitution did not emerge in 1950 from nothing. It evolved through colonial governance experiments, and several features trace directly to British Acts. Knowing this history helps you answer why certain institutional choices were made.

  • Regulating Act of 1773: First step by Parliament to regulate the East India Company; created the Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings) and a Supreme Court at Calcutta.
  • Pitt’s India Act of 1784: Established dual control — the Company managed commerce, the British government managed politics through a Board of Control.
  • Charter Acts (1813, 1833, 1853): Ended the Company’s trade monopoly (except tea and opium), centralised law-making, and introduced open competition for civil services.
  • Government of India Act 1858: After the 1857 revolt, the Crown took over; the Secretary of State for India ruled through the Governor-General (now Viceroy).
  • Indian Councils Act 1861: Introduced Indian members into the Viceroy’s legislative council and began decentralisation by restoring legislative powers to Bombay and Madras presidencies.
  • Indian Councils Act 1892: Introduced indirect election and budget discussion rights (but not voting on demands).
  • Indian Councils Act 1909 (Morley-Minto): Introduced separate electorates for Muslims and increased Indian membership in councils.
  • Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford): Introduced dyarchy in provinces — transferred subjects (Indian ministers) and reserved subjects (British officials).
  • Government of India Act 1935: The most important precursor — proposed an All-India Federation, provincial autonomy, dyarchy at the Centre (never implemented), and a Federal Court. Much of the Constitution’s administrative part is drawn from it.
  • Indian Independence Act 1947: Ended British rule, created two dominions (India and Pakistan), made Constituent Assembly sovereign, and ended treaty relations with princely states.

Making of the Constitution

  • Constituent Assembly: Set up under the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946). Members elected by provincial assemblies using single transferable vote; princely states nominated representatives. First met 9 December 1946.
  • Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha was temporary chairman; **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** became permanent president.
  • Drafting Committee: Set up 29 August 1947 under **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**. Other members included Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, K.M. Munshi, Mohammad Saadulla, B.L. Mitter, and D.P. Khaitan.
  • Objective Resolution: Moved by Nehru on 13 December 1946; adopted 22 January 1947. It became the Preamble.
  • Committees: Union Powers Committee (Nehru), Union Constitution Committee, Provincial Constitution Committee, Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights and Minorities (Patel), and others.
  • Adoption: The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. Some provisions (citizenship, elections, provisional Parliament) came into force on adoption day itself.

Preamble

The Preamble declares the source of authority (“We the people of India”), the nature of the state (Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic), the objectives (Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity), and the date of adoption (26 November 1949). The words **Socialist, Secular, and Integrity** were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976).

In the **Kesavananda Bharati case (1973)**, the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution and can be amended, but not so as to destroy the basic structure.

Salient Features

  • Lengthiest written constitution: Originally 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules; now over 470 Articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules.
  • Drawn from multiple sources: Government of India Act 1935 (structural), US Constitution (fundamental rights, judicial review), Irish Constitution (Directive Principles), British Constitution (parliamentary system, rule of law), Canadian Constitution (federalism with strong Centre), Australian Constitution (concurrent list), Weimar Constitution (emergency provisions), Soviet Constitution (Fundamental Duties).
  • Parliamentary system: Executive responsible to the legislature; nominal vs real executive (President vs Prime Minister).
  • Federal system with unitary bias: Dual government, division of powers, written constitution, supremacy of Constitution, independent judiciary — but Centre has overriding powers during emergencies.
  • Fundamental Rights: Justiciable rights in Part III (Articles 12–35), enforceable by Supreme Court (Article 32) and High Courts (Article 226).
  • Directive Principles of State Policy: Non-justiciable social and economic goals in Part IV (Articles 36–51), fundamental in governance.
  • Fundamental Duties: Added by the 42nd Amendment (1976), Article 51A; non-justiciable.
  • Secular state: No state religion; equal respect for all religions. The word “Secular” was added to the Preamble in 1976.
  • Independent judiciary: Single integrated judicial system; Supreme Court at the apex; collegium system for appointments.
  • Single citizenship: Only Indian citizenship; no separate state citizenship.
  • Universal adult suffrage: Article 326 — voting age 18 (reduced from 21 by the 61st Amendment, 1988).
  • Emergency provisions: National emergency (Article 352), President’s Rule (Article 356), Financial emergency (Article 360).
  • Three-tier government: Added by 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) — Panchayats and Municipalities.

Union and Its Territory

Article 1: India is a Union of States. States and territories are listed in the First Schedule. The territory of India includes states, union territories, and any acquired territories.

Article 2: Parliament may admit or establish new states. Article 3: Parliament may form new states, alter boundaries, or change names — but a bill affecting a state’s boundaries or name must be referred to that state legislature for its views (not binding on Parliament).

Citizenship

Articles 5–11: Citizenship at commencement (Articles 5–8), rights of migrants (Articles 6–7), persons of Indian origin abroad (Article 8), voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship (Article 9), continuance (Article 10), and Parliament’s power to regulate (Article 11). The Citizenship Act, 1955 governs acquisition and termination — by birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, and incorporation of territory. The CAA, 2019 amended it to provide accelerated citizenship to persecuted minorities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan.

Technique: Historical antecedents
Question: “The Government of India Act, 1935 is the biggest single source of the Indian Constitution. Examine.”
Answer: The 1935 Act contributed federal structure, provincial autonomy, emergency powers, public service commissions, the Federal Court (precursor to Supreme Court), and detailed administrative provisions. Over 250 provisions of the Constitution trace to it. However, the Constitution also introduced novel features absent from the Act — Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, adult suffrage, and a fully elected Constituent Assembly-derived sovereign republic.
Technique: Preamble and basic structure
Question: Can the Preamble be amended under Article 368?
Answer: Yes — the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held the Preamble is part of the Constitution and can be amended under Article 368. However, any amendment cannot alter the basic structure (sovereign, secular, democratic republic). The 42nd Amendment (1976) added Socialist, Secular, and Integrity; this was upheld because it did not destroy the basic structure.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Regulating Act (1773) with Pitt’s India Act (1784) — the latter created dual control, not the former.
  • Thinking the Constituent Assembly was directly elected — it was indirectly elected by provincial assemblies.
  • Believing Directive Principles are justiciable — they are not; Fundamental Rights are.
  • Assuming Article 3 creates states freely — Parliament must consult the affected state legislature (though its opinion is not binding).
  • Missing that citizenship is single — there is no state citizenship in India.

Memory Tricks

  • 1935 Act = Structural skeleton: Federalism, Public Service Commission, emergency, judiciary — all trace here.
  • PREAMBLE: People, Republic, Equality, Justice, Liberty, Fraternity — the soul.
  • SALIENT: Secular, Parliamentary, Federal (unitary bias), Independent judiciary, Single citizenship, Universal adult franchise.
  • ARTICLE 3: Parliament forms states; state legislature consulted (not bound).
Mains exam-day strategy
Always trace constitutional features back to their historical source when asked. For “salient features,” group them into structural (federalism, parliamentary), philosophical (secular, socialist, welfare), and procedural (amendment, emergency). Cite Article numbers and amendment numbers precisely — vague references lose marks.
