Key Concepts

The Constitution of India is not merely a legal document but a living framework that balances unity with diversity, ensuring democratic governance through distinct pillars like federalism, parliamentary sovereignty, and judicial independence. This lesson dissects the historical evolution from colonial rule to a sovereign republic, establishing the precise mechanics of how power is distributed between the Centre and States, and how Parliament, the Judiciary, and Constitutional Bodies function within this architecture. Mastery of these concepts is vital for answering GS II questions on federal disputes, parliamentary procedures, and judicial review.

Historical Underpinnings & Federalism

The Constitution was drafted by the Constituent Assembly between 1946 and 1950, transitioning India from a British Dominion to a Sovereign Republic. The architects chose a "Quasi-Federal" or "Cooperative Federal" structure rather than pure federalism to maintain national unity against the backdrop of partition. This means while states have autonomy, the Centre holds residual powers and can override states in emergencies.

Definition: Federalism is the division of power between a central government and constituent political units (states) where both operate directly on citizens but are bound by a supreme constitution.

The distribution of powers is governed by three specific lists in Part XI of the Constitution:

  • Union List (List I): Contains 97 subjects (e.g., Defence, Foreign Affairs) exclusively under the Centre's jurisdiction. The Parliament can make laws here even if states object.
  • State List (List II): Contains 66 subjects (e.g., Public Order, Police, Agriculture) exclusively under State jurisdiction. States cannot legislate here without Parliamentary consent.
  • Concurrent List (List III): Contains 47 subjects (e.g., Criminal Law, Education) where both Centre and States can make laws. In case of conflict, the Central law prevails over the State law.

The Rule: The Seventh Schedule to the Constitution is the operational map for federal division. If a subject is not listed in any of these three lists, it falls under the "Residuary Powers" which belong exclusively to the Parliament (Centre), ensuring central supremacy in new areas of governance.

Examiner Pattern: Questions often ask about the classification of specific subjects (e.g., "Is 'Education' Union or Concurrent?") or the validity of a State Act when it clashes with a Central Act on a Concurrent subject.

Separation of Powers & Parliament

The Constitution adopts a "Fused" system of separation of powers, unlike the strict US model. This means the Legislature (Parliament) and Executive are not completely separate; the Executive is drawn from the Legislature to ensure accountability.

Definition: The Parliament consists of three houses: Rajya Sabha (Council of States), Lok Sabha (House of the People), and the President. It holds supreme legislative authority.

The composition follows these exact formulas:

  1. Lok Sabha: Maximum strength is 543 members (530 from General Electorates, 20 from Anglo-Indians historically removed in 2008, and up to 2 nominated for Anglos/Other minorities). The minimum age to vote is 18; to contest, it is 25.
  2. Rajya Sabha: Maximum strength is 245 members. Members are elected by an electoral college of MLAs and MPs from states and UTs using proportional representation. There is no fixed term for the house as a whole; one-third members retire every two years, ensuring continuity.

The Rule: A Bill becomes law only after passing through specific stages: Introduction (Motion) -> First Reading (Notice) -> Second Reading (Debate & Committee Stage) -> Third Reading (Final Vote). Money Bills can ONLY be introduced in the Lok Sabha and cannot be amended by Rajya Sabha; they must be returned within 14 days or deemed passed.

Examiner Pattern: Questions frequently test the distinction between a Money Bill and an Ordinary Bill, or the procedure for passing a Constitutional Amendment (which requires Special Majority + Ratification by half state legislatures).

Judiciary & Constitutional Bodies

The Judiciary acts as the guardian of the Constitution, ensuring that laws passed by Parliament do not violate Fundamental Rights. The system is based on Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine.

Definition: Judicial Review is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive actions and declare them void if they violate the Constitution.

The Supreme Court of India has original, appellate, advisory, and writ jurisdictions. The Writ Jurisdiction (Articles 32 & 226) allows the court to issue five specific remedies: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto.

Constitutional Bodies: These are institutions created by the Constitution itself, distinct from statutory bodies created by Acts of Parliament. Key examples include:

  • NITI Aayog: Replaced the Planning Commission in 2015 to foster cooperative federalism through horizontal and vertical engagement.
  • Election Commission: An independent constitutional body responsible for conducting free and fair elections.
  • CAG (Comptroller and Auditor General): The watchdog of the economy, auditing government accounts.

Examiner Pattern: Students must distinguish between Constitutional Bodies (created by Constitution) and Statutory/Advisory Bodies (created by Parliament or Acts). Questions often ask about the tenure of CAG or the composition of NITI Aayog.

Technique: The "List Check" for Federal Subjects
Question: State 'A' passed an Act prohibiting the sale of alcohol in a specific district. The Union Government challenged this, citing Article 14 (Equality) and claiming it interferes with trade. Is this valid? Which List applies?
Solution: First, identify the subject matter: 'Sale of Alcohol'. This falls under Entry 8 of the State List (Intoxicating Liquors). Since it is in the State List, the State has exclusive power. However, if the Union argues it violates Fundamental Rights (Article 14), the Court must check if the law is arbitrary. The Supreme Court has ruled that while States can ban alcohol, they cannot do so in a way that discriminates against specific communities without reason. Key Takeaway: Always start by checking the Seventh Schedule Lists. If it's State List, the Centre cannot legislate unless there is an Emergency or under Article 254 (Union Law prevails).
Technique: The "Bill Type" Flowchart
Question: A Bill is introduced in Rajya Sabha regarding the reduction of taxes. Can it become a law? What if Lok Sabha rejects it?
Solution: First, determine if it is a Money Bill or Ordinary Bill. Since it deals with "reduction of taxes" (Entry 97 in Union List), it is a Money Bill. Rule Application: A Money Bill CANNOT be introduced in Rajya Sabha; it must start in Lok Sabha. Therefore, the premise of the question is flawed procedurally. If we assume an Ordinary Bill regarding taxes: It passes Rajya Sabha -> Goes to Lok Sabha -> If Lok Sabha rejects it (no amendment allowed for Money Bills, but rejection is possible for Ordinary Bills), the Bill dies unless Rajya Sabha insists and President agrees. Key Takeaway: Always check the subject matter first. Taxes = Union List = Money Bill characteristics. Procedure dictates Lok Sabha supremacy in financial matters.

Common Traps

  • Mistake 1: Confusing "Federal" with "Unitary". India is Quasi-Federal. Students often assume States can never be overridden; however, under Article 356 (President's Rule), the Centre can take over state administration temporarily.
  • Mistake 2: Thinking Rajya Sabha has equal power on Money Bills. It does not. It can only recommend or return a Money Bill within 14 days; otherwise, it is deemed passed in original form.
  • Mistake 3: Assuming all Constitutional Bodies are independent of Parliament. While they function independently, their creation and some amendments (like changing the Election Commission's composition) require Parliamentary Acts or Constitutional Amendments.

Memory Tricks

  • Mnemonic for Lists: Remember "S-C-U" order: State List comes first in logic for local issues, Concurrent for shared, Union for national security. Use the phrase "Union is Upper" (Residuary powers are with Union).
  • Bill Procedure: Think "Lok Sabha Leads on Money". If a bill has 'Rupees' or 'Tax', it starts in Lok Sabha.
  • Judiciary Types: Remember the 5 Writs as "H-M-P-C-Q" (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto). Associate Habeas with 'Freeing' a prisoner, and Quo Warranto with 'Who has the authority?'
Exam-Day Strategy for Polity
Strategy: When answering MCQs on Federalism, always look for keywords like "Residuary Powers" (Answer: Union) or "Concurrent List" (Answer: Central Law Prevails). For Judiciary questions, immediately check if the question asks about Original Jurisdiction (usually Centre vs State disputes) or Writ Jurisdiction (Individual rights). If a question mentions a specific body like NITI Aayog, remember it is NOT a Constitutional Body but a Statutory one created by an Act. Always prioritize Fundamental Rights over Directive Principles in case of conflict, as per Article 37.
