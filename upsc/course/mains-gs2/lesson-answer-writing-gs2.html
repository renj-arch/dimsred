Key Concepts

Directive Words in GS II

Directive words tell you what the examiner wants — describe, analyse, critically examine, discuss, evaluate, comment, or illustrate. Misreading the directive is the most common reason for lost marks in Mains GS II.

  • Discuss: Present arguments for and against, then conclude. A debate-style answer with multiple perspectives.
  • Analyse: Break the topic into components and explain how they relate. Example: “Analyse the federal features of the Indian Constitution” — list features (dual polity, Seventh Schedule, independent judiciary, written constitution) and show how each works.
  • Critically analyse / Critically examine: Analyse plus judge merits and demerits. Must include a balanced view — strengths, weaknesses, and a reasoned conclusion. Neutral tone; no unsupported ranting.
  • Evaluate / Assess: Weigh the value or impact. “Evaluate the effectiveness of MGNREGA” — coverage, wages, asset creation, leakages, and whether it reduced distress migration. Give a verdict with justification.
  • Comment: Give your informed opinion on a statement or issue. Shorter than “discuss”; take a position and support it.
  • Examine: Probe deeply into causes, effects, and implications. More analytical than descriptive.
  • Illustrate: Use examples, case studies, or data to clarify a point. “Illustrate with examples how judicial review works in India” — Kesavananda Bharati, Maneka Gandhi, Coelho, etc.
  • Enumerate / List: Simply list items without elaboration. Rare as a standalone directive in GS II.
  • Justify: Provide reasons in favour of a position or decision.
  • Compare and contrast: Show similarities and differences between two systems, schemes, or judgments (e.g., Indian vs US federalism).

Answer Structure

A high-scoring GS II answer follows a consistent architecture. The 250-word limit (15-mark question) or 150-word limit (10-mark question) demands discipline.

  • Introduction (15–20% of words): Define the core term, cite the constitutional article or recent context, and state your line of argument. Example: For “Article 356,” start with “Article 356 empowers the President to impose President’s Rule when constitutional machinery in a state has failed.”
  • Body (60–70% of words): Organise into 3–4 thematic paragraphs or bullet clusters. Use subheadings if the question is multi-part. Each paragraph: one idea, evidence (article number, scheme, judgment, data), and linkage back to the question.
  • Conclusion (10–15% of words): Forward-looking and balanced. Suggest reforms (2nd ARC, Punchhi Commission, Law Commission reports), cite the way forward, or restate the core finding. Never introduce a new fact here.

Multi-part questions: If a question has two parts (e.g., “What are the issues? Suggest measures”), divide the body accordingly and label them clearly. GS II questions often combine constitutional provisions with contemporary governance.

Content Enrichment for GS II

  • Constitutional anchoring: Cite Articles — 324 (Election Commission), 315–323 (UPSC/SPSC), 280 (Finance Commission), 148 (CAG), 352/356/360 (emergencies), 245–255 (legislative relations), 268–281 (financial relations), 368 (amendment), 74/75 (Council of Ministers), 53 (executive power of President), 123 (ordinances).
  • Schedules: Seventh Schedule (Union, State, Concurrent Lists), Tenth Schedule (anti-defection), Eleventh (Panchayats), Twelfth (Municipalities).
  • Judgments: Kesavananda Bharati (basic structure), Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (election dispute), S.R. Bommai (President’s Rule), Coelho (Ninth Schedule), Navtej Singh Johar (Section 377), Joseph Shine (adultery), Puttaswamy (privacy), Anuradha Bhasin (internet shutdowns).
  • Commissions: 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission, Punchhi Commission (Centre-state relations), Sarkaria Commission (Centre-state relations), L.M. Singhvi (local governance), N.N. Vohra (criminalisation of politics).
  • Schemes and institutions: MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJAY, Citizens Charter, Sevottam, e-Shram, MyGov, CIC, Lokpal, CVC, CAG, NCSC, NCST, NCBC.
  • Data: NITI Aayog MPI, NFHS-5, PLFS, NFSA coverage figures, election turnout, pendency in courts (National Judicial Data Grid).
  • Diagrams: Flowcharts for centre-state relations, separation of powers, or election process; tables for scheme comparisons. Draw neatly and label.

Word Limit and Time Management

  • 10-mark question: ~150 words, ~7 minutes.
  • 15-mark question: ~250 words, ~10 minutes.
  • Total GS II paper: 20 questions, 250 marks, 3 hours.

Stick to word limits — over-writing one question starves another. Use bullet points for readability but keep paragraphs for narrative directives (discuss, critically examine). Leave margins for additional points if you remember them mid-flow.

Common Traps

  • Writing a generic introduction (“In today’s globalised world…”) instead of addressing the specific question.
  • Ignoring the directive — describing when asked to critically evaluate.
  • No constitutional anchoring — opinions without article/scheme references score low in GS II.
  • One-sided answers — “critically examine” demands both sides; “evaluate” demands a verdict.
  • Exceeding word limits on early questions and rushing the last ones.
  • No conclusion — every answer must end with a forward-looking or balanced closing line.
  • Illegible handwriting or cluttered layout — the examiner scans hundreds of scripts; clarity wins.

Memory Tricks

  • Directive map: Discuss = for-and-against; Critically examine = merits-demerits + judgment; Evaluate = weigh + verdict; Comment = informed opinion.
  • Body rule: One idea per paragraph; one evidence per idea.
  • Conclusion: Reform + way forward + one line.
  • GS II toolkit: Article + Scheme + Judgment + Commission + Data — sprinkle 2–3 per answer.
Technique: Answering a 15-mark critically examine question
Question: “Critically examine the role of the Governor in state politics.”
Answer: Intro — Governor is the constitutional head (Article 153) and Centre’s representative (Article 154–163). Body — discretionary powers (Article 200, reserving bills), appointment of CM (hung Assembly), recommendation of President’s Rule (Article 356), and Chancellor role in universities. Criticism — partisan appointments, delay in assent, acting on Centre’s direction (S.R. Bommai limits discretion). Conclusion — Governor should act on aid and advice except in clearly discretionary areas; Punchhi Commission reforms (fixed tenure, removal by impeachment) should be implemented.
Technique: Diagram in GS II
Question: Explain the distribution of legislative powers between Centre and states.
Answer: Draw a three-box diagram — Union List (97 subjects: defence, foreign affairs, currency), State List (66 subjects: police, public health, agriculture), Concurrent List (47 subjects: education, marriage, adoption). Add residual powers with Parliament (Article 248). State that Parliament prevails on Concurrent List (Article 254), and during emergency Parliament can legislate on State List (Article 250).

Common Traps

  • Repeating the question in the introduction instead of defining terms.
  • Using first person (“I believe”) — maintain an objective, administrative tone.
  • Citing wrong article numbers — verify before writing.
  • No way forward — every GS II answer should end with reform suggestions.
  • Mixing up directives — “analyse” ≠ “comment” ≠ “evaluate.”

Memory Tricks

  • IBC: Introduction, Body, Conclusion — every answer.
  • ASJCD: Article, Scheme, Judgment, Commission, Data — enrich every body paragraph.
  • DIR: Define, Illustrate, Relate — structure for conceptual answers.
Mains exam-day strategy
Read the directive word twice before writing. Allocate time strictly (15-mark = 10 minutes). Use black ink for main text, blue only for subheadings if permitted. Draw one diagram per paper if relevant — it breaks monotony. Attempt all 20 questions; a partially answered question earns more than a blank one. In the last 10 minutes, complete any incomplete answers in point form rather than leaving blanks.
