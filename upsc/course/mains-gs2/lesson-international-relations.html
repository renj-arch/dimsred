Section 1: Principles & Evolution of Indian Foreign Policy

1.1 Constitutional and Historical Foundations

Article 51 of the Constitution (Directive Principles) mandates the State to "promote international peace and security," "maintain just and honourable relations between nations," "foster respect for international law and treaty obligations," and "encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration." While non-justiciable, Article 51 provides the constitutional vocabulary for India's foreign policy.

The historical foundations trace to the **Nehruvian consensus** (1947-1964): non-alignment, anti-colonialism, Afro-Asian solidarity, and faith in the UN. The 1962 Sino-Indian war shattered illusions of "Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai" and forced a strategic rethink — leading to defence modernisation, the 1971 Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation with the USSR, and the 1974 Pokhran-I nuclear test.

1.4 Evolution of Strategic Autonomy: From Non-Alignment to Multi-Alignment

The term "strategic autonomy" replaced "non-alignment" in official vocabulary post-1998. The distinction is critical for Mains:

  • Non-Alignment (1947-1991): Ideological — refusing to join either Cold War bloc. Moral high ground but limited leverage. NAM became a "talk shop" after Cold War.
  • Strategic Autonomy 1.0 (1998-2014): Post-Pokhran — nuclear weapon state status as autonomy enabler. Civil Nuclear Deal (2008) — US recognised India as "responsible nuclear power" without NPT/CTBT signature. India retained Russia defence ties while deepening US engagement.
  • Strategic Autonomy 2.0 / Multi-Alignment (2014-present): Issue-based, not bloc-based. India joins Quad (security), BRICS (Global South economics), SCO (regional security), I2U2 (West Asia minilateral), G20 (global governance) — simultaneously. The "coalition of the willing" approach.
PYQ 2019 GS2 Q3
"'Non-alignment' has lost its relevance in the contemporary world order." Critically examine this statement in the context of India's foreign policy evolution. (15 marks)
Model Answer Structure for PYQ 2019 Q3
  1. Introduction (40 words): Non-alignment (NAM) was a Cold War strategy — refusing bloc membership. Post-1991, India shifted to "strategic autonomy" → "multi-alignment." NAM as an *institution* is moribund; the *principle* (independent judgment) endures.
  2. Why NAM Lost Relevance (100 words): Cold War ended (1991) — no blocs to avoid. NAM expanded to 120 members — consensus paralysis. No collective action on Iraq, Libya, Syria, Ukraine. India's interests diverged from NAM majority (e.g., nuclear, trade, China). NAM summits ritualistic.
  3. Strategic Autonomy: The Enduring Core (120 words): India never abandoned independent decision-making. 1998 nuclear tests — defied US pressure. 2008 Nuclear Deal — secured NSG waiver *without* signing NPT. 2022 Ukraine — abstained at UN, bought Russian oil, kept S-400, deepened iCET with US. "Multi-alignment" = issue-based partnerships (Quad for Indo-Pacific, BRICS for Global South, SCO for Eurasia, I2U2 for West Asia).
  4. Nuance: Constraints on Autonomy (100 words): Economic interdependence (China trade deficit $85B+ FY23; US largest export market). Technology dependence (semiconductors, defence). Climate finance needs. UNSC reform blocked by China. Autonomy is *relative* — exercised *within* structural constraints.
  5. Conclusion (40 words): NAM the *movement* is obsolete; strategic autonomy the *principle* is India's permanent foreign policy DNA. The vocabulary changed from "non-alignment" to "multi-alignment" — but the refusal to outsource national interest remains constant.

Section 2: Neighbourhood Relations — Strategic Challenges & Diplomatic Frameworks

2.1 The Neighbourhood First Policy: Architecture and Implementation

Launched as a formal doctrine in 2014 (PM Modi's SAARC Summit speech, Kathmandu), "Neighbourhood First" operationalises the Indira Doctrine (1971) — regional primacy as non-negotiable. It has three pillars:

  1. Development Partnerships: Lines of Credit (LoCs), grants, capacity building (ITEC, ICCR scholarships). India's development assistance: ~$30 billion+ since 2014 (LoCs + grants) (Source: MEA Annual Reports; Exim Bank LoC Database).
  2. Connectivity Infrastructure: Cross-border rail (Jay Nagar-Bijalpura, Kurtha-Bijaypura, Haldibari-Chilahati), inland waterways (Protocol on Inland Water Transit & Trade with Bangladesh), energy grids (India-Bangladesh 1,600 MW, India-Nepal 400 kV Dhalkebar-Muzaffarpur), digital links (NKN to Bhutan/Nepal, BharatNet extension).
  3. Security & People-to-People: Joint military exercises (Surya Kiran-Nepal, Sampriti-Bangladesh, Mitra Shakti-Sri Lanka), coastal surveillance radar chains, visa liberalisation (e-visa for 160+ countries, SAARC visa exemption scheme), cultural exchanges (Buddhist circuit, Ramayan circuit).
Mains Keyword: "Asymmetric Federalism in Neighbourhood Policy"
India's neighbourhood policy is *asymmetric* — differentiated engagement based on each neighbour's strategic weight, regime type, and India's leverage. Bhutan = special relationship (hydro, security, currency peg). Nepal = open border, treaty obligations, hydropower. Bangladesh = treaty-based (LBA 2015), water sharing, connectivity. Sri Lanka = 13th Amendment pressure, economic aid, China counter. Maldives = "India First" toggle. Myanmar = security/Act East pivot, FMR review. Pakistan = terror = no talks. Afghanistan = humanitarian engagement, no Taliban recognition. In answers, show you understand this differentiation — don't treat "neighbourhood" as a monolith.

Solved Mini-Example: China Boundary Question
Question: "The India-China boundary dispute is not just about territory but about strategic dominance in Asia." Examine with reference to the post-Galwan trajectory. (10 marks)
Structure: (1) Galwan (June 2020) broke 45-year no-bloodshed norm — signalling China's willingness to use force for LAC revision. (2) Territory = leverage: Aksai Chin connects Xinjiang-Tibet (G219 highway); Arunachal = Tibetan Buddhism/Tawang leverage. (3) Post-Galwan: India moved from "boundary question" to "LAC crisis" framing — military mirror deployment, infrastructure acceleration (DSDBO, tunnels, 66 strategic roads), Quad deepening, economic decoupling (Apps ban, FDI curbs, PLI). (4) Strategic dominance: China seeks to confine India to South Asia; India's Indo-Pacific/Quad/SAGAR pushes beyond. (5) Conclusion: Boundary is the *manifestation*; Asian balance of power is the *cause*. Resolution requires political settlement — currently blocked by asymmetry in resolve and capability.

2.2.3 Nepal: Treaty Obligations and Trust Deficit

  • 1950 Peace and Friendship Treaty: Open border, national treatment, Indian security guarantee. Article 2: "Neither government shall tolerate any threat to the security of the other by a foreign aggressor." Nepal sees this as unequal; seeks revision.
  • Kalapani/Lipulekh Dispute: Nepal's 2020 map claiming Limpiyadhura, Lipulekh, Kalapani (370 sq km). India inaugurated Lipulekh road (May 2020) — Nepal protested. Boundary Working Group (BWG) meetings stalled.
  • Hydropower & Connectivity: India-Nepal Power Exchange Agreement (2022) — India to import 10,000 MW over 10 years. Cross-border rail: Jaynagar-Bijalpura (operational), Raxaul-Kathmandu (survey), Kerung-Kathmandu (China BRI).
  • Political Dimension: China's political inroads (CCP-Nepal Communist Party ties); "India Out" narrative in sections. India's response: High-level visits (PM 2014, 2018, 2022; EM 2023), LoCs ($1.65B+), vaccine diplomacy, Buddhist circuit.

2.2.4 Bhutan: The Special Relationship

  • 2007 Treaty Revision: Removed Article 2 (guidance on foreign policy); Bhutan sovereign in external relations. India remains security guarantor.
  • Hydropower: 2,000+ MW operational (Chukha, Kurichhu, Tala, Mangdechhu); 2,120 MW under construction (Punatsangchhu I & II, Kholongchhu). India buys surplus power; Bhutan's largest revenue source.
  • Doklam Tri-junction: Bhutan's sovereignty central to India's Siliguri Corridor security. India-Bhutan boundary talks (24 rounds) — Doklam unresolved.
  • China Factor: China-Bhutan boundary talks (25 rounds, 2023 MoU on "Three-Step Roadmap") — India watches closely. Any China-Bhutan deal on Doklam affects India directly.

2.2.5 Bangladesh: The Model Partnership

  • Land Boundary Agreement (2015): 111 Indian enclaves in Bangladesh, 51 Bangladeshi enclaves in India exchanged; residents chose citizenship. Constitutional 100th Amendment.
  • Teesta Water Sharing: 1983 ad hoc (39% India, 36% Bangladesh, 25% reserved). 2011 draft (37.5% each) — stalled due to West Bengal opposition. Critical for Bangladesh's lean season.
  • Connectivity: BBIN Motor Vehicle Agreement (Bhutan opted out); Protocol on Inland Water Transit & Trade (PIWTT); Akhaura-Agartala rail (inaugurated 2023); Maitree Express, Bandhan Express.
  • Security: Coordinated counter-insurgency (ULFA, NDFB camps flushed); Rohingya crisis — India provided relief, pressed Myanmar for repatriation.
  • Trade: $18B+ (FY23); India's largest export destination in South Asia. Duty-free access for Bangladesh (SAFTA LDC provisions).

2.2.6 Sri Lanka: The 13th Amendment and China's Footprint

  • 13th Amendment (1987): Indo-Lanka Accord — Provincial Councils, devolution of powers. India consistently presses for "full implementation" (including police/land powers). Sri Lanka: "13A Plus" — but no progress.
  • Economic Crisis (2022): India provided $4 billion support (credit lines, currency swap, fuel, fertilizer, medicines) — largest bilateral assistance. IMF programme supported.
  • China: Hambantota Port (99-year lease, 2017 — debt-for-equity); Colombo Port City (CIFC); BRI participation. India counters: Trincomalee oil tank farm (IOC), West Container Terminal (Adani), renewable energy projects, digital connectivity (UPI launched 2024).
  • Katchatheevu: 1974/1976 Agreements — ceded islet to Sri Lanka; fishing rights for Indian fishermen. Tamil Nadu demands retrieval. Periodic arrests of Indian fishermen by SL Navy.

2.2.7 Myanmar: The Act East Pivot and Security Nexus

  • Strategic Importance: Land bridge to Southeast Asia; border management (1,643 km, 4 NE states); insurgency management (NSCN-K, ULFA camps historically in Sagaing/Chin).
  • Free Movement Regime (FMR): 16 km border movement for hill tribes. India reviewing — fencing 1,643 km (10% done); FMR revision to 10 km / permit system.
  • Connectivity: Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport (Sittwe Port — operational 2023; Paletwa-Zorinpui road delayed); India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway (IMTTH — 1,360 km, 70% done); Rhi-Tiddim road.
  • Rohingya: India: "Illegal immigrants" (40,000+ in Jammu, Hyderabad, Delhi); not party to 1951 Refugee Convention. Humanitarian aid to Bangladesh (Operation Insaniyat 2017); development aid to Rakhine (India-Myanmar Rakhine State Development Programme).
  • Post-Coup (Feb 2021): India: "Deep concern"; engages SAC (junta) for border security/connectivity; supports ASEAN 5-Point Consensus; balances democracy rhetoric with strategic realism.

2.2.8 Afghanistan: Humanitarian Engagement Without Recognition

  • Investment: ~$3 billion (Salma Dam, Parliament, Zaranj-Delaram Highway, Stor Palace, 400+ projects).
  • Post-Taliban (Aug 2021): Operation Devi Shakti — evacuated 670+ (Indians, Afghans, Sikhs/Hindus). Technical team in Kabul (June 2022) for humanitarian coordination.
  • Position: No recognition of Taliban regime; humanitarian aid (wheat 50,000 MT via Chabahar/UN; medicines, COVID vaccines); UNSC Resolution 2593 (2021) — terror-free soil, inclusive govt, women's rights. Chabahar Port (Iran) as alternate access — 10-year MoU signed (2024).

Common Traps

  • Confusing QUAD (security/Indo-Pacific) with BRICS (economic/Global South) — different memberships and agendas.
  • Assuming India's UNSC bid is supported by all P5 — China opposes it; others support conditionally.
  • Thinking non-alignment means neutrality on every issue — India now takes position-based stances (e.g., condemning terrorism, supporting territorial integrity).
  • Missing that the Indus Waters Treaty survives wars — it survived 1965, 1971, and 1999 conflicts.
  • Overlooking that IMEC is an economic corridor, not a military alliance.
  • Confusing LAC (Line of Actual Control) with LoC (Line of Control) — LAC = India-China; LoC = India-Pakistan.
  • Thinking the 13th Amendment is fully implemented in Sri Lanka — it is not; police/land powers remain with Centre.
  • Assuming Bangladesh Teesta agreement is signed — it is not; West Bengal opposes.
  • Missing that Chabahar Port is in *Iran*, not Afghanistan — it provides India access *to* Afghanistan bypassing Pakistan.
  • Confusing FMR (Free Movement Regime) with visa-free travel — FMR is 16 km border zone for hill tribes only; under review.

Mains Exam-Day Strategy for International Relations
For IR questions: (1) Structure by bilateral, regional, multilateral levels. (2) Cite *specific agreements* (LEMOA, COMCASA, BECA, LBA 2015, IWT 1960, 13th Amendment, PESA, FRA, BIMSTEC Charter). (3) Reference *recent summits* (G20 2023, Quad Leaders' Summit, SCO, BRICS, Voice of Global South). (5) Mental maps: LAC sectors (Western, Middle, Eastern), McMahon Line, Sir Creek, Katchatheevu, Siliguri Corridor, Chabahar, Sittwe. (6) Always conclude with India's strategic autonomy as the unifying theme — issue-based coalitions, not bloc politics.

Critique: Soft Power — Potential vs Institutionalisation
(1) Fragmented architecture — MEA (ICCR, ITEC, Diaspora), Culture Ministry (ASI, Festivals, Museums), AYUSH (Yoga, Ayurveda), I&B (Films, Prasar Bharati), MeitY (DPI), Tourism — no single soft power coordinator; China has Confucius Institute HQ (Hanban/CLEC) + United Front Work Dept. (2) Metrics absent — no Indian Soft Power Index; Portland/USC indices rank India 27-30; no outcome measurement (alumni tracking, cultural impact, DPI adoption). (3) Funding — ICCR ₹250 cr/yr, ITEC ₹300 cr/yr vs China $1B+ (Confucius + Belt & Road cultural), British Council £200M, Goethe Institut €400M, Alliance Française €300M. (4) Diaspora vulnerabilities — Khalistan (Canada, UK, US), temple attacks, consular access delays, dual citizenship demand (not permitted). (5) Development diplomacy — EXIM Bank LoC delays (avg 3-5 years from signing to disbursement); project monitoring weak; tied aid (75% Indian content) criticised; no independent evaluation mechanism.
