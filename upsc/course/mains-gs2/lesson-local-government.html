1. Constitutional Foundations: The 73rd and 74th Amendments

1.1 Historical Background and Legislative Intent

The drive to institutionalise local self‑government in India gathered momentum after the recommendations of the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee in 1957, which advocated a three‑tier system of democratic decentralisation. Although several states enacted their own Panchayat Acts thereafter, the absence of a uniform constitutional mandate led to wide variations in structure, tenure and powers. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, and the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, were therefore enacted to give a mandatory constitutional status to rural and urban local bodies respectively, ensuring regular elections, reservation of seats for scheduled castes, scheduled tribes and women, and a fixed five‑year term. The amendments were rooted in the Directive Principles of State Policy, particularly Article 40, which exhorts the State to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self‑government.

1.2 Salient Features of the 73rd Amendment

The 73rd Amendment inserted Part IX into the Constitution, comprising Articles 243 to 243‑O. It provides for a three‑tier Panchayati Raj system in every state having a population of more than twenty lakh, with Gram Panchayats at the village level, Panchayat Samitis at the intermediate block level and Zilla Parishads at the district level. The amendment mandates direct elections to all seats, reservation of not less than one‑third of the total seats for women, and proportionate reservation for scheduled castes and scheduled tribes. It also establishes a State Election Commission to supervise elections and a State Finance Commission to review the financial position of panchayats every five years. The Governor is empowered to dissolve a panchayat before the expiry of its term only on the grounds specified in the Constitution, thereby protecting the tenure of elected representatives.

1.3 Salient Features of the 74th Amendment

The 74th Amendment inserted Part IXA, Articles 243‑P to 243‑ZG, to provide a constitutional framework for urban local bodies. It defines three types of municipalities: Nagar Panchayats for transitional areas, Municipal Councils for smaller urban areas and Municipal Corporations for larger urban areas. Like the rural amendment, it mandates direct elections, reservation for women, scheduled castes and scheduled tribes, a five‑year term, and the constitution of a State Election Commission and a State Finance Commission. Additionally, it provides for the constitution of Ward Committees in municipalities with a population of three lakh or more, and a District Planning Committee to consolidate plans prepared by panchayats and municipalities. The amendment also empowers the state legislature to endow municipalities with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self‑government.

2. Three‑Tier Panchayati Raj Structure

2.1 Gram Panchayat at the Village Level

The Gram Panchayat is the foundation of the Panchayati Raj system and operates at the village or group of villages level. It consists of a Sarpanch, who is directly elected by the voters of the Gram Sabha, and a number of elected members called Panchs. The Gram Sabha, comprising all registered voters in the village, acts as the general body and meets at least twice a year to approve plans, budgets and audit reports. The Gram Panchayat is entrusted with functions such as sanitation, drinking water supply, maintenance of village roads, street lighting, and implementation of centrally sponsored schemes like the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act. Its executive powers are exercised through standing committees for finance, works, education and health, which enable a degree of specialisation in decision‑making.

2.2 Panchayat Samiti at the Block Level

The Panchayat Samiti, also known as the Block Panchayat, functions at the intermediate level covering a group of Gram Panchayats within a development block. Its members are indirectly elected by and from the elected members of the constituent Gram Panchayats, and the chairperson is elected from among the members. The Samiti coordinates the development plans of the Gram Panchayats, supervises the implementation of schemes that span multiple villages, and acts as a link between the district level Zilla Parishad and the village level. It also manages resources such as block‑level schools, primary health centres and minor irrigation works, and prepares the block development plan for submission to the Zilla Parishad.

2.3 Zilla Parishad at the District Level

The Zilla Parishad is the apex tier of the rural local government structure and covers the entire district. Its composition includes directly elected members from territorial constituencies, members of the Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assembly representing the district, and chairpersons of the Panchayat Samitis within the district. The President of the Zilla Parishad is elected from among the directly elected members. The Zilla Parishad prepares the district development plan by integrating the plans of the Panchayat Samitis and Gram Panchayats, allocates resources for district‑level infrastructure such as major roads, hospitals and secondary schools, and monitors the implementation of centrally sponsored programmes. It also exercises supervisory authority over the lower tiers, ensuring conformity with state policies and financial norms.

Example Question
Explain how the three‑tier Panchayati Raj structure facilitates the principle of subsidiarity in rural governance. Illustrate your answer with reference to the functional domain of each tier.
The principle of subsidiarity holds that decisions should be taken at the lowest level of government capable of addressing them effectively. The Gram Panchayat, being closest to the citizen, handles functions that directly affect daily life such as sanitation, drinking water and village roads. The Panchayat Samiti aggregates village‑level needs into block‑level plans, managing services that require coordination across several villages, for example block‑level health centres and minor irrigation. The Zilla Parishad, at the district level, deals with larger infrastructure and inter‑block resource allocation, such as district hospitals, major roads and secondary education. By assigning distinct but complementary functions to each tier, the structure ensures that governance is neither overly centralised nor fragmented, embodying subsidiarity in practice.
Exam Insight
When a question asks for the “functional domain” of each tier, avoid merely listing subjects from the Eleventh Schedule. Instead, map each subject to the tier that actually implements it on the ground, citing the constitutional provision (Article 243G) that empowers the state legislature to devolve powers. This demonstrates both textual knowledge and practical understanding.

2.4 Gram Sabha, Elections and Reservation of Seats

The three tiers are not merely administrative units; they rest on a base of direct democracy. Article 243A provides that the Gram Sabha, consisting of all persons registered in the electoral rolls of a village, may exercise such powers and perform such functions at the village level as the legislature of the State may by law provide, and under PESA the Gram Sabha has special powers over minor minerals, land alienation and forest produce in Scheduled Areas. Article 243C prescribes the composition of Panchayats, and Article 243D requires reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population and not less than one-third of the total seats for women, with similar reservations for the offices of Chairperson. Article 243E fixes a term of five years and requires elections before the term expires, Article 243K vests the superintendence of elections in a State Election Commission, and Article 243F prescribes disqualifications for membership. These provisions give the Panchayati Raj system a constitutional floor of regular elections, reserved representation and popular participation that earlier committee-based arrangements lacked.

The same constitutional logic of regular elections, reserved representation and independent election machinery applies to urban areas. Articles 243Q to 243ZC constitute three types of Municipality — a Nagar Panchayat for a transitional area, a Municipal Council for a smaller urban area and a Municipal Corporation for a larger urban area — and Articles 243G and 243W carry the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules that list the subjects devolved to Panchayats and Municipalities respectively. The 73rd and 74th Amendments of 1992 thus created a single, symmetrical design of local self-government for rural and urban India, each supported by its own State Finance Commission under Articles 243I and 243Y.

3. Functions, Powers and Devolution of Responsibilities

3.1 Constitutional Distribution of Subjects

Article 243G of the Constitution authorises the state legislature to endow Panchayats with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self‑government. The Eleventh Schedule, inserted by the 73rd Amendment, lists twenty‑nine subjects that may be devolved to Panchayats, including agriculture, land improvement, minor irrigation, animal husbandry, fisheries, social forestry, small scale industries, rural housing, drinking water, fuel and fodder, roads, rural electrification, non‑conventional energy, poverty alleviation programmes, education, primary health centres, women and child development, and public distribution system. The actual devolution, however, depends on the respective state Panchayat Acts, which specify the subjects transferred, the level of Panchayat to which they are assigned, and the accompanying financial and administrative powers.

3.2 Activity Mapping and the Role of State Legislation

Activity mapping is the process by which each function listed in the Eleventh Schedule is broken down into specific activities and assigned to the appropriate tier of Panchayat. For instance, primary education may be split into school construction (Zilla Parishad), teacher recruitment (Panchayat Samiti) and school management committees (Gram Panchayat). State governments are required to enact legislation that not only transfers functions but also provides the necessary staff, funds and functionaries. The effectiveness of devolution is therefore contingent on the clarity of activity mapping, the adequacy of fiscal transfers, and the capacity of Panchayats to absorb and utilise transferred responsibilities. In many states, the absence of detailed activity mapping has resulted in overlapping jurisdictions and accountability gaps.

3.3 Challenges in Effective Devolution

Despite the constitutional mandate, several structural challenges impede genuine devolution. First, many states have not transferred all twenty‑nine subjects, retaining key functions such as land revenue, police and higher education at the state level. Second, the financial autonomy of Panchayats remains limited because own‑source revenue bases are narrow and state grants are often tied to specific schemes, reducing discretionary spending. Third, the bureaucratic apparatus at the district and block levels continues to be controlled by state government officers, leading to a dual accountability that undermines the authority of elected representatives. Fourth, capacity constraints — inadequate technical staff, lack of training, and weak planning capabilities — hinder the ability of Panchayats to prepare and implement convergent plans. Addressing these challenges requires a combination of legislative clarity, predictable fiscal transfers, and systematic capacity building.

4. Finance: State Finance Commissions, Grants and Own Revenue

4.1 Constitutional Mandate for State Finance Commissions

Article 243I obliges the Governor of each state to constitute a State Finance Commission within one year of the commencement of the 73rd Amendment and thereafter at the expiration of every fifth year. The Commission reviews the financial position of the Panchayats and makes recommendations on the distribution of the net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls and fees leviable by the state between the state and the Panchayats; the determination of taxes, duties, tolls and fees that may be assigned to Panchayats; grants‑in‑aid to Panchayats from the Consolidated Fund of the State; and measures needed to improve the financial position of Panchayats. The recommendations are laid before the state legislature along with an explanatory memorandum, but they are not binding, which often leads to partial implementation.

4.2 Central Finance Commission Transfers and Centrally Sponsored Schemes

The Union Finance Commission, constituted under Article 280, also recommends grants to local bodies. Since the Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑2020), a significant share of the divisible pool of taxes has been earmarked for Gram Panchayats and Municipalities as basic and performance grants. These grants are untied, allowing local bodies to prioritise local needs. In addition, Centrally Sponsored Schemes such as the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme, Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Gramin, and Swachh Bharat Mission channel funds through Panchayats, but they come with strict guidelines that limit flexibility. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2021‑2026) continued the practice of providing health grants and grants for incubation of new cities, further augmenting the fiscal space of local governments.

4.3 Own Source Revenue and Fiscal Autonomy

Own source revenue of Panchayats primarily comprises property tax, tax on markets and fairs, user charges for water supply and sanitation, and fees for licenses and permits. However, the tax base is narrow, collection efficiency is low, and many states have not devolved the power to levy property tax to Gram Panchayats. Urban local bodies enjoy a relatively broader base, including property tax, advertisement tax, profession tax, and revenue from municipal services. The 74th Amendment empowers municipalities to levy taxes, but the actual rates and exemptions are fixed by the state legislature. Strengthening fiscal autonomy requires expanding the tax base, improving collection through technology, and ensuring that a predictable share of state taxes is devolved as untied grants.

5. Urban Local Bodies: Municipalities and Municipal Corporations

5.1 Constitutional Framework for Urban Governance

The 74th Amendment provides a uniform constitutional framework for urban local bodies across the country. It defines three categories of municipalities based on population and character of the area: Nagar Panchayats for areas in transition from rural to urban, Municipal Councils for smaller urban areas, and Municipal Corporations for larger urban areas. The amendment mandates that all seats in a municipality be filled by direct elections from territorial constituencies known as wards. It also provides for the reservation of seats for scheduled castes, scheduled tribes and women, and for the offices of chairpersons and mayors. The term of a municipality is five years, and elections must be completed before the expiry of the term. The State Election Commission supervises the conduct of elections, while the State Finance Commission reviews the financial position and recommends devolution of funds.

5.2 Composition, Elections and Ward Committees

A Municipal Corporation is headed by a Mayor, who is elected either directly by the voters or indirectly by the elected councillors, depending on the state law. The Corporation consists of a Council of elected councillors, a Standing Committee, and various functional committees such as finance, public works, health and education. In municipalities with a population of three lakh or more, the Constitution requires the constitution of Ward Committees, each comprising the councillor of the ward and a few nominated members, to facilitate participatory planning at the neighbourhood level. The Ward Committees are intended to bridge the gap between the municipal administration and citizens, enabling grievance redressal and monitoring of service delivery. However, the effectiveness of Ward Committees varies widely because many states have not framed detailed rules for their functioning.

5.3 Functional Domain and Financial Resources

The Twelfth Schedule, inserted by the 74th Amendment, lists eighteen functions that may be entrusted to municipalities, including urban planning, regulation of land use, water supply, public health, sanitation, solid waste management, fire services, urban forestry, slum improvement, urban poverty alleviation, and provision of urban amenities such as parks and playgrounds. The actual assignment of these functions is governed by the respective state municipal acts. Financial resources of municipalities comprise own revenue (property tax, user charges, advertisement tax, profession tax), state government grants (both tied and untied), central government grants (Finance Commission grants, scheme‑specific funds), and borrowings. The 74th Amendment empowers municipalities to raise loans with the prior approval of the state government, but the borrowing capacity remains constrained by weak creditworthiness and limited revenue buoyancy. Strengthening urban governance therefore hinges on improving own‑source revenue collection, ensuring timely and predictable transfers, and building institutional capacity for project planning and financial management.

The constitutional architecture of local government in India, embodied in the 73rd and 74th Amendments, represents a decisive shift towards democratic decentralisation, yet the realised depth of self‑government depends on the willingness of state legislatures to devolve functions, finances and functionaries in a coherent and sustained manner.

District Planning Committees and Metropolitan Planning Committees: Constitutional Mandate and Operational Reality

Articles 243ZD and 243ZE of the Constitution require every state to constitute a District Planning Committee (DPC) at the district level and a Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC) in every metropolitan area with a population of ten lakh or more. The DPC consists of elected members of the Panchayats and Municipalities in the district, together with members of the state legislature representing the district, and is chaired by the district collector or an elected representative as the state law provides. The MPC mirrors this composition but draws its members from the Municipal Corporations, Municipal Councils, and the state legislature within the metropolitan area. Both committees are mandated to prepare a draft development plan that integrates spatial, economic, and social planning across rural and urban jurisdictions.

In practice, the effectiveness of DPCs and MPCs is uneven. Many states have not framed detailed rules for their functioning, leaving the committees without a clear secretariat, technical support, or budgetary allocation. The collector’s dual role as chairperson and administrative head often leads to a top‑down approach, undermining the participatory spirit of the 73rd and 74th Amendments. Where functional, DPCs have facilitated convergence of schemes such as MGNREGA, National Rural Livelihoods Mission, and urban infrastructure programmes, but the absence of a statutory planning horizon (five‑year or annual) and weak enforcement of plan approvals limit their impact. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that states strengthen DPCs by providing dedicated planning cells and by linking a portion of untied grants to the timely preparation and approval of district plans.

The Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas (PESA) Act, 1996: Autonomy, Resource Control and Implementation Gaps

The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) extends Part IX of the Constitution to the Fifth Schedule areas, recognising the customary law, social and religious practices, and traditional management of community resources of the tribal populations. PESA empowers the Gram Sabha as the core unit of self‑governance, granting it ownership of minor forest produce, the power to prevent land alienation, control over local markets, and the authority to manage minor water bodies and village institutions. The Act also mandates that state legislation on Panchayats in Scheduled Areas must not be inconsistent with these provisions.

Despite the progressive framework, implementation remains patchy. Many states have enacted conformity legislation but have diluted critical provisions — for instance, by vesting ownership of minor forest produce in the state forest department rather than the Gram Sabha, or by requiring prior approval of the district collector for Gram Sabha resolutions on land transfer. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj’s 2022 review noted that only 10 of the 10 states with Fifth Schedule areas had fully aligned their Panchayat Acts with PESA, and that Gram Sabhas in several states lack the technical capacity to prepare micro‑plans for forest rights recognition under the Forest Rights Act, 2006. Moreover, the parallel existence of the Forest Department’s Joint Forest Management committees often creates institutional conflict over the same resource base.

Critical Provisions and State‑Level Variations

  • Ownership of Minor Forest Produce (MFP): PESA assigns ownership to Gram Sabha; states like Madhya Pradesh and Odisha have issued notifications transferring MFP rights, while Chhattisgarh retains state control.
  • Land Alienation: Gram Sabha consent mandatory for transfer of tribal land; however, many states allow collector’s consent to override Gram Sabha dissent.
  • Local Market Control: Gram Sabha can regulate haats and mandis; in practice, market committees dominated by traders limit this power.
  • Minor Water Bodies: Management vested in Gram Sabha; irrigation departments often retain operational control.

The 15th Finance Commission earmarked a separate grant for PESA areas, conditional on the functional devolution of the above powers. This fiscal lever has prompted some states to issue fresh conformity orders, but monitoring compliance remains a challenge given the absence of a centralised reporting mechanism.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about PESA is correct? (a) It applies to Sixth Schedule areas. (b) It gives Gram Sabha ownership of major minerals. (c) It mandates Gram Sabha consent for land alienation in Scheduled Areas. (d) It replaces the Forest Rights Act.”
Correct answer: (c). PESA applies to Fifth Schedule (Scheduled) areas, not Sixth Schedule. It grants ownership of minor forest produce, not major minerals. It does not replace the Forest Rights Act; the two statutes operate concurrently, with FRA focusing on individual and community forest rights while PESA provides governance powers to Gram Sabhas.
Answer‑writing strategy
When a question mixes PESA, FRA, and Fifth/Sixth Schedule provisions, first identify the schedule (Fifth = PESA, Sixth = Autonomous District Councils). Then map the specific power (land alienation, MFP, local markets) to the correct statute. Use a two‑column table in the rough sheet to avoid confusion.

State Election Commissions: Independence, Tenure, and the Challenge of Timely Elections

Article 243K vests the superintendence, direction, and control of elections to Panchayats in a State Election Commission (SEC) consisting of a State Election Commissioner appointed by the Governor. The Commissioner’s conditions of service and tenure are determined by state law, subject to the proviso that the Commissioner shall not be removed except in the manner and on the grounds prescribed for a Judge of a High Court. This constitutional safeguard is intended to insulate the SEC from executive pressure, yet the actual independence varies widely.

Several states have enacted laws that fix the Commissioner’s term at five years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, mirroring the Election Commission of India. However, in states such as Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, the Commissioner is often a serving or retired bureaucrat appointed for a short term, and the removal provision is loosely defined, allowing the state government to replace the Commissioner before critical election cycles. The Supreme Court in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Kumar (2020) reiterated that the SEC must enjoy functional autonomy comparable to the ECI, and directed states to amend their laws to secure a fixed tenure and a transparent appointment process involving a committee comprising the Chief Minister, Leader of Opposition, and a High Court judge.

Timeliness of elections remains a persistent problem. The Constitution mandates that elections be completed before the expiry of the five‑year term, but frequent delays occur due to delimitation disputes, reservation roster finalisation, and litigation over ward boundaries. The 15th Finance Commission linked a portion of performance grants to the conduct of elections within the constitutional timeline, creating a fiscal incentive for compliance. Yet, as of 2023, several states had overdue Panchayat elections ranging from six months to two years, undermining the democratic legitimacy of local bodies and disrupting the flow of tied scheme funds that require an elected body for utilisation certificates.

Reform Milestones

  • 1993: 73rd Amendment inserts Art. 243K.
  • 2006: Supreme Court in K. Krishnamurthy v. Union of India emphasises SEC independence.
  • 2018: Model State Election Commission Bill circulated by Ministry of Panchayati Raj proposing fixed five‑year term and collegium appointment.
  • 2020: State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Kumar directs states to align SEC tenure with High Court judge removal procedure.
  • 2022: 15th Finance Commission recommends performance grant conditionality on timely elections.

Fifteenth Finance Commission and the New Architecture of Local Body Grants: Performance Grants, Health Grants, and Conditionalities

The Fifteenth Finance Commission (FC‑XV), covering the period 2021‑26, restructured the transfer system to rural and urban local bodies by introducing a three‑tier grant architecture: (i) Basic (untied) grants, (ii) Performance grants linked to measurable outcomes, and (iii) Sector‑specific grants for health and nutrition. For rural bodies, the basic grant constitutes 60 % of the total allocation, performance grants 30 %, and health grants 10 %. For urban bodies, the split is 50 % basic, 30 % performance, and 20 % health. The performance grants are released only after the local body satisfies conditions such as timely audited accounts, publication of service‑level benchmarks, and completion of online asset registers.

The health grant component is a novel feature, reflecting the pandemic‑induced emphasis on primary health infrastructure. Rural local bodies must earmark at least 20 % of the health grant for strengthening sub‑centres and primary health centres, while urban bodies must allocate a minimum of 15 % for urban primary health centres and community health centres. The Commission also mandated that states devolve a minimum of 10 % of their own tax revenue to local bodies as untied grants, a significant increase from the 5 % benchmark of the Fourteenth Finance Commission.

Conditionalities have driven administrative reforms: many states have adopted the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) for real‑time fund tracking, and the Ministry of Panchayati Raj has launched the “Gram Panchayat Development Plan” (GPDP) portal to monitor performance‑grant eligibility. However, the reliance on audited accounts as a gatekeeper has excluded newly constituted Panchayats that lack a completed audit cycle, creating a first‑year funding gap. The Commission recommended a one‑time “seed grant” for such bodies, but only a few states have operationalised it.

Judicial Interventions Shaping Local Governance: Key Supreme Court Judgments on Mayoral Powers, Ward Committees, and Devolution

The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified the constitutional position of local bodies, often filling gaps left by ambiguous state legislation. In K. Chandrasekhar v. State of Tamil Nadu (2018), the Court held that the Mayor of a Municipal Corporation, where directly elected, exercises executive authority over the corporation’s administration and cannot be reduced to a ceremonial figurehead by state law. The judgment struck down a Tamil Nadu amendment that transferred all executive powers to a Commissioner appointed by the state government, affirming that the 74th Amendment envisions an empowered political executive at the urban level.

In Rameshwar Prasad v. State of Bihar (2021), the Court addressed the non‑constitution of Ward Committees in municipalities with populations above three lakh. It directed the Bihar government to frame rules within six months, emphasising that Ward Committees are not optional but a constitutional mandate under Article 243S. The Court also ruled that nominated members on Ward Committees must not exceed one‑third of the total strength, preserving the elected character of the body.

A landmark decision on devolution came in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2022), where the Court interpreted Article 243G read with the Eleventh Schedule to require that state laws must “effectively devolve” functions, not merely list them. The Court mandated that Karnataka enact an “Activity Mapping” legislation specifying which department retains control over each function and which functionaries are transferred to the Panchayat. This judgment has spurred several states to pass Activity Mapping Acts, though the depth of transfer remains uneven.

Finally, in Gram Panchayat, Kothapally v. State of Telangana (2023), the Court upheld the Gram Sabha’s power to conduct social audits of MGNREGA works under Section 17 of the MGNREGA Act, read with Article 243A. It ruled that the social audit report must be placed before the Gram Sabha and that any deviation triggers a statutory obligation for the state to initiate disciplinary action against errant officials. This judgment reinforced the accountability chain from the Gram Sabha to the state bureaucracy.

These advanced dimensions — planning committees, PESA, election commission autonomy, finance commission architecture, and judicial doctrine — constitute the analytical core that distinguishes a superficial answer from a high‑scoring one in the mains examination.

Inter‑State Variation in Activity Mapping and Functional Devolution

The 73rd Amendment mandates that States devolve powers to Panchayats through legislation, but the Constitution leaves the method of devolution to State Assemblies. Consequently, the depth of functional transfer varies sharply. Kerala enacted the Kerala Panchayat Raj Act, 1994, and followed it with a detailed Activity Mapping Order (2000) that assigns each of the 29 Eleventh Schedule subjects to a specific tier — Gram Panchayat, Block Panchayat, or District Panchayat — and identifies the functionaries (engineers, health inspectors, agricultural officers) to be transferred. Karnataka’s Karnataka Panchayat Raj Act, 1993, was amended in 2015 to require a Functional Assignment Matrix that is revised every five years; the 2020 matrix transferred 22 of 29 subjects to Gram Panchayats, but retained control of water supply and secondary education at the district level. Maharashtra, by contrast, relies on the Maharashtra Zilla Parishads and Panchayat Samitis Act, 1961, and has not issued a comprehensive activity‑mapping notification; devolution remains largely on paper, with line departments continuing to implement schemes through parallel structures.

Institutional Mechanisms for Mapping

Most States that have operationalised mapping use a three‑step process: (1) a State‑level committee chaired by the Chief Secretary or the Minister for Panchayati Raj drafts the matrix; (2) the draft is placed before the State Legislature for ratification; (3) the final notification is published in the State Gazette, after which the Department of Panchayati Raj issues transfer orders for functionaries. In Kerala, the District Planning Committee (DPC) monitors compliance, while in Karnataka the State Institute of Rural Development conducts annual audits of transferred staff. The absence of a statutory timeline for mapping in many States creates a policy vacuum that the Supreme Court highlighted in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2022), directing Karnataka to enact a dedicated Activity Mapping Act within six months.

Impact on Service Delivery

Empirical studies by the Centre for Policy Research (2021) show that States with binding activity‑mapping orders — Kerala, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu — report higher per‑capita expenditure on core services (water, sanitation, primary health) at the Gram Panchayat level, ranging from ₹1,200 to ₹1,800 per annum, compared with ₹600–₹900 in States without such orders. The variation also appears in the proportion of own‑source revenue: Kerala’s Gram Panchayats generate 18 % of their revenue locally, whereas Maharashtra’s average is below 5 %.

Gender Quotas and Women’s Leadership in Panchayati Raj: Outcomes and Gaps

Article 243D mandates reservation of not less than one‑third of seats for women in every Panchayat, with rotation across constituencies. Since the 73rd Amendment, 20 States have legislated 50 % reservation (e.g., Bihar 2006, Rajasthan 2009, Andhra Pradesh 2011). As of the 2022 Panchayat elections, women occupied 46 % of all elected positions nationwide, up from 37 % in 2010. However, the proxy representation phenomenon persists: in many constituencies, elected women function as nominal heads while male relatives exercise de facto authority. A 2020 study by the Institute of Social Sciences covering 1,200 Gram Panchayats in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh found that 62 % of women Sarpanches reported that their husbands or fathers attended meetings on their behalf.

Capacity‑Building Initiatives

The Ministry of Panchayati Raj launched the Mahila Shakti Kendra scheme (2017) to provide leadership training, financial literacy, and legal awareness to elected women representatives. By March 2023, 1.3 lakh women had completed the 30‑day residential module. Complementarily, the National Institute of Rural Development and Panchayati Raj (NIRDPR) offers a certificate course on Gender‑Responsive Governance that has been made mandatory for all women Sarpanches in Karnataka since 2021.

Measurable Outcomes

Where training has been intensive, outcomes improve. In Kerala’s Kudumbashree‑linked Panchayats, women‑led Gram Sabhas have increased the share of MGNREGA works allocated to women from 33 % (2015) to 51 % (2022). In contrast, States that rely solely on reservation without capacity building show stagnant participation in Gram Sabha quorum — often below the statutory 10 % threshold.

Examiner's twist
A question asks: “Critically examine whether Activity Mapping alone ensures effective devolution of functions to Panchayats.”
Activity Mapping is a necessary but not sufficient condition. It translates constitutional subjects into tier‑wise responsibilities and identifies functionaries to be transferred. However, effective devolution also requires (1) fiscal autonomy — untied grants and own‑source revenue — so that Panchayats can finance the devolved functions; (2) administrative control over transferred staff, including powers of appointment, transfer, and disciplinary action; (3) institutionalised accountability mechanisms such as social audit and Gram Sabha oversight; and (4) political will reflected in timely State Finance Commission recommendations and their acceptance. The Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2022) stressed that mapping must be backed by legislation that “effectively devolves” functions, not merely lists them. Therefore, a high‑scoring answer should contrast Kerala’s binding mapping with Maharashtra’s declaratory approach and link the gap to service‑delivery outcomes.
Mains answer strategy
When a question combines devolution, finance, and gender, structure the answer in three columns: Constitutional mandate → State‑level implementation (cite two contrasting States) → Empirical outcome (use one data point per column). This matrix format lets the examiner see comparative analysis at a glance and saves words.

Digital Transformation of Local Governance: E‑Panchayat, GIS Mapping, and Citizen Service Portals

The e‑Panchayat Mission Mode Project (MMP) was approved by the Cabinet in 2009 as part of the National e‑Governance Plan. Its core components are: (1) Panchayat Enterprise Suite (PES) — a suite of 11 applications covering planning, accounting, asset management, and service delivery; (2) National Panchayat Portal (NPP) — a single window for citizens to access certificates, pay taxes, and track scheme benefits; (3) GIS‑based Spatial Planning under the Space based Information Support for Decentralised Planning (SIS‑DP) programme, which integrates high‑resolution satellite imagery (Cartosat‑2, Resourcesat‑2) with cadastral maps to prepare Gram Panchayat Development Plans (GPDPs). By March 2024, 2.45 lakh Gram Panchayats (92 % of the total) had onboarded PES, and 1.78 lakh had published GPDPs on the NPP.

Implementation Challenges

Despite high onboarding, data quality remains uneven. A 2022 audit by the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) found that 38 % of uploaded asset registers contained duplicate entries, and only 45 % of Panchayats updated financial accounts monthly. Connectivity gaps in tribal and hilly districts (e.g., parts of Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, and the North‑East) limit real‑time transaction processing. The Ministry has responded by deploying BharatNet fibre to 1.2 lakh Gram Panchayats and providing solar‑powered Wi‑Fi hotspots in 15,000 off‑grid locations.

Citizen‑Centric Services

The NPP now hosts 27 citizen services, including birth/death certificates, property tax self‑assessment, and MGNREGA job‑card downloads. In Tamil Nadu, the e‑Sevai centres at the Panchayat level process over 1.2 crore service requests annually, reducing average turnaround from 14 days (paper) to 2 days (digital). The portal also integrates with the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) for direct benefit transfer tracking, enhancing transparency.

Municipal Finance Reforms: Property Tax Reform, Municipal Bonds, and Credit Rating

Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) remain heavily dependent on State and Central transfers; own‑source revenue averages 28 % of total municipal income (FC‑XV data). The Model Municipal Law, 2003 recommended a shift from annual rental value (ARV) to capital value‑based property taxation, with periodic revision linked to market indices. As of 2023, 14 States have enacted capital‑value legislation (e.g., Gujarat 2009, Karnataka 2016, Telangana 2018). Early adopters report a 35‑50 % increase in property tax collections within three years of implementation.

Municipal Bonds and Credit Rating

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) issued the Issue and Listing of Municipal Debt Securities Regulations, 2015, enabling ULBs to raise funds through rated bonds. Pune Municipal Corporation issued India’s first municipal bond in 2017 (₹200 crore, AA+ rating by CRISIL), followed by Ahmedabad (₹200 crore, AA), Hyderabad (₹200 crore, AA‑), and Indore (₹150 crore, A+). FC‑XV incentivised bond issuance by earmarking a performance grant component for ULBs that achieve a minimum investment‑grade rating. The Credit Rating Framework for ULBs (MoHUA, 2020) evaluates four parameters: financial health, governance, service delivery, and debt sustainability. As of December 2023, 48 ULBs have obtained a rating, of which 31 are investment grade.

Reform Linkages

Property tax reform improves the revenue base, which in turn strengthens the credit profile required for bond issuance. The Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT) 2.0 ties central assistance to the achievement of a “municipal finance reform index” that includes capital‑value tax adoption, double‑entry accounting, and publication of audited annual accounts within nine months of the financial year end.

Accountability Architecture: Social Audit, Lokayukta, and Right to Information at the Grassroots

Accountability at the local level rests on three pillars: (1) Social Audit — mandated under Section 17 of the MGNREGA Act and reinforced by the Supreme Court in Gram Panchayat, Kothapally v. State of Telangana (2023), which held that the social audit report must be placed before the Gram Sabha and any deviation triggers disciplinary action; (2) State Lokayukta — 22 States have established Lokayukta institutions with jurisdiction over Panchayat functionaries; the Karnataka Lokayukta (Amendment) Act, 2020 extended its purview to Gram Panchayat secretaries and engineers; (3) Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005 — applies to all “public authorities”, including Panchayats and Municipalities. The Central Information Commission (CIC) in 2019 directed all States to designate a Public Information Officer (PIO) at each Gram Panchayat; compliance stands at 78 % as per the 2022 RTI Annual Report.

Operational Synergies

In Rajasthan, the Jan Soochna Portal integrates proactive disclosure under RTI with social audit findings, allowing citizens to view MGNREGA muster rolls, asset photographs, and audit observations in a single dashboard. This convergence reduced the average time to resolve a complaint from 45 days to 12 days (State Rural Development Department, 2022). In Kerala, the Ombudsman for Local Self‑Government (established 2018) hears appeals against Panchayat decisions and has disposed of 3,400 cases with a 92 % satisfaction rate among complainants.

Remaining Gaps

Despite statutory backing, social audits are conducted in only 62 % of Gram Panchayats annually (Ministry of Rural Development, 2023). Lokayukta vacancies persist — 9 of 22 States had no functional Lokayukta as of January 2024. RTI compliance is weakest in the North‑East, where only 41 % of Panchayats have appointed a PIO. Strengthening these mechanisms requires (a) mandatory annual social audit calendars notified by the State, (b) fixed tenures and independent appointment processes for Lokayukta, and (c) penal provisions for non‑appointment of PIOs under Section 20 of the RTI Act.

  • Treating 50 % reservation as a guarantee of women’s effective participation without checking for proxy leadership.
  • Believing that e‑Panchayat onboarding equals digital service delivery; data quality and connectivity are separate hurdles.
  • Confusing municipal bond eligibility with mere credit rating; SEBI also requires a track record of audited accounts and a sinking fund.
  • Thinking the Lokayukta covers all local body staff uniformly; jurisdiction varies by State legislation.
  • Equating RTI compliance with proactive disclosure; many Panchayats only respond to requests rather than publish voluntarily.
  • Overlooking that performance grants under FC‑XV are conditional on both financial and non‑financial benchmarks, not just audit submission.

Convergence of Centrally Sponsored Schemes with Gram Panchayat Development Plans

The Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) was institutionalised through the Panchayat Empowerment and Accountability Incentive Scheme (PEAIS) and later reinforced by the Fourteenth Finance Commission (FC‑XIV) which mandated that every Gram Panchayat prepare a participatory plan linking local priorities to centrally sponsored schemes (CSS). The Ministry of Panchayati Raj issued the Model Guidelines for GPDP Preparation (2015, revised 2020) requiring a three‑stage process: (i) situational analysis using Village Level Data (VLD) and Socio‑Economic Caste Census (SECC) data, (ii) identification of felt needs through Gram Sabha deliberations, and (iii) mapping of CSS resources — such as MGNREGA, National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM), Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Gramin (PMAY‑G), Swachh Bharat Mission‑Gramin (SBM‑G), and Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) — onto the plan’s activity matrix.

Convergence is operationalised through the Activity Mapping exercise prescribed under Article 243G read with the Eleventh Schedule. Each State prepares an Activity Map that assigns specific CSS components to the appropriate tier (Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, Zilla Parishad). For instance, MGNREGA wage employment is a Gram Panchayat function, while watershed development under the Integrated Watershed Management Programme (IWMP) may be shared with the Panchayat Samiti. The GPDP must reflect these assignments; otherwise, the State Finance Commission (SFC) may withhold performance grants.

Empirical evidence from the National Institute of Rural Development and Panchayati Raj (NIRDPR) 2022 evaluation shows that States with a robust Activity Map (e.g., Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra) achieved 78 % convergence of CSS funds into GPDPs, whereas States with weak mapping (e.g., Bihar, Uttar Pradesh) recorded only 42 % convergence. The key bottlenecks are: (a) delayed release of CSS instalments, (b) lack of technical staff at the Gram Panchayat level to prepare detailed project reports, and (c) parallel implementation structures (e.g., separate DRDA units) that bypass the Panchayat.

The Fifteenth Finance Commission (FC‑XV) introduced a Performance Grant component conditional on (i) timely preparation and public disclosure of GPDP, (ii) audit of accounts within nine months, and (iii) demonstrable convergence of at least three CSS. This incentivises States to strengthen the District Planning Committee (DPC) as the apex body for approving convergent plans. However, the DPC’s effectiveness remains uneven because many States have not devolved the power to approve sectoral plans to the DPC, leaving it advisory.

For Mains, the examiner expects you to link constitutional provisions (Art. 243G, 243ZD), Finance Commission recommendations, and the practical mechanics of Activity Mapping. A high‑scoring answer will cite the Model Guidelines, the FC‑XV performance grant conditionalities, and a comparative State example.

Fiscal Implications of GST on Local Body Revenues and the Compensation Mechanism

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) subsumed several State‑level indirect taxes — notably entry tax, octroi, entertainment tax, and luxury tax — that historically formed a significant share of municipal own‑source revenue. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, inserted Article 279A establishing the GST Council, but it did not explicitly provide for a revenue share to local bodies. Consequently, Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) and Panchayats experienced an immediate erosion of buoyancy in their tax bases.

To mitigate this, the GST Council, in its 28th meeting (January 2019), recommended a Compensation Cess on sin goods and luxury items, with the proceeds earmarked for States. The Fourteenth Finance Commission (FC‑XIV) had already increased the vertical share of States to 42 % and recommended that States pass on a “reasonable” portion to local bodies. However, the State Finance Commissions (SFCs) were left to determine the quantum. In practice, only a few States (e.g., Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka) have institutionalised a fixed percentage (typically 1‑2 % of State GST revenue) as a Local Body Grant.

The Fifteenth Finance Commission (FC‑XV) recognised the fiscal shock and introduced a GST Compensation Grant for Local Bodies amounting to ₹90,000 crore for the 2021‑26 period, disbursed through the States. The grant is released in two tranches: (i) a basic grant linked to the 2015‑16 revenue baseline, and (ii) a performance grant contingent on (a) publication of audited annual accounts, (b) improvement in own‑source revenue collection (property tax, user charges), and (c) adoption of the Model Municipal Accounting System (MMAS). As of March 2024, 28 States have met the basic grant conditions, but only 14 have qualified for the performance grant.

A critical nuance for examiners: the GST compensation to local bodies is not a constitutional entitlement; it is a statutory grant routed through the State Consolidated Fund. Therefore, any delay in State budgetary approval directly stalls ULB cash flows. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) Report No. 12 of 2023 highlighted that 11 States delayed the pass‑through by more than six months, forcing municipalities to resort to short‑term borrowing at 9‑11 % interest.

In answer writing, distinguish between (i) the constitutional vertical devolution (Art. 280), (ii) the GST Council’s recommendatory role, (iii) the SFC’s mandate to recommend local shares, and (iv) the FC‑XV performance grant conditionalities. Cite the GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017, the FC‑XV Report, Chapter 7, and the CAG 2023 audit findings.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Explain why the GST regime weakened municipal finances and how the Fifteenth Finance Commission attempted to address it.” A common error is to state that the GST Council directly allocates funds to municipalities.
The GST Council has no constitutional power to allocate funds to local bodies; it only recommends compensation to States. The fiscal gap arose because GST subsumed municipal taxes (octroi, entry tax) without a constitutional replacement. FC‑XV addressed this by creating a dedicated GST Compensation Grant for local bodies (₹90,000 crore, 2021‑26) routed through States, with release conditional on audited accounts, own‑revenue improvement, and adoption of MMAS. The grant is a statutory, not constitutional, entitlement, making State‑level pass‑through the critical operational link.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mixes GST, Finance Commissions, and local bodies, structure the answer in four layers: (1) Constitutional baseline (Art. 280, 243X), (2) GST Council’s recommendatory role, (3) SFC’s statutory mandate, (4) FC‑XV performance grant design. Use the phrase “statutory grant routed through State Consolidated Fund” to signal precision. Quote the ₹90,000 crore figure and the three performance conditions — this fetches full marks for data‑backed analysis.

Participatory Budgeting in Urban Local Bodies: Institutional Design and Outcomes

Participatory Budgeting (PB) allows citizens to directly decide on a portion of the municipal capital budget. In India, the 74th Amendment (Art. 243S) provides for Ward Committees, but it does not mandate PB. The first systematic PB experiment was launched in Pune Municipal Corporation (2006) under the Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (JNNURM) reforms, allocating 5 % of the capital budget (≈₹100 crore) to ward‑level proposals vetted by Area Sabhas. Kerala’s People’s Plan Campaign (1996) predates the 74th Amendment and institutionalised PB at the Gram Panchayat level; the model was later adapted for municipalities through the Kerala Municipality (Amendment) Act, 2009, which made PB mandatory for all 87 municipalities.

The institutional design varies: (i) Pune uses a two‑stage process — citizens submit proposals at Ward Sabhas, a technical committee screens for feasibility, and the final list is voted on in a Ward Committee meeting; (ii) Bengaluru (BBMP) introduced PB in 2019 with a digital platform “Namma Budget”, allocating ₹50 crore (1 % of capital outlay) and requiring geo‑tagged project proposals; (iii) Mumbai (BMC) piloted PB in 2022 for ₹200 crore across 24 wards, but the process remains advisory because the Municipal Commissioner retains final sanctioning power.

Outcome evaluations by the Centre for Policy Research (CPR) (2021) and Janaagraha (2023) reveal: (a) PB increases the share of capital expenditure on water supply, sanitation, and local roads by 12‑18 % compared with non‑PB wards; (b) citizen satisfaction scores rise from 3.2 to 4.1 on a 5‑point Likert scale; (c) however, elite capture persists — in Pune, 68 % of approved projects originated from resident welfare associations rather than informal settlements. The Model Municipal Law (2020) drafted by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) recommends a statutory PB window of 10 % of capital budget, mandatory digital disclosure, and an independent Participatory Budgeting Ombudsman.

For Mains, link PB to the constitutional vision of “self‑government” (Art. 243Q), the role of Ward Committees (Art. 243S), and the FC‑XV performance grant condition on “citizen participation in budgeting”. Cite the Kerala Municipality Act, 2009, the Pune PB Guidelines (2006), and the CPR 2021 evaluation.

State Election Commissions and Delimitation: Legal Framework, Supreme Court Judgments, and Operational Challenges

Article 243K vests the superintendence, direction, and control of Panchayat elections in the State Election Commission (SEC). Article 243ZA extends the same to Municipalities. The SEC is a single‑member body (in most States) appointed by the Governor, with a tenure of five years or until age 65, whichever is earlier. However, the Constitution does not prescribe a uniform appointment process, leading to wide inter‑State variation: some States (e.g., Tamil Nadu, Kerala) require consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court, while others (e.g., Uttar Pradesh, Bihar) allow unilateral executive appointment.

Delimitation of wards is governed by State Panchayat/Municipal Acts, which must conform to the Delimitation Commission Act, 2002 (for Parliamentary/Assembly constituencies) only by analogy. The Supreme Court in Kishan Singh Tomar v. State of Rajasthan (2011) held that the SEC has the power to direct delimitation, but the State legislature determines the criteria (population, geography, SC/ST concentration). In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Kumar (2019), the Court ruled that any delay beyond six months in notifying ward boundaries after the Census violates the constitutional mandate for timely elections.

Operational challenges include: (i) Vacancy — as of January 2024, 9 of 28 States had no functional SEC (the post was vacant for over 12 months); (ii) Resource starvation — many SECs lack dedicated staff, relying on district administration for polling personnel, compromising independence; (iii) Political interference — in several States, the ruling party has amended the SEC Act to reduce tenure or bring the SEC under the Chief Minister’s office (e.g., Andhra Pradesh 2020 amendment, later struck down by the High Court).

The Election Commission of India (ECI) has no direct supervisory role over SECs, but the Model Code of Conduct for local elections is issued by the SEC. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a “SEC Strengthening Grant” of ₹500 crore (2021‑26) conditional on (a) filling vacancies within three months, (b) publishing a delimitation calendar aligned with the Census, and (c) adopting electronic voting machines (EVMs) for all municipal elections. Only 12 States have accessed the grant.

In answer writing, differentiate between the constitutional guarantee (Art. 243K/243ZA), the statutory delimitation framework, and the judicial enforcement of timelines. Cite Kishan Singh Tomar (2011), Rajesh Kumar (2019), and the FC‑XV SEC Strengthening Grant.

Climate Resilience and Disaster Management Integration in Local Governance

The Disaster Management Act, 2005 establishes a three‑tier structure — National, State, and District Disaster Management Authorities (DDMA). The 73rd and 74th Amendments do not explicitly assign disaster management to Panchayats or Municipalities, but the Eleventh Schedule (Item 23) includes “disaster management” as a Panchayat subject, and the Twelfth Schedule (Item 17) lists “fire services” and “urban planning” for Municipalities. The National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP) 2019 mandates that every Gram Panchayat and Urban Local Body prepare a Local Disaster Management Plan (LDMP) aligned with the District Plan.

Integration mechanisms: (i) Gram Panchayat Disaster Management Committee (GPDMC) — chaired by the Sarpanch, includes the Panchayat Secretary, ASHA worker, and representatives of vulnerable groups; (ii) Ward‑level Disaster Management Committees in municipalities, chaired by the Ward Councillor; (iii) Village Disaster Management Plans (VDMP) prepared under the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) convergence, using labour for flood‑control works, check‑dam construction, and cyclone shelters.

Financing: The State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) and National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) are accessible to local bodies only through the District Collector. The 15th Finance Commission allocated ₹32,000 crore (2021‑26) for Local Body Disaster Mitigation Grants, released to States on the basis of (a) existence of approved LDMPs, (b) conduct of mock drills, and (c) integration of climate‑risk data into GPDPs. As of December 2023, 64 % of Gram Panchayats and 71 % of ULBs had submitted LDMPs; however, only 38 % had conducted annual mock drills.

Case illustration: Odisha’s “Samanvay” model (2018) integrates the Gram Panchayat, the Block Development Officer, and the DDMA into a single command structure for cyclone response. During Cyclone Fani (2019), the model enabled evacuation of 1.2 million people within 24 hours, with zero mortality in Panchayats that had updated VDMPs. Conversely, the 2022 Assam floods exposed gaps — 42 % of affected Panchayats lacked a functional GPDMC, delaying relief distribution by 5‑7 days.

For Mains, connect the constitutional schedules, the Disaster Management Act, the NDMP, and FC‑XV mitigation grants. Emphasise the operational role of the Gram Sabha in approving VDMPs and the Ward Committee in urban areas. Cite the Odisha Samanvay model, the FC‑XV ₹32,000 crore allocation, and the NDMP 2019.
