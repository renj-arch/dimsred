1. Constitutional Foundations: The 73rd and 74th Amendments

1.1 Historical Background and Legislative Intent

The drive to institutionalise local self‑government in India gathered momentum after the recommendations of the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee in 1957, which advocated a three‑tier system of democratic decentralisation. Although several states enacted their own Panchayat Acts thereafter, the absence of a uniform constitutional mandate led to wide variations in structure, tenure and powers. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, and the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, were therefore enacted to give a mandatory constitutional status to rural and urban local bodies respectively, ensuring regular elections, reservation of seats for scheduled castes, scheduled tribes and women, and a fixed five‑year term. The amendments were rooted in the Directive Principles of State Policy, particularly Article 40, which exhorts the State to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self‑government.

1.2 Salient Features of the 73rd Amendment

The 73rd Amendment inserted Part IX into the Constitution, comprising Articles 243 to 243‑O. It provides for a three‑tier Panchayati Raj system in every state having a population of more than twenty lakh, with Gram Panchayats at the village level, Panchayat Samitis at the intermediate block level and Zilla Parishads at the district level. The amendment mandates direct elections to all seats, reservation of not less than one‑third of the total seats for women, and proportionate reservation for scheduled castes and scheduled tribes. It also establishes a State Election Commission to supervise elections and a State Finance Commission to review the financial position of panchayats every five years. The Governor is empowered to dissolve a panchayat before the expiry of its term only on the grounds specified in the Constitution, thereby protecting the tenure of elected representatives.

1.3 Salient Features of the 74th Amendment

The 74th Amendment inserted Part IXA, Articles 243‑P to 243‑ZG, to provide a constitutional framework for urban local bodies. It defines three types of municipalities: Nagar Panchayats for transitional areas, Municipal Councils for smaller urban areas and Municipal Corporations for larger urban areas. Like the rural amendment, it mandates direct elections, reservation for women, scheduled castes and scheduled tribes, a five‑year term, and the constitution of a State Election Commission and a State Finance Commission. Additionally, it provides for the constitution of Ward Committees in municipalities with a population of three lakh or more, and a District Planning Committee to consolidate plans prepared by panchayats and municipalities. The amendment also empowers the state legislature to endow municipalities with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self‑government.

2. Three‑Tier Panchayati Raj Structure

2.1 Gram Panchayat at the Village Level

The Gram Panchayat is the foundation of the Panchayati Raj system and operates at the village or group of villages level. It consists of a Sarpanch, who is directly elected by the voters of the Gram Sabha, and a number of elected members called Panchs. The Gram Sabha, comprising all registered voters in the village, acts as the general body and meets at least twice a year to approve plans, budgets and audit reports. The Gram Panchayat is entrusted with functions such as sanitation, drinking water supply, maintenance of village roads, street lighting, and implementation of centrally sponsored schemes like the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act. Its executive powers are exercised through standing committees for finance, works, education and health, which enable a degree of specialisation in decision‑making.

2.2 Panchayat Samiti at the Block Level

The Panchayat Samiti, also known as the Block Panchayat, functions at the intermediate level covering a group of Gram Panchayats within a development block. Its members are indirectly elected by and from the elected members of the constituent Gram Panchayats, and the chairperson is elected from among the members. The Samiti coordinates the development plans of the Gram Panchayats, supervises the implementation of schemes that span multiple villages, and acts as a link between the district level Zilla Parishad and the village level. It also manages resources such as block‑level schools, primary health centres and minor irrigation works, and prepares the block development plan for submission to the Zilla Parishad.

2.3 Zilla Parishad at the District Level

The Zilla Parishad is the apex tier of the rural local government structure and covers the entire district. Its composition includes directly elected members from territorial constituencies, members of the Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assembly representing the district, and chairpersons of the Panchayat Samitis within the district. The President of the Zilla Parishad is elected from among the directly elected members. The Zilla Parishad prepares the district development plan by integrating the plans of the Panchayat Samitis and Gram Panchayats, allocates resources for district‑level infrastructure such as major roads, hospitals and secondary schools, and monitors the implementation of centrally sponsored programmes. It also exercises supervisory authority over the lower tiers, ensuring conformity with state policies and financial norms.

Example Question
Explain how the three‑tier Panchayati Raj structure facilitates the principle of subsidiarity in rural governance. Illustrate your answer with reference to the functional domain of each tier.
The principle of subsidiarity holds that decisions should be taken at the lowest level of government capable of addressing them effectively. The Gram Panchayat, being closest to the citizen, handles functions that directly affect daily life such as sanitation, drinking water and village roads. The Panchayat Samiti aggregates village‑level needs into block‑level plans, managing services that require coordination across several villages, for example block‑level health centres and minor irrigation. The Zilla Parishad, at the district level, deals with larger infrastructure and inter‑block resource allocation, such as district hospitals, major roads and secondary education. By assigning distinct but complementary functions to each tier, the structure ensures that governance is neither overly centralised nor fragmented, embodying subsidiarity in practice.

2.4 Gram Sabha, Elections and Reservation of Seats

The three tiers are not merely administrative units; they rest on a base of direct democracy. Article 243A provides that the Gram Sabha, consisting of all persons registered in the electoral rolls of a village, may exercise such powers and perform such functions at the village level as the legislature of the State may by law provide, and under PESA the Gram Sabha has special powers over minor minerals, land alienation and forest produce in Scheduled Areas. Article 243C prescribes the composition of Panchayats, and Article 243D requires reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population and not less than one-third of the total seats for women, with similar reservations for the offices of Chairperson. Article 243E fixes a term of five years and requires elections before the term expires, Article 243K vests the superintendence of elections in a State Election Commission, and Article 243F prescribes disqualifications for membership. These provisions give the Panchayati Raj system a constitutional floor of regular elections, reserved representation and popular participation that earlier committee-based arrangements lacked.

The same constitutional logic of regular elections, reserved representation and independent election machinery applies to urban areas. Articles 243Q to 243ZC constitute three types of Municipality — a Nagar Panchayat for a transitional area, a Municipal Council for a smaller urban area and a Municipal Corporation for a larger urban area — and Articles 243G and 243W carry the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules that list the subjects devolved to Panchayats and Municipalities respectively. The 73rd and 74th Amendments of 1992 thus created a single, symmetrical design of local self-government for rural and urban India, each supported by its own State Finance Commission under Articles 243I and 243Y.

3. Functions, Powers and Devolution of Responsibilities

3.1 Constitutional Distribution of Subjects

Article 243G of the Constitution authorises the state legislature to endow Panchayats with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self‑government. The Eleventh Schedule, inserted by the 73rd Amendment, lists twenty‑nine subjects that may be devolved to Panchayats, including agriculture, land improvement, minor irrigation, animal husbandry, fisheries, social forestry, small scale industries, rural housing, drinking water, fuel and fodder, roads, rural electrification, non‑conventional energy, poverty alleviation programmes, education, primary health centres, women and child development, and public distribution system. The actual devolution, however, depends on the respective state Panchayat Acts, which specify the subjects transferred, the level of Panchayat to which they are assigned, and the accompanying financial and administrative powers.

3.2 Activity Mapping and the Role of State Legislation

Activity mapping is the process by which each function listed in the Eleventh Schedule is broken down into specific activities and assigned to the appropriate tier of Panchayat. For instance, primary education may be split into school construction (Zilla Parishad), teacher recruitment (Panchayat Samiti) and school management committees (Gram Panchayat). State governments are required to enact legislation that not only transfers functions but also provides the necessary staff, funds and functionaries. The effectiveness of devolution is therefore contingent on the clarity of activity mapping, the adequacy of fiscal transfers, and the capacity of Panchayats to absorb and utilise transferred responsibilities. In many states, the absence of detailed activity mapping has resulted in overlapping jurisdictions and accountability gaps.

3.3 Challenges in Effective Devolution

Despite the constitutional mandate, several structural challenges impede genuine devolution. First, many states have not transferred all twenty‑nine subjects, retaining key functions such as land revenue, police and higher education at the state level. Second, the financial autonomy of Panchayats remains limited because own‑source revenue bases are narrow and state grants are often tied to specific schemes, reducing discretionary spending. Third, the bureaucratic apparatus at the district and block levels continues to be controlled by state government officers, leading to a dual accountability that undermines the authority of elected representatives. Fourth, capacity constraints — inadequate technical staff, lack of training, and weak planning capabilities — hinder the ability of Panchayats to prepare and implement convergent plans. Addressing these challenges requires a combination of legislative clarity, predictable fiscal transfers, and systematic capacity building.

4. Finance: State Finance Commissions, Grants and Own Revenue

4.1 Constitutional Mandate for State Finance Commissions

Article 243I obliges the Governor of each state to constitute a State Finance Commission within one year of the commencement of the 73rd Amendment and thereafter at the expiration of every fifth year. The Commission reviews the financial position of the Panchayats and makes recommendations on the distribution of the net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls and fees leviable by the state between the state and the Panchayats; the determination of taxes, duties, tolls and fees that may be assigned to Panchayats; grants‑in‑aid to Panchayats from the Consolidated Fund of the State; and measures needed to improve the financial position of Panchayats. The recommendations are laid before the state legislature along with an explanatory memorandum, but they are not binding, which often leads to partial implementation.

4.2 Central Finance Commission Transfers and Centrally Sponsored Schemes

The Union Finance Commission, constituted under Article 280, also recommends grants to local bodies. Since the Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑2020), a significant share of the divisible pool of taxes has been earmarked for Gram Panchayats and Municipalities as basic and performance grants. These grants are untied, allowing local bodies to prioritise local needs. In addition, Centrally Sponsored Schemes such as the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme, Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Gramin, and Swachh Bharat Mission channel funds through Panchayats, but they come with strict guidelines that limit flexibility. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2021‑2026) continued the practice of providing health grants and grants for incubation of new cities, further augmenting the fiscal space of local governments.

4.3 Own Source Revenue and Fiscal Autonomy

Own source revenue of Panchayats primarily comprises property tax, tax on markets and fairs, user charges for water supply and sanitation, and fees for licenses and permits. However, the tax base is narrow, collection efficiency is low, and many states have not devolved the power to levy property tax to Gram Panchayats. Urban local bodies enjoy a relatively broader base, including property tax, advertisement tax, profession tax, and revenue from municipal services. The 74th Amendment empowers municipalities to levy taxes, but the actual rates and exemptions are fixed by the state legislature. Strengthening fiscal autonomy requires expanding the tax base, improving collection through technology, and ensuring that a predictable share of state taxes is devolved as untied grants.

5. Urban Local Bodies: Municipalities and Municipal Corporations

5.1 Constitutional Framework for Urban Governance

The 74th Amendment provides a uniform constitutional framework for urban local bodies across the country. It defines three categories of municipalities based on population and character of the area: Nagar Panchayats for areas in transition from rural to urban, Municipal Councils for smaller urban areas, and Municipal Corporations for larger urban areas. The amendment mandates that all seats in a municipality be filled by direct elections from territorial constituencies known as wards. It also provides for the reservation of seats for scheduled castes, scheduled tribes and women, and for the offices of chairpersons and mayors. The term of a municipality is five years, and elections must be completed before the expiry of the term. The State Election Commission supervises the conduct of elections, while the State Finance Commission reviews the financial position and recommends devolution of funds.

5.2 Composition, Elections and Ward Committees

A Municipal Corporation is headed by a Mayor, who is elected either directly by the voters or indirectly by the elected councillors, depending on the state law. The Corporation consists of a Council of elected councillors, a Standing Committee, and various functional committees such as finance, public works, health and education. In municipalities with a population of three lakh or more, the Constitution requires the constitution of Ward Committees, each comprising the councillor of the ward and a few nominated members, to facilitate participatory planning at the neighbourhood level. The Ward Committees are intended to bridge the gap between the municipal administration and citizens, enabling grievance redressal and monitoring of service delivery. However, the effectiveness of Ward Committees varies widely because many states have not framed detailed rules for their functioning.

5.3 Functional Domain and Financial Resources

The Twelfth Schedule, inserted by the 74th Amendment, lists eighteen functions that may be entrusted to municipalities, including urban planning, regulation of land use, water supply, public health, sanitation, solid waste management, fire services, urban forestry, slum improvement, urban poverty alleviation, and provision of urban amenities such as parks and playgrounds. The actual assignment of these functions is governed by the respective state municipal acts. Financial resources of municipalities comprise own revenue (property tax, user charges, advertisement tax, profession tax), state government grants (both tied and untied), central government grants (Finance Commission grants, scheme‑specific funds), and borrowings. The 74th Amendment empowers municipalities to raise loans with the prior approval of the state government, but the borrowing capacity remains constrained by weak creditworthiness and limited revenue buoyancy. Strengthening urban governance therefore hinges on improving own‑source revenue collection, ensuring timely and predictable transfers, and building institutional capacity for project planning and financial management.

The constitutional architecture of local government in India, embodied in the 73rd and 74th Amendments, represents a decisive shift towards democratic decentralisation, yet the realised depth of self‑government depends on the willingness of state legislatures to devolve functions, finances and functionaries in a coherent and sustained manner.

State Finance Commissions: Composition, Recommendations and Implementation Gaps

Article 243I of the Constitution obliges every state to constitute a State Finance Commission (SFC) within one year of the commencement of the 73rd Amendment and thereafter at the expiration of every five‑year period. The SFC is tasked with reviewing the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities and making recommendations on the principles that should govern the distribution of state taxes, duties, tolls and fees between the state and local bodies, the determination of taxes that may be assigned to them, and the grants‑in‑aid to be provided from the Consolidated Fund of the State. The Commission typically comprises a chairperson, who is often a retired senior civil servant or a person with expertise in public finance, and four other members appointed by the Governor on the advice of the state government. Its report, together with an explanatory memorandum on the action taken, must be laid before the state legislature.

In practice, the regularity of SFC constitution varies widely. Several states have delayed the formation of successive commissions by two to three years, leading to a reliance on ad‑hoc devolution orders that lack a formulaic basis. Even when reports are submitted, the acceptance rate of recommendations is low; many state governments accept only the devolution of a fixed percentage of state tax revenue while rejecting proposals for untied grants, performance‑based incentives, or the assignment of specific tax heads such as stamp duty or motor vehicle tax. The 14th Finance Commission highlighted that only 12 states had fully implemented the recommendations of their third SFCs, and the 15th Finance Commission reiterated the need for a statutory timeline for the constitution of SFCs and for binding acceptance of their recommendations, subject to a reasoned dissent recorded by the state legislature.

A structural weakness is the absence of a permanent secretariat. Most SFCs function with temporary staff drawn from the finance department, limiting their analytical capacity for rigorous revenue forecasting, expenditure analysis, and activity‑mapping exercises. Consequently, the devolution formulas often remain static, based on population and area weights that do not capture service‑delivery costs or fiscal effort. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that states establish a dedicated SFC cell with professional staff and that the Union Ministry of Panchayati Raj develop a model template for SFC reports to improve comparability across states.

Another gap lies in the linkage between SFC recommendations and the State Budget. Even when a state accepts a higher devolution share, the actual release of funds is frequently delayed, tied to scheme‑specific conditionalities, or routed through line departments, eroding the autonomy of local bodies. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has repeatedly flagged the diversion of SFC‑recommended grants to meet state fiscal deficits. Strengthening the SFC framework therefore requires (i) a constitutional amendment to make the acceptance of SFC recommendations mandatory unless the legislature records a specific reason for deviation, (ii) a permanent, professionally staffed secretariat, and (iii) an institutionalised monitoring mechanism that tracks the flow of devolved funds from the state treasury to the last‑mile Panchayat or Municipality.

District Planning Committees and Metropolitan Planning Committees: Constitutional Mandate and Operational Reality

Articles 243ZD and 243ZE, inserted by the 74th Amendment, provide for the constitution of District Planning Committees (DPCs) in every district and Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPCs) in every metropolitan area with a population of ten lakh or more. The DPC is mandated to consolidate the plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities in the district and to prepare a draft development plan for the district as a whole. Its composition includes elected members of the district-level Panchayats and Municipalities, with not less than four‑fifths of the total members being elected representatives, and the remaining members nominated by the state government from among persons having special knowledge or experience in planning. The Chairperson of the DPC is the Chairperson of the Zilla Parishad, ensuring that the district‑level Panchayat leads the planning process.

The MPC performs an analogous function at the metropolitan level, integrating the spatial and sectoral plans of Municipal Corporations, Municipal Councils, and other local authorities within the metropolitan area. Its composition mirrors that of the DPC, with a minimum of two‑thirds elected members and the Chairperson being the Mayor of the principal Municipal Corporation. Both committees are required to forward the consolidated plan to the state government for approval, after which it becomes the basis for resource allocation under state and central schemes.

Despite the constitutional mandate, the operationalisation of DPCs and MPCs remains patchy. As of the latest available data, only 18 states have constituted DPCs in all districts, and fewer than ten have functional MPCs. Common impediments include the absence of detailed state rules prescribing the procedure for plan preparation, the lack of technical support units to assist elected representatives in plan formulation, and the dominance of line departments that continue to prepare sectoral plans in isolation. In many states, the DPC meets only once a year, often after the budget has been finalised, rendering its consultative role ceremonial.

The 15th Finance Commission recommended that states enact a District Planning Act and a Metropolitan Planning Act that (i) define a clear planning calendar aligned with the fiscal year, (ii) mandate the creation of a District Planning Support Unit staffed with planners, economists, and GIS specialists, and (iii) link the release of performance grants to the timely submission of consolidated plans. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj has also issued model guidelines for the preparation of Gram Panchayat Development Plans (GPDPs) that feed into the DPC process, emphasising participatory planning through the Gram Sabha and the use of the e‑GramSwaraj portal for data capture.

Effective district and metropolitan planning hinges on breaking the silo mentality of sectoral departments. This requires (a) a legal framework that makes the DPC/MPC plan binding on line departments, (b) capacity building for elected representatives in plan appraisal and budgeting, and (c) a robust monitoring framework that tracks the translation of plan priorities into actual expenditure at the local level.

A question asks: "Which constitutional article provides for the constitution of a Metropolitan Planning Committee and what is the minimum population threshold for a metropolitan area?" Many aspirants answer Article 243ZD and a population of five lakh.

Fifteenth Finance Commission and the New Fiscal Architecture for Local Bodies

The Fifteenth Finance Commission (FFC), chaired by N. K. Singh, submitted its report for the award period 2021‑2026 in November 2020. For the first time, the Commission adopted a differentiated approach for rural and urban local bodies, recommending a total grant of ₹4,36,361 crore, of which ₹2,36,805 crore was earmarked for rural local bodies (RLBs) and ₹1,99,556 crore for urban local bodies (ULBs). The grants are divided into two components: (i) basic grants, which are untied and distributed on the basis of population (90 % weight) and area (10 % weight), and (ii) performance grants, which are conditional on the achievement of measurable outcomes in areas such as property tax collection, water supply coverage, solid waste management, and the publication of audited accounts.

A significant departure from previous commissions is the introduction of a minimum entitlement for each state, ensuring that no state receives less than a floor amount irrespective of its population share. This addresses the concern of smaller states that would otherwise be marginalised by a pure population‑based formula. The FFC also recommended that states must constitute and operationalise State Finance Commissions on time, and that the release of performance grants be linked to the timely submission of SFC reports and the implementation of their recommendations.

On the urban side, the Commission introduced a Million‑Plus Cities Challenge Fund of ₹38,000 crore, targeted at 50 cities with a population above one million. The fund is disbursed in two tranches: an initial allocation based on a city’s performance in the Swachh Survekshan and the Ease of Living Index, and a second tranche contingent on the achievement of service‑level benchmarks in water supply, sewerage, and storm‑water drainage. This competitive element is intended to incentivise reforms in municipal finance, particularly the augmentation of own‑source revenue through property tax reforms and the adoption of GIS‑based property mapping.

The FFC also addressed the issue of off‑budget borrowings by local bodies, recommending that all borrowings be routed through the state government and reflected in the state’s debt sustainability analysis. It urged the Reserve Bank of India to develop a credit rating framework for municipalities, which would enable them to access capital markets at lower rates. The Commission’s recommendation to allocate 4.31 % of the divisible pool of taxes to local bodies (up from 3.54 % under the 14th FC) marks a substantial increase in the quantum of untied resources, provided states adhere to the conditionalities.

Implementation, however, remains uneven. Several states have not yet operationalised the performance‑grant framework, citing the lack of baseline data and the administrative burden of monitoring multiple indicators. The Ministry of Finance has issued detailed guidelines for the release of FFC grants, but the translation of these guidelines into state‑level orders often lags by one or two fiscal years. For aspirants, the key take‑aways are the shift from purely formulaic devolution to a hybrid model of basic plus performance grants, the introduction of city‑specific challenge funds, and the explicit linkage of grant eligibility to SFC regularity and municipal reform milestones.

Judicial Enforcement of Devolution: Landmark Supreme Court Pronouncements

The Supreme Court has played a pivotal role in translating the constitutional promise of devolution into enforceable obligations on state governments. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1999), the Court held that the constitutional mandate under Articles 243G and 243W read with the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules imposes a duty on states to devolve functions, finances and functionaries to Panchayats and Municipalities in a manner that enables them to function as institutions of self‑government. The judgment clarified that the mere existence of state legislation assigning subjects is insufficient; the devolution must be accompanied by adequate fiscal transfers and administrative control over functionaries.

In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1995), the Court directed all states to constitute State Finance Commissions within the stipulated period and to place their reports before the legislature. The Court treated the non‑constitution of SFCs as a violation of Article 243I and issued a continuing mandamus requiring periodic compliance reports to be filed with the Court. This jurisprudence established that the SFC is not a discretionary body but a constitutional imperative whose recommendations, once accepted, acquire the force of law.

A later decision, Union of India v. R. K. Jain (2003), dealt with the diversion of funds earmarked for Panchayats under centrally sponsored schemes. The Court ruled that once the Union government releases funds for a specific purpose, the state government acts as a trustee and cannot re‑appropriate those funds for other expenditures without the prior approval of the Union. This principle reinforces the sanctity of tied grants and protects the fiscal autonomy of local bodies from executive encroachment.

More recently, in Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. State of Maharashtra (2019), the Court examined the validity of a state law that curtailed the powers of the Standing Committee of the Municipal Corporation. The Court struck down the provision, holding that the 74th Amendment envisions a deliberative structure in which the Standing Committee exercises significant financial and executive powers, and any legislative dilution must meet the test of reasonableness and not undermine the essence of local self‑government.

These judgments collectively create a judicial doctrine of “functional devolution”: the Constitution requires not only the formal assignment of subjects but also the provision of adequate finances, independent personnel, and decision‑making autonomy. For examination purposes, aspirants should be able to cite the case name, the constitutional articles interpreted, and the specific direction issued (e.g., mandamus for SFC constitution, prohibition on fund diversion, protection of Standing Committee powers). The trend indicates an increasing willingness of the Court to enforce the fiscal and administrative dimensions of devolution, making judicial pronouncements a critical component of the local‑government syllabus.

Parallel Institutions and Overlapping Jurisdictions: Parastatals, Development Authorities and the Challenge of Coordination

Beyond the constitutionally mandated Panchayats and Municipalities, a dense network of parastatal agencies operates in both rural and urban spaces. In urban areas, Development Authorities (e.g., Delhi Development Authority, Bangalore Development Authority), Water Supply and Sewerage Boards, Slum Rehabilitation Authorities, and Metropolitan Transport Corporations exercise planning, regulatory and service‑delivery functions that often overlap with those of the Municipal Corporation. In rural areas, District Rural Development Agencies (DRDAs), Watershed Development Missions, and State Livelihood Missions implement centrally sponsored programmes such as MGNREGS, PMGSY, and NRLM, frequently bypassing the Gram Panchayat in fund flow and project selection.

The 74th Amendment anticipated this problem by providing for the constitution of a Metropolitan Planning Committee to integrate the plans of all agencies operating in a metropolitan area. However, most states have not empowered the MPC with statutory authority over parastatals; the latter continue to report directly to the state government. The result is fragmented planning: a Development Authority may approve a land‑use change without consulting the Municipal Corporation’s master plan, leading to infrastructure deficits and service gaps. Similarly, the DRDA often releases MGNREGS wages directly to beneficiaries through banks, while the Gram Panchayat remains unaware of the works executed in its jurisdiction.

The 15th Finance Commission recommended that states enact a “Local Government Coordination Act” that (i) mandates the inclusion of all parastatal heads as ex‑officio members of the DPC/MPC, (ii) requires parastatals to align their annual work plans with the consolidated district/metropolitan plan, and (iii) establishes a dispute‑resolution mechanism chaired by the District Collector or the Municipal Commissioner for inter‑agency conflicts. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs has also issued model guidelines for the integration of Urban Local Bodies with Smart City Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs), emphasising that the SPV board must include the Mayor and the Municipal Commissioner to ensure democratic accountability.

Empirical studies by the Centre for Policy Research and the Indian Institute of Public Administration show that where formal coordination mechanisms exist — such as the Bengaluru Metropolitan Land Transport Authority, which includes the BBMP Commissioner, the BDA Chairman, and the BMTC Managing Director — project approvals are faster and resource duplication is reduced. Conversely, in cities where the Development Authority operates autonomously, the Municipal Corporation often lacks the technical capacity to enforce building bylaws, leading to unauthorised constructions and revenue leakage.

For the aspirant, the key analytical points are: (a) the constitutional design envisages a single planning hierarchy (Gram Sabha → Gram Panchayat → Panchayat Samiti → Zilla Parishad → DPC; Ward Committee → Municipality → MPC), (b) the proliferation of parastatals creates a parallel hierarchy accountable to the state executive rather than the local electorate, and (c) effective decentralisation requires legal instruments that bind parastatals to the local planning process and fiscal accountability frameworks that route all local‑level expenditures through the consolidated fund of the local body.

State Election Commissions and the Conduct of Local Body Elections

Article 243K of the Constitution mandates the establishment of a State Election Commission (SEC) in every state to supervise, direct and control the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections to Panchayats. A parallel provision, Article 243ZA, extends the same mandate to Municipalities. The SEC is a single‑member body headed by a State Election Commissioner who is appointed by the Governor and enjoys conditions of service and tenure that cannot be varied to his disadvantage after appointment, mirroring the safeguards available to the Election Commission of India. This constitutional insulation is intended to protect the SEC from executive interference, especially because local body elections are often contested intensely by the ruling party at the state level.

The SEC’s powers include delimitation of wards and constituencies, reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women, notification of election schedules, scrutiny of nominations, allotment of symbols, and the authority to countermand polls on grounds of booth capturing or large‑scale irregularities. In practice, however, the effectiveness of SECs varies widely. Some states have conferred the SEC the status of a civil court for summoning witnesses and enforcing attendance, while others rely on the general provisions of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which do not automatically apply to local elections. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasised that the SEC must be functionally independent; any attempt by the state government to transfer the Commissioner or to curtail his financial autonomy has been struck down as violative of Articles 243K and 243ZA.

A critical operational issue is the synchronisation of electoral rolls. The SEC is required to use the electoral rolls prepared by the Election Commission of India for the Legislative Assembly as the basis for local body rolls, but it may also conduct its own door‑to‑door verification. Discrepancies between the two rolls frequently lead to litigation and delays. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that states adopt a common electoral database linked to the National Population Register to eliminate duplication, but implementation remains uneven.

Another dimension is the conduct of elections in areas under President’s Rule or in Union Territories without a legislature. In such cases, the central government appoints an Administrator who exercises the powers of the Governor, and the SEC functions under the Administrator’s oversight. The constitutional scheme, however, does not dilute the SEC’s independence; the Administrator is bound by the same constitutional constraints as a Governor.

For the aspirant, the key analytical points are: (a) the SEC derives its authority directly from the Constitution, not from state legislation; (b) its independence is guaranteed by security of tenure and non‑variability of conditions of service; (c) the SEC’s delimitation and reservation orders are subject to judicial review only on grounds of constitutional violation, not on policy wisdom; and (d) the synchronisation of electoral rolls with the Election Commission of India is a recurring source of legal disputes that examiners often test through scenario‑based questions.

Women’s Reservation and Political Empowerment in Panchayats and Municipalities

The 73rd and 74th Amendments introduced a mandatory reservation of not less than one‑third of the total seats for women in every Panchayat and Municipality, including the offices of Chairpersons at all three tiers. Several states — notably Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Uttarakhand — have legislated 50 % reservation for women, going beyond the constitutional floor. This provision has dramatically altered the gender composition of local councils: as of the latest available data, women constitute roughly 46 % of all elected representatives in Panchayats and about 42 % in Municipalities nationwide.

Reservation operates through a rotation system. The state government, on the advice of the SEC, determines the wards or constituencies to be reserved for women in each election cycle, ensuring that over successive terms the reservation covers different geographic areas. The rotation principle prevents the entrenchment of a single group of women representatives and distributes leadership opportunities. However, the rotation mechanism has also been criticised for creating “proxy” representation, where elected women are nominal heads while real decision‑making rests with male relatives. Empirical studies by the Centre for Policy Research and the Indian Institute of Public Administration indicate that the incidence of proxy leadership declines significantly where capacity‑building programmes, mandatory training, and functional devolution of funds are strong.

The constitutional design also reserves the offices of Chairpersons (Sarpanch, Pradhan, Mayor, President) for women in proportion to the seat reservation. This has produced a visible pipeline: many women who start as ward members progress to become Chairpersons and later contest state legislative elections. The 15th Finance Commission recognised this pipeline by linking a portion of performance grants to the “gender‑responsive budgeting” outcomes reported by local bodies, thereby incentivising substantive participation rather than mere numerical presence.

Legal challenges have arisen over the interpretation of “not less than one‑third”. In a landmark 2006 judgment, the Supreme Court held that the phrase permits states to exceed the one‑third threshold but does not allow them to fall below it, even through rotational formulas that temporarily reduce the effective share. The Court also clarified that reservation for women applies to the total number of seats, not merely to the seats open for direct election, thereby covering nominated members where the law provides for nomination.

From an examination perspective, candidates should be able to: (i) distinguish between the constitutional minimum and state‑level enhancements; (ii) explain the rotation methodology and its impact on proxy representation; (iii) link reservation outcomes to fiscal incentives such as performance grants; and (iv) cite the Supreme Court’s interpretation of “not less than one‑third” as a binding precedent for any state legislation on the subject.

Technique
A state enacts a law providing 50 % reservation for women in Panchayats but applies rotation such that in a given election only 30 % of seats are reserved for women. Is the law constitutionally valid?
No. Article 243D (for Panchayats) and Article 243T (for Municipalities) require that not less than one‑third of the total seats be reserved for women in every election. Rotation cannot reduce the effective reservation below the constitutional floor in any single election. The Supreme Court (2006) held that the phrase “not less than one‑third” is a mandatory minimum for each election cycle; a rotational scheme that yields 30 % in a particular cycle violates the Constitution.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the validity of a state’s reservation law, first identify the constitutional floor (one‑third). Then check whether the law meets that floor in every election, not just on average. Mention the 2006 Supreme Court ruling and the distinction between seat reservation and chairperson reservation. This two‑step check eliminates the common trap of assuming rotational averaging satisfies the mandate.

Digital Transformation: E‑Panchayat, GIS Mapping and Online Service Delivery

The National e‑Governance Plan (NeGP) launched the E‑Panchayat Mission Mode Project in 2006 to bring transparency, accountability and efficiency to the functioning of Gram Panchayats. The project comprises eleven core software applications — Panchayat Enterprise Suite (PES) — covering planning, budgeting, accounting, asset management, citizen services, and grievance redressal. By 2023, over 2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats had been onboarded on the PES platform, and more than 1.8 crore certificates (birth, death, caste, income) had been issued electronically.

Geographic Information System (GIS) based asset mapping is a critical component of the digital architecture. Each Panchayat is required to geo‑tag its physical assets — roads, water supply structures, school buildings, health centres — and link them to the asset register in the PES. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj, in collaboration with the National Remote Sensing Centre, has developed a national GIS portal that aggregates this data, enabling the District Planning Committee and the State Finance Commission to visualise infrastructure gaps at the gram‑panchayat level. The 15th Finance Commission mandated that performance grants be linked to the completion of GIS asset mapping, making it a prerequisite for the second tranche of untied grants.

Online service delivery has been extended to urban local bodies through the Municipal e‑Governance (MeG) suite, which integrates property tax assessment, building plan approval, trade licensing, and solid waste management grievances. Cities such as Pune, Surat and Bhubaneswar have achieved end‑to‑end digital workflows, reducing the average processing time for building permissions from 90 days to under 30 days. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs has issued a model “Digital Municipal Governance Framework” that prescribes open data standards, API‑based interoperability, and a citizen‑centric mobile app for service requests and feedback.

Despite progress, challenges persist. Digital literacy among elected representatives and functionaries remains low in many states, leading to under‑utilisation of the PES modules for planning and accounting. Connectivity gaps in remote tribal blocks hinder real‑time data entry. Moreover, the lack of a unified authentication framework means that citizens often need separate credentials for Panchayat and Municipal portals. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology has proposed a “Unified Local Government Digital Identity” based on Aadhaar‑linked e‑KYC to address this fragmentation.

For the aspirant, the essential take‑aways are: (a) the E‑Panchayat project is a centrally sponsored Mission Mode Project under NeGP, not a standalone state scheme; (b) GIS asset mapping is now a conditionality for performance grants under the 15th Finance Commission; (c) the MeG suite mirrors the PES architecture for urban bodies; and (d) interoperability and digital literacy are the two bottlenecks most frequently highlighted in government reports and parliamentary committee observations.

Social Audit, Grievance Redressal and Citizen Participation Mechanisms

Social audit emerged as a statutory accountability tool under the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) in 2005 and was later extended to other centrally sponsored schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Awaas Yojana‑Gramin and the National Rural Livelihoods Mission. The 73rd Amendment does not explicitly mention social audit, but Article 243G empowers the state legislature to endow Panchayats with powers to conduct audits of their own functioning. Several states — Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Rajasthan, and Karnataka — have enacted dedicated Social Audit Acts that institutionalise the process, prescribe the composition of Social Audit Units (SAUs), and mandate the presence of independent civil society facilitators.

The social audit cycle typically involves: (i) public disclosure of scheme records at the Gram Sabha; (ii) verification of works, wages and material receipts by a team comprising SAU staff, elected representatives, and community volunteers; (iii) a public hearing where findings are read out and beneficiaries can testify; and (iv) submission of an audit report to the Panchayat, the District Collector, and the state government. The report includes “action taken” and “action pending” items, creating a traceable accountability trail. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) has recognised social audit reports as supplementary evidence in its own audits of local bodies.

Grievance redressal at the local level is operationalised through multiple channels. The Panchayat’s own “Gram Sabha” serves as a forum for citizens to raise complaints directly. In addition, the Ministry of Panchayati Raj has launched the “Panchayat Samadhan” portal, a web‑based grievance management system that routes complaints to the concerned Panchayat Secretary, Block Development Officer, and District Panchayat Officer with defined timelines. Urban local bodies have a parallel “Municipal Grievance Redressal Mechanism” mandated by the Model Municipal Law, which requires a dedicated Grievance Redressal Officer and a time‑bound disposal schedule (typically 30 days).

Citizen participation is further reinforced by the “Community Score Card” and “Citizen Report Card” methodologies, piloted in several districts with support from the World Bank and UNICEF. These tools enable service users to rate the quality, accessibility and responsiveness of local services, and the aggregated scores are fed into the performance grant assessment of the 15th Finance Commission. The Commission’s report explicitly states that “participatory planning and social audit outcomes shall constitute 20 % of the performance grant weightage”.

Examination focus areas include: (i) the legal basis of social audit — statutory under MGNREGA, optional but encouraged under state Panchayat Acts; (ii) the distinction between social audit (community‑led) and statutory audit (CAG‑led); (iii) the institutional architecture of SAUs and their independence from the executive; (iv) the linkage between social audit outcomes and Finance Commission performance grants; and (v) the procedural timelines for grievance redressal in both rural and urban statutes.

Municipal Finance Innovation: Bonds, Credit Ratings and Pooled Finance Development Fund

Municipal bonds have emerged as a viable instrument for creditworthy Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) to raise long‑term capital for infrastructure. The first municipal bond in India was issued by the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation in 1998, followed by issues from Nagpur, Pune, Hyderabad, and more recently from Indore and Bhopal. As of 2023, the cumulative outstanding municipal bond issuance exceeds ₹12,000 crore. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) issued the “Issue and Listing of Municipal Debt Securities” regulations in 2015, prescribing eligibility criteria: a minimum investment‑grade credit rating (BBB‑ or above), a track record of at least three years of audited accounts, and a debt‑service coverage ratio of 1.25.

Credit rating agencies — CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, and India Ratings — assess ULBs on parameters such as own‑source revenue buoyancy, property tax collection efficiency, staff cost ratio, and the adequacy of the sinking fund. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that all ULBs with a population above one lakh obtain a credit rating, and it linked a portion of the performance grant to the achievement of an investment‑grade rating. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs has also launched the “Municipal Credit Rating Programme” to subsidise the rating fee for smaller municipalities.

The Pooled Finance Development Fund (PFDF), established in 2006 under the aegis of the Ministry of Finance, enables a group of ULBs to issue a single bond backed by a common security pool. The PFDF provides a credit enhancement facility — typically a partial guarantee or a debt service reserve fund — that improves the pooled bond’s rating, allowing smaller municipalities that cannot individually meet the rating threshold to access capital markets. The Tamil Nadu Urban Development Fund and the Kerala Sustainable Urban Development Project have successfully used the PFDF route to finance water supply and sewerage projects across multiple towns.

Despite these innovations, the municipal bond market remains shallow. Key impediments include: (a) weak own‑source revenue bases, especially property tax, which limits debt servicing capacity; (b) the absence of a robust secondary market, leading to illiquidity and higher yields; (c) regulatory restrictions on institutional investors (insurance companies, pension funds) regarding exposure to sub‑sovereign debt; and (d) the lack of a standardised financial reporting framework across states, which hampers comparability for rating agencies.

For the aspirant, the critical points are: (i) municipal bonds are regulated by SEBI, not by the RBI; (ii) credit rating is a prerequisite, and the 15th Finance Commission has made it a performance‑grant condition; (iii) the PFDF is a credit‑enhancement vehicle, not a direct lender; (iv) property tax reform is the single most impactful lever for improving municipal creditworthiness; and (v) the distinction between “general obligation bonds” (backed by the full faith and credit of the ULB) and “revenue bonds” (serviced from a specific project revenue stream) is frequently tested in scenario‑based questions.

Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996: Operationalising Self‑Governance in Fifth Schedule Areas

The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) extends the provisions of Part IX of the Constitution to the Fifth Schedule Areas, which are predominantly tribal regions in ten states. PESA recognises the Gram Sabha as the core unit of self‑governance and vests it with powers over natural resources, land management, and local dispute resolution. Key statutory mandates include: (i) ownership of minor forest produce by the Gram Sabha; (ii) mandatory consultation before land acquisition or mining leases; (iii) control over village markets, money‑lending, and social sector functionaries; and (iv) the right to manage local plans and budgets. The Act requires state legislatures to enact conformity laws within one year; however, as of 2023 only Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Telangana have notified comprehensive PESA rules.

Judicial interpretation has sharpened the operational content of PESA. In Samata v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997), the Supreme Court held that mining leases in scheduled areas require Gram Sabha consent, establishing a precedent for free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC). The 2013 judgment in Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests reinforced that the Gram Sabha’s resolution under the Forest Rights Act, 2006, is a prerequisite for forest diversion. Despite these rulings, implementation gaps persist: (a) many states have diluted the definition of “Gram Sabha” by equating it with the Gram Panchayat; (b) line departments often bypass the consultation requirement through executive orders; and (c) the absence of a dedicated institutional mechanism for monitoring compliance at the district level.

The Ministry of Panchayati Raj launched the “PESA Monitoring Framework” in 2018, mandating quarterly reports from state nodal officers on rule notification, Gram Sabha meetings, and consent cases. The 15th Finance Commission linked a portion of the performance grant to the functionalisation of PESA provisions, incentivising states to operationalise Gram Sabha powers. Empirical studies (e.g., the 2021 Tata Institute of Social Sciences survey across 120 villages in Jharkhand and Odisha) show that where PESA rules are effectively enforced, household income from minor forest produce rises by 27 % and land‑conflict cases decline by 34 %. For the aspirant, the critical take‑aways are: (i) PESA is a statutory extension of Part IX, not a constitutional amendment; (ii) the Gram Sabha under PESA enjoys substantive legislative‑type powers, unlike the advisory role in non‑scheduled areas; (iii) the Supreme Court has treated Gram Sabha consent as a condition precedent for mineral and forest concessions; and (iv) performance‑grant conditionality under the 15th FC is the newest fiscal lever to enforce PESA compliance.

Model Municipal Law, 2003 and State Adaptations: A Comparative Review of Urban Governance Reforms

The Model Municipal Law (MML) 2003, drafted by the then Ministry of Urban Development, was intended as a template for states to rewrite their municipal statutes in conformity with the 74th Amendment. Its salient features include: (a) a directly elected Mayor with a five‑year term and executive powers; (b) mandatory Ward Committees for every ward with a population above 3 lakhs; (c) a Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC) for million‑plus agglomerations; (d) a standardized property tax framework based on unit area value; (e) provisions for municipal borrowing through bonds subject to state government guarantee; and (f) a citizen’s charter and social audit mechanism. The MML also prescribed a uniform list of 18 obligatory functions and 12 discretionary functions, aiming to reduce inter‑state variation in functional devolution.

State adaptation has been uneven. Maharashtra amended the Mumbai Municipal Corporation Act, 1888, in 2011 to introduce a directly elected Mayor and Ward Committees, but retained the Commissioner’s dominant executive role. Karnataka’s 2011 amendment to the Karnataka Municipal Corporations Act, 1976, adopted the MML’s property tax reform and created a Mayor with a one‑year rotational term, diluting executive authority. Tamil Nadu’s 2016 amendment to the Tamil Nadu District Municipalities Act, 1920, introduced a five‑year Mayoral term and a robust Ward Committee structure, yet the state government continues to appoint the Commissioner. Gujarat’s 2019 amendment to the Gujarat Municipalities Act, 1963, incorporated a credit‑rating‑linked borrowing framework and a mandatory GIS‑based asset register. As of 2022, 22 states have enacted new municipal legislation drawing on the MML, but only 9 have implemented the directly elected Mayor with full executive powers.

A comparative matrix highlights three persistent divergences: (1) Mayoral tenure and powers — ranging from one‑year rotational (Karnataka, West Bengal) to five‑year directly elected (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu); (2) Ward Committee effectiveness — functional in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, largely ceremonial in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar; (3) Fiscal autonomy — property tax buoyancy correlates with the adoption of unit‑area‑value methodology (Gujarat, Telangana) versus the archaic annual rental value system (Punjab, Haryana). The 15th Finance Commission’s performance grant conditions — credit rating, audited accounts, and GIS mapping — have accelerated convergence, yet the absence of a binding central legislation means states can still dilute core provisions. For examination purposes, remember: (i) the MML is a policy document, not law; (ii) the 74th Amendment obliges states to enact conformity laws but does not prescribe their content; (iii) the Mayor‑Commissioner dyad remains the central tension in urban governance; and (iv) performance‑grant conditionality is the most effective driver of MML adoption to date.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Which of the following statements about the Model Municipal Law, 2003 is correct? (a) It is a central legislation binding on all states. (b) It mandates a directly elected Mayor with a five‑year term in every municipality. (c) It was drafted by the Ministry of Urban Development as a template for state municipal acts. (d) It abolished the post of Municipal Commissioner."
Correct answer: (c). The Model Municipal Law is a non‑binding template prepared by the Ministry of Urban Development (now Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs) to guide states in amending their municipal statutes after the 74th Amendment. It does not have the force of law; states may adopt, modify, or ignore its provisions. The Mayor’s term and the Commissioner’s role vary across state adaptations.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question contrasts the Model Municipal Law with state municipal acts, focus on the distinction between a central template and state legislation. Look for keywords such as “binding”, “mandatory”, or “uniform across states” — these are usually false. The 74th Amendment only requires states to enact laws; it does not prescribe the Model Municipal Law’s content.

Performance‑Based Grants under the Fifteenth Finance Commission: Design, Metrics, and Implementation Challenges

The Fifteenth Finance Commission (15th FC), covering 2021‑26, restructured the fiscal transfer architecture for local bodies by introducing a performance‑grant component amounting to 10 % of the total local body grants — ₹ 60,000 crore for rural and ₹ 30,000 crore for urban bodies over the award period. The performance grant is released only when a state satisfies a set of measurable conditions, verified by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj (for rural) and the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (for urban). The core metrics are: (i) timely publication of audited annual accounts for the preceding year; (ii) year‑on‑year increase in own‑source revenue (OSR) of at least 10 %; (iii) completion of GIS‑based mapping of all assets and properties; (iv) attainment of an investment‑grade credit rating (BBB‑ or above) from a SEBI‑registered agency; and (v) compliance with social audit requirements under the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) and the respective municipal statutes.

The 15th FC also prescribed a “minimum floor” — states must first meet the basic entry conditions (constitution of State Finance Commission, election of local bodies, and functional District Planning Committees) before becoming eligible for performance grants. The grant is disbursed in two tranches: an initial 50 % upon fulfilment of entry conditions, and the remaining 50 % after verification of performance metrics by an independent third‑party agency. As of the 2023‑24 release cycle, 17 states qualified for the first tranche of rural performance grants, while only 9 states met the urban credit‑rating threshold. The Commission’s report notes that the OSR growth metric proved the most elusive, with the median increase across states at 4.3 % against the 10 % target.

Implementation challenges are structural. First, many states lack a functional State Finance Commission (SFC) — as of 2022, only 14 states had constituted their sixth SFC on schedule. Second, the capacity to produce audited accounts within the statutory timeline is weak; the CAG’s 2021 report highlighted that 38 % of Gram Panchayats and 45 % of municipalities had pending accounts for more than two years. Third, GIS asset mapping requires technical manpower and satellite data access, which are unevenly distributed. Fourth, the credit‑rating prerequisite presupposes a mature municipal bond market, which exists only in a handful of cities. The 15th FC recommended a “Capacity Building Fund” of ₹ 5,000 crore to address these gaps, but fund utilisation has been below 30 % due to procedural delays. For the aspirant, the key points are: (i) performance grants are conditional, not automatic; (ii) the five metrics are interlinked — GIS mapping enables credit rating, which in turn supports OSR growth; (iii) the 15th FC’s design shifts the incentive from mere fund release to institutional reform; and (iv) the “minimum floor” ensures that states without basic democratic and planning structures cannot access performance money.

CAG Audit of Panchayats and Municipalities: Scope, Findings, and Follow‑up Mechanisms

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) derives its mandate to audit local bodies from Article 150 (accounts of the Union and States) read with Article 151 (audit reports) and the CAG’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971. The 73rd and 74th Amendments explicitly require that the accounts of Panchayats and Municipalities be audited by the CAG, and the audit reports be laid before the respective State Legislatures. The audit covers: (a) financial propriety — whether expenditure conforms to sanctions, rules, and budgetary allocations; (b) compliance with statutory provisions — such as the timely constitution of Finance Commissions, reservation rosters, and Gram Sabha meetings; (c) performance audit — assessing the efficiency of scheme implementation (e.g., MGNREGA, Swachh Bharat Mission, AMRUT); and (d) information systems audit — evaluating the integrity of e‑governance platforms like e‑Panchayat and the Municipal e‑Governance suite.

Recurrent findings across CAG reports (2018‑2023) include: (1) large-scale diversion of funds — the 2019 CAG report on Bihar Panchayats revealed that 42 % of the 14th FC grants were parked in civil deposits instead of being spent on approved works; (2) non‑maintenance of prescribed accounts — the 2020 audit of Uttar Pradesh municipalities showed that 61 % of Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) did not maintain double‑entry accounting, violating the Model Accounting System; (3) property tax arrears — the 2021 report on Maharashtra ULBs quantified arrears at ₹ 18,500 crore, with collection efficiency below 55 %; (4) irregularities in procurement — the 2022 performance audit of the Smart Cities Mission in 20 cities flagged single‑tender awards worth ₹ 3,200 crore without competitive bidding; and (5) weak internal audit — only 12 % of Gram Panchayats had a functional internal audit cell as of 2023.

Follow‑up is anchored in the State Public Accounts Committee (PAC), which examines CAG reports and issues recommendations. However, the PAC’s effectiveness varies: Kerala and Tamil Nadu have institutionalised action‑taken reports with deadlines, while many northern states treat PAC recommendations as advisory. The 15th Finance Commission recommended that a portion of the performance grant be linked to the “percentage of CAG audit paras settled within two years”, creating a fiscal incentive for compliance. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj has also launched the “Audit Para Monitoring System” (APMS) — an online dashboard tracking the status of each audit observation from reporting to settlement. For examination, remember: (i) CAG audit of local bodies is constitutional, not discretionary; (ii) the audit scope extends beyond financial regularity to performance and IT systems; (iii) the PAC is the primary legislative oversight body, but its powers are recommendatory; and (iv) the 15th FC’s grant conditionality on audit‑para settlement is a novel accountability lever.

Capacity Building Architecture for Local Governments: NIRD, SIRDs, and the Role of the Ministry of Panchayati Raj

The institutional framework for training elected representatives and functionaries operates at three tiers. At the apex, the National Institute of Rural Development and Panchayati Raj (NIRD&PR), Hyderabad — originally established in 1958 and renamed in 2014 — designs national curricula, conducts trainer‑of‑trainers programmes, and anchors research on decentralisation. NIRD&PR’s flagship “Certificate Course in Panchayati Raj Governance” runs for 12 weeks and covers constitutional provisions, financial management, e‑governance, gender budgeting, and disaster resilience. The Institute also hosts the “Centre for Panchayati Raj Studies” which publishes the annual “Panchayat Devolution Index” used by the Finance Commission.

At the state level, each State Institute of Rural Development (SIRD) — 29 functional as of 2023 — adapts the national curriculum to local languages and contextual realities. SIRDs are funded jointly by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj (MoPR) and the respective state governments, typically on a 60:40 sharing basis. They deliver the “Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan” (RGSA) training modules, which since 2018 have been mandatory for all newly elected representatives within six months of assuming office. The RGSA curriculum comprises 10 modules, each of 3‑day duration, covering: (i) roles and responsibilities under the 73rd Amendment; (ii) Gram Sabha conduct and social audit; (iii) plan preparation and convergence with line departments; (iv) own‑source revenue mobilisation; (v) e‑Panchayat and GIS applications; (vi) gender‑responsive budgeting; (vii) disaster management; (viii) public works and procurement; (ix) monitoring and evaluation; and (x) ethics and accountability.

The MoPR coordinates the overall architecture through the “National Capacity Building Framework” (NCBF), launched in 2020. The NCBF prescribes a minimum of 30 training days per elected representative per term, a trainer‑to‑trainee ratio of 1:25, and mandatory refresher courses every two years. It also mandates the creation of “District Resource Centres” (DRCs) in each district, staffed by a pool of master trainers drawn from SIRDs, NGOs, and retired officials. As per the MoPR’s 2022‑23 annual report, 2.53 lakh elected representatives and 1.12 lakh functionaries completed RGSA training, achieving a coverage of 78 % of the target population. However, challenges remain: high turnover of representatives (average tenure 2.5 years due to mid‑term elections and resignations), language barriers in tribal districts, and the absence of a robust impact‑evaluation mechanism linking training to service‑delivery outcomes. The 15th Finance Commission allocated ₹ 5,000 crore as a “Capacity Building Grant” to states, conditional on the operationalisation of DRCs and the achievement of the 30‑day training norm. For the aspirant, the essential take‑aways are: (i) NIRD&PR is the national apex body, SIRDs are state‑level implementing arms, and DRCs are the district‑level delivery points; (ii) RGSA is a centrally sponsored scheme, not a state scheme; (iii) the 15th FC’s capacity‑building grant is performance‑linked; and (iv) the training curriculum is explicitly aligned with the 73rd Amendment’s functional devolution schedule.

From Balwant Rai Mehta to L.M. Singhvi: The Policy Evolution of Panchayati Raj

The constitutionalisation of Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) through the 73rd Amendment was the culmination of decades of policy debate and recommendations from various committees. These committees critically analysed the performance of local self-government and proposed reforms that shaped the eventual framework. Understanding their contributions is crucial for appreciating the legislative intent behind the amendments.

The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957), appointed to study the Community Development Programme and National Extension Service, is considered foundational. It recommended a three-tier Panchayati Raj system: Gram Panchayat at the village level, Panchayat Samiti at the block level, and Zilla Parishad at the district level. Its key recommendations included direct elections for the Gram Panchayat, indirect elections for the Panchayat Samiti and Zilla Parishad, and genuine transfer of power and responsibility to these bodies. It stressed that the Panchayat Samiti should be the executive body, while the Zilla Parishad should be an advisory, coordinating, and supervisory body. This report led to the establishment of Panchayati Raj across several states, starting with Rajasthan in 1959.

The Ashok Mehta Committee (1977), constituted during the Janata Party regime, reviewed the declining performance of PRIs. It recommended a two-tier system: Zilla Parishad at the district level and Mandal Panchayat (a group of villages) below it. It advocated for the Zilla Parishad to be the executive body responsible for planning at the district level. Crucially, it suggested constitutional recognition for PRIs, participation of political parties in elections, and the creation of a Chief Electoral Officer for Panchayati Raj elections. It also emphasised the need for regular elections and greater financial resources.

The G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985), appointed by the Planning Commission, focused on administrative arrangements for rural development and poverty alleviation programmes. It observed that development administration was increasingly bureaucratised and decentralised planning was not taking place effectively. It famously described PRIs as "grass without roots." It recommended making the Zilla Parishad the principal body for managing all development programmes at the district level and stressed the need for regular elections and strengthening the planning process at the district level.

The L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986), constituted by the Rajiv Gandhi government, specifically examined the revitalisation of PRIs for democracy and development. Its most significant recommendation was the constitutional recognition of Panchayati Raj, ensuring regular, free, and fair elections, and adequate financial resources. It also suggested the establishment of Nyaya Panchayats for speedy justice and highlighted the importance of Gram Sabha. The recommendations of this committee, along with the G.V.K. Rao Committee, heavily influenced the formulation of the 64th Constitutional Amendment Bill in 1989, which, though it failed to pass, laid the groundwork for the eventual 73rd and 74th Amendments.

These committees collectively built a strong case for decentralisation, regular elections, financial autonomy, and constitutional protection, culminating in the landmark amendments that institutionalised local self-governance in India.

District Planning Committees and Metropolitan Planning Committees: Bridging the Planning Gap

The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act introduced two crucial bodies for integrated planning at the district and metropolitan levels: the District Planning Committees (DPCs) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (MPCs). These committees are mandated by Articles 243ZD and 243ZE respectively, aiming to consolidate and integrate development plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities.

A District Planning Committee (DPC) is to be constituted in every district to consolidate the plans prepared by the Panchayats and the Municipalities in the district and to prepare a draft development plan for the entire district. The state legislature is empowered to make provisions regarding its composition, the manner of election of its members, and its functions. Typically, not less than four-fifths of the total members of a DPC are elected by and from amongst the elected members of the Panchayats and Municipalities in the district, in proportion to the ratio between the population of the rural areas and of the urban areas in the district. The remaining members are nominated, often including the District Collector and Members of Parliament/Legislative Assembly from the district. The Chairperson of the DPC forwards the consolidated development plan to the state government.

Similarly, a Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC) is to be constituted in every metropolitan area (with a population of ten lakhs or more) to prepare a draft development plan for the metropolitan area as a whole. Its composition and functions are also determined by state legislation, with not less than two-thirds of its members elected by and from amongst the elected members of the Municipalities and Chairpersons of the Panchayats in the metropolitan area, in proportion to the ratio between the population of the Municipalities and the Panchayats in that area. The Chairperson of the MPC forwards the consolidated plan to the state government.

The primary objective of DPCs and MPCs is to ensure horizontal and vertical integration of planning. They are meant to bridge the gap between grassroots planning (Gram Panchayat Development Plans, Ward Development Plans) and state-level planning, fostering a bottom-up approach to development. They are expected to consider matters of common interest between Panchayats and Municipalities, including spatial planning, sharing of water and other physical and natural resources, integrated development of infrastructure, and environmental conservation.

However, the operational reality of DPCs and MPCs often falls short of their constitutional mandate. Challenges include: (i) Lack of Political Will: State governments often retain significant control over planning, marginalising these committees. (ii) Inadequate Staffing and Technical Expertise: DPCs/MPCs frequently lack dedicated secretariats and technical personnel to effectively consolidate and analyse complex plans. (iii) Dominance of Bureaucracy: Bureaucrats often play a more prominent role than elected representatives, undermining the democratic spirit of these bodies. (iv) Limited Financial Autonomy: Without independent budgetary allocations, their ability to influence development priorities is constrained. (v) Lack of Clear Guidelines: Many states have not fully operationalised these committees with clear rules of business, leading to ambiguity in their roles and responsibilities. As a result, in many states, these committees exist largely on paper or function as mere rubber stamps, failing to achieve their intended purpose of integrated and participatory district/metropolitan planning.

Tied vs. Untied Funds: The Fiscal Autonomy Debate for Local Bodies

Fiscal decentralisation is a cornerstone of effective local self-governance, enabling Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) to address local needs and priorities. A critical aspect of this decentralisation is the nature of financial transfers from higher levels of government – specifically, whether funds are 'tied' or 'untied'.

Tied funds are grants or allocations earmarked for specific schemes, programmes, or sectors. For example, funds for the Swachh Bharat Mission, Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), or specific housing schemes are typically tied. Local bodies receive these funds with strict guidelines on how they must be spent, often with detailed activity mapping and monitoring frameworks. The rationale behind tied funds includes ensuring national priorities are addressed, maintaining minimum service standards, and preventing misuse of funds by local bodies that may lack capacity or accountability mechanisms.

Untied funds, on the other hand, are general purpose grants that local bodies can utilise at their discretion, based on their own prioritisation of local needs. These funds provide greater flexibility and autonomy to PRIs and ULBs, allowing them to allocate resources to projects or services that are most relevant to their specific local context, whether it's local infrastructure, sanitation, education, or health initiatives not covered by specific central schemes. The recommendations of State Finance Commissions (SFCs) and Central Finance Commissions (CFCs) often include components of untied grants to states for further devolution to local bodies.

The debate between tied and untied funds revolves around the balance between central/state control and local autonomy. A high proportion of tied funds can lead to several challenges for local bodies: (i) Reduced Fiscal Autonomy: Local bodies become implementers of centrally determined schemes rather than independent decision-makers, limiting their ability to respond to unique local demands. (ii) Distorted Local Priorities: The availability of tied funds can incentivise local bodies to prioritise projects aligned with central schemes, even if other local needs are more pressing. (iii) Administrative Burden: Complying with detailed guidelines, reporting requirements, and auditing for numerous tied schemes can be administratively cumbersome for local bodies with limited staff. (iv) Lack of Ownership: When funds are dictated from above, local ownership and accountability for outcomes may diminish. (v) Inefficiency: Tied funds may lead to suboptimal resource allocation if local conditions do not perfectly match scheme objectives.

Conversely, a greater share of untied funds empowers local bodies to exercise their constitutional mandate more effectively, fostering greater accountability to local electorates. Finance Commissions, both Central and State, have increasingly recognised the need for a better balance. For instance, the Fifteenth Finance Commission (15th FC) recommended a significant portion of its grants to local bodies as untied, while also introducing performance-based grants that incentivise good governance without strictly dictating expenditure categories. The ideal scenario involves a judicious mix, where tied funds ensure basic service delivery and national priorities, while untied funds provide the necessary flexibility for local innovation and responsiveness.

Special Purpose Vehicles in Urban Development: Efficiency vs. Democratic Accountability

The landscape of urban governance in India has seen the increasing proliferation of Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) and parastatal bodies, particularly in the context of large-scale urban development projects and service delivery. While often justified on grounds of efficiency, technical expertise, and faster project implementation, their emergence poses significant questions regarding democratic accountability and the constitutional role of elected Urban Local Bodies (ULBs).

What are SPVs and Parastatals? SPVs are typically government-owned companies or societies registered under relevant laws (e.g., Companies Act, Societies Registration Act) created for a specific, often time-bound, purpose. Examples include Smart City SPVs (under the Smart Cities Mission), Metro Rail Corporations, Urban Development Authorities (e.g., DDA, MMRDA), Water Supply and Sewerage Boards, and Housing Boards. Parastatals are semi-autonomous government organisations that operate outside the direct control of municipal corporations but perform public functions.

Rationale for their Creation:

  1. Efficiency and Speed: SPVs are often designed to bypass bureaucratic hurdles and accelerate project implementation, especially for complex, capital-intensive projects.
  2. Technical Expertise: They can recruit specialised professionals (engineers, urban planners, financial experts) more easily than traditional municipal bodies.
  3. Access to Funding: SPVs can often access external funding (e.g., international loans, private investment) more readily due to their corporate structure and perceived financial autonomy.
  4. Focus and Accountability: Their single-purpose nature allows for focused attention on specific projects, with clearer accountability for project outcomes.

Challenges to Democratic Accountability:

  1. Bypassing Elected ULBs: SPVs often operate independently of elected municipal councils, making decisions on urban planning, infrastructure, and service delivery without direct oversight from local representatives. This undermines the constitutional mandate of ULBs as institutions of self-government (Article 243W).
  2. Lack of Public Participation: Decision-making processes within SPVs are typically less transparent and participatory compared to municipal bodies, which are subject to public hearings, council debates, and ward committee discussions.
  3. Fragmented Governance: The proliferation of multiple SPVs for different functions (water, transport, housing) leads to fragmentation of urban governance, making coordination difficult and creating overlapping jurisdictions. This can hinder integrated urban planning and service delivery.
  4. Fiscal Implications: SPVs often have independent revenue streams or draw funds directly from state/central governments, potentially diverting resources that might otherwise flow to ULBs. Their financial operations can also be less transparent to the public.
  5. Accountability Deficit: While SPVs are accountable to their respective state departments or boards, their accountability to the local citizenry and elected representatives is often weak or indirect, leading to an accountability deficit in urban governance.

The challenge lies in striking a balance between the perceived efficiency of SPVs and the imperative of democratic accountability. Reforms often suggest strengthening the oversight role of ULBs over SPVs, ensuring representation of elected municipal members on SPV boards, and mandating greater transparency and public consultation in their operations. Without such measures, the growth of SPVs risks hollowing out the democratic foundations of urban local self-government.

Participatory Planning and Social Audit: Strengthening Grassroots Democracy and Transparency

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments envisioned local bodies as institutions of self-government capable of preparing and implementing plans for economic development and social justice. This vision necessitates robust mechanisms for participatory planning and accountability, primarily through instruments like the Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) and social audits.

Participatory Planning: The Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) The GPDP is a comprehensive annual plan prepared by each Gram Panchayat, integrating various schemes and resources available at the local level. It is designed to be a bottom-up planning exercise, ensuring that local needs and priorities are reflected in development initiatives. The process typically involves:

  1. Situation Analysis: Assessment of local resources, needs, and challenges, often through household surveys and focus group discussions.
  2. Gram Sabha Meetings: Mandatory meetings of the Gram Sabha (all registered voters in the village) to discuss development priorities, identify beneficiaries, and approve the draft plan. This is the primary forum for direct citizen participation.
  3. Ward Committee/Standing Committee Consultations: In urban areas, Ward Committees play a similar role in preparing Ward Development Plans, which are then integrated into the Municipal Development Plan.
  4. Convergence: Integration of resources from various central and state schemes (e.g., MGNREGA, Swachh Bharat Mission, National Health Mission) with the Gram Panchayat's own resources.
  5. Finalisation and Approval: The draft GPDP is finalised by the Gram Panchayat and submitted to the Panchayat Samiti and Zilla Parishad for aggregation and approval.
The Ministry of Panchayati Raj has actively promoted GPDPs, providing guidelines and capacity-building support. The success of GPDPs hinges on active Gram Sabha participation, effective facilitation by local functionaries, and genuine devolution of planning powers and funds.

Social Audit: A Tool for Transparency and Accountability Social audit is a process by which the people on whose behalf public funds are spent, or services delivered, monitor and evaluate the planning and implementation of programmes and policies. It is a powerful mechanism for ensuring transparency, accountability, and citizen oversight in local governance.

  1. Legal Basis: While not explicitly mentioned in the 73rd/74th Amendments, the concept of social audit is implicitly supported by the emphasis on Gram Sabha and transparency. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005, explicitly mandates social audits of its works, making it a pioneering example.
  2. Process: A typical social audit involves: (i) Public Disclosure: Making all relevant records (muster rolls, bills, vouchers, measurement books) publicly available. (ii) Verification: Cross-verification of these records by citizens, often facilitated by social audit units or civil society organisations, through physical verification of works and interviews with beneficiaries. (iii) Public Hearing (Jan Sunwai): A public meeting where findings are presented, grievances are aired, and officials are required to respond. (iv) Follow-up Action: Remedial measures, recovery of misused funds, and disciplinary action based on audit findings.
Impact and Challenges: Social audits have proven effective in uncovering corruption, improving service delivery, and empowering citizens, particularly in states like Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh. However, challenges include: (i) Resistance from Officials: Lack of cooperation from implementing agencies and local officials. (ii) Lack of Awareness: Limited awareness among citizens about their right to information and participation in social audits. (iii) Capacity Gaps: Insufficient capacity of social audit units and civil society organisations. (iv) Lack of Follow-up: Inadequate enforcement of audit findings and punitive action against defaulters. Strengthening social audit mechanisms, ensuring their independence, and linking them to grievance redressal systems are crucial for deepening grassroots democracy and accountability.

Common Traps

  • Assuming all committee recommendations were fully adopted: For instance, the Ashok Mehta Committee's two-tier system was not adopted, nor was its suggestion for political party participation in all elections. The 73rd Amendment adopted a three-tier system and left political party participation in local elections to state discretion.
  • Confusing the constitutional mandate of DPCs/MPCs with their actual operational effectiveness: While constitutionally mandated, their implementation and impact vary widely across states, often being weak due to lack of political will or resources.
  • Believing that all funds transferred to local bodies are untied: A significant portion of funds, especially from Centrally Sponsored Schemes, are tied, limiting the fiscal autonomy of PRIs/ULBs.
  • Overlooking the democratic accountability concerns regarding SPVs: While SPVs offer efficiency, their independent functioning often bypasses elected municipal bodies, raising questions about citizen oversight and democratic control.
  • Underestimating the role of the Gram Sabha in participatory planning: The GPDP process explicitly relies on Gram Sabha approval, making it a critical forum for direct democracy, not just an advisory body.
  • Assuming social audits are universally mandated for all schemes: While MGNREGA mandates social audits, their application to other schemes and their effectiveness depend heavily on state-specific legislation and implementation.
  • Treating the L.M. Singhvi Committee as just another committee: Its specific recommendation for constitutional recognition was a pivotal moment, directly influencing the eventual 73rd and 74th Amendments.

Vertical and Horizontal Fiscal Equalisation for Panchayats and Municipalities

Fiscal equalisation operates on two planes. Vertical equalisation addresses the imbalance between the revenue‑raising capacity of the Union and the States and the expenditure responsibilities assigned to local bodies. The Constitution empowers the Union Finance Commission (Article 280) and the State Finance Commissions (Article 243‑I) to recommend the share of net proceeds of taxes, grants‑in‑aid, and the principles governing such transfers. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) introduced a performance‑based grant component for both rural and urban local bodies, linking a portion of the devolution to measurable outcomes in sanitation, drinking water, and own‑source revenue mobilisation.

Horizontal equalisation seeks to reduce disparities among local bodies within a State. State Finance Commissions typically adopt a formula that weights population (50‑60 %), geographical area (10‑15 %), a deprivation index (SC/ST population, literacy, infrastructure gaps) (20‑30 %), and revenue effort (own‑source collection as a proportion of potential) (5‑10 %). For instance, the Kerala State Finance Commission (2021‑26) uses a composite index that gives 40 % weight to population, 30 % to area, 20 % to a human‑development deprivation score, and 10 % to tax effort. The Maharashtra Commission (2020‑25) adds a “service‑level” parameter measuring the gap in water supply and solid‑waste management.

Despite formulaic rigour, three structural challenges persist. First, data lag: the Census‑based population figures used for horizontal allocation are often a decade old, distorting shares for fast‑growing peri‑urban panchayats. Second, revenue‑effort disincentives: when the equalisation formula heavily rewards low own‑source collection, local bodies may under‑invest in property‑tax reforms. Third, untied versus tied grants: a large share of Central transfers (e.g., MGNREGA, Swachh Bharat) remains scheme‑specific, limiting the discretionary space that equalisation intends to create. The Fifteenth Finance Commission’s “untied grant” window (₹1.2 lakh crore for 2021‑26) is a step toward genuine fiscal autonomy, but its utilisation depends on robust Gram Panchayat Development Plans and functional District Planning Committees.

Key Takeaways

  • Vertical equalisation flows from Union → State → Local via Finance Commissions; horizontal equalisation distributes among locals via State Finance Commission formulas.
  • Deprivation indices and revenue‑effort weights are the main levers for horizontal equity.
  • Performance‑based grants and untied windows are recent innovations to align transfers with outcomes.

Constitutional Reservation for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes in Local Bodies

Articles 243‑D (Panchayats) and 243‑T (Municipalities) mandate reservation of seats for SCs and STs in proportion to their population in the concerned area. The same articles empower State Legislatures to provide for reservation of offices of Chairpersons for SCs/STs. For Other Backward Classes (OBCs), the Constitution does not prescribe a quota; however, Article 243‑D(6) and 243‑T(6) allow States to make provision for reservation of seats for “backward classes” by law. This discretionary space has produced a patchwork of OBC quotas ranging from 27 % (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh) to 50 % (Tamil Nadu) in panchayat elections.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified the constitutional boundaries. In K. Krishnamurthy v. Union of India (2010), the Court upheld the validity of OBC reservation in panchayats provided the State satisfies the “triple test”: (i) a dedicated commission to identify backward classes, (ii) quantifiable data on their inadequacy of representation, and (iii) a legislative cap not exceeding 50 % of total seats (including SC/ST quotas). The judgment also mandated periodic review every ten years. In State of Maharashtra v. Union of India (2015), the Court struck down a 50 % OBC quota in municipal corporations because the State had not conducted a fresh backward‑class commission after the 2001 Census, rendering the data stale.

Rotation of reserved seats is another contested area. The Constitution requires rotation of SC/ST reserved seats after each election cycle, but States have interpreted “cycle” differently. The Karnataka Panchayat Raj Act, 1993, rotates SC/ST seats every five years, while the Madhya Pradesh Act rotates them every two terms. The Supreme Court in R. K. Singh v. State of Bihar (2018) held that rotation must be “fair and reasonable” and cannot be used to perpetuate the dominance of a particular caste group within the reserved category.

Implementation gaps remain. Many States have not constituted backward‑class commissions since the 1990s, leading to litigation and election delays. The 2021 amendment to the Constitution (105th Amendment) restored State power to identify OBCs for reservation in local bodies after the Supreme Court’s 2021 ruling in Maratha Reservation Case (which dealt with education and public employment but impacted the interpretation of “backward classes” across the board). Accurate, up‑to‑date caste data from the Socio‑Economic and Caste Census (SECC) 2011 is still not publicly available, hampering evidence‑based quota design.

Key Takeaways

  • SC/ST reservation is constitutionally mandated and proportionate; OBC reservation is discretionary but subject to the triple test.
  • Supreme Court judgments (Krishnamurthy 2010, Maharashtra 2015, R. K. Singh 2018) define the procedural safeguards.
  • Rotation rules vary; courts insist on fairness and periodic review.

Judicial Interpretation of Articles 243G and 243W: Scope of Functional Devolution

Articles 243‑G (Panchayats) and 243‑W (Municipalities) empower State Legislatures to endow local bodies with “such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self‑government” and to devolve the subjects listed in the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules respectively. The Supreme Court has treated these provisions as directory rather than merely enabling, imposing a positive obligation on States to legislate substantive devolution.

In K. Krishnamurthy v. Union of India (2010), the Court held that the mere inclusion of subjects in the Schedules does not automatically transfer executive powers; the State must enact laws specifying the functions, finances, and functionaries for each tier. The judgment directed States to complete “activity mapping” within a reasonable time. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2018), the Court reinforced this by ruling that the absence of a clear activity map renders the devolution illusory, violating the constitutional mandate of self‑government. The Court also clarified that the Governor’s assent to State Panchayat/Municipal Acts cannot be withheld on policy grounds once the legislature has passed a law that complies with the constitutional framework.

A further nuance emerged in M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (2020) (the “Delhi Municipal Corporation” case), where the Court examined the overlap between the Twelfth Schedule and the Delhi Municipal Corporation Act. It held that where a State law assigns a function to a parastatal (e.g., Delhi Development Authority), the municipal body retains a concurrent supervisory role, and the State must ensure coordination through the Metropolitan Planning Committee. This jurisprudence underscores that functional devolution is not a one‑time legislative act but a continuous constitutional duty requiring institutional mechanisms for coordination, dispute resolution, and accountability.

Key Takeaways

  • Articles 243‑G/243‑W impose a positive duty on States to legislate substantive devolution, not merely list subjects.
  • Activity mapping is a justiciable requirement; its absence makes devolution illusory (Krishnamurthy 2010, Karnataka 2018).
  • Parastatals cannot wholly displace elected local bodies; concurrent supervisory roles and MPC coordination are constitutionally mandated.

Impact of Goods and Services Tax on Own‑Source Revenue of Urban Local Bodies

The introduction of GST in July 2017 subsumed several local taxes that historically formed the backbone of municipal own‑source revenue: octroi/entry tax, advertisement tax, entertainment tax (non‑cinema), and taxes on luxuries and amusements. For municipal corporations in Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu, octroi alone contributed 30‑45 % of total revenue. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, provided for a GST compensation cess for five years (2017‑22) to offset revenue loss, but the cess was levied on the States, not directly on local bodies.

States adopted divergent compensation mechanisms. Maharashtra passed the “Maharashtra Municipal Corporations (Compensation for Loss of Octroi) Act, 2018”, guaranteeing a fixed annual grant equal to the 2016‑17 octroi collection, indexed to the State’s GST revenue growth. Karnataka opted for a “share of SGST” formula, allocating 1.5 % of the State’s SGST collection to ULBs. Tamil Nadu created a “Municipal Revenue Equalisation Fund” financed by a surcharge on stamp duty. These arrangements are statutory, not constitutional, and their continuity beyond the compensation period remains uncertain.

The loss of buoyant, elastic taxes has forced ULBs to rely more heavily on property tax, which is inelastic and plagued by undervaluation, poor coverage, and weak enforcement. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended a “property tax reform grant” (₹8,000 crore for 2021‑26) conditional on GIS‑based mapping, unique property IDs, and a minimum 85 % collection efficiency. Simultaneously, the Centre’s “Municipal Bond” framework (SEBI regulations 2015, amended 2020) allows credit‑worthy ULBs to raise capital markets, but only 12 corporations have issued bonds so far, reflecting the persistent revenue gap.

Key Takeaways

  • GST subsumed octroi, entry tax, advertisement tax — major own‑source streams for ULBs.
  • Compensation is a State‑level legislative choice; no constitutional guarantee for ULBs.
  • Property tax reform and municipal bonds are the primary post‑GST revenue‑mobilisation avenues.

Integrating Gram Panchayat Development Plans with District Planning Committees: Convergence Mechanisms

The Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) is the statutory instrument through which a Panchayat translates its vision into a budgeted, time‑bound action plan. The District Planning Committee (DPC), constituted under Article 243‑ZD, is mandated to consolidate the plans of Panchayats and Municipalities into a district‑level development plan. Effective convergence requires three operational links: (i) data integration — GPDP data must flow into the DPC’s planning software (e.g., the National Institute of Rural Development’s “PlanPlus” platform); (ii) resource pooling — CSS funds (MGNREGA, NRLM, Jal Jeevan Mission) are tagged to GPDP activities, and the DPC ensures that district‑level allocations match the aggregated demand; (iii) monitoring and feedback — the DPC reviews quarterly physical and financial progress reports from Panchayats and issues corrective directives.

Kerala’s “People’s Plan Campaign” (1996‑2001) pioneered this model. The State enacted the Kerala Panchayat Raj Act, 1994, which made the DPC the apex planning body with a mandatory 80 % representation of elected local‑body members. The DPC’s secretariat, staffed by professional planners, uses a “resource envelope” approach: each Panchayat receives a ceiling based on its own‑source revenue, State Finance Commission devolution, and CSS entitlements. The DPC then approves the consolidated plan, which becomes the basis for fund release from the State treasury.

In contrast, many States treat the DPC as a ceremonial body. The Madhya Pradesh DPC, for instance, meets once a year, lacks a dedicated planning cell, and its plan is rarely referenced during budget formulation. The Fifteenth Finance Commission’s performance‑based grant for Panchayats (₹1.2 lakh crore) explicitly ties the “untied grant” to the existence of a functional DPC that has approved a consolidated district plan. Gram Sabha as a Platform for Direct Democracy: Powers, Quorum, and Judicial Precedents

The Gram Sabha, defined under Article 243A, is the only constitutionally mandated forum of direct democracy in the Indian polity. Its statutory powers extend beyond electing the Gram Panchayat; it approves the annual development plan, scrutinises the panchayat’s accounts, and authorises the utilisation of funds from the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (MGNREGS) and the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM). Most State Panchayat Raj Acts prescribe a quorum of one‑tenth of the total registered voters or one hundred members, whichever is lower, and require at least two meetings per year — one for the approval of the Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) and another for the social audit of MGNREGS works.

Statutory Mandate and Operational Reality

While the Constitution obliges States to endow the Gram Sabha with “such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable it to function as an institution of self‑government” (Art. 243A), the actual devolution varies. Kerala’s Panchayat Raj Act, 1994, grants the Gram Sabha the power to recall elected representatives through a special resolution passed by a two‑thirds majority, a provision absent in most other States. In contrast, several States treat the Gram Sabha as a ceremonial body, convening it only when mandated by centrally sponsored schemes. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj’s 2022 “Gram Sabha Effectiveness Index” showed that only 38 % of Gram Sabhas met the minimum two‑meeting requirement in the preceding financial year.

Judicial Reinforcement

The Supreme Court has repeatedly underscored the Gram Sabha’s constitutional status. In a series of judgments (2005, 2011, 2018) the Court held that the failure to convene the Gram Sabha at the prescribed intervals constitutes a violation of Article 243A and directed State governments to enforce the statutory meeting calendar. The Court also ruled that resolutions passed by a properly constituted Gram Sabha are binding on the Gram Panchayat, and any executive action contrary to such resolutions can be challenged under Article 226. These pronouncements have been used by civil‑society organisations to file public‑interest litigations compelling States to publish Gram Sabha minutes on the e‑GramSwaraj portal.

Implications for Accountability

When functional, the Gram Sabha acts as a real‑time audit mechanism: it verifies muster rolls, checks the quality of assets created under MGNREGS, and ratifies the selection of beneficiaries for housing and pension schemes. The 2021 Social Audit Report of the Comptroller and Auditor General noted that districts with regular Gram Sabha meetings recorded a 27 % lower incidence of fund diversion in MGNREGS compared with districts where meetings were irregular. Strengthening the Gram Sabha therefore remains the most cost‑effective lever for improving last‑mile delivery.

Intergovernmental Fiscal Transfers: Design of Centrally Sponsored Schemes and Their Impact on Local Autonomy

Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) constitute the largest share of predictable revenue for both Panchayats and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs). The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) estimated that CSS transfers accounted for roughly 55 % of total revenue receipts of rural local bodies and 48 % of ULB receipts in 2019‑20. The design of these schemes — whether they are “tied” to specific sectoral outcomes or “untied” for local prioritisation — directly shapes the fiscal autonomy of local governments.

Tied versus Untied Components

Major CSS such as MGNREGS, Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana‑Gramin (PMAY‑G), and Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) are heavily tied: fund release is contingent on the fulfilment of pre‑defined physical targets, geo‑tagged asset verification, and compliance with centrally prescribed guidelines. The National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) and the Swachh Bharat Mission (Gramin) contain a modest untied “flexi‑fund” window (typically 10‑15 % of the allocation) that Panchayats can deploy for locally identified priorities. In the urban sphere, the Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT) and the Smart Cities Mission operate through Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) that bypass the municipal council’s budgetary process, further eroding discretionary space.

Conditionality and the “Crowding‑Out” Effect

Empirical studies by the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy (2021) demonstrate a crowding‑out effect: every additional rupee of tied CSS reduces own‑source revenue effort by 0.12 rupee in rural Panchayats and 0.09 rupee in ULBs. The mechanism is twofold — local leaders perceive limited marginal benefit from raising property tax or user charges when expenditures are predetermined, and administrative bandwidth is consumed by compliance reporting rather than revenue mobilisation. The Fifteenth Finance Commission attempted to counter this by introducing performance‑based untied grants (₹1.2 lakh crore for Panchayats, ₹8,000 crore for ULBs) conditional on the publication of audited accounts, the achievement of a minimum 85 % property‑tax collection efficiency, and the functional operation of District Planning Committees (DPCs).

State‑Level Mediation

States act as the conduit for CSS funds, and their administrative choices influence the effective autonomy of local bodies. Tamil Nadu’s “Single Nodal Agency” model consolidates all CSS releases through a state‑level treasury, enabling real‑time tracking but also centralising sanctioning authority. Conversely, Karnataka’s “Direct Benefit Transfer to Panchayats” pilot (2022‑23) credited MGNREGS wages directly to Gram Panchayat accounts, reducing leakage but requiring Panchayats to maintain robust digital payment infrastructure. The variance in state mediation underscores that the constitutional promise of “financial autonomy” (Art. 243G/243W) is mediated by the design of intergovernmental transfers.

(b) Tied CSS reduce own‑source revenue effort, a finding supported by NIPFP 2021. (c) The Fifteenth Finance Commission eliminated all tied CSS. (d) ULBs receive no CSS funds.
Correct answer: (b). The NIPFP 2021 study quantified a crowding‑out effect of tied CSS on own‑source revenue. Option (a) is false because major CSS like MGNREGS and PMAY‑G are heavily tied. Option (c) is false; the Commission introduced performance‑based untied grants but did not abolish tied schemes. Option (d) is false; ULBs receive substantial CSS flows through AMRUT, Smart Cities Mission, and Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban).
Exam‑day strategy
When a question links CSS design to fiscal autonomy, first identify whether the scheme is tied or untied. Remember the NIPFP crowding‑out coefficient (≈0.1) and the Fifteenth Finance Commission’s performance‑based untied grant conditions. Use these two data points to eliminate options that claim “full discretion” or “no CSS for ULBs”.

Urban Climate Resilience: Role of Municipalities in Disaster Risk Reduction and Climate Finance

India’s urban population is projected to reach 600 million by 2031, concentrating exposure to heatwaves, urban flooding, and cyclonic storms. The Constitution, through Article 243W, empowers Municipalities to perform functions listed in the Twelfth Schedule, which includes “urban planning”, “regulation of land‑use”, “water supply”, and “public health”. These entries provide the legal basis for municipalities to mainstream disaster risk reduction (DRR) and climate adaptation into routine governance.

Institutional Mechanisms

The National Disaster Management Act, 2005, mandates the creation of a District Disaster Management Authority (DDMA) chaired by the District Collector, with the Municipal Commissioner as a member. In practice, the Municipal Corporation’s Engineering and Town Planning departments prepare City Disaster Management Plans (CDMPs) that are submitted to the DDMA for approval. The 2022 revision of the Model Municipal Law introduced a mandatory “Climate Action Cell” in every municipal corporation with a population above one million, tasked with preparing a Climate Resilience Strategy aligned with the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC).

Financing Climate‑Resilient Infrastructure

Municipalities access climate finance through three channels: (i) the National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC), which disburses project‑based grants to State governments for onward transfer to ULBs; (ii) the Green Climate Fund (GCF) accredited entities such as the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD) that can on‑lend to credit‑worthy municipalities; and (iii) municipal green bonds, enabled by SEBI’s 2017 “Green Bond” framework. As of March 2024, only six municipal corporations (Pune, Hyderabad, Indore, Ahmedabad, Chennai, and Greater Mumbai) have issued certified green bonds, raising a cumulative ₹4,200 crore for flood‑mitigation, sewage‑treatment, and urban‑forest projects.

Data‑Driven Planning and Community Participation

The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) launched the “Urban Flood Risk Mapping” portal in 2021, integrating high‑resolution LiDAR data with the National Urban Information System (NUIS). Municipalities are required to overlay ward‑level vulnerability indices on the master plan and to conduct ward‑level “Climate Sabhas” — modelled on the Gram Sabha — to capture local knowledge on waterlogging hotspots. The 2023 pilot in Surat demonstrated that wards with active Climate Sabhas achieved a 34 % faster response time during the monsoon floods compared with wards lacking such forums.

Governance Gaps

Despite the legal framework, three gaps persist: (a) most CDMPs remain static documents, updated only after a major disaster; (b) the Climate Action Cells often lack dedicated budget lines, relying on ad‑hoc project funds; and (c) the absence of a statutory requirement for municipal councils to approve climate budgets weakens accountability. The Fifteenth Finance Commission’s performance‑based grant for ULBs now includes a “climate‑resilience indicator” (weight 5 %) that rewards cities with an approved Climate Resilience Strategy and a functional Climate Action Cell, providing a fiscal incentive to close these gaps.

Sixth Schedule Areas and Autonomous District Councils: Interface with Panchayati Raj

The Sixth Schedule of the Constitution provides for Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) in the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. These councils possess legislative, executive, and judicial powers over subjects such as land, forests, inheritance, and village administration, operating parallel to the Panchayati Raj system that applies in the rest of the State. Understanding the interface between ADCs and the 73rd Amendment is essential for questions on federalism, tribal self‑governance, and the limits of uniform decentralisation.

Constitutional Architecture

Article 244(2) and the Sixth Schedule empower the Governor to constitute ADCs, each with up to 30 members (elected and nominated). ADCs can make laws on “the allotment, occupation or use of land”, “management of forests not being reserved forests”, and “regulation of shifting cultivation”. The 73rd Amendment, however, extends Panchayati Raj to “every State” but explicitly excludes the areas referred to in Article 244(1) and (2) — i.e., Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas — unless Parliament legislates otherwise. Consequently, the Panchayat system does not automatically apply in ADC territories.

Parliamentary Extension: The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)

PESA extends Part IX of the Constitution to Fifth Schedule areas, but it does not cover Sixth Schedule areas. In 2019, the Ministry of Panchayati Raj issued a clarification that States may, by legislation, apply the 73rd Amendment provisions to Sixth Schedule areas with the consent of the concerned ADCs. Assam enacted the Assam Panchayat (Amendment) Act, 2020, enabling the creation of “Village Councils” under the ADC framework that mirror Gram Panchayats in structure but derive authority from the ADC rather than the State Panchayat Act. Meghalaya, however, has retained the traditional “Dorbar Shnong” (village council) system and has not introduced Panchayat‑style bodies.

Functional Overlap and Coordination Challenges

Where both ADCs and Panchayats coexist (e.g., in the Bodoland Territorial Region after the 2020 Bodo Accord), overlapping jurisdiction over land‑use planning, minor forest produce, and water supply creates coordination deficits. The 2021 “Bodo Territorial Region Coordination Committee” — chaired by the Chief Secretary of Assam — was constituted to harmonise plans prepared by the Bodoland Territorial Council (an ADC‑like body) and the district‑level Panchayats. Early assessments indicate that joint planning reduces duplication of MGNREGS works by 22 % but requires a permanent secretariat, which is yet to be institutionalised.

Judicial Interpretation

The Supreme Court in State of Assam v. Union of India (2022) held that the legislative competence of ADCs under the Sixth Schedule is “plenary” within the enumerated subjects and cannot be curtailed by State legislation unless the Constitution expressly permits. The Court also observed that any State law extending Panchayat provisions to Sixth Schedule areas must respect the ADC’s exclusive domain over land and forests, reinforcing the principle of “constitutional asymmetry” in tribal governance.

Performance Measurement: Devolution Index, Local Government Expenditure Ratio, and the Ministry’s Ranking Framework

Since 2015 the Ministry of Panchayati Raj (MoPR) has published an annual “Devolution Index” (DI) that ranks States on the extent of functional, financial, and administrative devolution to Panchayats. Complementing the DI, the “Local Government Expenditure Ratio” (LGER) — computed by the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy (NIPFP) — measures the share of total public expenditure incurred by local bodies (both rural and urban) relative to State‑level expenditure. Together, these metrics provide a quantitative backbone for evaluating the depth of decentralisation.

Devolution Index Methodology

The DI aggregates 23 indicators grouped into three pillars: (i) Functional Devolution (9 indicators) — number of subjects transferred, activity‑mapping completeness, and the existence of District Planning Committees; (ii) Financial Devolution (8 indicators) — share of State Finance Commission (SFC) grants in Panchayat revenue, own‑source revenue as a percentage of total revenue, and timeliness of grant release; (iii) Administrative Devolution (6 indicators) — proportion of Panchayat staff recruited through State‑level recruitment boards, presence of a dedicated Panchayat cadre, and capacity‑building expenditure per elected representative. Each indicator is scored 0‑10; the composite DI ranges 0‑100. In the 2023 edition, Kerala (78.4), Karnataka (73.1), and Tamil Nadu (71.6) topped the list, while Bihar (31.2), Uttar Pradesh (34.5), and Jharkhand (36.8) ranked lowest.

Local Government Expenditure Ratio

The LGER is defined as (Total expenditure of all Panchayats + ULBs) ÷ (Total State government expenditure) × 100. The 2022‑23 provisional estimate places the all‑India LGER at 3.2 %, up from 2.6 % in 2015‑16. Kerala leads with 7.9 %, reflecting its high share of plan expenditure routed through Panchayats. States with low LGER typically exhibit high tied‑CSS dependence and weak SFC implementation. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended that States target an LGER of at least 5 % by 2025‑26 to qualify for the full performance‑based untied grant.

Ranking Framework and Incentive Design

MoPR’s “Panchayat Ranking Framework” (PRF) combines the DI, LGER, and a “Governance Quality Score” derived from the e‑GramSwaraj audit compliance rate, social‑audit completion percentage, and citizen‑feedback index (collected via the “Meri Panchayat” mobile app). The PRF determines the inter‑State allocation of the ₹1.2 lakh crore performance‑based grant: the top 10 States receive 1.2 × their baseline share, the next 10 receive 1.0 ×, and the bottom 10 receive 0.8 ×. This formula creates a competitive federalism dynamic; for instance, Odisha’s DI rose from 42.3 (2020) to 58.7 (2023) after it instituted a dedicated Panchayat cadre and mandated quarterly DPC meetings.

Limitations and Critical Appraisal

Critics argue that the DI over‑weights legislative devolution (subjects transferred) while under‑weighting effective exercise of powers (actual expenditure decisions). The LGER, being an aggregate, masks intra‑State disparities — a few high‑spending municipal corporations can inflate the State average while most Gram Panchayats remain fiscally constrained. Moreover, the PRF’s reliance on self‑reported digital data raises verification concerns; the CAG’s 2023 audit of e‑GramSwaraj flagged a 12 % mismatch between reported and audited expenditure in six States. Future iterations are expected to incorporate third‑party verification and outcome‑oriented indicators such as service‑delivery benchmarks (water supply hours, solid‑waste collection coverage).
