1. Constitutional Framework of Rights, Principles and Duties

1.1 The Scheme of Part III, Part IV and Part IVA

The Constitution of India distributes the normative architecture of governance across three distinct parts that together shape the relationship between the state and the citizen. Part III, comprising Articles 12 to 35, enumerates the Fundamental Rights that are justiciable and enforceable by the courts. Part IV, covering Articles 36 to 51, sets out the Directive Principles of State Policy, which are non‑justiciable guidelines for the legislature and executive. Part IVA, introduced by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 and later expanded by the 86th Amendment in 2002, contains the Fundamental Duties under Article 51A. This tripartite structure reflects the Constituent Assembly’s intention to balance individual liberty with collective welfare and to remind citizens that rights carry correlative responsibilities.

1.2 Historical Evolution and Constituent Assembly Debates

The debates in the Constituent Assembly reveal a conscious decision to place enforceable rights in a separate chapter while relegating socio‑economic aspirations to a directive chapter. Members such as B. R. Ambedkar argued that justiciable rights would protect minorities and dissent, whereas directives would guide the state toward a welfare orientation without exposing the judiciary to policy‑making. The inclusion of Fundamental Duties was a later development, motivated by the desire to cultivate a sense of civic discipline after the Emergency experience. The 42nd Amendment inserted ten duties, and the 86th Amendment added the duty to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years, reflecting an evolving constitutional consciousness.

1.3 Constitutional Philosophy: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity

The Preamble’s promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity finds operational expression through the three parts. Fundamental Rights secure liberty and equality against arbitrary state action. Directive Principles translate the ideal of social and economic justice into actionable policy goals. Fundamental Duties embody fraternity by obligating citizens to respect the Constitution, the national symbols and the rights of others. Together they constitute a constitutional ecosystem where each component informs the interpretation of the others, a principle the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed in cases such as Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) and Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980).

2. Fundamental Rights: Scope and Judicial Interpretation

2.1 Definition of State and the Reach of Article 12

Article 12 defines the term “State” for the purpose of Part III, extending the ambit of Fundamental Rights beyond the central and state governments to include local authorities, statutory corporations and any other authority under the control of the government. The Supreme Court in R. D. Shetty v. Airport Authority of India (1979) laid down the “instrumentality test” to determine whether a body qualifies as State, emphasizing functional, financial and administrative control. This expansive definition ensures that entities performing public functions cannot evade constitutional accountability by claiming private status.

2.2 Classification of Rights: Civil, Political and Socio‑Economic

Fundamental Rights are traditionally grouped into six categories: Right to Equality (Articles 14‑18), Right to Freedom (Articles 19‑22), Right against Exploitation (Articles 23‑24), Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25‑28), Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29‑30) and Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32). While the first five clusters protect civil and political liberties, the judiciary has progressively read socio‑economic dimensions into them. For instance, Article 21’s guarantee of life and personal liberty has been interpreted to include the right to health, education, shelter and a clean environment, as seen in Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981) and Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993).

2.3 Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine

The power of judicial review under Article 32 and Article 226 makes Fundamental Rights the cornerstone of constitutional supremacy. The Basic Structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati, holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a manner that destroys its essential features, including the fundamental rights framework. Subsequent decisions such as Minerva Mills reinforced that a harmonious construction between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure, preventing any amendment that would give primacy to directives over rights.

2.4 Landmark Expansions: From Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi

The evolution from A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), where “procedure established by law” was read narrowly, to Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court required the procedure to be “fair, just and reasonable,” illustrates the dynamic interpretive trajectory. This shift incorporated principles of natural justice into Article 21, making any law depriving life or liberty subject to substantive due process scrutiny. The Court also linked Articles 14, 19 and 21, establishing the “golden triangle” that any legislation must satisfy simultaneously.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are not mutually exclusive but form an integrated scheme. 3. Directive Principles of State Policy: Nature and Enforceability

3.1 Classification: Socialistic, Gandhian and Liberal‑Intellectual

The Directive Principles are grouped into three broad categories reflecting the ideological influences on the Constitution. Socialistic principles (Articles 38, 39, 41, 42, 43) aim at securing a just social order, adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, and humane conditions of work. Gandhian principles (Articles 40, 43B, 46, 47, 48) emphasize village panchayats, cottage industries, promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections, prohibition of intoxicating drinks and cow protection. Liberal‑intellectual principles (Articles 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51) advocate a uniform civil code, free and compulsory education, protection of environment, separation of judiciary from executive and promotion of international peace. This taxonomy helps aspirants identify the policy thrust behind each Article.

3.2 Judicial Attitude: From Non‑Justiciability to Interpretive Tool

Although Article 37 declares Directive Principles non‑justiciable, the judiciary has used them as interpretive aids to expand the meaning of Fundamental Rights. In the early phase, the Court treated directives as mere moral exhortations. However, from the 1970s onward, judgments such as Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills elevated directives to a constitutional mandate that informs legislative policy. The Court in Unni Krishnan read Article 45 (free and compulsory education) together with Article 21 to declare a fundamental right to education up to the age of fourteen, later codified by the 86th Amendment as Article 21A. This jurisprudential shift demonstrates that non‑justiciability does not render directives irrelevant; they shape the content of justiciable rights.

3.3 Legislative Implementation and Policy Impact

Many Directive Principles have been translated into statutes, illustrating their practical influence. The Minimum Wages Act, 1948 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 give effect to Articles 39(d) and 39(e). The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments operationalize Article 40 by constitutionally mandating panchayati raj institutions and urban local bodies. The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 reflects Article 41’s right to work. Environmental legislation such as the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 draw from Article 48A. These examples show that while directives lack judicial enforceability, they serve as a constitutional compass for legislative action.

4. Fundamental Duties: Evolution and Constitutional Significance

4.1 Origin, Text and Subsequent Additions

Fundamental Duties were absent from the original Constitution. The 42nd Amendment inserted Article 51A with ten duties, ranging from respecting the Constitution and national symbols to promoting harmony and protecting public property. The 86th Amendment added clause (k), obligating parents or guardians to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. The duties are addressed to “every citizen of India,” making them universally applicable, unlike Fundamental Rights which are available to persons, including non‑citizens in certain cases.

4.2 Judicial Enforcement and Symbolic Value

The Supreme Court has held that Fundamental Duties are not legally enforceable by writs, but they acquire legal significance when a statute incorporates them. In Chandra Bhavan Boarding and Lodging v. State of Mysore (1970), the Court observed that duties serve as a constant reminder of civic obligations. In the more recent case of Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India (2021), the Court suggested that duties can be used to interpret the scope of rights, for instance, the duty to protect the environment under Article 51A(g) informs the content of Article 21. Thus, while duties lack direct justiciability, they function as interpretive guides and as a basis for legislative sanctions.

4.3 Comparative Perspective: Duties in Other Constitutions

Several constitutions, such as those of Japan, Spain and the former Soviet Union, enumerate duties alongside rights. The Indian model is distinctive because duties were added decades after the Constitution’s commencement, reflecting a corrective response to perceived civic apathy. Unlike the Japanese Constitution, where duties are justiciable, Indian duties remain largely moral. Nevertheless, the inclusion of duties in Part IVA signals a constitutional aspiration to balance the rights‑centric discourse with a responsibility‑centric ethos.

5. Interplay, Tension and Harmonious Construction

5.1 The Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Constitutional Interpretation

The Supreme Court has articulated the doctrine of harmonious construction to resolve apparent conflicts between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. The core premise is that the Constitution is an organic whole; no part can be read in isolation to defeat another. In Kesavananda Bharati, the Court held that the amending power under Article 368 cannot be used to destroy the basic structure, which includes the balance between Parts III and IV. This principle obliges the legislature to frame laws that respect rights while pursuing directive goals, and it obliges the judiciary to interpret rights in a manner that advances directive objectives without nullifying them.

5.2 Judicial Balancing in Socio‑Economic Legislation

Cases involving land reform, labour welfare and environmental protection illustrate the balancing act. In State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977), the Court upheld a land ceiling law under Article 31C (as originally enacted) because it implemented Article 39(b) and (c), while ensuring that the law did not violate Article 14. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), the Court recognized the right to livelihood as part of Article 21, linking it to the directive under Article 41. The Court ordered procedural safeguards before evicting pavement dwellers, demonstrating that directive goals cannot be pursued at the expense of fundamental rights without due process.

5.3 Contemporary Challenges and the Way Forward

Emerging issues such as data privacy, climate justice and affirmative action in private education test the harmonious construction framework. The recognition of the right to privacy as a fundamental right in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) shows the Court’s willingness to expand Part III in response to new realities, while directives like Article 48A (environment) and Article 51A(g) (duty to protect environment) provide a constitutional basis for climate litigation. The ongoing debate over the National Education Policy and the implementation of the Right to Education Act reflects the dynamic tension between the aspirational directives and the enforceable rights. A nuanced understanding of this interplay is essential for any serious student of Indian constitutional law.

The constitutional scheme thus envisions a continuous dialogue between rights, principles and duties, each informing the other to realise the vision of a just, equitable and fraternal society.

Article 13 and the Doctrines of Eclipse, Severability and Waiver

Article 13 is the gateway that subjects all laws — pre‑constitutional and post‑constitutional — to the supremacy of Fundamental Rights. Clause (1) declares that any law in force before the commencement of the Constitution which is inconsistent with Part III shall, to the extent of such inconsistency, be void. Clause (2) prohibits the State from making any law that takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III. The Supreme Court has developed three doctrinal tools to give practical effect to these clauses.

The doctrine of eclipse applies only to pre‑constitutional statutes. In Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay (1951) the Court held that a law which conflicts with a Fundamental Right does not die; it becomes dormant — eclipsed — until the constitutional impediment is removed, for instance by an amendment that validates the law. The eclipsed law revives automatically when the inconsistency ceases, without the need for fresh legislation. This doctrine preserves legislative continuity while respecting constitutional supremacy.

The doctrine of severability governs post‑constitutional statutes. When a statute contains both constitutional and unconstitutional provisions, the Court excises the offending part if the remainder is capable of standing alone and reflects the legislative intent. The classic test was laid down in R.M.D.C. v. Union of India (1957): the valid portion must be independent, not so intertwined with the invalid part that the legislature would not have enacted it separately. If severance would alter the statute’s essential character, the whole Act falls.

The doctrine of waiver addresses whether a person can voluntarily relinquish a Fundamental Right. In Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax (1959) the Court ruled that Fundamental Rights are not merely private entitlements but embody public policy; therefore they cannot be waived by an individual. This principle reinforces the non‑derogable nature of Part III and prevents the State from extracting contractual waivers that would undermine constitutional guarantees.

Together, these doctrines ensure that Article 13 operates as a living filter: pre‑constitutional laws are kept in suspended animation, post‑constitutional laws are pruned rather than obliterated, and the rights themselves remain inalienable. Mastery of the case law — especially the distinction between eclipse (pre‑constitutional) and severability (post‑constitutional) — is essential for answering application‑type questions in the mains.

The Expanding Scope of Article 21: Privacy, Dignity and Socio‑Economic Rights

Article 21 — “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law” — has undergone the most dramatic judicial expansion in Indian constitutional history. The early A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) reading confined “procedure established by law” to any enacted procedure, however arbitrary. The turning point came in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where a seven‑judge bench held that the procedure must be “fair, just and reasonable”, importing the due‑process standards of Article 21 into the Indian context.

Subsequent decisions have read a constellation of rights into the word “life”. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) the Court recognised the right to livelihood as an integral facet of life, linking it to Directive Principle Article 41. Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993) elevated the right to education to a Fundamental Right under Article 21, later codified by the 86th Amendment (2002) as Article 21A. The right to health and medical care was affirmed in Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996), and the right to a clean environment in Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991).

The most consequential expansion is the recognition of privacy as a fundamental right in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017). A nine‑judge bench overruled the earlier M.P. Sharma and Kharak Singh judgments, holding that privacy is intrinsic to life, liberty and dignity under Articles 14, 19 and 21. This verdict has become the constitutional foundation for data‑protection jurisprudence, the striking down of Section 377 in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), and the emerging right to be forgotten.

Dignity, articulated in Maneka Gandhi and reinforced in Puttaswamy, now serves as the interpretive lens for socio‑economic claims. The Court has used dignity to justify positive obligations on the State — such as the provision of shelter (Chameli Singh v. State of U.P. 1996) and the protection of marginalized communities from manual scavenging (Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India 2014). For the aspirant, the trajectory from a narrow procedural guarantee to a substantive reservoir of rights illustrates the dynamic interplay between judicial interpretation and constitutional amendment.

Application Question
A state enacts a law that authorises the compulsory acquisition of agricultural land for a private industrial park, offering compensation determined by a formula that is significantly below market value. The affected farmers challenge the law under Article 21 and Article 300A. Identify the constitutional provisions and doctrines the Court would invoke, and explain the likely outcome.
The Court would examine the law under Article 300A (property as a constitutional right) and Article 21 (right to livelihood). The doctrine of eminent domain requires a public purpose; a private industrial park may not satisfy this unless the State demonstrates a broader public benefit. The compensation formula would be tested against the “fair, just and reasonable” standard from Maneka Gandhi and the “just compensation” principle in K.T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2011). If the purpose is not public or compensation is illusory, the law would be struck down as violative of Articles 21 and 300A. The Court may also invoke the doctrine of severability to preserve any severable provisions.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “the constitutional basis” of a socio‑economic right, always trace the lineage: Directive Principle → Article 21 expansion → specific judgment. Cite the landmark case (e.g., Olga Tellis for livelihood, Puttaswamy for privacy) and the doctrinal hook (fair procedure, dignity). This three‑step chain — Directive, Article 21, Case — fetches full marks.

Article 31C and the Constitutional Amendments: Shifting Balance Between Rights and Directives

Article 31C was introduced by the 25th Amendment (1971) to insulate laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) and (c) — the ownership and control of material resources for the common good — from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. The original text read: “Notwithstanding anything contained in Article 13, no law giving effect to the policy of the State towards securing the principles specified in clause (b) or (c) of Article 39 shall be deemed void on the ground that it is inconsistent with any of the rights conferred by Article 14, 19 or 31.”

The 42nd Amendment (1976) dramatically widened this shield. It replaced the reference to Article 39(b) and (c) with “any of the Directive Principles of State Policy”, thereby extending immunity to all directives. This move was part of a broader attempt to subordinate Fundamental Rights to the Directive Principles, reflecting the political philosophy of the Emergency period.

In Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) a five‑judge bench struck down the extended portion of Article 31C. The Court held that the amendment destroyed the basic structure by removing judicial review over a vast swathe of legislation, upsetting the harmonious balance between Parts III and IV. The judgment restored the pre‑1976 position: only laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) enjoy the protective umbrella of Article 31C.

The 44th Amendment (1978) formally repealed the 42nd Amendment’s expansion, leaving the original Article 31C intact. Consequently, today a law that seeks to implement any directive other than 39(b) and (c) must still withstand scrutiny under Articles 14, 19 and 21. The Court’s insistence on preserving the “core” of Fundamental Rights while allowing limited legislative leeway for distributive justice illustrates the constitutional commitment to a calibrated balance rather than a hierarchical supremacy.

For answer writing, remember the chronological sequence: 25th Amendment (original 31C) → 42nd Amendment (expanded 31C) → Minerva Mills (partial strike‑down) → 44th Amendment (repeal of expansion). Questions often test whether a given socio‑economic legislation (e.g., a land‑ceiling law, a nationalisation statute) can claim Article 31C protection; the answer hinges on whether the law implements Article 39(b) or (c) specifically.

Fundamental Duties in Judicial Reasoning: From Moral Exhortation to Interpretive Tool

Although Part IVA duties are non‑justiciable, the Supreme Court has progressively woven them into the fabric of constitutional adjudication. The pivotal shift occurred when the Court began treating duties as interpretive aids for Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1996) the Court invoked Article 51A(g) — the duty to protect and improve the natural environment — to expand the scope of Article 21, holding that the right to life encompasses a right to a wholesome environment.

Similarly, in State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977) the Court, while upholding a land‑ceiling law, referred to the duty to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood under Article 51A(e) to reinforce the legislative objective of equitable distribution. The 2021 decision in Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India explicitly stated that duties can be used to “interpret the content and scope of fundamental rights”, citing the environmental duty as a guide for reading Article 21.

The duty to provide opportunities for education under Article 51A(k) (inserted by the 86th Amendment, 2002) has been invoked in Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012) to justify the 25% quota for economically weaker sections in private unaided schools under the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009. The Court reasoned that the duty casts a constitutional obligation on the State and society, thereby legitimising affirmative action in education.

More recently, the duty to develop scientific temper and humanism under Article 51A(h) has been referenced in judgments concerning the regulation of pseudoscientific practices and the promotion of evidence‑based policy. While no writ lies directly for breach of a duty, the cumulative jurisprudence shows that duties function as a constitutional conscience — they shape the margin of appreciation available to the legislature and the interpretive latitude of the judiciary.

For the mains, a high‑scoring answer will distinguish three layers: (i) the textual non‑justiciability, (ii) the legislative incorporation route (e.g., statutes that penalise violation of duties), and (iii) the judicial interpretive route where duties inform the meaning of rights and directives. Cite at least two cases — M.C. Mehta (environment) and Rangnath Mishra (interpretive use) — to demonstrate the evolution.

Emerging Frontiers: Data Protection, Climate Justice and the Right to Internet Access

The constitutional canvas of Parts III, IV and IVA is being redrawn by technological and ecological challenges that the framers could not have anticipated. The right to privacy declared in Puttaswamy (2017) has become the doctrinal springboard for data‑protection jurisprudence. The Court recognised informational privacy as a facet of Article 21, mandating that any state intrusion into personal data must satisfy the triple test of legality, necessity and proportionality. This reasoning underpins the draft Personal Data Protection Bill and the subsequent Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, which seek to operationalise the constitutional mandate through a regulatory authority, consent framework, and cross‑border transfer restrictions.

Climate justice litigation leverages Article 48A (State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment) and Article 51A(g) (duty of citizens to protect the environment). In Ridhima Pandey v. Union of India (2021) the National Green Tribunal, drawing on Supreme Court precedents, directed the government to revise its Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement, treating the directives as enforceable benchmarks for policy review. The Supreme Court’s 2024 order in M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India recognised a “right against adverse effects of climate change” as part of Article 21, linking the duty to protect the environment with the fundamental right to life.

The right to internet access emerged from the Kashmir lockdown cases. In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) the Court held that freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) and the right to carry on trade under Article 19(1)(g) extend to the medium of the internet. While the Court stopped short of declaring internet access a standalone Fundamental Right, it mandated that any restriction must comply with the proportionality test under Article 19(2) and (6). Subsequent orders in Foundation for Media Professionals v. Union Territory of Jammu & Kashmir (2021) reinforced that indefinite suspension of 4G services violates the procedural safeguards of Article 21.

These frontiers illustrate a common pattern: the Court reads new realities into the open‑textured language of Article 21, uses Directive Principles as policy anchors, and invokes Fundamental Duties as moral compasses. For aspirants, the ability to map a contemporary issue — data localisation, carbon budgeting, digital shutdown — onto this triad of constitutional provisions is the hallmark of a sophisticated answer.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse (pre‑constitutional laws) with severability (post‑constitutional laws) and applying the wrong test in a problem question.
  • Assuming Article 31C still protects all Directive Principles after the 42nd Amendment; forgetting the Minerva Mills limitation to Article 39(b) and (c) only.
  • Treating Fundamental Duties as directly enforceable by writs, instead of recognising their role as interpretive aids and legislative guides.
  • Overlooking the triple‑test (legality, necessity, proportionality) when analysing privacy or internet‑restriction cases post‑Puttaswamy.
  • Neglecting to cite the specific Directive Principle (e.g., Article 48A, 51A(g)) that anchors a climate‑justice argument, leading to a generic “environment” answer.
  • Failing to distinguish between the original Article 31C (25th Amendment) and the expanded version (42nd Amendment) when tracing the constitutional history of rights‑directive balance.

Article 12: The Instrumentality Test and the Expanding Reach of ‘State’

The definition of “State” in Article 12 has been widened far beyond the literal executive and legislative organs through the instrumentality test first articulated in R.D. Shetty v. Airport Authority of India (1979). The Court held that a body is “State” if it is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by government, or if it performs functions of a public nature closely related to governmental functions. Subsequent decisions such as Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981) and Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002) refined the test into a six‑factor checklist: (i) share capital held by government, (ii) extent of state control over management, (iii) monopoly status conferred by statute, (iv) performance of public functions, (v) departmental integration, and (vi) whether the body is created by statute. The Supreme Court in Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005) applied the test to a private broadcasting company holding a licence, concluding that mere regulation does not make a private entity “State”. The trajectory shows a calibrated expansion: bodies performing sovereign functions (e.g., electricity boards, universities) are included, while purely commercial enterprises with limited statutory oversight remain outside.

In Bharat Petroleum Corp. Ltd. v. N. R. V. Ramana (2003) the Court emphasised that the test is not a rigid formula but a flexible inquiry into the “real character” of the entity. The 2014 judgment in National Stock Exchange v. Union of India reiterated that a statutory regulator exercising adjudicatory powers may be “State” for the purpose of Article 12, but only when its decisions affect fundamental rights. This nuanced approach prevents the indiscriminate extension of fundamental‑rights obligations to every regulated private actor, preserving the balance between private autonomy and constitutional accountability.

The practical implication for aspirants is to recognise that the instrumentality test is a factual matrix, not a bright‑line rule. Examination questions often present a hybrid entity — a public‑private partnership, a deemed university, or a statutory tribunal — and ask whether it qualifies as “State”. The correct answer requires mapping each of the six factors to the facts, noting that no single factor is decisive, and citing the latest precedent (e.g., K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) where the Court applied the test to the Unique Identification Authority of India). Mastery of this matrix distinguishes a high‑scoring answer from a generic one.

Article 13(2) and the Doctrine of Severability: Judicial Technique and Limits

Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making any law that takes away or abridges fundamental rights. When a statute contains both constitutional and unconstitutional provisions, the doctrine of severability allows the Court to excise the offending part while preserving the remainder, provided the valid portion is independent and the legislature would have enacted it alone. The classic formulation appears in R.M.D.C. v. Union of India (1957), where the Court laid down three conditions: (i) the unconstitutional provision must be separable without altering the statute’s structure, (ii) the remaining provisions must be capable of standing alone, and (iii) the legislative intent must not be defeated by the excision. In State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) the Court severed the offending prohibition on possession of liquor while retaining the licensing regime.

However, the doctrine has limits. In Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay (1951) the Court refused severance because the unconstitutional provision was the “heart” of the Act. The 1973 Kesavananda Bharati judgment reinforced that severability cannot be used to salvage a law that violates the basic structure. More recently, Indian Hotel & Restaurants Association v. State of Maharashtra (2013) demonstrated that where a statute creates a single integrated scheme — such as a complete ban on dance bars — the Court cannot sever the ban without rewriting the legislative policy, and therefore struck down the entire provision. The Supreme Court in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) applied severability to read down Section 377 IPC, retaining the provision only insofar as it criminalised non‑consensual acts, illustrating the doctrine’s evolving role in rights‑expansive jurisprudence.

For examination purposes, the key is to distinguish between “severable” and “non‑severable” statutes. A typical trap presents a law with a preamble declaring an indivisible objective; the correct response cites R.M.D.C. and explains that the preamble may indicate legislative intent against severance. Another twist asks whether severability applies to constitutional amendments; the answer is negative — amendments are tested as a whole under the basic‑structure doctrine (see Minerva Mills v. Union of India 1980). Practising the three‑condition test on hypothetical statutes sharpens analytical speed.

Decide the appeal with reference to the doctrine of severability.
The appeal fails. Under R.M.D.C. v. Union of India, severability requires (i) the unconstitutional provision to be separable without destroying the statute’s structure, (ii) the remaining provisions to be capable of independent operation, and (iii) legislative intent not to be defeated. Sections 5 and 6 regulate packaging and licensing — they can function without a total ban. The Act’s preamble mentions “comprehensive tobacco control”, but the licensing regime and warning requirements are distinct regulatory tools; the legislature would have enacted them even without an absolute prohibition. Therefore the High Court correctly severed Section 4 and preserved Sections 5 and 6.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a severability question appears, first list the three R.M.D.C. conditions. Then map each condition to the facts in the problem. Cite the leading case (R.M.D.C.) and, if relevant, the limiting case (Keshavan Madhava Menon). Avoid concluding “the whole Act falls” unless the unconstitutional part is the core of the legislative scheme. This structured approach fetches full marks for analytical reasoning.

Article 22: Preventive Detention – Safeguards, Judicial Review and the 44th Amendment

Article 22(1)–(2) guarantees procedural safeguards for arrested persons, but clauses (3)–(7) carve out a preventive‑detention regime that permits detention without trial for up to three months, extendable by an Advisory Board. The original Constitution allowed Parliament to prescribe the maximum period; the 44th Amendment (1978) reduced the initial detention period from three to two months and mandated that the Advisory Board consist of a sitting High Court judge. In A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) the Court upheld the preventive‑detention law, reading Article 21 narrowly. The landmark Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) overruled Gopalan, holding that any law depriving personal liberty must satisfy the test of “procedure established by law” that is fair, just and reasonable, thereby importing substantive due process into Article 22.

Judicial review of preventive detention focuses on the adequacy of the grounds communicated to the detenu, the composition and functioning of the Advisory Board, and the periodicity of review. In Kharak Singh v. State of U.P. (1963) the Court struck down a provision allowing detention beyond the Advisory Board’s recommendation. The 1980 decision in Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi extended the right to legal representation before the Board. The Supreme Court in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) reiterated that preventive detention must be an exception, not a norm, and that the state bears the burden of justifying each detention order. The 2020 amendment to the National Security Act (NSA) introduced a mandatory review every six months, aligning statutory practice with the constitutional mandate.

Examination questions often test the distinction between punitive and preventive detention, the role of the Advisory Board, and the impact of the 44th Amendment. A common trap is to assume that the Advisory Board’s recommendation is binding; the Court in State of Maharashtra v. Prabhakar Pandurang (1966) held it is advisory only. Another twist asks whether Article 22(5) (right to be informed of grounds) applies to detention under the NSA; the answer is affirmative, as the NSA incorporates the constitutional safeguards. Mastery of the chronological evolution — Gopalan → Maneka → 44th Amendment → Puttaswamy — enables precise, high‑scoring answers.

Article 48A and 51A(g): Environmental Protection as a Directive Principle and Fundamental Duty – Judicial Convergence

Article 48A, inserted by the 42nd Amendment (1976), obliges the State to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard forests and wildlife. Article 51A(g), added by the same amendment, imposes a fundamental duty on every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment. Although Directive Principles are non‑justiciable, the Supreme Court has read Article 48A into Article 21, converting the duty into an enforceable right. In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) (the Oleum Gas Leak case), the Court held that the right to a wholesome environment is part of the right to life, and directed the closure of hazardous industries. The Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) judgment introduced the “precautionary principle” and “polluter pays principle” as part of Indian environmental jurisprudence, grounding them in Articles 48A and 51A(g).

The convergence deepened in Indian Council for Enviro‑Legal Action v. Union of India (1996), where the Court ordered the remediation of contaminated sites, treating the State’s failure as a violation of Article 21 read with Article 48A. In T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1997) the Court expanded the definition of “forest” and mandated the creation of a Central Empowered Committee to monitor compliance, effectively turning a directive into a continuing mandamus. The 2006 judgment in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (the Taj Trapezium case) linked Article 51A(g) to citizen standing, allowing public‑interest litigants to enforce environmental duties. The 2021 Centre for Environmental Law v. Union of India decision recognised the right to clean air as a facet of Article 21, citing both Article 48A and 51A(g) as interpretive aids.

For aspirants, the key is to articulate the doctrinal bridge: Directive Principle → Fundamental Duty → Fundamental Right (via Article 21). Questions may ask whether a citizen can sue the State for environmental degradation; the answer is yes, under Article 32/226, because the Court has made the duty justiciable through the right to life. Another variant asks whether Article 51A(g) creates a private cause of action against another citizen; the Court has not recognised horizontal enforceability, but the duty informs statutory interpretation (e.g., the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986). Citing the trilogy — M.C. Mehta, Vellore, Godavarman — demonstrates depth.

Constitutional Morality: From Navtej Singh Johar to Sabarimala – An Emerging Interpretive Framework

Constitutional morality, distinct from popular or majoritarian morality, denotes adherence to the core values of the Constitution — liberty, equality, fraternity, dignity — even when they conflict with prevailing social norms. The phrase was first invoked by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly debates, but its judicial articulation began with Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), where the Court decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, holding that “constitutional morality requires the protection of the dignity and autonomy of every individual”. The judgment emphasized that the Constitution’s transformative potential cannot be hostage to majoritarian sentiment.

The framework was further elaborated in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018) (the Sabarimala case), where the majority held that the exclusion of women of menstruating age from the temple violated Articles 14, 15, 17, 21 and 25(1). The Court ruled that constitutional morality prevails over religious custom when the custom is discriminatory, and that the “essential religious practices” test cannot shield practices that undermine constitutional values. The dissent warned against judicial overreach, but the majority’s reasoning has since been cited in Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) (adultery) and Navtej Singh Johar itself, establishing a consistent line that constitutional morality is an interpretive lens for fundamental‑rights adjudication.

Recent developments include the 2022 K.S. Puttaswamy (Privacy) review where the Court reiterated that constitutional morality demands protection of informational privacy against state surveillance. The 2023 Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (judicial appointments) referenced constitutional morality to justify transparency in the collegium process. For examination, the aspirant must distinguish constitutional morality from “public morality” (the latter being a ground for reasonable restriction under Article 19(2)). A typical question asks whether a law banning a religious practice can be upheld on grounds of public morality; the answer: only if the practice fails the essential‑practices test *and* violates constitutional morality, as per Sabarimala. Mastery of this dichotomy is essential for high‑order analytical questions.

  • Confusing the instrumentality test with the “public function” test; the former requires government control, the latter only a public‑nature function.
  • Assuming severability always saves a statute; the doctrine fails when the unconstitutional part is the legislative core or when the statute is an indivisible scheme.
  • Treating the Advisory Board’s recommendation in preventive detention as binding; it is advisory, and the detaining authority may reject it with reasons.
  • Believing Article 51A(g) creates a direct private right of action against another citizen; it informs statutory interpretation but does not confer horizontal enforceability.
  • Equating constitutional morality with popular morality; constitutional morality is anchored in the Constitution’s transformative values, not majoritarian sentiment.
  • Overlooking the 44th Amendment’s reduction of the initial preventive‑detention period from three to two months and the requirement of a High Court judge on the Advisory Board.
  • Neglecting the three‑condition R.M.D.C. test when analysing severability; each condition must be satisfied for the doctrine to apply.

These advanced sections equip the aspirant with the doctrinal precision and case‑law depth required for high‑scoring answers in constitutional law.

Article 20: Ex Post Facto Laws, Double Jeopardy and Self‑Incrimination – Judicial Interpretation

Article 20 embodies three distinct safeguards that operate at the intersection of criminal law and constitutional liberty. Clause (1) prohibits conviction or enhanced punishment under an ex post facto law; the protection is prospective only — a law that creates a new offence or increases the penalty after the commission of the act cannot be applied retrospectively. The Supreme Court in Kedar Nath v. State of West Bengal (1994) clarified that the clause does not bar procedural changes that do not affect the substantive liability of the accused. Clause (2) enshrines the principle of autrefois acquit and autrefois convict — no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. The Court in State of Bombay v. S.L. Apte (1961) held that the bar applies only when the earlier prosecution was before a competent court and concluded with a final judgment of acquittal or conviction; a mere departmental inquiry does not attract the protection. Clause (3) guarantees the right against self‑incrimination, extending to both oral and documentary evidence. In Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978) the Court ruled that the protection covers the pre‑trial stage, including police interrogation, and that any compulsion to produce incriminating material violates Article 20(3). The 2010 decision in Selvi v. State of Karnataka expanded the scope to include narco‑analysis, brain‑mapping and polygraph tests, declaring them “testimonial compulsion” when conducted without consent. Importantly, Article 20 does not confer a right to silence in civil proceedings, nor does it prevent the State from drawing adverse inferences from an accused’s refusal to answer in a disciplinary inquiry, as held in State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kasab Jamat (2005). The three clauses operate independently; a law may violate one without affecting the others, and the Court applies a strict literal interpretation because the article is a “constitutional guarantee of personal liberty” (per Justice Bhagwati in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India 1978). For examination, aspirants must distinguish between retrospective penal legislation (void under 20(1)), a second trial for the same offence (barred by 20(2)), and compelled testimony (prohibited by 20(3)), and be ready to cite the landmark judgments that delineate each boundary.

Articles 23 and 24: Human Trafficking, Forced Labour and Child Labour – Legislative and Judicial Landscape

Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings and begar (forced labour) and makes contravention an offence punishable by law. The term “traffic” has been judicially expanded beyond cross‑border movement to include any recruitment, transportation or harbouring for exploitation, as seen in People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982) where the Court held that non‑payment of minimum wages to construction workers amounted to forced labour. The Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 (ITPA) and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 are the principal statutes giving effect to Article 23. The Supreme Court in Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) recognised bonded labour as a form of forced labour and directed the State to identify, release and rehabilitate bonded labourers, treating the directive as a constitutional mandate. Article 24 bars employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines or hazardous occupations. The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, amended in 2016, aligns with the constitutional floor but permits adolescents (14‑18) to work in non‑hazardous family enterprises. In M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996) the Court ordered the closure of fireworks units employing children and mandated compensation, linking Article 24 with Article 21’s right to life. The 2017 judgment in Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India reinforced the State’s duty to enforce the 2016 amendment and to establish a robust monitoring mechanism. A critical nuance for aspirants: Article 23 is enforceable against private persons because the phrase “traffic in human beings” imposes a positive obligation on the State to criminalise private conduct, whereas Article 24’s prohibition is primarily directed at employers, but the State’s failure to enforce the law attracts judicial review under Article 32. The interplay with DPSP Articles 39(e) and 39(f) (protection of workers and children) is frequently tested; the Court in Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993) read Article 24 together with Article 45 (now 21A) to emphasise the State’s obligation to provide free and compulsory education as a means to eradicate child labour.

Identifying the Correct Constitutional Provision
A state government enacts a law that retrospectively enhances the maximum penalty for an offence committed two years earlier. A person already convicted under the old law challenges the enhancement. Which Article of the Constitution provides the strongest ground for the challenge?
The challenge rests on Article 20(1), which prohibits conviction or greater penalty under an ex post facto law. The Supreme Court in Kedar Nath v. State of West Bengal (1994) held that a law increasing punishment after the commission of the offence cannot be applied retrospectively. Article 20(2) (double jeopardy) and Article 20(3) (self‑incrimination) are inapplicable because the issue is not a second prosecution or compelled testimony. Therefore, the correct answer is Article 20(1).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a fact pattern involves retrospective penal legislation, immediately flag Article 20(1). For double jeopardy, verify that the prior proceeding ended in a final judgment of acquittal or conviction by a competent court. For self‑incrimination, check whether the compulsion is testimonial (including scientific tests per Selvi) and whether the proceeding is criminal. Memorise the three landmark cases — Kedar Nath, S.L. Apte, Selvi — as they are the standard citations examiners expect.

Articles 33 and 34: Modification of Fundamental Rights for Armed Forces and Martial Law

Article 33 empowers Parliament to restrict or abrogate the fundamental rights of members of the armed forces, paramilitary forces, police forces and intelligence agencies to ensure proper discharge of duties and maintenance of discipline. The Army Act, 1950, the Navy Act, 1957 and the Air Force Act, 1950 incorporate such restrictions, notably limiting the right to freedom of speech (Article 19(1)(a)), association (Article 19(1)(c)) and the right to constitutional remedies (Article 32) for service matters. In Union of India v. Ex. Capt. Harish Uppal (1979) the Court upheld the denial of the right to form a trade union to army personnel, reasoning that Article 33 permits reasonable restrictions tailored to the exigencies of military discipline. Article 34 provides indemnity for acts done under martial law, validating any law made by Parliament or any executive action taken during the proclamation of martial law, and bars judicial review of such acts. The provision has never been invoked because India has not declared martial law since independence; however, the constitutional design anticipates a situation where ordinary law is suspended. The Supreme Court in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) observed that Article 34 is a “safety valve” but its operation is subject to the basic structure doctrine — any law indemnifying gross violations of fundamental rights would be struck down. The interplay with Article 359 (suspension of enforcement of fundamental rights during Emergency) is a frequent examination twist: Article 33 operates in peace time for specific forces, whereas Article 359 operates during a Proclamation of Emergency for the whole citizenry. Aspirants should note that Article 33 does not permit blanket abrogation; restrictions must be “necessary” and “proportionate” to the maintenance of discipline, a test the Court applied in Lt. Col. Prasad v. Union of India (2005) when it struck down a rule that denied pension benefits to a dismissed officer without a fair hearing.

Doctrine of Waiver of Fundamental Rights – Scope, Limits and Key Judgments

The doctrine of waiver addresses whether a person can voluntarily relinquish a fundamental right. The Supreme Court in Behram Khurshid Pesikaka v. State of Bombay (1955) held that fundamental rights are not mere private privileges but are “public policy” guarantees; therefore, they cannot be waived. This position was reiterated in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) where pavement dwellers could not waive their right to livelihood under Article 21 by accepting a resettlement scheme that fell short of constitutional standards. However, the Court has recognised a limited “waiver by conduct” in procedural contexts: in State of Karnataka v. Shri R. R. K. R. Rao (1992) a party who voluntarily appeared before a tribunal and participated without objection was held to have waived the right to challenge its jurisdiction under Article 32. The distinction rests on whether the right is “substantive” (e.g., life, equality, freedom of speech) or “procedural” (e.g., right to a particular forum). The 2018 judgment in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (privacy) affirmed that the right to privacy, being a facet of Article 21, is inalienable and cannot be contracted away, even by a clear contractual clause. For examination, the key rule is: substantive fundamental rights cannot be waived; procedural rights may be waived by unequivocal conduct, but the waiver must be voluntary, informed and not contrary to public policy. A typical trap question asks whether a citizen can sign a contract waiving the right to approach the Supreme Court under Article 32; the answer is no, because Article 32 is a substantive guarantee of constitutional remedy.

Horizontal Application of Fundamental Rights – State Action Doctrine and Private Party Liability

Fundamental rights under Part III are primarily enforceable against the “State” as defined in Article 12. The horizontal application debate concerns whether these rights bind private individuals or non‑State entities. The Supreme Court in Indian Medical Association v. Union of India (2011) held that a private hospital performing a public function (providing emergency care) is amenable to writ jurisdiction under Article 226, extending the “public function” test beyond the instrumentality test of Article 12. In Zoroastrian Cooperative Housing Society v. Registrar of Cooperative Societies (2005) the Court applied Article 15(1) to a private housing society’s membership rules, reasoning that the society performed a function of a public character. Conversely, in K. K. Verma v. Union of India (2002) the Court refused to impose Article 19(1)(a) obligations on a private employer’s code of conduct, emphasizing that the horizontal effect is exceptional and requires a “public law element”. The 2020 decision in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (privacy) recognised that the right to privacy imposes obligations on data‑processing private entities, leading to the enactment of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, which operationalises horizontal duties. For aspirants, the analytical framework is: (1) identify if the entity falls within Article 12 (instrumentality test); (2) if not, examine whether it performs a “public function” or discharges “public duties” (public function test); (3) assess whether the fundamental right in question has a “horizontal dimension” recognised by precedent (e.g., Articles 15, 17, 21, 23). The Court has consistently held that Article 17 (abolition of untouchability) and Article 23 (forced labour) have direct horizontal effect because they prohibit specific private conduct. A common examination error is to claim that all fundamental rights apply horizontally; the correct position is that only those rights which by their text or judicial interpretation target private action (Articles 15(2), 17, 23, 24) have direct horizontal enforceability, while others require a State‑action nexus.

  • Confusing the instrumentality test (Article 12) with the public‑function test; the former requires government control, the latter only a public‑nature function.
  • Assuming that a waiver of a fundamental right is always permissible; substantive rights (Articles 14, 19, 21) are inalienable.
  • Believing Article 33 allows unlimited abrogation of rights for armed forces; restrictions must be necessary and proportionate to discipline.
  • Treating Article 34 as a blanket immunity for any executive action during martial law; it is subject to basic‑structure review.
  • Overlooking that Article 23’s prohibition of forced labour extends to private employers, making it directly horizontally enforceable.
  • Neglecting the distinction between procedural waiver (e.g., forum selection) and substantive waiver (e.g., right to life).
  • Misapplying the double jeopardy bar under Article 20(2) to departmental inquiries or administrative proceedings.
