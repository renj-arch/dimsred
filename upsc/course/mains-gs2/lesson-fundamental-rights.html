1. Constitutional Framework of Rights, Principles and Duties

1.1 The Scheme of Part III, Part IV and Part IVA

The Constitution of India distributes the normative architecture of governance across three distinct parts that together shape the relationship between the state and the citizen. Part III, comprising Articles 12 to 35, enumerates the Fundamental Rights that are justiciable and enforceable by the courts. Part IV, covering Articles 36 to 51, sets out the Directive Principles of State Policy, which are non‑justiciable guidelines for the legislature and executive. Part IVA, introduced by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 and later expanded by the 86th Amendment in 2002, contains the Fundamental Duties under Article 51A. This tripartite structure reflects the Constituent Assembly’s intention to balance individual liberty with collective welfare and to remind citizens that rights carry correlative responsibilities.

1.2 Historical Evolution and Constituent Assembly Debates

The debates in the Constituent Assembly reveal a conscious decision to place enforceable rights in a separate chapter while relegating socio‑economic aspirations to a directive chapter. Members such as B. R. Ambedkar argued that justiciable rights would protect minorities and dissent, whereas directives would guide the state toward a welfare orientation without exposing the judiciary to policy‑making. The inclusion of Fundamental Duties was a later development, motivated by the desire to cultivate a sense of civic discipline after the Emergency experience. The 42nd Amendment inserted ten duties, and the 86th Amendment added the duty to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years, reflecting an evolving constitutional consciousness.

1.3 Constitutional Philosophy: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity

The Preamble’s promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity finds operational expression through the three parts. Fundamental Rights secure liberty and equality against arbitrary state action. Directive Principles translate the ideal of social and economic justice into actionable policy goals. Fundamental Duties embody fraternity by obligating citizens to respect the Constitution, the national symbols and the rights of others. Together they constitute a constitutional ecosystem where each component informs the interpretation of the others, a principle the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed in cases such as Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) and Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980).

2. Fundamental Rights: Scope and Judicial Interpretation

2.1 Definition of State and the Reach of Article 12

Article 12 defines the term “State” for the purpose of Part III, extending the ambit of Fundamental Rights beyond the central and state governments to include local authorities, statutory corporations and any other authority under the control of the government. The Supreme Court in R. D. Shetty v. Airport Authority of India (1979) laid down the “instrumentality test” to determine whether a body qualifies as State, emphasizing functional, financial and administrative control. This expansive definition ensures that entities performing public functions cannot evade constitutional accountability by claiming private status.

2.2 Classification of Rights: Civil, Political and Socio‑Economic

Fundamental Rights are traditionally grouped into six categories: Right to Equality (Articles 14‑18), Right to Freedom (Articles 19‑22), Right against Exploitation (Articles 23‑24), Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25‑28), Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29‑30) and Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32). While the first five clusters protect civil and political liberties, the judiciary has progressively read socio‑economic dimensions into them. For instance, Article 21’s guarantee of life and personal liberty has been interpreted to include the right to health, education, shelter and a clean environment, as seen in Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981) and Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993).

2.3 Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine

The power of judicial review under Article 32 and Article 226 makes Fundamental Rights the cornerstone of constitutional supremacy. The Basic Structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati, holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a manner that destroys its essential features, including the fundamental rights framework. Subsequent decisions such as Minerva Mills reinforced that a harmonious construction between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure, preventing any amendment that would give primacy to directives over rights.

2.4 Landmark Expansions: From Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi

The evolution from A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), where “procedure established by law” was read narrowly, to Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court required the procedure to be “fair, just and reasonable,” illustrates the dynamic interpretive trajectory. This shift incorporated principles of natural justice into Article 21, making any law depriving life or liberty subject to substantive due process scrutiny. The Court also linked Articles 14, 19 and 21, establishing the “golden triangle” that any legislation must satisfy simultaneously.

3. Directive Principles of State Policy: Nature and Enforceability

3.1 Classification: Socialistic, Gandhian and Liberal‑Intellectual

The Directive Principles are grouped into three broad categories reflecting the ideological influences on the Constitution. Socialistic principles (Articles 38, 39, 41, 42, 43) aim at securing a just social order, adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, and humane conditions of work. Gandhian principles (Articles 40, 43B, 46, 47, 48) emphasize village panchayats, cottage industries, promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections, prohibition of intoxicating drinks and cow protection. Liberal‑intellectual principles (Articles 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51) advocate a uniform civil code, free and compulsory education, protection of environment, separation of judiciary from executive and promotion of international peace. This taxonomy helps aspirants identify the policy thrust behind each Article.

3.2 Judicial Attitude: From Non‑Justiciability to Interpretive Tool

Although Article 37 declares Directive Principles non‑justiciable, the judiciary has used them as interpretive aids to expand the meaning of Fundamental Rights. In the early phase, the Court treated directives as mere moral exhortations. However, from the 1970s onward, judgments such as Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills elevated directives to a constitutional mandate that informs legislative policy. The Court in Unni Krishnan read Article 45 (free and compulsory education) together with Article 21 to declare a fundamental right to education up to the age of fourteen, later codified by the 86th Amendment as Article 21A. This jurisprudential shift demonstrates that non‑justiciability does not render directives irrelevant; they shape the content of justiciable rights.

3.3 Legislative Implementation and Policy Impact

Many Directive Principles have been translated into statutes, illustrating their practical influence. The Minimum Wages Act, 1948 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 give effect to Articles 39(d) and 39(e). The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments operationalize Article 40 by constitutionally mandating panchayati raj institutions and urban local bodies. The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 reflects Article 41’s right to work. Environmental legislation such as the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 draw from Article 48A. These examples show that while directives lack judicial enforceability, they serve as a constitutional compass for legislative action.

4. Fundamental Duties: Evolution and Constitutional Significance

4.1 Origin, Text and Subsequent Additions

Fundamental Duties were absent from the original Constitution. The 42nd Amendment inserted Article 51A with ten duties, ranging from respecting the Constitution and national symbols to promoting harmony and protecting public property. The 86th Amendment added clause (k), obligating parents or guardians to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. The duties are addressed to “every citizen of India,” making them universally applicable, unlike Fundamental Rights which are available to persons, including non‑citizens in certain cases.

4.2 Judicial Enforcement and Symbolic Value

The Supreme Court has held that Fundamental Duties are not legally enforceable by writs, but they acquire legal significance when a statute incorporates them. In Chandra Bhavan Boarding and Lodging v. State of Mysore (1970), the Court observed that duties serve as a constant reminder of civic obligations. In the more recent case of Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India (2021), the Court suggested that duties can be used to interpret the scope of rights, for instance, the duty to protect the environment under Article 51A(g) informs the content of Article 21. Thus, while duties lack direct justiciability, they function as interpretive guides and as a basis for legislative sanctions.

4.3 Comparative Perspective: Duties in Other Constitutions

Several constitutions, such as those of Japan, Spain and the former Soviet Union, enumerate duties alongside rights. The Indian model is distinctive because duties were added decades after the Constitution’s commencement, reflecting a corrective response to perceived civic apathy. Unlike the Japanese Constitution, where duties are justiciable, Indian duties remain largely moral. Nevertheless, the inclusion of duties in Part IVA signals a constitutional aspiration to balance the rights‑centric discourse with a responsibility‑centric ethos.

5. Interplay, Tension and Harmonious Construction

5.1 The Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Constitutional Interpretation

The Supreme Court has articulated the doctrine of harmonious construction to resolve apparent conflicts between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. The core premise is that the Constitution is an organic whole; no part can be read in isolation to defeat another. In Kesavananda Bharati, the Court held that the amending power under Article 368 cannot be used to destroy the basic structure, which includes the balance between Parts III and IV. This principle obliges the legislature to frame laws that respect rights while pursuing directive goals, and it obliges the judiciary to interpret rights in a manner that advances directive objectives without nullifying them.

5.2 Judicial Balancing in Socio‑Economic Legislation

Cases involving land reform, labour welfare and environmental protection illustrate the balancing act. In State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977), the Court upheld a land ceiling law under Article 31C (as originally enacted) because it implemented Article 39(b) and (c), while ensuring that the law did not violate Article 14. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), the Court recognized the right to livelihood as part of Article 21, linking it to the directive under Article 41. The Court ordered procedural safeguards before evicting pavement dwellers, demonstrating that directive goals cannot be pursued at the expense of fundamental rights without due process.

5.3 Contemporary Challenges and the Way Forward

Emerging issues such as data privacy, climate justice and affirmative action in private education test the harmonious construction framework. The recognition of the right to privacy as a fundamental right in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) shows the Court’s willingness to expand Part III in response to new realities, while directives like Article 48A (environment) and Article 51A(g) (duty to protect environment) provide a constitutional basis for climate litigation. The ongoing debate over the National Education Policy and the implementation of the Right to Education Act reflects the dynamic tension between the aspirational directives and the enforceable rights. A nuanced understanding of this interplay is essential for any serious student of Indian constitutional law.

The constitutional scheme thus envisions a continuous dialogue between rights, principles and duties, each informing the other to realise the vision of a just, equitable and fraternal society.

Article 13 and the Doctrine of Eclipse: Judicial Mechanics

Article 13 is the constitutional gateway that subjects all laws — pre‑constitutional and post‑constitutional — to the supremacy of Fundamental Rights. Clause (1) declares that any law in force immediately before the commencement of the Constitution which is inconsistent with the provisions of Part III shall, to the extent of such inconsistency, be void. Clause (2) extends the same prohibition to laws made after 26 January 1950. The Supreme Court, in Bhikaji Narain v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), articulated the doctrine of eclipse: a pre‑constitutional statute that conflicts with a Fundamental Right does not die; it becomes dormant, eclipsed by the superior constitutional provision. When the constitutional impediment is removed — by amendment, by judicial interpretation narrowing the right, or by the expiry of the right’s applicability — the eclipse lifts and the statute revives automatically.

The doctrine operates only on pre‑constitutional laws. Post‑constitutional laws that violate Part III are void ab initio under Article 13(2); they never acquire legal existence and cannot be revived by subsequent constitutional amendment. This distinction was reinforced in State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills (1974), where the Court held that a law enacted after the Constitution cannot claim the protection of eclipse. The definition of “law” in Article 13(3) — inclusive of ordinances, orders, bye‑laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs or usages having the force of law — ensures a wide net. However, personal laws (e.g., Hindu, Muslim, Christian personal laws) have been treated as “law” only when they are codified or recognized by statute; uncodified personal law remains outside the direct reach of Article 13, a nuance that continues to generate litigation.

Judicial review under Article 13 is not a mere formality. The Court examines the extent of inconsistency — a law may be partially valid if the offending provision can be severed. The severability test (derived from R.M.D.C. v. Union of India, 1957) asks whether the remainder of the statute can stand independently after the unconstitutional portion is excised. If the valid and invalid parts are so intertwined that the legislature would not have enacted the former without the latter, the whole statute falls. This mechanistic approach preserves legislative intent while upholding constitutional supremacy.

In practice, the eclipse doctrine has been invoked in land reform legislation, labour statutes, and regulatory regimes that pre‑date 1950. For instance, the Madhya Pradesh Land Revenue Code provisions that conflicted with Article 31 (pre‑1978) were held eclipsed; after the 44th Amendment removed the right to property from Part III, those provisions revived without fresh legislation. The doctrine thus functions as a constitutional safety valve, allowing the legal order to adapt without legislative vacuum.

Article 21: Substantive Due Process and the Expanding Horizon of Life and Liberty

Article 21 — “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law” — has undergone a metamorphosis from a procedural guarantee to a substantive charter of rights. The early Gopalan (1950) reading confined “procedure established by law” to any enacted procedure, however arbitrary. The turning point came in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court read “procedure” as “fair, just and reasonable” procedure, importing the American due‑process standard. This interpretive leap transformed Article 21 into a reservoir of unenumerated rights: the right to livelihood (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985), the right to health (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal, 1996), the right to shelter (Chameli Singh v. State of U.P., 1996), the right to a clean environment (Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991), and the right to privacy (Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2017).

The Court’s methodology relies on purposive construction and the living tree doctrine: the Constitution is an organic instrument whose meaning evolves with societal needs. Each expansion is anchored in the text — “life” is read as “life with dignity”, “personal liberty” as “freedom from arbitrary restraint”. The Puttaswamy judgment, delivered by a nine‑judge bench, declared privacy a fundamental right intrinsic to Article 21, overruling the earlier Kharak Singh (1962) and MP Sharma (1954) positions. The judgment also laid down a three‑fold test for any intrusion: legality, necessity, and proportionality — a framework now applied in surveillance, data‑protection, and Aadhaar‑related litigation.

Substantive due process under Article 21 also interacts with Directive Principles. In State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977), the Court upheld land‑ceiling laws under Article 31C (as originally enacted) while insisting that the procedure for acquisition satisfy Article 21’s fairness requirement. Similarly, Olga Tellis linked the right to livelihood (Article 21) with the directive under Article 41 (right to work), ordering procedural safeguards before evicting pavement dwellers. This interplay demonstrates that Article 21 is not a static shield but a dynamic conduit through which directive aspirations acquire justiciable force.

Recent jurisprudence extends Article 21 to digital rights. In K.S. Puttaswamy (II) (2020), the Court recognized the right to informational privacy, laying groundwork for future data‑protection legislation. The Internet shutdown cases (Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, 2020) applied the proportionality test to restrictions on freedom of speech and movement, reinforcing that any deprivation of liberty — physical or virtual — must meet the Article 21 standard. The trajectory signals that Article 21 will continue to absorb emerging claims, from climate‑induced displacement to algorithmic discrimination, making it the most litigated and expansive provision in Part III.

Examiner's Trap: Distinguishing Eclipse from Void Ab Initio
A 1948 statute regulating trade unions conflicts with Article 19(1)(c). The statute is amended in 1960 to remove the conflict. In 1975, a new law on the same subject is enacted, but it violates Article 19(1)(c). Which of the following statements is correct? (a) The 1948 statute revives automatically after the 1960 amendment. (b) The 1975 law is void ab initio and cannot be revived by any future amendment. (c) Both (a) and (b) are correct. (d) Neither (a) nor (b) is correct.
Correct answer: (c). The 1948 statute is pre‑constitutional; its inconsistency with Article 19(1)(c) rendered it eclipsed under the doctrine of eclipse (Bhikaji Narain). When the 1960 amendment removed the inconsistency, the eclipse lifted and the statute revived automatically. The 1975 law is post‑constitutional; under Article 13(2) any law made after 26 January 1950 that contravenes a Fundamental Right is void ab initio and cannot be resurrected by subsequent amendment (Ambica Mills). Therefore both statements are correct.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the effect of a constitutional amendment on an old statute, first identify whether the statute is pre‑ or post‑constitutional. Pre‑constitutional laws attract the eclipse doctrine; post‑constitutional laws are void ab initio. Memorise the two landmark cases — Bhikaji Narain (eclipse) and Ambica Mills (void ab initio) — and the severability test from R.M.D.C. This two‑step classification solves 80 % of Article 13 MCQs.

Article 31C and the Constitutional Amendments: Scope, Limits and Judicial Review

Article 31C was introduced by the 25th Amendment Act, 1971 to insulate laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) and (c) — distribution of material resources and prevention of concentration of wealth — from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. The original text read: “Notwithstanding anything contained in Article 13, no law giving effect to the policy of the State towards securing the principles specified in clause (b) or clause (c) of Article 39 shall be deemed to be void on the ground that it is inconsistent with, or takes away or abridges any of the rights conferred by Article 14, Article 19 or Article 31.” This created a limited “directive‑principle primacy” zone.

The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 dramatically expanded Article 31C by replacing “clause (b) or clause (c) of Article 39” with “all or any of the principles laid down in Part IV”. The amendment also added a declaration that such laws shall not be questioned in any court on the ground of inconsistency with Fundamental Rights. This move aimed to place the entire Directive Principles chapter beyond judicial scrutiny, effectively subordinating Part III to Part IV.

The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) struck down the 42nd Amendment’s expansion. A five‑judge bench held that the amendment destroyed the “basic structure” by removing judicial review — a core feature of the Constitution. The Court restored Article 31C to its pre‑1976 form, i.e., protection only for laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c). The judgment re‑affirmed that the harmony between Parts III and IV is part of the basic structure; neither part can be made absolutely supreme over the other.

Subsequent jurisprudence clarified the operational contours. In Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981), the Court held that Article 31C protection applies only if the law “directly and substantially” implements Article 39(b) or (c). Mere incidental furtherance is insufficient. The State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977) decision, rendered before Minerva Mills, had upheld a land‑ceiling law under the original Article 31C, illustrating the pre‑1976 scope. Post‑Minerva, the Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) had already laid down that the amending power cannot alter the basic structure; Minerva Mills applied that principle to the specific amendment of Article 31C.

Today, Article 31C remains a narrow shield. Legislative attempts to invoke it for broader welfare statutes (e.g., the Right to Education Act, 2009) have been scrutinised: the Court in Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012) refused to extend Article 31C protection to the RTE Act because it did not directly implement Article 39(b) or (c). The provision thus stands as a constitutional reminder that directive‑principle primacy is exceptional, not general, and that judicial review remains the ultimate arbiter of the balance between rights and directives.

The Role of Fundamental Duties in Statutory Interpretation and Public Interest Litigation

Although Fundamental Duties under Article 51A are non‑justiciable, the Supreme Court has consistently used them as interpretive aids. In Chandra Bhavan Boarding and Lodging v. State of Mysore (1970), the Court observed that duties serve as a “constant reminder of civic obligations”. This principle was expanded in Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India (2021), where the Court held that the duty to protect the environment under Article 51A(g) informs the content of Article 21, thereby making environmental degradation a justiciable violation of the right to life.

Statutory interpretation frequently invokes Article 51A. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 have been read in light of Article 51A(g) to impose stricter compliance standards. In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987), the Court directed the closure of polluting tanneries, citing the duty to protect the environment as a constitutional mandate that supplements statutory provisions. Similarly, the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 has been interpreted with reference to Article 51A(k) (duty of parents to provide education), reinforcing the state’s obligation to ensure access.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) has become the primary vehicle for enforcing duties indirectly. The Court entertains PILs where the petitioner alleges a breach of a duty that correlates with a Fundamental Right. For instance, in Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996), the Court linked Article 51A(g) with Article 21 to order the remediation of hazardous waste sites. In People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2004), the duty to promote harmony (Article 51A(e)) was read alongside Article 14 to strike down discriminatory practices.

The 86th Amendment (2002) added Article 51A(k) — duty of parents to provide education — and the 97th Amendment (2011) inserted Article 51A(j) — duty to develop scientific temper. These additions have been cited in judgments concerning curriculum reform and scientific research funding. While duties cannot be directly enforced by writs, their presence in Part IVA creates a constitutional “soft law” layer that guides legislative drafting, executive policy, and judicial reasoning, effectively bridging the gap between aspirational directives and enforceable rights.

Emerging Jurisprudence: Privacy, Data Protection and Environmental Duties under Part III and IV

The last decade has witnessed a convergence of Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, and Fundamental Duties around three frontier issues: informational privacy, data governance, and climate justice. The nine‑judge bench in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) declared privacy a fundamental right intrinsic to Article 21, grounding it in the dignity and autonomy of the individual. The judgment explicitly referenced Article 51A(g) (duty to protect the environment) and Article 48A (directive to protect and improve the environment) to argue that privacy extends to ecological integrity — a novel doctrinal link.

Data protection, while not yet codified in a comprehensive statute, has been shaped by this privacy jurisprudence. The Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019 (subsequently withdrawn) and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (enacted) draw heavily on the Puttaswamy proportionality test: any data processing must be lawful, necessary, and proportionate. The Supreme Court in K.S. Puttaswamy (II) (2020) applied the test to the Aadhaar architecture, mandating purpose limitation and data minimisation — principles that now permeate the 2023 Act. The Court also recognised a “right to be forgotten” as a facet of informational privacy, aligning with comparative developments in the EU’s GDPR.

Climate litigation leverages the triad of Article 21 (right to life), Article 48A (directive to protect environment), and Article 51A(g) (duty to protect environment). In Ridhima Pandey v. Union of India (2021), a nine‑year‑old petitioner invoked these provisions to demand stricter climate targets. The Court, while not issuing a binding order, acknowledged the justiciability of climate obligations and directed the government to consider the petition in policy formulation. Similarly, M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2024) used Article 21 and Article 48A to order the protection of the Great Indian Bustard habitat, treating biodiversity loss as a violation of the right to a healthy environment.

These developments illustrate a doctrinal shift: the Court no longer treats Parts III, IV, and IVA as watertight compartments. Instead, it employs a harmonious construction that reads duties and directives as interpretive lenses for expanding rights. The result is a dynamic constitutional ecosystem where a citizen’s duty to protect the environment becomes a judicial tool to enforce the state’s directive to safeguard natural resources, which in turn reinforces the individual’s right to life. For aspirants, the key takeaway is that the “rights‑duties‑directives” triangle is now the primary analytical framework for any emerging constitutional challenge — be it algorithmic bias, genomic data, or inter‑generational climate equity.

Article 14: From Reasonable Classification to the Arbitrariness Test

The equality guarantee in Article 14 has travelled through three doctrinal phases. The early reasonable classification test, crystallised in State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952), required that any classification be founded on an intelligible differentia which has a rational nexus with the object sought to be achieved. This two‑pronged test dominated the 1950s and 1960s, allowing the State wide latitude to differentiate between groups so long as the basis was not “arbitrary” in a loose sense.

The second phase began with E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974), where Justice Bhagwati articulated that equality is a dynamic concept and that “arbitrariness” is the antithesis of equality. The Court moved from a classification‑centric inquiry to a direct scrutiny of whether the State action is arbitrary, unreasonable, or lacks a rational principle. This shift was consolidated in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the majority held that any law depriving a person of life or personal liberty must satisfy the test of reasonableness under Article 21, and by extension, the arbitrariness test under Article 14.

The third, contemporary phase refines the arbitrariness test into a structured proportionality analysis. In Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) (the triple talaq case), the Court applied a four‑step proportionality test — legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, and balancing — to strike down a personal law practice as manifestly arbitrary. The same framework was employed in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) to decriminalise consensual same‑sex relations, and in Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) to invalidate the adultery provision. The Court now asks: (i) whether the measure pursues a legitimate objective; (ii) whether it is rationally connected to that objective; (iii) whether a less restrictive alternative exists; and (iv) whether the burden imposed is disproportionate to the benefit secured.

Two important exceptions remain. First, Article 15(4) and 16(4) permit affirmative action for socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes; these are expressly saved from the arbitrariness test by constitutional text. Second, Article 31C (as limited by Minerva Mills v. Union of India, 1980) shields laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c) from Article 14 challenge, but only if the law does not violate the basic structure. The 42nd Amendment’s attempt to extend this shield to all Directive Principles was struck down, preserving the arbitrariness test for the bulk of socio‑economic legislation.

For answer‑writing, the examiner expects a clear chronological narrative: classification test → arbitrariness as a facet of equality → proportionality as the modern doctrinal tool. Cite the landmark judgments with years, and distinguish between “classification” (a legislative technique) and “arbitrariness” (a constitutional standard).

Article 19(1)(a): Prior Restraint, Hate Speech and the Architecture of Reasonable Restrictions

Freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) is not absolute; Article 19(2) enumerates eight grounds on which the State may impose “reasonable restrictions”. The Supreme Court has consistently held that prior restraint — censorship before publication — carries a heavy presumption of unconstitutionality. In Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950) and Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi (1950), the Court struck down pre‑publication bans, establishing that any prior restraint must be justified by a clear and present danger to a protected interest.

The “clear and present danger” test was refined in Superintendent, Central Prison v. Ram Manohar Lohia (1960) and later in S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram (1989), where the Court required a direct and proximate link between the speech and the threatened harm. The test was further calibrated in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015), which struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 for being vague and overbroad, and reiterated that restrictions must be narrowly tailored, clearly defined, and proportionate.

On hate speech, the Court has balanced dignity and public order against free expression. In Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. Union of India (2014), the Court declined to lay down a comprehensive definition but directed the Law Commission to examine the issue. The Law Commission’s 267th Report (2017) recommended a new provision criminalising incitement to hatred based on religion, race, caste, community, gender, sexual orientation, etc. The Supreme Court in Amish Devgan v. Union of India (2020) held that “hate speech” is not a separate ground under Article 19(2) but must be read within “public order”, “decency or morality”, or “incitement to an offence”. The Court emphasized the “contextual” approach: the same words may be permissible in a historical analysis but impermissible when used to mobilise violence.

The reasonable restriction architecture now follows a three‑step test derived from K.A. Abbas v. Union of India (1971) and refined in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): (i) the restriction must be prescribed by law (statutory, not executive fiat); (ii) it must fall within one of the eight enumerated grounds; (iii) it must satisfy the proportionality standard — legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, and balancing. The Court in Anuradha Bhasin applied this to internet shutdowns in Jammu & Kashmir, holding that indefinite suspension of internet access fails the necessity and balancing prongs.

Examiners frequently test the distinction between prior restraint and subsequent punishment, the scope of “public order” versus “law and order”, and the requirement that the restricting law be “reasonable” and not merely “rational”. A precise answer cites the chronological evolution: Romesh Thappar → Ram Manohar Lohia → Shreya Singhal → Anuradha Bhasin, and notes the Law Commission’s pending recommendations on hate speech.

Identifying the Correct Test for a Speech Restriction
A state enacts a law requiring all online news portals to obtain a government licence before publishing any article on “matters of public importance”. The licence can be denied if the authority believes the content may “disturb communal harmony”. A portal challenges the law under Article 19(1)(a). Which constitutional test should the Court apply, and what is the likely outcome?
Step 1 – Characterise the restriction: The law imposes a prior restraint (pre‑publication licensing). Step 2 – Identify the applicable standard: Prior restraints attract a heavy presumption of invalidity; the State must show a clear and present danger to a ground under Article 19(2) (here, “public order” or “decency or morality”). Step 3 – Apply the three‑step reasonable‑restriction test: (i) Is the restriction prescribed by law? Yes, a statute exists. (ii) Does it fall within an enumerated ground? “Communal harmony” is not a separate ground; it must be read into “public order”. (iii) Is it proportionate? The licence grants unguided discretion (“believes … may disturb”), failing the necessity and balancing prongs (Shreya Singhal; Anuradha Bhasin). Conclusion: The law is unconstitutional — it is a vague, overbroad prior restraint lacking procedural safeguards.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks “which test applies”, first classify the restriction (prior restraint, content‑based, content‑neutral). Then map it to the doctrinal lineage: prior restraint → clear and present danger → proportionality; content‑based → strict scrutiny (legitimate aim + proportionality); content‑neutral → intermediate scrutiny (time, place, manner). Write the three‑step test explicitly; examiners award marks for each step.

Article 31C After Minerva Mills: The Narrow Shield for Directive Principles

Article 31C, inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971) and expanded by the 42nd Amendment (1976), was designed to insulate laws giving effect to Directive Principles from Article 14 and Article 19 challenges. The original 25th Amendment protected only laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) — the distributive justice clauses. The 42nd Amendment widened the shield to “all or any of the Directive Principles”, and also barred judicial review of such laws.

In Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980), a five‑judge bench struck down the 42nd Amendment’s expansion on two grounds. First, the amendment destroyed the basic structure by removing judicial review, a core feature of constitutional supremacy. Second, the Court held that the Directive Principles cannot override Fundamental Rights; the two parts are meant to be harmonised, not placed in a hierarchy. Consequently, Article 31C today protects only laws enacted to give effect to Article 39(b) and (c), and even that protection is subject to the basic‑structure limitation — a law cannot, for instance, abrogate the right to equality in a manner that destroys the constitutional identity.

Subsequent jurisprudence has clarified the scope. In Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981), the Court held that Article 31C does not protect laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda judgment) if they violate fundamental rights. In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), a nine‑judge bench ruled that any Ninth Schedule law enacted after 1973 is open to challenge on the touchstone of basic structure, and Article 31C cannot be used as a blanket immunity.

The practical effect is narrow: a land‑reform law that redistributes ownership of material resources (Article 39(b)) or prevents concentration of wealth (Article 39(c)) may be shielded from Article 14/19 challenges, provided it does not violate the basic structure (e.g., by eliminating judicial review altogether). However, socio‑economic legislation aimed at other Directive Principles — such as Article 41 (right to work), Article 43 (living wage), or Article 48A (environment) — enjoys no Article 31C protection and must satisfy the ordinary reasonable‑restriction or arbitrariness tests.

For mains answers, distinguish three layers: (i) the original 25th Amendment protection (Article 39(b),(c) only); (ii) the 42nd Amendment’s overreach (struck down); (iii) the post‑Minerva Mills position — a limited, basic‑structure‑subject shield. Cite Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao (1981), and I.R. Coelho (2007) with precise holdings.

Judicial Review of Constitutional Amendments: Basic Structure, Constitutional Identity and the Kesavananda Legacy

The power of Parliament to amend the Constitution under Article 368 is not unlimited. The basic structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) by a 7‑6 majority, holds that certain core features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law — cannot be abrogated even by a constitutional amendment. The doctrine was a response to the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments which sought to curtail judicial review and expand legislative immunity.

Subsequent cases have refined the catalogue. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), the Court added free and fair elections to the basic structure. Minerva Mills (1980) included judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. Waman Rao (1981) extended the doctrine to amendments made after 24 April 1973. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) recognised secularism and federalism as basic features. I.R. Coelho (2007) held that the basic structure test applies to Ninth Schedule laws as well.

A newer conceptual layer — constitutional identity — emerged in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) (the NJAC case). The Court stated that the Constitution’s identity comprises its “core constitutional values” which are immutable. This formulation aligns with comparative doctrines (German “eternity clause”, South African “constitutional supremacy”) and signals that the basic structure is not a static list but a living principle protecting the Constitution’s essential character.

The doctrine operates as a substantive limitation on the amendment power: an amendment that destroys or damages a basic feature is void ab initio. The Court does not review the procedural correctness of an amendment (special majority, state ratification) but its substantive impact. The test is whether the amendment “alters the basic framework” or “changes the identity” of the Constitution. In practice, the Court has struck down only a handful of amendments — the 39th (1975) curtailing judicial review of election disputes, the 42nd (1976) expanding Article 31C and curbing judicial review, and the 99th (2014) establishing the NJAC.

For answer writing, present the evolution chronologically, list the recognised basic features with case citations, and explain the distinction between “basic structure” (judicially enumerated) and “constitutional identity” (a broader, principle‑based concept). Emphasise that the doctrine preserves the supremacy of the Constitution over Parliament’s constituent power.

The Doctrine of Proportionality in Article 21 Expansions: A Structured Framework

Since Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), Article 21 has been read as incorporating a substantive due‑process requirement: any law depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be “fair, just and reasonable”. The Supreme Court has progressively adopted a four‑step proportionality test to evaluate such deprivations, mirroring the German and Canadian models.

The four steps are: (1) Legitimate aim — the law must pursue a constitutionally permissible objective (e.g., public health, national security, morality). (2) Suitability (rational connection) — the measure must be rationally connected to the aim. (3) Necessity (least restrictive means) — there must be no less intrusive alternative that achieves the same objective. (4) Balancing (proportionality stricto sensu) — the benefits of the measure must outweigh the severity of the infringement on the right.

Key applications: In K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), the nine‑judge bench applied the test to the Aadhaar Act, holding that the mandatory linking of Aadhaar with bank accounts and mobile numbers failed the necessity and balancing prongs for certain categories of citizens. In Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (II) v. Union of India (2020), the Court struck down the provision allowing disclosure of Aadhaar data for “national security” without judicial oversight, finding it disproportionate. In Navtej Singh Johar (2018), the criminalisation of consensual same‑sex relations was held disproportionate because the State could not demonstrate a legitimate aim that justified the severe intrusion on dignity and autonomy.

The Court has also used proportionality to review preventive detention laws. In A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) the Court adopted a formalistic “procedure established by law” test, but post‑Maneka the proportionality framework requires that the detention order be based on relevant material, that the detenu be given an effective opportunity to represent, and that the duration be the minimum necessary. In Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab (1994), the Court upheld the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act’s detention provisions only after reading in procedural safeguards that satisfied the necessity and balancing steps.

For mains, the examiner expects a clear articulation of the four steps, citation of at least two post‑2017 judgments where the test was applied, and an explanation of how proportionality differs from the earlier “reasonableness” test (which was more deferential). Note that the test is also employed under Article 14 (arbitrariness) and Article 19(2) (reasonable restrictions), creating a unified doctrinal vocabulary across Parts III and IV.

  • Confusing the reasonable classification test (Article 14, pre‑1974) with the modern arbitrariness/proportionality test; the latter supersedes the former for most State actions.
  • Treating prior restraint as merely a “restriction” under Article 19(2) without invoking the heavy presumption of invalidity and the clear‑and‑present‑danger standard.
  • Assuming Article 31C protects all Directive Principles; after Minerva Mills it shields only Article 39(b) and (c) and remains subject to basic‑structure review.
  • Listing basic‑structure features without citing the specific judgment that recognised each (e.g., free and fair elections — Indira Gandhi 1975; judicial review — Minerva Mills 1980).
  • Applying the proportionality test mechanically without distinguishing its four prongs; examiners award marks for each prong analysed separately.
  • Overlooking that Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) are not directly enforceable but serve as interpretive aids; they cannot ground a writ petition on their own.
  • Misreading “law” in Article 13(3) to include uncodified personal laws; only codified or statutorily recognised personal laws fall within the definition.

Mastery of these advanced doctrinal layers enables precise, citation‑rich answers that distinguish a top‑rank script from a generic one.

Article 19(1)(g) and the Freedom of Trade: Balancing Economic Regulation and Fundamental Rights

Article 19(1)(g) guarantees every citizen the right to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business. The right is not absolute; clause (6) permits the State to impose reasonable restrictions in the interest of the general public, and to prescribe professional or technical qualifications. The Supreme Court has evolved a two‑stage test: first, whether the impugned measure falls within the enumerated grounds of clause (6); second, whether the restriction is reasonable, applying the proportionality framework that now governs most Part III adjudication.

In Excel Wear v. Union of India (1979) the Court held that a total ban on the manufacture of a product could be a reasonable restriction only if the State demonstrated a compelling public interest and the absence of a less restrictive alternative. The decision signalled a shift from the earlier “reasonable classification” approach to a stricter scrutiny that anticipates the modern proportionality test. State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kasab Jamat (2005) upheld a statutory prohibition on cow slaughter, finding that the legislation pursued a legitimate aim (preservation of cattle wealth) and that the restriction was proportionate because the State had provided for alternative livelihoods. By contrast, in K. T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2011) the Court struck down a land‑acquisition notification that effectively extinguished a plantation business without adequate compensation, ruling that the measure failed the necessity and balancing prongs of proportionality.

The jurisprudence shows three recurring themes. First, the State must articulate a concrete public‑interest objective — vague references to “economic development” are insufficient. Second, the restriction must be the least intrusive means; the Court frequently asks whether licensing, zoning, or phased implementation could achieve the same goal. Third, the impact on the claimant’s livelihood is weighed against the societal benefit, with the Court demanding empirical justification for severe deprivations. This doctrinal trajectory aligns Article 19(1)(g) with the proportionality standard applied under Article 21 and Article 14, creating a unified vocabulary for economic‑rights adjudication.

The Ninth Schedule and Judicial Review: The I.R. Coelho Decision and Its Aftermath

The Ninth Schedule, inserted by the First Amendment (1951), was designed to insulate land‑reform and other socio‑economic laws from judicial review. For decades the Schedule functioned as a “legislative safe‑haven”, but the Supreme Court progressively limited its immunity. The turning point came in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), a nine‑judge bench decision that held any law placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment) is subject to judicial review if it violates the basic structure of the Constitution.

The Court reasoned that the constituent power of Parliament under Article 368 cannot be used to amend the Constitution in a manner that destroys its basic features. Since the Ninth Schedule operates through constitutional amendments, each insertion after Kesavananda must be tested against the basic‑structure doctrine. The judgment explicitly overruled the earlier view in Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) that all Ninth‑Schedule laws were immune, and clarified that pre‑1973 insertions remain protected only insofar as they do not infringe fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure.

Post‑Coelho, the Supreme Court has applied the test in several cases. In Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) (though decided earlier, its reasoning was reaffirmed) the Court struck down clauses that curtailed judicial review. More recently, in Madhav Rao Scindia v. Union of India (2020) the Court examined a Ninth‑Schedule law on agricultural tenancy and held that the provision violated the equality guarantee under Article 14, thereby attracting basic‑structure scrutiny. The practical effect is that the Ninth Schedule no longer offers blanket immunity; it merely shifts the burden of proof to the challenger to demonstrate a basic‑structure violation.

For answer writing, candidates should note the chronological evolution: First Amendment (1951) → Fourth Amendment (1955) → Seventeenth Amendment (1964) → Kesavananda (1973) → Waman Rao (1981) → I.R. Coelho (2007). The key takeaway is that the basic‑structure doctrine acts as a constitutional “gatekeeper” for any legislative attempt to circumvent Part III through the Ninth Schedule.

Article 300A and the Right to Property: From Fundamental Right to Constitutional Right

Article 300A, inserted by the Forty‑fourth Amendment (1978), provides that “no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law”. The provision transformed the right to property from a fundamental right under the original Article 31 into a constitutional right enforceable only through High Court writ jurisdiction under Article 226. The Supreme Court in Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar v. State of Gujarat (1995) held that Article 300A does not confer a fundamental right; consequently, a violation cannot be remedied under Article 32. The Court emphasised that the phrase “authority of law” imports a requirement of legislative sanction and procedural fairness, but not the full panoply of Article 21 due‑process protections.

Subsequent decisions have refined the scope of “authority of law”. In State of Haryana v. Mukesh Kumar (2011) the Court ruled that a land‑acquisition notification issued without a valid statutory basis violated Article 300A, because the executive action lacked legislative authorisation. The judgment also imported the arbitrariness test from Article 14, holding that a deprivation that is “arbitrary, capricious or without reasonable nexus” to a public purpose is unconstitutional even under Article 300A. This convergence means that while the right is not a fundamental right, the State must still satisfy a reasonableness standard akin to the proportionality analysis applied to Article 19(1)(g).

Importantly, the Court has refused to read a “fair compensation” requirement into Article 300A, distinguishing it from the erstwhile Article 31(2). In K. T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2011) the Court reiterated that the adequacy of compensation is a matter for legislative policy, not judicial review, unless the compensation is so illusory as to amount to a fraud on the Constitution. For mains, candidates should contrast the three regimes: (i) pre‑1978 Article 31 (fundamental right with justiciable compensation), (ii) post‑1978 Article 300A (constitutional right, limited to authority of law and non‑arbitrariness), and (iii) the current doctrinal overlap where Article 14 arbitrariness and Article 21 proportionality inform Article 300A challenges.

Article 21 and the Right to Health: Judicial Expansion and Policy Implications

Although the Constitution does not enumerate a right to health, the Supreme Court has read it into Article 21 through a series of landmark judgments. In Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996) the Court declared that the right to life includes the right to emergency medical care, and directed the State to provide adequate health infrastructure. The judgment established a positive obligation on the State, moving beyond the negative “non‑interference” model of early Article 21 jurisprudence.

In Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (1995) the Court linked the right to health with the right to a wholesome environment, holding that hazardous industrial activity violating environmental norms also infringes Article 21. The decision introduced the “polluter pays” principle as a constitutional mandate. State of Punjab v. Mohinder Singh Chawla (1997) extended the right to health to include access to affordable medicines, directing the government to regulate drug prices in the public interest.

More recently, the Court has used the proportionality framework to assess health‑related restrictions. During the COVID‑19 pandemic, in In Re: Distribution of Essential Supplies (2021) the Court applied the four‑step test to evaluate lockdown measures, vaccine allocation policies, and oxygen supply chains, insisting that any deprivation of liberty or livelihood must be the least restrictive means to achieve the public‑health aim. The jurisprudence signals a shift from declaratory pronouncements to enforceable structural orders, often monitored through continuing mandamus.

For answer writing, candidates should map the evolution: (i) negative right (no deprivation without procedure) → (ii) positive right (State duty to provide health facilities) → (iii) structural enforcement (continuing mandamus, budgetary directions). Cite at least three cases across the three phases, and explain how the proportionality test now governs the balance between individual health claims and collective resource constraints.

Fundamental Duties and the Emerging Jurisprudence of Constitutional Morality

Article 51A, inserted by the Forty‑second Amendment (1976) and expanded by the Eighty‑sixth Amendment (2002), enumerates eleven fundamental duties. While not directly enforceable, the Supreme Court has increasingly invoked them as interpretive aids to flesh out the concept of “constitutional morality” — a normative standard that guides the exercise of State power and the interpretation of fundamental rights. In M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006) the Court held that constitutional morality requires adherence to the values underlying the Constitution, including the duties in Article 51A, when adjudicating the validity of reservations in promotions.

The duty to “develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform” (Article 51A(h)) was relied upon in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) to strike down Section 377, with the Court observing that a society that criminalises consensual same‑sex relations fails the test of constitutional morality. Similarly, in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2019) (Sabarimala), the majority opinion cited Article 51A(e) (renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women) to reinforce the judgment that the exclusionary practice violated Articles 14, 15 and 21.

These decisions illustrate a two‑way dynamic: fundamental duties inform the content of constitutional morality, which in turn shapes the scope of fundamental rights. The Court has also used duties to impose positive obligations on the State. In K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) the nine‑judge bench referenced Article 51A(g) (protect and improve the natural environment) while recognising the right to privacy as intrinsic to dignity, thereby linking environmental duty to the privacy‑dignity nexus.

For mains, aspirants should be able to articulate the “interpretive triangle”: (1) Fundamental Rights (Part III), (2) Directive Principles (Part IV), (3) Fundamental Duties (Part IVA). The triangle operates through constitutional morality, allowing duties to act as a bridge that converts aspirational directives into justiciable standards. Cite Nagaraj (2006), Navtej (2018), Sabarimala (2019) and Puttaswamy (2017) to demonstrate the doctrinal trajectory.
