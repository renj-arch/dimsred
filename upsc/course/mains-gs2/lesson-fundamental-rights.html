1. Constitutional Framework of Rights, Principles and Duties

1.1 The Scheme of Part III, Part IV and Part IVA

The Constitution of India distributes the normative architecture of governance across three distinct parts that together shape the relationship between the state and the citizen. Part III, comprising Articles 12 to 35, enumerates the Fundamental Rights that are justiciable and enforceable by the courts. Part IV, covering Articles 36 to 51, sets out the Directive Principles of State Policy, which are non‑justiciable guidelines for the legislature and executive. Part IVA, introduced by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 and later expanded by the 86th Amendment in 2002, contains the Fundamental Duties under Article 51A. This tripartite structure reflects the Constituent Assembly’s intention to balance individual liberty with collective welfare and to remind citizens that rights carry correlative responsibilities.

1.2 Historical Evolution and Constituent Assembly Debates

The debates in the Constituent Assembly reveal a conscious decision to place enforceable rights in a separate chapter while relegating socio‑economic aspirations to a directive chapter. Members such as B. R. Ambedkar argued that justiciable rights would protect minorities and dissent, whereas directives would guide the state toward a welfare orientation without exposing the judiciary to policy‑making. The inclusion of Fundamental Duties was a later development, motivated by the desire to cultivate a sense of civic discipline after the Emergency experience. The 42nd Amendment inserted ten duties, and the 86th Amendment added the duty to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years, reflecting an evolving constitutional consciousness.

1.3 Constitutional Philosophy: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity

The Preamble’s promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity finds operational expression through the three parts. Fundamental Rights secure liberty and equality against arbitrary state action. Directive Principles translate the ideal of social and economic justice into actionable policy goals. Fundamental Duties embody fraternity by obligating citizens to respect the Constitution, the national symbols and the rights of others. Together they constitute a constitutional ecosystem where each component informs the interpretation of the others, a principle the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed in cases such as Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) and Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980).

2. Fundamental Rights: Scope and Judicial Interpretation

2.1 Definition of State and the Reach of Article 12

Article 12 defines the term “State” for the purpose of Part III, extending the ambit of Fundamental Rights beyond the central and state governments to include local authorities, statutory corporations and any other authority under the control of the government. The Supreme Court in R. D. Shetty v. Airport Authority of India (1979) laid down the “instrumentality test” to determine whether a body qualifies as State, emphasizing functional, financial and administrative control. This expansive definition ensures that entities performing public functions cannot evade constitutional accountability by claiming private status.

2.2 Classification of Rights: Civil, Political and Socio‑Economic

Fundamental Rights are traditionally grouped into six categories: Right to Equality (Articles 14‑18), Right to Freedom (Articles 19‑22), Right against Exploitation (Articles 23‑24), Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25‑28), Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29‑30) and Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32). While the first five clusters protect civil and political liberties, the judiciary has progressively read socio‑economic dimensions into them. For instance, Article 21’s guarantee of life and personal liberty has been interpreted to include the right to health, education, shelter and a clean environment, as seen in Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981) and Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993).

2.3 Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine

The power of judicial review under Article 32 and Article 226 makes Fundamental Rights the cornerstone of constitutional supremacy. The Basic Structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati, holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a manner that destroys its essential features, including the fundamental rights framework. Subsequent decisions such as Minerva Mills reinforced that a harmonious construction between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure, preventing any amendment that would give primacy to directives over rights.

2.4 Landmark Expansions: From Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi

The evolution from A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), where “procedure established by law” was read narrowly, to Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court required the procedure to be “fair, just and reasonable,” illustrates the dynamic interpretive trajectory. This shift incorporated principles of natural justice into Article 21, making any law depriving life or liberty subject to substantive due process scrutiny. The Court also linked Articles 14, 19 and 21, establishing the “golden triangle” that any legislation must satisfy simultaneously.

3. Directive Principles of State Policy: Nature and Enforceability

3.1 Classification: Socialistic, Gandhian and Liberal‑Intellectual

The Directive Principles are grouped into three broad categories reflecting the ideological influences on the Constitution. Socialistic principles (Articles 38, 39, 41, 42, 43) aim at securing a just social order, adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, and humane conditions of work. Gandhian principles (Articles 40, 43B, 46, 47, 48) emphasize village panchayats, cottage industries, promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections, prohibition of intoxicating drinks and cow protection. Liberal‑intellectual principles (Articles 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51) advocate a uniform civil code, free and compulsory education, protection of environment, separation of judiciary from executive and promotion of international peace. This taxonomy helps aspirants identify the policy thrust behind each Article.

3.2 Judicial Attitude: From Non‑Justiciability to Interpretive Tool

Although Article 37 declares Directive Principles non‑justiciable, the judiciary has used them as interpretive aids to expand the meaning of Fundamental Rights. In the early phase, the Court treated directives as mere moral exhortations. However, from the 1970s onward, judgments such as Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills elevated directives to a constitutional mandate that informs legislative policy. The Court in Unni Krishnan read Article 45 (free and compulsory education) together with Article 21 to declare a fundamental right to education up to the age of fourteen, later codified by the 86th Amendment as Article 21A. This jurisprudential shift demonstrates that non‑justiciability does not render directives irrelevant; they shape the content of justiciable rights.

3.3 Legislative Implementation and Policy Impact

Many Directive Principles have been translated into statutes, illustrating their practical influence. The Minimum Wages Act, 1948 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 give effect to Articles 39(d) and 39(e). The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments operationalize Article 40 by constitutionally mandating panchayati raj institutions and urban local bodies. The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 reflects Article 41’s right to work. Environmental legislation such as the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 draw from Article 48A. These examples show that while directives lack judicial enforceability, they serve as a constitutional compass for legislative action.

4. Fundamental Duties: Evolution and Constitutional Significance

4.1 Origin, Text and Subsequent Additions

Fundamental Duties were absent from the original Constitution. The 42nd Amendment inserted Article 51A with ten duties, ranging from respecting the Constitution and national symbols to promoting harmony and protecting public property. The 86th Amendment added clause (k), obligating parents or guardians to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. The duties are addressed to “every citizen of India,” making them universally applicable, unlike Fundamental Rights which are available to persons, including non‑citizens in certain cases.

4.2 Judicial Enforcement and Symbolic Value

The Supreme Court has held that Fundamental Duties are not legally enforceable by writs, but they acquire legal significance when a statute incorporates them. In Chandra Bhavan Boarding and Lodging v. State of Mysore (1970), the Court observed that duties serve as a constant reminder of civic obligations. In the more recent case of Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India (2021), the Court suggested that duties can be used to interpret the scope of rights, for instance, the duty to protect the environment under Article 51A(g) informs the content of Article 21. Thus, while duties lack direct justiciability, they function as interpretive guides and as a basis for legislative sanctions.

4.3 Comparative Perspective: Duties in Other Constitutions

Several constitutions, such as those of Japan, Spain and the former Soviet Union, enumerate duties alongside rights. The Indian model is distinctive because duties were added decades after the Constitution’s commencement, reflecting a corrective response to perceived civic apathy. Unlike the Japanese Constitution, where duties are justiciable, Indian duties remain largely moral. Nevertheless, the inclusion of duties in Part IVA signals a constitutional aspiration to balance the rights‑centric discourse with a responsibility‑centric ethos.

5. Interplay, Tension and Harmonious Construction

5.1 The Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Constitutional Interpretation

The Supreme Court has articulated the doctrine of harmonious construction to resolve apparent conflicts between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. The core premise is that the Constitution is an organic whole; no part can be read in isolation to defeat another. In Kesavananda Bharati, the Court held that the amending power under Article 368 cannot be used to destroy the basic structure, which includes the balance between Parts III and IV. This principle obliges the legislature to frame laws that respect rights while pursuing directive goals, and it obliges the judiciary to interpret rights in a manner that advances directive objectives without nullifying them.

5.2 Judicial Balancing in Socio‑Economic Legislation

Cases involving land reform, labour welfare and environmental protection illustrate the balancing act. In State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977), the Court upheld a land ceiling law under Article 31C (as originally enacted) because it implemented Article 39(b) and (c), while ensuring that the law did not violate Article 14. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), the Court recognized the right to livelihood as part of Article 21, linking it to the directive under Article 41. The Court ordered procedural safeguards before evicting pavement dwellers, demonstrating that directive goals cannot be pursued at the expense of fundamental rights without due process.

5.3 Contemporary Challenges and the Way Forward

Emerging issues such as data privacy, climate justice and affirmative action in private education test the harmonious construction framework. The recognition of the right to privacy as a fundamental right in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) shows the Court’s willingness to expand Part III in response to new realities, while directives like Article 48A (environment) and Article 51A(g) (duty to protect environment) provide a constitutional basis for climate litigation. The ongoing debate over the National Education Policy and the implementation of the Right to Education Act reflects the dynamic tension between the aspirational directives and the enforceable rights. A nuanced understanding of this interplay is essential for any serious student of Indian constitutional law.

The constitutional scheme thus envisions a continuous dialogue between rights, principles and duties, each informing the other to realise the vision of a just, equitable and fraternal society.

Article 13 and the Doctrines of Eclipse, Severability and Waiver

Article 13 is the gateway that subjects all laws — pre‑constitutional and post‑constitutional — to the supremacy of Fundamental Rights. Clause (1) declares that any law in force before the commencement of the Constitution which is inconsistent with Part III shall, to the extent of such inconsistency, be void. Clause (2) prohibits the State from making any law that takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III. The Supreme Court has developed three doctrinal tools to give practical effect to these clauses.

The doctrine of eclipse applies only to pre‑constitutional statutes. In Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay (1951) the Court held that a law which conflicts with a Fundamental Right does not die; it becomes dormant — eclipsed — until the constitutional impediment is removed, for instance by an amendment that validates the law. The eclipsed law revives automatically when the inconsistency ceases, without the need for fresh legislation. This doctrine preserves legislative continuity while respecting constitutional supremacy.

The doctrine of severability governs post‑constitutional statutes. When a statute contains both constitutional and unconstitutional provisions, the Court excises the offending part if the remainder is capable of standing alone and reflects the legislative intent. The classic test was laid down in R.M.D.C. v. Union of India (1957): the valid portion must be independent, not so intertwined with the invalid part that the legislature would not have enacted it separately. If severance would alter the statute’s essential character, the whole Act falls.

The doctrine of waiver addresses whether a person can voluntarily relinquish a Fundamental Right. In Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax (1959) the Court ruled that Fundamental Rights are not merely private entitlements but embody public policy; therefore they cannot be waived by an individual. This principle reinforces the non‑derogable nature of Part III and prevents the State from extracting contractual waivers that would undermine constitutional guarantees.

Together, these doctrines ensure that Article 13 operates as a living filter: pre‑constitutional laws are kept in suspended animation, post‑constitutional laws are pruned rather than obliterated, and the rights themselves remain inalienable. Mastery of the case law — especially the distinction between eclipse (pre‑constitutional) and severability (post‑constitutional) — is essential for answering application‑type questions in the mains.

The Expanding Scope of Article 21: Privacy, Dignity and Socio‑Economic Rights

Article 21 — “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law” — has undergone the most dramatic judicial expansion in Indian constitutional history. The early A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) reading confined “procedure established by law” to any enacted procedure, however arbitrary. The turning point came in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where a seven‑judge bench held that the procedure must be “fair, just and reasonable”, importing the due‑process standards of Article 21 into the Indian context.

Subsequent decisions have read a constellation of rights into the word “life”. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) the Court recognised the right to livelihood as an integral facet of life, linking it to Directive Principle Article 41. Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993) elevated the right to education to a Fundamental Right under Article 21, later codified by the 86th Amendment (2002) as Article 21A. The right to health and medical care was affirmed in Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996), and the right to a clean environment in Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991).

The most consequential expansion is the recognition of privacy as a fundamental right in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017). A nine‑judge bench overruled the earlier M.P. Sharma and Kharak Singh judgments, holding that privacy is intrinsic to life, liberty and dignity under Articles 14, 19 and 21. This verdict has become the constitutional foundation for data‑protection jurisprudence, the striking down of Section 377 in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), and the emerging right to be forgotten.

Dignity, articulated in Maneka Gandhi and reinforced in Puttaswamy, now serves as the interpretive lens for socio‑economic claims. The Court has used dignity to justify positive obligations on the State — such as the provision of shelter (Chameli Singh v. State of U.P. 1996) and the protection of marginalized communities from manual scavenging (Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India 2014). For the aspirant, the trajectory from a narrow procedural guarantee to a substantive reservoir of rights illustrates the dynamic interplay between judicial interpretation and constitutional amendment.

Application Question

The Court would examine the law under Article 300A (property as a constitutional right) and Article 21 (right to livelihood). The doctrine of eminent domain requires a public purpose; a private industrial park may not satisfy this unless the State demonstrates a broader public benefit. The compensation formula would be tested against the “fair, just and reasonable” standard from Maneka Gandhi and the “just compensation” principle in K.T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2011). If the purpose is not public or compensation is illusory, the law would be struck down as violative of Articles 21 and 300A. The Court may also invoke the doctrine of severability to preserve any severable provisions.

Article 31C and the Constitutional Amendments: Shifting Balance Between Rights and Directives

Article 31C was introduced by the 25th Amendment (1971) to insulate laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) and (c) — the ownership and control of material resources for the common good — from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. The original text read: “Notwithstanding anything contained in Article 13, no law giving effect to the policy of the State towards securing the principles specified in clause (b) or (c) of Article 39 shall be deemed void on the ground that it is inconsistent with any of the rights conferred by Article 14, 19 or 31.”

The 42nd Amendment (1976) dramatically widened this shield. It replaced the reference to Article 39(b) and (c) with “any of the Directive Principles of State Policy”, thereby extending immunity to all directives. This move was part of a broader attempt to subordinate Fundamental Rights to the Directive Principles, reflecting the political philosophy of the Emergency period.

In Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) a five‑judge bench struck down the extended portion of Article 31C. The Court held that the amendment destroyed the basic structure by removing judicial review over a vast swathe of legislation, upsetting the harmonious balance between Parts III and IV. The judgment restored the pre‑1976 position: only laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) enjoy the protective umbrella of Article 31C.

The 44th Amendment (1978) formally repealed the 42nd Amendment’s expansion, leaving the original Article 31C intact. Consequently, today a law that seeks to implement any directive other than 39(b) and (c) must still withstand scrutiny under Articles 14, 19 and 21. The Court’s insistence on preserving the “core” of Fundamental Rights while allowing limited legislative leeway for distributive justice illustrates the constitutional commitment to a calibrated balance rather than a hierarchical supremacy.

For answer writing, remember the chronological sequence: 25th Amendment (original 31C) → 42nd Amendment (expanded 31C) → Minerva Mills (partial strike‑down) → 44th Amendment (repeal of expansion). Questions often test whether a given socio‑economic legislation (e.g., a land‑ceiling law, a nationalisation statute) can claim Article 31C protection; the answer hinges on whether the law implements Article 39(b) or (c) specifically.

Fundamental Duties in Judicial Reasoning: From Moral Exhortation to Interpretive Tool

Although Part IVA duties are non‑justiciable, the Supreme Court has progressively woven them into the fabric of constitutional adjudication. The pivotal shift occurred when the Court began treating duties as interpretive aids for Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1996) the Court invoked Article 51A(g) — the duty to protect and improve the natural environment — to expand the scope of Article 21, holding that the right to life encompasses a right to a wholesome environment.

Similarly, in State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977) the Court, while upholding a land‑ceiling law, referred to the duty to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood under Article 51A(e) to reinforce the legislative objective of equitable distribution. The 2021 decision in Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India explicitly stated that duties can be used to “interpret the content and scope of fundamental rights”, citing the environmental duty as a guide for reading Article 21.

The duty to provide opportunities for education under Article 51A(k) (inserted by the 86th Amendment, 2002) has been invoked in Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012) to justify the 25% quota for economically weaker sections in private unaided schools under the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009. The Court reasoned that the duty casts a constitutional obligation on the State and society, thereby legitimising affirmative action in education.

More recently, the duty to develop scientific temper and humanism under Article 51A(h) has been referenced in judgments concerning the regulation of pseudoscientific practices and the promotion of evidence‑based policy. While no writ lies directly for breach of a duty, the cumulative jurisprudence shows that duties function as a constitutional conscience — they shape the margin of appreciation available to the legislature and the interpretive latitude of the judiciary.

For the mains, a high‑scoring answer will distinguish three layers: (i) the textual non‑justiciability, (ii) the legislative incorporation route (e.g., statutes that penalise violation of duties), and (iii) the judicial interpretive route where duties inform the meaning of rights and directives. Cite at least two cases — M.C. Mehta (environment) and Rangnath Mishra (interpretive use) — to demonstrate the evolution.

Emerging Frontiers: Data Protection, Climate Justice and the Right to Internet Access

The constitutional canvas of Parts III, IV and IVA is being redrawn by technological and ecological challenges that the framers could not have anticipated. The right to privacy declared in Puttaswamy (2017) has become the doctrinal springboard for data‑protection jurisprudence. The Court recognised informational privacy as a facet of Article 21, mandating that any state intrusion into personal data must satisfy the triple test of legality, necessity and proportionality. This reasoning underpins the draft Personal Data Protection Bill and the subsequent Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, which seek to operationalise the constitutional mandate through a regulatory authority, consent framework, and cross‑border transfer restrictions.

Climate justice litigation leverages Article 48A (State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment) and Article 51A(g) (duty of citizens to protect the environment). In Ridhima Pandey v. Union of India (2021) the National Green Tribunal, drawing on Supreme Court precedents, directed the government to revise its Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement, treating the directives as enforceable benchmarks for policy review. The Supreme Court’s 2024 order in M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India recognised a “right against adverse effects of climate change” as part of Article 21, linking the duty to protect the environment with the fundamental right to life.

The right to internet access emerged from the Kashmir lockdown cases. In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) the Court held that freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) and the right to carry on trade under Article 19(1)(g) extend to the medium of the internet. While the Court stopped short of declaring internet access a standalone Fundamental Right, it mandated that any restriction must comply with the proportionality test under Article 19(2) and (6). Subsequent orders in Foundation for Media Professionals v. Union Territory of Jammu & Kashmir (2021) reinforced that indefinite suspension of 4G services violates the procedural safeguards of Article 21.

These frontiers illustrate a common pattern: the Court reads new realities into the open‑textured language of Article 21, uses Directive Principles as policy anchors, and invokes Fundamental Duties as moral compasses. Colourable Legislation and the Limits of Legislative Power under Article 13

Article 13(2) declares that the State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III, and any such law shall be void to the extent of the contravention. The doctrine of colourable legislation operates as a judicial tool to test whether a legislature, while acting within its formal competence, has in substance transgressed constitutional limits by enacting a law that indirectly achieves what it cannot do directly. The Supreme Court first articulated the principle in K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953), holding that a law which “in form” falls within legislative competence but “in substance” encroaches upon a prohibited field is colourable and therefore invalid. The test requires the court to examine the true nature and effect of the legislation, not merely its label.

The doctrine is distinct from the doctrine of pith and substance, which resolves conflicts between Union and State legislative entries. Colourable legislation addresses the vertical relationship between the legislature and the Constitution itself. In State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh (1952) the Court struck down a land‑reform statute that, while purporting to regulate tenancy, effectively abolished the right to property without compensation, a power the legislature lacked after the insertion of Article 31A. The Court emphasised that the legislature cannot “do indirectly what it cannot do directly.”

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the test. In R. C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) the Court held that a law which, under the guise of a regulatory measure, confiscates property without adequate compensation is colourable. The 44th Amendment (1978) removed the right to property from Part III, but the principle survives for other fundamental rights. For instance, in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) the Court examined whether the Aadhaar Act, framed as a welfare legislation, colourably infringed the right to privacy under Article 21. The majority concluded that the Act’s mandatory biometric collection, coupled with inadequate safeguards, went beyond a legitimate regulatory purpose and thus attracted the colourable legislation doctrine.

Practical application requires a two‑step inquiry: (i) identify the constitutional prohibition or limitation on legislative power; (ii) assess whether the impugned law, in its pith and substance, achieves the prohibited result. The burden of proof lies on the challenger to demonstrate that the legislation’s dominant purpose is to circumvent the constitutional bar. Courts have cautioned against mechanical invocation; mere incidental impact on a fundamental right does not render a law colourable. The doctrine therefore acts as a safeguard against legislative overreach while respecting the presumption of constitutionality.

The Doctrine of Pith and Substance in Federal Distribution of Powers and Its Impact on Fundamental Rights

The doctrine of pith and substance resolves apparent overlaps between Union and State legislative entries in the Seventh Schedule. It directs courts to ascertain the true character of a statute by examining its dominant purpose, rather than its incidental effects on another list. The principle was crystallised in State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951), where a State law prohibiting the sale of liquor was upheld despite incidental encroachment on the Union’s power over “industries” because the pith and substance of the law fell squarely within the State’s entry on “public health and sanitation.”

When a law’s pith and substance falls within a legislature’s competence, incidental trenching on fundamental rights does not automatically invalidate it. However, if the dominant purpose is to abridge a Part III right, the law fails the Article 13 test irrespective of its formal placement in the legislative list. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1965) the Court struck down a Central law on “preventive detention” that, while enacted under Entry 9 of List I (preventive detention), was in substance a measure to suppress political dissent, thereby violating Article 22. The Court held that the pith and substance of the statute was the curtailment of personal liberty, a purpose not authorised by the entry.

The interplay between pith and substance and fundamental rights became prominent in the Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) decision. The 42nd Amendment had expanded Article 31C to protect any law giving effect to any Directive Principle. The Court applied pith and substance to hold that the amendment’s dominant purpose was to immunise a wide swath of socio‑economic legislation from judicial review, thereby destroying the basic structure. The majority limited the protective shield of Article 31C to laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) only, reinforcing that the Constitution’s federal balance and rights framework cannot be altered by colourable use of legislative entries.

In recent years, the doctrine has been invoked in challenges to the Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime. The Constitution (101st Amendment) Act, 2016 created a concurrent GST Council. Petitioners argued that certain GST provisions, though enacted under the new concurrent entry, in substance encroached upon State fiscal autonomy guaranteed by Article 246. The Supreme Court, in Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022), applied pith and substance to uphold the GST framework, finding that the dominant purpose was the creation of a unified indirect tax market, a legitimate Union objective, and that any incidental impact on State powers was constitutionally permissible.

For aspirants, the key takeaway is that pith and substance is a dual‑edged tool: it protects legitimate federal legislation from being struck down on formalistic grounds, but it also serves as a lens to detect when a legislature uses its formal competence to achieve an unconstitutional end. Technique

The dominant purpose is to ensure access to healthcare for the indigent, a core public‑health objective. Incidental effects on the hospital’s commercial autonomy and property are secondary. Step 3: Apply colourable legislation test. The law does not seek to abolish the right to carry on occupation or to confiscate property; it imposes a reasonable regulatory condition linked to a legitimate public‑health goal. Judicial Activism versus Judicial Restraint in the Enforcement of Directive Principles

The tension between judicial activism and restraint shapes the enforcement of Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP). While Part IV is expressly non‑justiciable (Article 37), the Supreme Court has progressively treated DPSPs as interpretive aids, policy mandates, and, in limited circumstances, as enforceable obligations. The activist phase began with State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951), where the Court refused to enforce a communal‑reservation order, holding that DPSPs cannot override fundamental rights. The pendulum swung in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where the majority declared that DPSPs form part of the basic structure and that the State has a duty to implement them.

Activist judgments such as Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993) read the right to education into Article 21 by invoking Article 41 (right to work, education) and Article 45 (early childhood care). The Court directed the State to provide free education up to age 14, effectively converting a directive into a justiciable right. Similarly, in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (1997) the Court enforced the right to food by linking Article 47 (nutrition) with Article 21, issuing interim orders for the Public Distribution System.

Restraint‑oriented decisions caution against judicial overreach. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) the Court held that the President’s satisfaction under Article 356 is not justiciable, emphasising that political questions embedded in DPSPs belong to the executive. The Minerva Mills judgment (1980) limited Article 31C’s protective shield to Article 39(b) and (c), signalling that the Court will not allow the legislature to immunise all directive‑based laws from review. More recently, in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (2020) the Court declined to issue a mandamus for the implementation of the Uniform Civil Code (Article 44), stating that the directive addresses a matter of policy best left to Parliament.

The contemporary approach blends both strands: the Court uses DPSPs to expand the content of fundamental rights (activism) but refrains from directing specific legislative enactments (restraint). The “interpretive aid” model, articulated in M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1996) (environment), treats Article 48A and Article 51A(g) as guiding principles for statutory construction. The Court may issue continuing mandamus for environmental compliance, yet it avoids prescribing the exact legislative text.

For mains answers, distinguish three judicial postures: (i) Activist enforcement – converting directives into enforceable rights via Article 21 (Unni Krishnan, PUCL); (ii) Interpretive integration – using directives to construe statutes and fundamental rights (M. C. Mehta, Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985); (iii) Restraint – refusing to compel legislation or policy specifics (Bommai, Karnataka). Cite the doctrinal shift from “non‑justiciable” to “constitutional conscience” and note the Court’s self‑imposed limit: it will not become a “super‑legislature.”

The Role of the National Human Rights Commission and State Human Rights Commissions in Operationalising Fundamental Rights

The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 (PHRA) established the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) and State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs) as statutory bodies tasked with the promotion and protection of rights guaranteed under the Constitution and international covenants. While the NHRC lacks the power to issue binding writs, its recommendations carry significant moral and political weight, and its inquiries often trigger judicial or executive action.

The NHRC’s jurisdiction covers violations of fundamental rights by public servants, including custodial deaths, extrajudicial killings, and discrimination. Under Section 12 of the PHRA, the Commission can inquire suo motu or on a petition, summon witnesses, and recommend compensation, prosecution, or policy changes. In NHRC v. State of Gujarat (2009) the Supreme Court upheld the Commission’s recommendation for compensation to victims of the 2002 communal riots, treating the NHRC’s findings as credible evidence for the Court’s own orders.

SHRCs mirror the NHRC at the state level but face structural constraints: limited staff, dependence on state governments for funding, and restricted powers to investigate armed forces personnel (Section 19). The Justice J. S. Verma Committee (2013) recommended granting SHRCs independent investigative wings and making their recommendations binding on state governments, but the amendment remains pending.

The commissions also play a preventive role. The NHRC’s “Human Rights Education” programme, mandated under Section 12(h), has produced curricula for schools and police academies. Its annual reports highlight systemic issues — such as the overuse of preventive detention under the National Security Act — prompting legislative review. In 2021 the NHRC’s advisory on the use of facial‑recognition technology by law‑enforcement agencies led the Ministry of Home Affairs to issue guidelines aligning with the Puttaswamy privacy standards.

Judicial synergy is evident in Public Interest Litigations where the Court treats NHRC reports as prima facie evidence. In People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982) the Court relied on NHRC findings to direct compensation for bonded labourers. Conversely, the NHRC often refers cases to the Supreme Court under Article 32 when it detects a pattern of constitutional violation beyond its remedial capacity.

For aspirants, the key points are: (i) statutory basis (PHRA 1993, amended 2006); (ii) composition — Chairperson (former Supreme Court judge), four members (including a former High Court judge); (iii) powers — inquiry, recommendation, intervention in court proceedings; (iv) limitations — no suo motu power over armed forces, recommendations not binding, resource constraints; (v) impact — catalytic role in custodial‑reform jurisprudence, human‑rights education, and policy advisories. Answers that contrast the NHRC’s “soft power” with the Court’s “hard power” and cite the Gujarat riots and bonded‑labour cases demonstrate a nuanced understanding.

Constitutional Remedies: Article 32, 226 and the Expanding Scope of Public Interest Litigation

Article 32 confers the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights, described by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar as the “heart and soul” of the Constitution. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs not only for fundamental rights but also for “any other purpose,” giving them a broader supervisory jurisdiction. The interplay of these provisions has fuelled the growth of Public Interest Litigation (PIL), a uniquely Indian innovation that relaxes locus standi and procedural formalities.

The PIL trajectory began with Ratlam Municipal Council v. Vardhichand (1980), where the Supreme Court entertained a letter from a citizen as a writ petition concerning sanitation. In S. P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) the Court formally recognised “epistolary jurisdiction,” allowing any public‑spirited individual to approach the Court on behalf of a disadvantaged group. The landmark Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) extended PIL to bonded labour, directing the State to rehabilitate released labourers and awarding compensation.

Procedural innovations include: (i) relaxation of the “aggrieved person” requirement; (ii) appointment of amicus curiae and court‑appointed commissions for fact‑finding; (iii) continuing mandamus to monitor implementation (e.g., Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998) – the Hawala case, where the Court supervised CBI investigations); (iv) use of Article 142 to pass orders “necessary for doing complete justice,” such as the Supreme Court’s 2020 order directing the release of undertrial prisoners during the COVID‑19 pandemic.

High Courts have mirrored this expansion. The Delhi High Court’s 2018 judgment in Common Cause v. Union of India (right to die with dignity) used Article 226 to issue guidelines for passive euthanasia, later affirmed by the Supreme Court under Article 32. The Madras High Court’s 2021 order in S. R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu compelled the State to implement the Right to Education Act in private unaided schools, illustrating Article 226’s reach beyond fundamental rights.

Critiques focus on judicial overreach, forum shopping, and the risk of the Court becoming a “governance substitute.” The Supreme Court’s 2022 decision in R. K. Jain v. Union of India cautioned that PIL cannot be a substitute for legislative policy, and that courts must respect the separation of powers. Nonetheless, the remedial architecture under Articles 32 and 226 remains the primary engine for translating constitutional promises into enforceable outcomes.

For mains, structure answers around: (i) textual distinction — Article 32 (fundamental‑right‑only, Supreme Court) vs. Article 226 (broader, High Courts); (ii) procedural liberalisation — locus standi, epistolary jurisdiction, amicus curiae; (iii) remedial tools — writs, continuing mandamus, Article 142; (iv) landmark PILs — Ratlam, Bandhua Mukti Morcha, Vineet Narain, Common Cause; (v) judicial self‑restraint signals — R. K. Jain, S. R. Bommai. Cite at least two Supreme Court and one High Court PIL to demonstrate breadth.

  • Confusing colourable legislation with pith and substance; the former tests constitutional limits on a single legislature, the latter resolves federal entry overlaps.
  • Assuming Article 31C still shields all Directive Principles after the 42nd Amendment; Minerva Mills limited it to Article 39(b) and (c) only.
  • Treating NHRC recommendations as binding judicial orders; they are persuasive but not enforceable by writ.
  • Overstating PIL’s reach by claiming High Courts can issue writs for any policy matter under Article 226; the “any other purpose” clause still requires a legal right or duty.

The Doctrine of Proportionality in Restrictions on Fundamental Rights

The doctrine of proportionality has become the principal analytical tool for testing whether a law that curtails a fundamental right satisfies the “reasonable restriction” clauses of Articles 19(2) to 19(6) and the “procedure established by law” requirement of Article 21. The Supreme Court first articulated a structured proportionality test in Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016) and refined it in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), where a nine‑judge bench held that any limitation must meet four sequential sub‑tests: (i) a legitimate aim, (ii) a rational connection between the measure and the aim, (iii) necessity — the measure must be the least restrictive alternative, and (iv) a fair balance between the severity of the interference and the importance of the objective. The Court emphasized that the burden of justification rests on the State and that judicial review is not a mere “rubber‑stamp” but a substantive scrutiny of the legislative choice.

In practice, the proportionality analysis has reshaped the jurisprudence on speech, assembly, and privacy. For instance, in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) the Court struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act because the provision was vague and overbroad, failing the necessity and balancing prongs. Similarly, the 2020 judgment in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India applied proportionality to internet shutdowns in Jammu & Kashmir, requiring the State to demonstrate that a complete suspension was the least restrictive means to maintain public order. The doctrine also operates horizontally: in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) the Court used proportionality to invalidate Section 377, finding that the criminalisation of consensual same‑sex conduct was disproportionate to any legitimate state interest.

A critical nuance is the distinction between “strict proportionality” (the least restrictive means test) and “deferential proportionality” where the Court accords a margin of appreciation to the legislature in matters of economic policy or national security. The Supreme Court’s 2021 decision in K. S. Puttaswamy (II) v. Union of India (the Aadhaar case) illustrated this by upholding the Aadhaar Act’s mandatory enrolment for welfare benefits while striking down its compulsory linking with bank accounts and mobile numbers, thereby applying a calibrated proportionality standard. The Court’s willingness to engage in granular fact‑finding — appointing expert committees, seeking empirical data, and issuing continuing mandamus — marks a shift from the earlier “reasonableness” review under A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) to a rights‑centric, evidence‑based adjudication.

For mains answers, candidates should structure the discussion around the four‑prong test, cite at least two post‑Puttaswamy judgments, and contrast the Indian approach with the European Court of Human Rights’ “margin of appreciation” doctrine. Highlighting the Court’s use of Article 142 to fashion remedial measures that satisfy proportionality (e.g., the 2020 COVID‑19 undertrial release order) demonstrates an awareness of the doctrine’s remedial dimension.

Reasonable Classification under Article 14: Judicial Evolution and the Twin Test

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws, but it permits the State to classify persons or objects for legislative purposes provided the classification satisfies the “twin test” laid down in State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952): (i) the classification must be founded on an intelligible differentia which distinguishes those grouped together from those left out, and (ii) that differentia must have a rational relation to the object sought to be achieved by the legislation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the test is not a mere formalistic exercise; the differentia must be real and substantial, not illusory or arbitrary.

The evolution of the twin test can be traced through three doctrinal phases. In the early phase (1950s‑1970s) the Court applied a relatively deferential standard, upholding classifications in economic regulation such as the Maharashtra Agricultural Lands (Ceiling on Holdings) Act (1975) on the ground that the legislature was best placed to determine policy needs. The second phase, beginning with E. P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974), introduced the concept of “arbitrariness” as a synonym for violation of Article 14, expanding the scope beyond the twin test. The Court held that “equality is a dynamic concept” and that any State action that is arbitrary, discriminatory, or lacking in reasonableness falls foul of Article 14 even if a formal classification exists.

The third phase, crystallised in Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) and Navtej Singh Johar (2018), merged the arbitrariness principle with the twin test, requiring that the classification not only satisfy the two prongs but also survive a proportionality scrutiny when fundamental rights are implicated. In Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) the Court struck down the adultery provision (Section 497 IPC) because the classification based on gender lacked an intelligible differentia and was manifestly arbitrary. The Court also clarified that the “object” of the legislation must be legitimate under the Constitution; a classification aimed at furthering a discriminatory purpose (e.g., caste‑based exclusion) fails at the first prong itself.

Contemporary challenges involve the classification of beneficiaries in welfare schemes, the differentiation between citizens and non‑citizens in public employment, and the use of algorithmic decision‑making in governance. The 2022 judgment in K. S. Puttaswamy (III) v. Union of India (the “Aadhaar‑linkage” case) applied the twin test to the mandatory linking of Aadhaar with PAN, holding that the differentia (possession of Aadhaar) bore a rational nexus to the objective of curbing tax evasion, but the Court read down the provision to exclude those unable to obtain Aadhaar due to biometric failures, thereby embedding a proportionality‑style remedy within the Article 14 framework.

Technique: Structuring a Twin‑Test Answer
Question: “Examine the constitutional validity of a state law that provides reservation in promotions only for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, excluding Other Backward Classes. Use the Article 14 twin test.”

Yes, historical social and educational backwardness of SC/ST recognized in Constitution (Art. 341, 342). (3) Apply second prong: Does the differentia have rational nexus to the object? Fundamental Rights and International Human Rights Obligations: The Role of Treaty Interpretation

The Indian Constitution does not automatically incorporate international treaties into domestic law; Article 253 empowers Parliament to legislate for implementing treaties, and Article 51(c) directs the State to foster respect for international law. Nevertheless, the Supreme Court has developed a robust interpretive practice whereby ratified human rights treaties — particularly the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) — are used as interpretive aids for fundamental rights. In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) the Court relied on the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) to formulate guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace, stating that “international conventions and norms are to be read into the fundamental rights” in the absence of domestic legislation.

The interpretive methodology follows three principles articulated in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (1997) and reaffirmed in K. S. Puttaswamy (2017): (i) where domestic law is ambiguous, courts may look to international obligations to resolve the ambiguity; (ii) a treaty ratified by India creates a legitimate expectation that the State will act consistently with its provisions; (iii) however, a treaty cannot override an express constitutional provision or a clear statutory enactment. This approach was evident in National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India (2014), where the Court invoked the Yogyakarta Principles (soft law) and the ICCPR to recognise transgender persons’ right to self‑identified gender under Articles 14, 19 and 21.

A notable tension arises with the ICESCR, which contains socio‑economic rights that are largely mirrored in the Directive Principles (Part IV). The Court has treated the ICESCR as a “guiding star” for interpreting the Directive Principles and, by extension, for expanding the scope of Article 21. In Chameli Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1996) the Court held that the right to life includes the right to livelihood, drawing on Article 11 of the ICESCR (adequate standard of living). More recently, the 2021 judgment in M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (the “right to clean air” case) cited the Paris Agreement and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change to impose positive obligations on the State under Article 21, illustrating the growing influence of environmental treaties.

For mains, candidates should distinguish between “incorporation” (requiring legislation) and “interpretive use” (judicial tool). A high‑scoring answer will cite at least two cases where the Court used a specific treaty provision to fill a legislative vacuum, and will note the limitation expressed in Gramophone Company of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984) that treaties cannot be directly enforced without enabling legislation. Mentioning the 2023 Supreme Court reference in Re: Article 143 — Implementation of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities demonstrates awareness of contemporary developments.

The 44th Amendment, Article 300A and the Transformation of Property Rights

The 44th Constitutional Amendment (1978) abolished the fundamental right to property under Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31, relocating property protection to a new constitutional provision — Article 300A — which states: “No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law.” This shift transformed property from a justiciable fundamental right (enforceable under Article 32) to a constitutional legal right enforceable only under Article 226 (High Court writ jurisdiction) and ordinary civil suits. The amendment was a response to the judicial activism of the 1970s, particularly Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) where the Court held that the right to property was not part of the basic structure, and Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) which confirmed that Article 31C’s protection of laws giving effect to Directive Principles did not extend to property acquisitions after the 44th Amendment.

Post‑amendment jurisprudence has revolved around the phrase “authority of law”. In Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar v. State of Gujarat (1995) the Supreme Court held that “authority of law” means a validly enacted statute, not executive fiat, and that the law must satisfy the requirements of Article 14 (non‑arbitrariness) and Article 21 (procedure established by law). The Court further ruled in State of Haryana v. Mukesh Kumar (2011) that the adequacy of compensation is not a constitutional requirement under Article 300A, but the law must provide a mechanism for determination of compensation that is not illusory. This position was reiterated in K. T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2019), where the Court upheld a land acquisition law that provided for market‑value compensation but struck down a provision allowing the government to unilaterally reduce the award without judicial review.

The 44th Amendment also inserted Article 31A, 31B and 31C (the “land reform” provisions) into the Ninth Schedule, shielding certain agrarian reform laws from judicial review. However, the Supreme Court in I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 (the date of Kesavananda) are subject to basic‑structure review, thereby limiting the immunity of property‑related statutes. The interplay between Article 300A and the Ninth Schedule creates a layered protection regime: a law acquiring property must be “authority of law” under Article 300A, and if it is placed in the Ninth Schedule post‑1973, it must also survive basic‑structure scrutiny.

Contemporary relevance lies in the 2013 Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act (LARR Act), which replaced the 1894 Land Acquisition Act. The LARR Act’s provisions on social impact assessment, consent thresholds, and enhanced compensation were challenged under Article 300A. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (2020) the Supreme Court upheld the Act, emphasizing that “authority of law” includes procedural fairness and that the legislature may prescribe higher compensation standards than the constitutional minimum. The Court also noted that the LARR Act’s rehabilitation provisions give effect to Directive Principles (Articles 39(b), 39(c)), illustrating the harmonious construction between Article 300A and Part IV.

Non‑Derogable Rights and Emergency Provisions: Articles 352, 356 and 360

The Constitution permits three types of emergency — national emergency (Article 352), President’s Rule in states (Article 356), and financial emergency (Article 360). During a national emergency proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion, Article 358 automatically suspends the freedoms under Article 19, while Article 359 empowers the President to suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of other fundamental rights (except Articles 20 and 21). The 44th Amendment (1978) introduced crucial safeguards: (i) the proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority; (ii) the suspension under Article 359 cannot extend to Articles 20 and 21; (iii) the emergency must be reviewed every six months.

The Supreme Court’s landmark decision in A. D. M. Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976) — the “Habeas Corpus case” — held that during the 1975‑77 Emergency, the right to life under Article 21 could be suspended, and no judicial remedy was available. This judgment was effectively overruled by the 44th Amendment and later by K. S. Puttaswamy (2017), where the Court declared that the right to life and personal liberty is non‑derogable and forms part of the basic structure. Consequently, even during a validly proclaimed emergency, the State cannot deprive a person of life or liberty except according to a procedure that is fair, just and reasonable — a procedural due‑process standard imported from Article 21 jurisprudence.

Article 356 (President’s Rule) does not suspend fundamental rights, but it displaces the elected state government and vests executive and legislative powers in the President. The Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down strict judicial review standards: the proclamation must be based on objective material, not on the subjective satisfaction of the Governor; the majority test must be conducted on the floor of the Assembly; and the imposition of President’s Rule is subject to judicial scrutiny for mala fides or irrelevance. The Court also held that the dissolution of the Assembly cannot precede parliamentary approval, reinforcing federalism as a basic‑structure element.

Financial emergency under Article 360 has never been proclaimed. Its potential impact on fundamental rights is indirect: it empowers the Union to direct states to observe canons of financial propriety and to reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of the Union or the states. Scholars argue that a financial emergency could impinge on the right to livelihood (Article 21) and the right to equality (Article 14) if salary cuts are discriminatory. The 44th Amendment did not add specific non‑derogability clauses for Article 360, leaving the scope of judicial review largely untested.

For mains, a high‑quality answer will contrast the pre‑ and post‑44th Amendment emergency regime, cite the Jabalpur and Puttaswamy judgments to illustrate the evolution of non‑derogable rights, and explain the Bommai safeguards for Article 356. Including the parliamentary approval mechanism (special majority, six‑month review) and the distinction between suspension of Article 19 (automatic) versus other rights (presidential order) demonstrates precise constitutional knowledge.

Reasonable Restrictions on Freedom of Speech and Expression: Article 19(2) Jurisprudence

Article 19(1)(a) guarantees the right to freedom of speech and expression, but Article 19(2) permits the State to impose reasonable restrictions on eight enumerated grounds: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, and incitement to an offence. The Supreme Court has consistently held that any restriction must satisfy a two‑fold test: it must be authorised by law and it must be reasonable in the sense of being proportionate to the objective sought. In Romesh Thapar v. State of Madras (1950) the Court struck down a ban on the entry of a journal, emphasising that “public order” cannot be stretched to suppress mere criticism of government. Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi (1950) established that pre‑censorship is a restriction on the freedom itself and must be justified under Article 19(2). The Sakal Papers v. Union of India (1962) decision invalidated a newsprint control order that indirectly restricted circulation, reinforcing that indirect restrictions are equally subject to judicial scrutiny.

Later jurisprudence refined the proportionality analysis. Bennett Coleman v. Union of India (1973) held that a newsprint policy that limited the number of pages a newspaper could publish was an unreasonable restriction because it lacked a rational nexus with the stated objective of preventing monopoly. Indian Express v. Union of India (1985) extended protection to commercial speech, ruling that advertisement revenue is integral to the freedom of the press. The landmark Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, on the ground that the phrase “grossly offensive” was vague and overbroad, failing the test of reasonable restriction. The Court articulated a “clear and present danger” standard for restrictions on grounds of public order and incitement. In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) the Court laid down that internet shutdowns must be temporary, proportionate, and subject to periodic review, applying the proportionality doctrine to digital speech. The cumulative effect of these judgments is a robust framework where the State bears the burden of demonstrating that a restriction is narrowly tailored, employs the least restrictive means, and does not disproportionately impair the core of the right.

The Essential Religious Practices Test: Articles 25–28 and Judicial Determination

Articles 25 to 28 guarantee freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health. The Supreme Court developed the “essential religious practices” (ERP) test to distinguish protected religious conduct from secular or superstitious activities. In Shirur Mutt v. Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments (1954) the Court held that what constitutes an essential part of a religion must be ascertained with reference to the doctrines of that religion itself. Ratilal Panachand Gandhi v. State of Bombay (1954) applied the test to uphold a regulation of temple administration, ruling that management of property is not an essential religious practice. Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali (1961) clarified that practices rooted in superstition or social custom, even if ancient, do not qualify as essential.

The test has been both praised for protecting minority faiths and criticised for judicial overreach into theological terrain. In Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore (1958) the Court upheld the exclusion of non‑Brahmins from certain temple rituals, deeming them essential. Conversely, Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India (1994) held that offering prayers in a mosque is not an essential practice unless the mosque has a particular religious significance, a ruling that sparked debate. The Sabarimala Temple entry case (2018) saw a 4:1 majority hold that the exclusion of women of menstruating age was not an essential practice, emphasising constitutional morality over claimed tradition. In Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) the Court declared instant triple talaq unconstitutional, finding it not essential to Islam. The ERP test thus remains a pivotal, albeit contested, tool for balancing religious autonomy with the transformative goals of the Constitution.

Technique: Applying the ERP Test to a Hypothetical Ban on Animal Sacrifice
A state enacts a law prohibiting animal sacrifice in all religious festivals, citing animal welfare. A community challenges the law, claiming the sacrifice is an essential practice of their faith. How should the Court apply the ERP test?
The Court would first examine the community’s scriptures and traditions to determine whether animal sacrifice is regarded as indispensable to their religious doctrine. If the practice is shown to be a core tenet — mandated by sacred texts and consistently observed — it qualifies as essential. The State must then demonstrate that the prohibition is a reasonable restriction under Article 25(1) on grounds of public order, morality or health, and that it is proportionate: the law must be the least restrictive means to achieve the welfare objective. If a blanket ban is imposed without exemptions for essential practices, the Court is likely to strike it down as disproportionate, following the reasoning in Shirur Mutt and Sabarimala. A narrowly tailored regulation — for instance, requiring humane methods or limiting the number of animals — would have a better chance of surviving scrutiny.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks you to evaluate a restriction on religious practice, structure the answer in three steps: (1) identify the claimed practice and its textual basis; (2) apply the ERP test — is it essential according to the religion’s own tenets?; (3) assess the State’s justification under Article 25(1) using the proportionality framework (legitimate aim, rational connection, necessity, balancing). Cite Shirur Mutt, Sabarimala and Shayara Bano to show doctrinal depth.

Minority Rights and State Regulation: Articles 29 and 30 in Supreme Court Rulings

Article 29(1) protects the right of any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture to conserve the same, while Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to State‑aided educational institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste or language. Article 30(1) guarantees minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, and Article 30(2) bars discrimination in granting aid. The Supreme Court has interpreted these provisions to balance minority autonomy with State regulatory power. In St. Stephen’s College v. University of Delhi (1992) the Court upheld a minority college’s right to reserve seats for its community, subject to a reasonable percentage, affirming that administration includes admission policies. TMA Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002) laid down that the right to administer includes the power to appoint staff, set fees and design curricula, but the State may impose reasonable regulations to maintain academic standards.

Subsequent decisions refined the scope. P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005) held that unaided minority institutions cannot be compelled to follow State reservation policies, reinforcing autonomy. Islamic Academy v. State of Karnataka (2003) permitted the State to regulate fee structures in professional colleges to prevent profiteering, provided the regulation does not erode the minority character. The 93rd Constitutional Amendment (2005) inserted Article 15(5), enabling the State to make special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes in private unaided institutions, including minority ones, a move upheld in Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India (2008). The 103rd Amendment (2019) introduced a 10% economically weaker sections (EWS) quota, which the Court in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) held applicable to minority institutions, subject to the ceiling of 50% and the preservation of minority character. These rulings illustrate a dynamic equilibrium: the State may regulate to ensure fairness and quality, but cannot annihilate the minority’s right to administer.

Horizontal Application of Fundamental Rights: The State Action Debate and Private Actors

Article 12 defines “State” for the purpose of Part III, traditionally limiting fundamental rights to vertical enforcement against governmental authorities. However, the Supreme Court has progressively recognised a horizontal dimension, whereby certain fundamental rights bind private entities performing public functions or enjoying State‑conferred monopolies. In Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995) the Court held that medical services rendered by private hospitals fall within the purview of Article 21 (right to life) because health care is a public function. Zoroastrian Cooperative Housing Society v. Registrar, Co‑operative Societies (2005) extended the non‑discrimination principle of Article 15 to a private housing society that received State land at concessional rates, treating it as an instrumentality of the State.

The “public function” test was further elaborated in Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), where the Court laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace, addressing private employers by invoking the State’s obligation under Articles 14, 19(1)(g) and 21. In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) the Court read down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, emphasising that constitutional morality requires protection of dignity in private spheres. Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) struck down the adultery provision, holding that the State cannot enforce a law that treats women as property, thereby extending equality guarantees into marital relations. Comparative jurisprudence from Canada (Charter s.32) and South Africa (Bill of Rights s.8) informs the Indian approach: where a private body exercises governmental power or provides essential services, fundamental rights may apply horizontally. The doctrine remains evolving; the Court cautions against indiscriminate horizontalisation, insisting on a nexus with State action or public function.

Transformative Constitutionalism and Constitutional Morality in Rights Adjudication

Transformative constitutionalism, a concept articulated by Karl Klare and embraced by the Indian Supreme Court, envisions the Constitution as a charter for social and economic transformation, not merely a restraint on State power. Constitutional morality, a phrase used by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly, denotes adherence to the Constitution’s core values — justice, liberty, equality, fraternity — even when they conflict with majoritarian sentiment. In Navtej Singh Johar (2018) the Court invoked constitutional morality to decriminalise consensual same‑sex relations, stating that “the morality of the Constitution is the morality of the people”. Sabarimala (2018) relied on the same principle to strike down the exclusion of women, holding that religious practices cannot override constitutional guarantees of dignity and equality. Joseph Shine (2018) and K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) further entrenched constitutional morality as an interpretive lens for Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21.

This approach has reshaped the interplay between fundamental rights and directive principles. The Court in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) had already declared that the harmony between Parts III and IV is a basic structure feature. Transformative constitutionalism pushes that harmony towards a proactive reading: directive principles such as Articles 39(b), 39(c), 41, 43 and 48A are treated as enforceable obligations through the prism of fundamental rights. For instance, the right to a clean environment under Article 21 has been linked to Article 48A, compelling the State to adopt sustainable policies. The doctrine also informs judicial review of legislative amendments that seek to curtail rights; any amendment that undermines the transformative vision is vulnerable to basic‑structure challenge. Aspirants should be prepared to articulate how constitutional morality operates as a bridge between textual guarantees and the aspirational goals of the Directive Principles.

  • Confusing the “reasonable restriction” test under Article 19(2) with the “proportionality” test under Article 21; they are distinct though overlapping.
  • Assuming the Essential Religious Practices test freezes religious doctrine; the Court evaluates essentiality based on the religion’s own texts at the time of adjudication.
  • Treating minority institution autonomy as absolute; the State may impose reasonable regulations for academic standards, fee control and reservation within constitutional limits.
  • Believing horizontal application extends to all private conduct; it is limited to entities performing public functions or receiving substantial State benefits.
  • Equating constitutional morality with popular morality; the former is derived from the Constitution’s text and structure, not majoritarian views.
  • Overlooking the 93rd and 103rd Amendments’ impact on minority rights; they permit quotas in unaided minority institutions subject to the 50% ceiling and minority character preservation.
  • Neglecting the procedural safeguards in internet shutdown orders laid down in Anuradha Bhasin; any shutdown must be temporary, proportionate and subject to review.

The advanced sections above equip aspirants with the doctrinal precision and case‑law depth required for high‑scoring answers on the interplay of fundamental rights, directive principles and fundamental duties.

The Doctrine of Legitimate Expectation and Its Constitutional Anchoring in Article 14

The doctrine of legitimate expectation, borrowed from English administrative law, has been grafted onto the Indian constitutional scheme through the equality guarantee of Article 14. It protects a person’s reasonable expectation of being treated in a particular way by a public authority when that authority has, by representation or consistent practice, created such an expectation. The Supreme Court first recognised the doctrine in State of Punjab v. Ramdev Singh (1995) 3 SCC 641, holding that a public authority cannot act arbitrarily to defeat a legitimate expectation without affording a hearing. The Court linked the doctrine to the principle of non‑arbitrariness inherent in Article 14, stating that “fair play in action” demands that a promise or established practice be honoured unless a compelling public interest justifies its reversal.

Elements of the Doctrine

  • Representation or Practice: An explicit promise, policy statement, or a consistent course of conduct by the authority.
  • Reasonableness: The expectation must be reasonable; mere hope or wish does not suffice.
  • Reliance: The claimant must have acted on the expectation to his detriment or altered his position.
  • Procedural Fairness: Before withdrawing the benefit, the authority must give notice and an opportunity to be heard.

Judicial Development

In Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) the Court held that a government servant’s expectation of promotion based on a departmental circular could not be defeated arbitrarily. Later, in Food Corporation of India v. M/s. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries (1993) the Court extended the doctrine to commercial contracts, requiring a fair hearing before termination of a long‑standing supply arrangement. The most expansive formulation appears in Navjot Singh v. State of Punjab (2005) where the Court held that even a policy change affecting a class of beneficiaries must satisfy the “Wednesbury unreasonableness” test, reinforcing the link between legitimate expectation and the non‑arbitrariness strand of Article 14.

Limitations

The doctrine does not create a substantive right; it is a procedural safeguard. It cannot be invoked against a clear statutory provision that expressly empowers the authority to alter the scheme. In State of U.P. v. Rajesh Gautam (2007) the Court refused to enforce an expectation that conflicted with a statutory reservation policy, emphasising that legitimate expectation yields to legislative mandate. Moreover, the expectation must not be contrary to public policy or the Constitution itself.

The Right to Health as a Facet of Article 21: Judicial Evolution and Policy Consequences

The Supreme Court has progressively read the right to health into the right to life under Article 21, transforming a directive principle (Article 47) into an enforceable constitutional guarantee. The trajectory began with Vincent Panikurlangara v. Union of India (1987) where the Court held that the State’s obligation to provide medical aid to a person in custody flows from Article 21. In Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996) the Court declared that “the right to life includes the right to live with human dignity and all that goes along with it, namely, the bare necessaries of life such as adequate nutrition, clothing and shelter and facilities for reading, writing and expressing oneself in diverse forms, freely moving about and mixing and commingling with fellow human beings.” This pronouncement anchored health care as a core component of dignity.

Key Milestones

  • Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (1995) – recognised the right to emergency medical treatment as a fundamental right.
  • State of Punjab v. Mohinder Singh Chawla (1997) – held that the State must provide adequate medical facilities in prisons.
  • National Human Rights Commission v. State of Gujarat (2009) – directed the State to ensure free treatment for victims of communal violence.
  • K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) – the nine‑judge bench affirmed that privacy, bodily integrity and health are inseparable facets of Article 21.

Policy Impact

These judgments have compelled legislative action: the Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010; the National Health Mission (2013); and the Ayushman Bharat scheme (2018). Courts have also issued structural directions — for instance, the Supreme Court’s 2021 order in In Re: Distribution of Essential Supplies and Services During Pandemic mandated real‑time hospital bed tracking and oxygen allocation, illustrating how judicial enforcement translates into executive accountability.

Outstanding Issues

Despite the jurisprudential clarity, the right remains largely aspirational for marginalised groups. The Court has refrained from prescribing a specific health‑care financing model, leaving the “progressive realisation” standard of Article 47 to guide policy. The tension between judicial activism and fiscal federalism surfaces when states argue that central directives encroach on their exclusive public health powers under the State List.

Worked Answer Technique
Question: “Explain how the Supreme Court has transformed the directive principle of public health (Article 47) into an enforceable fundamental right under Article 21. Illustrate with two landmark judgments.”
Answer: The Court uses the expansive interpretation of “life” in Article 21 to import the State’s duty to protect health. In Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996) the Court held that denial of timely medical treatment in a government hospital violates Article 21, converting the directive into a justiciable right. In Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (1995) the Court ruled that the right to emergency medical care is a fundamental right, obligating the State to provide immediate treatment irrespective of the patient’s ability to pay. Both judgments rely on the dignity component of Article 21 and the non‑derogable nature of the right to life, thereby elevating Article 47 from a moral exhortation to a constitutional mandate enforceable through writ jurisdiction.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “judicial bridge” between a DPSP and a fundamental right, name the specific Article 21 expansion (e.g., health, environment, education) and cite the earliest and the most recent authoritative judgments. Use a two‑column table in the answer sheet: Column 1 – DPSP article; Column 2 – Article 21 case law. This visual cue scores high on structure marks.

The Concept of ‘State’ under Article 12: The ‘Instrumentality’ and ‘Public Function’ Tests After 2000

Article 12 defines “the State” for the purpose of Part III, extending fundamental‑rights obligations beyond the legislature and executive to any authority that qualifies as “other authorities”. The Supreme Court’s early test in R.D. Shetty v. Airport Authority of India (1979) laid down five indicia — financial, functional, administrative, governmental control and whether the body performs a public function. Post‑2000 jurisprudence has sharpened the “instrumentality” and “public function” prongs, especially in the context of privatisation and regulatory bodies.

Instrumentality Test

In Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002) the Court held that a society registered under the Societies Registration Act, substantially financed and controlled by the Government, is an “instrumentality of the State”. The decisive factors were: (i) 100 % government funding; (ii) government nominees on the governing body; (iii) the body’s objectives aligned with governmental policy. The Court clarified that mere registration under a statute does not confer State status; the degree of control is paramount.

Public Function Test

Zee Telefilms v. Union of India (2005) examined the Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI). The Court held that BCCI, despite performing functions of a public nature (organising national cricket, selecting teams), was not a “State” because it lacked statutory backing and governmental control. Conversely, in BCCI v. Cricket Association of Bihar (2015) the Court recognised that when a private body exercises powers delegated by statute — such as the National Anti‑Doping Agency — it becomes amenable to writ jurisdiction. The “public function” test therefore requires a statutory source of power or a function that is “essentially governmental”.

Regulatory Bodies and Tribunals

Regulators like the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) and the Competition Commission of India (CCI) have been held to be “State” under Article 12 because they are created by statute, exercise rule‑making and adjudicatory powers, and are accountable to Parliament. In Madhya Pradesh Vidhyut Mandal v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2019) the Court extended the definition to electricity distribution companies that operate under a licence granted by the State Electricity Regulatory Commission, emphasising the “public utility” character.

Practical Implications

The expanding definition means that private entities performing sovereign functions — such as private universities receiving substantial state grants, or private hospitals empanelled under government health schemes — can be subjected to fundamental‑rights scrutiny. Aspirants should be ready to apply the twin tests to novel fact patterns, especially in the context of public‑private partnerships and the growing role of non‑state actors in service delivery.

The Public Trust Doctrine, Article 48A and Judicial Enforcement of Environmental Directive Principles

The public trust doctrine, rooted in Roman law and revived in American jurisprudence, has been domesticated by the Indian Supreme Court as a constitutional principle that obligates the State to protect natural resources for present and future generations. The doctrine operates as a bridge between the non‑justiciable Directive Principle in Article 48A (“The State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment”) and the justiciable right to life under Article 21.

Foundational Cases

  • M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1997) – The Court held that the State, as trustee of natural resources, cannot lease forest land for commercial exploitation without ensuring ecological sustainability. The judgment invoked Article 48A and Article 21 to strike down the lease.
  • Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) – Recognised the “precautionary principle” and “polluter pays principle” as part of the public trust doctrine, linking them to Article 48A.
  • Intellectuals Forum v. State of A.P. (2006) – Extended the doctrine to groundwater, declaring that the State holds aquifers in trust and must regulate extraction.
  • T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (2008) – The Court ordered the creation of a “National Forest Policy” and mandated that any diversion of forest land require prior approval of the Central Government, reinforcing the trustee role.

Doctrinal Mechanics

The Court treats the public trust doctrine as a substantive limitation on legislative and executive action. When a law or policy threatens a resource held in trust — rivers, forests, wetlands, air — the burden shifts to the State to justify the interference as “necessary for public interest” and “proportionate”. This mirrors the proportionality analysis under Article 21 but is anchored in the directive principle, giving the Court a dual constitutional foothold.

Legislative Response

Parliament enacted the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 (amended 2003); and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, partly in response to judicial pronouncements. The National Green Tribunal (NGT), established in 2010, operationalises the trust doctrine by providing a specialised forum for environmental adjudication, with powers to enforce the “polluter pays” principle and order restitution.

Emerging Frontiers

Recent litigation has invoked the doctrine for climate justice: Ridhima Pandey v. Union of India (2021) argued that the State’s failure to meet its Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement breaches the public trust. The Court has not yet issued a final order, but the petition signals a shift from resource‑specific trusts to a planetary trust encompassing atmospheric commons.

  • Confusing the “instrumentality” test with the “public function” test — they are distinct; the former looks at governmental control, the latter at statutory delegation of sovereign functions.
  • Assuming every private body receiving state funds becomes a “State” under Article 12 — the quantum and nature of control matter, not mere funding.
  • Treating the public trust doctrine as a standalone fundamental right — it is a principle that informs the interpretation of Article 21 and Article 48A, not an independent justiciable right.
  • Overlooking the requirement of “proportionality” when the State invokes public interest to divert trust resources — the Court demands a rigorous balancing test.
  • Neglecting the procedural dimension of legitimate expectation — the doctrine does not create a substantive entitlement; it only mandates fair procedure before withdrawal.
  • Equating the right to health with a right to free universal healthcare — the Court has recognised the right to emergency and essential care, not an unlimited entitlement to all medical services.
  • Misreading Article 31C as a blanket shield for all DPSP‑implementing laws — the protection applies only to laws giving effect to Articles 39(b) and 39(c) and is subject to the basic‑structure test.

The Doctrine of Reading Down and Reading Up in Fundamental Rights Adjudication

The Supreme Court has developed two complementary interpretive techniques — reading down and reading up — to preserve the constitutionality of legislation while giving full effect to fundamental rights. Reading down involves narrowing the operative scope of a statutory provision so that it falls within the permissible limits of a fundamental right, thereby avoiding a declaration of invalidity. The classic illustration is R. v. Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex parte Simms (1999) applied in Indian context through State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat (2005), where the Court read down a cattle‑preservation law to exclude a total ban on slaughter of bulls and bullocks, holding that a complete prohibition would violate Article 19(1)(g). Reading up, by contrast, expands the protective ambit of a right by importing content from directive principles or international norms. In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) the Court read up Article 21 to include a right to a safe workplace, drawing on the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) and the directive principle in Article 39(d). The technique is not a licence for judicial legislation; the Court insists that the statutory language must be capable of bearing the narrowed or expanded meaning without doing violence to the legislative intent. In K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) the majority employed reading down to uphold the Aadhaar Act’s core architecture while striking down provisions that enabled mass surveillance, demonstrating that the doctrine operates as a surgical instrument rather than a blunt axe. Aspirants should note the procedural prerequisite: a challenge must be based on a specific constitutional provision, and the Court will not read down a law that is “plainly and unambiguously” unconstitutional. The doctrine also interacts with the principle of severability — if a provision cannot be read down without rewriting it, the Court may sever the offending portion under Article 13(1).

The Advisory Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 143 and Its Role in Clarifying Rights–Directive Tensions

Article 143 empowers the President to refer questions of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion. Although the opinion is not binding, it has shaped the contours of fundamental rights and directive principles on several occasions. The first major reference, In re: The Kerala Education Bill (1958), addressed the conflict between the right of minorities to establish educational institutions under Article 30 and the state’s power to regulate education under Article 45. The Court held that the directive principle could not override the fundamental right, establishing an early precedent for harmonious construction. In In re: Special Courts Bill (1979), the Court examined whether the creation of special courts for trial of offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act violated Article 14 and Article 21. The advisory opinion affirmed that legislative classification must satisfy the twin test of reasonable classification, reinforcing the doctrine of equality in the context of socio‑economic legislation. A more recent reference, In re: Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1992), though primarily inter‑state, touched upon the right to water as an aspect of Article 21 and the directive principle in Article 39(b). The Court’s opinion clarified that the state’s duty to ensure equitable distribution of natural resources is enforceable through judicial review when it impinges on fundamental rights. The advisory jurisdiction thus serves as a constitutional safety valve, allowing the executive to obtain authoritative guidance before enacting laws that may tread on the rights–directive interface. It also provides the Court an opportunity to articulate principled reasoning without the adversarial constraints of litigation, influencing subsequent judicial decisions. However, the non‑binding nature means that the government may choose to disregard the opinion, as seen in the aftermath of the In re: Presidential Reference on the Constitution (Ninety‑ninth Amendment) Act (2015) concerning the National Judicial Appointments Commission, where the Court’s advisory view on the basic structure was later confirmed in the binding judgment striking down the amendment.

Worked Example: Advisory Opinion vs Binding Precedent
A question asks: “The President refers a question under Article 143 on whether a proposed law implementing Article 48A (environment protection) would violate Article 19(1)(g). The Supreme Court advises that the law is constitutionally permissible. Later, a citizen challenges the enacted law under Article 32. Can the citizen rely on the advisory opinion as binding precedent?”
No. An advisory opinion under Article 143 is not a judgment under Article 141 and therefore does not constitute binding precedent. The citizen may cite the advisory opinion for its persuasive reasoning, but the Court in the subsequent writ petition is free to reach a different conclusion based on the full adversarial record. The binding authority remains the ratio decidendi of the later judgment, not the advisory opinion.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mentions Article 143, immediately flag the distinction between advisory and binding authority. Examiners often test whether you confuse the two. Remember: advisory opinions guide but do not bind; only judgments under Article 141 create stare decisis.

The Impact of the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments on the Realisation of Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles at the Grassroots

The 73rd (1992) and 74th (1992) Amendments inserted Parts IX and IXA, mandating a three‑tier system of Panchayats and Municipalities with regular elections, reservation for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women, and devolution of powers listed in the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules. These amendments operationalise several directive principles — notably Article 40 (organisation of village panchayats), Article 39(b) and (c) (distribution of resources), and Article 47 (public health) — by creating constitutionally protected local self‑government institutions. The Supreme Court in K. Krishnamurthy v. Union of India (2010) held that the amendments impose a mandatory duty on states to devolve functions, finances and functionaries, and that failure to do so is justiciable under Article 226. The Court linked the devolution mandate to the fundamental right to participate in governance implicit in Article 19(1)(a) and the right to equality under Article 14, reasoning that effective local democracy is a prerequisite for the meaningful exercise of these rights. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2015), the Court struck down a state law that postponed panchayat elections beyond the five‑year term, affirming that the constitutional guarantee of regular elections is a facet of the basic structure. The amendments also enable the enforcement of socio‑economic rights at the grassroots: the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005, relies on Gram Panchayats for implementation, turning the directive principle of the right to work (Article 41) into a legally enforceable entitlement. However, the practical realisation remains uneven. The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015) noted that only 30% of the functions listed in the Eleventh Schedule had been effectively transferred. Aspirants should be prepared to discuss the tension between the constitutional mandate and the political economy of state‑level resistance, and to cite the judicial doctrine that treats the amendments as a “constitutional guarantee of local self‑government” rather than a mere policy directive.

The Constitutional Recognition of the Right to Education under Article 21A and Its Interplay with Article 45

The 86th Amendment (2002) inserted Article 21A, making free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen a fundamental right. Simultaneously, Article 45 was amended to direct the state to provide early childhood care and education for children until the age of six. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act (RTE), 2009, gave statutory effect to Article 21A, prescribing norms for pupil‑teacher ratios, infrastructure, and a 25% reservation in private unaided schools for economically weaker sections. In Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012), the Supreme Court upheld the 25% quota, holding that the state’s obligation under Article 21A extends to ensuring access in private schools, and that the directive principle in Article 45 reinforces the state’s duty to provide pre‑primary education. The Court applied the doctrine of harmonious construction, reading Article 21A and Article 45 together to impose a continuum of state responsibility from early childhood to elementary education. In Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India (2014), the Court clarified that minority institutions under Article 30(1) are exempt from the RTE’s quota requirement, illustrating the interplay between fundamental rights (Article 30) and the newly created right (Article 21A). The judgment also affirmed that the state’s duty under Article 21A is not merely aspirational; failure to provide neighbourhood schools or to maintain prescribed standards is actionable under Article 32. The Comptroller and Auditor General’s 2017 report highlighted that 17% of habitations still lacked a primary school within one kilometre, underscoring the implementation gap. For examination purposes, the key analytical points are: (i) the transformation of a directive principle into a justiciable right; (ii) the use of Article 45 as an interpretive aid to expand the scope of Article 21A; (iii) the balancing of minority rights against the state’s educational obligation; and (iv) the role of the RTE Act as a legislative bridge converting constitutional mandate into enforceable entitlements.

The Doctrine of Constitutional Silence and the Judicial Creation of Unenumerated Rights

The Indian Constitution does not contain an exhaustive bill of rights; several crucial liberties have been recognised by the Supreme Court as implicit in the text, a process often described as “reading into” the Constitution. The doctrine of constitutional silence acknowledges that the framers deliberately left certain spaces open for judicial development. The seminal case is K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), where a nine‑judge bench declared the right to privacy a fundamental right under Article 21, despite its absence from the enumerated list. The Court traced the lineage to Kharak Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1962) and Gobind v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1975), showing a gradual judicial accretion. Similarly, the right to health was recognised in Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996) as an integral facet of Article 21, drawing on the directive principle in Article 47. The right to shelter emerged in Chameli Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1996), and the right to livelihood in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985). The Court employs a three‑step test: (1) the claimed right must be “essential to the dignity of the individual”; (2) it must have a “constitutional pedigree” traceable to the Preamble, the fundamental rights chapter, or the directive principles; and (3) its recognition must not upset the separation of powers by encroaching on legislative policy. In Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2018) (the Aadhaar judgment), the majority cautioned that the doctrine cannot be used to create positive obligations that require massive fiscal outlays without legislative sanction. The doctrine also interacts with the principle of transformative constitutionalism — the Constitution is viewed as a charter for social transformation, and judicial recognition of unenumerated rights is a tool to fulfil that promise. Aspirants should distinguish between “unenumerated rights” (derived from constitutional silence) and “non‑justiciable directive principles”; the former are enforceable, the latter are not, though they may inform the content of the former.

  • Confusing reading down with reading up — reading down narrows a statute to save it; reading up expands a right by importing external norms.
  • Treating an advisory opinion under Article 143 as binding precedent — it is only persuasive.
  • Assuming the 73rd/74th Amendments automatically devolve all Eleventh/Twelfth Schedule functions — devolution requires state legislation and fiscal transfer.
  • Believing Article 21A covers early childhood education — that remains a directive principle under Article 45.
  • Equating constitutional silence with judicial legislation — the Court’s three‑step test limits recognition to rights essential to dignity and traceable to constitutional text.
  • Overlooking the minority‑institution exemption in the RTE Act — Article 30(1) prevails over the 25% quota.
  • Neglecting the requirement of “constitutional pedigree” when arguing for a new unenumerated right — mere policy desirability is insufficient.

These advanced themes illustrate how the Constitution’s text, judicial doctrine, and legislative action continuously reshape the landscape of rights, principles and duties.

The Principle of Non-Retrogression of Rights and its Application in India

The principle of non-retrogression, also known as the 'no-rollback' rule, posits that once certain human rights standards or protections have been achieved, the state should not take measures that diminish or reverse these advancements without compelling justification. Originating in international human rights law, particularly in the context of socio-economic rights under the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), this doctrine has found increasing resonance in Indian constitutional jurisprudence.

In India, while not explicitly enshrined in the Constitution, the Supreme Court has implicitly applied this principle, particularly in environmental law and socio-economic rights cases. The Court often interprets Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty) and Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) like Article 48A (Protection and Improvement of Environment) to prevent the state from taking actions that would degrade existing environmental standards or roll back established socio-economic benefits. For instance, in cases concerning environmental protection, the Court has held that once a certain level of environmental quality or protection has been achieved through legislative or judicial action, the state cannot arbitrarily lower these standards. This was evident in judgments related to pollution control and forest conservation, where the Court emphasized the state's continuous obligation to improve, not degrade, the quality of life.

The doctrine is not absolute. A state may be permitted to take retrogressive measures if it can demonstrate a compelling public interest, that the measures are strictly necessary, and that they are proportionate to the aim pursued. The burden of proof lies heavily on the state to justify any such rollback. This principle serves as a crucial check on legislative and executive power, ensuring that policy changes do not undermine the progressive realization of fundamental rights and the aspirational goals set out in the DPSP. It reinforces the idea of a 'living Constitution' that continuously strives for higher standards of human dignity and welfare, rather than allowing for arbitrary reversals of progress. The application of non-retrogression is particularly vital in areas like public health, education, and social security, where withdrawal of state support can have severe implications for vulnerable populations.

Horizontal Application of Fundamental Rights: Evolving Jurisprudence on Private Actors

While fundamental rights primarily operate vertically, i.e., against the 'State' as defined in Article 12, the concept of their horizontal application—where rights are enforced against private individuals or non-state entities—has seen significant evolution. The Constitution explicitly provides for direct horizontal application in certain articles: Article 15(2) prohibits discrimination by private individuals in public places; Article 17 abolishes untouchability; Article 23 prohibits forced labour; and Article 24 prohibits child labour. These provisions directly bind private actors.

Beyond these explicit provisions, the Supreme Court has developed doctrines for indirect horizontal application. One approach is to hold the 'State' accountable for its positive obligation to protect fundamental rights from infringement by private actors. For example, in Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), the Court laid down guidelines to prevent sexual harassment at the workplace, effectively imposing duties on private employers, arguing that the state had a duty to ensure a safe working environment under Article 21. Similarly, the state's failure to prevent honour killings or caste-based violence by private individuals can be seen as a violation of its positive obligations under Articles 21 and 14.

Another avenue for indirect horizontal application arises when private entities perform 'public functions' or are deemed 'instrumentalities of the State' under Article 12. While the 'instrumentality' test (e.g., control, funding) primarily determines if a body is 'State' for vertical application, the 'public function' test has been invoked to subject private bodies performing essential public services (like healthcare, education, or infrastructure) to constitutional norms, even if they don't fully meet the Article 12 criteria. In Zee Telefilms Ltd. v. Union of India (2005), the Court held that while BCCI was not 'State' under Article 12, it was amenable to public law remedies due to its public functions in regulating cricket. More recently, the Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) judgment on privacy recognized that the right to privacy could be enforced against private entities holding personal data, necessitating a robust data protection regime.

The challenge lies in balancing individual autonomy and private contractual freedom with the imperative of constitutional values. The jurisprudence continues to evolve, increasingly recognizing that the pervasive influence of private power in modern society necessitates a broader application of fundamental rights to ensure substantive equality and justice. This often involves interpreting existing statutes in light of constitutional values or compelling the legislature to enact laws that regulate private conduct in conformity with fundamental rights.

Examiner's trap
A private hospital, not receiving any government funding, refuses emergency treatment to a road accident victim, leading to their death. Which of the following constitutional provisions are most likely to be invoked against the hospital or the state, considering the horizontal application of rights?
  1. Article 14 (Equality before law) and Article 21 (Right to life).
  2. Article 15(2) (Non-discrimination in public places).
  3. Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability).
  4. Article 19(1)(g) (Right to practice any profession).
The most appropriate answer is (1) Article 14 and Article 21. While the hospital is a private entity, the Supreme Court has recognized the 'Right to Health' as an integral part of Article 21. In Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996), the Court held that the state has a positive obligation to provide adequate medical facilities and that denial of timely medical treatment to an injured person by a government hospital is a violation of Article 21. This principle extends to private hospitals performing a public function, especially in emergencies. The state also has a positive obligation to ensure that private actors do not violate fundamental rights. Article 14 could be invoked if the refusal was discriminatory. Article 15(2) is specific to access to public places and does not directly cover emergency medical treatment refusal. Article 17 is specific to untouchability. Article 19(1)(g) relates to the hospital's right to practice its profession, which is subject to reasonable restrictions and public interest, not a basis for denying emergency care.
Mastering Interconnectedness
When analyzing questions on Fundamental Rights, DPSP, and Fundamental Duties, always look for their interconnectedness. Rarely will a complex Mains question focus on just one in isolation. For instance, a question on environmental protection might involve Article 21 (right to life), Article 48A (DPSP for environment), and Article 51A(g) (duty to protect environment). Similarly, socio-economic rights often link Article 21 to various DPSP. Demonstrating this holistic understanding, citing relevant cases that bridge these concepts, fetches higher marks.

The Concept of Positive Obligations of the State under Fundamental Rights and its Link to DPSP

Traditionally, fundamental rights were viewed primarily as negative injunctions, meaning they restrained the state from interfering with individual liberties. However, Indian jurisprudence, particularly since the expansion of Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty) post-Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), has increasingly recognized that the state also bears positive obligations—duties to act proactively to ensure the realization of rights. These positive obligations require the state to provide certain services, create necessary conditions, or protect individuals from harm, rather than merely refraining from interference.

Examples of positive obligations under fundamental rights are numerous. The right to life under Article 21, for instance, has been interpreted to include the right to health, requiring the state to provide adequate medical facilities (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal, 1996). It also encompasses the right to a clean environment, necessitating state action against pollution (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, various cases). The right to education, transformed into a fundamental right under Article 21A, imposes a clear positive obligation on the state to establish and maintain educational institutions. Similarly, Article 23 and 24, prohibiting forced labour and child labour, mandate the state to legislate and enforce laws to prevent these practices.

The link between these positive obligations and the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) is profound. DPSP, though non-justiciable, serve as the constitutional conscience and guide the state in formulating policies aimed at social and economic justice. The Supreme Court has frequently drawn upon DPSP to give content and meaning to the positive obligations under fundamental rights. For example, the right to livelihood under Article 21 was linked to Article 39(a) (right to an adequate means of livelihood) in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985). The right to health under Article 21 finds its roots in Article 47 (duty of the state to raise the level of nutrition and public health). This synergistic approach highlights how DPSP, while not directly enforceable, become indirectly enforceable by informing the scope of justiciable fundamental rights, thereby transforming the state's role from a mere abstainer to an active enabler of rights. This framework is central to India's commitment to transformative constitutionalism, aiming to achieve a substantive democracy where socio-economic justice is as important as civil and political liberties.

The Role of International Covenants and Customary International Law in Interpreting Fundamental Rights

The Indian Constitution, while comprehensive, is not an insular document. Indian courts frequently refer to international human rights instruments and customary international law to interpret and expand the scope of fundamental rights. This approach is rooted in Article 51 of the Constitution, which directs the state to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations, and Article 253, which empowers Parliament to legislate for implementing international treaties. However, in the absence of specific domestic legislation, courts have adopted a nuanced approach.

The primary method is 'harmonious construction,' where domestic law, particularly fundamental rights, is interpreted in conformity with international human rights norms, provided there is no direct conflict with existing statutory law. This principle was famously articulated in Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), where, in the absence of domestic law on sexual harassment at the workplace, the Supreme Court relied on the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) to formulate binding guidelines. These guidelines served as law until Parliament enacted the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

Similarly, in D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997), the Court formulated guidelines for arrest and detention, drawing inspiration from the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR). The right to privacy, recognized in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017), also acknowledged the influence of international instruments like the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) and the ICCPR. More recently, judgments concerning the rights of transgender persons (National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India, 2014) and the decriminalization of Section 377 IPC (Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, 2018) have explicitly referenced international human rights standards on dignity, non-discrimination, and bodily autonomy.

It is crucial to note the limitations: international law is not directly enforceable in India unless incorporated into domestic law by Parliament. Courts cannot use international law to override clear and unambiguous provisions of domestic legislation. However, it serves as a powerful interpretive tool, a source of legitimate expectation, and a persuasive authority that guides the progressive realization of human rights, pushing the boundaries of constitutional interpretation to align India's legal framework with global human rights standards and aspirations.

  • Common Traps

  • Confusing 'non-retrogression' with 'absolute prohibition': The principle of non-retrogression is not absolute; retrogressive measures can be justified under compelling circumstances, but the burden of proof is on the state.
  • Assuming all fundamental rights have direct horizontal application: Only specific articles (e.g., 15(2), 17, 23, 24) directly bind private actors. Most others apply indirectly through state obligations or the 'public function' test.
  • Treating positive obligations as equivalent to DPSP: While DPSP inform positive obligations, the latter are justiciable under fundamental rights, whereas DPSP themselves are not directly enforceable.
  • Believing international law is directly enforceable in India without domestic legislation: International treaties and customary law are persuasive and used for interpretation but are not self-executing in India unless incorporated by Parliament.
  • Overlooking the 'public function' test for private entities: Even if a private body is not an 'instrumentality of the State' under Article 12, it may still be subject to public law remedies if it performs significant public functions.
  • Ignoring the state's positive duty to protect rights from private actors: The state's omission to protect citizens from private violations of fundamental rights (e.g., violence, discrimination) can itself be a violation of its constitutional obligations.

The Basic Structure Doctrine: Evolution, Core Features and Limits on Constitutional Amendments

The basic structure doctrine is the judicial safeguard that prevents Parliament from altering the Constitution’s essential features through the amending power under Article 368. Its genesis lies in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where a 13‑judge bench held by a 7‑6 majority that while Parliament enjoys wide amending power, it cannot destroy the “basic structure” of the Constitution. The Court did not enumerate an exhaustive list but identified several pillars: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federalism, and the dignity of the individual secured by fundamental rights.

Subsequent jurisprudence refined the doctrine. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) the Court struck down the 39th Amendment’s clause that insulated the Prime Minister’s election from judicial review, affirming that free and fair elections form part of the basic structure. Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) added judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles as inviolable. Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) introduced the “doctrine of prospective overruling” for amendments made before 24 April 1973, while I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) held that any law placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 is subject to basic‑structure scrutiny.

The doctrine operates as a substantive limit on the constituent power. It does not curtail the procedural requirements of Article 368 (special majority, state ratification where required) but imposes a qualitative test: an amendment that abrogates or emasculates a basic feature is void. The Supreme Court has applied this test to strike down the 42nd Amendment’s attempt to curtail judicial review (Minerva Mills), the 99th Amendment establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 2015), and the 103rd Amendment’s 10 % EWS quota only to the extent it violated the 50 % ceiling (Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta, 2018).

Critics argue the doctrine grants unelected judges a veto over democratic amendment, while proponents maintain it preserves constitutional identity against majoritarian excess. The Court has repeatedly emphasised that the basic structure is not a static catalogue but evolves through judicial interpretation, anchored in the Constitution’s text, preamble, and the scheme of governance. For aspirants, the key is to recognise the doctrine as a “judicial safety valve” that co‑exists with parliamentary sovereignty within constitutional limits, and to be able to cite the landmark cases that expanded or clarified each basic feature.

Substantive Equality and Affirmative Action: The Constitutional Architecture of Reservations

Articles 15 and 16 embody the guarantee of non-discrimination and equal opportunity in public employment, but they also contain enabling clauses that permit the State to make special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs), Scheduled Castes (SCs), Scheduled Tribes (STs) and, since the 103rd Amendment (2019), economically weaker sections (EWS). The journey from formal equality to substantive equality is traced through a series of landmark judgments that have shaped the scope, limits and judicial review standards for reservation policies.

The Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) struck down caste‑based reservations in educational institutions, prompting the First Amendment (1951) which added Article 15(4). M.R. Balaji v. State of Mysore (1963) introduced the 50 % ceiling on reservations, a rule later affirmed in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) (the Mandal Commission case). The Mandal judgment also laid down the “creamy layer” exclusion for Other Backward Classes (OBCs), held that reservations cannot exceed 50 % except in extraordinary circumstances, and ruled that Article 16(4) is an enabling provision, not an exception to Article 16(1).

Subsequent cases refined the creamy‑layer test (Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India, 2008), extended it to SC/ST promotions (M. Nagaraj v. Union of India, 2006) and later held that the creamy‑layer concept does not apply to SC/STs (Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta, 2018). The 103rd Amendment inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6) providing up to 10 % reservation for EWS in admissions and public employment, excluding those covered by existing reservations. The Supreme Court upheld the amendment in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) by a 3‑2 majority, ruling that economic criteria alone can constitute a valid classification and that the 50 % ceiling is not an absolute bar for EWS quota.

The Court has also addressed reservations in promotions, requiring quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequacy of representation and maintenance of administrative efficiency (M. Nagaraj). The 2022 decision in Janhit Abhiyan clarified that the 50 % rule applies to the total of all reservations, but the EWS quota operates over and above the existing 50 % limit, effectively allowing a combined reservation of up to 60 %. Aspirants must be able to distinguish between “vertical” reservations (SC/ST/OBC/EWS) and “horizontal” reservations (women, persons with disabilities, ex‑servicemen), and to articulate the judicial standards — “compelling state interest”, “narrow tailoring”, and “proportionality” — that govern any affirmative‑action measure.

Worked Example: Applying the Creamy‑Layer Test
A state government issues a notification granting 27 % reservation in public employment to OBCs, but does not exclude the creamy layer. A candidate from an OBC family with annual parental income of ₹12 lakh challenges the notification. Decide the validity of the notification.
The notification is invalid. In Indra Sawhney (1992) the Supreme Court mandated exclusion of the creamy layer from OBC reservations to ensure that benefits reach the truly backward. The Court fixed the income threshold (revised periodically; currently ₹8 lakh per annum) as a proxy for creamy‑layer status. Since the state failed to apply the creamy‑layer filter, the notification violates Article 16(4) read with the equality guarantee under Article 14. The appropriate remedy is to quash the notification and direct the state to re‑issue it with the creamy‑layer exclusion in force.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks you to “critically examine” a reservation policy, structure the answer in three layers: (1) constitutional provision (Article 15/16 clause), (2) judicial test (50 % ceiling, creamy layer, quantifiable data, efficiency), (3) recent amendment or judgment impact (103rd Amendment, Janhit Abhiyan). Cite the case name, year and the specific ratio. This three‑step template scores high on both knowledge and analytical depth.

The Right to Information: Constitutional Foundations, Judicial Interpretation and the RTI Act 2005

The right to information (RTI) is not expressly enumerated in Part III, but the Supreme Court has read it into Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech and expression) and Article 21 (right to life and personal liberty). In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Raj Narain (1975) the Court held that the right to know is a necessary corollary of free speech, and in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1982) it declared that “open government” is a facet of the right to life. These pronouncements laid the doctrinal groundwork for the Right to Information Act, 2005, which operationalises the constitutional guarantee by providing a statutory regime for proactive disclosure, time‑bound responses, and an appellate mechanism.

The RTI Act establishes a three‑tier structure: Public Information Officers (PIOs) at the first level, First Appellate Authorities, and the Central/State Information Commissions (CIC/SICs) vested with powers of a civil court. Section 8 enumerates exemptions (national security, privacy, commercial confidence, etc.) that are subject to a “harm test” — the public authority must demonstrate that disclosure would cause identifiable harm. The Supreme Court in Central Board of Secondary Education v. Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011) clarified that the burden of proof lies on the authority invoking an exemption, and that the “public interest override” in Section 8(2) requires a balancing exercise.

Judicial interpretation has expanded the scope of “public authority” to include bodies substantially financed by government (Namit Sharma v. Union of India, 2013) and even private entities performing public functions (Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank v. State of Kerala, 2013). The Court has also held that the RTI Act does not violate the Official Secrets Act, 1923, because Section 22 of the RTI Act gives it overriding effect. In Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India (2019) the Supreme Court directed the Centre to fill vacancies in the CIC, emphasising that an ineffective commission renders the right illusory.

Recent amendments (RTI Amendment Act, 2019) altered the tenure, salary and service conditions of Information Commissioners, prompting criticism that the independence of the commissions is compromised. The Supreme Court in Common Cause v. Union of India (2020) upheld the amendments but read down the provision allowing the Centre to unilaterally decide terms, requiring consultation with the Chief Information Commissioner. For aspirants, the RTI regime illustrates how a judicially recognised implied right can be codified, how statutory exemptions are subjected to constitutional proportionality, and how institutional design (independence of commissions) determines the effectiveness of a fundamental right.

Separation of Powers and Judicial Review: The Institutional Balance in Rights Adjudication

The Constitution distributes legislative, executive and judicial powers among Parliament, the Executive and the Judiciary, but it does not adopt a rigid separation‑of‑powers model akin to the United States. Instead, it embraces a “checks‑and‑balances” framework where each organ exercises oversight over the others. Judicial review, enshrined in Articles 13, 32, 136, 226 and 227, is the principal mechanism by which the judiciary polices the constitutional boundaries of legislative and executive action, especially when fundamental rights are at stake.

The Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) declared judicial review a part of the basic structure, making it immune to constitutional amendment. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that sought to oust judicial review of constitutional amendments, reinforcing that the power of review is non‑derogable. The doctrine of “judicial restraint” has been articulated in cases such as S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), where the Court cautioned against substituting its own policy judgment for that of the elected branches, while still asserting the duty to intervene when constitutional limits are breached.

The tension between activism and restraint is evident in the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 2015). The Court struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act, holding that primacy of the judiciary in appointments is a basic feature, thereby preserving judicial independence from executive influence. Conversely, in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) the Court refused to interfere with the President’s satisfaction under Article 356, illustrating deference to executive discretion in certain political questions.

Recent jurisprudence shows a calibrated approach: the Court employs the “proportionality test” (legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, balancing) to review restrictions on rights (e.g., K.S. Puttaswamy, 2017 on privacy; Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, 2020 on internet shutdowns). It also uses “structured proportionality” to assess legislative encroachments on federal powers (G. N. Saibaba v. State of Maharashtra, 2022). Aspirants should be able to articulate the three‑tier standard of review — “strict scrutiny” for suspect classifications, “intermediate scrutiny” for gender‑based classifications, and “rational basis” for economic legislation — and to explain how the separation‑of‑powers principle informs the choice of standard.

Judicial Review of Ordinances: Constitutional Limits and Impact on Fundamental Rights

Article 123 (President) and Article 213 (Governor) empower the executive to promulgate ordinances when Parliament or the State Legislature is not in session, provided the executive is satisfied that circumstances exist requiring immediate action. Ordinances have the same force as an Act of Legislature but must be laid before the legislature within six weeks of its reassembly; otherwise they lapse. The Supreme Court has repeatedly examined whether this power can be used to bypass legislative scrutiny, especially when ordinances affect fundamental rights.

In R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) the Court held that the President’s satisfaction is justiciable and that an ordinance cannot be used to circumvent the legislative process. D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) struck down the practice of re‑promulgating ordinances without legislative approval, terming it a “fraud on the Constitution”. The Court ruled that repeated re‑promulgation without placing the ordinance before the legislature violates Article 123/213 and undermines democratic accountability.

The most recent authoritative pronouncement is Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017), where a seven‑judge bench held that an ordinance that has lapsed cannot be revived by a subsequent ordinance with the same provisions unless the legislature has enacted it. The Court also clarified that the “satisfaction” of the President/Governor is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fides, irrelevance, or non‑application of mind. Importantly, the judgment affirmed that fundamental rights can be curtailed by an ordinance only if the ordinance meets the test of reasonableness under Article 19(2)–(6) and the proportionality standard.

Practical implications: when an ordinance imposes restrictions on speech, movement, or property, the affected party can challenge it under Article 32/226 even before the legislature meets. The Court may issue an interim stay if the ordinance appears prima facie unconstitutional. Aspirants should note the distinction between “repromulgation” (impermissible) and “re‑enactment” after legislative debate (permissible), and be ready to cite the three landmark cases — Cooper, Wadhwa, Krishna Kumar Singh — as the doctrinal trilogy governing ordinance‑making power.
