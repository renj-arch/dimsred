1. Constitutional Framework of Rights, Principles and Duties

1.1 The Scheme of Part III, Part IV and Part IVA

The Constitution of India distributes the normative architecture of governance across three distinct parts that together shape the relationship between the state and the citizen. Part III, comprising Articles 12 to 35, enumerates the Fundamental Rights that are justiciable and enforceable by the courts. Part IV, covering Articles 36 to 51, sets out the Directive Principles of State Policy, which are non‑justiciable guidelines for the legislature and executive. Part IVA, introduced by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 and later expanded by the 86th Amendment in 2002, contains the Fundamental Duties under Article 51A. This tripartite structure reflects the Constituent Assembly’s intention to balance individual liberty with collective welfare and to remind citizens that rights carry correlative responsibilities.

1.2 Historical Evolution and Constituent Assembly Debates

The debates in the Constituent Assembly reveal a conscious decision to place enforceable rights in a separate chapter while relegating socio‑economic aspirations to a directive chapter. Members such as B. R. Ambedkar argued that justiciable rights would protect minorities and dissent, whereas directives would guide the state toward a welfare orientation without exposing the judiciary to policy‑making. The inclusion of Fundamental Duties was a later development, motivated by the desire to cultivate a sense of civic discipline after the Emergency experience. The 42nd Amendment inserted ten duties, and the 86th Amendment added the duty to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years, reflecting an evolving constitutional consciousness.

1.3 Constitutional Philosophy: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity

The Preamble’s promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity finds operational expression through the three parts. Fundamental Rights secure liberty and equality against arbitrary state action. Directive Principles translate the ideal of social and economic justice into actionable policy goals. Fundamental Duties embody fraternity by obligating citizens to respect the Constitution, the national symbols and the rights of others. Together they constitute a constitutional ecosystem where each component informs the interpretation of the others, a principle the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed in cases such as Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) and Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980).

2. Fundamental Rights: Scope and Judicial Interpretation

2.1 Definition of State and the Reach of Article 12

Article 12 defines the term “State” for the purpose of Part III, extending the ambit of Fundamental Rights beyond the central and state governments to include local authorities, statutory corporations and any other authority under the control of the government. The Supreme Court in R. D. Shetty v. Airport Authority of India (1979) laid down the “instrumentality test” to determine whether a body qualifies as State, emphasizing functional, financial and administrative control. This expansive definition ensures that entities performing public functions cannot evade constitutional accountability by claiming private status.

2.2 Classification of Rights: Civil, Political and Socio‑Economic

Fundamental Rights are traditionally grouped into six categories: Right to Equality (Articles 14‑18), Right to Freedom (Articles 19‑22), Right against Exploitation (Articles 23‑24), Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25‑28), Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29‑30) and Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32). While the first five clusters protect civil and political liberties, the judiciary has progressively read socio‑economic dimensions into them. For instance, Article 21’s guarantee of life and personal liberty has been interpreted to include the right to health, education, shelter and a clean environment, as seen in Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981) and Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993).

2.3 Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine

The power of judicial review under Article 32 and Article 226 makes Fundamental Rights the cornerstone of constitutional supremacy. The Basic Structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati, holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a manner that destroys its essential features, including the fundamental rights framework. Subsequent decisions such as Minerva Mills reinforced that a harmonious construction between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure, preventing any amendment that would give primacy to directives over rights.

2.4 Landmark Expansions: From Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi

The evolution from A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), where “procedure established by law” was read narrowly, to Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court required the procedure to be “fair, just and reasonable,” illustrates the dynamic interpretive trajectory. This shift incorporated principles of natural justice into Article 21, making any law depriving life or liberty subject to substantive due process scrutiny. The Court also linked Articles 14, 19 and 21, establishing the “golden triangle” that any legislation must satisfy simultaneously.

Example Question
Discuss how the Supreme Court has reconciled the apparent conflict between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles through the doctrine of harmonious construction. Illustrate with reference to at least two landmark judgments.
The Supreme Court has consistently held that Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are not mutually exclusive but form an integrated scheme. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Court declared that the basic structure includes the balance between Parts III and IV, preventing any amendment that would abrogate rights in favour of directives. In Minerva Mills (1980), the Court struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that gave primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights, affirming that harmony requires neither part to be subservient. Later, in State of Kerala v. N. M. Thomas (1976), the Court read Article 46 (promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections) together with Article 15(4) to uphold reservation policies, demonstrating that directives can inform the scope of rights without overriding them. The principle of harmonious construction thus mandates that courts interpret rights in a manner that advances directive goals while preserving the enforceability of rights.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “tension and balance,” structure the answer around three judicial phases: early strict separation (Gopalan), the harmonious construction era (Kesavananda, Minerva Mills), and the contemporary integrative approach (Unni Krishnan, Olga Tellis). Cite the specific Articles and the reasoning of the Court in each phase to demonstrate depth.

3. Directive Principles of State Policy: Nature and Enforceability

3.1 Classification: Socialistic, Gandhian and Liberal‑Intellectual

The Directive Principles are grouped into three broad categories reflecting the ideological influences on the Constitution. Socialistic principles (Articles 38, 39, 41, 42, 43) aim at securing a just social order, adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, and humane conditions of work. Gandhian principles (Articles 40, 43B, 46, 47, 48) emphasize village panchayats, cottage industries, promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections, prohibition of intoxicating drinks and cow protection. Liberal‑intellectual principles (Articles 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51) advocate a uniform civil code, free and compulsory education, protection of environment, separation of judiciary from executive and promotion of international peace. This taxonomy helps aspirants identify the policy thrust behind each Article.

3.2 Judicial Attitude: From Non‑Justiciability to Interpretive Tool

Although Article 37 declares Directive Principles non‑justiciable, the judiciary has used them as interpretive aids to expand the meaning of Fundamental Rights. In the early phase, the Court treated directives as mere moral exhortations. However, from the 1970s onward, judgments such as Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills elevated directives to a constitutional mandate that informs legislative policy. The Court in Unni Krishnan read Article 45 (free and compulsory education) together with Article 21 to declare a fundamental right to education up to the age of fourteen, later codified by the 86th Amendment as Article 21A. This jurisprudential shift demonstrates that non‑justiciability does not render directives irrelevant; they shape the content of justiciable rights.

3.3 Legislative Implementation and Policy Impact

Many Directive Principles have been translated into statutes, illustrating their practical influence. The Minimum Wages Act, 1948 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 give effect to Articles 39(d) and 39(e). The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments operationalize Article 40 by constitutionally mandating panchayati raj institutions and urban local bodies. The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 reflects Article 41’s right to work. Environmental legislation such as the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 draw from Article 48A. These examples show that while directives lack judicial enforceability, they serve as a constitutional compass for legislative action.

4. Fundamental Duties: Evolution and Constitutional Significance

4.1 Origin, Text and Subsequent Additions

Fundamental Duties were absent from the original Constitution. The 42nd Amendment inserted Article 51A with ten duties, ranging from respecting the Constitution and national symbols to promoting harmony and protecting public property. The 86th Amendment added clause (k), obligating parents or guardians to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. The duties are addressed to “every citizen of India,” making them universally applicable, unlike Fundamental Rights which are available to persons, including non‑citizens in certain cases.

4.2 Judicial Enforcement and Symbolic Value

The Supreme Court has held that Fundamental Duties are not legally enforceable by writs, but they acquire legal significance when a statute incorporates them. In Chandra Bhavan Boarding and Lodging v. State of Mysore (1970), the Court observed that duties serve as a constant reminder of civic obligations. In the more recent case of Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India (2021), the Court suggested that duties can be used to interpret the scope of rights, for instance, the duty to protect the environment under Article 51A(g) informs the content of Article 21. Thus, while duties lack direct justiciability, they function as interpretive guides and as a basis for legislative sanctions.

4.3 Comparative Perspective: Duties in Other Constitutions

Several constitutions, such as those of Japan, Spain and the former Soviet Union, enumerate duties alongside rights. The Indian model is distinctive because duties were added decades after the Constitution’s commencement, reflecting a corrective response to perceived civic apathy. Unlike the Japanese Constitution, where duties are justiciable, Indian duties remain largely moral. Nevertheless, the inclusion of duties in Part IVA signals a constitutional aspiration to balance the rights‑centric discourse with a responsibility‑centric ethos.

5. Interplay, Tension and Harmonious Construction

5.1 The Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Constitutional Interpretation

The Supreme Court has articulated the doctrine of harmonious construction to resolve apparent conflicts between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. The core premise is that the Constitution is an organic whole; no part can be read in isolation to defeat another. In Kesavananda Bharati, the Court held that the amending power under Article 368 cannot be used to destroy the basic structure, which includes the balance between Parts III and IV. This principle obliges the legislature to frame laws that respect rights while pursuing directive goals, and it obliges the judiciary to interpret rights in a manner that advances directive objectives without nullifying them.

5.2 Judicial Balancing in Socio‑Economic Legislation

Cases involving land reform, labour welfare and environmental protection illustrate the balancing act. In State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977), the Court upheld a land ceiling law under Article 31C (as originally enacted) because it implemented Article 39(b) and (c), while ensuring that the law did not violate Article 14. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), the Court recognized the right to livelihood as part of Article 21, linking it to the directive under Article 41. The Court ordered procedural safeguards before evicting pavement dwellers, demonstrating that directive goals cannot be pursued at the expense of fundamental rights without due process.

5.3 Contemporary Challenges and the Way Forward

Emerging issues such as data privacy, climate justice and affirmative action in private education test the harmonious construction framework. The recognition of the right to privacy as a fundamental right in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) shows the Court’s willingness to expand Part III in response to new realities, while directives like Article 48A (environment) and Article 51A(g) (duty to protect environment) provide a constitutional basis for climate litigation. The ongoing debate over the National Education Policy and the implementation of the Right to Education Act reflects the dynamic tension between the aspirational directives and the enforceable rights. A nuanced understanding of this interplay is essential for any serious student of Indian constitutional law.

The constitutional scheme thus envisions a continuous dialogue between rights, principles and duties, each informing the other to realise the vision of a just, equitable and fraternal society.

Article 13 and the Doctrine of Eclipse

Article 13 declares that any law inconsistent with the Fundamental Rights shall, to the extent of the inconsistency, be void. The doctrine of eclipse, first articulated in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), explains the fate of pre‑constitutional statutes that conflict with Part III. Such laws are not dead; they are merely eclipsed by the Fundamental Rights. When the constitutional provision that caused the eclipse is amended or repealed, the pre‑constitutional law revives automatically, provided it is not otherwise unconstitutional. The Supreme Court applied this principle in State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills (1974), holding that a pre‑Constitution law regulating the sale of intoxicating liquor, which had been eclipsed by Article 19(1)(g), revived after the 44th Amendment removed the relevant restriction. The doctrine does not apply to post‑constitutional laws; a statute enacted after 26 January 1950 that violates a Fundamental Right is void ab initio and cannot be revived by a later amendment. This distinction is crucial for legislative drafting: Parliament may cure a post‑constitutional defect only by enacting a fresh law, whereas a pre‑constitutional defect can be cured by constitutional amendment alone. The eclipse doctrine also interacts with Article 31A and Article 31B (the Ninth Schedule). Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule before the Kesavananda Bharati judgment (1973) enjoy immunity from judicial review, but the Supreme Court in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) held that even Ninth‑Schedule laws are subject to the basic‑structure test, limiting the eclipse analogy to ordinary statutes. Understanding the temporal boundary — pre‑ versus post‑constitutional — and the interplay with the Ninth Schedule is a frequent examiner twist.

Article 21: Substantive Due Process and the Right to Life

Article 21 — “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law” — has been transformed from a procedural guarantee into a substantive due‑process clause. The turning point was Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court read “procedure established by law” as “fair, just and reasonable procedure”, importing the American due‑process standard. Subsequent jurisprudence expanded “life” to include a bundle of rights: livelihood (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985), health (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal, 1996), education (Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh, 1993), environment (Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India, 1996), privacy (Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2017), and dignity in death (Common Cause v. Union of India, 2018). The Court has also used Article 21 to read down statutes that grant excessive discretionary power, as in Kharak Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1963) (surveillance) and R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu (1994) (press freedom). The “procedure established by law” test now requires that the law be reasonable, non‑arbitrary, and proportionate — a three‑pronged proportionality analysis (legitimate aim, rational connection, necessity, and balancing). This substantive reading aligns Indian jurisprudence with the “living constitution” approach and enables the judiciary to enforce socio‑economic directives (Articles 38, 39, 41, 48A) through the enforceable right to life. Examiners often ask to distinguish “procedure established by law” from “due process of law” and to illustrate the expansion with at least three landmark cases.

Examiner's Trap: Procedure vs. Substance
A question states: “Article 21 only guarantees a fair procedure; it does not confer any substantive rights.” Critically examine.
The statement reflects the pre‑Maneka Gandhi position (A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras, 1950). Post‑Maneka Gandhi, the Supreme Court held that “procedure established by law” must be fair, just and reasonable, thereby importing substantive due process. Consequently, Article 21 now protects substantive rights such as livelihood, health, privacy, and a clean environment. The correct answer: the statement is outdated; Article 21 has been interpreted to confer substantive rights through the requirement of a reasonable procedure.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “expansion of Article 21”, list the right, the case, and the year in a three‑column mental table. This prevents omission of the landmark case and shows chronological development, which examiners reward.

Article 31C: The Saving Clause and Its Judicial Trajectory

Article 31C, inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971) and expanded by the 42nd Amendment (1976), provides that laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) and (c) shall not be deemed void on the ground of inconsistency with Articles 14, 19, or 31. The original 1971 version protected only laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c). The 42nd Amendment widened the shield to any law implementing any Directive Principle, and declared that such laws could not be challenged in any court. In Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980), the Supreme Court struck down the 42nd Amendment’s expansion, holding that the blanket immunity destroyed the basic structure by removing judicial review — a core constitutional feature. The Court restored the pre‑1976 position: only laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) enjoy the Article 31C shield, and even they remain subject to judicial review for violation of the basic structure. Subsequent cases (Waman Rao v. Union of India, 1981; Bhim Singh v. Union of India, 2010) reaffirmed that the protection is not absolute; the law must genuinely implement the specified directives and cannot be a colourable exercise of power. The current test: (i) the law must be enacted to give effect to Article 39(b) or (c); (ii) it must not violate the basic structure; (iii) it is open to challenge on grounds of arbitrariness under Article 14. This nuanced balance is a favourite area for “distinguish” questions comparing the 25th and 42nd Amendment versions.

The Ninth Schedule: From Immunity to Basic‑Structure Scrutiny

The Ninth Schedule, created by the 1st Amendment (1951), was designed to insulate land‑reform and other socio‑economic laws from judicial review. Initially, the Schedule enjoyed absolute immunity under Article 31B. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Court held that the amending power could not destroy the basic structure, but left the Ninth Schedule untouched. The decisive shift came in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), where a nine‑judge bench ruled that any law placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda judgment) is subject to judicial review if it violates the basic structure. Laws inserted before that date remain immune, but the Court may still examine whether they damage the basic structure. The judgment introduced a “dual test”: (a) temporal — post‑1973 insertions are reviewable; (b) substantive — even pre‑1973 laws can be struck down if they abrogate fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure (e.g., equality, rule of law). This doctrine prevents the legislature from using the Ninth Schedule as a “constitutional dustbin” for controversial legislation. Examiners often ask to trace the evolution from absolute immunity to conditional review and to apply the dual test to a hypothetical amendment.

Fundamental Duties: From Moral Exhortation to Legal Obligation — Legislative and Judicial Developments

Part IVA (Article 51A) was introduced by the 42nd Amendment (1976) and expanded by the 86th Amendment (2002) adding the duty to provide education to children (Article 51A(k)). Although non‑justiciable, the duties have acquired legal traction through statutes and judicial interpretation. The Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 (amended 2003) criminalises disrespect to the flag and anthem, giving teeth to Article 51A(a). The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, operationalise Article 51A(g) (protect environment). In Rangnath Mishra v. Union of India (2021), the Supreme Court held that Fundamental Duties can be used as interpretive aids to expand Fundamental Rights, e.g., the duty to protect the environment informs the content of Article 21. The Court also suggested that the State may enact laws to enforce duties, and failure to do so could be challenged as a violation of the directive to promote a responsible citizenry. The 2020 National Education Policy explicitly links curriculum design to Article 51A(j) (develop scientific temper) and Article 51A(k). Comparative constitutions (Japan, Spain) make duties justiciable; India’s model remains aspirational but increasingly legislatively anchored. A nuanced answer must distinguish between moral exhortation, statutory enforcement, and judicial use as interpretive tools.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse with the doctrine of severability — eclipse applies only to pre‑constitutional laws, severability to any statute.
  • Treating “procedure established by law” as synonymous with “due process of law” without citing Maneka Gandhi.
  • Assuming Article 31C gives blanket immunity to all Directive‑Principle‑based laws; the 42nd Amendment expansion was struck down in Minerva Mills.
  • Believing the Ninth Schedule provides absolute immunity for all entries; post‑1973 insertions are reviewable under the basic‑structure test.
  • Stating Fundamental Duties are purely moral with no legal effect; several statutes and recent judgments give them enforceable dimensions.
  • Overlooking the temporal distinction in the Ninth Schedule (pre‑ vs post‑Kesavananda) when answering “reviewability” questions.
  • Failing to link the expansion of Article 21 to specific Directive Principles (e.g., Article 48A, 51A(g)) in socio‑economic rights questions.

Article 14: Reasonable Classification, Arbitrariness and the Emerging “Manifest Arbitrariness” Test

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws. The classical test, laid down in State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952), requires that any classification must be founded on an intelligible differentia which distinguishes persons or things grouped together from those left out, and that the differentia must have a rational relation to the object sought to be achieved by the legislation. Over the decades the Supreme Court has refined this two‑pronged test. In E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974) the Court introduced the concept of “arbitrariness” as a separate ground for striking down state action, holding that equality is a dynamic concept and that any action which is arbitrary, discriminatory or unreasonable violates Article 14 even if a formal classification can be articulated.

The “manifest arbitrariness” doctrine was crystallised in Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) where a five‑judge bench declared the practice of instant triple talaq unconstitutional. The Court held that a law or executive action which is “manifestly arbitrary” — i.e., lacking any reasonable basis, being capricious, or disproportionate to the object — fails the Article 14 test irrespective of whether a classification can be discerned. This shift moves the focus from the existence of a classification to the quality of the reasoning behind state action. Subsequent decisions such as Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) and Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) applied the manifest arbitrariness standard to strike down Section 377 IPC and the adultery provision respectively, reinforcing that the doctrine operates as a substantive due‑process check on legislative and executive power.

Importantly, the Court has clarified that the manifest arbitrariness test does not replace the traditional classification test; rather, it supplements it. In Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2019) (Sabarimala case) the majority observed that a law may survive the classification test yet fall foul of manifest arbitrariness if the underlying rationale is irrational or discriminatory. The practical implication for examinees is two‑fold: (i) when a statute creates a classification, apply the Anwar Ali Sarkar test first; (ii) if the classification appears facially neutral but the effect is grossly disproportionate or the legislative purpose is absent, invoke the manifest arbitrariness principle. The doctrine also extends to executive orders, policy guidelines and even judicial quasi‑legislative rules, making it a versatile tool in public‑law litigation.

Article 19(1)(a) in the Digital Age: Expanding Free Speech and the Limits of Reasonable Restrictions

The right to freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) has been interpreted expansively to include the freedom to receive and impart information through any medium. The landmark judgment in Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950) established that freedom of speech includes freedom of propagation of ideas, while Sakal Papers v. Union of India (1962) struck down a newsprint control order as an unreasonable restriction on the press. With the advent of the internet, the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) declared Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 unconstitutional for being vague and overbroad, thereby affirming that online speech enjoys the same constitutional protection as offline speech.

The Court has also recognised new dimensions such as the “right to be forgotten” (in K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) the privacy judgment) and the protection of journalistic sources (R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu (1994)). However, Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions on grounds of sovereignty, security, public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence, and integrity of India. The “reasonableness” test requires a proportionality analysis: the restriction must be narrowly tailored, pursue a legitimate aim, and be the least restrictive means. In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) the Court laid down a four‑step proportionality test for internet shutdowns — (i) the measure must be authorised by law; (ii) it must pursue a legitimate aim; (iii) it must be necessary in a democratic society; (iv) it must be proportionate to the need.

Recent legislative developments — the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, and the proposed Digital Personal Data Protection Act — have triggered fresh constitutional challenges. The Supreme Court in Foundation for Media Professionals v. Union of India (2022) stayed certain provisions of the 2021 Rules that mandated traceability of originators of messages, holding that such a requirement could chill free speech and violate the proportionality standard. For examination purposes, candidates must be able to (a) articulate the evolution from a “prior restraint” paradigm to a “proportionality” paradigm; (b) distinguish between content‑based and content‑neutral restrictions; and (c) apply the four‑step test to hypothetical scenarios involving social‑media regulation, internet shutdowns, or data‑localisation mandates.

Distinguish “reasonable restriction” from “manifest arbitrariness”
A state enacts a law prohibiting all political advertisements on social media during the 48‑hour period preceding an election, citing the need to prevent misinformation. The law applies uniformly to all parties and candidates. A challenger argues the law violates Article 19(1)(a) and Article 14. How should the Court analyse the challenge?
First, test the law under Article 19(2): the restriction must be reasonable. Apply the proportionality test — legitimate aim (preventing misinformation), suitability, necessity, and balancing. The blanket ban on all political ads is likely disproportionate because less restrictive alternatives (fact‑checking labels, targeted takedowns) exist. Second, under Article 14, examine whether the uniform classification (all political ads) has an intelligible differentia and rational nexus. The classification may survive the traditional test, but the Court can invoke “manifest arbitrariness” if the measure is capricious or lacks any reasonable basis. Since the blanket ban is over‑inclusive and not narrowly tailored, it is manifestly arbitrary. The Court would strike it down on both grounds.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks to “examine the constitutional validity” of a speech restriction, structure the answer in two layers: (1) Article 19(2) proportionality analysis — identify the ground, test necessity and least‑restrictive means; (2) Article 14 check — first apply the classification test, then flag manifest arbitrariness if the measure is over‑broad or irrational. Cite Shreya Singhal, Anuradha Bhasin, and Shayara Bano to demonstrate awareness of the evolving jurisprudence.

Article 21A: Right to Education – Statutory Architecture, Judicial Enforcement and Implementation Gaps

Inserted by the 86th Constitutional Amendment (2002), Article 21A makes free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen a fundamental right. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE Act) operationalises this guarantee. The Act mandates a 25 % reservation in private unaided schools for economically weaker sections (EWS) and disadvantaged groups, prescribes pupil‑teacher ratios, infrastructure norms, and a no‑detention policy up to Class VIII (later amended to allow detention after Class V and VIII). The Supreme Court in Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012) upheld the constitutional validity of the 25 % quota, holding that the State’s obligation under Article 21A extends to ensuring access in private schools, and that the provision does not violate Article 19(1)(g) or Article 30(1).

Judicial enforcement has focused on three fronts: (i) compliance with infrastructure and teacher‑availability norms — in Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India (2014) the Court directed states to submit compliance reports; (ii) the no‑detention policy — the 2019 amendment permitting detention was challenged in Kumar Shyam v. Union of India (2020), where the Court upheld the amendment as a reasonable restriction aimed at learning outcomes; (iii) the EWS quota implementation — several High Courts have issued mandamus to state governments to reimburse private schools per the per‑child cost formula, and to prevent arbitrary denial of admission.

Despite the robust legal framework, implementation gaps persist. The Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) 2023 indicates that only 73 % of rural children in the 6‑14 age group are enrolled in school, and learning levels remain low. Issues include delayed reimbursement to private schools, inadequate monitoring of teacher attendance, and the exclusion of children with disabilities from the 25 % quota in many states. The Supreme Court in National Coalition for Education v. Union of India (2021) emphasised that the right under Article 21A is not merely aspirational; the State must allocate sufficient financial resources and establish accountability mechanisms. For answer writing, candidates should link the constitutional mandate to the statutory scheme, cite the key judgments, and critically evaluate the gap between legal entitlement and ground‑level delivery.

Writ Jurisdiction under Articles 32 and 226: Comparative Scope, Public Interest Litigation and the Doctrine of Prospective Overruling

Article 32 confers the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights, while Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for both fundamental rights and “any other purpose”. The Supreme Court in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002) clarified that Article 32 is a fundamental right itself, whereas Article 226 is a constitutional power. Consequently, the Supreme Court’s writ jurisdiction is narrower in scope — limited to fundamental rights — but its orders are binding on all courts and authorities across India. High Courts enjoy wider territorial jurisdiction within their respective states and can entertain matters involving statutory or administrative rights.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) emerged through the relaxation of locus standi in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) and Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984). The Supreme Court and High Courts now entertain letters, postcards, and newspaper reports as writ petitions. The Court in Indian Council for Enviro‑Legal Action v. Union of India (1996) laid down guidelines to prevent misuse: (i) the petitioner must act bona fide; (ii) the matter must involve a substantial public interest; (iii) the court must be satisfied that no alternative remedy exists. The doctrine of prospective overruling, first articulated in Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) and refined in Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), allows the Court to declare a law unconstitutional but limit the effect of the declaration to future transactions, thereby protecting vested rights. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) the Court applied prospective overruling to the NJAC judgment, preserving appointments made before the decision.

For examination, the key distinctions are: (a) Article 32 is a guaranteed remedy; Article 226 is discretionary; (b) High Courts can issue writs for non‑fundamental rights; the Supreme Court cannot; (c) PIL expands access but is subject to judicial gatekeeping; (d) prospective overruling is a remedial tool, not a substantive right, and its invocation requires a clear judicial pronouncement. Candidates should be ready to draft a writ petition outline, identify the appropriate forum, and argue the maintainability of a PIL on the basis of the S.P. Gupta criteria.

  • Confusing the “reasonable classification” test with the “manifest arbitrariness” test — they operate at different analytical levels.
  • Treating every internet shutdown as per se unconstitutional without applying the four‑step proportionality test from Anuradha Bhasin.
  • Assuming Article 21A automatically guarantees quality education; the right is to free and compulsory *access*, not outcomes.
  • Believing High Courts can issue writs under Article 226 for enforcement of fundamental rights *only* — they can also act for “any other purpose”.
  • Overlooking the requirement of bona fide public interest and absence of alternative remedy when filing a PIL.
  • Misapplying prospective overruling as a blanket shield for all past actions; it applies only when the Court expressly limits retrospectivity.
  • Neglecting the distinction between Article 32 as a fundamental right and Article 226 as a constitutional power, leading to erroneous forum selection.

Article 15(4), 15(5) and 15(6): Reservations in Education, the Creamy Layer and the 103rd Amendment

Article 15(4) was inserted by the First Amendment (1951) to enable the State to make special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs) in admission to educational institutions. The Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) struck down a communal quota, prompting the amendment. Article 15(5), added by the 93rd Amendment (2005), extended the power to private unaided institutions, but the Court in T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002) and later in P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005) held that the State could not compel private unaided colleges to implement reservations unless they received State aid. The 103rd Constitutional Amendment (2019) introduced Article 15(6), providing up to 10 % reservation for economically weaker sections (EWS) not covered by existing quotas. The Court in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) upheld the amendment, ruling that the 50 % ceiling laid down in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) applies only to caste‑based reservations, not to economic criteria.

The creamy layer concept, first articulated in Indra Sawhney (the “Mandal judgment”), excludes socially advanced members of SEBCs from quota benefits. The Court prescribed an income ceiling (initially ₹1 lakh, revised periodically) and a set of occupational criteria. In Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India (2008) the Court reiterated that the creamy layer test must be applied at the time of each admission cycle, not merely at the time of policy formulation. The 103rd Amendment explicitly exempts EWS quota from the creamy layer requirement, a point of contention in subsequent litigation. For answer writing, candidates should distinguish three layers: (i) caste‑based SEBC quota under Article 15(4) subject to creamy layer; (ii) private unaided quota under Article 15(5) subject to State aid and creamy layer; (iii) EWS quota under Article 15(6) without creamy layer but within the 10 % cap.

Key Judicial Milestones

  • Champakam Dorairajan (1951) – struck down communal quota, leading to Art. 15(4).
  • Indra Sawhney (1992) – 50 % ceiling, creamy layer, exclusion of creamy layer from SEBC quota.
  • T.M.A. Pai (2002) – private unaided institutions not bound by State reservation policy.
  • P.A. Inamdar (2005) – reinforced autonomy of unaided colleges.
  • 93rd Amendment (2005) – inserted Art. 15(5) for private aided institutions.
  • 103rd Amendment (2019) – inserted Art. 15(6) for EWS.
  • Janhit Abhiyan (2022) – upheld EWS quota, clarified 50 % ceiling inapplicable.

Article 16(4), 16(4A) and 16(4B): Reservations in Public Employment, Carry Forward and the 77th/85th Amendments

Article 16(4) empowers the State to make reservations for backward classes in public employment. The Mandal Commission report (1980) recommended 27 % reservation for Other Backward Classes (OBCs), implemented by the V.P. Singh government in 1990. The Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney (1992) upheld the 27 % OBC quota, imposed the 50 % overall ceiling, and introduced the creamy layer exclusion. It also ruled that reservations could not be applied to promotions. To overcome this, the 77th Amendment (1995) inserted Article 16(4A), allowing reservation in promotions for SCs and STs. The 85th Amendment (2001) added the phrase “with consequential seniority”, ensuring that promoted SC/ST candidates retain seniority over general candidates.

Article 16(4B), inserted by the 81st Amendment (2000), permits the State to carry forward unfilled reserved vacancies to subsequent years, effectively breaching the 50 % ceiling for a particular year. The Court in M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006) upheld the constitutional validity of Articles 16(4A) and 16(4B) but laid down three conditions: (i) the State must demonstrate backwardness of the class; (ii) inadequacy of representation; (iii) maintenance of overall administrative efficiency. The Court also held that the creamy layer concept applies to SC/ST promotions, a position later softened by the 117th Amendment (2012) which removed the creamy layer requirement for SC/ST promotions. In Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018) the Court struck down the creamy layer exclusion for SC/ST promotions, affirming that the Presidential list under Article 341/342 is exhaustive.

For examination, the distinction between vertical (quota for entry) and horizontal (quota for promotion) reservations is critical. The carry‑forward rule under Article 16(4B) is a temporary breach of the 50 % ceiling, not a permanent expansion. Candidates should be able to trace the legislative response to each judicial pronouncement: Mandal → Indra Sawhney → 77th/81st/85th Amendments → Nagaraj → 117th Amendment → Jarnail Singh.

Technique: Applying the Creamy Layer Test
A state government issues a notification granting 27 % reservation in medical college admissions to OBC candidates. The notification does not mention any income ceiling. An OBC candidate with parental annual income of ₹12 lakh applies. The state argues that the creamy layer test is only a guideline. Decide the validity of the notification and the candidate’s eligibility.
The notification is invalid to the extent it omits the creamy layer exclusion. In Indra Sawhney (1992) the Supreme Court mandated that socially advanced members of SEBCs (the creamy layer) be excluded from reservation benefits. The Court prescribed an income ceiling (revised periodically) and occupational criteria. Subsequent judgments (Ashoka Kumar Thakur, 2008) require the test to be applied at each admission cycle. Therefore, the candidate with ₹12 lakh income falls within the creamy layer and is ineligible for the OBC quota. The state must amend the notification to incorporate the creamy layer criteria; otherwise the notification violates Article 15(4) read with the Indra Sawhney mandate.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to “critically examine” a reservation policy, structure the answer in three layers: constitutional provision, judicial interpretation (chronological), and legislative response. Cite the exact amendment number and year for each legislative change. Use a two‑column table in the margin to contrast vertical vs horizontal reservation, and creamy layer applicability across SC/ST/OBC/EWS. This visual cue helps the examiner see the analytical depth quickly.

Article 300A: The Right to Property as a Constitutional Right and Its Judicial Trajectory

Originally, the right to property was a fundamental right under Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31. The 44th Amendment (1978) repealed both and inserted Article 300A, which states: “No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law.” This converts the right into a constitutional (but non‑fundamental) right, meaning it cannot be enforced under Article 32 but can be challenged under Article 226. The Supreme Court in K.T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2011) held that Article 300A requires a valid law, not merely executive fiat, and that the law must satisfy the test of reasonableness under Article 14. In Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar v. State of Gujarat (1995) the Court ruled that the adequacy of compensation is not justiciable under Article 300A, but the procedure for acquisition must be fair and transparent.

The Land Acquisition Act, 1894 was replaced by the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR Act). The 2013 Act mandates social impact assessment, consent of 70‑80 % of affected families for private projects, and compensation at four times the market value in rural areas. The Supreme Court in K. K. Verma v. Union of India (2014) upheld the constitutional validity of the LARR Act, emphasizing that Article 300A does not freeze the quantum of compensation but requires a law that is not arbitrary. The 2015 amendment to the LARR Act (through an ordinance later ratified) diluted consent and social impact requirements for certain categories; the Court in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) (the privacy judgment) referenced Article 300A while discussing procedural fairness.

For answer writing, distinguish three phases: (i) fundamental right era (pre‑1978) with justiciable compensation; (ii) constitutional right era (post‑1978) where only “authority of law” is required; (iii) statutory regime under LARR Act where procedural safeguards are legislated. Note that Article 300A does not empower courts to review the adequacy of compensation, but they can strike down acquisition laws that are manifestly arbitrary or lack public purpose.

The Doctrine of Proportionality in Assessing Reasonable Restrictions under Article 19(2)–(6)

Article 19(1) guarantees six freedoms; clauses (2) to (6) permit reasonable restrictions on grounds such as sovereignty, public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to offence, and the interests of the general public. The Supreme Court moved from a “reasonableness” test to a structured proportionality analysis in Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016) and crystallised it in K.S. Puttaswamy (Privacy) v. Union of India (2017). The four‑step proportionality test requires: (i) a legitimate aim; (ii) a rational connection between the measure and the aim; (iii) necessity – the measure must be the least restrictive alternative; (iv) balancing – the benefit must outweigh the harm to the right.

In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) the Court applied proportionality to internet shutdowns in Jammu & Kashmir, holding that indefinite suspension fails the necessity and balancing stages. The Court mandated that orders under Section 144 CrPC or the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services Rules must be published, reasoned, and subject to periodic review. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) the Court struck down Section 66A of the IT Act for vagueness and disproportionality, emphasizing that the restriction must be narrowly tailored. The proportionality doctrine also informs the analysis of sedition (Section 124A IPC) in Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962) and its recent reconsideration in S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India (2022), where the Court directed the government to refrain from registering FIRs under the provision pending review.

Candidates should be able to map each ground of restriction to the proportionality steps. For instance, a law restricting speech on grounds of “public order” must demonstrate a concrete threat, not a speculative one, and must employ the least intrusive means (e.g., targeted takedown rather than blanket shutdown). The doctrine also applies to freedom of trade under Article 19(1)(g) read with Article 19(6), as seen in Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) where the Court upheld a ban on gutkha after finding it satisfied all four steps.

Interplay of DPSP and Fundamental Rights in Socio‑Economic Legislation: Minerva Mills, Unni Krishnan and the 42nd Amendment

The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to give primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights by inserting Article 31C (saving laws giving effect to Articles 39(b) and (c)) and by amending Article 368 to curtail judicial review. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) the Supreme Court struck down the portions of the 42nd Amendment that sought to make Directive Principles immune from judicial scrutiny, reaffirming that the basic structure includes the balance between Parts III and IV. The Court held that Article 31C could protect only laws genuinely implementing Articles 39(b) and (c), not any law vaguely linked to socio‑economic goals.

In Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993) the Court read Article 21 (right to life) with Article 41 (right to education) and Article 45 (early childhood care) to declare a fundamental right to education up to age 14. This judicial synthesis paved the way for the 86th Amendment (2002) inserting Article 21A. The Court in State of Tamil Nadu v. K. Shyam Sunder (2011) used the harmonious construction doctrine to uphold a law providing reservations in post‑graduate medical courses, balancing Article 15(4) with Article 38(2) (promote welfare). The 42nd Amendment’s attempt to elevate DPSP was thus neutralised by the basic structure doctrine, but the dialogue continues: recent judgments on the right to health (e.g., Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal 1996) treat DPSP as interpretive aids to expand the scope of Article 21.

For examination, the key analytical framework is: (i) identify the fundamental right invoked; (ii) locate the relevant Directive Principle; (iii) apply harmonious construction – neither part can be rendered nugatory; (iv) check whether the legislation falls within the protective umbrella of Article 31C (only Articles 39(b) and (c)); (v) assess basic structure compliance. This framework resolves apparent conflicts such as compulsory acquisition for public purpose (Article 300A) versus Directive Principles on equitable distribution (Article 39(b)).

  • Confusing the creamy layer test for OBC quotas with the EWS quota which has no creamy layer.
  • Assuming Article 16(4B) permanently raises the 50 % ceiling; it only permits carry‑forward of unfilled vacancies.
  • Treating Article 300A as a fundamental right enforceable under Article 32.
  • Applying the proportionality test mechanically without identifying the legitimate aim specific to each restriction ground.
  • Believing the 42nd Amendment gave Directive Principles supremacy over Fundamental Rights; Minerva Mills restored the balance.
  • Neglecting the requirement that Article 31C protection is limited to laws implementing Articles 39(b) and (c) only.
  • Review these traps carefully before the examination.
