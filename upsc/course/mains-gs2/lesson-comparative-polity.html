1. Comparative Constitutions

A constitution is the fundamental law of a state. Constitutions differ in source, flexibility, structure, and executive form. UPSC Mains frequently asks you to compare the Indian Constitution with those of other countries — not to rank them, but to identify what works where and why.

2. Written vs Unwritten Constitutions

Written constitution: A single codified document adopted by a constituent assembly and supreme over ordinary law. India (1950), the United States (1787), and France (1958) are written. Any law inconsistent with it can be struck down by the courts.

Unwritten constitution: No single document. The United Kingdom relies on statutes (Magna Carta, Bill of Rights), conventions (collective cabinet responsibility), common law, and authoritative works. Parliament is sovereign and can alter constitutional arrangements through ordinary legislation.

3. Rigid vs Flexible Constitutions

Rigid: Requires a special amendment procedure beyond ordinary lawmaking. India’s Article 368 governs amendments; some provisions need ratification by at least half the state legislatures. The US Constitution is rigid — amendments need two-thirds of both houses of Congress and ratification by three-fourths of the states.

Flexible: Can be amended by the ordinary legislative process. The UK is flexible — Parliament can change constitutional arrangements by a simple Act.

4. Federal vs Unitary Systems

Federal: Power is constitutionally divided between a central government and regional governments, each sovereign in its own sphere. The United States is a classic federation. India is federal in form but unitary in spirit — during emergencies, the Centre acquires overriding powers over states.

Unitary: Power is concentrated in a single central legislature. The United Kingdom is unitary — devolved administrations in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland exist only because Parliament permits them and can abolish them.

5. Parliamentary vs Presidential Systems

Parliamentary: The executive (Prime Minister and Cabinet) is drawn from the legislature and collectively responsible to it. India, the UK, Canada, and Australia follow this model. The government can fall if it loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha or House of Commons.

Presidential: The President is separately elected and serves a fixed term. The executive is not responsible to the legislature. The United States follows this model; Congress cannot remove the President except through impeachment.

Semi-presidential: France combines both — a directly elected President shares executive power with a Prime Minister responsible to the National Assembly.

6. Electoral Systems

First-Past-The-Post (FPTP): The candidate with the most votes in a constituency wins, even without a majority. Used in India (Lok Sabha), the UK (House of Commons), and the US (House of Representatives). It tends to produce stable governments but can exaggerate the winner’s seat share.

Proportional Representation (PR): Seats are allocated in proportion to votes received. Used for India’s Rajya Sabha (single transferable vote) and presidential election. It better reflects voter preferences but can produce fragmented legislatures and unstable coalitions.

Mixed-Member Proportional (MMP): Combines constituency seats with party-list seats to make the overall result proportional. Used in Germany and New Zealand.

7. Constitutional Bodies

Constitutional bodies derive authority directly from the Constitution, not from ordinary law, which insulates them from the executive.

  • Election Commission of India (Article 324): Superintends, directs, and controls elections to Parliament, state legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President. Modelled partly on the UK’s Electoral Commission but with far wider powers in India.
  • Union Public Service Commission (Articles 315–323): Conducts examinations for appointment to Union services and advises the President on disciplinary matters. Comparable to the US Office of Personnel Management but constitutionally entrenched.
  • Finance Commission (Article 280): Recommends distribution of tax revenues between the Centre and states, appointed every five years. Germany has a similar body — the Council of Fiscal Equalization — for centre–Länder transfers.
  • Comptroller and Auditor General (Article 148): Audits all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India and states. The US equivalent is the Government Accountability Office (GAO), but the CAG’s constitutional status gives it stronger protection.
  • National Commissions (SC, ST, OBC): Articles 338, 338A, and 338B establish commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes with powers of a civil court to investigate violations of safeguards.
Technique: Constitution comparison
Question: “The Indian Constitution is federal in form but unitary in substance. Comment.”
Answer: India has federal features — dual government, division of powers (Seventh Schedule), written constitution, independent judiciary. But it has unitary bias — Article 356 (President’s Rule), emergency provisions (Articles 352–360), single citizenship, and Parliament’s power to alter state boundaries (Article 3). During normal times it functions federally; during emergencies it becomes unitary. This flexibility is deliberate, not a defect.
Technique: Electoral system choice
Question: Why does India use FPTP for Lok Sabha but PR for Rajya Sabha?
Answer: FPTP in Lok Sabha gives voters a clear local representative and tends to produce stable majorities in the directly elected house. PR (single transferable vote) in Rajya Sabha ensures parties are represented in proportion to legislative strength, giving smaller parties and regional voices a place in the upper house.

Common Traps

  • Calling the UK constitution “unwritten” as if it does not exist — it is uncodified, not absent.
  • Assuming India is purely federal — remember the unitary bias during emergencies.
  • Confusing constitutional bodies (created by the Constitution) with statutory bodies (created by an Act of Parliament, e.g., the National Human Rights Commission).
  • Thinking FPTP always produces a majority — it can produce a hung parliament just as PR can.

Memory Tricks

  • WRUF: Written-Rigid-United-Federal — recall India’s key constitutional features.
  • Compare by axis: Source (written/unwritten), Flexibility (rigid/flexible), Structure (federal/unitary), Executive (parliamentary/presidential).
  • Constitutional bodies = created by Constitution; Statutory bodies = created by Statute.
Mains exam-day strategy
For comparative polity questions, anchor your answer to the Indian Constitution first, then bring in the foreign example. Use articles (e.g., Article 324 for the Election Commission) and schedules (e.g., Seventh Schedule for federal distribution). A comparison without constitutional anchoring scores poorly.

8. Basic Structure Doctrine: The Judicial Check on Constitutional Amendments

In constitutional law, the concept of basic structure represents a significant limitation on the amending power of the legislature. While Article 368 grants Parliament the authority to amend any part of the Constitution, it does not explicitly state that this power is absolute. The Supreme Court intervened to interpret this limit, establishing that certain features are so fundamental to the identity and spirit of the Constitution that they cannot be altered, even by a special majority.

The genesis of this doctrine lies in the Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) case. In this landmark judgment, a 13-judge bench of the Supreme Court held that while Parliament could amend the Constitution, it could not destroy its "basic structure." This was a direct response to the 42nd Amendment Act of 1976, which had attempted to place the power of amendment beyond judicial review. The Court struck down this clause, asserting that the power of amendment is not unlimited.

The basic structure includes several identifiable features:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution: The Constitution stands at the apex, and all laws must conform to it.
  • Sovereign, Democratic, Republican Character: India's status as a sovereign nation with a democratic form of government cannot be changed.
  • Secularism: Secularism is an integral part of the basic structure, derived from Preamble values and historical context.
  • Federal Character: The balance between the Centre and States cannot be destroyed; unitary bias during emergencies must remain within limits.
  • Separation of Powers: While India has a parliamentary system, the distinct functions of Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary must be maintained to prevent tyranny.
  • Rule of Law: No one is above the law, and due process must be followed.
  • Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Art. 21): The core protection of individual rights cannot be abrogated.

This doctrine effectively created a "super-legislature" check on the amending power. If an amendment violates the basic structure, it is void ab initio. For instance, in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Court reinforced secularism as part of the basic structure, invalidating the imposition of President's Rule in Karnataka on grounds that it violated this core principle.

Technique: The Amendment Test
Question: "Can Parliament amend Article 369 to change the distribution of powers between Centre and States?"
Solution: First, identify if the amendment touches the federal character (basic structure). If yes, check if it destroys the balance. Even with a special majority, if it alters the federal essence, the Court will strike it down under Kesavananda Bharati.
Exam-Day Strategy
When answering questions on constitutional amendments, always anchor your argument in Kesavananda Bharati. Mention that Article 368 is not a "parchment barrier" but is subject to judicial review. Use the phrase "integral part of the basic structure" to denote non-amendable features like Secularism and Federalism.

9. Parliamentary Privileges vs. Parliamentary Immunity: A Comparative Analysis

In parliamentary democracies, the functioning of the legislature relies on specific rights granted to its members and officers. These are broadly categorized into privileges, which are powers or immunities enjoyed by the House as a collective body, and immunities, which protect individual members from legal action for their speeches or votes within the House.

The distinction is crucial for understanding the limits of parliamentary sovereignty. In India, these privileges were historically inherited from the British Parliament but are now codified in the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha. Unlike the UK, where privileges evolved through case law (e.g., Rex v. The Speaker), India's privileges are largely statutory and conventional, subject to judicial scrutiny.

Key Privileges include:

  • Prior Notice: Members must give notice of private members' bills or motions (40 days for bills). This prevents the House from being overwhelmed by surprise legislation.
  • Right to Speak and Debate: A member cannot be legally prosecuted for anything said in the House. This ensures free speech without fear of defamation suits.
  • Right to Question Ministers: Members can question the executive on policy matters, a cornerstone of accountability.
  • Exemption from Arrest during Sessions: MPs cannot be arrested in civil cases while Parliament is in session (Art. 105), though they can be summoned for contempt proceedings.

Immunities vs. Privileges: Immunity is personal to the MP, protecting their speech and vote. Privilege is collective, protecting the dignity of the House. For example, if a member insults another member in the House, it is a privilege matter for the Speaker to decide, not a criminal offense under IPC. However, if an MP is caught outside the House committing a crime, they are not immune.

Comparison with the US: In the US Congress, members enjoy similar immunity (Speech and Debate Clause), but the scope of privilege is narrower. The US system relies heavily on internal committee investigations rather than judicial intervention for contempt issues, whereas India's Speaker has quasi-judicial powers to adjudicate contempt cases.

The Role of the Speaker: In India, the Speaker acts as the guardian of privileges. Their decisions are final unless challenged in court on grounds of violation of natural justice. The Supreme Court has held that the Speaker's power is not absolute and must be exercised fairly.

10. The Anti-Defection Law: Stability vs. Representation

The Tenth Schedule of the Constitution, introduced via the 52nd Amendment Act (1985), was enacted to curb "horse-trading" and political instability in Indian legislatures. Before this, governments frequently fell due to individual MPs switching sides for personal gain. The law disqualifies members if they voluntarily give up party membership or vote against the party whip without permission.

Mechanisms of Disqualification:

  1. Voting against Whip: If an MP votes contrary to the party's position, they are disqualified unless a 1/3rd majority of the party supports them in a meeting.
  2. Joining Another Party: An independent member or nominated member joining another party is automatically disqualified. A party member joining another party after nomination is disqualified unless a 2/3rd majority of the original party agrees to the transfer.
  3. Voluntary Resignation: Simply resigning from the party and joining another one leads to disqualification.

Authority to Decide: Originally, the Governor decided on disqualification. However, the 91st Amendment (2003) transferred this power to the Election Commission of India. This was done to ensure impartiality, as Governors were often seen as partisan appointees. The EC now decides within two months of a complaint being lodged.

Exceptions: The law allows for exceptions in cases of "merger" of parties. If a smaller party merges with a larger one, and the majority members agree, their disqualification is waived. This prevents small parties from being penalized for strategic alliances that strengthen democracy.

Criticism and Impact: Critics argue that the law discourages MPs from representing local constituents if their views differ from the party line, effectively turning them into "party robots."
