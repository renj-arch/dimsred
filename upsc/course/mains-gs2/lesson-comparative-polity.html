Key Concepts

Comparative Constitutions

A constitution is the fundamental law of a state. Constitutions differ in source, flexibility, structure, and executive form. UPSC Mains frequently asks you to compare the Indian Constitution with those of other countries — not to rank them, but to identify what works where and why.

Written vs Unwritten Constitutions

Written constitution: A single codified document adopted by a constituent assembly and supreme over ordinary law. India (1950), the United States (1787), and France (1958) are written. Any law inconsistent with it can be struck down by the courts.

Unwritten constitution: No single document. The United Kingdom relies on statutes (Magna Carta, Bill of Rights), conventions (collective cabinet responsibility), common law, and authoritative works. Parliament is sovereign and can alter constitutional arrangements through ordinary legislation.

Rigid vs Flexible Constitutions

Rigid: Requires a special amendment procedure beyond ordinary lawmaking. India’s Article 368 governs amendments; some provisions need ratification by at least half the state legislatures. The US Constitution is rigid — amendments need two-thirds of both houses of Congress and ratification by three-fourths of the states.

Flexible: Can be amended by the ordinary legislative process. The UK is flexible — Parliament can change constitutional arrangements by a simple Act.

Federal vs Unitary Systems

Federal: Power is constitutionally divided between a central government and regional governments, each sovereign in its own sphere. The United States is a classic federation. India is federal in form but unitary in spirit — during emergencies, the Centre acquires overriding powers over states.

Unitary: Power is concentrated in a single central legislature. The United Kingdom is unitary — devolved administrations in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland exist only because Parliament permits them and can abolish them.

Parliamentary vs Presidential Systems

Parliamentary: The executive (Prime Minister and Cabinet) is drawn from the legislature and collectively responsible to it. India, the UK, Canada, and Australia follow this model. The government can fall if it loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha or House of Commons.

Presidential: The President is separately elected and serves a fixed term. The executive is not responsible to the legislature. The United States follows this model; Congress cannot remove the President except through impeachment.

Semi-presidential: France combines both — a directly elected President shares executive power with a Prime Minister responsible to the National Assembly.

Electoral Systems

First-Past-The-Post (FPTP): The candidate with the most votes in a constituency wins, even without a majority. Used in India (Lok Sabha), the UK (House of Commons), and the US (House of Representatives). It tends to produce stable governments but can exaggerate the winner’s seat share.

Proportional Representation (PR): Seats are allocated in proportion to votes received. Used for India’s Rajya Sabha (single transferable vote) and presidential election. It better reflects voter preferences but can produce fragmented legislatures and unstable coalitions.

Mixed-Member Proportional (MMP): Combines constituency seats with party-list seats to make the overall result proportional. Used in Germany and New Zealand.

Constitutional Bodies

Constitutional bodies derive authority directly from the Constitution, not from ordinary law, which insulates them from the executive.

  • Election Commission of India (Article 324): Superintends, directs, and controls elections to Parliament, state legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President. Modelled partly on the UK’s Electoral Commission but with far wider powers in India.
  • Union Public Service Commission (Articles 315–323): Conducts examinations for appointment to Union services and advises the President on disciplinary matters. Comparable to the US Office of Personnel Management but constitutionally entrenched.
  • Finance Commission (Article 280): Recommends distribution of tax revenues between the Centre and states, appointed every five years. Germany has a similar body — the Council of Fiscal Equalization — for centre–Länder transfers.
  • Comptroller and Auditor General (Article 148): Audits all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India and states. The US equivalent is the Government Accountability Office (GAO), but the CAG’s constitutional status gives it stronger protection.
  • National Commissions (SC, ST, OBC): Articles 338, 338A, and 338B establish commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes with powers of a civil court to investigate violations of safeguards.
Technique: Constitution comparison
Question: “The Indian Constitution is federal in form but unitary in substance. Comment.”
Answer: India has federal features — dual government, division of powers (Seventh Schedule), written constitution, independent judiciary. But it has unitary bias — Article 356 (President’s Rule), emergency provisions (Articles 352–360), single citizenship, and Parliament’s power to alter state boundaries (Article 3). During normal times it functions federally; during emergencies it becomes unitary. This flexibility is deliberate, not a defect.
Technique: Electoral system choice
Question: Why does India use FPTP for Lok Sabha but PR for Rajya Sabha?
Answer: FPTP in Lok Sabha gives voters a clear local representative and tends to produce stable majorities in the directly elected house. PR (single transferable vote) in Rajya Sabha ensures parties are represented in proportion to legislative strength, giving smaller parties and regional voices a place in the upper house.

Common Traps

  • Calling the UK constitution “unwritten” as if it does not exist — it is uncodified, not absent.
  • Assuming India is purely federal — remember the unitary bias during emergencies.
  • Confusing constitutional bodies (created by the Constitution) with statutory bodies (created by an Act of Parliament, e.g., the National Human Rights Commission).
  • Thinking FPTP always produces a majority — it can produce a hung parliament just as PR can.

Memory Tricks

  • WRUF: Written-Rigid-United-Federal — recall India’s key constitutional features.
  • Compare by axis: Source (written/unwritten), Flexibility (rigid/flexible), Structure (federal/unitary), Executive (parliamentary/presidential).
  • Constitutional bodies = created by Constitution; Statutory bodies = created by Statute.
Mains exam-day strategy
For comparative polity questions, anchor your answer to the Indian Constitution first, then bring in the foreign example. Use articles (e.g., Article 324 for the Election Commission) and schedules (e.g., Seventh Schedule for federal distribution). A comparison without constitutional anchoring scores poorly.
