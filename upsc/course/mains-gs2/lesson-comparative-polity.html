Section 1: Comparative Constitutional Architecture

1.1 Sources and Forms of Constitutional Authority

The Indian Constitution derives its authority from a constituent assembly elected on a limited franchise (the 1946 provincial elections), adopting the document on 26 November 1949 and bringing it into force on 26 January 1950 [Source: Constitution of India, Preamble; Granville Austin, The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation, 1966]. The Preamble declares "We, the people of India" — a claim to popular sovereignty that contrasts with the UK, where constitutional authority rests on parliamentary sovereignty and a collection of statutes, conventions, and common law [Source: A.V. Dicey, Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution, 1885; Vernon Bogdanor, The New British Constitution, 2009]. India's Constitution is a single written document that is supreme over ordinary legislation. The United States Constitution (1787) shares this written, supreme character but was ratified by state conventions rather than a constituent assembly [Source: Pauline Maier, Ratification: The People Debate the Constitution, 1787–1788, 2010].

France's 1958 Constitution was approved by referendum (82.6% yes) under Article 89 of the 1946 Constitution, establishing the Fifth Republic after the Algerian crisis brought down the Fourth Republic. Charles de Gaulle's vision of a strong executive shaped its semi-presidential character. Canada's Constitution Act, 1867 (originally British North America Act) was an Act of the UK Parliament; patriation in 1982 via the Canada Act 1982 (UK) and Constitution Act 1982 (Canada) gave Canada full amendment autonomy, adding the Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Australia's Constitution (1901) was also a UK Act (Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900); the Australia Acts 1986 (UK and Australia) severed remaining UK legislative authority, but the Constitution itself remains an Act of the UK Parliament — a unique "statutory constitution" [Source: Australia Acts 1986; Helen Irving, To Constitute a Nation, 1999].

Key Distinction: Written vs. Unwritten (Uncodified)
India, US, France (1958), Canada (1867/1982), Australia (1901) have written constitutions — single or principal documents enacted by a constituent power. The UK, New Zealand, and Israel have uncodified constitutions — no single document; constitutional rules are scattered across statutes (Magna Carta 1215, Bill of Rights 1689, Act of Settlement 1701, Parliament Acts 1911/1949, European Communities Act 1972, Human Rights Act 1998, Scotland Act 1998), conventions (collective cabinet responsibility, monarch acts on advice, Salisbury Convention, Ponsonby Rule), common law (royal prerogatives, judicial review principles, Wednesbury unreasonableness), and authoritative works (Dicey, Bagehot, Jennings, Brazier).
Mains hook: "The UK constitution is uncodified, not unwritten." Use this precision in answers. Source: Bogdanor, The New British Constitution, 2009.

1.2 Rigidity vs. Flexibility: Amendment Procedures

Constitutional rigidity refers to the requirement of a special procedure for amendment, distinct from ordinary legislation. India's Article 368 provides three tiers: (i) simple majority of Parliament for certain provisions (e.g., creation of new states under Article 3, citizenship under Article 11, parliamentary privileges under Article 105, salaries/allowances under Article 81/181); (ii) special majority (two-thirds of members present and voting, and a majority of total membership of each House) for most amendments; (iii) special majority plus ratification by at least half the state legislatures for provisions affecting the federal structure (Articles 54, 55, 73, 162, 241, 279A, Chapter IV of Part V, Chapter V of Part VI, Chapter I of Part XI, any of the Lists in Seventh Schedule, representation of States in Parliament, Article 368 itself) [Source: Constitution of India, Article 368; Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, (1973) 4 SCC 225].

The US Constitution (Article V) is among the most rigid: amendments require two-thirds of both Houses of Congress or a convention called by two-thirds of state legislatures (never used), followed by ratification by three-fourths of states (38 of 50) — either by state legislatures or state conventions (used only for 21st Amendment). Only 27 amendments have succeeded in over 230 years; the first 10 (Bill of Rights) ratified 1791; 27th Amendment (congressional pay) took 202 years (proposed 1789, ratified 1992). The Equal Rights Amendment (1972) failed ratification despite 35/38 states — time limits and rescissions created constitutional uncertainty [Source: US Constitution, Article V; John Vile, The Constitutional Amending Process in American Political Thought, 1992].

Canada's Constitution Act, 1982 (Part V) establishes a general "7/50" formula (Section 38): resolutions of the Senate, House of Commons, and legislative assemblies of at least two-thirds of provinces (7) representing at least 50% of the population; unanimous consent (Section 41) for certain matters (monarchy, language rights, composition of Supreme Court, amending formula itself, offices of Queen/Governor General/Lieutenant Governors). The Meech Lake Accord (1987) and Charlottetown Accord (1992) failed — demonstrating the difficulty of constitutional change. Section 44 allows Parliament to amend exclusively federal matters; Section 45 allows provinces to amend provincial constitutions [Source: Constitution Act, 1982, Sections 38, 41, 44, 45; Peter Hogg, Constitutional Law of Canada, 5th ed.].

Australia (Section 128) requires an absolute majority in both Houses of Parliament (or one House twice if the other refuses), then approval by a majority of voters nationwide and a majority of voters in a majority of states (4 of 6) — a double majority that has defeated 36 of 44 referendums (success rate ~18%). Notable failures: 1999 Republic referendum (55% no), 1974 simultaneous elections, 1988 rights referendum. The "states' rights" argument often defeats national proposals [Source: Australian Constitution, Section 128; George Williams & David Hume, People Power: The History and Future of the Referendum in Australia, 2010].

France (Article 89) requires identical adoption by both Houses of Parliament (National Assembly and Senate), then either a referendum or a three-fifths majority of the two Houses meeting in Congress at Versailles (used for most amendments since 1995). The President can choose the referendum path (used for 2000 quinquennat amendment reducing presidential term from 7 to 5 years). 2008 amendment added QPC (priority preliminary ruling on constitutionality) [Source: French Constitution, Article 89; Guy Carcassonne, La Constitution, 2016].

The UK is uniquely flexible: Parliament can alter any constitutional arrangement by a simple Act of Parliament (e.g., European Communities Act 1972, Human Rights Act 1998, Scotland Act 1998, European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018, Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (repealed 2022)). No special procedure, no judicial review of constitutionality. This flexibility enabled Brexit via ordinary legislation but creates no entrenched protections [Source: A.V. Dicey, Law of the Constitution; House of Commons Library, Constitutional Reform since 1997, 2021].

Source: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, (1973) 4 SCC 225; Minerva Mills v. Union of India, (1980) 3 SCC 625.
PYQ 2018 (10 marks)
"Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is a limited power and it cannot be enlarged into absolute power." In light of this statement, explain whether Parliament under Article 368 can destroy the basic structure of the Constitution by expanding its amending power.
Answer must cite: Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — basic structure doctrine; Minerva Mills (1980) — judicial review is basic structure; Waman Rao (1981) — prospective application; I.R. Coelho (2007) — Ninth Schedule laws after 1973 subject to basic structure test. Conclusion: Parliament cannot destroy basic structure; amending power under Art 368 is constituent but limited. Source: UPSC Mains 2018 GS II Q2.
PYQ 2014 (15 marks)
"Constitutional amendments in India are neither too rigid nor too flexible." Critically examine.
Three-tier Art 368: simple majority (Art 3, 11, 169), special majority (most), special majority + state ratification (federal provisions). 105 amendments in 73 years — balance struck. Basic structure (Kesavananda) adds judicial check. Compare: US (27 in 230 yrs — too rigid), UK (unlimited flexibility — no entrenchment), Australia (double majority — 18% success). India's mix suits diversity and federalism. UPSC Mains 2014 GS II Q2.

1.3 Separation of Powers: Theory and Practice

Montesquieu's tripartite separation (legislative, executive, judicial) is a guiding principle but manifests differently. The US Constitution (Articles I, II, III) embodies a strict separation with checks and balances: Congress legislates, the President executes, the Supreme Court adjudicates; each branch has veto/override/appointment powers over the others. Madison in Federalist 47: "The accumulation of all powers... in the same hands... may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny" [Source: Montesquieu, The Spirit of the Laws, 1748; Federalist Nos. 47–51 (Madison/Hamilton/Jay)].

India follows the parliamentary model where the executive is drawn from and responsible to the legislature (Article 74: Council of Ministers headed by PM aids and advises President; Article 75: collective responsibility to Lok Sabha). Separation is functional, not personnel-based. The judiciary is independent (Articles 124, 217) but appointments involve the executive (collegium system evolved from Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India, 1993; NJAC Act 2014 struck down in 2015 as violating judicial independence — basic structure) [Source: Constitution of India, Articles 74, 75, 124, 217; Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India, (1993) 4 SCC 441; National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014 (struck down in Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India, 2015)].

The UK has a fusion of powers: the Prime Minister and Cabinet are members of Parliament; the Lord Chancellor (until 2005) straddled all three branches. The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 created the UK Supreme Court, separating the judiciary from the House of Lords. Executive dominates legislature via party discipline (payroll vote, whips) — "elective dictatorship" (Hailsham) [Source: Constitutional Reform Act 2005; Bogdanor, The New British Constitution].

Canada and Australia follow the Westminster model like India, but with a federal division of powers. France's 1958 Constitution creates a semi-presidential system: the President (directly elected) appoints the Prime Minister, who is responsible to the National Assembly (Article 21). Dual executive: President sets broad direction (foreign policy, defence), PM runs day-to-day government. Cohabitation (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002) tests this balance [Source: French Constitution, Articles 5, 8, 21; Maurice Duverger, A New Political System Model: Semi-Presidential Government, 1980].

PYQ Deconstruction: Separation of Powers (2019, 15 marks)
"Do you think that Constitution of India does not accept principle of strict separation of powers rather it is based on principle of 'checks and balances'? Explain."
Structure: (1) Thesis — India adopts parliamentary fusion with checks, not Montesquieu's strict separation. (2) Constitutional provisions — Arts 74, 75 (executive from legislature), 122/212 (parliamentary privileges), 124/217 (judicial independence). (3) Judicial interpretation — Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955): "Indian Constitution has not recognised the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity"; Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): basic structure includes separation of powers as a check, not a rigid wall. (4) Checks and balances examples — judicial review of legislation (Kesavananda), President's pocket veto, Parliament's power to amend (Art 368) subject to basic structure, legislative control over executive (Question Hour, No-confidence). (5) Conclusion — functional separation with checks suits India's plural society; strict separation would paralyse governance. Source: UPSC Mains 2019 GS II Q3; Granville Austin, The Indian Constitution.

1.4 Constitutional Supremacy vs. Parliamentary Sovereignty

The doctrine of constitutional supremacy holds that the Constitution is the highest law; any law inconsistent with it is void. India (Article 13), the US (Marbury v. Madison, 1803), Canada (Section 52, Constitution Act 1982), Australia (Section 109 + implied freedom cases), and France (Article 61 — Constitutional Council review) all embrace constitutional supremacy [Source: Constitution of India, Article 13; Marbury v. Madison, 5 US (1 Cranch) 137 (1803); Constitution Act 1982, Section 52; Australian Capital Television v. Commonwealth, (1992) 177 CLR 106; French Constitution, Article 61].

The UK uniquely adheres to parliamentary sovereignty: Parliament can make or unmake any law; no court can strike down an Act of Parliament. The Human Rights Act 1998 allows courts to issue "declarations of incompatibility" (Section 4) but cannot invalidate legislation. Jackson v. Attorney General (2005) left open whether courts might refuse to apply an Act that abolished judicial review or democratic elections — a potential "common law constitutionalism" limit. The Miller cases (2017, 2019) showed courts can review executive prerogative powers affecting parliamentary sovereignty [Source: A.V. Dicey, Law of the Constitution; Human Rights Act 1998, Section 4; Jackson v. Attorney General, [2005] UKHL 56; R (Miller) v. Secretary of State for Exiting the EU, [2017] UKSC 5; R (Miller) v. Prime Minister, [2019] UKSC 41].

Section 2: Federalism, Executive Systems & Judicial Review

2.1 Federalism: Design, Distribution, and Dynamics

Federalism divides sovereignty between at least two levels of government, each constitutionally autonomous in its sphere. K.C. Wheare's "classical federalism" requires: (1) written constitution, (2) independent judiciary, (3) division of powers, (4) supremacy of constitution, (5) amendment requiring federal consensus [Source: K.C. Wheare, Federal Government, 4th ed., 1963]. No country meets all criteria perfectly. Modern scholarship (Watts, Elazar, Lijphart) treats federalism as a spectrum — "federalism" is not a binary but a continuum of shared rule and self-rule.

2.1.1 India: Quasi-Federal with Unitary Bias

The Indian Constitution establishes a dual polity (Centre and States) with a three-fold legislative distribution in the Seventh Schedule: Union List (List I, originally 97 subjects, now 100 after 42nd/101st Amendments — taxes on services, GST), State List (List II, 61 subjects — police, public health, agriculture, land, water), Concurrent List (List III, 52 subjects — criminal law, marriage, contracts, education, forests, electricity, population control) [Source: Constitution of India, Seventh Schedule; 42nd Amendment (1976), 101st Amendment (2016 — GST)]. Residual powers vest in Parliament (Article 248) — unlike US (residual to states) or Canada (residual to Centre via POGG).

Unitary features: (i) Single citizenship (Article 5–11) — no state citizenship; (ii) Single integrated judiciary (Articles 124–147, 214–231) — HCs under SC; (iii) All-India Services (Article 312) — IAS/IPS serve Centre and States; (iv) Emergency provisions (Articles 352, 356, 360) convert federation into unitary system; (v) Governor as Centre's agent (Articles 155–156); (vi) Parliament's power to reorganise states (Article 3) by simple majority; (vii) Integrated audit (CAG, Article 148) and election machinery (ECI, Article 324); (viii) Centre's control over state legislation via Governor's reservation (Art 200) and President's assent; (ix) Art 249 (RS resolution) and Art 250 (emergency) allow Parliament to legislate on State List.

Granville Austin called this "cooperative federalism" — the Centre and States are partners, not rivals [Source: Granville Austin, The Indian Constitution, Ch. 8]. The Sarkaria Commission (1988) and Punchhi Commission (2010) recommended strengthening intergovernmental mechanisms (Inter-State Council, Article 263) and restricting Article 356 misuse [Source: Sarkaria Commission Report, 1988; Punchhi Commission Report, 2010]. The GST Council (Art 279A, 101st Amendment 2016) is a new federal institution — a constitutional body where Centre and States jointly decide tax rates, exemplifying "pooled sovereignty." Asymmetric federalism: Art 370 (J&K — abrogated 2019), Art 371A-J (special provisions for NE states, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Andhra, Karnataka) [Source: Constitution of India, Arts. 370, 371A-J; 5th/6th Schedules for tribal areas].

2.1.2 United States: Dual Federalism to Cooperative Federalism

The US Constitution enumerates federal powers (Article I, Section 8 — 18 clauses including commerce, tax, defence, currency, post office, patents, copyrights, bankruptcy, naturalisation, weights/measures); the Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers to states. The "police power" (health, safety, morals, education) is the core state domain [Source: US Constitution, Art. I, Sec. 8, Amend. X; McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 US (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819); Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 US (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824)].

Evolution: Dual federalism (layer cake, pre-1930s) → Cooperative federalism (marble cake, New Deal era, Wickard v. Filburn, 1942 — Commerce Clause expansion) → New Federalism (Reagan era, devolution, block grants) → Coercive federalism (conditional grants, South Dakota v. Dole, 1987 — drinking age 21 tied to highway funds) → Modern competitive federalism (states as laboratories, New State Ice Co. v. Liebmann, 1932, Brandeis J. dissent) [Source: Morton Grodzins, The American System, 1966; Timothy Conlan, From New Federalism to Devolution, 1998].

Key cases: McCulloch (implied powers, supremacy — "power to tax is power to destroy"); Gibbons (broad commerce power — navigation is commerce); Wickard (aggregation principle — even local wheat affects interstate commerce); Lopez (1995, limits on Commerce Clause — Gun-Free School Zones Act struck); Morrison (2000, VAWA civil remedy struck); NFIB v. Sebelius (2012, ACA individual mandate upheld as tax, not commerce; Medicaid expansion coercion limited) [Source: US Supreme Court Reports; Chemerinsky, Constitutional Law, 6th ed.].

Fiscal federalism: No constitutional finance commission. Federal grants-in-aid (categorical, block) create vertical fiscal imbalance. States have broad tax autonomy (sales, income, property) but no GST-style unified system. Unfunded mandates (Clean Air Act, ADA) strain state budgets [Source: Advisory Commission on Intergovernmental Relations (ACIR) reports].

2.1.3 Canada: Strong Provincial Autonomy

The Constitution Act, 1867 (Sections 91–95) divides powers: federal (peace, order, good government — POGG; trade & commerce; criminal law; banking; fisheries; patents; copyright; marriage/divorce; residual), provincial (property & civil rights; health; education; local works; municipalities; direct taxation; natural resources — Sec 92A added 1982), and concurrent (agriculture, immigration, old age pensions — federal paramountcy). The POGG power has three branches: emergency, national concern, and residual [Source: Constitution Act 1867, Secs. 91–95; Reference re Anti-Inflation Act, [1976] 2 SCR 373; R. v. Crown Zellerbach, [1988] 1 SCR 401].

Provinces control natural resources (Section 92A) and have opt-out rights with compensation for shared-cost programs (Section 40, 1982 Act). Quebec's distinct status and the "compact theory" (federation as a compact between founding peoples) make Canadian federalism uniquely asymmetrical. The Reference re Secession of Quebec (1998) established four principles: federalism, democracy, constitutionalism/rule of law, minority rights — secession requires negotiation. Clarity Act (2000) sets federal conditions for secession referendum [Source: Constitution Act 1982, Secs. 92A, 40; Reference re Secession of Quebec, [1998] 2 SCR 217; Kenneth McRoberts, Misconceiving Canada, 1997].

Equalisation Program (Section 36, Constitution Act 1982) — federal transfers to ensure comparable services at comparable taxation. Calculated by Finance Canada; "have-not" provinces receive payments. 2007 Expert Panel recommended reforms. Asymmetric: Quebec has unique immigration agreement, civil law system, distinct society recognition [Source: Constitution Act 1982, Sec. 36; Federal-Provincial Fiscal Arrangements Act].

2.1.4 Australia: Centralising Fiscal Federalism

The Constitution (Sections 51, 52, 109) gives the Commonwealth enumerated powers (trade & commerce, corporations, external affairs, taxation, marriage, immigration, aliens, currency, banking, insurance); states retain residual powers. Section 96 (financial assistance grants) and uniform income tax (since 1942, South Australia v. Commonwealth, 1942) have made the Commonwealth fiscally dominant — states rely on ~45% of revenue from Commonwealth grants [Source: Australian Constitution, Secs. 51, 52, 96, 109; South Australia v. Commonwealth (1942) 65 CLR 373; Commonwealth Grants Commission].

The "external affairs" power (Section 51(xxix)) allows the Commonwealth to implement treaties domestically, encroaching on state domains (e.g., Tasmanian Dam Case, 1983 — World Heritage Convention used to stop Franklin Dam). The Engineers' Case (1920) ended "implied intergovernmental immunities" and established broad Commonwealth powers. Williams v. Commonwealth (2014) limited executive spending power without legislation (school chaplaincy program). COAG (Council of Australian Governments, 1992–2020) replaced by National Cabinet (2020) for intergovernmental coordination [Source: Commonwealth v. Tasmania (1983) 158 CLR 1; Amalgamated Society of Engineers v. Adelaide Steamship Co (1920) 28 CLR 129; Williams v. Commonwealth (2014) 252 CLR 416].

2.1.5 France: Unitary with Decentralisation

France is constitutionally unitary (Article 1: "France shall be an indivisible, secular, democratic and social Republic"). The 1982–83 decentralisation laws (Defferre Laws) created regions, departments, and communes with elected councils and executive powers. The 2003 constitutional revision (Article 72) recognised "decentralised organisation" but retained Parliament's power to define local powers. No region has legislative autonomy — only regulatory/executive [Source: French Constitution, Arts. 1, 72; Lois Defferre 1982–83; Constitutional Law 2003-276].

New Caledonia has a unique "shared sovereignty" status (Title XIII, 1998 Nouméa Accord) — the only sui generis autonomy in France. Corsica has special status (2002) but not legislative power. The 2024 constitutional bill for Corsican autonomy within Republic is pending. Overseas territories (DOM/COM) have varying autonomy. Central state retains control via prefects (representatives of central government in departments) [Source: French Constitution, Title XIII; Organic Law 99-209].

Approximate placement on centralisation-decentralisation continuum. India sits between France and Australia — federal in form, unitary in emergency. Canada is most decentralised among federations.
Mains Answer Framework: Federalism Comparison
For "Compare Indian and US/Canadian/Australian federalism": (1) Constitutional design — written vs. unwritten, division of powers (lists vs. enumeration vs. residual), amendment role of states. (2) Fiscal federalism — tax assignment, transfer mechanisms (Finance Commission vs. Equalisation Grants vs. Grants Commission), conditional grants. (3) Judicial interpretation — key cases (S.R. Bommai for India; McCulloch, Wickard for US; Reference re Secession for Canada; Engineers' Case for Australia). (4) Asymmetry — special provisions (Art 370/371 for India; Quebec for Canada; NT/AC for Australia). (5) Cooperative mechanisms — Inter-State Council (Art 263), Zonal Councils vs. Council of Federation (Russia), COAG (Australia), First Ministers' Conferences (Canada). Source: Watts, Comparing Federal Systems; Punchhi Commission Report.
PYQ 2019 (15 marks)
"The Indian Constitution is federal in form but unitary in spirit." Critically examine.
Federal features: Dual polity, 7th Schedule, written Constitution, independent judiciary, bicameralism (Rajya Sabha), Art 368 federal ratification. Unitary features: Single citizenship, integrated judiciary, AIS, emergency provisions (Art 352/356/360), Governor as Centre's agent, Art 3 (state reorganisation), residuary powers with Centre, integrated audit/elections. S.R. Bommai: Federalism = basic structure; Art 356 subject to judicial review. GST Council = cooperative federalism. Conclusion: Quasi-federal by design (Wheare); unitary bias is emergency safety valve, not defect. UPSC Mains 2019 GS II Q2.
PYQ 2021 (15 marks)
"The GST Council is a unique federal institution." Discuss.
Art 279A: Constitutional body; Centre (1/3 vote weight) + States (2/3); consensus-based (no voting yet); GST rate slabs, thresholds, exemptions. Embodies "pooled sovereignty" — neither Centre nor States alone can decide. Compare: Canada's Finance Ministers' meetings (informal); Australia's GST Council (COAG-based, not constitutional); US — no federal consumption tax. Challenges: compensation cess expiry (2022), rate rationalisation, dispute resolution. UPSC Mains 2021 GS II Q2.

2.2 Executive Systems: Parliamentary, Presidential, Semi-Presidential

2.2.1 Parliamentary System: India, UK, Canada, Australia

Core features: (i) Executive (Prime Minister + Cabinet) drawn from legislature; (ii) Collective responsibility to lower house (Lok Sabha, House of Commons); (iii) Head of state (President/Monarch/Governor-General) distinct from head of government; (iv) Government falls if it loses confidence; (v) Dissolution power with head of state on PM's advice (usually).

India (Articles 74, 75, 78, 85): President is constitutional head; real executive power vests in Council of Ministers headed by PM (Art 74(1)). PM must be member of either House (Art 75(5)). Lok Sabha confidence is essential (Art 75(3)). President's powers are exercisable only on aid and advice of Council of Ministers (Art 74(1), Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab, 1974). Ordinance-making (Art 123) — legislative power when Parliament not in session; must be approved within 6 weeks of reassembly [Source: Constitution of India, Arts. 74, 75, 78, 85, 123; Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab, (1974) 2 SCC 831].

UK: PM is leader of majority party in Commons; Monarch's role is ceremonial (conventions: appoint PM, dissolve Parliament on advice, assent to bills). No written constitution codifies these — they operate through conventions (Cabinet Manual 2011). Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (repealed 2022) briefly constrained dissolution. Collective responsibility: ministers must publicly support Cabinet decisions or resign (Westland affair 1986) [Source: Cabinet Manual, 2011; Brazier, Constitutional Practice, 3rd ed.].

Canada: Governor-General (Monarch's representative) acts on PM's advice; PM must hold confidence of House of Commons. The King-Byng Affair (1926) established that GG can refuse dissolution if alternative government exists. The 2008 prorogation controversy (PM Harper advised prorogation to avoid confidence vote) tested reserve powers. Party discipline stricter than UK — "party solidarity" [Source: King-Byng Affair, 1926; Forsey, Royal Power of Dissolution].

Australia: Governor-General has reserve powers (dismissal, refusal of dissolution) — exercised in 1975 (Whitlam dismissal by GG Kerr on advice of Opposition leader Fraser). The "Palace letters" (2020 release) confirmed Queen/UK not involved. PM must have confidence of House of Representatives; Senate can block supply (1975 crisis) [Source: Whitlam Dismissal, 1975; Kerr, Matters for Judgment].

2.2.2 Presidential System: United States

President is both head of state and head of government, directly elected (via Electoral College) for a fixed 4-year term (max two terms, 22nd Amendment). Separate election from Congress; no collective responsibility to legislature; Congress cannot remove President except by impeachment (Article I, Sections 2–3; Article II, Section 4) [Source: US Constitution, Art. II; 22nd Amendment; Myers v. United States, 272 US 52 (1926); Nixon v. United States, 506 US 224 (1993)].

Cabinet is appointed by President with Senate confirmation; members are not legislators (incompatibility clause, Article I, Section 6). President has veto power (override by 2/3 of each House). No confidence votes; fixed term provides stability but risks gridlock (divided government). War powers: President is Commander-in-Chief; War Powers Resolution 1973 requires congressional authorisation for sustained deployment. Signing statements (controversial interpretive assertions) [Source: War Powers Resolution, 1973; Youngstown Sheet & Tube v. Sawyer, 343 US 579 (1952)].

2.2.3 Semi-Presidential System: France

Directly elected President (5-year term, renewable once, 2000 referendum) shares executive with Prime Minister responsible to National Assembly (Article 21). "Cohabitation" occurs when President and Assembly majority are from opposing parties (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002) — then PM controls domestic policy, President retains foreign/defence (Article 52) [Source: French Constitution, Arts. 5, 8, 21, 52; Duverger, Semi-Presidential Government; Elgie, Semi-Presidentialism, 2011].

2000 reform (5-year presidential term aligned with legislative term) reduced cohabitation probability. President appoints PM (Art 8); PM directs government action (Art 21); Cabinet collectively responsible to NA (Art 49) — can be toppled by censure motion. President can dissolve NA (Art 12) but not Senate. Article 16: emergency powers (used 1961 Algerian crisis). Constitutional Council reviews constitutionality of laws (Art 61) [Source: French Constitution, Arts. 8, 12, 16, 21, 49, 52, 61].

PYQ Deconstruction: Parliamentary vs Presidential (2022, 15 marks)
"The Indian Parliament is not a sovereign body like the British Parliament." Critically examine this statement in the context of judicial review and federalism.
(1) UK Parliament sovereignty — Dicey: Parliament can make/unmake any law; no judicial review of primary legislation. (2) Indian Parliament — limited by: written Constitution (Art 13), judicial review (Kesavananda basic structure), federal distribution (Seventh Schedule), fundamental rights (Part III). (3) Minerva Mills: "Limited amending power is basic structure." (4) S.R. Bommai: Federalism is basic structure; Centre cannot undermine states. (5) Contrast: UK — HRA 1998 s.4 only declarations; India — courts strike down laws. (6) Conclusion: Indian Parliament is sovereign within constitutional limits; British Parliament is legally omnipotent. Source: UPSC Mains 2022 GS II Q2; Dicey; Austin; Bommai.
PYQ 2014 (15 marks)
"Presidential system is better suited for India than parliamentary system." Critically examine.
Arguments for: Stability (fixed term), direct mandate, separation of powers, decisive leadership. Arguments against: Diversity needs consensus (parliamentary suits plural society), basic structure includes parliamentary democracy (Kesavananda), fixed term risks policy paralysis if President lacks legislative support, no confidence mechanism for accountability, US gridlock example. Indian context: 28 states, 100+ parties, coalition culture — parliamentary allows flexibility. Conclusion: Parliamentary system with reforms (anti-defection, electoral funding) is better suited. UPSC Mains 2014 GS II Q3.

2.3 Judicial Review: Scope, Intensity, and Dialogue

2.3.1 India: Broad, Textually Anchored, Basic Structure

Article 13 declares laws inconsistent with fundamental rights void. Articles 32 (Supreme Court) and 226 (High Courts) provide writ jurisdiction. Article 136 (special leave) and 141 (law declared by SC binding) give the Court expansive reach. The basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda, 1973) empowers the Court to review constitutional amendments — a power not explicitly granted [Source: Constitution of India, Arts. 13, 32, 136, 141, 226; Kesavananda Bharati; Minerva Mills; I.R. Coelho].

Judicial activism: Maneka Gandhi (1978) — "procedure established by law" (Art 21) read as "due process"; Vishaka (1997) — guidelines for sexual harassment (legislative vacuum filled); PUCL (2003) — right to food; Navtej Singh Johar (2018) — decriminalisation of homosexuality; Anuradha Bhasin (2020) — internet shutdowns subject to proportionality; Puttaswamy (2017) — privacy as fundamental right [Source: Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, (1978) 1 SCC 248; Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, (1997) 6 SCC 241; PUCL v. Union of India, (2003) 4 SCC 399; Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, (2018) 10 SCC 1; Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, (2020) 3 SCC 637; Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1].

PIL (Public Interest Litigation) relaxed locus standi (S.P. Gupta, 1981; Bandhua Mukti Morcha, 1984) — any public-spirited person can approach Court for violation of rights of disadvantaged groups. This is uniquely Indian — no direct equivalent in US (standing requires injury-in-fact) or UK (sufficient interest test, narrower) [Source: S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, (1981) Supp SCC 87; Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, (1984) 3 SCC 161].

2.3.2 United States: Marbury Legacy, Political Questions, Standards of Review

Marbury v. Madison (1803) established judicial review. The Court uses tiered scrutiny: strict scrutiny (suspect classifications — race, national origin; fundamental rights — voting, travel, privacy), intermediate scrutiny (gender, illegitimacy), rational basis (economic regulation) [Source: United States v. Carolene Products, 304 US 144 (1938) fn.4; Craig v. Boren, 429 US 190 (1976); Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 US 702 (1997)].

Political question doctrine (Baker v. Carr, 1962) limits review of certain issues (foreign policy, impeachment procedures, partisan gerrymandering — Rucho v. Common Cause, 2019). Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health (2022) overturned Roe v. Wade (1973), demonstrating the Court's power to reverse precedent. The "shadow docket" (emergency orders without full briefing/argument) has become controversial [Source: Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization, 597 US ___ (2022); Baude, Foreword: The Supreme Court's Shadow Docket, 2021].

2.3.3 Canada: Charter, Section 1, Notwithstanding Clause

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) guarantees rights subject to "such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society" (Section 1) — the Oakes test (proportionality: pressing objective, rational connection, minimal impairment, proportionate effect) [Source: Canadian Charter, Secs. 1, 33; R. v. Oakes, [1986] 1 SCR 103]. Section 33 (notwithstanding clause) allows Parliament or provincial legislatures to override Sections 2 and 7–15 for five years (renewable) — a legislative "dialogue" model where legislature has final say on rights balancing [Source: Charter, Sec. 33; Hogg & Bushell, The Charter Dialogue Between Courts and Legislatures, 1997].

Key cases: R. v. Morgentaler (1988, abortion); Vriend v. Alberta (1998, sexual orientation); Reference re Same-Sex Marriage (2004); Carter v. Canada (2015, assisted dying) [Source: Supreme Court of Canada Reports].

2.3.4 Australia: Implied Freedoms, No Bill of Rights

No comprehensive bill of rights. The High Court has implied freedom of political communication from the representative government structure (Sections 7, 24) — Australian Capital Television (1992), Lange v. Australian Broadcasting Corporation (1997) — two-step test: (1) does law burden political communication? (2) is it reasonably appropriate and adapted to a legitimate end? [Source: Australian Constitution, Secs. 7, 24; Australian Capital Television v. Commonwealth, (1992) 177 CLR 106; Lange v. ABC, (1997) 189 CLR 520]. Section 116 (religious freedom) and Section 80 (trial by jury) are narrow. Section 109 resolves federal-state inconsistency in favour of federal law.

Statutory charters exist in ACT (2004), Victoria (2006), Queensland (2019) — but no federal bill of rights despite 1988 referendum rejection [Source: Australian Human Rights Commission].

2.3.5 UK: HRA 1998, Declarations of Incompatibility

Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates ECHR. Courts must interpret legislation compatibly "so far as it is possible to do so" (Section 3). If impossible, issue declaration of incompatibility (Section 4) — Parliament may but need not amend. R (Jackson) v. Attorney General (2005) suggested common law constitutionalism might limit parliamentary sovereignty in extreme cases [Source: Human Rights Act 1998, Secs. 3, 4; R (Jackson) v. Attorney General, [2005] UKHL 56].

2.3.6 France: Ex Ante Review, Constitutional Council

Constitutional Council (Conseil Constitutionnel) reviews legislation before promulgation (Article 61). Referral by President, PM, Presidents of both Houses, 60 deputies/senators. Since 2008 (QPC — question prioritaire de constitutionnalité), courts can refer constitutional questions from ongoing litigation. The Council is not a supreme court; it does not hear appeals. Decisions are binding on all authorities [Source: French Constitution, Arts. 61, 61-1; Constitutional Law 2008-724; Stone Sweet, Governing with Judges, 2000].

PYQ 2020 (15 marks)
"Judicial review in India is wider in scope than in the USA." Do you agree? Substantiate with relevant case laws.
(1) US: Marbury established review but limited by political question doctrine, standing, ripeness, mootness; tiered scrutiny; no review of constitutional amendments. (2) India: Art 13 (express), Art 32/226 (writs), Art 136 (SLP) — broader access; Kesavananda — review of amendments (basic structure); Maneka Gandhi — due process reading of Art 21; PIL (S.P. Gupta, 1981) — relaxed locus standi; I.R. Coelho — Ninth Schedule laws post-1973 reviewable. (3) Counterpoint: US strict scrutiny is rigorous; Indian "proportionality" (Modern Dental College, 2016) similar but less structured. (4) Verdict: India's scope is wider textually and procedurally; US review is deeper in rights jurisprudence. Source: UPSC Mains 2020 GS II Q3; Seervai, Constitutional Law of India; Chemerinsky, Constitutional Law.
PYQ 2017 (15 marks)
"Judicial activism vs judicial overreach." Discuss with reference to Indian and US experiences.
Activism: Filling legislative/executive vacuum (India: Vishaka, Vineet Narain, PUCL; US: Brown v. Board, Miranda). Overreach: Policy-making, encroaching on elected branches (India: Subramanian Swamy (2014) — SC on CBI; Justice Karnan contempt; US: Lochner era, Dobbs criticism). India's PIL is unique activist tool; US has stricter standing. Both face legitimacy questions when unelected judges make policy. UPSC Mains 2017 GS II Q3.

Section 3: Constitutional Bodies & Electoral Systems

3.1 Constitutional Bodies: Design, Independence, and Accountability

Constitutional bodies derive authority directly from the Constitution, insulating them from executive control. India has a uniquely dense architecture of constitutional bodies. Comparisons with counterparts reveal design choices.

3.1.1 Election Commission (ECI) — Article 324

ECI superintends, directs, and controls elections to Parliament, state legislatures, President, and Vice-President. Composition: Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) + up to two Election Commissioners (since 1989). Appointment by President (Art 324(2)); removal of CEC like Supreme Court Judge (impeachment), ECs by President on CEC's recommendation [Source: Constitution of India, Art. 324; Election Commission (Conditions of Service) Act, 1991; Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India, 2023 — SC directed collegium for appointments].

Comparison: US — no federal election commission; states run elections; Federal Election Commission (FEC, 1974) only regulates campaign finance. UK — Electoral Commission (2001, statutory) regulates party finance and referendums; elections run by local returning officers. Canada — Elections Canada (independent agency, Chief Electoral Officer appointed by House of Commons resolution). Australia — Australian Electoral Commission (statutory, 1984). France — Constitutional Council oversees presidential/legislative elections; Ministry of Interior administers [Source: US FEC; UK Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000; Canada Elections Act; Australian Electoral Commission Act 1984; French Constitution, Art. 58, 61].

3.1.2 Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) — Articles 315–323

UPSC conducts examinations for Union services; advises President on recruitment, promotions, disciplinary matters. Chairman/members appointed by President; removal by President on Supreme Court inquiry report (Art 317). States have SPSCs (Art 315). [Source: Constitution of India, Arts. 315–323; State of UP v. Rajesh Gautam, (2007) 2 SCC 532].

Comparison: US — Office of Personnel Management (OPM, executive agency, not constitutional); merit system since Pendleton Act 1883. UK — Civil Service Commissioners (statutory, independent); recruitment by open competition. Canada — Public Service Commission (statutory, reports to Parliament). Australia — Australian Public Service Commission (statutory). France — Civil service corps (grands corps) recruited via competitive exams (ENA, now INSP) [Source: US OPM; UK Civil Service Commissioners; Canada Public Service Employment Act; Australian Public Service Act 1999; French fonction publique].

3.1.3 Finance Commission (FC) — Article 280

Constituted every five years; recommends distribution of net tax proceeds between Centre and states (vertical sharing) and among states (horizontal formula: population, area, forest cover, income distance, demographic performance, tax effort). Chairman + 4 members appointed by President. [Source: Constitution of India, Art. 280; Finance Commission Reports (I–XV); 15th FC (N.K. Singh) report, 2020].

Comparison: US — no constitutional finance commission; federal grants via formulas in statutes (Medicaid, highways); states have tax autonomy. Canada — Equalisation Program (Section 36, Constitution Act 1982) — federal transfers to ensure comparable services at comparable taxation; calculated by Finance Canada. Australia — Commonwealth Grants Commission (statutory, 1933) recommends horizontal fiscal equalisation (HFE) — "full equalisation" to average fiscal capacity. Germany — Finanzausgleich (fiscal equalisation) among Länder, constitutionalised (Article 107 GG) [Source: US Federal Grants; Canada Constitution Act 1982, Sec. 36; Australian CGC; German Basic Law, Art. 107; Watts, Comparing Federal Systems].

3.1.4 Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) — Article 148

CAG audits all expenditure from Consolidated Fund of India, states, and UTs; reports to President/Governor for laying before Parliament/legislature. Appointed by President; removal like Supreme Court Judge (Art 148(1)). [Source: Constitution of India, Art. 148; CAG's DPC Act, 1971; R.K. Jain v. Union of India, (1993) 4 SCC 119].

Comparison: US — Government Accountability Office (GAO, 1921, legislative branch agency); Comptroller General appointed by President with Senate consent, 15-year term. UK — National Audit Office (NAO, 1983); Comptroller and Auditor General appointed by Crown on PM's advice, reports to Parliament. Canada — Auditor General (Parliamentary officer, 1878); appointed by Governor in Council on PM's advice, 10-year term. Australia — Auditor-General (independent officer of Parliament, 1901); 10-year term. France — Cour des Comptes (constitutional, 1807); members irremovable [Source: US GAO Act; UK NAO; Canada Auditor General Act; Australian Auditor-General Act 1997; French Constitution, Art. 47-2].

3.1.5 National Commissions (SC, ST, OBC) — Articles 338, 338A, 338B

NCSC (Art 338), NCST (Art 338A, 89th Amendment 2003), NCBC (Art 338B, 102nd Amendment 2018 — constitutional status; 105th Amendment 2021 — restored state power to identify OBCs). Powers of civil court (summon, evidence, documents). [Source: Constitution of India, Arts. 338, 338A, 338B; 89th, 102nd, 105th Amendments; Mandal Commission Report, 1980].

Comparison: US — EEOC (Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, 1965, statutory) enforces civil rights laws; no constitutional commissions for specific groups. UK — EHRC (Equality and Human Rights Commission, 2007, statutory). Canada — Canadian Human Rights Commission (1977, statutory). Australia — AHRC (Australian Human Rights Commission, 1986, statutory). France — Defender of Rights (Défenseur des droits, 2011, constitutional, Art. 71-1) [Source: US EEOC; UK Equality Act 2010; Canada CHRC; Australia AHRC; French Constitution, Art. 71-1].

3.1.6 Attorney General (Art 76) & Advocate General (Art 165)

AGI is highest law officer of Union; appointed by President; holds office during pleasure; right of audience in all courts; advises Government on legal matters. State AG (Art 165) — similar role. Comparison: US — Attorney General (Cabinet member, heads DOJ); Solicitor General argues before SC. UK — Attorney General (minister, not Cabinet); advises Crown; Solicitor General deputy. Canada — Attorney General = Minister of Justice; dual role. Australia — Attorney General (minister); Solicitor General independent statutory officer. France — No direct equivalent; Ministry of Justice handles prosecution [Source: Constitution of India, Arts. 76, 165; Comparative constitutional law sources].

PYQ Deconstruction: Constitutional Bodies (2017, 10 marks)
"The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) is the guardian of the public purse. Discuss how the CAG's role has evolved and the challenges it faces in ensuring accountability."
(1) Constitutional mandate — Art 148, 149, 150, 151; DPC Act 1971. (2) Evolution: Pre-1976 — only audit of expenditure; Post-1976 (1976 amendment) — propriety audit, performance audit, environment audit, IT audit. (3) Key reports — 2G spectrum (2010), Coal block allocation (2012), Rafale (2018). (4) Challenges: (a) Post-audit nature — detects after expenditure; (b) No enforcement power — reports to legislature; (c) Staffing/ expertise gaps for complex sectors; (d) Politicisation of appointments; (e) Limited access to PPP/autonomous bodies. (5) Reforms — 2nd ARC: multi-member CAG, ex-ante audit for large projects, independent audit board. Source: UPSC Mains 2017 GS II Q4; CAG Reports; 2nd ARC Report 4.
PYQ 2023 (10 marks)
"Constitutional bodies vs Statutory bodies — explain the difference and its significance for institutional independence."
Constitutional bodies (Art 148, 280, 315–324, 338, 338A, 338B): Created by Constitution; amendment needed to alter; appointment/removal constitutionally protected (e.g., CAG removal like SC Judge). Statutory bodies (NHRC, CIC, Lokpal, CVC, NGT, TRAI, SEBI, RBI): Created by Act of Parliament; can be amended/repealed by simple majority; appointment/removal by executive (usually). Significance: Constitutional bodies have higher insulation — e.g., ECI (Art 324) vs NHRC (Protection of Human Rights Act 1993); UPSC (Art 315) vs SSC (staff selection, statutory). UPSC Mains 2023 GS II Q4.

3.2 Electoral Systems: Mechanics, Outcomes, and Reform Debates

3.2.1 First-Past-The-Post (FPTP)

India (Lok Sabha, State Assemblies), UK (House of Commons), USA (House of Representatives, state legislatures), Canada (House of Commons, provincial legislatures). Candidate with plurality wins; no majority required. Tends to produce: (i) Manufactured majorities (seat share > vote share); (ii) Two-party systems (Duverger's Law); (iii) Regional parties can win seats with concentrated support; (iv) Wasted votes (votes for losing candidates) [Source: Maurice Duverger, Political Parties, 1954; Arend Lijphart, Patterns of Democracy, 2012; Election Commission of India, Statistical Reports].

India 2019: BJP 37.4% votes → 55.8% seats (303/543). UK 2019: Conservatives 43.6% votes → 56.2% seats (365/650). India 2024: BJP 36.6% votes → 44.2% seats (240/543); INDIA bloc 38.4% votes → 43.1% seats (234/543) — coalition era returns. Disproportionality index (Gallagher): India ~12-15%, UK ~15-20% [Source: ECI Statistical Reports 2019, 2024; UK Electoral Commission].

3.2.2 Proportional Representation (PR)

Party-list PR (closed/open): seats allocated by vote share (Israel, Netherlands, South Africa). Single Transferable Vote (STV): multi-member districts, ranked ballots, quota (Droop/Hare) — Ireland, Malta, Australia (Senate), India (Rajya Sabha, State Legislative Councils, President/Vice-President elections) [Source: Constitution of India, Art. 80(4), 171(4), 55; Electoral College for President (Art 55); R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India, (1993) Supp (1) SCC 324].

Mixed-Member Proportional (MMP): Combines FPTP constituencies with party-list top-up to achieve overall proportionality — Germany, New Zealand, Scotland, Wales [Source: German Basic Law, Art. 38; New Zealand Electoral Act 1993; Scotland Act 1998].

3.2.3 Presidential Elections

India: Indirect, electoral college (MPs + MLAs), STV (Art 55). US: Electoral College (state electors = congressional delegation), winner-take-all in 48 states, majority of 538 needed (270). France: Direct, two-round runoff (if no majority in Round 1). Brazil: Direct, two-round runoff. [Source: Constitution of India, Art. 54–55; US Constitution, Art. II, 12th Amendment; French Constitution, Art. 7; Brazilian Constitution, Art. 77].

3.2.4 Anti-Defection Law: India's Tenth Schedule

52nd Amendment (1985) added Tenth Schedule: disqualification for voluntarily giving up party membership or voting/abstaining contrary to party whip. 91st Amendment (2003) removed "split" exception (1/3 members); now only "merger" (2/3) exempts. Kihoto Hollohan (1992): Speaker's decision subject to judicial review; Ravi S. Naik (1994): "voluntarily giving up" inferred from conduct [Source: Constitution of India, Tenth Schedule; 52nd, 91st Amendments; Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu, (1992) 2 SCC 321; Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India, (1994) 4 SCC 687].

Comparison: UK — no legal anti-defection; party discipline enforced by whips, deselection. US — no party discipline law; primary system empowers voters. Canada — party discipline strong but no legal sanction; floor-crossing allowed (e.g., Belinda Stronach 2005). Australia — party discipline via caucus; no constitutional provision [Source: UK Party Discipline; US Primary System; Canada Parliament of Canada Act; Australian Party Caucus Rules].

AV = Alternative Vote (ranked ballot). India uses FPTP for Lok Sabha, STV for Rajya Sabha. France uses two-round for both President and National Assembly.

4.2 Comparative Polity Answer-Writing Framework

The CAPJCD Framework for Comparative Answers
Constitutional Anchor: Start with the Indian Article/Schedule. Axis of Comparison: Name the dimension (federalism, JR, executive, electoral). Provision Comparison: Cite foreign counterpart (US Art V, UK convention, Canada Sec 92, etc.). Judicial Interpretation: Key cases from both systems (Kesavananda vs Marbury; Bommai vs Reference re Secession). Critique: Strengths/weaknesses, design trade-offs. Dialogue/Way Forward: Commission reports, reform proposals, contemporary relevance. Example: "Compare Indian and US judicial review" → Art 13/32/226 vs Marbury/Art III → Kesavananda (basic structure) vs Marbury (no amendment review) → India wider scope (amendments, PIL, Art 21 expansion) → US deeper tiers of scrutiny → Critique: Indian SC overburdened; US SC politicised appointments → Way forward: Judicial impact assessment, fixed terms for US justices.

4.3 Model Long-Answer Structure (250 words / 15 marks)

Question: "Critically examine the statement: 'The Indian Constitution is federal in form but unitary in spirit.' (2016, 15 marks)"
Model Answer Structure:
Introduction (35 words): The Indian Constitution establishes a dual polity with divided powers (Seventh Schedule) but incorporates unitary features (Art 356, single citizenship, integrated services) that activate during emergencies — a deliberate "quasi-federal" design (Wheare).

Body Paragraph 1 — Federal Features (60 words): Written supreme Constitution (Art 13); division of legislative (7th Schedule), executive (Art 73/162), financial (Art 268–293) powers; independent judiciary (Art 124/217) as federal arbiter; state autonomy in List II/III; Finance Commission (Art 280) for fiscal federalism. Cite S.R. Bommai: federalism is basic structure.

Body Paragraph 2 — Unitary Bias (60 words): Emergency provisions (Art 352/356/360) convert federation into unitary system; Art 356 (President's Rule) misused 100+ times pre-Bommai; single citizenship (Art 5–11); All-India Services (Art 312); Governor as Centre's agent (Art 155–156); Parliament can alter state boundaries (Art 3); residuary powers with Centre (Art 248).

Body Paragraph 3 — Judicial Balancing (50 words): S.R. Bommai (1994): Art 356 subject to judicial review; floor test mandatory; secularism/federalism basic structure. Rameshwar Prasad (2006): Governor's report must be material. Gujarat v. Mirzapur (2005): Cooperative federalism requires consultation. Post-Bommai, Art 356 use declined sharply.

Conclusion (45 words): The "unitary spirit" is a safety valve for national integrity, not a design defect. Punchhi Commission (2010): Art 356 as last resort; Governor's discretion curtailed; Inter-State Council (Art 263) institutionalised. India's federalism is "cooperative" (Austin) — dynamic, context-sensitive, and evolving with GST Council as new fiscal federalism model.
Model answer sources: Constitution of India (Arts cited); K.C. Wheare, Federal Government; Granville Austin, The Indian Constitution; S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, (1994) 3 SCC 1; Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India, (2006) 2 SCC 1; Punchhi Commission Report, 2010; GST Council (Art 279A, 101st Amendment).

4.4 Model Answer: "Compare and Contrast" (15 marks)

Question: "Compare the amendment procedures of the Indian and US Constitutions. Why has the US Constitution seen fewer amendments?" (15 marks)
Introduction (30 words): Both India (Art 368) and US (Art V) have rigid amendment procedures requiring supermajorities, but differ in federal role, judicial review of amendments, and political culture — explaining the 105 vs 27 amendment gap over 73/230 years.

Body 1 — Indian Procedure (55 words): Art 368: Three tiers — simple majority (Art 3, 11, 169), special majority (2/3 present & voting + majority total membership), special majority + half state ratification (federal provisions). Basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda) adds judicial review of amendments — Court can strike down amendments violating basic features (federalism, secularism, JR).

Body 2 — US Procedure (55 words): Art V: Two paths — (1) 2/3 both Houses + 3/4 state legislatures (26/27 used); (2) Convention called by 2/3 states + 3/4 ratification (never used). No judicial review of amendments (Coleman v. Miller, 1939 — political question). No time limit unless specified (27th Amendment took 202 years).

Body 3 — Why Fewer US Amendments (55 words): (1) Higher threshold: 2/3 Congress (vs India's special majority of present & voting); (2) No "simple majority" tier — all amendments need supermajority; (3) Partisan polarisation: 2/3 Congress requires bipartisan consensus; (4) State ratification: 38 states needed — small states have veto; (5) Judicial review substitute: SC interprets Constitution dynamically (Brown, Roe, Obergefell) reducing amendment pressure.

Conclusion (35 words): India's flexible tiers and judicial review allow evolutionary change; US rigidity channels change through judicial interpretation. Both serve their contexts: India's diversity needs adaptable Constitution; US federalism protects state sovereignty through amendment difficulty.
Sources: Constitution of India, Art 368; US Constitution, Art V; Kesavananda Bharati (1973); Coleman v. Miller, 307 US 433 (1939); Vile, Constitutional Amending Process; Austin, Indian Constitution.

4.5 Model Answer: Constitutional Bodies (10 marks)

Question: "The independence of the Election Commission of India is crucial for free and fair elections. Discuss the constitutional safeguards and suggest reforms." (10 marks)
Introduction (20 words): Article 324 vests superintendence, direction, and control of elections in ECI — a constitutional body designed for independence from executive interference.

Body 1 — Constitutional Safeguards (50 words): (1) Multi-member (CEC + 2 ECs) since 1989 — collective decision-making; (2) CEC removal like SC Judge (impeachment, Art 324(5)); (3) ECs removal only on CEC recommendation; (4) Conditions of service determined by Parliament (EC Act 1991) — not executive discretion; (5) Anoop Baranwal (2023): SC directed appointment via collegium (PM, LoP, CJI) — ending executive monopoly.

Body 2 — Challenges (40 words): (1) ECs lack removal protection equal to CEC; (2) Appointment still effectively executive-dominated pre-2023; (3) No financial autonomy — budget via Law Ministry; (4) Model Code of Conduct not legally enforceable; (5) Paid news, electoral bonds, foreign funding regulation gaps.

Body 3 — Reforms (30 words): 2nd ARC: Equal constitutional protection for all ECs; independent secretariat; statutory backing for MCC; state funding of elections (Indrajit Gupta); expenditure ceiling enforcement; simultaneous elections (Law Commission 270th) — with constitutional amendments.

Conclusion (10 words): ECI's institutional insulation must be deepened — from "independence" to "autonomy."
