1. Physics Paper II — Thermodynamics, Statistical Mechanics & Quantum Mechanics

2. Thermodynamics

  • Zeroth Law: Thermodynamic equilibrium, temperature as a fundamental property.
  • First Law of Thermodynamics: Conservation of energy, ΔU = Q - W (or ΔU = Q + W depending on sign convention), internal energy U as a state function, heat capacity (Cp, Cv), relation Cp - Cv = R for ideal gas.
  • Second Law of Thermodynamics: Kelvin-Planck statement, Clausius statement, entropy as a state function, entropy change calculations (dS = δQ_rev/T), entropy change for ideal gas, isolated systems, irreversibility.
  • Third Law of Thermodynamics: Third law (Nernst heat theorem), absolute zero unattainability, absolute entropy scale, residual entropy.
  • Thermodynamic Potentials: Internal energy U, enthalpy H = U + pV, Helmholtz free energy F = U - TS, Gibbs free energy G = H - TS, relations between them, Maxwell relations, Gibbs-Duhem equation, Clapeyron equation.
  • Thermodynamic Relations: T-dS equations (TdS = C_v dT + T(∂P/∂T)_V dV, TdS = C_p dT - T(∂V/∂T)_p dP), Joule-Thomson coefficient, inversion temperature.
  • Phase Equilibrium: Phase rule (F = C - P + 2), phase diagrams (P-T diagram, P-T diagram for water), phase transitions (first order, second order), Clausius-Clapeyron equation (dP/dT = L/(TΔv)).

3. Statistical Mechanics

  • Microcanonical Ensemble: Isolated system (N, V, E fixed), microcanonical ensemble, entropy S = k_B ln Ω (Boltzmann's formula), fundamental postulate of statistical mechanics (equal a priori probabilities).
  • Canonical Ensemble: Canonical ensemble (N, V, T fixed), Boltzmann distribution p_i = (1/Z) e^(-βE_i), partition function Z = Σ e^(-βE_i), thermodynamic relations (F = -k_B T ln Z, S = k_B ln Z + U/T, U = -∂lnZ/∂β, C_V = k_B β² ⟨(E - ⟨E⟩)²⟩).
  • Grand Canonical Ensemble: Grand canonical ensemble (μ, V, T fixed), grand partition function Ξ = Σ exp(-β(E_i - μN_i)), grand potential Ω = -k_B T ln Ξ, grand potential relations.
  • Quantum Statistics: Bose-Einstein statistics (Bose-Einstein statistics, Bose-Einstein condensation, Bose-Einstein condensate), Fermi-Dirac statistics (Fermi-Dirac statistics, Fermi energy, Fermi temperature), Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics (classical limit).
  • Blackbody Radiation: Blackbody radiation, Planck's radiation law (Planck's law), Stefan-Boltzmann law (j* = σT⁴), Wien's displacement law (λ_max T = b), Rayleigh-Jeans law, ultraviolet catastrophe resolved by Planck.
  • Fermi Gas: Fermi gas (ideal Fermi gas), Fermi energy E_F, Fermi temperature T_F, density of states, low temperature specific heat of electrons (C_v ∝ T), white dwarfs (electron degeneracy pressure).

4. Blackbody Radiation

  • Blackbody Radiation: Blackbody radiation (blackbody spectrum), Planck's law (Planck's radiation law: u(ν,T) = (8πhν³/c³)/(e^(hν/kT) - 1)), Stefan-Boltzmann law (j* = σT⁴), Wien's displacement law (λ_max T = b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K), Wien's displacement law (λ_max T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K).
  • Photoelectric Effect: Photoelectric effect (photoelectric effect), Einstein's photoelectric equation (E_k = hν - φ), threshold frequency, stopping potential.
  • Compton Effect: Compton effect (Compton effect), Compton wavelength shift (Δλ = (h/m_ec)(1 - cos θ)), Compton wavelength (λ_C = h/(m_e c) ≈ 2.426 × 10⁻¹² m).
  • De Broglie Hypothesis: de Broglie hypothesis (matter waves), de Broglie wavelength (λ = h/p), electron diffraction, Davisson-Germer experiment.
  • Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle: Heisenberg uncertainty principle (Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2, ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2), interpretation, applications (zero-point energy, tunneling).
  • Wave-Particle Duality: Wave-particle duality (light as particle and wave, matter as wave and particle), quantum mechanics (wave mechanics, matrix mechanics), Schrödinger equation (time-dependent Schrödinger equation: iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ, time-independent Schrödinger equation: Ĥψ = Eψ).
Technique: Blackbody Radiation
Question: Calculate the peak wavelength of blackbody radiation at T = 3000 K.
Answer: Wien's displacement law: λ_max T = b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K. λ_max = 2.898 × 10⁻³ / 3000 = 9.66 × 10⁻⁷ m = 966 nm (infrared region).

Memory Tricks

  • Thermodynamics Laws: Zeroth law (temperature), First law (energy conservation: ΔU = Q - W), Second law (entropy increases, entropy of isolated system never decreases), Third law (S → 0 as T → 0, absolute zero unattainable).
  • Quantum Mechanics: Schrödinger equation: iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ (time-dependent), Ĥψ = Eψ (time-independent), Heisenberg uncertainty principle: Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2, ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2.
  • Thermodynamic Potentials: Internal energy U = Q - W, Enthalpy H = U + pV, Helmholtz free energy F = U - TS, Gibbs free energy G = H - TS = U + pV - TS, differentials: dU = TdS - pdV, dH = TdS + VdP, dF = -SdT - pdV, dG = -SdT + Vdp.
  • Thermodynamic Potentials Relations: Maxwell relations: (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V, (∂S/∂P)_T = -(∂V/∂T)_P, (∂T/∂V)_S = -(∂P/∂S)_V, (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P.

5. Solid State Physics: Band Theory, Semiconductors & Superconductivity

The transition from classical free electron theory to the quantum mechanical band theory represents one of the most critical conceptual leaps in condensed matter physics. While Drude and Sommerfeld models successfully explained electrical conductivity and the Wiedemann-Franz law, they failed catastrophically in predicting specific heat capacities and optical properties. The resolution lies in Bloch's Theorem, which states that the wavefunction of an electron in a periodic potential can be written as ψ_k(r) = u_k(r)e^(ik·r), where u_k(r) has the same periodicity as the lattice. This leads directly to the formation of energy bands and forbidden gaps.

In a perfect crystal, electrons occupy allowed energy bands separated by band gaps. The Fermi level determines whether a material is a metal (Fermi level within a band), semiconductor (Fermi level in a gap with small width), or insulator (large gap). Doping mechanisms introduce impurity levels: n-type doping adds donor states just below the conduction band, while p-type doping introduces acceptor states just above the valence band. The Hall effect provides experimental verification of carrier type and concentration via R_H = 1/(ne) for single carriers, though sign reversal indicates mixed conduction or hole dominance.

Semiconductors exhibit temperature-dependent conductivity σ ∝ exp(-E_g/2k_BT), distinguishing them from metals where resistivity increases with temperature. The mass action law n0 p0 = n_i2 holds at thermal equilibrium, linking intrinsic carrier concentration to the band gap energy E_g. Direct band gap materials like Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) allow vertical photon transitions, making them ideal for LEDs and lasers, whereas indirect gap materials like Silicon require phonon assistance, limiting their optical efficiency.

Superconductivity introduces a macroscopic quantum state characterized by zero electrical resistance below a critical temperature T_c. The Meissner effect—the expulsion of magnetic fields—is the defining feature distinguishing superconductors from perfect conductors. Type I superconductors exhibit a sharp transition and complete diamagnetism until H_c is reached, while Type II materials allow partial flux penetration via vortices between H_c1 and H_c2. BCS theory explains conventional superconductivity through electron-phonon coupling forming Cooper pairs with binding energy 2Δ. The energy gap Δ(T) vanishes at T_c, following the relation 2Δ(0)/k_BT_c ≈ 3.53 for weak-coupling limits. High-temperature superconductors (cuprates) challenge BCS theory, suggesting strong correlation effects and d-wave pairing symmetry rather than s-wave.

Applications range from MRI machines using NbTi wires to quantum computing utilizing Josephson junctions. The critical current density J_c and critical magnetic field H_c define operational limits. Flux pinning in Type II materials enables levitation, a phenomenon exploited in maglev trains. Understanding the interplay between disorder, dimensionality, and interaction strength is essential for modern device physics.

Technique: Semiconductor Junction Analysis
Question: A pn-junction diode has p-type region with acceptor concentration N_A = 1018 cm-3 and n-type region with donor concentration N_D = 1016 cm-3. At T = 300 K, intrinsic carrier concentration n_i = 1.5 × 1010 cm-3. Calculate the built-in potential V0 assuming complete ionization. If a forward bias of 0.7 V is applied, estimate the change in depletion width compared to equilibrium.
Answer: The built-in potential is given by V0 = (k_B T / e) ln(N_A N_D / n_i2). Substituting values: k_B T/e ≈ 0.0259 V at 300 K. V0 = 0.0259 × ln((1018 × 1016)/(1.5×1010)2) = 0.0259 × ln(1034 / 2.25×1020) ≈ 0.0259 × ln(4.44×1013) ≈ 0.0259 × 31.5 ≈ 0.816 V. Under forward bias V_F = 0.7 V, the effective barrier becomes V0 - V_F = 0.116 V. The depletion width W ∝ sqrt(V_bi). Since voltage drops significantly, W decreases substantially. Specifically, W_F / W0 ≈ sqrt((V0 - V_F)/V0) ≈ sqrt(0.116/0.816) ≈ 0.38. Thus, the depletion region shrinks to about 38% of its equilibrium width.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Constants and Units
When solving solid state problems, always write down fundamental constants explicitly (e.g., k_B = 1.38 × 10-23 J/K, e = 1.6 × 10-19 C). Convert all concentrations to consistent units (cm-3 or m-3) before calculation. Explicitly state assumptions like 'complete ionization' or 'non-degenerate statistics'. If a question involves high-field effects, mention velocity saturation and mobility degradation briefly to show depth.

6. Nuclear Physics: Models, Reactions & Astrophysical Implications

Nuclear physics bridges the gap between particle physics and astrophysics. The liquid drop model treats the nucleus as an incompressible fluid with surface tension, volume energy, and Coulomb repulsion terms. This semi-empirical mass formula explains binding energies, fission barriers, and drip lines. However, it fails to predict magic numbers, leading to the shell model where nucleons occupy discrete orbitals defined by quantum numbers n, l, j. Magic numbers (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) correspond to closed shells with enhanced stability.

Nuclear reactions are governed by conservation laws: baryon number, lepton number, charge, energy-momentum, and angular momentum. Strong interactions dominate short-range processes (fusion, fission), while weak interactions govern beta decay. The Q-value determines exothermicity: Q = [M_initial - M_final]c2 > 0 for spontaneous reactions. Cross-sections σ measure reaction probability, often peaking at resonance energies described by Breit-Wigner formula.

In astrophysics, nucleosynthesis explains elemental abundance. Big Bang Nucleosynthesis produced H, He, Li in early universe. Stellar fusion chains (pp-chain, CNO cycle) power stars; heavier elements form via s-process and r-process in supernovae or neutron star mergers. The solar neutrino problem was resolved by discovering neutrino oscillations, implying non-zero neutrino masses beyond the Standard Model.

Radioactivity types include alpha (He nuclei), beta-minus (electron emission from neutron decay), beta-plus (positron emission), and gamma (photon emission). Half-life T1/2 = ln(2)/λ relates decay constant to activity A = λN. Geiger-Nuttall law links alpha decay half-life to energy: log(T1/2) ∝ 1/√E_alpha. Nuclear forces are charge-independent, saturating, and short-ranged (~1-2 fm), mediated by pion exchange in Yukawa theory.
