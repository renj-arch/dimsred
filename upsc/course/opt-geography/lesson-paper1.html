2. Geomorphology

  • Earth's Interior: Structure of earth (crust, mantle, core - outer & inner), evidence from seismology (P-waves & S-waves behavior), density, temperature, pressure conditions. Isostasy (Airy's & Pratt's hypotheses), isostatic adjustment, isostatic rebound (post-glacial rebound).
  • Plate Tectonics: Plate tectonics theory (Wilson, Morgan, McKenzie, Parker 1967-68) - lithosphere broken into plates (major: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, Antarctic; minor: Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Philippine, Arabian, Scotia), plate boundaries (divergent, convergent, transform), plate movements (rates ~2-15 cm/year), causes of plate movement (ridge push, slab pull, mantle convection currents), Wilson cycle (ocean basin opening & closing).
  • Earthquakes: Focus & epicenter, seismic waves (P-waves - primary/compressional, S-waves - secondary/shear, surface waves - Love & Rayleigh), seismograph & seismogram, Richter scale & moment magnitude scale (Mw), intensity (Modified Mercalli Intensity - MMI), distribution of earthquakes (circum-Pacific belt, Alpine-Himalayan belt, mid-ocean ridges), causes & effects, earthquake prediction & preparedness, seismic zoning of India (BIS zones II-V), earthquake-resistant design.
  • Volcanoes: Types (shield, composite/strato, cinder cone, lava dome, fissure), types of eruptions (Hawaiian, Strombolian, Vulcanian, Pelean, Plinian, Surtseyan, phreatic, phreatomagmatic), volcanic products (lava - pahoehoe & aa, pyroclastic materials - ash, lapilli, bombs, blocks, volcanic gases), volcanic hazards & mitigation, major volcanic belts (circum-Pacific ring of fire - ~75% of volcanoes, mid-ocean ridges, intraplate hotspots - Hawaii, Yellowstone), famous eruptions (Mount St. Helens 1980, Mount Pinatubo 1991, Krakatoa 1883, Mount Vesuvius 79 AD).
  • Weathering, Mass Wasting & Landforms: Weathering (physical/mechanical - frost action, insolation, exfoliation, block disintegration, granular disintegration; chemical - solution, carbonation, hydration, oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, leaching; biological - plants, animals, humans), factors affecting weathering (climate, rock type, topography, vegetation, time), soil erosion (sheet, rill, gully, splash), soil conservation (contour bunding, terracing, afforestation, check dams), grading (graded profile, graded stream), base level of erosion.
  • Geomorphic Processes & Landforms: Fluvial (river erosion - hydraulic action, corrosion/abrasion, attrition, solution; transportation - traction, saltation, suspension, solution; deposition - alluvial fans, floodplains, meanders, oxbow lakes, natural levees, deltas - arcuate, bird's foot, cuspate, estuarine), Glacial (erosion - plucking, abrasion; deposition - moraines, drumlins, eskers, kames, outwash plains; features - cirque, arête, horn, U-shaped valley, hanging valley, fjord, fiord), glacial depositional & erosional landforms, glacial lakes, moraines, glacier types (valley/alpine, continental/ice sheet, piedmont), periglacial processes (frost action, solifluction, patterned ground), aeolian (wind erosion - deflation, abrasion; deposition - sand dunes - barchan, seif, star, transverse, longitudinal; loess, desert pavement, yardang, zeugen), glacial & aeolian landforms.
  • Coastal Geomorphology: Waves (constructive & destructive), tides (spring & neap), currents, erosion (hydraulic action, corrosion, abrasion, solution), deposition (beaches, bars, spits, tombolos, barrier islands, lagoons), coastal landforms (sea cliffs, sea caves, sea arches, sea stacks, tide-dependent platforms, headlands & bays), coastal management & protection.
  • Karst Topography: Limestone topography - dissolution of limestone (carbonation), surface features (sinkholes/swallow holes, dolines, poljes, karst windows, disappearing streams), underground features (caves, caverns, stalactites, stalagmites, pillars, flowstone), distribution (Kentucky, Slovenia, Guangxi, Vindhyan & Bastar regions in India).
  • Gradation & Denudation: Gradation (degradation & aggradation), base level of erosion, peneplain, pediplain, panplain, rejuvenation (knick points, waterfalls, river terraces), drainage patterns (dendritic, trellis, radial, rectangular, parallel, annular, centripetal, barbed, discordant), drainage patterns & their significance.
Technique: Plate Tectonics
Question: 'Explain Plate Tectonics theory & its evidence.'
Answer: Plate tectonics - lithosphere broken into ~7 major & several minor plates floating on asthenosphere. Evidence: (1) Jigsaw fit of continents (South America & Africa), (2) Fossil evidence (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris, Lystrosaurus across continents), (3) Rock continuity across oceans, (4) Paleomagnetism & sea floor spreading (magnetic stripes symmetrical about mid-ocean ridges), (5) Earthquake & volcano distribution along plate boundaries, (6) GPS measurements of plate movement. Mechanisms: ridge push, slab pull, mantle convection currents. Results: fold mountains, rift valleys, volcanoes, earthquakes at plate boundaries.

Memory Tricks

  • Plate boundaries: Divergent = move apart (ridges), Convergent = move together (subduction/collision), Transform = slide past (faults).
  • Earthquake waves: P-waves = Primary/fastest, travel through solids & liquids; S-waves = Secondary/slower, solids only.
  • Weathering types: Physical = mechanical breakdown; Chemical = chemical change (solution, oxidation, carbonation).
  • Drainage patterns: Dendritic = tree-like (uniform rock), Radial = from central cone/volcano, Trellis = folded mountains (alternate hard/soft), Rectangular = jointed/faulted rocks, Annular = dome-shaped, Radial = from central cone/volcano.
Mains strategy
Physical geography answers: diagrams (plate boundaries, fault types, river meander, glacial features, karst features, atmospheric layers, heat budget, ocean currents map). Quote facts: Earth's radius ~6,371 km, crust thickness (oceanic 5-10 km, continental 30-50 km), mantle ~2,900 km thick, core ~3,485 km radius (outer core liquid, inner core solid). Quotes data: earthquakes ~500,000 detectable/year, ~100,000 felt, volcanism ~1,500 potentially active volcanoes worldwide.

3. Atmospheric Dynamics: The Ferrel Cell, Rossby Waves, and Monsoonal Mechanics

The global circulation model often oversimplifies the atmosphere as three distinct cells per hemisphere (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar). However, a rigorous understanding requires analyzing the Ferrel Cell, which operates via indirect thermal forcing rather than direct heating. Unlike the Hadley cell driven by equatorial insolation excess, the Ferrel cell is a transient circulation maintained by the eddy transport of momentum from the tropical and polar cells. This mechanism explains why the mid-latitude westerlies are stronger in winter when the temperature gradient between the pole and equator is steepest (maximum baroclinicity). The Rossby waves (planetary waves) arise due to the conservation of potential vorticity as air parcels move north or south across changing latitudes. These waves create the meandering jet streams that steer cyclones and anticyclones. In the context of Indian Geography, the monsoon is not merely a seasonal wind shift but a complex interaction between the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), and the Tibetan Plateau's thermal low. The withdrawal of the Southwest Monsoon in September is often delayed by the presence of residual heat over the Indo-Gangetic Plain, leading to "October rains" or post-monsoon showers, a phenomenon critical for understanding agricultural calendars in Punjab and Haryana.

Examiner's trap
Students often confuse the Coriolis force with the pressure gradient force. The PGI (Pressure Gradient Force) initiates wind flow from high to low pressure, while the Coriolis force deflects it (right in NH, left in SH). The balance between these two creates geostrophic wind. A common error is attributing trade winds solely to the ITCZ; they are also sustained by the return flow of the Hadley cell and surface friction which slows them down.
Exam-day strategy: Diagramming Atmosphere
When answering questions on atmospheric circulation, always draw the three-cell model but annotate the Ferrel cell with arrows indicating 'eddy transport' rather than direct heating. Label the Subtropical High (STH) clearly, as it acts as a barrier to moisture transport into Europe and North America, creating Mediterranean climates. For Indian monsoon questions, sketch the low-pressure trough over Tibet and the high pressure over the Asian continent in winter to visually demonstrate the reversal mechanism.

4. Oceanography: Thermohaline Circulation, Upwelling Zones, and Marine Chemistry

The ocean is not a static body but a dynamic system driven by thermohaline circulation (the global conveyor belt). This circulation is powered by differences in water density caused by temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline). Cold, salty water sinks in the North Atlantic (near Greenland and Labrador), driving deep currents that flow southward. Conversely, warm surface waters accumulate in the Pacific. The Upwelling Zones are critical for fisheries; they occur where winds blow parallel to coastlines (Easterlies or Westerlies) causing Ekman transport to push surface water away, allowing nutrient-rich deep water to rise. Key global upwelling regions include the coasts of Peru (Humboldt Current), California, and the Arabian Sea (Somali Current). In the Indian Ocean, the Somali Current reverses direction seasonally: flowing westward during the Southwest Monsoon and eastward during the Northeast Monsoon. This reversal creates a unique cyclonic gyre in the Arabian Sea during summer, which influences coastal upwelling off Mumbai and Gujarat, vital for India's shrimp and anchovy fisheries.

5. Biogeography: The Theory of Island Biogeography and Endemism

MacArthur and Wilson's Theory of Island Biogeography posits that species richness on an island is a dynamic equilibrium between immigration rates (from the mainland) and extinction rates. Small, isolated islands have high extinction rates and low immigration, leading to lower biodiversity. Large, close-to-mainland islands support higher diversity. This theory applies not just to oceanic islands but also to habitat "islands" like mountain tops or forest patches. In India, the Western Ghats act as a massive island of biodiversity surrounded by the Deccan Trap and the Eastern Ghats. The region hosts high levels of endemism (species found nowhere else), such as the Lion-tailed Macaque, Nilgiri Tahr, and numerous amphibians like the Horned Frog. The fragmentation of these habitats due to human activity reduces the effective area, increasing extinction risk—a direct application of the theory. Furthermore, Biogeographic Provinces (Wittaker's classification) help in understanding vegetation distribution; India is divided into regions like the Himalayan, Peninsular Indian, and Gangetic Plain provinces, each with distinct floristic compositions.

6. Population Geography: Demographic Transition, Urbanization Models, and Migration Theories

The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) describes the shift from high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates as a country develops. India is currently in Stage 4 or early Stage 5, characterized by a slowing population growth rate despite a large absolute increase. The Total Fertility Rate (TFR) has dropped below replacement level (2.1) in states like Kerala and Tamil Nadu but remains above 2.0 in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. This intra-country disparity highlights the importance of state-level policies. Regarding migration, the Push-Pull Theory explains rural-urban migration: push factors include landlessness, drought, and lack of education; pull factors include industrial jobs and better amenities. However, this model is insufficient for explaining circular migration or forced displacement due to climate change (e.g., climate refugees from coastal Andhra Pradesh). The Demographic Dividend occurs when the working-age population exceeds dependents, offering a window of economic growth if jobs are available; India's dividend is peaking now but risks turning into a demographic burden without sufficient employment generation.

7. Geomorphology: Tectonic Uplift vs. Erosion in the Himalayas

The Himalayas are young fold mountains formed by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates. The ongoing tectonic uplift (approx. 5 mm/year) competes with erosional denudation. Glaciers in the high Himalayas act as powerful agents of erosion, carving U-shaped valleys and cirques. However, the monsoon rains provide water for fluvial erosion. The Ganga-Brahmaputra-Meghna system is one of the world's largest sediment transporters, carrying silt from the Tibetan Plateau to the Bay of Bengal. This sediment load builds the massive Ganges Delta, which is prone to subsidence and sea-level rise. The concept of Base Level is crucial here; the sea level acts as the ultimate base level for the Ganga, but local base levels (like lakes or resistant rock layers) create river terraces. These terraces are archaeological goldmines, preserving human history from the Paleolithic to the Vedic period.
