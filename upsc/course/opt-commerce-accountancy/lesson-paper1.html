Commerce Paper I — Accounting & Company Law (Detailed)

Company Accounts

  • Issue of shares: Issue at par, premium, discount (prohibited except sweat equity), forfeiture & reissue of shares, surrender of shares, calls in arrears & calls in advance, interest on calls in arrears & calls in advance (Table F — 10% & 12% respectively), issue of debentures (at par/premium/discount, for cash, for consideration other than cash, as collateral security), interest on debentures, redemption of debentures (out of profits, out of capital, DMDR — Debenture Redemption Reserve 25% for listed, 10% for unlisted unless exempt), redemption out of proceeds of fresh issue/sinking fund/investment method.
  • Final accounts of companies: Schedule III format — balance sheet (equity & liabilities: share capital, reserves & surplus, money received against share warrants, share application money pending allotment, non-current liabilities: long-term borrowings, deferred tax liabilities, other long-term liabilities, long-term provisions; current liabilities: short-term borrowings, trade payables, other current liabilities, short-term provisions; assets: non-current assets — tangible/intangible/Capital WIP/intangible assets under development, non-current investments, deferred tax assets (net), long-term loans & advances, other non-current assets; current assets — current investments, inventories, trade receivables, cash & cash equivalents, short-term loans & advances, other current assets), statement of profit & loss (revenue from operations, other income, total revenue, expenses — cost of materials consumed, purchase of stock-in-trade, changes in inventories, employee benefits expense, finance costs, depreciation & amortisation, other expenses, total expenses, profit before tax, tax expense — current tax & deferred tax, profit for the period, other comprehensive income, total comprehensive income, EPS), cash flow statement (operating, investing, financing — direct & indirect method), statement of changes in equity.
  • Branch accounting: Debtors system, debtors & stock control system, stock & debtors system, final accounts system (wholesale & retail price), independent branches (foreign branches — conversion of foreign currency, translation of foreign branches — monetary items at closing rate, non-monetary at historical, income items at average rate, exchange difference to P&L).
  • Departmental accounting: Departmental trading & P&L account, allocation of common expenses (sales value, floor area, number of employees, time, direct allocation), inter-departmental transfers (at cost or at market price), unrealised profit elimination (1/3rd or 1/(1+margin%) of unsold stock).
  • Consignment accounts: Consignor & consignee, commission (ordinary, del-credere, del-credere & over-riding), valuation of unsold stock (cost or market price whichever lower, less proportionate expenses), abnormal loss (normal loss estimated & spread over good units; abnormal loss valued at cost and transferred to P&L), goods sent on consignment at cost or invoice price (with loading & reserve for unsold stock).
  • Joint venture accounts: Joint venture vs partnership vs consignment, methods — (1) separate set of books, (2) no separate set of books (each venturer records own transactions), (3) memorandum joint venture account method, settlement of accounts.
  • Royalty accounts: Minimum rent (dead rent), short-workings, recoupment of short-workings, strike & lock-out clause, sub-lease, accounting treatment in books of lessee & lessor.

Company Law in Detail

  • Company formation: Promoters (duties, liabilities, pre-incorporation contracts), incorporation (application to ROC, MoA — clauses: name, situation, object, liability, capital, subscription; AoA — contents, alteration, entrenchment), certificate of incorporation, commencement of business (declaration under Section 10A for companies with share capital).
  • Share capital: Authorised, issued, subscribed, called-up, paid-up capital; issue at par/premium/discount; share premium account (uses per Section 52), sweat equity, employee stock option scheme (ESOS), buyback (Section 68 — conditions: 25% of paid-up capital & free reserves, debt-equity ratio not exceeding 2:1 after buyback, declaration of solvency), redemption of preference shares (Section 55 — out of profits or fresh issue, DRR, premium out of premium account/profits), transfer & transmission of shares, forfeiture & reissue, lien on shares, calls on shares, surrender of shares.
  • Debentures: Types (secured/unsecured, registered/bearer, convertible/non-convertible, redeemable/irredeemable, first/second mortgage), issue at discount/premium, issue for consideration other than cash, as collateral security, interest on debentures (payable periodically, income tax deduction), redemption (out of profits — DRM/DRR, out of proceeds of fresh issue, purchase in open market), redemption methods (lump sum, instalments, purchase in open market, conversion into shares/debentures), DRR (25% for listed companies, 10% for unlisted — exemptions: infrastructure companies, banks, housing finance companies, etc.).
  • Company management: Board of directors (appointment, retirement by rotation, removal, remuneration, duties — fiduciary duties, duty of care, duty to act within powers, disclosure of interest, restriction on related party transactions — Section 188), key managerial personnel (KMD — MD, CEO, CFO, CS, whole-time director), meetings (board meetings — notice, quorum, agenda, minutes, resolutions — ordinary & special; general meetings — AGM & EGM, notice period 21 days, quorum, proxy, poll, e-voting, postal ballot).
  • Company Secretary: Definition, appointment, duties (filing returns, maintaining registers & records, signing annual return, convening meetings), liabilities.
  • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR): Section 135 — applicability (net worth ₹500 crore or turnover ₹1000 crore or net profit ₹5 crore), CSR committee, 2% of average net profits of preceding 3 years, activities (Schedule VII — eradicating hunger, education, gender equality, environmental sustainability, etc.), CSR report in board's report.
  • Winding up: Modes — voluntary (members' voluntary & creditors' voluntary), by tribunal (compulsory — grounds: inability to pay debts, just & equitable, default in filing RoC, acting against sovereignty/integrity/security of India), official liquidator, contributories, preferential payments (workmen's dues, government dues, secured creditors), declaration of solvency for voluntary winding up.
  • Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code 2016: Insolvency resolution process (corporate insolvency resolution process — CIRP, initiation by financial/operational creditor/corporate debtor, declaration of moratorium, appointment of interim resolution professional (IRP), constitution of committee of creditors (CoC), appointment of resolution professional (RP), invitation of resolution plans, approval by CoC (75% voting share), implementation, liquidation (liquidation order, liquidator, realisation of assets, distribution — waterfall: secured creditors, workmen & employees, central/state government debts, unsecured creditors, preference shareholders, equity shareholders), fast track insolvency resolution process (for small companies), cross-border insolvency (UNCITRAL Model Law adoption pending), personal insolvency (individuals & partnership firms), insolvency professionals & IPAs, information utilities (National e-Governance Services Ltd. — NeSL), adjudicating authority (NCLT for companies, NCLAT for appeals, DRT for individuals/Draft Appellate Tribunal for individuals).
  • SEBI regulations: SEBI (Listing Obligations & Disclosure Requirements) Regulations 2015 (LODR — listing agreement, continuous disclosure, related party transactions, corporate governance — composition of board, woman director requirement, independent directors — 50% for listed companies with non-executive chairman, 1/3 otherwise, audit committee, nomination & remuneration committee, stakeholders relationship committee), SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations 2015 (insider trading — definition, prohibited communications, trading window closure, code of conduct for designated persons, disclosures — initial disclosure, continual disclosure, annual disclosure), SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares & Takeovers) Regulations 2011 (open trigger 25%, creeping acquisition 5% per year, open offer 26% for listed companies, offer price — highest of negotiated price, volume weighted average price of last 26 weeks, highest price paid by acquirer in last 26 weeks, open offer timeline), SEBI (Prohibition of Fraudulent & Unfair Trade Practices) Regulations, SEBI (Issue of Capital & Disclosure Requirements) Regulations 2018 (ICDR — IPO & FPO eligibility, pricing, promoter contribution, lock-in, prospectus requirements), SEBI (Listing Obligations & Disclosure Requirements) — quarterly & annual disclosures, related party transactions policy, vigil mechanism/whistle blower, corporate social responsibility reporting.
  • Competition Law: Competition Act 2002 — anti-competitive agreements (horizontal — cartels: price fixing, market allocation, bid rigging, output restriction; vertical — tie-in arrangements, exclusive supply/distribution, refusal to deal, resale price maintenance), abuse of dominance (predatory pricing, barriers to entry, leverage in one market to another), combinations (mergers & acquisitions above thresholds — assets ₹1000 crore/turnover ₹3000 crore domestic, assets USD 1 billion with ₹500 crore in India / turnover USD 3 billion with ₹1500 crore in India cross-border), CCI powers, penalties.
Technique: Companies Act 2013 answer
Question: 'Discuss the provisions related to CSR under Companies Act 2013.'
Answer: Section 135 — applies to companies with net worth ₹500 crore or turnover ₹1000 crore or net profit ₹5 crore in any preceding financial year. Board must constitute CSR committee (3+ directors, at least 1 independent), formulate CSR policy, ensure spending 2% of average net profits of 3 preceding financial years on activities in Schedule VII (education, healthcare, rural development, environment, etc.), disclose in board report, disclose reasons if unspent (transfer to Unspent CSR Account & transfer to specified fund within 6 months), penalties for non-compliance (fine ₹50,000-₹25 lakh on company, imprisonment up to 3 years or fine ₹50,000-₹25 lakh or both for officer in default). Significance: India first country to mandate CSR; aligns business with social welfare.

Common Traps

  • Confusing provisional allotment with firm allotment in IPO.
  • Missing that buyback max 25% of paid-up capital & free reserves, debt-equity ≤ 2:1 post-buyback.
  • Assuming all companies need CSR — applies only to specified thresholds.
  • Confusing private company (max 200 members) with public company (no max).
  • Missing that LIFO is not permitted under Ind AS 2/AS 2 for inventory valuation.

Memory Tricks

  • Company types: Pvt Ltd (2-200 members), Public (7+ members, no max), OPC (1 member).
  • CSR: 2% rule.
  • Buyback: 25% cap, debt-equity 2:1 post-buyback.
  • Directors: rotation — 1/3rd retire every AGM.
Mains strategy
Company law answers: cite section numbers precisely, cite case laws (Salomon v. Salomon & Co — separate legal entity, Foss v. Harbottle — rule in Foss v Harbottle, majority rule & exceptions, Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co v Riche — ultra vires, Guinness v Land Corporation of Ireland — prospectus misstatement), quote section numbers accurately, discuss recent amendments (Companies Amendment Act 2020 — decriminalisation of certain offences, CSR unspent account). Draw formats: balance sheet Schedule III format, cash flow statement direct & indirect methods, share capital structure, debenture redemption process flowchart.

Insolvency Resolution Process: The Role of the Committee of Creditors (CoC)

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) introduced a paradigm shift from debtor-in-possession to creditor-in-control mechanisms. At the heart of this transformation lies the Committee of Creditors (CoC), a body constituted under Section 29A of the IBC. Unlike previous regimes where individual lenders held disparate powers, the CoC acts as a unified decision-making entity for all financial creditors in a corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP). The composition is strictly defined: it must comprise at least five members and no more than ten, representing not less than 66% of the total outstanding operational debt owed to financial creditors. Crucially, the Code mandates that all financial creditors are eligible for membership unless they have been adjudged insolvent or involved in fraud. Secured creditors (secured by immovable property) and unsecured creditors generally cannot be mixed on a pro-rata basis within the CoC; instead, secured creditors participate as equals with unsecured financial creditors regarding voting rights.

The most critical function of the CoC is exercising veto power. Under Section 30(2), any resolution plan proposed for approval must be accepted by a majority in value of not less than 66% of the financial creditors present and voting at the meeting. If the CoC rejects the plan, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) is statutorily barred from approving it, effectively ending the rescue attempt unless a fresh proposal is made. This threshold ensures that minority creditors cannot be forced into a plan they deem detrimental to their recovery prospects. Furthermore, the CoC has the authority to appoint an interim resolution professional (IRP) or replace the initial IRP with a new one if satisfied that the current professional is not acting in the interest of the corporate debtor. The CoC also decides on the appointment of a liquidation professional if the resolution process fails.

A significant procedural evolution occurred via the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) guidelines, which clarified that the CoC must meet within 14 days of its constitution. The voting weightage is strictly based on the value of debt owed to each member, not the number of lenders. For instance, a bank owing ₹90 crore holds more sway than ten small NBFCs owing ₹9 crore each combined, provided their individual stakes are aggregated correctly. This mechanism prevents fragmentation and ensures that the largest stakeholders drive the restructuring strategy. The CoC is also empowered to approve the budget for the resolution professional's remuneration and expenses, ensuring cost-efficiency in a distressed asset scenario.

Examiner's trap
A common error is assuming that operational creditors (suppliers, employees) have voting rights in the CoC. Correct answer: Operational creditors are not part of the CoC and cannot vote on the resolution plan under Section 30(2). Their claims are treated separately; if a resolution plan does not provide for their payment, they may file an application to NCLT for approval of the plan under Section 12A (if applicable) or seek separate relief, but they do not participate in the CoC voting dynamics. Another trap is thinking secured creditors sit outside the CoC entirely; they are full members unless specifically excluded due to fraud.
Exam-day strategy
When answering questions on IBC, always anchor your response to specific sections (e.g., Section 30 for CoC powers, Section 29A for eligibility). Use the flow: Constitution → Composition → Powers (Veto, Appointment) → Thresholds (66%). Mention the 14-day meeting rule as a procedural safeguard. Avoid generic statements about "bankruptcy"; use precise terms like "Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process" (CIRP).

Transfer Pricing Regulations: Arm's Length Principle and Documentation

Under the Income Tax Act, 1961, Chapter XIV-D deals with Transfer Pricing (TP), governed by Rule 10A of the Income Tax Rules. The core principle is the Arm's Length Principle (ALP), which mandates that transactions between related parties (domestic or international) must be priced as if they were conducted between independent entities in an open market. This prevents profit shifting to low-tax jurisdictions. The statute prescribes six specific methods to determine the ALP, listed in Section 92C: Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) method (most reliable but data-heavy), Resale Price Method, Cost Plus Method, Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM) (widely used due to availability of data), Transactional Profit Split Method, and Profit Split Method. The taxpayer must select the most appropriate method based on the nature of the transaction, though TNMM is often preferred for manufacturing and trading entities.

The regulatory framework requires strict documentation. Every entity engaged in international transactions with a related person must maintain a detailed Transfer Pricing Documentation (TPD), including a Master File (global strategy), Local File (entity-specific analysis), and Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) for large groups. The TPD must be prepared by the end of the financial year, though it can be submitted later with an extension. Failure to maintain TPD attracts penalties under Section 270AA(1A) — a penalty equal to 5% of the amount of international transaction or deemed international transaction, whichever is higher. The Assessing Officer (AO) has the power to examine the documentation and make adjustments if the ALP is not met. Recent amendments have tightened the definition of "related party" and introduced stricter scrutiny on cost-plus margins in service sectors.

The concept of Intuitiveness plays a role here: if a taxpayer claims a particular pricing method without supporting data, the AO can reject it. The law also allows for advance pricing arrangements (APA), where the tax authority and taxpayer agree in advance on the ALP methodology for future transactions, providing certainty. However, unilateral APAs are limited to cases where the taxpayer has not shifted profits aggressively. The documentation must be updated annually; outdated reports lead to automatic penalties. In cross-border disputes, Mutual Agreement Procedures (MAP) under Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) become relevant if adjustments result in double taxation.

Direct Taxes: Assessment Proceedings and Objections

The assessment process under the Income Tax Act is a structured sequence of events designed to verify income declarations. The primary modes are Self-Assessment (by the taxpayer), Regular Assessment (under Section 143(1) — scrutiny of returns for errors/arithmetic mistakes), and Scrutiny Assessment (under Section 143(3) — detailed examination where notice is issued under Section 148 if discrepancies are found). A crucial distinction lies in the timeline: regular assessment must be completed within one year from the end of the relevant assessment year, while scrutiny assessments have no statutory time limit but are generally expected to conclude within six months. If an assessee fails to comply with notice u/s 143(2), the AO can proceed unilaterally.

The Objection and Appeal mechanism is vital for rights protection. Under Section 154 (rectification of mistakes) and Section 153C (correction of errors in assessment orders), specific timelines apply. If an assessee disagrees with the assessment order, they must file a written objection under Rule 12 of the Income Tax Rules within three months from the end of the month in which the notice u/s 148 was issued or the date of communication of the order, whichever is earlier. The AO then issues an Order of Objection.

Consolidated Financial Statements under Ind AS 110

Ind AS 110, Consolidated Financial Statements, establishes the principle that an entity which controls one or more other entities must present consolidated financial statements. Control is defined as the power to govern the financial and operating policies of an investee so as to obtain benefits from its activities. The standard replaces the earlier “majority‑voting‑right” test with a three‑element control model: (1) power over the investee, (2) exposure, or rights, to variable returns from involvement with the investee, and (3) the ability to use power to affect those returns. The assessment of control is continuous; a parent must reassess whenever facts and circumstances indicate a change.

Consolidation procedures require the parent to combine like items of assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses and cash flows of the parent and its subsidiaries. Intra‑group balances, transactions, income and expenses are eliminated in full. Non‑controlling interests (NCI) are presented within equity, separate from the parent’s equity, and are measured at the proportionate share of the acquiree’s identifiable net assets at acquisition date (the “proportionate‑share” method) unless the entity elects the fair‑value method for a particular business combination. Goodwill arising on consolidation is recognised as an asset and tested for impairment annually or more frequently if indicators exist.

Ind AS 110 also addresses special purpose entities (SPEs) and structured entities. An entity consolidates an SPE when it has the power to direct the relevant activities of the SPE and is exposed to variable returns. The standard provides detailed guidance on “de‑facto” control, protective rights, and agency relationships. Disclosures include the nature and extent of significant restrictions on the ability to access or use assets of subsidiaries, the financial effect of changes in ownership interests that do not result in loss of control, and the nature of any contractual arrangements that may affect the consolidation conclusion.

From an examination perspective, candidates must be able to distinguish between control, joint control (Ind AS 111) and significant influence (Ind AS 28). A typical question may present a scenario where a parent holds 45 % voting rights but has a contractual right to appoint the majority of the board; the correct answer is that control exists because the parent has power over the relevant activities. Another frequent twist involves the treatment of NCI when a subsidiary issues additional shares to third parties — the parent’s share of equity changes, but control is retained; the adjustment is recognised directly in equity.

Audit of Internal Financial Controls under Section 143(3)(i) of the Companies Act

Section 143(3)(i) of the Companies Act, 2013 mandates that the auditor’s report on the financial statements of a company must state whether the company has adequate internal financial controls (IFC) with reference to financial statements and the operating effectiveness of such controls. This requirement applies to all companies except certain small companies and one‑person companies as notified. The audit of IFC is performed in accordance with Standards on Auditing (SA) 315 (Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement) and SA 330 (The Auditor’s Responses to Assessed Risks).

The auditor evaluates the design and implementation of controls relevant to the preparation of reliable financial statements. Key components include the control environment, risk assessment process, control activities, information and communication systems, and monitoring activities. The auditor obtains an understanding of the entity’s IFC by performing walkthroughs, inspecting documentation, and inquiring of management and those charged with governance. Testing of operating effectiveness involves selecting a sample of transactions and verifying that controls operated consistently throughout the period.

If the auditor identifies a material weakness — a deficiency, or combination of deficiencies, in IFC such that there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement of the financial statements will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis — the auditor must express an adverse opinion on IFC. A significant deficiency is less severe but still merits communication to those charged with governance. The auditor’s report on IFC is separate from the opinion on the financial statements; however, a material weakness in IFC often leads to a qualified or adverse opinion on the financial statements as well.

Recent amendments (Companies (Audit and Auditors) Amendment Rules, 2021) have clarified that the IFC report must be annexed to the auditor’s report and must contain a statement on the adequacy and operating effectiveness of IFC. The auditor is also required to report on the adequacy of the internal audit function, where applicable. In practice, examiners test the candidate’s ability to differentiate between a “design deficiency” and an “operating deficiency”, and to apply the concept of “reasonable assurance” rather than absolute assurance.

Worked Illustration: Material Weakness vs Significant Deficiency
A manufacturing company’s revenue recognition process relies on a single spreadsheet maintained by the sales manager. The spreadsheet is not password‑protected, and there is no independent review of journal entries posted from it. During the year, an error of ₹12 lakh (2 % of revenue) remained undetected until the year‑end audit. The auditor concludes that the control deficiency is a material weakness. Explain the reasoning and the impact on the auditor’s report.
The deficiency satisfies the definition of a material weakness because (a) the control design is absent — no segregation of duties, no independent review, and no access restrictions; (b) the error of ₹12 lakh, though quantitatively 2 % of revenue, is qualitatively material as it pertains to revenue recognition, a high‑risk area; (c) there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement could occur and not be prevented or detected on a timely basis. Consequently, the auditor must issue an adverse opinion on IFC under Section 143(3)(i) and, because the weakness relates to a significant account, also consider a qualified or adverse opinion on the financial statements. The auditor’s report will contain a separate paragraph titled “Report on Internal Financial Controls” stating the adverse opinion, and the main audit opinion paragraph will reference the IFC adverse opinion.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the auditor’s reporting responsibility on IFC, structure the answer as: (1) statutory requirement (Section 143(3)(i)), (2) applicable SAs (315, 330), (3) definition of material weakness and significant deficiency, (4) impact on IFC opinion and on financial statement opinion, (5) illustrative example. Cite the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Amendment Rules, 2021 for the annexure requirement. Avoid generic statements like “the auditor checks controls”; use precise terminology — “design effectiveness”, “operating effectiveness”, “reasonable assurance”.

Goods and Services Tax: Input Tax Credit Matching and Reversal Provisions

The GST regime under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (CGST Act) allows registered persons to claim Input Tax Credit (ITC) on inward supplies used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business. Sections 16 to 18 of the CGST Act, read with Rules 36 to 45 of the CGST Rules, prescribe the conditions, matching mechanism, and reversal provisions for ITC. The fundamental condition under Section 16(2) is that the recipient must be in possession of a tax invoice or debit note, the goods or services must have been received, the tax charged must have been paid to the government by the supplier, and the recipient must have furnished the return under Section 39.

The matching mechanism operates through the auto‑populated GSTR‑2A/2B (generated from suppliers’ GSTR‑1) and the recipient’s GSTR‑3B. Where the supplier has not uploaded the invoice or has not paid the tax, the credit appears as “pending” in GSTR‑2A/2B. The recipient may claim provisional credit up to 5 % of the eligible credit under Rule 36(4) (the “5 % provisional rule”), but the final credit is allowed only after matching. If the mismatch persists beyond the due date of filing the annual return (GSTR‑9) for the relevant financial year, the credit is reversed under Section 17(5) read with Rule 37.

Section 17(5) enumerates specific supplies on which ITC is not available (blocked credits), such as motor vehicles for personal use, club memberships, and certain construction services. Additionally, Rule 42 and Rule 43 prescribe the reversal of ITC attributable to exempt supplies and to non‑business purposes, respectively. The reversal is calculated on a pro‑rata basis using the turnover method: (Exempt turnover ÷ Total turnover) × Common credit. The reversed amount is added to output tax liability in the month of reversal and is eligible for re‑claim if the condition ceases in a subsequent period.

Recent notifications (e.g., Notification No. 14/2022‑Central Tax) have extended the time limit for availing ITC on invoices pertaining to FY 2021‑22 up to 30 November 2023, subject to payment of interest under Section 50. The concept of “deemed credit” under Section 18(1) applies when a person becomes liable to registration or opts for composition scheme; credit on inputs held in stock on the day immediately preceding the date of registration is allowed. Examiners often test the interplay between provisional credit, matching, and reversal — candidates must demonstrate the chronological flow: invoice receipt → GSTR‑2A reflection → provisional claim → matching → final allowance or reversal.

Business Combinations and Purchase Price Allocation under Ind AS 103

Ind AS 103, Business Combinations, applies the acquisition method to all business combinations, requiring the acquirer to recognise identifiable assets acquired, liabilities assumed, and any non‑controlling interest at their acquisition‑date fair values. The consideration transferred includes cash, equity instruments, contingent consideration, and settlement of pre‑existing relationships. Goodwill is measured as the excess of the consideration transferred plus the amount of any non‑controlling interest over the net of the acquisition‑date fair values of identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed.

The standard mandates a measurement period not exceeding one year from the acquisition date, during which the acquirer may adjust provisional amounts recognised at acquisition date to reflect new information about facts and circumstances that existed at the acquisition date. Adjustments are recognised retrospectively, with corresponding adjustments to goodwill. Identifiable intangible assets — such as customer relationships, technology, brand names, and non‑compete agreements — must be recognised separately from goodwill if they meet the separability or contractual‑legal criterion.

Contingent consideration is classified either as equity or a liability based on the terms of the arrangement. If classified as a liability, it is remeasured at fair value at each reporting date, with changes recognised in profit or loss. If classified as equity, it is not remeasured. The acquirer must also account for acquisition‑related costs (e.g., legal, advisory) as expenses in the period incurred, except for costs to issue equity or debt instruments, which are accounted for under Ind AS 32 and Ind AS 109 respectively.

Disclosure requirements are extensive: the name and description of the acquiree, acquisition date, percentage of voting equity interests acquired, primary reasons for the combination, qualitative description of the factors that make up goodwill, and a reconciliation of the carrying amount of goodwill at the beginning and end of the reporting period. A common examination scenario involves a step‑acquisition where the acquirer previously held an equity interest; the previously held interest is remeasured to fair value at acquisition date, with the gain or loss recognised in profit or loss. Candidates must also distinguish between a business combination and an asset acquisition — the latter does not give rise to goodwill and applies the cost‑accumulation method under Ind AS 16/38.

Corporate Governance: Independent Directors, Board Committees and the SEBI LODR Framework

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 (SEBI LODR) prescribe a comprehensive corporate governance framework for listed entities. Regulation 17 mandates that the board of directors shall have an optimum combination of executive and non‑executive directors, with at least one‑third of the board comprising independent directors where the chairperson is executive; if the chairperson is non‑executive, at least half the board must be independent. An independent director is defined under Regulation 16(1)(b) as a director who, apart from receiving director’s remuneration, has no material pecuniary relationship with the company, its promoters, or its associates, and who satisfies the criteria of independence specified in Schedule IV of the Companies Act, 2013.

Key board committees mandated by LODR include the Audit Committee (Regulation 18), Nomination and Remuneration Committee (Regulation 19), Stakeholders Relationship Committee (Regulation 20), and Risk Management Committee (Regulation 21 for top 1000 listed entities). The Audit Committee must comprise a minimum of three directors, with two‑thirds being independent, and must be chaired by an independent director. It oversees financial reporting, internal audit, vigil mechanism, and related‑party transactions. The Nomination and Remuneration Committee formulates criteria for director appointments, evaluates performance, and recommends remuneration policy. The Stakeholders Relationship Committee addresses grievances of shareholders, debenture holders, and other security holders.

Regulation 25 requires the board to conduct an annual performance evaluation of the board, its committees, and individual directors, including independent directors. The evaluation criteria must be disclosed in the annual report. Regulation 24 mandates that at least one independent director of the listed entity shall be a director on the board of an unlisted material subsidiary. The concept of “material subsidiary” is defined as a subsidiary whose income or net worth exceeds 10 % of the consolidated income or net worth of the listed entity.

Recent amendments (SEBI LODR (Amendment) Regulations, 2023) have introduced a “board skill matrix” disclosure, requiring the board to identify the skills/expertise/competencies required in the context of the company’s business and to disclose the names of directors who possess such skills. The amendments also tighten the tenure of independent directors — a person cannot serve as an independent director for more than two consecutive terms of five years each, and a cooling‑off period of three years is required before re‑appointment. Examiners frequently test the interplay between Companies Act provisions (Section 149, Schedule IV) and SEBI LODR, especially on the definition of “material pecuniary relationship” and the composition of the Audit Committee.

  • Confusing the control test under Ind AS 110 with the “majority voting rights” test — the three‑element model must be applied.
  • Treating a design deficiency in IFC as a material weakness without assessing operating effectiveness and materiality.
  • Assuming provisional ITC under Rule 36(4) is final credit; it is only a temporary allowance subject to matching.
  • Neglecting the measurement‑period adjustments in Ind AS 103 and recognising goodwill at acquisition date only.
  • Believing that an independent director can serve unlimited terms; the two‑term limit with cooling‑off period is mandatory under SEBI LODR.
  • Overlooking the requirement that the Audit Committee chairperson must be an independent director and that two‑thirds of its members must be independent.
  • Failing to differentiate between blocked credits under Section 17(5) and reversal of common credit under Rules 42/43.

These advanced topics collectively reinforce the depth required for high‑scoring answers in the optional paper.

Revenue Recognition under Ind AS 115: Five‑Step Model and Contract Modifications

Ind AS 115, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 April 2018, replaces Ind AS 18 and Ind AS 11. The standard applies to all contracts with customers except leases (Ind AS 116), insurance contracts (Ind AS 104) and financial instruments (Ind AS 109). The core principle is that an entity recognises revenue to depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers in an amount that reflects the consideration to which the entity expects to be entitled.

The Five‑Step Model

  1. Identify the contract(s) with a customer – a contract exists when it has commercial substance, parties have approved it, rights and payment terms are identifiable, and collectability is probable.
  2. Identify the performance obligations – distinct goods or services (or a series of distinct goods/services that are substantially the same) are separate performance obligations. A good or service is distinct if the customer can benefit from it on its own or together with readily available resources and the promise to transfer it is separately identifiable.
  3. Determine the transaction price – the amount of consideration expected, excluding amounts collected on behalf of third parties. Variable consideration (discounts, rebates, bonuses) is estimated using either the expected‑value method or the most‑likely‑amount method, constrained by the “highly probable” threshold. A significant financing component adjusts the promised consideration for the time value of money when the payment schedule provides a benefit to either party.
  4. Allocate the transaction price – allocate to each performance obligation on a relative stand‑alone selling price basis. If stand‑alone selling prices are not observable, use the adjusted market assessment approach, expected cost plus margin, or residual approach.
  5. Recognise revenue when (or as) the entity satisfies a performance obligation – revenue is recognised over time if one of three criteria is met (customer simultaneously receives and consumes benefits, entity’s performance creates or enhances an asset the customer controls, or performance does not create an asset with an alternative use and the entity has an enforceable right to payment for performance completed). Otherwise revenue is recognised at a point in time when control transfers.

Contract Modifications

A contract modification is a change in scope or price approved by the parties. Ind AS 115 requires the entity to account for a modification as either a separate contract (if the additional goods/services are distinct and the price reflects stand‑alone selling price) or as a termination of the old contract and creation of a new one (if the remaining goods/services are not distinct). If the modification is not accounted for as a separate contract, the entity updates the transaction price and measures progress towards complete satisfaction of the modified performance obligations.

Key Disclosures

Entities must disclose disaggregated revenue, contract balances (receivables, contract assets, contract liabilities), performance obligations, significant judgements (timing of satisfaction, variable consideration constraints), and assets recognised from costs to obtain or fulfil a contract.

Lease Accounting under Ind AS 116: Lessee and Lessor Accounting, Transition Approaches

Ind AS 116, applicable from 1 April 2019, replaces Ind AS 17. It introduces a single lessee accounting model requiring recognition of a right‑of‑use (RoU) asset and a lease liability for virtually all leases. The standard distinguishes between finance leases and operating leases for lessors, while lessees no longer classify leases.

Lessee Accounting

  • Initial measurement – Lease liability measured at the present value of lease payments not yet paid, discounted using the interest rate implicit in the lease or the lessee’s incremental borrowing rate. RoU asset measured at cost comprising the lease liability, initial direct costs, lease payments made before commencement, and dismantling/restoration obligations.
  • Subsequent measurement – Lease liability accretes interest (effective interest method) and is reduced by lease payments. RoU asset is depreciated over the shorter of lease term and useful life, unless ownership transfers or a purchase option is reasonably certain, in which case depreciation is over useful life. Re‑measurement occurs on lease modification, change in lease term, or change in variable lease payments linked to an index/rate.
  • Exemptions – Short‑term leases (≤12 months) and low‑value assets (≈₹3.5 lakh) may be expensed on a straight‑line basis.

Lessor Accounting

Lessors classify each lease as either a finance lease (substantially all risks/rewards transfer) or an operating lease. Finance leases result in a net investment in the lease (receivable) recognised at the present value of lease payments plus unguaranteed residual value. Operating leases continue to recognise the underlying asset and lease income on a straight‑line basis (or another systematic basis).

Transition Approaches

  1. Full retrospective – Apply Ind AS 116 to each prior reporting period presented, restating comparatives.
  2. Modified retrospective – Recognise the cumulative effect of initial application as an adjustment to the opening balance of retained earnings at the date of initial application (1 April 2019). The lessee measures the RoU asset at either its carrying amount as if Ind AS 116 had always been applied (using the incremental borrowing rate at the date of initial application) or at an amount equal to the lease liability adjusted for prepaid/accrued lease payments.

Disclosure Requirements

Lessee disclosures include a maturity analysis of lease liabilities, variable lease payments not included in the liability, RoU asset movements, and information on extension/termination options. Lessors disclose a maturity analysis of lease receivables (finance leases) and operating lease income.

Worked Example: Lease Classification and Measurement
Entity A enters a 5‑year lease for a machine on 1 April 2022. Annual lease payments are ₹10,00,000 payable at year‑end. The machine’s fair value is ₹45,00,000. The interest rate implicit in the lease is 8 %. The lease does not transfer ownership, nor is there a purchase option. Determine the lease classification for the lessor and compute the initial lease liability and RoU asset for the lessee.
Lessor classification: Since the lease term (5 years) is not the major part of the asset’s economic life (assume 10 years) and the present value of lease payments (₹10,00,000 × PV annuity factor 5 yr @8 % = ₹39,93,000) is less than substantially all of the fair value (₹45,00,000), the lease is an operating lease for the lessor.
Lessee initial measurement: Lease liability = ₹39,93,000 (present value of five payments). RoU asset = lease liability + initial direct costs (assume nil) + prepaid payments (none) = ₹39,93,000. The lessee will depreciate the RoU asset over 5 years (straight‑line ₹7,98,600 per year) and accrete interest on the liability (Year 1 interest = ₹39,93,000 × 8 % = ₹3,19,440).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a lease question appears, first identify whether you are asked from the lessee or lessor perspective. For lessee, write the two‑line journal entries for initial recognition (Dr RoU Asset, Cr Lease Liability) and subsequent measurement (Dr Interest Expense, Dr Lease Liability, Cr Cash; Dr Depreciation, Cr Accumulated Depreciation). For lessor, state the classification test (finance vs operating) and the resulting balance‑sheet treatment. Memorise the PV annuity factor for 5 years at 8 % (3.9927) to speed up calculations.

Financial Instruments under Ind AS 109: Classification, Measurement, Impairment (ECL) and Hedge Accounting

Ind AS 109, effective 1 April 2018, replaces Ind AS 39. It governs recognition, classification, measurement, derecognition, impairment and hedge accounting for financial assets and liabilities.

Classification and Measurement of Financial Assets

Financial assets are classified into three categories based on the entity’s business model for managing the assets and the contractual cash‑flow characteristics (solely payments of principal and interest – SPPI test):

  • Amortised Cost – Business model: hold to collect contractual cash flows; SPPI test passed. Measured at amortised cost using effective interest method.
  • Fair Value Through Other Comprehensive Income (FVTOCI) – Business model: hold to collect and sell; SPPI test passed. Measured at fair value; interest income, impairment gains/losses and foreign exchange gains/losses recognised in profit or loss; other fair‑value changes in OCI. On derecognition, cumulative OCI gain/loss reclassified to profit or loss.
  • Fair Value Through Profit or Loss (FVTPL) – All other assets (including equity instruments not designated at FVTOCI, derivatives, assets failing SPPI). Measured at fair value with all changes in profit or loss.

Equity instruments may be irrevocably designated at FVTOCI at initial recognition (no recycling).

Classification and Measurement of Financial Liabilities

Financial liabilities are generally measured at amortised cost. Exceptions: derivatives, liabilities held for trading, and those designated at FVTPL (fair value option) where designation eliminates or reduces an accounting mismatch.

Impairment – Expected Credit Loss (ECL) Model

Ind AS 109 replaces the incurred‑loss model with a forward‑looking ECL model applied to financial assets at amortised cost, FVTOCI debt instruments, lease receivables, contract assets and loan commitments. The model uses a three‑stage approach:

  1. Stage 1 (12‑month ECL) – No significant increase in credit risk (SICR) since initial recognition. Recognise 12‑month ECL.
  2. Stage 2 (Lifetime ECL – not credit‑impaired) – SICR but not credit‑impaired. Recognise lifetime ECL.
  3. Stage 3 (Lifetime ECL – credit‑impaired) – Objective evidence of impairment. Recognise lifetime ECL; interest revenue calculated on net carrying amount.

Significant increase in credit risk is assessed using reasonable and supportable forward‑looking information. A rebuttable presumption exists that credit risk has increased significantly when contractual payments are more than 30 days past due.

Hedge Accounting

Ind AS 109 aligns hedge accounting with risk management. Three hedge types are permitted:

  • Fair Value Hedge – Hedge of exposure to changes in fair value of a recognised asset/liability or firm commitment. Gain/loss on hedging instrument and hedged item recognised in profit or loss.
  • Cash Flow Hedge – Hedge of exposure to variability in cash flows attributable to a particular risk. Effective portion of gain/loss on hedging instrument recognised in OCI (cash flow hedge reserve); ineffective portion in profit or loss. Amounts in OCI reclassified to profit or loss when hedged cash flows affect profit or loss.
  • Net Investment Hedge – Hedge of foreign currency exposure to a net investment in a foreign operation. Accounted similarly to cash flow hedge.

Qualifying criteria: formal designation, documentation of risk management objective, hedge effectiveness (prospective and retrospective), and an economic relationship between hedged item and hedging instrument.

Corporate Restructuring: Schemes of Arrangement, Merger, Demerger and NCLT Process under Companies Act 2013

Sections 230‑232 of the Companies Act 2013 provide the statutory framework for compromises, arrangements, mergers and amalgamations. The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) is the adjudicating authority; appeals lie to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) and thereafter to the Supreme Court.

Scheme of Arrangement (Section 230)

A scheme may involve compromise with creditors or members, reconstruction, or amalgamation. The process: (1) Board approval; (2) Application to NCLT for convening meetings of creditors/members; (3) Notice with explanatory statement, valuation report, and auditor’s certificate; (4) Meetings – approval by majority in number representing three‑fourths in value of each class; (5) NCLT sanction after hearing objections; (6) Filing of certified copy with Registrar of Companies (RoC) within 30 days. The scheme becomes effective on the appointed date specified therein.

Merger by Absorption (Section 232)

Transferor company merges into transferee company; transferor dissolves without winding up. The scheme must provide for transfer of assets, liabilities, and employees. Shareholders of transferor receive shares in transferee based on a share‑exchange ratio certified by a registered valuer. Tax neutrality under Section 47(vi) of the Income‑Tax Act applies if conditions (continuity of business, shareholding ≥ 90 % etc.) are satisfied.

Demerger (Section 232 read with Section 2(19AA) of Income‑Tax Act)

An undertaking is transferred to a resulting company on a going‑concern basis. Shareholders of demerged company receive shares in resulting company proportionate to their holding. Conditions for tax neutrality: (i) transfer of undertaking as a going concern; (ii) shareholders of demerged company become shareholders of resulting company in same proportion; (iii) consideration discharged only by issue of shares.

Slump Sale (Section 2(42C) of Income‑Tax Act)

Transfer of one or more undertakings for a lump‑sum consideration without values assigned to individual assets/liabilities. Capital gains computed on the difference between lump‑sum consideration and net worth of the undertaking. No tax neutrality; however, Section 50B provides for computation of capital gains.

Fast‑Track Merger (Section 233)

Applicable for mergers between a holding company and its wholly‑owned subsidiary, or between two small companies (paid‑up capital ≤ ₹25 crore, turnover ≤ ₹100 crore). Approval by Regional Director (RD) instead of NCLT, provided no objection from creditors/members and ROC/RD consent.

Key Judicial Precedents

  • Miheer H. Mafatlal v. Mafatlal Industries Ltd (1997) – Supreme Court held that NCLT (then High Court) must ensure fairness to minority shareholders; valuation must be independent.
  • Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India (2012) – Clarified that indirect transfer of shares of an Indian company does not attract capital gains in India unless the transaction is a “transfer” under Section 2(47) read with Section 9(1)(i).
  • Tata Steel Ltd v. Cyrus Mistry (2021) – NCLAT/NCLT jurisdiction over oppression and mismanagement petitions in the context of scheme approvals.

International Taxation: DTAA Interpretation, BEPS Action Plans, GAAR and Equalisation Levy

India’s network of Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) follows the OECD Model Tax Convention. Interpretation principles are guided by the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) and domestic judicial pronouncements.

DTAA Tie‑Breaker Rules (Article 4)

For dual‑resident individuals, the tie‑breaker sequence: (i) permanent home; (ii) centre of vital interests; (iii) habitual abode; (iv) nationality; (v) mutual agreement procedure (MAP). For dual‑resident companies, place of effective management (POEM) is the decisive test. India’s POEM rules (Notification No. 9/2017) deem a company resident in India if its key management and commercial decisions are made in India.

BEPS Action Plans – India’s Implementation

  • Action 1 (Digital Economy) – India introduced Equalisation Levy (EL) in 2016 (6 % on online advertising) and expanded in 2020 (2 % on e‑commerce supply/services by non‑residents with turnover > ₹2 crore).
  • Action 6 (Treaty Abuse) – Principal Purpose Test (PPT) inserted in India’s DTAAs via MLI (Multilateral Instrument) ratified 2019; also domestic GAAR (Sections 95‑102, effective 1 April 2017).
  • Action 13 (Country‑by‑Country Reporting) – Master file, local file and CbCR filing for MNE groups with consolidated revenue ≥ ₹6,400 crore (≈€750 million).
  • Action 14 (Dispute Resolution) – MAP timelines and mandatory binding arbitration in updated treaties.

General Anti‑Avoidance Rule (GAAR)

Sections 95‑102 empower tax authorities to declare an “impermissible avoidance arrangement” (IAA) if the main purpose is to obtain a tax benefit and the arrangement lacks commercial substance. The onus shifts to the taxpayer to prove commercial substance. The Approving Panel (Chairperson: Principal Chief Commissioner) must approve GAAR invocation. Safe harbours exist for certain transactions (e.g., FII investments, specified restructuring).

Equalisation Levy (EL)

  • EL‑1 (2016) – 6 % on consideration for online advertisement or digital space provided by non‑resident to resident or Indian PE. Threshold: ₹1 lakh per year per service provider.
  • EL‑2 (2020) – 2 % on e‑commerce supply/services by non‑resident e‑commerce operator (turnover > ₹2 crore). Excludes transactions subject to EL‑1 or where the operator has a PE in India.

EL is not covered by DTAA; it is a separate levy under the Finance Act. Non‑deduction attracts interest (1 % per month) and penalty (equal to levy amount).

Key Cases

  • Azadi Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2003) – Supreme Court upheld the India‑Mauritius DTAA, ruling that treaty benefits cannot be denied merely because of treaty shopping; later addressed by PPT/MLI.
  • Vodafone (2012) – Clarified that share transfer of a foreign company holding Indian assets is not taxable in India absent specific anti‑avoidance provision; led to retrospective amendment (Section 9(1)(i) Explanation 5) and later GAAR.
  • Google India Pvt Ltd v. DCIT (2019) – Held that EL‑1 applies on gross consideration without deduction of TDS; confirmed constitutional validity.
  • Confusing the SPPI test with the business‑model test – both must be satisfied for amortised‑cost or FVTOCI classification.
  • Assuming all lease modifications are accounted for as separate contracts – only if the additional goods/services are distinct and priced at stand‑alone selling price.
  • Treating 12‑month ECL as the default for all financial assets – Stage 2 applies once SICR is identified, even if payments are not past due.
  • Overlooking the requirement that a demerger must transfer an “undertaking” on a going‑concern basis for tax neutrality under Section 2(19AA).
  • Believing GAAR can be invoked by any assessing officer – only the Approving Panel can sanction GAAR invocation.
  • Ignoring the POEM test for corporate residence in DTAA tie‑breaker – place of effective management overrides place of incorporation.
  • Failing to distinguish EL‑1 (advertising) from EL‑2 (e‑commerce) thresholds and rates – they are separate levies with different compliance obligations.

These advanced topics complete the core coverage required for high‑scoring answers in the optional paper.

Standard Costing: Advanced Variance Analysis and Investigation Decisions

Standard costing moves beyond basic price and usage variances into a structured framework for operational control. Material variances split into price (actual price − standard price) × actual quantity purchased, usage (actual quantity used − standard quantity allowed) × standard price, mix (actual mix proportion − standard mix proportion) × total actual quantity × standard price, and yield (actual yield − standard yield for actual input) × standard cost per unit of output. Labour variances mirror this: rate (actual rate − standard rate) × actual hours, efficiency (actual hours − standard hours allowed) × standard rate, idle time (idle hours × standard rate), mix and yield analogues. Variable overhead yields expenditure (actual variable overhead − standard rate × actual hours) and efficiency (actual hours − standard hours) × standard variable overhead rate. Fixed overhead splits into expenditure (actual − budgeted), volume (budgeted hours − standard hours for actual output) × standard fixed overhead rate, capacity (actual hours − budgeted hours) × standard rate, and efficiency (standard hours for actual output − actual hours) × standard rate. Sales variances complete the picture: price (actual price − budgeted price) × actual units, volume (actual units − budgeted units) × budgeted profit per unit, mix and quantity sub-variances under volume.

Investigation decisions rely on statistical control charts (Shewhart charts with 2σ warning limits and 3σ action limits) and cost-benefit thresholds. A variance is investigated if |variance| > k × σ, where k is chosen such that the expected cost of investigation (Ci) plus expected loss from non-investigation (Ln) is minimised. The economic investigation model sets the critical value V* = √(2 × Ci × σ² / Ln). Interrelationships matter: a favourable material price variance from cheaper raw material often drives an adverse usage variance; a favourable labour rate variance from hiring less-skilled workers drives adverse efficiency and idle time variances. Standards must be revised when permanent changes occur in technology, product design, or input prices — typically when cumulative variances exceed 5–10 % of standard cost for three consecutive periods. Controllable vs. non-controllable classification guides managerial accountability: price variances are often non-controllable (market-driven), while usage and efficiency are controllable.

Capital Budgeting: Risk Analysis, Real Options and Valuation of Flexibility

Traditional NPV uses a single risk-adjusted discount rate (RADR) derived from CAPM: ke = rf + β × (rm − rf). This assumes constant risk across time and ignores managerial flexibility. Certainty Equivalent (CE) approach adjusts cash flows instead: NPV = Σ (αt × CFt) / (1 + rf)ᵗ, where αt ∈ [0,1] reflects risk aversion. Sensitivity analysis identifies critical variables (selling price, volume, variable cost, initial outlay, project life) by computing % change in NPV per 1 % change in each variable; the switching value is the variable value at which NPV = 0. Scenario analysis evaluates best-case, base-case, and worst-case NPVs with joint probability distributions. Monte Carlo simulation models 1,000–10,000 trials sampling from input distributions (triangular, normal, beta) to generate an NPV probability distribution; key outputs are mean NPV, standard deviation, and probability of negative NPV.

Decision trees map sequential decisions under uncertainty: decision nodes (squares) for management choices, chance nodes (circles) for probabilistic outcomes. Each branch carries a probability and conditional cash flow; the optimal path maximises expected NPV by backward induction. Real options value managerial flexibility: option to delay (wait for information), option to expand (scale up if demand high), option to abandon (exit if demand low), option to contract (scale down), option to switch (change inputs/outputs). Valuation uses binomial lattice (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) or Black-Scholes adapted for real assets. For an abandonment option: put option value = X × e−rfT × N(−d₂) − V × N(−d₁), where V = present value of continuing project, X = salvage value, σ = volatility of project value. The option to expand is a call: C = V × N(d₁) − X × e−rfT × N(d₂). Real options typically increase project value by 10–30 % over passive NPV; ignoring them leads to systematic under-investment in R&D, natural resources, and technology platforms.

Worked Example: Real Option to Abandon
A project requires ₹50 crore initial outlay. Expected PV of future cash flows (V) = ₹55 crore. Volatility (σ) = 35 % p.a. Risk-free rate = 7 %. The project can be abandoned after 2 years for a salvage value (X) of ₹30 crore. Value the abandonment option using Black-Scholes and state the total project value including the option.
Step 1: Identify parameters. V = 55, X = 30, T = 2, rf = 0.07, σ = 0.35. Step 2: Compute d₁ = [ln(V/X) + (rf + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T) = [ln(55/30) + (0.07 + 0.35²/2)×2] / (0.35×√2) = [0.606 + (0.07 + 0.06125)×2] / 0.495 = [0.606 + 0.2625] / 0.495 = 1.754. d₂ = d₁ − σ√T = 1.754 − 0.495 = 1.259. Step 3: N(−d₁) = N(−1.754) = 0.0398; N(−d₂) = N(−1.259) = 0.104. Step 4: Put value = X·e−rfT·N(−d₂) − V·N(−d₁) = 30×e−0.14×0.104 − 55×0.0398 = 30×0.869×0.104 − 2.189 = 2.712 − 2.189 = ₹0.523 crore. Step 5: Passive NPV = 55 − 50 = ₹5 crore. Total project value = 5 + 0.523 = ₹5.523 crore. The abandonment option adds ₹0.523 crore (10.5 % of passive NPV).
Exam-Day Strategy
In numerical questions on real options, always state the option type (call/put), underlying asset (project value), strike (salvage/expansion cost), and time to expiry. If Black-Scholes is specified, show d₁ and d₂ calculations stepwise; if binomial, draw the two-step lattice with up/down factors u = eσ√Δt, d = 1/u, and risk-neutral probability p = (erfΔt − d)/(u − d). For sensitivity/scenario questions, present a table with variable, base value, % change, NPV change, and switching value. Never omit the decision rule: accept if NPV > 0 or option-adjusted NPV > 0.

Audit of Banking Companies: NPA Classification, Provisioning Norms and Long Form Audit Report

The audit of banking companies is governed by the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, RBI Master Directions, and Standards on Auditing (SAs 700, 701, 705, 706, 710, 720). Asset classification follows the 90-day overdue norm: an account turns Non-Performing Asset (NPA) when interest/principal remains overdue for > 90 days. Categories: Standard (no default), Sub-standard (NPA ≤ 12 months), Doubtful (NPA > 12 months) split into D1 (12–24 months), D2 (24–48 months), D3 (> 48 months), and Loss assets (uncollectible, identified by auditor/RBI). Provisioning norms (RBI Master Direction DBR.No.BP.BC.83/21.04.048/2014-15): Standard assets — 0.40 % for direct advances to agricultural/SME, 0.25 % for commercial real estate, 1.00 % for CRE-RH, 0.40 % for others; Sub-standard — 15 % (25 % for unsecured); Doubtful D1 — 25 % (40 % unsecured), D2 — 40 % (60 % unsecured), D3 — 100 %; Loss — 100 %. Restructuring frameworks (MSME restructuring 2019, Resolution Framework 1.0/2.0 for COVID) permit asset classification upgrade if restructuring is implemented before NPA date and specified conditions met.

The Long Form Audit Report (LFAR) is a statutory requirement under Section 30(1B) of the Banking Regulation Act. It covers: (i) capital adequacy (CRAR ≥ 9 % for scheduled commercial banks), (ii) asset quality and provisioning adequacy, (iii) income recognition (interest on NPA recognised only on realisation), (iv) connected lending and exposure limits (single borrower ≤ 15 % of Tier I capital, group ≤ 25 %), (v) KYC/AML compliance, (vi) fraud classification and reporting (Fraud Classification and Reporting Master Direction 2016), (vii) internal control and audit function (risk-based internal audit). The auditor must issue a Certificate on Compliance with RBI Directions alongside the main audit report. CARO 2020 does not apply to banking companies (exempted under clause 1(2)(iv)). Peer Review by ICAI is mandatory for auditors of banks; the Peer Review Board assesses compliance with SAs, engagement quality control, and independence. Key audit procedures include: independent verification of NPA identification (test-checking overdue reports, borrower-wise confirmation), re-computation of provisioning, evaluation of security valuation (immovable property valued every 3 years by approved valuers), and testing of interest application and penal interest logic.

Impairment of Non-Financial Assets under Ind AS 36: CGU Identification, Value in Use and Reversal Prohibition

Ind AS 36 applies to property, plant and equipment (Ind AS 16), intangible assets (Ind AS 38), goodwill (Ind AS 103), right-of-use assets (Ind AS 116), and investment property at cost model (Ind AS 40). Impairment testing frequency: annually for goodwill, intangible assets with indefinite useful life, and intangible assets not yet available for use; for other assets, only when indicators of impairment exist (external: market decline, adverse changes in technology/laws, interest rate increases; internal: obsolescence, physical damage, restructuring, worse-than-expected performance). A Cash-Generating Unit (CGU) is the smallest identifiable group of assets generating cash inflows largely independent of other assets. CGU identification follows management’s internal reporting structure; goodwill is allocated to CGUs or groups of CGUs expected to benefit from the business combination.

Recoverable amount = higher of Fair Value Less Costs of Disposal (FVLCD) and Value in Use (VIU). VIU = present value of future cash flows expected from continuing use and ultimate disposal. Cash flow projections: based on most recent budgets/forecasts (max 5 years unless longer justified), extrapolated using steady/terminal growth rate not exceeding long-term average growth for the product/industry/country. Discount rate: pre-tax rate reflecting current market assessments of time value of money and risks specific to the asset (not reflected in cash flows). For VIU, use pre-tax discount rate; for FVLCD, use post-tax rate. Impairment loss allocation: first to goodwill allocated to CGU, then pro-rata to other assets based on carrying amount, but not below highest of FVLCD, VIU, and zero. Reversal of impairment loss: prohibited for goodwill (Ind AS 36.124). For other assets, reversal permitted if estimates change, up to the carrying amount that would have existed (net of depreciation/amortisation) had no impairment been recognised. Disclosure requirements (Ind AS 36.130–134): events/circumstances leading to impairment/reversal, amount recognised in P&L/OCI, recoverable amount basis (VIU/FVLCD), key assumptions (growth rate, discount rate), sensitivity analysis for reasonably possible changes in key assumptions.

Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets under Ind AS 37: Constructive Obligations and Restructuring Provisions

Ind AS 37 distinguishes three categories. A provision is recognised when: (a) a present obligation (legal or constructive) exists from a past event; (b) an outflow of resources is probable (> 50 %); (c) a reliable estimate can be made. A constructive obligation arises when an entity’s past practice, published policy, or specific current statement creates a valid expectation in third parties that it will discharge certain responsibilities (e.g., environmental cleanup beyond legal requirement, voluntary product recall). A contingent liability is a possible obligation from past events confirmed only by future events, or a present obligation not probable or not measurable — not recognised, only disclosed unless remote. A contingent asset is a possible asset from past events confirmed by future events — not recognised, disclosed only when probable.

Restructuring provisions require a detailed formal plan identifying: business/part concerned, principal locations, employees affected, expenditures, implementation timeline. The plan must have been announced to affected parties or implementation started before the reporting date to raise a valid expectation. Only direct expenditures necessarily entailed by restructuring (e.g., termination benefits, lease termination penalties) qualify; future operating losses are excluded. Measurement: best estimate of expenditure to settle the obligation at reporting date. For a single obligation, use most likely outcome; for a large population, use expected value (probability-weighted). Discounting is required when the effect is material: use a pre-tax discount rate reflecting current market assessments of time value and risks specific to the liability; the unwinding of discount is recognised as finance cost. Reimbursements (e.g., insurance, indemnity) are recognised as a separate asset only when virtually certain (> 90–95 %). Onerous contracts: when unavoidable costs of meeting obligations exceed economic benefits, recognise a provision for the lower of cost of fulfilment and cost of termination. Disclosure (Ind AS 37.84–86): carrying amount at beginning/end, additional provisions, amounts used, unused amounts reversed, increase from discount unwinding, brief description of nature and timing, major assumptions, expected reimbursement.

  • Treating all variances as equally investigable — ignoring statistical control limits and cost-benefit thresholds leads to over-investigation of immaterial variances and under-investigation of systemic ones.
  • Using a single risk-adjusted discount rate for all project cash flows — fails to capture changing risk profiles over time and ignores real options embedded in the project.
  • Classifying a restructured loan as standard immediately after restructuring — RBI norms require a specified performance period (typically 1 year of satisfactory payment) before upgrade from NPA.
  • Applying post-tax discount rates to pre-tax cash flows in VIU calculation under Ind AS 36 — the standard mandates pre-tax discount rate for pre-tax cash flows; mismatch inflates VIU and understates impairment.
  • Recognising a provision for future operating losses under a restructuring plan — Ind AS 37 explicitly prohibits this; only direct incremental costs of restructuring qualify.
  • Assuming goodwill impairment can be reversed in subsequent periods if recoverable amount improves — Ind AS 36.124 imposes an absolute prohibition on goodwill impairment reversal.
  • Disclosing contingent liabilities only when probability exceeds 50 % — the threshold for disclosure is not remote (i.e., > 5–10 %), not probable; many entities under-disclose by applying the wrong threshold.
