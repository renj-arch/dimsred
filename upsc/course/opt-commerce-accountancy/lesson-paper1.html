Commerce Paper I — Accounting & Company Law (Detailed)

Company Accounts

  • Issue of shares: Issue at par, premium, discount (prohibited except sweat equity), forfeiture & reissue of shares, surrender of shares, calls in arrears & calls in advance, interest on calls in arrears & calls in advance (Table F — 10% & 12% respectively), issue of debentures (at par/premium/discount, for cash, for consideration other than cash, as collateral security), interest on debentures, redemption of debentures (out of profits, out of capital, DMDR — Debenture Redemption Reserve 25% for listed, 10% for unlisted unless exempt), redemption out of proceeds of fresh issue/sinking fund/investment method.
  • Final accounts of companies: Schedule III format — balance sheet (equity & liabilities: share capital, reserves & surplus, money received against share warrants, share application money pending allotment, non-current liabilities: long-term borrowings, deferred tax liabilities, other long-term liabilities, long-term provisions; current liabilities: short-term borrowings, trade payables, other current liabilities, short-term provisions; assets: non-current assets — tangible/intangible/Capital WIP/intangible assets under development, non-current investments, deferred tax assets (net), long-term loans & advances, other non-current assets; current assets — current investments, inventories, trade receivables, cash & cash equivalents, short-term loans & advances, other current assets), statement of profit & loss (revenue from operations, other income, total revenue, expenses — cost of materials consumed, purchase of stock-in-trade, changes in inventories, employee benefits expense, finance costs, depreciation & amortisation, other expenses, total expenses, profit before tax, tax expense — current tax & deferred tax, profit for the period, other comprehensive income, total comprehensive income, EPS), cash flow statement (operating, investing, financing — direct & indirect method), statement of changes in equity.
  • Branch accounting: Debtors system, debtors & stock control system, stock & debtors system, final accounts system (wholesale & retail price), independent branches (foreign branches — conversion of foreign currency, translation of foreign branches — monetary items at closing rate, non-monetary at historical, income items at average rate, exchange difference to P&L).
  • Departmental accounting: Departmental trading & P&L account, allocation of common expenses (sales value, floor area, number of employees, time, direct allocation), inter-departmental transfers (at cost or at market price), unrealised profit elimination (1/3rd or 1/(1+margin%) of unsold stock).
  • Consignment accounts: Consignor & consignee, commission (ordinary, del-credere, del-credere & over-riding), valuation of unsold stock (cost or market price whichever lower, less proportionate expenses), abnormal loss (normal loss estimated & spread over good units; abnormal loss valued at cost and transferred to P&L), goods sent on consignment at cost or invoice price (with loading & reserve for unsold stock).
  • Joint venture accounts: Joint venture vs partnership vs consignment, methods — (1) separate set of books, (2) no separate set of books (each venturer records own transactions), (3) memorandum joint venture account method, settlement of accounts.
  • Royalty accounts: Minimum rent (dead rent), short-workings, recoupment of short-workings, strike & lock-out clause, sub-lease, accounting treatment in books of lessee & lessor.

  • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR): Section 135 — applicability (net worth ₹500 crore or turnover ₹1000 crore or net profit ₹5 crore), CSR committee, 2% of average net profits of preceding 3 years, activities (Schedule VII — eradicating hunger, education, gender equality, environmental sustainability, etc.), CSR report in board's report.
  • Winding up: Modes — voluntary (members' voluntary & creditors' voluntary), by tribunal (compulsory — grounds: inability to pay debts, just & equitable, default in filing RoC, acting against sovereignty/integrity/security of India), official liquidator, contributories, preferential payments (workmen's dues, government dues, secured creditors), declaration of solvency for voluntary winding up.
  • Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code 2016: Insolvency resolution process (corporate insolvency resolution process — CIRP, initiation by financial/operational creditor/corporate debtor, declaration of moratorium, appointment of interim resolution professional (IRP), constitution of committee of creditors (CoC), appointment of resolution professional (RP), invitation of resolution plans, approval by CoC (75% voting share), implementation, liquidation (liquidation order, liquidator, realisation of assets, distribution — waterfall: secured creditors, workmen & employees, central/state government debts, unsecured creditors, preference shareholders, equity shareholders), fast track insolvency resolution process (for small companies), cross-border insolvency (UNCITRAL Model Law adoption pending), personal insolvency (individuals & partnership firms), insolvency professionals & IPAs, information utilities (National e-Governance Services Ltd. — NeSL), adjudicating authority (NCLT for companies, NCLAT for appeals, DRT for individuals/Draft Appellate Tribunal for individuals).

Technique: Companies Act 2013 answer
Question: 'Discuss the provisions related to CSR under Companies Act 2013.'
Answer: Section 135 — applies to companies with net worth ₹500 crore or turnover ₹1000 crore or net profit ₹5 crore in any preceding financial year. Board must constitute CSR committee (3+ directors, at least 1 independent), formulate CSR policy, ensure spending 2% of average net profits of 3 preceding financial years on activities in Schedule VII (education, healthcare, rural development, environment, etc.), disclose in board report, disclose reasons if unspent (transfer to Unspent CSR Account & transfer to specified fund within 6 months), penalties for non-compliance (fine ₹50,000-₹25 lakh on company, imprisonment up to 3 years or fine ₹50,000-₹25 lakh or both for officer in default). Significance: India first country to mandate CSR; aligns business with social welfare.

Insolvency Resolution Process: The Role of the Committee of Creditors (CoC)

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) introduced a paradigm shift from debtor-in-possession to creditor-in-control mechanisms. At the heart of this transformation lies the Committee of Creditors (CoC), a body constituted under Section 29A of the IBC. Unlike previous regimes where individual lenders held disparate powers, the CoC acts as a unified decision-making entity for all financial creditors in a corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP). The composition is strictly defined: it must comprise at least five members and no more than ten, representing not less than 66% of the total outstanding operational debt owed to financial creditors. Crucially, the Code mandates that all financial creditors are eligible for membership unless they have been adjudged insolvent or involved in fraud. Secured creditors (secured by immovable property) and unsecured creditors generally cannot be mixed on a pro-rata basis within the CoC; instead, secured creditors participate as equals with unsecured financial creditors regarding voting rights.

The most critical function of the CoC is exercising veto power. Under Section 30(2), any resolution plan proposed for approval must be accepted by a majority in value of not less than 66% of the financial creditors present and voting at the meeting. If the CoC rejects the plan, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) is statutorily barred from approving it, effectively ending the rescue attempt unless a fresh proposal is made. This threshold ensures that minority creditors cannot be forced into a plan they deem detrimental to their recovery prospects. Furthermore, the CoC has the authority to appoint an interim resolution professional (IRP) or replace the initial IRP with a new one if satisfied that the current professional is not acting in the interest of the corporate debtor. The CoC also decides on the appointment of a liquidation professional if the resolution process fails.

A significant procedural evolution occurred via the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) guidelines, which clarified that the CoC must meet within 14 days of its constitution. The voting weightage is strictly based on the value of debt owed to each member, not the number of lenders. For instance, a bank owing ₹90 crore holds more sway than ten small NBFCs owing ₹9 crore each combined, provided their individual stakes are aggregated correctly. This mechanism prevents fragmentation and ensures that the largest stakeholders drive the restructuring strategy. The CoC is also empowered to approve the budget for the resolution professional's remuneration and expenses, ensuring cost-efficiency in a distressed asset scenario.

Examiner's trap
A common error is assuming that operational creditors (suppliers, employees) have voting rights in the CoC. Correct answer: Operational creditors are not part of the CoC and cannot vote on the resolution plan under Section 30(2). Their claims are treated separately; if a resolution plan does not provide for their payment, they may file an application to NCLT for approval of the plan under Section 12A (if applicable) or seek separate relief, but they do not participate in the CoC voting dynamics. Another trap is thinking secured creditors sit outside the CoC entirely; they are full members unless specifically excluded due to fraud.
Exam-day strategy
When answering questions on IBC, always anchor your response to specific sections (e.g., Section 30 for CoC powers, Section 29A for eligibility). Use the flow: Constitution → Composition → Powers (Veto, Appointment) → Thresholds (66%). Mention the 14-day meeting rule as a procedural safeguard. Avoid generic statements about "bankruptcy"; use precise terms like "Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process" (CIRP).

Transfer Pricing Regulations: Arm's Length Principle and Documentation

Under the Income Tax Act, 1961, Chapter XIV-D deals with Transfer Pricing (TP), governed by Rule 10A of the Income Tax Rules. The core principle is the Arm's Length Principle (ALP), which mandates that transactions between related parties (domestic or international) must be priced as if they were conducted between independent entities in an open market. This prevents profit shifting to low-tax jurisdictions. The statute prescribes six specific methods to determine the ALP, listed in Section 92C: Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) method (most reliable but data-heavy), Resale Price Method, Cost Plus Method, Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM) (widely used due to availability of data), Transactional Profit Split Method, and Profit Split Method. The taxpayer must select the most appropriate method based on the nature of the transaction, though TNMM is often preferred for manufacturing and trading entities.

The regulatory framework requires strict documentation. Every entity engaged in international transactions with a related person must maintain a detailed Transfer Pricing Documentation (TPD), including a Master File (global strategy), Local File (entity-specific analysis), and Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) for large groups. The TPD must be prepared by the end of the financial year, though it can be submitted later with an extension. Failure to maintain TPD attracts penalties under Section 270AA(1A) — a penalty equal to 5% of the amount of international transaction or deemed international transaction, whichever is higher. The Assessing Officer (AO) has the power to examine the documentation and make adjustments if the ALP is not met. Recent amendments have tightened the definition of "related party" and introduced stricter scrutiny on cost-plus margins in service sectors.

The concept of Intuitiveness plays a role here: if a taxpayer claims a particular pricing method without supporting data, the AO can reject it. The law also allows for advance pricing arrangements (APA), where the tax authority and taxpayer agree in advance on the ALP methodology for future transactions, providing certainty. However, unilateral APAs are limited to cases where the taxpayer has not shifted profits aggressively. The documentation must be updated annually; outdated reports lead to automatic penalties. In cross-border disputes, Mutual Agreement Procedures (MAP) under Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) become relevant if adjustments result in double taxation.

Direct Taxes: Assessment Proceedings and Objections

The assessment process under the Income Tax Act is a structured sequence of events designed to verify income declarations. The primary modes are Self-Assessment (by the taxpayer), Regular Assessment (under Section 143(1) — scrutiny of returns for errors/arithmetic mistakes), and Scrutiny Assessment (under Section 143(3) — detailed examination where notice is issued under Section 148 if discrepancies are found). A crucial distinction lies in the timeline: regular assessment must be completed within one year from the end of the relevant assessment year, while scrutiny assessments have no statutory time limit but are generally expected to conclude within six months. If an assessee fails to comply with notice u/s 143(2), the AO can proceed unilaterally.

The Objection and Appeal mechanism is vital for rights protection. Under Section 154 (rectification of mistakes) and Section 153C (correction of errors in assessment orders), specific timelines apply. If an assessee disagrees with the assessment order, they must file a written objection under Rule 12 of the Income Tax Rules within three months from the end of the month in which the notice u/s 148 was issued or the date of communication of the order, whichever is earlier. The AO then issues an Order of Objection.

Consolidated Financial Statements under Ind AS 110

Ind AS 110, Consolidated Financial Statements, establishes the principle that an entity which controls one or more other entities must present consolidated financial statements. Control is defined as the power to govern the financial and operating policies of an investee so as to obtain benefits from its activities. The standard replaces the earlier “majority‑voting‑right” test with a three‑element control model: (1) power over the investee, (2) exposure, or rights, to variable returns from involvement with the investee, and (3) the ability to use power to affect those returns. The assessment of control is continuous; a parent must reassess whenever facts and circumstances indicate a change.

Consolidation procedures require the parent to combine like items of assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses and cash flows of the parent and its subsidiaries. Intra‑group balances, transactions, income and expenses are eliminated in full. Non‑controlling interests (NCI) are presented within equity, separate from the parent’s equity, and are measured at the proportionate share of the acquiree’s identifiable net assets at acquisition date (the “proportionate‑share” method) unless the entity elects the fair‑value method for a particular business combination. Goodwill arising on consolidation is recognised as an asset and tested for impairment annually or more frequently if indicators exist.

Ind AS 110 also addresses special purpose entities (SPEs) and structured entities. An entity consolidates an SPE when it has the power to direct the relevant activities of the SPE and is exposed to variable returns. The standard provides detailed guidance on “de‑facto” control, protective rights, and agency relationships. Disclosures include the nature and extent of significant restrictions on the ability to access or use assets of subsidiaries, the financial effect of changes in ownership interests that do not result in loss of control, and the nature of any contractual arrangements that may affect the consolidation conclusion.

From an examination perspective, candidates must be able to distinguish between control, joint control (Ind AS 111) and significant influence (Ind AS 28). A typical question may present a scenario where a parent holds 45 % voting rights but has a contractual right to appoint the majority of the board; the correct answer is that control exists because the parent has power over the relevant activities. Another frequent twist involves the treatment of NCI when a subsidiary issues additional shares to third parties — the parent’s share of equity changes, but control is retained; the adjustment is recognised directly in equity.

Audit of Internal Financial Controls under Section 143(3)(i) of the Companies Act

Section 143(3)(i) of the Companies Act, 2013 mandates that the auditor’s report on the financial statements of a company must state whether the company has adequate internal financial controls (IFC) with reference to financial statements and the operating effectiveness of such controls. This requirement applies to all companies except certain small companies and one‑person companies as notified. The audit of IFC is performed in accordance with Standards on Auditing (SA) 315 (Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement) and SA 330 (The Auditor’s Responses to Assessed Risks).

The auditor evaluates the design and implementation of controls relevant to the preparation of reliable financial statements. Key components include the control environment, risk assessment process, control activities, information and communication systems, and monitoring activities. The auditor obtains an understanding of the entity’s IFC by performing walkthroughs, inspecting documentation, and inquiring of management and those charged with governance. Testing of operating effectiveness involves selecting a sample of transactions and verifying that controls operated consistently throughout the period.

If the auditor identifies a material weakness — a deficiency, or combination of deficiencies, in IFC such that there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement of the financial statements will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis — the auditor must express an adverse opinion on IFC. A significant deficiency is less severe but still merits communication to those charged with governance. The auditor’s report on IFC is separate from the opinion on the financial statements; however, a material weakness in IFC often leads to a qualified or adverse opinion on the financial statements as well.

Recent amendments (Companies (Audit and Auditors) Amendment Rules, 2021) have clarified that the IFC report must be annexed to the auditor’s report and must contain a statement on the adequacy and operating effectiveness of IFC. The auditor is also required to report on the adequacy of the internal audit function, where applicable. In practice, examiners test the candidate’s ability to differentiate between a “design deficiency” and an “operating deficiency”, and to apply the concept of “reasonable assurance” rather than absolute assurance.

Worked Illustration: Material Weakness vs Significant Deficiency
A manufacturing company’s revenue recognition process relies on a single spreadsheet maintained by the sales manager. The spreadsheet is not password‑protected, and there is no independent review of journal entries posted from it. During the year, an error of ₹12 lakh (2 % of revenue) remained undetected until the year‑end audit. The auditor concludes that the control deficiency is a material weakness. Explain the reasoning and the impact on the auditor’s report.
The deficiency satisfies the definition of a material weakness because (a) the control design is absent — no segregation of duties, no independent review, and no access restrictions; (b) the error of ₹12 lakh, though quantitatively 2 % of revenue, is qualitatively material as it pertains to revenue recognition, a high‑risk area; (c) there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement could occur and not be prevented or detected on a timely basis. Consequently, the auditor must issue an adverse opinion on IFC under Section 143(3)(i) and, because the weakness relates to a significant account, also consider a qualified or adverse opinion on the financial statements. The auditor’s report will contain a separate paragraph titled “Report on Internal Financial Controls” stating the adverse opinion, and the main audit opinion paragraph will reference the IFC adverse opinion.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the auditor’s reporting responsibility on IFC, structure the answer as: (1) statutory requirement (Section 143(3)(i)), (2) applicable SAs (315, 330), (3) definition of material weakness and significant deficiency, (4) impact on IFC opinion and on financial statement opinion, (5) illustrative example. Cite the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Amendment Rules, 2021 for the annexure requirement. Avoid generic statements like “the auditor checks controls”; use precise terminology — “design effectiveness”, “operating effectiveness”, “reasonable assurance”.

Goods and Services Tax: Input Tax Credit Matching and Reversal Provisions

The GST regime under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (CGST Act) allows registered persons to claim Input Tax Credit (ITC) on inward supplies used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business. Sections 16 to 18 of the CGST Act, read with Rules 36 to 45 of the CGST Rules, prescribe the conditions, matching mechanism, and reversal provisions for ITC. The fundamental condition under Section 16(2) is that the recipient must be in possession of a tax invoice or debit note, the goods or services must have been received, the tax charged must have been paid to the government by the supplier, and the recipient must have furnished the return under Section 39.

The matching mechanism operates through the auto‑populated GSTR‑2A/2B (generated from suppliers’ GSTR‑1) and the recipient’s GSTR‑3B. Where the supplier has not uploaded the invoice or has not paid the tax, the credit appears as “pending” in GSTR‑2A/2B. The recipient may claim provisional credit up to 5 % of the eligible credit under Rule 36(4) (the “5 % provisional rule”), but the final credit is allowed only after matching. If the mismatch persists beyond the due date of filing the annual return (GSTR‑9) for the relevant financial year, the credit is reversed under Section 17(5) read with Rule 37.

Section 17(5) enumerates specific supplies on which ITC is not available (blocked credits), such as motor vehicles for personal use, club memberships, and certain construction services. Additionally, Rule 42 and Rule 43 prescribe the reversal of ITC attributable to exempt supplies and to non‑business purposes, respectively. The reversal is calculated on a pro‑rata basis using the turnover method: (Exempt turnover ÷ Total turnover) × Common credit. The reversed amount is added to output tax liability in the month of reversal and is eligible for re‑claim if the condition ceases in a subsequent period.

Recent notifications (e.g., Notification No. 14/2022‑Central Tax) have extended the time limit for availing ITC on invoices pertaining to FY 2021‑22 up to 30 November 2023, subject to payment of interest under Section 50. The concept of “deemed credit” under Section 18(1) applies when a person becomes liable to registration or opts for composition scheme; credit on inputs held in stock on the day immediately preceding the date of registration is allowed. Examiners often test the interplay between provisional credit, matching, and reversal — candidates must demonstrate the chronological flow: invoice receipt → GSTR‑2A reflection → provisional claim → matching → final allowance or reversal.

Business Combinations and Purchase Price Allocation under Ind AS 103

Ind AS 103, Business Combinations, applies the acquisition method to all business combinations, requiring the acquirer to recognise identifiable assets acquired, liabilities assumed, and any non‑controlling interest at their acquisition‑date fair values. The consideration transferred includes cash, equity instruments, contingent consideration, and settlement of pre‑existing relationships. Goodwill is measured as the excess of the consideration transferred plus the amount of any non‑controlling interest over the net of the acquisition‑date fair values of identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed.

The standard mandates a measurement period not exceeding one year from the acquisition date, during which the acquirer may adjust provisional amounts recognised at acquisition date to reflect new information about facts and circumstances that existed at the acquisition date. Adjustments are recognised retrospectively, with corresponding adjustments to goodwill. Identifiable intangible assets — such as customer relationships, technology, brand names, and non‑compete agreements — must be recognised separately from goodwill if they meet the separability or contractual‑legal criterion.

Contingent consideration is classified either as equity or a liability based on the terms of the arrangement. If classified as a liability, it is remeasured at fair value at each reporting date, with changes recognised in profit or loss. If classified as equity, it is not remeasured. The acquirer must also account for acquisition‑related costs (e.g., legal, advisory) as expenses in the period incurred, except for costs to issue equity or debt instruments, which are accounted for under Ind AS 32 and Ind AS 109 respectively.

Disclosure requirements are extensive: the name and description of the acquiree, acquisition date, percentage of voting equity interests acquired, primary reasons for the combination, qualitative description of the factors that make up goodwill, and a reconciliation of the carrying amount of goodwill at the beginning and end of the reporting period. A common examination scenario involves a step‑acquisition where the acquirer previously held an equity interest; the previously held interest is remeasured to fair value at acquisition date, with the gain or loss recognised in profit or loss. Candidates must also distinguish between a business combination and an asset acquisition — the latter does not give rise to goodwill and applies the cost‑accumulation method under Ind AS 16/38.

Corporate Governance: Independent Directors, Board Committees and the SEBI LODR Framework

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 (SEBI LODR) prescribe a comprehensive corporate governance framework for listed entities. Regulation 17 mandates that the board of directors shall have an optimum combination of executive and non‑executive directors, with at least half of the board comprising independent directors where the chairperson is executive; if the chairperson is non‑executive, at least one‑third of the board must be independent. An independent director is defined under Regulation 16(1)(b) as a director who, apart from receiving director’s remuneration, has no material pecuniary relationship with the company, its promoters, or its associates, and who satisfies the criteria of independence specified in Schedule IV of the Companies Act, 2013.

Key board committees mandated by LODR include the Audit Committee (Regulation 18), Nomination and Remuneration Committee (Regulation 19), Stakeholders Relationship Committee (Regulation 20), and Risk Management Committee (Regulation 21 for top 1000 listed entities). The Audit Committee must comprise a minimum of three directors, with two‑thirds being independent, and must be chaired by an independent director. It oversees financial reporting, internal audit, vigil mechanism, and related‑party transactions. The Nomination and Remuneration Committee formulates criteria for director appointments, evaluates performance, and recommends remuneration policy. The Stakeholders Relationship Committee addresses grievances of shareholders, debenture holders, and other security holders.

Regulation 25 requires the board to conduct an annual performance evaluation of the board, its committees, and individual directors, including independent directors. The evaluation criteria must be disclosed in the annual report. Regulation 24 mandates that at least one independent director of the listed entity shall be a director on the board of an unlisted material subsidiary. The concept of “material subsidiary” is defined as a subsidiary whose income or net worth exceeds 10 % of the consolidated income or net worth of the listed entity.

Recent amendments (SEBI LODR (Amendment) Regulations, 2021) introduced a “board skill matrix” disclosure, requiring the board to identify the skills/expertise/competencies required in the context of the company’s business and to disclose the names of directors who possess such skills. The Companies Act, 2013 limits independent directors to two consecutive five‑year terms with a three‑year cooling‑off period.hree years is required before re‑appointment. Examiners frequently test the interplay between Companies Act provisions (Section 149, Schedule IV) and SEBI LODR, especially on the definition of “material pecuniary relationship” and the composition of the Audit Committee.

  • Confusing the control test under Ind AS 110 with the “majority voting rights” test — the three‑element model must be applied.
  • Treating a design deficiency in IFC as a material weakness without assessing operating effectiveness and materiality.
  • Assuming provisional ITC under Rule 36(4) is final credit; it is only a temporary allowance subject to matching.
  • Neglecting the measurement‑period adjustments in Ind AS 103 and recognising goodwill at acquisition date only.
  • Believing that an independent director can serve unlimited terms; the two‑term limit with cooling‑off period is mandatory under SEBI LODR.
  • Overlooking the requirement that the Audit Committee chairperson must be an independent director and that two‑thirds of its members must be independent.
  • Failing to differentiate between blocked credits under Section 17(5) and reversal of common credit under Rules 42/43.

These advanced topics collectively reinforce the depth required for high‑scoring answers in the optional paper.

Revenue Recognition under Ind AS 115: Five‑Step Model and Contract Modifications

Ind AS 115, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 April 2018, replaces Ind AS 18 and Ind AS 11. The standard applies to all contracts with customers except leases (Ind AS 116), insurance contracts (Ind AS 104) and financial instruments (Ind AS 109). The core principle is that an entity recognises revenue to depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers in an amount that reflects the consideration to which the entity expects to be entitled.

The Five‑Step Model

  1. Identify the contract(s) with a customer – a contract exists when it has commercial substance, parties have approved it, rights and payment terms are identifiable, and collectability is probable.
  2. Identify the performance obligations – distinct goods or services (or a series of distinct goods/services that are substantially the same) are separate performance obligations. A good or service is distinct if the customer can benefit from it on its own or together with readily available resources and the promise to transfer it is separately identifiable.
  3. Determine the transaction price – the amount of consideration expected, excluding amounts collected on behalf of third parties. Variable consideration (discounts, rebates, bonuses) is estimated using either the expected‑value method or the most‑likely‑amount method, constrained by the “highly probable” threshold. A significant financing component adjusts the promised consideration for the time value of money when the payment schedule provides a benefit to either party.
  4. Allocate the transaction price – allocate to each performance obligation on a relative stand‑alone selling price basis. If stand‑alone selling prices are not observable, use the adjusted market assessment approach, expected cost plus margin, or residual approach.
  5. Recognise revenue when (or as) the entity satisfies a performance obligation – revenue is recognised over time if one of three criteria is met (customer simultaneously receives and consumes benefits, entity’s performance creates or enhances an asset the customer controls, or performance does not create an asset with an alternative use and the entity has an enforceable right to payment for performance completed). Otherwise revenue is recognised at a point in time when control transfers.

Contract Modifications

A contract modification is a change in scope or price approved by the parties. Ind AS 115 requires the entity to account for a modification as either a separate contract (if the additional goods/services are distinct and the price reflects stand‑alone selling price) or as a termination of the old contract and creation of a new one (if the remaining goods/services are not distinct). If the modification is not accounted for as a separate contract, the entity updates the transaction price and measures progress towards complete satisfaction of the modified performance obligations.

Key Disclosures

Entities must disclose disaggregated revenue, contract balances (receivables, contract assets, contract liabilities), performance obligations, significant judgements (timing of satisfaction, variable consideration constraints), and assets recognised from costs to obtain or fulfil a contract.

Lease Accounting under Ind AS 116: Lessee and Lessor Accounting, Transition Approaches

Ind AS 116, applicable from 1 April 2019, replaces Ind AS 17. It introduces a single lessee accounting model requiring recognition of a right‑of‑use (RoU) asset and a lease liability for virtually all leases. The standard distinguishes between finance leases and operating leases for lessors, while lessees no longer classify leases.

Lessee Accounting

  • Initial measurement – Lease liability measured at the present value of lease payments not yet paid, discounted using the interest rate implicit in the lease or the lessee’s incremental borrowing rate. RoU asset measured at cost comprising the lease liability, initial direct costs, lease payments made before commencement, and dismantling/restoration obligations.
  • Subsequent measurement – Lease liability accretes interest (effective interest method) and is reduced by lease payments. RoU asset is depreciated over the shorter of lease term and useful life, unless ownership transfers or a purchase option is reasonably certain, in which case depreciation is over useful life. Re‑measurement occurs on lease modification, change in lease term, or change in variable lease payments linked to an index/rate.
  • Exemptions – Short‑term leases (≤12 months) and low‑value assets (≈₹3.5 lakh) may be expensed on a straight‑line basis.

Lessor Accounting

Lessors classify each lease as either a finance lease (substantially all risks/rewards transfer) or an operating lease. Finance leases result in a net investment in the lease (receivable) recognised at the present value of lease payments plus unguaranteed residual value. Operating leases continue to recognise the underlying asset and lease income on a straight‑line basis (or another systematic basis).

Transition Approaches

  1. Full retrospective – Apply Ind AS 116 to each prior reporting period presented, restating comparatives.
  2. Modified retrospective – Recognise the cumulative effect of initial application as an adjustment to the opening balance of retained earnings at the date of initial application (1 April 2019). The lessee measures the RoU asset at either its carrying amount as if Ind AS 116 had always been applied (using the incremental borrowing rate at the date of initial application) or at an amount equal to the lease liability adjusted for prepaid/accrued lease payments.

Disclosure Requirements

Lessee disclosures include a maturity analysis of lease liabilities, variable lease payments not included in the liability, RoU asset movements, and information on extension/termination options. Lessors disclose a maturity analysis of lease receivables (finance leases) and operating lease income.

Worked Example: Lease Classification and Measurement
Entity A enters a 5‑year lease for a machine on 1 April 2022. Annual lease payments are ₹10,00,000 payable at year‑end. The machine’s fair value is ₹45,00,000. The interest rate implicit in the lease is 8 %. The lease does not transfer ownership, nor is there a purchase option. Determine the lease classification for the lessor and compute the initial lease liability and RoU asset for the lessee.
Lessor classification: Since the lease term (5 years) is not the major part of the asset’s economic life (assume 10 years) and the present value of lease payments (₹10,00,000 × PV annuity factor 5 yr @8 % = ₹39,93,000) is less than substantially all of the fair value (₹45,00,000), the lease is an operating lease for the lessor.
Lessee initial measurement: Lease liability = ₹39,93,000 (present value of five payments). RoU asset = lease liability + initial direct costs (assume nil) + prepaid payments (none) = ₹39,93,000. The lessee will depreciate the RoU asset over 5 years (straight‑line ₹7,98,600 per year) and accrete interest on the liability (Year 1 interest = ₹39,93,000 × 8 % = ₹3,19,440).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a lease question appears, first identify whether you are asked from the lessee or lessor perspective. For lessee, write the two‑line journal entries for initial recognition (Dr RoU Asset, Cr Lease Liability) and subsequent measurement (Dr Interest Expense, Dr Lease Liability, Cr Cash; Dr Depreciation, Cr Accumulated Depreciation). For lessor, state the classification test (finance vs operating) and the resulting balance‑sheet treatment. Memorise the PV annuity factor for 5 years at 8 % (3.9927) to speed up calculations.

Financial Instruments under Ind AS 109: Classification, Measurement, Impairment (ECL) and Hedge Accounting

Ind AS 109, effective 1 April 2018, replaces Ind AS 39. It governs recognition, classification, measurement, derecognition, impairment and hedge accounting for financial assets and liabilities.

Classification and Measurement of Financial Assets

Financial assets are classified into three categories based on the entity’s business model for managing the assets and the contractual cash‑flow characteristics (solely payments of principal and interest – SPPI test):

  • Amortised Cost – Business model: hold to collect contractual cash flows; SPPI test passed. Measured at amortised cost using effective interest method.
  • Fair Value Through Other Comprehensive Income (FVTOCI) – Business model: hold to collect and sell; SPPI test passed. Measured at fair value; interest income, impairment gains/losses and foreign exchange gains/losses recognised in profit or loss; other fair‑value changes in OCI. On derecognition, cumulative OCI gain/loss reclassified to profit or loss.
  • Fair Value Through Profit or Loss (FVTPL) – All other assets (including equity instruments not designated at FVTOCI, derivatives, assets failing SPPI). Measured at fair value with all changes in profit or loss.

Equity instruments may be irrevocably designated at FVTOCI at initial recognition (no recycling).

Classification and Measurement of Financial Liabilities

Financial liabilities are generally measured at amortised cost. Exceptions: derivatives, liabilities held for trading, and those designated at FVTPL (fair value option) where designation eliminates or reduces an accounting mismatch.

Impairment – Expected Credit Loss (ECL) Model

Ind AS 109 replaces the incurred‑loss model with a forward‑looking ECL model applied to financial assets at amortised cost, FVTOCI debt instruments, lease receivables, contract assets and loan commitments. The model uses a three‑stage approach:

  1. Stage 1 (12‑month ECL) – No significant increase in credit risk (SICR) since initial recognition. Recognise 12‑month ECL.
  2. Stage 2 (Lifetime ECL – not credit‑impaired) – SICR but not credit‑impaired. Recognise lifetime ECL.
  3. Stage 3 (Lifetime ECL – credit‑impaired) – Objective evidence of impairment. Recognise lifetime ECL; interest revenue calculated on net carrying amount.

Significant increase in credit risk is assessed using reasonable and supportable forward‑looking information. A rebuttable presumption exists that credit risk has increased significantly when contractual payments are more than 30 days past due.

Hedge Accounting

Ind AS 109 aligns hedge accounting with risk management. Three hedge types are permitted:

  • Fair Value Hedge – Hedge of exposure to changes in fair value of a recognised asset/liability or firm commitment. Gain/loss on hedging instrument and hedged item recognised in profit or loss.
  • Cash Flow Hedge – Hedge of exposure to variability in cash flows attributable to a particular risk. Effective portion of gain/loss on hedging instrument recognised in OCI (cash flow hedge reserve); ineffective portion in profit or loss. Amounts in OCI reclassified to profit or loss when hedged cash flows affect profit or loss.
  • Net Investment Hedge – Hedge of foreign currency exposure to a net investment in a foreign operation. Accounted similarly to cash flow hedge.

Qualifying criteria: formal designation, documentation of risk management objective, hedge effectiveness (prospective and retrospective), and an economic relationship between hedged item and hedging instrument.

Corporate Restructuring: Schemes of Arrangement, Merger, Demerger and NCLT Process under Companies Act 2013

Sections 230‑232 of the Companies Act 2013 provide the statutory framework for compromises, arrangements, mergers and amalgamations. The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) is the adjudicating authority; appeals lie to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) and thereafter to the Supreme Court.

Scheme of Arrangement (Section 230)

A scheme may involve compromise with creditors or members, reconstruction, or amalgamation. The process: (1) Board approval; (2) Application to NCLT for convening meetings of creditors/members; (3) Notice with explanatory statement, valuation report, and auditor’s certificate; (4) Meetings – approval by majority in number representing three‑fourths in value of each class; (5) NCLT sanction after hearing objections; (6) Filing of certified copy with Registrar of Companies (RoC) within 30 days. The scheme becomes effective on the appointed date specified therein.

Merger by Absorption (Section 232)

Transferor company merges into transferee company; transferor dissolves without winding up. The scheme must provide for transfer of assets, liabilities, and employees. Shareholders of transferor receive shares in transferee based on a share‑exchange ratio certified by a registered valuer. Tax neutrality under Section 47(vi) of the Income‑Tax Act applies if conditions (continuity of business, shareholding ≥ 90 % etc.) are satisfied.

Demerger (Section 232 read with Section 2(19AA) of Income‑Tax Act)

An undertaking is transferred to a resulting company on a going‑concern basis. Shareholders of demerged company receive shares in resulting company proportionate to their holding. Conditions for tax neutrality: (i) transfer of undertaking as a going concern; (ii) shareholders of demerged company become shareholders of resulting company in same proportion; (iii) consideration discharged only by issue of shares.

Slump Sale (Section 2(42C) of Income‑Tax Act)

Transfer of one or more undertakings for a lump‑sum consideration without values assigned to individual assets/liabilities. Capital gains computed on the difference between lump‑sum consideration and net worth of the undertaking. No tax neutrality; however, Section 50B provides for computation of capital gains.

Fast‑Track Merger (Section 233)

Applicable for mergers between a holding company and its wholly‑owned subsidiary, or between two small companies (paid‑up capital ≤ ₹25 crore, turnover ≤ ₹100 crore). Approval by Regional Director (RD) instead of NCLT, provided no objection from creditors/members and ROC/RD consent.

Key Judicial Precedents

  • Miheer H. Mafatlal v. Mafatlal Industries Ltd (1997) – Supreme Court held that NCLT (then High Court) must ensure fairness to minority shareholders; valuation must be independent.
  • Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India (2012) – Clarified that indirect transfer of shares of an Indian company does not attract capital gains in India unless the transaction is a “transfer” under Section 2(47) read with Section 9(1)(i).
  • Tata Steel Ltd v. Cyrus Mistry (2021) – NCLAT/NCLT jurisdiction over oppression and mismanagement petitions in the context of scheme approvals.

International Taxation: DTAA Interpretation, BEPS Action Plans, GAAR and Equalisation Levy

India’s network of Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) follows the OECD Model Tax Convention. Interpretation principles are guided by the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) and domestic judicial pronouncements.

DTAA Tie‑Breaker Rules (Article 4)

For dual‑resident individuals, the tie‑breaker sequence: (i) permanent home; (ii) centre of vital interests; (iii) habitual abode; (iv) nationality; (v) mutual agreement procedure (MAP). For dual‑resident companies, place of effective management (POEM) is the decisive test. India’s POEM rules (Notification No. 9/2017) deem a company resident in India if its key management and commercial decisions are made in India.

BEPS Action Plans – India’s Implementation

  • Action 1 (Digital Economy) – India introduced Equalisation Levy (EL) in 2016 (6 % on online advertising) and expanded in 2020 (2 % on e‑commerce supply/services by non‑residents with turnover > ₹2 crore).
  • Action 6 (Treaty Abuse) – Principal Purpose Test (PPT) inserted in India’s DTAAs via MLI (Multilateral Instrument) ratified 2019; also domestic GAAR (Sections 95‑102, effective 1 April 2017).
  • Action 13 (Country‑by‑Country Reporting) – Master file, local file and CbCR filing for MNE groups with consolidated revenue ≥ ₹6,400 crore (≈€750 million).
  • Action 14 (Dispute Resolution) – MAP timelines and mandatory binding arbitration in updated treaties.

General Anti‑Avoidance Rule (GAAR)

Sections 95‑102 empower tax authorities to declare an “impermissible avoidance arrangement” (IAA) if the main purpose is to obtain a tax benefit and the arrangement lacks commercial substance. The onus shifts to the taxpayer to prove commercial substance. The Approving Panel (Chairperson: Principal Chief Commissioner) must approve GAAR invocation. Safe harbours exist for certain transactions (e.g., FII investments, specified restructuring).

Equalisation Levy (EL)

  • EL‑1 (2016) – 6 % on consideration for online advertisement or digital space provided by non‑resident to resident or Indian PE. Threshold: ₹1 lakh per year per service provider.
  • EL‑2 (2020) – 2 % on e‑commerce supply/services by non‑resident e‑commerce operator (turnover > ₹2 crore). Excludes transactions subject to EL‑1 or where the operator has a PE in India.

EL is not covered by DTAA; it is a separate levy under the Finance Act. Non‑deduction attracts interest (1 % per month) and penalty (equal to levy amount).

Key Cases

  • Azadi Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2003) – Supreme Court upheld the India‑Mauritius DTAA, ruling that treaty benefits cannot be denied merely because of treaty shopping; later addressed by PPT/MLI.
  • Vodafone (2012) – Clarified that share transfer of a foreign company holding Indian assets is not taxable in India absent specific anti‑avoidance provision; led to retrospective amendment (Section 9(1)(i) Explanation 5) and later GAAR.
  • Google India Pvt Ltd v. DCIT (2019) – Held that EL‑1 applies on gross consideration without deduction of TDS; confirmed constitutional validity.
  • Confusing the SPPI test with the business‑model test – both must be satisfied for amortised‑cost or FVTOCI classification.
  • Assuming all lease modifications are accounted for as separate contracts – only if the additional goods/services are distinct and priced at stand‑alone selling price.
  • Treating 12‑month ECL as the default for all financial assets – Stage 2 applies once SICR is identified, even if payments are not past due.
  • Overlooking the requirement that a demerger must transfer an “undertaking” on a going‑concern basis for tax neutrality under Section 2(19AA).
  • Believing GAAR can be invoked by any assessing officer – only the Approving Panel can sanction GAAR invocation.
  • Ignoring the POEM test for corporate residence in DTAA tie‑breaker – place of effective management overrides place of incorporation.
  • Failing to distinguish EL‑1 (advertising) from EL‑2 (e‑commerce) thresholds and rates – they are separate levies with different compliance obligations.

Impairment of Assets under Ind AS 36

Ind AS 36 governs the impairment of non‑financial assets, including property, plant and equipment, intangible assets, goodwill, and right‑of‑use assets. The standard requires an entity to assess at each reporting date whether there is any indication that an asset may be impaired. If such indication exists, or for goodwill and intangible assets with indefinite useful lives, the entity must estimate the recoverable amount of the asset or the cash‑generating unit (CGU) to which it belongs. The recoverable amount is the higher of fair value less costs of disposal (FVLCD) and value in use (VIU). VIU is the present value of the future cash flows expected to be derived from the asset or CGU, discounted using a pre‑tax discount rate that reflects current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks specific to the asset.

Goodwill arising from a business combination must be allocated to each CGU or group of CGUs that is expected to benefit from the synergies of the combination. The allocation is performed at the acquisition date and is not revised. When testing goodwill for impairment, the carrying amount of the CGU (including allocated goodwill) is compared with its recoverable amount. Any impairment loss is first allocated to goodwill and then to the other assets of the CGU on a pro‑rata basis. Unlike other assets, an impairment loss recognised for goodwill cannot be reversed in subsequent periods.

For assets other than goodwill, a previously recognised impairment loss may be reversed if there has been a change in the estimates used to determine the recoverable amount since the last impairment loss was recognised. The reversal is limited to the carrying amount that would have been determined (net of depreciation or amortisation) had no impairment loss been recognised. The reversal is recognised in profit or loss immediately.

Disclosure requirements are extensive. Entities must disclose the events and circumstances that led to the recognition or reversal of impairment losses, the amount of impairment loss recognised or reversed, the nature and carrying amount of the asset or CGU, the recoverable amount and the basis for its determination (FVLCD or VIU), the discount rate used, and a sensitivity analysis showing how changes in key assumptions would affect the recoverable amount. For goodwill, the allocation across CGUs and the key assumptions used in the impairment test must be disclosed.

Practical application often involves determining the appropriate CGU. The CGU is the smallest identifiable group of assets that generates cash inflows largely independent of other assets. Judgement is required when assets are interdependent. The standard also addresses corporate assets (e.g., head office building) that do not generate independent cash flows; these are allocated to CGUs on a reasonable and consistent basis. In the Indian context, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) has considered impairment of goodwill in schemes of arrangement, emphasising the need for robust valuation reports and independent expert opinions.

Employee Benefits under Ind AS 19

Ind AS 19 prescribes the accounting for all employee benefits, classified into short‑term benefits, post‑employment benefits (defined contribution and defined benefit plans), other long‑term benefits, and termination benefits. Short‑term benefits such as wages, salaries, and paid annual leave are recognised as an expense when the employee renders the related service. Defined contribution plans (e.g., provident fund where the employer’s obligation is limited to contributions) are accounted for by recognising the contribution payable as an expense.

Defined benefit plans require actuarial valuation using the Projected Unit Credit Method (PUCM). Under PUCM, each period of service gives rise to an additional unit of benefit entitlement, and the obligation is measured as the present value of the defined benefit obligation (DBO) at the reporting date. Actuarial assumptions include the discount rate (based on market yields on high‑quality corporate bonds or government bonds of appropriate maturity), salary escalation rate, mortality rates (typically Indian Assured Lives Mortality tables), attrition rates, and expected return on plan assets. The net defined benefit liability (or asset) is the DBO minus the fair value of plan assets.

Net interest on the net defined benefit liability/asset is calculated by applying the discount rate to the net liability/asset at the start of the period, adjusted for contributions and benefit payments. Remeasurements — comprising actuarial gains and losses, the return on plan assets (excluding amounts included in net interest), and any change in the effect of the asset ceiling — are recognised immediately in other comprehensive income (OCI) and are not reclassified to profit or loss in subsequent periods. Past service cost, arising from plan amendments or curtailments, is recognised in profit or loss at the earlier of the date of the amendment or the date the related restructuring costs are recognised.

Curtailment occurs when an entity significantly reduces the number of employees covered by a plan; a settlement occurs when an entity enters into a transaction that eliminates all further obligations for part or all of the benefits. Both events require immediate recognition of the resulting gain or loss in profit or loss. Disclosure requirements include a reconciliation of the opening and closing balances of the DBO and plan assets, a sensitivity analysis showing the effect of changes in key actuarial assumptions, the maturity profile of the DBO, and a description of the plan’s funding policy and risk exposure.

In practice, Indian companies often maintain gratuity plans governed by the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, and leave encashment schemes. The actuarial valuation for these plans must comply with Ind AS 19 and the guidance issued by the Institute of Actuaries of India. The standard also interacts with Ind AS 12 (Income Taxes) for the tax treatment of plan assets and obligations, and with Ind AS 24 (Related Party Disclosures) when the plan is administered by a related entity.

Technique: Projected Unit Credit Method Calculation
A company operates a defined benefit gratuity plan. At 31 March 2024, the following data are available: opening DBO ₹12,00,000; current service cost ₹1,50,000; interest cost (discount rate 7%) ₹84,000; benefits paid ₹2,00,000; actuarial loss due to change in salary escalation assumption ₹60,000; fair value of plan assets at start ₹10,00,000; actual return on plan assets ₹70,000; contributions paid ₹1,20,000. Compute the closing DBO, closing plan assets, net defined benefit liability, and the amounts recognised in profit or loss and OCI for the year.
Step 1 – Closing DBO: Opening DBO ₹12,00,000 + Current service cost ₹1,50,000 + Interest cost ₹84,000 – Benefits paid ₹2,00,000 + Actuarial loss ₹60,000 = ₹12,94,000.
Step 2 – Closing Plan Assets: Opening assets ₹10,00,000 + Actual return ₹70,000 + Contributions ₹1,20,000 – Benefits paid ₹2,00,000 = ₹9,90,000.
Step 3 – Net Defined Benefit Liability: Closing DBO ₹12,94,000 – Closing assets ₹9,90,000 = ₹3,04,000 (liability).
Step 4 – Profit or Loss: Current service cost ₹1,50,000 + Net interest (Interest cost ₹84,000 – Interest income on assets ₹70,000 = ₹14,000) = ₹1,64,000 recognised in profit or loss.
Step 5 – OCI: Actuarial loss on DBO ₹60,000 – Return on plan assets (excluding interest income) ₹0 = ₹60,000 recognised in OCI (since actual return equals interest income, no excess return).
Result: Closing DBO ₹12,94,000; Closing plan assets ₹9,90,000; Net liability ₹3,04,000; P&L charge ₹1,64,000; OCI charge ₹60,000.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Allocate 12‑15 minutes for Ind AS 19 numericals. Memorise the PUCM steps: opening DBO → service cost → interest cost → benefits paid → actuarial gains/losses = closing DBO. For plan assets: opening assets → actual return → contributions → benefits paid = closing assets. Net interest = discount rate × (opening net liability). Remeasurements always go to OCI. Practice at least three past‑paper problems to internalise the flow.

GST Place of Supply Rules for Goods and Services

The place of supply determines whether a transaction is intra‑State (subject to CGST + SGST) or inter‑State (subject to IGST). For goods, Sections 10 and 11 of the IGST Act provide the framework. Section 10(1)(a) states that where goods are moved, the place of supply is the location where the movement terminates for delivery to the recipient. If goods are delivered to a person on the direction of a third person, the place of supply is the principal place of business of that person (Section 10(1)(b)). Where goods are assembled or installed at site, the place of supply is the place of installation (Section 10(1)(c)). Goods supplied on board a conveyance (e.g., aircraft, vessel) have the place of supply as the location where they are taken on board (Section 10(1)(d)). For imports, the place of supply is the location of the importer (Section 11(1)); for exports, it is the location outside India (Section 11(2)).

For services, the general rule under Section 12(2) is the location of the service recipient. However, specific categories override this rule. Services directly related to immovable property (e.g., architecture, construction) are supplied where the property is located (Section 12(3)). Restaurant and catering services are supplied where the service is actually performed (Section 12(4)). Training and performance appraisal services are supplied where the event is held (Section 12(5)). Admission to cultural, artistic, sporting, or educational events is supplied where the event is held (Section 12(6)). Transportation of goods (including mail) is supplied at the destination of the goods (Section 12(7)). Passenger transportation is supplied at the place of embarkation (Section 12(8)). Banking, financial, and insurance services are supplied at the location of the supplier (Section 12(9)). Telecommunication services are supplied at the place where the fixed line or billing address is located (Section 12(10)).

Cross‑border services are governed by Section 13. Export of services requires: (i) supplier located in India, (ii) recipient located outside India, (iii) place of supply outside India, (iv) payment received in convertible foreign exchange, and (v) supplier and recipient not merely establishments of the same person (Section 13(1)(a)). Import of services requires the reverse conditions (Section 13(1)(b)). For Online Information and Database Access or Retrieval (OIDAR) services supplied by a non‑resident to a non‑taxable person in India, the place of supply is India (Section 13(12)), making the non‑resident liable for GST registration and payment.

Illustration: A Mumbai‑based architect designs a building in Delhi. The place of supply is Delhi (immovable property), so IGST applies. A Chennai‑based software company provides cloud hosting to a client in Singapore. The place of supply is Singapore (recipient location), so it is an export of service if all conditions of Section 13(1)(a) are met; otherwise, it may be treated as an inter‑State supply subject to IGST. A non‑resident streaming platform supplies OIDAR services to Indian consumers; the place of supply is India, and the platform must register under GST and pay 18% IGST.

Recent amendments (Notification No. 10/2023‑Central Tax) clarified the place of supply for services supplied through electronic marketplaces, deeming the marketplace as the supplier for specified services. Understanding these rules is critical for correct tax invoicing, e‑way bill generation, and avoidance of interest and penalty for misclassification.

Corporate Social Responsibility under Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013

Section 135 mandates CSR for companies meeting any of the following thresholds in the immediately preceding financial year: net worth of ₹500 crore or more, turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more, or net profit of ₹5 crore or more. Such companies must constitute a CSR Committee of the Board with at least three directors, including at least one independent director. The Committee formulates a CSR Policy indicating activities from Schedule VII (e.g., eradicating hunger, promoting education, gender equality, environmental sustainability, protection of national heritage, rural development).

The Board must ensure that the company spends at least 2% of the average net profits of the three immediately preceding financial years on CSR activities. Net profit for this purpose is calculated under Section 198 (profit before tax, excluding certain items). If the company fails to spend the required amount, the unspent amount must be transferred within six months of the end of the financial year to a fund specified in Schedule VII (e.g., Prime Minister’s National Relief Fund) or to an unspent CSR account opened in a scheduled bank. For ongoing projects, the unspent amount may be carried forward for up to three financial years, provided the project is approved by the Board and the CSR Committee.

Impact assessment is mandatory for projects with an outlay of ₹1 crore or more, or where the company’s average CSR obligation exceeds ₹10 crore. The assessment must be undertaken by an independent agency and the report placed before the Board. Reporting requirements include a CSR Annual Report in the Board’s Report, detailing the CSR Policy, composition of the Committee, projects undertaken, amount spent, and reasons for any shortfall. Companies must also file Form CSR‑2 with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) annually.

Penalties for non‑compliance: the company is liable to a fine of not less than ₹50,000 and up to ₹25 lakh; every officer in default is liable to a fine of not less than ₹50,000 and up to ₹5 lakh, or imprisonment up to three years, or both. The 2021 amendment (Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2021) introduced the concept of “impact assessment” and allowed set‑off of excess CSR spending against future obligations for up to three years. The 2022 amendment (Companies (CSR Policy) Amendment Rules, 2022) clarified that administrative overheads cannot exceed 5% of total CSR expenditure.

Judicial precedent: In Technicolor India Pvt Ltd v. Registrar of Companies (NCLT, 2022), the Tribunal upheld the penalty imposed for failure to transfer unspent CSR amount to the specified fund within the prescribed timeline, emphasising that the statutory obligation is mandatory and not directory. Companies must therefore integrate CSR planning into their annual budgeting cycle and monitor project execution quarterly to avoid last‑minute shortfalls.

Key Auditing Standards and CARO 2020 Reporting Requirements

The Standards on Auditing (SAs) issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) form the backbone of audit quality. SA 200 establishes the overall objectives of the independent auditor and the requirement to conduct the audit in accordance with SAs. SA 300 deals with planning the audit of financial statements, requiring the auditor to establish an overall audit strategy and a detailed audit plan. SA 315 (Revised) focuses on identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement through understanding the entity and its environment, including internal control. SA 330 requires the auditor to design and implement responses to assessed risks, including tests of controls and substantive procedures.

SA 500 governs audit evidence, prescribing the concepts of sufficiency and appropriateness. SA 700 (Revised) addresses forming an opinion and reporting on financial statements, including the structure of the auditor’s report. SA 701 introduces Key Audit Matters (KAMs) for listed entities, requiring the auditor to communicate matters of most significance in the audit. SA 705 deals with modifications to the opinion (qualified, adverse, disclaimer). SA 706 covers Emphasis of Matter and Other Matter paragraphs. Other critical SAs include SA 230 (Audit Documentation), SA 240 (The Auditor’s Responsibilities Relating to Fraud), SA 250 (Consideration of Laws and Regulations), SA 260 (Communication with Those Charged with Governance), and SA 265 (Communicating Deficiencies in Internal Control).

The Companies (Auditor’s Report) Order, 2020 (CARO 2020) applies to every company except certain exempted classes (e.g., banking companies, insurance companies, one‑person companies, small companies). The auditor must report on 21 specific matters. Key clauses include: (i) fixed assets – whether the company has maintained proper records, physical verification, and title deeds; (ii) inventory – physical verification and discrepancies; (iii) loans granted – terms, interest, and repayment; (iv) deposits accepted – compliance with Sections 73‑76; (v) statutory dues – regular deposit and undisputed amounts; (vi) transactions with related parties – compliance with Sections 177 and 188; (vii) preferential allotment or private placement – compliance with Section 42; (viii) fraud – any fraud noticed or reported; (ix) internal audit – adequacy and scope; (x) cost records – maintenance under Section 148; (xi) Nidhi company – net owned funds; (xii) chit fund – compliance with Chit Funds Act; (xiii) NCLT orders – compliance; (xiv) CSR – amount spent and unspent; (xv) other matters as prescribed.

In practice, auditors must map each CARO clause to audit procedures performed. For instance, for clause (i), the auditor verifies the fixed asset register, observes physical verification, and examines title deeds for immovable property. For clause (viii), the auditor inquires of management, reviews whistle‑blower complaints, and evaluates the results of fraud risk assessment under SA 240. The auditor’s report under CARO 2020 is annexed to the main audit report and must be signed by the auditor. Non‑compliance with CARO reporting can attract disciplinary action under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

  • Confusing the recoverable amount hierarchy under Ind AS 36 – FVLCD and VIU are not interchangeable; the higher of the two is the recoverable amount.
  • Assuming actuarial gains/losses on defined benefit plans are recycled to profit or loss – they remain in OCI permanently.
  • Applying the general place‑of‑supply rule for services (recipient location) to immovable‑property‑related services – the specific rule (property location) overrides.
  • Treating CSR spending as a voluntary donation – the 2% obligation is statutory and non‑compliance attracts penal provisions.
  • Omitting Key Audit Matters in the auditor’s report for listed entities – SA 701 mandates KAM disclosure; failure is a reporting deficiency.
  • Believing CARO 2020 applies to all companies – small companies, one‑person companies, and certain financial sector entities are exempt.
  • Thorough revision of these advanced topics ensures confidence in tackling application‑based questions.

Accounting for Government Grants under Ind AS 20

Ind AS 20 prescribes the accounting treatment and disclosure requirements for government grants and other forms of government assistance. A government grant is defined as a transfer of resources to an entity in return for past or future compliance with certain conditions relating to the operating activities of the entity. The standard distinguishes between grants related to assets and grants related to income. Grants related to assets are presented in the balance sheet either as deferred income or by deducting the grant from the carrying amount of the asset. Grants related to income are recognised in profit or loss on a systematic basis over the periods in which the entity recognises as expenses the related costs for which the grant is intended to compensate.

Recognition criteria require that the entity will comply with the conditions attaching to the grant and that the grant will be received. The grant is recognised only when there is reasonable assurance that both conditions are met. For grants related to assets, the deferred income approach requires the grant to be recognised in profit or loss over the useful life of the asset, typically on a systematic basis consistent with the depreciation policy. The alternative presentation, deducting the grant from the asset’s carrying amount, results in lower depreciation charges. Entities must apply the chosen method consistently for each class of asset.

Disclosure requirements are extensive. Entities must disclose the accounting policy adopted for government grants, the nature and extent of grants recognised in the financial statements, unfulfilled conditions and other contingencies attaching to recognised grants, and an indication of other forms of government assistance from which the entity has directly benefited. If a grant becomes repayable, it is treated as a change in estimate and accounted for prospectively. The standard also addresses grants received in the form of non‑monetary assets, which are measured at fair value.

Practical issues often arise in determining whether a grant is related to an asset or to income. For example, a grant for the construction of a factory building is clearly asset‑related, whereas a grant for research and development expenditure may be income‑related if the expenditure is expensed as incurred. The entity must assess the primary condition of the grant. Another common area of judgement is the systematic basis for income recognition; the standard permits methods such as the proportion of costs incurred to total expected costs, or a straight‑line basis over the period of the grant.

From an examination perspective, candidates should be able to illustrate the journal entries for both presentation methods, compute the amount of grant recognised in profit or loss for a given period, and explain the impact on deferred tax. The interplay with Ind AS 12 (Income Taxes) is significant because the tax base of the asset differs under the two presentation methods, leading to different deferred tax consequences.

Segment Reporting under Ind AS 108

Ind AS 108 requires entities whose debt or equity instruments are traded in a public market, or that file financial statements with a securities regulator, to disclose information about their operating segments. An operating segment is a component of an entity that engages in business activities from which it may earn revenues and incur expenses, whose operating results are regularly reviewed by the chief operating decision maker (CODM) to make decisions about resources to be allocated to the segment and assess its performance, and for which discrete financial information is available.

The standard adopts a management approach: segment identification follows the internal reporting structure used by the CODM. Reportable segments are those that meet any of the quantitative thresholds — revenue, profit or loss, or assets — of 10 % or more of the combined total of all operating segments. If the total external revenue of reportable segments is less than 75 % of the entity’s total revenue, additional segments must be reported until the 75 % threshold is met. Entities may also voluntarily report segments that do not meet the thresholds if they believe the information is useful.

For each reportable segment, the entity must disclose segment revenue (external and inter‑segment), segment profit or loss, segment assets, segment liabilities, and the basis of measurement. Reconciliations of segment totals to the consolidated financial statements are required for revenue, profit or loss, assets, and liabilities. The reconciliation explains the differences arising from items not allocated to segments, such as corporate overheads, tax expense, and inter‑segment eliminations.

Disclosures also include information about products and services, geographical areas, and major customers. If a single external customer accounts for 10 % or more of the entity’s revenue, the entity must disclose that fact and the segment(s) reporting the revenue. The standard requires that segment information be prepared using the same accounting policies as the consolidated financial statements, except for specific measurement exemptions such as the use of a different depreciation method for segment reporting if it is used internally.

Common examination questions test the ability to identify reportable segments from a given set of internal reports, apply the 10 % and 75 % thresholds, prepare the reconciliation tables, and discuss the impact of changes in the CODM’s reporting structure. Candidates should also be familiar with the transition provisions for entities adopting Ind AS 108 for the first time, including the requirement to restate prior‑period segment information unless impracticable.

Technique: Threshold Application and Reconciliation
An entity has three operating segments A, B and C. External revenues are ₹120 cr, ₹80 cr and ₹30 cr respectively. Inter‑segment revenues are ₹20 cr (A to B), ₹10 cr (B to C) and ₹5 cr (C to A). Total consolidated revenue is ₹250 cr. Segment profit before tax: A ₹25 cr, B ₹15 cr, C ₹5 cr. Corporate overheads ₹10 cr. Identify reportable segments and prepare the revenue reconciliation.
Step 1: Compute total external revenue = ₹120 cr + ₹80 cr + ₹30 cr = ₹230 cr. Total revenue including inter‑segment = ₹250 cr (given). Step 2: Apply 10 % threshold on total revenue (₹250 cr) → ₹25 cr. All three segments exceed ₹25 cr in external revenue, so all are reportable. Step 3: Check 75 % rule: combined external revenue of reportable segments = ₹230 cr which is 92 % of ₹250 cr, so threshold satisfied. Step 4: Revenue reconciliation: Segment external revenue total ₹230 cr + inter‑segment revenue ₹35 cr = ₹265 cr. Less inter‑segment eliminations ₹35 cr = consolidated revenue ₹250 cr. The reconciliation table shows segment external revenue, inter‑segment revenue, eliminations, and consolidated revenue.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a segment‑reporting question appears, first list the quantitative thresholds (10 % of revenue, profit, assets) and the 75 % revenue rule. Compute totals once and reuse them for each segment. Prepare a skeleton reconciliation table with columns for each segment, eliminations, and consolidated totals; fill in numbers systematically to avoid arithmetic errors. Remember that inter‑segment revenue is eliminated in full, not proportionately.

Audit of Banks and Financial Institutions

The audit of banks and financial institutions is governed by a layered regulatory framework comprising the Companies Act 2013, the Banking Regulation Act 1949, Reserve Bank of India (RBI) directions, and the Standards on Auditing (SAs) issued by ICAI. SA 800 deals with special considerations in the audit of financial statements of banks, while SA 805 addresses the audit of financial statements of non‑banking financial companies (NBFCs). The auditor must obtain an understanding of the entity’s regulatory environment, including prudential norms on income recognition, asset classification, provisioning (IRACP), capital adequacy (Basel III), and exposure limits.

Key audit areas include verification of loan assets and advances, classification of assets into standard, sub‑standard, doubtful and loss categories, and the adequacy of provisions as per RBI guidelines. The auditor tests the effectiveness of the bank’s internal controls over credit appraisal, sanction, disbursement, monitoring, and recovery. Substantive procedures involve confirmation of balances with borrowers, examination of security documentation, valuation of collateral, and recomputation of provisioning requirements. The auditor also evaluates the bank’s investment portfolio classification (held‑to‑maturity, available‑for‑sale, held‑for‑trading) and the marking‑to‑market process.

Revenue recognition in banks follows the accrual basis subject to IRACP norms; interest on non‑performing assets (NPAs) is recognised only on realisation. The auditor must verify that income recognition policies are consistently applied and that any deviation is disclosed. Capital adequacy ratio (CAR) computation is another critical area; the auditor checks the composition of Tier I and Tier II capital, risk‑weighted assets, and compliance with the minimum CAR prescribed by RBI.

CARO 2020 applies to banking companies with specific exemptions; however, the auditor must still report on matters such as the maintenance of proper records of fixed assets, physical verification of cash and securities, and compliance with statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) and cash reserve ratio (CRR) requirements. The auditor’s report under Section 143(3)(i) on internal financial controls over financial reporting (ICFR) is mandatory for banks, and the audit of ICFR is integrated with the financial statement audit.

Emerging focus areas include the audit of digital lending platforms, cyber‑security controls, and the implementation of the Expected Credit Loss (ECL) model under Ind AS 109 for banks that have adopted Ind AS. The auditor must assess the reasonableness of forward‑looking macroeconomic assumptions used in the ECL model and the governance around model validation.

Advance Pricing Agreements and Mutual Agreement Procedure

Advance Pricing Agreements (APAs) provide a mechanism for taxpayers and tax authorities to agree in advance on the arm’s length price for international transactions or specified domestic transactions. India’s APA programme, introduced under Section 92CC of the Income‑Tax Act 1961, allows for unilateral, bilateral, and multilateral APAs. A unilateral APA involves the taxpayer and the Indian tax authority; a bilateral APA involves the Indian authority and the competent authority of a treaty partner; a multilateral APA extends to more than two jurisdictions.

The APA process begins with a pre‑filing consultation, followed by a formal application accompanied by a detailed functional, asset, and risk (FAR) analysis, comparability study, and proposed transfer pricing methodology. The taxpayer must also submit a roll‑back request if they wish to apply the agreed methodology to prior years (up to four preceding years). The tax authority evaluates the application, may conduct an independent analysis, and negotiates the terms. Once concluded, the APA is binding on both parties for the agreed period, typically five years, and provides certainty against transfer pricing adjustments.

The Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) under Article 25 of the OECD Model Tax Convention (incorporated in India’s DTAAs) resolves disputes arising from transfer pricing adjustments that result in double taxation. The taxpayer initiates MAP by submitting a request to the Indian competent authority within the time limit prescribed in the relevant DTAA (usually three years from the first notification of the adjustment). The competent authorities of the two contracting states endeavour to reach an agreement. If resolved, the adjustment is relieved; if not, the taxpayer may resort to domestic remedies.

Key practical considerations include the interaction between APA and MAP — an APA can prevent future disputes, while MAP addresses past adjustments. The taxpayer must maintain contemporaneous documentation as per Rule 10D and the master file/local file requirements under Country‑by‑Country Reporting (CbCR). The Indian tax authority publishes annual APA statistics; as of the latest data, the majority of APAs are unilateral, with a growing number of bilateral APAs with jurisdictions such as the United States, United Kingdom, Japan, and Singapore.

Examination questions often test the procedural steps, the distinction between unilateral and bilateral APAs, the roll‑back provisions, the time limits for MAP, and the interplay with the General Anti‑Avoidance Rule (GAAR). Candidates should be able to draft a high‑level APA application outline and explain the consequences of non‑compliance with APA terms.

Corporate Governance: Board Evaluation, Whistleblower Mechanism and ESG Reporting

The Companies Act 2013 and SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations 2015 (LODR) together establish a comprehensive corporate governance framework for listed entities. Section 178 mandates the constitution of a Nomination and Remuneration Committee (NRC) which is responsible for formulating criteria for evaluation of the performance of the board, its committees, and individual directors. The evaluation must be carried out at least once a year, and the results are disclosed in the annual report. The board’s evaluation covers aspects such as composition, diversity, effectiveness of processes, strategic direction, risk oversight, and stakeholder engagement.

Section 177 requires every listed company to establish a vigil mechanism (whistleblower mechanism) for directors and employees to report genuine concerns about unethical behaviour, fraud, or violation of the company’s code of conduct. The mechanism must provide for direct access to the chairperson of the Audit Committee in appropriate cases, and the identity of the whistleblower must be kept confidential. The Audit Committee oversees the functioning of the vigil mechanism and reviews the status of complaints. SEBI LODR further requires the company to disclose the details of the vigil mechanism on its website and in the annual report.

Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) reporting has gained prominence with the introduction of the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) framework by SEBI, applicable to the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation from FY 2022‑23. The BRSR requires disclosures on nine principles aligned with the National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC), covering areas such as ethics, transparency, accountability, sustainable products, employee well‑being, stakeholder engagement, human rights, environment, and inclusive growth. The report follows a “comply or explain” approach, and entities must provide quantitative metrics (e.g., greenhouse gas emissions, water consumption, diversity ratios) and qualitative narratives.

The integration of board evaluation, whistleblower mechanism, and ESG reporting reflects a shift from compliance‑driven governance to value‑driven governance. The NRC’s evaluation criteria increasingly include ESG competencies of directors. The Audit Committee’s oversight of the vigil mechanism extends to ESG‑related complaints, such as environmental violations or labour rights issues. Companies are expected to align their risk management framework with ESG risks, and the board’s risk committee (where constituted) monitors climate‑related financial risks as recommended by the Task Force on Climate‑related Financial Disclosures (TCFD).

From an examination perspective, candidates should be able to describe the statutory composition and role of the NRC and Audit Committee, outline the steps in a board evaluation process, explain the key features of an effective vigil mechanism, and summarise the BRSR structure and its linkage to the NGRBC principles. Case‑based questions may ask to identify governance deficiencies in a scenario and recommend corrective actions referencing specific sections of the Companies Act and LODR provisions.

  • Confusing the deferred income method with the asset‑deduction method for government grants — they lead to different depreciation and deferred tax outcomes.
  • Applying the 10 % quantitative threshold on segment profit instead of revenue, profit, or assets as required by Ind AS 108.
  • Overlooking the RBI’s IRACP norms when auditing bank loan classification and provisioning.
  • Assuming a unilateral APA automatically binds the foreign tax authority — only bilateral or multilateral APAs do.
  • Treating the vigil mechanism as optional for unlisted companies — Section 177 applies only to listed entities and certain prescribed classes.
  • Neglecting to reconcile segment totals to consolidated figures, a mandatory disclosure under Ind AS 108.
  • Failing to disclose the roll‑back period in an APA application, which limits the agreement to prospective years only.

Accounting for Share‑Based Payments under Ind AS 102

Ind AS 102 governs the recognition, measurement and disclosure of share‑based payment transactions. The standard applies to equity‑settled, cash‑settled and choice‑of‑settlement arrangements granted to employees, directors and other parties in exchange for goods or services. An equity‑settled transaction is measured at the fair value of the equity instruments granted at the grant date; that fair value is not subsequently re‑measured. The expense is recognised over the vesting period, with a corresponding increase in equity (share‑based payment reserve). For cash‑settled transactions, the liability is measured at fair value at each reporting date until settlement, and changes in fair value are recognised in profit or loss. Where a choice of settlement exists, the entity accounts for the component that creates a liability as cash‑settled and the remainder as equity‑settled. Vesting conditions are classified as service conditions, performance conditions or market conditions. Only service and non‑market performance conditions affect the estimate of the number of instruments expected to vest; market conditions are reflected in the grant‑date fair value and do not alter the vesting estimate. Modifications that increase the fair value of the instruments granted are accounted for as additional compensation; modifications that decrease fair value are ignored unless they are detrimental to the employee. Cancellations accelerate any unrecognised expense. Disclosures require the nature and extent of share‑based payment arrangements, the method used to determine fair value (e.g., Black‑Scholes, binomial), the range of exercise prices, weighted‑average remaining contractual life, and the effect on profit or loss and financial position.

Accounting for Non‑Current Assets Held for Sale and Discontinued Operations under Ind AS 105

Ind AS 105 sets out the criteria for classifying a non‑current asset (or disposal group) as held for sale. The asset must be available for immediate sale in its present condition, the sale must be highly probable, and management must be committed to a plan to sell within one year. Once classified, the asset is measured at the lower of its carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell. Depreciation and amortisation cease from the date of classification. The asset is presented separately in the balance sheet, and the associated liabilities of a disposal group are also presented separately. A discontinued operation is a component of an entity that either has been disposed of or is classified as held for sale and represents a separate major line of business or geographical area of operations. The post‑tax profit or loss of the discontinued operation, together with any gain or loss on disposal, is presented as a single line item in the statement of profit and loss, distinct from continuing operations. Comparative information is restated. Disclosures include a description of the discontinued operation, the revenue, expenses and pre‑tax profit or loss, the gain or loss recognised on disposal, and the net cash flows attributable to the operating, investing and financing activities of the discontinued operation. If the criteria for held‑for‑sale classification are no longer met, the asset is re‑classified and measured at the lower of its carrying amount before classification (adjusted for depreciation that would have been recognised) and its recoverable amount.

Examiner's trap
A company grants 10,000 equity‑settled share options to employees on 1 April 20X1. The fair value at grant date is ₹150 per option. The vesting period is three years. At 31 March 20X2, 2,000 options are forfeited because employees left. The entity incorrectly recognises expense for the full 10,000 options over three years. Compute the correct cumulative expense to be recognised at 31 March 20X2.
Total fair value = 10,000 × ₹150 = ₹15,00,000. Expense is recognised over the vesting period based on the number of options expected to vest. At the end of year 1, the estimate of options expected to vest is 8,000 (10,000 – 2,000 forfeited). Cumulative expense = (₹15,00,000 ÷ 3) × (8,000 ÷ 10,000) = ₹5,00,000 × 0.8 = ₹4,00,000. The entity must recognise ₹4,00,000, not ₹5,00,000.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question on share‑based payments appears, first identify the settlement type. For equity‑settled awards, lock the grant‑date fair value and spread it over the vesting period using the estimated number of instruments that will vest. Adjust the estimate each reporting date for forfeitures; do not re‑measure fair value. For cash‑settled awards, re‑measure the liability at each reporting date. Write the journal entries in the order: (1) recognise expense, (2) credit equity or liability. This systematic approach avoids the common error of re‑measuring equity‑settled awards.

Audit of Related Party Transactions and Disclosures under SA 550

SA 550 requires the auditor to obtain an understanding of the entity’s related party relationships and transactions sufficient to assess the risk of material misstatement. The auditor must inquire of management and those charged with governance about the identity of related parties, the nature of relationships, and the terms of transactions. The auditor evaluates whether the entity’s process for identifying related parties is complete, including reviewing shareholder registers, board minutes, and statutory filings. Risk assessment focuses on the possibility that related party transactions may not be conducted at arm’s length, may be concealed, or may be omitted from disclosures. Substantive procedures include testing a sample of transactions for proper authorisation, comparing terms with comparable uncontrolled transactions, and verifying that disclosures in the financial statements comply with Ind AS 24. The auditor also considers the need for a written representation from management confirming the completeness of related party disclosures. If the auditor identifies significant related party transactions that are not adequately disclosed, the auditor must communicate the matter to those charged with governance and consider the impact on the audit opinion. Documentation must include the identified related parties, the nature of transactions, the audit procedures performed, and the conclusions reached. The auditor’s report may include an emphasis‑of‑matter paragraph if the related party transactions are material and the disclosures are extensive but appropriate.

GST Anti‑Profiteering Mechanism and National Anti‑Profiteering Authority

Section 171 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 empowers the National Anti‑Profiteering Authority (NAA) to ensure that the benefit of reduced tax rates or input tax credit is passed on to recipients by way of commensurate reduction in prices. Profiteering is defined as the failure to reduce prices proportionate to the reduction in tax incidence. The NAA, constituted by the Central Government, comprises a chairman and technical members. Upon receipt of a complaint or suo‑motu information, the Standing Committee on Anti‑Profiteering examines the prima facie case and refers it to the Director General of Anti‑Profiteering (DGAP) for investigation. The DGAP collects data on cost structures, pricing policies, and tax incidence before and after the rate change. The investigation report is placed before the NAA, which may order the business to reduce prices, return the undue benefit to recipients with interest at 18 % per annum, and impose a penalty up to 10 % of the profiteered amount. Appeals against NAA orders lie with the High Court. The Finance Act, 2022 introduced a sunset clause: the NAA’s tenure was extended until 30 November 2022, after which the anti‑profiteering function was transferred to the Competition Commission of India (CCI) for a transitional period. Taxpayers must maintain detailed records of cost build‑up, price revisions, and tax incidence to demonstrate compliance. The mechanism applies to both goods and services, and the burden of proof rests on the taxpayer to show that price reductions have been passed on.

Liquidation Process under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) provides a structured liquidation framework when a resolution plan is not approved or the adjudicating authority orders liquidation. The process commences with the appointment of a liquidator by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) on the recommendation of the Committee of Creditors (CoC) or, in the absence of a CoC, by the NCLT itself. The liquidator takes custody of the corporate debtor’s assets, verifies claims, and forms a liquidation estate. Secured creditors may either relinquish their security interest and participate in the distribution or enforce their security outside the liquidation process. The liquidator realises assets through public auction, private sale, or other approved methods, and deposits proceeds into a liquidation account. The distribution waterfall under Section 53 prioritises: (1) insolvency resolution process costs and liquidation costs; (2) workmen’s dues for the preceding 24 months and secured creditors who have relinquished security; (3) wages and unpaid dues of employees (other than workmen) for the preceding 12 months; (4) financial debts owed to unsecured creditors; (5) government dues; (6) any remaining debts; (7) preference shareholders; (8) equity shareholders. The liquidator must complete the process within one year, extendable by 90 days with NCLT approval. A final report is submitted to the NCLT, which passes a dissolution order. Fast‑track liquidation under Section 59 applies to corporate debtors with assets below ₹1 crore and no pending litigation, allowing completion within 90 days. The liquidator’s remuneration is fixed by the CoC or NCLT and paid from the liquidation estate.

  • Confusing equity‑settled and cash‑settled share‑based payment measurement — equity‑settled uses grant‑date fair value only; cash‑settled requires re‑measurement each reporting date.
  • Failing to cease depreciation on assets classified as held for sale from the classification date.
  • Omitting the requirement to present discontinued operation results as a single line item in the statement of profit and loss.
  • Neglecting to obtain written representations from management on completeness of related party disclosures.
  • Assuming the NAA still operates after 30 November 2022 — its functions were transferred to the CCI.
  • Misordering the liquidation waterfall, especially placing government dues ahead of unsecured financial creditors.
  • Avoid these pitfalls to secure maximum marks.
