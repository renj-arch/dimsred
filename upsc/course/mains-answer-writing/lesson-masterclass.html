1. What the examiner actually marks

Every mains answer is read by an examiner processing hundreds of scripts in days. They are not looking for prose beauty — they are scanning for coverage of the demand dimensions. A 250-word answer that hits all dimensions scores higher than a 400-word answer that wanders.

The implicit rubric per question (12.5 marks ≈ 150–250 words):

What earns marks in a mains answer
ComponentWeightWhat it looks like
Demand coverage~50%Every part of the question addressed — definition AND dimensions AND examples
Accuracy~25%Correct facts, right constitutional provisions, correct data, no contradictions
Structure~15%Intro → dimensions → conclusion; paragraph breaks; logical flow
Value-add~10%Current example, scheme, committee, data point, diagram — the "plus" that separates 9 from 12
The brutal truth
Most students lose marks not from ignorance but from partial answers: writing a beautiful essay on one dimension while ignoring the question's second demand. Decode the question before writing — always.

2. Decoding the question

Every mains question has 2–4 hidden demands. Underline them before writing:

  • "Discuss" → present multiple dimensions + balanced view. Not one-sided argument.
  • "Examine" → analyse critically — strengths AND weaknesses, causes AND consequences.
  • "Evaluate" → judge with criteria — what works, what doesn't, verdict.
  • "Critically examine" → examine + take a position. The "critically" demands judgement, not neutrality.
  • "Suggest measures" → solutions only — but must be specific, actionable, multi-level.
  • "Elucidate with examples" → explanation + concrete instances. Abstract theory alone loses marks.
  • "In the context of" / "With reference to" → the topic is anchored to a specific recent event or statement — address that anchor explicitly.
  • Two-part questions ("…and suggest measures") → split the word budget roughly 60/40 between parts.
Decoding example
"Examine the impact of climate change on agriculture in India and suggest measures to make farming resilient."
Demands: (1) impacts — multi-dimensional (crop yield, water, pests, farmer income); (2) measures — specific, multi-level (policy, technology, insurance). Word split: ~140 words impacts, ~110 measures.

3. The answer architecture

The universal mains structure — works for every GS question:

Introduction (2–3 sentences, ~30 words)

  • Restate the question's core in your own words (shows you understood it).
  • Give a framing definition or context (one line).
  • Signal your structure: "This answer examines X through its economic, social and environmental dimensions."

Body — dimension paragraphs (~180–190 words)

Each dimension gets ONE paragraph following IDEA:

  • Idea/topic sentence — the dimension's claim.
  • Development — explain the mechanism (why/how it works).
  • Example — one concrete instance (scheme, event, data, place).
  • Analysis — significance, consequence, or limitation.

3–4 dimensions typical. Order dimensions logically: most important first, or chronological, or cause→effect.

Conclusion (~30–40 words)

  • Synthesis, not summary: state the overall assessment or way forward.
  • For "suggest measures" questions, end with the most important measure or a balanced verdict.
  • Never introduce new arguments in the conclusion.

4. The value-add toolkit

Value-add = one line that signals deeper preparation. Stock these per GS paper:

Value-add items by paper
PaperHigh-value additions
GS1Constitutional articles, historical dates/movements, geographic data (rivers, peaks, soils), schemes (PM-KISAN, MGNREGA), committee reports
GS2Constitutional provisions (Art 21, 32, 226, 368), landmark cases, institutions (EC, CAG, NHRC), bills/acts with years
GS3Scheme names + ministries, data points (ranks, indices, targets), international frameworks (SDGs, Paris Agreement), acts (RTI, NFSA, EIA)
GS4Thinkers (Aristotle, Kant, Gandhi, Ambedkar), constitutional values, committee recommendations (2nd ARC, Punchhi), real/illustrative examples

The rule: one accurate value-add beats three vague generalities. A wrong fact costs more than no fact — accuracy first, always.

5. Word economy — 150 vs 250 words

The word limit is a range, not a target:

  • 150-word questions: intro 2 sentences, 2–3 tight dimensions (~35 words each), conclusion 1 sentence. No examples unless they're one-liners.
  • 250-word questions: intro 3 sentences, 3–4 dimensions (~45 words each), conclusion 2 sentences. Room for one example per dimension.

Compression techniques:

  • Convert clauses to participles: "which was launched in 2015 and aims at" → "launched 2015, aiming at".
  • Use arrows in rough notes → expand only in final.
  • Cut filler: "It is important to note that", "In today's world", "As we all know" — zero marks, pure waste.
  • Numbers and names carry density: "MGNREGA (2005) guarantees 100 days" beats "a scheme guarantees days".

6. Diagrams, flowcharts and maps

For map-based and process questions, a diagram can replace 80 words of prose:

  • Maps: only for geography questions asking locations — label rivers, passes, peaks, soil regions. Practice drawing from memory.
  • Flowcharts: policy cycles, disaster management cycles, election process — boxes + arrows.
  • Diagrams: nitrogen cycle, food chain, court hierarchy, federal structure.
  • Rule: diagram must be labelled and explained in one line — a bare diagram earns nothing.

7. The self-evaluation rubric

After writing any practice answer, score it out of 12.5:

Self-evaluation checklist
CheckMarksQuestion to ask
Demand fully covered6Did I address every part of the question?
Facts accurate3Any wrong article, date, scheme name, or data point?
Structure clear2Intro → dimensions → conclusion, paragraphs broken?
Value-add present1.5At least one example/scheme/data point?

Target: 9+ consistently before the exam. Below 7 = rewrite the answer focusing only on demand coverage.

8. A worked 250-word answer, dissected

Question: "Digital India has transformed governance delivery. Discuss."

Intro (~30w): "Digital India, launched 2015, aims to transform governance through technology. Its impact spans efficiency, inclusion and transparency — this answer examines each."

Dimension 1 — Efficiency (~50w): e-governance reduced paperwork (DigiLocker, UMANG); DBT eliminated leakages (₹X lakh saved); online services cut queues. Example: land records digitisation in 10+ states.

Dimension 2 — Inclusion (~50w): CSCs extend services to rural areas; UPI enabled financial inclusion (100+ crore accounts); PM-KISAN payments direct to farmers' accounts. Example: ONDC for rural commerce.

Dimension 3 — Transparency (~50w): Online portals enable tracking (RTI, court cases); geo-tagged MGNREGA workers reduce corruption; Aadhaar-enabled verification prevents duplicates. Limitation: digital divide excludes elderly/rural without connectivity.

Conclusion (~30w): "Digital India is a governance revolution whose benefits are real but unevenly distributed; bridging the digital divide is the remaining challenge."

Why it scores: all demands covered, specific schemes named, balanced view (benefits + limitation), clean structure, ~230 words.

9. Practice plan

Phase 1 (weeks 1–2): Write 5 answers daily from previous-year questions. Score each with the rubric. Focus ONLY on demand coverage — ignore style.

Phase 2 (weeks 3–4): Same, but add value-add items deliberately — one scheme, one article, one data point per answer.

Phase 3 (last month): Timed full papers — 20 questions in 3 hours. No peeking at notes. Review only your weakest 5 answers per paper.

The compounding effect: answer-writing is a skill, not knowledge. 100 rewritten answers teach more than 100 read answers. Start today.
