What it is. The Personality Test carries 275 marks and is conducted by a board constituted by the Commission. It is not a test of knowledge alone; the Commission's own description is a test of the candidate's suitability for a career in public service. The board has your DAF (Detailed Application Form) in front of it, so your own record supplies much of the material.

What the board is actually reading

Judgement under uncertainty
A question with no clean answer is the point. The board wants to hear you reason, name the trade-off and commit to a position — not list every side and stop.
Balance and temperament
Can you disagree without becoming defensive? Can you accept a correction without collapsing? A calm "I had not considered that; my view would change because…" is a strong answer.
Integrity
If you do not know something, say so and reason from what you do know. Bluffing is the fastest way to lose the board, because the next question will find the edge of it.
Connection to the job
The board is asking whether you understand what a civil servant does: implement, arbitrate, explain, and carry responsibility when something goes wrong.

Your DAF is the question paper

Most interviews begin with what you wrote about yourself. Prepare the record, not a script.

Home state and district
Be ready for local administration, a recent development, a problem and what you would do about it. Know the basic geography and economy of the place you come from.
Education and optional
A question can go back to your graduation subject. Know why you chose the optional, and one idea from it you can explain simply.
Work experience
Expect "why did you leave?", "what did you learn?", "what would you do differently?". Answer with a specific incident, not a job description.
Hobbies and interests
Only claim what you can discuss for five minutes. A hobby is a door into temperament questions: discipline, patience, teamwork, failure.

Five ways good candidates lose marks

1 · Arguing with the board
Hold your position once; if the board pushes with a new fact, weigh it. Defensiveness reads as brittleness, not conviction.
2 · Reciting a coaching answer
The board has heard the template. A shorter, specific answer in your own words is stronger than a polished paragraph that answers a different question.
3 · Pretending to know
"I do not know" is acceptable. "I do not know, but here is how I would find out" is better. Invention is fatal.
4 · Ignoring the question
If asked about a problem in your district, do not pivot to a national scheme. Answer the question asked, then connect it upward if useful.
5 · No questions at the end
If invited to ask something, have one real question about the service or the role. Do not ask about the result or use it to flatter.
6 · Treating it as a formality
275 marks is more than an entire GS paper's weight in the final tally. Prepare it with the same seriousness as GS II.

Question bank

practice prompts

These are question shapes, not a predicted paper. Answer aloud, in two minutes, then ask what your answer revealed.

  • You are the district magistrate. A highway project is cleared, but a tribal community says the survey missed its sacred grove. What do you do first?
  • Your predecessor took a decision you believe was wrong but popular. Do you reverse it? How do you explain the change?
  • Should a civil servant have a political opinion? How do you keep it from becoming a political act?
  • What is the difference between being efficient and being effective in administration? Give an example from your own life.
  • A subordinate is honest but slow; another is fast but cuts corners. How do you allocate work?
  • Why should the state fund the arts when hospitals are short of beds?
  • You are asked to speak at a public meeting where the crowd is hostile to a decision you helped make. How do you begin?
  • What would you do if a minister asked you to do something lawful but clearly against the public interest?
  • Which is a better test of character: failure or success? Why?
  • Tell us about a time you changed your mind after hearing an argument. What changed it?
