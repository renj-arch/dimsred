The Devanagari script

U+0900–U+097F

Sanskrit is written in Devanagari for the UPSC paper. The grammar is more inflected than Hindi: case endings, dual forms, sandhi and compounds carry meaning. Do not substitute modern Hindi constructions for Sanskrit ones.

Direction and characters
Devanagari runs left to right. The page uses the Unicode range U+0900–U+097F. Write the language in its own script, not in a Latin transliteration.
Traps in Sanskrit
  • Use Sanskrit case endings and verb forms, not Hindi equivalents.
  • Resolve sandhi before you translate; do not treat the surface form as a separate word.

Your Sanskrit course path

5 modules · do them in order

Paper A is five skills, not one. Work through these modules, then use the workbook and script checker below. Each module links to the section of this page that carries the material.

1. Script and decoding
Do not read Sanskrit through a transliteration. Work directly in Devanagari (U+0900–U+097F, left-to-right). Spend the first sessions learning the conjuncts and half-forms you meet most: a slow first read costs minutes in the exam, a fast second read is the skill that scores.
2. Comprehension method
Read the passage once for the argument, once for the detail. Before answering, write the claim in one line in Sanskrit. Answer in the language of the question paper, cite the passage rather than your opinion, and keep each answer to two or three sentences. Inference questions reward "the passage suggests", not outside knowledge.
3. Precis method
Compress to roughly one-third of the original. Keep the claim, the reasons and the tone; drop examples and repetition. Do not add a conclusion the author did not write. After drafting, check every sentence still belongs to the Sanskrit register of the original — a precis that drifts into essay style loses marks.
4. Translation drills
Translate the same English paragraph both ways. Meaning and register beat word order. Watch the traps listed under the script section — for Sanskrit they are not generic: Use Sanskrit case endings and verb forms, not Hindi equivalents. Check your Sanskrit output with the script checker before comparing meaning.
5. Essay structure
250 words in Sanskrit: one opening that states the position, two developed points with an example each, one closing. Plan in Sanskrit for five minutes before writing. The examiner reads organisation and language control first; a correct but shapeless answer scores less than a structured one.

Suggested pace for a December attempt: one module per week, workbook every other day, script check on every written answer.

Practice workbook

Original study prompts · not PYQs

These are original tasks, not previous-year questions. Write your answers in Sanskrit; use the script checker below before you ask for feedback.

1. Comprehension
Read a 300–400 word Sanskrit passage. Answer in Sanskrit:
  1. What is the main claim?
  2. What are the two strongest supporting reasons?
  3. What does the writer want the reader to do?
Then close the passage and write a three-sentence summary.
2. Precis
Write a precis of the same passage in about one-third of its length. Keep the claim, the reasons and the tone. Do not add your opinion. Check the script before you compare with the original.
3. Usage and vocabulary
Choose ten words from the passage that you would not use in everyday speech. Write one Sanskrit sentence for each and note the English meaning. Then write one sentence using a common idiom from the passage.
4. Translate English → Sanskrit
Translate the paragraph below into Sanskrit. Then translate your version back into English without looking at the original. Compare meaning, not word order.
Reading a newspaper carefully is a skill that improves with practice. A reader who notices the main claim, the evidence and the tone can summarize an article in a few sentences. This habit helps in the examination because it saves time and prevents answers from becoming a list of disconnected points. It also builds vocabulary, which is useful in the translation section. Ten minutes a day is enough if the reading is active.
5. Translate Sanskrit → English
Take the first paragraph of your passage. Translate it into English. Underline the phrases that had no direct equivalent. Rewrite those phrases in plain English.
6. Short essay
Write 250 words in Sanskrit on one of these topics: the value of public libraries; why local languages matter in a democracy; how a city can protect its trees. Use a beginning, two developed points and a close.

Advanced Grammar: Sandhi and Compounds

Mastery of sandhi (euphonic combination) and samāsa (compound formation) is essential for both the translation and the essay components of Paper A. Sandhi governs how word‑final and word‑initial sounds interact, while samāsa determines the semantic relationship between compounded stems. A solid grasp of these topics reduces errors in parsing unseen passages and improves the fluency of your own Sanskrit composition.

Key sandhi categories

  • Svara‑sandhi – vowel coalescence (e.g., अ + इ → ए).
  • Vyañjana‑sandhi – consonant assimilation (e.g., त् + श् → च्छ्).
  • Visarga‑sandhi – treatment of final ः before following sounds.

Effective Answer Writing Strategies

Paper A evaluates not only linguistic knowledge but also the ability to present arguments clearly in Sanskrit. Adopt a three‑part structure for essay‑type questions: प्रस्तावना (introduction), मुख्यभागः (body), and उपसंहारः (conclusion). Allocate roughly 10 % of the time to planning, 80 % to writing, and 10 % to revision.

Time‑boxing tip
Use a mental stopwatch: 5 minutes for outline, 30 minutes for drafting, 5 minutes for polishing. Stick to the limit even if you feel unfinished; a complete, coherent answer scores higher than a perfect but incomplete one.

When translating, keep a glossary of recurring technical terms (e.g., धर्म, मोक्ष) to maintain consistency. For comprehension questions, underline keywords in the passage before writing the answer.

Self‑Assessment and Mock Practice

Periodic self‑testing reveals gaps that passive reading cannot. Create a personal question bank from previous years’ papers, standard textbooks, and model passages. After each mock, mark every error, categorize it (sandhi, vocabulary, syntax, comprehension), and schedule targeted revision.

Sanskrit Prosody and Metre

Understanding the rhythmic structure of Sanskrit verse is essential for both literary appreciation and precise translation. The classical metres (छन्दस्) are defined by the pattern of light (लघु) and heavy (गुरु) syllables in each pāda. The most common metre in the epics is the अनुष्टुभ्, consisting of four pādas of eight syllables each, with a fixed pattern of guru‑laghu in the last four positions. Mastery of the gaṇa‑based notation (य, र, त, भ, न, म, स, ज) allows rapid scansion of any verse.

  • Identify the gaṇa sequence for each pāda.
  • Mark the caesura (यति) after the fourth syllable.
  • Apply the rules of यति‑विराम to detect permissible variations.
Example: Anuṣṭubh Verse Analysis
रामो राजमणिः सदा विजयते रामं रमेशं भजे।
रामं रामं रामं रामं रामं रामं रामं रामं।
Scansion: गु ल गु ल | गु ल गु ल (×4). Each pāda follows the अनुष्टुभ् pattern; the caesura falls after the fourth syllable.

Manuscript Reading and Critical Edition

Working with palm‑leaf or paper manuscripts requires familiarity with scribal conventions such as ligatures, abbreviation marks (अवग्रह, विराम), and marginal glosses. A critical edition collates multiple witnesses, records variant readings in an apparatus criticus, and adopts a siglum system (e.g., A, B, C) for each manuscript. The editor must decide between lectio difficilior and lectio facilior, weighing external evidence (date, provenance) against internal criteria (grammatical consistency, metrical fit).

  1. Transcribe each folio faithfully, preserving original orthography.
  2. Normalize sandhi only in the edited text, not in the apparatus.
  3. Document emendations with justification in the commentary.
Tip
When collating manuscripts, always note the siglum and folio number for every variant; this prevents accidental conflation of readings.

Sanskrit Computational Linguistics Basics

Modern research increasingly employs computational tools for morphological analysis, sandhi splitting, and syntactic parsing. Finite‑state transducers built from Pāṇinian rules can generate all valid forms of a given dhātu. Libraries such as the Sanskrit Heritage Engine provide APIs for lemmatization and compound segmentation. When integrating these tools into a workflow, validate output against a manually annotated gold standard to measure precision and recall.

  • Use a rule‑based morphological generator for verb forms.
  • Apply a statistical sandhi splitter for unseen texts.
  • Evaluate parser output with standard metrics (F1, accuracy).

Sanskrit Syntax for Formal Composition

Formal Sanskrit prose follows a strict word‑order hierarchy: the verb typically occupies the final position, while the subject, object, and adjuncts precede it in a logical sequence. Mastery of the kriyā‑pada placement enables candidates to construct sentences that satisfy the examiners’ expectation of grammatical precision. The use of avyaya particles such as च, वा, and अपि must respect the sandhi rules governing vowel coalescence and consonant assimilation. Practising the conversion of simple sentences into the anvaya (linear) and pratipadika (inflected) forms sharpens the ability to manipulate case endings without altering meaning.

Illustrative Transformation
रामः ग्रामं गच्छति।
ग्रामं रामः गच्छति। (Object fronted for emphasis)

Lexical Enrichment through Synonym Chains

Expanding vocabulary is essential for the essay and translation components. A productive method is to build synonym chains around a core concept, each link introducing a nuance of register, tone, or semantic field. For instance, the concept “knowledge” can be traced from ज्ञानम् (general knowledge) to विद्या (disciplined learning), प्रज्ञानम् (intuitive insight), and बोधः (awakening). Writing short paragraphs that deliberately employ each synonym in context reinforces collocational awareness and prevents repetitive phrasing.

Practice Routine
Select a theme daily, list five synonyms, and compose a four‑sentence paragraph using each once. Review for sandhi compliance.

Translation Techniques for Administrative Texts

Administrative Sanskrit employs a compact, formulaic style: passive constructions, nominalised verbs, and honorific titles dominate. Translating such texts into English requires preserving the hierarchical tone while rendering the syntax readable. Key steps include: (1) identify the kartṛ (agent) hidden in the instrumental case; (2) convert nominalised verbs (करणीयम्) into active infinitives; (3) retain honorifics (श्रीमन्, महोदयः) as titles rather than literal translations. Consistent practice with gazette notifications and court orders builds the requisite register sensitivity.

Sanskrit Morphology: Derivational Processes

Derivational morphology in Sanskrit builds new lexical items from roots through the addition of primary and secondary suffixes. Primary suffixes (कृत्) attach directly to the verbal root and produce nouns, adjectives, or indeclinables that retain the root’s core meaning. Secondary suffixes (तद्धित) operate on already formed stems, generating further nouns, adjectives, or adverbs. Understanding the distinction between कृत् and तद्धित is essential for parsing compound words and for constructing precise administrative terminology.

Key primary suffixes include -अ (agent noun), -तृ (agent), -न (action noun), and -य (abstract noun). Each suffix triggers specific vowel‑gradation (गुण/वृद्धि) and may cause the insertion of an augment (आगम). For instance, the root भू + -तृ yields भवितृ (one who becomes). Secondary suffixes such as -इक, -य, -त modify the stem’s gender, number, or semantic nuance. The suffix -इक often forms adjectives denoting “pertaining to” (e.g., राजिक “royal”).

Derivation Example
Root: कृ (to do). Apply primary suffix -ण → करण (instrument). Then add secondary suffix -इक → कारणिक (relating to cause).
The sequence shows how a root passes through कृत् then तद्धित layers, each altering meaning and grammatical category.

Sanskrit Semantics: Contextual Nuances

Semantic precision in Sanskrit hinges on the interplay of वाच्य (literal meaning) and लक्षणा (implied meaning). In official documents, a term like धर्म may denote “law”, “duty”, or “righteousness” depending on the surrounding syntactic frame. Recognising the contextual markers—case endings, particles such as च, वा, and the presence of उपमान (simile)—allows the examiner to select the appropriate gloss.

Polysemy is resolved through संयोग (collocation) and विरोध (contrast). For example, राजा in राजा धर्मं रक्षति (the king protects dharma) carries a sovereign sense, whereas in राजा जनस्य सेवकः (the king is the servant of the people) it conveys a functional role. Mastery of these nuances improves translation fidelity and answer articulation.

Study Tip
Create a personal glossary of high‑frequency administrative terms with at least three contextual senses each; review weekly using flashcards.

Sanskrit Pragmatics in Official Correspondence

Official Sanskrit correspondence follows a concise register that blends classical vocabulary with administrative formulae. The opening salutation typically uses श्रीमान् or महोदय followed by the recipient’s title, while the closing employs भवदीयः or आज्ञया. Sentences are constructed in the passive voice to convey impartiality, and the use of कर्तृ‑less constructions (कर्तृविहीनम्) is standard. Familiarity with these conventions enables the aspirant to produce grammatically correct and stylistically appropriate drafts.

  • Identify the standard opening and closing formulae.
  • Practice converting active‑voice sentences into the passive administrative style.
  • Memorise the most frequent honorific compounds (e.g., श्रीमान्‌महोदयः).
Example: Transforming a Directive
Active: अधिकारी पत्रं प्रेषयति।
Passive (official): पत्रं अधिकारीणां प्रेष्यते।
The passive construction removes the agent, uses the instrumental plural अधिकारीणाम्, and employs the passive verb form प्रेष्यते.

Sanskrit Stylistics for Official Writing

Official Sanskrit prose demands clarity, dignity, and adherence to prescribed syntactic patterns. The writer must favour the कर्तृवाच्य (active voice) for directives, while the कर्मणीवाच्य (passive) suits descriptive reports. Vocabulary should draw from the शिष्ट register — words such as आदेशः, निर्देशः, प्रतिवेदनम् — avoiding colloquial forms. Sentence length is kept moderate; each clause typically ends with a विरामचिह्नः (full stop) or प्रश्नचिह्नः (question mark) to aid readability in printed orders.

Consistent use of संधि rules ensures phonetic smoothness. For instance, तत् + अर्थः → तदर्थः follows the वृद्धि sandhi. Compound formation (समास) should prefer तत्पुरुष and कर्मधारय types for precision, e.g., राजाज्ञा (royal order) rather than ambiguous द्वन्द्व constructions.

Sanskrit Lexicography and Word Formation

Understanding derivational morphology enables the creation of precise administrative terminology. Primary suffixes (कृत्) such as -तृ (agent), -न (action), and -य (instrument) attach to verbal roots to yield nouns like कर्तृ (doer), करणम् (instrument). Secondary suffixes (तद्धित) like -इय, -त्व, -मय form adjectives and abstract nouns: राजीय (royal), सत्यत्वम् (truthfulness), ज्ञानमयः (knowledge‑filled). Mastery of these processes reduces reliance on loanwords and preserves linguistic purity.

Derivation Example
Root: भू (to be) → भवितृ (future being) using -तृ; भवित्वम् (state of becoming) using -त्व.
Both forms follow standard कृत् and तद्धित patterns attested in धातुपाठ and उणादि lists.

Sanskrit Textual Criticism and Editorial Practice

Critical editing of Sanskrit manuscripts requires collating multiple witnesses, noting variant readings (पाठभेदाः), and reconstructing the most plausible archetype. The editor records each variant in a critical apparatus using sigla for manuscripts. Emendations are proposed only when a reading is demonstrably corrupt, supported by उपपत्ति (contextual fit) and अन्वय (syntactic coherence). The final text is presented with a stemma codicum diagram to illustrate manuscript relationships.

Editorial Tip
Always preserve the original orthography in the apparatus; normalize only the main text for readability.

Sanskrit Computational Morphology for Automated Parsing

Modern language‑technology pipelines require a precise formalisation of Sanskrit morphology. The pāṇinian sūtras can be mapped to finite‑state transducers that generate all valid surface forms from a lexical root and a set of morphological tags. This approach enables deterministic segmentation of sandhi‑joined words, automatic identification of kṛt and taddhita affixes, and reliable lemma extraction for large corpora.

Key Design Principles

  • Represent each dhātu with its gaṇa and lakāra features.
  • Encode sandhi rules as rewrite rules that operate on the phonemic tier before orthographic conversion.
  • Use a unified tagset (POS, case, number, gender, voice) compatible with the Universal Dependencies schema.

Sanskrit Rhetorical Figures in Official Discourse

Official Sanskrit correspondence historically employs a repertoire of alaṅkāra to convey authority, clarity, and decorum. Mastery of these figures aids both composition and critical reading of gazettes, orders, and policy documents.

Principal Figures

  1. Anuprāsa – alliteration used for mnemonic headings.
  2. Yamaka – repetition of a word with different meanings to stress dual aspects.
  3. Śleṣa – deliberate polysemy allowing a single phrase to address multiple audiences.
Composition Tip
When drafting a notification, place an anuprāsa in the subject line (e.g., “सुरक्षा सुव्यवस्था”) to improve recall; follow with a yamaka in the body to reinforce key obligations.

Recognising these devices prevents misinterpretation of ambiguous phrasing and ensures faithful translation into vernacular languages.

Sanskrit Discourse Structure in Official Reports

Official Sanskrit reports follow a predictable macro‑structure: प्रस्तावना (introduction), विषयविस्तारः (elaboration), निर्णयः (decision), and अनुबन्धः (appendices). Each segment uses a distinct register of vocabulary and a limited set of syntactic patterns, which makes the text amenable to systematic analysis.

The introduction typically employs the विधि mood with passive constructions such as कर्तव्यम् अस्ति. The elaboration section favours compound nouns (समासाः) and सन्धि‑rich sentences to convey dense information compactly. The decision part reverts to direct imperative forms (कुर्यात्) for clarity.

  • Identify the four macro‑segments in any given report.
  • Mark the dominant mood and voice in each segment.
  • Note recurring compound types (e.g., तत्पुरुष, बहुव्रीहि).
Example: Segment Identification
प्रस्तावना: अधीनस्थानां कार्यानि समीक्ष्यन्ते। विषयविस्तारः: राजकीययोजनानां क्रियान्वयनं विवृणोति। निर्णयः: सर्वे अधिकारीणां सहमति आवश्यकम्। अनुबन्धः: सांख्यिकाः तालिकाः संलग्नाः।
Solution: The passage contains all four macro‑segments in order. The introduction uses passive समीक्ष्यन्ते, the elaboration uses a tatpurusha compound राजकीययोजनानां, the decision uses imperative आवश्यकम्, and the appendix lists attachments.
Tip
When drafting, keep each macro‑segment under 150 words; this mirrors the brevity expected in UPSC answer scripts.

Sanskrit Lexical Registers for Legal Terminology

Legal Sanskrit draws on a specialised register that blends classical धर्मशास्त्र vocabulary with modern administrative coinages. Core terms such as अधिकारः, दायित्वम्, न्यायः retain their classical semantics but acquire procedural nuances.

Key strategies for mastering this register:

  1. Compile a bilingual glossary of the 200 most frequent legal lemmas.
  2. Practice converting Hindi/English legal clauses into Sanskrit using the glossary.
  3. Analyse at least three authentic government notifications for collocation patterns.

Regular exposure to the भारतस्य राजपत्रम् (Gazette of India) Sanskrit editions accelerates internalisation of the register.
