The Perso-Arabic script

U+0600–U+06FF

Urdu is written right to left in the Perso-Arabic script, normally in Nastaliq style. The paper tests standard written Urdu, including comprehension, precis, usage and translation. Keep the text right-to-left and use Urdu spellings.

Your Urdu course path

5 modules · do them in order

Paper A is five skills, not one. Work through these modules, then use the workbook and script checker below. Each module links to the section of this page that carries the material.

1. Script and decoding
Do not read Urdu through a transliteration. Work directly in Perso-Arabic (U+0600–U+06FF, right-to-left). Spend the first sessions learning the conjuncts and half-forms you meet most: a slow first read costs minutes in the exam, a fast second read is the skill that scores.
2. Comprehension method
Read the passage once for the argument, once for the detail. Before answering, write the claim in one line in Urdu. Answer in the language of the question paper, cite the passage rather than your opinion, and keep each answer to two or three sentences. Inference questions reward "the passage suggests", not outside knowledge.
3. Precis method
Compress to roughly one-third of the original. Keep the claim, the reasons and the tone; drop examples and repetition. Do not add a conclusion the author did not write. After drafting, check every sentence still belongs to the Urdu register of the original — a precis that drifts into essay style loses marks.
4. Translation drills
Translate the same English paragraph both ways. Meaning and register beat word order. Watch the traps listed under the script section — for Urdu they are not generic: Keep the text right-to-left; do not paste it into a left-to-right block without setting dir="rtl". Check your Urdu output with the script checker before comparing meaning.
5. Essay structure
250 words in Urdu: one opening that states the position, two developed points with an example each, one closing. Plan in Urdu for five minutes before writing. The examiner reads organisation and language control first; a correct but shapeless answer scores less than a structured one.

Suggested pace for a December attempt: one module per week, workbook every other day, script check on every written answer.

Practice workbook

Original study prompts · not PYQs

These are original tasks, not previous-year questions. Write your answers in Urdu; use the script checker below before you ask for feedback.

1. Comprehension
Read a 300–400 word Urdu passage. Answer in Urdu:
  1. What is the main claim?
  2. What are the two strongest supporting reasons?
  3. What does the writer want the reader to do?
Then close the passage and write a three-sentence summary.
2. Precis
Write a precis of the same passage in about one-third of its length. Keep the claim, the reasons and the tone. Do not add your opinion. Check the script before you compare with the original.
3. Usage and vocabulary
Choose ten words from the passage that you would not use in everyday speech. Write one Urdu sentence for each and note the English meaning. Then write one sentence using a common idiom from the passage.
4. Translate English → Urdu
Translate the paragraph below into Urdu. Then translate your version back into English without looking at the original. Compare meaning, not word order.
Reading a newspaper carefully is a skill that improves with practice. A reader who notices the main claim, the evidence and the tone can summarize an article in a few sentences. This habit helps in the examination because it saves time and prevents answers from becoming a list of disconnected points. It also builds vocabulary, which is useful in the translation section. Ten minutes a day is enough if the reading is active.
5. Translate Urdu → English
Take the first paragraph of your passage. Translate it into English. Underline the phrases that had no direct equivalent. Rewrite those phrases in plain English.
6. Short essay
Write 250 words in Urdu on one of these topics: the value of public libraries; why local languages matter in a democracy; how a city can protect its trees. Use a beginning, two developed points and a close.

Advanced Grammar Nuances

Mastering the subtleties of Urdu grammar is essential for scoring well in Paper A. The language distinguishes between masculine and feminine nouns not only in form but also in verb agreement, adjective inflection, and post‑position usage. A common error is treating all plural nouns as masculine; in Urdu, the plural of a feminine noun often retains feminine agreement. Additionally, the izafat construction (the genitive linker -e- / -i-) must match the gender and number of the head noun. Understanding the distribution of کا, کی, کے across registers helps avoid mismatched concord.

  • Identify the gender of each noun before applying agreement rules.
  • Practice converting singular sentences to plural while preserving gender concord.
  • Use authentic texts to spot izafat patterns in context.
Example: Gender‑sensitive agreement
لڑکی کتاب پڑھتی ہے۔ (The girl reads a book.)
لڑکیاں کتابیں پڑھتی ہیں۔ (The girls read books.)

Essay Structuring Strategies

A well‑organized essay demonstrates clarity of thought and command of the language. Begin with a concise thesis statement that outlines the central argument. Follow with three to four body paragraphs, each anchored by a topic sentence that directly supports the thesis. Use cohesive devices such as پہلے, دوسرے, اس کے علاوہ to guide the reader. Conclude by restating the thesis in light of the evidence presented, and add a reflective closing remark.

  1. Draft an outline before writing; allocate word limits per section.
  2. Employ varied sentence structures: simple, compound, and complex.
  3. Proofread for script accuracy, especially diacritics and hamza placement.
Tip for time management
Allocate the first 10 minutes to outline, 40 minutes to write, and 10 minutes to revise. This rhythm prevents rushed conclusions and ensures script fidelity.

Translation Strategies for Paper A

Effective translation in the Urdu paper requires a balance between literal fidelity and idiomatic fluency. Begin by segmenting the source text into logical units — clauses, sentences, or paragraphs — and identify the register (formal, literary, administrative). Preserve the syntactic hierarchy of the original while adapting word order to Urdu’s SOV pattern. Use glossaries of recurring administrative terms (e.g., حکومت, اعلان) to maintain consistency across the answer script.

  • Read the entire passage before translating to grasp context.
  • Mark culture‑specific references and decide whether to transliterate or explain.
  • Draft a rough version, then refine for grammatical agreement (gender, number, case).
  • Proofread for orthographic uniformity (see next section).
Quick Tip
Keep a personal “term bank” of 30–40 high‑frequency words; revising it daily reduces lookup time during the exam.

Orthographic Consistency Checks

Urdu orthography combines Perso‑Arabic letters with diacritical marks that are often omitted in printed matter. In the mains answer booklet, examiners expect consistent use of zer, zabar, pesh, and shadda where they affect meaning. Develop a checklist:

  1. Verify every ہ (do‑chashmi he) versus ۂ (goal he) according to grammatical role.
  2. Confirm ی (choti ye) and ے (bari ye) placement in final positions.
  3. Check nasalisation marks (ں) on nouns and verbs.
  4. Ensure ٔ (hamza) appears on آ, ئ, ؤ where required.
LetterCommon VariantRule
ہۂUse ۂ for feminine singular nouns in oblique case.
یےے only in final position of a word.
ں—Attach to preceding letter; never standalone.

Run the checklist after each translation draft; a single pass catches most orthographic slips.

Advanced Vocabulary Building

Expanding a precise lexical repertoire is essential for the descriptive and analytical parts of Paper A. Focus on synonym clusters that differentiate subtle shades of meaning, such as خوبصورت, حسین, دلکش for “beautiful”. Practice by writing a short paragraph each week where you deliberately replace a common word with a less‑used counterpart, then verify the nuance with a reliable dictionary.

  • Collect 10‑15 high‑frequency root words and list their derived forms.
  • Create flashcards that pair each root with three context‑specific collocations.
  • Review the cards using spaced repetition to cement long‑term recall.

Contextual Translation Techniques

Translation in the exam is not a literal word‑for‑word exercise; it demands awareness of register, idiom, and cultural reference. When you encounter a phrase like آپ کی خدمت میں, decide whether the target language requires a formal “at your service” or a more neutral “for you”. Use parallel texts — official gazettes, literary excerpts, and news editorials — to see how the same concept shifts across genres.

Tip
Maintain a personal glossary of recurring idiomatic patterns; revisit it before each mock test to reduce hesitation during the timed translation section.

Practice Passage Analysis

Analyzing a complete passage trains you to identify main ideas, supporting details, and the author’s tone in one reading. Begin by skimming for the thesis sentence, then mark cohesive devices such as conjunctions (لیکن, اس لیے) and referential pronouns. Summarise each paragraph in a single sentence; this habit sharpens both comprehension and precis writing.

Idiomatic Expressions in Urdu Prose

Urdu prose relies heavily on idioms that convey cultural nuance and emotional depth. Mastery of these expressions enables a candidate to produce writing that resonates with examiners and reflects a sophisticated command of the language. Common idioms such as “دل کو چھو لینا” (to touch the heart) or “آگ لگانا” (to ignite a controversy) appear frequently in editorial and literary texts.

  • Identify the literal and figurative meanings of each idiom.
  • Practice embedding idioms in formal essays without disrupting syntactic flow.
  • Consult reputable Urdu dictionaries to verify usage contexts.
Example: Idiom Usage
اس مضمون میں مصنف نے “دل کو چھو لینا” کا استعمال کر کے قارئین کے جذبات کو متحرک کیا ہے۔
The writer uses “دل کو چھو لینا” to evoke empathy, illustrating how idiomatic language can strengthen persuasive writing.

Syntactic Variation in Formal Writing

Formal Urdu writing exhibits a range of sentence structures, from simple declarative clauses to complex compound‑complex constructions. Understanding how to vary syntax improves readability and demonstrates grammatical flexibility. Key patterns include the use of subordinate clauses introduced by کہ, جس نے, and جب کہ, as well as the strategic placement of adverbial phrases for emphasis.

  1. Combine two simple sentences into a compound sentence using “اور” or “لیکن”.
  2. Transform a direct statement into an indirect one with کہ to practice reported speech.
  3. Rewrite a paragraph by alternating active and passive voice where appropriate.
Tip
When drafting an essay, first outline the logical flow in simple sentences, then enrich each point by adding subordinate clauses and varied connectors.

Rhetorical Devices in Urdu Prose

Understanding the stylistic tools that shape persuasive Urdu writing is essential for Paper A. Classical devices such as tashbih (simile), isti‘ara (metaphor), and kinaya (metonymy) appear frequently in editorials and literary essays. Their correct deployment not only enriches expression but also signals a candidate’s command of the language’s rhetorical tradition.

When analysing a passage, identify the device, note the two terms being compared, and explain the intended effect on the reader. Practice by rewriting a plain sentence using at least two devices, then compare the nuance.

Example: Transforming a Simple Statement
Original: شہر میں ترقی ہو رہی ہے۔
Rewritten: شہر ترقی کی راہ پر گامزن ہے، جیسے سورج صبح کی روشنی سے چمکتا ہے۔ (Uses simile and personification.)
Tip
Mark each rhetorical device in a different colour while reading; this visual cue speeds up identification during the exam.

Cohesion and Coherence in Formal Essays

A high‑scoring essay displays logical flow at both macro and micro levels. Macro cohesion is achieved through clear thesis statements, well‑defined paragraphs, and transitional phrases such as اس کے علاوہ, اس طرح, and نتیجے کے طور پر. Micro cohesion relies on pronoun reference, lexical chains, and consistent tense usage.

To improve cohesion, outline the essay first, then insert linking words deliberately. After drafting, read aloud to detect abrupt jumps or ambiguous references.

Error Analysis and Self‑Correction Techniques

Systematic error analysis turns mistakes into learning data. After each practice session, categorize errors into script, grammar, vocabulary, and style. Use a table to log frequency, then target the top two categories in the next study block.

Self‑correction is most effective when you rewrite the faulty sentence, explain the rule violated, and produce a correct version. This three‑step loop reinforces the underlying pattern.

Advanced Morphological Patterns

Urdu morphology relies heavily on root‑and‑pattern alternations borrowed from Arabic and Persian. A single triliteral root can generate dozens of forms through vowel changes, affixation, and the insertion of the hamza or the madd. Recognising these patterns enables rapid decoding of unfamiliar vocabulary in the mains paper. For instance, the root ك‑ت‑ب yields كتاب (book), كاتب (writer), مكتبة (library) and مكتوب (written). Mastery of the ten‑form system (فَعَلَ، فَعِلَ، فُعِلَ، أَفْعَلَ، تَفَاعَلَ، إِنْفَعَلَ، اِسْتَفْعَلَ، مُفَاعَلَ، مُفْعِلَ، مُسْتَفْعِلَ) is essential for both comprehension and essay writing.

Example
Root: س‑م‑ع (to hear). Forms: سمع (heard), سامع (listener), مستمع (audience), تسميع (recitation).
Identify the pattern (فَعَلَ، فَاعِل، مُفْتَعِل، تَفْعِيل) and note the semantic shift from action to agent to abstract noun.

Discourse Markers in Formal Urdu

Formal prose uses a limited set of discourse markers to signal logical relations. Common markers include لہذا (therefore), اس کے علاوہ (moreover), تاہم (however), and اس طرح (thus). Their placement at clause boundaries guides the examiner through argument structure. Overuse or misuse creates ambiguity; a well‑placed marker can replace a whole subordinate clause, tightening the essay.

Tip
When drafting, first outline the logical flow in English, then insert the appropriate Urdu marker at each transition. This prevents marker clustering and preserves coherence.

Comparative Stylistics for Translation

Translation between Urdu and English demands awareness of stylistic registers. Urdu favors nominalised constructions (کتاب لکھنا → “the writing of a book”) while English prefers verbal forms. Passive voice is more frequent in Urdu official documents; active voice is preferred in English academic writing. Lexical density also differs: a single Urdu compound (قانونِ اساسی) may require a phrase (“the fundamental law”) in English. Practising parallel texts sharpens the ability to preserve tone, register, and nuance.

Pragmatic Use of Register in Official Correspondence

Official Urdu correspondence demands a register that balances respect, clarity, and brevity. The choice of honorifics, verb forms, and lexical items signals the hierarchical relationship between sender and recipient. Writers should prefer the polite plural آپ and the respectful verb endings ‑یں / ‑یں گے over familiar forms. Noun‑phrase expansion with مہربانی فرما کر or براہ کرم softens directives without adding ambiguity.

Consistent use of the passive construction کیا گیا removes agency and focuses on action, a hallmark of bureaucratic style. Avoid colloquial idioms such as بہت اچھا in favour of ممتاز or اعلیٰ. The following example illustrates a standard request letter.

Model Request Letter (excerpt)
محترم ڈائریکٹر صاحب،
براہ کرم میری درخواست پر غور فرمائیں۔
The passage uses honorific address, polite plural, and a courteous modal phrase.

Morphosyntactic Alignment in Translating Legal Texts

Legal translation requires precise mapping of Urdu morphological categories to the target language’s syntactic slots. The Urdu ergative case marker نے appears only with transitive perfective verbs; translators must preserve this alignment by rendering the agent explicitly in languages lacking ergativity. Similarly, the postposition کو marks definite direct objects and dative experiencers; omission leads to loss of legal nuance.

Compound verbs such as قرار دena (to decide) combine a noun with a light verb. The light verb carries tense, aspect, and agreement, so the translation must retain the compound’s unitary semantics. A systematic checklist — case marking, verb complex, and clause linkage — reduces mistranslation risk.

Translation Checklist
