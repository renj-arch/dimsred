Metallurgy — Extraction Processes, Roasting, Calcination, Ellingham Diagram & Refining

Metallurgy is the science and technology of extracting metals from their ores and refining them for use. Only a few metals (Au, Pt, Ag, Cu) occur in native (free) state; most are found as compounds — oxides, sulfides, carbonates, halides, and silicates. The extraction process involves three main stages: (1) concentration of ore (removal of gangue — hydraulic washing, magnetic separation, froth flotation, leaching), (2) extraction of metal (converting the concentrated ore to metal via pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy, or electrometallurgy), and (3) refining (purification of crude metal). Key thermal processes: calcination (heating in limited air to decompose carbonates/hydroxides) and roasting (heating in excess air to convert sulfides to oxides). The Ellingham diagram plots ΔG° vs T for the formation of oxides, sulfides, and chlorides — the lower the line, the more stable the compound. Smelting uses a reducing agent (C, CO, H₂, or a more reactive metal) to reduce the oxide. Refining methods: distillation, electrolytic refining, zone refining, Mond's process, and van Arkel-de Boer process.

Part 1

⛷️ Occurrence of Metals and Concentration of Ores

Ores
Minerals vs Ores, Gangue, and Concentration Methods — Gravity Separation, Magnetic Separation, Froth Flotation, and Leaching
A mineral is a naturally occurring substance from which a metal may be extracted; an ore is a mineral from which the metal can be extracted economically and profitably. Gangue is the unwanted earthy material associated with the ore. Concentration (beneficiation) removes gangue. Hydraulic washing (gravity separation): based on density differences — lighter gangue is washed away by water (e.g., cassiterite SnO₂). Magnetic separation: magnetic ores (e.g., magnetite Fe₃O₄) are separated from non-magnetic gangue. Froth flotation: used for sulfide ores (e.g., ZnS, PbS) — collectors make ore particles hydrophobic, froth carries ore to surface. Leaching: chemical dissolution — cyanide leaching for Au/Ag: 4Au + 8CN⁻ + 2H₂O + O₂ → 4[Au(CN)₂]⁻ + 4OH⁻. Baeyer's process for bauxite: Al₂O₃ dissolves in hot NaOH; impurities (Fe₂O₃, SiO₂) remain as red mud.
Ores are concentrated by exploiting differences in physical properties (density, magnetic susceptibility, wettability) or by chemical leaching before extraction.
Part 2

🔥 Extraction: Calcination, Roasting, and Reduction

Pyrometallurgy
Calcination, Roasting, Smelting, and the Thermodynamics of Oxide Reduction
Calcination: heating ore below melting point in limited air (or absence of air). For carbonate/hydroxide ores. Examples: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂O, ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂. Roasting: heating ore in excess air to convert sulfides to oxides: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂; 2PbS + 3O₂ → 2PbO + 2SO₂. Roasting also removes volatile impurities (As₂O₃, Sb₂O₃). Smelting (reduction): oxide reduced to metal using a reducing agent. Carbon reduction (carbothermic): ZnO + C → Zn + CO; Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO. CO reduction: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (blast furnace). Hydrogen reduction: WO₃ + 3H₂ → W + 3H₂O (tungsten). Aluminothermic reduction: Cr₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Cr + Al₂O₃ (Goldschmidt thermite process). Self-reduction for sulfides: 2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂; Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂. Flux combines with gangue to form low-melting slag: acidic flux (SiO₂) for basic gangue; basic flux (CaO) for acidic gangue.
Calcination decomposes carbonates/hydroxides, roasting converts sulfides to oxides, and smelting reduces oxides to metals — the choice of reductant depends on thermodynamic feasibility.
Part 3

📊 The Ellingham Diagram

Thermodynamics
Gibbs Free Energy, Ellingham Diagram, Reducing Agents, and Limitations
The Ellingham diagram (H. J. T. Ellingham, 1944) plots ΔG° vs T for oxide formation (M + O₂ → MO₂) per mole O₂. Key features: (1) Most lines have positive slope (ΔS negative for gas-consuming reactions), except 2C + O₂ → 2CO which slopes down (ΔS positive). (2) Lower line = more stable oxide. (3) A metal can reduce the oxide of a metal whose line lies above it. (4) C is a good reducing agent above ~710°C (where C → CO line crosses FeO line). Aluminothermic reduction works because Al₂O₃ line is well below most other metal oxides. Limitations: (a) assumes pure reactants and products, (b) considers only oxide formation, (c) no kinetic information (only thermodynamic feasibility), (d) ignores slag formation or solution effects. Applications: predicting optimum reduction temperature, selecting reducing agents, designing extraction processes.
The Ellingham diagram is a thermodynamic roadmap for pyrometallurgy — it shows at a glance which reducing agent works for a given metal oxide at a given temperature.
Part 4

🧪 Refining and Purification of Metals

Purification
Distillation, Electrolytic Refining, Zone Refining, Mond's Process, and van Arkel-de Boer Method
Distillation: for volatile metals (Zn, Hg, Cd) — pure metal vaporizes, condenses; non-volatile impurities remain. Electrolytic refining: impure metal is anode, pure metal sheet is cathode, electrolyte contains metal salt. Impurities settle as anode mud (e.g., Cu refining: anode = impure Cu, cathode = pure Cu, electrolyte = CuSO₄/H₂SO₄; Ag, Au, Pt collect as anode slime). Zone refining: impurities are more soluble in molten than solid metal — a narrow molten zone moves along a rod, concentrating impurities at one end. Used for Si, Ge, Ga, B (semiconductors). Mond's process (carbonyl): for Ni — crude Ni + CO at 50°C forms volatile Ni(CO)₄, decomposes at 230°C to deposit pure Ni. van Arkel-de Boer process: for Ti, Zr, Hf — impure metal + I₂ forms volatile iodide (TiI₄), decomposes on hot filament at 1400°C to deposit pure metal. Chromatography: adsorption chromatography for rare earth separation. Blister copper (98.5% Cu) from Bessemer converter is electrolytically refined to 99.95% Cu.
Different refining methods exploit volatility, electrolysis, solubility differences, or volatility of compounds to achieve high-purity metals.
