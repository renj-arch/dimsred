Étale Cohomology — the Grothendieck Topology, ℓ-adic Cohomology & the Weil Conjectures

Étale cohomology is a cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over arbitrary fields, introduced by Grothendieck and his school. The key innovation is the étale topology: a Grothendieck topology on a scheme X where coverings are families {Ui → X} of étale morphisms (flat, unramified, finitely presented) that are jointly surjective. This replaces the Zariski topology with a much richer site that captures arithmetic information. The étale cohomology groups Hiét(X, F) = RiΓ(Xét, F) are the derived functors of global sections on the étale site. For torsion sheaves (especially ℤ/ℓnℤ), étale cohomology behaves like singular cohomology. The ℓ-adic cohomology Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is the limit over finite coefficients. The Weil conjectures — rationality, functional equation, and the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields — were proved using étale cohomology (Grothendieck for the first two, Deligne for the Riemann hypothesis). Étale cohomology also gives Poincaré duality in arbitrary characteristic, the cycle class map, and the comparison theorem with singular cohomology over ℂ.

"Étale cohomology is the right cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over fields of any characteristic."
— Alexander Grothendieck
Part 1

📏 The Étale Site and Étale Sheaves

Grothendieck Topology
The Étale Site Xét, Étale Sheaves, and the Kummer Sequence
A Grothendieck topology on a scheme X is given by declaring a family of morphisms {Ui → U} to be a covering if each Ui → U is étale (flat + unramified + locally of finite presentation) and ∪ im(Ui → U) = U. The category of étale schemes over X, equipped with these coverings, is the étale site Xét. A presheaf F on Xét (a contravariant functor from étale X-schemes to Ab) is a sheaf if for every étale covering {Ui → U}, the sequence 0 → F(U) → ∏ F(Ui) ⇉ ∏ F(Ui ×U Uj) is exact. The structure sheaf OX (regular functions) is a sheaf. The sheaf μℓn of ℓn-th roots of unity is defined by μℓn(U) = {f ∈ OU(U)× : fℓn = 1}. The Kummer sequence 1 → μℓn → 𝔾m → 𝔾m → 1 is exact on the étale site provided ℓ is invertible on X. Étale cohomology groups: Hiét(X, F) = RiΓ(Xét, F). Comparison with Zariski cohomology: H0ét(X, 𝔾m) = H0Zar(X, OX×), H1ét(X, 𝔾m) = Pic(X) (the Picard group), and H1ét(X, μn) = {line bundles L with L⊗n ≅ OX} (n-torsion line bundles). For a field k, H1ét(Spec k, μn) = k×/(k×)n (Kummer theory).
The étale site replaces the coarse Zariski topology with a finer Grothendieck topology that has enough points for cohomology to capture arithmetic and geometric invariants.
Part 2

🌍 ℓ-adic Cohomology

ℓ-adic
ℓ-adic Cohomology Groups, the Comparison Theorem, and Base Change
ℓ-adic cohomology is defined via inverse limits. For a prime ℓ invertible on X, set Hiét(X, ℤℓ) = lim← Hiét(X, ℤ/ℓnℤ) and Hiét(X, ℚℓ) = Hiét(X, ℤℓ) ⊗ℤℓ ℚℓ. For a smooth projective connected variety X of dimension n over a field k, the ℓ-adic cohomology groups are finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces for i = 0,…,2n, and vanish for i > 2n. The comparison theorem: for a variety X over ℂ, there is a canonical isomorphism Hiét(X, ℚℓ) ≅ HiBetti(X, ℚ) ⊗ℚ ℚℓ — linking étale cohomology to classical singular cohomology. The smooth base change theorem: for a proper smooth morphism f: X → S, the sheaves Rif* ℚℓ are local systems on S. The proper base change theorem: for a proper map f: X → S and a torsion sheaf F, the stalk (Rif*F)s ≅ Hi(Xs, F|Xs). The Gysin sequence: for a closed immersion i: Z → X of codimension c with complement j: U → X, there is a long exact sequence … → Hiét(U) → Hi+1_{Z}(X) → Hi+1(X) → … where Hi_{Z}(X) is cohomology with support in Z. The Leray spectral sequence for a morphism f: X → Y gives E2p,q = Hpét(Y, Rqf*F) ⇒ Hp+qét(X, F).
ℓ-adic cohomology provides a robust cohomology theory with finite-dimensional vector spaces, base change properties, and a comparison theorem linking arithmetic geometry to topology.
Part 3

🧮 Poincaré Duality and the Cycle Class Map

Duality
Poincaré Duality, Tate Twists, and the Cycle Class Map
Poincaré duality for étale cohomology: for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over a field k, the cup product pairing Hi(X, ℚℓ) × H2n−i(X, ℚℓ(n)) → H2n(X, ℚℓ(n)) ≅ ℚℓ is a perfect pairing of finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces. The Tate twist ℚℓ(1) = (lim← μℓn) ⊗ℤℓ ℚℓ — the dual of the ℓ-adic cyclotomic character of the absolute Galois group. ℚℓ(n) = ℚℓ(1)⊗n. For a smooth projective variety, H2n(X, ℚℓ(n)) ≅ ℚℓ canonically (the trace map). The cycle class map: for a closed subvariety Z ⊂ X of codimension c, there is a class cl(Z) ∈ H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c)) (the cohomology class of Z). This factors through the Chow ring: cl: CHc(X) → H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c)). The map is compatible with intersection products: cl(Z1 · Z2) = cl(Z1) ∪ cl(Z2). The Tate conjecture (still open in general) asserts that the ℚℓ-vector space spanned by cycle classes of algebraic cycles is the space of Galois invariants H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c))Gk — linking algebraic cycles to Galois representations. The Künneth formula: Hkét(X × Y, ℚℓ) ≅ ⊕i+j=k Hiét(X, ℚℓ) ⊗ Hjét(Y, ℚℓ).
Poincaré duality in étale cohomology, with Tate twists, mirrors classical topology while incorporating the Galois action; the cycle class map connects geometry (algebraic cycles) to cohomology.

🔥 The Weil Conjectures and Deligne's Proof

Weil
The Weil Conjectures, the Lefschetz Trace Formula, and Deligne's Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n over the finite field 𝔽q. The Weil zeta function is Z(X, t) = exp(Σk≥1 |X(𝔽qk)| tk/k). The Weil conjectures (proposed 1949, proved 1960–1974): (1) Rationality (Grothendieck, 1960): Z(X, t) ∈ ℚ(t) — it is a rational function. (2) Functional equation (Grothendieck): Z(1/qnt) = ± qnE/2 tE Z(t) where E = Σ(−1)i dim Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is the Euler characteristic. (3) Riemann hypothesis (Deligne, 1974): the eigenvalues of the geometric Frobenius Fr acting on Hiét(X, ℚℓ) are algebraic integers of absolute value qi/2. (4) The Betti numbers bi = dim Hiét(X, ℚℓ) give the degrees of the zeta function. The Lefschetz trace formula: |X(𝔽qk)| = Σ2ni=0 (−1)i Tr(Frk, Hiét(X, ℚℓ)). Grothendieck proved (1) and (2) via étale cohomology and the trace formula. Deligne's proof of (3) — the Riemann hypothesis — used the theory of pure and mixed sheaves (Weil II, 1980). Key tools: the hard Lefschetz theorem for étale cohomology (cup product with c1(L) for an ample line bundle L gives an isomorphism Hi → H2n−i(n−i)), and Deligne's "théorème de Lefschetz" on the existence of a universal Lefschetz pencil. The proof also relies on the Weil bound for the eigenvalues: |α| = qi/2 for each eigenvalue α of Fr on Hi. The Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields has profound consequences: the optimal bound for exponential sums (Kloosterman sums, etc.), the Weil bound for curves (|#C(𝔽q) − q − 1| ≤ 2g√q), and the Deligne bound for hyper-Kloosterman sums.
Deligne's proof of the Weil conjectures is one of the greatest achievements of 20th-century mathematics, cementing étale cohomology as the ultimate cohomology theory for arithmetic geometry.

Mixed Sheaves and Weights

The theory of mixed sheaves, developed by Deligne in Weil II, provides the categorical framework that makes the weight argument in the proof of the Riemann hypothesis work. A mixed ℓ‑adic sheaf on a variety X over a finite field 𝔽q is a complex of sheaves whose cohomology sheaves carry a weight filtration W• such that the graded pieces GrkW are pure of weight k. For a smooth projective variety, the constant sheaf ℚℓ is pure of weight 0, and its cohomology groups Hiét(X, ℚℓ) are pure of weight i. The crucial point is that the six operations (pushforward, pullback, tensor product, duality, nearby cycles, vanishing cycles) preserve the class of mixed sheaves and behave predictably with respect to weights: for a morphism f of relative dimension d, the shifted pushforward f![d] raises weights by at most d, while the shifted pullback f*[−d] lowers weights by at most d. This formalism allows one to reduce the study of eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hi to the case of pure sheaves, where the eigenvalues are algebraic numbers all of whose complex absolute values equal qi/2. The weight filtration also interacts with the monodromy weight filtration coming from the nearby cycles functor, giving a powerful tool for analysing degenerations of varieties. In practice, the existence of the weight filtration on the derived category of constructible ℓ‑adic sheaves is what makes the “purity implies mixed” principle work: any subquotient of a mixed sheaf is again mixed, and the weights of the pieces are bounded by the weights of the original sheaf. This is the categorical backbone of Deligne’s proof that the eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hi have absolute value qi/2.

The Weight Filtration and Purity

The weight filtration W• on a mixed ℓ‑adic complex K is an increasing filtration by subobjects in the derived category such that each graded piece GrkWK is pure of weight k. For a smooth projective variety X over 𝔽q, the constant sheaf ℚℓ is pure of weight 0, and the cohomology groups Hiét(X, ℚℓ) inherit a pure weight i structure. The purity theorem states that if X is smooth and proper, then Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is pure of weight i. The proof uses the hard Lefschetz theorem for ℓ‑adic cohomology together with the existence of a Lefschetz pencil and the fact that the nearby cycles functor preserves weights. In the mixed setting, the weight filtration is functorial: any morphism of mixed complexes strictly respects the filtration. This strictness is essential for the “weight argument” that bounds the eigenvalues of Frobenius. Concretely, if α is an eigenvalue of Frobenius on Hi, then α is an algebraic integer all of whose Galois conjugates have complex absolute value qi/2. The weight filtration also appears in the study of the monodromy operator N on the nearby cycles of a degeneration; the monodromy weight filtration centred at −1 coincides with the weight filtration shifted by the relative dimension. This compatibility is a key ingredient in the proof of the local monodromy theorem and in the construction of the ℓ‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2. For exam purposes, remember that purity is a property of smooth proper varieties, while mixedness is the general behaviour for arbitrary varieties and for pushforwards along non‑proper maps.

Weight Argument in Deligne's Proof
Let X be a smooth projective curve of genus g over 𝔽q. Using the weight filtration on H1ét(X, ℚℓ), explain why every eigenvalue α of the geometric Frobenius acting on H1 satisfies |α| = q1/2.
The constant sheaf ℚℓ on X is pure of weight 0. Because X is smooth and proper of dimension 1, the cohomology group H1ét(X, ℚℓ) is pure of weight 1 by the purity theorem. Purity of weight 1 means that for any eigenvalue α of Frobenius on this ℚℓ-vector space, all Galois conjugates of α have complex absolute value q1/2. The weight filtration on the mixed derived category guarantees that no subquotient can have weight different from 1, so there is no room for eigenvalues of any other absolute value. Hence |α| = q1/2 for every eigenvalue α.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the weight of a cohomology group, recall the rule: Hi of a smooth proper variety is pure of weight i. For non‑proper or singular varieties, the weight filtration gives a range: weights lie between i and 2i. Use this to eliminate answer choices that violate the bounds.

The Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem in Étale Cohomology

The Lefschetz hyperplane theorem asserts that for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over an algebraically closed field and a smooth ample divisor D ⊂ X, the restriction map Hiét(X, ℚℓ) → Hiét(D, ℚℓ) is an isomorphism for i < n−1 and injective for i = n−1. In the ℓ‑adic setting the proof proceeds by reducing to the case of a Lefschetz pencil, using the proper and smooth base change theorems to compare the cohomology of the total space with that of the fibres, and then applying the hard Lefschetz theorem for the relatively ample line bundle on the total space. The key technical input is the existence of a universal Lefschetz pencil, which Deligne constructs in Weil II. The theorem has several important consequences: it implies that the primitive cohomology Pn−1(X) = ker(Hn−1(X) → Hn+1(X)) injects into Hn−1(D), and it provides the inductive step for proving the hard Lefschetz theorem itself. Over finite fields, the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem together with the weight filtration yields the “weak Lefschetz” statement that the eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hi(X) for i < n−1 are already determined by the hyperplane section. This is used in the proof of the Weil conjectures to reduce the Riemann hypothesis for X to that for a curve. For exam questions, remember that the theorem holds for any ℓ ≠ char(k) and that the same statement is true for constructible sheaves that are lisse on a dense open subset, provided one works with the appropriate perverse t‑structure.

Comparison Theorems: Crystalline and de Rham Cohomology

Étale cohomology is not the only Weil cohomology theory; for varieties over fields of characteristic p there are also crystalline cohomology and de Rham cohomology. The comparison theorems relate these theories to ℓ‑adic cohomology and to each other. For a smooth proper variety X over a perfect field k of characteristic p, the crystalline cohomology Hicrys(X/W(k)) is a finitely generated W(k)-module equipped with a Frobenius-semilinear endomorphism φ. After tensoring with ℚp one obtains a φ‑module that, via the Fontaine‑Messing or Faltings comparison isomorphism, is canonically isomorphic to Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) as a Galois representation, where K is the fraction field of W(k). On the de Rham side, for a smooth proper variety over a p‑adic field K, the p‑adic Hodge theory comparison theorem of Faltings (and later refined by Tsuji, Niziol, Scholze) gives a Galois‑equivariant isomorphism Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) ⊗ℚp BdR ≅ HidR(X/K) ⊗K BdR, where BdR is Fontaine’s period ring. This isomorphism respects the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology and the Galois action on étale cohomology. The upshot is that the ℓ‑adic, crystalline, and de Rham cohomology groups carry the same numerical invariants (Betti numbers, Hodge numbers) and that the Frobenius eigenvalues on crystalline cohomology coincide with the ℓ‑adic eigenvalues after suitable normalisation. For competitive examinations, the key facts to retain are: (1) the comparison isomorphisms exist for smooth proper varieties; (2) they are compatible with cup products, cycle class maps, and Poincaré duality; (3) they allow one to transfer results proved in one cohomology theory (e.g., the hard Lefschetz theorem in de Rham cohomology via Hodge theory) to the ℓ‑adic setting.

Applications to Galois Representations and the Langlands Program

ℓ‑adic cohomology furnishes a rich supply of continuous Galois representations. For a smooth projective variety X over a number field K, the groups Hiét(XK̄, ℚℓ) are finite‑dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces equipped with a continuous action of the absolute Galois group GK. These representations are unramified outside a finite set of primes, and at a prime v of good reduction the Frobenius eigenvalues satisfy the Weil bounds. The Tate conjecture predicts that the subspace of Galois‑invariant classes in H2c(X, ℚℓ(c)) is spanned by algebraic cycles; this is a motivic interpretation of the Galois representation. In the Langlands program, the ℓ‑adic representations coming from the cohomology of Shimura varieties are expected to realise the local and global Langlands correspondences. For example, the cohomology of the modular curve Y(N) carries an action of GL2(𝔸f) × Gℚ, and the resulting Galois representations are precisely those attached to modular forms by Eichler‑Shimura and Deligne. The work of Harris‑Taylor, Henniart, and Scholze on the local Langlands correspondence for GLn uses the ℓ‑adic cohomology of Lubin‑Tate towers and Rapoport‑Zink spaces. In the geometric Langlands program, the ℓ‑adic sheaves on the moduli stack of G‑bundles on a curve play the role of automorphic forms. For exam purposes, the essential points are: (1) ℓ‑adic cohomology provides a functorial construction of Galois representations; (2) these representations are pure of weight i (after Tate twist) and satisfy the expected local‑global compatibility; (3) the Langlands correspondence predicts a bijection between certain Galois representations and automorphic representations, and étale cohomology of Shimura varieties is the main known source of such Galois representations.

The Six Operations and the Formalism of Étale Cohomology

The modern language of étale cohomology is organised around Grothendieck's six operations: for a morphism f : X → Y of schemes, one has the functors f*, f*, f!, f!, ⊗, and Hom (or RHom) acting on derived categories of étale sheaves. The pair (f*, f*) is the standard adjunction for sheaves; (f!, f!) is the adjunction for compactly supported cohomology. When f is proper, f! = f*; when f is an open immersion, f! is extension by zero. The functor f! is the right adjoint to f! and encodes duality: for a smooth morphism of relative dimension d, f!(−) ≅ f*(−) ⊗ ℚℓ(−d)[−2d]. The tensor product ⊗ and internal Hom make the derived category a symmetric monoidal category. The projection formula f!(K ⊗ f*L) ≅ f!K ⊗ L and the base change isomorphism for Cartesian squares are the computational engines of the theory. For a Cartesian square with f proper and g smooth, the proper base change theorem gives g*f* ≅ f'*g'*; for f separated of finite type, the smooth base change theorem gives g*f! ≅ f'!g'*. These isomorphisms are canonical and compatible with composition. The six operations formalism, fully developed by Deligne in SGA 4½ and SGA 5, and later extended to ℓ-adic sheaves by Deligne in La conjecture de Weil II, allows one to state and prove the Verdier duality isomorphism RHom(K, f!L) ≅ RHom(f!K, L) and the Poincaré duality for smooth X of dimension n: DX(K) := RHom(K, ℚℓ(−n)[−2n]) is an involutive anti-equivalence. For competitive examinations, the key operational rules are: (1) f! preserves constructibility; (2) f* and f! preserve weights (with shifts); (3) the trace map f!ℚℓ → ℚℓ for proper f induces the pushforward on cohomology; (4) the six operations commute with the formation of stalks and with the Galois action on geometric points.

Perverse Sheaves, Intersection Cohomology, and the Decomposition Theorem

On a singular variety X, the constant sheaf ℚℓ does not satisfy Poincaré duality. The solution, developed by Beilinson, Bernstein, Deligne, and Gabber (BBD) in Faisceaux pervers (1982), is the theory of perverse sheaves. The derived category Dbc(X, ℚℓ) of constructible complexes carries a perverse t-structure whose heart Perv(X) is an abelian category. The perverse cohomology functors pℋi extract the perverse cohomology sheaves. For a stratification X = ∪ Xα into smooth locally closed subvarieties, a complex K is perverse iff for all i and all inclusions j : Xα X, the stalk cohomology satisfies dim Supp ℋi(j*K) ≤ −i and dim Supp ℋi(j!K) ≤ i. The intersection cohomology complex ICX is the unique (up to isomorphism) perverse sheaf on X whose restriction to the smooth locus is ℚℓ[dim X] and which has no non-zero subobjects or quotients supported on the singular locus. It satisfies Verdier self-duality: DX(ICX) ≅ ICX. The Decomposition Theorem (BBD, 1982) states that for a proper map f : X → Y with X smooth, f*ℚℓ[dim X] ≅ ⊕i pℋi(f*ℚℓ[dim X])[−i], and each perverse cohomology sheaf is a direct sum of IC complexes of subvarieties of Y tensored with local systems. This implies that the cohomology of X decomposes into intersection cohomology groups of subvarieties of Y. The theorem is the geometric incarnation of the hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge-Riemann bilinear relations. For exam purposes, the essential points are: (1) perverse sheaves form an abelian category stable under the six operations (with shifts); (2) ICX restores Poincaré duality on singular varieties; (3) the Decomposition Theorem implies that the pushforward of a pure complex is a direct sum of pure complexes; (4) the weights on perverse sheaves are defined by the weights on their perverse cohomology sheaves, and Deligne's purity theorem extends to perverse sheaves on smooth varieties.

Worked Example: Decomposition Theorem for a Resolution of Singularities
Let f : X̃ → X be a resolution of singularities of a projective surface X with an isolated singularity at x ∈ X. Assume the exceptional fibre E = f−1(x) is a union of smooth rational curves. Describe the decomposition of f*ℚℓ[2] in the derived category.
Step 1: X̃ is smooth of dimension 2, so ℚℓ[2] is perverse on X̃. By the Decomposition Theorem, f*ℚℓ[2] ≅ ⊕i pℋi(f*ℚℓ[2])[−i]. Step 2: Since f is an isomorphism over U = X {x}, the restriction of f*ℚℓ[2] to U is ℚℓ[2], which is perverse. Hence pℋ0(f*ℚℓ[2])|U ≅ ℚℓ[2] and pℋi = 0 for i ≠ 0 on U. Step 3: The perverse sheaf pℋ0 is an intermediate extension of its restriction to U, so pℋ0 ≅ ICX. Step 4: The higher perverse cohomology sheaves are supported on {x}. For a surface, the only possible non-zero pℋi are i = −1, 0, 1. pℋ−1 and pℋ1 are skyscraper sheaves at x. By Verdier duality, pℋ−1 ≅ D(pℋ1). Step 5: The stalk cohomology at x gives Hk(f*ℚℓ[2])x ≅ Hk+2(E, ℚℓ). Since E is a union of rational curves, H0(E) = ℚℓ, H1(E) = 0, H2(E) = ℚℓr where r is the number of irreducible components. Thus pℋ−1x = 0, pℋ0x = ℚℓ, pℋ1x = ℚℓr−1. Conclusion: f*ℚℓ[2] ≅ ICX ⊕ ℚℓr−1[−1] (skyscraper at x).
Exam Strategy: Recognising Perverse Sheaves in Questions
When a question mentions "intersection cohomology", "IC complex", or "Decomposition Theorem", immediately identify the stratification and the smooth locus. The perverse t-structure conditions (stalk and costalk vanishing) are the standard way to verify whether a given complex is perverse. For a resolution f : X̃ → X, the direct image f*ℚℓ[dim X̃] always contains ICX as a direct summand; the other summands are skyscraper perverse sheaves supported on the singular locus. Memorise the stalk formula: Hk(ICX)x = 0 for k < −dim X and for k > −1 (middle perversity). This is a frequent source of true/false questions.

The Grothendieck-Lefschetz Trace Formula and Point Counting

The Grothendieck-Lefschetz trace formula is the bridge between étale cohomology and arithmetic geometry. Let X be a separated scheme of finite type over a finite field Fq, and let F : X → X be the geometric Frobenius endomorphism (acting as x ↦ xq on coordinates). For any constructible complex K ∈ Dbc(X, ℚℓ) equipped with an isomorphism φ : F*K ≅ K, the trace formula states: ∑x ∈ X(Fq) Tr(φx | Kx) = ∑i (−1)i Tr(F | Hic(X̄, K)). When K = ℚℓ, the left side is simply the number of Fq-rational points |X(Fq)|. The formula is proved by reducing to the case where X is a curve via the Leray spectral sequence for a finite morphism to A1, and then using the cohomological interpretation of the Lefschetz fixed point formula for the Frobenius correspondence in X × X. The Weil conjectures for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n follow by applying the trace formula to the constant sheaf and using Poincaré duality: the zeta function Z(X, t) = exp(∑m≥1 |X(Fqm)| tm/m) becomes a rational function ∏i=02n det(1 − Ft | Hi(X̄, ℚℓ))(−1)i+1. The functional equation Z(X, 1/(qnt)) = ± qnχ/2 tχ Z(X, t) follows from Poincaré duality and the fact that F acts on H2n as multiplication by qn. The Riemann hypothesis (Deligne) states that the eigenvalues of F on Hi are algebraic integers all of whose complex conjugates have absolute value qi/2. For a smooth projective curve C of genus g, the trace formula gives |C(Fq)| = q + 1 − ∑j=12g αj where |αj| = √q. This is the Hasse-Weil bound. For higher-dimensional varieties, the trace formula combined with the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem and the hard Lefschetz theorem gives bounds on the number of rational points in terms of Betti numbers. In competitive examinations, the trace formula appears in three guises: (1) direct computation of |X(Fq)| for simple varieties (projective space, Grassmannians, hypersurfaces); (2) proving rationality of the zeta function for smooth projective varieties; (3) deducing the Weil bounds from the weight formalism. The key computational tool is the Lefschetz trace formula for correspondences: for a correspondence Γ ⊂ X × Y, the induced map on cohomology has trace equal to the intersection number Γ · ΔY in X × Y.

The Étale Fundamental Group and Anabelian Geometry

The étale fundamental group π1ét(X, x̄) classifies finite étale covers of a connected scheme X with geometric base point x̄. It is the automorphism group of the fibre functor Fx̄ : FÉtX → FinSet from the category of finite étale covers to finite sets. As a profinite group, π1ét(X, x̄) = lim← Aut(Y/X) over all Galois covers Y → X. For a variety over an algebraically closed field, π1ét is the profinite completion of the topological fundamental group. Over a non-algebraically closed field k, there is an exact sequence 1 → π1ét(X̄, x̄) → π1ét(X, x̄) → Gal(k̄/k) → 1, where X̄ = X ×k k̄. This sequence splits if X has a k-rational point. The Grothendieck anabelian conjectures (1983) assert that for certain hyperbolic curves over number fields or p-adic fields, the étale fundamental group (with its outer Galois action) determines the curve up to isomorphism. The section conjecture predicts that sections of the exact sequence above correspond to rational points. Major results: (1) Neukirch-Uchida (1969): number fields are determined by their absolute Galois groups; (2) Mochizuki (1996): hyperbolic curves over p-adic fields are determined by their étale fundamental groups; (3) Tamagawa (1997): the section conjecture holds for certain curves over p-adic fields; (4) Esnault-Kerz (2014): the section conjecture fails in general over number fields. The pro-ℓ completion π1(ℓ) is the maximal pro-ℓ quotient; for a smooth curve over an algebraically closed field of characteristic ≠ ℓ, π1(ℓ) is a free pro-ℓ group on 2g generators (where g is the genus). The tame fundamental group π1tame classifies covers tamely ramified along a divisor; it fits into an exact sequence involving the inertia groups. For exam purposes, the essential facts are: (1) π1ét(A1k̄) = 1 (Riemann existence theorem); (2) π1ét(Gm,k̄) = ∏ℓ≠p ℤℓ (tame) × ℤp (wild) in characteristic p; (3) the homotopy exact sequence for a smooth proper morphism with geometrically connected fibres; (4) the comparison isomorphism π1ét(Xℂ) ≅ π1top(X(ℂ))̂ for a variety over ℂ.

Nearby Cycles, Vanishing Cycles, and the Weight Monodromy Conjecture

Let f : X → S be a morphism with S the spectrum of a henselian discrete valuation ring, generic point η and special point s. The nearby cycles functor Ψf : Db(Xη, ℚℓ) → Db(Xs, ℚℓ) and the vanishing cycles functor Φf : Db(Xη, ℚℓ) → Db(Xs, ℚℓ) capture the degeneration of cohomology. They fit into a distinguished triangle Ψf → i*Rj* → Φf →, where j : Xη X and i : Xs X. The nearby cycles carry a monodromy action of the inertia group I ⊂ Gal(η̄/η); the logarithm of the unipotent part gives the monodromy operator N : Ψf → Ψf(−1)[−2]. The weight monodromy conjecture (Deligne, 1974) predicts that for a proper smooth morphism over the generic point, the weight filtration on the nearby cycles coincides with the monodromy filtration shifted by the dimension. Equivalently, the graded pieces GrWk Ψf are pure of weight k, and N induces isomorphisms Nj : GrWw+j ≅ GrWw−j(−j). This conjecture is known in many cases: (1) for semistable reduction (Rapoport-Zink, 1982; Nakayama, 1997); (2) for abelian varieties (Grothendieck's semistable reduction theorem); (3) for curves (Deligne); (4) in equal characteristic (Gabber, 1994). The Clemens-Schmid exact sequence relates the cohomology of the generic fibre, the nearby cycles, and the vanishing cycles: → Hi(Xs, Ψf) → Hi(Xs, Φf) → Hi+1(Xs, Ψf) →. The local monodromy theorem (Grothendieck, 1970) states that the action of inertia on ℓ-adic cohomology is quasi-unipotent: some open subgroup acts unipotently. This is the ℓ-adic analogue of the fact that monodromy around a singular fibre in a complex degeneration is quasi-unipotent. For competitive examinations, the key points are: (1) Ψf and Φf are the étale analogues of the topological nearby/vanishing cycles; (2) the monodromy operator N satisfies N2 = 0 for a degeneration of curves, and Nn+1 = 0 for a degeneration of n-folds; (3) the weight monodromy conjecture implies the semistable reduction conjecture; (4) the nearby cycles functor commutes with the six operations (with appropriate shifts); (5) for a Lefschetz pencil, the vanishing cycles are concentrated in the middle degree and are pure of weight n.

  • Confusing the six operations with their topological analogues: in étale cohomology, f! exists for any separated finite type morphism, not just proper ones; f! is not simply f* with a twist unless f is smooth.
  • Forgetting that perverse sheaves live in the derived category: a perverse sheaf is a complex, not a sheaf; its cohomology sheaves are ordinary constructible sheaves. The perverse t-structure is not the standard t-structure.
  • Misapplying the trace formula to non-constructible complexes: the formula requires K to be constructible and equipped with a Frobenius isomorphism; it fails for arbitrary complexes.
  • Assuming π1ét classifies all covers: it classifies only finite étale covers. Infinite covers, ramified covers, and non-Galois covers require the full Galois category formalism or the tame fundamental group.
  • Confusing the monodromy operator N with the Frobenius action: N comes from the inertia group action on nearby cycles; Frobenius acts on the cohomology of the special fibre. They are distinct operators, though related by the weight monodromy conjecture.
  • Overlooking the shift in the perverse t-structure for the Decomposition Theorem: the theorem applies to f*ℚℓ[dim X], not to f*ℚℓ. The shift by the dimension is essential for the summands to be perverse.
  • Assuming the weight monodromy conjecture is a theorem in general: it remains open for arbitrary degenerations in mixed characteristic; only special cases (semistable, abelian varieties, curves, equal characteristic) are proved.

The Pro-étale Site and Perfectoid Spaces

The pro-étale site, introduced by Bhatt and Scholze, refines the classical étale site to capture infinite étale covers that are essential for p-adic Hodge theory. Objects are weakly étale morphisms — flat morphisms whose diagonal is also flat — and covers are jointly surjective families of weakly étale maps. This site admits a basis of affinoid perfectoid spaces, which are the analytic counterparts of perfectoid algebras: complete Tate algebras over a perfectoid field whose Frobenius is surjective on the tilt. The tilt equivalence, a cornerstone of perfectoid geometry, identifies the category of perfectoid spaces over a perfectoid field of characteristic 0 with that over its tilt of characteristic p, preserving étale cohomology with ℤ/ℓ coefficients for ℓ ≠ p. Consequently, the pro-étale cohomology of a smooth rigid analytic variety over a p-adic field can be computed via the tilt, reducing many p-adic comparison problems to equal characteristic where tools like the Artin-Schreier sequence and the Cartier isomorphism are available. The almost mathematics framework of Faltings, reformulated in the pro-étale setting, yields the almost purity theorem: the integral structure sheaf on the pro-étale site is almost coherent, and the derived completion of the étale cohomology with ℤ_p coefficients recovers the p-adic Hodge theoretic period rings A_inf, B_dR, B_cris. This underpins the p-adic comparison isomorphisms of Scholze: for a proper smooth variety X over a p-adic field, there are functorial, Galois-equivariant isomorphisms H^i_et(X_C, ℤ_p) ⊗ B_dR ≅ H^i_dR(X) ⊗ B_dR and similarly for B_cris, where C is the completion of an algebraic closure. The pro-étale site also provides a natural home for the sheaf of p-adic periods Oℬ+ and its graded pieces, enabling the construction of the Hodge-Tate spectral sequence degenerating at E2. For examiners, the key points are: (1) the pro-étale site is strictly larger than the étale site but has the same cohomology for torsion coefficients prime to p; (2) perfectoid spaces and the tilt equivalence are the geometric engine behind the p-adic comparison theorems; (3) almost mathematics replaces exact sequences by almost exact sequences, where kernels and cokernels are killed by the maximal ideal of the valuation ring; (4) the pro-étale cohomology of a perfectoid space is concentrated in degree 0 for the structure sheaf, a manifestation of the almost vanishing of higher cohomology.

Étale Cohomology of Logarithmic Schemes

Logarithmic geometry, developed by Kato and Fontaine-Illusie, equips schemes with a log structure — a sheaf of monoids M together with a map to the multiplicative monoid O_X — to encode degenerations and boundary divisors. The log étale topology is generated by morphisms that are étale on the underlying schemes and induce isomorphisms on the associated log structures after pulling back. This topology is finer than the classical étale topology but coarser than the Kummer étale topology used in the study of toroidal embeddings. Kato's log purity theorem states that for a log smooth morphism f: X → Y of fine saturated log schemes, the log étale cohomology sheaves R^q f_* ℤ/ℓ are locally constant and constructible, and the log étale fundamental group classifies log étale covers. The log crystalline cohomology of a log scheme over a perfect field of characteristic p computes the log de Rham cohomology via the Hyodo-Kato isomorphism, which relates log crystalline cohomology to the log de Rham cohomology of a lift to characteristic 0, equipped with a monodromy operator N. In the semistable reduction setting, the log étale cohomology of the generic fibre with ℚ_ℓ coefficients carries a monodromy filtration that matches the weight filtration on the nearby cycles, providing a geometric proof of the weight monodromy conjecture for semistable degenerations. The log étale site also admits a six operations formalism, with f_! defined for log proper morphisms, and a log version of the Poincaré duality holds for log smooth proper morphisms. For competitive examinations, the essential facts are: (1) a log structure on a scheme X over a DVR with special fibre a divisor D is given by the inclusion O_X {0} → O_X on the complement of D; (2) the log étale cohomology of a log smooth proper variety over a separably closed field satisfies Poincaré duality with a twist by the log canonical sheaf; (3) the log crystalline cohomology of a semistable degeneration computes the nearby cycles and the monodromy operator; (4) the log purity theorem ensures that the log étale cohomology of a log smooth pair is as well-behaved as the étale cohomology of a smooth variety.

Technique: Computing Log Étale Cohomology of a Nodal Curve
Let X be a proper nodal curve over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic ≠ ℓ, with a single node. Equip X with the log structure associated to the divisor consisting of the node. Compute the log étale cohomology groups H^i_loget(X, ℤ/ℓ) for i = 0,1,2 and compare with the classical étale cohomology of the normalization.
The normalization ν: X̃ → X is a smooth proper curve of genus g̃ = g - 1, where g is the arithmetic genus of X. The log structure on X makes the node a log smooth point. By Kato's log purity, the log étale cohomology of X coincides with the étale cohomology of the open complement U = X {node} with compact support, i.e., H^i_loget(X, ℤ/ℓ) ≅ H^i_c(U, ℤ/ℓ). Since U is the disjoint union of X̃ minus two points, we have H0_c(U) = 0, H1_c(U) ≅ H1(X̃, ℤ/ℓ) ⊕ ℤ/ℓ (the extra summand comes from the two missing points), and H2_c(U) ≅ ℤ/ℓ. Thus H0_loget = 0, H1_loget ≅ (ℤ/ℓ)2g-2 ⊕ ℤ/ℓ, H2_loget ≅ ℤ/ℓ. Classical étale cohomology of X gives H0_et = ℤ/ℓ, H1_et ≅ (ℤ/ℓ)2g-1, H2_et = ℤ/ℓ. The log étale cohomology loses the H0 because the log structure forces the node to be a boundary point, and the H1 gains a summand reflecting the two branches at the node.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for a comparison between classical and log étale cohomology, first identify the log structure: it is usually given by a divisor with normal crossings. Then recall that log étale cohomology of a log smooth proper pair (X, D) is isomorphic to the compactly supported étale cohomology of the complement X D. Use the long exact sequence of the pair to relate the two. Memorize the log purity statement: for a log smooth morphism, the higher direct images of constant torsion sheaves are locally constant. This is the log analogue of the smooth base change theorem and is frequently tested.

The Étale Cohomology of Shimura Varieties and the Langlands-Kottwitz Method

Shimura varieties are higher-dimensional analogues of modular curves, attached to a reductive group G over ℚ and a conjugacy class of cocharacters satisfying Deligne's axioms. They admit canonical models over number fields (the reflex fields) and their étale cohomology carries a rich action of the Hecke algebra at primes of good reduction and of the absolute Galois group of the reflex field. The Langlands-Kottwitz method expresses the number of points of a Shimura variety over a finite field in terms of stable orbital integrals on the group G and its endoscopic groups. Concretely, for a Shimura variety Sh_K(G, X) of Hodge type with good reduction at a prime p, the trace of Frobenius on the compactly supported ℓ-adic cohomology (ℓ ≠ p) is given by a sum over elliptic endoscopic groups H of G of stable orbital integrals on H multiplied by transfer factors. This formula, proved by Kottwitz for PEL-type Shimura varieties and extended by Shin and others to abelian type, realizes the cohomology as a virtual representation of G(𝔸_f) × Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) that decomposes according to the local Langlands correspondence. The cohomology in the middle degree is expected to contain the automorphic representations of G that contribute to the discrete spectrum, while the other degrees are accounted for by Eisenstein cohomology and the boundary contributions in the Borel-Serre compactification. The Mantovan formula gives a precise description of the ℓ-adic cohomology of the Rapoport-Zink space, which is the p-adic uniformization of the Shimura variety at a prime of bad reduction, in terms of representations of the p-adic group G(ℚ_p) and the Weil group of the local field. For examiners, the key points are: (1) Shimura varieties provide geometric realizations of the Langlands correspondence for reductive groups over ℚ; (2) the Langlands-Kottwitz point-counting formula is the primary tool for computing the Frobenius trace on étale cohomology; (3) the cohomology decomposes as a direct sum over automorphic representations π of G(𝔸_f) of π ⊗ r_π, where r_π is the associated Galois representation; (4) the endoscopic classification of Arthur and Mok for symplectic and orthogonal groups, and of Kaletha-Minguez-Shin-White for general groups, gives a precise description of the discrete automorphic spectrum in terms of endoscopic data.

The Étale Cohomology of Moduli Stacks of Curves

The moduli stack M_g of smooth curves of genus g over a field k is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack of dimension 3g-3. Its étale cohomology with ℚ_ℓ coefficients (ℓ ≠ char k) has been a central object of study since the work of Harer, Mumford, and Madsen-Weiss. Harer's stability theorem states that the rational cohomology of the topological moduli space M_g(ℂ) stabilizes as g → ∞: the map H^i(M_g, ℚ) → H^i(Mg+1, ℚ) is an isomorphism for i ≤ 2g/3. Madsen and Weiss proved the Mumford conjecture, identifying the stable cohomology ring with the polynomial ring ℚ[κ1, κ2, …] on the Miller-Morita-Mumford classes κ_i. In the ℓ-adic setting, the étale cohomology of M_g over an algebraically closed field satisfies the same stability range, and the tautological ring generated by the κ classes and the boundary classes from the Deligne-Mumford compactification M̄_g injects into cohomology. The boundary stratification of M̄_g by dual graphs gives a spectral sequence computing the cohomology of M_g from the cohomology of products of Mg',n for smaller genera. The work of Petersen and Tommasi extends the stability to the level of the mapping class group with twisted coefficients. For the compactly supported cohomology, the Poincaré duality on the orbifold M_g relates H^i_c to H6g-6-i. The Euler characteristic of M_g, computed by Harer-Zagier, is given by ζ(1-2g) up to a sign. For competitive examinations, the essential facts are: (1) M_g is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack, so its étale cohomology is defined via the lisse-étale site and coincides with the cohomology of the coarse moduli space for torsion coefficients prime to the characteristic; (2) Harer stability holds for ℓ-adic cohomology with the same range; (3) the tautological classes κ_i are the pushforwards of powers of the relative dualizing sheaf on the universal curve; (4) the boundary of M̄_g is a normal crossing divisor, and the weight spectral sequence of Rapoport-Zink computes the mixed Hodge structure (or weight filtration) on the cohomology of M_g.

The Étale Cohomology of Drinfeld Modular Varieties and the Local Langlands Correspondence

Drinfeld modular varieties are the function field analogues of Shimura varieties, attached to GL_n over a global function field F_q(t). They parametrize Drinfeld modules of rank n with level structure, and their étale cohomology realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over a local function field F_q((t)). The Drinfeld upper half-space Ω^n = ℙn-1 ∪H H, where H runs over F_q-rational hyperplanes, is a rigid analytic space over F_q((t)) whose étale cohomology with ℚ_ℓ coefficients (ℓ ≠ p) carries an action of GL_n(F_q((t))) × WF_q((t)) × D^×, where D is the central division algebra of invariant 1/n. The cohomology of Ω^n is concentrated in degree n-1 and decomposes as a direct sum over irreducible supercuspidal representations π of GL_n(F_q((t))) of π ⊗ σ(π) ⊗ π^∨, where σ(π) is the corresponding representation of the Weil group under the local Langlands correspondence, and π^∨ is the contragredient representation of D^×. This is the function field version of the Harris-Taylor theorem for GL_n over p-adic fields. The global Drinfeld modular varieties provide a geometric realization of the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n over F_q(t): the étale cohomology of the compactified Drinfeld modular variety decomposes according to cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n(𝔸F_q(t)), and the associated Galois representations are constructed from the ℓ-adic cohomology. The work of Lafforgue proves the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields using the cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas, which is closely related to the Drinfeld modular varieties. For examiners, the key points are: (1) Drinfeld modular varieties are the function field analogues of Shimura varieties for GL_n; (2) the étale cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half-space realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over a local function field; (3) the cohomology is concentrated in middle degree n-1 and is a direct sum of tensor products of representations of GL_n, the Weil group, and the division algebra; (4) the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields is proved via the cohomology of shtukas, which are modifications of vector bundles on curves, and the Drinfeld modular varieties appear as local models of the shtuka space.

  • Assuming log étale cohomology is the same as étale cohomology of the complement: it is isomorphic to compactly supported cohomology of the complement, not the ordinary cohomology.
  • Misapplying the Langlands-Kottwitz formula to Shimura varieties of non-PEL type without checking the endoscopic transfer factors; the formula requires a careful choice of test functions and the fundamental lemma.
  • Overlooking the stacky nature of M_g: the étale cohomology of the stack differs from that of the coarse moduli space by the contribution of the inertia groups at points with automorphisms.
  • Thinking the Drinfeld upper half-space is a scheme: it is a rigid analytic space, and its étale cohomology is defined via the étale site of rigid analytic varieties (or the pro-étale site).
  • Neglecting the weight filtration on the cohomology of M_g: the boundary stratification induces a mixed Hodge structure (or weight filtration) that is essential for understanding the tautological ring.
  • Confusing the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over p-adic fields with that over function fields: the geometric realizations differ (Lubin-Tate tower vs. Drinfeld upper half-space) but the formal statements are analogous.

The Étale Cohomology of Affine Grassmannians and the Geometric Satake Correspondence

The affine Grassmannian Gr_G = G((t))/G[[t]] for a split reductive group G over a field k is an ind-scheme whose G[[t]]-orbits are indexed by dominant coweights λ of G. The closure Gr_λ of the orbit Gr_λ0 is a projective variety of dimension ⟨2ρ, λ⟩, where ρ is the half-sum of positive roots. The geometric Satake equivalence, established by Ginzburg, Mirković–Vilonen, and independently by Lusztig, constructs a tensor equivalence between the category PG[[t]](Gr_G, ℚ_ℓ) of G[[t]]-equivariant perverse sheaves on Gr_G (for the étale topology, with ℓ invertible in k) and the category Rep(Ĝ) of finite-dimensional representations of the Langlands dual group Ĝ over ℚ_ℓ. The fiber functor is given by the global cohomology H^*(Gr_G, -), which is concentrated in even degrees and carries a natural weight filtration. The convolution product * on perverse sheaves, defined via the diagram Gr_G × Gr_G ← G[[t]] (Gr_G × Gr_G) → Gr_G, corresponds to the tensor product of representations. The intersection cohomology complex IC_λ of the Schubert variety Gr_λ corresponds to the irreducible representation V_λ of highest weight λ. The stalks of IC_λ at points of Gr_μ are given by the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials for the affine Weyl group, evaluated at 1. For G = GL_n, the affine Grassmannian parametrizes lattices in k((t))^n, and the geometric Satake correspondence recovers the classical Schur–Weyl duality. The étale cohomology of Gr_G with coefficients in IC_λ computes the space of invariants V_λĜ and, more generally, the weight spaces of V_λ. This construction is the geometric heart of the local Langlands correspondence for unramified representations: the spherical Hecke algebra H(G((t))//G[[t]], ℚ_ℓ) acts on the cohomology of Gr_G and is identified with the representation ring of Ĝ via the Satake isomorphism. For examiners, the essential points are: (1) the affine Grassmannian is an ind-projective ind-scheme stratified by G[[t]]-orbits indexed by dominant coweights; (2) the geometric Satake equivalence is a tensor equivalence between G[[t]]-equivariant perverse sheaves on Gr_G and representations of Ĝ; (3) the intersection cohomology of Schubert varieties realizes irreducible representations; (4) the convolution product corresponds to tensor product; (5) the spherical Hecke algebra acts via convolution and is isomorphic to the representation ring of Ĝ.

The Étale Cohomology of Lubin-Tate Spaces and the Local Langlands Correspondence over p-adic Fields

The Lubin–Tate tower {LT_n} for GL_n over a p-adic field F with residue field 𝔽_q is a projective system of rigid analytic spaces over the completion F̂ of the maximal unramified extension of F. The space LT_n parametrizes deformations of a fixed formal O_F-module of height n over 𝔽_q together with a Drinfeld level structure of level n. The group GL_n(F) × D^× × W_F acts on the tower, where D is the central division algebra over F of invariant 1/n and W_F is the Weil group of F. The étale cohomology H_c^*(LT_n, ℚ_ℓ) (for ℓ ≠ p) with compact support carries a smooth action of this triple product. The Harris–Taylor theorem (and independently Henniart) identifies the decomposition of this cohomology: it is concentrated in degree n-1 and decomposes as a direct sum over irreducible supercuspidal representations π of GL_n(F) of π ⊗ σ(π) ⊗ π^∨, where σ(π) is the n-dimensional representation of W_F corresponding to π under the local Langlands correspondence, and π^∨ is the contragredient representation of D^× under the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence. The nearby cycles complex RΨ on the Lubin–Tate tower relates the étale cohomology of the generic fiber to that of the special fiber, which is a union of Deligne–Lusztig varieties for GL_n over 𝔽_q. The weight spectral sequence of Rapoport–Zink computes the monodromy filtration on the cohomology, and the monodromy operator N corresponds to the nilpotent operator in the Weil–Deligne representation attached to σ(π). The Lubin–Tate spaces are the p-adic analogues of the Drinfeld upper half-space; both realize the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n in the étale cohomology of a tower of moduli spaces of formal groups (or Drinfeld modules). For n=2, the Lubin–Tate space is the p-adic upper half-plane, and its cohomology realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GL2(F) as constructed by Carayol. The key points for examiners are: (1) the Lubin–Tate tower parametrizes deformations of formal O_F-modules with level structure; (2) its compactly supported étale cohomology carries an action of GL_n(F) × D^× × W_F; (3) the cohomology is concentrated in middle degree n-1 and decomposes according to the local Langlands and Jacquet–Langlands correspondences; (3) the nearby cycles and weight spectral sequence relate the generic and special fibers; (4) the monodromy operator matches the Weil–Deligne structure.

The Bloch-Kato Conjecture and the Norm Residue Isomorphism Theorem

The Bloch–Kato conjecture, proved by Voevodsky, Rost, and others (completed 2011), asserts that for a field F of characteristic not equal to ℓ, the Galois symbol map K_n^M(F)/ℓ → H^n(F, μ_ℓ⊗n) from Milnor K-theory modulo ℓ to Galois cohomology (which is étale cohomology of Spec F) is an isomorphism. Here μ_ℓ is the group of ℓ-th roots of unity in a separable closure, and μ_ℓ⊗n is its n-th Tate twist. The norm residue isomorphism theorem states that this map is an isomorphism for all n ≥ 0 and all primes ℓ invertible in F. The proof uses the theory of motivic cohomology and the motivic Steenrod algebra. Voevodsky constructed the motivic stable homotopy category SH(F) and showed that the motivic cohomology groups Hp,q(F, ℤ/ℓ) agree with Milnor K-theory modulo ℓ for p=q=n and with Galois cohomology for p=n, q=n (after identifying Hn,n(F, ℤ/ℓ) ≅ H^n(F, μ_ℓ⊗n)). The key technical input is the computation of the motivic Steenrod operations and the proof that the motivic cohomology of a point is generated by the Milnor K-theory elements. The theorem implies the Milnor conjecture (the case ℓ=2, proved earlier by Voevodsky) and the Bloch–Kato conjecture for all ℓ. It also implies that the Galois cohomology ring H^*(F, μ_ℓ⊗*) is generated by degree-one elements (Kummer theory) with the only relations being the Steinberg relations {a, 1-a} = 0. For a finite field 𝔽_q, the theorem gives K_n^M(𝔽_q)/ℓ = 0 for n ≥ 2 and K1^M(𝔽_q)/ℓ = 𝔽_q^×/ℓ, matching the known Galois cohomology. For a p-adic field F, the theorem combines with local class field theory to describe the Galois cohomology ring. The norm residue isomorphism is the étale cohomological avatar of the isomorphism between motivic cohomology and higher Chow groups. For examiners, the essential facts are: (1) the Galois symbol K_n^M(F)/ℓ → H^n(F, μ_ℓ⊗n) is an isomorphism (norm residue isomorphism); (2) this identifies Milnor K-theory modulo ℓ with étale cohomology of Spec F; (3) the proof uses motivic homotopy theory and the motivic Steenrod algebra; (4) the Galois cohomology ring is generated by degree-one classes with Steinberg relations; (5) the case ℓ=2 is the Milnor conjecture, proved by Voevodsky (Fields Medal 2002); the general case completed by Voevodsky–Rost–Weibel et al.

The Étale Cohomology of Classifying Stacks and the Cohomology of Algebraic Groups

The classifying stack BG of a smooth affine algebraic group G over a field k is the quotient stack [*/G] where G acts trivially on a point. Its étale cohomology with coefficients in a torsion sheaf Λ (with ℓ invertible in k) is defined as the cohomology of the lisse-étale site of BG, or equivalently as the equivariant cohomology H_G^*(*, Λ). When G is finite and ℓ does not divide |G|, the cohomology vanishes above degree zero. When G is a finite ℓ-group, the cohomology H^*(BG, 𝔽_ℓ) is the group cohomology H^*(G, 𝔽_ℓ). For a connected reductive group G over an algebraically closed field, the étale cohomology H^*(BG, ℚ_ℓ) is a polynomial algebra on generators in even degrees: if G has rank r and exponents d1, …, d_r, then H^*(BG, ℚ_ℓ) ≅ ℚ_ℓ[c1, …, c_r] with deg(c_i) = 2d_i. These are the Chern classes of the universal G-bundle. For G = GL_n, the exponents are 1, 2, …, n, so H^*(BGL_n, ℚ_ℓ) ≅ ℚ_ℓ[c1, …, c_n] with deg(c_i) = 2i. For a non-connected group, the cohomology includes contributions from the component group π0(G). The étale cohomology of BG classifies characteristic classes of G-torsors: a class in H^*(BG, Λ) assigns to each G-torsor P → X a cohomology class in H^*(X, Λ) via the classifying map X → BG. The comparison with topological classifying spaces: if G is a complex reductive group, then H^*(BG(ℂ), ℚ_ℓ) ≅ H^*(BG, ℚ_ℓ) via the comparison theorem. The cohomology of BG for G a finite group scheme (e.g., μ_n, α_p) is computed by the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence for the extension 1 → G0 → G → π0(G) → 1. For examiners, the key points are: (1) H^*(BG, Λ) is the equivariant cohomology of a point; (2) for connected reductive G over an algebraically closed field, it is a polynomial algebra on even-degree generators indexed by the exponents of G; (3) these generators are the universal characteristic classes (Chern classes for GL_n); (4) for finite groups, it recovers group cohomology; (5) the lisse-étale site is the correct site for defining cohomology of stacks.

The Étale Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Higgs Bundles and Non-abelian Hodge Theory

The moduli space MDol(G) of semistable G-Higgs bundles on a smooth projective curve X over ℂ (or over a field of characteristic zero) is a quasi-projective variety carrying a ℂ^×-action (scaling the Higgs field). The non-abelian Hodge theorem (Simpson, Corlette, Donaldson) gives a homeomorphism between the underlying analytic space of MDol(G) and the character variety M_B(G) of representations of π1(X) into G(ℂ). The étale cohomology of MDol(G) with ℚ_ℓ coefficients (for a model over a number field) carries a mixed Hodge structure (or weight filtration) that matches the mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of M_B(G) via the non-abelian Hodge correspondence. The ℂ^×-action on MDol(G) induces a weight grading on the cohomology: the weight k part corresponds to the k-th graded piece of the perverse Leray filtration on the Hitchin fibration h: MDol(G) → A, where A is the Hitchin base. The decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration (Ngô, Chaudouard–Laumon) implies that the direct image Rh_* ℚ_ℓ decomposes as a direct sum of shifted intersection cohomology complexes of the strata of A. The support theorem of Ngô shows that the supports are the closures of the loci where the spectral curve has a given singularity type. The cohomology of MDol(G) is concentrated in even degrees and is pure of weight equal to degree (for the compactly supported cohomology of the stable locus). The Poincaré polynomial of MDol(GL_n) was computed by Hausel–Rodriguez-Villegas using the Weil conjectures over finite fields and the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula: it equals the sum over partitions λ of n of qd(λ) times the number of 𝔽_q-points of the corresponding nilpotent orbit closure. The "P = W" conjecture (de Cataldo–Hausel–Migliorini) states that the perverse Leray filtration on H^*(MDol(G)) induced by the Hitchin fibration coincides with the weight filtration on H^*(M_B(G)) induced by the mixed Hodge structure. This has been proved for G = GL_n and in many other cases. For examiners, the essential facts are: (1) the moduli space of Higgs bundles MDol(G) is a quasi-projective variety with a ℂ^×-action; (2) non-abelian Hodge theory gives a homeomorphism MDol(G) ≅ M_B(G); (3) the Hitchin fibration h: MDol(G) → A is a proper Lagrangian fibration; (4) the decomposition theorem for h gives a direct sum decomposition of Rh_* ℚ_ℓ; (5) the perverse Leray filtration matches the weight filtration (P = W conjecture); (6) the Poincaré polynomial is computed via point counting over finite fields and the trace formula.

Étale Cohomology of Artin Stacks and the Lisse‑étale Topos

The lisse‑étale site of an Artin stack X is defined by taking as objects smooth morphisms U → X with U a scheme, and covering families given by étale surjective families of such smooth morphisms. This site is strictly finer than the étale site of a coarse moduli space when one exists, and it captures the stacky inertia groups that are invisible on the coarse space. For a Deligne–Mumford stack the lisse‑étale topos coincides with the étale topos of the associated coarse moduli space after inverting the orders of the stabiliser groups, but for general Artin stacks the lisse‑étale topos retains the full automorphism data. The cohomology groups Hilis‑ét(X, ℚℓ) are defined as the derived functors of global sections on this topos. A fundamental result of Olsson (2007) shows that for a smooth Artin stack of finite type over a field, the lisse‑étale cohomology with ℚℓ coefficients satisfies Poincaré duality after a suitable Tate twist, and the cycle class map from the Chow groups of the stack lands in the expected degree. The proper base change theorem holds for representable proper morphisms of Artin stacks, and the Leray spectral sequence for a representable morphism f : X → Y takes the form E2p,q = Hplis‑ét(Y, Rqf*ℚℓ) ⇒ Hp+qlis‑ét(X, ℚℓ). When X is a global quotient stack [U/G] with G a linear algebraic group acting on a scheme U, the lisse‑étale cohomology identifies with the equivariant cohomology H*G(U, ℚℓ) defined via the Borel construction. This identification is crucial for computing the cohomology of moduli stacks such as BunG(C) of G-bundles on a curve, where the stack is not Deligne–Mumford but the lisse‑étale topos still yields a well‑behaved cohomology theory that matches the geometric Langlands predictions.

The Beilinson‑Lichtenbaum Conjecture and Motivic Cohomology

The Beilinson‑Lichtenbaum conjecture, proved in full generality by Voevodsky, Rost, and Weibel (culminating in the norm residue isomorphism theorem), asserts that for a smooth scheme X over a field k and integers n ≥ 0, q ≥ 0, the natural map from motivic cohomology Hn,qM(X, ℤ/ℓ) to étale cohomology Hnét(X, μℓ⊗q) is an isomorphism for n ≤ q and injective for n = q+1. Here motivic cohomology is defined as the hypercohomology of the motivic complex ℤ(q)M in the Nisnevich topology, and μℓ denotes the sheaf of ℓ‑th roots of unity. The conjecture bridges the world of algebraic cycles (via motivic cohomology) and Galois cohomology (via étale cohomology). The proof proceeds by constructing the motivic Steenrod algebra and establishing the Bloch‑Kato conjecture, which identifies the Galois cohomology ring H*(k, μℓ⊗*) with the Milnor K‑theory ring KM*(k)/ℓ. The norm residue isomorphism KMn(k)/ℓ ≅ Hn(k, μℓ⊗n) is the key computational tool. For a smooth projective variety X over a finite field 𝔽q, the conjecture implies that the eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hnét(X, ℚℓ(q)) are algebraic numbers whose complex absolute values are q(n‑q)/2, a statement that refines the Weil conjectures. In practice, the isomorphism allows one to compute étale cohomology groups in low degrees by working with algebraic cycles and Milnor K‑theory, which are often more accessible. For example, the group H2ét(X, μℓ⊗2) for a surface X is isomorphic to the ℓ‑torsion in the Chow group CH2(X) when ℓ is invertible in the base field, providing a direct link between the Brauer group and algebraic cycles.

Technique: Using the norm residue isomorphism to compute motivic cohomology
Let k be a field containing a primitive ℓ‑th root of unity, with ℓ ≠ char(k). Compute H2ét(Spec(k), μℓ⊗2) and identify it with a Milnor K‑group.
By the norm residue isomorphism theorem (Bloch‑Kato), for any field k containing μℓ there is a canonical isomorphism KM2(k)/ℓ ≅ H2(k, μℓ⊗2). The étale cohomology of Spec(k) is precisely Galois cohomology, so H2ét(Spec(k), μℓ⊗2) = H2(Gal(ksep/k), μℓ⊗2). The Milnor K‑group KM2(k) is generated by symbols {a,b} with a,b ∈ k× subject to the Steinberg relation {a,1‑a}=0. Modulo ℓ, the isomorphism sends the class of {a,b} to the cup product (a) ∪ (b) in Galois cohomology, where (a) ∈ H1(k, μℓ) is the Kummer class of a. Therefore H2ét(Spec(k), μℓ⊗2) ≅ KM2(k)/ℓ. If k = 𝔽q with ℓ | (q‑1), then KM2(𝔽q) = 0, so the cohomology group vanishes. If k = ℚ(μℓ), the group is non‑trivial and can be described via the ℓ‑part of the tame kernel of k.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for a comparison between motivic and étale cohomology, first check the weight‑degree range (n ≤ q) where the Beilinson‑Lichtenbaum isomorphism holds. Then invoke the norm residue isomorphism to replace the étale group by a Milnor K‑group modulo ℓ. This reduces the computation to a problem in algebraic K‑theory or Galois cohomology, which is often explicitly solvable using Kummer theory and the Steinberg relation.

Étale Cohomology of Derived Schemes and the Cotangent Complex

Derived algebraic geometry replaces ordinary schemes by derived schemes, i.e. locally ringed ∞‑topoi whose structure sheaf takes values in connective E∞‑ring spectra. The étale topology on a derived scheme X is defined by declaring a morphism f : U → X to be étale if it is étale on the underlying classical truncation π0(X) and the relative cotangent complex LU/X vanishes. The cotangent complex LX of a derived scheme controls its deformation theory and provides a derived analogue of the sheaf of Kähler differentials. For a derived scheme X over a field k of characteristic zero, the étale cohomology with ℚℓ coefficients can be computed via the pro‑étale site of Bhatt–Scholze, which yields a cohomology theory satisfying all the expected formal properties: proper base change, Poincaré duality for smooth proper derived schemes, and a cycle class map from the derived Chow groups. The derived de Rham cohomology of X (in the sense of Lurie) is related to étale cohomology by a derived version of the p‑adic comparison theorems: for a smooth proper derived scheme over a p‑adic field, there is a natural isomorphism between the derived de Rham cohomology tensored with BdR and the étale cohomology tensored with BdR, compatible with filtrations. The cotangent complex enters the picture through the Hodge‑to‑de Rham spectral sequence, which degenerates at E1 for smooth proper derived schemes over a field of characteristic zero, yielding a Hodge filtration on étale cohomology after p‑adic comparison. In the context of the geometric Langlands program, derived schemes appear naturally as derived moduli stacks of local systems; their étale cohomology carries an action of the categorical Hecke operators, and the cotangent complex governs the singularities of these stacks, which are typically not classical but derived smooth.

The Categorical Trace Formula and Geometric Langlands

The categorical trace formula, developed by Nadler and Yun, provides a sheaf‑theoretic incarnation of the Arthur‑Selberg trace formula for the geometric Langlands correspondence. Let X be a smooth projective curve over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic zero, and let G be a reductive group over k. The moduli stack LocSysG(X) of G-local systems on X is a derived Artin stack whose étale cohomology (with ℚℓ coefficients) is the target of the geometric Langlands functor. The categorical trace formula expresses the trace of the Hecke operators acting on the derived category of ℚℓ-sheaves on BunG(X) as an integral over LocSysG(X) of a certain character sheaf, which is constructed from the constant sheaf on the stack of G-bundles via the geometric Satake equivalence. Concretely, for a dominant coweight λ, the Hecke functor Hλ acts on Dlis‑ét(BunG(X), ℚℓ), and its trace is given by the cohomology of the fiber of the evaluation map Heckeλ → BunG(X) × BunG(X) over the diagonal. The formula identifies this trace with the cohomology of the corresponding Hitchin fiber on the de Rham side, after applying the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence. The categorical trace formula implies the decomposition of the Hecke category into a direct sum of categories indexed by the irreducible representations of the Langlands dual group LG, and it provides a geometric proof of the fundamental lemma for Lie algebras (Ngô’s theorem) by interpreting the orbital integrals as traces of Frobenius on the cohomology of affine Springer fibers. In the ℓ‑adic setting, the trace formula is compatible with the Grothendieck‑Lefschetz trace formula, allowing point counts over finite fields to verify the conjectural equality of traces on both sides of the Langlands correspondence.

Étale Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Sheaves on Surfaces

Let S be a smooth projective surface over an algebraically closed field k, and let v = (r, c1, χ) be a Mukai vector in the algebraic part of the cohomology H*(S, ℤ). The moduli space MH(v) of Gieseker‑stable sheaves with respect to an ample divisor H is a quasi‑projective variety (or an Artin stack if strictly semistable sheaves are included). Its ℓ‑adic étale cohomology carries a pure weight filtration when S is a K3 surface or an abelian surface, and the cohomology groups are concentrated in even degrees. Göttsche’s formula computes the generating series of the Betti numbers of the Hilbert schemes of points S[n] (the case r=1, c1=0) in terms of the Betti numbers of S. For higher rank, the work of Maulik, Shen, and Yin (2020) establishes a wall‑crossing formula for the Poincaré polynomials of MH(v) as the polarization H varies, expressing the difference in terms of the cohomology of nested Hilbert schemes of points on the surface. The étale cohomology of MH(v) admits a natural action of the cohomological Hall algebra (CoHA) of S, which is defined via the étale cohomology of the stack of all coherent sheaves on S. This CoHA action categorifies the Nakajima quiver variety construction and provides a geometric realization of the Heisenberg algebra action on the cohomology of Hilbert schemes. When S is a K3 surface, the Beauville‑Bogomolov form on H2(MH(v), ℚℓ) coincides with the Mukai pairing on the orthogonal complement of v in H*(S, ℚℓ), and the monodromy group of the local system R2π*ℚℓ over the base of a Lagrangian fibration is the orthogonal group of this form. These results are proved by combining the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin‑type fibration on the moduli space with the weight arguments coming from the Weil conjectures over finite fields, using the Grothendieck‑Lefschetz trace formula to relate point counts to Betti numbers.

Étale Cohomology of Formal Schemes and the Comparison with Rigid Analytic Geometry

Formal schemes provide a natural framework for studying degenerations and p‑adic families. The étale cohomology of a formal scheme 𝔛 over a complete discrete valuation ring 𝒪 is defined via the pro‑étale site of its adic generic fiber 𝔛_η, or equivalently by taking the limit of the étale cohomology of the thickenings 𝔛_n = 𝔛 ×𝒪 𝒪/πn+1. Raynaud’s generic fiber functor associates to a formal scheme 𝔛 a rigid analytic space 𝔛rig over the fraction field K. When 𝔛 is proper over 𝒪, a comparison theorem of Huber (and later refined by Scholze) asserts a canonical isomorphism H^iét(𝔛_η, ℚ_ℓ) ≅ H^iét(𝔛rig, ℚ_ℓ) for ℓ ≠ char(K). The proof uses the almost purity theorem for perfectoid algebras: the tilt 𝔛♭ of a perfectoid formal scheme yields an isomorphism of étale topoi between the characteristic‑p and characteristic‑0 fibers, allowing one to transfer cohomological statements across the characteristic divide. In the non‑proper case one must work with compactly supported cohomology and the theory of adic spaces; the comparison still holds for smooth formal schemes after a suitable alteration. These results are foundational for p‑adic Hodge theory, for the construction of the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence, and for the study of the cohomology of Shimura varieties at infinite level.

p‑adic Hodge Theory and Étale Cohomology: Fontaine’s Period Rings and the Comparison Isomorphisms

Let X be a smooth proper variety over a p‑adic field K. The ℓ‑adic étale cohomology groups H^iét(XK, ℚ_p) are continuous representations of the absolute Galois group G_K. Fontaine’s period rings BdR, Bcris, Bst, and BHT provide the target coefficients for comparison isomorphisms that relate these Galois representations to algebraic de Rham, crystalline, and log‑crystalline cohomology. The fundamental de Rham comparison is a G_K‑equivariant isomorphism H^iét(XK, ℚ_p) ⊗ℚ_p BdR ≅ H^idR(X/K) ⊗_K BdR, which respects filtrations and recovers the Hodge–Tate decomposition after passing to graded pieces. The crystalline comparison, due to Faltings and Tsuji, identifies H^iét(XK, ℚ_p) ⊗ Bcris with the crystalline cohomology of a smooth proper model over 𝒪_K tensored with Bcris, and it is compatible with Frobenius and the monodromy operator. Scholze’s perfectoid approach gives a unified proof of all comparisons by working on the pro‑étale site of X and using the almost mathematics of the tilt. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts which p‑adic Galois representations arise from geometry; the comparison isomorphisms are the main tool for verifying the conjecture in many cases, notably for abelian varieties and modular forms.

Worked Example: Hodge–Tate Weights of an Elliptic Curve
Let E be an elliptic curve over a p‑adic field K with good reduction. Compute the Hodge–Tate weights of the Galois representation V = H1ét(EK, ℚ_p) and explain how they reflect the Hodge filtration on H1dR(E/K).
The de Rham cohomology H1dR(E/K) is a two‑dimensional K‑vector space with Hodge filtration Fil0 = H1dR, Fil1 = H0(E, Ω1) (one‑dimensional), Fil2 = 0. The Hodge–Tate decomposition theorem gives a G_K‑equivariant isomorphism V ⊗ℚ_p ℂ_p ≅ ⊕j (H1-j(E, Ω^j) ⊗_K ℂ_p(-j)). Here H1(E, 𝒪_E) sits in degree 0 and H0(E, Ω1) in degree 1. Thus the Hodge–Tate weights are 0 (with multiplicity 1) and 1 (with multiplicity 1). The weight 0 corresponds to the quotient Fil0/Fil1 ≅ H1(E, 𝒪_E), and weight 1 corresponds to Fil1 ≅ H0(E, Ω1). The Tate twist ℂ_p(-1) shifts the weight by +1, matching the fact that the cyclotomic character has Hodge–Tate weight 1.

The Étale Cohomology of Torsors and Non‑abelian Galois Cohomology

For a sheaf of groups G on the étale site of a scheme X, the pointed set H1ét(X, G) classifies isomorphism classes of G‑torsors. When G is non‑abelian, the usual long exact sequence of abelian cohomology is replaced by an exact sequence of pointed sets: 1 → H0(X, H) → H0(X, G) → H0(X, Q) → H1(X, H) → H1(X, G) → H1(X, Q). The connecting map δ: H0(X, Q) → H1(X, H) sends a section of Q to the torsor obtained by pulling back the extension 1 → H → G → Q → 1. For G = GL_n, H1ét(X, GL_n) is the set of rank‑n vector bundles; for G = PGL_n, it classifies Azumaya algebras of degree n, and the boundary map to H2(X, 𝔾_m) recovers the Brauer group. Over a field k, H1(Gal(k^s/k), G) classifies k‑forms of the group G; the Tate–Shafarevich group Ш1(k, G) measures the failure of the local‑global principle for torsors. Giraud’s theory of non‑abelian cohomology extends this to H2 for gerbes banded by an abelian group, and to the classification of 2‑gerbes. These tools are essential for the study of rational points on homogeneous spaces, for the arithmetic of algebraic groups, and for the geometric Langlands program where G‑local systems are G‑torsors with flat connection.

The Étale Cohomology of Abelian Varieties: Tate Modules, Duality, and the Weil Pairing

Let A be an abelian variety of dimension g over a field k. For a prime ℓ ≠ char(k), the ℓ‑adic Tate module T_ℓ(A) = lim_← A[ℓ^n] is a free ℤ_ℓ‑module of rank 2g with a continuous action of Gal(k^s/k). The first étale cohomology group satisfies H1ét(Ak, ℤ_ℓ) ≅ T_ℓ(A)^∨, and Poincaré duality gives a perfect pairing H1 × H1 → H2 ≅ ℤ_ℓ(-1). This pairing is the ℓ‑adic Weil pairing on torsion points: e_ℓ: A[ℓ^n] × A^∨[ℓ^n] → μℓ^n. The dual abelian variety A^∨ represents the Picard functor, and the canonical isomorphism H1(A) ≅ H1(A^∨)^∨(-1) reflects the duality of Tate modules. The characteristic polynomial of Frobenius acting on T_ℓ(A) has integer coefficients independent of ℓ (Weil conjectures for abelian varieties, proved by Weil). The Honda–Tate theorem classifies isogeny classes of abelian varieties over finite fields by the Frobenius eigenvalues. Faltings’ proof of the Tate conjecture for abelian varieties shows that the ℓ‑adic Galois representation on T_ℓ(A) ⊗ ℚ_ℓ determines A up to isogeny. The weight filtration on H1 is pure of weight 1, and the Hodge–Tate decomposition in the p‑adic case recovers the Hodge structure of A.

The Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Local Systems on Curves and Geometric Langlands

Let X be a smooth projective curve over an algebraically closed field k, and let G be a reductive group. The moduli stack LocSys_G of G‑local systems on X is an Artin stack locally of finite type. Its ℓ‑adic étale cohomology H^*ét(LocSys_G, ℚ_ℓ) carries a natural action of the Hecke correspondences, which are defined by modifying a local system at a point of X. The geometric Langlands conjecture predicts an equivalence between the derived category of D‑modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G‑bundles and the derived category of coherent sheaves on LocSys^L G, where ^L G is the Langlands dual group. In the ℓ‑adic setting, this translates to a correspondence between perverse sheaves on Bun_G and constructible sheaves on LocSys^L G. The categorical trace formula, proved by Gaitsgory and Lurie, expresses the trace of Frobenius on the cohomology of Hecke correspondences as a sum over orbital integrals on the dual side, providing a geometric proof of the fundamental lemma. The purity of the cohomology of LocSys_G (weights ≤ expected) follows from the Weil conjectures applied to the moduli stack of shtukas, and it is a crucial input for the trace formula. Drinfeld’s work for GL2 and Lafforgue’s proof for GL_n over function fields establish the correspondence in the automorphic‑to‑Galois direction, while the full categorical equivalence remains a deep open problem for general G.

The Tate Conjecture and Algebraic Cycles in Étale Cohomology

The Tate conjecture, formulated by John Tate in 1963, predicts a precise relationship between algebraic cycles on a smooth projective variety X over a finitely generated field k and the Galois action on its ℓ-adic étale cohomology. For a prime ℓ different from the characteristic of k, the cycle class map sends a codimension-r algebraic cycle Z to a class cl(Z) ∈ H²ʳét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(r))Gal(k̄/k). The conjecture asserts that this map is surjective onto the Galois-invariant subspace. Equivalently, the subspace of H²ʳét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(r)) fixed by the absolute Galois group is spanned by classes of algebraic cycles defined over k.

When k is a finite field 𝔽q, the Frobenius endomorphism Frobq acts on cohomology. The Tate conjecture then predicts that the eigenvalues of Frobq on H²ʳét that are qr times roots of unity correspond exactly to algebraic cycles. This is equivalent to the statement that the order of the pole of the zeta function ζ(X, s) at s = r equals the rank of the group of codimension-r cycles modulo homological equivalence. Tate proved the conjecture for divisors (r = 1) on abelian varieties over finite fields, using the Honda–Tate classification. Faltings extended this to all abelian varieties over number fields as part of his proof of the Mordell conjecture. For r > 1, the conjecture remains open in general. It is known for K3 surfaces over finite fields (Nygaard–Ogus, Charles, Maulik) and for certain Shimura varieties (Kisin, Madapusi Pera). The conjecture for r = 2 on a surface is equivalent to the finite generation of the Néron–Severi group and the equality of numerical and homological equivalence — a statement known as the Tate conjecture for divisors on the surface.

The Tate conjecture is deeply intertwined with the standard conjectures of Grothendieck. Standard conjecture C (Künneth components of the diagonal are algebraic) implies the Tate conjecture for all r. Standard conjecture D (numerical equivalence equals homological equivalence) is equivalent to the semisimplicity of the category of motives, which would imply the Tate conjecture for abelian varieties. The conjecture also has a formulation for varieties over number fields, where the Galois group is replaced by the image of the Galois representation on cohomology. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts that the Galois representations appearing in the étale cohomology of smooth projective varieties over number fields are exactly those that are unramified outside a finite set and de Rham at places above ℓ. The Tate conjecture would imply that the ℓ-adic Galois representation on H²ʳét is determined by algebraic cycles, providing a bridge between the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture and the Hodge conjecture in characteristic zero via p-adic Hodge theory.

The Brauer Group and Étale Cohomology: H²(X, Gm)

The Brauer group Br(X) of a scheme X classifies Azumaya algebras up to Morita equivalence. Grothendieck reinterpreted it as the cohomological Brauer group Br'(X) = H²ét(X, 𝔾m)tors, the torsion subgroup of the second étale cohomology group with coefficients in the multiplicative group. For a regular integral scheme, the natural map Br(X) → Br'(X) is injective, and Gabber proved it is an isomorphism when X admits an ample line bundle. This identification allows the full power of étale cohomology to be applied to the Brauer group.

For a smooth projective variety X over a field k, the Kummer sequence 1 → μn → 𝔾m → 𝔾m → 1 (where the last map is x ↦ xn) yields a long exact sequence in étale cohomology. The connecting homomorphism H¹ét(X, 𝔾m) → H²ét(X, μn) identifies the n-torsion in the Picard group with a subgroup of H²ét(X, μn). Passing to the limit over n gives an exact sequence 0 → Pic(X) ⊗ ℚℓ/ℤℓ → H²ét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(1)) → Br(Xk̄){ℓ} → 0, where {ℓ} denotes the ℓ-primary torsion. Over a finite field 𝔽q, the Tate conjecture for divisors implies that the Frobenius action on H²ét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(1)) has eigenvalues that are q times roots of unity exactly on the image of the Néron–Severi group. The cokernel, which is the Brauer group, then corresponds to the remaining eigenvalues. The Artin–Tate conjecture gives a formula for the order of the Brauer group in terms of the leading coefficient of the zeta function: |Br(X)| = qχ(𝒪X) · |det(1 − Frobq | H²ét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(1)))| / |NS(X)| · qρ, where ρ is the Picard number.

For a curve C over a finite field, H²ét(C, 𝔾m) = 0 by Tsen's theorem (cohomological dimension 1). For a surface, the Brauer group can be nontrivial and is a birational invariant for smooth projective surfaces. The Manin obstruction to the Hasse principle for rational points on a variety X over a number field is given by the Brauer group: an adelic point (xv) ∈ X(𝔸k) is orthogonal to Br(X) under the Brauer–Manin pairing ∑v invv(A(xv)) = 0 for all A ∈ Br(X). The étale cohomological interpretation allows the computation of Br(X) via the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence Hᵖ(Gal(k̄/k), Hᑫét(Xk̄, 𝔾m)) ⇒ Hᵖ⁺ᑫét(X, 𝔾m). The low-degree terms give 0 → Pic(X) → Pic(Xk̄)Gal → Br(k) → Ker(Br(X) → Br(Xk̄)Gal) → H¹(Gal, Pic(Xk̄)) → H³(k, 𝔾m). This sequence is the fundamental tool for computing Brauer groups of varieties over non-algebraically closed fields.

Examiner's Trap: Brauer Group vs Cohomological Brauer Group
Let X be a smooth projective variety over a field k. A student claims: "The Brauer group Br(X) is always equal to H²_ét(X, G_m)." Explain why this is false in general, and state the precise conditions under which equality holds.
The statement confuses the Brauer group Br(X) (Azumaya algebras modulo Morita equivalence) with the cohomological Brauer group Br'(X) = H²_ét(X, G_m)_tors. There is always an injective map Br(X) → Br'(X). Gabber (1981) proved this is an isomorphism when X is a scheme admitting an ample line bundle (e.g., quasi-projective over an affine base). However, for non-quasi-projective schemes or non-Noetherian schemes, the map need not be surjective. A counterexample: let X be a non-projective proper variety over ℂ constructed by Hironaka; then Br(X) may be strictly smaller than H²_ét(X, G_m)_tors. For regular integral schemes, the map is injective, but surjectivity requires the existence of an ample line bundle. The key distinction: Br(X) classifies geometric objects (Azumaya algebras), while H²_ét(X, G_m) is a cohomological invariant. The equality is a theorem, not a definition.
Exam Strategy: Brauer Group Computations
When asked to compute Br(X) for a variety over a non-algebraically closed field, always write down the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence low-degree exact sequence. Identify the Galois module Pic(X_k̄) and compute its Galois cohomology. Remember that for curves over finite fields, Br = 0. For surfaces, the Artin–Tate formula relates |Br| to the zeta function and the Néron–Severi lattice. The Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only known obstruction to the Hasse principle for rational points on varieties with Br(X)/Br(k) finite — mention this if the question asks about rational points.

The Weil-étale Topos and Special Values of L-Functions

The Weil-étale topos, introduced by Lichtenbaum in 2005 and developed by Geisser, provides a topological framework for interpreting the special values of zeta and L-functions of varieties over finite fields and number fields. For a variety X over a finite field 𝔽q, the classical étale topos has cohomological dimension 2 dim X + 1, but the Frobenius action makes the Euler characteristic of the complex RΓét(X, ℤℓ(r)) behave like a complex of length 2 dim X. Lichtenbaum defined the Weil-étale topos XW as the topos of sheaves on the site whose objects are pairs (U, n) with U an étale scheme over X and n ∈ ℤ, with morphisms generated by étale morphisms and the Frobenius correspondence. The cohomology of the Weil-étale topos with coefficients in ℤℓ(r) fits into an exact triangle RΓW(X, ℤℓ(r)) → RΓét(X, ℤℓ(r)) → RΓét(X, ℤℓ(r)) where the second map is 1 − Frobq. This yields a long exact sequence relating Weil-étale cohomology to the coinvariants and invariants of Frobenius on étale cohomology.

The fundamental conjecture of Lichtenbaum, proved by Geisser for smooth varieties over finite fields, states that the Euler characteristic of the complex RΓW(X, ℤℓ(r)) equals the leading coefficient of the zeta function ζ(X, s) at s = r, up to a sign and a power of q. More precisely, χ(RΓW(X, ℤℓ(r))) = ± qχ(𝒪X)(r) · ζ*(X, r) where ζ* is the leading coefficient. This provides a cohomological interpretation of the special values, analogous to the analytic class number formula for number fields. For a number field K, the Weil-étale topos of Spec 𝒪K is defined using the action of the Weil group WK on the étale topos of Spec K̄. The cohomology groups HⁱW(Spec 𝒪K, ℤ(r)) are conjecturally related to the special values of the Dedekind zeta function ζK(s) at integers r ≤ 0. The Tamagawa number conjecture of Bloch and Kato, and its refinement by Fontaine and Perrin-Riou, predicts that the leading coefficient of the L-function of a motive M at a critical integer s = r is given by the determinant of the comparison isomorphism between de Rham and étale realizations, multiplied by the order of the torsion in the motivic cohomology groups. The Weil-étale topos provides a geometric home for these motivic cohomology groups.

Geisser and Schmidt constructed the Weil-étale topos for arithmetic schemes (schemes of finite type over ℤ) and proved a duality theorem: for a regular scheme X of finite type over ℤ, there is a perfect pairing HⁱW(X, ℤ(r)) × H³⁻ⁱW,c(X, ℤ(1−r)) → ℚ/ℤ, where the subscript c denotes compact support. This duality generalizes both Poincaré duality for varieties over finite fields and Artin–Verdier duality for rings of integers in number fields. The Weil-étale cohomology groups are finitely generated for regular arithmetic schemes, and their ranks are given by the orders of vanishing of the zeta function at integer points. The torsion subgroups are related to the Brauer group and the Chow groups of X. The construction uses the pro-étale site of Bhatt and Scholze to define the Weil-étale topos for general schemes, replacing the ad hoc site of Lichtenbaum with a more robust categorical framework.

Étale Cohomology of Toric Varieties and the Danilov–Jurkiewicz Theorem

Toric varieties provide a class of algebraic varieties whose geometry is entirely encoded by combinatorial data: a fan Σ in a lattice N ≅ ℤn. The Danilov–Jurkiewicz theorem (1987) gives a complete description of the étale cohomology ring of a smooth complete toric variety XΣ over an algebraically closed field k with coefficients in ℤ/ℓℤ (ℓ ≠ char k). The cohomology ring H*ét(XΣ, ℤ/ℓℤ) is isomorphic to the Stanley–Reisner ring of the fan Σ modulo the linear relations coming from the exact sequence 0 → M → ℤΣ(1) → Pic(XΣ) → 0, where M = Hom(N, ℤ) is the dual lattice and Σ(1) is the set of rays of the fan. Explicitly, H*ét(XΣ, ℤ/ℓℤ) ≅ (ℤ/ℓℤ)[xρ : ρ ∈ Σ(1)] / (ISR + Ilin), where ISR is generated by monomials ∏ρ∉σ xρ for cones σ ∈ Σ, and Ilin is generated by ∑ρ ⟨m, vρ⟩ xρ for m ∈ M, with vρ the primitive generator of ρ. The grading is given by deg xρ = 2, so the cohomology is concentrated in even degrees.

This result has several striking consequences. First, the étale cohomology of a smooth complete toric variety is independent of the characteristic of the base field (as long as ℓ ≠ char k), and coincides with the singular cohomology of the associated complex toric variety. Second, the odd-degree cohomology groups vanish, so the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence degenerates at E₁ and the Hodge numbers hp,q are zero for p ≠ q. Third, the cycle class map from the Chow ring A*(XΣ) to H²*ét(XΣ, ℤ/ℓℤ(*)) is an isomorphism, so the Tate conjecture holds trivially for all r. The Chow ring itself has the same presentation as the cohomology ring, with the same generators and relations. For a singular toric variety, the intersection cohomology (which coincides with the étale cohomology of a resolution) is described by the same combinatorial formula, but the ordinary cohomology may have odd-degree components coming from the singularities.

The proof uses the stratification of XΣ by torus orbits O(σ) ≅ (𝔾m)dim σ. The étale cohomology of a torus (𝔾m)d is the exterior algebra on d generators in degree 1. The spectral sequence of the stratification collapses at E₂ because all differentials vanish for weight reasons (the strata are tori, whose cohomology is pure of weight equal to degree). The E₂ page is the cochain complex of the fan with coefficients in the cohomology of the torus, which computes the Stanley–Reisner ring. The linear relations come from the exact sequence of tori 1 → TN → (𝔾m)Σ(1) → Pic(XΣ) ⊗ 𝔾m → 1. For non-complete toric varieties, the cohomology with compact support is described by a similar formula using the Stanley–Reisner ring of the fan with a different grading. The Danilov–Jurkiewicz theorem is a rare case where the full ring structure of étale cohomology can be computed explicitly for a large class of varieties, making toric varieties a crucial testing ground for conjectures in étale cohomology.

The Gersten Resolution and Bloch–Ogus Theory: Étale Cohomology and Chow Groups

The Gersten conjecture, proved by Quillen for algebraic K-theory and by Bloch–Ogus for étale cohomology, provides a resolution of the étale sheaf ℋq(μℓ⊗r) on a smooth variety X by a complex of flasque sheaves supported on the codimension-p points of X. For a smooth variety X over a field k with char k ≠ ℓ, the Bloch–Ogus complex is 0 → ℋq(μℓ⊗r) → ⊕x∈X⁽⁰⁾ ix* Hq(k(x), μℓ⊗r) → ⊕x∈X⁽¹⁾ ix* Hq−1(k(x), μℓ⊗r−1) → ⋯ where X⁽p denotes the set of points of codimension p, ix is the inclusion of the point, and the differentials are given by residue maps in Galois cohomology. This complex is a flasque resolution, so its global sections compute the étale cohomology H*ét(X, μℓ⊗r). The E₁ page of the associated spectral sequence is E₁p,q = ⊕x∈X⁽p Hq−p(k(x), μℓ⊗r−p), converging to Hp+qét(X, μℓ⊗r).

The Bloch–Ogus theory identifies the E₂ page with the Chow groups of X with coefficients in the Galois cohomology of the residue fields. In particular, E₂p,p ≅ CHp(X) ⊗ ℤ/ℓℤ when r = p and the base field is algebraically closed. The edge map H²pét(X, μℓ⊗p) → E₂p,p is the cycle class map modulo ℓ. The degeneration of the spectral sequence at E₂ (the Bloch–Kato conjecture, now a theorem of Voevodsky–Rost) implies that the cycle class map is injective and its image is the subgroup of classes that are unramified at all codimension-1 points. This gives a cohomological criterion for a class to be algebraic: a class in H²pét(X, μℓ⊗p) comes from an algebraic cycle if and only if its residues at all codimension-1 points vanish. The Gersten resolution also provides a proof of the purity theorem for étale cohomology: for a smooth closed subvariety Z ⊂ X of codimension c, the Gysin map Hⁱét(Z, μℓ⊗r) → Hⁱ⁺²cét(X, μℓ⊗r+c) is injective and its image is the kernel of the restriction to X Z.

The Bloch–Ogus theory extends to higher Chow groups and motivic cohomology. The motivic complex ℤ(r)mot on the Zariski site of X has cohomology sheaves ℋq(ℤ(r)mot) ≅ CHr(−, 2r−q), the higher Chow groups of Bloch. The étale realization map ℤ(r)mot → ℛε* μℓ⊗r (where ε is the map from the étale to the Zariski site) induces a map of spectral sequences from the motivic spectral sequence to the Bloch–Ogus spectral sequence. The Beilinson–Lichtenbaum conjecture (proved by Voevodsky) states that this map is an isomorphism on cohomology sheaves in degrees ≤ r and injective in degree r+1. This identifies motivic cohomology with étale cohomology in a range, and shows that the Gersten resolution for étale cohomology is the étale realization of the Gersten resolution for motivic cohomology. The weight filtration on étale cohomology corresponds to the coniveau filtration coming from the Gersten complex, and the graded pieces are given by the cohomology of the residue fields. This provides a complete description of the weight spectral sequence for smooth varieties over a field, generalizing the weight spectral sequence of Rapoport–Zink for varieties over finite fields.

  • Confusing the Tate conjecture (algebraic cycles span Galois invariants) with the Hodge conjecture (Hodge classes are algebraic) — they are characteristic-ℓ and characteristic-0 analogues but not equivalent; the Tate conjecture for a variety over a number field does not imply the Hodge conjecture for its complex fiber.
  • Assuming Br(X) = H²_ét(X, G_m) without checking the ample line bundle condition; the equality fails for non-quasi-projective proper varieties.
  • Thinking the Weil-étale topos is just the étale topos with a Frobenius action; it is a genuinely different topos whose cohomology computes the special values of L-functions, not the étale cohomology groups themselves.
  • Believing the Danilov–Jurkiewicz theorem applies to all toric varieties; it requires smoothness and completeness. For singular toric varieties, ordinary étale cohomology may have odd-degree parts, and intersection cohomology must be used.
  • Misapplying the Gersten resolution to non-smooth varieties; the complex is exact only for regular schemes. For singular varieties, one must use the Cousin complex or the resolution by the sheaf of cycles.
  • Confusing the Bloch–Ogus spectral sequence (coniveau spectral sequence) with the weight spectral sequence; the former computes étale cohomology from residue fields, the latter computes the weight filtration on the cohomology of a variety over a finite field.
  • Overlooking the Tate twist in the cycle class map: cl: CHr(X) → H²ʳ_ét(X, ℤ_ℓ(r)). The twist is essential for Galois equivariance and for the correct weight.

Étale Cohomology of Rapoport–Zink Spaces and Local Shimura Varieties

Rapoport–Zink spaces are formal schemes over the ring of integers of a p-adic field that uniformize the p-adic neighbourhoods of basic Newton strata in the special fibres of Shimura varieties of Hodge type. For a reductive group G over ℚₚ, a minuscule cocharacter μ, and an element b ∈ G(ℚₚ) satisfying the Kottwitz condition, the Rapoport–Zink space ℳ(G, b, μ) parametrizes deformations of a fixed p-divisible group with G-structure up to quasi-isogeny. Its generic fibre is a rigid analytic variety over ℚₚ whose étale cohomology realizes the local Langlands correspondence and the local Jacquet–Langlands correspondence. The cohomology groups Hⁱ_ét(ℳ(G, b, μ)̄, ℚₗ) for ℓ ≠ p carry a smooth action of G(ℚₚ) × J_b(ℚₚ) × Wℚₚ, where J_b is the σ-centralizer of b (an inner form of G) and Wℚₚ is the Weil group. The Harris–Taylor method and its generalization by Boyer, Dat, and others show that the alternating sum ∑ (-1)ⁱ Hⁱ_ét realizes the local Langlands correspondence for G in the sense that the G(ℚₚ) × Wℚₚ-equivariant part corresponds to the LLC, while the J_b(ℚₚ)-equivariant part gives the local Jacquet–Langlands transfer. The weight spectral sequence of Rapoport–Zink computes the monodromy filtration on this cohomology, and the nearby cycles complex RΨ(ℚₗ) on the special fibre is described by the cohomology of the Bruhat–Tits building of J_b. The uniformization theorem of Rapoport–Zink identifies the completion of a Shimura variety along a basic Newton stratum with a quotient of a Rapoport–Zink space by a discrete subgroup, allowing global automorphic forms to be studied via local geometry. The cohomology of Rapoport–Zink spaces also provides a geometric realization of the local Langlands correspondence for non-basic strata via the work of Fargues–Scholze on the geometrization of the local Langlands correspondence using the Fargues–Fontaine curve.

The Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Shtukas and the Global Langlands Correspondence for Function Fields

For a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽_q and a reductive group G over 𝔽_q, the moduli stack Sht_G^r of G-shtukas with r legs (or modifications) is an algebraic stack locally of finite type over 𝔽_q whose ℓ-adic cohomology (ℓ ≠ p) realizes the global Langlands correspondence for function fields. A G-shtuka over an 𝔽_q-scheme S is a G-bundle on X × S together with an isomorphism outside the graphs of r morphisms S → X (the legs) satisfying a boundedness condition. The stack Sht_G^r is stratified by the Harder–Narasimhan type of the underlying G-bundle, and its cohomology carries an action of the Hecke operators at the legs and of the Frobenius correspondence. The Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula applied to Sht_G^r expresses the trace of Frobenius composed with Hecke operators as a sum over fixed points, which are in bijection with G-bundles on X equipped with a Frobenius structure — i.e., global Langlands parameters. Lafforgue's proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields uses the cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas for GL_n, establishing a bijection between cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n(𝔸_X) and irreducible n-dimensional ℓ-adic representations of the étale fundamental group π₁^ét(X) that are pure of weight zero. The generalization to arbitrary reductive groups G by Vincent Lafforgue uses the excision technique in the étale cohomology of the moduli stack of G-shtukas, together with the geometric Satake equivalence and the theory of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian. The cohomology of Sht_G^r decomposes as a direct sum over automorphic representations π of G(𝔸_X) of π ⊗ r_π, where r_π is the associated Galois representation. The nearby cycles complex on the boundary of the compactification of Sht_G^r (the Drinfeld compactification) is controlled by the cohomology of smaller moduli stacks of shtukas for Levi subgroups, leading to an inductive proof of the correspondence. The weight filtration on the cohomology of Sht_G^r corresponds to the cuspidal support of automorphic representations, and the purity theorem (Weil II) ensures that the Galois representations r_π are pure of the expected weight.

Worked Example: Computing the Trace of Frobenius on Shtuka Cohomology
Let X = ℙ¹𝔽_q and G = GL₂. Consider the moduli stack ShtGL₂2 of rank-2 shtukas with two legs on X. A fixed point of the Frobenius-Hecke correspondence corresponds to a rank-2 vector bundle E on X with a Frobenius isomorphism Fr*E ≅ E at the two legs. Show that the trace of Frobenius on the compactly supported cohomology Hⁱ_c(ShtGL₂2, ℚₗ) equals the sum over isomorphism classes of such bundles of the trace of Frobenius on the stalk of the Hecke eigensheaf.
By the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula for stacks (Behrend), Tr(Fr, Hⁱ_c(Sht, ℚₗ)) = ∑x ∈ Sht(𝔽_q) Tr(Fr_x, (ℚₗ)_x) / |Aut(x)|. A point x ∈ ShtGL₂2(𝔽_q) is a rank-2 vector bundle E on ℙ¹𝔽_q with an isomorphism φ: Fr*E → E at the two legs. The automorphism group is the group of automorphisms of E commuting with φ. The Hecke eigensheaf A_π associated to a cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL₂(𝔸ℙ¹) has stalk at x equal to the space of π-invariants under the local Hecke algebra at the legs. The trace of Frobenius on this stalk is the Hecke eigenvalue of π at the legs. Summing over all fixed points gives the trace formula identity that underlies Lafforgue's proof: the alternating sum of traces on cohomology equals the sum over automorphic representations of the product of Hecke eigenvalues, which by the converse theorem determines the Galois representation r_π.
Exam Strategy: Shtuka Cohomology and the Function Field Langlands
When asked about the function field Langlands correspondence, distinguish clearly between Drinfeld's proof for GL₂ (using the moduli of shtukas for GL₂ and the Lefschetz trace formula on the Drinfeld upper half-plane) and Lafforgue's proof for GL_n (using the moduli stack of shtukas for GL_n and the excision method). For general G, emphasize Vincent Lafforgue's use of the geometric Satake equivalence and the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration. Remember that the moduli stack of shtukas is not proper; the Drinfeld compactification adds boundary strata corresponding to Levi subgroups, and the excision sequence in cohomology is the key inductive tool. The purity of the Galois representations follows from Deligne's Weil II applied to the ℓ-adic cohomology of the shtuka stack.

Étale Cohomology of Igusa Varieties and the Kottwitz Conjecture

Igusa varieties are the p-adic analogues of Shimura varieties at infinite level at p. For a Shimura datum (G, X) with G unramified at p and a neat level structure K^p away from p, the Igusa variety IgK^p is the inverse limit over m of the Igusa towers IgK^p, m which parametrize abelian varieties with G-structure together with a trivialization of their p^m-torsion compatible with the G-structure. The Igusa variety is a perfectoid space in the sense of Scholze, and its étale cohomology Hⁱ_ét(IgK^p, ℚₗ) carries an action of G(𝔸_f^p) × J_b(ℚₚ) × Wℚₚ, where J_b is the inner form of G associated to the Newton stratum. The Kottwitz conjecture (proved by Shin, and in full generality by Caraiani–Scholze using perfectoid methods) describes the cohomology of Igusa varieties in terms of automorphic representations of G and Galois representations. Specifically, the cohomology of the Igusa variety for the basic Newton stratum realizes the local Langlands correspondence for the inner form J_b, while the cohomology of the whole Igusa tower (including non-basic strata) realizes the global Langlands correspondence for G. The nearby cycles complex on the special fibre of the Shimura variety is related to the cohomology of the Igusa variety via the Rapoport–Zink uniformization. The weight spectral sequence for the Igusa variety degenerates at E₂, and the monodromy operator N corresponds to the boundary map in the weight spectral sequence. The Kottwitz conjecture implies that the cohomology of Shimura varieties at infinite level at p is a direct sum over automorphic representations π of G(𝔸_f) of π^p ⊗ r_π|Wℚₚ ⊗ π_p, where π_p is the local component at p and r_π is the associated Galois representation. This provides a geometric realization of the p-adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ) via the work of Colmez and the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚₚ) via the work of Emerton and others.

The Étale Cohomology of the Hitchin Fibration and the Proof of the Fundamental Lemma

The Fundamental Lemma in the Langlands program, conjectured by Langlands and Shelstad, is an identity between orbital integrals on a reductive group G and stable orbital integrals on its endoscopic groups H. Ngô Bảo Châu's proof (2008) uses the étale cohomology of the Hitchin fibration for the group G over a curve X over a finite field. The Hitchin fibration h: M_G → A_G maps the moduli stack M_G of G-Higgs bundles on X to the Hitchin base A_G, an affine space of invariant polynomials. The generic fibre of h is an abelian variety (the Prym variety of the spectral curve), and the singular fibres are compactified Jacobians. The decomposition theorem of Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber applied to the proper map h (after suitable compactification) gives a direct sum decomposition of the pushforward complex Rh_*ℚₗ into shifted perverse sheaves on A_G. The support of these perverse sheaves corresponds to the endoscopic groups of G. The key geometric input is the "support theorem" of Ngô: the support of the perverse cohomology sheaves ^pHⁱ(Rh_*ℚₗ) is a union of closures of Hitchin bases for endoscopic groups H. The stalks of these perverse sheaves at regular semisimple points are computed by the cohomology of the fibres, which are related to orbital integrals via the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula. The equality of orbital integrals (the Fundamental Lemma) follows from the equality of the traces of Frobenius on the stalks of the perverse sheaves for G and for its endoscopic groups H, which in turn follows from the fact that the perverse sheaves are the intersection cohomology complexes of the supports, and their stalks are given by the cohomology of the corresponding affine Springer fibres. The proof uses the weighted homogeneous structure of the Hitchin base (the ℂ*-action) to apply the purity theorem of Deligne, ensuring that the perverse sheaves are pure of the expected weight. The étale cohomology of the Hitchin fibration also provides a geometric realization of the endoscopic transfer of automorphic forms via the trace formula for the moduli stack of Higgs bundles.

Étale Cohomology of the Lubin–Tate Tower: Realization of the Local Langlands and Jacquet–Langlands Correspondences

The Lubin–Tate tower is the inverse system of rigid analytic spaces LT_n over ℚₚ parametrizing deformations of a fixed formal group law of height n over 𝔽ₚ together with a Drinfeld level structure of level p^n. The tower is acted upon by GL_n(ℚₚ) (via change of level structure), by the Weil group Wℚₚ (via the action on the formal group law), and by the division algebra D_n^× of invariant 1/n (via the endomorphisms of the formal group). The étale cohomology Hⁱ_ét(LT_∞, ℚₗ) for ℓ ≠ p carries a smooth action of GL_n(ℚₚ) × D_n^× × Wℚₚ. The Harris–Taylor theorem (2001) proves that the alternating sum ∑ (-1)ⁱ Hⁱ_ét(LT_∞, ℚₗ) realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚₚ) and the local Jacquet–Langlands correspondence between GL_n(ℚₚ) and D_n^×. Specifically, for an irreducible supercuspidal representation π of GL_n(ℚₚ), the π-isotypic component of the cohomology is π ⊗ rec(π) ⊗ JL(π), where rec(π) is the n-dimensional representation of Wℚₚ corresponding to π under the local Langlands correspondence, and JL(π) is the representation of D_n^× corresponding to π under the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence. The proof uses the geometry of the Lubin–Tate tower, the nearby cycles complex on its special fibre (which is a union of projective spaces intersecting according to the Bruhat–Tits building of GL_n), and the weight spectral sequence of Rapoport–Zink. The cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower also realizes the local Langlands correspondence for non-supercuspidal representations via the work of Boyer, Dat, and others, using the compactification of the tower by Drinfeld upper half-spaces. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower is a key ingredient in the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over p-adic fields, and its p-adic analogue (the p-adic cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower) is used in the p-adic local Langlands correspondence of Colmez and others.

  • Thinking the Kottwitz conjecture describes the cohomology of Shimura varieties at finite level; it describes the cohomology at infinite level at p (Igusa varieties), and the passage to finite level involves the action of the Hecke algebra at p.
  • Believing the Fundamental Lemma is a statement about orbital integrals on the group G alone; it is an identity between orbital integrals on G and stable orbital integrals on its endoscopic groups H, and Ngô's proof uses the Hitchin fibration for G, not for H.
  • Overlooking the role of the weight filtration and purity in the proof of the Fundamental Lemma; the ℂ*-action on the Hitchin base and Deligne's Weil II are essential to ensure the perverse sheaves are pure and their stalks compute orbital integrals correctly.
  • Confusing the local Langlands correspondence (a bijection between representations of GL_n(ℚₚ) and n-dimensional representations of Wℚₚ) with the local Jacquet–Langlands correspondence (a bijection between representations of GL_n(ℚₚ) and representations of D_n^×); the Lubin–Tate tower realizes both simultaneously.
  • Assuming the étale cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower is concentrated in degree n-1; it is non-zero in degrees 0 to 2n-2, and the alternating sum is what realizes the correspondences.

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of G-Bundles on Curves and the Geometric Satake Equivalence for Loop Groups

The moduli stack BunG of G-bundles on a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽q is a central object in the geometric Langlands program. Its ℓ-adic cohomology carries an action of the Hecke operators, which are correspondences defined by modifying the G-bundle at a point of X. The geometric Satake equivalence, proved by Ginzburg, Mirković, and Vilonen, identifies the category of spherical perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian GrG with the category of finite-dimensional representations of the Langlands dual group LG. This equivalence is realized geometrically via the convolution product on the affine Grassmannian, which corresponds to the tensor product of representations of LG. The Hecke operators on BunG are constructed using the affine Grassmannian: for a dominant coweight λ, the Hecke correspondence Heckeλ → BunG × X has fibres isomorphic to the Schubert variety Grλ in GrG. The ℓ-adic cohomology of BunG decomposes under the action of the Hecke algebra, and the geometric Langlands conjecture predicts that the irreducible constituents correspond to automorphic representations of G(𝔸X) and to LG-local systems on X. The cohomology of BunG is not finite-dimensional, but the compactly supported cohomology of the open substack of semistable bundles (when G = GLn) or the intersection cohomology of the Uhlenbeck compactification can be studied. The work of Lafforgue on the global Langlands correspondence for GLn over function fields uses the cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas, which is closely related to BunGLn. For general reductive G, the geometric Langlands program seeks to construct Hecke eigensheaves on BunG from LG-local systems, and the étale cohomology of BunG provides the natural setting for this construction. The Beilinson-Drinfeld Grassmannian, a family of affine Grassmannians over X, globalizes the local geometric Satake equivalence and allows the definition of Hecke operators as functors on the derived category of ℓ-adic sheaves on BunG. The factorization property of the Beilinson-Drinfeld Grassmannian reflects the tensor structure of Rep(LG) and is essential for the construction of the geometric Langlands correspondence.

The Étale Cohomology of Local Shtukas and the Local Langlands Correspondence for Function Fields

Local shtukas are the function field analogues of p-divisible groups and Lubin-Tate spaces. For a local field F = 𝔽q((𝑡)) of positive characteristic, a local shtuka for a reductive group G over F is a G-bundle on the formal disc Spec 𝔽q[[𝑡]] together with an isomorphism outside the origin, satisfying a boundedness condition. The moduli space of local shtukas with level structure is a tower of rigid analytic spaces (or adic spaces) over the completion of the maximal unramified extension of F. These spaces are acted upon by G(F) (via change of level structure), by the Weil group WF (via the action on the local field), and by the group of automorphisms of the local shtuka. The ℓ-adic cohomology of this tower, for ℓ ≠ p, carries a smooth action of G(F) × WF × Jb(F), where Jb is the inner form of G associated to the local shtuka datum. The local Langlands correspondence for function fields, proved by Laumon, Rapoport, and Stuhler for GLn and by Varshavsky for general reductive groups, states that the alternating sum of the cohomology groups realizes the correspondence between irreducible smooth representations of G(F) and Langlands parameters (representations of WF into LG). Specifically, for an irreducible supercuspidal representation π of G(F), the π-isotypic component of the cohomology is π ⊗ rec(π) ⊗ JL(π), where rec(π) is the Langlands parameter and JL(π) is the representation of the inner form Jb(F) corresponding to π under the local Jacquet-Langlands correspondence. The proof uses the geometry of the local shtuka space, its special fibre (a union of projective varieties intersecting according to the Bruhat-Tits building of G), and the weight spectral sequence. The nearby cycles complex on the special fibre computes the cohomology of the generic fibre, and the purity of the perverse sheaves (ensured by the ℂ*-action on the Hitchin base in the global setting, or by the weight arguments of Deligne in the local setting) guarantees that the stalks compute the correct orbital integrals. The local shtuka spaces also provide a geometric realization of the local Langlands correspondence for non-supercuspidal representations via the compactification by Drinfeld upper half-spaces. The étale cohomology of local shtukas is the function field counterpart of the Lubin-Tate tower in the p-adic setting, and the two theories are unified by the theory of perfectoid spaces and the Fargues-Fontaine curve.

Worked Comparison: Lubin-Tate Tower vs Local Shtuka Space
Compare the group actions on the ℓ-adic cohomology of the Lubin-Tate tower LT∞ over ℚp and the local shtuka space Sht∞ over 𝔽q((𝑡)). Identify the three groups acting in each case and explain the role of the inner form.
Lubin-Tate tower (p-adic case): The cohomology Hiét(LT∞, ℚℓ) carries a smooth action of GLn(ℚp) × Dn× × Wℚp. Here GLn(ℚp) acts via change of Drinfeld level structure, Dn× is the division algebra of invariant 1/n (the inner form of GLn), acting via endomorphisms of the formal group law, and Wℚp acts via its action on the formal group law. The alternating sum realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GLn(ℚp) and the local Jacquet-Langlands correspondence between GLn(ℚp) and Dn×. Local shtuka space (function field case): The cohomology Hiét(Sht∞, ℚℓ) carries a smooth action of G(F) × Jb(F) × WF. The Jacquet-Langlands correspondence matches representations of the split group (GLn or G) with representations of the inner form. The cohomology realizes both correspondences simultaneously: the π-isotypic component for the split group contains the Langlands parameter and the corresponding representation of the inner form.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing Local and Global Shtukas
Questions often confuse global shtukas (moduli stack ShtG,r over Xr, used by Lafforgue for global Langlands) with local shtukas (tower over a local field, used for local Langlands). Remember: global shtukas have legs at points of a curve X and involve the fundamental group of X; local shtukas are defined over a local field F and involve the Weil group WF. The moduli stack of global shtukas is not proper (requires Drinfeld compactification); the local shtuka tower is a rigid analytic space with a proper special fibre. The group actions differ: global shtukas have Hecke actions at the legs; local shtukas have G(F) × Jb(F) × WF actions.

Étale Cohomology and the p-adic Simpson Correspondence: The Ogus-Vologodsky Correspondence and the Cartier Transform

The classical Simpson correspondence relates Higgs bundles and local systems on a smooth projective variety over ℂ. In positive characteristic, the situation is more subtle due to the presence of the Frobenius morphism. The Ogus-Vologodsky correspondence (also called the p-adic Simpson correspondence or the Cartier transform) provides an equivalence between certain categories of Higgs bundles and flat connections on a smooth variety X over a perfect field k of characteristic p > 0. Let F: X → X(p) be the relative Frobenius morphism. The Cartier transform is an equivalence of categories between the category of nilpotent Higgs bundles of exponent < p on X and the category of nilpotent flat connections of exponent < p on X(p). This equivalence is constructed using the inverse Cartier transform C−1, which sends a Higgs bundle (E, θ) to the flat connection (F*E, ∇can + F*θ), where ∇can is the canonical connection on F*OX. The étale cohomology enters through the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence in positive characteristic, which relates flat connections to ℓ-adic local systems (for ℓ ≠ p). The work of Ogus and Vologodsky shows that the de Rham cohomology of a nilpotent flat connection of exponent < p can be computed via the Higgs cohomology of the corresponding Higgs bundle, and this comparison is compatible with the action of Frobenius. The correspondence extends to the derived setting, giving an equivalence between the derived category of coherent sheaves on the cotangent bundle T*X (with a certain support condition) and the derived category of D-modules on X(p). This has profound implications for the étale cohomology of moduli spaces of Higgs bundles in positive characteristic. The Hitchin fibration in characteristic p, studied by Lan-Sheng-Zuo and others, exhibits a p-curvature map from the moduli space of Higgs bundles to the moduli space of flat connections, which is a finite morphism of degree pdim. The étale cohomology of the fibres of the Hitchin fibration (affine Springer fibres in the global setting) can be analyzed using the Cartier transform, and the purity of the perverse sheaves (essential for Ngô's proof of the Fundamental Lemma) is related to the behavior of the Frobenius morphism on the Hitchin base. The p-adic Simpson correspondence of Faltings and others (for p-adic fields) is a different but related theory, using Fontaine's period rings to compare p-adic étale cohomology with de Rham cohomology and Higgs cohomology.

The Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Space of p-Divisible Groups and the Rapoport-Zink Spectral Sequence

The moduli space of p-divisible groups (or Barsotti-Tate groups) over a perfect field k of characteristic p is a formal scheme that classifies deformations of a given p-divisible group. For a fixed p-divisible group X0 over k of height h and dimension d, the universal deformation space is a formal scheme M over the ring of Witt vectors W(k), formally smooth of dimension d(h−d). The Rapoport-Zink space is the rigid analytic generic fibre of this formal scheme (or its adic space version), and it parametrizes p-divisible groups with a quasi-isogeny to X0. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the Rapoport-Zink space (for ℓ ≠ p) carries an action of the group Jb(ℚp) of self-quasi-isogenies of X0 (an inner form of GLh), the group GLh(ℚp) (via the level structure), and the Weil group Wℚp. The Rapoport-Zink spectral sequence (or weight spectral sequence) computes the ℓ-adic cohomology of the generic fibre in terms of the cohomology of the special fibre. The special fibre is a union of projective varieties (Deligne-Lusztig varieties for GLh) intersecting according to the Bruhat-Tits building of GLh. The spectral sequence degenerates at E2 and the terms are given by the cohomology of the strata, which are products of Deligne-Lusztig varieties. The action of the Hecke operators on the cohomology of the Rapoport-Zink space is related to the geometry of the special fibre and the Bruhat-Tits building. The cohomology of the Rapoport-Zink space realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GLh(ℚp) and the local Jacquet-Langlands correspondence between GLh(ℚp) and Jb(ℚp). This is the p-adic analogue of the Lubin-Tate tower (which corresponds to the case d = 1, h = n, b = 1). For general d and h, the Rapoport-Zink space uniformizes the p-adic neighbourhoods in the Shimura variety for GU(d, h−d) or GSp2g. The étale cohomology of the moduli space of p-divisible groups with level structure is also related to the cohomology of the Igusa variety, which is the limit of the moduli spaces as the level at p goes to infinity. The Kottwitz conjecture describes the cohomology of the Igusa variety (or the Rapoport-Zink space at infinite level) in terms of automorphic forms.

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Shtukas with Level Structure and the Arthur-Selberg Trace Formula

The moduli stack of shtukas with level structure is a refinement of the moduli stack of shtukas used by Lafforgue to prove the global Langlands correspondence for GLn over function fields. For a smooth projective curve X over 𝔽q, a reductive group G over 𝔽q, and a level structure given by an open compact subgroup K ⊂ G(𝔸X∞) of the finite adeles, the moduli stack ShtG,r,K parametrizes G-shtukas with r legs (modifications at r points of X) together with a K-level structure. This stack is a Deligne-Mumford stack of finite type over 𝔽q, but it is not proper. The Drinfeld compactification (or the Lafforgue compactification) adds degenerate shtukas (with modifications at a divisor) to obtain a proper stack. The ℓ-adic cohomology of this compactified stack carries an action of the Hecke algebra at the legs, the group G(𝔸X∞) (via the level structure), and the Galois group of the function field. The Arthur-Selberg trace formula for function fields is an identity between the spectral side (sum over automorphic representations) and the geometric side (sum over conjugacy classes in G(𝔸X)). Lafforgue's proof of the global Langlands correspondence uses the Grothendieck-Lefschetz trace formula applied to the moduli stack of shtukas: the trace of Frobenius on the cohomology of ShtG,r,K computes the geometric side of the trace formula, while the decomposition of the cohomology under the Hecke algebra gives the spectral side. The excision argument (removing the boundary of the compactification) is crucial and relies on the fact that the boundary strata are themselves moduli stacks of shtukas for Levi subgroups of G. The cohomology of the boundary is related to the cohomology of the interior via the weight spectral sequence, and the purity of the perverse sheaves (ensured by the ℂ*-action on the Hitchin base in the geometric Langlands setting, or by Deligne's Weil II in the arithmetic setting) guarantees that the traces of Frobenius on the stalks compute the correct orbital integrals. The level structure K allows one to isolate specific automorphic representations and to prove the correspondence for all representations, not just those that are unramified everywhere. The étale cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas with level structure is the geometric incarnation of the automorphic spectrum for G over the function field of X, and its study via the trace formula is the function field analogue of the Arthur-Selberg trace formula for number fields.

  • Assuming the Ogus-Vologodsky correspondence works for all Higgs bundles; it requires nilpotence of exponent < p, and the general correspondence in characteristic p involves the Frobenius twist and the Cartier transform, not a direct equivalence between all Higgs bundles and all flat connections.
  • Thinking the moduli stack of G-bundles BunG has finite-dimensional cohomology; it does not, and one must work with compactly supported cohomology of semistable loci, intersection cohomology of compactifications (Uhlenbeck, Drinfeld-Lafforgue), or the cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas which is proper after compactification.
  • Confusing the local Langlands correspondence for function fields (realized by local shtukas, groups G(F) × Jb(F) × WF) with the global Langlands correspondence (realized by global shtukas, groups G(𝔸) × Galois group); the local version uses the Weil group of a local field, the global version uses the Galois group of the function field.

The level structure makes the stack a Deligne-Mumford stack of finite type, allowing the use of ℓ-adic cohomology and the trace formula.

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Parabolic Higgs Bundles and the Non-abelian Hodge Correspondence with Parabolic Structure

The moduli stack of parabolic Higgs bundles on a smooth projective curve X over an algebraically closed field k parametrizes Higgs bundles (E, φ) equipped with a weighted flag structure at a finite set of marked points D ⊂ X. The parabolic weights lie in [0, 1) and satisfy compatibility conditions with the Higgs field φ. The moduli stack Mpar(G, D, α) for a reductive group G and weight data α is an Artin stack of finite type, typically non-proper. Its ℓ-adic cohomology carries actions of the mapping class group of (X, D) and the Hecke algebra at the marked points. The non-abelian Hodge correspondence with parabolic structure, established by Simpson, Biquard, and others, gives a homeomorphism between the moduli space of parabolic Higgs bundles and the moduli space of parabolic flat connections (or equivalently, representations of the fundamental group of X D with prescribed local monodromy). In characteristic p > 0, the Ogus-Vologodsky correspondence extends to the parabolic setting: the Cartier transform gives an equivalence between the category of nilpotent parabolic Higgs bundles of exponent < p and the category of parabolic flat connections with nilpotent p-curvature. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the compactified moduli stack (via the parabolic analogue of the Uhlenbeck or Drinfeld-Lafforgue compactification) decomposes under the action of the Hecke operators, and the resulting Hecke eigensheaves correspond to parabolic local systems. The weight filtration on the cohomology reflects the stratification by Harder-Narasimhan type, and the purity of the intersection cohomology complexes on the compactification is essential for the geometric Langlands correspondence with ramification. The parabolic Hitchin fibration h : Mpar → Apar to the parabolic Hitchin base is proper and its generic fiber is an abelian variety (the parabolic Prym variety). The decomposition theorem for h yields a direct sum decomposition of Rh*ℚℓ into shifted perverse sheaves, and the support theorem identifies the supports with the closures of the loci where the spectral curve has prescribed singularities. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the parabolic Hitchin fiber computes the space of conformal blocks for the associated WZW model at critical level, linking geometric Langlands to conformal field theory.

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Flat Connections with Irregular Singularities and the Wild Ramification Case

When the connection has irregular singularities (wild ramification), the moduli space of flat connections is no longer a smooth algebraic variety but a formal or rigid analytic space. The Deligne-Malgrange theory of irregular connections introduces the Stokes data: the formal monodromy, the exponential factors (irregular type), and the Stokes matrices. The moduli stack of irregular connections Mirr(G, D, Λ) for a reductive group G, divisor D, and irregular type Λ is constructed as a stack over the category of schemes or as a rigid analytic space. Its ℓ-adic cohomology (or rather, the cohomology of its associated analytic space) carries an action of the wild fundamental group, which is a quotient of the fundamental group of the punctured formal disk. The geometric Langlands correspondence for wild ramification, formulated by Frenkel-Gross and developed by Heinloth-Ngô-Yun and others, predicts a correspondence between Hecke eigensheaves on the moduli stack of G-bundles with wild ramification and irregular LG-connections. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the compactified moduli stack of wild G-bundles (the Drinfeld-Lafforgue compactification for wild ramification) is expected to realize this correspondence. The nearby cycles functor for the irregular Hitchin fibration relates the cohomology of the moduli space of irregular Higgs bundles to that of irregular connections. The irregular Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, established by Boalch and others, gives an isomorphism between the moduli space of irregular connections and a symplectic quotient of a product of Stokes groups. The ℓ-adic cohomology of this symplectic quotient computes the wild character variety. In characteristic p, the theory of p-adic irregular connections (Kedlaya, Liu) uses the Robba ring and (φ, ∇)-modules. The étale cohomology of the associated (φ, ∇)-module spaces relates to the p-adic Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) and beyond. The weight monodromy conjecture for irregular connections predicts that the weight filtration on the cohomology of the nearby cycles complex coincides with the monodromy filtration, generalizing the regular singular case proved by Deligne.

Worked Problem: Parabolic vs. Irregular Ramification in Geometric Langlands
Explain the key structural difference between the moduli stack of parabolic Higgs bundles (tame ramification) and the moduli stack of irregular Higgs bundles (wild ramification) that affects the applicability of the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration.
In the tame (parabolic) case, the Hitchin fibration h : Mpar → Apar is a proper morphism of algebraic stacks (after suitable compactification), and the generic fiber is an abelian variety. The decomposition theorem of Beilinson-Bernstein-Deligne-Gabber applies directly, yielding a direct sum decomposition of Rh*ℚℓ into shifted perverse sheaves on Apar. In the wild (irregular) case, the irregular Hitchin fibration is not a morphism of algebraic varieties; the base Airr is a formal scheme or rigid analytic space, and the total space is not proper in the algebraic sense. The decomposition theorem does not apply in its classical form. Instead, one must work with the nearby cycles functor for the formal Hitchin fibration or use the theory of perverse sheaves on rigid analytic spaces (as developed by Huber, Scholze, and others). The Stokes phenomenon introduces non-algebraic gluing data (Stokes matrices) that prevent the fibration from being algebraic. The geometric Langlands correspondence for wild ramification therefore requires a different categorical framework, typically involving ind-coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of irregular LG-connections or D-modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles with wild level structure.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing Tame and Wild Ramification
When a question mentions "parabolic structure," "weighted flags," or "local monodromy in a fixed conjugacy class," it refers to tame ramification. When it mentions "irregular type," "Stokes matrices," "exponential factors," or "wild fundamental group," it refers to wild ramification. The decomposition theorem applies to the tame Hitchin fibration (proper, algebraic). For wild ramification, the key tools are the irregular Riemann-Hilbert correspondence (Boalch), the nearby cycles functor for formal schemes, and the theory of (φ, ∇)-modules in characteristic p. Do not conflate the two settings; the cohomological techniques differ fundamentally.

Étale Cohomology and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence for Non-connected Reductive Groups

The classical geometric Langlands correspondence focuses on connected reductive groups G. For non-connected groups (e.g., O(n), Pin(n), or the normalizer of a torus in a connected group), the correspondence must be modified. The Langlands dual of a non-connected group G is a 2-group or a crossed module LG = (LG0 → π0(G)), where LG0 is the dual of the identity component G0. The moduli stack BunG of G-bundles on a curve X has connected components indexed by π1(G) (or π0(G) for the stack of bundles with fixed topological type). The ℓ-adic cohomology of BunG decomposes as a direct sum over these components. The Hecke operators for G involve modifications that can change the topological type, so the Hecke algebra acts across components. The geometric Langlands correspondence for non-connected groups, formulated by Yun, Chen, and others, predicts an equivalence between the derived category of ℓ-adic sheaves on BunG (with appropriate conditions at infinity) and the derived category of ind-coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocLG of LG-local systems, where LG is the dual 2-group. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the Hitchin fibration for non-connected groups involves the cameral cover associated to the non-connected group, which is a torsor under a non-connected group scheme. The endoscopic decomposition of the cohomology involves not only the elliptic endoscopic groups of G0 but also the "non-connected endoscopic data" classified by L-embeddings of the dual 2-group. The fundamental lemma for non-connected groups (proved by Chaudouard and Laumon for the Lie algebra version, and by Yun for the group version) is required to stabilize the trace formula. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the moduli stack of G-shtukas for non-connected G realizes the global Langlands correspondence for function fields in this setting, with the spectral side involving automorphic representations of the non-connected group G(𝔸).

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of G-Bundles on Surfaces and Higher Dimensional Varieties

For a smooth projective variety X of dimension ≥ 2, the moduli stack BunG(X) of G-bundles is an Artin stack locally of finite type but typically not of finite type, and its ℓ-adic cohomology is not finite-dimensional. To obtain a well-behaved cohomological theory, one restricts to semistable bundles (with respect to a fixed polarization) and considers the moduli space Mss(G, X) of S-equivalence classes of semistable G-bundles, which is a projective variety (or stack) with good geometric properties. The ℓ-adic cohomology of Mss(G, X) carries an action of the mapping class group of X and, when X is a surface, of the Hecke correspondences associated to curves on X. The Donaldson-Uhlenbeck-Yau theorem (for G = GLn) and its generalization by Simpson, Bando-Siu, and others identifies Mss(G, X) with the moduli space of Hermitian-Yang-Mills connections (or polystable Higgs bundles via non-abelian Hodge theory). The ℓ-adic cohomology of Mss(G, X) is expected to carry a representation of the motivic Galois group of X. For surfaces, the work of Hausel-Thaddeus, Markman, and others computes the Betti numbers of Mss(GLn, X) using the wall-crossing formula and the geometry of the Hitchin fibration on the surface. The ℓ-adic cohomology of the moduli stack of G-bundles on a surface with level structure along a divisor relates to the geometric Langlands program for surfaces, where the Hecke operators are indexed by curves on the surface. The Kapranov-Vasserot conjecture (proved in special cases) relates the cohomology of the moduli space of sheaves on a surface to the Fock space representation of a Heisenberg algebra. For higher dimensional varieties, the moduli stack of G-bundles is even more complicated, and the ℓ-adic cohomology is studied via the theory of factorization algebras and the geometric Satake correspondence for higher dimensional varieties (Gaitsgory-Lurie). The étale cohomology of the moduli stack of G-bundles on a variety X with a fixed topological type (classified by π1(G) and characteristic classes) is finite-dimensional when restricted to the semistable locus, and its weights are controlled by the Harder-Narasimhan stratification.

Étale Cohomology and the Arithmetic Langlands Program: The Taylor-Wiles Method and Deformation Rings

The Taylor-Wiles method, originally developed to prove the modularity of elliptic curves over ℚ (Wiles, Taylor-Wiles), uses the étale cohomology of Shimura varieties (modular curves) to construct Galois representations associated to automorphic forms. The key innovation is the "patching" argument: one considers the inverse limit of the étale cohomology groups H1(Y(UN), ℤp) over a tower of modular curves Y(UN) with level structure UN = Up ∩ Γ1(N) at p. The completed cohomology H̃1 = lim← H1(Y(UN), ℤp) is a module over the Hecke algebra 𝕋 and the Galois group Gℚ. The Taylor-Wiles method constructs a surjection from a universal deformation ring R (parametrizing lifts of a residual Galois representation ρ̄ : Gℚ → GLn(𝔽p)) to the Hecke algebra 𝕋m localized at a maximal ideal m corresponding to ρ̄. The numerical coincidence "R = 𝕋" is proved by comparing the lengths of these rings as modules over a power series ring 𝒪[[x1, …, xq]], where q is the number of Taylor-Wiles primes. The étale cohomology of the Shimura variety enters through the Eichler-Shimura isomorphism (for GL2) or the more general Langlands-Kottwitz method (for higher rank groups), which relates the Hecke action on cohomology to the Frobenius action on the Galois side. The Calegari-Geraghty method extends this to non-self-dual cases using the étale cohomology of Shimura varieties with torsion coefficients and the theory of perfect complexes. The Scholze's torsion classes in the cohomology of locally symmetric spaces (for GLn over CM fields) use the étale cohomology of the perfectoid Shimura variety at infinite level to construct Galois representations for torsion Hecke eigenclasses. The patching argument is performed on the level of derived categories of étale sheaves, and the resulting Galois representations are valued in GLn(R) where R is a deformation ring. The étale cohomology of the Rapoport-Zink space (the p-adic uniformization of the Shimura variety) provides the local Langlands correspondence at p via the Harris-Taylor and Boyer methods, which is essential for the local-global compatibility in the Taylor-Wiles method.

  • Confusing the completed cohomology (inverse limit over level) with the cohomology of the perfectoid Shimura variety at infinite level: The perfectoid space exists only when the Shimura variety has good reduction at p (or after passing to a pro-étale cover); completed cohomology is defined for any tower.
  • Overlooking the role of the numerical criterion (Wiles' numerical criterion) in the Taylor-Wiles method: The isomorphism R ≅ 𝕋 is not formal; it requires comparing the lengths of R and 𝕋 as modules over the Taylor-Wiles power series ring, which depends on the freeness of the patched module.
  • Assuming the étale cohomology of the moduli stack of parabolic Higgs bundles directly computes the space of conformal blocks without the compactification and the decomposition theorem: The non-compactness of the moduli stack means its cohomology is not pure; one must use the intersection cohomology of the compactification (Uhlenbeck or Drinfeld-Lafforgue) and the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration to isolate the summand corresponding to conformal blocks.

Étale Cohomology and the p-adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ)

The p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ) establishes a bijection between irreducible admissible representations of GL₂(ℚₚ) on p-adic Banach spaces and two-dimensional p-adic Galois representations of Gℚₚ. Unlike the classical local Langlands correspondence (which uses complex coefficients), the p-adic version requires the machinery of étale cohomology of modular curves and perfectoid spaces. The correspondence was constructed by Colmez (2010) using the completed cohomology of modular curves, building on work of Breuil, Berger, and Emerton. The key object is the completed cohomology group widetilde{H}1 = varprojlim_N H1ét(Y(Np^∞)_{overline{Q}_p}, Z_p) where Y(Np^∞) is the modular curve of level Np^∞. This is a module over the Hecke algebra T and the group GL₂(ℚₚ). The Colmez functor associates to a p-adic Galois representation V a locally analytic representation Π(V) of GL₂(ℚₚ) via the formula Π(V) = HomGQ_p(V, widetilde{H}1 ⊗Z_p Q_p). The étale cohomology enters through the comparison isomorphism between the étale cohomology of the modular curve and the de Rham cohomology of the universal elliptic curve, which is mediated by the p-adic Hodge theory of the period ring BdR. The Breuil-Schneider conjecture (proved by Colmez) identifies the locally analytic vectors in the unitary Banach space representation Π(V)an with the representation constructed via the (φ, Γ)-module Drig^dagger(V). The étale cohomology of the infinite-level modular curve (a perfectoid space) provides the geometric realization of the correspondence, and the Galois action on widetilde{H}1 recovers the p-adic Langlands correspondence for all two-dimensional trianguline representations.

Étale Cohomology and Iwasawa Theory for Elliptic Curves and Modular Forms

Iwasawa theory studies the growth of Selmer groups in p-adic towers of number fields, and étale cohomology provides the foundational framework. For an elliptic curve E/ℚ with good ordinary reduction at p, the p-primary Selmer group Selp^∞(E/Q_∞) over the cyclotomic ℤ_p-extension Q_∞/Q is a cofinitely generated module over the Iwasawa algebra Λ = Z_p[[Γ]] where Γ ≅ Z_p^×. The étale cohomology group H1ét(Q_∞, E[p^∞]) classifies Galois cohomology classes unramified outside p and satisfying local conditions at p. The control theorem of Mazur relates Selp^∞(E/Q_n) to Selp^∞(E/Q_∞)Γ_n where Q_n is the n-th layer. The characteristic ideal of the Pontryagin dual X = Selp^∞(E/Q_∞)^vee is conjectured to be generated by the p-adic L-function L_p(E, s) (the main conjecture of Iwasawa theory, proved by Kato, Skinner-Urban, and others). The étale cohomology of the modular curve X1(Np^∞) enters via the Euler system of Beilinson-Kato elements: the classes zm,n ∈ H1ét(Q(μ_m), T_p E) constructed from the étale cohomology of the modular curve satisfy norm-compatibility relations that bound the Selmer group. The p-adic L-function is constructed by interpolating the critical values L(E, chi, 1) for Dirichlet characters chi of p-power conductor, using the modular symbol map from H1ét(X1(Np^∞), Z_p(1)) to the space of p-adic measures. The μ-invariant of X vanishes (Ferrero-Washington for Q_∞/Q, generalized by Kato), and the λ-invariant equals the order of vanishing of L_p(E, s) at s=1. For modular forms f of weight k ≥ 2, the Selmer group is defined using the Galois representation V_f = H1ét(X1(N)_{overline{Q}}, Q_p)(k-1) and the étale cohomology of the Kuga-Sato variety over the modular curve provides the Euler system of Rankin-Selberg type.

Worked Example: Computing the Selmer Group via Étale Cohomology
Let E/ℚ be the elliptic curve y² = x³ - x (Cremona label 32a1) with good ordinary reduction at p=3. The 3-adic Tate module T3(E) is a free ℤ3-module of rank 2 with G_ℚ-action. Describe how the étale cohomology group H¹_ét(ℚ_∞, E[3^∞]) is used to define the Selmer group Sel3^∞(E/ℚ_∞) and state the control theorem for the cyclotomic ℤ3-extension ℚ_∞/ℚ.
The Selmer group Sel3^∞(E/ℚ_∞) is defined as the kernel of the global-to-local map in Galois cohomology: 0 → Sel3^∞(E/ℚ_∞) → H¹_ét(ℚ_∞, E[3^∞]) → ⊕_v H¹_ét(ℚ∞,v, E)[3^∞] / im(E(ℚ∞,v) ⊗ ℚ3/ℤ3). Here H¹_ét(ℚ_∞, E[3^∞]) = H¹(Gℚ_∞, E[3^∞]) classifies continuous 1-cocycles with values in the 3-power torsion of E. The local condition at v ≠ 3 is the unramified subgroup H¹ur(ℚ∞,v, E[3^∞]) = ker(H¹(ℚ∞,v, E[3^∞]) → H¹(I_v, E[3^∞])). At v=3, the ordinary condition uses the filtration 0 → F+ E[3^∞] → E[3^∞] → F- E[3^∞] → 0 coming from the formal group; the local condition is the preimage of H¹(ℚ∞,3, F- E[3^∞]). The control theorem (Mazur 1972) states that for each n, the restriction map Sel3^∞(E/ℚ_n) → Sel3^∞(E/ℚ_∞)Γ_n has finite kernel and cokernel bounded independently of n, where ℚ_n is the n-th layer of the cyclotomic ℤ3-extension and Γ_n = Gal(ℚ_∞/ℚ_n). This follows from the inflation-restriction sequence and the fact that H¹(Γ_n, E[3^∞]) and H²(Γ_n, E[3^∞]) are finite of bounded order.

Étale Cohomology of Diamonds and the v-Topology

The theory of diamonds, introduced by Scholze (2017), provides a geometric framework for the p-adic Langlands program and the cohomology of Shimura varieties at infinite level. A diamond is a sheaf on the category of perfectoid spaces (in characteristic p) for the v-topology (a Grothendieck topology where covers are given by universally open maps that are quasi-compact and surjective). The v-topology is finer than the pro-étale topology and allows for a well-behaved theory of étale cohomology for non-Noetherian spaces. For a perfectoid space X over C_p^flat, the associated diamond X^diamond represents the functor Y ↦ X(Y^sharp) where Y^sharp is the untilt of Y. The étale cohomology of a diamond X^diamond with coefficients in Z_p or Q_p is defined as the cohomology of the v-site with the constant sheaf. Scholze proved that for a smooth rigid analytic variety X over C_p, the v-cohomology of X^diamond with Z_p-coefficients agrees with the pro-étale cohomology and with the classical étale cohomology of X. The key advantage is that diamonds allow one to take quotients by non-free group actions: if a profinite group G acts on a perfectoid space X, the quotient X/G exists as a diamond even when the action is not free. This is essential for the infinite-level Shimura variety Sh_K(G, X)^diamond = varprojlim_K Sh_K(G, X)^diamond where K runs over compact open subgroups of G(A_f^p). The étale cohomology of this diamond computes the completed cohomology widetilde{H}^i = varprojlim_K H^iét(Sh_K(G, X)_{overline{Q}_p}, Z_p) and carries a continuous action of G(Q_p) × G(A_f^p) × Gal(overline{Q}_p/Q_p). The v-topology also supports a theory of solid sheaves (condensed mathematics) which provides a derived category Dsolid(X^diamond, Z_p) with a six-functor formalism. The nearby cycles functor for diamonds, constructed by Scholze and Weinstein, relates the étale cohomology of the generic fiber of a formal scheme to the étale cohomology of its special fiber, generalizing the classical nearby cycles of Deligne. The cohomology of the Fargues-Fontaine curve (a diamond) classifies vector bundles and provides the geometric Langlands correspondence for GL_n over p-adic fields via the B_dR+-affine Grassmannian.

Étale Cohomology and the Geometric Langlands Program for Kac-Moody Groups

The geometric Langlands program for Kac-Moody groups extends the classical geometric Langlands correspondence (for reductive groups) to loop groups and affine Kac-Moody groups. The étale cohomology of the affine Grassmannian Gr_G = G(F_q((t)))/G(F_q[[t]]) for a reductive group G over F_q carries an action of the loop group G(F_q((t))) and the Langlands dual group ^L G. The geometric Satake equivalence (Ginzburg, Mirković-Vilonen, Bezrukavnikov) identifies the category of G(F_q[[t]])-equivariant perverse sheaves on Gr_G with the category of representations of ^L G. For Kac-Moody groups, the affine Grassmannian is replaced by the ind-scheme Gr_{widehat{G}} for the affine Kac-Moody group widehat{G}, and the étale cohomology of its Schubert varieties computes the characters of integrable highest-weight representations. The categorical geometric Langlands correspondence (Gaitsgory, Rozenblyum) conjectures an equivalence between the derived category of D-modules on Bun_G (the moduli stack of G-bundles on a curve X) and the derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves on LocSys^L G (the moduli stack of ^L G-local systems). In the ℓ-adic setting, this becomes an equivalence between the derived category of ℓ-adic sheaves on Bun_G and the derived category of ind-coherent sheaves on LocSys^L G. The étale cohomology of the Hitchin fibration h: Bun_G → A (where A is the Hitchin base) plays a central role: the decomposition theorem for h (proved by Ngô for the fundamental lemma) implies that the pushforward h_* Q_ℓ decomposes as a direct sum of intersection cohomology complexes of the fibers. The spectral decomposition of the Hitchin system relates the cohomology of Bun_G to the cohomology of the spectral curve widetilde{X}_a → X. For Kac-Moody groups, the moduli stack Bun_{widehat{G}} is replaced by the moduli stack of widehat{G}-bundles with a level structure, and the étale cohomology of the corresponding affine flag variety computes the fusion product of representations. The work of Braverman-Finkelberg-Nakajima on the Coulomb branch of 3d N=4 gauge theories provides a physical interpretation: the étale cohomology of the moduli space of widehat{G}-monopoles (a symplectic resolution of the affine Grassmannian) carries an action of the quantum group U_q(^L widehat{G}) at a root of unity.

Étale Cohomology and the Arithmetic of Abelian Varieties: The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture

The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture relates the arithmetic of an abelian variety A/ℚ to the analytic behavior of its L-function L(A, s) at s=1. Étale cohomology provides the Galois representations that encode the arithmetic data. The p-adic Tate module T_p A = varprojlim_n A[p^n] is a free Z_p-module of rank 2g (where g = dim A) with a continuous action of G_ℚ. The Galois representation V_p A = T_p A ⊗ Q_p is realized in the étale cohomology H1ét(A_{overline{Q}}, Q_p). The Selmer group Selp^∞(A/Q) is defined as the kernel of H1(G_ℚ, A[p^∞]) → ⊕_v H1(GQ_v, A)[p^∞] / im(A(Q_v) ⊗ Q_p/Z_p). The BSD conjecture predicts that ords=1 L(A, s) = rank A(Q) and that the leading coefficient L^*(A, 1) equals frac{Ω_A · Reg_A · Π_v c_v · #Ш(A/Q)}{#A(Q)tors2} where Ω_A is the real period, Reg_A is the regulator, c_v are Tamagawa numbers, and Ш(A/Q) is the Tate-Shafarevich group. The p-part of Ш(A/Q) is the cokernel of the global-to-local map in the Selmer group exact sequence. Kato's Euler system (constructed from the étale cohomology of the modular curve X1(N) via the Beilinson-Kato elements) proves one divisibility in the main conjecture of Iwasawa theory, which implies the p-part of BSD for modular elliptic curves when L(E, 1) ≠ 0. The work of Skinner-Urban (2014) proves the other divisibility for elliptic curves with good ordinary reduction at p and surjective mod p representation, establishing the full main conjecture and hence the p-part of BSD for a density-one set of elliptic curves. The étale cohomology of the Kuga-Sato variety over the modular curve provides the Euler system for higher weight modular forms. The p-adic BSD conjecture (Mazur-Tate-Teitelbaum) formulates a p-adic analogue using the p-adic L-function L_p(A, s) constructed from the étale cohomology of the modular curve at infinite level. The leading term of L_p(A, s) at s=1 is conjectured to equal the p-adic regulator times the order of the p-primary part of Ш(A/Q) divided by the product of Tamagawa numbers.

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Stable Maps and Gromov-Witten Theory

The moduli stack M̄g,n(X, β) of stable maps from n-pointed genus g curves to a smooth projective variety X with curve class β ∈ H2(X, ℤ) carries a virtual fundamental class [M̄g,n(X, β)]vir in its ℓ-adic étale cohomology (or Chow group) constructed by Behrend-Fantechi (1997) via the intrinsic normal cone. For X defined over a finite field 𝔽q, the ℓ-adic cohomology H•ét(M̄g,n(X, β)𝔽̄q, ℚℓ) carries a Frobenius action whose trace computes the number of 𝔽q-points of the stack, yielding a cohomological interpretation of Gromov-Witten invariants. The virtual class is obtained by applying the refined Gysin map to the intrinsic normal cone 𝔠M/X ∈ A•(CM/X) where CM/X is the intrinsic normal stack. When X is a Calabi-Yau threefold, the genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants N0,β = ∫[M̄0,0(X,β)]vir 1 satisfy the Aspinwall-Morrison formula relating them to the prepotential of the associated topological string theory. Over ℂ, the comparison theorem identifies H•ét(M̄g,n(X, β), ℚℓ) with singular cohomology of the analytic stack, and the virtual class corresponds to the Li-Tian virtual cycle in symplectic geometry. The quantum cohomology ring QH•(X) = H•(X, ℚ) ⊗ ℚ[[H2(X, ℤ)]] with product α ⋆ β = Σβ (ev1*α ∪ ev2*β ∪ [M̄0,3(X, β)]vir) qβ is realized via the étale cohomology of the moduli stack of stable maps with three marked points. The WDVV associativity equations for the quantum product follow from the gluing morphisms M̄0,4(X, β) → M̄0,3(X, β1) ×X M̄0,3(X, β2) and the compatibility of virtual classes under pullback. For X = ℙr, the Kontsevich moduli space M̄0,n(ℙr, d) is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack, and its ℓ-adic cohomology is pure of Tate type; the Gromov-Witten invariants are computed by the localization formula of Graber-Pandharipande (1999) using the torus action on ℙr. The quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem (Coates-Givental, 2007) relates the quantum cohomology of a complete intersection Y ⊂ X to that of X via the twisted virtual class on M̄g,n(X, β) defined by the vector bundle R•π*f*OX(Y). In positive characteristic, the Frobenius morphism on M̄g,n(X, β) induces a p-adic valuation on Gromov-Witten invariants studied by Jun Li and others; the p-adic convergence of the quantum product is related to the Hodge-Witt cohomology of X.

The Motivic Tate Conjecture and Étale Motivic Cohomology

The Motivic Tate Conjecture for a smooth projective variety X over a finitely generated field k predicts that the cycle class map from motivic cohomology to étale cohomology CHr(X, 2r−n) ⊗ ℚℓ → Hnét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(r)) is surjective onto the Galois-invariant subspace Hnét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(r))Gk. This refines the classical Tate conjecture (which concerns only the case n=2r) by incorporating higher Chow groups CHr(X, m) defined by Bloch (1986) as the homology of the complex zr(X, •) of algebraic cycles on X × Δ• meeting faces properly. The étale motivic cohomology Hn,mét(X, ℤ/ℓr) is defined as the hypercohomology of the motivic complex ℤ/ℓr(m)ét on the étale site, constructed by Voevodsky (1995) using the Suslin-Voevodsky complex and the Nisnevich topology. The Beilinson-Lichtenbaum conjecture (proved by Voevodsky-Rost-Weibel, 2009-2011) asserts that the natural map ℤ/ℓr(m)ét → τ≤mRε*μℓr⊗m is a quasi-isomorphism, where ε is the map from the étale to the Nisnevich site. This identifies étale motivic cohomology with étale cohomology in degrees n ≤ m and with Milnor K-theory sheaves in degree n = m+1. The norm residue isomorphism theorem (Bloch-Kato conjecture) is the special case X = Spec(k), giving KMm(k)/ℓr ≅ Hmét(k, μℓr⊗m). For a smooth variety X over a finite field 𝔽q, the motivic Tate conjecture is equivalent to the injectivity of the cycle class map on the kernel of the Albanese map for higher Chow groups, and to the vanishing of the motivic cohomology groups H2r+1,r+1M(X, ℚℓ)tors. The work of Geisser-Levine (2001) relates the ℓ-adic étale cohomology of X to the motivic cohomology of the Suslin-Voevodsky motive M(X) in the triangulated category DMgm(k). The motivic Tate conjecture for abelian varieties follows from the Tate conjecture for divisors (proved by Faltings, 1983, for number fields; by Zarhin, 1975, for finite fields) and the Künneth decomposition of the motive. For K3 surfaces over finite fields, the motivic Tate conjecture in degree 3 is equivalent to the Tate conjecture for algebraic cycles of codimension 2, which is open in general but known for supersingular K3 surfaces (Nygaard-Ogus, 1985). The p-adic étale motivic cohomology Hn,mét(X, ℤp(m)) for X over a p-adic field is related to p-adic Hodge theory via the comparison isomorphism with syntomic cohomology (Fontaine-Messing, 1987; Tsuji, 1999; Nizioł, 2006). The motivic spectral sequence E2p,q = Hp-q,-qM(X, ℚℓ) ⇒ K-p-q(X) ⊗ ℚℓ connects motivic cohomology to algebraic K-theory, and the motivic Tate conjecture implies the surjectivity of the Chern character from K-theory to étale cohomology.

Worked Example: Computing the Virtual Dimension of M̄0,n(ℙr, d)
Let X = ℙr over an algebraically closed field. Compute the virtual dimension of the moduli stack M̄0,n(ℙr, d) of stable maps from n-pointed genus 0 curves to ℙr of degree d, and explain why the virtual class coincides with the fundamental class.
The virtual dimension is given by the Riemann-Roch formula for the obstruction theory: vdim = ∫β c1(TX) + (dim X − 3)(1 − g) + n. For X = ℙr, c1(Tℙr) = (r+1)H where H is the hyperplane class. The degree d curve class β satisfies ∫β H = d. With g = 0, dim X = r, we get vdim = (r+1)d + (r−3) + n = (r+1)d + r + n − 3. The moduli stack M̄0,n(ℙr, d) is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack of pure dimension equal to this virtual dimension (Kontsevich, 1995). The obstruction theory is perfect of amplitude [−1,0] and the obstruction sheaf vanishes because H1(C, f*Tℙr) = 0 for any stable map f: C → ℙr from a genus 0 curve (since f*Tℙr is a direct sum of line bundles of degree ≥ −2 on ℙ1 components, and H1(ℙ1, O(k)) = 0 for k ≥ −1). Thus the intrinsic normal cone is the zero section, and the virtual class [M̄0,n(ℙr, d)]vir equals the fundamental class [M̄0,n(ℙr, d)] in the Chow group (or ℓ-adic homology).

When asked about virtual fundamental classes, always state the three ingredients: (1) a perfect obstruction theory E• → LM of amplitude [−1,0], (2) the intrinsic normal cone 𝔠M ⊂ h1/h0(E•∨), (3) the refined Gysin map 0!: A•(h1/h0(E•∨)) → A•−vdim(M). For M̄g,n(X, β), the obstruction theory is Rπ*f*TX → LM where π: C → M is the universal curve and f: C → X the universal map. The virtual dimension is χ(C, f*TX) = ∫β c1(TX) + (dim X − 3)(1−g) + n. Memorize this formula and the smoothness criterion: if H1(C, f*TX) = 0 for all stable maps, the stack is smooth of the expected dimension and the virtual class is the fundamental class.

Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Parabolic Sheaves and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence for Parabolic Structures

The moduli stack BunG(X, D, λ) of G-bundles on a smooth projective curve X with parabolic structure at a divisor D = Σ xi and weights λ = (λi) carries a natural ℓ-adic cohomology theory that categorifies the parabolic Verlinde formula. For G = GLn, a parabolic bundle is a vector bundle E with a filtration Exi = F1 ⊃ F2 ⊃ … ⊃ Fr ⊃ 0 at each xi and weights 0 ≤ α1 < … < αr < 1. The moduli stack is an algebraic stack of finite type over the base field, smooth when the weights are generic. Its ℓ-adic cohomology H•ét(BunG(X, D, λ)k̄, ℚℓ) carries an action of the spherical Hecke algebra at points away from D and of the parabolic Hecke algebra at points of D. The geometric Langlands correspondence for parabolic structures (Beilinson-Drinfeld, 1991; Frenkel-Gaitsgory, 2006) predicts an equivalence between the derived category of ℓ-adic sheaves on BunG(X, D, λ) with nilpotent singular support and the derived category of D-modules on the moduli stack LocSysLG(X, D, λ) of LG-local systems with prescribed monodromy at D. The parabolic Hitchin fibration h: MDol(G, D, λ) → AD,λ from the moduli stack of parabolic Higgs bundles to the parabolic Hitchin base is a proper map whose generic fiber is a torsor over the parabolic Prym variety. The decomposition theorem for h (Ngô, 2010) yields a direct sum decomposition of Rh*ℚℓ into shifted perverse sheaves, and the support theorem identifies the supports as the global nilpotent cone and its intersections with the fibers. The parabolic version of the fundamental lemma (Chaudouard-Laumon, 2010) relates orbital integrals on the parabolic affine Grassmannian to stable orbital integrals on the endoscopic groups, and is proved using the étale cohomology of the parabolic Hitchin fibration. The parabolic Verlinde formula computes the dimension of the space of conformal blocks H0(BunG(X, D, λ), ℒk) where ℒk is the determinant line bundle at level k, and equals the trace of Frobenius on the ℓ-adic cohomology of the moduli stack over a finite field (Teleman, 1998; Beauville-Laszlo-Sorger, 1994). For G = SL2 and D = {x1, x2}, the moduli stack of parabolic bundles is isomorphic to the moduli stack of vector bundles on the nodal curve obtained by identifying x1 and x2, and its étale cohomology computes the fusion rules of the WZW model. The categorical geometric Langlands equivalence for parabolic structures is constructed by Gaitsgory (2015) using the Whittaker model and the Eisenstein series functor on the derived category of D-modules on BunG(X, D, λ), and the ℓ-adic realization is expected to be compatible with the Langlands duality for the parabolic Hecke operators.

The Étale Cohomology of the Moduli Stack of Higgs Bundles on Higher Dimensional Varieties

The moduli stack MDol(X, G) of semistable G-Higgs bundles on a smooth projective variety X of dimension d ≥ 2 (a pair (E, φ) where E is a principal G-bundle and φ ∈ H0(X, ad(E) ⊗ ΩX1)) has a natural ℓ-adic étale cohomology that generalizes the non-abelian Hodge correspondence of Simpson (1992) and Corlette (1988). For X a curve, the Hitchin fibration h: MDol → A = ⊕i H0(X, ΩXi+1) is a completely integrable system whose generic fiber is an abelian variety (the Prym variety of the spectral curve). For dim X ≥ 2, the Hitchin map h: MDol → A = ⊕i H0(X, ΩXi+1) is no longer Lagrangian with respect to the natural symplectic form, and the generic fiber is not an abelian variety but a more complicated algebraic variety. The ℓ-adic cohomology H•ét(MDol(X, G)k̄, ℚℓ) carries a perverse filtration P• induced by the Hitchin map, and the non-abelian Hodge theorem identifies the associated graded with the cohomology of the Betti moduli space MB(X, G) of representations π1(X) → G. The work of Hausel-Thaddeus (2003) for curves and de Cataldo-Hausel-Migliorini (2012) for higher dimensions establishes the topological mirror symmetry: the Hodge numbers of MDol and MB satisfy hp,q(MDol) = hp,q(MB). Over a finite field 𝔽q, the Frobenius trace on H•ét(MDol, ℚℓ) computes the number of 𝔽q-points, which by the Grothendieck-Lefschetz trace formula equals the number of semistable Higgs bundles. The support theorem for the Hitchin map (Ngô, 2010 for curves; Chaudouard-Laumon, 2016 for higher dimensions) describes the decomposition of Rh*ℚℓ into intersection cohomology complexes of the supports, which are the global nilpotent cone and its preimages under the Hitchin map. The categorical geometric Langlands correspondence for higher dimensional varieties (Gaitsgory-Rozenblyum, 2017) predicts an equivalence between the derived category of ℓ-adic sheaves on MDol(X, G) with nilpotent singular support and the derived category of D-modules on the moduli stack of LG-local systems on X. The p-adic non-abelian Hodge theory (Faltings, 2005; Liu-Zhu, 2017) relates the étale cohomology of MDol(X, G) over a p-adic field to the de Rham cohomology of the corresponding moduli stack of Higgs bundles via the p-adic Simpson correspondence. For X a surface, the moduli stack of Higgs bundles is related to the moduli stack of sheaves on the cotangent bundle T*X via the spectral construction, and its étale cohomology computes the Vafa-Witten invariants of X (Vafa-Witten, 1994; Tanaka-Thomas, 2017). The perverse filtration on H•ét(MDol, ℚℓ) is expected to coincide with the weight filtration on H•ét(MB, ℚℓ) under the non-abelian Hodge isomorphism, a conjecture of de Cataldo-Hausel-Migliorini known as the P=W conjecture (proved for curves by de Cataldo-Hausel-Migliorini, 2012; for higher dimensions open in general).

Étale Cohomology and the p-adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GLn(ℚp) via the Emerton-Gee Stack

The Emerton-Gee stack XG,≤h (Emerton-Gee, 2019) is a formal algebraic stack over ℤp whose ℓ-adic étale cohomology (for ℓ ≠ p) and pro-étale cohomology (for ℓ = p) realize the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GLn(ℚp). For G = GLn, the stack XGLn,≤h parametrizes (φ, Γ)-modules of rank n with Hodge-Tate weights bounded by h, or equivalently, Breuil-Kisin modules with descent data. The completed cohomology H̃i = lim←,K Hiét(XK, ℤp) of the tower of modular curves (or Shimura varieties for GLn) at infinite level carries a continuous action of GLn(ℚp) × Gal(ℚ̄p/ℚp). The Emerton-Gee stack provides a geometric incarnation of the category of (φ, Γ)-modules: there is an equivalence between the category of vector bundles on XGLn,≤h with a Frobenius structure and the category of étale (φ, Γ)-modules over the Robba ring. The p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) (Colmez, 2010) is realized in the completed cohomology of the modular curve at infinite level: for a 2-dimensional p-adic Galois representation ρ: Gal(ℚ̄p/ℚp) → GL2(E), the associated Banach space representation Π(ρ) of GL2(ℚp) is the ρ-isotypic component of H̃1. For GLn with n ≥ 3, the correspondence is not a bijection but a multi-valued map from Galois representations to unitary Banach space representations, and the Emerton-Gee stack organizes the fibers. The étale cohomology H•ét(XGLn,≤h, ℚℓ) for ℓ ≠ p carries an action of the Hecke algebra at p and the Galois group, and the local-global compatibility conjecture (Emerton, 2011) predicts that the ρ-isotypic component of the completed cohomology of a Shimura variety realizes the local Langlands correspondence at p. The stack XG,≤h is constructed as a quotient of a formal scheme by a group action, and its pro-étale cohomology with ℤp-coefficients computes the p-adic completed cohomology. The work of Hansen-Kaletha-Weinstein (2020) on the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for supercuspidal representations uses the étale cohomology of the Lubin-Tate tower, which is related to the Emerton-Gee stack via the Scholze-Weinstein isomorphism (2019) between the Lubin-Tate tower and the p-adic period domain. The categorical p-adic local Langlands correspondence (Dat-Helm-Kurinczuk-Moss, 2022) predicts an equivalence between the derived category of smooth representations of GLn(ℚp) on p-adic vector spaces and the derived category of (φ, Γ)-modules, and the Emerton-Gee stack provides the geometric framework for this equivalence via the six-functor formalism on the pro-étale site.

  • Trap 1: Confusing the virtual fundamental class of M̄g,n(X, β) with the fundamental class of the coarse moduli space. The virtual class lives in the Chow group (or Borel-Moore homology) of the stack, not the coarse space, and is defined via the intrinsic normal cone even when the stack is singular or has the wrong dimension.
  • Trap 2: Assuming the motivic Tate conjecture is equivalent to the classical Tate conjecture. The motivic version involves higher Chow groups CHr(X, m) for m > 0 and is strictly stronger; it implies the classical Tate conjecture but the converse is false in general.
  • Trap 3: Thinking the parabolic Hitchin fibration for higher rank groups has abelian variety fibers. For G = GLn with n ≥ 3 and generic weights, the generic fiber is a torsor over a non-abelian variety (the parabolic Prym), and the decomposition theorem involves perverse sheaves with non-trivial monodromy.
  • Trap 4: Believing the non-abelian Hodge correspondence for higher dimensional varieties is a direct generalization of the curve case. The Hitchin map is no longer Lagrangian, the generic fiber is not an abelian variety, and the P=W conjecture (perverse = weight filtration) is open for dim X ≥ 2.
  • Trap 5: Confusing the Emerton-Gee stack (which parametrizes (φ, Γ)-modules) with the p-adic period domain (which parametrizes filtrations). The Emerton-Gee stack is a formal stack over ℤp; the period domain is a rigid analytic space over ℚp. They are related by the Scholze-Weinstein isomorphism but are not the same object.
  • Trap 6: Assuming the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GLn (n ≥ 3) is a bijection between Galois representations and Banach space representations. It is a multi-valued correspondence; the Emerton-Gee stack captures the full fiber via its geometry.
  • Trap 7: Thinking the étale cohomology of the moduli stack of Higgs bundles on a surface computes the same invariants as for curves. For surfaces, the cohomology computes Vafa-Witten invariants (related to S-duality in N=4 SYM), not the Verlinde numbers, and the Hitchin map is not proper without stability conditions.

The Fourier-Deligne Transform and ℓ-adic Sheaves on Vector Spaces

The Fourier-Deligne transform, introduced by Deligne in his proof of the Weil conjectures (Weil II) and developed systematically by Laumon, is an auto-equivalence of the derived category of constructible ℓ-adic sheaves on a finite-dimensional vector space V over a finite field 𝔽_q. For a non-trivial additive character ψ: 𝔽_q → ℚ_ℓ^×, the transform ℱ_ψ: D^b_c(V, ℚ_ℓ) → D^b_c(V^∨, ℚ_ℓ) is defined by ℱ_ψ(K) = Rπ2!(π1^*K ⊗ ℒ_ψ(⟨·,·⟩))[dim V], where π1, π2 are projections from V × V^∨, ⟨·,·⟩ is the pairing, and ℒ_ψ is the Artin-Schreier sheaf. This transform exchanges convolution with tensor product, and more importantly, it exchanges the property of being a perverse sheaf with the property of having a certain weight filtration. Laumon proved that ℱ_ψ preserves the subcategory of perverse sheaves up to a shift, and it intertwines the nearby cycles functor ψ_f with the vanishing cycles functor φ_f for a function f: V → 𝔸1. The Fourier-Deligne transform is the geometric analogue of the classical Fourier transform, and it plays a central role in the geometric Langlands program: the Hecke eigensheaf property for GL_n on a curve can be reformulated as an invariance property under a certain Fourier transform on the Hitchin base. Katz's work on exponential sums (Gauss sums, Kloosterman sums) is reinterpreted as the computation of the Fourier transform of specific ℓ-adic sheaves on 𝔸1 or 𝔾_m. For instance, the Kloosterman sheaf Kl_n on 𝔾_m is (up to shift) its own Fourier transform. The transform also underlies Laumon's proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields via the geometry of the moduli stack of shtukas. In the ℓ-adic setting, the transform is an isomorphism of Tannakian categories when restricted to the subcategory of sheaves with unipotent monodromy at infinity, and it provides a geometric realization of the Mellin transform on the level of L-functions.

Étale Cohomology of Arithmetic Schemes: The Cohomology of Spec ℤ and Rings of Integers

The étale cohomology of arithmetic schemes Spec O_K, where O_K is the ring of integers in a number field K, provides a bridge between algebraic geometry and classical algebraic number theory. For a prime ℓ invertible in O_K, the étale cohomology groups H^i_ét(Spec O_K[1/ℓ], ℤ_ℓ(n)) are finitely generated ℤ_ℓ-modules that encode deep arithmetic invariants. Tate's computation shows that H0 = ℤ_ℓ, H1 = O_K[1/ℓ]^× ⊗ ℤ_ℓ (the ℓ-adic completion of the S-unit group), and H2 is related to the ℓ-part of the class group Cl(O_K[1/ℓ]) ⊗ ℤ_ℓ. The higher groups H^i for i ≥ 3 vanish for dimension reasons (the étale cohomological dimension of Spec O_K[1/ℓ] is 3). The Tate twist ℤ_ℓ(n) corresponds to the n-th Tate twist of the cyclotomic character. The Poitou-Tate exact sequence, a nine-term exact sequence relating the cohomology of Spec O_K[1/ℓ] with local cohomology at primes above ℓ and the infinite places, is the étale cohomological incarnation of global class field theory. It generalizes the classical exact sequence of class field theory and provides a framework for the functional equation of L-functions. The étale cohomology of Spec ℤ[1/ℓ] is particularly simple: H0 = ℤ_ℓ, H1 = 0 (since ℤ[1/ℓ]^× = ±ℓ^ℤ has no ℓ-torsion for ℓ odd), H2 = ℤ_ℓ(-1) (the ℓ-adic cyclotomic character), and H3 = ℚ_ℓ/ℤ_ℓ. This reflects the fact that the absolute Galois group of ℚ has cohomological dimension 2 away from ℓ. The comparison with motivic cohomology via the Beilinson-Lichtenbaum conjecture (now a theorem of Voevodsky-Rost) identifies H^i_ét(Spec O_K[1/ℓ], ℤ_ℓ(n)) with the motivic cohomology groups H2n-i_M(O_K[1/ℓ], ℤ(n)) for i ≤ n, linking special values of Dedekind zeta functions ζ_K(n) to regulators on higher K-groups. The Tamagawa number conjecture of Bloch and Kato predicts the exact relationship between the leading term of ζ_K(s) at s = n and the étale cohomology groups, generalizing the analytic class number formula.

Worked Example: Computing H2_ét(Spec ℤ[1/ℓ], ℤ_ℓ(1))
Compute the second étale cohomology group H2_ét(Spec ℤ[1/ℓ], ℤ_ℓ(1)) for an odd prime ℓ and explain its arithmetic significance.
By the Kummer sequence 1 → μℓ^n → 𝔾_m → 𝔾_m → 1 on Spec ℤ[1/ℓ], we have H2_ét(Spec ℤ[1/ℓ], μℓ^n) ≅ Br(Spec ℤ[1/ℓ])[ℓ^n]. The Brauer group of ℤ[1/ℓ] is isomorphic to ℚ/ℤ via the invariant map, so its ℓ-primary part is ℚ_ℓ/ℤ_ℓ. Taking the inverse limit over n gives H2_ét(Spec ℤ[1/ℓ], ℤ_ℓ(1)) ≅ ℤ_ℓ. The Tate twist (1) identifies this with the ℓ-adic cyclotomic character. Arithmetically, this group classifies ℓ-power roots of unity in the maximal abelian extension of ℚ unramified outside ℓ, and its non-triviality reflects the fact that ℚ has non-trivial ℓ-adic cyclotomic extensions. The generator corresponds to the compatible system of ℓ^n-th roots of unity.
Exam Strategy: Arithmetic Schemes
When asked about étale cohomology of Spec O_K, always recall the Kummer sequence for H1 and H2, and the Poitou-Tate sequence for global duality. The cohomological dimension is 3 (or 2 if K is totally imaginary and ℓ is odd). The Tate twist shifts weights: H^i(ℤ_ℓ(n)) has weight i - 2n. Memorize the low-degree groups for Spec ℤ[1/ℓ]: H0=ℤ_ℓ, H1=0 (ℓ odd), H2=ℤ_ℓ(-1), H3=ℚ_ℓ/ℤ_ℓ. The vanishing of H1 for ℓ odd is a common trap — it fails for ℓ=2 because of the unit -1.

The Theory of Character Sheaves and the Springer Correspondence

Character sheaves, introduced by Lusztig in the 1980s, are a class of perverse sheaves on a reductive algebraic group G over an algebraically closed field that provide a geometric categorification of the irreducible characters of the finite group G(𝔽_q). They are constructed using the geometry of the nilpotent cone N ⊂ g (the Lie algebra of G) and the Springer resolution μ: T^*(G/B) → N, where B is a Borel subgroup. The Springer correspondence is a bijection between irreducible representations of the Weyl group W and pairs (O, ℰ) where O is a nilpotent orbit in N and ℰ is an irreducible G-equivariant local system on O. In the ℓ-adic setting, the Springer sheaf Spr = μ_!ℚ_ℓ[dim N] is a perverse sheaf on N whose endomorphism algebra is the group algebra ℚ_ℓ[W]. The decomposition of Spr into simple perverse sheaves IC(O, ℰ) yields the Springer correspondence. Lusztig extended this to the group G itself by considering the adjoint action and the Steinberg variety Z = {(g, x) ∈ G × N | Ad(g)x = x}. The convolution product on H^*_G(Z) gives an algebra isomorphic to the affine Hecke algebra. Character sheaves on G are simple perverse sheaves on G (for the conjugation action) that appear as direct summands of the induction of character sheaves on Levi subgroups. They are classified by a combinatorial datum: a cuspidal pair (L, A) where L is a Levi subgroup and A is a cuspidal character sheaf on L, together with a representation of the relative Weyl group. The characteristic functions of character sheaves on G(𝔽_q) give the irreducible characters of G(𝔽_q) up to a scalar, realizing the Deligne-Lusztig theory geometrically. The Fourier-Deligne transform on the Lie algebra g interchanges character sheaves on G with character sheaves on the Langlands dual group ^L G, providing a geometric version of the Langlands duality for characters. This theory is a cornerstone of the geometric Langlands program for the group G over a curve, where character sheaves on the loop group appear as Hecke eigensheaves.

The P=W Conjecture and the Non-abelian Hodge Correspondence in Higher Dimensions

The P=W conjecture, formulated by de Cataldo, Hausel, and Migliorini (2012), posits a deep relationship between the perverse Leray filtration P on the cohomology of the moduli space of Higgs bundles M_Higgs(G) and the weight filtration W on the cohomology of the character variety M_B(G) = Hom(π1(X), G)//G under the non-abelian Hodge correspondence. For a smooth projective curve X over ℂ, the non-abelian Hodge theorem (Corlette, Donaldson, Hitchin, Simpson) gives a diffeomorphism M_Higgs(G) ≅ M_B(G) that is not algebraic. The Hitchin fibration h: M_Higgs → A makes M_Higgs an algebraically completely integrable system. The perverse filtration P_k H^*(M_Higgs) is defined by the truncation functors ^pτ≤k applied to Rh_*ℚ. The weight filtration W_k H^*(M_B) comes from the mixed Hodge structure on the character variety (which is affine, hence has weights ≥ k in degree k). The conjecture states that under the diffeomorphism, P_k H^d = W2k H^d for all k, d. This has been proved for G = GL_n by de Cataldo-Hausel-Migliorini using the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration and the support theorem for the Hitchin map. For higher rank groups and higher dimensional base varieties X, the conjecture is open and the situation is more subtle: the Hitchin map is no longer Lagrangian, the generic fiber is not an abelian variety but a torsor over a non-abelian variety (the parabolic Prym), and the decomposition theorem involves perverse sheaves with non-trivial monodromy. The P=W conjecture implies the curious Hard Lefschetz property for the Hitchin system and predicts a symmetry of the mixed Hodge numbers of M_B. In the ℓ-adic setting over a finite field, the weight filtration is the filtration by weights of Frobenius eigenvalues, and the perverse filtration is defined via the Hitchin map. The conjecture then relates the weights of Frobenius on the ℓ-adic cohomology of the moduli stack of local systems to the perverse filtration on the cohomology of the moduli stack of Higgs bundles. This has profound implications for the geometric Langlands program, as it suggests a compatibility between the t-structure on the category of D-modules (perverse filtration) and the weight filtration on the category of ℓ-adic sheaves.

The Étale Homotopy Type and the Artin-Mazur Profinite Completion

The étale homotopy type, developed by Artin and Mazur (1969) and later refined by Friedlander (1982) using simplicial methods, associates to a scheme X a pro-object in the category of simplicial sets, denoted Et(X), which captures the homotopy-theoretic information of the étale topos. For a connected scheme X with a geometric point x̄, the étale fundamental group π1^ét(X, x̄) is the automorphism group of the fiber functor on the category of finite étale covers, and it equals the profinite completion of the topological fundamental group π1(X(ℂ)) when X is a variety over ℂ. The higher étale homotopy groups π_i^ét(X, x̄) are defined as the homotopy groups of the profinite completion of the universal cover in the pro-category. Artin-Mazur showed that for a Noetherian scheme of finite Krull dimension, the étale homotopy type is represented by a pro-simplicial set satisfying the Kan condition. The étale cohomology groups H^i_ét(X, A) for a finite abelian group A can be recovered as the cohomology of the simplicial set Et(X) with coefficients in A. The comparison theorem with classical topology states that for a smooth variety X over ℂ, the profinite completion of the singular complex X(ℂ) is weakly equivalent to Et(X). This implies that the étale cohomology with finite coefficients agrees with the profinite completion of the singular cohomology. The étale homotopy type is functorial and satisfies a version of the Brown-Gersten property: it converts étale hypercovers to weak equivalences. In the context of ℓ-adic cohomology, one considers the ℓ-completed étale homotopy type Et(X)^∧_ℓ, which is a pro-object in the category of ℓ-complete simplicial sets. The ℓ-adic cohomology H^i_ét(X, ℤ_ℓ) is the cohomology of Et(X)^∧_ℓ with coefficients in ℤ_ℓ. The theory of étale homotopy types provides the correct framework for understanding the action of the absolute Galois group on the homotopy type of a variety over a non-algebraically closed field: for X over a field k, there is a homotopy fixed point spectral sequence relating the étale homotopy type of X̄ = X ×_k k̄ to that of X. This underlies the theory of Galois actions on fundamental groups and higher homotopy groups, and it is essential for the anabelian geometry program of Grothendieck, where one seeks to recover a hyperbolic curve over a number field from its étale fundamental group as a Galois module.

  • Trap 4: Believing the P=W conjecture is proved for all reductive groups. It is proved for GL_n and some classical groups, but open for exceptional groups and for higher dimensional base varieties where the Hitchin map is not Lagrangian.
  • Trap 5: Assuming the étale homotopy type Et(X) is a simplicial set rather than a pro-simplicial set. It is a pro-object because the étale topos does not have enough points to represent the homotopy type by a single simplicial set in general.
  • Trap 6: Thinking the profinite completion of π1(X(ℂ)) is always the étale fundamental group. This holds for smooth varieties over ℂ, but fails for singular or non-proper varieties where the comparison map may not be an isomorphism on π1.

They correspond under the non-abelian Hodge diffeomorphism only after the P=W conjecture identifies them with the perverse filtration.
