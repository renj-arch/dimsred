Étale Cohomology — the Grothendieck Topology, ℓ-adic Cohomology & the Weil Conjectures

Étale cohomology is a cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over arbitrary fields, introduced by Grothendieck and his school. The key innovation is the étale topology: a Grothendieck topology on a scheme X where coverings are families {Ui → X} of étale morphisms (flat, unramified, finitely presented) that are jointly surjective. This replaces the Zariski topology with a much richer site that captures arithmetic information. The étale cohomology groups Hiét(X, F) = RiΓ(Xét, F) are the derived functors of global sections on the étale site. For torsion sheaves (especially ℤ/ℓnℤ), étale cohomology behaves like singular cohomology. The ℓ-adic cohomology Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is the limit over finite coefficients. The Weil conjectures — rationality, functional equation, and the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields — were proved using étale cohomology (Grothendieck for the first two, Deligne for the Riemann hypothesis). Étale cohomology also gives Poincaré duality in arbitrary characteristic, the cycle class map, and the comparison theorem with singular cohomology over ℂ.

"Étale cohomology is the right cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over fields of any characteristic."
— Alexander Grothendieck
Part 1

📏 The Étale Site and Étale Sheaves

Grothendieck Topology
The Étale Site Xét, Étale Sheaves, and the Kummer Sequence
A Grothendieck topology on a scheme X is given by declaring a family of morphisms {Ui → U} to be a covering if each Ui → U is étale (flat + unramified + locally of finite presentation) and ∪ im(Ui → U) = U. The category of étale schemes over X, equipped with these coverings, is the étale site Xét. A presheaf F on Xét (a contravariant functor from étale X-schemes to Ab) is a sheaf if for every étale covering {Ui → U}, the sequence 0 → F(U) → ∏ F(Ui) ⇉ ∏ F(Ui ×U Uj) is exact. The structure sheaf OX (regular functions) is a sheaf. The sheaf μℓn of ℓn-th roots of unity is defined by μℓn(U) = {f ∈ OU(U)× : fℓn = 1}. The Kummer sequence 1 → μℓn → 𝔾m → 𝔾m → 1 is exact on the étale site provided ℓ is invertible on X. Étale cohomology groups: Hiét(X, F) = RiΓ(Xét, F). Comparison with Zariski cohomology: H0ét(X, 𝔾m) = H0Zar(X, OX×), H1ét(X, 𝔾m) = Pic(X) (the Picard group), and H1ét(X, μn) = {line bundles L with L⊗n ≅ OX} (n-torsion line bundles). For a field k, H1ét(Spec k, μn) = k×/(k×)n (Kummer theory).
The étale site replaces the coarse Zariski topology with a finer Grothendieck topology that has enough points for cohomology to capture arithmetic and geometric invariants.
Part 2

🌍 ℓ-adic Cohomology

ℓ-adic
ℓ-adic Cohomology Groups, the Comparison Theorem, and Base Change
ℓ-adic cohomology is defined via inverse limits. For a prime ℓ invertible on X, set Hiét(X, ℤℓ) = lim← Hiét(X, ℤ/ℓnℤ) and Hiét(X, ℚℓ) = Hiét(X, ℤℓ) ⊗ℤℓ ℚℓ. For a smooth projective connected variety X of dimension n over a field k, the ℓ-adic cohomology groups are finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces for i = 0,…,2n, and vanish for i > 2n. The comparison theorem: for a variety X over ℂ, there is a canonical isomorphism Hiét(X, ℚℓ) ≅ HiBetti(X, ℚ) ⊗ℚ ℚℓ — linking étale cohomology to classical singular cohomology. The smooth base change theorem: for a proper smooth morphism f: X → S, the sheaves Rif* ℚℓ are local systems on S. The proper base change theorem: for a proper map f: X → S and a torsion sheaf F, the stalk (Rif*F)s ≅ Hi(Xs, F|Xs). The Gysin sequence: for a closed immersion i: Z → X of codimension c with complement j: U → X, there is a long exact sequence … → Hiét(U) → Hi+1_{Z}(X) → Hi+1(X) → … where Hi_{Z}(X) is cohomology with support in Z. The Leray spectral sequence for a morphism f: X → Y gives E2p,q = Hpét(Y, Rqf*F) ⇒ Hp+qét(X, F).
ℓ-adic cohomology provides a robust cohomology theory with finite-dimensional vector spaces, base change properties, and a comparison theorem linking arithmetic geometry to topology.
Part 3

🧮 Poincaré Duality and the Cycle Class Map

Duality
Poincaré Duality, Tate Twists, and the Cycle Class Map
Poincaré duality for étale cohomology: for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over a field k, the cup product pairing Hi(X, ℚℓ) × H2n−i(X, ℚℓ(n)) → H2n(X, ℚℓ(n)) ≅ ℚℓ is a perfect pairing of finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces. The Tate twist ℚℓ(1) = (lim← μℓn) ⊗ℤℓ ℚℓ — the dual of the ℓ-adic cyclotomic character of the absolute Galois group. ℚℓ(n) = ℚℓ(1)⊗n. For a smooth projective variety, H2n(X, ℚℓ(n)) ≅ ℚℓ canonically (the trace map). The cycle class map: for a closed subvariety Z ⊂ X of codimension c, there is a class cl(Z) ∈ H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c)) (the cohomology class of Z). This factors through the Chow ring: cl: CHc(X) → H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c)). The map is compatible with intersection products: cl(Z1 · Z2) = cl(Z1) ∪ cl(Z2). The Tate conjecture (still open in general) asserts that the ℚℓ-vector space spanned by cycle classes of algebraic cycles is the space of Galois invariants H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c))Gk — linking algebraic cycles to Galois representations. The Künneth formula: Hkét(X × Y, ℚℓ) ≅ ⊕i+j=k Hiét(X, ℚℓ) ⊗ Hjét(Y, ℚℓ).
Poincaré duality in étale cohomology, with Tate twists, mirrors classical topology while incorporating the Galois action; the cycle class map connects geometry (algebraic cycles) to cohomology.
Part 4

🔥 The Weil Conjectures and Deligne's Proof

Weil
The Weil Conjectures, the Lefschetz Trace Formula, and Deligne's Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n over the finite field 𝔽q. The Weil zeta function is Z(X, t) = exp(Σk≥1 |X(𝔽qk)| tk/k). The Weil conjectures (proposed 1949, proved 1960–1974): (1) Rationality (Grothendieck, 1960): Z(X, t) ∈ ℚ(t) — it is a rational function. (2) Functional equation (Grothendieck): Z(1/qnt) = ± qnE/2 tE Z(t) where E = Σ(−1)i dim Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is the Euler characteristic. (3) Riemann hypothesis (Deligne, 1974): the eigenvalues of the geometric Frobenius Fr acting on Hiét(X, ℚℓ) are algebraic integers of absolute value qi/2. (4) The Betti numbers bi = dim Hiét(X, ℚℓ) give the degrees of the zeta function. The Lefschetz trace formula: |X(𝔽qk)| = Σ2ni=0 (−1)i Tr(Frk, Hiét(X, ℚℓ)). Grothendieck proved (1) and (2) via étale cohomology and the trace formula. Deligne's proof of (3) — the Riemann hypothesis — used the theory of pure and mixed sheaves (Weil II, 1980). Key tools: the hard Lefschetz theorem for étale cohomology (cup product with c1(L) for an ample line bundle L gives an isomorphism Hi → H2n−i(n−i)), and Deligne's "théorème de Lefschetz" on the existence of a universal Lefschetz pencil. The proof also relies on the Weil bound for the eigenvalues: |α| = qi/2 for each eigenvalue α of Fr on Hi. The Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields has profound consequences: the optimal bound for exponential sums (Kloosterman sums, etc.), the Weil bound for curves (|#C(𝔽q) − q − 1| ≤ 2g√q), and the Deligne bound for hyper-Kloosterman sums.
Deligne's proof of the Weil conjectures is one of the greatest achievements of 20th-century mathematics, cementing étale cohomology as the ultimate cohomology theory for arithmetic geometry.
