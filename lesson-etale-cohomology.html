Étale Cohomology — the Grothendieck Topology, ℓ-adic Cohomology & the Weil Conjectures

Étale cohomology is a cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over arbitrary fields, introduced by Grothendieck and his school. The key innovation is the étale topology: a Grothendieck topology on a scheme X where coverings are families {Ui → X} of étale morphisms (flat, unramified, finitely presented) that are jointly surjective. This replaces the Zariski topology with a much richer site that captures arithmetic information. The étale cohomology groups Hiét(X, F) = RiΓ(Xét, F) are the derived functors of global sections on the étale site. For torsion sheaves (especially ℤ/ℓnℤ), étale cohomology behaves like singular cohomology. The ℓ-adic cohomology Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is the limit over finite coefficients. The Weil conjectures — rationality, functional equation, and the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields — were proved using étale cohomology (Grothendieck for the first two, Deligne for the Riemann hypothesis). Étale cohomology also gives Poincaré duality in arbitrary characteristic, the cycle class map, and the comparison theorem with singular cohomology over ℂ.

"Étale cohomology is the right cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over fields of any characteristic."
— Alexander Grothendieck
Part 1

📏 The Étale Site and Étale Sheaves

Grothendieck Topology
The Étale Site Xét, Étale Sheaves, and the Kummer Sequence
A Grothendieck topology on a scheme X is given by declaring a family of morphisms {Ui → U} to be a covering if each Ui → U is étale (flat + unramified + locally of finite presentation) and ∪ im(Ui → U) = U. The category of étale schemes over X, equipped with these coverings, is the étale site Xét. A presheaf F on Xét (a contravariant functor from étale X-schemes to Ab) is a sheaf if for every étale covering {Ui → U}, the sequence 0 → F(U) → ∏ F(Ui) ⇉ ∏ F(Ui ×U Uj) is exact. The structure sheaf OX (regular functions) is a sheaf. The sheaf μℓn of ℓn-th roots of unity is defined by μℓn(U) = {f ∈ OU(U)× : fℓn = 1}. The Kummer sequence 1 → μℓn → 𝔾m → 𝔾m → 1 is exact on the étale site provided ℓ is invertible on X. Étale cohomology groups: Hiét(X, F) = RiΓ(Xét, F). Comparison with Zariski cohomology: H0ét(X, 𝔾m) = H0Zar(X, OX×), H1ét(X, 𝔾m) = Pic(X) (the Picard group), and H1ét(X, μn) = {line bundles L with L⊗n ≅ OX} (n-torsion line bundles). For a field k, H1ét(Spec k, μn) = k×/(k×)n (Kummer theory).
The étale site replaces the coarse Zariski topology with a finer Grothendieck topology that has enough points for cohomology to capture arithmetic and geometric invariants.
Part 2

🌍 ℓ-adic Cohomology

ℓ-adic
ℓ-adic Cohomology Groups, the Comparison Theorem, and Base Change
ℓ-adic cohomology is defined via inverse limits. For a prime ℓ invertible on X, set Hiét(X, ℤℓ) = lim← Hiét(X, ℤ/ℓnℤ) and Hiét(X, ℚℓ) = Hiét(X, ℤℓ) ⊗ℤℓ ℚℓ. For a smooth projective connected variety X of dimension n over a field k, the ℓ-adic cohomology groups are finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces for i = 0,…,2n, and vanish for i > 2n. The comparison theorem: for a variety X over ℂ, there is a canonical isomorphism Hiét(X, ℚℓ) ≅ HiBetti(X, ℚ) ⊗ℚ ℚℓ — linking étale cohomology to classical singular cohomology. The smooth base change theorem: for a proper smooth morphism f: X → S, the sheaves Rif* ℚℓ are local systems on S. The proper base change theorem: for a proper map f: X → S and a torsion sheaf F, the stalk (Rif*F)s ≅ Hi(Xs, F|Xs). The Gysin sequence: for a closed immersion i: Z → X of codimension c with complement j: U → X, there is a long exact sequence … → Hiét(U) → Hi+1_{Z}(X) → Hi+1(X) → … where Hi_{Z}(X) is cohomology with support in Z. The Leray spectral sequence for a morphism f: X → Y gives E2p,q = Hpét(Y, Rqf*F) ⇒ Hp+qét(X, F).
ℓ-adic cohomology provides a robust cohomology theory with finite-dimensional vector spaces, base change properties, and a comparison theorem linking arithmetic geometry to topology.
Part 3

🧮 Poincaré Duality and the Cycle Class Map

Duality
Poincaré Duality, Tate Twists, and the Cycle Class Map
Poincaré duality for étale cohomology: for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over a field k, the cup product pairing Hi(X, ℚℓ) × H2n−i(X, ℚℓ(n)) → H2n(X, ℚℓ(n)) ≅ ℚℓ is a perfect pairing of finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces. The Tate twist ℚℓ(1) = (lim← μℓn) ⊗ℤℓ ℚℓ — the dual of the ℓ-adic cyclotomic character of the absolute Galois group. ℚℓ(n) = ℚℓ(1)⊗n. For a smooth projective variety, H2n(X, ℚℓ(n)) ≅ ℚℓ canonically (the trace map). The cycle class map: for a closed subvariety Z ⊂ X of codimension c, there is a class cl(Z) ∈ H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c)) (the cohomology class of Z). This factors through the Chow ring: cl: CHc(X) → H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c)). The map is compatible with intersection products: cl(Z1 · Z2) = cl(Z1) ∪ cl(Z2). The Tate conjecture (still open in general) asserts that the ℚℓ-vector space spanned by cycle classes of algebraic cycles is the space of Galois invariants H2cét(X, ℚℓ(c))Gk — linking algebraic cycles to Galois representations. The Künneth formula: Hkét(X × Y, ℚℓ) ≅ ⊕i+j=k Hiét(X, ℚℓ) ⊗ Hjét(Y, ℚℓ).
Poincaré duality in étale cohomology, with Tate twists, mirrors classical topology while incorporating the Galois action; the cycle class map connects geometry (algebraic cycles) to cohomology.
Part 4

🔥 The Weil Conjectures and Deligne's Proof

Weil
The Weil Conjectures, the Lefschetz Trace Formula, and Deligne's Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n over the finite field 𝔽q. The Weil zeta function is Z(X, t) = exp(Σk≥1 |X(𝔽qk)| tk/k). The Weil conjectures (proposed 1949, proved 1960–1974): (1) Rationality (Grothendieck, 1960): Z(X, t) ∈ ℚ(t) — it is a rational function. (2) Functional equation (Grothendieck): Z(1/qnt) = ± qnE/2 tE Z(t) where E = Σ(−1)i dim Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is the Euler characteristic. (3) Riemann hypothesis (Deligne, 1974): the eigenvalues of the geometric Frobenius Fr acting on Hiét(X, ℚℓ) are algebraic integers of absolute value qi/2. (4) The Betti numbers bi = dim Hiét(X, ℚℓ) give the degrees of the zeta function. The Lefschetz trace formula: |X(𝔽qk)| = Σ2ni=0 (−1)i Tr(Frk, Hiét(X, ℚℓ)). Grothendieck proved (1) and (2) via étale cohomology and the trace formula. Deligne's proof of (3) — the Riemann hypothesis — used the theory of pure and mixed sheaves (Weil II, 1980). Key tools: the hard Lefschetz theorem for étale cohomology (cup product with c1(L) for an ample line bundle L gives an isomorphism Hi → H2n−i(n−i)), and Deligne's "théorème de Lefschetz" on the existence of a universal Lefschetz pencil. The proof also relies on the Weil bound for the eigenvalues: |α| = qi/2 for each eigenvalue α of Fr on Hi. The Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields has profound consequences: the optimal bound for exponential sums (Kloosterman sums, etc.), the Weil bound for curves (|#C(𝔽q) − q − 1| ≤ 2g√q), and the Deligne bound for hyper-Kloosterman sums.
Deligne's proof of the Weil conjectures is one of the greatest achievements of 20th-century mathematics, cementing étale cohomology as the ultimate cohomology theory for arithmetic geometry.

Mixed Sheaves and Weights

The theory of mixed sheaves, developed by Deligne in Weil II, provides the categorical framework that makes the weight argument in the proof of the Riemann hypothesis work. A mixed ℓ‑adic sheaf on a variety X over a finite field 𝔽q is a complex of sheaves whose cohomology sheaves carry a weight filtration W• such that the graded pieces GrkW are pure of weight k. For a smooth projective variety, the constant sheaf ℚℓ is pure of weight 0, and its cohomology groups Hiét(X, ℚℓ) are pure of weight i. The crucial point is that the six operations (pushforward, pullback, tensor product, duality, nearby cycles, vanishing cycles) preserve the class of mixed sheaves and behave predictably with respect to weights: for a morphism f of relative dimension d, the shifted pushforward f![d] raises weights by at most d, while the shifted pullback f*[−d] lowers weights by at most d. This formalism allows one to reduce the study of eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hi to the case of pure sheaves, where the eigenvalues are algebraic numbers all of whose complex absolute values equal qi/2. The weight filtration also interacts with the monodromy weight filtration coming from the nearby cycles functor, giving a powerful tool for analysing degenerations of varieties. In practice, the existence of the weight filtration on the derived category of constructible ℓ‑adic sheaves is what makes the “purity implies mixed” principle work: any subquotient of a mixed sheaf is again mixed, and the weights of the pieces are bounded by the weights of the original sheaf. This is the categorical backbone of Deligne’s proof that the eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hi have absolute value qi/2.

The Weight Filtration and Purity

The weight filtration W• on a mixed ℓ‑adic complex K is an increasing filtration by subobjects in the derived category such that each graded piece GrkWK is pure of weight k. For a smooth projective variety X over 𝔽q, the constant sheaf ℚℓ is pure of weight 0, and the cohomology groups Hiét(X, ℚℓ) inherit a pure weight i structure. The purity theorem states that if X is smooth and proper, then Hiét(X, ℚℓ) is pure of weight i. The proof uses the hard Lefschetz theorem for ℓ‑adic cohomology together with the existence of a Lefschetz pencil and the fact that the nearby cycles functor preserves weights. In the mixed setting, the weight filtration is functorial: any morphism of mixed complexes strictly respects the filtration. This strictness is essential for the “weight argument” that bounds the eigenvalues of Frobenius. Concretely, if α is an eigenvalue of Frobenius on Hi, then α is an algebraic integer all of whose Galois conjugates have complex absolute value qi/2. The weight filtration also appears in the study of the monodromy operator N on the nearby cycles of a degeneration; the monodromy weight filtration centred at −1 coincides with the weight filtration shifted by the relative dimension. This compatibility is a key ingredient in the proof of the local monodromy theorem and in the construction of the ℓ‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2. For exam purposes, remember that purity is a property of smooth proper varieties, while mixedness is the general behaviour for arbitrary varieties and for pushforwards along non‑proper maps.

Weight Argument in Deligne's Proof
Let X be a smooth projective curve of genus g over 𝔽q. Using the weight filtration on H1ét(X, ℚℓ), explain why every eigenvalue α of the geometric Frobenius acting on H1 satisfies |α| = q1/2.
The constant sheaf ℚℓ on X is pure of weight 0. Because X is smooth and proper of dimension 1, the cohomology group H1ét(X, ℚℓ) is pure of weight 1 by the purity theorem. Purity of weight 1 means that for any eigenvalue α of Frobenius on this ℚℓ-vector space, all Galois conjugates of α have complex absolute value q1/2. The weight filtration on the mixed derived category guarantees that no subquotient can have weight different from 1, so there is no room for eigenvalues of any other absolute value. Hence |α| = q1/2 for every eigenvalue α.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the weight of a cohomology group, recall the rule: Hi of a smooth proper variety is pure of weight i. For non‑proper or singular varieties, the weight filtration gives a range: weights lie between i and 2i. Use this to eliminate answer choices that violate the bounds.

The Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem in Étale Cohomology

The Lefschetz hyperplane theorem asserts that for a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over an algebraically closed field and a smooth ample divisor D ⊂ X, the restriction map Hiét(X, ℚℓ) → Hiét(D, ℚℓ) is an isomorphism for i < n−1 and injective for i = n−1. In the ℓ‑adic setting the proof proceeds by reducing to the case of a Lefschetz pencil, using the proper and smooth base change theorems to compare the cohomology of the total space with that of the fibres, and then applying the hard Lefschetz theorem for the relatively ample line bundle on the total space. The key technical input is the existence of a universal Lefschetz pencil, which Deligne constructs in Weil II. The theorem has several important consequences: it implies that the primitive cohomology Pn−1(X) = ker(Hn−1(X) → Hn+1(X)) injects into Hn−1(D), and it provides the inductive step for proving the hard Lefschetz theorem itself. Over finite fields, the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem together with the weight filtration yields the “weak Lefschetz” statement that the eigenvalues of Frobenius on Hi(X) for i < n−1 are already determined by the hyperplane section. This is used in the proof of the Weil conjectures to reduce the Riemann hypothesis for X to that for a curve. For exam questions, remember that the theorem holds for any ℓ ≠ char(k) and that the same statement is true for constructible sheaves that are lisse on a dense open subset, provided one works with the appropriate perverse t‑structure.

Comparison Theorems: Crystalline and de Rham Cohomology

Étale cohomology is not the only Weil cohomology theory; for varieties over fields of characteristic p there are also crystalline cohomology and de Rham cohomology. The comparison theorems relate these theories to ℓ‑adic cohomology and to each other. For a smooth proper variety X over a perfect field k of characteristic p, the crystalline cohomology Hicrys(X/W(k)) is a finitely generated W(k)-module equipped with a Frobenius-semilinear endomorphism φ. After tensoring with ℚp one obtains a φ‑module that, via the Fontaine‑Messing or Faltings comparison isomorphism, is canonically isomorphic to Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) as a Galois representation, where K is the fraction field of W(k). On the de Rham side, for a smooth proper variety over a p‑adic field K, the p‑adic Hodge theory comparison theorem of Faltings (and later refined by Tsuji, Niziol, Scholze) gives a Galois‑equivariant isomorphism Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) ⊗ℚp BdR ≅ HidR(X/K) ⊗K BdR, where BdR is Fontaine’s period ring. This isomorphism respects the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology and the Galois action on étale cohomology. The upshot is that the ℓ‑adic, crystalline, and de Rham cohomology groups carry the same numerical invariants (Betti numbers, Hodge numbers) and that the Frobenius eigenvalues on crystalline cohomology coincide with the ℓ‑adic eigenvalues after suitable normalisation. For competitive examinations, the key facts to retain are: (1) the comparison isomorphisms exist for smooth proper varieties; (2) they are compatible with cup products, cycle class maps, and Poincaré duality; (3) they allow one to transfer results proved in one cohomology theory (e.g., the hard Lefschetz theorem in de Rham cohomology via Hodge theory) to the ℓ‑adic setting.

Applications to Galois Representations and the Langlands Program

ℓ‑adic cohomology furnishes a rich supply of continuous Galois representations. For a smooth projective variety X over a number field K, the groups Hiét(XK̄, ℚℓ) are finite‑dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces equipped with a continuous action of the absolute Galois group GK. These representations are unramified outside a finite set of primes, and at a prime v of good reduction the Frobenius eigenvalues satisfy the Weil bounds. The Tate conjecture predicts that the subspace of Galois‑invariant classes in H2c(X, ℚℓ(c)) is spanned by algebraic cycles; this is a motivic interpretation of the Galois representation. In the Langlands program, the ℓ‑adic representations coming from the cohomology of Shimura varieties are expected to realise the local and global Langlands correspondences. For example, the cohomology of the modular curve Y(N) carries an action of GL2(𝔸f) × Gℚ, and the resulting Galois representations are precisely those attached to modular forms by Eichler‑Shimura and Deligne. The work of Harris‑Taylor, Henniart, and Scholze on the local Langlands correspondence for GLn uses the ℓ‑adic cohomology of Lubin‑Tate towers and Rapoport‑Zink spaces. In the geometric Langlands program, the ℓ‑adic sheaves on the moduli stack of G‑bundles on a curve play the role of automorphic forms. For exam purposes, the essential points are: (1) ℓ‑adic cohomology provides a functorial construction of Galois representations; (2) these representations are pure of weight i (after Tate twist) and satisfy the expected local‑global compatibility; (3) the Langlands correspondence predicts a bijection between certain Galois representations and automorphic representations, and étale cohomology of Shimura varieties is the main known source of such Galois representations.

  • Confusing purity of weight i with mixedness: remember that only smooth proper varieties give pure cohomology; any non‑proper or singular variety yields mixed weights.
  • Forgetting the Tate twist in Poincaré duality: the perfect pairing is Hi × H2n−i(n) → ℚℓ, not Hi × H2n−i.
  • Misapplying the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem to non‑ample divisors: the theorem requires the divisor to be ample (or at least the hyperplane section of a projective embedding).
  • Assuming the comparison isomorphisms hold for arbitrary ℓ = p: the crystalline–étale comparison works for ℓ = p, but the ℓ‑adic comparison with de Rham cohomology is a p‑adic Hodge theory statement and does not hold for ℓ ≠ p.
  • Overlooking the role of the weight filtration in Deligne’s proof: the bound on Frobenius eigenvalues comes from the fact that Hi is pure of weight i, which is a consequence of the weight filtration on mixed sheaves.
  • These traps highlight the subtle points that examiners often test.
