Knot Theory — From Trefoil to Khovanov Homology

Knot theory studies embeddings of S¹ in S³ (or ℝ³) up to ambient isotopy — deformations without cutting. It's a subfield of low-dimensional topology with deep connections to algebra (braid groups, Yang-Baxter equation), quantum invariants (Jones polynomial), and categorical structures (Khovanov homology). The classification of knots remains fundamentally open.

"Knot theory is the study of closed curves in 3-space — the simplest nontrivial objects in topology."
— Dale Rolfsen
Part 1

🪢 Knot Invariants — Distinguishing the Inseparable

Coloring and Crossing
Reidemeister Moves, Tricolorability, and the Alexander Polynomial
A knot is an embedding S¹ → ℝ³; two knots are isotopic if one can be deformed into the other without passing through itself. The three Reidemeister moves (twist, poke, slide) generate all isotopies between knot diagrams. A knot invariant is a quantity unchanged by Reidemeister moves. Tricolorability: a knot is tricolorable if each crossing can be colored with 3 colors such that at each crossing, either all three or only one color meet. The trefoil is tricolorable; the figure-8 is not. The Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t) is computed from a knot diagram via the skein relation: Δ_{L+} − Δ_{L-} = (t^{1/2} − t^{-1/2})Δ_{L0}. For the trefoil, Δ(t) = t − 1 + t^{-1}. The knot group π₁(ℝ³\K) is the fundamental group of the complement — it's a powerful invariant computed via the Wirtinger presentation from crossings.
Knot invariants separate knots into equivalence classes — tricolorability is the simplest; the Alexander polynomial is the oldest algebraic invariant.
Part 2

🧶 Braid Groups and the Jones Polynomial

Braiding and Quantum Trace
Braid Groups, the Jones Polynomial, and the Yang-Baxter Equation
A braid on n strands weaves strands without backtracking. Artin's braid group B_n has generators σ_i (cross strand i over i+1) with relations σ_iσ_{i+1}σ_i = σ_{i+1}σ_iσ_{i+1} (braid relation) and σ_iσ_j = σ_jσ_i for |i-j|≥2. Every knot is the closure of some braid (Alexander's theorem). The Jones polynomial V_K(t) ∈ ℤ[t^{±1/2}] satisfies V_{L+} − tV_{L-} = (1 − t^{1/2})V_{L0} with V_{unknot} = 1. It detects the chirality of the trefoil (V(3₁) = t + t³ − t⁴, V(mirror) = t^{-1} + t^{-3} − t^{-4}). The Jones polynomial arises from von Neumann algebras or the Yang-Baxter equation R∈End(V⊗V) satisfying (R⊗I)(I⊗R)(R⊗I) = (I⊗R)(R⊗I)(I⊗R). Solutions to YBE give braid group representations and knot invariants via the trace of the braid word.
The Jones polynomial links knot theory to statistical mechanics, quantum groups, and operator algebras — a revolutionary connection.
Part 3

🔬 Khovanov Homology — Categorifying the Jones Polynomial

From Polynomial to Homology
Khovanov Homology — When Knot Invariants Become Bigraded Vector Spaces
Khovanov homology (2000) categorifies the Jones polynomial: instead of a polynomial V_K(t), we get a bigraded homology theory Kh^{i,j}(K) whose graded Euler characteristic Σ (−1)^i t^j dim Kh^{i,j} = V_K(t). It assigns a chain complex to a knot diagram whose homology is an invariant. The complex is built from Kauffman states (resolutions of crossings). Khovanov homology is strictly stronger than the Jones polynomial — it distinguishes knots that the Jones polynomial cannot. The Lee-Rasmussen spectral sequence relates Khovanov homology to the s-invariant, a concordance invariant that gives lower bounds on slice genus and detects exotic smooth structures on ℝ⁴. The s-invariant of a positive torus knot T(p,q) is (p-1)(q-1). Heegaard Floer homology (Ozsváth-Szabó) provides a different categorification for 3-manifolds, with knot Floer homology giving the genus of a knot.
Khovanov homology turns the Jones polynomial into a richer algebraic structure — the homology is a knot invariant, not just its Euler characteristic.
Part 4

🌊 Concordance and 4-Dimensional Topology

When Knots Bound Surfaces
Slice Knots, Concordance Groups, and Exotic ℝ⁴
A knot K is slice if it bounds a smooth properly embedded disk in B⁴ — it's the boundary of a surface in 4D. The concordance group C has knots modulo slice knots — the operation is connected sum. The trefoil is not slice (it has nonzero Arf invariant). The Levine-Tristram signature σ_ω(K) is the signature of the symmetrized Alexander matrix evaluated on S¹. For alternating knots, the signature is computed from the knot diagram. The Rasmussen s-invariant from Khovanov homology gives a homomorphism s: C → 2ℤ — it's a powerful concordance obstruction. The topological slice genus is the minimal genus of a locally flat surface in B⁴ bounding K. Freedman's theorem (1982) classified topological 4-manifolds using Casson handles — showing that every knot with trivial Alexander polynomial is topologically slice. This led to the existence of exotic ℝ⁴: smooth structures on ℝ⁴ that are homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to standard ℝ⁴ — a phenomenon unique to dimension 4.
Knot concordance is the gateway to 4-dimensional topology — exotic ℝ⁴ and the classification of 4-manifolds hinge on slice knots.

HOMFLY-PT Polynomial and the Two-Variable Generalization

The HOMFLY-PT polynomial PK(ℓ, m) is a two-variable invariant that simultaneously generalizes the Alexander polynomial and the Jones polynomial. Discovered independently by Hoste, Ocneanu, Millett, Freyd, Lickorish, and Yetter (HOMFLY) and by Przytycki and Traczyk (PT) in 1985, it satisfies the skein relation ℓ PL+ + ℓ⁻¹ PL- + m PL0 = 0 with Punknot = 1. Setting ℓ = i t⁻¹ᐟ², m = i(t¹ᐟ² − t⁻¹ᐟ²) recovers the Jones polynomial VK(t); setting ℓ = i, m = i(t¹ᐟ² − t⁻¹ᐟ²) yields the Alexander polynomial ΔK(t) up to a factor. The polynomial is invariant under all three Reidemeister moves and takes values in ℤ[ℓ±1, m±1]. For the right-handed trefoil, P(ℓ, m) = ℓ⁻² m⁻² + ℓ⁻² − ℓ⁻⁴ m⁻². The HOMFLY-PT polynomial distinguishes many knots that the Jones polynomial cannot, such as the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway mutants (11n34 and 11n42), though it fails for some mutant pairs. It arises from the representation theory of the quantum group Uq(slN) via the Reshetikhin–Turaev construction, where the parameter ℓ corresponds to qN and m to q − q⁻¹. The polynomial also satisfies a substitution property: PK(ℓ, m) = Pmirror(K)(ℓ⁻¹, m). Its degree bounds give lower bounds on the braid index and the crossing number. For alternating knots, the span in ℓ equals the crossing number, a result due to Morton and Traczyk. The HOMFLY-PT polynomial is a central object in the study of quantum invariants and provides a bridge between knot theory and Lie algebra representations.

Knot Floer Homology and the Thurston Norm

Knot Floer homology, introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó (2003) and independently by Rasmussen (2003), is a bigraded homology theory HFK−(S³, K) associated to a knot K in the 3-sphere. It categorifies the Alexander polynomial in the sense that its graded Euler characteristic recovers ΔK(t). The homology groups are modules over ℤ[U] and carry a Maslov grading and an Alexander grading. A fundamental theorem states that the top Alexander grading in which HFK− is nontrivial equals the Seifert genus g(K) of the knot. Moreover, a knot is fibered if and only if the homology in that top grading is isomorphic to ℤ[U]. This provides a purely homological characterization of fibered knots, which are knots whose complement fibers over the circle. Knot Floer homology also detects the Thurston norm on the second homology of the knot complement: for a knot in a 3-manifold, the Thurston norm of a homology class is the minimal complexity of a surface representing that class. In the case of a knot complement, the Thurston norm of the generator of H₂(S³K, ∂) is 2g(K)−1. The knot Floer homology package includes a spectral sequence from the knot Floer complex to the Heegaard Floer homology of the ambient manifold, and a surgery formula that computes the Heegaard Floer homology of Dehn surgeries on K. The invariant is computable via grid diagrams and has been implemented in software such as KnotJob. It distinguishes many knots that the Jones polynomial and Khovanov homology cannot, including some mutant pairs. The concordance invariant τ(K) derived from knot Floer homology gives a lower bound on the slice genus and is a homomorphism from the concordance group to ℤ. For alternating knots, τ(K) equals the negative of the signature divided by two. Knot Floer homology has become a cornerstone of low-dimensional topology, linking symplectic geometry, contact geometry, and 3-manifold invariants.

Worked Example: Computing τ for the Right-Handed Trefoil
Using the knot Floer homology of the right-handed trefoil 3₁, determine the concordance invariant τ(3₁) and verify that it gives a lower bound on the slice genus.
The knot Floer homology HFK−(3₁) is supported in Alexander gradings −1, 0, 1. The top Alexander grading is 1, and the homology there is ℤ[U] in Maslov grading 0. The invariant τ(K) is defined as the minimal Alexander grading of a non-torsion element in the homology of the associated graded complex. For the trefoil, the non-torsion element lives in Alexander grading 1, so τ(3₁) = 1. The slice genus g₄(3₁) satisfies g₄(3₁) ≥ |τ(3₁)| = 1. Since the trefoil is not slice (its signature is −2), the slice genus is exactly 1. This matches the fact that the trefoil bounds a punctured torus in B⁴ but no disk.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Categorification Patterns
When a question asks for a categorification of a polynomial invariant, identify the homology theory whose graded Euler characteristic returns that polynomial. For the Alexander polynomial, the answer is knot Floer homology (or Heegaard Floer homology for 3-manifolds). For the Jones polynomial, it is Khovanov homology. For the HOMFLY-PT polynomial, it is triply-graded Khovanov–Rozansky homology. Memorize the correspondence: polynomial → homology theory → grading shift. This pattern appears frequently in advanced topology questions.

Hyperbolic Knots and the Volume Conjecture

By Thurston's hyperbolic Dehn surgery theorem, the complement of a non-satellite, non-torus knot in S³ admits a complete hyperbolic metric of finite volume. Such knots are called hyperbolic knots. The hyperbolic volume vol(S³K) is a topological invariant of the knot. For the figure-eight knot 4₁, the volume is 2.02988… = 2 v₃, where v₃ ≈ 1.01494 is the volume of an ideal tetrahedron in ℍ³. The volume conjecture, formulated by Kashaev (1997) and extended by Murakami and Murakami, relates the asymptotic behavior of the colored Jones polynomial JN(K; q) at q = e2πi/N to the hyperbolic volume: 2π limN→∞ (log |JN(K; e2πi/N)|)/N = vol(S³K). This conjecture connects quantum invariants to classical geometry and has been verified for many knots, including the figure-eight knot and torus knots (where the volume is zero). The colored Jones polynomial is the Jones polynomial of the knot colored by the N-dimensional irreducible representation of Uq(sl₂). The conjecture implies that the growth rate of the colored Jones polynomial detects hyperbolicity. A stronger version, the AJ conjecture, posits that the recurrence relation for the colored Jones polynomial (the non-commutative A-polynomial) specializes to the classical A-polynomial, which encodes the SL₂(ℂ) character variety of the knot complement. The volume conjecture remains open in general but has spurred deep interactions between quantum topology, hyperbolic geometry, and representation theory. Computationally, the volume can be obtained from SnapPy using ideal triangulations. The Chern–Simons invariant also appears in the complexified version of the conjecture. Hyperbolic volume provides a lower bound for the crossing number: vol(K) ≤ v₈ × c(K), where v₈ ≈ 3.66386 is the volume of a regular ideal octahedron. This inequality is sharp for alternating knots.

Satellite Knots and the JSJ Decomposition

A satellite knot is constructed by taking a knot K (the pattern) embedded in a solid torus V and embedding V into S³ via a nontrivial knot J (the companion) so that the core of V maps to J. The resulting knot is denoted K(J). The JSJ decomposition (Jaco–Shalen–Johannson) of a 3-manifold cuts it along incompressible tori into Seifert-fibered and hyperbolic pieces. For a knot complement S³K, the JSJ tori correspond to essential tori that are not boundary-parallel. A knot is a satellite if and only if its complement contains an essential torus. The pattern knot K must be a nontrivial knot in the solid torus that is not a core curve; the companion J is a nontrivial knot in S³. The genus of a satellite knot satisfies g(K(J)) ≥ g(K) + g(J) (Schubert's formula). The Alexander polynomial of a satellite is ΔK(J)(t) = ΔK(t) ΔJ(t) where ΔK is the Alexander polynomial of the pattern as a knot in the solid torus (with the variable corresponding to the meridian of the solid torus). The knot group of a satellite is an amalgamated free product of the pattern group and the companion group over ℤ×ℤ. The JSJ decomposition is unique up to isotopy and provides a canonical way to decompose any knot into prime factors: torus knots, hyperbolic knots, and satellite knots with hyperbolic or Seifert-fibered patterns. The hyperbolic pieces have finite volume, and the Seifert-fibered pieces are cable spaces. The JSJ decomposition is algorithmically computable from a triangulation of the knot complement. It plays a central role in the classification of 3-manifolds and in the proof of the geometrization conjecture. For example, the Whitehead double of a knot is a satellite with pattern the Whitehead link component in the solid torus; its Alexander polynomial is 1, yet it is not slice in general. The JSJ decomposition also underlies the proof that knot complements determine the knot (Gordon–Luecke theorem): if two knots have homeomorphic complements, they are equivalent or mirror images.

The Slice-Ribbon Conjecture and Ribbon Knots

A knot is slice if it bounds a smoothly embedded disk in B⁴. A knot is ribbon if it bounds a disk in B⁴ that has only ribbon singularities when projected to S³ — equivalently, it bounds an immersed disk in S³ with only ribbon self-intersections (where one arc passes over another with no triple points). Every ribbon knot is slice, but the converse is the famous slice-ribbon conjecture, open since Fox (1962). The conjecture asserts that every smoothly slice knot is ribbon. It is known to hold for knots of genus one (Casson–Gordon) and for 2-bridge knots (Lisca). The conjecture has implications for the structure of the concordance group: if true, the subgroup of slice knots equals the subgroup generated by ribbon knots. Ribbon knots can be characterized by the existence of a ribbon move: a band surgery on the unlink that produces the knot. The Alexander polynomial of a ribbon knot factors as ΔK(t) = f(t) f(t⁻¹) (Fox–Milnor condition). The Casson–Gordon invariants, derived from branched covers, give obstructions to being ribbon that are stronger than the Alexander polynomial. For example, the knot 6₁ has Alexander polynomial 1 but is not ribbon (its Casson–Gordon invariant is nontrivial). The Rasmussen s-invariant and the Ozsváth–Szabó τ-invariant give lower bounds on the slice genus; for ribbon knots, s = τ = 0. The slice-ribbon conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the 4-dimensional smooth Poincaré conjecture holds for homotopy 4-spheres obtained by Gluck twists on slice disks. Recent work by Akbulut–Matveyev and others has produced potential counterexamples, but none have been confirmed. The conjecture remains a central open problem in 4-dimensional topology, linking knot theory, handlebody theory, and the topology of 4-manifolds.

  • Confusing the HOMFLY-PT polynomial with the Jones polynomial: the former has two variables and specializes to the latter; they are not interchangeable.
  • Assuming that Khovanov homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial — it categorifies the Jones polynomial; knot Floer homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial.
  • Thinking that all knots with trivial Alexander polynomial are slice — Freedman's theorem says they are topologically slice, but not necessarily smoothly slice (e.g., the Conway knot).
  • Misapplying the volume conjecture: it relates the colored Jones polynomial at roots of unity to hyperbolic volume, not the ordinary Jones polynomial.
  • Believing that satellite knots are always composite — a satellite knot can be prime (e.g., the Whitehead double of a prime knot).
  • Confusing the slice genus with the Seifert genus — the slice genus is the minimal genus of a surface in B⁴, while the Seifert genus is the minimal genus in S³; they can differ.
  • Overlooking that the JSJ decomposition is unique up to isotopy and that the pieces are either Seifert-fibered or hyperbolic; this uniqueness is essential for knot classification.

These advanced topics extend the foundational material and provide the depth required for research-level understanding and competitive examination in topology.

The Signature and the Levine–Tristram Signature

The classical knot signature σ(K) is a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group to the integers, defined as the signature of the symmetric matrix V + Vᵀ where V is a Seifert matrix for K. It satisfies σ(K₁ # K₂) = σ(K₁) + σ(K₂) and changes sign under mirror image. The Levine–Tristram signature σ_ω(K) generalises this by evaluating the Hermitian form (1−ω)V + (1−ω̄)Vᵀ at a unit complex number ω ≠ 1. As a function of ω ∈ S¹ it is piecewise constant, jumping only at roots of the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t). The integral of σ_ω over the circle gives the Tristram–Levine invariant, which bounds the four‑dimensional slice genus: |σ_ω(K)| ≤ 2g₄(K) for all ω. For alternating knots the signature equals the negative of the writhe of a reduced alternating diagram, providing a purely diagrammatic computation. The signature also detects non‑slice knots with trivial Alexander polynomial, such as the Conway knot, where σ = 0 but the Tristram–Levine signatures at certain roots of unity are non‑zero, obstructing smooth sliceness. In the concordance group the signature map has infinite‑rank image, and its kernel contains all algebraically slice knots. The Levine–Tristram signatures refine this by giving a family of homomorphisms indexed by S¹, whose joint kernel is precisely the subgroup of knots with vanishing Casson–Gordon invariants.

The Alexander Module and Blanchfield Pairing

The Alexander module A(K) = H₁(Ẋ; ℤ[t±1]) of the infinite cyclic cover Ẋ of the knot complement is a finitely generated torsion module over the Laurent polynomial ring ℤ[t±1]. Its order ideal is generated by the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t). The module carries a nonsingular sesquilinear pairing, the Blanchfield pairing Bl: A(K) × A(K) → ℚ(t)/ℤ[t±1], which is Hermitian with respect to the involution t ↦ t⁻¹. This pairing is a concordance invariant: if K is slice then A(K) admits a metaboliser, i.e. a submodule M with M = M^⊥. The existence of a metaboliser is equivalent to the vanishing of the Witt class of the Blanchfield form in the algebraic concordance group. For a knot with Δ_K(t) = 1 the Alexander module is trivial, yet the Blanchfield pairing can be non‑trivial on the torsion submodule of the homology of the double branched cover, giving rise to Casson–Gordon invariants. The Blanchfield pairing also determines the Levine–Tristram signatures: σ_ω(K) equals the signature of the Hermitian form obtained by evaluating Bl at t = ω. In particular, the metaboliser condition forces all σ_ω(K) to vanish, providing an algebraic proof that slice knots have zero Tristram–Levine signatures. The module structure can be read from a Seifert matrix V: A(K) ≅ ℤ[t±1]ⁿ / (Vᵀ − tV)ℤ[t±1]ⁿ, and the pairing is induced by (Vᵀ − tV)⁻¹. This presentation makes the Blanchfield form computable from any Seifert surface.

Worked Example: Computing the Levine–Tristram Signature
Let K be the right‑hand trefoil with Seifert matrix V = [[1,1],[0,1]]. Compute σ_ω(K) for ω = −1.
Form the Hermitian matrix H(ω) = (1−ω)V + (1−ω̄)Vᵀ. For ω = −1 we have 1−ω = 2 and 1−ω̄ = 2. Thus H(−1) = 2(V + Vᵀ) = 2[[2,1],[1,2]] = [[4,2],[2,4]]. The eigenvalues are 6 and 2, both positive, so the signature is 2. Since the trefoil is positive, σ_ω = 2 for all ω not a root of Δ_K(t) = t²−t+1. At ω = e±iπ/3 the matrix becomes singular and the signature jumps. The result matches the known fact that the trefoil has σ = 2 and σ_ω = 2 away from the roots.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a concordance obstruction, first check the Alexander polynomial. If Δ_K(t) = 1, the classical signature and all Levine–Tristram signatures vanish; move to Casson–Gordon invariants or the Blanchfield pairing on the double branched cover. Remember that the Blanchfield pairing is a complete algebraic concordance invariant for knots with Δ_K(t) ≠ 1, so a metaboliser calculation often decides sliceness in exam problems.

The Knot Quandle and its Homology

A quandle is a set Q with a binary operation ▷ satisfying (i) a ▷ a = a, (ii) the map x ↦ x ▷ a is a bijection for each a, and (iii) (a ▷ b) ▷ c = (a ▷ c) ▷ (b ▷ c). The fundamental quandle Q(K) of a knot K is generated by arcs of a diagram with relations at crossings: the over‑arc acts on the under‑arc by conjugation in the knot group. Q(K) is a complete invariant of knots up to mirror image and orientation reversal. Quandle cohomology, introduced by Carter, Jelsovsky, Kamada, Langford and Saito, assigns cohomology groups Hⁿ_Q(Q; A) with coefficients in an abelian group A. The second cohomology group classifies quandle 2‑cocycles, which yield state‑sum invariants of knotted surfaces in four dimensions. For a finite quandle X, a 2‑cocycle φ: X × X → A gives a Boltzmann weight at each crossing; the product over all crossings yields a polynomial invariant of the knot, often stronger than the fundamental quandle itself. The dihedral quandle R_n of order n (with operation a ▷ b = 2b − a mod n) produces the Fox n‑coloring invariant; its cocycle invariants detect chirality for many knots that the Jones polynomial cannot distinguish. Quandle homology also provides a framework for the categorification of the Alexander polynomial: the chain complex of a knot diagram with coefficients in the Alexander quandle ℤ[t±1] yields a homology theory isomorphic to knot Floer homology in certain cases. The quandle cocycle invariant is computable in polynomial time for a fixed finite quandle, making it attractive for algorithmic knot recognition.

The Khovanov–Rozansky Homology and Matrix Factorizations

Khovanov–Rozansky homology categorifies the sl(N) Reshetikhin–Turaev invariants, generalising Khovanov’s original categorification of the Jones polynomial (the N = 2 case). The construction assigns to a link diagram a chain complex of matrix factorisations of the potential w = xN+1 over a polynomial ring. Each crossing contributes a two‑term complex of graded matrix factorisations; the total complex is obtained by tensoring these local pieces. The homology groups Hi,j_N(L) are triply graded (homological, quantum, and a‑grading) and are invariant under Reidemeister moves up to homotopy equivalence. For N = 1 the theory reduces to the odd Khovanov homology of Ozsváth–Rasmussen–Szabó. The Euler characteristic of Hi,j_N(L) recovers the sl(N) polynomial, which specialises to the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial after a change of variables. The theory satisfies a spectral sequence converging to the original Khovanov homology when N = 2, and to the Heegaard Floer homology of the branched double cover for N = 1. Matrix factorisation techniques also yield a functorial tangle invariant: to each tangle one assigns a complex of (A,A)-bimodules over the arc algebra, and composition of tangles corresponds to derived tensor product. This functoriality underlies the proof of the invariance under Reidemeister moves and provides a framework for the categorified Reshetikhin–Turaev TQFT. Computationally, the homology can be calculated via the foam‑based graphical calculus of Mackaay–Stošić–Vaz, which reduces the problem to linear algebra over ℤ or ℚ. The sl(3) homology distinguishes many mutant pairs that the Jones polynomial and Khovanov homology cannot, such as the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway knots.

The Rasmussen s‑Invariant and its Properties

The Rasmussen s‑invariant arises from the Lee deformation of Khovanov homology. Lee’s complex C_Lee(K) is a filtered chain complex over ℚ whose homology is two‑dimensional, generated by two canonical classes v_+ and v_- with quantum gradings s(K) ± 1. The integer s(K) is a concordance homomorphism: s(K₁ # K₂) = s(K₁) + s(K₂) and s(−K) = −s(K). It provides a lower bound on the smooth slice genus: |s(K)| ≤ 2g₄(K). For quasi‑alternating knots (including all alternating knots) s(K) equals the classical signature σ(K). The invariant detects the non‑sliceness of many knots with trivial Alexander polynomial, notably the Conway knot (s = 0 but not slice) and the (2, 2k+1) torus knots (s = 2k). The s‑invariant also gives a proof of the Milnor conjecture on the slice genus of torus knots: g₄(Tp,q) = (p−1)(q−1)/2. Combined with the Ozsváth–Szabó τ‑invariant from knot Floer homology, one obtains the inequality |τ(K)| ≤ g₄(K) and the relation s(K) = 2τ(K) for all knots where both are defined, though this equality is known to fail for certain satellite knots. The s‑invariant is computable from a reduced Khovanov complex using the spectral sequence from Khovanov to Lee homology; the filtration degree of the surviving generators yields s. In practice, for diagrams up to 12 crossings the s‑invariant can be tabulated and used as a quick sliceness test in examinations.

  • Confusing the Levine–Tristram signature with the classical signature: the former depends on a unit complex parameter and can be non‑zero even when σ = 0.
  • Assuming the Blanchfield pairing is trivial whenever Δ_K(t) = 1; the pairing lives on the torsion module of the double branched cover and can detect non‑slice knots with trivial Alexander polynomial.
  • Treating the knot quandle as a complete invariant without noting the mirror‑image ambiguity; Q(K) ≅ Q(mirror(K)) as quandles.
  • Believing Khovanov–Rozansky homology for N = 2 is identical to Khovanov homology; it is a different categorification (matrix factorisations vs. Bar‑Natan’s cobordisms) though they are conjecturally equivalent.
  • Using the Rasmussen s‑invariant as a sufficient condition for sliceness; s = 0 does not imply slice (e.g., the Conway knot).
  • Overlooking that the s‑invariant is defined over ℚ; over ℤ the Lee homology may have torsion that obscures the filtration.
  • Mixing the sl(N) polynomial with the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial without the correct variable substitution; the sl(N) polynomial is a one‑variable specialisation of HOMFLY‑PT at a = q^N.
The advanced sections above extend the foundational material with precise algebraic and categorical tools that appear regularly in research‑level topology examinations.
