Knot Theory — From Trefoil to Khovanov Homology

Knot theory studies embeddings of S¹ in S³ (or ℝ³) up to ambient isotopy — deformations without cutting. It's a subfield of low-dimensional topology with deep connections to algebra (braid groups, Yang-Baxter equation), quantum invariants (Jones polynomial), and categorical structures (Khovanov homology). The classification of knots remains fundamentally open.

"Knot theory is the study of closed curves in 3-space — the simplest nontrivial objects in topology."
— Dale Rolfsen
Part 1

🪢 Knot Invariants — Distinguishing the Inseparable

Coloring and Crossing
Reidemeister Moves, Tricolorability, and the Alexander Polynomial
A knot is an embedding S¹ → ℝ³; two knots are isotopic if one can be deformed into the other without passing through itself. The three Reidemeister moves (twist, poke, slide) generate all isotopies between knot diagrams. A knot invariant is a quantity unchanged by Reidemeister moves. Tricolorability: a knot is tricolorable if each crossing can be colored with 3 colors such that at each crossing, either all three or only one color meet. The trefoil is tricolorable; the figure-8 is not. The Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t) is computed from a knot diagram via the skein relation: Δ_{L+} − Δ_{L-} = (t^{1/2} − t^{-1/2})Δ_{L0}. For the trefoil, Δ(t) = t − 1 + t^{-1}. The knot group π₁(ℝ³\K) is the fundamental group of the complement — it's a powerful invariant computed via the Wirtinger presentation from crossings.
Knot invariants separate knots into equivalence classes — tricolorability is the simplest; the Alexander polynomial is the oldest algebraic invariant.
Part 2

🧶 Braid Groups and the Jones Polynomial

Braiding and Quantum Trace
Braid Groups, the Jones Polynomial, and the Yang-Baxter Equation
A braid on n strands weaves strands without backtracking. Artin's braid group B_n has generators σ_i (cross strand i over i+1) with relations σ_iσ_{i+1}σ_i = σ_{i+1}σ_iσ_{i+1} (braid relation) and σ_iσ_j = σ_jσ_i for |i-j|≥2. Every knot is the closure of some braid (Alexander's theorem). The Jones polynomial V_K(t) ∈ ℤ[t^{±1/2}] satisfies V_{L+} − tV_{L-} = (1 − t^{1/2})V_{L0} with V_{unknot} = 1. It detects the chirality of the trefoil (V(3₁) = t + t³ − t⁴, V(mirror) = t^{-1} + t^{-3} − t^{-4}). The Jones polynomial arises from von Neumann algebras or the Yang-Baxter equation R∈End(V⊗V) satisfying (R⊗I)(I⊗R)(R⊗I) = (I⊗R)(R⊗I)(I⊗R). Solutions to YBE give braid group representations and knot invariants via the trace of the braid word.
The Jones polynomial links knot theory to statistical mechanics, quantum groups, and operator algebras — a revolutionary connection.
Part 3

🔬 Khovanov Homology — Categorifying the Jones Polynomial

From Polynomial to Homology
Khovanov Homology — When Knot Invariants Become Bigraded Vector Spaces
Khovanov homology (2000) categorifies the Jones polynomial: instead of a polynomial V_K(t), we get a bigraded homology theory Kh^{i,j}(K) whose graded Euler characteristic Σ (−1)^i t^j dim Kh^{i,j} = V_K(t). It assigns a chain complex to a knot diagram whose homology is an invariant. The complex is built from Kauffman states (resolutions of crossings). Khovanov homology is strictly stronger than the Jones polynomial — it distinguishes knots that the Jones polynomial cannot. The Lee-Rasmussen spectral sequence relates Khovanov homology to the s-invariant, a concordance invariant that gives lower bounds on slice genus and detects exotic smooth structures on ℝ⁴. The s-invariant of a positive torus knot T(p,q) is (p-1)(q-1). Heegaard Floer homology (Ozsváth-Szabó) provides a different categorification for 3-manifolds, with knot Floer homology giving the genus of a knot.
Khovanov homology turns the Jones polynomial into a richer algebraic structure — the homology is a knot invariant, not just its Euler characteristic.
Part 4

🌊 Concordance and 4-Dimensional Topology

When Knots Bound Surfaces
Slice Knots, Concordance Groups, and Exotic ℝ⁴
A knot K is slice if it bounds a smooth properly embedded disk in B⁴ — it's the boundary of a surface in 4D. The concordance group C has knots modulo slice knots — the operation is connected sum. The trefoil is not slice (it has nonzero Arf invariant). The Levine-Tristram signature σ_ω(K) is the signature of the symmetrized Alexander matrix evaluated on S¹. For alternating knots, the signature is computed from the knot diagram. The Rasmussen s-invariant from Khovanov homology gives a homomorphism s: C → 2ℤ — it's a powerful concordance obstruction. The topological slice genus is the minimal genus of a locally flat surface in B⁴ bounding K. Freedman's theorem (1982) classified topological 4-manifolds using Casson handles — showing that every knot with trivial Alexander polynomial is topologically slice. This led to the existence of exotic ℝ⁴: smooth structures on ℝ⁴ that are homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to standard ℝ⁴ — a phenomenon unique to dimension 4.
Knot concordance is the gateway to 4-dimensional topology — exotic ℝ⁴ and the classification of 4-manifolds hinge on slice knots.

HOMFLY-PT Polynomial and the Two-Variable Generalization

The HOMFLY-PT polynomial PK(ℓ, m) is a two-variable invariant that simultaneously generalizes the Alexander polynomial and the Jones polynomial. Discovered independently by Hoste, Ocneanu, Millett, Freyd, Lickorish, and Yetter (HOMFLY) and by Przytycki and Traczyk (PT) in 1985, it satisfies the skein relation ℓ PL+ + ℓ⁻¹ PL- + m PL0 = 0 with Punknot = 1. Setting ℓ = i t⁻¹ᐟ², m = i(t¹ᐟ² − t⁻¹ᐟ²) recovers the Jones polynomial VK(t); setting ℓ = i, m = i(t¹ᐟ² − t⁻¹ᐟ²) yields the Alexander polynomial ΔK(t) up to a factor. The polynomial is invariant under all three Reidemeister moves and takes values in ℤ[ℓ±1, m±1]. For the right-handed trefoil, P(ℓ, m) = ℓ⁻² m⁻² + ℓ⁻² − ℓ⁻⁴ m⁻². The HOMFLY-PT polynomial distinguishes many knots that the Jones polynomial cannot, such as the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway mutants (11n34 and 11n42), though it fails for some mutant pairs. It arises from the representation theory of the quantum group Uq(slN) via the Reshetikhin–Turaev construction, where the parameter ℓ corresponds to qN and m to q − q⁻¹. The polynomial also satisfies a substitution property: PK(ℓ, m) = Pmirror(K)(ℓ⁻¹, m). Its degree bounds give lower bounds on the braid index and the crossing number. For alternating knots, the span in ℓ equals the crossing number, a result due to Morton and Traczyk. The HOMFLY-PT polynomial is a central object in the study of quantum invariants and provides a bridge between knot theory and Lie algebra representations.

Knot Floer Homology and the Thurston Norm

Knot Floer homology, introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó (2003) and independently by Rasmussen (2003), is a bigraded homology theory HFK−(S³, K) associated to a knot K in the 3-sphere. It categorifies the Alexander polynomial in the sense that its graded Euler characteristic recovers ΔK(t). The homology groups are modules over ℤ[U] and carry a Maslov grading and an Alexander grading. A fundamental theorem states that the top Alexander grading in which HFK− is nontrivial equals the Seifert genus g(K) of the knot. Moreover, a knot is fibered if and only if the homology in that top grading is isomorphic to ℤ[U]. This provides a purely homological characterization of fibered knots, which are knots whose complement fibers over the circle. Knot Floer homology also detects the Thurston norm on the second homology of the knot complement: for a knot in a 3-manifold, the Thurston norm of a homology class is the minimal complexity of a surface representing that class. In the case of a knot complement, the Thurston norm of the generator of H₂(S³K, ∂) is 2g(K)−1. The knot Floer homology package includes a spectral sequence from the knot Floer complex to the Heegaard Floer homology of the ambient manifold, and a surgery formula that computes the Heegaard Floer homology of Dehn surgeries on K. The invariant is computable via grid diagrams and has been implemented in software such as KnotJob. It distinguishes many knots that the Jones polynomial and Khovanov homology cannot, including some mutant pairs. The concordance invariant τ(K) derived from knot Floer homology gives a lower bound on the slice genus and is a homomorphism from the concordance group to ℤ. For alternating knots, τ(K) equals the negative of the signature divided by two. Knot Floer homology has become a cornerstone of low-dimensional topology, linking symplectic geometry, contact geometry, and 3-manifold invariants.

Worked Example: Computing τ for the Right-Handed Trefoil
Using the knot Floer homology of the right-handed trefoil 3₁, determine the concordance invariant τ(3₁) and verify that it gives a lower bound on the slice genus.
The knot Floer homology HFK−(3₁) is supported in Alexander gradings −1, 0, 1. The top Alexander grading is 1, and the homology there is ℤ[U] in Maslov grading 0. The invariant τ(K) is defined as the minimal Alexander grading of a non-torsion element in the homology of the associated graded complex. For the trefoil, the non-torsion element lives in Alexander grading 1, so τ(3₁) = 1. The slice genus g₄(3₁) satisfies g₄(3₁) ≥ |τ(3₁)| = 1. Since the trefoil is not slice (its signature is −2), the slice genus is exactly 1. This matches the fact that the trefoil bounds a punctured torus in B⁴ but no disk.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Categorification Patterns
When a question asks for a categorification of a polynomial invariant, identify the homology theory whose graded Euler characteristic returns that polynomial. For the Alexander polynomial, the answer is knot Floer homology (or Heegaard Floer homology for 3-manifolds). For the Jones polynomial, it is Khovanov homology. For the HOMFLY-PT polynomial, it is triply-graded Khovanov–Rozansky homology. Memorize the correspondence: polynomial → homology theory → grading shift. This pattern appears frequently in advanced topology questions.

Hyperbolic Knots and the Volume Conjecture

By Thurston's hyperbolic Dehn surgery theorem, the complement of a non-satellite, non-torus knot in S³ admits a complete hyperbolic metric of finite volume. Such knots are called hyperbolic knots. The hyperbolic volume vol(S³K) is a topological invariant of the knot. For the figure-eight knot 4₁, the volume is 2.02988… = 2 v₃, where v₃ ≈ 1.01494 is the volume of an ideal tetrahedron in ℍ³. The volume conjecture, formulated by Kashaev (1997) and extended by Murakami and Murakami, relates the asymptotic behavior of the colored Jones polynomial JN(K; q) at q = e2πi/N to the hyperbolic volume: 2π limN→∞ (log |JN(K; e2πi/N)|)/N = vol(S³K). This conjecture connects quantum invariants to classical geometry and has been verified for many knots, including the figure-eight knot and torus knots (where the volume is zero). The colored Jones polynomial is the Jones polynomial of the knot colored by the N-dimensional irreducible representation of Uq(sl₂). The conjecture implies that the growth rate of the colored Jones polynomial detects hyperbolicity. A stronger version, the AJ conjecture, posits that the recurrence relation for the colored Jones polynomial (the non-commutative A-polynomial) specializes to the classical A-polynomial, which encodes the SL₂(ℂ) character variety of the knot complement. The volume conjecture remains open in general but has spurred deep interactions between quantum topology, hyperbolic geometry, and representation theory. Computationally, the volume can be obtained from SnapPy using ideal triangulations. The Chern–Simons invariant also appears in the complexified version of the conjecture. Hyperbolic volume provides a lower bound for the crossing number: vol(K) ≤ v₈ × c(K), where v₈ ≈ 3.66386 is the volume of a regular ideal octahedron. This inequality is sharp for alternating knots.

Satellite Knots and the JSJ Decomposition

A satellite knot is constructed by taking a knot K (the pattern) embedded in a solid torus V and embedding V into S³ via a nontrivial knot J (the companion) so that the core of V maps to J. The resulting knot is denoted K(J). The JSJ decomposition (Jaco–Shalen–Johannson) of a 3-manifold cuts it along incompressible tori into Seifert-fibered and hyperbolic pieces. For a knot complement S³K, the JSJ tori correspond to essential tori that are not boundary-parallel. A knot is a satellite if and only if its complement contains an essential torus. The pattern knot K must be a nontrivial knot in the solid torus that is not a core curve; the companion J is a nontrivial knot in S³. The genus of a satellite knot satisfies g(K(J)) ≥ g(K) + g(J) (Schubert's formula). The Alexander polynomial of a satellite is ΔK(J)(t) = ΔK(t) ΔJ(t) where ΔK is the Alexander polynomial of the pattern as a knot in the solid torus (with the variable corresponding to the meridian of the solid torus). The knot group of a satellite is an amalgamated free product of the pattern group and the companion group over ℤ×ℤ. The JSJ decomposition is unique up to isotopy and provides a canonical way to decompose any knot into prime factors: torus knots, hyperbolic knots, and satellite knots with hyperbolic or Seifert-fibered patterns. The hyperbolic pieces have finite volume, and the Seifert-fibered pieces are cable spaces. The JSJ decomposition is algorithmically computable from a triangulation of the knot complement. It plays a central role in the classification of 3-manifolds and in the proof of the geometrization conjecture. For example, the Whitehead double of a knot is a satellite with pattern the Whitehead link component in the solid torus; its Alexander polynomial is 1, yet it is not slice in general. The JSJ decomposition also underlies the proof that knot complements determine the knot (Gordon–Luecke theorem): if two knots have homeomorphic complements, they are equivalent or mirror images.

The Slice-Ribbon Conjecture and Ribbon Knots

A knot is slice if it bounds a smoothly embedded disk in B⁴. A knot is ribbon if it bounds a disk in B⁴ that has only ribbon singularities when projected to S³ — equivalently, it bounds an immersed disk in S³ with only ribbon self-intersections (where one arc passes over another with no triple points). Every ribbon knot is slice, but the converse is the famous slice-ribbon conjecture, open since Fox (1962). The conjecture asserts that every smoothly slice knot is ribbon. It is known to hold for knots of genus one (Casson–Gordon) and for 2-bridge knots (Lisca). The conjecture has implications for the structure of the concordance group: if true, the subgroup of slice knots equals the subgroup generated by ribbon knots. Ribbon knots can be characterized by the existence of a ribbon move: a band surgery on the unlink that produces the knot. The Alexander polynomial of a ribbon knot factors as ΔK(t) = f(t) f(t⁻¹) (Fox–Milnor condition). The Casson–Gordon invariants, derived from branched covers, give obstructions to being ribbon that are stronger than the Alexander polynomial. For example, the knot 6₁ has Alexander polynomial 1 but is not ribbon (its Casson–Gordon invariant is nontrivial). The Rasmussen s-invariant and the Ozsváth–Szabó τ-invariant give lower bounds on the slice genus; for ribbon knots, s = τ = 0. The slice-ribbon conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the 4-dimensional smooth Poincaré conjecture holds for homotopy 4-spheres obtained by Gluck twists on slice disks. Recent work by Akbulut–Matveyev and others has produced potential counterexamples, but none have been confirmed. The conjecture remains a central open problem in 4-dimensional topology, linking knot theory, handlebody theory, and the topology of 4-manifolds.

  • Confusing the HOMFLY-PT polynomial with the Jones polynomial: the former has two variables and specializes to the latter; they are not interchangeable.
  • Assuming that Khovanov homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial — it categorifies the Jones polynomial; knot Floer homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial.
  • Thinking that all knots with trivial Alexander polynomial are slice — Freedman's theorem says they are topologically slice, but not necessarily smoothly slice (e.g., the Conway knot).
  • Misapplying the volume conjecture: it relates the colored Jones polynomial at roots of unity to hyperbolic volume, not the ordinary Jones polynomial.
  • Believing that satellite knots are always composite — a satellite knot can be prime (e.g., the Whitehead double of a prime knot).
  • Confusing the slice genus with the Seifert genus — the slice genus is the minimal genus of a surface in B⁴, while the Seifert genus is the minimal genus in S³; they can differ.
  • Overlooking that the JSJ decomposition is unique up to isotopy and that the pieces are either Seifert-fibered or hyperbolic; this uniqueness is essential for knot classification.

These advanced topics extend the foundational material and provide the depth required for research-level understanding and competitive examination in topology.

The Signature and the Levine–Tristram Signature

The classical knot signature σ(K) is a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group to the integers, defined as the signature of the symmetric matrix V + Vᵀ where V is a Seifert matrix for K. It satisfies σ(K₁ # K₂) = σ(K₁) + σ(K₂) and changes sign under mirror image. The Levine–Tristram signature σ_ω(K) generalises this by evaluating the Hermitian form (1−ω)V + (1−ω̄)Vᵀ at a unit complex number ω ≠ 1. As a function of ω ∈ S¹ it is piecewise constant, jumping only at roots of the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t). The integral of σ_ω over the circle gives the Tristram–Levine invariant, which bounds the four‑dimensional slice genus: |σ_ω(K)| ≤ 2g₄(K) for all ω. For alternating knots the signature equals the negative of the writhe of a reduced alternating diagram, providing a purely diagrammatic computation. The signature also detects non‑slice knots with trivial Alexander polynomial, such as the Conway knot, where σ = 0 but the Tristram–Levine signatures at certain roots of unity are non‑zero, obstructing smooth sliceness. In the concordance group the signature map has infinite‑rank image, and its kernel contains all algebraically slice knots. The Levine–Tristram signatures refine this by giving a family of homomorphisms indexed by S¹, whose joint kernel is precisely the subgroup of knots with vanishing Casson–Gordon invariants.

The Alexander Module and Blanchfield Pairing

The Alexander module A(K) = H₁(Ẋ; ℤ[t±1]) of the infinite cyclic cover Ẋ of the knot complement is a finitely generated torsion module over the Laurent polynomial ring ℤ[t±1]. Its order ideal is generated by the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t). The module carries a nonsingular sesquilinear pairing, the Blanchfield pairing Bl: A(K) × A(K) → ℚ(t)/ℤ[t±1], which is Hermitian with respect to the involution t ↦ t⁻¹. This pairing is a concordance invariant: if K is slice then A(K) admits a metaboliser, i.e. a submodule M with M = M^⊥. The existence of a metaboliser is equivalent to the vanishing of the Witt class of the Blanchfield form in the algebraic concordance group. For a knot with Δ_K(t) = 1 the Alexander module is trivial, yet the Blanchfield pairing can be non‑trivial on the torsion submodule of the homology of the double branched cover, giving rise to Casson–Gordon invariants. The Blanchfield pairing also determines the Levine–Tristram signatures: σ_ω(K) equals the signature of the Hermitian form obtained by evaluating Bl at t = ω. In particular, the metaboliser condition forces all σ_ω(K) to vanish, providing an algebraic proof that slice knots have zero Tristram–Levine signatures. The module structure can be read from a Seifert matrix V: A(K) ≅ ℤ[t±1]ⁿ / (Vᵀ − tV)ℤ[t±1]ⁿ, and the pairing is induced by (Vᵀ − tV)⁻¹. This presentation makes the Blanchfield form computable from any Seifert surface.

Worked Example: Computing the Levine–Tristram Signature
Let K be the right‑hand trefoil with Seifert matrix V = [[1,1],[0,1]]. Compute σ_ω(K) for ω = −1.
Form the Hermitian matrix H(ω) = (1−ω)V + (1−ω̄)Vᵀ. For ω = −1 we have 1−ω = 2 and 1−ω̄ = 2. Thus H(−1) = 2(V + Vᵀ) = 2[[2,1],[1,2]] = [[4,2],[2,4]]. The eigenvalues are 6 and 2, both positive, so the signature is 2. Since the trefoil is positive, σ_ω = 2 for all ω not a root of Δ_K(t) = t²−t+1. At ω = e±iπ/3 the matrix becomes singular and the signature jumps. The result matches the known fact that the trefoil has σ = 2 and σ_ω = 2 away from the roots.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a concordance obstruction, first check the Alexander polynomial. If Δ_K(t) = 1, the classical signature and all Levine–Tristram signatures vanish; move to Casson–Gordon invariants or the Blanchfield pairing on the double branched cover. Remember that the Blanchfield pairing is a complete algebraic concordance invariant for knots with Δ_K(t) ≠ 1, so a metaboliser calculation often decides sliceness in exam problems.

The Knot Quandle and its Homology

A quandle is a set Q with a binary operation ▷ satisfying (i) a ▷ a = a, (ii) the map x ↦ x ▷ a is a bijection for each a, and (iii) (a ▷ b) ▷ c = (a ▷ c) ▷ (b ▷ c). The fundamental quandle Q(K) of a knot K is generated by arcs of a diagram with relations at crossings: the over‑arc acts on the under‑arc by conjugation in the knot group. Q(K) is a complete invariant of knots up to mirror image and orientation reversal. Quandle cohomology, introduced by Carter, Jelsovsky, Kamada, Langford and Saito, assigns cohomology groups Hⁿ_Q(Q; A) with coefficients in an abelian group A. The second cohomology group classifies quandle 2‑cocycles, which yield state‑sum invariants of knotted surfaces in four dimensions. For a finite quandle X, a 2‑cocycle φ: X × X → A gives a Boltzmann weight at each crossing; the product over all crossings yields a polynomial invariant of the knot, often stronger than the fundamental quandle itself. The dihedral quandle R_n of order n (with operation a ▷ b = 2b − a mod n) produces the Fox n‑coloring invariant; its cocycle invariants detect chirality for many knots that the Jones polynomial cannot distinguish. Quandle homology also provides a framework for the categorification of the Alexander polynomial: the chain complex of a knot diagram with coefficients in the Alexander quandle ℤ[t±1] yields a homology theory isomorphic to knot Floer homology in certain cases. The quandle cocycle invariant is computable in polynomial time for a fixed finite quandle, making it attractive for algorithmic knot recognition.

The Khovanov–Rozansky Homology and Matrix Factorizations

Khovanov–Rozansky homology categorifies the sl(N) Reshetikhin–Turaev invariants, generalising Khovanov’s original categorification of the Jones polynomial (the N = 2 case). The construction assigns to a link diagram a chain complex of matrix factorisations of the potential w = xN+1 over a polynomial ring. Each crossing contributes a two‑term complex of graded matrix factorisations; the total complex is obtained by tensoring these local pieces. The homology groups Hi,j_N(L) are triply graded (homological, quantum, and a‑grading) and are invariant under Reidemeister moves up to homotopy equivalence. For N = 1 the theory reduces to the odd Khovanov homology of Ozsváth–Rasmussen–Szabó. The Euler characteristic of Hi,j_N(L) recovers the sl(N) polynomial, which specialises to the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial after a change of variables. The theory satisfies a spectral sequence converging to the original Khovanov homology when N = 2, and to the Heegaard Floer homology of the branched double cover for N = 1. Matrix factorisation techniques also yield a functorial tangle invariant: to each tangle one assigns a complex of (A,A)-bimodules over the arc algebra, and composition of tangles corresponds to derived tensor product. This functoriality underlies the proof of the invariance under Reidemeister moves and provides a framework for the categorified Reshetikhin–Turaev TQFT. Computationally, the homology can be calculated via the foam‑based graphical calculus of Mackaay–Stošić–Vaz, which reduces the problem to linear algebra over ℤ or ℚ. The sl(3) homology distinguishes many mutant pairs that the Jones polynomial and Khovanov homology cannot, such as the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway knots.

The Rasmussen s‑Invariant and its Properties

The Rasmussen s‑invariant arises from the Lee deformation of Khovanov homology. Lee’s complex C_Lee(K) is a filtered chain complex over ℚ whose homology is two‑dimensional, generated by two canonical classes v_+ and v_- with quantum gradings s(K) ± 1. The integer s(K) is a concordance homomorphism: s(K₁ # K₂) = s(K₁) + s(K₂) and s(−K) = −s(K). It provides a lower bound on the smooth slice genus: |s(K)| ≤ 2g₄(K). For quasi‑alternating knots (including all alternating knots) s(K) equals the classical signature σ(K). The invariant detects the non‑sliceness of many knots with trivial Alexander polynomial, notably the Conway knot (s = 0 but not slice) and the (2, 2k+1) torus knots (s = 2k). The s‑invariant also gives a proof of the Milnor conjecture on the slice genus of torus knots: g₄(Tp,q) = (p−1)(q−1)/2. Combined with the Ozsváth–Szabó τ‑invariant from knot Floer homology, one obtains the inequality |τ(K)| ≤ g₄(K) and the relation s(K) = 2τ(K) for all knots where both are defined, though this equality is known to fail for certain satellite knots. The s‑invariant is computable from a reduced Khovanov complex using the spectral sequence from Khovanov to Lee homology; the filtration degree of the surviving generators yields s. In practice, for diagrams up to 12 crossings the s‑invariant can be tabulated and used as a quick sliceness test in examinations.

  • Confusing the Levine–Tristram signature with the classical signature: the former depends on a unit complex parameter and can be non‑zero even when σ = 0.
  • Assuming the Blanchfield pairing is trivial whenever Δ_K(t) = 1; the pairing lives on the torsion module of the double branched cover and can detect non‑slice knots with trivial Alexander polynomial.
  • Treating the knot quandle as a complete invariant without noting the mirror‑image ambiguity; Q(K) ≅ Q(mirror(K)) as quandles.
  • Believing Khovanov–Rozansky homology for N = 2 is identical to Khovanov homology; it is a different categorification (matrix factorisations vs. Bar‑Natan’s cobordisms) though they are conjecturally equivalent.
  • Using the Rasmussen s‑invariant as a sufficient condition for sliceness; s = 0 does not imply slice (e.g., the Conway knot).
  • Overlooking that the s‑invariant is defined over ℚ; over ℤ the Lee homology may have torsion that obscures the filtration.
  • Mixing the sl(N) polynomial with the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial without the correct variable substitution; the sl(N) polynomial is a one‑variable specialisation of HOMFLY‑PT at a = q^N.

The Thurston–Bennequin Invariant and Legendrian Knot Theory

Legendrian knot theory studies knots in the standard contact three‑space (ℝ³, ξ_std = ker(dz − y dx)) up to Legendrian isotopy. Two classical invariants, the Thurston–Bennequin number tb and the rotation number r, together with the underlying topological knot type, often determine the Legendrian isotopy class for many knot families. For a front projection (x,z) of a Legendrian knot, tb equals the writhe of the diagram minus the number of cusps divided by two, while r equals half the difference between the number of down‑cusps and up‑cusps. The Bennequin inequality tb + |r| ≤ 2g₃(K) − 1, where g₃(K) is the three‑genus, provides a sharp obstruction to the existence of Legendrian representatives with given invariants. Eliashberg and Fraser classified Legendrian unknots: they are uniquely determined by (tb, r) satisfying tb + |r| ≤ −1. For more complicated knots, the classification is incomplete; however, the combination of tb, r, and the Chekanov–Eliashberg differential graded algebra (DGA) yields a powerful invariant. The DGA is generated by Reeb chords of the Legendrian knot, with differential counting rigid holomorphic disks in the symplectization. Its stable tame isomorphism class is a complete invariant for Legendrian knots up to Legendrian isotopy in many cases. Augmentations of the DGA to a field produce linearized contact homology groups, which are computable and distinguish many Legendrian knots with identical classical invariants. The theory also connects to transverse knots via the transverse push‑off, where the self‑linking number sl = tb − r gives a transverse invariant. In examinations, typical questions ask to compute tb and r from a given front diagram, to verify the Bennequin bound, or to explain why two Legendrian representatives of the same topological knot with different (tb, r) cannot be Legendrian isotopic.

The Triply Graded HOMFLY‑PT Homology

The HOMFLY‑PT polynomial P(a, z) is a two‑variable invariant of oriented links that specializes to the Jones polynomial (a = q², z = q − q⁻¹) and the Alexander polynomial (a = 1). Khovanov and Rozansky constructed a triply graded homology theory Hi,j,k(L) whose graded Euler characteristic recovers P(a, z). The three gradings are homological (i), quantum (j), and a‑grading (k) corresponding to the a‑variable. The construction uses matrix factorizations of the potential w = xN+1 for each N, then takes a limit as N → ∞, or equivalently uses the graphical calculus of foams introduced by Mackaay, Stošić, and Vaz. The chain complex is built from a link diagram by assigning a two‑term complex of graded matrix factorizations to each crossing, then tensoring these local complexes. Invariance under Reidemeister moves is proved up to homotopy equivalence of chain complexes, and the resulting homology groups are functorial with respect to link cobordisms. The theory categorifies the entire HOMFLY‑PT polynomial, meaning that the Poincaré polynomial ∑ a^k q^j t^i dim Hi,j,k equals P(a, q) after the substitution a = q^k. For the unknot, the homology is one‑dimensional in grading (0,0,0). The triply graded homology distinguishes many mutant pairs that the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial cannot, such as the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway knots. It also admits a spectral sequence converging to the original Khovanov homology (the N = 2 sl(2) theory) and another to the knot Floer homology of the branched double cover. Computationally, the foam‑based calculus reduces the problem to linear algebra over ℤ or ℚ, and software such as the “HOMFLY‑PT homology calculator” can handle diagrams up to 12 crossings. In examinations, students may be asked to describe the grading shifts induced by Reidemeister moves, to compute the homology of the Hopf link, or to explain how the a‑grading encodes the a‑variable of the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial.

Worked Example: Computing tb and r from a Front Diagram
Consider the front projection of a Legendrian trefoil with three crossings and four cusps (two up, two down). The writhe of the diagram is +3. Compute the Thurston–Bennequin number tb and the rotation number r.
The Thurston–Bennequin number is tb = writhe − (number of cusps)/2 = 3 − 4/2 = 3 − 2 = 1. The rotation number is r = (down‑cusps − up‑cusps)/2 = (2 − 2)/2 = 0. Thus (tb, r) = (1, 0). The Bennequin inequality gives tb + |r| = 1 ≤ 2g₃ − 1 = 2·1 − 1 = 1, which is satisfied with equality, indicating this Legendrian trefoil is maximal.
Exam Strategy
When a front diagram is given, count crossings with sign for writhe and cusps by orientation. Remember that tb and r are integers; tb can be negative. The Bennequin bound is a quick check for maximality. For triply graded homology, focus on the grading shifts: Reidemeister I adds a contractible summand shifting (i,j,k) by (−1,−1,0) or (+1,+1,0); Reidemeister II and III induce homotopy equivalences preserving all three gradings.

The A‑Polynomial and SL(2,ℂ) Character Varieties

The A‑polynomial A_K(M, L) ∈ ℤ[M±1, L±1] of a knot K ⊂ S³ encodes the SL(2,ℂ) character variety of the knot complement. Introduced by Cooper, Culler, Gillet, Long, and Shalen in 1994, it is defined as the elimination ideal of the eigenvalue equations for the meridian μ and longitude λ acting on the representation variety. Concretely, for a representation ρ: π₁(S³ K) → SL(2,ℂ), let M and L be the eigenvalues of ρ(μ) and ρ(λ) respectively (with the convention that the eigenvalues are M, M⁻¹ and L, L⁻¹). The set of (M, L) arising from irreducible representations forms an algebraic curve; its Zariski closure is the zero set of A_K(M, L). The polynomial is normalized so that A_K(M, L) = A_K(M⁻¹, L⁻¹) and A_K(1, 1) = 0. For the unknot, A = L − 1. For the trefoil, A = (L − 1)(L + M⁶). The A‑polynomial detects the knot genus: the Newton polygon of A_K gives a lower bound on the genus via the degree in L. It also relates to the colored Jones polynomial through the AJ conjecture, which posits that the non‑commutative A‑ideal annihilates the colored Jones sequence. The A‑polynomial is computable via SnapPy or by hand for two‑bridge knots using the Riley polynomial. In examinations, typical tasks include computing the A‑polynomial for a given two‑bridge knot from its continued fraction expansion, explaining how the A‑polynomial specializes to the Alexander polynomial when L = 1, or describing the relationship between the A‑polynomial and the character variety of the knot group.

The Upsilon Invariant from Knot Floer Homology

The Upsilon invariant Υ_K(t) is a piecewise linear, continuous function Υ_K: [0, 2] → ℝ introduced by Ozsváth, Stipsicz, and Szabó in 2014. It is derived from the knot Floer homology complex CFK^∞(K) by considering a family of filtered chain complexes parameterized by t ∈ [0, 2]. For each t, one defines a filtration level and takes the minimal grading of a homogeneous element generating the homology in the top Alexander grading; the resulting integer divided by 2 gives Υ_K(t). The invariant satisfies Υ_K(0) = Υ_K(2) = 0, Υ_K(t) = Υ_K(2 − t), and ΥK₁ # K₂(t) = ΥK₁(t) + ΥK₂(t). It provides a lower bound on the smooth slice genus: |Υ_K(t)| ≤ t·g₄(K) for all t ∈ [0, 2]. For alternating knots, Υ_K(t) = −t·σ(K)/2 for t ∈ [0, 1] and symmetric thereafter. The Upsilon invariant detects non‑slice knots with trivial Alexander polynomial, such as the (2, 2k+1) torus knots where ΥT2,2k+1(t) = −t·k for t ∈ [0, 1]. It also gives a proof of the Milnor conjecture on torus knot slice genus. Computationally, Υ_K can be read off from the knot Floer homology staircase diagram or from the grid complex. In examinations, students may be asked to compute Υ_K for a given knot from its CFK^∞ staircase, to verify the concordance homomorphism property, or to use Υ_K to obstruct sliceness of a specific knot.

The Colored Jones Polynomial and the AJ Conjecture

The colored Jones polynomial J_N(K; q) is a sequence of Laurent polynomials indexed by N ∈ ℕ, obtained by coloring the knot with the N‑dimensional irreducible representation of U_q(sl₂). It generalizes the ordinary Jones polynomial (N = 2). The AJ conjecture, formulated by Garoufalidis and Lê, states that the non‑commutative A‑ideal of the knot, generated by the recurrence relation satisfied by J_N(K; q), quantizes the classical A‑polynomial. Concretely, let Â(M, L, q) be the operator obtained by replacing M → q^N and L → shift operator in N. Then Â annihilates the sequence J_N(K; q). In the limit q → 1, Â reduces to the classical A‑polynomial A_K(M, L). The conjecture has been proved for all two‑bridge knots, torus knots, and many other families. The colored Jones polynomial also satisfies the volume conjecture: 2π limN→∞ (log |J_N(K; e2πi/N)|)/N = Vol(S³ K) for hyperbolic knots. This connects quantum invariants to hyperbolic geometry. Computationally, J_N can be obtained from the Kauffman bracket skein module of the knot complement or via the quantum group R‑matrix. In examinations, typical questions involve writing the recurrence for the colored Jones polynomial of the trefoil, explaining the relationship between the AJ conjecture and the A‑polynomial, or using the volume conjecture to estimate the hyperbolic volume from the growth of J_N.

Virtual Knot Theory and the Virtual Braid Group

Virtual knot theory, introduced by Kauffman in 1996, extends classical knot theory by allowing virtual crossings — artifacts of representing a knot diagram on a surface of higher genus when projected to the plane. A virtual knot is an equivalence class of Gauss codes modulo Reidemeister moves and the virtual Reidemeister moves that involve virtual crossings. Unlike classical knots, virtual knots need not embed in ℝ³; they correspond to stable equivalence classes of knots in thickened surfaces Σ × I. The virtual braid group VBₙ, defined by Kamada and others, is generated by classical braid generators σᵢ (satisfying the braid relations) and virtual generators τᵢ (satisfying the symmetric group relations) together with mixed relations σᵢ τⱼ = τⱼ σᵢ for |i − j| > 1 and σᵢ τᵢ₊₁ τᵢ = τᵢ₊₁ τᵢ σᵢ₊₁. Every virtual knot admits a virtual braid representation via a virtual analogue of Alexander's theorem. The virtual Jones polynomial and virtual Khovanov homology extend the classical invariants; notably, the virtual Jones polynomial can detect non‑classicality — a virtual knot with non‑trivial virtual Jones polynomial is not classical. The Kishino knot is a famous example of a non‑classical virtual knot with trivial fundamental group and trivial Jones polynomial but non‑trivial virtual Khovanov homology. The virtual Alexander polynomial Δᵥ(t) lives in ℤ[t^±¹] and satisfies Δᵥ(t) = Δᵥ(t⁻¹); for classical knots it reduces to the ordinary Alexander polynomial. Virtual knot invariants are computable via the virtual knot group and its representations into the virtual braid group. In examinations, typical tasks include writing the virtual Reidemeister moves, computing the virtual Jones polynomial for a given virtual knot diagram using the Kauffman bracket with virtual crossings treated as neither over nor under, or proving that a given virtual knot is non‑classical by exhibiting a non‑trivial virtual invariant.

Knot Contact Homology and Legendrian Invariants

Knot contact homology, developed by Ekholm, Etnyre, Ng, and Sullivan, associates a differential graded algebra (DGA) to a Legendrian knot in the standard contact ℝ³ (or more generally in a contact 3‑manifold). The generators are Reeb chords of the Legendrian knot, and the differential counts rigid holomorphic disks in the symplectization ℝ × ℝ³ with boundary on the Lagrangian cylinder over the knot. The resulting homology, called Legendrian contact homology (LCH), is an invariant of the Legendrian isotopy class. The Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA (ℤ/2 or ℤ coefficients) admits augmentations — DGA maps to ℤ/2 — and the set of augmentations up to equivalence carries rich structure. The linearized contact homology with respect to an augmentation ε is the homology of the linearized complex; its Poincaré polynomial is the augmentation polynomial Aug(λ), a two‑variable polynomial in λ and μ that specializes to the A‑polynomial when the Legendrian knot is the maximal Thurston–Bennequin representative. The ruling polynomial R(z) counts normal rulings of the front projection and equals the augmentation polynomial for many knots. The Legendrian knot atlas tabulates LCH for knots up to 10 crossings. The Bennequin inequality tb + |r| ≤ 2g₃ − 1 bounds the Thurston–Bennequin number tb and rotation number r by the Seifert genus g₃; equality characterizes maximal Legendrian representatives. The transverse invariant θ ∈ LCH distinguishes transverse knots that are Legendrian isotopic after stabilization. Computationally, the DGA can be computed from the front diagram using the Ng resolution or the cord algebra. In examinations, students may be asked to write the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA for the standard Legendrian trefoil, compute its augmentations, derive the augmentation polynomial, or explain how the ruling polynomial recovers the A‑polynomial for the maximal tb representative.

Worked Example: Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA of the Legendrian Right‑Hand Trefoil
Consider the standard front projection of the Legendrian right‑hand trefoil with tb = 1, r = 0. It has three cusps (two left, one right) and two Reeb chords a (short) and b (long). Write the differential ∂ on the DGA generated by a, b over ℤ/2, and find all augmentations ε: {a, b} → ℤ/2.
The front diagram has crossings corresponding to the two classical crossings of the trefoil. The differential is determined by counting immersed disks with corners at Reeb chords. For the right‑hand trefoil with the standard orientation, the only rigid disks give ∂a = 1 + ab + ba and ∂b = 0 (over ℤ/2, ab = ba). An augmentation ε satisfies ε(∂a) = 0 and ε(∂b) = 0. Since ∂b = 0, ε(b) is free. For ∂a = 1 + ab + ba = 1 + 2ab = 1 (mod 2), we need ε(1) = 1, so ε(1 + ab + ba) = 1 + ε(a)ε(b) + ε(b)ε(a) = 1 + 2ε(a)ε(b) = 1. This forces 1 = 0 in ℤ/2, a contradiction — wait, the correct differential for the standard Legendrian trefoil (tb = 1) is ∂a = 1 + aba, ∂b = 0. Over ℤ/2, aba = a²b. An augmentation requires ε(1 + a²b) = 1 + ε(a)²ε(b) = 0 ⇒ ε(a)²ε(b) = 1. Since ε(a), ε(b) ∈ {0,1}, the only solution is ε(a) = 1, ε(b) = 1. Thus there is exactly one augmentation ε(a)=1, ε(b)=1. The linearized complex has ∂^ε a = ε(∂a) + ... = 0, ∂^ε b = 0, so linearized contact homology is generated by a, b in appropriate gradings. The augmentation polynomial Aug(λ, μ) = λ + μ + λμ (up to units) specializes to the A‑polynomial A(M, L) = L + M⁶ − 1 for the trefoil when λ = M², μ = L.
Exam Strategy: Contact Homology Computations
When asked to compute the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA, first draw the front projection with all cusps and crossings labeled. Identify Reeb chords (strands connecting left cusps to right cusps). Use the Ng resolution to replace each crossing by a 4‑valent vertex and count immersed polygons with corners at chords. Remember that over ℤ/2 the differential simplifies because signs disappear. For augmentations, set up the system ε(∂x) = 0 for each generator x; solve in {0,1}. The augmentation polynomial is the resultant of the augmentation equations. If the question asks for the ruling polynomial, count normal rulings of the front diagram — each ruling corresponds to an augmentation. The Bennequin inequality tb + |r| ≤ 2g₃ − 1 is a quick check: if a Legendrian knot achieves equality, its augmentation polynomial equals the A‑polynomial (Ng's theorem).

Dehn Surgery, L‑Spaces, and the Berge Conjecture

Dehn surgery on a knot K ⊂ S³ produces a closed 3‑manifold S³p/q(K) by removing a tubular neighborhood of K and regluing a solid torus so that the meridian of the solid torus maps to the slope p/q on ∂(S³ νK). The L‑space conjecture (Boyer–Gordon–Watson) posits that an irreducible rational homology 3‑sphere is an L‑space (i.e., its Heegaard Floer homology ĤF has minimal rank |H₁|) if and only if its fundamental group is not left‑orderable. For knots in S³, the L‑space knot condition is that some positive surgery yields an L‑space. L‑space knots are fibered, have positive Alexander polynomial coefficients alternating in sign, and their knot Floer homology is determined by the Alexander polynomial (the staircase property). The Berge conjecture classifies knots in S³ that admit a Dehn surgery yielding a lens space: they are precisely the Berge knots, a finite list of 12 families of knots in the 3‑sphere, including torus knots, cable knots, and certain hyperbolic knots. The conjecture is known for many cases via Heegaard Floer homology and the cyclic surgery theorem (Culler–Gordon–Luecke–Shalen), which states that if a knot admits two distinct cyclic surgeries, then it is a torus knot or a cable of a torus knot. The property R conjecture (proved by Kronheimer–Mrowka–Ozsváth–Szabó using monopole Floer homology) states that 0‑surgery on a non‑trivial knot never yields S¹ × S². The cosmetic surgery conjecture asserts that no two distinct slopes produce homeomorphic manifolds; it is known for many classes. The Alexander polynomial of an L‑space knot satisfies Δ_K(t) = ∑i=0^n (-1)^i ta_i with a_i strictly increasing. The knot Floer homology ĤFK(K) of an L‑space knot is supported in a single δ‑grading and its Poincaré polynomial equals the Alexander polynomial. In examinations, typical questions include: given a knot's Alexander polynomial, determine if it can be an L‑space knot; compute the Heegaard Floer homology of +1 surgery on the right‑hand trefoil (which is the lens space L(3,1)); or explain how the cyclic surgery theorem restricts the possible lens space surgeries on a hyperbolic knot.

The Knot Group, Peripheral System, and Property R

The knot group G_K = π₁(S³ K) is the fundamental group of the knot complement. It admits a peripheral system consisting of a meridian μ (a loop linking K once) and a longitude λ (a loop parallel to K with zero linking number). The pair (μ, λ) is well‑defined up to conjugation and inversion. The Wirtinger presentation gives G_K = ⟨ x₁, …, xₙ | xᵢ xⱼ = xₖ xᵢ ⟩ where each relation corresponds to a crossing. The Alexander module is the first homology of the infinite cyclic cover, a module over ℤ[t^±¹]; its order ideal is the Alexander polynomial. The character variety X(G_K) = Hom(G_K, SL(2,ℂ))//SL(2,ℂ) contains the representation variety of irreducible representations. The A‑polynomial A_K(M, L) ∈ ℤ[M^±¹, L^±¹] is the defining polynomial of the closure of the set of eigenvalues (M, L) of ρ(μ), ρ(λ) for irreducible ρ. Property R states that 0‑surgery on a non‑trivial knot never yields S¹ × S²; equivalently, the knot group does not admit a homomorphism onto ℤ with kernel normally generated by the longitude. This was proved by Kronheimer, Mrowka, Ozsváth, and Szabó (2004) using monopole Floer homology. The Smith conjecture (proved using hyperbolic geometry and the orbifold theorem) states that a non‑trivial knot cannot be the fixed point set of a finite order diffeomorphism of S³. The knot group determines the knot complement for prime knots (Gordon–Luecke theorem). The peripheral system is a complete invariant for prime knots up to orientation reversal. The knot quandle (already covered) is a weaker invariant derived from the conjugation action of G_K on itself. In examinations, students may be asked to write the Wirtinger presentation for a given diagram, compute the Alexander polynomial from the Fox calculus, describe the peripheral system for the trefoil (μ = x, λ = x⁻¹y⁻¹xy²), or explain why Property R implies the cosmetic surgery conjecture for 0‑surgery.

Categorification of the Colored Jones Polynomial and the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev Invariants

The colored Jones polynomial J_N(K; q) assigns to each N ∈ ℕ a Laurent polynomial obtained by coloring the knot with the N‑dimensional irreducible representation V_N of U_q(sl₂). The sequence {J_N} satisfies a linear recurrence relation with coefficients in ℤ[q^±¹, M^±¹, L^±¹] where M = q^N and L shifts N → N+1. The AJ conjecture (Garoufalidis–Lê) asserts that this recurrence quantizes the A‑polynomial. Categorification seeks a homology theory whose graded Euler characteristic recovers J_N. For N=2, Khovanov homology categorifies J2. For general N, the Khovanov–Rozansky sl(N) homology H_N(K) categorifies J_N; it is a triply graded homology theory (homological, quantum, and a‑grading) whose Euler characteristic is J_N. The Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev (WRT) invariant τ_M(K) at a root of unity q = e2πi/r is a 3‑manifold invariant of the pair (S³, K) obtained from the modular tensor category of U_q(sl₂) representations. The volume conjecture (Kashaev, Murakami–Murakami) states that for hyperbolic knots, 2π limN→∞ (log |J_N(K; e2πi/N)|)/N = Vol(S³ K). The colored HOMFLY‑PT homology (or triply graded homology) categorifies the colored HOMFLY‑PT polynomial and is related to the DAHA (double affine Hecke algebra) representation theory. The super‑A‑polynomial encodes the recurrence for the colored HOMFLY‑PT polynomial. Computationally, sl(N) homology can be computed via matrix factorizations (Khovanov–Rozansky) or foam cobordisms (Mackaay–Vaz). The colored Jones polynomial for the trefoil is J_N = q(N²−1)/2 ∑k=0N−1 q-Nk [N−1 choose k]_q [N+k choose k]_q where [n]_q = (q^n − q−n)/(q − q−1). In examinations, typical tasks include writing the recurrence for J_N of the figure‑eight knot, explaining the relationship between the WRT invariant and the colored Jones polynomial at roots of unity, or describing how the sl(N) homology differential is constructed from the MOY calculus.

The Ozsváth–Szabó Spectral Sequence from Khovanov Homology to Knot Floer Homology

In 2005 Ozsváth and Szabó constructed a spectral sequence whose E² page is the reduced Khovanov homology Kh(K) of a knot K ⊂ S³ (with a specific bigrading shift) and whose E^∞ page is the hat version of knot Floer homology HFK̂(K). The construction proceeds by interpreting the cube of resolutions that underlies Khovanov homology as a filtered chain complex in the Heegaard Floer setting. Each vertex of the cube corresponds to a complete resolution of the knot diagram; the edges correspond to saddle cobordisms. In Heegaard Floer theory, these saddles induce maps on the chain complex CFK̂ associated to a Heegaard diagram adapted to the knot. The filtration is given by the number of 1-resolutions used, which corresponds to the homological grading in Khovanov homology. The differential on the E¹ page is the sum of the internal Khovanov differentials (which preserve the filtration) and the maps induced by the 1-handles attached during the surgery exact triangle. The spectral sequence collapses at E² for alternating knots, reflecting the fact that both theories are determined by the knot signature and the Alexander polynomial in that case. For non-alternating knots the higher differentials can be non-trivial; they encode subtle 4-dimensional information. A key consequence is the inequality rank HFK̂(K) ≤ rank Kh(K), which implies that the Rasmussen s-invariant, defined as a certain grading shift in Khovanov homology, satisfies |s(K)| ≤ 2g₄(K) where g₄ is the smooth 4-ball genus. The spectral sequence also respects the δ-grading (the difference between the homological and quantum gradings) and the Alexander grading on the Floer side. In examinations, students may be asked to describe the filtration on the cube of resolutions, explain why the spectral sequence implies the rank inequality, or compute the E² page for the right-handed trefoil and verify that it collapses to HFK̂ which has total rank 3.

The Kauffman Bracket Skein Module of Arbitrary 3-Manifolds

For an oriented 3-manifold M, the Kauffman bracket skein module S(M) is the ℤ[A^±¹]-module generated by isotopy classes of framed links in M modulo the Kauffman bracket relations: a positive crossing equals A times the 0-resolution plus A⁻¹ times the ∞-resolution, and a trivial unknot component disjoint from the rest of the link can be removed at the cost of multiplying by −A² − A⁻². When M = S³, S(S³) ≅ ℤ[A^±¹] generated by the empty link; the image of a knot K is its Jones polynomial V_K(A⁻⁴) up to a normalization factor. For M = S¹ × S², the module is the polynomial ring ℤ[A^±¹][x] where x is the core circle S¹ × {pt}. For a general closed 3-manifold, S(M) is a finitely generated ℤ[A^±¹]-module whose structure reflects the topology of M. At a root of unity A = eπi/2r, the quotient of S(M) by the ideal generated by the Chebyshev polynomial of degree r yields a finite-dimensional vector space whose dimension equals the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev invariant τ_r(M). The skein module of a thickened surface Σ × I carries a natural algebra structure given by stacking; this is the skein algebra, which is closely related to the character variety of π₁(Σ) into SL(2,ℂ). The skein module of a knot complement S³ N(K) is a module over the skein algebra of the torus T² × I, and its structure encodes the A-polynomial. Computationally, S(M) can be approached via Heegaard splittings: a Heegaard diagram gives a presentation of S(M) as a quotient of the skein algebra of the Heegaard surface by the relations coming from the attaching curves. In examinations, typical tasks include computing S(S¹ × S²), explaining the relation between the skein module at roots of unity and the WRT invariant, or describing how the skein module of a knot complement detects the peripheral system.

Worked Example: Skein Module of the Solid Torus
Compute the Kauffman bracket skein module S(D² × S¹) of the solid torus as a ℤ[A^±¹]-module.
A framed link in D² × S¹ can be isotoped to lie in a standard neighborhood of the core {0} × S¹. Every component is a parallel copy of the core with some framing. The skein relations allow us to resolve any crossing between parallel strands. The result is that S(D² × S¹) is a free ℤ[A^±¹]-module with basis {e_n | n ≥ 0} where e_n is the n-fold parallel copy of the core with zero framing (the blackboard framing). The multiplication (stacking) makes this a commutative algebra isomorphic to ℤ[A^±¹][x] where x = e1. The relation for a trivial unknot component gives e0 = 1 and the loop value −A² − A⁻² corresponds to the empty link. Thus S(D² × S¹) ≅ ℤ[A^±¹][x] as an algebra.
Exam Strategy: Skein Modules and Roots of Unity
When a question asks about the dimension of the skein module at a root of unity, recall that the WRT invariant τ_r(M) is the dimension of S(M) ⊗ℤ[A^±¹] ℂ where A = eπi/2r and the Chebyshev relation is imposed. For S³ this dimension is 1; for S¹ × S² it is r−1. Memorize these two values and the fact that the skein module of a knot complement is a module over the torus skein algebra generated by the meridian and longitude.

The Milnor μ-Invariants and Link Homotopy Classification

The Milnor μ-invariants are a family of integer invariants μ_L(i₁ i₂ … i_k) associated to an oriented n-component link L = L₁ ∪ … ∪ L_n, defined for any multi-index (i₁, …, i_k) with entries in {1, …, n} and no adjacent equal indices. They are derived from the Magnus expansion of the longitudes in the free group F_n = ⟨x₁, …, x_n⟩. Let G = π₁(S³ L) be the link group. The lower central series G = G₁ ⊇ G₂ ⊇ … is defined by Gq+1 = [G, G_q]. The quotient G / Gq+1 is the nilpotent completion of class q. Milnor showed that the longitudes ℓ_i ∈ G can be expressed in the free group modulo Gq+1 as products of commutators; the coefficients in the Magnus expansion ℓ_i = ∏ (1 + μ(i₁…i_k) Xi₁…Xi_k) are the μ-invariants. The first non-vanishing μ-invariants of length k are well-defined integers; higher-order invariants are defined modulo the greatest common divisor of all lower-order invariants that are proper subsequences (the indeterminacy). The μ-invariants of length 2 are the pairwise linking numbers lk(L_i, L_j). For the Borromean rings, all linking numbers vanish but μ(123) = ±1, detecting the essential triple linking. A fundamental theorem of Milnor and Habegger–Lin states that two links are link-homotopic (each component may pass through itself but not through other components) if and only if all their μ-invariants agree. The μ-invariants are also realized as Massey products in the cohomology ring H*(S³ L; ℤ). In examinations, students may be asked to compute the μ-invariants of the Whitehead link (μ(1122) = ±1), explain the indeterminacy formula, or describe how the vanishing of all μ-invariants of length ≤ 3 implies the link is link-homotopic to the unlink.

The Gordian Distance and the Unknotting Number Problem

The Gordian distance d(K₁, K₂) between two knots is the minimum number of crossing changes required to transform a diagram of K₁ into a diagram of K₂. The unknotting number u(K) = d(K, unknot) is the most basic measure of knot complexity. A crossing change can be realized by a Dehn surgery of coefficient ±1 on a small circle linking the two strands; equivalently, it corresponds to a genus-1 cobordism in B⁴ between the two knots. This gives the inequality g₄(K) ≤ u(K), where g₄(K) is the smooth 4-ball genus (the minimum genus of a smoothly embedded surface in B⁴ bounded by K). The knot signature σ(K) provides a lower bound: |σ(K)|/2 ≤ g₄(K) ≤ u(K). The Rasmussen s-invariant, coming from Khovanov homology, satisfies |s(K)|/2 ≤ g₄(K) and is often sharper than the signature. For alternating knots, u(K) = (c(K) − |σ(K)|)/2 where c(K) is the crossing number, a result proved by Murasugi and Thistlethwaite using the Jones polynomial. The Gordian distance satisfies the triangle inequality and is symmetric. The unknotting number is notoriously difficult to compute; for example, u(10₁₃₉) = 3 was determined only in 2014 using a combination of Heegaard Floer homology and geometric arguments. The 4-dimensional clasp number c₄(K), the minimum number of transverse double points of an immersed disk in B⁴ bounded by K, satisfies c₄(K) ≤ u(K) and can be strictly smaller. In examinations, typical questions ask to prove that u(K) ≥ |σ(K)|/2, compute u for the figure-eight knot (u = 1), or explain why the unknotting number of a connected sum satisfies u(K₁ # K₂) ≤ u(K₁) + u(K₂) with equality not always holding.

The Casson–Gordon Invariants and the Algebraic Concordance Group

The algebraic concordance group, introduced by Levine in 1969, classifies knots up to algebraic concordance: two knots are algebraically concordant if their Seifert forms are Witt equivalent. Levine proved that the algebraic concordance group is isomorphic to ⊕_∞ ℤ ⊕ ⊕_∞ ℤ/2 ⊕ ⊕_∞ ℤ/4, where the infinite ℤ summands are detected by the Levine–Tristram signatures σ_ω(K) for ω ∈ S¹, the ℤ/2 summands by the Arf invariant, and the ℤ/4 summands by higher-order linking forms. The natural map from the smooth concordance group 𝒞 to the algebraic concordance group has a large kernel. Casson and Gordon (1978, 1986) defined invariants σ(K, χ) that vanish for slice knots, providing obstructions to a knot being slice even when it is algebraically slice. The construction uses the 2-fold branched cover Σ₂(K) of S³ along K. A character χ: H₁(Σ₂(K)) → S¹ of prime power order extends to a representation of π₁(Σ₂(K)) and defines a 4-manifold W_χ obtained by taking the 2-fold cover of B⁴ branched along a slice disk (if one exists) and extending the representation. The Casson–Gordon invariant is the signature defect σ(W_χ) − σ(W), where W is the 2-fold cover of B⁴ branched along a Seifert surface. For a slice knot, this defect vanishes for all χ. The invariants take values in ℚ/ℤ and are computable from the Seifert form and the character. They detect many knots that are topologically slice (bound a locally flat disk in B⁴) but not smoothly slice. Freedman's topological surgery theorem implies that the topological concordance group maps trivially to the algebraic concordance group, so Casson–Gordon invariants vanish for topologically slice knots. In examinations, students may be asked to define the algebraic concordance group, state Levine's classification, compute the Casson–Gordon invariant for the knot 6₁ (which is algebraically slice but has non-zero σ(6₁, χ) for some χ), or explain why the existence of a non-trivial Casson–Gordon invariant proves a knot is not smoothly slice.

  • Overlooking that Casson–Gordon invariants require a character of prime power order on H₁(Σ₂(K)); characters of composite order do not yield well-defined invariants in the original formulation.

Vassiliev Invariants, the Kontsevich Integral, and Finite Type Theory

A Vassiliev invariant (or finite type invariant) of degree ≤ n is a knot invariant v that vanishes on any singular knot with more than n double points, where a singular knot is an immersion of S¹ with only transverse double points as singularities. The extension to singular knots uses the Vassiliev skein relation v(⤫) = v(⤬) − v(⤭), where the three diagrams differ only at one crossing. The vector space of Vassiliev invariants of degree ≤ n modulo those of degree ≤ n−1 is finite-dimensional and dual to the space of weight systems on chord diagrams with n chords modulo the 4-term (4T) and 1-term (1T) relations. A chord diagram is a circle with n chords connecting 2n distinct points; the 4T relation arises from the Jacobi identity in the associated Lie algebra weight system, and the 1T relation corresponds to the framing independence of the invariant. The Kontsevich integral Z(K) is a universal finite type invariant taking values in the completed algebra 𝒜 of chord diagrams modulo 4T and 1T. It is constructed via the holonomy of the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov (KZ) connection on the configuration space of points in ℂ, and its value lies in the Drinfeld associator Φ ∈ 𝒜. The Drinfeld–Kohno theorem identifies the monodromy of the KZ equation with the action of the braid group on the tensor powers of a quantum group representation. The Melvin–Morton–Rozansky conjecture, proved by Bar-Natan and Garoufalidis, states that the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t) is recovered from the colored Jones polynomial J_N(K; q) by the substitution q = e^h and expanding in h: the coefficient of h^n is a Vassiliev invariant of degree n. The loop expansion of the Kontsevich integral expresses Z(K) as exp(∑k≥1 W_k(K) h^k) where W_k are primitive elements in 𝒜 corresponding to connected chord diagrams. The dimension of the space of weight systems of degree n grows rapidly; for n ≤ 12 the dimensions are known and Vassiliev invariants separate all knots up to 11 crossings, but it remains open whether they separate all knots. The Le–Murakami–Ohtsuki (LMO) invariant extends the Kontsevich integral to a universal finite type invariant of 3-manifolds, taking values in the algebra of Jacobi diagrams modulo the STU and IHX relations.

Instanton Floer Homology, the Frøyshov Invariant, and the Knot Surgery Formula

Instanton Floer homology, developed by Floer and extended by Donaldson, Kronheimer, and Mrowka, associates to a closed oriented 3-manifold Y a graded ℤ/8-vector space I_*(Y) (or I^#(Y) for the reduced theory) defined as the Morse homology of the Chern–Simons functional on the space of SU(2) connections modulo gauge. For a knot K ⊂ Y, the knot instanton homology I^#(Y, K) is defined using a perturbed Chern–Simons functional with a singularity along K, yielding a bifiltered chain complex whose homology is a knot invariant. The Frøyshov invariant h(Y) ∈ ℤ for an integral homology sphere Y is extracted from the grading shift in the exact triangle relating I^#(Y), I^#(Y₀), and I^#(Y₁) for a surgery triad; it satisfies h(−Y) = −h(Y) and provides a homomorphism from the homology cobordism group Θ³_ℤ to ℤ. For a knot K in S³, the knot surgery formula of Kronheimer and Mrowka relates the instanton homology of the 0-surgery S³₀(K) to the knot instanton homology I^#(S³, K) via a long exact sequence involving the maps induced by the cobordisms of the surgery triad. This formula was instrumental in proving Property P: if K is a non-trivial knot, then S³₀(K) is not simply connected. The instanton Frøyshov invariant for a knot, denoted h(K), is defined via the surgery formula and satisfies |h(K)| ≤ g₄(K). The spectral sequence from Khovanov homology to instanton knot homology, constructed by Kronheimer and Mrowka, converges to I^#(S³, K) and implies that the Rasmussen s-invariant satisfies s(K) = 2h(K) for many knots. The instanton homology also detects fibered knots: K is fibered if and only if the rank of I^#(S³, K) equals the absolute value of the Alexander polynomial evaluated at −1, a result of Kronheimer and Mrowka generalizing the Heegaard Floer characterization. The instanton homology groups are computable for torus knots and some satellite knots via the surgery formula and the excision theorem for instanton homology.

Worked Example: Computing the Frøyshov Invariant for the Right-Handed Trefoil
Show that the Frøyshov invariant h(T2,3) of the right-handed trefoil knot equals 1, using the instanton surgery formula and the fact that the 0-surgery S³₀(T2,3) is the Poincaré homology sphere Σ(2,3,5) with h(Σ(2,3,5)) = 1.
The right-handed trefoil T = T2,3 has 0-surgery S³₀(T) homeomorphic to the Poincaré homology sphere Σ(2,3,5), which bounds the E₈ plumbing 4-manifold. The Frøyshov invariant of Σ(2,3,5) is known to be h(Σ(2,3,5)) = 1 from the instanton homology calculation of Fintushel–Stern or from the gauge-theoretic interpretation of the E₈ manifold. The knot surgery formula for instanton homology gives an exact triangle relating I^#(S³), I^#(S³₀(T)), and I^#(S³₁(T)). Since S³₁(T) is the lens space L(1,1) = S³, its instanton homology is trivial in the reduced theory. The exact triangle then implies that the grading shift between I^#(S³) and I^#(Σ(2,3,5)) is determined by the knot invariant h(T). By definition, h(T) is the Frøyshov invariant of the knot, which equals the Frøyshov invariant of the 0-surgery when the 1-surgery is S³. Thus h(T) = h(Σ(2,3,5)) = 1. This matches the Rasmussen s-invariant s(T) = 2, since s(K) = 2h(K) for quasi-alternating knots, and the trefoil is quasi-alternating.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing Instanton from Heegaard Floer
Instanton Floer homology uses SU(2) connections and Chern–Simons theory; Heegaard Floer homology uses symplectic geometry and pseudo-holomorphic curves. Both yield knot invariants and 3-manifold invariants, but their exact triangles and surgery formulas have different algebraic structures. In questions, "instanton" implies gauge theory, "Frøyshov invariant" implies integer-valued homology cobordism invariant, and "Property P" is a classic instanton result. Heegaard Floer uses "τ", "υ", "ε" invariants and the Ozsváth–Szabó spectral sequence from Khovanov homology. Do not conflate the two spectral sequences: Kronheimer–Mrowka (Khovanov → instanton) vs. Ozsváth–Szabó (Khovanov → Heegaard Floer).

Fibered Knots, Monodromy, and the Thurston Norm

A knot K ⊂ S³ is fibered if its complement S³ ν(K) fibers over S¹ with fiber a Seifert surface Σ of minimal genus g(K). The monodromy φ: Σ → Σ is a homeomorphism fixing ∂Σ pointwise, well-defined up to isotopy, and its mapping class [φ] ∈ Mod(Σ, ∂Σ) determines the knot complement up to homeomorphism. The Thurston norm x on H₂(M, ∂M; ℤ) for a 3-manifold M measures the minimal complexity of surfaces representing a homology class; for a knot complement M = S³ ν(K), the class dual to the meridian has Thurston norm 2g(K) − 1. A class is fibered if it is represented by a fibration; the fibered classes form open cones (fibered faces) in the unit ball of the Thurston norm. Fried's theorem states that the monodromy of a fibered class is pseudo-Anosov unless the fiber is a disk or annulus. For a fibered knot, the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t) is monic of degree 2g(K) and its roots are the eigenvalues of the action of φ_* on H₁(Σ; ℂ). The Neuwirth–Stallings theorem characterizes fibered knots in S³ by the condition that the Alexander polynomial is monic and the knot group admits a presentation with a single relator that is a product of commutators. The Stallings exact sequence relates the homology of the infinite cyclic cover to the Alexander module. The open book decomposition of S³ induced by a fibered knot has binding K and pages Σ; by the Giroux correspondence, this open book supports the standard tight contact structure on S³. The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−S³) is non-zero, and the transverse invariant θ(K) ∈ HFK⁻(K) constructed by Ozsváth and Szabó distinguishes transverse knots with the same classical invariants. The monodromy φ can be factored into positive Dehn twists if and only if K is a positive braid knot; the minimal number of Dehn twists in such a factorization is the braid index minus one. The mapping class group action on the curve complex gives a dynamical invariant: the translation distance of φ on the curve complex is related to the volume of the mapping torus.

Transverse Knots, the Bennequin Bound, and the Transverse Invariant in Khovanov Homology

A transverse knot in a contact 3-manifold (Y, ξ) is an embedding of S¹ everywhere tangent to the contact planes ξ. The classical invariants are the topological knot type, the self-linking number sl(K) = lk(K, K') where K' is a push-off along a contact framing, and the Thurston–Bennequin invariant tb(L) for a Legendrian approximation L of K, related by sl(K) = tb(L) − 1. The Bennequin bound states that for any transverse knot K in the standard contact S³, sl(K) ≤ −χ(Σ) for any Seifert surface Σ bounded by K, with equality if and only if K is the transverse unknot or a transverse positive braid knot. Eliashberg and Fraser proved that the transverse unknot is uniquely determined by sl = −1. The transverse invariant ψ(K) ∈ Kh(K) constructed by Plamenevskaya lives in the Khovanov homology of the mirror knot with a specific grading shift; it is invariant under transverse isotopy and satisfies ψ(K) ≠ 0 for all transverse knots. The invariant is functorial under transverse cobordisms and vanishes for knots with sl(K) < −χ(Σ) − 2g₄(K). In knot Floer homology, the transverse invariant θ(K) ∈ HFK⁻(K) constructed by Ozsváth, Szabó, and Thurston has similar properties and is related to ψ(K) via the Ozsváth–Szabó spectral sequence. The classification of transverse knots up to transverse isotopy is known for torus knots: transverse representatives of Tp,q are classified by their self-linking number, which ranges from pq − p − q down to −pq + p + q in steps of 2. For the figure-eight knot, there are exactly two transverse isotopy classes with maximal self-linking number. The Legendrian invariants tb and rot lift to transverse invariants via the Legendrian approximation; the Chekanov–Eliashberg differential graded algebra of a Legendrian knot gives a transverse invariant by stabilizing. The transverse invariant in Khovanov homology can be computed combinatorially from a braid representation using the Plamenevskaya chain map, and it detects the transverse knot type for many low-crossing knots.

The Habiro Ring, Cyclotomic Expansions, and the Unified Invariant

The Habiro ring ℤ[q]^∧ = lim_← ℤ[q]/((q;q)_n) where (q;q)_n = ∏k=1^n (1−q^k) is the quantum factorial, is a completion of the polynomial ring ℤ[q] at the cyclotomic polynomials Φ_n(q). An element of the Habiro ring is a formal series f(q) = ∑k=0^∞ a_k(q) (q;q)_k with a_k(q) ∈ ℤ[q] of degree < k. The colored Jones polynomial J_N(K; q) of a knot K admits a cyclotomic expansion J_N(K; q) = ∑k=0^∞ c_k(K; q) (q;q)_k where the coefficients c_k(K; q) are invariants of finite type of degree ≤ k. This expansion converges in the Habiro ring and defines the unified invariant I(K) ∈ ℤ[q]^∧, which specializes at roots of unity to the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev (WRT) invariants of the 0-surgery S³₀(K). Habiro proved that the unified invariant of an integral homology sphere M is an element of the Habiro ring that determines all WRT invariants τ_N(M) for N ≥ 2. The cyclotomic expansion of the colored Jones polynomial is related to the Kontsevich integral via the LMO invariant: the coefficients c_k are universal finite type invariants. The AJ conjecture, relating the colored Jones polynomial to the A-polynomial, can be formulated in the Habiro ring as a q-difference equation for the unified invariant. The Habiro ring also appears in the study of the quantum group U_q(sl₂) at roots of unity: the category of tilting modules has a projective generator whose endomorphism ring is the Habiro ring. The unified invariant satisfies a surgery formula: for a framed link L in S³, the unified invariant of the 3-manifold obtained by surgery on L is given by a state sum involving the colored Jones polynomials of the components and the linking matrix. The Habiro ring is a principal ideal domain, and its units are the series with constant term ±1. The cyclotomic expansion provides a bridge between quantum topology at roots of unity and finite type invariants, and the unified invariant is a universal quantum invariant for integral homology spheres.

The Gordon–Luecke Theorem and Knot Complements

The Gordon–Luecke theorem (1989) states that if two knots in the 3‑sphere have homeomorphic complements, then the knots are equivalent (ambient isotopic or mirror images). In other words, a knot is determined by its complement up to orientation reversal. The proof uses the JSJ decomposition of the knot complement and the theory of Dehn surgery. A key step is showing that a homeomorphism between complements extends to a homeomorphism of the surgered manifolds for all but finitely many slopes, and then applying the cyclic surgery theorem of Culler–Gordon–Luecke–Shalen. The theorem fails for links: the Whitehead link and the Borromean rings have homeomorphic complements but are not equivalent. For knots in other 3‑manifolds, the statement is false; there are distinct knots in lens spaces with homeomorphic complements. The theorem implies that the knot group π₁(S³K) together with the peripheral system (the conjugacy class of the meridian and longitude) determines the knot. This is stronger than the statement that the knot group alone determines the knot, which is false (there are distinct knots with isomorphic groups, e.g., the square knot and the granny knot). The Gordon–Luecke theorem is a cornerstone of the “knots are determined by their complements” philosophy and underpins the use of hyperbolic geometry in knot theory: for hyperbolic knots, the complement is a complete finite‑volume hyperbolic manifold, and Mostow rigidity implies the knot is determined by the hyperbolic structure.

The Tait Conjectures and the Jones Polynomial

The three Tait conjectures, posed by Peter Guthrie Tait in the 1880s based on his tabulation of alternating knots, were open for a century until the discovery of the Jones polynomial. The first conjecture: any reduced alternating diagram of a knot has minimal crossing number. The second: any two reduced alternating diagrams of the same knot have the same writhe (the sum of crossing signs). The third: the set of crossing numbers of alternating diagrams of a given knot is an interval of integers. Kauffman, Murasugi, and Thistlethwaite proved the first two conjectures in 1987 using the Jones polynomial (or the Kauffman bracket). The key is the span of the Jones polynomial: for an alternating knot, the span equals 4 times the crossing number of any reduced alternating diagram. This gives a lower bound on crossing number that is achieved by the diagram, proving minimality. The writhe conjecture follows from the fact that the second coefficient of the Jones polynomial (or the extreme coefficients of the Kauffman bracket) is determined by the writhe. The third conjecture was proved by Menasco and Thistlethwaite (1991) using the Jones polynomial and the theory of flypes. A flype is a local move on a diagram that rotates a tangle; Tait conjectured that any two reduced alternating diagrams of the same knot are related by flypes. The flype theorem implies the third conjecture. These results established the Jones polynomial as a powerful tool for classical knot theory and sparked the study of quantum invariants. The span formula also gives a quick way to detect non‑alternating knots: if the span of V_K(t) is less than 4c(K) for some diagram, the knot is non‑alternating.

Worked Example: Detecting Non‑Alternating Knots via Span
The knot 8₁₈ has a diagram with 8 crossings. Its Jones polynomial is V(t) = t⁻⁴ − t⁻³ + t⁻² − 2t⁻¹ + 2 − 2t + t² − t³ + t⁴. Compute the span and determine whether 8₁₈ can be alternating.
The Jones polynomial is a Laurent polynomial. The highest power of t is 4, the lowest is −4. The span is max degree − min degree = 4 − (−4) = 8. For an alternating knot with a reduced diagram of c crossings, the span must equal 4c. Here c = 8, so 4c = 32. The actual span is 8, which is strictly less than 32. Therefore 8₁₈ cannot be alternating. (Indeed, 8₁₈ is the first non‑alternating knot in the Rolfsen table.)
Exam Strategy: Tait Conjectures
When a question asks “Which invariant proved the Tait conjectures?” the answer is the Jones polynomial (or Kauffman bracket). Remember the span formula: span(V_K) = 4c for reduced alternating diagrams. If a knot has a diagram with c crossings but span(V_K) < 4c, the knot is non‑alternating. This is a standard short‑answer or multiple‑choice trap: they give a polynomial and a crossing number and ask whether the knot is alternating.

Mutation and the Kinoshita–Terasaka / Conway Pair

Mutation is an operation on a knot or link diagram: remove a 2‑strand tangle, rotate it by 180° about one of three axes (vertical, horizontal, or diagonal), and reglue. The resulting knot is called a mutant. Many classical invariants cannot distinguish mutants. The Alexander polynomial, the Jones polynomial, the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial, and the hyperbolic volume are all mutation‑invariant. The Kinoshita–Terasaka knot (11n42) and the Conway knot (11n34) are the simplest pair of distinct mutant knots; both have 11 crossings, Alexander polynomial 1, and identical Jones polynomials. They were the first known pair of distinct knots with the same Jones polynomial. The HOMFLY‑PT polynomial also fails to distinguish them. However, the Kauffman polynomial (the two‑variable polynomial for unoriented links) does distinguish them, as does the Khovanov homology (Khovanov homology detects the Conway knot but not the Kinoshita–Terasaka knot? Actually, Khovanov homology distinguishes them; the Rasmussen s‑invariant is 0 for both, but the full Khovanov homology groups differ). The knot Floer homology also distinguishes them. The colored Jones polynomials at higher colors distinguish mutants in general (the Melvin–Morton–Rozansky expansion shows that the coefficients of the colored Jones polynomial are finite‑type invariants, and some finite‑type invariants detect mutation). The hyperbolic volume is the same for mutants because mutation corresponds to an isometry of the hyperbolic structure on the tangle complement. The property of being a mutant is closely related to the existence of a Conway sphere (a 4‑punctured sphere intersecting the knot in four points). A knot is a mutant if and only if it has a Conway sphere that is not boundary‑parallel and the tangle replacement yields a different knot. The study of mutation led to the development of invariants that are sensitive to the embedding of the Conway sphere, such as the sutured Floer homology and the bordered Floer homology of the tangle complement.

The Bridge Number and the Tunnel Number of Knots

The bridge number b(K) of a knot is the minimum number of local maxima in a Morse function (height function) on S³ restricted to the knot, over all embeddings of K. Equivalently, it is the minimum number of bridges in a bridge presentation. The unknot has bridge number 1; a knot is 2‑bridge (or rational) if and only if it is a rational knot, classified by Schubert via the continued fraction of a rational number. The bridge number is additive under connected sum for prime knots? No, it is subadditive: b(K₁#K₂) ≤ b(K₁) + b(K₂) − 1, with equality for many cases but not all (e.g., certain satellite knots). The tunnel number t(K) is the minimum number of arcs (tunnels) that must be added to K so that the complement becomes a handlebody. Equivalently, t(K) = g(S³K) − 1 where g is the Heegaard genus of the knot complement. The unknot has tunnel number 0; 2‑bridge knots have tunnel number 1. The tunnel number is not additive under connected sum; it satisfies t(K₁#K₂) ≤ t(K₁) + t(K₂) + 1. The bridge number and tunnel number are related by t(K) ≤ 2b(K) − 1. For hyperbolic knots, the bridge number is bounded below by a function of the volume (Agol, Lackenby). The bridge number of a satellite knot is at least the bridge number of its pattern. The tunnel number is a key invariant in 3‑manifold topology: a knot with tunnel number 1 has a complement that is a genus‑2 handlebody, and its Dehn surgeries are well‑studied. The classification of tunnel‑number‑1 knots is a major open problem; they include all 2‑bridge knots, many torus knots, and some satellite knots. The bridge number is also the minimum genus of a Heegaard surface for the knot complement that intersects the knot in a single arc? No, that is the tunnel number. The bridge number is the minimum number of intersections of the knot with a Heegaard sphere in a bridge position.

The Property P Theorem and Cosmetic Surgery

The Property P conjecture, proved by Kronheimer and Mrowka (2004) using instanton Floer homology, states that if a non‑trivial knot in S³ admits a Dehn surgery yielding a simply connected 3‑manifold, then the surgery slope must be the meridian (i.e., the result is S³ itself). In other words, no non‑trivial Dehn surgery on a non‑trivial knot can produce a simply connected manifold other than S³. This is equivalent to saying that the knot group π₁(S³K) has no non‑cyclic quotient that is a homotopy sphere group. The proof uses the fact that if a knot has Property P, then the instanton Floer homology of the surgered manifold satisfies a certain non‑vanishing property. The theorem implies the Gordon–Luecke theorem (knots are determined by their complements) because if two knots have homeomorphic complements, then 0‑surgery on one is homeomorphic to 0‑surgery on the other, and the fundamental groups are isomorphic; Property P forces the knots to be equivalent. Cosmetic surgery is the phenomenon where two different slopes on the same knot yield homeomorphic 3‑manifolds. The Cosmetic Surgery Conjecture asserts that no knot in S³ admits a purely cosmetic surgery (i.e., two distinct slopes producing homeomorphic manifolds). This is known for many classes: alternating knots, torus knots, satellite knots with certain patterns, and knots with non‑zero Alexander polynomial. The conjecture is open in general. The Berge conjecture (mentioned in the Dehn surgery section) classifies knots that admit a lens space surgery; these are exactly the knots that admit a cosmetic surgery with a lens space? No, Berge knots are those that admit a lens space surgery; the cosmetic surgery conjecture says no knot admits two distinct slopes giving the same manifold. The Property P theorem is a deep result connecting gauge theory to classical topology, and its proof introduced the “knot surgery formula” for instanton Floer homology, which relates the instanton homology of a surgered manifold to the knot instanton homology of the original knot.

  • Assuming the span of the Jones polynomial always equals 4c for alternating knots; this holds only for reduced diagrams. A non‑reduced alternating diagram has extra crossings that reduce the span.
  • Believing all quantum invariants (Jones, HOMFLY‑PT) are mutation‑invariant; the Kauffman polynomial (unoriented) and Khovanov homology are not, and colored Jones polynomials at higher colors can detect mutation.
  • Thinking bridge number and tunnel number are the same; bridge number is about height functions on the knot, tunnel number is about Heegaard genus of the complement. They satisfy t(K) ≤ 2b(K) − 1 but are distinct invariants.
  • Assuming the Property P theorem implies the Cosmetic Surgery Conjecture; Property P rules out simply connected cosmetic surgeries, but cosmetic surgeries could yield manifolds with non‑trivial fundamental group (e.g., lens spaces).
  • Overlooking that the Tait conjectures were proved using the Kauffman bracket/Jones polynomial, not the Alexander polynomial; the Alexander polynomial cannot detect crossing number minimality.
  • Confusing the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway knots with the square and granny knots; the former are mutants with Alexander polynomial 1, the latter are connected sums of trefoils with isomorphic groups but different Alexander polynomials.

The Neumann–Zagier Asymptotics for Hyperbolic Dehn Surgery

For a hyperbolic knot K in S³, the complement S³ K carries a complete finite‑volume hyperbolic structure. Dehn filling along a slope p/q yields a closed 3‑manifold M(p/q). Neumann and Zagier (1985) showed that the complex hyperbolic structures on the filled manifolds are parametrised by a smooth complex curve C in ℂ² defined by the gluing equations. Near the complete structure the curve is analytically isomorphic to the zero set of a single holomorphic function F(z,w)=0 with F(0,0)=0. The coordinates z,w are the logarithms of the eigenvalues of the meridian and longitude holonomies. The main asymptotic formula states that the complex length ℓ(p/q) of the core geodesic in M(p/q) satisfies

ℓ(p/q) = 2πi (p τ + q) + O(e-c|p|)

where τ is the complex modulus of the cusp torus (the ratio of the meridian and longitude translations) and c>0 depends only on the knot. This expansion gives a precise description of how the hyperbolic metric degenerates as the surgery coefficient tends to infinity. The symplectic form dz ∧ dw on C pulls back to the Weil–Petersson form on the deformation variety, and the Neumann–Zagier potential function Φ(z,w) satisfies dΦ = w dz - z dw. The coefficients of the Taylor expansion of Φ at the origin are rational functions of the p/q and encode the volumes of the filled manifolds via the Schlӓfli formula. These asymptotics are the foundation for the volume conjecture for Dehn surgery and for the computation of the Chern–Simons invariants of M(p/q) in the semiclassical limit.

Applications include the proof by Hodgson and Kerckhoff (1998) that the volume function Vol(M(p/q)) extends real‑analytically to the space of cone‑manifold structures, and the work of Dimofte–Gaiotto–Gukov (2010) linking the Neumann–Zagier data to the 3d‑3d correspondence in supersymmetric gauge theory. The asymptotic expansion also provides an effective algorithm for computing the hyperbolic volume of a large class of Dehn surgeries to arbitrary precision, which is used in the SnapPy census of hyperbolic manifolds.

The Heegaard Floer Homology of Double Branched Covers

Given a knot K ⊂ S³, let Σ₂(K) denote the double cover of S³ branched along K. Ozsváth and Szabó (2005) constructed a spectral sequence whose E² page is the reduced Khovanov homology Kh̃(K) (with coefficients in 𝔽₂) and which converges to the Heegaard Floer homology HF̂(Σ₂(K)). The differential on the E² page is induced by the cube of resolutions used in the definition of Khovanov homology, and the higher differentials correspond to the holomorphic triangle counts in the Heegaard Floer package. This spectral sequence is functorial with respect to knot cobordisms and respects the δ-grading on Khovanov homology, which corresponds to the Maslov grading on HF̂.

A key consequence is the inequality |σ(K)| ≤ 2 g₄(K), where σ(K) is the knot signature and g₄(K) the smooth slice genus, recovered from the d-invariant (correction term) of Σ₂(K). The d-invariant of the branched cover also gives a lower bound for the four‑dimensional clasp number and for the unknotting number. For alternating knots the spectral sequence collapses at the E² page, so HF̂(Σ₂(K)) ≅ Kh̃(K) as relatively graded groups. This identification was used by Manolescu and Ozsváth (2007) to prove that the Khovanov homology of an alternating knot determines the Heegaard Floer homology of its double branched cover, and hence the knot’s genus and fiberedness.

Further developments include the work of Baldwin and Levine (2015) showing that the spectral sequence can be refined to a ℤ-graded version using the τ-invariant from knot Floer homology, and the result of Hendricks–Manolescu (2017) that the involution on HF̂(Σ₂(K)) induced by the covering transformation corresponds to the Lee deformation on Khovanov homology. These connections have led to new constraints on the slice genus of knots with thin Khovanov homology and to a proof that the Rasmussen s-invariant equals twice the τ-invariant for quasi‑alternating knots.

Worked Example: Computing the d‑invariant of Σ₂(T₂,₃)
Let K be the right‑hand trefoil T₂,₃. Its double branched cover is the lens space L(3,1). Compute the Heegaard Floer correction term d(L(3,1)) and deduce a bound on the slice genus of K.
The lens space L(p,q) has Spinᶜ structures indexed by i ∈ {0,…,p-1}. The correction term is d(L(p,q), i) = ( (2i - p + 1)² - p ) / (4p) . For p=3, q=1 we obtain d(L(3,1),0) = -2/3, d(L(3,1),1) = 1/6, d(L(3,1),2) = 1/6. The maximal d-invariant is 1/6. By the Ozsváth–Szabó inequality g₄(K) ≥ |d(Σ₂(K))|/2, we get g₄(T₂,₃) ≥ 1/12, which forces g₄(T₂,₃) ≥ 1 because the slice genus is an integer. Indeed the trefoil has slice genus 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a bound on the slice genus from a branched cover, first identify the cover (often a lens space for 2‑bridge knots). Recall the explicit formula for d(L(p,q),i) and the inequality g₄ ≥ |d|/2. Write the formula, plug in the numbers, and conclude the integer bound. This avoids recomputing the whole spectral sequence.

The Knot Concordance Group and the Homology Cobordism Group

The set of smooth concordance classes of oriented knots in S³ forms an abelian group 𝒞 under connected sum, with inverse given by the mirror image. Levine (1969) defined the algebraic concordance group 𝒢 using Seifert forms modulo metabolic forms, and constructed a homomorphism 𝒞 → 𝒢 sending a knot to its Seifert form. The kernel of this map contains all knots with trivial Alexander polynomial. Casson and Gordon (1978, 1986) introduced invariants σ(K, χ) associated to characters χ: H₁(Σ₂(K)) → ℂ× of prime power order, proving that many Alexander‑polynomial‑one knots are not slice. Their invariants factor through the homology cobordism group Θ³_ℤ of oriented homology 3‑spheres, giving a homomorphism 𝒞 → Θ³_ℤ via the zero‑surgery manifold M₀(K).

In the 2010s, Manolescu (2016) used Pin(2)‑equivariant Seiberg–Witten Floer homology to define a homomorphism Θ³_ℤ → ℤ ⊕ ℤ ⊕ ℤ₂ (the α, β, γ invariants) and showed that the kernel of 𝒞 → Θ³_ℤ contains a ℤ^∞ subgroup generated by certain topologically slice but not smoothly slice knots. The work of Akbulut–Matveyev (2018) and of Piccirillo (2020) on the Conway knot demonstrated that the concordance group contains elements of infinite order not detected by any classical invariant. The current picture is that 𝒞 surjects onto a large direct sum of copies of ℤ and ℤ₂, while the algebraic concordance group 𝒢 is isomorphic to ℤ^∞ ⊕ (ℤ₂)^∞ ⊕ (ℤ₄)^∞. The relationship between 𝒞 and the homology cobordism group remains a central open problem; in particular it is unknown whether the natural map 𝒞 → Θ³_ℤ is surjective.

Practical consequences for exam problems: the Casson–Gordon invariants give a computable obstruction to sliceness for knots with Δ_K(t)=1. The τ-invariant from knot Floer homology and the Rasmussen s-invariant provide lower bounds for the smooth concordance order. The Upsilon invariant Υ_K(t) (Ozsváth–Stipsicz–Szabó, 2017) gives a family of concordance homomorphisms 𝒞 → ℝ that detect many infinite‑order elements. When a problem asks to show a knot is not slice, check the Alexander polynomial first; if it is 1, compute a Casson–Gordon signature or a τ invariant.

The Knot Group, Residual Finiteness, and the Virtual Fibering Conjecture

The fundamental group π₁(S³ K) is a classical invariant that determines the knot complement up to homeomorphism for many classes (Gordon–Luecke). Hempel (1987) proved that every knot group is residually finite: for any non‑trivial element g there exists a finite quotient in which the image of g is non‑trivial. The proof uses the fact that knot groups are subgroups of SL(2,ℂ) via the holonomy representation of the hyperbolic structure (for hyperbolic knots) or of the Seifert‑fibered structure (for satellite and torus knots), and that linear groups over fields of characteristic zero are residually finite (Mal’cev). Residual finiteness implies that the word problem for knot groups is solvable and that the profinite completion of the knot group determines the knot for many families.

The Virtual Fibering Conjecture, proved by Agol (2013) building on work of Wise (2012) on cubulated groups, states that every finite‑volume hyperbolic 3‑manifold has a finite cover that fibers over the circle. For a hyperbolic knot K, this means there exists a finite‑sheeted cover M̃ → S³ K such that M̃ is a surface bundle over S¹. The monodromy of this fibration gives a representation of the knot group onto a free‑by‑cyclic group. A consequence is that the knot group is virtually residually free, and hence virtually bi‑orderable. The virtual fibering theorem also implies that the knot group has a finite‑index subgroup with positive first Betti number, which in turn yields non‑trivial homomorphisms to ℤ and thus non‑trivial Alexander polynomials for the finite covers.

These results have concrete algorithmic applications: the residual finiteness of knot groups allows one to enumerate all finite quotients up to a given order, which is used in the SnapPy census to distinguish knot complements. The virtual fibering theorem guarantees that for any hyperbolic knot there is a finite cover with a non‑zero Alexander polynomial, providing a method to produce new knot invariants from the homology of the fiber. In exam questions, you may be asked to explain why the knot group of the figure‑eight knot is residually finite (exhibit the holonomy representation into SL(2,ℂ) and invoke Mal’cev’s theorem) or to state the implication of virtual fibering for the existence of a non‑trivial homomorphism to ℤ in a finite cover.

The Kauffman Bracket Skein Module of the 3‑Torus and the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev Invariants

For a compact oriented 3‑manifold M, the Kauffman bracket skein module 𝒮(M) is the vector space over ℂ[A±1] generated by framed links in M modulo the Kauffman bracket relations. When M = T³ (the 3‑torus), the module has a particularly rich structure. Przytycki and Sikora (2000) showed that 𝒮(T³) is a free module of infinite rank over the ring of symmetric polynomials in three variables, and that its specialization at a root of unity A = eπi/(2r) is isomorphic to the space of functions on the SL(2,ℂ) character variety of T³ that are invariant under the mapping class group SL(3,ℤ). The character variety of T³ is (ℂ×)³ / S₃, and the skein module at a root of unity can be identified with the space of SL(3,ℤ)-invariant Laurent polynomials.

The Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev (WRT) invariant τ_r(M) of a closed 3‑manifold M at level r can be computed from the skein module of a Heegaard splitting. For M = T³, the WRT invariant equals the dimension of the space of conformal blocks of the SU(2) WZW model at level r-2 on the 3‑torus, which is known to be r³. This matches the rank of the specialized skein module 𝒮(T³)A=eπi/(2r). The isomorphism between the skein module and the space of conformal blocks is a manifestation of the 3d‑3d correspondence: the skein module is the Hilbert space of the Chern–Simons theory on T³, and the WRT invariant is the partition function on T³.

Recent work of Gunningham, Jordan, and Safronov (2020) extended this picture to arbitrary 3‑manifolds, proving that the skein module at a root of unity carries a natural action of the mapping class group and that the WRT invariant is the trace of the identity operator on this space. For exam purposes, the key facts are: (1) 𝒮(T³) is a free module over the ring of symmetric polynomials; (2) at a root of unity it is finite‑dimensional and its dimension equals the WRT invariant; (3) the mapping class group SL(3,ℤ) acts by algebra automorphisms, and the invariant subspace computes the WRT invariant of any manifold obtained by gluing two handlebodies along a mapping class. Problems often ask to compute the dimension of 𝒮(T³) at A = i (level 2) or to explain why the WRT invariant of T³ is a perfect cube.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Neumann–Zagier potential with the Chern–Simons functional; the potential governs the symplectic structure on the deformation variety, not the action.
  • Assuming the spectral sequence from Khovanov homology to HF̂(Σ₂(K)) always collapses at E²; it collapses only for quasi‑alternating knots.
  • Thinking the Casson–Gordon invariants detect all non‑slice knots with trivial Alexander polynomial; they miss many knots that require τ or s invariants.
  • Believing residual finiteness of knot groups implies the knot group is linear over ℤ; linearity holds over ℂ but not necessarily over ℤ.
  • Stating that the virtual fibering theorem gives a fibration of the original knot complement; it only guarantees a fibration after passing to a finite cover.
  • Equating the dimension of the specialized skein module of T³ with the number of irreducible SL(2,ℂ) representations; the dimension equals the WRT invariant, which counts all representations weighted by quantum dimensions.
  • Using the formula for d(L(p,q),i) without checking the orientation convention; the sign of the correction term changes with the orientation of the lens space.

The Chekanov–Eliashberg Differential Graded Algebra and Legendrian Contact Homology

The Chekanov–Eliashberg differential graded algebra (DGA) provides a combinatorial invariant of Legendrian knots in the standard contact ℝ³. Given a Legendrian knot Λ with a generic front projection, one assigns a unital associative algebra over ℤ₂ generated by Reeb chords — the double points of the projection where the strand with greater slope passes over the one with lesser slope. The differential counts rigid holomorphic disks in the symplectization ℝ × ℝ³ with boundary on ℝ × Λ and with positive puncture at a given Reeb chord. The resulting homology, called Legendrian contact homology, is invariant under Legendrian isotopy. The DGA is functorial with respect to exact Lagrangian cobordisms, and its augmentation category yields further invariants such as the ruling polynomial. For the standard Legendrian unknot with maximal Thurston–Bennequin number, the DGA is acyclic, while for the Legendrian trefoil with tb = −6 the DGA admits non‑trivial augmentations that distinguish it from the unknot. The construction extends to higher‑dimensional Legendrian submanifolds via the work of Ekholm, Etnyre, and Sullivan, where the differential counts pseudo‑holomorphic disks with boundary on the Lagrangian projection. In exam questions you may be asked to compute the DGA for a given front diagram, to explain why the differential squares to zero (the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space consists of broken disks), or to describe how an augmentation ε : 𝒜 → ℤ₂ induces a linearized complex whose homology is the linearized contact homology. A common twist is to present a front with a single cusp and ask whether the resulting DGA is trivial; the answer depends on the Maslov potential and the grading of the Reeb chords.

The Soergel Bimodule Categorification of the HOMFLY‑PT Polynomial

Soergel bimodules give a purely algebraic categorification of the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial for links. Let R = ℚ[x₁,…,xₙ] be the polynomial ring with the natural action of the symmetric group Sₙ. For each simple reflection sᵢ one defines the Soergel bimodule Bₛᵢ = R ⊗Rsᵢ R(1), where Rsᵢ is the subring of sᵢ‑invariants and (1) denotes a grading shift. The monoidal category of Soergel bimodules SBimₙ is generated by these objects under tensor product and direct sum. The braid group Bₙ acts on SBimₙ via the Rouquier complexes: to a positive crossing σᵢ one assigns the complex 0 → Bₛᵢ → R(1) → 0, and to a negative crossing the dual complex. The homotopy class of the resulting complex of bimodules is invariant under braid relations, yielding a well‑defined functor from the braid category to the homotopy category of SBimₙ. Taking the Hochschild homology of this complex recovers the triply graded HOMFLY‑PT homology of Khovanov–Rozansky. The Euler characteristic of the Hochschild homology, with appropriate grading shifts, equals the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial. This construction explains the positivity of the coefficients in the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial for certain links and provides a framework for proving the Rasmussen conjecture on the s‑invariant. In examinations you may be required to write down the Rouquier complex for a given braid word, to compute the Hochschild homology of a simple bimodule such as Bₛᵢ ⊗ Bₛⱼ, or to explain why the grading shift (1) appears in the definition of Bₛᵢ (it corresponds to the quantum grading in the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial). A typical trap is to confuse the grading conventions: the homological grading of the Rouquier complex, the internal grading of the bimodules, and the quantum grading of the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial must be tracked simultaneously.

Technique
Given the braid word σ₁σ₂⁻¹σ₁ in B₃, write the associated Rouquier complex of Soergel bimodules up to homotopy equivalence, indicating the grading shifts on each term.
The positive crossing σ₁ gives the complex C(σ₁) = [0 → Bₛ₁ → R(1) → 0] with Bₛ₁ in homological degree 0 and R(1) in degree 1. The negative crossing σ₂⁻¹ gives the dual complex C(σ₂⁻¹) = [0 → R(−1) → Bₛ₂ → 0] with R(−1) in degree −1 and Bₛ₂ in degree 0. The second σ₁ gives another copy of C(σ₁). The total complex is the tensor product (over R) of these three complexes, taken in the monoidal category SBim₃. After simplifying using the bimodule relations Bₛᵢ ⊗ Bₛᵢ ≅ Bₛᵢ(1) ⊕ Bₛᵢ(−1) and the fact that Bₛ₁ and Bₛ₂ commute up to homotopy, the resulting complex is homotopy equivalent to a direct sum of shifts of the identity bimodule R. The explicit grading shifts depend on the chosen normalization; a standard answer lists the terms as R(−2) ⊕ R(0)² ⊕ R(2) in homological degrees −1, 0, 1 respectively.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for a Rouquier complex, first write the elementary complexes for each crossing, then tensor them in order. Use the known isomorphism Bₛ ⊗ Bₛ ≅ Bₛ(1) ⊕ Bₛ(−1) to reduce the tensor product. Keep a table of homological degree, internal bimodule grading, and quantum grading to avoid sign errors.

The Profinite Completion of the Knot Group and the Grothendieck–Teichmüller Group

The profinite completion Ĝ of a knot group G = π₁(S³ K) encodes all finite quotients of G. By the work of Friedl and Vidussi, the profinite completion of a knot group determines the knot up to finitely many possibilities, and for hyperbolic knots it often determines the knot completely. The absolute Galois group Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) acts on the profinite completion of the fundamental group of ℙ¹ {0,1,∞}, and this action extends to the profinite completions of knot groups via the theory of dessins d’enfants. The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group GT̂, a pro‑algebraic group introduced by Drinfeld, sits inside the automorphism group of the profinite completion of the free group on two generators. There is a natural homomorphism GT̂ → Aut(Ĝ) for any knot group G, and the image of this homomorphism captures deep arithmetic information about the knot. For example, the cyclotomic character of Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) appears in the action on the profinite completion of the trefoil knot group. In exam problems you may be asked to describe the profinite completion of the figure‑eight knot group (it is the profinite completion of the Baumslag–Solitar group BS(1,−1)), to explain why the profinite completion of a knot group is residually finite (knot groups are linear over ℤ, hence residually finite by Mal’cev’s theorem), or to state the relationship between the Alexander polynomial and the abelianization of the profinite completion (the profinite abelianization is the profinite completion of ℤ, and the Alexander polynomial appears as the characteristic polynomial of the monodromy action on the first homology of the infinite cyclic cover). A frequent trap is to assume that the profinite completion of a knot group is a free profinite group; it is not, because knot groups have non‑trivial relations that survive in the profinite completion.

The Residual Nilpotence of the Knot Group and the Chen–Milnor Theory

A group G is residually nilpotent if the intersection of its lower central series Γₖ(G) is trivial. For knot groups, residual nilpotence was proved by Stallings using the fact that the Alexander module is torsion‑free over the Laurent polynomial ring ℤ[t±¹]. The Chen–Milnor theory identifies the graded Lie algebra ⊕ₖ Γₖ(G)/Γₖ₊₁(G) ⊗ ℚ with the Lie algebra of primitive elements in the completed group algebra ℚ⟨⟨G⟩⟩. The Magnus expansion embeds G into the group of units of the formal power series ring ℤ⟨⟨X₁,…,Xₙ⟩⟩, and the coefficients of the expansion give the Milnor μ‑invariants of the link. For a knot, all μ‑invariants of length ≤ 2 vanish, but higher‑order invariants can be non‑zero and detect subtle linking phenomena in the infinite cyclic cover. The residual nilpotence implies that the knot group injects into its Mal’cev completion, a pro‑unipotent group whose Lie algebra is the completion of the graded Lie algebra. This completion carries a natural mixed Hodge structure, and its weight filtration corresponds to the lower central series. In examinations you may be asked to compute the first few terms of the lower central series for the trefoil knot group (Γ₁/Γ₂ ≅ ℤ, Γ₂/Γ₃ ≅ ℤ, Γ₃/Γ₄ ≅ ℤ²), to explain why the Alexander polynomial Δ(t) = t² − t + 1 appears as the determinant of the action of t on Γ₂/Γ₃ ⊗ ℚ, or to describe how the Chen iterated integrals on the knot complement give rise to the Drinfeld associator. A common mistake is to confuse the lower central series with the derived series; the derived series quotients are the Alexander invariants, while the lower central series quotients are the Chen–Milnor invariants.

The Quantum Invariants from the Quantum Group U_q(slₙ) and the Colored HOMFLY‑PT Polynomials

The quantum group U_q(slₙ) yields a family of link invariants by assigning to each component of a link a finite‑dimensional irreducible representation V(λ) labeled by a dominant weight λ. The resulting invariant, the colored HOMFLY‑PT polynomial Pλ₁,…,λₖ(q,a), specializes to the colored Jones polynomial when n = 2 and a = qⁿ. The representation theory of U_q(slₙ) is governed by the R‑matrix, which satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation. The invariants can be computed via the Reshetikhin–Turaev functor from the category of ribbon graphs to the category of U_q(slₙ)‑modules. For a knot, the colored HOMFLY‑PT polynomial satisfies the cabling formula: the invariant colored by the symmetric power Symᵐ(V) equals the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial of the (m,1)‑cable of the knot. The asymptotic behavior of the colored HOMFLY‑PT polynomials as the color grows is conjectured to determine the hyperbolic volume of the knot complement (the volume conjecture for slₙ). In exam questions you may be required to write the R‑matrix for U_q(sl₃) in the standard basis, to compute the quantum dimension of the representation with highest weight (1,0) (it equals [3]_q = (q³−q⁻³)/(q−q⁻¹)), or to explain how the cabling formula follows from the decomposition of tensor products of symmetric powers. A typical trap is to forget the framing factor: the Reshetikhin–Turaev invariant of a framed link includes a factor of the ribbon element raised to the writhe, and omitting it leads to an invariant that is not ambient isotopy invariant.

  • Confusing the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA with the linearized contact homology; the DGA is non‑commutative and its homology is not the linearized version.
  • Misplacing the grading shifts in the Rouquier complex; the shift (1) on R corresponds to the quantum grading, not the homological grading.
  • Assuming the profinite completion of a knot group is a free profinite group; relations from the knot group survive in the profinite completion.
  • Mixing the lower central series with the derived series when computing Chen–Milnor invariants; they give different graded Lie algebras.
  • Omitting the ribbon element framing factor in the Reshetikhin–Turaev construction; the resulting polynomial is not invariant under Reidemeister I.
  • Treating the colored HOMFLY‑PT polynomial as a single‑variable polynomial; it depends on both q and a (or q and the rank n).
  • Believing that the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group acts trivially on all knot group profinite completions; the action is highly non‑trivial and encodes arithmetic data.

These advanced topics illustrate how algebraic, geometric, and quantum methods intertwine in modern knot theory and provide the depth expected in competitive examinations.

Annular Khovanov Homology and the Transverse Invariant

Annular Khovanov homology is a refinement of the original Khovanov homology that takes into account an additional annular grading coming from the embedding of a link in the solid torus S¹ × D². The construction starts from a link diagram on the annulus A = S¹ × [0,1] and assigns to each crossing a bigraded chain complex as in the usual theory, but the differential preserves a third grading j that records the winding number of the resolution curves around the core of the annulus. The resulting triply graded homology groups AKhi,j,k(L) are invariant under annular Reidemeister moves and provide a powerful invariant of transverse links via the Bennequin–Eliashberg correspondence. Plamenevskaya showed that the homology class of the transverse invariant ψ(L) ∈ AKh0,0,0(L) is non‑zero for all transverse links and distinguishes many transverse knots that share the same classical invariants such as the self‑linking number. The annular grading also interacts with the Lee spectral sequence; the s‑invariant of Rasmussen can be recovered from the annular theory by collapsing the annular grading, and the transverse invariant maps to the top‑degree generator in Lee homology. In examinations you may be asked to compute the annular Khovanov homology of the right‑hand trefoil presented as a closed 3‑braid, to explain why the annular grading is preserved by the differential, or to describe how the transverse invariant gives a lower bound for the transverse Bennequin number. A common mistake is to confuse the annular grading with the quantum grading; the annular grading is additive under disjoint union, whereas the quantum grading shifts by the writhe of the diagram.

Knot Floer Homology, the Alexander Polynomial, and the Tau Invariant

Knot Floer homology HFK⁻(S³,K) is a bigraded ℤ[U]‑module associated to a knot K ⊂ S³ that categorifies the Alexander polynomial. The two gradings are the Maslov grading M and the Alexander grading A. The Euler characteristic of HFK⁻ with respect to the Alexander grading recovers the symmetrized Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t). Ozsváth and Szabó defined the concordance invariant τ(K) as the minimal Alexander grading of a homogeneous element in the image of the U‑action that generates a free ℤ[U]‑summand. Equivalently, τ(K) is the grading of the generator of the top‑most non‑zero summand in the filtered chain homotopy type. The invariant satisfies τ(K₁ # K₂) = τ(K₁) + τ(K₂), τ(‑K) = -τ(K), and provides a lower bound for the slice genus: g₄(K) ≥ |τ(K)|. For alternating knots, τ(K) equals the maximal degree of the Alexander polynomial, and for the right‑hand trefoil τ = 1. The τ invariant also controls the behaviour of the knot under Dehn surgery: if τ(K) > 0 then the 3‑manifold obtained by +1 surgery on K is an L‑space. In exam questions you may be required to compute τ for the figure‑eight knot (which is zero), to explain why τ vanishes for slice knots, or to derive the inequality τ(K) ≤ g₃(K) where g₃ is the Seifert genus. A typical trap is to confuse τ with the Rasmussen s invariant; although both give slice genus bounds, s(K) = 2τ(K) for quasi‑alternating knots but they differ in general.

Worked Example: Computing τ for the Right‑Hand Trefoil
Given the standard diagram of the right‑hand trefoil as the (2,3) torus knot, determine τ(K) using the knot Floer homology of torus knots.
The knot Floer homology of the (p,q) torus knot with p,q > 0 is known to be supported in Alexander gradings 0,1,…, (p‑1)(q‑1)/2. For the (2,3) trefoil the maximal Alexander grading is 1. The U‑action lowers Alexander grading by 1 and Maslov grading by 2. The top‑most free ℤ[U] summand is generated in Alexander grading 1, so τ = 1. This matches the slice genus bound g₄ = 1 and the fact that the trefoil is not slice.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a concordance invariant, first identify whether the knot is alternating, quasi‑alternating, or a torus knot. For alternating knots τ equals the maximal Alexander degree, which can be read off from the Alexander polynomial. For torus knots use the known formula τ(Tp,q) = (p‑1)(q‑1)/2. If the knot is a connected sum, apply additivity. Always check the sign convention: τ(‑K) = -τ(K).

Symplectic Khovanov Homology and the Fukaya Category of the Cotangent Bundle

Symplectic Khovanov homology, introduced by Seidel and Smith, constructs a link invariant by interpreting the Khovanov chain complex as the Floer homology of a pair of Lagrangian submanifolds in the symplectic manifold X_n = Hilb^n(A), the Hilbert scheme of n points on the annulus A. The Lagrangians L0 and L1 correspond to the two resolutions of each crossing, and their intersection points are in bijection with the Kauffman states. The differential counts pseudo‑holomorphic strips with boundary on L0 ∪ L1, reproducing the Khovanov differential. This geometric model yields a natural action of the braid group on the derived Fukaya category D^πFuk(X_n), and the resulting categorical invariant is equivalent to the original Khovanov homology after passing to cohomology. The symplectic perspective explains the functoriality of Khovanov homology under link cobordisms: a cobordism induces a Lagrangian correspondence, and the associated functor on Fukaya categories recovers the map on homology. In examinations you may be asked to describe the correspondence between Kauffman states and intersection points, to explain why the symplectic construction automatically yields invariance under Reidemeister moves, or to outline how the braid group action on D^πFuk(X_n) categorifies the Burau representation. A frequent error is to assume that the symplectic model works for all links without the technical hypothesis that the link diagram is in bridge position relative to the projection X_n → Sym^n(A).

Four‑Dimensional Gauge Theory Invariants: The Kapustin–Witten Equations and Knot Homology

The Kapustin–Witten equations are a topological twist of N = 4 super‑Yang–Mills theory on a four‑manifold M with a compact gauge group G. When M = ℝ × Y with Y a three‑manifold containing a knot K, solutions to the equations with a prescribed singularity along ℝ × K give rise to a category of boundary conditions that is conjecturally equivalent to the category of B‑branes in the A‑model on the Hitchin moduli space of Y K. The resulting homology theory, often called knot homology from gauge theory, is expected to categorify the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev invariants and to recover Khovanov homology for G = SU(2) and the HOMFLY‑PT homology for higher rank groups. The equations depend on a parameter t ∈ ℂℙ¹; at t = 0, ∞ they reduce to the Haydys–Witten equations, while at t = 1 they become the Vafa–Witten equations. The parameter t corresponds to the quantum parameter q in the polynomial invariants. In exam problems you may be required to write the Kapustin–Witten equations in local coordinates, to explain the role of the Nahm pole boundary condition in creating the knot singularity, or to describe how the S‑duality of the gauge theory predicts a symmetry exchanging q q⁻¹ in the categorified invariant. A common pitfall is to confuse the Kapustin–Witten twist with the Donaldson–Witten twist; the former preserves two supercharges and yields a topological field theory with a complexified gauge group, while the latter preserves one supercharge and computes Donaldson invariants.

The Slice Genus, the Four‑Dimensional Clasp Number, and the Concordance Group

The slice genus g₄(K) of a knot K is the minimal genus of a smoothly embedded oriented surface in B⁴ bounded by K. The four‑dimensional clasp number c₄(K) is the minimal number of transverse double points (clasps) of an immersed disk in B⁴ with boundary K. Both invariants are concordance invariants and satisfy c₄(K) ≤ g₄(K). The knot concordance group 𝒞 is the abelian group of concordance classes of knots under connected sum, with inverse given by the mirror image. Levine’s algebraic concordance group 𝒞^alg is defined via Seifert forms and maps onto 𝒞 with kernel the group of algebraically slice knots. The Casson–Gordon invariants provide obstructions to a knot being slice that are not detected by the Alexander polynomial. Recent work of Piccirillo showed that the Conway knot is not slice despite being algebraically slice, using the Rasmussen s invariant and the τ invariant from knot Floer homology. In examinations you may be asked to prove that g₄(K) ≥ |τ(K)|, to compute the clasp number of the figure‑eight knot (which is 1), or to explain why the concordance group contains a ℤ^∞ summand generated by the family of torus knots T2,2k+1. A typical trap is to assume that c₄(K) = 0 implies K is slice; an immersed disk with no double points is an embedded disk, so c₄(K) = 0 is equivalent to sliceness, but the converse requires the disk to be smoothly embedded, not just topologically flat.

  • Confusing the annular grading with the quantum grading in annular Khovanov homology; they behave differently under disjoint union and Reidemeister moves.
  • Treating τ and s as interchangeable; they agree for quasi‑alternating knots but diverge for many non‑alternating examples.
  • Assuming the symplectic Khovanov construction works for arbitrary link diagrams without the bridge position hypothesis.
  • Mixing the Kapustin–Witten twist with the Donaldson–Witten twist; they preserve different numbers of supercharges and compute different invariants.
  • Believing that a vanishing clasp number automatically guarantees a smoothly embedded slice disk; the immersed disk must be regular homotopic to an embedding.
  • Forgetting that the concordance group map to the algebraic concordance group has a large kernel; algebraic sliceness does not imply smooth sliceness.
  • Remember to check framing conventions when computing invariants from gauge theory or symplectic geometry.

The Twisted Alexander Polynomial and Non‑Abelian Representations

The twisted Alexander polynomial extends the classical Alexander invariant by incorporating a representation of the knot group into a linear group, typically GL(n, F) for a field F. Given a knot K with group π₁(S³K) and a homomorphism ρ: π₁(S³K) → GL(n, F), one forms the chain complex of the infinite cyclic cover of the knot exterior with coefficients twisted by ρ. The resulting Reidemeister torsion, after normalising by the determinant of ρ on the peripheral subgroup, yields a rational function ΔK,ρ(t) in the variable t that specialises to the ordinary Alexander polynomial when ρ is the abelianisation map. Crucially, the twisted polynomial can detect non‑triviality of knots that have trivial Alexander polynomial, and it provides obstructions to a knot being fibered: if K is fibered then ΔK,ρ(t) is monic for every ρ that factors through the monodromy representation. The construction works equally well for links, where one uses a multivariable version with one variable per component. In practice, one often chooses ρ to be a representation into SL(2, ℂ) coming from a point on the SL(2, ℂ) character variety; the resulting polynomial is then a Laurent polynomial in t whose degree gives a lower bound for the Thurston norm of the knot complement. A standard computational technique is to present the knot group by a Wirtinger presentation, lift the relators to the group ring ℤ[π₁] [t±1], and compute the determinant of the resulting matrix after applying ρ. The twisted invariant is sensitive to the choice of ρ; different components of the character variety can give distinct polynomials, and the collection of all such polynomials is a powerful invariant of the knot. Recent work shows that the twisted Alexander polynomial determines the SL(2, ℂ) character variety for many two‑bridge knots, and it plays a central role in the proof of the Property P theorem for certain families.

The L‑Space Conjecture and Heegaard Floer Homology of Knot Surgeries

An L‑space is a rational homology three‑sphere whose Heegaard Floer homology ĤF(Y) has minimal possible rank, namely |H₁(Y; ℤ)|. The L‑space conjecture (due to Boyer, Gordon, and Watson) asserts that an irreducible rational homology sphere is an L‑space if and only if its fundamental group is not left‑orderable. For knot complements, this translates into a statement about Dehn surgeries: a knot K in S³ is called an L‑space knot if some positive surgery S³p/q(K) yields an L‑space. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that for an L‑space knot the knot Floer homology ĤFK(K) is completely determined by the Alexander polynomial; in particular it is “thin”, supported in a single diagonal δ = A − M where A is the Alexander grading and M the Maslov grading. The surgery formula for Heegaard Floer homology then gives an explicit description of ĤF(S³p/q(K)) in terms of the knot Floer complex. A key consequence is that the set of slopes producing L‑spaces is an interval [2g(K)−1, ∞) for positive surgeries, where g(K) is the Seifert genus. The conjecture also predicts that if a knot admits a positive L‑space surgery then the knot is fibered and strongly quasipositive. Counterexamples to the original formulation (e.g. certain graph manifolds) have led to refined versions involving the π₁‑orderability condition. In examinations you may be asked to compute the knot Floer homology of a torus knot T(p,q) and verify that it is an L‑space knot, to explain why the figure‑eight knot is not an L‑space knot (its knot Floer homology has thickness two), or to use the surgery formula to determine whether S³5(K) is an L‑space for a given knot. A frequent pitfall is to confuse the L‑space condition for the surgery manifold with the property that the knot itself has thin knot Floer homology; the latter is necessary but not sufficient for all slopes.

Worked Example: Twisted Alexander Polynomial of the Figure‑Eight Knot
Let K be the figure‑eight knot with group presentation ⟨ a,b | a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹ a = b a b⁻¹ a⁻¹ b ⟩. Consider the representation ρ: π₁ → SL(2, ℂ) sending a ↦ ( 1 1; 0 1 ) and b ↦ ( 1 0; -1 1 ). Compute the twisted Alexander polynomial ΔK,ρ(t) up to units in ℂ[t±1].
1. Write the Wirtinger presentation with meridians x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄ and relators r₁ = x₁ x₂ x₁⁻¹ x₃⁻¹, r₂ = x₂ x₃ x₂⁻¹ x₄⁻¹, r₃ = x₃ x₄ x₃⁻¹ x₁⁻¹, r₄ = x₄ x₁ x₄⁻¹ x₂⁻¹. 2. Abelianise by sending each x_i ↦ t. 3. Form the 4 × 4 Jacobian matrix Jij = ∂r_i/∂x_j in the group ring ℤ[π₁][t±1]. 4. Apply ρ to each group element, obtaining a matrix over M₂(ℂ)[t±1]. 5. Delete the last row and column (Fox calculus) and compute the determinant. 6. The result simplifies to ΔK,ρ(t) = (t² − 3t + 1)² up to multiplication by a unit ±t^k. This polynomial is non‑trivial, confirming that the figure‑eight knot is detected by this twisted invariant even though its ordinary Alexander polynomial is t² − 3t + 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a twisted Alexander polynomial, first write down a Wirtinger presentation and the chosen representation explicitly. Compute the Fox derivatives once, then substitute the representation matrices; this avoids re‑deriving the Jacobian for each representation. Remember to normalise by the determinant of the peripheral image so that the result is independent of the chosen meridian. For L‑space questions, recall the thinness criterion: if the knot Floer homology is not supported in a single δ‑grading, the knot cannot be an L‑space knot. Use the surgery formula only after confirming the knot is an L‑space knot; otherwise the formula does not simplify to the minimal rank condition.

The Knot Group, L²‑Invariants, and the Atiyah Conjecture

The L²‑Betti numbers of a knot complement M = S³K are defined using the von Neumann dimension of the homology of the universal cover with coefficients in the group von Neumann algebra 𝒩(π₁(M)). For a knot group, the first L²‑Betti number b₁(2)(M) vanishes because π₁(M) is infinite and has a non‑trivial centre (the peripheral subgroup). The second L²‑Betti number b₂(2)(M) equals the ordinary second Betti number, which is zero for a knot exterior. The interesting invariant is the L²‑torsion τ(2)(M), a positive real number that can be computed from the Fuglede–Kadison determinant of the Laplacian on the universal cover. For a knot complement, τ(2)(M) coincides with the exponential of the hyperbolic volume when M is hyperbolic, and more generally it is related to the Mahler measure of the Alexander polynomial. The Atiyah conjecture (proved for many classes of groups, including knot groups that are residually finite and satisfy the determinant condition) predicts that the L²‑Betti numbers of any finite CW‑complex with fundamental group G are rational numbers with denominators bounded by the least common multiple of the orders of finite subgroups of G. Since knot groups are torsion‑free, the conjecture implies that all L²‑Betti numbers are integers; indeed they are zero for knot complements. A deeper consequence is that the L²‑Alexander torsion, a one‑parameter family of invariants τ(2)_K(t) obtained by twisting the chain complex with the abelianisation π₁ → ℤ evaluated at t ∈ S¹, determines the Thurston norm and the fiberedness of the knot. Recent work of Friedl and Vidussi shows that the vanishing of τ(2)_K(t) for all t characterises the unknot among knots with trivial Alexander polynomial. In examinations you may be asked to state the definition of L²‑torsion, to explain why the Atiyah conjecture holds for knot groups (using residual finiteness and the determinant class property), or to compute the L²‑Alexander torsion of a torus knot T(p,q) and verify that it recovers the classical Alexander polynomial up to a factor of (tpq−1).

The Categorification of the Colored Jones Polynomial via Foam Homology

Foam homology, introduced by Khovanov and Rozansky and later refined by Mackaay, Stošić, and Vaz, provides a combinatorial categorification of the colored Jones polynomial J_N(K; q) for each integer N ≥ 2. The construction assigns to a link diagram a chain complex whose objects are formal direct sums of foams — decorated cobordisms between MOY graphs (trivalent graphs with edges labelled by integers 1,…,N−1) — and whose differential is a sum of local foam relations (the “zip” and “unzip” moves, the “digon removal”, and the “square switch”). The homology of this complex, denoted Hfoam_N(K), is a triply graded vector space (homological, quantum, and “color” grading) whose graded Euler characteristic recovers J_N(K; q). For N = 2 the theory reduces to the original Khovanov homology. The foam relations are derived from the representation theory of the quantum group U_q(sl_N) and the category of sl_N‑webs. A key technical result is the invariance of foam homology under the Reidemeister moves, proved by constructing explicit chain homotopy equivalences using the “Gaussian elimination” technique on the foam complex. The theory also admits a “deformed” version where one introduces a potential w = xN+1 and works with matrix factorisations; this yields the Khovanov–Rozansky homology which categorifies the HOMFLY‑PT polynomial. In practice, computing foam homology for a given knot involves reducing the diagram to a planar graph, applying the MOY calculus to resolve crossings into webs, and then evaluating the resulting foam complex using the local relations. The computational complexity grows rapidly with N, but for torus knots and two‑bridge knots there are closed formulas. Examiners may ask you to describe the MOY resolution of a positive crossing into a sum of two webs, to write down the foam differential for the digon removal relation, or to explain why the foam homology of the unknot is a one‑dimensional vector space concentrated in a single grading. A common mistake is to confuse the quantum grading in foam homology (which comes from the q‑degree of the colored Jones polynomial) with the homological grading (which comes from the chain complex); they shift differently under the Reidemeister moves.

The Knot Contact Homology and the Augmentation Category

Knot contact homology, developed by Ekholm, Etnyre, Ng, and Sullivan, is a Legendrian invariant constructed from the Chekanov–Eliashberg differential graded algebra (DGA) of a Legendrian knot in the standard contact ℝ³. The DGA is generated by Reeb chords of the Legendrian knot, with differential counting rigid holomorphic disks in the symplectisation ℝ × ℝ³ with boundary on the Lagrangian ℝ × Λ. An augmentation is a DGA map ε: (𝒜,∂) → (ℂ,0); the set of augmentations carries a natural structure of an A_∞‑category, the augmentation category Aug_+(Λ). Its objects are augmentations, and morphism spaces are given by the linearised contact homology LCH^ε(Λ). The augmentation category is invariant under Legendrian isotopy and provides a powerful refinement of the classical Legendrian invariants (Thurston–Bennequin number, rotation number, and the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA itself). For a given topological knot type, the augmentation category can distinguish Legendrian representatives that have identical classical invariants and even isomorphic DGAs. A major computational tool is the cord algebra (or “knot DGA”) which can be read off from a front projection using the Ng resolution. The augmentation category also admits a graded version when the Legendrian knot is equipped with a Maslov potential; the grading is by the Conley–Zehnder index of Reeb chords. Recent results show that the augmentation category of a Legendrian knot determines the knot contact homology of its Lagrangian filling, and that for torus knots the category is equivalent to a category of modules over a certain quiver algebra. In examinations you may be asked to write down the DGA of the standard Legendrian trefoil from its front diagram, to find all augmentations over ℂ, to compute the linearised homology for a chosen augmentation, or to explain why the augmentation category is a Legendrian isotopy invariant (the key point is that a Legendrian isotopy induces a DGA quasi‑isomorphism, which sends augmentations to augmentations and induces an A_∞‑functor on the augmentation categories). A frequent trap is to assume that the existence of an augmentation implies the Legendrian knot is fillable; while every fillable Legendrian admits an augmentation, the converse is false — there are augmentations that do not come from exact Lagrangian fillings.

  • Confusing the twisted Alexander polynomial with the ordinary Alexander polynomial; the former depends on a representation and can be non‑trivial even when the latter is trivial.
  • Assuming that every knot with thin knot Floer homology is an L‑space knot; thinness is necessary but not sufficient for all surgery slopes.
  • Neglecting the determinant class condition when invoking the Atiyah conjecture for knot groups; residual finiteness alone does not guarantee the conjecture.
  • Mixing the quantum grading and the homological grading in foam homology; they shift differently under Reidemeister moves and must be tracked separately.
  • Believing that any augmentation of the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA arises from an exact Lagrangian filling; augmentations exist for non‑fillable Legendrians as well.
  • Overlooking the normalisation by the peripheral determinant in twisted Alexander polynomial computations, leading to an invariant that changes under change of meridian.
  • Applying the L‑space surgery formula to knots that are not L‑space knots; the formula only simplifies to the minimal rank condition for genuine L‑space knots.

These advanced topics illustrate how modern knot theory intertwines algebraic, geometric, and categorical methods to produce invariants that are both computable and conceptually deep.

The Solvable Filtration of the Knot Concordance Group

The knot concordance group 𝒞 consists of oriented knot types modulo smooth concordance in the four‑ball. Cochran, Orr and Teichner introduced a filtration 𝒞 = 𝒞₀ ⊇ 𝒞₁ ⊇ 𝒞₂ ⊇ … where a knot lies in 𝒞ₙ if it bounds a smooth, properly embedded disk in a homology four‑ball whose fundamental group has derived length at most n. The zeroth term 𝒞₀ is the whole group; 𝒞₁ is the subgroup of knots with vanishing Arf invariant and Alexander polynomial of the form Δ(t) = f(t)f(t⁻¹). Higher terms are detected by higher‑order Alexander invariants associated to metabelian and solvable quotients of the knot group. For each n there exist knots that are n‑solvable but not (n+1)‑solvable, proving the filtration is strict. The construction uses iterated satellite operations with pattern knots having prescribed Alexander modules. The associated graded quotients 𝒞ₙ/𝒞ₙ₊₁ contain large free abelian summands detected by von Neumann ρ‑invariants of the corresponding metabelian covers. This filtration refines the classical algebraic concordance group of Levine and provides a bridge to the homology cobordism group of three‑manifolds via the zero‑surgery map. Examiners often ask for the definition of n‑solvability, the role of the derived series of the knot group, or why the Arf invariant obstructs membership in 𝒞₁.

Higher Order Alexander Invariants and the Cochran–Orr–Teichner Theory

Given a knot K with group G = π₁(S³ K), let G⁽ⁿ⁾ denote the n‑th term of the derived series. The quotient Γₙ = G / G⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾ is a poly‑torsion‑free‑abelian (PTFA) group for n ≥ 1. The n‑th order Alexander module is the homology H₁(Mₙ; ℤΓₙ) of the infinite cyclic cover Mₙ of the knot complement corresponding to the abelianisation map G → ℤ. These modules are finitely presented over the Noetherian ring ℤΓₙ and their Blanchfield‑type linking forms yield higher‑order signatures σₙ(K, ω) for characters ω : Γₙ → S¹. Cochran, Orr and Teichner proved that if K is n‑solvable then all k‑th order signatures vanish for k ≤ n and all k‑th order Alexander modules are metabolic. Conversely, non‑vanishing of a higher‑order signature provides an obstruction to n‑solvability. The theory also produces invariants of the homology cobordism group via the zero‑surgery manifold M₀(K). In practice one computes the first‑order Alexander module from a presentation of the knot group using Fox calculus; the second‑order module requires a presentation of the metabelian quotient, which can be obtained from the Alexander module of the infinite cyclic cover. Examiners may ask to write the Fox derivative matrix for the trefoil, to explain why the ring ℤΓ₁ is a (non‑commutative) Ore domain, or to describe how a metaboliser for the Blanchfield form gives a Lagrangian in the higher‑order linking form.

Worked Example: First‑Order Alexander Module of the Right‑Hand Trefoil
Compute the first‑order Alexander module of the right‑hand trefoil knot and determine whether it is metabolic.
The trefoil group has presentation G = ⟨ x, y | x² = y³ ⟩. Abelianisation sends both generators to t. The Alexander matrix from Fox calculus is [ 1 + t + t² , - (1 + t) ]. The first‑order Alexander module is the cokernel of this 1×2 matrix over ℤ[t, t⁻¹], which is isomorphic to ℤ[t, t⁻¹] / (t² - t + 1). The Blanchfield linking form on this cyclic module is non‑degenerate and the module is not metabolic because the Alexander polynomial Δ(t) = t² - t + 1 is not a norm from the quadratic extension ℚ(√-3). Hence the trefoil is not 1‑solvable, confirming its non‑triviality in 𝒞₁.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a higher‑order obstruction, first identify the derived quotient needed (metabelian for first order, etc.). Write down the relevant PTFA group and the associated Ore localisation. Then state the vanishing theorem: if the knot is n‑solvable, all k ≤ n signatures vanish and the k‑th order Alexander modules are metabolic. Use this as a checklist rather than recomputing the full module each time.

Non‑Abelian Reidemeister Torsion and the Turaev Invariant

Classical Reidemeister torsion is defined for a finite CW‑complex with a representation of its fundamental group into GL(n, 𝔽). For a knot complement X = S³ K, Turaev refined the torsion to a well‑defined invariant τ(X, ρ) taking values in the group of units of the representation ring modulo the subgroup generated by the image of the peripheral subgroup. The invariant depends on a choice of Euler structure, which for a knot complement is canonically determined by the meridian. When ρ : π₁(X) → SL(2, ℂ) is an irreducible representation, the torsion distinguishes knots that have identical Alexander polynomials and even identical character varieties. The computation proceeds by choosing a presentation of the knot group, forming the chain complex of the universal cover with coefficients twisted by ρ, and evaluating the determinant of the boundary maps after a suitable basis change. The result is a rational function in the eigenvalues of the meridian and longitude images. For the figure‑eight knot, the torsion for the discrete faithful representation recovers the hyperbolic volume via the complexified Chern‑Simons invariant. Examiners may ask to explain why the torsion is independent of the cell decomposition up to multiplication by units, to write the formula for the torsion of a two‑bridge knot in terms of the Riley polynomial, or to describe how the Turaev invariant behaves under connected sum (it multiplies). A common mistake is to confuse the sign ambiguity of the classical torsion with the refined sign‑determined Turaev torsion; the latter requires a homology orientation and an Euler structure.

The Knot Group and the SU(2) Representation Variety

The set of conjugacy classes of representations π₁(S³ K) → SU(2) carries a natural real algebraic structure. Its dimension at a representation ρ is given by the first cohomology group H¹(π₁; 𝔰𝔲(2)_Ad ρ). For a non‑trivial knot, the trivial representation is always present and is isolated if and only if the knot is not a torus knot. The variety often contains curves of irreducible representations; the number of such curves and their intersection pattern with the reducible locus are knot invariants. The Casson invariant of the zero‑surgery manifold M₀(K) equals half the signed count of irreducible SU(2) representations of the knot group. For alternating knots, the SU(2) representation variety is completely described by the A‑polynomial restricted to the unit circle. The work of Kronheimer and Mrowka shows that the existence of a non‑abelian SU(2) representation for every non‑trivial knot follows from the non‑vanishing of instanton Floer homology. In practice, one computes the character variety by eliminating the trace of the meridian from the Riley equations; the resulting plane curve is the SU(2) character variety. Examiners may ask to derive the Riley polynomial for the figure‑eight knot, to explain why the trivial representation contributes zero to the Casson invariant, or to state the relationship between the SU(2) representation variety and the A‑polynomial.

The Knot Group and the L²‑Betti Numbers of Knot Complements

For a knot complement X = S³ K with fundamental group G, the L²‑Betti numbers bₖ⁽²⁾(X) are defined using the von Neumann dimension of the L²‑homology of the universal cover. Lück’s approximation theorem relates them to the ordinary Betti numbers of finite covers. For any knot, b₀⁽²⁾ = b₃⁽²⁾ = 0 and b₁⁽²⁾ = b₂⁽²⁾ = 0 because the group is infinite and has deficiency one. The vanishing of all L²‑Betti numbers is a consequence of the fact that knot groups are of type FP and have a finite classifying space of dimension two. However, the L²‑torsion τ⁽²⁾(X) is a non‑trivial invariant; it equals the Mahler measure of the Alexander polynomial when the latter is non‑zero. For fibered knots, the L²‑torsion coincides with the exponential of the entropy of the monodromy. The Atiyah conjecture predicts that τ⁽²⁾(X) is an integer multiple of the logarithm of an algebraic integer; this is known for many classes of knots, including alternating and torus knots. Examiners may ask to state Lück’s approximation theorem, to compute the L²‑torsion of the trefoil from its Alexander polynomial Δ(t) = t² - t + 1, or to explain why the vanishing of L²‑Betti numbers does not imply the knot group is amenable.

  • Assuming that a vanishing first‑order Alexander module implies the knot is slice; higher‑order obstructions can still be non‑trivial.
  • Neglecting the Euler structure when computing Turaev torsion; the sign‑refined invariant is not well defined without it.
  • Treating the SU(2) representation variety as a finite set for all knots; many knots have positive‑dimensional components.
  • Believing that L²‑Betti numbers detect knot genus; they vanish for all knots, so they give no genus information.
  • Mixing the Mahler measure of the Alexander polynomial with the ordinary Mahler measure; the L²‑torsion uses the logarithmic Mahler measure on the unit circle.
  • Overlooking the requirement that the representation be irreducible when applying the Casson invariant formula; reducible representations contribute zero after perturbation.

The Arf Invariant and the Quadratic Refinement of the Intersection Form

The Arf invariant is a classical knot invariant taking values in ℤ/2ℤ that detects whether a knot is a slice knot in the sense of bounding a smoothly embedded disc in the four‑ball. It arises from the quadratic refinement of the intersection form on the first homology of a Seifert surface. Given a Seifert matrix V for a knot K, the symmetrized matrix A = V + Vᵀ defines a bilinear form on H₁(Σ; ℤ/2ℤ). A quadratic refinement q satisfies q(x+y) = q(x) + q(y) + x·y (mod 2). The Arf invariant is the Arf invariant of this quadratic form, computed as the sum over a symplectic basis of q(eᵢ)q(fᵢ) modulo 2. For the trefoil knot the Arf invariant is 1, while for the figure‑eight knot it is 0. The invariant is additive under connected sum and vanishes for any knot that bounds a smoothly embedded disc in B⁴. Consequently, a knot with Arf invariant 1 cannot be slice. The Arf invariant also appears in the classification of knot cobordism: the map from the knot concordance group to ℤ/2ℤ sending a knot to its Arf invariant is a homomorphism whose kernel contains the subgroup of algebraically slice knots. In the context of the Levine–Tristram signatures, the Arf invariant equals the signature at the root of unity eπi modulo 2. Examiners often ask to compute the Arf invariant from a given Seifert matrix, to prove that the Arf invariant of the connected sum is the sum modulo 2, or to explain why the Arf invariant of a knot with Alexander polynomial Δ(t) = 1 must be zero.

The Sato–Levine Invariant and the Classification of 2‑Component Links up to Link Homotopy

The Sato–Levine invariant is an integer‑valued invariant of oriented 2‑component links that classifies them up to link homotopy, i.e. ambient isotopy allowing each component to pass through itself but not through the other component. For a link L = K₁ ∪ K₂ with linking number zero, the invariant is defined using the Milnor μ‑invariants of length three. Choose a based fundamental group presentation of the link complement; the invariant β(L) equals the coefficient of the commutator [x₁, x₂] in the Magnus expansion of the longitude of one component, taken modulo the linking number. Equivalently, it can be computed from a Seifert surface for one component intersected with the other component, yielding a quadratic form whose Arf invariant gives β(L) mod 2. The Sato–Levine invariant is additive under connected sum of links and changes sign under orientation reversal of one component. Two 2‑component links with linking number zero are link homotopic if and only if they have the same Sato–Levine invariant. For the Whitehead link the invariant is ±1, while for the Hopf link (linking number ±1) the invariant is not defined because the linking number is non‑zero. The invariant also appears in the study of the second homotopy group of the link complement and in the classification of link maps up to homotopy. Examiners may ask to compute β for a given link diagram using the band‑sum formula, to show that the invariant vanishes for a split link, or to relate the Sato–Levine invariant to the Milnor μ‑invariant μ₁₂₃ of the associated 3‑component link obtained by adding a trivial component.

Worked Example: Computing the Arf Invariant from a Seifert Matrix
Given the Seifert matrix V = [[1, 1], [0, 1]] for the right‑hand trefoil, compute the Arf invariant.
First form the symmetrized matrix A = V + Vᵀ = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Reduce modulo 2 to obtain A₂ = [[0, 1], [1, 0]] over ℤ/2ℤ. Choose the standard symplectic basis e₁ = (1,0), f₁ = (0,1). The quadratic refinement q is defined by q(v) = vᵀ V v (mod 2). Compute q(e₁) = (1,0) V (1,0)ᵀ = 1 (mod 2). Compute q(f₁) = (0,1) V (0,1)ᵀ = 1 (mod 2). The Arf invariant is q(e₁) q(f₁) = 1·1 = 1 (mod 2). Hence the Arf invariant of the trefoil is 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Arf invariant, first write down the Seifert matrix, then form V+Vᵀ, reduce mod 2, and pick any symplectic basis. The quadratic form q(v)=vᵀVv (mod 2) is quick to evaluate. For the Sato–Levine invariant, remember it is only defined for linking number zero; if the linking number is non‑zero, state that the invariant is not applicable. Practice the band‑sum formula on the Whitehead link and the Borromean rings to build speed.

The Non‑Commutative Alexander Polynomial and the Free Differential Calculus

The non‑commutative Alexander polynomial, also known as the Fox–Milnor invariant, lives in the group ring ℤ[π₁(S³K)] and refines the classical Alexander polynomial by retaining the full non‑abelian information of the knot group. Using Fox free differential calculus, one associates to a presentation of the knot group a Jacobian matrix of derivatives of the relators with respect to the generators. The ideal generated by the (n‑1)×(n‑1) minors of this matrix, after abelianizing the group ring to ℤ[t±1], yields the classical Alexander polynomial. The non‑commutative version keeps the matrix over the non‑abelian group ring, providing a complete invariant for knots up to mutation in many cases. For a knot with presentation ⟨x₁,…,xₙ | r₁,…,rₙ₋₁⟩, the Fox derivatives ∂rⱼ/∂xᵢ lie in ℤ[Fₙ] where Fₙ is the free group on the generators. The Alexander matrix is the (n‑1)×n matrix of these derivatives evaluated under the canonical map Fₙ → π₁. The invariant is the elementary ideal of this matrix; its order (the greatest common divisor of the minors) is the non‑commutative Alexander polynomial. It distinguishes the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway mutants, which share the same classical Alexander polynomial. The invariant also behaves well under satellite operations: the non‑commutative Alexander polynomial of a satellite knot can be expressed in terms of those of the pattern and companion. Examiners may ask to write the Fox derivatives for the standard Wirtinger presentation of the trefoil, to compute the first elementary ideal, or to explain why the non‑commutative Alexander polynomial is invariant under Reidemeister moves.

Twisted Reidemeister Torsion for Metabelian Representations

Twisted Reidemeister torsion extends the classical Reidemeister torsion by twisting the chain complex of the universal cover with a representation of the knot group into a linear group, typically GL(n, ℂ) or a metabelian quotient. For a knot complement X, let ρ: π₁(X) → G be a representation where G is a metabelian group (an extension of an abelian group by an abelian group). The twisted chain complex C_*(X̃) ⊗ℤ[π₁] ℂ[G] yields a torsion invariant τ_ρ(X) ∈ ℂ[G]/±G. When G is the metabelian quotient π₁/π₁'', the torsion recovers the higher‑order Alexander invariants of Cochran–Orr–Teichner. The torsion is computed by choosing a cellular decomposition of X, lifting to the universal cover, and forming the boundary matrices with coefficients in ℂ[G]. After a suitable basis change, the determinant of the boundary map gives a rational function in the group ring. For the figure‑eight knot, the metabelian torsion detects the non‑triviality of the second derived quotient and distinguishes it from the trefoil. The invariant is multiplicative under connected sum and changes by a unit under change of cellular structure. A key computational tool is the Fox calculus presentation of the metabelian group ring, which reduces the torsion to a determinant of a matrix with entries in a Laurent polynomial ring in several variables. Examiners may ask to define the metabelian quotient, to write the twisted chain complex for a given 2‑complex, or to explain why the torsion vanishes for a knot with trivial Alexander polynomial but non‑trivial metabelian torsion.

Heegaard Floer Homology of the Knot Complement and the Knot Floer Complex

Heegaard Floer homology, introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó, assigns to a closed 3‑manifold a suite of invariants; for a knot complement one considers the relative invariant HFK⁻(S³, K) which is a module over the polynomial ring ℤ[U]. The knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K) is a free, finitely generated chain complex over ℤ[U] whose homology is HFK⁻(K). Its filtered chain homotopy type is a complete invariant for many knots, including all alternating knots. The complex carries a bifiltration (Alexander and Maslov) and the associated spectral sequence converges to the Heegaard Floer homology of the ambient manifold. The Euler characteristic of HFK⁻(K) recovers the symmetrized Alexander polynomial. The τ‑invariant, defined as the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁻(K), gives a lower bound for the slice genus and detects fiberedness: a knot is fibered iff HFK⁻(K) has rank one in its top Alexander grading. The knot Floer complex also admits a combinatorial description via grid diagrams, making it computable for knots up to 12 crossings. The invariant behaves under connected sum by a tensor product formula, and under concordance by a mapping cone formula. Examiners may ask to define the τ‑invariant, to compute HFK⁻ for the trefoil using a grid diagram, or to explain why the knot Floer homology of a knot complement determines the Thurston norm of the knot complement.

  • Assuming the Sato–Levine invariant is defined for links with non‑zero linking number; it is only defined when the linking number vanishes.
  • Treating the non‑commutative Alexander polynomial as an element of ℤ[t±1] instead of the group ring ℤ[π₁]; the abelianization loses the mutation‑detecting power.
  • Neglecting the metabelian quotient when computing twisted Reidemeister torsion; the torsion for the abelianization reduces to the classical Alexander polynomial.
  • Believing that Heegaard Floer homology of the knot complement is the same as the knot Floer homology of the knot in S³; the former is a 3‑manifold invariant, the latter is a relative invariant with a bifiltration.

  • Mixing the combinatorial grid‑diagram algorithm with the holomorphic definition; the grid algorithm computes the same invariant but the grading shifts must be tracked carefully.

The Cosmetic Surgery Conjecture and its Implications for Knot Complements

The cosmetic surgery conjecture asserts that if a knot K in the three‑sphere admits two distinct Dehn surgeries yielding homeomorphic oriented three‑manifolds, then the surgeries must be the same slope. Equivalently, no knot in S³ admits a pair of non‑trivial cosmetic surgeries. The conjecture was formulated by Gordon in the early 1990s and remains open in full generality, although it has been proved for many large classes of knots. For torus knots the conjecture follows from the classification of lens space surgeries due to Moser and the cyclic surgery theorem of Culler–Gordon–Luecke–Shalen. For hyperbolic knots, the work of Ni–Wu (2015) shows that cosmetic surgeries cannot occur when the knot complement is an L‑space, and the result extends to all knots with non‑zero Alexander polynomial using Heegaard Floer homology. The conjecture has deep connections with the Property R theorem (Gabai, 1987) and the Berge conjecture on lens space surgeries. A counterexample would produce a knot whose complement admits two distinct fillings giving the same manifold, forcing the knot group to have two distinct peripheral structures with identical filled groups — a situation that would contradict known rigidity results for Kleinian groups. Examiners often ask to state the conjecture precisely, to explain why the cyclic surgery theorem implies it for torus knots, or to outline the Heegaard Floer proof for L‑space knots.

Involutive Heegaard Floer Homology and the Knot Invariants

Involutive Heegaard Floer homology, introduced by Hendricks–Manolescu (2017), refines the ordinary Heegaard Floer groups by incorporating the conjugation involution on the Heegaard diagram. For a knot K in S³ one obtains a bifiltered chain complex CFK⁻(K) equipped with an involutive chain map ι that squares to the identity up to chain homotopy. The resulting involutive knot Floer homology HFI⁻(K) carries two gradings (Alexander and Maslov) and a ℤ/2‑action. The involutive correction terms d and d̄ give lower bounds for the four‑dimensional slice genus that are sometimes sharper than the Ozsváth–Szabó τ‑invariant. In particular, for the knot 10₁₃₉ the involutive invariant detects non‑slice status while τ vanishes. The construction also yields an involutive version of the concordance invariant Υₖ(t) which is a piecewise‑linear function on [0,2] with slopes in ℤ. The involutive package is functorial under knot cobordisms, leading to a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group to a group of involutive complexes. Examiners may request the definition of the involutive chain map, the computation of HFI⁻ for the right‑hand trefoil, or an explanation of why the involutive correction terms are concordance invariants.

Worked Example: Computing the Involutive τ‑Invariant for the Trefoil
Let K be the right‑hand trefoil. Using the standard genus‑one Heegaard diagram, write down the filtered chain complex CFK⁻(K) over ℤ[U]. Identify the involutive chain map ι on generators, then determine the involutive homology HFI⁻(K). From the U‑torsion submodule extract the involutive correction term d(K) and compare it with the ordinary τ‑invariant.
The trefoil complex has two generators x, y with Alexander gradings 0 and −1, Maslov gradings 0 and −1, and differential ∂x=Uy, ∂y=0. The involution swaps x and y up to U‑multiplication, giving ι(x)=Uy, ι(y)=x. The mapping cone construction yields HFI⁻ generated by a single class in Alexander grading 0 with Maslov grading 0, and a U‑tower in negative gradings. The minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class is 0, so d(K)=0. The ordinary τ‑invariant for the right‑hand trefoil is 1, showing the involutive correction can be strictly smaller.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for an involutive invariant, first write the ordinary knot Floer complex from a grid diagram, then describe the involution on generators. The mapping‑cone formula is the standard way to compute HFI⁻; memorise the grading shifts (Maslov −1, Alexander unchanged) for the cone differential. Practice the trefoil and figure‑eight examples until the steps are automatic.

Bordered Heegaard Floer Homology and the Knot Floer Complex

Bordered Heegaard Floer homology, developed by Lipshitz–Ozsváth–Thurston (2008), assigns to a three‑manifold with parametrised boundary a module over the bordered algebra 𝒜(Z) associated to a pointed matched circle Z. For a knot complement X=S³\ν(K) the torus boundary yields a type‑D structure ĈFᴰ(X) over 𝒜(T²). Gluing a solid torus with slope p/q corresponds to tensoring with the type‑A module ĈFᴬ(p/q) and taking homology, reproducing the Heegaard Floer groups of the surgered manifold. The knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K) can be recovered as the box‑tensor product of ĈFᴰ(X) with the standard type‑A module for the meridian. This perspective makes the surgery exact triangle a formal consequence of the bordered pairing theorem. Moreover, the bordered invariant is computable via the combinatorial algorithm of bordered sutured Floer homology, allowing explicit calculations for knots up to 14 crossings. Examiners may ask to define the bordered algebra for the torus, to describe the type‑D structure of the trefoil complement, or to explain how the knot Floer complex arises from the bordered pairing.

The Satellite Formula in Knot Floer Homology and Applications

The satellite formula, proved by Hedden–Levine (2016) using bordered Floer theory, expresses the knot Floer complex of a satellite knot P(K) in terms of the pattern P in a solid torus and the companion knot K. Let P be a knot in the solid torus V=S¹×D² with winding number w. The formula states that CFK⁻(P(K)) is quasi‑isomorphic to the mapping cone of a chain map between two complexes built from the bordered invariant of P and the knot Floer complex of K. When w=0 the satellite is a connected sum of K with a knot in S³, and the formula reduces to the tensor product rule. For non‑zero winding number the construction involves the 𝒜‑module structure on the pattern’s bordered invariant and the U‑action on CFK⁻(K). Applications include a proof that the τ‑invariant is additive under connected sum, a computation of the ε‑invariant for cables, and a proof that the knot Floer homology of a satellite detects the winding number. Examiners may request the statement of the satellite formula, a sketch of the mapping‑cone construction for the (2,1)‑cable, or an explanation of why the formula implies the concordance invariance of the ε‑invariant.

The Epsilon Invariant and its Relation to the Concordance Group

The ε‑invariant, introduced by Hom (2014), is a concordance invariant taking values in {−1,0,1} derived from the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K). It is defined by examining the filtered chain homotopy type of the complex after setting U=0 and looking at the homology in the top Alexander grading. If the homology is supported in a single Maslov grading, ε=0; otherwise the sign of the grading shift determines ε=±1. The invariant is a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group 𝒞 to ℤ/2, and it satisfies ε(K#J)=ε(K)+ε(J) modulo 2. It provides a lower bound for the four‑dimensional clasp number and detects non‑slice knots that have vanishing τ and Υ invariants, such as the knot 12ₙ₈₈₀. The ε‑invariant also interacts with the involutive correction terms: for any knot, ε(K)≡d(K) (mod 2). Examiners may ask to compute ε for the figure‑eight knot (which is 0), to prove that ε is a concordance homomorphism, or to explain how ε distinguishes the Kinoshita–Terasaka and Conway mutants.

  • Treating the bordered algebra for the torus as the same as the algebra for a higher‑genus surface; the torus algebra is generated by two idempotents and four Reeb chords.
  • Applying the satellite formula without checking the winding number; the formula simplifies dramatically for winding number zero.
  • Believing ε takes values in ℤ rather than {−1,0,1}; the invariant is defined modulo 2 but the sign carries extra information.
