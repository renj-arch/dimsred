Tensor Calculus & Differential Forms — Tensors, Exterior Algebra & Cartan Calculus

Deep dive into Tensor Calculus & Differential Forms — the tensor product of vector spaces (the universal property: T: V×W → U bilinear factors through V⊗W), symmetric and exterior algebras (S(V) = T(V)/⟨v⊗w−w⊗v⟩, Λ(V) = T(V)/⟨v⊗v⟩), tensor fields on manifolds (sections of tensor bundles T^r_s M), differential forms (alternating covariant tensors, the de Rham complex Ω^*(M) with exterior derivative d: Ω^k → Ω^{k+1} satisfying d² = 0, the de Rham cohomology H^*_{dR}(M)), the Cartan calculus (the Lie derivative £_X, interior product ι_X, and the Cartan formula £_X = dι_X + ι_X d), integration of forms over chains (Stokes' theorem ∫_∂c ω = ∫_c dω), the Riemann curvature tensor R(X,Y)Z = ∇_X∇_Y Z − ∇_Y∇_X Z − ∇_{[X,Y]}Z and the Bianchi identities, Hodge theory (the Hodge star ⋆, the codifferential δ = ⋆ d ⋆, the Hodge decomposition theorem), and applications to gauge theory (the Yang–Mills curvature F = dA + A∧A).

Part 1

🧮 Tensors and Multilinear Algebra

Linear Algebra
Tensor Products, Universal Properties, and Tensor Fields on Manifolds
The tensor product V ⊗ W of vector spaces satisfies the universal property: for every bilinear map φ: V×W → U, there exists a unique linear map φ̃: V⊗W → U such that φ(v,w) = φ̃(v⊗w). The tensor product of a (r,s)-tensor and a (p,q)-tensor is a (r+p, s+q)-tensor. The contraction C: T^{r}_{s} → T^{r-1}_{s-1} pairs a covariant index with a contravariant index. A tensor field of type (r,s) on a manifold M is a smooth section of the bundle T^r_s M = (TM)^{⊗r} ⊗ (T^*M)^{⊗s}. In coordinates (x^i) with basis ∂_i for TM and dx^i for T^*M, a tensor writes as T = T^{i_1…i_r}_{j_1…j_s} ∂_{i_1}⊗…⊗∂_{i_r}⊗dx^{j_1}⊗…⊗dx^{j_s}. The transformation law under coordinate change x → y: T'^{i}_{j} = (∂y^i/∂x^k)(∂x^ℓ/∂y^j) T^k_ℓ — contravariant indices transform with the Jacobian, covariant with its inverse. The metric tensor g = g_{ij} dx^i ⊗ dx^j is a symmetric (0,2)-tensor field that defines the Riemannian structure. The Kronecker delta δ^i_j and the Levi-Civita symbol ε_{i_1…i_n} are the canonical invariant tensors.
Tensors are the multilinear generalization of vectors and covectors — their coordinate transformation laws encode the geometry of the manifold.
Part 2

📐 Differential Forms and the de Rham Complex

Exterior Algebra
Differential Forms, the Exterior Derivative, and de Rham Cohomology
A differential k-form ω is a smooth section of Λ^k T^*M — a totally antisymmetric covariant k-tensor field. In coordinates: ω = (1/k!) ω_{i_1…i_k} dx^{i_1} ∧ … ∧ dx^{i_k}. The wedge product (ω∧η)(X_1,…,X_{p+q}) = (1/p!q!) Σ_σ sgn(σ) ω(X_{σ(1)},…,X_{σ(p)}) η(X_{σ(p+1)},…,X_{σ(p+q)}) makes Ω^*(M) = ⊕ Ω^k(M) into a graded-commutative algebra: ω∧η = (−1)^{|ω||η|} η∧ω. The exterior derivative d: Ω^k → Ω^{k+1} is the unique antiderivation satisfying d(f) = df (the differential of f), d² = 0, and d(ω∧η) = dω∧η + (−1)^{|ω|} ω∧dη. In coordinates: d(ω_{i_1…i_k} dx^{i_1}∧…∧dx^{i_k}) = (∂ω_{i_1…i_k}/∂x^j) dx^j∧dx^{i_1}∧…∧dx^{i_k}. The de Rham cohomology H^k_{dR}(M) = ker d_k / im d_{k-1} measures closed forms modulo exact forms. For compact oriented M, Poincaré duality: H^k_{dR}(M) ≅ (H^{n-k}_{dR}(M))^*. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence computes de Rham cohomology from a cover. The de Rham theorem states H^k_{dR}(M) ≅ H^k(M; ℝ) — de Rham cohomology equals singular cohomology with real coefficients.
Differential forms with the exterior derivative form the de Rham complex, whose cohomology encodes the global topology of the manifold.
Part 3

⚙️ Cartan Calculus: Lie Derivatives and Interior Products

Calculus
The Lie Derivative, the Interior Product, Cartan's Magic Formula, and Stokes' Theorem
The Lie derivative £_X: Ω^k → Ω^k measures the change of a form along the flow φ_t of a vector field X: £_X ω = d/dt|_{t=0} φ_t^* ω. The interior product ι_X: Ω^k → Ω^{k-1} contracts a form with X: (ι_X ω)(X_1,…,X_{k-1}) = ω(X, X_1,…,X_{k-1}). Cartan's magic formula: £_X = d ι_X + ι_X d — the Lie derivative is the graded commutator of d and ι_X. This implies £_X is a derivation commuting with d. The Poincaré lemma: every closed form is locally exact — if dω = 0, then locally ω = dη. For a chain c (a formal sum of smooth simplices) and a k-form ω, integration ∫_c ω is defined by pulling back to the simplex. Stokes' theorem: ∫_{∂c} ω = ∫_c dω — the cornerstone connecting differential calculus to topology. The divergence theorem, Green's theorem, and the classical Stokes' theorem are special cases. The integration of forms over oriented manifolds uses a partition of unity subordinate to an oriented atlas. The change of variables formula ∫_M f^* ω = deg(f) ∫_N ω for a smooth map f: M → N of compact oriented manifolds, where deg(f) is the Brouwer degree.
Cartan's magic formula unifies the Lie derivative, exterior derivative, and interior product — Stokes' theorem is the fundamental theorem of calculus on manifolds.
Part 4

🔥 Hodge Theory, Curvature, and Gauge Theory

Riemannian
The Hodge Star, the Codifferential, the Ricci and Einstein Tensors, and Yang–Mills
On an oriented Riemannian manifold (M^n, g), define the Hodge star ⋆: Ω^k → Ω^{n-k} by ⋆(dx^{i_1}∧…∧dx^{i_k}) = √det g/(n−k)! ε^{i_1…i_k}_{i_{k+1}…i_n} dx^{i_{k+1}}∧…∧dx^{i_n}. It satisfies ⋆⋆ = (−1)^{k(n−k)} id. The codifferential δ = (−1)^{n(k+1)+1} ⋆ d ⋆: Ω^k → Ω^{k-1} is the formal adjoint of d with respect to the L² inner product ⟨ω,η⟩ = ∫ ω∧⋆η. The Hodge Laplacian Δ = dδ + δd. The Hodge decomposition theorem: every k-form ω decomposes uniquely as ω = dα + δβ + γ where γ is harmonic (Δγ = 0). Thus H^k_{dR}(M) ≅ ℋ^k(M) — harmonic forms represent de Rham cohomology classes. The Riemann curvature tensor R(X,Y)Z = ∇_X∇_Y Z − ∇_Y∇_X Z − ∇_{[X,Y]}Z. In coordinates: R^ℓ_{ijk} = ∂_i Γ^ℓ_{jk} − ∂_j Γ^ℓ_{ik} + Γ^m_{jk} Γ^ℓ_{im} − Γ^m_{ik} Γ^ℓ_{jm}. The Bianchi identities: R_{ijkl} + R_{iklj} + R_{iljk} = 0 (first) and ∇_i R_{jklm} + ∇_j R_{kilm} + ∇_k R_{ijlm} = 0 (second). The Ricci tensor R_{ij} = R^k_{ikj} and the scalar curvature R = g^{ij} R_{ij}. In gauge theory, the curvature of a connection A on a principal bundle is F = dA + A∧A (with the Lie algebra-valued exterior derivative). The Yang–Mills equations d_A ⋆ F = 0 are the Euler–Lagrange equations of the Yang–Mills action S_YM = ∫ Tr(F∧⋆F). The Maxwell equations in differential form: dF = 0, d⋆F = J.
Hodge theory establishes a deep link between analysis (harmonic forms) and topology (de Rham cohomology) — the curvature tensor is the central object in Riemannian geometry and gauge theory.

Topological Obstructions, Fiber Bundles, and Global Analysis

While the de Rham complex provides a powerful local-to-global framework, it often obscures the underlying geometric topology of the manifold unless one explicitly considers fiber bundles. A critical advancement beyond standard calculus is understanding how differential forms live on principal bundles and associated vector bundles. The transition from flat Euclidean space to curved manifolds introduces curvature as an obstruction to global trivialization. For instance, the existence of a non-vanishing vector field on a sphere S2 is impossible due to topological constraints (the Poincaré-Hopf theorem), which can be analyzed via the Euler class in cohomology. This section explores how the exterior derivative interacts with connections on bundles, where the curvature form F represents the failure of parallel transport to be path-independent.

In advanced applications, one must distinguish between intrinsic geometry (defined by the metric) and extrinsic topology (defined by homotopy groups). The Hodge decomposition theorem relies heavily on the compactness of the manifold; on non-compact manifolds, the space of L2 harmonic forms may be trivial even if de Rham cohomology is not. This distinction is vital for physics applications like quantum field theory on curved backgrounds. Furthermore, the concept of characteristic classes (Chern, Pontryagin) allows one to integrate differential forms over cycles to obtain topological invariants that are independent of the metric choice. These invariants classify bundles up to isomorphism and are essential for understanding gauge anomalies.

The interplay between local coordinates and global topology often reveals "twisted" structures where standard tensor calculus breaks down without modification. For example, on a manifold with boundary or corners (like a domain in fluid dynamics), the integration by parts formula requires careful handling of boundary terms that correspond to physical fluxes. The exterior derivative d is defined intrinsically via the antisymmetrization of partial derivatives, but its adjoint δ depends entirely on the metric and orientation. When analyzing gauge fields, the potential A is a connection 1-form, and the field strength F=dA+Awedge A is a curvature 2-form. The equation d_A star F = 0 (Yang-Mills) is non-linear due to the Awedge A term, contrasting sharply with the linear Maxwell equations in flat space where F=dA. This non-linearity leads to soliton solutions and instantons, which are topologically stable configurations.

Technique: Computing Characteristic Classes via Chern-Weil Theory
Question: Consider a complex vector bundle E of rank 2 over a compact oriented surface Σ. Let A be a connection on E with curvature form F. If the first Chern class c1(E) is represented by the closed 2-form (i)/(2π) tr(F), calculate the integral of this form over Σ given that locally F = dA and globally it satisfies the non-abelian Bianchi identity d_A F = 0. Assume the metric is flat for simplicity.
Solution: The first Chern class is defined as [c1(E)] = [(i)/(2π) tr(F)] in de Rham cohomology. Since d_A F = 0, the form ω = (i)/(2π) tr(F) is closed (dω=0). By Stokes' theorem, if Σ has no boundary, ∫_Σ d(…) = 0. However, the integral of a closed form representing a cohomology class depends only on the topology. Using the Chern-Weil homomorphism, we know that (1)/(2π) ∫_Σ tr(F) is an integer (the Chern number). If the bundle is trivial, this integral is 0. If it is the Hopf bundle over S2, the integral is 1. The calculation confirms that while locally F=dA suggests exactness, globally the trace of the curvature integrates to a topological invariant. Specifically, ∫_Σ (i)/(2π) tr(F) = c1(E)[Σ] ∈ mathbb{Z}.
Exam-Day Strategy: Identifying Metric Dependence
When solving problems involving the codifferential δ or the Hodge Laplacian Δ, always check if the metric g is specified. If the problem states "flat space" or implies Euclidean coordinates, assume star star = (-1)k(n-k). However, in general relativity contexts, remember that Δ is not simply dδ + δ d without verifying the boundary conditions. A common trap is assuming ∫_M ⟨ dα, β ⟩ = ∫_M ⟨ α, dβ ⟩ holds for non-compact manifolds without checking decay conditions at infinity.

Geometric Flows, Deformation Theory, and Non-Linear Dynamics

The study of Cartan calculus extends naturally to the analysis of geometric flows, where tensor fields evolve under differential operators. The Ricci flow, for example, describes a process that smooths out the curvature of a Riemannian manifold over time, governed by the equation frac{∂ gij}{∂ t} = -2 Rij. Understanding this requires mastering the evolution of tensors under diffeomorphisms and the interaction between the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature. In competitive exams, questions often ask for the stability of solutions or the formation of singularities in finite time. This connects to Perelman's work on the Poincaré conjecture, where the topology of the manifold dictates the long-term behavior of the flow.

Another advanced topic is the deformation of complex structures and Kähler geometry. On a Kähler manifold, the complex structure J satisfies J2 = -I, and the fundamental form ω(X,Y) = g(JX,Y) is closed (dω=0). The condition that the metric is compatible with the complex structure leads to the existence of holomorphic forms. Perturbing the complex structure while preserving the Kähler condition is a subtle problem involving Hodge theory on moduli spaces. This is relevant in string theory compactifications where the shape of extra dimensions affects physical constants.

Furthermore, one must consider the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic curvature when embedding manifolds into higher-dimensional spaces. The Gauss-Codazzi equations relate the Riemann curvature tensor of the submanifold to the second fundamental form (extrinsic curvature). This is crucial in general relativity for studying black hole horizons or minimal surfaces in AdS space. In such scenarios, the mean curvature vector plays a role analogous to the scalar curvature in pure geometry. Questions may present a surface defined implicitly and ask for its Gaussian curvature using the first and second fundamental forms derived from the metric.

Technique: Analyzing Ricci Flow Singularities
Question: Consider the 2-dimensional Ricci flow (∂ g)/(∂ t) = -2 R g on a compact surface. If the initial metric has Gaussian curvature K(x,0) = f(x), derive the evolution equation for the scalar curvature R(t) and determine if the flow can develop a finite-time singularity.
Solution: In 2 dimensions, the Ricci tensor is proportional to the metric: Rij = (1)/(2) R gij. The scalar curvature R evolves according to (∂ R)/(∂ t) = Δ R + 2 R2, where Δ is the Laplacian. This is a non-linear parabolic PDE. Since the term 2 R2 is positive, regions of high positive curvature grow rapidly. If the initial data has sufficiently large positive curvature concentration, the solution may blow up in finite time (forming a singularity).
