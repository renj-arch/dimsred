Quantum Field Theory — Fields, Canonical Quantization, Feynman Diagrams & Renormalization

Quantum field theory (QFT) is the language of modern particle physics. It combines quantum mechanics with special relativity by promoting fields, rather than particles, to the fundamental objects. Particles appear as quantised excitations of those fields, and interactions are described by local terms in a Lagrangian.

“The theory of fields is the only way we know to combine quantum mechanics with relativity.”
A central lesson of twentieth-century physics
Part 1

Classical Fields and the Lagrangian Formalism

Classical Fields
Actions, equations of motion and symmetries

A field φ(x) assigns a value to every point of spacetime. The dynamics follow from the action S = ∫L d⁴x, where L is the Lagrangian density. For a real scalar field with mass m, the Klein–Gordon Lagrangian is

L = ½(∂μφ)(∂μφ) − ½m²φ² − V(φ),

and the Euler–Lagrange equation gives (□ + m²)φ = 0, with □ = ∂μ∂μ. For a complex scalar field, a U(1) phase symmetry produces a conserved Noether current. The Dirac field ψ describes spin-½ particles and obeys (iγμ∂μ − m)ψ = 0. Gauge fields Aμ describe spin-1 particles; their field strength Fμν = ∂μAν − ∂νAμ is invariant under Aμ → Aμ + ∂μα.

Symmetries are central: Noether’s theorem relates continuous symmetries to conserved currents, and local (gauge) symmetries dictate the form of interactions. The Standard Model is a gauge theory with gauge group SU(3)C × SU(2)L × U(1)Y.

Part 2

Canonical Quantization and Fock Space

Quantization
From oscillators to particles

In canonical quantization, the field and its conjugate momentum become operators satisfying equal-time commutation relations. Expanding a free scalar field in Fourier modes gives

φ(x) = ∫ d³k/((2π)³ 2Ek) [ake−ik·x + a†keik·x],

where ak and a†k are annihilation and creation operators. The vacuum |0⟩ is annihilated by every ak, and excited states are built by acting with creation operators. This is the Fock space: a Hilbert space with a variable number of particles. The energy of a mode is Ek = √(k²c² + m²c⁴) (in units with c = 1, Ek = √(k² + m²)).

Equal-time commutation relations imply the Feynman propagator

DF(x − y) = ∫ d⁴k/((2π)⁴) e−ik·(x−y)/(k² − m² + iε),

which describes the amplitude for a disturbance to propagate between two spacetime points. The iε prescription implements the correct time ordering and boundary conditions.

Part 3

Feynman Diagrams and Scattering Theory

Perturbation Theory
Vertices, propagators and cross sections

Interactions are treated perturbatively by expanding the time-ordered exponential of the interaction Hamiltonian. Wick’s theorem reduces each term to products of propagators, and the result can be represented graphically by Feynman diagrams. Internal lines are propagators; vertices carry coupling constants and enforce momentum conservation; external lines represent asymptotic particles.

For a theory with interaction λφ⁴/4!, the vertex factor is −iλ and the tree-level 2→2 scattering amplitude receives contributions from s-, t- and u-channel diagrams. The invariant amplitude M is related to the differential cross section by

dσ/dΩ = |M|²/(64π²s)

for 2→2 scattering in the centre-of-mass frame (for equal masses; the general expression includes the initial flux and phase-space factors). Loops introduce integrals over undetermined momenta and are the origin of both precision predictions and ultraviolet divergences.

Part 4

Gauge Theories, Symmetry Breaking and Renormalization

The Standard Model
QED, Yang–Mills, the Higgs mechanism and running couplings

Quantum electrodynamics (QED) is the U(1) gauge theory of the electromagnetic field coupled to charged fermions. Its predictions for the electron magnetic moment and Lamb shift agree with experiment to many significant figures. Non-Abelian Yang–Mills theories, such as SU(3) colour and SU(2) weak isospin, have self-interacting gauge bosons; this leads to asymptotic freedom in QCD and confinement at low energies.

In the Standard Model, the electroweak symmetry SU(2)L × U(1)Y is spontaneously broken by the Higgs field. The Higgs potential V(φ) = −μ²|φ|² + λ|φ|⁴ has a nonzero vacuum expectation value, giving masses to the W and Z bosons and to fermions through Yukawa couplings while leaving the photon massless.

Loop corrections diverge in the ultraviolet. Renormalization absorbs these divergences into a finite number of measurable parameters (masses, couplings and field normalisations), order by order in perturbation theory. The renormalisation group describes how couplings run with energy scale; QCD becomes weak at high energies, while the electromagnetic coupling grows slowly. A theory is renormalizable when all divergences can be absorbed this way, as is the case for the Standard Model.

Part 5: The S-Matrix Bootstrap and Unitarity Constraints

Moving beyond the Standard Model's specific particle content, we enter the realm of the S-matrix program. Here, one does not assume a Lagrangian but rather derives constraints on scattering amplitudes based solely on fundamental principles: Lorentz invariance, unitarity (probability conservation), analyticity, and crossing symmetry. This approach is crucial for understanding why certain interactions are forbidden or how high-energy behavior dictates low-energy effective theories.

Landmark Case: The Partial Wave Analysis

In the study of 2-to-2 scattering a + b → c + d, we decompose the amplitude M(s, t) into partial waves a_l(s). Unitarity requires that the S-matrix eigenvalues lie on the unit circle in the complex plane. For elastic scattering below inelastic thresholds, this implies |S_l| = 1, or S_l = e2iδ_l where δ_l is the phase shift. The optical theorem emerges naturally here: the imaginary part of the forward scattering amplitude (t=0) is proportional to the total cross-section.

Examiner Twist: Students often confuse the partial wave expansion in position space with momentum space. In QFT, we work in momentum space, but the unitarity bound |Re(a_l)| ≤ 1/2 (derived from S = 1 + 2i a_l) places a strict limit on coupling strengths at high energies. If a theory yields an amplitude growing as s2 (like φ4 contact terms at loop level without renormalization), it violates unitarity at some scale Λ ∼ 4π v, signaling the need for new physics (e.g., Higgs boson) to restore consistency.

Exception: Non-Perturbative Effects

Feynman diagrams represent perturbative expansions. However, non-perturbative phenomena like instantons or solitons cannot be seen in any finite order of the coupling constant g. These are essential for understanding vacuum structure (theta vacua) and anomalies. A classic example is the resolution of the strong CP problem via the Peccei-Quinn mechanism, which introduces a new global symmetry to dynamically set the QCD θ parameter to zero.

Technique: Deriving the Unitarity Bound for Scalar Scattering
Consider a theory with a scalar field φ and a quartic interaction Lint = -(λ)/(4!)φ4. Calculate the tree-level amplitude for 2 → 2 scattering. Then, consider the one-loop "s-channel" diagram. Show that unitarity is violated at high energies if no new degrees of freedom (like a Higgs) are introduced to cancel the growing terms.
Solution:
At tree level, Mtree ∼ -iλ. This is constant with energy s.
At one loop in the s-channel, we have a bubble diagram. The amplitude behaves as Mloop ∝ λ2 ln(s/μ2). While logarithmic growth is slow, consider a theory where the coupling runs or if we look at dimension-6 operators generated by integrating out heavy states.
The critical test is the partial wave a0 = (1)/(32π) ∫-11 d(cosθ) M(s, t). If M grows as s (as in non-renormalizable theories or without a Higgs), then for large s, |a0| exceeds 1/2. Specifically, in the Standard Model without the Higgs, longitudinal W-boson scattering (W_L W_L → W_L W_L) grows as s/v2. The amplitude M ≈ s/v2. The partial wave a0 ≈ s/(16π v2). Unitarity requires |a0| < 1, implying Ecm < √(16π)v ≈ 1.2 TeV. Since the LHC discovered the Higgs at 125 GeV, it confirmed that the Higgs mechanism tames this growth, preserving unitarity.

Part 6: Anomalies, Global Symmetries, and 't Hooft Matching

While local gauge symmetries must be exact (to preserve renormalizability and charge conservation), global symmetries can be anomalous. This distinction is vital for understanding why certain particles exist and how they interact.

Mechanism: The Triangle Anomaly

In chiral gauge theories, the path integral measure is not invariant under chiral transformations due to the Jacobian determinant arising from the regularization of fermion loops. This leads to the Adler-Bell-Jackiw anomaly. For a theory to be consistent, the sum of anomalies over all fermion generations must vanish. In the Standard Model, the cancellation between quarks and leptons in each generation is a non-trivial fact that constrains particle content.

't Hooft Anomaly Matching

This powerful principle states that if a global symmetry is exact at high energies (UV) but spontaneously broken or realized differently at low energies (IR), the anomaly coefficients must match. Since anomalies are topological and insensitive to mass scales, they provide a bridge between UV Lagrangians and IR effective theories (like QCD chiral perturbation theory). This was instrumental in establishing the existence of composite particles (tetraquarks, pentaquarks) or ruling out certain models.

Technique: Checking Anomaly Cancellation in a New Generation
Propose a new fermion generation containing a vector-like quark Q and a chiral lepton doublet L. Determine the condition on the charges such that the [SU(2)]2 U(1) and [U(1)]3 anomalies vanish.
Solution:
For [SU(2)]2 U(1)_Y, the anomaly is proportional to Σdoublets Y. In one generation of SM: Quarks (3 × 1/6) + Leptons (1 × -1/2) = 1/2 - 1/2 = 0.
For [U(1)]3, we sum Y3. SM: 6 × (1/6)3 + 3 × (-1/3)3 + 2 × (1/2)3 + 1 × (-1)3 = 0.
If adding a new chiral fermion, one must ensure Σ Ynew3 = 0 and gauge anomaly cancellation. Vector-like fermions (same charge for L and R) do not contribute to chiral anomalies but add to the mass spectrum.

Part 7: Effective Field Theory (EFT) Power Counting and Wilsonian Renormalization

Renormalization is often taught as "absorbing infinities," but the modern Wilsonian view is about separating scales. We integrate out high-energy modes to obtain an Effective Lagrangian containing an infinite series of operators suppressed by powers of a heavy mass scale M.

Applied Scenario: Chiral Perturbation Theory (chiPT)

QCD at low energies involves massive hadrons, not quarks. We construct an EFT using Goldstone bosons (pions) parameterizing spontaneous symmetry breaking. The Lagrangian is Leff = L2 + L4 + …, where L2 contains the kinetic terms and pion masses, L4 contains four-pion interactions suppressed by 1/Λ_chi2. This allows precision predictions for pion scattering lengths (a0) which match experimental data perfectly.

Examiner Twist: Power Counting Rules

In non-relativistic EFTs (like Pionless EFT for nuclear physics), one must carefully count powers of momentum Q. A naive expansion fails if the theory is "singular" (e.g., near a bound state). The modern power counting (Erb-Pineda-Stefanovici) adjusts the scaling of loop momenta to match experimental data, often requiring non-perturbative resummation of specific diagrams.

Technique: Constructing the Leading Order EFT for Fermion Scattering
Write down the leading order effective Lagrangian for two heavy fermions scattering via a contact interaction, assuming a heavy mediator M has been integrated out. Explain why dimension-6 operators dominate at low energy compared to dimension-4.
Solution:
The full theory has L = psi i not∂ psi + (g2)/(M2) (psipsi)2. Integrating out M generates a four-fermion operator (C)/(M2)(psiΓpsi)(psiΓpsi). This is dimension-6. At energy E ≪ M, the amplitude M ∼ C E0 / M2 (constant contact). Dimension-4 operators describe free propagation. The coefficient of the dim-6 operator runs with scale according to the Renormalization Group equations in the EFT, encoding the physics of the heavy mediator into a single parameter.
