Cobordism Theory — Cobordism Groups, Thom Spaces & the Pontryagin-Thom Construction

Cobordism theory studies smooth manifolds up to the equivalence relation of cobordism: two closed n-manifolds M and N are cobordant if their disjoint union M⊔N bounds a compact (n+1)-manifold W. This notion, introduced by Pontryagin and systematized by Thom, turns the set of equivalence classes into a graded ring Ω_* under disjoint union (addition) and Cartesian product (multiplication). Thom's theorem (1954) identifies the unoriented cobordism ring Ω_*^O with a polynomial ring over ℤ/2, a landmark result that initiated the modern theory of cobordism. The Pontryagin-Thom construction reduces the computation of cobordism groups to stable homotopy theory via the Thom spectrum MG. Complex cobordism (MU) connects to formal group laws through Quillen's theorem: π_*(MU) is the Lazard ring. The Adams-Novikov spectral sequence uses MU to compute stable homotopy groups of spheres. Cobordism also underpins the Hirzebruch signature theorem, genera (L-genus, Â-genus, Todd genus, Witten genus), and the theory of topological modular forms (TMF).

"Cobordism is the most computable of all generalized cohomology theories."
— John Milnor
Part 1

📏 Cobordism Groups

Foundations
Cobordism Groups, the Cobordism Ring, and Thom's Theorem
A closed smooth n-manifold M is null-cobordant (or bounds) if M = ∂W for some compact (n+1)-manifold W. Two closed n-manifolds M, N are cobordant (written M ~ N) if M⊔N is null-cobordant — i.e., there is a compact (n+1)-manifold W with ∂W = M⊔N. The unoriented cobordism group Ω_n^O is the set of equivalence classes; the operation is disjoint union, which makes each group a vector space over ℤ/2 because every manifold is its own inverse (M⊔M = ∂(M×[0,1])). The oriented cobordism group Ω_n^SO requires all manifolds and bordisms to be oriented. Examples: Ω_0^O ≅ ℤ/2 (a point is not a boundary since the boundary of a 1-manifold has an even number of points), Ω_1^O = 0 (the circle S^1 bounds the disk D^2), Ω_2^O ≅ ℤ/2 (the real projective plane ℝℙ² is not orientable and does not bound a 3-manifold; but 2ℝℙ² ≅ ℝℙ²#ℝℙ² bounds). The graded abelian group Ω_*^O = ⊕_{n≥0} Ω_n^O becomes a cobordism ring with multiplication given by Cartesian product. Thom's theorem (1954): Ω_*^O ≅ ℤ/2[x_2, x_4, x_5, x_6, x_7, x_8, ...] where there is one generator in each degree n not of the form 2ᵏ−1. The generator in degree 2 is represented by ℝℙ²; in degree 4 by ℝℙ⁴; and so on. For oriented cobordism, Ω_*^SO ⊗ ℚ ≅ ℚ[ℂℙ², ℂℙ⁴, ...] — a polynomial ring over ℚ on generators in even degrees.
Cobordism groups classify manifolds up to the relation of bounding a common manifold; the unoriented cobordism ring is a polynomial algebra over ℤ/2 with one generator in most degrees.
Part 2

🌍 The Pontryagin-Thom Construction

Thom Spaces
The Pontryagin-Thom Map, Thom Spectra, and MG
The Pontryagin-Thom construction is a bridge between manifold geometry and stable homotopy theory. Given a closed smooth n-manifold Mⁿ, embed it in ℝ^{n+k} for large k. The normal bundle ν has a tubular neighborhood T ≅ D(ν) (the disk bundle of ν). Collapse the complement of T to a point: this yields the Pontryagin-Thom map τ: S^{n+k} → Th(ν), where Th(ν) = D(ν)/S(ν) is the Thom space of ν (disk bundle modulo boundary sphere). For a G-structure on M (e.g., orientation, spin, complex structure), the normal bundle is classified by a map ν: M → BG(k). The resulting map S^{n+k} → Th(γ_k) (where γ_k is the universal bundle over BG(k)) represents an element of π_{n+k}(Th(γ_k)). Taking k → ∞ gives the Thom spectrum MG: the sequence of Thom spaces {Th(γ_k)} with suspension maps Σ Th(γ_k) → Th(γ_{k+1}). The Pontryagin-Thom theorem states: π_n(MG) ≅ Ω_n^G — the nth stable homotopy group of the Thom spectrum MG is precisely the cobordism group of n-dimensional G-manifolds. For unoriented cobordism, G = O and π_*(MO) ≅ Ω_*^O. For oriented cobordism, G = SO. For complex cobordism, G = U. The spectrum MO has π_*(MO) ≅ ℤ/2[x_2, x_4, x_5, ...] as a graded ring. The Thom isomorphism theorem: H^*(Th(γ_k)) ≅ H^*(BG(k)) (a shift), which is used to compute the cohomology of Thom spectra via the Serre spectral sequence.
The Pontryagin-Thom construction translates the geometric problem of classifying manifolds up to cobordism into the homotopy-theoretic problem of computing stable homotopy groups of Thom spectra.
Part 3

🧮 Complex Cobordism and Formal Group Laws

MU
Complex Cobordism, the Lazard Ring, and Quillen's Theorem
Complex cobordism MU is the Thom spectrum for the unitary group U — that is, the spectrum whose spaces are Th(γ_k) for the universal complex k-plane bundles over BU(k). The coefficient ring MU_* = π_*(MU) is a graded ring concentrated in even degrees: MU_* ≅ ℤ[x_1, x_2, ...] with |x_i| = 2i. Quillen's theorem (1969): the coefficient ring π_*(MU) is canonically isomorphic to the Lazard ring L — the universal ring for 1-dimensional formal group laws. A 1-dimensional formal group law over a ring R is a power series F(x,y) = x + y + Σ_{i,j≥1} a_{ij} x^i y^j ∈ R[[x,y]] satisfying associativity, commutativity, and unit axioms. The Lazard ring L is the polynomial ring ℤ[a_{ij}]/(relations from formal group law axioms), graded such that |a_{ij}| = 2(i+j−1). Quillen proved that the formal group law on MU comes from the universal formal group law via the canonical map L → MU_*. The formal group law of a complex-oriented cohomology theory E is encoded by its formal group law F_E(x,y) = sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^E x^i y^j, where the coefficients are images of the Lazard generators. The Conner-Floyd theorem: MU^*(X) ⊗_{MU_*} ℤ ≅ H^*(X, ℤ). More generally, any complex-oriented cohomology theory E is a module over MU via the universal property of the Lazard ring. The Adams-Novikov spectral sequence (ANSS) is an important computational tool: E₂^{s,t} = Ext_{MU_*MU}^s(MU_*, MU_*(X)) ⇒ π_{t−s}(X). For X = S⁰, the ANSS computes the stable homotopy groups of spheres. The MU_*MU-comodule structure encodes deep number-theoretic information — the chromatic filtration organizes stable homotopy theory by heights of formal group laws (the Morava stabilizer group at each prime and height n).
Complex cobordism MU carries the universal formal group law; Quillen's identification with the Lazard ring makes MU the universal complex-oriented cohomology theory and the foundation for chromatic stable homotopy theory.
Part 4

🔥 Cobordism in Algebraic Topology and Applications

Genera
The Hirzebruch Signature Theorem, Genera, and Topological Modular Forms
A genus is a ring homomorphism φ: Ω_*^SO → R from the oriented cobordism ring to a commutative ring R. Each genus is determined by its values on ℂℙ^{2k} (the even-dimensional complex projective spaces). The Hirzebruch signature theorem: for a closed oriented 4k-manifold M, the signature sign(M) satisfies sign(M) = ⟨L(p), [M]⟩, where L(p) is the Hirzebruch L-genus — a certain multiplicative sequence in the Pontryagin classes of M. The Â-genus (A-hat genus) is the genus corresponding to the Dirac operator index; it vanishes on manifolds that admit a Riemannian metric of positive scalar curvature (Lichnerowicz theorem). The Todd genus Td(M) is the genus associated to complex cobordism; for a complex manifold, Td(M) = χ(M, O_M) by the Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch theorem. The Witten genus is a genus for string manifolds (those with w₁ = 0, w₂ = 0, and p₁/2 = 0) taking values in modular forms; it is the ∫-genus in elliptic cohomology. The Ochanine elliptic genus is a multiplicative genus with values in the ring of modular forms. The Stolz-Teichner program proposes that supersymmetric quantum field theories provide cocycles for cohomology theories: 0-dimensional SUSY field theories → K-theory; 1-dimensional SUSY field theories → TMF (topological modular forms). TMF is the spectrum of topological modular forms constructed by Hopkins-Miller and Goerss-Hopkins-Miller: it is the universal elliptic cohomology theory and its coefficient ring π_*(TMF) is the ring of integral modular forms. The Ando-Hopkins-Rezk orientation MString → TMF lifts the Witten genus to a map of ring spectra, showing that every string manifold has a TMF-orientation. The Hopkins-Mahowald theorem relates the Hurewicz image of the sphere spectrum in TMF to modular forms. These ideas interconnect cobordism, number theory, and mathematical physics at the deepest level.
Genera are cobordism invariants that connect manifold topology to number theory and modular forms; topological modular forms (TMF) represent the universal elliptic cohomology theory and receive an orientation from string cobordism.

Unoriented Cobordism and the Thom Spectrum MO

The unoriented cobordism ring Ω*O was the first cobordism ring to be completely computed. Thom proved in 1954 that the Pontryagin–Thom construction yields an isomorphism ΩnO ≅ πn(MO), where MO is the Thom spectrum of the universal real vector bundle over BO. The homotopy groups of MO are known to be a polynomial algebra over the field 𝔽2 on one generator in each dimension that is not a power of two. Concretely,

Ω*O ≅ 𝔽2[xi | i ≠ 2k], |xi| = i.

The generators can be represented by the real projective spaces ℝℙi for i not a power of two. The Stiefel–Whitney numbers give a complete set of invariants: two closed smooth manifolds are unoriented cobordant if and only if all their Stiefel–Whitney numbers agree. This result makes unoriented cobordism a purely algebraic theory over 𝔽2 and provides the foundation for the Adams spectral sequence based on MO.

Key computational tools

  • The Thom isomorphism H*(MO; 𝔽2) ≅ H*(BO; 𝔽2) identifies the cohomology of MO with the polynomial algebra 𝔽2[w1, w2, …] on the universal Stiefel–Whitney classes.
  • The Steenrod algebra action on H*(MO) is determined by the Wu formula, allowing the Adams spectral sequence to collapse at the E2-page and yield the polynomial description above.
  • The Conner–Floyd theorem for MO states that MO*(X) ⊗MO* 𝔽2 ≅ H*(X; 𝔽2), showing that unoriented cobordism is the universal 𝔽2-oriented cohomology theory.

Oriented Cobordism and the Thom Spectrum MSO

Oriented cobordism Ω*SO is richer because the coefficient ring contains torsion. Thom’s Pontryagin–Thom construction gives ΩnSO ≅ πn(MSO), where MSO is the Thom spectrum of the universal oriented bundle over BSO. Rationally, the ring is a polynomial algebra on the even‑dimensional complex projective spaces:

Ω*SO ⊗ ℚ ≅ ℚ[ℂℙ2, ℂℙ4, ℂℙ6, …].

Integrally, the structure was determined by Wall (1960) and later refined by Milnor and Stong. The ring Ω*SO is a polynomial algebra over ℤ on generators in dimensions 4k, together with ℤ/2‑torsion in dimensions 8k+1 and 8k+2. The Pontryagin numbers and the Stiefel–Whitney numbers (mod 2) together form a complete set of invariants for oriented cobordism. In particular, a closed oriented manifold bounds an oriented manifold if and only if all its Pontryagin numbers and all its Stiefel–Whitney numbers vanish.

Generators and relations

  • For each k ≥ 1 there is a generator y4k which can be taken as the Milnor hypersurface H2k,2k ⊂ ℂℙ2k × ℂℙ2k.
  • The 2‑torsion generators are detected by the Kervaire invariant and the Arf invariant of the associated quadratic forms on middle‑dimensional homology.
  • The Hirzebruch signature theorem expresses the signature of a 4k‑manifold as a polynomial in Pontryagin numbers; this polynomial is the L‑genus and gives the image of the signature homomorphism Ω4kSO → ℤ.
Worked example: computing an oriented cobordism class
Determine whether the product manifold ℂℙ2 × ℂℙ2 is zero in Ω8SO.
The oriented cobordism group Ω8SO is free abelian of rank two, generated by ℂℙ4 and ℂℙ2 × ℂℙ2. The Pontryagin numbers of ℂℙ2 × ℂℙ2 are p12 = 4 and p2 = 0, while for ℂℙ4 they are p12 = 25 and p2 = 10. Since the Pontryagin numbers of ℂℙ2 × ℂℙ2 are not all zero, the manifold is not null‑cobordant. Hence its class in Ω8SO is non‑zero.
Exam strategy
When a question asks for the cobordism class of a product, compute the Pontryagin numbers using the Whitney sum formula for tangent bundles. Compare the resulting numbers with the known basis of Ω*SO ⊗ ℚ; if they are linearly independent from the basis elements, the class is non‑zero. Memorise the first few Pontryagin numbers of ℂℙ2k and of the Milnor hypersurfaces to speed up calculations.

Spin Cobordism and the Anderson–Brown–Peterson Spectral Sequence

Spin cobordism Ω*Spin classifies manifolds equipped with a spin structure, i.e. a lift of the structure group from SO to Spin. The Pontryagin–Thom isomorphism identifies ΩnSpin with πn(MSpin), the homotopy groups of the Thom spectrum of the universal spin bundle over BSpin. Anderson, Brown, and Peterson (1966) constructed a spectral sequence

E2s,t = ExtA(1)s,t(H*(MSpin; 𝔽2), 𝔽2) ⇒ πt−s(MSpin)̂2,

where A(1) is the subalgebra of the Steenrod algebra generated by Sq1 and Sq2. This spectral sequence computes the 2‑completed spin cobordism groups and reveals the close relationship between MSpin and the real K‑theory spectrum KO. In low dimensions the groups are:

  • Ω0Spin ≅ ℤ, generated by a point.
  • Ω1Spin = Ω2Spin = 0.
  • Ω3Spin = 0.
  • Ω4Spin ≅ ℤ, generated by the K3 surface (signature −16).
  • Ω5Spin = Ω6Spin = 0.
  • Ω7Spin = 0.
  • Ω8Spin ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ, generated by the K3 surface squared and the Bott manifold.

The Â‑genus gives a ring homomorphism Ω*Spin → ℤ which is an isomorphism after tensoring with ℚ. The Anderson–Brown–Peterson spectral sequence also shows that the 2‑primary torsion in spin cobordism is detected by the KO‑theory orientation MSpin → KO.

Stratified Cobordism and Manifolds with Singularities

Classical cobordism theory assumes smooth closed manifolds. In many geometric problems one encounters spaces with singularities, such as algebraic varieties or orbit spaces of group actions. Stratified cobordism, developed by Quinn (1988) and extended by Siegel and others, provides a framework where the objects are stratified spaces (e.g. Whitney stratified sets) and the cobordisms are stratified maps satisfying transversality conditions on each stratum. The resulting cobordism groups Ω*strat fit into a long exact sequence relating them to the ordinary cobordism groups of the links of the strata.

Key features include:

  • The Thom–Mather isotopy theorem ensures that a stratified cobordism can be decomposed into elementary cobordisms along each stratum.
  • For a cone on a manifold L, the stratified cobordism class is determined by the ordinary cobordism class of L together with a choice of normal data.
  • The intersection homology groups of Goresky–MacPherson provide invariants of stratified spaces that are compatible with stratified cobordism; in particular, the signature of a Witt space is a stratified cobordism invariant.
  • Applications include the classification of singular algebraic varieties up to cobordism and the study of the topology of moduli spaces with boundary strata.

Surgery Theory and the Classification of High‑Dimensional Manifolds

Surgery theory, created by Browder, Novikov, Sullivan, and Wall in the 1960s, uses cobordism as a tool to classify manifolds up to diffeomorphism or homeomorphism in dimensions ≥ 5. The central object is the surgery exact sequence

⋯ → Ln+1(π1X) → 𝒮(X) → [X, G/O] → Ln(π1X) → ⋯

where 𝒮(X) is the structure set of manifolds homotopy equivalent to a given Poincaré complex X, [X, G/O] is the set of normal invariants (a cobordism group of degree‑one normal maps), and L*(π1X) are the Wall surgery obstruction groups. The map [X, G/O] → Ln(π1X) is the surgery obstruction; its kernel consists of normal invariants that can be realized by a homotopy equivalence.

Important consequences:

  • The s‑cobordism theorem: an h‑cobordism between simply‑connected manifolds of dimension ≥ 5 is trivial if and only if its Whitehead torsion vanishes.
  • The classification of exotic spheres: the group Θn of homotopy n‑spheres up to h‑cobordism fits into an exact sequence involving the stable homotopy groups of spheres and the image of the J‑homomorphism.
  • For simply‑connected manifolds, the surgery obstruction groups Ln(ℤ) are 8‑periodic: L4k ≅ ℤ (signature), L4k+2 ≅ ℤ/2 (Arf invariant), and the odd groups vanish.
  • The Novikov conjecture on homotopy invariance of higher signatures is equivalent to the injectivity of the assembly map in the surgery exact sequence for the group C*‑algebra.
  • Confusing unoriented and oriented cobordism invariants; Stiefel–Whitney numbers alone do not detect oriented cobordism.
  • Assuming the Pontryagin numbers generate all torsion in Ω*SO; 2‑torsion requires Stiefel–Whitney numbers as well.
  • Overlooking the role of the Anderson–Brown–Peterson spectral sequence in computing spin cobordism; it is not merely the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence.
  • Treating stratified cobordism as ordinary cobordism of the underlying space; the link data and normal structures are essential.
  • Misapplying the surgery exact sequence to low‑dimensional manifolds (n ≤ 4) where the theory fails without additional hypotheses.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the structure set 𝒮(X) and the set of normal invariants [X, G/O]; they are related but not identical.
  • Forgetting that the Â‑genus vanishes on manifolds admitting positive scalar curvature, a key obstruction in spin geometry.

Mastering these advanced topics equips you to tackle the deepest problems in manifold topology and stable homotopy theory.
