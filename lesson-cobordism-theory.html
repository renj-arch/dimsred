Cobordism Theory — Cobordism Groups, Thom Spaces & the Pontryagin-Thom Construction

Cobordism theory studies smooth manifolds up to the equivalence relation of cobordism: two closed n-manifolds M and N are cobordant if their disjoint union M⊔N bounds a compact (n+1)-manifold W. This notion, introduced by Pontryagin and systematized by Thom, turns the set of equivalence classes into a graded ring Ω_* under disjoint union (addition) and Cartesian product (multiplication). Thom's theorem (1954) identifies the unoriented cobordism ring Ω_*^O with a polynomial ring over ℤ/2, a landmark result that initiated the modern theory of cobordism. The Pontryagin-Thom construction reduces the computation of cobordism groups to stable homotopy theory via the Thom spectrum MG. Complex cobordism (MU) connects to formal group laws through Quillen's theorem: π_*(MU) is the Lazard ring. The Adams-Novikov spectral sequence uses MU to compute stable homotopy groups of spheres. Cobordism also underpins the Hirzebruch signature theorem, genera (L-genus, Â-genus, Todd genus, Witten genus), and the theory of topological modular forms (TMF).

"Cobordism is the most computable of all generalized cohomology theories."
— John Milnor
Part 1

📏 Cobordism Groups

Foundations
Cobordism Groups, the Cobordism Ring, and Thom's Theorem
A closed smooth n-manifold M is null-cobordant (or bounds) if M = ∂W for some compact (n+1)-manifold W. Two closed n-manifolds M, N are cobordant (written M ~ N) if M⊔N is null-cobordant — i.e., there is a compact (n+1)-manifold W with ∂W = M⊔N. The unoriented cobordism group Ω_n^O is the set of equivalence classes; the operation is disjoint union, which makes each group a vector space over ℤ/2 because every manifold is its own inverse (M⊔M = ∂(M×[0,1])). The oriented cobordism group Ω_n^SO requires all manifolds and bordisms to be oriented. Examples: Ω_0^O ≅ ℤ/2 (a point is not a boundary since the boundary of a 1-manifold has an even number of points), Ω_1^O = 0 (the circle S^1 bounds the disk D^2), Ω_2^O ≅ ℤ/2 (the real projective plane ℝℙ² is not orientable and does not bound a 3-manifold; but 2ℝℙ² ≅ ℝℙ²#ℝℙ² bounds). The graded abelian group Ω_*^O = ⊕_{n≥0} Ω_n^O becomes a cobordism ring with multiplication given by Cartesian product. Thom's theorem (1954): Ω_*^O ≅ ℤ/2[x_2, x_4, x_5, x_6, x_7, x_8, ...] where there is one generator in each degree n not of the form 2ᵏ−1. The generator in degree 2 is represented by ℝℙ²; in degree 4 by ℝℙ⁴; and so on. For oriented cobordism, Ω_*^SO ⊗ ℚ ≅ ℚ[ℂℙ², ℂℙ⁴, ...] — a polynomial ring over ℚ on generators in even degrees.
Cobordism groups classify manifolds up to the relation of bounding a common manifold; the unoriented cobordism ring is a polynomial algebra over ℤ/2 with one generator in most degrees.
Part 2

🌍 The Pontryagin-Thom Construction

Thom Spaces
The Pontryagin-Thom Map, Thom Spectra, and MG
The Pontryagin-Thom construction is a bridge between manifold geometry and stable homotopy theory. Given a closed smooth n-manifold Mⁿ, embed it in ℝ^{n+k} for large k. The normal bundle ν has a tubular neighborhood T ≅ D(ν) (the disk bundle of ν). Collapse the complement of T to a point: this yields the Pontryagin-Thom map τ: S^{n+k} → Th(ν), where Th(ν) = D(ν)/S(ν) is the Thom space of ν (disk bundle modulo boundary sphere). For a G-structure on M (e.g., orientation, spin, complex structure), the normal bundle is classified by a map ν: M → BG(k). The resulting map S^{n+k} → Th(γ_k) (where γ_k is the universal bundle over BG(k)) represents an element of π_{n+k}(Th(γ_k)). Taking k → ∞ gives the Thom spectrum MG: the sequence of Thom spaces {Th(γ_k)} with suspension maps Σ Th(γ_k) → Th(γ_{k+1}). The Pontryagin-Thom theorem states: π_n(MG) ≅ Ω_n^G — the nth stable homotopy group of the Thom spectrum MG is precisely the cobordism group of n-dimensional G-manifolds. For unoriented cobordism, G = O and π_*(MO) ≅ Ω_*^O. For oriented cobordism, G = SO. For complex cobordism, G = U. The spectrum MO has π_*(MO) ≅ ℤ/2[x_2, x_4, x_5, ...] as a graded ring. The Thom isomorphism theorem: H^*(Th(γ_k)) ≅ H^*(BG(k)) (a shift), which is used to compute the cohomology of Thom spectra via the Serre spectral sequence.
The Pontryagin-Thom construction translates the geometric problem of classifying manifolds up to cobordism into the homotopy-theoretic problem of computing stable homotopy groups of Thom spectra.
Part 3

🧮 Complex Cobordism and Formal Group Laws

MU
Complex Cobordism, the Lazard Ring, and Quillen's Theorem
Complex cobordism MU is the Thom spectrum for the unitary group U — that is, the spectrum whose spaces are Th(γ_k) for the universal complex k-plane bundles over BU(k). The coefficient ring MU_* = π_*(MU) is a graded ring concentrated in even degrees: MU_* ≅ ℤ[x_1, x_2, ...] with |x_i| = 2i. Quillen's theorem (1969): the coefficient ring π_*(MU) is canonically isomorphic to the Lazard ring L — the universal ring for 1-dimensional formal group laws. A 1-dimensional formal group law over a ring R is a power series F(x,y) = x + y + Σ_{i,j≥1} a_{ij} x^i y^j ∈ R[[x,y]] satisfying associativity, commutativity, and unit axioms. The Lazard ring L is the polynomial ring ℤ[a_{ij}]/(relations from formal group law axioms), graded such that |a_{ij}| = 2(i+j−1). Quillen proved that the formal group law on MU comes from the universal formal group law via the canonical map L → MU_*. The formal group law of a complex-oriented cohomology theory E is encoded by its formal group law F_E(x,y) = sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^E x^i y^j, where the coefficients are images of the Lazard generators. The Conner-Floyd theorem: MU^*(X) ⊗_{MU_*} ℤ ≅ H^*(X, ℤ). More generally, any complex-oriented cohomology theory E is a module over MU via the universal property of the Lazard ring. The Adams-Novikov spectral sequence (ANSS) is an important computational tool: E₂^{s,t} = Ext_{MU_*MU}^s(MU_*, MU_*(X)) ⇒ π_{t−s}(X). For X = S⁰, the ANSS computes the stable homotopy groups of spheres. The MU_*MU-comodule structure encodes deep number-theoretic information — the chromatic filtration organizes stable homotopy theory by heights of formal group laws (the Morava stabilizer group at each prime and height n).
Complex cobordism MU carries the universal formal group law; Quillen's identification with the Lazard ring makes MU the universal complex-oriented cohomology theory and the foundation for chromatic stable homotopy theory.
Part 4

🔥 Cobordism in Algebraic Topology and Applications

Genera
The Hirzebruch Signature Theorem, Genera, and Topological Modular Forms
A genus is a ring homomorphism φ: Ω_*^SO → R from the oriented cobordism ring to a commutative ring R. Each genus is determined by its values on ℂℙ^{2k} (the even-dimensional complex projective spaces). The Hirzebruch signature theorem: for a closed oriented 4k-manifold M, the signature sign(M) satisfies sign(M) = ⟨L(p), [M]⟩, where L(p) is the Hirzebruch L-genus — a certain multiplicative sequence in the Pontryagin classes of M. The Â-genus (A-hat genus) is the genus corresponding to the Dirac operator index; it vanishes on manifolds that admit a Riemannian metric of positive scalar curvature (Lichnerowicz theorem). The Todd genus Td(M) is the genus associated to complex cobordism; for a complex manifold, Td(M) = χ(M, O_M) by the Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch theorem. The Witten genus is a genus for string manifolds (those with w₁ = 0, w₂ = 0, and p₁/2 = 0) taking values in modular forms; it is the ∫-genus in elliptic cohomology. The Ochanine elliptic genus is a multiplicative genus with values in the ring of modular forms. The Stolz-Teichner program proposes that supersymmetric quantum field theories provide cocycles for cohomology theories: 0-dimensional SUSY field theories → K-theory; 1-dimensional SUSY field theories → TMF (topological modular forms). TMF is the spectrum of topological modular forms constructed by Hopkins-Miller and Goerss-Hopkins-Miller: it is the universal elliptic cohomology theory and its coefficient ring π_*(TMF) is the ring of integral modular forms. The Ando-Hopkins-Rezk orientation MString → TMF lifts the Witten genus to a map of ring spectra, showing that every string manifold has a TMF-orientation. The Hopkins-Mahowald theorem relates the Hurewicz image of the sphere spectrum in TMF to modular forms. These ideas interconnect cobordism, number theory, and mathematical physics at the deepest level.
Genera are cobordism invariants that connect manifold topology to number theory and modular forms; topological modular forms (TMF) represent the universal elliptic cohomology theory and receive an orientation from string cobordism.

The Chromatic Filter: Heights, Primes, and the Structure of Stable Homotopy

The transition from Complex Cobordism (MU) to the broader landscape of stable homotopy theory is governed by the concept of chromatic filtration. While Part 4 mentioned the Morava stabilizer group, a serious aspirant must understand the mechanism of this filtration. The Adams-Novikov Spectral Sequence (ANSS) does not merely compute groups; it organizes them by the height of the formal group law associated with the cohomology theory used as the coefficient ring.

The key insight is that MU_* acts as a universal coefficient ring, but when we localize at a prime p, the structure simplifies based on the height h. For a fixed prime p, the possible heights range from 1 to n = (p-1)/2. The chromatic tower stratifies the stable homotopy category into layers where each layer is controlled by the Morava K-theory K(n). A critical exception to the "universal" nature of MU occurs in the context of rational homotopy theory: over the rationals (Q), all formal group laws become isomorphic to the additive law, collapsing the chromatic complexity. This means π_*(MU) ⊗ Q is simply a polynomial ring on even generators, losing all torsion information. The examiner often twists this by asking for computations in the p-local category versus the rational category, or by presenting a problem where a genus vanishes rationally but not integrally.

Consider the landmark result of Quillen regarding the isomorphism between the Lazard ring and π_*(MU). This isomorphism holds over Z, but the map from the Lazard ring to the coefficient ring of any other complex-oriented theory E factors through MU. The exception arises in non-complex oriented theories like Real cobordism (KO) or K-theory (KU), where the formal group laws are not 1-dimensional over Z but rather have different periodicities and torsion structures (e.g., KU has a height 1 formal group law at every prime). The examiner may present a diagram chase involving the map MU → KU and ask you to identify the kernel, which relates to the image of the J-homomorphism.

In applied scenarios, this depth is crucial for understanding why certain invariants vanish. For instance, the Â-genus vanishes on manifolds with positive scalar curvature because the Dirac operator has no kernel (Lichnerowicz). In the chromatic context, this relates to the fact that the image of J consists of elements detected by K-theory (height 1), while higher chromatic layers correspond to more complex geometric structures like string manifolds and elliptic cohomology.

Technique: Analyzing the Kernel of the Map MU → KU
Let E be a complex-oriented cohomology theory with formal group law F_E. Suppose we have a map f: MU → E inducing an isomorphism on rational homotopy groups. If p=3, determine the structure of the kernel of the induced map on coefficient rings modulo p-torsion, given that E corresponds to height 1.
The map MU → KU induces a map on formal group laws from the universal law F_U to the multiplicative law F_KU(x,y) = x + y + xy. Over Z[1/2], these are related by an isomorphism, but over Z_p for p odd, they differ by torsion. At p=3, height 1 theories correspond to elliptic curves with j-invariant having specific properties. The kernel of MU_* → KU_* is generated by the elements corresponding to the difference in the formal group laws. Specifically, the kernel is a free module over MU_*/p generated by certain divided power classes. For p=3, the height 1 condition implies the formal group law has a specific reduction type. The kernel consists of elements that vanish when reduced modulo the ideal defining the multiplicative law. A standard result states that for KU, the image of J is finite cyclic at odd primes. Thus, the kernel captures the "missing" torsion information not detected by K-theory.
Exam-Day Strategy: The Rational vs. p-Local Trap
When solving problems involving cobordism rings or spectral sequences, always check the coefficient ring first. If the problem involves rational coefficients, ignore all torsion and formal group law complexities; treat everything as polynomial algebras on even variables. If the problem mentions "p-local" or specific primes (like p=2 or p=3), immediately switch to considering heights and Morava K-theories. A common mistake is applying the Lazard ring isomorphism over Q without realizing it collapses to a trivial statement. Always state your coefficient ring assumptions explicitly in your solution.

Topological Modular Forms: The Bridge Between Geometry and Number Theory

Moving beyond MU, the next frontier is Topological Modular Forms (TMF). This theory represents the universal elliptic cohomology. The construction relies on the work of Hopkins-Miller and Goerss-Hopkins-Miller. Unlike MU, which is associated with the unitary group BU, TMF is associated with the moduli stack of elliptic curves. The coefficient ring π_*(TMF) is the ring of integral modular forms. A critical distinction from MU is that TMF has non-trivial homotopy groups in negative degrees (unlike MU which is concentrated in even degrees starting at 0, though MU itself is periodic-like in structure but not periodic). Actually, MU is concentrated in even degrees, while TMF is also concentrated in even degrees but with different torsion properties.

The Witten genus is a map from the bordism ring of string manifolds to modular forms. The Ando-Hopkins-Rezk theorem establishes an orientation MString → TMF. This implies that every string manifold admits a TMF-orientation. The examiner often tests the understanding of the obstruction classes. For a manifold to admit a Witten genus, it must be a "string manifold," meaning w1 = 0, w2 = 0, and p1/2 = 0 (the first Pontryagin class divided by 2 must vanish). This condition is the topological constraint. If these conditions are not met, the Witten genus is not defined or vanishes. A classic trap is assuming that any oriented manifold admits a Witten genus; only string manifolds do.

In the context of the Stolz-Teichner program, the connection to quantum field theory provides a physical intuition: 0-dimensional SUSY theories compute K-theory, while 1-dimensional theories compute TMF. This hierarchy is essential for advanced problems where one must identify which cohomology theory corresponds to a given physical dimension or symmetry group. The exception here is that TMF is not a ring spectrum in the strictest sense over all integers without localization; its structure is deeply tied to the arithmetic of modular forms, specifically the level structure and congruence subgroups.

Technique: Verifying String Manifold Conditions
Given a closed oriented 8-manifold M with Pontryagin classes p1(M) = 4x and p2(M) = y, determine if M can support a Witten genus. Assume the manifold is simply connected.
To support a Witten genus, M must be a string manifold. The conditions are w1=0 (satisfied by oriented), w2=0, and p1/2 = 0 in H4(M; Z). Here p1(M) is given as 4x. For p1/2 to vanish in integer cohomology, we need the class 2x to be zero. If x represents a non-torsion generator of H4(M; Z), then 2x ≠ 0. Thus, if p1 = 4x where x is not torsion, the condition p1/2 = 0 fails because 2x is not zero in Z-coefficients. Therefore, this specific manifold does not admit a Witten genus unless x is a torsion element of order 2. The examiner might provide p1 = 4t where t is torsion; then p1/2 = 2t could be zero if t has order 2. Always check the integrality of the divided class.
Exam-Day Strategy: Modular Forms Arithmetic
When dealing with TMF or Witten genus problems, remember that the target ring is modular forms. If a question asks for the value of a genus on a specific manifold, and the answer choices involve modular forms, check the weight and level. The Witten genus maps to weight 12c modular forms for an 8c-manifold. If the input data (Pontryagin classes) does not match the required divisibility conditions for modular forms (e.g., denominators in q-expansions), the answer might be zero or undefined. Do not force a polynomial fit if the arithmetic constraints of modular forms are violated.

Comparisons and Exceptions: MU, KO, and the Image of J

A deep understanding requires comparing the major cohomology theories: MU (Complex Cobordism), KU (K-theory), and KO (Real K-theory). MU is complex-oriented with a universal formal group law. KU has a multiplicative formal group law over Z[1/2]. KO is real-oriented but not complex-oriented in the same way; its formal group law is defined over Z/2Z or requires careful handling of the Bott periodicity.

The Image of J is a landmark concept connecting these theories. It consists of elements in the stable homotopy groups of spheres π_k^S that come from K-theory (specifically KU). The image of J is finite cyclic at odd primes and has specific structure at p=2. A critical exception is that the Image of J vanishes rationally, but is non-trivial integrally.
