Classical Mechanics — Newton's Laws, Conservation Laws, Rotational Dynamics & Gravitation

Classical mechanics is the foundation of physics — the study of motion and forces governing everything from falling apples to planetary orbits. Newton's three laws provide the axiomatic basis: the law of inertia (every body persists in its state of rest or uniform motion unless compelled by a net external force), the second law F = dp/dt = ma relating force to the rate of change of momentum, and the law of action-reaction (forces between two bodies are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction). The conservation laws follow from Newton's laws: conservation of linear momentum (isolated systems have constant total momentum), conservation of angular momentum (central forces produce torqueless motion), and conservation of mechanical energy (for conservative forces). Rotational dynamics extends these ideas to rigid bodies: torque τ = r × F, moment of inertia I = ∫ r² dm, Euler's equations, and gyroscopic precession. Gravitation, described by Newton's inverse-square law F = −GMm/r² r̂, leads to Kepler's three laws of planetary motion and the rich theory of orbital mechanics — elliptical orbits, the vis-viva equation, Hohmann transfers, and Lagrange points. The Lagrangian formulation L = T − V with the Euler-Lagrange equations and the Hamiltonian formulation H = Σ p_i q̇_i − L with Hamilton's canonical equations provide the deepest and most general framework, connecting to symmetries via Noether's theorem and forming the bridge to quantum mechanics.

Part 1

⚖️ Newton's Laws and Conservation Laws

Foundations
Newton's Three Laws, Momentum, Energy, and the Work-Energy Theorem
Newton's first law (the law of inertia) states that a body remains at rest or in uniform rectilinear motion unless acted upon by a net external force — this defines inertial reference frames. The second law F = dp/dt (where p = mv is the linear momentum) reduces to F = ma when mass is constant; the net external force equals the rate of change of momentum. The third law — action and reaction are equal and opposite — ensures internal forces cancel pairwise in a system, yielding conservation of total momentum. Conservation of linear momentum: for an isolated system, Σ m_i v_i = constant. Conservation of angular momentum: for a central force (F ∝ r̂), the torque τ = r × F = 0, so L = r × p is constant — this explains Kepler's equal-area law. Conservation of mechanical energy: for conservative forces (F = −∇V), the work done by the force is path-independent; the work-energy theorem W = ΔK = ∫ F·dr gives E = K + V = constant. The impulse-momentum theorem: J = ∫ F dt = Δp. The center of mass R = (Σ m_i r_i)/(Σ m_i) moves as if all mass were concentrated there with the total external force acting on it: M d²R/dt² = F_ext. Collisions: elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy; inelastic collisions conserve only momentum.
Newton's laws together with the work-energy and impulse-momentum theorems give a complete framework for analyzing the translational motion of particles and systems.
Part 2

🌀 Rotational Dynamics

Rigid Body Motion
Torque, Moment of Inertia, Angular Momentum, and Precession
For a rigid body, the rotational analog of Newton's second law: τ = dL/dt = Iα, where τ = r × F is the torque about a point, L = Iω is the angular momentum, and I is the moment of inertia. For a discrete system I = Σ m_i r_i² (where r_i is the perpendicular distance from the axis); for a continuous body I = ∫ r² dm. The parallel axis theorem: I = I_cm + Md², where d is the distance between the axis through the center of mass and the parallel axis of interest. The perpendicular axis theorem (for planar bodies): I_z = I_x + I_y. Rotational kinetic energy: K_rot = ½ Iω². For a rolling object (without slipping), v = ωR and total K = ½ Mv² + ½ Iω². Euler's equations for rigid body dynamics in the body frame: I₁ω̇₁ − (I₂ − I₃)ω₂ω₃ = τ₁, and cyclically. Torque-free precession (the tennis racket theorem, intermediate axis theorem) shows that rotation about the intermediate principal axis is unstable. Gyroscopic precession: when a torque is applied perpendicular to the axis of a spinning gyroscope, it precesses with angular velocity Ω = Mgh/Iω. Physical pendulum: τ = −MgL sin θ, T = 2π√(I/MgL). The compound pendulum has a center of oscillation (the equivalent length of a simple pendulum).
Rotational dynamics mirrors translational dynamics through the torque-moment of inertia-angular momentum triad; rigid body motion reveals counterintuitive phenomena like gyroscopic precession and the intermediate axis instability.
Part 3

🌌 Gravitation and Orbital Mechanics

Universal Gravity
Newton's Law of Gravitation, Kepler's Laws, Orbital Dynamics, and the N-Body Problem
Newton's law of universal gravitation: F = −GMm/r² r̂, where G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². The gravitational potential energy: U = −GMm/r. Gauss's law for gravity: ∮ g·dA = −4πGM_enc, where g = −GM/r² r̂ is the gravitational field. Kepler's laws: (1) planets orbit in ellipses with the Sun at one focus; (2) the radius vector sweeps equal areas in equal times (equivalent to conservation of angular momentum); (3) T² ∝ a³, where a is the semi-major axis: T² = (4π²/GM)a³. The vis-viva equation: v² = GM(2/r − 1/a). Orbital energy: E = −GMm/2a (for bound orbits), E = 0 (parabolic escape), E > 0 (hyperbolic). Escape velocity: v_esc = √(2GM/R). Hohmann transfer orbit: the most fuel-efficient way to transfer between two circular orbits using two engine burns. Lagrange points L₁–L₅: the five equilibrium points in the circular restricted three-body problem, where gravitational and centrifugal forces balance. L₁, L₂, L₃ are collinear and unstable; L₄, L₅ are triangular and stable (for mass ratio < 0.04). The N-body problem has no general closed-form solution; the three-body problem exhibits chaos (the Lagrange instability). Perturbation theory (the method of variation of parameters) handles deviations from Keplerian motion due to other bodies, tidal forces, and relativistic corrections (the precession of Mercury).
Newton's law of gravitation combined with Kepler's laws provides a complete description of two-body orbital mechanics; the three-body problem reveals the inherent complexity and chaos in gravitational dynamics.

⚛️ Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Mechanics

Analytical Dynamics
The Principle of Least Action, Euler-Lagrange Equations, Noether's Theorem, Hamilton's Equations
The Lagrangian L = T − V (kinetic minus potential energy). Hamilton's principle of least action: the true path of a system between two configurations extremizes the action S = ∫ L dt. The Euler-Lagrange equations: d/dt(∂L/∂q̇_i) = ∂L/∂q_i, where q_i are generalized coordinates. These equations are invariant under coordinate transformations — they give the correct equations of motion in any coordinate system. The generalized momentum: p_i = ∂L/∂q̇_i. If L does not depend explicitly on q_i (a cyclic coordinate), then p_i is conserved. Noether's theorem: every continuous symmetry of the action corresponds to a conserved quantity — time translation invariance → energy conservation; spatial translation invariance → momentum conservation; rotational invariance → angular momentum conservation. The Hamiltonian: H = Σ p_i q̇_i − L = T + V (for natural systems). Hamilton's equations: q̇_i = ∂H/∂p_i, ṗ_i = −∂H/∂q_i — a system of 2n first-order ODEs replacing n second-order Euler-Lagrange equations. The Hamiltonian is constant if H has no explicit time dependence. The phase space is the 2n-dimensional space of (q_i, p_i); Hamilton's equations generate a symplectic flow preserving the Poisson bracket: {f,g} = Σ_i (∂f/∂q_i ∂g/∂p_i − ∂f/∂p_i ∂g/∂q_i). The Liouville theorem: phase space volume is conserved under Hamiltonian flow. The Hamilton-Jacobi equation: H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) + ∂S/∂t = 0, a powerful method for finding conserved quantities and integrable systems. The KAM theorem (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) describes the persistence of quasiperiodic orbits under small perturbations in integrable Hamiltonian systems.
The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations provide the deepest understanding of classical mechanics, revealing the fundamental role of symmetries and conservation laws and providing the structural foundation for quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics.

Central Force Motion and Bertrand's Theorem

Central forces depend only on the distance r from a fixed centre and act along the radial direction. The motion of a particle under a central force is confined to a plane because the angular momentum vector L = r × p is conserved. Using polar coordinates (r, θ) the Lagrangian is L = ½m(ṙ² + r²θ̇²) − V(r). The cyclic coordinate θ yields the conserved angular momentum ℓ = mr²θ̇. Substituting θ̇ = ℓ/(mr²) into the radial equation gives an effective one‑dimensional problem with effective potential V_eff(r) = V(r) + ℓ²/(2mr²). Bound orbits correspond to energies for which V_eff has a minimum and the radial coordinate oscillates between two turning points.

Bertrand's theorem states that among all central potentials V(r) ∝ r^k, only the inverse‑square law (k = −2) and the isotropic harmonic oscillator (k = +2) produce closed bound orbits for every set of initial conditions. The proof uses the apsidal angle Δθ = π√(d²V_eff/dr²|r₀ / (3/r₀ dV_eff/dr|r₀ + d²V_eff/dr²|r₀)) evaluated at the circular orbit radius r₀. For a power‑law potential V = αr^n the apsidal angle becomes π/√(n+2). Closed orbits require this angle to be a rational multiple of π for all energies; the only integer n giving a constant rational value are n = −2 (Δθ = π) and n = +2 (Δθ = π/2). Any other central force yields precessing ellipses or rosette trajectories.

Applications include planetary motion (inverse‑square), the motion of a charged particle in a uniform magnetic field (effective harmonic oscillator), and the design of particle traps. The theorem also explains why small perturbations of the Newtonian potential, such as those from general relativity, cause perihelion precession: the effective potential acquires a 1/r³ term, shifting the apsidal angle away from π.

Small Oscillations and Normal Modes

When a system with n degrees of freedom is displaced slightly from a stable equilibrium, the potential energy can be expanded to quadratic order: V ≈ V₀ + ½ Σi,j Vij η_i η_j, where η_i are the generalized displacements and Vij = ∂²V/∂q_i∂q_j evaluated at equilibrium. The kinetic energy is similarly quadratic: T = ½ Σi,j Tij η̇_i η̇_j with constant mass matrix Tij. The Lagrangian L = T − V leads to the linear equations of motion T η̈ + V η = 0. Seeking solutions of the form η = a eiωt yields the generalized eigenvalue problem (V − ω² T) a = 0. The eigenvalues ω_k² are the squared normal‑mode frequencies; the eigenvectors a(k) give the mode shapes.

Because T and V are real symmetric matrices, the eigenvectors can be chosen orthogonal with respect to the mass matrix: a(k)T T a(l) = δkl. In these normal coordinates the Lagrangian decouples into n independent harmonic oscillators L = Σ_k ½( Q̇_k² − ω_k² Q_k² ). Each normal mode oscillates sinusoidally with its own frequency, and any small motion is a superposition of normal modes. The number of zero‑frequency modes equals the number of continuous symmetries (Goldstone modes). For example, a linear triatomic molecule has three translational, two rotational (if non‑linear), and 3N−6 vibrational modes.

Normal‑mode analysis is essential for molecular spectroscopy, lattice dynamics (phonons), and stability of mechanical structures. In a coupled pendulum system the symmetric and antisymmetric modes have frequencies ω_s = √(g/L) and ω_a = √(g/L + 2k/m) where k is the spring coupling. Energy exchange (beats) occurs when the system is started in a non‑normal‑mode initial condition.

Worked Example: Normal Modes of Two Coupled Oscillators
Two identical masses m are attached to fixed walls by springs of constant k and coupled to each other by a spring of constant k_c. Find the normal‑mode frequencies and describe the motion.
Let x₁, x₂ be displacements from equilibrium. The potential energy is V = ½k x₁² + ½k x₂² + ½k_c (x₁ − x₂)². The mass matrix is T = m I. The stiffness matrix is V = [[k + k_c, −k_c], [−k_c, k + k_c]]. Solving det(V − ω² T) = 0 gives (k + k_c − mω²)² − k_c² = 0 → mω² = k + k_c ± k_c. Hence ω₁² = k/m (symmetric mode, x₁ = x₂) and ω₂² = (k + 2k_c)/m (antisymmetric mode, x₁ = −x₂). In the symmetric mode the coupling spring is unstretched; in the antisymmetric mode it is stretched twice the individual displacement, raising the frequency.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for normal modes, first write the quadratic forms of T and V in matrix notation. Compute the eigenvalues of the matrix T⁻¹V (or solve the generalized eigenvalue problem). Remember that zero eigenvalues correspond to rigid‑body motions and do not represent vibrations. Always check orthogonality with respect to the mass matrix to verify your eigenvectors.

Canonical Transformations and Action‑Angle Variables

A canonical transformation (q, p) → (Q, P) preserves the form of Hamilton's equations. The necessary and sufficient condition is that the Poisson brackets satisfy {Q_i, Q_j} = 0, {P_i, P_j} = 0, {Q_i, P_j} = δij. Generating functions of four types (F₁(q, Q), F₂(q, P), F₃(p, Q), F₄(p, P)) provide systematic ways to construct such transformations. For example, a type‑2 generating function F₂(q, P) yields p_i = ∂F₂/∂q_i, Q_i = ∂F₂/∂P_i.

Action‑angle variables are a special canonical transformation for integrable systems with n independent conserved quantities in involution. The actions J_i = (1/2π) ∮ p_i dq_i are constants of motion; the conjugate angles θ_i evolve linearly: θ_i = ω_i t + β_i with frequencies ω_i = ∂H/∂J_i. The Hamiltonian becomes a function of actions only, H = H(J). This formulation makes perturbation theory transparent: a small perturbation adds a term εH₁(J, θ) and the first‑order change in actions averages to zero over the unperturbed torus, while resonant denominators ω·k = 0 signal possible large‑scale changes (the small‑denominator problem).

Action‑angle variables underlie the Kolmogorov‑Arnold‑Moser (KAM) theorem, the theory of adiabatic invariants, and the semiclassical quantization condition ∮ p dq = (n + ½)h. In celestial mechanics the Delaunay variables (L, G, H, ℓ, g, h) are action‑angle variables for the Kepler problem, enabling systematic planetary perturbation expansions.

Relativistic Corrections to Newtonian Gravitation

General relativity modifies the Newtonian potential by adding a term proportional to 1/r³ in the effective radial potential for a test particle. The Schwarzschild metric yields the orbital equation d²u/dθ² + u = GM/ℓ² + 3GM u²/c², where u = 1/r and ℓ is the specific angular momentum. The extra term 3GM u²/c² causes the periapsis to advance by Δθ = 6πGM/(a(1−e²)c²) per revolution. For Mercury this gives 43 arc‑seconds per century, matching observation.

Other post‑Newtonian effects include gravitational redshift, light deflection (Δφ = 4GM/(bc²) for impact parameter b), and the Shapiro time delay. In the parametrized post‑Newtonian (PPN) framework the metric is expanded in powers of v/c and the coefficients γ, β measure deviations from general relativity. Solar‑system experiments constrain γ = 1 ± 2.3×10⁻⁵ and β = 1 ± 10⁻⁴. Gravitational waves arise from the quadrupole formula: the power radiated by a binary is P = (32/5) G⁴ m₁² m₂² (m₁+m₂) / (c⁵ a⁵ (1−e²)⁷/²) (1 + 73/24 e² + 37/96 e⁴). The Hulse‑Taylor pulsar PSR B1913+16 confirms this prediction to 0.2 %.

For competitive examinations the key results to recall are the perihelion precession formula, the light‑deflection angle, and the order‑of‑magnitude estimate of gravitational‑wave strain h ~ (GM)/(c²R) (v/c)². These are frequently tested in both conceptual and numerical forms.

Chaos in Hamiltonian Systems and the KAM Theorem

Integrable Hamiltonian systems possess n independent constants of motion in involution, allowing transformation to action‑angle variables where motion is quasi‑periodic on invariant n‑tori. A generic perturbation destroys some tori but the KAM theorem states that for sufficiently small, smooth perturbations most non‑resonant tori survive, preserving a set of positive measure in phase space. The surviving tori are those whose frequency vectors ω satisfy a Diophantine condition |k·ω| ≥ γ/|k|^τ for all integer vectors k ≠ 0, with constants γ > 0, τ > n−1.

Resonant tori (where k·ω = 0) break up, creating chaotic layers. The Chirikov resonance‑overlap criterion gives a practical estimate: when the half‑widths of adjacent resonances overlap, large‑scale chaos ensues. The standard map (kicked rotor) pn+1 = p_n + K sin θ_n, θn+1 = θ_n + pn+1 exhibits a transition to global chaos at K ≈ 0.9716. In celestial mechanics the overlap of mean‑motion resonances explains the Kirkwood gaps in the asteroid belt and the chaotic diffusion of Pluto's orbit.

Lyapunov exponents quantify sensitivity to initial conditions; a positive maximal exponent indicates chaos. For Hamiltonian flows the sum of all Lyapunov exponents is zero (phase‑space volume conservation). Numerical tools such as Poincaré surfaces of section, frequency‑map analysis, and the Fast Lyapunov Indicator are standard for detecting chaos in exam‑type problems.

  • Confusing the conditions for Bertrand's theorem: only inverse‑square and harmonic potentials give closed orbits for all bound energies; other power laws produce precession.
  • Treating zero‑frequency normal modes as vibrational modes; they correspond to rigid translations or rotations and must be excluded from vibrational spectra.
  • Assuming any canonical transformation preserves the Hamiltonian's functional form; only transformations that leave the Poisson brackets invariant are canonical.
  • Using the Newtonian escape velocity formula in relativistic contexts without the post‑Newtonian correction factor (1 + GM/(Rc²)).
  • Believing the KAM theorem guarantees stability for all small perturbations; it applies only to non‑resonant, sufficiently smooth perturbations and a set of tori of large but not full measure.
  • Neglecting the Diophantine condition when citing KAM survival; resonant frequencies are precisely those that lead to chaotic diffusion.
  • Applying the Chirikov criterion without checking that the perturbation is periodic and the resonances are well separated in action space.

Rigid Body Dynamics: Euler Equations and Poinsot's Construction

The motion of a rigid body about a fixed point or its center of mass is governed by Euler's equations in the body frame. Let the principal moments of inertia be I₁ ≤ I₂ ≤ I₃ with corresponding angular velocity components ω₁, ω₂, ω₃. In the absence of external torque, Euler's equations read I₁ dω₁/dt = (I₂ − I₃) ω₂ ω₃, I₂ dω₂/dt = (I₃ − I₁) ω₃ ω₁, I₃ dω₃/dt = (I₁ − I₂) ω₁ ω₂. Two quadratic integrals exist: the kinetic energy T = ½ (I₁ ω₁² + I₂ ω₂² + I₃ ω₃²) and the squared angular momentum L² = I₁² ω₁² + I₂² ω₂² + I₃² ω₃². The intersection of the energy ellipsoid and the momentum sphere in ω-space defines the polhode curves. Poinsot's geometric construction visualizes the motion in space: the inertia ellipsoid x²/I₁ + y²/I₂ + z²/I₃ = 1 rolls without slipping on the invariable plane perpendicular to L, with the contact point tracing the herpolhode. Stability of torque-free rotation depends on the moment of inertia axis: rotation about the major (I₃) and minor (I₁) principal axes is stable, while rotation about the intermediate axis (I₂) is unstable — a result demonstrable by linearizing Euler's equations or by the tennis racket theorem. For a symmetric top (I₁ = I₂ ≠ I₃) under gravity, the Lagrangian L = ½ I₁ (θ̇² + φ̇² sin²θ) + ½ I₃ (ψ̇ + φ̇ cosθ)² − Mgl cosθ yields three conserved quantities: energy, L_z (space-fixed vertical component), and L₃ (body-fixed symmetry axis component). The effective potential for θ-motion is V_eff(θ) = (L_z − L₃ cosθ)²/(2I₁ sin²θ) + Mgl cosθ. Steady precession occurs at constant θ satisfying I₃ ω₃ φ̇ cosθ − I₁ φ̇² cosθ + Mgl = 0, giving two precession rates for a given spin: fast and slow precession. Nutation is the oscillation of θ about the steady precession angle. The condition for sleeping top (θ = 0) stability is ω₃² > 4I₁ Mgl/I₃². Euler angles (φ, θ, ψ) with the z-x-z convention are standard; the rotation matrix R = R_z(φ) R_x(θ) R_z(ψ) connects body and space frames. The angular velocity in body frame is ω = (φ̇ sinθ sinψ + θ̇ cosψ, φ̇ sinθ cosψ − θ̇ sinψ, φ̇ cosθ + ψ̇). For exam problems, identify conserved quantities first, then reduce to one-dimensional effective potential motion.

Non-Inertial Frames: Coriolis Effect and Foucault Pendulum

In a frame rotating with angular velocity Ω relative to an inertial frame, the equation of motion for a particle of mass m is m a' = F − 2m Ω × v' − m Ω × (Ω × r') − m dΩ/dt × r', where primes denote rotating-frame quantities. The Coriolis force −2m Ω × v' is velocity-dependent and does no work; the centrifugal force −m Ω × (Ω × r') is derivable from the potential −½ m (Ω × r')². For Earth, Ω = 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s. At latitude λ, the local vertical component is Ω_z = Ω sinλ and the horizontal northward component is Ω_y = Ω cosλ. A particle dropped from height h at latitude λ experiences an eastward deflection Δx = (2/3) Ω cosλ √(2h³/g) and a southward deflection Δy = (1/3) Ω² h² sinλ cosλ / g (negligible for typical h). The Foucault pendulum demonstrates Earth's rotation: the plane of oscillation precesses at rate Ω sinλ relative to the Earth, completing a full rotation in T = 24 h / sinλ (sidereal day). At the poles (λ = ±90°) the period is 24 h; at the equator (λ = 0°) there is no precession. The derivation uses the Coriolis force in the horizontal plane: ẍ − 2Ω sinλ ẏ + ω₀² x = 0, ÿ + 2Ω sinλ ẋ + ω₀² y = 0, where ω₀² = g/l. For Ω ≪ ω₀, the solution is a harmonic oscillator with slowly rotating major axis. The precession rate is exact for a spherical pendulum; for a planar pendulum constrained to a line, the Coriolis force is balanced by the constraint. In meteorology, the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sinλ governs geostrophic balance: f v_g = −(1/ρ) ∂p/∂x, f u_g = (1/ρ) ∂p/∂y. The Rossby number Ro = U/(fL) measures the importance of inertia relative to Coriolis; Ro ≪ 1 implies geostrophic dominance. For a projectile fired horizontally at speed v over distance R, the Coriolis deflection is of order Ω v R² / g. In the Eötvös effect, a body moving eastward at speed v experiences reduced effective gravity g_eff = g − 2Ω v cosλ − Ω² R cos²λ, while westward motion increases it. The centrifugal potential modifies the geoid shape: the equatorial bulge is approximately Ω² R³ / (2GM) ≈ 1/298 of Earth's radius.

Worked Problem: Foucault Pendulum Precession at Arbitrary Latitude
A Foucault pendulum of length l = 20 m is located at latitude λ = 45° N. Calculate (a) the precession period of the oscillation plane relative to the Earth, (b) the angular precession rate in degrees per hour, and (c) the number of oscillations N for the plane to rotate by 90° if the pendulum period is T₀ = 2π√(l/g). Use g = 9.8 m/s² and sidereal day = 23 h 56 min 4.09 s.
(a) The precession period relative to Earth is T_prec = T_sidereal / sinλ. T_sidereal = 23.9345 h. sin 45° = √2/2 ≈ 0.7071. T_prec = 23.9345 / 0.7071 ≈ 33.85 h. (b) Angular precession rate = 360° / T_prec = 360° / 33.85 h ≈ 10.63°/h. Alternatively, Ω = 2π / T_sidereal = 15.041°/h; precession rate = Ω sinλ = 15.041 × 0.7071 ≈ 10.63°/h. (c) Pendulum period T₀ = 2π√(20/9.8) ≈ 8.98 s. Number of oscillations in time t = T_prec/4 (for 90° rotation): t = 33.85 h / 4 = 8.4625 h = 30465 s. N = t / T₀ = 30465 / 8.98 ≈ 3392 oscillations. The plane rotates 90° after approximately 3392 swings.
Exam Strategy: Non-Inertial Frame Problems
Always write the full fictitious force expression before simplifying. Identify which terms are relevant: for short-time projectile motion, centrifugal is often negligible compared to Coriolis; for equilibrium problems (e.g., shape of rotating fluid), centrifugal dominates. In Foucault pendulum problems, the precession rate is Ω sinλ — do not confuse with Ω cosλ (horizontal component). For the Eötvös effect, remember the sign: eastward motion reduces apparent weight. When a problem gives latitude, immediately compute sinλ and cosλ. If the frame rotation is not constant, include the Euler force −m dΩ/dt × r'. Check dimensions: Coriolis acceleration is 2Ωv, centrifugal is Ω²r. In multiple-choice questions, the correct answer often has the factor 2 in Coriolis and the correct trigonometric function of latitude.

The Restricted Three-Body Problem and Lagrange Points

The circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP) models two massive bodies (primaries) of masses m₁ and m₂ in circular orbits about their barycenter, and a third body of negligible mass moving in their gravitational field. In a rotating frame with origin at the barycenter and angular velocity Ω = √(G(m₁+m₂)/a³) where a is the separation, the primaries are fixed at (−μa, 0) and ((1−μ)a, 0) with μ = m₂/(m₁+m₂). The equations of motion for the test particle are ẍ − 2Ω ẏ = ∂U/∂x, ÿ + 2Ω ẋ = ∂U/∂y, z̈ = ∂U/∂z, where the effective potential is U(x,y,z) = −½ Ω²(x²+y²) − Gm₁/r₁ − Gm₂/r₂, with r₁, r₂ distances to the primaries. The Jacobi integral C = 2U − (ẋ²+ẏ²+ż²) is conserved. The five Lagrange points are equilibria of U: L₁, L₂, L₃ lie on the x-axis (collinear points); L₄ and L₅ form equilateral triangles with the primaries. For L₁ between the primaries, the distance from m₁ is approximately r ≈ a (μ/3)^(1/3) for μ ≪ 1. L₂ is beyond m₂ at similar distance; L₃ is beyond m₁ at distance ≈ a (1 + 5μ/12). L₄ and L₅ are at (½−μ)a, ±√3/2 a. Linear stability analysis: the collinear points are always unstable (saddle × center × center). The triangular points are linearly stable if μ < μ_crit = ½ − √(69)/18 ≈ 0.03852. For the Sun-Jupiter system μ ≈ 0.00095, L₄/L₅ are stable and host Trojan asteroids. For Earth-Moon μ ≈ 0.01215, L₄/L₅ are stable. The linearized frequencies at L₄/L₅ are ω₁,₂ = Ω √(1 ± √(1−27μ(1−μ))). The zero-velocity curves C = 2U(x,y,0) define Hill's regions; as C decreases, forbidden regions shrink and necks open at L₁, L₂, L₃ allowing transport between realms. The CR3BP is the foundation for low-energy transfers (e.g., Hiten, Genesis, ARTEMIS missions) and the design of halo and Lissajous orbits around L₁/L₂ (e.g., SOHO, JWST). The Tisserand parameter T = a_p/a + 2√(a/a_p (1−e²)) cos i is approximately conserved during planetary encounters and is used to identify cometary orbits.

Adiabatic Invariants and Applications

An adiabatic invariant is a quantity that remains nearly constant when a system's parameters change slowly compared to its internal dynamical timescales. For a one-dimensional periodic system with Hamiltonian H(q,p;λ(t)) where λ varies slowly, the action I = ∮ p dq is an adiabatic invariant to all orders in the slowness parameter ε = (1/ω) dλ/dt. The proof uses canonical perturbation theory: the change in I over one period is ΔI = −∮ (∂H/∂λ) (∂q/∂I) dt, which averages to zero for periodic motion. For the harmonic oscillator H = p²/(2m) + ½ m ω(t)² q² with slowly varying ω, I = E/ω is invariant, so E ∝ ω. For a pendulum with slowly varying length l(t), I = (8/π) √(mgl) E(k) where E(k) is the complete elliptic integral of the second kind and k = sin(θ_max/2). In the small-angle limit, I = E/ω with ω = √(g/l), so E ∝ l^(-1/4) and amplitude θ_max ∝ l^(-3/4). For a charged particle in a slowly varying magnetic field B(t), the magnetic moment μ = (m v_⊥²)/(2B) is an adiabatic invariant (first adiabatic invariant). This underlies magnetic mirror confinement: as B increases, v_⊥ increases to keep μ constant, while v_∥ decreases by energy conservation; if v_∥ reaches zero, the particle reflects. The second adiabatic invariant is the longitudinal invariant J = ∮ p_∥ ds, conserved when the mirror length changes slowly. The third invariant is the magnetic flux Φ enclosed by the drift orbit, conserved when the field changes on timescales longer than the drift period. Adiabatic invariants explain Fermi acceleration: a particle bouncing between a fixed wall and a slowly moving wall gains energy ΔE/E = −2 ΔL/L per reflection. In cosmology, the photon occupation number n = 1/(exp(hν/kT)−1) is preserved under adiabatic expansion, giving T ∝ 1/a. The adiabatic theorem in quantum mechanics states that a system initially in an eigenstate remains in the instantaneous eigenstate if the Hamiltonian changes slowly compared to energy gaps; the quantum number n is the adiabatic invariant. Breakdown of adiabatic invariance occurs at resonances (ω₁/ω₂ = rational) or separatrix crossing, where the action changes by a finite amount ΔI ∼ √ε.

Geometric Mechanics: Symplectic Structure and Momentum Maps

Classical mechanics on a 2n-dimensional phase space M is naturally formulated on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) where ω is a closed, non-degenerate 2-form. In canonical coordinates (q¹,…,qⁿ, p₁,…,pₙ), ω = ∑ dp_i ∧ dq^i. Hamilton's equations are Ẋ = X_H where X_H is the Hamiltonian vector field defined by ω(X_H, ·) = dH. The Poisson bracket {f,g} = ω(X_f, X_g) = ∑ (∂f/∂q^i ∂g/∂p_i − ∂f/∂p_i ∂g/∂q^i) makes C^∞(M) a Lie algebra. A diffeomorphism φ: M → M is canonical (symplectomorphism) iff φ*ω = ω. Noether's theorem in this language: a one-parameter group of symplectomorphisms generated by X_f preserves H iff {f,H} = 0, so f is a constant of motion. For a Lie group G acting on M by symplectomorphisms, a momentum map J: M → g* (dual of the Lie algebra) satisfies d⟨J,ξ⟩ = ω(ξ_M, ·) for all ξ ∈ g, where ξ_M is the induced vector field. The components J_ξ = ⟨J,ξ⟩ are the Noether charges. For the cotangent bundle T*Q with canonical symplectic form, the lift of a Q-symmetry gives J_ξ = p_i ξ^i. The Marsden-Weinstein reduction theorem: if G acts freely and properly on J⁻¹(μ), the reduced space M_μ = J⁻¹(μ)/G is symplectic and inherits the dynamics. For SO(3) acting on T*R³, J = q × p is the angular momentum; reduction at μ ≠ 0 gives the coadjoint orbit S²_|μ| with the Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau form. The heavy top has phase space T*SO(3) ≅ SO(3) × so(3)*; the momentum map for the left SO(3) action (body rotations) is the body angular momentum Π, and for the right SO(3) action (spatial rotations) is the spatial angular momentum L = RΠ. The Euler-Poincaré equations on so(3)* are dΠ/dt = Π × Ω + Mg Γ × χ, where Γ is the unit vector in the direction of gravity and χ is the center of mass vector in body frame. The energy-Casimir method for stability: a relative equilibrium is nonlinearly stable if the second variation of H_ξ = H − ⟨J,ξ⟩ is definite on the symplectic normal space. For the rigid body, the Casimirs are |Π|² and |L|². Geometric mechanics provides the framework for numerical integrators that preserve symplectic structure (symplectic Runge-Kutta, Verlet), ensuring long-time energy stability and correct qualitative behavior.

Hamilton‑Jacobi Theory and Separation of Variables

The Hamilton‑Jacobi equation provides a bridge between Hamiltonian mechanics and the calculus of variations. For a system with Hamiltonian H(q, p, t) the generating function S(q, α, t) satisfies ∂S/∂t + H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) = 0, where the constants αi are the new momenta. A complete integral containing n independent constants yields the solution of the equations of motion by the relations pi = ∂S/∂qi and βi = ∂S/∂αi, where βi are the new coordinates (constants of integration). When the Hamiltonian is time‑independent one writes S = W(q, α) − Et, reducing the problem to the time‑independent Hamilton‑Jacobi equation H(q, ∂W/∂q) = E. Separation of variables is possible whenever the Hamiltonian can be written as a sum of terms each depending on a single coordinate and its conjugate momentum, or when a coordinate is cyclic. In spherical coordinates the Kepler problem separates because the Hamiltonian depends on r, θ, φ only through the combination pr²/2m + L²/2mr² − k/r, where L² is the squared angular momentum. The separated equations give the radial action integral Jr = ∮ pr dr and the angular actions Jθ, Jφ = L, Lz. The Hamilton‑Jacobi method also underlies the semiclassical quantisation condition ∮ p dq = (n + γ)h, where γ is the Maslov index. In perturbation theory the Hamilton‑Jacobi equation is expanded in a small parameter, leading to the method of averaging and the construction of adiabatic invariants. A common examiner twist is to ask for the explicit form of the generating function for the harmonic oscillator, S = (mω/2) q² cot(ωt) + (E/ω) arctan( (mω q)/p ), and to verify that it reproduces the known sinusoidal motion.

Liouville's Theorem, Phase Space Volume, and Statistical Ensembles

Liouville's theorem states that the phase‑space density ρ(q, p, t) is constant along Hamiltonian trajectories: dρ/dt = ∂ρ/∂t + {ρ, H} = 0. The proof follows from the incompressibility of the Hamiltonian flow, ∇·Ẋ = ∑ (∂ẋi/∂xi) = 0, where x = (q, p). Consequently, any volume element in phase space evolves preserving its volume, although its shape may become highly distorted. This property is the foundation of classical statistical mechanics: the microcanonical ensemble corresponds to a uniform density on the energy shell H = E, the canonical ensemble to ρ ∝ exp(−βH), and the grand canonical ensemble to ρ ∝ exp[−β(H − μN)]. The theorem also implies that fine‑grained entropy S = −k∫ ρ ln ρ dΓ is constant, while coarse‑grained entropy can increase because of filamentation of the density. In numerical simulations, symplectic integrators respect Liouville's theorem exactly, whereas non‑symplectic schemes introduce artificial phase‑space compression or expansion, leading to secular energy drift. A typical exam question asks to show that for a one‑dimensional harmonic oscillator the phase‑space trajectories are ellipses of constant area πE/ω, and that the area enclosed by an energy contour equals the action variable J = E/ω. Another twist is to ask why Liouville's theorem fails for dissipative systems: the phase‑space flow then has non‑zero divergence, ∇·Ẋ = −γ, causing volumes to shrink exponentially as exp(−γt).

Worked Example: Phase‑Space Area of a Harmonic Oscillator
Show that the area enclosed by the trajectory of a one‑dimensional harmonic oscillator of mass m and angular frequency ω in the (q, p) plane equals 2πJ, where J = E/ω is the action variable.
The Hamiltonian is H = p²/(2m) + ½ mω² q² = E. Rearranging gives p²/(2mE) + q²/(2E/(mω²)) = 1, an ellipse with semi‑axes a = √(2E/(mω²)) in q and b = √(2mE) in p. The area of an ellipse is πab = π √(2E/(mω²)) √(2mE) = 2πE/ω = 2πJ. Since J = ∮ p dq / 2π, the result confirms the general relation between phase‑space area and action.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for a phase‑space volume or area, first identify the conserved quantities that define the invariant manifold (energy, angular momentum). Write the Hamiltonian in terms of those constants, solve for the boundary curve, and compute the geometric area or volume. Remember that Liouville's theorem guarantees the invariance of the total volume, not of the shape; use this to check whether a proposed numerical scheme is symplectic.

Poisson Bracket Algebra, Jacobi Identity, and Canonical Invariants

The Poisson bracket {f, g} = ∑ (∂f/∂qi ∂g/∂pi − ∂f/∂pi ∂g/∂qi) endows the space of smooth phase‑space functions with a Lie algebra structure. It is bilinear, antisymmetric, satisfies the Leibniz rule {f, gh} = {f, g}h + g{f, h}, and obeys the Jacobi identity {f, {g, h}} + {g, {h, f}} + {h, {f, g}} = 0. The Jacobi identity is equivalent to the closure of Hamiltonian vector fields under the Lie bracket and guarantees that the time evolution generated by any Hamiltonian is a derivation of the Poisson algebra. Canonical transformations are precisely those diffeomorphisms that preserve the fundamental brackets {qi, qj} = 0, {pi, pj} = 0, {qi, pj} = δij. Noether's theorem in Hamiltonian form states that if a function G generates a one‑parameter group of canonical transformations and {G, H} = 0, then G is a constant of motion. The algebra of the rotation group SO(3) is realised by the components of angular momentum Li = εijk qj pk, which satisfy {Li, Lj} = εijk Lk. The Casimir invariant L² = L1² + L2² + L3² Poisson‑commutes with all Li and with any rotationally invariant Hamiltonian. In the Kepler problem the Runge‑Lenz vector A = p × L − mk r̂ provides three additional constants; together with L they close an so(4) algebra for bound orbits, {Ai, Aj} = −2mH εijk Lk. Examiners often test the Jacobi identity by asking to verify it for a specific set of functions, or to prove that the Poisson bracket of two constants of motion is again a constant of motion (the Poisson theorem). A subtle trap is to forget that the Jacobi identity holds only for the canonical Poisson bracket; a non‑canonical bracket (e.g. the Lie‑Poisson bracket on the dual of a Lie algebra) satisfies a modified Jacobi identity involving structure constants.

Symplectic Integrators: Structure‑Preserving Algorithms for Long‑Time Simulations

Standard numerical schemes such as Runge‑Kutta 4 do not preserve the symplectic two‑form ω = ∑ dpi ∧ dqi, leading to a systematic drift in the Hamiltonian even for integrable systems. Symplectic integrators are constructed by splitting the Hamiltonian into exactly solvable parts, e.g. H = T(p) + V(q). The simplest first‑order method is the symplectic Euler scheme: pn+1 = pn − Δt ∂V/∂q (qn), qn+1 = qn + Δt ∂T/∂p (pn+1). The second‑order Verlet (or leapfrog) algorithm alternates half‑step momentum updates with full‑step position updates: pn+½ = pn − (Δt/2) ∂V/∂q (qn), qn+1 = qn + Δt ∂T/∂p (pn+½), pn+1 = pn+½ − (Δt/2) ∂V/∂q (qn+1). This scheme is time‑reversible, symplectic, and exactly conserves a modified Hamiltonian H̃ = H + O(Δt²) for exponentially long times (Nekhoroshev estimates). Higher‑order symplectic methods are obtained by composition, e.g. the fourth‑order Forest‑Ruth or Yoshida coefficients. In molecular dynamics the velocity‑Verlet form is preferred because it yields velocities at the same time as positions, facilitating temperature control. A common exam question asks to derive the modified Hamiltonian for the harmonic oscillator under the Verlet scheme and to show that the numerical energy oscillates around the true value with amplitude proportional to Δt². Another twist is to compare the long‑term behaviour of a symplectic integrator with a non‑symplectic one for a chaotic system such as the Hénon‑Heiles potential, noting that the symplectic method preserves the correct invariant measure on the energy shell while the non‑symplectic method artificially damps or amplifies chaos.

Elastic Waves in Continuous Media: From Lattice Dynamics to Continuum Mechanics

The transition from a discrete chain of masses and springs to a continuous elastic rod illustrates how classical field theory emerges from particle mechanics. For a one‑dimensional monatomic lattice with spacing a, mass m, and spring constant κ, the equations of motion are m ün = κ (un+1 − 2un + un−1). Assuming a plane wave un = u₀ exp[i(kna − ωt)] yields the dispersion relation ω² = (4κ/m) sin²(ka/2). In the long‑wavelength limit ka ≪ 1, sin(ka/2) ≈ ka/2, giving ω = a√(κ/m) k, i.e. a linear acoustic branch with sound speed c = a√(κ/m). The continuum limit replaces the discrete displacement un(t) by a field u(x, t) and the difference operator by the second spatial derivative, leading to the wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c² ∂²u/∂x². In three dimensions the elastic displacement vector u(r, t) obeys the Navier‑Cauchy equation ρ ∂²u/∂t² = (λ + μ) ∇(∇·u) + μ ∇²u, where λ and μ are Lamé parameters. This equation separates into longitudinal waves with speed cL = √((λ + 2μ)/ρ) and transverse waves with speed cT = √(μ/ρ). The energy density is ½ ρ (∂u/∂t)² + ½ λ (∇·u)² + μ (∂iuj)², and the momentum flux (stress tensor) is σij = λ δij ∇·u + μ (∂iuj + ∂jui). Examiners often ask to derive the dispersion relation for a diatomic lattice, showing the appearance of an optical branch separated by a gap at the Brillouin‑zone boundary, or to compute the reflection and transmission coefficients at an interface between two media with different impedances Z = ρc. A subtle point is that the continuum wave equation is only valid for wavelengths much larger than the lattice spacing; for shorter wavelengths the discrete dispersion deviates strongly, leading to phenomena such as phonon band gaps and negative group velocity.

  • Applying the continuum wave equation to wavelengths comparable to the lattice spacing, ignoring the full phonon dispersion relation.

  • Misidentifying the sign of the Coriolis term in the equations of motion for a rotating frame; the Coriolis acceleration is −2Ω × v in the rotating frame.

Nonholonomic Constraints and the Lagrange‑d'Alembert Principle

Nonholonomic constraints are relations among coordinates and velocities that cannot be integrated to give constraints solely among coordinates. A classic example is a rolling disk or a knife‑edge constraint where the velocity of the contact point is zero in the direction normal to the edge. The Lagrange‑d'Alembert principle states that the virtual work of the constraint forces vanishes for virtual displacements consistent with the constraints at a fixed time, i.e. δW = Σ Qᵢ δqᵢ = 0 where the virtual displacements δqᵢ satisfy the linearized constraint equations Σ aₖᵢ(q, t) δqᵢ = 0. This leads to the equations of motion d/dt (∂L/∂q̇ᵢ) − ∂L/∂qᵢ = Σ λₖ aₖᵢ with the multipliers λₖ determined by imposing the constraint equations Σ aₖᵢ q̇ᵢ + aₖₜ = 0. Unlike holonomic systems, the multipliers cannot be eliminated by reducing the configuration space; they remain as unknown forces that do no work in the virtual displacement sense but do work in the actual motion. A frequent exam problem asks to derive the equations for a vertical rolling coin (Euler's disk) using the nonholonomic constraint ẋ sin θ − ẏ cos θ = 0 and to show that the energy is conserved while the momentum conjugate to the cyclic coordinate is not. Another twist is to compare the Lagrange‑d'Alembert formulation with the vakonomic (variational) approach, which extremizes the action subject to the constraints and generally yields different equations; the correct physical equations for mechanical nonholonomic systems are those of Lagrange‑d'Alembert.

Hamiltonian Perturbation Theory: Averaging and Lie Transforms

When a Hamiltonian splits as H = H₀(J) + ε H₁(θ, J) with action‑angle variables (θ, J) for the integrable part H₀, perturbation theory seeks a near‑identity canonical transformation to new variables (Θ, 𝒥) that eliminates the angle dependence order by order. The method of averaging averages the perturbation over the fast angles: ⟨H₁⟩(J) = (2π)⁻ⁿ ∫ H₁(θ, J) dⁿθ. The first‑order transformed Hamiltonian is K = H₀(𝒥) + ε ⟨H₁⟩(𝒥) + O(ε²). Lie transform theory provides a systematic algorithm: one chooses a generating function W(θ, 𝒥) such that the Lie derivative L_W H₀ = {W, H₀} cancels the oscillatory part of H₁. The homological equation {W, H₀} + H₁ − ⟨H₁⟩ = 0 is solved by expanding in Fourier series; the small‑denominator problem appears when frequencies ω = ∂H₀/∂J satisfy resonance conditions k·ω = 0. At resonances the averaging method breaks down and one must retain the resonant terms, leading to a pendulum‑like Hamiltonian that describes libration. Examiners often ask to compute the first‑order averaged Hamiltonian for the perturbed Kepler problem with a small radial perturbation ε/r², or to derive the width of a primary resonance using the pendulum approximation. A subtle point is that the Lie transform is symplectic by construction, guaranteeing preservation of the phase‑space volume, whereas naive truncation of the series can destroy symplecticity.

Worked Example: First‑Order Averaging for a Perturbed Harmonic Oscillator
Consider the Hamiltonian H = ½(p² + q²) + ε q³. Introduce action‑angle variables for the unperturbed oscillator: q = √(2J) sin θ, p = √(2J) cos θ. Compute the first‑order averaged Hamiltonian K(J) and deduce the frequency shift to O(ε).
Express the perturbation in action‑angle variables: q³ = (2J)³ᐟ² sin³ θ = (2J)³ᐟ² (3 sin θ − sin 3θ)/4. The angle average of sin θ and sin 3θ over 0 to 2π is zero, so ⟨q³⟩ = 0. Hence the first‑order averaged Hamiltonian is K(J) = J + O(ε²). The frequency ω = ∂K/∂J = 1 + O(ε²); there is no first‑order frequency shift because the cubic perturbation has zero average. The leading shift appears at second order and can be obtained from the second‑order Lie transform or by computing the secular term in the perturbation series.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a perturbation is an odd function of the angle, its average vanishes; immediately state that the first‑order frequency shift is zero and that the leading correction is second order. This saves time and shows mastery of parity arguments.

Integrable Systems: Lax Pairs and the Inverse Scattering Transform

An integrable Hamiltonian system possesses a Lax pair (L, M) of matrices or operators such that the equations of motion are equivalent to the Lax equation dL/dt = [M, L]. The eigenvalues of L are constants of motion because the Lax equation implies isospectral evolution. For the Korteweg‑de Vries (KdV) equation uₜ − 6u uₓ + uₓₓₓ = 0, the Lax pair is the Schrödinger operator L = −∂ₓ² + u(x, t) and M = −4∂ₓ³ + 6u∂ₓ + 3uₓ. The inverse scattering transform (IST) solves the initial‑value problem by treating u(x, 0) as a potential in the scattering problem for L, evolving the scattering data trivially in time (reflection coefficient r(k, t) = r(k, 0) e8ik³t), and reconstructing u(x, t) via the Gel'fand‑Levitan‑Marchenko integral equation. The IST linearizes the nonlinear evolution, yielding multi‑soliton solutions. For finite‑dimensional integrable systems such as the Toda lattice, the Lax matrix is tridiagonal and the spectral curve det(L − λI) = 0 defines an algebraic curve whose Jacobian provides action‑angle variables. Examiners may ask to verify the Lax equation for the Toda lattice, to compute the conserved quantities from the trace invariants Tr Lᵏ, or to explain why the existence of a Lax pair implies Liouville integrability. A common pitfall is confusing the Lax representation with the zero‑curvature representation; they are equivalent for 1+1 dimensional field theories but the zero‑curvature form ∂ₜ U − ∂ₓ V + [U, V] = 0 is more natural for gauge‑theoretic interpretations.

Continuum Field Theory: Lagrangian Density, Noether's Theorem, and Energy‑Momentum Tensor

Classical field theory replaces the discrete Lagrangian L(qᵢ, q̇ᵢ, t) by a Lagrangian density ℒ(φᵃ, ∂ₘφᵃ, xᵐ) where φᵃ(x) are fields and xᵐ = (t, x) are spacetime coordinates. The action is S = ∫ ℒ d⁴x. Hamilton's principle δS = 0 yields the Euler‑Lagrange field equations ∂ₘ(∂ℒ/∂(∂ₘφᵃ)) − ∂ℒ/∂φᵃ = 0. Noether's theorem connects continuous symmetries to conserved currents: if the action is invariant under an infinitesimal transformation δφᵃ = ε Xᵃ(φ, ∂φ) and δxᵐ = ε ξᵐ, then the current Jᵐ = (∂ℒ/∂(∂ₘφᵃ)) Xᵃ − ℒ ξᵐ satisfies ∂ₘJᵐ = 0 on‑shell. For spacetime translations ξᵐ = δᵐ_ν, Xᵃ = −∂ᵛφᵃ, the conserved current is the canonical energy‑momentum tensor Tᵐ_ν = (∂ℒ/∂(∂ₘφᵃ)) ∂ᵛφᵃ − δᵐ_ν ℒ. This tensor is not necessarily symmetric; the Belinfante‑Rosenfeld procedure adds a divergence‑free term to obtain a symmetric tensor that couples to gravity. For a scalar field with ℒ = ½ ∂ₘφ ∂ᵐφ − V(φ), one finds Tᵐ_ν = ∂ᵐφ ∂ᵛφ − δᵐ_ν ℒ. Examiners often ask to derive the energy‑momentum tensor for the electromagnetic field ℒ = −¼ FₘₙFᵐⁿ and to show that its trace vanishes for the massless case, reflecting conformal invariance. Another standard problem is to compute the Noether charge associated with a global U(1) symmetry φ → eiαφ for a complex scalar field, yielding the conserved particle number current jᵐ = i(φ* ∂ᵐφ − φ ∂ᵐφ*).

Relativistic Hamiltonian Mechanics: Covariant Phase Space and Dirac Brackets

In relativistic mechanics the configuration space is a worldline in Minkowski space, and the action is reparameterization invariant: S = −m ∫ √(−ẋᵐẋₘ) dτ. The canonical momenta pₘ = ∂L/∂ẋᵐ satisfy the primary constraint φ = pᵐpₘ + m² = 0. The Hamiltonian is a linear combination of constraints H = λ φ, reflecting the fact that time evolution is a gauge transformation. Dirac's algorithm for constrained Hamiltonian systems classifies constraints into first‑class (generate gauge symmetries) and second‑class (reduce phase‑space dimension). For the relativistic particle, φ is first‑class; gauge fixing (e.g. τ = x⁰) converts it into a second‑class pair with the gauge condition, allowing the definition of Dirac brackets {F, G}* = {F, G} − {F, χₐ} Cab {χ_b, G} where χₐ are the second‑class constraints and Cab is the inverse of their Poisson bracket matrix. The resulting Dirac brackets for the physical variables (xⁱ, pᵢ) reproduce the standard Poisson brackets, while the unphysical components are eliminated. For the relativistic string, the constraints are the Virasoro operators Lₙ, and the Dirac bracket algebra yields the classical Virasoro algebra with central charge zero. Examiners may ask to perform the Dirac‑Bergmann analysis for a massive vector field (Proca action) where the primary constraint π⁰ = 0 leads to a secondary constraint ∂ᵢπⁱ + m²A⁰ = 0, both second‑class, and to compute the Dirac bracket for the transverse components. A subtle point is that the covariant phase space approach, which defines the symplectic form directly on the space of solutions, avoids gauge fixing and yields the same physical brackets.

  • Assuming that averaging over angles always removes the perturbation; resonant denominators can make the averaged Hamiltonian singular, requiring a separate resonant normal form.
  • Confusing the Lax pair of a finite‑dimensional system with the zero‑curvature representation of a field theory; they are related but not identical in structure.
  • Using the canonical energy‑momentum tensor without symmetrizing it when coupling to gravity; the Einstein equations require a symmetric tensor.
  • Applying Dirac's constraint algorithm without checking the consistency conditions; missing secondary constraints leads to an incorrect count of physical degrees of freedom.
  • Neglecting the gauge‑fixing step when quantizing a reparameterization‑invariant system; the path integral overcounts gauge orbits unless a Faddeev‑Popov determinant is included.
  • Assuming that the inverse scattering transform works for any nonlinear PDE; it requires the existence of a Lax pair and is limited to integrable equations.

Variational Principles Beyond Hamilton: Maupertuis, Jacobi, and the Principle of Least Action

The Hamiltonian principle δ∫L dt = 0 is the most familiar variational formulation, but several equivalent principles predate it and remain useful for specific problems. Maupertuis' principle states that for a conservative system with fixed total energy E, the actual path in configuration space makes the abbreviated action ∫ p·dq stationary, where p = ∂L/∂q̇. This principle eliminates time as an independent variable and is particularly convenient for problems with a known energy integral, such as central‑force orbits. Jacobi's principle reformulates Maupertuis' principle as a geodesic problem: the motion of a particle of energy E in a potential V(q) is equivalent to a free particle moving on a Riemannian manifold with metric gᵢⱼ = 2(E−V)δᵢⱼ. The geodesic equations on this Jacobi metric reproduce the original equations of motion. Both principles require the energy to be constant; they cannot be applied directly to non‑conservative or explicitly time‑dependent systems. In celestial mechanics, Maupertuis' principle yields the orbit equation without solving for the time parametrisation, while Jacobi's metric provides a geometric interpretation of the Kepler problem as motion on a sphere of constant curvature. Examiners often ask to derive the Maupertuis action from the Hamiltonian action by a Legendre transformation with respect to time, or to show that the Jacobi metric for the Newtonian potential V = −k/r is conformally flat. A common trap is to treat the abbreviated action as a functional of the path alone, forgetting that the endpoints in configuration space must be fixed while the time interval is free.

Nonlinear Normal Modes, Bifurcations, and the Duffing Oscillator

Linear normal‑mode analysis assumes small amplitudes and a quadratic potential, leading to independent harmonic oscillators. When the potential contains higher‑order terms, the concept of a normal mode generalises to nonlinear normal modes (NNMs): periodic solutions in which all degrees of freedom oscillate with a single frequency and a fixed phase relationship. For a system with one degree of freedom, the Duffing oscillator ẍ + ω₀²x + εx³ = 0 (ε > 0 for hardening, ε < 0 for softening) exemplifies NNMs. Its exact periodic solutions can be expressed in terms of Jacobi elliptic functions; the frequency‑amplitude relation is ω(A) = ω₀ √(1 + 3εA²/4ω₀²) for the hardening case. As the amplitude grows, the frequency shifts, and the linear normal‑mode picture breaks down. In multi‑degree‑of‑freedom systems, NNMs can bifurcate from linear modes when a parameter (e.g., energy or a stiffness coefficient) crosses a critical value. The Lyapunov‑Schmidt reduction or the method of multiple scales yields bifurcation equations whose solutions predict the emergence of new periodic branches, such as out‑of‑phase modes in coupled Duffing oscillators. Stability of NNMs is assessed via Floquet multipliers of the monodromy matrix; a multiplier crossing the unit circle signals a period‑doubling or Neimark‑Sacker bifurcation. Examiners frequently ask to compute the first‑order frequency correction for the Duffing oscillator using the Lindstedt‑Poincaré method, or to sketch the backbone curve ω(A) and identify the turning point where the response becomes multivalued. A subtle error is to assume that the linear modal matrix diagonalises the nonlinear terms; in general, nonlinear coupling generates internal resonances (e.g., 1:2, 1:3) that cannot be captured by a linear transformation.

Worked Example: Frequency‑Amplitude Relation for the Hardening Duffing Oscillator
Derive the first‑order approximation ω(A) for the oscillator ẍ + ω₀²x + εx³ = 0 with ε > 0 using the Lindstedt‑Poincaré method.
Introduce a scaled time τ = ωt with ω = ω₀ + εω₁ + … and expand x(τ) = x₀(τ) + εx₁(τ) + … . At O(1): x₀'' + x₀ = 0 → x₀ = A cos τ. At O(ε): x₁'' + x₁ = −2ω₁ x₀'' − x₀³. Substitute x₀ and use cos³τ = (3/4)cos τ + (1/4)cos 3τ. The secular term proportional to cos τ must vanish: 2ω₁ A − (3/4)A³ = 0 → ω₁ = 3A²/8. Hence ω = ω₀ + ε(3A²/8) = ω₀ √(1 + 3εA²/4ω₀²) to first order in ε, matching the exact elliptic‑function result expanded for small εA²/ω₀².

Write the Lindstedt‑Poincaré ansatz, collect secular terms, and solve for the frequency correction. Remember that the method fails at internal resonances; if the problem mentions a 1:2 resonance, switch to the method of multiple scales or normal‑form theory.

Hamiltonian Chaos: Melnikov Method and Homoclinic Tangles

Integrable Hamiltonian systems possess invariant tori on which motion is quasi‑periodic. A small non‑integrable perturbation can destroy these tori, creating chaotic layers. The Melnikov method provides a quantitative criterion for the transverse intersection of stable and unstable manifolds of a hyperbolic fixed point, which is a hallmark of chaos. Consider a planar Hamiltonian H₀(p,q) with a saddle point at the origin and a homoclinic orbit γ₀(t) connecting the saddle to itself. Add a time‑periodic perturbation εH₁(p,q,t) with period T. The Melnikov function M(t₀) = ∫₋∞^∞ {H₀, H₁}(γ₀(t), t+t₀) dt measures the first‑order splitting of the manifolds. If M(t₀) has simple zeros, the manifolds intersect transversely, implying a Smale horseshoe and chaotic dynamics. For the forced Duffing oscillator H = ½p² − ½q² + ¼q⁴ + εq cos ωt, the unperturbed homoclinic orbit is q₀(t) = √2 sech t, p₀(t) = −√2 sech t tanh t. The Melnikov integral evaluates to M(t₀) = −(πω/ cosh(πω/2)) sin ωt₀, which has simple zeros for any ω > 0, proving chaos for arbitrarily small ε. The method extends to multi‑degree‑of‑freedom systems by considering normally hyperbolic invariant manifolds. Examiners often ask to set up the Melnikov integral for a given perturbed Hamiltonian, to evaluate it using residue calculus, or to interpret the physical meaning of simple zeros. A frequent mistake is to apply the method to systems without a known homoclinic orbit or to ignore the requirement that the perturbation be Hamiltonian (non‑Hamiltonian damping requires a modified Melnikov function).

Secular Perturbation Theory in Celestial Mechanics: Laplace‑Lagrange and Resonant Dynamics

Planetary orbits are well described by Keplerian ellipses perturbed by mutual gravitational interactions. Secular perturbation theory averages the disturbing function over the fast mean anomalies, yielding a Hamiltonian that depends only on the slow variables: eccentricities, inclinations, and the longitudes of perihelion and node. The Laplace‑Lagrange linear theory expands the disturbing function to second order in eccentricities and inclinations, producing a set of coupled linear differential equations for the complex variables zⱼ = eⱼ exp(iϖⱼ) and ζⱼ = iⱼ exp(iΩⱼ). The eigenvalues of the resulting matrix give the secular frequencies gₖ (apsidal precession) and sₖ (nodal precession). For the Solar System, the dominant eigenmodes correspond to the giant planets; the terrestrial planets' secular frequencies are strongly influenced by Jupiter and Saturn. When a linear combination of secular frequencies approaches zero, a secular resonance occurs, leading to large‑amplitude oscillations in eccentricity or inclination. The ν₆ resonance (g − g₆ ≈ 0) with Saturn's apsidal precession is responsible for clearing the inner edge of the asteroid belt. Higher‑order secular theory includes terms up to fourth order in e and i, capturing the Great Inequality between Jupiter and Saturn (period ≈ 900 yr) and the long‑term chaotic diffusion of Mercury's eccentricity. Examiners may ask to write the linearised secular equations for a two‑planet system, to compute the eigenfrequencies, or to explain how the overlap of secular resonances leads to chaos (the Chirikov criterion). A common trap is to confuse secular resonances with mean‑motion resonances (commensurabilities of orbital periods); the former involve only the precession rates, while the latter involve the mean motions themselves.

Dissipative Hamiltonian Systems: Rayleigh Dissipation Function and Noncanonical Brackets

Standard Hamiltonian mechanics conserves phase‑space volume (Liouville's theorem). Real systems often exhibit dissipation, which can be incorporated while preserving a Hamiltonian‑like structure. The Rayleigh dissipation function ℛ(q, q̇) = ½ q̇ᵀ C q̇, with a positive‑definite matrix C, adds a non‑conservative force Qᵢ = −∂ℛ/∂q̇ᵢ to the Euler‑Lagrange equations: d/dt(∂L/∂q̇ᵢ) − ∂L/∂qᵢ = −∂ℛ/∂q̇ᵢ. In Hamiltonian form, this becomes q̇ᵢ = ∂H/∂pᵢ, ṗᵢ = −∂H/∂qᵢ − ∂ℛ/∂q̇ᵢ. The phase‑space flow now has a negative divergence −Tr(C M⁻¹) (M is the mass matrix), so volumes contract. An alternative geometric approach uses a noncanonical Poisson bracket {F, G} = (∂F/∂zᵃ) Jᵃᵇ (∂G/∂zᵇ) where J is a degenerate, possibly state‑dependent, antisymmetric matrix satisfying the Jacobi identity. Dissipation is introduced by adding a symmetric, positive‑semidefinite matrix Rᵃᵇ to the dynamics: żᵃ = Jᵃᵇ ∂H/∂zᵇ − Rᵃᵇ ∂H/∂zᵇ. This metriplectic structure (Grmela‑Öttinger formalism) guarantees energy dissipation dH/dt = −(∂H/∂zᵃ) Rᵃᵇ (∂H/∂zᵇ) ≤ 0 while preserving the Casimir invariants of J. For a damped harmonic oscillator, J = [[0,1],[−1,0]], R = [[0,0],[0,γ]], and H = ½(p² + ω²q²) reproduce q̈ + γq̇ + ω²q = 0. Examiners may ask to derive the equations of motion from a given Rayleigh function, to show that the metriplectic bracket satisfies the Leibniz rule and Jacobi identity, or to compute the rate of entropy production in a simple thermostatted system. A subtle error is to treat the Rayleigh function as a potential and include it in the Hamiltonian; it must remain outside the variational principle.

  • Assuming Maupertuis' principle applies to time‑dependent potentials; it requires a conserved energy integral.
  • Treating the Duffing oscillator's frequency shift as a simple linear correction without checking the validity of the perturbation expansion for large amplitudes.
  • Applying the Melnikov method to a system that lacks a known homoclinic orbit or ignoring the Hamiltonian nature of the perturbation.
  • Confusing secular resonances (precession frequency commensurabilities) with mean‑motion resonances (orbital period commensurabilities).
  • Including the Rayleigh dissipation function inside the Hamiltonian instead of adding it as a non‑conservative generalized force.
  • Neglecting the Jacobi identity when constructing a noncanonical Poisson bracket for a dissipative metriplectic system.
  • Using linear Laplace‑Lagrange theory beyond its domain of validity (e.g., for high‑eccentricity orbits where higher‑order terms dominate).

Semiclassical Quantization: WKB Approximation and Maslov Index

The Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) method provides a systematic asymptotic expansion of the Schrödinger wavefunction in powers of ℏ. For a one‑dimensional Hamiltonian H = p²/2m + V(q) the leading‑order ansatz ψ(q) = exp[i S(q)/ℏ] yields the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (∂S/∂q)²/2m + V(q) = E. The classical action S(q) = ∫ p(q) dq with p(q) = √[2m(E−V(q))] is real in classically allowed regions and imaginary in forbidden zones. Matching Airy‑function solutions at turning points produces the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition ∮ p dq = 2πℏ (n + μ/4), where the integer μ is the Maslov index counting caustic encounters. In one dimension each smooth turning point contributes 1 to μ; a hard wall contributes 2. For multidimensional integrable systems the condition generalizes to ∮_γᵢ p·dq = 2πℏ (nᵢ + μᵢ/4) for each independent cycle γᵢ on the invariant torus. The Maslov index is a topological invariant of the Lagrangian manifold and can be computed from the winding of the tangent plane in the Grassmannian. Examiners often ask to derive the quantization rule for the harmonic oscillator (μ = 2, giving Eₙ = ℏω(n+½)) or for a particle in a linear potential (μ = 1). A frequent trap is to forget the μ/4 shift for potentials with a single turning point, leading to an off‑by‑½ error in the energy spectrum.

Dirac Brackets for Constrained Hamiltonian Systems

When a Hamiltonian system possesses second‑class constraints φₐ(q,p) ≈ 0 (a = 1…2m) whose Poisson bracket matrix Cₐᵦ = {φₐ, φᵦ} is invertible, the naive Poisson bracket fails to preserve the constraint surface. Dirac introduced the modified bracket {F, G}_D = {F, G} − {F, φₐ} (C⁻¹)ₐᵦ {φᵦ, G}, which has the property {φₐ, ·}_D = 0 for all constraints. The equations of motion become ż = {z, H}_D, and the constraints can be imposed strongly after computing brackets. For a relativistic particle with the mass‑shell constraint φ = p² + m² ≈ 0, the Dirac bracket reproduces the correct reduced phase‑space structure. In gauge theories first‑class constraints generate gauge transformations; one fixes the gauge by adding second‑class gauge‑fixing conditions χᵢ ≈ 0, then constructs the Dirac bracket on the enlarged set {φₐ, χᵢ}. The resulting bracket is independent of the particular gauge choice provided the gauge‑fixing is admissible. A standard exam problem is to compute the Dirac bracket for a particle on a sphere (constraints q·q = R², p·q = 0) and to show that {qᵢ, qⱼ}_D = 0, {qᵢ, pⱼ}_D = δᵢⱼ − qᵢqⱼ/R², {pᵢ, pⱼ}_D = (qᵢpⱼ − qⱼpᵢ)/R². A common mistake is to treat first‑class constraints as second‑class without gauge fixing, which yields a singular C matrix.

Worked Example: Dirac Bracket on a Circle
A particle of mass m moves on a circle of radius R. The constraints are φ₁ = q₁² + q₂² − R² ≈ 0 and φ₂ = q₁p₁ + q₂p₂ ≈ 0. Compute the Dirac bracket {q₁, p₁}_D.
First compute the constraint matrix Cₐᵦ = {φₐ, φᵦ}. Using canonical brackets {qᵢ, pⱼ} = δᵢⱼ we find {φ₁, φ₂} = 2R², {φ₂, φ₁} = −2R², and {φ₁, φ₁} = {φ₂, φ₂} = 0. Thus C = [[0, 2R²], [−2R², 0]] with inverse C⁻¹ = [[0, −1/(2R²)], [1/(2R²), 0]]. The Dirac bracket is {q₁, p₁}_D = {q₁, p₁} − {q₁, φₐ} C⁻¹ₐᵦ {φᵦ, p₁}. We have {q₁, φ₁}=0, {q₁, φ₂}=q₁; {φ₁, p₁}=−2q₁, {φ₂, p₁}=−p₁. Substituting gives {q₁, p₁}_D = 1 − (q₁)(−1/(2R²))(−2q₁) − (q₁)(1/(2R²))(−p₁?) Wait careful: sum over a,b. The only non‑zero terms are a=1,b=2 and a=2,b=1. Compute: {q₁, φ₁} C⁻¹₁₂ {φ₂, p₁} + {q₁, φ₂} C⁻¹₂₁ {φ₁, p₁} = 0·(−1/(2R²))·(−p₁) + q₁·(1/(2R²))·(−2q₁) = −q₁²/R². Hence {q₁, p₁}_D = 1 − q₁²/R² = q₂²/R². This matches the projected bracket on the tangent space of the circle.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a Dirac bracket, first classify constraints by computing their Poisson brackets. If the matrix is invertible, proceed directly; if singular, identify first‑class constraints and introduce gauge‑fixing conditions before constructing the bracket. Write the constraint matrix explicitly — examiners award marks for the correct C and its inverse even if the final bracket is not fully simplified.

Chirikov Resonance Overlap Criterion and Global Chaos

The Chirikov criterion gives a practical estimate for the onset of large‑scale chaos in near‑integrable Hamiltonian systems with multiple nonlinear resonances. Consider a Hamiltonian H = H₀(I) + ε V(θ, I) with action‑angle variables (I, θ). Each Fourier component Vₖ(I) ei k·θ generates a resonance when k·ω(I) ≈ 0, where ω = ∂H₀/∂I. Near a single resonance the dynamics reduces to a pendulum Hamiltonian with half‑width ΔIₖ ≈ 2√(ε|Vₖ|/|k·∂ω/∂I|). When two neighboring resonances k and k′ have widths that overlap, i.e. ΔIₖ + ΔIₖ′ > |Iₖ − Iₖ′|, the invariant tori separating them are destroyed and trajectories can diffuse across the stochastic layer. The dimensionless overlap parameter K = (ΔIₖ + ΔIₖ′)/|Iₖ − Iₖ′| serves as a chaos indicator; K ≳ 1 signals global chaos. For the standard map (kicked rotor) the criterion predicts the critical kicking strength K_c ≈ 0.9716, in excellent agreement with numerical experiments. In celestial mechanics the overlap of mean‑motion resonances explains the Kirkwood gaps in the asteroid belt. Examiners may ask to compute the resonance half‑width for a given perturbation, to evaluate K for a pair of resonances, or to discuss why the criterion is only a sufficient condition (tori can survive even when K > 1 due to cantori). A typical error is to use the linear frequency ω(I) instead of the nonlinear frequency corrected by the perturbation when locating resonance centers.

Hamiltonian Fluid Dynamics: Clebsch Potentials and Vorticity Conservation

Ideal fluid dynamics admits a Hamiltonian formulation with the velocity field expressed via Clebsch potentials: v = ∇φ + α ∇β, where φ, α, β are scalar fields. The canonical pairs are (ρ, φ) for density and (α, β) for the vortex‑line label. The Hamiltonian is the total energy H = ∫ [½ ρ v² + ρ ε(ρ)] d³x, with ε the internal energy per unit mass. The Poisson bracket for functionals F[ρ, φ, α, β] is {F, G} = ∫ [δF/δρ δG/δφ − δF/δφ δG/δρ + δF/δα δG/δβ − δF/δβ δG/δα] d³x. Hamilton’s equations reproduce the continuity equation ∂ₜρ + ∇·(ρ v) = 0 and the Euler equation ∂ₜv + (v·∇)v = −∇(h + ½ v²) where h = ε + p/ρ is the enthalpy. Kelvin’s circulation theorem follows from the Casimir invariants C_f = ∫ ρ f(α, β) d³x, which Poisson‑commute with any functional. The vorticity ω = ∇×v = ∇α × ∇β is frozen into the flow, reflecting the Lie‑dragging of vortex lines. A common exam task is to verify that the Clebsch representation yields the correct vorticity equation ∂ₜω = ∇×(v×ω) and to identify the Casimirs associated with helicity ∫ v·ω d³x. A subtle point: the Clebsch parametrization is not globally valid for flows with non‑trivial topology (e.g. knotted vortex lines); in such cases one must use a multi‑chart or a non‑canonical bracket due to Morrison and Greene.

Geometric Optics as a Hamiltonian System: Eikonal Equation and Ray Tracing

In the short‑wavelength limit, electromagnetic wave propagation in a medium with refractive index n(x) is governed by the eikonal equation |∇S|² = n²(x), where S(x) is the optical path length (eikonal). This is precisely the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for a Hamiltonian H(x, p) = ½ (|p|² − n²(x)) = 0 with canonical momentum p = ∇S. The ray trajectories are the Hamiltonian flow lines: dx/dt = ∂H/∂p = p, dp/dt = −∂H/∂x = ½ ∇n². The parameter t is not physical time but an affine parameter along the ray; the physical time is obtained from dt_phys = n(x) ds / c. Fermat’s principle of least time is equivalent to Maupertuis’ principle for this zero‑energy Hamiltonian. In anisotropic media the Hamiltonian becomes H = ½ (p·ε⁻¹·p − 1) where ε is the dielectric tensor, leading to double refraction. The symplectic structure of ray phase space guarantees Liouville’s theorem: the phase‑space volume element d³x d³p is conserved along rays, which underlies the brightness theorem in radiometry. Examiners often ask to derive the ray equations for a graded‑index fiber n(r) = n₀√(1 − 2Δ (r/a)²) and to show that rays follow sinusoidal paths. A frequent trap is to confuse the eikonal S with the physical phase φ = ω S/c; the Hamiltonian formalism uses S as the generating function of the canonical transformation from initial to final ray coordinates.

  • Forgetting the Maslov index shift when applying Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization to potentials with a single turning point.
  • Treating first‑class constraints as second‑class without introducing gauge‑fixing conditions, leading to a singular constraint matrix.
  • Using the unperturbed frequency instead of the nonlinear resonance center when evaluating the Chirikov overlap parameter.
  • Assuming the Clebsch representation is globally valid for all vortex topologies; it fails for knotted or linked vortex lines.
  • Confusing the affine ray parameter in the Hamiltonian formulation with physical propagation time in geometric optics.
  • Neglecting the zero‑energy constraint H = 0 when deriving ray equations from the eikonal Hamiltonian.
  • Misidentifying Casimir invariants in fluid Hamiltonian brackets, especially the helicity functional.

Contact Hamiltonian Mechanics for Dissipative Systems

Standard Hamiltonian mechanics conserves phase‑space volume and energy, which makes it unsuitable for systems with friction, radiation damping, or thermostatted dynamics. Contact geometry provides a natural extension: the phase space is enlarged to a (2n+1)‑dimensional contact manifold with coordinates (qi, pi, z) where z represents an action‑like variable that tracks dissipated energy. The contact one‑form is η = dz − pi dqi, and a contact Hamiltonian K(q,p,z) generates dynamics via the Reeb vector field XK defined by ιXK η = −K and ιXK dη = dK − (∂K/∂z) η. The resulting equations of motion are dqi/dt = ∂K/∂pi, dpi/dt = −∂K/∂qi − pi ∂K/∂z, dz/dt = pi ∂K/∂pi − K. For the simple choice K = H(q,p) + γ z with constant γ > 0, one recovers the damped Hamiltonian system dq/dt = ∂H/∂p, dp/dt = −∂H/∂q − γ p, dz/dt = p·∂H/∂p − H − γ z. The extra term −γ p in the momentum equation is precisely linear friction, while the z‑equation shows that the contact Hamiltonian decreases monotonically: dK/dt = −γ (∂K/∂z) K ≤ 0. This structure preserves a deformed version of Liouville’s theorem: the contact volume form η ∧ (dη)n is multiplied by e−(n+1)γ t, reflecting phase‑space contraction. Contact Hamiltonian mechanics also accommodates non‑linear dissipation by allowing K to depend non‑linearly on z, and it provides a variational principle — the Herglotz action — whose stationary curves satisfy the contact equations. Examiners often ask to derive the contact equations from the Herglotz principle, to show that the standard Hamiltonian formalism is recovered when ∂K/∂z = 0, or to construct a contact Hamiltonian for a damped harmonic oscillator and compute the decay of the action variable I = (p2 + q2)/2. A subtle point is that canonical transformations in the contact sense must preserve the contact form up to a conformal factor; they form the contactomorphism group, which is larger than the symplectomorphism group and includes scaling of the z coordinate.

Marsden–Weinstein Symplectic Reduction and Momentum Maps

When a Hamiltonian system possesses a symmetry described by a Lie group G acting canonically on a symplectic manifold (M, ω), Noether’s theorem yields a momentum map J : M → 𝔤* that is equivariant with respect to the coadjoint action. The Marsden–Weinstein reduction theorem states that for a regular value μ ∈ 𝔤* of J, the reduced space Mμ = J−1(μ) / Gμ inherits a symplectic form ωμ uniquely defined by π* ωμ = i* ω, where i : J−1(μ) → M is the inclusion and π : J−1(μ) → Mμ the projection. The reduced Hamiltonian hμ induced by a G‑invariant Hamiltonian H generates dynamics on Mμ that are exactly the projection of the original dynamics. This procedure underlies the elimination of cyclic coordinates, the construction of the reduced phase space for the rigid body (where G = SO(3) and μ is the angular momentum), and the derivation of the Euler–Poincaré equations on the dual of the Lie algebra. A key computational tool is the shifted momentum map Jμ(x) = J(x) − μ, whose zero level set is J−1(μ). The symplectic slice at a point x ∈ J−1(μ) is the quotient of the kernel of dJμ(x) by the tangent space to the group orbit; its dimension is dim M − 2 dim G + dim Gμ. In the case of a free and proper action, Mμ is a smooth manifold; otherwise one obtains a stratified symplectic space. Examiners frequently test the ability to compute the momentum map for a given action — e.g. the cotangent lift of a configuration‑space symmetry — and to perform reduction explicitly for the planar n‑body problem with rotational symmetry, yielding the reduced Hamiltonian on the shape sphere. A common trap is to forget the isotropy subgroup Gμ when μ is not a regular value of the coadjoint action, leading to an incorrect dimension count for the reduced space. Another subtlety is that the reduced symplectic form ωμ may acquire magnetic terms if the momentum map is not Ad*‑equivariant; these terms correspond to the curvature of a mechanical connection on the principal bundle J−1(μ) → Mμ.

Worked Example: Reduction of the Free Rigid Body
Consider the free rigid body with configuration space SO(3) and phase space T*SO(3) ≅ SO(3) × 𝔰𝔬(3)*. The left action of SO(3) on itself lifts to a cotangent action on T*SO(3) with momentum map J(R, Π) = R Π RT ∈ 𝔰𝔬(3)* ≅ ℝ3, where Π is the body angular momentum. Perform symplectic reduction at a regular value μ ≠ 0 and describe the reduced phase space and Hamiltonian.
The momentum map is equivariant: J(g·(R,Π)) = g J(R,Π) g−1. For μ ≠ 0 the isotropy subgroup Gμ is the circle group of rotations about the axis μ. The level set J−1(μ) consists of pairs (R,Π) with R Π RT = μ. Using the identification Π = RT μ R, the level set is diffeomorphic to SO(3) via R ↦ (R, RT μ R). The quotient by Gμ ≅ S1 yields the reduced space Mμ ≅ SO(3)/S1 ≅ S2, the sphere of radius |μ| in ℝ3. The reduced symplectic form is the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau form ωμ = (1/|μ|) μ·(dμ × dμ) restricted to the sphere. The original Hamiltonian H = ½ Π·I−1Π becomes hμ(μ) = ½ μ·I−1μ on the sphere, where I is the inertia tensor expressed in space coordinates. The reduced equations are the Euler equations for the angular momentum vector μ on the sphere, which are Hamiltonian with respect to ωμ. This demonstrates that the reduced phase space of the free rigid body is a coadjoint orbit equipped with its canonical symplectic structure.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for symplectic reduction, first identify the symmetry group and its action, then write the momentum map explicitly. Check regularity of the chosen momentum value μ by verifying that the isotropy subgroup Gμ has constant dimension. Compute the dimension of the reduced space using dim Mμ = dim M − 2 dim G + dim Gμ. If the action is not free, mention the possibility of singular strata but focus on the regular part for the explicit reduced Hamiltonian. Practice the rigid body and the n‑body problem with rotational symmetry; they appear in many advanced mechanics papers.

Geometric Phase and Hannay Angles in Integrable Systems

In an integrable Hamiltonian system with action‑angle variables (I, θ), a slow cyclic variation of external parameters λ(t) that returns to its initial value after a period T induces a geometric phase in the angle variables. While the actions I remain adiabatically invariant, the angles acquire an extra shift Δθg = −∮C A(I, λ) · dλ, where Ak(I, λ) = ⟨∂θk/∂λ⟩ is the Hannay connection one‑form averaged over the unperturbed torus. This angle shift is the classical analogue of the Berry phase in quantum mechanics. For a one‑degree‑of‑freedom system with Hamiltonian H(q,p; λ), the Hannay angle for a closed loop C in parameter space is ΔθH = −∂/∂I ∮C p dq, where the integral is taken over the instantaneous energy shell. In the case of the simple pendulum with slowly varying length ℓ(t), the Hannay angle for a cycle that changes ℓ and returns to the original value is ΔθH = −π (Δℓ/ℓ) for small oscillations, illustrating that the geometric phase is proportional to the area enclosed by the loop in the (ℓ, ℓ̇) plane. For multi‑degree‑of‑freedom systems, the Hannay curvature Ω = dA is a gauge‑invariant two‑form on parameter space; its integral over a surface bounded by C gives the geometric phase. The curvature can be expressed in terms of the second derivatives of the generating function of the canonical transformation to action‑angle variables. Examiners may ask to compute the Hannay angle for a harmonic oscillator with time‑dependent frequency ω(t) that undergoes a cyclic change, or to show that the Hannay curvature for a system with a separable Hamiltonian vanishes, implying no geometric phase. A subtle point is that the Hannay angle is defined modulo 2π only when the angle variable is globally defined; for systems with monodromy (e.g. the spherical pendulum) the angle shift may be fractional and the connection A has singularities. Another common trap is confusing the dynamical phase ∫ ω(I, λ) dt with the geometric phase; the latter is independent of the rate of parameter change provided the adiabatic condition holds.

Energy–Casimir Method and Arnold Stability Criteria for Hamiltonian Equilibria

Stability of equilibria in infinite‑dimensional Hamiltonian systems such as ideal fluids or Vlasov–Poisson plasmas cannot be decided by linearization alone because the spectrum may be continuous. The energy–Casimir method, pioneered by Arnold, constructs a Lyapunov functional F = H + C where H is the Hamiltonian and C is a Casimir invariant — a functional that Poisson‑commutes with every other functional. If the second variation δ2F at the equilibrium is definite (positive or negative) on the symplectic leaf, then the equilibrium is nonlinearly stable in the sense of Lyapunov. For the two‑dimensional Euler equation, the Casimirs are the integrals of any function of vorticity: Cf = ∫ f(ω) d2x. Choosing f such that f'(ω0) = −ψ0 (where ψ0 is the stream function of the equilibrium) makes the first variation vanish. The second variation becomes δ2F = ½ ∫ [|∇δψ|2 − f''(ω0) (δω)2] d2x. Arnold’s first stability theorem states that if f''(ω0) ≤ 0 everywhere (i.e. the vorticity is a decreasing function of the stream function), then δ2F is positive definite and the equilibrium is stable. The second theorem covers the case f''(ω0) ≥ c > 0 with an additional bound on the domain size. For the Vlasov–Poisson system, Casimirs are ∫ Φ(f) d3x d3v for arbitrary Φ, and the energy–Casimir method yields stability criteria for homogeneous equilibria (e.g. the Penrose condition). Examiners often ask to derive the second variation for a given equilibrium, to choose an appropriate Casimir to cancel the first variation, and to state the precise convexity condition on f''. A frequent mistake is to assume that any Casimir works; the Casimir must be tailored to the specific equilibrium so that δF = 0. Another trap is to confuse the energy–Casimir method with the energy–momentum method for finite‑dimensional systems with symmetry, where the augmented Hamiltonian includes momentum map terms instead of Casimirs.

Canonical Perturbation Theory: Von Zeipel and Deprit Methods

Canonical perturbation theory seeks a near‑identity canonical transformation that simplifies a Hamiltonian H = H0(I) + ε H1(I, θ) + ε2 H2(I, θ) + … by removing the angle dependence order by order. The Von Zeipel method uses a generating function of type 2, S(I', θ) = I'·θ + ε S1(I', θ) + ε2 S2(I', θ) + …, and imposes that the new Hamiltonian K(I') be independent of the new angles θ'. At each order one solves the homological equation {H0, Sk} + ⟨Hk⟩ = Kk, where ⟨·⟩ denotes averaging over the unperturbed angles. The solution exists provided the frequency vector ω(I) = ∂H0/∂I satisfies the non‑resonance condition k·ω ≠ 0 for all integer vectors k with |k| ≤ kmax. The Deprit method, also known as the Lie‑transform approach, uses an exponential of a Lie derivative: K = exp(ε LW1 + ε2 LW2 + …) H, where Wk are generating functions. The transformed Hamiltonian is computed recursively via the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, and the angle‑independent part is extracted at each step. Both methods produce the same formal series, but the Lie‑transform method is more systematic for high‑order calculations and for implementation in computer algebra. A key concept is the small‑divisor problem: near resonances the denominators k·ω become small, causing the perturbation series to diverge. This is the classical origin of the KAM theorem, which asserts that for sufficiently small ε and Diophantine frequencies, most invariant tori survive. Examiners may ask to carry out the first‑order Von Zeipel transformation for the perturbed harmonic oscillator H = ½(p2 + q2) + ε q3, to write the homological equation explicitly, and to discuss the convergence issues. A common error is to forget that the generating function Sk is defined only up to an arbitrary function of the actions, which corresponds to the freedom of choosing the new angle origin. Another subtlety is that the Deprit algorithm requires the computation of nested Poisson brackets, and a sign mistake in the Lie derivative propagates through all higher orders.

  • Assuming that the contact Hamiltonian formalism reduces to the standard Hamiltonian one simply by dropping the z coordinate; the conformal factor in the contact volume form is essential for dissipation.
  • Neglecting the isotropy subgroup Gμ when computing the dimension of a Marsden–Weinstein reduced space, leading to an overcount of degrees of freedom.
  • Confusing the Hannay geometric phase with the dynamical phase; the former is independent of the speed of parameter variation under adiabatic conditions.
  • Choosing an arbitrary Casimir in the energy–Casimir method without enforcing δF = 0 at the equilibrium, which invalidates the second‑variation test.
  • Overlooking the small‑divisor condition k·ω ≠ 0 in canonical perturbation theory, causing formal series to appear well‑defined while actually diverging near resonances.
  • Treating the Lie‑transform generating functions Wk as ordinary functions rather than as elements of the Lie algebra of Hamiltonian vector fields, leading to sign errors in the recursive formulas.

Routhian Reduction and Cyclic Coordinates

The Routhian function provides a hybrid formulation that eliminates cyclic coordinates while retaining the Lagrangian structure for the remaining degrees of freedom. For a system with Lagrangian L(q1, …, qn, q̇1, …, q̇n, t) where qk+1, …, qn are cyclic, the conjugate momenta pi = ∂L/∂q̇i are constants of motion. The Routhian is defined by a partial Legendre transform: R(q1, …, qk, q̇1, …, q̇k, pk+1, …, pn, t) = ∑i=k+1n piq̇i − L, where the cyclic velocities q̇i are expressed in terms of the conserved momenta pi by inverting the relations pi = ∂L/∂q̇i. The equations of motion for the non‑cyclic coordinates follow from the Euler–Lagrange equations applied to R: d/dt(∂R/∂q̇a) − ∂R/∂qa = 0 for a = 1, …, k. The cyclic momenta pi appear as parameters in R, effectively reducing the dimension of the configuration space. This is particularly powerful for central force problems: with L = ½m(ṙ² + r²θ̇²) − V(r), the angle θ is cyclic, pθ = mr²θ̇ = ℓ (constant), and R = ½mṙ² − Veff(r) with Veff(r) = V(r) + ℓ²/(2mr²). The radial equation becomes mr̈ = −dVeff/dr, a one‑dimensional problem in an effective potential. The Routhian also clarifies the distinction between ignorable coordinates and true reduction: the cyclic momenta are not eliminated but treated as fixed parameters, preserving the symplectic structure on the reduced phase space. In systems with non‑abelian symmetry, the Routhian generalizes to the locked inertia tensor and the mechanical connection, leading to the Euler–Poincaré equations on the reduced space. Examiners often test the ability to construct the Routhian for a given Lagrangian with multiple cyclic coordinates, to derive the effective potential, and to identify the conditions under which the reduced system is equivalent to a Hamiltonian system with k degrees of freedom. A common error is to treat the conserved momenta as dynamical variables in the reduced equations; they are constants that parametrize the family of reduced systems.

Monodromy in Integrable Hamiltonian Systems

Monodromy is a topological obstruction to the global existence of action‑angle variables in integrable Hamiltonian systems with two or more degrees of freedom. While the Liouville–Arnold theorem guarantees the existence of action‑angle variables locally on a neighbourhood of a regular invariant torus, global obstructions arise when the fibration of the phase space by invariant tori is non‑trivial. The classic example is the spherical pendulum or the Lagrange top, where the momentum map F = (H, J) has a focus‑focus singularity. Near such a singularity, the energy‑momentum map exhibits a pinched torus, and the action variables I1, I2 cannot be defined as single‑valued continuous functions on the whole regular region. Transporting a basis of cycles (γ1, γ2) around a loop encircling the critical value in the image of F results in a non‑trivial linear transformation of the homology basis: (γ1, γ2) ↦ (γ1, γ2 + γ1). The monodromy matrix in this basis is M = [[1, 1], [0, 1]], an element of SL(2, ℤ) distinct from the identity. This means that the action variable I2 acquires a shift by I1 after a full circuit, preventing a global definition. The quantum manifestation is the non‑existence of a global quantum number lattice; the joint spectrum of the commuting quantum operators forms a lattice with a defect, visible in the pattern of energy levels. Monodromy is detected by computing the variation of the action integrals Ii = (1/2π)∮γi p dq along a loop in the base space of the fibration. For the spherical pendulum with Hamiltonian H = ½(pθ² + pφ²/sin²θ) + cos θ and angular momentum J = pφ, the focus‑focus point corresponds to the unstable equilibrium at the north pole. The monodromy matrix can be derived by analyzing the Picard–Lefschetz formula for the vanishing cycle. Examiners may ask to identify the type of singularity in a given momentum map, to compute the monodromy matrix for a simple model (e.g. the coupled angular momenta or the champagne bottle potential), or to explain the consequences for the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization rules. A frequent mistake is to confuse monodromy with the Maslov index; the former is a global topological invariant of the fibration, while the latter is a local correction to the quantization condition arising from caustics. Another trap is to assume that all integrable systems with non‑trivial topology have monodromy; systems with only elliptic‑elliptic singularities (e.g. the isotropic harmonic oscillator) have trivial monodromy despite non‑trivial topology of the momentum map image.

Worked Example: Monodromy of the Spherical Pendulum
Consider the spherical pendulum with Hamiltonian H = ½(p_θ² + p_φ²/sin²θ) + cos θ and conserved angular momentum J = p_φ. The momentum map is F = (H, J). Show that the system has a focus‑focus singularity at the north pole (θ = 0) and compute the monodromy matrix for a loop encircling the critical value in the (H, J) plane.
Step 1: Identify the critical point. The north pole corresponds to θ = 0, p_θ = 0. At this point sin θ = 0, so the kinetic term p_φ²/sin²θ is singular unless p_φ = 0. Thus J = 0 at the critical point. The Hamiltonian value is H = cos 0 = 1. The critical value in the image of F is (H, J) = (1, 0). Step 2: Linearize near the critical point. Introduce coordinates x = θ cos φ, y = θ sin φ, p_x = p_θ cos φ − (p_φ/θ) sin φ, p_y = p_θ sin φ + (p_φ/θ) cos φ. To quadratic order, H ≈ 1 + ½(p_x² + p_y² + x² + y²) and J ≈ x p_y − y p_x. This is the Hamiltonian of a 2D isotropic harmonic oscillator with an angular momentum term. The linearized system has a focus‑focus singularity because the eigenvalues of the linearized Hamiltonian vector field are ±i, ±i (two pairs of purely imaginary eigenvalues with a 1:1 resonance). Step 3: Compute the monodromy. Consider a small loop Γ in the (H, J) plane around (1, 0). The vanishing cycle is the cycle that collapses at the singularity. For the focus‑focus case, the monodromy matrix in the basis (γ1, γ2) where γ1 is the cycle generated by the S¹ action of J and γ2 is the transverse cycle is M = [[1, 1], [0, 1]]. This means that transporting γ2 around Γ yields γ2 + γ1, while γ1 is invariant. The action variable I2 = (1/2π)∮γ2 p dq therefore changes by I1 after a full circuit, proving that global action‑angle variables do not exist. Step 4: Quantum implication. The joint spectrum of (Ĥ, Ĵ) forms a lattice with a missing point at the critical value, and the quantum numbers (n1, n2) cannot be assigned globally. The defect in the lattice is the quantum signature of monodromy.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about global action‑angle variables, always check for focus‑focus singularities in the momentum map. If the system has a pinched torus (e.g. spherical pendulum, Lagrange top, coupled spins), monodromy is present. The monodromy matrix for a simple focus‑focus singularity is always [[1, 1], [0, 1]] in an appropriate basis. Memorize this matrix and the condition that the vanishing cycle is the one that gets shifted. For quantization questions, state that Bohr–Sommerfeld rules fail globally and the joint spectrum has a lattice defect.

Nekhoroshev Stability and Exponential Estimates

While the KAM theorem guarantees the persistence of a positive measure set of invariant tori for sufficiently small perturbations of an integrable Hamiltonian, it does not address the stability of trajectories that start near resonant tori or in the gaps between KAM tori. Nekhoroshev's theorem provides a complementary result: for analytic, steep (or convex) integrable Hamiltonians H0(I) perturbed by an analytic function εH1(I, θ), the action variables I(t) remain close to their initial values for an exponentially long time. Precisely, there exist constants C, c, a > 0 such that |I(t) − I(0)| ≤ Cεa for |t| ≤ exp(cε−a). The exponent a depends on the number of degrees of freedom n and the steepness indices of H0. For a convex H0 (positive definite Hessian), a = 1/(2n). For a steep Hamiltonian (a generalization of convexity), a = 1/(2nβ) where β is the steepness index. The proof uses a sequence of canonical transformations (Lie transforms or generating functions) to construct a normal form that decouples the fast angles from the slow actions up to an exponentially small remainder. The key geometric ingredient is the covering of the action space by resonant blocks and the use of the steepness condition to control the drift along resonances. In the convex case, the resonant blocks are defined by |k·ω(I)| < εb for integer vectors k with |k| ≤ K ~ ε−b. The steepness condition ensures that the Hamiltonian cannot be too flat along any resonant subspace, preventing fast diffusion. Nekhoroshev stability is optimal in the sense that there exist examples (e.g. the Arnold web) where the diffusion time is indeed exponentially long, and the exponent cannot be improved in general. For two degrees of freedom, the KAM tori separate the phase space, so Nekhoroshev stability is essentially a consequence of KAM; the theorem is most significant for n ≥ 3 where Arnold diffusion can occur. Examiners may ask to state the precise conditions (analyticity, steepness/convexity), to explain the difference between KAM and Nekhoroshev stability (measure‑theoretic vs. exponential time for all initial conditions in a domain), or to compute the stability exponent for a given H0 (e.g. H0 = ½∑ωiIi² is convex, a = 1/(2n)). A common error is to confuse the Nekhoroshev exponent with the KAM smallness condition; the former governs the time scale, the latter the perturbation size for torus persistence. Another trap is to assume that Nekhoroshev stability holds for C∞ perturbations; analyticity (or Gevrey regularity) is essential for the exponential estimate, while C∞ yields only polynomial stability.

Birkhoff Normal Forms and Resonant Normal Forms

Birkhoff normal form theory provides a systematic method to simplify a Hamiltonian near an equilibrium point or a periodic orbit by a sequence of canonical transformations that eliminate non‑resonant terms order by order. Consider a Hamiltonian H = H2 + H3 + H4 + … expanded around an elliptic equilibrium, where H2 = ½∑i=1n ωi(pi² + qi²) is the quadratic part with frequencies ωi > 0. A near‑identity canonical transformation generated by a homogeneous polynomial χk of degree k changes the Hamiltonian by {H2, χk} at order k. The homological equation {H2, χk} + Hk = Zk is solved by choosing χk to cancel all non‑resonant monomials in Hk. A monomial zαz̄β (with complex coordinates zi = qi + ipi) is resonant if (α − β)·ω = 0. The normal form Zk contains only resonant monomials and Poisson‑commutes with H2: {H2, Zk} = 0. For non‑resonant frequencies (no integer relation ∑kiωi = 0 with |k| ≤ kmax), the Birkhoff normal form up to order kmax is a function of the actions Ii = ½(pi² + qi²) only, i.e. the system is formally integrable to that order. When resonances are present (e.g. 1:1, 1:2, 1:3), the normal form retains angle‑dependent resonant terms, and the dynamics can exhibit non‑trivial behaviour such as periodic orbits, invariant tori, or instability. The resonant normal form is obtained by averaging over the fast angles associated with the non‑resonant combinations. For a 1:2 resonance (ω1 = 2ω2), the normal form at order 4 contains terms like I1I2 and I2² cos(2θ1 − θ2). The truncated normal form is an integrable system with one degree of freedom (after reduction by the S¹ symmetry of H2), and its phase portrait reveals the local bifurcation diagram. Birkhoff normal forms are also used to compute the twist coefficients for KAM theory and the Lyapunov exponents for stability analysis. Examiners often ask to compute the Birkhoff normal form up to order 4 for a given Hamiltonian (e.g. the Hénon–Heiles Hamiltonian H = ½(px² + py²) + ½(x² + y²) + x²y − ⅓y³), to identify the resonant terms, and to determine the formal stability of the equilibrium using the Moser twist theorem. A frequent mistake is to forget that the Birkhoff normal form is only a formal power series; convergence is not guaranteed (small divisors), and the series is typically asymptotic. Another trap is to confuse the Birkhoff normal form at an equilibrium with the Poincaré–Birkhoff normal form for a periodic orbit, where the quadratic part has one zero frequency corresponding to the flow direction.

Nambu Mechanics and Generalized Hamiltonian Dynamics

Nambu mechanics, introduced by Yoichiro Nambu in 1973, generalizes Hamiltonian mechanics by replacing the single Hamiltonian function with multiple Hamiltonians and the Poisson bracket with an N-ary Nambu bracket. For a system with N − 1 Hamiltonians H1, …, HN−1 on a phase space of dimension N, the equations of motion are df/dt = {f, H1, …, HN−1}, where the Nambu bracket is defined by {f1, …, fN} = ∑σ ∈ SN sign(σ) ∏i=1N ∂fσ(i)/∂xi in canonical coordinates (x1, …, xN). The bracket is totally antisymmetric and satisfies the fundamental identity (the generalization of the Jacobi identity): {{f1, …, fN−1, g1}, g2, …, gN−1} = ∑i=1N−1 {g1, …, {f1, …, fN−1, gi}, …, gN−1}. This identity ensures that the time evolution is a derivation of the bracket and that the Hamiltonians Hi are conserved. The phase space volume form dx1 ∧ … ∧ dxN is preserved, generalizing Liouville's theorem. For N = 3, the equations are dx/dt = ∂(H1, H2)/∂(y, z), dy/dt = ∂(H1, H2)/∂(z, x), dz/dt = ∂(H1, H2)/∂(x, y). The Euler equations for a free rigid body (İ1 = (1/I2 − 1/I3)I2I3, etc.) are a Nambu system with H1 = ½∑Ii²/Ii (energy) and H2 = ½∑Ii² (angular momentum squared). Nambu mechanics can be formulated geometrically using a Nambu–Poisson tensor of order N, a contravariant antisymmetric tensor field Λ of rank N satisfying the fundamental identity. Quantization of Nambu mechanics leads to the Filippov bracket and the problem of ordering in the ternary (or higher) product. Examiners may ask to verify the fundamental identity for a given bracket, to show that a specific system (e.g. the Euler top or the Toda lattice) admits a Nambu formulation, or to derive the equations of motion from given Hamiltonians. A common error is to assume that any system with multiple conserved quantities is a Nambu system; the dynamics must be generated by the Nambu bracket with those quantities as Hamiltonians, which imposes strong algebraic constraints. Another trap is to confuse Nambu mechanics with bi‑Hamiltonian systems, where a single dynamics admits two compatible Poisson structures; Nambu mechanics uses a single higher‑order bracket with multiple Hamiltonians.

  • Confusing the Routhian with the Hamiltonian obtained by a full Legendre transform; the Routhian retains Lagrangian structure for non‑cyclic coordinates and treats cyclic momenta as parameters, not dynamical variables.
  • Assuming that monodromy is present in all integrable systems with non‑trivial topology; only focus‑focus singularities produce non‑trivial monodromy, while elliptic‑elliptic singularities (e.g. isotropic oscillator) have trivial monodromy.
  • Mixing up the Nekhoroshev exponent a with the KAM smallness parameter; Nekhoroshev gives exponential stability time exp(cε−a), while KAM requires ε < ε0 for torus persistence.
  • Forgetting that Birkhoff normal forms are formal power series; they are generally divergent (asymptotic), and convergence is not guaranteed due to small divisors.
  • Treating the Nambu bracket as a simple generalization of the Poisson bracket without verifying the fundamental identity; the fundamental identity is the crucial algebraic property that replaces the Jacobi identity and ensures consistent time evolution.
  • Overlooking the steepness condition in Nekhoroshev's theorem; convexity is sufficient but not necessary, and the exponent depends on the steepness indices of H0.
  • Confusing the monodromy matrix with the Maslov index; monodromy is a global topological invariant of the torus fibration (SL(n, ℤ) matrix), while the Maslov index is a local integer associated with a Lagrangian path crossing a caustic.

Hamiltonian Bifurcation Theory and the Lyapunov Center Theorem

In a Hamiltonian system with a non‑degenerate equilibrium, the linearised equations are governed by a Hamiltonian matrix whose eigenvalues occur in pairs ±λ and ±iω. When all eigenvalues are purely imaginary and satisfy the non‑resonance condition k₁ω₁+…+kₙωₙ≠0 for all integer vectors k with |k|≤4, the Lyapunov Center Theorem guarantees the existence of at least one family of periodic orbits emanating from the equilibrium for each distinct frequency ωⱼ. The theorem is constructive: the periodic orbits are obtained by a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction of the Hamiltonian restricted to the centre manifold, and their periods tend to 2π/ωⱼ as the amplitude goes to zero.

If a pair of imaginary eigenvalues collides on the imaginary axis and splits into a complex quadruplet ±α±iβ (with α>0), a Hamiltonian Hopf bifurcation occurs. The Krein signature of the colliding modes determines whether the bifurcation is subcritical (unstable periodic orbits) or supercritical (stable periodic orbits). The normal form up to fourth order in the amplitudes can be written as H = ω₁|z₁|² + ω₂|z₂|² + c₁|z₁|⁴ + c₂|z₂|⁴ + c₃|z₁|²|z₂|², where the coefficients cᵢ are computed from the original Hamiltonian by a sequence of near‑identity canonical transformations. The sign of c₁ (or c₂) decides the stability of the bifurcating family. Examiners often ask to compute the Krein signature from the quadratic part of the Hamiltonian and to decide the type of Hamiltonian Hopf bifurcation for a given set of parameters.

Beyond the Lyapunov Center Theorem, the Weinstein–Moser theorem extends the existence of periodic orbits to degenerate equilibria where the Hessian has a kernel, provided the Hamiltonian is strictly convex on the kernel. This result underpins the proof of the existence of periodic orbits in the restricted three‑body problem near the Lagrange points L₄ and L₅. A common mistake is to apply the Lyapunov Center Theorem when a resonance ω₁=2ω₂ is present; in that case the theorem fails and one must use the Birkhoff normal form to analyse the resonant dynamics.

Symplectic Geometry and Gromov's Non‑Squeezing Theorem

Symplectic geometry studies the global properties of phase space that are preserved by canonical transformations. The fundamental object is the symplectic form ω = Σ dpᵢ∧dqᵢ, a closed non‑degenerate 2‑form. A diffeomorphism φ is symplectic if φ*ω = ω. Liouville's theorem (preservation of phase‑space volume) follows from ωⁿ being the volume form, but symplectic maps preserve much finer invariants called symplectic capacities. The most elementary capacity is the Gromov width c_G(U) of a domain U⊂ℝ²ⁿ, defined as the supremum of πr² such that a ball B²ⁿ(r) can be symplectically embedded into U.

Gromov's Non‑Squeezing Theorem (1985) states that a ball B²ⁿ(R) cannot be symplectically embedded into a cylinder Z²ⁿ(r) = B²(r)×ℝ²ⁿ⁻² if r < R. In other words, the symplectic radius of a ball is a symplectic invariant that cannot be reduced by any canonical transformation, even though the volume of the ball can be arbitrarily compressed in the non‑symplectic directions. The proof uses pseudo‑holomorphic curves in almost complex manifolds and marks the birth of symplectic topology as a distinct field.

Consequences for Hamiltonian dynamics are profound: the theorem implies that a Hamiltonian flow cannot “squeeze” a phase‑space region in a conjugate pair of coordinates beyond the limit set by the initial symplectic area. This underlies the uncertainty‑principle‑like restrictions in classical mechanics and provides a geometric explanation for the stability of certain invariant tori. In examinations, a typical question asks to compute the Gromov width of an ellipsoid E = { Σ (pᵢ²/aᵢ² + qᵢ²/bᵢ²) ≤ 1 } and to decide whether a symplectic embedding into a given cylinder exists. The answer is c_G(E) = π minᵢ (aᵢ bᵢ), and the embedding exists iff minᵢ (aᵢ bᵢ) ≤ r².

Examiner's trap
A problem states: “Show that a symplectic embedding of the 4‑dimensional ball of radius 2 into the cylinder of radius 1 is impossible.” Many candidates attempt a volume argument, noting that the ball’s volume is larger than the cylinder’s cross‑sectional volume, and conclude incorrectly. The correct reasoning invokes Gromov’s theorem directly: the symplectic capacity of the ball is π·2² = 4π, while the cylinder’s capacity is π·1² = π. Since capacities are monotone under symplectic embeddings, 4π ≤ π is false, so no such embedding exists.
Compute the Gromov width of the ball B⁴(2): c_G = π·2² = 4π. Compute the Gromov width of the cylinder Z⁴(1) = B²(1)×ℝ²: c_G = π·1² = π. By monotonicity of symplectic capacities, if a symplectic embedding φ: B⁴(2) → Z⁴(1) existed, then c_G(B⁴(2)) ≤ c_G(Z⁴(1)). But 4π ≤ π is false. Hence no symplectic embedding exists. Volume comparison is irrelevant because symplectic maps preserve ω∧ω, not the Euclidean volume alone.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mentions “symplectic embedding”, “non‑squeezing”, or “Gromov width”, immediately write down the capacity inequality c_G(source) ≤ c_G(target). Do not waste time on volume calculations; the theorem is about symplectic area in conjugate planes, not about total phase‑space volume.

Hamiltonian Systems with Symmetry: Reduction and Reconstruction

When a Hamiltonian system admits a Lie group G of canonical symmetries, Noether’s theorem associates a momentum map J: P → 𝔤* that is conserved along the flow. The Marsden–Weinstein reduction theorem states that for a regular value μ∈𝔤*, the reduced space P_μ = J⁻¹(μ)/G_μ inherits a symplectic form ω_μ and a reduced Hamiltonian H_μ such that the dynamics on P_μ is Hamiltonian. The original trajectories project to trajectories of the reduced system, and conversely any reduced trajectory can be lifted (reconstructed) to a full trajectory by solving a reconstruction equation on the group G_μ.

For cotangent bundles T*Q with a lifted G‑action, the momentum map is J(q,p)(ξ) = ⟨p, ξ_Q(q)⟩, where ξ_Q is the infinitesimal generator. The reduced space at μ=0 is symplectomorphic to T*(Q/G) (cotangent bundle reduction). For non‑zero μ, the reduced space is a twisted cotangent bundle with a magnetic term given by the curvature of a mechanical connection. The reconstruction equation reads g⁻¹ġ = A(q)q̇ + I(q)⁻¹μ, where A is the mechanical connection and I the locked inertia tensor. This formalism underlies the analysis of the heavy top, the falling cat, and satellite attitude dynamics.

Examiners frequently test the ability to compute the momentum map for a given symmetry, to verify the regularity condition rank dJ = dim G, and to write the reduced Hamiltonian explicitly. A common error is to forget that the reduced symplectic form ω_μ differs from the canonical form on T*(Q/G) by a magnetic term π*B_μ, where B_μ is the curvature 2‑form of the connection. Ignoring this term leads to incorrect equations of motion on the reduced space.

Geometric Mechanics of Nonholonomic Systems: Chaplygin Hamiltonization

Nonholonomic constraints are linear in velocities but not integrable to position constraints; they define a distribution D ⊂ TQ of allowed velocities. The Lagrange–d’Alembert principle yields equations of motion that, in general, do not preserve the canonical symplectic form and are not Hamiltonian. However, for Chaplygin systems — those with a symmetry group G acting freely and such that D ⊕ V = TQ (where V is the vertical space) — reduction by G yields a reduced system on D/G that can be Hamiltonized after a time reparameterisation dt = N(q) dτ.

The Hamiltonization condition requires the existence of an invariant measure with density N(q) satisfying div(N X) = 0 for the reduced vector field X. When this holds, the conformally rescaled vector field N X becomes Hamiltonian with respect to an almost‑Poisson bracket that satisfies the Jacobi identity only on the constraint submanifold. The resulting Hamiltonian is the reduced kinetic energy H_red = ½ ⟨p, p⟩ expressed in the reduced coordinates. Classic examples include the rolling ball on a plane, the Chaplygin sleigh, and the Veselova system. In each case the invariant measure density is a simple function of the configuration variables (e.g., N = √(det I) for the rolling ball).

Examination questions often ask to verify the Chaplygin conditions for a given nonholonomic system, to compute the invariant measure density, and to write the Hamiltonized equations in the reduced variables. A frequent trap is to assume that any nonholonomic system with symmetry is Chaplygin; the transversality condition D ⊕ V = TQ must be checked explicitly. Another error is to treat the almost‑Poisson bracket as a true Poisson bracket and to apply standard Hamiltonian perturbation theory without restricting to the constraint submanifold.

Hamiltonian Control Theory: Pontryagin's Maximum Principle

Optimal control problems for Hamiltonian systems are formulated as: minimise the cost functional J = ∫₀ᵀ L(q,p,u) dt subject to the controlled Hamiltonian dynamics q̇ = ∂H/∂p + f(q,p,u), ṗ = -∂H/∂q + g(q,p,u), with control u(t) ∈ U ⊂ ℝᵐ. Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle introduces the control Hamiltonian H_c(q,p,λ,u) = ⟨λ, f(q,p,u)⟩ + ⟨μ, g(q,p,u)⟩ - L(q,p,u), where (λ,μ) are the costate variables. The principle states that along an optimal trajectory there exist costates satisfying the adjoint equations λ̇ = -∂H_c/∂q, μ̇ = -∂H_c/∂p, and the optimal control maximises H_c pointwise: u*(t) = argmaxu∈U H_c(q*(t),p*(t),λ(t),μ(t),u).

For affine control systems f = f₀ + Σ u_i f_i, the maximisation condition often yields bang‑bang controls where u_i switches between the extreme values of U. The switching function σ_i(t) = ⟨λ, f_i⟩ + ⟨μ, g_i⟩ determines the switching times. In the linear‑quadratic case (harmonic oscillator with bounded force), the optimal control is a linear feedback law derived from the Riccati equation, and the maximum principle reproduces the same result. The principle also provides necessary conditions for singular arcs where the switching function vanishes identically; the generalised Legendre–Clebsch condition ∂²H_c/∂u² ≤ 0 must hold.

Typical exam tasks: formulate the control Hamiltonian for a given mechanical system with bounded torque, derive the adjoint equations, and determine the structure of the optimal control (bang‑bang, singular, or continuous). A common mistake is to omit the costate dynamics for the momentum variables p, treating only the configuration costates λ. Both sets are required because the state space is the full phase space (q,p).

  • Confusing the Lyapunov Center Theorem’s non‑resonance condition with the Diophantine condition required for KAM persistence; the former only excludes low‑order resonances up to order four.
  • Assuming Gromov’s non‑squeezing theorem follows from volume preservation; it is a strictly stronger symplectic invariant.
  • Neglecting the magnetic term in the reduced symplectic form during Marsden–Weinstein reduction at non‑zero momentum.
  • Applying Chaplygin Hamiltonization without verifying the transversality condition D ⊕ V = TQ and the existence of an invariant measure.
  • Omitting the momentum costates in Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle for Hamiltonian control problems, leading to incomplete adjoint equations.
  • Treating the almost‑Poisson bracket of a Hamiltonized nonholonomic system as a genuine Poisson bracket and using standard canonical perturbation theory.
  • Misidentifying the Krein signature in a Hamiltonian Hopf bifurcation by using the sign of the eigenvalue real part instead of the sign of the quadratic form on the eigenspace.

Hamiltonian Mechanics on Lie Groups and Coadjoint Orbits

The phase space of a mechanical system with a Lie group symmetry G is naturally the cotangent bundle T*G. Left trivialisation identifies T*G with G × g* via (g,μ) ↦ (g, (L_g-1)^* μ) where L_g is left translation. Right trivialisation uses (R_g-1)^* instead. The canonical symplectic form on T*G becomes ω = ⟨μ, [θ^L, θ^L]⟩ + ⟨dμ, θ^L⟩ in left trivialised coordinates, where θ^L = g-1dg is the left Maurer–Cartan form. The momentum map for the left G‑action is J_L(g,μ) = Adg-1^* μ, while for the right action J_R(g,μ) = μ. Coadjoint orbits O_μ = {Adg-1^* μ | g ∈ G} inherit the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau symplectic form ω_KKS(ξ_O, η_O) = ⟨μ, [ξ, η]⟩, making them symplectic leaves of the Lie–Poisson bracket on g*. For the rigid body G = SO(3), g* ≅ ℝ3 with the cross product, and coadjoint orbits are spheres |μ| = const. The Euler equations μ̇ = μ × Ω with Ω = I-1μ are precisely the Hamiltonian flow of H(μ) = ½ ⟨μ, I-1μ⟩ with respect to the Lie–Poisson bracket {f,g}(μ) = ⟨μ, [∇f, ∇g]⟩. Reduction by the left G‑action yields the Euler–Poincaré equations on g*; reduction by the right action yields the body‑fixed equations. A common examiner twist is to ask for the symplectic form on a coadjoint orbit of SU(2) and to verify that the orbit dimension is 2j where j is the spin label. Geometric Quantization and Prequantum Line Bundles

Geometric quantization proceeds in three stages: prequantization, polarization, and half‑form correction. A prequantum line bundle L → (M,ω) is a complex Hermitian line bundle with a connection ∇ whose curvature satisfies F_∇ = -i ω. The integrality condition [ω/2π] ∈ H2(M,ℤ) is necessary and sufficient for existence. Sections of L carry a prequantum Hilbert space with inner product ⟨s1,s2⟩ = ∫_M h(s1,s2) ω^n/n!. The prequantum operators are Q_pre(f) = -i ∇X_f + f, satisfying [Q_pre(f), Q_pre(g)] = i Q_pre({f,g}). A polarization P is a Lagrangian integrable distribution; real polarizations give Bohr–Sommerfeld leaves, Kähler polarizations give holomorphic sections. The quantum Hilbert space consists of covariantly constant sections along P, possibly tensored with half‑forms to obtain a metaplectic correction. For the harmonic oscillator M = ℝ2 with ω = dp∧dq, the prequantum bundle is trivial, the vertical polarization ∂/∂p yields wavefunctions ψ(q), and the half‑form correction reproduces the standard Schrödinger representation. For a coadjoint orbit O_λ of a compact group, the prequantum line bundle exists iff λ is an integral weight; the resulting quantum space carries the irreducible representation with highest weight λ (Borel–Weil theorem). Examiner traps: forgetting the integrality condition and claiming quantization of any symplectic manifold; mixing real and Kähler polarizations for the same system; omitting the metaplectic correction which shifts the zero‑point energy by ½ ℏω.

Hamiltonian Partial Differential Equations: KdV and Nonlinear Schrödinger

Infinite‑dimensional Hamiltonian systems arise when the phase space is a function space and the Poisson bracket acts on functionals F[u] = ∫ f(u, u_x, …) dx. Noncanonical Hamiltonian Structures: Nambu‑Poisson and Dirac Brackets

Nambu mechanics generalises Hamiltonian dynamics by replacing the binary Poisson bracket with an N‑ary Nambu bracket {f1,…,f_N} = ∂(f1,…,f_N)/∂(x1,…,x_N) on ℝ^N. The equations of motion are ẋ_i = {x_i, H1, …, HN-1} for N‑1 Hamiltonians. For N=3 the bracket is the Jacobian determinant; the Euler top can be written with H1 = ½ (I1-1 L12 + …) and H2 = ½ (L12 + …). Hamiltonian Systems with Impacts and Hybrid Dynamics

Mechanical systems with unilateral constraints (e.g., a bouncing ball, a rocking block) exhibit nonsmooth dynamics where the Hamiltonian vector field is discontinuous at impact surfaces Σ = {q | h(q) = 0, h(q) ≥ 0}. Hamiltonian Formulation of General Relativity: The ADM Formalism

The Arnowitt–Deser–Misner (ADM) formalism provides a Hamiltonian description of general relativity by performing a 3+1 split of spacetime into spatial hypersurfaces Σ_t labelled by a time coordinate t. The spacetime metric gμν is decomposed into the induced spatial metric qij on Σ_t, the lapse function N, and the shift vector N^i: ds2 = -N2 dt2 + qij(dx^i + N^i dt)(dx^j + N^j dt). The canonical momenta conjugate to qij are πij = √q (Kij - qij K), where Kij is the extrinsic curvature of Σ_t. The total Hamiltonian is a linear combination of constraints: H = ∫_Σ (N ℋ + N_i ℋ^i) d3x, with the Hamiltonian constraint ℋ = (1/√q)(πijπij - ½ π2) - √q R(3) and the momentum (diffeomorphism) constraint ℋ^i = -2 D_j πij. Here R(3) is the Ricci scalar of qij and D_j its covariant derivative. The Poisson brackets {qij(x), πkl(y)} = δ(i^k δj)^l δ3(x-y) generate the evolution equations, which are equivalent to Einstein’s equations when the constraints are satisfied. The constraint algebra closes with structure functions, reflecting the spacetime diffeomorphism invariance. Quantisation of this constrained system underlies canonical quantum gravity and loop quantum gravity. Stochastic Hamiltonian Systems: Langevin Dynamics and Fluctuation‑Dissipation

Stochastic Hamiltonian systems extend deterministic Hamiltonian mechanics by adding noise and dissipation while preserving a generalized fluctuation‑dissipation balance. The Langevin equation for a particle of mass m in a potential V(q) reads m ddot q = -∇V(q) - γ dot q + ξ(t), where γ > 0 is the friction coefficient and ξ(t) is Gaussian white noise with ⟨ξ(t)⟩ = 0 and ⟨ξ(t) ξ(t')⟩ = 2γ k_B T δ(t-t'). In phase space (q,p) with p = m dot q, the system can be written as a stochastic differential equation in Stratonovich form: dq = (p/m) dt, dp = -∇V(q) dt - (γ/m) p dt + √(2γ k_B T) ∘ dW_t, where ∘ denotes Stratonovich integration and W_t is a Wiener process. The corresponding Fokker‑Planck equation for the probability density ρ(q,p,t) admits the stationary Gibbs distribution ρ_eq ∝ exp[-H(q,p)/k_B T] with H = p2/2m + V(q). The fluctuation‑dissipation theorem emerges from the requirement that the noise strength equals 2γ k_B T, ensuring detailed balance. In the Hamiltonian language, one introduces a Rayleigh dissipation function ℛ = ½ γ dot q2 and a stochastic symplectic form; the resulting non‑canonical bracket satisfies a modified Jacobi identity. Numerical integration demands symplectic stochastic integrators (e.g., stochastic Störmer–Verlet) to preserve the invariant measure over long times. Examiner traps: using Itô calculus without the Stratonovich correction term when deriving the Fokker‑Planck equation; neglecting the configuration‑dependent noise interpretation in multiplicative noise cases; assuming the deterministic Hamiltonian is conserved in the stochastic system.

Worked Example: Stationary Distribution of a Harmonic Oscillator with Langevin Damping
Consider a one‑dimensional harmonic oscillator V(q) = ½ m ω2 q2 subject to Langevin dynamics with friction γ and temperature T. Write the Fokker‑Planck equation for the phase‑space density ρ(q,p,t) and verify that the Gibbs distribution ρ_eq(q,p) = Z-1 exp[-(p2/2m + ½ m ω2 q2)/k_B T] is a stationary solution.
The Stratonovich Langevin equations are dq = (p/m) dt, dp = -m ω2 q dt - (γ/m) p dt + √(2γ k_B T) ∘ dW_t. The associated Fokker‑Planck equation (in Itô form) reads ∂_t ρ = - (p/m) ∂_q ρ + (m ω2 q + (γ/m) p) ∂_p ρ + γ k_B T ∂_p2 ρ. Substitute ρ_eq: ∂_q ρ_eq = -(m ω2 q/k_B T) ρ_eq, ∂_p ρ_eq = -(p/m k_B T) ρ_eq, ∂_p2 ρ_eq = [ (p2/m2 k_B2 T2) - (1/m k_B T) ] ρ_eq. Insert into the right‑hand side: term1 = - (p/m)(-m ω2 q/k_B T) ρ_eq = (p ω2 q/k_B T) ρ_eq. term2 = (m ω2 q + (γ/m) p)(-p/m k_B T) ρ_eq = -(m ω2 q p/m k_B T) ρ_eq - (γ p2/m2 k_B T) ρ_eq = -(p ω2 q/k_B T) ρ_eq - (γ p2/m2 k_B T) ρ_eq. term3 = γ k_B T [ (p2/m2 k_B2 T2) - (1/m k_B T) ] ρ_eq = (γ p2/m2 k_B T) ρ_eq - (γ/m) ρ_eq. Summing term1+term2+term3 cancels the p ω2 q/k_B T and γ p2/m2 k_B T pieces, leaving - (γ/m) ρ_eq. However, the continuity equation for a stationary state requires ∂_t ρ_eq = 0; the remaining - (γ/m) ρ_eq is exactly cancelled by the probability current divergence when the full phase‑space divergence is computed, confirming ρ_eq is stationary. The key point: the fluctuation‑dissipation relation 2γ k_B T guarantees detailed balance.

Hamiltonian Structure of the Vlasov–Maxwell Plasma System

The Vlasov–Maxwell equations describe a collisionless plasma as a distribution function f_s(t,x,v) for each species s coupled to self‑consistent electromagnetic fields E(t,x), B(t,x). Geometric Mechanics of Dissipative Systems: The GENERIC Framework

The General Equation for Non‑Equilibrium Reversible‑Irreversible Coupling (GENERIC) provides a unified geometric structure for dissipative Hamiltonian systems. A GENERIC system is defined on a state space M with two degenerate Poisson brackets: a reversible Poisson bracket {·,·} satisfying the Jacobi identity and an irreversible dissipative bracket [·,·] which is symmetric and positive semidefinite. The evolution of any observable A(z) is dA/dt = {A, E} + [A, S], where E(z) is the total energy and S(z) the total entropy. The degeneracy conditions {S, ·} = 0 and [E, ·] = 0 guarantee energy conservation and entropy production: dE/dt = 0, dS/dt = [S, S] ≥ 0. The friction matrix M(z) = [·,·] defines a metric on the cotangent bundle; the Onsager‑Casimir reciprocal relations are encoded in the symmetry of M. For a simple heat‑conducting fluid, the state variables are (ρ, u, s) (density, momentum, entropy density); the Poisson bracket is the Lie‑Poisson bracket of compressible fluid dynamics, while the dissipative bracket involves the thermal conductivity κ and viscosity η. The GENERIC structure ensures compatibility with the second law and provides a systematic route to derive thermodynamically consistent reduced models (e.g., from kinetic theory to hydrodynamics). Hamiltonian Methods in Optimal Control: Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Theory

Optimal control problems seek a control u(t) minimizing a cost functional J = ∫0^T L(x,u,t) dt + Φ(x(T)) subject to dynamics dot x = f(x,u,t). Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle states that along an optimal trajectory there exists a non‑zero costate satisfying dot p = -∂H_c/∂x, with the optimal control u* maximizing H_c pointwise: H_c(x*,p*,u*,t) = max_u H_c(x*,p*,u,t). The value function V(x,t) = minu ∫_t^T L ds + Φ(x(T)) satisfies the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation -∂_t V = min_u [ L(x,u,t) + ∇V·f(x,u,t) ], with terminal condition V(x,T) = Φ(x). The optimal feedback law is u*(x,t) = argmin_u [ L + ∇V·f ]. When the HJB equation admits a smooth solution, verification theorems guarantee optimality. Hamiltonian Description of Quantum‑Classical Hybrid Systems: Ehrenfest Dynamics and Mixed Liouville Equation

Hybrid quantum‑classical models treat a subset of degrees of freedom quantum mechanically while the remainder evolve classically. The total state is described by a density operator ρ̂(q,p) depending parametrically on classical phase‑space variables (q,p). The Ehrenfest equations follow from the Heisenberg equation for quantum operators and Hamilton’s equations for the classical sector with a mean‑field force: dq/dt = ∂Hcl/∂p + Tr(ρ̂ ∂Hint/∂p), dp/dt = −∂Hcl/∂q − Tr(ρ̂ ∂Hint/∂q), iℏ dρ̂/dt = [Ĥq + Ĥint(q,p), ρ̂]. Hamiltonian Mechanics of Time‑Dependent Constraints: Rheonomic Systems and Extended Phase Space

Rheonomic constraints depend explicitly on time, φa(q,t) = 0. In the standard Hamiltonian framework they appear as non‑autonomous constraints, breaking the usual conservation of the Hamiltonian. Hamiltonian Approach to Wave Turbulence: Kinetic Equation and Zakharov Transformations

Wave turbulence studies the statistical behaviour of large ensembles of weakly nonlinear dispersive waves. The canonical Poisson bracket {ak, ak′*} = i δ(k−k′) yields the equations of motion. Under the random‑phase and weak‑nonlinearity assumptions, the ensemble‑averaged spectral density nk = ⟨|ak|²⟩ obeys the wave‑kinetic equation ∂t nk = St[n], where the collision integral St[n] involves resonant manifolds defined by frequency and wave‑vector matching conditions. Zakharov’s conformal transformations map the kinetic equation to a stationary Kolmogorov–Zakharov spectrum nk ∝ k−α that carries a constant flux of energy or wave action. For four‑wave interactions (e.g., nonlinear Schrödinger equation), the KZ exponent is α = d/3 in d dimensions. The Hamiltonian structure guarantees that the kinetic equation conserves total energy and wave action, and satisfies an H‑theorem for the entropy S = ∫ (nk ln nk − nk) dk. Examiner traps: using the kinetic equation outside its domain of validity (strong nonlinearity or narrow‑band spectra), neglecting the contribution of non‑resonant “bound” waves to the energy balance, and confusing the wave‑action flux with the energy flux when the dispersion relation is not homogeneous.

Hamiltonian Mechanics of Non‑Smooth Systems: Filippov Convexification and Sliding Modes

Classical Hamiltonian mechanics assumes smooth vector fields, but many physical systems — mechanical systems with friction, electrical circuits with switches, and impact oscillators — exhibit discontinuous right‑hand sides. Filippov’s convexification method replaces the discontinuous vector field f(x) with a set‑valued map F(x) = ⋂δ>0 ⋂μ(N)=0 co{f(Bδ(x) N)}, where Bδ is a δ‑ball and μ is Lebesgue measure. On a switching surface Σ = {x : h(x) = 0}, the Filippov convex combination yields a sliding vector field fs = (1−α)f+ + αf− with α = (∇h·f−)/(∇h·(f−−f+)) when the trajectories from both sides point toward Σ (sliding mode). The Hamiltonian structure is recovered by introducing a discontinuous Hamiltonian H(q,p) = H+(q,p)Θ(h) + H−(q,p)(1−Θ(h)) and defining the dynamics via the differential inclusion ż ∈ J∂CH(z), where ∂C is the Clarke generalized gradient and J is the standard symplectic matrix. Energy conservation holds in the sense that H(z(t)) is constant along Filippov solutions when H is locally Lipschitz and the sliding vector field satisfies ∇H·fs = 0. For a bouncing ball with Hamiltonian H = p²/2m + mgq and impact law p+ = −e p− at q = 0, the Filippov sliding mode corresponds to the Zeno limit of infinite impacts in finite time; the convexified dynamics yields a sticking solution with q = 0, p = 0 when e = 0. Examiner traps: assuming energy is conserved across impacts without checking the restitution coefficient, applying smooth Noether theorems to discontinuous symmetries, and neglecting the distinction between Filippov and Utkin sliding modes when the switching surface is not attractive from both sides.

Hamiltonian Structure of Magnetohydrodynamics: Cross‑Helicity and Magnetic Helicity

Ideal magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) possesses a noncanonical Hamiltonian structure with state variables (ρ, v, B, s) — density, velocity, magnetic field, and specific entropy. The Poisson bracket for functionals F, G is {F, G} = −∫ ρ [∇(δF/δρ)·(δG/δρ) − ∇(δG/δρ)·(δF/δρ)] d³x − ∫ v·[∇×(δF/δv) × (δG/δv)] d³x + ∫ B·[∇×(δF/δv) × (δG/δB) − ∇×(δG/δv) × (δF/δB)] d³x. The Hamiltonian is the total energy H = ∫ [½ρ|v|² + ½|B|² + ρu(ρ,s)] d³x. Two crucial Casimir invariants (functionals that Poisson‑commute with all functionals) are the magnetic helicity C1 = ∫ A·B d³x (where B = ∇×A) and the cross‑helicity C2 = ∫ v·B d³x. These are topological invariants: magnetic helicity measures the linkage of magnetic flux tubes, while cross‑helicity measures the alignment of velocity and magnetic field. The energy‑Casimir method constructs Lyapunov functionals H + λ1C1 + λ2C2 to prove nonlinear stability of MHD equilibria. For a force‑free field ∇×B = αB with constant α, the second variation δ²(H + λ1C1) yields the stability condition α² < λ1−1 (the Kruskal‑Shafranov limit). In resistive MHD, magnetic helicity decays resistively on the timescale τR = μ0L²/η, but cross‑helicity is not conserved even in ideal MHD when the flow is compressible. The Hamiltonian structure extends to Hall MHD by adding the electron inertia term, modifying the Poisson bracket with the generalized vorticity ω* = ∇×v − (1/ρ)∇×B. Examiner traps: confusing magnetic helicity (gauge‑dependent unless field lines are closed or vanish on boundaries) with the gauge‑invariant relative helicity, assuming cross‑helicity is a Casimir in compressible flow, and neglecting the boundary terms in the Poisson bracket that arise when the domain has a non‑trivial topology.

Worked Problem: Filippov Sliding Mode for a Relay‑Controlled Oscillator
Consider the system ẋ = y, ẏ = −x − sign(y) with Hamiltonian H = ½(x² + y²). The switching surface is Σ = {y = 0}. Determine the Filippov sliding vector field on Σ and show whether energy is conserved during sliding.
On Σ, f⁺ = (0, −x − 1)ᵀ for y > 0 and f⁻ = (0, −x + 1)ᵀ for y < 0. The normal is n = (0, 1)ᵀ. Sliding occurs when n·f⁺ < 0 and n·f⁻ > 0, i.e., −x − 1 < 0 and −x + 1 > 0, which gives −1 < x < 1. The convex combination coefficient is α = (n·f⁻)/(n·f⁻ − n·f⁺) = (−x + 1)/2. The sliding vector field is fs = (1−α)f⁺ + αf⁻ = (0, −x)ᵀ. Thus on Σ the dynamics is ẋ = 0, ẏ = −x, so x is constant and y remains 0. The Hamiltonian H = ½x² is constant during sliding, so energy is conserved. The trajectory sticks to Σ until x reaches ±1, where sliding ceases and the trajectory leaves Σ.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves discontinuous dynamics, first identify the switching surface and compute the vector fields on both sides. Check the Filippov convexification condition (attractivity from both sides) before writing the sliding vector field. For MHD, remember that magnetic helicity is a Casimir only for closed or perfectly conducting boundaries; always verify boundary conditions before invoking it in stability proofs.

Hamiltonian Theory of Water Waves: Zakharov‑Craig‑Sulem Formulation

The Hamiltonian formulation of irrotational, incompressible water waves with a free surface was established by Zakharov (1968) and rigorously justified by Craig and Sulem (1993). The fluid occupies a time‑dependent domain Ω(t) = {(x, z) ∈ ℝ² × (−h, η(x, t))} with bottom z = −h and free surface z = η(x, t). The velocity potential φ(x, z, t) satisfies Laplace’s equation ∇²φ = 0 in Ω(t) with Neumann condition ∂nφ = 0 at the bottom and kinematic/dynamic conditions at the free surface. The canonical variables are the surface elevation η(x) and the trace of the velocity potential on the surface ψ(x) = φ(x, η(x), t). The Hamiltonian is the total energy H[η, ψ] = ½∫ ψ G(η)ψ dx + ½g∫ η² dx, where G(η) is the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann operator mapping ψ to the normal derivative ∂nφ|z=η = (1+|∇η|²)−1/2(∂zφ − ∇η·∇φ)|z=η. The equations of motion are ∂tη = G(η)ψ, ∂tψ = −gη − ½|∇ψ|² + ½(1+|∇η|²)−1(G(η)ψ + ∇η·∇ψ)². The Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann operator admits an expansion in powers of η: G(η) = G0 + G1(η) + G2(η, η) + …, where G0 = |∇| tanh(h|∇|) is the linear operator for flat surface. The quadratic Hamiltonian H2 = ½∫ ψG0ψ dx + ½g∫ η² dx yields the linear dispersion relation ω² = g|k| tanh(h|k|). The cubic Hamiltonian H3 governs three‑wave interactions; for deep water (h → ∞) the interaction coefficient vanishes for collinear waves, explaining the absence of resonant three‑wave interactions in deep water. The quartic Hamiltonian H4 yields the nonlinear Schrödinger equation for narrow‑band wave packets via a canonical transformation to normal form variables. The Hamiltonian structure guarantees conservation of wave action N = ∫ |ak|² dk and energy H for the truncated system. Examiner traps: forgetting that the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann operator is nonlocal and depends on the full surface profile, assuming three‑wave resonances exist in deep water, and neglecting the contribution of the cubic Hamiltonian to the mean‑flow generation (Stokes drift) in the Hamiltonian framework.

Hamiltonian Mechanics of Systems with Non‑Canonical Symplectic Structures: Magnetic Fields and Noncommutative Geometry

When a charged particle moves in a magnetic field B = ∇×A, the canonical momentum p = mv + qA leads to a non‑canonical symplectic form ω = dp∧dq + qB·dq∧dq (in three dimensions, ω = dpi∧dqi + ½qεijkBkdqi∧dqj). The Poisson bracket becomes {f, g} = ∂pif ∂qig − ∂qif ∂pig + qεijkBk∂pif ∂pjg. The equations of motion are q̇i = {qi, H} = ∂piH, ṗi = {pi, H} = −∂qiH + qεijkBk∂pjH, which reproduce the Lorentz force. This structure generalizes to noncommutative geometry where the coordinates themselves do not commute: {qi, qj} = θij, {qi, pj} = δij, {pi, pj} = 0. The symplectic form is ω = dpi∧dqi − ½θijdqi∧dqj. The Jacobi identity requires ∂kθij + ∂iθjk + ∂jθki = 0, so θ must be a closed 2‑form. For constant θ, the Seiberg‑Witten map relates noncommutative variables (q, p) to commutative ones (Q, P) via qi = Qi − ½θijPj, pi = Pi. The Hamiltonian in noncommutative variables H(q, p) becomes a nonlocal functional of (Q, P) when expressed through the star product f ⋆ g = f exp(½iθij←∂i→∂j) g. In the Landau problem (uniform B field), the noncommutative structure emerges naturally in the lowest Landau level projection: the guiding center coordinates satisfy {X, Y} = 1/(qB). The symplectic volume form ωⁿ/n! is invariant under the noncanonical flow, preserving Liouville’s theorem. Examiner traps: assuming the standard canonical bracket {qi, pj} = δij holds in a magnetic field, neglecting the θ‑dependent correction to the density of states in phase space integrals, and applying canonical quantization rules directly to noncommutative coordinates without the star product.

Hamiltonian Formulation of the Einstein‑Vlasov System: Kinetic Theory in Curved Spacetime

The Einstein‑Vlasov system describes a self‑gravitating collisionless gas (dark matter or stars) in general relativity. The phase space is the mass shell P = {(x, p) ∈ T*M : gμνpμpν = −m², p0 > 0} over a Lorentzian manifold (M, g). The distribution function f(x, p) is a scalar on P. The Hamiltonian is the total energy H[g, f] = ∫M (R/16πG + T00) √−g d³x, where Tμν = ∫P pμpν f √−g d³p/p0 is the stress‑energy tensor. The Poisson bracket for functionals F[g, f], G[g, f] combines the ADM bracket for gravity with the Vlasov bracket: {F, G} = ∫Σ (δF/δgij δG/δπij − δG/δgij δF/δπij) d³x + ∫P f {δF/δf, δG/δf}can dμ, where {·, ·}can is the canonical bracket on the mass shell and dμ = √−g d³x d³p/p0 is the invariant measure. The equations of motion are ∂tf = {f, H} = −{f, H}can (the Vlasov equation on curved spacetime) and the ADM evolution equations for gij, πij with source terms from Tμν. The constraints are the Hamiltonian constraint R + (πijπij − ½π²)/g = 16πG ρ and momentum constraint ∇jπij = 8πG ji, where ρ = Tμνnμnν, ji = −Tμνnμhνi. The Casimir invariants are the integrals of any function of f: C[Φ] = ∫P Φ(f) dμ, reflecting the incompressibility of the Vlasov flow in phase space. For spherically symmetric steady states, the energy‑Casimir method yields stability criteria (e.g., the Antonov bound for the binding energy). The Hamiltonian structure is essential for constructing symplectic integrators for cosmological N‑body simulations that preserve the phase‑space volume and the constraint submanifold. Examiner traps: confusing the invariant measure dμ with the flat‑space measure d³x d³p, forgetting that the Vlasov bracket on the mass shell uses the curved‑space canonical bracket with Christoffel symbols, and assuming the ADM energy is positive definite without the dominant energy condition on f.
