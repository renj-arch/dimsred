Classical Mechanics — Newton's Laws, Conservation Laws, Rotational Dynamics & Gravitation

Classical mechanics is the foundation of physics — the study of motion and forces governing everything from falling apples to planetary orbits. Newton's three laws provide the axiomatic basis: the law of inertia (every body persists in its state of rest or uniform motion unless compelled by a net external force), the second law F = dp/dt = ma relating force to the rate of change of momentum, and the law of action-reaction (forces between two bodies are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction). The conservation laws follow from Newton's laws: conservation of linear momentum (isolated systems have constant total momentum), conservation of angular momentum (central forces produce torqueless motion), and conservation of mechanical energy (for conservative forces). Rotational dynamics extends these ideas to rigid bodies: torque τ = r × F, moment of inertia I = ∫ r² dm, Euler's equations, and gyroscopic precession. Gravitation, described by Newton's inverse-square law F = −GMm/r² r̂, leads to Kepler's three laws of planetary motion and the rich theory of orbital mechanics — elliptical orbits, the vis-viva equation, Hohmann transfers, and Lagrange points. The Lagrangian formulation L = T − V with the Euler-Lagrange equations and the Hamiltonian formulation H = Σ p_i q̇_i − L with Hamilton's canonical equations provide the deepest and most general framework, connecting to symmetries via Noether's theorem and forming the bridge to quantum mechanics.

Part 1

⚖️ Newton's Laws and Conservation Laws

Foundations
Newton's Three Laws, Momentum, Energy, and the Work-Energy Theorem
Newton's first law (the law of inertia) states that a body remains at rest or in uniform rectilinear motion unless acted upon by a net external force — this defines inertial reference frames. The second law F = dp/dt (where p = mv is the linear momentum) reduces to F = ma when mass is constant; the net external force equals the rate of change of momentum. The third law — action and reaction are equal and opposite — ensures internal forces cancel pairwise in a system, yielding conservation of total momentum. Conservation of linear momentum: for an isolated system, Σ m_i v_i = constant. Conservation of angular momentum: for a central force (F ∝ r̂), the torque τ = r × F = 0, so L = r × p is constant — this explains Kepler's equal-area law. Conservation of mechanical energy: for conservative forces (F = −∇V), the work done by the force is path-independent; the work-energy theorem W = ΔK = ∫ F·dr gives E = K + V = constant. The impulse-momentum theorem: J = ∫ F dt = Δp. The center of mass R = (Σ m_i r_i)/(Σ m_i) moves as if all mass were concentrated there with the total external force acting on it: M d²R/dt² = F_ext. Collisions: elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy; inelastic collisions conserve only momentum.
Newton's laws together with the work-energy and impulse-momentum theorems give a complete framework for analyzing the translational motion of particles and systems.
Part 2

🌀 Rotational Dynamics

Rigid Body Motion
Torque, Moment of Inertia, Angular Momentum, and Precession
For a rigid body, the rotational analog of Newton's second law: τ = dL/dt = Iα, where τ = r × F is the torque about a point, L = Iω is the angular momentum, and I is the moment of inertia. For a discrete system I = Σ m_i r_i² (where r_i is the perpendicular distance from the axis); for a continuous body I = ∫ r² dm. The parallel axis theorem: I = I_cm + Md², where d is the distance between the axis through the center of mass and the parallel axis of interest. The perpendicular axis theorem (for planar bodies): I_z = I_x + I_y. Rotational kinetic energy: K_rot = ½ Iω². For a rolling object (without slipping), v = ωR and total K = ½ Mv² + ½ Iω². Euler's equations for rigid body dynamics in the body frame: I₁ω̇₁ − (I₂ − I₃)ω₂ω₃ = τ₁, and cyclically. Torque-free precession (the tennis racket theorem, intermediate axis theorem) shows that rotation about the intermediate principal axis is unstable. Gyroscopic precession: when a torque is applied perpendicular to the axis of a spinning gyroscope, it precesses with angular velocity Ω = Mgh/Iω. Physical pendulum: τ = −MgL sin θ, T = 2π√(I/MgL). The compound pendulum has a center of oscillation (the equivalent length of a simple pendulum).
Rotational dynamics mirrors translational dynamics through the torque-moment of inertia-angular momentum triad; rigid body motion reveals counterintuitive phenomena like gyroscopic precession and the intermediate axis instability.
Part 3

🌌 Gravitation and Orbital Mechanics

Universal Gravity
Newton's Law of Gravitation, Kepler's Laws, Orbital Dynamics, and the N-Body Problem
Newton's law of universal gravitation: F = −GMm/r² r̂, where G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². The gravitational potential energy: U = −GMm/r. Gauss's law for gravity: ∮ g·dA = −4πGM_enc, where g = −GM/r² r̂ is the gravitational field. Kepler's laws: (1) planets orbit in ellipses with the Sun at one focus; (2) the radius vector sweeps equal areas in equal times (equivalent to conservation of angular momentum); (3) T² ∝ a³, where a is the semi-major axis: T² = (4π²/GM)a³. The vis-viva equation: v² = GM(2/r − 1/a). Orbital energy: E = −GMm/2a (for bound orbits), E = 0 (parabolic escape), E > 0 (hyperbolic). Escape velocity: v_esc = √(2GM/R). Hohmann transfer orbit: the most fuel-efficient way to transfer between two circular orbits using two engine burns. Lagrange points L₁–L₅: the five equilibrium points in the circular restricted three-body problem, where gravitational and centrifugal forces balance. L₁, L₂, L₃ are collinear and unstable; L₄, L₅ are triangular and stable (for mass ratio < 0.04). The N-body problem has no general closed-form solution; the three-body problem exhibits chaos (the Lagrange instability). Perturbation theory (the method of variation of parameters) handles deviations from Keplerian motion due to other bodies, tidal forces, and relativistic corrections (the precession of Mercury).
Newton's law of gravitation combined with Kepler's laws provides a complete description of two-body orbital mechanics; the three-body problem reveals the inherent complexity and chaos in gravitational dynamics.
Part 4

⚛️ Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Mechanics

Analytical Dynamics
The Principle of Least Action, Euler-Lagrange Equations, Noether's Theorem, Hamilton's Equations
The Lagrangian L = T − V (kinetic minus potential energy). Hamilton's principle of least action: the true path of a system between two configurations extremizes the action S = ∫ L dt. The Euler-Lagrange equations: d/dt(∂L/∂q̇_i) = ∂L/∂q_i, where q_i are generalized coordinates. These equations are invariant under coordinate transformations — they give the correct equations of motion in any coordinate system. The generalized momentum: p_i = ∂L/∂q̇_i. If L does not depend explicitly on q_i (a cyclic coordinate), then p_i is conserved. Noether's theorem: every continuous symmetry of the action corresponds to a conserved quantity — time translation invariance → energy conservation; spatial translation invariance → momentum conservation; rotational invariance → angular momentum conservation. The Hamiltonian: H = Σ p_i q̇_i − L = T + V (for natural systems). Hamilton's equations: q̇_i = ∂H/∂p_i, ṗ_i = −∂H/∂q_i — a system of 2n first-order ODEs replacing n second-order Euler-Lagrange equations. The Hamiltonian is constant if H has no explicit time dependence. The phase space is the 2n-dimensional space of (q_i, p_i); Hamilton's equations generate a symplectic flow preserving the Poisson bracket: {f,g} = Σ_i (∂f/∂q_i ∂g/∂p_i − ∂f/∂p_i ∂g/∂q_i). The Liouville theorem: phase space volume is conserved under Hamiltonian flow. The Hamilton-Jacobi equation: H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) + ∂S/∂t = 0, a powerful method for finding conserved quantities and integrable systems. The KAM theorem (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) describes the persistence of quasiperiodic orbits under small perturbations in integrable Hamiltonian systems.
The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations provide the deepest understanding of classical mechanics, revealing the fundamental role of symmetries and conservation laws and providing the structural foundation for quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics.
