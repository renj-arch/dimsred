Calculus of Variations — The Mathematics of Optimal Paths

Calculus of variations finds functions that minimize or maximize functionals — integrals depending on a function and its derivatives. From the brachistochrone problem (fastest slide) to the Euler-Lagrange equations, Noether's theorem, geodesics in curved space, minimal surfaces, and the direct method in Sobolev spaces, it's the mathematical foundation of classical mechanics, optimal control, and geometric analysis.

"Nature is thrifty in all its actions" — "The shortest path between two truths in the real domain passes through the imaginary domain."
— Pierre de Fermat / Jacques Hadamard
Part 1

🎯 Euler-Lagrange Equation — The First Variation

Stationary Action
The Euler-Lagrange Equation — Finding Stationary Points of Functionals
The fundamental problem: minimize J[y] = ∫_a^b L(x,y,y') dx subject to y(a)=α, y(b)=β. The first variation δJ = 0 leads to the Euler-Lagrange equation: ∂L/∂y − d/dx(∂L/∂y') = 0. This is a necessary condition for y to be an extremum. For L independent of y (∂L/∂y = 0), we get ∂L/∂y' = constant (first integral). For L independent of x, we get the Beltrami identity: L − y' ∂L/∂y' = constant. The brachistochrone (fastest path under gravity) yields a cycloid: x = a(θ − sinθ), y = a(1 − cosθ). The catenary (hanging chain) minimizes potential energy: y = a cosh(x/a). The geodesic on a surface minimizes distance — the Euler-Lagrange equation gives the geodesic equation ∇_γ'γ' = 0.
The Euler-Lagrange equation is the master equation of optimization in infinite dimensions — it finds the path that nature takes.
Part 2

🔗 Noether's Theorem — Symmetry and Conservation

Conservation
Noether's Theorem — Every Symmetry Yields a Conserved Quantity
Noether's theorem (1918): for every one-parameter family of transformations that leaves the action integral invariant, there is a corresponding conserved quantity along solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations. If L is invariant under time translation, energy H = y'∂L/∂y' − L is conserved. Under spatial translation, momentum p = ∂L/∂y' is conserved. Under rotation, angular momentum is conserved. Under gauge transformations, charge is conserved. This is the deepest link between symmetry and physics — every conservation law in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, and general relativity comes from a symmetry. Noether's theorem holds for Hamiltonian systems as well — the Poisson bracket of the conserved quantity with the Hamiltonian is zero.
Noether's theorem is the most beautiful result in mathematical physics — symmetry creates conservation laws, and conservation laws reveal the deep structure of dynamics.
Part 3

🌀 Hamiltonian Mechanics and the Hamilton-Jacobi Equation

Phase Space
Hamiltonian Mechanics — The Legendre Transform and Phase Flow
The Hamiltonian H(q,p) = p·q̇ − L(q, q̇) is the Legendre transform of L with p = ∂L/∂q̇. Hamilton's equations: dq/dt = ∂H/∂p, dp/dt = −∂H/∂q. The flow on phase space (q,p) is symplectic — it preserves the 2-form dp∧dq (Liouville's theorem). The Hamilton-Jacobi equation ∂S/∂t + H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) = 0 is a first-order PDE for the action function S. If we can solve H-J, we integrate Hamilton's equations. The H-J equation is the classical limit of the Schrödinger equation. The principle of least action (Hamilton's principle): the true path of a mechanical system is a stationary point of the action integral. The Euler-Lagrange and Hamiltonian formulations are equivalent — the Legendre transform is the bridge.
Hamiltonian mechanics is geometry on phase space — symplectic structure, conservation of phase space volume, and the deep connection to quantum mechanics via H-J.
Part 4

⚡ Direct Methods and Modern Variational Theory

Existence
Direct Method — Existence of Minimizers in Sobolev Spaces
The direct method (Tonelli, Hilbert) proves existence of minimizers without solving EL: (1) Take a minimizing sequence y_n; (2) Show y_n is bounded in a suitable space (e.g., W^{1,p}); (3) Extract a weakly convergent subsequence; (4) Show the functional is lower semicontinuous. Convexity of L in y' (L_{p_i p_j} ≥ 0) gives lower semicontinuity. The space of functions of bounded variation (BV) handles functionals with jumps. Minimal surfaces minimize area: the Euler-Lagrange equation gives the mean curvature zero condition. The Plateau problem (find minimal surface with given boundary) was solved by Douglas and Rado (Fields Medal, 1936). Γ-convergence (De Giorgi) studies limits of variational problems — used in homogenization and phase transitions. The Mountain Pass Theorem and Palais-Smale condition find critical points of non-convex functionals.
The direct method gives existence of minimizers through compactness and convexity — you don't need to solve the equation to know the optimum exists.
