Calculus of Variations — The Mathematics of Optimal Paths

Calculus of variations finds functions that minimize or maximize functionals — integrals depending on a function and its derivatives. From the brachistochrone problem (fastest slide) to the Euler-Lagrange equations, Noether's theorem, geodesics in curved space, minimal surfaces, and the direct method in Sobolev spaces, it's the mathematical foundation of classical mechanics, optimal control, and geometric analysis.

"Nature is thrifty in all its actions" — "The shortest path between two truths in the real domain passes through the imaginary domain."
— Pierre de Fermat / Jacques Hadamard
Part 1

🎯 Euler-Lagrange Equation — The First Variation

Stationary Action
The Euler-Lagrange Equation — Finding Stationary Points of Functionals
The fundamental problem: minimize J[y] = ∫_a^b L(x,y,y') dx subject to y(a)=α, y(b)=β. The first variation δJ = 0 leads to the Euler-Lagrange equation: ∂L/∂y − d/dx(∂L/∂y') = 0. This is a necessary condition for y to be an extremum. For L independent of y (∂L/∂y = 0), we get ∂L/∂y' = constant (first integral). For L independent of x, we get the Beltrami identity: L − y' ∂L/∂y' = constant. The brachistochrone (fastest path under gravity) yields a cycloid: x = a(θ − sinθ), y = a(1 − cosθ). The catenary (hanging chain) minimizes potential energy: y = a cosh(x/a). The geodesic on a surface minimizes distance — the Euler-Lagrange equation gives the geodesic equation ∇_γ'γ' = 0.
The Euler-Lagrange equation is the master equation of optimization in infinite dimensions — it finds the path that nature takes.
Part 2

🔗 Noether's Theorem — Symmetry and Conservation

Conservation
Noether's Theorem — Every Symmetry Yields a Conserved Quantity
Noether's theorem (1918): for every one-parameter family of transformations that leaves the action integral invariant, there is a corresponding conserved quantity along solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations. If L is invariant under time translation, energy H = y'∂L/∂y' − L is conserved. Under spatial translation, momentum p = ∂L/∂y' is conserved. Under rotation, angular momentum is conserved. Under gauge transformations, charge is conserved. This is the deepest link between symmetry and physics — every conservation law in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, and general relativity comes from a symmetry. Noether's theorem holds for Hamiltonian systems as well — the Poisson bracket of the conserved quantity with the Hamiltonian is zero.
Noether's theorem is the most beautiful result in mathematical physics — symmetry creates conservation laws, and conservation laws reveal the deep structure of dynamics.
Part 3

🌀 Hamiltonian Mechanics and the Hamilton-Jacobi Equation

Phase Space
Hamiltonian Mechanics — The Legendre Transform and Phase Flow
The Hamiltonian H(q,p) = p·q̇ − L(q, q̇) is the Legendre transform of L with p = ∂L/∂q̇. Hamilton's equations: dq/dt = ∂H/∂p, dp/dt = −∂H/∂q. The flow on phase space (q,p) is symplectic — it preserves the 2-form dp∧dq (Liouville's theorem). The Hamilton-Jacobi equation ∂S/∂t + H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) = 0 is a first-order PDE for the action function S. If we can solve H-J, we integrate Hamilton's equations. The H-J equation is the classical limit of the Schrödinger equation. The principle of least action (Hamilton's principle): the true path of a mechanical system is a stationary point of the action integral. The Euler-Lagrange and Hamiltonian formulations are equivalent — the Legendre transform is the bridge.
Hamiltonian mechanics is geometry on phase space — symplectic structure, conservation of phase space volume, and the deep connection to quantum mechanics via H-J.
Part 4

⚡ Direct Methods and Modern Variational Theory

Existence
Direct Method — Existence of Minimizers in Sobolev Spaces
The direct method (Tonelli, Hilbert) proves existence of minimizers without solving EL: (1) Take a minimizing sequence y_n; (2) Show y_n is bounded in a suitable space (e.g., W^{1,p}); (3) Extract a weakly convergent subsequence; (4) Show the functional is lower semicontinuous. Convexity of L in y' (L_{p_i p_j} ≥ 0) gives lower semicontinuity. The space of functions of bounded variation (BV) handles functionals with jumps. Minimal surfaces minimize area: the Euler-Lagrange equation gives the mean curvature zero condition. The Plateau problem (find minimal surface with given boundary) was solved by Douglas and Rado (Fields Medal, 1936). Γ-convergence (De Giorgi) studies limits of variational problems — used in homogenization and phase transitions. The Mountain Pass Theorem and Palais-Smale condition find critical points of non-convex functionals.
The direct method gives existence of minimizers through compactness and convexity — you don't need to solve the equation to know the optimum exists.

Second Variation and Stability of Extremals

The Euler–Lagrange equation gives a necessary condition for a functional to have an extremum, but it does not distinguish between minima, maxima, or saddle points. The second variation δ²J provides this discrimination. For a functional J[y] = ∫ₐᵇ L(x, y, y') dx with a stationary path y₀ satisfying the Euler–Lagrange equation, consider a variation y = y₀ + εη where η(a)=η(b)=0. Expanding J to second order in ε yields δ²J = ½∫ₐᵇ (Lyy η² + 2Lyy' η η' + Ly'y' η'²) dx. The quadratic form Q[η] = ∫ₐᵇ (P η'² + Q η²) dx with P = Ly'y' and Q = Lyy − d/dx Lyy' must be positive definite for a strict local minimum. Legendre's condition requires P(x) > 0 for all x ∈ [a,b]; if P vanishes at some point, the extremal may be a weak minimum only. Jacobi's condition strengthens this by demanding that the Jacobi accessory equation −d/dx(P u') + Q u = 0 has no conjugate points in (a,b]. A conjugate point is a value c ∈ (a,b] where a nontrivial solution u vanishes at both a and c. The presence of a conjugate point indicates that the second variation can be made negative, so the stationary path is not a minimum. These conditions are essential in problems such as the brachistochrone, where the cycloid is a true minimum only up to the first conjugate point. In optimal control, the second variation underpins the sufficient conditions for optimality and the design of numerical shooting methods.

Corner Conditions and Weierstrass–Erdmann Conditions

Classical variational theory assumes the extremal y(x) is continuously differentiable. Many practical problems, however, admit extremals with corners — points where y' has a jump discontinuity. At such a corner x = c, the Euler–Lagrange equation holds on each smooth segment, but additional transversality conditions must be satisfied. The Weierstrass–Erdmann corner conditions state that the quantities ∂L/∂y' and L − y' ∂L/∂y' are continuous across the corner. Equivalently, the momentum p = ∂L/∂y' and the Hamiltonian H = y' p − L do not jump. These conditions follow from requiring the first variation to vanish for variations that are allowed to shift the corner location. If L does not depend explicitly on x, the Beltrami identity L − y' ∂L/∂y' = constant holds globally, so the Hamiltonian is automatically continuous. If L depends on x, the continuity of H imposes a nontrivial relation linking the slopes on either side of the corner. Corner conditions appear in problems of refraction (Fermat's principle with piecewise constant refractive index), in the shape of a hanging chain with a point mass, and in optimal control with state constraints where the optimal trajectory may have a kink. The Weierstrass excess function E(x, y, y', p) = L(x, y, p) − L(x, y, y') − (p − y') ∂L/∂y'|y' provides a further necessary condition: E ≥ 0 for a minimum (Weierstrass condition). This condition is stronger than Legendre's and can detect minima that are not weak minima.

Worked Example: Corner in a Refraction Problem
A light ray travels from point A(0,0) in medium 1 (refractive index n₁) to point B(d, h) in medium 2 (refractive index n₂), crossing the interface y = 0 at x = c. The travel time functional is T = ∫ n(y) √(1 + y'²) dx. Find the condition that determines the crossing point c.
The Lagrangian in each medium is Lᵢ = nᵢ √(1 + y'²). Since L does not depend explicitly on x, the Hamiltonian H = y' ∂L/∂y' − L = −nᵢ / √(1 + y'²) is constant along each segment. At the interface y = 0, the corner conditions require continuity of ∂L/∂y' = nᵢ y' / √(1 + y'²) and of H. Continuity of ∂L/∂y' gives n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, which is Snell's law. Continuity of H yields the same relation because H = −nᵢ cos θᵢ. Thus the crossing point c is determined by geometry: tan θ₁ = c / 0? Actually A is at (0,0) on the interface, so the ray in medium 1 starts at the interface; the problem reduces to finding the angle in medium 2 that reaches B. The condition is n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ with tan θ₂ = (d − c)/h. This determines c uniquely.
Exam Strategy: Corner Conditions
When a problem asks for the optimal path with a possible kink, first write the Lagrangian on each smooth region. Apply the Euler–Lagrange equation on each region, then impose continuity of ∂L/∂y' and L − y' ∂L/∂y' at the junction. If the Lagrangian is independent of x, the Hamiltonian continuity is automatic; otherwise it gives an extra equation. Remember that the Weierstrass excess function must be non‑negative for a minimum — check it if the question asks for sufficiency.

Field Theory and the Jacobi Condition

A field of extremals is a one‑parameter family of solutions to the Euler–Lagrange equation that fills a region of the (x, y) plane without intersections. If a field exists, the functional can be written as a line integral of a exact differential, and the Hilbert invariant integral I = ∫ (L dx + (∂L/∂y') dy) is path‑independent. The existence of a field is equivalent to the absence of conjugate points along the extremal. The Jacobi condition states that a stationary path gives a weak minimum if and only if the interval (a, b] contains no point conjugate to a. Conjugate points are zeros of the solution u(x) of the Jacobi accessory equation −d/dx(P u') + Q u = 0 with u(a)=0, u'(a)=1. The first conjugate point marks the loss of optimality; beyond it, the second variation can be made negative by a suitable variation. In the calculus of variations on manifolds, the Jacobi equation governs the behavior of geodesic deviations, and conjugate points correspond to focal points of the exponential map. The Morse index theorem relates the number of conjugate points (counted with multiplicity) to the number of negative eigenvalues of the second variation, providing a topological count of instability directions. Field theory also underlies the method of characteristics for first‑order PDEs and the construction of complete integrals of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

Optimal Control and Pontryagin's Maximum Principle

Optimal control extends the calculus of variations to systems governed by differential equations ẋ = f(x, u, t) with control u(t) constrained to a set U. The problem is to minimize a cost functional J = φ(x(t_f), t_f) + ∫₀t_f L(x, u, t) dt subject to boundary conditions. Pontryagin's Maximum Principle (1962) provides necessary conditions that generalize the Euler–Lagrange equations. Introduce the Hamiltonian H(x, p, u, t) = p·f(x, u, t) − L(x, u, t) where p(t) is the costate (adjoint) vector. The principle states that along an optimal trajectory (x*, u*), there exists a nontrivial costate p(t) such that: (1) ẋ* = ∂H/∂p, (2) ṗ = −∂H/∂x, (3) H(x*, p, u*, t) = maxu∈U H(x*, p, u, t) for almost all t, and (4) transversality conditions at the endpoints. The maximization condition replaces the stationarity condition ∂H/∂u = 0 when U has interior; if the maximum occurs on the boundary of U, the optimal control may be bang‑bang (switching between extreme values). The costate p(t) corresponds to the Lagrange multiplier in the variational formulation, and the Hamiltonian is constant if the problem is autonomous. Pontryagin's principle is the cornerstone of modern optimal control, underpinning applications in aerospace trajectory optimization, economics (optimal growth), and robotics. The principle also leads to the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation in dynamic programming, providing a sufficient condition for optimality via viscosity solutions.

Variational Principles in General Relativity

Einstein's field equations derive from a variational principle: the Einstein–Hilbert action S = (1/16πG) ∫ R √−g d⁴x + S_matter, where R is the Ricci scalar, g the metric determinant, and S_matter the matter action. Varying the metric gμν yields the field equations Gμν = 8πG Tμν. The action is invariant under diffeomorphisms, and Noether's theorem applied to this symmetry gives the covariant conservation law ∇_μ Tμν = 0. The geodesic equation for a test particle follows from the principle of extremal proper time δ∫ √(−gμν dx^μ dx^ν) = 0, which is a variational problem on a curved manifold. The second variation of the proper time functional leads to the Jacobi equation for geodesic deviation, describing tidal forces. In quantum gravity approaches, the path integral over metrics generalizes the classical variational principle. The ADM formalism casts general relativity as a Hamiltonian system with constraints, where the Hamiltonian is a linear combination of the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints, reflecting the diffeomorphism invariance. The study of critical points of the Einstein–Hilbert action on manifolds with boundary requires the Gibbons–Hawking–York boundary term to make the variational principle well‑posed. These variational structures unify gravity with other gauge theories and provide the mathematical framework for black hole thermodynamics and the holographic principle.

Variational Methods for Elliptic Partial Differential Equations

The calculus of variations provides a natural framework for the existence and regularity theory of solutions to elliptic partial differential equations. A typical model problem is the Dirichlet problem for the Poisson equation −Δu = f in Ω, u = g on ∂Ω. Its weak formulation seeks u ∈ H¹(Ω) with u − g ∈ H₀¹(Ω) such that ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx = ∫_Ω f v dx for all v ∈ H₀¹(Ω). This is precisely the Euler–Lagrange equation of the quadratic functional J(u) = ½∫_Ω |∇u|² dx − ∫_Ω f u dx. The direct method of the calculus of variations shows that a minimizer exists because J is coercive and weakly lower semicontinuous on the affine space g + H₀¹(Ω). The Lax–Milgram theorem gives an alternative proof via the Riesz representation theorem, but the variational viewpoint extends naturally to nonlinear problems such as the p‑Laplacian −div(|∇u|p−2∇u) = f, where the associated functional J_p(u) = (1/p)∫_Ω |∇u|^p dx − ∫_Ω f u dx is strictly convex for p > 1. Minimizers of J_p are weak solutions, and regularity theory (De Giorgi–Nash–Moser for p = 2, DiBenedetto–Manfredi for p ≠ 2) yields Hölder continuity of ∇u. The mountain‑pass theorem and linking arguments provide critical points for functionals that are not globally convex, leading to multiple solutions for semilinear equations −Δu = λu + |u|q−2u. These variational techniques form the backbone of modern nonlinear analysis and are indispensable for proving existence of solutions to geometric PDEs such as the prescribed mean curvature equation and the Yamabe problem.

In the context of systems, the Euler–Lagrange equations become a coupled system of elliptic equations. For a vector‑valued map u: Ω → ℝ^m, the functional J(u) = ∫_Ω W(∇u) dx with a polyconvex stored‑energy function W leads to the equilibrium equations of nonlinear elasticity. The existence of minimizers relies on the Ball–Murat theorem on weak continuity of determinants, which ensures that the cofactor and determinant of ∇u are weakly continuous under appropriate growth conditions. This interplay between variational structure and compensated compactness is a hallmark of the modern theory.

Relaxation, Gamma‑Convergence, and the Direct Method in Nonconvex Problems

When the integrand f(x, u, ∇u) is not convex in the gradient variable, the direct method may fail because the functional is not weakly lower semicontinuous. The remedy is relaxation: one replaces the original functional by its lower semicontinuous envelope, which can be characterized by the quasiconvex envelope of the integrand. For integral functionals of the form I(u) = ∫_Ω f(∇u) dx, the relaxed functional is Ī(u) = ∫_Ω Qf(∇u) dx, where Qf is the greatest quasiconvex function bounded above by f. This result, due to Morrey and later refined by Acerbi–Fusco, shows that minimizers of the relaxed problem correspond to minimizing sequences of the original problem that may develop fine‑scale oscillations (microstructures). Gamma‑convergence, introduced by De Giorgi and Franzoni, provides a robust notion of convergence for variational problems. A sequence of functionals F_n Γ‑converges to F if (i) for every u_n → u, liminf F_n(u_n) ≥ F(u), and (ii) for every u there exists a recovery sequence u_n → u with limsup F_n(u_n) ≤ F(u). Gamma‑convergence preserves minimizers: if u_n minimizes F_n and u_n → u, then u minimizes F. This theory underpins homogenization (where F_ε(u) = ∫_Ω f(x/ε, ∇u) dx Γ‑converges to a homogenized functional with an effective integrand obtained by a cell problem), phase‑transition models (Modica–Mortola functionals Γ‑converge to perimeter functionals), and discrete‑to‑continuum limits in lattice systems. The combination of relaxation and Gamma‑convergence allows one to pass to the limit in nonconvex variational problems while retaining information about the microstructure of minimizing sequences.

A practical illustration is the derivation of the effective behavior of a composite material with rapidly oscillating coefficients. The energy functional E_ε(u) = ∫_Ω a(x/ε)|∇u|² dx Γ‑converges to E₀(u) = ∫_Ω A^hom ∇u·∇u dx, where the homogenized tensor A^hom is computed by solving a periodic cell problem. The Gamma‑limit captures the macroscopic response without resolving the microscopic oscillations, and the recovery sequence constructs the corrector that accounts for the fine structure. This variational approach to homogenization is more flexible than classical two‑scale expansion because it does not require smoothness of the coefficients and works directly in the Sobolev space setting.

Gamma‑Convergence of a Singularly Perturbed Functional
Consider the family of functionals F_ε(u) = ∫01 (ε u'² + (1/ε) W(u)) dx defined for u ∈ H¹(0,1) with W(u) = (u²−1)². Determine the Gamma‑limit as ε → 0 and describe the limiting minimizers.
The Modica–Mortola theorem states that F_ε Γ‑converges (with respect to the L¹ topology) to the functional F₀(u) = c₀ Per({u = 1}) if u ∈ BV(0,1; {−1,1}) and F₀(u) = +∞ otherwise, where c₀ = ∫−11 √(2W(s)) ds = 4/3. The perimeter term counts the number of jumps between the two wells −1 and 1. Minimizers of F_ε develop sharp transition layers of width O(ε) connecting the wells; in the limit they become piecewise constant functions taking only the values −1 and 1, with the total variation equal to the number of interfaces times 2. The Gamma‑limit captures the energy cost of each interface, and any sequence of minimizers converges to a function that minimizes the perimeter, i.e., a function with the fewest possible jumps compatible with the boundary conditions.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Gamma‑limit of a singularly perturbed functional, first identify the potential wells of the non‑convex part. Compute the optimal profile by solving the associated ODE ε u' = √(2W(u)) and evaluate the action integral to obtain the surface tension constant. Then state the limiting functional in terms of the perimeter (or total variation) of the set where u equals each well. Remember that Gamma‑convergence is defined with respect to a specific topology; always specify the topology (usually L¹ or weak H¹) in your answer.

Variational Inequalities, Obstacle Problems, and Free Boundaries

Many physical problems lead to variational inequalities rather than equalities. The classical obstacle problem asks for the equilibrium position of an elastic membrane constrained to lie above a given obstacle ψ. The solution minimizes the Dirichlet integral J(u) = ½∫_Ω |∇u|² dx over the convex set K = {v ∈ H¹(Ω) : v ≥ ψ a.e., v = g on ∂Ω}. The Euler–Lagrange condition becomes a variational inequality: find u ∈ K such that ∫_Ω ∇u·∇(v−u) dx ≥ 0 for all v ∈ K. This is equivalent to the complementarity system −Δu ≥ 0, u ≥ ψ, (−Δu)(u−ψ) = 0. The free boundary ∂{u > ψ} ∩ Ω is the unknown interface where the membrane detaches from the obstacle. Regularity theory (Caffarelli, 1977) shows that the free boundary is analytic except possibly at a set of singular points of lower dimension. The same structure appears in the Stefan problem for phase change, where the latent heat introduces a variational inequality for the enthalpy. In financial mathematics, American option pricing leads to a parabolic variational inequality (the Black–Scholes operator with an early‑exercise constraint). The discrete analogue yields the binomial tree method. Numerical approximation of variational inequalities uses finite element methods combined with active‑set strategies or penalization (the latter replaces the constraint by a penalty term (1/ε)∫ (u−ψ)₋² dx). Convergence rates depend on the regularity of the free boundary; adaptive mesh refinement near the free boundary restores optimal rates.

For systems, the Signorini problem (contact elasticity) leads to a vector‑valued variational inequality with a non‑penetration condition on the contact surface. The existence of solutions follows from the Stampacchia theorem on monotone operators. The free boundary in contact problems is the contact set, and its regularity is a deep topic connected to the regularity of solutions to the thin obstacle problem. These variational inequalities are unified by the theory of maximal monotone operators and the subdifferential of convex functionals, providing a powerful abstract framework for both elliptic and parabolic problems.

Numerical Approximation of Variational Problems: Finite Element and Discrete Calculus of Variations

The practical computation of minimizers relies on discretizing the variational problem. The finite element method (FEM) approximates the infinite‑dimensional space V by a finite‑dimensional subspace V_h ⊂ V spanned by piecewise polynomial basis functions on a mesh of size h. The discrete problem seeks u_h ∈ V_h minimizing J(u_h). Céa's lemma guarantees that for convex quadratic functionals, the error in the energy norm satisfies ‖u−u_h‖_V ≤ C infv_h∈V_h ‖u−v_h‖_V, yielding optimal convergence rates O(h^k) for polynomials of degree k provided the exact solution has sufficient regularity. For nonlinear problems, the discrete Euler–Lagrange equations form a system of nonlinear algebraic equations solved by Newton‑type methods. The discrete calculus of variations (also called variational integrators) constructs numerical schemes for Hamiltonian systems by discretizing the action principle directly. A discrete Lagrangian L_d(q_k, qk+1) approximates the action integral over one time step. The discrete Euler–Lagrange equations D₂L_d(qk−1, q_k) + D₁L_d(q_k, qk+1) = 0 define a symplectic map that preserves the discrete symplectic form and, by a discrete Noether theorem, exactly conserves momentum maps associated with symmetries. This geometric structure preservation leads to excellent long‑time energy behavior compared to standard Runge–Kutta methods. For PDEs, space‑time finite element methods and discontinuous Galerkin schemes can be derived from a space‑time variational principle, yielding adaptive algorithms that control both spatial and temporal errors. The combination of a posteriori error estimation (based on the dual weighted residual method) with goal‑oriented mesh refinement allows efficient computation of quantities of interest such as drag coefficients in fluid dynamics or stress intensity factors in fracture mechanics.

In the context of nonconvex problems, the discrete functional may have spurious local minima that do not correspond to minimizers of the continuous problem. Gamma‑convergence of the discrete functionals to the continuous one ensures that global minimizers converge, but practical algorithms may get trapped in local minima. Techniques such as convexification‑relaxation, continuation methods, or stochastic optimization (simulated annealing) are employed to approach the global minimum. The discrete‑to‑continuum Gamma‑convergence theory also provides a rigorous foundation for atomistic‑to‑continuum coupling methods (e.g., the quasicontinuum method), where the energy of a crystal lattice is approximated by a continuum functional in regions of smooth deformation and by the full atomistic model near defects.

Variational Principles in Quantum Mechanics: Path Integrals and Semiclassical Analysis

The Feynman path integral formulation of quantum mechanics expresses the transition amplitude ⟨x_f, t_f | x_i, t_i⟩ as a formal sum over all paths x(t) connecting the endpoints, weighted by exp(i S[x]/ℏ) where S[x] = ∫ L(x, ẋ, t) dt is the classical action. Although the measure on the space of paths is not a countably additive measure, the path integral can be given rigorous meaning via Wiener measure for imaginary time (Euclidean path integrals) or through the theory of Fresnel integrals. The stationary phase approximation (ℏ → 0) recovers the classical principle of least action: the dominant contributions come from paths that make the action stationary, i.e., solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equations. The next order yields the Van Vleck–Pauli–Morette determinant, which encodes the stability of the classical trajectory and leads to the semiclassical propagator. Gutzwiller's trace formula expresses the quantum density of states as a sum over classical periodic orbits, linking spectral statistics to classical chaos. In quantum field theory, the path integral over field configurations is the starting point for perturbation theory (Feynman diagrams) and non‑perturbative methods such as instanton calculus and lattice gauge theory. The Euclidean action for a scalar field φ is S_E[φ] = ∫ (½|∇φ|² + V(φ)) d⁴x, and the partition function Z = ∫ Dφ e−S_E[φ]/ℏ is a functional integral of the type studied in the calculus of variations. The rigorous construction of such measures for interacting fields in four dimensions remains an open problem (the Yang–Mills mass gap). Nevertheless, the variational structure underlies the renormalization group, where effective actions at different scales are obtained by integrating out high‑frequency modes, a process that can be viewed as a sequence of Gamma‑convergences in the space of probability measures.

Semiclassical analysis also provides a bridge between quantum mechanics and symplectic geometry. The WKB ansatz ψ(x) = a(x) ei S(x)/ℏ leads to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for the phase S and transport equations for the amplitude a. The Maslov index corrects the phase at caustics where the projection of the Lagrangian manifold onto configuration space becomes singular. This geometric quantization program, developed by Hörmander, Duistermaat, and Guillemin–Sternberg, shows that the spectrum of a quantum Hamiltonian can be approximated by the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization conditions on invariant tori of the classical integrable system. For chaotic systems, random matrix theory predicts universal spectral fluctuations, and the variational principle for the quantum action (the Gutzwiller trace formula) provides a semiclassical explanation. These deep connections illustrate how the calculus of variations, originally a tool for classical mechanics, permeates the foundations of quantum theory.

  • Remember that each advanced variational framework demands verification of its specific structural hypotheses before conclusions can be drawn.

Variational Principles in Continuum Mechanics: Elasticity and Fluid Dynamics

The calculus of variations provides the natural language for formulating the equilibrium and evolution equations of continuous media. In nonlinear elasticity the stored‑energy functional W(∇u) = ∫Ω W(∇u(x)) dx is minimized over admissible deformations u : Ω → ℝ³ that satisfy prescribed boundary conditions. The first variation yields the Euler–Lagrange system Div ∂W/∂F = 0, where F = ∇u is the deformation gradient. Polyconvexity of W (Ball, 1977) guarantees weak lower semicontinuity and thus existence of minimizers in the Sobolev space W¹ᵖ(Ω;ℝ³) for p > 3. In incompressible fluid dynamics the action functional ∫ (½|v|² − p(div v)) dxdt, with p a Lagrange multiplier enforcing div v = 0, leads via the Euler–Poincaré reduction to the Euler equations for ideal fluids. The Arnold interpretation identifies geodesics on the group of volume‑preserving diffeomorphisms with fluid trajectories, linking stability of steady flows to the second variation of the kinetic energy on the Lie algebra. For viscous fluids the Navier–Stokes equations arise as the gradient flow of the dissipation functional ∫ |∇v|² dx in the Wasserstein‑type metric induced by the kinetic energy, a perspective that underlies modern proofs of existence for weak solutions (Lions, 1996). These variational structures also guide numerical discretizations: mixed finite element methods for elasticity respect the inf‑sup condition derived from the saddle‑point formulation, while variational integrators for fluids preserve the symplectic structure of the underlying Hamiltonian system.

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Evolution Equations: Gradient Flows and Minimizing Movements

Many nonlinear parabolic and hyperbolic equations can be recast as gradient flows of an energy functional E in a metric space (X, d). The De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme constructs a discrete trajectory by solving the recursive minimization problem uk+1 ∈ argminu∈X { E(u) + (1/2τ) d²(u, uk) } for a time step τ > 0. As τ → 0 the piecewise constant interpolants converge to a curve u(t) satisfying the energy‑dissipation inequality E(u(t)) + ½∫₀ᵗ |u'|²(s) ds + ½∫₀ᵗ |∂E|²(u(s)) ds ≤ E(u(0)), where |u'| is the metric derivative and |∂E| the local slope. This framework, developed by Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré (2005), applies to the heat equation as the gradient flow of the Dirichlet energy in L², to the porous medium equation as the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy in the Wasserstein space (Otto, 2001), and to the Allen–Cahn equation as the L²‑gradient flow of the Ginzburg–Landau functional. The minimizing movement scheme also provides a constructive proof of existence for curves of maximal slope in geodesically convex metric spaces, and it yields a natural time‑discretization that preserves energy dissipation at the discrete level. In the context of geometric evolution, the mean curvature flow of hypersurfaces is the L²‑gradient flow of the area functional; its minimizing movement approximation (Almgren–Taylor–Wang, 1993) leads to the thresholding scheme of Merriman–Bence–Osher, which is widely used in image processing. The theory extends to non‑convex energies via the notion of λ‑convexity along generalized geodesics, allowing treatment of functionals with double‑well potentials that appear in phase‑field models.

Worked Example: Minimizing Movement for the Heat Equation
Let Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Consider the Dirichlet energy E(u) = ½∫Ω |∇u|² dx defined on H¹₀(Ω) with the L² metric. Show that the minimizing movement scheme with time step τ produces the implicit Euler discretization of the heat equation.
For a given uk ∈ H¹₀(Ω), the minimization problem is uk+1 = argminv∈H¹₀ { ½∫|∇v|² + (1/2τ)∫|v − uk|² }. The first variation yields ∫∇uk+1·∇φ + (1/τ)∫(uk+1 − uk)φ = 0 for all φ ∈ H¹₀(Ω). This is precisely the weak form of ( uk+1 − uk )/τ − Δuk+1 = 0, i.e. the implicit Euler scheme for ∂tu = Δu. Hence the minimizing movement scheme recovers the standard time discretization of the heat equation.

When asked to identify a gradient flow structure, first write the energy functional and the metric. Then compute the first variation of the energy and the metric derivative. The Euler–Lagrange equation of the minimizing movement scheme will match the target PDE if the metric is chosen correctly. Practice with the heat equation, porous medium equation, and Allen–Cahn to recognize the pattern.

Variational Principles in Optimal Transport: Monge–Kantorovich and Wasserstein Gradient Flows

Optimal transport provides a variational formulation for moving mass distributions with minimal cost. Given probability measures μ, ν on ℝⁿ, the Monge problem seeks a map T pushing μ forward to ν that minimizes ∫ |T(x) − x|² dμ(x). Kantorovich’s relaxation replaces maps by transport plans π ∈ Π(μ, ν) and minimizes ∫ |x − y|² dπ(x, y). The minimal value defines the squared Wasserstein‑2 distance W₂²(μ, ν). The Benamou–Brenier dynamic formulation expresses W₂² as the infimum of ∫₀¹ ∫ |vₜ|² dμₜ dt over curves (μₜ) connecting μ to ν with velocity fields vₜ satisfying the continuity equation ∂ₜμₜ + div(μₜ vₜ) = 0. This identifies the Wasserstein space as a formal Riemannian manifold where the tangent space at μ consists of gradients ∇φ with φ ∈ H¹(μ). Gradient flows in this geometry correspond to solutions of ∂ₜμ = div(μ ∇(δE/δμ)), where δE/δμ is the first variation of the energy E. The heat equation is the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy E(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dx (μ = ρ dx) in W₂, while the porous medium equation ∂ₜρ = Δρᵐ arises from the internal energy ∫ ρᵐ/(m−1) dx. The JKO scheme (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto, 1998) constructs these flows via the minimizing movement τ‑step: μk+1 = argminν { E(ν) + (1/2τ) W₂²(ν, μk) }. This variational time discretization preserves the dissipation inequality and provides a powerful tool for proving existence, uniqueness, and long‑time asymptotics for a wide class of diffusion equations, including those with non‑local interactions and confinement potentials.

Variational Methods for Inverse Problems: Regularization and Tikhonov Functionals

Inverse problems ask to recover an unknown parameter u from noisy observations y = A(u) + η, where A is a forward operator between Banach spaces. The ill‑posedness (non‑continuity of A⁻¹) is overcome by variational regularization: minimize the Tikhonov functional Jα(u) = ½‖A(u) − y‖²Y + α R(u) over u ∈ X. The fidelity term measures data misfit, while the regularizer R encodes prior information (smoothness, sparsity, piecewise constancy). Classical choices are R(u) = ½‖u‖²H¹ (Tikhonov), R(u) = ‖∇u‖L¹ (total variation, Rudin–Osher–Fatemi), and R(u) = ‖u‖ℓ¹ (ℓ¹‑regularization for sparse recovery). Existence of minimizers follows from the direct method if R is coercive and lower semicontinuous and A is continuous. Convergence of regularized solutions uα to a true solution as noise level δ → 0 and α = α(δ) → 0 with δ²/α → 0 is guaranteed under a source condition (e.g., existence of a Lagrange multiplier in the range of A*). For nonlinear A, the tangential cone condition ensures stability of the iterates in iterative regularization methods such as the Landweber iteration or the iteratively regularized Gauss–Newton method. Bregman distances associated with convex R provide a natural tool for analyzing convergence rates in Banach spaces. Modern developments include variational regularization with learned regularizers (plug‑and‑play, deep equilibrium models) where R is replaced by an implicit prior defined by a denoising network, and the resulting optimization is tackled by proximal algorithms whose fixed points correspond to stationary points of a generalized Tikhonov functional.

Stochastic Calculus of Variations: Malliavin Calculus and Applications

Malliavin calculus extends the calculus of variations to functionals of Brownian motion, providing a differential calculus on the Wiener space (Ω, ℱ, ℙ). For a smooth cylindrical functional F = f(W(h₁), …, W(hₙ)) with hᵢ ∈ L²([0,T]), the Malliavin derivative DtF = Σᵢ ∂ᵢf(W(h₁), …, W(hₙ)) hᵢ(t) is an element of L²(Ω × [0,T]). The operator D is closable from L²(Ω) into L²(Ω; L²([0,T])), defining the Sobolev space 𝔻¹,². Its adjoint δ (the Skorokhod integral) satisfies the duality relation 𝔼[F δ(u)] = 𝔼[⟨DF, u⟩L²] for u ∈ Dom(δ). The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator L = −δD has a spectral decomposition with eigenvalues −k, and the Meyer inequalities give equivalence of norms in 𝔻ᵏ,ᵖ. A central result is the integration‑by‑parts formula: for F ∈ 𝔻¹,² and u ∈ Dom(δ), 𝔼[F δ(u)] = 𝔼[∫₀ᵀ DtF ut dt]. This yields the Clark–Ocone representation F = 𝔼[F] + ∫₀ᵀ 𝔼[DtF | ℱt] dWt, which is the martingale representation theorem in a constructive form. Applications include the computation of Greeks in mathematical finance (Δ, Γ, Vega) via Malliavin weights, the proof of Hörmander’s theorem on hypoellipticity of diffusion operators using the Malliavin covariance matrix, and the analysis of stochastic partial differential equations where the solution’s law is shown to be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. The calculus also underpins the Stein–Malliavin method for normal approximation of Wiener functionals, giving quantitative bounds in the Wasserstein distance via the fourth moment theorem (Nualart–Peccati, 2005).

Variational Methods for Fractional and Nonlocal Operators

Fractional calculus of variations extends the classical Euler–Lagrange framework to functionals involving nonlocal operators such as the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^s with s ∈ (0,1). The typical energy functional reads J(u) = ½ ∫∫ℝⁿ×ℝⁿ |u(x)−u(y)|² / |x−y|n+2s dx dy − ∫_Ω f u dx, defined on the fractional Sobolev space H^s₀(Ω). The first variation yields the nonlocal Euler–Lagrange equation (−Δ)^s u = f in Ω, u = 0 in ℝⁿ∖Ω. Existence and uniqueness follow from the Lax–Milgram theorem on H^s₀(Ω) because the Gagliardo seminorm [u]H^s² is coercive and the bilinear form is continuous. Regularity theory, developed by Caffarelli, Silvestre, and others, shows that solutions are C^α up to the boundary for α < s, and the boundary behaviour exhibits a characteristic s‑Hölder singularity. The Caffarelli–Silvestre extension technique embeds the nonlocal problem into a local degenerate elliptic equation in ℝⁿ⁺¹₊: div(y1−2s ∇U) = 0 with Neumann data proportional to (−Δ)^s u. This extension converts variational problems for fractional operators into weighted local problems, enabling the use of classical tools such as De Giorgi–Nash–Moser estimates and monotonicity formulas. Applications include phase‑field models with nonlocal interactions (the fractional Allen–Cahn equation), image denoising via fractional total variation, and anomalous diffusion in heterogeneous media. The Gamma‑convergence of fractional perimeters to the classical perimeter as s → 1⁻ and to a multiple of the total variation as s → 0⁺ provides a rigorous bridge between nonlocal and local variational models.

Variational Principles in Geometric Measure Theory: Minimal Surfaces and the Plateau Problem

Geometric measure theory furnishes a variational framework for the Plateau problem: find a surface of least area spanning a given boundary. The area functional A(Σ) = ℋⁿ(Σ) is defined on rectifiable n‑currents or integral varifolds in ℝn+1. The direct method requires compactness in the space of integral currents with bounded mass and boundary mass; Federer–Fleming compactness theorem guarantees a subsequential limit that is an integral current. Almgren’s regularity theory (1968) proves that area‑minimizing integral currents are smooth except for a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most n−2. For codimension one, the singular set is empty for n ≤ 6 (Simons’ theorem) and discrete for n = 7 (Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti). The first variation formula δA(Σ)(X) = ∫_Σ H·X dℋⁿ yields the minimal surface equation H = 0, where H is the mean curvature vector. The second variation leads to the stability operator L = Δ_Σ + |A|², where A is the second fundamental form. Stability (non‑negative second variation) is equivalent to the existence of a positive Jacobi field. The Morse index of a minimal surface counts the number of negative eigenvalues of L and governs the topology of the moduli space. Recent breakthroughs include the solution of the Willmore problem (minimizing ∫ H²) by Marques–Neves (2014) using min‑max theory, and the proof of the Freedman–He–Wang conjecture on the existence of infinitely many closed minimal hypersurfaces in positively curved manifolds. In the parametric setting, the Douglas–Rado approach minimizes the Dirichlet energy ∫ |∇u|² over maps u: D → ℝ³ with prescribed boundary, yielding conformal minimal immersions. The interplay between varifold compactness, Allard’s regularity theorem, and the monotonicity formula underpins the modern existence theory for minimal submanifolds with free boundaries.

Worked Example: First Variation of Fractional Dirichlet Energy
Let Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ be bounded, s ∈ (0,1). Define the fractional Dirichlet energy E_s(u) = ½ ∫∫ℝⁿ×ℝⁿ (u(x)−u(y))² / |x−y|n+2s dx dy for u ∈ H^s₀(Ω). Compute the first variation δE_s(u; φ) for a test function φ ∈ C_c^∞(Ω) and deduce the Euler–Lagrange equation.
Consider u + ε φ. Then E_s(u+ε φ) = ½ ∫∫ (u(x)−u(y)+ε(φ(x)−φ(y)))² / |x−y|n+2s dx dy. Differentiate with respect to ε at ε = 0: δE_s(u; φ) = ∫∫ (u(x)−u(y))(φ(x)−φ(y)) / |x−y|n+2s dx dy. Split the integral: ∫ φ(x) [∫ (u(x)−u(y)) / |x−y|n+2s dy] dx − ∫ φ(y) [∫ (u(x)−u(y)) / |x−y|n+2s dx] dy. By symmetry of the kernel, the two inner integrals are equal up to sign. Hence δE_s(u; φ) = 2 ∫_Ω φ(x) (−Δ)^s u(x) dx, where (−Δ)^s u(x) = Cn,s P.V. ∫ℝⁿ (u(x)−u(y)) / |x−y|n+2s dy. Setting δE_s(u; φ) = 0 for all φ yields (−Δ)^s u = 0 in Ω, the fractional Laplace equation.

Identify the kernel’s homogeneity (e.g., |x−y|−n−2s) to name the operator (fractional Laplacian, fractional p‑Laplacian). In multiple‑choice questions, the correct Euler–Lagrange equation will be the one where the test function appears linearly inside a single integral against the nonlocal operator applied to the unknown. Variational Methods for Shape Optimization and Topological Derivatives

Shape optimization seeks a domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ minimizing a cost functional J(Ω) = ∫_Ω f(u_Ω) dx + Per(Ω) subject to a state equation, typically −Δ u_Ω = g in Ω, u_Ω = 0 on ∂Ω. The shape derivative (Eulerian derivative) dJ(Ω; V) = limt→0 (J(Φ_t(Ω))−J(Ω))/t for a deformation field V is computed via the material derivative method or the adjoint state technique. The adjoint p solves −Δ p = ∂_u f(u_Ω) in Ω, p = 0 on ∂Ω, and the shape gradient takes the boundary integral form dJ(Ω; V) = ∫∂Ω (∂_n u_Ω ∂_n p − f(u_Ω) + κ) V·n dσ, where κ is the mean curvature. This yields a descent direction V = −(∂_n u ∂_n p − f + κ) n for gradient‑based algorithms (level‑set, phase‑field). Topological derivatives extend the concept to nucleation of a small hole B_ε(x₀) ⊂ Ω: D_T J(x₀) = limε→0 (J(Ω∖B_ε)−J(Ω)) / (εⁿ ω_n) for n ≥ 2, and a logarithmic scaling in n = 2. For the Dirichlet energy J(Ω) = ½ ∫_Ω |∇u|² dx − ∫_Ω f u dx, the topological derivative is D_T J(x₀) = −∇u(x₀)·∇p(x₀) + f(x₀) u(x₀) (up to a dimension‑dependent constant). This provides a rigorous criterion for hole insertion in topological optimization (e.g., the SIMP method). The combination of shape and topological derivatives leads to algorithms that can change topology, essential for structural design. Existence of optimal shapes in the class of quasi‑open sets is guaranteed by the Buttazzo–Dal Maso theorem using γ‑convergence of Dirichlet forms. Regularity of optimal shapes (C1,α boundaries) follows from the work of De Philippis–Mazzoleni (2015) under a perimeter penalisation.

Variational Approach to Bifurcation Theory and Critical Point Theory

Bifurcation of solutions to nonlinear Euler–Lagrange equations is studied via the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction and the Morse theory of the associated functional. Consider a family of functionals J_λ(u) = ½ ∫_Ω |∇u|² dx − λ ∫_Ω F(u) dx on H¹₀(Ω), where F is a C² nonlinearity. The trivial branch u ≡ 0 consists of critical points for all λ. The linearised operator at zero is L_λ = −Δ − λ F'(0). Bifurcation occurs at eigenvalues λ_k of −Δ where the kernel of L_λ is nontrivial. The Crandall–Rabinowitz theorem gives a local bifurcation curve when the eigenvalue is simple and the transversality condition ∂_λ L_λ u_k ∉ Range(L_λ) holds. Global bifurcation is captured by Rabinowitz’s global bifurcation theorem: the connected component of nontrivial solutions emanating from (λ_k,0) is either unbounded or meets another bifurcation point. Morse theory relates the topology of sublevel sets J_λ^c = {u : J_λ(u) ≤ c} to the critical groups C_q(J_λ, u) = H_q(J_λ^c ∩ U, J_λ^c ∩ U ∖ {u}) for an isolated critical point u. The Morse inequalities ∑u rank C_q(J_λ, u) t^q = ∑k β_k t^k + (1+t) Q(t) (with β_k Betti numbers of the underlying manifold) constrain the number and type of critical points. The Mountain Pass theorem (Ambrosetti–Rabinowitz) guarantees a critical point of saddle type when J_λ has a “mountain pass” geometry: J_λ(0)=0, J_λ(e) < 0 for some e, and J_λ(u) ≥ α > 0 on a sphere around 0. The linking theorem generalises this to infinite‑dimensional settings with a splitting H = V ⊕ W. Applications include the existence of multiple solutions for semilinear elliptic equations with superlinear nonlinearities, periodic solutions of Hamiltonian systems via the action functional, and the study of phase transitions in Ginzburg–Landau models where the Morse index of the vortex solutions determines their stability.

Variational Principles in Statistical Mechanics: Large Deviations and Rate Functions

The variational structure of large deviation principles (LDP) provides a bridge between probability and calculus of variations. For a sequence of random variables X_n on a Polish space 𝒳 satisfying an LDP with speed a_n → ∞ and good rate function I: 𝒳 → [0,∞], the probability of a rare event A scales as ℙ(X_n ∈ A) ≈ exp(−a_n infx∈A I(x)). The rate function I is the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the scaled cumulant generating function Λ(θ) = limn→∞ a_n⁻¹ log 𝔼[exp(a_n ⟨θ, X_n⟩)]: I(x) = supθ {⟨θ, x⟩ − Λ(θ)}. This is precisely a convex variational problem. In the context of empirical measures L_n = n⁻¹ ∑i=1^n δX_i of i.i.d. samples, Sanov’s theorem gives the rate function I(μ) = H(μ | ν) (relative entropy) for the reference law ν. The Gibbs variational principle states that for a Hamiltonian H on a configuration space, the free energy F(β) = −β⁻¹ log Z(β) equals inf_μ {∫ H dμ + β⁻¹ H(μ | ν)} where the infimum runs over probability measures μ. The minimiser is the Gibbs measure μ_β ∝ e−β H ν. This variational characterisation underpins the equivalence of ensembles in statistical mechanics. For interacting particle systems, the Donsker–Varadhan rate function for the empirical process of a Markov chain is I(μ) = supf>0 ∫ (L f)/f dμ, where L is the generator. The contraction principle maps LDPs through continuous maps, yielding variational formulas for macroscopic observables. In mean‑field models (Curie–Weiss, spherical spin glasses), the rate function becomes a finite‑dimensional variational problem whose minimisers exhibit phase transitions as the temperature parameter varies. The Γ‑convergence of the sequence of rate functions as the system size grows provides a rigorous derivation of the limiting free energy and the identification of the order parameter.

Variational Methods in Machine Learning: Variational Inference and Energy‑Based Models

Variational inference reframes Bayesian posterior approximation as an optimisation problem over a family of tractable distributions. Given a model with latent variables z and observed data x, the marginal likelihood log p(x) is bounded below by the evidence lower bound (ELBO) L(q) = 𝔼q[log p(x,z)] − 𝔼q[log q(z)], where q(z) is a variational density. Maximising L(q) is equivalent to minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(q‖p(z|x)). In mean‑field variational Bayes, q factorises as ∏i qi(zi), yielding coordinate‑ascent updates that are often available in closed form for conjugate exponential families. For non‑conjugate models, the reparameterisation gradient (Kingma & Welling, 2014) enables stochastic optimisation of the ELBO by expressing samples z = g(ε,ϕ) with ε ∼ p(ε) and differentiable g. This underpins variational autoencoders (VAEs), where the encoder outputs parameters ϕ(x) of a Gaussian q and the decoder defines p(x|z). The ELBO decomposes into a reconstruction term and a regulariser that matches the aggregate posterior to the prior.

Energy‑based models (EBMs) define an unnormalised density pθ(x) = exp(−Eθ(x))/Z(θ) with energy function Eθ. Learning maximises the log‑likelihood ∇θ log pθ(x) = −∇θEθ(x) + 𝔼pθ[∇θEθ(x)]. The intractable expectation over the model distribution is approximated by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) or by contrastive divergence, which runs a short Gibbs chain initialised at data. The variational perspective appears when a tractable proposal q is used to bound the partition function: log Z(θ) ≤ 𝔼q[Eθ] + KL(q‖p0) for a reference measure p0. This yields a variational upper bound on the negative log‑likelihood, leading to algorithms such as variational contrastive divergence and the wake‑sleep algorithm. Recent work connects EBMs with score‑based generative models, where the score ∇x log p(x) is estimated by denoising score matching, a variational problem that minimises the Fisher divergence.

In structured prediction, variational inference provides a unified framework for approximate MAP inference. The marginal polytope of a graphical model is relaxed to the local polytope, and the Bethe free energy approximates the negative log partition function. Minimising the Bethe free energy over pseudo‑marginals yields the belief propagation fixed‑point equations. For models with cycles, the convexified Bethe free energy (Wainwright & Jordan, 2008) guarantees convergence of message‑passing algorithms. These variational methods are essential for large‑scale probabilistic graphical models in computer vision and natural language processing.

Variational Principles in Information Geometry: Fisher–Rao Metric and Exponential Families

Information geometry equips the manifold of probability distributions with a Riemannian structure derived from the Fisher information matrix. For a parametric family p(x|θ) with θ ∈ Θ ⊂ ℝd, the Fisher–Rao metric is gij(θ) = 𝔼θ[∂i log p ∂j log p]. This metric is invariant under sufficient statistics and is the unique (up to scale) invariant metric on the statistical manifold (Čencov’s theorem). The geodesics of this metric correspond to curves of minimal information distance, and the squared geodesic distance locally approximates the Kullback–Leibler divergence: KL(pθ‖pθ+dθ) = ½ gij dθi dθj + o(‖dθ‖2).

Exponential families p(x|θ) = h(x) exp(θi Ti(x) − ψ(θ)) have a dually flat structure. The natural parameters θ and the expectation parameters ηi = 𝔼[Ti] are related by the Legendre transform of the log‑partition function ψ: η = ∇ψ(θ), θ = ∇φ(η) with φ(η) = supθ{θiηi − ψ(θ)}. The Fisher metric becomes the Hessian of ψ: gij = ∂i∂jψ. The α‑connections ∇(α) interpolate between the exponential connection (α = 1) and the mixture connection (α = −1); the Levi‑Civita connection corresponds to α = 0. The Pythagorean theorem holds for dual geodesics: for any p, q, r with q on the α‑geodesic from p to r, the divergence satisfies D(α)(p,r) = D(α)(p,q) + D(α)(q,r). This underpins the generalised Pythagorean theorem used in expectation‑maximisation and variational inference.

Applications include natural gradient descent, where the parameter update Δθ = −η g−1 ∇θL follows the steepest descent direction in the Fisher geometry, leading to faster convergence in neural network training. In variational inference, the natural gradient of the ELBO with respect to the variational parameters yields the mirror descent algorithm in the dual space. The information‑geometric view also clarifies the relationship between the EM algorithm and variational Bayes: the E‑step computes the projection onto the exponential family under the mixture connection, while the M‑step performs the projection under the exponential connection. These dual projections minimise the KL divergence alternately, guaranteeing monotonic increase of the likelihood.

Technique: Deriving the ELBO for a Gaussian Variational Autoencoder
Consider a VAE with prior p(z) = N(0, I) and decoder p(x|z) = N(μθ(z), σ2I). The encoder outputs qφ(z|x) = N(μφ(x), diag(σφ2(x))). Write the ELBO L(φ,θ;x) explicitly and show how the reparameterisation trick yields a Monte Carlo estimator of its gradient.
The ELBO is L = 𝔼q[log p(x|z)] − KL(q(z|x)‖p(z)). The reconstruction term: 𝔼q[log N(x; μθ(z), σ2I)] = −(D/2)log(2πσ2) − (1/(2σ2))𝔼q[‖x − μθ(z)‖2]. The KL term between two Gaussians has closed form: KL = ½ ∑j(σφ,j2 + μφ,j2 − 1 − log σφ,j2). To estimate ∇φL, sample ε ∼ N(0,I) and set z = μφ(x) + σφ(x) ⊙ ε. Then ∇φL ≈ ∇φ[log p(x|z)] − ∇φKL, where the first term is computed by back‑propagation through μθ(z) and the second term is analytic. This yields a low‑variance stochastic gradient for joint optimisation of φ and θ.

Variational Methods for Mean Field Games and Mean Field Control

Mean field games (MFGs) describe the limit of N‑player stochastic differential games as N → ∞, where each agent interacts with the empirical distribution of states. The Nash equilibrium is characterised by a coupled system: a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation for the value function u(t,x) and a Fokker–Planck (FP) equation for the population density m(t,x). Lasry & Lions (2007) showed that this system arises as the optimality conditions of a variational problem. The MFG Lagrangian is L(m,u) = ∫0T∫ℝd [½|∇u|2 + F(x,m)] m dx dt + ∫ℝd G(x,m(T)) m(T) dx, subject to the continuity equation ∂tm − div(m∇u) = 0. The first variation with respect to u yields the HJB equation −∂tu + ½|∇u|2 = F(x,m) with terminal condition u(T) = G(x,m(T)); variation with respect to m gives the FP equation. This variational structure guarantees existence of solutions under convexity of F and G in m and enables numerical methods based on convex optimisation (e.g., primal‑dual algorithms).

Mean field control (MFC) considers a single optimiser steering the distribution m via a control α that enters the drift of the McKean–Vlasov SDE dXt = b(Xt,mt,αt) dt + σ dWt. The cost functional J(α) = 𝔼[∫0T f(Xt,mt,αt) dt + g(XT,mT)] is minimised over adapted controls. The dynamic programming principle leads to a master equation on the space of probability measures, which can be derived from a variational principle on the Wasserstein space. The Benamou–Brenier formulation of optimal transport provides a convex reformulation: minimise ∫0T∫ [½|v|2 + f(x,m)] m dx dt subject to ∂tm + div(mv) = 0. This is a convex optimisation problem in the pair (m,w=mv) and can be solved by proximal algorithms. The connection to optimal transport also yields regularity results: if f is displacement convex, the optimal density m remains absolutely continuous.

Applications include crowd motion modelling, where F encodes congestion costs, and financial mathematics, where MFGs describe systemic risk in interbank lending. The variational approach extends to major‑minor player games, where a major agent influences the mean field, leading to a coupled system of a standard HJB for the major player and an MFG system for the minor players. Recent work on learning MFGs uses the variational formulation to design actor‑critic algorithms that converge to the mean field equilibrium without requiring a model of the dynamics.

Variational Methods for Phase Field Models of Fracture and Damage

Phase field fracture regularises the sharp crack topology by a continuous damage field d ∈ [0,1], where d=0 denotes intact material and d=1 fully broken. The total energy functional, following Francfort & Marigo (1998) and Bourdin et al. (2000), is E(u,d) = ∫Ω [ g(d) ψ+(∇u) + ψ−(∇u) ] dx + Gc ∫Ω [ (d2)/(2ℓ) + (ℓ/2)|∇d|2 ] dx. Here u is the displacement, ψ+ and ψ− are the tensile and compressive parts of the elastic energy density (spectral decomposition), g(d) = (1−d)2 is the degradation function, Gc is the critical energy release rate, and ℓ is the regularisation length scale. The Ambrosio–Tortorelli functional Γ‑converges to the Griffith fracture energy as ℓ → 0, providing a rigorous variational convergence result.

The Euler–Lagrange equations are a coupled system: the mechanical equilibrium div( g(d) σ+ + σ− ) = 0, and the damage evolution −ℓ2Δd + d = (2ℓ/Gc) g′(d) ψ+ with the irreversibility constraint d ≥ dprev. The irreversibility is enforced via a history field H(x) = maxs≤t ψ+(∇u(s)), leading to a quasi‑static evolution that satisfies a global stability condition and an energy balance. The variational structure enables the use of alternate minimisation (staggered scheme): for fixed d, solve the linear elasticity problem for u; for fixed u, solve the damage problem, which is a convex obstacle problem due to the irreversibility constraint. Convergence of the staggered scheme to a local minimiser is guaranteed under suitable conditions.

Extensions include anisotropic fracture, where the crack surface energy depends on the normal direction via a metric tensor A(n), leading to a phase field energy ∫ [ (d2)/(2ℓ) + (ℓ/2) ∇d·A∇d ] dx. For dynamic fracture, the kinetic energy ½ρ|u̇|2 is added, and the evolution follows a gradient flow with inertia. The variational framework also accommodates hydraulic fracture by coupling the phase field with a fluid pressure field p in the crack, adding a term ∫ p d dx to the energy. Numerical implementation uses finite elements with adaptive mesh refinement near the diffuse crack; the length scale ℓ must be resolved by at least 3–5 elements to capture the correct energy dissipation. The phase field method has become the standard variational approach for complex fracture patterns in brittle and ductile materials.

Variational Methods for Topological Data Analysis: Persistent Homology and Variational Persistence

Topological data analysis (TDA) extracts shape information from point clouds via persistent homology. Given a filtration of simplicial complexes Kt indexed by a scale parameter t, the persistence diagram Dgm(K) records birth–death pairs (b,d) of homology classes. The bottleneck distance dB(D1,D2) = infγ supp∈D1 ‖p − γ(p)‖∞ defines a metric on diagrams. Variational methods enter when one seeks to optimise a functional of the persistence diagram, e.g., in topological loss functions for machine learning. The persistence landscape λk(t) = k-th largest value of max(0, t−b, d−t) over points (b,d) provides a Hilbert space embedding, enabling gradient‑based optimisation.

Variational persistence considers the problem of finding a function f on a domain that minimises a regularised functional J(f) = E(f) + α Pers(f), where Pers(f) measures the total persistence of sublevel sets of f. For a piecewise‑linear function on a simplicial complex, the persistence of sublevel sets can be expressed via the discrete Morse theory of Forman. The gradient of the persistence functional with respect to vertex values can be computed using the persistence pairing: each paired critical simplex contributes a term that pushes its function value toward its pair. This leads to algorithms for topological simplification (removing noise) and for enforcing topological constraints in shape optimisation, e.g., preserving the number of holes in a design.

In optimal transport, the Wasserstein distance between persistence diagrams can be formulated as a variational problem on the space of measures. The sliced Wasserstein distance projects diagrams onto lines and computes 1‑D Wasserstein distances, which are differentiable almost everywhere. This enables the use of persistence diagrams as layers in neural networks (persistence images, persistence landscapes). Recent work on variational autoencoders for topological data uses the ELBO with a topological regulariser that matches the persistence diagram of the latent space to a target diagram. The theoretical foundation relies on the stability theorem: ‖Dgm(f) − Dgm(g)‖∞ ≤ ‖f − g‖∞, which guarantees that small perturbations in the function lead to small changes in the diagram, making gradient‑based optimisation well‑posed.

  • Neglecting the irreversibility constraint in phase field fracture; omitting the history field leads to unphysical healing and incorrect energy dissipation.
  • Treating the Fisher–Rao metric as Euclidean; natural gradient updates require the inverse Fisher matrix, which is often approximated but not identical to the identity.
  • Assuming the mean field game variational problem is always convex; convexity of the coupling cost F in the measure argument is essential for uniqueness.
  • Using the bottleneck distance as a differentiable loss; it is non‑differentiable at points where the optimal matching changes, so smoothed variants (e.g., persistence landscapes) are preferred for gradient descent.
  • Overlooking the Γ‑convergence requirement ℓ → 0 with mesh size h ≪ ℓ; if ℓ is fixed and h is too coarse, the discrete energy does not approximate the Griffith energy.
  • Misapplying the stability theorem for persistence diagrams to functions with different domains; the theorem requires a common domain and the supremum norm.

Variational Methods for PDE‑Constrained Optimization and Optimal Control of Distributed Parameter Systems

Optimal control of systems governed by partial differential equations leads to variational problems where the state variable satisfies a PDE constraint. The Lagrangian functional combines the cost functional J(y,u) with the weak form of the state equation via an adjoint variable p. For a linear elliptic state equation Ay = Bu + f in a Hilbert space V, the reduced cost functional j(u) = J(S(u),u) is differentiable and its gradient is obtained by solving the adjoint equation A*p = Jy(y,u) followed by j'(u) = B*p + Ju(y,u). The first‑order optimality system (state, adjoint, gradient equation) is a coupled elliptic system that can be discretized by finite elements. For time‑dependent problems (parabolic or hyperbolic), the adjoint runs backward in time, yielding a space‑time variational formulation. Inequality constraints on the control (e.g., box constraints ua ≤ u ≤ ub) lead to variational inequalities; the optimality condition becomes a projection formula u = P[ua,ub](−B*p/α) for a Tikhonov regularization term α‖u‖². Second‑order sufficient conditions involve the Hessian of the Lagrangian projected onto the critical cone; they guarantee local quadratic convergence of Newton‑type methods. In practice, the optimize‑then‑discretize and discretize‑then‑optimize approaches coincide for linear‑quadratic problems but differ for nonlinear PDEs; the former preserves the continuous adjoint structure, while the latter yields exact discrete gradients. Large‑scale problems are solved with Krylov subspace methods preconditioned by multigrid or domain decomposition applied to the saddle‑point KKT system. Applications include shape optimization of aerodynamic bodies, parameter identification in groundwater flow, and optimal heating in thermal processes.

Variational Methods for Image Processing: Total Variation, Mumford–Shah, and Nonlocal Models

Image denoising, segmentation, and reconstruction are formulated as minimization of energy functionals balancing data fidelity and regularization. The Rudin–Osher–Fatemi (ROF) model minimizes E(u) = ∫Ω |∇u| dx + (λ/2)∫Ω (u−f)² dx over functions of bounded variation BV(Ω). The total variation term ∫|∇u| preserves edges while removing noise; its subdifferential yields the Euler–Lagrange inclusion −div(p) + λ(u−f) = 0 with p ∈ ∂|∇u|, |p| ≤ 1. The Mumford–Shah functional MS(u,K) = ∫ΩK |∇u|² dx + αℋ¹(K) + β∫Ω (u−f)² dx couples a piecewise‑smooth image u with a discontinuity set K of finite length. Ambrosio–Tortorelli approximation replaces K by a phase field v ∈ [0,1] and a penalized energy ∫ (v²|∇u|² + ε|∇v|² + (1−v)²/(4ε)) dx, which Γ‑converges to Mumford–Shah as ε→0. Nonlocal means and nonlocal total variation replace the local gradient by weighted differences ∫Ω w(x,y)|u(x)−u(y)| dy, where the weight w encodes patch similarity; this yields better texture preservation. Primal‑dual algorithms (Chambolle–Pock) solve the saddle‑point formulation minu maxp ⟨∇u,p⟩ − δ|p|≤1(p) + (λ/2)‖u−f‖² with step sizes τ,σ satisfying τσ‖∇‖² < 1. For the Mumford–Shah approximation, alternating minimization in u and v converges to a critical point. These variational models underpin modern medical imaging (CT, MRI), remote sensing, and computer vision pipelines.

Variational Methods for Geometric Evolution Equations: Mean Curvature Flow and Willmore Flow

Geometric evolution equations arise as gradient flows of geometric energies on the space of submanifolds. The mean curvature flow (MCF) is the L²‑gradient flow of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫Σ dℋⁿ⁻¹; a hypersurface Σt evolves by normal velocity V = H, where H is the mean curvature. The weak formulation uses the De Giorgi minimizing movements scheme: given Σk, define Σk+1 as a minimizer of A(Σ) + (1/2τ) dist²(Σ, Σk) where dist is the L² distance between surfaces. This time‑discrete variational scheme converges to Brakke’s varifold solution. The Willmore flow is the L²‑gradient flow of the Willmore energy W(Σ) = ∫Σ H² dℋⁿ⁻¹; the normal velocity is V = −ΔΣH − H(|A|² − ½H²). Its fourth‑order nature requires careful discretization; a common approach is the parametric finite element method with a semi‑implicit time stepping that treats the highest‑order term implicitly. For axisymmetric surfaces, the Willmore flow reduces to a one‑dimensional PDE for the generating curve, allowing analytical study of pinch‑off singularities. The Helfrich energy EH = ∫ (κ/2)(H−H0)² + κGK dA models lipid bilayers; its gradient flow yields a coupled system for the surface and the spontaneous curvature H0. Variational time discretization (minimizing movements) provides a unifying framework: each step solves a geometric variational problem, and Γ‑convergence of the discrete energies to the continuous energy ensures convergence of the scheme. Numerical implementations use surface finite elements on evolving triangulations with mesh regularization (tangential redistribution) to avoid degeneration. Applications include vesicle dynamics, image segmentation via active contours, and grain boundary motion in materials science.

Variational Methods for Homogenization and Multiscale Modeling

Homogenization of composite materials with rapidly oscillating coefficients is naturally formulated as a variational convergence problem. Given a family of integral functionals Fε(u) = ∫Ω W(x, x/ε, ∇u) dx with W periodic in the fast variable y = x/ε, the Γ‑limit as ε→0 is a homogenized functional Fhom(u) = ∫Ω Whom(∇u) dx. The homogenized energy density Whom(ξ) = infφ∈W1,pper(Y) ∫Y W(y, ξ+∇φ(y)) dy is obtained by solving a cell problem on the unit cell Y. For convex W, the cell problem has a unique solution; for nonconvex energies (e.g., martensitic phase transformations), the Γ‑limit involves quasiconvexification: Whom = QWper, the quasiconvex envelope of the periodic energy. The variational multiscale method (VMS) decomposes the solution space into coarse and fine scales; the fine‑scale problem is solved locally on patches, yielding a modified coarse‑scale equation with a residual‑based stabilization term. In the heterogeneous multiscale method (HMM), the macroscopic solver queries a microscopic model at selected quadrature points to compute the effective stress. For stochastic homogenization, the coefficient field is a stationary ergodic random field; the homogenized energy density is obtained almost surely by a subadditive ergodic theorem. Numerical homogenization uses representative volume elements (RVEs) with periodic or Dirichlet boundary conditions; the size of the RVE must exceed the correlation length to achieve statistical representativity. Error estimates for the FE‑HMM method show convergence rates O(H + ε/η + η) where H is the macro mesh size, ε the micro scale, and η the sampling domain size. These variational multiscale techniques are essential for modeling porous media, polycrystalline metals, and biological tissues.

Variational Methods for Liquid Crystals and Complex Fluids

The equilibrium configurations of nematic liquid crystals are minimizers of the Oseen–Frank energy E(n) = ∫Ω (½K₁(div n)² + ½K₂(n·curl n)² + ½K₃|n×curl n²) dx subject to the unit‑length constraint |n| = 1. The Euler–Lagrange equations form a degenerate elliptic system with a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the constraint. For the one‑constant approximation K₁ = K₂ = K₃ = K, the energy reduces to (K/2)∫|∇n|² dx, and the minimizer is a harmonic map into the sphere S²; singularities (defects) are described by the homotopy group π₁(S²) = 0 for line defects and π₂(S²) = ℤ for point defects. The Landau–de Gennes theory replaces the director n by a symmetric traceless tensor order parameter Q; the bulk potential fb(Q) = (A/2)tr(Q²) − (B/3)tr(Q³) + (C/4)(tr(Q²))² drives the isotropic‑nematic transition. The total free energy F(Q) = ∫ (L/2)|∇Q|² + fb(Q) dx is minimized in H¹(Ω; S₀) where S₀ is the space of symmetric traceless 3×3 matrices. Γ‑convergence of the Landau–de Gennes energy to the Oseen–Frank energy as the nematic order parameter tends to a uniaxial state has been proved. For smectic liquid crystals, the layer structure is captured by a complex order parameter ψ = ρ eiφ with a free energy containing a gradient term |∇ψ|² and a potential favoring |ψ| = ψ₀; the resulting Euler–Lagrange equation is a Ginzburg–Landau type PDE. In complex fluids such as polymeric solutions, the conformation tensor C evolves by a gradient flow of the free energy F(C) = ∫ (½ tr(C) − ½ log det C) dx coupled with the Navier–Stokes equations; the variational structure yields an energy‑dissipation law d/dt F = −∫ (2η|D|² + (1/λ) tr((C−I)²)) dx. Numerical approximation uses finite elements with a projection step to enforce det C > 0. These variational models underpin the design of liquid crystal displays, responsive materials, and microfluidic devices.

  • Confusing the Γ‑limit of a periodic integral functional with the pointwise limit of the integrand; Γ‑convergence requires the recovery sequence and the liminf inequality, not pointwise convergence.
  • Assuming the cell problem in homogenization always has a unique solution; for nonconvex energies multiple minimizers may exist, and the homogenized density is the quasiconvex envelope.
  • Treating the unit‑length constraint in Oseen–Frank as a simple penalty; the Lagrange multiplier is essential for the correct defect structure and energy scaling.
  • Neglecting the tangential redistribution in surface finite elements for geometric flows; without it, mesh distortion destroys convergence.
  • Using the L² gradient flow for Willmore energy without implicit treatment of the fourth‑order term; explicit schemes suffer from severe time‑step restrictions τ ≤ Ch⁴.
  • Overlooking the subadditive ergodic theorem in stochastic homogenization; the homogenized energy density is defined almost surely, not by a single periodic cell.
  • Confusing the Ambrosio–Tortorelli approximation parameter ε with the physical interface thickness; ε must tend to zero for Γ‑convergence, while the physical thickness is fixed.

Variational Methods for Stochastic Partial Differential Equations

The calculus of variations extends naturally to stochastic settings where the unknown is a random field and the functional involves expectations of integrals driven by noise. A typical stochastic variational problem seeks a process u minimizing the expected value of an energy functional J(u) = 𝔼[∫0T ∫Ω L(x, t, u, ∇u) dx dt + Φ(u(T))] subject to a stochastic PDE constraint such as the stochastic heat equation ∂tu = Δu + f(u) + σ(u) ξ, where ξ denotes space‑time white noise. The first variation leads to a stochastic Euler–Lagrange equation that is a backward stochastic PDE (BSPDE) for the adjoint process p. Under suitable Lipschitz and monotonicity assumptions on the coefficients, the existence of a unique minimizer follows from the direct method in the space L²(Ω; L²(0,T; H¹(Ω))) combined with the Itô isometry. The stochastic maximum principle, due to Bismut (1973) and later refined by Peng (1990), provides necessary optimality conditions in the form of a forward–backward stochastic differential equation (FBSDE) system. Numerically, the stochastic gradient descent algorithm uses Monte Carlo samples of the noise to approximate the gradient of the expected cost; variance reduction techniques such as control variates and multilevel Monte Carlo are essential for feasible computation. Applications include optimal control of stochastic fluid flows, stochastic parameter estimation in climate models, and risk‑sensitive portfolio optimization where the cost functional contains an exponential utility leading to a logarithmic transformation of the value function.

Variational Principles in Nonlinear Elasticity and Fracture Mechanics

In finite‑strain elasticity the stored‑energy function W(F) depends on the deformation gradient F = ∇y and satisfies frame indifference W(RF) = W(F) for all rotations R ∈ SO(3). The total energy E(y) = ∫Ω W(∇y) dx − ∫Ω f·y dx − ∫∂Ω g·y ds is minimized over admissible deformations y ∈ W1,p(Ω; ℝ³) with p > 3 to ensure continuity. Polyconvexity (Ball, 1977) — the existence of a convex function of (F, cof F, det F) representing W — guarantees weak lower semicontinuity and thus existence of minimizers. For fracture, the Griffith energy adds a surface term: E(y, Γ) = ∫Ω\Γ W(∇y) dx + Gc ℋ²(Γ), where Γ is the crack set and Gc the critical energy release rate. The variational approach to fracture (Francfort & Marigo, 1998) treats (y, Γ) as a pair minimizing the total energy under an irreversibility constraint Γ(t₁) ⊂ Γ(t₂). The Ambrosio–Tortorelli elliptic regularization replaces the sharp crack by a phase‑field variable d ∈ [0,1] with energy Eε(y, d) = ∫ (g(d) W(∇y) + (Gc/4ε)(d−1)² + Gcε|∇d|²) dx, where g(d) = (1−d)² + η. Γ‑convergence as ε → 0 recovers the Griffith functional. Numerically, a staggered scheme alternates between solving the elasticity equation for y with fixed d and a damage evolution equation for d with fixed y, ensuring energy decay at each step. This framework captures crack nucleation, branching, and coalescence without explicit tracking of the crack topology, and underlies modern computational fracture codes used in aerospace and civil engineering.

Variational Methods for Optimal Control of Stochastic Systems

Stochastic optimal control problems minimize a cost functional J(u) = 𝔼[∫₀ᵀ L(t, X_t, u_t) dt + Φ(X_T)] subject to the controlled diffusion dX_t = b(t, X_t, u_t) dt + σ(t, X_t, u_t) dW_t. The dynamic programming principle leads to the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation −∂_t V = inf_u { L + b·∇V + ½ tr(σσᵀ ∇²V) } with terminal condition V(T, x) = Φ(x). When the control enters linearly in the drift and the noise is independent of u, the HJB equation becomes a semilinear PDE amenable to viscosity solution theory (Crandall–Lions, 1983). The stochastic maximum principle provides an alternative: introduce the adjoint process p_t solving the backward SDE dp_t = −[∂_x H(t, X_t, u_t, p_t, q_t)] dt + q_t dW_t, p_T = ∂_x Φ(X_T), where the Hamiltonian H = L + p·b + tr(qᵀσ). The optimal control satisfies ∂_u H = 0 almost surely. For linear‑quadratic problems with L = ½(uᵀRu + XᵀQX), b = AX + Bu, σ = Σ, the Riccati equation Ṗ = −PA − AᵀP + PBR⁻¹BᵀP − Q, P(T)=0 gives the optimal feedback u_t = −R⁻¹BᵀP(t)X_t. In risk‑sensitive control the cost is J_θ(u) = (1/θ) log 𝔼[exp(θ ∫ L dt + θ Φ)], leading to a transformed HJB with an exponential nonlinearity; the optimal control remains linear in the state but the Riccati equation acquires an additional term θ P Σ Σᵀ P. Numerical solution uses policy iteration combined with Monte Carlo regression (Longstaff–Schwartz) for high‑dimensional state spaces, and sparse grid collocation for the HJB PDE when dimension ≤ 5.

Variational Methods for Geometric Analysis: The Yamabe Problem and Prescribed Curvature

The Yamabe problem asks whether a given compact Riemannian manifold (Mⁿ, g₀) with n ≥ 3 admits a conformal metric g = u4/(n−2) g₀ of constant scalar curvature. The scalar curvature transforms as R_g = u-(n+2)/(n−2) (−c_n Δg₀ u + Rg₀ u) where c_n = 4(n−1)/(n−2). The problem reduces to minimizing the Yamabe functional Y(u) = (∫_M (c_n |∇u|² + Rg₀ u²) dvolg₀) / (∫_M u2n/(n−2) dvolg₀)(n−2)/n over positive u ∈ H¹(M). The infimum is the Yamabe invariant λ(M, [g₀]). Aubin (1976) and Schoen (1984) proved existence of a minimizer using the concentration‑compactness principle; the positive mass theorem in general relativity enters the proof for the remaining cases. For prescribed scalar curvature R(x) > 0, one seeks u solving −c_n Δ u + Rg₀ u = R u(n+2)/(n−2). Variational methods apply when the functional satisfies the Palais–Smale condition; otherwise, blow‑up analysis and the Kazdan–Warner obstruction ∫_M ∇R·X u2n/(n−2) = 0 for conformal Killing fields X must be examined. The prescribed Gauss curvature problem on surfaces (n=2) leads to the Liouville equation Δ u + Kg₀ = K e2u, where the Moser–Trudinger inequality controls the exponential nonlinearity. These variational techniques also underlie the solution of the Nirenberg problem on the sphere and the construction of constant Q‑curvature metrics in higher dimensions.

Variational Methods for Quantum Field Theory: Effective Action and Renormalization

In quantum field theory the generating functional Z[J] = ∫ 𝒟φ exp(i/ℏ (S[φ] + ∫ Jφ)) defines the effective action Γ[φ_cl] as the Legendre transform of W[J] = −iℏ log Z[J] with respect to the source J. The effective action satisfies the quantum Euler–Lagrange equation δΓ/δφ_cl = J and encodes all one‑particle‑irreducible (1PI) correlation functions. Perturbatively, Γ = S + ℏ Γ¹ + ℏ² Γ² + … where each loop order corresponds to a sum over 1PI Feynman diagrams. Renormalization is the procedure of absorbing ultraviolet divergences into redefinitions of the bare parameters (mass, coupling, field strength). In the variational language, one introduces a cutoff Λ and defines the Wilsonian effective action S_Λ[φ] by integrating out modes with momenta > Λ. The exact renormalization group equation (Polchinski, 1984) ∂_Λ S_Λ = ½ ∫ (δS_Λ/δφ)·Δ_Λ·(δS_Λ/δφ) − ½ tr(Δ_Λ·δ²S_Λ/δφ²) describes the flow of the effective action with the cutoff. Fixed points of this flow correspond to scale‑invariant quantum field theories; the Gaussian fixed point yields free fields, while nontrivial fixed points (e.g., Wilson–Fisher in 4−ε dimensions) describe critical phenomena. The variational principle for the effective action underlies the background field method, where one expands around a classical background φ̄ and computes Γ[φ̄] by a functional determinant: Γ¹[φ̄] = (i/2) log det(δ²S/δφ²|φ̄). This determinant is regularized via zeta‑function or dimensional regularization, and its variation yields the one‑loop effective equations of motion. Modern applications include the functional renormalization group for quantum gravity (asymptotic safety) and the variational optimization of tensor network states in many‑body physics.

  • Assuming polyconvexity is equivalent to convexity of W(F); polyconvexity requires convexity in the extended variable (F, cof F, det F).
  • Neglecting the irreversibility constraint Γ(t₁) ⊂ Γ(t₂) in variational fracture; without it the model allows crack healing, contradicting Griffith’s theory.
  • Treating the HJB equation as a classical PDE when the value function is only a viscosity solution; verification theorems require the candidate to be C¹,² or a viscosity supersolution/subsolution pair.
  • Overlooking the Kazdan–Warner obstruction when solving the prescribed curvature equation on manifolds with conformal Killing fields; the obstruction is necessary for existence.
  • Identifying the Wilsonian effective action with the 1PI effective action; they differ by a Legendre transform and generate different sets of diagrams.
  • Using the Gaussian fixed point as the only renormalization group fixed point in dimensions < 4; the Wilson–Fisher fixed point governs critical exponents in statistical physics.

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Schrödinger Equations: Ground States and Orbital Stability

The focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS) i∂tu + Δu + |u|p−1u = 0 on ℝn possesses a conserved energy E(u) = ½∫|∇u|² − 1/(p+1)∫|u|p+1 and mass M(u) = ∫|u|². Ground states are standing waves u(t,x) = eiωtφω(x) where φω solves the elliptic equation −Δφ + ωφ = |φ|p−1φ. Variational characterization: for a fixed mass m > 0, minimizers of E(u) under the constraint M(u) = m exist precisely when the problem is L²‑subcritical (p < 1 + 4/n) or L²‑critical (p = 1 + 4/n) with mass below the sharp Gagliardo–Nirenberg threshold. The sharp inequality ∫|u|p+1 ≤ CGN (∫|∇u|²)n(p−1)/4 (∫|u|²)(p+1)−n(p−1)/4 (Weinstein 1983) yields the optimal constant and identifies the ground state as the unique (up to symmetries) optimizer.

Orbital stability of the set of ground states follows from the Grillakis–Shatah–Strauss (GSS) framework (1987). One computes the linearized operator L+ = −Δ + ω − p|φ|p−1 and L− = −Δ + ω − |φ|p−1. The Vakhitov–Kolokolov criterion d/dω M(φω) > 0 guarantees that the Hessian of the constrained energy has exactly one negative direction, which together with the kernel generated by gauge and translation invariances yields stability. In the L²‑supercritical regime (p > 1 + 4/n) the mass derivative becomes negative and the standing waves are strongly unstable; blow‑up solutions with minimal mass are constructed via concentration‑compactness (Merle 1993). The variational approach also underpins the classification of minimal‑mass blow‑up solutions for the critical NLS (Kenig–Merle 2006) and the proof of scattering for radial data below the ground state threshold (Duyckaerts–Holmer–Roudenko 2008).

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Wave Equations: Concentration Compactness and Blow‑up

The energy‑critical focusing wave equation □u = |u|4/(n−2)u on ℝ1+n (n ≥ 3) conserves the energy E(u,∂tu) = ½∫(|∂tu|² + |∇u|²) − (n−2)/2n ∫|u|2n/(n−2). The stationary solutions satisfy the elliptic Yamabe equation −ΔW = |W|4/(n−2)W, whose positive radial solution W(x) = (1 + |x|²/(n(n−2)))−(n−2)/2 (Aubin–Talenti) gives the threshold energy E(W,0). Kenig and Merle (2008) introduced a variational roadmap: assume a counterexample to global existence and scattering, extract a minimal‑energy blow‑up solution via a profile decomposition (Bahouri–Gérard 1999) adapted to the wave equation, and then prove rigidity using a localized virial/Morawetz estimate. The concentration‑compactness principle for the wave equation states that any bounded sequence in the energy space Ḣ¹ × L² can be decomposed into a sum of rescaled profiles plus a remainder that vanishes in the Strichartz norm. This decomposition is the nonlinear analogue of the linear profile decomposition and is essential for identifying the “critical element” that carries the minimal energy.

The variational structure also yields the classification of finite‑time blow‑up solutions with energy equal to E(W,0) (Duyckaerts–Kenig–Merle 2011). In the subcritical case (p < 1 + 4/(n−2)) the energy functional is coercive on the Nehari manifold, and global existence follows from the conservation laws. For the critical exponent, the lack of compactness due to scaling invariance is precisely overcome by the concentration‑compactness machinery. The same ideas have been extended to the Maxwell–Klein–Gordon and Yang–Mills systems (Krieger–Schlag–Tataru 2009) and to the wave maps problem (Krieger–Schlag 2007), where the target geometry introduces additional topological constraints that are handled by a variational argument on the homotopy class.

Technique: Profile Decomposition for the Energy‑Critical Wave Equation
Explain how the Bahouri–Gérard profile decomposition is used to prove the existence of a minimal‑energy blow‑up solution for the focusing energy‑critical wave equation.
  1. Start with a sequence of initial data (u0,k, u1,k) in Ḣ¹ × L² with energies converging to the infimum Ec of energies of blow‑up solutions.
  2. Apply the linear profile decomposition: there exist profiles (ULj), scaling parameters λkj, time shifts tkj, and space shifts xkj such that the sequence equals a sum of rescaled linear evolutions plus a remainder wkJ whose Strichartz norm tends to zero as J → ∞ uniformly in k.
  3. By the Pythagorean expansion of the energy, the total energy splits as the sum of the profile energies plus the remainder energy. Since the remainder is small in Strichartz norm, a perturbation lemma (stability of the nonlinear flow) shows that the nonlinear evolution of the full data is approximated by the sum of the nonlinear evolutions of each profile.
  4. If all profiles generated global scattering solutions, the original sequence would also scatter, contradicting the assumption. Hence at least one profile yields a blow‑up solution. By minimality of Ec, that profile must have energy exactly Ec and its nonlinear evolution is the desired minimal‑energy blow‑up solution.
  5. The resulting solution is almost periodic modulo symmetries, a property that is then exploited in the rigidity step (localized virial/Morawetz) to rule out its existence, completing the Kenig–Merle proof of scattering below the ground state energy.

If coercivity fails, look for a concentration‑compactness or profile decomposition argument; the existence of a “critical element” is often the key step in a Kenig–Merle type proof.

Variational Methods for Hamiltonian Partial Differential Equations: Symplectic Structure and Invariant Manifolds

Many dispersive and wave PDEs can be written as infinite‑dimensional Hamiltonian systems ∂tu = J∇H(u) where J is a skew‑adjoint symplectic operator (e.g., J = i for NLS, J = (0,1;−1,0) for the wave equation) and H is the Hamiltonian functional (energy). The phase space is a symplectic Hilbert space equipped with the symplectic form Ω(u,v) = ⟨Ju,v⟩. Variational principles arise from the action functional S(u) = ∫(½⟨J∂tu,u⟩ − H(u)) dt; critical points of S on a suitable loop space correspond to periodic or quasi‑periodic solutions. The Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction (or Nash–Moser iteration) reduces the infinite‑dimensional problem to a finite‑dimensional bifurcation equation whose solutions give rise to invariant tori.

KAM theory for PDEs (Kuksin 1993, Pöschel 1996, Bourgain 1990s) proves the persistence of a Cantor family of quasi‑periodic solutions for small perturbations of completely integrable Hamiltonian PDEs such as the linear Schrödinger or wave equation on the torus. The main steps are: (1) construct a Birkhoff normal form up to high order to isolate the resonant part of the Hamiltonian; (2) verify the Melnikov non‑resonance conditions on the frequencies; (3) apply a Newton‑type KAM iteration with tame estimates to conjugate the perturbed system to an integrable normal form. The variational formulation provides a natural setting for the Nash–Moser scheme because the action functional is smooth on Sobolev spaces and its Hessian is a self‑adjoint operator with a well‑understood spectrum. Recent work (Berti–Bolle 2013, Feola–Iandoli 2020) extends KAM to PDEs with unbounded perturbation (e.g., derivative NLS) by using a variational Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction on the space of Sobolev loops.

Invariant manifolds (center, stable, unstable) for Hamiltonian PDEs are constructed via the Lyapunov–Perron method or by variational characterization as graphs of the gradient of a generating function. For the nonlinear wave equation with a potential, the center‑stable manifold consists of solutions that converge to a ground state as t → +∞; its codimension equals the number of unstable eigenvalues of the linearized operator. The variational structure guarantees that the stable manifold is Lagrangian with respect to the symplectic form, a fact used in the proof of asymptotic stability of solitary waves (Buslaev–Perelman 1995, Soffer–Weinstein 1999).

Variational Methods for Optimal Transport on Metric Measure Spaces: Displacement Convexity and Ricci Curvature

The L²‑Wasserstein space P₂(X) over a complete separable metric space (X,d) is the set of Borel probability measures with finite second moment, equipped with the distance W₂(μ,ν) = (infπ∈Π(μ,ν) ∫ d(x,y)² dπ)½. McCann (1997) introduced displacement interpolation: for μ₀, μ₁ ∈ P₂(ℝⁿ) with densities, the geodesic μt = ((1−t)Id + tT)#μ₀ where T = ∇φ is the optimal transport map. A functional F: P₂(X) → ℝ∪{+∞} is K‑displacement convex if F(μt) ≤ (1−t)F(μ₀) + tF(μ₁) − ½K t(1−t)W₂(μ₀,μ₁)² for all geodesics. The entropy functional Ent(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dvol (with μ = ρ vol) is K‑displacement convex exactly when the underlying space satisfies the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,∞) (Sturm 2006, Lott–Villani 2009).

The synthetic Ricci curvature lower bound CD(K,N) (with dimension bound N ∈ [1,∞]) is defined by requiring displacement convexity of the Rényi entropy UN(μ) = −∫ ρ1−1/N dvol with a precise distortion coefficient. This condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and implies a host of functional inequalities: the Talagrand inequality W₂(μ,ν)² ≤ (2/K) Ent(ν|μ), the logarithmic Sobolev inequality, and the Poincaré inequality. The Otto calculus (2001) provides a formal Riemannian structure on P₂(ℝⁿ) where the tangent space at μ is identified with L²(μ) gradients, and the gradient flow of the entropy is the heat flow. Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré (2008) rigorously developed the theory of gradient flows in metric measure spaces, showing that the heat flow is the unique EVIK (evolution variational inequality) gradient flow of the entropy. This variational viewpoint yields sharp heat kernel estimates, contraction of the heat flow in W₂, and the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus in a non‑smooth setting.

Applications include the proof of the Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality for CD(K,N) spaces (Cavalletti–Mondino 2017), the localization technique for the Brunn–Minkowski inequality, and the study of Ricci limit spaces (Cheeger–Colding theory). In data science, the displacement convexity of the Kullback–Leibler divergence underlies the convergence analysis of the JKO scheme for sampling algorithms (Wibisono 2018). The variational formulation also connects to the Schrödinger problem (entropic optimal transport) where the dynamic formulation minimizes the relative entropy with respect to the Wiener measure, leading to the Schrödinger bridge and its applications in generative modeling.

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Elliptic Systems: Morse Theory and Critical Groups

Nonlinear elliptic systems arise when several unknown functions are coupled through a variational functional. The standard scalar Euler–Lagrange equation generalises to a system −Δu = ∇uF(x,u) where u = (u₁,…,uₘ) and F is a C¹ potential. Critical points of the associated functional J(u) = ∫(½|∇u|² − F(x,u)) dx are weak solutions. Morse theory provides a topological count of critical points by relating the homology of sublevel sets J⁽ᶜ⁾ = {u : J(u) ≤ c} to the critical groups Cq(J,u₀) = Hq(J⁽ᶜ⁾ ∩ U, J⁽ᶜ⁾ ∩ U {u₀}) for an isolated critical point u₀ with J(u₀)=c. If the functional satisfies the Palais–Smale condition and the critical groups are nontrivial in several degrees, the Morse inequalities force the existence of multiple solutions. The computation of critical groups often uses the splitting lemma: near a nondegenerate critical point the functional is equivalent to a quadratic form, so Cq ≅ ℤ if q equals the Morse index and zero otherwise. For degenerate points one employs Gromoll–Meyer theory or Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction to finite dimensions. A typical application is the Lane–Emden system −Δu = vᵖ, −Δv = uᵠ in a bounded domain with Dirichlet data. The associated functional is strongly indefinite; one constructs a linking geometry on the Nehari–Pohozaev manifold and computes critical groups at zero and at infinity to obtain at least two nontrivial solutions via the Morse relation Σ (−1)ᵠ rank Cq(J,u) = Σ (−1)ᵠ rank Hq(X, J⁽ᵃ⁾). The method extends to Hamiltonian elliptic systems where the functional is the action integral on a loop space, and the critical groups detect periodic orbits through the Conley index.

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Eigenvalue Problems: Nehari Manifold and Fibering Maps

Consider the quasilinear eigenvalue problem −Δpu = λ|u|p−2u + μ|u|q−2u in Ω, u=0 on ∂Ω, with 1 < p < q < p* (Sobolev critical exponent). The energy functional Jλ,μ(u) = (1/p)∫|∇u|ᵖ − (λ/p)∫|u|ᵖ − (μ/q)∫|u|ᵠ is not bounded below on the whole Sobolev space W₀¹ᵖ(Ω). The Nehari manifold N = {u ≠ 0 : ⟨J'(u),u⟩ = 0} provides a natural constraint on which J is bounded from below and any minimiser is a nontrivial critical point. The fibering map φu(t) = J(tu) for t>0 reduces the problem to a one‑dimensional analysis: φ'u(t)=0 ⇔ tu ∈ N. The shape of φu depends on the sign of μ and the relative size of λ with respect to the first eigenvalue λ₁(p) of −Δp. If μ>0 and λ<λ₁(p), φu has a unique global minimum giving a ground state. If μ<0, φu may have a local maximum and a local minimum, leading to two distinct Nehari components N⁺ and N⁻. Minimising on each component yields a ground state and a higher energy solution (a mountain‑pass type). The Pohozaev identity ∫(N/p)|∇u|ᵖ = ∫(N/p)λ|u|ᵖ + ∫(N/q)μ|u|ᵠ rules out nontrivial solutions in star‑shaped domains when λ≥λ₁(p) and μ≥0. For systems, the Nehari manifold becomes a product of manifolds or a single manifold defined by ⟨J'(u),u⟩=0, and the fibering map is replaced by a multi‑parameter map φ(t₁,…,tₘ)=J(t₁u₁,…,tₘuₘ). Critical points of φ correspond to solutions of the coupled system. The method is robust for problems with critical growth, where the lack of compactness is handled by concentration‑compactness on the Nehari manifold.

Variational Methods for Kinetic Equations: Boltzmann and Vlasov–Poisson Functionals

Kinetic equations describe the evolution of a particle density f(t,x,v) in phase space. The Boltzmann equation ∂tf + v·∇xf = Q(f,f) has a variational structure when written in terms of the entropy functional H(f) = ∫ f log f dx dv. The H‑theorem states d/dt H(f(t)) = −D(f) ≤ 0 where D(f) = −∫ Q(f,f) log f dv ≥ 0 is the entropy dissipation. This gradient‑flow structure in the space of probability measures with the Wasserstein metric (or the Fisher–Rao metric for the linearised collision operator) yields existence of global weak solutions via the DiPerna–Lions renormalisation technique. For the Vlasov–Poisson system ∂tf + v·∇xf + E·∇vf = 0, E = −∇φ, −Δφ = ρ−1, the energy–Casimir functional ℰ(f) = ∫ (½|v|² + φ) f dx dv + ∫ Φ(f) dx dv is conserved along trajectories for any convex Φ. Choosing Φ(s)=s log s gives the free energy ℰ(f) = ∫ f log f + ½∫|∇φ|² dx. The stationary states are minimisers of ℰ under the mass constraint ∫ f = M. The existence of minimisers follows from the concentration‑compactness principle applied to the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality. Orbital stability of these steady states is proved by the energy–Casimir method: if the second variation δ²ℰ(f₀) is positive definite on the tangent space of the mass constraint, then any solution starting near f₀ stays near the orbit of f₀. The linearised operator around a Maxwellian yields the celebrated Landau damping phenomenon, which can be captured by a variational formulation in analytic Gevrey spaces (Mouhot–Villani 2011). For the relativistic Vlasov–Maxwell system, the Hamiltonian structure is given by the total energy ℰ = ∫ (√(1+|v|²) f + ½(|E|²+|B|²)) dx dv, and the Poisson bracket is the Lie–Poisson bracket on the dual of the Lie algebra of canonical transformations. Variational methods also produce global weak solutions for the Boltzmann equation with soft potentials via the entropy dissipation estimate and the velocity averaging lemma.

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Schrödinger Equations with Magnetic Fields

The magnetic nonlinear Schrödinger equation i∂tψ = (−i∇−A)²ψ + Vψ − |ψ|p−2ψ in ℝᴺ, with magnetic potential A∈C¹(ℝᴺ,ℝᴺ) and electric potential V∈C(ℝᴺ), has the conserved energy functional E(ψ) = ½∫|(−i∇−A)ψ|² + ½∫V|ψ|² − (1/p)∫|ψ|ᵖ. The magnetic gradient (−i∇−A) is gauge invariant: under ψ↦eiχψ, A↦A+∇χ the functional is unchanged. The diamagnetic inequality |∇|ψ|| ≤ |(−i∇−A)ψ| allows comparison with the non‑magnetic case. Ground states are minimisers of E on the Nehari manifold N = {ψ≠0 : ⟨E'(ψ),ψ⟩=0}. The fibering map φ(t)=E(tψ) yields a unique tψ>0 for each ψ with ∫|(−i∇−A)ψ|² + ∫V|ψ|² > 0. If V≥0 and p<2* (Sobolev critical exponent), the functional satisfies the Palais–Smale condition on N and a minimiser exists. For sign‑changing V, one splits N into N⁺ and N⁻ according to the sign of ⟨E''(ψ)ψ,ψ⟩ and obtains a ground state on N⁺ and a bound state on N⁻ via the mountain‑pass theorem. The magnetic field B = curl A influences the asymptotic profile: in the semi‑classical limit h→0, the ground state concentrates at points where the effective potential Veff(x) = V(x) + ½|B(x)| attains its minimum (Helffer–Morame 2001). Recent work extends these methods to systems with spin‑orbit coupling and to the magnetic Gross–Pitaevskii equation for Bose–Einstein condensates.

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Elasticity: Polyconvexity and Existence Theorems

The stored‑energy function W(F) of a hyperelastic material depends on the deformation gradient F ∈ ℝ³ˣ³. Classical existence theory requires coercivity W(F) ≥ c₁|F|ᵖ − c₂ and weak lower semicontinuity of the total energy I(u) = ∫_Ω W(∇u) dx. Ball (1977) showed that quasiconvexity in the sense of Morrey is the necessary and sufficient condition for weak lower semicontinuity, but verifying quasiconvexity is generally intractable. Polyconvexity, introduced by Ball, replaces quasiconvexity by convexity of W as a function of the minors of F: W(F) = g(F, cof F, det F) with g convex. This condition is stronger than quasiconvexity but amenable to analysis because the minors are null‑Lagrangians — their integrals depend only on boundary data. Under polyconvexity, coercivity, and the physical requirement W(F) → +∞ as det F → 0⁺ (preventing interpenetration), the direct method yields existence of minimisers in the Sobolev space W¹ᵖ(Ω;ℝ³) for p > 3. The proof uses the weak continuity of the determinant and cofactor mappings (Reshetnyak’s theorem) to pass to the limit in the nonlinear terms. For incompressible materials the constraint det ∇u = 1 is handled by a Lagrange multiplier (pressure) and the existence theory extends to the space of orientation‑preserving mappings with finite distortion. Recent developments include existence for p = 3 using the notion of very weak solutions and the theory of Cartesian currents to capture cavitation and fracture.

Variational Methods for Phase Transitions: Modica–Mortola Functional and Gamma‑Convergence

The Modica–Mortola functional E_ε(u) = ∫_Ω (ε|∇u|² + ε⁻¹W(u)) dx with a double‑well potential W(u) = (1−u²)²/4 approximates the perimeter functional as ε → 0. The Gamma‑convergence framework (De Giorgi, 1975) provides the rigorous passage: E_ε Gamma‑converges in L¹(Ω) to E₀(u) = c₀ Per_Ω({u=1}) where c₀ = ∫−11 √(2W(s)) ds = 2√2/3 and Per_Ω denotes the Caccioppoli perimeter. The liminf inequality uses the coarea formula and the one‑dimensional optimal profile u_ε(x) = tanh(x/√2ε). The recovery sequence is built by convolving the characteristic function of a set of finite perimeter with the optimal profile. This variational approximation underpins numerical methods such as the Allen–Cahn equation ∂_t u = Δu − ε⁻²W'(u), which is the L² gradient flow of E_ε. Gamma‑convergence also handles anisotropic surface energies by replacing |∇u|² with a(∇u/|∇u|)|∇u|² where a is a positive homogeneous degree‑two function. The limiting functional becomes an anisotropic perimeter ∫∂*E a(ν_E) dℋ². Extensions to vector‑valued order parameters (e.g. u: Ω → ℝᵐ) lead to the study of minimal partitions and the Cahn–Hilliard functional ∫ (ε|∇u|² + ε⁻¹W(u) + ε|Δu|²) which Gamma‑converges to the Willmore energy. The quantitative estimate |E_ε(u) − E₀(u)| ≤ Cε|log ε| for well‑prepared sequences is crucial for error analysis in diffuse‑interface simulations.

Thus the asymmetry introduces a volume term that penalises the less favourable phase, leading to a selection mechanism in the limit ε → 0.

Variational Methods for Geometric Measure Theory: Varifolds, Rectifiability, and Allard Regularity

Varifolds generalise surfaces by replacing a smooth submanifold with a Radon measure on the Grassmann bundle G_k(ℝⁿ) of unoriented k‑planes. A k‑varifold V is a measure on Ω × G_k(ℝⁿ); its weight measure ‖V‖ is the projection onto Ω. The first variation δV is defined by δV(X) = ∫ div_S X d‖V‖ for vector fields X. A varifold is stationary if δV = 0. Rectifiable varifolds have weight concentrated on a countably k‑rectifiable set and admit an approximate tangent plane ‖V‖‑a.e. Allard’s regularity theorem (1972) states: if a stationary integral varifold V has density Θ^k(‖V‖, x) = 1 at a point and the excess E(V, x, r) = r−k ∫B_r(x) |S − T_x|² d‖V‖ is sufficiently small, then V is a C¹,α graph near x. The proof uses a harmonic approximation lemma and a Campanato‑type iteration. This theorem is the cornerstone for regularity of area‑minimising currents and for the existence of smooth minimal hypersurfaces in generic metrics (Almgren–Pitts min‑max theory). For higher codimension, the density hypothesis must be strengthened to Θ = 1 and the excess smallness yields C¹,α regularity except on a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most k−2. The monotonicity formula d/dr (r−k ‖V‖(B_r)) = r−k−1 ∫B_r |(x−x₀)^⊥|² / |x−x₀|² d‖V‖ controls density and is essential for blow‑up analysis. Recent work extends Allard’s theorem to varifolds with bounded mean curvature in the sense of δV = H ‖V‖ with H ∈ L^p, p > k.

Variational Methods for Stochastic Homogenization: Subadditive Ergodic Theorem and Correctors

Consider the random integral functional I_ε(u, ω) = ∫_Ω f(x/ε, ∇u, ω) dx where f(y, ξ, ω) is stationary ergodic in y and convex in ξ. The subadditive ergodic theorem (Akcoglu–Krengel) applied to the minimal energy m(ξ, A, ω) = inf{ ∫_A f(y, ξ+∇φ, ω) dy : φ ∈ W₀¹ᵖ(A) } yields the almost sure existence of the homogenised density f_hom(ξ) = limR→∞ m(ξ, Q_R, ω) / |Q_R|. The limit is deterministic and convex. The corrector φ_ξ(y, ω) solves the Euler–Lagrange equation −div(∂_ξ f(y, ξ+∇φ_ξ, ω)) = 0 in ℝⁿ with sublinear growth at infinity. Existence of correctors in the whole space is delicate; for p > 1 one constructs them via the Lax–Milgram theorem on large boxes with periodic boundary conditions and passes to the limit using the subadditive ergodic theorem. The two‑scale convergence method (Nguetseng, Allaire) provides a deterministic characterisation: u_ε ⇀ u weakly in W¹ᵖ and ∇u_ε two‑scale converges to ∇u(x) + ∇_y φ(x, y). The homogenised functional is I_hom(u) = ∫_Ω f_hom(∇u) dx. Quantitative stochastic homogenization (Armstrong–Smart, Gloria–Otto) establishes rates of convergence |I_ε(u) − I_hom(u)| ≤ C ε^α under finite‑range dependence or spectral gap assumptions, using the corrector equation and the Helffer–Sjöstrand representation of the variance. For non‑convex integrands, the quasiconvex envelope appears in the limit, and the subadditive ergodic theorem must be applied to the relaxed energy.

Variational Methods for Inverse Problems: Bayesian Variational Formulation and Posterior Consistency

In a Bayesian inverse problem one seeks the posterior distribution μ^y of an unknown parameter u ∈ X given data y = G(u) + η, where G: X → Y is the forward map and η ∼ N(0, Γ) is Gaussian noise. The prior μ₀ is often a Gaussian measure on a separable Banach space X. By the Radon–Nikodym theorem, the posterior density with respect to the prior is dμ^y/dμ₀(u) ∝ exp(−Φ(u; y)) with the potential Φ(u; y) = ½‖Γ−1/2(y − G(u))‖². The variational formulation of the posterior (the Bayes–Laplace principle) states that μ^y minimises the Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(ν ‖ μ^y) = ∫ log(dν/dμ^y) dν over all probability measures ν on X. Equivalently, μ^y is the unique minimiser of the free energy functional F(ν) = ∫ Φ(u; y) dν(u) + KL(ν ‖ μ₀). This variational perspective underpins variational inference where one restricts ν to a tractable family (e.g. Gaussian) and minimises F. Posterior consistency asks whether μ^y concentrates around the true parameter u^† as the noise level δ → 0. Under the Hellinger metric d_Hell(μ^y, δu^†), consistency holds if the prior puts sufficient mass near u^† in the sense of small ball probabilities μ₀(B_ε(u^†)) ≥ exp(−C ε−α) and the forward map is locally Lipschitz. The Bernstein–von Mises theorem in infinite dimensions (under smoothness and non‑degeneracy) asserts that the posterior is asymptotically Gaussian with covariance given by the inverse of the Hessian of Φ at the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator. For nonlinear G, the MAP estimator solves the Tikhonov‑type optimisation min_u Φ(u; y) + R(u) where R(u) = −log dμ₀/dλ(u) is the Onsager–Machlup functional. Recent work connects the variational formulation to Wasserstein gradient flows for sampling (e.g. the JKO scheme for the Fokker–Planck equation).

Variational Methods for Free Boundary Problems: Alt–Caffarelli and Bernoulli Problems

Free boundary problems arise when the domain of a minimiser is itself unknown and must be determined as part of the variational principle. The classical Alt–Caffarelli functional J(u) = ∫Ω (|∇u|² + χ{u>0}) dx, where χ denotes the characteristic function, models the shape of a jet emerging from a nozzle. Its Euler–Lagrange conditions yield Δu = 0 in {u>0} together with the free boundary condition |∇u| = 1 on ∂{u>0} ∩ Ω. Alt and Caffarelli (1981) proved existence of a minimiser in the class of non‑negative functions with prescribed boundary data, and established that the free boundary is locally a C1,α graph except for a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most n−1. The monotonicity formula Φ(r) = r−n ∫Br |∇u|² dx + r−n+1 |{u>0} ∩ Br| is the key tool; its derivative controls the density of the positivity set and yields the non‑degeneracy estimate supBr u ≥ c r.

The Bernoulli problem replaces the constant term by a prescribed function Q(x) on the free boundary: minimise ∫ (|∇u|² + Q(x)² χ{u>0}) dx. This models electrochemical machining and tumour growth. The free boundary condition becomes |∇u| = Q(x). When Q is Lipschitz and bounded away from zero, the same monotonicity techniques give C1,α regularity of the reduced free boundary. Singular points may appear where the free boundary touches the fixed boundary or where Q vanishes; blow‑up analysis shows that singularities are modelled by homogeneous global solutions of degree 3/2 in two dimensions. Recent work by De Silva and Savin (2015) extends regularity to the case where Q is merely Dini continuous, using a refined epiperimetric inequality.

Numerical approximation of free boundary problems typically employs a phase‑field regularisation: replace χ{u>0} by a smooth double‑well potential Wε(u) = (1/ε)W(u/ε) with W(s) = s²(1−s)². The resulting Ambrosio–Tortorelli functional Γ‑converges to the sharp interface functional as ε → 0. Finite element discretisations combined with adaptive mesh refinement near the diffuse interface achieve optimal convergence rates. In practice, the choice of ε balances interface resolution against the conditioning of the discrete system; a common heuristic is ε ≈ 3h where h is the local mesh size.

Variational Methods for Nonlocal Operators: Fractional Perimeter and Nonlocal Minimal Surfaces

Nonlocal variational problems replace the classical perimeter by the fractional perimeter Pers(E) = ∫E ∫Ec |x−y|−n−s dx dy, where s ∈ (0,1). The associated energy functional for a set E ⊂ ℝn is the s‑perimeter; its minimisers are called s‑minimal surfaces. The first variation yields the nonlocal mean curvature Hs(x) = ∫ℝn (χEc(y) − χE(y)) |x−y|−n−s dy, and the Euler–Lagrange equation for a critical set is Hs = 0 on ∂E. Caffarelli, Roquejoffre and Savin (2010) proved that s‑minimal surfaces are C1,α smooth outside a singular set of dimension at most n−2, and that the singular set is empty for n ≤ 2. The proof adapts the classical De Giorgi improvement of flatness to the nonlocal setting, using the extension problem of Caffarelli–Silvestre which realises the fractional Laplacian as a Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map on a half‑space in ℝn+1.

The fractional Allen–Cahn functional Eε(u) = ε2s−1 ∫ |(−Δ)s/2u|² dx + (1/ε) ∫ W(u) dx Γ‑converges to a constant multiple of Pers as ε → 0. This provides a phase‑field approximation for numerical computation. The nonlocal nature introduces dense stiffness matrices; fast multipole methods or hierarchical matrices (H‑matrices) reduce the complexity from O(N²) to O(N log N). For the obstacle problem with the fractional Laplacian, the minimiser of ∫ |(−Δ)s/2u|² dx − ∫ fu dx subject to u ≥ ψ satisfies the complementarity conditions u ≥ ψ, (−Δ)su ≥ f, (u−ψ)((−Δ)su−f) = 0. The optimal regularity is u ∈ C1,s and the free boundary is C1,α for s > 1/2 (Caffarelli, Salsa, Silvestre 2008).

Applications include image segmentation with nonlocal total variation, where the fidelity term is coupled with the fractional perimeter to preserve fine textures, and models of phase separation in materials with long‑range interactions. The nonlocal isoperimetric inequality states that balls minimise Pers among sets of given volume; the proof uses the symmetric decreasing rearrangement and the Riesz potential representation of the fractional perimeter.

Worked Example: First Variation of Fractional Perimeter
Let E ⊂ ℝn be a smooth set and consider a smooth vector field V compactly supported in ℝn. Define the flow Φt(x) = x + tV(x) and the perturbed sets Et = Φt(E). Compute the first variation d/dt|t=0 Pers(Et) and show that it equals ∫∂E Hs(x) V(x)·ν(x) dℋn−1(x), where ν is the outer unit normal.
Start from the definition Pers(E) = ∫E ∫Ec |x−y|−n−s dx dy. Under the flow, the characteristic function χEt(x) = χE(Φ−t(x)). Differentiate under the integral sign using the transport theorem: d/dt χEt = −∇χE·V = −δ∂E V·ν. The derivative of the double integral splits into two symmetric terms. After a change of variables and using the antisymmetry of the kernel, one obtains d/dt Pers(Et) = 2 ∫E ∫Ec (V(x)−V(y))·(x−y) |x−y|−n−s−2 dx dy. Restricting the inner integral to a neighbourhood of ∂E and passing to the limit yields the nonlocal mean curvature Hs(x) = ∫ℝn (χEc(y)−χE(y)) |x−y|−n−s dy. The final expression is ∫∂E Hs(x) V(x)·ν(x) dℋn−1(x). This matches the classical formula with Hs replacing the classical mean curvature.

Remember the extension problem: the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)s can be realised as a Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map on ℝn+1+, which often simplifies regularity arguments.

Variational Methods for Mean Field Games with Common Noise and the Master Equation

Mean field games (MFG) describe the limit of N‑player stochastic differential games as N → ∞. The standard MFG system couples a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function u(t,x) with a Fokker–Planck equation for the population density m(t,x). When a common noise W0 affects all players, the density becomes a random measure m(t,·,ω) and the value function depends on the whole measure flow. The master equation, introduced by Lions, is a PDE on the space of probability measures P(ℝd) that encodes the value function U(t,x,μ) as a function of the current state x and the current distribution μ. Its variational structure was clarified by Cardaliaguet, Delarue, Lasry and Lions (2019): the master equation is the first variation of a suitable action functional on the space of flows of measures.

Consider the stochastic control problem for a representative player: minimise 𝔼[∫0T L(Xs, αs, μs) ds + G(XT, μT)] subject to dXs = αs ds + σ dWs + σ0 dW0,s, where μs = Law(Xs|W0). The associated Lagrangian on the space of measure flows is 𝔸(μ) = 𝔼[∫0T ∫ L*(x, −∂tμt−div(μtb), μt) dx dt + ∫ G(x, μT) dμT(x)], where L* is the convex conjugate of L in the velocity variable. The master equation ∂tU + H(x, ∂xU, μ) − σ0²/2 Tr(∂μ²U) + ∫ ∂μU(x,μ)(y)·Hp(y, ∂xU(y,μ), μ) dμ(y) = 0 is precisely the condition that the first variation of 𝔸 vanishes. Here ∂μU denotes the Lions derivative on P(ℝd).

Existence of classical solutions to the master equation requires monotonicity conditions (Lasry–Lions monotonicity) and sufficient regularity of the data. The variational formulation enables the use of displacement convexity on the Wasserstein space to obtain a priori estimates. For numerical approximation, the master equation can be discretised by a particle method: represent μ by N particles and solve the resulting finite‑dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi equation, or by a finite‑difference scheme on a grid in the measure space using the linear functional derivative. Recent work by Chassagneux, Crisan and Delarue (2022) proves convergence of the particle method with rate N−1/2 under Lipschitz assumptions.

Variational Methods for Density Functional Theory: Hohenberg–Kohn and Kohn–Sham Functionals

Density functional theory (DFT) provides a variational framework for the ground‑state energy of a many‑electron system. The Hohenberg–Kohn theorem (1964) states that the ground‑state energy E0 = infρ≥0, ∫ρ=N F[ρ] + ∫ vext(r)ρ(r) dr, where F[ρ] is a universal functional of the electron density ρ, independent of the external potential vext. The exact F[ρ] = T[ρ] + Vee[ρ] contains the kinetic energy T[ρ] and the electron‑electron interaction Vee[ρ]. The Kohn–Sham construction introduces a non‑interacting reference system with the same density: F[ρ] = Ts[ρ] + ½∫∫ ρ(r)ρ(r')/|r−r'| dr dr' + Exc[ρ], where Ts[ρ] is the kinetic energy of non‑interacting electrons and Exc[ρ] is the exchange‑correlation functional. The Euler–Lagrange equations for the constrained minimisation yield the Kohn–Sham equations (−½Δ + veff(r)) φi(r) = εi φi(r) with veff = vext + vH + vxc, where vH is the Hartree potential and vxc = δExc/δρ.

The mathematical analysis of DFT focuses on the existence of minimisers for the Levy–Lieb functional FLL[ρ] = infΨ→ρ ⟨Ψ|T+Vee|Ψ⟩, where the infimum runs over antisymmetric wavefunctions yielding density ρ. Lieb (1983) proved that FLL is convex and lower semicontinuous on L1∩L3, and that a minimiser exists for any reasonable vext. The Kohn–Sham iteration is a fixed‑point map on the density; its convergence is not guaranteed for arbitrary approximations of Exc. The local density approximation (LDA) ExcLDA[ρ] = ∫ ρ(r) εxc(ρ(r)) dr uses the exchange‑correlation energy per particle of the homogeneous electron gas. Generalised gradient approximations (GGA) add dependence on |∇ρ|, while meta‑GGAs include the kinetic energy density τ(r) = ½∑|∇φi|². Hybrid functionals mix a fraction of exact exchange from Hartree–Fock.

From a variational perspective, the Kohn–Sham equations are the critical points of the energy functional on the manifold of Slater determinants. The second variation yields the random phase approximation (RPA) for excitation energies. Recent rigorous work by Friesecke, Kniely and others (2020) establishes Γ‑convergence of the discrete Kohn–Sham model (plane‑wave or finite‑element basis) to the continuum model, with error estimates depending on the regularity of the exact density. The optimised effective potential (OEP) method treats the exchange‑correlation potential as a multiplicative operator determined by the condition that the functional derivative of the total energy with respect to the orbitals vanishes, leading to an integral equation for vxc.

Variational Methods for Neural Network Training: Loss Landscapes, Implicit Regularization, and Generalization

Training a neural network amounts to minimising an empirical risk functional Rn(θ) = (1/n)∑i=1n ℓ(fθ(xi), yi) over parameters θ ∈ ℝp. The loss landscape is non‑convex and high‑dimensional; nevertheless, gradient‑based methods often find global minima with zero training error. A variational viewpoint interprets the continuous‑time limit of gradient descent as the gradient flow of Rn in the parameter space equipped with the Euclidean metric. When the network is over‑parameterised (p ≫ n), the set of global minima forms a manifold of dimension p−n. The implicit regularisation of gradient flow selects the minimum Euclidean norm solution among all interpolating parameters, which for linear models coincides with the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse solution.

For homogeneous networks (e.g. ReLU networks with no biases), the loss is scale‑invariant: Rn(cθ) = Rn(θ) for c>0. The gradient flow on the sphere ‖θ‖=1 converges to a critical point of the normalised loss. The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime arises when the initialisation scale tends to infinity; the network evolves linearly in function space and the training dynamics are governed by the kernel K(x,x') = ∇θfθ₀(x)·∇θfθ₀(x'). In this regime, the variational problem becomes convex in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space associated with K, and gradient descent converges to the minimum‑norm interpolant in that RKHS.

Generalisation bounds based on uniform stability or algorithmic robustness can be derived from the variational structure. For gradient descent with step size η and T steps, the uniform stability is O(ηT/n) under Lipschitz and smoothness assumptions. The PAC‑Bayesian framework provides a variational bound: for any posterior distribution Q on parameters, the expected risk is bounded by the empirical risk plus KL(Q‖P)/n, where P is a prior. Choosing Q as a Gaussian centred at the trained parameters yields a bound that correlates with the flatness of the minimum (small Hessian eigenvalues). Recent work by Dziugaite and Roy (2017) optimises this bound numerically to obtain non‑vacuous generalisation certificates for deep networks. The information‑bottleneck principle suggests that the network learns a representation that minimises I(X;T) − β I(T;Y), where T is the hidden layer activation; this is a variational problem over conditional distributions p(t|x).

Variational Methods for Nonlinear Elasticity: Quasiconvexity, Rank‑One Convexity, and Relaxation

In nonlinear elasticity the stored‑energy density W(F) depends on the deformation gradient F ∈ ℝ3×3. The total energy functional I(y) = ∫Ω W(∇y) dx is minimized subject to boundary conditions. Because W is typically non‑convex (frame indifference forces W(R F) = W(F) for all rotations R), the direct method of the calculus of variations fails: minimizing sequences may develop fine‑scale oscillations (microstructures). The correct notion of convexity for vector‑valued problems is quasiconvexity (Morrey, 1952): W is quasiconvex at F if for every bounded domain D and every φ ∈ W1,∞0(D;ℝ3), ∫D W(F+∇φ) dx ≥ |D| W(F). Quasiconvexity is equivalent to lower semicontinuity of I with respect to weak convergence in W1,p. A weaker but more tractable condition is rank‑one convexity: the map t ↦ W(F+ta⊗b) is convex for all a,b ∈ ℝ3. Every quasiconvex function is rank‑one convex, but the converse is false (Šverák, 1992). The quasiconvex envelope QW is the largest quasiconvex function ≤ W; the relaxed functional I**(y) = ∫Ω QW(∇y) dx provides the correct lower semicontinuous envelope. For multi‑well energies (e.g. martensitic phase transformations) the relaxation leads to laminate microstructures described by the lamination convex hull. The celebrated Ball–James theorem (1987) characterises exact stress‑free microstructures as gradients of piecewise affine maps whose gradients lie in the energy wells and satisfy the Hadamard jump condition (rank‑one connections). Numerically, relaxation is approximated by finite‑element discretisations with mesh‑dependent regularisation or by phase‑field models that penalise interfacial energy, yielding Γ‑convergence to the relaxed problem as the interfacial parameter → 0.

Variational Methods for Phase Field Models of Fracture: Ambrosio–Tortorelli Approximation and Γ‑Convergence

The variational approach to brittle fracture (Francfort–Marigo, 1998) seeks a pair (u, Γ) minimising the Griffith functional E(u,Γ) = ∫Ω\Γ W(∇u) dx + Gc ℋd‑1(Γ), where u is the displacement, Γ the crack set, W the elastic energy density, Gc the critical energy release rate, and ℋd‑1 the (d‑1)‑dimensional Hausdorff measure. Direct minimisation is hindered by the free‑discontinuity nature of Γ. Ambrosio and Tortorelli (1990, 1992) introduced a phase‑field regularisation using a scalar damage variable v ∈ [0,1] (1 = intact, 0 = fully broken) and a small parameter ε > 0: Eε(u,v) = ∫Ω (v²+ηε) W(∇u) dx + Gc ∫Ω ( (1‑v)²/(4ε) + ε|∇v|² ) dx, with ηε = o(ε) to avoid degeneracy. The second term is a Modica–Mortola approximation of the perimeter functional; as ε → 0 it Γ‑converges (in the topology of L1 × weak H1) to the Griffith functional, with the crack set identified as the jump set of the limit v. The Γ‑limit proof uses the Modica–Mortola theorem for the damage part and the lower semicontinuity of the elastic energy under the constraint v ∈ {0,1}. The Ambrosio–Tortorelli functional is the basis for most modern numerical fracture codes (finite elements, staggered or monolithic solvers). Extensions include anisotropic fracture toughness (Bourdin–Larsen, 2003), dynamic fracture (Larsen–Ortner–Süli, 2010), and coupling with plasticity (Miehe–Welschinger–Hofacker, 2010). The length‑scale parameter ε controls the width of the diffusive crack; mesh size h must satisfy h ≪ ε to resolve the transition layer, leading to the practical rule ε ≈ 3–5 h. Recent work (Bourdin, 2021) establishes convergence rates for the discrete scheme under mesh‑adaptivity.

Worked Example: Γ‑Convergence of a One‑Dimensional Ambrosio–Tortorelli Functional
Consider Ω = (0,1), W(u') = ½(u')², Gc = 1, ηε = 0. For a given ε > 0 define Eε(u,v) = ∫01 [ v² ½(u')² + (1‑v)²/(4ε) + ε(v')² ] dx. Show that any sequence (uε,vε) with uniformly bounded energy has a subsequence converging in L1 × weak H1 to a limit (u,v) where v ∈ {0,1} a.e., and the limit energy equals the Griffith energy ∫01 ½(u')² dx + ℋ0({x : v(x) = 0}).
Step 1 – Compactness. Boundedness of Eε gives ∫ ε(vε')² ≤ C, so vε is bounded in H1. By Rellich, a subsequence converges strongly in L2 and a.e. to v. The Modica–Mortola term forces (1‑vε)²/(4ε) to be bounded in L1; standard arguments (Modica, 1987) imply v ∈ {0,1} a.e. and the limit of the damage term is exactly the perimeter of the set {v=0}, i.e. the number of jump points ℋ0. Step 2 – Lower bound for elastic part. Since vε → v a.e. and 0 ≤ vε ≤ 1, Fatou's lemma yields liminf ∫ vε² ½(uε')² ≥ ∫ v² ½(u')². Because v ∈ {0,1}, the integrand vanishes on the crack set, reproducing the Griffith elastic energy on the uncracked region. Step 3 – Recovery sequence. Given a piecewise smooth u with jumps at points xi, construct vε as the optimal profile of the Modica–Mortola functional around each xi (the hyperbolic tangent solution of εv'' = (1‑v)/(2ε)) and set uε = u. One checks that the energy converges to the Griffith energy. Hence Γ‑convergence holds.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Γ‑limit of a phase‑field functional, first identify the Modica–Mortola part (perimeter approximation) and the bulk part (elastic energy weighted by the damage variable). State the compactness result (bounded energy ⇒ vε → v ∈ {0,1}) and then apply the standard lower‑bound / recovery‑sequence scheme. Remember the scaling: the interfacial energy is (1‑v)²/(4ε) + ε|∇v|²; the factor 1/4 is crucial for the exact perimeter constant. If the problem includes a small ηε, note that it only prevents degeneracy and does not affect the Γ‑limit provided ηε = o(ε).

Variational Methods for Optimal Transport: Entropic Regularization, Sinkhorn Algorithm, and the JKO Scheme

Classical optimal transport (Monge–Kantorovich) minimises ∫X×Y c(x,y) dπ(x,y) over couplings π with marginals μ, ν. The problem is a linear program on a huge space; for discrete measures with n points the cost matrix has size n². Entropic regularization (Cuturi, 2013) adds a negative entropy term ε ∫ log(dπ/d(μ⊗ν)) dπ, yielding a strictly convex problem whose solution is of the form dπε = exp((f(x)+g(y)‑c(x,y))/ε) dμ(x) dν(y). The potentials f,g satisfy the Sinkhorn fixed‑point equations: f(x) = ‑ε log ∫ exp((g(y)‑c(x,y))/ε) dν(y), and symmetrically for g. The Sinkhorn algorithm alternates these updates; it converges linearly and each iteration costs O(n²) for dense costs, but can be accelerated with low‑rank approximations or GPU parallelism. As ε → 0, πε Γ‑converges to an optimal transport plan (Cominetti–San Martín, 1994). The JKO scheme (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto, 1998) provides a variational time discretisation of the Wasserstein gradient flow of a functional F(ρ) (e.g. entropy, interaction energy): given ρk, define ρk+1 = argminρ { F(ρ) + (1/(2τ)) W2²(ρ,ρk) }. This is a proximal point algorithm in the Wasserstein space (ℙ2(ℝd), W2). The scheme converges as τ → 0 to the gradient flow curve solving the continuity equation ∂tρ + ∇·(ρ∇(δF/δρ)) = 0. For the Boltzmann entropy F(ρ) = ∫ ρ log ρ, the flow is the heat equation; for interaction energies it yields aggregation‑diffusion equations. The JKO scheme is the theoretical backbone of many numerical methods (particle methods, finite volumes) and of the Wasserstein proximal gradient algorithms used in machine learning (e.g. training generative models). Recent work (Peyré–Cuturi, 2019) combines entropic regularization with the JKO step, leading to the entropic JKO scheme where each proximal subproblem is solved by Sinkhorn iterations, providing a fully discrete, scalable algorithm for high‑dimensional gradient flows.

Variational Methods for Inverse Problems: Sparsity‑Promoting Regularization, Bregman Iterations, and Exact Recovery

Inverse problems seek to recover an unknown signal x ∈ ℝn from noisy linear measurements y = Ax + η, where A ∈ ℝm×n (m ≪ n). The variational formulation minimises a data‑fidelity term plus a regulariser: J(x) = ½‖Ax‑y‖₂² + λ R(x). For sparse signals, R(x) = ‖x‖₁ (ℓ¹ norm) promotes sparsity (Basis Pursuit Denoising, Chen–Donoho–Saunders, 1998). The restricted isometry property (RIP) of A (Candès–Tao, 2005) guarantees exact recovery of k‑sparse vectors when δ2k < √2‑1. When A does not satisfy RIP, Bregman iterations (Osher–Burger–Goldfarb–Xu–Yin, 2005) accelerate convergence: starting with p0 = 0, iterate xk+1 = argminx { ½‖Ax‑y‖₂² + λ DRpk(x,xk) }, pk+1 = pk + Aᵀ(y‑Axk+1), where DRp is the Bregman distance associated with R. For R = ‖·‖₁ this reduces to a sequence of weighted ℓ¹ problems solvable by soft‑thresholding. The method converges in a few iterations to a solution of the original ℓ¹ problem, and under a source condition (existence of a dual certificate) it achieves exact recovery even for non‑RIP matrices. Extensions include total variation regularisation (Rudin–Osher–Fatemi, 1992) for piecewise constant images, nuclear norm for low‑rank matrix completion (Candès–Recht, 2009), and analysis sparsity (cosparsity) where R(x) = ‖Ωx‖₁ with a frame Ω. The primal‑dual hybrid gradient (PDHG, Chambolle–Pock, 2011) solves the saddle‑point formulation minx maxp { ⟨Ax,p⟩ ‑ ½‖p‑y‖₂² + λ R(x) } with O(1/k) ergodic convergence. Recent non‑convex regularisers (ℓp, 0 < p < 1, SCAD, MCP) are handled by iteratively reweighted ℓ¹ (Candès–Wakin–Boyd, 2008) or difference‑of‑convex algorithms, with convergence to critical points under Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz assumptions.

Variational Methods for Geometric Flows: Ricci Flow, Perelman's Entropy Functional, and the W‑Functional

Ricci flow ∂t g = ‑2 Ric(g) (Hamilton, 1982) deforms a Riemannian metric g on a closed manifold M by its Ricci curvature. Hamilton showed short‑time existence and that the flow preserves positivity of curvature operators. The breakthrough for the Poincaré conjecture came from Perelman's (2002‑2003) introduction of two monotone quantities. The Perelman entropy W(g,f,τ) = ∫M [ τ(|∇f|² + R) + f ‑ n ] (4πτ)‑n/2 e‑f dvolg, where R is scalar curvature, f a smooth function, τ > 0 a scale parameter, and the integral is constrained by ∫ (4πτ)‑n/2 e‑f dvol = 1. Under the coupled system ∂t g = ‑2 Ric, ∂t f = ‑Δf + |∇f|² ‑ R + n/(2τ), ∂t τ = ‑1, the functional satisfies d/dt W = 2τ ∫ |Ric + ∇²f ‑ g/(2τ)|² (4πτ)‑n/2 e‑f dvol ≥ 0. Monotonicity of W rules out non‑trivial breathers and gives a no‑local‑collapsing theorem: if W is bounded below at some time, then the flow cannot collapse volume at small scales. The reduced length l(x,τ) and reduced volume Ṽ(τ) = ∫ (4πτ)‑n/2 e‑l dvol provide a second monotone quantity, directly linked to the asymptotic soliton structure at singularities. Perelman's surgery procedure cuts off high‑curvature regions (κ‑solutions) and continues the flow, using the monotonicity of W and Ṽ to control the geometry of the pieces. The variational interpretation: W is the logarithm of the partition function of a statistical mechanical system on the space of metrics; its critical points are gradient shrinking Ricci solitons (Ric + ∇²f = g/(2τ)). The W‑functional also appears in the optimal transport formulation of Ricci flow (Lott–Villani, 2009; Sturm, 2006) where displacement convexity of the entropy on the Wasserstein space corresponds to lower bounds on Ricci curvature. Recent work (Bamler, 2020) uses the entropy‑heat flow coupling to obtain quantitative stratification of singular sets and to prove uniqueness of the flow through singularities for 3‑manifolds.

Variational Principles for the Navier–Stokes Equations and the Leray–Hopf Theory

The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations ∂tu + (u·∇)u − νΔu + ∇p = f, ∇·u = 0 on a domain Ω ⊂ ℝ3 admit a formal variational structure when viewed through the lens of the energy functional E(u) = ½∫Ω|u|2 dx. The viscous dissipation D(u) = ν∫Ω|∇u|2 dx and the work of external forces W(u) = ∫Ωf·u dx combine to give the energy balance d/dt E(u) + D(u) = W(u). Leray (1934) and Hopf (1951) constructed global weak solutions by minimizing the time-integrated action ∫0T[½‖∂tu‖H−12 + ν‖∇u‖L22 − ⟨f, u⟩] dt over divergence-free vector fields, using the Galerkin approximation and compactness arguments (Aubin–Lions lemma). The resulting Leray–Hopf solutions satisfy the energy inequality E(u(t)) + 2ν∫0t‖∇u(s)‖L22 ds ≤ E(u0) + 2∫0t⟨f(s), u(s)⟩ ds for almost every t. Uniqueness and regularity of these solutions in three dimensions remain open (Millennium Prize problem). The variational approach extends to the Navier–Stokes–Voigt regularization (adding α2‖∇∂tu‖L22), the hyperviscous model (ν(−Δ)mu), and the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations where the action includes a martingale term. The pressure p acts as a Lagrange multiplier enforcing ∇·u = 0, and the Helmholtz projection ℙ onto divergence-free fields yields the projected equation ∂tu + ℙ(u·∇)u + νAu = ℙf with A = −ℙΔ the Stokes operator. The attractor dimension estimates (Foias–Temam, 1979) use the variational structure to bound the fractal dimension of the global attractor by c(ν−3‖f‖L2)2/3.

Variational Methods for the p‑Laplacian and Nonstandard Growth Conditions

The p‑Laplacian Δpu = div(|∇u|p−2∇u) arises as the Euler–Lagrange operator of the Dirichlet integral ∫Ω|∇u|p dx for p ∈ (1, ∞). For p ≠ 2 the functional is non‑quadratic, leading to degenerate (p > 2) or singular (p < 2) ellipticity. The natural energy space is the Sobolev space W1,p0(Ω), and the direct method yields existence of minimizers for the p‑Dirichlet problem with homogeneous boundary data. The Euler–Lagrange equation −Δpu = f admits a unique weak solution for f ∈ W−1,p′(Ω) by the Minty–Browder theorem on monotone operators. Regularity theory (Ural’tseva, 1968; DiBenedetto, 1983) establishes that local minimizers are C1,α for some α ∈ (0,1), with the optimal exponent α = α(n,p) known explicitly. For nonstandard growth functionals ∫ΩΦ(∇u) dx where Φ is an N‑function satisfying the Δ2 condition, the Orlicz–Sobolev space W1,Φ(Ω) replaces W1,p. The Lavrentiev phenomenon — a gap between the infimum in W1,Φ and in smoother classes — can occur when Φ grows too rapidly (Ball–Mizel, 1985). Variable exponent spaces W1,p(x)(Ω) with p ∈ C0,log(Ω̄) model electrorheological fluids; the modular ∫|∇u|p(x) dx lacks homogeneity, and the log‑Hölder continuity of p(x) is essential for density of smooth functions and the validity of the Poincaré inequality. The (p,q)‑Laplacian −Δpu − Δqu combines two growth regimes and appears in models of non‑Newtonian fluids with shear‑thinning/thickening transitions. Critical point theory on W1,p uses the Palais–Smale condition, which holds for subcritical growth (p* = np/(n−p) for p < n) but fails at the critical exponent, requiring concentration‑compactness (Lions, 1984) or the Nehari manifold method.

Worked Problem: p‑Laplacian Eigenvalue
Find the first eigenvalue λ1(p) of the Dirichlet p‑Laplacian on a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝn, i.e. the smallest λ > 0 such that −Δpu = λ|u|p−2u in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω has a nontrivial solution.
The first eigenvalue is given by the Rayleigh quotient λ1(p) = inf{∫Ω|∇u|p dx / ∫Ω|u|p dx : u ∈ W1,p0(Ω), u ≠ 0}. The infimum is attained by a positive eigenfunction u1 ∈ C1,α(Ω̄) (Anane, 1987). Simplicity holds: any eigenfunction corresponding to λ1 is a scalar multiple of u1. The variational characterization implies monotonicity in p: if 1 < p < q then λ1(p)1/p ≤ λ1(q)1/q (Lindqvist, 1993). As p → ∞, λ1(p)1/p → 1/R where R is the inradius of Ω (Juutinen–Lindqvist–Manfredi, 1999). The limit problem is the ∞‑Laplacian Δ∞u = ⟨∇u, D2u ∇u⟩ = 0 with the eigenvalue condition |∇u| = Λ on ∂Ω, where Λ = 1/R.
Exam Strategy: p‑Laplacian Scaling
When a question asks for the scaling of λ1(p) under domain dilation Ω → tΩ, use the homogeneity: ∇u(t·) = t∇u, so ∫|∇u|p scales as tp−n and ∫|u|p scales as t−n. Hence λ1(p; tΩ) = t−pλ1(p; Ω). This quick check eliminates wrong options in multiple‑choice questions.

Variational Principles for the Vlasov–Maxwell System and Plasma Physics

The Vlasov–Maxwell system describes a collisionless plasma: ∂tfs + v·∇xfs + (qs/ms)(E + v×B)·∇vfs = 0 for each species s, coupled to Maxwell’s equations ∂tE = c2∇×B − j, ∂tB = −∇×E, ∇·E = ρ, ∇·B = 0 with ρ = Σsqs∫fs dv, j = Σsqs∫v fs dv. Low (1958) and Ye & Morrison (1992) identified a Hamiltonian structure on the cotangent bundle of the diffeomorphism group of phase space. The action functional A(f, A, φ) = ∫[Σs∫fs(½ms|v|2 + qsφ − qsv·A) dv dx − ½∫(|E|2 − |B|2) dx] dt, with E = −∇φ − ∂tA, B = ∇×A, yields the Vlasov–Maxwell equations via Hamilton’s principle δA = 0. The particle relabeling symmetry gives the Casimir invariants CΦ(f) = ∫Φ(f) dx dv for any smooth Φ, leading to the energy–Casimir method for nonlinear stability of equilibria (Guo, 1996). The Vlasov–Poisson subsystem (B = 0) has the free energy functional H(f) = ½∫|v|2f dv dx + ½∫|∇φ|2 dx with φ solving −Δφ = ∫f dv − 1. The H‑theorem dH/dt ≤ 0 holds for the Vlasov–Fokker–Planck equation with collision operator. For the relativistic Vlasov–Maxwell system, the action uses the Lorentz‑invariant phase space measure and the particle Hamiltonian h = √(m2c4 + c2|p − qA|2) + qφ. Global existence of weak solutions (DiPerna–Lions, 1989) uses renormalization techniques; strong solutions exist locally in time (Glassey–Strauss, 1986) and globally for small data (Pallard, 2001). The variational structure underpins geometric particle‑in‑cell methods that preserve the discrete symplectic form and Casimirs.

Variational Methods for the Ginzburg–Landau Model of Superconductivity

The Ginzburg–Landau functional Gκ(ψ, A) = ∫Ω[|(∇ − iκA)ψ|2 + (κ2/2)(1 − |ψ|2)2 + |curl A|2] dx models a type‑II superconductor in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ2 or ℝ3. Here ψ : Ω → ℂ is the order parameter, A : Ω → ℝ2 (or ℝ3) the magnetic potential, and κ > 0 the Ginzburg–Landau parameter. The Euler–Lagrange equations are the covariant Schrödinger equation −(∇ − iκA)2ψ = κ2ψ(1 − |ψ|2) and the Ampère law −ΔA = ⟨iψ, (∇ − iκA)ψ⟩. For κ < 1/√2 the model exhibits type‑I behavior (Meissner state); for κ > 1/√2 vortices form a triangular Abrikosov lattice. Bethuel–Brezis–Hélein (1994) proved that as κ → ∞, minimizers develop quantized vortices with degree ±1 at points a1, …, aN minimizing the renormalized energy W(a1, …, aN) = −π Σi≠j didj log|ai − aj| + π Σi di H(ai) where H is the boundary interaction term. The vortex number N ∼ κ2|log κ| for the ground state in a simply connected domain. The Γ‑limit of κ−2Gκ as κ → ∞ is the sum of the vortex core energy πN|log κ| and the renormalized energy (Sandier–Serfaty, 2000). For the time‑dependent Ginzburg–Landau equations ∂tψ = (∇ − iκA)2ψ + κ2ψ(1 − |ψ|2), ∂tA = −∇φ − J with J = ⟨iψ, (∇ − iκA)ψ⟩, the energy dissipation d/dt Gκ = −∫(|∂tψ|2 + |∂tA|2) dx holds. The mixed flow (ψ, A) → (eiφψ, A + ∇φ) is a gauge symmetry; the Coulomb gauge div A = 0 fixes it. Numerical methods use the gauge‑invariant finite element discretization (Du–Gunzburger–Peterson, 1992) preserving the energy law at the discrete level.

Variational Principles for the N‑Body Problem and Celestial Mechanics

The classical N‑body problem with Newtonian potential V(q) = −Σi<j</sub> mimj/|qi − qj| has the action functional A(q) = ∫[½ Σ mi|q̇i|2 + Σi<j</sub> mimj/|qi − qj|] dt on the configuration space (ℝ3)N Δ where Δ is the collision set. The Euler–Lagrange equations are miq̈i = Σj≠i mimj(qj − qi)/|qi − qj|3. The direct method fails due to the lack of coercivity (the potential is negative and unbounded below near collisions). Marchal (1990) and Chenciner (2002) used the Sobolev space H1(S1, (ℝ3)N) for T‑periodic loops and the minimax principle on the loop space with the symmetry group SO(3) × S1 to find periodic solutions avoiding collisions (the “figure‑eight” choreography for N = 3, Chenciner–Montgomery, 2000). The Kepler problem (N = 2) is integrable; the regularized Levi‑Civita or Kustaanheimo–Stiefel transformations remove the collision singularity and yield a harmonic oscillator on S3. For N ≥ 3, the Sundman inequality ∫|q̇|2 dt ≥ c(∫|q|−1 dt)2 controls the action near collisions. The Jacobi–Maupertuis principle reformulates the problem as a geodesic flow on the configuration manifold with the conformal metric (E + V(q))Σ midqi2 for fixed energy E > 0. The Maupertuis action ∫√(2(E + V)) √(Σ mi|dqi|2) is minimized by physical trajectories. Variational methods also construct heteroclinic and homoclinic orbits connecting relative equilibria (central configurations), using the mountain‑pass theorem on the energy surface. The restricted three‑body problem admits a variational formulation in the rotating frame with the Jacobi integral as a first integral; the Lagrange points L4, L5 are minimizers of the effective potential for mass ratio μ < 0.0385 (Routh’s criterion).
