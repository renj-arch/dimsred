Part 1

🧲 Monopoles and the Seiberg-Witten Equations

Monopoles
The Seiberg–Witten Equations: Dirac Operator and Curvature
Let M be a closed oriented 4-manifold with a Spinc-structure (a lift of the structure group of TM from SO(4) to Spinc(4) = (Spin(4) × U(1))/ℤ₂). A Spinc-structure determines a determinant line bundle L = det⁺(W⁺) (the line bundle associated to the U(1) factor) and a pair of spinor bundles W⁺, W⁻ (complex rank 2 and 1 respectively). The Seiberg–Witten equations for a pair (A, ψ) where A is a unitary connection on L and ψ is a section of W⁺ are: (1) The Dirac equation: D̸_A ψ = 0, where D̸_A: Γ(W⁺) → Γ(W⁻) is the Dirac operator coupled to A. (2) The curvature equation: F_A⁺ = (ψ⊗ψ*)_₀, where F_A⁺ = (F_A + *F_A)/2 is the self-dual part of the curvature, and (ψ⊗ψ*)_₀ = ψ⊗ψ* − (|ψ|²/2) id is the trace-free Hermitian endomorphism of W⁺. The equations are invariant under gauge transformations g: M → U(1) acting as (A, ψ) → (A − 2 d log g, gψ). The moduli space M(L) of solutions modulo gauge equivalence is a compact oriented manifold of dimension d(L) = c₁(L)² − (2χ(M) + 3σ(M))/4 (where χ(M) is the Euler characteristic and σ(M) is the signature). The perturbed Seiberg–Witten equations F_A⁺ = (ψ⊗ψ*)_₀ + iη (where η is a self-dual 2-form) are used to achieve transversality — for a generic perturbation η, the moduli space is smooth.
The Seiberg–Witten equations couple a gauge field to a spinor; solutions (monopoles) form a compact moduli space whose dimension is computed by characteristic classes of the Spinc-structure.
Part 2

🏆 Seiberg-Witten Invariants of 4-Manifolds

Invariants
Definition of Seiberg–Witten Invariants, Wall-Crossing, and Vanishing Theorems
The Seiberg–Witten invariant SW_M(L) ∈ ℤ for a Spinc-structure with determinant L is defined as follows. When the moduli space M(L) has formal dimension d = 0, the invariant is the signed count of points in M(L) (with sign determined by orientations of the determinant line bundle of the Dirac operator). When d > 0, SW_M(L) is the evaluation of the top cohomology class (the Euler class of a certain line bundle over M(L) raised to the power d/2) against the fundamental class. The wall-crossing formula (Seiberg–Witten, 1994): when the dimension d(L) = 0, the Seiberg–Witten invariant changes by ±1 under a change of metric that crosses a wall where the moduli space becomes reducible (i.e., ψ = 0 solutions exist). The walls correspond to harmonic self-dual 2-forms η satisfying ∫_M η ∧ c₁(L) = 0. The vanishing theorem (Witten): if a closed 4-manifold M has b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1 and admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, then all Seiberg–Witten invariants vanish. Conversely, if SW_M(L) ≠ 0 for some L, then M admits no metric of positive scalar curvature. The Kronheimer–Mrowka theorem: if M is a 4-manifold with b₂⁺(M) ≥ 2, the Seiberg–Witten invariants are diffeomorphism invariants (independent of the choice of metric and perturbation). The simple type conjecture (Taubes, Kronheimer–Mrowka): for many 4-manifolds, all Seiberg–Witten invariants vanish unless d(L) = 0 — the simple type condition.
Seiberg–Witten invariants are powerful diffeomorphism invariants of smooth 4-manifolds; they detect the existence of positive scalar curvature metrics and distinguish smooth structures.

🔗 Relationship with Donaldson Theory

Donaldson
The Witten Conjecture: Equivalence of Seiberg–Witten and Donaldson Invariants
Donaldson theory (Donaldson, 1982) defined invariants of smooth 4-manifolds using the moduli space of anti-self-dual (ASD) connections on a principal SU(2)-bundle. The Donaldson polynomial invariants q_m: H₂(M) → ℤ are determined by the cohomology ring of the ASD moduli space. The Witten conjecture (1994): the Donaldson invariants are equivalent to the Seiberg–Witten invariants via the formula: D_{M,w}(z) = Σ_{L} SW_M(L) exp(⟨c₁(L), z⟩/2) where the sum is over all Spinc-structures L whose first Chern class is characteristic (c₁(L) ≡ w₂(M) mod 2). This was motivated by the physical equivalence of N = 2 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory and the Abelian monopole theory via Seiberg–Witten duality (Seiberg–Witten, 1994): the low-energy effective action of N = 2 SU(2) SYM is described by a Seiberg–Witten curve and a prepotential that encodes the Donaldson invariants. The proof of the Witten conjecture was completed by Feehan–Leness (2003) using the PU(2) monopole equations — a non-abelian generalization of the Seiberg–Witten equations that interpolates between the ASD equations and the abelian Seiberg–Witten equations via a blow-up analysis. The Kotschick–Morgan–Taubes theorem gives an alternative proof for Kähler surfaces. The equivalence shows that Seiberg–Witten theory is strictly easier to work with computationally while capturing the same information: the Seiberg–Witten invariants determine the Donaldson invariants, and vice versa.
The Witten conjecture (proved by Feehan–Leness) establishes the equivalence of the two great gauge-theoretic invariants of 4-manifolds, showing Seiberg–Witten theory subsumes Donaldson theory.

🌊 Applications to Symplectic Geometry and Contact Topology

Applications
Taubes's Theorem, Symplectic 4-Manifolds, and Contact Invariants
Taubes's theorem (1994–1996): Let (M, ω) be a closed symplectic 4-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 1. Then for the canonical Spinc-structure K (whose determinant line bundle is the canonical bundle K = ∧²⁺T*M), the Seiberg–Witten invariant satisfies SW_M(K) = ±1. For all other Spinc-structures, the Seiberg–Witten invariant is zero. This remarkable result implies that the Seiberg–Witten invariants detect the symplectic structure: a 4-manifold with non-vanishing Seiberg–Witten invariants admits a symplectic structure if and only if the basic classes are related by the adjunction formula. The adjunction inequality: for any smoothly embedded surface Σ ⊂ M of genus g(Σ), we have 2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ ⟨c₁(L), Σ⟩ + Σ · Σ whenever SW_M(L) ≠ 0 — providing powerful restrictions on embeddings of surfaces. The symplectic Thom conjecture (proved by Ozsváth–Szabó and Taubes): a symplectic surface in a symplectic 4-manifold minimizes the genus in its homology class. The contact Seiberg–Witten invariants (Kronheimer–Mrowka, 1995): for a contact 3-manifold (Y, ξ), there is a contact invariant in the monopole Floer homology of Y that detects the overtwisted vs. tight classification. The Weinstein conjecture — that every contact structure on a closed 3-manifold has a closed Reeb orbit — was proved by Taubes (2007) using Seiberg–Witten theory. The embedded contact homology (ECH) (Hutchings, 2002) is a topological invariant of 3-manifolds defined using Seiberg–Witten theory and holomorphic curves, and Taubes proved ECH ≅ SWF (Seiberg–Witten Floer homology).
Seiberg–Witten theory has profound applications to symplectic and contact geometry: it detects symplectic structures, constrains surface embeddings, and computes contact invariants.

The Intricate Structure of Seiberg-Witten Equations and Moduli Spaces

The Seiberg-Witten equations are a system of non-linear partial differential equations defined on a smooth, closed 4-manifold M. Their profound utility stems from their ability to probe the smooth structure of M. To understand their intricate structure, one must first grasp the underlying geometric setup. The equations are formulated for a Spinᶜ structure on M, which is a choice of a complex line bundle L and a Spinᶜ connection A on L, along with a Spinᶜ bundle W. The bundle W is a complex vector bundle of rank 2, locally isomorphic to M × ℂ², equipped with a Hermitian metric and a compatible connection induced by A. This W is the bundle of Spinᶜ spinors, and its sections are spinor fields, denoted as ψ.

The equations themselves consist of two coupled components:

  1. The Dirac Equation: DAψ = 0. Here, DA is the Spinᶜ Dirac operator, which acts on sections of W. This operator is a first-order elliptic differential operator that incorporates the connection A. The Dirac equation essentially states that the spinor field ψ must be a harmonic spinor with respect to the chosen connection.
  2. The Curvature Equation: F⁺(A) = σ(ψ). This equation relates the self-dual part of the curvature of the connection A, denoted F⁺(A), to a quadratic expression in the spinor field ψ, denoted σ(ψ). The term σ(ψ) is a self-dual 2-form constructed from the spinor field, specifically σ(ψ) = (ψ, γjψ)dxj ∧ dxk, where γj are gamma matrices and the expression is properly antisymmetrized and projected onto the self-dual part of the 2-forms. The self-dual part of the curvature F⁺(A) is obtained by decomposing the curvature 2-form F(A) into its self-dual and anti-self-dual components, F(A) = F⁺(A) + F⁻(A), with respect to the Hodge star operator on the 4-manifold.

Solutions (A, ψ) to these coupled equations are called Seiberg-Witten monopoles. The set of all solutions, modulo gauge transformations, forms the Seiberg-Witten moduli space, denoted M(M, L). A gauge transformation is a map g: M → U(1) that acts on the connection A by A ↦ A + 2i g⁻¹dg and on the spinor field ψ by ψ ↦ gψ. The moduli space is the central object from which the Seiberg-Witten invariants are extracted. For generic choices of Riemannian metric g and a perturbation term η (a self-dual 2-form), the moduli space M(M, L) is a compact, oriented manifold of dimension d = (c₁(L)² - (2χ(M) + 3τ(M)))/4, where χ(M) is the Euler characteristic and τ(M) is the signature of M. The dimension d is crucial: if d ≠ 0, the invariant is usually zero unless d is even and certain conditions are met. If d = 0, the invariant is simply the signed count of points in the moduli space. The regularity of the moduli space is ensured by choosing a generic metric and perturbation, which makes the linearized operator surjective. Compactness is a deeper result, relying on a priori estimates for solutions. The orientation of the moduli space is determined by a choice of orientation for the determinant line bundle of the linearized Seiberg-Witten operator. The existence of these properties allows for the definition of robust invariants that are independent of the chosen metric and perturbation, provided certain topological conditions are met, particularly b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1.

Computational Aspects: Basic Classes, Wall-Crossing, and Metric Dependence

The Seiberg-Witten invariants, SW_M(L), are integers associated with a smooth 4-manifold M and a Spinᶜ structure L. Their computation and interpretation involve several nuanced concepts, including basic classes, the role of the metric, and wall-crossing phenomena. The invariant is essentially a count of points in the moduli space M(M, L) of solutions to the Seiberg-Witten equations, weighted by their signs, when the expected dimension of the moduli space is zero. If the expected dimension is positive, the invariant is defined by pairing the fundamental class of the moduli space with appropriate cohomology classes, typically powers of the Chern class of the universal bundle over the moduli space, to yield an integer.

A Spinᶜ structure L is called a basic class if its Seiberg-Witten invariant SW_M(L) is non-zero. For a given 4-manifold, there are typically only a finite number of basic classes. These basic classes carry significant topological information about the manifold. The set of basic classes is often denoted by SWM(M). The first Chern class c₁(L) of a basic class L is a characteristic class, meaning c₁(L) ≡ w₂(M) mod 2, where w₂(M) is the second Stiefel-Whitney class of M. This condition is fundamental and restricts the possible candidates for basic classes.

While the Seiberg-Witten invariants themselves are independent of the choice of Riemannian metric on M (for b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1), the moduli space of solutions *does* depend on the metric. This independence is achieved through a process called wall-crossing. As the Riemannian metric g (or the perturbation term η) is varied, the moduli space M(M, L) can undergo changes in its topology. Specifically, the dimension of the moduli space can change, or new solutions can appear or disappear. These changes occur when the metric crosses a "wall" in the infinite-dimensional space of all possible metrics. However, the *net count* of solutions (or the pairing with cohomology classes) remains constant across these walls, ensuring the invariant is well-defined. This stability is a powerful feature of the theory, making the invariants robust topological tools.

The perturbation term η, a self-dual 2-form, is often introduced into the curvature equation as F⁺(A) = σ(ψ) + η. This term is crucial for ensuring the regularity of the moduli space, meaning that the linearized Seiberg-Witten operator is surjective at every solution. Without a generic perturbation, the moduli space might be singular, making a well-defined count or integration difficult. The choice of η must be generic enough to avoid singularities, but the final invariant is independent of this choice, again due to wall-crossing arguments. The actual computation of these invariants often involves sophisticated techniques, including localization formulas in quantum field theory, or geometric arguments involving gluing and blow-up constructions for specific manifolds. For example, for a symplectic 4-manifold (M, ω) with b₂⁺ ≥ 1, Taubes's theorem states that only the canonical Spinᶜ structure K (whose determinant line bundle is the canonical bundle K = ∧²⁺T*M) can have a non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariant, and SW_M(K) = ±1. This provides a direct computational result for a significant class of manifolds.

Understanding the Vanishing Theorem
Consider a closed 4-manifold M. If M admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, what can be concluded about its Seiberg-Witten invariants, assuming b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1?
A) All Seiberg-Witten invariants are non-zero.
B) All Seiberg-Witten invariants vanish.
C) Only the Seiberg-Witten invariant for the canonical Spinᶜ structure vanishes.
D) The Seiberg-Witten invariants are undefined in this case.
The correct answer is B) All Seiberg-Witten invariants vanish.
Explanation: This question directly tests knowledge of Witten's Vanishing Theorem. The theorem states that if a closed 4-manifold M has b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1 and admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, then all its Seiberg-Witten invariants vanish. Conversely, if SW_M(L) ≠ 0 for some L, then M admits no metric of positive scalar curvature. This theorem provides a powerful obstruction to the existence of positive scalar curvature metrics, making Seiberg-Witten invariants a crucial tool in differential geometry. The presence of positive scalar curvature simplifies the Dirac equation, often forcing the spinor field ψ to be identically zero, which in turn implies the vanishing of the curvature term and thus the absence of non-trivial solutions, leading to vanishing invariants.
Mastering Gauge Theory Concepts
When dealing with advanced topics like Seiberg-Witten theory, focus on understanding the "why" behind each component. Why is a Spinᶜ structure necessary? Why is a perturbation term used? Why does the invariant become metric-independent despite the moduli space being metric-dependent? Connecting these theoretical underpinnings to their practical implications (e.g., detecting symplectic structures, ruling out positive scalar curvature) will solidify your understanding and help you tackle complex analytical questions. Break down the equations into their geometric and analytical components, and visualize the objects involved (bundles, connections, spinors) where possible.

Seiberg-Witten Floer Homology: Bridging 3- and 4-Manifold Topology

While Seiberg-Witten theory is primarily known for its invariants of 4-manifolds, it also gives rise to powerful invariants for 3-manifolds, known as Seiberg-Witten Floer homology (SWF). This theory, developed by Kronheimer and Mrowka, extends the ideas of finite-dimensional Morse theory to an infinite-dimensional setting. For a closed 3-manifold Y, SWF is constructed by considering the configuration space of U(1)-connections and Spinᶜ spinors on Y. This space is an infinite-dimensional manifold, and the Seiberg-Witten equations on Y define a functional whose critical points correspond to solutions of the equations. The homology groups are then defined by counting trajectories between these critical points, similar to how Morse homology is constructed.

Specifically, the critical points of the Chern-Simons-Dirac functional on Y are solutions to the 3-dimensional Seiberg-Witten equations. These equations are a reduction of the 4-dimensional equations to a 3-manifold boundary. The gradient flow lines between these critical points correspond to solutions of the 4-dimensional Seiberg-Witten equations on Y × ℝ. The SWF groups, denoted SWF*(Y, L), are graded Z-modules (or Z₂-modules in some contexts) associated with a Spinᶜ structure L on Y. They are invariants of the oriented diffeomorphism class of Y.

One of the most significant aspects of SWF is its deep connection to other Floer homologies and its ability to distinguish between different types of 3-manifolds and their structures. For instance, SWF plays a crucial role in contact topology. For a contact 3-manifold (Y, ξ), where ξ is a contact structure, Kronheimer and Mrowka defined a contact invariant in the monopole Floer homology of Y. This invariant can distinguish between tight and overtwisted contact structures, a fundamental classification problem in 3-dimensional contact geometry. An overtwisted contact structure is characterized by the presence of an overtwisted disk, and the contact invariant can detect its absence, indicating a tight structure.

Furthermore, Taubes proved a monumental result in 2007, confirming the Weinstein conjecture for all closed 3-manifolds. The conjecture states that every contact structure on a closed 3-manifold has at least one closed Reeb orbit. Taubes's proof relied heavily on Seiberg-Witten theory, specifically by showing that the Embedded Contact Homology (ECH) of a 3-manifold, which counts certain holomorphic curves in its symplectization, is isomorphic to its Seiberg-Witten Floer homology (ECH ≅ SWF). This isomorphism allowed the translation of results from gauge theory to the realm of contact geometry, ultimately proving the conjecture.

The relationship between SWF and other Floer theories, such as Heegaard Floer homology (HFH) developed by Ozsváth and Szabó, is also a rich area of study. While distinct in their construction (HFH uses Heegaard decompositions and holomorphic disks), there are deep isomorphisms and exact triangles relating these theories, demonstrating their complementary nature in probing 3-manifold topology. For example, for certain classes of 3-manifolds, there are known isomorphisms between SWF and HFH, highlighting the robust nature of these invariants across different theoretical frameworks.

Unveiling Exotic Smooth Structures with Seiberg-Witten Invariants

One of the most remarkable applications of Seiberg-Witten theory is its power to detect and distinguish exotic smooth structures on 4-manifolds. An exotic smooth structure on a topological manifold M is a smooth manifold M' that is homeomorphic to M but not diffeomorphic to M. Such phenomena are unique to dimensions 4 and higher, with dimension 4 being particularly complex. For instance, in dimensions other than 4, if two smooth manifolds are homeomorphic, they are often diffeomorphic (e.g., in dimension 3, the Poincaré conjecture implies this for spheres). However, in dimension 4, there exist infinitely many distinct smooth structures on R⁴ (exotic R⁴s) and on many compact 4-manifolds.

Seiberg-Witten invariants provide a powerful tool for distinguishing these structures. If two smooth 4-manifolds M₁ and M₂ are diffeomorphic, then their Seiberg-Witten invariants must be identical. Therefore, if SW_M₁(L) ≠ SW_M₂(L) for some Spinᶜ structure L, then M₁ and M₂ cannot be diffeomorphic, even if they are homeomorphic. This provides a direct method for demonstrating the existence of exotic smooth structures.

A classic example involves the work of Donaldson, which predates Seiberg-Witten theory but is deeply connected via the Witten conjecture. Donaldson's theorem on the diagonalizability of intersection forms showed that if a closed, simply connected 4-manifold has a definite intersection form, then it must be diagonalizable over the integers. This result, derived from Donaldson invariants, immediately implied the existence of exotic smooth structures on certain topological 4-manifolds. For example, the E₈ manifold, a topological 4-manifold with an intersection form that is definite but not diagonalizable, cannot admit a smooth structure. This was a groundbreaking result, demonstrating the topological manifold exists but cannot be smoothed.

Seiberg-Witten theory refined and extended these insights. For instance, Taubes's work on symplectic 4-manifolds showed that if a 4-manifold admits a symplectic structure, its Seiberg-Witten invariants for the canonical Spinᶜ structure are ±1. This provides a criterion: if a 4-manifold has vanishing Seiberg-Witten invariants for all Spinᶜ structures, it cannot admit a symplectic structure. This helps distinguish smooth structures by showing that some smooth structures can carry a symplectic form while others, homeomorphic to them, cannot.

The existence of exotic R⁴s is another striking application. Friedman and Gompf constructed specific examples of exotic R⁴s using gauge theory. While Seiberg-Witten invariants are typically defined for compact manifolds, their principles extend to open manifolds through various compactification or end-related techniques. The ability of Seiberg-Witten invariants to obstruct smooth h-cobordisms is also critical. An h-cobordism between two manifolds M₀ and M₁ is a manifold W such that its boundary is M₀ ∪ M₁ and both M₀ and M₁ are deformation retracts of W. The h-cobordism theorem states that if M₀ and M₁ are simply connected and of dimension ≥ 5, then an h-cobordism implies M₀ and M₁ are diffeomorphic. However, this theorem fails in dimension 4. Seiberg-Witten invariants can be used to show that certain h-cobordisms are not products, thus demonstrating the existence of non-diffeomorphic but h-cobordant 4-manifolds, which are essentially exotic smooth structures.

Beyond Standard Theory: Generalizations and Deeper Connections

The impact of Seiberg-Witten theory extends far beyond its initial formulation, inspiring numerous generalizations and revealing profound connections to other areas of mathematics and theoretical physics. These extensions often aim to capture more refined information or to apply the core ideas to different geometric settings.

One significant generalization is the development of Vafa-Witten invariants. These invariants arise from a different perspective in quantum field theory, specifically from N=4 supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory on a 4-manifold. While Seiberg-Witten theory is based on a U(1) gauge group and abelian monopoles, Vafa-Witten theory considers higher-rank gauge groups (e.g., SU(N) or SO(N)) and their associated moduli spaces of solutions to generalized gauge theory equations. These invariants are expected to provide richer information about the underlying 4-manifold, particularly for manifolds with b₂⁺ = 0, where standard Seiberg-Witten invariants often vanish. The mathematical rigorous construction of Vafa-Witten invariants is an active area of research, often involving techniques from algebraic geometry and stable sheaves.

The relationship between Seiberg-Witten theory and Topological Quantum Field Theory (TQFT) is also fundamental. Edward Witten's original motivation for introducing Seiberg-Witten equations stemmed from their appearance as the low-energy effective theory of N=2 supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory. This connection places Seiberg-Witten theory firmly within the framework of TQFT, where topological invariants are derived from quantum field theories. TQFTs provide a robust axiomatic framework for constructing topological invariants of manifolds of various dimensions, and Seiberg-Witten theory serves as a prime example of a TQFT in dimension 4. This perspective suggests that the invariants are not just numbers but components of a larger, coherent topological structure.

Furthermore, Seiberg-Witten theory has deep implications for string theory and M-theory. The Seiberg-Witten duality, which posits an equivalence between different descriptions of a quantum field theory, was a key insight from the original work of Seiberg and Witten. This duality has had profound consequences for understanding non-perturbative phenomena in quantum field theories and their gravitational counterparts. In string theory, the low-energy effective actions of certain string compactifications are described by N=2 supersymmetric gauge theories, and thus Seiberg-Witten theory provides a mathematical framework for analyzing their properties, such as the spectrum of BPS states and the geometry of their moduli spaces. The theory also connects to mirror symmetry, a duality between different geometric descriptions of Calabi-Yau manifolds, where gauge theory invariants on one side correspond to enumerative invariants on the mirror side.

Open problems and future directions in Seiberg-Witten theory include extending the theory to non-compact manifolds, developing effective computational methods for more complex manifolds, and exploring its connections to other emerging areas of mathematics like homological mirror symmetry and categorification. The theory continues to be a vibrant field of research, constantly revealing new insights into the topology and geometry of low-dimensional manifolds.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Metric Dependence vs. Invariant Independence: A common misconception is that because the Seiberg-Witten moduli space depends on the chosen metric, the invariants also depend on it. Remember, the *invariants* are metric-independent for b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1 due to wall-crossing phenomena, even though the *moduli space itself* changes with the metric.
  • Misinterpreting the Vanishing Theorem: Students sometimes incorrectly assume that if a manifold has positive scalar curvature, it *must* have non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants. The theorem states the opposite: positive scalar curvature implies *vanishing* invariants (for b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1).
  • Overlooking the Role of Spinᶜ Structure: The Seiberg-Witten equations are defined for a specific Spinᶜ structure L. Forgetting this dependence or assuming it's always the canonical structure can lead to errors, especially when discussing basic classes.
  • Ignoring the Perturbation Term: While often simplified in introductory discussions, the generic perturbation term η is crucial for ensuring the regularity of the moduli space, which is necessary for the invariants to be well-defined.
  • Generalizing 4-Manifold Properties to Other Dimensions: The existence of exotic smooth structures and the unique complexity of 4-manifolds are specific to this dimension. Do not assume similar phenomena occur in dimensions 3 or 5 without specific evidence.
  • Mixing Up Donaldson and Seiberg-Witten Theory: While the Witten conjecture establishes their equivalence, they are distinct theories with different underlying equations (ASD connections vs. monopoles). Understand their individual formulations before appreciating their equivalence.
  • Assuming All Spinᶜ Structures are Basic Classes: Only Spinᶜ structures L for which SW_M(L) ≠ 0 are called basic classes. Many Spinᶜ structures will have vanishing invariants.

The Adjunction Inequality and the Thom Conjecture via Seiberg-Witten Theory

The adjunction inequality is a powerful application of Seiberg-Witten theory to the topology of smoothly embedded surfaces in 4-manifolds. Let M be a closed, oriented, smooth 4-manifold with b₂⁺(M) ≥ 2, and let Σ ⊂ M be a smoothly embedded, connected, oriented surface of genus g(Σ) ≥ 1 representing a homology class [Σ] ∈ H₂(M;ℤ) with [Σ]·[Σ] ≥ 0. If the Seiberg-Witten invariant SW_M(𝔰) is non-zero for some Spinᶜ structure 𝔰 with determinant line bundle L, then the inequality

2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ [Σ]·[Σ] + |⟨c₁(L), [Σ]⟩|

holds. Kronheimer and Mrowka established this inequality using Donaldson theory; the Seiberg-Witten approach provides a more streamlined proof and extends to a broader class of manifolds. The Thom conjecture, which states that a smoothly embedded complex curve in ℂℙ² of degree d has minimal genus among all smoothly embedded surfaces representing the same homology class, follows immediately by taking M = ℂℙ², where the canonical class K = c₁(L) = −3H (H the hyperplane class) gives the bound g ≥ (d−1)(d−2)/2. The adjunction inequality also implies the generalized Thom conjecture for symplectic 4-manifolds: if Σ is a symplectic surface in a symplectic 4-manifold (M,ω) with b₂⁺ ≥ 2, then the genus of any smoothly embedded surface representing the same homology class is at least the genus of Σ. The proof uses the fact that the canonical class K_ω is a basic class with SW_M(K_ω) = ±1 (Taubes's theorem). The inequality is sharp for symplectic surfaces, which realize equality. For manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, the inequality holds for the chamber determined by the period point of the metric, and wall-crossing formulas describe how the bound changes when the metric crosses a wall.

Key Consequences

  • The inequality gives a lower bound on the genus of any smoothly embedded surface in terms of its self-intersection and pairing with basic classes.
  • It provides obstructions to the existence of smoothly embedded spheres with positive self-intersection (e.g., no smoothly embedded 2-sphere of self-intersection +1 in a manifold with non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariant).
  • It implies the minimal genus property for symplectic surfaces, a cornerstone of symplectic topology.

Seiberg-Witten Theory on Symplectic 4-Manifolds: Taubes's SW = Gr Theorem

A profound result connecting Seiberg-Witten theory to symplectic geometry is Clifford Taubes's theorem equating Seiberg-Witten invariants with Gromov invariants (a variant of Gromov-Witten invariants counting pseudoholomorphic curves). Let (M,ω) be a closed symplectic 4-manifold with b₂⁺(M) ≥ 2. Taubes proved that the Seiberg-Witten invariant SW_M(𝔰) for the canonical Spinᶜ structure 𝔰_ω (with determinant line bundle K_ω⁻¹) is equal to ±1, and more generally, for any Spinᶜ structure 𝔰 with c₁(𝔰) = K_ω + 2PD(A) where A ∈ H₂(M;ℤ), the invariant SW_M(𝔰) equals the Gromov invariant Gr_M(A) counting pseudoholomorphic curves in the class A (with appropriate multiplicities). This equality, often written as SW = Gr, has far-reaching implications. It implies that the Seiberg-Witten basic classes of a symplectic 4-manifold are precisely the classes of the form K_ω + 2PD(A) where A is represented by a pseudoholomorphic curve. Moreover, the Seiberg-Witten invariant of such a class is the signed count of such curves. The proof uses a delicate analysis of the Seiberg-Witten equations on the symplectic manifold, showing that solutions concentrate near pseudoholomorphic curves as a parameter (the scaling of the metric) goes to infinity. This result also gives a new proof of the non-vanishing of Seiberg-Witten invariants for symplectic manifolds and provides a bridge between gauge theory and symplectic topology. It leads to the Taubes constraints on the Seiberg-Witten basic classes of symplectic manifolds: if K is a basic class, then K·ω ≥ 0, with equality only for K = ±K_ω. These constraints are instrumental in distinguishing symplectic manifolds from non-symplectic ones and in proving the non-existence of symplectic structures on certain 4-manifolds.

Applications

  • Classification of symplectic structures on 4-manifolds via basic classes.
  • Proof of the Weinstein conjecture for contact 3-manifolds via Seiberg-Witten Floer homology.
  • Derivation of the adjunction formula for pseudoholomorphic curves from the Seiberg-Witten adjunction inequality.
Worked Example: Adjunction Inequality Application
Let M be a closed, oriented 4-manifold with b₂⁺(M) ≥ 2 and a non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariant for a Spinᶜ structure 𝔰 with c₁(𝔰) = K. Suppose Σ ⊂ M is a smoothly embedded surface of genus 2 with [Σ]·[Σ] = 4 and ⟨K, [Σ]⟩ = 6. Does the adjunction inequality allow such an embedding?
The adjunction inequality states 2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ [Σ]·[Σ] + |⟨K, [Σ]⟩|. Here g(Σ) = 2, so left side = 2·2 − 2 = 2. Right side = 4 + |6| = 10. Since 2 ≥ 10 is false, the inequality is violated. Therefore, no such smoothly embedded surface can exist in M under the given hypotheses.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Adjunction Inequality Setups
When a problem gives a 4-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 2, a basic class K, and a homology class with self-intersection and genus, immediately write down the adjunction inequality. Check if the inequality holds; if it fails, the embedding is impossible. Remember that the inequality is necessary but not sufficient for existence. For b₂⁺ = 1, note the chamber dependence: the inequality holds for the chamber containing the period point of the metric used to define the invariant.

Gluing Formulas and the Seiberg-Witten Invariant for Connected Sums

The behavior of Seiberg-Witten invariants under connected sum is governed by a vanishing theorem and a gluing formula. If M = M₁ # M₂ is a connected sum of two oriented 4-manifolds with b₂⁺(M₁) > 0 and b₂⁺(M₂) > 0, then all Seiberg-Witten invariants of M vanish. This is because the neck-stretching argument shows that any solution on M would give rise to solutions on M₁ and M₂ with a matching condition that forces the invariants to be zero. More precisely, if both summands have b₂⁺ ≥ 1, then SW_M ≡ 0. This result is a key tool for proving that certain 4-manifolds are not decomposable as connected sums of manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 1. For connected sums where one summand has b₂⁺ = 0 (e.g., ℂℙ² or S²×S²), the invariants can be non-zero and are computed by a gluing formula involving the invariants of the other summand and the Donaldson invariants of the negative-definite piece (or the Seiberg-Witten invariants of the blow-up). The blow-up formula states that if M̃ = M # ℂℙ² is the blow-up of M at a point, then the Seiberg-Witten invariants of M̃ are related to those of M by SWM̃(K ± E) = SW_M(K), where E is the exceptional divisor class (E·E = −1). This formula allows one to compute invariants of blow-ups and to relate basic classes of M and M̃. The connected sum formula for manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 is more subtle and involves wall-crossing terms. When M = M₁ # M₂ with b₂⁺(M₁) = 1 and b₂⁺(M₂) = 0, the Seiberg-Witten invariant of M can be expressed as a sum over basic classes of M₁ of the product of the invariant of M₁ and a certain invariant of M₂ (often a Donaldson invariant). These gluing formulas are essential for computing invariants of complicated manifolds built from simpler pieces and for understanding the geography of 4-manifolds.

Key Points

  • Connected sum of two manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 1 ⇒ all SW invariants vanish.
  • Blow-up formula: SWM#ℂℙ²(K ± E) = SW_M(K).
  • Glueing along 3-manifolds: more general formulas involve Seiberg-Witten Floer homology.

The Seiberg-Witten Equations on Manifolds with Boundary: Contact Structures and the Ozsváth-Szabó Invariant

Extending Seiberg-Witten theory to 4-manifolds with boundary leads to a rich interplay with contact geometry. Let Y be a closed, oriented 3-manifold equipped with a contact structure ξ. The Seiberg-Witten equations on the symplectization ℝ × Y (with a suitable metric) have solutions that correspond to Reeb orbits of ξ. Kronheimer, Mrowka, and others used this connection to define invariants of contact structures. In particular, Ozsváth and Szabó defined an invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y) (the plus version of Heegaard Floer homology, which is isomorphic to monopole Floer homology) for a contact structure ξ on Y. This invariant is constructed by considering the Seiberg-Witten equations on a 4-manifold with boundary Y and a symplectic filling, and it satisfies a gluing property: if (W,ω) is a symplectic filling of (Y,ξ), then the Seiberg-Witten invariant of the closed manifold obtained by capping off W with a suitable cap is related to c(ξ). The invariant c(ξ) distinguishes contact structures that are not distinguished by classical invariants (e.g., the Eliashberg class). It also gives obstructions to the existence of symplectic fillings: if c(ξ) = 0, then (Y,ξ) cannot be the boundary of a symplectic 4-manifold with certain properties. The Seiberg-Witten equations on manifolds with cylindrical ends (ℝ × Y) are central to the definition of monopole Floer homology, which is the 3-dimensional counterpart of the 4-dimensional Seiberg-Witten invariants. The analytic setup involves weighted Sobolev spaces and a careful treatment of the asymptotic behavior of solutions. The resulting Floer homology groups are isomorphic to Heegaard Floer homology (established by Kutluhan, Lee, Taubes and independently by Colin, Ghiggini, Honda), providing a gauge-theoretic interpretation of the combinatorial Heegaard Floer invariants. This isomorphism has led to numerous applications, including Manolescu's disproof of the triangulation conjecture in dimensions ≥5 and the classification of tight contact structures on certain 3-manifolds.

Significance

  • Provides a gauge-theoretic invariant of contact structures.
  • Connects Seiberg-Witten theory to Heegaard Floer homology.
  • Yields obstructions to symplectic fillings and Stein fillings.
  • Confusing the adjunction inequality for b₂⁺ ≥ 2 with the version for b₂⁺ = 1 (chamber-dependent).
  • Assuming SW = Gr holds for all symplectic manifolds without the condition b₂⁺ ≥ 2.
  • Forgetting that the blow-up formula changes the basic class by ±E, not just adding E.
  • Misapplying the connected sum vanishing theorem when one summand has b₂⁺ = 0.
  • Overlooking the role of the contact invariant c(ξ) in distinguishing contact structures; it lives in HF⁺(−Y), not HF⁻ or HF^∞.
  • Thinking that the Ozsváth-Szabó contact invariant is defined for all contact structures; it vanishes for overtwisted contact structures.
  • Neglecting the metric dependence of the moduli space on manifolds with boundary when setting up the gluing analysis.

The Witten Conjecture and the Feehan-Leness Program

Edward Witten's 1994 conjecture proposed that the Seiberg-Witten invariants of a smooth, closed, oriented 4-manifold M with b2+ ≥ 2 determine the Donaldson invariants through a universal formula. This equivalence would imply that the abelian gauge theory of Seiberg-Witten monopoles encodes the same information as the non-abelian SU(2) Yang-Mills theory underlying Donaldson's polynomial invariants. A proof was developed by Paul Feehan and Thomas Leness in a series of papers using a sophisticated analysis of the PU(2) monopole equations — a one-parameter family interpolating between the Seiberg-Witten equations and the anti-self-dual Yang-Mills equations. The argument requires a deep understanding of the compactification of the moduli spaces of PU(2) monopoles, gluing theory for singular solutions, and the identification of contributions from the ends of the moduli space with Seiberg-Witten basic classes. The universal formula expresses the Donaldson series as a sum over basic classes K of terms involving SWM(K) multiplied by explicit universal functions of the intersection form. This result realizes Witten's physical intuition from N=2 supersymmetric gauge theory and provides a powerful computational tool: Donaldson invariants, notoriously difficult to compute directly, can be obtained from the often more accessible Seiberg-Witten invariants. The program also clarifies the relationship between the two theories' wall-crossing phenomena, showing that the chamber dependence of Donaldson invariants for b2+ = 1 matches the wall-crossing of Seiberg-Witten invariants under the same universal transformation.

Wall-Crossing Formulas and the Chamber Structure for b2+ = 1 Manifolds

On a 4-manifold M with b2+ = 1, the Seiberg-Witten invariant depends on a choice of chamber in the positive cone of H2(M; ℝ). The positive cone has two components separated by the walls WK = {ω ∈ H2(M; ℝ) | ω·K = 0} for each characteristic class K with SWM(K) ≠ 0. When the period point (the self-dual harmonic 2-form determined by the metric) crosses a wall, the invariant changes by a wall-crossing term. The wall-crossing formula, derived by Li and Liu and independently by Kotschick, Morgan, and Taubes, states that for a basic class K of simple type, the difference between the invariants in the two adjacent chambers is ±1 (more generally, the change equals the sign of the intersection pairing evaluated on K and the wall-crossing direction). This chamber dependence is crucial for applications: the adjunction inequality for b2+ = 1 holds only in the chamber where the period point pairs positively with the basic class. Moreover, the wall-crossing formula is essential for the blow-up formula and for computing invariants of manifolds obtained by rational blow-downs. The chamber structure also interacts with the Donaldson invariants via the Witten conjecture: the chamber dependence of Donaldson invariants for b2+ = 1 is mirrored by the Seiberg-Witten wall-crossing under the universal formula. A thorough understanding of the chamber structure requires analyzing the moduli space of solutions to the perturbed Seiberg-Witten equations with a generic self-dual 2-form perturbation; the walls correspond to perturbations for which the moduli space contains reducible solutions. The number of chambers grows with the number of basic classes, and the invariant in each chamber can be computed recursively from a chosen reference chamber using the wall-crossing formula.

Worked Example: Wall-Crossing on a Dolgachev Surface
Let E(1) be the rational elliptic surface (ℂℙ2 blown up at 9 points) and let E(1)p,q be the Dolgachev surface obtained by a logarithmic transform of multiplicities (p,q) with p,q ≥ 2 coprime. This manifold has b2+ = 1 and two basic classes ±K with K·K = 0. The Seiberg-Witten invariant in one chamber is SW+(K) = 1, SW+(−K) = 0. Compute the invariant in the opposite chamber.
The wall for K is {ω | ω·K = 0}. Since K·K = 0, the wall is a hyperplane tangent to the positive cone. Crossing the wall in the direction where ω·K changes from positive to negative, the wall-crossing formula gives SW−(K) = SW+(K) − 1 = 0. For the other basic class −K, the wall is the same (since (−K)·K = 0). Crossing in the same direction, ω·(−K) changes from negative to positive, so the formula gives SW−(−K) = SW+(−K) + 1 = 1. Thus the basic classes swap their non-zero invariant when crossing the wall. This matches the known fact that the Dolgachev surface has two chambers with the roles of ±K interchanged.
Exam Strategy: Chamber Dependence
When a problem asks for the Seiberg-Witten invariant on a manifold with b2+ = 1, always check whether the chamber is specified. If not, state the invariant in a chosen chamber and describe how it changes under wall-crossing. Remember that the adjunction inequality 2g−2 ≥ C·C + |K·C| holds only for basic classes K with SW(K) ≠ 0 in the chamber where the period point pairs positively with K. A common trap is to apply the inequality without verifying the chamber condition.

Seiberg-Witten Invariants and the Geography of Simply-Connected Complex Surfaces

The Seiberg-Witten invariant provides a powerful tool for distinguishing homeomorphic but non-diffeomorphic complex surfaces and for constraining the geography of simply-connected complex surfaces of general type. For a minimal complex surface S of general type with b2+ ≥ 2, the canonical class KS is a basic class with SWS(KS) = ±1 (the sign depends on the orientation convention). Other basic classes satisfy constraints derived from the adjunction inequality and the structure of the moduli space. The Seiberg-Witten invariant also yields new proofs of classical results such as the Castelnuovo-de Franchis theorem and informs the Enriques-Kodaira classification for surfaces with b2+ = 1. A landmark application is the proof by Friedman and Morgan, and independently by Okonek and Van de Ven, that the Dolgachev surfaces E(1)p,q are homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the rational elliptic surface E(1) (which has all SW invariants zero). The basic classes of the Dolgachev surface are ±K with K·K = 0 and SW = ±1 in appropriate chambers, while E(1) has no basic classes. This provided a landmark infinite family of exotic simply-connected 4-manifolds. Further, the Seiberg-Witten invariant can prove that certain simply-connected complex surfaces with the same Chern numbers are not diffeomorphic, by comparing their sets of basic classes; for instance, certain Horikawa surfaces have basic classes that differ from those of standard Horikawa surfaces with identical Chern numbers, yielding exotic pairs. The geography problem — which pairs (c12, c2) can be realized by minimal complex surfaces of general type — is constrained by the Seiberg-Witten basic classes: the canonical class satisfies KS·KS = c12, and the adjunction inequality restricts the possible curves. The Seiberg-Witten invariant also plays a role in the proof of the Thom conjecture for complex surfaces (the genus of a smooth algebraic curve in ℂℙ2 is minimized by its degree) and in the proof of the symplectic Thom conjecture via Taubes's SW = Gr theorem.

Monopole Floer Homology, Heegaard Floer Homology, and the Triangulation Conjecture

Monopole Floer homology, developed by Kronheimer and Mrowka, is the 3-dimensional analogue of the 4-dimensional Seiberg-Witten invariants. For a closed, oriented 3-manifold Y with a Spinc structure 𝔰, the monopole Floer homology groups HM•(Y, 𝔰) are defined using the Seiberg-Witten equations on the cylinder ℝ × Y with a suitable perturbation. There are three flavors: HM−, HM∧ (or HM+), and HM∞, which fit into a long exact sequence. The groups are modules over the ring ℤ[U] where U has degree −2. A monumental result, proved independently by Kutluhan-Lee-Taubes and by Colin-Ghiggini-Honda, establishes an isomorphism between monopole Floer homology and Heegaard Floer homology (defined combinatorially by Ozsváth and Szabó): HM•(Y, 𝔰) ≅ HF•(Y, 𝔰). This isomorphism has profound consequences. It provides a gauge-theoretic interpretation of the combinatorial Heegaard Floer invariants, allowing techniques from analysis to be applied to problems in low-dimensional topology. One striking application is Ciprian Manolescu's disproof of the triangulation conjecture in dimensions ≥ 5. The triangulation conjecture asked whether every topological manifold admits a piecewise-linear (PL) structure, equivalently a triangulation. Manolescu used the isomorphism to construct a homology 3-sphere with non-zero Rokhlin invariant but vanishing μ-invariant (the Frøyshov invariant in monopole Floer homology), which by Galewski-Stern and Matumoto's work implies the existence of non-triangulable manifolds in dimensions 5 and higher. The monopole Floer homology also yields invariants of contact structures: the Ozsváth-Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF+(−Y) corresponds under the isomorphism to an element in HM+(−Y) defined by counting solutions to the Seiberg-Witten equations on the symplectization ℝ × Y with a suitable perturbation. This invariant vanishes for overtwisted contact structures and distinguishes many tight contact structures. The functoriality of monopole Floer homology under cobordisms gives rise to maps that correspond to the Seiberg-Witten invariants of 4-manifolds with boundary, providing a TQFT structure that underlies the gluing formulas for Seiberg-Witten invariants.

The Seiberg-Witten Invariant as a Topological Quantum Field Theory: Cobordism Maps and Functoriality

The Seiberg-Witten invariants can be organized into a topological quantum field theory (TQFT) in the sense of Atiyah-Segal. To each closed, oriented 3-manifold Y with a Spinc structure 𝔰, one assigns the monopole Floer homology group HM•(Y, 𝔰). To each smooth, oriented cobordism W: Y0 → Y1 with a Spinc structure 𝔰W restricting to 𝔰0, 𝔰1 on the boundary, one assigns a map SWW,𝔰W: HM•(Y0, 𝔰0) → HM•(Y1, 𝔰1) defined by counting solutions to the Seiberg-Witten equations on the completed cobordism Ŵ = (−∞,0] × Y0 ∪ W ∪ [0,∞) × Y1 with appropriate perturbations. These maps satisfy the composition law: for a composite cobordism W2 ∘ W1, the induced map is the composition of the individual maps. When W is a closed 4-manifold (i.e., Y0 = Y1 = ∅), the map is a homomorphism from ℤ to ℤ, which is multiplication by the Seiberg-Witten invariant SWW(𝔰W). This TQFT structure is essential for the gluing formulas: if a closed 4-manifold M is cut along a 3-manifold Y into two pieces W1, W2, then the Seiberg-Witten invariant of M can be computed as the pairing of the relative invariants of W1 and W2 in the Floer homology of Y. The TQFT also provides a framework for understanding the wall-crossing phenomenon: the change in the invariant when the period point crosses a wall corresponds to the contribution of a reducible solution in the cobordism map. The functoriality of monopole Floer homology under contact surgery (e.g., Legendrian surgery) gives rise to the contact invariant and its properties. The TQFT structure is also crucial for the proof of the isomorphism with Heegaard Floer homology, as both theories are shown to be isomorphic as TQFTs. The analytic foundations of this TQFT require careful treatment of the moduli spaces on non-compact manifolds with cylindrical ends, using weighted Sobolev spaces and a gluing theorem for solutions with exponential decay.

  • Assuming the wall-crossing formula for b2+ = 1 is always ±1; the sign depends on the orientation of the moduli space and the direction of crossing, and the formula can be more complicated for non-simple type manifolds.
  • Overlooking that the Seiberg-Witten invariant of a complex surface of general type with b2+ ≥ 2 is ±1 for the canonical class, but the sign is not determined by the complex structure alone; it depends on the orientation of the determinant line bundle.
  • Thinking that monopole Floer homology and Heegaard Floer homology are merely isomorphic as groups; the isomorphism respects the full TQFT structure, including the cobordism maps and the contact invariant.

Seiberg-Witten Stable Homotopy Refinement and the Bauer-Furuta Invariant

The classical Seiberg-Witten invariant is an integer obtained by counting solutions to the monopole equations on a closed, oriented 4-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 2. Bauer and Furuta constructed a refinement that takes values in the stable homotopy groups of spheres, or more precisely in the stable cohomotopy group π₀ˢ(Σ^∞ B𝔾) where 𝔾 is the gauge group. The construction uses a finite-dimensional approximation of the Seiberg-Witten map between Hilbert spaces of configurations, obtained by spectral truncation of the Dirac operator and the self-dual projection of the curvature. The resulting map between finite-dimensional vector bundles over the configuration space is equivariant under the circle action that rotates the spinor. By taking the Conley index of the gradient flow of the Chern-Simons-Dirac functional, one obtains a stable map between representation spheres S(V) → S(W) whose stable homotopy class is the Bauer-Furuta invariant BF(M, 𝔰). When b₂⁺ ≥ 2, the invariant is independent of the metric and perturbation, and its degree recovers the integer Seiberg-Witten invariant. For manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, the invariant depends on a chamber choice, mirroring the wall-crossing of the integer invariant. The stable homotopy refinement can detect exotic smooth structures that the integer invariant cannot distinguish; for instance, it yields a non-trivial element for certain homotopy K3 surfaces. The Bauer-Furuta invariant also satisfies a blow-up formula: BF(M # ℂℙ², 𝔰 # ±E) = Σ^ℂ BF(M, 𝔰) where Σ^ℂ denotes suspension by the complex representation of U(1). This suspension property is crucial for the proof of Furuta's 10/8 theorem (for simply-connected spin 4-manifolds), which states that a smooth, closed, simply-connected spin 4-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 2 satisfies b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ| + 2. In the simply-connected case, the proof uses the fact that the Bauer-Furuta invariant of a spin manifold takes values in the stable homotopy group πkˢ with k = (b₂ - 2)/2, and the non-vanishing of this invariant forces a lower bound on b₂. The refinement has been extended to manifolds with boundary, yielding a TQFT with values in the stable homotopy category, and to families of 4-manifolds, producing invariants in parametrized stable homotopy theory.

Seiberg-Witten Theory on 4-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 0 and the Role of the Period Point

When b₂⁺ = 0, the space of self-dual harmonic 2-forms is trivial, so the Seiberg-Witten equations have no reducible solutions for a generic perturbation. The moduli space is then a compact, oriented 0-dimensional manifold for any metric and perturbation, and the Seiberg-Witten invariant is a well-defined integer without chamber dependence. However, the invariant can be non-zero only for Spinᶜ structures whose first Chern class c₁(𝔰) satisfies c₁(𝔰)² = 2χ + 3σ, where χ is the Euler characteristic and σ the signature. For rational and ruled surfaces, the basic classes are precisely ±c₁(K) where K is the canonical class, and the invariant is ±1. The period point, which in the b₂⁺ > 0 case determines the chamber by the sign of the self-dual part of the perturbation, becomes irrelevant because there is no self-dual harmonic form to pair with. Nevertheless, the period point still appears in the definition of the perturbation: one adds a self-dual 2-form η to the curvature equation, and the invariant is independent of η as long as η is not in the image of the period map from H²(M; ℝ) to H⁺(M; ℝ) (which is zero). The blow-up formula for b₂⁺ = 0 manifolds takes the form SW(M # ℂℙ², 𝔰 # ±E) = SW(M, 𝔰), where E is the exceptional class with E² = -1. This differs from the b₂⁺ ≥ 2 case where the blow-up has two basic classes K ± E, each with invariant equal to SW(M, K). The reason is that for b₂⁺ = 0 the moduli space on the blow-up is a disjoint union of two copies of the moduli space on M, but the orientations of the two components are opposite, causing cancellation unless the invariant is defined with a sign convention that accounts for the orientation of the determinant line. In practice, one often works with the invariant defined using the orientation of the determinant line bundle over the configuration space, which yields the simple blow-up formula above. The vanishing theorem for manifolds with b₂⁺ = 0 and a metric of positive scalar curvature implies that the Seiberg-Witten invariant vanishes for all Spinᶜ structures, which gives a quick proof that such manifolds cannot admit symplectic structures. This is consistent with the fact that symplectic 4-manifolds have b₂⁺ ≥ 1 by Taubes's theorem.

Computing the Seiberg-Witten invariant of a blow-up
Let M be a simply-connected complex surface with b₂⁺ = 1 and Seiberg-Witten invariant SW(M, K) = 1 for the canonical class K. Compute SW(M # ℂℙ², K + E) and SW(M # ℂℙ², K - E) where E is the exceptional divisor class with E² = -1.
For a 4-manifold with b₂⁺ = 1, the blow-up formula states that the basic classes of M # ℂℙ² are K ± E, and the Seiberg-Witten invariants satisfy SW(M # ℂℙ², K ± E) = SW(M, K). Since SW(M, K) = 1, both SW(M # ℂℙ², K + E) and SW(M # ℂℙ², K - E) equal 1. Note that the sign of the invariant is the same for both basic classes because the orientation of the moduli space on the blow-up is determined by the orientation of the determinant line on M and the choice of orientation for the exceptional divisor; the standard convention yields equal signs. If M had b₂⁺ ≥ 2, the formula would be SW(M # ℂℙ², K ± E) = SW(M, K) as well, but the invariant of M would be independent of the chamber, whereas for b₂⁺ = 1 one must specify the chamber for M and the corresponding chamber for the blow-up. In this example, the chamber is fixed by the choice of period point on M, and the blow-up inherits the same chamber because the exceptional divisor has negative self-intersection and does not affect the period point.
Exam-day strategy
When a question asks for the Seiberg-Witten invariant of a blow-up, first identify b₂⁺ of the original manifold. If b₂⁺ = 0, the invariant of the blow-up equals the invariant of the original manifold for each basic class K ± E. If b₂⁺ ≥ 1, the basic classes are K ± E and the invariant is the same as the original invariant, but be careful to state the chamber dependence for b₂⁺ = 1. Always recall that E² = -1 and that the adjunction inequality for the exceptional divisor forces the basic classes to be exactly K ± E. Write down the blow-up formula explicitly before substituting numbers.

Seiberg-Witten Invariants and the 10/8 Conjecture for Spin 4-Manifolds

The 10/8 conjecture, attributed to Furuta, predicts that a smooth, closed, spin 4-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 2 satisfies b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ| + 2, where σ is the signature. The conjecture is known to hold for simply-connected manifolds by work of Furuta using the Bauer-Furuta stable homotopy invariant. The proof proceeds by considering the Seiberg-Witten map for a spin structure 𝔰, which in this case has c₁(𝔰) = 0. The finite-dimensional approximation yields a map between representation spheres S(V) → S(W) where V and W are real representations of the group Pin(2) (the double cover of O(2)) that acts on the spinor bundle. The dimensions of V and W are related to the Betti numbers: dim V - dim W = (b₂ - 2)/2 - (b₂⁺ - 1) = (b₂ - 2b₂⁺)/2. Using the fact that the Bauer-Furuta invariant is non-zero in the stable homotopy group πkˢ(Pin(2)) with k = (b₂ - 2)/2, and applying the Adams spectral sequence for Pin(2), one obtains a lower bound on k in terms of the signature. The signature enters because the intersection form of a spin manifold is even, and by Rokhlin's theorem σ ≡ 0 (mod 16). The Pin(2)-equivariant stable homotopy groups have a periodicity of 8, and the non-vanishing of the invariant forces k ≥ (5/4)|σ| + 1, which translates to b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ| + 2. The conjecture remains open for non-simply-connected spin manifolds; the best known general bound is b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ| + 1. Manolescu's work on Pin(2)-equivariant Seiberg-Witten Floer homology also disproved the Triangulation Conjecture in high dimensions by showing that the Pin(2)-equivariant Seiberg-Witten Floer homology of certain homology 3-spheres has a non-trivial Frøyshov invariant, which obstructs the existence of a triangulation. The 10/8 conjecture is closely related to the geography problem for spin 4-manifolds: the set of pairs (χ, σ) realized by smooth spin 4-manifolds is constrained by the inequality. For simply-connected spin manifolds, the geography is largely understood: the region b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ| + 2, σ ≡ 0 (mod 16), and b₂⁺ ≥ 2 is exactly the set of realizable pairs, with the boundary realized by K3 surfaces and certain other constructions.

Seiberg-Witten Equations on Non-Kähler Symplectic 4-Manifolds and the Taubes Constraints

Taubes's SW = Gr theorem asserts that for a closed symplectic 4-manifold (M, ω) with b₂⁺ ≥ 2, the Seiberg-Witten invariant of the canonical Spinᶜ structure 𝔰_ω (whose determinant line is the anticanonical bundle K⁻¹) is ±1, and all other basic classes satisfy the adjunction inequality with equality only for symplectic surfaces. The theorem holds for any symplectic form, not only Kähler forms. For non-Kähler symplectic manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 2, the Seiberg-Witten invariant of the canonical class is still ±1, but there may be additional basic classes coming from the topology of the fibration. Taubes proved that the Seiberg-Witten invariant of any symplectic 4-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 2 is non-zero for the canonical class, and that the set of basic classes is finite and symmetric under c₁ → -c₁. Moreover, the basic classes satisfy the inequality c₁(𝔰) · [ω] ≤ 0, with equality only for the canonical class. This constraint follows from the fact that the Seiberg-Witten equations with a large perturbation parameter tω have solutions only when the spinor is concentrated near a pseudo-holomorphic curve representing the Poincaré dual of c₁(𝔰). For non-Kähler manifolds, the pseudo-holomorphic curves are not algebraic, but the Gromov invariant still counts them. The SW = Gr theorem identifies the Seiberg-Witten invariant with the Gromov invariant, which counts pseudo-holomorphic curves in the symplectization. This identification implies that the Seiberg-Witten invariant is independent of the choice of symplectic form within a deformation class, and that it vanishes for symplectic forms that are not deformation-equivalent to the given one. The theorem also yields the symplectic Thom conjecture: a smoothly embedded surface Σ in a symplectic 4-manifold with [Σ] · [ω] > 0 satisfies genus(Σ) ≥ (1/2)([Σ]² - [Σ] · c₁(K)) + 1, with equality if and only if Σ is a symplectic surface. For non-Kähler manifolds, this gives a purely symplectic proof of the genus bound without using algebraic geometry. The constraints on basic classes have been used to classify symplectic structures on 4-manifolds with small Betti numbers, for example showing that the only symplectic structures on ℂℙ² # nℂℙ² for n ≤ 8 are the standard blow-ups of the Fubini-Study form.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Classification of Lefschetz Fibrations over the 2-Sphere

A Lefschetz fibration is a smooth map f: M → S² from a closed, oriented 4-manifold to the 2-sphere with only non-degenerate critical points, each modeled on (z₁, z₂) ↦ z₁² + z₂² in local complex coordinates. The Seiberg-Witten invariants of M constrain the possible Lefschetz fibrations. Smith proved that if M admits a Lefschetz fibration with genus g ≥ 2 and b₂⁺(M) ≥ 2, then the canonical class K of the fibration (the first Chern class of the vertical tangent bundle) is a basic class with Seiberg-Witten invariant ±1. Moreover, any basic class 𝔰 satisfies the adjunction inequality for the fiber class F: 2g(F) - 2 ≥ |c₁(𝔰) · F| + F². Since F² = 0 and c₁(𝔰) · F = 2g - 2 for the canonical class, the inequality is an equality, forcing the fiber to be a symplectic surface for any symplectic form compatible with the fibration. Conversely, if M has a basic class 𝔰 with SW(M, 𝔰) ≠ 0 and there exists a class F with F² = 0, c₁(𝔰) · F = 2g - 2 > 0, and 𝔰 · F = 0, then M admits a Lefschetz fibration with fiber genus g after possibly blowing up. The Seiberg-Witten invariant of the total space of a Lefschetz fibration can be computed via the relative invariants of the fiber and the monodromy factorization in the mapping class group. In particular, the invariant is the product of the invariants of the fiber and the monodromy, which in the case of a genus-1 fibration (an elliptic surface) reduces to the product formula SW(M) = SW(F) · SW(base) = 0 unless the base has b₂⁺ ≥ 1. For higher genus, the invariant is more subtle but can be expressed in terms of the Gromov invariants of the fiber and the monodromy. The classification of Lefschetz fibrations on a given 4-manifold up to isomorphism is equivalent to the classification of factorizations of the monodromy into Dehn twists, and the Seiberg-Witten basic classes provide obstructions to the existence of such factorizations. For example, a simply-connected 4-manifold with b₂⁺ = 1 and vanishing Seiberg-Witten invariants in the relevant chamber (such as ℂℙ² # nℂℙ² for n ≥ 2 in the chamber where the invariant vanishes) cannot admit a Lefschetz fibration with genus ≥ 2. This result has been used to show that some exotic 4-manifolds do not admit genus-2 Lefschetz fibrations, even though they are symplectic.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the 11/8 Conjecture for Spin 4-Manifolds

The 11/8 conjecture strengthens the 10/8 inequality by predicting that for a closed, simply‑connected spin 4‑manifold the second Betti number satisfies b₂ ≥ (11/8)|σ|, where σ is the signature. A proof would likely require a refinement of the Seiberg‑Witten monopole equations—such as a stable homotopy or K‑theoretic obstruction—beyond the index-theoretic methods that yield the 10/8 bound. Furuta’s 10/8 theorem uses a finite‑dimensional approximation and the Bauer‑Furuta stable homotopy invariant. The 11/8 conjecture would follow from a similar refinement if the Seiberg‑Witten map exhibited a non‑trivial K‑theoretic obstruction of order 2. Manolescu’s Pin(2)‑equivariant Seiberg‑Witten Floer homology has ruled out certain smooth group actions on 4‑manifolds and produced new constraints compatible with the 11/8 bound, but a complete proof remains open.

In practice, the conjecture serves as a diagnostic: a candidate simply‑connected spin 4‑manifold violating b₂ ≥ (11/8)|σ| cannot admit a smooth structure. The K3 surface has b₂ = 22 and σ = −16, giving 22 ≥ (11/8)·16 = 22, so it saturates the bound. Exotic Dolgachev surfaces E(1)ₚ,𝑞 (p,q relatively prime) have b₂ = 10 and σ = −8, yielding 10 ≥ 11, which fails; consequently they cannot be spin, consistent with their odd intersection form.

Seiberg-Witten Invariants and the Geography of Non-Simply-Connected 4-Manifolds

The geography problem asks which pairs (χₕ, c₁²) are realized by minimal complex surfaces of general type. For simply‑connected surfaces, Seiberg‑Witten basic classes give sharp restrictions: the Bogomolov‑Miyaoka‑Yau inequality c₁² ≤ 3χₕ and the Noether inequality c₁² ≥ 2χₕ − 6. When π₁ is non‑trivial, the invariant is defined for each spinᶜ structure, and the set of basic classes may be larger, allowing more flexibility.

Friedman and Morgan showed that for a 4‑manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 2 and π₁ ≠ 1, the Seiberg‑Witten invariant of a spinᶜ structure 𝔰 can be non‑zero even when c₁(𝔰)² > 2χ + 3σ, provided the universal cover has non‑trivial invariants. This yields symplectic 4‑manifolds with π₁ = ℤ and c₁² = 4χₕ, lying outside the simply‑connected Noether line. The adjunction inequality for embedded surfaces in such manifolds acquires correction terms depending on the fundamental group; roughly, 2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ [Σ]² + |c₁(𝔰)·[Σ]| + δ(Σ), where δ(Σ) measures the failure of π₁(Σ) → π₁(M) to be injective. Consequently, the geography of non‑simply‑connected 4‑manifolds is governed by basic classes on the manifold and its finite covers.

Exam Technique: Identifying Basic Classes in Non-Simply-Connected Manifolds
Let M be a closed, oriented 4‑manifold with b₂⁺(M) = 3 and π₁(M) = ℤ. Suppose the Seiberg‑Witten invariant SW(M, 𝔰) is non‑zero for a spinᶜ structure 𝔰 with c₁(𝔰)² = 12 and χ(M) = 4, σ(M) = −2. Determine whether 𝔰 can be a basic class coming from a symplectic structure on M.
First compute the expected dimension of the Seiberg‑Witten moduli space: d = (c₁(𝔰)² − 2χ(M) − 3σ(M))/4 = (12 − 8 + 6)/4 = 10/4 = 2.5. The index theorem forces d to be an integer for any spinᶜ structure; a non‑integer value means no such 𝔰 exists. The hypothesis is therefore inconsistent. If instead c₁(𝔰)² = 14, then d = (14 − 8 + 6)/4 = 3, which is odd; for a generic metric the invariant vanishes when d is odd. For a symplectic manifold the canonical class K satisfies c₁(K)² = 2χ + 3σ = 2, giving d = 0. Hence a basic class with c₁² = 12 cannot be the canonical class of a symplectic structure. Conclusion: 𝔰 is not a symplectic basic class.

Seiberg-Witten Theory on Exotic ℝ⁴ and the Failure of Smooth h‑Cobordism

Exotic ℝ⁴ are smooth manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard ℝ⁴—a purely 4‑dimensional phenomenon. The Seiberg‑Witten invariant vanishes on ℝ⁴ because b₂⁺ = 0, but the monopole equations on the end‑periodic cylindrical manifold ℝ × S³ with a suitable perturbation detect the exotic smooth structure via the Bauer‑Furuta stable homotopy invariant. The Seiberg‑Witten map on the cylindrical end defines an element in a stable homotopy group; a non‑trivial element obstructs a diffeomorphism to the standard ℝ⁴.

Freedman’s topological h‑cobordism theorem guarantees that any h‑cobordism between simply‑connected 4‑manifolds is homeomorphic to a product. The smooth h‑cobordism conjecture fails: there exist smooth h‑cobordisms between exotic ℝ⁴ that are not diffeomorphic to a product. The Seiberg‑Witten invariant of the h‑cobordism, viewed as a cobordism map in monopole Floer homology, can be non‑zero for certain spinᶜ structures, obstructing trivialization. Akbulut and Matveyev constructed a family of exotic ℝ⁴ indexed by a real parameter t and showed that the Bauer‑Furuta invariant takes distinct values for different t, proving uncountably many distinct exotic ℝ⁴. The invariant is computed via spectral flow of the Dirac operator on the cylindrical end, combining Seiberg‑Witten theory with spectral geometry.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Adjunction Genus Formula for Embedded Surfaces in Symplectic 4-Manifolds

For a smoothly embedded oriented surface Σ of genus g in a symplectic 4‑manifold (M, ω) with [Σ]·[ω] > 0, the adjunction inequality states 2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ [Σ]² + |[Σ]·c₁(K)|, where K is the canonical class. Equality holds if and only if Σ is a symplectic surface (ω|Σ is a volume form). This follows from the Seiberg‑Witten invariant of the canonical spinᶜ structure being ±1, together with the existence of a monopole solution whose spinor vanishes along a symplectic representative of the homology class.

The proof perturbs the Seiberg‑Witten equations by a large multiple of ω, forcing curvature to concentrate near a symplectic surface Poincaré dual to c₁(K). The index theorem for the linearized operator then yields the genus bound. Sharpness implies the concentration set is a smooth symplectic surface, gauge‑equivalent to Taubes’s construction. This formula proves the symplectic Thom conjecture: any smoothly embedded surface in ℂℙ² with positive symplectic area has genus at least the algebraic degree‑genus bound. It also restricts smoothly embedded −1 spheres in manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 2; such a sphere would violate the inequality unless it is symplectic (in which case the adjunction formula forces [Σ]·c₁(K) = −1, and the inequality is not directly applicable for negative self‑intersection). The theory extends to surfaces with boundary in symplectic fillings, where the genus bound involves the Thurston‑Bennequin invariant of the Legendrian boundary.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the 4‑Dimensional Smooth Poincaré Conjecture

The smooth Poincaré conjecture in dimension 4 asserts that every smooth, closed, simply‑connected 4‑manifold homotopy equivalent to S⁴ is diffeomorphic to S⁴. Seiberg‑Witten theory gives an obstruction: if a homotopy 4‑sphere Σ had a non‑trivial invariant, it would have a basic class with c₁² = 0 and d = 0, forcing the invariant to be ±1. However, the invariant vanishes because b₂⁺ = 0 and the monopole equations have no solutions for a generic metric. This vanishing shows Seiberg‑Witten invariants cannot distinguish a homotopy 4‑sphere from S⁴.

Refined invariants—the Bauer‑Furuta stable homotopy invariant and Manolescu’s Pin(2)‑equivariant Seiberg‑Witten Floer homology—have been applied. Manolescu’s disproof of the Triangulation Conjecture used Pin(2)‑equivariant Floer homology; the same techniques constrain smooth structures on homotopy 4‑spheres. If a homotopy 4‑sphere Σ bounds a contractible 5‑manifold, the Pin(2)‑equivariant invariant of the boundary must vanish, restricting the intersection form of any smooth 4‑manifold with boundary Σ. Another approach notes that an exotic S⁴ would yield an exotic ℝ⁴ by removing a point. Since exotic ℝ⁴ are detected by the Bauer‑Furuta invariant, a non‑trivial invariant for some exotic ℝ⁴ would imply an exotic S⁴. Conversely, if all exotic ℝ⁴ have trivial Bauer‑Furuta invariant, the smooth Poincaré conjecture holds. Current computations for known exotic ℝ⁴ families yield the trivial element, consistent with the conjecture but not proving it.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Existence of Einstein Metrics on Four-Manifolds

Einstein metrics on smooth four-manifolds are Riemannian metrics whose Ricci tensor is a constant multiple of the metric. The Seiberg-Witten invariant provides a powerful obstruction to such metrics. In 1995 LeBrun proved that if a closed oriented four-manifold X admits an Einstein metric and has b₂⁺(X) ≥ 2, then its Seiberg-Witten invariant vanishes for every spinᶜ structure. The argument uses the Weitzenböck formula for the Dirac operator coupled to the spinᶜ connection; an Einstein metric forces constant scalar curvature, and the resulting inequality on the determinant line bundle curvature forces the moduli space to be empty for a generic perturbation. Consequently, any four-manifold with a non‑zero Seiberg-Witten invariant — for example, a complex surface of general type with c₁² > 0 — cannot carry an Einstein metric. This result extends earlier work of Hitchin and Thorpe on topological obstructions and shows that the Seiberg-Witten invariant detects an analytic constraint beyond the Hitchin-Thorpe inequality.

The obstruction sharpens for Kähler-Einstein metrics. Here the Seiberg-Witten basic classes coincide with the canonical class and its multiples, and the invariant is ±1. LeBrun's theorem then implies a Kähler-Einstein metric can exist only when the canonical class is torsion or the manifold is rational or ruled. For non‑Kähler Einstein metrics, vanishing of the invariant is necessary but not sufficient; manifolds with vanishing Seiberg-Witten invariants may still lack Einstein metrics, as shown by LeBrun and others using the Yamabe invariant. The interplay among the Seiberg-Witten invariant, the Yamabe constant, and Einstein metrics remains active, with work by Gursky and LeBrun linking the Weyl curvature functional to the Seiberg-Witten equations.

An important corollary concerns connected sums of complex surfaces. If X = X₁ # X₂ with both summands having b₂⁺ ≥ 1 and non‑zero Seiberg-Witten invariants, then X admits no Einstein metric. This follows from the blow‑up formula and the fact that b₂⁺(X) = b₂⁺(X₁) + b₂⁺(X₂) ≥ 2. The result yields a large class of simply‑connected four-manifolds that are topologically well understood but analytically rigid, illustrating how the gauge-theoretic Seiberg-Witten invariant translates into a global differential-geometric restriction.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Yamabe Invariant of Four-Manifolds

The Yamabe invariant Y(M) of a smooth closed manifold M is the supremum over all conformal classes of the total scalar curvature of the unit-volume metric in that class. In dimension four, LeBrun showed in 1999 that the Seiberg-Witten invariant gives a sharp upper bound for Y(M). Specifically, if M has b₂⁺(M) ≥ 1 and a basic class K with SW(K) ≠ 0, then Y(M) ≤ 4π√(2χ(M) + 3σ(M)) − K², where χ is the Euler characteristic and σ the signature. When equality holds, the metric realizing the Yamabe invariant is conformally Kähler and the basic class is represented by the canonical class of a complex structure. This inequality refines the earlier bound of Petean and Yun and connects the analytic Yamabe problem to the topology of the Seiberg-Witten moduli space.

The proof uses the Seiberg-Witten equations with a large parameter t multiplying the self-dual curvature. As t → ∞, solutions concentrate on a symplectic surface Poincaré dual to the basic class, and the energy identity yields the Yamabe bound. The argument also shows that if Y(M) > 0 then M has a basic class with K² ≥ 0, and if Y(M) = 4π√(2χ + 3σ) then M is a complex surface with a Kähler-Einstein metric. For manifolds with b₂⁺ = 0 the Yamabe invariant is always positive and the Seiberg-Witten invariant vanishes, so the bound is vacuous; this matches the fact that S⁴ and ℝℙ⁴ have positive Yamabe invariant but no Seiberg-Witten basic classes.

Applications include computing the Yamabe invariant for many complex surfaces. For a minimal surface of general type with c₁² > 0, the invariant equals 4π√(2χ + 3σ) − c₁², strictly less than the bound for ℂℙ². This shows the Yamabe invariant distinguishes smooth structures on homeomorphic four-manifolds: exotic Dolgachev surfaces have different Yamabe invariants from the standard rational surface. The Seiberg-Witten invariant thus bridges conformal geometry and the differential topology of four-manifolds.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Classification of Symplectic Four-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1

Symplectic four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 occupy a special place in the geography problem because their Seiberg-Witten invariants depend on a chamber determined by the period point of the symplectic form. Taubes's SW = Gr theorem identifies the Seiberg-Witten invariant of the canonical spinᶜ structure with the Gromov invariant counting pseudoholomorphic curves. For b₂⁺ = 1, the Gromov invariant can change when the symplectic form crosses a wall where the evaluation of the canonical class on the symplectic form changes sign. Li and Liu proved that the set of symplectic deformation equivalence classes is finite for a fixed intersection form, and the Seiberg-Witten invariant distinguishes them. The wall-crossing formula, derived by Li and Liu and independently by Okonek and Teleman, expresses the difference between invariants in adjacent chambers as a signed sum over basic classes. This formula computes the invariant for any chamber once known for one, typically the chamber containing the symplectic form.

A key application is classifying symplectic structures on rational and ruled surfaces. For ℂℙ² blown up at k points (k ≤ 8), the Seiberg-Witten invariant vanishes for all spinᶜ structures, reflecting the existence of positive scalar curvature metrics. For k ≥ 9, the blow‑up formula produces basic classes with non‑zero invariant, and the chamber structure matches the classification of symplectic forms by McDuff and Salamon. The invariant also detects symplectic structures on manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to standard ones, such as exotic Dolgachev surfaces E(1)ₖ. In those cases the basic class is the canonical class of a complex structure, and the invariant is ±1 in the chamber determined by the symplectic form.

The theory extends to non‑complex symplectic manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1. Using the adjunction inequality for symplectic surfaces bounds the genus of embedded surfaces representing a given homology class. Combined with the wall-crossing formula, this constrains possible symplectic forms. For example, if a manifold has a basic class K with K² = 0 and SW(K) ≠ 0, any symplectic form must evaluate positively on K, forcing the period point into a specific chamber. This interplay between gauge theory and symplectic topology is a cornerstone of modern four-manifold theory.

Seiberg-Witten Theory on Four-Manifolds with Cylindrical Ends and the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer Index Theorem

To define monopole Floer homology for a three-manifold Y, one studies the Seiberg-Witten equations on the cylindrical four-manifold Y × ℝ. Solutions must have finite energy, forcing exponential convergence to critical points of the Chern-Simons-Dirac functional on Y as the ℝ coordinate tends to ±∞. The linearization on the cylinder is a first-order elliptic operator of Dirac type. Its Fredholm index is computed by the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer (APS) index theorem for manifolds with boundary Y ⊔ (−Y). The APS theorem expresses the index as the integral of a characteristic form over the interior plus a boundary correction given by the eta invariant of the tangential operator on Y. In this context, the tangential operator is the odd signature operator coupled to the spinᶜ connection, and its eta invariant equals the spectral flow of the Chern-Simons-Dirac functional along a path of connections.

Kronheimer and Mrowka used this index formula to define a relative grading on the monopole Floer chain complex. The grading difference between two critical points equals the spectral flow of the Hessian of the Chern-Simons-Dirac functional along a connecting path, which by the APS theorem equals the index of the linearized Seiberg-Witten operator on a cylinder with appropriate asymptotics. This grading is essential for proving monopole Floer homology groups are independent of the metric and perturbation on Y. Moreover, the APS index theorem underpins the gluing theorem: the Seiberg-Witten invariant of a closed four-manifold X split along Y computes as a pairing of relative invariants in the monopole Floer homology of Y. Relative invariants count solutions on the two halves with cylindrical ends, and the gluing formula follows from a neck-stretching argument identifying the moduli space on X with the fiber product over the Floer homology of Y.

The APS index theorem also enters the excision property for the Bauer-Furuta stable homotopy invariant. When a four-manifold with boundary is given a cylindrical end, the Seiberg-Witten map between configuration spaces becomes a map between Hilbert manifolds whose linearization is a Fredholm operator of index zero. The stable homotopy type of the Conley index of this map is the Bauer-Furuta invariant. The APS theorem guarantees the index of the linearized operator on the cylinder is zero, a necessity for a well-defined Conley index. This connection among index theory, gauge theory, and Floer homology illustrates how the Seiberg-Witten equations unify four-dimensional topology.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Blow-Up Formula for Basic Classes

The blow-up formula describes how Seiberg-Witten basic classes and invariants behave under blowing up a point, i.e. forming the connected sum X # (−ℂℙ²). If X is a smooth, closed, oriented four‑manifold with b₂⁺(X) ≥ 2 and K ∈ H²(X; ℤ) is a basic class with SWX(K) ≠ 0, then the blown‑up manifold X̃ = X # (−ℂℙ²) has exactly two basic classes K̃ = K ± E, where E denotes the Poincaré dual of the exceptional sphere (with E² = −1). The invariants satisfy SWX̃(K ± E) = SWX(K). This result follows from neck‑stretching along the separating three‑sphere and the gluing theorem for Seiberg-Witten moduli spaces. The formula extends inductively to multiple blow‑ups: for X # k(−ℂℙ²) the basic classes are K ± E₁ ± … ± Ek with the same invariant. A crucial consequence is that the Seiberg-Witten invariant is a diffeomorphism invariant of the underlying smooth four‑manifold, because any two smooth structures on a given topological manifold become diffeomorphic after sufficiently many blow‑ups (stable classification). The blow‑up formula also underlies the proof that the Seiberg-Witten invariant of a symplectic four‑manifold is ±1 for the canonical class K = −c₁(X, ω), since ℂℙ² blown up at a point admits a symplectic form with canonical class K ± E.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Seiberg-Witten Invariants of 4-Manifolds with b2+ = 2 and the Geography Problem

The geography problem asks which pairs (χh, c₁²) can be realized by minimal complex surfaces of general type, or more generally by smooth, simply‑connected four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2. Seiberg-Witten theory provides sharp restrictions. For a symplectic four‑manifold X with b₂⁺ = 2, Taubes’s theorem SW = Gr implies that the canonical class K = −c₁(X, ω) is a basic class with SW(K) = ±1. The adjunction inequality for an embedded surface Σ of genus g representing a class A with A·A ≥ 0 reads 2g − 2 ≥ A·A + |⟨K, A⟩|. Applying this to a symplectic surface representing a multiple of the fiber class in a Lefschetz fibration yields the inequality c₁² ≤ 2χh + 3σ, where χh = (χ + σ)/4 is the holomorphic Euler characteristic and σ is the signature. Research has shown that for simply‑connected complex surfaces with b₂⁺ = 2 the basic classes are precisely ±K, and the Seiberg-Witten invariant vanishes for all other spinc structures. This forces the geography of such manifolds to lie in the region bounded by the Noether line c₁² = 2χh − 6 and the Bogomolov-Miyaoka-Yau line c₁² = 3χh. Moreover, the wall‑crossing formula for b₂⁺ = 1 manifolds, when applied to a one‑parameter family of metrics crossing a wall determined by a basic class, shows that the invariant changes by ±1, which in turn restricts the possible values of the period point [ω] ∈ H²(X; ℝ). These constraints have been used to classify symplectic four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 up to deformation equivalence.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Seiberg-Witten Equations on 4-Manifolds with Torsion Spinc Structures

A spinc structure on a four‑manifold X is called torsion if its determinant line bundle L has finite order in H²(X; ℤ). For such structures the Seiberg-Witten equations admit a natural perturbation by a closed two‑form η representing a de Rham class that lifts the torsion Chern class. The perturbed equations are DAΨ = 0 and FA⁺ = σ(Ψ) + iη⁺, where DA is the Dirac operator coupled to the connection A on L, and σ(Ψ) is the quadratic spinor term. It has been proved that for b₂⁺(X) ≥ 2 the Seiberg-Witten invariant of a torsion spinc structure is well defined and independent of the generic perturbation η. Moreover, if X admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, then all torsion invariants vanish. This vanishing theorem is a direct analogue of the non‑torsion case and follows from the Weitzenböck formula for the Dirac operator together with the fact that the perturbation term η⁺ can be made arbitrarily small in C⁰ norm. Torsion invariants play a central role in the construction of monopole Floer homology for three‑manifolds: the chain complex is generated by critical points of the Chern‑Simons‑Dirac functional on the three‑manifold, which correspond precisely to solutions of the Seiberg-Witten equations on the cylinder ℝ × Y with torsion spinc structure. The relative grading on this complex is given by the spectral flow of the Hessian, which by the Atiyah‑Patodi‑Singer index theorem equals the index of the linearized Seiberg-Witten operator on the cylinder. The resulting monopole Floer homology groups HM•(Y, 𝔰) are invariants of the three‑manifold Y equipped with a torsion spinc structure 𝔰, and they satisfy a surgery exact triangle analogous to the one in Heegaard Floer homology.

The Seiberg-Witten equations give a powerful obstruction to the existence of Riemannian metrics with positive scalar curvature on four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 2. These applications illustrate how Seiberg-Witten theory refines the classical differential‑topological obstructions and connects them to the geography of four‑manifolds.

For a smooth four‑manifold X with b₂⁺ = 1 the set of cohomology classes that can be represented by symplectic forms is called the symplectic cone. If K is a basic class with SW(K) ≠ 0, the wall WK = { [ω] ∈ H²(X; ℝ) | ⟨[ω], K⟩ = 0 } separates the cone into two components. The Seiberg-Witten invariant changes by ±1 when the period point crosses WK, according to the wall‑crossing formula. Consequently, a class [ω] lies in the symplectic cone if and only if it evaluates positively on every basic class K with SW(K) ≠ 0 and satisfies the Taubes constraints: [ω]² > 0 and ⟨[ω], K⟩ > 0 for the canonical class K = −c₁(X, ω). This description has been extended to manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 that are not necessarily symplectic, showing that the existence of a symplectic form is equivalent to the existence of a class [ω] with [ω]² > 0 and ⟨[ω], K⟩ > 0 for all basic classes. This criterion has been used to prove that certain simply‑connected four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, such as exotic Dolgachev surfaces, admit no symplectic structure because their basic classes force any candidate period point to lie on the wrong side of a wall. The symplectic cone description also underlies the classification of symplectic four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 up to deformation equivalence, a program completed using the Seiberg-Witten invariants of the manifolds obtained by blowing up and down.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Obstruction to Positive Scalar Curvature on 4-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1

For a smooth, closed, oriented four-manifold X with b₂⁺ = 1, the Seiberg-Witten invariant depends on a choice of chamber in H²(X;ℝ) determined by the period point of the Riemannian metric. A fundamental result of LeBrun states that if X admits a Riemannian metric of positive scalar curvature, then the Seiberg-Witten invariant SW(𝔰) must vanish for every spinᶜ structure 𝔰 whose determinant line bundle c₁(𝔰) evaluates non-negatively on the period point [ω_g] of the metric. In other words, the invariant in the chamber containing the metric's period point is zero. This sharpens the Hitchin-Thorpe inequality, which uses only the Euler characteristic χ and signature σ. The proof uses the Weitzenböck formula for the Dirac operator coupled to the spinᶜ connection; positive scalar curvature forces the harmonic spinor to vanish, so the moduli space is empty and the invariant vanishes. The wall-crossing formula then implies that the invariant in any other chamber differs by at most ±1 from the invariant in the positive scalar curvature chamber. Consequently, if X has a basic class K with SW(K) ≠ 0 in some chamber, X cannot admit a metric of positive scalar curvature unless the period point lies on the opposite side of the wall orthogonal to K. This criterion shows that many simply-connected four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, such as certain Dolgachev surfaces E(1)p,q, admit no metric of positive scalar curvature because their basic classes force the invariant to be non-zero in every chamber compatible with the intersection form.

The chamber structure for b₂⁺ = 1 is particularly simple: the walls are the hyperplanes W_K = { [ω] ∈ H²(X;ℝ) | ⟨[ω], K⟩ = 0 } for each basic class K. The Seiberg-Witten invariant is constant on each connected component of the complement of these walls. When the period point crosses a wall W_K, the invariant changes by ±1 according to the wall-crossing formula. For a metric of positive scalar curvature, the period point [ω_g] must lie in a chamber where the invariant vanishes. If X has a basic class K with K² ≥ 0, then the wall W_K divides H²(X;ℝ) into two half-spaces; the invariant in the half-space where ⟨[ω], K⟩ > 0 is often non-zero (e.g., for symplectic manifolds, the canonical class K = -c₁(X,ω) satisfies ⟨[ω], K⟩ > 0 and SW(K) = ±1). Therefore, the existence of a basic class with non-negative square and non-zero invariant in the positive chamber obstructs positive scalar curvature. This obstruction is strictly stronger than the Hitchin-Thorpe inequality; for example, the manifold ℂℙ² # (-ℂℙ²) satisfies the Hitchin-Thorpe inequality but has no basic classes, so the Seiberg-Witten obstruction is vacuous. In contrast, the Dolgachev surface E(1)2,3 has b₂⁺ = 1, χ = 12, σ = -8, and a basic class K with K² = 0 and SW(K) = ±1, which rules out positive scalar curvature even though χ + σ = 4 > 0.

An important subtlety is that the Seiberg-Witten invariant used in the obstruction must be the one computed in the chamber determined by the metric's period point. If one computes the invariant in a different chamber (e.g., the chamber containing a symplectic form), the invariant may be non-zero even though the manifold admits a positive scalar curvature metric in another chamber. This is why the trap of applying the LeBrun bound without checking the chamber is so common. The correct statement is: if X admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, then for the chamber C containing the period point [ω_g], SW_C(K) = 0 for all K. Equivalently, the invariant in the chamber of the metric vanishes. This chamber-dependent vanishing is the precise obstruction.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Existence of Einstein Metrics on Four-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1

The existence of Einstein metrics on four-manifolds is a central problem in differential geometry. Seiberg-Witten theory provides powerful obstructions. For a closed, oriented four-manifold X with b₂⁺ = 1, a theorem of LeBrun states that if X admits an Einstein metric, then either the Einstein metric has non-positive scalar curvature, or X is diffeomorphic to a connected sum of ℂℙ² and copies of -ℂℙ². In the positive scalar curvature case, the Seiberg-Witten invariant must vanish in the chamber determined by the Einstein metric. Moreover, if X admits an Einstein metric with positive scalar curvature, then the Yamabe invariant λ(X) is positive and realized by the Einstein metric. LeBrun's bound on the Yamabe invariant in terms of basic classes then forces strong restrictions on the intersection form and the basic classes. Specifically, if K is a basic class with SW(K) ≠ 0, then K² ≤ 0, and if K² = 0 then the invariant must vanish in the chamber of the Einstein metric. This implies that many four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 and non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants cannot admit Einstein metrics. For example, the Dolgachev surface E(1)p,q has a basic class with K² = 0 and non-zero invariant, so it admits no Einstein metric. On the other hand, ℂℙ² # n(-ℂℙ²) for n ≤ 8 admits Einstein metrics (Page metrics for n=1,2; Chen-LeBrun-Weber for n=3,4; etc.), and these manifolds have vanishing Seiberg-Witten invariants. The non-existence of Einstein metrics on manifolds with non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants follows from the Weitzenböck formula: an Einstein metric with positive scalar curvature forces the harmonic spinor to vanish, so the moduli space is empty. For negative scalar curvature, the Seiberg-Witten equations can have solutions, but the invariant is still constrained by the sign of the scalar curvature. The classification of Einstein four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 remains open, but Seiberg-Witten theory has narrowed the possibilities considerably.

A related result concerns the Hitchin-Thorpe inequality for Einstein metrics: any Einstein four-manifold satisfies 2χ(X) ± 3σ(X) ≥ 0. Seiberg-Witten theory sharpens this inequality. For a four-manifold with b₂⁺ = 1 and a basic class K, the inequality becomes K² ≤ 2χ + 3σ if the invariant is non-zero in the appropriate chamber. This is exactly the LeBrun bound on the Yamabe invariant specialized to Einstein metrics. The equality case corresponds to Kähler-Einstein metrics on complex surfaces. For b₂⁺ = 1, the only known Einstein metrics with positive scalar curvature are on ℂℙ² # n(-ℂℙ²) for n ≤ 8, and these have vanishing Seiberg-Witten invariants. The non-existence of Einstein metrics on manifolds with non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants provides a clear distinction between standard and exotic smooth structures: exotic smooth structures on ℂℙ² # n(-ℂℙ²) for n ≥ 2 (such as the Dolgachev surfaces for n=5) cannot admit Einstein metrics because they have non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Yamabe Invariant of Four-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1

The Yamabe invariant λ(X) of a smooth closed manifold X is the supremum over all conformal classes of the normalized total scalar curvature. For four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, LeBrun derived a sharp upper bound for λ(X) in terms of the Seiberg-Witten basic classes and the chamber structure. Let X be a four-manifold with b₂⁺ = 1 and suppose K is a basic class with SW(K) ≠ 0 in some chamber C. Then for any Riemannian metric g on X, the Yamabe invariant satisfies λ(X) ≤ 0 if K² ≤ 0, while if K² > 0 the bound gives a negative upper bound proportional to √(K²), forcing λ(X) < 0. More precisely, if the period point [ω_g] lies in a chamber where SW_C(K) ≠ 0, the bound applies. This is a direct consequence of the Weitzenböck formula and the existence of a solution to the Seiberg-Witten equations for the spinᶜ structure with determinant K. The bound is sharp: for complex surfaces of general type with b₂⁺ = 1, the canonical class K satisfies K² > 0 and the Yamabe invariant is realized by the Kähler-Einstein metric, achieving equality. For manifolds with K² = 0, such as the Dolgachev surfaces, the bound gives λ(X) ≤ 0, consistent with the non-existence of positive scalar curvature metrics. The chamber dependence is crucial: if the period point lies in a chamber where the invariant vanishes, the bound does not apply, and λ(X) could be positive. This explains why some manifolds with non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants in one chamber can still have positive Yamabe invariant if the period point is chosen in a different chamber. The Yamabe invariant is a conformal invariant, so it does not depend on the chamber; the bound must hold for all metrics, hence it uses the invariant in the chamber of the metric. This interplay between conformal geometry and gauge theory is a hallmark of Seiberg-Witten theory.

An important application is the computation of the Yamabe invariant for simply-connected four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1. For ℂℙ² # n(-ℂℙ²) with n ≤ 8, the Seiberg-Witten invariant vanishes, so the bound gives no restriction, and indeed these manifolds admit metrics of positive scalar curvature (Page metrics, etc.). For n ≥ 9, the Seiberg-Witten invariant is non-zero for some spinᶜ structures, and the bound forces λ(X) ≤ 0. In fact, LeBrun proved that for n ≥ 9, λ(ℂℙ² # n(-ℂℙ²)) = 0. For exotic Dolgachev surfaces E(1)p,q, the basic class has K² = 0, so λ(X) ≤ 0, and it is known that λ(X) = 0. The Yamabe invariant thus distinguishes between standard and exotic smooth structures on the same topological manifold: the standard ℂℙ² # 5(-ℂℙ²) has λ > 0 (for n=5 ≤ 8), while the exotic Dolgachev surface has λ = 0. This is a striking example of how Seiberg-Witten theory detects exotic smooth structures through analytic invariants.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Classification of Symplectic Four-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2

The classification of symplectic four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 is a major application of Seiberg-Witten theory. For a symplectic four-manifold (X, ω) with b₂⁺ = 2, the canonical class K = -c₁(X, ω) is a basic class with SW(K) = ±1 (Taubes's SW = Gr theorem). The symplectic cone, i.e., the set of cohomology classes represented by symplectic forms, is a union of open chambers in H²(X;ℝ) bounded by walls orthogonal to basic classes. Unlike the b₂⁺ = 1 case, for b₂⁺ ≥ 2 the Seiberg-Witten invariant is independent of the metric (it is a diffeomorphism invariant); the walls are relevant for the symplectic cone, not for the invariant itself. A conjecture, proved in many cases by Li-Liu and others, states that a class [ω] ∈ H²(X;ℝ) with [ω]² > 0 can be represented by a symplectic form if and only if ⟨[ω], K⟩ > 0 for every basic class K with SW(K) ≠ 0. This generalizes the b₂⁺ = 1 criterion. Moreover, the geography of symplectic four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 is constrained by the adjunction inequality: for any embedded symplectic surface Σ of genus g ≥ 1 representing a class A with A² ≥ 0, we have 2g - 2 ≥ A² + |⟨K, A⟩|. This inequality, combined with the existence of the canonical class, restricts the possible intersection forms and basic classes. For simply-connected symplectic four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2, significant progress toward classification up to deformation equivalence has been made using Seiberg-Witten invariants and the blow-up formula. The basic classes are either the canonical class K and its negative -K, or there may be additional basic classes. The geography problem for b₂⁺ = 2 asks which pairs (χ, σ) can be realized by simply-connected symplectic four-manifolds. Seiberg-Witten theory gives the necessary condition that there exists a basic class K with K² = 2χ + 3σ (for minimal complex surfaces of general type). Known examples include complex surfaces of general type, hypersurfaces in ℂℙ³, and certain elliptic surfaces.

A key tool is the blow-up formula: if X̃ = X # (-ℂℙ²) is the blow-up of X at a point, then the Seiberg-Witten basic classes of X̃ are K ± E, where E is the exceptional class, and SWX̃(K ± E) = SW_X(K). This allows one to reduce the classification to minimal symplectic manifolds (those with no embedded symplectic spheres of self-intersection -1). The Seiberg-Witten invariant distinguishes different symplectic structures on the same smooth manifold: for example, on ℂℙ² # 2(-ℂℙ²) there are two distinct symplectic structures with different canonical classes, detected by the Seiberg-Witten invariant. When the symplectic form crosses a wall of the symplectic cone, the symplectic deformation class changes, but the Seiberg-Witten invariant remains constant. This has been used to prove that certain four-manifolds admit no symplectic structure because any candidate period point would lie on the wrong side of a wall for some basic class.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Geography of 4-Manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3

The geography problem for smooth four-manifolds asks which pairs of integers (χ, σ) can be realized as the Euler characteristic and signature of a closed, simply-connected, smooth four-manifold. For b₂⁺ ≥ 3, the problem is significantly more complex than for b₂⁺ = 1,2. Seiberg-Witten theory provides powerful constraints through the adjunction inequality and the existence of basic classes. If X is a simply-connected four-manifold with b₂⁺ ≥ 3 and a basic class K with SW(K) ≠ 0, then for any embedded surface Σ of genus g representing a homology class A with A² ≥ 0, the adjunction inequality gives 2g - 2 ≥ A² + |⟨K, A⟩|. Applying this to a basis of H₂(X;ℤ) yields inequalities relating χ, σ, and K². In particular, K² ≤ 2χ + 3σ (the LeBrun bound). For simply-connected manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3, the geography is known to be all pairs (χ, σ) satisfying the obvious necessary conditions: χ ≥ 0, σ ≡ 0 (mod 16) for spin manifolds, and the Hitchin-Thorpe inequality 2χ ± 3σ ≥ 0. However, Seiberg-Witten theory shows that not all such pairs are realized by symplectic or complex manifolds. The existence of a basic class with K² > 0 forces χ + σ > 0, which holds for complex surfaces of general type. For non-symplectic manifolds, the Seiberg-Witten invariant may vanish, and the geography is less constrained. The work of Gurtas, Akhmedov-Park-Sakuma, and others has constructed simply-connected four-manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3 realizing many pairs (χ, σ) using Luttinger surgery (which preserves Seiberg-Witten invariants) and knot surgery (which modifies them predictably). The Seiberg-Witten invariant of the resulting manifold is determined by that of the original manifold, so the geography of manifolds with non-zero Seiberg-Witten invariants is essentially that of the building blocks. For spin manifolds, the 10/8 theorem (proved by Furuta for spin 4-manifolds) gives the stronger bound b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ|, which restricts the geography further. The 11/8 conjecture would improve this to b₂ ≥ (11/8)|σ|. Seiberg-Witten theory, combined with gauge-theoretic techniques, has thus mapped out the geography for b₂⁺ ≥ 3 to a large extent, showing that the only obstructions are the classical ones plus the 10/8 inequality for spin manifolds.

An important distinction is between the geography of all smooth manifolds and the geography of symplectic manifolds. For symplectic manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3, the canonical class K = -c₁(X, ω) is a basic class with SW(K) = ±1, and the adjunction inequality becomes an equality for symplectic surfaces. This forces the geography of symplectic manifolds to lie in a smaller region. In particular, for minimal symplectic manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3, we have K² ≥ 0 and χ + σ > 0. The classification of symplectic four-manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3 is an active area of research; Seiberg-Witten theory provides the basic constraints, but the full geography is not yet known. The Seiberg-Witten invariant also distinguishes exotic smooth structures on the same topological manifold for b₂⁺ ≥ 3. For example, exotic ℝ⁴'s have b₂⁺ = 0, but there are exotic manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3 detected by Seiberg-Witten invariants. The stable classification of four-manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3 is governed by the intersection form and Seiberg-Witten invariants. The geography problem for non-simply-connected manifolds with b₂⁺ ≥ 3 is even richer, involving the fundamental group and the Seiberg-Witten invariants of the universal cover.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Period Point for Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1

For a closed, oriented four‑manifold X with b₂⁺ = 1 the space of Riemannian metrics splits into two chambers separated by walls. Each wall is the orthogonal complement in H²(X;ℝ) of a basic class K with non‑zero Seiberg‑Witten invariant. The period point of a metric g is the projection of a self‑dual harmonic form ω_g onto the positive cone 𝒞⁺ = {α ∈ H²(X;ℝ) | α² > 0}. The chamber containing the period point determines which Seiberg‑Witten invariant SW_X(𝔰, ω) is computed; crossing a wall changes the invariant by the wall‑crossing formula

SW_X⁺(𝔰) − SW_X⁻(𝔰) = (−1)(K²+σ)/4,

where K = c₁(𝔰) and σ is the signature of X. This formula, established by Li–Liu and Okonek–Teleman, shows that the invariant depends on the chamber, not just the diffeomorphism type. Consequently, the period point becomes a crucial extra datum when using Seiberg‑Witten invariants to distinguish exotic smooth structures on manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, such as the Dolgachev surfaces E(1)p,q. In the symplectic setting, Taubes's SW=Gr theorem identifies the canonical class K_ω = −c₁(X, ω) as a basic class with SW(K_ω) = ±1, and the period point of a symplectic form ω lies in the chamber where ⟨[ω], K_ω⟩ > 0. LeBrun's Yamabe bound for b₂⁺ = 1 applies only when the period point lies in the chamber where a basic class has non‑zero invariant. Understanding the chamber structure and the position of the period point is therefore essential for any application of Seiberg‑Witten theory to geography, scalar curvature, or the classification of symplectic forms on four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Gluing Formula for Cobordisms in the TQFT Framework

The Seiberg‑Witten invariant extends to a topological quantum field theory (TQFT) in the sense of Atiyah: to each closed, oriented three‑manifold Y with a spinᶜ structure 𝔰 one assigns the monopole Floer homology groups HM_*(Y, 𝔰), and to each smooth, oriented cobordism W from Y₀ to Y₁ a homomorphism SW(W) : HM_*(Y₀, 𝔰₀) → HM_*(Y₁, 𝔰₁). When a cobordism is decomposed as W = W₁ ∪_Y W₂ along a common boundary Y, the gluing formula states that the map for the composite cobordism equals the composition of the maps for the pieces, up to a sign determined by the spectral flow of the Dirac operator on the cylindrical neck ℝ × Y. More precisely,

SW(W₂ ∪_Y W₁) = (−1)SF(D_Y) SW(W₂) ∘ SW(W₁),

where SF(D_Y) is the spectral flow of the family of Dirac operators on Y induced by the gluing parameter. This formula was established by Kronheimer–Mrowka using the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer index theorem for manifolds with cylindrical ends, and later refined by Baldwin and others to track grading shifts. The gluing formula is the computational engine behind many applications: it allows one to compute the Seiberg‑Witten invariants of manifolds obtained by knot surgery or Luttinger surgery by cutting along a torus and analysing the resulting pieces. It also underlies Manolescu's disproof of the triangulation conjecture, where Pin(2)-equivariant Seiberg‑Witten Floer homology detects non-triviality in homology spheres and the gluing formula propagates this through a hypothetical triangulation. At the level of stable homotopy, Bauer–Furuta invariants refine the TQFT to maps of spectra; a gluing formula at that level would detect exotic smooth structures invisible to integer-valued invariants.

Worked Example: Wall‑Crossing for a b₂⁺ = 1 Manifold
Let X be a simply‑connected four‑manifold with b₂⁺ = 1, b₂⁻ = 2, and intersection form ⟨1⟩ ⊕ 2⟨−1⟩. Suppose X has a basic class K with K² = −3 and SW_X(K) = 1 in the chamber where the period point ω satisfies ⟨ω, K⟩ > 0. Compute the Seiberg‑Witten invariant in the opposite chamber.
The wall‑crossing formula gives SW_X⁺(K) − SW_X⁻(K) = (−1)(K²+σ)/4. Here σ = b₂⁺ − b₂⁻ = 1 − 2 = −1. Then (K²+σ)/4 = (−3 − 1)/4 = −1, so (−1)−1 = −1. With SW_X⁺(K) = 1 we obtain 1 − SW_X⁻(K) = −1, hence SW_X⁻(K) = 2. Therefore the invariant in the opposite chamber equals 2.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for the Seiberg‑Witten invariant in a specific chamber, first identify the basic class K and the signature σ. Compute the exponent (K²+σ)/4 carefully; a sign error here flips the wall‑crossing term. Remember that the invariant in the chamber containing the symplectic form (if one exists) is always ±1 for the canonical class. For b₂⁺ = 1 manifolds, the period point determines the chamber; always check whether the given metric or symplectic form places the period point on the positive or negative side of the wall orthogonal to K.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Contact Invariant in Monopole Floer Homology

Monopole Floer homology HM_*(Y, 𝔰) assigns to a closed, oriented three‑manifold Y with a spinᶜ structure 𝔰 a ℤ‑graded abelian group invariant under diffeomorphisms. Given a contact structure ξ on Y, there is a distinguished element c(ξ) ∈ HM_*(−Y, 𝔰_ξ), where 𝔰_ξ is the spinᶜ structure induced by ξ and −Y denotes Y with reversed orientation. This contact invariant is constructed by counting solutions to the Seiberg‑Witten equations on the symplectization ℝ × Y with a perturbation forcing convergence to a Reeb orbit at the positive end and a fixed reference configuration at the negative end. Fundamental properties proved by Kronheimer–Mrowka–Ozsváth–Szabó include: (1) c(ξ) is invariant under contactomorphisms; (2) c(ξ) = 0 if ξ is overtwisted; (3) c(ξ) ≠ 0 if ξ is Stein fillable (more generally, strongly symplectically fillable). Via the isomorphism between monopole Floer homology and Heegaard Floer homology established by Kutluhan–Lee–Taubes, Colin–Ghiggini–Honda, and Taubes, the contact invariant corresponds to the Ozsváth–Szabó contact class c(ξ) ∈ HF+(−Y, 𝔰_ξ). This bridge allows translation of results between the two theories. Applications include obstructions to exact symplectic cobordisms, distinguishing contact structures in the same homotopy class, and proving the Weinstein conjecture for certain three‑manifolds. In Manolescu's disproof of the triangulation conjecture, Pin(2)-equivariant Seiberg‑Witten Floer homology and the contact invariant's behavior under connected sums yield a contradiction for a hypothetical triangulation of a homology sphere.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Obstruction to Positive Scalar Curvature on Four-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2

For a closed, oriented four‑manifold X with b₂⁺ ≥ 2, the Seiberg‑Witten invariant provides a sharp obstruction to metrics of positive scalar curvature. LeBrun proved that if X admits a metric g with scalar curvature s_g > 0 everywhere, then SW_X(𝔰) = 0 for every spinᶜ structure 𝔰. For b₂⁺ = 2 the invariant depends on a period point ω in the positive cone 𝒞⁺ ⊂ H²(X;ℝ); walls are orthogonal to basic classes. LeBrun's L²-estimate refines the vanishing theorem: if a basic class K has SW_X(K) ≠ 0 in the chamber determined by ω, then any metric g satisfies

∫_X s_g² dvol_g ≥ 32π² K².

In particular, if K² > 0 the integral is strictly positive, which is compatible with positive scalar curvature, but the inequality forces a lower bound on the L²-norm of the scalar curvature. For b₂⁺ = 2, the existence of two linearly independent basic classes with positive square typically forces the Yamabe invariant Y(X) to be negative, ruling out positive scalar curvature altogether. Examples include simply-connected manifolds with intersection form 2⟨1⟩ ⊕ n⟨−1⟩ admitting basic classes with K² > 0; these admit no positive scalar curvature metric. The obstruction lifts to covers: if the universal cover has b₂⁺ ≥ 2 and a basic class with positive square, the same bound prevents positive scalar curvature on the original manifold. This circle of ideas connects with the non-existence of Einstein metrics on many four-manifolds, since an Einstein metric with positive scalar curvature would contradict the LeBrun bound.

Seiberg-Witten Theory and the Classification of Simply-Connected Four-Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 via Basic Classes

The geography problem for simply-connected four-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 asks which pairs (χ, σ) can be realised by smooth structures on a given topological manifold. Freedman's classification reduces the problem to the intersection form; for b₂⁺ = 2 the indefinite forms are sums of hyperbolic planes and ±E₈ summands, constrained by rank and signature. Seiberg‑Witten theory imposes strong constraints: if X has a basic class K, then K is a characteristic element of the intersection form and satisfies the adjunction inequality 2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ Σ·Σ + |⟨K, Σ⟩| for any smoothly embedded surface Σ with Σ·Σ ≥ 0. Applying this to a basis of H₂(X;ℤ) yields the inequality K² ≤ 2χ + 3σ (the LeBrun bound). The set of basic classes is finite and symmetric (K −K); SW_X(K) is an integer-valued function on this set. Morgan–Szabó showed that for simply-connected manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 and non-zero Seiberg‑Witten invariant, the basic classes determine the invariant up to an overall sign. The blow-up formula SWX#ℂP̄²(K ± E) = ± SW_X(K) controls basic classes under blow-ups. Starting from exotic manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 (e.g., Dolgachev surfaces) and using knot or Luttinger surgery, one constructs infinitely many exotic structures with b₂⁺ = 2. Akhmedov–Park–Sakuma used knot surgery and Luttinger surgery to construct simply-connected manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 realising many pairs (χ, σ) satisfying the necessary congruence and Hitchin–Thorpe conditions. For spin manifolds, Furuta's 10/8 theorem gives b₂ ≥ (10/8)|σ|, restricting geography further. The 11/8 conjecture would improve this to b₂ ≥ (11/8)|σ|. At present, the geography of symplectic manifolds with b₂⁺ = 2 is not fully known; Taubes's SW = Gr theorem identifies the canonical class as a basic class with invariant ±1, and the adjunction inequality becomes an equality for symplectic surfaces, forcing K² ≥ 0 and χ + σ > 0 for minimal symplectic manifolds. The interplay between basic classes, the blow-up formula, and surgery constructions remains the primary tool for mapping the smooth geography in this range.
