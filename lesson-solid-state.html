Solid State — Crystal Lattices, Unit Cells, Packing, Defects & Band Theory

Solid state chemistry deals with the structure, properties, and synthesis of solid materials. Crystalline solids have a regular repeating arrangement of constituent particles in three dimensions — the crystal lattice. The smallest repeating unit is the unit cell, characterized by its lattice parameters (a, b, c, α, β, γ). Seven crystal systems exist: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, rhombohedral, monoclinic, and triclinic. The Bravais lattices (14 total) describe all possible distinct lattice types. Packing efficiency measures how densely the lattice is filled: simple cubic (52.4%), body-centered cubic (68%), face-centered cubic (74%). The coordination number is the number of nearest neighbors. Solids also exhibit defects (point, line, and plane defects) that strongly influence properties. Band theory explains electrical conductivity: the valence band and conduction band are separated by a band gap — conductors have overlapping bands, semiconductors have small gaps, and insulators have large gaps.

Part 1

🔷 Crystal Lattices and Unit Cells

Crystallography
Crystal Systems, Bravais Lattices, Miller Indices, and X-Ray Diffraction
A crystal lattice is an infinite array of points in space, each with identical surroundings. The unit cell is the smallest parallelepiped that generates the entire lattice by translations. The seven crystal systems are distinguished by symmetry: cubic (a=b=c, α=β=γ=90°), tetragonal (a=b≠c, all 90°), orthorhombic (a≠b≠c, all 90°), hexagonal (a=b≠c, α=β=90°, γ=120°), rhombohedral (a=b=c, α=β=γ≠90°), monoclinic (a≠b≠c, α=γ=90°, β≠90°), and triclinic (a≠b≠c, α≠β≠γ≠90°). The 14 Bravais lattices combine these systems with centering types: primitive (P), body-centered (I), face-centered (F), and base-centered (C). Miller indices (hkl) describe crystal planes: they are the reciprocals of the intercepts on the axes, cleared of fractions. The interplanar spacing d_hkl = a / √(h² + k² + l²) for cubic crystals. Bragg's law: nλ = 2d sinθ, the condition for constructive interference in X-ray diffraction from crystal planes. X-ray diffraction is used to determine crystal structures — the positions and intensities of diffraction peaks reveal the atomic arrangement.
The seven crystal systems and 14 Bravais lattices classify all possible crystalline arrangements; Miller indices and Bragg's law enable precise structural determination.
Part 2

📦 Packing in Solids

Close Packing
Hexagonal Close Packing, Cubic Close Packing, Voids, and Packing Efficiency
Close packing arranges spheres to minimize empty space. In one dimension, spheres touch along a line. In two dimensions, hexagonal close packing gives the densest layer (coordination number 6). In three dimensions, layers stack in two principal ways: ABAB pattern gives hexagonal close packing (hcp), and ABCABC pattern gives cubic close packing (ccp), which is the same as the face-centered cubic (fcc) lattice. Both hcp and ccp have 74% packing efficiency and coordination number 12. In body-centered cubic (bcc), packing efficiency is 68% with coordination number 8. In simple cubic, packing efficiency is only 52.4% with coordination number 6. Voids (interstitial sites) exist between spheres: tetrahedral voids (coordination number 4, radius ratio 0.225–0.414) and octahedral voids (coordination number 6, radius ratio 0.414–0.732). In ccp, there are 8 tetrahedral and 4 octahedral voids per unit cell. The radius ratio rule (r⁺/r⁻) predicts the coordination geometry in ionic crystals: 0.155–0.225 (trigonal), 0.225–0.414 (tetrahedral), 0.414–0.732 (octahedral), 0.732–1 (cubic). Structure types: NaCl (rock salt, octahedral), CsCl (cubic), ZnS (zinc blende, tetrahedral/cubic), wurtzite (tetrahedral/hexagonal).
Close packing achieves 74% space utilization; the type and distribution of voids determine the structures of ionic and metallic crystals.
Part 3

⚡ Defects in Solids

Imperfections
Point Defects, Line Defects, Planar Defects, and Their Effects on Properties
Point defects involve one or two lattice sites. Vacancy: a missing atom or ion. Interstitial: an extra atom in a normally vacant interstitial site. Substitutional impurity: a foreign atom replaces a host atom. Schottky defect: a pair of vacancies (one cation, one anion) to maintain electroneutrality — common in highly ionic compounds like NaCl. Frenkel defect: a cation leaves its site and occupies an interstitial site, leaving a vacancy — common in AgCl, ZnS. F-centers: an electron trapped at an anion vacancy, giving color to the crystal (e.g., NaCl turns yellow when heated in Na vapor). Line defects (dislocations): edge dislocation (extra half-plane of atoms) and screw dislocation (spiral ramp). Dislocations enable plastic deformation — their motion via slip explains why actual strengths are much lower than theoretical values. Planar defects: grain boundaries (between crystallites), twin boundaries, and stacking faults (errors in the layer sequence in close-packed structures). Non-stoichiometric defects: deviation from ideal stoichiometry due to excess metal or nonmetal, giving rise to semiconducting or color-center properties.
Defects are not always undesirable — they determine mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, color, and diffusion in solids.
Part 4

🔌 Band Theory and Electrical Properties

Electronic Structure
Band Gap, Conductors, Semiconductors, Insulators, and Magnetic Properties
Band theory describes the electronic structure of solids: atomic orbitals overlap to form continuous bands of energy levels. The valence band is the highest occupied band; the conduction band is the lowest empty band. The band gap E_g separates them. Conductors (metals): the valence and conduction bands overlap (or the conduction band is partially filled), allowing free electron flow. Insulators: large band gap (E_g > 3 eV) — electrons cannot jump to the conduction band. Semiconductors: small band gap (E_g < 3 eV) — electrons can be thermally excited to the conduction band. Intrinsic semiconductors: pure Si (E_g = 1.1 eV) or Ge (E_g = 0.7 eV). Extrinsic semiconductors: doped with impurities — n-type (donor atoms like P in Si, extra electrons) and p-type (acceptor atoms like B in Si, holes). The conductivity σ = n e μ (n = carrier concentration, μ = mobility) increases exponentially with temperature for semiconductors. Magnetic properties: diamagnetic (all electrons paired, weakly repelled), paramagnetic (unpaired electrons, attracted), ferromagnetic (permanent magnetic moments aligned, e.g., Fe, Co, Ni), antiferromagnetic (opposite alignment cancels), ferrimagnetic (unequal opposite alignment, e.g., Fe₃O₄). The Curie temperature T_C is where ferromagnetism disappears.
Band theory explains the vast range of electrical conductivities in solids; doping semiconductors enables modern electronics and optoelectronics.

Phase Transitions and Polymorphism in Crystalline Solids

Crystalline solids often exist in more than one structural arrangement, a phenomenon called polymorphism. Each polymorph corresponds to a distinct free‑energy minimum; the stable form at a given temperature and pressure is the one with the lowest Gibbs free energy. First‑order transitions involve a discontinuous change in volume, enthalpy, and entropy, and they exhibit latent heat. Classic examples include the α‑β quartz transition at 573 °C, the martensitic transformation in steel (austenite to martensite), and the graphite‑to‑diamond transition at high pressure. Second‑order (continuous) transitions show no latent heat; instead, the heat capacity, compressibility, or magnetic susceptibility diverge at the critical point. The ferromagnetic‑paramagnetic transition at the Curie temperature and the superconducting transition are typical second‑order cases. Landau theory provides a phenomenological framework: the free energy is expanded in powers of an order parameter η, and the symmetry of the high‑temperature phase dictates which terms are allowed. For a structural transition, η may be a strain component or a displacement pattern. The coefficient of the quadratic term changes sign at the transition temperature, driving the system into a lower‑symmetry state. Polymorphism is crucial in pharmaceuticals, where different crystal forms of the same molecule can have markedly different solubility and bioavailability. In competitive examinations, questions often ask to identify the order of a transition from calorimetric data or to predict the number of polymorphs expected for a given composition using the Gibbs phase rule.

Thermodynamics of Defect Formation and Equilibrium Concentrations

The equilibrium concentration of point defects is governed by the balance between the enthalpy required to create a defect and the entropy gain from the increased number of configurations. For a Schottky defect pair in an ionic crystal MX, the formation reaction can be written as a perfect lattice → cation vacancy + anion vacancy. The standard Gibbs free energy of formation ΔG_f = ΔH_f – TΔS_f. Assuming the configurational entropy dominates, ΔS_f ≈ k ln W where W is the number of ways to distribute n defect pairs over N lattice sites. Minimising the free energy yields the familiar expression n/N = exp(–ΔH_f / 2kT) for Schottky pairs (the factor 2 appears because two vacancies are created per pair). For Frenkel defects, where a cation moves to an interstitial site, the concentration is n/N = √(N_i/N) exp(–ΔH_f / 2kT), with N_i the number of interstitial sites. The pre‑exponential factor reflects the ratio of available interstitial to lattice sites. In real crystals, defect formation enthalpies range from 1–3 eV; for NaCl the Schottky enthalpy is about 2.3 eV per pair. Pressure influences defect concentrations through the volume change ΔV_f associated with defect creation: (∂ln n/∂P)_T = –ΔV_f / kT. Doping introduces extrinsic defects that can dominate the intrinsic concentration at moderate temperatures; for example, adding Ca²⁺ to NaCl creates cation vacancies to maintain charge neutrality, raising the vacancy concentration far above the intrinsic level. Understanding these relationships allows one to predict ionic conductivity, diffusion coefficients, and the temperature dependence of mechanical properties.

Arrhenius Plot for Defect Concentration
Calculate the equilibrium concentration of Schottky defect pairs in NaCl at 800 K given a formation enthalpy of 2.3 eV per pair. Assume the configurational entropy term is negligible and use k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹.
The equilibrium fraction of Schottky pairs is n/N = exp(–ΔH_f / 2kT). Substituting ΔH_f = 2.3 eV, k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹, T = 800 K gives exponent = –2.3 / (2 × 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ × 800) = –2.3 / 0.1379 ≈ –16.68. Therefore n/N = e⁻¹⁶·⁶⁸ ≈ 5.7 × 10⁻⁸. This corresponds to roughly 57 defect pairs per million lattice sites.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives ΔH_f in eV, keep the energy units consistent by using k in eV K⁻¹ (8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹). This avoids an extra conversion step and reduces arithmetic errors. Always check whether the defect reaction creates one or two vacancies; the factor of 2 in the denominator of the exponent is a frequent source of mistakes.

Electronic Structure of Transition Metal Oxides and Mott Insulators

Band theory based on independent electrons successfully describes simple metals and semiconductors, but it fails for many transition‑metal oxides where strong electron‑electron correlations dominate. In a Mott insulator, the valence band is partially filled according to band theory, yet the material is an insulator because the on‑site Coulomb repulsion U splits the band into lower and upper Hubbard bands separated by an energy gap ≈ U. The Hubbard model captures this physics: H = –t ∑⟨ij⟩σ (c†iσcjσ + h.c.) + U ∑_i ni↑ni↓, where t is the hopping integral and U the intra‑orbital repulsion. When U ≫ t, electrons localise to avoid double occupancy, producing an insulating state. Classic examples include NiO (U ≈ 8 eV, t ≈ 1 eV), CoO, and Fe₂O₃. The metal‑insulator transition can be driven by bandwidth control (pressure, chemical substitution) or by filling control (doping). In VO₂, a Peierls‑type dimerisation combined with correlation effects yields a first‑order transition at 340 K from a high‑temperature metallic rutile phase to a low‑temperature monoclinic insulating phase. Spectroscopic techniques such as photoemission and X‑ray absorption reveal the Hubbard bands and the quasiparticle weight near the Fermi level. In competitive examinations, typical questions ask to distinguish a charge‑transfer insulator (where the gap is between O 2p and metal 3d states) from a Mott‑Hubbard insulator (gap within the metal 3d manifold) and to predict the effect of increasing bandwidth on the insulating gap.

Magnetic Ordering Mechanisms: Superexchange, Double Exchange, and RKKY Interaction

Magnetic order in solids arises from quantum‑mechanical exchange interactions between localized or itinerant spins. Superexchange operates in insulating oxides where magnetic cations are separated by non‑magnetic anions (e.g., O²⁻). The Goodenough‑Kanamori rules state that 180° cation‑anion‑cation pathways with half‑filled orbitals favour antiferromagnetic coupling, while 90° pathways or orbitals with different occupancy can give ferromagnetic coupling. MnO (rocksalt) exemplifies antiferromagnetic superexchange with a Néel temperature of 118 K. Double exchange occurs in mixed‑valence oxides such as La₁₋ₓSrₓMnO₃, where Mn³⁺ (t₂g³e_g¹) and Mn⁴⁺ (t₂g³e_g⁰) coexist. The e_g electron can hop between neighbouring Mn sites only if the core t₂g spins are parallel, leading to ferromagnetic metallic behaviour for x ≈ 0.3. The kinetic energy gain of the itinerant electron drives the alignment. The Ruderman‑Kittel‑Kasuya‑Yosida (RKKY) interaction mediates coupling between localized magnetic moments via conduction electrons in metals and intermetallics. The exchange integral oscillates with distance as J(R) ∝ cos(2k_F R)/R³, producing complex magnetic structures such as helices or spin‑density waves in rare‑earth metals (e.g., Gd, Tb). In competitive examinations, candidates are often required to apply the Goodenough‑Kanamori rules to predict the sign of the exchange in a given crystal structure, or to explain why double‑exchange systems exhibit colossal magnetoresistance near the Curie temperature.

Applications of Band Engineering: Heterostructures, Quantum Wells, and Topological Insulators

Modern solid‑state devices exploit precise control of band alignment at interfaces. In a semiconductor heterojunction, the relative positions of the conduction‑band minimum and valence‑band maximum of the two materials define the band‑offset type. Type‑I (straddling) alignment confines both electrons and holes in the same layer, as in GaAs/AlGaAs quantum wells used for lasers. Type‑II (staggered) alignment separates electrons and holes into different layers, enabling spatially indirect excitons and long‑lived charge separation for photovoltaic applications. Type‑III (broken‑gap) alignment, seen in InAs/GaSb, places the conduction band of one material below the valence band of the other, allowing tunnelling devices such as resonant‑tunnelling diodes. Quantum confinement in wells thinner than the exciton Bohr radius quantises the subband energies, shifting optical transitions to higher energies; the energy spacing scales as 1/L² for an infinite well of width L. Two‑dimensional materials (graphene, MoS₂, phosphorene) provide atomically thin channels where the band structure can be tuned by stacking order, twist angle, or external fields. Twisted bilayer graphene near the magic angle (≈ 1.1°) exhibits flat bands leading to correlated insulating and superconducting states. Topological insulators such as Bi₂Se₃ possess an inverted bulk band gap and gapless surface states protected by time‑reversal symmetry; the surface Dirac cone exhibits spin‑momentum locking, promising for spintronic devices. In competitive examinations, problems may ask to sketch band diagrams for a given heterostructure, calculate the ground‑state energy of a particle in a finite quantum well, or identify the topological invariant (Z₂ index) from the parity eigenvalues at time‑reversal‑invariant momenta.

  • Assuming all transition‑metal oxides are Mott insulators; some are charge‑transfer insulators where the gap involves ligand p states.
  • Misapplying the Goodenough‑Kanamori rules to 90° pathways without checking orbital occupancy.
  • Neglecting the temperature dependence of the pre‑exponential factor when plotting Arrhenius graphs for defect concentrations.
  • Treating quantum‑well subband spacing as linear in well width instead of the inverse‑square dependence.
  • Overlooking that topological surface states require an odd number of Dirac cones and time‑reversal symmetry protection.
  • Forgetting that the RKKY interaction oscillates with distance, leading to possible non‑collinear magnetic structures.
