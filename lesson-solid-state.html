Solid State — Crystal Lattices, Unit Cells, Packing, Defects & Band Theory

Solid state chemistry deals with the structure, properties, and synthesis of solid materials. Crystalline solids have a regular repeating arrangement of constituent particles in three dimensions — the crystal lattice. The smallest repeating unit is the unit cell, characterized by its lattice parameters (a, b, c, α, β, γ). Seven crystal systems exist: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, rhombohedral, monoclinic, and triclinic. The Bravais lattices (14 total) describe all possible distinct lattice types. Packing efficiency measures how densely the lattice is filled: simple cubic (52.4%), body-centered cubic (68%), face-centered cubic (74%). The coordination number is the number of nearest neighbors. Solids also exhibit defects (point, line, and plane defects) that strongly influence properties. Band theory explains electrical conductivity: the valence band and conduction band are separated by a band gap — conductors have overlapping bands, semiconductors have small gaps, and insulators have large gaps.

"The properties of solids are determined not by their perfections, but by their imperfections."
— Frederick Seitz
Part 1

🔷 Crystal Lattices and Unit Cells

Crystallography
Crystal Systems, Bravais Lattices, Miller Indices, and X-Ray Diffraction
A crystal lattice is an infinite array of points in space, each with identical surroundings. The unit cell is the smallest parallelepiped that generates the entire lattice by translations. The seven crystal systems are distinguished by symmetry: cubic (a=b=c, α=β=γ=90°), tetragonal (a=b≠c, all 90°), orthorhombic (a≠b≠c, all 90°), hexagonal (a=b≠c, α=β=90°, γ=120°), rhombohedral (a=b=c, α=β=γ≠90°), monoclinic (a≠b≠c, α=γ=90°, β≠90°), and triclinic (a≠b≠c, α≠β≠γ≠90°). The 14 Bravais lattices combine these systems with centering types: primitive (P), body-centered (I), face-centered (F), and base-centered (C). Miller indices (hkl) describe crystal planes: they are the reciprocals of the intercepts on the axes, cleared of fractions. The interplanar spacing d_hkl = a / √(h² + k² + l²) for cubic crystals. Bragg's law: nλ = 2d sinθ, the condition for constructive interference in X-ray diffraction from crystal planes. X-ray diffraction is used to determine crystal structures — the positions and intensities of diffraction peaks reveal the atomic arrangement.
The seven crystal systems and 14 Bravais lattices classify all possible crystalline arrangements; Miller indices and Bragg's law enable precise structural determination.
Part 2

📦 Packing in Solids

Close Packing
Hexagonal Close Packing, Cubic Close Packing, Voids, and Packing Efficiency
Close packing arranges spheres to minimize empty space. In one dimension, spheres touch along a line. In two dimensions, hexagonal close packing gives the densest layer (coordination number 6). In three dimensions, layers stack in two principal ways: ABAB pattern gives hexagonal close packing (hcp), and ABCABC pattern gives cubic close packing (ccp), which is the same as the face-centered cubic (fcc) lattice. Both hcp and ccp have 74% packing efficiency and coordination number 12. In body-centered cubic (bcc), packing efficiency is 68% with coordination number 8. In simple cubic, packing efficiency is only 52.4% with coordination number 6. Voids (interstitial sites) exist between spheres: tetrahedral voids (coordination number 4, radius ratio 0.225–0.414) and octahedral voids (coordination number 6, radius ratio 0.414–0.732). In ccp, there are 8 tetrahedral and 4 octahedral voids per unit cell. The radius ratio rule (r⁺/r⁻) predicts the coordination geometry in ionic crystals: 0.155–0.225 (trigonal), 0.225–0.414 (tetrahedral), 0.414–0.732 (octahedral), 0.732–1 (cubic). Structure types: NaCl (rock salt, octahedral), CsCl (cubic), ZnS (zinc blende, tetrahedral/cubic), wurtzite (tetrahedral/hexagonal).
Close packing achieves 74% space utilization; the type and distribution of voids determine the structures of ionic and metallic crystals.
Part 3

⚡ Defects in Solids

Imperfections
Point Defects, Line Defects, Planar Defects, and Their Effects on Properties
Point defects involve one or two lattice sites. Vacancy: a missing atom or ion. Interstitial: an extra atom in a normally vacant interstitial site. Substitutional impurity: a foreign atom replaces a host atom. Schottky defect: a pair of vacancies (one cation, one anion) to maintain electroneutrality — common in highly ionic compounds like NaCl. Frenkel defect: a cation leaves its site and occupies an interstitial site, leaving a vacancy — common in AgCl, ZnS. F-centers: an electron trapped at an anion vacancy, giving color to the crystal (e.g., NaCl turns yellow when heated in Na vapor). Line defects (dislocations): edge dislocation (extra half-plane of atoms) and screw dislocation (spiral ramp). Dislocations enable plastic deformation — their motion via slip explains why actual strengths are much lower than theoretical values. Planar defects: grain boundaries (between crystallites), twin boundaries, and stacking faults (errors in the layer sequence in close-packed structures). Non-stoichiometric defects: deviation from ideal stoichiometry due to excess metal or nonmetal, giving rise to semiconducting or color-center properties.
Defects are not always undesirable — they determine mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, color, and diffusion in solids.
Part 4

🔌 Band Theory and Electrical Properties

Electronic Structure
Band Gap, Conductors, Semiconductors, Insulators, and Magnetic Properties
Band theory describes the electronic structure of solids: atomic orbitals overlap to form continuous bands of energy levels. The valence band is the highest occupied band; the conduction band is the lowest empty band. The band gap E_g separates them. Conductors (metals): the valence and conduction bands overlap (or the conduction band is partially filled), allowing free electron flow. Insulators: large band gap (E_g > 3 eV) — electrons cannot jump to the conduction band. Semiconductors: small band gap (E_g < 3 eV) — electrons can be thermally excited to the conduction band. Intrinsic semiconductors: pure Si (E_g = 1.1 eV) or Ge (E_g = 0.7 eV). Extrinsic semiconductors: doped with impurities — n-type (donor atoms like P in Si, extra electrons) and p-type (acceptor atoms like B in Si, holes). The conductivity σ = n e μ (n = carrier concentration, μ = mobility) increases exponentially with temperature for semiconductors. Magnetic properties: diamagnetic (all electrons paired, weakly repelled), paramagnetic (unpaired electrons, attracted), ferromagnetic (permanent magnetic moments aligned, e.g., Fe, Co, Ni), antiferromagnetic (opposite alignment cancels), ferrimagnetic (unequal opposite alignment, e.g., Fe₃O₄). The Curie temperature T_C is where ferromagnetism disappears.
Band theory explains the vast range of electrical conductivities in solids; doping semiconductors enables modern electronics and optoelectronics.
